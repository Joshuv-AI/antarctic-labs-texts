Gauging the complex SYK model
Ziruo Zhang, Cheng Peng

Abstract
Motivated by SYK-like models describing near-BPS black holes in string/M-theory, we consider gauging the U ⁡ ( 1 ) \mathrm{U}(1) symmetry of the complex SYK model in the presence of a Wilson line with charge k k . At a fixed background gauge field, solutions to the Schwinger-Dyson equations display vastly different properties from those at a fixed real chemical potential. In the partition function and the two-point function, the integral over the gauge field is performed either directly or via a large N N saddle point approximation, and both results are consistent with exact diagonalization data. From the behaviour of the two-point function at large N N , we deduce that the conformal symmetry at low energies is preserved at fixed κ = k / N = 0 \kappa=k/N=0 , but broken at κ ≠ 0 \kappa\neq 0 . In addition, we find that there is maximal chaos for all k k .

We consider gauging the global

2

2

2.1
Gauge fixing

It is convenient to carry out our subsequent analysis in a fixed gauge. The one-dimensional equivalent of the Lorenz gauge,
\partial_{\tau}A_{\tau}=0
, can always be reached by the gauge transformation

\alpha(\tau)=\frac{\tau u}{\beta}-\int_{0}^{\tau}d\tau^{\prime}A_{\tau}(\tau^{\prime})\,,\quad u\equiv\int_{0}^{\beta}d\tau^{\prime}A_{\tau}(\tau^{\prime})\,,

(2.4)

so that the transformed gauge field is a constant

A_{\tau}^{\prime}=A_{\tau}+\partial_{\tau}\alpha=\frac{u}{\beta}=\text{const}\,.

(2.5)

Note that (
2.4
) is a well-defined
\mathrm{U}(1)
transformation since
\alpha(\tau+\beta)=\alpha(\tau)
. The residual gauge transformations must preserve the Lorenz gauge, which means the gauge transformation parameters satisfy
\partial_{\tau}^{2}\alpha=0
. This means that
\alpha
is at most linear in
\tau
. The only option compatible with
\alpha
being periodic in
\tau
modulo
2\pi
is

\alpha(\tau)=\frac{2\pi n\tau}{\beta}+\alpha_{0}\,,\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}\,,\quad\alpha_{0}=\text{const}\,.

(2.6)

These are the constant (
n=0
) and large gauge transformations under which
u\mapsto u+2\pi n
. They are fixed by restricting the range of
u
to be
(-\pi,\pi]
. Different values of
u
in this range are not gauge-equivalent to each other, since they are not related by a well-defined gauge transformation. Strictly speaking, the gauge fixing procedure introduces ghost fields, but the path integral over ghost fields in an abelian theory is just an overall numerical factor that is independent of
u
.

The partition function of the theory can be computed by first performing the path integral over the fermions and then path integrating (it is actually only an ordinary integral) over
u

\displaystyle

\displaystyle Z_{k}=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}du\,e^{-iku}\widetilde{Z}(u)\,,\quad\widetilde{Z}(u)\equiv\int\prod_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{D}[\psi_{i}^{\dagger}]\mathcal{D}[\psi_{i}]e^{-S[u,\psi,\psi^{\dagger}]}\,,

(2.7)

\displaystyle S[u,\psi,\psi^{\dagger}]\equiv\int_{0}^{1}d\tau\left[\sum_{i=1}^{N}\psi_{i}^{\dagger}\left(\partial_{\tau}-iu\right)\psi_{i}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j_{1}<\ldots<j_{q/2}\\
k_{1}<\ldots<k_{q/2}\end{subarray}}\beta J_{j_{1}\cdots j_{q/2},k_{1}\cdots k_{q/2}}\psi_{j_{1}}^{\dagger}\cdots\psi_{j_{q/2}}^{\dagger}\psi_{k_{1}}\cdots\psi_{k_{q/2}}\right]

In the above, we have changed coordinates to
\tau^{\prime}=\tau/\beta\in[0,1]
so that the imaginary time direction is a unit circle, and the theory is characterized by the dimensionless coupling
\beta J_{j_{1}\cdots j_{q/2},k_{1}\cdots k_{q/2}}
.

2.3
Anomaly free condition and charge quantisation

Since we want to gauge the
\mathrm{U}(1)
symmetry, the theory must be free of gauge anomalies. In a quantum mechanics on
S^{1}
, there is a possible anomaly under large gauge transformations with nonzero winding number. This can be seen both in the Hamiltonian and path integral formalisms. In the Hamiltonian formalism, we have chosen an operator ordering in
\hat{Q}
such that it takes integer spaced values from
-\frac{N}{2}
to
\frac{N}{2}
. Therefore, the Hilbert space will be a direct sum of projective
\mathrm{U}(1)
representations if
N
is odd. Meanwhile, the Wilson line carries charge
k
, which is also a projective representation if
k\notin\mathbb{Z}
. The total system is therefore anomaly free if

k+\frac{N}{2}\in\mathbb{Z}\,.

(2.17)

Alternatively, a more direct way to get this condition is by writing the partition function as a sum over charge sectors. Given that
\hat{Q}
commutes with
\hat{H}_{0}
,

\displaystyle Z_{k}

\displaystyle=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}du\,e^{-iku}\widetilde{Z}(u)=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}du\,e^{-iku}\Tr_{\mathcal{H}}e^{iu\hat{Q}-\hat{H}_{0}}=\sum_{Q=-\frac{N}{2}}^{\frac{N}{2}}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}du\,e^{iu(Q-k)}\Tr_{\mathcal{H}_{Q}}e^{-\hat{H}_{0}}\,.

(2.18)

The exponential factor
e^{iu(Q-k)}
is invariant under large gauge transformations of
u
if and only if the condition (
2.17
) holds. From the integral over
u
in this expression, we also learn that the values of
k
giving nonzero values of
Z_{k}
coincide with the allowed values of
Q
, which are

k=-\frac{N}{2},-\frac{N}{2}+1,\ldots,\frac{N}{2}-1,\frac{N}{2}\,.

(2.19)

On the other hand, one can imagine computing the path integral for
\widetilde{Z}(u)
. The chosen operator ordering in the Hamiltonian formalism corresponds to a regularisation of the path integral where the fermion partition function
\widetilde{Z}(u)
acquires an overall sign
(-1)^{Nn}
under a gauge transformation with winding number
n
. The charge
k
Wilson line transforms as
(-1)^{2kn}
under the same large gauge transformation. In order for the total system to be gauge invariant, one again needs (
2.17
). We shall see this explicitly when considering the regularisation of the large
N
on-shell action in Section
4
.

These observations are just another manifestation of previous results in
[
28
,
29
]
, see also
[
30
]
. As emphasized in
[
28
]
, our situation is identical to the “parity anomaly” in 3d Chern-Simons-matter theories, and (
2.17
) is exactly the same as the quantization condition for the Chern-Simons level. This is not surprising since a theory like (
2.1
) can be obtained by dimensionally reducing a 3d Chern-Simons-matter theory on a compact 2d manifold.

is a solution of (
3.7
) at
u+2\pi n
.

3.1
Properties of the on-shell action and 2-point function

Since the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}(u)
in (
2.11
) is not hermitian, observables of the theory have rather unusual properties, which we aim to introduce and derive in this section.
6
6

6

There are other non-Hermitian SYK-like models such as
[
31
]
and references therein, and it is interesting to make contact with those models.
These in turn descend to properties of solutions to the Schwinger-Dyson equations which are not commonly seen in the literature.

We consider the partition function

\widetilde{Z}(u)=\Tr_{\mathcal{H}}e^{-\hat{H}(u)}\,,

(3.8)

and the 2-point function

\widetilde{G}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\equiv\frac{1}{N}\Tr_{\mathcal{H}}\left[e^{-\hat{H}(u)}\text{T}\hat{\psi}_{i}^{\dagger}(\tau_{2})\hat{\psi}_{i}(\tau_{1})\right]\,,

(3.9)

where T is the time ordering operator. In the large
N
saddle point approximation, they are computed by sums over solutions
G_{0}
to the Schwinger-Dyson equations

\displaystyle\widetilde{Z}(u)

\displaystyle=\int\mathcal{D}[G]\mathcal{D}[\Sigma]e^{-S[u,G,\Sigma]}=\sum_{G_{0}\,\in\,\text{SD sols}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Delta(G_{0})}}e^{-S[u,G_{0},\Sigma_{0}]}(1+\mathcal{O}(N^{-1}))\,,

(3.10)

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})

\displaystyle=\int\mathcal{D}[\psi^{\dagger}]\mathcal{D}[\psi]\frac{1}{N}\psi_{i}^{\dagger}(\tau_{2})\psi_{i}(\tau_{1})e^{-S[u,\psi,\psi^{\dagger}]}=\int\mathcal{D}[G]\mathcal{D}[\Sigma]G(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})e^{-S[u,G,\Sigma]}

\displaystyle=\sum_{G_{0}\,\in\,\text{SD sols}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Delta(G_{0})}}e^{-S[u,G_{0},\Sigma_{0}]}\left[G_{0}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})+\mathcal{O}(N^{-1})\right]\,,

where
\Sigma_{0}
is determined in terms of
G_{0}
via the second equation of (
3.7
), and
\Delta(G_{0})
is the determinant of the kinetic operator appearing at quadratic order in the expansion of
\widetilde{S}=S/N
around
G_{0}
. Concretely, it is the determinant of the operator sandwiched between two
\delta G
’s in (
6.2
). Notice that
\Delta(G_{0})\sim\mathcal{O}(N^{0})
and it is subleading in
1/N
. Using the operator definitions (
3.8
) and (
3.9
), one can derive various properties of
\widetilde{Z}
and
\widetilde{G}
. These in turn descend to properties of
G_{0}
and the on-shell action, if we assume that they are satisfied independently for each solution of the Schwinger-Dyson equations.

KMS condition

As usual,
\widetilde{G}
is anti-periodic on the circle and satisfies the KMS condition

\widetilde{G}(\tau+1)=-\widetilde{G}(\tau)\,,

(3.11)

where we again use time translational invariance to express
\widetilde{G}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})
as a function of
\tau_{12}\equiv\tau_{1}-\tau_{2}
. To show this we notice that for
\tau\in(-1,0)
, we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}(\tau+1)=-\frac{1}{N}\Tr_{\mathcal{H}}\left[e^{-\hat{H}}\hat{\psi}_{i}(\tau+1)\hat{\psi}_{i}^{\dagger}(0)\right]=-\frac{1}{N}\Tr_{\mathcal{H}}\left[e^{\tau\hat{H}}\hat{\psi}_{i}(0)e^{-(\tau+1)\hat{H}}\hat{\psi}_{i}^{\dagger}(0)\right]

(3.12)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{N}\Tr_{\mathcal{H}}\left[\hat{\psi}_{i}(\tau)e^{-\hat{H}}\hat{\psi}_{i}^{\dagger}(0)\right]=-\frac{1}{N}\Tr_{\mathcal{H}}\left[e^{-\hat{H}}\hat{\psi}_{i}^{\dagger}(0)\hat{\psi}_{i}(\tau)\right]=-\widetilde{G}(\tau)\,.

A minus sign appears in the first equality because the time ordering includes a sign if two fermionic operators are swapped. The second to last equality uses the cyclicity of the trace. Notice that a cyclic permutation of fermionic operators in the trace does not introduce any sign, as it is clear by representing each operator in the trace as a matrix.

Consequently, the same should hold for each solution
G_{0}
, i.e.

\widetilde{G}_{0}(\tau+1)=-\widetilde{G}_{0}(\tau)\,.

(3.13)

We can therefore expand
G_{0}
in Matsubara frequencies which are half integer multiples of
2\pi

\omega_{n}=2\pi\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\,.

(3.14)

Reality of
\widetilde{Z}

Usually, the reality of
\widetilde{Z}
follows directly from the hermiticity of
\hat{H}
. But here this is not obvious since the Hamiltonian
\hat{H}(u)
in (
2.11
) is not hermitian. Nevertheless,
\widetilde{Z}(u)
is real because

\displaystyle\widetilde{Z}(u)^{*}

\displaystyle=\left(\Tr_{\mathcal{H}}e^{iu\hat{Q}-\hat{H}_{0}}\right)^{*}=\bigg(\sum_{Q=-N/2}^{N/2}e^{iuQ}\Tr_{\mathcal{H}_{Q}}e^{-\hat{H}_{0}}\bigg)^{*}=\sum_{Q=-N/2}^{N/2}e^{-iuQ}\Tr_{\mathcal{H}_{Q}}e^{-\hat{H}_{0}}=\widetilde{Z}(-u)

(3.15)

\displaystyle=\sum_{Q=-N/2}^{N/2}e^{-iuQ}\sum_{n=1}^{\dim\mathcal{H}_{Q}}\langle n|\hat{C}^{2}e^{-\hat{H}_{0}}\hat{C}^{2}|n\rangle=\sum_{Q=-N/2}^{N/2}e^{-iuQ}\sum_{n=1}^{\dim\mathcal{H}_{Q}}\langle n|\hat{X}^{\dagger}\hat{K}e^{-\hat{H}_{0}}\hat{C}|n\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{Q=-N/2}^{N/2}e^{-iuQ}\sum_{n=1}^{\dim\mathcal{H}_{Q}}(\hat{C}|n\rangle)^{\dagger}e^{-\hat{H}_{0}}\hat{C}|n\rangle=\sum_{Q=-N/2}^{N/2}e^{-iuQ}\Tr_{\mathcal{H}_{-Q}}e^{-\hat{H}_{0}}=\widetilde{Z}(u)\,.

In the second line,
\{|n\rangle\}
is the basis of
\mathcal{H}_{Q}
in (
2.15
) and
\hat{C}
is the charge conjugation operator defined in (
2.12
). The second equality of the second line uses the fact that
\hat{C}
commutes with
\hat{H}_{0}
, like any symmetry generator of the original hamiltonian, and
(-1)^{\frac{N(N-1)}{2}}\hat{C}=\hat{X}^{\dagger}\hat{K}
. To get to the last line, note that
\langle n|\hat{X}^{\dagger}=(\hat{C}|n\rangle)^{T}
and
\hat{K}e^{-\beta\hat{H}_{0}}\hat{C}|n\rangle=(e^{-\beta\hat{H}_{0}}\hat{C}|n\rangle)^{\dagger\;T}=((\hat{C}|n\rangle)^{\dagger}e^{-\beta\hat{H}_{0}})^{T}
. The second to last equality uses the fact that
\{\hat{C}|n\rangle\}
is a basis for
\mathcal{H}_{-Q}
, as stated in (
2.16
). The last equality follows from a relabelling of charges
Q\mapsto-Q
. A partial result of the above is that the partition function of
\hat{H}_{0}
over the charge
k
and charge
-k
sectors are equal, i.e.

Z_{k}=\Tr_{\mathcal{H}_{k}}e^{-\hat{H}_{0}}=\Tr_{\mathcal{H}_{-k}}e^{-\hat{H}_{0}}=Z_{-k}\,.

(3.16)

Assuming that the contribution in (
3.10
) of each Schwinger-Dyson solution to
\widetilde{Z}
is real, one gets that

\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Delta(G_{0})}}e^{-S[u,G_{0},\Sigma(G_{0})]}\in\mathbb{R}\,,\quad\text{and is an even function of $u$}\,.

(3.17)

Complex conjugate of
\widetilde{G}

One important peculiarity of having a nonzero gauge field and a non-hermitian Hamiltonian (
2.11
) is that
\widetilde{G}
is not real. Firstly, note that the 2-point function for
\tau\in(0,1)
can be written as

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}(\tau)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{N}\Tr_{\mathcal{H}}\left[e^{-(1-\tau)\hat{H}}\hat{\psi}_{i}(0)e^{-\tau\hat{H}}\hat{\psi}_{i}^{\dagger}(0)\right]=-\frac{1}{N}\Tr_{\mathcal{H}}\left[e^{iu(1-\tau)\hat{Q}-(1-\tau)\hat{H}_{0}}\hat{\psi}_{i}(0)e^{iu\tau\hat{Q}-\tau\hat{H}_{0}}\hat{\psi}_{i}^{\dagger}(0)\right]

(3.18)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{Q=-N/2}^{N/2-1}e^{iu\tau+iuQ}\Tr_{\mathcal{H}_{Q}}\left[e^{-(1-\tau)\hat{H}_{0}}\hat{\psi}_{i}(0)e^{-\tau\hat{H}_{0}}\hat{\psi}_{i}^{\dagger}(0)\right]

To get the last equality, we used the fact that if
|n\rangle
has charge
Q
, then
\hat{\psi}_{i}^{\dagger}(0)|n\rangle
(when nonzero) has charge
Q+1
. The state with maximum charge
Q=N/2
does not contribute since it is annihilated by all
\hat{\psi}_{i}^{\dagger}(0)
. Similarly, the 2-point function for
\tau\in(-1,0)
is

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\Tr_{\mathcal{H}}\left[e^{-(1+\tau)\hat{H}}\hat{\psi}_{i}^{\dagger}(0)e^{\tau\hat{H}}\hat{\psi}_{i}(0)\right]=\frac{1}{N}\Tr_{\mathcal{H}}\left[e^{iu(1+\tau)\hat{Q}-(1+\tau)\hat{H_{0}}}\hat{\psi}_{i}^{\dagger}(0)e^{-iu\tau\hat{Q}+\tau\hat{H}_{0}}\hat{\psi}_{i}(0)\right]

(3.19)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{Q=-N/2+1}^{N/2}e^{iu\tau+iuQ}\Tr_{\mathcal{H}_{Q}}\left[e^{-(1+\tau)\hat{H}_{0}}\hat{\psi}_{i}^{\dagger}(0)e^{\tau\hat{H}_{0}}\hat{\psi}_{i}(0)\right]\,.

Now, making use of the charge conjugation symmetry like in (
3.15
), the complex conjugate of the 2-point function on
\tau\in(0,1)
can be written as

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}(\tau;u)^{*}=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{Q=-N/2}^{N/2-1}e^{-iu\tau-iuQ}\Tr_{\mathcal{H}_{Q}}\left[\hat{\psi}_{i}(0)e^{-\tau\hat{H}_{0}}\hat{\psi}_{i}^{\dagger}(0)e^{-(1-\tau)\hat{H}_{0}}\right]=\widetilde{G}(\tau;-u)

(3.20)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{Q=-N/2}^{N/2-1}e^{-iu\tau-iuQ}\sum_{n=1}^{\dim\mathcal{H}_{Q}}\langle n|\hat{C}^{2}\hat{\psi}_{i}(0)e^{-\tau\hat{H}_{0}}\hat{\psi}_{i}^{\dagger}(0)e^{-(1-\tau)\hat{H}_{0}}\hat{C}^{2}|n\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{Q=-N/2}^{N/2-1}e^{-iu\tau-iuQ}\sum_{n=1}^{\dim\mathcal{H}_{Q}}\langle n|\hat{X}^{\dagger}\hat{K}\hat{\psi}_{i}^{\dagger}(0)e^{-\tau\hat{H}_{0}}\hat{\psi}_{i}(0)e^{-(1-\tau)\hat{H}_{0}}\hat{C}|n\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{Q=-N/2}^{N/2-1}e^{-iu\tau-iuQ}\sum_{n=1}^{\dim\mathcal{H}_{Q}}(\hat{C}|n\rangle)^{\dagger}e^{-(1-\tau)\hat{H}_{0}}\hat{\psi}_{i}^{\dagger}(0)e^{-\tau\hat{H}_{0}}\hat{\psi}_{i}(0)\hat{C}|n\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{Q=-N/2}^{N/2-1}e^{-iu\tau-iuQ}\Tr_{\mathcal{H}_{-Q}}\left[e^{-(1-\tau)\hat{H}_{0}}\hat{\psi}_{i}^{\dagger}(0)e^{-\tau\hat{H}_{0}}\hat{\psi}_{i}(0)\right]

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{Q=-N/2+1}^{N/2}e^{-iu\tau+iuQ}\Tr_{\mathcal{H}_{Q}}\left[e^{-(1-\tau)\hat{H}_{0}}\hat{\psi}_{i}^{\dagger}(0)e^{-\tau\hat{H}_{0}}\hat{\psi}_{i}(0)\right]=-\widetilde{G}(-\tau;u)=\widetilde{G}(1-\tau;u)\,.

In the third line, we have used the commutation relations (
2.13
). The second to last equality holds by comparing with (
3.19
), and the last equality uses the KMS condition (
3.11
).

Assuming that the contribution in (
3.10
) of each Schwinger-Dyson solution to
\widetilde{G}
satisfies the same relations, and using (
3.17
), one gets that

G_{0}(\tau;u)^{*}=G_{0}(1-\tau;u)=G_{0}(\tau;-u)\,,\quad\tau\in(0,1)

(3.21)

or in terms of the real and imaginary parts,

\displaystyle\re G_{0}(1-\tau;u)

\displaystyle=\re G_{0}(\tau;u)\,,\quad\im G_{0}(1-\tau;u)=-\im G_{0}(\tau;u)\,,

(3.22)

\displaystyle\re G_{0}(\tau;-u)

\displaystyle=\re G_{0}(\tau;u)\,,\quad\im G_{0}(\tau;-u)=-\im G_{0}(\tau;u)\,.

In words, the real part of
G_{0}
is symmetric under reflection about
\tau=\frac{1}{2}
, or
u\mapsto-u
, whereas its imaginary part is antisymmetric. Substituting (
3.21
) into the complex conjugate of the second equation in (
3.7
), one finds that
\Sigma_{0}
also satisfies
\Sigma_{0}(\tau;u)^{*}=\Sigma_{0}(1-\tau;u)=\Sigma_{0}(\tau,-u)
. In frequency space, (
3.21
) and the same equation for
\Sigma_{0}
are equivalent to

G_{0}(\omega_{n};u)^{*}=-G_{0}(\omega_{n};u)=G_{0}(-\omega_{n};-u)\,,\quad\Sigma_{0}(\omega_{n};u)^{*}=-\Sigma_{0}(\omega_{n};u)=\Sigma_{0}(-\omega_{n};-u)

(3.23)

The Fourier coefficients of
G_{0}
and
\Sigma_{0}
are purely imaginary, as opposed to
G_{0}(\omega_{n})^{*}=G_{0}(-\omega_{n})
and
\Sigma_{0}(\omega_{n})^{*}=\Sigma_{0}(-\omega_{n})
for the usual case when
\widetilde{G}
is real. Both relations half the degrees of freedom in the Fourier coefficients, but in a different way.

Boundary values of
\widetilde{G}

The boundary values of
\re\widetilde{G}
at
\tau=0^{+},1^{-}
is dictated by the canonical commutation relations
\{\psi_{i},\psi_{i}^{\dagger}\}=1\;\forall i
. Specifically, we must have

\displaystyle 2\re\widetilde{G}(0^{+})

\displaystyle=2\re\widetilde{G}(1^{-})=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\left(\widetilde{G}(\epsilon)+\widetilde{G}(1-\epsilon)\right)=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\left(\widetilde{G}(\epsilon)-\widetilde{G}(-\epsilon)\right)

(3.24)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{N}\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\Tr_{\mathcal{H}}e^{-\hat{H}}\Big[\hat{\psi}_{i}^{\dagger}(0)\hat{\psi}_{i}(-\epsilon)+\hat{\psi}_{i}(\epsilon)\hat{\psi}_{i}^{\dagger}(0)\Big]=-\widetilde{Z}\,.

The first and second equalities follow from (
3.21
), and the last equality uses the canonical commutation relation. Assuming that (
3.24
) holds for the contribution of each Schwinger-Dyson solution to
\widetilde{G}
and
\widetilde{Z}
together with the relation (
3.10
), one obtains

\re G_{0}(0^{+})=\re G_{0}(1^{-})=-\frac{1}{2}\,.

(3.25)

In a similar fashion, the boundary values of
\im\widetilde{G}
are determined by the expectation value of
\hat{Q}
:

\displaystyle\im\widetilde{G}(0^{+})

\displaystyle=-\im\widetilde{G}(1^{-})=\frac{1}{2i}\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\left(\widetilde{G}(\epsilon)-\widetilde{G}(1-\epsilon)\right)=\frac{1}{2i}\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\left(\widetilde{G}(\epsilon)+\widetilde{G}(-\epsilon)\right)

(3.26)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2iN}\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\Tr_{\mathcal{H}}e^{-\hat{H}}\Big[\hat{\psi}_{i}^{\dagger}(0)\hat{\psi}_{i}(-\epsilon)-\hat{\psi}_{i}(\epsilon)\hat{\psi}_{i}^{\dagger}(0)\Big]=\frac{1}{iN}\Tr_{\mathcal{H}}e^{-\hat{H}}\hat{Q}=-\frac{1}{N}\partial_{u}\widetilde{Z}\,.

The first and second equalities follow from (
3.21
), while the last equality comes from the
u
-dependence of
\hat{H}
in (
2.11
). Substituting (
3.10
) into (
3.26
) and equating the contributions from each solution, one obtains

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Delta(G_{0})}}e^{-S[u,G_{0},\Sigma_{0}]}\im G_{0}(0^{+})(1+\mathcal{O}(N^{-1}))=-\frac{1}{N}\partial_{u}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Delta(G_{0})}}e^{-S[u,G_{0},\Sigma_{0}]}(1+\mathcal{O}(N^{-1}))\right]

(3.27)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Delta(G_{0})}}e^{-S[u,G_{0},\Sigma_{0}]}\left(\partial_{u}\widetilde{S}+\frac{\partial_{u}\Delta(G_{0})}{2N\Delta(G_{0})}+\mathcal{O}(N^{-1})\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\Delta(G_{0})}}e^{-S[u,G_{0},\Sigma_{0}]}\left(\partial_{u}\widetilde{S}+\mathcal{O}(N^{-1})\right)\,.

Note that (
3.17
) has been used in the first equality when taking the imaginary part of
\widetilde{G}
. Equating the leading order terms then gives

\im G_{0}(0^{+})=\partial_{u}\widetilde{S}\,.

(3.28)

3.2
Conformal solutions

Following the same steps as in
[
9
]
, we redefine
\widetilde{\Sigma}(\omega_{n})\equiv\Sigma(\omega_{n})-iu
or equivalently
\widetilde{\Sigma}(\tau)=\Sigma(\tau)-iu\delta(\tau)
, which simplifies the Schwinger-Dyson equations to

\displaystyle G(\omega_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{i}{\omega_{n}-i\widetilde{\Sigma}(\omega_{n})}\approx-\frac{1}{\widetilde{\Sigma}(\omega_{n})}\,,

(3.29)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Sigma}(\tau)

\displaystyle=-iu\delta(\tau)+(\beta J)^{2}G(\tau)^{\frac{q}{2}}\left(-G(-\tau)\right)^{\frac{q}{2}-1}=(\beta J)^{2}G(\tau)^{\frac{q}{2}}\left(-G(-\tau)\right)^{\frac{q}{2}-1}\,.

The first approximation is valid at frequencies that are small relative to the effective coupling, that is,
|\omega_{n}|\ll(\beta J)^{2}
(or equivalently at time scales
\Delta\tau\gg 1/(\beta J)^{2}
), while the equality in the second line holds if we are only interested in the domain
\tau\in(0,1)
away from the singular points
\tau=0,1
.
The simplified equations are invariant under diffeomorphisms of the circle, and admit conformal invariant solutions.

Let us emphasize that we only anticipate this enhanced conformal symmetry to occur in the deep IR
|\omega_{n}|\ll(\beta J)^{2}
, where the gauge field could in principle get heavily renormalised, so there is no reason to expect a priori that the expectation value of
u
is the same as the
u
we started with in the UV (
3.7
). From now on, we denote the renormalized gauge field in the IR by
u_{\text{IR}}
. As derived in Appendix
A
, a generic conformal solution in the domain
\tau\in(-1,1)
satisfying the various properties derived in Section
3.1
must have the form

G_{0}(\tau)=-ge^{iu_{\text{IR}}\tau}\bigg|\frac{\pi}{\sin(\pi\tau)}\bigg|^{2\Delta}\left[e^{-\frac{iu_{\text{IR}}}{2}}\Theta(\tau)-e^{\frac{iu_{\text{IR}}}{2}}\Theta(-\tau)\right]\,,\quad g\in\mathbb{R}\,,\quad\sgn(g)=(-1)^{\lfloor\frac{u_{\text{IR}}+\pi}{2\pi}\rfloor}\,.

(3.30)

The Fourier transform of this ansatz is

G_{0}(\omega_{n})=-g(2\pi)^{2\Delta}\frac{i(-1)^{n}\Gamma(1-2\Delta)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{3}{2}+n-\frac{u_{\text{IR}}}{2\pi}-\Delta\right)\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}-n+\frac{u_{\text{IR}}}{2\pi}-\Delta\right)}\,.

(3.31)

To be precise, the Fourier integral is only convergent for
\Delta<\frac{1}{2}
, but we use the analytic continuation of the formula when
\Delta
is beyond this range. Plugging this into the IR equations (
3.29
), one observes that we must have
\Delta=1/q
in order for
\Sigma(\omega_{n})G(\omega_{n})
to be independent of
n
, and the equations also determine the coefficient
g
to be

g=(-1)^{\lfloor\frac{u_{\text{IR}}+\pi}{2\pi}\rfloor}\left[\frac{\left(1-\frac{2}{q}\right)\sin\frac{2\pi}{q}}{2\pi(\beta J)^{2}\left(\cos u_{\text{IR}}+\cos\frac{2\pi}{q}\right)}\right]^{\frac{1}{q}}\,.

(3.32)

Since
g
must be real, and
q
is even, the quantity in the square bracket must be real and positive, i.e.

\cos u_{\text{IR}}+\cos\frac{2\pi}{q}>0\,,\quad u_{\text{IR}}\in\left(-\frac{\pi(q-2)}{q},\frac{\pi(q-2)}{q}\right)\mod 2\pi\,.

(3.33)

Thus the allowed values of
u_{\text{IR}}
lie in intervals which are centered around
2\pi n
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, with a spread of
\left(-\frac{\pi(q-2)}{q},\frac{\pi(q-2)}{q}\right)\subset(-\pi,\pi)
around the centre
2\pi n
. Explicitly, we can thus write

u_{\text{IR}}=\widetilde{u}_{\text{IR}}+2\pi n\,,\quad\widetilde{u}_{\text{IR}}\in\left(-\frac{\pi(q-2)}{q},\frac{\pi(q-2)}{q}\right)\ .

(3.34)

In addition, the integer
n
labelling the centre of each allowed interval of
u_{\text{IR}}
also determines the sign of
g
in (
3.30
) since

\left\lfloor\frac{u_{\text{IR}}+\pi}{2\pi}\right\rfloor=\left\lfloor\frac{\widetilde{u}_{\text{IR}}+\pi}{2\pi}\right\rfloor+n=n\,.

(3.35)

The last equality follows from

\frac{\widetilde{u}_{\text{IR}}+\pi}{2\pi}\in\left(\frac{1}{q},1-\frac{1}{q}\right)\,.

(3.36)

To summarize, we expect that the solutions to (
3.7
) within
(\beta J)^{-2}\ll\tau\ll 1-(\beta J)^{-2}
are well approximated by the conformal solution

G_{0}(\tau)=-(-1)^{n}\left[\frac{\left(1-\frac{2}{q}\right)\sin\frac{2\pi}{q}}{2\pi(\beta J)^{2}\left(\cos u_{\text{IR}}+\cos\frac{2\pi}{q}\right)}\right]^{\frac{1}{q}}e^{iu_{\text{IR}}(\tau-\frac{1}{2})}\bigg[\frac{\pi}{\sin(\pi\tau)}\bigg]^{2/q}\,.

(3.37)

The actual solution to (
3.7
) will differ from (
3.37
) when
\tau
is within distance
\sim(\beta J)^{-2}
to the points
\tau=0,1
since (
3.37
) diverges near
\tau=0,1
, while
G_{0}(\tau)
must be finite in order to satisfy (
3.25
).

One can follow the same steps as in
[
9
]
to derive the Luttinger-Ward identity expressing the difference between the boundary values of
G_{0}
in terms of
u_{\text{IR}}
. Since (
3.37
) is identical to the conformal solution in the presence of a chemical potential under the identification
2\pi\mathcal{E}=-iu_{\text{IR}}
, where
\mathcal{E}
is the spectral asymmetry parameter used in
[
9
]
, the result is simply an analytic continuation of the formula in
[
9
]
:

\frac{1}{2i}(G_{0}(0^{+})-G_{0}(1^{-}))=\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{q}\right)\frac{\sin u_{\text{IR}}}{\cos u_{\text{IR}}+\cos\big(\frac{2\pi}{q}\big)}+\frac{1}{2\pi}\log\left[\frac{\cos\big(\frac{\pi}{q}-\frac{u_{\text{IR}}}{2}\big)}{\cos\big(\frac{\pi}{q}+\frac{u_{\text{IR}}}{2}\big)}\right]\,.

(3.38)

Due to the range of
u_{\text{IR}}
in (
3.33
), the
\log
term is real, which is consistent with the expectation from (
3.21
) that the left hand side is real.

3.3
Numerical solution to Schwinger-Dyson equations

We can solve the Schwinger-Dyson equations (
3.7
) numerically using the standard iterative method in
[
12
]
. Specifically, we sample the interval
(0,1)
at
M
points

\tau_{r}=\frac{r-1/2}{M}\,,\quad r=1\,,\ldots\,,M

(3.39)

where
M
is a large even positive integer, after which
G
and
\Sigma
are represented by the lists of their values at these points
\{G(\tau_{r})\}
,
\{\Sigma(\tau_{r})\}
. Alternatively, via the discrete Fourier transform, one can represent the same data using lists of Fourier coefficients
\{G(\omega_{r})\}
and
\{\Sigma(\omega_{r})\}
, where the sample frequencies are

\omega_{r}=2\pi\left(r-\frac{1}{2}-\frac{M}{2}\right)\,,\quad r=1\,,\ldots\,,M\,.

(3.40)

Since
M
is even, the frequencies are symmetrically distributed around zero, with the same number of positive and negative frequencies. The algorithm to solve (
3.7
) begins with a set of initial data
\{G_{0}(\omega_{r})\}
that is preferably a good approximation to the exact solution. In this discussion, the subscript
k
on
G_{k}
labels the number of iterations, and
G_{k}
is the result after
k
iterations. In the
k^{\text{th}}
iteration,
\{G_{k-1}(\omega_{r})\}
is converted to
\{G_{k-1}(\tau_{r})\}
using the inverse Fourier transform and plugged into the second equation of (
3.7
) to give
\{\Sigma_{k-1}(\tau_{r})\}
. After another Fourier transform to
\{\Sigma_{k-1}(\omega_{r})\}
, we update the result from
\{G_{k-1}(\omega_{r})\}
to
\{G_{k}(\omega_{r})\}
using the rule

G_{k}(\omega_{r})=(1-x)G_{k-1}(\omega_{r})+x\frac{i}{\omega_{r}-u_{\text{uv}}-i\Sigma_{k-1}(\omega_{r})}\,,

(3.41)

where
x\in(0,1)
is an arbitrary weighting factor. Note that if
\{G_{k-1}(\omega_{r})\}
and
\{G_{k}(\omega_{r})\}
are equal, it means that
\{G_{k}(\omega_{r})\}
satisfies (
3.7
) and the solution has been found. To test this, we compute an error estimate

\Delta G_{k}=\frac{1}{M}\left[\sum_{r=1}^{M}\big|G_{k}(\omega_{r})-G_{k-1}(\omega_{r})\big|^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}

(3.42)

after each iteration. If
\Delta G_{k}<\epsilon
for some chosen precision tolerance
\epsilon
, the algorithm terminates and the result is taken as an approximate solution. If
\Delta G_{k}>\Delta G_{k-1}
, that is if the updated data is a worse approximation to the solution than the result of the previous iteration, the weighting factor
x
is halved. This is a numerical trick in
[
12
]
that seems to improve convergence. Unless otherwise stated, we shall consider the model with
q=4
and the initial value of
x=1/2
.

Using the algorithm above, we want to find all possible solutions for
G(\tau)
as
u\in(-\pi,\pi)
is varied. Firstly, all of the solutions that we found at large
\beta J
agree with the conformal solution for
(\beta J)^{-1}\ll\tau\ll 1-(\beta J)^{-1}
, regardless of the value of
u
. See Figure
1
for an illustrative example. This is in contrast with the case of a chemical potential
\mu
, where there is a gapped phase when
|\mu|\gg J
. One can understand this difference from the Hamiltonian in (
2.11
). The magnitudes of contributions to the partition function depend on the real part
\hat{H}_{0}
of
\hat{H}
and are not affected by
u
, which only controls the imaginary part of
\hat{H}
. In the case of a chemical potential, the magnitudes of contributions vary with
\mu
because there is a competition between the terms
\mu\hat{Q}
and
\hat{H}_{0}
, which are both real.

In addition, we observe that the solutions (such as Figure
1
) satisfy the relations (
3.21
), (
3.22
), (
3.25
), and (
3.38
). Some of these should only be true when charge conjugation symmetry is preserved by
\hat{H}_{0}
. This is an important motivation for choosing the charge conjugation invariant operator ordering of
\hat{H}_{0}
in (
2.11
). This would later allow us to compare results from exact diagonalization with those from the large
N
saddle point analysis.

It turns out that the numerics in this case are more subtle than the case of a chemical potential; there are multiple fixed points of the iterative algorithm and therefore the numerics is very sensitive to the initial data provided at the beginning of the iterations. To better handle this sensitivity, we scan over
u
in small increments. Namely, we start with
u=\Delta u/2
where
\Delta u=\pi/M
for some large integer
M
and take a constant
G(\tau)=-\frac{1}{2}
as initial data. The solution found is then fed as initial data to find the solution at a slightly larger value of
u=3\Delta u/2
. This is then iterated, where the solution at each value of
u
is used as initial data for
u+\Delta u
. When
u=\pi-\Delta u/2
is reached at the
M
-th step, the next point
u=\pi+\Delta u/2
is not in the range
(-\pi,\pi)
, but it can be brought back to
u=-\pi+\Delta u/2
via a large gauge transformation. We thus take the solution for
u=\pi-\Delta u/2
, apply the gauge transformation
G\mapsto e^{-2\pi i\tau}G
, and use this as initial data to solve for the solution at
u=-\pi+\Delta u/2
. Repeating this process allows us to scan over
u\in(-\pi,\pi)
repeatedly. We also applied this method to scan over
u
in negative increments. Each numerical solution at a fixed
u
is fitted against the conformal solution (
3.37
) to determine
u_{\text{IR}}
, and the left panel of Figure
2
shows the plot of
u_{\text{IR}}
as a function of
u
obtained in this way.

Importantly, there are infinitely many solutions with different values of

which solve (

. For each solution, the best-fit value of

. In the left panel of Figure

\widetilde{Z}(u+2\pi n)=(-1)^{Nn}\widetilde{Z}(u)
, which is in turn equivalent to the charge quantization (

).

Alternatively, the situation is gauge equivalent to working with the
single
branch of solutions with
n=0
, but spread across
u\in\mathbb{R}
instead of
u\in(-\pi,\pi)
, as we will explain in the following. Consider
G_{0}(\tau;u_{0},n)
, the solution at
u=u_{0}\in(-\pi,\pi)
on the branch
n
. Due to the invariance of (

) under large gauge transformations as discussed below (

),

must be a solution at

, and we checked this numerically on every solution found in the left panel of Figure

. The values of
u_{\text{IR}}
obtained from the gauge transformed solutions are plotted in the right panel of Figure

, where points which are related by a gauge transformation are displayed in the same colour. In addition, it is simple to check that multiplying the conformal ansatz with parameter
u_{\text{IR}}=u_{\text{IR},0}
in (

) by
e^{-2\pi in\tau}
shifts the parameter to
u_{\text{IR}}=u_{\text{IR},0}-2\pi n
. Therefore
e^{-2\pi in\tau}G_{0}(\tau;u_{0},n)
will have a fitted

that is on the branch

, according to the definition (

), (

). This is numerically confirmed by the range of

in the right panel of Figure

G_{0}(\tau;u-2\pi n,0)\equiv e^{-2\pi in\tau}G_{0}(\tau;u,n)\,,\quad u\in(-\pi,\pi)\,.

(3.43)

Since

, working with the gauge transformed solutions for every

will extend the range of

from
(-\pi,\pi)
to
(-\infty,\infty)
. As

, we find that the fitted values of

, which are the maximum or minimum values allowed by the consistency condition (

) when

.

3.4
Large q analysis

As an aside, we note that the Schwinger-Dyson equations (
3.7
) can be solved analytically in the large
q
limit, by adapting Appendix A.1 of
[
32
]
. This method of taking the large
q
limit is distinct from that in Appendix C of
[
25
]
. Firstly, we redefine

G(\tau)\rightarrow e^{iu\tau}G(\tau)\,,\quad\Sigma(\tau)\rightarrow e^{iu\tau}\Sigma(\tau)\,,

(3.44)

so that in terms of the new variables,
u
is absent from the equations

\partial_{\tau_{1}}G(\tau_{12})+\int_{0}^{1}d\tau_{3}\Sigma(\tau_{13})G(\tau_{32})=-\delta(\tau_{12})\,,\quad\Sigma(\tau)=(\beta J)^{2}G(\tau)\left[-G(\tau)G(-\tau)\right]^{\frac{q}{2}-1}\,.

(3.45)

In exchange, the KMS condition is modified to

G(\tau\pm 1)=-e^{\mp iu}G(\tau)\,,\quad\Sigma(\tau\pm 1)=-e^{\mp iu}\Sigma(\tau)\,.

(3.46)

We consider the ansatz

G(\tau)=\left(\mathcal{Q}-\frac{1}{2}\sgn(\tau)\right)e^{\frac{1}{q}g(\tau)}\,,\quad\mathcal{Q}\equiv i\im G(0^{\pm})\,,

(3.47)

where
\mathcal{Q}
and
g
are assumed to be
\mathcal{O}(1)
, and we want to solve for
g(\tau)
in the range
\tau\in(0,1)
. For the moment,
\mathcal{Q}
is an undetermined parameter which specifies the boundary condition of
G
. It turns out that the equations determining
g(\tau)
are most conveniently written in terms of the combinations

g_{\pm}(\tau)\equiv\frac{1}{2}\left(g(\tau)\pm g(-\tau)\right)\,.

(3.48)

Before deriving these equations however, we first examine the boundary conditions for
g_{\pm}
because they will be used in the process.

Boundary conditions

From the properties of solutions to the Schwinger-Dyson equations in (
3.25
) and (
3.26
), we know that
G(0^{\pm})=\mathcal{Q}\mp\frac{1}{2}
. On the ansatz (
3.47
), this implies

g(0^{\pm})=2\pi iqn_{\pm}\,,\qquad n_{\pm}\in\mathbb{Z}\,,

(3.49)

which in turn implies

g_{\pm}(0^{+})=\pi iq(n_{+}\pm n_{-})\,.

(3.50)

The reality property
G(\tau)^{*}=-G(-\tau)
still holds after the redefinition (
3.44
). This implies
g(\tau)^{*}=g(-\tau)+2\pi iqn_{\text{cc}}
. Evaluating on
\tau=0^{+}
, we see that the integer ambiguity is determined to be
n_{\text{cc}}=-n_{+}-n_{-}
, i.e.

g(\tau)^{*}-g(-\tau)=-2\pi iq(n_{+}+n_{-})\,.

(3.51)

In addition, the modified KMS condition (
3.46
) implies

\displaystyle g(1-\tau)

\displaystyle=g(-\tau)+q\log\left(\frac{1+2\mathcal{Q}}{1-2\mathcal{Q}}\right)-iqu+2\pi iql\,,

(3.52)

\displaystyle g(\tau-1)

\displaystyle=g(\tau)-q\log\left(\frac{1+2\mathcal{Q}}{1-2\mathcal{Q}}\right)+iqu+2\pi iqm\,,\qquad l,m\in\mathbb{Z}\,.

Subtracting the second equation from the complex conjugate of the first equation gives
m=-l
upon using (
3.51
). One also needs to note that
\mathcal{Q}
is imaginary by its definition (
3.47
). Taking the sum of the two equations in (
3.52
) then gives

g_{+}(1-\tau)=g_{+}(\tau)\,.

(3.53)

In other words,
g_{+}
is symmetric or even under the reflection
\tau\mapsto 1-\tau
about the axis
\tau=\frac{1}{2}
. Evaluating this at
\tau=0^{+}
gives the boundary condition
g_{+}(1^{-})=\pi iq(n_{+}+n_{-})
. Evaluating the difference of the two equations in (
3.52
) at
\tau=0^{+}
then determines
g_{-}(1^{-})
. In summary,
g_{\pm}
need to satisfy (
3.53
) and the boundary conditions

\displaystyle g_{+}(0^{+})

\displaystyle=g_{+}(1^{-})=\pi iq(n_{+}+n_{-})\,,\quad g_{-}(0^{+})=\pi iq(n_{+}-n_{-})\,,

(3.54)

\displaystyle g_{-}(1^{-})

\displaystyle=\pi iq(n_{-}-n_{+})+q\log\left(\frac{1+2\mathcal{Q}}{1-2\mathcal{Q}}\right)-iqu+2\pi il\,.

Equations for
g_{\pm}

We proceed to derive the differential equations satisfied by
g_{\pm}
. Substituting the ansatz (
3.47
) into the equation for
\Sigma
in (
3.45
) gives

\Sigma(\tau)=(\beta J)^{2}\left(\frac{1}{4}-\mathcal{Q}^{2}\right)^{\frac{q}{2}-1}\left(\mathcal{Q}-\frac{1}{2}\sgn(\tau)\right)e^{\frac{1}{q}g(\tau)+\left(1-\frac{2}{q}\right)g_{+}(\tau)}\,.

(3.55)

Using
\partial_{\tau_{1}}G(\tau_{12})=-\partial_{\tau_{2}}G(\tau_{12})
, setting
\tau_{1}=0
, and relabelling
\tau=-\tau_{2}
,
\tau^{\prime}=\tau_{3}
, the first equation of (
3.45
) becomes

\partial_{\tau}G(\tau)+\int_{0}^{1}d\tau^{\prime}\Sigma(-\tau^{\prime})G(\tau^{\prime}+\tau)=-\delta(\tau)\,.

(3.56)

Despite the additional exponential factors in (
3.46
), we still have
\Sigma(-\tau^{\prime})G(\tau^{\prime}+\tau)=\Sigma(1-\tau^{\prime})G(\tau^{\prime}-1+\tau)
. Using this rewriting in (
3.56
) and redefining
\tau^{\prime}-1\rightarrow\tau^{\prime}
then gives

\partial_{\tau}G(\tau)+\int_{-1}^{0}d\tau^{\prime}\Sigma(-\tau^{\prime})G(\tau^{\prime}+\tau)=-\delta(\tau)\,.

(3.57)

Notice that the range of
\tau^{\prime}
has been shifted with respect to (
3.56
). Let
\tau>0
. After substituting the ansatz (
3.47
) into (
3.57
) and multiplying it by
qG(\tau)^{-1}
, one obtains

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}g(\tau)+(\beta\mathcal{J})^{2}\int_{-1}^{0}d\tau^{\prime}

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{Q}-\frac{1}{2}\sgn(\tau^{\prime}+\tau)\right)e^{\frac{1}{q}g(-\tau^{\prime})+\left(1-\frac{2}{q}\right)g_{+}(-\tau^{\prime})+\frac{1}{q}g(\tau+\tau^{\prime})-\frac{1}{q}g(\tau)}=0\,,

(3.58)

\displaystyle(\beta\mathcal{J})^{2}

\displaystyle\equiv q(\beta J)^{2}\left(\frac{1}{4}-\mathcal{Q}^{2}\right)^{\frac{q}{2}-1}\,.

We assume that
\beta\mathcal{J}
is kept constant as
q\rightarrow\infty
. On the other hand, (
3.56
) relabeled using
\tau\rightarrow-\tau
is

\partial_{\tau}G(-\tau)-\int_{0}^{1}d\tau^{\prime}\,\Sigma(-\tau^{\prime})G(\tau^{\prime}-\tau)=\delta(-\tau)\,.

(3.59)

Like before, we consider
\tau>0
, substitute (
3.47
) into (
3.59
), and multiply the equation by
qG(-\tau)^{-1}
. This gives

\partial_{\tau}g(-\tau)-(\beta\mathcal{J})^{2}\int_{0}^{1}d\tau^{\prime}\left(\mathcal{Q}+\frac{1}{2}\sgn(\tau-\tau^{\prime})\right)e^{\frac{1}{q}g(-\tau^{\prime})+\left(1-\frac{2}{q}\right)g_{+}(-\tau^{\prime})+\frac{1}{q}g(\tau^{\prime}-\tau)-\frac{1}{q}g(-\tau)}=0\,.

(3.60)

If we redefine the dummy integration variable in (
3.58
) as
\tau^{\prime}\rightarrow-\tau^{\prime}
and add or subtract (
3.60
), we have

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}g_{+}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\frac{(\beta\mathcal{J})^{2}}{2}\int_{0}^{1}d\tau^{\prime}\sgn(\tau-\tau^{\prime})e^{\left(1-\frac{2}{q}\right)g_{+}(\tau^{\prime})}+\mathcal{O}(q^{-1})\,,

(3.61)

\displaystyle\partial_{\tau}g_{-}(\tau)

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{Q}(\beta\mathcal{J})^{2}\int_{0}^{1}d\tau^{\prime}e^{\left(1-\frac{2}{q}\right)g_{+}(\tau^{\prime})}+\mathcal{O}(q^{-1})\,.

Taking the
\tau
derivative of (
3.61
) then gives

\partial_{\tau}^{2}g_{+}(\tau)=(\beta\mathcal{J})^{2}e^{\left(1-\frac{2}{q}\right)g_{+}(\tau)}+\mathcal{O}(q^{-1})\,,\quad\partial_{\tau}^{2}g_{-}(\tau)=\mathcal{O}(q^{-1})\,,\quad\tau>0\,.

(3.62)

Going back to the equation for
g_{-}
in (
3.61
), we see from the expression for
\partial_{\tau}^{2}g_{+}
in (
3.62
) that

\partial_{\tau}g_{-}(\tau)=-\mathcal{Q}\int_{0}^{1}d\tau^{\prime}\partial_{\tau^{\prime}}^{2}g_{+}(\tau^{\prime})=-\mathcal{Q}\,\partial_{\tau^{\prime}}g_{+}(1^{-})+\mathcal{Q}\,\partial_{\tau^{\prime}}g_{+}(0^{+})=2\mathcal{Q}\,\partial_{\tau^{\prime}}g_{+}(0^{+})\,.

(3.63)

In the last equality, we have used the symmetry (
3.53
) to rewrite
\partial_{\tau^{\prime}}g_{+}(1^{-})=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{g_{+}(1)-g_{+}(1-\epsilon)}{\epsilon}=\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{g_{+}(0)-g_{+}(\epsilon)}{\epsilon}=-\partial_{\tau^{\prime}}g_{+}(0^{+})
.

Solutions for
g_{\pm}

Let us first focus on the solution for
g_{+}
. The general solution to the differential equation in (
3.62
) is

e^{\left(1-\frac{2}{q}\right)g_{+}(\tau)}=\frac{2v^{2}}{(\beta\mathcal{J})^{2}\left(1-\frac{2}{q}\right)\sin^{2}(v\tau+b)}\,.

(3.64)

Imposing
g_{+}(1-\tau)=g_{+}(\tau)
implies that
v+2b=\pi n
, where
n
is an odd integer since
b
is defined modulo
\pi
. Eliminating
b=\frac{\pi n}{2}-\frac{v}{2}
allows us to rewrite
\sin^{2}(v\tau+b)=\cos^{2}(v\tau-v/2)
. Subsequently, imposing
g_{+}(0^{+})=g_{+}(1^{-})=\pi iq(n_{+}+n_{-})
in (
3.54
) implies

\frac{2v^{2}}{(\beta\mathcal{J})^{2}\left(1-\frac{2}{q}\right)\cos^{2}\left(\frac{v}{2}\right)}=1\,,\quad v\in(0,\pi)\,,

(3.65)

since
q
is even. This allows us to determine
v
given
\beta\mathcal{J}
. We assume that this equation has no solutions for
v\notin\mathbb{R}
. Note that
v
and
-v
give the same solution for
g_{+}
, so we can restrict to
v>0
without loss of generality. However,
v>\pi
must be excluded, since otherwise
G(\tau)
will have singularities in the range
\tau\in(0,1)
. This gives the range
v\in(0,\pi)
. Since
q-2
is even, there is a choice of positive or negative roots in

e^{\frac{1}{q}g_{+}(\tau)}=e^{\pi i(n_{+}+n_{-})}\left[\frac{\cos\left(\frac{v}{2}\right)}{\cos\left(v\tau-\frac{v}{2}\right)}\right]^{\frac{2}{q-2}}\,,

(3.66)

which is determined by
g_{+}(0^{+})=g_{+}(1^{-})=\pi iq(n_{+}+n_{-})
.

We now turn to the solution for
g_{-}
. Plugging the solution for
g_{+}
into (
3.63
) gives

\partial_{\tau}g_{-}(\tau)=-\frac{4\mathcal{Q}v\tan\frac{v}{2}}{1-\frac{2}{q}}\,,

(3.67)

and therefore

g_{-}(\tau)=-\frac{4\mathcal{Q}v\tan\frac{v}{2}}{1-\frac{2}{q}}\tau+b\,,\quad b\in\mathbb{C}\,.

(3.68)

The boundary condition
g_{-}(0^{+})=\pi iq(n_{+}-n_{-})
in (
3.54
) then sets
b=\pi iq(n_{+}-n_{-})
, while the boundary condition for
g_{-}(1^{-})
implies

iu=\log\left(\frac{1+2\mathcal{Q}}{1-2\mathcal{Q}}\right)+\frac{4\mathcal{Q}v\tan\frac{v}{2}}{q-2}+2\pi in\,,\quad n=n_{-}-n_{+}+l\,.

(3.69)

The
2\pi\mathbb{Z}
ambiguity in
u
arises from the invariance of the Schwinger-Dyson equations (
3.5
) under large gauge transformations. If we fix the gauge by restricting
u\in(0,2\pi)
, the integer
n
in (
3.69
) is fixed for a given
\mathcal{Q}
such that
u
is within
(0,2\pi)
.

<-2\pi\,,\quad\text{if}\;\;n\geq 2\end{cases}\,,

(3.70)

Appendix A
Conformal ansatz

We want to constrain the 2-point function
\widetilde{G}
using
\text{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})
conformal invariance in the presence of the constant gauge field
u_{\text{IR}}
, and thereby also constrain solutions
G_{0}
to the Schwinger-Dyson equations. This discussion is an adaptation of
[
25
,
53
]
. From an “active” point of view, a conformal transformation acts as

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\tau\mapsto\tau^{\prime}\,,\quad\tan\left(\pi\tau^{\prime}\right)=\frac{a\tan\left(\pi\tau\right)+b}{c\tan\left(\pi\tau\right)+d}\,,\quad a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{R}\,,\quad ad-bc=1\,,

(A.1)

\displaystyle\psi_{i}(\tau)\mapsto\psi_{i}^{\prime}(\tau^{\prime})=\left(\frac{d\tau^{\prime}}{d\tau}\right)^{-\Delta}\psi_{i}(\tau)\,,\quad A_{\tau}(\tau)\mapsto A_{\tau^{\prime}}(\tau^{\prime})=\frac{d\tau}{d\tau^{\prime}}A_{\tau}(\tau)\,.

This coordinate transformation is the pullback to the interval
(-1/2,1/2)
of the fractional linear action of
\text{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})
on
\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
via the map
\tau\mapsto\tan(\pi\tau)
. Here
\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
denotes the one point compactification of
\mathbb{R}
where
\pm\infty
are identified, corresponding to the identification of
\pm 1/2
in the interval. Since
\tan^{-1}
is ambiguous mod
\pi
, the branch of
\tau^{\prime}
should be chosen such that the function
\tau^{\prime}(\tau)
is continuous. As a diffeomorphism,
\tau^{\prime}(\tau)
covers
S^{1}
exactly once (winding number 1) and
\tau^{\prime}\mapsto\tau^{\prime}+\beta
as
\tau\mapsto\tau+\beta
. The important point to note here is that the gauge field after the conformal transformation is not what we started with:
u_{\text{IR}}
is mapped to
u_{\text{IR}}\frac{d\tau}{d\tau^{\prime}}
. To bring the gauge field back to its original value, we need to perform a compensating
\mathrm{U}(1)
gauge transformation with parameter

\alpha(\tau^{\prime})=u_{\text{IR}}(\tau^{\prime}-\tau(\tau^{\prime}))\,.

(A.2)

This transformation is single-valued on
S^{1}
since
\alpha(\tau^{\prime}+1)=\alpha(\tau^{\prime})
, from the fact that conformal transformations have winding number
1
. Putting the conformal and compensating gauge transformations together, the fields transform as

\psi_{i}(\tau)\mapsto\psi_{i}^{\prime}(\tau^{\prime})=e^{iu_{\text{IR}}(\tau^{\prime}-\tau)}\left(\frac{d\tau^{\prime}}{d\tau}\right)^{-\Delta}\psi_{i}(\tau)\,.

(A.3)

The corresponding Ward identity is

\widetilde{G}(\tau^{\prime}(\tau_{1}),\tau^{\prime}(\tau_{2}))=e^{iu_{\text{IR}}(\tau^{\prime}(\tau_{1})-\tau^{\prime}(\tau_{2})-\tau_{1}+\tau_{2})}\left[\frac{d\tau^{\prime}}{d\tau}(\tau_{1})\frac{d\tau^{\prime}}{d\tau}(\tau_{2})\right]^{-\Delta}\widetilde{G}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})\,.

(A.4)

For all
\text{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})
transformations in (
A.1
), one can check that

\displaystyle\frac{d\tau^{\prime}}{d\tau}(\tau_{1})\frac{d\tau^{\prime}}{d\tau}(\tau_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{\sec^{2}(\pi\tau_{1})\sec^{2}(\pi\tau_{2})}{\Big[\big(a\tan(\pi\tau_{1})+b\big)^{2}+\big(c\tan(\pi\tau_{1})+d\big)^{2}\Big]\Big[\big(a\tan(\pi\tau_{2})+b\big)^{2}+\big(c\tan(\pi\tau_{2})+d\big)^{2}\Big]}

(A.5)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sin^{2}(\pi\tau^{\prime}_{12})}{\sin^{2}(\pi\tau_{12}\big)}\,,\quad\tau^{\prime}_{12}\equiv\tau^{\prime}(\tau_{1})-\tau^{\prime}(\tau_{2})\,.

The Ward identity (
A.4
) can then be written as

\widetilde{G}(\tau^{\prime}_{12})=\frac{e^{iu_{\text{IR}}\tau^{\prime}_{12}}\sin^{-2\Delta}(\pi\tau^{\prime}_{12})}{e^{iu_{\text{IR}}\tau_{12}}\sin^{-2\Delta}(\pi\tau_{12})}\,\widetilde{G}(\tau_{12})\,.

(A.6)

In the large
N
saddle point expansion (
3.10
), the
\tau
dependence of
\widetilde{G}
must be carried by the solutions
G_{0}
to the Schwinger-Dyson equations, which must therefore satisfy the same conformal Ward identities. It is clear that the identities are solved by the ansatz

G_{0}(\tau)=e^{iu_{\text{IR}}\tau}\bigg|\frac{\pi}{\sin(\pi\tau)}\bigg|^{2\Delta}\left[A\Theta(\tau)+B\Theta(-\tau)\right]\,,\quad A,B\in\mathbb{C}\,,

(A.7)

if
\sgn(\tau^{\prime}_{12})=\sgn(\tau_{12})
. In order to be consistent with the KMS condition (
3.13
), it is essential to allow for a discontinuity at
\tau=0
where the operators coincide. Also, this ansatz is only valid for
\tau\in(-1,1)
. For other values of
\tau
, additional pieces proportional to
\Theta(\pm\tau-n)
, where
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
, should be added to be consistent with the KMS condition.

Now, imposing the KMS condition on (
A.7
) gives

Ae^{iu_{\text{IR}}}+B=0\,,

(A.8)

which is solved by

A=-ge^{-\frac{iu_{\text{IR}}}{2}}\,,\quad B=ge^{\frac{iu_{\text{IR}}}{2}}\,,\quad g\in\mathbb{C}\,.

(A.9)

Applying the reality condition (
3.21
) to the conformal ansatz implies that the coefficient
g
is real. Thus far, the ansatz is constrained to be

G_{0}(\tau)=-ge^{iu_{\text{IR}}\tau}\bigg|\frac{\pi}{\sin(\pi\tau)}\bigg|^{2\Delta}\left[e^{-\frac{iu_{\text{IR}}}{2}}\Theta(\tau)-e^{\frac{iu_{\text{IR}}}{2}}\Theta(-\tau)\right]\,,\quad g(u)\,.

(A.10)

A final constraint can be put on the sign of
g
using (
3.25
). The boundary value of
G_{0}
in the conformal ansatz is

\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\re G_{0}(\epsilon)=-\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0^{+}}\left(\frac{g\cos\frac{u_{\text{IR}}}{2}}{\epsilon^{2\Delta}}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{1-2\Delta})\right)\,.

(A.11)

In the actual solution, the divergent term proportional to
\epsilon^{2\Delta}
will be regularized to something close to the finite value of
-\frac{1}{2}
. For this regularization to be smooth, the coefficient of the divergent term must be negative. This implies

\sgn(g)=\sgn\left(\cos\frac{u_{\text{IR}}}{2}\right)=(-1)^{\lfloor\frac{u_{\text{IR}}+\pi}{2\pi}\rfloor}\,.

(A.12)
