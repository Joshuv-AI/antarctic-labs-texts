Sustained remission after discontinuation of ALK inhibition in ALK-positive histiocytosis
Authors: Paul G Kemps, Jennifer L Picarsic, Sébastien Héritier, Jean Donadieu, Julien Haroche, Laure Farnault, Stefania Gaspari, Dmitry A Evseev, Daria S Osipova, Alexander E Druy, Milen Minkov, Susan Picton, Andreas Beilken, Robert Möhle, Miloš B Kuzmanović, Hitomi S Okuma, Chung W Chow, Martin A Campbell, Gregory S Phillips, Yuhang Zhou, Gaurav Goyal, Ronald S Go, Kee Kiat Yeo, Bryan A Sisk, Joanna L Weinstein, Patrick K Campbell, Eli L Diamond, Jean-François Emile
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

ALK-positive histiocytosis is a rare histiocytic neoplasm first described in 2008 and recently classified as a distinct entity [1, 2]. The disease is typically driven by KIF5B::ALK fusions and often involves the neurologic system [3, 4]. We previously conducted the largest study of ALK-positive histiocytosis to date, investigating 31 children and 8 adults with the disease [4]. Our study demonstrated robust responses in all 11 patients treated with ALK inhibition, including 10 with neurologic involvement. However, the durability of response and potential for therapy cessation remained uncertain. Here, we present long-term outcomes of our original 11 cases, along with 16 additional patients, demonstrating universal responses to ALK inhibition and sustained remissions after therapy discontinuation.

A total of 27 patients with ALK-positive histiocytosis treated with ALK inhibitors were identified through our prior study [4] and subsequent consultations. Apart from the original 11 cases [4–7], 5 patients were previously published and are reported with extended follow-up (cases 2, 10, 15, 16, and 24) [3, 8–11]. Informed consent was obtained from patients and/or their legal representatives when required, or a waiver of consent was obtained for retrospective research. Diagnosis was based on established criteria [4]; central pathology review was performed for all cases with fusions other than KIF5B::ALK. Clinical and molecular information was extracted from the medical records by the treating physicians and shared in a standardised, deidentified format. Response to treatment was classified as complete response (CR), partial response (PR), stable disease (SD), or progressive disease (PD), as described previously [4, 12]. Response was based on formal clinical interpretations of imaging when available; the less favourable response was prioritised if multiple imaging methods were used that showed discrepant responses (i.e., PR by magnetic resonance imaging vs. CR by positron emission tomography) [4, 12]. Objective response was defined as CR or PR. Adverse events were graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events, version 5.0.

The cohort comprised 16 children and 11 adults (Table S1), with a median age of 13 years (range: 2 months – 70 years). The patients represented the broad clinical spectrum of the disease, from infants with liver and haematopoietic involvement (Group 1A; n = 1), to other patients with multisystemic disease (Group 1B; n = 15), and patients with single-system neurologic or non-neurologic disease (Group 2; n = 7 and n = 4, respectively) [4]. Twenty (74%) patients had neurologic involvement; 13 (48%) had pulmonary disease manifestations. KIF5B::ALK was identified in 21 patients, whereas DCTN1::ALK was detected in 3 cases, and CLTC::ALK in a single patient. In 2 patients, ALK rearrangement was exclusively confirmed by fluorescence in situ hybridisation.

Fifteen (56%) patients received ALK inhibitors as first-line therapy, while the others received it as second- or further-line therapy (Table S1). Alectinib was most frequently administered, followed by crizotinib, lorlatinib, and brigatinib. One patient was treated with alectinib after crizotinib induced an anaphylactic reaction on the first day of administration [4]. Another switched from alectinib to lorlatinib due to alectinib-induced haemolytic anaemia. Median time on targeted treatment was 2.0 years (range: 0.5–7.5 years). Median follow-up since ALK inhibitor initiation was 3.7 years (range: 0.6–7.5 years).

An objective response to ALK inhibition was observed in 27/27 patients (Fig. 1; Table S1), with CR in 15/27 (56%) and PR in 12/27 (44%). Response was generally rapid, with a median duration to first objective response of 2 months (range 2 weeks – 6.5 months). For example, patient 1 with classic infantile disease with liver and haematopoietic involvement and accompanying hyperinflammatory syndrome became afebrile within 5 days of treatment, with a marked decrease in serum C-reactive protein and prompt normalisation of platelet and haemoglobin levels (Fig. 2A). By week 8 of treatment, ultrasound showed reduction in hepato- and splenomegaly. Response was classified as PR in several patients (no. 6, 7, 11, 12, 17, 19, and 21) based on residual, non-enhancing neurologic abnormalities on magnetic resonance imaging that were compatible with treated disease, but residual active disease could not be excluded (Table S1; Fig. 2B). In all 3 patients evaluated by positron emission tomography (no. 6, 11, and 12), the other sites of disease had completely resolved.

During follow-up, 19 (70%) patients discontinued treatment – including 10 children and 9 adults (Fig. 1; Table S1). In 11 patients, this was an elective decision in favour of observation, made in the context of clinical remission. The others discontinued therapy due to toxicity (n = 5), lack of adherence (n = 1), fertility preservation (n = 1), or another medical condition (n = 1). Before treatment cessation, 11 had achieved PR and 8 CR. Disease relapse occurred in only 1 patient (#16), an adult with multisystemic disease harbouring a DCTN1::ALK fusion who had previously attained a CR with alectinib [10]. Skin lesions recurred when targeted treatment was halted after 3 years, but response was quickly recaptured after reinitiation of alectinib (Fig. 1). The remaining 18 patients all had a sustained response after ALK inhibitor discontinuation, with a median treatment-free remission of 1.8 years (range: 0.1–3.0 years). For example, patient 2 with infantile multisystemic disease with lung, liver, kidney, and skin involvement had a CR to crizotinib, which was discontinued after 1 year, with sustained remission over the next 2 years of follow-up (Fig. 2C). Patient 17 – a 7-month-old girl with an isolated brain tumour – had a PR to lorlatinib, which was discontinued after 2.4 years, with a stable non-enhancing brain lesion and sustained clinical remission at 21 months after therapy cessation (Fig. 2D). Finally, adult patient 27 with widespread skin nodules had a sustained PR, without any new lesions, at 5 months after alectinib discontinuation (Fig. S1).

Adverse events were observed in 21/27 (78%) patients; most were grade 1 or 2 events that required no or minimal intervention (Table S2). Grade 3 events occurred in 10/27 (37%) patients, such as hypercholesterolaemia in 2 children receiving lorlatinib. Two patients experienced a grade 4 adverse event (crizotinib-induced anaphylaxis or alectinib-induced haemolysis). Nine (33%) patients required a dose reduction and/or temporary/permanent treatment discontinuation.

Our international collaborative study affirms the efficacy of ALK inhibition in the treatment of ALK-positive histiocytosis, demonstrating universal responses in an expanded and clinically diverse cohort. While adverse events were common, they were largely manageable. The adoption of this novel therapy is transformative for a disease often marked by aggressive clinical manifestations and poor responses to conventional (chemo)therapies [4]. Moreover, the consistent responses across age groups, disease presentations, and fusion subtypes highlight ALK dependency as the defining pathological feature of the disease and support early targeted intervention in patients with severe disease.

Our data reveal that ALK inhibition can be successfully stopped, with sustained remissions in almost all patients who discontinued therapy. Interestingly, response was maintained regardless of whether CR or PR was achieved prior to cessation, suggesting that residual radiologic abnormalities may not reflect active disease. Our results sharply contrast to the high relapse rates seen upon discontinuation of BRAF and/or MEK inhibition in patients with Langerhans cell histiocytosis or Erdheim-Chester disease [13–15]. While the biological basis for this divergence remains to be elucidated, our findings challenge the prevailing notion that targeted therapy with kinase inhibitors is unable to induce lasting remission. The potential for limited-duration targeted treatment is particularly relevant to paediatric and young adult populations, where long-term safety and toxicity are critical concerns. Future studies need to define the optimal duration of ALK inhibition.
