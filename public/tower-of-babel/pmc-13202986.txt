Molecular biomarkers of cognitive impairment and neuropsychiatric symptoms after stroke in late-life-enriched cohorts: a scoping review
Authors: Andrea Calderone, Rosaria de Luca, Francesco Corallo, Angela Marra, Francesca Antonia Arcadi, Carmela Casella, Angelo Quartarone, Rocco Salvatore Calabrò
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPost-stroke cognitive impairment (PSCI) and neuropsychiatric symptoms (NPS) are frequent in late-life stroke populations and contribute to long-term disability and care needs. Molecular biomarkers may enable earlier risk stratification and trial enrichment, but the literature remains fragmented across biomarker classes, sampling windows, and phenotype definitions.MethodsWe conducted a systematic scoping review following PRISMA-ScR and JBI guidance. PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Scopus, the Cochrane Library, and EBSCOhost were searched from inception to 29 January 2026. Eligible cohorts were defined by an explicit minimum age threshold of ≥ 60 years or a cohort mean/median age ≥ 60 years. To strengthen translational interpretability, the final synthesis additionally used an explicit phenotyping framework for PSCI and NPS, a NOS-informed methodological quality appraisal, a consistency analysis of core biomarker families (NfL, GFAP, and hs-CRP/CRP), a China-versus-non-China subgroup analysis, and a shared/specific biomarker mapping.ResultsThe evidence map comprised 105 studies, predominantly observational, acute-phase, and blood-based. Age eligibility was predominantly indirect: 5/105 studies used an explicit minimum age threshold of ≥ 60 years, whereas 100/105 qualified on cohort mean/median age ≥ 60 years. The literature was geographically concentrated in China (78/105), and methodological quality was uneven, with 86 studies in the higher-quality stratum, 14 in the moderate stratum, and 5 in the lower stratum. NfL (5/5) and GFAP (2/2) showed uniformly positive adjusted associations, mainly in prognostic analyses, whereas CRP-family findings were more heterogeneous (5/7 positive, 1 mixed/attenuated, 1 null). China studies were more often acute, prospective, and PSCI-oriented, whereas non-China studies more often examined inflammation-linked NPS and later-phase phenotypes. Shared biomarker candidates across PSCI and NPS were most evident for NfL and GFAP, while CRP-family markers were shared but more confounding-sensitive.ConclusionsBiomarker research for PSCI and NPS in late-life-enriched stroke cohorts is rapidly expanding but remains heterogeneous in phenotyping, timing, confounder structure, and reporting quality. Harmonized outcome definitions, quality-stratified interpretation, phase-aware serial sampling, and externally validated prediction frameworks with calibration and utility evaluation are priorities for biomarker qualification and clinical implementation. The resulting evidence map is most directly applicable to late-life-enriched rather than strictly age-restricted ≥ 60-year stroke populations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08106-0.

Introduction

Stroke remains a major cause of disability in older adults and contributes substantially to late-life cognitive decline. Post-stroke cognitive impairment (PSCI) spans a continuum from subtle executive deficits to dementia and affects functional independence, medication adherence, social participation, and long-term institutionalization risk [1]. Pre-stroke cognitive vulnerability and shared vascular and neurodegenerative pathways amplify this burden, which helps explain why dementia trajectories often accelerate after an index cerebrovascular event [2]. Older stroke survivors are at disproportionate risk because cerebrovascular injury often interacts with accumulated vascular risk and co-pathology. Yet biomarker research frequently pools broad age ranges, which can obscure late-life-specific patterns that matter for translation.

Stroke also acts as a biological stressor that can unmask or hasten brain aging. Vascular contributions to cognitive impairment and dementia describe a spectrum in which infarcts, hemorrhage, small vessel disease, and hypoperfusion interact with neurodegenerative processes and reduce cognitive reserve [3]. Post-stroke dementia frameworks emphasize mixed pathology, domain-specific profiles, and the importance of timing. Early cognitive deficits may partially recover, whereas later decline can reflect progressive neurovascular and neurodegenerative injury [4].

Clinical definitions and care pathways for PSCI have progressed, yet key uncertainties remain. Contemporary statements propose structured screening, staged diagnostic assessment, and longitudinal follow-up that consider stroke subtype, lesion burden, and premorbid cognition [5]. Evidence-based recommendations also highlight limited harmonization of cognitive phenotyping and inconsistent adjustment for confounders such as education, mood, and vascular risk [6]. This heterogeneity limits comparability across studies and slows translation of candidate biomarkers into prognostic tools and trial stratification.

Neuropsychiatric symptoms (NPS) represent an additional determinant of late-life outcomes after stroke. Depression, anxiety, apathy, fatigue, sleep disturbances, emotional lability, and psychotic symptoms frequently co-occur and worsen disability and caregiver strain [7]. Post-stroke depression is particularly consequential because it is associated with poorer rehabilitation engagement, lower quality of life, recurrent vascular events, and mortality [8]. Apathy can independently predict poorer functional trajectories and reduced participation in therapy, even when depressive symptoms are accounted for [9]. Anxiety is also common and may persist into chronic phases, with clinical heterogeneity that complicates measurement and mechanistic inference [10].

Pathobiology linking vascular injury to PSCI and NPS involves the neurovascular unit and multiple converging molecular cascades. Neurovascular coupling failure, endothelial dysfunction, and blood–brain barrier disruption promote edema, impaired clearance of neurotoxic proteins, and immune cell trafficking into injured brain tissue [11]. Secondary injury mechanisms include oxidative stress, excitotoxicity, microglial and astroglial activation, and systemic immune dysregulation that may extend beyond the acute lesion and influence network-level dysfunction relevant to cognition and behavior [12].

Molecular biomarkers measured in accessible biological matrices offer a route to quantify these processes in vivo and to support translational pipelines. Neurofilament light chain (NfL) reflects neuroaxonal injury and can be measured in blood, providing a scalable marker of central nervous system damage [13]. Cohort data support associations between early NfL elevations, infarct burden, and clinical outcomes, suggesting a plausible link between acute neuroaxonal injury and later cognitive or neuropsychiatric sequelae [14]. Glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) and S100B may capture astroglial injury and blood-brain barrier perturbation, although specificity and timing effects remain challenging [15, 16]. Neurodegeneration-related measures, including phosphorylated tau species, may capture co-pathology that is triggered or unmasked by vascular injury [17]. Standardization challenges extend to emerging biomarker classes such as extracellular vesicles and other circulating carriers, where isolation methods and analytical pipelines strongly influence interpretability [18].

Current evidence remains fragmented across biomarker classes, biological matrices, assay platforms, sampling windows, and outcome definitions. This fragmentation is not a purely academic limitation: it represents a translational bottleneck that prevents biomarker candidates from progressing along a credible qualification pathway toward clinical use. In practice, studies often differ in stroke subtype composition, baseline cognitive vulnerability, and confounder control, while pre-analytical handling and assay reporting are inconsistently described. These issues undermine cross-study comparability, inflate the risk of false-positive signals, and make external validation difficult, particularly when omics approaches introduce multiplicity and model overfitting concerns. Existing reviews have highlighted inconsistent findings and limited prognostic consensus, but they rarely provide a structured, age-specific roadmap that explicitly links biomarker domains to post-stroke cognitive and neuropsychiatric phenotypes in older adults, where comorbidity burden and mixed pathology are most likely to drive heterogeneity [19].

Measurement heterogeneity in PSCI and NPS further impairs translation because identical biomarker signals can map to different constructs depending on instrument choice, diagnostic thresholds, timing of assessment, and whether premorbid cognition and mood are accounted for. Under these conditions, effect-size pooling risks generating misleading summary estimates that obscure biologically meaningful patterns and misguide downstream validation. What is needed at this stage is a systematic evidence map that clarifies “what has been tested, in whom, when, and how” and that identifies the minimum methodological standards required to move from exploratory associations to clinically actionable risk stratification and trial enrichment. By explicitly mapping biomarker-outcome links across post-stroke phases, matrices, and platforms, this scoping review is designed to support translational decision-making: prioritizing candidate biomarkers and biomarker panels for replication, highlighting harmonization targets for future cohorts, and delineating the design features most likely to accelerate clinically relevant biomarker development in late-life-enriched stroke cohorts [20].

This scoping review maps molecular biomarkers associated with PSCI and NPS after ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke in late-life-enriched stroke cohorts, operationalized by study-level age criteria rather than exclusively strict minimum-age thresholds across acute, subacute, and chronic phases. It identifies which biomarker domains have been investigated in relation to PSCI and NPS in late-life-enriched stroke cohorts; characterizes the biological matrices and assay platforms used and the extent of methodological variability that may hinder reproducibility; maps how biomarker sampling and outcome ascertainment have been timed across post-stroke phases; and synthesizes recurring methodological patterns and gaps that currently limit translation toward prognostic stratification, trial enrichment, and treatment development. The resulting evidence map provides a practical blueprint for biomarker prioritization and harmonized study design in late-life post-stroke brain health. Its direct applicability is strongest for late-life-enriched stroke populations and should be extrapolated cautiously to strictly age-defined cohorts aged ≥ 60 years. The evidence map is additionally stratified by phenotype class, methodological quality, core biomarker consistency, geography, and shared versus outcome-specific biomarker signals to better support translational prioritization.

Materials and methods

Protocol and reporting framework

A scoping review design (see Supplementary Material 1 and 2) was selected to map a heterogeneous evidence base spanning biomarker classes, analytical platforms, biological matrices, sampling windows, and outcome definitions, which limited feasibility for meta-analysis. The protocol was registered on OSF (Version 1.0; 27 January 2026; OSF registration: 10.17605/OSF.IO/DSHA2). Conduct and reporting followed the PRISMA Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) [21] and Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) guidance for scoping reviews [22, 23]. Additional methodological support for protocol development, conduct, and reporting of scoping reviews was drawn from established methodological papers [24–27]. To strengthen translational interpretability in the final synthesis, we added two explicit analytic layers: a prespecified conceptual framework for PSCI and NPS phenotyping, and a structured methodological quality and risk-of-bias appraisal of the observational biomarker literature using a Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS)-informed operationalization. The appraisal was not used as an exclusion criterion. Instead, it was used to stratify the evidence map, tables, and discussion into higher-credibility versus more exploratory evidence strata. This approach preserved the breadth expected in a scoping review while reducing flat interpretation of methodologically heterogeneous biomarker signals.

PECO evaluation

The PECO framework (Population, Exposure, Comparator, Outcomes) guided eligibility and data charting for this scoping review. The population comprised human adults with confirmed ischemic stroke, intracerebral hemorrhage, or subarachnoid hemorrhage. An older-adult focus was operationalized through study-level age criteria: a minimum age threshold of 60 years or a mean/median age of at least 60 years. After full-text adjudication, no included study remained in an unclear/not reported age category, and no final study relied on subgroup-only extraction. This strategy maximized capture of ageing-related stroke biomarker studies but broadened the evidence map beyond strictly age-restricted cohorts aged ≥ 60 years. Exposure comprised molecular biomarkers measured in human biological matrices. Eligible biomarkers included proteins and peptides, metabolites, lipids, cytokines and chemokines, neuroaxonal injury markers, glial injury markers, neurodegeneration-related markers, microRNAs (miRNAs) and other non-coding RNAs, extracellular vesicle cargo, and multi-omics signatures when the molecular layer and analytic pipeline were reported. Comparators included stroke survivors without PSCI or without NPS, alternative outcome strata, healthy controls, or within-cohort contrasts across biomarker distributions when explicit control groups were not available. Outcomes comprised PSCI, mild cognitive impairment, dementia, domain-specific cognitive performance, and NPS assessed with validated instruments, structured interviews, or explicitly stated diagnostic criteria. For the final synthesis, these outcomes were additionally organized into prespecified phenotype classes: screening-defined global PSCI, diagnosis-oriented cognitive impairment/MCI/dementia outcomes, and syndrome-specific NPS domains including depression, anxiety, apathy, fatigue, delirium, sleep disturbance, and composite neuropsychiatric burden. Because no single diagnostic gold standard was used consistently across the literature, validated syndrome-specific instruments and explicit diagnostic criteria were charted separately and interpreted as sources of heterogeneity rather than collapsed into a single construct.

Inclusion criteria

Eligibility required original, peer-reviewed human studies that reported at least one molecular biomarker and at least one cognitive or neuropsychiatric outcome after stroke, together with a quantitative biomarker-outcome association or group comparison. Inclusion required a late-life-focused cohort defined by a minimum age threshold of 60 years or a mean or median age of at least 60 years. After full-text review, no included study relied on subgroup-only extraction, and studies with unclear age eligibility were excluded. Accordingly, the final evidence map is interpreted as representing late-life-enriched stroke cohorts rather than exclusively age-restricted populations aged ≥ 60 years. Inclusion required a confirmed stroke diagnosis and a clearly reported index event timing, with biomarker sampling time reported as hours, days, weeks, or months after onset or admission. Eligible outcomes were additionally assigned to one of the prespecified phenotype classes described above so that screening-defined global PSCI, diagnosis-oriented cognitive endpoints, and syndrome-specific NPS outcomes were synthesized separately rather than treated as interchangeable constructs. Inclusion required outcome ascertainment using validated tools or accepted diagnostic approaches, such as cognitive screening instruments, neuropsychological batteries, clinical diagnoses of mild cognitive impairment or dementia, and validated NPS scales for depression, apathy, anxiety, psychosis-spectrum symptoms, sleep disturbance, delirium, fatigue, or global neuropsychiatric burden. Inclusion allowed any post-stroke phase and extracted timing to support stratification into acute, subacute, and chronic windows. Inclusion required sufficient methodological detail to chart the biomarker matrix, assay platform or analytic method, and measurement units or normalization approach for omics data, together with enough outcome detail to identify the construct and threshold applied.

Exclusion criteria

Exclusion applied to animal studies, in vitro studies, and purely mechanistic reports without patient-level cognitive or neuropsychiatric outcomes. Exclusion applied to studies that measured only neuroimaging, electrophysiological, digital, or clinical markers without a molecular biomarker component. Exclusion applied to reports that focused solely on acute stroke diagnosis, stroke subtype discrimination, or mortality prediction without any linkage to cognition or neuropsychiatric symptoms. Exclusion applied to conference abstracts, posters, dissertations, editorials, letters without original data, and narrative reviews when full methods and results were not available for charting. Exclusion applied to case reports and small case series that did not permit meaningful mapping of biomarker–outcome relationships, with a prespecified minimum sample size threshold applied when cohorts were extremely small or non-comparable. Exclusion applied to cohorts that did not meet the late-life-focused age criteria or had age eligibility that was unclear/non-verifiable at full-text review. Exclusion applied to studies where stroke could not be confirmed or where post-stroke timing of biosampling or outcome assessment was not reported and could not be reasonably inferred from the text.

Information sources

Electronic searches were conducted in PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Scopus, the Cochrane Library, and EBSCOhost. PubMed was used to capture MEDLINE-indexed biomedical literature and controlled vocabulary relevant to stroke and molecular biomarkers. Embase was used to broaden coverage of European and pharmacological literature and to capture biomarker studies not indexed in MEDLINE. Web of Science and Scopus were selected to enhance interdisciplinary coverage and support robust citation tracking for biomarker discovery studies. The Cochrane Library was searched to identify relevant controlled trials and biomarker substudies and to locate high-quality references for backward searching. EBSCOhost was used as an interface to access additional health and behavioral science databases available to the institution, including nursing and psychology-relevant content that can be underrepresented elsewhere. Searches were conducted from database inception to 29 January 2026 to avoid time-restriction bias and to capture early foundational biomarker work that predates current assay platforms.

Search strategy

Search strategies combined controlled vocabulary and free-text terms for stroke, cognitive impairment and dementia, neuropsychiatric outcomes, and molecular biomarkers. Syntax was adapted to each database (MeSH in PubMed; Emtree in Embase), with database-specific field tags and limits (human studies and English language) applied when available. Search strategy quality was strengthened through an internal peer review using the Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies (PRESS) framework prior to final execution [28]. Full database-specific strategies and translation details are provided in Supplementary Table S2D1, with supporting transparency logs in Supplementary Tables S2D2–S2D3.

Selection of sources of evidence

Records were exported from each database, merged, and deduplicated prior to screening. Duplicate removal followed a validated multistep approach to minimize both false duplicates and residual duplication across databases [29]. Title and abstract screening was performed independently by two reviewers (AC and RSC) using Rayyan to support blinded decisions and efficient conflict identification [30]. Before formal screening, the reviewers completed a calibration exercise on a sample of records to harmonize eligibility interpretations.

Disagreements at any stage were resolved by discussion. If consensus could not be reached, the final decision was made by RSC, and the resolution pathway was logged (Supplementary Table S2E1). Interrater agreement was quantified by two reviewers (AC and RSC) using Cohen’s kappa at the title (0.71) and abstract (0.74) stage to document screening consistency [31]. Kappa interpretation followed established benchmarks to contextualize agreement levels without using kappa as a determinant of study eligibility [32]. Full-text screening was performed independently by the same two reviewers, and disagreements were resolved through discussion. A third reviewer adjudicated unresolved conflicts to ensure consistent application of eligibility criteria (AQ).

Data charting process, data items, and critical appraisal of individual sources of evidence

Data charting was performed using a piloted extraction form aligned with scoping review recommendations that emphasize structured capture of both descriptive and analytic fields. Charting followed an iterative approach, and the extraction form was refined after pilot testing on a subset of included studies. Two reviewers extracted data independently for an initial subset to ensure field clarity and consistent interpretation. Single-extractor charting with second-reviewer verification was then applied to the remaining studies to balance rigor and feasibility in a large, heterogeneous evidence base. Review team procedures incorporated pragmatic recommendations for managing higher-volume scoping workflows while maintaining transparency and auditability. Data were charted in Microsoft Excel with version control and locked fields to reduce transcription errors. The workbook structure followed published guidance to support traceability between extracted items and the source text.

Charted variables included bibliographic data, country, setting, study design, recruitment strategy, and sample size. Participant descriptors included age distribution, sex, education when available, vascular risk profile, premorbid cognitive status when reported, and stroke severity indices. Stroke phenotype included ischemic or hemorrhagic type, lesion characteristics when available, and diagnostic criteria aligned with contemporary clinical and tissue-based definitions. Timing variables included time from stroke onset to biomarker sampling and time from stroke onset to cognitive or neuropsychiatric assessment. Biomarker descriptors included analyte name, biological matrix, preanalytical handling when reported, assay platform, units, detection limits, and whether single-marker or multiplex approaches were used. Outcome descriptors included phenotype class, symptom domain, assessment instrument, diagnostic or scale threshold, education correction when relevant, and whether the biomarker was evaluated in a same-time diagnostic/classification or longitudinal/prognostic role. We also charted whether cognitive and NPS measures co-occurred within the same report to support the shared/specific biomarker mapping. The National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke - Canadian Stroke Network harmonization framework informed extraction of vascular cognitive impairment-relevant operational definitions and cognitive domain reporting when studies referenced those standards [33]. Analytic fields included statistical model type, covariates, adjustment strategy, handling of multiple testing, effect direction, and whether biomarker associations were cross-sectional or predictive. Clinical correlates extracted included functional outcome measures, recurrence, mortality, imaging correlates when integrated, and rehabilitation exposure when described.

In this synthesis, observational studies were additionally appraised using a NOS-informed operationalization adapted across cohort, case-control, cross-sectional, and trial-embedded observational biomarker studies. Unreported items were conservatively scored 0. Studies were then stratified into higher-, moderate-, and lower-quality bands for interpretive purposes only. The appraisal was not used as an exclusion criterion. Instead, it was used to contextualize evidence credibility, to separate more robust from exploratory signals, and to inform the core-biomarker consistency analysis, the China-versus-non-China subgroup analysis, and the shared/specific biomarker mapping.

Narrative synthesis framework

Synthesis followed a descriptive mapping approach consistent with scoping review objectives and JBI-aligned presentation guidance [22]. Evidence was grouped by biomarker domain, including neuroaxonal injury and neurodegeneration markers, glial activation markers, immune and inflammatory signaling markers, and emerging biomarker classes. Evidence was also grouped by prespecified phenotype class, separating screening-defined global PSCI, diagnosis-oriented MCI/dementia outcomes, depression, apathy, anxiety, fatigue, delirium, sleep disturbances, and composite neuropsychiatric burden. Timing strata were applied to distinguish acute, subacute, and chronic biomarker-outcome patterns. Results were presented through structured tables that cross-tabulated biomarkers against outcomes, biological matrices, assay platforms, and sampling timepoints. Narrative synthesis highlighted convergence and inconsistency across studies, with explicit attention to confounding structures, older-adult specificity, methodological quality level, geography, and feasibility of translation to prognostic stratification and treatment development. Dedicated supplementary analyses addressed methodological quality and risk-of-bias appraisal, core biomarker consistency, geographic subgroup patterns, and shared versus outcome-specific biomarker families across PSCI and NPS.

Results

Study selection and screening yield

The database search identified 2,603 records from six databases, including PubMed (n = 121), Web of Science (n = 143), Cochrane Library (n = 46), Embase (n = 127), EBSCOhost (n = 15), and Scopus (n = 2,151), with no records from registers (n = 0). Before screening, 658 duplicate records were removed, no records were excluded by automation tools (n = 0), and 4 non-English articles were removed. This process left 1,941 records for title and abstract screening. During screening, 1,782 records were excluded as reviews, case reports, or conference abstracts. Full texts were sought and retrieved for 159 reports, with no reports unavailable (n = 0). After eligibility assessment, 54 reports were excluded because they did not report an eligible molecular biomarker (n = 31) or did not meet the older-adult criteria, or those criteria were unclear and could not be verified (n = 23). After full-text adjudication, no included study remained in an unclear/not reported age category. A total of 105 studies were included in the evidence map [34–138]. Evidence landscape in late-life-enriched stroke cohorts

The evidence base (see Supplementary Material 3) was geographically concentrated and methodologically uneven. Studies conducted in China accounted for 78 of the 105 included reports, whereas 27 were conducted outside China. When grouped by region, the remaining evidence was distributed across Europe (n = 15), North America (n = 3), Oceania (n = 2), and other Asian/non-Chinese settings (n = 7). Observational designs predominated, especially prospective cohorts (72/105), followed by retrospective cohorts (11/105), case-control studies (11/105), cross-sectional studies (8/105), two trial-embedded biomarker analyses, and one other/unclear design. Participants were typically recruited during acute hospitalization or early recovery, and most studies focused on ischemic stroke cohorts, with fewer investigations addressing hemorrhagic stroke or mixed cerebrovascular populations. Age-eligibility classification was predominantly indirect: 5/105 studies (4.8%) used an explicit minimum age threshold of ≥ 60 years, whereas 100/105 studies (95.2%) qualified on the basis of cohort mean/median age ≥ 60 years; no included study relied on subgroup-only extraction or remained in an unclear/not reported age category after full-text review. Accordingly, the evidence map is best interpreted as representing late-life-enriched stroke cohorts rather than strictly age-restricted populations aged ≥ 60 years.

When reorganized according to the prespecified phenotype framework, most studies primarily addressed screening-defined global PSCI (n = 56) or depression (n = 39), with much smaller literatures for MCI/dementia outcomes (n = 2), fatigue (n = 5), apathy (n = 1), anxiety (n = 1), and delirium (n = 1). NOS-informed methodological appraisal further showed that 86/105 studies fell into the higher-quality stratum, 14/105 into the moderate stratum, and 5/105 into the lower stratum, with lower ratings clustering mainly in cross-sectional or narrowly selected cohorts with limited temporality and flow reporting (Supplementary Material 4). These results indicate that positive biomarker findings are not methodologically equivalent across the corpus and should be interpreted through a quality-stratified lens rather than as a flat evidence map.

This evidence map also highlights a consistent translational orientation. Most studies linked biospecimens collected early after the index event to downstream clinical phenotypes assessed during follow-up, with multivariable modeling used to integrate biomarkers with demographic and clinical covariates. Prediction-oriented reporting was common in studies proposing biomarkers as stratification tools, often supported by discrimination metrics and threshold-based classification. Multi-analyte approaches were present but less frequent, and they were usually reported as panel-level signatures rather than as isolated single-marker effects [34, 49, 54, 64, 67]. Cognitive outcomes and phenotyping frameworks

Cognitive outcomes were mapped in the majority of the included studies, either as the primary endpoint or as part of a broader recovery phenotype. In this synthesis, however, cognitive outcomes were not treated as a single interchangeable construct. In this synthesis, outcome phenotyping was reorganized into screening-defined global PSCI, diagnosis-oriented cognitive impairment/MCI/dementia outcomes, and syndrome-specific NPS classes, thereby making explicit the conceptual framework used. Global cognitive screening remained the most common cognitive approach. Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) and Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) were the dominant tools, often complemented by education adjustments and study-specific diagnostic thresholds. PSCI definitions were typically operationalized as dichotomized impairment status at prespecified follow-up timepoints. Several cohorts also reported continuous cognitive scores, enabling analyses of dose-response patterns and incremental prediction performance. MoCA-based definitions at approximately 90 days were frequently used to anchor post-acute cognitive classification, including education-corrected scoring approaches in some large cohorts [34, 39]. Cognitive outcomes assessed at three months were also common in studies that paired acute biosampling with short- to mid-term follow-up, including work using serum-based glial injury markers and metalloproteinase pathways [35, 37]. A smaller subset of studies incorporated cognitive phenotyping beyond global screening. These reports included domain-oriented assessments, working memory measures, and rehabilitation-relevant cognitive constructs, often framed as clinically meaningful improvement or recovery classification rather than impairment diagnosis. Rehabilitation-phase studies used functional and cognitive endpoints together, reflecting pragmatic translational questions tied to post-stroke recovery trajectories.

Biomarker domains investigated for PSCI

The cognitive biomarker landscape spanned neuroinjury markers, inflammatory and immune mediators, vascular and metabolic indices, endothelial and extracellular matrix remodeling markers, and multi-marker or omics-informed signatures. Neuroaxonal injury markers were frequently evaluated in relation to PSCI. Plasma phosphorylated NfL measured in the acute window was examined as a prognostic biomarker for MoCA-defined PSCI at around 90 days, typically using regression models adjusted for clinical severity and vascular risk features [34]. Glial or astrocytic injury signals were less frequently assessed but were represented through serum GFAP measured early after admission and related to PSCI defined at three months using MMSE-based thresholds [35]. Some studies evaluated combined neuroinjury and systemic risk signatures, such as homocysteine alongside brain-injury associated proteins, within acute sampling frameworks and three-month cognitive endpoints [36].

Markers of extracellular matrix remodeling and endothelial function were repeatedly represented. Serum MMP-9 and related protease–inhibitor pairs were commonly sampled early during hospitalization and then linked to three-month PSCI classification using MoCA and MMSE-derived definitions [37, 38]. These studies typically operationalized exposure using biomarker distributions, such as quartiles, and evaluated associations using adjusted logistic models. Such designs aligned with the broader pattern in the cognitive literature, where single-analyte biomarkers were embedded within conventional clinical prediction baselines.

Cardiometabolic and vascular risk biomarkers formed another major domain. Uric acid and derived indices were evaluated in multiple cohorts that used serum sampling early after admission, with cognitive outcomes measured at one week or three months depending on the design [39, 40]. Several studies incorporated renal function normalization or composite indices to contextualize uric acid signals, reflecting clinical interpretability priorities in stroke cohorts [39]. Homocysteine was assessed across multiple designs and follow-up windows, including cohorts with three-month cognitive endpoints and longer follow-up in select studies [43, 44]. These analyses often treated homocysteine both as a continuous predictor and as a categorized exposure to support clinically actionable thresholds.

Emerging molecular classes were represented through circulating non-coding RNAs and metabolite signatures. Several studies investigated microRNAs measured in serum or plasma using quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) pipelines, often linking candidate miRNAs to PSCI status defined by MoCA and MMSE thresholds [45, 46]. Multi-miRNA panels were also examined in early post-stroke windows, including designs that paired discovery stages with validation assays and reported classification performance for short-term PSCI endpoints [47]. Metabolomics approaches were applied to classify cognitive impairment using serum-derived metabolite panels, with mass spectrometry-based workflows and multivariable discrimination reporting [48]. In addition, immune-metabolic pathway mapping was represented in cohorts focused on complement activation and the tryptophan–kynurenine axis. These studies used longitudinal cognitive measurements and linked circulating immune or metabolic intermediates to cognitive level or trajectory patterns over extended follow-up [49–51].

Genetic and cytokine-focused translational studies were less frequent but contributed mechanistic diversity. Genetic variants in neurotrophic signaling were examined in relation to functional and cognitive recovery constructs in rehabilitation settings [52]. Cytokine biomarkers, including interleukin-6, were evaluated in prospective cohorts with repeated cognitive assessment, supporting time-linked analyses of inflammatory signaling and cognitive change [53]. Neuropsychiatric outcomes and measurement approaches

Neuropsychiatric phenotypes were mapped in approximately half of the included studies, with post-stroke depression representing the dominant construct. In the synthesis, depression, anxiety, apathy, fatigue, delirium, sleep disturbance, and composite neuropsychiatric measures were treated as related but non-equivalent phenotype classes rather than collapsed into a single NPS category. Depression was typically operationalized through structured diagnostic criteria anchored to DSM frameworks and then quantified using validated severity scales, most commonly Hamilton-based instruments. Early-onset and late-onset depression subtypes were explicitly differentiated in some cohorts, with follow-up windows ranging from two weeks to several months. Several studies also assessed co-occurring symptom domains, including anxiety and fatigue, although these outcomes were less frequently represented and were measured using heterogeneous instruments. Clinical translation in this domain often emphasized early risk classification. Many studies collected biospecimens during admission or shortly thereafter and evaluated associations with depressive outcomes defined at two weeks, one month, or three to six months. Prediction-oriented reporting was common, including ROC-derived thresholds and incremental discrimination metrics, which were often positioned as clinically actionable tools for stratifying follow-up intensity. Biomarker domains investigated for neuropsychiatric symptoms

Inflammation-related biomarkers formed the most consistent NPS domain. High-sensitivity CRP measured in the acute period was evaluated as a predictor of major depression at six months and was also linked to symptom severity within prospective cohorts using structured diagnostic interviews and Hamilton-based severity scales [54]. Several studies focused on circulating microRNAs as translational candidates for early depression prediction. These investigations typically combined discovery profiling with qRT-PCR validation and evaluated depression status at two weeks or three months using Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM)-consistent criteria and Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD)-based thresholds [55, 56]. Integrated peripheral-organ and immune signatures were also represented. For example, studies combining markers of gut barrier injury with cytokines examined associations with depressive phenotypes and severity measures, reflecting systems-level translational hypotheses [57]. Metabolomics approaches were used to derive urinary signatures for depression classification, with multivariable modelling and receiver operating characteristic (ROC)-based performance reporting anchored to DSM-defined depressive disorder frameworks [58]. Additional immune mediators, including macrophage migration inhibitory factor, were evaluated in acute-phase sampling designs and linked to subsequent depression outcomes [60]. Several reports broadened the NPS landscape beyond depression. Biomarker associations with depressive symptoms over time were explored in longitudinal datasets with repeated symptom scoring, supporting trajectory-based analyses rather than single timepoint classification [75]. A small subset examined apathy using validated instruments and inflammatory markers within subacute or early follow-up windows [65]. Anxiety was less frequently assessed but appeared in studies that combined depression and anxiety screening tools within a shared cohort framework, including blood-based neuroinjury signals evaluated against symptom scales [72]. Fatigue emerged as a recurring construct in selected studies that linked inflammatory markers and glycemic indices to fatigue outcomes measured during recovery phases [81].

Matrices and assay platforms used in the included literature

Blood-based matrices dominated the evidence base. Serum was the most frequently reported matrix, followed by plasma and whole-blood laboratory measures. Urine was used in a small subset of studies, mainly in metabolomics or oxidative stress marker panels linked to depression phenotypes [58, 87]. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) appeared rarely and was generally confined to multimodal biomarker frameworks rather than routine clinical pipelines. Assay platforms reflected a mix of routine clinical laboratory testing and specialized translational methods. Immunoassay-based measurements were common for single-analyte proteins and inflammatory markers, while digital immunoassays were used in selected cohorts for neuroaxonal injury biomarkers [34]. qRT-PCR was the dominant platform for microRNA studies and was frequently paired with discovery approaches such as microarrays before targeted validation [55]. Mass spectrometry-based workflows supported metabolomics and proteomic profiling in a smaller subset, often reported as discriminative panels rather than single biomarkers [48, 58]. Several studies provided detailed analytic metadata, including coefficient of variation reporting for immunoassays and explicit storage conditions and sampling protocols, particularly in large cohorts designed for biomarker qualification [67, 92].

Timing of biosampling and outcome ascertainment across post-stroke phases

Biosampling was most often performed during the acute phase, commonly within the first 24 to 48 h after admission or stroke onset. These designs linked early molecular measurements to outcomes measured later, with three months serving as a frequent anchor timepoint for PSCI classification [34, 37, 67]. Subacute sampling was less frequent and typically occurred during inpatient rehabilitation or early follow-up visits, often in studies focused on neuropsychiatric outcomes and oxidative stress biology [87]. Chronic-phase biosampling appeared in a smaller subset of studies that targeted long-term recovery phenotypes, including rehabilitation trial frameworks with baseline sampling months to years after stroke [92].

Outcome ascertainment clustered around clinically meaningful follow-up windows. Cognitive outcomes were frequently assessed at approximately three months, reflecting common clinical practice for post-stroke cognitive screening and rehabilitation planning [34, 35, 37]. Earlier cognitive assessment windows also appeared, including one-week or two-week evaluations in cohorts focusing on early cognitive vulnerability [41, 47]. Neuropsychiatric outcomes were often assessed in the early post-stroke period, including two-week depression classification, and then again at one to six months for persistent symptom phenotypes [54–56]. Longer-term follow-up beyond one year was present in selected cohorts and was most often used to map cognitive trajectories or repeated symptom outcomes in longitudinal designs [49, 50]. Recurring methodological and analytical patterns

Most studies used association-based statistical frameworks aligned with clinically interpretable prediction. Regression modelling dominated, particularly logistic regression for dichotomized PSCI and depression outcomes. Covariate adjustment commonly incorporated demographics, education, stroke severity, vascular risk profiles, and acute treatments. Some studies adopted incremental prediction reporting, including discrimination and reclassification metrics, when biomarkers were positioned as additive predictors beyond standard clinical models [67]. ROC-derived cut-offs were frequently used to support threshold-based clinical translation in both cognitive and neuropsychiatric contexts, particularly for early risk identification [54, 77].

Multi-marker approaches followed two recurring patterns. Omics-derived studies typically used discovery and validation pipelines, reporting discriminative panels and classification performance while maintaining clinical outcome definitions anchored to standard cognitive or depression instruments [48, 55]. Clinical laboratory composites and ratio-based indices were also reported, often designed to capture systemic physiology and to retain interpretability in routine care settings [39, 82]. Longitudinal modelling appeared in cohorts with repeated cognitive or symptom assessments and was used to relate biomarkers to change patterns rather than single timepoint outcomes [49, 75].

Safety and adverse events were infrequently reported in biomarker association studies. Explicit adverse event monitoring appeared primarily in interventional designs that paired biomarker measurement with therapeutic exposure, such as neuromodulation combined with cognitive training, where systematic session-level monitoring was described [86]. Consistency analysis of core biomarker families

A family-level synthesis was performer of the three core biomarker families most repeatedly highlighted across the corpus: NfL, GFAP, and the CRP family (hs-CRP/CRP) (Supplementary Material 5). NfL studies (n = 5) and GFAP studies (n = 2) showed uniformly positive adjusted associations, predominantly in prognostic rather than same-time diagnostic settings. Most positive NfL and GFAP studies measured the biomarker in the acute phase and linked it to later PSCI or NPS outcomes, whereas one NfL study addressed late-phase rehabilitation response rather than incident PSCI.

CRP-family findings were more heterogeneous. Across the seven informative CRP/hs-CRP studies, five were positive, one was mixed or attenuated after fuller adjustment, and one was null. Heterogeneity arose from phenotype branch (PSCI vs. depression/apathy/fatigue), analytic role (concurrent vs. longitudinal/prognostic), phase of sampling, and confounder structure, particularly in chronic fatigue cohorts where richer adjustment attenuated apparent associations. Taken together, these patterns suggest that NfL and GFAP currently behave as more coherent candidate prognostic markers in this literature, whereas CRP-family signals remain more outcome- and phase-dependent.

Geographic subgroup analysis: China versus Non-China

Geographic subgroup analysis confirmed marked concentration of the mapped literature in China (78/105 studies), with 27 studies conducted outside China (Supplementary Material 6). China studies were more often acute (76.9% vs. 33.3%), prospective (73.1% vs. 55.6%), and PSCI-oriented (57.7% vs. 40.7%), with greater representation of metabolic/lipid/endocrine and vascular biomarker models. In contrast, non-China studies more often addressed inflammation-focused biomarkers, chronic or early-chronic phases, and NPS phenotypes such as fatigue, anxiety, delirium, or chronic depressive symptoms.

Methodological architecture also differed across settings. Unadjusted or limited analyses were more common outside China (59.3% vs. 14.1%), lower/moderate NOS-informed ratings were more frequent in non-China studies, and mixed findings were more common outside China (25.9% vs. 9.0%), although crude positive-association prevalence was broadly similar (70.4% non-China vs. 74.4% China). These subgroup differences should not be interpreted as geography-only biological differences. Rather, they reflect a different mix of study architectures, biomarker domains, outcome constructs, and sampling windows that shape how biomarker effects become visible in each literature segment.

Shared and outcome-specific biomarkers across PSCI and NPS

Shared biomarker mapping showed that cross-literature sharing was more common than same-study dual validation (Supplementary Material 7). NfL and GFAP emerged as the clearest shared candidates across PSCI and NPS branches, whereas the CRP-family was widely shared across PSCI, depression, apathy, and fatigue but was materially more heterogeneous and more sensitive to confounder structure. Same-study dual-outcome evidence was sparse and usually asymmetric: biomarkers often tracked one branch more clearly than the other even when both cognitive and neuropsychiatric scales were collected.

Within the current corpus, several biomarkers remained outcome-specific at the mapped-literature level. PSCI-specific signals included FGF-21, galectin-3, neuroglobin after intracerebral hemorrhage, TSPO, lipoprotein(a), and urinary AD7c-NTP, whereas NPS-specific signals included 25-hydroxyvitamin D, albumin, MDA/oxidative stress panels, AIP/UHR, and several endocrine or psychosocial-stress-linked biomarkers. The most defensible translational conclusion at present is therefore not that single biomarkers have already been validated to predict both outcome families simultaneously, but rather that a limited set of candidate bridge biomarkers now warrants direct same-cohort co-modeling.

Discussion

Qualitative synthesis and what this review adds

This scoping review maps a translational evidence base in which molecular biomarkers are repeatedly proposed as tools to refine prognosis, stratify follow-up, and enrich trials in late-life-enriched stroke cohorts, rather than being reported only as mechanistic correlates of recovery [139]. Several included studies exemplify a pragmatic “early biospecimen, later phenotype” architecture, where admission biomarkers are linked to PSCI at around three months and modeled alongside stroke severity and vascular risk profiles, with incremental prediction reporting in some cohorts [34, 67, 69]. Similar approaches appear in neuropsychiatric phenotypes, where early inflammatory or regulatory signals are measured during hospitalization and related to depression outcomes at weeks to months, often using validated diagnostic frameworks and severity scales [54–56].

The map also indicates that translational intent is increasingly expressed through model-based outputs, including discrimination metrics, cut-off based classification, and in selected reports, reclassification analysis [67, 78]. Reporting consistency remains variable, which matters because model development, validation, and transparent reporting are central for clinical interpretability and external uptake [140, 141]. Risk-of-bias and applicability considerations become particularly relevant when prediction models are trained in single-center cohorts, when outcome definitions vary, or when pre-analytical procedures are incompletely reported [142, 143]. Should late-life post-stroke brain health be approached with a single cross-cutting biomarker, or does the observed heterogeneity justify a phase- and phenotype-specific biomarker strategy that treats PSCI and NPS as partially overlapping but not interchangeable translational targets [49, 72]? The analyses sharpen this question in three ways. First, the literature cannot be interpreted as a flat pool because methodological quality was uneven, with lower ratings clustering in cross-sectional or narrowly selected cohorts. Second, the core-biomarker consistency analysis suggests that NfL and GFAP currently behave more coherently than CRP-family markers, whose signals appear more dependent on phenotype branch, timing, and confounder control. Third, cross-literature sharing between PSCI and NPS is more established than same-study dual validation, meaning that a ‘shared’ biomarker should currently be interpreted as a candidate translational bridge rather than as a proven dual-outcome predictor.

Translation also depends on how performance is communicated. Several biomarker studies emphasize AUC reporting, yet clinical usefulness often depends on calibration, net benefit, and decision consequences rather than discrimination alone [144–146]. This distinction is highly relevant when biomarkers are proposed as screening enrichers or triggers for more intensive cognitive or psychiatric monitoring in late-life-enriched stroke cohorts [67, 75]. Biological interpretation in translational terms

The included evidence clusters around biological domains that plausibly converge on cognition and behavior through neurovascular unit dysfunction, systemic immune activation, and neuroinjury burden, while also suggesting that different biomarkers may serve distinct translational roles. Neuroaxonal injury markers such as NfL are positioned as scalable blood-based signals that can reflect acute injury burden and potentially enrich risk for later cognitive impairment or broader mental health outcomes [34, 72]. Glial injury markers, including GFAP, provide a complementary layer that may capture astroglial response and barrier-related perturbation, with signals reported for both PSCI and post-stroke depression in prospective cohorts [35, 74]. Endothelial and extracellular matrix remodeling biology also appears repeatedly, including MMP-9 and related markers, which have been linked to cognitive impairment and to depression phenotypes after acute ischemic stroke [38, 70].

Metabolic and immune-metabolic pathways widen the translational landscape toward systemic physiology that is already embedded in routine care. Uric acid related indices, cystatin C, and composite metabolic markers were evaluated as predictors of cognitive impairment or fatigue constructs, suggesting that clinically accessible analytes are being repurposed for brain health phenotyping in older survivors [39, 81, 110]. Longitudinal data from Nor-COAST highlight immune and immune-metabolic biology, including inflammatory biomarkers and kynurenine pathway intermediates associated with PSCI and cognitive trajectories, which supports a model in which systemic immune activation may contribute to sustained vulnerability beyond the acute injury window [49–51].

Regulatory biomarkers and multi-analyte strategies add another translational layer. MicroRNA studies often use discovery-to-validation pipelines and propose blood-based signatures for PSCI or depression classification, yet reproducibility is sensitive to pre-analytical handling and qPCR reporting standards [55, 56]. Metabolomics signatures for PSCI and depression were reported in serum or urine, typically as panel-based discriminative models, which increase potential utility but also heighten sensitivity to analytical variability and reporting completeness [48, 59]. Taken together, these multi-analyte approaches remain promising, but their translational value is likely to depend heavily on assay harmonization, reporting completeness, and cross-setting reproducibility.

A smaller but clinically consequential subset of studies addresses neurodegeneration-linked biology. Plasma amyloid and tau measures can help interpret mixed pathology in late life, where vascular injury may interact with pre-existing neurodegenerative vulnerability [116]. These findings do not imply that stroke-related cognitive outcomes are neurodegenerative by default, but they support the need to consider co-pathology when late-life-enriched cohorts show divergent cognitive trajectories despite similar vascular injury profiles [49, 116].

Clinical phenotyping and timing as the central translational bottleneck

Outcome heterogeneity remains a central barrier to clinical translation. PSCI is most often operationalized using global screening instruments, yet cut-offs, education corrections, and timing windows vary across cohorts, which can change case definitions even when the same instrument is used [34, 39]. This variability also extends to how impairment is framed, since some studies focus on screening-defined PSCI, while others approximate broader constructs such as cognitive impairment with no dementia, domain-specific deficits, or cognitive trajectories. These choices influence both prevalence and the apparent strength of biomarker associations, particularly when cognitive outcomes are measured at a single follow-up timepoint rather than longitudinally [67]. Harmonization efforts and calls to standardize PSCI measurement highlight that inconsistent phenotyping reduces comparability and can obscure phase-specific signals that might otherwise be clinically informative [20, 33].

This heterogeneity was not treated merely as descriptive noise. Screening-defined global PSCI, diagnosis-oriented cognitive outcomes, and syndrome-specific NPS classes were interpreted as adjacent but non-equivalent constructs, and the same principle was applied to depression, anxiety, apathy, fatigue, delirium, and secondary sleep/composite measures. This distinction matters because a biomarker may appear inconsistent when the underlying clinical construct changes, even if the biological signal itself is stable.

Neuropsychiatric phenotyping shows similar fragmentation. Early-onset post-stroke depression is assessed within weeks in some studies, while others focus on discharge outcomes or six-month phenotypes, and the choice of timepoint can change whether biomarkers reflect acute stress biology, persistent immune activation, or evolving psychosocial vulnerability [55, 61, 71]. Symptom domains beyond depression are less consistently operationalized, and overlap between NPS and cognition can complicate interpretation when fatigue, sleep disturbance, apathy, or anxiety co-occur with cognitive complaints. Statistical summaries may also mislead when odds ratios are interpreted as markers of clinical performance, because association strength is not equivalent to discrimination or utility [147]. A pragmatic question therefore follows for clinicians and trialists: which phase-specific biomarker signal is actionable, and what is the optimal timing to sample and interpret it for PSCI and NPS in late-life-enriched stroke cohorts [49, 72]?

Dichotomization of continuous cognitive scores may further reduce information and introduce threshold sensitivity, particularly in older adults, where baseline reserve, premorbid performance, and sensory limitations can affect test performance independently of stroke biology [148]. Thresholding can also shift classification at the margins, which matters when biomarker effects are modest and when models are calibrated to a specific outcome definition. This becomes especially relevant when cognition is used as a binary endpoint for prediction models, because performance can vary depending on where thresholds are set and whether cognition is modeled continuously or categorically [34, 67]. Follow-up timing adds another layer. A three-month anchor is common and clinically intuitive, yet the biological meaning of a biomarker measured during admission may differ when the outcome is assessed at two weeks, three months, or one year, because these windows capture different mixtures of acute injury, early recovery, and longer-term vulnerability [34, 39].

From association to qualification: what the evidence suggests about readiness

The mapped literature shows meaningful progress in association studies and early prediction, yet readiness for biomarker qualification varies. Many reports are observational cohorts, which can generate credible signals when designed and reported transparently, but they remain vulnerable to selective reporting, incomplete covariate capture, and context-specific effects [149, 150]. Prognostic biomarker studies would also benefit from consistent adherence to reporting frameworks for marker studies, especially when biomarkers are proposed for risk stratification rather than exploratory correlation [151]. Prediction models require particularly careful reporting because clinical uptake depends on clear specification of predictors, outcomes, timing, and validation procedures, rather than on performance claims alone [140]. The NOS-informed appraisal reinforces that biomarker nomination is not equivalent to biomarker qualification. Although 86 studies fell into the higher-quality stratum, only a minority reported calibration or clinical utility, internal validation, or external validation, and lower-quality studies were enriched for cross-sectional designs with limited temporality and incomplete flow. Recurring signals supported mainly by lower-quality single-center studies should therefore be treated as hypothesis-generating, whereas repeated findings from higher-quality prospective or multicenter cohorts deserve greater priority for replication and qualification.

Diagnostic and classification-oriented studies, including panel-based microRNA and metabolomics signatures, commonly rely on ROC metrics, while fewer reports evaluate calibration, decision consequences, or transportability [48, 59]. Reporting standards for diagnostic accuracy can improve interpretability, particularly when thresholds, reference standards, and test reproducibility are central to translation [152]. Methodological appraisal tools for diagnostic accuracy are also relevant when studies aim to classify PSCI or depression phenotypes, because bias in patient selection, index test conduct, and reference standards can inflate performance estimates [153]. In parallel, prediction model studies increasingly use multivariable frameworks and reclassification analyses, yet risk-of-bias and applicability considerations remain essential when models are trained and tested in limited settings, when sample sizes are modest relative to model complexity, or when phenotyping varies across sites [142, 143]. These issues are amplified in multi-omic contexts, where multiplicity, feature selection, and internal validation choices can materially change apparent performance.

Clinical translation ultimately requires a shift from association to qualification, with an emphasis on reproducibility, calibration, and usefulness in the intended population. External validation, prespecified modelling strategies, and transparent reporting of missing data handling are central steps in that progression [140, 142]. Genetic association and genetic risk inference appear less frequently in this map, but when present they interact with the same translation constraints. Clear reporting standards for genetic association studies support interpretability and reduce ambiguity in downstream synthesis, especially when results are stratified by age or rehabilitation context [52, 158].

Implications for translational research and therapeutic development

The evidence map supports a phase-aware translational strategy. Early neuroinjury and glial injury signals may be most informative for acute risk enrichment and triage toward intensified cognitive monitoring, while immune and immune-metabolic signals may be particularly relevant for longer-term trajectories and for NPS phenotypes such as depression, fatigue, and delirium-related vulnerability [49, 72, 100]. Hemostasis and endothelial biology may provide clinically feasible markers that integrate vascular stress biology with cognitive outcomes when combined with severity measures, functional covariates, and standardized follow-up windows [69, 78]. Phase-aware translation also encourages matching biomarkers to clinical decisions. Admission markers may support early identification of high-risk patients who merit structured cognitive screening at three months, while later sampling might better inform persistent vulnerability and longer-term trajectory models in older survivors [49, 51]. The China-versus-non-China subgroup analysis further suggests that the field is currently split between acute hospital-based PSCI modelling and a more inflammation- and NPS-oriented later-phase literature outside China. This reinforces the need for harmonized multicenter study architectures that bridge these partly separate evidence streams rather than allowing geography-linked design habits to define the future biomarker agenda.

Translation should move beyond discrimination alone. Decision-analytic evaluation can clarify whether using a biomarker to trigger monitoring or intervention yields net clinical benefit under realistic threshold choices, which is essential when downstream actions carry costs, burdens, or risks [146]. This is particularly relevant when interventions are resource-intensive, such as structured neuropsychological evaluation, stepped-care psychiatric follow-up, or trial enrichment strategies in older populations with high comorbidity burden [67, 75]. Model performance frameworks that integrate discrimination, calibration, and clinical usefulness provide a more complete bridge from association to implementation than AUC reporting alone [144, 145]. In practical terms, this means that future translational studies should report not only whether a biomarker separates cases from non-cases, but also whether predicted risks are well calibrated and whether a biomarker-guided strategy improves decision-making at clinically plausible thresholds [144–146].

Finally, assay and platform standards matter for reproducibility. MicroRNA studies should adhere to qPCR reporting guidance, and metabolomics panels should follow minimum reporting standards to reduce variability in signature transfer across laboratories and cohorts [154–156]. Consistent pre-analytical metadata, batch handling, and normalization strategies are not minor technicalities in this setting, because they determine whether a signal can be replicated and whether a panel can be implemented beyond a single center [154, 155]. These design features can accelerate the shift from exploratory signals toward clinically credible biomarker candidates, especially when paired with multicenter validation and harmonized phenotyping [140, 142].

Strengths and limitations

This scoping review provides a structured evidence map of biomarker domains, matrices, assay platforms, timing windows, and outcome phenotypes in late-life-enriched stroke cohorts. The work aligns with established scoping review methodology and reporting guidance, supporting transparency and reproducibility in how evidence was identified and charted. Furthermore, it adds four safeguards that strengthen translational interpretability: an explicit phenotype framework, a NOS-informed methodological appraisal, a core-biomarker consistency analysis, and dedicated geographic and shared/specific biomarker analyses. The map captures both clinically accessible analytes and emerging signature-based approaches, and it clarifies how frequently the literature targets prediction and stratification aims rather than only mechanistic association. However, its age-specific applicability is narrower than that of a strictly age-restricted review because most included studies met eligibility through study-level mean/median age rather than an explicit minimum age threshold.

Several limitations reflect both the underlying literature and the constraints inherent to a scoping approach. Marked heterogeneity in phenotyping, follow-up timing, and assay reporting limited direct comparability and precluded pooled effect estimation, which is consistent with scoping methodology but restricts inference on magnitude and certainty of effects. Cognitive outcomes were often defined using global screening instruments, yet cut-offs, education adjustments, and diagnostic thresholds varied, and NPS outcomes were dominated by depression with less consistent coverage of anxiety, apathy, fatigue, delirium, and sleep-related syndromes, which complicates cross-study synthesis and limits phenotype-specific conclusions. Many studies relied on a single biosampling timepoint and a single follow-up assessment, reducing the ability to distinguish acute injury signals from subacute repair processes or longer-term vulnerability, especially in older adults where trajectories can diverge over time. Only 5 included studies used an explicit minimum age threshold of ≥ 60 years, whereas 100 were included because the cohort mean/median age was ≥ 60 years. The evidence map should therefore be interpreted as representing late-life-enriched rather than strictly age-restricted older-adult cohorts, and age-specific biomarker effects may have been diluted in mixed-age samples.

The evidence base was also largely observational and frequently single-center, which increases susceptibility to selection bias and residual confounding even when multivariable adjustment is reported, particularly when premorbid cognition, baseline mood, frailty, rehabilitation exposure, and medication use are incompletely captured or inconsistently modelled. Geographic concentration and under-representation of diverse health systems and populations may further limit transportability of prediction signals, especially when baseline risk and care pathways differ across settings. Model-focused studies often emphasized discrimination metrics, while external validation, calibration reporting, and transparent specification of predictor handling were less consistently evident, raising the possibility of optimistic performance in development samples and limiting immediate clinical readiness. Similar concerns apply to diagnostic or classification-oriented biomarker studies, where bias in patient selection, index test conduct, and reference standards can inflate apparent accuracy when reporting is incomplete or thresholds are derived post hoc. The China-versus-non-China subgroup analysis was therefore interpreted descriptively rather than causally, because geography and study architecture were too strongly entangled to isolate purely population-level biological differences.

Finally, reproducibility is constrained when pre-analytical procedures, assay metadata, and normalization strategies are not fully reported. This is particularly relevant for microRNA and metabolomics pipelines, where analytic variability can materially affect measured signals and the stability of proposed panels across laboratories and cohorts. The review necessarily relied on published reporting and did not have access to individual participant data, which limits the ability to standardize definitions post hoc, resolve overlapping cohorts, or perform consistent subgroup analyses by stroke subtype, timing window, or comorbidity profiles. In addition, the methodological appraisal was deliberately NOS-informed rather than a single design-specific tool for every possible study architecture; this was a pragmatic choice for a mixed observational biomarker corpus, but it means that some prediction-model-specific or diagnostic-test-specific biases may still be undercaptured. The new consistency, geographic, and shared/specific analyses should therefore be read as structured secondary syntheses within a scoping framework, not as meta-analytic proof of causality or a definitive hierarchy of biomarker performance.

Future directions

Next-generation translational studies should prioritize harmonized phenotyping and phase-specific serial sampling. Repeated cognitive and neuropsychiatric assessments linked to serial biospecimens would clarify whether biomarker trajectories outperform single timepoint measures and whether signals differ between early recovery and longer-term vulnerability windows [49–51]. External validation should become a default expectation for prediction models and multi-marker panels, particularly for metabolomics and microRNA classifiers that may be sensitive to analytic pipeline differences and cohort-specific biology [59, 154–157].

Prospective multicenter cohorts and trial-embedded biosampling frameworks are well positioned to support calibration assessment, transportability testing, and decision-analytic evaluation of biomarker-guided strategies [144–146]. Genetic and epigenetic signals, when pursued, should be reported with established genetic association reporting guidance to maximize interpretability and support synthesis [158]. Several focused questions can help frame a research agenda and keep future work decision-oriented. Which sampling schedule is minimally sufficient to capture the transition from acute injury to subacute repair and then to chronic vulnerability in late-life-enriched stroke cohorts? Which combination of biomarkers, neuroaxonal injury, glial injury, immune-metabolic intermediates, and regulatory RNAs yields stable prediction across sites when pre-analytical handling and assays are harmonized? Can distinct biomarker signatures reliably distinguish transient PSCI from progressive cognitive decline that reflects mixed vascular and neurodegenerative pathology [116, 157], and should this distinction be embedded in trial eligibility criteria for disease-modifying interventions? Which neuropsychiatric domains beyond depression, such as apathy, anxiety, fatigue, sleep disturbance, and delirium, deserve dedicated biomarker qualification pathways, and what is the optimal core battery to measure them without increasing participant burden? Finally, can the field converge on a feasible phase-aware biomarker core set that is scalable in routine care, robust across populations, and informative for trial enrichment in late-life post-stroke brain health?

Conclusions

This scoping review maps 105 studies linking molecular biomarkers to PSCI and NPS in late-life-enriched stroke cohorts and shows a rapidly expanding but methodologically heterogeneous translational literature. Evidence is strongest for blood-based sampling in the acute phase linked to follow-up phenotypes, with recurrent evaluation of neuroinjury signals, inflammatory and immune-metabolic pathways, and composite laboratory indices, alongside emerging microRNA and metabolomics signatures. The analyses sharpen prioritization. NfL and GFAP currently show the most coherent adjusted cross-study signals among the core biomarker families, whereas CRP-family markers appear more phenotype- and phase-dependent. Shared biomarker evidence across PSCI and NPS is presently stronger at the cross-literature level than within same-study dual-outcome models, and the mapped literature remains heavily concentrated in China. Harmonized phenotyping, phase-aware serial sampling, standardized assay reporting, quality-stratified interpretation, and rigorous validation and utility evaluation represent the most direct steps to move promising candidates from exploratory association toward clinically actionable stratification tools in late-life-enriched stroke cohorts. Because most included studies met age eligibility through cohort mean/median age rather than strict minimum-age thresholds, direct extrapolation to strictly age-defined populations aged ≥ 60 years should be undertaken with caution.

A final translational message emerges from this map. The most credible pathway to clinical impact will likely come from phase-aware biomarker strategies that align sampling timepoints and biomarker selection with the intended clinical decision, rather than from searching for a single universal marker. Prospective multicenter cohorts and trial-embedded biospecimen programs that combine standardized cognitive and neuropsychiatric phenotyping with external validation and decision-focused evaluation can convert promising signals into implementable tools. Such an approach would support earlier identification of stroke survivors from late-life-enriched clinical populations at highest risk for persistent cognitive and neuropsychiatric sequelae and would provide a clearer platform for biomarker-guided trial enrichment and targeted intervention development.
