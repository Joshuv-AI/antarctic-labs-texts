Hofmeister effect on the local structure, network mechanics, and water retention of soy protein isolate-tannic acid-κ-carrageenan ternary gels
Authors: Tiantong Lan, Ziming Wang, Yange Fang, Hao Zhang, Jingsheng Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the effects of Cl− and SCN− at different concentrations on the structural characteristics and gel properties of soy protein isolate-tannic acid-κ-carrageenan ternary composite gels. Ion type and concentration exerted distinct effects on gel aggregation, mechanical strength, surface charge, and water retention. At 10% KCl, the gel exhibited the smallest particle size (382 nm), the highest hardness (2008 g), and the highest absolute zeta potential (34.5 mV), whereas the maximum water-holding capacity (91.5%) was observed at 20% KCl. For the KSCN-treated gels, the smallest particle size (450 nm) and highest hardness (848 g) were observed at 10% KSCN, the highest absolute zeta potential (42.1 mV) at 20% KSCN, and the maximum water-holding capacity (87.1%) at 25% KSCN. Overall, Cl− produced a stronger and more compact gel network than SCN−. These findings demonstrate the potential of the Hofmeister effect for tailoring protein-based composite gels.Keywords: Soybean isolate protein, Hofmeister effect, Gel mechanical properties, Salt ions

Introduction

Hydrogels are three-dimensional networks formed by hydrophilic macromolecules through physical association or chemical crosslinking, capable of immobilizing a large amount of water and exhibiting a certain degree of viscoelasticity (Nath et al., 2022). Protein hydrogels offer advantages such as high water content, tunable network structures, and abundant raw material sources, allowing them to serve as an important matrix for structural and textural modulation and the loading of functional components (Wang, Wang, et al., 2024). Among them, soy protein isolate (SPI) possesses high nutritional value, good gelling properties, water-holding capacity, and interfacial activity, making it a commonly used raw material for constructing plant protein gels (Hu et al., 2025). However, gels formed from soy protein isolate alone are prone to problems such as an inhomogeneous network structure, insufficient mechanical strength, and limited water-holding capacity, which restrict their broader practical applications (Tu et al., 2025; Zhao et al., 2026). Therefore, modulating protein structure and improving functional properties through covalent and non-covalent interactions among polyphenols, polysaccharides, and proteins has become a research direction of widespread interest. Current studies have shown that, compared with single-protein gels, the addition of appropriate polyphenols and polysaccharides can modulate gel properties, such as enhancing gel strength and making the network structure denser (Yan et al., 2025; Yang et al., 2021).

Tannic acid (TA) is a natural polyphenol containing multiple galloyl groups, and the pyrogallol and catechol groups in its molecule provide multiple binding sites, enabling it to bind to proteins through non-covalent interactions such as hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions (Zhang et al., 2021). Under suitable oxidative conditions, its phenolic hydroxyl groups can also be converted into quinone structures, which undergo covalent reactions with nucleophilic groups in proteins, such as amino and sulfhydryl groups (Xu et al., 2024). All of the above effects can alter the aggregation and cross-linking behavior of proteins, thereby regulating the formation process of the gel network (Wang, Lan, et al., 2024). Faber et al. (2026) showed that the hardness and deformability of faba bean protein gels treated with TA increased significantly. Guo et al. (2021) found that SPI-TA covalent complexes can form hydrogels with a water-holding capacity of 95.5 ± 0.052%. κ-Carrageenan (KC) is an anionic polysaccharide containing sulfate groups with good thickening and gelling properties, and can participate in the construction of protein gel networks through electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, hydration, and chain entanglement (Fang et al., 2025; Wang, Jiang, et al., 2025). Man et al. (2023) constructed an SPI-chlorogenic acid-KC system, and their study showed that the addition of KC further improved properties such as viscoelasticity and thermal stability of the hydrogel, indicating the feasibility of co-constructing SPI composite hydrogels using polyphenols and polysaccharides. Therefore, incorporating both TA and KC into the SPI system can combine the multi-site binding properties of polyphenols with the rheological modulating effects of polysaccharides, providing a basis for constructing SPI-TA-KC ternary composite gels. On this basis, further modulating the microstructure, rheological properties, texture, and water-holding capacity of the gel can help broaden its potential industrial applications.

Salt solution soaking can serve as an external post-treatment method, modulating the microstructure, viscoelasticity, mechanical properties, and water-holding capacity of protein hydrogels without altering the initial formulation (Lin et al., 2020). Furthermore, both SPI and KC contain charged groups and are relatively sensitive to the ionic environment of the system. Variations in salt type and concentration may lead to differences in protein aggregation state, the degree of polysaccharide chain association, and gel network structure (Patel et al., 2023). The Hofmeister effect reflects the specific influence of different ions on the hydration, dissolution, and aggregation behavior of biological macromolecules (Shao et al., 2025). Its effects typically include direct interactions of ions with charged or nonpolar sites of macromolecules, as well as indirect effects arising from the alteration of interfacial hydration by ions (Moghaddam & Thormann, 2019). For anions, Cl− is located relatively toward the salting-out side of the Hofmeister series, while SCN− is on the strongly salting-in side (Wang, Wu, et al., 2024). The former facilitates macromolecular dehydration, hydrophobic association, and chain aggregation, while the latter exhibits a strong tendency to associate with macromolecules, which may weaken hydrophobic interactions and promote structural loosening. For example, He et al. (2018) found that the addition of high-affinity ammonium sulfate significantly enhanced hydrophobic interactions and chain entanglements within the gelatin gel, which resulted in treated hydrogels with exceptional ultimate strengths that surpassed those of conventional protein gels. Li et al. (2022) found that strong hydrophobic interactions and chain bundles could be introduced between protein chains through direct immersion, which endowed the hydrogel with excellent robustness and flexibility. In the range from Br− to SCN−, these are chaotropic ions, which generally weaken hydrophobic interactions and destabilize macromolecular structures (Chhetri, 2024). The study by Wang, Dong, et al. (2023) found that the order of anion effects on storage modulus, hardness, and gelation temperature was SO42− > Cl− > SCN−, and that SCN− caused gel softening and solubilization. However, the effects of ions also depend on the protein surface properties and other components in the system, and cannot be simply inferred based solely on their positions in the Hofmeister series. Furthermore, existing studies have mainly focused on the effects of salt ions on single-protein gels or protein-polysaccharide binary gels, and systematic research on the regulatory patterns of different salt types and concentrations on the structure and properties of SPI-TA-KC ternary gels is still lacking. Therefore, it is necessary to systematically evaluate the changes in the composite hydrogel after salt treatment from aspects such as system microstructure, micromorphology, rheology, texture, and water state, in order to establish the relationship between gel structure and macroscopic properties.

Based on this, the present study constructed a ternary composite hydrogel using SPI, TA, and KC as raw materials. The gel was then soaked in different concentrations of KCl and KSCN. This allowed a comparison of the regulatory effects of Cl− and SCN− while keeping K+ constant. Given the concentration-dependent effects of Hofmeister ions and the use of immersion treatment for preformed gels, relatively high concentrations of KCl and KSCN (5–25%, w/v) were selected to promote ion diffusion into the gel network and induce measurable concentration-dependent changes under high-salt conditions. The structural changes of the gels were characterized by UV spectroscopy, Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy, particle size, potential, surface hydrophobicity, and cryo-scanning electron microscopy, while the effects of salt type and concentration on the gel network, texture, and water state were evaluated using dynamic rheology, gel hardness, water-holding capacity, and low-field nuclear magnetic resonance. This study aimed to explore the associations between the structural characteristics and macroscopic properties of the gels. The raw materials and modification method employed in this study are beneficial for realizing the high-value utilization of agricultural by-products and promoting the application of plant proteins.

Materials and methods

Materials

Soybean protein isolate (BR, CAS Number: 9010-10-0); Tannic acid (AR, CAS Number: 1401-55-4); κ-Carrageenan (BR, CAS Number: 11114-20-8); KCl (AR, CAS Number: 7447-40-7); KSCN (GR, CAS Number: 333-20-0). All the materials and reagents were purchased from Shanghai Yuanye Bio-Technology Co., Ltd.

Preparation of SPI-TA-KC complex

The method described by Li et al. (2023) was used with minor modifications. Weigh 1 g of soy protein isolate and dissolve it in 50 mL of deionized water, followed by magnetic stirring at 25 °C for 2 h. Weigh 0.17 g of TA and dissolve it in 50 mL of deionized water, followed by magnetic stirring at 25 °C for 1 h. The protein solution was mixed with the tannic acid solution in equal volumes and allowed to stand for 1 h to ensure complete complexation, yielding the SPI-TA solution. No pH adjustment was performed during the preparation process, and the pH of the final composite system was approximately 5.5. Slowly add 1 g of KC powder to the composite solution under magnetic stirring at 50 °C. The mixture was continuously stirred at 50 °C until the KC was fully hydrated and no visible particles remained, resulting in a visually homogeneous dispersion. No further volume adjustment was performed after the addition of KC, and the nominal final volume of the system was approximately 100 mL. Based on this nominal volume, the final concentrations of SPI, TA, and KC were 10, 1.7 and 10 mg/mL, respectively. After thorough mixing, the mixture was allowed to stand at room temperature to facilitate complete complexation and gel formation, yielding the SPI-TA-KC composite gel.

Separate KCl and KSCN solutions were prepared by dissolving 5, 10, 15, 20, or 25 g of each salt in deionized water and adjusting the final volume to 100 mL. Once the salt solutions were fully dissolved, the cylindrical SPI-TA-KC composite gel (5 cm in diameter and 4 cm in height) was immersed in 100 mL of the corresponding salt solution and left to stand at 4 °C for 12 h. After salt soaking, the gels were removed from the solutions, and the residual solution on the gel surface was gently blotted with filter paper. No additional washing step was performed. The resultant samples were designated as STK-0%KCl, STK-5%KCl, STK-10%KCl, STK-15%KCl, STK-20%KCl, STK-25%KCl和STK-0%KSCN, STK-5%KSCN, STK-10%KSCN, STK-15%KSCN, STK-20%KSCN, STK-25%KSCN, respectively. A portion of each solution was freeze-dried for 48 h to obtain composite powders for subsequent use.

Ultraviolet spectrum

The method described by Li et al. (2024) was used with minor modifications. Dissolve all samples synthesized in Section 2.2 in deionized water respectively to prepare solutions with a concentration of 0.2 mg/mL. Scan the samples over a wavelength range of 200–400 nm using a UV–vis spectrophotometer (UV-2600i, SHIMADZU, Japan). After performing a baseline correction using deionized water.

Fourier-transform infrared spectra

The method described by Wang, Li, et al. (2022) was used with minor modifications. Weigh 2 mg of the lyophilized sample prepared in Section 2.2 and mix it with 200 mg of potassium bromide. Grind the mixture in a mortar until thoroughly blended. Press the sample for 1 min under the tablet press, then place the pressed thin sheet into the instrument (VERTEX 70, Bruker, Germany). Infrared spectroscopy measurements were performed under the following conditions: scanning range 4000–400 cm−1, resolution 4 cm−1, and 32 scans. Use potassium bromide flakes from unmixed lyophilized samples as blank control.

Particle size, polydispersity index and zeta potential

The method described by Zhao, Zhang, et al. (2022) was used with minor modifications. Dissolve the lyophilized sample in deionized water to prepare a 1 mg/mL solution. Measure the particle size, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential of the sample using a nanoparticle size analyzer (ZSU3200, Malvern PANalytical, UK) via dynamic light scattering (DLS).

Rheological properties

The method described by Hu et al. (2023) was used with minor modifications. The storage modulus and loss modulus of ternary composite gels made from soy protein isolate were determined using a rotational rheometer (MCR 302, Anton Paar, Austria). Place the prepared composite gel sample at the center of the parallel plate with a 1 mm gap between plates. Remove excess sample using a scraper. Measurements were performed at 25 °C using a PP25 probe. The strain amplitude was set to 0.1%, which was confirmed to be within the linear viscoelastic region by strain sweep tests, and the shear frequency was swept from 0.1 to 10 Hz.

Gel strength

The method described by Long et al. (2025) was used with minor modifications. The gel strength was measured using a texture analyzer (TA-XT, Stable Micro Systems, UK). A flat-ended cylindrical probe (P/0.5, 12.7 mm in diameter) was used. After installing the probe, force and height calibrations were performed. Cylindrical gel samples with a height of 20 mm and a diameter of 25 mm were placed centrally beneath the probe. The probe descended at a constant speed of 1 mm/s until a trigger force of 5 g was reached and then penetrated the gel to a depth of 10 mm. After testing, the probe returned to its pre-test position at a speed of 1 mm/s. Gel strength was defined as the maximum force recorded during the penetration test.

Water distribution

The method described by Chu et al. (2024) was used with minor modifications. The water distribution within gel samples was analysed using a pulse nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectrometer (MesoMR23-040V-I, Shanghai Electronic Technology CO., LTD., China). The Carr-Purcell-Meiom-Gill (CPMG) pulse sequence was employed, with a proton resonance frequency of 200 kHz, 10,000 echo repetitions, and four scan cycles. Relaxation times (T₂) was analysed using MultiExp Inv software.

Cryo scanning electron microscope (Cryo-SEM)

The method described by Yan et al. (2021) was used with minor modifications. After rapid freezing in liquid nitrogen, the sample was transferred to a sample preparation chamber under vacuum using a cryo-preparation transfer system. It was then sublimated at −90 °C for 30 min, followed by sputter-coating with gold at 10 mA for 60 s. Imaging was performed using a cryogenic high-resolution scanning electron microscope (Regulus8100, Hitachi Co., Ltd., Japan) at a cold stage temperature of −140 °C and an accelerating voltage of 3 kV, and a magnification of 500 × .

Apparent salt uptake

The apparent salt uptake of the gels was determined using a gravimetric method based on the mass-balance principle. The entire remaining soaking solution was collected, transferred into Petri dishes, vacuum freeze-dried for 48 h, and weighed immediately after removal from the vacuum freeze-dryer. For each concentration of both salts, a salt-solution blank was prepared using the same initial volume and concentration of salt solution as those used for the corresponding treatment group, but without the addition of gel. The blank was subjected to the same transfer, vacuum freeze-drying, and weighing procedures to correct for potential residue losses and mass variations arising during sample collection, transfer, and freeze-drying. A gel-leachate blank was also prepared by immersing the gel in an equal volume of deionized water under the same conditions as those used for salt treatment. The resulting soaking solution was collected and subjected to the same transfer, vacuum freeze-drying, and weighing procedures to correct for soluble components released from the gel matrix during immersion. Data analysis

ANOVA analysis of variance was performed on the experimental data using BM SPSS Statistics 26 software. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05. Each treatment was replicated three times in parallel experiments. Results are presented as mean values ± standard deviation. Origin 2021 software was employed for graphical representation. Correlation analysis was performed on the zeta potential, particle size, surface hydrophobicity, water-holding capacity, hardness, and rheological properties of the gel after salt treatment.

Results and discussion

Determination of ultraviolet spectra

Ultraviolet spectroscopy enables the investigation of changes in the microenvironment of amino acids by analysing variations in the intensity and position of their ultraviolet absorption peaks, thereby revealing interactions between proteins and small molecules (Ma et al., 2024). As shown in Fig. 1(A) and (B), the ultraviolet absorption spectra of the SPI-TA-KC composite gel treated with different concentrations of KCl and KSCN are presented respectively. The untreated SPI-TA-KC gel exhibited a major absorption peak at approximately 262 nm. For comparison, the absorption maxima of SPI, SPI-TA, and SPI-KC were located at approximately 259, 264, and 259 nm, respectively. The absorption peak of SPI originates from the absorption of tyrosine, tryptophan, and phenylalanine residues within the protein (Wang, Liu, et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2017). Because the incorporation of KC did not alter the peak position of SPI, whereas the addition of TA shifted the absorption maximum from 259 to 264 nm, the peak at 262 nm in the ternary gel was considered to be mainly associated with the spectral contribution of TA. Similarly, Xu et al. (2025) reported that the incorporation of TA induced a red shift in the UV absorption peak of soy protein fibrils, indicating that TA can alter the spectral characteristics of soy protein-based systems. The absorption peak intensities of all treated samples exhibited a significant reduction, accompanied by a pronounced red shift phenomenon. Furthermore, with increasing ion concentration, the ultraviolet absorption peaks demonstrated a trend of initially decreasing before subsequently increasing, indicating that the ionic environment altered the microenvironment around the aromatic residues of the protein and the interaction state among components within the gel. During the increase in ionic concentration, the amino acid groups on the protein surface are initially further buried. Upon reaching a certain concentration, the tyrosine and tryptophan chromophores concealed within the protein are progressively exposed to the external environment. This may be attributed to the multiple phosphate groups attached to the soy protein isolate, which enhance the protein's solubility and increase electrostatic repulsion between protein molecules by amplifying the net negative charge. Consequently, this promotes the expansion and unfolding of the protein structure (Jia et al., 2023). Lucas et al. (2009) found that salt ions can diffuse through protein matrices in different ways and interact with characteristic groups of the aromatic amino acid side chains, thereby affecting their spectroscopic behavior.

(A) UV spectra of the STK gel treated with KCl; (B) UV spectra of the STK gel treated with KSCN.

The degree of change in the protein peak intensity induced by the two ions at the same concentration differs, and it is evident that SCN− exerts a greater influence. This aligns with the Hofmeister anion series (Gregory et al., 2022), where Cl− is positioned in the middle of the series and has a relatively weak ability to affect protein structure. In contrast, SCN−, located on the rightmost side of the series, is a chaotropic ion. It disrupts the hydration layer of the protein, significantly reducing both intra- and intermolecular interactions, thereby leading to protein structural destabilization and even denaturation (Wang, Dong, et al., 2025). Paladino et al. (2022) showed that SCN− possesses an extremely abundant number of binding sites, thereby exerting a strong influence on proteins.

Determination of surface hydrophobicity

Surface hydrophobicity is indicative of the degree of tertiary structure unfolding of the protein molecule, the extent of exposed hydrophobic groups, and the strength of intermolecular interactions (Hu et al., 2024). The greater the binding of bromophenol blue to hydrophobic sites on the protein surface, the higher the surface hydrophobicity of the sample. Fig. 3(A) and (B) show the surface hydrophobicity of SPI-TA-KC composite gels modified with KCl and KSCN salt solutions respectively. The hydrophobicity of KCl-modified sample surfaces exhibited an initial increase followed by a decrease with rising KCl concentration, peaking at a 10.26% enhancement. Beyond a 20% KCl concentration, the binding capacity showed a marked reduction. This change may be attributed to the increasing concentrations of K+ and Cl−, which alter the hydration environment surrounding the protein through a salting-out effect. The resulting reduction in protein hydration may promote intramolecular and intermolecular hydrophobic association and induce conformational rearrangement, thereby exposing previously buried hydrophobic sites. Consequently, the BPB-binding amount increased, indicating greater exposure of hydrophobic sites on the sample surface. Previous studies have demonstrated that TA can interact with SPI through hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions, leading to changes in protein conformation and surface properties (Wang, Wang, et al., 2023). Therefore, the incorporation of TA in the SPI-TA-KC network may influence the accessibility of hydrophobic groups during salt-induced structural rearrangement. Zhang et al. (2025) also observed that increasing salt concentration led to heightened hydrophobicity on the surface of pea proteins. As the KCl concentration increases further, the KCl solution induces partial protein denaturation or aggregation through the salting-out effect, thereby reducing the surface exposure of hydrophobic groups and consequently diminishing surface hydrophobicity. In contrast, the surface hydrophobicity of potassium thiocyanate-modified samples decreased, yet no significant difference in surface hydrophobicity was observed with increasing concentration (p > 0.05). The primary reason is that SCN− is a typical chaotropic ion, which denatures proteins. It disrupts the hydrophobic interactions within the protein, causing the hydrophobic groups to become reburied or dispersed, thereby reducing surface hydrophobicity (Tan et al., 2025). Similarly, a study by Liang et al. (2026) indicated that the Hofmeister effect can influence the exposure of hydrophobic residues in proteins. It is noteworthy that low-concentration KSCN treatment already exerted a considerable influence on the surface hydrophobicity of the protein, which is also consistent with the conclusion from UV and FTIR analyses that KSCN had a greater impact on the system. Furthermore, combined with the results in Section 3.6, at 10% KCl concentration, the BPB binding amount reached its maximum, accompanied by a significant increase in hardness, indicating that moderately exposed hydrophobic sites may facilitate intermolecular association and network reinforcement.

Determination of particle size, polydispersity index (PDI) and zeta potential

As shown in Fig. 4(A) and (B), the particle size and PDI of the SPI-TA-KC composite gel modified with KCl and KSCN salt solutions, respectively, are presented. The particle size of the KCl-modified samples decreased significantly (p < 0.05), with the smallest reaching 382 nm, corresponding to a polydispersity index of 0.23. This is because, in the salt solution, K+ and Cl− accumulate around the gel molecules, shielding the electrostatic repulsion between the molecules and allowing attractive forces to dominate, which leads to the contraction of the gel structure. Additionally, K+ and Cl− bind with water molecules, disrupting the hydration layer around the gel molecules and weakening the supporting effect of water molecules, thereby causing the gel to collapse and its particle size to decrease (Zhang & Cremer, 2006). In addition to salt-induced aggregation of SPI, the particle size variation of the SPI-TA-KC ternary gel may also be affected by the interactions among protein, polyphenol, and polysaccharide components. TA has been reported to form noncovalent interactions with SPI, while KC can interact with SPI through hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions, contributing to the formation of composite networks (Lu et al., 2023; Wang, Wang, et al., 2023). With changes in KSCN concentration, the gel particle size initially decreases and then increases. At low concentrations, KSCN initially acts on the gel molecules by disrupting hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions between them (Wang, Qiao, et al., 2022), leading to localized loosening of the gel network without complete structural disintegration, resulting in a slight reduction in particle size. At a concentration of 10%, SCN− further disrupts the intermolecular interactions within the gel, triggering collapse of the gel network. This results in a sharp decrease in particle size to 450 nm, corresponding to a polydispersity index of 0.17. Thereafter, at concentrations of 10%–20%, the destructive effect of SCN− reached equilibrium with the stable state following gel network collapse, with no significant difference in particle size (p > 0.05). As the concentration further increases, the strong hydration capacity of SCN− and its direct interaction with the gel molecules take over (Wang, Dong, et al., 2023), leading to the complete disruption of the gel network structure and the formation of loose aggregates, resulting in a significant increase in particle size (p < 0.05). Bourke et al. (2023) also confirmed in their study that the Hofmeister effect can affect the structure of substances, leading to changes in their particle size.

(A) Particle size and PDI of the STK gel treated with KCl; (B) Particle size and PDI of the STK gel treated with KSCN; (C) Zeta potential of the STK gel treated with KCl; (D) Zeta potential of the STK gel treated with KSCN.

As shown in Fig. 4(C) and (D), the zeta potential of the SPI-TA-KC composite gel modified with KCl and KSCN salt solutions, respectively, are presented. The potential of all samples was negative, owing to the predominance of negatively charged amino acids on the surface of soy protein isolate. The addition of KC also resulted in an increase in the absolute value of the complex's potential (Lu et al., 2023). The absolute values of the modified samples' potentials were significantly reduced (p < 0.05). Among these, the gel modified with KCl exhibited the lowest absolute potential value of 27.2 mV, while modification with KSCN yielded the lowest absolute potential value of 30.1 mV. This is mainly due to the fact that at low concentrations, both KCl and KSCN begin to induce an ion screening effect, partially neutralizing the negative charge on the gel surface, resulting in a decrease in the absolute value of the zeta potential (Ma et al., 2025). As the concentration increases, the screening effect is further enhanced, resulting in the absolute value of the zeta potential reaching its minimum (Wang, Wu, et al., 2024). As the concentration of KSCN increases, the specific adsorption capacity of the ions also begins to take effect, with a large amount of SCN− potentially directly adsorbing onto the gel surface, leading to a significant increase in the absolute value of the zeta potential. However, once adsorption approaches saturation, the extremely high negative charge density on the surface may generate a strong repulsive force, forcing some of the adsorbed SCN− to rearrange or become disordered, thereby reducing the absolute value of the surface potential again. The study by Bourke et al. (2023) has also demonstrated that the ion screening effect has an influence on the zeta potential.

Determination of rheological properties

Fig. 5. (A) and (B) show the storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) of the gels modified with KCl and KSCN, respectively. The storage modulus (G′) reflects the energy stored by the material under external force, indicating the elasticity and structural strength of the gel, while the loss modulus (G″) reflects the energy dissipated by the material under external force, indicating the viscosity of the gel and the intermolecular mobility. It can be observed that the G′ values of all samples are higher than G″, indicating that the samples have formed a predominantly elastic gel structure. Previous studies have shown that TA can associate with soy protein fibrils through hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions, thereby promoting the formation of a denser gel network and significantly enhancing gel strength and viscoelastic moduli (Xu et al., 2025). The incorporation of KC can further modulate hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, and electrostatic interactions within the system, thereby improving the rheological properties of the gel (Lou et al., 2025). On this basis, salt ions may induce rearrangement of the pre-existing SPI-TA-KC network through charge screening, modification of the hydration environment, and ion-specific interactions. Compared to untreated gels, those treated with KCl and KSCN exhibit higher G' values, indicating that salt ions can effectively enhance the gel network properties. Specifically, KCl increased the G' value to a greater extent, which is consistent with the findings of Wang, Dong, et al. (2023). This weakens intermolecular repulsive forces, causing the molecules to bind more tightly together. Consequently, the network structure contracts and becomes more compact (Wang, Zeng, et al., 2022). In addition, Cl− binds to the water molecules surrounding the gel, leading to a reduction in the hydration layer on the surface of the gel molecular chains. This enhances the hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions between the gel molecules, promoting closer association of the molecular chains and increasing the ability to resist external forces, which is manifested as an increase in G'. At a KCl concentration of 20%, the storage modulus and loss modulus are at their highest. In the study by Ma et al. (2025), it was also found that salt ions can increase the G′ of the SPI-konjac glucomannan gel, and that ionic strength has a significant impact on the rheological properties of the hydrogel. At a concentration of 5%, the ion-shielding effect predominates in gels modified with KSCN, resulting in an increase in G'. As the concentration reaches 10%, the disruptive effect of SCN− on hydrogen bonds and intermolecular interactions within the gel begins to manifest. In the study by Qiao et al. (2021), it was also found that SCN− can directly bind to the protein backbone, weakening the hydrogen bonds between polypeptide chains, thereby affecting the gel. At a concentration of 15%, the storage modulus reaches its maximum value, indicating that at this concentration, the structural disruption effect of SCN− and the network rearrangement effect achieve an optimal balance. At 25%, the disruptive effect of the ions significantly increases, becoming the dominant factor. This excessive disruption of the gel's network structure substantially weakens the intermolecular forces between chains, resulting in a marked decrease in the storage modulus. Zhao, Li, et al. (2022) also came to a similar conclusion in their research.

(A) Rheological properties of the STK gel treated with KCl; (B) Rheological properties of the STK gel treated with KSCN.

Determination of gel hardness

Gel hardness plays a crucial role in determining suitability for various food applications. As shown in Fig. 6(A and B), the gel hardness after treatment with different concentrations of KCl and KSCN is presented. Compared to the unmodified gel, both KCl- and KSCN-treated gels exhibited significantly enhanced hardness (p < 0.05), with both ions displaying a similar trend. This is because, at low concentrations, the ions effectively screen or neutralize the electrostatic repulsion between the gel molecular chains, causing the gel network to contract and become denser, which is macroscopically manifested as an increase in hardness. In addition, ions compete for the hydrated water molecules surrounding the protein, weakening the hydrogen bonds between the protein and its hydrated water molecules, and promoting hydrophobic association between protein molecules, which also contributes to an increase in gel hardness (Li et al., 2022). However, as the concentration increases further, the large number of ions requiring hydration may disrupt the originally uniform and continuous network structure of the gel molecules, leading to a decrease in hardness. Compared to KCl, gels treated with KSCN exhibit lower hardness. This is attributable to the highly disruptive nature of the SCN−, which not only disrupts structure through competitive hydration like Cl− but also directly undermines the hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonds that maintain the network structure (Lai et al., 2023). This behavior aligns with the Hofmeister anion series. These results are generally corroborated by the rheological tests, indicating that both salt treatments enhanced the mechanical properties of the gels, and the strengthening effect of KCl was overall greater than that of KSCN. However, for the KCl-treated samples, G′ reached its maximum at 20%, whereas hardness peaked at 10%; for the KSCN-treated groups, the peaks of G′ and hardness appeared at 15% and 10%, respectively. This indicates that although both G′ and hardness reflect the mechanical properties of the gel, the former characterizes network elasticity under small-amplitude oscillatory conditions, while the latter reflects deformation resistance under large-deformation compression; therefore, their specific concentration-dependent responses are not entirely consistent.

(A) Gel hardness of the STK gel treated with KCl; (B) Gel hardness of the STK gel treated with KSCN.

Determination of the water holding capacity of the gel

As shown in Fig. 7(A and B), the water holding capacity of the gel after treatment with different concentrations of KCl and KSCN are presented. The water holding capacity of all modified gels was significantly reduced (p < 0.05), and the changes in water holding capacity exhibited a similar trend with increasing concentrations of both ions. The main reason for the decrease in water holding capacity of the gel modified by KCl is that the ion screening effect in the solution reduces the function of hydrophilic groups within the gel, and the high-concentration KCl solution causes shrinkage and partial dehydration of the gel network (Lin et al., 2020). In addition to its ion screening effect on the gel, KSCN can also directly disrupt the hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions that the gel strongly relies on (Yang et al., 2023), leading to changes in the water holding capacity of the gel network. The study by Lin et al. (2020) also indicates that the addition of salt leads to an increase in the pore size of the gel matrix, thereby reducing the water holding capacity of the hydrogel. However, the water holding capacity of all treated gels remained at 80% or above, indicating that the network structure of the gel still possesses a certain stability under high-salt conditions. This suggests that high water holding capacity can be maintained while achieving other modification goals for the gel. Combined with the rheological and hardness results, it can be seen that salt treatment strengthened the mechanical network of the gel but reduced its water-holding capacity to a certain extent. This indicates that although ion-induced network contraction and molecular aggregation made the gel more robust, they may also compress the network pores and expel some of the originally loosely bound water, thereby causing a decrease in water-holding capacity. This is consistent with the findings of Yang et al. (2025).

(A) Water holding capacity of the STK gel treated with KCl; (B) Water holding capacity of the STK gel treated with KSCN.

Determination of water distribution

The water distributions of the gels treated with different concentrations of KCl and KSCN are presented in Fig. 8(A) and (B). The three relaxation peaks of the gel sample correspond to three types of water: the shortest relaxation time, T21, corresponds to the water with the poorest mobility within the gel (bound water); the intermediate relaxation time, T22, corresponds to the immobilized water; and the longest relaxation time, T23, corresponds to the free water within the gel (Yu et al., 2024). After treatment with KCl solution, the relaxation peaks of the gel shifted to the left, indicating that the mobility of water within the gel decreased and the amount of more tightly bound water increased (Zhang et al., 2023). The main reason is that the ions in the salt solution shield the negative charges, thereby weakening the repulsive forces between molecules. This allows the gel molecular chains to associate more tightly, leading to contraction of part of the network structure and the expulsion of some of the originally loosely bound water, which is consistent with the results obtained for water-holding capacity. However, the two parameters are not entirely equivalent. Both water distribution and WHC are influenced by water-matrix interactions, network continuity, and pore structure. Low-field nuclear magnetic resonance primarily reflects the mobility and microenvironment of water within the gel, whereas WHC additionally depends on the ability of the gel network to resist contraction and water expulsion during centrifugation. In addition, the treated gel peaks became significantly broader, indicating that the internal structure of the gel has become inhomogeneous and the pore size distribution has widened. Wang, Dong, et al. (2025) similarly observed that the peaks of the gel became lower and broader following treatment with salt ions. For the gel treated by KSCN, the relaxation peak of T22 first narrows and then broadens as the concentration of the salt solution increases. SCN− is a strongly chaotropic ion, characterized by weak hydrophilicity and strong hydrophobicity, which leads to more complex changes in the gel structure (Xue et al., 2022). First, at low concentrations, SCN− primarily functions to screen charges and disrupt the hydrogen bond network, making water molecules more mobile. This causes partial contraction or collapse of the gel network structure, leading to the “squeezing out” of semi-bound water molecules from the network. Consequently, the water retained within the gel becomes more homogeneous, resulting in a narrower peak. At higher concentrations, the disruptive ability of SCN− further manifests, leading to drastic conformational changes within the gel. This results in decreased internal homogeneity, which is reflected in the broadening of the peak.

(A) Water distribution of the STK gel treated with KCl; (B) Water distribution of the STK gel treated with KSCN.

Determination of Cryo-scanning electron microscopy (Cryo-SEM)

As shown in Fig. 9, the untreated gel exhibited a typical three-dimensional porous structure, characterized by irregularly shaped pores, a broad pore-size distribution, and relatively thin pore walls in some regions. After treatment with KCl and KSCN, the gels retained their porous frameworks; however, marked changes were observed in network compactness, pore-wall continuity, and pore uniformity, indicating that the salt ions induced structural reorganization of the SPI-TA-KC composite gel network. KCl treatment markedly promoted contraction and densification of the gel network. Specifically, as the KCl concentration increased, the originally large and irregular pores gradually became smaller, accompanied by enhanced interconnection between the pore walls. At a KCl concentration of 10%, a relatively uniform and continuous honeycomb-like network was formed. This denser and more compact gel network may account for the highest hardness observed at this concentration. With a further increase in KCl concentration, some pore walls became noticeably thicker, and pore coalescence occurred, resulting in elongated elliptical or fissure-like structures. These observations indicate that although higher ionic concentrations continued to promote contraction of the gel network, they also intensified localized aggregation and reduced structural uniformity. This was consistent with the pronounced broadening of the water-distribution peaks at KCl concentrations of 15% and 20%, which similarly indicated an inhomogeneous internal structure of the gel. Compared with KCl treatment, the KSCN-treated gels exhibited a more heterogeneous and disrupted network morphology. At a concentration of 5%, the gel network appeared relatively disordered, which may account for the comparatively large particle size and low hardness observed in this group. The pronounced broadening of the water-distribution peak at the same concentration further supported the presence of an inhomogeneous internal gel structure. With increasing KSCN concentration, partial pore contraction and localized rearrangement of the gel framework occurred; however, the continuity of the pore walls and the uniformity of the pore-size distribution remained markedly lower than those of the KCl-treated gels. Large irregular pores, fissures, and lamellar aggregates were also observed. This may be attributed to the fact that SCN− is a typical chaotropic anion capable of altering protein hydration and intermolecular noncovalent interactions, thereby weakening the pre-existing network structure (Wang, Dong, et al., 2023). At a KSCN concentration of 20%, the gel exhibited a relatively uniform network structure, whereas a further increase to 25% resulted in more pronounced structural disruption by SCN−.

Cryo-SEM of the STK gel treated with KCl/KSCN at 500× magnification.

Determination of apparent salt uptake

As shown in Fig. 10(A) and (B), the apparent salt uptake of the gels treated with either KCl or KSCN increased significantly with increasing salt concentration (p < 0.05). Specifically, the apparent salt uptake increased progressively from 0.96 to 4.32 g following KCl treatment and from 0.90 to 4.39 g following KSCN treatment. In contrast to the continuous increase in apparent salt uptake mass, the apparent salt uptake percentage of both salts reached a relatively high level at 10%, followed by a decrease and subsequent stabilization. This variation may be associated with salt-induced contraction of the gel network and the resulting reduction in the capacity of the pores to accommodate liquid, which is generally consistent with the overall decrease in the water-holding capacity of the salt-treated gels. The similar overall trends observed for KCl and KSCN indicate that salt concentration was an important factor affecting both the apparent salt uptake mass and uptake percentage. It should be noted that the gravimetric method employed in this study could not distinguish between ions adsorbed onto gel components and free salts that diffused into or remained within the pore liquid of the gel. Therefore, the obtained values should not be regarded as the actual residual salt content within the gels. (See Fig. 11.)

Correlation analysis

Because the measured parameters may exhibit nonlinear or nonmonotonic relationships, and distance correlation analysis is better suited to identifying dependencies under such conditions, it was employed to evaluate the intrinsic associations among the potential, particle size, surface hydrophobicity, water-holding capacity, hardness, and rheological properties of the treated samples (Székely et al., 2007). Among the examined parameters, the distance correlation coefficient between potential and water-holding capacity was the highest (0.825), indicating a strong dependence between the charge state of the system and its water-retention capacity. This relationship may arise because ions can alter the surface charge and aggregation behavior of protein particles through electrostatic screening, thereby modulating the uniformity and pore structure of the gel network, as well as its ability to immobilize water (Liu et al., 2026). The distance correlation coefficient between surface hydrophobicity and hardness was 0.772, indicating a strong association between changes in the surface hydrophobic characteristics of the protein and gel hardness. Moreover, the distance correlation coefficient between surface hydrophobicity and G' was 0.544, further suggesting that surface hydrophobic characteristics were associated not only with the overall hardness of the gel but also potentially with the elasticity of the gel network. The distance correlation coefficient between water-holding capacity and G' was 0.765, indicating a strong covariation between the water-retention capacity of the gel and network elasticity. The correlation coefficient between water-holding capacity and hardness was 0.521, suggesting that the water state may contribute to the development of the macroscopic mechanical properties of the gel, although hardness is not determined solely by water-holding capacity. The correlation coefficient between hardness and G' was 0.710, indicating a high degree of consistency between these two mechanical parameters. Because hardness reflects the resistance of the gel to compression under large deformation, whereas G' characterizes its elastic response under small-amplitude oscillatory conditions, the strong correlation between these parameters indicates that ion-induced changes in the gel network were simultaneously reflected in both static textural properties and dynamic rheological behavior. In contrast, particle size exhibited generally low distance correlation coefficients with the other parameters. Its correlation with hardness was relatively higher, at 0.528, whereas the correlation coefficients with potential, surface hydrophobicity, water-holding capacity, and G' were 0.339, 0.333, 0.371, and 0.377, respectively. Overall, distance correlation analysis revealed coupled relationships between the structural characteristics and macroscopic properties of the gels. Among these factors, charge state, surface hydrophobicity, and water-retention behavior may be important correlates of the mechanical properties of the gel network.

Conclusion

This study focused on SPI-TA-KC ternary composite gels and investigated the effects of Hofmeister anions on gel structure, texture, and water retention by varying the concentrations of KCl and KSCN while maintaining K+ as the common cation. Changes in gel structure were characterized using UV spectroscopy, FTIR, particle size, potential, surface hydrophobicity, and Cryo-SEM. Rheological properties, gel hardness, water-holding capacity, and low-field nuclear magnetic resonance were further evaluated to assess macroscopic performance and water state, thereby enabling a multiscale analysis from molecular interactions and aggregation behavior to gel properties. The results showed that Cl− and SCN− exerted distinctly different regulatory effects on SPI-TA-KC gels. Salt treatment reduced the absolute potential, facilitating closer contact and network rearrangement among gel components, while changes in particle size and BPB-binding amount indicated marked differences in aggregation behavior and the exposure of hydrophobic sites induced by the two anions. KCl treatment generally promoted network contraction and structural densification. The 10% KCl-treated gel exhibited the smallest particle size (382 nm) and the highest hardness (2008 g). In contrast, KSCN treatment caused a non-monotonic change in particle size, and the hardness of the 10% KSCN-treated gel was only 848 g. All samples exhibited an elastic-dominant behavior, with G' remaining higher than G". However, the KCl-treated gels generally showed higher G' and hardness values, indicating that KCl-induced structural rearrangement was more favorable for the formation of an effective network with greater elasticity and resistance to deformation. Cryo-SEM observations further supported these differences, showing that KCl treatment generally produced a denser and more continuous network, whereas KSCN treatment resulted in a comparatively heterogeneous and locally disrupted microstructure. These microstructural differences were consistent with the mechanical results. Although KSCN induced more pronounced changes in local structure and surface hydrophobicity, these changes did not translate into greater macroscopic network reinforcement; rather, its reinforcing effect was comparatively weaker, indicating that the extent of local structural perturbation cannot be directly equated with the degree of network reinforcement. The salt-treated gels retained relatively high water-holding capacities, reaching 91.5% for the 20% KCl group and 87.1% for the 25% KSCN group, although both values were lower than that of the untreated gel. Low-field nuclear magnetic resonance analysis showed that salt treatment altered water mobility and the microenvironments of water within the gels. The shift of some relaxation peaks toward shorter T2 values indicated reduced mobility of the water retained within the gel network, whereas the decrease in WHC suggested that network contraction may have simultaneously expelled part of the weakly bound water. Therefore, T2 and WHC reflect different aspects of water behavior, namely the mobility of the water remaining within the gel and the overall capacity of the gel to retain water under external force, respectively. The mechanical reinforcement of the gel network and water retention thus did not change in a simply synchronous manner. Overall, the observed macroscopic properties of the gels may be associated with the combined changes in molecular aggregation, network continuity, structural contraction, and water mobility.

Unlike previous studies on the Hofmeister effect in single-protein systems or protein-polysaccharide binary systems, the present work systematically examined the effects of different Cl− and SCN− concentrations on SPI-TA-KC protein-polyphenol-polysaccharide ternary composite gels and explored the associations between their structural characteristics, including local structural responses and aggregation behavior, and their macroscopic properties, including network mechanics and water retention. It should be noted that the comparison between KCl and KSCN was based on the same mass-based concentrations rather than equimolar concentrations. Because the two salts have different molar masses, identical mass-based concentrations correspond to different molar concentrations and ionic strengths, and the water activity and osmotic pressure of the systems may also differ. Therefore, the observed differences may have resulted from the combined effects of anion specificity, actual ion concentration, and the overall physicochemical properties of the solutions. Further comparisons under equimolar or equal-ionic-strength conditions are required to distinguish more precisely between anion-specific effects and concentration effects. In addition, KSCN was used in this study mainly as a representative anion at the strongly chaotropic end of the Hofmeister series for comparison of ion-specific effects. Its residual level and food safety should be further evaluated before its potential use in food systems can be assessed. By contrast, the KCl-treated gels combined relatively high hardness with favorable water-holding capacity, indicating their potential as candidate matrices for dysphagia-oriented gel foods. However, this potential requires further validation using standardized tests established by the International Dysphagia Diet Standardisation Initiative (IDDSI), together with assessments of salt residues, sensory quality, and digestive properties.
