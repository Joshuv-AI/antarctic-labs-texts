Anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody-negative granulomatosis with polyangiitis manifesting through pulmonary nodules and a genital lesion
Authors: Yuriko Mikami-Saitoh, Hiroyuki Kyoyama, Yusuke Hirata, Satoshi Kikuchi, Kosuke Sakai, Shintaro Mikami, Gaku Moriyama, Nobuyuki Koyama, Morihiro Higashi, Akihiro Ishizu, Kazutsugu Uematsu
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 72-year-old female presented with bilateral pulmonary nodules before undergoing surgery for hysteroptosis. Transbronchial biopsy did not lead to a definitive diagnosis. The right mass in the upper lobe was resected through video-assisted thoracic surgery. Pathological findings showed granulomatosis with polyangiitis. However, the patient was negative for serum proteinase 3-anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody. Although the nodule in the left lower lobe progressed, the serum inflammatory reaction yielded negative results. Resection of the nodule in the left lower lobe revealed identical pathological findings with those of the right pulmonary mass. Following total hysterectomy for hysteroptosis, the pathological findings indicated granulomatosis with polyangiitis.Keywords: Granulomatosis with polyangiitis, Anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody-negative, Female genital granulomatosis

Introduction

Granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA) is necrotizing granulomatous inflammation typically involving the upper and lower respiratory tract, as well as the kidneys. The condition is accompanied by necrotizing vasculitis predominantly affecting small- and medium-sized vessels [1]. Therefore, the diagnostic criteria include symptoms related to the upper respiratory tract, lungs, kidneys, or vasculitis [2]. Serum anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCAs) directed against proteinase 3-ANCA (PR3-ANCA) are often helpful for the diagnosis of GPA [3]. In this article, we report the case of a patient who presented with bilateral pulmonary nodules and hysteroptosis. The patient did not develop symptoms included in the diagnostic criteria, and was negative for the presence of serum PR3-and myeloperoxidase (MPO)-ANCA. Following the resection of bilateral nodules through video-assisted thoracic surgery (VATS) and total hysterectomy for hysteroptosis, she was diagnosed with GPA. Thus far, few cases of GPA have been diagnosed based on the presence of bilateral pulmonary nodules and a female genital lesion in patients without typical symptoms.

Case report

A 72-year-old female was referred to our hospital due to the presence of bilateral pulmonary masses. The masses were detected using chest radiography (Fig. 1A), which was performed prior to surgical treatment for hysteroptosis. The patient was an ex-smoker (0.5 pack daily from 20 to 50 years of age) and had worked at a factory producing automobile parts. She had suffered from hyperthyroidism in her 30s, hysteroptosis at the age of 66 years, and a parotid gland tumor at the age of 67 years. On examination, the body temperature of the patient was 36.5 °C, blood pressure was 114/68 mmHg, and percutaneous oxygen saturation on room air was 98 % with a pulse of 107/min. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography revealed a mass (diameter: 40 mm) with a cavity at the right lung apex (Fig. 1B) and a nodule (diameter: 20 mm) with cavity in the left S8 (Fig. 1C). The levels of C-reactive protein were not elevated (0.1 mg/dL), and the white blood cell count was 7300/μL. The concentrations of all examined serum tumor markers, including carcinoembryonic antigen, cytokeratin 19 fragment (CYFRA21-1), pro-gastrin-releasing peptide, and soluble interleukin-2 receptor (sIL-2R), were within the normal range (Table). Sputum smear and culture for bacteria or acid-fast bacteria, as well as an interferon-gamma (IFNγ) release assay yielded negative findings. Further examination through fluorodeoxyglucose-positron emission tomography-computed tomography showed hypermetabolic focuses in the right pulmonary mass, left pulmonary nodule, and uterus cervix. Transbronchial biopsy was performed on the mass in the right S1 thrice and the nodule in the left S8. The pathological findings showed granulomas and necrotic tissues with aggregation of inflammatory cells, including neutrophils and plasma cells, without any fungal organisms and acid-fast bacteria. However, a definitive diagnosis was not reached. Amoxicillin and clavulanate were administered for 1 month; however, the bilateral nodules progressed. Eight months after the first visit to our department, right upper pulmonary lobectomy was performed through VATS.

Pathological examination of the mass in the right S1 revealed necrotizing granulomas, which were partly characterized by palisading histiocytes (Fig. 2A and B). Furthermore, pulmonary vessels were involved in the granuloma. Although GPA was primarily considered, the possibility of necrotizing sarcoid granulomatosis, tuberculosis, or other granulomatous infections could not be ruled out. Ziehl–Neelsen and Grocott staining did not reveal the presence of any bacteria. Furthermore, polymerase chain reaction and culture did not detect tuberculosis or nontuberculous mycobacterium. Pseudomonas was cultured from the resected mass. The pathological findings suggested GPA; nevertheless, the patient was negative for PR3-and MPO-ANCA and had normal renal function. In addition, occult blood was not detected in the urine, and we did not observe any otolaryngologic abnormalities.

Pathological findings of the lung lesions. The pathological findings of the resected right-upper lung showed geographic and basophilic necrosis surrounded by palisading histiocytes (hematoxylin and eosin [H&E] staining: 100 × ) (A). Additionally, destruction of the elastic laminae of the pulmonary vessels involved in the granuloma was observed (Elastica Masson [EM] staining: 100 × ) (B). The pathological findings of the resected left-lower lung resembled those of the right-upper lung (H&E staining: 100 × ) (C). Moreover, a microabscess was present in the pulmonary blood vessel wall adjacent to the necrotic lesion (EM staining: 100 × ) (D). Scale bar: 100 μm.

Four months after the right upper lobectomy, the pulmonary nodule in the left lower lobe had progressed. Since the possibility of lung cancer could not be denied at that time, segmentectomy of the left S8 and S9 was performed by VATS. The pathological findings were similar to those recorded for the right pulmonary mass (Fig. 2C and D). Total hysterectomy was performed 9 months after segmentectomy of the left lung. Magnetic resonance imaging before undergoing total hysterectomy showed a high signal on T1-weighted contrast-enhanced images at the utero-vaginal border region (Fig. 3). Moreover, a necrotizing granuloma similar to the lung mass was observed in the vaginal fornix (Fig. 4A and B). Based on these pathological findings, we diagnosed this patient with GPA involving pulmonary and female genital lesions.

At 3 years and 4 months after resection of bilateral pulmonary nodules, a new nodule was detected in the right upper lobe of the lung, which remained asymptomatic. During observation, we noted an increase in the size of the nodules, accompanied by cavity formation (Fig. 5). The pathological findings obtained through transbronchial biopsy of these lesions were not specific. Nevertheless, recurrence of GPA was suspected, leading to treatment with oral prednisolone (30 mg daily). The treatment resulted in the immediate disappearance of the nodules; consequently, the dose of prednisolone was tapered and maintained at 5 mg daily.

Chest computed tomography (CT) at the time of recurrence of pulmonary lesions. Follow-up CT revealed the presence of multiple cavitary nodules in the right residual lung (right), where lesions had not been detected (left) after the right upper pulmonary lobectomy.

Discussion

The present patient, who did not have respiratory symptoms or an inflammatory response, presented with bilateral pulmonary nodules with cavity. The features of the pulmonary nodules with cavity in the right upper lobe and the left lower lobe were suggestive of malignancies and the presence of acid-fast bacteria, including tuberculosis, fungi, etc. After performing transbronchial biopsies of the bilateral nodules, which did not lead to a diagnosis, the right mass in the upper lobe was resected through VATS. The pathological findings primarily indicated GPA. Lung nodules are the most common findings associated with GPA on chest computed tomography, detected in approximately 40–70 % of patients. Moreover, cavitation in nodules occurs in approximately 25 % of nodules measuring >20 mm [4]. Therefore, the detection of bilateral pulmonary nodules in the present case is compatible with GPA. Thereafter, the size of the nodule in the left lower lobe was increased. The same diagnosis was suspected for both the left nodule and the right mass; hence, the treatment for GPA, including cyclophosphamide and prednisolone, was considered. Nevertheless, analyses for PR3-ANCA or other inflammatory reactions yielded negative results. Since malignancies or other infectious diseases could not be ruled out, the nodule in the left lower lobe was resected to complete the diagnosis. Pathological analysis revealed identical features to those observed for the right pulmonary mass. Following a total hysterectomy for hysteroptosis, the pathological findings also indicated GPA. New lung lesions appeared at 3 years and 4 months after the resection of bilateral pulmonary nodules; nevertheless, the patient remained asymptomatic. The nodules disappeared immediately after initiating treatment with prednisolone. The pathological findings could not rule out the possibility of necrotizing sarcoid granulomatosis, tuberculosis, or other granulomatous infections. However, such infections have not been detected thus far. In the present case, the patient was diagnosed with GPA based on the pathological findings of surgical resection of bilateral pulmonary nodules through VATS and a total hysterectomy for hysteroptosis. If a single biopsy, despite obtaining a sufficient amount, cannot confirm a diagnosis of vasculitis with granulomatous inflammation caused by a systemic disease, additional biopsy may be required to adequately obtain specimens from remote lesions for confirmation of the diagnosis.

During the clinical course, the patient did not develop any respiratory symptoms or signs of inflammation, and remained negative for serum ANCA. GPA is typically characterized by systemic symptoms (e.g., fever, weight loss, muscle pain, or arthritic joint pain), accompanied by local symptoms, such as upper airway, respiratory, renal, and other vasculitis symptoms (e.g., purpura, ecchymosis, polyneuritis, and headache). However, the present patient exhibited atypical symptoms of GPA, indicating low levels of inflammation. According to the 2022 American College of Rheumatology/European League Against Rheumatism criteria for the classification of GPA, positivity for PR3-ANCA is important for the diagnosis of GPA. Based on those criteria, this case is not classifiable as GPA [5]. Patients with GPA often show positivity for PR3-or MPO-ANCA. However, patients with GPA may occasionally be negative for ANCA or have a low titer. A previous study examined the association between demographic characteristics, disease manifestations, and clinical outcomes in ANCA-positive and ANCA-negative GPA. The results showed that 15 of 365 patients (4 %) had ANCA-negative GPA [6]. Additionally, the investigators reported that renal involvement was less frequent in ANCA-negative versus ANCA-positive GPA. A literature review showed that 21 of 133 patients (16 %) diagnosed with GPA, microscopic polyangiitis, or eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA) were negative for ANCA. Of those, 16 were diagnosed with EGPA, and only four were diagnosed with GPA [7]. Another report focused on differences in the sensitivity of the assay for the detection of ANCA. The investigators stated that indirect immunofluorescence may be able to detect antigens other than MPO and PR3 owing to its slightly higher sensitivity versus other methods. However, the enzyme-linked immuno-sorbent assay, which is commonly used for the detection of MPO- or PR3-ANCA, has higher specificity than indirect immunofluorescence [8]. Isolating Pseudomonas from surgical lung specimen implied the possibility of the presence of ANCA against bactericidal/permeability increasing protein (BPI-ANCA) [9]. BPI-ANCA has also been identified in various diseases, including cystic fibrosis and inflammatory bowel disease [10]. Moreover, BPI-ANCA positivity has been reported to be associated with inflammatory status in COPD patients [9]. Nevertheless, our patient had not exhibited symptoms of these conditions. Her smoking history (0.5 pack daily from 20 to 50 years of age) might suggest the presence of Pseudomonas colonization in the bronchial region. We think that involvement of BPI-ANCA was less likely due to her lack of inflammatory symptoms, although we did not assess BPI-ANCA.

A report exhibited a case of ANCA-negative and CRP-negative GPA localized to the lung [11]. The patient presented with a mass lesion in the apical portion of the right lung. Four years after the lobectomy of the right lung, another nodule appeared in the opposite lung, indicating a long-term progression. Miloslavsky EM et al. found that ANCA-negative patients with GPA had lower Birmingham Vasculitis Activity Score for Wegener's Granulomatosis at trial entry than PR3-ANCA-positive patients with GPA [6]. From these observations, the absence of ANCA positivity might be associated with low disease activity or chronic progression. Furthermore, the absence of elevated CRP might be attributed to low disease activity.

Several cases of female genital lesions in GPA have been previously reported [12], although these manifestations are rare [13]. Notably, in one case, lung and uterine involvement was detected, along with positivity for c-ANCA [14]. When different organs are involved and systemic GPA is suspected, it is unusual for patients to be negative for PR3-or MPO-ANCA in the absence of an elevated inflammatory response. Therefore, an aggressive biopsy through VATS is recommended for the definitive diagnosis of lung lesions.
