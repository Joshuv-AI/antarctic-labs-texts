Recent advances in artificial intelligence across interventional pulmonology: a narrative review
Authors: Shaheen Rizly, Anthony Saleh, Keerthana Keshava, Noah Reisman, Stephen J. Peterson, Janice M. Ayuda, Jeremy A. Weingarten
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectiveArtificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly applied across interventional pulmonology (IP), though the transition from technical innovation to actual clinical benefit remains a work in progress. This narrative review examines the current evidence of key AI subtypes including machine learning (ML), deep learning (DL), and radiomics, and their roles across the major domains of IP such as lung cancer screening, bronchoscopy, endobronchial ultrasound (EBUS), pleural disease, and therapeutic airway interventions.MethodsA structured search was conducted across PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science, Scopus, ScienceDirect, and Google Scholar for studies published between April 2006 and January 2026 using terms related to AI and IP. Preclinical, retrospective, prospective, and translational studies were eligible. Given the heterogeneity in study designs, AI methodologies, and outcome measures, findings were synthesized qualitatively.Key Content and FindingsIn lung cancer screening, AI applied to low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) and chest radiography (CXR) improves nodule detection and malignancy risk stratification. Radiomics extends these capabilities by characterizing lesion biology, predicting molecular features, guiding biopsy targeting, and improving mediastinal staging. Multimodal approaches using radio-pathomic signatures and liquid biopsy data have also been shown to improve prognostication and personalized decision-making. In bronchoscopy, AI has been studied in airway image interpretation, lesion classification, real-time navigation, and trainee assessment. EBUS-based AI models improve lymph node and peripheral lesion characterization, while AI-assisted rapid on-site evaluation (ROSE) approaches expert-level performance in specimen adequacy assessment, malignant cell detection, and cytologic subtyping. In pleural disease, AI tools combining imaging, ultrasound (US), cytology, and clinical data further improve the detection and classification of effusions. Therapeutically, AI-driven quantitative imaging supports patient selection and procedural planning in bronchoscopic lung volume reduction (BLVR), airway stenosis and stenting, and prediction of tracheostomy timing and decannulation readiness. Despite these advancements, most AI applications remain investigational, with evidence largely derived from retrospective, single-center studies and limited impact on meaningful clinical outcomes.ConclusionsAI holds genuine potential to enhance diagnosis and procedural guidance across IP. However, its clinical utility in routine practice still requires prospective multicenter validation, standardized datasets, workflow integration, cost-effectiveness assessment, explainability, and human-centered implementation. AI should currently be viewed as an adjunct to, rather than a replacement for, interventional pulmonologists.

Introduction

Interventional pulmonology (IP) has rapidly evolved over the past two decades from a nascent subspecialty into a recognized discipline offering a wide array of consultative and procedural services to patients with thoracic malignancy, anatomic airway disease, and pleural disease (1). Diagnostic techniques such as bronchoscopy, endobronchial ultrasound (EBUS), navigational and robotic bronchoscopy, and medical thoracoscopy have transformed the management of lung cancer as well as airway obstruction and pleural disease, often reducing the need for more invasive surgical approaches (2). IP also plays a therapeutic role in managing central airway obstruction (CAO), advanced emphysema, and recurrent pleural effusions through interventions such as tumor debulking, airway stenting, bronchoscopic lung volume reduction (BLVR), and pleural catheter placement (3,4). Despite these advances, the success of such procedures is dependent on the operator, with variability in diagnostic yield and interpretation of endoscopic and ultrasound (US) findings across clinicians and centers (5,6).

Artificial intelligence (AI) is a broad field of computer science encompassing computational systems designed to perform tasks that typically require human intelligence, including pattern recognition, reasoning, problem-solving, and decision-making (7). In medicine, AI refers to algorithms or systems that autonomously analyze complex data, from electronic health records (EHR) to imaging studies, to support or automate clinical tasks such as diagnosis, risk stratification, and treatment selection (7). Machine learning (ML) is a subdomain of AI focused on algorithms that learn from data without being explicitly programmed with task-specific rules, identifying patterns and relationships within large datasets to construct models that generalize to new, unseen data (8). The general idea of ML in medicine involves collecting historical input data (features) and output data (labels), training a model to map features to labels, and then applying the model to new cases for prediction or classification. Deep learning (DL) is a specialized subset of ML characterized by artificial neural networks with multiple layers, particularly convolutional neural networks (CNNs), which automatically extract hierarchical features from raw, high-dimensional data such as medical images (9). Unlike traditional ML, which often relies on hand-engineered features, DL models learn relevant features directly from data to excel in image classification, segmentation, and object detection. Radiomics is a field at the intersection of medical imaging and data science, focused on the high-throughput extraction of quantitative features, including shape, intensity, texture, and higher-order statistics, from standard-of-care medical images such as computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), or positron emission tomography (PET) scans (10). The combination of radiomics with clinical, genomic, and other ‘omics’ data is a major focus of current research, aiming to develop comprehensive models for diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment response prediction (10). The impact of AI has been most pronounced in thoracic imaging, where DL and CNN algorithms for low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) and chest radiography (CXR) have shown high accuracy in detecting and risk stratifying lung nodules with several systems now already integrated into clinical systems (11). These advances have improved diagnostic consistency and reduced interobserver variability, particularly in lung cancer screening and interstitial lung disease assessment (12). Given that IP heavily relies on the interpretation of multimodal imaging and requires rapid decision support, these developments naturally provide a foundation for extending into the realm of IP-related procedures, as such systems can process and fuse heterogeneous data sources at a scale and speed that surpasses human capabilities (13). Early applications of AI, especially in bronchoscopy, EBUS, and airway imaging, have already shown improvements in diagnostic accuracy and procedural efficiency with positive results in lesion detection and guidance during procedures (6). However, there is a significant gap between the technical capabilities of AI and its clinical value, as most AI applications in IP are still in early stages of development and tested mostly on retrospective, single-center studies with ongoing issues such as generalizability and data (6,14). Given the rapid expansion of AI and its increasing intersection with IP, a comprehensive synthesis of the current evidence is both timely and essential. This narrative review aims to summarize key applications of AI across the major domains of IP while recognizing some of the limitations and challenges. We present this article in accordance with the Narrative Review reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1-0204/rc).

Methods

A comprehensive literature search was conducted to identify relevant studies evaluating the role of AI in IP. Electronic databases including PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Web of Science, Scopus, ScienceDirect, and Google Scholar were systematically searched for articles published between April 2006 and January 2026. Search strategies incorporated combinations of keywords and controlled vocabulary terms related to AI and IP, including “artificial intelligence”, “machine learning”, “deep learning”, “radiomics”, “interventional pulmonology”, “lung cancer screening”, “lung nodules”, “bronchoscopy”, “endobronchial ultrasound”, “rapid on-site evaluation”, “pleural effusion”, “bronchoscopic lung volume reduction”, “airway obstruction”, “airway stenosis”, “airway stenting”, and “tracheostomy”. Reference lists of included articles were manually reviewed to identify additional relevant studies. Studies were considered eligible if they evaluated or described AI-based applications in diagnostic or therapeutic domains of IP. Eligible study designs included preclinical studies, simulation-based investigations, retrospective and prospective clinical studies, feasibility studies, and translational research. Review articles, meta-analyses, and consensus statements were also included to provide context and identify emerging trends. Studies were limited to those published in English. Given the evolving nature of AI research in this field, no restrictions were placed on sample size, study setting, or geographic region. Study selection was performed through independent screening of titles and abstracts by the authors, followed by full-text review for eligibility. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus. Data were synthesized qualitatively due to the heterogeneity in study design, methodologies, and outcome measures, precluding formal meta-analysis. The review focused on summarizing key technological approaches, clinical applications, and reported performance metrics of AI systems across IP, while also highlighting methodological limitations and gaps in the current evidence base. AI in lung cancer screening

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, largely due to delayed diagnosis and limitations in current screening paradigms (15). AI, particularly DL and radiomics, is increasingly being used in healthcare systems not only for improving detection but also for refining risk stratification and improving clinical decision-making.

AI in LDCT

LDCT has significantly reduced mortality in high-risk populations, but several challenges continue to persist including high false-positive rates and inter-reader variability leading to inefficiencies in diagnosis and management (16). Landmark DL models have shown improvements in lung cancer detection using LDCT. The foundational study by Ardila et al. developed a three-dimensional (3D) CNN that outperformed radiologists in reducing both false positives and false negatives when prior imaging was unavailable (17). For interventional pulmonologists, these improvements are not merely diagnostic but have the potential to reduce unnecessary bronchoscopic procedures for benign nodules and prioritize earlier referral of high-risk nodules. Subsequent models have further added to this approach. The Sybil model predicts future lung cancer risk from a single LDCT scan without requiring clinical input, achieving the 1-year areas under the curve (AUCs) of 0.86–0.94 across multiple cohorts (18). However, external testing of Sybil in an Asian health checkup cohort revealed population-dependent limitations in generalizability, with a 6-year AUC for future cancers in never/light smokers of only 0.56 (19). In contrast, the lung cancer prediction convolutional neural network (LCP-CNN) showed strong performance, achieving an AUC of 0.93 in external validation and outperforming the traditional statistical PanCan model while performing comparably to thoracic radiologists (20). In a larger external analysis across three European screening trials, AUC reached up to 0.98 for cancers diagnosed within 1 year. Notably, in clinically relevant indeterminate nodules (5–15 mm), LCP-CNN outperformed PanCan (AUC 0.95 vs. 0.91) and reduced false positives by 39.4% at 100% sensitivity (21).

Beyond detection, AI is also shaping screening strategies. Lee et al. showed that AI used as a pre-screener, whereby radiologists only interpret examinations with a positive AI result, can safely reduce radiologist workload while preserving sensitivity and improving specificity (22). AI may not only serve as diagnostic adjunct but also as a gatekeeper for subsequent interventional pathways, determining which patients ultimately undergo invasive evaluation, as one retrospective analysis of prospectively collected data from the European 4-IN-THE-LUNG-RUN screening trial showed that AI functioning as a first reader had significantly fewer negative misclassifications than radiologists (0.8% vs. 11.1%) and would have reduced missed clinical referrals from 11.8% to 2.9% (23). AI-based interpretation systems have also been shown to improve workflow efficiency by reducing lung lesion review time by over 50% (and up to 87% for nodules) and overall documentation time by 30–40%, with a significant reduction in administrative burden (24).

Retrospective studies have also shown that AI-assisted reading improves nodule detection sensitivity and interobserver agreement for both malignancy risk categories and management recommendations (25). It can also reclassify nodules to reach early diagnosis in 42% of malignant nodules from prior-year CT scans while reducing follow-up for benign nodules (26). In the UKLS trial, AI achieved the lowest 3-month negative misclassification rate (9.1%) compared to human readers (18.2–63.6%) and identified rapid growth in all histologically confirmed cancers at the earliest follow-up (27).

The evidence base is now transitioning from retrospective to prospective validation. The first prospective randomized controlled trial (RCT) by Hwang et al. (2026) provides pragmatic evidence that AI increases identification of nodules that may require intervention by showing that AI-assisted LDCT interpretation in applied clinical settings significantly increased detection of Lung-RADS-positive nodules and follow-up LDCT recommendations without increasing interpretation time (28). However, AI’s impact is modality and workflow-dependent, as shown in the LungIMPACT trial where AI-based prioritization in the lung cancer diagnostic pathway had no significant impact on time to CT, time to diagnosis, or stage at diagnosis (33). One model-based cost-effectiveness analysis revealed that AI-supported clinician risk stratification is cost-effective [incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) $4,485/life-year gained] in clinical settings with malignancy prevalence exceeding 5%, but becomes less cost-effective below this threshold (34). This highlights uncertainty, although AI-assisted CT interpretation increases sensitivity for nodule detection without major loss of specificity, the supporting evidence is mostly retrospective and heterogeneous with no convincing demonstration that these gains necessarily mean consistent cost-effectiveness in clinical practice or improved patient outcomes (35). No prospective studies have yet evaluated whether using AI changes bronchoscopy referral patterns, reduces unnecessary procedures, or improves diagnostic yield (6). The ongoing Benzaquen et al. RCT (NCT05704920), which evaluates whether AI-assisted multidisciplinary team analysis increases definitive nodule classification by 3 months, may provide the first direct evidence linking AI to procedural decision-making timelines (36).

AI in CXR

While LDCT remains the standard for lung cancer screening, there has been renewed interest in AI applied to CXR given its accessibility and lower cost. Across multiple meta-analyses, DL models for lung cancer detection on CXR have shown high diagnostic performance with pooled sensitivities approaching 93–95% and specificities around 87–94% (29,37). Individual models often report even higher performance in curated datasets; for example, DL algorithms have achieved AUCs of 0.92–0.99 and, in some studies, outperformed the majority of physicians in nodule detection tasks (38). However, these performances are not consistently reproducible in clinical settings, where external validation studies show marked heterogeneity (39). AI-assisted CXR interpretation has been shown to be a reader-support tool. AI improves sensitivity for nodule detection and reduces false positives, particularly among less experienced readers, while modestly improving workflow efficiency (43). Prospective data further support this role, with AI-assisted interpretation increasing detection of clinically actionable nodules without significantly increasing false referrals (42). However, these advances remain largely diagnostic and even in prospective randomized settings, improved accuracy has not reliably translated into downstream changes in clinical decision-making or patient outcomes (46).

Another studied AI application is risk stratification from CXR. DL-based fusion CNN models such as CXR-LC, which use minimal clinical inputs, achieve AUCs comparable to established risk models while identifying more incident lung cancers than traditional eligibility criteria, including in patients otherwise excluded from screening (44,47). Similarly, newer DL architectures such as CXR-RANet have shown strong long-term predictive performance, with AUCs approaching 0.90 at shorter prediction horizons (45). These findings suggest that AI-enhanced CXR may function as a ‘pre-screening’ or triage tool to identify patients for LDCT or interventional evaluation.

Needless to say, such improvements in diagnostic performance have not yet translated into meaningful clinical outcomes. The largest prospective randomized trial to date, LungIMPACT, found no significant differences in time to CT, diagnosis, treatment, or stage at diagnosis despite AI-based prioritization of CXRs, which highlights that improvements in image interpretation alone may not overcome system-level bottlenecks (33). Similarly, while retrospective studies suggest AI may allow for earlier incidental cancer detection and earlier specialty referral, there is still an absence of prospective evidence proving stage shift or mortality reduction (48). AI models trained on curated datasets are also susceptible to performance degradation in low-prevalence or heterogeneous populations, with some commercial systems demonstrating sensitivity as low as 35% in real-world screening environments (49).

Radiomics and AI for risk stratification

Radiomics extends beyond detection by extracting high-dimensional quantitative features from imaging data, capturing tumor characteristics such as heterogeneity, texture, and spatial complexity that are not discernible to the human eye (50). With ML, these features further improve risk prediction and prognostication of malignancies. Meta-analyses have shown strong diagnostic performance for radiomics-based models in differentiating benign from malignant nodules, with pooled AUCs of 0.91 across independent systematic reviews (51,52). A multicenter prospective study confirmed that a combined radiomics-clinical model achieved AUCs of 0.87–0.90, outperforming either modality alone (53). The application of AI-based radiomics with established clinical risk prediction models has been shown to potentially reduce unnecessary procedures and prioritize high-risk lesions. Kammer et al. showed that a combined biomarker model combining clinical variables, CT radiomics, and serum biomarkers [high-sensitivity cytokeratin 19 fragment antigen 21-1 (hsCYFRA 21-1)] significantly improved diagnostic accuracy over the Mayo Clinic model alone (AUC 0.80 vs. 0.72), correctly reclassified intermediate-risk indeterminate pulmonary nodules, and in clinical utility analysis reduced invasive procedures from 62.9% to 50.6% in the intermediate-risk benign population while shortening median time to cancer diagnosis from 60 to 21 days (54,55). Radiomics also allows for prognostic modeling as shown by a DL-based 3D CNN that had a significant 2-year overall survival in both radiotherapy (AUC 0.70) and surgery patients (AUC 0.71) that surpassed traditional clinical models with consistency against test-retest and inter-reader variations (56). Furthermore, AI-driven LDCT analysis also enables extraction of multiple biomarkers from a single scan, including automated coronary artery calcium quantification and emphysema assessment, thereby allowing simultaneous evaluation of lung cancer risk, cardiovascular disease, and other comorbidities, which maximizes the clinical value of screening (57-59).

The addition of radiomic features derived from CT and PET imaging to bronchoscopy planning can also better characterize lesion biology and guide biopsy targeting. This has direct implications, informing whether a lesion should undergo bronchoscopic biopsy, surgical resection, or surveillance. PET/CT-based radiomics models have predicted tumor invasiveness and high-risk histopathological features, with AUCs of 0.93 for distinguishing invasive adenocarcinoma from preinvasive lesions [adenocarcinoma in situ (AIS)/minimally invasive adenocarcinoma (MIA)] and AUCs of 0.82–0.85 for predicting high-risk features including grade III histology, lymphovascular invasion, pleural invasion, and spread through air spaces (STAS), with significant stratification of progression-free and overall survival (60). Radiomics can also capture intratumoral heterogeneity, identifying biologically aggressive subregions within lesions. One prospective study provided the most direct evidence that radiomics can guide biopsy site selection to capture tumor heterogeneity, as it demonstrated that CT-texture-guided targeted biopsies captured distinct mutational profiles, with over 10% of mutations exclusive to one biopsy site in the majority of patients, and entropy-rich clusters associated with STK11 mutations (61). Another study developed a biopsy-slot region of interest (ROI)-based clinical-radiomics model that predicted tumor-rich targets with AUCs of 0.942 (training) and 0.926 (validation), directly improving biopsy target selection and potentially reducing nondiagnostic procedures (62). Marinelli et al. (2026) further showed that using radiomics with procedural standardization improved molecular adequacy for next-generation sequencing in CT-guided lung biopsy (AUC 0.88), with particular benefit in subsolid lesions (63). These approaches are relevant for advanced bronchoscopic techniques such as robotic-assisted bronchoscopy, where precise targeting of high-risk regions may improve diagnostic yield. One prospective dual-institution study showed that a composite radiomics-clinical model predicted aggressive micropapillary/solid histologic patterns with an AUC of 0.91 in external validation, with implications for identifying high-risk subregions requiring targeted sampling (64). Another study demonstrated that habitat imaging capturing intratumoral heterogeneity achieved an AUC of 0.750, and a triple-feature model using intratumoral, peritumoral, and clinical features achieved an AUC of 0.932 (65).

Radiomics also has direct relevance to mediastinal staging and EBUS-guided transbronchial needle aspiration (EBUS-TBNA) target selection as one meta-analysis showed that CT-based radiomics achieved pooled sensitivity of 0.84, specificity of 0.82, and AUC of 0.90 for predicting lymph node metastasis in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) (66). For occult mediastinal lymph node metastasis (clinically negative nodes harboring microscopic disease), one study showed that a radiomics signature from the primary tumor predicted occult mediastinal lymph node metastasis with 91.1% accuracy and AUC of 0.972 (67), and a more recent meta-analysis confirmed pooled sensitivity of 0.85 and specificity of 0.78 (AUC 0.89) in internal validation, though external validation performance was more moderate (AUC 0.80) (68). Another DL model applied directly to EBUS images, when integrated with ROI information, CT lymph node size, and PET/CT findings, achieved an AUC of 0.914 for predicting lymph node metastasis, thus significantly outperforming models using EBUS images alone (69). These tools could inform which lymph node stations to prioritize during EBUS-TBNA, potentially reducing the number of needle passes needed and improving staging accuracy.

Additionally, radiogenomic approaches allow non-invasive prediction of molecular alterations, including epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK), and programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1) status. A comprehensive meta-analysis across 124 studies had pooled sensitivities of 0.75 for EGFR and ALK prediction using radiomics-based models, with combined clinical-radiomics models improving EGFR sensitivity to 0.81 (70). However, pooled estimates from meta-analyses suggest more modest performance than many individual studies report, and the field continues to face major reproducibility challenges due to non-standardized imaging protocols, feature extraction pipelines, and limited multicenter validation (70,71). These approaches may guide bronchoscopic sampling strategies and prioritize molecular testing on limited specimens, supporting the emergence of ‘virtual biopsy’ concepts when tissue acquisition is challenging. Radiogenomics is best viewed as a complement to biopsy rather than a replacement, with real-world impact dependent on harmonized protocols, leakage-free external validation, and integration with multi-omics decision support (71).

Multimodal radio-pathomics

The convergence of radiomics with pathomics (quantitative features extracted from digitized histopathology slides) is an emerging frontier in lung cancer diagnostics with direct implications for IP. Radio-pathomic models use complementary information from macroscopic imaging and microscopic tissue architecture by capturing tumor biology at multiple scales. One study developed a multimodal radiopathomics framework that combined CT radiomics with hematoxylin and eosin staining (H&E) whole-slide image pathomics, achieving C-indices of 0.71–0.74 for predicting disease-free survival in resectable lung adenocarcinoma, outperforming either modality alone and demonstrating predictive value for adjuvant chemotherapy benefit (72). Another study showed that using radiopathomic models significantly outperformed single-modality approaches across three clinical contexts: predicting recurrence in early-stage NSCLC, immunotherapy response in advanced NSCLC, and chemotherapy response in SCLC (73). Ji et al. (2025) demonstrated that a dual-pathway fusion of radiomics and pathomics achieved AUCs of 0.85–0.99 for EGFR mutation prediction and enabled effective risk stratification for survival (74). These findings suggest that when bronchoscopic specimens are obtained, combining pre-procedural radiomic signatures with post-procedural pathomic analysis may improve prognostication and treatment selection beyond what either modality provides independently.

The use of radiomics with liquid biopsy as a multimodal approach has also been shown, as one study developed an ML-based multi-omics model using clinical features, CT radiomics, and circulating cell-free DNA across patients with indeterminate pulmonary nodules, that significantly outperformed single-omics models and reduced unnecessary invasive procedures for benign nodules by 10.9–35% while avoiding delayed treatment for lung cancer by 3.1–38.8% (75). Another study showed that fusing CT DL, CT radiomics, and peripheral blood immune profiles achieved an AUC of 0.81 for lung cancer diagnosis in symptomatic patients, compared to 0.69–0.73 for single-modality signatures (76). These multimodal approaches could inform decisions on performing bronchoscopies by better stratifying which patients require invasive tissue sampling versus continued surveillance or liquid biopsy monitoring.

The concept of ‘digital biopsy’ combining radiomics, pathomics, genomics, and clinical data is also emerging as a framework for characterizing tumors (77). For IP, this multimodal approach has several implications: (I) pre-procedural radiomics can identify high-risk lesions and optimal biopsy targets; (II) AI-enhanced ROSE can provide immediate feedback on adequacy during bronchoscopy; (III) post-procedural radio-pathomics can improve prognostication and treatment selection; and (IV) radiogenomics may prioritize molecular testing when tissue is limited, as Figure 2 illustrates. To adopt such technologies will require data standardization across imaging and pathology and be conducted in prospective, multi-center studies (78,79).

AI in bronchoscopy

The use of AI in bronchoscopy is shifting the field from purely operator-dependent visual assessment towards computer-assisted diagnostic approaches that are more standardized. In particular, DL models such as CNNs and vision transformers have been applied to several bronchoscopic modalities to support lesion detection and reduce interobserver variability, which potentially could improve diagnostic yield. Recent studies have explored AI applications in bronchoscopic imaging, EBUS, cytologic rapid on-site evaluation (ROSE), and procedural navigation, often in combination with radiomic analysis of imaging data as shown in Figure 3. In this section, we review the emerging evidence across these domains and discuss the current methodological limitations, as well as areas requiring further investigation.

AI-assisted bronchoscopic image interpretation

White light bronchoscopy (WLB)

WLB is an established diagnostic bronchoscopic technique that uses the reflectance or scattering properties of white light from tissue to examine the macroscopic appearance of airways (80). DL models have been applied to analyze images from WLB to detect and classify malignancy. For example, a CNN-based diagnostic model trained on 2238 bronchoscopic images showed a strong diagnostic performance for distinguishing benign from malignant airway lesions, achieving an AUC of 0.940. The model performance was also comparable to that of experienced bronchoscopists and exceeded that of less-experienced clinicians, while completing analysis of 467 test images in approximately 30 seconds. In addition, the model demonstrated moderate capability in pathological subtyping, achieving an overall accuracy of 0.745 in differentiating squamous cell carcinoma from adenocarcinoma (81). A DL-based multiscale convolutional block attention module (MCBAM) has also been developed to interpret bronchoscopic images by identifying abnormal airway findings. The model was trained using 2,900 images from 615 patients and showed high diagnostic accuracy across several tasks, including distinguishing normal mucosa from abnormal lesions, differentiating benign from malignant lesions, and classifying images into normal, benign, or malignant categories. When compared with physicians, the system showed diagnostic performance similar to experienced bronchoscopists while completing image analysis faster (82).

Optical coherence tomography (OCT)

Endobronchial optical coherence tomography (EB-OCT) is an imaging modality capable of providing near-histologic resolution of airway wall structures. Recent studies have explored the use of AI to assist with automated morphometric analysis of these images. A DL-based segmentation approach applied to EB-OCT images from patients with asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and normal airways was able to demonstrate high accuracy for automated measurement of airway lumen and wall dimensions, with results closely matching manual measurements and identifying airway bifurcations (83). Advances in OCT hardware combined with AI-based image analysis have also allowed high-resolution visualization and 3D quantification of small airway structures. An ultrahigh-resolution diffractive OCT system paired with a DL segmentation system was able to produce automated 3D measurement of airway tissue compartments, including airway smooth muscle, in vivo in experimental models, with potential applications for studying small airway disease (84).

Inhalation injury assessment

Bronchoscopy remains the standard for evaluating burn inhalation injury, but grading with the abbreviated injury score is subjective and varies between clinicians. Recent studies suggest that AI may help standardize this process. In one study, a model trained on over 1,000 bronchoscopic images from burn patients was able to classify both the presence and severity of inhalation injury (none, mild, moderate, severe) with very high accuracy (approximately 98%), performing at or above the level of expert clinicians while providing rapid, consistent assessments (85). Another study evaluated a DL approach using bronchoscopic images from patients with inhalation injury and achieved an overall accuracy of approximately 86% for grading severity. The model identified key visual features such as soot deposition, mucosal injury, airway casts, etc., and showed potential clinical relevance by correlating with duration of mechanical ventilation, thus providing a more objective and clinically meaningful assessment compared with traditional grading systems (86). However, these studies are based on small, retrospective, single-center cohorts and larger multicenter studies are needed to confirm generalizability and clinical utility.

AI in EBUS

EBUS is a bronchoscopic technique that uses US imaging with bronchoscopy to visualize and biopsy mediastinal, hilar, and peripheral lesions. Unlike conventional bronchoscopy, which relies on direct visualization, EBUS provides live, high-resolution US images of surrounding tissues, allowing for more precise localization and characterization of lesions (87). Radial EBUS (rEBUS), which provides 360-degree circumferential images, is particularly effective for localizing and sampling peripheral pulmonary lesions (PPLs), with a pooled diagnostic yield of 70.6% and a low complication rate of 2.8% according to meta-analysis (88). Linear EBUS, which offers real-time forward-viewing US, is primarily used for mediastinal and hilar lymph node staging, with reported median sensitivity of 89% and median negative predictive value of 91% for mediastinal staging in lung cancer (89). EBUS-guided transbronchial needle aspiration (EBUS-TBNA) is recommended as the first-line minimally invasive diagnostic tool for mediastinal and hilar lymphadenopathy (89). Although EBUS is traditionally utilized without AI algorithms, newer AI applications have started to improve several aspects of EBUS, including lymph node and peripheral lesion characterization, ROSE, malignancy detection and multiclass classification and subtyping.

Lymph node characterization

The interpretation of sonographic features, such as lymph node size, shape, echogenicity, etc., during EBUS-TBNA remains subjective and operator-dependent. AI has been used to explore more objective and reproducible assessments of lymph node malignancy. A recent meta-analysis evaluated the performance of AI-assisted EBUS from 12 different studies involving over 6,000 lymph nodes using pathology as the reference standard. Across these studies, AI models analyzing B-mode US images demonstrated a pooled sensitivity of 75% and specificity of 88%, with an overall AUC of 0.90. Notably, retrospective studies showed higher sensitivity but lower specificity compared to prospective studies, pointing towards the impact of study design and potential bias in reported performance (90). Several individual studies illustrate how these models are being validated. For example, one study trained an AI algorithm on segmented EBUS images from 140 patients and then tested it on a separate validation cohort. The model analyzed lymph node structure directly from US images and achieved an accuracy of approximately 73%, with a specificity exceeding 90%, making it helpful in identifying benign nodes when tissue sampling is limited or inconclusive (91). Another study applied a CNN architecture to EBUS video-derived images from patients undergoing staging, extracting thousands of frames for analysis. The model evaluated subtle image patterns within lymph nodes and demonstrated high overall accuracy with strong specificity which helps guide selection of the most suspicious nodes for biopsy during procedures (92). A more recent study focused on larger datasets using an ensemble of DL models trained on over 2,000 lymph node images and tested on an independent dataset showed an overall accuracy of approximately 80% with very high specificity but lower sensitivity (93). This pattern suggests that while AI may be reliable in confirming malignancy, it may miss some malignant nodes thus reinforcing its role as an adjunct rather than a replacement for biopsy. Furthermore, ML models have also been able to identify complex imaging patterns not readily apparent to the human eye and have outperformed conventional sonographic classification systems based on predefined US features with reported accuracies exceeding 95% (94). Regardless, most studies are retrospective and involve single-center datasets with heterogeneous methodologies.

EBUS for peripheral lesions

Several studies have explored the use of AI algorithms in EBUS for predicting benign vs malignant tumors in peripheral lesions. One, for example, developed a computer-aided diagnosis (CADx) system using CNN-based DL model trained on EBUS with guide sheath (EBUS-GS) images of pulmonary lesions that was able to distinguish malignant from benign pulmonary lesions by identifying subtle imaging features and complex ultrasonographic patterns often difficult to discern through conventional interpretation such as texture, shape, grayscale patterns, etc. (95). Another CADx system using CNNs trained on annotated datasets of EBUS achieved diagnostic accuracies of 83–85% for distinguishing benign from malignant pulmonary lesions and outperforming traditional image analysis methods (96). Applying test-time augmentation (TTA), a technique that generates multiple transformed versions of an image during inference and aggregates predictions, as an adjunct to a CNN further improved the classification accuracy of DL models (97). Again, most of these AI models have been evaluated using retrospective datasets and have not undergone external validation or prospective randomized clinical trials; thus, their clinical utility remains investigational and requires further validation.

AI for ROSE

ROSE of cytology specimens is used in IP to provide immediate assessment of sample adequacy and preliminary diagnostic information during EBUS-TBNA and bronchoscopic sampling of PPLs (98). ROSE involves preparing and rapidly staining cytology smears, which are then evaluated by cytopathology personnel or trained pulmonologists at the bedside or via telepathology, allowing for real-time feedback to the proceduralist, enabling immediate decisions regarding the need for additional sampling, triage of material for ancillary studies and reduction in the number of needle passes or repeat procedures (99). Regardless, the impact of ROSE on diagnostic yield remains debated. While ROSE has been shown to reduce the number of needle passes and improve specimen adequacy in some studies, evidence regarding its ability to improve diagnostic accuracy or reduce repeat procedures remains inconsistent and is also limited by the availability of cytopathologists. AI-assisted cytology analysis may help address some of these limitations by standardizing the assessment of specimen adequacy and reducing reliance on on-site cytopathologists. AI has been tested on ROSE of cytology specimens primarily through DL models that analyze digital cytology images to assess specimen adequacy and provide preliminary diagnostic classification, including malignancy detection and lung cancer subtyping.

Multiple studies have shown that AI systems can achieve diagnostic accuracy comparable to that of experienced cytopathologists in distinguishing benign from malignant cells and in subtyping lung cancer during ROSE. For example, a DL-based ROSE system trained on over 6,000 cytological images achieved internal and external test accuracies of 92.97% and 90.26%, respectively, for identifying malignant cells, with almost perfect agreement with expert cytopathologists for common lung cancer subtypes (100). A separate multicenter study compared remote pathologist review (E-ROSE) and an AI-based system (AI-ROSE) to analyze biopsy sample images with final histology serving as the reference standard, and both showed very high diagnostic performance, although the AI system was somewhat less specific than remote expert review (101). Additional work suggests that AI enhances speed and consistency rather than replacing expert evaluation, as demonstrated by a ResNet-18 DL model applied to Diff-Quik-stained respiratory cytology images that achieved diagnostic performance comparable to cytopathologists with improved efficiency when combined with human interpretation (102).

Beyond binary classification, recent efforts have focused on developing AI systems capable of providing more detailed subclassification of lung cancer during ROSE. A hierarchical DL model integrating whole-slide ROSE images with serum biomarkers classified lesions in a stepwise manner: benign versus malignant, followed by major malignancy groups and NSCLC subtypes. In a dataset of 811 ROSE slides, the model showed strong performance for benign versus malignant classification, malignancy subtyping and NSCLC subtyping with similar diagnostic agreement to cytopathologists (103). Other studies have looked at combining classification with spatial localization of malignant cells to better replicate the workflow of cytopathologists. DL models applied to cytologic EBUS images showed interpretability and confidence in live assessment by achieving high accuracy across patch-based, image-based, and patient-level analyses, while simultaneously identifying malignant cell regions (104). Similarly, a multiscale UNet-based CNN model showed excellent performance in segmenting malignant cell clusters on ROSE-stained slides, allowing rapid evaluation of specimen adequacy and reducing the need for repeat sampling (105). Despite these advances, most model performance may vary across tumor subtypes, staining techniques, and image quality, and accuracy tends to decline in challenging cases such as low-cellularity or atypical samples.

AI in bronchoscopic navigation and procedural training

Accurate navigation through the bronchial tree is a foundational bronchoscopy skill, yet it remains challenging for trainees and can be difficult to teach consistently. Recent studies suggest that AI may provide real-time anatomical recognition and automated feedback during simulated bronchoscopy training.

One RCT showed that AI feedback may help novices perform not only faster but also more systematic and complete bronchoscopies in a simulated setting. An AI-based bronchial segment identification system was evaluated on 20 novice bronchoscopists training on a mannequin and showed that participants receiving AI feedback performed significantly better than those training with written instructions alone, with greater diagnostic completeness, more structured progression through the bronchial tree, and shorter procedure times (106). A subsequent randomized trial compared AI-guided bronchoscopy training with expert tutor guidance in 40 critical-care physicians. After 30 minutes of simulation-based training, the AI-guided group demonstrated significantly improved efficiency, including shorter mean intersegmental time, shorter overall procedure time, and fewer unnecessary revisits to previously examined segments. Although diagnostic completeness and structured progression were similar between groups, the AI system improved procedural efficiency without increasing cognitive workload. This is particularly relevant in critical care, where shorter and more efficient bronchoscopy may be clinically important in mechanically ventilated patients who are vulnerable to hypoxemia and physiologic instability during prolonged procedures (107).

In addition to training systems, AI models have also been developed for direct anatomical interpretation of bronchoscopic images. In one study, an EfficientNetB1 DL-based model was trained on 8,688 bronchoscopic images to distinguish the carina and main bronchi under conditions of image rotation and partial obscuration, reflecting actual procedural challenges, and outperformed most human experts with comparable results to the most experienced pulmonologist (108). This study reveals a practical use case for AI as a decision-support tool during video bronchoscopy, particularly for operators such as anesthesiologists who may have less frequent exposure to detailed bronchial anatomy than pulmonologists.

More advanced navigational systems are also beginning to emerge. An AI ‘co-pilot’ bronchoscope robot was recently developed using a shared-control algorithm trained on expert bronchoscopic videos, designed to help novice operators steer a bronchoscope into distal airways beyond the fifth generation while maintaining safer luminal centering and fewer steering errors. In both in vitro and in vivo testing, novice users were able to navigate as safely and effectively as experienced operators, which may eventually help reduce the learning curve and improve access to bronchoscopy in settings with limited specialist expertise (109).

A recent systematic review identified 20 studies on AI in bronchoscopy, categorized into three domains: airway anatomy and navigation (9 studies), computer-aided detection and classification of nodules on EBUS (7 studies), and ROSE of cytology specimens (4 studies). Among 16 assessment studies, AI performed comparably to human raters in 12 and superiorly in 4. Notably, only 4 studies evaluated real-world implementation, all within airway navigation or training and all favored AI guidance over no AI, though exclusively in simulated settings (110). However, there was also limited clinical applicability given the dynamic nature of airway navigation, as most studies assessed AI using static image classification rather than real-time bronchoscopy.

Regardless, the current evidence base for AI in bronchoscopic navigation and training remains limited. Most studies have been performed in simulated settings and clinical validation of such data is still lacking. For example, when tested in a clinical setting, an AI-based bronchoscopic navigation training intervention did not demonstrate significant improvement in performance, with no meaningful differences in diagnostic completeness, structured progression or procedure time (111). Excessive reliance on AI guidance could theoretically impair independent skill development, a concern analogous to overdependence on GPS for navigation. For now, these systems appear promising as adjunctive training and decision-support tools rather than replacements for expert supervision.

AI in pleural diseases

Diagnosing the etiology of pleural effusions remains challenging, as traditional methods often yield inconclusive results, as historically, thoracenteses are non-diagnostic in 21–26% of effusions and often limited by sensitivity and specificity (112). Consequently, patients frequently undergo additional procedures like medical thoracoscopy and video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery. These methods not only introduce risks associated with radiation exposure and invasiveness but also contribute to delays in diagnosis depending on procedural availability (113). AI in pleural imaging and detection

CXR and CT-based AI models

Commercially available DL tools for CXR interpretation have shown strong performance for both pleural effusion and pneumothorax detection. One multicenter study across four commercial AI platforms showed how AI could be used in triage by achieving AUCs of 0.94–0.97 for pleural effusion diagnosis and 0.89–0.97 for pneumothorax diagnosis with negative predictive values reaching up to 100%, though accuracy fell for smaller findings and complex films and false-positive rates remained higher than routine radiology reports, suggesting these tools only as adjuncts to expert interpretation (114). In another multi-center retrospective study, a U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-cleared AI model achieved AUCs of 0.979 for pneumothorax and 0.987 for tension pneumothorax, with sensitivities above 94% and specificities above 92%, performing consistently across patient subgroups (115). The model’s ability to flag tension pneumothorax is particularly relevant in IP, where recognizing early post-procedural or spontaneous pneumothorax can directly influence management. These findings are supported by broader pooled data as one meta-analysis reported a summary AUC of 0.97 for DL-based pneumothorax detection, with pooled sensitivity and specificity of 84% and 96% that were broadly comparable to physician-level performance (116). That said, over half of included studies carried a high risk of bias and reliance on open or retrospective datasets limits confidence in generalizability. These models may also mislead by confounding features, such as chest tubes, skin folds, pleural thickening, etc., which can mimic or mask pneumothorax and reduce diagnostic accuracy.

On CT, a radiomics-based study reported accuracies approaching 99% for pneumothorax detection using gradient boosting methods, suggesting that these models could capture subtle imaging features and help prioritize urgent cases (117). However, the study was retrospective and single-center with no external validation, and as with radiomics research more broadly, reproducibility across different institutions and scanner types remains an open question. Moreover, AI applications in pleural effusion characterization are less mature but clinically interesting. AI-driven CT scoring models have achieved AUCs up to 0.83 for distinguishing complicated from uncomplicated parapneumonic effusions, which could prove to be helpful in imaging-based risk stratification for pleural infection (118). While still preliminary, this aligns with a broader push toward using quantitative imaging to identify patients who may benefit from earlier drainage, intrapleural therapy, or surgical referral.

AI-augmented pleural US

Lung US interpretation is highly operator-dependent, which creates variability across clinicians and limits its use in settings without experienced sonographers. DL models using CNNs have been developed to automatically detect and classify pleural effusions in real time, drawing on features such as anechoic fluid collections, pleural line irregularities, associated consolidation or atelectasis, and lung sliding characteristics (119). In trauma settings, one flexible DL model (Pleff-Net) achieved overall sensitivity of 0.90, rising to 0.97 for large effusions, with retained accuracy even for trace effusions (120). Another multi-label DL model, trained on over 7,500 LUS images, achieved AUCs up to 0.94 for pleural effusion detection and significantly improved reader accuracy (from 87.5% to 95.6%) and inter-reader agreement, although performance declined in external validation datasets, reflecting the known sensitivity of US-based AI models to variations in acquisition technique, operator experience, and device heterogeneity (121). Beyond detection, one segmentation-based DL model for automated pleural effusion that outlines effusion boundaries reported performance exceeding inter-expert agreement with a tendency to avoid uncertain or high-risk regions. This observation may have implications for safer thoracentesis planning; however, the findings included mostly simple effusions, which may affect generalizability to more complex pleural disease (122). Supporting these trends, a recent meta-analysis reported pooled sensitivity of 0.92, specificity of 0.96, and AUC of 0.98 for pleural fluid detection in AI-based lung US models, although with limited generalizability due to significant heterogeneity across studies (123).

AI may also be most impactful in pleural US by improving image acquisition, as one study showed that AI-guided systems enabled non-experts to obtain diagnostic-quality scans in 98.3% of cases, with performance comparable to experienced sonographers through real-time feedback and automated quality assessment, albeit with longer acquisition times (124). This has also been shown in resource-limited intensive care unit (ICU) settings, as one study demonstrated that AI assistance improved non-expert diagnostic accuracy from around 68% to >93% while reducing interpretation time and increasing confidence (125). Furthermore, US features such as septation and loculation are beneficial for procedural planning, with one study reporting specificities approaching 98–99% for complicated effusions (126). More broadly, AI-driven quantitative US analysis may extend beyond detection to include automated pleural fluid volume estimation and characterization, as advanced imaging techniques including pixel-density analysis and integration with multimodal data may allow non-invasive differentiation of exudative versus transudative effusions and reduce reliance on invasive procedures (127). However, these approaches remain largely investigational with ongoing challenges regarding data standardization and model transparency.

AI-driven diagnostic and prognostic modeling

Effusion classification and malignancy prediction

Distinguishing malignant pleural effusion (MPE) from benign etiologies and, in particular, from tuberculous pleural effusion (TPE), is clinically relevant in pleural disease. Conventional approaches rely on cytology, lab values and invasive sampling etc all of which have well-recognized limitations in sensitivity and turnaround time. AI has emerged not as a replacement, but as an extension of clinical reasoning by recognizing patterns across imaging and clinical data that are difficult to synthesize at the bedside.

On the imaging side, AI can detect subtle morphologic signatures, such as pleural nodularity, irregular thickening, loculations, etc., as one DL model applied to thoracic CT was able to develop a two-stage architecture combining segmentation and classification, achieving an AUC of 0.84 in external validation with high sensitivity (89.4%) but only moderate specificity (65.1%) (128). This imbalance is worth emphasizing as such models are well-suited for screening malignancy, but less reliable as standalone rule-in tools. Ozcelik et al. reported a more balanced profile (sensitivity 87.5%, specificity 92.9%), although on a smaller dataset, suggesting that performance may vary depending on the characteristics of the dataset and model training (129). Radiomics-based models take this a step further by adding imaging features to clinical variables, as shown by one combined radiomic-clinical model that outperformed either approach alone (130). The strength of this approach lies in its alignment with clinical decision-making in reality. Clinicians rarely rely on imaging or labs in isolation and hybrid models that incorporate both appear more accurate. However, the incremental gain over simpler models must be weighed against its increased complexity and reduced interpretability.

Beyond binary classification, ML models have also shown strong performance in multi-class etiological differentiation. One study developed a gradient boosting model using over 2,200 patients and 18 selected features, achieving AUCs exceeding 0.91 across validation cohorts (131). Importantly, the most influential variables, pleural lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), protein, and adenosine deaminase (ADA), mirror the existing traditional diagnostic criteria, suggesting that these models are not discovering entirely new biology, but rather refining and weighting known relationships in a more systematic way. Similarly, Chen et al. demonstrated that even simplified models using only age, ADA, and LDH, outperformed conventional threshold-based approaches (132). This is particularly relevant in resource-limited settings, where minimal-variable models may offer the greatest clinical impact.

The distinction between TPE and MPE deserves separate attention, as it represents a dilemma with overlapping clinical and biochemical features. In a smaller but practical study, four ML algorithms using 17 clinical and laboratory variables showed that even relatively simple models can outperform traditional heuristics in ambiguous cases, although on a modest scale (133). In contrast, a multicenter prospective cohort study provided a more comprehensive example, by not just demonstrating excellent internal and external validation with balanced sensitivities and specificities, but also interpretability. By incorporating SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), the model allows clinicians to visualize how individual variables contribute to each prediction, thereby addressing the ‘black-box’ problem, one of the major barriers in AI use (134). Other models targeting TPE using routine clinical and pleural fluid parameters have shown high accuracy. Studies by Li et al. and Ren et al. reported AUCs exceeding 0.95 and sensitivities/specificities above 90% using ML-based support vector machine and random forest approaches, respectively, often outperforming ADA alone (135,136). While single biomarkers such as ADA remain useful, they are inherently limited by fixed thresholds, whereas ML models can capture nonlinear interactions between variables that better reflect the underlying disease processes.

Expanding beyond traditional biomarkers, newer models are beginning to incorporate broader clinical and biochemical data, as one model developed a nomogram that combines symptoms, imaging findings, tumor markers, and even serum metal ion concentrations, achieving an AUC of 0.94 for MPE identification (137). However, such models raise practical questions regarding cost, accessibility and reproducibility. Moreover, while high AUC values are frequently reported, clinicians should remain cautious in interpreting these metrics into bedside decisions, particularly when specificity is suboptimal or when models lack interpretability.

AI in pleural fluid cytology and biomarkers

Cytological analysis of pleural fluid is central to diagnosing MPE, but its limitations are well recognized due to variable sensitivity and frequent ambiguity in diagnosis between reactive mesothelial cells and malignancy. Recent advances in DL are beginning to provide more scalable and information-rich interpretation of cytology slides. For example, large-scale DL models trained on whole-slide images show strong performance in distinguishing benign from malignant effusions, with AUCs exceeding 0.90 and approaching expert-level accuracy (138,139). Beyond classification, some models have extended into more meaningful territory, predicting the primary site of metastatic disease and even inferring genetic alterations such as ALK and KRAS mutations directly from cytology morphology, although with variable accuracy across targets (138). While this remains exploratory, it shows that morphological patterns may encode underlying genomic information that could offer a more rapid, low-cost adjunct in settings with limited access to tissue or molecular testing.

Equally important is the role of AI in reducing interobserver variability and its potential as a standardization tool in diverse practice settings, as one study showed how weakly supervised models, trained without detailed pixel-level annotations, were able to achieve diagnostic accuracy comparable to senior cytopathologists while outperforming less experienced readers (139). Notably, the same cytologic patterns that challenge AI, such as mesothelial hyperplasia or low-cellularity specimens, also remain difficult for human interpretation, which shows how these systems are learning clinically relevant features rather than artificial signals. There have also been efforts to improve interpretability, with attention-based and multi-scale models highlighting the regions that drive predictions in ways that align closely with ground truth, offering needed transparency for clinical use, an especially important step in visually driven fields like cytopathology (140).

Beyond diagnosis, AI-driven models are beginning to inform prognosis and enable more individualized risk stratification. ML-based nomograms combining clinical variables, such as treatment type, effusion burden, coexisting pericardial effusion, etc., have shown high predictive accuracy (AUCs up to 0.96) for survival outcomes in patients with MPE (141). These models often derive their strength from utilizing familiar clinical features rather than relying solely on novel biomarkers. Despite these aforementioned advances, performance may decline in low-cellularity samples, which are the very cases where diagnostic assistance is most needed.

Radiomics and integrative AI in pleural malignancies

Radiomics and DL are increasingly redefining malignant pleural mesothelioma (MPM) by extracting quantitative features from imaging and histopathology that extend beyond human interpretation. This is particularly valuable in MPM, where there are limitations in conventional assessments given the diffuse growth patterns and heterogeneity. In digital pathology, a DL-based CNN (MesoNet) showed how AI could not only predict but also uncover clinically relevant tumor biology, as it trained on whole-slide images and outperformed traditional histologic evaluation in predicting overall survival and identified stromal features such as inflammation and cellular diversity as key prognostic markers (142). On CT imaging, automated volumetric analysis addresses a major limitation of modified Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (mRECIST; a conventional imaging-based response criterion assessing viable tumor by enhancement) in MPM. A fully automated DL model for volumetric MPM segmentation achieved strong correlation with expert manual segmentation (r=0.851) and demonstrated that higher baseline tumor volume predicts worse survival, though clinically meaningful volume thresholds remain undefined and agreement with mRECIST was only modest (143). AI has also been explored for differentiating MPM from other pleural diseases. A 3D-CNN using fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-PET/CT improved discrimination between MPM and benign pleural disease when combined with clinical variables such as age, sex, and SUVmax, reaching an AUC of 0.896 (144). Similarly, a ResNet-3D-18-based CT model trained on pretreatment CT scans distinguished MPM from metastatic pleural disease with an AUC of 0.840 in an independent test cohort and also generated imaging features that predicted overall survival (145). These dual diagnostic-prognostic capabilities are appealing, though current data remain largely retrospective with only small-sized cohorts. Radiomics is also being explored for treatment stratification. A transfer learning model using baseline CT predicted early therapeutic response with an AUC of 86.7% and accuracy of 87.5%, suggesting that routine imaging may identify non-responders early (146). Meanwhile, integrative ‘fusion’ models combining radiomics and DL have achieved even higher performance, with AUCs up to 0.978 and sensitivity up to 97.2% for detecting occult pleural dissemination, potentially helping avoid futile surgery (147). Several limitations exist in these studies, including variability in datasets, small sample sizes, and risk of overfitting, as one study pointed out varying accuracies across AI models that range widely from roughly 78% to nearly 100% (148).

AI in therapeutic interventional procedures

BLVR

AI-enabled quantitative imaging is most developed in BLVR, where success depends less on the bronchoscopy itself and more on choosing the right patient and target lobe. Contemporary guidance recommends quantitative CT assessment of emphysema distribution, lobar volumes, and fissure integrity, with potential targets generally requiring lobar emphysematous destruction and intact fissures to minimize collateral ventilation (149). Commercial platforms such as StratX and Imbio work through automated or semi-automated lobe segmentation, emphysema quantification, and fissure analysis, determining candidacy and avoiding anatomically unsuitable targets (149,150). In the Mayo Clinic BLVR pathway, more than one-third of referred patients were excluded because quantitative CT findings were incompatible with valve therapy and patients who achieved full or partial target-lobe collapse after endobronchial valve (EBV) placement had significant spirometric improvement (151). Similarly, 3D CT automated analysis has been used to quantify low-density emphysema burden and post-procedural lobar volume change, with a small cohort showing a median forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1) increase of 34% and 16.6% reduction in hyperinflation after BLVR (152). A particularly relevant AI application is automated fissure integrity scoring. A DL-derived fissure integrity score predicted successful target-lobe volume reduction after EBV treatment with an AUC of 0.83 and using an 89.5% fissure integrity threshold, the test set achieved 81.6% accuracy, 90.9% specificity and a high negative predictive value of 95.5% (153). This is clinically useful because a low fissure score may help exclude poor candidates noninvasively, but the modest positive predictive value of 62.5% also reminds us that fissure integrity alone is not enough. Valve response is still influenced by other factors, including small residual fissure defects, fissure location, collateral ventilation physiology, emphysema severity, and procedural issues such as valve malposition or incomplete occlusion (153). Functional imaging could further improve the selection process for BLVR by moving beyond anatomy alone. In a pilot single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT)/CT study using AutoLung 3D software, EBV therapy produced marked reductions in treated-lobe ventilation, perfusion, and volume, with compensatory increases in adjacent lobes (154). However, these regional physiologic changes correlated only weakly with FEV1 and 6-minute walk distance, which reveals an important limitation: better lobar mechanics do not always translate into functional improvement, especially in advanced COPD where deconditioning, vascular disease, and residual hyperinflation may dominate over symptom (154).

Airway stenosis and CAO

CAO comprises a group of malignant and benign conditions that narrow the tracheobronchial tree leading to dyspnea, respiratory failure, and significant morbidity, with lung cancer and post-intubation or inflammatory stenoses among the most common etiologies (155). Current guidelines recommend evaluation with CT imaging and bronchoscopy, with therapeutic interventions such as stenting reserved for refractory cases, though these carry notable complication rates including migration and granulation tissue formation (156). While CT provides excellent anatomical detail, it is limited in assessing dynamic and mucosal disease, and agreement with bronchoscopy is imperfect. Moreover, bronchoscopic grading of stenosis is highly subjective, with poor interobserver reliability (157). AI could address such limitations by automating segmentation and providing objective decision support across the continuum of airway disease management (158). For example, DL-based airway segmentation models have consistently shown high accuracy in enabling reliable and reproducible quantification of luminal narrowing and airway wall characteristics (159,160). Importantly, AI-based quantification markedly improves consistency compared to human interpretation, with significantly higher interobserver agreement than expert grading alone (157). Furthermore, AI-assisted bronchoscopy models have also achieved diagnostic accuracies comparable to experienced bronchoscopists and automated systems by providing precise, objective stenosis measurements (108,161). AI has also shown value in detecting early or subclinical airway changes, nearly doubling detection rates in post-treatment settings compared to visual assessment, suggesting a role in earlier intervention and improved longitudinal monitoring (162). Beyond diagnosis, AI is also having an impact on procedural planning and intervention. In one study, 3D reconstruction-guided planning reduced operative time (40 vs. 48 minutes) and stent migration rates (5% vs. 17%) in malignant CAO (163). Computational fluid dynamics models also provide functional insight into airflow limitation by highlighting that critical thresholds of luminal narrowing (roughly 70%) correspond to marked physiologic compromise and may better correlate with symptoms than static measurements alone (164,165). AI-driven softwares have also developed patient-specific stent design using CT-based modeling and 3D printing, with early studies showing high anatomical congruence and symptomatic improvement, although complications such as mucus plugging and granulation tissue remain comparable to conventional stents (166,167). Despite these advances, most AI applications in airway intervention remain in early clinical stages with limited prospective validation and data on long-term outcomes. While FDA-cleared platforms for quantitative airway analysis are available, their impact on decision-making and patient outcomes is not yet well established (168).

Tracheostomy and tracheal interventions

Tracheostomy and tracheal interventions are essential in airway management of critically ill patients, yet they remain very operator-dependent and are associated with limitations such as procedural risks and variability in timing, etc. Traditional approaches rely on clinical judgment, US, and CT imaging for anatomical assessment and planning, but these methods are limited by subjectivity and inconsistent predictive accuracy. In this context, AI has been studied to improve procedural planning, risk stratification, and longitudinal airway management. Early use of AI has been most evident in US-guided tracheostomy, where point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) improves first-pass success and anatomical localization, and AI-enhanced image interpretation is beginning to improve these workflows through automated landmark identification and decision support (169). Experimental DL models using US data have shown good predictive performance for difficult airway assessment, although multicenter validation is still lacking (170). Beyond imaging, ML models using ICU EHR data have shown strong performance in predicting tracheostomy requirement and optimal timing, with several studies reporting AUCs approaching or exceeding 0.9 and identifying clinically intuitive predictors such as secretion burden, gas exchange parameters, and neurologic status (171,172). In post-tracheostomy care, AI predicts decannulation readiness and ventilator weaning, with validated clinical scores and ML models achieving high discrimination (AUC roughly 0.89), as well as novel approaches such as DL-based breathing sound analysis that allow early detection of airway obstruction with sensitivities up to 0.94 (173,174). Despite strong technical performances, most studies remain proof-of-concept or retrospective, with a notable absence of prospective head-to-head clinical trials and limited regulatory-approved tools specific to tracheostomy.

Challenges, limitations, future directions

The expanse of AI into the field of IP is clear, but several gaps still exist between technological development and its true clinical impact. While a small number of commercially available systems, most notably robotic-assisted bronchoscopy platforms, have achieved regulatory clearance and widespread clinical use, their AI components are largely confined to navigation and guidance rather than autonomous decision-making, with lacking evidence on patient-centered outcomes (175). Although the majority of AI applications in IP, including bronchoscopic image analysis, airway segmentation, EBUS lymph node classification, and pleural disease detection, have shown encouraging performance metrics, they still remain investigational and have only been reported in retrospective, single-center datasets with minimal prospective validation (110).

A central limitation is the so-called ‘proof-of-concept to practice chasm’, whereby most studies emphasize technical performance metrics such as accuracy or AUC rather than clinically significant endpoints such as diagnostic yield, procedural success, complication reduction, or cost-effectiveness (176). External validation is rare, and randomized controlled trials are virtually nonexistent, which limits confidence in generalizability and actual clinical performance (110). These challenges are compounded by fundamental data limitations, as most models are trained on small, retrospective datasets derived from single institutions which increases the risk of overfitting and reduces applicability across other clinical settings (177). There is also variability in expert annotation, particularly in bronchoscopy and EBUS imaging, which introduces labeling bias, along with heterogeneity in imaging equipment, procedural technique, and patient population, all of which further degrade model performance outside the training environment (178,179).

AI systems also face significant barriers to integrating into human workflow, as deploying them in real-time procedural environments such as bronchoscopy suites require low-latency processing and interfaces with existing imaging and EHR system requirements that are rarely addressed in current studies (180). Poor interface design and lack of human-centered development also contribute to cognitive overload and alert fatigue which reduces clinician trust, particularly in high-stakes settings (181,182). The ‘black box’ nature of many DL models is also a major barrier, as clinicians may be reluctant to rely on recommendations that lack transparent reasoning, despite the availability of explainability tools such as SHAP and Grad-CAM (183,184).

Other complex limitations include regulatory, medicolegal, and economic considerations. Current regulatory pathways, including the FDA 510(k) process, allow the approval of many AI-enabled devices without requiring prospective clinical trials or demonstration of improved outcomes, which raises concerns regarding their true clinical effectiveness and generalizability (185). Liability also becomes an issue when AI recommendations conflict with clinician judgment, particularly given the opacity of many models and the absence of standardized governance frameworks (186). Furthermore, although AI is frequently described as cost-saving, most studies fail to account for implementation and operational costs, with insufficient data showing proven financial benefit (187). Additionally, the infrastructure required for AI use, including high-performance computing, data integration systems, specialized personnel, etc., creates disparities between well-resourced academic centers and community settings.

With regard to ethical concerns, particularly related to bias and equity, several AI models trained on datasets from tertiary care centers may not extrapolate to certain populations and have been shown to underdiagnose diseases in underserved groups (188,189). Ensuring fairness requires systematic subgroup analysis and standardization embedded with equity-focused design principles throughout the AI lifecycle (190). At the same time, the risk of clinician deskilling must be considered, as overreliance on AI may reduce independent clinical reasoning and procedural expertise if not carefully integrated into training and practice (191). To ensure safe and effective human-AI collaboration, human-in-the-loop and human-AI partnership models must be developed to aim for balanced automation with clinician oversight (192,193).

Looking forward, several strategies could potentially address the aforementioned barriers. Federated learning offers a mechanism to develop more generalizable models by using multicenter data without compromising patient privacy, while multimodal AI systems using imaging, physiologic, and clinical data may provide more personalized decision-making (194). Advances in real-time computer vision and edge computing are also paving the way for intra-procedural AI tools that can improve safety by identifying airway landmarks, thereby guiding navigation. Future systems could function as a ‘digital co-pilot’ during procedures using augmented reality (AR) or extended reality (ER) interfaces (195). Digital twin models, which allow patient-specific simulation of airway anatomy and procedural strategies prior to intervention, could also potentially improve precision and reduce complications (196). The field must shift towards prospective, multicenter studies that evaluate clinically significant outcomes rather than relying on retrospective performance metrics alone, alongside standardized reporting to improve reproducibility and transparency (190). Emerging foundation models may allow scalable deployment across multiple IP tasks despite limited labeled datasets, while AI-driven simulation platforms could accelerate skill acquisition and standardize training across institutions (176,197). The successful use of AI will require a strong emphasis on human-centered design and equitable deployment with interfaces that reduce cognitive burden rather than add to it. At the same time, efforts must also be made to ensure diversity in training datasets by conducting subgroup performance analyses and addressing algorithmic bias to avoid exacerbating the already existing healthcare disparities (189). Ultimately, the goal is not autonomous AI, but augmented intelligence, where AI serves as an adjunct while preserving the central role of the interventional pulmonologist.

Conclusions

AI is reshaping the landscape of IP, with expanding applications across diagnostic imaging, bronchoscopic procedures, pleural disease evaluation, and therapeutic interventions. Across these domains, AI has consistently demonstrated strong technical performance in tasks such as lesion detection, risk stratification, lymph node classification, and procedural guidance. Emerging multimodal approaches, including radiomics, radio-pathomics, and integration with liquid biopsy, etc., further highlight the potential of AI to capture complex tumor biology and support more personalized, data-driven clinical decision-making.

However, the majority of available evidence is derived from retrospective, single-center studies with limited external validation, and there is a notable absence of prospective trials showing improvements in clinically relevant outcomes. Some of its limitations also include data heterogeneity, model generalizability, workflow integration, interpretability, and cost-effectiveness, etc. These barriers highlight the need for a shift in focus from model performance metrics toward real-world clinical utility.

Future directions in AI for IP will likely involve the development of reliable and generalizable models through multicenter collaboration that involves the use of multimodal data to generate real-time decision-support systems within procedural workflows. Advances in federated learning, explainable AI, and AR-guided interventions may further strengthen the human-AI collaboration. Ultimately, the successful integration of AI into IP will depend on rigorous prospective validation, thoughtful implementation, and maintaining a human-in-the-loop approach to ensure that these technologies augment, rather than replace, clinical expertise.
