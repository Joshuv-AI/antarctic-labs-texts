Renal hilar paraganglioma with atypical clinical presentation and biochemical functionality: A case report of diagnostic pitfalls
Authors: Koosha Kamali, Mostafa Rajabi, Mohammadreza Padooiy Nooshabadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Paragangliomas arising from the renal hilum are exceptionally rare and may mimic other retroperitoneal neoplasms on imaging. We report a 26-year-old normotensive man presenting with dull left flank pain. Biochemical evaluation showed elevated plasma free metanephrine and normetanephrine despite normal catecholamine levels. CT angiography demonstrated a thick-walled, multiseptated cystic hilar mass with renal vein encasement, while PET/CT showed low metabolic activity. Left nephrectomy with mass resection was performed, and histopathology with positive Synaptophysin, Chromogranin A, Vimentin, and S100 staining confirmed paraganglioma. This case highlights the diagnostic value of biochemical testing and histopathology in atypical renal hilar masses.

Introduction

Paraganglia are neural crest derived neuroendocrine cell groups distributed throughout the body. Paragangliomas are rare, highly vascular tumors arising from sympathetic or parasympathetic extra adrenal paraganglia.1 Among extra-adrenal paragangliomas, the retroperitoneum is the most common site of occurrence, with the organ of Zuckerkandl being the predominant location. In contrast, involvement of uncommon sites such as the kidney and renal hilum is exceedingly rare.2,3

Sympathetic paragangliomas arise from extra-adrenal chromaffin tissue and may secrete catecholamines, leading to clinical manifestations such as hypertension, tachycardia, and diaphoresis. However, some tumors remain clinically silent despite significant biochemical catecholamine excess, which may delay diagnosis and increase the risk of unexpected complications.4 Although paragangliomas are typically hypervascular lesions with avid contrast enhancement, no specific radiologic feature reliably distinguishes them from other retroperitoneal tumors.2 This diagnostic challenge becomes more pronounced in cases with atypical anatomical locations and absent clinical symptoms.

Herein, we present a rare case of renal hilar retroperitoneal paraganglioma with atypical clinical and radiologic features, leading to diagnostic uncertainty with other retroperitoneal neoplasms. This case emphasizes the importance of considering paraganglioma in the differential diagnosis of renal hilar masses to facilitate accurate diagnosis and appropriate management.

Patient information

A 26-year-old male smoker presented with a 5-month history of dull left flank pain. He had no family history of endocrine tumors or related hereditary disorders. He also had no history of hypertension or symptoms suggestive of catecholamine excess, including palpitations, diaphoresis, headache, flushing, or paroxysmal hypertensive episodes.

Clinical findings

Physical examination was unremarkable except for mild upper abdominal tenderness, with a blood pressure of 135/80 mmHg and no palpable mass or abdominal bruit.

Diagnostic assessment

Routine laboratory investigations, including complete blood count, liver and renal function tests, serum electrolytes, and urinalysis, were within normal limits. Tumor markers were also unremarkable. Plasma catecholamine levels were normal; however, plasma free metanephrine (104 pg/mL) and normetanephrine (198 pg/mL) levels were elevated, along with increased 24-h urinary normetanephrine excretion (728 mcg/day), while urinary vanillylmandelic acid (VMA) and metanephrine levels remained within reference ranges.

Abdominal ultrasonography revealed a 7 × 5 cm solid-cystic mass in the left retroperitoneum adjacent to the renal hilum.

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) of the abdomen and pelvis revealed a 76 × 70 × 57 mm thick-walled, multiseptated solid-cystic mass with marked enhancement in the left retroperitoneum, adjacent to the left renal hilum and in close contact with the left renal vein and artery. A draining vessel from the intervertebral region communicating with the mass was also noted (Fig. 1). CT angiography demonstrated a 70 × 65 mm round mass at the left renal hilum, anterior-inferior to the left renal artery, showing extensive central cystic changes and a hyper vascular margin, with encasement and dilation of the left renal vein up to 18 mm (Fig. 2). Whole-body 18F-FDG PET/CT revealed a well-defined mixed-density round mass in the left perinephric fossa, measuring 71 × 68 × 56 mm, with minimal FDG uptake (SUVmax 1.8). The mass was attached to the fourth part of the duodenum, the left renal vein, and the left adrenal gland. The left renal vein and the proximal portion of the gonadal vein appeared dilated. No evidence of distant hypermetabolic lesions or pathologic lymphadenopathy was identified.

Therapeutic intervention

Open radical nephrectomy with en-bloc excision of the retroperitoneal mass was performed due to the tumor's invasion into the renal vein and its adherence to the renal hilum, necessitating concomitant nephrectomy. Gross examination revealed a well-circumscribed 7.5 × 6 × 4.5 cm mass composed of soft red-brown tissue with cystic changes and hemorrhagic areas. Follow-up and outcomes

Microscopic examination and immunohistochemical staining confirmed the diagnosis of a retroperitoneal paraganglioma. Tumor cells showed positive staining for Synaptophysin, Chromogranin A, and Vimentin, with S100 positivity in sustentacular cells, while negative staining was observed for CD34, CK, CK7, SOX10, PAX8, and Ki67. Massive necrosis was present, but surgical margins were negative for tumor. A small fragment of adrenal tissue was identified far from the tumor, and two reactive lymph nodes were noted. The left kidney was histologically unremarkable with no evidence of malignancy. It is noteworthy that, despite repeated attempts to reach the patient for follow-up visits and genetic testing, the patient did not attend; however, his overall general condition was satisfactory.

Discussion

We report a rare case of retroperitoneal paraganglioma arising from the renal hilum, an uncommon anatomical location. Although the tumor was biochemically active with elevated metanephrine levels, the patient lacked the typical clinical manifestations. The unusual location, absence of specific radiological features, and atypical clinical presentation resulted in a diagnostic challenge.

Paragangliomas arising from sympathetic chromaffin tissue can secrete excess catecholamines, leading to manifestations such as hypertension, tachycardia, hyperhidrosis, and hypertensive crises. However, the presence and the severity of clinical symptoms do not always correlate with the degree of catecholamine excess, and some patients may remain clinically silent despite significant biochemical activity, likely due to individual variations in catecholamine sensitivity and/or adrenergic receptor desensitization. Therefore, the absence of typical symptoms does not exclude a functional paraganglioma.4

Measurement of plasma free or urinary fractionated metanephrines is considered the preferred biochemical test due to its higher sensitivity compared with direct catecholamine measurements. Metanephrines are continuously produced through catecholamine metabolism within chromaffin tumor cells by catechol-O-methyltransferase, providing more stable biomarkers and allowing detection of hormonally active tumors even in asymptomatic patients.17 In our patient, despite the absence of clinical manifestations and normal circulating catecholamine levels, plasma free metanephrine and normetanephrine levels were elevated, which is consistent with the known diagnostic superiority of metanephrine measurements and the variable clinical expression of catecholamine-secreting paragangliomas.

Clinically silent paragangliomas are often detected incidentally during imaging studies or through surveillance of individuals with hereditary predisposition. On imaging, they usually appear as well-circumscribed hypervascular masses with intense contrast enhancement, often located adjacent to major vessels. MRI may demonstrate the characteristic T2 hyperintense light bulb sign; however, imaging findings are not always specific and may overlap with other retroperitoneal tumors, particularly neurogenic and mesenchymal neoplasms.17,18 In our case, the unusual renal hilar location, extensive cystic and necrotic changes, and close relationship with the renal vessels resulted in diagnostic uncertainty, with the lesion initially considered as a possible retroperitoneal sarcoma, vascular-origin tumor, or other soft tissue neoplasm rather than a paraganglioma. Therefore, biochemical evaluation and histopathological confirmation remain essential for accurate diagnosis. The diagnosis is supported by the characteristic neuroendocrine morphology and immunohistochemical positivity for markers such as Chromogranin A and Synaptophysin, along with absence of epithelial markers such as cytokeratin 5.

Functional imaging is reserved for selected cases, including suspected multifocal or metastatic disease or when anatomical imaging is inconclusive. Although 18F-FDG PET/CT can be useful, particularly in metastatic or SDHx-related paragangliomas, its uptake is not solely determined by tumor presence and varies according to biological behavior of tumor and the variable glycolytic activity among different molecular subtypes. Furthermore, extensive cystic degeneration and necrotic components, as observed in our patient, may reduce the overall metabolic activity because tracer accumulation reflects viable tumor tissue. Therefore, low FDG avidity should not exclude the diagnosis of paraganglioma, particularly in localized tumors.17

Surgical resection remains the mainstay of treatment for localized paragangliomas and provides definitive therapy. However, manipulation of hormonally active tumors may cause a massive catecholamine release, resulting in severe hemodynamic instability; therefore, appropriate preoperative preparation with alpha-adrenergic blockade and volume expansion is essential to minimize perioperative complications. Intraoperative and postoperative monitoring are required due to the risk of blood pressure fluctuations and hypotension after tumor removal. In our case, radical nephrectomy with en-bloc removal of the retroperitoneal mass due to its close anatomical relationship with the renal hilum and renal vessels, which made isolated tumor excision challenging. Similar cases of renal hilar paragangliomas requiring concomitant nephrectomy have also been reported, reflecting the complexity of surgical management in this uncommon location. Long-term surveillance is recommended because of the potential risk of recurrence or metastatic disease, even after apparently complete resection.17
