Functional and flavour-enhancing properties of Staphylococcus sp. from Napham
Authors: Debashree Borthakur, Bipin Kumar Sharma, Twesigye Duncan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Napham, traditional fermented food of the Bodo community in Assam, is produced from tender shoots of Colocasia esculenta and dried fish, and was recently granted Geographical Indication (GI) status. Despite its cultural and nutritional significance, its beneficial microbiota remains insufficiently characterized. This study aimed to identify multifunctional bacterial isolates with both functional and flavor-enhancing properties for use as starter cultures. Forty-eight isolates were screened for Gram reaction, enzymatic activities (protease, lipase, carbohydrate fermentation), and biosafety traits. Four isolates (NAP/1, NAP/2, NAP/3, NAP/4) exhibiting a preliminary phenotypic safety profile based on in vitro screening were evaluated for gastrointestinal stress tolerance traits, including tolerance to acid (pH 2.0), phenol (0.4%), salt (8% NaCl), as well as auto-aggregation capacity and adhesion to chicken crop epithelial cells. Among them, isolate NAP/4 exhibited comparatively higher tolerance, strong adhesion, and high auto-aggregation, and demonstrated a favorable in vitro safety profile, including γ-hemolysis and broad antibiotic susceptibility. In curd and rice beverage models, NAP/4 enhanced sensory qualities and produced diverse flavor volatiles, supporting its candidacy as a multifunctional food-fermenting isolate prior to genomic validation. 16S rRNA sequencing revealed NAP/4 as Staphylococcus sp. (GenBank accession: PQ471484.1). Collectively, these findings highlight NAP/4 as a promising candidate for further genomic evaluation toward potential application in controlled fermentation systems and clean-label formulations. However, whole-genome sequencing and in vivo validation remain essential next steps to confirm safety and functional efficacy at the molecular level.

Introduction

Microbial fermentation is a complex bioconversion process in which bacteria, yeasts, and molds transform carbohydrates into organic acids, alcohols, and other metabolites under anaerobic or microaerophilic conditions1,2. Traditionally employed for food preservation, fermentation has evolved into a key biotechnological approach that enhances the nutritional value, digestibility, safety, and sensory attributes of foods3. Fermented foods constitute an important component of global diets, encompassing dairy, cereals, vegetables, legumes, and animal-derived substrates4. Their diverse microbial consortia contribute to product stabilization and generate bioactive compounds with established health benefits5. Many microorganisms associated with fermented foods also exhibit probiotic characteristics, including modulation of gut microbiota, reinforcement of mucosal barrier integrity, and inhibition of pathogenic bacteria6. As consumer preference increasingly favors natural, functional, and minimally processed foods, fermented products have gained prominence as effective carriers of beneficial microbes and metabolites7. Traditional fermented foods from biodiversity-rich regions such as Northeast India are particularly noteworthy. These foods reflect long-standing indigenous knowledge, community-specific preparation practices, and ecologically adapted microbial consortia8–10. Despite their significance, many of these products remain undercharacterized in contemporary food biotechnology.

Among them, Napham, a naturally fermented product made from tender shoots of Colocasia esculenta and dried fish, holds cultural and nutritional importance and was recently granted Geographical Indication (GI) status in Assam, India11. It is produced by the Bodo community through spontaneous fermentation under ambient conditions and is valued for its distinctive organoleptic attributes12. In contrast to globally well-studied fermented foods such as kimchi, natto, fermented fish, and dairy fermentations, whose microbial biodiversity and functional attributes are extensively documented, Napham remains largely unexplored, despite its unique plant–fish substrate combination and spontaneous fermentation process that may support distinct microbial consortia. As demand for clean-label and health-oriented foods intensifies, traditional fermented products such as Napham are increasingly recognized as valuable but underutilized microbial reservoirs, harboring isolates with both functional attributes and aroma-generating potential.

Despite its cultural importance, Napham has received limited scientific scrutiny. A clear research gap remains in understanding its microbial diversity, the functional attributes of its constituent microorganisms, and their roles in shaping sensory attributes. Furthermore, existing studies on fermented foods from Northeast India rarely adopt integrative biochemical, molecular, and metabolomic methodologies. This lack of comprehensive multidisciplinary investigation constrains the systematic characterization and biotechnological exploitation of Napham as a source of functionally valuable microorganisms for food biotechnology.

Probiotics are live microorganisms that confer health benefits when administered in adequate amounts and have attracted growing scientific interest due to their diverse physiological effects, including modulation of gut microbiota, reinforcement of intestinal barrier function, and regulation of immune responses13,14. Extensive research has demonstrated the contributions of lactic acid bacteria (LAB) and select non-LAB taxa to probiotic functionality. Essential attributes include resistance to gastric acidity and bile salts, adhesion to intestinal epithelial cells, production of antimicrobial compounds such as organic acids, bacteriocins, and exopolysaccharides (EPS), and the absence of virulence determinants15,16. LAB also play a pivotal role in flavor development through proteolytic and lipolytic pathways that generate peptides, amino acids, and fatty acids17–22. These intermediates are subsequently converted into volatile compounds including esters, aldehydes, ketones, alcohols, and sulfur-containing derivatives that determine the sensory characteristics of fermented products23,24. Isolates capable of contributing to both functional attributes and desirable flavor formation are increasingly valued in the development of clean-label, consumer-focused functional foods25–28.

According to previous studies, robust non-LAB organisms such as Bacillus subtilis DSM 32315, DE111® and B. coagulans MTCC 5856 have demonstrated industrial relevance due to their spore-forming capacity, environmental resilience, immunomodulatory effects, and favorable contributions to flavor development29–34. These microorganisms are broadening opportunities for developing multifunctional starter cultures for diverse fermented and non-fermented food systems. Despite these advancements, Napham remains a scientifically unexplored substrate. No comprehensive studies have evaluated its microbial composition, functional attributes, or volatile metabolite profile using integrated biochemical assays, 16S rRNA gene sequencing, and GC–MS-based analyses. The present study addresses this gap through a combined microbiological, molecular, biochemical, and aroma-focused framework, while also acknowledging the socio-cultural context of traditional fermentation practices. Beyond its cultural significance, traditional fermented foods such as Napham offer opportunities for translation into standardized and commercially viable functional food products. The identification of robust, multifunctional microbial isolates from indigenous fermentations can support the development of defined starter cultures and value-added food products while preserving traditional sensory characteristics. In this context, the present study was designed to address the following objectives.

The objectives of this investigation were fourfold. First, to isolate microbial strains from Napham and perform preliminary phenotypic and biochemical characterization. Second, to evaluate selected isolates for technological suitability, stress tolerance, and adhesion properties relevant to food fermentation. Third, to assess aroma-generating potential using enzymatic assays and GC–MS profiling. Finally, to determine taxonomic identity through 16S rRNA gene sequencing.

Results

Isolation and morphological characterization

For the present investigation, ten Napham samples were obtained, as depicted in Fig. 1. A total of forty-eight bacterial isolates exhibited distinct morphological characteristics on NAM and MRS agar, and were characterized based on their phenotypic properties and biochemical profiles, including Gram reaction and catalase activity. Among the isolates, twenty isolates (NAP/1–NAP/20) were identified as Gram-positive cocci lacking catalase activity, whereas twenty-eight isolates (NAP/21–NAP/48) were identified as Gram-positive rods with catalase activity, as tabulated in Table 1. All forty-eight isolates demonstrated varying degrees of endospore formation and were able to proliferate anaerobically at 37 °C in MRS medium. All forty-eight isolates, representing both Gram-positive cocci (NAP/1–NAP/20) and Gram-positive rods (NAP/21–NAP/48), were first characterized by detailed microscopic examination and conventional biochemical assays in accordance with Bergey’s Manual of Determinative Bacteriology35 to establish preliminary phenotypic profiles. Subsequently, all isolates were subjected to enzymatic and functional screening. The DNase test is an important assay for assessing microbial virulence, as deoxyribonuclease production enables the degradation of host nucleic acids, aiding immune evasion and increasing pathogenic potential. The extracellular DNase activity of isolates NAP/1–NAP/4 was assessed using DNase agar medium. Absence of clearance zones in all isolates confirmed that DNase production was not detected. No clearance zones were observed around the colonies, indicating absence of detectable DNase activity under the tested conditions, as tabulated in Table 3.

Assessment of antibiotic susceptibility constitutes a critical component of biosafety evaluation for prospective functional isolates, particularly to mitigate the potential dissemination of antimicrobial resistance determinants. The entire dataset of inhibition zones for all microbial isolates evaluated against the chosen antibiotics has been presented in Supplementary Table 1. In this study, four microbial isolates (NAP/1, NAP/2, NAP/3, and NAP/4) were subjected to disc diffusion assays against antibiotics spanning various pharmacological classes—including fluoroquinolones, oxazolidinones, aminoglycosides, carbapenems, macrolides, β-lactams, and chloramphenicol. Experimental protocols followed the standards set by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI, 2024)36. While NAP/1 retained sensitivity to several fluoroquinolones and aminoglycosides, its resistance to antibiotics across multiple classes, including fluoroquinolones and oxazolidinones, as tabulated in Table 4, warrants caution for functional application. Isolate NAP/4 displayed a broad-spectrum susceptibility profile, with positive responses to all tested antibiotics. The inhibition zone diameters exceeded standard thresholds across fluoroquinolone, aminoglycoside, oxazolidinone, macrolide, β-lactam, carbapenem, and chloramphenicol classes. This comprehensive sensitivity pattern highlights NAP/4 as a promising candidate for further evaluation as a multifunctional food-fermenting isolate. As shown in Fig. 2, statistically significant differences in antibiotic susceptibility profiles were observed among the isolates (p < 0.05), with isolate NAP/4 exhibiting uniform sensitivity across all tested antibiotics, meeting preliminary in vitro biosafety parameters under the tested conditions. Selection based on safety and enzymatic activity

Forty-eight isolates (NAP/1–NAP/48) were initially screened for key functional traits, including proteolytic, lipolytic, and carbohydrate fermentation activities. Among them, four isolates (NAP/1–NAP/4) consistently demonstrated superior enzymatic performance across these traits, as illustrated in Fig. 3. The selected isolates exhibited consistent growth under the tested conditions. Based on this combined functional and phenotypic suitability, the four isolates were chosen for subsequent functional evaluation. The selected isolates underwent preliminary in vitro biosafety screening and exhibited phenotypic traits consistent with non-hemolytic and DNase-negative behavior, and susceptibility to key clinical antibiotics. Characterization for functional properties

Thermal tolerance was an important functional trait, indicating an isolate’s ability to withstand temperature fluctuations during food processing, storage, and gastrointestinal transit. The thermal resilience of isolates NAP/1–NAP/4 was assessed at temperatures ranging from 4 °C to 40 °C, representing both refrigeration and physiological conditions. Viability was monitored following incubation at 4, 28, 37, and 40 °C for 24 h, and the results are presented in Fig. 4. Among the isolates, NAP/4 showed the strongest thermotolerance, with viability rising from 4.76 ± 0.34% at 4 °C, 61.82 ± 0.65% at 28 °C to 96.84 ± 0.93% at 37 °C and remaining high at 82.18 ± 1.26% at 40 °C. NAP/1 exhibited moderate thermotolerance, with viability ranging from 47.94% at 28 °C to 80.84% at 37 °C and 61.73% at 40 °C, along with 21.39% survival at 4 °C, as tabulated in Table 5. NAP/2 showed lower heat tolerance but comparatively better cold stability (28.54% at 4 °C), maintaining 39.83–68.23% viability across 28–40 °C. Statistical summary

The Kruskal–Wallis analysis demonstrated significant differences in the biochemical, functional and organoleptic properties of microbial isolates obtained from Napham (p < 0.05). The functional performance exhibited considerable variation across isolates (H-statistic = 14.79, p = 1.2 × 10⁻⁴), as did enzymatic activities associated with the biosynthesis of flavor compounds (H-statistic = 9.60, p = 8.2 × 10⁻³). Sensory evaluation of curd and rice beverages fermented with the selected isolates, analyzed using one-way ANOVA, supported these observations (p < 0.001). Among the tested isolates, NAP/4 consistently achieved the highest acceptability scores, recording 7.67 for curd and 6.40 for rice beverage. Post hoc Tukey’s HSD analysis further validated that NAP/4 was significantly superior to the other isolates in terms of both organoleptic attributes and biochemical performance (p < 0.05). Moreover, Kruskal–Wallis analysis of the association between volatile flavor metabolites and sensory attributes revealed a strong correlation for NAP/4 (H-statistic = 7.53, p = 6.10 × 10⁻³). Key metabolites, including propionic acid, 3-hexanol, heptane, and oxalic acid, were identified as principal contributors to its enhanced flavor profile. Collectively, these findings identify isolate NAP/4 as a strong candidate for further evaluation as a multifunctional food-fermenting isolate for the development of high-quality fermented foods.

Molecular Identification and phylogenetic analysis

Isolate NAP/4, collected from aroma-active fermented delicacies, was taxonomically classified using 16S rRNA gene sequencing as Staphylococcus sp. isolate N1 (GenBank accession: PQ471484.1). Phylogenetic analysis conducted in MEGA X, employing the Weighbor algorithm with the Jukes–Cantor nucleotide substitution model, revealed that isolate NAP/4 clustered closely within the Staphylococcus genus, supported by strong bootstrap values (>90%). These results validate the taxonomic placement of NAP/4 within the Staphylococcus lineage and underscore its potential as a functional and flavor-enhancing agent in fermented food products. Discussion

Napham exemplifies how indigenous fermentation practices contribute simultaneously to food preservation, flavor development, nutritional enhancement, and the maintenance of unique microbial ecosystems. Beyond its cultural relevance, Napham represents a biologically rich fermentation niche shaped by a distinctive plant–fish substrate combination and spontaneous household-level processing conditions. Such environments are increasingly recognized as reservoirs of non-conventional microbial taxa with multifunctional technological potential37. The present study advances this perspective by demonstrating that Napham harbors Staphylococcus sp. with combined functional attributes and aroma-generating capacity—an attribute rarely documented in fermented food research. Integrative studies combining microbial characterization with applied perspectives have highlighted how indigenous fermentation microbiota can underpin both functional food development and broader socio-economic benefits when translated into community-based production systems38,39.

The isolation of morphologically diverse Gram-positive bacterial isolates from Napham reflects the selective pressures imposed by its unique plant–fish substrate and spontaneous fermentation conditions. The dominance of Gram-positive taxa is consistent with fermented food ecosystems characterized by low water activity, elevated salt content, and progressive acidification, conditions under which Gram-negative bacteria are typically outcompeted. Morphological heterogeneity among the isolates suggests niche differentiation and adaptive specialization, likely driven by variations in carbohydrate availability, protein content from dried fish, and microaerophilic household fermentation environments. Such diversity provides a rational basis for functional screening, as phenotypically distinct isolates often display divergent metabolic and technological capabilities relevant to functional starter culture development.

Protease, lipase, and carbohydrate fermentation activities were specifically selected for evaluation due to their pivotal roles in fermented food systems. Proteolytic and lipolytic enzymes contribute directly to flavor development by releasing peptides, amino acids, and fatty acids that are subsequently transformed into aroma-active metabolites40–42. Carbohydrate fermentation reflects the ability of microbial isolates to efficiently metabolize available sugars, ensuring acid production, microbial stability, and effective performance as starter cultures in both dairy and cereal-based substrates43. While LAB particularly Lactobacillus spp. has traditionally been emphasized for these functions, the present findings demonstrate that Staphylococcus sp. NAP/4 exhibits comparable enzymatic versatility. Unlike LAB, which primarily channel carbohydrate metabolism through homo- or heterolactic pathways, Staphylococcus species possess broader metabolic networks that support mixed acid fermentation, amino acid catabolism, and lipid transformation. This metabolic flexibility likely underpins the observed dual functionality of NAP/4, enabling both functional resilience and enhanced aroma biosynthesis. The combined assessment of enzymatic traits therefore highlights Staphylococcus sp. as an underappreciated yet technologically relevant contributor to fermented food quality.

The absence of hemolytic activity and DNase production supports a preliminary phenotypic safety profile of the isolates and aligns with preliminary in vitro safety indicators. Although Staphylococcus is frequently viewed through a pathogenic lens due to clinically relevant species such as S. aureus, it is increasingly recognized that coagulase-negative Staphylococcus isolates associated with food fermentations are genetically and functionally distinct. The γ-hemolytic phenotype observed in NAP/4, together with DNase negativity, supports its classification as it did not exhibit in vitro markers commonly associated with virulence supporting further investigation under controlled experimental conditions.

Antibiotic susceptibility profiling revealed notable isolate-dependent differences with direct relevance to food safety and regulatory considerations. Isolate NAP/1 exhibited resistance to multiple antibiotics, including agents commonly used in clinical practice, which may limit its suitability for application in food systems due to the potential risk of antimicrobial resistance dissemination. Accordingly, despite its enzymatic competence, NAP/1 was not considered for downstream food application. Although intrinsic resistance in certain food-associated bacteria is well documented, current regulatory frameworks for starter cultures emphasize the selection of isolates devoid of acquired or transferable resistance determinants.

In contrast, isolate NAP/4 exhibited susceptibility to all tested antibiotics, thereby meeting preliminary in vitro biosafety screening parameters and strengthening its candidacy for further evaluation as a potential starter culture. These findings highlight the importance of incorporating antibiotic susceptibility profiling into isolate selection workflows to ensure consumer safety and regulatory compliance. Isolate NAP/3 showed a moderate susceptibility profile, retaining sensitivity to ciprofloxacin, moxifloxacin, gentamicin, amikacin, linezolid, azithromycin, ampicillin, imipenem, and chloramphenicol, but was resistant to ofloxacin, levofloxacin, and neomycin. The observed resistance towards fluoroquinolone and aminoglycoside classes may indicate efflux-mediated or target-site modification mechanisms, though the overall susceptibility to several clinically relevant antibiotics supports its potential use under controlled conditions. Isolate NAP/2 displayed a largely favorable susceptibility pattern, remaining sensitive to most of the antibiotics tested, including fluoroquinolones, neomycin, imipenem, and chloramphenicol, although resistance to azithromycin, gentamicin, amikacin, moxifloxacin and ampicillin was observed. While these isolate-specific resistance traits warrant monitoring, the broad sensitivity across multiple antibiotic classes supports its biosafety profile. Among the four isolates evaluated, isolate NAP/4 exhibited the most favorable antibiotic susceptibility profile, followed by NAP/2 and NAP/3, whereas NAP/1 showed comparatively higher resistance, particularly to critical antibiotics, thereby reducing its suitability for functional application. These findings reinforce the importance of rigorous antibiotic susceptibility testing as a critical component of the preliminary safety assessment of functional candidates, particularly when considering their potential future application in food systems or use in vulnerable populations.

Although phenotypic safety screening provides an initial indication of biosafety, definitive confirmation requires comprehensive genomic characterization. Whole-genome sequencing is essential to exclude the presence of latent virulence determinants, toxin genes, or transferable antimicrobial resistance elements. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as preliminary and subject to molecular validation prior to any regulatory or industrial consideration.

The selection of candidate isolates for further functional and technological evaluation was guided by a combined assessment of biosafety attributes and enzymatic functionality. Isolates exhibiting γ-hemolysis, absence of DNase activity, and susceptibility to clinically relevant antibiotics were prioritized based on phenotypic characteristics consistent with preliminary in vitro safety indicators. Concurrently, robust proteolytic, lipolytic, and carbohydrate-fermenting activities were considered critical, as these traits underpin both fermentation efficiency and flavor development. This integrated selection strategy enabled the identification of isolates that demonstrated phenotypic safety under the tested in vitro conditions, together with technological competence, thereby supporting their candidacy for further evaluation as potential starter or adjunct cultures in fermented food systems.

Across stress tolerance assays, Staphylococcus sp. NAP/4 demonstrated consistent physiological robustness under acidic, osmotic, thermal, and phenolic stress conditions, maintaining substantial viability across all tested parameters. Notably, it exhibited the highest tolerance among the evaluated isolates in the present study. Such resilience may reflect adaptation to fluctuating environmental conditions during spontaneous Napham fermentation and supports its suitability for controlled fermentation processes. The comparable stress tolerance ranges for other food-associated fermentative bacteria have been reported in the literature44. The strong auto-aggregation and epithelial surface adhesion properties observed in vitro further indicate stable cell–cell interaction and surface association capacity, traits that may contribute to fermentation stability and microbial persistence within food matrices45. Collectively, these characteristics highlight its techno-functional robustness for diverse fermentation environments.

For thermal tolerance, NAP/4 was the most thermotolerant isolate, retaining over 80% viability at 40 °C without any pre-conditioning. This level of resilience is comparable to Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus GG and Lactobacillus acidophilus NCFM, which typically require thermal adaptation to maintain viability above 50% under comparable conditions44,46. The inherent heat tolerance of NAP/4 offers a clear technological advantage for functional applications in dynamic food systems—such as fermented beverages, shelf-stable products, and encapsulated formulations—where stability under fluctuating temperatures is essential.

For acid tolerance, the findings identify isolate NAP/4 as the most acid-tolerant isolate, exhibiting survival rates comparable to several well-established functional isolates. Under highly acidic conditions (pH 2.0), NAP/4 demonstrated markedly higher resilience than Lactobacillus fermentum MTCC 8711, which typically shows ~50–55% survival47, and Limosilactobacillus reuteri DSM 17938, reported to maintain 40–60% viability48. Its performance was also comparable to acid-resilient Bacillus sp. isolated from Teles, which retain approximately 80% viability under similar gastric stress conditions49. Collectively, these comparisons underscore the exceptional acid tolerance of NAP/4 and support its suitability for functional applications requiring survival through the gastric environment.

For salt tolerance, NAP/4 demonstrated the strongest resilience to osmotic stress, showing consistent survival across all tested salt concentrations and emerging as the most osmotolerant isolate. Its performance is comparable to Tetragenococcus halophilus (tolerant up to 10% NaCl)50 and Lactobacillus sakei, which maintains more than 80% viability at 6% NaCl51. NAP/4 retained approximately 75% viability at 6% NaCl and more than 60% at 8% NaCl, underscoring its suitability for salt-rich functional foods and functional applications exposed to osmotic stress.

For phenol tolerance, NAP/4 exhibited the highest level of resistance, clearly outperforming isolates NAP/1 and NAP/3, and markedly exceeding the tolerance of NAP/2. Notably, the ~98% viability of NAP/4 at 0.4% phenol surpasses the values reported for Lactobacillus salivarius UCC118 and Pediococcus pentosaceus (60–63%)52,53. This exceptional phenol tolerance underscores the strong potential of NAP/4 for survival and functionality in phenol-rich intestinal environments.

For auto-aggregation test, the trends likely arise from isolate-specific cell surface determinants, including EPS, adhesins, and aggregation-promoting proteins that govern microbial interaction and persistence. Notably, NAP/4 consistently outperformed the other isolates, exhibiting functional levels comparable to Lactobacillus plantarum and Enterococcus faecium54, thereby identifying it as the most promising candidate for advanced functional evaluation.

For adhesion assay, NAP/4 exhibited adhesion levels comparable to Lactobacillus plantarum WCFS1 (60–70%)55 and Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus GG (~65%)56. This strong adhesive capacity highlights NAP/4 as a promising candidate for functional food and nutraceutical applications, particularly those targeting enhanced mucosal adherence, intestinal barrier support, and competitive exclusion of pathogenic microorganisms.

Milk was sterilized to eliminate native microbiota, ensuring that curd formation resulted exclusively from the introduced Napham isolates. Under these controlled conditions, isolates NAP/1–NAP/4 fermented lactose to lactic acid, leading to pH reduction and casein coagulation. Their proteolytic and lipolytic activities further contributed to the development of desirable sensory attributes57,58. Isolate selection was guided by acidification rate, enzymatic performance, and gastrointestinal stress resilience, with taxonomic identities confirmed by 16S rRNA gene sequencing. Sterilized, uninoculated milk served as the negative control. Similarly, sterilization of the rice substrate ensured that fermentation was driven solely by Napham isolates NAP/1–NAP/4. These isolates hydrolyzed rice starch into fermentable sugars and generated low levels of ethanol and other metabolites in the absence of yeast, while modulating pH and enhancing sensory characteristics. Their metabolic versatility underscores their suitability for traditional rice beverage fermentation. Unfermented sterile rice was used as the negative control. Collectively, the successful fermentation of both milk and rice substrates under controlled conditions demonstrates the translational potential of isolate NAP/4 for incorporation into industrial dairy- and cereal-based fermentation systems without requiring major process modifications.

The sensory evaluation revealed statistically significant differences among the curd and rice beverage samples, particularly with respect to aroma and texture, indicating meaningful variation across the formulations. To elucidate the biochemical basis underlying these organoleptic modifications, GC–MS analysis was performed on curd and rice beverage samples inoculated using NAP/4. In contrast, uninoculated control samples exhibited negligible or undetectable levels of volatile compounds, confirming that the flavor-active metabolites detected in the fermented products were primarily derived from the metabolic activity of isolate NAP/4. The GC–MS profiles revealed a diverse array of bioactive volatile compounds, including esters, aldehydes, alcohols, ketones, and organic acids, which are recognized as key contributors to the sensory characteristics of fermented foods. Notably, propionic acid, a metabolite commonly associated with cheese-like aroma notes, may have contributed to the pronounced umami attributes perceived during sensory evaluation. Additionally, oxalic acid likely imparted a citrus-like sourness, enhancing the tangy freshness of the final products, while heptane has been reported to contribute subtle sweet notes59. Collectively, these metabolites enriched the sensory profile with complex nuances, including citrus-sour, umami, and aromatic undertones, which were either absent or present only at trace levels in the control samples. Overall, the presence of these flavor-active metabolites corroborates the sensory analysis results and underscores the potential of isolate NAP/4 as a promising functional culture for the development of value-added fermented food products.

The current investigation underscores the functional potential and volatile metabolite profiles of microbial isolates obtained from ethnic fermented delicacies of Northeast India. Notably, isolate NAP/4 (Staphylococcus sp.) exhibited pronounced tolerance to acidic conditions, robust cell adhesion capacity, and the ability to generate prominent aroma-active compounds, thereby indicating its prospective application in the development of functional food products.

When benchmarked against established functional standards, isolate NAP/4 (Staphylococcus sp.) exhibited exceptional resilience across multiple physiological stress conditions. At pH 2.0, NAP/4 maintained a survival rate of 89.66 ± 1.08%. This value falls within ranges reported for Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus GG (typically ~50%) and Bifidobacterium animalis subsp. lactis BB-12 (80–85%) under similar experimental conditions60,61. Similarly, NAP/4 retained 55.17 ± 1.29% viability at 8% NaCl. This level of halotolerance is comparable to Tetragenococcus halophilus62 and some Lactobacillus species63. The isolate’s phenol tolerance (98.27 ± 0.83% at 0.4% phenol) markedly outperforms documented values for Lactobacillus salivarius UCC11852, and Pediococcus pentosaceus (60–63%), reflecting robust detoxification and stress-adaptation mechanisms52,53. Its high auto-aggregation capacity (68.94% after 5 h) and epithelial adhesion efficiency (65.28%) fall within ranges reported for selected food-associated fermentative bacteria, including Lactobacillus plantarum and Bacillus coagulans, which have been documented to exhibit gastrointestinal persistence under experimental conditions64,65. Collectively, these results indicate that NAP/4 demonstrates functional performance within ranges reported for food-associated fermentative isolates under comparable experimental conditions66–68, underscoring its potential as a candidate for further investigation in flavor-enhancing and techno-functional applications in controlled fermented food systems.

In addition to its functional attributes, isolate NAP/4 (Staphylococcus sp.) was found to synthesize multiple aroma-active volatile organic compounds, including propionic acid, oxalic acid, and heptane, which are recognized contributors to flavor development in fermented foods. The concurrent expression of functional attributes and the capacity for aroma-active metabolite production underscores the potential of NAP/4 as a multifunctional culture for the development of fermented foods with enhanced functional and flavor characteristics. Based on the GC–MS analysis, the volatile and non-volatile metabolites detected in the NAP/4–fermented samples can be interpreted in terms of their known odor and taste characteristics. Organic acids constituted a prominent group among the identified compounds and are recognized as key contributors to flavor development in fermented systems. Citric acid and oxalic acid, both odorless yet strongly acidic, are commonly associated with sharp and lemony sour taste attributes. Propionic acid, characterized by a rancid odor and sour, mildly cheese-like taste, is a well-documented metabolite in fermented dairy products and contributes to savory and complex flavor notes59. Oleic acid, exhibiting a peculiar lard-like odor and taste, may impart subtle fatty and mouth-coating characteristics, influencing overall flavor depth. Alcohols and related compounds were also detected and are known to enhance aroma complexity. The presence of 3-hexanol, with an alcohol-like odor and medicine-like taste, and cyclohexanol, described as camphor-like, indicates the formation of secondary alcohols during fermentation59. Oleyl alcohol, despite its faint odor, may contribute indirectly by modifying mouthfeel rather than acting as a dominant aroma compound. Amino acid–related compounds such as L-histidine, which is odorless but sweet-tasting, may contribute mild sweetness and act as a flavor-modulating component, particularly in acidic matrices. In addition, a series of hydrocarbons—including hexane, heptane, octane, decane, undecane, dodecane, tridecane, tetradecane, and octadecane—were identified. These compounds are typically associated with gasoline-like, fuel-like, or faint hydrocarbon odors and are often reported as background volatiles in fermented foods59. While they are not primary flavor determinants, certain hydrocarbons, such as heptane and tetradecane, have been associated with mild sweet or waxy notes when present at low concentrations. Collectively, the detected metabolites encompass organic acids, alcohols, amino acids, and hydrocarbons, reflecting diverse microbial metabolic pathways such as carbohydrate fermentation, amino acid catabolism, and lipid-derived metabolic pathways that are operative during fermentation69. The presence of these compounds, many of which are recognized flavor-active or flavor-modulating metabolites in fermented foods, supports the role of isolate NAP/4 in the biosynthesis of chemically diverse metabolites with relevance to fermented food applications.

While isolate NAP/4 demonstrates robust functional properties for both functional use and flavor enhancement, its translational relevance extends to practical food industry applications as well as broader socio-economic contexts. Based on its strong acidification capacity, enzymatic activity, gastrointestinal resilience, and aroma-generating potential, isolate NAP/4 may be deployed either as a defined starter or adjunct culture to enhance flavor complexity and functional value in existing dairy- and cereal-based fermentation systems. Beyond technological and functional attributes, traditional fermented foods are increasingly recognized for their role in supporting rural livelihoods, preserving indigenous knowledge, and enabling community-level entrepreneurship through localized food production and value addition70. Recent studies further emphasize that scientific valorization of traditional fermented foods—when coupled with standardized fermentation practices—can facilitate innovation-driven value addition, support small-scale food enterprises, and contribute to sustainable local economies71–73. Nevertheless, successful commercial application necessitates thorough evaluation of multiple scale-up factors, including preservation of cellular viability and metabolic functionality during large-scale cultivation, formulation stability over extended storage periods, and compliance with established food safety and regulatory frameworks (e.g., EFSA, FSSAI, FDA). Economic feasibility, particularly with respect to cost-effective growth substrates and the achievement of high production yields, also remains critical. Technological strategies such as lyophilization, microencapsulation, and bioreactor process optimization can substantially improve shelf-life, stability, and targeted delivery. Ultimately, pilot-scale validation combined with sensory evaluation in industrial food matrices will be essential to translate laboratory findings into commercially viable products, while ensuring batch-to-batch consistency in metabolite production and maintenance of isolate purity during repeated propagation cycles.

The present investigation provides the first comprehensive evidence that Napham, a traditional fermented food of Northeast India, serves as a reservoir of multifunctional microorganisms with relevance extending beyond conventional LAB–centered paradigms. Through an integrated microbiological, biochemical, molecular, and metabolomic framework, Staphylococcus sp. (NAP/4) was identified that uniquely combines robust functional attributes with pronounced aroma-generating capacity. The concurrent expression of stress tolerance, epithelial adhesion, preliminary in vitro biosafety assessment, and volatile metabolite production distinguishes NAP/4 as a dual-functional isolate with both techno-functional and technological value. This work contributes to broadening the current landscape and challenges the traditional taxonomic boundaries that have historically constrained the development of functional attributes. Application of NAP/4 in controlled dairy and cereal fermentations resulted in improved sensory scores and distinct GC–MS aroma profiles. These findings indicate preliminary translational potential and support further investigation toward its possible use as a defined starter or adjunct culture. Importantly, the systematic integration of biosafety assessment, functional performance, and metabolic output presented in this study provides a structured framework that may inform future food safety evaluations and regulatory decision-making for novel candidates for functional starter culture. This integrated approach is especially relevant for regulatory authorities evaluating the safety and functional suitability of microbial isolates for food use. Overall, the findings underscore the biotechnological value of indigenous fermented food Napham and highlight its potential to drive innovation in functional research, sustainable industrial fermentation, and culturally rooted functional food development.

Microbial isolates exhibiting tolerance to gastrointestinal stress conditions and adhesion capacity are increasingly investigated for their potential functional roles in fermented food systems. However, regulatory recognition as a probiotic requires comprehensive genomic and in vivo validation. Although the safety assessment conducted in the present study was comprehensive, it was limited to in vitro evaluations; therefore, whole-genome sequencing in combination with toxicological investigations using appropriate model systems will be necessary to meet regulatory expectations and to confirm safety at both molecular and genetic levels. Moreover, the functional efficacy of the selected isolates was not examined in animal or human models, which restricts definitive conclusions regarding gastrointestinal colonization, immunomodulatory activity, and long-term health benefits. Direct assessment of antagonistic activity against enteric pathogens was not performed; however, the antibiotic susceptibility profiles generated provide valuable insight into the functional attributes and safety of the isolates. In addition, GC–MS analysis was confined to a limited set of volatile compounds, and the application of a broader, untargeted metabolomics strategy could reveal additional bioactive metabolites of functional or sensory relevance. Finally, although OD₆₀₀ measurements were employed as a convenient indicator of microbial growth and stress tolerance, future studies will incorporate CFU–based enumeration to enable more accurate viability assessment and to strengthen the evaluation of functional attributes.

Based on the present findings, several future research directions are warranted to further substantiate and extend the applicability of the study outcomes. Whole-genome sequencing of isolate N1 should be undertaken to comprehensively elucidate the genetic determinants underlying its probiotic functionality, while simultaneously screening for putative virulence factors and antimicrobial resistance genes to ensure biosafety. In vivo safety assessments and gastrointestinal colonization studies employing rodent models or controlled human trials are necessary to validate functional attributes. Furthermore, the performance of the isolates should be investigated in non-dairy substrates, including plant-based foods and functional beverages, to ascertain broader industrial applicability. Culture-independent microbiome-level investigations, encompassing metagenomic sequencing and targeted quantitative PCR assays using Staphylococcus, LAB, and yeast-specific primers, should be implemented to determine microbial prevalence, ecological abundance, and habitat specificity during Napham fermentation. Additional validation using human intestinal epithelial cell models, such as Caco-2 and HT-29, is recommended to assess adhesion, colonization potential, and host–microbe interactions in a physiologically relevant gastrointestinal context. Finally, detailed acidification kinetics should be examined in future studies using milk as a substrate under controlled fermentation conditions to better characterize fermentation dynamics and functional performance.

The cold chain was meticulously preserved to ensure microbial survival, and each sample was processed within 4 h of acquisition.

Isolation and cultivation of microbial isolates

Every Napham sample was homogenized in sterile physiological (isotonic) saline solution (0.85% NaCl) to maintain osmotic balance and preserve bacterial viability. Serial dilutions ranging from 10⁻¹ to 10⁻⁶ were then prepared from the homogenized samples. From each dilution, 100 µL aliquots were spread in triplicate onto Nutrient Agar (NAM) (HiMedia, India) for enumeration of total viable bacteria, and de Man, Rogosa, and Sharpe (MRS) agar (HiMedia, India) for the selective isolation of LAB. The inoculated plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24–48 h to facilitate optimal microbial growth and colony development. Following incubation, colonies exhibiting distinct morphological characteristics including shape (circular or irregular), size (small, medium, large), pigmentation, margin (entire, undulate, or lobate), elevation (flat, raised, or convex), and surface appearance (smooth or rough) were selected and repeatedly sub-cultured on fresh agar media plates to obtain pure axenic isolates. Only well-isolated colonies displaying consistent morphology across replicates were re-streaked to obtain axenic cultures. This approach ensured the selection of phenotypically diverse and stable isolates while avoiding redundant recovery of morphologically similar colonies. The purified isolates were preserved in NAM and MRS broth (HiMedia, India) to assess growth consistency and stability upon successive sub-culturing. The preliminary phenotypic characterization was performed based on colony morphology and growth characteristics in accordance with taxonomic criteria outlined in Bergey’s Manual of Determinative Bacteriology35. Isolates exhibiting robust and stable growth were selected for subsequent functional and safety assessments. For long-term preservation, cultures were maintained on NAM agar slants at 4 °C and stored in 20% glycerol at –80 °C for subsequent analyses.

Morphological and biochemical profiling of isolates

Preliminary identification of the isolates was performed by closely examining colony morphology, followed by Gram staining75, and conventional biochemical assays such as catalase and oxidase tests76. These assessments facilitated the distinction between Gram-negative and Gram-positive bacteria, while providing insight into metabolic activities potentially involved in the synthesis of flavor compounds. Data gathered from morphological and biochemical analyses guided the selection of candidate isolates for further functional validations.

Characterization of enzymatic activities contributing to flavor development

The microbial isolates were screened for enzyme-related activities pertinent to flavor enhancement in fermented food products. Protease activity was assessed using skim milk agar (HiMedia, India), with the emergence of clear halos signifying hydrolysis of casein40,41. Lipolytic capability was tested with the help of tributyrin agar (HiMedia, India), where the formation of clear zones surrounding colonies indicated fat breakdown42. Carbohydrate fermentation potential was analyzed using phenol red broth supplemented with various sugars—sucrose, glucose, arabinose, mannitol and lactose—utilizing Durham tubes to detect gas and acid production. Positive findings revealed active sugar catabolism43. These enzymatic properties act as essential markers of the isolates’ capacity to contribute to flavor development in fermented foods57,58.

Biosafety evaluation of isolates

To ensure the safety of microbial isolates proposed for use in culinary products, comprehensive in vitro evaluations were conducted to detect possible virulence attributes, such as hemolytic activity, deoxyribonuclease (DNase) production, and antibiotic susceptibility.

The hemolytic property of each isolate was assessed using blood agar plates to examine their cytotoxic effects. Following isolation, colonies were streaked onto 5% sheep blood agar and maintained at 37 °C for 24 h in a B.O.D. incubator to assess hemolytic activity. Hemolysis was categorized as α (partial), β (complete), or γ (absent). Isolates exhibiting α- or β-hemolytic reactions were considered potentially harmful and were eliminated from subsequent functional screening77.

Deoxyribonuclease (DNase) activity was evaluated on DNase agar containing toluidine blue (HiMedia, India). After incubating for 48 h at 37 °C, transparent zones surrounding the colonies were indicative of DNase activity. Isolates lacking such clear zones were deemed DNase-negative and regarded as non-pathogenic78.

The antibiotic susceptibility pattern of each isolate was determined using the Kirby–Bauer disk diffusion method, following the guidelines of the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI, 2024)36,79. Bacterial inocula were uniformly spread onto NAM plates, and antibiotic-impregnated disks containing a range of therapeutically significant antibiotics—commonly prescribed for treating infections caused by Gram-positive bacteria—were carefully placed on the medium’s surface. The antibiotics tested included Linezolid (30 µg), Ciprofloxacin (5 µg), Azithromycin (30 µg), Gentamicin (50 µg), Ofloxacin (2 µg), Ampicillin (2 µg), Amikacin (30 µg), Levofloxacin (5 µg), Neomycin (30 µg), Imipenem (10 µg), Chloramphenicol (50 µg), and Moxifloxacin (5 µg). These antibiotics were selected for their high clinical relevance, encompassing major therapeutic classes—fluoroquinolones, aminoglycosides, macrolides, β-lactams, and oxazolidinones—which are frequently recommended for treating respiratory, urinary, gastrointestinal, skin, and bloodstream infections80. Following incubation at 37 °C for 18–24 h, the inhibition zone diameters were measured and interpreted according to CLSI breakpoint guidelines81. Antimicrobial susceptibility profiles (R–Resistant; S–Susceptible) were evaluated in accordance with CLSI M100, 34th Edition (2024). Breakpoints for zone diameters were applied based on antibiotic-specific standards, with zones measuring ≥16–21 mm categorized as Susceptible (S) and ≤12–15 mm as Resistant (R), depending on the specific antimicrobial agent. Only isolates demonstrating susceptibility to essential clinical antibiotics were considered suitable for further analysis.

Selection of isolates

Isolates exhibiting pronounced protease, lipase, and carbohydrate fermentation capabilities, together with negative hemolytic and DNase activities and sensitivity to key therapeutic antibiotics, were preliminarily selected for further evaluation. These shortlisted isolates were subsequently subjected to comprehensive functional characterization, and were then introduced into food matrices such as curd and rice beverage to assess their direct influence on flavor, aroma, and overall sensory attributes of the fermented products.

All pipettes and glassware were disinfected before application. The adhesive capacity of the microbial isolates was assessed as per the procedure with minimal adjustments85. Starter culture application in fermented foods

Starter culture inocula were prepared from freshly grown bacterial cultures to ensure high metabolic activity and reproducibility across fermentation trials. Each selected isolate was cultivated in nutrient broth (NAM) at 37 °C for 24–48 h until reaching the exponential growth phase. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 10 min, washed twice with sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove residual growth medium, and resuspended in sterile PBS. The cell density was adjusted to approximately 1 × 10⁸ CFU/mL by spectrophotometric measurement (OD₆₀₀) and confirmed by viable plate counts on NAM agar. These standardized suspensions were used immediately as inocula for curd and rice beverage fermentation experiments.

To assess the technological applicability of promising isolates, each isolate was evaluated as a starter culture for the preparation of curd. The evaluation of organoleptic properties and acceptability of fermented curd and rice beverage prepared with the shortlisted isolates were assessed through sensory analysis. A group of eight trained panelists (equally representing both genders, aged 20–30 years; mean age: 24) voluntarily participated following a review of the Participant Information Sheet and the provision of written informed consent, in accordance with ethical guidelines. The panel size was selected in accordance with established sensory evaluation guidelines (ISO 8586 and ASTM E1958). The protocol for sensory testing received ethical approval from the Research Integrity and Ethics Review Board of Assam Don Bosco University, Tapesia Gardens, Assam, India, having Reference No. ADBU-RIERB/25/2. A 1-h training session prior to assessment was conducted for the panelists, introducing them to sensory descriptors and the scoring procedure with reference standards to ensure uniformity and reliability in evaluations. Sensory evaluations were performed in three replicates under standardized environmental settings (25 ± 2 °C, stable humidity conditions, white illumination) in individual compartments to control environmental factors on panelist responses. Fermented samples were served in a randomized sequence in sterile, coded, single-use polystyrene vessels. To preserve double-blind conditions and mitigate bias, neither the assessors nor the sample administrators were informed of the microbial isolates utilized in the preparation of the curd and rice beverage. Curd samples (20–30 mL) were presented at 10 ± 2 °C, and rice beverage samples (20–30 mL) at 8 ± 2 °C to ensure accurate sensory assessment.

To prevent cross-flavor contamination, panelists cleansed their palates with lukewarm water and consumed unsalted soda crackers between samples. Organoleptic parameters assessed included visual appeal, aroma, flavor, consistency, mouthfeel, and overall acceptability. The assessment was guided by a structured sensory evaluation form, employing a 10-point hedonic scale, provided as Supplementary File 1. This standardized scale is widely used in consumer preference studies, enabling panelists to rate their liking for various features of a product. Panelists rated samples on a 10-point hedonic scale, with 1 indicating “dislike extremely” and 10 indicating “like extremely” 92.

Overall ratings were expressed in terms of mean ± standard deviation (SD) across panelists and replicates. Isolates demonstrating superior sensory performance in curd and rice beverage preparations were designated as the most suitable starter culture candidates. Differences among groups were evaluated with the help of one-way ANOVA, and multiple comparisons were carried out using Tukey’s post hoc test. A threshold of p < 0.05 was used to determine statistical significance.

Analysis of volatile compounds

Volatile metabolite analysis of fermented and unfermented curd and rice beverage was performed via GC–MS to characterize the aroma-active compounds generated by the selected microbial isolates. One-milliliter portions of homogenized fermented curd and rice beverage were extracted using liquid–liquid partitioning with ethyl acetate in a 1:1 (v/v) proportion. Resulting mixtures were subjected to centrifugation at 5000 rpm for 5 min to isolate the organic phase. For improved volatility and chromatographic performance, derivatization was carried out using a 3:1:1 (v/v/v) combination of BSTFA, pyridine, and ethyl acetate. The treated extracts were subsequently incubated overnight at 37 °C.

The derivatized extracts were subjected to analysis on a Clarus 680 GC interfaced with a Clarus 600C MS (PerkinElmer, USA) using an Elite-5MS capillary column (60 m × 0.25 mm ID × 0.25 μm film thickness). The GC oven program commenced at 60 °C for 3 min, then increased to 200 °C at 5 °C/min and held for 3 min, followed by a rapid ramp to 300 °C at 60 °C/min and maintained for 10 min. A 1 µL volume of each sample was injected through a programmable split/splitless injector maintained at 280 °C with a 20:1 split ratio. Mass spectrometric analysis was performed in EI mode at 70 eV using helium as the carrier gas at 1 mL/min, scanning over a range of 50–600 m/z.

Metabolites were annotated using TurboMass software (PerkinElmer, USA) by matching spectra against the NIST 2014 Mass Spectral Library. Compounds with a library match score of ≥90% were considered. Based on odor descriptors documented in food chemistry references, volatile analytes were classified59. While retention indices (RI) were not determined owing to the absence of n-alkane standards, consistent signals across replicate analyses were emphasized. The volatiles detected comprised esters, alcohols, ketones, and organic acids, each associated with sweet, fruity, umami, and citrus-like sensory notes. Overall, the results demonstrate the metabolic potential of the selected isolates to generate a wide range of aroma-active compounds, thereby improving the organoleptic properties of fermented products.

Molecular identification and phylogenetic analysis

Identification of the selected bacterial isolates at the genus level was carried out using 16S rRNA gene sequencing following preliminary morphological and biochemical characterization. For molecular identification, the isolates were submitted to Biokart India Pvt. Ltd., Bengaluru, for sequencing analysis. Genomic DNA was extracted using the Xploregen™ DNA Extraction Kit, following the manufacturer’s instructions with minor modifications to enhance DNA yield and purity. Briefly, 1 ml of Xploregen gDNA Extraction Buffer™ 1 was added to a beaded vial containing the bacterial sample, and the mixture was horizontally vortexed at maximum speed for 10 min to ensure effective cell lysis. Subsequently, 300 μl of Xploregen gDNA Extraction Buffer™ 2 was added, followed by vortexing for an additional 7 min. The lysate was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 3 min at room temperature, and 800 μl of the resulting supernatant was carefully transferred to a sterile 2 ml microcentrifuge tube.

To this supernatant, 200 μl of Xploregen gDNA Extraction Buffer™ 3 was added and briefly vortexed, followed by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 2 min. Again, 800 μl of the supernatant was transferred to a fresh sterile 2 ml tube, mixed with 1 ml of Xploregen gDNA Extraction Buffer™ 4, and briefly vortexed. Aliquots of 700 μl of the lysate were then loaded onto a spin column, and this step was repeated until the entire lysate was processed. The column was washed sequentially with 600 μl of Xploregen gDNA Extraction Buffer™ 5 and Buffer™ 6, each followed by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 2 min. An additional dry spin was performed for 5 min at 10,000 rpm to remove residual wash buffer. The spin column was subsequently placed into a sterile 1.5 ml tube, and genomic DNA was eluted by adding 30 μl of Xploregen gDNA Extraction Buffer™ 7 directly to the center of the membrane, followed by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 5 min. The eluted DNA was collected, and all elution fractions were retained for downstream applications.

The extracted genomic DNA was used as a template for polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification of the 16S rRNA gene using universal primers: 16S Forward (GGATGAGCCCGCGGCCTA) and 16S Reverse (CGGTGTGTACAAGGCCCGG). Each PCR reaction was performed in a final volume of 50 μl containing 1 μl of template DNA, 2 μl each of forward and reverse primers, 4 μl of 2.5 mM dNTPs, 10 μl of 10× Taq DNA polymerase assay buffer, 1 μl of Taq DNA polymerase (3 U/ml), and nuclease-free Milli-Q water to volume. The amplification protocol consisted of an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 180 s, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 60 s, annealing at 50 °C for 60 s, and extension at 72 °C for 120 s, with a final extension step at 72 °C for 420 s. The PCR products, yielding fragments of approximately 1300–1400 bp, were resolved on 1.2% agarose gels, stained with ethidium bromide, and visualized under UV illumination to confirm successful amplification93. Forward- and reverse-strand PCR products were purified prior to sequencing.

The obtained 16S rRNA gene sequences were analyzed using nucleotide BLAST for similarity searches against the NCBI GenBank database. Identification at the species level was assigned when sequence similarity of ≥99% was observed with a reference isolate sequence. Multiple sequence alignments were performed using phylogenetic analysis software, and phylogenetic trees were constructed in MEGA X using distance-based methods. Genetic distances were calculated using the Jukes–Cantor distance correction model, and the robustness of the inferred phylogenetic relationships was evaluated by bootstrap analysis with 1000 resampling iterations. The validated 16S rRNA gene sequence corresponding to the selected isolate was subsequently deposited in the GenBank database under an assigned accession number.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were carried out in triplicate. All data are reported as mean ± SD, and statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 30 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The Shapiro–Wilk test assessed data normality. Group differences were evaluated using Kruskal–Wallis and one-way ANOVA, with Tukey’s HSD applied for post hoc comparisons. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered significant.
