Rainbow Trees in Hypercubes
Nicholas Crawford, Maya Sankar, Carl Schildkraut, Sam Spiro

Abstract
We prove that every proper edge-coloring of the n n -dimensional hypercube Q n Q_{n} contains a rainbow copy of every tree T T on at most n n edges. This result is best possible, as Q n Q_{n} can be properly edge-colored using only n n colors while avoiding rainbow cycles.



1
Introduction

One of the most well-studied graphs is the
n
-dimensional hypercube

Q_{n}
, whose vertex set consists of elements of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{n}
, with two vertices adjacent if and only if they differ in exactly one coordinate.

Motivated by problems related to Schrijver’s Conjecture and the rainbow Turán problem (which we define in greater detail below), the first author, King, and the fourth author
[
4
]
initiated the study of which rainbow subgraphs can appear in a properly edge-colored subgraphs of the hypercube. Specifically, the simplest case of the main conjectures made in
[
4
]
is the following, where here a
rainbow
subgraph of an edge-colored graph
G
is a subgraph of
G
where every edge has a distinct color.

Conjecture 1.1

(
[
4
]
)
.

Every proper edge-coloring of the
n
-dimensional hypercube contains a rainbow copy of every tree
T
with at most
n
edges.

1.1
is best possible, in the sense that if
H
is not a tree with at most
n
edges, then
Q_{n}
possesses an edge-coloring without a rainbow copy of
H
. Indeed, consider the Cayley edge-coloring of
Q_{n}
, i.e., the coloring where an edge
uv
whose vertices differ on their
i
th coordinates is given color
i
. This coloring has only
n
colors and no rainbow cycles.

1.1
was proven for a few classes of trees in
[
4
]
, specifically for trees which either contain many leaves or which contain a long pendant path. In this article, we give a full resolution of
1.1
by proving the following stronger statement, which appeared as
[
4
, Conjecture 3]
. Here and throughout,
\delta(G)
denotes the minimum degree of the graph
G
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
G
be a properly edge-colored subgraph of a hypercube
Q_{n}
. Then, for every tree
T
with at most
\delta(G)
edges, there exists a rainbow copy of
T
in
G
.

Our proof of
1.2
proceeds by a delicate induction argument, resulting in a more general (albeit more technical) statement
3.2
. Although the precise statement of
3.2
is too involved to state here, it informally says that any sufficiently “well-dispersed” embedding of the first half of
E(T)
can be extended to a rainbow embedding of all of
T
.
Such an embedding of the first half of
E(T)
can be constructed relatively easily, as discussed in
2.5
.

1.1
Related Results and Historical Context

A growing interest within extremal combinatorics is to study sufficient conditions for combinatorial objects to contain “rainbow” substructures. Here we highlight a few problems around the theme of finding rainbow subgraphs in properly edge-colored graphs, and we refer the interested reader to the surveys
[
12
,
15
]
for a more complete overview of this topic.

Perhaps the most famous problem of this form is the Ryser–Brualdi–Stein conjecture. The usual phrasing of this conjecture states that every Latin square of order
n
contains a transversal of size
n-1
, with the existence of a transversal of size
n
being guaranteed whenever
n
is odd. Equivalently, this conjecture says that any proper edge-coloring of
K_{n,n}
using
n
colors contains a rainbow matching of size
n-1
, with a rainbow matching of size
n
existing whenever
n
is odd. A large number of partial results have been obtained for this problem, such as work of Shor
[
14
]
in 1982 showing the existence of transversals of size
n-O(\log^{2}n)
, though this work contained an error which was later corrected by Hatami and Shor in 2008
[
6
]
. This bound stood for many years until recent work of Keevash, Pokrovskiy, Sudakov, and Yepremyan
[
9
]
who in 2020 proved the existence of transversals of size
n-O(\log n/\log\log n)
. Very recently, breakthrough work of Montgomery
[
11
]
verified the Ryser–Brualdi–Stein conjecture for all large even
n
. We refer readers to
[
11
]
for the most up-to-date information on the Ryser–Brualdi–Stein conjecture.

Another problem in a similar spirit is Graham’s rearrangement conjecture from 1971, which says that for any prime
p
and
a_{1},\ldots,a_{d}
non-zero distinct elements of
\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}
, there exists a rearrangement of the elements
a_{i_{1}},\ldots,a_{i_{d}}
such that the partial sums
\sum_{j=1}^{t}a_{i_{j}}
are all distinct. By translating this problem appropriately, one can show that this conjecture is equivalent to showing that a certain class of edge-colored directed graphs, or
digraphs
, contain long rainbow directed paths. In particular, Graham’s rearrangement conjecture, as well as various strengthenings thereof, would be implied by the following question asked very recently in 2025 by Bucić, Frederickson, Müyesser, Pokrovskiy, and Yepremyan
[
3
]
; here a proper edge-coloring of a digraph is one in which no two directed edges sharing either a common source or a common sink are given the same color.

Question 1.3

(
[
3
]
)
.

If
D
is a properly edge-colored digraph such that every vertex has both in-degree and out-degree equal to
d
, does
D
contain a rainbow directed path of length
d-1
?

This question in turn is a significant strengthening of the following conjecture of Schrijver
[
13
]
which itself is a strengthening of a conjecture of Andersen
[
2
]
who made the same statement only for the case
G=K_{n}
.

Conjecture 1.4

(Schrijver
[
13
]
)
.

If
G
is a properly edge-colored
d
-regular graph, then
G
contains a rainbow path of length
d-1
.

In both these problems the quantity
d-1
is best possible due to a construction of Maamoun and Meyniel
[
10
]
, the simplest version of which starts by taking the hypercube
Q_{d-1}
, equipped with the Cayley edge-coloring described above, and then adding all of the diagonals of
Q_{d-1}
as edges in a
d
th color. Schrijver’s conjecture has been asymptotically resolved by Bucić, Frederickson, Müyesser, Pokrovskiy, and Yepremyan
[
3
]
, and we refer the interested reader to their paper for more on Graham’s rearrangement conjecture and on problems related to finding rainbow paths in graphs and digraphs.

The last related problem we discuss in full is the rainbow Turán problem, which was originally initiated by Keevash, Mubayi, Sudakov, and Verstraëte
[
8
]
in 2007. Given a family of graphs
\mathcal{F}
, let
\mathrm{ex}^{*}(n,\mathcal{F})
denote the maximum number of edges in an
n
-vertex graph
G
admitting a proper edge-coloring containing no element of
\mathcal{F}
as a rainbow subgraph. For example, the hypercube
Q_{d}
together with its Cayley edge-coloring contains no rainbow cycle, so taking
d\approx\log n
implies
\mathrm{ex}^{*}(n,\mathcal{C})=\Omega(n\log n)
where
\mathcal{C}
denotes the set of all cycles. Determining a corresponding upper bound for
\mathrm{ex}^{*}(n,\mathcal{C})
has been a significant area of study and, after a number of preceding works
[
5
,
16
,
7
]
, was largely solved in 2025 by Alon, Bucić, Sauermann, Zakharov, and Zamir
[
1
]
who showed both that
\mathrm{ex}^{*}(n,\mathcal{C})\leq n(\log n)^{1+o(1)}
as well as some interesting connections between this problem and additive number theory.

Our main problem of interest for this paper,
1.1
arose from trying to find a rainbow analog of the Erdős–Sós Conjecture from extremal graph theory, which states that every tree
T
on
d
edges has the same Turán number as the star on
d
edges. The most natural analog of this conjecture, namely that
\mathrm{ex}^{*}(n,T)=\mathrm{ex}^{*}(n,K_{1,d})
for every tree
T
on
d
edges, is false essentially due to the same construction of Maamoun and Meyniel
[
10
]
for Schrijver’s conjecture mentioned above. To circumvent this, the authors in
[
4
]
considered a relative rainbow Turán number
\mathrm{ex}^{*}(G,T)
, which is defined to be the maximum number of edges in a subgraph
G^{\prime}\subseteq G
which can be properly edge-colored to avoid rainbow copies of
T
. It was then asked in
[
4
]
which host graphs
G
have the property that
\mathrm{ex}^{*}(G,T)=\mathrm{ex}^{*}(G,K_{1,d})
for all
d
-edge trees. In particular, it was conjectured that the hypercube might have this property, with the motivation for this particular choice of host coming both from the fact that the Maamoun–Meyniel construction shows that graphs slightly denser than the hypercube can fail to have this property, as well as from the canonical coloring of the hypercube showing that
\mathrm{ex}^{*}(Q_{d},F)
is only interesting to study when
F
does not contain a cycle. Our main result
1.2
can thus be seen as a minimum degree analog of the conjecture that
\mathrm{ex}^{*}(Q_{n},T)=\mathrm{ex}^{*}(Q_{n},K_{1,d})
for all
d
-edge trees
T
.

2
Preliminaries

In this section we introduce some of the necessary definitions and basic lemmas for our main arguments. For convenience, we split our discussion into three parts: one focused on the high-level proof ideas and basic observations that we use, one on properties of trees, and one on embeddings into the hypercube.

2.1
Proof Ideas

In this subsection we discuss some of the high-level proof ideas of our argument. For this, the following basic definitions will be useful.

Definition 2.1

(Infinite hypercube)
.

The (infinite)
hypercube

Q_{\infty}
is the graph whose vertex set is
\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}
and whose edge set is given by connecting two vertices
x,y
if they differ in exactly one coordinate. Given an edge
e:=xy\in E(Q_{\infty})
, the
coordinate
of
e
is the unique
i\in\mathbb{N}
at which
x
and
y
differ.

Throughout this paper we are solely concerned with finite subgraphs of
Q_{\infty}
, and as such we could alternatively consider each of our graphs as subgraphs of some finite hypercube
Q_{N}
(with vertex set
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{N}
) rather than
Q_{\infty}
. We use
Q_{\infty}
throughout to emphasize that the dimensionality of the ambient hypercube in which we are working has no importance.

Definition 2.2

(Homomorphisms and embeddings)
.

Given two graphs
F,G
, a map
\phi\colon V(F)\to V(G)
is a
homomorphism
if
\phi
maps edges of
F
to edges of
G
; that is, if for every edge
xy\in E(F)
we have
\phi(x)\phi(y)\in E(G)
. A homomorphism
\phi
is called an
embedding
if it is injective.

We frequently abuse notation by writing
\phi(xy)
for an edge
xy\in E(F)
to denote the edge
\phi(x)\phi(y)
of
G
. Similarly, given a set of edges
E\subseteq E(F)
we write
\phi(E):=\{\phi(e):e\in E\}
. We often write
\phi\colon F\to G
rather than the more precise
\phi\colon V(F)\to V(G)
for ease of notation.

Ultimately we aim to show that if
G\subseteq Q_{\infty}
is a properly edge-colored graph and
T
is a tree with
e(T)\geq\delta(G)
, then there exists a homomorphism
\phi\colon T\to G
which is both an embedding and has the property that distinct edges of
T
map to edges of
G
with distinct colors. The distinct colors condition alone is easy to ensure by iteratively constructing
\phi
to avoid previously used colors, so in some sense our main challenge will be defining
\phi
so that distinct vertices of
T
map to distinct vertices of
G
. We will do this via the following key lemma regarding the geometry of the hypercube.

Observation 2.3

(Non-closedness of walks in
Q_{\infty}
)
.

Let
x_{0}x_{1}\cdots x_{m}
be a walk in
Q_{\infty}
. If the number of distinct coordinates used by the
m
edges
x_{0}x_{1},\ldots,x_{m-1}x_{m}
is strictly more than
m/2
, then
x_{0}\neq x_{m}
.

Proof.

By the pigeonhole principle, there must be some coordinate
j
which is the coordinate of exactly one edge
x_{i-1}x_{i}
with
1\leq i\leq m
. This implies that

(x_{0})_{j}=\cdots=(x_{i-1})_{j}\neq(x_{i})_{j}=\cdots=(x_{m})_{j},

proving that
x_{0}
and
x_{m}
are distinct vertices.
∎

Hence, to ensure that two vertices
u,v
of
T
map to distinct vertices of
Q_{\infty}
under a given homomorphism
\phi\colon T\to Q_{\infty}
, it suffices to show that the edge set of the unique path from
u
to
v
in
T
is mapped to a set of edges of
Q_{\infty}
encompassing strictly more than
\mathrm{dist}_{T}(u,v)/2
distinct coordinates.

Motivated by the above, we aim to iteratively construct the homomorphism
\phi\colon T\to G
in a manner that never repeats colors (which is necessary for the image of
\phi
to be rainbow) and in a manner that avoids repeating coordinates as much as possible (with the intent to apply
2.3
to prove that
\phi
is an embedding). The following observation quantifies how much we can repeat avoiding coordinates.

Observation 2.4

(Extending a rainbow homomorphism)
.

Let
G
be a properly edge-colored subgraph of
Q_{\infty}
and fix a vertex
x\in V(G)
. Let
X_{\mathrm{col}}
be a finite subset of the colors used by edges of
G
and
X_{\mathrm{coor}}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
a finite set of coordinates. Suppose that there are at least
r
neighbors
x^{\prime}
of
x
in
G
such that the edge
xx^{\prime}
has color in
X_{\mathrm{col}}
and coordinate in
X_{\mathrm{coor}}
. If
\lvert X_{\mathrm{coor}}\rvert+\lvert X_{\mathrm{col}}\rvert-r<\delta(G)
then there is an edge
xy\in E(G)
with color not in
X_{\mathrm{col}}
and coordinate not in
X_{\mathrm{coor}}
.

We always apply
2.4
in the context of extending a homomorphism
\phi^{\prime}\colon(T-w)\to G
to a homomorphism
\phi\colon T\to G
, where
w
is a leaf of a tree
T
. Writing
v\in V(T)
for the sole neighbor of
w
, one should interpret the vertex
x
in
2.4
as the image
\phi^{\prime}(v)
and the vertex
y
as a potential choice for
\phi(w)
so that the edge
\phi(vw)
avoids a set of forbidden colors
X_{\mathrm{col}}
, representing the colors of edges used by
\phi(T-w)
, together with a set of forbidden coordinates
X_{\mathrm{coor}}
.

Proof.

Let
N(x)
be the set of neighbors of
x
in
G
. Further, let
N_{\mathrm{col}}(x)
denote the set of neighbors
x^{\prime}
with
xx^{\prime}
using a color in
X_{\mathrm{col}}
, and define
N_{\mathrm{coor}}(x)
similarly. We observe that

\displaystyle\lvert N(x)\setminus(N_{\mathrm{col}}(x)\cup N_{\mathrm{coor}}(x))\rvert

\displaystyle=\lvert N(x)\rvert-\lvert N_{\mathrm{col}}(x)\rvert-\lvert N_{\mathrm{coor}}(x)\rvert+\lvert N_{\mathrm{col}}(x)\cap N_{\mathrm{coor}}(x)\rvert

\displaystyle\geq\delta(G)-\lvert X_{\mathrm{coor}}\rvert-\lvert X_{\mathrm{col}}\rvert+r\geq 1,

where the first inequality used both that
G
has a proper edge coloring to imply that at most one neighbor of
x
uses any given color from
X_{\mathrm{col}}
, and also that
G\subseteq Q_{\infty}
implies that at most one neighbor of
x
uses any given coordinate from
X_{\mathrm{coor}}
. The inequality above implies that there exists a neighbor
y
of
x
satisfying the desired properties.
∎

Remark 2.5
.

For trees
T
and graphs
G
with
e(T)=\delta(G)
, iterated applications of
2.4
with
r=0
allows us to build a homomorphism which maps approximately half the edges of
T
into
G
while avoiding any repetition of colors or coordinates.

2.5
motivates the primary strategy for our proof: given a tree
T
and an edge-colored graph
G\subseteq Q_{\infty}
into which we aim to embed
T
, we first strategically pick some subtree
T^{\prime}\subseteq T
with about half as many edges as
T
and then embed
T^{\prime}
into
G
without any repetitions in coordinates or colors. From here, we order the remaining vertices of
T
in some clever way and iteratively embed each vertex while avoiding all previously used colors as well as a carefully chosen set of previously used coordinates (which notably can be no larger than the set guaranteed in
2.4
) at each step. While this proof ends up being entirely elementary, we emphasize that the exact choices of the subtree
T^{\prime}
, the ordering of the remaining vertices, and the set of forbidden coordinates to choose at each step are extremely delicate.

Before closing this subsection, we record the
r=1
case of
2.4
separately, as we make repeated use of this special case.

Observation 2.6

(Extending a rainbow homomorphism, special case)
.

Let
G
be a properly edge-colored subgraph of
Q_{\infty}
and fix a vertex
x\in V(G)
. Let
X_{\mathrm{col}}
be a finite subset of the colors used by edges of
G
and
X_{\mathrm{coor}}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
a finite set of coordinates, and suppose there is an edge
xx^{\prime}\in E(G)
incident to
x
whose color is in
X_{\mathrm{col}}
and whose coordinate is in
X_{\mathrm{coor}}
. If
\lvert X_{\mathrm{col}}\rvert+\lvert X_{\mathrm{coor}}\rvert\leq\delta(G)
then there is an edge
xy\in E(G)
incident to
x
with color not in
X_{\mathrm{col}}
and coordinate not in
X_{\mathrm{coor}}
.

2.2
Trees

For the remainder of the paper we will consider all trees
T
to be rooted, with the root vertex typically denoted as
v_{0}
unless stated otherwise. We now establish some structural definitions of trees.

Definition 2.7

(Properties of trees)
.

Given a tree
T
with root
v_{0}
, we define the following notions.

\bullet
5pt

For a vertex
v\in V(T)
, the
descendants
of
v
are the vertices
w\in V(T)
for which the unique path between
v_{0}
and
w
in
T
contains
v
.

\bullet
5pt

A
leaf
is a vertex of
T
with no descendants other than itself. Notably,
v_{0}
is not considered to be a leaf unless
T
consists of a single vertex.

\bullet
5pt

For a vertex
v\in V(T)
, we write
T(v)
for the subtree of
T
rooted at
v
induced by its descendants (including
v
). We write
T-v
for the subtree of
T
rooted at
v_{0}
induced by the vertex set
V(T)\setminus V(T(v))
.

\bullet
5pt

For a vertex
v\in V(T)
, the
level

\lambda(v)
of
v
is defined to be the distance between
v
and
v_{0}
, i.e.
\lambda(v):=\mathrm{dist}_{T}(v,v_{0})
. We define

\lambda_{\max}(v):=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{descendants}\\
w\text{ of }v\text{ in }T\end{subarray}}\lambda(w)

to be the greatest level among all of the descendants of
v
.

\bullet
5pt

Define
\lfloor T/2\rfloor
and
\lceil T/2\rceil
to be the subtrees of
T
rooted at
v_{0}
induced by the vertex sets

\displaystyle V(\lfloor T/2\rfloor)

\displaystyle=\{v\in V(T):\lambda(v)\leq\lfloor\lambda_{\max}(v)/2\rfloor\}\text{ and}

\displaystyle V(\lceil T/2\rceil)

\displaystyle=\{v\in V(T):\lambda(v)\leq\lceil\lambda_{\max}(v)/2\rceil\}.

In other words,
\lfloor T/2\rfloor
is the union of the first halves of all paths from
v_{0}
to a leaf of
T
, excluding the middle edges of odd-length paths, and
\lceil T/2\rceil
is the same with middle edges of odd-length paths included. We include an example of this in
Figure

2
.

Ultimately, our embedding of
T
will be constructed by starting with an embedding of
\lfloor T/2\rfloor
. Because of this, our embedding will be hardest to complete whenever

is large, as this means there will be more restrictions in the colors we need to avoid after embedding the edges in
\lfloor T/2\rfloor
. As such, it is useful to characterize which trees have

as large as possible. Our characterization relies on spiders, which informally are trees formed by subdividing the edges of a star

. More precisely we work with the following.

Definition 2.8

(Spiders)
.

A
spider
is a rooted tree
T
with root
v_{0}
for which
\deg(v)\leq 2
for every
v\in V(T)\setminus\{v_{0}\}
. For each leaf
w
of
T
, the unique path from
v_{0}
to
w
is called a
leg
of
T
; the legs partition the edge set of

. We say a spider is

if all of its legs have even length.

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
T
be a rooted tree.

(1)

It holds that
e(T)\geq 2e(\lfloor T/2\rfloor)
.

(2)

If
e(T)=2e(\lfloor T/2\rfloor)
, then
T
is an even spider.

(3)

If
e(T)=2e(\lfloor T/2\rfloor)+1
, then either
\lfloor T/2\rfloor=\lceil T/2\rceil
or
T
is a spider with exactly one odd-length leg.

Proof.

Let
v_{0}
be the root of
T
. We write
E_{1}
for the set of edges incident to
v_{0}
but not a leaf and
E_{2}
for the set of edges incident to leaves of
T
but not
v_{0}
. The key inequality which will enable us to conclude all three properties is

e(T)-e(\lceil T/2\rceil)-\lvert E_{2}\rvert\geq e(\lfloor T/2\rfloor)-\lvert E_{1}\rvert.

(2.1)

To prove (
2.1
), we present an injection
\iota
from
E(\lfloor T/2\rfloor)
to
E(T)\setminus E(\lceil T/2\rceil)
which maps
E_{1}\setminus E_{2}
to
E_{2}\setminus E_{1}
. The idea is that
\iota
should reflect edges in about the midpoint of maximal paths from
v_{0}
in
T
(e.g. in the special case where
T
is a path
v_{0}\cdots v_{\ell}
rooted at
T
, the map
\iota
should map
v_{i-1}v_{i}
to
v_{\ell-i}v_{\ell-i+1}
). We expand on this intuition in the next two paragraphs.

Let
e
be an edge in
\lfloor T/2\rfloor
, and let
v
and
w
be the endpoints of
e
, chosen so that
w
is a descendant of
v
. Set
d:=\lambda(w)
and
\ell:=\lambda_{\max}(w)
, and let
v_{0}v_{1}\cdots v_{\ell}
be a path from
v_{0}
to an arbitrary maximum-level descendant of
w
, so that
v_{d}=w
and
v_{d-1}=v
. Set
\iota(e)=v_{\ell-d}v_{\ell-d+1}
. Because
w\in V(\lfloor T/2\rfloor)
, we have
d\leq\ell/2
. In particular,

\ell-d+1\geq\ell/2+1>\lceil\ell/2\rceil\geq d.

This implies firstly that
v_{\ell-d+1}
is a descendant of
w=v_{d}
, and thus
\lambda_{\max}(v_{\ell-d+1})=\ell
. We conclude that
\ell-d+1>\lceil\lambda_{\max}(v_{\ell-d+1})/2\rceil
, yielding that
v_{\ell-d+1}\not\in V(\lceil T/2\rceil)
. Therefore the edge
v_{\ell-d}v_{\ell-d+1}
lies in
E(T)\setminus E(\lceil T/2\rceil)
. Furthermore, if
e\in E_{1}
then
d=1
and
\ell>1
, so the edge
\iota(e)=v_{\ell-1}v_{\ell}
is incident to the vertex
v_{\ell}
and thus in
E_{2}
.

We must now argue that
\iota
is injective, which we do by providing a procedure
\pi
which reverses the computation of
\iota
. Indeed, given an edge
e^{\prime}:=vw\in E(T)\setminus E(\lceil T/2\rceil)
with
\lambda(v)<\lambda(w)
, we can recover
\ell=\lambda_{\max}(w)
and
d=\ell-\lambda(v)
. As
e^{\prime}\not\in E(\lceil T/2\rceil)
, we have
\lceil\ell/2\rceil<\ell-d+1
, or equivalently
\lfloor\ell/2\rfloor\geq d
. Let
v_{0}v_{1}\cdots v_{\ell}
be a path from
v_{0}
to an arbitrary descendant of
w
of maximum level, and let
\pi(e^{\prime})=v_{d-1}v_{d}

Hypercubes and Embeddings

We now establish some definitions surrounding hypercubes and homomorphisms of trees.

Definition 2.10

(Homomorphisms into the hypercube)

Let

to

The Algorithm Terminates in a Rainbow Path-Distinct Embedding Avoiding

Let
\Phi\colon T\to G
be the result of this algorithm. We check each of our required conditions in turn.

The map
\Phi
is a rainbow homomorphism
. Indeed, every step of our algorithm specifies that the map
\Phi

\Phi

The map
\Phi

\Phi

; we imposed in Steps 1 and 3 that these edges be mapped to edges with different coordinates. If
v=t_{j}
then this edge set is
B_{j}
; we imposed in Step 1 that these edges be mapped in different coordinates. Finally, if
v=u_{i}
then this set has size
1
and there is nothing to show.

The map
\Phi
is injective
. Recall that
\Phi
restricts to an injective map on each of the subtree
S_{1},\ldots,S_{k},T_{1},\ldots,T_{m}
by Steps 6, 7, and 8. It remains to show that the images of the substructures (i.e., singleton vertices or subtrees)
v_{0},u_{1},\ldots,u_{\ell},S_{1},\ldots,S_{k},T_{1},\ldots,T_{m}
are vertex-disjoint under
\Phi
.

The remainder of the proof largely follows by repeated applications of
2.12
to pairs of subtrees
(T^{\prime},T^{\prime\prime})
of
T
, with the corresponding homomorphisms
(\Phi^{\prime},\Phi^{\prime\prime})
always assumed to be the restrictions of
\Phi
to
(T^{\prime},T^{\prime\prime})
. When checking
2.12
(a), we often use the fact that
\Phi
is path-distinct on
\lceil T/2\rceil
(and thus on any subtree thereof as well).

First we show that, for any
1\leq i\leq\ell
, the map
\Phi
does not identify
u_{i}
with any vertex of
T-u_{i}
. We apply
2.12
to
T^{\prime}=T-u_{i}
and
T^{\prime\prime}=\{u_{i}\}
. Condition (a) follows because
\Phi
is path-distinct on
\lceil T/2\rceil\supseteq\lceil T^{\prime}/2\rceil
and condition (b) is trivial. Condition (c) requires that no edge in
\Phi(\lceil T^{\prime}/2\rceil)
has the same coordinate as
\Phi(v_{0}u_{i})
. We have
E(\lceil T^{\prime}/2\rceil)=\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{B}\cup\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}\}\cup\{v_{0}u_{i^{\prime}}:i^{\prime}\neq i\}
, so this follows from Step 4.
We conclude by
2.12
that
\Phi
does not identify
u_{i}
with any vertex of
T-u_{i}
.

Next, we show that
\Phi
is injective on
\{v_{0}\}\cup T_{1}\cup\cdots\cup T_{m}
. Fix
1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}\leq m
; we apply
2.12
to
T^{\prime}=T_{j_{1}}
and
T^{\prime\prime}=\{v_{0}t_{j_{2}}\}\cup T_{j_{2}}
. Condition (a) follows because
\Phi
is path-distinct on
\lceil T^{\prime}/2\rceil
by Step 7 and is path-distinct on
\lceil T/2\rceil\supseteq\lceil T^{\prime\prime}/2\rceil
.
For (b) and (c), Step 7 asserts that no edge of
E(\lceil T^{\prime}/2\rceil)
receives the same coordinate under
\Phi
as any edge in
\{v_{0}t_{j_{1}}\}\cup E(\lceil T^{\prime\prime}/2\rceil)=\{v_{0}t_{j_{1}}\}\cup B_{j_{2}}
, and moreover Step 1 asserts that
\Phi
is distinctly directed on
\{v_{0}t_{j_{1}}\}\cup E(\lceil T^{\prime\prime}/2\rceil)\subseteq\mathcal{B}
. By
2.12
, it follows that
\Phi
does not identify any vertex of
T_{j_{1}}
with any vertex of
T_{j_{2}}
for any
1\leq j_{1}<j_{2}\leq m
; moreover, taking
j_{2}=m
, we see that
\Phi
does not identify any vertex of
T_{j_{1}}
with
v_{0}
for
j_{1}<m
. As
\Phi
is injective on
\{v_{0}t_{m}\}\cup T_{m}
by Step 8, we conclude that
\Phi
is injective on
\{v_{0}\}\cup T_{1}\cup\cdots\cup T_{m}
.

Next, we show that
\Phi
identifies no vertex of
S_{i}
(if
i=1
) or
S_{i}-s^{(i)}_{2\lambda_{i}}
(if
i\geq 2
) with any vertex of
T-s_{i}
(which we recall has vertex set
V(T)-V(S_{i})
). Fix
1\leq i\leq k
. We apply
2.12
to
T^{\prime}=S_{i}
(if
i=1
) or
T^{\prime}=S_{i}-s^{(i)}_{2\lambda_{i}}
(if
i\geq 2
) and
T^{\prime\prime}=T-s_{i}
. Condition (a) holds as
\Phi
is distinctly directed on
E(\lceil T^{\prime}/2\rceil)\subseteq A_{i}\cup\{e_{1}\}
and path-distinct on
\lceil T/2\rceil\supseteq\lceil T^{\prime\prime}/2\rceil
. For (b) and (c), observe that

E(\lceil T^{\prime}/2\rceil)\cup\{v_{0}s_{i}\}\subseteq\mathcal{A}\cup\{e_{1}\}\quad\text{ and }\quad E(\lceil T^{\prime\prime}/2\rceil)\subseteq\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{B}\cup\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}\}\cup\{v_{0}u_{1},\ldots,v_{0}u_{\ell}\}.

Steps 1–4 assert that no edge
e^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}\cup\{e_{1}\}
receives the same coordinate under
\Phi
as any
e^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{B}\cup\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}\}\cup\{v_{0}u_{1},\ldots,v_{0}u_{\ell}\}
if
e^{\prime}\neq e^{\prime\prime}
. Conditions (b) and (c) follow, so
2.12
implies that
\Phi
identifies no vertex of
S_{i}
(if
i=1
) or
S_{i}-s_{2\lambda_{i}}^{(i)}
(if
i\geq 2
) with any vertex of
T-s_{i}
.

Thus far, we have shown that
\Phi
identifies each leaf
u_{i}
with no other vertex of
T
, that
\Phi
is injective on
\{v_{0}\}\cup T_{1}\cup\cdots\cup T_{m}
, and that
\Phi
identifies no vertex of
S_{i}
(if
i=1
) or
S_{i}-s^{(i)}_{2\lambda_{i}}
(if
i\geq 2
) with any vertex of
T-s_{i}
. To complete the proof that
\Phi
is injective, we need only show that
\Phi
never identifies any two vertices in the set
\{s^{(i)}_{2\lambda_{i}}:2\leq i\leq k\}
.

Fix indices
2\leq h<i\leq k
. To show that
\Phi(s^{(i)}_{2\lambda_{i}})\neq\Phi(s^{(h)}_{2\lambda_{h}})
, we apply
2.3
to the walk

\Phi(s^{(i)}_{2\lambda_{i}})\cdots\Phi(s^{(i)}_{1})\Phi(s_{i})\Phi(v_{0})\Phi(s_{h})\Phi(s^{(h)}_{1})\cdots\Phi(s^{(h)}_{2\lambda_{h}})

in
Q_{\infty}
, which has length
2\lambda_{i}+2\lambda_{h}+2
. Note that all
\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{h}+2
edges of the path

s^{(i)}_{\lambda_{i}-1}\cdots s^{(i)}_{1}s_{i}v_{0}s_{h}s^{(h)}_{1}\cdots s^{(h)}_{\lambda_{h}+1}

are contained in the set
A_{i}\cup A_{h}\cup\{e_{h},f_{h}\}
; thus, by Steps 1, 3, and 5, their images under
\Phi
have distinct coordinates.
It follows by
2.3
that
\Phi
cannot identify two vertices of the form
s^{(h)}_{2\lambda_{h}}
and
s^{(i)}_{2\lambda_{i}}
, completing the proof that
\Phi
is injective.

The homomorphism
\Phi
maps no vertex to
z_{\mathrm{bad}}
.
We first show that most vertices of
T
do not map to
z_{\mathrm{bad}}
by applying
2.12
. The lemma is applied to the tree
T^{\prime}=T-\{s^{(i)}_{2\lambda_{i}}:2\leq i\leq k\}
formed by removing the leaves
s_{2\lambda_{2}}^{(2)},\ldots,s_{2\lambda_{k}}^{(k)}
from
T
, and to a rooted tree
T^{\prime\prime}
with one vertex. Let
\Phi^{\prime}\colon T^{\prime}\to Q_{\infty}
be the restriction of
\Phi
to
T^{\prime}
, and let
\Phi^{\prime\prime}\colon T^{\prime\prime}\to Q_{\infty}
map the single vertex to
z_{\mathrm{bad}}
. Condition (a) of
2.12
follows because
\Phi
is path-distinct on
\lceil T/2\rceil\supseteq\lceil T^{\prime}/2\rceil
and condition (b) is trivial because
E(T^{\prime\prime})=\emptyset
. Condition (c) requires that no edge of

E(\lceil T^{\prime}/2\rceil)=\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{B}\cup\{e_{1}\}\cup\{v_{0}u_{1},\ldots,v_{0}u_{\ell}\}

uses the coordinate
\vec{q}
, which follows from Steps 1, 2, and 4. Thus, the hypotheses of
2.12
are satisfied, and we conclude that no vertex of
T^{\prime}
is mapped to
z_{\mathrm{bad}}
by
\Phi
.

It remains to show that the vertices of
V(T)\setminus V(T^{\prime})=\{s_{2\lambda_{2}}^{(2)},\ldots,s_{2\lambda_{k}}^{(k)}\}
are not mapped to
z_{\mathrm{bad}}
. Fix
2\leq i\leq k
and consider the path
v_{0}s_{i}s^{(i)}_{1}\cdots s^{(i)}_{\lambda_{i}+1}
of length
\lambda_{i}+2
, all of whose edges are contained in
A_{i}\cup\{e_{i},f_{i}\}
.
These edges are mapped to edges with distinct coordinates by Steps 1, 3, and 5.
Thus, applying
2.3
to the walk
z_{\mathrm{bad}}\Phi(v_{0})\Phi(s_{i})\Phi(s^{(i)}_{1})\cdots\Phi(s^{(i)}_{2\lambda_{i}})

in
Q_{\infty}
, which has length
2\lambda_{i}+2
, we conclude that
z_{\mathrm{bad}}\neq\Phi(s^{(i)}_{2\lambda_{i}})
.
Hence,
\Phi
maps no vertex of
T
to
z_{\mathrm{bad}}
.

∎

Finally, we use
3.2
to conclude our main result.

Proof of
1.2
.

Let
G
be a properly edge-colored subgraph of
Q_{\infty}
and
T
a tree with
e(T)\leq\delta(G)
. We construct a rainbow embedding
\Phi:T\to G
.

By repeatedly applying
2.4
, it is straightforward to construct a doubly distinct homomorphism
\phi:\lfloor T/2\rfloor\to G
. Indeed, order the edges of
\lfloor T/2\rfloor
as
e_{1},e_{2},\ldots
so that
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{i}\}
induces a connected subgraph of
T
for each
1\leq i\leq e(\lfloor T/2\rfloor)
. Define
\phi(e_{1})
arbitrarily and for
i=2,3,\ldots
, iteratively define
\phi(e_{i})
to avoid the sets
X_{\mathrm{col}}
and
X_{\mathrm{coor}}
of colors and coordinates, respectively, used by any edge in
\phi(\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{i-1}\})
. To do so we apply
2.4
as in the proof of
3.2
; the hypothesis of
2.4
is satisfied for
r=0
because

\lvert X_{\mathrm{col}}\rvert+\lvert X_{\mathrm{coor}}\rvert=2(i-1)\leq 2e(\lfloor T/2)-2\leq e(T)-2<\delta(G),

with the center inequality using
2.9
.
Applying
3.2
to the resulting
\phi
(and choosing
z_{\mathrm{bad}}
arbitrarily among vertices of
Q_{\infty}
satisfying the necessary condition) gives a rainbow embedding of
T
into
G
, proving the result.
∎

4
Concluding Remarks

In this paper we resolved
1.1
by showing that every proper edge-coloring of
Q_{n}
contains a rainbow copy of every tree on at most
n
edges. This result, together with Schrijver’s Conjecture, naturally motivates the following broad question: given a graph
G
, what trees
T
are such that every proper edge-coloring of
G
contains a rainbow copy of
T
? Perhaps the most natural special case of this question is the following, which can be viewed as a vast generalization of Schrijver’s Conjecture.

Question 4.1
.

Does there exist some
C>0
such that for every graph
G
of minimum degree
d
and every tree
T
on at most
d-C
edges, every proper edge-coloring of
G
contains a rainbow copy of
T
?

We can say slightly more to
4.1
than what is given by
1.2
: while
1.2
is stated only in terms of subgraphs of the hypercube, the exact same proof can be made to work for a slightly broader class of graphs. Specifically, we say that a graph
G
is
matching-separable
if for every edge
xy\in E(G)
, there exists a matching
M_{xy}
such that
x
and
y
lie in distinct connected components of
G-M_{xy}
. We note that our current proof can be extended to give the following.

Theorem 4.2
.

If
G
is a matching-separable graph of minimum degree at least
d
, then every proper edge-coloring of
G
contains a copy of every rainbow tree
T
on at most
d
edges.

Note that every subgraph of the hypercube is matching-separable, since if
xy\in E(G)\subseteq E(Q_{n})
is an edge whose vertices differ on the
i
th coordinate, then taking
M_{xy}
to be the set of all edges of
G
whose vertices differ on the
i
th coordinate gives the desired property. Thus this result in particular recovers
1.2
. Moreover, there exist matching-separable graphs which are not subgraphs of a hypercube. Examples of such graphs include odd cycles of length at least 5 (via taking
M_{e}
to consist of the two edges incident to
e
), as well as the Petersen graph (via choosing
M_{e}
such that
G-M_{e}
is the disjoint union of two 5-cycles).

Sketch of Proof.

We construct our rainbow embedding
\Phi\colon V(T)\to V(G)
exactly like we do in the proof of
1.2
, except now when choosing the image of some edge
e\in V(T)
, instead of making sure
\Phi(e)
does not use the same coordinate of some set of previously mapped to edges
\{\Phi(e_{1}),\ldots,\Phi(e_{t})\}
, we instead make sure that
\Phi(e)
does not lie in any of the matchings
M_{\Phi(e_{i})}
.

Observe that in order for a homomorphism
\Phi
of
T
to satisfy
\Phi(u)=\Phi(v)
, it must be the case that for each edge
e^{\prime}
in the path from
u
to
v
in
T
, some other edge
e^{\prime\prime}
in this path satisfies
\Phi(e^{\prime\prime})\in M_{\Phi(e^{\prime})}
, as otherwise
\Phi(u),\Phi(v)
would be in distinct connected components of
G=M_{\Phi(e^{\prime})}
(and hence in particular not be equal to each other). As such, to guarantee
\Phi(u)\neq\Phi(v)
it suffices for more than half the edges
e^{\prime}
in the path from
u
to
v
to satisfy that no other edge in the path maps into
M_{\Phi(e^{\prime})}
, with this condition being exactly analogous to our proofs of injectivity for the hypercube which relied on
2.3
. Moreover, each
M_{\Phi(e^{\prime})}
set forbids at most one neighbor for any given vertex of
G
because
M_{\Phi(e^{\prime})}
is a matching, so the exact same proof of
2.4
goes through if we replace the condititon of
G
being a subgraph of a hypercube to being matching-separable.
∎

Another direction, in the spirit of
1.3
, is to consider directed graphs. In particular, our proof can be used to show that any properly edge-colored directed graph
D
whose underlying graph is a subgraph of
Q_{n}
and which has minimum out-degree and in-degree at least
d
contains a rainbow copy of every directed tree on at most
d
edges. It is possible to weaken these degree conditions somewhat depending on the structure of
T
. Specifically, if
T
is a directed tree such that every vertex has in-degree at most 1 (which is equivalent to saying
T
has some “root” vertex and every arc is directed away from the root), then only minimum out-degree at least
d
is needed for our present proof to work. It is not clear to us what degree conditions should be necessary in order to guarantee that directed subgraph of
Q_{n}
contain rainbow copies of a given tree
T
.

Lastly, it is natural to ask if one can replace the condition of
1.2
that
G
has minimum degree at least
d
with the condition that it has
average
degree at least
d
. Indeed, this is the main conjecture that was made in
[
4
]
and serves as a rainbow analog of the classic Erdős–Sós Conjecture. We do not currently know how to adapt our proof to the average degree setting even in relatively simple cases. In particular, even the following case of this problem remains open for
d\geq 5
.

Conjecture 4.3
.

If
G
is a subgraph of a hypercube
Q_{n}
and if
G
has average degree at least
d
, then every proper edge-coloring of
G
contains a rainbow copy of the path on
d
edges.
