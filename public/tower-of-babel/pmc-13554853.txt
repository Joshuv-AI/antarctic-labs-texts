The Interval
Authors: Aabha Divya
Published: 2026

Every mechanical circulatory support decision is made twice. The first decision is whether to support the circulation, and it is often made in minutes and under duress. The second is how to use the time that support has purchased. It unfolds over hours, months, or years and often carries far less certainty than the first. We have become expert at making the first decision. This issue of JACC: Case Reports is about the second.

The premise of this mini collection is that a mechanical support device primarily answers a hemodynamic question. It does not establish the diagnosis, identify the mechanism, or define the endpoint. Support is not a destination. It is a period of borrowed physiological stability in which the actual clinical work must be done. The cases assembled in this issue illuminate the dimensions of that interval, each requiring a distinct form of clinical reasoning.

Support as a Diagnostic Platform

The circuit is often described as a bridge, but these case reports depict it as a window. Refractory ventricular fibrillation in a patient with a fixed, moderate coronary stenosis suggests a clear cause. The underlying mechanism was confirmed to be dynamic by provocation testing performed during venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), made possible only by the support that made the procedure survivable. Also in this collection, cases of refractory postcardiotomy right ventricular failure are explained post mortem, thus revealing diffuse lipomatous replacement behind preoperative imaging, which was initially interpreted as normal, by echocardiogram and cardiac magnetic resonance. Additionally, bedside assessment of the inferior vena cava can differentiate autonomic dysfunction with orthostatic hypotension from right ventricular failure in patients with left ventricular assist devices (LVADs), thereby avoiding invasive testing. In each case, support did not directly provide answers but created the conditions that allowed the questions to be asked.

The Clock: Escalation Before Certainty

Some decisions need to be made before the diagnostic interval closes. Traditionally, postinfarction papillary muscle rupture is stabilized until surgery can be performed. In 1 of the case reports, this condition is treated early with transcatheter edge-to-edge repair under mechanical support, and the accompanying editorial discusses what would be required to establish the approach more broadly. In another report, ventricular free-wall rupture with severe mitral regurgitation is managed with ECMO. Revascularization of left main bifurcation and chronic total occlusion disease after failed coronary artery bypass grafting is performed under combined venoarterial ECMO and intra-aortic balloon pump support. In these cases, support is not to buy time for deliberation, but to enable immediate intervention, thus making it survivable. Identifying which scenario applies may be the most critical judgment in the shock room.

Beyond the Algorithm

No guidelines anticipate the treatment of patients in this section. Extracorporeal cardiopulmonary resuscitation is used in periviable pregnancy with a fetus in situ, a scenario in which maternal resuscitation and obstetric considerations must be weighed together and in real time. A preterm neonate with unrepaired transposition undergoes coarctation stenting through ECMO venous cannula access. An LVAD recipient with an abdominal aortic aneurysm is treated with a combination of renal autotransplantation and endovascular repair, an approach the accompanying editorial describes as creativity beyond the algorithm. Constrictive tuberculous pericarditis with severe ventricular dysfunction is managed with ECMO-supported pericardiectomy. These reports demonstrate disciplined improvisation when the evidence base is exhausted.

The Price of Durable Support

Durable support converts an acute crisis into a chronic condition with its own complications, and this collection is candid about them. Aortic regurgitation with dynamic aortic thrombus during LVAD support illustrates how the device alters the very physiology used to evaluate it. Systemic candidemia related to an LVAD declares itself not with fever or drainage but with intracranial hemorrhage, a presentation that tests the signals on which routine infection surveillance depends. Catastrophic antiphospholipid syndrome produces extensive circuit thrombosis in a young peripartum patient, requiring plasmapheresis, high-intensity anticoagulation, and ultimately transplantation. The shared lesson is worth stating plainly: in the supported patient, the conventional indicators of infection, thrombosis, and valvular function are not merely less sensitive. They reflect physiology fundamentally altered by the device.

Separation: Weaning as Its Own Problem

Weaning from support has received less attention than cannulation, and these cases suggest why it merits more. When severe low-flow, low-gradient aortic stenosis coexists with postarrest myocardial stunning, the failure to wean from support reflects a fixed obstruction rather than a recovering ventricle. Postcardiotomy right ventricular failure raises the same problem from the opposite direction. Separation from support is not simply the conclusion of a care episode. Instead, it is a separate physiological issue with its own differential diagnosis, requiring the same careful evaluation as the decision to cannulate.

The Common Thread

The value of these cases does not lie in their rarity. It lies in the decisions made after mechanical support restored the circulation but before the clinical problem was solved. A device can reverse hemodynamic collapse, create procedural options, and preserve time for diagnosis. It can also distort native physiology, obscure conventional clinical signals, and introduce new modes of failure. The supported patient therefore demands a different level of vigilance. Clinicians must continue to test the presumed diagnosis, reassess the mechanism of shock, and determine whether the current strategy continues to serve its intended purpose. In the end, hemodynamic stability should never be mistaken for clinical resolution.

This principle unites the reports in this collection. Some teams used support to uncover an unexpected diagnosis. Others used it to make an otherwise prohibitive intervention possible. Several confronted complications created or transformed by the device itself (Figure 1). In every case, the decisive act was not the initiation of support. It was the disciplined clinical reasoning that followed.

The Interval: Navigating the Space Between Support and Resolution

These reports also demonstrate the enduring value of the case report. A well-selected case does more than document an unusual event. It exposes a failure in routine assumptions, identifies a physiological principle, or expands the boundaries of what can be treated. The authors represent Japan, China, Brazil, Spain, Germany, the United Kingdom, Poland, India, Israel, New Zealand, Singapore, and the United States. Many trainees served as first authors. By analyzing complex physiology, articulating clinical decisions, and defending those decisions in the literature, they help advance the next generation of mechanical circulatory support clinicians. The issue is equally indebted to the associate editors and peer reviewers who held each contribution to the standard the format demands, and to JACC: Case Reports Editor-in-Chief Gilbert H.L. Tang, MD, MS, MBA.

I invite readers to consider each case not as a record of what the device accomplished but as an account of how the clinical team used the time it created. Mechanical circulatory support creates an interval between collapse and outcome, and decisions made within that interval determine whether support serves as a bridge to recovery, transition, or replacement or as an endpoint without benefit. The circuit can sustain the circulation, but it cannot define the destination.
