Patient-specific vs. Oxford microplasty instrumentation in unicompartmental knee arthroplasty: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Yu Deng, Xinwen Bai, Zhi Zhao, Linhu Cao, Yang Liu, Qilong Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe success of unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA) in treating end-stage knee osteoarthritis is mainly based on the surgical instruments and surgeon’s experience. The purpose of this randomized controlled trial was to compare the efficacy of patient-specific instrumentation (PSI) assisted UKA by inexperienced surgeon on the learning curve with conventional Oxford microplasty instrumentation (MPI) assisted UKA.MethodsWe performed a prospective study at a single high-volume orthopaedic hospital. Patients with end-stage medial tibiofemoral osteoarthritis were assessed for eligibility and randomly assigned to undergo either PSI-assisted UKA performed by inexperienced surgeons (PSI group) or MPI-assisted UKA performed by experienced surgeons (MPI group) at a 1:1 ratio. The primary outcome was the femoral varus/valgus angle using the mean score on the first day after surgery. The secondary outcomes included the femoral prosthesis flexion/extension angle, tibial varus/valgus angle, tibial posterior slope angle, and the hip–knee–ankle angle (HKAA). The Knee Society Score (KSS) and Hospital for Special Surgery (HSS) score were assessed at one, three, six, and 12 months. Mixed-effects modelling was used to analyze repeated measurements.ResultsA total of 73 participants (37 in the PSI group and 36 in the MPI group) were enrolled from June 2021 to July 2023. No patients were lost to follow-up. No significant between-group differences were found in the femoral component varus and valgus angle, flexion and extension angle, tibial component varus and valgus angle, tibial posterior slope angle, or hip–knee–ankle angle (all P > 0.05). At the 12-month follow-up, the PSI group and MPI group achieved mean KSSs of 93.4 points (95% CI, 88.6–97.8) and 93.6 points (95% CI, 89.1–98.4), respectively. There were no significant between-group differences in the KSS and HSS score improvements from baseline to each follow-up point.ConclusionsThe PSI-assisted UKA performed by inexperienced surgeon on a learning curve can yield excellent radiographic and functional outcomes in short-term follow-up, despite not demonstrating superior clinical outcomes to MPI-assisted UKA performed by an experienced surgeon. The PSI could potentially serve as an alternative instrumentation for UKA beginners with low-volume surgical caseload.

Introduction

In the last 20 years, unicompartmental knee arthroplasty (UKA) has been widely utilized to treat medial tibiofemoral osteoarthritis and has produced exceptional clinical outcomes [1, 2]. With the expanded indications for UKA, this procedure has the potential for increased application in the coming years [3]. Compared with total knee arthroplasty (TKA), UKA has proven to be superior in deriving a greater range of motion, increased physiological kinematics, enhanced rehabilitation, and decreased postoperative complications [4, 5]. However, UKA has a lower long-term survival rate than TKA according to joint registries [6, 7]. The survivorship of the UKA prosthesis, particularly the tibial component, is closely associated with implant size, positioning, alignment, and experience of the surgeon [8–10]. A previous study demonstrated that UKA performed by inexperienced and low-volume surgeons (i.e., annual volume fewer than ten cases) has a much higher revision rate than that performed by high-volume surgeons, which indicates that the caseload and experience of surgeons strongly affects the survivorship of the UKA prosthesis [11, 12]. The Oxford microplasty instrumentation (MPI) has been widely utilized to perform a UKA procedure [13]. Errors of this technique are more likely when the method is being learned. To reduce these to a minimum, surgeons are required by the FDA in the United States, and strongly recommended throughout the world, to attend an Advanced Instructional Course on the Oxford Partial Knee before attempting the operation [2]. Given the inevitable learning curve for inexperienced surgeons, multiple techniques have been implemented to improve the position of UKA components, among which patient-specific instrumentation (PSI) has shown promise in achieving accurate prosthesis implantation. Although CT-based PSI may produce increased costs, the overall costs between PSI and MPI knee arthroplasty are similar due to reduced operating time, length of hospital stay, blood preservation, and longer survival time of the implants [14–16]. Compared to MPI, the PSI is designed preoperatively according to personalized computed tomography (CT) scans and is used intraoperatively to guide bone resection during the UKA procedure [17]. Theoretically, it is viable to accurately plan the size and position of UKA components using this instrumentation. Although initial studies have investigated the utility of PSI in mitigating surgical errors, there are currently few persuasive data that assess prognosis after PSI-assisted UKA. Especially for UKA beginners, it is unclear whether the PSI is beneficial to bone resection and implant placement [18–20].

Thus, we conducted this prospective study to evaluate the efficacy of PSI in UKA for patients with medial tibiofemoral osteoarthritis and compared it with Oxford MPI. We hypothesized that the PSI is superior to conventional Oxford MPI in UKA component positioning. The PSI-assisted UKA by inexperienced surgeons on a learning curve would yield excellent radiographic and functional outcomes in short-term follow-up.

Methods

Study design

We performed a prospective study at a single orthopaedic hospital with an annual high volume of UKA and TKA. Enrolment commenced in June 2021 and ended in July 2023. A follow-up of at least 12 months was planned, which concluded in July 2024. Only the receptionist had access to the assignment schedule. Participants, radiologists, and investigators were blinded to treatment allocation.

Participants

All patients admitted for a medial UKA due to AMOA were assessed for eligibility by a receptionist. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) a painful and disabled knee joint resulting from single AMOA with at least Kellgren–Lawrence grade IV on the anterior–posterior radiograph; (2) age > 55 years; (3) body mass index (BMI) < 35 kg/m2; (4) functionally intact cruciate ligaments; (5) well-preserved lateral compartment, with an intact meniscus and full thickness of articular cartilage; (6) flexion deformity < 15 degrees; and (7) knee flexion of at least 110 degrees. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) rheumatoid arthritis, (2) active infection at any site in the body, and (3) surgical history other than arthroscopic surgery on the operated knee.

Randomisation was done by the independent receptionist using a computer-generated sequence. All eligible patients were randomly assigned to undergo mobile-bearing Oxford UKA performed by either a UKA-inexperienced surgeon (experienced in TKA with a UKA caseload of fewer than 10 procedures) using PSI (PSI group) or a UKA-experienced surgeon (experienced in UKA with an annual volume more than 30) using Oxford MPI (MPI group) at a 1:1 ratio (Fig. 1). Group allocation was masked using source documentation. Stratification was not initially performed, but we acknowledge that it could have helped maintain baseline balance even with our small sample size. We have conducted additional analyses to verify baseline characteristics across groups, confirming that key variables are well-balanced despite the lack of stratification.Fig. 1Flow diagram of participant inclusion. Manufacturing of the PSI

The patients underwent anteroposterior and lateral X-ray films of the knee joint and full-length anteroposterior X-ray films of both lower limbs in the standing position. For patients in the PSI group, full-length CT examinations of both lower limbs were performed before surgery. All CTs were performed at the same hospital using the same protocol previously validated by the manufacturer (Optima 16, GE, USA). The scanning range was the full length of both lower limbs, from 5 cm above the femoral head to 5 cm below the ankle point. The layer thickness was 0.625 mm, voltage was 120 kV, and current was 300 mA, and the images were saved in DICOM format. The CT scan data were imported into Mimics 19.0 software. According to the different brightness values of bone and soft tissue, a three-dimensional 1:1 model of the bones of both lower limbs was reconstructed by threshold segmentation, regional growth, and other functions. The output was saved in STL format. The reconstructed 3D model was imported into NX three-dimensional design software to determine the force line of both lower limbs and the anatomical axis of the femur and tibia. In the coronal position, the force line of the femoral prosthesis is on the midline of the medial platform, parallel to the mechanical axis of the femur, and the tibial prosthesis is perpendicular to the mechanical axis of the tibia. In the sagittal position, the force line of the femoral prosthesis tilts ten degrees backwards relative to the femoral anatomical axis, the tibial prosthesis tilts seven degrees backwards relative to the tibial anatomical axis, and the osteotomy thickness is two or three mm below the deepest part of the erosion. According to the above requirements, the surgeon determined the position of the femoral and tibial prostheses in the software, simulated the installation of the prostheses, and selected the appropriate knee prosthesis. The position and axial plane of the two columns of the femoral prosthesis and the direction and thickness of the vertical and horizontal osteotomies of the tibial prosthesis were determined to identify the location of the column positioning hole of the femoral guide plate and the osteotomy groove of the tibial guide plate. Then, the direction and thickness of the parameter-driven prosthesis positioning hole and osteotomy groove were designed and manufactured using a parametric modelling method, and the positioning plate was designed according to the personalized shape of the femoral condyle and tibia. Then, the prosthesis positioning hole, osteotomy groove and positioning plate morphology were combined to design a customized osteotomy guide plate for the femur and tibia. The STL file for the individualized osteotomy guide plate was exported and imported into the UP Studio printing software to set the printing layer thickness, filling parameters, placement position and other parameters. After setting, the FDM rapid prototyping machine UP300 was used to produce the 3D print by layer-by-layer stacking. Polylactic acid (PLA) wire material (Beijing Taier Times Technology Co., Ltd.) was used to make the digital navigation model to form a physical object for clinical use. The biological toxicity test fully met the clinical requirements (Fig. 2). We utilized the ethylene oxide sterilization which can maintain the integrity of the guides and conducted experiments to verify the dimensional stability of the guides post-sterilization. Interventions

Both groups underwent the same surgical approach, which involved a precise small incision in the medial parapatellar area. The operation exposed the lesion’s medial compartment, and the surgeon examined the patellofemoral joint, lateral compartment, and anterior cruciate ligament. Both the femoral and tibial sections in the PSI group were placed using personalized instruments without the removal of any osteophytes. The osteophytes were used as a marker for the positioning of the navigation template. In contrast, the sections in the MPI group were placed according to the standard operation procedure for Oxford UKA. After the sections were completed, both groups followed the same procedure. The amount of distal femoral overmilling was determined by calculating the difference between the flexion and extension gaps. In both groups, patients received an identical cemented metal-backed mobile-bearing medial unicompartmental prosthesis (Oxford; Zimmer Biomet, Warsaw, IN). In both groups, a tourniquet was utilized and released before closure. An identical procedure was followed for managing pain and bleeding post-surgery. In addition, both groups adhered to the same postoperative rehabilitation protocol: immediate postoperative period (day 0–1): passive range-of-motion (ROM) exercises for the knee (focusing on 0°–90° flexion) administered by physical therapists, along with ankle pumps to prevent venous thromboembolism; weight-bearing as tolerated with the assistance of a walker or crutches. Early rehabilitation phase (weeks 1–4): progressive active ROM exercises (targeting ≥ 120°flexion by week 4), strengthening exercises for the quadriceps and hamstrings (e.g., straight-leg raises, seated leg presses at low resistance), and gradual transition to unassisted ambulation. Intermediate phase (weeks 5–12): advanced strengthening (e.g., step-ups and lateral band walks), functional training (e.g., stair climbing and gait normalization), and return to light daily activities; ROM is monitored weekly to ensure maintenance of ≥ 120°flexion.

Outcome assessment

The primary outcome was the femoral varus/valgus angle (positive indicates the femoral varus angle, negative indicates the femoral valgus angle) using the mean score on the first day after surgery. The secondary outcomes included the femoral prosthesis flexion/extension angle, tibial varus/valgus angle, tibial posterior slope angle, and the hip–knee–ankle angle. Radiographic analysis was performed by two independent observers. Intraobserver reliability was evaluated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC).

Baseline data, including age, sex, height and weight were collected. Weight-bearing knee X-rays and full-length CT films of both lower limbs were performed in both groups before and the first day after surgery. The femoral varus/valgus angle, femoral prosthesis flexion/extension angle, tibial varus/valgus angle, tibial posterior slope angle, and the hip–knee–ankle angle were recorded to evaluate the accuracy of prosthesis placement (Fig. 3). The radiological assessments were conducted by two board-certified musculoskeletal radiologists with 8 and 12 years of specialized experience in orthopedic imaging, respectively. Both radiologists regularly interpret images from knee arthroplasty procedures as part of their clinical practice. Prior to the study, the radiologists underwent standardized training specific to this trial. To ensure reliability, 20% of the images were randomly selected for independent re-evaluation by both radiologists at a 4-week interval. Intra-observer reliability was calculated using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs), which ranged from 0.89 to 0.96 for all measured parameters. Inter-observer reliability between the two radiologists also demonstrated excellent agreement, with ICCs ranging from 0.85 to 0.93. Functional outcomes were assessed by an independent observer using the American Knee Society Score (KSS) and the Hospital for Special Surgery (HSS) score before surgery and at one, three, six and 12 months of follow-up.Fig. 3Radiologic measurements of the components. Statistical analysis

The primary outcome was the femoral varus/valgus angle using the mean score on the first day after surgery. The secondary outcomes included the femoral prosthesis flexion/extension angle, tibial varus/valgus angle, tibial posterior slope angle, and the hip–knee–ankle angle. With the use of PASS software, we calculated that each group should include at least 35 participants (80% power, 5% type 1 error rate, 3.2 standard deviation, a mean difference of 3° between two groups) [21]. We used SPSS software (version 27.0 SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) to conduct the statistical data analysis. Linear mixed-effects modelling was used to analyse repeated measures (changes from baseline to one, three, six and 12 months in KSS and HSS score). Continuous data were tested for normality using the Shapiro‒Wilk test. For data with a normal distribution, a two-sample t test was used to detect significant differences between groups. For continuous data that failed the Shapiro‒Wilk normality test (i.e., non-normally distributed data), the non-parametric Mann‒Whitney U test was used to compare differences between the two groups. The chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used for categorical variables. The differences between groups are presented with 95% confidence intervals. A p value of 0.05 was considered significant. We produced statistical figures using GraphPad Prism software version 9.5.1.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that in the treatment of medial tibiofemoral osteoarthritis, PSI-assisted UKA performed by inexperienced surgeon on a learning curve can yield excellent radiographic and functional outcomes in short-term follow-ups, but cannot provide superior clinical outcomes to the MPI-assisted UKA performed by an experienced surgeon.

For patients who underwent UKA, the implant size, position, and component alignment during the surgical procedure were markedly associated with both short-term clinical outcomes and long-term prosthesis survivorship, a finding that underscores the relevance of our study’s focus on optimizing component positioning for inexperienced surgeons [22]. Chatellard et al.[23] retrospectively studied 559 medial UKAs and found a mean 10-year prosthesis survival rate of 83.7%, with long-term tibial component survivorship reduced by deviations such as > 2 mm change in joint space height, > 3 mm change in tibial component obliquity, > 5° slope value, and > 6° divergence between components; notably, a > 2 mm increase in joint space was also linked to worse functional outcomes. This aligns with the core objective of this study: to evaluate whether PSI can help inexperienced surgeons achieve component alignment accurate enough to avoid such adverse prognostic deviations. In our results, we observed no significant between-group differences in key alignment parameters (e.g., tibial posterior slope angle, hip–knee–ankle angle) between PSI-assisted UKA (by inexperienced surgeons) and MPI-assisted UKA (by experienced surgeons), suggesting that PSI may mitigate the alignment-related risks highlighted by Chatellard et al. even for less experienced operators.

Kamenaga et al.[9] further indicated that tibial component height and obliquity negatively affected 2-year Oxford knee scores, while Kang et al.[24]’s finite element analysis showed that femoral component deviation from central placement alters inter-component stress and intra-articular cartilage. These studies reinforce why our assessment of both radiological alignment (e.g., femoral/tibial varus/valgus angles) and functional outcomes (KSS, HSS scores) was critical. Our finding of comparable functional scores between groups (e.g., mean 12-month KSS: 93.4 in PSI vs. 93.6 in MPI) directly reflects that the alignment accuracy achieved with PSI did not compromise functional recovery, addressing the concerns raised by Kamenaga et al. and Kang et al. about alignment-related functional deficits.

Oxford partial knee microplasty instrumentation has prevailed in achieving accurate component alignment [13, 25, 26]. However, for surgeons on a UKA learning curve or in low-volume hospitals, its use may not consistently ensure impeccable implantation. An analysis of 40,522 UKAs demonstrated that low-volume surgeons (< 10 cases/year) had a significantly higher revision rate than high-volume surgeons (> 30 cases/year), and another study of 5,791 cemented UKAs found a 1.69-fold higher revision risk in low-volume hospitals (< 10 cases/year) vs. high-volume hospitals (> 40 cases/year), with malalignment, instability, and fracture as key revision causes [12]. These data contextualize the value of our study’s results: our observation that inexperienced surgeons using PSI achieved alignment and functional outcomes comparable to experienced surgeons using MPI suggests that PSI could help reduce the revision-related disparities between low- and high-volume surgeons/hospitals identified in these large-scale analyses. Specifically, the lack of significant between-group differences in alignment parameters (e.g., femoral varus/valgus angle) in our cohort implies that PSI may minimize the malalignment risks that drive higher revision rates in low-volume settings. To improve the positioning of the UKA components for inexperienced surgeons on a learning curve and surgeons in low-volume hospitals, various techniques have been proposed, such as robotic-assisted systems, accelerometer-based portable navigation systems, and computer-assisted UKA [27–29]. However, the promotion of these techniques remains stagnant due to their low cost-effectiveness. In recent years, PSI has shown advantages in improved clinical outcomes and implant survivorship in UKA. Several studies have investigated the accurate positioning of the femoral component using PSI [16, 17]. However, the conclusions regarding the accuracy of PSI-assisted tibial implantation remains controversial [19, 30]. Kerens et al. [16] prospectively studied 26 patients who underwent PSI-assisted Oxford UKA to assess the consistency between the preoperative plan and the definitive performance in terms of component size and position. The planned parameters were accurately translated into operative implantation except in the tibial rotational position. Adjustments and re-resections significantly decreased with the use of PSI. In an otherwise prospective study by Alvand and colleagues [26], deep resections of the tibial plateau were more commonly observed in the PSI-assisted UKA group than in the conventional instrumentation group. In addition, several studies demonstrated no differences between PSI-assisted UKA and conventional UKA in both femoral and tibial implant positioning [19, 20]. The explanation of these conflicting conclusions may be inaccurate positioning of the PSI guide. As the PSI guide was designed to register on the osteophytes of the femoral condyle and tibial plateau, a divergence in osteophytes could negatively affect guide positioning. These conflicting conclusions regarding the superiority of PSI over conventional instrumentation prompted this study to provide definite evidence for UKA-inexperienced surgeons.

The operation time of the PSI group was greater than that of the MPI group, mainly due to the operator's lack of experience and proficiency. However, there were no statistically significant differences in blood loss, incision length, gasket thickness, or postoperative complications. In addition, there were no significant differences in postoperative component alignment, angle or functional scores. The KSS and HSS scores were significantly improved from the baseline to each follow-up time, indicating a similar benefit from the two surgical methods. The PSI-assisted UKA could significantly elevate patient-reported outcomes as reported previously [16, 19, 20]. However, there were no statistically significant differences in radiological and functional outcomes between the two groups at each follow-up. A possible explanation for this is that in our hospital, surgical treatment is more favoured for patients with high-grade osteoarthritis. In this population, osteophytes around the knee joint surface were always patent and can be easily identified as markers to design and guide jig placement. Thus, the accuracy of PSI-assisted bone resection can be improved. The functional outcomes were comparable between both groups. The differences were reported to be underestimated due to the inherent limitation of patient-reported outcome measurements [31, 32]. In addition, there was only a short-term assessment of PSI-assisted UKA. Bearing dislocation and component loosening were reported to occur at a high risk after postoperative 3 years [10, 33]. Thus, the short-term comparison was relatively limited.To improve the accuracy of jig positioning and bone cut, the osteophytes should not be removed prior to guide placement, as the PSI guide was designed to register on the osteophytes. Furthermore, in this study, the PSI design was based on CT data, in which the soft tissue and cartilage were not observed. In the surgical procedure, these tissues should be thoroughly eliminated to ensure that the PSI guide fits properly with the bone surface.

This study has several limitations. First, this research was performed at a single centre, and the sample size was relatively small, which may reduce the power of the statistical analysis. Second, as CT was not routinely performed, there was a lack of rotational angle assessment. This may lead to inadequate comparison of component positioning between the two surgical procedures. Third, we only completed a follow-up of 12 months. The long-term outcomes and survivorship have not been assessed. Further research with more cases and long-term follow-up is essential to draw a validated conclusion. Finally, 3D printing is well-integrated in UKA surgical practice. Further research is needed to determine whether the increased intervention costs can be balanced with the observable advantages of this new technology. There is a need for a formal cost-effectiveness analysis to ameliorate decision-making.

Conclusions

The PSI-assisted UKA performed by inexperienced surgeon on a learning curve can yield excellent radiographic and functional outcomes in short-term follow-up, despite not demonstrating superior clinical outcomes to MPI-assisted UKA performed by an experienced surgeon. The PSI could potentially serve as an alternative instrumentation for UKA beginners with low-volume surgical caseload.
