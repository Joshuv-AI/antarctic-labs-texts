Development and characterization of high-efficiency cell-adapted live attenuated vaccine candidate against African swine fever
Authors: Min Ho Kim, Ashan Subasinghe, Yongkwan Kim, Hyeok-Il Kwon, Yehjin Cho, Kiramage Chathuranga, Ji-Won Cha, Ji-Yoon Moon, Ji-Hyeon Hong, Jin Kim, Seung-Chul Lee, Niranjan Dodantenna, Nuwan Gamage, W A Gayan Chathuranga, Yeonji Kim, In-Joong Yoon, Joo Young Lee, In Pil Mo, Weonhwa Jheong, Sung-Sik Yoo, Jong-Soo Lee
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAfrican swine fever (ASF), a contagious and lethal haemorrhagic disease of domestic pigs and wild boars, poses a significant threat to the global pig industry. Although experimental vaccine candidates derived from naturally attenuated, genetically engineered, or cell culture-adapted ASF virus have been tested, no commercial vaccine is accepted globally. We developed a safe and effective cell-adapted live attenuated vaccine candidate (ASFV-MEC-01) by serial passage of a field isolate in CA-CAS-01-A cells. ASFV-MEC-01, isolated via repeated plaque purification using next-generation sequencing analysis, was obtained at passage 18 and showed significant attenuation in 4- and 6-week-old pigs. ASFV-MEC-01 conferred 100% protection against challenge with lethal parental ASFV, which correlated with high ASFV-specific humoral and cellular immune responses. Additionally, ASFV-MEC-01 was not detected in blood until 28 days post-inoculation. Global transcriptome analysis showed that ASFV-MEC-01 lacking 12 genes triggered stronger innate antiviral responses than the parental virus, as exemplified by high levels of mRNA encoding interferon regulatory and inflammatory genes in PAM cells. Ectopic expression of most deleted genes increased replication of DNA viruses by suppressing production of interferons and pro-inflammatory cytokines. Among the genes deleted from ASFV-MEC-01, MGF100-1R interacted specifically with the scaffold dimerization domain of TBK1, thereby preventing TBK1 dimerization and impairing TBK1-mediated type I IFN and NF-κB signalling. These results suggest that attenuation of ASFV-MEC-01 may be mediated by induction of stronger type I IFN and NF-κB signalling within the host innate immune system. Thus, ASFV-MEC-01 could be a safe and effective live attenuated ASFV vaccine candidate with commercial potential.KEYWORDS: African swine fever, CA-CAS-01-A cell, live-attenuated virus, ASFV-MEC-01, vaccine, Type I IFN, MGF100-1R

Introduction

African swine fever virus (ASFV) is the sole member of the genus Asfivirus (family, Asfarviridae). ASFV is a large, enveloped and structurally complex virus harbouring a single covalently closed double-stranded DNA genome of 170–193 kb that encodes 150–167 genes (depending on the strain isolated) [1]. ASFV has a predominant tropism for monocyte and macrophage lineage cells, and is currently classified into at least 24 genotypes based on the p72 major capsid protein gene (B646L) [2]. African swine fever (ASF), caused by ASFV, is a highly contagious, haemorrhagic and lethal viral disease of swine. The mortality rate of nearly 100% causes significant economic losses to the swine industry in affected countries [3]. The highly virulent ASFV genotype II was introduced into Georgia in 2007, and since 2018 the virus has emerged in China and other Asian countries, causing tremendous economic losses [4].

To date, there is no safe and effective ASF vaccine. Outbreaks are still controlled primarily through testing and slaughter. Over the past decades, various strategies have been adopted to develop safe and effective vaccines, including subunit vaccines, inactivated vaccines, viral vector vaccines, and live attenuated vaccines [5]. However, this pursuit has been limited by the inherent complexity of ASFV itself, alongside incompletely understood protective immune responses [6].

Recently, several studies report that naturally attenuated strains and genetically modified strains featuring different virulence-related gene deletions induce strong protection against highly virulent ASFV [7-11]. However, approval of these live attenuated vaccine candidates for commercial use poses certain challenges. For example, most genetically modified live attenuated ASFVs exhibit restricted cell tropism and replicate preferentially only in porcine primary macrophages and monocytes, which poses a significant obstacle to large-scale commercial production [11-13]. By contrast, adaptation of various ASFV field isolates for replication in established cell lines is accompanied by significant genomic modifications and decreased virulence in swine [14-16]. Nevertheless, genetic modifications introduced into the viral genome during cell passage sometimes leads to loss of replication capacity within porcine primary cells, as well as reduced immunogenicity [16]. Therefore, development of a safe and effective live attenuated ASFV that provides robust protective efficacy, along with feasible commercial-scale production, remains a significant area of interest.

Recently, we established the ASFV-permissive CA-CAS-01-A cell line [17]. Here, we report development of the ASFV-MEC-01 virus from a field isolate using CA-CAS-01-A cells. ASFV-MEC-01 harbours 12 gene deletions in the left variable region (LVR), and is a safe and effective live attenuated vaccine candidate. ASFV-MEC-01 virus showed a strong safety profile in 4- and 6-week-old pigs, and conferred 100% protection against lethal challenge with parental ASFV. Detailed transcriptome analysis revealed that infection with ASFV-MEC-01 triggers stronger upregulation of genes associated with host antiviral immunity than the parental virus. We also examined the possible role of the deleted genes in immune evasion. Further investigations revealed that MGF100-1R interacts with TBK1, thereby impairing TBK1-mediated type I interferon (IFN) and NF-κB signalling in host immune cells. The ASFV-MEC-01 virus is an effective live attenuated vaccine candidate that shows a good balance between safety and protective efficacy, making it suitable for field-level use.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and viruses

Primary PAM (Optipharm, Korea) and immortalized PAM (3D4/21) (ATCC CRL-2843) cells were cultured in RPMI medium (Cytiva, USA), and CA-CAS-01-A cells were cultured in αMEM medium (Gibco, USA), and HEK293 T (ATCC CRL-11268) and PK-15 (ATCC CCL-33) cells were cultured in DMEM medium (Cytiva, USA). All cell culture mediums were supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic (Gibco, USA). All the cell lines and primary cells used in this study were maintained in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator at 37°C. Isolation of Korea/Wild boar/Hwacheon/2020 virus was carried out as described previously [18]. Genome sequencing was performed by next-generation sequencing (NGS) (Celemics Inc., Korea), and confirmed the same genotype as ASFV Georgia 2007/1 (genotype II). All experiments dealing with ASFV were conducted following the standard operating procedure (SOP) in the biosafety level 3 (BSL-3) laboratory of the National Institute of Wildlife Disease Control and Prevention (NIWDC) of the Republic of Korea. Herpes simplex virus-GFP (HSV-GFP), Adenovirus-GFP (ADV-GFP), and Vaccinia virus-GFP (VACV-GFP) viruses were used in in vitro phenotypic analysis.

Serial passage of ASFV in CA-CAS-01-A cells

CA-CAS-01-A cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 105 cells/T25 flask in αMEM medium supplemented with 2% FBS and 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin. Twelve hours after cell seeding, culture media was replaced with virus production-serum-free medium (VP-SFM) (Gibco™) and infected with isolated ASFV. Twenty-four hours later, again media were changed with VP-SFM supplemented with 2% FBS, and 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin, in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator at 37°C. On day 7 cell supernatant and cell pellet were collected separately. The cell pellet was subjected to two-time freezing and thawing, and it was re-suspended with the supernatant. Then, 2 ml of the suspension was continuously passaged similarly up to 18 cycles, and resulted supernatant being used for the plaque purification.

Plaque purification

CA-CAS-01-A cells were seeded at a density of 4 × 105 cells/well in 6 well plates. The cells were inoculated with 1 ml of 10-fold serially diluted supernatant collected at 18 passages and incubated for 24 h. Then cells were washed with sterilized PBS and overlaid with 3 mL of 0.8% SeaPlaque agarose (Lonza, Switzerland) containing MEM medium and incubated in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator at 37°C for 7 days. The viruses with isolated plaques were recovered from CA-CAS-01-A cells

Next-generation sequencing

ASFV DNA was extracted from infected cells as described previously [18]. Next-generation sequencing was performed by Celemics, Korea. Briefly, approximately 500 ng of DNA was fragmented into segments between 150 and 250 bp using the Bioruptor Pico Sonication System (Diagenode, Belgium). Sequencing libraries were then hybridized with an ASFV DNA capture probe (Celemics, Korea). Streptavidin-coated paramagnetic beads were then added and allowed to bind to the biotinylated capture probes. An external magnetic field was then applied and unbound DNA was removed. The captured DNA was then eluted from the magnetic beads by digestion of the cRNA capture probes, and then purified. The enriched DNA was sequenced on a NextSeq 550 instrument (Illumina, USA) with 2 × 151 bp reads.

qPCR analysis

ASFV virus DNA copy number was indirectly quantified using commercially available VetMAX™ African Swine Fever Virus Detection Kit (ThermoFisher™, A28809) targeting the ASFV P72 gene (B646L). RT-PCR was performed in the QuantStudio™ 6 Flex Real-Time PCR (Applied Biosystems™, 4485691) machine using the Quant Studio Real-Time PCR Software according to the manufacturer's instructions for a total of 40 cycles.

Virus growth kinetics

Primary PAMs were grown as a monolayer in a 12-well plate (SPL, Korea) for 12 h. The PAMs were then infected with parental ASFV or ASFV-MEC-01 at an MOI of 0.1, and the culture supernatant and cells were collected at 2, 24, 48, 72, 96, and 120 h (three replicates each). Collected cell pellets were freeze-thawed three times and pooled with the culture supernatants. The Ct value for the ASFV DNA template was calculated by qPCR analysis.

Animal experiments

ASFV-free and antibody-seronegative pigs (4–6 weeks old) were used to test the virulence of Korea/Wild boar/Hwacheon/2020 strain and the ASFV-MEC-01 strain, as well as for the vaccine safety and efficacy experiments. During the experiments, animals were housed in closed containers in the BSL-3 facility at the NIWDC, with access to water and food ad libitum and a 12 h dark/light cycle (ambient temperature, 18–23°C, 40–60% relative humidity). All animals were acclimated for 7 days before the experiment.

To evaluate the virulence of the Korea/Wild boar/Hwacheon/2020 strain in domestic pigs, animals were divided into test and control groups. The test group received an IM injection of 102 HAD50 Korea/Wild boar/Hwacheon/2020 strain, while the control group received PBS. Clinical signs and rectal temperatures were monitored on days 0, 4, 7, 10, 11, and 15 post-inoculation. All pigs were necropsied immediately after death or at the end of the experimental period. The humane endpoint was met when pigs exhibited markedly reduced activity and lay down. Spleen, heart, lung, liver and kidney samples were collected from dead or euthanized pigs.

The safety and efficacy of ASFV-MEC-01 were evaluated in two distinct vaccination regimes. In the first experiment, 6-week-old pigs were divided into test and mock vaccination groups. The test group received an IM injection of 105 HAD50 ASFV-MEC-01 in a prime-boost vaccination regimen on days 0 and 14, while the control group was injected with the same volume of PBS. At 28 days post-vaccination (DPV), both groups were challenged with 102 HAD50 Korea/Wild boar/Hwacheon/2020 strain via IM injection. Rectal temperature, survival, and blood sample collection, were conducted on the days depicted in Figure 2A. In the second experiment, 4-week-old pigs were divided into test and mock vaccination groups, which were vaccinated similarly on days 0 and 21. Again, the control group received PBS. Both groups were challenged similarly on 35 DPV. Clinical symptoms, survival, and blood sample collection, were conducted on the days depicted in Figure 3A.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

ASFV-specific antibody titres were assessed using the ID Screen ASFV indirect ELISA (ID.vet, France), according to the manufacture's guidelines. Briefly, negative control, positive control, and test serum were diluted (1:20) in dilution buffer. Subsequently, 200 µL of these diluted samples were placed in wells pre-coated with ASFV p32, p62, and p72 antigens and incubated for 45 minutes at 21⁰C. Following this, the wells underwent a triple wash with 300 µL of wash buffer and were then exposed to 100 µL of 1X anti-multi-species HRP conjugate for 30 minutes at 21⁰C. After another washing step, 100 μL of substrate solution was added to the plates and allowed to react for 15 minutes at 21⁰C in darkness, followed by halting the reaction with 100 µL of stop solution. The optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm wavelength using an Apollo LB 913 ELISA reader (Berthold technologies, USA). ASFV-specific antibody titres were expressed as a sample to positive ratio (S/P)% (S/P % = [ODsample − ODNC ÷ ODPC − ODNC]× 100). When the S/P% value was 40% or higher, the animals were regarded as ASFV antibody-positive.

Cell culture supernatants were used to evaluate the secreted porcine IFN-α, IFN-β, IL-12 and IL-6 by sandwich ELISA. Commercial ELISA kits for Porcine IFN-α (R&D system, USA), Porcine IFN-β (CUSABIO, China), and Porcine IL-6 (CUSABIO, China) were employed following the manufacturer's protocols.

Analysis of cell-mediated immune responses

The frequency of vaccine-induced ASFV-specific IFN-γ responses was analysed in PBMCs by stimulating with parental ASF virus. Briefly, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were separated from whole blood of second vaccination experiment animals on 28 DPV by density gradient centrifugation with Histopaque 1077 (Sigma-Aldrich, Spain) in SepMate-50 as per manufacturers’ instructions (Stemcell Technologies, Canada). PBMCs were seeded in 12 well cell culture plates at a density of 5 × 106 cells/well and stimulated with either 106 HAD50 of parental ASFV or 3 µg/well of phytohemagglutinin as a positive control. Following 36 h incubation at 37 °C, cell supernatants were collected and porcine IFN-γ ELISA was conducted following manufacturer recommendations.

RNA-Seq (Quant-seq) analysis

Total RNA was extracted from primary PAM cells at 24 h after ASFV WT or ASFV-MEC-01 infection, with 3 biological replicates per group using the NucleoSpin RNA extraction kit (MACHEREY-NAGEL, Germany) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The quality and integrity of total RNA samples were assessed using the Agilent 4200 TapeStation (Agilent technologies) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The Quant-Seq 3′ mRNA-Seq was commercially commissioned to Ebiogen. In brief, the cDNA library was generated using the Quant-Seq 3′ mRNA-Seq library prep kit FWD for Illumina (Lexogen) and trimming was done with BBDuk (> average Q20). Sequencing was done on the NextSeq 500/550 75 bp SE system (Illumina) and aligned to a Sus scrofa reference sequence Sscrofa11.1 (NCBI) with the bowtie2 using bedtools. Normalization of read counts was done by EdgeR and analysed the differential expression of genes with fold changes >1.5 and P values <0.05 on ExDEGA (Ebiogen).

Plasmids

The complete nucleotide sequence of MGF100-1R, MGF110-8L, MGF110-10L-14L, and MGF300-1L were synthesized (Genescript, China), and cloned into pIRES-Flag and/or pEXPR-Strep vectors. Generation of the IFN-β promoter and luciferase reporter plasmids are described elsewhere [19]. The cGAS pIRES-3×Flag-tagged plasmid was kindly gifted by Jae U. Jung (Department of Cancer Biology, Lerner Research Institute, Cleveland Clinic). Full-length STING and IRF3-5D genes were cloned into the Flag-tagged pIRES vector. Full-length TBK1 subcloned into pIRES-Flag, pIRES-V5, and pEBG-GST vectors. Truncated TBK1 deletion mutants were PCR-amplified and subcloned into a pEBG-GST vector.

Plasmid transfection and virus infection

3D4/21 and PK15 cells were transfected with indicated plasmids using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, USA). HEK293T cells were transfected with indicated plasmids using polyethyleneimine (PEI, Polysciences, USA). Cell infection was carried out in reduced serum (1% FBS) containing media for 2 h. The uninfected virus was removed by replacing the culture supernatant with a complete medium. The fluorescence intensity was measured from cell extracts harvested at 12 h post-infection (hpi) and 24 hpi using the GloMax-Multi Detection System (Promega, USA). Viral titres were determined by standard plaque assay in Vero cells. Images were acquired using an Olympus IX71 microscope fitted with a U-LH100HG fluorescence excitation light source. Poly dA:dT (Invivogen, USA) was transfected with Lipofectamine 2000.

Immunoblotting

Cells were washed with PBS and lysed with Radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (RIPA) buffer (25 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1% NP-40, and 1% sodium deoxycholate), which were supplemented with protease inhibitor (PI) cocktail (Roche) and 1 mM Na3VO4 (Sigma). Equal amounts of total protein in whole-cell lysates were subjected to SDS-PAGE followed by immunoblotting with the indicated antibodies.

Pulldown and co-immunoprecipitation assays

Cells were washed with PBS and lysed with RIPA buffer supplemented with 1 mM Na3VO4 and PI cocktail. Cell lysates were pre-cleared by incubation with plain sepharose 6B beads (Cytiva) for 1 h at 4°C. The pre-cleared cell lysates were incubated overnight with a 50% slurry of glutathione sepharose 4B (GST) beads (Cytiva) for GST pulldown at 4°C. For co-immunoprecipitation analysis, pre-cleared lysates were incubated overnight at 4°C with specified antibodies. Subsequently, immune complexes were pulled down using protein A/G PLUS-agarose beads (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) for 3–4 hours at 4°C. The immunoprecipitated complexes were washed thrice with the lysis buffer before proceeding to immunoblot analysis.

Luciferase reporter assay

HEK293 T cells cultured in 12-well cell culture plates were transfected with 400 ng of IFN-β driving luciferase (firefly) plasmid, the internal control TK-Renilla luciferase (Renilla) reporter plasmid and relevant individual plasmids using PEI. At 24 h post-transfection, the cell layers were washed with PBS and lysed with 1× Passive Lysis buffer (Promega, E194A) for 15 min. Finally, the luciferase activity was estimated using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, E1980) according to the manufacturer's protocol. The values indicate the firefly luciferase activity normalized to the Renilla luciferase activity.

Confocal microscopy

To confirm the interaction between ASFV-MGF100-1R and TBK1, PK15 cells were cultivated in eight-well chamber slides (ibid) and transfected with plasmids encoding Strep-MGF100-1R along with either Flag-TBK1 or Flag-MGF100-1R. At 24 hours post-transfection, cells were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde (Sigma) in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) for 10 minutes. Subsequently, the cells were permeabilized with 100% methanol at -20°C for 20 minutes and then blocked with 2% BSA in PBS at room temperature for 1 hour. Then samples were incubated with primary antibodies in a blocking buffer overnight at 4°C. After three washes with PBS-T (PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20), the cells were incubated with the appropriate secondary antibody for 1 hour at room temperature. Then cells were treated with RNase and stained with DAPI (4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole) at room temperature for another 10 minutes, washed three times in PBS-T, and mounted in mounting solution. Images were acquired under a Nikon laser scanning confocal microscope (C2plus) and analysed using NIS-Elements software.

Results

The ASFV isolate South Korea/wild boar/hwacheon/2020 is highly virulent in domestic pigs

The clinical courses and pathology of ASF in domestic pigs can vary from peracute to chronic depending on a variety of factors, including the immune status of the animals and the virulence of the ASFV isolates [20]. To assess the virulence of South Korea/Wild boar/Hwacheon/2020 isolate (ASFV-WT) in conventional domestic pigs, two groups of conventional pigs were either non-infected or intramuscularly infected at a dose of 102 HAD50 per head of ASFV-WT (Figure S1A). Pigs infected with ASFV-WT displayed clinical signs, including increased body temperature (>40.5 C⁰) by 4 days post-infection (Figure S1B). The clinical signs in South Korea/Wild boar/Hwacheon/2020 virus-infected pigs developed into more severe manifestations in the following days, and all pigs died or necropsied within 15 days post-infection (Figure S1C). Postmortem macroscopic lesion scoring revealed that pigs infected with ASFV-WT had significantly higher median cumulative macroscopic lesion scores, compared to the control group of pigs (Figure S1D). Internal organs, including the spleen, heart, lungs, liver, kidney and blood collected from dead or euthanized pigs, were analysed for viremia load by RT-PCR. As shown in Figure S1E, all tested organs were strongly positive for viral DNA, with the spleen exhibiting the highest viral load, as observed before [21]. These results indicated that the South Korea/Wild boar/Hwacheon/2020 strain isolated from wild boar is highly virulent in domestic pigs.

Generation and characterization of CA-CAS-01-A cells adapted ASFV strain

The adaptation of ASFV in heterologous cell lines is usually accompanied by a progressive loss of virulence when inoculated into domestic pigs [14-16,22]. To obtain a cell-adapted ASFV strain that replicates effectively in an established cell line, the South Korea/Wild boar/Hwacheon/2020 virus was serially passaged into the CA-CAS-01-A cell line. Replication of ASFV in CA-CAS-01-A cells increased with the increasing number of passages, and a specific single clone of ASFV was isolated via plaque purification after 18 passages. To assess the genetic changes acquired during the adaptation, next-generation sequencing (NGS) was performed, and the genomic alignment was performed with the original ASFV strain, South Korea/Wild boar/Hwacheon/2020 (GenBank: OR159219.1). Consensus sequence alignments revealed the complete consecutive 12 known gene deletions in the LVR, partial deletion in the N terminal region of the MGF360-8L and 9 uncharacterized sequence deletions (Figure 1(A), (B) and table S1). To validate the precision of identified genomic deletion, an RT-qPCR assay was performed using viral DNA and primers specifically targeting the deleted genes (Primers used for the RT-qPCR are listed in Table S2). Unlike the parent virus, the DNA of the passaged virus failed to exhibit positive amplification curves for none of the completely deleted 12 genes identified from NGS analysis while showing positive amplification curves for the p72 gene (Figure 1(C)). However, since the MGF360-8L gene was partially deleted (959 bp to 478 bp deletion in MGF360-8L, which encodes in the 24,014 bp to 24 973 bp region in the ASFV genome), a positive amplification curve was detected in the RT-qPCR analysis (Figure S2). Further, conventional PCR assays were also performed on DNA from both the parental and passage viruses with specific primers that target deleted genes (Primers used for the PCR are listed in table S3). Specific PCR products of all completely deleted 12 genes and the partially deleted gene with expected molecular size were detected in the parental virus but not in the passaged virus (Figure 1(D)). The absence of amplification curves for the 12 completely deleted genes in the passaged virus and the presence of expected PCR products in the parental virus demonstrates that RT-qPCR and conventional PCR can serve as effective DIVA (Differentiating Infected from Vaccinated Animals) assays. Collectively, these results validate the consecutive 12 gene deletions in the left variable region of the passage virus, and it was designated as ASFV-MEC-01.

Comparative growth kinetics of ASFV-MEC-01 in primary swine macrophages

Swine monocytes and macrophages are the primary target cells for ASFV infection [23]. Adaptation in cell lines reported a decrease in the ability of ASFV to infect and replicate in primary macrophage cells [16,24]. Hence, it was crucial to evaluate whether ASFV-MEC-01 could replicate in primary swine macrophages. Ex vivo replication kinetics of ASFV-MEC-01 were assessed in primary PAMs compared to the parental ASFV in multistep growth curves. Primary PAMs were infected either with parental ASFV or ASFV-MEC-01 at a MOI of 0.1 and samples were collected at indicated time points after infection. The replication kinetics of ASFV-MEC-01 was similar to parental ASFV, with no significant difference in growth rate and virus titre throughout the experiment period (Figure 1(E)). These results revealed that identified gene deletion in the left variable region doesn't significantly affect the tropism of the ASFV-MEC-01 to the primary swine macrophages.

In vivo safety and protective efficacy of ASFV-MEC-01 virus as a vaccine candidate

To determine the potential use of ASFV-MEC-01 as a live attenuated vaccine candidate, its virulence was examined in pigs through two distinct inoculation and subsequent challenge experiments. First, the effective immunization dose 105 HAD50 was selected via a preliminary dose determination experiment where the 103.5 HAD50 and 104.5 HAD50 showed 66% survival, and the 105.5 HAD50 vaccinated group showed 100% protection, normal body temperature, less viremia, and high antibody titre (Figure S3). In the first experiment, two groups (n = 3/group) of four-weeks-old conventional pigs were intramuscularly vaccinated with either 105 HAD50 of ASFV-MEC-01 along with booster vaccination at 14 DPV or mock vaccinated with the same volume of PBS (Figure 2(A)). During the post-vaccine observation period, all three animals vaccinated with the ASFV-MEC-01 virus remained clinically normal and disease-free with no significant increase in body temperature similar to the mock vaccinated animal (Figure 2(B)). The post-vaccine observations indicated that 12 gene deletions in the left variable region attenuate the virulence of parental ASFV in domestic pigs.

To assess the ability of the ASFV-MEC-01 vaccine to protect animals against virulent parental ASFV, all vaccinated pigs were challenged with IM inoculation of 102 HAD50 parental ASFV at 28 DPV (Figure 2(A)). As shown in Figure 2B, C, all mock-vaccinated animals developed fever with ASFV-associated clinical signs and died within 11 days post-challenge (DPC). Conversely, pigs vaccinated with ASFV-MEC-01 survived and remained clinically normal during the post-challenge observation period. The level of systemic viremia was evaluated by detecting the viral genome in blood using the qPCR. As expected, animals that received a mock vaccination displayed a persistently high level of systemic viremia from 7 to 11 DPC until the animals died. Importantly, pigs vaccinated with ASFV-MEC-01 exhibited a moderate to minimal presence of systemic viremia (Blood, oral swab and rectal swab) by 18 DPC, showing a decreasing trend by 21 DPC (Figure 2(D), Figure S4A-B). At the point of death or experiment termination, all pigs were subjected to necropsies. During the post-mortem examination mock-vaccinated control pigs exhibited acute manifestations of the disease, including haemorrhagic splenomegaly, multifocal hemorrhagic lymphadenitis, and hemorrhagic necrosis in multiple organs. In contrast, pigs vaccinated with ASFV-MEC-01 showed fewer pathological manifestations with significantly lower scores for macroscopic lesions (Figure 2(E)).

Increasing evidence highlighted the relationship between the level of humoral immune responses and protective immunity [25,26]. Hence, the kinetics of ASFV-specific antibody responses in the serum were examined with an ASFV p32, p62, and p72 antigen-specific ELISA. As depicted in Figure 2F, pigs vaccinated with ASFV-MEC-01 showed ASFV-specific IgG responses at 7 days after the first vaccination. The second vaccination dose bolstered the antibody response, reaching peak titres at 21 days. While the antibody titre slightly declined in two pigs on the day of the challenge, it steadily increased until the termination of the experiment.

In the second experiment, two groups of six-weeks-old conventional pigs were intramuscularly vaccinated with either 105 HAD50 of ASFV-MEC-01 (n = 4), followed by a booster shot at 21 days post-vaccination, or were mock vaccinated with an equivalent volume of PBS (n = 2) (Figure 3(A)). Similar to the first experiment, all pigs vaccinated with the ASFV-MEC-01 vaccine remained free from typical ASFV-induced fever or clinical signs during the post-vaccination monitoring period (Figure 3(B)). Following the virus challenge, three pigs vaccinated with ASFV-MEC-01 showed a transient rise in body temperature at 7 DPC and gradually turned to normal and all animals were clinically healthy at the end of the 14 days post-challenge observation period. Conversely, all pigs that received the mock vaccination displayed severe ASFV-related clinical symptoms and died within 10 DPC (Figure 3(C)). Among the four pigs vaccinated with the ASFV-MEC-01 vaccine, two pigs showed systemic viremia at 7 DPV, after which the viremia levels gradually declined and were undetectable by 35 DPV. Following the virus challenge, pigs vaccinated with ASFV-MEC-01 progressively decreased the viremia until 14 DPC (Figure 3(D)). Similar to the observations in the first experiment, pigs vaccinated with ASFV-MEC-01 exhibited antibody responses after the first vaccination, further increased after the booster vaccination, and continued to rise following the challenge (Figure 3(E)).

The cellular immune responses are thought to have a significant role in protective immunity against ASFV [27,28]. The frequency of ASFV-specific IFNγ producing T cells induced by immunization correlates to the degree of protection from ASFV challenge [29]. To assess the ASFV-MEC-01 vaccination effect on generating ASFV-specific IFNγ-producing T cells, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC) isolated at 28 days post-vaccination were stimulated again with the parental ASFV strain and the level of secreted IFNγ was measured using ELISA. Importantly, pigs that received the ASFV-MEC-01 vaccine displayed a significant level of ASFV-specific IFNγ-producing T cell responses at 28 days post-vaccination (Figure 3(F)). Altogether, these findings suggest that the identified 12 gene deletions in LVR of the ASFV-MEC-01 virus substantially attenuated its virulence in vivo while retaining the capability to induce a protective immune response against a lethal parental virus.

Global transcriptome analysis of IFN and pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion in ASFV-MEC-01 and ASFV-infected primary PAMs

To understand the potential mechanism of ASFV-MEC-01 attenuated virulence, gene expression signatures in ASFV-MEC-01 or ASFV-WT infected primary PAMs were analysed by RNA sequencing analysis at 24 hours post-infection. Compared with the parental ASFV infection, in total 220 differentially expressed genes (log2 fold change >1.5 and p-value < 0.05), including 103 upregulated and 117 downregulated genes were identified in ASFV-MEC-01 infected PAMs (Figure 4(A)). Interestingly, PAMs infected with the ASFV-MEC-01 strain exhibited heightened expression of genes regulating the immune response against viruses, including interferon stimulatory genes and inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, and complement pathway genes relative to the parental strains (Figure 4(B), (C)).

To further validate the ASFV-MEC-01 induced pronounced immune regulatory gene expression, a set of genes that encode well-characterized key innate molecules of the antiviral responses were subjected to RT-qPCR analysis. Consistent with the RNA-seq results, ASFV-MEC-01 infection shows significantly increased expression of genes encoding IFN-β, pro-inflammatory cytokines, and ISGs compared to the parental virus (Figure 4(D)). Moreover to check the presence of ASFV-WT and ASFV-MEC-01 related IFN and pro-inflammatory cytokine production phenotypes, both viruses were infected in primary PAM cells with 0.1, 0.5 and 1 MOI for 18 hours. Cell supernatant was analysed to detect the secreted IFN-β, IFN-α, IL-6, and IL-12. As shown in the results (Figure 4(E)), ASFV-MEC-01 significantly enhanced the production of type 1 IFNs and pro-inflammatory cytokines compared to ASFV-WT infection. Overall, these results suggest that attenuated ASFV-MEC-01 strains have the potential to boost the activation of innate immune responses and facilitate the induction of effective adaptive immune responses, thereby instigating a protective immune response against a lethal parental virus.

MGF100-1R, MGF110-8L, MGF110-10L-14L, and MGF300-1L negatively regulate antiviral responses

To validate the effect of deleted genes on virulence and type-1 IFN signalling responses, we transiently transfected characterized 12 deleted genes and control plasmid into 3D4/21 cells followed by ADV-GFP infection. Protein expression was confirmed by immunoblotting (Figures S5A–K). As shown in Figure 5A–C, gene-transfected cells showed significantly high ADV-GFP replication when compared to control plasmid-transfected cells. Moreover, we measured the amount of IFN-β (Figure 5(D)), IL-6 (Figure 5(E)), IFN-α (Figure 5(F)), and IL-12 (Figure 5(G)) secreted from the virus-infected cells using ELISA. Consistent with the virus replication results, we found that the deleted gene overexpressed cells secreted fewer IFNs and cytokines than the control cells (Figure 5(D–G)). Among the 12 deleted genes, MGF100-1R, MGF110-8L, MGF110-10L-14L, and MGF300-1L were chosen for further study due to their significantly enhanced viral replication compared to other deleted genes. Subsequently, we assessed their capacity to modulate host antiviral responses. These genes were stably overexpressed in 3D4/21 cells, and the expression was confirmed by immunoblotting. Subsequently, the cells were infected with HSV-GFP (Figure 6(A–D)) and VACV-GFP (Figures S6A–D) as surrogate viruses since both viruses are sensitive to interferons like ASFV [30]. As shown in Figures 6(A–D) and S6A-S6D, HSV-GFP and VACV-GFP replication in MGF100-1R, MGF110-8L, MGF110-10L-14L, and MGF300-1L overexpressing cells was significantly higher than that in control cells. Consistent with the results of virus replication, ELISA results demonstrated that all tested genes led to a suppression of IFN-β and IL-6 secretion (Figure 6(E–H), S6E–H). Thus, the increased virus replication observed in cells overexpressing MGF100-1R, MGF110-8L, MGF110-10L-14L, and MGF300-1L appears to be directly linked to the compromised production of type I IFNs and pro-inflammatory cytokines.

MGF100-1R suppresses cGAS-STING-mediated type I IFN and NF-κB signalling

Among the selected genes, MGF100-1R excels in immune evasion. Thus, MGF100-1R is prioritized for detailed analysis of its immune regulatory mechanism. After internalization, ASFV dsDNA is detected by the cGAS sensor, triggering cGAS-STING-dependent type I IFN and NF-κB signalling [31,32]. We examined the phosphorylation of IFN and NF-κB signalling molecules in MGF100-1R overexpressing 3D4/21 (Figure 7(A)) and PK15 (Figure 7(B)) cells after ADV-GFP infection. Results indicate that cells overexpressing MGF100-1R display a noticeable reduction in ADV-GFP-induced phosphorylation of TBK1, IRF3, IĸBα, p65, and STAT1 when compared to control cells (Figure 7(A, B)). Consistent with the reduction in type I IFN and NFκB signalling, MGF100-1R overexpressing cells exhibited lower mRNA expression of genes encoding IFNs, ISGs, and pro-inflammatory cytokines in both 3D4/21 and PK15 cells following ADV-GFP infection (Figure 7(C, D)). These findings strongly indicate that the MGF100-1R plays a pivotal role in the evasion of host immune responses by dampening the cGAS-STING-mediated type I IFN and NF-κB signalling.

MGF100-1R antagonizes the cGAS-STING pathway by targeting TBK1

It has been reported that several ASFV-encoded proteins are involved in the inhibition of type I IFN production by modulating the cGAS-STING signalling axis in different levels [33], and our results indicated that HSV-GFP (Figure 6(E)) and VACV-GFP (Figure S6E) induces very low levels of IFN-β and IL-6 production in MGF100-1R expressing cells than control cells. Therefore, to investigate which step in the cGAS-STING signalling is targeted by MGF100-1R, we performed a luciferase promoter assay by coexpressing MGF100-1R with several IFN-related genes. The results showed that MGF100-1R inhibited Poly (dA:dT)-STING-, cGAS- STING-, 2′,3′cGAMP- STING-, STING-, and TBK1-induced IFN-β promoter activity in a dose-dependent manner. However, activation of the IFN-β promoter induced by IRF3-5D was not affected by the increased expression of MGF100-1R (Figure 8(A)), indicating that MGF100-1R specifically inhibited the cGAS-STING pathway at the TBK1 level. Next, to determine whether MGF100-1R interacts with TBK1, we conducted co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) assays with Flag-MGF100-1R and GST-TBK1 transfected HEK293 T cell lysates using an anti-Flag antibody. As shown in Figure 8B, results show clear interaction between overexpressed TBK1 and MGF100-1R. Furthermore, the interaction was further validated by the findings presented in Figure 8C, where we observed a distinct interaction between overexpressed MGF100-1R and endogenous TBK1. In line with these Co-IP results, confocal microscopy analysis confirmed the co-localization of MGF100-1R with TBK1 in both ectopic overexpression and endogenous conditions (Figure 8(D, E)). These results strongly suggest that ASFV MGF100-1R counteracts cGAS-STING signalling by targeting TBK1.

MGF100-1R targets CCD1 domain of TBK1 to inhibit TBK1 dimerization

To further dissect the mechanism of action of MGF100-1R-mediated cGAS-STING signalling inhibition at the TBK1 level, we assessed the domains of TBK1 that are responsible for the interaction with MGF100-1R. Truncated GST-TBK1 fragments (Figure 8(F)) were co-expressed together with Flag-MGF100-1R in HEK293 T cells and tested for their ability to bind to MGF100-1R in a GST pulldown assay. As shown in Figure 8G, results revealed that 385–729 aa region of TBK1, which comprised of CCD1 and CCD2 is critical for the MGF100-1R interaction. Further narrowing down of the binding domain specifically reveals that it is not the CCD2, but rather the CCD1 region that is crucial for the interaction with MGF100-1R (Figure 8(H)). TBK1 typically forms homodimers, primarily through intermolecular interactions in the CCD1. Structural studies suggest that TBK1 dimerization aids in the transautophosphorylation of a crucial serine residue (S172) within the kinase's activation loop [34-36]. Given that MGF100-1R interacts with TBK1's CCD1, this knowledge prompted us to ask whether MGF100-1R attenuates the activation of TBK1 by inhibiting its dimerization. To explore this, we evaluated the impact of MGF100-1R on TBK1 dimerization by transfecting HEK293T cells with GST-tagged and V5-tagged TBK1 expression plasmids alongside increasing amounts of Flag-tagged MGF100-1R plasmids. The pulldown analysis demonstrated a notable decrease in TBK1 dimerization with increasing levels of MGF100-1R (Figure 8(I)). An intact TBK1 dimer undergoes K63-linked polyubiquitination on lysines 30 and 401, and these modifications are required for TBK1 activity [37]. Hence, we examined the effect of MGF100-1R on K63-linked polyubiquitination of TBK1. As shown in Figure 8J, MGF100-1R significantly inhibits the K63-linked polyubiquitination of TBK1 in a dose-dependent manner. Since K63-linked ubiquitination and dimerization of TBK1 leads to its Ser172 phosphorylation [37], finally, we assessed the impact of MGF100-1R on the Ser172 phosphorylation of TBK1. As expected, results demonstrate a significant reduction of Ser172 phosphorylation of TBK1 in an MGF100-1R dose-dependent manner (Figure 8(K)). Collectively, these data indicate that ASFV MGF100-1R specifically targets the CCD1 domain of TBK1 to obstruct its homodimerization, thereby impairing K63-linked polyubiquitination and Ser172 phosphorylation, thus disrupting TBK1 activation.

Discussion

ASF remains one of the most contagious and deadly swine viral diseases, causing a tremendous economic burden. Despite the steady progress of ASFV vaccine research and development worldwide, there is currently no globally recognized vaccine or treatment for ASFV. Among various vaccine development strategies, classical vaccines based on inactivated ASFV have proven ineffective due to their inability to induce effective CTL responses [38,39]. In addition, the complexity of ASFV, as well as gaps in our understanding of its protective mechanisms and specific antigens, have hindered the development of efficient subunit vaccines [39]. However, live attenuated ASF vaccine candidates, developed either through cell line adaptation or genetic modification technologies, are thought currently to be the most promising avenue for vaccine development [22,40-42].

Here, we describe a cell-adapted live ASFV-MEC-01 virus derived from a field isolate. This adapted virus shows significant safety and efficacy as a live attenuated ASFV vaccine candidate. First, the ASFV-MEC-01 virus was obtained after 18 passages of a field isolate in CA-CAS-01-A cells, and its genetic stability up to passage 38 was confirmed by repeated plaque purification and NGS. ASFV-MEC-01 lacks 12 consecutive genes, coupled with partial deletion of a multigene family, in the LVR of the genome but still showed growth kinetics comparable with those of the parental strain. Second, we found that ASFV-MEC-01 was avirulent in 4- and 6-week-old pigs, and induced robust ASFV-specific humoral and cellular immune responses without viremia for up to 28 DPV. Third, vaccination with the ASFV-MEC-01 virus conferred 100% protection against challenge with the lethal parental ASFV, and challenged pigs had no clinical symptoms or viremia. Fourth, the ASFV-MEC-01 virus-induced significantly higher expression of genes associated with innate antiviral responses than the parental virus in primary PAMs. Fifth, ectopic expression of most deleted genes increased the replication of DNA viruses by suppressing the production of IFNs and pro-inflammatory cytokines. Finally, among the deleted genes, MGF100-1R was identified as one of the main virulent genes, and plays a role in antagonizing type I IFN and NF-κB responses by specifically inhibiting TBK1 dimerization.

Generally, ASFV exhibits narrow cellular tropism and infects and propagates only in porcine primary macrophages and monocytes derived from peripheral blood or other tissues [43]. Therefore, stable cell lines are required for large-scale production of a commercial, genetically stable, live ASF vaccine. Recently, research has focused on developing cell lines for the generation of live attenuated ASFV vaccine candidates, and a lot of cell lines susceptible to ASFV infection have been introduced [44]. However, cell-adapted ASFVs from certain cell lines show a reduced ability to replicate in primary target cells due to genetic mutations acquired during adaptation [16,24]. In addition, cell-adapted ASFVs also show low virulence and compromised immunogenicity in pigs, resulting in no protection against challenge with homologous virus [16,45].

In our previous study, we reported that CA-CAS-01-A is an ASFV-permissive homogeneous cell line capable of sustaining ASFV replication (18). In the present study, after 18 rounds of ASFV passage, we isolated the ASFV-MEC-01 virus though plaque purification and NGS. Genetic stability was confirmed up to passage 38 (no gene deletions or additions at 38th passage), which is a desirable characteristic for a potential candidate live attenuated vaccine. Genetic stability means that pigs immunized with ASFV-MEC-01 are able to generate ASFV-specific antibodies, along with a marked increase in the population of ASFV-specific IFN-γ-producing cells compared with that in their unvaccinated counterparts. These findings are consistent with those of previous studies [46], and indicate a consistent pattern with respect to the role of humoral and cellular immunity in protection against ASFV.

Comparative genomic analysis demonstrated that ASFV possesses an “open” pan-genome containing 151–174 open reading frames [47]. Notably, statistical data indicate that a number of deletions and mutations in the MGF genes located in the LVR generate attenuated ASFV strains while preserving their protective immunogenicity in the field [42]. For instance, the ASFV-G-ΔMGF recombinant virus derived from the highly virulent ASFV Georgia 2007 isolate by specific deletion of six genes from MGF360 to MGF505 results in complete attenuation and confers protection against a challenge with a virulent homologous virus [11]. Deletion of MGF360 and MGF505 from the virulent ASFV isolate Benin 97/1 has the same effect [48]. Recently, the Arm07ΔMGF cell-adapted strain derived from the virulent Armenia2007 strain lacking 11 MGF genes in the LVR showed significantly reduced virulence in pigs, and provided protection against the parental virus [15]. Moreover, a recent study has demonstrated the development of live attenuated ASFV vaccines VNUA-ASFV-LAVL2 by serial passage of a field isolate in porcine alveolar macrophages and immortalized porcine alveolar macrophage cell line. This approach has provided 100% protection against the challenge of contemporary pandemic ASFV field isolate [49]. Similar to previous findings, we found that the ASFV-MEC-01 virus harbouring 12 consecutive complete and one partial gene deletion also showed complete attenuation in pigs and provided protective immunity against the virulent parental virus (Figures 2 and 3), however, we still do not understand the mechanism by which the deleted MGF genes induce virus attenuation and protective immunity.

The presence of viremia, as well as the persistence of live attenuated ASFV strains in pigs would be potential drawbacks that could lead to reversion-to-virulence and/or environmental contamination [50]. Several effective live attenuated ASFV vaccines, including ASFV-G-ΔI177L/ΔLVR, ASFV-G-ΔMGF, and BeninΔMGF, trigger persistent viremia after vaccination [11,22]. Previous reports highlight the instability induced by LVR mutations, leading to continuous replication [15,24] this continued viral replication raises concerns about the potential risk of the virus regaining virulence, followed by unintended spread. Here, we found that ASFV-MEC-01 was cleared rapidly from the blood, while pigs were still able to sustain protective immunity, as evident in the challenge studies (Figure 2(D), 3D, and S4). In the 4-week pig experiment, ASFV-MEC-01 was eliminated from the blood by 28 DPV, and by 35 DPV in the 6-week experiment. Postmortem examination revealed no evident viremia in the organs from ASFV-MEC-01-immunized pigs, and no clinical or pathological lesions (Figure 2(E)). These features of ASFV-MEC-01 are considered very significant, as the lack of viremia may be related to the deletion of the MGF genes.

Due to its high pathogenicity, most pigs infected with virulent ASFV die before acquiring adequate protective immunity [51]. The pathogenicity of virulent ASFV involves interference with the host innate immune responses. Many studies of the ASFV-host interactome report that type I IFN and NFκB pathways are the primary targets of virulent ASFV proteins [52]. Indeed, the virulent Armenia/07 virus counteracts the synthesis of IFN-β, a key mediator between the innate and adaptive immune responses [53], and IFN treatment inhibits ASFV replication significantly both in-vitro and in vivo [54,55]. These findings reinforce the idea that differences in ASFV virulence versus attenuation may be based on ASFV-mediated innate immune modulation. In line with this, comprehensive gene expression analyses of primary PAMs infected with ASFV-MEC-01 or parental ASFV suggest that the attenuated virulence of ASFV-MEC-01 results primarily from enhanced activation of host type I IFN and inflammatory responses (Figure 4). In other words, genes deleted from ASFV-MEC-01 during adaptation are involved in regulating type I IFN and inflammatory responses. To confirm this, we performed virus replication and cytokine analysis experiments, which confirmed that most of the deleted MGF genes suppressed the production of IFNs and pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby enhancing the replication of DNA viruses. Among the deleted MGF genes, we selected MGF100-1R (due to its potential for higher virulence than the other genes tested) and examined the underlying mechanism. However, as reported by Liu et al, the deletion of MGF100-1R in the ASFV Chinese strain resulted in a similar virulence level as the parental virus [56]. The attenuation and protection provided by the ASFV-MEC-01 is not an effect of single gene deletion but the simultaneous deletion of several genes. In addition, we are currently undertaking experiments to examine whether other MGF genes deleted from the ASFV-MEC-01 strain act via specific molecular mechanisms.

TBK1 is a central kinase acting at the intersection of the cGAS-STING-activated type I IFN and NF-kB signalling pathways [57]. Because TBK1 plays a central role in the innate immune system, pathogens, especially viruses, have evolved numerous strategies to evade host antiviral immune responses by targeting TBK1 to achieve successful infection [58]. Some ASFV proteins interact with TBK1 and interfere with downstream innate immune signalling pathways. Sun et al. reported that A137R interacts with TBK1 and increases its degradation through autophagy-mediated lysosomal degradation [59]. Additionally, DP96R inhibits TBK1 phosphorylation [60], and p1215L promotes TBK1 degradation through the E3 ligase RNF128 [61]. In this study, we identified a novel mechanism used by ASFV MGF100-1R to avoid host antiviral innate immune responses. A luciferase assay-based functional genomic analysis and protein-protein interaction analyses identified TBK1 as a target molecule of MGF100-1R, and confirmed that MGF100-1R interacts with the CDD1 of TBK1. Consequently, MGF100-1R inhibits TBK1 dimerization and abrogates TBK1 K63-linked ubiquitination and phosphorylation of Ser 172 (Figure 8). Taken together, the results suggest a mechanism involving host IFN signalling and deleted MGF genes is associated with attenuation and viremia of the ASFV-MEC-01 strain.

Several critical factors are essential for the development and commercialization of an effective and ideal live attenuated ASFV. First, it must be safe and effective for pigs of all ages, including young pigs. Second, a cell line suitable for mass-production, while maintaining the genetic stability of the attenuated virus, is needed. Third, the developed strain must not be susceptible to virus shedding or reversion-to-virus, and must be capable of DIVA.

To summarize, we report the development of the ASFV-MEC-01 virus, a safe and effective live attenuated vaccine candidate, from a field isolate using the CA-CAS-01-A cell line. The ASFV-MEC-01 virus could be a vaccine candidate that fulfils all of the critical requirements for an ASF vaccine, and will pave the way for future development of attenuated vaccines for controlling ASFV.
