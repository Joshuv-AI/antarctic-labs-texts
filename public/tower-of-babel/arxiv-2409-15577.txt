Partial-wave projection of relativistic three-body amplitudes
Raúl A. Briceño, Caroline S. R. Costa, Andrew W. Jackura

Abstract
We derive the integral equations for partial-wave projected three-body scattering amplitudes, starting from the integral equations for three-body amplitudes developed for lattice QCD analyses. The results, which hold for generic three-body systems of spinless particles, build upon the recently derived partial-wave projected one-particle exchange, a primary component of the relativistic framework proven to satisfy S S matrix unitarity. We derive simplified expressions for factorizable short-distance interactions, 𝒦 3 \mathcal{K}_{3} , in two equivalent formalisms — one symmetric under particle interchange and one asymmetric. For the asymmetric case, we offer parameterizations useful for amplitude analysis. Finally, we examine toy models for 3 ​ π 3\pi systems at unphysically heavy pion masses with total isospins 0 , 1 , 0,1, and 2 2 .

III
Separable Parameterizations

In this section, we consider a special class of parameterizations, where the asymmetric K matrix,
\widehat{\mathcal{K}}_{3}
, can be written as a separable function. For such parameterizations, we will show that the integral equations for
\widehat{\mathcal{T}}
in Eq. (
17
) have an algebraic solution. We subsequently comment on separable parameterizations for the symmetric K matrix. We begin this section by first defining this class of parameterizations. As discussed in Sec.
II.4
, incorporating features like isospin leads to a straightforward modification of all the expressions after performing the partial wave projection. As a result, here we focus in deriving the building blocks in the
LS
-basis for our elastic scattering process, and leave the expressions in an enlarged

II.4
implicit.

III.1
Separable K matrix parameterizations

In order to justify the separable parameterizations of
\widehat{\mathcal{K}}_{3}
, we begin by outlining some minimal properties it must satisfy. Above threshold,
\widehat{\mathcal{K}}_{3}
is a purely real function. Because of the spurious singularities of the spherical harmonic at threshold, the partial wave projected
\widehat{\mathcal{K}}_{3}
must include barrier factors that exactly cancel these singularities. These barrier factors,
\mathcal{B}_{LS}(k,s)
, need to cancel the singularities associated with the threshold of the pair sub-system as well as the threshold associated with the pair-spectator system. Because of this, we define the barrier factors as

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{LS}(k,s)=k^{L}q_{k}^{\star\,S}\,.

(33)

The remaining part of the K matrix is meromorphic in the remaining energy variables, allowing us to choose the separable parameterization as

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{K}}_{3;L^{\prime}S^{\prime},LS}^{J}(p,k)=\mathcal{B}_{L^{\prime}S^{\prime}}(p,s)f_{L^{\prime}S^{\prime}}(p^{2})\,\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{3;L^{\prime}S^{\prime},LS}^{J}(s)\,f_{LS}(k^{2})\,\mathcal{B}_{LS}(k,s)\,.

(34)

This definition ensures that the remainder functions,
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{3}
and
f_{LS}
, are real and free of on-shell singularities in a domain near the three-body threshold of the complex (
s
,
\sigma_{k}
,
\sigma_{p}
)-hyperplane. Consequently, one can parameterize these by Taylor expansions in this region. Note, since
\sigma_{k}=\sigma_{k}(s,k^{2})
, we can freely choose to have the expansion in either
\sigma_{k}
,
\sigma_{p}
variables or in terms of
k^{2}
,
p^{2}
, and we have chosen the latter.

The functions
f_{LS}(k^{2})
include residual sub-channel momentum dependence which we are free to choose, e.g.,

\displaystyle f_{LS}(k^{2})=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\alpha_{LS}^{(j)}\,k^{2j}\,,

(35)

for some
n
and real parameters
\alpha_{LS}
. Finally, the reduced K matrix
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{3}
is a function of
s
only, thus we can write it as, e.g.,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{3;L^{\prime}S^{\prime},LS}^{J^{P}}(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=0}^{n^{\prime}}\frac{\beta_{L^{\prime}S^{\prime},LS}^{J^{P}\,(j)}}{s_{0}^{(j)}-s}+\sum_{j=0}^{n^{\prime\prime}}\gamma_{L^{\prime}S^{\prime},LS}^{J^{P}\,(j)}\,s^{j}\,,

(36)

for real parameters
\beta_{L^{\prime}S^{\prime},LS}^{J^{P}}
and
\gamma_{L^{\prime}S^{\prime},LS}^{J^{P}}
for some
n^{\prime}
and
n^{\prime\prime}
. For convenience, we write the matrix elements of Eq. (

\displaystyle\left[\widehat{\mathcal{K}}_{3}^{J^{P}}(p,k)\right]_{L^{\prime}S^{\prime},LS}=\left[h(p)\right]_{L^{\prime}S^{\prime}}\,\left[\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{3}^{J^{P}}(s)\right]_{L^{\prime}S^{\prime},LS}\,\left[h(k)\right]_{LS}\,,

(37)

where the

This class of separable parameterizations can be generalized to include a sum over any number of terms of the form of Eq. (
37

from Fredholm theory,

\displaystyle\left[\widehat{\mathcal{K}}_{3}^{J^{P}}(p,k)\right]_{L^{\prime}S^{\prime},LS}=\sum_{j}\left[h(p)\right]_{L^{\prime}S^{\prime};j}\,\left[\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{3}^{J^{P}}(s)\right]_{L^{\prime}S^{\prime},LS;j}\,\left[h(k)\right]_{LS;j}\,.

(38)

This additional index can be absorbed by enlarging the space in which

III.2

\widehat{\mathcal{T}}

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{L}}^{J^{P}}(p,s)

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{k}\,\mathcal{L}^{J^{P}}(p,k)\cdot h(k)\,,

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{3}-\mathcal{M}_{2,S^{\prime}}(\sigma_{p})\tilde{\rho}(\sigma_{p})\right)\cdot h(p)-\int_{p^{\prime}}\,\mathcal{D}^{J^{P}}(p,p^{\prime})\cdot\tilde{\rho}(\sigma_{p^{\prime}})\,h(p^{\prime})\,,

(53)

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{R}}^{J^{P}}(s,k)

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{p}h(p)\cdot\mathcal{R}^{J^{P}}(p,k)\,,

\displaystyle=h(k)\cdot\left(\frac{1}{3}-\mathcal{M}_{2,S^{\prime}}(\sigma_{k})\tilde{\rho}(\sigma_{k})\right)-\int_{k^{\prime}}\,h(k^{\prime})\rho(\sigma_{k}^{\prime})\cdot\mathcal{D}^{J^{P}}(k^{\prime},k)\,.

(54)

IV
Bound-state spectator amplitudes

In this section, we consider the case where the two-particle pairs can form a bound state. This is worthwhile for two reasons. First, for sufficiently heavy quark masses, lattice QCD calculations have found that both the
\sigma
and the
\rho
appear to be bound
[
91
,
66
,
74
,
92
]
. For these unphysical masses, exploratory calculations of scattering processes involving these states have been performed in Refs.
[
75
,
82
]
, among others.
Second, as shown in Refs.
[
18
,
86
]
, the consideration of two-body bound states provides some of the best checks of the three-body formalism. In Sec.
V
, we use this limiting case to provide some checks on partial wave projection performed in Sec.
II
. As in Sec.
III
, we consider only amplitudes in the
LS
basis. Isospin and multi-channel processes can be incorporated using the steps presented in Sec.
II.4
.

We define the resulting two-body scattering amplitude as
\mathcal{M}^{J^{P}}_{\varphi b}
, where
b
denotes a bound two-body system and
\varphi
is the remaining third scalar particle. Since we work with generic scalar particles, each pair combination in principle has a distinct bound state. Thus, the reaction becomes
b_{k}+\varphi_{k}\to b_{p}+\varphi_{p}
. The amplitude can be written as the sum of two terms,

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{J^{P}}_{\varphi b}=\mathcal{M}^{J^{P}}_{\varphi b,\mathcal{D}}+\mathcal{M}^{J^{P}}_{\varphi b,\rm df},

(55)

where the first comes from following the LSZ prescription to
\mathcal{D}^{J^{P}}
and the second comes from
\mathcal{M}_{3,\textrm{df}}^{J^{P}}
. We begin by defining the first of these. We make use of Eq. (
10
), which we rewrite here for convenience

\mathcal{D}^{J^{P}}(p,k)=\mathcal{M}_{2}(\sigma_{p})\cdot d^{J^{P}}(p,k)\cdot\mathcal{M}_{2}(\sigma_{k}).

(56)

In the presence of two-body bound states,
\mathcal{M}_{2}
has poles of the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{2}(\sigma_{k})=-\frac{g_{k,b}^{2}}{\sigma_{k}-\sigma_{k,b}}+\mathcal{O}\!\left((\sigma_{k}-\sigma_{k,b})^{0}\right)

(57)

where
g_{k,b}
denotes the residue of the scattering amplitude at the bound state pole and
\sigma_{k,b}
is the pole location. The label
k
emphasizes that the bound state is one of the pair associated with spectator
k
. The bound state mass is then
\sqrt{\sigma_{k,b}}
, and
g_{k,b}
can be understood as the coupling between the composite and two-particle scattering states. From Eq. (
56
), we see that
\mathcal{D}^{J^{P}}
has these same poles. Assuming there is only one such bound state in each pair, we see that

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{J^{P}}(p,k)=\left(-\frac{g_{p,b}}{\sigma_{p}-\sigma_{p,b}}\right)\,g_{p,b}g_{k,b}\,d^{J^{P}}(q_{p,b},q_{k,b})\left(-\frac{g_{k,b}}{\sigma_{k}-\sigma_{k,b}}\right)+\cdots\,,

(58)

where the ellipses denote terms which are not simultaneous poles in both
\sigma_{p}
and
\sigma_{k}
. We have introduced
q_{k,b}
as the relative momentum of the bound state and the spectator, given by

\displaystyle q_{k,b}=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{s}}\,\lambda^{1/2}(s,m_{k}^{2},\sigma_{k,b})\,.

(59)

Having identified the pole structure of
\mathcal{D}^{J^{P}}
, we can use the LSZ reduction formula to obtain the definition for
\mathcal{M}^{J^{P}}_{\varphi b,\mathcal{D}}
,

\mathcal{M}^{J^{P}}_{\varphi b,\mathcal{D}}=\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma_{p}\to\sigma_{p,b}\\
\sigma_{k}\to\sigma_{k,b}\end{subarray}}\frac{(\sigma_{p}-\sigma_{p,b})(\sigma_{k}-\sigma_{k,b})}{g_{p,b}g_{k,b}}\,\mathcal{D}^{J^{P}}(p,k),

(60)

or equivalently,

\mathcal{M}^{J^{P}}_{\varphi b,\mathcal{D}}=g_{p,b}g_{k,b}\,d^{J^{P}}(q_{p,b},q_{k,b})\,.

(61)

So far, we have only applied the LSZ prescription to
\mathcal{D}^{J^{P}}
. Next, we will analyze the prescription to the divergent free part of the amplitude, considering both the symmetric and asymmetric cases.

IV.1
LSZ for asymmetric representation

In a similar manner, we proceed to derive the

V.1
Review of numerical technique for coupled-channel systems

We set to compute
\mathcal{M}_{\sigma\pi}^{T(J^{P})}
and
\mathcal{M}_{\rho\pi}^{T(J^{P})}

\displaystyle\left[\mathcal{M}^{T(J^{P})}_{\varphi b}\right]_{\beta\alpha}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\begin{subarray}{c}\sigma_{p}\to\sigma_{\beta}\\
\sigma_{k}\to\sigma_{\alpha}\end{subarray}}\frac{(\sigma_{p}-\sigma_{\beta})(\sigma_{k}-\sigma_{\alpha})}{g_{\beta}g_{\alpha}}\left[\mathcal{M}^{T(J^{P})}_{3}(p,k)\right]_{\beta\alpha}\,,

\displaystyle=g_{\beta}g_{\alpha}\,\left[d^{T(J^{P})}(q_{\beta},q_{\alpha})\right]_{\beta\alpha}+\left[\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\varphi b}^{\,T(J^{P})}(q_{\beta},s)\cdot\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}(s)\cdot\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{\varphi b}^{T(J^{P})}(s,q_{\alpha})\right]_{\beta\alpha},

(74)

where
\alpha,\beta=LSI
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\varphi b}^{\,T(J^{P})}
, and
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{\varphi b}^{T(J^{P})}
are simple extensions of
\widetilde{\mathcal{L}}_{\varphi b}^{J^{P}}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{R}}_{\varphi b}^{J^{P}}
, defined in Eqs. (
63
) and (
64
), in the
LSI
-basis. Since all particles are identical, we need not to distinguish species type on the bound state poles and residues and simply label them by
\alpha

I=0
,
S=0
belongs to the
\sigma
, while
I=1
,
S=1
belongs to the
\rho

As detailed in the preceding sections, all quantities can be written in terms of the amputated ladder amplitude,
d^{\,T(J^{P})}
, which is the
LSI
-space extension of Eq. (
10
). We summarize the key steps needed to find numerical solutions of the integral equation for
d^{T(J^{P})}
. The integral equation for
d^{T(J^{P})}
follows from the generalization of Eqs. (
6
) and (
10
) to the
LSI
basis,

\displaystyle d^{\,T(J^{P})}(p,k)=-\mathcal{G}^{T(J^{P})}(p,k)-\int_{k^{\prime}}\mathcal{Q}^{T(J^{P})}(p,k^{\prime})\cdot d^{\,T(J^{P})}(k^{\prime},k)\,,

(75)

where the kernel
\mathcal{Q}^{T(J^{P})}
is

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}^{T(J^{P})}_{\beta\alpha}(p,k)=\mathcal{G}^{T(J^{P})}_{\beta\alpha}(p,k)\,\mathcal{M}_{2,\alpha}(\sigma_{k})\,.

(76)

We remind the reader that
\mathcal{M}_{2,\alpha}=\mathcal{M}_{2,IS}
in the
LSI
-basis.

Recall from Eq. (
5
) that the OPE propagator includes a cut-off function
H
. This cutoff function effectively sets an upper limit on the integral above, represented by
k^{\prime}_{\mathrm{max}}
. Therefore, the integral Eq. (
9
) becomes

\displaystyle\int_{k^{\prime}}\rightarrow\int_{0}^{k^{\prime}_{\mathrm{max}}}\!\textrm{d}k^{\prime}\,\frac{k^{\prime 2}}{(2\pi)^{2}\omega_{k}^{\prime}}\,.

Two commonly used options for defining
H
are a smooth or a hard cut-off. The smooth cut-off has proven advantageous when considering the formalism for describing finite-volume quantities
[
32
,
17
]
. Unfortunately, defining an analytic function that is exactly equal to
1
in the physical region above the three-particle threshold is impossible. Having an analytic cut-off function is key to be able to deform contours, which is generally needed for solving integral equations for
\mathcal{M}_{3}

[
86
]
. As a result, such a smooth function breaks the desired analytic properties for scattering amplitudes.

Alternatively, one can introduce a hard cut-off in momentum space and fix
H=1
everywhere within this domain. In the following numerical results, we will use this and set the maximum momentum to ensure that none of the parameterizations used for
\mathcal{M}_{2}
have unphysical singularities. The minimal but not necessarily sufficient criteria for this requires that
\sigma_{k}\geq 0
.

Equation (
75
) is a system of Fredholm integral equations of the second kind, for which algorithms for numerical solutions are well-known, see e.g.,
[
93
]
. First we note that the kernel has a pole singularity at
k^{\prime}=q_{\alpha}
(or
\sigma_{k}=\sigma_{\alpha}
) corresponding to the bound state of
\mathcal{M}_{2}
. To circumvent this pole, we use Cauchy’s theorem to deform the integration path to a contour in the complex
k^{\prime}
-plane like that presented in Ref.
[
86
]
. We then follow the Nyström method, which approximates the integral in Eq. (
75
) by a quadrature rule over discrete momenta in the integration interval. Let
N_{k}
be the number of momenta appearing in one channel and
N_{c}
be the total number of channels in
LSI
space.
9
9

9

In general, we can choose different contours and number of mesh points per channel. In this work, we choose that each channel has the same contour and mesh.
Choosing a sufficient contour, we replace the integral in Eq. (
75
) by a quadrature rule of order
N_{k}
. For some target
T(J^{P})
, this leads to the approximate equation

\displaystyle d_{\beta\alpha}^{\,T(J^{P})}(p,k)=-\mathcal{G}_{\beta\alpha}^{T(J^{P})}(p,k)-\sum_{\gamma}\sum_{j=0}^{N_{k}-1}\overline{\mathcal{Q}}_{\beta\gamma}^{T(J^{P})}(p,k_{j})\,d^{\,T(J^{P})}_{\gamma\alpha}(k_{j},k)\,,

(77)

where
k_{j}
are the mesh points in momentum space, and the modified kernel is

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{Q}}_{\beta\alpha}^{T(J^{P})}(p,k_{j})\equiv\mathcal{Q}^{T(J^{P})}_{\beta\alpha}(p,k_{j})\frac{k_{j}^{2}}{(2\pi)^{2}\,\omega_{k_{j}}}\,\Delta_{j},

(78)

with
\Delta_{j}

10
10

10

We use Gauss-Legendre quadrature, which requires performing a variable transformation to relate the standard weights to the form shown in Eq. (

Finally, we evaluate

Using standard computational linear algebra, we solve the approximate linear system,

\displaystyle\mathbf{d}

\displaystyle=-\mathbf{G}-\mathbf{\bar{Q}}\cdot\mathbf{d}\,,

\displaystyle=-\left[\mathbf{1}+\mathbf{\bar{Q}}\right]^{-1}\cdot\mathbf{G}\,,

(79)

where the solution

\displaystyle[\mathbf{d}]_{mn}=d_{\beta\alpha}^{T(J^{P})}(k_{i},k_{j})\,.

(80)

Similarly,
[\mathbf{G}]_{mn}=\mathcal{G}^{T(J^{P})}_{\beta\alpha}(k_{i},k_{j})

[\mathbf{\bar{Q}}]_{mn}=\overline{\mathcal{Q}}^{T(J^{P})}_{\beta\alpha}(k_{i},k_{j})

Once a solution for

V.2
Parameterizations considered

Here we consider a simple class of parameterizations that can generate a two-body bound state for
S=0
and
1
. We parameterize the amplitude via the phase shift
\delta_{S,I}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{2,IS}(\sigma_{k})=\frac{16\pi\sqrt{\sigma_{k}}}{q_{k}^{\star}\cot\delta_{S,I}-iq_{k}^{\star}}\,,

(81)

where the symmetry factor of Eq. (
15
) is
1/2
since the pions are treated as identical isovector states. As exploited extensively in previous work
[
88
,
18
,
94
]
, an S-wave leading order effective range expansion (ERE) can be used to generate two-body bound states. As a result, we only consider parameterizations for the
S=0
two-body amplitude defined by

\displaystyle q^{\star}_{k}\cot\delta_{0,I}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{a_{0,I}},

(82)

where
a_{0,I}
is the scattering length in the
I=0
or
2
channel. For
a_{0,I}>0
, the resulting two-body amplitudes would have a two-body bound state with real value residues.

For a P-wave bound state, the use of a leading order ERE leads to unphysical residue for bound states in the amplitude.
Partial wave projected amplitudes near threshold must be kinematically suppressed by barrier factors,

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{2,IS}(\sigma_{k})\propto q_{k}^{\star\,2S}.

(83)

Near the pole, the amplitude is proportional to
g_{\alpha}^{2}
,
g_{\alpha}
being the bound state coupling of the
S=1,I=1
channel, cf. Eq. (
57
).
In order for these two conditions to be simultaneously satisfied, the couplings must be imaginary,

\displaystyle g_{\alpha}^{2}\propto q_{\alpha}^{\star\,2S}=(i\kappa_{\alpha})^{2S},

(84)

where
\kappa_{\alpha}
is the binding momentum, i.e., Eq. (
16
) evaluated at
\sigma_{k}=\sigma_{\alpha}
. A simple exercise shows that this condition would not be respected by a P-wave bound state generated from a leading order ERE parametrization.

Instead, for the isovector P-wave systems, we consider a Breit-Wigner (BW) parameterization

\displaystyle q^{\star}_{k}\cot\delta_{1,1}

\displaystyle=\frac{(m_{\mathrm{BW}}^{2}-\sigma_{k})}{\sqrt{\sigma_{k}}\,\Gamma_{1}^{\mathrm{BW}}(\sigma_{k})},\qquad\Gamma_{1}^{\mathrm{BW}}(\sigma_{k})=\frac{g^{2}_{\mathrm{BW}}}{6\pi\sigma_{k}}q^{\star 2}_{k}.

(85)

For carefully chosen values of
m_{\mathrm{BW}}
and
g_{\mathrm{BW}}
, one can ensure that Eq. (
84
) is satisfied for a P-wave bound state.
It is worth noting that a BW near threshold is equivalent to a next-to-leading order ERE, that is the latter would also have been a reasonable choice.

VI
Summary and outlook

Using the results derived in Ref.
[
19
]
for the OPE, we have constructed integral equations for partial-wave projected three-body relativistic scattering amplitudes. The integral equations are presented in Sec.
II
, with details presented in Appendix
A
, for two equivalent formalisms where the three-body K matrix is symmetric or asymmetric under particle interchange. While a pracitioner can choose either framework for analyses, we advocate for the asymmetric formalism due to the relative ease for parameterizing the three-body K matrix, which is illustrated in our numerical applications in Sec.
V
. In particular, a class of flexible parameterizations useful for data analysis are presented in Sec.
III
, where
\mathcal{K}_{3}
is factorizable in the kinematics of the initial and final state allowing one to parameterize
\mathcal{K}_{3}
in a manner similar to analyses in the two-body sector. In addition, it is shown that factorizable parameterizations for
\mathcal{K}_{3}
reduce the computational complexity of the integral equations.

In Sec.
IV
, we consider the scenario where one of the two-particle pairs forms a bound state. In particular, we show how the LSZ formalism can be used to reconstruct two-body amplitudes from the three-body amplitudes. Finally, in Sec.
V
, we explore numerical solutions of the three-body amplitudes for toy models for the
3\pi
channels in total isospin
T=2,1,0
that include the
\rho
and
\sigma
as
\pi\pi
bound states. Using the expressions derived in Sec.
IV
for the asymmetric formalism, we find that our results satisfy two-body unitarity below the three-body threshold for all models considered, as well as the expected threshold behavior for partial-wave projected amplitudes.

Given the rapid developments in this line of research, it is worthwhile summarizing some key outstanding problems related to the scattering theory of three-body systems. First, in this work, we considered amplitudes that have not been symmetrized under the interchange of the spectator. Using the notation used in the literature, these are the
\mathcal{M}_{3}^{(u,u)}
. The next step is to symmetrize these and construct amplitudes that may be used to generate Dalitz plots, as was done, for example, in Ref.
[
29
]
, for the
T(J^{P})=3(0^{-})
lattice QCD calculation. Using formalism presented in, for example, Ref.
[
16
]
, we believe this should be straightforward. As already mentioned, the formalism presented here is built from the partial wave projection of the OPE performed in Ref.
[
19
]
, which only assumed that the particles involved had no intrinsic spin. Lifting such an assumption, although technical, can and will be done. Additionally, including coupled two- and three-particle systems, while attempted in Ref.
[
33
]
, requires further investigation. Going beyond these immediate problems, one can envision formulating dispersive representations for the three-body amplitudes. As the connection between scattering theory and lattice QCD matures for few-body systems, preserving S matrix principles such as unitarity and analyticity is vital to exploring the excited QCD spectrum.

Appendix A
Partial wave projection

We follow the procedure as presented in Refs.
[
16
,
19
]
and further references therein, namely first projecting to definite helicity amplitudes of definite
J
, then forming definite
J^{P}
amplitudes by taking linear combinations to the
LS
basis.

Given the three body helicity amplitude
\mathcal{M}_{3;\ell^{\prime}\lambda^{\prime},\ell\lambda}
, one can expand it in terms of amplitudes of definite total angular momentum
J
of the three particle system:

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{3;\ell^{\prime}\lambda^{\prime},\ell\lambda}(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{J=J_{\min}}^{\infty}(2J+1)\mathcal{M}_{3;\ell^{\prime}\lambda^{\prime},\ell\lambda}^{J}(p,k)\,d_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}^{(J)}(\theta_{pk})\,,

(95)

where
J_{\min}=\max(\lvert\lambda\rvert,\lvert\lambda^{\prime}\rvert)
,
d
is the Wigner matrix elements,
12
12

12

Note this
d
is not to be confused with the amputated ladder amplitude, Eq. (
10
), that has been defined in Sec.
II.1
.
and
\theta_{pk}
is the CM frame scattering angle
\theta_{pk}
, defined through

\cos\theta_{pk}=\mathbf{\hat{p}}\cdot\mathbf{\hat{k}}
. Using the orthogonality relation for Wigner
d
matrices, we use Eq. (
95
) to project the helicity amplitudes to definite angular momentum
J

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{3;\ell^{\prime}\lambda^{\prime},\ell\lambda}^{J}(p,k)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\cos\theta_{pk}\,d^{J}_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}(\theta_{pk})\mathcal{M}_{3;\ell^{\prime}\lambda^{\prime},\ell\lambda}(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{k}).

(96)

As discussed in detail in Ref.
[
19
]
, one can transform from the helicity-state basis, whose corresponding partial waves do not have definite parity, to the spin-orbit state basis using the spin-orbit coupling coefficients
\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}^{(\ell)}
to be defined below. This allows one to obtain amplitudes that have both definite angular momentum and parity, which can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{3;L^{\prime}S^{\prime},LS}^{(\ell^{\prime}\ell),J^{P}}(p,k)=\sum_{\lambda^{\prime},\lambda}\mathcal{P}_{\lambda^{\prime}}^{(\ell^{\prime})}(^{2S^{\prime}+1}L^{\prime}_{J})\,\mathcal{M}_{3;\ell^{\prime}\lambda^{\prime},\ell\lambda}^{J}(p,k)\,\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}^{(\ell)}(^{2S+1}L_{J})\,.

(98)

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell,\lambda}\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}^{(\ell)}(^{2S^{\prime}+1}L^{\prime}_{J})\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}^{(\ell)}(^{2S+1}L_{J})=\delta_{L^{\prime}L}\delta_{S^{\prime}S}\,.

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\ell^{\prime}\lambda^{\prime},\ell\lambda}(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{k})=(2\pi)^{3}\,2\omega_{k}\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{k})\,\delta_{\ell^{\prime}\ell}\delta_{\lambda^{\prime}\lambda}\,\widetilde{\rho}(\sigma_{k})+\mathcal{G}_{\ell^{\prime}\lambda^{\prime},\ell\lambda}(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{k}).

(109)

Finally, the remaining object appearing in Eq. (
105
) needing to be defined is
\widehat{\mathcal{T}}
,

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{T}}_{\ell^{\prime}\lambda^{\prime},\ell\lambda}(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=\widehat{\mathcal{K}}_{3;\ell^{\prime}\lambda^{\prime},\ell\lambda}(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle\hskip-25.6073pt-\sum_{\ell_{1},\lambda_{1}}\sum_{\ell^{\prime}_{1},\lambda^{\prime}_{1}}\sum_{\ell_{2},\lambda_{2}}\int_{\mathbf{p}^{\prime}}\int_{\mathbf{q}^{\prime}}\int_{\mathbf{k}^{\prime}}\widehat{\mathcal{K}}_{3;\ell^{\prime}\lambda^{\prime},\ell_{1}\lambda_{1}}(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{p}^{\prime})\Gamma_{\ell_{1}\lambda_{1},\ell^{\prime}_{1}\lambda^{\prime}_{1}}(\mathbf{p}^{\prime},\mathbf{q}^{\prime})\widehat{\mathcal{L}}_{\ell^{\prime}_{1}\lambda^{\prime}_{1},\ell_{2}\lambda_{2}}(\mathbf{q}^{\prime},\mathbf{k}^{\prime})\,\widehat{\mathcal{T}}_{\ell_{2}\lambda_{2},\ell\lambda}(\mathbf{k}^{\prime},\mathbf{k}).

(110)

Here we note that there is a freedom in the definition of
\widetilde{\rho}
. Throughout the main body of this work, we use the minimal definition, Eq. (
14
). In Appendix
B
, we discuss the possible shifts in the definition
\widetilde{\rho}
, as well as to why this might be necessary.

We can apply the above mentioned procedure to obtain the partial wave projection of the different building blocks. For a given functional form of
\widehat{\mathcal{K}}_{3}
, one can use the analogues of Eqs. (
96
), (
100
), to project this to definite
J
and subsequently to the
JLS
basis

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{K}}_{3;\ell^{\prime}\lambda^{\prime},\ell\lambda}^{J}(p,k)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\mathrm{d}\cos\theta_{pk}\,d^{J}_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}(\theta_{pk})\,\widehat{\mathcal{K}}_{3;\ell^{\prime}\lambda^{\prime},\ell\lambda}(\mathbf{p},\mathbf{k})\,,

(111)

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{K}}_{3;L^{\prime}S^{\prime},LS}^{J^{P}}(p,k)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda^{\prime},\lambda}\mathcal{P}_{\lambda^{\prime}}^{(\ell^{\prime})}(^{2S^{\prime}+1}L^{\prime}_{J})\,\widehat{\mathcal{K}}_{3;\ell^{\prime}\lambda^{\prime},\ell\lambda}^{J}(p,k)\,\mathcal{P}_{\lambda}^{(\ell)}(^{2S+1}L_{J})\,.

(112)

The only element that remains to be partial-wave projected is the term proportional to the
\delta
function appearing in the two rescattering functions defined in Eqs. (
107
) and (
108
). To do this, we write the
\delta
function in the spherical basis,

\displaystyle\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{k})=\frac{\delta(p-k)}{k^{2}}\,\delta^{(2)}(\Omega_{p}-\Omega_{k}),

(113)

where as defined in Ref.
[
19
]
,
\Omega_{k}
is the solid angle of the pair in the CM frame, i.e. the vector
-\mathbf{\hat{\mathbf{k}}}

\delta

\displaystyle\delta^{(2)}(\Omega_{p}-\Omega_{k})

\displaystyle=\sum_{J,m_{J},\lambda}\frac{2J+1}{4\pi}\,D_{m_{J}\lambda}^{(J)}(\Omega_{p})D_{m_{J}\lambda}^{(J)\,*}(\Omega_{k})\,,

\displaystyle=\sum_{J,\lambda}\frac{2J+1}{4\pi}\,d_{\lambda\lambda}^{J}(\theta_{pk})\,,

(114)

where we have used the fact that
\sum_{m_{J}=-J}^{J}D_{m_{J}\lambda^{\prime}}^{(J)\,*}(-\mathbf{\hat{p}})\,D_{m_{J}\lambda}^{(J)}(-\mathbf{\hat{k}})=d_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}^{(J)}(\theta_{pk})\,
with
\cos\theta_{pk}=(-\mathbf{\hat{k}})\cdot(-\mathbf{\hat{p}})
.
With this, the
\delta
function term appearing in Eqs.
107
and
108
becomes

\displaystyle(2\pi)^{3}2\omega_{k}\delta^{(3)}(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=(2\pi)^{3}2\omega_{k}\frac{\delta(p-k)}{k^{2}}\,\delta^{(2)}(\Omega_{p}-\Omega_{k})\,,

\displaystyle=\frac{(2\pi)^{2}\omega_{k}}{k^{2}}\delta(p-k)\sum_{J,\lambda}(2J+1)\,d_{\lambda\lambda}^{J}(\theta_{pk})

(115)

The first equality of Eq. (
114

\displaystyle D_{m_{J}\lambda}^{(J)}(\Omega_{p})

\displaystyle=\int\!\textrm{d}\Omega_{k}\,\delta(\Omega_{p}-\Omega_{k})\,D_{m_{J}\lambda}^{(J)}(\Omega_{k})\,,

(116)

\displaystyle=\int\!\textrm{d}\Omega_{k}\,\sum_{J^{\prime},m_{J}^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime}}\frac{2J+1}{4\pi}\,D_{m_{J}^{\prime}\lambda^{\prime}}^{(J^{\prime})}(\Omega_{p})D_{m_{J}^{\prime}\lambda^{\prime}}^{(J^{\prime})\,*}(\Omega_{k})\,D_{m_{J}\lambda}^{(J)}(\Omega_{k})\,,

(117)

\displaystyle=\sum_{J^{\prime},m_{J}^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime}}\frac{2J+1}{4\pi}\,D_{m_{J}^{\prime}\lambda^{\prime}}^{(J^{\prime})}(\Omega_{p})\int\!\textrm{d}\Omega_{k}\,D_{m_{J}^{\prime}\lambda^{\prime}}^{(J^{\prime})\,*}(\Omega_{k})\,D_{m_{J}\lambda}^{(J)}(\Omega_{k})\,,

(118)

\displaystyle=\sum_{J^{\prime},m_{J}^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime}}\frac{2J+1}{4\pi}\,D_{m_{J}^{\prime}\lambda^{\prime}}^{(J^{\prime})}(\Omega_{p})\,\left(\frac{4\pi}{2J+1}\delta_{JJ^{\prime}}\delta_{m_{J}m_{J}^{\prime}}\delta_{\lambda\lambda^{\prime}}\right)\,,

(119)

\displaystyle=D_{m_{J}\lambda}^{(J)}(\Omega_{p})\,,

(120)

where we emphasize the the normalization of the Wigner D matrices is due to the fact that we have integer spin systems and describe the particle orientation by only a polar and azimuthal angle. Using Eq. (
115
), and the analogues of Eqs. (

), (

), we can project the
\delta

JLS

\Gamma

) is projected to these two basis as

\displaystyle\Gamma^{J}_{\ell^{\prime}\lambda^{\prime},\ell\lambda}(p,k)

\displaystyle=\frac{(2\pi)^{2}\omega_{k}}{k^{2}}\delta(p-k)\,\delta_{\ell^{\prime}\ell}\delta_{\lambda^{\prime}\lambda}\,\widetilde{\rho}(\sigma_{k})+\mathcal{G}^{J}_{\ell^{\prime}\lambda^{\prime},\ell\lambda}(p,k)\,,

(121)

\displaystyle\Gamma^{J}_{L^{\prime}S^{\prime},LS}(p,k)

\displaystyle=\frac{(2\pi)^{2}\omega_{k}}{k^{2}}\delta(p-k)\,\delta_{L^{\prime}L}\delta_{S^{\prime}S}\,\widetilde{\rho}(\sigma_{k})+\mathcal{G}^{J}_{L^{\prime}S^{\prime},LS}(p,k)\,.

\mathcal{T}

Appendix B
Shifting the phase space,
\widetilde{\rho}

There is freedom in defining the phase space,
\widetilde{\rho}
, appearing in the definition of both
\widehat{\mathcal{M}}_{3,\textrm{df}}
and
\mathcal{M}_{3,\textrm{df}}
, in Eqs. (
105
) and (
123
), respectively. Throughout this work, we have assumed the simplest definition for
\widetilde{\rho}
, given by Eq. (
14
). This freedom stems from the fact that the two-body scattering amplitude,
\mathcal{M}_{2,\ell}
, must be scheme-independent, but one can modify the phase space and the two-body K matrix
\mathcal{K}_{2,\ell}
simultaneously, in such a way that
\mathcal{M}_{2,\ell}
is invariant
[
20
]
.

To make this more explicit, let us write
\mathcal{M}_{2,\ell}
in terms of two-body K matrix,
\mathcal{K}_{2,\ell}
, and the standard two-body phase space,
\rho
, defined in Eq. (
15
),

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{2,\ell}^{\,-1}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{K}_{2,\ell}^{\,-1}-i\rho\,,

(127)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{2,\ell}^{\,-1}+\widetilde{\rho}\,,

(128)

where we in the second equality we introduced

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{2,\ell}^{\,-1}\equiv\mathcal{K}_{2,\ell}^{\,-1}-i(1-H)\,\rho\,.

(129)

This makes it evident that
\mathcal{M}_{2,\ell}
is invariant under a simultaneously shift of
\mathcal{K}_{2,\ell}
and
\widetilde{\rho}
of the form,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{2,\ell}^{\,-1}

\displaystyle\to\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{2,\ell}^{\,-1}-\frac{\widetilde{I}_{\rm PV}^{(\ell)}}{q_{k}^{\star 2\ell}}\,,

(130)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\rho}

\displaystyle\to\widetilde{\rho}+\frac{\widetilde{I}_{\rm PV}^{(\ell)}}{q_{k}^{\star 2\ell}},

(131)

where
\widetilde{I}_{\rm PV}^{(\ell)}
has to be a real and non-singular function of
\sigma_{k}
to ensure unitarity, and the barrier factors have been introduced to guarantee the correct threshold behavior of
\mathcal{M}_{2,\ell}
.

Reference
[
88
]
showed that one can use this freedom to generalize previous finite-volume three-body formalism
[
32
]
to describe systems where the
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{2,\ell}
can have poles. Because
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{2,\ell}
depends on the cut off, these poles are unphysical, but they can appear for systems where there are two-body bound states and/or resonances. A minimal choice introduced in there is to use
\widetilde{I}_{\rm PV}^{(\ell)}
to move these poles away from the kinematic region considered.

Because
\mathcal{D}
only depends on
\mathcal{M}_{2,\ell}
, as opposed to
\widetilde{\mathcal{K}}_{2,\ell}
or
\widetilde{\rho}
, it is unaffected by this shift. Meanwhile,
\mathcal{M}_{3,\rm df}
does depend on
\widetilde{\rho}
, implying that the functional form does change. For example, the
\mathcal{L}
function within the symmetric formalism, Eq. (
124
), will be shifted to

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\ell^{\prime}\lambda^{\prime},\ell\lambda}({\mathbf{p}},{\mathbf{k}})

\displaystyle\to\mathcal{L}_{\ell^{\prime}\lambda^{\prime},\ell\lambda}({\mathbf{p}},{\mathbf{k}})-\frac{\widetilde{I}_{\rm PV}^{(\ell)}}{b_{p}^{\star 2\ell}}\mathcal{M}_{2,\ell}(\sigma_{p})\,2\omega_{p}\,(2\pi)^{3}\delta^{3}(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{k})-\mathcal{D}^{(u,u)}_{\ell^{\prime}\lambda^{\prime},\ell\lambda}({\mathbf{p}},{\mathbf{k}})\frac{\widetilde{I}_{\rm PV}^{(\ell)}}{q_{k}^{\star 2\ell}}.

(132)

Note, this shift leads to an implicit redefinition of
\mathcal{K}_{3}
to absorb this modification to
\mathcal{M}_{3}
.

Because this shift is done for partial-wave projected two-body amplitudes, it does not introduce any further subtleties in the partial-wave projecting procedure outlined in Appendix
A
. In other words, one can use the formalism presented in the main body after making the global replacement

\displaystyle\widetilde{\rho}\,\delta_{S^{\prime}S}

\displaystyle\to\widetilde{\rho}\,\delta_{S^{\prime}S}+\frac{\widetilde{I}_{\rm PV}^{(S)}}{q_{k}^{\star 2S}}.

(133)

These artifacts of the finite-volume formalism are not of immediate relevance for the infinite-volume formalism, which is the concern of this work. But, it is important to keep these details in mind, if one is interest in using lattice QCD results for
\mathcal{K}_{3}
, which may require performing these shifts in
\widetilde{\rho}
to then determine physical partial wave projected amplitudes.
