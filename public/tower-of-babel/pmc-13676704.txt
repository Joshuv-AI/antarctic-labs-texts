Protocol for high-throughput identification of genes involved in pyroptotic cell death by live microscopy
Authors: Aleksandra Bajger, Lukasz S. Borowski, Roman J. Szczesny, Michal P. Wandel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryIntracellular lipopolysaccharide activates caspase-4/5 triggering pyroptotic cell death. We present a protocol for a high-throughput loss-of-function screening approach to identify interferon-gamma-stimulated genes regulating this pathway. We detail procedures for cell reverse-transfection with a custom-made small interfering RNA (siRNA) library in 384-well plates and priming with interferon-gamma. We then describe steps for transfection with lipopolysaccharide to induce pyroptosis and live-cell fluorescence microscopy using the IncuCyte S3 system to quantify cell death in real time without fixation. This approach enables reliable, high-throughput screening of cell death.

Before you begin

Ability of individual cells to recognise and respond to threats depend on presence of membrane-bound or cytosolic pattern recognition receptors (PRR). PRRs are responsible for detection of unique microbial molecular structures, known as pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), which are evolutionary conserved and crucial for the pathogen survival.1,2 Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) is a major component of the outer membrane of Gram-negative bacteria, and a strong immunomodulator. In mammals, LPS recognition depends on two separate pathways. Toll-like 4 receptor in complex with MD2 senses extracellular LPS,3,4 while intracellular LPS is detected by a noncanonical inflammasome.5 Caspase-4/-5 (CASP4/5) in humans and caspase-11 in mice are major components of the noncanonical inflammasome.6,7,8 These caspases can be activated directly via binding hydrophobic lipid A of free LPS to their N-terminal CARD domain,9 or recognize LPS within membranes with positive curvature.10 Activated CASP4/11 processes gasdermin-D (GSDMD) to cause membrane rupture and execute pro-inflammatory cell death – pyroptosis, which is critical for endotoxic shock in mice, and to protect against cytosol invading bacteria.11,12,13 To promote the noncanonical inflammasome activity in non-immune human cells, the presence of interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) is required,14,15 however, the exact role of IFN-γ stimulated genes (ISGs) in the formation and activation of noncanonical inflammasome is not fully understood.

This protocol describes a robust, high-throughput method for systematic identification of ISGs involved in noncanonical inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis. Our approach combines siRNA loss-of-function screening with real-time live fluorescence microscopy using the IncuCyte S3 system to monitor pyroptotic cell death triggered by intracellular LPS. First, epithelial HeLa cells are transfected with siRNA library prepared in 384-well format and designed against ISGs selected by evaluating the transcriptional response of cells and tissues stimulated with IFN-γ,16,17 further referred as the siRNA library (Data S1). Following 36 h of knockdown, cells are primed with IFN-γ to stimulate expression of ISGs. Next, after 12 h of priming cells are transfected with LPS from Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium to induce cell death. Cells are visualized with fluorescence microscopy using the IncuCyte S3 system for direct real-time imaging of live, unfixed cells. Later, results are calculated as a ratio of dead cells to all cells in the population. If particular gene is involved in the pyroptosis, its silencing causes decreased levels of cell death compared to negative controls.

The protocol below describes the specific steps for using HeLa cells. However, we have also applied this protocol for high-throughput siRNA transfection in human melanoma MJS (also known as MelJuSo) and 293 cells.18,19

Hence, cell lines different from HeLa, or primary cells, provided they can be transfected efficiently with siRNA and respond to IFN-γ, as well as various siRNA libraries can be used with this protocol. With small modifications, this protocol can be used in screening where cells are challenged with different inhibitors, inflammatory agonists and/or infected with intracellular pathogens.

Innovation

Pyroptosis is a critical inflammatory cell death pathway with important roles in host defense, autoinflammatory diseases, and cancer.5,20 Understanding the molecular mechanisms regulating pyroptosis has significant implications for developing therapeutic interventions. Our protocol provides researchers with a practical, reproducible method for systematic identification of genes controlling this important cellular process.

High-throughput assays carried out in a 384-well plate format enable to collect large amount of data in a single experiment while maintaining statistical power and experimental efficiency. However, if the performed assay involves samples visualization with fluorescence microscopy, this can take significant amount of time, and thus requires samples fixation.

In many immune processes threat detection leads to the death of a challenged cell, and fixation of such sample can lead to artifacts and false-positive results. In this protocol, IncuCyte S3 system is used for fluorescence microscopy and direct cell death detection without cell fixation. This machine allows interference-free continuous monitoring of cells directly in the incubator environment with integrated image analysis enabling more accurate and physiologically relevant data collection.

In addition, our protocol has been optimized to avoid media replacement steps; not only to simplify the screening procedure but also to improve data quality in high-throughput formats by limiting unnecessary disturbance, stress or cell loss.

Advancements that we implemented in our high-throughput analysis open a new opportunity for studies of immune pathways, as well as all processes where sample fixation in experimental design may disturb obtained results.

Expected outcomes

The protocol enables systematic identification of genes regulating pyroptosis through high-throughput siRNA screening coupled with live-cell imaging, avoiding fixation-related artifacts inherent to endpoint assays. SiCTRL and siCASP4 were used in this screening as negative and positive controls, respectively. Challenge of IFN-γ primed HeLa cells with transfected bacterial LPS triggers formation of noncanonical inflammasome, the activation of which leads to cell death. However, when genes involved in the pathway are silenced (e.g., CASP4) the cell death is blocked. Application of fluorescent dyes makes it possible to distinguish dead cells (PI-positive) from the entire population (SYTO 9 Green positive) and their quantification (Figures 5A and 5B). To analyze screening data, calculate and log-transform mean values of cell death from three replicates. Then normalize these values to the control (siCTRL + IFN-γ) using the median. Next calculate robust z-scores based on the median, and present the data on a logarithmic scale. Genes whose silencing reduces the PI-to-SYTO 9 ratio compared to negative controls shows decreased values of robust z-scores and are identified as candidate pyroptosis regulators. To further confirm the accuracy of the protocol siRNA library also targeted genes, whose role in the pyroptosis was already established, such as CASP4 or GSDMD. Limitations

In this protocol Thermo Fisher Silencer Select siRNA custom-made library was used. According to producer information, knock-down on mRNA level is achieved 24–48 h post transfection. However, this may not affect the corresponding protein level. Likely, reduction of the levels of some proteins may require longer culturing time, while this protocol shortens the entire experiment to 48 h. It was experimentally established that in the case of CASP4 it is an adequate time for the protein to be efficiently downregulated (Figure 6). For each screening experiment, one needs to determine the time sufficient to observe expected phenotype or its reversion with a positive/negative control for the assay. It is possible that very stable proteins may still be present in the cell proteome upon applied culturing time and hence silencing of this particular target may not be efficient enough to result in the expected phenotype, leading to false negative results in the screen.Figure 6Time course of caspase-4 silencingCells were treated with siRNA as indicated and after different time periods cells were primed with IFN-γ. Also, it is advised to evaluate the assay used for hit identification to assess its robustness. One method for this evaluation is to calculate the Z′ factor, which integrates the dynamic range of the measured signal between positive and negative controls and data variability into a single value.21,22 Assessing the Z′ factor is beneficial not only during the development phase of the assay but also while conducting the screening process, as it helps monitor data quality across different plates. A suboptimal Z′ factor, particularly in complex cell-based assays, does not necessarily invalidate the screening process23,24 but highlights the potential for misidentifying hits. This consideration should be factored in when planning the validation of obtained results. A less robust assay increases the likelihood of misidentifying candidate hits, and may require validating a larger number of candidates.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

Inefficient downregulation of studied proteins (related to major step high-throughput reverse siRNA transfection of HeLa cells).

Potential solution

Try to increase the experiment duration but be aware that it should last less than 60 h overall due to cell death occurring at later time points (applies to step 23).

Problem 2

Observed knock-down is not complete (related to major step high-throughput reverse siRNA transfection of HeLa cells).

Potential solution

Increase the concentration of siRNAs or design new siRNA (applies to point 5–7 of before you begin section and/or step 2 and 4). Do not exceed the final concentration of 20 nM (in case of Silencer Select siRNA).

Problem 3

Cells do not reach the confluency of 90–95% whilst they are transfected with LPS (related to major step: LPS transfection, fluorescent staining, and data visualization).

Potential solutions

Increase the concentration of cells in siRNA-transfected wells (applies to step 19 and 20). Depending on the cell line, cells concentration in the screening needs to be adjusted prior to final experiment.

Problem 4

LPS transfection is not efficient (related to major step LPS transfection, fluorescent staining, and data visualization).

Potential solution

Increase the LPS concentration in the screening or try to change transfection reagent (e.g., to RNAiMAX) (applies to step 32).

Problem 5

Available live-imaging machine has only one fluorescent channel (related to major step LPS transfection, fluorescent staining, and data visualization).

Potential solution

If the machine used for imaging allows for Differential Interference Contrast (DIC) microscopy, only dye for visualization of dead cells can be used. In such cases, results would be obtained by calculating a ratio of dead cells to confluency (applies to step 38 and 39).

Problem 6

Multidrop is not available (related to step 10, 12, 19, 20, 28, 36 and 37).

Potential solution

Multi-channel pipette can be used alternatively.
