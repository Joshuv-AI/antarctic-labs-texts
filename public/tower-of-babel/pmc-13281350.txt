Investigation of optimal range of gestational weight gain for twin pregnancies according to Chinese specific body mass index categories
Authors: Yue Xiao, Yao Cheng, Xiaoxuan Zou, Yuzhi Deng, Fei Liang, Qiuping Ma, Guoqiang Sun, Yaling Ding, Haiyan Liu, Yingying Cao, Haishan Yang, Yi Zhong, Meina Hu, Jieyun Yin, Ying Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere is a lack of data on the optimal gestational weight gain (GWG) in twin pregnancies in China.MethodsA multicenter retrospective study was conducted, containing 1247 twin pregnancies in both North and South China. Optimal GWG was defined as the interquartile range of GWG across pre-pregnancy body mass index stratum among low-risk women. The composite outcome was defined as any occurrence of preterm delivery, small for gestational age, large for gestational age, and hypertensive disorders during pregnancy.ResultsThe study found that the optimal total gestational weight gain was 16–21.80 kg for underweight, 15.35–21.50 kg for normal weight, 12.10–20.25 kg for overweight, and 5.50–18.50 kg for obese subgroups. Corresponding gestational weight gain rates were 0.43–0.61 kg/week, 0.42–0.59 kg/week, 0.34–0.55 kg/week, and 0.15–0.51 kg/week. The proposed gestational weight gain ranges by our study were lower than the provisional Institute of Medicine (IOM) twin recommendation but higher than the Chinese Nutrition Society’s singleton recommendation. Additionally, 46.11% of participants gained appropriate weight according to our suggestion, compared to 43.48% and 23.82% for the Institute of Medicine of twin guideline and the Chinese Nutrition Society’s singleton recommendation, respectively. Furthermore, the association between the composite outcome with insufficient GWG defined by our suggestion was the strongest [adjusted odds ratio (95% confidence interval) = 1.74 (1.21–2.49)] among recommendations. Noteworthy, regional differences were observed. Southern Chinese women had a lower prevalence of adverse outcomes with proposed appropriate GWG ranges (80.78%) compared to the IOM twin guideline (82.04%). while northern women showed a slightly higher incidence of adverse outcomes under the proposed guidelines (65.67%) versus the IOM guideline (64.46%). Meanwhile, insufficient GWG was also more strongly linked to adverse outcomes in northern women (adjusted OR = 2.35) compared to southern women (adjusted OR = 1.15). These results indicating that northern women may need a higher optimal GWG threshold.ConclusionOur study emphasizes the necessity of establishing official gestational weight gain guideline for Chinese twin pregnancies, and regional-specific guidelines may need to be considered.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-026-09148-4.Keywords: Gestational weight gain, Twin pregnancies, Pregnancy outcomes

Background

The rapid economic development and the resulting social pressures, along with the implementation of China’s new birth policy, have contributed to higher maternal age at pregnancy, especially for first-time mothers [1]. This, in turn, has resulted in decreased fertility in older adult women and an increased reliance for assisted reproductive technology (ART), jointly leading to an increasing trend of twinning rates in China from 2012 onwards [2]. It is well known that twin pregnancies have greater nutritional requirements and higher baseline risk compared to singleton gestations, making them more susceptible to perinatal morbidity and mortality [3, 4]. For example, twins account for a quarter of low-birth-weight babies, approximately half of all twins are born prematurely, and one-sixth of all neonatal deaths are twins [5–7]. Therefore, more attention should be given to twin pregnancy.

Appropriate gestational weight gain (GWG) is essential for maternal health, fetal development, and lifelong health [8, 9]. There is ample evidence suggesting that insufficient GWG is closely associated with preterm delivery (PTD), fetal growth restriction and even stillbirth, in both singleton and twin pregnancies [10, 11]. On the other hand, excessive GWG is more likely to be linked with gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), hypertensive disorders during pregnancy (HDP), large for gestational age (LGA) infants, cesarean delivery, and postpartum weight retention [12, 13]. As a result, GWG was widely recognized as a modifiable risk factor for adverse pregnancy outcomes [14].

Based on rigorous analysis, the Institute of Medicine (IOM) [15] and the Chinese Nutrition Society (CNS) [16] have released official recommendations for singleton pregnancies. In 2009, the IOM provided only provisional recommendations based on data from a single historic study, which reflected the interquartile range (IQR) of weight gain among Caucasian pregnancies with twins weighting over 2500 g at term [15]. These recommendations were specific to different body mass index (BMI) categories: 16.8–24.5 kg for normal weight women (BMI = 18.5–24.9 kg/m2), 14.1–22.7 kg for overweight women (BMI = 25.0–29.9 kg/m2), and 11.3–19.1 kg for obese women (BMI ≥ 30.0 kg/m2). However, the IOM guidelines did not have enough data to provide optimal weight gain recommendations for underweight women, and it may not be suitable for Chinese twin pregnancies due to inherent differences in BMI classification compared to Western populations, as well as variations in body composition, dietary habits, and cultural perceptions of pregnancy and delivery [17–20]. In 2018, Chen and coworkers recruited 6925 women delivering live and non-malformed twins in Wuhan, which was the largest sample size to explore the optimal GWG among Chinese twin pregnancies to date [17]. However, this single-center study did not exclude women with adverse pregnancy outcomes when calculating the optimal GWG ranges [17]. Other studies also attempted to investigate the optimal GWG among Chinese twins, but inconsistent results were obtained due to small sample sizes, varied statistical methods, and different exclusion criteria [18, 21–23]. Therefore, no consensus has been reached regarding Chinese twin pregnancies. As a result, the optimal GWG for Chinese twin pregnancies is yet to be determined.

The Qinling-Huaihe Line divides China into North and South regions, which have distinct differences in climate, food flavors [24, 25], and air pollution levels [26]. Recent studies have shown that dietary patterns during pregnancy varies between these two regions[27, 28]. It has also been recognized that a mother’s diet before and during pregnancy [29], as well as exposure to ambient temperature [30] and air pollution [31], can impact fetal growth and pregnancy outcomes. Hence, it is important to consider region differences when establishing GWG recommendations for Chinese twin gestations.

Therefore, based on a multicenter retrospective study, our objective was to (1) explore the optimal GWG ranges of Chinese twin pregnancies, (2) examine whether the proposed GWG ranges can improve pregnancy outcomes in comparison to the existing recommendations (i.e., the IOM twin guideline [15], the CNS singleton guideline [16], and Chen’s recommendation for twin gestations [17]), (3) evaluate whether these GWG ranges have similar applicability in both northern and southern Chinese.

Methods

Study participants

This retrospective study was conducted in three cities in mainland China, including Taicang (31.19°N) and Wuhan (30.59°N) that are located in Southern China, and Beijing (39.90°N) located in Northern China. Enrollment was made for pregnant women who delivered twins: (1) between January 2015 and December 2020 in the affiliated Taicang People’s Hospital of Soochow University; (2) between January 2021 to November 2021 in the Maternal and Child Health Hospital of Hubei province; (3) between January 2014 to December 2021 in any hospital or community healthcare center in Haidian District of Beijing. Pregnant women were considered eligible if they had regular prenatal care till delivery and maintained complete data during the study period. The exclusion criteria included: (1) abortions or stillbirths; (2) lack of data on height, pre-pregnancy weight or maternal weight at delivery; (3) genetic or major structural fetal malformations of any one of the twins. Ultimately, a total of 1247 women were included in the final analysis.

The flow chart of the exclusion and inclusion process of our study population is presented in Fig. 1. Data were extracted and aggregated in an anonymous way. The study was approved by the ethics committee of the above mentioned centers.

Maternal and infantile characteristics

At the first prenatal visit, which typically takes place between 8 and 16 gestational weeks, we collected maternal information including age, height, pre-pregnancy weight, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), gestational age, obstetric history, previous medical history, and mode of conception. Gestational age was confirmed by measuring the crown-rump length in the first trimester. For pregnancies resulting from in vitro fertilization, gestational age was determined from based on the date of embryo transfer. Gestational complications were obtained from medical records after delivery. Furthermore, we retrieved infant birth weight, sex, gestational age at delivery, delivery mode, and Apgar scores from medical records or birth certificates.

Anthropometric measurement

Maternal weight was measured at their prenatal visit by skilled staff to the nearest 0.1 kg in light clothing using a calibrated beam scale, and maternal height was measured to the nearest 0.1 cm without shoes using a portable stadiometer. Pre-pregnancy BMI (kg/m2), calculated by dividing self-reported pre-conceptional weight by the square of the height, was divided into four categories according to the standard set by the Working Group on Obesity in China [32]: underweight (< 18.5 kg/m2), normal (18.5–23.9 kg/m2), overweight (24–27.9 kg/m2), and obese (≥ 28 kg/m2). Total GWG (kg) was calculated by subtracting the pre-pregnancy weight from the weight at delivery, and GWG rate (kg/week) was then determined by dividing the total GWG by the number of gestational weeks until delivery.

Outcomes of interest

Our primary outcome was a composite outcome, which was defined as any occurrence of PTD, SGA, LGA, and HDP. PTD was defined as delivery before 37 weeks of gestation or 34 weeks of gestation [33]. SGA and LGA were defined as one of the twins with a gestational-age-adjusted birth weight below the 10th percentile and above the 90th percentile, respectively [34]. HDP encompassed gestational hypertension (GH) and preeclampsia (PE). GH was characterized by new-onset hypertension (SBP ≥ 140 mmHg or DBP ≥ 90 mmHg) after 20 weeks of gestation in a pre-conceptional normotensive woman, while PE was defined as new-onset GH with either proteinuria (≥ 300 mg/24 hours or a protein/creatinine ≥ 0.3 or > 2 + by dipstick on 2 or more occasions) or features of end-organ dysfunction after 20 weeks of gestation [35]. GDM was diagnosed between 24 and 28 weeks of gestation when any of the following criteria were met on the 75 g oral glucose tolerance test: fasting plasma glucose level at least 5.1 mmol/L, at least 10 mmol/L at 1 h, and at least 8.5 mmol/L at 2h [36]. As interventions after a GDM diagnosis may affect GWG and thus lead to reverse causality, GDM was not included in the composite outcome.

Statistical analysis

Basic analysis

Data were expressed as mean (SD) for continuous variables and number (%) for categorical variables. The distributions of major characteristics of women with different pre-pregnancy BMI categories were compared using one-way ANOVA, χ2 test, or Fisher exact tests, as appropriate.

Optimal GWG ranges exploration

As recommended by a previous study [37], the exploration of ideal GWG was conducted within a low-risk subgroup that met the following criteria: (1) gave birth between 37 and 42 weeks; (2) did not experience any unfavorable gestational complications, such as HDP, LGA, SGA, and GDM; (3) did not have chronic hypertension or pre-pregnancy diabetes (collectively referred to as previous medical history). The optimal GWG ranges for twin pregnancies by BMI category were calculated as the IQRs of total GWG or GWG rates in the low-risk subset.

Comparisons between recommendations

Total GWG or GWG rate was expressed categorically as insufficient, within, or excessive relative to the recommendations according to different criteria (Table 1). Incidences of pregnancy outcomes among those with sufficient GWG defined by different criteria were compared. In addition, logistic regression models were used to estimate the associations between GWG categories and pregnancy outcomes. Adjusted odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) were calculated after controlling for region, maternal age, pre-pregnancy BMI, parity, previous medical history, mode of conception and sex combination. Subgroup analyses were performed according to pre-pregnancy BMI groups and regions. Due to the small sample size in the obese and overweight groups, we performed both separate analyses for overweight and obese women and a combined analysis for comparison.

Results

Basic Characteristics

A total of 1247 participants with twin pregnancies were included in this multicenter retrospective study. As shown in Tables 2, 127 (10.18%), 805 (64.55%), 258 (20.69%), and 57 (4.57%) participants were classified as underweight, normal weight, overweight and obese, respectively. A more detailed presentation of basic characteristics across study centers was shown in Supplementary Table 1. Overall, the underweight group had the youngest age but the highest rate of chronic hypertension. As BMI category increased, there was a gradual increase in rates of GDM, HDP, and LGA, but a steady decrease in the rate of SGA. Pre-conceptional BMI between the southern [22.20 (3.33) kg] and northern population [22.03 (3.01) kg] was not statistically different, while the latter group gained significantly more weight during pregnancy than the former group [averaged GWG of 17.67 kg versus 16.16 kg, P for t−test<0.001].

After dividing the sample of 203 individuals into low-risk group, it was found that there were 17 underweight, 137 normal weight, 39 overweight, and 10 obese individuals. The optimal GWG ranges obtained by calculating the IQR for each pre-pregnancy BMI category were 16–21.80 kg for underweight, 15.35–21.50 kg for normal weight, 12.10–20.25 kg for overweight, and 5.50–18.50 for obese. The corresponding GWG rates were 0.43–0.61 kg/week, 0.42–0.59 kg/week, 0.34–0.55 kg/week, and 0.15–0.51 kg/week (Table 1).

In the entire population, the proportion of women who had appropriate GWG according to our study (46.11%) or Chen’s recommendations (58.62%) was higher than the IOM twin guideline (43.68%) and the CNS singleton guideline (23.82%). This trend was consistent in both the northern and southern stratum (Fig. 2). Noteworthy, the southern subgroup had a slightly lower percentage of adequate GWG but a higher percentage of insufficient GWG compared to its counterpart, regardless of which criterion was performed.

Subgroup analysis

In the underweight group, insufficient GWG, according to our recommendation, was associated with an increased risk of the composite outcome (adjusted OR = 4.56, 95%CI = 1.19–17.44). In the normal weight group, the result consistently showed that insufficient GWG defined by our suggestion was related to the highest risk of the composite outcome. In the overweight group, neither insufficient nor excessive GWG was associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes. A similar lack of association was observed in the obese group, likely due to the limited sample size. When overweight and obese women were combined, insufficient GWG did not significantly affect the composite outcome (adjusted OR = 1.03, 95% CI = 0.52–2.01), nor did excessive GWG (adjusted OR = 1.15, 95% CI = 0.57–2.34). These findings are consistent with the results of the separate analyses (Supplementary Table 3). After stratifying by regions, the unfavorable effect of insufficient GWG was more prominent in the northern (adjusted OR = 1.84, 95%CI = 1.21–2.80) Chinese population than its southern (adjusted OR = 0.96, 95%CI = 0.62–1.51) counterparts (Supplementary Table 4).

Discussion

The current study aimed to determine the proper GWG among Chinese twin pregnancies in exploratory analyses. Our findings supported the view that the optimal GWG range for Asian twin pregnancies may be lower than the IOM twin guideline [22, 23, 38, 39].

Our study indicated that both insufficient and excessive GWG were associated with PTD before 37 gestational weeks, which was in line with previous studies on both twin [40] and singleton pregnancies [41]. Low weight gain may indicate poor maternal nutrition, inadequate expansion of plasma volume, and an increased risk of infection and/or inflammation [42, 43]. Conversely, excessive weight gain, often seen in women who were obese before pregnancy, is linked to inflammation and infection [44–46]. It should be noted that inflammation and infection are well-known risk factors for premature rupture of membranes and preterm labor [47]. Our finding that elevated risk of HDP among pregnancies with excessive GWG was in agreement with previous studies of twin pregnancies [19, 20, 48]. Additionally, our study showed that high GWG increased the risk of PE but not GH, which was consistent with a previous study [49]. It may be partly explained by the evidence that edema, a common symptom of PE, is more noticeable than GH [50].

The majority of twin pregnancies exceeded the recommended GWG for singleton guideline by CNS, indicating that this guideline may not be applicable for multiple gestations in China. Our study also found that more than half of the participants gained weight during pregnancy that did not align with the IOM twin recommendations, indicating the need for adjusted guidelines specifically for Chinese women [38, 51]. Similarly, various studies proposed different weight gain ranges for twin pregnancies, which were generally lower than the existing IOM recommendations. For instance, Dai et al. [51] argued that the IOM guidelines recommended significantly higher GWG than is appropriate for twin pregnancies in China. They have proposed adjusting the current IOM recommendations by subtracting 2 kg from both the upper and lower limits of the IOM values, or using the IQR of weight gain for mothers of appropriately grown for gestational age infants as the recommendations. In addition, Wang et al. found that nearly half of Chinese Han women with twin pregnancies had GWG below the IOM guidelines [18], and Lin et al. suggested that the IQR of total GWG in normal weight women with twin pregnancies was lower than those of the IOM guidelines [23]. A study in Japan also suggested that the optimal GWG for Japanese women with twin pregnancies may be lower than the recommended values of the IOM guidelines [39].

In additional research exploring appropriate GWG for twin pregnancies in China, several perspectives align with the findings of this study. A study in southeastern China, after excluding preterm delivered pregnancies or twins with low birth weight, found that the recommended GWG derived from 597 low-risk normal-weight twin pregnancies was between 15.3 and 22.5 kg [23], which was quite similar to the corresponding range revealed by our study. Additionally, Li et al. [22] studied 998 twin pregnancies without complications and with favorable neonatal outcomes and established the optimal GWG, which was 16–21.50 kg for underweight (whereas our suggestion was 16–21.80 kg), 15–21.10 kg for normal weight (whereas our suggestion was 15.35–21.50 kg), and 12–21 kg for the overweight (whereas our suggestion was 12.10–20.25 kg). The optimal GWG recommendations derived by Li were almost identical to those of our study [22].

In our study, the primary focus was the composite outcome rather than individual outcomes. Although insufficient GWG was associated differently with individual outcomes between northern and southern populations, the composite outcome showed a higher OR for the insufficient group in the north. Our proposed GWG ranges were lower than the IOM guidelines. While our proposed ranges were linked to lower composite outcome rates among southern women compared to the IOM guideline, this advantage was not observed in northern women. Taken together, these findings suggest that northern Chinese women with twin pregnancies may need a higher optimal GWG threshold to reduce adverse outcomes. Similarly, a singleton study in Lanzhou (Northern China) [52] reported higher optimal GWG ranges than a relevant study conducted in HongKong (Southern China) [53]. It is important to note that BMI is a simple anthropometric measure, and body composition can provide additional valuable information. It is broadly recognized that maternal lean mass is a stronger predictor of birth weight than total GWG [54, 55]; in contrast, excess maternal adipose mass could plausibly lead to HDP [56]. Several factors support the hypothesis that northern Chinese women may have less lean mass than their southern counterparts at the same BMI. First, the climate in Northern China is colder than Southern China, which leads to a higher body fat percentage in northern Chinese [57]. Second, studies have shown a negative interaction between pre-pregnancy BMI and gestational PM2.5 exposure on birthweight gain [58]. Additionally, longitudinal research found that PM2.5 exposure during mid-to-late pregnancy is linked to increased maternal adiposity in the years following delivery [59]. Collectively, these findings indicate that air pollution may promote maternal fat accumulation rather than lean mass gain, especially among women in Northern China, where air pollution levels are higher. Third, the traditional northern dietary pattern involves a lower consumption of protein products compared to the southern dietary pattern [24, 25]. Consequently, if they were to obtain equal amount of GWG increase, northern pregnancies may gain less lean mass compared to southern pregnancies. Therefore, it may be reasonable to propose region-specific optimal GWG ranges to better support maternal and fetal health.

Strengths and Limitations

The study compared the IOM twin guideline and the CNS singleton guideline to see how they affected pregnancy outcomes in Chinese twin pregnancies. Meanwhile, the current study conducted sophisticated statistical methods to explore optimal GWG ranges. The findings highlighted the need for specific weight gain guidelines for Chinese twin pregnancies and provided theoretical basis for the development of specific GWG guidelines for Chinese twin pregnancies.

Nevertheless, the current study also has some limitations. Firstly, our study was unable to differentiate PTD subtypes, as the effect of GWG on spontaneous and indicated PTD might be different [60]. Secondly, we did not consider other adverse perinatal outcomes that may be relevant to establishing an optimal GWG, such as low Apgar scores and poor fetal growth, due to their low rates in our sample. Thirdly, the GWG ratio was calculated according to an assumed constant weight gain rate throughout pregnancy, but the weight gain of pregnant women showed a curve change throughout the pregnancy. We were unable to calculate the appropriate weight gain ranges for each gestational period due to the lack of continuous monitoring of weight during pregnancy. Fourthly, the lack of information on chorionicity in the Beijing center prevented us from adjusting for this potential confounding factor. Although we used the neonatal sex combination as a proxy, it is still considered suboptimal [61]. Fifthly, the sample size of the current study may not be large enough, especially the number of obesity, this may affect the results of the group. Finally, our study period (2014–2021) partially overlapped with the COVID-19 pandemic. While we did not collect specific data on pandemic-related disruptions, we recognize that changes in healthcare access, lifestyle behaviors, and stress during this time could have affected GWG and pregnancy outcomes. Future research should account for these potential confounding factors.

Conclusion

In summary, the current study emphasizes the importance of establishing official GWG guideline for Chinese twin pregnancies. Further studies are needed to produce reliable GWG guidelines for twin pregnancies in China, and the regional differences may need to be considered.
