Ventricular Arrhythmias
Authors: Marc C. Engels
Published: 2026

Wide complex tachycardia (WCT) poses diagnostic challenges. Making the correct diagnosis on an electrocardiogram (ECG) with WCT is critical, as misdiagnosis can have serious consequences. This can be challenging, as not all WCT is ventricular tachycardia (VT). About 70% to 80% of all ECGs with WCT are in fact VT. The remaining differential diagnosis includes supraventricular tachycardia (SVT) with aberrancy (15%-25%), pre-excited SVT (1%-5%), and ventricular paced rhythm or artifact (1%-5%).1

There are several algorithms to distinguish VT from SVT, and none of them are by any means perfect. In this special issue ECG challenge, several cases illustrate these challenges.

Roig et al2 illustrate a case of WCT that is aberrantly conducted atrial fibrillation with the Ashman phenomenon, in which a short-long RR sequence initiates right bundle branch block aberrancy.

Conversely, López Pena et al3 and Kim et al4 describe cases of narrow complex tachycardia that turns out to be VT that originates close to the conduction system. The ability to penetrate the conduction system early gives rise to a narrow QRS complex, which can easily be confused with SVT.

Weinstein et al5 are on heightened alert when there is concern for polymorphic VT in a patient who is drug loaded on dofetilide. Typically, these patients are admitted to the hospital for close monitoring of potential proarrhythmic side effects of the medication, as QT prolongation can lead to torsades de pointes. Careful inspection of all leads showed regular RR intervals marching through. In this case, the tracings were the result of artifact.

Zernikow et al6 show a case of polymorphic VT in a patient who had undergone heterotopic heart transplantation, a state in which both the donor heart and the native heart are in situ. The donor heart was in VT, but the patient’s native heart was in sinus rhythm and able to sustain the patient’s hemodynamic state enough for him to not develop syncope.

If this is all confusing, Bhardwaj et al7 go back to the basics, teaching the classic concepts of capture and fusion beats, the presence of which are pathognomonic for VT.

Finally, Vo et al8 and Byers and Beer9 describe challenging VT ablation cases from the cardiac crux and epicardial right ventricular outflow tract substrate, respectively, in a patient with Brugada syndrome. The cardiac crux is an epicardial structure. There were 2 distinct premature ventricular contraction morphologies with superior axis that were deemed to be different exit sites from the crux. Endocardial ablation with “kissing lesions” from the right and left ventricle terminated the premature ventricular contraction. Brugada syndrome is characterized by repolarization abnormalities harboring risk of VT and sudden cardiac death. Recently, epicardial ablation of right ventricular outflow tract substrate has been demonstrated to result in normalization of the ECG. Whether this correlates with improved clinical outcomes remains to be seen.

In summary, WCT is a daunting rhythm that requires careful consideration of all diagnostic possibilities. It will be interesting to see whether artificial intelligence–assisted ECG interpretation will deliver on its promise to improve WCT diagnosis. For now, a critical appraisal of the ECG is still mandatory.
