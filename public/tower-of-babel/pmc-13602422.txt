Operative Efficiency and Short‐Term Outcomes of Robotic Transabdominal Preperitoneal Inguinal Hernia Repair: A Prospective Single‐Surgeon Cohort Study
Authors: Yusef Hafez, Aleksandar Strkalj, Joseph Do Woong Choi, Mark Bukofzer, Stephen Pillinger, Christos Apostolou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundRobotic transabdominal preperitoneal inguinal hernia repair (R‐TAPP IHR) is a promising minimally invasive tool for inguinal hernias. This study aimed to evaluate short‐term postoperative outcomes following R‐TAPP IHR from a high‐volume robotic surgeon who has passed the initial robotic learning curve.MethodsA single‐surgeon, single‐institution prospective cohort study was conducted from August 2023 to October 2025. Adults who completed an elective R‐TAPP IHR were consecutively enrolled. We collected data including baseline demographics and operative characteristics. Postoperative data was assessed and collected via structured telephone follow‐up on postoperative day 1 (POD‐1), POD‐7, and POD‐28, evaluating pain scores, postoperative complications, and hospital readmissions.ResultsA total of 89 patients underwent 119 repairs. The median console duration was 21 min. The overall operative duration from incision to closure was a median of 38 min, while total theatre time (wheels in to wheels out) was a median of 68 min. These times continued to decrease over the duration of the study. Most patients were discharged on the same day, with a median length of stay of 0 days. Reported pain scores decreased over time, with median values of 4 (out of 10) on POD‐1, 2 on POD‐7, and 0 by POD‐28. Clavien–Dindo grades I–II complications occurred in 4.5% of cases. No grade III–IV complications were observed, and no recurrences were identified during follow‐up.ConclusionThis study demonstrates that R‐TAPP IHR in the post‐learning curve setting is highly efficient and is associated with favourable short‐term outcomes.

Introduction

Inguinal Hernia Repair (IHR) is a very common general surgical procedure, accounting for over 50 000 operations in Australia [1]. Over time, minimally invasive approaches have increasingly supplanted open approaches due to improved postoperative recovery, better postoperative pain outcomes and earlier return to activities of daily living [2, 3]. Within this evolving landscape, Robotic Inguinal Hernia Repair (RIHR) has emerged as an alternate form of minimally invasive surgery [2].

RIHR aims to improve patient outcomes and intraoperative challenges compared to traditional approaches [4, 5]. RIHR provides improved ergonomics, camera control by the surgeon, improved magnification and view, and compensation for minor tremors, potentially fostering improved dissections and more precise surgeries [5, 6, 7]. Robotic surgery also allows for “lifting of the abdominal wall” by the ports attached to the cart (clutch), reducing the pneumoperitoneum pressure [1]. This benefit may confer physiological advantages in patients with cardiopulmonary comorbidities by reducing the haemodynamic and respiratory burden associated with insufflation [1].

In our institution, there is increasing adoption of RIHR, and we have previously demonstrated its feasibility and safety with six experienced robotic hernia surgeons [1]. Existing literature suggests that RIHR may be comparable to the laparoscopic approach in overall complications, surgical site infections, haematoma or seroma formation. Some studies have also suggested that RIHR is associated with reduced hernia recurrence compared to laparoscopic approaches [7].

A frequently cited limitation of RIHR is the increased operation time and cost [7]. Operative time is however expected to decrease with greater surgeon experience [7]. This was shown in our recent study where the median console time, knife to skin and skin closure times, and total theatre turnover times were comparatively lower than previously reported. In addition, our department has a Simple Robotic Hernia Management Committee (SRHMC) which continues to meet monthly to update our protocol to improve resource utilisation. Results from a preliminary study at our institutions demonstrated how this was successfully implemented to minimise cost and improve theatre efficiency.

While RIHR has been shown to be safe and feasible, much of the existing literature is derived from early adoption phases or mixed‐experience cohorts. There is limited evidence evaluating operative efficiency and short‐term outcomes in a post‐learning curve cohort. As such, the reported increase in operative durations of R‐TAPP IHR in comparison to the laparoscopic approach may reflect learning curve effects rather than inherent limitations of the robotic approach.

To address this gap, we present a single‐surgeon cohort beyond the initial learning curve, thereby minimising inherent surgeon‐related variability. This study aims to evaluate operative efficiency, short‐term outcomes, and feasibility of R‐TAPP IHR within a controlled and more consistent surgical environment.

Methods

Study Design and Protocol

A prospective cohort study was conducted using the da Vinci Xi system from August 2023 to October 2025. Consecutive adult patients who underwent elective R‐TAPP IHR were eligible, including those that had recurrent inguinal hernia, inguino‐scrotal hernia, previous prostatectomy/cystectomy, incarcerated hernia, and previous pelvic radiation. The exclusion criteria included emergency IHR, concurrent bowel/other resections, and major comorbidities precluding general anaesthesia. Baseline demographics and operative details were collected from each patient. Thirty‐day postoperative outcomes were assessed via structured telephone follow‐up on postoperative day 1 (POD‐1), POD‐7 and POD‐28 evaluating pain scores, postoperative complications, and hospital readmissions.

Data Analysis

Analysis of results was completed using SPSS Statistics (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables were presented as frequency (n, %) and non‐normal distributions were reported as median with range or interquartile range (IQR).

In the comparison between early and late phase results, we performed a Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and a fisher's exact test for categorical variables. The correlations between operative duration variables, postoperative pain scores, and length of stay were assessed using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient (ρ).

To assess predictors of ongoing pain, defined as a postoperative pain score greater than one on a numerical 0–10 pain score, a binary logistic regression analysis was completed. Variables entered in this model included total console time, whether a bilateral repair was completed, body mass index (BMI), and pain score at POD‐7. Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness‐of‐fit test, and explanatory power was evaluated using Nagelkerke R2. Further, a linear regression analysis was performed to evaluate predictors of length of stay. Statistical significance was defined as a two‐sided p‐value < 0.05. Effect estimates are reported with 95% confidence intervals where appropriate.

Robotic Transabdominal Preperitoneal IHR Technique

The robotic transabdominal preperitoneal (R‐TAPP) technique has been previously described in our multi‐surgeon cohort study [1]. The robotic general surgery programme utilises two da Vinci Xi systems and is supported by a dedicated robotic team including surgeons, anaesthetists, and nursing staff. Pneumoperitoneum was established and three robotic ports were inserted under direct vision. A peritoneal flap was created and the preperitoneal space developed to expose the myopectineal orifice. Hernia sacs were reduced, and key anatomical structures including the inferior epigastric vessels, vas deferens, and gonadal vessels were identified and preserved. A self‐gripping mesh (Progrip: Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN) was placed to cover the myopectineal orifice without fixation. The peritoneal flap was then closed using a running barbed suture, and the abdomen was desufflated prior to port removal.

Results

Patient Demographics

A total of 119 R‐TAPP IHR were performed in 89 patients. Patient demographics are per Table 1. Of the 89 patients, the median age was 66, male patients made up 83% of the patient cohort, and the mean BMI of patients was 25.96. Bilateral IHR comprised 30 patients. A total of 27 patients reported preoperative pain related to the hernia, 3 patients took preoperative analgesia, and three patients had presented due to hernia recurrence. Follow‐up data were available for 82 patients (92%) at POD‐1, 80 patients (90%) at POD‐7, and 76 patients (85%) at POD‐28.

Most patients were discharged on the same day (or within 24 h), with the median length of stay of 0 days. The median postoperative pain score peaked at POD‐1 (median score of 4 out of 10) which decreased progressively to POD‐28. Within 30 days, there were few postoperative complications with complications limited to Grade 1–2 Clavien‐Dindo events. The Clavien‐Dindo Grade 2 events involved two cases who had postoperative bleeding requiring blood transfusions related to a pre‐existing bleeding disorder. For the Clavien‐Dindo Grade 1 patients, three patients were postoperative haematomas or seromas, all of which were managed non‐operatively. No hernia recurrence was observed.

Operative Duration Over Time

Spearman's correlation demonstrated negative correlations between consecutive case number and all unilateral operative duration measures. Consecutive case number correlated with console time (ρ = −0.561, p < 0.001), knife‐to‐skin duration (ρ = −0.616, p < 0.001), and total theatre time (ρ = −0.640, p < 0.001), indicating reduced operative times over the study period (Figures 1, 2, 3).

Postoperative Pain Score Correlations

Pain scores demonstrated moderate to strong positive correlations across postoperative time points on Spearman's correlation. Pain on discharge correlated with pain at POD‐1 (ρ = 0.577, p = 0.001); however, no significant correlation existed with POD‐7 (ρ = 0.362, p = 0.054) or POD‐28 (ρ = 0.279, p = 0.151). Pain at POD‐1 correlated with POD‐7 (ρ = 0.482, p < 0.001) and POD‐28 (ρ = 0.393, p < 0.001) pain. A strong correlation was observed between POD‐7 and POD‐28 pain scores (ρ = 0.557, p < 0.001).

Binary logistic regression analysis (N = 70) identified that POD‐7 pain independently predicted ongoing pain at POD‐28 (OR 1.95, 95% CI 1.38–2.74, p < 0.001). Each one‐point increase in POD‐7 pain was found to increase the odds of pain at POD‐28 by 95%. However, operative duration, bilateral surgery, and BMI were not independently associated with POD‐28 pain. No significant correlations were identified between operative duration variables and postoperative pain scores at discharge, POD‐1, POD‐7, or POD‐28 (all p > 0.05).

Operative Duration and Length of Stay

Operative duration showed a correlation with length of stay. Console time (ρ = 0.353, p < 0.001), knife‐to‐skin duration (ρ = 0.312, p = 0.003), and total theatre time (ρ = 0.245, p = 0.022) were each positively correlated with length of stay, indicating that longer operative duration was associated with prolonged inpatient recovery. In linear regression analysis, operative duration remained a significant predictor of length of stay (β = 0.441, p < 0.001).

Length of Stay and Pain

Length of stay was moderately correlated with pain at discharge (ρ = 0.448, p = 0.012). No significant correlation was observed between operative duration and postoperative pain scores.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that in a post‐learning curve setting, R‐TAPP IHR can be performed with a high degree of operative efficiency and is well suited to a day‐case model. We observed short operative durations, low levels of postoperative pain, and minimal postoperative complications. Importantly, our findings suggest that previously reported time‐related disadvantages of RTAPP‐IHR may be reflective of early adoption inefficiencies, rather than limitations of this technique itself.

Increasing surgeon robotic experience demonstrated statistically significant improvement in efficiency, with reduced operative time seen amongst all intraoperative time variables. Improvements in operative efficiencies and outcomes are well described in the literature, with studies such as Kudsi et al. demonstrating, using Cumulative Sum methodology, significant reductions in operative time and surgical site events after 138 cases [8]. In contrast, we observed comparable reductions in intraoperative durations after only 30 cases. However, the operating surgeon had already performed 100 robotic cases prior to the study's commencement. As such, these findings are better interpreted as procedural optimisation within a post‐learning curve phase rather than early skill acquisition. This distinction indicates that surgeons who have surpassed the robotic learning curve phase may achieve greater operative outcomes more rapidly than previously reported in the current literature when adopting R‐TAPP IHR. Importantly, in institutions where surgeons have already surpassed the robotic learning curve, R‐TAPP can be integrated efficiently into clinical practice with minimal transitional inefficiencies.

A commonly cited limitation of RIHR is prolonged operative duration in comparison to laparoscopic (L‐IHR) and open (O‐IHR) techniques. However, our findings challenge this perception. In our post‐learning curve cohort, the median unilateral operative time was 38 min, which is substantially lower than previously reported. For example, the unilateral operative duration time of RIHR in the RIVAL RCT was 75.5 min, where surgeons were still in the initial robotic learning curve phase [9]. While longer operative times have been attributed to factors including docking and undocking of the robot or related to peritoneal closure requiring a running suture closure rather than tacks, we suggest that these explanations may be confounded by surgeon and institutional experience. When considered alongside the initial lower operative time of R‐TAPP IHR that is frequently reported, and the significant reductions in console time, skin‐to‐skin duration, and total theatre time demonstrated after the first 30 unilateral cases, these findings reinforce that operative duration in RIHR is highly experience‐dependent. It seems that the current literature may not adequately account for the magnitude of this post‐learning curve effect. Therefore, in a post‐learning curve setting, we found that R‐TAPP IHR can be performed with a high level of efficiency, with operative times approaching, and potentially surpassing laparoscopic repair times.

A significant correlation existed between operative duration and length of stay; however, not postoperative pain. Increased operative duration was associated with a higher likelihood of discharge beyond 24 h. However, most of our patients had a length of hospital stay less than 24 h, with a minority of patients requiring a one‐day stay and only two patients requiring a 2‐day admission. These findings are similar to those reported in the ROLAIS trial where 95.9% of r‐TAPP patients had a same‐day admission, which was lower than their laparoscopic group [10]. In comparison to other approaches, this was lower than reported length of stay with L‐IHR and O‐IHR having an average length of stay of 2.2 and 3.6 days, respectively [11]. While overall length of stay is shown to be favourable in a surgeon who has surpassed the robotic learning curve, we suggest that as procedural refinement continues to improve in the post‐learning curve setting, the likelihood of prolonged hospital stay may further decrease.

There were good postoperative safety outcomes in our single‐surgeon analysis. Postoperative pain scores were acceptably low and support prior studies that describe low and equivalent pain scores compared to L‐IHR, as described in our multi‐surgeon analysis [1]. No patients required conversion to open surgery, and postoperative complications were limited to Grade 1–2 Clavien–Dindo events. Complications amongst patients were not associated with increased operative time nor were they seen to occur in the early phases in this study. These findings are consistent with existing literature [8, 12].

Limitations of this study include the external validity and reproducibility of the results (particularly in institutions with minimal/no robotic experience), as the robotic staff and surgeon were from an established high‐volume robotic unit. Secondly, we did not directly compare outcomes with L‐IHR or O‐IHR; rather, we compared our results with the reported literature which included heterogenous population groups, surgeons at differing career stages, and using different RIHR approaches. Third, not all patients followed up on POD‐1 and POD‐28, with 93% and 85% of patients following up respectively. Additionally, outcomes were measured up to 30 days; thus, longer‐term events may have been under‐reported. Lastly, the authors are aware of global accessibility issues to robotic technology due to expense, although preliminary results from this institution described how the cost of RIHR could be effectively minimised to sustain a robotic hernia surgery program.

Conclusion

R‐TAPP IHR is feasible and safe as a day‐only procedure within a high‐volume robotic centre with a defined RIHR protocol. In this post‐learning curve setting, operative times were substantially lower than previously reported, suggesting that time‐related disadvantages may reflect early adoption inefficiencies rather than intrinsic limitations of the technique. Early postoperative pain and operative duration were associated with short‐term recovery outcomes, identifying potential targets for perioperative optimisation.
