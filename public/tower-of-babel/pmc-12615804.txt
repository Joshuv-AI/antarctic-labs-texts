Influence of mechanical strength on the characteristics of aluminium profiled glazed fire door
Authors: Bartłomiej Sędłak, Marzena Jakimowicz, Krzysztof Kuczyński
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study examined the impact of mechanical fatigue—caused by repeated opening and closing—on the fire resistance and airtightness of single-leaf glazed aluminium-profiled fire doors with EI 30 and EI 60 ratings. Twelve door specimens were tested, with ten undergoing mechanical cycling ranging from 50,000 to 1,000,000 cycles prior to fire resistance evaluation. The research included air permeability measurements, gap assessments, self-closing performance, and full-scale fire resistance tests based on integrity and insulation criteria. While fire resistance was not significantly affected by mechanical fatigue—even after one million cycles—a substantial increase in air permeability was observed. This occurred despite only minor changes in rebate gap dimensions, suggesting that frequent use could notably impair smoke control performance. Additionally, the greatest structural deformations were recorded within the first five minutes of fire exposure, highlighting a discrepancy with current European testing methodology. The findings indicated that while fire resistance remains stable under extensive use, smoke tightness may degrade considerably, warranting further investigation.

Introduction

The critical role and vulnerability of fire doors in service

Fire doors are a critical component in the fire safety strategy of buildings. They are required by national building codes in many European countries (e.g1,2. , to be installed in compartment walls and at access points to specific rooms across a wide range of structures, including public buildings3,4, high-rise buildings5–7, and special-purpose structures like tunnels8 or shelters9,10. Their primary function is to facilitate the safe evacuation of occupants and ensure the safety of rescue teams by restraining the spread of fire and smoke.

An adequate fire rating of doors means that the fire can be restrained in a given spot for some time, while an adequate smoke control rating should ensure proper visibility and lower concentration of toxic gases along the evacuation routes. Nevertheless, both properties may be meaningless if the door is not equipped with a self-closing mechanism. Smoke-tight and fire-resistant doors fulfil their functions only when closed11,12. Ensuring their self-closing capability is pivotal if they are installed in evacuation routes. That is why the lasting self-closing performance (service life) is an equally important feature of fire doors. However, according to commonly used European procedures13, each of the above characteristic is determined independently. The self-closing capability is not only critical for evacuation routes but also for any installation context, since a fire door left open cannot fulfil its protective function. Classified fire doors are designated by ratings such as EI 30 or EI 60, where “E” denotes integrity (flame and hot gas tightness) and “I” denotes insulation (thermal protection), and the number indicates the time in minutes for which these criteria are fulfilled under exposure to the ISO 83414 standard fire curve. In real fire conditions, fire doors might have to operate correctly after many opening-closing cycles. During an evacuation, only a few building users will remember to close the door through which they escape the fire. A functional self-closing door mechanism or a spring installed in the hinge shall ensure its effective closing. Any door that does not meet this requirement above shall not be approved for use. That is why a structure approved by an inspector can be considered utterly safe in light of the referenced phenomenon. A question arises as to what happens to the door during the further life cycle of a building structure. The door can be exposed to hundreds of thousands of opening-closing cycles during use. Their number depends on the time that has elapsed from installing the door in the structure and on the door’s location, which is related to the expected traffic intensity to which the door will be exposed. Most manufacturers regularly maintain doors exposed to such frequent influences, but one can easily imagine a fire occurring during maintenance intervals. According to European regulatory practice, certified fire doors must be subject to periodic inspection and maintenance. The inspection intervals vary depending on national legislation and the manufacturer’s requirements and typically range from 6 to 12 months. Unfortunately, in many cases they involve additional fees, and therefore the servicing of doors takes place only when visible damage occurs.

The self-closing issue is not the only problem related to using fire doors. Even if the door closes correctly after many thousands of cycles, one cannot be sure it fulfils the given fire resistance or smoke control rating requirements. During use, the door can be subject to various failures that do not lead to problems with its correct closing but might deteriorate its fire characteristics. Previous studies and field inspections have shown that door locks or hinges may become damaged, leading to increased rebate gaps and a loss of edge tightness under elevated temperature15–17. When hinges become misaligned, the gaps between the door leaf and frame may become too big to be replenished by an intumescent seal active during a fire. Gross and Haberman18 provided a quantitative model linking narrow door gaps to elevated leakage rates, showing that small increases in gap size can lead to significant airflow increments—thus, disturbances such as hinge misalignment can detrimentally affect tightness under pressure differences. Additionally, Gorgulu and Merdan19 showed through finite element analysis that suboptimal hinge positioning leads to increased localized stress and deformation in door systems, suggesting that even minor hinge misplacement can degrade mechanical stability and, by extension, sealing integrity. It should be highlighted that an intumescent seal may be missing from the door edge or frame if it has come apart due to the door leaf’s continuous movements. This component type is typically glued to the door leaf or frame. Its becoming detached might cause hazards in case of fire. Inspection schemes had repeatedly identified faulty or missing intumescent or smoke seals as the most common failures in fire doors, present in over 60% of inspected assemblies – such defects undermine the door’s ability to seal gaps during a fire20. Brittle materials, including but not limited to some types of fire-resistant boards, are particularly susceptible to cracking or discontinuities, which in turn reduce thermal insulation capacity17. Discontinuities formed in the board structure might result in the door leaf losing its (fire) insulating properties. These degradation mechanisms, frequently documented in inspections and numerical modelling studies, highlight the complexity of maintaining reliable fire door performance throughout long-term use.

The examples above are the easiest to imagine and the most common types of damage caused by using the door. One should remember that other structural items or accessories of fire doors can also be subject to failure. The discussed issues reveale a vital problem. We are likely unaware that we often use fire doors whose technical conditions can be described as defective for quite some time.

Theoretically, a correctly performed service life test (opening-closing cycles) of the product, checking the self-closing performance and verifying the door’s condition after the test should answer how long the door can function without adjustments. Because the tests are performed independently of the fire resistance or smoke control tests, they cannot be used as the basis to determine whether a fire occuring in the maintenance interval (if performed) will not contribute to the fire propagating from one room to another faster than formally required for the structure. This paper aims to verify the influence of numerous self-closing operations on the door’s fire resistance.

From the structural point of view, fire doors can be divided into three types, depending on the component materials. There are wooden doors, steel doors with face plates, and doors with metal profiles (steel or aluminium). Doors can also be divided depending on the number of leaves (single-leaf, double-leaf, etc.) or their opening method (hinged, sliding, rolling, etc.). They can all be suspended on various types of frames and installed in any of the several supporting constructions, which can also be used as the basis for their division.

In order to obtain an unequivocal answer to the question above, it would be ideal to verify all possible door combinations, considering the applied construction materials of the door leaf and frame, the number of leaves and the opening method. Each door should be subjected to cycle testing followed by fire resistance tests. Nevertheless, this would entail significant financial and time expenditures (if there are many opening-closing cycles, the tests last for months).

The tests done under this study were limited to only one type of design – a single leaf, hinged, aluminium-profiled glazed door. Twelve door samples (six declared as EI 30 rated and six as EI 60 rated) were tested for fire resistance. Ten of them were subjected to varying numbers of opening-closing cycles before starting the furnace.

State of the art and research gap

The available literature contains hardly any publications directly on the influence of mechanical strength on fire door properties. The topics related to the discussed aspects were mentioned by this paper’s authors in21. The article summarised only the test results for four door samples rated as EI 30 for no more than 200,000 opening-closing cycles. A relatively low number of tests performed did not provide sufficient information to solve the problem mentioned in this paper. In addition to the referenced publication, there are some articles on a similar topic. As part of15, field tests were conducted on several structures located in South Korea. The aim was to determine the failure rate and cause with regard to the fire door age. Inspections revealed that the primary failures were related to door opening-closing cycles, exceeding the acceptable gaps or wear of accessories such as door closers. All the faults mentioned could be caused by numerous opening-closing cycles of the door leaves when the structures were used. The first of the issues, discussed in detail in22,23, is particularly dangerous. Problems related to maintaining adequate characteristics of fire doors are also presented in16, analysing litigation cases resulting from discrepancies between the real and declared door properties.

Fire resistance tests on doors exposed to other loads were performed under17. The simulated loads were not related to numerous opening-closing cycles of the door leaves but to seismic shocks. The fire resistance of the doors subjected to cyclic loads simulating an earthquake decreased by as much as 70%. The criteria were mainly exceeded because of the gaps formed between the door leaf and frame, caused, e.g. by loosened hinges. Loads resulting from numerous opening-closing cycles of the door leaves were expected to be less destructive than those caused by an earthquake. Nevertheless, in the absence of systematic studies, it remained unclear whether such mechanical fatigue could lead to measurable degradation of fire resistance. This uncertainty motivated the present investigation.

In recent years, other tests had been conducted to determine the fire resistance of doors. Paper24 presents the test results for wooden and steel doors exposed to a natural fire characteristic for hotel rooms. Capote et al.25 analyzed the thermal behavior of EI₂-60 fire doors under ISO 83414 furnace exposure. Using infrared thermography alongside thermocouples, they observed layer separation within the door, which promoted convective heat transfer and accelerated temperature rise on the unexposed face. The study identified three stages of thermal response and highlighted how such mechanisms reduce insulation performance. The authors concluded that preventing material separation and improving bonding techniques are crucial for enhancing fire door resistance. N. Ayman et al.26 compared the results of fire resistance tests of a steel door depending on the applied fixing structure. The expected 30-minute fire resistance rating was achieved only for a rigid fixing structure. Wu et al.27 conducted a fire resistance test on a steel fire door without internal infill, analyzing the test data to evaluate its fire-resistant performance. The findings indicated that the absence of insulation affected the door’s ability to withstand fire exposure, highlighting the importance of internal materials in fire door design. H. W. Seo et al.28 verified the fire insulation of doors characterised only by fire integrity. Similar tests were performed as part of20, verifying temperature increases on the unexposed door surface depending on the mineral wool type used in the door. The tests revealed that the mineral wool layer’s thickness contributed to the fire insulation more than its density. Papers29,30 summarised the fire insulation results for various design doors depending on the door leaf opening direction. The test results confirmed the need to verify the doors’ fire resistance on both sides. Thanasoulas et al.31 conducted large-scale fire tests on sliding doors to assess their fire resistance in building applications. Their study found that sliding doors could meet fire resistance requirements, with performance influenced by the side of fire exposure.

C. Panno et al.32 performed tests to improve the fire resistance of wooden doors. Using cement board prototypes as additional lining improved the door’s fire insulation and integrity up to thirty minutes. Innovative solutions intended to improve the fire characteristics of fire doors were also tested under several other studies carried out in recent years. The applied solutions included dedicated soundproofing inserts33, vacuum insulation panels34, calcium silicate boards35, Kapok tree (Ceiba pentandra) fibres mixed with gypsum36,37, a dedicated intumescent coat for wooden doors38, earth material core39 or double seals used in threshold-free doors40.

Studies verifying the door’s fire resistance using numerical methods are also worth mentioning. Hugi et al.41 studied the thermal response of a butted steel door frame under ISO 83414 fire. Temperatures on the room side were measured and compared with numerical simulations, which showed good agreement. The study highlighted how frame design and insulation affected fire resistance, providing insights for improving steel door performance. Tabaddor et al.42 performed a thermo-mechanical analysis of steel double fire doors under a fire endurance test. Using finite element modeling, they captured thermal and mechanical behavior, including thermal contact effects. Model predictions matched experimental results, demonstrating the value of numerical simulations in evaluating fire door performance. Moro et al.43 investigated an innovative fire door design that combines optimized insulating materials and structural plates to enhance resistance to thermal deformation, demonstrating the value of computational modeling for performance prediction. Similarly, Tripathi et al.44 used numerical simulations to assess fire door behavior under realistic fire scenarios, highlighting the role of virtual testing in complementing experimental methods. The exploration of novel materials had been addressed by Kyaw Oo D’Amore et al.45, who modeled thermal performance in shipboard fire doors, showing that material selection can significantly influence fire resistance. Structural improvements had also been examined: Khalifa et al.27 demonstrated that incorporating internal stiffeners into the door leaf enhanced mechanical stability during fire exposure, validated by both experiments and numerical simulations. For specialized applications, Boscariol et al.46 performed a thermo-mechanical analysis for naval fire doors, providing insight into thermal and mechanical interactions under extreme conditions, while Zhang et al.47 developed a thermal model for large fire doors in nuclear reactors, emphasizing the importance of detailed thermal analysis for critical infrastructure. Prieler et al.48 developed a numerical model to predict the fire resistance of gypsum-sheathed stud walls with embedded steel doors. The model simulated thermal and mechanical behavior and was validated against experimental tests, showing reliable predictions of fire performance. Nurić et al.49 applied finite element methods to simulate fire effects, confirming the utility of numerical modeling for evaluating fire resistance and supporting design optimization. Finally, Lim50 investigated the fire resistance performance of double leaf fire doors using a thermo-mechanical model, focusing on the meeting stile gap. The door was modeled as a simply supported beam under thermal gradients, and a deformation criterion was proposed linking gap size to maximum allowable deflection. Experimental validation on insulated and non-insulated doors confirmed the model’s accuracy, highlighting that proper control of gap size is critical for maintaining structural integrity and fire resistance.

The available literature lacks papers based on which one could unequivocally answer the question of the service time after which a fire door loses its fire characteristics. Regarding the above, numerous tests discussed in successive chapters were performed to answer the study question.

Aim, scope and hypotheses

This research directly addresses the identified gap by providing a systematic experimental investigation into the effects of long-term mechanical wear. The study quantifies the impact of up to one million opening-closing cycles – simulating decades of use in high-traffic areas – on both the fire resistance (EI criteria) and the airtightness (as a proxy for smoke control) of a specific and common type of door: single-leaf, fully glazed, aluminium-profiled fire doors. The study aims to determine whether operational fatigue can degrade these critical performance characteristics before visible damage occurs, with significant implications for maintenance regimes and safety assurance.

Based on the reviewed literature and preliminary observations, we formulated two key hypotheses:

Mechanical cycling will progressively increase air permeability due to cumulative wear of seals and misalignment of components.Fire resistance (E/I criteria) will remain unaffected regardless of cycle count (up to 1 million), as intumescent seal activation during fire exposure compensates for pre-existing minor gaps.

Materials and methods

This study involved testing the fire resistance of twelve samples (six declared as EI2 30 fire-rated, marked as 1.1 ÷ 1.6 and six samples declared as EI2 60 fire-rated marked as 2.1 ÷ 2.6) of hinged, single-leaf aluminium-profiled glazed door. The test specimens were fully glazed with no transverse division bar. The door frame’s outer dimensions were 1,120 × 2,070 mm (width x height), and the door leaf was 1,018 × 2,011 mm. The door leaf and the door frame jambs and header were made of identical three-chamber aluminium profiles. The profile, with cross-section dimensions of 46.0 × 80.0 mm, was made of two aluminium shapes connected with a thermal spacer made of glass fibre-reinforced polyamide. The middle chamber’s space between thermal spacers and inner walls of aluminium shapes was filled with insulation inserts made of two F-type gypsum boards, 15 mm thick each. For samples marked as 2.1 ÷ 2.6, additional insulation inserts made of the same board type were also used in the outer chambers. In the case of samples marked as 2.5 and 2.6, an additional threshold made of a 20 mm high aluminium profile was installed along the bottom edge. The door was glazed with a dedicated fire-resistant glass pane; it was 17 mm thick for the samples marked as 1.1 ÷ 1.6 and 26 mm thick for the samples marked as 2.1 ÷ 2.6. The glazing was fixed to the profiles using steel angles covered with an aluminium glazing bead. The door featured three triple-winged hinges, three anti-theft bolts, a single-point lock, an aluminium door handle with a steel core and an overhead door closer. Each of the tested door sets was fixed using steel frame screws in a rigid fixing structure made of autoclaved aerated concrete header and blocks. The gaps between the tested door frame and the fixing structure were tightly filled with polyurethane foam and coated with gypsum mortar. All door specimens originated from the same manufacturer and represented the same product type. For reasons of confidentiality, the manufacturer’s name is not disclosed; this decision is intentional and does not affect the technical validity of the findings. All doors were installed strictly according to the manufacturer’s installation instructions and the respective product approval conditions. A representative photo of the tested doors is presented in Fig. 1, while the information regarding the structure of the samples and installation is given in Table 1.

Before the fire resistance tests, ten samples (marked as 1.2 ÷ 1.6 and 2.2 ÷ 2.6) were subjected to numerous opening-closing cycles of the door leaves (mechanical strength). Samples marked as 1.1 and 2.1 were subjected only to fire resistance tests and were supposed to confirm the manufacturer’s declared fire resistance rating. For the referenced samples, only the door leaves self-closing capability was tested. They were opened manually to over a 90-degree angle, kept in an open position and then released to close using the door closer mounted on the door leaf.

The opening-closing cycles for the tested samples were conducted using a dedicated, mobile testing apparatus designed in accordance with EN 119151. The device was mounted directly onto the door leaf for each test. It was equipped with a pneumatic actuator that controlled the rotational movement of the door leaf and an automatic cycle counter. The apparatus was capable of adapting the reference velocity of the door leaf movement according to EN 119151, depending on the leaf’s mass. The measuring equipment included a cycle counter and means for measuring the velocity and time of door opening and closing with an accuracy of ± 5%. The machine’s mobile arms pressed the door handle and then pushed the door leaf open at a rate suited to the door leaf weight. After that motion, the machine’s arm returned to its original position, and the door leaf was closed with the door closer. For samples 1.2 and 2.2, 50,000 cycles were performed; for samples 1.3 and 2.3–100,000 cycles; for samples 1.4 and 2.4–200,000 cycles; for sample 1.5 and 2.5–500,000 cycles; and for sample 1.6 and 2.6–1,000,000 opening-closing cycles.

A million opening-closing cycles corresponds roughly to approximately100 cycles/day over approximately 27 years, or approximately 200 cycles/day over approximately 14 years. Most interior fire doors in commercial buildings likely see substantially fewer operations; high-traffic locations (e.g., main corridor or cross-corridor doors or doors between circulation hubs) can, however, approach hundreds of daily operations. We therefore used 1,000,000 cycles to bound worst-case wear effects (hinge/closer play, latch alignment, and cumulative edge set) before the fire test. Our goal was to probe whether extreme but plausible wear amplified known thermo-mechanical failure modes (bowing-induced unlatching, local leakage/flaming at the latch edge) rather than to imply that all doors will reach one million cycles in service.

For samples 1.3 and 1.4, before and after cycle testing, the rebate gaps were measured to check for significant mutual displacement of the door leaf and frame (the measuring points are shown in Fig. 2). The operating forces were measured to check the tested door’s performance and ergonomics. Moreover, the structural and sealing components and the door fittings were visually inspected for wear. Additionally, air permeability through the test specimen was tested before and after the cycles. For samples 1.5, 1.6, 2.5 and 2.6, the described operations were performed before the test and after each 100,000 door opening-closing cycles.

The air permeability test was conducted on a sample mounted in a masonry wall using a dedicated box connected to the measuring unit. Figure 3 shows the photos taken during the permeability test.

The test involved using a specific series of test pressures (positive and negative) and measuring the amount of air migrating through the sample for the given pressure. The test aimed to determine the changes in the air permeability of doors exposed to opening-closing cycles. The air flow was verified at the positive and negative pressure values of 50, 100, 150, 200, 250, 300, 450, and 600 Pa. The amount of air necessary to maintain pressure in a tight test chamber closed with the tested sample (built-in door) for which air permeability was determined referring to the total area and the opening gap length was the test result. The test result for each measurement step was recalculated to standard air flow conditions (standard temperature – T0 = 293 K; standard pressure – P0 = 101.3 kPa) separately for the opening’s contact line and the measured sample’s surface according to the inner dimensions of the sample’s frame.

Fire resistance tests were performed after testing the cycles and air permeability (for selected samples) in a vertical furnace with clear opening dimensions of 3.7 × 3.7 m, at the Building Research Institute (ITB) in Pionki (Poland). The tests were conducted following the procedure defined in EN 1634-152. The specimens were mounted in a rigid supporting wall fixed in a steel testing frame. A schematic of the furnace and installation configuration is provided in Fig. 4.

The present experiment was designed to isolate thermo-mechanical failure mechanisms of fire resistance (E – integrity/I - insulation) during ISO 83414 furnace exposure, using realistic aluminum-framed glazed doors. The ISO 83414 standard heating curve was selected to ensure comparability with the majority of published fire-door studies and provide a conservative, repeatable thermal exposure for mechanistic interpretation. Previous studies (e.g., Joyeux24; Capote et al.25, M. Tabaddor et al.42 document bowing-induced integrity failure and insulation loss under such fire conditions. We therefore instrumented full-scale doors with thermocouples and displacement measurements, and compared as-manufactured versus mechanically cycled specimens to test whether long-term operational fatigue shifted the door’s thermo-mechanical response envelope.

We prioritized time-resolved leaf temperatures to detect hot-spots and deflection measurements to track thermally-induced bowing, and gap evolution at the door leaf lock edge as a proxy for integrity (E-criterion) risk; these had been identified as key predictors of failure in experimental and numerical studies of fire doors.

Discussion

Impact of mechanical cycling on airtightness and implications for smoke control

Analysing the test results shown in Figs. 6 and 7, one can notice that numerous opening-closing cycles (mechanical performance) increased the amount of air migrating through the door. For sample 1.5, after 500,000 cycles, the air flow value increased by approximately 60% (at the test pressure of 50 Pa). Interestingly, such a considerable door air permeability change occurred at a minor enlargement of the rebate gap sizes measured according to Fig. 3, i.e. 0.2% to 1.56%. According to Table 2, the gap sizes increased by a maximum of 1.20% for sample 1.6 (after one million opening-closing cycles) and 1.56% for sample 2.6 (after one million opening-closing cycles) The changes concerning the door leaf closing time, similar to the gap size change, were considered insignificant – for sample 1.6, the door leaf closing time was 2.43% longer than that measured before the performance test; for sample 2.6, it was longer by 2.87% compared to the value measured before one million opening-closing cycles. Cyclic loading likely caused permanent deformation of the soft rebate seals, micro-wear on the contact surfaces of the lock and strike plate, and minimal loosening of hinges. This created microscopic leakage paths that were significant for air penetration (as shown by the permeability test) but too small to be detected by a gap gauge. Fortunately, these micro-gaps are effectively sealed during a fire by the expansion of intumescent seals, which explained the maintained fire resistance. This observation was consistent with earlier findings from the literature. Gross and Haberman18 demonstrated that even very small increases in door gaps can cause disproportionately high air leakage rates, which was fully in line with the permeability trends recorded here. Similarly, the numerical analyses of Görgülü and Merdan19 showed that minor hinge misplacements led to significant stress concentrations and loss of sealing capacity—again reflecting the sensitivity of smoke control performance to micro-scale deformations. Capote et al.25 and Yoon16 further highlighted that local imperfections at the frame–leaf interface could accelerate fire spread or legal disputes regarding door performance. Our results complemented these studies by showing that, while intumescent seals in aluminium glazed doors were sufficient to maintain the EI criteria even after one million cycles.

The observed significant variations in air permeability indicated that the opening-closing cycles had a notable impact on the smoke control performance of the doors. In accordance with EN 1634-357, smoke control testing is carried out under pressure differentials of 10, 25, and 50 Pa, both at ambient temperature (20 °C for class Sa) and at elevated temperature (200 °C for class S200). The maximum permissible leakage rate for single-leaf doors, as specified in EN 13501-258, is 20 m3/h. The results provide a quantitative measure of this impact. The relative increase in air leakage is the critical finding. For instance, the 60% increase in leakage measured for specimen 1.5 demonstrates the potential scale of degradation. A door that initially meets the smoke leakage requirement at the upper limit of 20 m3/h would, after a similar 60% increase, reach a leakage of 32 m3/h, far exceeding the permissible limit. Even a door with a comfortable initial safety margin (e.g., 12 m3/h) would, after the same relative degradation, reach 19.2 m3/h, critically approaching the failure threshold. This analysis shows that mechanical fatigue from normal operation is a plausible and significant factor that can cause a loss of smoke control performance during a door’s service life. Consequently, these findings highlight that smoke control performance cannot be assured over time without considering mechanical usage. The clear degradation of airtightness underscores the importance of dedicated maintenance and periodic inspection of seals based on the door’s usage intensity, independent of its other classifications.

Impact of mechanical cycling on fire resistance

The door functionality after the opening-closing cycles was maintained for all measured samples. That is why it was possible to conduct fire resistance tests whose results are summarised in Tables 3 and 4 and shown in Figs. 8 and 9. Detailed analysis suggests that numerous opening-closing cycles did not negatively influence the door’s fire resistance rating. No significant changes in the samples subjected to cycle testing were observed for fire insulation compared to the reference samples (samples 1.1 and 2.1 marked as C-0 in the diagrams). The temperature increases within the rating period expected for the tested doors (30 and 60 min) were similar for all tested samples. After that time, the differences between temperature rises for each sample increased. The results indicated that mechanical cycling up to 1,000,000 cycles did not systematically change the insulation (I) temperature histories on the unexposed face. The nearly overlapping temperature curves across cycled and uncycled specimens suggested that conductive/radiative heat transfer through the glazing and profiles dominated the I-criterion response and remained insensitive to moderate changes in edge play produced by cycling. This alignment was consistent with prior door studies in which insulation failure is governed primarily by section build-up and thermal gradients, rather than by small pre-fire geometric deviations.

Evaluating the influence of cycles on maintaining integrity was much more complex. According to the results summarised in Table 3, for sample 1.2 (50,000 cycles), fire integrity was exceeded in the 7th minute of the test when the cotton pad applied to the bottom part of the door leaf lock edge caught fire. Before using the cotton pad, single flames were observed to penetrate the gap between the door leaf and frame in the referenced surface. When the cotton pad was applied, it caught fire once another flame occurred. A similar phenomenon was observed for sample 1.4 (200,000 cycles). In this test, similar to sample 1.2, single flames penetrated the unexposed side of the test specimen at the bottom part of the door leaf lock edge. It was observed a minute later than for sample 1.2, and the cotton pad applied to the reference spot did not catch fire. In both cases, flames occurred due to significant displacement of the bottom part of the door leaf’s profile on the door leaf lock edge against the jamb (Fig. 10c). The flames appeared within less than a minute and were not continuous; the fire lasted several seconds and disappeared afterwards. Interestingly, a considerable relative deformation was recorded for the bottom part of the door leaf lock edge after one million cycles. In this case, no flames were observed to penetrate outside the test chamber. In specimen 1.2, three short-lived flame-through events were observed at the bottom lock-edge corner. Each lasted approximately 2–3 s and coincided with a local lock-edge gap of 11.6 mm and a threshold gap of 10.4 mm. In specimen 1.4, two similar events were recorded, each lasting less than 2 s, with corresponding lock-edge and threshold gaps of 12.7 mm and 10.8 mm, respectively. In contrast, specimen 1.6 exhibited the largest bottom lock-edge displacement during the fire test; however, the pre-test measurements indicated a lock-edge gap of 11.8 mm and a threshold gap of only 8.8 mm, and no visible flame-through was recorded in this case. The occurrence of brief flame-through in specimens 1.2 and 1.4 appeared to be influenced by the combined effect of the lock-edge gap and the threshold gap at the bottom corner. Both specimens exhibited relatively large openings in this critical location (11.6–12.7 mm at the door leaf lock edge and 10.4–10.8 mm at the threshold), which might have facilitated short flame leakage. In contrast, specimen 1.6, although showing the highest bottom-edge displacement during fire exposure, had a smaller pre-test threshold gap (8.8 mm). This tighter configuration might have allowed the intumescent seals to close the threshold gap more effectively, preventing flame penetration. These findings suggested that local gap geometry and sealing efficiency, rather than the overall magnitude of door leaf displacement, govern the likelihood of transient flame-through. Moreover, the manner of integrity failure could be challenged from the fire safety point of view. A short-lasting minor flame in the gap between the door leaf’s bottom corner and the frame, even if repeated several times, would be unlikely contribute to fire propagation to the adjacent zone.

This nuance indicates that Hypothesis 2 should be interpreted with some caution: while the overall fire resistance classification (EI) was not compromised even after one million cycles, local and short-lived integrity losses were observed in some specimens. These events underline that operational wear might not reduce the declared rating, but could still create transient leakage phenomena at critical locations.

The integrity (E) is controlled by thermo-mechanical deformation and hardware restraint. We observed the characteristic early-time peak in lock-edge displacement (first minutes of exposure), consistent with classical bowing of a door leaf subjected to through-thickness temperature gradients and edge restraint. As reported elsewhere, this deformation concentrated at the door leaf lock edge where latching resisted rotation, promoting gap opening if tolerances were marginal.

For aluminum-profiled assemblies, elevated-temperature property loss and relatively high thermal expansion amplified those deformations, especially where the profile/glazing system must maintain compression on seals and beads. Eurocode59 based material data and reviews60,61 predict rapid stiffness/strength decay and significant thermal expansion for common Al alloys under ISO 83414 heating—mechanisms that plausibly explained the measured displacement trends.

The absence of a clear degradation in integrity performance across the cycled series (within the resolution of our sample size) suggested that long-term operational wear at the selected levels did not introduce defects large enough to dominate failure, e.g., gross latch misalignment for the tested design. This observation was consistent with full-scale door studies where integrity is often lost due to thermal actions during the test (bowing, hardware softening) rather than pre-existing small deviations, provided that the door remains functional.

Early-stage deformation and its implications for testing standards

Continuous displacement measurement during the test confirmed a phenomenon significant for the fire resistance testing methodology, observed in glazed aluminium partitions54,62. Quantitatively, the maximum displacements observed at the first five minutes were 26 mm for EI 30 specimens and 32 mm for EI 60 specimens, whereas the corresponding values at the 10th minute were 15 mm and 7 mm. This finding indicated that the peak deformation of the tested doors was not captured by the conventional first measurement point recommended in EN 1363-155 (10th minute for EI 30 and EI 60). This is a critical observation for testing methodology, as it suggested that the most severe structural challenge occured during the initial heating phase, potentially going unrecorded in standard tests. This phenomenon was consistent with previous findings on aluminium structures54,62, where the high thermal conductivity of aluminium leads to rapid heating and loss of strength before the furnace temperature has even stabilized. Tests performed under this study revealed the most considerable displacement occurring within the first five minutes of the test. In contrast, the displacement value in minute 10 of the test was far lower.

Study limitations and transferability of results

It is crucial to emphasize that these conclusions were derived from tests on a single, specific product: a fully glazed, single-leaf aluminium-profiled door with a particular design of profiles, seals, and hardware. The robustness of the aluminium profile system likely contributed to the stability of the main gap dimensions. Therefore, the results – particularly the outstanding stability of fire resistance after up to 1 million cycles – should not be directly and unconditionally extrapolated to other fire door designs without further validation.

However, the fundamental degradation mechanisms identified, such as the wear of soft seals, micro-movements in hardware, and the resulting disproportionate increase in air leakage are universal. It is therefore reasonable to expect that other door types (e.g., steel or timber) would also experience a degradation in airtightness/smoke control under similar mechanical cycling. The key uncertainty lies in the rate of this degradation and the potential point of failure for fire resistance, which could differ significantly based on materials, construction, and hardware. For instance, steel doors might be more prone to permanent deformation, while wooden doors could suffer from compression and wear in different ways.

Furthermore, due to the limited sample size (one specimen per test group), statistical analysis could not be performed. Therefore, any observations about trends or the absence of significant effects should be interpreted with caution. The results should be considered preliminary, highlighting tendencies rather than providing statistically confirmed evidence. Future studies with larger sample sets are necessary to validate the observed patterns.

Conclusion

This study involved testing the fire resistance of twelve samples of glazed aluminium-profiled doors. Ten of them were subjected to numerous opening-closing cycles. For some samples, air permeability was verified before the tests and after a specific number of cycles. Based on the test results, the following conclusions can be drawn:

For regulatory practice and maintenance schedules, these results underscore that a fire door’s compliance with its fire resistance (EI) rating after years of service cannot be used as an indicator of its preserved smoke control capability. The inspection criteria and intervals, particularly for doors in high-traffic areas, should account for mechanical usage to ensure both safety functions are maintained.
