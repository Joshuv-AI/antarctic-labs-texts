Orchestrating multi-state QTL analysis with Bioconductor
Authors: Christina B. Del Azodi, Amelia M. Dunstone, Davis J. McCarthy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe scope of many Quantitative Trait Locus (QTL) mapping studies has increased to include different cellular and environmental states. However, drawing biologically relevant conclusions from the large, high-dimensional data that come from multi-state QTL mapping studies is not straightforward.ResultsTo address this problem, we introduce two R packages, QTLExperiment and multistateQTL. The QTLExperiment package provides a robust container for storing and manipulating QTL summary statistics and associated metadata. Building upon existing Bioconductor infrastructure and conventions, this object class is consistent, user-friendly, and well-documented. The multistateQTL package introduces tools to facilitate the analysis of multi-state QTL data stored in a QTLExperiment container. This package provides methods for statistical analysis, quantification of sharing, classification of multi-state QTL associations, visualization of the data, and more. It also provides flexible methods for simulating multi-state QTL summary statistics with user-defined properties. The packages QTLExperiment and multistateQTL are open-source and are freely available on Bioconductor (https://www.bioconductor.org/packages/QTLExperiment and https://bioconductor.org/packages/multistateQTL).ConclusionsThe packages QTLExperiment and multistateQTL provide an intuitive and convenient workflow for downstream analysis of QTL summary statistics from multiple states. Together these tools can enable researchers to better understand how gene regulation differs across states, as well as discover QTLs that are unique to a subset of states. Multi-state QTL analysis can lead to the discovery of disease-relevant QTLs which may otherwise be masked in bulk QTL studies, highlighting the importance of developing open-source software tools for this purpose.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12859-026-06472-6.

Background

Quantitative Trait Locus (QTL) mapping studies test for associations between genetic variants such as single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and changes in a molecular trait like gene expression (eQTL) or chromatin accessibility (caQTL). These associations provide insights into the regulatory basis of traits. As sequencing technologies advance, the size and scope of QTL mapping studies continue to grow, particularly with the increasing adoption of assays that quantify molecular traits at single-cell resolution. A major area of growth has been in expanding the cellular contexts in which QTL are assessed, for example in different tissues [1, 2], in specific cell-types [3–6], or during specific developmental stages [7]. QTL studies have also expanded to consider environmental [8, 9] and population [10, 11] contexts. We refer to studies that test for QTL associations across more than one of these contexts as multi-state QTL studies.

There are many established frameworks for performing QTL mapping and a growing number of tools for the joint analysis of multi-state QTL [12–14]. The output from these tools is a set of summary statistics for each association in each state, including the estimated effect size, the variance or standard error around that estimate, and often a test-statistic describing the significance of the association (e.g., false discovery rate, FDR, or local false sign rates, LFSRs [15]). This big, high-dimensional data presents a number of challenges, including how to describe global patterns of sharing and how to identify groups of related associations, and specific associations that may be of biological interest. To help facilitate these goals, here we introduce two packages: QTLExperiment: a container for storing and manipulating QTL summary statistics and associated metadata; and multistateQTL: a toolkit for simulating, analyzing, and visualizing multi-state QTL summary statistics.

The QTLExperiment container is designed to store important metadata along with the summary statistics to allow for robust data merging and subsetting, with checks in place to reduce the potential for labeling mistakes. The multistateQTL package builds on QTLExperiment to aid users in the analyses of complex QTL datasets.

These packages are intended to help the community: feedback or feature requests are appreciated and can be submitted on GitLab (https://gitlab.svi.edu.au/biocellgen-public/qtlexperiment/-/issues; https://gitlab.svi.edu.au/biocellgen-public/multistateqtl/-/issues).

Implementation

The QTLExperiment and multistateQTL packages are open-source R packages. Both packages take advantage of cutting-edge R programming tools to ensure speed and memory efficiency. The sumstats2qtle() function can load over 20 million beta, error, and p-value summary statistics from 10 states in 10 min (10.4 ± 0.42 min, n = 5). This scale of data is equivalent to unfiltered results from chromosome 1 or whole-genome cis-eQTLs within 100 kb of the gene TSS. Once loaded into a QTLExperiment object, calling significant tests takes 3 s (2.59 ± 0.32 s, n = 10), filtering to keep only the top hits per feature takes 18 s (18.00 ± 0.91 s, n = 10), and calculating and plotting pairwise sharing on those top hits takes less than a second (0.18 ± 0.11 s). These experiments were performed on an HPC cluster using 2 logical CPUs (AMD EPYC 9534 64-Core Processor), 128GB RAM and HDD storage. The software used included R version 4.5.1, Bioconductor version 3.22, and OS version Rocky Linux 9.6 (Blue Onyx).

The QTLExperiment class is a child class of SummarizedExperiment [16] from Bioconductor. This enables QTLExperiment to leverage the efficient design of SummarizedExperiment objects, particularly with respect to storing large matrices in R. Since the object will typically have betas, errors and p-values/LFSRs for the same association tests, this results in rectangular data where the dimensions match for each assay. For this reason, the object matches the assumptions of SummarizedExperiment, and it was not necessary to use the more flexible (and therefore complicated) object MultiAssayExperiment [17].

The QTLExperiment and multistateQTL packages utilize existing CRAN and Bioconductor packages for efficient data manipulation and plotting. For example, vroom [18] is used for loading data with parallel processing when multiple cores are available and collapse [19], which uses C/C + + based functions, is used for intensive data transformations. The plotting functions use ggplot2 [20] and ComplexHeatmap [21].

The QTLExperiment container

The QTLExperiment container is a lightweight, S4 class object for storing summary statistics and metadata from QTL mapping workflows. It extends the SummarizedExperiment class and is organized such that rows represent QTL associations between a feature and a variant, columns represent states, and assays contain various summary statistics (Fig. 1a). Features can refer to genes, open chromatin regions (peaks), or other molecular phenotypes.

The QTLExperiment class inherits the slots rowData, colData and assays from the parent class SummarizedExperiment, with some additional requirements on the slots enabling the storage of multi-state QTL data. The class has two required assays: “betas”, for storing the estimated QTL effect sizes; and “errors”, for the quantities characterising the uncertainty (typically standard errors) around each effect size estimate. Any number of additional assays (e.g., test-statistics, known/simulated betas) can be stored. The rowData stores metadata about each QTL association. The two required rowData columns are feature_id and variant_id, which store the feature names (e.g., gene names or chromatin regions) and the variant information (i.e., SNP names), respectively. Each row is required to have a unique combination of these two components. The colData stores metadata related to the states and has one required column, state_id. The state ID names must be unique. Additional metadata that needs to be stored with the instance, but does not directly relate to associations or states, for example covariance matrices used for QTL mapping, can be stored in the metadata list.

The QTLExperiment object stores data in a robust fashion, keeping metadata organized and special fields protected when the object is manipulated. Due to the importance of the feature_id, variant_id, and state_id information to the object, there are several protective measures in place to maintain the validity of this data. When updating these fields, the methods in QTLExperiment ensure that the new values provided are sensible (e.g., new values should have a one-to-one mapping with the old values). In addition, overwriting the colData or rowData of a QTLExperiment object will not delete data from the protected fields. The package also includes specific functions for the protected fields, namely state_id(), feature_id() and variant_id(), which provide a shorthand for viewing or changing these fields in the row or column metadata. The package includes methods for subsetting the object, as well as for binding rows and columns. Together these features make the QTLExperiment object user-friendly while also preserving important properties about the data.

Finally, the package contains a suite of functions to convert user data into a QTLExperiment object. This includes a function to read in QTL summary statistics from separate files for each state. This function is flexible, allowing the user to provide QTL summary statistics as output from a range of common QTL mapping software tools or data resources (e.g., FastQTL [22], LIMIX [23], tensorQTL [24], GTEx [2], etc.), by specifying where key data is stored in those files. It also includes specific functions to read in joint multi-state QTL summary statistics from common intermediate tools (e.g., mashr [13]).

The multistateQTL toolkit

The multistateQTL package provides a wealth of tools to assist with analyzing multi-state QTL summary statistics. It includes functions for filtering and imputing missing assay data, calling significance, calculating pairwise sharing across states, selecting top QTL hits, and categorizing QTL by their pattern across states (Fig. 1).

Results

Here we demonstrate the functionality available in the QTLExperiment and the multistateQTL packages using gene expression data from GTEx [2], applying the typical steps performed in a multi-state QTL workflow (Fig. 1b). This analysis includes the 10 tissue types with the largest sample sizes in GTEx version 8, specifically: Adipose - Subcutaneous, Artery - Tibial, Esophagus - Mucosa, Lung, Muscle - Skeletal, Nerve - Tibial, Skin - Not Sun Exposed (Suprapubic), Skin - Sun Exposed (Lower leg), Thyroid and Whole Blood.

The data includes whole-genome cis-eQTLs (SNPs within 1 Mb upstream and downstream of the gene transcription start site). We used the sumstats2qtle() function to create a QTLExperiment object from the filtered GTEx summary statistics. As the raw data files have a total size of 156GB on disk, we used 478GB RAM and 4 CPUs for the loading step of this analysis. This object has assays for the betas (effect sizes), errors (standard error of the effect sizes), and p-values (nominal p-values) from association testing. We also performed an analysis of summary statistics from chromosome 1 only, to demonstrate the results possible with fewer computational resources.

Dealing with missing data

Some common downstream analysis steps cannot handle missing data in the QTLExperiment object, specifically in the betas and errors assays. Thus, we developed methods to filter and/or impute missing data. The filtering function getComplete() allows the user to specify a minimum number of states with complete data for the QTL to be retained. The imputation function replaceNAs() allows the user to specify for each assay how the NAs should be replaced. This flexible approach could be used to assign NA effect sizes a value of zero, NA p-values a value of one, and NA standard errors the row (i.e., test) median values. We used the getComplete() function to remove ~ 45.1 million QTL tests that had missing data in one or more of the 10 GTEx tissues, leaving ~ 125.9 million QTL for the downstream analysis. This QTLExperiment object takes up about 51GB of memory in RStudio, as determined by the lobstr package [25].

Calling significant associations

QTL tests can be called as significant using either a one-step or two-step threshold approach using the callSignificance() function. There are also inbuilt methods to perform multiple testing correction within each state, with options including FDR or Bonferroni [26] correction. The one-step threshold approach simply calls QTL as significant in a given state if the test statistic specified by the user is below a given threshold (e.g., LFSR < 0.05). The two-step threshold approach allows for a more lenient threshold for calling significance in additional states if the QTL is significant at the stricter threshold in at least one state. This more flexible approach can help minimize the impact of differential power across states, which commonly arises when different states (e.g., tissue, cell type) have different sample sizes. For our GTEx analysis, we applied the two-step threshold approach (p-values < 0.01 for the first threshold, p-values < 0.05 for the second).

The two-step threshold results in more global and multi-state effects, by allowing some states to be significant at a higher (but still reasonable) threshold. For instance, using the two-step threshold with 0.01 and 0.05, if 3 states have p-values below 0.01 and the remaining states have values between 0.01 and 0.05, this will be considered a global effect with significance in every state. The use of different significance thresholds for different tests is similar to the p-value comparisons performed when determining significance using the Benjamini–Hochberg method (False Discovery Rate, FDR) [26]. When determining significance using the FDR, p-values are sorted in ascending order and each value is compared with a different threshold, until a non-significant test is found (a test that is above its threshold). The key difference is that the two-step threshold in multistateQTL is applied to relatively few tests, as n is the number of states, and not the number of association tests (the combination of feature IDs and variant IDs). In a later Sect. “Two-step threshold for calling significance” we investigate the FDR of the two-step method with different sample size distributions.

Summarizing results by feature

In single-state QTL mapping, results are often filtered to only include the top QTL per feature (e.g., the top eSNP per gene). However, in multi-state QTL analysis this filtering is complicated because the top QTL may be different in different states for reasons either biological (e.g., lead-switching) or technical (e.g., linkage disequilibrium, LD, between eSNPs). This complication can be avoided by summarizing multi-state QTL results on the feature level. The runSignificantFeature() function adds a summary of the features with significant QTL in each state to the metadata of the QTLExperiment object for downstream analysis. In addition, the getTopHits() function can be used to select the top QTL per feature either globally, where the test with the lowest test statistic per feature across all states is selected, or at the state-level, where the tests with the lowest test statistic per feature per state (in states where the feature is significant) are selected. For our GTEx analysis, filtering to keep the top hits per gene per state resulted in 143,986 QTL used for the downstream analysis.

Calculating pairwise sharing

To quantify the degree of pairwise QTL sharing between states, we implement the classification approach used in mashr [13] in our runPairwiseSharing() function. In this approach, a QTL is considered shared if it is significant in either state and the effect sizes are within a factor of each other. In the mashr paper, Urbut et al. define effects to be shared between tissues if the effects have the same sign and are within a factor of 2 in size. In the runPairwiseSharing() function, the factor argument is specified as a number between 0 and 1. The default value of 0.5 is equivalent to the ‘factor of 2’ used in the mashr paper, as the comparison is performed as a ratio. Users can increase or decrease this factor to require higher or lower similarity between effect sizes, respectively. At the extreme ends, setting the factor to zero tests for a shared direction regardless of magnitude (Supplementary Fig. 5), while setting the function parameter to the absolute value (FUN = abs) tests for shared magnitude regardless of direction. For our GTEx analysis, we calculated the pairwise sharing on the top QTL hits using the default approach and then plotted these results using the plotPairwiseSharing() function. Without any multi-tissue integration being applied, we found between 31% and 68% of QTL were shared between tissue pairs, with similar tissues (e.g., “skin - sun exposed” and “skin - not sun exposed”) having the most sharing (Fig. 2a).

We introduce standard definitions for categories of multi-state QTL based on their pattern of sharing and effect direction across states. The broad categories describe QTL as “global” if they are significant across all states, “multi-state” if they are significant across only a subset of states, and “unique” if they are specific to a single state. Global and multi-state QTL are further categorized by the pattern of the effect sizes in states where the QTL is significant. If the QTL has the same effect direction in all significant states, it is considered “shared”. If the QTL has significant effects in the opposite direction in at least one state, it is considered “diverging”. After calling significant tests, the runTestMetrics() function can be applied to categorize QTL using these definitions. It also calculates the variance in effect sizes across states where the QTL is significant and reports the number of significant states for each QTL. For our GTEx analysis, after applying runTestMetrics(), we visualized and quantified these multi-state QTL categories in two similar (“skin - sun exposed” and “skin - not sun exposed”) and two very different (“skin - sun” and whole blood) tissues using the plotCompareStates() function (Fig. 2b). For the two skin tissues, most of the significant QTL are shared (53,283), and there are very few diverging QTL (169). In contrast, there are more diverging QTL (4,430) when comparing “skin - sun exposed” with whole blood tissue. The results are similar in the analysis of chromosome 1 SNPs, but roughly 10 times smaller. For the two skin tissues, there are 5118 shared QTL, and 16 diverging QTL. There are 442 diverging QTL between the “skin - sun exposed” tissue and whole blood (Supplementary Fig. 6b).

Analysis of other molecular phenotypes. While the GTEx analysis demonstrates the use of QTLExperiment and multistateQTL with an eQTL bulk data set, the S4 class also supports other molecular phenotypes in the feature_id slot. We next describe an analysis of single-nuclei ATAC-seq data from Benaglio et al. [27]. This study includes peripheral blood mononuclear cells from 13 individuals. The summary statistics including effect sizes and p-values were downloaded from Zenodo (10.5281/zenodo.7375095) and used under its CC BY 4.0 licence. We loaded in data for 15 cell-types, including subtypes of B cells, NK cells, monocytes, megakaryocytes and T cells. In comparison to the GTEx analysis, the state_id() now represents cell types instead of tissue types, and the feature_id() now stores chromatin sites instead of genes. The chromosome, as well as the starting and ending coordinates of the chromatin sites are stored in the rowData() of the QTLExperiment object.

We analysed the data according to the standard multi-state QTL workflow (Fig. 1b). Rows of association tests were removed using the getComplete() function if there were NA values in one or more cell-types. We then used the replaceNAs() function to impute any remaining missing values according to the default parameters. Significant tests were identified using the same two-step threshold used for the GTEx analysis, i.e., p-values < 0.01 for the first threshold, p-values < 0.05 for the second threshold. At this stage, five cell-types were removed as they had no significant features. These cell-types included memory B-cells, adaptive NK cells, conventional dendritic cells, megakaryocytes and regulatory CD4 + T-cells. For the remaining 10 cell-types, top hits were obtained by taking the most significant SNP for each chromatin site and cell-type. Using a factor of 0.5 in the runPairwiseSharing() function, we found that between 28% and 92% of caQTLs were shared between the cell-types (Fig. 3a). The runPairwiseSharing() function was used to classify caQTLs as multi-state, unique, or global effects. We identified 111 multi-state shared effects and 283 unique effects (Fig. 3b).

Two-step threshold for calling significance

The multistateQTL package has the option to determine significant tests using a simple approach, a multiple-testing approach, or a two-step approach. To investigate the false discovery rate when using the two-step threshold we performed an analysis of the results of a single-cell eQTL mapping study of healthy and diseased lung tissue [6]. We chose the 10 cell-types with the largest number of donors from this paper, which included Monocyte-derived_macrophage, Inflammatory_monocyte, AT2, Ciliated, moDC, Monocyte, cDC2, Proliferating_-_Imm, Arteriole, Secretory_-_SCGB1A1+_MUC5B+. Each cell-type was present in at least 94 donors. The cell-types can be grouped into three broad lineages: Immune, Epithelial, and Endothelial cells (Supplementary Fig. 1a).

We obtained a null dataset by, for each feature, permuting the values of the pseudo-bulk expression data (Supplementary Fig. 1b). Next we defined five experimental groups, where the groups have different levels of inequality in the sample sizes across the states (Supplementary Fig. 2a). We used the Gini coefficient to measure the degree of inequality in each group. The unbiased Gini coefficient takes values from 0 (no inequality, all values are equal), to 1 (a large degree of inequality). Group 1 has completely equal sample sizes, with 77 donors in each group, and a Gini coefficient of 0. In contrast, Group 5 has the most unequal sample sizes, with sample sizes ranging from 41 in the smallest state to 113 donors in the largest (Gini coefficient: 0.19). Notably, each group has a total sample size of 770, so that the overall sample size is controlled across the groups. We also ensured that each state had at least 40 donors, so there was nominally sufficient power to perform QTL mapping.

The permutation null data were downsampled to the desired number of donors for each state. QTL mapping was performed using tensorQTL [24] for each group and state independently. The standard workflow for multi-state QTL data (Fig. 1b) was followed to load in the summary statistics, remove rows with incomplete data, and call significance. We used the two-step threshold with 0.01 as the first threshold and 0.05 as the second threshold to identify significant tests. We replaced the state names with numbers S1 through to S10, where S1 is the state with the largest number of donors, and S10 has the smallest number of donors.

The proportion of discoveries in the data can be thought of as the FDR, as any discoveries in the empirical null data must be false discoveries. The results show that the false discovery rate is controlled for all groups below the second threshold of 0.05, and is only slightly above the lower threshold of 0.01 (minimum value: 0.0137, maximum value: 0.0139, Supplementary Fig. 2b). As the groups represent different degrees of unequal sample sizes, this result indicates that the two-step threshold controls the FDR when sample sizes are unequal, provided the sample sizes are at least 40 in each group. The figures also show a trend of more false discoveries for the states with fewer donors (Supplementary Fig. 2c).

As QTL mapping was performed independently for each state, we also looked at the FDR control after using the multi-state correction method mashr (version 0.2.79). This method uses a two-step empirical Bayes procedure to estimate between-tissue sharing and context specificity of eQTLs. We followed the method outlined in the ‘eQTL analysis outline’ vignette (https://stephenslab.github.io/mashr/articles/eQTL_outline.html), but modified the workflow to make use of QTLExperiment functions. The posterior effect sizes, posterior standard errors, and LFSRs were loaded into a QTLExperiment object (Supplementary Fig. 3). We then applied the two-step threshold to identify significant tests (LFSR < 0.01 for the first threshold, LFSR < 0.05 for the second threshold). The FDR is lower when using LFSRs, with proportions below 0.00002 for all groups (Supplementary Fig. 4b). As with the analysis involving p-values, there is a trend where the states with fewer states have higher rates of false discoveries (Supplementary Fig. 4c).

Simulation tools

The multistateQTL toolkit provides a framework to simulate realistic multi-state QTL summary statistics. The simulation framework consists of two parts: first, the estimation of key parameters from empirical QTL summary statistics; and second, the sampling of QTL summary statistics from these parameters given the user defined structure.

The default parameters available in multistateQTL were estimated from eSNP: eGene pairs tested on chromosome 1 in GTEx (v8) for the 10 tissues with the largest sample sizes. We also estimated the parameters using the whole-genome cis1mb data (Supplementary Fig. 7). Users can estimate parameters from their own empirical summary statistic data to simulate datasets with desired characteristics. The shape and rate parameters define gamma distributions for the following components: the mean absolute value of effect sizes across states for significant QTL tests (betas.sig), the coefficient of variation (CV) of significant effect sizes across states (cv.sig), the mean absolute value of effect sizes across all null tests (betas.null), and the CV of null effect sizes across all null tests (cv.null). Gamma distributions provide a good fit for these quantities, and simulated data demonstrates distributional characteristics highly concordant with real data (Supplementary Fig. 8).

The estimated parameters from the whole-genome data and the chromosome 1 data were highly correlated (r = 0.998). The null distributions were estimated to be gamma(2.136, 13.890) for the genome-wide data and gamma(2.151, 14.199) for chromosome 1. Similarly, the significant effects distribution were gamma(1.056, 22.913) and gamma(1.056, 22.638) for the whole-genome analysis and chromosome 1 respectively (Supplementary Table 1).

The simulation tool allows for the simulation of four types of associations: (1) Global, where the simulated effect size is approximately equal across all states; (2) Unique, where the association is only significant in one state; (3) Multi-state, where the association is significant in a subset of states (i.e., state-groups), and (4) Null, where the association has no significant effects in any state. First, each test is randomly assigned as one of the above types according to the proportions specified by the user. For multi-state QTL, each state is assigned to a state-group, either randomly or according to user-defined groups, then each multi-state QTL is assigned randomly to one of the state-groups. For unique QTL, the QTL is randomly assigned to a single state.

Simulated mean effect sizes for all non-null QTL are sampled from gamma(beta.sig.shape, beta.sig.rate) distributions and are randomly assigned a positive or negative effect direction. Then for each state where that QTL is significant, an effect size is sampled from N(mean effect size, σ), where σ is user defined (default = 0.1). Effect sizes for null QTL are sampled from gamma(beta.null.shape, beta.null.rate) and are randomly assigned a positive or negative effect direction. Standard errors for each QTL for each state are simulated by sampling from gamma(cv.sig.shape, cv.sig.rate) or gamma(cv.null.shape, cv.null.rate) for significant and null QTL, respectively, and multiplying the sampled CV by the absolute value of the simulated beta for that QTL in that state.

Using this simulation framework, multi-state QTL summary statistics can be simulated to represent different real-world scenarios. For example, we can simulate a simple scenario where most QTL are shared (Fig. 4a). Here we consider simulated QTL as shared if they are significant in either state and have simulated betas within a factor of 0.75. States were called as significant using the two-step threshold approach, with one state having an LFSR < 0.1 and the remaining states having an LFSR < 0.2. We can also specify different σ to be used for each state, allowing us to simulate a scenario where there is more variability in effect sizes in some states (e.g., S5: σ = 1, and S6: σ = 2; Fig. 4b), which could happen if those states have fewer data or lower quality data available for QTL mapping. Finally, we can simulate scenarios with structure between states (e.g., two state-groups in Fig. 4c), which would be expected, for example, if we were looking at QTL across different subgroups of tissues. Plotting the number of simulated QTL that are significant in different sets of states provides a more detailed look at the structure of QTL sharing within our simulation (Fig. 4d). Finally, plotting and clustering the multi-state QTL by their sampled betas provides a detailed look at the properties of specific QTL (Fig. 4e). Note that the heatmap and UpSet plotting functions allow for state and test level variables stored in the QTLExperiment colData and rowData, respectively, to be used to annotate the plots with contextual information (e.g., state-groups, number of significant QTL per state, and simulated mean beta; Fig. 4).

Discussion

We have demonstrated the use of QTLExperiment and multistateQTL for the analysis of eQTL and caQTL mapping results, from whole-genome GTEx data and sn-ATAC seq data, respectively. Our results show the versatility of the object class, easily performing multi-state analyses of summary statistics from different assays. The state_id() slot was used to store tissue types in the GTEx analysis, and in the caQTL analysis it stored cell-types. Other interpretations of state IDs are also compatible with the object.

While the QTLExperiment object is designed to be used with eQTL summary statistics, it also supports a variety of other summary statistics, provided the results include an effect size and a measure of uncertainty. It can be particularly useful when the results have been obtained in different states, for instance GWAS or TWAS [28], where the data have been obtained independently for multiple tissues or cell-types. The QTLExperiment package can then be used in conjunction with mashr or another multi-state integration method. While feature ID typically refers to a molecular phenotype, such as gene expression, chromatin accessibility or histone acetylation, it can also support whole-body phenotypes such as weight, BMI, age, or disease status. Similarly, the variant ID, while typically used for a SNP, in the case for TWAS, could be used for the phenotype of interest (as TWAS looks at the association between a whole body phenotype and predicted gene expression). The feature ID and variant ID are flexible slots and can alternatively be thought of as the dependent and independent variable of the regression model or association tests that were used to obtain the summary statistics.

Though QTLExperiment and multistateQTL provide several improvements to multi-state QTL workflows, there are a number of limitations that could be addressed in future versions. Some of these limitations include the speed of operations and the RAM requirement. Increasing RAM can help to speed up the operations: for an object with 20 million rows, the data loading step takes 10 min if there is 256GB of RAM, but takes 15 min if using 64GB of RAM. In the whole genome analysis with 120 million rows, we used 478 GB of RAM, but users with fewer resources would need to perform operations in chunks. Another way to speed up the package in a future version could be the use of compiled C or C + + code for time-intensive operations. There is also room for new features to be added to improve the user-experience, for instance new customisation options to figures, and we welcome suggestions from the community.

An area that complicates QTL analysis is the determination of causal SNPs. In the getTopHits() function, we filter to one SNP per feature by taking the most significant SNP, similar to the method used in GTEx. More sophisticated methods are available to identify the causal SNP, for instance the R tool CausalEQTL [29] or fine-mapping [30]. Such methods could be used in combination with QTLExperiment and multistateQTL as an alternative way to select rows of interest and as ways to deepen the understanding of likely causal effects in a given study.

Conclusions

The QTLExperiment and multistateQTL R packages provide a user-friendly approach to loading, storing, manipulating, and analyzing multi-state QTL association summary statistics and provide tools for visualizing these data. Together these tools can be used to facilitate the analysis of high-dimensional multi-state QTL data to describe global patterns of gene regulation across states and highlight specific QTL and clusters of QTL that may be of particular biological interest. As the scope of QTL mapping studies grows to include more and higher resolution cellular and environmental contexts, software tools that facilitate the management and standardization of QTL data and workflows will become critical.
