Abstract
. Root Cause Analysis (RCA) is a critical tool for investigating adverse events in healthcare and improving patient safety. However, existing RCA training programs are often limited by high resource demands, leading to insufficient training and inconsistent implementation. To address this challenge, we present an AI-powered 3D simulation game that helps healthcare professionals develop RCA skills through interactive, immersive simulations. This approach offers a cost-effective, scalable, and accessible alternative to traditional training. The prototype simulates an RCA investigation following a death in the ICU, where learners interview five virtual avatars representing ICU team members to investigate the incident and complete a written report. The system enables natural, life-like interactions with avatars via large language models (LLMs), emotional text-to-speech, and AI-powered animations. An additional LLM component provides formative and summative feedback to support continual improvement. We conclude by outlining plans to empirically evaluate the system’s efficacy.

2.
Related Work

2.1.
Root Cause Analysis in Healthcare

Root Cause Analysis (RCA) emerged in the 1990s as part of the broader quality improvement movement in healthcare, when professionals began recognizing that hospitals were becoming increasingly hazardous environments for patients
(
Emanuel et al. 2009
)
.
RCA was identified by the Institute of Medicine as a key analytic tool for investigating adverse events and preventing error recurrence
(
Havens and Boroughs 2000
)
. The Joint Commission began requiring its accredited hospitals to conduct RCAs following sentinel events in the 1990s
(
Sherwin 2011
)
. RCA has since become widely adopted in both clinical and administrative domains, supported by oversight organizations such as the Institute for Healthcare Improvement
(
for Healthcare Improvement 2015
)
and the Veterans Health Administration
(
Bagian et al. 2001
;
U.S. Department of Veterans Affairs 2025
)
.

RCA’s value lies in its ability to uncover root causes that may not be apparent through surface-level investigation. It promotes honest communication by avoiding individual blame and focusing instead on structural and process improvements
(
The Joint Commission 2025
;
Iedema et al. 2006
)
.
The approach has proven effective both in U.S. and internationally. Within the U.S. Veterans Health Administration, RCA was central to a successful patient safety program
(
Bagian et al. 2001
)
. In Japan, for example, RCA was also seen as a foundational tool for promoting safer care
(
Wocher 2015
)
. Scholars and professional associations also support RCA’s value, noting its power to drive culture change and enhance organizational communication
(
Wachter 2010
;
Vincent 2003
)
.

Despite its widespread adoption, the effectiveness of RCA in practice has often been limited by inconsistent execution. Studies have found that many RCA teams lack sufficient training, with personnel often unfamiliar with RCA methodology or lacking the skills needed to conduct thorough investigations
(
Kung et al. 2016
;
Lambton and Mahlmeister 2010
)
. Prior studies have piloted short, simulation-based or case-based interventions for radiology residents, nursing students, and other clinicians
(
Kung et al. 2016
;
Lambton and Mahlmeister 2010
;
Aboumrad et al. 2019
)
.
Notwithstanding these efforts, RCA training remains inadequate in many settings, largely due to limitations such as high implementation costs, time constraints, and limited accessibility. This underscores the potential for AI-powered, simulation-based platforms to provide scalable, cost-effective, and accessible RCA training for healthcare professionals.

Prototype Overview

This prototype, building on the foundation of
Nurse Town

(
Hu et al. 2025
)
, is a Unity-based 3D simulation game that aims to facilitate RCA training among healthcare professionals. The prototype supports simulation of a textbook scenario, where learners conduct RCAs on an adverse incident in the ICU
(
Campbell and Daley 2017
)
. We integrated LLMs, emotional text-to-speech (TTS), and generative animations to create life-like virtual avatars. LLM is also used to assess user performance. GPT-4o
(
OpenAI 2024
)
was selected for its demonstrated capabilities in linguistic and reasoning tasks
(
Bokken et al. 2006
)
.

Learners begin by reviewing the background description of the ICU incident. They are then asked to conduct interviews with the five ICU team members, each offering different perspectives. After all interviews, learners will complete a written RCA report. An LLM component will provide feedback on the report as well as learners’ interview strategies and techniques. Figure

3.1.
Simulation Scenario: ICU Failure Case Study

The prototype features a textbook simulation scenario, where a patient died due to communication failures in the ICU team
(
Campbell and Daley 2017
)
. A complete narrative of this incident is provided in Appendix A.1.
Learners are tasked to conduct an RCA on this incident. Through interviews with the ICU team members, learners will investigate, analyze, and report on the case’s direct causes (e.g., misinterpretation of wristband color), root causes (e.g., lack of standardized protocols, shift hours that leads to fatigue), and action recommendations (e.g, improved patient admission procedures).
Five virtual characters are available to be interviewed. Each character, from the primary nurse to the anesthesiologist, offers a unique perspective based on their roles and experiences. Detailed descriptions of the characters are shown in Appendix A.2.

3.2.
LLM-Based Virtual Avatars

The prototype includes LLM-powered virtual avatars that represent the five ICU team members involved in the incident (Table A1). Each avatar is designed to mimic real healthcare professionals, capable of engaging in fluid, context-sensitive conversations. We developed the system instruction prompts through iterative testing and refinement to ensure that the conversations feel authentic and are consistent with the avatars’ roles and experiences. A sample interview with the primary nurse is included in Appendix B.

To further enhance realism, each avatar is assigned random states-of-mind (e.g., defensive, confuse, frustrated, etc.) at the start of the interviews. The avatars will respond in different ways accordingly, instead of giving plain factual answers. For example, a "defensive" interviewee may tend to avoid blame and shift responsibility while a "self-reflective" one will openly acknowledge mistakes. Descriptions of all five states-of-mind and comparisons of communication styles can be found in Appendix A.3. Learners need to adapt their strategies to uncover the full picture. In addition, the randomness in interview experience mitigates boredom and facilitates continued learning.

3.3.
Emotional Text-to-Speech

The system has a text-to-speech component that convert the LLM-generated text responses to life-like speeches.
To facilitate communication, we required the voices to be able to convey nuanced emotional states such as fatigue, hesitation, and urgency.
However, these traits are often missing in conventional TTS systems, including OpenAI’s Whisper
(
OpenAI 2022
)
which
Nurse Town
uses.
We ultimately adopted ElevenLabs’ voice design and cloning tools
(
ElevenLabs 2025
)
, which allowed us to define vocal traits tailored for each character and generate highly specific, context-appropriate voices. A detailed description of the voice designs is included in Appendix C. These synthesized voices were integrated via API into our system, enabling dynamic emotional responses that align with character roles and state-of-minds.

3.4.
Real-time Facial Expression and Lip Sync

To further enhance realism, we used NVIDIA’s Audio2Face
(
NVIDIA 2025
)
for facial animations and lip sync of the virtual avatars. Audio2Face is an AI-driven tool that produces blendshape animations based on audio input, capturing both accurate mouth movements and subtle emotional expressions. It offers several pre-trained facial models, from which we selected the most appropriate for each avatar. This component generated time-stamped blendshape data (e.g., jaw, lips, eyes, brows) along with emotion metrics representing the intensity of various affective states (e.g., joy, sadness, fear) from streamed audio. These were mapped to our Unity character models to enable dynamic and expressive facial animations as the avatars speak.

3.6.
LLM-Based Assessment of Student Performance

The LLM-based assessment system offers a dual-layer evaluation process. In the formative assessment, the learner’s performance is continuously monitored through their interactions with the virtual avatars. As they conduct interviews, the system analyzes the quality of their questions, their ability to follow up on vague or inconsistent responses, and their skill in identifying key issues during the conversation. When all interviews are completed, the LLM will provide formative feedback on their interview strategies and communications skills to help students refine their approaches.

For the summative assessment, learners will be provided with an RCA template (Appendix D) that we developed according to Center of Medicare and Medicaid Services’ official guidance
(
Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services 2013
)
. They are required to complete and upload a comprehensive report based on the ICU failure scenario. The submission is then examined by LLM on articulation of the problem, identification of direct and root cause, and action recommendation. Overall structure and clarity of the report are also considered. These assessment provide feedback that help learners develop and strengthen their interviewing techniques and their skills to analyze and synthesize complex information to form a structured, well-argued RCA report.
Full rubrics and sample assessment reports are included in Appendix E and Appendix F.

4.
Discussion

This work illustrates how AI technologies can be combined to facilitate RCA training for healthcare professionals. By integrating LLMs, emotional TTS, and AI-powered animations, the system delivers an interactive and immersive learning experience. Coupled with automated LLM-based assessments, it enables learners to practice essential RCA skills and receive personalized feedback, with minimal requirements for resources compared to traditional training.

Despite its strengths, limitations of the system should be acknowledged. While the system simulates a realistic RCA process, it may not capture the full complexity of in-person team dynamics, particularly non-verbal cues, interprofessional tensions, or organizational politics that can influence real RCA meetings. Additionally, while emotional variability is introduced via predefined states of mind, these do not yet account for dynamic changes in emotional response during the course of an interview. Lastly, the simulation currently focuses on a single case scenario. Broader generalizability will require the development of additional modules covering a wider range of adverse events and system failures.

5.
Conclusion and Future Work

This proof-of-concept demonstrates the feasibility and potential impact of using AI-powered simulations to train healthcare professionals on RCA. The system provides an accessible environment where learners can develop investigative, communication and analytical skills necessary for effective RCA, without the logistical constraints of in-person workshops and simulation labs. Our results highlight the potential of this approach in better supporting individual learning and professional development and contributing to broader organizational goals of improving care quality.

We acknowledge that this system has not yet been empirically evaluated. Therefore, we plan to conduct user studies
to assess the usability and effectiveness of the system.
First, a formative evaluation will gather qualitative feedback on the system’s design and implementation from target users through usability studies. These insights will guide iterative refinements to optimize learner experience. Then, we will compare our approach to traditional RCA training in a controlled experimental study. Participants will complete pre- and post-training assessments measuring knowledge, confidence, and skills in RCA. Statistical analyses will compare within- and between-subjects changes in key measures. Future work will also expand the scenario library, support multi-user collaboration, and explore dynamic emotional responses from avatars to deepen engagement and empathy-building.

Appendix A
The ICU Case Scenario

A.1.
A Narrative of the Incident

A patient was admitted to the unit for IV antibiotic treatment for pneumonia. Initially, the patient was alert, stable, and in good spirits. During the admission process, the primary nurse asked the patient about allergies and, upon learning about a penicillin allergy, left to retrieve the appropriate wristbands. The nurse returned and applied a red wristband to indicate "no blood draw" and a blue wristband, believing it indicated "allergy"—a convention from their other job. However, at this hospital, a blue wristband signals "Do Not Resuscitate" (DNR). This discrepancy was not recognized at the time.

Shortly after the antibiotics were started, the patient began experiencing dizziness, trouble breathing, and difficulty swallowing—classic signs of anaphylaxis. The nurse responded by stopping the infusion, applying oxygen, and calling for help. When the patient became unresponsive, the nurse initiated CPR and called a code.

The code team arrived promptly and began resuscitation efforts. The junior medical student started chest compressions, the ICU nurse applied defibrillator pads, and the respiratory therapist managed the airway. As the ICU nurse prepared to administer epinephrine, they noticed the blue wristband and asked, “Is this patient DNR?” The team paused to verify the code status, halting defibrillation and medications.

The primary nurse, caught off guard, was unsure and left the room to locate the patient’s chart. During this delay, the patient’s rhythm changed from shockable (ventricular tachycardia) to asystole. When the chart was finally reviewed, it confirmed the patient was a full code. Resuscitation efforts resumed, but it was too late. After additional rounds of CPR, the patient was pronounced dead.

This case underscores several critical issues: a lack of standardized wristband colors across hospitals, unclear protocols around wristband application, poor organization of supplies, communication breakdowns during emergencies, and fatigue-induced errors from the primary nurse who had worked long hours and held two jobs.

A.3.
States-of-Mind of Virtual Avatars

This section outlines different potential states of mind that virtual avatars may adopt during simulations. These states shape how characters respond to questioning and influence the depth and tone of information shared with learners.

Defensive

Avatars in this state deflect blame and minimize personal responsibility. They are often vague when probed and may blame others or external circumstances.

Example Dialogue:
“Well, placing wristbands isn’t really my job. I assumed the ED had handled it. The cabinet was a mess anyway.”

Self-Reflective and Honest

These avatars are transparent and self-critical. They openly acknowledge mistakes, provide detailed accounts of what went wrong, and express regret.

Example Dialogue:
“I should’ve double-checked the wristband color. I just assumed, and that was a serious mistake on my part.”

Confused or Uncertain

These avatars show uncertainty or give inconsistent responses. They may need more follow-up and clarification from the learner.

Example Dialogue:
“I think… it was the blue band that made everyone pause? Or maybe someone said something about DNR… I’m not totally sure.”

Overly Professional or Detached

These avatars respond in a formal, emotionless manner. They may answer only direct questions without offering elaboration or personal insight.

Example Dialogue:
“At the time of arrival, the patient was in V-tach. I began airway management. I cannot comment on code status decisions.”

Frustrated

Avatars in this state highlight systemic problems and express dissatisfaction with organizational practices. They may avoid discussing their own mistakes.

Example Dialogue:
“This wouldn’t have happened if we had standardized wristband colors. It’s ridiculous how disorganized things are here.”

Appendix B
Sample Interview With the Primary Nurse – Frustrated

Learner:
Hello, I’m a nurse at the hospital. I’m here to talk about the ICU incident earlier.

Primary Nurse – Frustrated:
Hi. I’m here to discuss that, too. It’s been… quite overwhelming, to say the least. Where would you like to start?

Learner:
Why did the patient have delayed therapy?

Primary Nurse – Frustrated:
It was mainly the confusion over the wristband color. At my other job, a blue wristband means something different—it indicates an allergy. Here, it means DNR. There was a mix-up because of that.

Learner:
Have you ever been confused with the wristband colors before?

Primary Nurse – Frustrated:
Not until now. This was the first time, and I didn’t realize the difference until it was too late. It’s frustrating that different hospitals use different systems. It makes things complicated.

Learner:
How can we prevent this from happening again?

Primary Nurse – Frustrated:
Standardizing wristband colors across hospitals would help, so there’s no room for confusion. Clearer communication and organization of wristbands in the supply cabinet would also make a difference. It’s hard to juggle different systems when we’re already exhausted.

Learner:
You mentioned organizing the supply cabinet. Why?

Primary Nurse – Frustrated:
The wristbands are all over the place in the cabinet. It’s really disorganized, which makes it challenging to quickly find the right ones, especially when you’re in a stressful situation. Keeping things in order would definitely help avoid mistakes.

Learner:
How exactly can we better organize the supply cabinet?

Primary Nurse – Frustrated:
Labeling shelves would be a start, so everything’s clear and easy to find. Maybe have separate sections or bins for each wristband type. That way, there’s no fumbling around, especially when time is critical.

Learner:
What else do you think may have contributed to the mistake?

Primary Nurse – Frustrated:
Honestly, being fatigued played a role. Working long hours and managing multiple responsibilities can cloud judgment. Plus, I assumed placing wristbands was the ED’s job, which was a misunderstanding on my part.

Learner:
Why were you fatigued?

Primary Nurse – Frustrated:
I’ve been working a lot—36 hours over the past few days and an extended 12-hour shift yesterday because of a colleague’s sick call. Plus, I’m juggling two jobs. It’s just been a lot to handle.

Learner:
Do you think we should limit the maximum hours a nurse can work?

Primary Nurse – Frustrated:
That could definitely help. We need to make sure nurses aren’t overworked because fatigue can really impact decision-making. Having limits could ensure we’re at our best to provide safe patient care.

Learner:
What do you think is a reasonable number of hours?

Primary Nurse – Frustrated:
Maybe something like a maximum of 12-hour shifts with adequate time off in between. And perhaps also limiting the number of consecutive days worked, so there’s a balance between patient safety and nurse well-being.

Learner:
The wristband colors and fatigue— which do you think is more important?

Primary Nurse – Frustrated:
They’re both important, but standardizing wristband colors might be more immediately impactful in preventing mix-ups like this one. Fatigue, though, is a broader issue that affects overall performance and safety. Both need attention.

Appendix C
Voice Design for ICU Simulation Characters

In the ICU Failure Simulation, each virtual character is carefully designed with distinct vocal characteristics and narrative purposes. These design choices support immersive learning and help students practice investigative interviewing with realistic, emotionally varied avatars.

Primary Nurse
: The voice of the primary nurse is that of a middle-aged, overworked American clinician. It carries a weary, slightly breathy tone that reflects physical and emotional fatigue. Although professional, the voice may sound strained or exasperated at times, conveying frustration and defensiveness. This character draws attention to how cumulative stress, unclear responsibilities, and mismatched expectations across hospital systems can contribute to critical decision-making failures.

Code Team Medical Student
: This character speaks with a hesitant, soft-spoken tone that often includes pauses and rising inflections, signaling uncertainty and a lack of confidence. The voice is youthful and tentative, capturing the internal conflict of wanting to help but feeling unsure of one’s role. The narrative intent here is to highlight the challenges junior team members face in high-pressure situations and the importance of cultivating assertiveness and clear communication in clinical teams.

Code Team Nurse
: The ICU nurse has a direct, no-nonsense voice—clear, experienced, and slightly impatient at times. While authoritative, it maintains a sense of empathy and professionalism. This character is designed to reflect the voice of experience: someone deeply invested in patient safety, but frustrated by institutional inconsistencies like the lack of standardized DNR indicators. Their voice reinforces the theme of system-level advocacy from frontline staff.

Code Team Doctor
: The doctor leads the code with a calm and commanding presence. The voice is steady and clipped, with a tone that balances urgency with control. Frustration may surface subtly in moments of procedural delay. This character represents effective leadership constrained by unclear workflows and miscommunications. The narrative goal is to show how even strong team leads can be impeded by institutional inefficiencies.

Anesthesiologist
: The anesthesiologist speaks in a low, pragmatic tone—concise and focused. Though largely task-oriented, their voice occasionally carries a note of disbelief or concern when commenting on system flaws, such as the sole reliance on wristbands to indicate code status. Their role in the narrative is to draw attention to the need for better safety mechanisms and visual indicators during critical events.

•
