Sustainable polyolefin upcycling using liquid organic hydrogen carrier based hydrogen delivery and hydrocracking
Authors: Taeeun Kwon, Seoyoung Ahn, Taehyup Kim, Jong Hun Kang, Soohwa Jeong, Ki Hyuk Kang, Wangyun Won, Insoo Ro
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Globally, 89% of post-consumer plastic waste is landfilled or incinerated, exacerbating environmental pollution, while less than 0.1% is chemically recycled. Polyolefin plastics, nearly half of global plastic production, can be converted into liquid fuels via hydrogenolysis or hydrocracking but require external hydrogen gas. Here, we report a tandem catalytic process combining decalin dehydrogenation with polyethylene hydrocracking, eliminating the need for external hydrogen by using decalin as a liquid organic hydrogen carrier. Among several Pt/zeolite catalysts evaluated, a bifunctional Pt/HZSM-5 catalyst is identified as the most effective, achieving high PE conversion and selectivity toward liquid fuels. Comprehensive techno-economic and life-cycle assessments are conducted for three configurations: a one-step tandem reaction that directly integrates decalin utilization, a two-step process where dehydrogenation and hydrocracking occur sequentially under hydrogen-rich conditions, and an H2-direct process that supplies hydrogen externally. The results demonstrate that utilizing in situ-generated hydrogen from decalin significantly enhances both economic viability and environmental performance compared to conventional external hydrogen methods, with the one-step tandem approach emerging as the most efficient and sustainable pathway. This tandem catalytic system provides a sustainable and economically viable pathway for upcycling abundant polyolefin waste into valuable liquid fuels, advancing circular economy goals and mitigating plastic pollution.

Introduction

Plastics include a wide variety of polymeric materials renowned for their malleability, which has driven a substantial increase in production—from 1.7 million tons in 1954 to 413.8 million tons by 20231. Approximately 75% of plastic waste ends up in landfills, where it can persist for decades, breaking down into harmful microplastics2. Among these, polyolefin plastics such as polyethylene (PE) and polypropylene (PP) stand out due to their extensive use in packaging and disposable products; they account for more than half of global plastic production largely because of their short lifecycle3. To address the mounting environmental challenges, extensive research has focused on developing efficient recycling methods, particularly through catalytic upcycling processes. Traditional chemical recycling methods, such as pyrolysis and catalytic pyrolysis, convert plastics into pyrolysis oil but are hindered by high operating temperatures (350–900 °C) and low selectivity for high-value products4. Recent advancements have lowered the reaction temperature to 200–300 °C using supported metal catalysts, significantly enhancing selectivity toward liquid fuels5–14. These catalytic upcycling methods—primarily involving hydrogenolysis and hydrocracking reactions—offer higher selectivity for valuable products under milder conditions5–9,15–20. Key strategies include optimizing the geometric and electronic structure of catalysts5,15,16, balancing metal-acid properties17,18, controlling acidity and pore structure21,22, and improving catalyst dispersion with solvents13,23. Importantly, the final product distribution in these processes is highly sensitive to hydrogen pressure and concentration7,19,24: low-pressure conditions (10–20 bar) tend to favor terminal C–C bond cleavage producing low-value methane, whereas high-pressure hydrogen (30–60 bar) promotes internal C–C bond cleavage to yield high-value liquid fuels7,25.

Despite these advancements, current analyses largely overlook the complete lifecycle of hydrogen use in catalytic upcycling25. Conventional hydrogen production methods, such as steam methane reforming, emit up to 9–10 kg of CO2-eq. per kg of H2, undermining decarbonization efforts26. Recognizing this challenge, our group recently proposed a tandem catalysis strategy that integrates plastic depolymerization with in situ hydrogen generation via methanol aqueous phase reforming (APR)24. However, this approach still faces critical gaps: we have yet to conduct comprehensive techno-economic analysis (TEA) and life-cycle assessments (LCA) to evaluate capital and operation costs as well as net carbon emissions—especially given that the methanol APR process itself produces CO227. These limitations have spurred interest in greener hydrogen carriers, such as liquid organic hydrogen carriers (LOHCs) and ammonia, which offer cleaner storage and transportation options28. However, integrating these systems with plastic upcycling faces unresolved catalytic challenges. For instance, Ru/C catalysts used for high-density polyethylene (HDPE) hydrogenolysis with decalin—a typical LOHC—yield only about 5% conversion due to inefficient dehydrogenation23. Similarly, Pt/USY catalysts have underperformed with tetralin compared to using external hydrogen because current catalysts cannot simultaneously drive tetralin dehydrogenation and plastic hydrogenolysis29. Thus, designing a bifunctional catalyst that effectively couples LOHC dehydrogenation with plastic upcycling remains a formidable challenge.

In this study, we address this challenge by developing an efficient bifunctional catalyst capable of tandem decalin dehydrogenation and PE hydrocracking without an external hydrogen supply. Our findings reveal that zeolite-supported Pt catalysts—particularly Pt/HZSM-5 with a low Pt loading of 0.5%—excel in driving these tandem reactions, a performance attributed to their optimal metal–acid balance (MAB). Comprehensive TEA and LCA assessments were conducted on three cases: (i) a one-step tandem reaction that directly integrates decalin utilization into the plastic valorization step, (ii) a two-step process where decalin dehydrogenation and PE hydrocracking occur sequentially under a hydrogen-rich environment, and (iii) an H2-direct process that bypasses the LOHC step by supplying hydrogen directly. The analyses demonstrate that using in situ-generated hydrogen from decalin significantly enhances both economic viability and environmental performance compared to conventional methods relying on external hydrogen gas—with the one-step tandem approach emerging as the most promising pathway. These results not only validate the sustainability of our one-step tandem reaction but also deepen our understanding of the underlying catalytic mechanisms, paving the way for more efficient processing of polyolefin plastic waste.

Results

Tandem decalin dehydrogenation/PE hydrocracking reaction

PE hydrocracking was conducted using decalin as an alternative hydrogen source, potentially offering a viable alternative to the current PE upcycling process that relies on an external hydrogen gas supply5,6,8,13,15–17,23,30,31. A series of zeolite-supported Pt catalysts with similar SiO2/Al2O3 ratios (20–30) were employed to investigate the influence of catalytic properties. As shown in Fig. 1a, PE conversion varied significantly depending on the zeolite support. Conversion was lowest for 0.5Pt/HY(30) (38.4%) and moderate for 0.5Pt/HMOR(20) (45.6%), and highest for 0.5Pt/HBEA(25) (83.8%) and 0.5Pt/HZSM-5(30) (86.9%). To understand these variations, characterizations focused on particle size, dispersion, acid site density, and the Brønsted-to-Lewis acid site ratio (BAS/LAS). As shown in Table 1, CO chemisorption revealed similar particle sizes and dispersions across the catalysts, indicating that Pt particle characteristics were not the primary factor influencing reactivity. Instead, differences in acid site properties were evident among the zeolite types. NH3-TPD analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1) showed that total acid density was lowest for 0.5Pt/HY(30) (1.1 mmol/g), moderate for 0.5Pt/HZSM-5(30) and 0.5Pt/HBEA(25) (2.0 mmol/g), and highest for 0.5Pt/HMOR(20) (3.2 mmol/g). Pyridine-DRIFTS analysis (Supplementary Fig. 2) further revealed variations in the BAS/LAS ratio, ranging from 0.12 for 0.5Pt/HY(30) to 4.03 for 0.5Pt/HMOR(20). Metal-acid balance (MAB), a key factor for both PE hydrocracking and decalin dehydrogenation18,32–36, was calculated for each catalyst (Table 1). The high MAB catalyst, with limited BAS, favored decalin dehydrogenation over PE hydrocracking31,34,36. In contrast, 0.5Pt/HZSM-5(30) and 0.5Pt/HBEA(25), with moderate MABs, exhibited higher PE hydrocracking reactivity. However, further decreasing the MAB, as in 0.5Pt/HMOR(20), shifted the reaction towards decalin decomposition, lowering PE conversion. These results underscore the importance of optimizing MAB to facilitate the tandem decalin dehydrogenation/PE hydrocracking reaction. Despite a high PE conversion at moderate MAB, the carbon balance varied across the catalysts (Fig. 1a, Table 1, and Supplementary Figs. 4, 5). Decalin dehydrogenation to tetralin produces three hydrogen molecules, and subsequent conversion to naphthalene releases two additional hydrogen molecules (Fig. 1b). Side reactions, such as ring opening and isomerization, produce various by-products, adversely affecting the carbon balance and complicating the separation process. Identifiable product yields, determined by gas chromatograph equipped with a mass spectrometer (GC-MS), were 98.3% for 0.5Pt/HZSM-5(30), 75.1% for 0.5Pt/HBEA(25), 85.9% for 0.5Pt/HMOR(20), and 70.2% for 0.5Pt/HY(30) (Fig. 1a and Supplementary Fig. 6). Among the liquid products, compounds with more than 10 carbon atoms, such as methyl-naphthalene, were detected. These polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs) are undesirable due to their limited utility as liquid fuels37. Further analysis showed that PAH selectivity strongly correlated with the zeolite pore size (Fig. 1c)38. For instance, the small pores of ZSM-5 (0.63 nm) restricted the diffusion of larger PAH molecules like methyl-naphthalene (0.68 nm) and dimethyl-naphthalene (0.77 nm), resulting in lower PAH formation. In contrast, larger-pore zeolites such as MOR (0.70 nm), BEA (0.74 nm), and Y-zeolite (0.81 nm) facilitated the diffusion of these species, increasing PAH formation. This size-selective diffusion behavior underscores the role of pore structure in minimizing PAH formation while affecting the presence of unidentified species, which further complicates product separation and reduces the suitability of the liquid products as fuels.

To further optimize the tandem reaction, we conducted experiments with varying reaction time, temperature, pressure, and decalin/PE ratios (Supplementary Table 3). Longer reaction times increased PE conversion and gas product yields (Fig. 1d), but extending the reaction beyond 10 h did not improve conversion; instead, it led to over-cracking and excessive gas production. As the reaction progressed, the liquid product darkened, indicating more extensive cracking and the formation of a broader range of isomers and aromatics (Supplementary Fig. 7). Next, temperature and pressure effects on PE hydrocracking were also examined. Since decalin dehydrogenation is an endothermic process39, higher reaction temperatures were expected to increase hydrogen production and, consequently, PE conversion. Indeed, the conversion improved with increasing temperatures up to 300 °C, beyond which no further conversion improvements were observed (Fig. 1e and Supplementary Fig. 8), likely due to the endothermic nature of cracking and the exothermic nature of hydrogenation40. Excessive temperatures (e.g., 325 °C) resulted in over-cracking and higher gas yields. Reaction pressure (0–30 bar) and decalin/PE weight ratio (0.25–10.0) were also varied, but these factors had minimal impact on PE conversion, which remained in the range of 75–85% (Supplementary Figs. 9 and10). These findings indicate that reaction time and temperature are the most critical parameters in determining the efficiency of the tandem reaction. Based on these findings, the optimal reaction conditions were determined to be 300 °C and 10 bar for 10 h, which were used in subsequent experiments. Additionally, when methylcyclohexane and cyclohexane were employed as alternative hydrogen sources, comparable PE conversions were also observed, confirming the broader applicability of the tandem system to a range of LOHCs (Supplementary Fig. 11 and Supplementary Note 2).

Effect of acid and metal sites on catalytic performance

To investigate the role of acid sites in the tandem decalin dehydrogenation/PE hydrocracking reaction, we synthesized a series of 0.5Pt/HZSM-5 catalysts with varying SiO2/Al2O3 ratios. MAB plays a crucial role in determining the reaction pathways for both decalin dehydrogenation reaction and PE hydrocracking, as previously established34,41–43. Catalyst characterization involved NH3-TPD for total acid site density (Supplementary Fig. 12), CO chemisorption for Pt particle size and dispersion, and pyridine-DRIFTS for evaluating BAS/LAS ratios (Supplementary Fig. 13 and Supplementary Table 4). The results indicated that increasing the SiO2/Al2O3 ratio decreases both the total acid site density and the BAS/LAS ratio due to reduced Al content (Supplementary Table 5). As noted earlier, an optimal MAB is essential for maximizing PE conversion: at low MAB, limited metal content restricts decalin dehydrogenation31,34,41,42,44–46, whereas, at high MAB, the low acid site density limits the β-scission of C–C bonds in PE31,41,42,44–46. Consistent with previous findings34,41,42, PE conversion initially increased with MAB, reaching a maximum PE conversion of 90.7% for 0.5Pt/HZSM-5(80) (Fig. 2a), while the highest liquid product yield of 72.4% was also observed with this catalyst (Fig. 2b). Product selectivity analysis (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Fig. 14) showed a preference for n-alkanes with suppressed iso-alkane formation as MAB increased. To further confirm the role of acid sites, a control experiment was conducted using pyridine to poison these sites17. For 0.5Pt/HZSM-5(30), PE conversion dropped significantly from 86.9 to 19.1% upon pyridine poisoning (Table 2), underscoring the importance of acid sites in both decalin dehydrogenation and PE hydrocracking. Since pyridine poisons both LAS and BAS (as reflected in the pyridine-DRIFTS spectra, Fig. 2d), isolating their individual contributions was challenging. To specifically assess the role of LAS, a 0.5Pt/SiO2 catalyst, which inherently lacks BAS, was tested. As expected, the DRIFTS spectrum of 0.5Pt/SiO2 exhibited only LAS signals with no BAS peaks (Fig. 2d), consistent with previous work47. While 0.5Pt/SiO2 produced substantial hydrogen during decalin dehydrogenation (12.2 mg), its PE conversion was only 7.3% (Table 2 and Supplementary Fig. 15), indicating ineffective hydrogen utilization for PE hydrocracking in the absence of BAS (Fig. 2e). Effective PE hydrocracking requires hydrogen activation on Pt sites and subsequent hydrogenation of intermediates facilitated by BAS48. Without BAS, as in the 0.5Pt/SiO2 system, hydrogen utilization is inefficient, resulting in limited C–C bond cleavage (Fig. 2e). This observation aligns with the results with pyridine-poisoned 0.5Pt/HZSM-5(30) catalysts, where suppressed isomerization produced primarily n-alkanes with minimal iso-alkanes (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 15a). Furthermore, 0.5Pt/SiO2 exhibited high selectivity for tetralin and PAHs (Supplementary Fig. 15b), highlighting Pt’s stronger dehydrogenation capability relative to its C–C bond cleavage ability11,49,50. Together, these findings emphasize that tandem decalin dehydrogenation and PE hydrocracking processes require the cooperative action of both metal sites and BAS. To explore the role of Pt, we evaluated the catalytic performance as a function of Pt loading. Initial experiments with HZSM-5(30) showed a PE conversion of 36.6% without Pt compared to 86.9% with the 0.5Pt/HZSM-5(30) catalyst (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 16). The presence of Pt significantly enhanced both PE conversion and liquid yield by promoting decalin dehydrogenation and PE hydrocracking, increasing the liquid yield from 25.6% to 71.2% (Fig. 3a, b). Additionally, thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and differential thermal analysis (DTA) of spent catalysts highlighted that Pt reduces coke formation. The spent HZSM-5(30) catalyst lost 20.4% of its weight, compared to only 8.3% for 0.5Pt/HZSM-5(30) (Fig. 3c, d). Moreover, DTA revealed a peak decomposition temperature of 551 °C for HZSM-5(30) versus 488 °C for 0.5Pt/HZSM-5(30) (Fig. 3e, f). While hard coke is generally defined within the 400–600 °C range51, coke on zeolites can be divided into two types depending on the deposition site52: Coke I (in the mesoporous matrix) combusts at lower temperatures (450–470 °C), whereas Coke II (in micropores) is more difficult to remove (520–530 °C)52. Finally, to further elucidate the role of Pt, we investigated the catalytic performance of HZSM-5(30) with varying Pt loadings (0.1–5%). While the 0.5Pt/HZSM-5(30) catalyst demonstrated high PE conversion and liquid yield, lower Pt loadings (0.1–0.5%) did not significantly change product selectivity due to the relatively low Pt site density (2.0–7.2 µmolPt/gcatalyst, Supplementary Table 6). In contrast, the 5.0Pt/HZSM-5(30) catalyst, with a Pt site density of 42.7 µmolPt/gcatalyst, exhibited a marked shift in selectivity, with decreased iso-alkane formation and increased PAH selectivity due to enhanced decalin dehydrogenation and subsequent naphthalene formation at Pt sites (Supplementary Fig. 17). Although the higher Pt loading led to increased hydrogen production from PAHs, this hydrogen did not contribute effectively to PE hydrocracking, underscoring the importance of maintaining an optimal MAB for selective liquid fuel production in tandem reactions.

Tracing hydrogen sources and mechanistic insights in tandem decalin dehydrogenation/PE hydrocracking

To elucidate the mechanism of tandem decalin dehydrogenation/PE hydrocracking and identify the hydrogen sources, we used n-dodecane and decalin-d18 as surrogates for PE and decalin, respectively. Mass spectrometry (MS) analysis of gaseous products (Supplementary Fig. 18) revealed that deuterium gas (D2, m/z = 4) and deuterium-substituted species—including methane, ethane, propane, and butane—were detected exclusively in reactions with decalin-d18 (Fig. 4a, b). These results indicate that deuterium from decalin-d18 actively participates in C–C bond cleavage during the reaction. As illustrated in Fig. 4c, decalin dehydrogenation generates D2, which in turn facilitates C–C bond cleavage, yielding deuterium-substituted products. The observed H2 (m/z = 2) in reactions with decalin-d18 likely originates from hydrogen released during alkane dehydrogenation on Pt sites. Adsorption of decalin or PE onto Pt promotes this hydrogen release, leading to recombination as H2, HD, or D2. To confirm the presence of deuterium-substituted liquid products, ATR-FTIR analysis was conducted following established methodologies53,54. The spectra for reactions with regular decalin showed peaks at 2960 cm−1 and 2910 cm−1, corresponding to CH3 and CH2 stretching vibrations, respectively (Fig. 4d)53. In reactions with decalin-d18, additional C-D stretching vibrations were observed at 2090 cm−1 and 2210 cm−1, associated with CD2 and CD3 groups (Fig. 4e)53,54. These peaks were absent in reactions with regular decalin, indicating deuterium incorporation into the products. To exclude potential interference from unreacted decalin-d18, a control experiment was conducted with decalin reduced to one-tenth of its original amount while keeping dodecane levels constant. gas chromatograph equipped with flame ionization detector (GC-FID) analysis confirmed the absence of residual decalin, as it had predominantly converted to naphthalene (Supplementary Fig. 20). Despite complete decalin conversion, C-D peaks persisted (Fig. 4e), confirming their association with reaction products rather than unreacted decalin. Collectively, the detection of deuterium-substituted products in both gaseous and liquid phases support the role of decalin as an effective hydrogen source in the PE hydrocracking process.

To clarify the mechanistic advantage of the tandem decalin dehydrogenation/PE hydrocracking process, we conducted control experiments using PE and decalin separately under identical conditions (Supplementary Table 7). When the reaction was performed with decalin alone, short hydrocarbons (C1–C6), cycloalkanes, aromatics, indene, and naphthalene were observed. In contrast, the PE-only reaction produced n-alkanes, iso-alkanes, cycloalkanes, and some aromatics, but not indene or naphthalene, indicating that these multi-ring species originated from decalin transformation. Notably, the PE conversion without decalin was 64.6%, whereas the tandem system achieved 86.9%, highlighting decalin’s role as an effective in situ hydrogen source.

To better elucidate coupling between the two reactions, we quantified hydrogen generation and consumption across systems. In the decalin-only case, 1.27 mmol of H2 was detected in the gas phase, and the total hydrogen generated (calculated from product distributions) was 2.22 mmol. In contrast, in the tandem system, only 0.17 mmol of H2 remained in the gas phase, while calculated hydrogen production increased to 3.56 mmol. This indicates that hydrogen released from decalin was immediately consumed during PE hydrocracking, shifting the decalin dehydrogenation equilibrium forward, consistent with Le Chatelier’s principle—a phenomenon referred to as the “pulling effect”. As a result, both hydrogen release and PE hydrocracking are enhanced through this synergistic interaction, demonstrating that the integrated tandem system exhibits a clear mechanistic advantage over separate or sequential processes.

Stability and versatility of Pt/HZSM-5(30) for industrial plastic upcycling applications

To evaluate the industrial potential of 0.5Pt/HZSM-5(30) in tandem decalin dehydrogenation/PE hydrocracking, we assessed its stability and versatility. Changes in the liquid product composition were also assessed via color index analysis, revealing a correlation with the selectivity for unsaturated hydrocarbons (Supplementary Fig. 23). A scaled-up reaction further validated the catalyst’s performance, showing consistent product selectivity (Supplementary Fig. 24). Catalyst stability from cycle 1 to 5 was evaluated by STEM, NH3-TPD, CO chemisorption, and pyridine-DRIFTS analyses. CO chemisorption revealed modest particle growth upon reuse, though catalytic activity remained largely unchanged (Fig. 5g, h; Supplementary Fig. 25, and Supplementary Table 9). Pyridine-DRIFTS confirmed preserved BAS/LAS ratios (Fig. 5i and Supplementary Table 10), and N2 adsorption–desorption isotherms showed minimal framework degradation (Supplementary Fig. 26), suggesting the catalyst’s long-term stability. Techno-economic analysis

To assess the economic and technical feasibility of the tandem decalin dehydrogenation/PE hydrocracking process using 0.5% Pt/HZSM-5 (SiO2/Al2O3 = 30), we performed a comprehensive TEA and LCA based on experimental data (Supplementary Fig. 27). Key downstream separation steps—such as hydrocarbon phase separation and aromatics extraction using sulfolane—were integrated into the process design and reflected in the block flow diagrams (Supplementary Fig. 28). Three process configurations were evaluated: (1) a one-step process where decalin functions simultaneously as solvent and hydrogen source in a single reactor; (2) a two-step process in which decalin dehydrogenation and PE hydrocracking occur in separate units; and (3) a H2-direct process that bypasses LOHC dehydrogenation by directly supplying hydrogen. In all cases, the product stream undergoes initial filtration to remove unreacted plastic, followed by tailored separation strategies.

The gas separation subsystem—applied across all configurations—recovers butane and LPG fractions via sequential distillation columns56. The two-step and H2-direct processes incorporate pressure swing adsorption for hydrogen purification and recycling. The liquid fuel separation subsystem produces gasoline- and diesel-range products, and in the one-step process, additional extractive distillation with sulfolane is employed to selectively recover BTX-range aromatics. All residual fractions are routed to a heat and power generation unit to satisfy process energy demands.

Figure 7a summarizes the annual cost contributions of individual subsystems (detailed in Supplementary Table 13). Among all subsystems, the plastic waste valorization subsystem represents the largest capital cost driver, primarily due to the high reactor cost and continuous plastic feed (10,000 kg/h). Although the one-step process is efficient, it requires an additional sulfolane unit process to separate aromatics produced during hydrocracking, thereby increasing equipment and reagent costs. Capital costs associated with the heat and power generation subsystem represent the second-highest share among all processes, accounting for 11.1%, 13.3%, and 14.3% of the total capital investment in the one-step, two-step, and H2-direct processes, respectively. This is primarily due to the capital-intensive installation of boilers and turbo generators. Carbon flow analysis (as indicated by the arrows in Fig. 7a) shows that when normalizing the total carbon input from plastic waste and decalin to 100%, the one-step process achieves efficiencies of 65.1% for liquid fuel, 3.8% for LPG, and 9.7% for butane. In contrast, the two-step process yields efficiencies of 20.8%, 30.2%, and 12.3%, respectively, while in the H2-direct process—where LDPE is the sole carbon input—the efficiencies are 30.7%, 44.5%, and 18.1%. This lower carbon efficiency in the two-step and H2-direct processes translates to reduced fuel production, with the one-step process generating 56,988 tons/yr compared to 24,440 tons/yr for the others. Discussion

In summary, our study demonstrates that tandem decalin dehydrogenation/PE hydrocracking is an effective method for converting waste plastics into valuable liquid fuels. Using a bifunctional Pt/HZSM-5 catalyst system, we achieved a high plastic conversion of 90.7% and a liquid fuel selectivity of 72.4% without requiring external hydrogen. Catalyst optimization—including variations in Pt loadings (0–5.0%) and SiO2/Al2O3 ratios (23–400)—emphasized the critical balance between metal and acid functionalities needed to drive both reactions efficiently. Mechanistic studies confirmed the key role of in situ-generated hydrogen, as evidenced by deuterium incorporation in products during reactions with decalin-d18. The 0.5Pt/HZSM-5(30) catalyst exhibited excellent stability, maintaining high conversion and selectivity over three consecutive cycles, with performance comparable to that of the fresh catalyst. Moreover, the catalyst effectively upcycled a range of PE feedstocks, including LDPE and HDPE, achieving conversions between 80.2 and 82.3% with liquid fuel selectivity ranging from 52.7 to 68.1%.

Economic and environmental assessments revealed that the one-step process is superior, offering lower decalin consumption, higher carbon efficiency, and reduced energy demand compared to both the H2-direct and two-step processes. The one-step approach achieves the lowest global warming potential of 0.413 kg CO2-eq. per kg of fuel and a MSP of $3.5/GGE. Although additional sulfolane is required for separation, this process remains the most cost-effective and environmentally favorable option, delivering higher fuel yields, lower greenhouse gas emissions, and improved resource efficiency while avoiding the energy and infrastructure demands typically associated with hydrogen compression and the separate energy-intensive dehydrogenation step in conventional LOHC systems. Beyond decalin, other hydrogen-donor LOHC systems (e.g., the methylcyclohexane/toluene pair) may also enable PE conversion. Carrier selection should be guided by plant-level TEA/LCA, weighing H2 storage capacity, (de)hydrogenation energetics, solvent stability and recovery losses, reaction rate/selectivity and fuel yield, materials compatibility and safety, and the resulting GWP and MSP. These findings not only deepen our understanding of efficient plastic waste upcycling but also lay the groundwork for developing more sustainable and economically viable recycling technologies, advancing both waste management and circular economy objectives.

Catalyst preparation

Before platinum addition, NH4-form zeolites were transformed into H-form zeolites by treating under air (99.999%, Union Gas) at 550 °C for 6 h. A series of Pt/zeolite catalysts were prepared by a conventional wet impregnation method using a solution of TAPN dissolved in deionized water. Various H-form zeolite supports with different topologies and SiO2/Al2O3 ratios were blended with TAPN solutions at room temperature for 2 h. After evaporating the water at 50 °C, the solid was dried overnight (for at least 6 h) at 100 °C. The catalysts were calcined under air at 400 °C for 3 h at a ramping rate of 2.5 °C/min, subsequently treated under 10% H2/Ar (99.999%, Union Gas) at 400 °C for 3 h at a ramping rate of 2.5 °C/min. Before the reaction, the catalyst was prepared via ex situ reduction under 10% H2/Ar flow at 400 °C for 1.5 h at a ramping rate of 5 °C/min. The prepared Pt/zeolite catalysts with ZSM-5, zeolite beta, zeolite mordenite, and zeolite Y will be termed Pt/HZSM-5, Pt/HBEA, Pt/HMOR, and Pt/HY, respectively. In addition, the Pt catalysts with different SiO2/Al2O3 ratios will be termed Pt/zeolite(SiO2/Al2O3 = x), where x represents the SiO2/Al2O3 ratio. If the Pt loading is not specified as 0.5 wt%, it is referred to as y in the notation yPt/HZSM-5(30).

Catalytic activity test

Catalytic activity tests were conducted in a stainless-steel autoclave sealed with a spiral wound gasket (Hanwoul Engineering CO., LTD). A pre-treated catalyst was introduced into the reactor along with PE, decalin, and a glass-coated magnetic stir bar. The reactor was purged with 30 bar Ar gas three times before being filled to the reaction pressure (0–30 bar). It was then heated to the reaction temperature (250–325 °C) over about an hour, with the temperature being closely monitored by a thermocouple inserted inside. Stirring commenced at a rate of 700 rpm once the temperature had reached 150 °C. Upon completion of the reaction, the reactor was rapidly cooled to room temperature using an ice bath. The gas and liquid products were then transferred to a gas bag and a centrifuge tube, respectively. To facilitate the recovery of any residual materials, toluene was added to the mixture of liquid and catalyst during the transfer to the centrifuge, along with a mesitylene solution of 125 mmol/L as an internal standard. The mixture underwent centrifugation to separate dissolved substances from the unreacted reactants and catalysts, which were then dried in a vacuum oven at 40 °C for 24 h. The weight of the centrifuge tube, containing the reaction products and catalyst, was measured, and the initial weight of the empty tube and the added catalyst were deducted to determine the amount of unreacted reactant. Alkane products were identified using an Agilent GC-MS. Product analysis was carried out using an Agilent 8890 GC System gas chromatograph, equipped with both a FID and a thermal conductivity detector (TCD), employing a GS-Carbon PLOT column for gas analysis and an HP-1 column for liquids. To evaluate the color characteristics of the liquid products, Yellow Index measurements were performed according to ASTM E313, and Gardner color values were determined based on ASTM D1544. A xenon lamp was used as the light source for all measurements.

To investigate the reaction mechanism, isotope analysis was performed using decalin-d18 (C10D18). The reaction procedure was identical to that described above, substituting decalin-d18 for decalin and dodecane for PE. The gas and liquid products obtained using decalin-d18 were analyzed using mass spectroscopy (HPR-20 R&D, HIDEN Analytical) and attenuated total reflectance fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (ATR-FTIR, Bruker, Billerica, MA) for deuterium tracing, respectively. The reusability test proceeded as follows: After reacting for 10 or more hours to achieve complete conversion into an extractable form, the used catalyst was collected. Following the mixture’s drying, the spent catalyst underwent a two-step regeneration process. First, it was calcined at 500 °C with an airflow of 80 mL/min. Then, it was pre-treated at 400 °C under a 10% H2/Ar flow at 50 mL/min. This procedure was repeated multiple times to secure enough catalyst for the next step. Pyridine poisoning was carried out as follows: The catalyst was ex situ reduced with a ramp rate of 5 °C/min, maintaining a temperature of 400 °C for 1.5 h under a 10% H2/Ar flow of 50 mL/min. Pyridine (3.65 g, 46.1 mmol) was added dropwise with a pipette to poison 600 mg of 0.5Pt/HZSM-5(30), 40 times the acid site density estimated by NH3-TPD to ensure sufficient poisoning. The mixture was dried in a vacuum desiccator for 12 h at room temperature and immediately used in the reaction.

Following this, the catalysts were subjected to heating up to 800 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C/min, under an airflow of 50 mL/min, using an alumina cell.

Techno-economic analysis (TEA)

Based on the experimental data presented in the previous sections, we developed an integrated process for PE hydrocracking using in situ generated hydrogen from decalin, referred to as the one-step process. Using this hydrogen source, we compared the hydrogen supply requirements of a conventional hydrocracking reaction to those of a reaction integrating decalin dehydrogenation into the hydrocracking process. In this comparison, we first considered the hydrogen produced by decalin dehydrogenation (1st step), which was then supplied to PE hydrocracking (2nd step), referred to as the two-step utilization.

A process model was developed using the commercial software Aspen Plus to process 240 tons of plastic waste per day. Equipment size was determined, and capital costs were calculated based on the heat and material balance obtained from the process model60. Some equipment costs, such as those for distillation columns and pumps, were estimated using the Aspen Process Economic Analyzer61. The cost for other equipment, such as reactors and boilers, were calculated using an exponential scaling expression based on cost and size data from the literature62,63:18\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{{New}}} \,{{\mbox{Cost}}}={\left({{{Base}}} \,{{\mbox{Cost}}}\right)\left(\frac{{{New \, Size}}}{{{Base \, Size}}}\right)}^{n}$$\end{document}NewCost=BaseCostNewSizeBaseSizen

All the costs were adjusted to a common basis year of 2022 using the Chemical Engineering Plant Cost Index64. Variable operating costs included raw materials (i.e., plastic waste, decalin makeup), utilities (i.e., cooling water, steam), and waste disposal cost (i.e., ash). Fixed operating costs included salaries, property insurance, and maintenance costs65. Detailed economic parameters and assumptions are provided in Supplementary Table 12. Following the specification of all the reactions and downstream separations, we determined the heating and cooling requirements of the process. To address the high costs associated with using industrial utilities such as steam and cooling water for heating and cooling, we implemented heat integration. This approach utilizes the excess energy released from high-temperature units to meet the heating requirements of low-temperature units by designing a heat exchanger network between multiple hot and cold streams66. By performing heat integration, the heating and cooling requirements were significantly reduced across all processes. In the one-step process, heating and cooling demands decreased by 59% and 60%, respectively. For the two-step process, reductions of 51% and 49% were observed, while the H2-direct process saw decreases of 56% and 47%, respectively (detailed results in Supplementary Table 16).

Life-cycle assessment (LCA)

To evaluate the environmental impact of the process, we conducted a life-cycle assessment (LCA) following the guideline provided by the International Standard Organization67,68, using SimaPro 8.5.2 with the Ecoinvent database 3.9.1. The LCA followed four key steps: (a) defining goals and scope, (b) performing life-cycle inventory analysis, (c) conducting life-cycle impact assessment, and (d) interpreting the life-cycle results69. The system boundary was defined from plastic waste to liquid fuel, with the functional unit set to 1 kg of liquid fuel. To avoid allocating impacts to byproducts such as liquid petroleum gas and electricity, we employed system expansion70.
