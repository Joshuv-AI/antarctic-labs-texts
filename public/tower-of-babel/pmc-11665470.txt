Academic procrastination in Ecuadorian university students: An explanatory model based on academic motivation
Authors: Xavier Fabricio Reyes Trelles, Pedro Israel Alvarado Cevallos, Katherine Paola Calle Torres, Jhessenia Natalia Galarza Parra
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcademic procrastination is a multifaceted variable that encompasses different affective, cognitive, and behavioral factors, and has a high incidence in university students with important negative academic, personal, and social consequences. As such, this study aims to explain the levels of academic procrastination in relation to academic motivation and certain sociodemographic variables. The sample was composed of 117 students belonging to the University of Cuenca, Ecuador, they completed a questionnaire of sociodemographic variables, academic motivation scale and academic procrastination scale. Results showed high levels of intrinsic and extrinsic motivation, as well as low levels of amotivation, together with a medium level of total academic motivation and academic procrastination in the participants. The logistic regression model with ordinal response evidenced a 19 % reduction in fit when considering the dimensions of intrinsic motivation, extrinsic motivation, and amotivation, only intrinsic motivation was statistically significant in explaining academic procrastination, while total academic motivation and sociodemographic variables did not contribute significantly to the model. These findings confirm the importance of academic motivation, mainly intrinsic motivation, in the understanding of academic procrastination. The theoretical and practical implications of the results are discussed, highlighting their relevance in explaining this phenomenon and benefiting students.Keywords: Academic procrastination, Academic motivation, Intrinsic motivation, College students, Self-determination theory

Introduction

Motivation is conceived as an internal process that drives the individual and is related to a set of internal and external factors [1], involving multiple biological variables acquired through experience and its consequences [2,3]. This internal state stimulates, directs, and maintains behavior from its beginning until the achievement of a goal [4] and is linked to personal development through the feeling of accomplishment obtained from a satisfactory execution of challenging tasks [5].

Self-determination theory (SDT) has been utilized to explore and understand academic motivation [3], because it provides a broad framework for understanding human motivation. Three basic psychological needs are proposed: autonomy, competence, and social relatedness, which satisfy optimal functioning and intrinsic motivation. This claim is supported by the research of Pelikan et al. [6], who report that satisfaction of basic needs is essential for intrinsic motivation and learning across countries and settings. Accordingly, Deci and Ryan [7,8] reaffirm the proposal of three dimensions of motivation: extrinsic, intrinsic, and amotivation.

Consistently with other authors [3,9,10], which conceptualize these dimensions as follows: Extrinsic motivation refers to behavior that is driven by desirable rewards from the environment, whether provided by others or self-administered. Intrinsic motivation alludes to the inherent satisfaction derived from the activity itself, without the need for external rewards or obligations. Finally, amotivation is characterized by a behavioral reluctance to engage in a task [11], manifested by lack of energy, frustration, apathy, stagnation, procrastination, noncompliance, and reduced effort.

Motivation is defined either as trait (general predisposition) or a state (specific situation) towards the learning process, influenced by mutual interaction [12]. In either case, it results from a combination of personality trails, interpersonal relationships, and environmental factors [13], which propel students to solve problems, persist, and overcome highly demanding tasks. Moreover, motivation enhances the sense of satisfaction and personal well-being, improves the effectiveness and efficiency of academic performance, and optimizes study strategies [14]. Simultaneously, motivation has emerged as a crucial explanatory factor in understanding related phenomena such as academic dropout, frustration, low performance at various educational levels, academic success, and academic procrastination [9,10,15,16].

In this context, Tisocco and Liporace [17] indicate that academic motivation directly influences procrastination and significantly determines academic performance based on the principles of SDT. Academic procrastination is a multifaceted variable that encompasses various affective, cognitive, and behavioral aspects within the academic environment [18]. Although lacking a universal definition, it is commonly conceptualized as an act of delaying academic activities, despite foreseeable consequences [19], to such an extent that their completion becomes unlikely, leading to psychological discomfort and unpleasant emotions such as anxiety [20,21]. Moreover, it impairs the ability to make decisions and resolve conflicts related to academic responsibilities or environmental demands [22].

Various theoretical perspectives seek to explain the origin and impact of academic procrastination. From the psychodynamic perspective, it is viewed as a defense mechanism against distress-inducing tasks that threaten one's identity [23]. According to behaviorism, these behaviors persist or recur due to their favorable outcomes or the absence of unfavorable consequences [24]. From the cognitive perspective, academic procrastination is seen as the result of irrational beliefs, low frustration tolerance, unrealistic goal-setting, poor coping strategies, and difficulties in planning, particularly when associated with unpleasant emotions stemming from doubts about one's skills and abilities [25,26]. From a neuroscientific standpoint, it is regarded as a failure of self-regulation [27,28], with the comprehensive neuro theoretical model of procrastination, based on brain structural features, indicating that genetic abnormalities in self-control, emotional regulation, and episodic procrastination networks serve as the neuroanatomical substrates involved [29].

Senécal et al. [30] propose that academic procrastination fundamentally stems from an attentional problem that extends beyond emotional, temporal, or personality trait mismanagement. During their university years, students undergo a period of intellectual discovery and personal growth within a potentially overwhelming context, often resulting in the postponement of academic activities [31]. Approximately 90 % of Ecuadorian university students are in the stage of emerging adulthood [32], which is characterized by uncertainty about their career paths [33]. Those who opt for university education are influenced by the regime imposed by their context or the reality of being a student [34].

Zumárraga-Espinosa [35] indicates a high prevalence of academic procrastination at any level among Ecuadorian university students, with similar results found in Peruvian students. Meanwhile, the majority of Colombian students show medium to low levels [36], with comparable findings in Spain [12] and among Canadian students [37]. Although these percentages vary significantly among students [38], Yurtseven and Doğan [39] and Sirin [40] consider it the primary issue in university education. Academic procrastination has significant impacts on productivity [20], mental health, and academic success [37]. Additionally, high anxiety is proposed as a cause of stress, and illness [41], and is associated with negative academic and non-academic outcomes [42].

Given its significance, several researchers have proposed a variety of variables as potential predictors of academic procrastination. These include cognitive factors [12,43], emotional aspects [44], basic needs [42], personality traits [45], academic factors [46,47], and sociodemographic variables [40,48]. Moreover, researchers have explored the interrelationships among these variables aiming to establish an explanatory model, recognizing that it may not be possible to explain academic procrastination with a single variable alone [40].

Similarly, a variable that stands out for its results and the theoretical foundations of SDT [41] or psychological drive theory [49] is academic motivation and its dimensions [38,39,46,[50], [51], [52], [53]], with special emphasis on intrinsic motivation [54,55]. These aspects emphasize the necessity of identifying the associations between a set of independent variables and a dependent variable.

These aspects underscore the importance of identifying the factors associated with academic procrastination, which is highly prevalent among university students, particularly in the Ecuadorian and Latin American contexts. Many studies have reported differing findings regarding the relevance of gender in motivational processes across various cultural contexts [56,57]. Mainly, Pelikan et al. [6] highlight the importance of examining sociodemographic variables, such as gender and age, in the study of procrastination, given the statistical significance of these variables in their results across various cultural settings. This phenomenon results in significant educational, personal, and social repercussions, underscoring the importance of research in this field.

This study is necessary as it underscores the theoretical and methodological value of addressing the dynamics of the mentioned variables within the framework of human motivation, as well as utilizing multivariate analysis techniques, which are crucial for understanding these phenomena and designing effective intervention strategies. Therefore, the research question was: How do dimensions of motivation and sociodemographic variables contribute to explaining academic procrastination among university students in the context of Ecuador?

To address this question, it is essential to identify the associated factors. In this context, the main objective is to explain the levels of academic procrastination based on the independent variables: total motivation level, intrinsic motivation level, extrinsic motivation level, amotivation level, and sociodemographic variables. Based on prior research and univariate associations, the research hypothesis (H1) was that levels of academic procrastination are explained by the level of intrinsic motivation, the level of extrinsic motivation, and the level of amotivation. The null hypothesis was that levels of academic procrastination are not explained by the level of intrinsic motivation, the level of extrinsic motivation, and the level of amotivation.

Method

The research adopted a quantitative approach, characterized by a non-experimental cross-sectional design with an explanatory scope. This choice was made to address the underlying causes of the phenomenon and the conditions in which it manifests itself [58].

Participants

The population consisted of 166 students in the third semester of the Faculty of Psychology at the University of Cuenca in Ecuador. The sample size was calculated, as suggested by Althubaiti [59], based on the type of logistic regression analysis, using the following parameters: Effect size f2 = 0.13, α error probability = 0.05, Power (1-β probability) = 0.90, number of tested predictors = 3, and total number of predictors = 3. Consequently, the total sample size was 114, with an actual power of 0.90. We used the G∗Power calculator. The initial sample size was 117 to account for potential data cleaning, but this was not necessary, so we worked with this number of responses.

A total of 117 students (75.2 % female and 24.8 % male), aged between 18 and 21 years (M = 18.68, SD = .7), participated in the study. Fifty-seven point three percent reported being in a relationship, while 42.7 % did not have a partner. Regarding employment status, 18.8 % were both working and studying, and 3.4 % were parents. Additionally, 23.9 % reported a low socioeconomic level, 68.4 % reported a medium level, and 7.7 % reported a high level. It should be noted that participants studying more than one career (2) and those who reported exceptional situations one month before data collection, such as financial crises (1) and bereavement (2), which could influence motivation and academic procrastination levels, were excluded.

Instruments

Questionnaire of sociodemographic variables. A specific questionnaire was designed to collect data on sociodemographic variables. The following variables were included: age, sex, employment status, socioeconomic perception, relationship status, and presence of children.

Academic motivation. To assess academic motivation, the Academic Motivation Scale (AMS) developed by Vallerand and Blanchard [13] was employed. This scale captures various motivators driving university students and consists of seven dimensions grouped into three broader categories: Intrinsic Motivation (IM), Extrinsic Motivation (EM), and Amotivation (AM). The dimensions within these categories include motivation for knowledge, achievement, stimulating experiences, external regulation, introjected regulation, and identified regulation, reflecting demotivation. Additionally, the scale provides a total score for total academic motivation (TAM).

The Spanish adaptation was conducted by Núñez et al. [60], and for this research, the Ecuadorian adaptation was utilized, along with the scales provided by Medina [26] who reported Cronbach's alpha coefficients of α = .94 for IM, α = .92 for EM, α = .91 for AM, and α = .90 for TAM. This author reported that the internal structure of the instrument consisted of three dimensions: IM, EM, and AM. In the context of this study, Cronbach's alpha coefficients of α = .88 for IM, α = .90 for EM, α = .83 for AM, and α = .90 for TAM were found. McDonald's omega coefficients were similar: IM = .88, EM = .91, AM = .82. The scale comprises 28 items rated on a Likert-type scale ranging from 1 = Not at all to 7 = Completely. For interpretation of the dimensions IM (items 2, 4, 6, 9, 11, 13, 16, 18, 20, 23, 25, 27), EM (items 1, 3, 7, 8, 10, 14, 15, 17, 21, 22, 24, 28), Amotivation (items 5, 12, 19, 26) and TAM, the scores of the items are summed and categorized into levels according to the detail in Table 1.

Academic procrastination. The Academic Procrastination Scale (APS), developed by Busko [61], was utilized to evaluate the tendency toward procrastination in the academic environment. The version translated and adapted to the Peruvian context by Álvarez [25] was employed, consisting of 16 items grouped into a single total dimension. This format mirrors the original scale, without considering the dimensions of Academic Self-Regulation and Procrastination of Activities. The Peruvian author reported an internal consistency with a Cronbach's alpha coefficient of α = .80, while in the internal consistency analysis conducted in this research, a coefficient of α = .71 was obtained, however, McDonald's Omega could not be calculated. The items are scored using a Likert-type scale, with 1 = Never and 5 = Always. The total score is obtained by adding the items, and the following total levels are established: Low, 16 to 34; Medium, 35 to 56; High, 57 to 80 academic procrastination (PA; [62]). It is important to note that a higher score indicates a greater propensity toward academic procrastination.

Procedure

Initially, a letter of interest was requested from the School of Psychology to participate in the research. Subsequently, a research protocol was submitted to the Human Research Ethics Committee (CEISH) of the University of Cuenca for approval and subsequent execution. Finally, each of the selected participants was contacted, informed of the purpose and nature of the study, and written informed consent was obtained. The entire process was carried out following the general ethical principles established by the American Psychological Association [63], ensuring confidentiality, respect, voluntariness, integrity, and dignity of the participants. For data collection, the instruments were transcribed verbatim into the Google Forms application. Students accessed the forms through a provided link, which were administered in the classroom by two researchers, they were not incentivized to complete the forms.

Students accessed the forms through a provided link and were administered in the classroom by two researchers deleted, following a predefined order: Sociodemographic Variables Questionnaire, Academic Motivation Scale, and Academic Procrastination Scale. The approximate duration was 15 min per participant. The data obtained were coded and prepared for analysis using Microsoft Excel 2019 version 17.0 software.

Data analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS version 28.0.1 statistical software. Univariate descriptive statistics were calculated, including measures of central tendency (arithmetic mean) and measures of absolute dispersion (minimum, maximum, and standard deviation). Next, correlations between the independent variables and the dependent variable, as well as among the independent variables themselves, were examined. To assess the normality of the dependent variable (academic procrastination) and independent variables (level of intrinsic motivation, level of extrinsic motivation, and level of amotivation), the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was utilized, with a significance level set at 5 %. Given that only one variable exhibited a normal distribution (academic procrastination p = .200), Spearman's Rho nonparametric correlation, with a significance level of 5 %, was chosen to analyze the correlations. Following this, a logistic regression analysis with ordinal response was performed. In this analysis, the dependent variable comprised the levels of procrastination (low, medium, and high), while the independent variables included the level of intrinsic motivation, the level of extrinsic motivation, and the level of amotivation (categorized as low, medium, and high).

The selection of the logistic regression model with ordinal response was motivated by its applicability when the dependent variable is categorical (ordinal), meaning its categories possess a natural order, such as low, medium, and high levels. This analytical approach aims to model the cumulative probability up to each category, given their ordered nature, employing a generalized linear model with a logit link function. Each equation in this model compares the probability of belonging to a specific category or those with a code lower than it, against the probability of belonging to categories with codes higher than it [64,65]. Furthermore, the internal consistency of the instruments used to measure the attributes was assessed through Cronbach's alpha coefficient to ensure reliability [66]. This method yields more robust results, especially for instruments with Likert-type responses featuring five or more categories and when an adequate number of participants are involved [67].

Regarding statistically significant correlations, moderate negative correlations were observed between academic procrastination and intrinsic motivation, extrinsic motivation, and total academic motivation. Conversely, a strong positive correlation was noted between extrinsic motivation and intrinsic motivation, as well as between these two factors and total academic motivation. Similarly, a weak negative correlation was found between amotivation and intrinsic motivation and extrinsic motivation. Notably, no statistically significant correlations were identified between the other analyzed variables.

Preliminary analysis of the variables contemplated for the model

Initially, an ordinal regression analysis was conducted, considering the total level of academic motivation, the level of extrinsic motivation, the level of intrinsic motivation, and the level of amotivation as independent variables. However, this model did not demonstrate a good fit, and a similar outcome was observed when incorporating the proposed sociodemographic variables as covariates. Therefore, it was determined to simplify the model by focusing solely on the independent variables: the level of extrinsic motivation, the level of intrinsic motivation, and the level of amotivation.

Multicollinearity was also tested, and results indicated its absence, as Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) values were below the threshold of 5: intrinsic motivation level = 1.38, extrinsic motivation level = 1.35, and amotivation = 1.06.

Discussion

The aim of this research, in line with previous studies, was to elucidate the levels of academic procrastination among university students of the School of Psychology based on academic motivation, its dimensions, and sociodemographic variables (independent variables). The research hypothesis was validated, as the dimensions of academic motivation confirmed the model, resulting in a 19 % reduction in the adjustment. It should be emphasized that only intrinsic motivation showed statistical significance in explaining academic procrastination. Additionally, sociodemographic variables or total academic motivation were excluded due to preliminary analyses.

A predominance of high levels of intrinsic and extrinsic motivation was observed, along with a low level of amotivation, corresponding to a high-level academic motivation profile as proposed by Güdül et al. [52]. However, when applying the corresponding scale, a medium level of total academic motivation was identified, consistent with other studies in populations with similar characteristics [26,68]. Similarly, the average level of academic procrastination was higher in these students. Although studies in the Peruvian population tend to report high levels [69,70] and other studies indicate an incidence exceeding 60 % [12,35], this result supporting the high prevalence of academic procrastination among university students. Despite the fact that in collectivist societies, such as Ecuador, where individuals tend to feel perceived and judged as integral members of a collective [71], this may contribute to the lower levels of procrastination observed in the Honduran population compared to the Spanish population in the study by Hidalgo et al. [72].

Furthermore, the study has revealed moderate negative relationships between intrinsic motivation, extrinsic motivation, and total academic motivation with academic procrastination in this population. These findings are consistent with previous research [38,42,47,55,73]. Furthermore, they have been evidenced across different cultural contexts, as seen in studies conducted in multiple countries by Pelikan et al. [6] and Reci and Hamzallari [74]. Leading to the conclusion that academic motivation predicts academic procrastination. It is explicitly noted that the higher the level of the former, the lower the latter [43,52]. Moreover, both are significant predictors of academic performance [47].

Regarding the dimensions of academic motivation, there is no clear consensus on the explanatory relationship of each of them. While Yurtseven et al. [39], Oram and Rogers [42] and Cerino [55] find a moderate-weak positive relationship with amotivation, surprisingly, the results of this study do not agree. This dimension is characterized by a low intention to act, an absence of self-determination, a lack of connection between actions and outcomes, and low attention to internal or external rewards [75,76]. Therefore, it is crucial to take it into account and manage it [49].

The literature presents conflicting views regarding the negative relationship between extrinsic motivation and academic procrastination. While this study and others [52,55] support this relationship, several studies do not corroborate it [42]. Even the model proposed by Cavusoglu and Karatas [41] from the SDT suggests eliminating extrinsic motivation due to its lack of statistical significance, arguing that it only indirectly affects academic procrastination through intrinsic motivation. This perspective aligns with the findings of this study, which did not find statistical significance for this variable. Additionally, Brownlow and Reasinger [77] suggested that intrinsic motivation better inhibits academic procrastination behaviors than extrinsic motivation, as it regulates behavior through the activity itself [78], leading to more satisfactory outcomes [79].

All the reviewed studies confirmed moderate to strong negative relationships between intrinsic motivation and academic procrastination, except for the study by Yurtseven and Doğan [39], which stands out as the most comprehensive in explaining this relationship [30,49,54,55]. Additionally, Lee [80] asserts that intrinsic motivation has unique effects that surpass cognitive variables such as perfectionism [38] or life satisfaction [52], as well as satisfaction of basic needs [42].

Conversely, sociodemographic variables showed no significant relationships, consistent with findings from other studies [40,48]. It is noteworthy that in most research, sociodemographic variables are often not analyzed, suggesting that intrinsic motivation is typically better explained by internal factors rather than external or contextual ones. However, these results contradict many other studies that have found statistically significant relationships with variables such as gender, particularly the meta-analysis by Yu and Deng [57], and age in different contexts [6,56,74].

In conclusion, this study added that various motivational forces, particularly intrinsic motivation, play a significant role in predicting and explaining academic procrastination by influencing individuals' attitudes towards learning. This underscores the importance of considering students' expectations, aspirations, and desires for self-improvement in understanding procrastination behavior [20,68]. The model proposed by Senécal et al. [30] highlights the role of self-regulation, which explains 25 % of procrastination, aligning with the principles of SDT. This suggests that internal regulation enables greater autonomy in academic activities when individuals have internalized and accepted the value of a task [7].

Regarding the practical implications, in light of these results, universities can play a key role in reducing procrastination by recognizing the importance of intrinsic motivation and promoting it through various strategies. As noted by van Eerde and Klingsieck [81] in their meta-analysis, cognitive-behavioral programs consistently reduce procrastination over time and improve students' mental health.

In light of the study's limitations, there are several areas for improvement. Firstly, increasing the number of participants could lead to more balanced comparisons and potentially reveal significant relationships regarding sociodemographic variables. Additionally, the cross-sectional design employed in this study prevents the establishment of causal inferences. Future research utilizing longitudinal designs could measure the effects of variables throughout the teaching-learning process [12], allowing for comparisons between data collected at the beginning and end of the academic period to track progress and changes.

Regarding validity, the study could benefit from incorporating additional instruments that measure related but distinct constructs to establish convergent and divergent validity criteria. Moreover, it is important to acknowledge the limitations inherent in self-report measures. The use of the Peruvian adaptation of the motivation scale in the Ecuadorian population, while justified, emphasizes the need for an Ecuadorian specific adaptation of the original instrument to ensure cultural and contextual relevance. Finally, the lack of consensus on which procrastination scale to use underscores the importance of selecting a scale that best fits the research context and objectives. Most use the scale developed by Tuckman [82] or the one created by Solomon and Rothblum [83], which are recommended for use.

Despite the aforementioned limitations, the findings carry significant practical implications regarding academic procrastination, particularly concerning academic motivational factors which address cognitive and emotional aspects. Among these factors, intrinsic motivation emerges as the most consistent in explaining procrastination. Therefore, it is imperative to bolster these motivational aspects to foster learning and mitigate the adverse consequences associated with academic procrastination, particularly on students' mental health. The percentage of females is much larger, which raises questions about the generalizability or extrapolation of the data, especially considering the small sample of 117 participants.

Future research should focus on exploring how cognitive-affective factors may influence academic motivation [49], with a particular emphasis on self-regulation, self-efficacy, and self-esteem over external reinforcement [84]. Malkoç and Mutlu [43] model suggests that academic motivation and self-efficacy together explain approximately 25 % of the variance in levels of academic procrastination. Additionally, Yurtseven and Doğan [39] propose that problem-solving ability could be an important precursor factor, while Moreta and Durán [44] highlight the role of academic self-regulation, restructuring, and cognitive reevaluation in feedback mechanisms shaping academic motivation. Furthermore, it is important to consider emotional variables such as depression and anxiety, which can modify motivation and increase procrastination [30,47]. Froment and de-Besa Gutiérrez [12] also suggest that teacher credibility may directly influence academic motivation.
