Impact of opioid use disorder as a safety risk in elective total hip arthroplasty: an imperative for routine preoperative screening
Authors: David Maman, Yaniv Steinfeld, Yaron Berkovich
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTotal hip arthroplasty (THA) utilization is rising worldwide, but the concurrent opioid epidemic has introduced new perioperative safety challenges. Opioid use disorder (OUD) is a chronic relapsing condition that may complicate surgical management and increase preventable adverse events.MethodsDuring the study period from January 1, 2016 to December 31, 2022, a total of 1,957,284 elective primary THA cases were identified in the National Inpatient Sample. Of these, 12,285 (0.6%) had a documented diagnosis of opioid use disorder (OUD). After 1:1 propensity score matching, 12,285 OUD cases were matched to 12,285 non-OUD controls with comparable baseline characteristics.ResultsPatients with OUD had significantly longer mean length of stay (p < 0.001), higher hospital charges (approximately 15% higher, p < 0.001), and increased rates of in-hospital postoperative complications compared with matched controls.ConclusionsPatients with OUD undergoing THA experience significantly worse inpatient outcomes and higher costs. These findings highlight OUD as a modifiable surgical-safety risk factor and support the implementation of routine preoperative OUD screening and optimization protocols to improve patient safety and resource utilization.

Introduction

Total hip arthroplasty (THA) is among the most frequently performed orthopedic procedures, with demand projected to increase dramatically in coming decades [1]. Advances in implant design and perioperative care have made THA highly successful for end-stage hip arthritis, yet substantial postoperative pain is common and often managed with opioids [2, 3]. In the context of the ongoing opioid epidemic, this reliance on opioid analgesia has raised concerns about patients who have opioid use disorder (OUD) at the time of surgery. The United States continues to grapple with an opioid crisis that was declared a public health emergency in 2017 [4, 5]. OUD is defined by DSM-5 criteria as a problematic pattern of opioid use leading to significant impairment or distress, encompassing what was previously termed opioid abuse or dependence [4].

Approximately 1% of the U.S. population has OUD in recent years, a figure that has trended upward in parallel with rising availability of prescription and illicit opioids [6, 7]. Among surgical patients, even those without formal OUD who use opioids preoperatively are known to have higher risks of perioperative complications and worse outcomes [8–11]. Patients with established OUD represent an extreme on this spectrum of opioid exposure, often exhibiting tolerance, opioid-induced hyperalgesia, and endocrine or other comorbid complications that can complicate surgical care [3, 4, 12]. THA candidates with OUD pose unique challenges; they tend to be younger and have a higher burden of medical and psychosocial comorbidities compared to other arthroplasty patients [11, 13].

Importantly, routine, standardized preoperative screening for OUD is not universally implemented in orthopedic pathways, increasing the risk that OUD goes unrecognized if patients are not forthcoming and leading to an incomplete understanding of perioperative risk [3, 4]. Prior studies suggest that OUD or preoperative opioid exposure while relatively uncommon among arthroplasty patients has been increasing and is associated with higher complication rates, longer hospital stays, and greater costs [9–12]. However, the impact of OUD specifically in the THA population remains under-examined, as prior research often combined hip and knee arthroplasty or focused on TKA [8–12]. Furthermore, to our knowledge, no published studies have directly tested whether routine preoperative identification of OUD mitigates these risks or quantified the cost-benefit of implementing universal OUD screening prior to arthroplasty [3–5]. In light of the growing burden of OUD and the critical importance of optimizing patients before elective surgery, we undertook a comprehensive analysis of outcomes of primary THA in patients with OUD. We hypothesized that OUD would be an independent predictor of poorer inpatient outcomes. We also sought to discuss the implications of these findings for preoperative practice, particularly the potential value of universal OUD screening as a perioperative safety measure and targeted interventions.

Methods

We queried the Nationwide Inpatient Sample (NIS) for all eligible admissions between January 1, 2016 and December 31, 2022. Our a priori hypothesis was that opioid use disorder would be independently associated with worse inpatient outcomes following elective total hip arthroplasty. The NIS is a publicly available, all-payer hospitalization database that approximates a 20% stratified sample of U.S. inpatient stays, and it is the largest inpatient database in the United States. Each discharge in the NIS is weighted to enable national estimates, and all analyses in this study applied the designated discharge weights to produce nationally representative results. This study was deemed exempt from institutional review board approval due to the use of de-identified, publicly available data.

We identified all adult patients (age ≥ 18 years) who underwent primary total hip arthroplasty between January 1, 2016 and December 31, 2022. THA cases were extracted using the appropriate ICD-10-PCS procedure codes for primary total hip replacement. We excluded any admissions that were coded as non-elective or emergency, to focus on elective primary arthroplasties. We also selected only THA cases do to OA based on ICD10 codes. Patients with pathological hip fractures, malignant tumors, or other non-degenerative indications for THA were excluded if identified, to create a more homogeneous elective osteoarthritis cohort. Within this THA cohort, we defined exposure to opioid use disorder using ICD-10-CM diagnosis codes. OUD was identified by the presence of diagnostic code F11.x. We did not differentiate sub-classifications of F11 (e.g., F11.1x for abuse, F11.2x for dependence) because these subcategories were infrequently specified and collectively represented a very small fraction of cases. All Covid-19 cases were excluded,

Given the observational design, we employed propensity score matching to balance baseline characteristics between the OUD and non-OUD groups and mitigate confounding. The propensity score represented the probability of having OUD, conditional on observed covariates. We calculated the score for each patient via a logistic regression model including demographics and comorbidities. Covariates for matching included age, sex, race, primary payer (Medicare, Medicaid, private, self-pay), socioeconomic status (median income quartile by ZIP code), and a range of comorbid conditions.

Each OUD patient was matched to one non-OUD patient using nearest-neighbor matching without replacement (1:1 match). A caliper of 0.1 standard deviations of the propensity logit was used to ensure close matches, and matching was performed without regard to outcome variables. The quality of matching was evaluated by comparing baseline characteristics between groups; standardized mean differences < 0.1 were deemed indicative of good balance. After matching, the cohorts of OUD and non-OUD patients were carried forward for outcome analysis.

This study adheres to STROBE guidelines for observational safety research using administrative databases.

The primary outcome was length of stay (LOS). Secondary outcomes included total hospital charges and in-hospital postoperative complications. Patient and hospital characteristics and annual trends (2016–2022) were evaluated descriptively but were not considered outcome measures.

We first described the unadjusted characteristics of OUD vs. non-OUD THA patients prior to matching, comparing variables with chi-square tests for categorical data and t-tests for continuous data. After propensity matching, we assessed covariate balance and then compared outcomes between the matched cohorts.

Categorical outcomes (complication rates, discharge disposition) were compared using chi-square analysis on matched pairs. A two-tailed p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all comparisons.

As detailed in Table 1, out of 1,957,284 individuals who underwent THA, 12,285 (0.6%) had a documented diagnosis of OUD. Compared to those without OUD, these patients were notably younger (mean age 60.9 vs. 66.2 years, P < 0.01) and exhibited a distinct comorbidity burden.

Patients in the OUD group demonstrated significantly higher prevalence of obesity (30.7% vs. 24.4%, P < 0.01), chronic pulmonary disease (16.2% vs. 6.5%, P < 0.01), renal insufficiency (9.8% vs. 6.9%, P < 0.01), liver pathology (3.2% vs. 1.2%, P < 0.01), and anemia (7.8% vs. 5.6%, P < 0.01). They also showed increased rates of osteoporosis (5.5% vs. 4.6%, P < 0.01), cerebrovascular disease (4.8% vs. 3.9%, P < 0.01), and congestive heart failure (1.7% vs. 1.2%, P < 0.01).

In contrast, the OUD cohort had lower representation of females (53.9% vs. 56.3%, P < 0.01), as well as reduced rates of hypertension (50.8% vs. 52.5%, P < 0.01) and dyslipidemia (37.2% vs. 43.7%, P < 0.01). There were no statistically significant differences observed between groups in the prevalence of type 2 diabetes, thyroid disorders, or prior myocardial infarction.

Following 1:1 propensity score matching, patients with OUD experience significantly higher rates of several perioperative complications compared to matched controls, as shown in Table 5. Blood loss anemia (25.7% vs. 24.2%, P < 0.01), blood transfusion (5.0% vs. 3.5%, P < 0.01), and acute kidney injury (3.4% vs. 2.2%, P < 0.01) were all more common in the OUD group.

Postoperative pain diagnoses were markedly higher in patients with OUD (6.3% vs. 2.0%, P < 0.01), as were intraoperative fractures (1.5% vs. 0.7%, P < 0.01) and hip dislocations (0.2% vs. 0.1%, P = 0.03). While prolonged mechanical ventilation (> 24 h) was observed more frequently in the OUD group (0.2% vs. 0.1%), this difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.07).

Based on our findings, the presence of OUD is associated with an average increase of $14,445 in hospital charges per THA case ($77,330 vs. $62,885). Although OUD was present in only 0.6% of patients, this small subgroup contributes disproportionately to overall costs.

Assuming universal preoperative OUD screening using a urine drug test (~$75 per patient), the cost to screen 100,000 patients would be approximately $7.5 million. If this strategy identified and allowed optimization of care for the 600 patients (0.6%) with OUD, it could potentially avoid up to $8.67 million in excess hospital charges (600 × $14,445), yielding net savings of over $1 million, not including potential reductions in complications and downstream utilization.

Discussion

This nationwide matched analysis of elective THA patients demonstrates that a diagnosis of OUD is significantly associated with worse inpatient outcomes and increased healthcare costs. Even after rigorous propensity score matching, OUD remained an independent predictor of higher rates of complications, prolonged LOS, and greater hospital charges. These findings have direct clinical and policy implications as OUD continues to rise among surgical candidates [4, 13].

These findings underscore the patient-safety dimension of OUD, a risk factor that is frequently modifiable through preoperative identification and coordinated perioperative care.

Clinical impact of OUD on THA outcomes

The observed increase in OUD prevalence among THA patients from 0.53% in 2016 to 0.97% in 2022 reflects the broader trajectory of the opioid crisis [14, 15]. Although OUD patients represented only 0.6% of all THA cases, they accounted for a disproportionate burden of complications and costs [16, 17]. After matching, patients with OUD experienced significantly more blood loss anemia, blood transfusions, acute kidney injury, and intraoperative fractures complications consistent with previous literature showing increased risks in opioid-exposed arthroplasty patients [11, 18, 19]. Notably, the diagnosis of postoperative pain an inherently subjective outcome was over three times higher in the OUD group, underscoring the complex pain profiles and altered pain perception in this population [20, 21].

These results are clinically consistent with known pathophysiology. Chronic opioid use is associated with opioid-induced hyperalgesia, impaired immune function, endocrine disturbances, and poor bone quality [22–24], all of which can increase perioperative risks. From a surgical standpoint, these vulnerabilities necessitate proactive, individualized perioperative strategies, including careful fluid management, bone health optimization, and multimodal analgesia protocols that minimize opioid exposure [25].

Our findings should also be interpreted in the context of prior work on preoperative opioid exposure in arthroplasty. Not all studies have demonstrated uniformly worse outcomes. For example, Huang et al. reported no significant association between preoperative opioid use and short-term outcomes in an Australian cohort undergoing primary hip and knee arthroplasty, within a different health-care system and using prescription-based definitions of exposure rather than diagnosed OUD [26]. In contrast, other studies have shown increased risks associated with preoperative opioid use, including higher complication rates and early revision after THA and revision THA [16, 27, 28]. Differences in exposure definitions (diagnosed OUD versus any preoperative opioid use), procedure type (primary versus revision THA), outcome measures (patient-reported outcomes versus in-hospital complications and early revision), and health-system context likely explain some of the heterogeneity across studies.

Perioperative safety opportunity: preoperative screening

Despite these risks, OUD is frequently underrecognized in the orthopedic setting [29]. Our study excluded all emergency and revision THAs, as well as COVID-19-related admissions [30], focusing exclusively on elective THA for osteoarthritis [31]. This homogeneity reinforces the strength of our findings and highlights that even in optimized scenarios, OUD remains a substantial risk factor.

Currently, no formal requirement exists for routine OUD screening during orthopedic preoperative evaluations. As a result, many patients with clinically relevant opioid dependence may undergo THA without the surgical team being fully aware of their risk profile [32]. Incorporating systematic screening using validated tools or urine drug testing - could allow for early identification, referral to addiction specialists, and multidisciplinary optimization prior to surgery [33, 34].

Economic considerations and cost-effectiveness

From a surgical-safety and systems-efficiency perspective, the economic burden of OUD in THA is striking. The economic burden of OUD in THA is striking. In our matched cohort, OUD was associated with an additional $14,445 in mean hospital charges per patient [11]. Given a prevalence of 0.6%, the excess cost attributable to OUD is substantial even at the population level [35]. A simple screening strategy such as a $75 urine drug test could feasibly identify patients at risk and facilitate targeted interventions [4]. In our modeled scenario, universal screening in 100,000 THA candidates could yield net savings exceeding $1 million, not accounting for reduced complications, readmissions, or downstream care [36]. This supports the rationale for including OUD screening in value-based surgical pathways [37].

Clinical implications for surgical safety

Incorporating OUD screening into routine preoperative evaluation can enhance surgical safety by identifying high-risk patients before anesthesia induction. A standardized protocol involving anesthesia, orthopedics, and pain specialists could mitigate preventable complications, reduce costs, and improve postoperative recovery.

Limitations

This study has limitations inherent to administrative database research. Diagnosis coding accuracy may vary [38, 39], and granular data such as opioid dosage, duration, and route of administration were unavailable. Furthermore, ICD-10 codes do not reliably distinguish between OUD subtypes (e.g., dependence vs. misuse), nor do they capture patients with undiagnosed or undisclosed opioid use [40]. Importantly, the NIS includes only in-hospital data; long-term outcomes, including reoperation or readmission, were not evaluated [41].

Despite these limitations, our study leveraged a large, nationally representative dataset and employed robust matching techniques to minimize confounding [42]. The exclusion of COVID-related and non-elective cases further strengthens internal validity and relevance to elective arthroplasty practice [43].

Conclusion

Patients with OUD undergoing elective primary THA face measurably worse short-term outcomes, including higher rates of complications, longer hospital stays, and greater costs. These findings identify OUD as a preventable perioperative safety hazard and underscore the importance of integrating systematic OUD screening and optimization into standard preoperative workflows.
