Fast ℓ 1 \ell_{1} -Regularized EEG Source Localization Using Variable Projection
Jack Michael Solomon, Rosemary Anne Renaut, Matthias Chung

Abstract
Electroencephalograms (EEG) are invaluable for treating neurological disorders, however, mapping EEG electrode readings to brain activity requires solving a challenging inverse problem. Due to the time series data, the use of ℓ 1 \ell_{1} regularization quickly becomes intractable for many solvers, and, despite the reconstruction advantages of ℓ 1 \ell_{1} regularization, ℓ 2 \ell_{2} -based approaches such as sLORETA are used in practice. In this work, we formulate EEG source localization as a graphical generalized elastic net inverse problem and present a variable projected algorithm ( VPAL ) suitable for fast EEG source localization. We prove convergence of this solver for a broad class of separable convex, potentially non-smooth functions subject to linear constraints and include a modification of VPAL that reconstructs time points in sequence, suitable for real-time reconstruction. Our proposed methods are compared to state-of-the-art approaches including sLORETA and other methods for ℓ 1 \ell_{1} -regularized inverse problems.

Introduction

An electroencephalogram (EEG) is a well-established diagnostic tool for recording brain activity and detecting potential neurological disorders. The EEG is widely used in the diagnosis of neurological disorders such as epilepsy and the treatment of traumatic brain events such as strokes or injuries. In clinical settings, EEGs have several advantages over other brain imaging techniques–they are inexpensive, non-invasive, and directly measure neuron activity on the brain’s cortical surface
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
.

An array of
p
electrodes on the scalp measures electrical signals produced by neuron activity. The forward process, which describes the causal relationship between current densities on the cortical surface of the brain and voltage detected by the electrodes, is modeled by the
lead field matrix

\mathbf{L}
. Assuming that the dipole orientation at each source location is perpendicular to the surface of the brain, a scalar can represent the density at each source location, and thus
\mathbf{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times n}
with
n
denoting the number of source locations. Hence, each column of
\mathbf{L}
maps the
current density
at time
t
, of
source locations

\mathbf{x}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
to the voltage
\mathbf{b}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
measured by the electrodes on the scalp
[
4
,
5
,
6
]
. We model the source locations on the cortical surface as vertices of a surface mesh and consider temporal dynamics. That is, at a fixed time
t
, we may describe the relationship by

\mathbf{b}(t)=\mathbf{L}\mathbf{x}(t)+\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\varepsilon}$}(t),

(1)

where
\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\varepsilon}$}(t)
describes additive noise in the measuring process. The resolution of the cortical surface mesh depends on
n
. For clinical use, it is necessary to infer which regions of the cortical surface are active from the electrode readings, and thus an
inverse problem
must be solved. Moreover,
real-time
reconstruction of high-resolution neural brain activity is desirable.

For simplicity of notation, we assume an EEG records time dynamic data
\mathbf{b}(t)
at discrete time points
t=1,\ldots,T
. In short we write
\mathbf{B}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{b}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{b}_{T}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times T}
.
Inference of the electrical activity at each source location for each time point,
t
, is commonly referred to as
source localization
and we represent these values by
\mathbf{X}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{x}_{T}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times T}
. The underlying
inverse problem
is formulated as: given observations
\mathbf{B}
, lead field matrix
\mathbf{L}
and assumption on the noise distribution
\text{\boldmath$\mathbf{\varepsilon}$}(t)
, reconstruct the temporal brain activity on the cortical mesh
\mathbf{X}
. As a variational inverse problem, this can be formulated, typically under Gaussian noise assumptions, as a least-squares problem

\displaystyle\argmin_{\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times T}}\ \sum_{i=1}^{T}\left\|\mathbf{L}\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{b}_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\argmin_{\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times T}}\left\|\mathbf{L}\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{B}\right\|_{{\rm F}}^{2},

(2)

where
\left\|\,\cdot\,\right\|_{2}
is defined as the
2
-norm and
\left\|\,\cdot\,\right\|_{{\rm F}}
denotes the Frobenius norm.
Equation

2
does not consider a time-dependent dynamic solution and each
\mathbf{x}_{i}
may be solved independently.

Typically, this inverse problem is significantly underdetermined because the number of locations,
n
, at which estimates of the activity are required, far exceeds the number of EEG electrodes, i.e.,
p\ll n
. At the same time, noise contributions are significant
[
7
,
8
]
. Regularization is required to stabilize the inverse solution. Thus, we consider solving the
regularized
inverse problem

\displaystyle\argmin_{\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times T}}\left\|\mathbf{L}\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{B}\right\|_{{\rm F}}^{2}+\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{X}),

(3)

where
\mathcal{R}\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
encodes prior knowledge as a regularizer,
[
9
]
.
Proposed methods for solving this inverse problem largely vary in how prior information is included through the regularization term. One common choice is Tikhonov regularization i.e.,
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{X})=\mu\|\mathbf{X}\|_{\rm F}^{2}

[
9
]
. This serves as the foundation for
standardized low-resolution brain electromagnetic tomography
(
sLORETA
), where the inverse problem is solved using matrix pseudo inverses, and is a standard choice in practice
[
10
]
.

Although these straightforward
\ell_{2}
-regularized approaches are very fast, they frequently overestimate the extent of activated regions
[
11
,
12
,
13
]
. To address these limitations, and to incorporate the neurological understanding that realistic brain activity would be localized to only a few locations
[
3
]
,
\ell_{1}
-based priors were introduced promoting sparsity on the reconstructed meshes.
Setting
\mathcal{R}(\mathbf{X})=\left\|\mathbf{X}\right\|_{1,1}
, where
\left\|\mathbf{C}\right\|_{1,1}=\left\|\mathbf{c}\right\|_{1}
,
\mathbf{c}={\rm vec}(\mathbf{C})
denotes the vectorized form of matrix
\mathbf{C}
and
\left\|\,\cdot\,\right\|_{1}
is the vector 1-norm, is commonly referred to as
LASSO regression

[
14
]
. Due to the separability of the
\ell_{1}
-norm, each time point remains independent of other time points. Thus, while effective in constructing sparse signals, an
\ell_{1}
-regularization term alone fails to capture the time dynamics of the data in EEG source localization
[
13
,
15
]
.

Other approaches have leveraged combinations of
\ell_{1}
- and
\ell_{2}
-norms to recover sparse reconstructions, while also enforcing smoothness in the reconstructed signal over time
[
16
,
13
,
17
,
18
,
19
]
. Models that use a linear combination of

\ell_{1}
- and
\ell_{2}
-regularization terms are often referred to as
elastic net
regularization
[
20
]
.
Generalized elastic net
regularized models are elastic net regularized models that include operators in the
\ell_{1}
- and
\ell_{2}
-terms. The discretized
total-variation
operator (
TV
), which approximates the derivative at each entry of the vector via a finite difference approximation, is a common choice
[
21
]
. In imaging, this computes the sum of the absolute differences between each pixel and its adjacent pixels. Pairing this with an
\ell_{1}
-norm often reduces noise in the reconstruction and maintains sharp edges
[
22
]
. In this work, we frame the EEG source localization problem as a generalized-elastic net regularized optimization problem and use total variation operators for both the spatial reconstruction and the time dynamics of the signal, hence removing the time-independence of each
\mathbf{x}_{i}
.

Approaches and Challenges.
Despite the observed advantages of elastic net approaches, corresponding algorithms are typically computationally expensive preventing real-time reconstructions due to the non-smooth
\ell_{1}
term. Solving the inverse problem is further challenging when working with dynamic datasets and high-resolution meshes since this results in data too large for
\ell_{1}
solvers to handle
[
23
,
24
]
–a high-resolution mesh may contain on the order of
10,\!000
nodes measured across hundreds of time points. In the context of EEG reconstruction, high-resolution meshes can be mitigated by dividing up the cortical mesh into different regions and performing reconstruction on the smaller problems. However, this may result in inaccurate artifacts on the edges of these regions. For clinical applications, an EEG algorithm that can quickly perform reconstructions, or even compute localizations in real-time is desirable. Unfortunately, due to the significant computational burden associated with elastic net approaches, reconstructions are currently confined to post-hoc analysis. Because of these challenges, computationally less demanding methods with quick turnaround are currently preferred over elastic net approaches
[
10
]
. Thus there is a need for an elastic net solver capable of handling larger data efficiently.

Contributions.
Our work has several key contributions:
Our first contribution is phrasing the EEG problem as a large-scale variational inverse problem regularized by graph total variation. This extends existing approaches and enables high-resolution inversion
[
21
]
. To address this large-scale inverse problem, we introduce a time-dynamic
variable-projected augmented Lagrangian
(
VPAL
) method. This method builds upon the approaches in
[
24
]
, which are limited by specific gradient updates. Our method offers greater flexibility in the update steps, leading to improved computational efficiency and outperforming existing state-of-the-art approaches. We present a novel convergence proof for the
VPAL
approach on a non-restrictive class of functions, applicable to broader settings including nonlinear forward models. For EEG applications, we introduce a sequential version of our
VPAL
-based method, where reconstruction at each time point is accelerated by using information from the previous time point and is suitable for near real-time data reconstruction. Numerical comparisons against existing state-of-the-art methods, such as
sLORETA
, are also provided illustrating the benefits of our approach.

Background and Problem Setup

We consider a general elastic net model with an
\ell_{1}
regularization term on the spatial data and an
\ell_{2}
regularization term for temporal data. This model is founded on the common assumption that a sparse set of active brain regions generates the measured electrode signals and noise contributions are significant
[
3
]
. Sparsity in the total variation of mesh values is imposed to suppress non-pertinent sharp changes in activation values, thereby isolating significant signals and mitigating noise. Time regularization on the other hand intends to reduce noise and enforce smooth changes in activation over time, based on the assumption that changes in the activation values are smooth in time assuming a fine enough discretization of time points. An
\ell_{2}
term is therefore suitable for time regularization due to the smoothing effect it will have on the result
[
26
]
. For the remainder of this paper, sparsity is used to refer to sparsity in the total variation of the mesh as opposed to the number of non-zero entries of the mesh.

Let the mesh of the cortical surface be represented as a temporal graph network consisting of
n
nodes and
m
edges corresponding to the vertices and edges in the mesh respectively with the value of each node dependent on time point
t
. The resulting optimization problem can be formulated as

\argmin_{\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times T}}\quad f(\mathbf{X})=\tfrac{1}{2}\left\|\mathbf{L}\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{B}\right\|_{{\rm F}}^{2}+\tfrac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\left\|\mathbf{X}\mathbf{D}_{1}\right\|_{{\rm F}}^{2}+\mu\left\|\mathbf{D}_{2}\mathbf{X}\right\|_{1,1},

(4)

with
\lambda,\mu>0
The matrix
\mathbf{D}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times T-1}
is the finite time-difference operator and
\mathbf{D}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is the finite-difference operator
[
21
]
on the cortical mesh. Note while
Equation

2
can be formulated as
T
independent optimization problems, with the introduction of the time regularization term we established a dependence between the solutions
(\mathbf{x}_{i})_{i=1}^{T}
.

The operation with
\mathbf{D}_{1}
can be represented as

\displaystyle\mathbf{X}\mathbf{D}_{1}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}_{1}-\mathbf{x}_{2},&\mathbf{x}_{2}-\mathbf{x}_{3},&\ldots,&\mathbf{x}_{T-1}-\mathbf{x}_{T}\end{bmatrix},

(5)

where we omit the time-frequency scaling constant, noting that it is absorbed into the regularization weighting parameter
\lambda
.

The
graphTV
operator,
\mathbf{D}_{2}
, requires more attention. Using graph theory notation, the
\ell_{1}
-norm of the total variation of a graph,
G=(V,E)
, where
V
represents the vertices and
E
the edges, is given as

\displaystyle\left\|{\tt graphTV}(G)\right\|_{1}=\sum_{(u,v)\in E}|w_{(u,v)}(u.\textnormal{value}-v.\textnormal{value})|

(6)

with graph
G
either directed or undirected, and
w_{(u,v)}
the weight of the edge
(u,v)
. We may state the
graphTV
operation for the EEG problem as

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}_{2}\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{W}(\mathbf{P}_{1}\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{P}_{2}\mathbf{X}),

(7)

where
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times m}
is a diagonal matrix consisting of the weights of the edges.
Further,
\mathbf{P}_{1},\mathbf{P}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
are permutation matrices that index
\mathbf{X}
to facilitate the subtraction of each node’s value from only its immediate neighbors. This definition of
graphTV
is analogous to total variation (
TV
) for images, see
Figure

1
.

One common approach for solving elastic net regularized problems involves splitting methods
[
27
]
. These methods introduce an auxiliary variable to decouple the regularization term from the rest of the objective function. This reformulation allows the problem to be expressed as an augmented Lagrangian, which combines the original objective with a penalty term that enforces consistency between the original variable and the newly introduced surrogate variable. Then, an alternating optimization procedure is employed updating the primary variable, the auxiliary variable and the Lagrange multipliers. This procedure iteratively minimizes the augmented Lagrangian with respect to each variable while holding the other fixed. This splitting strategy often simplifies the optimization process, as each subproblem becomes easier to solve than the original, non-split problem.

The
Alternating Direction Method of Multipliers
(
ADMM
) is a standard approach to solving general elastic net regularized problems
[
23
]

ADMM
applies to separable optimization problems, subject to linear constraints i.e.,

\displaystyle\argmin_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}\;

\displaystyle q(\mathbf{x})+r(\mathbf{y})

(8)

\displaystyle\;\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{R}\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{v},

(9)

with
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{p}},\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{p}\times n_{x}},\mathbf{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{p}\times n_{y}},
and

\displaystyle h(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y};\mathbf{c})=\varphi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y};\mathbf{c})+r(\mathbf{y}),

where

denotes the Lagrange multipliers and

\mathbf{y}

\mathcal{J}_{\mathbf{Z}}(\mathbf{x})
denote the Jacobian of
\mathbf{Z}(\mathbf{x})
. Then,

\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\left(f_{\rm proj}(\mathbf{x})\right)=\mathbf{A}^{\top}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{w})+\tfrac{\eta^{2}}{2}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\left(\left\|\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_{2}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{Z}(\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{c}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)+\mu\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\left(\left\|\mathbf{Z}(\mathbf{x})\right\|_{1}\right)\\
=\mathbf{A}^{\top}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{w})+\eta^{2}(\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_{2}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_{2}\mathbf{x}+\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_{2}^{\top}\mathbf{c}-\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_{2}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}(x)+\mathcal{J}_{\mathbf{Z}}(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_{2}\mathbf{x}+\mathcal{J}_{\mathbf{Z}}(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\mathbf{Z}(\mathbf{x})+\mathcal{J}_{\mathbf{Z}}(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\mathbf{c})+\mu\,\mathcal{J}_{\mathbf{Z}}(\mathbf{x})^{\top}{\rm sign\!}\left(\mathbf{Z}(\mathbf{x})\right)\\
=\mathbf{A}^{\top}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{w})+\eta^{2}\left(\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_{2}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_{2}\mathbf{x}+\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_{2}^{\top}\mathbf{c}-\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_{2}^{\top}\mathbf{Z}(\mathbf{x})\right)+\mathcal{J}_{\mathbf{Z}}(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\left(\underbrace{\eta^{2}\left(-\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_{2}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{Z}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{c}\right)+\mu\,{\rm sign\!}\left(\mathbf{Z}(\mathbf{x})\right)}_{=\bf 0}\right)\\
=\mathbf{A}^{\top}(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{w})+\lambda^{2}\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_{2}^{\top}\left(\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_{2}\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{c}-\mathbf{Z}(\mathbf{x})\right).

(22)

Thus, away from points where
f_{\rm proj}
is not differentiable, the gradient of the projected problem is the same as if
\mathbf{Z}(\mathbf{x})
were constant in
\mathbf{x}
. That is, in the projected space of optimal
\mathbf{y}
, we need not consider the dependence of
\mathbf{y}
, and can solve this as a univariate optimization problem. The variable projected approach reflects this intuition by updating
\mathbf{x}
through a line search as if
\mathbf{y}
were constant and then updates
\mathbf{y}
to be optimal at the new location. Ignoring the non-differentiability will cause difficulties in an iterative optimization routine and more rigorous analysis is given in
Section

3.2
. The full algorithm is given in
Algorithm

2
and with appropriate assumptions, is shown to converge for the non-differentiable EEG objective function.

The work
[
24

-regularized inverse problems and focused on using the negative gradient direction for the

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{(j+1)}=\mathbf{x}^{(j)}-\alpha^{(j)}\mathbf{s}^{(j)}.

To perform an update step of
\mathbf{x}
via line search, we require an appropriate step size selection
\alpha^{(j)}
. There are various strategies to obtain this step size. We consider two approaches.

In the first approach, we use the optimal step size. Due to the setup of this problem, this optimal step size can be computed inexpensively with the main computational cost being the evaluation of the soft thresholding. We solve the following 1-dimensional optimization problem

\argmin_{\alpha}\ f_{\textnormal{proj}}(\mathbf{x}^{(j)}-\alpha\mathbf{s}^{(j)})=\argmin_{\alpha}\tfrac{1}{2}\left\|\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{x}^{(j)}-\alpha\mathbf{s}^{(j)})-\mathbf{w}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\\
+\tfrac{\eta^{2}}{2}\left\|\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_{2}(\mathbf{x}^{(j)}-\alpha\mathbf{s}^{(j)})-\mathbf{y}^{(j)}+\mathbf{c}_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\mu\left\|\mathbf{Z}(\mathbf{x}^{(j)}-\alpha\mathbf{s}^{(j)})\right\|_{1}.

(24)

Hence, efficient minimization can be attained through the application of a simple one-dimensional iterative solver, where previous
\alpha^{(j)}
’s may be utilized to accelerate convergence.

Another approach is to ignore the nonlinearity of the optimization problem
Equation

12
and use the linearized optimal step size corresponding to the linear conjugate gradient method. Empirically we observe that this linearization is sufficient and gives good estimates, and we can always fall back to the the optimal stepsize in case this one fails. To obtain the linearized step size, we ignore the nonlinear shrinkage term in
Equation

19
and instead solve

\argmin_{\alpha}\ f_{\textnormal{proj}}(\mathbf{x}^{(j)}-\alpha\mathbf{s}^{(j)})=\tfrac{1}{2}\left\|\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{x}^{(j)}-\alpha\mathbf{s}^{(j)})-\mathbf{w}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\tfrac{\eta^{2}}{2}\left\|\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_{2}(\mathbf{x}^{(j)}-\alpha\mathbf{s}^{(j)})-\mathbf{y}^{(j)}+\mathbf{c}_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(25)

In this case, we can obtain the optimal closed-form solution

\displaystyle\alpha^{(j)}=\frac{\mathbf{s}^{(j)\top}{\rm vec}(\mathbf{L}^{\top}\mathbf{L}\mathbf{X}^{(j)}-\mathbf{L}^{\top}\mathbf{B}+\eta^{2}\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\top}\mathbf{D}_{2}\mathbf{X}+\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\top}\mathbf{D}_{2}(\mathbf{C}_{k}-\mathbf{Y}^{(j)})+\lambda^{2}\mathbf{X}^{(j)}\mathbf{D}_{1}\mathbf{D}_{1}^{\top})}{\mathbf{s}^{(j)\top}{\rm vec}(\mathbf{L}^{\top}\mathbf{L}\mathbf{S}^{(j)}+\eta^{2}\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\top}\mathbf{D}_{2}\mathbf{S}^{(j)}+\lambda^{2}\mathbf{S}^{(j)}\mathbf{D}_{1}\mathbf{D}_{1}^{\top})}.

(26)

There are several options for the computation of
\beta^{(j)}
in the nonlinear conjugate gradient update and the interested reader is referred to
[

]
. In our implementation, we use a combination of the Fletcher-Reeves (FR)
[

and Polak-Ribiére (PR)
[

\displaystyle\beta^{(j+1)}_{\textnormal{FR}}=\frac{\mathbf{g}^{j+1}(\mathbf{y}^{(j+1)})^{\top}\mathbf{g}^{j+1}(\mathbf{y}^{(j+1)})}{\mathbf{g}^{j}(\mathbf{y}^{(j)})^{\top}\mathbf{g}^{j}(\mathbf{y}^{(j)})},

(27)

and the PR update by

\displaystyle\beta^{(j+1)}_{\textnormal{PR}}=\frac{\mathbf{g}^{j+1}(\mathbf{y}^{(j+1)})^{\top}(\mathbf{g}^{j+1}(\mathbf{y}^{(j+1)})-\mathbf{g}^{j}(\mathbf{y}^{(j)}))}{\mathbf{g}^{j}(\mathbf{y}^{(j)})^{\top}\mathbf{g}^{j}(\mathbf{y}^{(j)})}.

(28)

A more robust computation of
\beta^{(j+1)}
is given as follows

\displaystyle\beta^{(j+1)}=\begin{cases}-\beta^{(j+1)}_{\textnormal{FR}},&\textnormal{for }\;\beta^{(j+1)}_{\textnormal{PR}}<-\beta^{(j+1)}_{\textnormal{FR}},\\[6.45831pt]
\beta^{(j+1)}_{\textnormal{PR}},&\textnormal{for }\;\big|\beta^{(j+1)}_{\textnormal{PR}}\big|\leq\beta^{(j+1)}_{\textnormal{FR}},\\[6.45831pt]
\beta^{(j+1)}_{\textnormal{FR}},&\textnormal{for }\;\beta^{(j+1)}_{\textnormal{PR}}>\beta^{(j+1)}_{\textnormal{FR}}.\end{cases}

(29)

Although we prove convergence for the
\beta_{\rm PR}
update with a particular line search discussed in
Section

3.2

\beta
computation given in
Equation

29
performed better numerically.

[

Note that

We consider the setup of the problem as stated in

Assumptions and Settings.
We now assume that
\varphi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
is smooth and strictly convex in both
\mathbf{x}
, and
\mathbf{y}
, and

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{x})=\argmin_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{y}}}\;h(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}).

Denoting

\displaystyle{\bf 0}\in\nabla_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}\varphi(\hat{\mathbf{x}},\hat{\mathbf{y}})+\partial_{\mathbf{y}}r(\hat{\mathbf{y}}),

To prove convergence of our algorithm, the following choices are made for the step size selection and
\beta

10

\displaystyle\varphi(\mathbf{x}^{(j)}+\alpha^{(j)}\mathbf{s}^{(j)})\leq\varphi(\mathbf{x}^{(j)})-\delta\left\|\alpha^{(j)}\mathbf{s}^{(j)}\right\|^{2}+\epsilon_{j}.

(34)

Furthermore, our convergence results assume the PR update of
\beta
is given by
Equation

28

Assumption 1
.

The lower level set,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\epsilon}(\mathbf{x}^{0},\mathbf{y}^{0})=\{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\;|\;h(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\leq h(\mathbf{x}^{0},\mathbf{y}^{0})+\epsilon\}\subset{\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}+n_{y}}}

.

There exists a neighborhood

of

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\varphi(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{1})-\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\varphi(\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{y}_{2})\right\|=\left\|\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{1})-\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{y}_{2})\right\|\leq L_{\mathbf{g}}\left\|(\mathbf{x}_{1}-\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{y}_{1}-\mathbf{y}_{2})\right\|

Additionally, a closed form solution to

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{x}_{1})-\mathcal{Z}(\mathbf{x}_{2})\right\|\leq L_{\mathcal{Z}}\left\|\mathbf{x}_{1}-\mathbf{x}_{2}\right\|,

Theorem 3.4
.

Let

We first note that selection of each

\displaystyle\textbf{0}\in\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}\varphi(\mathbf{x}^{(j)},\mathbf{y}^{(j)})+\partial_{\mathbf{y}}r(\mathbf{y}^{(j)}),

1.

In the first case,

\displaystyle\liminf_{j\rightarrow\infty}\left\|\mathbf{g}^{j}(\mathbf{y}^{(j)})\right\|=\liminf_{j\rightarrow\infty}\left\|\mathbf{s}^{(j)}-\beta^{(j)}\mathbf{s}^{(j-1)}\right\|\leq\liminf_{j\rightarrow\infty}\left\|\mathbf{s}^{(j)}\right\|+\liminf_{j\rightarrow\infty}\left\|\beta^{(j)}\mathbf{s}^{(j-1)}\right\|

\mathbf{Z}(\cdot)
and compactness of the lower level set implies that
\mathbf{Z}(\cdot)
is also Lipschitz continuous and
Assumption

2

4.1
A Windowed Approach

We will illustrate the performance of
VPAL
compared to
ADMM
and
FISTA
in
Section

4.3
. However,
ADMM
,
FISTA
, and
VPAL
compute the entire dynamics simultaneously, limiting real-time potential.
We now discuss how to use a
moving window
approach based on
VPAL
to instead compute later time points sequentially, where a
window
refers to a collection of time points in sequence, i.e.
W_{j}=\{t_{j},t_{j+1},\dots,t_{j+w}\}
. Heuristically, this approach first computes the reconstruction for a window and then uses this reconstruction as an initial guess to compute the reconstruction for the next window. If the windows are small enough, the previous reconstruction should be a good initialization and facilitate fast accurate convergence.

We can frame this mathematically as follows: assume we are given a solution
\mathbf{X}^{(W_{1})}=[\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{x}_{w+1}]
using data
\mathbf{B}^{(W_{1})}=[\mathbf{b}_{1},\mathbf{b}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{b}_{w+1}]
. Moving to later windows of time,
W^{2},W^{3},\dots,W^{J}
, the reconstruction generated from the previous window can be used to initialize the estimation of the current time window. Implementation of this algorithm, denoted as
VPAL
W
, is summarized in
Algorithm

3
.

In our given implementation, the windows need not be disjoint. To ensure smooth transition between reconstructions at adjacent windows, and thus maintain time dependencies, we opt to have the last time point in the previous window overlap with the first time point in the current window. Furthermore, the windows need not be the same length, so long as the initial guess is sliced so it is an appropriate size to initialize the next window. For simplicity, we use a configuration where all windows are of the same length and we reconstruct one new time point in each window–that is

\displaystyle W^{k}=\{t_{j},t_{j+1},\dots t_{j+w}\}\textnormal{ and }W^{k+1}=\{t_{j+w},t_{j+w+1},\dots t_{j+2w}\},

5
Discussion

In
Section

4.3
, we compared the performance of
sLORETA
,
ADMM
,
VPAL
,
FISTA
and
VPAL
W
on the
small
source-localization task. Consistent with expectations for this highly underdetermined EEG source localization problem, the relative error for all algorithms is substantial, as depicted in
Figure

3
. However, as illustrated in
Figure

2
, all methods successfully localized the active region, exhibiting qualitatively comparable levels of performance.
Table

1
confirms that
ADMM
achieved the best performance in terms of PSNR, RE, SSIM while all other methods showed similar performance in these metrics. All
\ell_{1}
-regularized methods resulted in reconstructions with sparser

\mathbf{D}_{2}\mathbf{X}
than
sLORETA
, except for
FISTA
, but it is difficult to compare the sparsity of
\ell_{1}
-regularized against each other due to varied sparsity parameter,
\mu
. Moreover,
VPAL
W
performed best with respect to SED, and all
\ell_{1}
-regularized methods significantly outperformed
sLORETA
in accuracy metrics except for SED. Nevertheless, regarding the runtime,
VPAL
and
VPAL
W
outperform
ADMM
and
FISTA
on this small simulation study, but are outperformed by the instantaneous reconstruction of
sLORETA
, as expected. This motivates us to investigate the scalability of the algorithms on larger datasets.

Improved runtime for
VPAL
and
VPAL
W
is further apparent in the results of
Section

4.4
. For all resolutions of the mesh,
n
, tested,
ADMM
is consistently slower than our proposed methods by several orders of magnitude. As depicted in
Figure

5
, the runtime of
ADMM
increases more rapidly with respect to mesh size
n
compared to the other two algorithms. This leads to
ADMM
becoming computationally intractable for high-resolution datasets.
Both
VPAL
and
VPAL
W
scale more favorably than
ADMM
, with
VPAL
W
exhibiting superior computational efficiency compared to
VPAL
for larger mesh sizes.

We also note that while
ADMM
outperforms
VPAL
W
for the small mesh,
VPAL
W
offers better performance metrics for the medium-sized mesh in PSNR. Qualitatively,
VPAL
W
produces better reconstructions than
VPAL
. This is particularly evident in the largest dataset visualization where
VPAL
captures the strongest signal and fails to identify some other signals (
Figure

4
).
VPAL
W
, on the other hand, identified all the signals present in the ground truth.

As
T
is varied (see
Figure

6
), we observe that
VPAL
W
has slower runtimes than
VPAL
for small values of
T
. However, the growth rate of the runtime of
VPAL
W
is slower on average than that of
VPAL
and thus
VPAL
W
offers improved runtime for
T
large.
VPAL
W
offers the additional advantage of nearly real-time reconstruction of incoming data points. For small meshes, the algorithm can reconstruct incoming time points in
0.538\pm 0.023
seconds on average. For the medium and large datasets, the average reconstruction time per time point is scaled to
2.465\pm 0.020
and
25.667\pm 2.607
seconds, respectively.

The efficient runtime of
VPAL
and
VPAL
W
benefit significantly from our data-efficient implementation of the
graphTV
operator as defined in
Equation

7
, as well as our linearized step size selection routine. The
graphTV
operator can be efficiently implemented in MATLAB as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}_{2}\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{w}\odot(\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{n}_{1},:)-\mathbf{X}(\mathbf{n}_{2},:)),

(53)

where
\mathbf{w}
is the diagonal of
\mathbf{W}
. Here,
\mathbf{n}_{1}(i),\mathbf{n}_{2}(i)
are the indices of the two nodes that define the
i
-th edge. For the EEG application, we assume unweighted, undirected edges, and thus
\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{I}_{m}
. We note that, due to implementing the mesh as a graph, this is an efficient computation, depending linearly on the number of edges
m
.

6
Conclusions & Outlook

In this work, we presented a novel approach to address the EEG source localization inverse problem and leverage a data-efficient graph total variation (
graphTV
) operator on the cortical mesh to regularize spatial aspects of the reconstruction coupled with regularization on the time dynamics. Our main contribution was the introduction of two fast solvers for this inverse problem–the
variable projected augmented Lagrangian nonlinear conjugate gradient
algorithm (
VPAL
) and the windowed variation,
VPAL
W
. Additionally, we verified the theoretical backing to our
VPAL
algorithm, proving convergence for separable convex functions subject to linear constraints–in particular, the EEG source localization problem.

We investigated the performance of our proposed methods in terms of both runtime and reconstruction quality. Our novel algorithms demonstrated comparable performance to standard elastic net solvers like
ADMM
and
FISTA
while offering significantly faster runtimes and superior scalability.
VPAL
W
is particularly efficient in handling large datasets and is well-suited for real-time applications, such as monitoring brain activity in clinical settings.

While our study highlights the advantages of our novel approach, it also has several limitations. Notably, hyperparameter selection was performed using a brute-force approach, and further statistical and theoretical analysis is needed to optimize this process. Additionally, more rigorous termination criteria are required. Furthermore, our evaluation relied on synthetic datasets, and future work will involve testing our methods on real-world data to assess their clinical applicability.

In future research, we aim to investigate applications of variable projection-based approaches in solving nonlinear inverse problems and further develop theoretical foundations. We also plan to explore the potential of
VPAL
W
as a filtering technique in data assimilation. The proposed
graphTV
operator and algorithms have broad applicability to various data-intensive graph-based inverse problems, including those involving different graph structures (e.g., directed, weighted, etc.).

A.2

Hyperparameter selection is challenging in inverse problems and several strategies exist for the estimation of the optimal regularization and penalty parameters
\mu,\lambda,\eta

[
9
,
44
,
45
,
24
]

\ell_{2}

FISTA

As parameter estimation is not the focus of this paper, we adopt a naive brute-force approach for the estimation of suitable parameters and admit the limitation that rigorous statistical analysis is required for more optimal computation of these values.

Parameters used in the comparison experiment were selected by varying
\mu

[10^{-5},10^{2}]
is a suitable range for the selection of both values and that the value of

\eta=10
in our experiments.

Appendix B
Derivations

B.1
Equivalent Vectorized Forms of
f
and
\mathcal{L}_{\rm aug}

In this section, we show the derivation of the vectorized form of our objective function and augmented Lagrangian. The objective function is given by
Equation

4
,

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{X})=\tfrac{1}{2}\left\|\mathbf{L}\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{B}\right\|_{{\rm F}}^{2}+\tfrac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\left\|\mathbf{X}\mathbf{D}_{1}\right\|_{{\rm F}}^{2}+\mu\left\|\mathbf{D}_{2}\mathbf{X}\right\|_{1,1}.

(54)

If we define

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{L}}=\mathbf{I}_{T}\otimes\mathbf{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{nT\times nT},

(55)

Kronecker identities imply
[
46
]

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{L}\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{B}\right\|_{{\rm F}}^{2}=\left\|\tilde{\mathbf{L}}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\right\|_{{\rm F}}^{2}.

(56)

Analogously, for

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_{2}=\mathbf{I}_{T}\otimes\mathbf{D}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{mT\times nT},

(57)

we have

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{D}_{2}\mathbf{X}\right\|_{1,1}=\left\|\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_{2}\mathbf{x}\right\|_{1}

(58)

Lastly, letting

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{D}_{1}}=\mathbf{D}_{1}^{\top}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n(T-1)\times nT},

(59)

gives

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{X}\mathbf{D}_{1}\right\|_{{\rm F}}^{2}=\left\|\tilde{\mathbf{D}_{1}}\mathbf{x}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(60)

All together, the vectorized form of our objective function can be written as

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\tilde{\mathbf{L}}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\right\|_{2}+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\left\|\tilde{\mathbf{D}_{1}}\mathbf{x}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\mu\left\|\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_{2}\mathbf{x}\right\|_{1}

(61)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\mathbf{L}}\\
\tilde{\lambda\mathbf{D}_{1}}\end{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}-\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{b}\\
\textbf{0}_{n(T-1)}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\mu\left\|\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_{2}\mathbf{x}\right\|_{1}.

(62)

Denoting
\mathbf{A}=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{\mathbf{L}}\\
\tilde{\lambda\mathbf{D}_{1}}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{Tp+(T-1)n\times Tn}
and
\mathbf{w}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{b}\\
\textbf{0}_{n(T-1)}\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}^{Tp+n(T-1)}}
, we obtain

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{w}\right\|_{2}+\mu\left\|\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_{2}\mathbf{x}\right\|_{1}.

(63)

Analogously,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\rm aug}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y};\mathbf{c})=\tfrac{1}{2}\left\|\tilde{\mathbf{L}}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\tfrac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\left\|\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_{1}\mathbf{x}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\tfrac{\eta^{2}}{2}\left\|\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_{2}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{c}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\mu\left\|\mathbf{y}\right\|_{1}-\left\|\mathbf{c}\right\|_{2}^{2}

(64)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{2}\left\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{w}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\tfrac{\eta^{2}}{2}\left\|\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_{2}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{c}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\mu\left\|\mathbf{y}\right\|_{1}-\left\|\mathbf{c}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(65)

B.2
ADMM
Update

Here we provide details on the formulation of the least-squares problem in
ADMM
as the solution to a Sylvester equation for the EEG inverse problem. Let us restate the objective function in
Equation

11
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\textnormal{aug}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y};\mathbf{C})=\tfrac{1}{2}\left\|\mathbf{L}\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{B}\right\|_{{\rm F}}^{2}+\tfrac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\left\|\mathbf{X}\mathbf{D}_{1}\right\|_{{\rm F}}^{2}+\mu\left\|\mathbf{Y}\right\|_{1,1}+\tfrac{\eta^{2}}{2}\left\|\mathbf{D}_{2}\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{Y}+\mathbf{C}\right\|_{{\rm F}}^{2}+\left\|\mathbf{C}\right\|_{{\rm F}}^{2}.

Consistent with the
ADMM
approach, we want to minimize this function with respect to
\mathbf{X}
. Ignoring constant terms with respect to
\mathbf{X}
and letting
\mathbf{G}=\mathbf{Y}-\mathbf{C}
we may simplify the corresponding optimization problem as

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{X}}\ h(\mathbf{X})=\min_{\mathbf{X}}\tfrac{1}{2}\left\|\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{L}\\
\eta\mathbf{D}_{2}\end{bmatrix}\mathbf{X}-\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{B}\\
\eta\mathbf{G}\end{bmatrix}\right\|_{{\rm F}}^{2}+\tfrac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\left\|\mathbf{X}\mathbf{D}_{1}\right\|_{{\rm F}}^{2}.

(66)

By defining

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{L}\\
\eta\mathbf{D}_{2}\end{bmatrix}\textnormal{ and }\mathbf{K}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{B}\\
\eta\mathbf{G}\end{bmatrix},

(67)

we can rewrite
h
as

\displaystyle h(\mathbf{X})=\tfrac{1}{2}\left\|\mathbf{H}\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{K}\right\|_{{\rm F}}^{2}+\tfrac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\left\|\mathbf{X}\mathbf{D}_{1}\right\|_{{\rm F}}^{2}.

(68)

The gradient with respect to
\mathbf{X}
of the additive terms in
h
can be computed as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{X}}\left\|\mathbf{X}\mathbf{D}_{1}\right\|_{{\rm F}}^{2}=\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{X}}{\rm tr\!}\left(\mathbf{D}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{D}_{1}\right)=\mathbf{X}\mathbf{D}_{1}\mathbf{D}_{1}^{\top}+\mathbf{X}\mathbf{D}_{1}\mathbf{D}_{1}^{\top}=2\mathbf{X}\mathbf{D}_{1}\mathbf{D}_{1}^{\top}

(69)

and

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{X}}\left\|\mathbf{H}\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{K}\right\|_{{\rm F}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{X}}{\rm tr\!}\left((\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{H}^{\top}-\mathbf{K}^{\top})(\mathbf{H}\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{K})\right)

(70)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial\mathbf{X}}({\rm tr\!}\left(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{X}\right)-{\rm tr\!}\left(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{H}^{\top}\mathbf{K}\right)-{\rm tr\!}\left(\mathbf{K}^{\top}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{X}\right)+{\rm tr\!}\left(\mathbf{K}^{\top}\mathbf{K}\right))

(71)

\displaystyle=2\mathbf{H}^{\top}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{H}^{\top}\mathbf{K}-\mathbf{H}^{\top}\mathbf{K}=2\mathbf{H}^{\top}\mathbf{H}-2\mathbf{H}^{\top}\mathbf{K}.

(72)

Hence the gradient of
h
is given by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial h}{\partial\mathbf{X}}=\mathbf{H}^{\top}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{H}^{\top}\mathbf{K}+\lambda^{2}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{D}_{1}\mathbf{D}_{1}^{\top}.

(73)

According to first-order optimality condition and assumed convexity we obtain a global minimizer by equating to
\bf 0
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbf{H}^{\top}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{X}+\lambda^{2}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{D}_{1}\mathbf{D}_{1}^{\top}=\mathbf{H}^{\top}\mathbf{K}.

(74)

Note this is the Sylvester Equation which can be solved efficiently using standard approaches. For our implementation of
ADMM
we utilize MATLAB’s specialized Sylvester Equation solver,
sylv
.

B.3
VPAL
Gradient Computation

In this section, we provide details for the gradient computation used in
Algorithm

2
.
We compute the gradient of
Equation

11
with respect to
\mathbf{X}
assuming
\mathbf{C}
to be constant and
\mathbf{Y}
given according to soft-thresholding as computed in
Equation

19
, i.e.,
\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{Z}(\mathbf{X})
. We begin with the vectorized form of our augmented Lagrangian,
Equation

16
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\textnormal{aug}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y};\mathbf{c})=\tfrac{1}{2}\left\|\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{w}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\tfrac{\eta^{2}}{2}\left\|\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_{2}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{c}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\mu\left\|\mathbf{y}\right\|_{1}-\tfrac{\eta^{2}}{2}\left\|\mathbf{c}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

Taking the gradient with respect to
\mathbf{x}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}=\tilde{\mathbf{L}}^{\top}(\tilde{\mathbf{L}}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b})+\lambda^{2}\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_{1}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_{1}\mathbf{x}+\eta^{2}\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_{2}^{\top}(\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_{2}\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{c}),

(75)

where
\tilde{\mathbf{L}},\tilde{\mathbf{D}_{1}}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_{2}
are defined the same way as in
Section

B.1
. To avoid computation of the large matrices formed from the Kronecker product, we note

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{L}}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle={\rm vec}(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{X}),

(76)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_{2}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle={\rm vec}(\mathbf{D}_{2}\mathbf{X}),

(77)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{L}}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{L}}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{I}_{T}\otimes\mathbf{L}^{\top}){\rm vec}(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{X})={\rm vec}(\mathbf{L}^{\top}\mathbf{L}\mathbf{X})

(78)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_{1}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{D}_{1}^{\top}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{n})\mathbf{x}={\rm vec}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{D}_{1})={\rm vec}(\mathbf{I}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{D}_{1}).

(79)

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_{1}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{D}_{1}}\mathbf{x}=\tilde{\mathbf{D}}_{1}^{\top}{\rm vec}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{D}_{1})

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{D}_{1}\otimes\mathbf{I}_{n}){\rm vec}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{D}_{1})={\rm vec}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{D}_{1}\mathbf{D}_{1}^{\top}).

(80)

All together, the vectorized gradient is computed as

\displaystyle\mathbf{g}={\rm vec}(\mathbf{L}^{\top}(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{B}))+\lambda^{2}{\rm vec}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{D}_{1}\mathbf{D}_{1}^{\top})+\eta^{2}{\rm vec}(\mathbf{D}_{2}^{\top}(\mathbf{D}_{2}\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{Y}+\mathbf{C})).

(81)
