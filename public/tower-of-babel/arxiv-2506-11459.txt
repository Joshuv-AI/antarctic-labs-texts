Equations defining Jacobians with Real Multiplication
Rahul Mistry, Ramesh Sreekantan

Abstract
If C : y 2 = x ⁡ ( x − 1 ) ​ ( x − a 1 ) ​ ( x − a 2 ) ​ ( x − a 3 ) C:y^{2}=x(x-1)(x-a_{1})(x-a_{2})(x-a_{3}) is genus 2 2 curve a natural question to ask is: Under what conditions on a 1 , a 2 , a 3 a_{1},a_{2},a_{3} does the Jacobian J ⁡ ( C ) J(C) have real multiplication by ℤ ⁡ [ Δ ] {\mathds{Z}}[\sqrt{\Delta}] for some Δ > 0 \Delta>0 . Over a hundred years ago Humbert [ Hum99 ] gave an answer to this question for Δ = 5 \Delta=5 and Δ = 8 \Delta=8 . In this paper we use work of Birkenhake and Wilhelm [ BW03 ] along with some classical results in enumerative geometry to generalize this to all discriminants, in principle. We also work it out explicitly in a few more cases.

Introduction

1.1
Curves of genus
2

Let

C=C_{a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}}:y^{2}=x(x-1)(x-a_{1})(x-a_{2})(x-a_{3})

be a genus

. The Jacobian

is a principally polarized Abelian surface. A natural question to ask is -‘What is the endomorphism ring of

, as the points

and

determine the level two structure. Most Abelian surfaces have endomorphism ring

1.2
Endomorphism rings of Abelian surfaces

There are four possibilities for the endomorphism ring of an Abelian surface over
{\mathds{C}}
.

•

\operatorname{End}(A)\supseteq{\mathds{Z}}
and generically one has equality.

•

\operatorname{End}(A)\supseteq{\mathds{Z}}[\Delta]
where
\Delta>0
- this happens on surfaces which are called Humbert surfaces

. The moduli of products of elliptic curves corresponds to the case
\Delta=1
. Here too, generically one has equality.

•

\operatorname{End}(A)\supseteq{\mathcal{O}}
where
{\mathcal{O}}
is an order in a indefinite quaternionic algebra over
{\mathds{Q}}
- either a division algebra
B
or a subring of
M_{2}({\mathds{Q}})
. This happens on curves called Shimura curves, in the first case, or modular curves, in the second. In this case as well generically one has equality.

•

\operatorname{End}(A)={\mathcal{O}}_{D}
, where
{\mathcal{O}}_{D}
is a subring of
M_{2}(K)
where
K={\mathds{Q}}(\sqrt{D})
is an imaginary quadratic field. In this case

is isogenous to the self product of an elliptic curve with complex multiplication by
{\mathds{Q}}(\sqrt{D})
.

1.3
The endomorphism ring and the Neron-Severi group

The endomorphism ring of an Abelian variety is closely connected with its Neron-Severi group. There is an involution on the endomorphism ring of the Abelian variety determined by the principal polarization called the Rosati involution. The Neron-Severi group can be identified with the elements of the endomorphism ring fixed under this involution.

In the opposite direction, let
L_{0}
denote the class of the polarization and

PNS(A)=L_{0}^{\perp}
denote its orthogonal complement in the Neron-Severi group. This is usually called the
Primitive Neron-Severi
group.

Recall that the
Clifford Algebra
of a lattice
\Lambda
with a quadratic form
Q
is defined to be

CL(\Lambda,Q)=T(\Lambda)/<x\otimes x-Q(x)1>

and the dimension of
CL(\Lambda,Q)=2^{\operatorname{dim}_{{\mathds{Q}}}(\Lambda\otimes{\mathds{Q}})}
. Here
T(\Lambda)
is the Tensor algebra

T(\Lambda)=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{\infty}\bigotimes^{k}\Lambda={\mathds{Z}}\bigoplus\Lambda\bigoplus\Lambda\otimes\Lambda\bigoplus\cdots

From the Hodge Index Theorem the intersection pairing is negative definite on
PNS(A)
. Let

<,>=-2(,)
. Then

is a positive definite bilinear form on
PNS(A)
called the

in

<D,D>=(D,L_{0})^{2}-2(D,D)

Let

denote the corresponding quadratic form.

The endomorphism ring is then the Clifford algebra of the primitive Neron-Severi with respect to the negative of the intersection pairing.

\operatorname{End}(A)=Cl(PNS(A),H)

For instance if the Abelian surface
A
has real multiplication the rank of the Neron-Severi, usually called the Picard number
\rho(A)\geq 2
. If
\rho(A)=2
, as it is generically, and

and the moduli of Abelian surfaces where there is an element in

of Humbert invariant
\Delta

.

When
\rho(A)=3
, then
\operatorname{End}(A)
is an indefinite quaternion algebra and when
\rho(A)=4
,
\operatorname{End}(A)
is and order in
M_{2}(K)
where

is a CM field.

Hence the question becomes - under what conditions on
a_{1},a_{2}
and
a_{3}
is

. Humbert gave a criterion under which the rank of the Neron-Severi is

. He then used it to answer the question in the case when
\Delta=5
.

Hashimoto and Murabayashi
[
HM95
]
gave a modern exposition of Humbert’s theorem and used that to obtain equations of some Shimura curves. In this article we generalize there work. There are two new ingredients - the first is a theorem of Birkenhake-Wilhelm generalizing Humbert’s criteria for
{\mathds{Z}}[\sqrt{5}]
and
{\mathds{Z}}[\sqrt{8}]
and the second is an observation that a curious coincidence of enumerative geometry allows us to find certain relevant curves.

Birkenhake and Wilhelm
[
BW03
]
generalized Humbert’s criterion to infinitely many
\Delta

The aim of this paper is to show that their work can be used to obtain conditions on
a_{1},a_{2}
and
a_{3}
as well along the lines of Humbert. In principle this can be done in an infinite number of cases but in practice it may be a little difficult.

2.3
Humbert’s Theorem

We can now state Humbert’s theorem.

Theorem 2.1

(Humbert)
.

[

]
Let
(A,L_{0})
be a principally polarized Abelian surface and let
{\mathds{P}}^{2}_{A}
be its Kummer plane. Suppose there exists a conic

in
{\mathds{P}}^{2}_{A}
passing through 5 of the 15 points

and meeting the remaining line tangentially, then the Abelian surface

has real multiplication by
{\mathds{Z}}[\sqrt{5}]
. Conversely, if

has real multiplication by
{\mathds{Z}}[\sqrt{5}]
then there exists such a conic in
{\mathds{P}}^{2}_{A}
.

The class of the curve
\phi^{*}\circ\pi^{*}(Q)
in the Neron-Severi pulls back to a multiple of the class of the principle polarization

hence is
not
a new element of the Neron-Severi. The reason this theorem holds is the following lemma.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
Q
be a rational curve. If

is a double cover such that the ramification points are singular. Then
D
is reducible and has two components
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
such that
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
meet at the ramification points.

Proof.

Let
\tilde{f}:{\tilde{D}}\rightarrow{\tilde{Q}}
be the map between the normalizations induced by the map

. This is a unramified double cover and
{\tilde{Q}}\simeq{\mathds{P}}^{1}
. However since
{\mathds{P}}^{1}
is simply connected it has no unramified double covers. Hence it consists of two components
{\tilde{D}}_{1}
and
{\tilde{D}}_{2}
. The images
D_{1}
and
D_{2}
under the map to
D
are the two components of

and the points of intersection are the ramification points.

∎

One can then see that the pull back of one of the components

or
D_{2}
is a new element of the Neron-Severi group.

In Humbert’s case the conic

meets the ramified sextic

only at double points. The double cover

of

in

has nodes at those points and hence satisfies the conditions of Lemma

is the union of two components

and
D_{2}
.

Note that the involution

. Hence

is orthogonal to the polarization class. Hence the primitive Neron-Severi contains

and the endomorphism ring contains

[
BW03
]
(Proposition 6.4) compute this self intersection and from that one has that the Abelian surface has real multiplication by

4
Computations

In this section we use ideas of the previous sections to compute the equations. The idea is the following. We want to find a rational curve of degree
d
passing through
k
points and meeting the lines of the sextic
S
at
3d-k-1
points of even multiplicity - where we count points of multiplicity
2r

r
times. We know such a curve always exists and it will meet
S
at two other points. The condition we want is that the two points coincide - so that
S
and the rational degree
d
curve meet at
3d
double points.

For
d>2
it seems to be computationally easier to do the following. Find conditions on the degree
d
polynomials so that the corresponding curves pass through
3d
double points. Then impose further conditions so that the curve is rational.

A homogeneous equation of degree
d
in
3
variables has
N=\binom{d+2}{2}=\frac{(d+1)(d+2)}{2}
coefficients, so the space of degree
d
curves is
N-1
dimensional.

The condition that such a curve passes through a point determines a linear relation among the coefficients and so the dimension drops by
1
. The condition that points are in ‘general position’ translates to the fact that the corresponding linear relations are linearly independent. Hence the space of degree
d
curves passing through
k
points in general position is of dimension
N-k-1
.

The condition that it is tangent to a line also drops the dimension by
1
. Hence the space of degree
d
curves passing through
k
points and tangent to
3d-k
lines is of dimension
\binom{d+2}{2}=\frac{(d+1)(d+2)}{2}-(k+(3d-k))=\frac{(d-1)(d-2)}{2}
. Note that this is the genus of a smooth curve of degree
d
. We then need to impose conditions that the curves are rational - that is, have geometric genus
0
. This adds a further
g
conditions resulting in a
0
dimensional set of rational curves passing through the
3d
double points. We see that this imposes conditions on the coefficients - which are computable in terms of
a_{1},a_{2}
and
a_{3}
.

We can equally well first perform the blowing up to get conditions under which a degree
d
curve is rational. This will a
3d-1
dimensional space. Then use the other conditions to ensure that the curves pass through the required number of points and are tangent to the required number of lines.

The algorithm works particularly well in degree
3
. If
d=3
then
\binom{d+2}{2}-1=3d=9
so there exist a unique cubic curve through
9
points. However, in general it not
rational
. In order to get rationality we have to impose one more condition - that the cubic is singular. This is guaranteed by the vanishing of the discriminant of the cubic. Hence the algorithm is –

Step 1. Compute the equation of the
possibly smooth cubic
through the nine points of an admissible tuple. The coefficients will be determined by
a_{1},a_{2}
and
a_{3}
.

Step 2. Compute the discriminant of this cubic to get a polynomial
F
in
a_{1},a_{2}
and
a_{3}
. When this is
0
the corresponding Jacobian will have real multiplication.

We recall some necessary background and theory and then illustrate the algorithm using SAGE.

The material in this section is standard and can be found in an textbook on algebraic geometry or algebraic curves, for instance Hartshorne

A

\displaystyle f:\sum_{i+j+k=d}c_{ijk}x^{i}y^{j}z^{j}=c_{d00}x^{d}+c_{d-110}x^{d-a}y^{1}+\dots+c_{00d}z^{d}

Let

f_{s}:=\frac{\partial f}{\partial s}

be the formal partial derivative of
f
with respect to the variable

Theorem 4.1

(Euler’s Identity)
.

Let
f\in{\mathds{C}}[x_{1},...,x_{n}]
be a non-constant homogeneous polynomial of degree
d>0
. Then the following identity

\displaystyle d\cdot f=x_{1}f_{x_{1}}+\cdots+x_{n}f_{x_{n}}

holds.

Proof.

The formal partial derivative operator is linear with respect to addition, that is, for any two polynomials
g_{1},g_{2}\in{\mathds{C}}[x_{1},...,x_{n}]
,
(g_{1}+g_{2})_{x_{i}}={g_{1}}_{x_{i}}+{g_{2}}_{x_{i}}
. Hence it suffices to check this identity on a monomial of degree
d
.

Let
f=Cx_{1}^{i_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{i_{n}}
with
i_{1}+\cdots i_{n}=d
and some constant
C\in{\mathds{C}}
. With respect to some variable
x_{j}
,
x_{j}\cdot f_{x_{j}}=i_{j}\cdot Cx_{1}^{i_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{i_{n}}=i_{j}\cdot f
. Summing over
j=1,...,n
we get
\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}f_{x_{j}}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}i_{j}f=d\cdot f

∎

We need this result for the following simple corollary.

Corollary 4.2
.

If all partial derivatives of a non-constant polynomial
f\in{\mathds{C}}[x,y,z]
vanish at some
p\in{\mathds{P}}^{2}({\mathds{C}})
then necessarily
f(p)=0
as well.

The

of a plane curve is defined to be

We have the following degree-genus formula for planar curves

\displaystyle g_{g}(C)=\frac{(d-1)(d-2)}{2}-\sum_{x\in Sing(C)}\frac{1}{2}r_{x}(r_{x}-1)

(2)

where

is the order of the singularity at

Proof.

This can be found in numerous places, for instance Corollary 2.4,
[

]
∎

4.3
Vertex covers and products of lines

For the purpose of computation it is useful to find a generating set of the set of degree
d
curves passing thought
k
points. As we will see in the case of
(4,2)
and
(3,3)
it is easy to find degree
2
curves passing through
4
points or
3
points as products of two lines.

One might wonder for any

whether one can find degree
d
curves passing through
k
points as products of
d
of the lines
l_{i}
. We can frame this in terms of the graph and in some cases use that to find such curves. A
vertex cover

{\tilde{V}}
of a graph is a subset of the set of vertices
V
such that
every edge
is adjacent to a element of
{\tilde{V}}
.

For instance, for the graph
(4,2)
in Section
5.1.2
for the square graph
v_{1}v_{2}v_{3}v_{4}
the sets
\{v_{1},v_{3}\}
and
\{v_{2},v_{4}\}
are vertex covers but
\{v_{1},v_{2}\}
is not as the edge
\overline{v_{3}v_{4}}
is not covered. The vertex covers correspond to the required conics -

\{v_{i},v_{j}\}\leftrightarrows l_{i}l_{j}

As the degree increases and as the number of points of tangency increases, we can use this idea to find a convenient generating set of degree

5.3
Remarks on higher degree curves

In this section we outline the procedure for higher degree curves. The procedure is similar- only computationally harder.

The space of curves of degree
d
in
{\mathds{P}}^{2}
,
S_{d}
is
\frac{(d+1)(d+2)}{2}-1
dimensional. Hence the condition that the curve passes through
3d-1
double points does not determine a unique curve. In fact there is a
\frac{(d+1)(d+2)}{2}-1-(3d-1)=\frac{(d-1)(d-2)}{2}=g
family of degree
d
curves through
3d-1
points. Let
T_{d}
be this family. Note that the dimension is the geometric genus of a generic fibre of this family - namely a smooth curve of degree
d
.

Suppose
F_{{\underline{t}}}=F_{t_{0},\dots,t_{g}}
is the equation of such a curve
C_{\underline{t}}
. The condition that it has a singular point is given by
\operatorname{disc}(F_{{\underline{t}}})=0
. The set of such equations determines a codimensional
1
subvariety
T_{1}
of the space of degree
d
curves passing through the
3d-1
double points

Over
T_{1}
, the set of singular points is

\rm{Sing}(C_{T_{1}})=V((F_{{\underline{t}}})_{x},(F_{{\underline{t}}})_{y},(F_{{\underline{t}}})_{z}).

namely the set of common zeroes of all the partial derivatives. Over
T_{1}
, there is at least one section
\sigma
of the
{\mathds{P}}^{2}
bundle over
T_{1}
such that
\sigma({\underline{t}})
is a singular point of
C_{{\underline{t}}}
.

We can then blow up the point
\sigma_{T}
in
{\mathds{P}}^{2}_{T}
and consider the strict transform of
\tilde{C}_{T_{1}}
of
C_{T_{1}}
. This is a new family over
T_{1}
with generic geometric genus one less than that of
C_{T}
. The subvariety of such curves with
\operatorname{disc}(\tilde{F}_{{\underline{t}}})=0
– where
\tilde{F}_{{\underline{t}}}
is the equation of
\tilde{C}_{{\underline{t}}}
– is again of codimension one. Repeating this process
g
times results in a
0
dimensional family of degree
d
curves of genus
0
- that is, rational curves, passing through
3d-1
double points.

Recall that we considered the resultant

\operatorname{Res}(\operatorname{disc}(g_{0}),\dots,\operatorname{disc}(g_{3d-k}))

which is a function of
t_{0},\dots,t_{3d-k}
and the
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}
. This parametrized the space of degree
d
curves passing through
k
points and tangent to
3d-k
lines. Considering

F_{d,k}(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})=\operatorname{Res}{(\operatorname{disc}(F),\operatorname{disc}(\tilde{F}),\dots,\operatorname{disc}(g_{0}),\dots,\operatorname{disc}(g_{3d-k}))}

results in a function of
a_{1},a_{2}
and
a_{3}
and is the modular equation corresponding to
\Delta(d,k)
.

5.4
Remarks on higher genus curves

We have considered only genus
0
(rational) curves, However, this does not cover all possible
\Delta
. Birkenhake and Wilhelm
[
BW03
]
, Theorem 7.6 show that one sometimes needs to use higher genus curves. The theorem asserts that if the Kummer plane admits a
g
-dimensional linear system passing through
3d
double points then the corresponding Abelian surface has real multiplication by some
\Delta
determined by
d,k
and
g
, where
k
is the number of ponts
q_{ij}
that the linear system passes through.

This can also be treated in the same way. For similar reasons as above in general, for the Kummer plane of any Abelian surface, one will have a
g

g
and degree
d
curves passing through
3d-1
double points. These curves will meet the sextic at two other points and when they coincide it means that the corresponding Abelian surface has real multiplication by some
\Delta>0

a_{1},a_{2}
and
a_{3}
