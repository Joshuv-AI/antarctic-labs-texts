Total Astragalus Saponins Modulate Ferroptosis Pathways in Cigarette Smoke and LPS-Induced Models of COPD: An in vitro and in vivo Investigation
Authors: Qing Wang, Xuehua Wang, Ling Han, Yue Sun, Junhong Zhang, Haojie Su, Fanlu Liu, Jingjing Wu, Yue Lu, Leng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeChronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a progressive respiratory condition characterized by persistent airflow limitation and chronic airway inflammation. Emerging evidence indicates that ferroptosis contributes to disease pathogenesis. The current therapeutic strategies cause adverse side effects and frequent exacerbations. Astragalus membranaceus (Fisch.) Bunge, a traditional Chinese herb with a long-standing history for treating pulmonary disorders, benefits patients with COPD. The primary active constituents are total astragalus saponins (AST); however, their application in COPD management remains underexplored. This study aimed to investigate the impact of AST on ferroptosis in COPD.MethodsA COPD murine model was developed through exposure to cigarette smoke (CS) and lipopolysaccharide (LPS). We evaluated the impacts of AST on respiratory function, pulmonary index, lung pathology, and serum reactive oxygen species (ROS) and glutathione (GSH) levels in the COPD mice. Beas-2B cells were stimulated with cigarette smoke extract (CSE) to evaluate GSH, malondialdehyde (MDA), ROS, lipid peroxidation, Fe2⁺, and membrane integrity. The expression levels of ferroptosis-related genes and proteins were analyzed by quantitative polymerase chain reaction and Western blot in the COPD mice and CSE-induced cells.ResultsIn COPD mice (n=8), AST treatment significantly improved respiratory function (Penh, TV, EF50, EF75), reduced tracheal wall thickening and inflammatory cell infiltration (p<0.001), decreased serum ROS and increased GSH (p<0.05), and regulated ferroptosis-related gene and protein expression (p<0.05). In CSE-stimulated Beas-2B cells, AST restored cell viability, increased GSH, decreased MDA and ROS production, reduced lipid peroxidation and intracellular Fe2⁺ accumulation, preserved membrane integrity, and upregulated GPX4 and FTH1 while downregulating ACSL4 and PTGS2 (p<0.05).ConclusionOur findings suggest that AST exerts protective effects against CS and LPS-induced COPD in mice and CSE-induced injury in Beas-2B cells that are associated with modulation of ferroptosis-related markers. These results provide preliminary evidence supporting continued investigation of AST in preclinical COPD models and offer potential directions for future mechanistic research.Keywords: COPD, total astragalus saponins, ferroptosis, cigarette smoke, inflammation

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a progressive chronic respiratory condition characterized by obstructive ventilatory defects and persistent airflow limitation, which is irreversible and may lead to significant chronic respiratory failure1,2 As the third leading cause of death worldwide, it affects approximately 10% of the global adult population.3,4 The pathogenesis of COPD involves multiple mechanisms, including chronic airway inflammation and oxidative stress.5 Cigarette smoking is the primary risk factor for COPD, and chronic exposure to harmful particles in cigarette smoke (CS) induces persistent oxidative injury and the death of airway and alveolar epithelial cells, which is central to the onset and progression of the disease.6 Although Western pharmaceutical interventions benefit COPD management, their long-term use is frequently associated with various adverse effects. Conversely, traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) has demonstrated promising results in the treatment of COPD.7

The pathogenesis of COPD involves injury to both bronchial and alveolar epithelium.

In the proximal airways, CS exposure is associated with reduced bronchial epithelial barrier function, potentially driven by an imbalance in the antioxidant response.8 In the distal lung, alveolar epithelial cell death drives emphysematous destruction. Recent studies have identified ferroptosis as a key mechanism underlying epithelial cell injury and disease progression in COPD.9 Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent programmed cell death distinct from apoptosis, characterized by iron overload, glutathione (GSH) depletion, reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, and lipid peroxidation.10,11 Extracellular Fe3⁺ is transported into cells via transferrin (TRF) and reduced to Fe2⁺. Excess intracellular Fe2⁺ reacts with hydrogen peroxide to generate ROS and drive lipid peroxidation, ultimately triggering cellular ferroptosis].12 GSH serves as a critical defensive system against ROS and is essential for maintaining cellular redox homeostasis.13 Its depletion in COPD contributes to epithelial cell death and disease progression geting ferroptosis through modulation of iron metabolism, lipid peroxidation, and antioxidant defense represents a promising therapeutic strategy for respiratory diseases. However, effective pharmacological interventions that specifically modulate ferroptosis in COPD remain limited.

Astragalus membranaceus (Fisch.) Bunge is a commonly used Qi-tonifying herb in TCM and is widely applied in the treatment of various respiratory diseases, including COPD, asthma, and pulmonary fibrosis.14,15 Total astragalus saponins (AST), the main bioactive component of Astragalus membranaceus, consists of astragaloside I, II, III, and IV. The primary constituent astragaloside IV (AS-IV) has been documented to exert anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and immunomodulatory activities,16 and to regulate the ferroptosis signaling pathway via the Nrf2/SLC7A11/GPX4 axis in lung injury models.17 It has also been reported to protect against lung injury and pulmonary fibrosis in COPD.18 However, whether AST can alleviate CS-induced COPD and whether this protective effect is associated with ferroptosis-related pathways remain to be investigated.

We hypothesized that AST exerts protective effects against CS-induced epithelial injury through modulation of ferroptosis-related markers. Given that bronchial epithelial cells constitute the primary targets of inhaled cigarette smoke, we employed a Beas-2B cell model exposed to CSE to investigate the underlying cellular mechanisms, while a mouse model of COPD induced by CS combined with LPS was used to evaluate the systemic protective effects of AST. This study aimed to test whether AST exerts protective effects against CS-induced COPD and whether this effect is associated with ferroptosis-related pathways.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals and Reagents

AST (C28H32O17, MW:640.54, CAS:17429–69-5, HPLC≥98%) was purchased from Refenstein Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Ferrostatin-1 (FER-1) was obtained from MCE Company (CAS: 347174–05-4). LPS was obtained from Sigma Company (CAS: 97808–43-0). Ambroxol hydrochloride (AMH) tablets were obtained from Shandong Yuxin Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. (approval number: H20010425). Acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4; ab155282) and ferritin heavy chain (ab75973) were purchased from Abcam (Cambridge, MA, USA). Antibodies against glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4, 52455S), prostaglandin-endoperoxide synthase 2 (PTGS2, 12282S), ferritin heavy chain 1 (FTH1, 3998S), nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2, 12721S), heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1, 70081S), tubulin (2148), BCL-2-associated X (BAX, 2772T) and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH, 2118S) were obtained from Cell Signaling Technology (MA, USA). All primers were synthesized by Thermo Fisher Scientific.

Animals and Modeling

The experiment included forty-eight BALB/c mice (20–22 g, Certificate No: 430727231102400653). All experimental protocols were approved by the Guangzhou Boyao Biosciences Co., Ltd. Ethics Committee (approval number: IAEC-K-230807-01). The mice were purchased from Guangdong Sijia Jingda Biology Science and Technology Co. (Guangzhou, China) and subsequently acclimated for 3 days, consistent with the minimum acclimation standard for rodents recommended by the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.19 The mice are housed in a specific pathogen-free environment, with the room temperature maintained at 20°C. The relative humidity is controlled between 45% and 55%, and the air is exchanged 10 times per hour. Additionally, the lighting is set to alternate on a 12-hour light/12-hour dark cycle. The animal experiments in this study involved CS exposure for establishing the COPD model. To prevent harmful smoke contamination and disturbance to other animals in the institutional animal facility, we conducted the animal experiments in an independent laboratory with a separate dedicated room.

Subsequently, the mice were randomly divided into six groups (n = 8 per group): control (CON), model (MOD), AMH-positive control, FER-1, low-dose AST (AST-L), and high-dose AST (AST-H). The cohort size of 8 animals per group was determined by a priori power analysis (G*Power 3.1, one-way ANOVA, six groups, α = 0.05, power = 0.80, anticipated effect size Cohen’s f ≥ 0.60). During the experiment, the mice had ad libitum access to food and water, and their body weights were recorded daily.

On days 1 and 14, mice in both the MOD group and the treatment groups were administered 50 microliters of LPS solution (0.15 g/L) via intranasal instillation, resulting in a dosage of 7.5 μg per mouse per administration. From Days 2 to 13 and Days 15 to 30, mice were exposed to CS twice daily in a custom-made 20-L transparent chamber.20–22 Each exposure session consisted of lighting two Double Happiness cigarettes sequentially, with a 30-min interval between them. From Day 9 onward, mice in the AST-L and AST-H groups received AST suspension dissolved in 5% sodium carboxymethyl cellulose (CAS: 9085–26-1, McLean) at 30 and 60 mg/kg by oral gavage, respectively. Concurrently, mice in the AMH group received 20 mg/kg AMH via oral gavage, and those in the FER-1 group received 5 mg/kg FER-1 via intraperitoneal injection. Mice in both the CON and MOD groups were administered an equivalent volume of 5% sodium carboxymethyl cellulose via oral gavage once daily for 3 weeks, with the dose adjusted according to daily body weight changes. For details on the experimental procedure, refer to Figure 1A. After intervention, pulmonary function was measured non-invasively in all mice. Subsequently, the mice were anesthetized with 0.3% sodium pentobarbital, and blood and lung tissue samples were collected for subsequent analysis.

Respiratory Index

We measured the pulmonary function of each mouse using a non-invasive mouse pulmonary function analyzer (Zander Instrument Co., Ltd.). Measurements were performed only after the mice had achieved stable breathing. We conducted these assessments prior to tissue collection on the day of sampling to ensure accurate evaluation of drug effects. The parameters determined included enhanced pause (Penh), tidal volume (TV), expiratory flow at 50% tidal volume (EF50), and expiratory flow at 75% tidal volume (EF75).

Pulmonary Index

The whole lungs of mice in each group were harvested, blotted gently with filter paper to remove surface moisture, and weighed to obtain the lung wet weight. The pulmonary index was calculated as the ratio of lung wet weight to body weight, using the formula: Pulmonary index = lung wet weight (g)/body weight (kg)*100%.

Hematoxylin-Eosin (HE) Staining

The right upper lung tissue of each mouse was fixed through injection with 4% paraformaldehyde (Boster, China). Subsequently, the tissues were sectioned at 4-µm thickness for HE staining and examined under light microscopy. Histological evaluation included assessment of alveolar architecture, alveolar destruction, alveolar epithelial integrity, and inflammatory cell infiltration. Based on the microscopic observations, the scoring system was defined as follows: 0 indicated no discernible lesions; 1 denoted slight alveolar enlargement accompanied by minimal inflammatory cell infiltration; 2 corresponded to moderate alveolar enlargement with scattered inflammatory cell infiltration; and 3-4 reflected severe alveolar enlargement, pronounced inflammatory cell infiltration, and partial alveolar destruction.

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

Mouse serum was used in suitable quantities to assess GSH and ROS levels using ELISA kits (Mei-Mian, Shanghai, China), following the manufacturer’s guidelines.

Cell Culture

Beas-2B cells were obtained from Wuhan Shang’en Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). The cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA), and maintained at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere of 95% air and 5% CO2.

Cigarette Smoke Extract (CSE)

An unfiltered cigarette containing 13 mg tar, 1.2 mg nicotine, and 14 mg carbon monoxide was used for preparation. Mainstream CS was drawn into a syringe by an injection pump at a constant rate of 8 mL/s. Next, 10 mL of serum-free medium was added to the apparatus, and the smoke was fully bubbled and mixed with the medium. This procedure was repeated five times at a constant rate. The resulting CSE solution was adjusted to pH 7.4, sterilized using a 0.22-μm filter (Millipore, USA), and defined as 100% CSE. This solution was then diluted to the desired concentration with serum-free medium and used within 30 min of preparation.

MTT Assay

To evaluate the effects of the indicated agents on cell viability, Beas-2B cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5000 cells/well. Cells were treated with various concentrations of CSE (0.5%, 1%, 3%, 5%, 10%, 15%, and 20%), FER-1 (0, 0.5, 1, 2, 5 μM), and AST (0, 2.5, 5, 10, 20, 40, 80, 160, 320 μg·mL−1) for 24 h. After incubation, the culture medium was discarded, and 100 μL of serum-free RPMI-1640 medium containing 10% MTT (3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide; Sigma, USA) was added to each well. The plates were subsequently incubated at 37 °C for 4 h. Then, the MTT solution was removed, and 100 μL of dimethyl sulfoxide was added to each well. The absorbance was measured at 570 nm.

To investigate the protective effect of AST against CSE-induced cytotoxicity in Beas-2B cells, cells were divided into four groups: control (CON) group, 5% CSE group (MOD), 5% CSE + 10 μg/mL AST group (AST-L), and 5% CSE + 20 μg/mL AST group (AST-H). After the corresponding intervention, cell viability was assessed as described above.

Cell Model and Intervention

Beas-2B cells were plated in six-well culture plates (Thermo, China). The experiment included four groups: CON, 5% CSE, 5% CSE+10 µg/mL AST (AST-L), and 5% CSE+20 µg/mL AST (AST-H).

Membrane Permeability Assay

Beas-2B cells in good condition and logarithmic growth phase were selected. The cells were divided into five groups: CON, 5% CSE, AST-L, AST-H, and FER-1. The concentration of FER-1 was 2 μM. The DiOC18 (3) green-fluorescent probe (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Fluorescence images were captured under a confocal microscope, and fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software from three random fields per well. The mean fluorescence intensity was calculated for statistical analysis.

MDA Assay, GSH Assay, and Fe2⁺ Assay

The levels of GSH (cat. no. A006-2-1), MDA (cat. no. A003-1-2), and Fe2+ (cat. no. BC5415) in Beas-2B cells from each group were measured using Solarbio kits (Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

ROS Assay

To detect intracellular ROS levels, Beas-2B cells were seeded in 96-well plates. After 24 h of drug intervention, cells were incubated with DCFH-DA (Solarbio, Beijing, China, cat. no. CA1410) diluted in serum-free medium (1:1000, final concentration 10 μM) at 37°C for 30 min. The medium was then removed, and cells were washed three times with PBS to remove extracellular DCFH-DA. Fluorescence images were captured under an inverted fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Japan) at excitation 488 nm and emission 525 nm. Mean fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software from three random fields per well, and the average value was calculated for statistical analysis.

Lipid Peroxidation Assay

Beas-2B cells were seeded into confocal dishes. Except for the CON group, cells in all other groups were treated with 5% CSE, followed by treatment with AST-H, FER-1, or AMH for 24 h, respectively. The cells were incubated with C11 BODIPY 581/591 (10 μM, ABclonal) for 1 h. Images of the fluorescence signals were captured using a confocal fluorescence microscope (FV3000; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) at an excitation wavelength of 488 nm.

Real-Time Quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was isolated from lung tissues or Beas-2B cells using Trizol reagent (AG, China). Reverse transcription was performed using the 4X EZscript Reverse Transcription Mix II (EZB-RT2GQ, EZBioscience). Real-time PCR was performed on a ViiA7 system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in a 10 μL reaction volume containing 5 μL of 2X SYBR Green Pro Taq HS Premix (ROX plus) (AG11718, AG), 4.6 μL of cDNA template, and 0.2 μL each of forward and reverse primers. The thermocycling conditions consisted of an initial denaturation at 95°C for 30s, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 5s and annealing/extension at 60°C for 30s. GAPDH was used as the internal reference gene. Relative gene expression was quantified using the 2^(−ΔΔCt) method. Details of the primer sequences are provided in Supplementary Table 1 and 2.

Western Blot

Proteins were extracted from lung tissues or Beas-2B cells using radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (CST, 9806S) supplemented with 1% phosphatase and 1%protease inhibitors. Protein concentration was quantified using a bicinchoninic acid assay kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Total proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes. The membranes were blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin in Tris-buffered saline containing Tween 20 for 1 h, then incubated with primary antibodies (dilutions: anti-GPX4 1:1000, anti-ACSL4 1:10,000, anti-PTGS2 1:1000, anti-ferritin 1:1000, anti-FTH1 1:1000, anti-Nrf2 1:1000, anti-HO-1 1:1000, anti-BAX 1:1000, anti-β-tubulin 1:5000, and anti-GAPDH 1:5000) overnight at 4 °C. The next day, the membranes were probed with horseradish peroxidase-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (1:5000; cat. no. 7074S; Cell Signaling Technology, Inc.) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using a Bio-Rad imaging system (Bio-Rad Biosciences, Hercules, CA, USA). The relative protein expression levels were quantified using ImageJ software and normalized against the internal control (β-tubulin or GAPDH).

Statistical Analysis

Experimental data were analyzed using SPSS version 26.0, with results presented as the mean ± SEM (), and the statistical analysis results were plotted using GraphPad Prism 9.5. When the data were normally distributed and exhibited homogeneity of variances, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used. Multiple comparisons were conducted using Tukey’s test when groups demonstrated significant differences. Conversely, when the data were normally distributed but displayed heterogeneity of variances, one-way ANOVA was used, and multiple comparisons were performed using Scheffe’s test in the presence of significant intergroup differences. Non-parametric tests were applied when the data did not conform to a normal distribution. P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Effect of AST on Lung Tissue Injury in COPD Mice

The weights of the mice were systematically recorded every 5 days (Figure 1B). Pulmonary coefficient was used as a key index of lung inflammation and edema. The MOD group showed a significant increase in the pulmonary index relative to that in the CON group. Compared with the MOD group, all treatment groups showed varying degrees of reduction in pulmonary coefficient. Notably, the FER-1 and AST-H groups showed the most obvious improvements (Figure 1C).

Relative to the CON group, tidal volume, EF50, and EF75 were significantly reduced in the MOD group, whereas the Pech value was markedly increased. These results indicate that small airway obstruction occurs during the onset and progression of COPD. Comparison with the MOD group, the FER-1 and AST-H groups exhibited a significant decrease in Pech values accompanied by a notable increase in tidal volume. Furthermore, the AST group showed elevated EF50 and EF75 levels. However, no obvious changes in EF75 values were observed in the AMH, FER-1, or AST-L groups (Figure 1D).

The Pathological changes in mouse lung tissues are shown in Figure 1E. In the CON group, the tracheal wall was smooth, alveoli were well-organized, and no inflammatory cell infiltration was observed around the lung parenchyma, airways, and blood vessels. In contrast, the MOD group showed obvious inflammatory cell infiltration in the lung interstitium, severe tracheal wall thickening, alveolar wall disruption, and irregular enlargement and fusion of alveoli, leading to pulmonary bullae formation. Compared with the MOD group, the AST, FER-1, and AMH groups exhibited alleviated inflammatory cell infiltration and attenuated tracheal wall thickening. The pathological score of the lung tissue in the MOD group was significantly elevated compared with that in the CON group. Compared with the MOD group, the pathological scores of the AMH, FER-1, AST-L, and AST-H groups were significantly reduced.

Given that ferroptosis is driven by iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, we next examined whether AST modulates ferroptosis-related signaling. The relative mRNA expression of ACSL4 and TRF were decreased in the AST‑treated groups relative to the MOD group (Figure 2B). Similarly, protein expression was then detected and is shown in Figure 2C. GPX4 protein expression was markedly reduced in the MOD group versus the CON group, whereas AST treatment restored GPX4, ferritin, and FTH1 protein expression. Additionally, AST treatment significantly reversed the protein levels of PTGS2, ACSL4 in mouse lung tissues as compared with the MOD group. Collectively, these data suggest that AST is associated with the modulation of ferroptosis-related proteins alongside alleviation of oxidative stress.

Protective Effect of AST on Beas-2B Cells Induced by CSE

We examined the impact of varying concentrations of CSE on the viability of Beas-2B cells. As depicted in Figure 3A, exposure to 0.5% and 1% CSE had minimal effects on cell viability. However, a progressive decline in cell viability was observed with increasing CSE concentrations. Notably, 3% CSE significantly diminished cell viability relative to the 0% CSE control group. A 5% CSE concentration decreased cell viability to approximately 50%. Accordingly, 5% CSE was selected as the modeling concentration for subsequent experiments based on our findings. We next assessed the effects of varying FER-1 concentrations on Beas-2B cell viability. As shown in Figure 3B, FER-1 at concentrations up to 5 μM did not significantly affect cell viability. We further examined whether different concentrations of AST influenced cell viability. The results, presented in Figure 3C, demonstrate that AST at concentrations up to 320 μg/mL exerted negligible effects on Beas-2B cell viability following 24 h of treatment. Based on these findings, 10 μg/mL and 20 μg/mL AST were selected as the low- and high-dose concentrations for subsequent experiments.

We examined the effect of AST on cell viability in the presence of 5% CSE (Figure 3D). The results showed a marked reduction in Beas-2B cell viability in the MOD group compared with the CON group, confirming that 5% CSE significantly reduced cell viability. Furthermore, compared with the MOD group, cell survival rates in both the AST-L and AST-H groups were significantly increased. These results indicate that AST protects against CSE-induced cytotoxicity in Beas-2B cells. To exclude the involvement of apoptosis in AST-mediated protection, we examined the effects of AST on apoptosis-related genes in Beas-2B cells (Figure 3E). Compared with the CON group, the relative mRNA expression of Caspase-3, Bax, and Bcl2 showed no significant changes in the MOD group. Similarly, mRNA expression of these genes remained unchanged following AST treatment. Additionally, we examined the impact of AST on BAX protein expression (Figure 3F). Protein levels did not differ significantly between the MOD and CON groups, or among the AMH, FER-1, AST-L, and AST-H groups compared with the MOD group. These results indicate that AST had no obvious effect on apoptosis-related gene expression in CSE-stimulated Beas-2B cells. These results suggest that apoptosis is unlikely to be the primary mechanism underlying AST-mediated protection in this model.

The green-fluorescent probe DiOC18 (3) was used to evaluate the effects of AST on cell membrane integrity in Beas-2B cells (Figure 3G). Relative to the CON group, Beas-2B cells in the MOD group showed obvious cell membrane damage, including membrane rupture and shrinkage. Conversely, the AST, AMH, and FER-1 groups showed significantly increased DiOC18 fluorescence intensity compared with the MOD group, suggesting preserved cell membrane integrity.

We examined the effect of AST on the expression of ferroptosis-related genes in CSE-induced Beas-2B cells (Figures 5D and E). Compared with the CON group, the MOD group exhibited significant upregulation of ACSL4, PTGS2, and TRFC mRNA expression, alongside notable downregulation of GPX4 mRNA. In contrast, compared with the MOD group, GPX4 mRNA expression was significantly elevated following AST treatment, whereas ACSL4, TRFC, and PTGS2 were markedly downregulated. Furthermore, significant differences in the expression of these genes were observed in the AST-H and FER-1 groups compared with the MOD group. We further investigated the effects of AST on the levels of ferroptosis-related proteins in CSE-induced Beas-2B cells (Figure 5F). Compared with the CON group, the MOD group exhibited significant upregulation of PTGS2 and ACSL4 protein expression, alongside marked downregulation of GPX4 and FTH1, an effect that was effectively reversed by AST-H treatment. In contrast, the AST-L and FER-1 groups showed decreasing trends in PTGS2 and ACSL4 expression and increasing trends in GPX4 and FTH1 protein expression; however, these changes were not statistically significant.

To further explore whether the cytoprotective effect of AST involves ferroptosis-related mechanisms, we examined the expression of ferritin and GPX4 in Beas-2B cells subjected to 5% CSE alone or in combination with the ferroptosis inducer erastin (Figure 5G). Compared with the CON group, 5% CSE treatment significantly decreased GPX4 and ferritin expression in the MOD group, and these reductions were effectively restored by AST-H treatment. Furthermore, erastin intervention further reduced GPX4 and ferritin expression relative to the 5% CSE group, suggesting that erastin may enhance CSE-induced ferroptosis.

Discussion

Emerging evidence indicates that Chinese herbal compounds hold great potential in modulating ferroptosis in the respiratory system for the treatment of respiratory diseases, including COPD.23 Astragalus membranaceus in particular has been widely studied for its anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activities in pulmonary disorders.24,25 Prior studies have largely focused on the purified monomer astragaloside IV in lung injury and pulmonary fibrosis models, leaving the role of AST in CS-induced COPD unclear. In the present study, AST significantly improved respiratory function and attenuated lung injury in mice with COPD induced by CS and LPS, accompanied by modulation of ferroptosis-related markers and alleviation of oxidative stress. In CSE-stimulated Beas-2B cells, AST restored cell viability, reduced lipid peroxidation and intracellular Fe2⁺ accumulation, and modulated ferroptosis-related markers. These findings suggest that AST exerts protective effects against CS-induced injury that are associated with modulation of ferroptosis-related pathways and alleviation of oxidative stress.

The combination of chronic CS and repeated LPS instillation is widely used to model both the chronic inflammatory insult and acute exacerbation episodes characteristic of COPD.20–22 In this context, AST significantly improved respiratory function, as evidenced by reduced Penh and increased tidal volume, and attenuated lung injury characterized by reduced inflammatory cell infiltration and tracheal wall thickening. These improvements were accompanied by decreased pulmonary index and ameliorated histopathological scores, indicating that AST confers protective effects against COPD induced by CS and LPS. GPX4, a selenium-dependent glutathione peroxidase, utilizes GSH to reduce toxic lipid hydroperoxides to harmless fatty alcohols, thereby preventing membrane lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis.26,27 GSH depletion is a key hallmark of ferroptosis.28 Conversely, ACSL4, a member of the long-chain acyl-CoA synthetase family, is a key enzyme regulating lipid composition, particularly phosphatidylethanolamine, which promotes lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis. TRF mediates iron uptake. Ferritin and its heavy chain subunit FTH1 sequester intracellular iron, and lysosomal degradation of ferritin releases free iron to promote ferroptosis.29,30 PTGS2 is commonly monitored as a downstream indicator of ferroptosis-associated lipid peroxidation.31 Consistent with this framework, our in vivo data showed that AST elevated GSH levels, decreased ROS accumulation, restored GPX4 expression, and reduced the levels of ACSL4, PTGS2, ferritin, and FTH1, as well as decreased TRF mRNA expression in mice exposed to CS and LPS. These findings suggest that the protective effects of AST in vivo are associated with modulation of ferroptosis-related markers. To investigate whether bronchial epithelial cells contribute to these alterations, we subsequently examined the effects of AST in CSE-stimulated Beas-2B cells.

Beas-2B cells are normal human bronchial epithelial cells widely used in in vitro COPD modeling. To exclude the involvement of apoptosis in the protective effects of AST, we examined the expression of apoptosis-related markers. Bcl2, Bax, and Caspase-3 are key regulators of the intrinsic apoptotic pathway.32,33 After CSE modeling and AST administration, both the mRNA and protein levels of Bax remained unchanged in the MOD and AST groups compared with the CON group, and the mRNA levels of Bcl2 and Caspase-3 showed no significant alterations, suggesting that AST did not attenuate CSE-induced cell death through apoptosis. Membrane rupture is a characteristic morphological feature of ferroptosis. Quantitative DiOC18 analysis revealed that AST, AMH, and FER-1 all restored cell membrane integrity in CSE-exposed Beas-2B cells; the comparable efficacy of AST and the ferroptosis inhibitor FER-1 suggests that this protection is associated with ferroptosis-related modulation.

Nrf2 is a master transcription factor of the antioxidant response, and its downstream target HO-1 catalyzes heme degradation to exert cytoprotective effects.34 In CSE-exposed Beas-2B cells, AST reduced MDA and ROS, increased GSH levels, and upregulated Nrf2/HO-1 protein expression, consistent with alleviation of oxidative stress. However, whether Nrf2/HO-1 activation directly contributes to ferroptosis inhibition remains to be established. Beyond oxidative stress, we next examined whether AST modulates ferroptosis-specific markers in CSE-exposed Beas-2B cells. AST decreased intracellular Fe2⁺ levels and reduced lipid peroxidation. At the mRNA level, AST restored GPX4 expression and reduced ACSL4, PTGS2, and TRFC levels. At the protein level, AST restored GPX4 and FTH1 expression and reduced ACSL4 and PTGS2 levels. These in vitro findings were concordant with our in vivo observations. Erastin, a classic ferroptosis inducer widely used in experimental research, selectively inhibits the cystine/glutamate antiporter to deplete intracellular GSH and trigger lipid peroxidation-dependent cell death.35,36 To further validate the involvement of ferroptosis, we combined CSE with erastin and evaluated GPX4 and ferritin expression in Beas-2B cells. Erastin further reduced GPX4 and ferritin expression relative to CSE alone, whereas co-treatment with AST-H appeared to attenuate this effect, suggesting that AST may counteract erastin-enhanced ferroptotic stress. These findings suggest that AST-mediated protection in Beas-2B cells is associated with modulation of ferroptosis-related markers.

AMH is a mucolytic agent that promotes mucus clearance and is commonly used as a symptomatic treatment for respiratory diseases, including COPD. In contrast, AST-H showed comparable efficacy to AMH in improving respiratory function and reducing lung injury. AST and the ferroptosis inhibitor FER-1 showed comparable efficacy in restoring cell membrane integrity and modulating GPX4 and ACSL4 expression, suggesting that AST-mediated protection is associated with ferroptosis-related pathway modulation. Building upon prior evidence for the protective effects of AS-IV in lung injury and COPD models,17,18 our findings indicate that the total saponin extract similarly modulates ferroptosis-related markers in this experimental COPD model. The presence of multiple astragalosides (I, II, III, and IV) within the total extract may confer broader protective effects through potential synergistic interactions, although the individual contributions of each component require further investigation. Notably, the effects of AST on ferroptosis appear to be context-dependent. Whereas AST has been shown to promote ferroptosis in gastric cancer cells via SIRT3 upregulation,37 our data indicate an anti-ferroptotic role in experimental COPD models both in vivo and in vitro. This discrepancy likely reflects tissue-specific microenvironments and disease contexts, highlighting the importance of interpreting AST effects within specific disease settings.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. Although we explored the preliminary association between AST and ferroptosis-related markers in experimental COPD models, genetic validation experiments are needed to establish causal necessity rather than pharmacological association. The crosstalk between oxidative stress and ferroptosis signaling remains to be clarified. Additionally, our in vitro findings were obtained in Beas-2B bronchial epithelial cells; future studies are warranted to validate these effects in alveolar epithelial cells to more comprehensively assess the protective potential of AST in COPD. Furthermore, the potential involvement of alternative cell death modalities, such as pyroptosis or necroptosis, cannot be excluded. Finally, future studies are needed to identify the specific bioactive constituents and direct molecular targets of AST. As a multi-component extract, AST may have off-target effects due to the presence of multiple bioactive constituents; this was not systematically assessed in the current study. Transcriptomic, proteomic, or network pharmacology approaches in future investigations would help comprehensively elucidate AST’s specificity and safety.

Conclusion

This study suggests that AST ameliorates experimental COPD, and these effects were accompanied by changes in ferroptosis-related markers. These findings provide preliminary evidence supporting continued investigation of AST in preclinical COPD models and offer potential directions for future mechanistic research.
