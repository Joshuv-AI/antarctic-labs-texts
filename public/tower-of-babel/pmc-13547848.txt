STK11 Mutations and Deletions Define an Aggressive Molecular Subgroup of Cervical Adenocarcinoma
Authors: Emma W Robinson, Elisabeth A Murphy, Anaseidy Albanez, Sonam Tulsyan, Hong Lou, Yi Xie, Weiyin Zhou, Qian Yang, Tawnjerae Joe, Tongwu Zhang, Jia Liu, Wen Luo, Dongjing Wu, Herbert Higson, Mathias Viard, Mary Carrington, Matthew Oberley, Hesler Morales, Lisa Mirabello, Enrique Alvirez, Roberto Orozco, Eduardo Gharzouzi, Michael Dean
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCervical adenocarcinoma accounts for 15%–20% of cervical cancers and is associated with poorer survival and reduced response to screening and immunotherapy compared with squamous cell carcinoma (SCC). The genomic drivers underlying this molecular subgroup remain incompletely characterized. Whole‐exome sequencing was performed on 302 invasive cervical cancers from Guatemala and Venezuela. Structural variation analysis was conducted using SNP‐array and whole‐genome sequencing data. Findings were replicated in more than 4600 additional cervical cancer samples from TCGA, AACR Project GENIE, MSKCC, and Caris datasets. TP53 mutations were more frequent in adenocarcinoma than SCC, particularly in HPV‐negative tumors. STK11 alterations, including mutations and focal deletions, were significantly enriched in HPV‐positive adenocarcinomas compared with SCC and affected 23% of adenocarcinomas overall. Whole‐genome analyses identified recurrent focal deletions, inversions, chromosomal rearrangements, and breakage‐fusion‐bridge events involving chromosome 19p and STK11 that were not detected by exome sequencing alone. STK11 alterations were associated with younger age at diagnosis, poorer overall survival, and inferior outcomes following immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) therapy. STK11 alterations significantly co‐occurred with YAP1 amplification but were largely mutually exclusive with PIK3CA mutation. Cervical adenocarcinomas also demonstrated significantly lower CD274 (PD‐L1) expression than SCC. STK11 alterations define a distinct molecular subgroup of cervical adenocarcinoma characterized by structural disruption of chromosome 19p, younger age at onset, and poorer clinical outcomes. These findings have implications for molecular classification and future targeted therapeutic approaches in cervical cancer.Keywords: adenocarcinoma, cervical cancer, human papillomavirus, STK11 tumor suppressor, whole exome sequencing

Introduction

Cervical cancer accounts for over 600,000 new cases and 340,000 new deaths annually [1]. Over 90% of cases and deaths occur in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs) [2]. Guatemala has one of the highest rates of cervical cancer in the world, has incomplete cervical cancer screening [3], and there are few studies of invasive cervical cancer in the country [2]. Characterizing the disease in LMICs is therefore critical to reducing disease incidence and mortality.

Cervical cancer is defined by two major histological types: adenocarcinoma (AD) and squamous cell carcinoma (SCC). Most cervical cancer cases are SCC, and the two histological types are epidemiologically and prognostically distinct [4]. According to WHO criteria, adenocarcinomas can be further divided into adenosquamous tumors, HPV‐positive adenocarcinomas, and largely HPV‐negative gastric‐type carcinomas [5, 6]. While HPV16 is the most common driver of both tumor types, HPV18 and HPV45 are found more frequently in AD than SCC, and distinct sublineages of HPV16 confer elevated risk of AD [7, 8]. AD patients are frequently diagnosed at a younger age than SCC patients with more advanced disease, have poorer 5‐year survival, and higher recurrence [9, 10]. Furthermore, cytology is less effective at detecting AD than SCC, leading to diagnoses at more advanced stages [11]. Despite these histological classifications, both AD and SCC tumors are treated similarly [12] with conventional treatments consisting of surgery, chemotherapy, and radiation. There are few approved targetted therapies. We have previously reported that PIK3CA mutation is present in 32% of cervical cancer cases, and YAP1 amplification (12% of tumors) is associated with poorer survival [13]. To identify additional molecular alterations of cervical adenocarcinoma, we analyzed invasive cervical tumors from Guatemala and Venezuela using whole‐exome sequencing, SNP‐array analysis, and whole‐genome sequencing approaches. We investigated associations between genomic alterations, histological subtype, HPV subtype, age at diagnosis, and clinical outcomes, with particular focus on structural and mutational disruption of STK11 in cervical adenocarcinoma.

Materials and Methods

Patient Recruitment

Primary and treatment‐naïve tumor biopsies and matching blood samples were collected from women (21–86 years of age) evaluated for cervical cancer, before any non‐surgical therapy, at the Instituto Nacional de Cancerología (INCAN) in Guatemala and Hospital Central Universitario (Dr. Antonio Maria Pineda) in Venezuela from 2011 to 2015. The IRBs in Guatemala (Comité de Investigación del Instituto de Cancerología, VPH‐0112) and Venezuela (Comisión de Ética y Bioética del Hospital Central Universitario, CB‐01/12) approved the study, and all patients provided informed consent. Trained personnel completed questionnaires with reproductive and risk factor variables. Data on weight or BMI were not obtained. All samples were coded.

Clinical Staging and Histological Subtype

In Guatemala, clinical staging was performed at the time of biopsy using the FIGO system. Over 90% of the samples are Stage II or III. Histological subtype was determined from H&E‐stained tumor slides and recorded in the pathology report. Adenocarcinomas and adenosquamous tumors were combined for analyses and compared to squamous cell carcinoma. None of the specimens are from cone surgery or hysterectomy. These procedures, if clinically indicated, were performed after the sample for this study was obtained. A portion of the records could not be located, and an independent review and retrospective subclassification according to the current WHO/IECC criteria was not feasible. In Venezuela, at the time of collection, there was no functioning pathology service, and neither histology nor stage information could be obtained.

Sample Handling and DNA Extraction

Tissue was immediately placed in RNAlater, frozen at −20°C, and blood samples were directly frozen at −20°C. Samples were transported to the NCI, and cervical tumor tissue was extracted with the Zymo Research Kit and whole blood with Qiagen. Purified DNA was quantified and quality controlled by Nanodrop and Qubit.

Whole Exome Sequencing

Whole‐exome sequencing was performed on randomly selected samples, based on DNA availability. Where available, tumor and normal (blood) samples of CESC patients were analyzed to identify somatic variants. DNA libraries were created from 200 ng of tumor or blood DNA using the Kapa HyperPlus Kit and xGen Dual Index UMI Adapters, according to the manufacturer's protocol. Each multiplexed sequencing library was then sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000. Read lengths of 2 × 150 bp were used, yielding an average coverage of 500X for tumor samples and 50X–100X for normal (blood) samples. Samples were quality‐controlled, aligned to the human hg38 genome build, and variant calling was performed using Manta, Muse, Mutect2, Strelka, and Vardict. Variants that occurred in both tumor and normal DNA across the paired samples were excluded, and a panel of normals was used to filter tumor‐only samples.

Verification of Mutational Data

Mutational data for 315 genes known to be frequently mutated in cancer [14] were analyzed further, and a subset of 24 of these genes was designated as “cervical cancer driver genes” based on previous research [15, 16, 17]. Each mutation found in the 315 cancer genes was further evaluated to determine if it was true or an artifact. For tumor‐only analysis, mutations were excluded if they met any of the following conditions: (1) having an overall allelic frequency of greater than 0.01% in gnomAD v 4.1 [18]; (2) being designated as “Benign” or “Likely Benign” in ClinVar [19]; (3) having a variant allele frequency (VAF) of less than 2% from whole‐exome sequence (WES) mutation calling; and (4) not found as somatic mutations in tumor‐normal pairs but observed in multiple tumor‐only samples. For tumor‐normal pair analysis, mutations were excluded from the dataset if they were designated as “Benign” or “Likely Benign” in ClinVar (RRID:SCR_006169) [19].

HPV Typing

Cervical tumor DNA was amplified using the GP5+/6+HPV L1 gene primers and Sanger‐sequenced. Sequences were used in a BLAST (RRID:SCR_004870) search to identify the HPV type. In addition, selected samples were sequenced with an Ion Torrent overlapping primer set targeting 14 HPV types [20]. Not all samples contained sufficient DNA for HPV typing after whole‐exome sequencing. To fill in missing HPV type data and validate HPV typing performed using the above methods, we aligned exome sequencing data against 65 HPV types. Because this method is less sensitive than other methods, only samples with confirmed integration sites or with more than 100 viral reads were called positive, and ambiguous samples were labeled “unknown” rather than “negative.” Samples labeled as negative were negative in one or both PCR‐based assays.

Mutational Signature Analysis

Somatic SNVs from VCF files were processed using SigProfilerMatrixGenerator (Python, RRID:SCR_024202) to generate single‐base substitution (SBS96) mutational count matrices based on the human reference genome (GRCh37). SNVs were categorized into 96 trinucleotide contexts following standard COSMIC (RRID:SCR_002260) definitions. De novo mutational signatures were extracted from the SBS96 matrix using SigProfilerExtractor (RRID:SCR_023121) with default NMF‐based workflows. A range of potential signature numbers was evaluated, and the final solution was automatically selected based on signature stability, reproducibility, and sample reconstruction accuracy. Extracted signatures were then compared with COSMIC v3 reference mutational signatures using cosine similarity, and each de novo signature was assigned to its closest COSMIC match. Signature exposures were estimated per sample and normalized to the total mutation burden. Samples with insufficient SNV counts were excluded from de novo extraction to ensure robust signature assignment. Some samples originally classified as HPV‐negative had strong APOBEC signatures and were designated as HPV “hit and run” samples [21].

Structural Variant Calling of Latin American Tumors

Amplifications and deletions of specific gene regions were determined using the allelic ratio of variants from either the WES or SNP array data. The ASCAT (Allele‐Specific Copy Number Analysis of Tumors) (RRID:SCR_016868), GISTIC (RRID:SCR_000151), and MoChA (MOsaic CHromosomal Alterations) programs were used, and the analyses incorporated results from all three methods. ASCAT derives copy‐number profiles of tumor cells, accounting for normal cell admixture and tumor aneuploidy, thereby inferring tumor purity and ploidy [22]. GISTIC identifies regions of the genome that are significantly amplified or deleted and was applied to tumor‐normal pairs as well as tumor‐only samples [23]. MoChA calls mosaic chromosomal alterations starting from phased VCF files [24]. As the GISTIC and MoChA deletions were not orthogonally validated, to be conservative, only homozygous deletions were included in the analysis of mutations and deletions. Targeted analysis was performed for amplification of the YAP1, CD274 (PD‐L1), EGFR, and ERBB2 (HER2) genes and deletions of STK11 and PTEN.

Focal Deletions in the STK11 Gene in Whole‐Genome Sequence (WGS)

Focal deletions in cervical cell lines (6 in total) and 14 TCGA (RRID:SCR_016637) CESC samples were identified by manual review in IGV of WGS. Junction reads were used to determine the breakpoints of the deletions. The copy number of selected chr19 genes was used to identify TCGA tumors with 19p copy number loss. Manual review of WGS data was used to determine the breakpoints of deletion events. Putative breakage‐fusion‐bridge (BFB) events were identified by the characteristic stair‐step pattern of read depth and the presence of inverted reads spaced 500–2000 bp apart due to digestion and deletion of the remainder of the chromosome to the telomere [13].

Whole Genome Analysis of Cervical Cancer Cell Lines

Cervical cancer cell lines (Tulsyan, in preparation) are presented as supporting data. They were sequenced as previously described [13] and analysis was performed with Oxford Nanopore pipelines and on the Cancer Genomics Cloud (Cancer Genomics Cloud) and by manual review in IGV.

Replication in Publicly Available Datasets

The TCGA [17], AACR Project GENIE (AACR), and MSKCC data were accessed via cBioPortal (cBioPortal for Cancer Genomics) [25]. Data on Japanese cervical cancer are from Hirose 2020 [26].

Statistical Analyses

Chi‐squared and Fisher's exact tests were used to investigate differences in mutational frequency by histology and HPV type for each gene. Associations between mutated genes were also examined using chi‐squared tests and Fisher's exact tests. Wilcoxon tests were conducted to investigate differences in mutational frequencies by age. Results were evaluated in external samples using the Caris, TCGA, and AACR datasets. RStudio (R version 4.4.0) was used to conduct selected analyses.

Results

Demographics

Histological data were available for 74% of the 302 Latin American samples, and 78% were squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), and 22% were adenocarcinoma (AD). The median age of the cohort was 51.5 years (IQR 44.0–61.0) (Table 1, Figure S1). The median age was similar for SCC (51.2) and AD (51.8). The most common HPV type was HPV16 (29%), followed by HPV18 (18%) and HPV45 (12%). HPV16 is more frequent, and HPV18 and 45 are less frequent in SCC compared to AD (HPV16 33% in SCC, 17% in AD; HPV18/45 24% and 20% in SCC 31%–23% in AD) (Table 1).

Cancer Gene Mutational Analysis

To identify the most frequently mutated genes in Latin American cervical cancer, we performed whole‐exome sequencing on 302 samples (Figure 1; Figure S2) and identified mutations and copy‐number variations. As previously described, the most frequently mutated gene was PIK3CA (35%) [16]. Other frequently mutated genes included FAT1 (18%), FBXW7 (12%), and STK11 (10%) (Table S1). We identified genomic amplification and deletions including gain of chr3q (PIK3CA/TP63), chr7p11 (EGFR), chr9p24 (CD274/PD‐L1), chr11q (YAP1), chr17q12 (ERBB2), and loss of 19p (STK11) (Figure S3). In total, 14% of tumors had STK11 deletion, whereas 5% of tumors had YAP1 amplification (Figure S4).

Comparison of Gene Variants in Cervical AD and SCC

The mutation frequencies in AD and SCC were compared (Figure 2A). Two genes (TP53 and STK11) were significantly more frequently mutated in AD than in SCC. Significantly more individuals with adenocarcinoma (21%) had a TP53 mutation than those with SCC (6%) (χ 2 = 4.4; p = 0.0037). In adenocarcinomas, 30% harbored an STK11 mutation, compared with 9% of SCCs (p = 0.0002). The association between TP53 and AD is known [27] but the association between STK11 mutation and cervical adenocarcinoma has not been investigated. The frequency of STK11 deletions by histology trended in the same direction as mutations: 20% of ADs had STK11 deletion, versus 12% in SCC. In total, 23% of individuals with cervical cancer in our dataset had an alteration in STK11, 35% of individuals with adenocarcinoma had an alteration in STK11 compared with 19% of individuals with SCC (p = 0.026). STK11 alterations are the most prevalent cancer gene mutations in cervical adenocarcinoma in this dataset.

Research has shown that ~0.3% of cervical tumors are HPV‐negative, and these are predominantly AD with TP53 mutations [28]. We identified 20 Latin American samples as HPV negative. Nine AD (out of 48 total) and nine SCC (159 total). Mutational signatures of tumors classified a subset of HPV‐negative tumors as “HPV hit‐and‐run” based on a strong APOBEC signature (Tables S2, S3). Of the most frequently mutated genes, only STK11 mutations were significantly more frequent in HPV‐positive AD (Figure S5A). Sensitivity tests indicate that missing histology data were unlikely to account for the elevated STK11 mutation rate in AD (Table S4).

The higher prevalence of STK11 mutations in AD was replicated in other datasets (Table 2). In the AACR dataset, 8.3% of 325 ADs and 4.3% of 345 SCCs had an STK11 mutation (p = 0.035). In the Caris CodeAI dataset, 8% of 828 ADs and 4% of 2380 SCCs had an STK11 mutation (p = 2.5e‐06). This trend held but was not significant in a Japanese dataset [29], MSKCC [30], and our cervical cell lines. Only in TCGA were STK11 mutations more frequent in SCC, but this was not significant (Figure 2B). In datasets with HPV information, STK11 mutations were more frequent in HPV‐positive tumors (Figure 5A). In Caris CodeAI, STK11 deletions occurred significantly more frequently in AD (9.0%) than SCC (4.7%) (p = 7e‐06). This trend was observed but not significant in the Japanese (22), MSKCC, and cervical cancer cell line datasets. STK11 deletions occurred more frequently in SCC in the TCGA and AACR datasets, but this was not significant (Figure 2B, Figure S5B). Interestingly, the expression of STK11 is very low in normal cervical ectocervix, the precursor to adenocarcinomas (Figure 2C).

Whereas TCGA PanCancer analysis identified seven cases of homozygous deletion. Our GISTIC and WGS analysis identified 13 additional homozygous or focal deletions in STK11 (41 bp–1.1 MB) (Figure 3B, Table S5). GISTIC values in TCGA tumors for chr19p genes showed that STK11 had low copy number in 12 samples, with a focal high‐copy‐number region centromeric to STK11, consistent with BFB events (Figure S7, Table S6). Long‐read WGS enabled resolution of a 10.3 MB BFB event in SNU‐902 cervical cancer cells (Figure S7). The site of BFB events ranged from just centromeric to STK11 (1.8 MB) to 43.5 MB distal, with a cluster of six BFBs at 7.5–8.5 MB. One sample shows integration of HPV51 at chr19:12.79 MB, and 10‐fold amplification of JUNB (Figure S8). A total of 20 STK11 deletions were identified in SNP array data from Latin American cervical cancer samples, and 17 tumors had focal loss of STK11 (Figure 3, Figure S4B). We compiled all STK11 mutations, and 82% are loss‐of‐function or recurrent. In Guatemala, STK11‐mutant AD individuals had an average age of 42, significantly lower than STK11‐WT AD (54; p = 0.0083) (Figure 4A; Figure S9A). There was no difference in the average ages of STK11‐deleted individuals with AD (54) compared to STK11‐WT (54). In Caris, STK11 alteration was associated with significantly younger age in both SCC and AD (Figure 4B) and lower STK11 gene expression (Figure 5B). This finding was replicated in AACR, where STK11‐mutated ADs had an average age of 44, and STK11‐WT ADs 49 (p = 0.029) (Figure 4C). STK11 deletions were associated with younger age, but this was not significant (Table S7). These findings indicate that STK11 mutation is associated with an earlier age of diagnosis, especially in individuals with AD.

STK11 deletions and mutations were combined in TCGA and Guatemala. In TCGA, HPV16+ SCC tumors were less likely to harbor an STK11 alteration (p = 2.0e‐04) (Figure 4D). HPV45+ tumors were more likely to have an STK11 alteration (p = 0.0028). In Caris, STK11 alterations were less frequent in HPV16‐positive samples across both adenocarcinoma and SCC (Figure 4E). No STK11 mutations or deletions were observed in HPV‐negative individuals (Figure 1; Figure S9B). In Guatemala, HPV45+ individuals were more likely to have STK11 alterations (Figures S9C, S9D).

Association of STK11 Alteration With Other Features

We previously established that PIK3CA mutation and YAP1 amplification are mutually exclusive [31]. Therefore, we evaluated the co‐occurrence between PIK3CA mutation, YAP1 amplification, and STK11 alteration. In TCGA, 9% of STK11‐WT tumors had YAP1 amplification, compared to 32% of STK11‐altered tumors (p = 0.004) (Figure S10A). There was also a difference seen in Caris SCC, with 14% of STK11‐altered samples having YAP1 amplification versus 7% in STK11 WT (p = 0.015) (Figure S10A). In AACR and Caris datasets, STK11 alterations are mutually exclusive with PIK3CA mutation (p < 0.00001, in Caris) (Figure S10C, D). In SCC, the PIK3CA mutation is significantly and negatively correlated with the STK11 alteration in Caris, Guatemala, and TCGA (Figure S11A). In Caris, tumor mutation burden is significantly higher in SCC than in AD, as previously reported; however, STK11 alterations are not associated with higher mutation burden or microsatellite instability (MSI) (Figure S11B, S11C).

Caris individuals with STK11‐altered adenocarcinomas had poorer overall survival (HR = 1.54, 95% CI: 1.11–2.15) (p = 0.011) (Figure 5A). We identified 1065 Caris subjects who received ICI therapy, and STK11‐altered adenocarcinoma subjects had poorer survival (HR = 2.35, 95% CI: 1.3–4.2, p = 0.003) (Figure 5B). The uncommon STK11‐altered/PIK3CA‐mutated samples had poorer survival (HR = 1.9; 95% CI: 1.3–2.8; p = 0.002) (Figure 5C). While age and ancestry are significant cofactors, the impact on the hazard ratio was minimal (Figure S12). Therefore, STK11 alterations are associated with poorer survival overall in cervical cancer and in patients treated with ICI.

Immune Escape Mutations

Mutations in genes involved in immune escape occurred relatively frequently in this sample: 16% of individuals had at least one mutation in HLA‐A, HLA‐B, HLA‐C, B2M, or CASP8; a deletion in HLA‐A, HLA‐B, or HLA‐C; or an amplification of CD274/PD‐L1. The most frequently mutated immune gene was CASP8 (7%) (Figure S13). The frequency of immune escape mutations did not differ significantly between AD and SCC; however, CD274 expression is significantly lower in TCGA AD compared to SCC (p = 0.0001) (Figure S14).

Recent research has shown that 0.3% of cervical tumors are HPV‐negative, and these are predominantly AD with TP53 mutations [28]. We identified 20 Latin American samples as HPV‐negative, 9 AD (out of 48 total), and 9 SCC (out of 159 total). Mutational signatures of tumors classified a subset of HPV‐negative tumors as “HPV hit‐and‐run” based on a strong APOBEC signature (Tables S3, S4).

Discussion

In the US, the 5‐year survival for invasive cervical cancer has improved modestly [10]. Identifying key oncogenic pathways is critical to subdividing cervical cancers and informing targeted therapies. We identified STK11 as the most frequently altered gene in cervical adenocarcinoma. Alterations to STK11 include mutations, focal and homozygous gene deletions, and WGS is required to identify all alterations [32]. Individuals with STK11‐altered adenocarcinomas are diagnosed at a younger age and have poorer survival [29]. STK11‐altered tumors are more frequently HPV18‐ or HPV45‐positive and less likely to be HPV16+ than STK11‐WT tumors. However, no STK11 alterations were detected in HPV‐negative tumors, although HPV‐negative, STK11‐mutated tumors have been observed [33]. YAP1 amplification is more common in individuals with STK11 alterations, whereas PIK3CA mutations are less common and are mutually exclusive. This data suggests that STK11‐altered tumors are a distinct molecular subgroup of cervical adenocarcinomas (Figure 5D).

STK11 is a known tumor suppressor causing Peutz‐Jeghers Syndrome (PJS), which has an increased likelihood of cancer, including adenocarcinoma [34, 35, 36, 37]. STK11 phosphorylates and activates AMP‐activated protein kinase (AMPK) [38], increasing ATP production, promoting cellular survival, but decreasing cell proliferation. Activation of the STK11/AMPK pathway also inhibits the mTOR pathway [38]. The most common gynecological cancer among women with PJS is cervical adenocarcinoma. STK11 is the third most frequently mutated gene in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) and is associated with a poor response to immunotherapy [39, 40]. STK11 mutations are associated with lower PD‐L1 expression and T cell infiltration compared to normal tissue [41].

In cervical cancer, we show that STK11 mutation is associated with an earlier age of diagnosis and poorer survival. However, little is known regarding the function of STK11 in cervical cancer. In the lung, STK11 mutation results in decreased YAP1 phosphorylation and signaling [42, 43]. Our data suggest an interaction between these two pathways in cervical cancer. We find that STK11 alterations are more common in HPV18 and HPV45‐positive tumors compared to HPV16‐containing tumors, as seen for other cancer gene mutations [44].

Using WGS and a SNP array, we identified focal STK11 deletions undetected by WES, as in lung cancer [32]. We identified loss of the STK11 region in multiple tumors, with a signature of BFB cycles [45] centromeric to STK11. WGS identified inverted reads with staggered ends and DNA amplification, characteristic of BFB events [13]. While there are multiple examples of BFB events leading to oncogene amplification, such as ERBB2 [46] and YAP1 [13], ours is, to our knowledge, the first report of BFB associated with frequent loss of one allele of a tumor suppressor gene.

The only FDA‐approved immunotherapy for cervical cancer, Pembrolizumab, is more effective in cervical SCC than cervical AD [47]. Furthermore, the expression of CD274/PD‐L1, the target of Pembrolizumab, is significantly higher in SCCs than ADs [48] (Figure S14). Given low PD‐L1 expression and the limited efficacy of immunotherapy in STK11‐mutated lung adenocarcinoma, Pembrolizumab may be less effective in cervical AD, in part due to the high frequency of STK11 alterations. In Caris, STK11‐altered adenocarcinomas responded poorly to ICI therapy. Whether the effect of STK11 loss of function on the immune response to lung tumors and cervical cancers is similar remains to be determined.

HPV infection causes over 98% of cervical cancer. However, there is a subset of cervical cancer that is HPV negative. HPV‐negative cervical cancer is predominantly AD and is frequently TP53‐mutated [28, 49, 50]. In our study, half of the HPV‐ADs had TP53 mutations, but individuals with HPV‐negative tumors did not differ in age at collection.

This study represents an extensive investigation of cervical cancer in LMICs and the largest study in Latin America, a region with a high disease burden [1]. We evaluated our findings using nearly 7000 cervical tumors from public datasets (TCGA and AACR) and Caris. The TCGA dataset is an outlier, as no individuals with adenocarcinoma had STK11 mutations or deletions, whereas STK11 alterations were associated with AD in all other studies, albeit at varying frequencies. TCGA tumors showed a similar distribution of stage to those of the Latin American cohort (Figure S15). Our study demonstrates the importance of using multiple cohorts from distinct backgrounds.

Our study has several limitations. Although we sequenced 302 tumors, histology was known for only 222, and histology and independent review and reclassification according to current WHO/IECC criteria were not possible. Future studies with centralized pathology review and subclassification are needed to address the role of STK11 alteration in adenocarcinoma subtypes. HPV type was known for 253, and a matched normal sample was unavailable for some tumors. We could not verify the HPV‐negative tumors through either whole genome sequencing or immunohistochemistry P16 staining. Given the low frequency of AD, we may have lacked the power to detect additional mutated cancer genes. The study included subjects with missing demographic data, and clinical outcomes were unavailable from Guatemala or Venezuela. Therefore, we were unable to investigate survival in these populations. The deletions inferred from GISTIC and MoChA were not orthogonally validated but excluded from combined analysis. STK11 deletions could not be validated by immunohistochemistry; however, McCabe et al. documented that cervical cancer cell lines with homozygous mutations lack detectable STK11/LKB1 protein [51]. Clinical outcomes data in Caris codeAI are derived from insurance claims and are incomplete for certain variables, such as tumor stage and age at sample collection. Therefore, the results are exploratory and hypothesis‐generating. It will be essential to determine the functional consequences of STK11 loss in cervical cells, particularly in adenocarcinomas.

Few FDA‐approved targeted therapies for cervical cancer currently exist. Identifying genetic subgroups of cervical cancer is essential to developing targeted therapies. We have demonstrated that PIK3CA‐mutated and YAP1‐amplified tumors represent distinct subsets of cervical cancer that may uniquely respond to treatments [52]. In this study, we have identified STK11‐altered tumors as an additional group that may be relevant as STK11‐targeted therapies are developed. These findings support further investigation of therapeutic strategies for patients with STK11‐altered cervical tumors.
