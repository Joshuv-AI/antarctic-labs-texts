Risk prediction of early-onset myocardial infarction using plasma proteomics, conventional risk factors, and polygenic risk score
Authors: Zhong-Yue Liu, Fei Fang, Yu-Wen Qian, Ji-Mei Gu, Jun-Yu Zhao, Jie-Qiong Lyu, Meng-Yuan Miao, Hai-Peng Wang, Cai-Long Chen, Guo-Chong Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and objectivesEpidemiological trends indicate a concerning rise in early-onset cases of myocardial infarction (MI). We aimed to assess and compare the ability of plasma proteomics, conventional risk factors, and polygenic risk score (PRS) for the risk prediction of early-onset myocardial infarction (EOMI).MethodsIncluded were 13,097 participants aged 50 or younger, without prevalent cardiovascular diseases. The participants were randomly divided into training and validation sets. EOMI was defined as MI diagnosed before age 55. In the training set, 2,093 plasma proteins were assessed for the associations with incident EOMI using Cox proportional hazards regression models. Important proteins were selected by the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression to develop protein-based models. The predictive performance of protein-based models, conventional risk factors, and PRS, either alone or as combinations, was assessed in the validation set.ResultsTwo protein-based models were constructed using 22 key proteins selected by LASSO. Each standard-deviation increment of a weighted protein score was associated with a 2.57-fold higher risk of EOMI. Incorporating this protein score (ΔC-index = 0.125; 95% CI: 0.040, 0.213), a protein panel (ΔC-index = 0.189; 95% CI: 0.065, 0.276), or other conventional risk factors (ΔC-index = 0.158; 95% CI: 0.039, 0.239) each significantly improved the predictive performance over a basic model including age, sex, and race/ethnicity, whereas adding PRS did not. The combination of the protein panel and conventional risk factors demonstrated the best discrimination ability (C-index = 0.875; 95% CI: 0.814, 0.935).ConclusionsPlasma proteomics enhanced the risk prediction for EOMI beyond conventional risk factors and PRS. These findings may have implications for risk stratification and personalized prevention which prevent or delay the onset of myocardial infarction among relatively younger population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12986-025-01040-w.Keywords: Risk prediction, Early-onset myocardial infarction, Proteomics, UK biobank

Introduction

Myocardial infarction (MI) is the leading cause of premature death worldwide [1]. While the majority of cases occur at a relatively older age and the absolute incidence of MI remains low in younger adults, epidemiological trends indicate a concerning rise in early-onset cases [2]. As compared with older patients, young patients have a higher risk of recurrent cardiovascular disease (CVD) and premature death [3–5]. Early identification of at-risk young individuals is a critical strategy to reduce the disease burden and extend the healthy life expectancy.

In epidemiological research and clinical practices, cardiovascular risk prediction models, which are derived using conventional cardiovascular risk factors, have been commonly used for risk prediction, early prevention, or treatment optimization [6]. However, the predictive performance of these traditional risk models (e.g., the Framingham Risk Score, the Pooled Cohort Equations), which are primarily derived from middle-aged and older population, remains suboptimal for younger population [7]. This is because age is a dominant component in these algorithms, leading to very low calculated risk scores in young adults even in the presence of other risk factors [7]. Furthermore, for a same conventional cardiovascular risk factor (e.g., cigarette smoking and obesity), the contribution to early-onset and that to later-onset MI could vary substantially [8], and there are unique risk factors (e.g., substance use) for early-onset cases [9–11]. These indicate the need for more specific approaches to accurately capture cardiovascular risk in young individuals.

Recent epidemiological evidence suggests that genetic risk is more strongly associated with, and may serve as a better predictor of MI in early life [6, 12, 13], although the utility of genetic information alone in risk prediction remains a subject of ongoing debate [12, 14]. On the other hand, with the advancement of high-throughput proteomic technologies, protein-based models have been demonstrated to enhance the prediction of lifespan MI events in the general population [15–17]. Nevertheless, the value of proteomics in predicting the occurrence of early-onset MI (EOMI), for which there are distinct pathobiological features and risk factors as compared with late-onset cases [18, 19], is yet to be evaluated. To this point, no prior studies have systematically compared the values of conventional risk factors, genetic information, and proteomic biomarkers, either individually or jointly, in predicting the risk of EOMI.

To fill the research gaps, using data from the UK Biobank (UKB) study with a large set of phenotypic, genomic, and plasma proteomic data, the present study aimed to: (i) identify plasma proteomic biomarkers robustly predictive of EOMI risk; and (ii) assess and compare the predictive performance of proteomic biomarkers, conventional cardiovascular risk factors, and genetic information (i.e., polygenic risk score [PRS]).

Methods

Study population

The UKB is a large population cohort that recruited more than 500,000 participants from 22 assessment centers across England, Scotland, and Wales during 2006–2010. Among these, a subset of randomly selected participants (N = 53,026) had information on 2,941 protein analytes (reflecting 2,923 unique proteins) measured across Olink panels in plasma samples collected at baseline [20]. The UKB received ethical approval from the research ethics committee (reference for UK Biobank 11/NW/0382) and all participants provided written informed consent.

Our present analysis focused on EOMI occurring at ≤ 55 years old. We excluded participants aged > 50 years old to allow for sufficient time lag between exposure and outcome, leaving 13,591 potentially eligible participants with measurement of plasma protein analytes. Among these, we excluded 295 participants with a history of CVD at baseline, 75 participants with first MI occurring after the age of 55, and 126 participants missing genetic data. The final analytic sample consisted of 13,097 eligible participants. There participants were further randomly divided into a training set (N = 9,168) and a validation set (N = 3,929) in a 7:3 ratio (Figure S1).

Assessment of conventional risk factors

Besides age, sex, and race/ethnicity, 11 traditional risk factors were considered in the current study according to the American Heart Association PREVENT (Predicting Risk of CVD Events) risk score [21], including smoking, physical activity, body mass index (BMI), systolic blood pressure (SBP), total and high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), creatinine-based estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) [22], and antihypertensive, lipid-lowering, and antidiabetic medications. Race/ethnicity was self-reported and coded as White, non-White, and unknown. Smoking status was categorized as never, former, or current, with further divisions of current smoking based on pack-years (< 10, 10 to < 50, ≥ 50). Physical activity was self-reported using the short form of the International Physical Activity Questionnaire and was coded as total metabolic equivalent-h/week to capture both intensity and duration. Information on history of medication use was collected through a touchscreen-based interview led by trained nurses. Further details on the assessment of anthropometric/blood biomarkers have been reported elsewhere [23].

Polygenic risk score

The PRS for coronary artery disease (CAD) was obtained from the UKB and constructed through a Bayesian-based meta-analysis (LDpred2) tailored to the trait, which strategically integrated genetic data from diverse ancestral populations to enhance cross-population generalizability [24]. This approach incorporated genome-wide variants and utilized a continuous shrinkage prior to account for linkage disequilibrium, avoiding the need for traditional pruning and clumping. The PRS for each participant was calculated as the sum of variant-specific contributions, with each allele dosage scaled by its posterior effect size. Furthermore, the score underwent principal component-based ancestry centering and standardization within ancestry groups to minimize ancestral bias and control for population stratification. In this study, we used the standard PRS, which was derived entirely from external genome-wide association study data [25].

Plasma proteomics

The Olink Explore 3,072 platform was used to quantify a total of 2,941 protein analytes (reflecting 2,923 unique proteins), which comprise 8 panels (Cardiometabolic, Cardiometabolic [II], Inflammation, Inflammation [II], Neurology, Neurology [II], Oncology, and Oncology [II]). The details on the sample collection, plasma sample preparation, plasma profiling using the Olink technology, and quality control have been described elsewhere [20]. There were 830 proteins with 25% or more of their data below the lowest level of detection or with more than 50% of data failing quality control, which were excluded from the present analysis. For the remaining 2,093 proteins included, missing values were imputed by half of the lowest values detected. Data were then standardized using rank-based inverse normal transformation followed by z-score normalization to ensure comparability and standardization across the proteins. Panel distribution for these 2,093 proteins is shown in Figure S2.

Ascertainment of EOMI

EOMI was defined as the first occurrence of MI at ≤ 55 years old [22]. The data provided by UKB were used to ascertain MI cases via algorithms, which were generated based on electronic health records including hospital admissions and death registry, using International Classification of Diseases (ICD)−9 and ICD−10 codes. The specific code list can be found on the UKB website (https://biobank.ndph.ox.ac.uk/showcase/refer.cgi?id=460).

Statistical analyses

Population characteristics were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables or number (%) for categorical variables stratified by training and validation samples. Differences in participant characteristics between groups were compared using t-test or chi-squared tests where appropriate. Additionally, standardized mean differences (SMD) were calculated using R package tableone to quantify covariate balance and standardize effect magnitude interpretation. To visualize the baseline proteomic separation between groups, we performed orthogonal projections to latent structures - discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) on the 2093 proteins in a matched sample (1:2 case-control, matched on age and sex).

The entire development process of protein-based models for EOMI prediction was confined to the training set. Cox proportional hazard regression models were conducted to examine the associations between individual proteins and incident EOMI, adjusting for age, sex, and race/ethnicity. To facilitate comparison, hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the associations between individual proteins and EOMI were reported as per 1-SD increment in the relative level of each protein. Bonferroni corrections were applied to assess significant associations (P < 0.05), taking into account the number of proteins tested (K = 2,093). The results were shown in volcano plot to illustrate both the strength (HRs) and significance (-log10 P) of the associations. Enrichment analysis was performed on the significant proteins derived from Cox proportional hazard regressions. Gene ontology (GO) analyses, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and Reactome enrichment analysis were performed using the R package clusterProfiler. Only terms with false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and enriched proteins ≥ 3 were considered significant. To understand the complexity of protein-protein interaction (PPI) networks and identify key proteins, the PPI network analysis was performed using the STRING database (https://string-db.org/).

To select important proteins, we used the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) Cox regression models with no covariates forced in the model (i.e., LASSO regression was restricted to significant proteins in preceding analysis). Five-fold cross-validation was used in the whole training set to select the subset of proteins (and obtain their coefficients) to maximize the C-index for prediction. C-index plot and coefficients distribution curves were generated to visualize the LASSO Cox regression results. Thus far, two protein-based models were constructed using the selected proteins, including a protein risk score and a protein panel. The protein risk score was generated by computing the weighted sum of the selected proteins (coefficients from LASSO Cox model as weights), while the protein panel referred to direct modeling the selected proteins without weighting.

We then verified the robustness of the selected protein features and their predictive performance in the validation set. By incorporating each important protein individually into the model (single protein model), we assessed its discrimination ability using the C-index. To assess the combined power of the proteins, we also established stepwise protein panel model by sequentially adding one important protein to the preceding model based on the descending rank of absolute LASSO coefficient values. Additionally, we assessed the C-index for the basic model (age, sex, and race/ethnicity) when augmented with either individual protein models or the stepwise protein panel models.

Lastly, we evaluated the predictive performance of the conventional risk factors, PRS, and protein-based models individually, as well as their pairwise and triple combinations in the validation set. The protein risk score for each participant in the validation set was generated by computing the weighted sum of the LASSO-selected proteins using the coefficients (weights) derived from and fixed in the training set. The prediction performance of the model was evaluated by discrimination and reclassification. The discrimination ability of the model was assessed by C-index. Bootstrapping with 500 replicates was used to estimate the C-index increase (Δ C-index) (95% CIs) using R package CsChange to determine the differences between models. The reclassification ability of models was evaluated by the 10-year net reclassification improvement (NRI) and the 10-year integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) using the R package survIDINRI. Given the potential sex differences, we further performed two sensitivity analyses to validate the predictive performance of the protein-based models. First, we incorporated menopausal status for women into the conventional risk factor model. Second, we redefined EOMI by using sex-specific age thresholds (< 55 years for men, < 65 for women), thereby expanding our case group to test whether the utility of plasma proteomics for EOMI risk prediction is sensitive to a specific age threshold.

Results

Participant characteristics

Mean age for the 13,097 participants was 45.4 (SD = 3.0) years and 7,195 (54.9%) were female. Participant characteristics were comparable between the training set and the validation set (all P-values > 0.05; SMD < 0.05) (Table 1). During a median follow-up of 10.0 years, 102 incident cases of EOMI were recorded. Among the 102 incident cases, 26 (25.5%) occurred in women, with the age at event ranged from 41 to 55 years. As expected, participants who developed EOMI, as compared with those without EOMI, were more likely to have traditional cardiovascular risk factors at baseline (Table S1). Specifically, they were more likely to be current smokers, and had higher BMI, SBP, total cholesterol, and HbA1c levels, along with lower levels of HDL-C. OPLS-DA analysis, validated by permutation testing (P = 0.05), demonstrated a distinct baseline plasma protein profile in EOMI cases compared to age/sex-matched controls (Figure S3). This suggested that individuals who later experienced an EOMI event already had a significantly altered plasma proteomic profile at baseline, approximately 10 years prior to the clinical occurrence of the event.

Development of protein-based models

In the training set, after the adjustment for age, sex, and race/ethnicity and Bonferroni corrections, 83 out of the 2,093 proteins were significantly and positively associated with incident EOMI, and there were no significant inverse associations (Fig. 1A and B). Panel distribution for these 83 significant proteins is shown in Figure S2. The top 5 proteins which had the most significant associations with incident EOMI were: WAP four-disulfide core domain protein 2 (HR = 2.27), growth/differentiation factor 15 (GDF15) (HR = 2.07), C-X-C motif chemokine 17 (CXCL17) (HR = 1.99), tumor necrosis factor receptor superfamily member (TNFRSF) 10B (HR = 2.23), and serine/threonine-protein kinase receptor R3 (HR = 2.27).

Within the PPI network (Fig. 1C), several interactions are strongly supported by multiple evidence types (e.g., homology, co-expression, experimental validation), including hepatitis A virus cellular receptor 2 and galectin-9 (combined score = 0.999), or GDF15 and TGF-beta receptor type-2 (combined score = 0.960). Meanwhile, T-cell surface glycoprotein CD4 (CD4), TNFRSF1A, and interleukin-18 (IL18), emerged as the top 3 proteins exhibiting elevated values of degree centrality. Enrichment analyses implicated several biological pathways related to these 83 significant proteins (Fig. 1D), such as immune activation (e.g., leukocyte regulation, cytokine signaling), extracellular matrix organization, and infectious disease responses (e.g., viral interactions, tuberculosis).

In subsequent LASSO Cox regression analysis, 22 important proteins were identified and their coefficients were obtained for incident EOMI prediction (Figure S4 A and B). The proteins with the top 5 absolute coefficients were cysteine-rich protein 2, AMBP, beta-defensin 4 A, CXCL17, and PC4 and SFRS1-interacting protein (PSIP1) (Table S1). On the basis of these 22 proteins, two protein-based models (i.e., the protein risk score and the protein panel) were constructed as described in the "Methods" section. Correlation between the genetic (PRS) and protein risk score was very modest (Pearson r = 0.039), suggesting that they reflect non-overlapping aspects of disease etiology and provide complementary value for risk prediction.

Validation of candidate proteins for EOMI prediction

For the 22 LASSO-selected proteins, the majority remained associated with incident EOMI in the validation set, except DEFB4A/DEFB4B and PSIP1 (Table S2). After adjusting for age, sex, and race/ethnicity, the protein risk score was associated with incident EOMI in a linear dose-response manner (P-nonlinearity = 0.84), with an HR of 2.57 (95% CI: 1.81, 3.63) for each SD increment of the score (Figure S5).

We defined a basic model incorporating demographic variables (i.e., age, sex, and race/ethnicity) with limited discrimination ability in the validation set (C-index = 0.660; 95% CI: 0.546, 0.773). Among the 22 selected proteins, 11 proteins individually had a C-index ≥ 0.660 (Table S3 and Fig. 2). CUB domain-containing protein 1 (CDCP1) (C-index = 0.789) exhibited the best discrimination ability, followed by GDF15 (C-index = 0.767), proline-rich acidic protein 1 (PRAP1) (C-index = 0.725), IL18 (C-index = 0.716), and mesothelin (C-index = 0.707).

In the sequential addition of proteins to the preceding model, the C-index of the protein panel gradually increased, reaching 0.849 after all 22 proteins were included (Table S3 and Fig. 2). However, the addition of the final 3 proteins yielded very limited gain in the C-index of the model, showing almost no improvement (ΔC-index = 0.001). The C-index values for the individual protein models, stepwise protein panel models, and their combinations with the basic model are shown in Table S3.

Discrimination and reclassification ability of predictive models

When compared to the basic model, the inclusion of protein panel demonstrated the most substantial improvement in discrimination ability for EOMI risk (ΔC-index = 0.189; 95% CI: 0.065, 0.276), followed by other conventional risk factors (ΔC-index = 0.158; 95% CI: 0.039, 0.239) and the protein risk score (ΔC-index = 0.125; 95% CI: 0.040, 0.213). Notably, all three models achieved statistically significant enhancements in 10-year risk reclassification (i.e., IDI and continuous NRI) (Table 2).

The addition of protein panel to conventional risk factors also significantly improved the predictive performance for incident EOMI (Table 2), further supporting the superiority of the protein panel. Specially, the addition of the protein panel to the conventional risk factor model yielded a 10-year continuous NRI of 0.457 (95% CI: 0.234, 0.571), indicating that 45.7% of individuals were correctly reclassified regarding their 10-year risk of EOMI. Similarly, the IDI of 0.052 (95% CI: 0.035, 0.327) reflects a meaningful improvement in the separation of risk distributions between cases and non-cases. However, adding the PRS to the basic model or to the model with conventional risk factors yielded no improvement either in discrimination or in reclassification ability (Table 2). Among the combinations of different models, the combination of conventional risk factors and protein panel demonstrated the highest predictive capacity for EOMI risk with a C-index of 0.875 (95% CI: 0.814, 0.935).

In the sensitivity analyses, incorporating female menopausal status into the conventional risk factor model led to a very modest improvement in the C-index for conventional risk factor model (0.817 to 0.823), and the predictive superiority of the protein panel remained robust (Table S4). Using a sex-specific thresholds to redefine EOMI definition (< 55 years for men, < 65 for women), the number of cases was expanded to be 167, whereas the results remained consistent with our original findings (Table S5).

Discussion

In this study, we developed and validated two protein-based models using 22 proteins for EOMI risk prediction. Protein-based models substantially improved risk prediction when combined with basic model, whereas the addition of PRS resulted in little improvement in the prediction of EOMI. The combination of conventional risk factors and protein panel demonstrated the highest predictive capacity for EOMI risk. Moreover, we demonstrated that protein panel model outperformed conventional risk factors and PRS for the prediction of EOMI in adults aged 50 years or younger.

Cases of MI in young adults exhibit distinct risk factor profiles compared to those in older adults [9–11]. While traditional cardiovascular risk factors such as hypertension, cigarette smoking, and obesity have been shown to be more prevalent in early-onset cases [8], some nontraditional risk factors, such as substance use and high socioeconomic vulnerability, have been proposed as important contributors to early-onset cases [9, 10]. Thus, traditional methods of assessing risk factors provide a basic framework for cardiovascular risk assessment but may not be sufficiently accurate to predict early-onset cases. Additionally, the comprehensive assessment of traditional factors typically involves multimodal data collection approaches (e.g., verbal interviews, laboratory analyses, and medical record reviews) and necessitates various trained healthcare personnel. The process may also present standardization challenges due to the inherent subjectivity, representing a relatively low-efficiency approach for EOMI risk stratification.

PRS has emerged as a promising tool for assessing lifetime CVD risk [14]. By including 4 PRSs generated from 194, 46 K, 1.5 M, and 6 M SNPs along with conventional risk factors, Isgut et al. reported that highly elevated PRSs were better predictors for MI risk early in life than later [12]. However, in the current study, the addition of PRS to the basic model yielded only a 3% improvement in C-index. The suboptimal performance of PRS in the current study may be due to our use of the universal PRS for total CAD, while EOMI may have a stronger genetic predisposition than late-onset MI. On the basis of the whole-genome sequencing of 1,239 Koreans with 596 EOMI cases, a genome-wide analyses study identified distinct genomic loci associated with EOMI, which is absent in the previous PRS for CAD/MI [26]. Thus, future studies utilizing age-stratified PRS or models incorporating gene-by-age interaction terms may yield better performance for predicting early-onset cases. Evidence is also available that PRS should be combined with traditional risk factors for optimal risk discrimination [12, 14], as relying solely on genetic information may not provide sufficient accuracy. However, in the current study, the C-index was similar before and after adding the PRS to the conventional risk model (0.817 vs. 0.814).

To date, several prospective studies have examined the relationship between broad proteomic profiling and the risk of MI, but they did not consider the timing of MI events (i.e., early-onset vs. late-onset) [15, 16]. Given that EOMI is not merely a younger version of late-onset MI but a distinct entity [18, 19], it is necessary to study EOMI as a separate endpoint. Further, atherosclerosis, the underlying pathology of most MI events, is recognized to begin in early adolescence, with subclinical vascular changes detectable in young individuals long before clinical symptoms [27]. Previous evidence also revealed that a substantial group of proteins change systematically with aging [28]. In the current study, we excluded individuals older than 50 years at baseline, which is helpful for the identification of proteins that are specifically associated with the initiation and progression of atherosclerosis in its earlier stages, rather than those reflecting the cumulative burden of aging and chronic disease.

Previous studies on proteomics and lifespan MI often included a larger number of cases, providing greater statistical power to identify a broader range of proteins, potentially including those specific to EOMI. Nevertheless, this does not preclude the comparison between our and the previous findings, since the primary focus of our study was the utility of proteomics for EOMI prediction. Indeed, there are some overlaps between the 22 important proteins identified in the present study and those key proteins highlighted in previous studies using the Olink platforms [15, 29], including GDF15, MMP12, and CDCP1. Besides these proteins, our findings highlighted the important role of PRAP1 in the prediction of EOMI in adults aged 50 years or younger. The sole addition of PRAP1 to the basic model improved the C-index from 0.660 to 0.739. As a lipid-binding protein involved in lipid absorption, PRAP1 facilitates triglyceride transport through interactions with microsomal triglyceride transfer protein, influencing lipid metabolism [30]. Although evidence directly linking PRAP1 to MI is still limited, a previous in vitro study also suggested its role in maintaining vascular integrity [31].

To assess the combined power of the proteins, we developed two protein-based predictive models using the 22 LASSO-selected proteins. Interestingly, the model incorporating the unweighted protein panel demonstrated superior performance compared to the LASSO-weighted protein risk score. This may indicate that the penalization applied by LASSO, while necessary for feature selection, slightly underestimated the collective strength of the protein-outcome associations. A relatively simple model combining basic covariates (i.e., age, sex, and race/ethnicity) with the protein panel demonstrated predictive superiority, slightly surpassing the model based on conventional risk factors while maintaining a remarkable advantage over the model that included basic covariates and the PRS. The findings can be interpreted in light of the distinct nature of these biomarkers. The PRS represents a static, lifelong genetic predisposition. Conventional risk factors are clinical phenotypes that are routinely available. In contrast, the plasma proteome offers a dynamic snapshot of ongoing biological processes, integrating genetic predisposition, environmental exposures, and current subclinical pathophysiological changes leading to myocardial ischemia [32]. Some traditional risk factors like smoking may exert their deleterious effects partly by upregulating pro-inflammatory proteins (e.g., CCL11, CXCL16) and proteolytic enzymes (e.g., MMP-12), which are key drivers of plaque vulnerability and rupture [33–35], the most common etiology for acute MI in young adults [18]. Consequently, the proteomic signature might identify individuals with a heightened biological response to these conventional insults, thereby explaining the heterogeneity in risk among individuals with similar traditional risk factor profiles. Notably, the protein panel can provide sufficient accuracy in predicting EOMI (C-index ≈ 0.85) without requiring supplementary of conventional risk factors or PRS. Hence, protein-based models reduce reliance on complex clinical data collection, making them potentially practical for resource-limited settings.

While the absolute improvement in C-index appears modest (ΔC-index ≈ 0.05) when adding the protein panel to conventional risk factors, incremental improvements in discrimination can be clinically meaningful for risk stratification, particularly for a condition like EOMI where early intervention is critical. Meanwhile, given its excellent reclassification ability, the integration of proteomic panels into clinical practice could be more appropriate for comprehensive risk assessment when resources permit, particularly in high-risk population. Nevertheless, the practical aspects of implementing a protein-based predictive model (e.g., cost, turnaround time, and scalability) are essential considerations for real-world adoption. Our protein-based models have already represented a significant reduction from the initial 2,093 proteins to a focused panel of 22 proteins, substantially lowering the analytical burden and cost compared to the full proteomic profiling. Given that targeted assays (e.g., multiplex immunoassays) are increasingly available and cost-effective for measuring a limited number of proteins [36], future research should explore whether a further refined panel could maintain predictive performance with fewer proteins, which would advance genuine clinical translation.

Strengths of our study include the long follow-up period and the high-throughput proteomic analysis of a large, community-based sample. The exclusion of individuals over 50 years can enhance the specificity of protein identification. Additionally, we employed LASSO to select a smaller subset of proteins for predicting EOMI, and performed internal validation to minimize the risk of overfitting. Despite its merits, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although internal validation demonstrated robust performance, the relatively low event-per-variable ratio remains a limitation, and further validation in larger external cohorts is warranted. Second, plasma proteins from the Olink platform were measured in relative (as opposed to absolute) concentrations, limiting the ability to identify a clinically relevant protein levels from these data. Finally, the participants in the current study were predominantly of White European ancestry. This significantly limits the generalizability of our findings, as the prevalence of risk factors, genetic architecture, and proteomic profiles can vary substantially across different ancestral groups. It is therefore crucial that our findings are validated in independent, multi-ethnic cohorts before any potential clinical application.

Conclusions

Overall, the present study identified a suite of proteins robustly associated with the risk of EOMI in adults aged 50 years or younger. Some of the proteins (e.g., PRAP1) have not been previously linked to MI and may be related to the biological pathways specific for the development EOMI. More importantly, a simple protein panel model outperformed conventional CVD risk factors and greatly exceeded PRS for EOMI risk prediction. While challenges remain in standardization and cost, plasma proteomics holds the potential to revolutionize the early prediction of EOMI by enhancing risk stratification and personalized prevention.
