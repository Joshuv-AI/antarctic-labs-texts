Emotion regulation in Premenstrual Dysphoric Disorder and Premenstrual Syndrome: a systematic review
Authors: Ellen Lambert, Myra Hunter, Hannah Cocker, Caroline Gurvich, Trudie Chalder
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposePremenstrual Dysphoric Disorder (PMDD) and severe cases of Premenstrual Syndrome (PMS) are characterised by fluctuations in mood and behaviour across the menstrual cycle and cause severe and disabling symptoms for some females. Difficulties with emotion regulation (ER) are considered key experiences of people with PMDD and severe PMS. The present review aims to summarise relevant research on ER in PMDD and PMS and integrate results in the extended process model (EPM) of ER. We aimed to answer whether group differences in ER exist between people with and without PMDD and PMS, and whether variations in ER exist across menstrual phases.MethodsA systematic literature search was conducted following PRISMA guidelines in MEDLINE, PsycInfo, EMBASE, PubMed, CINAHL and CENTRAL until 11th April 2024. Articles were included that administered an ER questionnaire or related experimental task, examined people with PMDD or PMS confirmed via prospective ratings of symptoms over 1 + months, and which compared the target sample with healthy controls or within the same individual at least twice over 2 cycle phases. Study quality was assessed using the Newcastle Ottawa Scale and a Menstrual Quality Assessment tool developed for the review.ResultsAfter full text screening and systematic quality assessment, 22 studies (n = 1529 participants) were included. Findings suggest possible difficulties in the identification and selection stages of ER in individuals with PMDD and PMS, such as heightened emotional reactivity and challenges with emotional clarity. There was also some evidence indicating a reliance on less adaptive ER strategies, including increased rumination, particularly in the late-luteal phase. However, the evidence regarding difficulties in ER implementation remains limited and inconsistent, with few experimental studies available.ConclusionsWhile there is some indication that individuals with PMDD and PMS may experience difficulties in emotion identification and strategy selection, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Further research is necessary to clarify these processes and their potential variability across the menstrual cycle. Future studies should prioritise longitudinal and experimental designs to gain a deeper understanding of ER in PMDD and PMS and inform the development of phase-sensitive therapeutic strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03587-y.

Introduction

The majority of reproductive-age females experience the menstrual cycle and most consider this a natural process that is manageable. However, a small percentage of hormone-sensitive individuals suffer from Premenstrual Dysphoric Disorder (PMDD) [1] and severe Premenstrual Syndrome (PMS) [2]. PMDD and severe PMS are characterised by an abnormal cyclical pattern of emotional and somatic symptoms that occur during the luteal phase of the menstrual cycle (i.e., two weeks before menses) and remit at the onset of or during menses.

PMDD was added to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (Fifth Edition; DSM-5) as a recognised mood disorder in 2013 to reflect the most severe end of the PMS continuum. To meet DSM-5 criteria for PMDD, individuals must experience five or more of eleven listed symptoms during the luteal phase, with at least one being an emotional experience such as depressed mood, mood swings, irritability, or anger, among others [3]. Importantly, as PMDD is a time-oriented diagnosis not a symptom-oriented diagnosis, symptoms must remit with the onset of the follicular phase. Furthermore, symptoms must cause clinically significant distress or interfere with occupational or social activities or relationships. Diagnoses require the cyclical symptom pattern to be demonstrated via prospective daily self-report ratings for 2 or more months.

PMDD and severe PMS are linked to considerable psychological distress and impairment [4, 5]. A global survey of people with PMDD found extremely high rates of lifetime suicidal ideation (72%), suicide attempts (34%), and self-harming behaviours (51%) [6]. A recent meta-analysis examining suicidal ideation and behaviours indicates that those with PMDD face a heightened risk of both suicidal thoughts and attempts, whereas individuals with PMS appear to be at increased risk for suicidal ideation but not for attempts [7]. Additionally, a Swedish population-based matched cohort study reported that individuals with clinically diagnosed PMDD were nearly twice as likely to due by suicide relative to matched controls without PMDD [8]. It is therefore thought that the presence of PMDD and severe PMS may in part contribute to the increased risk of affective disorders and suicidal behaviours in females worldwide [9–11].

Much of the research on PMDD has concentrated on uncovering the underlying biological mechanisms. Current evidence suggests that individuals with PMDD exhibit an atypical neurobiological response to the normal cyclical changes in ovarian hormones and their metabolites throughout the menstrual cycle, leading to abnormal emotional responses in those affected [12–14]. This is supported by impressive experimental data showing that hormone levels are comparable in individuals with and without PMDD, and that the rise in estradiol and progesterone levels during the luteal phase, as opposed to steady-state hormonal levels, is associated with the onset of PMDD symptoms [15].

An important yet underexplored area of research concerns the influence of hormone sensitivity on cognitive, emotional, and social processes that are relevant to both suicidality and PMDD, particularly emotion regulation (ER) [16]. Extensive research over the past several decades has shown that ER represents an important transdiagnostic process in psychopathology, with impairments in ER observed across a wide range of common mental health conditions [17, 18] and several influential models have been developed to explain its role in psychological disorders [19]. Despite this, PMDD has largely been overlooked in major reviews of ER, likely due to the relatively early stage of research into premenstrual disorders [17]. Nonetheless, PMDD is characterised by symptoms such as emotional overwhelm and difficulty coping with everyday stressors [20]. Emerging evidence suggests that these secondary psychological symptoms, such as feeling overwhelmed, unable to cope, or out of control, may be more predictive of premenstrual functional impairment than core emotional symptoms like low mood or mood instability, or physical symptoms [21].

In recent years, two significant and comprehensive reviews have advanced our understanding of PMDD by examining premenstrual changes in cognition and behaviour across the menstrual cycle [22], as well as the neuroimaging correlates associated with emotional distress and dysregulation in the disorder [23]. Together, these reviews have shed light on both the psychological mechanisms underlying PMDD and the disorder-specific neural patterns related to emotion processing. Key findings suggest that individuals with PMDD exhibit a premenstrual attentional bias toward negative cognitive content, including increased rumination, heightened perceived stress, lower self-acceptance, and reduced feelings of social connection [22]. Additionally, neuroimaging evidence indicates altered corticolimbic responses to emotional stimuli, characterised by heightened amygdala reactivity and reduced activation in fronto-cortical regions compared to individuals without PMDD [23].

Despite its potential to enhance our understanding of the psychological processes underlying both suicidality and PMDD, no effort has yet been made to synthesise the existing cross-sectional and longitudinal data on ER processes in PMDD and severe PMS. A systematic review is urgently needed to organise the available evidence within the framework of a well-established ER model. The extended process model (EPM) of ER [19] offers a comprehensive understanding of the ER process, providing valuable insights into the specific deficits in ER that may characterise individuals with PMDD and severe PMS. Gaining a deeper understanding of these ER processes is essential for informing and refining therapeutic interventions tailored to the unique needs of this population. The present review seeks to fill this gap in the literature and contribute to the development of a psychological model for PMDD and severe PMS.

Emotion regulation processes

ER is a complex, multi-stage process that involves various regulatory stages that involves various regulatory stages before and after the implementation of regulatory strategies [19]. The original process model of ER [24], arguably the most influential theoretical ER framework, identifies five distinct families of regulatory strategies, each occurring at different stages of the emotional process. The first four families (situation selection, situation modification, attentional deployment, cognitive change), operate before the emotion is fully formed, while response modulation occurs afterward to adjust the emotional response [24]. These families are embedded within four stages of the ER cycle, as described in the EPM [19]. The cycle begins with identification, where the need or opportunity for regulation is recognised. The next stage, selection, involves choosing a regulatory strategy from available options. Once selected, the strategy is implemented through specific tactics in the implementation stage. Finally, the entire ER process is monitored to assess whether the desired emotional outcome has been achieved.

During the identification stage, emotional states are first perceived, a process influenced by an individual’s emotional reactivity. Effective perception requires skills such as emotional awareness and clarity; failures can occur at this stage if emotions are misinterpreted or misrepresented. The subsequent evaluation process assesses whether the emotion requires regulation and whether ER would be beneficial. Beliefs about the efficacy of ER strategies play a key role here, as low self-efficacy can lead to failure in initiating regulation when it is needed. Once the decision to regulate is made, the selection stage begins, during which available ER strategies are considered and evaluated based on factors such as available resources, emotional intensity, and the costs and benefits of each option. Errors can arise if adaptive strategies are underutilised or maladaptive strategies are overvalued, potentially leading to the habitual use of ineffective regulation methods. Finally, during the implementation stage, the chosen strategy is applied, which directly influences the emotional experience if successful. If the strategy fails, ER is ineffective [19].

The current study

Given that the EPM provides a framework for understanding both stable individual differences in ER and dynamic, within-person fluctuations across time and contexts [19], the present review was guided by the following research questions:Do individuals with PMDD or PMS exhibit deficits in ER processes (specifically identification, selection, and implementation) compared to individuals without PMDD or PMS?Do individuals with PMDD or PMS show phase-specific ER impairments (e.g., during the perimenstrual, luteal or late-luteal phase) relative to other phases of the menstrual cycle (e.g. during the follicular or mid-follicular phase)?

To address these questions, we systematically reviewed the existing literature on ER in PMDD and PMS, categorising relevant self-report measures and experimental paradigms according to the three core stages of the EPM.

Methods

Search strategy and selection criteria

Up to April 2024, a systematic review of electronic databases was carried out for relevant papers, using MEDLINE, PsycInfo, EMBASE, PubMed, CINAHL, and CENTRAL using title, abstract, and MeSH terms. Search terms were structured around PMDs (concept 1) and ER (concept 2). Search terms are listed in Supplementary Material 1.

Eligibility was defined using the Population, Interventions, Comparators and Outcomes framework (PICOS) [25]. Inclusion criteria were (a) original peer reviewed articles involving assessment using an ER scale or task, (b) comparison of a PMS or PMDD sample with a non-PMS or non-PMDD sample or within the same sample across distinct cycle phases, (c) samples of regularly menstruating adults not using contraceptives or psychiatric medication, (d) PMDD or PMS status confirmed via prospective ratings of symptoms over at least 1 month, (e) conducted in clinical or non-clinical settings. Our definition of ER followed Gross’ EPM [19] including constructs such as emotional reactivity, emotional awareness and clarity, beliefs about emotions and ER (identification), the use of specific ER strategies (selection), and the effective implementation of ER. Consistent with Gross [26], we delineated ER from other related constructs such as global emotional intelligence and unconscious defense mechanisms.

Exclusion criteria were (a) neuroimaging and physiological studies without behavioural data, (b) samples including adolescents, possible perimenopause, and/or intellectual disabilities, (c) samples taking oral contraceptives or other hormone-influencing medications, (d) studies of premenstrual exacerbation of other mental health disorders, (e) PMDD or PMS status confirmed via cross-sectional screening measures. To ensure a comprehensive review, we did not exclude samples with psychiatric comorbidities or taking antidepressant medication. See Supplementary Material 2 for full PICOS table.

Full study review

This systematic review was preregistered in PROSPERO (CRD42023399348). It was conducted in alignment with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidance (Supplementary Material 3) [27]. Articles were screened by two reviewers (EL, HC) to identify those potentially meeting inclusion criteria. All were screened by EL and a subset (20%) by HC. Agreement and interrater reliability were excellent (92%; Cohen's Kappa = 0.75). Full texts of remaining articles were assessed for eligibility and reasons for exclusion coded. Data extraction

Data were extracted from 07/08/23 to 11/04/24 on study characteristics (lead author name, place of origin, publication year, study design and duration), sample characteristics (sample size, mean age, PMDD or PMS screening method), assessment material (self-report questionnaire/interview/experimental task, menstrual phase definition and method of measurement), analysis method, key ER outcomes and other psychological outcomes.

Quality assessment

Quality was assessed by one reviewer following the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale criteria (NOS) [28] for case–control and prospective studies. Scores of 7–9 were high quality, scores of 4–6 were high risk of bias and scores of 0–3 were very high risk of bias [29]. Studies were also evaluated using a Menstrual Risk of Bias checklist developed for this review and based on published recommendations for menstrual science “How to study the menstrual cycle” [13]. No cut off scores for quality were used. The tool was used to comment on weaknesses and potential for bias. For NOS and MQA templates, see Supplementary Material 4.

Data synthesis

Studies meeting the inclusion criteria were reviewed for their use of ER measures, including questionnaire subscales and experimental tasks. According to the EPM, the ER cycle is initiated when an individual detects a discrepancy between their current or anticipated emotional state and a desired emotional state [30]. Measures assessing individuals’ emotional reactivity, via valence and arousal ratings, were therefore categorised under the identification stage. This stage also included assessments of emotional awareness (the ability to attend to emotions) and emotional clarity (the perceived ability to identify and understand one's emotions).

In line with Gross and colleagues [19, 31], beliefs about emotions and ER, especially maladaptive beliefs, play a critical role in initiating regulation. As such, measures that assess beliefs about one’s regulatory capacity, such as the “Limited Access to Strategies” subscale of the Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale (DERS) [32], were also included in this stage. This subscale reflects perceived helplessness in managing emotions (e.g., “I believe there is nothing I can do to make myself feel better”). Since the identification stage involves the decision of whether or not to engage in regulation, such beliefs may inhibit ER before it begins.

Following identification, the ER process moves to the selection stage, where an appropriate strategy is chosen from available options, and then to implementation, where the selected strategy is executed through specific tactics [30]. Drawing on the distinction made by McRae and Gross [31] between ER frequency (typically assessed via self-report) and effectiveness (often examined through experimental tasks), we categorised self-report data under the selection stage, and experimental measures of ER success under the implementation stage.

Self-report measures classified under the selection stage assessed the use of strategies associated with the five families of ER processes. These included: Attentional deployment (e.g., mindfulness, rumination), Cognitive change (e.g., reappraisal, acceptance), Situation selection (e.g., choosing to avoid emotionally challenging environments or individuals), Situation modification (e.g., problem-solving or seeking social support), and Response modulation strategies (e.g., suppressing emotional expression).

Although most self-report measures of strategy use were assigned to the selection stage, exceptions were made for instruments assessing general regulatory success or failure. For example, items from the ASQ (e.g., “I am able to let go of my feelings”) and subscales from the DERS (e.g., Goal-Directed Behavior, Impulse Control) and the WOCS (e.g., Self-Controlling Action) that reflect difficulties in executing regulation were classified under the implementation stage. These were grouped alongside experimental tasks that directly assessed ER efficacy.

Results

Our literature search identified 10,016 articles after de-duplication. All were screened at title and abstract level and 502 were assessed against inclusion and exclusion criteria. Of those, 24 were eligible for extraction and quality assessment. There was variability in PMDD and PMS screening approaches, with detailed information about methods and criteria presented in Supplementary Material 5. Meta-analysis was not possible due to considerable heterogeneity amongst studies. Therefore, a systematic review with narrative synthesis was conducted [33]. Data and materials are available upon reasonable request from ellen.r.lambert@kcl.ac.uk.

Risk of bias

Risk of bias scores on the NOS ranged from 4 to 8, of a total possible score of 9. Studies with an unacceptable quality rating (a score significantly below risk of bias thresholds (3 or below) on the NOS) [34] or other problems related to screening (only part of the sample completed prospective screening for PMDs) [35] were excluded (N = 2). Most studies (N = 14 studies; 63.6%) were classified ‘high risk of bias’ according to NOS criteria. N = 8 studies (36.4%) were classified ‘high quality’ according to NOS criteria. A summary of quality assessment scores (NOS; MQA) is presented in Supplementary Material 6.

Study characteristics

N = 22 studies were eligible for data extraction after full-text screening and quality assessment screening (for a list of studies see Supplementary Material 7), gathering N = 1529 participants (PMDD/PMS: N = 806 & Controls: N = 723). Samples overlapped in four instances (see Tables 2 and 3), but all articles were included as different methodological approaches were used. Studies varied in approaches to defining menstrual cycle phases, with the majority comparing outcomes between the follicular and late-luteal phase. N = 12 studies (54.5%) confirmed menstrual cycle phase via measurements of estradiol (E2), progesterone (P4) and/or luteinising hormone (LH) in blood, saliva, and/or urine. N = 10 studies (48%) used only self-reports and counting methods to ascertain menstrual phase. Menstrual phase definitions and verification methods are presented in Tables 2 and 3. Key findings are displayed in Tables 2 and 3 in detail and more concisely in Fig. 2. Cross-sectional, case–control results (Table 2) are arranged separately to prospective, within-person outcomes (Table 3). Days of the menstrual cycle are all presented in a format whereby day 1 represents the first day or onset of the menstrual period, unless otherwise specified. N = 6 studies (28.5%) employed a cross-sectional design and did not measure ER outcomes across multiple phases of the cycle. N = 16 studies (72.7%) compared outcomes between cycle phases, with the majority measuring the follicular and late-luteal phase. Of the longitudinal studies, N = 2 report prospective data only. The remaining studies had both case–control and prospective data that was extractable, therefore the relevant data is presented in both sections (Tables 2 and 3). Emotion regulation processes

The main findings of our review are displayed within the EPM in Fig. 2. A more detailed summary of results regarding the different stages of the ER process are presented below.

Identification stage

Eleven studies addressed processes related to the identification stage of ER, focusing on emotion emotional reactivity, emotional awareness, emotional clarity, and beliefs about access to ER strategies. Of these, eight employed experimental designs. Notably, seven studies assessed these outcomes across distinct phases of the menstrual cycle, allowing for examination of phase-specific variation in identification-related processes.

Emotional reactivity

Three experimental studies [36–38] investigated emotional reactivity in individuals with PMDD compared to controls using the International Affective Picture System (IAPS), a standardised set of emotionally evocative images [39]. Across all three studies, individuals with PMDD consistently reported higher negative valence and arousal ratings in response to unpleasant images, regardless of menstrual phase. These elevated valence and arousal ratings reflect heightened emotional reactivity, the intensity of emotional responses. The effects were of small to medium magnitude, and most group differences did not reach statistical significance, likely due to limited statistical power. These findings suggest heightened emotional reactivity to negative stimuli in individuals with PMDD, independent of cycle phase. In contrast, group differences in response to positive stimuli were less consistent. One study [38] reported a large effect wherein controls rated positive images more negatively than individuals with PMDD, a finding observed across both menstrual phases.

Regarding phase-specific effects, two studies [37, 38] assessed emotional reactivity, measured by ratings of valence and arousal, during both the late-luteal and mid-follicular phases. Neither study found consistent differences in emotional reactivity in the late-luteal phase relative to mid-follicular. One study [38] reported increased reactivity to positive images in both PMDD and control groups during the late-luteal phase. Another study [37] observed increased reactivity in response to negative social images during the late-luteal phase among individuals with PMDD, but not for non-social negative images. Due to insufficient statistical reporting, effect sizes for within-subject phase differences could not be calculated.

Emotional awareness

Evidence from two studies suggests that individuals with PMDD report lower emotional awareness compared to controls, as measured by the DERS [32], specifically its subscale assessing difficulties in attending to and acknowledging emotions [40, 41]. Both studies found elevated scores in the PMDD group, indicating reduced emotional awareness. However, one study did not report subscale-level results [40], limiting the ability to draw firm conclusions regarding emotional awareness specifically. In both studies, emotional awareness was treated as a trait-like characteristic rather than a dynamic process, and no information was provided on the menstrual cycle phase during which participants completed the measures.

One exploratory study [42] examined alexithymia in individuals with PMS, categorising participants by high and low depression levels. While the study reported higher alexithymia (so reduced emotional awareness) in PMS participants with high depression scores, and lower levels in those with low depression compared to controls, the absence of a validated premenstrual symptom measure introduces a high risk of bias. Given these methodological limitations and the lack of replication, the findings should be interpreted with caution and do not allow for any firm conclusions.

Emotional clarity

Self-report and experimental evidence suggest that individuals with PMDD may experience reduced emotional clarity compared to controls. Two studies employed the emotional clarity subscale of the DERS [32], which assesses difficulties in understanding and making sense of one’s emotions [40, 41]. Both studies reported higher scores in the PMDD group, indicating lower emotional clarity. However, only one of these studies reported results at the subscale level [41], limiting the specificity of conclusions drawn from the other [40]. As with emotional awareness, both studies treated emotional clarity as a stable trait and did not assess changes across menstrual cycle phases or specify cycle timing during data collection.

Supporting these self-reported deficits, one high-quality experimental study [43] found evidence of reduced emotional clarity in PMDD participants using the Facial Discrimination Task [44]. Compared to controls, individuals with PMDD demonstrated greater misjudgments, particularly a neutral-to-sad bias, and impaired specificity in facial emotion recognition during the late-luteal phase, suggesting difficulties in differentiating between subtle emotional expressions. These impairments were not observed in controls and were less pronounced during the mid-follicular phase, suggesting a phase-specific exacerbation of emotion differentiation difficulties. However, it remains unclear whether this impairment reflects difficulties in recognising others' emotions, one's own emotions, or both.

In contrast, four studies using the Emotional Face Processing Task [45] did not consistently support emotional clarity impairments in PMDD [46–49]. These studies assessed accuracy and reaction times in identifying emotional expressions. Two studies reported reduced clarity in PMDD participants during the mid-follicular phase relative to controls, but this effect was not replicated in the late-luteal phase [46, 47]. Moreover, phase-based comparisons within the PMDD group showed lower emotional clarity in the late-luteal phase than the mid-follicular phase, although these differences did not reach statistical significance. The remaining two studies found no significant group or phase differences [48, 49], limiting the reliability of conclusions drawn from this paradigm.

Limited access to ER strategies

Evidence from self-report measures suggests that individuals with PMDD perceive themselves as having limited access to effective ER strategies. Two studies employed the “Limited Access to Strategies” subscale of the DERS [32] to assess this belief in individuals with PMDD compared to controls [40, 41]. This subscale captures the perception that, once emotionally distressed, few or no viable strategies are available to regulate emotional states. Both studies reported higher scores among participants with PMDD, indicating a lower perceived ability to effectively manage emotions. However, only one study provided subscale-specific data [41], limiting interpretability of the findings in the other study [40].

As with other identification-stage constructs, both studies conceptualised beliefs about ER strategy access as a stable trait and did not examine variations across menstrual cycle phases or report when during the cycle the data were collected. This limits conclusions about potential phase-specific patterns in these self-perceptions.

Selection stage

Eleven studies examined the selection and reported use of ER strategies. All studies employed self-report measures to assess the frequency and type of ER strategies used, comparing individuals with PMDD or PMS to control groups. Notably, none of the included studies utilised experimental methodologies to investigate ER strategy selection. Of the 11 studies, eight reported data across distinct phases of the menstrual cycle, allowing for some insight into potential phase-dependent variation in ER strategy use.

Attentional deployment

In relation to attentional deployment strategies, one high-quality study assessed mindfulness in individuals with PMDD compared to controls [50]. Findings indicated significantly lower mindfulness scores among PMDD participants, with a medium effect size. However, mindfulness was conceptualised as a trait, and data were not disaggregated by menstrual cycle phase, limiting insights into potential cycle-related fluctuations.

A greater number of studies examined rumination. Four studies, most rated as high quality, used self-report measures to compare levels of rumination in individuals with PMDD or PMS and controls [51–54]. One study employed the well-validated Ruminative Response Scale and treated rumination as a trait-level variable, without menstrual cycle phase-specific data collection or analysis [52]. The remaining three studies conceptualised rumination as a dynamic, context-sensitive process and used Ecological Momentary Assessment (EMA) to capture daily fluctuations [51, 53, 54]. These studies consistently reported elevated rumination in individuals with PMDD and PMS during the late-luteal phase compared to the follicular phase, a pattern not observed in control participants. Notably, the EMA studies relied on a single-item measure focused on the uncontrollability facet of rumination, specifically, the inability to manage or disengage from repetitive negative thinking.

Taken together, these findings suggest that individuals with PMDD and severe PMS may engage more persistently with negative thoughts and may demonstrate reduced use of attention-shifting or disengagement as regulatory strategies. The consistent late-luteal increase in rumination highlights a potential phase-specific vulnerability in attentional deployment among those with PMDD and severe PMS.

Cognitive change

Cognitive change strategies, particularly reappraisal and acceptance, have been moderately investigated in individuals with PMDD and PMS. Five studies of varying methodological quality examined the use of reappraisal in PMDD and PMS populations compared to controls [41, 50, 55–57]. Some evidence from both high-quality [50] and lower-quality studies [56, 57] indicated reduced use of reappraisal among individuals with PMDD and PMS. However, other studies reported null or inconsistent findings [41, 55], suggesting variability in results. Two studies examined changes in reappraisal across menstrual cycle phases, but the evidence was inconclusive, largely due to methodological limitations such as a lack of clearly defined cycle phase demarcation (i.e., no specific days provided for phase classification) [56, 57].

Acceptance-based strategies have been assessed across six studies of mixed quality [40, 41, 51, 52, 54, 55]. Three of these reported lower acceptance of emotional experiences [40, 41] or lower self-acceptance [54] in individuals with PMDD compared to controls. In contrast, two studies examining acceptance coping in response to stress found no significant differences between groups [52, 55]. Only one study explored menstrual cycle phase differences in acceptance and reported reduced self-acceptance during the late-luteal phase compared to the follicular phase in individuals with PMDD [54].

Collectively, these findings suggest a tendency for individuals with PMDD and severe PMS to engage less frequently in cognitive change strategies such as reappraisal and acceptance relative to healthy controls. However, the inconsistency of findings and the limited number of well-controlled, phase-sensitive studies highlight the need for further research using precise methodological designs and robust cycle phase assessments.

Response modulation

Response modulation strategies, including expressive suppression or emotional inhibition, were explored in three studies examining individuals with PMDD and controls [41, 57, 58]. Overall, the studies did not reveal clear differences between groups, suggesting that individuals with PMDD do not consistently use suppression more or less frequently than controls. However, when examining menstrual cycle phases, two studies found that suppression was self-reported as being more prominent during the early luteal and follicular phases, compared to the late luteal and premenstrual phases, in samples of PMDD participants [57, 58].

It is important to note that all three studies included in this review were identified as having a high risk of bias, which calls into question the reliability of their conclusions. These methodological limitations underscore the need for further studies with stronger designs to better understand the use of response modulation strategies in PMDD and severe PMS across menstrual phases.

Situation selection

Avoidance and distancing strategies were categorised within the situation selection stage of the EPM, as they involve managing emotions by deliberately choosing situations that minimise exposure to emotional triggers or stressors. Two studies investigated avoidance/distancing strategies as coping mechanisms in individuals with PMDD or PMS and controls [52, 55].

Both studies provide some evidence that individuals with PMDD and PMS engage in more avoidance/distancing compared to controls. Specifically, one study found that people with PMS and high depression scores reported higher levels of behavioural efforts to escape or avoid stressful feelings (e.g., eating, drinking, or substance use) and distancing efforts (e.g., emotionally detaching from a situation), relative to controls. However, this effect was significantly smaller in individuals with PMS and low depression scores, suggesting that depression, rather than PMS alone, may drive this behaviour [55].

Another study found significantly higher avoidance coping (including mental disengagement, behavioural disengagement, denial, and alcohol/drug use) in people with PMDD or PMS compared to controls [52]. It should be noted that both studies had a high risk of bias, which warrants caution in interpreting these findings. Future research with more rigorous designs is needed to confirm the role of avoidance strategies in PMDD and severe PMS.

Situation modification

Seeking social support and engaging in problem-solving are both considered situation modification strategies within the extended process model (EPM) of emotion regulation, as they aim to change aspects of a situation to reduce its emotional impact. Two studies, both assessed as having a high risk of bias, examined the use of these strategies in individuals with PMDD or PMS compared to controls in the context of coping with stressful events [52, 55]. Neither study found significant group differences in the use of social support or problem-solving.

Although these findings suggest no clear differences in the use of these strategies between groups, the high risk of bias limits the strength of this conclusion. Further research using more rigorous methodologies is needed to clarify whether individuals with PMDD or PMS differ from controls in their use of situation modification strategies to manage emotional distress.

Implementation stage

A total of six studies were identified that provided insights into ER implementation. Among these, two studies utilised experimental designs to investigate the efficacy of ER strategies, while four studies relied on self-reported questionnaires. Of the studies reviewed, three assessed outcomes across distinct menstrual cycle phases.

ER efficacy (experimental tasks)

Experimental research plays a crucial role in assessing whether individuals with PMDD and severe PMS face challenges in effectively implementing ER strategies. One high-quality study found evidence of deficits in ER implementation in people with PMDD. In this study, participants were instructed to apply a specific ER strategy: perspective taking/distancing [59, 60]. Group differences emerged on the negative ‘far’ trials of the task during the late luteal phase, suggesting that individuals with PMDD had greater difficulty distancing themselves and focusing on objective facts when viewing negative emotional scenes. However, during the follicular phase, their performance was comparable to that of the control group.

Another study, which had a high risk of bias [61], examined attentional biases toward emotional stimuli using the German Emotional Stroop Task [62]. This study did not find evidence of deficits in ER implementation in individuals with PMS. Both groups displayed high accuracy on the task, with participants in the PMS group reacting faster than controls in both the late-follicular (medium effect size) and late-luteal phases (small to medium effect size). Additionally, there was no clear evidence of ER impairment in the late-luteal phase compared to the late-follicular phase for either group [61].

ER self-evaluation (self-report measures)

The remaining studies focused on participant-reported success in implementing emotional regulation (ER) strategies. Four studies of varying quality [40, 41, 55, 58] examined self-control and emotional tolerance in individuals with PMDD or PMS compared to controls, using a range of self-report measures. Some evidence indicated ER implementation deficits in people with PMDD, particularly in areas such as impulse control [40, 41], emotion adjustment (i.e., the ability to show flexibility in changing emotional reactions), and emotional tolerance (i.e., facing emotions rather than avoiding them) [58]. However, one study reported null results regarding differences in self-control between individuals with PMS and controls [55].

Three additional studies of varying quality examined attempts at direct action in response to emotional experiences [40, 41, 55]. Higher quality studies [40, 41] found that individuals with PMDD experienced more difficulty completing tasks when experiencing negative emotions compared to controls. Additionally, one study found that participants with PMS reported higher levels of confrontational coping in response to stressful events than controls [55]. Phase differences were reported in only one study, which suggested some impairment in emotion adjustment/flexibility and emotional tolerance during the premenstrual phase of the menstrual cycle in individuals with PMDD compared to the follicular phase [58].

Discussion

Diagnostic criteria for PMDD highlight emotional disturbances as a core component [3], and studies examining global scores from questionnaires assessing ER difficulties provide support for the hypothesis that individuals with PMDD experience ER problems [41]. However, the precise nature of these ER difficulties and how they vary across the menstrual cycle in individuals with PMDD and severe PMS, as compared to healthy controls, remains unclear. Recent randomised controlled trials (RCTs) evaluating the impact of emotion-focused therapies for PMDD suggest that people with PMDD may benefit from interventions that specifically target ER skills [63]. One such evidence-based emotion-focused therapy intervention [64], which taught emotional awareness, understanding of maladaptive emotions, and strategies for transforming emotions, was found to improve participants' ability to regulate their emotions while reducing PMDD symptoms, depression, and stress [63]. This suggests that addressing ER impairments could be beneficial for individuals with PMDD and severe PMS.

To better understand when ER processes are impaired and to refine therapeutic interventions accordingly, a more nuanced analysis is needed. This review aimed to summarise existing research on ER in PMDD and PMS by categorising the findings according to the widely cited process model of ER [19]. We included 22 studies and analysed them for relevant findings. Specifically, we examined whether individuals with PMDD or PMS experience difficulties in identifying emotions, selecting appropriate ER strategies, or implementing ER processes compared to individuals without PMDD or PMS. Additionally, we sought to identify variations in ER across different menstrual cycle phases.

Our results suggest that individuals with PMDD and PMS report difficulties in the identification of emotions, selection of ER strategies, and implementation of ER processes. However, experimental studies, considered the most robust evidence, were limited in number and have not provided a definitive picture of substantial ER deficits. The majority of studies focused on the identification and selection stages of ER, with only 27% investigating the implementation stage. This imbalance limits our ability to draw strong conclusions about the effectiveness of ER in individuals with PMDs.

Moreover, while our review primarily focused on individuals with PMDD and PMS and ensured that samples were assessed for premenstrual symptoms using prospective methods, we did not exclusively include clinical samples with verified PMDD diagnoses. Consequently, some participants may have included false positives or subclinical PMDD cases. However, given the dimensional nature of premenstrual disorders, with previous research suggesting that PMDD severity exists on a continuum in the general population [12], and considering the scarcity of psychological research focused on clinical samples, we believe this approach was both justified and informative.

Problems in the Identification stage

The identification stage of ER involves processes such as emotional reactivity, emotional awareness, clarity, and understanding, all of which are essential for initiating effective ER. Without adequate emotional reactivity and accurate emotional awareness and clarity, individuals may struggle to recognise and label their emotions, making subsequent regulation more challenging.

Our review found that individuals with PMDD exhibit heightened sensitivity to, and stronger emotional reactions to, negative stimuli compared to healthy controls. According to the EPM [19], stronger emotional responses, characterised by higher negative valence and arousal, create a more challenging emotional context from the earliest stages of ER. This heightened reactivity can undermine an individual’s ability to select and apply effective regulation strategies later in the process. Although the differences observed were not statistically large, the consistent patterns across studies indicate that emotional reactivity is disrupted in people with PMDD. Furthermore, some evidence suggests that emotional reactivity may fluctuate across the menstrual cycle, which warrants further exploration. Future research should aim to employ larger sample sizes and track emotional reactivity over multiple menstrual cycles to better understand its dynamic nature in individuals with and without PMDD. Additionally, because maladaptive ER strategies are often selected in situations characterised by high negative valence and high arousal [65–67], it would be beneficial for future studies to investigate the relationship between these emotional qualities and the selection of ER strategies in individuals with PMDs.

Deficits in emotional awareness and clarity are closely linked to the use of less adaptive ER strategies, which in turn contribute to poorer ER outcomes and worse psychopathology [68]. Our review found that individuals with PMDD report lower emotional awareness and clarity compared to controls, based on two studies with significant group differences and large effect sizes. Both studies measured emotional awareness and clarity at a single timepoint, and neither specified menstrual cycle phases, limiting our understanding of phase-related variability. However, a high-quality experimental study found evidence of emotional clarity deficits in individuals with PMDD during the late-luteal phase compared to the mid-follicular phase. Specifically, the study reported poorer performance and a negative interpretation bias when interpreting neutral and affective facial expressions. While these findings point to potential emotional clarity deficits, inconsistencies across studies and paradigms suggest that more research is needed to clarify these results. Future research should examine how individuals with PMDD identify and understand the types and origins of their emotions, and whether they can objectively differentiate between subtle variations in their own emotional experiences, as opposed to those of others.

The identification process is also influenced by an individual’s valuation of emotions, including beliefs about their goodness, utility, and tolerability [69]. Our review found evidence for a self-reported lack of access to ER strategies in people with PMDD compared to controls. This suggests that individuals with PMDD may believe they have fewer or no effective ways to manage their emotions once upset. Such beliefs about emotional malleability are linked to poorer well-being, increased psychological distress, and higher levels of psychopathology [70, 71]. This indicates the importance of examining how beliefs about ER strategies influence psychological distress in individuals with PMDD. Furthermore, future research could explore whether maladaptive beliefs about emotions lead to the selection of less adaptive ER strategies, such as avoiding emotions instead of reappraising them.

When considering the identification stage as a whole, it becomes clear that heightened sensitivity to negative stimuli, combined with reduced emotional awareness and clarity, as well as dysfunctional beliefs about ER abilities, may result in difficulties initiating effective ER. If individuals with PMDD are unaware of or struggle to clarify their emotions, they may fail to recognise the need for regulation or to select an appropriate strategy. Moreover, if they hold beliefs that their ER efforts are ineffective, they may be less likely to engage in regulation, potentially leading to increased negative affect. Future experimental research should explore how heightened emotional sensitivity impacts the initiation of ER, as well as the roles of emotional awareness, clarity, and dysfunctional beliefs in this process. Psychoeducational interventions targeting emotional understanding and behavioural experiments designed to challenge dysfunctional ER beliefs may help enhance emotional awareness and clarity, thereby improving the initiation of effective ER strategies when needed.

Problems in the Selection stage

To identify potential alterations in the selection process of ER strategies, we reviewed studies investigating the use of attention deployment, cognitive change, and response modulation strategies in individuals with PMDD and PMS. The literature primarily focuses on which strategies people with PMDD and PMS tend to endorse, with all studies in this stage relying on self-reports. The strategies explored include mindfulness and rumination (attention deployment), cognitive reappraisal and acceptance (cognitive change), suppression (response modulation), avoidance/distancing (situation selection), and social support/problem solving (situation modification).

All studies on attention deployment suggested that individuals with PMDD and PMS tend to use certain strategies, particularly rumination, more frequently and others, such as mindfulness, less frequently than controls. There is strong evidence of elevated rumination in people with PMDD and PMS compared to controls. Notably, all three studies that measured rumination across menstrual phases found increased rumination, especially its uncontrollable component, during the late-luteal phase compared to the follicular phase in individuals with PMDD and PMS, while this pattern was not observed in controls. These findings align with previous reviews linking ER strategies to psychopathology [17]. Together, the evidence suggests that people with PMDD and severe PMS may fixate on negative thoughts and struggle to disengage from them or focus on the present moment, compared to those without PMDD or PMS. However, more research is needed to better understand the relationship between rumination and mindfulness in this population.

Regarding cognitive change strategies, most studies indicated that people with PMDD and PMS tend to use certain strategies, like cognitive reappraisal, acceptance of emotions, and self-acceptance, less frequently than healthy controls. This is consistent with previous research on ER strategies in other populations [17]. However, some studies found null or opposing results, possibly due to the use of heterogeneous self-report measures for assessing cognitive change constructs. One study reported reduced self-acceptance during the late-luteal phase compared to the follicular phase in individuals with PMDD, although phase differences in reappraisal were inconclusive. Taken together, these findings suggest that people with PMDD and PMS may use fewer cognitive change strategies than healthy controls, though more research is needed to confirm these results and explore whether cognitive change strategies are less frequently used at specific menstrual phases.

Regarding response modulation strategies, there was no clear evidence of group differences in expressive suppression, where individuals attempt to inhibit the outward expression of their emotions. However, there was evidence suggesting that PMDD participants report higher levels of suppression during the early-luteal and follicular phases, compared to the late-luteal and premenstrual phases. This may imply that suppression is less available or less effective for people with PMDD during the symptomatic phases of their menstrual cycle, making it harder to use as an ER strategy. Future research could further investigate this by using experimental tasks that require participants to switch between different ER strategies, including suppression.

When it comes to situation selection strategies, most studies in the review suggested that people with PMDD and PMS may use certain strategies, like avoidance or distancing, more frequently than controls. This is consistent with other research linking these strategies to ER difficulties in other mental health disorders [17]. However, some studies found that situation selection may not be impaired in PMS unless depression is also present. This confounding factor limits the ability to draw clear conclusions about the role of avoidance and distancing in PMS and highlights the importance of gold-standard prospective assessments for identifying PMDD and PMS cases, separate from comorbid depressive symptoms. Finally, there was no clear evidence of impairment in situation modification strategies (e.g., seeking social support or problem solving) between people with PMDD or PMS and controls.

When considering the selection stage as a whole, it appears that individuals with PMDD and severe PMS tend to rely on specific strategies, particularly rumination, more frequently than controls. However, current research does not provide clear answers as to whether individuals with PMDD and PMS struggle to shift between different ER strategies or whether they place varying levels of importance on certain strategies. Furthermore, the existing studies do not shed light on how effectively these strategies are applied. Self-reported frequency of ER strategy use may not accurately reflect the success of the strategy. It is possible that someone might frequently employ a strategy (such as rumination or avoidance) without successfully managing their emotional responses, which could lead to poor ER outcomes.

To better understand these dynamics, more research is needed to explore why people with PMDD and severe PMS tend to use certain strategies more or less often than healthy individuals, and whether these strategies are effective for them. Therapeutic interventions at this stage of ER may benefit from either broadening the range of available strategies or guiding individuals to identify and implement the most effective strategies for their emotional needs.

Problems in the Implementation stage

Experimental research is essential for determining whether individuals with PMDD and severe PMS struggle to effectively execute ER strategies. A key finding from this review is the limited number of studies in the implementation stage of the EPM, particularly experimental studies that assess the successful application of ER strategies. While more research has focused on alterations in earlier stages, such as identification and selection of strategies, there is less clarity about whether individuals with PMDD and PMS also face difficulties when it comes to carrying out these strategies.

Of the six studies included in this review, only two employed experimental designs. One high-quality study found that individuals with PMDD struggled to implement distancing strategies during emotionally negative trials in the late luteal phase, suggesting a phase-specific difficulty with perspective-taking. During the follicular phase, however, their performance was more similar to that of healthy controls. In contrast, another experimental study found no evidence of ER implementation difficulties in individuals with PMS. This study reported no attentional biases or emotional interference during the Emotional Stroop Task, and interestingly, PMS participants responded faster than controls across both menstrual cycle phases, calling into question the generalisability of implementation deficits in PMS.

In addition to the experimental studies, self-report studies provide some insight into ER implementation in PMDD and PMS, though findings are somewhat inconsistent. Several studies found that individuals with PMDD reported greater difficulties with impulse control, emotion adjustment (e.g., emotional flexibility), and emotion tolerance (e.g., confronting rather than avoiding emotions). However, not all studies found these deficits, with some reporting no differences in self-control between PMS and control groups. Higher-quality studies suggested that people with PMDD experience more interference when completing tasks under emotional distress, and one study also noted that PMS participants used confrontive coping strategies more often than controls. Only one study examined menstrual phase differences and found some evidence that emotional flexibility and tolerance were reduced in PMDD cases during the premenstrual phase compared to the follicular phase.

Overall, these findings do not suggest a broad impairment in ER implementation for individuals with PMDD and PMS. Rather, difficulties may emerge under specific emotional conditions or at particular phases of the menstrual cycle. The challenges appear less related to a general inability to implement ER strategies and more related to difficulties in applying strategies in a deliberate, contextually appropriate manner.

However, it is important to interpret self-report results with caution. Subjective assessments may conflate difficulties in ER implementation with issues in earlier stages of the ER process, such as poor emotional clarity (identification stage) or the selection of ineffective strategies. Therefore, perceived difficulties in implementing ER may, in fact, reflect disruptions earlier in the regulatory sequence. To address this, more experimental and real-world studies are needed to clarify whether self-reported ER difficulties in PMDD are truly indicative of implementation impairments or are the result of upstream deficits in the regulatory process. Such clarification is crucial to ensure that therapeutic interventions, such as ER strategy training, are properly targeted and effective.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of the current review is the organisation of our findings into a process model of ER guided by the EPM [19, 24]. This approach allows for a nuanced understanding of where within the ER process individuals with PMDD and PMS may experience specific difficulties, offering critical insights for the design of tailored therapeutic interventions. To enhance the scope and inclusivity of the review, a broad range of terms related to ER and its individual components were used in the search strategy. Although this was not a systematic review focused exclusively on clinically confirmed PMDD diagnoses, another notable strength lies in the inclusion of studies that employed prospective symptom tracking over a minimum of one menstrual cycle, most over two. This is particularly important given the limitations of retrospective reporting, which is often subject to recall bias [12].

Nonetheless, this review also has several limitations. First, despite adhering to PRISMA guidelines [72, 73] and conducting a full review of all 469 studies that met the inclusion criteria, it is still possible that some relevant research was inadvertently excluded. Additionally, small sample sizes and the restriction to English-language publications limit both the statistical power and generalisability of the findings. Another concern is the inconsistency in the timing of assessments across studies. Many grouped participants into broad cycle phases (e.g., follicular vs. luteal), which risks obscuring more fine-grained, within-phase variations [13]. Combined with varied approaches to PMDD and PMS identification and assessment, this contributes to conceptual ambiguity and hampers the field's ability to develop a coherent picture of ER functioning in PMDD and PMS, and any potential differences in ER between people with PMDD and subclinical PMDD or PMS cases. Future studies should adopt standardised and validated diagnostic tools, such as the Daily Record of Severity of Problems (DRSP) [74] or the Carolina Premenstrual Assessment Scoring System (C-PASS) [75], as well as reliable cycle phase verification techniques like LH testing or cycle day counting.

The methodological approach of many included studies also presents challenges. Most relied heavily on self-report measures and cross-sectional data. While self-reports provide valuable insight into individuals' subjective experiences, they are limited in their ability to capture implicit or automatic ER processes and often require high levels of self-awareness, which can compromise validity. Moreover, there is a notable lack of experimental and ambulatory studies that directly observe ER behaviour in real-time or naturalistic settings. Future research would benefit from incorporating repeated measures, ecological momentary assessment (EMA), and laboratory-based paradigms to assess ER in a more dynamic and objective manner. In addition, the conceptualisation and operationalisation of ER varied considerably across studies. Terms like “emotional tolerance” were used inconsistently and measured using a range of instruments that may not assess equivalent constructs. This heterogeneity complicates the interpretation of findings and calls into question the construct validity of some ER subscales. Therefore, caution is warranted when drawing conclusions based on these measures.

Finally, our review is inherently limited by its narrative nature. Given the diversity of methodologies, measures, and populations across the included studies, a meta-analytic synthesis was not feasible at this stage. Furthermore, the ER process taxonomy we employed was developed using a bottom-up approach, informed by existing theoretical models and author descriptions. While this approach allowed for a practical classification of available measures, future reviews should evaluate the structure of this taxonomy to validate its robustness and applicability.

Although this review focused on PMDD and PMS, Premenstrual Exacerbation (PME) represents a related but distinct condition that warrants further investigation. PME involves the cyclical worsening of pre-existing psychiatric symptoms and is likely more prevalent than PMDD in the general population. While excluded here to maintain diagnostic clarity, future research should explore ER processes in PME, as hormone-related shifts may interact differently with chronic symptomatology. Additionally, there is emerging evidence that ER strategies fluctuate across the menstrual cycle in individuals without PMDD and PMS. Including studies that track cycle-based changes in ER in general nonclinical samples or transdiagnostic samples could enhance understanding of how hormonal variations influence emotional functioning more broadly. This represents an important direction for future research.

Conclusion

This review suggests that individuals with PMDD and PMS experience some difficulties in the ER process across the identification, selection, and implementation stages. A consistent and notable finding is the heightened rumination observed in PMDD and PMS cases, particularly during the late-luteal phase of the menstrual cycle. This ruminative thinking is likely to amplify emotional distress and impede their ability to disengage from negative thoughts, which in turn disrupts the ability to process emotions adaptively.

However, most studies relied on self-report measures and cross-sectional designs, limiting our ability to draw definitive conclusions regarding the extent of ER deficits or their impact across emotional contexts and menstrual cycle phases. The findings are further complicated by small sample sizes, inconsistent menstrual phase identification, and the influence of comorbid conditions, such as depression. As a result, while there is evidence suggesting that individuals PMDD and PMS experience ER difficulties, more robust experimental research is needed to clarify these issues.

In clinical practice, the results suggest that interventions targeting ER skills may be beneficial, but more research is required to develop phase-specific and individualised approaches. Future studies should prioritise longitudinal designs, experimental methods, and ecological momentary assessments to capture ER processes in real time. Additionally, standardising diagnostic tools and measurement techniques will be critical to enhancing the reliability of findings in PMDD. Based on results in the review, therapeutic interventions should not only aim to improve ER strategies but also address underlying emotional awareness, clarity deficits, and maladaptive beliefs about ER.

In conclusion, while current evidence suggests that ER difficulties exist in PMDD and PMS, further research is necessary to refine our understanding and develop targeted interventions that address the complex and dynamic nature of ER challenges across the menstrual cycle.
