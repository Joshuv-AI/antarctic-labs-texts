A unique case report of Wildervanck syndrome in association with facial nerve paresis and arachnodactyly
Authors: P. Surekha, V. Kavitha, Pravallika Penugonda, Mallikarjun M. Heralgi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Wildervanck syndrome, also called as cervico-oculo-acoustic syndrome. This is characterized by the triad of Klippel–Feil syndrome, Duane retraction syndrome, and congenital deafness. Other associations described are cardiovascular and musculoskeletal abnormalities. Some reports, in fact, state that nearly one percent of hearing-impaired females may be affected by Wildervanck syndrome. In our case report, a child who had the complete triad of the syndrome along with arachnodactyly (long slender digits), camptodactyly, short stature, and facial nerve paresis has been described.

Introduction

Wildervanck syndrome, also known as cervico-oculo-acoustic syndrome, is characterized by the triad of Klippel–Feil syndrome (KFS) (abnormal fusion of two/more cervical vertebra), Duane retraction syndrome (DRS), and congenital deafness.[1] The prevalence, as per Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man database, is <1/1000,000.[2] The etiology is unknown, but Wildervanck suggested that it is due to polygenic heredity with sex limitation to the female, though McKusick raised the possibility of sex-linked dominance with fatal effect in the male.[13] It has female-to-male preponderance of 10:1.[1] The first case was described in 1952 by Wildervanck, till date only 90 cases of Wildervanck syndrome with the complete triad have been described.[3] In addition, other clinical features that manifest with Wildervanck syndrome are bilateral DRS,[4] facial nerve paralysis,[5] congenital heart malformations (atrial septal defect),[6] crocodile tears,[7] Dandy–walker syndrome,[7] cleft palate,[8] camptodactyly,[9] Sprengel’s deformity, cervicomedullary neuroschisis, brainstem hypoplasia, mental retardation, absent/deformed auricles, and severe inner ear anomalies.[10]

Case Report

A 15-yearold girl presented to our outpatient department with complaints of blurring of vision in both eyes at a distance for 2 years. There was no previous history of having consulted an ophthalmologist.

The child was born to a couple with a history of consanguineous marriage (second degree), delivered normally at full term from an uncomplicated pregnancy. A mild developmental delay in the form of delayed speech acquisition and below-par scholastic performance in school was noted. She attained menarche at the age of 14 years with normal menstrual cycles and secondary sexual characteristic features. A medical report of the child was presented to us by the parents wherein the child was evaluated in a multidisciplinary approach. An endocrinologist evaluated the child for short stature and the findings noted in the medical report were: X-ray of cervical and thoracic spine revealed: Fusion of cervical vertebra; cervicodorsal scoliosis; loss of normal cervical lordosis and butterfly thoracic vertebra (T4-T5). However, parents did not have the x-ray films of the same. Ultrasound abdomen and pelvis was of normal study. Hearing impairment (moderate sensorineural deafness) as confirmed by audiogram was noted. Cardiovascular and respiratory systems were normal; however, there was speech delay as opined by pediatrician.

None of the family members was found to have congenital anomalies.

On general examination, we found that the child had short stature (height <3rd percentile), weight of 10th percentile, subtle left face turn, short neck [Figure 1a], mild left sided lagophthalmos [Figure 1b] with good bells phenomenon, less prominent nasolabial fold on left side of face [Figure 1b], deviation of mouth to right side [Figure 1b], low posterior hairline [Figure 1c], arachnodactyly [Figure 1d and e], camptodactyly [Figure 1d], and Steinberg thumb sign: When the first digit of a closed fist extend beyond the ulnar border of palm of the hand [Figure 1e] and limitation of neck movements. Other cranial nerve functions were normal.

Additional features of Wildervanck syndrome. (a) Short neck and minimal deviation of angle of mouth to right side; (b) Lagophthalmos; Presence of right nasolabial fold which is prominent as compared to left side; (c) Low posterior hair line; (d and e) Arachnodactyly of all fingers: (d) Camptodactyly of little fingers in both hands; (e) Positive Steinberg thumb sign

On ophthalmic examination, child’s uncorrected visual acuity (UCVA): Oculus Dexter (OD)-6/18, N6; Oculus Sinister (OS)-6/12, N6. Her best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA): OD-+3.00 D Sphere and -2.50 D Cylinder at 180 degree 6/9, N6; OS-+2.50 D Sphere and-1.50 D Cylinder at 180 degree 6/9, N6.

Ocular examination: Child was orthophoric in the primary position [Figure 2b]. However, on extraocular movements, there was limitation of abduction and adduction in OD; limitation of abduction associated with widening of palpebral fissure on attempted abduction and narrowing of palpebral fissure on adduction in OS, with no upshoot or downshoot in OU, suggestive of Type 3 DRS in OD and Type 1 DRS in OS [Figure 2a and c]. Anterior and posterior segment findings were normal. A diagnosis of Wildervanck syndrome with arachnodactyly, camptodactyly, short stature, facial nerve paresis, and bilateral regular, with the rule astigmatism (mixed)) and refractive amblyopia was made.

Duane retraction syndrome. (a) Limitation of abduction in OD and narrowing of palpebral fissure in OS; (b) Orthotropia in primary position; (c) Limitation of adduction in OD and limitation of abduction associated with widening of palpebral fissure in OS

Treatment: The child was advised spectacles, observation for lagophthalmos, and regular follow-up.

Discussion

Till date, 90 cases of Wildervanck syndrome with the complete triad (KFS, DRS, and congenital deafness) have been described.[3] Apart from the presence of a complete triad, other anomalies described in our patient may be considered as coincidental or an extended part of the genetic disorder. It is important to note that the child was born to parents who had consanguineous marriage; however, none of the patient’s family members had congenital anomalies.

Most of the cases with Wildervanck syndrome described in the literature had unilateral DRS. However, few reports have mentioned bilateral DRS.[4] Our patient too had bilateral DRS with no deviation in the primary position.

One-third of the patients with Wildervanck syndrome have been described to have hearing loss. Although Wildervanck stated that deafness should be sensorineural, cases with conductive or mixed loss have also been reported.[1] Audiometry in our patient revealed a moderate degree of sensorineural deafness. Arachnodactyly diagnosed based on wrist and Steinberg thumb sign is usually associated with Marfan syndrome,[1112] but our patient paradoxically had short stature. Till date, only one article on Wildervanck syndrome in association with facial nerve paralysis has been reported.[5] Gupte et al. found an association of atrial septal defect (ASD) in this syndrome.[6] Haciyakupoglu described an association of this syndrome with crocodile tears and Dandy Walker syndrome.[7] Numerous congenital anomalies are described along with the Wildervanck syndrome, including cleft palate,[8] camptodactyly,[9] Sprengel’s deformity, cervicomedullary neuroschisis, brainstem hypoplasia, mental retardation, absent/deformed auricles, and severe inner ear anomalies.[10] Apart from the classical triad of Wildervanck syndrome, our patient presented with arachnodactyly[12] (unique feature described in our report) along with camptodactyly, short stature, and left facial nerve paresis.

Conclusion

Although most patients of DRS have only ocular features, many associated systemic anomalies have been observed. Hence, it is very important to perform a thorough systemic examination to rule out other associated congenital anomalies. It is necessary to investigate for additional musculoskeletal abnormalities and cardiac malformations. Early diagnosis and timely intervention will have a positive impact on patient’s quality of life.

Limitation of our case report

X-ray films were not available with the parents.
