Development and Validation of a Disability Risk Prediction Model for Older Adults Based on Machine Learning: A Multi-Algorithm Comparison with SHAP Interpretation
Authors: Shaoting Yang, Heting Liang, Yamin Peng, Yumeng Zhang, Qingyun Mao, Honghong Wen, Jiawen Tang, Xiaoli Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo develop a predictive model for disability risk in older adults using machine learning algorithms.MethodsA convenience sample of 13,809 older adults (aged ≥60 years) was recruited from seven medical institutions, three communities, and five nursing homes in Zunyi City, Guizhou Province. Participants were randomly divided into a training set (n = 9667) and a validation set (n = 4142) at a 7:3 ratio. Disability status was used as the outcome variable. Nine machine learning algorithms—logistic regression, decision tree, random forest, XGBoost, LightGBM, support vector machine, artificial neural network, K‑nearest neighbor, and naïve Bayes—were used to construct prediction models. Model performance was evaluated using area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), accuracy, and other metrics, and the best‑performing model was selected. The SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) method was used for interpretability analysis of the optimal model.ResultsAmong the 13,809 participants, 5308 (38.44%) were identified as having disability. Among the nine models, LightGBM achieved the highest AUC (0.859), accuracy (0.792), precision (0.771), sensitivity (0.651), specificity (0.880), and F1 score (0.706).ConclusionAmong the developed prediction models, the LightGBM‑based model demonstrated superior overall predictive performance in internal validation, providing a reference for disability management in older adults.

Introduction

Disability refers to a state of physical limitation or loss of activity capacity due to aging, disease, or physical/mental impairments.1 The occurrence of disability not only reduces the overall quality of life of older adults2 and increases the risk of cognitive impairment,3 frailty,4 and adverse emotional outcomes,5 but also places heavy burdens on family caregivers and public health systems.6 In recent years, the concept of intrinsic capacity (IC) proposed by the World Health Organization (WHO) emphasizes that older adults often experience a decline in IC before the onset of disease.7 IC encompasses five domains: cognition, sensory function, vitality, motor function, and psychological status, with heterogeneous patterns of impairment. Identifying different types of IC impairment can help detect populations at high risk of disability at an earlier stage.

As population aging accelerates in China, the number of disabled older adults continues to rise.8 It is projected that by 2030, the proportion of disabled older adults will reach 57% of the total elderly population.9 However, the progression of disability can be delayed. Early identification and intervention of risk factors for disability in older adults may improve quality of life and reduce the incidence of adverse outcomes. Therefore, early recognition and active management of disability in older adults-and thereby improving their late-stage quality of life-is an urgent public health issue that requires broad societal attention and participation.

In recent years, research on disability prediction in older adults has increased, but most studies have primarily used traditional logistic regression with relatively simple model construction.10,11 With the rapid development of information technology, a large number of machine learning algorithms have been applied in the medical field to build more accurate prediction models,12 offering a more precise approach to disability risk assessment in older adults. Therefore, this study constructed disability risk prediction models using nine machine learning algorithms, compared their performance, and applied the SHAP method13 to interpret the overall decision direction of the optimal model, aiming to provide a reference for health care professionals to rapidly assess and identify older adults at high risk of disability and implement timely targeted interventions.

Methods

Study Population

Using convenience sampling, we enrolled 13,809 older adults aged ≥60 years from seven medical institutions, three communities, and five nursing homes in Zunyi City, Guizhou Province, between October 2022 and September 2023. Inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥60 years; (2) ability to communicate normally; (3) voluntary participation with informed consent. Exclusion criteria were: (1) severe mental illness or severe cognitive impairment precluding communication; (2) severe physical illness precluding cooperation; (3) presence of malignant tumors or other severe comorbidities with an expected survival <3 months. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Zunyi Medical University (KLL2022-814) and was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided informed consent. The data used in this study were derived from a large cross‑sectional survey conducted between October 2022 and September 2023 in Zunyi City, Guizhou Province, China. A subset of this dataset (n = 8427) was previously used in a study by Zhang et al7 to explore patterns of intrinsic capacity impairment in older adults with chronic diseases. The present study includes the full sample (N = 13,809) and addresses a completely different research question—namely, the development and validation of a machine learning‑based predictive model for disability risk. The analytical methods, outcome variables, and study objectives are distinct from those of the previously published paper. No overlap exists in the results, figures, or core conclusions.

Sample Size Estimation

Sample size was determined using the events per variable (EPV) criterion,14 with a minimum EPV of 10 to ensure robust results. Candidate variables referred to all potential predictors considered prior to model development.15 A total of 25 candidate variables were included. Based on the literature, the prevalence of disability in older adults was approximately 11.6%.16 Accounting for a 20% attrition rate, the minimum required sample size was calculated as 25×10 × (1 + 20%) / 11.6% ≈ 2586. To further enhance model stability, we expanded recruitment beyond the minimum requirement and ultimately included 13,809 valid cases after quality control, providing sufficient statistical power for model development and validation.

Assessment Instruments

Disability Assessment

Disability was assessed using measures of basic activities of daily living (BADL) and instrumental activities of daily living (IADL). BADL was evaluated with the Barthel Index (BI),17 which assesses 10 domains including feeding, grooming, and dressing. Total scores range from 0 to 100, with a score <100 defined as disability. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for this scale was 0.865.18 IADL was assessed using the Lawton Instrumental Activities of Daily Living Scale,19 which includes eight items such as shopping, transportation, cooking, and housekeeping. Endorsement of “needs help” or “unable to perform” on any item was defined as disability. The Cronbach’s α coefficient for this scale was 0.932.20 Following the approach of previous studies,21,22 Participants meeting either BI <100 or any IADL impairment were classified as disabled.

Cognitive Function Assessment

Cognitive function was assessed using the Chinese Mini-Mental State Examination (CMMS),23 which consists of 30 items with a total score of 30, covering time orientation (5 points), place orientation (5 points), memory registration (3 points), attention and calculation (5 points), delayed recall (3 points), language (8 points), and visuospatial ability (1 point). Higher scores indicate better cognitive function.

Nutritional Status Assessment

Nutritional status was evaluated using the Mini-Nutritional Assessment Short-Form (MNA-SF),24 which includes six items: BMI, weight change in the past 3 months, mobility, dietary intake, acute illness or psychological stress in the past 3 months, and neuropsychological status. Total scores range from 0 to 14, with higher scores indicating better nutritional status. The Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.703.

Walking Ability Assessment

Walking ability was assessed using a revised version of the Geriatric Balance and Functional Walking Ability Assessment Scale,25–27 which comprises 10 items covering walking (5 items) and balance (5 items), including level walking, walking at different speeds, turning and stopping, stepping over obstacles, stair climbing and descent reporting, static standing, and dynamic balance recovery. Each item has standardized instructions. Total scores range from 0 to 30, with higher scores indicating better walking ability.

Frailty Assessment

Frailty was assessed using the FRAIL Scale,28 which includes five items: fatigue, resistance, ambulation, illness, and loss of weight. Each item is scored 1 for “yes” and 0 for “no”. Total scores range from 0 to 5, with higher scores indicating greater frailty. Scores of 0 indicate robust status, 1–2 indicate prefrailty, and ≥3 indicate frailty. The Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.70.

Psychological Status Assessment

Depressive symptoms were assessed using the 15-item Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS-15).29 Scores ≥5 were defined as depression.

Fall Risk Assessment

Fall risk was assessed using the Chinese version of the Self-Rated Fall Risk Questionnaire,30,31 which includes 12 items with dichotomous scoring. Total scores range from 0 to 14, with higher scores indicating greater fall risk.

General Information Questionnaire

A general information questionnaire designed by the research team collected demographic and health-related data, including age, sex, height, weight, ethnicity, education level, marital status, residence type, living arrangement, monthly household income per capita, insurance type, alcohol use, smoking status, social engagement frequency, physical exercise frequency, perceived social support, hearing impairment, vision impairment, and multimorbidity.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 27.0. Missing data were handled using multiple imputation with five repetitions. The proportions of missing data for each variable in the final analysis were as follows: BMI 0.37%, CMMS 0.28%, Nutrition Score 0.97%, Fall Risk Score 1.04%, while the outcome variable and all other covariates had no missing data. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, with group comparisons using Z‑tests or chi‑square tests. Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation, and non‑normally distributed variables as median with interquartile range [M (P25, P75)]. Feature selection was performed using R 4.5.1. Participants were randomly divided into training (n = 9667) and validation (n = 4142) sets in a 7:3 ratio. Statistically significant factors (P < 0.05) identified through univariate analysis were further screened using LASSO regression with 10‑fold cross‑validation (glmnet package, alpha = 1). The optimal penalty parameter λ was selected according to the one‑standard‑error rule (lambda.1se), i.e, the largest λ within one standard error of the minimum cross‑validated deviance, to obtain a parsimonious set of predictors. The resulting non‑zero coefficients identified the final feature variables. These feature variables served as input predictors, with disability status as the outcome, to construct nine machine learning models using Python 3.10: logistic regression (LR), random forest (RF), decision tree (DT), support vector machine (SVM), XGBoost, artificial neural network (ANN), K‑nearest neighbor (KNN), LightGBM, and naïve Bayes (NB). Hyperparameters were optimized using grid search. Ten‑fold cross‑validation was applied to train the models, where the training set was divided into 10 equal parts, with 9 parts used for training and 1 part for validation in each iteration. The final model performance was taken as the average of the 10 iterations. Model performance was evaluated using area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, and F1 score. The optimal model was interpreted using SHAP.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

Among the 13,809 participants, 5308 (38.44%) were classified as having disability. The sample included 6973 men (50.50%) and 6836 women (49.50%), with a mean age of 71.19 ± 7.10 years. A total of 992 participants (7.18%) were from ethnic minorities, 471 (3.41%) had a college degree or above; 11,789 (85.35%) were married; 7074 (51.23%) lived in urban areas, and 6735 (48.77%) in rural areas; 12,721 (92.12%) lived with family members, and 317 (2.30%) were self-financed; 7108 (51.47%) had a monthly household income per capita ≥2000 CNY, and 6701 (48.53%) had <2000 CNY. The final selected variables included age, fall risk score, CMMS score, walking ability test score, nutritional status score, weekly physical exercise frequency, alcohol use, presence of vision impairment, presence of multimorbidity, and frailty status, for a total of 10 items. Collinearity diagnostics were performed on the 10 selected feature variables, and all variance inflation factors (VIF) were <5 (see Table 3), indicating no collinearity issues. To further explore the interrelationships among the 10 feature variables, a correlation heatmap was generated (Figure 3). As shown in the heatmap, most pairwise correlations among the selected features were low to moderate (r < 0.4). The strongest correlation was observed between frailty status and fall risk score (r = 0.625). No correlation exceeded the commonly accepted multicollinearity threshold (e.g, r > 0.8), consistent with the VIF results (all VIF < 5). Discussion

Disability Prevalence and Model Performance

In this large sample of 13,809 older adults from Southwest China, the overall prevalence of disability was 38.44%, which is higher than the national average of approximately 11.6% reported in previous studies.16 This difference may be attributed to the older age of our participants and the combined use of both BADL and IADL criteria.

In recent years, several studies have attempted to develop machine learning models for predicting functional decline or disability in older adults. A recent systematic review of disability risk prediction models reported that the AUC or C‑statistic of these models ranged from 0.650 to 0.853,32 suggesting that there remains considerable room for improvement in predictive performance. For instance, one study using random forest to predict unmet activities of daily living (ADL) needs reported an AUC of approximately 0.752,33 while another prospective cohort study based on CHARLS data used six machine learning algorithms to predict the 3‑year disability risk in 2175 healthy older adults and found that the XGBoost model achieved the best performance with an AUC of 0.803.34 In comparison, our LightGBM model achieved an AUC of 0.859 in the validation set, outperforming traditional logistic regression (AUC = 0.832) and demonstrating a meaningful improvement in discriminative ability. Moreover, unlike many previous models that relied on extensive assessments or electronic health records, our model requires only 10 easily obtainable variables—all collectable through brief questionnaires or simple bedside assessments—which substantially enhances its feasibility for rapid screening in community and primary care settings. This combination of strong predictive performance and practical usability distinguishes our model from prior prediction tools.

Feature Variables for Disability in Older Adults

The “black box” nature of machine learning models can make internal decision processes difficult to interpret. To enhance transparency, we applied SHAP to the LightGBM model. Figure 5 shows that walking ability, fall risk score, frailty status, CMMS score, and age were the five most influential variables. Walking ability was the leading risk factor: when walking ability score fell below 23, disability risk increased markedly. This threshold is suggested as a community screening cut-off, with focused gait and balance training recommended.35,36 Fall risk score ranked second; a score >1 shifted the risk from negative to positive and continued to rise. High fall risk can lead to fractures, prolonged bed rest, and functional decline,37,38 we suggest using a fall risk score >1 as a screening cut-off for high-risk populations, followed by early comprehensive assessment and intervention. Frailty status ranked third: both prefrailty and frailty were associated with higher disability risk. Frailty is a state of decreased physiological reserve across multiple systems, characterized by increased vulnerability to stressors.39 Sarcopenia directly impairs mobility,40 while chronic inflammation and mitochondrial dysfunction accelerate functional decline.41 Given the dynamic reversibility of frailty, early screening and comprehensive interventions (e.g, exercise, nutritional support) may delay its progression.42 Cognitive function (CMMS score) ranked fourth: a score <22 was associated with significantly increased risk, suggesting that mild cognitive impairment should prompt inclusion in disability risk management. Age ranked fifth: when age exceeded 72 years, the SHAP value turned from negative to positive; we propose 72 years as a practical cut-off for community-based disability risk screening, with comprehensive geriatric assessment and multidisciplinary continuity of care.

In addition, lack of daily exercise, vision impairment, multimorbidity, and low nutritional score were associated with increased disability risk, whereas moderate alcohol consumption (compared with abstinence) showed a protective association. Lack of daily exercise can accelerate sarcopenia and balance decline.43 Moderate alcohol consumption may have potential benefits for cardiovascular outcomes in older adults with high cardiovascular burden,44 consistent with our finding that moderate alcohol consumption was associated with lower disability risk. However, other studies have shown that chronic excessive alcohol use impairs balance and coordination and increases fall risk.45 A nutritional score <11 increased disability risk, consistent with evidence that malnutrition accelerates sarcopenia and immune decline.46 Vision impairment directly affects mobility and accelerates functional deconditioning.47,48 Multimorbidity increases physiological burden and the risk of polypharmacy, amplifying disability risk.49,50 All the above factors can be improved through community-based health education and behavioral interventions. Health care institutions should strengthen the management of chronic diseases to prevent the transition from a single chronic condition to multimorbidity, thereby delaying the onset of disability. Together, these variables constitute a multidimensional prediction framework encompassing physical function, frailty status, cognitive ability, lifestyle, and disease status, providing actionable entry points for screening in both community and hospital settings.

Strengths and Limitations

This study has several strengths. First, it is a multi‑center study collecting data from seven medical institutions, three communities, and five nursing homes in Zunyi City, providing sufficient data and diverse sources, which enhances stable and reliable statistical inference, improves prediction accuracy and generalizability, and reduces the limitation of single‑center studies. Second, disability in older adults is influenced by multiple factors; this study included comprehensive indicators covering demographics, lifestyle, physical function, cognition, and psychology, enabling the model to better capture potential risk factors and enhance predictive ability. Third, comparing multiple machine learning algorithms offers advantages over a single method: algorithm diversity improves overall performance, enhances the comprehensiveness and interpretability of feature selection, and better addresses data complexity and uncertainty, compensating for the limitations of any single method. Fourth, the application of SHAP analysis provides clinically interpretable risk thresholds that bridge the gap between predictive modeling and clinical practice. Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. The cross‑sectional design precludes causal inference, and our model has only been internally validated, so its generalizability to external populations cannot yet be assessed. In addition, although we used well‑validated standardized instruments, including the Barthel Index and the Lawton IADL scale for assessment, we acknowledge the absence of a comprehensive geriatric assessment conducted by multidisciplinary teams and the lack of objective measures for both covariates and the outcome. These omissions may limit the depth of clinical phenotyping and represent a notable limitation of this study. Future work should prioritize external validation in independent cohorts and incorporate objective measures into model development, with the goal of establishing longitudinal prediction models that capture dynamic changes in risk factors over time. These efforts will be critical to translating our predictive model into meaningful clinical and public health practice.

Conclusion

This study constructed disability risk prediction models for older adults using nine machine learning algorithms. Internal validation showed that the LightGBM model achieved the best overall predictive performance (AUC = 0.859), significantly outperforming traditional logistic regression and several previously reported ML models. The model requires only 10 readily accessible clinical variables, making it practical for rapid screening in community and primary care settings. SHAP interpretability analysis further identified clinically meaningful risk thresholds including but not limited to walking ability < 23, fall risk > 1, CMMS < 22, age > 72, and nutritional score < 11 which are actionable and facilitate rapid identification of high-risk individuals by healthcare professionals. These unique findings provide a data-driven reference for disability management in older adults and lay the foundation for future implementation studies and external validation in diverse populations.
