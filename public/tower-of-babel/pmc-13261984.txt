Surgical management of Omphalocele–Exstrophy–Imperforate Anus–Spinal Defect (OEIS) complex: A case report
Authors: Odilbek Sidikov, Bahrom Kh Mirzakarimov, Halilullo T Ajimamatov, Umidjon Sh Mamajonov, Shuxrat Israilov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe OEIS complex—comprising omphalocele, cloacal exstrophy, imperforate anus, and spinal defects—remains among the most uncommon and severe neonatal malformations, and its reconstruction demands carefully sequenced operations. We describe a male neonate managed through a two-stage strategy. On the second day of life, omphalocele repair, adhesiolysis, terminal ileostomy, and cecal resection were undertaken, while bladder closure was intentionally postponed. At eight months, hemibladder approximation and abdominal wall reconstruction followed. Eleven-month follow-up showed stable clinical status, functioning ileostomy, and resolving right-sided hydronephrosis. This case supports staged reconstruction as a feasible pathway in this rare entity.Keywords: OEIS complex, Omphalocele, Cloacal exstrophy, Imperforate anus, Neonatal surgery, Staged repair

Introduction

The Omphalocele–Exstrophy–Imperforate Anus–Spinal Defect (OEIS) complex is one of the most severe congenital malformation syndromes, involving a combination of defects affecting the abdominal wall, urinary bladder, gastrointestinal tract, and spine.1 The estimated incidence ranges from 1 in 200,000 to 1 in 400,000 live births,2 making it an ultra-rare condition for which individual case reports with complete management details remain of significant educational and scientific value.3

Because of the involvement of multiple organ systems, management requires a coordinated multidisciplinary team including pediatric surgeons, neonatologists, pediatric urologists, and orthopedic specialists.4 The accepted surgical strategy involves a staged approach: initial neonatal stabilization and emergency interventions are performed first, followed by definitive reconstruction of the bladder and abdominal wall in subsequent operations.2

We report a case of OEIS complex in which a staged surgical strategy was applied, detailing both the first-stage neonatal procedure and the second-stage exstrophy closure performed at eight months of age, along with early follow-up outcomes. This report aims to contribute to the limited literature on complete staged management of this complex anomaly.

Case presentation

Patient history and initial presentation

A male newborn with OEIS complex was admitted to the pediatric surgery department. The infant was the fourth child of non-consanguineous parents and was born vaginally at 38 weeks of gestation with a birth weight of 3200 g. The mother was 32 years old and reported experiencing psychological stress and several infections during pregnancy. Prenatal care was limited, with only three antenatal visits. Ultrasonography performed at 3 and 5 months of gestation did not detect any fetal anomalies. The mother had no history of previous miscarriages, and her three prior children were all healthy.

On examination, the newborn presented with four major anomalies consistent with OEIS complex: omphalocele of the abdominal wall, cloacal exstrophy with an exposed intestinal plate between two hemibladders, imperforate anus, and lumbar spina bifida. Abdominal ultrasound revealed right-sided hydronephrosis with megaureter. The infant was immediately placed in an incubator, and given the severity of the defects, was urgently transferred to the pediatric surgical department for intervention (Fig. 1, Fig. 2).

First-stage surgical intervention (Day 2 of Life)

On the second day of life, the infant underwent the first-stage emergency surgical procedure under general anesthesia. The operative plan was to address immediately life-threatening conditions while intentionally deferring the cloacal exstrophy repair to allow patient physiological stabilization, as recommended for staged OEIS management.2

The following procedures were performed during the first-stage operation:

•Exploratory laparotomy: Laparotomy revealed that the terminal ileum and cecum were fused with the exstrophic intestinal plate. The colon appeared as a cord-like atretic structure. Multiple adhesions were identified throughout the abdominal cavity.

•Cecum and appendix resection: The cecum and appendix, which were fused to the exstrophic plate and non-viable, were resected.

The cloacal exstrophy was not addressed during this first stage, as the hemibladder plates were left in situ pending patient stabilization and future reconstruction. This decision was consistent with the established staged protocol for OEIS complex.2,5

Postoperative course after first stage

Following the first-stage procedure, the patient was transferred to the neonatal intensive care unit. Total parenteral nutrition and intravenous broad-spectrum antibiotics were initiated. Vital signs and urine output were carefully monitored. The ileostomy functioned adequately in the early postoperative period. The omphalocele repair site remained intact without signs of dehiscence or infection. The patient was gradually weaned to enteral feeds via the ileostomy and discharged home at six weeks of age in stable condition with outpatient follow-up. On serial postoperative assessment following the first stage, the abdominal wall closure remained well approximated with a flat, tension-free contour and no dehiscence; the terminal ileostomy was viable and pink with progressive enteral output; and the in-situ hemibladder plates stayed moist and well vascularised, without necrosis, retraction, or local infection, confirming a satisfactory anatomical result of the first-stage repair prior to discharge.

Second-stage surgical intervention (Age 8 Months)

At eight months of age, the patient was re-admitted for the planned second-stage procedure targeting the cloacal exstrophy. By this time, the infant's weight was approximately 7.2 kg and he was in satisfactory general condition. Preoperative imaging confirmed the anatomy of the hemibladder plates and the position of the urinary orifices.

The second-stage operation included:

•Bladder plate approximation and closure: The two hemibladder plates were mobilized and brought together in the midline. The bladder was reconstructed by approximating the detrusor muscle and mucosa in two layers, creating a closed bladder reservoir.

•Abdominal wall reconstruction: The infraumbilical abdominal wall was reconstructed in layers over the closed bladder.

•Urinary drainage: Bilateral ureteral stents and a suprapubic catheter were placed to ensure adequate urinary drainage during the healing period.

The operative time was approximately 220 minutes. Intraoperative blood loss was minimal and no transfusion was required. The patient was returned to the pediatric intensive care unit postoperatively.

Postoperative course after second stage and follow-up

The second-stage procedure was well tolerated. Postoperatively, the patient received intravenous antibiotics, analgesia, and careful fluid management. Urinary output through the suprapubic catheter was adequate. The ureteral stents were removed at two weeks postoperatively, and the suprapubic catheter was removed at four weeks. The bladder closure remained intact on follow-up imaging. At the most recent follow-up at 11 months of age, the patient was alive and in satisfactory condition. The ileostomy was functioning well. There were no signs of wound infection or bladder dehiscence. Renal ultrasound showed improvement in the right-sided hydronephrosis compared to the initial presentation. With respect to the results of the second-stage repair, the reconstructed bladder formed a closed, watertight reservoir with an intact suture line, and the infraumbilical abdominal wall healed as a symmetric, flat closure without fistula or wound dehiscence. Functionally, urinary drainage was satisfactory after removal of the stents and suprapubic catheter, the ileostomy continued to produce normally, and serial renal ultrasound documented progressive regression of the right-sided hydronephrosis from moderate to mild over the follow-up period. Further reconstructive stages, including anorectal reconstruction and genitourinary reconstruction, are planned in a multidisciplinary setting as the child grows.

Discussion

OEIS complex represents one of the most severe congenital malformation syndromes affecting the lower abdominal wall and pelvic organs. It encompasses omphalocele, cloacal exstrophy, imperforate anus, and spinal defects.5 The proximal intestine is often dilated, with the distal colon appearing as a cord-like blind-ending structure, which accounts for the invariable imperforate anus in these patients.4 The incidence of 1 in 200,000–1 in 400,000 live births makes it one of the rarest neonatal surgical emergencies.2

The pathogenesis of OEIS complex relates to abnormal embryological development during the fifth week of fetal life, when the cloaca normally divides into the urogenital sinus anteriorly and the hindgut posteriorly.6 The leading hypothesis involves underdevelopment of the lateral mesodermal folds leading to premature rupture of the cloacal membrane before its fusion with the urorectal septum, resulting in the spectrum of defects seen in OEIS complex.7 An alternative hypothesis by Feldkamp et al. attributes the condition to an early defect involving the caudal eminence.8 In addition, rare chromosomal abnormalities, such as 1p36 deletion, have been reported in association with the OEIS phenotype.9

The cornerstone of management is a staged surgical approach.2 In the current case, the first stage was performed as a neonatal emergency and focused on survival-critical interventions: omphalocele repair, bowel diversion via terminal ileostomy, and cecal resection. Consistent with reported staged protocols, the cloacal exstrophy was intentionally deferred, as immediate closure is associated with higher morbidity in critically ill neonates.2,5 The second stage at eight months targeted bladder plate approximation and closure, following adequate growth and stabilization of the infant.

This case demonstrates several important surgical and management principles. First, the staged approach allowed the neonate to survive the initial critical period and achieve adequate weight gain before the more complex reconstructive procedure. Second, the omphalocele repair and bowel diversion were successfully performed in the neonatal period without major complications. Third, the second-stage bladder closure at eight months was feasible and well-tolerated, consistent with reports in the literature describing delayed primary closure of the exstrophic bladder plates.2,10

A limitation of this report is that long-term outcomes, including continence, renal function, and sexual development, are not yet available given the patient's age. Future reconstructive stages, including anorectal pull-through and genitourinary reconstruction, are planned. These limitations are inherent to reporting on a condition that requires follow-up over many years, and do not diminish the educational value of documenting the staged approach and its early outcomes. The rarity of OEIS complex means that each published case, even with incomplete long-term follow-up, contributes meaningfully to the evidence base available to pediatric surgeons managing this condition.

Conclusion

OEIS complex is a rare and severe congenital anomaly requiring a carefully planned, multidisciplinary staged surgical approach. In this case, the first-stage neonatal procedure addressed omphalocele repair and bowel diversion, while exstrophy closure was intentionally deferred and successfully completed at eight months of age as the second stage. This report illustrates that staging the surgical repair—beginning with life-saving neonatal interventions and proceeding to exstrophy closure after patient stabilization—is a viable and reproducible strategy. Documentation of complete staged management pathways, even at early follow-up, is essential for advancing understanding of this ultra-rare condition.
