1.
Introduction

For any integer
n
, let
\zeta_{n}
denote a primitive
n
-th root of unity. For a prime
p
and a positive integer
k
, we denote the field with
p^{k}
elements as
\mathbf{F}_{p^{k}}
. The matrix
(\zeta_{p}^{ij})
with
i,j\in\{0,1,\cdots,p-1\}
is a Vandermonde matrix with non-zero determinant. It was Chebotarev, in 1926, who first noticed that any square submatrix of the above inherits the same property (see
[
16
]
). He proved

Theorem 1
.

If
I,J\subseteq\{0,1,2,\cdots p-1\}
with
|I|=|J|
then the matrix

(\zeta_{p}^{ij})_{i\in I,j\in J}
has non-zero determinant.

After Chebotarev’s initial proof, several others have emerged in the literature; Dieudonne
[
6
]
, Evans and Isaacs
[
8
]
, Goldstein et al.
[
11
]
, Tao
[
17
]
, Frenkel
[
10
]
,
just to mention a few. Several approaches were used but all require some algebraic information. Chebotarev’s original idea was to show that the
determinats of the matrices in question, which are elements of the
p
-th cyclotomic field
\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{p})
over
\mathbf{Q}
, do not vanish in the
p
-adic completion
\mathbf{Q}_{p}(\zeta_{p})
of
\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{p})
. Tao
[
17
]
constructs a polynomial in
n
variables and differentiates it repeatedly to obtain an integer-coefficient polynomial
P
where
P(1,1,\cdots,1)
is not a multiple of
p
, contradicting his cyclotomic integer lemma. In addition he proves the equivalence between Chebotarev’s theorem and a Fourier uncertainty principle in
\mathbf{Z}_{p}
. The proof in
[
8
]
employs homogeneous polynomials, and a theorem by Mitchell
[
14
]
, while the method used in
[
11
]
is much more algebraic and also establish Tao’s uncertainty principle in an algebraic context. Finally, Frenkel’s method involves a single-variable polynomial in
\mathbf{Z}[\zeta_{p}][x]
that reduces to a polynomial in
\mathbf{Z}_{p}[x]
with an overly large root multiplicity.

If
n
is not a single prime, we still have the Vandermonde matrix
(\zeta_{n}^{ij})_{0\leq i,j\leq n-1}
which is nonsingular, but we can find examples of square submatrices of the original that are singular (see
[
4
]
). On the other hand, no counterexample of a singular principal submatrix when
n
is a square-free integer is known. (By definition, a principal submatrix of
(\zeta_{n}^{ij})_{0\leq i,j\leq n-1}
is any submatrix
(\zeta_{n}^{ij})_{i,j\in I}
for some
I\subseteq\{0,1,\cdots,n-1\}
). The following conjecture was first stated in
[
3
]
, with a more general version presented in
[
4
]
.

Conjecture 1
.

If
n
is square-free then all principal submatrices of
(\zeta_{n}^{ij})
with
i,j\in\{0,1,\cdots,n-1\}

have nonzero determinant.

In
[
3
]
, the conjecture was confirmed for all
2\times 2
principal submatrices. Even more was actually proved: for
n\geq 2
, all principal
2\times 2
submatrices of
(\zeta_{n}^{ij})_{0\leq i,j\leq n-1}
are invertible if and only if
n
is square-free (see Corollary 2.14 in
[
3
]
).
In addition to the above, in
[
4
]
it was proved that, for
n>4
, all principal
3\times 3
submatrices of

(\zeta_{n}^{ij})_{0\leq i,j\leq n-1}
are invertible if and only if
n
is square free (see Theorem 1.1 in
[
4
]
).
These results also imply that any
(n-2)\times(n-2)
and any
(n-3)\times(n-3)
principal submatrix
of
(\zeta_{n}^{ij})_{0\leq i,j\leq n-1}
is non-singular, see Section
6
.

The truth of Conjecture
1
has important implications for problems connected to bases of finite dimensional linear spaces. For instance, in
[
3
]
, it is shown that two ordered bases of
\mathbf{C}^{n}
are
woven
(this means that we can drop some elements of one basis as long as we replace them with the corresponding elements of the other basis, and so keep the basis property) if and only if all principal minors of the change-of-basis matrix are nonsingular. When the two bases of
\mathbf{C}^{n}
in consideration are the usual basis
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\}
and the Fourier basis
\{u_{i}=(1,\zeta_{n}^{i},\zeta_{n}^{2i},\ldots,\zeta_{n}^{(n-1)i}),i=0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
the validity of Conjecture
1
for the dimension
n
implies that for any subset
I
of
\{0,1,\ldots,n-1\}
a vector
x\in\mathbf{C}^{n}
can be reconstructed by the values
x_{i}
,
i\in I
, and
\widehat{x}_{j}
,
j\notin I
. Here
\widehat{x}
is the Fourier Transform of the vector
x
(in the cyclic group of order
n
) or, in other words, the coefficients of
x
written in the Fourier basis
\{u_{i}\}
.

If
n=pr
with
p,r
distinct primes, then
\mathbf{Z}_{p}\times\mathbf{Z}_{r}\cong\mathbf{Z}_{n}
under the group isomorphism that sends

(1)

\mathbf{Z}_{p}\times\mathbf{Z}_{r}\ni(i_{p},\,i_{r})\longrightarrow i\in\mathbf{Z}_{n},

with
i\equiv i_{p}\cdot r+i_{r}\cdot p\pmod{pr}
. So
i\equiv i_{p}r\pmod{p}
and
i\equiv i_{r}p\pmod{r}
.

[The map above is easily seen to be a group homomorphism. Since the groups have the same cardinality, it suffices to show that its kernel is trivial to prove it is an isomorphism. If
(a,b)\in\mathbf{Z}_{p}\times\mathbf{Z}_{r}
is such that
ra+bp\equiv 0\pmod{pr}
, then
p\mid ra
and
r\mid bp
. Since
p
and

are coprime, it follows that

and
r\mid b
and thus
(a,b)=(0,0)
as desired.]

Assume now that a primitive
n
-th root
\zeta_{n}
of
1
is fixed. Using the isomorphism above, for every

, we have

(2)

\zeta_{n}^{i}=(\zeta_{n}^{r})^{i_{p}}\cdot(\zeta_{n}^{p})^{i_{r}}=\zeta_{p}^{i_{p}}\cdot\zeta_{r}^{i_{r}},

where
\zeta_{p}=\zeta_{n}^{r}
and
\zeta_{r}=\zeta_{n}^{p}
are primitive

-th and

-th roots of unity, respectively.
Note also that for every

we get

\zeta_{n}^{ij}=\zeta_{p}^{ri_{p}j_{p}}\cdot\zeta_{r}^{pi_{r}j_{r}}.

If
I\subseteq\mathbf{Z}_{n}
, (where
n=pr
), we define

(3)

I_{r}^{k}=\{i\in I\mid i_{r}=k\}

for each

.
Observe that
I=\dot{\bigcup}_{k}\,I_{r}^{k}
.

In this paper, we heavily use the approach introduced by P.E. Frenkel in
[
10
]
to partially address

. Our first main theorem deals with the case where

for some odd prime

Theorem A
.

Assume that
n=2p
with
p

I,J\subseteq\mathbf{Z}_{n}
with
|I_{2}^{k}|=|J_{2}^{k}|
, for

. Then the matrix

has nonzero determinant.

As principal submatrices are exactly those with
I=J
(in the notation of

) we get the Corollary that follows. We remark however, that
principal submatrices are indeed a special case, as for example in the group
\mathbf{Z}_{2}\times\mathbf{Z}_{5}

the subsets

I=\{(0,1),(0,2),(0,3),(1,3)\}
and
J=\{(0,0),(0,1),(0,4),(1,1)\}
satisfy
|I_{2}^{0}|=|J_{2}^{0}|=3
and
|I_{2}^{1}|=|J_{2}^{1}|=1
. In addition, to describe the isomorphism (
1
) in this case, note that any element
(a,b)\in\mathbf{Z}_{2}\times\mathbf{Z}_{5}
maps to

in
\mathbf{Z}_{10}
. Consequently, the sets

and

are mapped to the subsets
\{2,4,6,1\}
and
\{0,2,8,7\}
of

, respectively.

Corollary 1
.

If
n=2p
, where

is an odd prime, then every principal submatrix of
(\zeta_{n}^{ij})_{0\leq i,j\leq n-1}
has non-zero determinant.

In the general case where

and

are distinct odd primes, we can establish an analogue of

and

must be a primitive element in

.

(Zhang)
Let

. Suppose that

-th primitive root of unity in

Our main theorem reads:

Theorem B
.

Let

, where
p
and

are distinct odd primes such that

\mathbf{Z}_{r}
and

. If

As with
1
,

implies:

.

Assume

, where
p
and

While the inductive proof of

extends to more than two primes, our application of Zhang’s Theorem restricts us to the two-prime case. As this paper was nearing publication, we learned that Caragea et al.
[
5
]
had improved Zhang’s Theorem by removing the primitivity condition and verifying

[

]
also improved Zhang’s Theorem by lifting the primitivity restriction and also significantly lowering the bounds on the field’s characteristic.

In
[
17
]
, T. Tao proved that Chebotarev’s theorem is equivalent to an improved uncertainty principle for complex-valued functions that asserts:

|\mathrm{supp}(f)|+|\mathrm{supp}(\widehat{f})|\geq p+1,

for every non-zero complex function
f:\mathbf{Z}_{p}\to\mathbf{C}
, where
\widehat{f}
is the Fourier transform of

defined on the dual group
\widehat{\mathbf{Z}_{p}}
. A. Biró
[
1
]
and R. Meshulam
[

]
also independently established this lower bound on the sum of the supports of

and
\widehat{f}
.

Assume now that
G=\mathbb{Z}_{r}\times\mathbb{Z}_{m}
with
(r,m)=1
, and let
\widehat{G}
be its dual group. Since
\widehat{G}
is isomorphic to

we can also choose to view
\widehat{G}
as the group
\mathbb{Z}_{r}\times\mathbb{Z}_{m}
. For

we can now define both

and

to be the subset

.

Using a similar argument to that of Tao in
[

]

(see

Proposition 1
.

Let

be an odd prime. If

is a non-zero function then for at least one of

or

, we have

|\mathrm{supp}(f)\cap G_{i}|+|\mathrm{supp}(\widehat{f}\,)\cap\widehat{G}_{i}|\geq p+1.

and

Proposition 2
.

Let
p
and
r
be distinct odd primes, with
p
being primitive in
\mathbf{Z}_{r}
and
p>\Gamma_{r}
. If
f:G:=\mathbf{Z}_{r}\times\mathbf{Z}_{p}\to\mathbf{C}
is a non-zero function, then for at least one

, we have

|\mathrm{supp}(f)\cap G_{i}|+|\mathrm{supp}(\widehat{f})\cap\widehat{G}_{i}|\geq p+1.

2.
Preliminaries

We begin with a lemma whose proof relies on fundamental concepts from algebraic number theory, briefly outlined here. Assume that
K
is an algebraic number field of degree
[K:\mathbf{Q}]=n
with
K/\mathbf{Q}
a Galois extension. If
R
is the ring of algebraic integers of
K
then any prime
p\in\mathbf{Z}
satisfies

pR=\prod_{i=1}^{r}P_{i}^{e},

where
\{P_{i}\}
are the distinct prime ideals of
R
lying above the ideal
p\mathbf{Z}
, and
e:=e(P_{i}/p)
is the ramification index of
P_{i}
in
K/\mathbf{Q}
, which is the same for every
i
as the extension is Galois. Additionally, the residual degree
f_{i}:=f(P_{i}/p)
of
P_{i}
in
K/\mathbf{Q}
is the same constant
f
for each
i=1,\ldots,r
, and satisfies
f=[R/P_{i}:\mathbf{Z}/p\mathbf{Z}]
. Furthermore,

(4)

n=efr.

Both, the ramification index and the residual degree follow some transitivity rules, in the sense that if
L/K
is a Galois extension,
S
the ring of algebraic integers of
L
and
Q
a prime ideal of
S
above
P
then

\displaystyle f(Q/p)

\displaystyle=f(Q/P)\cdot f(P/p),

\displaystyle e(Q/p)

\displaystyle=e(Q/P)\cdot e(P/p).

The transitivity holds more general for separable extensions but we only need it here for Galois extensions. All the above, and much more, can be found in any book of Algebraic Number Theory, see for example Chapter 11 in
[
15
]
.

Assume now that
n=pm
with
m
coprime to
p
and let
K=\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{n})
be a cyclotomic field with
R=\mathbf{Z}[\zeta_{n}]
its ring of algebraic integers. In this special case the decomposition of
pR
into prime ideals in
R
is given as

pR=\prod_{i=1}^{r}P_{i}^{e}

where
e=\phi(p)=p-1
and
r=\frac{\phi(m)}{h}
with
h
being the order of
p
in the multiplicative group
\mathbf{Z}_{m}^{*}
. Furthermore,
f(P_{i}/p)=h
and the norm of the ideals
P_{i}
equals
N(P_{i})=p^{h}
, for all
i=1,\cdots,r
.
For the general theorem regarding the factorization of a rational prime
p
into prime ideals in the ring of integers
\mathbf{Z}[\zeta_{t}]
of a cyclotomic field
\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{t})
with
p\mid t
, see Theorems 8.7 and 8.8 in
[
12
]
.

We are now ready to prove our first lemma which generalizes Lemma 1 in
[
10
]
.

Lemma 3
.

Let
p
be an odd prime and
n=pm
for some integer
m
such that
(p,m)=1
. The ideal
\langle 1-\zeta_{p}\rangle=(1-\zeta_{p})\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_{n}]
is prime (and hence maximal) in
\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_{n}]
if and only if the order of
p
in
\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{*}
is
\phi(m)
and so
\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{*}
is cyclic and
p
is one of its generators. In this case, the quotient

\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_{n}]/\langle 1-\zeta_{p}\rangle=\mathbf{F}_{p^{\phi(m)}}

is a finite field of characteristic
p
and order
p^{\phi(m)}
. Moreover,
m
can only be one of
m=1,2,4
,
q^{k}
, or
2q^{k}
for some odd prime
q
and a positive integer
k
.

Proof.

Let
L=\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{n})
and
K=\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{p})
be cyclotomic fields, with
S=\mathbf{Z}[\zeta_{n}]
and
R=\mathbf{Z}[\zeta_{p}]
their ring of algebraic integers. Clearly
[L:\mathbf{Q}]=\phi(n)=\phi(p)\phi(m)
,
[K:\mathbf{Q}]=\phi(p)
and thus
[L:K]=\phi(m)
. Furthermore,
L=K(\zeta_{m})
is also a Galois extension of
K
of degree
\phi(m)
.

It is well known, see for example Section 11.3 Proposition N in
[
15
]
, that the ideal
P=(1-\zeta_{p})R
is a prime ideal of
R
, and it is the only ideal of
R
above
p\mathbf{Z}
. In addition

pR=P^{p-1}.

Hence the ramification index
e(P/p)
of
P
in
K/\mathbf{Q}
is
e(P/p)=p-1
and its residual degree
f(P/p)=1
.
Now, if
\{Q_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r}
is the set of prime ideals of
S
lying above
p
then

pS=\prod_{i=1}^{r}Q_{i}^{e(Q/p)}

where
Q=Q_{1}
,
e(Q/p)=p-1
and
r=\frac{\phi(m)}{h}
where
h
is the order of
p
in the multiplicative group
\mathbf{Z}_{m}^{*}
, by our preliminary remarks on the decomposition of a rational prime into prime ideals in cyclotomic fields. In addition,
f(Q/p)=h
and the norm of
Q_{i}
is
N(Q_{i})=p^{h}
.

Observe now that
\{Q_{i}\}_{i=1}^{r}
is exactly the set of prime ideals of
S
above
P
as well, while the extension
L/K
is also Galois, and thus

PS=\prod_{i=1}^{r}Q_{i}^{e(Q/P)}.

We conclude that the ideal
PS=(1-\zeta_{p})\mathbf{Z}[\zeta_{n}]
is prime in
S=\mathbf{Z}[\zeta_{n}]
if and only if
1=r=\frac{\phi(m)}{h}
. Hence the first part of the lemma follows.

By the transitivity of the ramification index we get

p-1=e(Q/p)=e(Q/P)\cdot e(P/p)=e(Q/P)\cdot(p-1).

Hence
e(Q/P)=1
.
Thus, in the case that

we get
PS=Q
and so

h=f(Q/p)=[S/Q:\mathbf{Z}/p\mathbf{Z}]=[\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_{n}]/\langle 1-\zeta_{p}\rangle:\mathbf{Z}_{p}].

We conclude that
\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_{n}]/\langle 1-\zeta_{p}\rangle
is a finite field of order
p^{h}=p^{\phi(m)}
.

The final part of the lemma states the well-known fact, first proved by Gauss, that the only integers

for which
\mathbf{Z}_{m}^{*}
is cyclic are
1,2,4,q^{k}
, or
2q^{k}
for some odd prime
q
and integer

.
∎

The above lemma easily implies:

Lemma 4
.

Assume
p,r
are distinct odd primes such that
p
is primitive in
\mathbf{Z}_{r}
.
Then

\mathbf{Z}[\zeta_{pr}]/\langle 1-\zeta_{p}\rangle=\mathbf{F}_{p^{r-1}}

and the image
\bar{\zeta_{r}}
of
\zeta_{r}\in\mathbf{Z}[\zeta_{pr}]
in
\mathbf{Z}[\zeta_{pr}]/\langle 1-\zeta_{p}\rangle
is also a primitive
r
-th root of unity in the field
\mathbf{Z}[\zeta_{pr}]/\langle 1-\zeta_{p}\rangle
.

Proof.

We only need to show that
\bar{\zeta_{r}}
has order
r
in
\mathbf{Z}[\zeta_{pr}]/\langle 1-\zeta_{p}\rangle
.
Clearly
\bar{\zeta_{r}}^{r}=\bar{1}
and suppose that

\bar{\zeta_{r}}^{k}=\bar{1}
for some
0<k<r
.
Hence
\zeta_{r}^{k}-1\equiv 0\pmod{(1-\zeta_{p})}
and so

(1-\zeta_{p})\mid(\zeta_{r}^{k}-1)
in
Z[\zeta_{pr}]
.
If
L=\mathbf{Q}[\zeta_{pr}]
,
M=\mathbf{Q}[\zeta_{r}]
and
K=\mathbf{Q}[\zeta_{p}]
then
[L:M]=p-1
and
[L:K]=r-1
. Additionally, the norm
N_{L/Q}(1-\zeta_{p})=p^{r-1}
by
3
, or we can compute it directly as follows:

N_{L/Q}(1-\zeta_{p})=N_{L/K}(N_{K/Q}(1-\zeta_{p}))=N_{L/K}\left(\prod_{i=0}^{p-1}(1-\zeta_{p}^{i})\right)=N_{L/K}(\Phi_{p}(1))=N_{L/K}(p)=p^{r-1}.

For
0<k<r
, as

ranges from
0
to
r-1
, the set
\{\zeta_{r}^{ik}\}
covers all primitive
r
-th roots of unity. Hence

N_{L/Q}(1-\zeta_{r}^{k})=N_{L/M}(N_{M/Q}(1-\zeta_{r}^{k}))=N_{L/M}\left(\prod_{i=0}^{r-1}(1-\zeta_{r}^{ik})\right)=N_{L/M}(\Phi_{r}(1))=N_{L/M}(r)=r^{p-1}.

If
(1-\zeta_{p})\mid(\zeta_{r}^{k}-1)
in
\mathbf{Z}[\zeta_{pr}]
we should have

p^{r-1}=N_{L/Q}(1-\zeta_{p})\,\big|\,N_{L/Q}(\zeta_{r}^{k}-1)=r^{p-1}.

But this last division occurs in
\mathbf{Z}
, leading to an obvious contradiction and thus completing the proof of the lemma.
∎

We conclude the preliminaries of Algebraic Number Theory with two remarks.

Remark 1
.

For every odd prime
p
, the element
1-\zeta_{p}
in
\mathbf{Z}(\zeta_{p})
does not divide
2
in
\mathbf{Z}(\zeta_{p})
. If it did, then
N_{\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{p})/\mathbf{Q}}(1-\zeta_{p})
should divide
N_{\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{p})/\mathbf{Q}}(2)
in
\mathbf{Z}
, leading to a contradiction since
N_{\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{p})/\mathbf{Q}}(1-\zeta_{p})=p
and
N_{\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{p})/\mathbf{Q}}(2)=2^{p-1}
.

Remark 2
.

Let
D
be a Dedekind Domain and
P=\langle a\rangle\neq 0
a prime principal ideal of

. For any non-zero element

, there exists an integer

so that

divides

. This follows from the unique factorization of the ideal
\langle d\rangle
into a product of finite powers of prime ideals; that is,

if and only if

is a factor in the prime factorization of
\langle d\rangle
and thus

.

For any ring

we denote by

.
The following is Lemma 2 in
[

]
and for completeness we include its proof here.

Lemma 5

(Frenkel)

Let
\mathbf{F}
be a finite field of characteristic

be a polynomial of degree
<p
. If
0\neq a\in\mathbf{F}
is a root of
g(x)
with multiplicity
t
then

t<|\mathrm{Supp}(g(x))|.

Proof.

We induct on the degree of
g(x)
, with the base case, that of constant polynomials being trivially true.
Assume now the lemma holds for all polynomials of degree
<k
, for some fixed
1\leq k<p
, and take
g(x)
of degree
k
. If
g(0)=0
, then
|\mathrm{Supp}(g(x))|=|\mathrm{Supp}(g(x)/x)|
and
a
is also a root of
g(x)/x
with multiplicity
t
. By induction the lemma holds for
g(x)/x
and thus for
g(x)
. So we may assume that
g(0)\neq 0
. Then
|\mathrm{Supp}(g^{\prime}(x))|=|\mathrm{Supp}(g(x))|-1
and
a
is a root of
g^{\prime}(x)
with multiplicity
\geq t-1
.
Because
g(x)
is of positive degree
k<p
, its derivative
g^{\prime}(x)\neq 0
. So the lemma holds for
g^{\prime}(x)
and therefore also for
g(x)
.
∎

The case

Throughout this section

Proof of Theorem

The theorem is equivalent to saying that if numbers

and thus

is some

that is not divisible by

.
Thus we obtain the polynomial

(5)

r(x)=\sum_{j\in J}z_{j}x^{j}\in\mathbf{Z}[\zeta_{p}][x]

which vanishes at
\zeta_{n}^{i}
for all

1-\zeta_{p}
.

Using the isomorphism and notation from (

) and (

, we get
\zeta_{2p}^{i}=\zeta_{p}^{i_{p}}\cdot(-1)^{i_{2}}
,
for every

Next we define a polynomial in two variables

g(x,y)=\sum_{j\in J}z_{j}x^{j_{p}}y^{j_{2}}\in\mathbf{Z}[\zeta_{p}][x,y].

We clearly have

g(\zeta_{p}^{2i_{p}},(-1)^{i_{2}})=\sum_{j\in J}z_{j}\zeta_{p}^{2i_{p}j_{p}}(-1)^{i_{2}j_{2}}=\sum_{j\in J}z_{j}\zeta_{n}^{ij}=0

for all

.

Consider the sets

for

.

Case 1.
Assume first that

and thus

. Consequently, we have

and

. Thus the polynomial

has roots

, for all

. So

is divisible by

. Observe that all elements

of

in

Case 2.

nor

is an empty set.

Consider the polynomials

(9)

\displaystyle T_{0}(x)

\displaystyle:=g(x,1)=\sum_{j\in J}z_{j}x^{j_{p}}=S_{0}(x)+S_{1}(x)

(10)

\displaystyle T_{1}(x)

\displaystyle:=g(x,-1)=\sum_{j\in J}z_{j}x^{j_{p}}(-1)^{j_{2}}=S_{0}(x)-S_{1}(x),

where

and

.
In view of equation (
7

T_{0}(\zeta_{p}^{2i_{p}})=0
for all

,
and similarly

for all

\prod_{i\in I_{2}^{1}}(x-\zeta_{p}^{2i_{p}})
divides

(11)

\displaystyle(x-\bar{1})^{|I_{2}^{0}|}\,\,

\displaystyle(x-\bar{1})^{|I_{2}^{1}|}\,\,

4.
The case
n=pr

We begin this section with the definition of
\Gamma_{r}
as provided in Section 3 of
[
18
]
; interested readers can refer to
[
18
]
for further details and motivation.
Denote by

the determinant of the

Vandermonde matrix whose

-entry is given as

, for

. So,

V_{n}(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})=\prod_{1\leq i\leq j\leq n}(x_{i}-x_{j}).

For any odd prime

and any fixed

Note that

grows at least exponentially on

. If we choose

and work with

Nevertheless, as it was already noted in
[

(Remark 3.3) for every prime

Proof of Theorem

As in the case of

z_{j}\in\mathbf{Q}(\zeta_{n})
for

\langle 1-\zeta_{p}\rangle

\mathbf{Z}[\zeta_{n}]

(13)

r(x)=\sum_{j\in J}z_{j}x^{j}\in\mathbf{Z}[\zeta_{n}][x]

\zeta_{n}^{i}

Using the notation from (

(14)

g(x,y)=\sum_{j\in J}z_{j}x^{j_{p}}y^{j_{r}}\in\mathbf{Z}[\zeta_{n}][x,y].

We clearly have

(15)

g(\zeta_{p}^{ri_{p}},\zeta_{r}^{pi_{r}})=\sum_{j\in J}z_{j}\zeta_{p}^{ri_{p}j_{p}}\zeta_{r}^{pi_{r}j_{r}}=\sum_{j\in J}z_{j}\zeta_{n}^{ij}=0

Consider the sets

(16)

5.
An uncertainty principle

In this section we give a generalization of Tao’s uncertainty principle. His proof (see
[
17
]
)
was based on the following observation: Assume that
G=\mathbf{Z}_{n}
is a cyclic group of order
n
and let

f:G\to\mathbf{C}
be a non-zero function.
If
|\mathrm{supp}(f)|+|\mathrm{supp}(\widehat{f}\,)|\leq n
, then

|\mathrm{supp}(f)|\leq n-|\mathrm{supp}(\widehat{f}\,)|=|\{\lambda\in\widehat{G}\mid\widehat{f}(\lambda)=0\}|.

Thus, if
A=\mathrm{supp}(f)\subseteq G=\mathbf{Z}_{p}
, there exists a subset
B\subseteq\widehat{G}=\mathbf{Z}_{p}

such that
|A|=|B|
and
\widehat{f}(b)=0
for all
b\in B
. In particular, the linear map
T:l^{2}(A)\to l^{2}(B)
defined by
T:g\to\widehat{g}|_{B}
is singular as
T(f)=0
and
f\neq 0

(by
l^{2}(A)
is denoted the set of functions
g:G\to\mathbf{C}
which are

0
out of
A
).
Observe now that the matrix of
T
is precisely the submatrix
(\zeta_{n}^{ji})_{i\in A,j\in B}
of
(\zeta_{n}^{kl})_{0\leq k,l\leq n-1}
.
Using the same observation we can show
Theorem 6
below.

(The notation used is as in the introduction. In particular we write any element of
\mathbf{Z}_{r}\times\mathbf{Z}_{m}
as an integer modulo
rm
using the isomorphism
\mathbf{Z}_{r}\times\mathbf{Z}_{m}\cong\mathbf{Z}_{rm}
that maps any vector
(i_{r},i_{m})
to
i\equiv i_{r}m+i_{m}r\pmod{rm}
and we write
G_{i}=\{i\}\times\mathbf{Z}_{m}
, for all
i=0,\cdots,r-1
.)

Theorem 6
.

Let
r,m
be positive integers with
(r,m)=1
.

(a)

Suppose
f:G=\mathbf{Z}_{r}\times\mathbf{Z}_{m}\to\mathbf{C}
is a non-zero function satisfying

(24)

|\mathrm{supp}(f)\cap G_{k}|+|\mathrm{supp}(\widehat{f}\,)\cap\widehat{G}_{k}|\leq m,\ \text{ for all }k=0,1,\cdots,r-1.

If the sets
I\subseteq G
,
J\subseteq\widehat{G}
satisfy

(25)

|I\cap G_{k}|=|J\cap\widehat{G}_{k}|,\ \text{ for all }k=0,1,\cdots,r-1,

and
\mathrm{supp}(f)\subseteq I
,
\widehat{f}|_{J}=0
then the matrix
(\zeta_{rm}^{ji})_{i\in I,j\in J}
is singular. There is at least one such pair
I\subseteq G
,
J\subseteq\widehat{G}
, for each such
f
.

(b)

Conversely, if
I\subseteq G=\mathbf{Z}_{r}\times\mathbf{Z}_{m}
and
J\subseteq\widehat{G}=\mathbf{Z}_{r}\times\mathbf{Z}_{m}
satisfy (
25
)
and the matrix
(\zeta_{rm}^{ji})_{i\in I,j\in J}
is singular then there exists
f:G=\mathbf{Z}_{r}\times\mathbf{Z}_{m}\to\mathbf{C}
with
\mathrm{supp}(f)\subseteq I
and
\widehat{f}|_{J}=0
satisfying (
24
).

Observe that if
r=1
and
m=p
in
Theorem 6
we get the cyclic
p
-group
G=\{1\}\times\mathbf{Z}_{p}
, for which Chebotarev’s theorem holds (i.e. all the square submatrices
(\zeta_{p}^{ij})
are nonsingular), and thus we recover Tao’s uncertainty principle.

Proof.

(a)
Assume first that
f:G=\mathbf{Z}_{r}\times\mathbf{Z}_{m}\to\mathbf{C}
is a non-zero function satisfying (
24
). If
I,J
satisfy the hypothesis in (a), then the matrix
(\zeta_{rm}^{ji})_{i\in I,j\in J}
defines a linear map
T:l^{2}(I)\to l^{2}(J)
that maps any
g\in l^{2}(I)
to
\widehat{g}|_{J}
. Since
T(f)=\widehat{f}|_{J}=0
for the non-zero function
f
(as
\mathrm{supp}(f)\subseteq I
), the matrix
(\zeta_{rm}^{ji})_{i\in I,j\in J}
is singular.

To complete the proof of (a) it remains to show that, for the given
f
, such a pair
I,J

really exists. For every
k=0,1,\cdots,r-1
, define
I_{k}=\mathrm{supp}(f)\cap G_{k}
. The fact that

|\mathrm{supp}(f)\cap G_{k}|+|\mathrm{supp}(\widehat{f}\,)\cap\widehat{G}_{k}|\leq m

implies that there exist set
J_{k}\subseteq\widehat{G}_{k}
with
|I_{k}|=|J_{k}|
such that
\widehat{f}(b)=0
for all
b\in J_{k}
. Let
I=\bigcup_{k=0}^{r-1}I_{k}
and
J=\bigcup_{k=0}^{r-1}J_{k}
. Clearly
I=\mathrm{supp}{f}
,
\widehat{f}|_{J}=0
and
I,J
satisfy (
25
).

(b) Conversely, suppose
(\zeta_{rm}^{ji})_{i\in I,j\in J}
is a singular matrix, where
I\subseteq G
and
J\subseteq\widehat{G}
satisfy (
25
). The singularity of the matrix implies that the corresponding linear map
T
maps a nonzero function to zero; that is, there exists a nonzero function
f:\mathbf{Z}_{r}\times\mathbf{Z}_{m}\to\mathbf{C}
such that
\mathrm{supp}(f)\subseteq I
and
T(f)=\widehat{f}|_{J}=0
.
Let
k\in\{0,1,\cdots,r-1\}
, then

|\mathrm{supp}(f)\cap G_{k}|\leq|I\cap G_{k}|,

since
\mathrm{supp}(f)\subseteq I
. Also

|\mathrm{supp}(\widehat{f})\cap\widehat{G}_{k}|\leq m-|J\cap G_{k}|=m-|I\cap G_{k}|,

where the first inequality follows from the fact that
|\widehat{G}_{k}|=m
and
\widehat{f}|_{J}=0
, while the last equality follows by hypothesis.
Adding the above we get
|\mathrm{supp}(f)\cap G_{k}|+|\mathrm{supp}(\widehat{f}\,)\cap\widehat{G}_{k}|\leq m
for all
k=0,1,\cdots,r-1
.

This completes the proof of the theorem.

6.
Complementary matrices

Let
A
be an
n\times n
matrix and write
[n]
for the set
\{1,2,\cdots n\}
. For every
I\subseteq[n]
we denote by
I^{c}
the complementary subset
[n]\setminus I
. If
I,J\subseteq[n]
with
|I|=|J|
we write
A_{I,J}
for the
|I|\times|I|
submatrix of
A
obtained from
A
by removing all rows whose indices do not belong to
I
and all columns whose indices do not belong to
J
. Observe that

A_{I,J}=A^{t}_{J,I}
for all
I,J
. If
I=J
we simply write
A_{I}
and this is a principal submatrix of
A
.

It is known, that a principal submatrix of a matrix
A
is non-singular if and only if its complementary principal submatrix is also non-singular(see for example Proposition 5.4 in
[
2
]
for a more general result). We present here a simple proof of this fact based on
Jacobi’s complementary minor theorem, which states:

Theorem 7

(Jacobi)
.

Assume
A
is an invertible
n\times n
matrix over a field
K
and let
I,J\subseteq[n]
with
|I|=|J|
. Then

\det(A_{I,J})=(-1)^{\sum I+\sum J}\cdot\det A\cdot\det((A^{-1})_{J^{c},I^{c}}).

With the use of the adjoint
\adj A
of A we have
A^{-1}=\frac{1}{\det A}\cdot\adj A
and so

(A^{-1})_{J^{c},I^{c}}=\frac{1}{\det A}\cdot(\adj A)_{J^{c},I^{c}}=\frac{1}{\det A}\cdot((\adj A)^{t})_{I^{c},J^{c}}

Hence Jacobi’s formula is translated to

(26)

\det(A_{I,J})=(-1)^{\sum I+\sum J}\cdot\frac{\det(A)}{\det(A)^{|I^{c}|}}\cdot\det(((\adj A)^{t})_{I^{c},J^{c}}).

We can now prove

Proposition 3
.

Let
A=(\omega^{k,l})_{0\leq k,l\leq n-1}
where
\omega
a primitive
n
-th root of unity. For any
I,J\subseteq[n]
with
|I|=|J|
the submatrix
A_{I,J}
is invertible if and only if
A_{I^{c},J^{c}}
is invertible. In particular, for
I=J
we have that the principal minor
\det{A_{I}}
is non zero if and only if the principal minor
\det{A_{I^{c}}}
is non zero.

Proof.

The matrix
A
is the character table of the cyclic group
C_{n}
of order
n
and as such it is invertible and satisfies

A\cdot\bar{A}^{t}=n\cdot I_{n}.

This along with the formula for the adjoint
\adj A
of
A
implies that
\det(A)\cdot\bar{A}=n\cdot(\adj A)^{t}
and thus

det(A)\cdot(\bar{A})_{I^{c},J^{c}}=n\cdot((\adj A)^{t})_{I^{c},J^{c}}

for any
I,J\subseteq[n]
with
|I|=|J|
. If
k=n-|I|
, taking determinants to the above equation we get

(27)

(\det(A))^{k}\cdot\det((\bar{A})_{I^{c},J^{c}})=n^{k}\cdot\det(((\adj A)^{t})_{I^{c},J^{c}})

Clearly
\det((\bar{A})_{I^{c},J^{c}})=\overline{\det(A_{I^{c},J^{c}})}
and we substitute to Jacobi’s formula (
26
) to get

\det(A_{I,J})=(-1)^{\sum I+\sum J}\cdot\frac{\det(A)}{(\det(A))^{k}}\cdot\frac{(\det(A))^{k}}{n^{k}}\cdot\overline{\det(A_{I^{c},J^{c}})}=(-1)^{\sum I+\sum J}\cdot\frac{\det(A)}{n^{k}}\cdot\overline{\det(A_{I^{c},J^{c}})}.

Hence
\det(A_{I,J})\neq 0
if and only if
\det(A_{I^{c},J^{c}}))\neq 0
and the proposition follows.

∎
