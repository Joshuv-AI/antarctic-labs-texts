What does it mean for a 3D star-shaped scatterer to be small in the time domain?
M. Kachanovska, A. Savchuk

Abstract
In the frequency domain wave scattering problems, obstacles can be effectively replaced by point scatterers as soon as the wavelength of the incident wave exceeds significantly their diameter. The situation is less clear in the time domain, where recent works suggest the presence of an additional temporal scale that quantifies the smallness of the obstacle. In this paper we argue that this is not necessarily the case, and that it is possible to construct asymptotic models with an error that does not deteriorate in time, at least in the case of a sound-soft scattering problem by a star-shaped obstacle in 3D.



1
Introduction and problem setting

1.1
Introduction

This work is motivated by a question and analysis posed in the recent paper by P. Martin
[
21
]
: when considering wave scattering by a particle in the time domain, what does it mean for a particle to be ’small’? Limiting the discussion to the three-dimensional case, it is well-known that in the frequency domain, for the problem of wave scattering of a wave of wavelength
\lambda
by a particle of characteristic size
\varepsilon
, one can effectively replace the particle by a point scatterer as soon as
\varepsilon\ll\lambda
, cf.
[
20
, Section 8.1]
. Under these conditions, one can expect the asymptotic model to produce an absolute error in the scattered far-field of
O(\varepsilon(\varepsilon/\lambda)^{p})
, for some
p\geq 0
, with hidden constants independent of
\lambda
and
\varepsilon
. Since the magnitude of the scattered field is
O(\varepsilon)
, a particle is ’small’ if
\varepsilon\ll\lambda
.

In the time-domain, there are two parameters: the smallest wavelength
\lambda_{min}
of the approximately band-limited signal and the final simulation time
T
. In other words, one can expect that the absolute
L^{\infty}(0,T)
-error of an asymptotic model behaves as
O(\varepsilon(\varepsilon/\lambda_{min})^{p}T^{q})
, where
q>0
, see e.g.
[
11
]
. Although in many works, cf.
[
30
]
for the Foldy-Lax model for scattering by a cluster of small particles,
[
3
]
for scattering by a small sphere,
[
22
]
by a small particle in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, or
[
13
]
by a small particle inside a bounded domain, the estimates are stated without explicit dependence in the final time, from the proofs of these estimates one can deduce the degeneration of the error bounds with time as
t\rightarrow+\infty
.

Considering a more general class of asymptotic time-dependent problems, the estimates of the above type were also obtained in the context of Minnaert-type resonances in
[
17
, Theorem 1.1]
,
[
19
, Theorem 5.3]
. An alternative approach is taken in the paper
[
2
]
modelling plasmonic resonances. There, the approximations are based on modal expansions; in this case an extra scale is introduced by an abrupt truncation of the Fourier-transformed signal in frequency. The error estimates are then based on an interplay between the simulation time, size of the particle and the cut-off frequency.

Importantly, all these estimates seem to indicate that one has to assign an additional temporal scale to define the ’smallness’ of the particle. In other words, we expect the particle to be small if
\varepsilon\ll\lambda T^{-\alpha}
, for some
\alpha>0
, where
T
is a final simulation time.

In this work we concentrate on sound-soft scattering by a small particle, and prove that such a deterioration of the error in time is not intrinsic to asymptotic models that replace a particle by a point scatterer, and that it is possible to construct a model with an error not deteriorating in time, at least in the situation of a single scatterer. The mathematical meaning of ’not deteriorating in time’ will be explained in the course of the article. The reason for this is that, roughly speaking, in 3D, the local energy of the scattered field decays fast, and constructing an accurate asymptotic model mimicking such a behaviour is not a difficult task. We provide quantitative bounds on the local energy of the field scattered by a small particle and the error of the field when approximating the particle by a point source valid for long times.

1.2
Problem setting

Let
\Omega
be a smooth domain in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
\Omega^{c}:=\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
its exterior. We assume that
\Omega\subseteq B_{1}(\boldsymbol{0})
, which implies that
\operatorname{diam}\Omega\leq 1
. Additionally, let
\Omega^{c}
be connected. Without loss of generality, let
\boldsymbol{0}\in\Omega
. The family of the domains
\Omega^{\varepsilon}
is defined by applying a contraction to
\Omega
:

\Omega^{\varepsilon}:=\{\boldsymbol{x}^{\varepsilon}=\varepsilon\boldsymbol{x},\;\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega\},\quad\Gamma^{\varepsilon}:=\partial\Omega^{\varepsilon},\,0<\varepsilon\leq 1.

We study the wave equation, posed in the exterior
\Omega^{\varepsilon,c}=\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\overline{\Omega^{\varepsilon}}
, more precisely, we look for
u^{\varepsilon},\,v^{\varepsilon}:\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\times\Omega^{\varepsilon,c}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, s.t.

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\partial_{t}u^{\varepsilon}-v^{\varepsilon}=0,\qquad\partial_{t}v^{\varepsilon}-\Delta u^{\varepsilon}=0\text{ in }\mathbb{R}_{>0}\times\Omega^{\varepsilon,c},\\
&\gamma_{0}^{\varepsilon}u^{\varepsilon}\equiv\left.u^{\varepsilon}\right|_{\Gamma^{\varepsilon}}=0,\quad u^{\varepsilon}(0,\boldsymbol{x})=u_{0}(\boldsymbol{x}),\quad v^{\varepsilon}(0,\boldsymbol{x})=v_{0}(\boldsymbol{x}).\end{split}

(1)

The initial data
u_{0},\,v_{0}
is sufficiently regular, defined in all of
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, but supported away from the scatterer.
More precisely, let
R_{0}>r_{0}>1
, and
\mathcal{O}_{0}:=B_{R_{0}}(0)\setminus\overline{B_{r_{0}}(0)}
be a spherical shell enclosing
\overline{\Omega}
. We assume that, with a regularity exponent
k_{reg}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

\displaystyle v_{0}\in H^{k_{reg}}(\mathbb{R}^{3}),\quad u_{0}\in H^{k_{reg}+1}(\mathbb{R}^{3}),\quad\operatorname{supp}u_{0},\;\operatorname{supp}v_{0}\subset\mathcal{O}_{0}.

(2)

The above implies in particular that
\left.u_{0}\right|_{\Omega^{\varepsilon,c}}\in H^{k_{reg}+1}_{0}(\Omega^{\varepsilon,c})
.

In what follows, we will employ the notation
\boldsymbol{u}^{\varepsilon}:=\left(\begin{matrix}u^{\varepsilon}\\
v^{\varepsilon}\end{matrix}\right)
,
\boldsymbol{u}_{0}:=\left(\begin{matrix}u_{0}\\
v_{0}\end{matrix}\right)
.
We have the following energy identity, obtained by testing the first equation in (
1
) by
\Delta u^{\varepsilon}
, the second equation by
v^{\varepsilon}
, and next integrating by parts:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}{E}^{\varepsilon}=0,\text{ where }{E}^{\varepsilon}(t):=\frac{1}{2}\left(\|\nabla u^{\varepsilon}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega^{\varepsilon,c})}+\|v^{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega^{\varepsilon,c})}^{2}\right).

Thus, the total energy remains constant. On the other hand, the local energy

\displaystyle{E}^{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}^{\varepsilon}(t);K):=\frac{1}{2}\left(\|\nabla u^{\varepsilon}(t)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(K)}+\|v^{\varepsilon}(t)\|_{L^{2}(K)}^{2}\right)

defined on a
bounded
set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
is known to behave differently. From the deep results of
[
4
]
, it follows that for sufficiently regular initial data
{E}^{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}^{\varepsilon}(t);K)
decays as
t\rightarrow+\infty
, with a rate of decay being defined by the geometry of the obstacle and the regularity of the data. In particular, when in (
2
)
k_{reg}=1
, it holds that

{E}^{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}^{\varepsilon}(t);K)\leq C(K;\Omega^{\varepsilon})\log^{-1}t.

This estimate can be improved for star-shaped obstacles
[
26
,
16
,
23
]
to
{E}^{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}^{\varepsilon}(t);K)\leq C(K;\Omega^{\varepsilon})\mathrm{e}^{-c_{\varepsilon}t}.

In all these cases the dependence of the estimates on the parameter
\varepsilon
does not seem to be explicit. On the other hand, it is expected that when
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
, the field
\boldsymbol{u}^{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{x},t)
should approach the solution
\boldsymbol{u}^{inc}
of the initial-value problem (
1
) posed in the space
\mathbb{R}^{3}
(i.e. the obstacle ’disappears’), for which the strong Huygens principle holds true: the local energy
E^{\varepsilon}(\boldsymbol{u}^{inc}(t);K)=0
for
t>t_{K}>0
. Thus, the effect of the obstacle on the time-dependence of the field
\boldsymbol{u}^{\varepsilon}
can be measured by studying the scattered field, namely the difference between the solution of the Cauchy-problem in the free space and the solution to (
1
). We formalize it below.

First, let
\boldsymbol{u}^{inc}:\,\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a solution to the free space wave equation:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\partial_{t}u^{inc}-v^{inc}=0,\qquad\partial_{t}v^{inc}-\Delta u^{inc}=0\text{ in }\mathbb{R}_{>0}\times\mathbb{R}^{3},\\
&u^{inc}(0,\boldsymbol{x})=u_{0},\quad v^{inc}(0,\boldsymbol{x})=v_{0}.\end{split}

(3)

The scattered field
\boldsymbol{u}_{sc}^{\varepsilon}:=\boldsymbol{u}^{\varepsilon}-\left.\boldsymbol{u}^{inc}\right|_{\Omega^{\varepsilon,c}}
then solves the boundary-value problem:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\partial_{t}u^{\varepsilon}_{sc}-v^{\varepsilon}_{sc}=0,\qquad\partial_{t}v^{\varepsilon}_{sc}-\Delta u^{\varepsilon}_{sc}=0\text{ in }\mathbb{R}_{>0}\times\Omega^{\varepsilon,c},\\
&\gamma_{0}^{\varepsilon}u^{\varepsilon}_{sc}=-\gamma_{0}^{\varepsilon}u^{inc},\qquad u^{\varepsilon}_{sc}(0,\boldsymbol{x})=0,\quad v^{\varepsilon}_{sc}(0,\boldsymbol{x})=0.\end{split}

(4)

Questions studied in the paper

In this manuscript we will restrict our attention (see Remark
1
) to a sub-class of obstacles satisfying a so-called non-trapping assumption, see Proposition 13.3.1 in

.

Assumption 1
.

\Omega

\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\in\Omega

-norm of the solution

(i.e. measured locally) depend on

and

1
. Remark that in what follows we concentrate solely on one component

of the field

Recent research in the field of time-domain asymptotic methods has given rise to new models, cf. e.g.
[
30
,
11
,
3
,
22
]
, for the problem (
1
). Such models allow to simplify the computations significantly, cf.
[

, by incorporating important information about the dynamics of the problem (

4
.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we describe a strategy to estimate the local energy decay with the help of the resolvent estimates in the Laplace domain. Next we obtain a suitable estimate on the resolvent; the key ingredients are existing estimates in the
\varepsilon

3

2
Local energy decay estimates

Let us define the energy of the initial data, which quantifies the regularity of the incident field:

\displaystyle E_{0}^{k_{reg}}:=\frac{1}{2}\left(\|u_{0}\|_{H^{k_{reg}+1}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{2}+\|v_{0}\|_{H^{k_{reg}}(\mathbb{R}^{3})}^{2}\right).

The key result of this section reads.

Theorem 1
.

Assume that
k_{reg}\geq 6
.
There exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
, s.t. for all
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon_{0}
, for the problem described in Section
1.2
, the following energy decay bounds on the scattered field hold true. With
\gamma_{\Omega}>0
that depends on the obstacle only, the scattered field measured in the far-field region
K_{\operatorname{ff}}
satisfies, with
t=R_{0}+R_{\operatorname{ff}}+\tau
,

\displaystyle\|u^{\varepsilon}_{sc}(t)\|_{L^{2}(K_{\operatorname{ff}})}\leq{C}_{\operatorname{ff}}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\varepsilon\sqrt{E_{0}^{k_{reg}}},&\tau\leq 0,\\
\varepsilon^{k_{reg}-1}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\gamma_{\Omega}}{\varepsilon}\tau}\sqrt{E_{0}^{k_{reg}}},&\tau>0,\end{array}\right.

where the constant
C_{\operatorname{ff}}
depends on
R_{\operatorname{ff}}
,
r_{\operatorname{ff}}
,
R_{0}
,
r_{0}
,
k_{reg}
,
\gamma_{\Omega}
, but is independent of
\varepsilon
,
t
.

Let us comment on the above result. For small times, the scattered field behaves as
O(\varepsilon)
, which is consistent with the known asymptotic frequency-domain behaviour
[
20
, Section 8.2]
. Here is what happens for longer times. The wave emitted by the source
\boldsymbol{u}_{0}
located in
\mathcal{O}_{0}=B_{R_{0}}\setminus\overline{B_{r_{0}}}
, reaches the boundary of the small obstacle at the time
t\approx R_{0}
(remark that the speed of the wave propagation is
1
), next gets reflected and reaches the farthest observation point on
\partial B(\boldsymbol{0},R_{\operatorname{ff}})
at
t_{*}=R_{0}+R_{\operatorname{ff}}+O(\varepsilon)
, where
O(\varepsilon)
accounts for the size of the obstacle, and finally decays exponentially fast with
(t-t_{*})
. The decay rate depends on the obstacle size, and is more pronounced as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
. In other words, the behaviour is getting close to the free space case, where the wave should vanish after this point in time due to the Huygens principle. To understand the exponential decay rate stated in the theorem, we remark that
\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\gamma_{\Omega}}{\varepsilon}(t+\frac{1}{\gamma_{\Omega}}m\varepsilon\log\varepsilon)}=\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\gamma_{\Omega}}{\varepsilon}(t+\frac{1}{\gamma_{\Omega}}m\varepsilon\log\varepsilon^{-1})}=\varepsilon^{m}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\gamma_{\Omega}}{\varepsilon}t}
, in other words, after the time
m\varepsilon\log\varepsilon^{-1}
the wave becomes by a factor of
O(\varepsilon^{m})
smaller. An interesting by-product of the analysis is the effect of the regularity of the field on the subsequent behaviour of the wave, which shows that higher regularity fields seem to have a significantly smaller amplitude after
t_{*}
than fields of a lower regularity.

Remark 1
.

Up to the best of the authors’ knowledge, in the literature most estimates of such type are obtained using either of the two approaches: based on the Morawetz estimates
[
25
,
26
,
24
,
14
,
15
]
(for star-shaped obstacles), or by Laplace contour deformation techniques in the spirit of
[
4
]
, that, however, rely on the use of bounds on the cut-off resolvent of the underlying operator. Obtaining such bounds in the case when scatterers are ’trapping’ relies on microlocal/semi-classical analysis techniques, and optimal estimates depend on the region
K
where the scattered wave is measured, see
[
5
]
. The effect of trapping can be shown to be less pronounced in the far-field then in the near-field. We do not believe that quite elementary techniques used in this paper allow to account properly for such subtle effects in this case, but think that our bounds are close to optimal in the non-trapping case of Assumption
1
.

2.1
Strategy of the proof of Theorem
1
and auxiliary notation

We follow the following approach as used e.g. in
[
4
]
. We analyze the analyticity properties of the Laplace transform of the scattered field and exploit them in a deformation of the inverse Laplace integral, to obtain close to optimal in the time domain estimates. These analyticity properties depend in particular on the geometry of the domain
\Omega^{\varepsilon}
.

2.1.1
Representation formula

Let us define a family of operators on the space
V^{\varepsilon}:=H^{1}_{0}(\Omega^{\varepsilon,c})\times L^{2}(\Omega^{\varepsilon,c})

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}^{\varepsilon}:=\left(\begin{matrix}0&\operatorname{Id}\\

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\boldsymbol{u}^{\varepsilon}-\mathbb{B}^{\varepsilon}\boldsymbol{u}^{\varepsilon}=0,\quad t>0,\quad\boldsymbol{u}^{\varepsilon}(0)=\left.\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\right|_{\Omega^{\varepsilon,c}}.

Applying the Laplace transform, defined as follows for

\displaystyle\hat{\varphi}(s):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-st}\varphi(t)dt,\quad\Re s\geq 0,

\displaystyle(s-\mathbb{B}^{\varepsilon})\hat{\boldsymbol{u}}^{\varepsilon}=\left.\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\right|_{\Omega^{\varepsilon,c}}.

Our starting point is the following representation formula for the solution of (
1

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}^{\varepsilon}(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{i\mathbb{R}+\mu}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{st}}{(\mu_{*}-s)^{p}}(s-\mathbb{B}^{\varepsilon})^{-1}(\mu_{*}-\mathbb{B}^{\varepsilon})^{p}\left.\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\right|_{\Omega^{\varepsilon,c}}ds,\quad 0<\mu<\mu_{*}

(5)

We would like to rewrite this representation formula for the scattered field. We will start with the incident field. Let us introduce a family of operators on the space which we denote with an abuse of notation by
V^{0}:=H^{1}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{3})\times L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}:=\left(\begin{matrix}0&\operatorname{Id}\\

Then the incident field can be written analogously to (

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{u}^{inc}(t)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{i\mathbb{R}+\mu}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{st}}{(\mu_{*}-s)^{p}}(s-\mathbb{B})^{-1}(\mu_{*}-\mathbb{B})^{p}\boldsymbol{u}_{0}ds,\quad 0<\mu<\mu_{*},\quad p>1.

Similarly, let

Let us introduce the notation:

\displaystyle\chi_{r,R}:=\chi_{R}(1-\chi_{r/2}),\quad\chi_{0,R}:=\chi_{R},

so that

and vanishes for

•

using these results, we will show that the integration contour (

In view of the above discussion, let us define the following operator, for

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\varepsilon}_{D}(s):=\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}_{D}(s)-\mathcal{R}_{0}^{\varepsilon}(s),\quad\mathcal{D}^{\varepsilon}_{D}(s):\,L^{2}(\Omega^{\varepsilon,c})\rightarrow L^{2}(\Omega^{\varepsilon,c}).

Our goal now is to obtain estimates on

explicit in
\varepsilon
, in certain operator norms. One could have argued that applying a simple scaling argument

2.2
A Krein-like resolvent formula for
\mathcal{D}^{\varepsilon}_{D}

Let
s\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
,
f\in L^{2}(\Omega^{\varepsilon,c}),\;p^{\varepsilon}:=\mathcal{D}^{\varepsilon}_{D}(s)f.

Then, by definition,
p^{\varepsilon}\in H^{2}(\Omega^{\varepsilon,c})
is a unique solution to

\displaystyle\begin{split}(s^{2}-\Delta)p^{\varepsilon}=0\text{ in }\Omega^{\varepsilon,c},\qquad\gamma_{0}^{\varepsilon}p^{\varepsilon}=-\gamma_{0}^{\varepsilon}\mathcal{R}_{0}(s)f.\end{split}

(13)

Let us introduce the solution operator for the exterior Dirichlet problem:

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}^{\varepsilon}_{D}(s):\,H^{1/2}(\Gamma^{\varepsilon})\rightarrow H^{1}(\Omega^{\varepsilon,c}),\quad\mathcal{N}^{\varepsilon}_{D}g:=v_{g},

where

\displaystyle(s^{2}-\Delta)v_{g}=0\text{ in }\Omega^{\varepsilon,c},\qquad\gamma_{0}^{\varepsilon}v_{g}=g.

In this case, by definition,

\displaystyle p^{\varepsilon}=\mathcal{D}^{\varepsilon}_{D}(s)f=-\mathcal{N}^{\varepsilon}_{D}(s)\gamma_{0}^{\varepsilon}\mathcal{R}_{0}(s)f.

(14)

Our goal is to express

via
\chi_{a\varepsilon}\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}_{D}(s)\chi_{b\varepsilon}
, with

fixed, which will contain an important geometric information about the obstacle (and for which the estimates available in the literature can be easily adapted). We see immediately that the heterogeneity is concentrated on the boundary rather than in the right-hand side; on the other hand, to use
\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}_{D}(s)
, we need to have vanishing boundary conditions. To deal with this, we will use a well-chosen lifting of
\gamma_{0}^{\varepsilon}\mathcal{R}_{0}(s)f
, concentrated in the vicinity of
\Omega^{\varepsilon}
.

Reducing the inhomogeneous Dirichlet problem to the homogeneous one.
Let us set

as below and rewrite

\displaystyle p^{\varepsilon}(s)=\chi_{m\varepsilon}p^{\varepsilon}_{sc}(s,z)+{r}^{\varepsilon}(s,z),\qquad m\geq 2,

where

satisfies (

replaced by

in the first equation, i.e.

\displaystyle p^{\varepsilon}_{sc}(s,z)=-\mathcal{N}^{\varepsilon}_{D}(z)\gamma_{0}^{\varepsilon}\mathcal{R}_{0}(s)f.

(16)

It appears that for

in a manner close to optimal (this will be discussed later). On the other hand, using that

in

, we verify that

satisfies

\displaystyle(s^{2}-\Delta){r}^{\varepsilon}={\left([\Delta,\chi_{m\varepsilon}]-(s^{2}-z^{2})\chi_{m\varepsilon}\right)p^{\varepsilon}_{sc}(s,z)}\quad\text{ in }\Omega^{\varepsilon,c},\qquad\gamma_{0}^{\varepsilon}{r}^{\varepsilon}=0.

(17)

The equality on the trace follows by remarking that
\gamma_{0}^{\varepsilon}r^{\varepsilon}=\gamma_{0}^{\varepsilon}p^{\varepsilon}-\gamma_{0}^{\varepsilon}\chi_{m\varepsilon}p^{\varepsilon}_{sc}
,
\Omega^{\varepsilon}\subseteq B_{\varepsilon}
, and
\chi_{m\varepsilon}=1
on

due to

, see Figure

. Let us now introduce a new differential operator

\displaystyle L_{\delta}(s,z):=[\Delta,\chi_{\delta}]-(s^{2}-z^{2})\chi_{\delta}=\left(\Delta\chi_{\delta}\right)+2\nabla\chi_{\delta}\nabla-(s^{2}-z^{2})\chi_{\delta}.

Thus, using the above notation and the definition of
\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}_{D}
,

\displaystyle r^{\varepsilon}(s,z)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}_{D}(s)L_{m\varepsilon}(s,z)p^{\varepsilon}_{sc}(s,z)\equiv\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}_{D}(s)\chi_{2m\varepsilon}L_{m\varepsilon}(s,z)p^{\varepsilon}_{sc}(s,z),

(18)

where we use the fact that
\operatorname{supp}L_{m\varepsilon}(s,z)\varphi\subseteq\operatorname{supp}\chi_{m\varepsilon}
for all
\varphi
, and that
\chi_{2m\varepsilon}=1
on
\operatorname{supp}\chi_{m\varepsilon}
, cf. Figure

. We have made appear the quantity
\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}_{D}(s)\chi_{2m\varepsilon}
, but our goal is to have
\chi_{b\varepsilon}\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}_{D}(s)\chi_{2m\varepsilon}
.

A new expression for

involving only the truncated resolvent
\chi_{c\varepsilon}\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}_{D}\chi_{b\varepsilon}
.

The key idea is to study the difference between

and

, defined below:

\displaystyle r^{\varepsilon}_{\operatorname{ff}}:=r^{\varepsilon}-r^{\varepsilon}_{\operatorname{nf}},\quad r^{\varepsilon}_{\operatorname{nf}}:=\chi_{n\varepsilon}r^{\varepsilon},\quad n\geq 2.

(19)

With (

\displaystyle r^{\varepsilon}_{\operatorname{nf}}=\chi_{n\varepsilon}\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}_{D}(s)\chi_{2m\varepsilon}L_{m\varepsilon}(s,z)p^{\varepsilon}(s,z),

where now the resolvent

On the other hand, we remark that

We simplify the right-hand side; we choose

\displaystyle(1-\chi_{2m\varepsilon})(s^{2}-\Delta)r^{\varepsilon}=(1-\chi_{2m\varepsilon})([\Delta,\chi_{m\varepsilon}]-(s^{2}-z^{2})\chi_{m\varepsilon})p^{\varepsilon}_{sc}(s,z)=0,

\displaystyle(s^{2}-\Delta)r^{\varepsilon}_{\operatorname{ff}}

Next, we replace in the above

by (

\displaystyle(s^{2}-\Delta_{0})\mathbb{E}^{\varepsilon}r^{\varepsilon}_{\operatorname{ff}}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}^{\varepsilon}[\Delta,\chi_{2m\varepsilon}]\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}_{D}(s)\chi_{2m\varepsilon}L_{m\varepsilon}(s,z)p^{\varepsilon}_{sc}(s,z).

\displaystyle r^{\varepsilon}_{\operatorname{ff}}=\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}_{0}(s)[\Delta,\chi_{2m\varepsilon}]\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}_{D}(s)\chi_{2m\varepsilon}L_{m\varepsilon}(s,z)p^{\varepsilon}_{sc}(s,z).

\displaystyle r^{\varepsilon}(s,z)

\displaystyle=\left(\mathcal{R}_{0}^{\varepsilon}(s)[\Delta,\chi_{2m\varepsilon}]+\chi_{2m\varepsilon}\right)\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}_{D}(s)\chi_{2m\varepsilon}L_{m\varepsilon}(s,z)p^{\varepsilon}_{sc}(s,z)

where we used that
\chi_{4m\varepsilon}=1
on
\operatorname{supp}\chi_{2m\varepsilon}
. Replacing in (

)
r^{\varepsilon}(s,z)
by the rhs of the above and
p^{\varepsilon}_{sc}(s,z)
by the rhs of (
16
), and recalling the definition (

(Krein-like formula for the resolvent)
.

For all
s,z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
,

,

, the following identity holds true

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\varepsilon}_{D}(s)=\mathcal{R}^{\varepsilon}_{D}(s)-\mathcal{R}_{0}^{\varepsilon}(s)=-\left(\chi_{m\varepsilon}+Q_{\varepsilon}(s,z)\right)\mathcal{N}^{\varepsilon}_{D}(z)\gamma_{0}^{\varepsilon}\mathcal{R}_{0}(s),

\displaystyle L_{m\varepsilon}(s,z)=[\Delta,\chi_{m\varepsilon}]-(s^{2}-z^{2})\chi_{m\varepsilon}.

In what follows, we will estimate all the terms occurring in the above expression for

2.3.1
Estimates involving the free resolvent

In this section we summarize all the estimates related to the free resolvent. In our statements we will often resort to the fact that the free (cut-off) resolvent
s\mapsto\chi_{R}\mathcal{R}_{0}(s)\chi_{R}\in\mathcal{L}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3}),H^{j}(\mathbb{R}^{3}))
,
j=0,1,2
, is an entire function of
s\in\mathbb{C}
, see e.g.
[
8
, Theorem 3.1]
. We start by studying
L^{2}
-bounds in
\Omega^{\varepsilon,c}
.

Proposition 3

(Bounds in
\Omega^{\varepsilon,c}
)
.

Let
c>0
,
R>r>c\varepsilon
. Then for all
v\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
, s.t.
\operatorname{supp}v\subseteq\overline{B_{c\varepsilon}}
,

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{R}_{0}(s)v\|_{L^{2}(B_{r,R})}\lesssim\mathrm{e}^{|\Re s|_{-}(R+c\varepsilon)}\frac{R^{3/2}}{r-c\varepsilon}(c\varepsilon)^{3/2}\|v\|_{L^{2}(B_{c\varepsilon})},\quad s\in\mathbb{C}.

Proof.

We start with the explicit identity

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{R}_{0}(s)v\right)(\boldsymbol{x})=\int_{B_{c\varepsilon}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-s\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\|}}{4\pi\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\|}v(\boldsymbol{y})d\boldsymbol{y},

(23)

which we then estimate in two different ways. First of all, using a very rough
L^{\infty}
-bound on the integral kernel, and next the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we obtain that

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{R}_{0}(s)v\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{r,R})}\leq\frac{\mathrm{e}^{|\Re s|_{-}(R+c\varepsilon)}}{4\pi(r-c\varepsilon)}\|v\|_{L^{1}(B_{c\varepsilon})}\lesssim\frac{\mathrm{e}^{|\Re s|_{-}(R+c\varepsilon)}}{(r-c\varepsilon)}(c\varepsilon)^{3/2}\|v\|_{L^{2}(B_{c\varepsilon})}.

The desired far-field bound follows from
\|\mathcal{R}_{0}(s)v\|_{L^{2}(B_{r,R})}\lesssim R^{3/2}\|\mathcal{R}_{0}(s)v\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{r,R})}
.

∎

Next, we will need bounds on
\gamma_{0}^{\varepsilon}\mathcal{R}_{0}(s)f
. We will need them written in a rather peculiar manner, adapted to the statement of Theorem
2
that will follow later.
Let us introduce auxiliary projection operators defined onto the space of constants and functions orthogonal to constants, namely,

\displaystyle\mathbb{S}_{0}:=\operatorname{span}\{1\},\quad H^{1/2}_{\perp}(\Gamma^{\varepsilon}):=\{\psi\in H^{1/2}(\Gamma^{\varepsilon}):(\psi,1)_{L^{2}(\Gamma^{\varepsilon})}=0\}.

Let
\mathbb{P}_{0}^{\varepsilon}
be an

-orthogonal projection onto the space
\mathbb{S}_{0}
, and

\mathbb{P}_{\perp}^{\varepsilon}=\mathbb{I}-\mathbb{P}_{0}^{\varepsilon}
onto the space

. The proposition below shows that
\mathbb{P}_{0}^{\varepsilon}\gamma_{0}^{\varepsilon}\mathcal{R}_{0}(s)f
scales as

, while
\mathbb{P}_{\perp}^{\varepsilon}\gamma_{0}^{\varepsilon}\mathcal{R}_{0}(s)f
as

.

Proposition 4
.

Assume that
\operatorname{supp}f\subseteq\overline{\mathcal{O}}_{0}=\overline{B_{r_{0},R_{0}}}
, where
R_{0}>r_{0}>1
are like in (
2
).
Then, for
0<\varepsilon\leq 1
,

,

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{P}_{0}^{\varepsilon}\gamma_{0}^{\varepsilon}\mathcal{R}_{0}(s)f\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma^{\varepsilon})}\lesssim\varepsilon R_{0}^{3/2}(r_{0}-\varepsilon)^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{|\Re s|_{-}(R_{0}+\varepsilon)}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{O}_{0})},

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{P}_{\perp}^{\varepsilon}\gamma_{0}^{\varepsilon}\mathcal{R}_{0}(s)f\|_{H^{1/2}(\Gamma^{\varepsilon})}\lesssim\varepsilon^{3/2}R_{0}^{3/2}(r_{0}-\varepsilon)^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{|\Re s|_{-}(R_{0}+\varepsilon)}(|s|+(r_{0}-\varepsilon)^{-1})\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{O}_{0})}.

This proposition is immediate from Lemmas
3
,
4
, proven below, thus we leave its proof to the reader.

Lemma 3
.

Let
u\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega^{\varepsilon})
. Then

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{P}_{0}^{\varepsilon}\gamma_{0}^{\varepsilon}u\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma^{\varepsilon})}\lesssim\varepsilon\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega^{\varepsilon})},\qquad\|\mathbb{P}^{\varepsilon}_{\perp}\gamma_{0}^{\varepsilon}u\|_{H^{1/2}(\Gamma^{\varepsilon})}\lesssim\varepsilon^{3/2}\|\nabla u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{\varepsilon})}.

Proof.

To prove the first inequality, we use an explicit characterization of the projection operator
\mathbb{P}_{0}^{\varepsilon}
:

\displaystyle|\mathbb{P}_{0}^{\varepsilon}\gamma_{0}^{\varepsilon}u|=\frac{|(\gamma_{0}^{\varepsilon}u,1)_{L^{2}(\Gamma^{\varepsilon})}|}{\|1\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Gamma^{\varepsilon})}}\leq\frac{\|\gamma_{0}^{\varepsilon}u\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma^{\varepsilon})}}{\|1\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma^{\varepsilon})}}\leq\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega^{\varepsilon})}.

The desired inequality follows from the above and
\|1\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma^{\varepsilon})}\lesssim\varepsilon
.

To show the second bound in the statement, we use
[
11
, Proposition 4.3]
:

\displaystyle\|\mathbb{P}_{\perp}^{\varepsilon}\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{0}u\|_{H^{1/2}(\Gamma^{\varepsilon})}\leq C|\mathbb{P}_{\perp}^{\varepsilon}\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{0}u|_{H^{1/2}(\Gamma^{\varepsilon})}=C|\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{0}u|_{H^{1/2}(\Gamma^{\varepsilon})},\quad\forall 0<\varepsilon\leq 1.

Using the definition of the Sobolev-Slobodeckij seminorm and the mean-value theorem,

\displaystyle|\gamma^{\varepsilon}_{0}u|_{H^{1/2}(\Gamma^{\varepsilon})}

\displaystyle=\iint_{\Gamma^{\varepsilon}\times\Gamma^{\varepsilon}}\frac{|u(\boldsymbol{x})-u(\boldsymbol{y})|^{2}}{\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\|^{3}}d\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{x}}d\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{y}}\leq\varepsilon^{3}\iint_{\Gamma^{1}\times\Gamma^{1}}\frac{\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}({B_{\varepsilon}})}}{\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\|}d\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{x}}d\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{y}}.

The Lebesgue integral above is finite as a weakly-singular integral.
∎

Lemma 4
.

Assume that
f
is like in Proposition
4
. Then, for all
s\in\mathbb{C}
,

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{R}_{0}(s)f\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{\varepsilon})}\lesssim R_{0}^{3/2}(r_{0}-\varepsilon)^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{|\Re s|_{-}(R_{0}+\varepsilon)}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{O}_{0})},

\displaystyle\|\nabla\mathcal{R}_{0}(s)f\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{\varepsilon})}\lesssim R_{0}^{3/2}(|s|+(r_{0}-\varepsilon)^{-1})(r_{0}-\varepsilon)^{-1}\mathrm{e}^{|\Re s|_{-}(R_{0}+\varepsilon)}\|f\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{O}_{0})}.

Proof.

We show one bound only, since the remaining one is obtained in a similar manner. By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{R}_{0}(s)f\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{\varepsilon})}

\displaystyle=\left\|\int_{\mathcal{O}_{0}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-s\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\|}}{4\pi\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\|}f(\boldsymbol{y})d\boldsymbol{y}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{\varepsilon})}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{R_{0}^{3/2}\mathrm{e}^{|\Re s|_{-}(R_{0}+\varepsilon)}}{\operatorname{dist}(\operatorname{supp}f,B_{\varepsilon})}\|f\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\frac{R_{0}^{3/2}\mathrm{e}^{|\Re s|_{-}(R_{0}+\varepsilon)}}{r_{0}-\varepsilon}\|f\|_{L^{2}}.

∎

Theorem 2

Let

By the lifting lemma
[

4
Discussion and extension of the results

We have studied the problem of the sound-soft scattering by a small 3D star-shaped particle of an incident wave that obeys the wave equation with inhomogeneous initial conditions, and have proven that replacing the particle by a point source does not deteriorate the solution error in time.
An interesting extension is the 2D case, where the Huygens principle no longer holds true; we remark that the techniques used in this paper apply almost verbatim, with the difficulty being passing from the Laplace domain to the time domain, see
[
6
]
. Another possible extension of the work is the analysis of the scattering by many particles, or of the situations of resonances.
