Trust Region Reward Optimization and Proximal Inverse Reward Optimization Algorithm
Yang Chen, Menglin Zou, Jiaqi Zhang, Yitan Zhang, Junyi Yang, Gael Gendron, Libo Zhang, Jiamou Liu, Michael J. Witbrock

Abstract
Inverse Reinforcement Learning (IRL) learns a reward function to explain expert demonstrations. Modern IRL methods often use the adversarial (minimax) formulation that alternates between reward and policy optimization, which often lead to unstable training. Recent non-adversarial IRL approaches improve stability by jointly learning reward and policy via energy-based formulations but lack formal guarantees. This work bridges this gap. We first present a unified view showing canonical non-adversarial methods explicitly or implicitly maximize the likelihood of expert behavior, which is equivalent to minimizing the expected return gap. This insight leads to our main contribution: Trust Region Reward Optimization (TRRO), a framework that guarantees monotonic improvement in this likelihood via a Minorization-Maximization process. We instantiate TRRO into Proximal Inverse Reward Optimization (PIRO), a practical and stable IRL algorithm. Theoretically, TRRO provides the IRL counterpart to the stability guarantees of Trust Region Policy Optimization (TRPO) in forward RL. Empirically, PIRO matches or surpasses state-of-the-art baselines in reward recovery, policy imitation with high sample efficiency on MuJoCo and Gym-Robotics benchmarks and a real-world animal behavior modeling task. 1 1 1 The implementation is available at https://github.com/PolynomialTime/PIRO .

2.1
Maximum Entropy RL

MaxEnt RL characterizes the optimal behavior as a policy

\displaystyle J(\pi,r)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\rho^{\pi}}\left[r({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})\right]+{\mathcal{H}}(\pi),\;{\mathcal{H}}(\pi)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\rho^{\pi}}[-\log(\pi({\mathbf{a}}|{\mathbf{s}}))].

\displaystyle({\mathcal{B}}_{r}^{\pi}Q)({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})=r({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})+\gamma\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{s}}^{\prime}\sim P(\cdot|{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})}[V({\mathbf{s}}^{\prime})],\;V({\mathbf{s}})=\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{a}}\sim\pi(\cdot|{\mathbf{s}})}[Q({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})-\log\pi({\mathbf{a}}|{\mathbf{s}})].

The operator

3

that maximizes

in (
MaxEnt-RL

Maximum Likelihood IRL

ML-IRL bypasses the nested loop in MaxEnt IRL by jointly updating the reward and policy

In this section, we introduce

(TRRO), a theoretical IRL framework that enforces stability by producing a guaranteed increase on the likelihood of expert behavior. To our knowledge, it provides the

Maximizing the surrogate ensures progress on the original objective. In light of this, TRRO plays a role in inverse RL analogous to Trust Region Policy Optimization (TRPO)

Proximal Inverse Reward Optimization Algorithm

In this section, we develop a practical algorithm,
Proximal Inverse Reward Optimization
(PIRO). It approximates the theoretical guarantee of TRRO, enabling adpatively larger reward update steps, efficient policy optimization and accommodating continuous state-action spaces. It operates under realistic constraint of finite expert demonstrations
{\mathcal{D}}_{E}=\{({\mathbf{s}}_{0},{\mathbf{a}}_{0},\ldots)\}

Adaptive reward update.
The original scale factor

Related Work

Adversarial IRL.

Predominant IRL methods follow an adversarial learning paradigm (see GAIL
[
19
]
and discriminator-actor-critic (DAC)
[
24
]
), with AIRL variants
[
10
,
11
,
12
]
and extensions
[
48
,
47
,
8
,
9
]
as key representatives. As argued in
[
33
]
, this also includes methods that do not explicitly adopt a min-max game formulation but implicitly learn from its adversarial dynamics, such as classic approaches like Apprenticeship Learning
[
1
,
2
]
and Max-Ent IRL
[
52
,
53
]
. Recent work
[
39
]
unifies these adversarial methods through the concept of Moment Matching (a.k.a. Integral Probability Metric)
[
26
]
, offering a broader perspective on their underlying principles.
Building on this, recent methods further improve adversarial IRL by providing sample-efficient policy update mechnisms such as FILTER
[
40
]
(resets the learner to expert states) and HyPE
[
33
]
(a hybrid-RL based IRL algorithm that trains on a mixture of online and expert data to curtail unnecessary exploration in policy updates). In contrast to all these methods, our approach is non-adversarial and features principled stable reward learning.

Non-adversarial IRL.
We expand the discussion on non-adversarial IRL methods in the introduction and Sec.
3
. Coherent Soft Imitation Learning (CSIL)
[
44
]
simplifies the idea of non-adversarial IRL with a two-stage procedure: it first extracts a reward function from a max-likelihood policy with a reference policy and then trains a policy based on this reward. BC-IRL
[
41
]
minimizes the mean squared loss rather than maximizing the likelihood, but with no guarantee on stability. Least-squares inverse Q-learning (LSIQ)
[
3
]
penalizes the reward function magnititude and give its theoretical support; PIRO does so implicitly in its practical implementation of reward update constraints. To handle distributional shift due to limited state-action coverage, some methods adopt the model-based paradigm and conservative updates — either on the policy (Offline ML-IRL
[
51
]
) or on the reward function (CLARE
[
49
]
).
In contrast, our PIRO is model-free and leverages online rollouts.
Another recent method, SFM
[
22
]
, minimizes the imitation gap by matching expert Successor Features (i.e., predictions of future state occupancies under a policy). A technically related method is P
2
IL
[
43
]
, which applies the proximal point method to stabilize soft Q-function learning under linear MDP assumptions. Our method addresses general MDPs with explicit rewards.

Stable Inverse Optimal Control.
A line of work in inverse optimal control uses trust-region or Lyapunov-based methods
[
37
,
5
,
42
]
to ensure stability but requires knowledge of system dynamics and second-order optimization, limiting scalability. PIRO, in contrast, is model-free and relies only on first-order optimization, making it more practical for real-world applications.

8
Conclusion

We propose Proximal Inverse Reward Optimization (PIRO), a novel non-adversarial, practical IRL algorithm that stabilizes reward learning by approximating Trust Region Reward Optimization (TRRO) – a novel theoretical framework guaranteeing monotonic improvement in the likelihood of expert behavior. Experiments MuJoCo and Gym Robotics benchmarks show that PIRO achieves stable training, accurate and robust reward recovery, high sample efficiency, and good reward transfer capability. This work provides a theoretical foundation for stabilizing IRL, and we hope it provides a new perspective for designing more robust IRL algorithms.

Limitations.
Despite its advantages, PIRO has limitations. First, while it stabilizes reward learning, the overall training stability also depends on a stable policy optimizer, especially in high-dimensional and complex-dynamics settings. Second, the dependency on on-policy sampling may reduce sample efficiency in environment interactions, potentially limiting scalability to sample-expensive tasks.

Future work.
First, improving the efficiency of policy optimization by incorporating resets to expert states
[
40
,
33
]
may substantially reduce computational cost. Second, exploring alternative policy alignment measures beyond likelihood (e.g., statistical divergences other than KL) may open new paradigms for stable IRL. Finally, on the application side, extending PIRO to real-world scenarios — such as learning reward models and policies for aligning large language models with human feedback — offers a promising path to improving agent performance in practice.

Lemma 5
.

The total variation distance between
\pi_{1}(\cdot|{\mathbf{s}})
and
\pi_{2}(\cdot|{\mathbf{s}})
is upper-bounded as follows:

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{TV}}(\pi_{1}(\cdot s),\pi_{2}(\cdot|{\mathbf{s}}))=\frac{1}{2}\|\pi_{1}(\cdot|{\mathbf{s}})-\pi_{2}(\cdot|{\mathbf{s}})\|_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{{\mathbf{a}}\in{\mathcal{A}}}\left|\pi_{1}({\mathbf{a}}|{\mathbf{s}})-\pi_{2}({\mathbf{a}}|{\mathbf{s}})\right|\leq\frac{|{\mathcal{A}}|\epsilon}{2(1-\gamma)}.

(18)

Proof.

We start by analyzing the sensitivity of the policy to changes in soft
Q
-function. The difference in
\pi_{1}({\mathbf{a}}|{\mathbf{s}})
and
\pi_{2}({\mathbf{a}}|{\mathbf{s}})
arises from the difference in their respective soft
Q
-functions,
Q_{1}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})
and
Q_{2}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})
. Expanding the policies gives:

\displaystyle\pi_{1}({\mathbf{a}}|{\mathbf{s}})-\pi_{2}({\mathbf{a}}|{\mathbf{s}})=

\displaystyle\frac{\exp\left(Q_{1}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})\right)}{\sum_{a^{\prime}}\exp\left(Q_{1}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}}^{\prime})\right)}-\frac{\exp\left(Q_{2}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})\right)}{\sum_{{\mathbf{a}}^{\prime}}\exp\left(Q_{2}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}}^{\prime})\right)}.

(19)

This softmax-like function is
\frac{1}{\alpha}
-Lipschitz continuous
[
14
]
with
\alpha
being the temperature in the energy-based model (w.l.o.g., we assume
\alpha=1
in this paper). This means small changes in
Q
lead to proportionally small changes in the softmax output. This allows us to approximate the policy difference for small deviations in
Q
. Thus, the policy difference can be bounded as:

\left|\pi_{1}({\mathbf{a}}|{\mathbf{s}})-\pi_{2}({\mathbf{a}}|{\mathbf{s}})\right|\leq\left|Q_{1}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})-Q_{2}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})\right|.

(20)

Summing over actions, the (doubled) total variation distance becomes:

\displaystyle\|\pi_{1}(\cdot|{\mathbf{s}})-\pi_{2}(\cdot|{\mathbf{s}})\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\mathbf{a}}\in{\mathcal{A}}}\left|\pi_{1}({\mathbf{a}}|{\mathbf{s}})-\pi_{2}({\mathbf{a}}|{\mathbf{s}})\right|\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{{\mathbf{a}}\in{\mathcal{A}}}\left|Q_{1}(({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}}))-Q_{2}(({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}}))\right|.

(21)

We bound
\left|Q_{1}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})-Q_{2}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})\right|
by:

\displaystyle\max_{{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}}}\left|Q_{1}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})-Q_{2}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})\right|

(22)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\epsilon+\gamma\max_{({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})}\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{s}}^{\prime}\sim P(\cdot|({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}}))}\left[V_{1}({\mathbf{s}}^{\prime})-V_{2}({\mathbf{s}}^{\prime})\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\epsilon+\gamma\max_{{\mathbf{s}}^{\prime},{\mathbf{a}}^{\prime}}\Bigg|\log\sum_{{\mathbf{a}}^{\prime}}\exp\left(Q_{1}({\mathbf{s}}^{\prime},{\mathbf{a}}^{\prime})\right)-\log\sum_{{\mathbf{a}}^{\prime}}\exp\left(Q_{2}({\mathbf{s}}^{\prime},{\mathbf{a}}^{\prime})\right)\Bigg|

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{\leq}

\displaystyle\epsilon+\gamma\max_{({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})}\left|Q_{1}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})-Q_{2}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})\right|,

where inequality
(a)
uses the fact that for any two sets of values
\{x_{i}\}
and
\{y_{i}\}
,

\left|\log\sum_{i}\exp(x_{i})-\log\sum_{i}\exp(y_{i})\right|\leq\max_{i}|x_{i}-y_{i}|.

(23)

Rearranging Eq. (
22

\max_{({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})}\left|Q_{1}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})-Q_{2}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})\right|\leq\frac{\epsilon}{1-\gamma}.

Finally, according to Eq. (
21

\displaystyle\|\pi_{1}(\cdot|{\mathbf{s}})-\pi_{2}(\cdot|{\mathbf{s}})\|_{1}

\displaystyle\leq|{\mathcal{A}}|\left|Q_{1}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})-Q_{2}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|{\mathcal{A}}|\epsilon}{1-\gamma}.

(24)

Lemma 6
.

Let
d_{t}^{\pi_{1}}({\mathbf{s}})
and
d_{t}^{\pi_{2}}({\mathbf{s}})
denote the state marginal distributions at time
t
under each policy, starting from the same initial distribution
\eta
. Then, for any
t\geq 0
,

D_{\mathrm{TV}}(d_{t}^{\pi_{1}},d_{t}^{\pi_{2}})=\frac{1}{2}\|d_{t}^{\pi_{2}}-d_{t}^{\pi_{1}}\|_{1}\leq tD_{\mathrm{TV}}^{\max}(\pi_{1},\pi_{2}),

(25)

where

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{TV}}^{\max}(\pi_{1},\pi_{2})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\max_{{\mathbf{s}}}D_{\mathrm{TV}}(\pi_{1}(\cdot|{\mathbf{s}}),\pi_{2}(\cdot|{\mathbf{s}}))=\frac{\epsilon}{1-\gamma}~~~~~\text{(Lemma~\ref{lem:tv})}

(26)

is the worst-case total variation distance between
\pi_{2}
and
\pi_{1}
over all states.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
t
.

Base case
(
t=0
):
At
t=0
,
d_{0}^{\pi_{2}}=d_{0}^{\pi_{1}}=\eta
(the initial distribution), so

\|d_{0}^{\pi_{2}}-d_{0}^{\pi_{1}}\|_{1}=0,

(27)

which satisfies the bound.

Inductive step
:
Suppose that at time
t
,

D_{\mathrm{TV}}(d_{t}^{\pi_{1}},d_{t}^{\pi_{2}})\leq tD^{\max}_{\mathrm{TV}}(\pi_{1},\pi_{2}).

(28)

We now show that the same holds at time
t+1
.

The state marginals evolve according to

d_{t+1}^{\pi}({\mathbf{s}}^{\prime})=\sum_{{\mathbf{s}}}d_{t}^{\pi}({\mathbf{s}})\sum_{{\mathbf{a}}}\pi({\mathbf{a}}|{\mathbf{s}})P({\mathbf{s}}^{\prime}|{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}}).

Thus,

\displaystyle d_{t+1}^{\pi_{2}}({\mathbf{s}}^{\prime})-

\displaystyle d_{t+1}^{\pi_{1}}({\mathbf{s}}^{\prime})=\sum_{{\mathbf{s}}}\sum_{\mathbf{a}}\big(d_{t}^{\pi_{2}}({\mathbf{s}})\pi_{2}({\mathbf{a}}|{\mathbf{s}})-d_{t}^{\pi_{1}}({\mathbf{s}})\pi_{1}({\mathbf{a}}|{\mathbf{s}})\big)P({\mathbf{s}}^{\prime}|{\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}}).

(29)

Taking the
L^{1}
norm and using the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\|d_{t+1}^{\pi_{2}}-d_{t+1}^{\pi_{1}}\|_{1}\leq

\displaystyle\;\sum_{{\mathbf{s}}}\left\|d_{t}^{\pi_{2}}({\mathbf{s}})\pi_{2}(\cdot|{\mathbf{s}})-d_{t}^{\pi_{1}}({\mathbf{s}})\pi_{1}(\cdot|{\mathbf{s}})\right\|_{1}.

(30)

Now expand the difference inside:

\displaystyle d_{t}^{\pi_{2}}({\mathbf{s}})\pi_{2}({\mathbf{a}}|{\mathbf{s}})-d_{t}^{\pi_{1}}({\mathbf{s}})\pi_{1}(a|{\mathbf{s}})=(d_{t}^{\pi_{2}}({\mathbf{s}})-d_{t}^{\pi_{1}}({\mathbf{s}}))\pi_{2}({\mathbf{a}}|{\mathbf{s}})+d_{t}^{\pi_{1}}({\mathbf{s}})\left(\pi_{2}({\mathbf{a}}|{\mathbf{s}})-\pi_{1}({\mathbf{a}}|{\mathbf{s}})\right).

(31)

Using triangle inequality again:

\displaystyle\left\|d_{t}^{\pi_{2}}({\mathbf{s}})\pi_{2}(\cdot|{\mathbf{s}})-d_{t}^{\pi_{1}}({\mathbf{s}})\pi_{1}(\cdot|{\mathbf{s}})\right\|_{1}\leq|d_{t}^{\pi_{2}}({\mathbf{s}})-d_{t}^{\pi_{1}}({\mathbf{s}})|+d_{t}^{\pi_{1}}({\mathbf{s}})\|\pi_{2}(\cdot|{\mathbf{s}})-\pi_{1}(\cdot|{\mathbf{s}})\|_{1}.

(32)

Thus,

\displaystyle\|d_{t+1}^{\pi_{2}}-d_{t+1}^{\pi_{1}}\|_{1}\leq\sum_{{\mathbf{s}}}|d_{t}^{\pi_{2}}({\mathbf{s}})-d_{t}^{\pi_{1}}({\mathbf{s}})|+\sum_{{\mathbf{s}}}d_{t}^{\pi_{1}}({\mathbf{s}})\|\pi_{2}(\cdot|{\mathbf{s}})-\pi_{1}(\cdot|{\mathbf{s}})\|_{1}.

(33)

The first term of the right-hand side is simply

\|d_{t}^{\pi_{2}}-d_{t}^{\pi_{1}}\|_{1}=2D_{\mathrm{TV}}(d_{t}^{\pi_{1}},d_{t}^{\pi_{2}}),

(34)

and the second term is at most

2D_{\mathrm{TV}}^{\max}(\pi_{2},\pi_{1}),

(35)

since
d_{t}^{\pi_{1}}
is a distribution and
\|\pi_{2}(\cdot|{\mathbf{s}})-\pi_{1}(\cdot|{\mathbf{s}})\|_{1}\leq 2D_{\mathrm{TV}}^{\max}(\pi_{2},\pi_{1})
for all
{\mathbf{s}}
.

Therefore,

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{TV}}(\rho_{t+1}^{\pi_{1}},d_{t+1}^{\pi_{2}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|d_{t+1}^{\pi_{2}}-d_{t+1}^{\pi_{1}}\|_{1}\leq

\displaystyle D_{\mathrm{TV}}(d_{t}^{\pi_{1}},d_{t}^{\pi_{2}})+D_{\mathrm{TV}}^{\max}(\pi_{2},\pi_{1}).

(36)

Applying the inductive hypothesis:

D_{\mathrm{TV}}(d_{t}^{\pi_{1}},d_{t}^{\pi_{2}})\leq tD_{\mathrm{TV}}^{\max}(\pi_{1},\pi_{2}),

(37)

we conclude

D_{\mathrm{TV}}(d_{t+1}^{\pi_{1}},d_{t+1}^{\pi_{2}})\leq(t+1)D_{\mathrm{TV}}^{\max}(\pi_{1},\pi_{2}).

(38)

Thus, the claim holds for
t+1
, completing the induction.
∎

Lemma 7
.

Under MaxEnt RL, let
\pi_{1}({\mathbf{a}}|{\mathbf{s}})
and
\pi_{2}({\mathbf{a}}|{\mathbf{s}})
be two policies defined over a finite action set
{\mathcal{A}}

(39)

Proof.

We start from the softmax policy expression:

\displaystyle\log\pi_{i}({\mathbf{a}}|{\mathbf{s}})=Q_{i}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})-\log\sum_{{\mathbf{a}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{A}}}\exp(Q_{i}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}}^{\prime})),\quad i=1,2.

(40)

So the difference is:

\displaystyle\log\pi_{1}({\mathbf{a}}|{\mathbf{s}})-\log\pi_{2}({\mathbf{a}}|{\mathbf{s}})=Q_{1}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})-Q_{2}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})-

(41)

\displaystyle\left[\log\sum_{{\mathbf{a}}^{\prime}}\exp(Q_{1}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}}^{\prime}))-\log\sum_{{\mathbf{a}}^{\prime}}\exp(Q_{2}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}}^{\prime}))\right].

Following from the Lipschitz continuity of the

function with Lipschitz constant 1 under

-norm, we have

\displaystyle\left|\log\sum_{\mathbf{a}}\exp(Q_{1}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}}))-\log\sum_{\mathbf{a}}\exp(Q_{2}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}}))\right|\leq\|Q_{1}-Q_{2}\|_{\infty}=\frac{\epsilon}{1-\gamma}~~~~\text{(Lemma~\ref{lem:tv})}.

(42)

Combining everything:

\displaystyle|\log\pi_{1}({\mathbf{a}}|{\mathbf{s}})-\log\pi_{2}({\mathbf{a}}|{\mathbf{s}})|

(43)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle|Q_{1}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})-Q_{2}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})|+\left|\log\sum_{\mathbf{a}}\exp(Q_{1}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}}))-\log\sum_{\mathbf{a}}\exp(Q_{2}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}}))\right|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 2\|Q_{1}-Q_{2}\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2\epsilon}{1-\gamma}.

Lemma 10
.

The discounted entropy difference is bounded by

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{2}}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\log\pi_{2}({\mathbf{a}}_{t}|{\mathbf{s}}_{t})\right]-\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{1}}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\log\pi_{1}({\mathbf{a}}_{t}|{\mathbf{s}}_{t})\right]\right|

We express the expected discounted sum as

\small\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\log\pi({\mathbf{a}}_{t}|{\mathbf{s}}_{t})\right]=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{s}}\sim d_{t}^{\pi}}\left[\sum_{\mathbf{a}}\pi({\mathbf{a}}|{\mathbf{s}})\log\pi({\mathbf{a}}|{\mathbf{s}})\right].

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\Bigg(\sum_{\mathbf{s}}d_{t}^{\pi_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{new}}}}({\mathbf{s}})\sum_{\mathbf{a}}\pi_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{new}}}({\mathbf{a}}|{\mathbf{s}})r_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{new}}}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})-\sum_{\mathbf{s}}d_{t}^{\pi_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{old}}}}({\mathbf{s}})\sum_{\mathbf{a}}\pi_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{old}}}({\mathbf{a}}|{\mathbf{s}})r_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{new}}}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})\Bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\left|\sum_{\mathbf{s}}d_{t}^{\pi_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{new}}}}({\mathbf{s}})\left(\sum_{\mathbf{a}}\left(\pi_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{new}}}({\mathbf{a}}|{\mathbf{s}})-\pi_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{old}}}({\mathbf{a}}|{\mathbf{s}})\right)r_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{new}}}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})\right)\right|+

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\left|\sum_{\mathbf{s}}\left(d_{t}^{\pi_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{new}}}}({\mathbf{s}})-d_{t}^{\pi_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{old}}}}({\mathbf{s}})\right)\sum_{\mathbf{a}}\pi_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{old}}}({\mathbf{a}}|{\mathbf{s}})r_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{new}}}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})\right|~~~~~\text{(triangle inequality)}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\sum_{\mathbf{s}}d_{t}^{\pi_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{new}}}}({\mathbf{s}})\cdot 2RD_{\mathrm{TV}}(\pi_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{new}}}(\cdot|{\mathbf{s}}),\pi_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{old}}}(\cdot|{\mathbf{s}}))+\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}R\|d_{t}^{\pi_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{new}}}}-d_{t}^{\pi_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{old}}}}\|_{1}~~~~~\text{(Lemma~\ref{lemma:state-marginal})}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\left(2RD_{\mathrm{TV}}^{\max}(\pi_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{new}}},\pi_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{old}}})+2tRD_{\mathrm{TV}}^{\max}(\pi_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{new}}},\pi_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{old}}})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\left(2(t+1)RD_{\mathrm{TV}}^{\max}(\pi_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{new}}},\pi_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{old}}})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\left((t+1)\frac{R|{\mathcal{A}}|\epsilon_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{old}}}({\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{new}})}{1-\gamma}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{R|{\mathcal{A}}|\epsilon_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{old}}}({\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{new}})}{1-\gamma}\left(\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}t+\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{R|{\mathcal{A}}|\epsilon_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{old}}}({\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{new}})}{1-\gamma}\left(\frac{\gamma}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}+\frac{1}{1-\gamma}\right)

=\frac{R|{\mathcal{A}}|\epsilon_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{old}}}({\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{new}})}{(1-\gamma)^{3}}.~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

(57)

The bound of the term
\Delta_{\text{entropy}}({\mathbf{s}})
directly follows Lemma
10
:

\displaystyle\Delta_{\text{entropy}}({\mathbf{s}})

\displaystyle\leq\Bigg(\frac{2|{\mathcal{A}}|}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}+\frac{(2R+(1+\gamma)\log|{\mathcal{A}}|)|{\mathcal{A}}|}{(1-\gamma)^{3}}

(58)

\displaystyle+\frac{(2R+(1+\gamma)\log|{\mathcal{A}}|)|{\mathcal{A}}|\gamma}{(1-\gamma)^{4}}\Bigg)\epsilon_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{old}}}({\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{new}}).

Finally, combining Eq. (
57
) and Eq. (
58
), we complete the proof by

\displaystyle\ell_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{old}}}({\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{new}})-\ell({\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{new}})

(59)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{s}}\sim\rho^{\pi_{E}}}\left[V_{r_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{new}}}}^{\pi_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{new}}}}({\mathbf{s}})-V_{r_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{new}}}}^{\pi_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{old}}}}({\mathbf{s}})\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\Delta_{\text{reward}}({\mathbf{s}})+\Delta_{\text{entropy}}({\mathbf{s}})~~~~~\text{(Eq.~(\ref{eq:boundE})$+$Eq.~(\ref{eq:boundR}))}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{2|{\mathcal{A}}|}{(1-\gamma)^{2}}+\frac{(5-\gamma)R|{\mathcal{A}}|+(\gamma-\gamma^{2}+2)|{\mathcal{A}}|\log|{\mathcal{A}}|}{(1-\gamma)^{4}}\right)\epsilon_{{\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{old}}}({\bm{\theta}}_{\mathrm{new}}).

A Unified View of Non-adversarial IRL

Let
\mathrm{Cov}_{p({\mathbf{x}})}(\kappa_{1}({\mathbf{x}}),\kappa_{2}({\mathbf{x}}))\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{p({\mathbf{x}})}[\kappa_{1}({\mathbf{x}})\cdot\kappa_{2}({\mathbf{x}})]-\mathbb{E}_{p({\mathbf{x}})}[\kappa_{1}({\mathbf{x}})]\cdot\mathbb{E}_{p({\mathbf{x}})}[\kappa_{2}({\mathbf{x}})]
denote the covariance of two functions
\kappa_{1}({\mathbf{x}}),\kappa_{2}({\mathbf{x}})
under the distribution
p({\mathbf{x}})
.
We first show an equivalent expression of
\ell({\bm{\theta}})
.

Lemma 11
.

The likelihood objective has the following equivalent expression:

\displaystyle\ell({\bm{\theta}})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\rho^{\pi_{E}}}[r_{\bm{\theta}}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})]-\mathbb{E}_{\rho^{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}}}[r_{\bm{\theta}}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})]

(60)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\rho^{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}}}\left[\frac{\rho^{\pi_{E}}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})}{\rho^{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})}r_{\bm{\theta}}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})\right]-\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\rho^{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}}}\left[\frac{\rho^{\pi_{E}}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})}{\rho^{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})}\right]}_{\equiv 1}\times\mathbb{E}_{\rho^{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}}}[r_{\bm{\theta}}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})]

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Cov}_{\rho^{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})}\left(\frac{\rho^{\pi_{E}}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})}{\rho^{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})},\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}r_{\bm{\theta}}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})\right).

We next show that the KL-based
f
-IRL
[
30
]
essentially maximizes the likelihood of expert demonstrations (minimize the imitation gap). Recall from the main text that
f
-IRL assumes a state-only reward function,
r_{\bm{\theta}}({\mathbf{s}})
, and seeks to match the expert’s state marginal distribution by minimizing an
f
-divergence objective:

L_{f}({\bm{\theta}})\coloneqq D_{f}(\rho^{\pi_{E}}({\mathbf{s}})\|\rho^{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}}({\mathbf{s}})),

(61)

where
\rho^{\pi}({\mathbf{s}})
denotes the state marginal of the occupancy measure such that
\rho^{\pi}({\mathbf{s}},{\mathbf{a}})=\rho^{\pi}({\mathbf{s}})\pi({\mathbf{a}}|{\mathbf{s}})
. It has been shown in
[
30
, Appendix A2]
that if
D_{f}
is taken as the KL divergence, then
\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}L_{f}({\bm{\theta}})
can be reduced to the following analytical form:

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}L_{f}({\bm{\theta}})=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\rho^{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}}(\tau)}\left[\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{s}}\sim\tau}\left[\frac{\rho^{\pi_{E}}({\mathbf{s}})}{\rho^{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}}({\mathbf{s}})}\right]\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{s}}\sim\tau}[r_{\bm{\theta}}({\mathbf{s}})]\right]-

(62)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\rho^{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}}(\tau)}\left[\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{s}}\sim\tau}\left[\frac{\rho^{\pi_{E}}({\mathbf{s}})}{\rho^{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}}({\mathbf{s}})}\right]\right]\times\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\rho^{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}}(\tau)}\left[\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{s}}\sim\tau}[r_{\bm{\theta}}({\mathbf{s}})]\right],

where
\rho^{\pi_{\theta}}(\tau)
denotes the trajectory distribution under the reward
r_{\bm{\theta}}
and
\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{s}}\sim\tau}[\cdot]
denotes the expectation w.r.t. states over the cumulative state visitation frequency determined by a given trajectory. To show that KL-based
f
-IRL essentially maximizes
\ell({\bm{\theta}})
, it suffices to show that Eq. (
62
) is propotional to Eq. (
60
). To proceed, we first notice that
\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\rho^{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}}(\tau)}\left[\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{s}}\sim\tau}[\cdot]\right]\equiv(1-\gamma)\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{s}}\sim\rho^{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}}({\mathbf{s}})}[\cdot]
as both represent the state marginal of the occupancy measure. Given this equivalence relationship, we can reduce the second term in the right-hand side of Eq. (
62
) to the following term:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\rho^{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}}(\tau)}\left[\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{s}}\sim\tau}\left[\frac{\rho^{E}({\mathbf{s}})}{\rho^{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}}({\mathbf{s}})}\right]\right]\times\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\rho^{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}}(\tau)}\left[\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{s}}\sim\tau}[r_{\bm{\theta}}({\mathbf{s}})]\right]

(63)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\gamma)\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{s}}\sim\rho^{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}}({\mathbf{s}})}\left[\frac{\rho^{E}({\mathbf{s}})}{\rho^{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}}({\mathbf{s}})}\right]\times\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{s}}\sim\rho^{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}}({\mathbf{s}})}\left[r_{\bm{\theta}}({\mathbf{s}})\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\gamma)\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{s}}\sim\rho^{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}}({\mathbf{s}})}\left[r_{\bm{\theta}}({\mathbf{s}})\right].

We next investigate the first term in the right-hand side of Eq. (
62
):

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\rho^{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}}(\tau)}\left[\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{s}}\sim\tau}\left[\frac{\rho^{\pi_{E}}({\mathbf{s}})}{\rho^{\pi_{\theta}}({\mathbf{s}})}\right]\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{s}}\sim\tau}[r_{\theta}({\mathbf{s}})]\right]

(64)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\rho^{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}}(\tau)}\Bigg[-\mathrm{Cov}_{\tau}\left(\frac{\rho^{\pi_{E}}({\mathbf{s}})}{\rho^{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}}({\mathbf{s}})},\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}r_{\bm{\theta}}({\mathbf{s}})\right)+\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{s}}\sim\tau}\left[\frac{\rho_{E}({\mathbf{s}})}{\rho^{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}}({\mathbf{s}})}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}r_{\bm{\theta}}({\mathbf{s}})\right]\Bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\rho^{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}}(\tau)}\left[-\mathrm{Cov}_{\tau}\left(\frac{\rho^{\pi^{\pi_{E}}}({\mathbf{s}})}{\rho^{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}}({\mathbf{s}})},\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}r_{\bm{\theta}}({\mathbf{s}})\right)\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\tau\sim\rho^{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}}(\tau)}\left[\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{s}}\sim\tau}\left[\frac{\rho^{\pi_{E}}({\mathbf{s}})}{\rho^{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}}({\mathbf{s}})}\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}r_{\bm{\theta}}({\mathbf{s}})\right]\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mathrm{Cov}_{\rho^{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}}({\mathbf{s}})}\left(\frac{\rho^{\pi^{\pi_{E}}}({\mathbf{s}})}{\rho^{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}}({\mathbf{s}})},\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}r_{\bm{\theta}}({\mathbf{s}})\right)+(1-\gamma)\mathbb{E}_{\rho^{\pi_{E}}({\mathbf{s}})}[\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}r_{\bm{\theta}}({\mathbf{s}})].

Combining Eq. (
63
) and Eq. (
64
), we have

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}L_{f}({\bm{\theta}})=

\displaystyle-\mathrm{Cov}_{\rho^{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}}({\mathbf{s}})}\left(\frac{\rho^{\pi^{\pi_{E}}}({\mathbf{s}})}{\rho^{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}}({\mathbf{s}})},\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}r_{\bm{\theta}}({\mathbf{s}})\right)+(1-\gamma)\mathbb{E}_{\rho^{\pi^{\pi_{E}}}({\mathbf{s}})}[\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}r_{\bm{\theta}}({\mathbf{s}})]-(1-\gamma)\mathbb{E}_{{\mathbf{s}}\sim\rho^{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}}({\mathbf{s}})}\left[r_{\bm{\theta}}({\mathbf{s}})\right]

(65)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\mathrm{Cov}_{\rho^{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}}({\mathbf{s}})}\left(\frac{\rho^{\pi^{\pi_{E}}}({\mathbf{s}})}{\rho^{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}}({\mathbf{s}})},\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}r_{\bm{\theta}}({\mathbf{s}})\right)+(1-\gamma)\mathrm{Cov}_{\rho^{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}}({\mathbf{s}})}\left(\frac{\rho^{\pi^{\pi_{E}}}({\mathbf{s}})}{\rho^{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}}({\mathbf{s}})},\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}r_{\bm{\theta}}({\mathbf{s}})\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\gamma\mathrm{Cov}_{\rho^{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}}({\mathbf{s}})}\left(\frac{\rho^{\pi^{\pi_{E}}}({\mathbf{s}})}{\rho^{\pi_{\bm{\theta}}}({\mathbf{s}})},\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}r_{\bm{\theta}}({\mathbf{s}})\right).

Comparison Between Theoretical and Adaptive

To further validate our theoretical analysis, we conduct an additional experiment on

7

.

We compare this theoretical
C
against the adaptive
C
method (bounded in
[0.001,10]

8
