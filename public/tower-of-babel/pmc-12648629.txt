Anaphylaxis During CAR T‐Cell Infusion in an HIV‐Positive Patient With High‐Grade B‐Cell Lymphoma: A Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Mostafa F Mohammed Saleh, Abdulrahman Nasiri, Ahmed Alahmadi, Khalid Alsuhaibani, Nawaf Aldawsari, Hadeel Samrkandi, Naeem Chaudhri, Syed O Ahmed, Mahmoud Aljurf, Alfadel Alshaibani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractChimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T‐cell therapy is a highly effective treatment for relapsed or refractory large B‐cell lymphoma (LBCL), but clinical experience in people living with HIV (PLWH) remains limited due to their exclusion from pivotal trials. We report the case of a 55‐year‐old man with HIV and high‐grade B‐cell lymphoma who developed severe anaphylaxis during axicabtagene ciloleucel (axi‐cel) infusion, requiring early termination after approximately 50% of the planned dose was delivered. The patient experienced only Grade 1 cytokine release syndrome and no neurotoxicity. Despite incomplete infusion, he achieved a sustained partial metabolic response on PET/CT imaging at Days +30, +60, and +110 post‐infusion. HIV remained well‐controlled throughout, and no infectious complications were observed. This case highlights both the feasibility of administering CAR T‐cell therapy in virologically suppressed PLWH and the potential for clinical benefit even with partial cell delivery. In addition, it draws attention to anaphylaxis as a rare but serious infusion‐related adverse event. To our knowledge, this is the first report of anaphylaxis to axi‐cel in a PLWH. The case underscores the need for enhanced pharmacovigilance and pre‐infusion risk assessment. It also supports growing evidence that PLWH should not be categorically excluded from CAR T‐cell access or clinical trials.Keywords: anaphylaxis, axicabtagene ciloleucel, CAR T‐cell therapy, case report, cytokine release syndrome, HIV, immunocompromised host, reaction, large B‐cell lymphoma, PLWH

Introduction

Chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) T‐cell therapy targeting CD19 has revolutionized the treatment landscape for relapsed or refractory large B‐cell lymphomas (LBCL), demonstrating impressive response rates and durable remissions in a subset of patients. Axicabtagene ciloleucel (axi‐cel), a second‐generation CD28‐based CAR T‐cell product, was initially approved for the treatment of LBCL after at least two prior lines of therapy [1]. However, the Phase 3 ZUMA‐7 trial demonstrated that axi‐cel as a second‐line treatment for primary refractory or early relapsed LBCL significantly improves event‐free and overall survival compared to standard salvage chemoimmunotherapy plus transplant, leading to an expanded indication for CAR T‐cell therapy in earlier lines [2]. While cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and immune effector cell‐associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) are the most commonly encountered toxicities, acute infusion‐related anaphylaxis remains exceedingly rare and underreported. The incidence of such reactions may be underestimated, particularly in cases involving early termination of infusion or concurrent immune suppression.

The safety and efficacy of CAR T‐cell therapy in patients living with HIV remain incompletely characterized due to their historic exclusion from most pivotal trials. However, emerging real‐world data and case series suggest that with appropriate viral suppression and multidisciplinary care, HIV‐positive patients may achieve clinical outcomes comparable to the general population [2, 3]. Nevertheless, these patients often present unique clinical challenges, including drug–drug interactions, immune reconstitution, opportunistic infection risk, and impaired B‐cell recovery, which may influence the timing and composition of treatment regimens, including the administration of CD20‐directed therapies like rituximab [4].

Herein, we report a rare case of a 55‐year‐old HIV‐positive male with high‐grade B‐cell lymphoma who developed an anaphylactic reaction during infusion of axi‐cel, limiting the administered dose to approximately 50%. Despite incomplete CAR T‐cell delivery, the patient achieved a sustained partial metabolic response with no evidence of progressive disease over 8‐month follow‐up period. This case underscores the feasibility of CAR T‐cell therapy in immunocompromised patients and highlights the importance of recognizing and managing uncommon but serious infusion‐related toxicities.

Case Presentation

A 55‐year‐old male with a history of well‐controlled HIV infection (on ART) and reactive hepatitis C serology presented in February 2024 with 1 month of progressive right upper quadrant abdominal discomfort and unintentional weight loss. Initial imaging revealed multiple hepatic lesions, and a liver core biopsy was suggestive of classical Hodgkin lymphoma. However, repeat biopsy on March 31, 2024, confirmed the diagnosis of high‐grade LBCL, with Ki‐67 proliferation index of 80%–90%. Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) testing was negative for C‐MYC rearrangement. Staging PET‐CT showed extensive FDG‐avid nodal and bulky extranodal disease, particularly within the liver, and MRI brain ruled out CNS involvement but showed sequelae of prior cryptococcal infection.

Due to bilateral renal involvement observed radiographically, the patient was deemed at high risk for CNS disease. A cerebrospinal fluid evaluation via lumbar puncture was negative for malignant cells. Given the patient's CD4 count was markedly suppressed, rituximab was initially withheld due to concerns regarding infectious risk. He was started on R‐CVP (rituximab withheld) for tumor lysis prophylaxis, followed by DA‐EPOCH for subsequent cycles. Rituximab was incorporated intermittently based on CD4 recovery, with a total of six cycles administered by September 2024. Interim PET scan after four cycles revealed a partial metabolic response. However, by December 2024, after bridging with R‐DHAP, PET imaging demonstrated disease progression with increased hepatic FDG activity and new pulmonary and marrow involvement.

Given the aggressive disease biology and progression on chemoimmunotherapy, the patient was referred for CAR T‐cell therapy. HIV viral load was undetectable at the time of leukapheresis. He subsequently underwent lymphodepleting chemotherapy (fludarabine with 20% dose reduction due to renal considerations, and cyclophosphamide), and on January 1, 2025, received axi‐cel. During the infusion, he developed an acute anaphylactic reaction approximately halfway through the product administration, with symptoms including hypotension, flushing, and respiratory compromise. The infusion was stopped immediately, and the patient was managed with epinephrine, antihistamines, and corticosteroids. He received approximately 50% of the intended CAR T‐cell dose. Post‐infusion, he experienced only Grade 1 CRS, with no evidence of ICANS.

The Day +30 PET‐CT scan (February 2, 2025) showed a partial metabolic response with marginal regression of the FDG‐avid hepatic lesions, though a retroperitoneal nodule demonstrated increased uptake. On Day +60 (March 16, 2025), repeat PET confirmed further marginal metabolic improvement in hepatic lesions and stability of the retroperitoneal finding, consistent with ongoing partial response (Deauville Score 4). The patient remained asymptomatic with no B symptoms or signs of progressive disease. HIV remained controlled, and cardiac function was preserved on echocardiography.

A subsequent PET‐CT scan on April 23, 2025 (Day +110) showed relative metabolic stability of the hepatic lesions when compared to earlier scans, with persistent SUVmax values (notably, segment 6/7 lesion with SUVmax 6.6 vs. 6.0 in February). Importantly, there were no new sites of FDG‐avid disease. A new focal FDG‐avid signal was observed in the proximal duodenum without any corresponding CT abnormality, deemed indeterminate and possibly inflammatory. Given the plateau in metabolic response and the unchanged hepatic SUV, the case was re‐discussed in the multidisciplinary lymphoma tumor board on April 28, 2025. The consensus from the MTD was that the patient had stable residual hepatic disease. Due to concern for potential residual viable tumor and to better characterize the nature of the persistent hepatic uptake, an abdominal MRI was recommended. The MRI abdomen with contrast, performed on June 25, 2025, showed multiple stable subcapsular hepatic lesions with imaging features suggestive of treated lymphoma and residual disease activity; tissue biopsy was recommended. The patient was referred to interventional radiology for image‐guided liver biopsy; however, he deferred the procedure. The patient's clinical condition remained stable, and no new systemic symptoms were reported. At a follow‐up Lymphoma MDT meeting on July 7, 2025, it was noted that the liver lesions remained stable on recent PET imaging, and the team recommended repeating the PET‐CT in 3–4 months for continued surveillance (Figure 1).

Discussion

CAR T‐cell therapy has transformed the therapeutic landscape for relapsed or refractory LBCL, offering high rates of durable remissions in patients who previously had limited treatment options. However, pivotal trials of CAR T‐cell therapies, including ZUMA‐1 and JULIET, systematically excluded people living with HIV (PLWH), leaving a critical gap in evidence for this immunocompromised subgroup. As a result, clinical experience with CAR T‐cell therapy in HIV‐positive patients remains limited to retrospective series and case reports. Our case contributes to this evolving literature by reporting a partial and sustained metabolic response in an HIV‐positive patient with high‐grade B‐cell lymphoma, despite the occurrence of a severe anaphylactic reaction that curtailed CAR T‐cell infusion to approximately 50% of the planned dose.

Anaphylactic reactions during CAR T‐cell infusion are exceptionally rare and not previously reported in pivotal clinical trials. In a recent case report by Morin et al., a 77‐year‐old man experienced grade V anaphylaxis with cardiac arrest within minutes of tisagenlecleucel administration, requiring intensive cardiopulmonary resuscitation. Post‐resuscitation analysis confirmed markedly elevated serum tryptase, supporting a mast cell‐mediated allergic mechanism [5]. A subsequent search of the US Food and Drug Administration Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) database revealed 12 cases of anaphylaxis across various CAR T products, with four associated with cardiac arrest. Notably, the most likely culprits include product‐related excipients such as DMSO, Dextran 40, or albumin rather than the CAR construct itself, especially when reactions occur upon first exposure.

Our patient similarly developed an acute anaphylactic reaction during the infusion of axi‐cel, with symptoms necessitating immediate cessation of infusion. While the exact mechanism remains unclear, the event was temporally related to product administration and was managed successfully with standard anaphylaxis protocol. Remarkably, the patient still demonstrated a detectable therapeutic response, achieving a partial metabolic remission on serial PET/CT imaging despite receiving only half of the intended dose. This suggests that even submaximal CAR T‐cell delivery may be sufficient to induce disease control in selected patients, possibly due to early expansion and persistence of the infused CAR T‐cell population.

Importantly, Maus et al. previously described the first case of IgE‐mediated anaphylaxis following CAR T‐cell infusion, in which a patient receiving mRNA‐electroporated mesothelin‐targeted CAR T cells developed cardiac arrest after the third infusion. The reaction was confirmed by elevated serum tryptase and attributed to murine‐derived scFv immunogenicity and an intermittent dosing schedule that permitted sensitization. This landmark observation underscores that murine‐based CAR constructs and dosing intervals may predispose to severe hypersensitivity, reinforcing the need for humanized CAR designs and careful infusion strategies, and it parallels the anaphylaxis observed in our case [6].

Beyond infusion‐related reactions, the broader question of CAR T‐cell therapy's feasibility in PLWH warrants attention. Historically excluded due to concerns about manufacturing failure, viral reactivation, and poor immune reconstitution, PLWH have increasingly been shown to tolerate CAR T‐cell therapy with outcomes comparable to HIV‐negative patients. In a recent review by Hattenhauer et al., six published cases of HIV‐positive patients with DLBCL treated with axi‐cel demonstrated an overall response rate of 67%, including three complete and one partial remission [2]. Importantly, all patients had suppressed viral loads, and manufacturing was successful even in individuals with low CD4 counts (as low as 52 cells/µL). Toxicities such as CRS and ICANS were manageable and did not appear more frequently than in the general population.

Our case further aligns with data from the DESCAR‐T registry, which represents the largest real‐world cohort of PLWH receiving CAR T‐cell therapy to date. In this French multicenter study of 24 patients with B‐cell lymphoma treated with axi‐cel, the overall response rate at 90 days was 50%, with a 42% complete remission rate. The 12‐month progression‐free and overall survival rates were 40% and 55%, respectively. CRS and ICANS rates were consistent with broader CAR T‐cell experience, with no unique safety signals related to HIV status [3]. These findings support the premise that PLWH with undetectable viral loads and adequate performance status should not be excluded from CAR T‐cell therapy.

Our patient had multiple factors complicating standard therapy, including CD4 lymphopenia that precluded rituximab during early chemotherapy cycles, and extensive extranodal disease with high CNS risk. Despite these high‐risk features, he responded to axi‐cel after failing multiple lines of therapy, including DA‐R‐EPOCH and dose‐adjusted R‐DHAP. The emergence of partial metabolic responses across three consecutive PET scans (Days +30, +60, and +110) suggests a plateau in disease activity, with no evidence of progression. This prolonged disease control, achieved with partial CAR T‐cell exposure, adds to the growing evidence that PLWH can derive meaningful benefit from CAR T‐cell therapy when appropriately selected.

Another relevant clinical consideration is the challenge of managing treatment‐related complications in PLWH. While our patient did not experience infectious complications or CAR T‐cell–related neurotoxicity, such risks are heightened in the immunocompromised population. Of note, two deaths in the DESCAR‐T registry were attributed to infectious and neurological complications (Pseudomonas sepsis and progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy), emphasizing the need for close post‐infusion surveillance and multidisciplinary care coordination in this setting. In our case, careful viral load monitoring, infectious prophylaxis, and ART continuation were instrumental in achieving a favorable outcome.

Finally, the underreporting of rare adverse events such as anaphylaxis during CAR T‐cell infusion underscores the importance of pharmacovigilance and comprehensive documentation. As access to CAR T‐cell therapy expands globally and into more diverse patient populations, including those with HIV, the emergence of uncommon toxicities must be anticipated. Manufacturers and investigators should consider modifications to infusion protocols, such as slower infusion rates or excipient removal strategies, particularly in patients with prior allergic histories or heightened immunologic risk.
