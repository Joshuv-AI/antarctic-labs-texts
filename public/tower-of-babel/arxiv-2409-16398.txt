shot noise in a phenomenological model of a marginal Fermi liquid
Yi-Ming Wu, Josephine J. Yu, S. Raghu

Abstract
The strange metal is a mysterious non-Fermi liquid which shows linear-in- T T resistivity behavior at finite temperatures, and, as found in recent experiment, vanishingly small shot noise in the linear-in- T T regime. Here, we investigate the shot noise of a strange metal based on a phenomenological model of marginal Fermi liquid (MFL), where fermions couple to some collective boson mode, leading to T T -linear scattering rate at finite T T . It is found that in the diffusive regime where the MFL scattering length is small compared to the system size, the shot noise vanishes, and the thermal noise becomes a temperature- and voltage-independent constant. Introducing additional impurity scattering increases the shot noise, and is probably consistent with the current experiment.

\mathbf{k}

\displaystyle\left(Z^{-1}\partial_{t}+\bm{v}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{r}}-\nabla_{\bm{r}}\tilde{V}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{k}}+\partial_{t}\text{Re}\Sigma^{R}\partial_{\omega}\right)F(\bm{r},t,\bm{k},\omega)

(1)

\displaystyle=I_{\text{col}}(\bm{r},t,\bm{k},\omega)

\displaystyle Z^{-1}

\displaystyle=1-\partial_{\omega}\text{Re}\Sigma^{R}(\bm{r},t,\bm{k},\omega),

(2)

\displaystyle\bm{v}_{\bm{k}}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\bm{k}}\epsilon_{\bm{k}},~\epsilon_{\bm{k}}=\epsilon_{\bm{k}}^{(0)}+\text{Re}\Sigma^{R}(\bm{r},t,\bm{k},\omega),

\displaystyle\tilde{V}

\displaystyle=V(\bm{r})+\text{Re}\Sigma^{R}(\bm{r},t,\bm{k},\omega),

Solving the Boltzmann equation in its full form is a hopeless endeavor, even with moderate Fermi liquid-like scattering. For non-Fermi liquids, additional complexity arises due to the singular fermion self energy. For instance, the quasiparticle residue

II.2
Caveats and assumptions

While a rigorous solution of the Boltzmann equation is impossible, we can make a series of assumptions to simplify the task. Here, we reiterate these common assumptions in the interest of clarity. Having disposed of these caveats, we then proceed with the results.

First of all, we will be interested in steady state solutions and will neglect the time-dependence of the distribution function. In this case, the singular behavior of
Z^{-1}
can be excluded.
This is a reasonable approximation akin to neglecting any transient behavior of the system in short times after the voltage bias has been applied.

Our next simplifying approximation is to neglect self-energy effects on the single-particle lifetime. This is usually dangerous for a more violent non-Fermi liquid, but for the MFL (as we will justify below), restricting our attention to Fermi surface with such an approximation can indeed produce linear-in-
T
resistivity, and we thus study the current noise within the same framework.
Mathematically, the approximation is reflected in the “on shell” condition for the distribution function, namely

F(\bm{r},\bm{k},\omega)\propto F(\bm{r},\bm{k})\delta(\omega-{\epsilon^{(0)}_{\bm{k}}}).

(3)

It is precisely this assumption that allows us to proceed with a semiclassical approach to solving the Boltzmann equation and eventually leads to a simple interpretation of the collision integral.

Under these two assumptions, the
Boltzmann steady-state kinetic equation governing the simplified distribution function
F(\bm{r},\bm{k})
is

(\bm{v}_{\bm{k}}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{r}}+\mathbf{E}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{k}})F(\bm{r},\bm{k})=I_{\text{col}}(\bm{r},\bm{k}),

(4)

where
\bm{v}_{\bm{k}}
is the velocity. We have further assumed that the position dependence for the self energy is weak here, such that only the electric field contributes to the potential.

Depending on the size of the system and the strength of the electric field, there exist two practical ways to proceed. If the system is large and the field is weak, as in the regime where linear response theory applies, one argues that the spatial dependence of the distribution function is weak, and thus the first term
\bm{v}_{\bm{k}}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{r}}
can be dropped
[
61
,
62
,
63
,
64
]
. However, in mesoscopic systems with large applied field, the fermion chemical potential can be better understood as a local quantity
\mu(\bm{r})
whose gradient is the electric field. The
\bm{k}
-dependence of
F(\bm{r},\bm{k})
through
\epsilon_{\bm{k}}
can now be understood as dependence on
\epsilon_{\bm{k}}-\mu(\bm{r})
. Therefore, the
\bm{E}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{k}}
operator can be absorbed into the first term
\bm{v}_{\bm{k}}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{r}}
if the spatial gradient is interpreted as a total derivative. In this way
\bm{E}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{k}}
is effectively dropped
[
65
,
66
,
67
,
68
]
. We adopt the second approach, as it is more relevant to the shot noise problem. We also note from our calculations that in both approaches, keeping both
\bm{v}_{\bm{k}}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{r}}
and
\bm{E}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{k}}
simultaneously leads to incorrect transport coefficients due to double counting.

Finally, we make a few assumptions related to the setup of the system. First, we shall ignore dependence on spatial components perpendicular to
\bm{E}
. For our purposes (see Fig.
1
), this means that we assume the distribution function is invariant along the
y
direction. Second, we assume that the small voltage bias is small enough (compared to the Fermi energy) such that the only charge carriers contributing to transport are close to the Fermi energy, so
v_{k}=v_{F}
.
With the assumptions described above, the Boltzmann equation takes the simpler form

v_{F}\frac{\partial F(\bm{r},\bm{k})}{\partial x}=I_{\text{col}}(\bm{r},\bm{k}).

(5)

subject to some proper boundary conditions which will be specified below.

II.3
Strategy and notation

Before embarking on applying the above mentioned formalism to our MFL model,
here we detail our strategy for solving the (simplified) Boltzmann equation (

the partial wave decomposition

F(\bm{r},\bm{k})=F_{s}(x,\epsilon)+\cos\theta_{\bm{k}}F_{p}(x,\epsilon)+...

(6)

where
\epsilon=\epsilon_{\bm{k}}
and all higher order partial waves are omitted. In this rewriting, we invoke the assumption that the system is invariant along the
y

F_{s}
and
F_{p}

x

The

\bm{k}\rightarrow-\bm{k}
), while the
p

\cos\theta_{\bm{k}}F_{p}
does not. Since the current in the
x

With this decomposition, the Boltzmann equation can be rewritten as two coupled equations. To this end, we substitute Eq.(
6

5

i) integrate over all
\theta_{\bm{k}}
and ii) multiply Eq.(
5

\cos\theta_{\bm{k}}
and then integrate over
\theta_{\bm{k}}

\displaystyle v_{F}\partial_{x}F_{p}(x,\epsilon)

\displaystyle=I_{\text{col},s},

(7)

\displaystyle v_{F}\partial_{x}F_{s}(x,\epsilon)

\displaystyle=I_{\text{col},p},

where the collision integrals are decomposed into partial wave components as well. This decomposition also makes clear the effects of different parts of the collision integral. In particular,
I_{\text{col},s}
corresponds to energy relaxation while
I_{\text{col},p}
corresponds to momentum relaxation. In Appendix
B

We will use the so-called relaxation time approximation to express
I_{\text{col},p}
in terms of a characteristic relaxation rate
\tau(x,\epsilon)

I_{\text{col},p}=-\frac{1}{\tau(x,\epsilon)}F_{p}(x,\epsilon),

(8)

In fact, as will be shown, this approximation can be derived when some proper ansatz for

has been used.
The scattering time
\tau(x,\epsilon)
is generically defined in terms of the distribution function and ultimately encodes the important information about various scattering mechanisms in the system. Under the assumption that there is no interference of scattering processes, the collision integrals and therefore scattering rates of different mechanisms should add.

There are two main ansatzes for the distribution function which are commonly used to solve the Boltzmann equation: the “local-temperature ansatz” and the “diffusion ansatz.” The first is a distribution function which takes the equilibrium form but with a spatially-varying temperature profile
[
32
]
:

F_{s}(x,\epsilon)=\tanh\frac{\epsilon+|e|Ex}{2T(x)}.

(9)

This approach is justified when the dominant scattering is inelastic, and the scattering length is much smaller compared to the system size
L
.
The second ansatz applies
when the scattering is dominated by elastic scattering, like in the strong impurity scattering case,
where the dynamics are diffusion-dominated
[
31
]
:

F_{s}(x,\epsilon)=\frac{1}{2}\left[F_{R}(\epsilon)+F_{L}(\epsilon)\right]+\frac{x}{L}\left[F_{R}(\epsilon)-F_{L}(\epsilon)\right].

(10)

In both ansatz, we will impose the following boundary condition

F_{s}(\pm L/2,\epsilon)=\tanh\frac{\epsilon\pm|e|V/2}{2T(x)}

(11)

In the first ansatz, the specific scattering mechanisms (encoded in the collision integral) will determine the temperature profile
T(x)
; in the second ansatz, they will determine the mean free path. Below we will use Eq.(
9
) for case where MFL scattering dominates, and use Eq.(
10
) for the case where impurity scattering dominates.

Regardless of which ansatz we use to find the distribution functions
F_{s}(x,\epsilon)

Our formalism of computing the average current and noise power is based on the non-equilibrium field theory, which is different from the Boltzmann-Langevin approach but yields the same results (see Appendix

For instance, the local current density is given by

III
Phenomenological model of a marginal Fermi liquid

Having established the formalism, we now focus on applying it to the MFL model to explain the observations reported in
[
17
]
.

Below, we describe this model and report its salient features, including the intermediate results necessary for computing the Fano factor.

III.1
Model of MFL

In the phenomenological MFL model, fermions couple to some bosonic mode whose spectral function exhibits frequency/temperature scaling:

B(\bm{q},\Omega)=\frac{1}{\pi\omega_{D}}\tanh\frac{\Omega}{2T}

(16)

where
\omega_{D}
is a characteristic frequency associated with the bosonic fluctuation spectrum.
We assume that the notion of “local quantum criticality” and neglect any singular momentum dependence of the boson fluctuation spectrum. As fermions near the Fermi surface scatter off of these bosonic modes, they acquire some nontrivial self energy correction even at the one-loop level. If we identify the imaginary part of the retarded self energy
\text{Im}\Sigma^{R}(\epsilon)
as
\frac{1}{2\tau_{\text{MFL}}}
, it is easy to see (as shown in Appendix
A
)

\tau_{\text{MFL}}(\epsilon)=\frac{1}{2g^{2}}\frac{1}{\epsilon}\tanh\frac{\epsilon}{2T}

(17)

where
g
is the effective MFL coupling strength.

At low frequencies
\epsilon\ll T
, the quasiparticle scattering rate
\tau_{\text{MFL}}^{-1}\sim T
is comparable to the quasiparticle energy, and long-lived quasiparticles cease to exist.
Though
\tau_{\text{MFL}}
is a single-particle property, we will soon find that the transport scattering rate
\tau_{\text{M}}
due to the MFL interaction is closely related.

In addition to the MFL scattering, we also assume some impurity scattering from a local disorder
V_{\text{dis}}(\bm{r})
, whose correlation is
\overline{V_{\text{dis}}(\bm{r})V_{\text{dis}}(\bm{r}^{\prime})}=g(\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime})
. In the self-consistent Born approximation, this disorder potential, after disorder average, gives rise to some finite self energy
i\frac{1}{2\tau_{\text{im}}}
, where the impurity scattering time
\tau_{\text{im}}
is determined by the correlation
g(\bm{r})
. The same
\tau_{\text{im}}
also determines the transport scattering rate that enters the collision integral.

III.2
Collision integrals and scattering time in the MFL

In the presence of both MFL scattering and impurity scattering, the total collision integral captures both effects, namely we have
I_{\text{col}}=I_{\text{im}}+I_{\text{MFL}}
. We leave the explicit forms of
I_{\text{im}}
and
I_{\text{MFL}}
in Appendix
B
, which, after the partial wave decomposition, become
I_{\text{col},s}=I_{\text{im},s}+I_{\text{MFL},s}
and
I_{\text{col},p}=I_{\text{im},p}+I_{\text{MFL},p}
where

I_{\text{im},s}=0,~~I_{\text{im},p}=-\frac{1}{\tau_{\text{im}}}F_{p}(x,\epsilon),

(18)

and

\displaystyle I_{\text{MFL},s}

\displaystyle=g^{2}\int d\epsilon^{\prime}\left\{[F_{s}(x,\epsilon^{\prime})-F_{s}(x,\epsilon)]\frac{}{}\right.

(19)

\displaystyle\left.-[1-F_{s}(x,\epsilon)F_{s}(x,\epsilon^{\prime})]\tanh\frac{\epsilon^{\prime}-\epsilon}{2T(x)}\right\},

\displaystyle I_{\text{MFL},p}

\displaystyle=g^{2}\int d\epsilon^{\prime}\left[F_{s}(x,\epsilon^{\prime})\tanh\frac{\epsilon^{\prime}-\epsilon}{2T(x)}-1\right]F_{p}(x,\epsilon).

In deriving
I_{\text{MFL}}
we have assumed that the bosons are also in local equilibrium such that the same local temperature
T(x)
defined for the fermions and also be defined for the bosons.

If the MFL scattering is turned off, i.e.
g=0
, the system becomes a conventional dirty metal. In this case, inserting Eq.(
18
) into Eq.(
7
) yields a simple diffusion equation for the

-wave component, namely
\partial_{x}^{2}F_{s}(x,\epsilon)=0
. The solution subject to proper boundary condition is exactly the second ansatz that we have discussed in Eq.(
10

\tau_{\text{im}}=\infty
, we will solve Eq.(
7
) with the first ansatz in Eq.(
9
). The advantage of the local temperature ansatz in this case becomes manifest after we substitute it into Eq.(

) and perform the integral,

I_{\text{MFL},s}=0,~~I_{\text{MFL},p}=-\frac{1}{\tau_{\text{M}}(x,\epsilon)}F_{p}(x,\epsilon),

(20)

where the MFL transport time is

\tau_{\text{M}}(x,\epsilon)=\frac{1}{2g^{2}(\epsilon+|e|Ex)}\tanh\frac{\epsilon+|e|Ex}{2T(x)}.

(21)

Eq.(
20
) is remarkable in the sense that, even if we started with some elastic scattering in which fermions indeed transfer energy, the resulting

still vanishes. In this way, we may think of the MFL scattering as sort of a temperature- and energy-dependent elastic impurity scattering. Moreover, comparing Eq.(

17

\tau_{\text{M}}(x,\epsilon)
can be simply obtained by replacing
\epsilon
in
\tau_{\text{MFL}}(\epsilon)
with
\epsilon+|e|Ex
and promoting
T
to
T(x)
.
The formal similarity between Eq.(
20
) and (
18
) allows for the definition of the total transport time

\tau^{-1}(x,\epsilon)=\tau_{\text{M}}^{-1}(x,\epsilon)+\tau_{\text{im}}^{-1},

(22)

such that
I_{\text{col},p}=-F_{p}(x,\epsilon)/\tau(x,\epsilon)
, which coincides with the relaxation time approximation commonly used for deriving Drude conductivity in a conventional dirty metal, and thus justifies our previous assumption in Eq.(

In fact,
I_{\text{col},s}=0
as inferred from Eq.(

and assume the bosons are also in local equilibrium with the same

[

\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\epsilon I_{\text{col},s}(x,\epsilon)=0,~\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\epsilon\epsilon I_{\text{col},s}(x,\epsilon)=0,

(23)

for every spatial position

IV
Results

We now present the solutions of the Boltzmann equation (
7
) and the resulting shot noise based on the formalism outlined above. Depending on the relative strength between the MFL scattering and the impurity scattering, three different cases will be discussed in order: i) the clean limit with only MFL scattering dominates, ii) when both MFL scattering and impurity scattering are present but the latter is treated as perturbation and iii) impurity scattering dominates and MFL scattering is treated as perturbation.

IV.1
Clean MFL

Solution of the differential equation in (

We first discuss the case when
\tau_{\text{im}}=\infty
such that
\tau(x,\epsilon)=\tau_{\text{M}}(x,\epsilon)
. Using Eq.(

in Eq.(

where we have introduced

In Fig.

for various ratios of

The peculiar property of MFL can be seen from the particular energy dependence of

where
\nu_{0}
is the density of states near Fermi level,

is the effective mass and

The fact that the total noise power depends on neither

\mathcal{S}_{\text{shot}}=\mathcal{S}(T_{0},V)-\mathcal{S}(T_{0},0)=0.

(29)

The vanishing of shot noise is purely due to the specific energy dependence of

and is therefore is a unique property of a MFL. Interestingly, the specific form of

\displaystyle=\frac{W}{L}\frac{7\zeta(3)}{4\pi^{2}g^{2}}\frac{ne^{2}}{m}\frac{V}{\overline{T}}=GV.

(30)

V
Conclusion and Discussion

In this paper, we investigate the intimate relation between the linear-in-
T
resistivity and the vanishing shot noise of a strange metal, inspired by the recent experiment on YRS. We consider a phenomenological marginal Fermi liquid model, which describes fermions coupled to a collective boson mode with
\Omega/T
scaling spectrum. We also include a disorder potential that becomes relevant at the lowest temperatures. Our approach is built on the non-equilibrium quantum field theory, in which the quantum Boltzmann equation is viewed as the saddle point equation, and fluctuations around the saddle point lead to current noise. We have demonstrated that this approach, in the simplest case with only impurity scattering (i.e. a conventional dirty metal), coincides with the Boltzmann-Langevin approach.
With several approximations and simplifications stated in Sec.
II
, we have derived and solved the Boltzmann equation in a more generic setting, and obtained the current noise for the three cases: 1) with only MFL scattering, 2) with large MFL scattering and small impurity scattering and 3) with large impurity scattering and small MFL scattering.

Our main result is given by Eq.(
28
) and (
29
): the total current noise power is a constant if the system is dominated by the MFL scattering, and therefore the shot noise vanishes. Adding impurity scattering perturbatively results in a finite but small temperature-dependent shot noise, as shown in the plot of Fano factor in Fig.
3
(b). If instead the system is dominated by the impurity scattering and the MFL scattering is treated as a perturbation, as is more relevant in the
T\to 0
limit, the results for shot noise are shown in Fig.
4
. In the limit when the MFL scattering is completely switched off, the shot noise reduces to Nagaev’s result and the Fano factor saturates to
1/3
as
T\to 0
. Introducing MFL scattering tends to reduce the Fano factor, but the amount of reduction depends on the strength of MFL coupling and is thus not universal.

We emphasize that the vanishing of shot noise is a unique feature of MFL scattering. Had we adopted another type of boson that leads to a more violent non-Fermi liquid behavior from the boson-fermion coupling, we would not obtain vanishing shot noise within the same theoretical treatment. Of course, that would also likely spoil the linear-in-
T
resistivity behavior. This perhaps unveils the fact that both linear-in-
T
resistivity and vanishing shot noise are two intimately related unique features of the strange metal phase.

Lastly, we note that our treatment of the Boltzmann equation relies on the on-shell approximation in Eq.(
3
). A more rigorous yet rather involved way is to keep the full non-Fermi liquid spectral function in the collision integral and all renormalization effects from the singular fermion self energy. Sorting out the contributions from these terms that have been neglected here, to our knowledge, is still an open problem for a general non-Fermi liquid.
It is also, in principle, straightforward to go beyond the accuracy of Boltzmann-Langevin and include higher order corrections to the noise power within the field-theoretic framework. We have outlined the derivation of higher order terms in Appendix
C.2
, A detailed discussion about these corrections is left for future studies.

Appendix A
MFL self energy at equilibrium

Here we demonstrate the calculation of MFL self energy in the absence of external fields, by using both the equilibrium Matsubara frequency formalism.
The one-loop self energy in the Matsubara frequency formalism is given by

\displaystyle\Sigma(\bm{k},i\epsilon_{n})=g_{0}^{2}T\sum_{m}\int\frac{d^{2}\bm{p}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{D(\bm{p}-\bm{k},i\epsilon_{m}-i\epsilon_{n})}{i\epsilon_{m}-\xi_{\bm{p}}}

(39)

\displaystyle=g_{0}^{2}T\sum_{m}\int\frac{d^{2}\bm{p}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\Omega\frac{-B(\Omega)}{\Omega-i\epsilon_{m}+i\epsilon_{n}}\frac{1}{i\epsilon_{m}-\xi_{\bm{p}}}

where
B(\Omega)
is the boson spectral function given in Eq.(
16
). After performing the frequency summation, we can replace
i\epsilon_{n}
with
\omega+i0^{+}
to obtain the retarded self energy,

\Sigma^{R}(\bm{k},\omega)=\frac{g_{0}^{2}\nu_{0}}{\pi\omega_{D}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\xi d\Omega\tanh\frac{\Omega}{2T}\frac{n_{F}(\xi)+n_{B}(\Omega)}{\xi-\Omega-\omega-i0^{+}}.

(40)

The imaginary part of
\Sigma^{R}(\bm{k},\omega)
is therefore easily obtained as

\displaystyle\text{Im}

\displaystyle\Sigma^{R}(\bm{k},\omega)=g^{2}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\Omega\tanh\frac{\Omega}{2T}\left[n_{F}(\omega+\Omega)+n_{B}(\Omega)\right]

(41)

\displaystyle=g^{2}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\Omega\tanh\frac{\Omega}{2T}\left[\coth\frac{\Omega}{2T}-\tanh\frac{\omega+\Omega}{2T}\right]

\displaystyle=g^{2}\omega\coth\frac{\omega}{2T}

where we have defined the dimensionless coupling constant
g^{2}=g_{0}^{2}\nu_{0}/(\pi\omega_{D})
. If we idenfity
\text{Im}\Sigma^{R}(\bm{k},\omega)
as
\frac{1}{2\tau_{\text{MFL}}}
, then the equilibrium MFL lifetime is

\tau_{\text{MFL}}=\frac{1}{2g^{2}}\frac{1}{\omega}\tanh\frac{\omega}{2T}

(42)

which is consistent with the non-equilbirum MFL transport time given in Eq.(
21
).

Appendix B
General formalism of kinetic theory

Our starting point is
a coupled boson-fermion problem, expressed in Keldysh fields, namely,

\displaystyle S_{\text{f}}

\displaystyle=\int d\eta\bar{\Psi}(\eta)\hat{G}_{0}^{-1}\Psi(\eta),

(43)

\displaystyle S_{\text{b}}

\displaystyle=\int d\eta d\eta^{\prime}\Phi(\eta)\hat{D}^{-1}(\eta,\eta^{\prime})\Phi(\eta^{\prime}),

\displaystyle S_{\text{int}}

\displaystyle=g_{0}\int d\eta\bar{\psi}_{a}(\eta)\gamma_{ab}^{\alpha}\psi_{b}(\eta)\phi^{\alpha}(\eta).

Here
\eta=(\bm{r},t)
,
a,b=1,2
and
\alpha=q,c
and the summation over repeated indices is assumed. The vertex matrices
\gamma^{\alpha}
are
\gamma^{c}=\sigma_{0}
, the
2\times 2
identity matrix, and
\gamma^{q}=\sigma_{1}
is the first Pauli matrix, and
\Psi=(\psi_{1},\psi_{2})^{T}
and
\Phi=(\phi^{c},\phi^{q})^{T}
are the two-component Keldysh fields for fermions and bosons respectively. The fermionic Green’s function (both bare and fully dressed) has the structure

\hat{G}=\begin{pmatrix}G^{R}&G^{K}\\
0&G^{A}\end{pmatrix},~~\hat{G}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}[G^{R}]^{-1}&[G^{-1}]^{K}\\
0&[G^{A}]^{-1}\end{pmatrix},

(44)

while the bosonic Green’s function is

\hat{D}=\begin{pmatrix}D^{K}&D^{R}\\
D^{A}&0\end{pmatrix},~~\hat{D}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&[D^{A}]^{-1}\\
[D^{R}]^{-1}&[D^{-1}]^{K}\end{pmatrix},

(45)

where the superscripts
R,A
and
K
stand for retarded, advanced and Keldysh components. For the fermion part,
G^{K}=G^{R}F-FG^{A}
where
F
is the generalized Fermi distribution function. And similarly
D^{K}=D^{R}F_{b}-F_{b}D^{A}
with
F_{b}
the generalized boson distribution function. With this definition it’s easy to see that
[G^{-1}]^{K}=-[G^{R}]^{-1}G^{K}[G^{A}]^{-1}=[G^{R}]^{-1}F-F[G^{A}]^{-1}
and similarly
[D^{-1}]^{K}=-[D^{R}]^{-1}D^{K}[D^{A}]^{-1}=[D^{R}]^{-1}F_{b}-F_{b}[D^{A}]^{-1}
.
In equilibrium, the fermion and boson distribution funciton take the familiar forms

\displaystyle F(\epsilon)

\displaystyle=1-2n_{F}(\epsilon)=\tanh\frac{\epsilon}{2T},

(46)

\displaystyle F_{b}(\epsilon)

\displaystyle=1+2n_{B}(\epsilon)=\coth\frac{\epsilon}{2T}

Note that in the fermion sector, we start with the free fermion action, for which
\hat{G}_{0}
is local in space. In the bosonic sector, however, we inherit the assumption that the Green’s function
\hat{D}(\eta,\eta^{\prime})
already contains dynamically generated retardation effect. This is different from the free boson Green’s function
\hat{D}_{0}
.

From Eq.(
43
), one can integrate out the bosons to obtain the fermion-only action

\displaystyle S_{\text{f}}

\displaystyle=\int d\eta\bar{\psi}_{a}(\eta)[\hat{G}_{0}^{-1}]_{ab}\psi_{b}(\eta)

(47)

\displaystyle-\frac{g_{0}^{2}}{4}\int dxdx^{\prime}\bar{\psi}_{a}(\eta)\gamma_{ab}^{\alpha}\psi_{b}(\eta)D_{\alpha\beta}(\eta,\eta^{\prime})\bar{\psi}_{c}(\eta^{\prime})\gamma^{\beta}_{cd}\psi_{d}(\eta^{\prime}).

We next introduce a fluctuating bilocal field
\tilde{G}_{ab}(\eta,\eta^{\prime})=-i\psi_{a}(\eta)\bar{\psi}_{b}(\eta^{\prime})
, and enforce this relation using a Lagrange multiplier field
\tilde{\Sigma}_{ab}(\eta,\eta^{\prime})
. In practice, this is to insert the resolution of identity

\displaystyle 1\equiv\int\mathcal{D}[\tilde{G},\tilde{\Sigma}]\exp

\displaystyle\left\{\int d\eta d\eta^{\prime}\tilde{\Sigma}_{ab}(\eta,\eta^{\prime})\right.

(48)

\displaystyle\left.\left[\tilde{G}_{ba}(\eta^{\prime},\eta)+i\psi_{b}(\eta^{\prime})\bar{\psi}_{a}(\eta)\right]\right\}.

The
\tilde{G}
-
\tilde{\Sigma}
only action can be obtained after further integrating out the fermions,

S[\tilde{G},\tilde{\Sigma}]=-i\text{Tr}\ln(1-\tilde{G}_{0}\tilde{\Sigma})-i\tilde{G}\tilde{\Sigma}-\frac{g_{0}^{2}}{4}(\tilde{G}\gamma^{\beta}\tilde{G}\gamma^{\alpha}D_{\alpha\beta}).

(49)

Note in the last part, the first
\tilde{G}
is
\tilde{G}(\eta,\eta^{\prime})
, while the second is
\tilde{G}(\eta^{\prime},\eta)
.
The Dyson equation and the consistent equation for the self energy are obtained by taking the saddle point of
S[\tilde{G},\tilde{\Sigma}]
:

\displaystyle\frac{\delta S}{\delta\tilde{\Sigma}}=0~\to~\hat{G}=(1-\hat{G}_{0}\hat{\Sigma})^{-1}\hat{G}_{0}=(\hat{G}_{0}^{-1}-\hat{\Sigma})^{-1}

(50)

\displaystyle\frac{\delta S}{\delta\tilde{G}}=0~\to~\hat{\Sigma}=i\frac{g_{0}^{2}}{2}\gamma^{\beta}\hat{G}\gamma^{\alpha}D_{\alpha\beta},

where
\hat{G}
and
\hat{\Sigma}
are the saddle point solutions of
\tilde{G}
and
\tilde{\Sigma}
.
From the first equation, we can see that
\hat{\Sigma}
has the same matrix structure as
\hat{G}^{-1}
, which we denote as

\hat{\Sigma}=\begin{pmatrix}\Sigma^{R}&\Sigma^{K}\\
0&\Sigma^{A}\end{pmatrix}.

(51)

With this the equations for the Green’s functions can be written as

\displaystyle{}^{-1}

\displaystyle=[G^{R}_{0}]^{-1}-\Sigma^{R},

(52)

\displaystyle[G^{A}]^{-1}

\displaystyle=[G^{A}_{0}]^{-1}-\Sigma^{A},

\displaystyle F[G^{A}_{0}]^{-1}-[G^{R}_{0}]^{-1}F

\displaystyle=\Sigma^{K}-(\Sigma^{R}F-F\Sigma^{A}).

In fact, for the free Green’s functions we can set
G_{0}^{R}=G_{0}^{A}=G_{0}
, then the last equation in above can be rewritten as

-[G_{0}^{-1},F]=\Sigma^{K}-(\Sigma^{R}F-F\Sigma^{A}).

(53)

All the operators in the above equation should be treated as a matrix and the operator product is implemented as the matrix product.

The equations for
\Sigma^{R},\Sigma^{A}
and
\Sigma^{K}
are obtained from the second equation, which are explicitly

\displaystyle\Sigma^{R}(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})=i\frac{g_{0}^{2}}{2}

\displaystyle\left(G^{R}(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})D^{K}(\eta_{2},\eta_{1})\right.

(54)

\displaystyle\left.+G^{K}(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})D^{A}(\eta_{2},\eta_{1})\right)

\displaystyle\Sigma^{A}(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})=i\frac{g_{0}^{2}}{2}

\displaystyle\left(G^{A}(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})D^{K}(\eta_{2},\eta_{1})\right.

\displaystyle\left.+G^{K}(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})D^{R}(\eta_{2},\eta_{1})\right)

\displaystyle\Sigma^{K}(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})=i\frac{g_{0}^{2}}{2}

\displaystyle\left[G^{K}(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})D^{K}(\eta_{2},\eta_{1})\right.

\displaystyle-\left(G^{R}(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})\right.-

\displaystyle\left.G^{A}(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})\right)\left(D^{R}(\eta_{2},\eta_{1})-D^{A}(\eta_{2},\eta_{1})\right)].

where in the last line the causality results in
G^{R}(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})D^{R}(\eta_{2},\eta_{1})=G^{A}(\eta_{1},\eta_{2})D^{A}(\eta_{2},\eta_{1})=0
.

When the system is exposed to some static electric field, the fermion density couples to the scalar field
V(\eta)\equiv V(\bm{r})
. Passing this field to the Keldysh contour and assuming the potential is identical along the forward and backward path, the only relevant fields is the classical component
V^{c}(\eta)=\frac{1}{2}(V^{+}(\eta)+V^{-}(\eta))
. The quantum component
V^{q}(\eta)=\frac{1}{2}(V^{+}(\eta)-V^{-}(\eta))
vanishes, although it can be kept nonzero in order to generate density correlation functions before setting to zero at the end of calculation. With this potential, and assuming the quadratic dispersion, the free fermion inverse Green’s functions are given by

[G^{R(A)}_{0}]^{-1}=i\partial_{t}-\frac{(-i\nabla_{\bm{r}})^{2}}{2m}-V^{c}(\bm{r})\pm i0^{+}.

(55)

We are now in a position to solve the coupled equations Eq.(
52
) and Eq.(
54
) perturbatively. Eq.(
53
) after Wigner transform becomes

\displaystyle-i

\displaystyle\left[\partial_{t}+\bm{v}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{r}}-\left(\nabla_{\bm{r}}V^{c}(\bm{r})\right)\cdot\nabla_{\bm{k}}\right]F(\eta,k)

(56)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Sigma^{K}(\eta,k)-2i\text{Im}\Sigma^{R}(\eta,k)F(\eta,k)

\displaystyle-i\left(\nabla_{\bm{r}}\text{Re}\Sigma^{R}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{k}}-\partial_{t}\text{Re}\Sigma^{R}\partial_{\omega}\right.

\displaystyle\left.-\nabla_{\bm{k}}\text{Re}\Sigma^{R}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{r}}+\partial_{\omega}\text{Re}\Sigma^{R}\partial_{t}\right)F(\eta,k).

where we remind that
\eta=(\bm{r},t)
and
k=(\bm{k},\omega)
.
In a Fermi liquid system, one argues that the distribution function
F(\eta,k)
can be approximated by
F(\bm{r},t,\bm{k})
, with the frequency peaked at
\omega=\epsilon_{\bm{k}}+V^{r}(\bm{r})+\text{Re}\Sigma^{R}(\eta,k)
. This “mass-shell” argument depends on the existence of well-defined quasi-particles. Hence in our case of non-Fermi liquid, abandoning the
\omega
-dependence is not justified and we will keep the full
\omega
-dependence of
F
. We can introduce

\displaystyle Z^{-1}(\eta,k)

\displaystyle=1-\partial_{\omega}\text{Re}\Sigma^{R}(\eta,k)

(57)

\displaystyle\bm{v}^{*}(\eta,k)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\bm{k}}[\epsilon_{\bm{k}}+\text{Re}\Sigma^{R}(\eta,k)]

\displaystyle\tilde{V}(\eta,k)

\displaystyle=V^{c}(\bm{r})+\text{Re}\Sigma^{R}(\eta,k)

and rewrite Eq.(
58
) as follows

\left(Z^{-1}\partial_{t}+\bm{v}^{*}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{r}}-\nabla_{\bm{r}}\tilde{V}\cdot\nabla_{\bm{k}}+\partial_{t}\text{Re}\Sigma^{R}\partial_{\omega}\right)F(\eta,k)=I_{\text{col}},

(58)

and the right hand side is defined as the collision integral
I_{\text{col}}=i\Sigma^{K}(\eta,k)+2\text{Im}\Sigma^{R}(\eta,k)F(\eta,k)
, which is evaluated as

\displaystyle I_{\text{col}}=-\frac{g_{0}^{2}}{2}

\displaystyle\left\{\int_{p}[G^{R}(\eta,p)-G^{A}(\eta,p)][F(\eta,p)-F(\eta,k)]D^{K}(\eta,p-k)\right.

(59)

\displaystyle-\left.\int_{p}[G^{R}(\eta,p)-G^{A}(\eta,p)][1-F(\eta,p)F(\eta,k)][D^{R}(\eta,p-k)-D^{A}(\eta,p-k)]\right\}

If we define

G^{R}(\eta,k)-G^{A}(\eta,k)=-i2\pi A(\eta,k),~~D^{R}(\eta,q)-D^{A}(\eta,q)=-i2\pi B(\eta,q)

(60)

where
A
and
B
are the fermion and boson spectral functions respectively, we can write the above equation as

\displaystyle I_{\text{col}}(\eta,k)={2}\pi^{2}g_{0}^{2}

\displaystyle\int_{p}A(\eta,p)\left\{[F(\eta,p)-F(\eta,k)]F_{b}(\eta,p-k)-[1-F(\eta,p)F(\eta,k)]\right\}B(\eta,p-k)

(61)

Below we discuss, based on a few examples, about the explicit form of the collision integral in Eq.(
59
) or Eq.(
61
). Like in the main text, we will be seeking for the steady state solution of the distribution functions, which are thus
t
-independent. We also assume, in the presence of the electric field pointing towards
x
-direction, the distribution functions therefore depend only on
x
so the notation
\eta
is reduced to
x
and
F(\eta,k)\to F(x,k)
.

B.1
impurity scattering

Here we consider a very simple model: free electrons moving in an electric field and getting scattered by impurities. The impurity is described by a purely classical disorder potential
V_{dis}(\bm{r})
, which we assume to have zero mean and the following correlation

\overline{V_{dis}(\bm{r})V_{dis}(\bm{r}^{\prime})}=g(\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime}).

(62)

This is obtained from assuming the disorder distribution function to be

P[V]=\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\int d\bm{r}d\bm{r}^{\prime}V_{dis}(\bm{r})g^{-1}(\bm{r}-\bm{r}^{\prime})V_{dis}(\bm{r}^{\prime})\right].

(63)

Since we only consider the classical component of
V_{dis}
, this correlator plays the role of
D^{K}
in Eq.(
59
), and other components
D^{R(A)}
are absent. As a result, the collision integral from this model is

I_{\text{im}}(x,\bm{k})=2\pi\int_{\bm{p}}g(\bm{k}-\bm{p})\left[F(x,\bm{p})-F(x,\bm{k})\right]\delta(\epsilon_{\bm{p}}-\epsilon_{\bm{k}}).

(64)

Here we have used the on-shell approximation for
F(x,k)
, meaning
F(x,k)=F(x,\bm{k})\delta(\omega-\epsilon_{\bm{k}})
. The delta-function is then from the fact that the impurity scattering is elastic which does not transfer energy.

Next, we make use of Eq.(
6
) and write
F(x,\bm{k})
in terms of
F_{s}(x,\epsilon)
and
F_{p}(x,\epsilon)
, and define

I_{\text{im},s}=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{d\theta_{\bm{k}}}{2\pi}I_{\text{im}}(x,\bm{k}),~I_{\text{im},p}=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{d\theta_{\bm{k}}}{2\pi}I_{\text{im}}(x,\bm{k})\cos\theta_{\bm{k}}.

(65)

A straightforward calculation then shows,

I_{\text{im},s}=0,~~I_{\text{im},p}=-\frac{1}{\tau_{\text{im}}}F_{p}(x,\epsilon),

(66)

where the impurity scattering time is defined as

\frac{1}{\tau_{\text{im}}}=\nu_{0}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}d\theta g(\theta)(1-\cos\theta).

(67)

B.2
electron-electron scattering

The collision integral from electron-electron scattering vanishes if we use the bare electron interaction in Eq.(
59
). The familar expression for the electron-electron collision integral is obtained by using the RPA dressed interaction in Eq.(
59
). Let’s assume the bare, short range electron-electron interaction is
U
, and the boson mode is introduced via the Hubbard-Stratonovich transformation. We use the convention that the boson-fermion coupling constant is
g_{0}=1
such that the bare boson kernel is
\hat{D}_{0}^{-1}=U^{-1}\sigma_{1}
. The RPA boson Green’s funciton is then

\hat{D}^{-1}=\hat{D}_{0}^{-1}+\hat{\Pi}_{0},

(68)

where
\hat{\Pi}_{0}
is the one-loop fermionic bubble. Since
\hat{\Pi}_{0}
plays the same role as the boson self energy, like in Eq.(
51
) we can write it as

\hat{\Pi}_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\Pi_{0}^{A}\\
\Pi_{0}^{R}&\Pi_{0}^{K}\end{pmatrix}

(69)

where the regarded, advanced and Keldysh bubbles are (we have suppressed the spatial dependence)

\displaystyle\Pi_{0}^{R(A)}(\bm{q},\omega)=-\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{d^{2}\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{F(\bm{k}+\bm{q})-F(\bm{k})}{\omega+\epsilon_{\bm{k}}-\epsilon_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}}\pm i0^{+}},

(70)

\displaystyle\Pi_{0}^{K}(\bm{q},\omega)=-i\pi\int\frac{d^{2}\bm{k}}{(2\pi)^{2}}[F(\bm{k})F(\bm{k}+\bm{q})-1]\delta(\omega+\epsilon_{\bm{k}}-\epsilon_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}}).

Inverting Eq.(
68
), one obtains for the retarded, advanced and Keldysh Green’s functions

\displaystyle D^{R(A)}

\displaystyle=(U^{-1}+\Pi_{0}^{R(A)})^{-1}

(71)

\displaystyle D^{K}

\displaystyle=-D^{R}\Pi_{0}^{K}D^{A}.

These relations, after Wigner transform, lead to

\displaystyle D^{K}(q)

\displaystyle=i\pi|D^{R}(q)|^{2}\int_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}[F(\bm{k}^{\prime})F(\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q})-1]\delta(\omega+\epsilon_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}-\epsilon_{\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q}}),

(72)

\displaystyle D^{R}(q)-D^{A}(q)

\displaystyle=-i\pi|D^{R}(q)|^{2}\int_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}[F(\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q})-F(\bm{k}^{\prime})]\delta(\omega+\epsilon_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}-\epsilon_{\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q}}).

Substituting this into Eq.(
59
), and make use of the on-shell approximation for the distribution functions, as well as Fermi liquid like fermion spectral function, we obtain

\displaystyle I_{ee}=-\frac{\pi}{2}\int_{\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{q}}|D^{R}(\bm{q},\epsilon_{\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q}}-\epsilon_{\bm{k}^{\prime}})|^{2}

\displaystyle\left\{[F(\bm{k}+\bm{q})-F(\bm{k})][F(\bm{k}^{\prime})F(\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q})-1]-[F(\bm{k}+\bm{q})F(\bm{k})-1][F(\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q})-F(\bm{k}^{\prime})]\right\}

(73)

\displaystyle\times\delta(\epsilon_{\bm{k}}-\epsilon_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}}-\epsilon_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}+\epsilon_{\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q}}).

Using the relation
F=1-2n_{F}
, it is easy to see that the collision integral can be rewritten as

\displaystyle I_{ee}=-4\pi\int_{\bm{k}^{\prime},\bm{q}}|D^{R}(\bm{q},\epsilon_{\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q}}-\epsilon_{\bm{k}^{\prime}})|^{2}

\displaystyle\left\{n_{F}(\bm{k}^{\prime})n_{F}(\bm{k}+\bm{q})[1-n_{F}(\bm{k})][1-n_{F}(\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q})]\right.

(74)

\displaystyle-n_{F}(\bm{k})n_{F}(\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q})[1-n_{F}(\bm{k}^{\prime})][1-n_{F}(\bm{k}+\bm{q})]\}\times\delta(\epsilon_{\bm{k}}-\epsilon_{\bm{k}+\bm{q}}-\epsilon_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}+\epsilon_{\bm{k}^{\prime}+\bm{q}}).

which is the well known formula for the e-e scattering.

B.3
MFL scattering

As the last example, we discuss how the MFL scattering due to the coupling to the boson mode in Eq.(

\displaystyle I_{\text{MFL}}={{2}\pi g^{2}}

\displaystyle\int\frac{d\epsilon_{\bm{p}}d\theta_{\bm{p}}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\left\{\left[F(\bm{r},\bm{p})-F(\bm{r},\bm{k})\right]\right.

(77)

\displaystyle-\left.\left[1-F(\bm{r},\bm{p})F(\bm{r},\bm{k})\right]\tanh\frac{\epsilon_{\bm{p}}-\epsilon_{\bm{k}}}{2T(x)}\right\},

where the dimensionless coupling constant
g^{2}

41

Appendix C
Field-theoretic description of current and noise

To study transport properties of the model discussed above, we first obtain the current operator. For simplicity, we keep assuming the quadratic dispersion in Eq.(
55
). In this case, introducing coupling to external gauge field is simply replacing
-i\nabla_{\bm{r}}
with
-i\nabla_{\bm{r}}-e\bm{A}
. If we focus on the paramagnetic contribution to the current, it amounts to changing
G_{0}^{-1}
to
G_{0}^{-1}+e\bm{v}\cdot\bm{A}
where
\hat{\bm{v}}=-i\nabla_{\bm{r}}/m
. To get the current operator and its correlations, we consider the quantum component of this vector potential, which couples to the fermion fields as

S_{A}=\int dx\bar{\psi}_{a}(x)\gamma_{ab}^{q}\psi_{b}(x)e\bm{v}\cdot\bm{A}^{q}(x).

(78)

This is nothing but the vector potential couples linearly to the fluctuating field
\tilde{G}
.
After integrating out the fermions, we obtain

\displaystyle S_{A}[\tilde{G},\tilde{\Sigma}]

\displaystyle=S[\tilde{G},\tilde{\Sigma}]-i\text{Tr}\ln\left(1+(G_{0}^{-1}-\tilde{\Sigma})^{-1}\gamma^{q}e\bm{v}\cdot\bm{A}^{q}\right)

(79)

\displaystyle\approx S[\tilde{G},\tilde{\Sigma}]-i\text{Tr}\ln\left(1+\tilde{G}\gamma^{q}e\bm{v}\cdot\bm{A}^{q}\right)

\displaystyle=S[\tilde{G},\tilde{\Sigma}]-i\text{Tr}\left(\tilde{G}\gamma^{q}e\bm{v}\cdot\bm{A}^{q}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{i}{2}\text{Tr}\left(\tilde{G}\gamma^{q}e\bm{v}\cdot\bm{A}^{q}\tilde{G}\gamma^{q}e\bm{v}\cdot\bm{A}^{q}\right)

where
S[\tilde{G},\tilde{\Sigma}]
is given in Eq.(
49
), and in the second line we have used the saddle point condition that leads to Dyson’s equation. In general
\tilde{G}
and
\tilde{\Sigma}
fluctuate independently, However, once we demand
\delta S[\tilde{G},\tilde{\Sigma}]/\delta\tilde{\Sigma}=0
, which leads to the Dyson’s equation, the fluctuations of
\tilde{G}
and
\tilde{\Sigma}
are pinned together. As a result, only fluctuations of
\tilde{G}
need to be considered in the following analysis. From the above equation we can identify the current operators as

\bm{J}(x):=\frac{1}{2}\left.\frac{\delta S_{A}}{\delta\bm{A}^{q}(x)}\right|_{\bm{A}^{q}=0}=-i\frac{e}{2}\text{Tr}(\tilde{G}(x,x^{\prime})\gamma^{q}\bm{v})|_{x=x^{\prime}}.

(80)

Since
\tilde{G}
is a fluctuating field, so is the current operator.
Near the saddle point, we can write
\tilde{G}=\hat{G}+\delta G
where
\hat{G}
is the saddle point Green’s function, and
\delta G
accounts for fluctuations. It is this fluctuation
\delta{G}
that leads to the noises of the current.

C.1
Fluctuations above the saddle point

To study the noise effect of the current, we need to rewrite the
\tilde{G}
-
\tilde{\Sigma}
action in terms of the fluctuation fields. We adopt the convention by Kamenev
[
68
]
that

\displaystyle\tilde{G}=\mathcal{U}^{-1}\circ\begin{pmatrix}G^{R}&0\\
i\delta_{G}&G^{A}\end{pmatrix}\circ\mathcal{U},

(81)

\displaystyle\tilde{\Sigma}=\mathcal{U}^{-1}\circ\begin{pmatrix}\Sigma^{R}&\Sigma^{K}-\Sigma^{R}\circ F+F\circ\Sigma^{A}\\
i\delta_{\Sigma}&\Sigma^{A}\end{pmatrix}\circ\mathcal{U}

where

\mathcal{U}=\begin{pmatrix}1&F\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},~~\mathcal{U}^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&-F\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}.

(82)

So we have, for example,

\displaystyle\tilde{G}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}G^{R}-iF\circ\delta_{G}&G^{R}\circ F-F\circ G^{A}-iF\circ\delta_{G}\circ F\\
i\delta_{G}&i\delta_{G}\circ F+G^{A}\end{pmatrix}

(83)

\displaystyle=\hat{G}+i\begin{pmatrix}-F\circ\delta_{G}&-F\circ\delta_{G}\circ F\\
\delta_{G}&\delta_{G}\circ F\end{pmatrix}.

With this definition, Eq.(
49
) can be rewritten in terms of
\delta_{G}
and
\delta_{\Sigma}
fields, and the functional integral is now over these new fluctuating fields. Expanding the new action to Gaussian level,
S[\delta_{G},\delta_{\Sigma}]
contains two part. The first part contains linear in
\delta_{G}
and
\delta_{\Sigma}
terms. Requiring
\frac{\delta S}{\delta[\delta_{G,\Sigma}]}|_{\delta_{G,\Sigma}=0}=0
reproduces the above equations in Eq.(
50
). So these linear terms vanish at the saddle point. The second part, which is relevant for fluctuations, is quadratic in
\delta_{G}
(note there are no terms containing
\delta_{\Sigma}^{2}
or
\delta_{G}\delta_{\Sigma}
under this convention). After Wigner transform it is given by

iS[\delta_{G}]=\frac{g^{2}}{4}\int dxdkdp\delta_{G}(x,k)K(x,k,p)\delta_{G}(x,p)

(84)

where

K(x,k,p)=i[F(x,k)-F(x,p)]\left\{[F(x,p)-F(x,k)]D^{K}(x,p-k)+[F(x,k)F(x,p)-1](D^{R}(x,p-k)-D^{A}(x,p-k))\right\}.

(85)

In the case of MFL, the bosonic Green’s functions are given by Eqs.(
16
),(
60
) and (
75
) and thus

K(x,k,p)=2\nu_{0}[F(x,k)-F(x,p)]\left\{[F(x,p)-F(x,k)]+[F(x,k)F(x,p)-1]\tanh\frac{\omega^{\prime}-\omega}{2T}\right\}.

(86)

Note if
F
is the equilibrium distribution function, this kernel
K
vanishes identically.
The non-equilibrium distribution function
F(x,k)
is solved from the saddle point equations, in particular, Eq.(
61
). For this Gaussian fluctuations, the average
\braket{\delta_{G}}=0
, and the correlation
\braket{\delta_{G}(x,k)\delta_{G}(x^{\prime},p)}
is determined by the inverse of
K
. Since
K
vanishes at equilibrium,
\delta_{G}
-correlation contains a singular piece which requires care in practice.

The Gaussian fluctuations of
\delta_{G}
can be further decomposed to linear level by introducing a noise field
\xi
via Hubbard-Stratonovich transformation, resulting in a term like
\delta_{G}\xi
. This Gaussian noise field has zero mean and its correlation is given by
K(x,k,p)
. And it enters the saddle point equation, making Eq.(
61
) into the Boltzmann-Langevin (BL) equation. One could in principle solve the BL equation to obtain the current fluctuations.

Here we avoid introducing the noise field. Instead, we express the current in terms of the fluctuating field
\tilde{G}
as in Eq.(
80
). It can be also written in terms of
\delta_{G}
,

\displaystyle\bm{J}(x)

\displaystyle=-i\frac{e}{2}\lim_{x\to x^{\prime}}\left(G^{R}\circ F-F\circ G^{A}-iF\circ\delta_{G}\circ F+i\delta_{G}\right)\bm{v}

(87)

\displaystyle=-\pi e\int_{k}A(x,k)F(x,k)\bm{v}_{\bm{k}}+\frac{e}{2}\int_{k}[1-F^{2}(x,k)]\delta_{G}(x,k)\bm{v}_{\bm{k}}.

From this, it is easy to see that the average current is

\braket{\bm{J}(x)}:=-\left.\frac{i}{2}\frac{\delta\ln Z[\bm{A}]}{\delta\bm{A}^{q}(x)}\right|_{\bm{A}^{q}\to 0}=-\pi e\int_{k}A(x,k)F(x,k)\bm{v}_{\bm{k}}

(88)

due to the fact that
\braket{\delta_{G}}=0
. For a Fermi liquid in a steady (DC) external field,

so the above expression reduces to

\braket{\bm{J}(x)}=-\frac{e}{2}\sum_{\bm{k}}F(x,\bm{k})\bm{v}_{\bm{k}},

(89)

which is the well known result for Fermi liquid. From this it is clear that to induce a finite current, the inversion symmetry must be broken, otherwise the momentum summation leads to zero.
For non-Fermi liquid, however, we have to keep the full frequency dependence of
A(x,k)
and
F(x,k)
.

As an example, let’s try to calculate the average current density for the MFL scattering. Using Eq.(

\displaystyle\braket{\bm{J}(x)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{e\nu_{0}}{2}\int d\epsilon\int\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}(F_{s}+\cos\theta F_{p})v_{F}\cos\theta

(90)

\displaystyle=-\frac{e\nu_{0}v_{F}}{4}\int d\epsilon F_{p}(x,\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\frac{e\nu_{0}v_{F}^{2}}{4}\int d\epsilon\tau_{ee}(x,\epsilon)\partial_{x}F_{s}(x,\epsilon).

With the assumption of
F_{s}(x,\epsilon)

the average current is given by

\displaystyle\braket{\bm{J}(x)}=\frac{e\nu_{0}v_{F}^{2}}{4}

\displaystyle\int d\epsilon\tau(x,\epsilon)\text{sech}^{2}\frac{\epsilon-\mu(x)}{2T(x)}\times

(91)

\displaystyle\left[\frac{eE}{2T(x)}-\frac{\epsilon-\mu(x)}{2T^{2}(x)}\frac{\partial T(x)}{\partial x}\right].

For fixed
x

\epsilon
. If
\tau_{ee}

\braket{\bm{J}}=\sigma E
where
\sigma=ne^{2}\tau/m
. With MFL scattering time, we obtain

\braket{\bm{J}}=\frac{7\zeta(3)e^{2}v_{F}^{2}\nu_{0}D_{0}}{4\pi^{2}}\frac{E}{\bar{T}}\equiv\frac{ne^{2}\tau(\bar{T})}{m}E,

(92)

where we have defined
\bar{T}
as some average temperature,

{\bar{T}}^{-1}=\frac{1}{L}\int_{-L/2}^{L/2}dx\frac{1}{T(x)}.

(93)

Note that this
\bar{T}
is different from
T_{0}
and the deviation is set by the electric field

. Keeping such deviations means we go beyond the linear response regime.
In Fig.
5

we show the properly normalized resistivity as a function of temperature. We see that as long as the temperature is not vanishingly small compare to the applied voltage,
\rho(T_{0})
shows linear dependence on

, the environment temperature.

C.2
Current noise

We define the current fluctuation as the spatially averaged one, which is given by

\delta I(t)=\frac{1}{\Omega}\int d\bm{r}\delta\bm{J}(\bm{r},t)\cdot\Delta S

The noise power at temperature

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(T,V)

\displaystyle=\int dt\braket{\delta I(t)\delta I(0)+\delta I(0)\delta I(t)}

(95)

\displaystyle=-\frac{W^{2}}{2\Omega^{2}}\int d\bm{r}d\bm{r}^{\prime}dt\left.\frac{\delta^{2}\ln Z[\bm{A}]}{\delta\bm{A}^{q}(x)\delta\bm{A}^{q}(x^{\prime})}\right|_{\bm{A}^{q}\to 0}

Using the expression for

introduced above, it is easy to see that

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(T,V)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2}W^{2}}{2\Omega^{2}}\int d\bm{r}d\bm{r}^{\prime}dt\left\{\braket{\text{Tr}\left[\tilde{G}(x,x^{\prime})\gamma^{q}\bm{v}\tilde{G}(x^{\prime},x)\gamma^{q}\bm{v}\right]}-\braket{\text{Tr}\left[\tilde{G}(x,x)\gamma^{q}\bm{v}\right]\text{Tr}\left[\tilde{G}(x^{\prime},x^{\prime})\gamma^{q}\bm{v}\right]}+\braket{\text{Tr}\left[\tilde{G}(x,x)\gamma^{q}\bm{v}\right]}^{2}\right\}.

(96)

Making use of Eq.(
80
), we can also rewrite the above expression as

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(T,V)

\displaystyle=\frac{W^{2}}{4\Omega^{2}}\int d\bm{r}d\bm{r}^{\prime}dt\left\{e^{2}\braket{\text{Tr}\left[\tilde{G}(x,x^{\prime})\gamma^{q}\bm{v}\tilde{G}(x^{\prime},x)\gamma^{q}\bm{v}\right]}+4\braket{\bm{J}(x)\bm{J}(x^{\prime})}-4\braket{\bm{J}}^{2}\right\}.

(97)

The first term in Eq.(
96
) (contains both JN noise and shot noise as we will see below) can be evaluated as

\displaystyle\int d\bm{r}d\bm{r}^{\prime}dt\braket{\text{Tr}\left[\tilde{G}(x,x^{\prime})\gamma^{q}\bm{v}\tilde{G}(x^{\prime},x)\gamma^{q}\bm{v}\right]}

(98)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d\bm{r}\sum_{k}\bm{v}_{\bm{k}}^{2}[F^{2}(x,k)-1][G^{R}(x,k)-G^{A}(x,k)]^{2}-\int d\bm{r}\sum_{k}\bm{v}_{\bm{k}}^{2}[F^{2}(x,k)-1]^{2}\braket{\delta_{G}(x,k)\delta_{G}(x,k)}.

Here the integration over
t
is equivalent to integrate over the relative time
t-t^{\prime}
and thus restrict the two frequencies in
F(x,k)
and
F(x^{\prime},p)
to be the same. Similar facts also applies to the space integration. In the last line
d\bm{r}
should be understood as the center of mass coordinate.

The last two terms in Eq.(
96
) lead to

\displaystyle\int d\bm{r}d\bm{r}^{\prime}dt\left\{-\braket{\text{Tr}\left[\tilde{G}(x,x)\gamma^{q}\bm{v}\right]\text{Tr}\left[\tilde{G}(x^{\prime},x^{\prime})\gamma^{q}\bm{v}\right]}+\braket{\text{Tr}\left[\tilde{G}(x,x)\gamma^{q}\bm{v}\right]}^{2}\right\}

(99)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int d\bm{r}d\bm{r}^{\prime}dt\sum_{p,k}\bm{v}_{\bm{k}}\bm{v}_{\bm{p}}[1-F^{2}(x,k)][1-F^{2}(x^{\prime},p)]\braket{\delta_{G}(x,k)\delta_{G}(x^{\prime},p)}.

It is apparent that in Eq.(
99
) the contribution at
k=p
is canceled by the second part of Eq.(
98
), so the total result is well regularized and we only need to consider the contributions with
k\neq p
in Eq.(
99
). Therefore, the total noise power can be calculated as

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}(T,V)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2}W^{2}}{2\Omega^{2}}\int d\bm{r}\sum_{k}\bm{v}_{\bm{k}}^{2}[F^{2}(x,k)-1][G^{R}(x,k)-G^{A}(x,k)]^{2}

(100)

\displaystyle+\frac{e^{2}W^{2}}{2\Omega^{2}}\int d\bm{r}d\bm{r}^{\prime}dt\sum_{p,k}\bm{v}_{\bm{k}}\bm{v}_{\bm{p}}[1-F^{2}(x,k)][1-F^{2}(x^{\prime},p)]\braket{\delta_{G}(x,k)\delta_{G}(x^{\prime},p)}

There are two contributions in this expression: the first one is the leading contribution and includes to Nagaev’s treatment based on Boltzmann-Langivan formalism. To see this in a simple dirty Fermi gas system, one realizes that
G^{R}=[\omega-\epsilon+i\frac{1}{2\tau_{\text{im}}}]^{-1}
and hence

\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}(G^{R}-G^{A})^{2}=-2\tau_{\text{im}}.

(101)

If we approximate
F(x,k)
by
F_{s}(x,k)
which is identical to
1-2n_{F}(x,k)
then the leading contribution reduces to
[
31
]

\mathcal{S}=\frac{2e^{2}v_{F}^{2}\nu_{0}W}{L}\int_{-\frac{1}{2}}^{\frac{1}{2}}\frac{dx}{L}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}d\epsilon n_{F}(x,\epsilon)[1-n_{F}(x,\epsilon)]\tau_{\text{im}}.

(102)

For the MFL scattering, or more generally, any scattering that has dependence on energy, to the leading accuracy one modifies the scattering time in the integrand properly and arrives at Eq.(
14
). Of course there are corrections, including the second line of Eq.(
100
), which are not considered here.
