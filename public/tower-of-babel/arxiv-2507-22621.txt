Configurational density of states of finite classical systems
Sergio Davis, Boris Maulén

Abstract
In this work, using a microcanonical framework, we provide an explicit inversion formula that allows the calculation of the configurational density of states from the total density of states without resorting to the inversion of the Laplace transform. From this formula, several results can be obtained for the thermodynamics of classical systems composed of a few degrees of freedom, while at the same time recovering the well-known behavior in the thermodynamic limit.



1
Introduction

The configurational density of states (CDOS) contains all the information from the interaction potential that is relevant to the thermodynamical properties. Actual computation of the CDOS, however, is a
challenge for most interaction potentials in statistical mechanics. Monte Carlo methods such as Wang-Landau simulation
[
1
,
2
]
among others
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]
allow for the determination of the CDOS but are sometimes limited by its high computational cost.

In this work, we show that precise knowledge of the total density of states is sufficient to recover the CDOS. We present an explicit formula that derives the CDOS from the total density of states for a system of

N
classical particles where
N
is finite, in a purely microcanonical framework, by solving a generalized Abel’s integral equation. This allows us to obtain the unique probability densities of kinetic and potential
energies that are compatible with a given total density of states. The usefulness of this approach is illustrated in this work by deriving the microcanonical distribution of particle velocities for a system with
constant heat capacity, without the need of inverting the Laplace transform as in the canonical approach.

2
Total and configurational density of states

Consider a classical system of
N
particles with Hamiltonian

\mathcal{H}(\bm{R},\bm{P})=K(\bm{P})+\Phi(\bm{R}),

(1)

where
K(\bm{P})
is the kinetic energy term, given as usual by

K(\bm{P})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\bm{p}_{i}^{2}}{2m_{i}},

(2)

and
\Phi(\bm{R})
is the potential energy term, including arbitrary interactions but only dependent on the configurational degrees of freedom
\bm{R}
.
If the system is isolated, having fixed total energy
E
, its distribution of microstates will be described by the microcanonical ensemble,

P(\bm{R},\bm{P}|E)=\frac{\delta\big(\mathcal{H}(\bm{R},\bm{P})-E\big)}{\Omega(E)},

(3)

where
\Omega(E)
is the total density of states, given by

\Omega(E)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\int d\bm{R}d\bm{P}\,\delta\big(\mathcal{H}(\bm{R},\bm{P})-E\big).

(4)

We can use the
N
-particle kinetic density of states

\Omega_{K}(K;N)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\int d\bm{p}_{1}\ldots d\bm{p}_{N}\,\delta\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\bm{p}_{i}^{2}}{2m_{i}}-K\right)

(5)

in order to perform the integration on
\bm{P}
. Upon the change of variables

\bm{u}_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{\bm{p}_{i}}{\sqrt{2m_{i}K}}

(6)

we can write (
5
) as

\Omega_{K}(K;N)=W_{N}K^{\frac{3N}{2}-1}

(7)

with

W_{N}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\prod_{i=1}^{N}(2m_{i})^{\frac{3}{2}}\times\left[\int d\bm{u}_{1}\ldots d\bm{u}_{N}\delta\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{u}_{i}^{2}-r^{2}\right)\right]_{r=1}

(8)

a constant, and such that the integral in brackets can be obtained as

\int d\bm{u}_{1}\ldots d\bm{u}_{N}\delta\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bm{u}_{i}^{2}-r^{2}\right)=\frac{1}{2r}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\int d\bm{u}_{1}\ldots d\bm{u}_{N}\Theta\left(r^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{u}_{i}^{2}\right)=\frac{1}{2r}\frac{\partial V_{3N}(r)}{\partial r}

(9)

where

V_{3N}(r)=\frac{2\,(\pi)^{\frac{3N}{2}}r^{3N}}{3N\Gamma\left(\frac{3N}{2}\right)}

(10)

is the volume of the
3N
-dimensional hypersphere
[
8
]
of radius
r
. Therefore we finally have

W_{N}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{1}{\Gamma\big(\frac{3N}{2}\big)}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\big(2\pi m_{i}\big)^{\frac{3}{2}},

(11)

and we can rewrite (
4
) as

\Omega(E)=\int d\bm{R}\left[\int d\bm{P}\,\delta\big(K(\bm{P})-E+\Phi(\bm{R})\big)\right]=W_{N}\int d\bm{R}\Big[E-\Phi(\bm{R})\Big]_{+}^{\frac{3N}{2}-1}.

(12)

At this point, we introduce the configurational density of states, defined by

\mathcal{D}(\phi)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\int d\bm{R}\,\delta\big(\Phi(\bm{R})-\phi\big),

(13)

and such that, for any function
G(\phi)
we have

\int d\bm{R}\,G(\Phi(\bm{R}))=\int_{0}^{\infty}d\phi\,\mathcal{D}(\phi)G(\phi).

(14)

Using the property (
14
) on the rightmost integral in (
12
), we finally have

\Omega(E)=W_{N}\int_{0}^{E}d\phi\,\mathcal{D}(\phi)\big(E-\phi\big)^{\frac{3N}{2}-1},

(15)

which gives
\Omega(E)
as an integral transform of
\mathcal{D}(\phi)
.

In the next section we will show that, given
\Omega(E)
for a particular system, the integral equation in (
15
) can be solved for
\mathcal{D}(\phi)
.

3
An inversion formula

We can prove that, for a given
\Omega(E)
under certain conditions such as the existence of its derivatives up to the
3(N+1)/2
-th order, the
corresponding
\mathcal{D}(\phi)
is unique, and is formally given by

\mathcal{D}(\phi)=\dfrac{1}{\Gamma\big(\frac{3N}{2}\big)\Gamma(1-\lambda)}\dfrac{1}{W_{N}}\dfrac{d}{d\phi}\int_{0}^{\phi}d\varepsilon\hskip 2.84544pt\dfrac{\Omega^{(m+1)}(\varepsilon)}{(\phi-\varepsilon)^{\lambda}},

(16)

with

\lambda\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{1}{2}\big(N\;\text{mod}\;2\big)=\begin{cases}0\;\text{for}\;\text{even}\;N,\\[5.0pt]
\frac{1}{2}\;\text{for}\;\text{odd}\;N,\end{cases}

(17)

and

m\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{3N}{2}-\lambda-1.

(18)

For even
N
, the formula in (
16
) simplifies to

\mathcal{D}(\phi)=\frac{\Omega^{\big(\frac{3N}{2}\big)}(\phi)}{W_{N}\Gamma\big(\frac{3N}{2}\big)},

(19)

while for odd
N
, we have

\mathcal{D}(\phi)=\frac{1}{W_{N}\Gamma(\frac{3N}{2})}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{\phi}\frac{dt}{\sqrt{\phi-t}}\Omega^{\big(\frac{3N}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\big)}(t)

(20)

which can be nicely written in terms of the Caputo fractional derivative
[
9
]
as

\mathcal{D}(\phi)=\frac{\mathbb{D}^{\frac{3N}{2}}\Omega(\phi)}{W_{N}\Gamma(\frac{3N}{2})},

(21)

under the definition

\mathbb{D}^{\nu}f(t)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{1}{\Gamma(n-\nu)}\int_{0}^{t}du\,(t-u)^{n-\nu-1}f^{(n)}(u),\qquad n-1<\nu<n.

(22)

4
Proof of the inversion formula

Differentiating (
15
) with respect to
E
on both sides, we have

\left(\frac{3N}{2}-1\right)\int_{0}^{\infty}d\phi\hskip 2.84544pt\mathcal{D}(\phi)\Theta(E-\phi)(E-\phi)^{\frac{3N}{2}-2}=\frac{1}{W_{N}}\frac{\partial\Omega(E)}{\partial E},

(23)

and, in general, the
m
-th derivative of (
15
) gives

\prod_{k=1}^{m}\left(\frac{3N}{2}-k\right)\int_{0}^{\infty}d\phi\hskip 2.84544pt\mathcal{D}(\phi)\Theta(E-\phi)(E-\phi)^{\frac{3N}{2}-1-m}=\frac{1}{W_{N}}\hskip 2.84544pt\Omega^{(m)}(E)

(24)

so the integral on the left-hand side is

\int_{0}^{E}d\phi\hskip 2.84544pt\mathcal{D}(\phi)(E-\phi)^{\frac{3N}{2}-1-m}=\frac{\Gamma\big(\frac{3N}{2}-m\big)}{\Gamma\big(\frac{3N}{2}\big)}\,\frac{1}{W_{N}}\hskip 2.84544pt\Omega^{(m)}(E).

(25)

Now, the value of
m
can be chosen so that the exponent

\lambda\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{3N}{2}-m-1

(26)

is such that
0<\lambda<1
. This can be acomplished by the choice

m=\begin{cases}\frac{3N}{2}-1\quad\text{if}\,\,N\,\text{is even},\\[10.0pt]
\frac{3(N-1)}{2}\quad\text{if}\,\,N\,\text{is odd},\end{cases}

(27)

such that we have
\lambda=0
for even
N
, and
\lambda=1/2
for odd
N
. We arrive at the integral equation

\int_{0}^{E}d\phi\hskip 2.84544pt\mathcal{D}(\phi)(E-\phi)^{\lambda}=\frac{\Gamma(\lambda+1)}{\Gamma\big(\frac{3N}{2}\big)}\,\frac{1}{W_{N}}\hskip 2.84544pt\Omega^{(m)}(E),

(28)

which can be solved for
\mathcal{D}(\phi)
. In order to solve (
29
), we recast it by differentiating on both sides with respect to the energy
E
, thus obtaining

\int_{0}^{E}d\phi\hskip 2.84544pt\dfrac{\mathcal{D}(\phi)}{(E-\phi)^{1-\lambda}}=\dfrac{\Gamma(\lambda)}{\Gamma\big(\frac{3N}{2}\big)}\dfrac{1}{W_{N}}\hskip 2.84544pt\Omega^{(m+1)}(E).

(29)

The integral equation in (
29
) is a generalized form of the
Abel’s integral equation

[
10
,
11
]
, whose resolution is shown in detail in the Appendix. In particular,
using the solution given in (
A-10
) with
\mu=1-\lambda
, we obtain that the configurational density of states
\mathcal{D}(\phi)
is of the form

\mathcal{D}(\phi)=\dfrac{1}{\Gamma\big(\frac{3N}{2}\big)\Gamma(1-\lambda)}\dfrac{1}{W_{N}}\dfrac{d}{d\phi}\int_{0}^{\phi}d\varepsilon\hskip 2.84544pt\dfrac{\Omega^{(m+1)}(\varepsilon)}{(\phi-\varepsilon)^{\lambda}}.

(30)

5
The case of constant microcanonical heat capacity

Consider a system with microcanonical caloric curve

E=\alpha k_{B}T_{E},

(31)

that is, the energy is proportional to the temperature. Hence, the microcanonical heat capacity
C_{E}
is constant, and given by

C_{E}=\left(\frac{\partial E}{\partial T}\right)_{E}=\alpha k_{B}.

(32)

The microcanonical inverse temperature, defined by

\frac{1}{k_{B}T_{E}}=\beta_{\Omega}(E)=\frac{\partial}{\partial E}\ln\Omega(E),

(33)

is then

\beta_{\Omega}(E)=\frac{\alpha}{E},

(34)

and by integration we obtain the total density of states as

\Omega(E)=\Omega_{0}E^{\alpha},

(35)

with
\Omega_{0}
an integration constant. Upon replacing the
(m+1)
-th derivative of (
35
),

\Omega^{(m)}(\varepsilon)=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}{\Gamma(\alpha-m)}\Omega_{0}\,E^{\alpha-m-1}

(36)

into (
16
) we have

\mathcal{D}(\phi)=\frac{1}{\Gamma\big(\frac{3N}{2}\big)\Gamma(1-\lambda)}\hskip 2.84544pt\frac{\Omega_{0}}{W_{N}}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}{\Gamma(\alpha-m)}\frac{d}{d\phi}\int_{0}^{\phi}d\varepsilon\hskip 2.84544pt\frac{\varepsilon^{\alpha-m-1}}{(\phi-\varepsilon)^{\lambda}}.

(37)

The integral in (
37
) can be easily computed with the aid of the beta function. The result is

\int_{0}^{\phi}d\varepsilon\hskip 2.84544pt\frac{\varepsilon^{\alpha-m-1}}{(\phi-\varepsilon)^{\lambda}}=B(1-\lambda,\alpha-m)\hskip 2.84544pt\phi^{\alpha-m-\lambda}=\frac{\Gamma(1-\lambda)\Gamma(\alpha-m)}{\Gamma(\alpha+1-m-\lambda)}\hskip 2.84544pt\phi^{\alpha-m-\lambda}.

(38)

Then, after performing the derivative with respect to
\phi
on (
38
) , using the definition of
\lambda
given in (
26
) and simplifying, we finally obtain for a system with
constant microcanonical heat capacity
C_{E}=\alpha k_{B}
and arbitrary size
N\geq 1
, that its configurational density of states is

\mathcal{D}(\phi)=D_{0}\,\phi^{\alpha-\frac{3N}{2}},

(39)

where the constant
D_{0}
is given by

D_{0}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{\Omega_{0}}{W_{N}}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}{\Gamma\big(\frac{3N}{2}\big)\Gamma(\alpha+1-\frac{3N}{2})}.

(40)

A common assumption, mostly in short-range interacting systems, is that the energy
E
is extensive, that is,
E=\varepsilon N
. If we also assume that the temperature is intensive (independent of
N
),
then it follows that
\alpha=aN
with
c_{E}=ak_{B}
the heat capacity per atom.

6
Density of states with a concave region

As another example of the application of our main result in (
16
), we will now consider the density of states

\Omega(E)=\Omega_{0}\big(1+b^{2}(E-\mu)^{2}\big)E^{\alpha}

(41)

with
\alpha>0
,
b\geq 0
and
\mu>0
, such that the microcanonical heat capacity is no longer constant for
b\neq 0
. The microcanonical inverse temperature
\beta_{\Omega}
, defined in
(
33
), is for this model given by

\beta_{\Omega}(E)=\frac{\partial}{\partial E}\ln\Omega(E)=\frac{\alpha}{E}+\frac{2b^{2}(E-\mu)}{1+b^{2}(E-\mu)^{2}}.

(42)

For
b\mu>\sqrt{\alpha(\alpha+2)}
, the density of states
\Omega(E)
in (
41
) has two inflection points, at
E=E_{m}\pm\Delta
, where

\displaystyle E_{m}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{\mu(\alpha+1)}{\alpha+2},

(43)

\displaystyle\Delta

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{\sqrt{(b\mu)^{2}-\alpha(\alpha+2)}}{b(\alpha+2)},

(44)

such that
\beta_{\Omega}(E)<0
for
\big|E-E_{m}\big|<\Delta
. This kind of region is usually associated with metastable states in a first-order phase transition
[
12
]
, such that the
canonical energy distribution is bimodal, as shown for instance in Ref.
[
13
]
.

We can readily compute the CDOS associated to (
41
) by writing it as a linear combination of constant heat capacity terms, namely

\Omega(E)=\Omega_{0}\big(1+(b\mu)^{2}\big)E^{\alpha}+\Omega_{0}(b^{2})E^{\alpha+2}-2\Omega_{0}(b^{2})\mu E^{\alpha+1}.

(45)

Due to the linearity of the derivatives and integral in (
16
), we can directly apply the result in (
39
) term by term, obtaining

\mathcal{D}(\phi)=\frac{\Omega_{0}\Gamma(\alpha+1)}{W_{N}\,\Gamma\left(\frac{3N}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(\alpha+1-\frac{3N}{2}\right)}G(\phi;\alpha,b,\mu,N)\phi^{\alpha-\frac{3N}{2}}

(46)

where we have defined the function

G(\phi;\alpha,b,\mu,N)=1+(b\mu)^{2}+\frac{b^{2}(\alpha+1)(\alpha+2)\phi^{2}}{\left(\alpha+1-\frac{3N}{2}\right)\left(\alpha+2-\frac{3N}{2}\right)}-\frac{2(b^{2})\mu\,(\alpha+1)\phi}{\alpha+1-\frac{3N}{2}}.

(47)

for convenience. Taking
\alpha=aN
we obtain in the the thermodynamic limit that

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}G(\phi;\alpha,b,\mu,N)=1+\frac{b^{2}}{\left(1-\frac{3}{2a}\right)^{2}}\left[\phi-\left(1-\frac{3}{2a}\right)\mu\right]^{2},

(48)

then
\mathcal{D}(\phi)
takes a similar form to
\Omega(E)
, with new parameters

\displaystyle b^{\prime}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{2ab}{2a-3},

(49)

\displaystyle\mu^{\prime}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{\mu(2a-3)}{2a}.

(50)

7
Microcanonical velocity distribution for constant heat capacity

The microcanonical distribution of configurations is

P(\bm{R}|E)=\frac{\Omega_{K}\big(E-\Phi(\bm{R});N\big)}{\Omega(E)}=\frac{W_{N}}{\Omega(E)}\Big[E-\Phi(\bm{R})\Big]_{+}^{\frac{3N}{2}-1}

(51)

so the corresponding distribution of potential energies is given by

P(\phi|E)=\int d\bm{R}\,P(\bm{R}|E)\delta\big(\Phi(\bm{R})-\phi\big)=\frac{W_{N}}{\Omega(E)}\Big[E-\phi\Big]_{+}^{\frac{3N}{2}-1}\mathcal{D}(\phi).

(52)

This kind of expression corresponds to a
q
-canonical distribution, with

q\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=1-\frac{2}{2-3N}

(53)

as noted by Naudts and Baeten
[
14
]
. Because
\Omega(E)
determines
\mathcal{D}(\phi)
, the microcanonical potential energy distribution in (
52
) is completely determined by
\Omega(E)
alone.
The same is true for the microcanonical distribution of single-particle velocities, as we have

P(\bm{p}_{1},\ldots,\bm{p}_{N}|E)=\frac{1}{\Omega(E)}\int d\bm{R}\,\delta\left(\frac{\bm{p}_{1}^{2}}{2m_{1}}+\sum_{i=2}^{N}\frac{\bm{p}_{i}^{2}}{2m_{i}}+\Phi(\bm{R})-E\right).

(54)

The single-particle momentum distribution is obtaining by integrating out the remaining momenta,

P(\bm{p}_{1}|E)=\int d\bm{p}_{2}\ldots d\bm{p}_{N}\;P(\bm{p}_{1},\ldots,\bm{p}_{N}|E),

(55)

hence, calling
\bm{p}_{1}\rightarrow\bm{p}
and
m_{1}\rightarrow m
, we have

P(\bm{p}|E)=\frac{1}{\Omega(E)}\int d\bm{p}_{2}\ldots d\bm{p}_{N}\int d\bm{R}\,\delta\left(E-\Phi(\bm{R})-\frac{\bm{p}^{2}}{2m}-\sum_{i=2}^{N}\frac{\bm{p}_{i}^{2}}{2m_{i}}\right).

(56)

By using (
14
) to introduce the CDOS, the single-particle momentum distribution simplifies to

\begin{split}P(\bm{p}|E)&=\frac{1}{\Omega(E)}\int d\bm{p}_{2}\ldots d\bm{p}_{N}\int d\phi\,\mathcal{D}(\phi)\,\delta\left(E-\phi-\frac{\bm{p}^{2}}{2m}-\sum_{i=2}^{N}\frac{\bm{p}_{i}^{2}}{2m_{i}}\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{\Omega(E)}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\tilde{K}\int d\phi\,\mathcal{D}(\phi)\,\delta\big(E-k(\bm{v})-\phi-\tilde{K}\big)\Omega_{K}(\tilde{K};N-1)\end{split}

(57)

where we have replaced
\bm{v}=\bm{p}/m
and defined

k(\bm{v})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{m\bm{v}^{2}}{2}

(58)

to be the single-particle kinetic energy. Making the change of variables from
\bm{p}
to
\bm{v}=\bm{p}/m
, we can write the single-particle velocity distribution as

P(\bm{v}|E)=m\,P(\bm{p}=m\bm{v}|E),

(59)

and replacing (
5
), finally leads to

P(\bm{v}|E)=\frac{m\,W_{N-1}}{\Omega(E)}\int_{0}^{E-k(\bm{v})}d\phi\,\big[E-k(\bm{v})-\phi\big]^{\frac{3(N-1)}{2}-1}\mathcal{D}(\phi).

(60)

The distribution
P(\bm{v}|E)
is, in general, different from the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution for finite
N
, but converges to it in the thermodynamic limit. In the case developed in
Section
5
, we have

P(\bm{v}|E)=\left(\frac{m}{2\pi}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}{E^{\alpha}\Gamma(\alpha-1/2)}\Big[E-\frac{m\bm{v}^{2}}{2}\Big]_{+}^{\alpha-\frac{3}{2}},

(61)

a formula previously obtained by Ray and Graben
[
15
]
by inversion of the Laplace transform. This expression can, in principle, be used for the precise determination of the heat capacity in
atomistic computer simulations, from collected samples of atomic velocities. By taking
E=\varepsilon N
and
\alpha=aN
, we can rewrite (
61
) as

P(\bm{v}|\varepsilon,N)=\left(\frac{m}{2\pi\,\varepsilon}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}N^{-\frac{3}{2}}\frac{\Gamma(aN+1)}{\Gamma(aN-1/2)}\left[1-\left(\frac{m\bm{v}^{2}}{2\varepsilon N}\right)\right]_{+}^{aN-\frac{3}{2}}

(62)

which is a
q
-Gaussian distribution
[
16
]
with

q=1-\frac{2}{2aN-3}<1.

(63)

The fact that
q<1
in (
63
) makes the model in (
61
)
subcanonical

[
17
]
, therefore incompatible with the superstatistical formalism
[
18
]
.
In the thermodynamic limit,
N\rightarrow\infty
, so
q\rightarrow 1
and we recover the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}P(\bm{v}|\varepsilon,N)=\left(\frac{m}{2\pi k_{B}T_{E}}\right)^{\frac{3}{2}}\exp\Big(-\frac{m\bm{v}^{2}}{2k_{B}T_{E}}\Big)

(64)

with

k_{B}T_{E}=\frac{E}{\alpha}=\frac{\varepsilon}{a}.

(65)

The mean kinetic energy per particle is proportional to the temperature, in a finite-size version of the equipartition theorem,

\big<k\big>_{E}=\frac{3E}{2(\alpha+1)}=\frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}\right)k_{B}T_{E}\leq\frac{3}{2}k_{B}T_{E},

(66)

where equality is reached in the limit
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
. On the other hand, the relative variance of the kinetic energy per particle is

\frac{\big<(\delta k)^{2}\big>_{E}}{\big<k\big>_{E}^{2}}=\frac{2}{3}-\frac{5}{3(\alpha+2)}\leq\frac{2}{3},

(67)

agreeing in the limit
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
with the value of
2/3
for the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution, as expected.

8
Concluding remarks

We have presented the inversion formula (
16
), giving the configurational density of states in terms of the total density of states.
This exact expression may provide some insights on the features of first-order phase transitions in finite systems.

Appendix: Generalized Abel’s integral equation

The generalized Abel’s integral equation is given by

\int_{a}^{t}dt^{\prime}\dfrac{y(t^{\prime})}{(t-t^{\prime})^{\mu}}=g(t).

(A-1)

In order to solve (
A-1
), we integrate between
a
and
x
after multiplying on both sides by
(x-t)^{\mu-1}
, i.e.

\int_{a}^{x}dt\hskip 2.84544pt(x-t)^{\mu-1}\int_{a}^{t}dt^{\prime}\dfrac{y(t^{\prime})}{(t-t^{\prime})^{\mu}}=\int_{a}^{x}dt\hskip 2.84544pt\dfrac{g(t)}{(x-t)^{1-\mu}}.

(A-2)

Consider the left side of (
A-2
). By use of the Dirichlet’s formula for reversal of the integration order, namely

\int_{a}^{x}dt\int_{a}^{t}dt^{\prime}\hskip 2.84544ptF(x,t,t^{\prime})=\int_{a}^{x}dt^{\prime}\int_{t^{\prime}}^{x}dt\hskip 2.84544ptF(x,t,t^{\prime}),

(A-3)

we can recast the left side of (
A-2
) in the following way

\int_{a}^{x}dt\int_{a}^{t}dt^{\prime}(x-t)^{\mu-1}(t-t^{\prime})^{-\mu}\hskip 2.84544pty(t^{\prime})=\int_{a}^{x}dt^{\prime}\left\{\int_{t^{\prime}}^{x}dt\hskip 2.84544pt(x-t)^{\mu-1}(t-t^{\prime})^{-\mu}\right\}y(t^{\prime}).

(A-4)

The advantage of the previous reversal of the integration order is that we can compute the integral within the curly brackets in (
A-4
) independently. In this sense, after defining a new variable
s
as

s=\dfrac{t-t^{\prime}}{x-t^{\prime}}

(A-5)

we obtain

\int_{t^{\prime}}^{x}dt\hskip 2.84544pt(x-t)^{\mu-1}(t-t^{\prime})^{-\mu}=\int_{0}^{1}ds\hskip 2.84544pts^{-\mu}(1-s)^{\mu-1}=B(1-\mu,\mu),

(A-6)

where
B(1-\mu,\mu)
is the beta function, which is related with the gamma function by

B(1-\mu,\mu)=\dfrac{\Gamma(1-\mu)\Gamma(\mu)}{\Gamma(1-\mu+\mu)}=\dfrac{\Gamma(1-\mu)\Gamma(\mu)}{\Gamma(1)}=\Gamma(1-\mu)\Gamma(\mu).

(A-7)

Hence, the integral within the curly brackets in (
A-4
) becomes

\int_{t^{\prime}}^{x}dt\hskip 2.84544pt(x-t)^{\mu-1}(t-t^{\prime})^{-\mu}=\Gamma(1-\mu)\Gamma(\mu).

(A-8)

Using this result in the integral equation (
A-2
),

\int_{a}^{x}dt^{\prime}\hskip 2.84544pty(t^{\prime})=\dfrac{1}{\Gamma(1-\mu)\Gamma(\mu)}\int_{a}^{x}dt\hskip 2.84544pt\dfrac{g(t)}{(x-t)^{1-\mu}},

(A-9)

and invoking the fundamental theorem of calculus, we finally obtain the desired solution of the generalized Abel’s integral equation, namely

y(x)=\dfrac{1}{\Gamma(1-\mu)\Gamma(\mu)}\hskip 2.84544pt\dfrac{d}{dx}\int_{a}^{x}dt\hskip 2.84544pt\dfrac{g(t)}{(x-t)^{1-\mu}}.

(A-10)
