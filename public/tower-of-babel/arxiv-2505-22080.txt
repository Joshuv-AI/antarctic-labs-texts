Abstract
We develop a model of strategic competition between global currencies. Issuers choose their commitment levels for currency internationalization, while users—interconnected in a trade network—choose their usage of each currency for trade settlement. Our theoretical findings highlight not only the advantage of the status-quo issuer in maintaining dominance, but also the conditions under which an emerging issuer can attract users, potentially leading to a multi-currency payment system. The network centrality of users plays a key role in shaping both their currency choices and the strategic commitment levels of issuers. Our framework offers testable implications for the share of global currencies for trade settlement by linking the network structure, the strategy of issuers and the currency choice of users. Keywords: global currency; trade network; US-China competition; subgame perfect equilibrium JEL Classification: C7; D6; D8; F3; F4

Introduction

Throughout history, global powers have typically risen in stages—first by expanding international trade, then by developing dominant financial centers, and finally by issuing the world’s leading reserve currency
(
Dalio 2021
)
. Today, the United States appears to be in the final stage of this cycle, while China is in the first phase.
Since the end of World War II, the US dollar (USD) has been the dominant currency for international payments, invoicing, and foreign reserves, lowering borrowing costs and amplifying the US global influence.
Meanwhile, China has surpassed the US in global trade share since 2013, raising questions about whether it could challenge the USD’s dominance.
From this perspective, China’s next steps—liberalizing its capital account and strengthening its financial institutions—are critical to the future of the international monetary order. To support the international use of the renminbi (RMB), China has offered loans and aid to build infrastructure globally and facilitated trade settlement in RMB through initiatives such as the Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) and the Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank (AIIB), while also signing bilateral currency swap agreements. Yet, China has not fully committed to RMB internationalization. It maintains a relatively closed capital account, and many of its initiatives are regional or bilateral in scope—lacking the global scope of institutions built under the Bretton Woods system by the US
(
Gourinchas and
Rey 2007
, see)
.
Consequently, despite China’s dominant trade share, the use of the RMB as a global payment currency remains remarkably limited. In 2023, only 3% of international trade was settled in RMB, less than one-tenth of 44% settled in USD.

This disparity underscores that a dominant trade volume does not necessarily translate into strong incentives to pursue currency internationalization, nor does it imply a dominant international currency.

This paper addresses two interrelated questions: why is the RMB’s share for trade settlement so low and why does China not commit more to RMB internationalization despite China’s trade dominance?
To answer the questions, we focus on the role of currencies as a medium of exchange, following the observation of
Eichengreen 2011
that currency internationalize has often started by encouraging the use in trade invoicing and settlement.
We show that the positions of countries in the global trade network are key determinants for interdependence between the currency choice of users and the commitment levels of issuers.

First, we show that an issuer’s commitment level is endogenously shaped by the trade network structure, as the effectiveness of currency internationalization depends on the position of users with a strong currency preference in a trade network.
The trade payment networks for 2000 and 2021 as shown in Figure
1
reveal that
by 2021 China had become the second-largest importer, with its total payment volume growing 11 times compared to 2000 and its centrality increased 3.5 times, making it the second most influential country in the network in 2021. However, China’s centrality increased at a much slower rate than its import volume, while
the US retained the highest centrality, remaining the most critical country in the network.
The high centrality of the US stems from 1) its imports from many countries, 2) its large bilateral import volumes, and 3) the high centralities of its important trading partners, meaning the US imports heavily from key importers who also import heavily from other key economies. The last factor is what China lacks relative to the US and explains the slower growth of centrality of China than its trade volume, which leads to China’s restrained commitment to RMB internationalization.

China’s top importing partners include Australia, Brazil, Indonesia, Malaysia, Thailand, and Vietnam, whose import volumes are relatively low, and they do not import significantly from other key economies in the network.

We conclude that China’s commitment for RMB internationalization is insufficient to challenge the status of the USD for now as China expects the increase in the RMB usage by other countries to remain low, even when it raises its commitment level, because of China’s low centrality relative to the US.

Second, we show that the centralily of countries in the global trade network is crucial for their currency adoption. The currency choice of users exhibits strategic complementarity—users prefer currencies widely accepted by their trading partners. Even if a key trading partner prefers a particular currency, it may not be optimal for a country’s users to adopt it unless it is also usable for settling trade with other partners. Therefore, it is necessary to analyze the network-wide incentives rather than the bilateral ties alone to understand the currency choices.
We conclude that the less central positions of China and its major trading partners in the global trade network than the US and its major trading partners result in lower adoptions of China’s RMB than the USD.

We extend the network game framework introduced in
Ballester et al. 2006
, replacing the single-action choice with a multi-currency allocation problem.

The framework with single-action choice has been applied to analyze various problems, including the crime networks
(
Ballester et al. 2010
)
, peer effects in education
(
Calvó-Armengol et al. 2009
)
, and financial interactions
(
Acemoglu et al. 2015

.
This network game is then integrated into a framework of strategic competition among currency issuers.
This approach allows us to analyze the interaction between competing issuers and interconnected users. For instance, while the issuers can attract users by raising their commitment levels at a cost, the network structure can influence how each user responds to the commitment levels.
Our model reveals four key findings. First, the status-quo currency issuer can maintain its monopoly even under symmetric conditions, i.e., when users face the same costs for settling trade with either currency. Second, greater trade integration strengthens this advantage by amplifying network spillovers. Third, the more central a country is in a trade network, the more concentrated its currency allocation is for trade settlement. Finally, the issuer of the currency preferred by central users commits more to currency internationalization and achieves greater adoption. Together these results suggest that deglobalization and the protectionist policies by the US are likely to weaken the first mover advantage of the US and lower its centrality in the global trade network, leading to a lower adoption of USD by other countries in the future.

While the model provides insights into superpower competition, it is not specifically suited for currency competition, as currency is generally non-excludable and thus should not be modeled as a club good. Moreover, the model does not have a network structure – a crucial factor for determining the currency choice.

These approaches reflect the idea that countries are more likely to adopt a currency that is already widely used. On the other hand, our framework provides a theoretical foundation for examining how a country’s position in the global trade network influences its choice of settlement currency. It also suggests that a country’s strategies for currency internationalization—such as financial institutional development and capital account openness—are shaped by its network position, alongside other factors like political partisanship, trade openness, and legal origin

A two-currency model

There are two currency issuers
\{a,b\}
. They issue currency
a
and currency
b
, respectively.
Each issuer
p\in\{a,b\}
sequentially decides whether to internationalize its currency and, if so, chooses a
commitment level

e_{p}\in[0,1]
. This commitment level can be interpreted as the efforts to manage an open capital account and establish or maintain institutions, swap lines and infrastructures that expands the market for the currency, thereby enhancing its liquidity.
In this model,
a
is the first mover and
b
is the second mover.

There is a finite set of users
N=\{1,2,3,…,n\}

(n\geq 2)
interconnected in a weighted directed network.
Each user
i
must settle
m_{i}
units of international transactions with the available currencies. Throughout this paper, we assume that each country has a representative user who decides on the currencies to settle its imports. In our model, it matters little whether the users decide the allocation of currencies to settle their imports or exports (see Section
3.1
).

We assume that settling imports with an international currency incurs costs that fluctuate depending on the exchange rates. These costs are mitigated by two factors: the liquidity of the currency and the network externalities of using the same currency as trading partners. We assume that the liquidity of a currency is determined by the commitment level of the issuer to promote its currency.
Users’ objective is to minimize the cost of import settlement with the two international currencies.

Countries (importers) are naturally more influenced by the currency choices made by their dominant trading partners (exporters). To capture this, we represent the network as a weighted directed graph, where links represent the flow of payments and therefore, originate from importers to exporters.
Each link
ij
from
i
to
j

\{i,j\}\subset N

w_{ij}\geq 0
, indicating how much

j
’s decision, or the dependency of
i
on
j
. When
w_{ij}>0
,
j

i
’s usage of the same currency are strategic complements from
i
’s perspective. For simplicity, we assume that
w_{ii}=0
for all
i\in N
. Then, we can define the network as
\bm{w}=[w_{ij}]
, which is a zero-diagonal non-negative square matrix.

The game unfolds in three stages:

(i)

The status-quo issuer
a
chooses whether to internationalize its currency and, if so, its commitment level
e_{a}\in[0,1]
.

(ii)

Given
a

b
chooses whether to internationalize its currency and, if so, its commitment level
e_{b}\in[0,1]
.

(iii)

Given the two issuers’ choices, each user
i
decides the usage of each currency to settle its imports such that
x_{ia}+x_{ib}=m_{i}
for all
i\in N
.

The cost for user
i
to settle
x_{ip}

p

p

C_{ip}(\bm{x_{p}},e_{p})=x_{ip}\big[\underbrace{\vphantom{\sum_{j\neq i}}\epsilon_{p}}_{\text{exchange rate risk}}-\underbrace{\sum_{j\neq i}w_{ij}x_{jp}}_{\text{network externality}}-\underbrace{\vphantom{\sum_{j\neq i}}f_{ip}(e_{p})}_{\text{liquidity}}\big],

where

p

[\epsilon_{p}-\sum_{j\neq i}w_{ij}x_{jp}-f_{ip}(e_{p})]

p
. The term
\epsilon_{p}
is a random variable with variance
\sigma_{p}

e_{p}
.

We assume that users have

\displaystyle\mathscr{C}_{ip}(\bm{x_{p}},e_{p})\coloneqq\frac{\eta}{2}Var(C_{ip})+E[C_{ip}]=\frac{\beta_{p}}{2}x_{ip}^{2}-\sum_{j\neq i}w_{ij}x_{ip}x_{jp}-f_{ip}(e_{p})x_{ip},

where

The convex cost associated with the exchange rate risk gives users an incentive to diversify their currency use.

Each user

a
and
b

\min_{0\leq x_{ia},x_{ib}\leq m_{i}}v_{i}(\bm{x_{a}},\bm{x_{b}},e_{a},e_{b})=\mathscr{C}_{ia}+\mathscr{C}_{ib}\quad\text{s.t. }x_{ia}+x_{ib}=m_{i}.

(1)

Each issuer
p\in\{a,b\}
solves the utility maximization problem given by

\max_{e_{p}}u_{p}(e_{p},e_{-p})=\begin{cases}X_{p}(e_{p},e_{-p})-kc_{p}(e_{p})&\mbox{if $0\leq e_{p}\leq 1$}\\
0&\mbox{if $e_{p}=\emptyset$,}\end{cases}

(2)

where

is the total usage of currency

3
The equilibrium

We find the subgame perfect Nash equilibrium (SPE) by solving the problem backward. We begin this section with the currency choice problem of users given the commitment levels for currency internationalization chosen by issuers.

3.1
The users

Each user minimizes its cost given the commitment levels chosen by issuers and the currency choices made by other users.
We assume that when there is only one issuer internationalizing its currency, users settle all their transactions in the only available international currency; and if both issuers give up on internationalizing their currencies, users cannot settle their imports using any international currency.

If neither issuer internationalizes their currency, trade must be settled in local currencies, a scenario not modeled in this paper as we focus on cases where at least one international currency is available.

Lemma 1

Lemma
1
states that the difference in the usage of the two currencies by each user is proportional to its (weighted) Katz centrality. When
\gamma_{i}(e_{a},e_{b})>0~\forall i

\gamma_{i}(e_{a},e_{b})<0~\forall i

b

. Intuitively, when a user becomes more central in the network, its currency use becomes more uneven. This is because a more central user benefits more from matching the currency with its trading partners. Moreover, a central user’s currency choice influences others (directly and indirectly), which in turn raises the central user’s benefit of using the currency. Thus, a user with a higher centrality has the incentive to use the currency that it prefers more.

Since the Katz centrality is calculated based on paths originating from each node, the direction of links, that is, how users take others’ behavior into account, is important in determining the currency choice. We believe that in a currency choice problem, the directed links should represent the flow of payments and therefore, originate from the importers to the exporters.
This is because an importer prefers to pay the exporter in a currency commonly used by both of them to minimize exchange costs. Similarly, an exporter prefers to receive payments in a currency it can use to pay its own exporters, reducing future conversion needs. In both cases, users make currency decisions based on their payment destinations. Thus, the direction of the links reflects how much weight users place on each other’s currency choices, with the flow of payments naturally representing these interdependent decisions.
Therefore, no matter whether we let the importers or the exporters decide the transaction settlement, their decisions are still proportional to the (weighted) Katz centralities in terms of the payment flow. Hence, it matters little whether the users decide the currencies to settle their imports or exports in our model. For simplicity, we assume that the users decide the currencies to settle their imports and thus
m_{i}
is user
i
’s total import.

5

5

If we assume that the users decide the currencies to settle their exports, the only change in the solution for the currency choice problem is that
m_{i}
becomes user
i
’s total export.

3.2
The issuers

Given the responses of users, the payoffs to the two issuers for their commitment levels
(e_{a},e_{b})
are well-defined. We make the following two assumptions on the choices of issuers.

(i)

If
u_{p}\leq 0
, then
p
does not internationalize its currency. If
u_{p}>0
, then
p
internationalizes its currency with a commitment level
e_{p}\in[0,1]
.

(ii)

Consider two distinct choices
e_{p}
and
e^{\prime}_{p}
. If
u_{p}(e_{p})=u_{p}(e^{\prime}_{p})
, then
p
chooses
\max\{e_{p},e^{\prime}_{p}\}
.

Assumption
(i)
states that an issuer does not internationalize its currency unless it is beneficial for itself. This assumption is essential for the existence of the Nash equilibria.
Assumption
(ii)
states that the issuers choose a higher commitment level if multiple choices yield the same payoff. This assumption allows us to investigate the best that issuers would do to internationalize their currencies.
Although the players have infinitely many actions to consider, we can show that the best responses exist at every subgame, and thus the SPE exists.

Lemma 2
.

Suppose that assumption
(i)
holds. Then, the game has at least one subgame-perfect Nash equilibrium.

Therefore, by backward induction, we obtain the SPE. By assumption
(ii)
, ties are broken and we can choose a unique SPE where issuers do their best to internationalize their currencies.

Let
M\coloneqq\sum_{i\in N}m_{i}
be the total demand for international currencies.
It is worth noting that when

Intuitively, when the cost of currency internationalization is very high relative to the benefit from global usage, the first mover can deter the second mover with a low commitment level. Our model yields testable implications and can serve as a guide for designing and conducting further empirical studies on the effects of countries’ positions in the network on their currency choice.

6
Conclusion

We develop a game-theoretic model of strategic competition between currency issuers, where issuers sequentially set commitment levels to the internationalization of their currencies, while users—interconnected in a trade network—decide the usage of each currency for their trade settlement. Users face exchange rate risk, and the costs for settling trade depend on the choices of trading partners and the commitment levels of issuers.

Our analysis reveals structural barriers to the internationalization of an emerging currency, explaining why being a dominant trading partner in bilateral trade is insufficient to achieve dominant currency status. The findings elucidate China’s low commitment level and the RMB’s limited global adoption despite its trade dominance, offering testable hypotheses to guide future empirical work on currency competition and network effects. We obtain the following key findings.

First, the status-quo issuer can deter the challenger and maintain its monopoly by committing strongly to the internationalization of its currency, benefiting the users. By committing early to high financial openness and institutional support, the status-quo issuer attracts all users, deterring the entry of the emerging issuer by raising the cost of market-sharing. This explains the US’s first-mover strategy—establishing the Bretton Woods Institutions—to maintain the USD dominance. Therefore, the emerging issuer, such as China, faces prohibitive costs to establish itself as an issuer of a new global currency, let alone to become a dominant issuer.
However, a multipolar global payment system becomes more likely when the cost of currency internationalization becomes sufficiently low relative to the potential benefits.

Second, international trade integration amplifies the status-quo issuer’s advantage. This is because greater trade interdependence magnifies network spillovers, thus increases the effectiveness of issuers’ commitment to internationalizing their currencies, enabling the status-quo issuer to deter the emergence of new global currencies more effectively. This mechanism explains the increasing reliance on the USD for invoicing and settlement as international trade becomes more integrated, even when countries trade more with non-US partners.

Third, users’ currency concentration—measured by the Gini index of their currency allocations—is positively correlated with their network centrality. In other words, central users are expected to concentrate currency usage, whereas peripheral users are more likely to diversify. This pattern arises because central users gain greater benefits from aligning their currency choice with that of their trading partners. Additionally, a central user’s currency decisions exert broader influence (both directly and indirectly) on the choices of others, which further amplifies the payoff from using a particular currency. Hence, highly central users have stronger incentives to allocate more transactions to the currency that best serves their interests.

Fourth, an issuer gains a strategic advantage when users favoring its currency occupy central network positions. This result highlights how network position, not just trade volume, shapes currency competition. The US’s high centrality and domestic user bias toward the USD incentivize strong commitment. On the other hand, China’s initiatives, such as the BRI, AIIB and swap agreements, are often regional or bilateral and face challenges in scaling globally, which can be seen as attempts to internationalize its currency with a lower commitment level than the US. However, China may have a stronger incentive to internationalize the RMB if its network centrality is enhanced (e.g., stronger ties among its trading partners) and if its domestic economy and financial market continue to develop, leading to potential greater adoption of the RMB by users over time.

These insights offer a roadmap for empirical studies linking trade network data, issuer policy decisions, and currency adoption patterns. Our findings suggest that China needs to further benefit users, especially its domestic users, to internationalize the RMB. This involves reducing the risk of using the RMB for trade settlement through financial market development to increase the RMB liquidity. Moreover, it is crucial for China as a global currency issuer to convince Chinese firms to settle trade in the RMB, which can be achieved by boosting the domestic economy, thus enhancing the liquidity for users in China. When users in China have a strong preference for home currency over the status-quo, a higher centrality of China in the global trade network would support the internationalization of the RMB since it strengthens China’s incentive to raise its commitment level and allows the strong preference of users in China to spread through the network.

There are important limitations to our model. First, we interpret China’s low commitment as reflecting bilateral or regional agreements and capital account constraints, contrasting with US global institutions and open capital accounts. However, the model does not explicitly account for issuers’ strategies in targeting specific users or regions. Second, our analysis relies on the linear-quadratic perceived cost functions of users, derived from the mean-variance preferences. Then, first-order conditions produce a system of linear equations, allowing us to link the users’ currency allocations to their positions in the trade network. Although this functional form is restrictive, such quadratic functions can be seen as the second-order Taylor approximations of general convex functions that represent risk aversion, providing a balance between theoretical clarity and analytical tractability. This approach aligns with foundational work in financial economics
(
Markowitz 1952
, e.g.,)
and network games
(
Ballester et al. 2006
, e.g.,)
, while enabling closed-form solutions that connect equilibrium outcomes to network structure.
