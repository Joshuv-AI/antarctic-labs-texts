Investigation of the clinical value of artificial intelligence-derived prognostic signature in cervical cancer based on machine learning algorithms
Authors: Chao Xu, Hang Li, Yuxuan Wu, Qiuming Yao, Yanbo Zhong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWomen around the world are troubled by life-threatening cervical cancer. It is urgent to identify a biomarker to improve the prognosis of cervical cancer patients. Based on gene expression profiles and single-cell sequencing data obtained from public databases, we performed dimensionality reduction and clustering analyses, Scissor analysis, WGCNA, and machine-learning modeling using 10 base algorithms and their 101 individual or combined strategies. We finally screened 24 consensus prognostic genes to develop a novel model artificial intelligence-derived prognostic signature (AIDPS), based on C-index which was detected in six validation datasets (TCGA_Test, TCGA_Entire, CGCI-HTMCP-CC, GSE39001, GSE44001, and GSE52903). AIDPS demonstrated modest but consistent prognostic performance across multiple independent cervical cancer cohorts, with an average C-index of 0.665.The accuracy of AIDPS in predicting CESC was significantly better than that of other clinical characteristics including age, pathological TNM stages, and grade. In conclusion, our study developed a consensus model AIDPS, an effective strategy to further guide the clinical management and individualized treatment of cervical cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-05182-y.Keywords: Cervical cancer, Prognosis, Machine learning, AIDPS, Clinical guidance

Introduction

Globally, cervical cancer (CESC) remains the most common gynecological malignancy [1]. Squamous cell carcinoma and adenocarcinoma are the two main subtypes of cervical cancer. Cervical cancer accounts for around 0.6 million new cases and 0.3 million deaths worldwide in 2020 [2]. In China, 65,000 new cases of cervical cancer occur each year, resulting in 25,000 deaths [3].The symptoms of early cervical cancer are typically nonexistent, while those of late cervical cancer can develop into low and high grade squamous intra-epithelial lesion, carcinoma in situ, and finally to invasive cervical cancer [4]. Preventing cervical cancer involves two main choices: HPV vaccination and cervical cancer screening [5]. Cervical cancer screening is beneficial for a dramatic decrease in the incidence and mortality of CESC, which detects early precursor lesions called cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) [6]. Cervical cancer develops slowly, beginning with CIN and progressing to invasive cancer. Invasive cervical cancer has a 5-year survival ratio of 16.5% with unsatisfactory prognosis, compared to 91.5% for carcinoma in situ [7]. Invasive and metastatic cervical cancer patients would benefit from finding new therapeutic targets to block them. Hence, it is urgent to identify the biomarker to detect precancerous lesions as early as possible and significantly improve the prognosis of cervical cancer.

Cervical cancer has been shown to vary in terms of its development and prognosis owing to heterogeneity of the tumor microenvironment (TME) [8]. In immune evasion as well as cervical tumor initiation, immune cells interact with the TME [9]. As a result of its heterogeneous manifestations, there is currently no standard treatment and prognostic strategy for metastatic cervical cancer. It may be possible to use miRNA-mediated regulation of TME as an adjunct to current therapeutic strategies to prevent and treat cancer based on the correlation between TME and dysregulation of miRNA [10]. In recent decades, immunotherapies have changed treatment strategy for cancer, harnessing the immune system to fight cancer. Early clinical trials of immunotherapies such as vaccine therapy and adoptive cell therapy (ACT) have been conducted for cervical cancer and gained significant attraction [11]. It is worth noting, however, that some immunotherapies only induce limited immune responses [12]. Therefore, there is an imperative need to find novel personalized management and combination treatment strategies to significantly improve the prognosis of patients with cervical cancer.

With the development of digital healthcare, deep machine-learning technique was utilized for rapid segmentation and classification of individual cell types in different stages of cervical cancer [13]. Quantified morphologic characteristics can link to the prediction and prognosis of clinical outcomes via artificial intelligence (AI) [14]. In order to obtain specific biomarkers for the prognosis and therapy of cervical cancer, we developed and validated an artificial intelligence-derived prognostic signature (AIDPS) in various cohorts based on the consensus prognostic genes intersection from scissor algorithm and weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA). This study will provide novel insights into the treatment and prognosis of advanced and recurrent cervical cancer.

Methods

Data sources

RNA expression profiles and corresponding clinical data of CGCI-HTMCP-CC were acquired from the NCI Genomic Data Commons (GDC) Data Portal (n = 117). The data of GSE39001 (n = 37), GSE52903 (n = 55), and GSE44001 (n = 300) from the GEO database were considered as the validation group. All data with fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) was converted into transcript per million (TPM) format and log2 was converted for subsequent analysis. Data correction was conducted using the normalizeBetweenArrays function of the “limma” R package.

Single cell RNA sequencing analysis

Single cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) dataset was derived from GSE208653, containing two HPV-negative normal, two HPV-positive normal, two HPV-positive high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions (HSIL) and three HPV-positive cancer samples. ScRNA-seq analysis was conducted using “Seurat” R package. Under the criteria of cell quality control is that the mitochondrial content should be less than 20%, and the count of limit interval of UMI and gene is 200-100000 and 200–9000, respectively. Data normalization, selection of highly variable genes (2000), data transformation (vars.to.regress = c(“s.core”, “G2M.Score”)) were analyzed using the functions NormalizeData, FindVariableFeatures, and ScaleData of the “Seurat” R package, respectively. Batch effects were processed using “harmony”. Dimensional reduction methods such as UMAP and t-distributed stochastic neighbor-embedding (t-SNE) were utilized to visualize scRNA-seq data in various cell dimensions to be clustered by Louvain algorithm. We performed the FindAllMarkers function to screen differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between cell types. The Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method was applied for multiple testing correction, and genes with adjusted P values < 0.05, log2(fold change [FC]) > 0.25, and an expression ratio > 0.1 were considered significant.

Epigenetic regulation-related genes were obtained from the 720 epigenetic-related gene sets downloaded from the Epifactors database (v1.7.3). These genes were processed via “ConsensusClusterPlus” R package to generate molecular subtypes in the TCGA-CESC cohort. The optimal cluster number was determined by using consensus score matrix, CDF curve, PAC score and Nbclust.

WGCNA

The WGCNA of TCGA-CESC was generated using the “WGCNA” R package. The appropriate soft threshold β was measured to meet the standard of scale-free network. In addition, the weighted adjacency matrix was converted to a topological overlap matrix (TOM) and the corresponding dissimilarity (1-TOM) was obtained. The dynamic tree-cutting method was applied for module identification. In order to identify significant gene modules associated with clinical features, the modules with the highest correlation were selected for further analysis. Genes with both high gene significance (GS) and module membership (MM) were defined as hub genes.

Cell annotation analysis

We identified several cell markers including epithelial cells, fibroblast cells, endothelial cells, T cells, NK cells, B cells, Myeloid cells, neutrophil markers, and mast cells. In addition, epithelial cells and neutrophils were isolated and applied for exploring tumor heterogeneity.

Analysis of epithelial cells and neutrophils cell subgroups

We performed copy number variation (CNV) of normal epithelial cells to identify tumor cell subsets using InferCNV software. We used monocle2 software to perform pseudotime analysis for epithelial cell subsets with the dimension reduction algorithm DDRTree to get the process of cell differentiation. We used decouple R software for transcription factor analysis of various cell subpopulations. The activity score was measured based on a cell-by-cell basis to show the number of cells enriched in transcription factor (TF) and its downstream regulators. PROGENy model was utilized to predict the activity of tumor-associated pathways. The communication between cell types was analyzed using liana software by the function of “natmi”, “connectome”, “logfc”, “sca”, “cellphonedb”.

Establishment of the risk model

We used Scissor software (available at https://github.com/sunduanchen/Scissor) to correlate scRNA-seq data with survival data from the TCGA-CESC dataset. The alpha value was set to 0.05, while all other parameters were left at their default settings to identify negative and positive cells related to survival.

DEGs between Scissor-positive and Scissor-negative cells were identified using the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method for multiple testing correction. Genes with adjusted P values < 0.05, log2FC > 0.15, and an expression ratio > 0.1 were considered as differentially expressed genes.

The gene set of risk genes was obtained by intersection of DEGs and the module hub genes by WGCNA analysis. Univariate Cox analysis was performed to extract cervical cancer-related genes with prognostic value. The risk models were built using 101 machine learning methods and conclude a risk score for each patient. According to the cutoff value of the group determined by the surv_cutpoint function, patients with cervical cancer were divided into high-risk and low-risk groups.

Artificial intelligence-derived prognostic signature

To develop a consensus prognosis model AIDPS with high precision and stable performance, we integrated 10 machine learning algorithms. The comprehensive algorithms include random forest (RSF), elastic network (Enet), least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO), Ridge, Stepwise Cox, CoxBoost, partial least squares regression for Cox (plsRcox), supervised principal component (SuperPC), gradient boosting machine (GBM), and survival support vector machine (Survival-SVM).

The signature generation procedure is as follows: (a) univariate Cox regression identifies prognostic genes in the TCGA-CESC cohort; (b) 101 candidate machine-learning methods derived from 10 base algorithms used individually or in combination were evaluated to fit prognostic models within the leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) framework in the TCGA-CESC cohort; (c) all models were detected in six validation datasets (TCGA_Test, TCGA_Entire, CGCI-HTMCP-CC, GSE39001, GSE44001, and GSE52903); (d) the Harrell Consistency Index (C-index) was calculated in each validation dataset, and the model with the highest average C-index was considered the best.

Statistics

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.1.3). Categorical variables were compared by Chi-square test and continuous variables were compared by Wilcoxon rank sum test or t test. For analyses involving multiple comparisons, the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method was used for multiple testing correction, and adjusted P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.The best cutoff value was determined using survminer R package. The prognostic model based on CoxBoost + GBM does not have a simple formula like Lasso regression, as it is a machine learning-based approach rather than a traditional regression model. Univariate Cox regression and Kaplan-Meier analysis were performed using survival R package. Compare c-indexes for different variables using CompareC software.

ScRNA-seq analysis of CESC patients

After performing dimensionality reduction cluster analysis, a total of 31 clusters were obtained (Fig. 1A). We observed the distribution of cells from samples on the tSNE diagram (Fig. 1B) including HPV-negative normal, HPV-positive normal, HPV-positive HSIL and HPV-positive cancer samples. Significant cell markers were identified in each cluster in Fig. 1C. Each cluster was annotated as nine cell types (Fig. 1D). According to the expression of marker genes, we identified epithelial cell markers (“EPCAM”, “KRT18”, “KRT19”, “CDH1”), fibroblast marker (“DCN”, “THY1”, “COL1A1”, “COL1A2”), endothelial cell marker (“PECAM1”, “CLDN5”, “FLT1”, “RAMP2”), T cell marker (“CD3D”, “CD3E”, “CD3G”, “TRAC”), NK cell marker (“NKG7”, “GNLY”, “NCAM1”, “KLRD1”), B cell marker (“CD79A”, “IGHM”, “IGHG3”, “IGHA2”), myeloid cell marker (“LYZ”, “MARCO”, “CD68”, “FCGR3A”), neutrophil marker (“FCGR3B”, “CSF3R”), mast cell marker (“KIT”, “MS4A2”, “GATA2”) in Fig. 1E. We estimated the proportion of each cell types in each sample in Fig. 1F. HPV-negative normal samples contained higher endothelial and B cells than HPV-positive samples, while HPV-positive samples had more neutrophil cells than HPV-negative normal cells (Fig. 1G).

Clustering analysis of epithelial cells

Epithelial cells were isolated for dimensional reduction clustering analysis. The tSNE diagram of Fig. 2A, B showed 27 clusters and the distribution of each sample. We performed cell locus analysis on these epithelial cells, suggesting State2 (normal cells) may be the starting point of tumor analysis and differentiation. Normal cells bifurcated into two types of State1 (HSIL lesion direction) or State3 (tumor cells). In addition, State1 also developed into State3 (from lesion to tumor) in Fig. 2C–E. We also performed CNV analysis on the cells of each state. The CNV score of the three states showed State3 > State1> State2 (Fig. 2F). The CNV score of each sample showed HPV-positive cancer sample > HPV-positive HSIL sample > HPV-positive normal sample > HPV-negative normal sample (Fig. 2G). These results indicated that State2 is the starting point. Pearson correlation analysis between CNV score and pseudotime was conducted, showing a positive correlation between two groups (r = 0.32, P < 2.2e-16) in Fig. 2H.

Clustering analysis of neutrophils

Neutrophils were isolated for dimensional reduction cluster analysis. Seven clusters were shown in the tSNE diagram (Fig. 3A), and the distribution of each sample was exhibited in Fig. 3B. Pearson correlation analysis was applied for each cell type, showing cell subgroups were similar with each other except for c4 cells (Fig. 3C). The expression of marker genes in each cell population was visualized in Fig. 3D, E. In order to show the functional differences between c4 and other cell subgroups, we employed the enrichment analysis for c4 and c1 cell subgroups. The results indicated c4 was closely related to RNA splicing, while other cell groups such as c1 were mainly related to immunity (Fig. 3F, G). Subsequently, cell locus analysis of neutrophils, confirmed seven cell tracing directions from State1 to State7. HPV-negative normal cells occupy the beginning State region, HPV-positive HSIL cells occupy the middle State region, and HPV-positive cancer cells occupy the end State region (Fig. 3H-J). As shown in Fig. 3K, the proportion of seven states in each sample was presented.

Identification of epigenetic gene sets based on scRNA-seq and bulk RNA-seq data

We totally obtained 1615 scissor+ (positive) cells and 1540 scissor- (negative) cells in the TCGA-CESC cohort. Scissor+ cells symbolized a better impact on overall survival (OS) than scissor- cells. In the tSNE diagram of Fig. 4A, we showed the classification of, scissor+ cells, scissor- cells, and background cells. It was found that expression level of scissor+ cells in fibroblasts, endothelium and neutrophils was relatively higher, while expression level of scissor- cells in NK and T cells was relatively lower (Fig. 4B). The composition of scissor + and scissor- cells showed that epithelial, fibroblast and endothelial cells mainly exist in scissor+ cells, and T cells, myeloid cells and tumor cells mainly exist in scissor- cells (Fig. 4C). 4390 DEGs between scissor + and scissor- cells were presented in a volcano map (Fig. 4D). Based on the expression of 720 epigenetically associated genes, all CESC samples were initially grouped into k (k = 2–9) clusters. The ratio of the cumulative distribution function (CDF) curve of the consistency score matrix indicates that the best number is obtained when k = 2, resulting in two clusters (C1 and C2) (Fig. 4E, F). In the process of WGCNA, the correlation between the modules and immune cluster, age, TNM stages and other clinical traits was further calculated. The green module had the highest correlation with module-trait relationship of the clusters (Fig. 4G). The correlation coefficient between GS and MM reached 0.89 in the green module, indicating that the superior quality of gene module (Fig. 4H). 136 overlapping genes were selected for subsequent analysis through the intersection of results of scissor algorithm and WGCNA (Fig. 4I).

Construction of risk model AIDPS

According to the expression profiles of 136 overlapping genes, 24 prognostic genes were selected through univariate Cox analysis, based on their prognostic significance (p < 0.05) in the TCGA-Train dataset. To further investigate the biological relevance of the 24 prognostic genes included in the AIDPS model, KEGG and GO enrichment analyses were performed. KEGG analysis showed that these genes were significantly enriched in the cell cycle pathway (Supplementary Fig. S1A). Consistently, GO analysis demonstrated that these genes were mainly enriched in biological processes related to cell cycle, cell division, mitotic cell cycle, mitotic cell cycle process, chromosome segregation, nuclear division, mitotic nuclear division, and sister chromatid segregation (Supplementary Fig. S1B). These findings indicate that the 24 prognostic genes are predominantly involved in cell-cycle progression and mitotic regulation, supporting the biological relevance of the AIDPS signature in cervical cancer. The detailed functional annotation of the 24 prognostic genes is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

These 24 genes were subsequently included in our machine learning-based integration program to develop epigenetic-related genetic markers. The selection process, which involved evaluating the impact of each gene on overall survival, ensured that only genes with significant prognostic value were included, helping to minimize the risk of overfitting. In the TCGA dataset, we evaluated 101 candidate machine-learning methods derived from 10 base algorithms within the LOOCV framework, and further calculate the C-index for each model in all validation datasets (Fig. 5A). Interestingly, the best model was a combination of CoxBoost and GBM, with the highest average C-index (0.665), and this combined model had a leading C-index validated in other datasets. Next, the risk score for each patient was calculated using the expression of the prognostic gene by the optimal model. All patients were divided into high-risk group and low-risk group, according to the optimal threshold. As shown in the Fig. 5B, the OS of patients in the high-risk group was significantly lower than that in the low-risk group (P < 0.05). Meta-cohort which combined all samples also showed the similar results between risk groups (P < 0.05) in Fig. 5B.

Evaluation of the risk model AIDPS

The AUC of predicting 1-year, 3-year and 5-year OS in each cohort was exhibited in Fig. 6A with a high prognostic value. We presented the C-index [95% confidence interval] for each cohort (Fig. 6B). All results indicated that AIDPS has a stable and robust performance across multiple independent cohorts. We compared the performance of AIDPS with that of other clinics in predicting prognosis. As shown in Fig. 6C, the accuracy of AIDPS was significantly better than that of other features including age, pathological TNM stage, and tumor stage except for TCGA-Test and CGCI-HTMCP-CC datasets.

Comparison of AIDPS with existing prognostic models

To compare the performance of AIDPS with other researchers’ prognostic models, we conducted a comprehensive search of prognostic models in the published literature. We excluded prognostic model of miRNA due to lack of miRNA information in validation datasets of some chips. Finally, 52 prognostic models (including mRNA and lncRNA prognostic models) were collected. These characteristics were associated with various biological processes, such as immune response, autophagy, ferroptosis, stemness, epithelio-mesenchymal transformation, cuproptosis, hypoxia, glycolysis, lipogenesis, vitamin D, epigenetics, N6-methyladenosine, aging, WNT, and drug sensitivity. We performed univariate Cox regression between each prognostic model in all datasets, and observed that only the model AIDPS had significant association with prognostic outcomes among the validation cohorts (Fig. 7A). It demonstrates the stability of AIDPS. In addition, the C-index of AIDPS was compared with other characteristics. Notably, AIDPS showed better performance in each dataset than almost all models (Fig. 7B).

Discussion

As far as gynecological oncology is concerned, women around the world are troubled by cervical cancer. According to World Health Organization (WHO), high cervical cancer incidence regions have chronic HPV infection rates of 20%, but other regions with low cervical cancer incidence have only 5% [15]. There is a substantial burden associated with cervical cancer in terms of prevalence, incidence, and mortality [16]. There is some evidence that HPV vaccination is effective in preventing cervical cancer in the long run, yet actual results are unknown [17]. Few biomarkers are effective for prediction and treatment of cervical cancer, which have been the focus of research. We constructed and validated model AIDPS based on 24 consensus prognostic genes through a combination of 10 machine learning algorithms. Compared with published CESC gene signatures, AIDPS showed modest but consistent prognostic performance across multiple independent cohorts. Our innovative AIDPS showed preliminary prognostic capability but requires clinical validation before implementation.

Medical decision-making can be enhanced by machine learning, as evidenced by a growing number of studies [18]. The application of machine learning to a variety of medical conditions shows promise for prognosis with high accuracy [19]. The integration of machine-learning techniques for genomic discovery could be essential for accurately evaluating the efficacy of prognosis and response to treatment for CESC patients. In this study, we applied 10 common machine-learning algorithms to construct biomedical prognostic signatures. Several algorithms such as RSF, LASSO, CoxBoost, and Stepwise Cox were used for variable screening and dimensionality reduction, and the integration of CoxBoost and GBM, which achieved the highest average C-index (0.665) across the validation cohorts, was identified as the optimal model for cervical cancer prognosis. Here, we developed AIDPS, a computational model that integrates gene expression data and generate individual prognostic model based on cervical cancer’s distinctive genome-wide traits. In this study of machine-learning in CESC, we confirmed the model AIDPS was significantly associated with prognostic outcomes across multiple CESC cohorts with high prognostic value. We note that most models perform well on their own training datasets and some external datasets (e.g. Shi, Xu), but poorly on others. The reason may be poor generalization of the model from overfitting. Our model has dimensionally reduced by machine learning algorithms with better extrapolation potential. There is considerable evidence that clinicopathologic characteristics, like TNM stage, histologic grade, and Gleason score, are directly linked to the prognosis of cancer [20]. The accuracy of AIDPS in predicting CESC was significantly better than that of other clinical characteristics including age, pathological TNM stages, and grade. In this study, AIDPS gave the modest performance in prediction of metastases among patients with cervical cancer, independent of the TNM stage and tumor stage. Overall, our study offered an effective strategy to the clinical management and individualized treatment of CESC.

In this study, we screened consensus prognostic genes and constructed consensus model AIDPS based on bulk RNA-seq and scRNA-seq data. Dimensional reduction clustering was utilized to visualize scRNA-seq data for various cell types and identified the epithelial cells and neutrophils with high proportion. CESC has specific differentiation patterns. It is vital to determine the level of differentiation on cervical cancer histopathology [21].We suggested normal cells may be the starting point of differentiation, and normal cells and HSIL lesion ultimately bifurcate into tumor cells. CNV score of epithelial cells had a positive correlation with pseudotime. Cell locus analysis of neutrophils confirmed seven cell tracing directions from State1 to State 7. HPV-negative normal cells occupy the beginning State region, HPV-positive HSIL cells occupy the middle State region, and HPV-positive cancer cells occupy the end State region. It is of greatly medical and economic importance to identify biomarkers in progression and treatment of patients with HSIL and cervical cancer [22].

Limitation

This study demonstrated the prognostic value of AIDPS in cervical cancer for the first time, which had important clinical implications for CESC. However, there are some limitations in this study. First, it is lack of the estimation of genomic alterations, immune cell infiltration, and immunotherapy of patients with cervical cancer in low AIDPS and high AIDPS groups. Second, further research should focus on the validation of AIDPS in more clinical trials and identify the underlying mechanism of cervical cancer to offer evidence for personalized targeted therapy.Third, given the complexity of the model relative to sample sizes in some validation datasets, overfitting remains a potential concern that could affect generalizability to other populations.Fourth, although the CoxBoost + GBM framework showed robust prognostic performance, its interpretability remains more limited than that of conventional regression-based models because it does not provide a simple coefficient-based formula or a direct ranking of gene importance.

Conclusion

Our study developed a consensus model AIDPS, an effective strategy to further guide the clinical management and individualized treatment of cervical cancer.
