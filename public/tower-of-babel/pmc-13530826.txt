Poster 206. Chronic Distal Biceps Reconstruction Using Achilles Allograft Demonstrates Excellent Satisfaction and Functional Recovery: A Retrospective Cohort Study with Mean 4-Year Follow-Up
Authors: Larry Chen, Emily Berzolla, Samy Shelbaya, Isabella Jazrawi, Nathaniel G. Mercer, James Messina, Laith Jazrawi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:Chronic distal biceps tendon ruptures present a significant surgical challenge due to tendon retraction, scarring, fibrosis, and compromised tissue quality that often render primary repair impossible. In these difficult cases, tendon reconstruction with an allograft has emerged as a viable alternative for restoring elbow flexion and forearm supination. Among the available graft options, the Achilles tendon allograft offers favorable length, strength, and tissue handling properties. Despite its increasing use, the existing literature on long-term outcomes remains limited, particularly regarding patient-reported satisfaction, cosmetic appearance, and objective strength recovery. This study aimed to evaluate both clinical and functional outcomes following distal biceps tendon reconstruction with Achilles tendon allograft performed using the BRAIDS technique (Biceps Reconstruction with Achilles Interposition and Durable Suture). We hypothesized that this approach would yield excellent patient satisfaction and functional recovery, even in the presence of residual cosmetic deformities.Methods:We conducted a retrospective cohort study of patients with chronic distal biceps tendon ruptures who underwent reconstruction with Achilles tendon allograft performed by a single fellowship-trained surgeon between 2010 and 2022. All patients had a minimum of 12 months of follow-up. Patient-reported outcome measures included the Quick Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand (QuickDASH) and the American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) Self-Care subscore. Additional survey-based data assessed global satisfaction, willingness to undergo surgery again, symptom resolution, and overall functional improvement using a 7-point Likert scale. Patients also rated cosmetic satisfaction, focusing on both muscle contour and scar visibility. Objective evaluation of muscle performance was performed using isokinetic dynamometry to compare flexion and supination strength and endurance between the operative and contralateral arms. Statistical analysis included paired t-tests, with significance defined as p < 0.05.Results:Fourteen male patients met inclusion criteria, with a mean age of 46.4 years (SD 8.0, range 29-58) and mean BMI of 32.3 kg/m² (SD 5.3, range 24-42). The average interval from injury to surgery was 47.0 weeks (SD 31.5, range 12-96), and mean clinical follow-up was 47.1 months (SD 45.3, range 13-157). At final evaluation, patients demonstrated excellent upper extremity function, with a mean QuickDASH of 3.57 ± 4.61 and an ASES Self-Care score of 97.4 ± 6.16. All patients (100%) reported satisfaction with their current condition, and 93% expressed willingness to undergo the procedure again. On the global functional improvement scale, 21.4% rated themselves 'a very great deal better,' 50.0% 'a great deal better,' and 28.6% 'a good deal better.' Notably, 71.4% achieved complete symptom resolution, while no patients reported neutral or worsened outcomes. Cosmetic concerns were frequently noted, with 57.1% of patients identifying visible biceps asymmetry (28.6% mild, 28.6% moderate). Despite this, 64.3% expressed satisfaction or high satisfaction with overall arm appearance. Nearly all patients (93%) reported visible scarring, though 71.4% were satisfied with their scar's cosmetic outcome. Objective strength testing demonstrated near-complete restoration of function. There was no significant difference in flexion peak torque between the operative and contralateral arms (33.4 ± 10.8 vs. 33.6 ± 13.8 ft-lb; p = 0.943). Supination torque on the operative side trended lower but did not reach statistical significance (4.19 ± 1.53 vs. 5.11 ± 1.61 ft-lb; p = 0.077). Measures of endurance revealed no significant differences in fatigue for either flexion or supination between arms.Conclusions:Distal biceps tendon reconstruction with Achilles tendon allograft provides excellent functional restoration and high patient satisfaction in the treatment of chronic ruptures, even when performed in the setting of significant tendon retraction and poor tissue quality. Patients consistently reported meaningful improvement in daily function, minimal disability, and a strong willingness to undergo the procedure again. While cosmetic deformities and visible scarring were commonly observed, these did not substantially detract from overall satisfaction. Objective dynamometric testing confirmed robust recovery of flexion strength and endurance, with only minor nonsignificant deficits in supination strength. Taken together, these findings support Achilles tendon allograft reconstruction as a durable and reliable solution for chronic distal biceps tendon injuries, capable of restoring both objective performance and patient quality of life over long-term follow-up.
