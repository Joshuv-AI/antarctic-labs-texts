I
INTRODUCTION

Over the past few decades, stochastic differential equations (SDEs) evolving on Lie groups have played an important role in mathematics
[
1
]
. Among them, SDEs on the special orthogonal group
\text{SO}(n)
have attracted significant research attention due to their wide range of applications in robotics, molecular dynamics, and quantum control
[
2
,
3
]
.

For existing applications, it is important to develop geometry-preserving numerical schemes (GPNS) for SDEs on
\text{SO}(n)
. The purpose of a GPNS is to generate approximate solutions that remain entirely within the Lie group. Therefore, numerical schemes for Euclidean SDEs, such as those in
[
4
]
and
[
5
]
, cannot be directly applied to SDEs on Lie groups, as Euclidean methods do not account for the geometric constraint,
and lead to trajectories drifting away from the manifold
over long time scales.

It is natural to try to extend ordinary differential equation (ODE) numerical methods
on
\text{SO}(n)

[
6
]
to SDEs on
\text{SO}(n)
. This leads to numerical analysis schemes based on Stratonovich-SDEs (S-SDEs). But recently
[
7
]
showed that when the diffusion term is state dependent the Stratonovich scheme converges to the wrong SDE. The problem is that numerical schemes converge to Ito-SDEs (I-SDEs) and so numerical schemes must be based on the I-SDE not the S-SDE. Another way to see this is that ODEs evolve in the tangent plane whereas I-SDEs also contain a ‘pinning-drift’
[
7
]
in the normal to the Lie-Group.

Regarding GPNS for SDEs on
\text{SO}(n)
,
[
8
]
and
[
9
]
introduce GPNS for SDEs on general manifolds, where their approaches rely on either projection onto the manifold or solving Euclidean ODEs. As a result, they lack closed-form solutions. Specifically for
\text{SO}(n)
,
[
10
]
proposed a stochastic Lie EM (SL-EM) scheme with a strong convergence order of
\mathcal{O}(\delta^{\frac{1}{2}})
when the diffusion term is constant.
[
11
]
developed a stochastic Lie RKMK method for state-independent SDEs on
\text{SO}(n)
, and proved the strong convergence order
\mathcal{O}(\delta^{k})
for the
k
-th order Runge–Kutta method. Recently,
[
7
]
established a strong convergence rate of
\mathcal{O}(\delta^{\frac{1-\epsilon}{2}})
for state-dependent SDEs on
\mathrm{SO}(n)
, where
\epsilon
can be arbitrarily small.

All of the above works rely on the Magnus expansion of the exponential map on Lie groups
[
12
]
. However, explicitly computing the exponential map, such as using the Rodrigues formula on
\text{SO}(n)
, can be exponentially ill-conditioned
[
13
]
. Moreover, in practice, the exponential map is often approximated using the Padé approximation with scaling and squaring
[
10
]
, which involves computing a rational function of a matrix. Therefore, computing the exponential map on Lie groups can be computationally expensive.

An alternative approach avoiding computing the exponential map is the tangent space parametrization (TaSP), which originally emerged as a method for solving ODEs on Riemannian manifolds. Convergence results for TaSP applied to geometric SDEs remain quite limited.
[
14
]
proposed a TaSP scheme for SDEs on Stiefel manifolds, but no convergence results were provided.
[
15
]
showed convergence
when the Stiefel manifold reduces to a sphere.

Motivated by this research gap, we propose the TaSP method as a numerical scheme for SDEs on
\text{SO}(n)
and analyze its convergence rate. The contributions of this paper are summarized as follows:

•

We introduce the stochastic tangent space parametrization (S-TaSP) for state-dependent multiplicative SDEs on
\text{SO}(n)
. In contrast to the S-TaSP method on the Stiefel manifold, where the iteration is based on numerically solving quadratic matrix Riccati equations. The Lie group structure of
\text{SO}(n)
provides a simple and explicit framework to iterate.

•

We provide a convergence analysis of S-TaSP on
\text{SO}(n)
, showing a strong covergence rate of
\mathcal{O}(\delta^{\frac{1-\epsilon}{2}})
, which matches the convergence order of previous work while avoiding the computation of the exponential map.

•

We validate our theoretical results through numerical simulation, illustrating the effectiveness of our method.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In Section II, we reviews the geometry of
\text{SO}(n)
and the formulation of SDEs on
\text{SO}(n)
. In Section III, we introduces the proposed S-TaSP scheme for
\text{SO}(n)
. In Section IV, we presents the convergence analysis of the S-TaSP scheme. Section V provides numerical simulations to validate the theoretical results, and Section VI concludes the paper. Proof details are included in the Appendix.

II
PROBLEM STATEMENT

In this section, we first review the geometry of the special orthogonal group
\text{SO}(n)
. We then present the multiplicative SDE on
\text{SO}(n)
and formally introduce our problem.

II-A

Geometry of
\text{SO}(n)

The special orthogonal group
\text{SO}(n)=\{R\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\mid R^{T}R=I,\det(R)=1\}
is the set of all
n\times n
orthogonal matrices with determinant
1
. The associated Lie algebra
\mathfrak{so}(n)=\{\mathcal{Z}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\mid\mathcal{Z}^{T}=-\mathcal{Z}\}
is defined as all
n\times n
skew-symmetry matrix.

Given a point
R\in\text{SO}(n)
, we can write the tangent space at
R
as
T_{R}\text{SO}(n)=\{R\mathcal{Z}\mid\mathcal{Z}\in\mathfrak{so}(n)\}
. The normal space of
\text{SO}(n)
at
R
is defined as the orthogonal complement of
T_{R}\text{SO}(n)
in
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, which can be written as
N_{R}\text{SO}(n)=\{R\mathbf{C}\mid\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},\mathbf{C}=\mathbf{C}^{T}\}
. Projecting a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
onto the tangent space
T_{R}\text{SO}(n)
can be done by the projection operator
\Pi_{R}(X)=\frac{1}{2}(A-R^{T}A^{T}R)
.

II-B

Multiplicative SDE on
\text{SO}(n)

The multiplicative S-SDE on
\text{SO}(n)
is given by

\displaystyle dR=RB_{o,S}(R,t)dt+\sum_{j=1}^{d}RB_{j}(R,t)\circ dW_{j},

(1)

where
B_{o,S},B_{j}
are
n\times n
matrices,
W_{j}
are independent Brownian motions with variance
dt
and
\circ
denotes the Stratonovich integral. To ensure that the solution
R(t)
remains in
\text{SO}(n)
, it is necessary and sufficient that
B_{o,S}(R,t)
and all
B_{j}(R,t)
are skew-symmetric. Using the Stratonovich-to-Itô conversion formula
[
2
,
7
]
, the equivalent I-SDE in (
1
) is given by

\displaystyle dR=RB_{o,I}(R)dt+\sum_{j=1}^{d}RB_{j}(R)dW_{j}

(2)

where the Itô drift term
B_{o,I}
is expressed as

\displaystyle\begin{cases}B_{o,I}=B_{o,S}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}K_{j}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}B_{j}^{2},\\
K_{j}=\sum_{r,s=1}^{n}\frac{\partial B_{j}}{R_{rs}}(RB_{j})_{rs}.\end{cases}

From (
2
), note that all
K_{j}
are skew-symmetric, while the term
\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}B_{j}^{2}
is symmetric. Therefore, the Itô drift term
RB_{o,I}
contains normal components. This normal component highlights a fundamental difference between SDEs and ODEs on
\text{SO}(n)
, where in the latter,
K_{j}=B_{j}=0
. Additionally, it introduces challenges in developing numerical schemes of SDEs on
\text{SO}(n)
.

II-C

Problem Definition

In this paper, we aim to develop geometry-preserving numerical schemes (GPNS) for SDE (
2
) on
\text{SO}(n)
based on tangent space parametrization. A GPNS involves discretizing a time interval
[0,T]
into
M
equal time steps, each of size
\delta=T/M
. At each time step, the algorithm updates the state variable
\hat{R}_{t}
based on the drift and diffusion terms of the SDE, yielding an approximate solution
\hat{R}(t):[0,T]\to\text{SO}(n)
. A GPNS for the SDE in (
2
) is said to achieve a strong convergence order
p
if the expected error satisfies

\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{0\leq t\leq T}\|R(t)-\hat{R}(t)\|^{2}\right]^{\frac{1}{2}}\leq C\delta^{p},

where
C
is a constant independent of the step size
\delta
.

III
STOCHASTIC TaSP NUMERICAL SCHEME

In this section, we propose the S-TasP method for the SDE (
2
) on
\text{SO}(n)
. Suppose we are at the point
R_{0}\in\text{SO}(n)
and aim to move in the direction of the tangent vector
R_{0}\mathcal{Z}
, where
\mathcal{Z}\in\mathfrak{so}(n)
. A naive update of the form
R_{0}+R_{0}\mathcal{Z}
will generally not preserve the constraint for
\text{SO}(n)
. The key idea of the TaSP method is to introduce a normal correction term
R_{0}\mathbf{C}\in N_{R_{0}}\text{SO}(n)
, such that the updated point
R=R_{0}+R_{0}\mathcal{Z}+R_{0}\mathbf{C}
remains on the Lie group
\text{SO}(n)
.

In
[
14
]
, the authors studied the SDEs on Stiefel manifold, where the correction
\mathbf{C}
is obtained by solving a matrix Riccati equation numerically. In this work, we show that the Lie group structure allows a simple and explicit correction term on
\text{SO}(n)
, as stated in the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

Let
R_{0}\in\text{SO}(n)
and
\mathcal{Z}\in\mathfrak{so}(n)
. If
I-\mathcal{Z}^{T}\mathcal{Z}
is positive definite, then the normal correction term
\mathbf{C}_{0}\in N_{R_{0}}\text{SO}(n)
that ensures
R=R_{0}+R_{0}\mathcal{Z}+R_{0}\mathbf{C}\in\text{SO}(n)
is given by

\mathbf{C}=\sqrt{I-\mathcal{Z}^{T}\mathcal{Z}}-I,

where
\sqrt{A}
denotes the unique positive definite square root of the positive definite matrix
A
.

The correction term proposed in Theorem
1
is a key component for establishing the S-TasP method for SDEs on
\text{SO}(n)
. For a small time interval
[0,\delta]
and initial state
R_{0}
, the real solution of (
2
) can be parametrized by the tangent vector
\mathcal{Z}(t)
as

\displaystyle R(t)

\displaystyle=R_{0}(I+\mathcal{Z}(t)+\mathbf{C}(t)),\forall t\in[0,\delta].

(3)

Taking differentials of (
3
) we obtain

\displaystyle dR=R_{0}d\mathcal{Z}+R_{0}d\mathbf{C}

(4)

Projecting (
4
) onto the tangent space
T_{R_{0}}\text{SO}(n)
, we have

\displaystyle\Pi_{R_{0}}(dR)=\Pi_{R_{0}}(R_{0}d\mathcal{Z}+R_{0}d\mathbf{C})=R_{0}d\mathcal{Z}.

(5)

The last equality holds since the
\mathbf{C}
is symmetric and hence
R_{0}d\mathbf{C}\in N_{R_{0}}\text{SO}(n)
, while
R_{0}d\mathcal{Z}\in T_{R_{0}}\text{SO}(n)
.
Substituting the SDE (
2
) into (
5
), we obtain

\displaystyle d\mathcal{Z}=

\displaystyle R_{0}^{T}\Pi_{R_{0}}(RB_{o,I}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}RB_{j}dW_{j})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(B_{o,I}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}B_{j}^{2}\right)dt+\sum_{j=1}^{d}B_{j}dW_{j}.

(6)

which is a standard Euclidean SDE. In this case,
the S-TaSP scheme applies the one-step Euler
method at time
t=0
for the SDE (
6
), yielding

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}_{0}=\left(B_{o,I}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{d}B_{j}^{2}\right)\delta+\sum_{j=1}^{d}B_{j}\sqrt{\delta}\epsilon_{j},\epsilon_{j}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1).

Then the S-TaSP scheme updates the state variable
R_{1}
by

\displaystyle R_{1}=R_{0}(I+\mathcal{Z}_{0}+\mathbf{C}_{0})=R_{0}(\mathcal{Z}_{0}+\sqrt{I-\mathcal{Z}_{0}^{T}\mathcal{Z}_{0}})

after which we restart the process from the new point
R_{1}
. The whole process of TaSP can be summarized in Algorithm
1
.

IV
CONVERGENCE ANALYSIS

To analyze the convergence of the S-TaSP scheme, we impose the following assumptions, which are adapted from standard assumptions for SDE numerical scheme on Euclidean spaces or Lie groups
[
4
,
7
]
.

Assumption 1
.

The term
B_{o,I}
and
B_{j},j\in[d]
are
L
-Lipschitz continuous on
\text{SO}(n)
, i.e., there exist an
L\in\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle\begin{cases}B_{o,I}(R)-B_{o,I}(R^{\prime})\leq L\|R-R^{\prime}\|_{F},\\
B_{J}(R)-B_{J}(R^{\prime})\leq L\|R-R^{\prime}\|_{F},j\in[d].\end{cases}

Assumption 2
.

The term
B_{o,I}
and
B_{j},j\in[d]
are bounded on
\text{SO}(n)
with bounds
C
.

Then we carry out our convergence analysis by introducing the immediate sequence
R_{\delta}
as

\displaystyle R_{\delta}(t)-R_{0}=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\hat{R}(t)B_{o,I}(\hat{R}(t))dt

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\hat{R}(t)\sum_{j=1}^{d}B_{j}(\hat{R}(t))dW_{j}(t)

In this case, the convergence analysis of the S-TaSP scheme is divided into two parts

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|\hat{R}(t)-R_{\delta}(t)\|_{F}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|a_{\delta}(t)\|_{F}^{2}\right]+2\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|\nu_{\delta}(t)\|_{F}^{2}\right],

where

\displaystyle a_{\delta}(t)=R_{\delta}(t)-\hat{R}(t),\quad\nu_{\delta}(t)=\hat{R}(t)-R(t).

We analyze the above two terms in the following lemmas.

Lemma 1
.

Under Assumptions
1
and
2
, the error between
\hat{R}(t)
and the
R_{\delta}(t)
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|\hat{R}(t)-R_{\delta}(t)\|_{F}^{2}\right]\leq\mathcal{O}(\delta^{1-\epsilon}),

for any arbitrary small
\epsilon>0
.

The proof of Lemma
1
is given in Appendix.

Lemma 2
.

Under Assumptions
1
and
2
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|R_{\delta}(t)-R(t)\|_{F}^{2}\right]\leq\mathcal{O}(\delta^{1-\epsilon}).

Proof:
It follows Result IIIb in
[
15
]
.
\blacksquare

Combining the two lemmas, we have the main result of the convergence order
\mathcal{O}(\delta^{\frac{1-\epsilon}{2}})
of the S-TaSP scheme as follows.

Theorem 2
.

Under Assumptions 1 and 2, the S-TaSP scheme converges to the real solution
R(t)
of the SDE (
2
) on
\text{SO}(n)
with strong order
\frac{1-\epsilon}{2}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|\hat{R}(t)-R(t)\|_{F}^{2}\right]\leq\mathcal{O}(\delta^{1-\epsilon}),

for any arbitrary small
\epsilon>0
.

F

VI
Conclusion

In this paper, we have developed a numerical scheme for simulating SDEs on the special orthogonal group
\text{SO}(n)
. The proposed scheme is based on tangent space parametrization and avoids computing cost of the exponential map. We also provide a convergence analysis of the numerical scheme, and numerical simulation illustrates the effectiveness of our results.

For future work, we aim to extend the stochastic tangent space parametrization scheme to general Riemannian manifolds and analyze the convergence performance.

VII-A

Proof of Theorem
1

For the simplicity, we introduce some notations before the proof. For any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
, we denote
E(\lambda)
as the
2\times 2
block
[\begin{smallmatrix}0&\lambda\\
-\lambda&0\end{smallmatrix}]
and and
D(\lambda)
as the
2\times 2
block
[\begin{smallmatrix}\lambda&0\\
0&\lambda\end{smallmatrix}]
. For any
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{l}
, and
n\geq 2l
, we denote the block-diagonal
\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
matrix as

\displaystyle E_{n}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{l})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{diag}(E(\lambda_{1}),E(\lambda_{2}),\dots,E(\lambda_{l}),0),

\displaystyle D_{n}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{l})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{diag}(D(\lambda_{1}),D(\lambda_{2}),\dots,D(\lambda_{l}),1).

Proof:

We write
R=R_{0}(I+\mathcal{Z}+\mathbf{C})
. Thus, it suffices to show that
I+\mathcal{Z}+\mathbf{C}\in\text{SO}(n)
.

Since
\mathcal{Z}
is skew-symmetric, by the real Schur decomposition, there exists an orthogonal matrix
P
and a sequence
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
such that:

\mathcal{Z}=P\Theta P^{T},\quad\Theta=E_{n}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{l}).

Since
\mathcal{Z}^{T}\mathcal{Z}=P\Theta^{T}\Theta P^{T}
, we have

\displaystyle I-\mathcal{Z}^{T}\mathcal{Z}=P(I-\Theta^{T}\Theta)P^{T},

\displaystyle I-\Theta^{T}\Theta=D_{n}(1-\lambda_{1}^{2},1-\lambda_{2}^{2},\dots,1-\lambda_{l}^{2}).

Since
I-\mathcal{Z}^{T}\mathcal{Z}
is positive definite, we have
1-\lambda_{l}^{2}>0
for all
l
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\sqrt{I-\mathcal{Z}^{T}\mathcal{Z}}=P\Lambda P^{T},

\displaystyle\Lambda=D_{n}(\sqrt{1-\lambda_{1}^{2}},\sqrt{1-\lambda_{2}^{2}},\dots,\sqrt{1-\lambda_{l}^{2}}).

We now examine
I+\mathcal{Z}+\mathbf{C}
. By expanding terms, we write

I+\mathcal{Z}+\mathbf{C}=P(\Theta+\Lambda)P^{T}.

Since
|\lambda_{l}|<1
, there exists an angle
\theta_{l}\in[-\pi/2,\pi/2]
such that
\lambda_{l}=\sin(\theta_{l})
and
\sqrt{1-\lambda_{l}^{2}}=\cos(\theta_{l})
. Setting

\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}=P\tilde{\Theta}P^{T},\quad\tilde{\Theta}=E_{n}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2},\dots,\theta_{l}).

it follows that
\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}\in\mathfrak{so}(n)
. By properties of the matrix exponential, we have

\exp(\tilde{\mathcal{Z}})=P\exp(\tilde{\Theta})P^{T},

where

\displaystyle\exp(\tilde{\Theta})=

\displaystyle D_{n}(\cos(\theta_{1}),\cos(\theta_{2}),\dots,\cos(\theta_{l}))

\displaystyle+E_{n}(\sin(\theta_{1}),\sin(\theta_{2}),\dots,\sin(\theta_{l}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Lambda+\Theta.

Thus,
\exp(\tilde{\mathcal{Z}})=I+\mathcal{Z}+\mathbf{C}
, and since
\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}
is skew-symmetric, we have
\exp(\tilde{\mathcal{Z}})\in\text{SO}(n)
, which completes the proof.
\blacksquare

Lemma 4
.

For any
\mathcal{Z}\in\mathfrak{so}(n)
with
I-\mathcal{Z}^{T}\mathcal{Z}
is positive definite, there holds

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{C}-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{Z}^{2}\|_{F}\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\mathcal{Z}\|_{F}^{4},

where
\|\cdot\|_{F}
is the Frobenius norm.

Proof:

Let
\mathcal{Z}=P\Theta P^{T}
be the Schur decomposition of
\mathcal{Z}
as in the proof of Theorem
1
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\sqrt{I-\mathcal{Z}^{T}\mathcal{Z}}=PD_{n}(\sqrt{1-\lambda_{1}^{2}},\dots,\sqrt{1-\lambda_{l}^{2}})P^{T}\\
I+\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{Z}^{2}=PD_{n}\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{1}^{2},\dots,1-\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{l}^{2}\right)P^{T}\\
\mathcal{Z}^{4}=PD_{n}\left(\lambda_{1}^{4},\dots,\lambda_{l}^{4}\right)P^{T}\end{cases}

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\mathbf{C}-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{Z}^{2}=PD_{n}\left(e_{1},\dots,e_{l}\right)P^{T},\\
e_{i}=\sqrt{1-\lambda_{i}^{2}}-1+\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{i}^{2}.\end{cases}

Since
I-\mathcal{Z}^{T}\mathcal{Z}
is positive definite, we have
\theta_{i}<1
for all
i
. Thus, we have

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{i}^{4}\leq e_{i}=\sqrt{1-\lambda_{i}^{2}}-1+\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{i}^{2}\leq 0,

which implies that

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{C}-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{Z}^{2}\|_{F}=

\displaystyle\sqrt{2\sum_{i=1}^{l}\left(e_{i}\right)^{2}}\leq\sqrt{2\sum_{i=1}^{l}(\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{i}^{4})^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\|\mathcal{Z}^{4}\|_{F}\leq\frac{1}{2}\|\mathcal{Z}\|_{F}^{4},

which completes the proof.

Denote

\displaystyle\hat{R}_{m}=

\displaystyle E_{k}=

\displaystyle\mathbf{C}_{k}-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{Z}_{k}^{2}=\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{Z}_{k}^{4}).

Thus, we have

\displaystyle\hat{R}_{m}-R_{\delta}(m\delta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\hat{R}_{k}\left(\mathcal{Z}_{k}-B_{e,k}\delta-\sum_{j=1}^{d}B_{j}(\hat{R}_{k})\sqrt{\delta}\epsilon_{j,k}\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\hat{R}_{k}\left(\mathcal{Z}_{k}^{2}-\sum_{j=1}^{d}B_{j}^{2}(\hat{R}_{k})\right)+\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\hat{R}_{k}E_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{R}_{k}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}(E^{b}_{k}+E^{\delta}_{k})+\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\hat{R}_{k}E_{k}.

where

\displaystyle E^{b}_{k}=\frac{1}{2}\delta\hat{R}_{k}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\sum_{j=1}^{d}B_{j}^{2}(\hat{R}_{k})(\epsilon_{j,k}^{2}-1)

\displaystyle E^{\delta}_{k}=\frac{1}{2}\delta\hat{R}_{k}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\sum_{j^{\prime}\neq j}B_{j}(\hat{R}_{k})B_{j^{\prime}}(\hat{R}_{k})\epsilon_{j,k}\epsilon_{j^{\prime},k}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\delta^{\frac{3}{2}}\hat{R}_{k}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}B_{o,I}(\hat{R}_{k})\sum_{j=1}^{d}B_{j}(\hat{R}_{k})\epsilon_{j,k}+\frac{1}{2}\delta^{2}\hat{R}_{k}B_{e,k}^{2}.

Then we have, for
t\in[m\delta,(m+1)\delta]

\displaystyle\hat{R}(t)-R_{\delta}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{R}_{m}-R_{\delta}(m\delta)+R_{\delta}(m\delta)-R_{\delta}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\hat{R}_{k}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}E^{b}_{k}+\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\hat{R}_{k}E_{k}+(t-m\delta)\hat{R}_{m}B_{o,I}(\hat{R}_{m})

\displaystyle+\hat{R}_{k}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}E^{\delta}_{k}+\hat{R}_{m}\sum_{j=1}^{d}B_{j}(\hat{R}_{m})\big(W_{j}(t)-W_{j}(m\delta)\big).

Taking the expectation on both sides, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{0\leq t\leq T}\|\hat{R}(t)-R_{\delta}(t)\|_{F}^{2}\right]\leq 5a+5b+5c+5d+5e,

\displaystyle\begin{cases}a=\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{m\in[M]}\|\hat{R}_{k}\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}E^{b}_{k}\|_{F}^{2}\right],\\

where

.

[
15
]

c

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{m\in[M]}\|\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\hat{R}_{k}E_{k}\|_{F}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle M\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}\|\hat{R}_{k}E_{k}\|_{F}^{2}\right]\leq 9M\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{M-1}\|E_{k}\|_{F}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\|E_{k}\|_{F}^{2}\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\|\mathcal{Z}_{k}\|_{F}^{8}\leq\frac{1}{4}\left(\|B_{e,m}\delta+\sqrt{\delta}\sum_{j=1}^{d}B_{j}(\hat{R}_{k})\epsilon_{j,k}\right)^{8}
