An anisotropic Alt-Caffarelli problem of higher order
Marius Müller

Abstract
. We study a higher order version of the Alt-Caffarelli problem in two dimensions, where the Dirichlet energy is replaced by an anisotropic bending energy. This extends a previous study of the isotropic case in [ 40 ] . It turns out that smooth anisotropies do not affect the optimal C 2 , 1 C^{2,1} -regularity of minimizers. The proof requires an anisotropic version of an estimate by Frehse for the fundamental solution of the bilaplacian. This generalization paves the way for further studies of various free boundary problems of higher order.

1.
Introduction

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a smooth bounded domain, here in most cases
n=2
. We seek to minimize

(1.1)

\mathcal{E}(u):=\int_{\Omega}[\mathrm{div}(A(x)\nabla u(x))]^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x+|\{u>0\}|\quad\textrm{among all $u\in W^{2,2}(\Omega):u|_{\partial\Omega}=u_{0},$}

where the boundary datum
u_{0}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
satisfies
u_{0}>0
and the
coefficient matrix

A\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{n\times n})
is symmetric and
uniformly elliptic
, i.e. there exists
\lambda>0
such
\lambda|\xi|^{2}\leq(A(x)\xi,\xi)
for all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Moreover,
|\{u>0\}|
is shorthand for the Lebesgue measure of
\{x\in\Omega:u(x)>0\}.

This problem can be regarded as a higher order version of the
Alt-Caffarelli free boundary problem
[
2
]
for anisotropic materials. In the classical problem one minimizes

(1.2)

\mathcal{F}(u):=\int_{\Omega}A(x)\nabla u(x)\cdot\nabla u(x)\;\mathrm{d}x+|\{u>0\}|\quad\textrm{among all $u\in W^{1,2}(\Omega):u|_{\partial\Omega}=u_{0}.$}

Existence and optimal regularity of minimizers of
\mathcal{F}
have been studied by Alt and Caffarelli in
[
2
]
. The authors show (best possible) global
W^{1,\infty}
-regularity of any minimizer
u
in any dimension and smoothness of the free boundary
\partial\{u>0\}
for
n=2
. Later on, the regularity of the free boundary has also been investigated in higher dimensions, with positive smoothness results for
n\in\{3,4\}
and counterexamples for
n=7
, cf.
[
11
,
15
,
29
]
. The authors of these articles specialize on the case
A=I_{n}
, but Alt and Caffarelli remark in
[
2
]
that this is not a restriction, despite their proof relies substantially on mean value properties of (super)harmonic functions. Such mean value properties have been established for (super)solutions of
-\mathrm{div}(A\nabla u)=0
in
[
13
]
, though a rigorous derivation requires extra details, provided by Blank and Hao in
[
7
]
. A detailed study of the minimization of (
1.2
) for general
A
can be found in
[
3
,
18
]
. Nonconstant coefficients are actually important in the applications: As already observed in
[
23
]
, one needs nonconstant coefficients for the description of an axissymetric jet flow coming out of a nozzle, which is one of the main applications of the problem. Also degenerate, nonlinear and nonlocal free boundary problems of Alt-Caffarelli-type have been considered, see e.g.
[
1
,
14
,
12
]

and references therein.

The functionals
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{F}
have one thing in common: minimizers have to find a balance between
not bending too much
and
being nonpositive on a large set
. Due to the fact that reaching the zero level requires bending, these interests are conflicting. There is however one major difference between
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{F}
: The maximum principle implies that each minimizer
u\in W^{1,2}(\Omega)
of
\mathcal{F}
must satisfy
u\geq 0
. This can easily be seen by comparison with
\mathcal{F}(u^{+})
, where
u^{+}=\mathrm{max}(u,0)\in W^{1,2}(\Omega)
(which is admissible due to the positivity of
u_{0}
). In particular,
\Omega
is divided into the sets
\{u>0\}
and
\{u=0\}
. For functions
u\in W^{1,2}(\Omega)
with small values of

\mathcal{F}(u)
,
\{u=0\}
is hence expected to be a large set which can be thought of as a
flat island
inside
\Omega
.

On the contrary, minimizers of
\mathcal{E}
behave differently. In the case of
A=I_{2}
it has been obtained in
[
40
,
42
]
that minimizers
u
satisfy
u\in C^{2,1}(\overline{\Omega})
and
\nabla u\neq 0
on
\{u=0\}
in dimension
n=2
. As a consequence,
u
is
not
flat around the zero level but inevitably changes sign and drops below the zero level. Hence,
\Omega
is divided into
\{u>0\}
and
\{u<0\}
, both limited by the
C^{2,1}
-interface
\{u=0\}
, which has Lebesgue measure zero (unlike in the classical problem). This article retrieves a similar behavior in the nonconstant coefficient case. Considering that the first summand of
\mathcal{E}
measures elastic bending, this seems natural — flatness at the zero level would require unnecessary bending.

The functional
\mathcal{E}
has recently been studied for
A=I_{n}
, e.g. in
[
16
,
17
,
40
]
. We believe it deserves some attention due to its possible applications. One mechanical application (which is in most cases accompanied by an additional obstacle constraint) is the description of how elastic bodies contact rough solid surfaces. An example for this is given by
gecko adhesion
to tree surfaces see e.g.
[
28
,
45
,
33
]
. Here, the most accurate functional describing the energy is not
\mathcal{E}
but rather the
adhesive Willmore bending energy

(1.3)

\mathcal{W}(u):=\int_{\Omega}H[u]^{2}\;\mathrm{d}S+|\{u>0\}|,

where
H[u]:=\mathrm{div}(\frac{\nabla u}{\sqrt{1+|\nabla u|^{2}}})
denotes the mean curvature of the graph of
u
and
\mathrm{d}S:=\sqrt{1+|\nabla u|^{2}}\;\mathrm{d}x
is the surface element. Minimizers of (
1.3
) have been studied mathematically in dimension
n=1
by Miura
[
38
,
39
]
in different settings. A two-dimensional approach based on Kirchhoff-Love plate theory is proposed in
[
45
]
.
If
|\nabla u|
is assumed small the functional
\mathcal{W}
is well approximated by
\mathcal{E}
with
A=I_{2}
. Since this is only an approximation it seems reasonable to study how robust the behavior of minimizers is with respect to changes of the operator. Furthermore,
L:=-\mathrm{div}(A\nabla)
appears (up to a term of order zero) as a linearized version of the
anisotropic mean curvature
, cf.
[
44
]
and references therein. At first sight, the mechanical application only seems convincing when the rough solid is not deformable, leading to the obstacle constraint
u\geq 0
. However, if the touched solid itself is deformable then it might seem reasonable to relax this constraint. In some applications it is observed that deformations of the solid must be taken into account — for example on microscopic scale due to the effect of
elasto-capillarity

[
6
]
.

The original application of the classical problem (
1.2
) was to describe the flow of a fluid through a nozzle, or alternatively the wake left behind by a fluid after it hits an obstacle. Higher order operators can also play a role in this type of application. Indeed, as seen in
[
27
, Chapter 3]
, the stream function of a creeping fluid is described by the biharmonic equation
\Delta^{2}\psi=0
. However, the functional
\mathcal{E}
does not seem suitable for this application, as no flat islands are found in energy-minimal configurations. Therefore, it is not possible to identify the
shadow zone
of the fluid flow.
If one intends to resemble the aforementioned
flat island behavior
in a higher order setting, it seems more natural to study

(1.4)

\mathcal{G}(u):=\int_{\Omega}[\mathrm{div}(A(x)\nabla u(x))]^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x+|\{u\neq 0\}|.

This has been done in
[
25
]
in the case of
A=I_{n}
,
n\geq 2
, obtaining entirely different regularity results (such as impossibility of
C^{2}
-regularity for
\Omega=B_{1}(0)
with constant boundary data). A functional similar to
\mathcal{G}
also appears in the shape optimization problem for the buckling load of a clamped plate, see e.g.
[
4
,
46
]
.

In the past there have been many contributions involving higher order free boundary problems for the
isotropic
biharmonic operator, see e.g.
[
22
,
10
,
9
,
43
]
on the (parabolic) biharmonic obstacle problem and on higher order variational inequalities. Such problems seem to be less studied for anisotropic bending energies (at least in dimension
n\geq 2
) — to the best of our knowledge we have only found
[
21
,
31
]
.

The isotropic results in
[
22
,
10
,
9
,
43
]
rely on one crucial observation: If
V
is the fundamental solution of
(-\Delta)^{2}
in dimension
n\geq 2
then

(1.5)

\partial^{2}_{x_{j}x_{j}}V-\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta V\geq-1\qquad\textrm{for all $j=1,...,n$.}

This bound is remarkable given that both summands have a singularity near
x=0
for any
n\geq 2
. Formula (
1.5
) can be verified easily by direct computation using that (cf.
[
37
, Chapter 7.3]
)

(1.6)

V(x)=\begin{cases}c_{n}|x|^{4-n}&n\geq 5,n=3\quad(c_{n}>0\textrm{ a constant}),\\
-\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\log|x|&n=4,\\
\frac{1}{8\pi}|x|^{2}\log|x|&n=2.\end{cases}

For higher dimensions
n\geq 3
, (
1.5
) even holds true with
0
instead of
-1
on the right hand side, cf.
[
22
, p. 11]
. Better estimates exist in the special case of dimension
n=2
: also a bound from above can be obtained in (
1.5
) and the mixed derivative
\partial^{2}_{x_{1}x_{2}}V
is also bounded from above and below.
Estimate (
1.5
) provides an important link between the Laplacian
\Delta V
and the full second derivative
D^{2}V
, which is an integral part of the arguments in
[
22
,
10
,
9
,
43
,
40
]
. More precisely, in dimension
n=2
one has

(1.7)

D^{2}V=\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta V\cdot I_{2}+M,\qquad\textrm{where}\quad M=\begin{pmatrix}\partial_{x_{1}x_{1}}^{2}V-\frac{1}{2}\Delta V&\partial^{2}_{x_{1}x_{2}}V\\
\partial^{2}_{x_{1}x_{2}}V&\partial_{x_{2}x_{2}}^{2}V-\frac{1}{2}\Delta V\end{pmatrix}\quad\textrm{has only bounded entries.}

We refer to this from now on as
Frehse’s observation
and shall propose a generalisation of it in this article to operators of the form
(-\mathrm{div}(A\nabla))^{2}
for nonconstant coefficients
A
.
If the coefficients
A
are analytic, a fundamental solution
V_{A}
for
(-\mathrm{div}(A\nabla))^{2}
was constructed in
[
30
, Chapter III]
. Due to the less explicit form it is nonstandard to obtain a result like (
1.7
) in this case. As already observed in
[
22
]
, (
1.7
) carries over to distributional solutions
u
of
(-\Delta)^{2}u=\mu
, where
\mu
is a finite Radon measure. Therefore, the generalization to anisotropic settings requires the study of
[-\mathrm{div}(A\nabla)]^{2}u=\mu
.
It turns out that
I_{2}
in (
1.7
) must be replaced by
A^{-1}
. This is in our opinion nontrivial and requires the consideration of a special frame of matrices that are orthonormal in a certain Riemannian metric associated to the operator. For details we refer to Section
3
.

The anisotropic version of Frehse’s observation can actually be seen as a main novelty of this article. The present result about the anisotropic higher order Alt-Caffarelli problem is only one use case: Once the anisotropic Frehse observation is obtained, the proof becomes just a modification of the methods in
[
40
,
42
]
using the mean value properties derived in
[
7
]
. In our opinion the result paves the way for further study of various higher order free boundary problems,
[
22
,
10
,
9
,
43
]
provides only an incomplete list. The details shall be subject of future research.

1.1.
Main results

Unless stated otherwise we assume that
A\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2})
is symmetric and uniformly elliptic. Our approach would actually work with minor modifications for less regular coefficients, precisely with
A\in C^{4,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega};\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2})
. It becomes however visible, for example in the proof of Lemma
6.10
, that the argument must be adapted in case that
A\not\in C^{4,\alpha}
. Throughout the article we work in dimension
n=2
and define for a suitably smooth
u
function
Lu:=-\mathrm{div}(A\nabla u)
and
L^{2}u:=L(Lu)
. Furthermore,
\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}_{sym}
denotes the set of symmetric
2\times 2
-matrices.
Our first main theorem deals with the anisotropic Frehse observation, which we formulate here for solutions of the measure valued equation
L^{2}u=\mu
.

Theorem 1.1

(Anisotropic Frehse Observation)
.

Let
u\in W^{2,2}_{loc}(\Omega)
be such that for some finite Radon measure
\mu
one has
L^{2}u=\mu
in the sense of distributions, i.e.

(1.8)

\int_{\Omega}LuL\varphi\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\varphi\;\mathrm{d}\mu\qquad\textrm{for all }\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega).

Then, there exists a Borel measurable function
K:\Omega\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}_{sym}
which is locally bounded and smooth in
(\Omega\times\Omega)\setminus\{(x,x):x\in\Omega\}
and
H\in C^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}_{sym})
such that

(1.9)

D^{2}u(x)=\tfrac{1}{2}(-Lu(x))A(x)^{-1}+\int_{\Omega}K(x,y)\;\mathrm{d}\mu(y)+H(x)\qquad\textrm{for almost every $x\in\Omega$}.

The proof is based on the study of Green’s function
G_{L^{2}}
for the Navier problem

(1.10)

\begin{cases}L^{2}G_{L^{2}}(x,\cdot)=\delta_{x}&\textrm{in $\Omega$}\\
G_{L^{2}}(x,\cdot)=LG_{L^{2}}(x,\cdot)=0&\textrm{on $\partial\Omega$}\end{cases}.

The representation in (
1.9
) a posteriori also holds for
G_{L^{2}}(z,\cdot)
with
\mu=\delta_{z}
(the Dirac measure with point mass at
z\in\Omega
), yielding that
D_{y}^{2}G_{L^{2}}(z,y)
can be represented up to locally bounded terms by
\frac{1}{2}(-LG_{L^{2}}(z,y))A(y)^{-1}.
This is again remarkable as both terms have a logarithmic singularity at
y=z
.
An important observation needed in the proof is that
G_{L^{2}}
is locally given by a term of the form

(1.11)

G_{L^{2}}(x,y)=\psi_{x}(y)\log(\psi_{x}(y))+\textrm{smoother terms},\quad\textrm{where}\quad\psi_{x}(y)=A(y)^{-1}(y-x)\cdot(y-x).

A further crucial step is the following: if
\{A_{1}(y)=A(y),A_{2}(y),A_{3}(y)\},(y\in\overline{\Omega})
is an
orthogonal frame
of
\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}_{sym}
with respect to the
associated Riemannian metric

g_{A}(y)(M_{1},M_{2}):=\mathrm{tr}(A(y)^{-1}M_{1}M_{2}A(y)^{-1})
(
y\in\overline{\Omega},M_{1},M_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}_{sym}
), then
\mathrm{div}(A_{i}\nabla(\psi_{x}\log\psi_{x}))
is bounded for
i=2,3
(but not for
i=1
).

The second main result applies these findings to the higher order Alt-Caffarelli problem and generalizes the results of
[
40
,
42
]
. Recall that we seek to minimize
\mathcal{E}
given in (
1.1
) in the admissible set

\mathcal{A}(u_{0}):=\{u\in W^{2,2}(\Omega):u-u_{0}\in W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)\}.
Existence of minimizers in
\mathcal{A}(u_{0})
is a standard result, see Proposition
4.3
, but uniqueness is in general false, even for
A=I_{2}
(see
[
40
, Section 8]
).

Theorem 1.2

(Higher Order Alt-Caffarelli Problem)
.

Let
u\in\mathcal{A}(u_{0})
be a minimizer of
\mathcal{E}
. Then,
u\in C^{2,1}(\overline{\Omega})\cap C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega}\setminus\{u=0\})
,
\nabla u\neq 0
on
\{u=0\}
and the set
\{u<0\}
is a union of finitely many
C^{2,1}
-domains compactly contained in
\Omega
. Moreover,
Lu|_{\partial\Omega}=0
and

(1.12)

2\int_{\Omega}LuL\varphi\;\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\{u=0\}}\frac{1}{|\nabla u|}\varphi\,\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}\qquad\textrm{for all }\varphi\in W^{2,2}(\Omega)\cap W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega).

We remark that the
C^{2,1}
regularity is optimal, even for
A=I_{2}
. Indeed,
[
17
, Section 6]
shows that even in one dimension
u\not\in C^{3}(\Omega)
and
[
40
, Section 10]
exposes minimizers for radial data which do not lie in
C^{3}(\Omega)
. Another way to see this is as follows: (
1.12
) and
[
41
, Section 2.3]
imply that
Lu
can not lie in
C^{1}(\Omega)
if
\{u=0\}\neq\emptyset
(which can indeed be satisfied for each minimizer, cf. Example
4.7
).

A crucial property in Theorem
1.2
is that
\nabla u\neq 0
on
\{u=0\}
for each minimizer
u\in\mathcal{A}(u_{0})
. The main ingredient in its proof is a blow-up technique that excludes points
x_{0}
with
u(x_{0})=0,\nabla u(x_{0})=0
. This blow-up is constructed as follows: with the aid of Theorem
1.1
and variational inequalities one can show local semiconvexity of the minimizer. Thereupon, a version of the Aleksandrov theorem yields a second order Taylor expansion at each Lebesgue point of
D^{2}u
. In particular, if
x_{0}
is a Lebesgue point of
D^{2}u
and
u(x_{0})=0,\nabla u(x_{0})=0
one can identify a
2
-homogeneous blow-up profile for
u
at
x_{0}
. Given this explicit profile we can study how the Lebesgue measure term behaves around
x_{0}
and rule out the existence of
x_{0}
with variational arguments.

The case that
x_{0}
is a non-Lebesgue point of
D^{2}u
has to be treated seperately. As a consequence of Theorem
1.1
(and some additional treatment of atoms of
\mu
) non-Lebesgue points of
D^{2}u
must also be non-Lebesgue points of
-Lu
. Since
-Lu
is easily seen to be
L
-superharmonic, properties of
L
-superharmonic functions can be used to exclude the existence of such
x_{0}
. More precisely, we use that
L
-superharmonic functions must attain large values around non-Lebesgue points (see Lemma
2.4
). Notice that large values of
-Lu
account for a
very convex
behavior of
u
around
x_{0}
(again using Theorem
1.1
). Such very convex behavior can not occur in energy-minimal configurations.

The article is structured as follows. In Section
2
we collect preliminaries about elliptic differential operators, such as consequences of
[
7
]
for supersolutions of elliptic equations and some first properties of Green’s function. In Section
3
we prove Theorem
1.1
. In Section
4
we collect some basic facts and consequences of Theorem
1.1
about minimizers of
\mathcal{E}
based on
[
40
, Section 2 and 3]
. Section
5
is devoted to the analysis of the nodal set of minimizers using methods from
[
40
, Section 4]
. Section
6
deals with the derivation of (
1.12
) using inner variation techniques. Moreover, applying techniques from
[
42
]
for higher order measure valued equations we obtain the optimal regularity for minimizers.

2.
Preliminaries about elliptic operators

For
A\in W_{loc}^{1,1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}),A(x)=(a_{ij}(x))_{i,j=1}^{d}
we define the
matrix divergence

\mathbf{div}(A)\in L^{1}_{loc}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2})
via
\mathbf{div}(A(x)):=\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}\partial_{i}a_{ij}(x)e_{j}
. For
A_{1},A_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
matrices we denote by
A_{1}:A_{2}:=\mathrm{tr}(A_{1}^{T}A_{2})
the
Hilbert-Schmidt inner product
of
A_{1},A_{2}
. For
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
we use the
dot product notation

x\cdot y:=x^{T}y
. As explained above we denote
L:=-\mathrm{div}(A\nabla)
. Furthermore, we say for
f\in L^{1}(\Omega)
that
x_{0}\in\Omega
is a
Lebesgue point
of
f
if
\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\fint_{B_{r}(x_{0})}f\;\mathrm{d}x=:f^{*}(x_{0})
exists and

\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\fint_{B_{r}(x_{0})}|f(x)-f^{*}(x_{0})|\;\mathrm{d}x=0.

Here,
\fint_{B}:=\frac{1}{|B|}\int_{B}
denotes the average integral. For us the term
almost everywhere
refers to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. If another measure
\mu
is addressed, we write
\mu
-almost everywhere
instead. The
characteristic function
of a set
M\subset\Omega
is denoted by
\chi_{M}
. Finally, we say that a domain
\Omega^{\prime}
is
compactly contained
in
\Omega
and write
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\subset\Omega
if
\overline{\Omega^{\prime}}\subset\Omega
.

2.1.
Supersolutions

In this section we summarize some properties of
L
and its
weak supersolutions
of precisely defined as follows.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded domain. We say that
u\in L^{1}_{loc}(\Omega)
satisfies
Lu\geq 0
weakly, if

(2.1)

\int uL\varphi\;\mathrm{d}x\geq 0\qquad\textrm{for all }\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)\;\textrm{such that $\varphi\geq 0$}.

One property of interest is the regularity of such supersolutions. The following classical lemma shows the optimal possible regularity.

Lemma 2.2

(Regularity of weak supersolutions, see
[
5
,
47
]
)
.

Each
u\in L^{1}_{loc}(\Omega)
that satisfies
Lu\geq 0
weakly lies in
W^{1,q}_{loc}(\Omega)
for all
q\in[1,\frac{n}{n-1})
. Moreover, there exists some Radon measure
\mu
on
\Omega
such that

(2.2)

\int_{\Omega}uL\varphi\;\mathrm{d}x=\int\varphi\;\mathrm{d}\mu\qquad\textrm{for all }\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega).

Sketch of Proof.

The proof is based on the observation that a finite signed Radon measure lies in
W^{-1,r}(\Omega)
for all
r\in(1,\frac{n}{n-1})
(which is clear since
W_{0}^{1,r^{\prime}}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow C(\overline{\Omega})
for
r^{\prime}=\frac{r}{r-1}>n
).
∎

Such weak supersolutions enjoy a
mean value property
which has been stated by Caffarelli in
[
13
]
and proved rigorously by Blank and Hao in
[
7
]
.

Lemma 2.3

(
[
7
, Theorem 6.3 and Corollary 6.4]
)
.

Let
L
be as above. Then for any
x_{0}\in\Omega
there exists an increasing family of sets
(D_{R}(x_{0}))_{R\in(0,\infty)}
such that

\mathrm{(i)}

B_{cR}(x_{0})\subset D_{R}(x_{0})\subset B_{CR}(x_{0})
for all
R\in(0,\infty)
with
c,C>0
independent of
x_{0}
,

\mathrm{(ii)}

for any
v\in L^{1}_{loc}(\Omega)
that satisfies
Lv\geq 0
weakly

[
7

v_{R}(x_{0}):=\fint_{D_{R}(x_{0})}v\;\mathrm{d}x

R

R\in(0,R_{0})

\mathrm{(iii)}

v^{*}(x):=\lim_{R\rightarrow 0}v_{R}(x)\in\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}\qquad\textrm{for all }x\in\Omega.

Next we collect some facts about this pointwise representative.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
v^{*}
be a pointwise representative of some
v\in L^{1}_{loc}(\Omega)
that satisfies
Lv\geq 0
weakly. Moreover, let
x_{0}\in\Omega.

\mathrm{(i)}

v^{*}
is lower semicontinuous, i.e. for all sequences
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\Omega
such that
x_{n}\rightarrow x_{0}

(2.4)

v^{*}(x_{0})\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}v^{*}(x_{n}).

\mathrm{(ii)}

One has

(2.5)

v^{*}(x_{0})=\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\left(\inf_{D_{r}(x_{0})}v^{*}\right).

\mathrm{(iii)}

If
v^{*}(x_{0})<\infty
, then
x_{0}
is automatically a Lebesgue point of
v^{*}
.

Proof.

For (i) we refer to
[
7
, Corollary 6.4]
. Now we turn to (ii).
Choose an arbitrary sequence
r_{n}\rightarrow 0
. Let
x_{n}\in D_{r_{n}}(x_{0})
be chosen in such a way that

(2.6)

\inf_{D_{r_{n}}(x_{0})}v^{*}\leq v^{*}(x_{n})\leq\inf_{D_{r_{n}}(x_{0})}v^{*}+\tfrac{1}{n}.

Since
x_{n}\in D_{r_{n}}(x_{0})\subset B_{Cr_{n}}(x_{0})
we conclude that
x_{n}\rightarrow x_{0}
. Using (i) for
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and (
2.6
) together we find

(2.7)

v^{*}(x_{0})\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}v^{*}(x_{n})\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(\inf_{D_{r_{n}}(x_{0})}v^{*}+\tfrac{1}{n}\right)=\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(\inf_{D_{r_{n}}(x_{0})}v^{*}\right).

Furthermore, the elementary fact that
\inf_{D_{r_{n}}(x_{0})}v^{*}\leq v^{*}(x_{0})
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
yields

(2.8)

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(\inf_{D_{r_{n}}(x_{0})}v^{*}\right)\leq v^{*}(x_{0}).

The previous two equations imply the claimed equality.
For (iii) we estimate

(2.9)

\displaystyle\fint_{B_{cr}(x_{0})}|v^{*}(x)-v^{*}(x_{0})|\;\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{|B_{cr}(x_{0})|}\int_{B_{cr}(x_{0})}|v^{*}(x)-v^{*}(x_{0})|\;\mathrm{d}x\leq\frac{1}{|B_{cr}(x_{0})|}\int_{D_{r}(x_{0})}|v^{*}(x)-v^{*}(x_{0})|\;\mathrm{d}x

(2.10)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{C}{c}\right)^{n}\frac{1}{|B_{Cr}(x_{0})|}\int_{D_{r}(x_{0})}|v^{*}(x)-v^{*}(x_{0})|\leq\left(\frac{C}{c}\right)^{n}\fint_{D_{r}(x_{0})}|v^{*}(x)-v^{*}(x_{0})|\;\mathrm{d}x.

Now by the triangle inequality

(2.11)

\displaystyle\fint_{D_{r}(x_{0})}|v^{*}(x)-v^{*}(x_{0})|\;\mathrm{d}x=\fint_{D_{r}(x_{0})}|v^{*}(x)-\inf_{D_{R}(x_{0})}v^{*}+\inf_{D_{R}(x_{0})}v^{*}-v^{*}(x_{0})|\;\mathrm{d}x

(2.12)

\displaystyle\leq\fint_{D_{r}(x_{0})}|v^{*}(x)-\inf_{D_{r}(x_{0})}v^{*}|\;\mathrm{d}x+|\inf_{D_{r}(x_{0})}v^{*}-v^{*}(x_{0})|=\fint_{D_{r}(x_{0})}v^{*}(x)\;\mathrm{d}x-\inf_{D_{r}(x_{0})}v^{*}+(v^{*}(x_{0})-\inf_{D_{R}(x_{0})}v^{*})

(2.13)

\displaystyle=v_{r}(x_{0})-\inf_{D_{r}(x_{0})}v^{*}+(v^{*}(x_{0})-\inf_{D_{r}(x_{0})}v^{*}),

where
v_{r}
is as in Lemma
2.3
. Due to Lemma
2.3
we have
v_{r}(x_{0})\leq v^{*}(x_{0})
. This allows us to estimate

(2.14)

\fint_{D_{r}(x_{0})}|v^{*}(x)-v^{*}(x_{0})|\;\mathrm{d}x\leq 2(v^{*}(x_{0})-\inf_{D_{r}(x_{0})}v^{*}).

Using (ii) and
v^{*}(x_{0})<\infty
we infer that

\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\fint_{D_{r}(x_{0})}|v^{*}(x)-v^{*}(x_{0})|\;\mathrm{d}x=0.

Thereupon, (
2.10
) yields the claim.
∎

An important corollary is a
strong maximum principle
for supersolutions.

Corollary 2.5

(Strong maximum principle)
.

Suppose that
v\in L^{1}_{loc}(\Omega)
solves
Lv\geq 0
weakly in
\Omega
and there exists
m\in\mathbb{R}
such that and
v\geq m
for almost every
x\in\Omega
. If there exists some
x_{0}\in\Omega
such that
v^{*}(x_{0})=m
then
v(x)=m
for almost every
x\in\Omega.

Proof.

This follows the lines of the classical maximum principle. For convenience we sketch the argument. Let
\Omega_{1}:=\{x\in\Omega:v^{*}(x)=m\}
and
\Omega_{2}:=\{x\in\Omega:v^{*}(x)>m\}
. Notice that by the lower semicontinuity of
v^{*}
(cf. Lemma
2.4
),
\Omega_{2}
is open. Moreover, if
x_{1}\in\Omega_{1}
then
v\geq m
a.e. and Lemma
2.3
(ii) yields

(2.15)

m\leq\fint_{D_{r}(x_{1})}v(x)\;\mathrm{d}x=v_{r}(x_{1})\leq v^{*}(x_{1})=m.

We conclude (again using
v\geq m
a.e.) that
v\equiv m
a.e. on
D_{r}(x_{1})
. In particular,
v^{*}\equiv m
on
D_{r}(x_{1})
and thus
D_{r}(x_{1})\subset\Omega_{1}
. This shows that
\Omega_{1}
is also open. We have observed that
\Omega=\Omega_{1}\cup\Omega_{2}
is the union of two disjoint open sets. Since
\Omega
is connected, one of these sets must be empty. Since by assumption
\Omega_{1}\neq\emptyset
we find
\Omega_{2}=\emptyset
. The claim follows.
∎

2.2.
Green’s function in 2D

We will now look at
Green’s functions

G_{L}
and
G_{L^{2}}
for the Dirichlet and Navier problem respectively. This means that for all
x\in\Omega
we consider the unique solution
G_{L}(x,\cdot)
of

(2.16)

\begin{cases}LG_{L}(x,\cdot)=\delta_{x}&\textrm{in $\Omega$}\\
G_{L}(x,\cdot)=0&\textrm{on $\partial\Omega$}\end{cases}

and the unique solution
G_{L^{2}}(x,\cdot)
of

(2.17)

\begin{cases}L^{2}G_{L^{2}}(x,\cdot)=\delta_{x}&\textrm{in $\Omega$}\\
G_{L^{2}}(x,\cdot)=LG_{L^{2}}(x,\cdot)=0&\textrm{on $\partial\Omega$}\end{cases}.

For details on existence and uniqueness of
G_{L},G_{L}^{2}
we refer to
[
35
]
. From now on we will evaluate
G_{L}
pointwise, always referring to the unique pointwise representative in the sense of (
2.3
).
We recall some basic facts for
G_{L}
and
G_{L^{2}}
in the following

Lemma 2.6
.

For all
q\in[1,2)
there holds
G_{L}(x,\cdot)\in W^{1,q}_{0}(\Omega)
and
G_{L^{2}}(x,\cdot)\in W^{3,q}(\Omega)\cap W^{1,q}_{0}(\Omega)
.

\mathrm{(i)}

For all
x,y\in\Omega,x\neq y
one has
G_{L}(x,y)=G_{L}(y,x)
.

\mathrm{(ii)}

One has
LG_{L^{2}}(x,\cdot)=G_{L}(x,\cdot)
. In particular
LG_{L^{2}}(x,\cdot)\in W_{0}^{1,q}(\Omega)
and for a.e.
(x,y)\in\Omega\times\Omega

(2.18)

G_{L^{2}}(x,y)=\int_{\Omega}G_{L}(x,z)G_{L}(z,y)\;\mathrm{d}z.

We will from now on also evaluate
G_{L^{2}}
pointwise referring to (
2.18
).

\mathrm{(iii)}

For all
x,y\in\Omega,x\neq y
one has
G_{L^{2}}(x,y)=G_{L^{2}}(y,x).

\mathrm{(iv)}

For all
x,y\in\Omega
one has
G_{L}(x,y)\geq 0
.

Proof.

The claimed regularity of
G_{L}
is a consequence of
[
35
, Chapter 8]
. Assertion (i) was already observed in
[
35
, p.62]
. Assertion (ii) follows from the fact that
v:=LG_{L^{2}}(x,\cdot)
must be a solution of

(2.19)

\begin{cases}Lv=\delta_{x}&\textrm{in $\Omega$}\\
v=0&\textrm{on $\partial\Omega$}\end{cases}

which implies by uniqueness that

v=G_{L}(x,\cdot).

In particular, we have

(2.20)

G_{L^{2}}(x,y)=\int_{\Omega}G_{L}(y,z)LG_{L^{2}}(x,z)\;\mathrm{d}z=\int_{\Omega}G_{L}(y,z)v(x,z)\;\mathrm{d}z=\int_{\Omega}G_{L}(y,z)G_{L}(x,z)\;\mathrm{d}z.

We infer also that
LG_{L^{2}}(x,\cdot)=G_{L}(x,\cdot)\in W_{0}^{1,q}(\Omega)
for each
q\in[1,2)
. Elliptic regularity yields
G_{L^{2}}(x,\cdot)\in W^{3,q}(\Omega)
for any
q\in(1,2)
(and as a consequence also for
q=1
).
(iii) follows from (i) and (ii). (iv) is a consequence of
[
26
, Theorem 1.1]
.
∎

An important observation is that both fundamental solutions can (up to smoother perturbations) be represented in terms of the function

(2.21)

\psi_{x}:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\quad y\mapsto A(y)^{-1}(y-x)\cdot(y-x).

This representation is described by the following lemma.

Lemma 2.7

(Connection between
\psi_{x}
and Green’s function)
.

Let
L,G_{L},G_{L^{2}}
be as above.

\mathrm{(i)}

There exists
c_{1}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega};(0,\infty))
such that

G_{L}(x,y)=-c_{1}(x)\log(\psi_{x}(y))+f_{1}(x,y),

for some
f_{1}:\Omega\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
f_{1}(x,\cdot)\in W^{1,p}_{loc}(\Omega)
for any
p\in(1,\infty)
. Moreover, for any
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\subset\Omega
one has
\sup_{x\in\Omega^{\prime}}||f_{1}(x,\cdot)||_{W^{1,p}(\Omega^{\prime})}<\infty
.

\mathrm{(ii)}

For
c_{1}
as in point
\mathrm{(i)}
one has

G_{L^{2}}(x,y)=c_{1}(x)\psi_{x}(y)\log\psi_{x}(y)+f_{2}(x,y),

for some
f_{2}:\Omega\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
f_{2}(x,\cdot)\in W^{3,p}_{loc}(\Omega)
for any
p\in(1,\infty)
. Moreover, for any
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\subset\Omega
one has
\sup_{x\in\Omega^{\prime}}||f_{2}(x,\cdot)||_{W^{3,p}(\Omega)}<\infty
.

Proof.

We define only for this proof
F=G_{L}
and
G=G_{L^{2}}
. First we prove point (i).
One readily checks that for
y\in\Omega
one has

\nabla_{y}\log\psi_{x}(y)=\frac{2}{\psi_{x}(y)}A(y)^{-1}(y-x)+h_{x}(y),

where
h_{x}=(h_{1,x},h_{2,x})^{T}
is given by

h_{i,x}(y):=\frac{1}{\psi_{x}(y)}\partial_{i}A^{-1}(y)(y-x)\cdot(y-x)
for
i=1,2
.
Since
A^{-1}
is also uniformly elliptic, we have
\psi_{x}(y)\geq\theta|x-y|^{2}
for some
\theta>0
. As a consequence,
h_{x}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
\log(\psi_{x})\in W^{1,3/2}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.
For the rest of the proof we denote by
\mathrm{div}(h_{x})
the distributional divergence of
h_{x}
. We next compute
-\mathrm{div}_{y}(A\nabla_{y}\log\psi_{x})
in the sense of distributions. For simplicity of notation we use the convention that unless stated otherwise all differential operators fall on the
y
-variable. Let
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
be arbitrary.

(2.22)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\nabla\varphi\cdot A\nabla\log\psi_{x}\;\mathrm{d}y=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\nabla\varphi(y)\cdot A(y)\tfrac{2}{\psi_{x}(y)}A(y)^{-1}(y-x)\;\mathrm{d}y+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\nabla\varphi\cdot h_{x}\;\mathrm{d}y

(2.23)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0+}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus B_{\varepsilon}(x)}\nabla\varphi(y)\cdot\tfrac{1}{\psi_{x}(y)}(y-x)\;\mathrm{d}y+\mathrm{div}(h_{x})(\varphi).

Now we write

(2.24)

\frac{1}{\psi_{x}(y)}(y-x)=\frac{1}{\psi_{y}(x)}(y-x)+\frac{\psi_{x}(y)-\psi_{y}(x)}{\psi_{x}(y)\psi_{y}(x)}(y-x).

Using that that
|\psi_{x}(y)-\psi_{y}(x)|\leq C|y-x|^{3}
and
\psi_{x}(y),\psi_{y}(x)\geq c|y-x|^{2}
for some
c,C>0
we write

(2.25)

\frac{1}{\psi_{x}(y)}(y-x)=\frac{1}{\psi_{y}(x)}(y-x)+r(x,y)

for some uniformly bounded Borel measurable function
r:(\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2})\setminus\{(x,x):x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. Applying this in (
2.23
) we obtain

(2.26)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\nabla\varphi\cdot A\nabla\log\psi_{x}\;\mathrm{d}y=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0+}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus B_{\varepsilon}(x)}\frac{2}{A(x)^{-1}(y-x)\cdot(y-x)}\nabla\varphi(y)\cdot(y-x)\;\mathrm{d}y+\mathrm{div}(h_{x}+2r(x,\cdot))(\varphi).

With
\mathrm{div}_{y}(\frac{1}{A(x)^{-1}(y-x)\cdot(y-x)}(y-x))=0
we infer

(2.27)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\nabla\varphi\cdot A\nabla\log\psi_{x}\;\mathrm{d}y

(2.28)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0+}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\partial B_{\varepsilon}(x)}\frac{2\varphi(y)}{A(x)^{-1}(y-x)\cdot(y-x)}(y-x)\cdot(y-x)\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}(y)+\mathrm{div}(h_{x}+2r(x,\cdot))(\varphi).

(2.29)

\displaystyle=-4\pi\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0+}\fint_{\partial B_{1}(0)}\frac{\varphi(x+\varepsilon z)}{A(x)^{-1}z\cdot z}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}(z)+\mathrm{div}(h_{x}+r(x,\cdot))(\varphi)=-d_{1}(x)\varphi(x)+\mathrm{div}(h_{x}+2r(x,\cdot))(\varphi),

where
d_{1}(x):=4\pi\fint_{\partial B_{1}(0)}\frac{1}{A(x)^{-1}z\cdot z}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}(z)
(which clearly lies in
C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega};(0,\infty))
). Hence
\log\psi_{x}
is a distributional solution of

(2.30)

-\mathrm{div}(A(\log\psi_{x}))=-d_{1}(x)\delta_{x}+\mathrm{div}(h_{x}+2r(x,\cdot)).

This implies that in the sense of distibutions on
\Omega
one has

(2.31)

-\mathrm{div}(A(\log\psi_{x}+d_{1}(x)F(x,\cdot)))=\mathrm{div}(h_{x}+2r(x,\cdot)).

By regularity theory (cf.
[
5
]
) and the fact that
h_{x}+2r(x,\cdot)\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
we obtain that

(2.32)

\log\psi_{x}+d_{1}(x)F(x,\cdot)\in W^{1,p}_{loc}(\Omega)\quad\textrm{for all }p\in[1,\infty),

which implies point (i), with
c_{1}=\frac{1}{d_{1}}
and
f_{1}(x,y):=F(x,y)+c_{1}(x)\log\psi_{x}(y)
. For
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\subset\Omega
one can bound the boundary values of
\log\psi_{x}
uniformly in
x\in\Omega^{\prime}
and this yields the uniform estimate of the
W^{1,p}(\Omega^{\prime})
-norm of
f_{1}(x,\cdot)
.
Now we prove point (ii). Let
p\in(1,\infty).
We compute, defining

w_{i}(y):=\partial_{i}A^{-1}(y)(y-x)\cdot(y-x),\qquad i=1,2\qquad\textrm{and $w=(w_{1},w_{2})^{T}$},

(2.33)

\displaystyle\mathrm{div}(A\nabla(\psi_{x}\log\psi_{x}))(y)=\mathrm{div}\{A(y)(\log\psi_{x}(y)+1)[2A(y)^{-1}(y-x)+w(y)]\}

(2.34)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{div}\{(\log\psi_{x}(y)+1)(2(y-x)+A(y)w(y))\}

(2.35)

\displaystyle=(\log\psi_{x}(y)+1)[4+\mathrm{div}(A(y)w(y))]+\tfrac{1}{\psi_{x}(y)}[2A(y)^{-1}(y-x)+w(y)]\cdot[2(y-x)+A(y)w(y)]

(2.36)

\displaystyle=4\log\psi_{x}(y)+8+(\log\psi_{x}(y)+1)\mathrm{div}(A(y)w(y))+\tfrac{4}{\psi_{x}(y)}(y-x)\cdot w(y)+\tfrac{1}{\psi_{x}(y)}w(y)\cdot A(y)w(y).

Using that by point (i)
\log\psi_{x}=-d_{1}(x)F(x,\cdot)+f_{1}(x,\cdot)
for some
d_{1}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
and
f_{1}(x,\cdot)\in W^{1,p}_{loc}(\Omega)
we infer

(2.37)

\displaystyle\mathrm{div}(A\nabla(\psi_{x}\log\psi_{x}))(y)

\displaystyle=-4d_{1}(x)F(x,y)+4f_{1}(x,y)+8

(2.38)

\displaystyle\quad+(\log\psi_{x}(y)+1)\mathrm{div}(A(y)w(y))+\tfrac{4}{\psi_{x}(y)}(y-x)\cdot w(y)+\tfrac{1}{\psi_{x}(y)}w(y)\cdot A(y)w(y).

One readily checks that
y\mapsto 8+(\log\psi_{x}(y)+1)\mathrm{div}(A(y)w(y))+\tfrac{4}{\psi_{x}(y)}(y-x)\cdot w(y)+\tfrac{1}{\psi_{x}(y)}w(y)\cdot A(y)w(y)
lies in
W^{1,p}_{loc}(\Omega)
for all
p\in[1,\infty)
. In particular, we have shown

-L(\psi_{x}\log\psi_{x})=-4d_{1}(x)F(x,\cdot)+h(x,y)

for some
h(x,\cdot)\in W^{1,p}_{loc}(\Omega)
, any
p\in[1,\infty)
. Notice further that by Lemma
2.6

F(x,\cdot)=LG_{L^{2}}(x,\cdot)
. Therefore we have

(2.39)

L(\psi_{x}\log\psi_{x}-4d_{1}(x)G_{L^{2}}(x,\cdot))=-h(x,\cdot)\in W^{1,p}_{loc}(\Omega)

for any
p\in[1,\infty)
. Using elliptic regularity we infer that

\psi_{x}\log\psi_{x}-4d_{1}(x)G_{L^{2}}(x,\cdot)\in W^{3,p}_{loc}(\Omega)
for any
p\in(1,\infty)
(and as a consequence also for
p=1
).
The claim follows.
∎

Lemma 2.8

(Second derivative of
G_{L^{2}}
)
.

Let
G_{L},G_{L^{2}}
be as above. Then
G_{L},G_{L^{2}}\in C^{\infty}(\Omega\times\Omega\setminus\{(x,x):x\in\Omega\})
. Moreover, for each
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\subset\Omega
there exists some
C=C(\Omega^{\prime})>0
such that for all
(x,y)\in\Omega^{\prime}\times\Omega^{\prime}
such that
x\neq y
in one has

|D_{x}^{2}G_{L^{2}}(x,y)|+|D_{y}^{2}G_{L^{2}}(x,y)|\leq C(|\log(y-x)|+1).

Proof.

The claimed smoothness of
G_{L}
(i.e.
G_{L}(x,\cdot)\in C^{\infty}(\Omega\setminus\{x\})
for any
x\in\Omega
) is easily checked since for each
x\in\Omega
one has
LG_{L}(x,\cdot)=0
distributionally on
\Omega\setminus\{x\}
, see (
2.17
). Here we used
[
20
, Theorem 6.33]
to obtain smoothness of such distributional solutions. The smoothness of
G_{L^{2}}(x,\cdot)
on
\Omega\setminus\{x\}
follows then from the fact that
LG_{L^{2}}(x,\cdot)=G_{L}(x,\cdot)\in C^{\infty}(\Omega\setminus\{x\})
, cf. Lemma
2.6
.
Choose now
\Omega^{\prime}
as in the statement. By Lemma
2.7
, symmetry (cf. Lemma
2.6
) and the fact that
W^{3,p}_{loc}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow C^{2}(\Omega)
for any
p>2
it suffices to show the claimed estimates for
(x,y)\mapsto\psi_{x}(y)\log(\psi_{x}(y))
.
Furthermore, by symmetry it suffices to show the estimate for
|D_{y}^{2}[\psi_{x}(y)\log(\psi_{x}(y))]|
for fixed
x\in\Omega^{\prime}
and
y\in\Omega^{\prime}\setminus\{x\}
considered as a variable. To this end observe that

(2.40)

\partial^{2}_{y_{i}y_{j}}[\psi_{x}(y)\log\psi_{x}(y)]=\partial^{2}_{y_{i}y_{j}}\psi_{x}(y)(\log\psi_{x}(y)+1)+\frac{\partial_{y_{j}}\psi_{x}(y)\partial_{y_{i}}\psi_{x}(y)}{\psi_{x}(y)}.

Using that by (
2.21
)
\psi_{x}(y)\geq\theta|x-y|^{2}
for some
\theta>0
and for some
C^{\prime},C^{\prime\prime}>0
one has

|\partial_{y_{i}}\psi_{x}(y)|\leq C^{\prime}|x-y|
and
|\partial^{2}_{y_{i}y_{j}}\psi_{x}(y)|\leq C^{\prime\prime}
we find

|D_{y}^{2}[\psi_{x}(y)\log\psi_{x}(y)]|\leq C^{\prime\prime}(|\log(|\psi_{x}(y))|+1)+\frac{C^{\prime 2}}{\theta}\leq C(\log|x-y|+1),

where
C>0
is chosen suitably large.
∎

3.
An anisotropic version of Frehse’s observation

We recall from the introduction Frehse’s observation in two dimensions.

Lemma 3.1

(Frehse’s observation – I)
.

Let
V=V_{(-\Delta)^{2}}
be the fundamental solution for
(-\Delta)^{2}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Then
\Delta V
has a logarithmic singularity at
x=0
but
\partial_{11}^{2}V-\partial_{22}^{2}V
and
\partial^{2}_{12}V
are bounded functions on
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
.

The proof is via direct computation using the explicit formula for
V
, i.e.
V(x)=\frac{1}{8\pi}|x|^{2}\log|x|
, cf. (
1.6
).
Another way to look at this to write

(3.1)

D^{2}V(x)=\frac{1}{2}\Delta V(x)\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}+\frac{1}{2}(\partial^{2}_{11}V-\partial^{2}_{22}V)\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}+\partial^{2}_{12}V\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}

and observe that the last two summands are bounded by Lemma
3.1
. One can interpret Frehse’s observation now in the following way: Up to a bounded perturbation one has that
D^{2}V
is determined by
\frac{1}{2}\Delta VI_{2\times 2}
. With this observation one can reformulate Frehse’s observation.

Lemma 3.2

(Frehse’s observation – II)
.

Let
V=V_{(-\Delta)^{2}}
be the fundamental solution for
(-\Delta)^{2}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Then

(3.2)

D^{2}V(x)=\frac{1}{2}\Delta V(x)I_{2}+N(x)\quad\textrm{for all }x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\},

where
N:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}_{sym}
is a bounded Borel measurable function that is smooth on
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
.

Frehse’s observation has consequences for Green’s function
G_{(-\Delta)^{2}}
in
\Omega
. Indeed, one readily checks

(3.3)

G_{(-\Delta)^{2}}(x,y)=V(x-y)+\varphi_{x}(y),\quad\textrm{where $\varphi_{x}$ solves}\qquad\begin{cases}(-\Delta)^{2}\varphi_{x}=0&\textrm{in $\Omega$}\\
\varphi_{x}=-V(x-\cdot)&\textrm{on $\partial\Omega$}\end{cases}.

For any subdomain
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\subset\Omega
one has smoothness of
(x,y)\mapsto\varphi_{x}(y)
on
\Omega^{\prime}\times\Omega^{\prime}
. Due to this fact the behavior of
D_{y}^{2}G_{(-\Delta)^{2}}
is up to a smooth perturbation determined by
D^{2}V
, yielding that

(3.4)

D_{y}^{2}G_{(-\Delta)^{2}}(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{y}G_{(-\Delta)^{2}}(x,y)I_{2}+N(x,y)\quad\textrm{for all }(x,y)\in\Omega\times\Omega:x\neq y,

where
N:\Omega\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}_{sym}
is locally(!) bounded, Borel measurable and smooth on
\Omega\times\Omega\setminus\{(x,x):x\in\Omega\}
.

For this formula one can indeed formulate an anisotropic generalization. One would like to replace
-\Delta
by
L=-\mathrm{div}(A\nabla)
and must replace
I_{2}
by a suitable matrix such that the remainder term indeed defines a bounded function. Pursuing this we obtain

Proposition 3.3

(Anisotropic Green’s function)
.

There exists a function
N:\Omega\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}_{sym}
which is Borel measurable, locally bounded and smooth on
\Omega\times\Omega\setminus\{(x,x):x\in\Omega\}
such that
G_{L^{2}}
satisfies

(3.5)

D_{y}^{2}G_{L^{2}}(x,y)=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{div}_{y}(A(y)\nabla_{y}G_{L^{2}}(x,y))A(x)^{-1}+N(x,y)\quad\textrm{for all }(x,y)\in\Omega\times\Omega:x\neq y.

We will prove this proposition later on after some preparations. Note first that the matrices appearing in (
3.1
), more precisely
\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix},\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}
and
\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}
, form an orthonormal basis of
(\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}_{sym},:)
(where “
:
” denotes the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product). An important step in the proof of the above proposition is to replace the Hilbert-Schmidt product by a suitable Riemannian metric on
\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}_{sym}
which fits to the anisotropy. This metric already appears in the following lemma which again deals with properties of the comparison function
\psi_{x}\log\psi_{x}
examined in Lemma
2.7
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\psi_{x}
be as in (
2.21
). Define on
\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}_{sym}
the smooth Riemannian metric

g(y)(M_{1},M_{2}):=\mathrm{tr}(A^{-1}(y)M_{1}M_{2}A^{-1}(y))\qquad(y\in\overline{\Omega}).

Then there exists a smooth orthonormal frame
y\mapsto\{A_{1}(y),A_{2}(y),A_{3}(y)\}
on
\overline{\Omega}
of
\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}_{sym}
with
A_{1}(y)=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}A(y)
for all
y\in\overline{\Omega}
. Moreover, for
i=2,3
the function
\mathrm{div}(A_{i}\nabla(\psi_{x}\log\psi_{x}))
is bounded, Borel measurable and smooth on
\overline{\Omega}\setminus\{x\}
.

Proof.

We claim first that for
y\in\overline{\Omega}
the matrices
\tilde{A}_{1}(y):=A(y),\tilde{A}_{2}(y):=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix},\tilde{A}_{3}(y):=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{pmatrix}
are linearly independent in
\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}_{sym}
. Indeed, if e.g.
\tilde{A}_{1}(y)
were a linear combination of
\tilde{A}_{2}(y),\tilde{A}_{3}(y)
then there would exist
\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}
such that

(3.6)

A(x)=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{2}&\lambda_{3}\\
\lambda_{3}&-\lambda_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

This is impossible, since
A(x)
is positive definite but the determinant of the matrix on the right hand side is nonpositive. The existence of the claimed orthonormal frame is now a consequence of the Gram-Schmidt process.
Notice that
A_{2},A_{3}\perp A
with respect to
g
implies
\mathrm{tr}(A_{i}(y)A(y)^{-1})=0
for all
y\in\Omega
.
With the definition
w_{j}(y):=\partial_{j}A(y)^{-1}(y-x)\cdot(y-x)\;(j=1,2)
and
w=(w_{1},w_{2})^{T}
we can compute for
i=2,3
(again with the convention that all differential operators are applied to the
y
-variable)

(3.7)

\displaystyle\mathrm{div}(A_{i}\nabla(\psi_{x}\log\psi_{x}))(y)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{div}\{(\log(\psi_{x}(y))+1)[2A_{i}(y)A(y)^{-1}(y-x)+A_{i}(y)w(y)]\}

(3.8)

\displaystyle=(\log\psi_{x}(y)+1)[2\mathrm{div}(A_{i}(y)A(y)^{-1}(y-x))+\mathrm{div}(A_{i}(y)w(y))]

(3.9)

\displaystyle+\tfrac{1}{\psi_{x}(y)}[2A(y)^{-1}(y-x)+w(y)]\cdot[2A_{i}(y)A(y)^{-1}(y-x)+A_{i}(y)w(y)].

Using that for a matrix-valued function
B\in C^{1}
one has
\mathrm{div}(B(y)(y-x))=\mathrm{tr}(B(y))+\mathbf{div}(B(y))\cdot(y-x)
we can simplify the first term and obtain

(3.10)

\displaystyle\mathrm{div}(A_{i}\nabla(\psi_{x}\log\psi_{x}))(y)

(3.11)

\displaystyle=(\log\psi_{x}(y)+1)2\mathbf{div}(A_{i}(y)A(y)^{-1})\cdot(y-x)+(\log\psi_{x}(y)+1)\mathrm{div}(A_{i}(y)w(y))

(3.12)

\displaystyle\qquad+\tfrac{1}{\psi_{x}(y)}2A(y)^{-1}(y-x)\cdot 2A_{i}(y)A(y)^{-1}(y-x))+\tfrac{1}{\psi_{x}(y)}A(y)^{-1}(y-x)\cdot A_{i}(y)w(y).

Notice that the first, second and fourth summand are continuous on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
(also in
y=x
!) and therefore certainly bounded in
\overline{\Omega}
. For the third summand we observe that

g(y):=2\frac{A(y)^{-1}(y-x)\cdot A_{i}(y)A(y)^{-1}(y-x)}{\psi_{x}(y)}=2\frac{A(y)^{-1}(y-x)\cdot A_{i}(y)A(y)^{-1}(y-x)}{A(y)^{-1}(y-x)\cdot(y-x)}

satisfies

\sup_{y\in\overline{\Omega}}|g(y)|\leq\frac{2}{\theta}||A^{-1}A_{i}A^{-1}||_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}<\infty,

where
\theta>0
is such that
\psi_{x}(y)\geq\theta|x-y|^{2}
. All in all, we infer that
g
is bounded and the claim follows.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

There exists
M_{0}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2})
and
N_{0}:\Omega\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
Borel measurable, locally bounded and smooth on
\Omega\times\Omega\setminus\{(x,x):x\in\Omega\}
such that

(3.13)

D_{y}^{2}(\psi_{x}\log\psi_{x})(y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\mathrm{div}_{y}(A(y)\nabla_{y}(\psi_{x}\log\psi_{x})(y))M_{0}(y)+N_{0}(x,y)\quad\textrm{for all }(x,y)\in\Omega\times\Omega:x\neq y.

Moreover,
M_{0}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}A(x)^{-1}

Proof.

Let
y\mapsto\{A_{1}(y)=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}A(y),A_{2}(y),A_{3}(y)\}
be the orthonormal frame constructed in Lemma
3.4
. For this proof we again use the convention that all differential operators fall on the
y
-variable. Since for any
y\in\Omega\setminus\{x\}
one has

there exist unique real numbers

such that

(3.14)

D^{2}(\psi_{x}\log\psi_{x})(y)=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\theta_{i}(y)A_{i}(y).

Next we compute an explicit formula for
\theta_{i}(y)
(
i=1,2,3
). Defining
h_{ij}(y):=\mathrm{tr}(A_{i}(y)A_{j}(y))
we obtain

(3.15)

\sum_{i=1}^{3}\theta_{i}(y)h_{ij}(y)=\mathrm{tr}(D^{2}(\psi_{x}\log\psi_{x})(y)A_{j}(y))=\mathrm{div}(A_{j}\nabla(\psi_{x}\log\psi_{x}))(y)+\mathbf{div}(A_{j})(y)\cdot\nabla(\psi_{x}\log\psi_{x})(y).

Notice that the matrix
(h_{ij}(y))_{ij=1}^{3}
must be invertible, since it is the matrix representing the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product with respect to the basis
\{A_{1}(y),A_{2}(y),A_{3}(y)\}
of
\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}_{sym}
. Let
(h^{kl}(y))_{k,l=1}^{3}
be its inverse. We infer that

(3.16)

\theta_{k}(y)=\sum_{l=1}^{3}h^{kl}(y)[\mathrm{div}(A_{l}\nabla(\psi_{x}\log\psi_{x}))(y)+\mathbf{div}(A_{l})(y)\cdot\nabla(\psi_{x}\log\psi_{x})(y)].

As a consequence of (
3.14
) we infer

(3.17)

\displaystyle D^{2}(\psi_{x}\log\psi_{x})(y)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\left(\sum_{l=1}^{3}h^{il}(y)[\mathrm{div}(A_{l}\nabla(\psi_{x}\log\psi_{x}))(y)+\mathbf{div}(A_{l})(y)\cdot\nabla(\psi_{x}\log\psi_{x})(y)]\right)A_{i}(y)

(3.18)

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{3}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{3}h^{il}(y)A_{i}(y)\right)[\mathrm{div}(A_{l}\nabla(\psi_{x}\log\psi_{x}))(y)+\mathbf{div}(A_{l})(y)\cdot\nabla(\psi_{x}\log\psi_{x})(y)].

Observe with Lemma
3.4
and the local boundedness of

(3.19)

y\mapsto\nabla(\psi_{x}\log\psi_{x})(y)=2(\log\psi_{x}(y)+1)A(y)^{-1}(y-x)

that all summands with summation index
l\neq 1
are locally bounded and smooth in
\Omega\times\Omega\setminus\{(x,x):x\in\Omega\}
. Furthermore,

(3.20)

y\mapsto\left(\sum_{i=1}^{3}h^{i1}(y)A_{i}(y)\right)\mathbf{div}(A_{1})(y)\cdot\nabla(\psi_{x}\log\psi_{x})(y)

is locally bounded, also because of (
3.19
). With these observations and
A_{1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}A
we infer that

(3.21)

D^{2}(\psi_{x}\log\psi_{x})(y)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\mathrm{div}(A\nabla(\psi_{x}\log\psi_{x}))(y)\left(\sum_{i=1}^{3}h^{i1}(y)A_{i}(y)\right)+N_{0}(x,y),

where
N_{0}:\Omega\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
is Borel measurable, (locally) bounded and smooth on
\Omega\times\Omega\setminus\{(x,x):x\in\Omega\}
. We can now define
M_{0}:\overline{\Omega}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2},M_{0}(y):=\sum_{i=1}^{3}h^{i1}(y)A_{i}(y)
and observe that
M_{0}
is smooth on
\overline{\Omega}
to obtain the structure in the statement.
It remains to show that
M_{0}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}A(x)^{-1}
. Notice that by (
2.36
)

(3.22)

\mathrm{div}(A\nabla(\psi_{x}\log\psi_{x}))(y)=4\log\psi_{x}(y)+g(y)

for some locally bounded function
g:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}

With the notation that
A(y)^{-1}=(a^{ij}(y))_{i,j=1}^{2}
one computes (cf. (
2.40

(3.23)

\displaystyle\partial^{2}_{ij}(\psi_{x}(y)\log\psi_{x}(y))

(3.24)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{\psi_{x}(y)}[2A(y)^{-1}(y-x)\cdot e_{i}+\partial_{i}A(y)^{-1}(y-x)\cdot(y-x)][2A(y)^{-1}(y-x)\cdot e_{j}+\partial_{j}A(y)^{-1}(y-x)\cdot(y-x)]

(3.25)

\displaystyle\quad+(\log\psi_{x}(y)+1)[2a^{ij}(y)+2\partial_{i}A(y)^{-1}(y-x)\cdot e_{j}+2\partial_{j}A(y)^{-1}(y-x)\cdot e_{i}+\partial^{2}_{ij}A^{-1}(y)(y-x)\cdot(y-x)].

This implies that

(3.26)

D^{2}(\psi_{x}\log\psi_{x})(y)=2(\log\psi_{x}(y)+1)A(y)^{-1}+f(x,y)

for some locally bounded

. From (

) we infer that

(3.27)

M_{0}(x)=\sqrt{2}\frac{D^{2}(\psi_{x}\log\psi_{x})(y)}{\mathrm{div}(A\nabla(\psi_{x}\log\psi_{x}))(y)}-\sqrt{2}\frac{N_{0}(x,y)}{\mathrm{div}(A\nabla(\psi_{x}\log\psi_{x}))(y)}.

Using (
3.22
) and (
3.26
) and

we obtain
M_{0}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}A(x)^{-1}
.
The claim follows.
∎

3.1.
Consequences for higher order measure-valued equations

The previous insights about the fundamental solution can be applied to describe distributional solutions of the measure valued equation

, for some Radon measure
\mu
on
\Omega
. This is due to the fact that such measure valued solutions can be represented with the aid of Green’s function.

Lemma 3.8
.

Suppose that
\mu
is a Radon measure on
\Omega
and
u\in W^{2,2}_{loc}(\Omega)
solves
L^{2}u=\mu
on
\Omega
weakly, i.e.

(3.44)

\int_{\Omega}LuL\varphi\;\mathrm{d}x=\int\varphi\;\mathrm{d}\mu\qquad\textrm{for all $\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)$}.

Then there exists
w\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)
such that

(3.45)

u(x)=\int_{\Omega}G_{L^{2}}(x,y)\;\mathrm{d}\mu(y)+w(x).

Proof.

Define

(3.46)

\bar{u}(x)=\int_{\Omega}G_{L^{2}}(x,y)\;\mathrm{d}\mu(y).

We conclude from
[
35
, Theorem 6.1]
that
w:=u-\bar{u}
must be a distributional solution of
L^{2}w=0
. Standard regularity theory (cf.
[
20
, Theorem 6.33]
) implies
w\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)
. The claim follows.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

We have observed in Lemma
3.8
that for all
x\in\Omega

(3.47)

u(x)=\bar{u}(x)+w(x)=\int_{\Omega}G_{L^{2}}(x,y)\;\mathrm{d}\mu(y)+w(x)\qquad\textrm{for some $w\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)$}.

Define
N:=\{x\in\Omega:\mu(\{x\})>0\}
. Since the set of atoms of
\mu
is at most countable we have that
N
is a Lebesgue null set.
One readily checks that with
H:=D^{2}w\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)
one has

Fix
x\in\Omega\setminus N
such that (
3.48
) holds. With (
3.43
) we conclude that for all

for some Borel measurable and bounded

which is smooth on
\Omega\times\Omega\setminus\{(x,x):x\in\Omega\}.

Since

we have that (
3.49
) holds for
\mu
-almost every
y\in\Omega
. We obtain for

as in (
3.48
)

D^{2}u(x)=-\frac{1}{2}\left(\int_{\Omega}L_{x}G_{L^{2}}(x,y)\;\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\right)A(x)^{-1}+\int_{\Omega}\tilde{N}(x,y)\;\mathrm{d}\mu(y)+H(x).

We conclude with the notation of (

D^{2}u(x)=-\frac{1}{2}(L\bar{u}(x))A(x)^{-1}+\int_{\Omega}\tilde{N}(x,y)\;\mathrm{d}\mu(y)+H(x).

Using that

the claim follows.

Lemma 4.1

D_{\Omega,A}>0
such that

\lVert v\rVert_{W^{2,2}}\leq D_{\Omega,A}\lVert Lv\rVert_{L^{2}}
for all
v\in W^{2,2}(\Omega)\cap W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega).

We next obtain an upper bound for the energy infinum.

Lemma 4.2
.

\inf_{w\in\mathcal{A}(u_{0})}\mathcal{E}(w)\leq|\Omega|.

Proof.

Standard regularity theory implies the existence of some
u_{*}\in\mathcal{A}(u_{0})
such that
-\mathrm{div}(A\nabla u_{*})=0
almost everywhere in
\Omega
. Then

(4.1)

\inf_{w\in\mathcal{A}(u_{0})}\mathcal{E}(w)\leq\mathcal{E}(u_{*})=\int_{\Omega}[\mathrm{div}(A\nabla u_{*})]^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x+|\{u_{*}>0\}|=|\{u_{*}>0\}|\leq|\Omega|.

The claim follows.
∎

Proposition 4.3

(Existence of minimizers)
.

The infimum
\inf_{w\in\mathcal{A}(u_{0})}\mathcal{E}(w)
is attained by some
u\in\mathcal{A}(u_{0})
.

Proof.

Let
(u_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset\mathcal{A}(u_{0})
be such that

\mathcal{E}(u_{n})\rightarrow\inf_{w\in\mathcal{A}(u_{0})}\mathcal{E}(w).

Due to Lemma
4.2
we may assume that
\mathcal{E}(u_{n})\leq|\Omega|+1
for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
. This also implies that

\lVert Lu_{n}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\mathcal{E}(u_{n})\leq|\Omega|+1
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
This and Lemma
4.1
imply that
(u_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is bounded in
W^{2,2}(\Omega)
. In particular, there exists some
u\in W^{2,2}(\Omega)
and such that after passing to a subsequence we may suppose that
u_{n}\rightharpoonup u
in
W^{2,2}(\Omega)
. As a consequence,
Lu_{n}\rightharpoonup Lu
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
and thus

(4.2)

\int_{\Omega}(\mathrm{div}(A\nabla u))^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x=\lVert Lu\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\lVert Lu_{n}\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}=\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\Omega}(\mathrm{div}(A\nabla u_{n}))^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x.

Due to the fact that
\mathcal{A}(u_{0})\subset W^{2,2}(\Omega)
is convex and closed (and thus weakly closed) we find that
u\in\mathcal{A}(u_{0})
. Moreover, due to the compact embedding
W^{2,2}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\Omega)
one has
u_{n}\rightarrow u
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
and thus (possibly passing to a further subsequence) we may assume that
u_{n}(x)\rightarrow u(x)
for all
x\in\Omega\setminus N
, where
N
is a Lebesgue null set. If
x\in\Omega\setminus N
is such that
u(x)>0
then there must exist some
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
u_{n}(x)>0
for all
n\geq n_{0}
. Hence we have

\chi_{\{u>0\}}(x)\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\chi_{\{u_{n}>0\}}(x)
for all
x\in\Omega\setminus N
.
Fatou’s lemma implies

(4.3)

|\{u>0\}|=\int_{\Omega}\chi_{\{u>0\}}\;\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{\Omega}\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\chi_{\{u_{n}>0\}}\;\mathrm{d}x\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\Omega}\chi_{\{u_{n}>0\}}\;\mathrm{d}x=\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}|\{u_{n}>0\}|.

This estimate and (
4.2
) together yield

\mathcal{E}(u)\leq\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathcal{E}(u_{n})=\inf_{w\in\mathcal{A}(u_{0})}\mathcal{E}(w).

Due to the fact that
u\in\mathcal{A}(u_{0})
we infer that
u
is a minimizer of
\mathcal{E}
in
\mathcal{A}(u_{0})
as claimed.
∎

4.2.
Estimates for minimizers

Lemma 4.4
.

There exists some constant
C_{\Omega,A}>0
such that each minimizer
u\in\mathcal{A}(u_{0})
satisfies

(4.4)

\lVert u-u_{0}\rVert_{W^{2,2}}\leq C_{\Omega,A}(1+\lVert\mathrm{div}(A\nabla u_{0})\rVert_{L^{2}}).

Proof.

Let
L
and
D_{\Omega,A}
be as in Lemma
4.1
.
In particular one has
\lVert L^{-1}w\rVert_{W^{2,2}}\leq D_{\Omega,A}\lVert w\rVert_{L^{2}}
for all
w\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. Now let
u\in\mathcal{A}(u_{0})
be a minimizer. Noting that
(u-u_{0})\in W^{2,2}(\Omega)\cap W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)
and writing
u-u_{0}=L^{-1}(L(u-u_{0}))
we find

\lVert u-u_{0}\rVert_{W^{2,2}}\leq D_{\Omega,A}\lVert Lu-Lu_{0}\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq D_{\Omega,A}(\lVert Lu\rVert_{L^{2}}+\lVert Lu_{0}\rVert_{L^{2}}).

Lemma
4.2
implies that

\lVert Lu\rVert_{L^{2}}^{2}=\int_{\Omega}|\mathrm{div}(A\nabla u)|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x\leq\mathcal{E}(u)\leq|\Omega|
. Thereupon we infer

\lVert u-u_{0}\rVert_{W^{2,2}}\leq D_{\Omega,A}(\sqrt{|\Omega|}+\lVert Lu_{0}\rVert_{L^{2}}).

Replacing
D_{\Omega,A}
by
C_{\Omega,A}:=D_{\Omega,A}(1+\sqrt{|\Omega|})
the claim follows.
∎

As a consequence we obtain that
u>0
in an explicit tubular neighborhood of
\partial\Omega
.

Lemma 4.5
.

There exists a constant
C_{\Omega,A}
such that for each minimizer
u\in\mathcal{A}(u_{0})
of
\mathcal{E}
and
x\in\overline{\Omega}

(4.5)

u(x)\geq\inf_{\overline{\Omega}}u_{0}-C_{\Omega,A}(1+\lVert Lu_{0}\rVert_{L^{2}})\sqrt{\mathrm{dist}(x,\partial\Omega)}.

Proof.

Let
x_{0}\in\partial\Omega
be such that
|x-x_{0}|=\mathrm{dist}(x,\partial\Omega)
. Moreover, let
S_{\Omega}
be the Sobolev constant of the embedding
W^{2,2}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow C^{0,\frac{1}{2}}(\overline{\Omega})
. Then for
x\in\Omega
one has

(4.6)

\displaystyle u(x)

\displaystyle=u(x)-u_{0}(x)+u_{0}(x)=(u-u_{0})(x)-(u-u_{0})(x_{0})+u_{0}(x)

(4.7)

\displaystyle\geq-\lVert u-u_{0}\rVert_{C^{0,\frac{1}{2}}}\sqrt{|x-x_{0}|}+u_{0}(x)\geq-S_{\Omega}\lVert u-u_{0}\rVert_{W^{2,2}}\sqrt{\mathrm{dist}(x,\partial\Omega)}+\inf_{\overline{\Omega}}u_{0}.

Using that by Lemma
4.4

\lVert u-u_{0}\rVert_{W^{2,2}}\leq C_{\Omega,A}(1+\lVert Lu_{0}\rVert_{L^{2}})
and replacing
C_{\Omega,A}
by
S_{\Omega}C_{\Omega,A}
the claim follows.
∎

With the above results we obtain examples of minimizers with empty and nonempty free boundary.

Example 4.6

(Minimizers with empty free boundary)
.

Let
C_{\Omega,A}
be as in Lemma
4.5
. Suppose that
u_{0}\equiv c
for some constant
c>C_{\Omega,A}\sqrt{\mathrm{diam}(\overline{\Omega})}
. We claim that then the unique minimizer
u
of
\mathcal{E}
in
\mathcal{A}(u_{0})
is given by
u\equiv c
. Indeed, let
u\in\mathcal{A}(u_{0})
be a minimizer. By Lemma
4.5
we have for any
x\in\Omega

(4.8)

u(x)\geq c-C_{\Omega,A}\sqrt{\mathrm{dist}(x,\partial\Omega)}\geq c-C_{\Omega,A}\sqrt{\mathrm{diam}(\overline{\Omega})}>0.

In particular, we have
|\{u>0\}|=|\Omega|
. We conclude from Lemma
4.2
that

(4.9)

|\Omega|\geq\mathcal{E}(u)=\int_{\Omega}(Lu)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x+|\{u>0\}|\geq|\{u>0\}|\geq|\Omega|.

This implies that all inequalities must be equalities and hence
Lu=0
almost everywhere on
\Omega
. The unique solution
u\in\mathcal{A}(u_{0})
to

(4.10)

\begin{cases}Lu=0&\textrm{in $\Omega$}\\
u=u_{0}\equiv c&\textrm{on $\partial\Omega$}\end{cases}

is given by
u\equiv c
, so that this is the only possibilty for a minimizer.

Example 4.7

(Minimizers with nonempty free boundary)
.

Suppose that
\Omega=B_{1}(0)
and
u_{0}\equiv c
for some constant
c<\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{32}}\left(\int_{B_{1}(0)}\mathrm{div}(A(x)x)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x\right)^{-1/2}
. Define
v:B_{1}(0)\rightarrow\mathbb{R},v(x):=2c|x|^{2}-c
. One readily checks that
v\in\mathcal{A}(u_{0})
and

(4.11)

\mathcal{E}(v)=16c^{2}\int_{B_{1}(0)}(\mathrm{div}(A(x)x)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x+\frac{\pi}{2}<\pi=|\Omega|.

In particular one has
\inf_{w\in\mathcal{A}(u_{0})}\mathcal{E}(w)<|\Omega|
. We claim that in this case the free boundary
\{u=0\}
must be nonempty for each minimizer
u\in\mathcal{A}(u_{0})
. Indeed, if
\{u=0\}
were empty then by continuity
u>0
in
\Omega
, which leads to

\inf_{w\in\mathcal{A}(u_{0})}\mathcal{E}(w)=\mathcal{E}(u)\geq|\{u>0\}|=|\Omega|,

contradicting (
4.11
).

4.3.
Regularity away from the free boundary

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
u\in\mathcal{A}(u_{0})
be a minimizer. Then
u\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega}\setminus\{u=0\})
. Moreover
L^{2}u=0
on
\Omega\setminus\{u=0\}
and
Lu=0
on
\partial\Omega
.

Proof.

We divide the proof into two steps.
Step 1.
Interior regularity on
\Omega\setminus\{u=0\}
. Define
\Omega^{\prime}:=\Omega\setminus\{u=0\}
and let
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega^{\prime})
. By continuity of
u
there exists some
\delta>0
such that
|u|\geq\delta
on
\mathrm{spt}(\varphi)
. In particular, one has for
t\in(-\frac{\delta}{\lVert\varphi\rVert_{\infty}},\frac{\delta}{\lVert\varphi\rVert_{\infty}})
that

\{u+t\varphi>0\}=\{u>0\}.

Hence we conclude that for
t\in(-\frac{\delta}{\lVert\varphi\rVert_{\infty}},\frac{\delta}{\lVert\varphi\rVert_{\infty}})

(4.12)

\frac{\mathcal{E}(u+t\varphi)-\mathcal{E}(u)}{t}=\frac{1}{t}\int_{\Omega}[L(u+t\varphi)]^{2}-[Lu]^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x=2\int_{\Omega}LuL\varphi+t\int_{\Omega}(L\varphi)^{2}.

Since the right hand side has a limit as
t\rightarrow 0
we conclude that
t\mapsto\mathcal{E}(u+t\varphi)
is differentiable in
t=0
. Due to the fact that
u
is a minimizer,
t\mapsto\mathcal{E}(u+t\varphi)
must attain a minimium at
t=0
. Therefore,

(4.13)

0=\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{E}(u+t\varphi)\Big|_{t=0}=\lim_{t\rightarrow 0}\left(2\int_{\Omega}LuL\varphi\;\mathrm{d}x+t\int_{\Omega}(L\varphi)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x\right)=2\int_{\Omega}LuL\varphi\;\mathrm{d}x=2\int_{\Omega^{\prime}}LuL\varphi\;\mathrm{d}x.

In particular,
v:=Lu
satisfies
Lv=0
on
\Omega^{\prime}
in the sense of distributions. This implies by
[
20
, Theorem 6.33]
that
v\in C^{\infty}(\Omega^{\prime}).
We have obtained
Lu\in C^{\infty}(\Omega^{\prime})
and thus elliptic regularity yields
u\in C^{\infty}(\Omega^{\prime})
.

Step 2.
Boundary regularity.
For
\delta>0
define
\Omega_{\delta}:=\{x\in\Omega:\mathrm{dist}(x,\Omega^{C})<\delta\}
. It is well-known that for
\delta>0
small enough,
\Omega_{\delta}
is a bounded domain with smooth boundary. Moreover,
\partial\Omega_{\delta}=\partial\Omega\cup\Gamma
, where
\Gamma:=\{x\in\Omega:\mathrm{dist}(x,\Omega^{C})=\delta\}
is a smooth submanifold of
\Omega
. Possibly shrinking
\delta
we may assume by Lemma
4.5
that
u>0
on
\Omega_{2\delta}
. In particular, Step 1 implies that
u\in C^{\infty}(\Omega_{2\delta})
and thus
v:=u|_{\Gamma}\in C^{\infty}(\Gamma)
. Now let
w\in W^{2,2}(\Omega_{\delta})
be the unique function such that
w=u_{0}
on
\partial\Omega
,
w=u,\nabla w=\nabla u
on
\Gamma
and

(4.14)

\int_{\Omega}(Lw)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x=\min\left\{\int_{\Omega}(Lf)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x:f\in W^{2,2}(\Omega_{\delta}):f=u_{0}\;\textrm{on $\partial\Omega$},\;f=u,\nabla f=\nabla u\;\textrm{on $\Gamma$}\right\}.

We claim that
w=u|_{\Omega_{\delta}}
. To this end define
v\in W^{2,2}(\Omega)
via

(4.15)

v(x)=\begin{cases}w(x)&x\in\Omega_{\delta}\\
u(x)&x\not\in\Omega_{\delta}\end{cases}.

One readily checks that
v\in\mathcal{A}(u_{0})
and hence
\mathcal{E}(u)\leq\mathcal{E}(v)
. Since
u>0
on
\Omega_{\delta}
we have however
\{v>0\}\leq\{u>0\}
and therefore

(4.16)

\int_{\Omega}(Lu)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{\Omega}(Lv)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x.

Using that
u=v
on
\Omega_{\delta}^{C}
and
v=w
on
\Omega_{\delta}
we find

(4.17)

\int_{\Omega_{\delta}}(Lu)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{\Omega_{\delta}}(Lw)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x.

Since
u|_{\Omega_{\delta}}
is admissible for the minimization problem in (
4.14
) and
w
is a minimizer of this problem we conclude from (
4.17
) that
u|_{\Omega_{\delta}}
must be another minimizer of the problem in (
4.14
). Due to strict convexity of the minimization problem, its minimizer is unique and therefore
w=u|_{\Omega_{\delta}}
. It is now standard to deduce from (
4.14
) that
w=u|_{\Omega_{\delta}}
satisfies

(4.18)

\begin{cases}L^{2}w=0\qquad\textrm{in $\Omega_{\delta}$}\\
w|_{\partial\Omega_{\delta}}=u|_{\partial\Omega_{\delta}}\in C^{\infty}(\partial\Omega_{\delta})\\
\nabla w|_{\Gamma}=\nabla u|_{\Gamma}\in C^{\infty}(\Gamma)\\
Lw|_{\partial\Omega}=0\in C^{\infty}(\partial\Omega)\end{cases},

i.e.
w=u|_{\Omega_{\delta}}
solves a mixed Dirichlet-Navier problem for the operator
L^{2}
. Standard regularity theory and smoothness of
\partial\Omega_{\delta}
implies that
w\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega}_{\delta})
and
Lw=0
pointwise on
\partial\Omega
.
The claimed regularity for
u
follows from Step 1 and 2.
∎

4.4.
Variational inequalities

In this section, we use certian perturbations to obtain differential inequalities that describe minimizers.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
u\in\mathcal{A}(u_{0})
be a minimizer. Then for each
\varphi\in W^{2,2}(\Omega)\cap W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)
with
\varphi\geq 0
one has

(4.19)

\int_{\Omega}LuL\varphi\;\mathrm{d}x\leq 0.

Proof.

Let
\psi\in W^{2,2}(\Omega)\cap W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)
be such that
\psi\leq 0
. Then for all
t\geq 0
one has

(4.20)

|\{u+t\psi>0\}|\leq|\{u>0\}|\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\mathcal{E}(u)\leq\mathcal{E}(u+t\psi).

In particular for
t>0
one has

(4.21)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathcal{E}(u+t\psi)-\mathcal{E}(u)}{t}=\frac{1}{t}\int_{\Omega}[L(u+t\psi)]^{2}-[Lu]^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x+\frac{|\{u+t\psi>0\}|-|\{u>0\}|}{t}

(4.22)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{t}\int_{\Omega}[Lu+tL\psi]^{2}-[Lu]^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x=2\int_{\Omega}LuL\psi\;\mathrm{d}x+t\int_{\Omega}[L\psi]^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x.

Letting
t\rightarrow 0
we find

(4.23)

0\leq 2\int_{\Omega}LuL\psi\;\mathrm{d}x.

Now let
\varphi\in W^{2,2}(\Omega)\cap W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)
be such that
\varphi\geq 0
. Considering
\psi:=-\varphi
in (
4.23
) the claim follows.
∎

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
u\in\mathcal{A}(u_{0})
be a minimizer. Then there exists a finite Radon measure supported on
\{u=0\}
such that

(4.24)

\int_{\Omega}LuL\varphi\;\mathrm{d}x=-\int\varphi\;\mathrm{d}\mu\qquad\textrm{for all }\varphi\in W^{2,2}(\Omega)\cap W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega).

Proof.

We show that (
4.24
) in several steps.

Step 1.
We construct a Radon measure
\mu
such that (
4.24
) holds true for all
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
.

Define
T:C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by
Tf:=-\int_{\Omega}LuLf\;\mathrm{d}x
. Lemma
4.9
implies that
Tf\geq 0
for all
f\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
such that
f\geq 0
. By the Riesz-Markow-Kakutani Theorem (cf.
[
19
, Theorem 1.39]
) there exists a Radon measure
\mu
on
\Omega
such that

Tf=\int_{\Omega}f\;\mathrm{d}\mu
for all
f\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
.
This shows (
4.24
) for all
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega).

Step 2.
We show that
\mathrm{spt}(\mu)
is a compact subset of
\Omega
. As a consequence of this and the Radon measure property
\mu
is finite.

To this end notice that by Lemma
4.5
there exists some
\delta>0
such that
u>0
on
\Omega_{\delta}:=\{x\in\Omega:\mathrm{dist}(x,\Omega)<\delta\}
. We show that
\mathrm{spt}(\mu)\subset\Omega\setminus\Omega_{\delta}
. Indeed, for any
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega_{\delta})
one has by Lemma
4.8

(4.25)

\int_{\Omega}\varphi\;\mathrm{d}\mu=-\int_{\Omega}LuL\varphi\;\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\Omega}L^{2}u\;\varphi\;\mathrm{d}x=0.

This implies that
\mathrm{spt}(\mu)\cap\Omega_{\delta}=\emptyset
and thus
\mathrm{spt}(\mu)\subset\Omega\setminus\Omega_{\delta}
. Due to the fact that
\Omega\setminus\Omega_{\delta}:=\{x\in\Omega:\mathrm{dist}(x,\Omega^{C})\geq\delta\}
is compact we infer the claim. The same argument can be repeated with
\Omega\setminus\{u=0\}
in place of
\Omega_{\delta}
to deduce that
\mathrm{spt}(\mu)\subset\{u=0\}
.

Step 3.
We show that (
4.24
) holds true for any
\varphi\in W^{2,2}(\Omega)\cap W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)
.

Let
\delta>0
be as in Step 2. Further choose a function
\rho\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
such that
\rho\equiv 1
on
\Omega\setminus\Omega_{\delta}
. For arbitrary given
\varphi\in W^{2,2}(\Omega)\cap W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)
we can write
\varphi=\varphi\rho+\varphi(1-\rho)
. Now
\varphi\rho
is compactly supported in
\Omega
and hence lies in
W^{2,2}_{0}(\Omega)
. In particular, there exists
(\psi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
such that
\psi_{n}\rightarrow\varphi\rho
in
W^{2,2}(\Omega)
. Therefore,
L\psi_{n}\rightarrow L(\varphi\rho)
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. Since
W^{2,2}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})
we also have uniform convergence of
(\psi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
on
\overline{\Omega}
. This and the fact that
\mu
is a finite measure implies

(4.26)

\int_{\Omega}LuL(\varphi\rho)\;\mathrm{d}x=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\Omega}LuL\psi_{n}\;\mathrm{d}x=-\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int_{\Omega}\psi_{n}\;\mathrm{d}\mu=-\int_{\Omega}\varphi\rho\;\mathrm{d}\mu.

Now using that
u\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega}\setminus\{u=0\})
and
\varphi(1-\rho)
is supported on
\{u>0\}
we find that
Lu
is smooth in an open neighborhood of
\mathrm{spt}(\varphi(1-\rho))
. In view of this and

Lu=0
on
\partial\Omega
by Lemma
4.8
one readily computes with some integrations by parts

(4.27)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}LuL(\varphi(1-\rho))\;\mathrm{d}x=0.

Since
\varphi(1-\rho)=0
on
\Omega\setminus\Omega_{\delta}
and
\mu(\Omega_{\delta})=0
by Step 2 we also have

\int_{\Omega}\varphi(1-\rho)\;\mathrm{d}\mu=0.

Thus we conclude

(4.28)

\int_{\Omega}LuL(\varphi(1-\rho))\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\varphi(1-\rho)\;\mathrm{d}\mu.

Adding this and (
4.26
) the claim follows.
∎

Lemma 4.11
.

Let
u\in\mathcal{A}(u_{0})
be a minimizer. Then
u\in W^{3,q}(\Omega)
for all
q\in[1,2)
. In particular,
u\in W^{2,p}(\Omega)
for any
p\in[1,\infty)
and
u\in C^{1,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
for all
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Moreover, (
4.24
) is valid for each
\varphi\in W^{2,s}(\Omega)\cap W_{0}^{1,s}(\Omega)
for any
s\in(1,\infty)
.

Proof.

For the regularity assertions we only need to show that
u\in W^{3,q}_{loc}(\Omega)
for any
q\in[1,2)
. The rest of the claimed regularity follows from the boundary regularity in Lemma
4.8
and Sobolev embeddings in dimension two. We therefore fix
q\in[1,2)
and show
W^{3,q}_{loc}(\Omega)
-regularity. We may without loss of generality assume that
q\in(1,2)
as the case
q=1
follows immediately from the cases with higher integrability. Fix therefore
q\in(1,2)
.
By Lemma
A.1
we may choose a sequence of functions
(g_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset L^{1}(\Omega)
such that

\lVert g_{n}\rVert_{L^{1}}\leq\mu(\Omega)
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
and for every
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)

(4.29)

\int_{\Omega}g_{n}\varphi\;\mathrm{d}x\rightarrow\int_{\Omega}\varphi\;\mathrm{d}\mu\qquad(n\rightarrow\infty).

By
[
8
, Theorem 8]
there exist
(v_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset W_{0}^{1,q}(\Omega)
such that

(4.30)

\int_{\Omega}v_{n}L\varphi\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}g_{n}\varphi\;\mathrm{d}x\qquad\textrm{for all }\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)

and some
C>0
independent of
n
such that
\lVert v_{n}\rVert_{W^{1,q}}\leq C\lVert g_{n}\rVert_{L^{1}}
. In particular
\lVert v_{n}\rVert_{W^{1,q}}\leq C\mu(\Omega)
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Up to a subsequence
(v_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converges weakly in
W_{0}^{1,q}(\Omega)
to some
v\in W^{1,q}_{0}(\Omega)
. From (
4.30
) we deduce that

(4.31)

\int_{\Omega}vL\varphi\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\varphi\;\mathrm{d}\mu\qquad\textrm{for all }\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega).

By Lemma
4.1
there exists a unique
\tilde{u}\in W^{2,2}(\Omega)\cap W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)
such that
L\tilde{u}=v-Lu_{0}
pointwise in
\Omega.
Since
v,Lu_{0}\in W^{1,q}(\Omega)
we find that
\tilde{u}\in W^{3,q}_{loc}(\Omega)
. Now we define
\overline{u}:=\tilde{u}+u_{0}
. Then, clearly
\overline{u}\in W^{3,q}(\Omega)
and
\overline{u}
is a solution of

(4.32)

\begin{cases}L\overline{u}=v&\textrm{in $\Omega$}\\
\overline{u}=u_{0}&\textrm{on $\partial\Omega$}\end{cases}.

In particular, for each
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
one has by (
4.31
)

(4.33)

\int_{\Omega}L\overline{u}L\varphi\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}vL\varphi\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\varphi\;\mathrm{d}\mu.

Using Lemma
4.10
we find that

(4.34)

\int_{\Omega}L(u+\overline{u})L\varphi\;\mathrm{d}x=0\qquad\textrm{for all }\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega).

This implies by
[
20
, Theorem 6.33]
that
L(u+\overline{u})\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)
, whereupon elliptic regularity yields
u+\overline{u}\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)
. This and the fact that
\tilde{u}\in W^{3,q}_{loc}(\Omega)
show that
u\in W^{3,q}_{loc}(\Omega)
.
The claim is shown. It remains to show the validity of (
4.24
) for any
\varphi\in W^{2,s}(\Omega)\cap W_{0}^{1,s}(\Omega)
(any
s>1
).
To this end let
\varphi\in W^{2,s}(\Omega)\cap W_{0}^{1,s}(\Omega)
, in particular
\varphi|_{\partial\Omega}=0
.

Intermediate claim.
We claim that
\varphi
is a
W^{2,s}
-limit of a sequence
(\varphi_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\subset C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})\cap W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)
. To this end notice that the
Dirichlet Laplacian

\Delta_{0}
on
L^{s}(\Omega)
is a densely defined and sectorial operator with domain
W^{2,s}(\Omega)\cap W_{0}^{1,s}(\Omega)
. Hence
\Delta_{0}
generates an analytic semigroup
(e^{t\Delta_{0}})_{t\geq 0}
in
L^{s}(\Omega)
. Next set
\varphi_{j}:=e^{\frac{1}{j}\Delta_{0}}
for all
j\in\mathbb{N}.
By
[
36
, Proposition 2.1.1]
it follows that
\varphi_{j}
lies in the domain of
\Delta_{0}^{k}
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
and hence in
C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})
. Since
\varphi|_{\partial\Omega}=0
we deduce
\varphi\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega})\cap W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)
. Moreover, since
\varphi
lies in the domain of
\Delta_{0}
we have by
[
36
, Proposition 2.1.4(iv)]

(4.35)

\lVert\Delta_{0}\varphi-\Delta_{0}\varphi_{j}\rVert_{L^{s}}=\lVert\Delta_{0}(e^{\frac{1}{j}\Delta_{0}}\varphi-\varphi)\rVert_{L^{s}}\rightarrow 0\qquad(j\rightarrow\infty).

Since
\Delta_{0}:W^{2,s}(\Omega)\cap W_{0}^{1,s}(\Omega)\rightarrow L^{s}(\Omega)
is an isomorphism we conclude that
\lVert v-v_{j}\rVert_{W^{2,s}}\rightarrow 0
(
j\rightarrow\infty
). The intermediate claim follows. Now Lemma
4.10
yields that

(4.36)

\int_{\Omega}LuL\varphi_{j}\;\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\Omega}\varphi_{j}\;\mathrm{d}\mu\qquad\textrm{for all }j\in\mathbb{N}.

Using that
\varphi_{j}\rightarrow\varphi
in
W^{2,s}(\Omega)
and
u\in W^{2,\frac{s}{s-1}}(\Omega)
we find that

\int_{\Omega}LuL\varphi_{j}\;\mathrm{d}x\rightarrow\int_{\Omega}LuL\varphi\;\mathrm{d}x
as
j\rightarrow\infty
.
Moreover, using that
W^{2,s}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})
we find that
\varphi_{j}\rightarrow\varphi
uniformly on
\overline{\Omega}
and hence

\int_{\Omega}\varphi_{j}\;\mathrm{d}\mu\rightarrow\int_{\Omega}\varphi\;\mathrm{d}\mu
as
j\rightarrow\infty.

The previous two equations and (
4.36
) imply

\int_{\Omega}LuL\varphi\;\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\Omega}\varphi\;\mathrm{d}\mu.

Since
\varphi\in W^{2,s}(\Omega)\cap W^{1,s}_{0}(\Omega)
was arbitrary, the claim follows.
∎

Lemma 4.12
.

Let
u\in\mathcal{A}(u_{0})
be a minimizer. Then
v:=Lu
satisfies
v\leq 0
almost everywhere.

Proof.

Let
\psi\in L^{2}(\Omega)
be arbitrary such that
\psi\geq 0
almost everywhere. By Lemma
4.1
we can find
\varphi\in W^{2,2}(\Omega)\cap W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)
such that
L\varphi=-\psi
, in particular
L\varphi\leq 0
pointwise. By the maximum principle (cf.
[
32
, Chapter II Theorem 5.2]
) one has that
\varphi\leq 0
. Applying Lemma
4.9
we find

(4.37)

\int_{\Omega}v\psi\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}Lu(-L\varphi)\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}LuL(-\varphi)\;\mathrm{d}x\leq 0.

Since
\psi\in L^{2}(\Omega)
was arbitrary such that
\psi\geq 0
almost everywhere, we may consider the case of
\psi=\chi_{\{v>0\}}
and infer that
v\leq 0
almost everywhere.
∎

Lemma 4.13
.

Let
u\in\mathcal{A}(u_{0})
be a minimizer. Then,

\limsup_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{|\{0<u<\varepsilon\}|}{\varepsilon}<\infty.

Proof.

Let
\theta:=\inf_{\overline{\Omega}}u_{0}>0
. Lemma
4.5
yields that there exists some
\delta>0
such that
u>\tfrac{\theta}{2}
on
\Omega_{\delta}:=\{x\in\Omega:\mathrm{dist}(x,\Omega^{C})<\delta\}
. Choose some function
\varphi^{*}\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
such that
\varphi^{*}\equiv 1
on
\Omega_{\delta}^{C}
. This is possible as
\Omega_{\delta}^{C}
is compactly contained in
\Omega.
In particular, for
\varepsilon<\frac{\theta}{2}
one has

(4.38)

|\{0<u<\varepsilon\}|=|\{0<u<\varepsilon\}\cap\Omega_{\delta}^{C}|\leq|\{0<u<\varepsilon\varphi^{*}\}|.

Moreover,

(4.39)

\displaystyle|\{0<u<\varepsilon\varphi^{*}\}|

\displaystyle=|\{u>0\}|-|\{u-\varepsilon\varphi^{*}>0\}|

(4.40)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}(u)-\int_{\Omega}[Lu]^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x-\left(\mathcal{E}(u-\varepsilon\varphi^{*})-\int_{\Omega}[L(u-\varepsilon\varphi^{*})]^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x\right)

(4.41)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}(u)-\mathcal{E}(u-\varepsilon\varphi^{*})+\int_{\Omega}[L(u-\varepsilon\varphi^{*})]^{2}-[Lu]^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x

(4.42)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}[L(u-\varepsilon\varphi^{*})]^{2}-[Lu]^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x=-2\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}LuL\varphi^{*}\;\mathrm{d}x+\varepsilon^{2}\int_{\Omega}[L\varphi^{*}]^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x.

Using (
4.38
) and the previous computation we find that for
\varepsilon<\tfrac{\theta}{4}
one has

(4.43)

\frac{|\{0<u<\varepsilon\}|}{\varepsilon}\leq\frac{|\{0<u<\varepsilon\varphi^{*}\}|}{\varepsilon}\leq-2\int_{\Omega}LuL\varphi^{*}\;\mathrm{d}x+\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}[L\varphi^{*}]^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x.

Using Lemma
4.2
wa can now derive

(4.44)

\limsup_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{|\{0<u<\varepsilon\}|}{\varepsilon}\leq-2\int_{\Omega}LuL\varphi^{*}\;\mathrm{d}x\leq 2\lVert Lu\rVert_{L^{2}}\lVert L\varphi^{*}\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq 2\sqrt{\mathcal{E}(u)}\lVert L\varphi^{*}\rVert_{L^{2}}\leq 2\sqrt{|\Omega|}\lVert L\varphi^{*}\rVert_{L^{2}}.

The claim follows.
∎

4.5.
Semiconvexity

The formula in Theorem
1.1
gives information about the full second derivative of a distributional solution of
L^{2}u=\mu
. Since each minimizer
u\in\mathcal{A}(u_{0})
solves an equation of the type
L^{2}u=-\mu
(see Lemma
4.10
) we can find a representation of the full second derivative of
u
.

Lemma 4.14
.

Let
u\in\mathcal{A}(u_{0})
be a minimizer and
v:=Lu
. Then there exists some locally bounded and Borel measurable
K:\Omega\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}_{sym}
which is smooth on
\Omega\times\Omega\setminus\{(x,x):x\in\Omega\}

and some
H\in C^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}_{sym}^{2\times 2})
such that for almost every
x\in\Omega

(4.45)

D^{2}u(x)=-\frac{1}{2}v(x)A(x)^{-1}+\int_{\Omega}K(x,y)\;\mathrm{d}\mu(y)+H(x).

Proof.

By Lemma
4.10

w:=-u
solves
L^{2}w=\mu
distributionally. By Theorem
1.1
we find that there exists some locally bounded and Borel measurable
\tilde{K}:\Omega\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}_{sym}
which is smooth on
\Omega\times\Omega\setminus\{(x,x):x\in\Omega\}
and
\tilde{H}\in C^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}_{sym})
such that

(4.46)

D^{2}w=-(Lw(x))A(x)^{-1}+\int_{\Omega}\tilde{K}(x,y)\;\mathrm{d}\mu(y)+\tilde{H}(x).

Setting
K:=-\tilde{K},H:=-\tilde{H}
and noticing that
D^{2}w=-D^{2}u,Lw=-Lu
the claim follows.
∎

As a consequence we obtain the local semiconvexity of
u
.

Lemma 4.15

(Local Semiconvexity)
.

Let
u\in\mathcal{A}(u_{0})
be a minimizer and
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\subset\Omega
a convex subdomain. Then there exists a constant
M=M(\Omega^{\prime})>0
such that
x\mapsto u(x)+\frac{1}{2}M|x-x_{0}|^{2}
defines a convex function on
\Omega^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Fix
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\subset\Omega
a convex subdomain. Let
K(x,y)=(k_{ij}(x,y))_{i,j=1}^{2}
and
H(x)=(h_{ij}(x))_{i,j=1}^{2}
be as in Lemma
4.14
.
We claim first that there exists some
M>0
such that for each
x\in\Omega^{\prime}
the matrix
D^{2}u(x)+MI_{2}
is positive definite. Since
v\leq 0
by Lemma
4.12
we have that
x\mapsto-v(x)A(x)^{-1}
is positive definite.
Hence it suffices to show that there exists
M^{\prime}>0
such that for each
x\in\Omega^{\prime}

(4.47)

\int_{\Omega}K(x,y)\;\mathrm{d}\mu(y)+H(x)+M^{\prime}I_{2\times 2}

is positive definite. To this end it suffices to show that each entry of
x\mapsto\int_{\Omega}K(x,y)\;\mathrm{d}\mu(y)+H(x)
is bounded below on
\Omega^{\prime}
(since then
M^{\prime}
can be chosen in such a way that the matrix is strictly diagonally dominant).
Now notice that there exists
C_{1}>0
such that
|K(x,y)|\leq C_{1}
for all
(x,y)\in\Omega^{\prime}\times\mathrm{spt}(\mu)
and
C_{2}>0
such that
|H(x)|\leq C_{2}
for all
x\in\Omega^{\prime}
. This implies that for all
i,j\in\{1,2\}
there holds

(4.48)

\int_{\Omega}k_{ij}(x,y)\;\mathrm{d}\mu(y)+h_{ij}(x)\geq\int_{\Omega}(-C_{1})\;\mathrm{d}\mu(y)-C_{2}=-C_{1}\mu(\Omega)-C_{2}.

The required boundedness from below and hence the claim follows. Having now some
M>0
such that
D^{2}u(x)+MI_{2}
is positive definite we obtain that
\hat{u}:\Omega^{\prime}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\hat{u}(x)=u(x)+\frac{1}{2}M|x-x_{0}|^{2}
satisfies

(4.49)

D^{2}\hat{u}(x)=D^{2}u(x)+MI_{2},

which is positive definite on
\Omega^{\prime}
. In particular,
\hat{u}
is convex on
\Omega^{\prime}
.
∎

The derived semiconvexity will be used often in the sequel to find blow-up profiles for minimizers
u\in\mathcal{A}(u_{0})
. This is done using a special version of Aleksandrov’s theorem, derived in
[
40
, Lemma 4.1]
.

Lemma 4.16

(Aleksandov’s theorem, cf.
[
40
, Lemma 4.1]
)
.

Let
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
a bounded and convex domain and
f\in W^{2,2}(\Omega^{\prime})\cap C^{1}(\Omega^{\prime})
be such that
x\mapsto f(x)+\frac{1}{2}M|x-x_{0}|^{2}
is convex for some
M\in\mathbb{R}
. If
x_{0}\in\Omega^{\prime}
is a Lebesgue point of
D^{2}f
then

(4.50)

f(x)-f(x_{0})-\nabla f(x_{0})\cdot(x-x_{0})-\tfrac{1}{2}(x-x_{0})^{T}(D^{2}f)^{*}(x_{0})(x-x_{0})=o(|x-x_{0}|^{2}),

where
(D^{2}f)^{*}(x_{0}):=\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\frac{1}{|B_{r}(x_{0})|}\int_{B_{r}(x_{0})}D^{2}f(y)\;\mathrm{d}y
.

5.
Analysis of the nodal set

In this section we have a closer look at the nodal set
\{u=0\}
, using methods of
[
40
, Section 4]
. This set a priori consists of

•

Regular nodal points
x_{0}\in\Omega
, where
\nabla u(x_{0})\neq 0.

•

Singular nodal points
x_{0}\in\Omega
, where
\nabla u(x_{0})=0.

We intend so show that there exist no singular nodal points. To this end, we study the singular nodal set
\{x_{0}\in\Omega:u(x_{0})=0,\nabla u(x_{0})=0\}
. We divide the singular nodal set into
good points and bad points
.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
x_{0}\in\Omega
be such that
u(x_{0})=0
and
\nabla u(x_{0})=0
. Define
v=Lu
. Notice that
-v
satisfies
-v\geq 0
and
L(-v)\geq 0
and let
(-v)^{*}
be given as in (
2.3
).
We say that
x_{0}
is a
good point
if
(-v)^{*}(x_{0})<\infty
. Moreover
x_{0}
is a
bad point
if
(-v)^{*}(x_{0})=\infty
.

5.1.
Good points

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
u\in\mathcal{A}(u_{0})
be a minimizer and
x_{0}\in\Omega
be such that
(-v)^{*}(x_{0})<\infty
. Then

\mathrm{(i)}

x_{0}
is not an atom of
\mu
.

\mathrm{(ii)}

x_{0}
is a Lebesgue point of
D^{2}u
.

Proof.

First we show (i). Suppose that
x_{0}
is an atom of
\mu
, i.e.
\mu(\{x_{0}\})>0
. Define
c:=\mu(\{x_{0}\})
. One readily checks that
\tilde{\mu}=\mu-c\delta_{x_{0}}
(where
\delta_{x_{0}}
denotes the Dirac measure with point mass at
x_{0}
) is also a Radon measure. With this, Lemma
3.8
and the fact that
L^{2}(-u)=\mu
by Lemma
4.10
one obtains that there exists
w\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)
such that

(5.1)

-u(x)=\int_{\Omega}G_{L^{2}}(x,y)\;\mathrm{d}\mu(y)+w(x)=cG_{L^{2}}(x,x_{0})+\int_{\Omega}G_{L^{2}}(x,y)\;\mathrm{d}\tilde{\mu}(y)+w(x).

One infers from
[
40
, Corollary 3.5]
that for almost every
x\in\Omega
there holds

(5.2)

-v(x)=-Lu(x)=cLG_{L^{2}}(x,x_{0})+\int_{\Omega}LG_{L^{2}}(x,y)\;\mathrm{d}\tilde{\mu}(y)+Lw(x).

Due to the fact that by Lemma
2.6
we have
LG_{L^{2}}(x,x_{0})=G_{L}(x,x_{0})\geq 0
we conclude that for almost every
x\in\Omega
one has

-v(x)\geq cG_{L}(x,x_{0})+Lw(x).
In particular, for all
r>0
we have

(5.3)

\fint_{D_{r}(x_{0})}(-v)(x)\;\mathrm{d}x\geq c\fint_{D_{r}(x_{0})}G_{L}(x,x_{0})\;\mathrm{d}x+\fint_{D_{r}(x_{0})}Lw(x)\;\mathrm{d}x.

The last integral tends to the bounded quantity
Lw(x_{0})
as
r\rightarrow 0
(which can readily be checked using
B_{cr}(x_{0})\subset D_{r}(x_{0})\subset B_{Cr}(x_{0})
, cf. Lemma
2.3
). We next claim that

(5.4)

\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\fint_{D_{r}(x_{0})}G_{L}(x,x_{0})\;\mathrm{d}x=\infty.

To this end observe that by Lemma
2.7
there exists a continuous positive function
c_{1}
and a continuous function
f_{1}
such that
G_{L}(x,x_{0})=-c_{1}(x)\log(A(x_{0})^{-1}(x-x_{0})\cdot(x-x_{0}))+f_{1}
.
Using that for some
D>0
one has
A(x_{0})^{-1}(x-x_{0})\cdot(x-x_{0})\leq D|x-x_{0}|^{2}
we compute for any
x\in D_{r}(x_{0})

(5.5)

G_{L}(x,x_{0})\geq-c_{1}(x)\log(D|x-x_{0}|^{2})+f_{1}(x)\geq-c_{1}(x)\log(DC^{2}r^{2})+f_{1}(x).

Here we have used once again that
D_{r}(x_{0})\subset B_{Cr}(x_{0})
(cf. Lemma
2.3
). We obtain for
r>0
suitably small

(5.6)

\fint_{D_{r}(x_{0})}G_{L}(x,x_{0})\;\mathrm{d}x\geq-\log(DC^{2}r^{2})\fint_{D_{r}(x_{0})}c_{1}(x)\;\mathrm{d}x+\fint_{D_{r}(x_{0})}f_{1}(x)\;\mathrm{d}x.

Now
\fint_{D_{r}(x_{0})}c_{1}(x)\;\mathrm{d}x\rightarrow c_{1}(x_{0})>0
and
\fint_{D_{r}(x_{0})}f_{1}(x)\;\mathrm{d}x\rightarrow f_{1}(x_{0})\in\mathbb{R}
as
r\rightarrow 0
. This and the fact that

\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}(-\log(DC^{2}r^{2}))=\infty

yield (
5.4
). Using this finding in (
5.3
) yields

(5.7)

(-v)^{*}(x_{0})=\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\fint_{D_{r}(x_{0})}(-v)(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\infty,

contradicting the assumption
(-v)^{*}(x_{0})<\infty.
Hence (i) is shown. We proceed with (ii). First observe that by Lemma
4.14
there exists some locally bounded and Borel measurable
K:\Omega\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}_{sym}
which lies is smooth on
\Omega\times\Omega\setminus\{(x,x):x\in\Omega\}

and some
H\in C^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}_{sym}^{2\times 2})
such that for a.e.
x\in\Omega

(5.8)

D^{2}u(x)=-v(x)A(x)^{-1}+\int_{\Omega}K(x,y)\;\mathrm{d}\mu(y)+H(x).

We show that
x_{0}
is a Lebesgue point of each of the summands appearing in the above equation. Clearly,
x_{0}
is a Lebesgue point of
H
as
H
is smooth. By Lemma
2.4
,
x_{0}
is also a Lebesgue point of
(-v)
with
\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\fint_{B_{r}(x_{0})}(-v)(x)=(-v)^{*}(x_{0})
. Using this and
(-v)^{*}(x_{0})\in[0,\infty)
it is a direct consequence of Lemma
A.2
(with
f=v
and
g=a_{ij}
where
a_{ij}
is any entry of
A
) that
x_{0}
is a Lebesgue point of the first summand of (
5.8
).
Next we investigate the Lebesgue point property of the second summand. Observe

(5.9)

\displaystyle\fint_{B_{r}(x_{0})}\left|\int_{\Omega}K(x,y)\;\mathrm{d}\mu(y)-\int_{\Omega}K(x_{0},y)\;\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\right|\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\fint_{B_{r}(x_{0})}\left|\int_{\Omega}K(x,y)-K(x_{0},y)\;\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\right|\;\mathrm{d}x

(5.10)

\displaystyle\leq\fint_{B_{r}(x_{0})}\int_{\Omega}|K(x,y)-K(x_{0},y)|\;\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\;\mathrm{d}x

(5.11)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\fint_{B_{r}(x_{0})}|K(x,y)-K(x_{0},y)|\;\mathrm{d}x\;\mathrm{d}\mu(y),

where we used Tonelli’s theorem in the last step. Due to the fact that
K
is locally bounded in
\Omega\times\Omega
(say,
|K|\leq C
on
\overline{B_{r_{0}}(x_{0})}\times\mathrm{spt}(\mu)
for some
r_{0}>0
suitably small) we find that

(5.12)

\fint_{B_{r}(x_{0})}|K(x,y)-K(x_{0},y)|\;\mathrm{d}x\leq 2C\qquad\textrm{for all }r>0\;\textrm{sufficiently small}.

Notice that for any
y\in\Omega\setminus\{x_{0}\}
the map
x\mapsto K(x,y)-K(x_{0},y)
is smooth in a neighborhood of
x_{0}
. Therefore,

(5.13)

\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\fint_{B_{r}(x_{0})}|K(x,y)-K(x_{0},y)|\;\mathrm{d}x=0\qquad\textrm{for all }y\in\Omega\setminus\{x_{0}\}.

Since
x_{0}
is not an atom of
\mu
(by (i)) we infer

(5.14)

\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\fint_{B_{r}(x_{0})}|K(x,y)-K(x_{0},y)|\;\mathrm{d}x=0\qquad\textrm{for $\mu$-a.e. $y\in\Omega$.}

This and (
5.12
) allow us to apply the dominated convergence theorem in (
5.11
) and infer

(5.15)

\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\fint_{B_{r}(x_{0})}\left|\int_{\Omega}K(x,y)\;\mathrm{d}\mu(y)-\int_{\Omega}K(x_{0},y)\;\mathrm{d}\mu(y)\right|\;\mathrm{d}x=0.

We obtain that
x_{0}
is also a Lebesgue point of the second summand of (
5.8
). It is shown that every summand on the right hand side of (
5.8
) has a Lebesgue point at
x_{0}
. We infer that
x_{0}
is a Lebesgue point of
D^{2}u
and hence the claim.
∎

Lemma 5.3

(Blow-up Analysis)
.

Let
u\in\mathcal{A}(u_{0})
be a minimizer and
x_{0}
be a good point, i.e.
u(x_{0})=0,\nabla u(x_{0})=0
and
(-v)^{*}(x_{0})<\infty
. Let
B:=\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\fint_{B_{r}(x_{0})}D^{2}u(x)\;\mathrm{d}x
(which exists due to Lemma
5.2
). Then

\mathrm{(i)}

for all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
one has
\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\frac{u(x_{0}+rz)}{r^{2}}=\tfrac{1}{2}z^{T}Bz.

The convergence is actually uniform on compact subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with respect to
z
.

\mathrm{(ii)}

Either
B=0
or
B
is positive definite.

Proof.

Lemma
5.2
and Lemma
4.11
imply that
u\in W^{2,2}(\Omega)\cap C^{1}(\Omega)
and
D^{2}u
has a Lebesgue point at
x_{0}
. Moreover, Lemma
4.15
implies that there exists an open neighborhood
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\subset\Omega
of
x_{0}
and
M>0
such that
x\mapsto u(x)+\tfrac{1}{2}M|x-x_{0}|^{2}
is convex. Then, Lemma
4.16
yields that

(5.16)

u(x)-u(x_{0})-\nabla u(x_{0})\cdot(x-x_{0})-\tfrac{1}{2}(x-x_{0})^{T}B(x-x_{0})=o(|x-x_{0}|^{2}).

Since
u(x_{0})=0,\nabla u(x_{0})=0
we have

u(x)=\tfrac{1}{2}(x-x_{0})^{T}B(x-x_{0})+o(|x-x_{0}|^{2}).

Looking at
x=x_{0}+rz
for some
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
one readily infers

(5.17)

u(x_{0}+rz)=\tfrac{1}{2}r^{2}z^{T}Bz+o(r^{2}|z|^{2}).

Since
|z|^{2}
is bounded on a compact subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, (i) follows.
Now we turn to (ii). By Lemma
4.13
there exists
C>0
such that for all
\varepsilon>0
small enough

(5.18)

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle\geq\frac{|\{x\in\Omega:0<u(x)<\varepsilon\}|}{\varepsilon}.

We aim to estimate the term on the right hand side from below using the asymptotics shown in (i). To this end let
\tau>0
be arbitrary. For
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small such that
B_{\tau\sqrt{\varepsilon}}(x_{0})\subset\Omega
we may compute

(5.19)

\displaystyle|\{x\in\Omega:0<u(x)<\varepsilon\}|\geq|\{x\in B_{\tau\sqrt{\varepsilon}}(x_{0}):0<u(x)<\varepsilon\}|

(5.20)

\displaystyle=|x_{0}+\sqrt{\varepsilon}\{z\in B_{\tau}(0):0<u(x_{0}+\sqrt{\varepsilon}z)<\varepsilon\}|=\varepsilon|\{z\in B_{\tau}(0):0<u(x_{0}+\sqrt{\varepsilon}z)<\varepsilon\}|

(5.21)

\displaystyle=\varepsilon\left|\left\{z\in B_{\tau}(0):0<\tfrac{u(x_{0}+\sqrt{\varepsilon}z)}{\varepsilon}<1\right\}\right|.

This and (
5.18
) yield that for
\varepsilon>0
small enough

(5.22)

C\geq\left|\left\{z\in B_{\tau}(0):0<\frac{u(x_{0}+\sqrt{\varepsilon}z)}{\varepsilon}<1\right\}\right|.

Due to the fact that
\frac{u(x_{0}+\sqrt{\varepsilon}z)}{\varepsilon}\rightarrow\frac{1}{2}z^{T}Bz
uniformly in
z\in\overline{B_{\tau}(0)}
we obtain that for
\varepsilon>0
small enough

(5.23)

\left|\left\{z\in B_{\tau}(0):0<\tfrac{u(x_{0}+\sqrt{\varepsilon}z)}{\varepsilon}<1\right\}\right|\geq\left|\left\{z\in B_{\tau}(0):\tfrac{1}{4}<\tfrac{1}{2}z^{T}Bz<\tfrac{3}{4}\right\}\right|.

This and (
5.22
) yield that for
\tau>0
arbitrary

C\geq\left|\left\{z\in B_{\tau}(0):\tfrac{1}{4}<\tfrac{1}{2}z^{T}Bz<\tfrac{3}{4}\right\}\right|.

Since
C
is independent of
\tau
we obtain

(5.24)

C\geq\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left|\left\{z\in B_{n}(0):\tfrac{1}{4}<\tfrac{1}{2}z^{T}Bz<\tfrac{3}{4}\right\}\right|=\left|\left\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\tfrac{1}{4}<\tfrac{1}{2}z^{T}Bz<\tfrac{3}{4}\right\}\right|.

It remains to show that
B=0
or
B
is positive definite. First note that
B
is by definition symmetric. Therefore, there exists an orthogonal matrix
S\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
and eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
such that

(5.25)

S^{T}BS=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}&0\\
0&\lambda_{2}\end{pmatrix}=:\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}).

Recall that
B=0
iff
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}=0
and
B
is positive definite iff
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}>0
. Hence we investigate the properties of
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
. Before we start doing so, note that the substitution
w=Sz
on the right hand side of (
5.24
) yields

(5.26)

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle\geq\left|\left\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\tfrac{1}{4}<\tfrac{1}{2}(\lambda_{1}w_{1}^{2}+\lambda_{2}w_{2}^{2})<\tfrac{3}{4}\right\}\right|=\left|\left\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\tfrac{1}{2}<\lambda_{1}w_{1}^{2}+\lambda_{2}w_{2}^{2}<\tfrac{3}{2}\right\}\right|.

Intermediate claim 1.

One eigenvalue must be nonnegative and if there is a strictly negative eigenvalue, the other one must be strictly positive.

To this end note that
Lu\leq 0
almost everywhere, i.e.

(5.27)

0\leq\mathrm{div}(A(x)\nabla u(x))=A(x):D^{2}u(x)+\mathbf{div}(A)(x)\cdot\nabla u(x)\qquad\textrm{for a.e. $x\in\Omega$}.

Since
x_{0}
is a Lebesgue point of
D^{2}u,A,\mathbf{div}(A),\nabla u
and furthermore
B=\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\fint_{B_{r}(x_{0})}D^{2}u(x)\;\mathrm{d}x
one readily checks with the aid of Lemma
A.2
that

(5.28)

0\leq A(x_{0}):B+\mathbf{div}(A)(x_{0})\cdot\nabla u(x_{0})=A(x_{0}):B=\mathrm{trace}(A(x_{0})^{T}B)=\mathrm{trace}(A(x_{0})B).

Now compute with
e_{1}=(1,0)^{T},e_{2}=(0,1)^{T}

(5.29)

\displaystyle\mathrm{trace}(A(x_{0})B)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{trace}(A(x_{0})S\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})S^{T})=\mathrm{trace}((S^{T}A(x_{0})S)\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}))

(5.30)

\displaystyle=e_{1}^{T}(S^{T}A(x_{0})S)\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})e_{1}+e_{2}^{T}(S^{T}A(x_{0})S)\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})e_{2}

(5.31)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}e_{1}^{T}(S^{T}A(x_{0})S)e_{1}+\lambda_{2}e_{2}^{T}(S^{T}A(x_{0})S)e_{2}=\lambda_{1}(Se_{1})^{T}A(x_{0})(Se_{1})+\lambda_{2}(Se_{2})^{T}A(x_{0})(Se_{2}).

Define
\theta_{i}:=(Se_{i})^{T}A(x_{0})(Se_{i})
for
i=1,2.
Then
\theta_{i}>0
for all
i
as
A(x_{0})
is positive definite. Moreover, (
5.28
) and (
5.31
) imply

0\leq\theta_{1}\lambda_{1}+\theta_{2}\lambda_{2}.

This and
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}>0
imply the intermediate claim.

Intermediate claim 2.

\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}\geq 0
. Suppose for a contradiction that
\lambda_{1}<0
. Intermediate claim 1 yields that then
\lambda_{2}>0
. Now (
5.26
) yields

(5.32)

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle\geq\left|\left\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\tfrac{1}{2}<\lambda_{1}w_{1}^{2}+\lambda_{2}w_{2}^{2}<\tfrac{3}{2}\right\}\right|=\left|\left\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\tfrac{1}{2}+(-\lambda_{1})w_{1}^{2}<\lambda_{2}w_{2}^{2}<\tfrac{3}{2}+(-\lambda_{1})w_{1}^{2}\right\}\right|

(5.33)

\displaystyle\geq\left|\left\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{\lambda_{2}}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}+(-\lambda_{1})w_{1}^{2}\right)}<w_{2}<\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{\lambda_{2}}\left(\tfrac{3}{2}+(-\lambda_{1})w_{1}^{2}\right)}\right\}\right|.

Using Tonelli’s Theorem the above Lebesgue measure can be computed with the following expression

(5.34)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{\lambda_{2}}\left(\tfrac{3}{2}+(-\lambda_{1})w_{1}^{2}\right)}-\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{\lambda_{2}}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}+(-\lambda_{1})w_{1}^{2}\right)}\right)\;\mathrm{d}w_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda_{2}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\tfrac{3}{2}+(-\lambda_{1})w_{1}^{2}}+\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}+(-\lambda_{1})w_{1}^{2}}}\;\mathrm{d}w_{1}

(5.35)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{\lambda_{2}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\tfrac{3}{2}+(-\lambda_{1})w_{1}^{2}}}\;\mathrm{d}w_{1}=\infty,

leading to the contradiction
C\geq\infty
in (
5.33
). Intermediate claim 2 is shown.

Intermediate claim 3.
If
\lambda_{1}=0
then
\lambda_{2}=0
. Indeed, suppose for a contradiction that
\lambda_{1}=0
and
\lambda_{2}\neq 0
. Due to intermediate claim
2
one infers that
\lambda_{2}>0
. From (
5.26
) we infer that

(5.36)

C\geq\left|\left\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\tfrac{1}{2}<\lambda_{2}w_{2}^{2}<\tfrac{3}{2}\right\}\right|\geq\left|\mathbb{R}\times\left(\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2\lambda_{2}}},\sqrt{\tfrac{3}{2\lambda_{2}}}\right)\right|=\infty.

Intermediate claim 3 is shown. Intermediate claim 2 and 3 lead to the conclusion that either
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}=0
(i.e.
B=0
) or
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}>0
(i.e.
B
positive definite).
∎

5.1.1.
Analysis of the remaining blow-up profiles

The argument in Lemma
5.3
exposes two possible blow-up profiles for
u
around a good point of the singular nodal set in the sense of Definition
5.1
. The first one is a strictly convex quadratic function
z\mapsto z^{T}Bz
for some positive definite matrix
B
and the second one is a flat blow-up
z\mapsto 0.
We will rule out both blow-up profiles in the following.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
u,x_{0},B
be as in Lemma
5.3
. Then
B
is not positive definite.

Proof.

Suppose for a contradiction that
B
is positive definite.
Recall that Lemma
4.15
yields an open convex neighborhood
\Omega^{\prime}\subset\subset\Omega
of
x_{0}
and
M>0
such that
x\mapsto u(x)+\tfrac{1}{2}M|x-x_{0}|^{2}
is convex. Thereupon, Lemma
4.16
and the fact that
u(x_{0})=0,\nabla u(x_{0})=0
yield the following Taylor-type expansion in a neighborhood of
x_{0}

(5.37)

u(x)=\tfrac{1}{2}(x-x_{0})^{T}B(x-x_{0})+o(|x-x_{0}|^{2}).

From this and the fact that
B
is positive definite we conclude that
x_{0}
is a strict local minimum of
u
and
u
grows quadratically away from
x_{0}
. In particular, there exists
r_{0}>0
and
\beta>0
such that

(5.38)

0<u(x)<\beta|x-x_{0}|^{2}\qquad\textrm{for all $x\in B_{r_{0}}(x_{0})\setminus\{x_{0}\}$.}

Fix
r\in(0,r_{0})
. Choose some
\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(B_{r}(x_{0}))
such that
-1\leq\psi\leq 0
and
\psi\equiv-1
on
B_{\frac{r}{2}}(x_{0})
. Since
u\in\mathcal{A}(u_{0})
is a minimizer we find that for each
\varepsilon>0
one has
\mathcal{E}(u)\leq\mathcal{E}(u+\varepsilon\psi)
and thus we obtain

(5.39)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(u)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{E}(u+\varepsilon\psi)=\mathcal{E}(u)+2\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}LuL\psi\;\mathrm{d}x+\varepsilon^{2}\int_{\Omega}[L\psi]^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x+|\{u+\varepsilon\psi>0\}|-|\{u>0\}|

(5.40)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}(u)+2\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}(-\psi)\;\mathrm{d}\mu+\varepsilon^{2}\int_{\Omega}[L\psi]^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x-|\{x\in B_{r}(x_{0}):0\leq u(x)<\varepsilon(-\psi(x))\}|.

Since
\chi_{B_{\frac{r}{2}}(x_{0})}\leq(-\psi)\leq\chi_{B_{r}(x_{0})}
the second summand can be estimated by

(5.41)

2\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}(-\psi)\;\mathrm{d}\mu\leq 2\varepsilon\mu(B_{r}(x_{0})).

Moreover,

(5.42)

\displaystyle|\{x\in B_{r}(x_{0}):0<u(x)<\varepsilon(-\psi(x))\}|

\displaystyle\geq|\{x\in B_{\frac{r}{2}}(x_{0}):0<u(x)<\varepsilon(-\psi(x))\}|,

(5.43)

\displaystyle=|\{x\in B_{\frac{r}{2}}(x_{0}):0<u(x)<\varepsilon\}|=|\{x\in B_{\frac{r}{2}}(x_{0}):u(x)<\varepsilon\}|,

where we used in the last step that by (
5.38
)
u>0
on
B_{r}(x_{0})\setminus\{x_{0}\}
(and
\{x_{0}\}
is a Lebesgue null set). Using the upper estimate of (
5.38
) in (
5.42
) we find

(5.44)

\displaystyle|\{x\in B_{r}(x_{0}):0<u(x)<\varepsilon(-\psi(x))\}|

\displaystyle\geq|\{x\in B_{\frac{r}{2}}(x_{0}):\beta|x-x_{0}|^{2}<\varepsilon\}|

(5.45)

\displaystyle=|B_{\frac{r}{2}}(x_{0})\cap B_{\sqrt{\frac{\varepsilon}{\beta}}}(x_{0})|=\pi\min\{(\tfrac{r}{2})^{2},\tfrac{\varepsilon}{\beta}\}.

Plugging this and (
5.41
) in (
5.40
) we obtain

(5.46)

\mathcal{E}(u)\leq\mathcal{E}(u)+2\varepsilon\mu(B_{r}(x_{0}))+\varepsilon^{2}\int_{\Omega}[L\psi]^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x-\pi\min\{(\tfrac{r}{2})^{2},\tfrac{\varepsilon}{\beta}\}

Choosing
\varepsilon>0
so small that
\tfrac{\varepsilon}{\beta}<(\tfrac{r}{2})^{2}
we can rearrange and find

(5.47)

\frac{\pi\varepsilon}{\beta}-2\varepsilon\mu(B_{r}(x_{0}))\leq\varepsilon^{2}\int_{\Omega}[L\psi]^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x.

Dividing by
\varepsilon
and letting
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
we find

\frac{\pi}{\beta}-2\mu(B_{r}(x_{0}))\leq 0.

Letting now
r\downarrow 0
we obtain
\frac{\pi}{\beta}-2\mu(\{x_{0}\})\leq 0.

Since by Lemma
5.2

x_{0}
is not an atom of
\mu
we have
\mu(\{x_{0}\})=0
and hence the inequality above produces a contradiction.
∎

The remaining possibility of a flat blow-up profile can be excluded by means of a maximum principle which applies for
v=Lu
.

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
u,x_{0},B
be as in Lemma
5.3
. Then
B\neq 0
.

Proof.

Suppose for a contradiction that
B=0
. Recalling the definition of
B
in Lemma
5.3
we obtain that

(5.48)

\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\fint_{B_{r}(x_{0})}D^{2}u(x)\;\mathrm{d}x=0.

Now define
v:=Lu
and compute for almost every
x\in\Omega

(5.49)

-v(x)=\mathrm{div}(A(x)\nabla u(x))=A(x):D^{2}u(x)+\mathbf{div}(A)(x)\cdot\nabla u(x).

Using that
A
is smooth and
D^{2}u\in L^{2}(\Omega)
has a Lebesgue point at
x_{0}
(cf. Lemma
5.2
(ii)) we obtain with Lemma
A.2
that

(5.50)

(-v)^{*}(x_{0})=\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\fint_{B_{r}(x_{0})}(-v)(x)\;\mathrm{d}x=A(x_{0}):0+\mathbf{div}(A)(x_{0})\cdot\nabla u(x_{0})=0.

In particular,
(-v)^{*}(x_{0})=0
. Notice that by Lemma
4.12

(-v)^{*}(x)\geq 0
for all
x\in\Omega.
Therefore
(-v)^{*}
attains a minimum at
x_{0}
. Moreover, notice that distibutionally there holds
L(-v)=-L^{2}u=\mu
, i.e.
L(-v)\geq 0
weakly. Hence we can apply the strong maximum principle (Corollary
2.5
) to conclude that
(-v)^{*}\equiv 0
in
\Omega
. By definition of
v
we infer that that
Lu=0
a.e. in
\Omega
. Thus,
u\in W^{2,2}(\Omega)
solves

(5.51)

\begin{cases}Lu=0&\textrm{in $\Omega$}\\
u=u_{0}&\textrm{on $\partial\Omega$}\end{cases}.

The maximum principle for
L
yields

u(x)\geq\inf_{\overline{\Omega}}u_{0}>0
for all
x\in\Omega
.
A contradiction, as
u(x_{0})=0.

∎

Corollary 5.6
.

The singular nodal set does not contain any good points in the sense of Definition
5.1
.

Proof.

Assume that
x_{0}\in\Omega
is a good point of the singular nodal set. Lemma
5.3
implies that for all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{2}

(5.52)

\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\frac{u(x_{0}+rz)}{r^{2}}=\tfrac{1}{2}z^{T}Bz,

for some
B\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}_{sym}
, which is either positive definite or
B=0
. The first case is ruled out by Lemma
5.4
and the second case is ruled out by Lemma
5.5
.
∎

5.2.
Bad points

Next we will also rule out the existence of bad points in the singular nodal set, i.e. points
x_{0}
where
u(x_{0})=0,\nabla u(x_{0})=0
and
(-v)^{*}(x_{0})=\infty
. To this end we first show a regularity result for isolated points on
\{u\leq 0\}
, which we will use later on.

Lemma 5.7
.

Let
u\in\mathcal{A}(u_{0})
be a minimizer and
x_{0}
be an isolated point of
\{u\leq 0\}
. Then
u
is smooth in a neighborhood of
x_{0}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
x_{0}
is an isolated point of
\{u\leq 0\}
, i.e.
u(x_{0})\leq 0
and there exists some
r>0
such that
u(x)>0
for all
x\in B_{r}(x_{0})\setminus\{x_{0}\}
. Notice that by possibly shrinking
r
we can ensure that
B_{2r}(x_{0})\subset\Omega
. Observe that by continuity one has
u(x_{0})=0
. Notice that then

(5.53)

|\{u>0\}\cap B_{r}(x_{0})|=|B_{r}(x_{0})\setminus\{x_{0}\}|=|B_{r}(x_{0})|-|\{x_{0}\}|=|B_{r}(x_{0})|.

Next let
w\in W^{2,2}(B_{r}(x_{0}))
be such that

(5.54)

\int_{B_{r}(x_{0})}(Lw)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x=\inf\left\{\int_{B_{r}(x_{0})}(Lv)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x\;\Big|\;v\in W^{2,2}(B_{r}(x_{0}))\;\textrm{s.t.}\;v_{\mid_{\partial B_{r}(x_{0})}}=u_{\mid_{\partial B_{r}(x_{0})}},\nabla v_{\mid_{\partial B_{r}(x_{0})}}=\nabla u_{\mid_{\partial B_{r}(x_{0})}}\right\}.

Now define
\tilde{u}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
via

(5.55)

\tilde{u}(x):=\begin{cases}u(x)&x\in\Omega\setminus B_{r}(x_{0})\\
w(x)&x\in B_{r}(x_{0})\end{cases}.

One readily checks that
\tilde{u}\in\mathcal{A}(u_{0})
. Moreover,

(5.56)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{E}(\tilde{u})-\mathcal{E}(u)=\int_{B_{r}(x_{0})}(Lw)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x+|\{w>0\}\cap B_{r}(x_{0})|-\int_{B_{r}(x_{0})}(Lu)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x-|\{u>0\}\cap B_{r}(x_{0})|.

By (
5.54
) one has

(5.57)

\int_{B_{r}(x_{0})}(Lw)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x\leq\int_{B_{r}(x_{0})}(Lu)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x.

Moreover, one concludes from (
5.53
) that

|\{w>0\}\cap B_{r}(x_{0})|\leq|B_{r}(x_{0})|=|\{u>0\}\cap B_{r}(x_{0})|.

Using this and (
5.57
) in (
5.56
) we find
0\leq\mathcal{E}(\tilde{u})-\mathcal{E}(u)\leq 0
. This implies that all the inequalities we have used, in particular (
5.57
), must hold with equality. Thus,

(5.58)

\int_{B_{r}(x_{0})}(Lu)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{B_{r}(x_{0})}(Lw)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x.

We infer that
u|_{B_{r}(x_{0})}
is the minimizer of the variational problem in (
5.54
). One readily checks by standard Euler-Lagrange methods that minimizers of this problem satisfy

(5.59)

\int_{B_{r}(x_{0})}LuL\varphi\;\mathrm{d}x=0\qquad\textrm{for all }\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(B_{r}(x_{0})).

By
[
20
, Theorem 6.33]
we conclude
u\in C^{\infty}(B_{r}(x_{0}))
and the claim follows.
∎

The subsequent lemma shows that all bad points are isolated.

Lemma 5.8
.

Let
u\in\mathcal{A}(u_{0})
be a minimizer and
x_{0}\in\Omega
be such that
u(x_{0})=0,\nabla u(x_{0})=0
and
(-v)^{*}(x_{0})=\infty
. Then
x_{0}
is an isolated point of
\{u\leq 0\}.

Proof.

Recall from Lemma
4.14
that for almost every
x\in\Omega
there holds

(5.60)

D^{2}u(x)=(-v)(x)A(x)^{-1}+\int_{\Omega}K(x,y)\;\mathrm{d}\mu(y)+H(x)

for some locally bounded and Borel measurable
K:\Omega\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}_{sym}
which is smooth on
\Omega\times\Omega\setminus\{(x,x):x\in\Omega\}
and
G\in C^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}_{sym})
. Let
r_{0}>0
be such that
\overline{B_{r_{0}}(x_{0})}\subset\Omega
.
Due to the fact that
|K|
and
|H|
are bounded on
\overline{B_{r_{0}}(x_{0})}
we conclude that there exists
C>0
and
\theta>0
such that for any
y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
x\in\overline{B_{r_{0}}(x_{0})}

(5.61)

y^{T}D^{2}u(x)y\geq(-v)(x)y^{T}A(x)^{-1}y-C|y|^{2}=(-v)(x)\theta|y|^{2}-C|y|^{2}=\left(-\theta v(x)-C\right)|y|^{2}.

By Lemma
2.4

(-v)^{*}(x_{0})=\infty
yields

\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\inf_{B_{r}(x_{0})}v^{*}=\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\inf_{B_{cr}(x_{0})}v^{*}\geq\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\inf_{D_{r}(x_{0})}v^{*}=\infty.

Hence there exists
r_{1}\in(0,r_{0})
such that
\inf_{B_{r_{1}}(x_{0})}v^{*}>\frac{2C}{\theta}
. This and (
5.61
) yield that for a.e.
x\in B_{r_{1}}(x_{0})

(5.62)

y^{T}D^{2}u(x)y\geq C|y|^{2}.

In particular,
D^{2}u(x)-\frac{C}{2}I
is positive definite for a.e.
x\in B_{r_{1}}(x_{0}).
This implies convexity of
x\mapsto u(x)-\frac{C}{4}|x-x_{0}|^{2}
. Since
\nabla\left(u(x)-\frac{C}{4}|x-x_{0}|^{2}\right)\big|_{x=x_{0}}=\nabla u(x_{0})=0
,
x_{0}
is a critical point of
x\mapsto u(x)-\frac{C}{4}|x-x_{0}|^{2}
. Since critical points of convex functions are always global minima we find

(5.63)

u(x)-\frac{C}{4}|x-x_{0}|^{2}\geq u(x_{0})-\frac{C}{4}|x_{0}-x_{0}|^{2}=0\qquad\textrm{for all $x\in B_{r_{1}}(x_{0})$.}

We conclude that
u(x)\geq\frac{C}{4}|x-x_{0}|^{2}
for all
x\in B_{r_{1}}(x_{0})
. This implies that
u>0
on
B_{r_{1}}(x_{0})\setminus\{x_{0}\}
and hence
x_{0}
is an isolated point of
\{u\leq 0\}.

∎

As a corollary we obtain the nonexistence of bad points in the singular nodal set

Corollary 5.9
.

The singular nodal set does not contain any bad points in the sense of Definition
5.1
.

Proof.

Assume that there exists some bad point
x_{0}
in the singular nodal set, i.e.
u(x_{0})=0,\nabla u(x_{0})=0
and
(-v)^{*}(x_{0})=\infty
. By Lemma
5.8
,
x_{0}
is an isolated point of
\{u\leq 0\}.

Furthermore, by Lemma
5.7
,
u
is smooth in a neighborhood of
x_{0}
. This also implies that
v=Lu
is smooth in a neighborhood of
x_{0}
. Due to this fact we obtain

(5.64)

\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\fint_{D_{r}(x_{0})}(-v)(x)\;\mathrm{d}x=(-v)(x_{0})\in\mathbb{R}.

On the contrary,
x_{0}
is a bad point, i.e.

\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\fint_{D_{r}(x_{0})}(-v)(x)\;\mathrm{d}x=(-v)^{*}(x_{0})=\infty.

A contradiction.
∎

As a consequence of this and Corollary
5.6
we obtain

Lemma 5.10
.

Let
u\in\mathcal{A}(u_{0})
be a minimizer. Then for each
x_{0}\in\{u=0\}
one has
\nabla u(x_{0})\neq 0
(i.e.
x_{0}
is a regular point). In particular,
\{u=0\}
is a compact
C^{1,\alpha}
-submanifold of
\Omega
.

Proof.

That each point in the nodal set is regular follows from Corollary
5.9
and Corollary
5.6
. The submanifold property follows then immediately from the fact that
u\in C^{1,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
, cf. Lemma
4.11
.
∎

6.
A measure-valued equation

Next we use
inner variation techniques
to characterize the measure
\mu
in Lemma
4.10
explicitly. To this end we first derive a lemma about an expansion of
Lu
under domain variation.

Lemma 6.1

(Domain variation)
.

Let
\psi=(\psi_{1},\psi_{2})\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Define for
t\in\mathbb{R}
the function
\varphi_{t}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
by
\varphi_{t}(x):=x+t\psi(x)
. Then there exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that for all
t\in(-\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{0})
one has
\varphi_{t}(\Omega)=\Omega
and
\varphi_{t}
is a diffeomorphism of
\Omega
. Moreover, one has for any
u\in W^{2,2}(\Omega)
the expansion

(6.1)

L(u\circ\varphi_{t})=(Lu)\circ\varphi_{t}+R_{t}+tX_{t}+t^{2}Y_{t}\qquad\textrm{as $t\rightarrow 0$}

with

(6.2)

\displaystyle-R_{t}

\displaystyle=(A-A\circ\varphi_{t}):D^{2}u\circ\varphi_{t}+(\mathbf{div}(A)-\mathbf{div}(A)\circ\varphi_{t})\cdot\nabla u\circ\varphi_{t},

(6.3)

\displaystyle-X_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}a_{ij}(2\partial_{i}\psi\cdot(\partial_{j}\nabla u)\circ\varphi_{t}+\partial^{2}_{ij}\psi\cdot\nabla u\circ\varphi_{t})+\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}(\partial_{j}a_{ij})\partial_{i}\psi\cdot\nabla u\circ\varphi_{t},

(6.4)

\displaystyle-Y_{t}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}a_{ij}\partial_{i}\psi\cdot(D^{2}u\circ\psi_{t})\partial_{j}\psi.

Furthermore, if
u\in W^{3,q}(\Omega)
for some
q\in[1,\infty)
then

(6.5)

X_{0}=L(\nabla u\cdot\psi)-\sum_{m=1}^{2}L(\partial_{m}u)\psi_{m}.

Proof.

The diffeomorphism property of
\varphi_{t}
for
|t|
small enough is trivial. It remains to show the expansion of
L(u\circ\varphi_{t})
. To this end, first compute

(6.6)

\displaystyle\partial_{i}(u\circ\varphi_{t})

\displaystyle=\partial_{i}\varphi_{t}\cdot\nabla u\circ\varphi_{t}=(e_{i}+t\partial_{i}\psi)\cdot\nabla u\circ\varphi_{t}=\partial_{i}u\circ\varphi_{t}+t\partial_{i}\psi\cdot\nabla u\circ\varphi_{t},

(6.7)

\displaystyle\partial^{2}_{ij}(u\circ\varphi_{t})

\displaystyle=\partial_{j}\varphi_{t}\cdot(D^{2}u\circ\varphi_{t})\partial_{i}\varphi_{t}+\partial^{2}_{ij}\varphi_{t}\nabla u\circ\varphi_{t}=(e_{j}+t\partial_{j}\psi)\cdot(D^{2}u\circ\varphi_{t})(e_{i}+t\partial_{i}\psi)+t\partial^{2}_{ij}\psi\cdot\nabla u\circ\varphi_{t}

(6.8)

\displaystyle=\partial^{2}_{ij}u\circ\varphi_{t}+t\partial_{j}\psi\cdot(\partial_{i}\nabla u)\circ\varphi_{t}+t\partial_{i}\psi\cdot(\partial_{j}\nabla u)\circ\varphi_{t}+t\partial^{2}_{ij}\psi\cdot\nabla u\circ\varphi_{t}+t^{2}\partial_{j}\psi\cdot(D^{2}u\circ\varphi_{t})\partial_{i}\psi.

Using this we obtain

(6.9)

\displaystyle-L(u\circ\varphi_{t})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{div}(A\nabla(u\circ\varphi_{t}))=A:D^{2}(u\circ\varphi_{t})+\mathbf{div}(A)\cdot\nabla(u\circ\varphi_{t})

(6.10)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}a_{ij}\partial^{2}_{ij}(u\circ\varphi_{t})+\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}\partial_{j}(a_{ij})\partial_{i}(u\circ\varphi_{t})

(6.11)

\displaystyle=A:D^{2}u\circ\varphi_{t}+\mathbf{div}(A)\cdot\nabla u\circ\varphi_{t}+t\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}a_{ij}\partial_{j}\psi\cdot\partial_{i}\nabla u\circ\varphi_{t}+t\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}a_{ij}\partial_{i}\psi\partial_{j}\nabla u\circ\varphi_{t}

(6.12)

\displaystyle\qquad+t\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}a_{ij}\partial^{2}_{ij}\psi\cdot\nabla u\circ\varphi_{t}+t\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}\partial_{j}(a_{ij})\partial_{i}\psi\cdot\nabla u\circ\varphi_{t}-t^{2}Y_{t}.

Since
a_{ij}=a_{ji}
we may exchange the role of
i
and
j
in
\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}a_{ij}\partial_{j}\psi\cdot\partial_{i}\nabla u\circ\varphi_{t}
and conclude

(6.13)

\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}a_{ij}\partial_{j}\psi\cdot\partial_{i}\nabla u\circ\varphi_{t}+\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}a_{ij}\partial_{i}\psi\cdot\partial_{j}\nabla u\circ\varphi_{t}=2\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}a_{ij}\partial_{j}\psi\cdot\partial_{i}\nabla u\circ\varphi_{t}.

Moreover,

(6.14)

A:D^{2}u\circ\varphi_{t}+\mathbf{div}(A)\cdot\nabla u\circ\varphi_{t}=-(Lu)\circ\varphi_{t}+(A-A\circ\varphi_{t}):D^{2}u\circ\varphi_{t}+(\mathbf{div}(A)-\mathbf{div}(A)\circ\varphi_{t})\cdot\nabla u\circ\varphi_{t}.

Using these computations in (
6.12
) we find

(6.15)

\displaystyle-L(u\circ\varphi_{t})=-(Lu)\circ\varphi_{t}+(A-A\circ\varphi_{t}):D^{2}u\circ\varphi_{t}+(\mathbf{div}(A)-\mathbf{div}(A)\circ\varphi_{t})\cdot\nabla u\circ\varphi_{t}

(6.16)

\displaystyle\qquad-t^{2}Y_{t}+t\left(2\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}a_{ij}\partial_{j}\psi\partial_{i}\nabla u\circ\varphi_{t}+\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}a_{ij}\partial^{2}_{ij}\psi\cdot\nabla u\circ\varphi_{t}+\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}\partial_{j}(a_{ij})\partial_{i}\psi\cdot\nabla u\circ\varphi_{t}\right)

(6.17)

\displaystyle=-(Lu)\circ\varphi_{t}-R_{t}-t^{2}Y_{t}-tX_{t}.

The claim follows by multiplying by
(-1)
. It remains to show formula (
6.5
) for
X_{0}
. First one readily checks that for
f,g\in W^{2,q}(\Omega)
one has

L(fg)=g(Lf)-2A\nabla f\cdot\nabla g+(Lg)f.

In particular,

(6.18)

L(\nabla u\cdot\psi)=L\left(\sum_{m=1}^{2}\partial_{m}u\psi^{m}\right)=\sum_{m=1}^{2}L(\partial_{m}u\psi^{m})=\sum_{m=1}^{2}L(\partial_{m}u)\psi_{m}-2A\nabla(\partial_{m}u)\cdot\psi_{m}+\partial_{m}uL(\psi_{m}).

Observe that

(6.19)

\displaystyle-\sum_{m=1}^{2}\partial_{m}uL\psi_{m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j,m=1}^{2}\partial_{m}u(a_{ij}\partial^{2}_{ij}\psi_{m}+\partial_{j}a_{ij}\partial_{i}\psi_{m})=\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}a_{ij}\partial^{2}_{ij}\psi\cdot\nabla u+\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}\partial_{j}a_{ij}\partial_{i}\psi\cdot\nabla u,

(6.20)

\displaystyle 2\sum_{m=1}^{2}A\nabla(\partial_{m}u)\cdot\nabla\psi_{m}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{i,j,m=1}^{2}a_{ij}\partial_{j}(\partial_{m}u)\partial_{i}\psi^{m}=2\sum_{i,j=1}^{2}a_{ij}\partial_{i}\psi\cdot\partial_{j}(\nabla u).

Evaluating (
6.3
) at
t=0
we see that the terms of the previous two equations appear and conclude

(6.21)

L(\nabla u\cdot\psi)=\sum_{m=1}^{2}L(\partial_{m}u)\psi_{m}+X_{0}.

The claim follows.
∎

Based on this we can derive an equation representing the measure
\mu
obtained in Lemma
4.10
.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
u\in\mathcal{A}(u_{0})
be a minimizer and
\mu
be as in Lemma
4.10
. Then for all
\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2})
one has

(6.22)

-\int_{\Omega}\chi_{\{u>0\}}\,\mathrm{div}(\psi)\;\mathrm{d}x=2\int_{\Omega}\psi\cdot\nabla u\;\mathrm{d}\mu.

Proof.

Let
\psi=(\psi_{1},\psi_{2})\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Define for
t\in\mathbb{R}
the function
\varphi_{t}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
by
\varphi_{t}(x):=x+t\psi(x)
. As discussed in Lemma
6.1
, there exists
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that for all
t\in(-\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{0})
one has
\varphi_{t}(\Omega)=\Omega
and
\varphi_{t}
is a diffeomorphism of
\Omega
. Define
u_{t}:=u\circ\varphi_{t}.

From Lemma
6.1
follows that

(6.23)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(Lu_{t})^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}[(Lu)\circ\varphi_{t}]^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x+2\int_{\Omega}(Lu)\circ\varphi_{t}R_{t}\;\mathrm{d}x+2t\int_{\Omega}(Lu)\circ\varphi_{t}X_{t}\;\mathrm{d}x+\int_{\Omega}R_{t}^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x+O(t^{2}),

Now recall that
A
is smooth, which is why we can expand

(6.24)

A-A\circ\varphi_{t}=-t\sum_{k=1}^{2}\partial_{k}A\psi_{k}+O(t^{2}),\quad\mathbf{div}(A)-\mathbf{div}(A)\circ\varphi_{t}=-t\partial_{k}\mathbf{div}(A)\psi_{k}+O(t^{2})=-t\mathbf{div}(\partial_{k}A)\psi_{k}+O(t^{2}).

Using this in the definition of
R_{t}
(cf. (
6.2
)) we find

\int_{\Omega}R_{t}^{2}=O(t^{2})
and

(6.25)

2\int_{\Omega}(Lu)\circ\varphi_{t}R_{t}\;\mathrm{d}x=2t\int_{\Omega}Lu\circ\varphi_{t}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{2}\psi_{k}\left(\partial_{k}A:D^{2}u\circ\varphi_{t}+\mathbf{div}(\partial_{k}A)\cdot\nabla u\circ\varphi_{t}\right)\;\right)\mathrm{d}x+O(t^{2}).

This implies

(6.26)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(Lu_{t})^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}[(Lu)\circ\varphi_{t}]^{2}+2t\int_{\Omega}Lu\circ\varphi_{t}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{2}\psi_{k}\left(\partial_{k}A:D^{2}u\circ\varphi_{t}+\mathbf{div}(\partial_{k}A)\cdot\nabla u\circ\varphi_{t}\right)\right)\;\mathrm{d}x

(6.27)

\displaystyle\qquad+2t\int_{\Omega}(Lu)\circ\varphi_{t}X_{t}\;\mathrm{d}x+O(t^{2}).

Note next that for
t\in(-\varepsilon_{0},\varepsilon_{0})

(6.28)

\int_{\Omega}[(Lu)\circ\varphi_{t}]^{2}\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}(Lu)^{2}\mathrm{det}(D\varphi_{t}^{-1})\;\mathrm{d}x

and

(6.29)

\mathrm{det}(D\varphi_{t}^{-1})=\frac{1}{\mathrm{det}(D\varphi_{t}\circ\varphi_{t}^{-1})}=\frac{1}{1+t\mathrm{div}(\psi)\circ\varphi_{t}^{-1}+O(t^{2})}=1-t\mathrm{div}(\psi)+O(t^{2}).

Therefore we have

(6.30)

\int_{\Omega}[(Lu)\circ\varphi_{t}]^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}(Lu)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x-t\int_{\Omega}(Lu)^{2}\mathrm{div}(\psi)\;\mathrm{d}x+O(t^{2}).

Using this in (
6.27
) yields

(6.31)

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(Lu_{t})^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}(Lu)^{2}\;\mathrm{d}x-t\int_{\Omega}(Lu)^{2}\mathrm{div}(\psi)\;\mathrm{d}x

(6.32)

\displaystyle\quad+2t\int_{\Omega}Lu\circ\varphi_{t}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{2}\psi_{k}\left(\partial_{k}A:D^{2}u\circ\varphi_{t}+\mathbf{div}(\partial_{k}A)\cdot\nabla u\circ\varphi_{t}\right)\right)\;\mathrm{d}x+2t\int_{\Omega}(Lu)\circ\varphi_{t}X_{t}\;\mathrm{d}x+O(t^{2}).

Moreover, we can use (
6.29
) to compute

(6.33)

|\{u_{t}>0\}|=|\varphi_{t}^{-1}(\{u>0\})|=\int_{\Omega}\chi_{\{u>0\}}\mathrm{det}(D\varphi_{t}^{-1})\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}\chi_{\{u>0\}}\;\mathrm{d}x-t\int_{\Omega}\chi_{\{u>0\}}\mathrm{div}(\psi)\;\mathrm{d}x+O(t^{2}).

This and (
6.32
) imply

(6.34)

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(u_{t})=\mathcal{E}(u)-t\int_{\Omega}\chi_{\{u>0\}}\mathrm{div}(\psi)\;\mathrm{d}x-t\int_{\Omega}(Lu)^{2}\mathrm{div}(\psi)\;\mathrm{d}x

(6.35)

\displaystyle\qquad+2t\int_{\Omega}Lu\circ\varphi_{t}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{2}\psi_{k}\left(\partial_{k}A:D^{2}u\circ\varphi_{t}+\mathbf{div}(\partial_{k}A)\cdot\nabla u\circ\varphi_{t}\right)\right)\;\mathrm{d}x+2t\int_{\Omega}(Lu)\circ\varphi_{t}X_{t}\;\mathrm{d}x+O(t^{2}).

Given this we can compute the derivative of
t\mapsto\mathcal{E}(u_{t})
at
t=0
, i.e.

(6.36)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{E}(u_{t})\Big|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}\chi_{\{u>0\}}\mathrm{div}(\psi)\;\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}(Lu)^{2}\;\mathrm{div}(\psi)\;\mathrm{d}x

(6.37)

\displaystyle\qquad+2\int_{\Omega}Lu\left(\sum_{k=1}^{2}\psi_{k}(\partial_{k}A:D^{2}u+\mathbf{div}(\partial_{k}A)\cdot\nabla u)\right)\;\mathrm{d}x+2\int_{\Omega}LuX_{0}\;\mathrm{d}x.

Using that by Lemma
4.11
and (
6.5
)
X_{0}=L(\nabla u\cdot\psi)-\sum_{k=1}^{2}L(\partial_{k}u)\psi_{k}
we find

(6.38)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{E}(u_{t})\Big|_{t=0}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}\chi_{\{u>0\}}\mathrm{div}(\psi)\;\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}(Lu)^{2}\;\mathrm{div}(\psi)\;\mathrm{d}x

(6.39)

\displaystyle\quad+2\int_{\Omega}Lu\left(\sum_{k=1}^{2}\psi_{k}(\partial_{k}A:D^{2}u+\mathbf{div}(\partial_{k}A)\cdot\nabla u-L(\partial_{k}u))\right)\;\mathrm{d}x+2\int_{\Omega}LuL(\psi\cdot\nabla u)\;\mathrm{d}x.

Next observe that for all
k=1,2
one has (again since
u\in W^{3,q}(\Omega)
by Lemma
4.11
)

(6.40)

\displaystyle\partial_{k}(Lu)=-\partial_{k}A:D^{2}u-\mathbf{div}(\partial_{k}A)\cdot\nabla u+L(\partial_{k}u).

Thus,

\sum_{k=1}^{2}\psi_{k}(\partial_{k}A:D^{2}u+\mathbf{div}(\partial_{k}A)\cdot\nabla u-L(\partial_{k}u))=-\psi\cdot\nabla Lu.

Hence, (
6.39
) yields

(6.41)

\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{E}(u_{t})\Big|_{t=0}=\int_{\Omega}\chi_{\{u>0\}}\mathrm{div}(\psi)\;\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}(Lu)^{2}\;\mathrm{div}(\psi)\;\mathrm{d}x-2\int_{\Omega}Lu\psi\cdot\nabla Lu+2\int_{\Omega}.LuL(\psi\cdot\nabla u)

Using Lemma
4.10
and Lemma
4.11
(needed due to the fact that
\psi\cdot\nabla u\in W^{2,q}(\Omega)\cap W_{0}^{1,q}(\Omega)
only for
q<2
) we find

(6.42)

\int_{\Omega}LuL(\psi\cdot\nabla u)\;\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\Omega}\psi\cdot\nabla u\;\mathrm{d}\mu.

This and the fact that
\mathrm{div}((Lu)^{2}\psi)=(Lu)^{2}\mathrm{div}(\psi)+\nabla(Lu)^{2}\cdot\psi=(Lu)^{2}\mathrm{div}(\psi)+2Lu\nabla Lu\cdot\psi
yield in (
6.41
)

(6.43)

\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{E}(u_{t})\Big|_{t=0}=-\int_{\Omega}\chi_{\{u>0\}}\mathrm{div}(\psi)\;\mathrm{d}x-\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{div}((Lu)^{2}\psi)\;\mathrm{d}x-2\int_{\Omega}\psi\cdot\nabla u\;\mathrm{d}\mu.

Since
u=u\circ\varphi_{0}=u_{0}
is a minimizer of
\mathcal{E}
we find
\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{E}(u_{t})\big|_{t=0}=0
. Moreover, since
v=Lu\in W_{0}^{1,q}(\Omega)
by Lemma
4.8
we have
(Lu)^{2}\psi\in W_{0}^{1,1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2})
so that

(6.44)

\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{div}((Lu)^{2}\psi)\;\mathrm{d}x=0.

All in all we have

(6.45)

0=-\int_{\Omega}\chi_{\{u>0\}}\mathrm{div}(\psi)\;\mathrm{d}x-2\int_{\Omega}\psi\cdot\nabla u\;\mathrm{d}\mu.

The claim follows.
∎

We obtain further information by analyzing the left hand side of (
6.22
) with the Gauss divergence theorem. In the following
\partial^{*}\{u>0\}
refers to the
reduced boundary
of
\{u>0\}
, cf.
[

, Chapter 5]
.

Lemma 6.3
.

Each component of
\{u>0\}
has
C^{1}
-boundary. Moreover,
\partial\{u>0\}=\partial^{*}\{u>0\}=\partial\Omega\cup\{u=0\}
with outward pointing unit normal
\nu=-\frac{\nabla u}{|\nabla u|}
. Furthermore,

(6.46)

-\int_{\Omega}\chi_{\{u>0\}}\mathrm{div}(\psi)\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\{u=0\}}\frac{1}{|\nabla u|}\psi\cdot\nabla u\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}\qquad\textrm{for all }\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2}).

The proof that
\partial\{u>0\}=\partial^{*}\{u>0\}=\partial\Omega\cup\{u=0\}
would be a word by word copy of

As a consequence of the previous two lemmas we infer

Lemma 6.4
.

Let
u\in\mathcal{A}(u_{0})
be a minimizer and
\mu
be as in Lemma
4.10
. Then for all
\psi\in C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})
one has

(6.47)

2\int_{\Omega}\psi\cdot\nabla u\;\mathrm{d}\mu=\int_{\{u=0\}}\frac{1}{|\nabla u|}\psi\cdot\nabla u\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}

Proof.

If we additionally assume
\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
this is a direct consequence of Lemma
6.2
and Lemma
6.3
.
We show first that (
6.47
) holds for all
\psi\in C_{0}^{0}(\Omega):=\{\varphi\in C^{0}(\overline{\Omega}):\mathrm{spt}(\varphi)\;\textrm{is compactly contained in $\Omega$}\}
.
For
\psi\in C_{0}^{0}(\Omega)
one can choose
(\psi_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\subset C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
such that
\psi_{j}\rightarrow\psi
uniformly on
\overline{\Omega}
. In particular,

(6.48)

2\int_{\Omega}\psi_{j}\cdot\nabla u\;\mathrm{d}\mu=\int_{\{u=0\}}\frac{1}{|\nabla u|}\psi_{j}\cdot\nabla u\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}\qquad\textrm{for all $j\in\mathbb{N}$.}

Since
\sup_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}|\psi_{j}(x)\cdot\nabla u(x)-\psi(x)\cdot\nabla u(x)|\rightarrow 0
and
\sup_{x\in\{u=0\}}|\psi_{j}(x)\cdot\tfrac{\nabla u}{|\nabla u|}(x)-\psi(x)\cdot\tfrac{\nabla u}{|\nabla u|}(x)|\rightarrow 0
as
j\rightarrow\infty

we can pass to the limit in (
6.48
) and obtain

(6.49)

2\int_{\Omega}\psi\cdot\nabla u\;\mathrm{d}\mu=\int_{\{u=0\}}\frac{1}{|\nabla u|}\psi\cdot\nabla u\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}.

Hence (
6.47
) holds on
C_{0}^{0}(\Omega)
. Next let
\psi\in C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})
be arbitrary. Recall that by Lemma
4.5

\{u=0\}
is a compact subset of
\Omega
. Therefore one can choose
\eta\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
such that
\eta\equiv 1
on
\{u=0\}
. Writing
\psi=\psi\eta+\psi(1-\eta)

4.10

\mathrm{spt}(\mu)\subset\{u=0\}

(6.50)

2\int_{\Omega}\psi\cdot\nabla u\;\mathrm{d}\mu=2\int_{\Omega}(\psi\eta)\cdot\nabla u\;\mathrm{d}\mu=\int_{\{u=0\}}\frac{1}{|\nabla u|}(\psi\eta)\cdot\nabla u\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}=\int_{\{u=0\}}\frac{1}{|\nabla u|}\psi\cdot\nabla u\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}.

∎

.

Let

\mu(A)=\int_{A\cap\{u=0\}}\frac{1}{2|\nabla u|}\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}\qquad\textrm{ for all Borel sets $A\subset\Omega$.}

\eta\in C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})

\int_{\Omega}\eta\;\mathrm{d}\mu=\int_{\{u=0\}}\frac{1}{2|\nabla u|}\eta\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}.

6.1.
Optimal regularity

Remark 6.6
.

Let
u\in\mathcal{A}(u_{0})
be a minimizer. From Lemma
4.10
and Lemma
6.5
follows that

(6.56)

\int_{\Omega}LuL\varphi\;\mathrm{d}x=-\int_{\Omega}\varphi\;\mathrm{d}\mu=-\int_{\{u=0\}}\frac{1}{2|\nabla u|}\varphi\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{H}^{1}\qquad\textrm{for all $\varphi\in W^{2,2}(\Omega)\cap W_{0}^{1,2}(\Omega)$}.

Notice that we have used
W^{2,2}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})
. Next we study the regularity of solutions of this equation.

Lemma 6.7
.

Suppose that
u\in\mathcal{A}(u_{0})
is a minimizer. Then
u\in W^{3,p}(\Omega)
for all
p\in[1,\infty)
. In particular, for any
\beta\in(0,1)
one has
u\in C^{2,\beta}(\overline{\Omega})
and
\{u=0\}
is a
C^{2,\beta}
-submanifold.

Proof.

Choose again an open neighborhood
\Omega^{\prime}
of
\{u=0\}
such that
\nabla u\neq 0
on
\Omega^{\prime}
and choose
\eta\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega^{\prime})
such that
\eta=1
on
\{u=0\}
.
Define
\Gamma:=\{u=0\}
and
Q:=\frac{1}{2|\nabla u|}\eta
and
v:=Lu
. Then
v

Lemma 6.8
.

Suppose that

be a minimizer. Then
\{u<0\}
has finitely many connected components

,

. Moreover, the sets

Proof.

Since
\{u=0\}
is compact, it has only finitely many connected components
S_{1},...,S_{N}
. Now fix

By the Jordan-Brouwer separation theorem (applicable since

is a compact

-hypersurface, cf.
[
34

)

divides

into two domains, one of which is bounded and lies inside
\Omega
. We call this domain

and observe that
\partial G_{i}=S_{i}
. Since

on
\partial G_{i}
and
Lu\leq 0
(cf. Lemma
4.12
) we infer from the (classical) maximum principle that either
u=0
on
G_{i}
or
u<0
on
G_{i}
. Since the first possibility is ruled out by the fact that

on
G_{i}
.

Intermediate claim

are pairwise disjoint.
To this end note that for

since

by construction and

due to the fact that

on
G_{j}
and
u=0
on
S_{i}
. In particular, we have

is an open and closed subset of

is connected, we infer

It remains to show that

\{u<0\}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}G_{i}.

To this end assume that there exists some

such that

.
Define

. Then

and there exists some

such that

.
In particular

for some

must touch

\frac{d}{dt}u(x_{0}+ty)\big|_{t=R}=\nabla u(x_{0}+Ry)\cdot y=\pm|\nabla u(x_{0}+Ry)|\neq 0

Hence

must change sign at

for all

we infer that there exists

such that

for all

for all

Lemma A.1
.

(A.2)

One readily checks (due to finiteness of
\mu

is smooth and the support
\mathrm{spt}(g_{n})
lies in
\mathrm{spt}(\mu)+\overline{B_{\frac{1}{n}}(0)}
, i.e. it is compactly contained in
\Omega
for
n
large enough. One readily checks by Fubini’s theorem that for
f\in C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})

(A.3)

\int_{\Omega}g_{n}f\;\mathrm{d}x=\int_{\Omega}f\chi_{\Omega}*\varphi_{n}\;\mathrm{d}\mu.

Now
f\chi_{\Omega}*\varphi_{n}
converges to
f
on uniformly on compact subsets of
\Omega
. From this and the fact that
\mathrm{spt}(\mu)\subset\Omega
is compact we infer (
A.1
).
∎

Lemma A.2
.

Suppose that
f\in L^{1}(\Omega)
and
g\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
. Let
x_{0}\in\Omega
be a Lebesgue point of
f
and of
g
and

(A.4)

f^{*}(x_{0})=\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\fint_{B_{r}(x_{0})}f(x)\;\mathrm{d}x\in\mathbb{R},\qquad g^{*}(x_{0})=\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\fint_{B_{r}(x_{0})}g(x)\;\mathrm{d}x\in\mathbb{R}.

Then,
x_{0}
is a Lebesgue point of
fg\in L^{1}(\Omega)
and

(A.5)

\lim_{r\rightarrow 0}\fint_{B_{r}(x_{0})}f(x)g(x)\;\mathrm{d}x=f^{*}(x_{0})g^{*}(x_{0}).

Proof.

Let
f,g,f^{*}(x_{0})g^{*}(x_{0})
be as in the statement. Then

(A.6)

\displaystyle\fint_{B_{r}(x_{0})}|f(x)g(x)-f^{*}(x_{0})g^{*}(x_{0})|\;\mathrm{d}x=\fint_{B_{r}(x_{0})}|(f(x)-f^{*}(x_{0}))g(x)+f^{*}(x_{0})(g(x)-g^{*}(x_{0}))|\;\mathrm{d}x

(A.7)

\displaystyle\leq\fint_{B_{r}(x_{0})}|f(x)-f^{*}(x_{0})|\;\lVert g\rVert_{L^{\infty}}+|f^{*}(x_{0})|\;|g(x)-g^{*}(x_{0}))|\;\mathrm{d}x

(A.8)

\displaystyle=\lVert g\rVert_{L^{\infty}}\fint_{B_{r}(x_{0})}|f(x)-f^{*}(x_{0})|\;\mathrm{d}x+|f^{*}(x_{0})|\fint_{B_{r}(x_{0})}|g(x)-g^{*}(x_{0})|\;\mathrm{d}x\rightarrow 0\quad(r\rightarrow 0).

The claim follows.
∎
