A computational model for short-range van der Waals interactions between beams and shells
A. Borković, M. H. Gfrerer, R. A. Sauer

Abstract




Abstract

We consider potential-based interactions between beams (or fibers) and shells (or membranes) using a coarse-grained approach with focus on van der Waals attraction and steric repulsion. The involved 6D integral over volumes of a beam and a shell is split into a 5D analytical pre-integration over the beam’s cross section and a surrogate plate tangential to the closest point on the shell, and the remaining 1D numerical integration along the beam’s axis. This general inverse-power interaction potential is added to the potential energies of the Bernoulli-Euler beam and the Kirchhoff-Love shell. The total potential energy is spatially discretized using isogeometric finite elements, and the nonlinear weak form of quasi-static equilibrium is solved using the continuation method. We provide error estimates and convergence analysis, together with two intriguing numerical examples. The developed approach provides excellent balance between accuracy and efficiency for small separations.

Keywords
: interaction potential; van der Waals attraction; coarse-grained approach; contact mechanics; fiber-membrane interaction; beam-shell contact

Introduction

Intermolecular forces are the underlying cause of many observable phenomena, such as friction, adhesion, surface tension, and mechanical contact. Among them, van der Waals (vdW) attraction is arguably the most universal because it exists between uncharged molecules due to transient fluctuations in charge distributions. This force remains significant across both short and long distances, causing not only the cohesion of condensed matter but also an adhesion between macroscopic bodies. Modeling vdW interactions is challenging, as it is influenced by multiple contributing factors, retardation effects, and exhibits non-additive behavior
[
39
]
. A widely used approximation for estimating vdW forces between bodies is the pairwise summation method, which treats the total interaction as the sum of all individual point-point interactions. The reliability of this approximation depends strongly on the molecular geometry and type
[
53
,
2
]
. In this study, we assume that the pairwise summation approach is valid and that the vdW potential can be described using an inverse sixth-power law w.r.t. the point-pair distance.

Numerical modeling of intermolecular interactions is commonly performed using molecular dynamics or Monte Carlo simulations
[
29
]
. An alternative to these approaches is a
coarse-grained
model based on the homogenization and coarse-graining of the underlying molecular system. This method strikes a favorable balance between accuracy and computational efficiency
[
3
,
46
,
47
,
18
]
by combining the underlying physics of molecular interactions with the efficiency of continuum-based contact formulations
[
57
]
. In this framework, interactions are classified as either occurring within a single body (intrasolid) or between separate bodies (intersolid), allowing the interaction potential between two bodies to be expressed as a function of the gap vector. However, solving the boundary value problem for potential-based interactions at relevant time and length scales remains computationally intensive, largely due to the steep gradients of configuration-dependent interaction forces at small separations.

The interaction of biological membranes with fiber-like macromolecules plays a key role in processes such as adhesion of elastin fibers and smooth muscle cells

Fiber-membrane interaction also arises in the production of nanofiber membranes, which are widely used as biomimetic and mechanically stable surface coatings

The obtained results are in partial agreement with those of molecular dynamics simulations. Finally, it should be noted that vdW interactions also play an important role in the electroless plating process that allows the combination of multiple heterogeneous structures and provides a many possibilities for 4D printed structures

This brief literature overview suggests that vdW interactions between fibers and membranes are a critical topic across various fields of engineering and mechanics, which is yet to be fully understood. Aiming to advance the state-of-the-art in this area, the present study proposes an accurate and efficient computational model for vdW fiber-membrane interactions, based on the well-established finite element (FE) methodology. The literature on classical mechanical contact between beams and shells is scarce
[

,

]
, and the focus is mostly on beam-shell coupling
[

]
. Recently, the coarse-grained model has been incorporated into structural beam and shell theories
[

,

,

, offering a favorable compromise between accuracy and computational efficiency. A section-section approach, introduced in
[

,

[

,

,

,

[

[

,

]
. Motivated by these studies, we propose a computational formulation suited for short-range vdW fiber-membrane (or beam-shell) interactions. The approach is based on the analytical pre-integration of the interaction potential between the beam cross section and a surrogate plate representing the shell. For a decisive range of small separations, this approach promises good balance between accuracy and efficiency by: (i) capturing the main property of interaction force – scaling w.r.t. the gap, and (ii) reducing the 6D to 1D numerical integration. The resulting interaction potential is added to the total potential energy of a system consisting of a Bernoulli-Euler beam and a Kirchhoff-Love shell. The total potential is spatially discretized and solved using the isogeometric FE approach
[
27
]
.

The modeling of intermolecular interactions between fibers and membranes is inherently complex and therefore simplified here by the following assumptions:

•

The total beam-shell interaction equals the pairwise summation (integration) of point-pair interactions.

•

The point-point interaction potential is modeled as an inverse-power law of the point-pair distance.

•

Only one beam and one shell are considered, and many-body effects are neglected.

•

Any influences of a surrounding medium and retardation effects are neglected.

•

There is no redistribution of particles or charges inside the bodies; that is, we are dealing with dielectric or nonconducting materials.

•

The density distributions of particles and physical constants over the interacting bodies are homogeneous at the initial configuration.

•

Fibers have circular cross sections.

The main contributions of the present paper are: (i) development of a new approach for beam-shell vdW interactions using section-surrogate plate concept, (ii) derivation of the computational formulation that enables accurate and efficient modeling of highly nonlinear quasi-static interactions between deformable 3D fibers and membranes, and (iii) thorough numerical analysis of two fundamental and non-trivial examples concerning peeling of an adhering fiber from a membrane and bending of a membrane by an adhering fiber. To the best of our knowledge, no experimental or numerical examples suitable for direct comparison are available in the existing literature. These two examples are carefully selected to represent fundamental cases of fiber-membrane interaction and to serve as benchmarks for future studies. Furthermore, we provide all details on the variations and linearizations required for reproducibility, and we develop a novel disk-sphere vdW law that enables the computation of reference solutions.

2
Potential-based beam-shell interactions

The concept of point-pair interaction potentials and their integration over the interacting beam and shell using the coarse-grained method are revisited in this section. The basic idea of the section-surrogate plate approach is introduced, and the general form of the equation of motion is presented.

2.1
Coarse-graining of the beam-shell interaction potential

Let us consider particles
i
and
j
that interact via a potential field modeled as an inverse power law w.r.t. their distance
r_{ij}
. A point-point interaction potential of
m^{\text{th}}
order,
{\Pi}^{m}_{\operatorname{P-P}}
, is the energy required to separate these particles from
r_{ij}
to infinite separation, i.e.,

\displaystyle{\Pi}_{\operatorname{P-P}}^{m}(r_{ij})=k_{m}\,r_{ij}^{-m},

(1)

where
k_{m}
is a physical constant. The interaction force is obtained as the gradient of this potential w.r.t. the distance; it acts on both particles with the same intensity but in the opposite direction. By the pairwise summation concept
[
29
]
, the volume interaction potential between a beam (B) and a shell (S) becomes

\displaystyle\Pi_{\operatorname{{B-S}_{PW}}}^{m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\mathrm{B}}\sum_{j\in\mathrm{S}}\Pi_{\operatorname{{P-P}}}^{m}(r_{ij}).

(2)

Let us approximate the pairwise summation as a volume integral over both bodies by using the coarse-graining procedure, introduced in
[
46
]
,

\displaystyle\Pi_{\operatorname{{B-S}_{PW}}}^{m}\approx\Pi_{\operatorname{B-S}}^{m}=\int_{{v}_{\mathrm{B}}}\int_{{v}_{\mathrm{S}}}\beta_{\mathrm{B}}\,\beta_{\mathrm{S}}\,{\Pi}_{\operatorname{P-P}}^{m}\differential{v_\mathrm{S}}\differential{v_\mathrm{B}},

(3)

where
v_{k}

(k=\mathrm{B},\mathrm{S})
are volumes, while
\beta_{k}
are particle volume densities; both at the current configuration. Since we assume that the interacting property of an elementary volume is conserved during deformation, we have
\beta_{k\mathrm{0}}\differential{V}_{k}=\beta_{k}\differential{v_k}
.
This fact allows us to calculate an interaction potential at the current configuration by integrating over the reference volume
V_{k}
, using the reference particle densities
\beta_{k0}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\Pi_{\operatorname{B-S}}^{m}

\displaystyle=\int_{{v}_{\mathrm{B}}}\int_{{v}_{\mathrm{S}}}\beta_{\mathrm{B}}\,\beta_{\mathrm{S}}\,k_{m}\,\norm{\tilde{\ve{x}}_\mathrm{B} - \tilde{\ve{x}}_\mathrm{S}}^{-m}\differential{v_\mathrm{S}}\differential{v_\mathrm{B}}

(4)

\displaystyle=\int_{V_{\mathrm{B}}}\int_{V_{\mathrm{S}}}\beta_{\mathrm{B0}}\,\beta_{\mathrm{S0}}\,k_{m}\,\norm{\ve{x}_\mathrm{B} - \ve{x}_\mathrm{S}}^{-m}\differential{V_\mathrm{S}}\differential{V_\mathrm{B}},

where
\tilde{\bm{x}}_{k}
and
\bm{x}_{k}
are the current positions of elementary volumes w.r.t. spatial and material coordinates, respectively. Calculating this integral for practical time and space resolutions is computationally expensive, which motivates us to employ a more efficient model.

For interactions between a beam and a shell, one approach would be to consider a pre-integration over a beam’s cross-section and a shell’s normal fiber. This would leave us with 3D numerical integration, which is still quite a demanding computational task. In the next subsection, we propose a decomposition of integral (
4
) into 5D analytical pre-integration and 1D numerical integration.

2.2
Beam-shell interaction via disk-surrogate plate interaction

Following the section-surrogate cylinder idea from
[
25
]
, we pursue an efficient computational approach for beam-shell interaction. Let us consider a beam and a shell, as illustrated in Fig.
1
.

Figure 1
:
Beam-shell interaction. The interaction between a beam’s disk-shaped cross section C and a shell is approximated as an interaction between disk C and an infinite plate, tangential to the shell at the closest point. The hat accent designates a quantity evaluated at the closest point.

By integrating the potential over the area of the beam’s cross section,
A_{\mathrm{B}}
, the interaction reduces to a 4D integral: one along the length of the beam axis,
L_{\mathrm{B}}
, and three over the shell volume. Recall that the interaction potential is the inverse-power law w.r.t. gap (
1
). If the gap between the beam and the shell is small, the contribution to the interaction potential comes only from very small region. This observation enables the approximation of the disk-shell interaction by a disk-surrogate plate interaction, where the surrogate plate is an infinite-area plate tangent to the shell at the point closest to the disk and has the same thickness as the shell. The main advantage of this approach is that we can analytically integrate over the volume of the surrogate plate,
V_{\mathrm{SP}}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\Pi_{\operatorname{B-S}}^{m}

\displaystyle=\int_{L_{\mathrm{B}}}\int_{A_{\mathrm{B}}}\int_{V_{\mathrm{S}}}\beta_{\mathrm{B0}}\,\beta_{\mathrm{S0}}\,k_{m}\,\norm{\ve{x}_\mathrm{B} - \ve{x}_\mathrm{S}}^{-m}\differential{V_\mathrm{S}}\differential{A_\mathrm{B}}\differential{s_\mathrm{B}}\approx\int_{L_{\mathrm{B}}}\beta_{\mathrm{B0}}\,\beta_{\mathrm{S0}}\,k_{m}\Pi_{\operatorname{D-SP}}^{m}\differential{s_\mathrm{B}},

(5)

\displaystyle\Pi_{\operatorname{D-SP}}^{m}

\displaystyle:=\int_{A_{\mathrm{B}}}\int_{V_{\mathrm{SP}}}\,\norm{\ve{x}_\mathrm{B} - \ve{x}_\mathrm{S}}^{-m}\differential{V_\mathrm{SP}}\differential{A_\mathrm{B}},

where
\Pi_{\operatorname{D-SP}}^{m}
is the disk-surrogate plate interaction law. In this way, the beam-shell interaction potential is approximated with a 1D integral over the beam length. We will refer to this approach as the surrogate beam-shell model (SBS). An exact analytical integration of the interaction potential over a disk and an infinite plate has been obtained in
[
7
]
for an arbitrary exponent
m
.

Let us emphasize that the closest point-pairs dominate the interaction when bodies are in close proximity, and the exponent of the potential is
m>3
. This short-range effect leads to steep gradients of the interacting force, reflecting a complex equilibrium state between repulsive and attractive forces. Addressing the high sensitivity of interaction forces at the interface is crucial for accurate simulations but also computationally demanding. On the other hand, description of long- and moderate-range effects is less involved since the distributions of interaction forces are smoothed. Although these effects can be important for some problems
[
6
]
, they are not considered in this paper.

2.3
Lennard-Jones potential and equation of motion

To model adhesion due to vdW forces realistically, repulsive effects must also be included. The repulsion develops between bodies in close vicinity due to overlapping electron clouds. This repulsion effect is observed as
contact
from a macroscopic point of view. vdW and steric interactions exist for practically all bodies, making them one of the most common forces in nature. By modeling the repulsive steric potential with an inverse-power law with
m=12
, and adding it to the vdW potential, we obtain the well-known Lennard-Jones (LJ) potential between two particles with distance
r

\displaystyle\Pi_{\operatorname{P-P}}^{\operatorname{LJ}}(r)=4\epsilon\left[\left(\frac{\sigma}{r}\right)^{12}-\left(\frac{\sigma}{r}\right)^{6}\right]=k_{6}\,r^{-6}+k_{12}\,r^{-12},

(6)

where
\sigma
is the distance at which the potential is zero, while
\epsilon
is the minimum value of the potential. In our numerical analysis, we exclusively consider LJ beam-shell interactions that stem from the integration of the point-point LJ potential (
6
).

The computational modeling of potential-based interactions between bodies requires solving an appropriate (initial) boundary value problem. The strong form of this problem consists of the equation of motion (balance of linear momentum), boundary and initial conditions, and a constitutive relation. To derive the weak form, we define the total potential energy of a system involving elastic bodies,
\Pi_{\mathrm{tot}}
. If we neglect inertial effects and consider interaction between a beam and a shell, this energy consists of the strain energy,
\Pi_{\mathrm{str}}=\Pi_{\mathrm{B,str}}+\Pi_{\mathrm{S,str}}
, the work of external forces,
\Pi_{\mathrm{ext}}=\Pi_{\mathrm{B,ext}}+\Pi_{\mathrm{S,ext}}
, and the interaction potential,
\Pi_{\operatorname{B-S}}^{m}
. By the principle of stationary total potential energy, the weak form of the boundary value problem follows

\displaystyle\delta\Pi_{\mathrm{tot}}(\bm{x}_{\mathrm{B}},\bm{x}_{\mathrm{S}})=\delta\Pi_{\mathrm{str}}-\delta\Pi_{\mathrm{ext}}+\delta\Pi_{\operatorname{B-S}}^{m}=0,

(7)

and it must hold for all admissible variations of the configuration of beam and shell. Potential energies of beams and a shells are derived and varied in the next section, while the interaction potential energy is derived in Section
4
and varied in Section
5
.

The obtained equilibrium equation (
7
) is spatially discretized using the isogeometric approach
[
8
]
, details of which are omitted for brevity. The resulting system of algebraic equations is highly nonlinear, and a continuation approach is employed in which the forcing term is incremented, allowing the equilibrium configurations to be determined at a finite number of quasi-time steps. The Newton-Raphson method is employed to solve the system of equations within each increment, with details of the required linearizations provided in Appendices
A
,
B
,
D
, and
E
.

3
Potential energy of beams and shells

For the sake of completeness, the beam and shell models that are employed in this work are briefly discussed. Since our goal is the efficient modeling of interactions between thin beams and shells, the Bernoulli-Euler (BE) beam and Kirchhoff-Love (KL) shell models are employed. Both of these theories reduce general deformable 3D continua via plausible assumptions. By assuming that one dimension of the 3D body is much smaller than the other two, we obtain the mechanical model of a shell. If we further assume that the fibers perpendicular to the midsurface of the shell are rigid and perpendicular to the deformed midsurface, we obtain the KL shell theory. This mechanical model allows the 3D body to be described solely by the position of the shell’s midsurface.

On the other hand, by assuming that one dimension of the 3D body is much smaller than the other two, we obtain a mechanical model of a beam. By assuming that the cross sections are rigid and perpendicular to the deformed beam axis, the BE beam model is found. Due to additional dimensional reduction, in comparison with the KL shell, the BE beam model requires one rotational DOF (twist angle) besides the position of the axis.

To describe the deformation of beams and shells, we introduce a reference configuration that coincides with the initial, undeformed state. The kinematic quantities for both the reference and current configurations can be obtained in an analogous manner. We will distinguish the former by using capital letters. In the following, Greek indices take the values
\alpha=1,2
for the shell, and
\alpha=2,3
for the beam model, while Latin indices take the values
i=1,2,3
.

\tilde{\Pi}_{\mathrm{S,str}}:=\frac{1}{2}\bar{\bm{E}}\colon\mathbb{\bar{C}}\colon\bar{\bm{E}}=\frac{1}{2}\bar{\bm{E}}\colon\bar{\bm{S}},\quad\bar{\bm{S}}=\lambda\tr(\iveq{E}{}{})\bm{I}+2\mu\bar{\bm{E}},

\mu
are Lamé constants. Furthermore, we assume that both the plane stress and the plane strain conditions are satisfied, which allows us to express components of the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor as

\bar{S}^{\alpha\beta}=2\mu\big(\bar{G}^{\alpha\nu}\bar{G}^{\beta\gamma}+\frac{\nu}{1-\nu}\bar{G}^{\alpha\beta}\bar{G}^{\nu\gamma}\big)\bar{E}_{\nu\gamma}=\bar{C}^{\alpha\beta\nu\gamma}\bar{E}_{\nu\gamma}.

(26)

The variation of the strain energy

\delta{\Pi}_{\mathrm{S,str}}:=\int_{V_{\mathrm{S}}}\delta\tilde{\Pi}_{\mathrm{S,str}}\differential{\bar V}_{\mathrm{S}}=\int_{A_{\mathrm{S}}}\int_{-h/2}^{h/2}\bar{S}^{\alpha\beta}\,\delta\bar{E}_{\alpha\beta}\differential{\zeta}\bar{J}_{\text{S}}\differential{\theta^1}\differential{\theta^2}

(27)

can be analytically integrated along the shell’s thickness by neglecting higher order terms w.r.t.
\zeta
, i.e.,

\delta\Pi_{\mathrm{S,str}}=\int_{A_{\mathrm{S}}}\big({N}^{\alpha\beta}\delta E_{\alpha\beta}+{M}^{\alpha\beta}\delta K_{\alpha\beta}\big)J_{\text{S}}\differential{\theta^1}\differential{\theta^2},

(28)

where

\displaystyle{N}^{\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=hC^{\alpha\beta\nu\gamma}{E}_{\nu\gamma},\quad{M}^{\alpha\beta}=\frac{h^{3}}{12}C^{\alpha\beta\nu\gamma}{K}_{\nu\gamma},

(29)

are section forces and section moments.

For the variation of the shell’s strain energy in (
28
), we require the variation of the membrane strains

\delta E_{\alpha\beta}=\frac{1}{2}(\bm{g}_{\alpha}\cdot\delta\bm{u}_{\text{S},\beta}+\bm{g}_{\beta}\cdot\delta\bm{u}_{\text{S},\alpha}),

(30)

and the variation of the change of curvatures

\delta K_{\alpha\beta}=\delta b_{\alpha\beta}=\delta(\bm{g}_{\alpha,\beta}\cdot\bm{n})=\bm{n}\cdot\left(\delta\bm{u}_{\text{S},\alpha\beta}-{\Gamma}^{\gamma}_{\alpha\beta}\cdot\delta\bm{u}_{\text{S},\gamma}\right),

(31)

where we have used
\delta\bm{n}=-(\bm{n}\cdot\bm{g}_{\alpha})\bm{g}^{\alpha}
, while
{\Gamma}^{\gamma}_{\alpha\beta}=\bm{g}_{\alpha,\beta}\cdot\bm{g}^{\gamma}
are Christoffel symbols of the second kind. Linearization of the variation of the shell’s strain energy is given in Appendix
A
.

3.2.1
Metric of the beam axis

In the reference configuration, the beam axis is a continuous space curve
\bm{R}_{\text{B}}(\xi)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
with the tangential basis vector
\bm{A}_{1}(\xi)=\partial\bm{R}_{\mathrm{B}}/\partial\xi
, while
\xi
is a parametric convective coordinate, Fig.
3
.

Figure 3
:
Schematic representation of the beam model in reference and current configurations.

At each point along the beam axis, we define an orthonormal basis
\{\bm{T},\bm{A}_{2},\bm{A}_{3}\}
where
\bm{T}=\bm{A}_{1}/\norm{\ve{A}_1}
while
\bm{A}_{\alpha}
are aligned with the principal axes of the beam cross sections. Vectors
\bm{A}_{\alpha}
, often referred to as
directors
, can be defined in several ways, either by the Frenet-Serret frame, Bishop frame, or some other algorithm.

The position vector of the current configuration is
\bm{r}_{\mathrm{B}}=\bm{R}_{\mathrm{B}}+\bm{u}_{\mathrm{B}}
, where
\bm{u}_{\mathrm{B}}
is the displacement vector. The tangent in the current configuration is

\displaystyle\bm{t}=\frac{\partial\bm{r}^{\text{}}_{\text{B}}}{\partial s}=\frac{\partial\bm{r}^{\text{}}_{\text{B}}}{\partial\xi}\frac{\partial{\xi}}{\partial s}=\bm{r}_{\mathrm{B},1}\xi_{,s}=\bm{a}_{1}/j_{\mathrm{B}},\quad j_{\mathrm{B}}=\sqrt{a}=\norm{\iv{a}{}{1}}=\sqrt{\bm{a}_{1}\cdot\bm{a}_{1}},

(32)

where
j_{\mathrm{B}}
is the Jacobian of the coordinate transformation between physical (arc length) and parametric convective coordinate,
\differential s=j_{\mathrm{B}}\differential\xi
.

Directors in the current configuration are found by rotating the directors from the reference configuration as
\bm{a}_{\alpha}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\bm{A}_{\alpha}
, where
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
is a rotation tensor, i.e.,

\boldsymbol{\Lambda}:\mathbb{R}^{3}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{3},\;\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\mathrm{T}}=\bm{I},\;\det\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=1.

(33)

There are many ways to parametrize the rotation tensor, one of them being via the rotation vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}
,
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}=\mathrm{exp}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\times})
,
where
(\cdot)_{\times}
represents the skew-symmetric tensor of the vector
(\cdot)
.
Since we assume rigid cross sections that remain orthogonal to the beam axis, the current triad
\left\{\bm{t},\bm{a}_{2},\bm{a}_{3}\right\}
is orthonormal and uniquely defined by the position of the beam axis and a single scalar rotation parameter.

Let us consider the so-called smallest rotation (SR) algorithm, which consists of two steps. First, we rotate the
\bm{A}_{\alpha}
vectors into the plane with normal
\bm{t}
by the smallest angle, and obtain vectors
\bm{a}_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{SR}}
, i.e.,

\bm{a}_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{SR}}=\bm{A}_{\alpha}-\frac{\bm{A}_{\alpha}\cdot\bm{t}}{t_{T}}\left(\bm{t}+\bm{T}\right),\quad t_{T}:=1+\bm{T}\cdot\bm{t},\quad\alpha=2,3.

(34)

Second, vectors
\bm{a}_{\alpha}^{\mathrm{SR}}
are rotated by an angle
\varphi
in the current cross-sectional plane

\begin{bmatrix}\bm{a}_{2}\\
\bm{a}_{3}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\cos\varphi&\sin\varphi\\
-\sin\varphi&\cos\varphi\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{a}_{2}^{\mathrm{SR}}\\
\bm{a}_{3}^{\mathrm{SR}}\end{bmatrix}.

(35)

The SR algorithm is accurate and straightforward but has two limitations: (i) singularity for
\bm{T}\cdot\bm{t}
, and (ii) loss of objectivity for sparse FE meshes. The singularity issue is easily addressed with an incremental update of rotations, implemented here. Moreover, resolving the interaction force field in envisioned applications requires dense FE meshes, which alleviates the objectivity issue.

Covariant and contravariant components of the metric of the beam axis are

\bm{a}_{i}\cdot\bm{a}^{j}=\delta_{i}^{j},\quad a_{ij}=\bm{a}_{i}\cdot\bm{a}_{j},\quad a^{ij}=\bm{a}^{i}\cdot\bm{a}^{j},\quad[a^{ij}]=[a_{ij}]^{-1},\quad\det[a_{ij}]=a=\bm{a}_{1}\cdot\bm{a}_{1},

(36)

while the derivatives of the basis vectors are

\bm{a}_{i,1}=\Gamma^{k}_{i1}\bm{a}_{k},\quad\begin{bmatrix}\bm{a}_{1,1}\\
\bm{a}_{2,1}\\
\bm{a}_{3,1}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\Gamma&a{k}_{3}&-a{k}_{2}\\
-{k}_{3}&0&{k}_{1}\\
{k}_{2}&-{k}_{1}&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bm{a}_{1}\\
\bm{a}_{2}\\
\bm{a}_{3}\end{bmatrix},

(37)

where
{k}_{i}
are the components of the curvature vector with respect to the reciprocal basis
\bm{a}^{i}
.
{k}_{1}
represents the torsion of the cross section, while
{k}_{\alpha}
are components of the curvature of the beam axis,

{k}_{1}=\bm{g}_{2,1}\cdot\bm{g}_{3}=\bm{g}^{\text{SR}}_{2,1}\cdot\bm{g}^{\text{SR}}_{3}+\varphi_{,1},\quad{k}_{2}=-\bm{a}_{1,1}\cdot\bm{a}_{3}/a,\quad{k}_{3}=\bm{g}_{1,1}\cdot\bm{g}_{2}/a.

(38)

The components of the curvature vector are identical w.r.t. both the reference and current bases, analogous to the shell model, see Eqs. (
15
) and (
19
).

3.2.2
Metric of an equidistant line

The position vector and basis vectors at an equidistant line are

\displaystyle\bar{\bm{r}}_{\mathrm{B}}

\displaystyle=\bm{r}+\eta\bm{a}_{2}+\varsigma\bm{a}_{3},

(39)

\displaystyle\bar{\bm{a}}_{1}

\displaystyle=\bar{\bm{r}}_{\mathrm{B},1}=\bm{a}_{1}+\eta\bm{a}_{2,1}+\varsigma\bm{a}_{3,1}=a_{0}\bm{a}_{1}-\varsigma k_{1}\bm{a}_{2}+\eta k_{1}\bm{a}_{3},\quad\bar{\bm{a}}_{\alpha}=\bm{a}_{\alpha},

where
a_{0}=1-\eta k_{3}+\varsigma k_{2}
is the
shifter
. The metric of an equidistant line is

\displaystyle\bar{a}_{ij}

\displaystyle:=\bar{\bm{a}}_{i}\cdot\bar{\bm{a}}_{j},\quad\det[\bar{a}_{ij}]=\bar{a}=a_{0}^{2}\;a,\quad\bar{a}_{11}=\bar{\bm{a}}_{1}\cdot\bar{\bm{a}}_{1}\approx(1-2\eta k_{3}+2\varsigma k_{2})a,

(40)

where we approximate by neglecting higher order terms w.r.t. cross-sectional coordinates. The contravariant basis vectors are

\displaystyle\bar{\bm{a}}_{i}\cdot\bar{\bm{a}}^{j}

\displaystyle=\delta_{i}^{j},\quad\bar{a}^{ij}=\bar{\bm{a}}^{i}\cdot\bar{\bm{a}}^{j},\quad[\bar{a}^{ij}]=[\bar{a}_{ij}]^{-1},

(41)

and the Jacobian of the coordinate transformation is

\displaystyle\differential{\bar s}

\displaystyle=\bar{j}_{\text{B}}\differential{\xi}=\sqrt{\bar{a}}\differential{\xi}.

(42)

Analogous to Eqs. (
36
), (
38
), and (
40
), we can calculate all required quantities in the reference configuration:
K_{1}
,
K_{\alpha}
,
A_{ij}
,
\bar{A}_{ij}
,
J_{\mathrm{B}}
, and
A
.

Due to BE assumptions, non-zero components of the Green-Lagrange strain tensor are
\epsilon_{11}
and
\epsilon_{1\alpha}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\bar{\epsilon}_{11}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\bar{a}_{11}-\bar{A}_{11})=\epsilon_{11}-\eta\kappa_{3}+\varsigma\kappa_{2},

(43)

\displaystyle\bar{\epsilon}_{12}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\bar{a}_{12}-\bar{A}_{12})=-\varsigma\kappa_{1},\quad\bar{\epsilon}_{13}=\frac{1}{2}(\bar{a}_{13}-\bar{A}_{13})=-\eta\kappa_{1},

\displaystyle\epsilon_{11}

\displaystyle:=a_{11}-A_{11},\quad\kappa_{1}:=k_{1}-K_{1},\quad\kappa_{\alpha}:=a(k_{\alpha}-K_{\alpha}),

where
\epsilon_{11}
is the axial strain of the beam axis,
\kappa_{1}
is the torsional curvature of the cross section, and
\kappa_{\alpha}
are the bending curvatures of the beam axis. These quantities represent
reference strains
of the BE beam.

3.2.3
Variation of the beam potential energy

To define components of the second Piola-Kirchhoff stress
\bar{S}^{ij}
, we employ the Saint Venant-Kirchhoff material model, impose conditions
\bar{S}^{22}=\bar{S}^{33}=0
, neglect higher-order terms w.r.t. cross-sectional coordinates, and set off-diagonal terms in the reciprocal metric to
0
, i.e.,

\bar{S}^{11}=E\bar{a}^{11}\bar{a}^{11}\bar{\epsilon}_{11}\approx\frac{E}{a^{2}}\bar{\epsilon}_{11},\;\bar{S}^{12}=2\mu\bar{a}^{11}\bar{a}^{22}\bar{\epsilon}_{12}\approx 2\frac{\mu}{a}\bar{\epsilon}_{12},\;\bar{S}^{13}=2\mu\bar{a}^{11}\bar{a}^{33}\bar{\epsilon}_{13}\approx 2\frac{\mu}{a}\bar{\epsilon}_{13},

(44)

where
E
is Young’s modulus. The variation of the strain energy

\delta{\Pi}_{\mathrm{B,str}}=\int_{V_{\mathrm{B}}}\bar{S}^{1j}\delta\bar{\epsilon}_{1j}\differential{\bar V}_{\mathrm{B}}=\int_{L_{\mathrm{B}}}\int_{A_{\mathrm{B}}}\bar{S}^{1j}\delta\bar{\epsilon}_{1j}\differential{\eta}\differential{\varsigma}\bar{J}_{\mathrm{B}}\differential{\xi}

(45)

can now be analytically integrated by assuming
\bar{J}_{\mathrm{B}}=J_{\mathrm{B}}
, i.e.,

\delta{\Pi}_{\mathrm{B,str}}=\int_{\xi}(N\delta\epsilon_{11}+M^{i}\delta\kappa_{i})J_{\mathrm{B}}\differential{\xi},

(46)

where section force and moments are

N:=\frac{EF}{a}\epsilon_{11},\quad M^{1}:=\frac{\mu I_{t}}{\sqrt{a}}\kappa_{1},\quad\quad M^{2}:=\frac{EI_{\varsigma\varsigma}}{a}\kappa_{2},\quad\quad M^{3}:=\frac{EI_{\eta\eta}}{a}\kappa_{3},

(47)

while cross-sectional geometric properties are

F:=\int_{A_{\mathrm{B}}}\differential{\eta}\differential{\varsigma},\;{I}_{\varsigma\varsigma}:=\int_{A_{\mathrm{B}}}\varsigma^{2}\differential{\eta}\differential{\varsigma},\;{I}_{\eta\eta}:=\int_{A_{\mathrm{B}}}\eta^{2}\differential{\eta}\differential{\varsigma},\;{I}_{t}:=\int_{A_{\mathrm{B}}}\left(\eta^{2}+\varsigma^{2}\right)\differential{\eta}\differential{\varsigma}.

(48)

To completely define the variation of the strain energy in (
46
), variations of the reference strains are required. The variation of the tangential basis vector is
\delta\bm{a}_{1}=\delta\bm{u}_{\mathrm{B},1}
, while the variation of the other two basis vectors,
\bm{a}_{\alpha}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\cdot\bm{A}_{\alpha}
, requires variation of the rotation tensor, i.e.,

\delta\bm{a}_{\alpha}=\delta\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\cdot\bm{A}_{\alpha}=\delta\bm{w}_{\times}\cdot\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\cdot\bm{A}_{\alpha}=\delta\bm{w}_{\times}\cdot\bm{a}_{\alpha}=\delta\bm{w}\times\bm{a}_{\alpha}.

(49)

where
\delta\bm{w}
represents a multiplicative variation of the rotation vector. Components of this vector can be determined from conditions
\delta\bm{w}\times\bm{t}=\delta\bm{t}
and
\delta\bm{w}\cdot\bm{t}=\delta\bm{a}_{2}^{\mathrm{SR}}\cdot\bm{a}_{3}^{\mathrm{SR}}+\delta\varphi
, i.e.,

\delta\bm{w}=(\delta\varphi-\frac{\bm{T}\cdot(\bm{t}\times\delta\bm{u}_{\mathrm{B,1}})}{\sqrt{a}\;t_{T}})\bm{t}+\frac{\bm{t}\times\delta\bm{u}_{\mathrm{B},1}}{\sqrt{a}}=\bm{t}\delta\varphi+\left(\bm{I}-\frac{\bm{t}\otimes\bm{T}}{t_{T}}\right)\frac{\bm{t}\times\delta\bm{u}_{\mathrm{B},1}}{\sqrt{a}},

(50)

and the variations of basis vectors
\bm{a}_{\alpha}
follow as

\displaystyle\delta\bm{a}_{2}

\displaystyle=\delta\bm{w}\times\bm{a}_{2}=\bm{a}_{3}\delta\varphi-\frac{1}{\sqrt{a}}\left[\bm{t}\otimes\bm{a}_{2}+\frac{\bm{a}_{3}\otimes(\bm{T}\times\bm{t})}{t_{T}}\right]\cdot\delta\bm{u}_{\mathrm{B},1},

(51)

\displaystyle\delta\bm{a}_{3}

\displaystyle=\delta\bm{w}\times\bm{a}_{3}=-\bm{a}_{2}\delta\varphi-\frac{1}{\sqrt{a}}\left[\bm{t}\otimes\bm{a}_{3}-\frac{\bm{a}_{2}\otimes(\bm{T}\times\bm{t})}{t_{T}}\right]\cdot\delta\bm{u}_{\mathrm{B},1}.

Finally, the variations of reference strains (
43
) can be found as

\displaystyle\delta\epsilon_{11}

\displaystyle=\bm{a}_{1}\cdot\delta\bm{u}_{\mathrm{B,1}},

(52)

\displaystyle\delta\kappa_{1}

\displaystyle=\delta(\bm{a}_{2,1}\cdot\bm{a}_{3})=\bm{a}_{3}\cdot\delta\bm{a}_{2,1}+\bm{a}_{2,1}\cdot\delta\bm{a}_{3},

\displaystyle\delta\kappa_{2}

\displaystyle=\delta(-\bm{a}_{1,1}\cdot\bm{a}_{3})=-\bm{a}_{3}\cdot\delta\bm{u}_{\mathrm{B},11}-\bm{a}_{1,1}\cdot\delta\bm{a}_{3}),

\displaystyle\delta\kappa_{3}

\displaystyle=\delta(\bm{a}_{1,1}\cdot\bm{a}_{2})=\bm{a}_{2}\cdot\delta\bm{u}_{\mathrm{B},11}+\bm{a}_{1,1}\cdot\delta\bm{a}_{2},

which, with a bit of long but straightforward calculation, can be written as

\displaystyle\delta\kappa_{1}

\displaystyle=\delta\varphi_{,1}+\frac{1}{a\,t_{T}}\left[\frac{(\bm{t}\cdot\bm{T})_{,1}(\bm{T}\times\bm{t})}{t_{T}}+\Gamma(\bm{T}\times\bm{t})-(\bm{T}\times\bm{t})_{,1}\right]\cdot\delta\bm{u}_{\mathrm{B},1},

(53)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{a}\left(K_{2}\bm{a}_{2}+K_{3}\bm{a}_{3}\right)\cdot\delta\bm{u}_{\mathrm{B},1}-\frac{\bm{T}\times\bm{t}}{a\,t_{T}}\cdot\delta\bm{u}_{\mathrm{B},11},

\displaystyle\delta\kappa_{2}

\displaystyle=(\Gamma\,\bm{a}_{3}-\frac{K_{3}(\bm{T}\times\bm{t})}{t_{T}})\cdot\delta\bm{u}_{\mathrm{B},1}-\bm{a}_{3}\cdot\delta\bm{u}_{\mathrm{B},11}+aK_{3}\,\delta\varphi,

\displaystyle\delta\kappa_{3}

\displaystyle=(-\Gamma\,\bm{a}_{2}+\frac{K_{2}(\bm{T}\times\bm{t})}{t_{T}})\cdot\delta\bm{u}_{\mathrm{B},1}+\bm{a}_{2}\cdot\delta\bm{u}_{\mathrm{B},11}-aK_{2}\,\delta\varphi.

Regarding the external forces, let us consider the work of the point moment,
\bm{M}
, since it is used in our numerical example. If the external moment acts at point
\xi_{c}
, the variation of the external work is

\delta\Pi_{\mathrm{ext}}=\bm{M}\cdot\delta\bm{w}=(\bm{M}\cdot\bm{t})\delta\varphi-(\bm{M}\cdot\bm{t})\frac{\bm{T}\times\bm{t}}{\sqrt{a}\;t_{T}}\cdot\delta\bm{u}_{\text{B},1}+\frac{\bm{M}\times\bm{t}}{\sqrt{a}}\cdot\delta\bm{u}_{\text{B},1},

(54)

where all quantities are evaluated at
\xi=\xi_{c}
. Linearizations of variations of all required quantities are provided in Appendix
B
.

5
Variation of the SBS potential

In this section, the D-PT potential is varied in the context of the SBS approach. While the D-HS and D-PT formulations have already benn derived in
[
7
]
, the SBS introduces a specific feature: the gap is determined from the unilateral closest-point condition, see Fig.
1
. For clarity, we will designate such a potential as the disk-surrogate plate (D-SPT) potential,
\phi
. This potential is a function of two quantities: (i) the distance between the beam’s axis and the shell’s midsurface,
d
, or the gap between outer surfaces,
g
, and (ii) the cosine of the angle between the disk and the surrogate plate,
\cos\alpha
, see Figs.
4
b and
10
. As already noted in Subsection
4.3
, the difference between approaches that employ
d
and
g
exists, but it is negligible. Moreover, employing the gap
g
as the variable implies finding the closest-point projection between the section’s outer perimeter and the shell, which complicates the formulation without a significant improvement in accuracy. Therefore, we consider the D-SPT potential as a function of the distance and the cosine of the angle, i.e.,
\phi=\phi(d,\cos\alpha)
.

5.1
Variation of the distance and the angle

In the following, the hat symbol marks a quantity evaluated at the closest-point projection of the section’s center onto a shell. The distance vector between the point on a beam axis,
\bm{r}^{\text{}}_{\text{B}}(\xi)
, and the corresponding closest point on a shell,
\bm{r}^{\text{}}_{\text{S}}(\hat{\theta}^{\alpha})
,

\bm{d}(\bm{r}^{\text{}}_{\text{B}}(\xi),\bm{r}^{\text{}}_{\text{S}}(\hat{\theta}^{\alpha}))=\hat{\bm{d}}=\bm{r}^{\text{}}_{\text{B}}(\xi)-\bm{r}_{\text{S}}(\hat{\theta}^{\alpha})=\bm{r}^{\text{}}_{\text{B}}-\hat{\bm{r}}^{\text{}}_{\text{S}},

(62)

is defined by the unilateral closest-point condition

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{d}}\cdot\hat{\bm{g}}_{\alpha}

\displaystyle=0,

(63)

where
\hat{\bm{g}}_{\alpha}
are tangential basis vectors of the shell midsurface at the closest point, see Fig.
1
. The shell normal is colinear with the distance vector at the closest point, i.e.,

\hat{\bm{g}}_{3}=\hat{\bm{n}}=\frac{\hat{\bm{d}}}{d},\quad d=\sqrt{\hat{\bm{d}}\cdot\hat{\bm{d}}}.

(64)

To find the variation of the potential, let us define the derivative w.r.t. the parametric coordinate at the closest point as

\frac{\partial(.)}{\partial\hat{\theta}^{\alpha}}:=\frac{\partial(.)}{\partial\theta^{\alpha}}\bigg\rvert_{\theta^{\alpha}=\hat{\theta}^{\alpha}}.

(65)

The variation of the distance is

\delta d=\hat{\bm{n}}\cdot\delta\hat{\bm{d}},

(66)

while the variation of the distance vector can be represented as

\displaystyle\delta\hat{\bm{d}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\hat{\bm{d}}}{\partial\bm{r}^{\text{}}_{\text{\text{B}}}}\cdot\delta\bm{r}^{\text{}}_{\text{B}}+\frac{\partial\hat{\bm{d}}}{\partial\hat{\bm{r}}^{\text{}}_{\text{\text{S}}}}\cdot\delta\hat{\bm{r}}^{\text{}}_{\text{\text{S}}}+\frac{\partial\hat{\bm{d}}}{\partial\hat{\theta}^{\alpha}}\;\delta\hat{\theta}^{\alpha}=\delta\bm{r}_{\text{B}}-\delta\hat{\bm{r}}_{\text{S}}-\hat{\bm{g}}_{\alpha}\;\delta\hat{\theta}^{\alpha},

(67)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\hat{\bm{d}}}{\partial\bm{r}^{\text{}}_{\text{\text{B}}}}

\displaystyle:=\bm{I},\quad\frac{\partial\hat{\bm{d}}}{\partial\hat{\bm{r}}^{\text{}}_{\text{\text{S}}}}:=-\bm{I},\quad\frac{\partial\hat{\bm{d}}}{\partial\hat{\theta}^{\alpha}}:=-\bm{\hat{g}}_{\alpha}.

By inserting Eq. (
67
) in Eq. (
66
), the variation of the distance becomes

\delta d=\hat{\bm{n}}\cdot(\delta\bm{r}_{\text{B}}-\delta\hat{\bm{r}}_{\text{S}}-\hat{\bm{g}}_{\alpha}\;\delta\hat{\theta}^{\alpha}).

(68)

Finally, due to orthogonality condition (
63
), we obtain

\delta d=\hat{\bm{n}}\cdot(\delta\bm{r}_{\text{B}}-\delta\hat{\bm{r}}_{\text{S}})=\hat{\bm{n}}\cdot(\delta\bm{u}_{\text{B}}-\delta\hat{\bm{u}}_{\text{S}}),

(69)

where
\delta\bm{r}_{\text{B}}=\delta\bm{u}_{\text{B}}
and
\delta\hat{\bm{r}}_{\text{S}}=\delta\hat{\bm{u}}_{\text{S}}
. A specific feature of the present formulation is that the distance depends not only on the positions of the interacting objects but also on the closest-point condition. In other words, it is not only the position of the shell that changes, but also the position of the closest point. Therefore, the variation w.r.t. the shell depends on the position of the shell and the coordinates
\hat{\theta}^{\alpha}
of the closest point. We must first vary quantities w.r.t. the configuration for a fixed
\hat{\theta}^{\alpha}
, and then w.r.t.
\hat{\theta}^{\alpha}
itself.

Regarding the angle between the cross section and the surrogate plate, its cosine is represented in
[
7
]
as a function of the cosine of its complementary angle, i.e.,

\cos\alpha=\sqrt{1-t_{n}^{2}},\quad t_{n}:=\bm{t}\cdot\hat{\bm{n}}=\cos\bar{\alpha},\quad\text{with}\quad\alpha+\bar{\alpha}=\pi/2.

(70)

Therefore, the variation of
\cos\alpha
is

\delta\cos\alpha=-\frac{t_{n}}{\cos\alpha}(\hat{\bm{n}}\cdot\delta\bm{t}+\bm{t}\cdot\delta\hat{\bm{n}}).

(71)

5.2
Variation of the disk-surrogate plate potential

The variation of the D-SPT interaction potential,
\phi=\phi(d,\cos\alpha)
, is given by

\displaystyle\delta\phi

\displaystyle=\phi_{,d}\;\delta d+\phi_{,\cos\alpha}\;\delta\cos\alpha.

(72)

The first term gives

\displaystyle\phi_{,d}\;\delta d=\phi_{,d}\;\hat{\bm{n}}\cdot(\delta\hat{\bm{u}}_{\text{B}}-\delta\hat{\bm{u}}_{\text{S}})=\bm{f}_{\text{B}}\cdot\delta\bm{u}_{\text{B}}+\bm{f}_{\text{S}}\cdot\delta\hat{\bm{u}}_{\text{S}}=\bm{f}\cdot\delta\bm{u}_{\text{B}}-\bm{f}\cdot\delta\hat{\bm{u}}_{\text{S}},

(73)

where
\bm{f}:=\phi_{,d}\;\hat{\bm{n}}
is the D-SPT interaction force. Actually, this quantity has dimensions of [force/length], but we will refer to it as the
interaction force
in the following.
The second term gives

\displaystyle\phi_{,\cos\alpha}\;\delta\cos\alpha=-\phi_{,\cos\alpha}\;\frac{t_{n}}{\cos\alpha}(\hat{\bm{n}}\cdot\delta\bm{t}+\bm{t}\cdot\delta\hat{\bm{n}})=w_{c}(\hat{\bm{n}}\cdot\delta\bm{t}+\bm{t}\cdot\delta\hat{\bm{n}}),

(74)

where

\displaystyle w_{c}:=-\phi_{,\cos\alpha}\;\frac{t_{n}}{\cos\alpha}=-\phi^{R}_{,\cos\alpha}t_{n}\quad\text{and}\quad\phi^{R}_{,\cos\alpha}:=\frac{\phi_{,\cos\alpha}}{\cos\alpha}.

(75)

We introduce a reduced potential
\phi^{R}
to avoid a singularity issue when
\cos\alpha\rightarrow 0

[
7
]
. The variation of the tangent is given in Eq. (
E3
), while the variation (linear increment) of the normal is given in Appendix
D
. With these expressions at hand, we get

\displaystyle\phi_{,\cos\alpha}\;\delta\cos\alpha

\displaystyle=\frac{w_{c}}{\sqrt{j_{\mathrm{B}}}}(\hat{\bm{n}}-t_{n}\bm{t})\cdot\delta\bm{u}_{\mathrm{B},1}

(76)

\displaystyle+\frac{w_{c}}{d}(\bm{t}-(\bm{t}\cdot\hat{\bm{g}}_{\alpha})\bar{\hat{\bm{g}}}^{\alpha}-t_{n}\hat{\bm{n}})(\delta\bm{u}_{\text{B}}-\delta\hat{\bm{u}}_{\text{S}})-w_{c}(\bm{t}\cdot\bar{\hat{\bm{g}}}^{\beta})\hat{\bm{n}}\cdot\delta\hat{\bm{u}}_{\text{S},\beta}

\displaystyle=\bm{f}_{\mathrm{CB}}\cdot\delta\bm{u}_{\mathrm{B},1}+\bm{f}_{\mathrm{C}}\cdot(\delta\bm{u}_{\text{B}}-\delta\bm{\hat{u}}_{\text{S}})-\bm{f}_{\mathrm{CS}}^{\beta}\cdot\delta\bm{\hat{u}}_{\text{S},\beta},

\displaystyle\bm{f}_{\mathrm{CB}}

\displaystyle:=\frac{w_{c}}{\sqrt{j}}(\hat{\bm{n}}-t_{n}\bm{t})=w_{c}\tilde{\bm{f}}_{\mathrm{CB}},

\displaystyle\bm{f}^{\beta}_{\mathrm{CS}}

\displaystyle:=w_{c}(\bm{t}\cdot\bar{\hat{\bm{g}}}^{\beta})\hat{\bm{n}}=w_{c}\tilde{\bm{f}}^{\beta}_{\mathrm{CS}},

\displaystyle\bm{f}_{\mathrm{C}}

\displaystyle:=\frac{w_{c}}{d}(\bm{t}-(\bm{t}\cdot\hat{\bm{g}}_{\alpha})\bar{\hat{\bm{g}}}^{\alpha}-t_{n}\hat{\bm{n}})=w_{c}\tilde{\bm{f}}_{\mathrm{C}}.

Due to the variation of potential w.r.t. the angle, we obtain a contribution to the interaction force that is work-conjugate to the virtual displacements,
\bm{f}_{\mathrm{C}}
, and interaction moments that are work-conjugate to the variations of the tangent vectors of the beam and the shell,
\bm{f}_{\mathrm{CB}}
and
\bm{f}_{\mathrm{CS}}
. For the implementation, it is convenient to express

\displaystyle\delta t_{n}

\displaystyle=\delta(\bm{t}\cdot\hat{\bm{n}})=\tilde{\bm{f}}_{\mathrm{CB}}\cdot\delta\bm{u}_{\mathrm{B},1}+\tilde{\bm{f}}_{\mathrm{C}}\cdot(\delta\bm{u}_{\text{B}}-\delta\hat{\bm{u}}_{\text{S}})-\tilde{\bm{f}}_{\mathrm{CS}}\cdot\delta\hat{\bm{u}}_{\text{S},\beta}.

(77)

The variation of the D-SPT potential should be integrated along the beam length to find the complete variation of the beam-shell potential, i.e.,

\displaystyle\delta\Pi_{\mathrm{B-S}}

\displaystyle=\int_{L_{\mathrm{B}}}\delta\phi\differential{s_\mathrm{B}}=\int_{L_{\mathrm{B}}}(\bm{f}+\bm{f}_{\mathrm{C}})\cdot\delta\bm{u}_{\text{B}}\differential{s_\mathrm{B}}-\int_{L_{\mathrm{B}}}(\bm{f}+\bm{f}_{\mathrm{C}})\differential{s_\mathrm{B}}\cdot\delta\hat{\bm{u}}_{\text{S}}

(78)

\displaystyle+\int_{L_{\mathrm{B}}}\bm{f}_{\mathrm{CB}}\cdot\delta\bm{u}_{\mathrm{B},1}\differential{s_\mathrm{B}}-\int_{L_{\mathrm{B}}}\bm{f}_{\mathrm{CS}}\differential{s_\mathrm{B}}\cdot\delta\hat{\bm{u}}_{\text{S},\beta}

In the following, we will consider three formulations: (i) full formulation (FF) that employs complete Eq. (
78
), (ii) reduced formulation (RF1) given by Eq. (
73
) that neglects contributions from the variation of angle, and (iii) reduced formulation (RF2) that neglects both the variation w.r.t. angle and the orientation of the cross section by adopting
\phi=\phi(d,1)
.

Details on the linearization of SBS contribution to the total potential energy are presented in Appendices
D
and
E
, while the variation of orthogonality condition is given in Appendix
C
.

7
Conclusions

This paper presents the first computational formulation for potential-based interactions between deformable beams and shells. The approach is founded on coarse-graining the interaction potential and on the analytical pre-integration of the disk-surrogate plate interaction, which approximates the disk-shell interaction. In this way, the original 6D integral of the interaction potential is reduced to a 1D integral.

To enable rigorous error estimation, we derived an analytical disk-sphere vdW law. The obtained upper bound of the approximation error for small separations is within the reasonable limits, considering the problem at hand. Thorough numerical analysis shows that the developed beam-shell formulation is capable of modeling fundamental cases of interaction between deformable fibers and membranes.

The main advantages of the proposed approach are efficiency and good approximation of the interaction force acting on the beam. The main disadvantage is a rather poor approximation of the interaction force on the shell, since it is approximated here with a set of point forces. This issue could be addressed, for example, by employing the two half-pass approach
[
44
]
, in which the forces on the shell could be calculated from a fiber-surrogate cylinder interaction.

The present work has potential applications in several fields of engineering, for example, in the study of cellular membranes and their interactions with various objects, such as fibers and proteins. Future research will focus on the tangential component of the interaction force and accompanying energy dissipation.

Appendix F
Derivation of the disk-sphere interaction potential

In this Appendix, we derive an analytical vdW law for the disk-sphere interaction, which was used in Section
4.2
for error estimation. We start from the point-sphere vdW law
[
30
]
,

\Pi_{\mathrm{P-S}}(t)=\frac{4\pi R_{\mathrm{S}}}{3t^{3}(t+2R_{\mathrm{S}})},

(F1)

where
t
is the distance between the sphere’s center and the point. A schematic of the disk-sphere interaction is shown in Fig.
27
, illustrating the special case in which the disk is perpendicular to the tangential plane of the sphere at the closest point.

Figure 27
:
Interaction between a disk and a sphere in a perpendicular orientation.

In other words, the disk lies in the plane passing through the sphere’s center. Using the designations defined in Fig.
27
, we can express the distance between the sphere’s center and an arbitrary point of the disk,
t
, as

t=\sqrt{d^{2}+r^{2}-2dr\cos\beta},\quad\text{with}\quad g=d-R_{\mathrm{S}}-R_{\mathrm{D}},

(F2)

where
g
is the gap between the sphere and the disk. By integrating the point-sphere potential over the area of the disk, we obtain the disk-sphere vdW interaction potential

\Pi_{\mathrm{D-S}}=2\int_{0}^{\pi}\int_{0}^{R_{\mathrm{D}}}\Pi_{\mathrm{P-S}}(t)\,r\differential{r}\differential{\beta},

(F3)

which has the analytical solution

\Pi_{\mathrm{D-S}}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{3R_{\mathrm{S}}}\left(1+\frac{R_{\mathrm{S}}^{6}+(R_{\mathrm{D}}^{2}-3d^{2})R_{\mathrm{S}}^{4}+3(d^{4}-R_{\mathrm{D}}^{4})R_{\mathrm{S}}^{2}-(d^{2}-R_{\mathrm{D}}^{2})^{3}}{\left\{\left[d^{2}-(R_{\mathrm{D}}+R_{\mathrm{S}})\right]^{2}\left[d^{2}+(R_{\mathrm{D}}+R_{\mathrm{S}})\right]^{2}\right\}^{3/2}}\right).

(F4)

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first derivation of an analytical disk-sphere vdW law. Arguably, this law could be used as a second-order approximation for short-range vdW beam-shell interactions, where the section-shell interaction could be approximated with a section-spherical shell interaction.
