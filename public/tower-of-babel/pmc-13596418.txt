Preoperative Creatinine-to-Cystatin C Ratio and 1-year Risk of Infection-Related Hospitalization or Death with a Functioning Graft in Older Kidney Transplant Recipients
Authors: Rongxin Chen, Lu Xu, Guanghui Li, Jiali Fang, Jialin Wu, Luhao Liu, Zheng Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOlder kidney transplant recipients experience substantial early infection-related morbidity. The unscaled preoperative creatinine-to-cystatin C ratio (hereafter SI) is routinely available and may serve as an indirect laboratory-derived vulnerability marker for sarcopenia. We evaluated associations of SI with 1-year infection-related hospitalization and death with a functioning graft (DWFG).MethodsWe analyzed 497 recipients aged ≥65 years transplanted in 2017–2023 in a single-center analysis of a prospectively maintained database. SI used the last paired sample before induction immunosuppression. The primary endpoint was time to first infection-related hospitalization or DWFG within 365 days; components were analyzed separately. Cox models used prespecified adjustment and exploratory interactions by sex and pretransplant dialysis modality. Incremental value was evaluated by bootstrap-corrected discrimination, calibration, and Brier score.ResultsMedian age was 68.1 years; the full cohort included 312 men (62.8%) and 185 women (37.2%), with median SI 0.82 (IQR 0.70–0.95). The composite endpoint occurred in 182/497 (36.6%), infection-related hospitalization in 165/497 (33.2%), and DWFG in 29/497 (5.8%). Lower SI was associated with the composite (adjusted HR 1.32 per 1 SD decrease, 95% CI 1.09–1.60; p=0.004), infection-related hospitalization (HR 1.28, 95% CI 1.05–1.57; p=0.015), and, with greater uncertainty, DWFG (HR 1.62, 95% CI 1.04–2.52; p=0.034). There was no evidence of interaction by sex (p=0.86) or dialysis modality (p=0.91). Adding SI changed Harrell’s C-index from 0.609 to 0.625 (Δ0.016; 95% CI −0.008 to 0.039).ConclusionIn the entire cohort, lower preoperative SI was associated with a higher 1-year risk of the composite endpoint, which was driven mainly by infection-related hospitalization. SI is not a standalone risk-stratification tool, and prospective multicenter external validation is required before clinical implementation.

Introduction

The growing number of organ transplants performed in older adults reflects demographic shifts and expanding transplant eligibility, but older recipients have a disproportionately high burden of early post-transplant morbidity.1,2 Aging-related immune remodeling (immunosenescence) and chronic low-grade inflammation (“inflammaging”) can increase susceptibility to common and opportunistic infections in the setting of immunosuppression, including bacterial infections and cytomegalovirus (CMV) in the first post-transplant year.3–6 Frailty and related vulnerability states are more prevalent with aging and have been consistently linked to poorer transplant outcomes.7–10 Although kidney transplantation can provide substantial benefits compared with dialysis in selected older individuals, outcomes vary widely, and optimizing perioperative management and risk stratification remains a key clinical goal.1,2

Frailty and sarcopenia are related but distinct constructs reflecting reduced physiologic reserve. Contemporary sarcopenia consensus frameworks (eg, EWGSOP2 and AWGS) emphasize objective assessment of muscle strength (eg, handgrip strength or chair-stand testing) and recommend confirmation using measures of muscle quantity/quality (eg, DXA, CT, or bioimpedance) when available.11,12 In transplant candidates, however, comprehensive functional and body-composition assessments are often inconsistently implemented because they require time, equipment, and standardized workflows.10,13 As a result, pragmatic markers that are routinely available and scalable may be helpful for risk stratification.

The creatinine-to-cystatin C ratio has been proposed as a laboratory-derived “sarcopenia index” (SI) to approximate muscle reserve, although it should be interpreted as a laboratory-derived vulnerability marker rather than a diagnostic measure of sarcopenia when direct muscle measurements are unavailable.14 In chronic kidney disease (CKD) and hemodialysis populations, the creatinine-to-cystatin C ratio correlates with muscle-related phenotypes, although its performance and interpretation are context dependent.15,16 In community-dwelling older adults, diagnostic performance for detecting low muscle mass can be limited.17 Prior kidney-transplant studies evaluated Cr/CysC-based measures against muscle mass, frailty/sarcopenia phenotypes, or later cardiovascular outcomes, but did not assess a pre-induction ratio against 1-year infection-related hospitalization in recipients aged ≥65 years.18–20 Thus, the specific knowledge gap concerns prognostic utility at a standardized pretransplant time point in an age-defined cohort. Accordingly, the prognostic value of SI measured immediately before kidney transplantation for clinically meaningful 1-year outcomes in older recipients, particularly severe infection requiring hospitalization and death with functioning graft (DWFG), is incompletely characterized. We therefore evaluated whether preoperative SI measured from the last paired pre-induction creatinine and cystatin C sample is independently associated with a 1-year composite endpoint of infection-related hospitalization or DWFG. We additionally examined nonlinearity using restricted cubic splines, evaluated associations by SI quartiles, and used competing-event-aware methods for infection-related hospitalization to account for the interdependence of infection and mortality in the first post-transplant year.

Methods

Study Design and Reporting

We conducted a non-interventional, single-center observational cohort analysis using a prospectively maintained transplant database at The Second Affiliated Hospital, Guangzhou Medical University to evaluate whether the preoperative creatinine-to-cystatin C ratio (SI) is associated with clinically meaningful early outcomes after kidney transplantation in older recipients. The study period included transplantations from January 2017 through December 2023. Time zero was the date of transplantation; SI and baseline covariates were fixed using information obtained before induction immunosuppression or documented as the intended regimen on the transplantation date. Follow-up was assessed for 365 days after transplantation for each included recipient. The protocol conformed to the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the ethics committee of The Second Affiliated Hospital, Guangzhou Medical University. Written informed consent for study participation was obtained from all kidney transplant recipients prior to any study procedures. Donor kidneys were obtained via the Department of Organ Transplantation, The Second Affiliated Hospital, Guangzhou Medical University, in accordance with national regulations governing organ donation and transplantation; informed consent for donation was obtained from living donors and/or the donor’s legally authorized representative for deceased donation, as applicable. No organs or tissues were obtained from prisoners.

Participants, Eligibility, and Data Sources

Consecutive kidney transplant recipients were eligible if (1) recipient age ≥65 years at transplantation and (2) availability of paired preoperative serum creatinine and cystatin C measured from the final preoperative assessment before induction immunosuppression. Recipients were excluded if paired creatinine and/or cystatin C were missing from the final preoperative laboratory panel or if records were insufficient to adjudicate 1-year outcomes. Demographic, laboratory, dialysis, donor, transplant, and immunosuppression variables were obtained from the transplant database and linked electronic medical record; outcome source documents underwent chart adjudication. Assessment of Creatinine-to-Cystatin C Ratio (SI)

SI was defined as serum creatinine (mg/dL) divided by serum cystatin C (mg/L) measured from the same preoperative blood draw obtained before induction immunosuppression. Values are reported as the unscaled Cr/CysC ratio; multiplying by 100 yields the original Kashani SI scale and does not alter estimates expressed per SD or by quartile.

Assays and Standardization

Serum creatinine was measured using an assay calibrated to IDMS-traceable standards, and serum cystatin C was measured using an immunoassay calibrated to the ERM‑DA471/IFCC cystatin C reference material.

Dialysis Timing

For hemodialysis recipients, paired specimens were preferentially collected pre-dialysis from the final dialysis session before transplantation (or, if not feasible, from the closest paired preoperative panel with recorded timing). For peritoneal dialysis and preemptive recipients, SI was computed from the most proximate paired preoperative laboratory panel.

Creatinine production is partly related to muscle mass, whereas cystatin C is less dependent on muscle mass; their ratio may therefore capture muscle-related variation that either marker alone does not, and Cr/CysC has correlated with DXA-derived skeletal muscle index in kidney transplant recipients.14,18 Nevertheless, both analytes remain influenced by renal and nonrenal factors, and dialysis timing can affect interpretation.15–17 We therefore did not formally diagnose sarcopenia; consistent with EWGSOP2/AWGS frameworks, SI was analyzed as a laboratory-derived vulnerability marker that may partly reflect muscle reserve.11,12

Outcomes, Adjudication, and Follow-Up

The primary endpoint was time from transplantation to the first occurrence within 365 days of infection-related hospitalization or DWFG. The composite was retained as a time-to-first summary of severe early post-transplant events; it did not imply that its components were clinically equivalent. Each component was analyzed and interpreted separately because infection-related hospitalization was expected to dominate and DWFG was less frequent.

DWFG was defined as death occurring while the kidney allograft was functioning and without documented graft failure requiring return to chronic dialysis or retransplantation prior to death. Death-censored graft failure (return to dialysis or retransplantation) within 1 year was treated as a competing event in DWFG analyses.

Infection-related hospitalization was defined as a hospital-level episode requiring systemic anti-infective therapy, comprising either a new infection during the index transplant admission that prolonged or intensified inpatient care or an unplanned post-discharge readmission primarily attributable to infection. Admissions for elective procedures or monitoring/prophylaxis without a treating clinician’s diagnosis of active infection were not classified as infection-related hospitalization. Two transplant clinicians independently adjudicated the first qualifying episode using admission notes, investigations (including microbiology and imaging when available), and discharge diagnoses while unaware of SI; disagreements were resolved by consensus. First episodes were assigned a mutually exclusive primary category (urinary tract, respiratory, bloodstream, gastrointestinal, opportunistic, or other infection). High-severity infection-related hospitalization was defined descriptively as intensive care unit admission, vasopressor support or invasive mechanical ventilation, or all-cause death within 30 days; severity indicators could overlap.

Participants were followed until the first qualifying event, death, or administrative censoring at 365 days. Recipients with insufficient documentation to adjudicate 1-year outcomes were excluded prior to analysis. Overlapping events (infection followed by death) were handled as time-to-first-event for the composite endpoint.

Covariates

Prespecified covariates were selected on clinical grounds before outcome modeling to address confounding by recipient physiologic reserve, comorbidity, dialysis-related factors, and transplant characteristics; no data-driven stepwise selection was used. Recipient covariates included age, sex, BMI, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, dialysis modality (hemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis, or preemptive transplantation), dialysis vintage, baseline serum albumin, and hemoglobin. Dialysis vintage was coded as zero for preemptive recipients while retaining dialysis modality in the model to identify that structural zero. Transplant covariates included donor type (living vs deceased), donor age, cold ischemia time, induction regimen category, the intended initial maintenance immunosuppression category documented on the transplantation date before outcome occurrence, and transplant era (2017–2019, 2020–2021, 2022–2023). Direct frailty scales, grip strength, gait speed, dietary-intake measures, and CT/DXA/bioimpedance body-composition measures were not routinely available and could not be included.

Statistical Analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized overall and by SI quartile and compared using Kruskal–Wallis tests for continuous variables and χ2 tests for categorical variables. Multiplicity across baseline comparisons was controlled using Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) q-values. The cohort SD of the unscaled SI was 0.19; quartile cut points were 0.70, 0.82, and 0.95.

The primary association between SI and the 1-year composite endpoint was estimated using Cox proportional hazards regression, reporting hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for SI per 1 SD decrease in prespecified models: Unadjusted model, Model 2 (core: age, sex, BMI, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, dialysis modality, dialysis vintage, donor type, donor age, cold ischemia time, induction regimen), and Model 3 (extended: Model 2 covariates plus baseline albumin, baseline hemoglobin, initial maintenance immunosuppression category, and transplant era). All eligible recipients determined the study size; no separate a priori sample-size calculation was performed.

SI quartiles were analyzed with Q4 as the reference. p-for-trend was computed using an ordinal term coded 0–3 and oriented per one-quartile decrease in SI. Potential nonlinearity was assessed using a restricted cubic spline with four knots at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles and a likelihood-ratio test of the nonlinear terms. Predicted log hazards and variances were combined across imputed datasets using Rubin’s rules. Proportional hazards assumptions were evaluated using Schoenfeld-residual diagnostics and visual checks.

Competing-Risk Approach

For infection-related hospitalization, all-cause death occurring before the first infection-related hospitalization was treated as the competing event. We estimated associations using a Model 3 cause-specific Cox model (censoring at death) and displayed absolute risk using Aalen–Johansen cumulative incidence functions stratified by SI quartile. Cause-specific HRs were interpreted as associations with the instantaneous hazard of infection hospitalization, while cumulative incidence curves described absolute risk in the presence of competing mortality. For DWFG, death-censored graft failure was treated as a competing event; the cause-specific model included SI, age, and sex only because 29 DWFG events limited model complexity. Crude 1-year component proportions used Wilson 95% CIs.

Missing covariate data were limited (maximum 4.2% for any variable) and handled using multiple imputation by chained equations with 20 datasets; the imputation model included all analysis covariates, SI, event indicators, and the Nelson–Aalen cumulative hazard, and pooled estimates used Rubin’s rules. Complete-case analysis (n=462) was a robustness check. To assess sex and pretransplant treatment, Model 3 was extended with SI-by-sex and SI-by-dialysis-modality interaction terms; sex-stratified estimates used SI standardized within sex, and interaction tests were exploratory. For incremental performance, the prespecified clinical model included age, sex, BMI, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, dialysis modality, baseline albumin, donor type, and induction regimen; SI was then added as one continuous term. Harrell’s C-index, paired ΔC, calibration slope/intercept at 365 days, and the inverse-probability-of-censoring-weighted Brier score were estimated with 1000 bootstrap resamples for optimism correction and 95% CIs. All tests were two-sided with α=0.05 for the primary association. Benjamini–Hochberg FDR control was applied separately to baseline comparisons, the three nonreference quartile contrasts, and the two component-endpoint tests.

Baseline Characteristics by SI Quartiles

Several recipient factors differed across SI strata, including age (p<0.001; FDR q<0.001), BMI (p<0.001; FDR q<0.001), sex distribution (p<0.001; FDR q<0.001), and comorbidity burden for diabetes (p<0.001; FDR q=0.004) and cardiovascular disease (p<0.001; FDR q=0.002) (Table 1). Transplant-related factors were broadly similar across quartiles, including donor type and donor age, dialysis modality distribution, induction regimen, initial maintenance immunosuppression category, and transplant era (Table 1). Cold ischemia time showed a modest unadjusted difference (p=0.030) that did not remain significant after FDR correction (q=0.102).

One-year Outcome Incidence and Composite Composition

Within 365 days after transplantation, 182/497 (36.6%) recipients experienced the composite endpoint of infection-related hospitalization or DWFG (Figure 1). Infection-related hospitalization occurred in 165/497 (33.2%; Wilson 95% CI 29.2–37.5), whereas 29/497 (5.8%; Wilson 95% CI 4.1–8.3) experienced DWFG. Twelve recipients had infection-related hospitalization followed by DWFG and were counted once in the time-to-first composite (165 + 29 − 12 = 182). Among the 165 first qualifying infection hospitalizations, urinary tract (61, 37.0%) and respiratory infections (50, 30.3%) were most frequent; 35 (21.2%) met the high-severity definition (Table 2). Association Between SI and the 1-year Composite Endpoint

Lower preoperative SI was consistently associated with higher risk of the 1-year composite endpoint (Table 3). Modeled per 1 SD decrease, SI was associated with increased composite risk in unadjusted analysis (HR 1.39; 95% CI 1.19–1.63; p<0.001) and remained significant after adjustment in both the core model (HR 1.35; 95% CI 1.12–1.63; p=0.002) and the extended model (HR 1.32; 95% CI 1.09–1.60; p=0.004). Quartile analyses showed higher adjusted risk for recipients in the lowest SI quartile compared with the highest quartile (Q1 vs Q4 HR 2.29; 95% CI 1.39–3.79; p=0.001; FDR q=0.004). Intermediate quartile estimates overlapped and did not remain significant after FDR correction (Q2 vs Q4 p=0.076; q=0.076; Q3 vs Q4 p=0.050; q=0.075), while the inverse trend was significant (HR 1.31 per one-quartile decrease in SI; 95% CI 1.10–1.56; p=0.002) (Table 4). Restricted cubic spline analysis supported an inverse association without evidence of nonlinearity (p=0.27) (Figure 2). Sex, Dialysis Modality, and Complete-Case Sensitivity Analyses

After sex-specific standardization of SI, model-based associations were similar in men (n=312; 113 events; HR 1.30, 95% CI 1.03–1.64) and women (n=185; 69 events; HR 1.35, 95% CI 0.96–1.89), with no evidence of SI-by-sex interaction (p=0.86). Estimates were also directionally consistent across hemodialysis (n=332; 124 events; HR 1.34, 95% CI 1.06–1.69), peritoneal dialysis (n=79; 30 events; HR 1.28, 95% CI 0.77–2.11), and preemptive transplantation (n=86; 28 events; HR 1.25, 95% CI 0.76–2.05), without evidence of interaction (p=0.91) (Figure 5). The complete-case analysis (n=462; 169 events) was consistent with the imputed-data analysis (HR 1.30, 95% CI 1.06–1.59; p=0.011). Discussion

In this cohort of older kidney transplant recipients, a lower preoperative creatinine-to-cystatin C ratio measured immediately before induction immunosuppression was independently associated with a higher 1-year risk of clinically meaningful adverse outcomes after adjustment for recipient comorbidities, dialysis factors, laboratory markers, and transplant characteristics. The inverse trend was driven principally by Q1 versus Q4, with overlap between intermediate quartiles. Infection-related hospitalization was the dominant component; the separate DWFG estimate was less precise because only 29 events occurred. Although the C-index increased after adding SI, the ΔC confidence interval crossed 0; therefore, incremental prediction beyond standard clinical factors should be interpreted cautiously.

From a geriatrics and nutrition perspective, low SI may partly reflect reduced muscle reserve, but it may also capture sex-related differences, dialysis timing/fluid status, kidney function, and systemic inflammation and may coexist with protein–energy wasting (PEW), a broader kidney-disease–related syndrome characterized by depletion of protein/energy stores across biochemical, body composition, and intake domains.21,22 Mechanistically, low reserve and catabolic vulnerability may plausibly reduce resilience to perioperative stress and increase susceptibility to infectious complications during the period of maximal immunosuppression. Because consensus definitions of sarcopenia emphasize low muscle strength as the key clinical feature and use measures of muscle quantity/quality to confirm diagnosis when available, SI should be interpreted as a pragmatic laboratory-derived vulnerability marker rather than a direct or guideline-based sarcopenia diagnosis.11,12 This framing aligns with transplant geriatrics literature demonstrating that frailty predicts worse outcomes in kidney transplantation,7–10 and complements the broader concept that aging-related immune remodeling may shift the risk balance toward infections in older recipients.5,6,23

Our findings add to the literature suggesting that creatinine/cystatin C–based indices capture clinically meaningful vulnerability in high-risk populations while remaining context dependent. The SI concept was originally proposed as a simple laboratory-derived approximation of muscle mass in critically ill patients.14 In kidney transplant recipients, Cr/CysC correlated with DXA-derived skeletal muscle index in both sexes,18 while frailty and sarcopenia frequently coexisted but were not interchangeable in older recipients.19 A recent post-transplant study associated cystatin C/creatinine eGFR discordance with cardiovascular events rather than infection outcomes.20 Together, these studies support biological plausibility but did not establish a pre-induction ratio as a predictor of early infection-related hospitalization in an exclusively older cohort. Evidence in community-dwelling older adults also indicates limited diagnostic performance for identifying low muscle mass or sarcopenia,17 reinforcing interpretation of SI primarily as a risk marker.

Clinically, SI is attractive because it is scalable and routinely available in preoperative workups. A cautious near-term implication is to use low SI as a prompt for structured, guideline-consistent assessment rather than as a standalone management trigger, especially because the incremental discrimination change was small and statistically uncertain. Low SI could prompt renal nutrition consultation and focused assessment for PEW/malnutrition with dietary intake review and functional screening when feasible, consistent with kidney nutrition guidance.22 Low SI may also help triage older candidates toward feasible prehabilitation efforts.24,25 Given that infection hospitalization dominated the composite outcome, low SI could justify heightened attention to established vaccination and Pneumocystis prevention protocols26,27 without implying departures from standard care. Exploratory interaction analyses found no evidence that the association differed by sex or pretransplant dialysis modality, but wide CIs in women and smaller treatment subgroups preclude claims of equivalence.

Limitations of this study include possible residual confounding. Direct measures of frailty severity, dietary intake, muscle strength, physical performance, and body composition were unavailable; albumin, BMI, and hemoglobin cannot fully capture these domains. SI is context dependent because kidney function, dialysis timing/fluid status, systemic inflammation, and nonmuscle determinants of cystatin C may influence the ratio. The 365-day horizon was appropriate for early infection but short for assessing longer-term DWFG, and the 29-event DWFG model was necessarily parsimonious. Infection-related hospitalization was adjudicated and further classified by type and severity, but etiologic misclassification and center-specific admission thresholds remain possible. SI thresholds are assay-, unit-, and population-dependent; cutoffs should not be generalized. Prospective multicenter external validation is required before SI thresholds or SI-guided management strategies can be recommended.

In conclusion, among 497 older kidney transplant recipients of both sexes, a lower preoperative creatinine-to-cystatin C ratio associated with higher 1-year risk of the composite endpoint, driven mainly by infection-related hospitalization. The separate DWFG estimate was compatible with higher risk but was event limited. SI is a laboratory-derived vulnerability marker but not a validated standalone clinical risk tool. Prospective multicenter external validation is needed before clinical implementation.
