Towards a feature-based regulatory benchmark for user-centred SaMD design in cancer care
Authors: Saba Kheirinejad, Christopher L Brett, Robert L Davis, David L Schwartz, Arash Shaban-Nejad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSoftware as a medical device (SaMD) is increasingly central to cancer care, yet current regulatory frameworks from the International Medical Device Regulators, the US Food and Drug Administration and the European Medical Device Regulation lack clear premarket standards for evaluating usability, accessibility and emotional/cultural appropriateness for intended users. This gap is particularly problematic in oncology, where patients, caregivers and clinicians navigate high cognitive load, emotional stress and complex care coordination that can make poorly designed tools ineffective or even harmful.This paper introduces a structured, feature-based benchmark grounded in human-centred design and user-centred design principles to address this critical gap and enable integration into early regulatory review. The benchmark defines measurable design criteria for premarket review, with a focus on usability, accessibility, emotional safety and clinical applicability, providing both developers and regulators with a practical checklist. By embedding these standards early in the product lifecycle, the framework supports regulatory consistency, reduces postmarket usability failures and improves patient trust, engagement and clinical outcomes.While developed for oncology care, the benchmark principles are adaptable across diseases, offering a replicable pathway to align SaMD design with both clinical requirements and the real-world needs of patients and healthcare systems.Keywords: Software Design, BMJ Health Informatics, Delivery of Health Care, Health Equity, Health Information Interoperability

Introduction to SaMD in oncology

Software as a medical device (SaMD) refers to software intended for one or more medical purposes that functions independently of a hardware medical device. It is classified as a medical device when it meets specific criteria, such as diagnosing, treating, monitoring, preventing or alleviating diseases.1 In oncology, SaMD is emerging as a transformative solution, with applications in symptom monitoring,2 risk detection, tumour diagnosis3 and treatment.4 These applications hold significant promise for improving patient outcomes and streamlining clinical workflows. However, their success depends not only on technical performance but also on how well they align with the needs, expectations and contexts of end users.

Cancer care presents a uniquely high-stakes, emotionally taxing and cognitively demanding context compared with many other diseases. Patients frequently undergo complex, multimodal treatments, manage variable symptoms and interact with diverse specialists across fragmented systems. These demands make usability, emotional safety and interface clarity especially vital. If user-centred SaMD can succeed in oncology, it can inform best practices across other domains.

Aligning SaMD solutions with end-user needs is especially evident in oncology, where the complexity of care, high-stakes clinical decisions and emotional burden on patients and caregivers demand thoughtful human-centred design.5 6 In this context, end users, including patients, caregivers and clinicians, bring distinct needs and challenges that must be systematically addressed to ensure effectiveness, usability and adoption.

For patients, engaging with SaMD in oncology often requires interpreting and understanding complex medical information, managing multifaceted symptom profiles and adhering to rigorous treatment protocols. When SaMD tools fail to accommodate variations in health literacy, emotional resilience and technological proficiency, they risk excluding or overwhelming those most in need of support.5 Poorly designed interfaces and inadequate guidance can exacerbate distress rather than reduce it, undermining patient adherence and clinical outcomes. Caregivers who serve as essential intermediaries in oncology care require tools that are intuitive, informative and capable of supporting timely decision-making.7 8 Their ability to navigate these platforms directly influences care coordination and symptom management. Clinicians, meanwhile, operate within high-pressure environments marked by complex data-intensive workflows, where SaMD must integrate seamlessly with existing systems and processes, from imaging and laboratory data to patient-reported outcomes.9

To be clinically effective, SaMD tools must integrate seamlessly into oncology workflows, providing actionable insights without adding cognitive or administrative burden. Even the most technically advanced solutions will be overlooked in practice if they lack intuitive interfaces or fail to support timely decision-making. Oncology care is inherently multidisciplinary, involving a range of diverse clinical roles, from oncologists and radiologists to palliative care teams, each with distinct informational needs and interaction patterns. Without early and continuous engagement with these users, developers risk overlooking critical functionality or usability features, reducing real-world effectiveness and adoption.10

Background and rationale

While current regulatory frameworks, such as those from the International Medical Device Regulators Forum (IMDRF),1 11 the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)12 and the European Medical Device Regulation (MDR),13 establish protocols around clinical safety, data privacy and interoperability, they lack clear, structured criteria for determining whether these digital tools are usable, emotionally appropriate and patient-centred.

Specifically, there are no formal benchmarks or a standard set of criteria for assessing whether a SaMD meaningfully adheres to human-centred design (HCD) or user-centred design (UCD) principles before regulatory approval. This gap is particularly problematic in oncology, where patients often face physical limitations, emotional distress and cognitive fatigue, factors that significantly affect their ability to interact with digital tools.

Oncology serves as a stress test for user-centred SaMD, where design flaws have high consequences. Cancer risk increases with age, with half of all cases diagnosed in individuals over 67.14 Older adults often have lower digital literacy, making UCD essential for accessibility and usability for this key population.

Some may argue that so-called ‘soft’ factors, such as software usability, fall outside the scope of regulation, assuming market forces will naturally weed out poor designs. However, in oncology, poorly designed interfaces can directly undermine adherence and patient safety, making usability a legitimate regulatory concern.

To illustrate the implications of inadequate design, a 68-year-old patient with breast cancer undergoing chemotherapy has been considered. She was given a SaMD app to track side effects, log symptoms, receive medication reminders and stay in touch with her care team. In practice, the app failed to meet her needs. The interface relied on dense medical jargon, the screen had low contrast and small text difficult for a patient with vision issues to read, and reminder features were inconsistent. On her next oncology visit, her care team discovered she had missed several medication doses and failed to report escalating symptoms, delaying interventions and increasing distress.

This case reflects a broader systemic gap: in the absence of premarket usability standards formally adopted by regulators, design flaws are often identified postdeployment, when corrections are costly or impossible. Current SaMD evaluations rely heavily on retrospective feedback, leaving developers and reviewers without a concrete, actionable checklist of what constitutes high-quality UCD. Consequently, critical features such as accessibility, error tolerance, emotional tone and engagement scaffolding are inconsistently implemented or absent.

This paper responds to that gap by introducing a structured, feature-based benchmark for evaluating UCD in oncology-focused SaMDs. Unlike evaluations that depend on postmarket testing or subjective feedback, the proposed framework identifies specific design features that can be objectively reviewed and assessed upstream in the development life cycle. It empowers regulators, designers and clinical teams to ensure SaMD products meet baseline standards for usability, safety, accessibility and ethical engagement before entering the market.

Proposed benchmark: a feature-based standard for user-centred SaMD design

To address the absence of explicit user-centred evaluation criteria in current SaMD regulatory frameworks, we propose a feature-based benchmark grounded in HCD and UCD principles. Unlike conventional approaches that rely on postmarket usability testing or subjective user satisfaction metrics, this benchmark outlines tangible, observable features that can be reviewed and evaluated during pre-market assessment.

Each benchmark item functions as a design requirement that supports usability, accessibility, emotional safety and inclusivity. These measures are particularly critical in oncology, where users may face physical, emotional and cognitive challenges that make interaction difficult. The proposed framework prioritises simplicity, clarity, emotional safety, progressive engagement and accessibility, which offers regulators and developers a practical checklist to ensure that SaMD products align with real-world needs. The benchmark is structured into seven key design categories (figure 1), each consisting of specific requirements (table 1) that reflect best practices in UCD for health contexts. The categories can be ordered by user importance or by regulatory risk context (eg, significance of information and condition severity per IMDRF’s SaMD framework).11 Perceived importance may also vary by stakeholder (eg, clinicians vs patients vs regulators).

To evaluate SaMD features consistently and in line with regulatory risk considerations, each feature i is assigned a performance score Si on a scale from 0 to 5, reflecting how well the SaMD meets the benchmark for that feature. Additionally, each feature receives a weight Wi, assigned by the evaluator to reflect its importance, considering the SaMD’s overall clinical risk category as defined by the IMDRF framework. This risk classification is based on the significance of the information the SaMD provides (eg, to treat or diagnose, to drive clinical management or to inform clinical management) and the severity of the healthcare condition (critical, serious or non-serious). The weight Wi therefore, captures the evaluator’s judgement on how critical the feature is within that specific risk context.

This scoring approach ensures that features more critical to the SaMD’s risk profile have a greater influence on the overall evaluation. SaMDs intended for higher-risk applications, such as those designed to treat or diagnose critical conditions, will have key features assigned higher weights, reflecting their importance to safety and effectiveness. In such cases, a low score (0–5) on a highly weighted feature will significantly reduce the final score, making it more difficult for the device to meet the passing threshold. While the threshold itself may remain constant across SaMD types, the weighting system ensures that high-risk devices must perform well on their most critical features to pass, whereas lower-risk devices can still pass even if certain features have lower weights. While additional stakeholder-specific weights could be introduced to capture different perspectives, this benchmark is primarily designed to support consistent and risk-informed assessments by regulators.

Benchmark development approach

The benchmark proposed in this study was developed through a theory-informed, principle-driven process grounded in established user experience (UX) design principles such as accessibility, usability, functionality, personalisation and ethical data stewardship. These principles were derived from international standards such as the ISO 9241 series (Parts 210, 11, 171 and 125)15 and Nielsen’s usability heuristics.16

To ensure contextual relevance, we reviewed literature on digital health and oncology-specific SaMDs to identify key user needs and design challenges. Drawing on these insights, benchmark items were iteratively refined to emphasise patient experience, interface clarity and ethical engagement.

Discussion

The rapid growth of SaMD, especially in cancer care, offers great potential for monitoring, education and self-management. Yet, current regulatory frameworks from IMDRF,1 11 FDA12 and the European MDR13 prioritise clinical validation, technical functionalities and interoperability but lack structured criteria for evaluating whether a SaMD is usable, emotionally appropriate and centred on the lived experience of end users.

This gap has significant implications for the usability and real-world effectiveness of SaMD systems. Without clear user-centred benchmarks, regulators are left to infer usability and accessibility from high-level claims or postmarket feedback. For oncology patients, who may face fatigue, emotional distress or cognitive overload, this regulatory blind spot can result in tools that are functionally compliant but practically unusable.

While the FDA’s human factors engineering guidance,12 IMDRF principles11 and ISO 9241–21015 outline general usability principles and promote consideration of user needs, they primarily emphasise safety through risk mitigation and postdevelopment testing. These efforts are critical but often occur late and offer limited guidance for embedding UCD principles early in the design process. They also lack specificity for populations such as patients with cancer.

The proposed benchmark provides a structured, predeployment framework that translates UCD/HCD principles into auditable, regulator-facing design criteria across usability, accessibility, patient safety, ethical design and engagement. By translating abstract values from standards such as the ISO 9241 series (eg, ISO 9241–210, ISO 9241–11, ISO 9241–171 and ISO 9241–125)15 into a practical, domain-specific tool, this benchmark complements existing regulatory instruments and fills a critical gap. It enables regulators and developers to evaluate usability and UCD early in development rather than only at the point of compliance.

The value of our proposed benchmark lies in its flexibility and relevance across different use cases. While tailored to cancer care, its principles are broadly transferable to other domains. For example, in a lower-risk digital health context such as sleep management applications, the benchmark could assess whether recommendations are presented in a clear and supportive manner, whether messaging avoids anxiety-inducing language around sleep quality and whether users retain control over goal adjustments and notifications. This illustrates how the framework can support evaluation across both high-stakes and everyday digital health tools.

This flexibility also allows the benchmark to function as a shared standard that may inform the following:

Ultimately, this benchmark addresses a structural gap in SaMD oversight. As digital health tools become integral to care, regulatory decisions must evolve beyond technical functionality to include features that shape user trust, adoption and outcomes. The benchmark could be piloted within FDA or IMDRF workflows, for example, in 510(k) presubmission meetings context,17 to enable structured early-stage assessment of UCD before market clearance.

It is important to acknowledge that additional features, such as peer support18 and integrated communication with providers,19 can enhance user engagement and enrich the overall patient experience. These elements were deliberately excluded as optional enhancements rather than essential requirements to balance feasibility and broad applicability across diverse SaMD use cases.

Although primarily designed for premarket evaluation, the framework is equally applicable to postdeployment assessment. Given that SaMDs often undergo iterative updates after release, periodic application can ensure that changes preserve or enhance usability, safety, accessibility and ethical integrity, while addressing emerging user needs and evolving regulations.

The design principles emphasised in this benchmark, including simplicity, low cognitive burden, emotional safety and seamless integration into users’ daily routines, also resonate with broader paradigms in sociotechnical transition research that advocate for calm and non-disruptive technology integration. For example, the CalmMobility paradigm introduced by20 highlights the importance of technologies that support users without creating additional cognitive, emotional or behavioural burdens, emphasising humane and context-sensitive system design rather than efficiency-driven or technology-push approaches. Although originally developed in the context of sustainable mobility systems, this perspective aligns closely with the goals of user-centred digital health design. By promoting tools that operate quietly within users’ routines while supporting well-being and decision-making, the proposed benchmark contributes to a broader shift towards more humane and emotionally sustainable digital infrastructures in healthcare.

This benchmark is presented as a structured, theory-informed and practice-grounded starting point to catalyse a broader conversation among regulators, developers and clinical stakeholders. While empirical validation is a necessary next step, its foundations in established human factors principles, international usability standards and oncology-specific needs provide a credible basis for pilot testing within FDA and IMDRF workflows. Wider adoption and iterative refinement could lead to formalised guidance, enhance regulatory consistency, reduce postmarket usability failures and improve patient trust, engagement and outcomes.

Limitations

Despite its potential utility, this work has several limitations. First, the proposed benchmark is conceptual and has not yet been empirically validated through regulatory pilots or real-world evaluations of SaMD systems. Future work should test the framework in practical settings, such as regulatory review processes, institutional procurement assessments or evaluations of existing digital health applications. Second, the scoring model involves assigning feature weights and performance scores, which may introduce subjectivity depending on the evaluator, regulatory context or cultural setting in which the SaMD is deployed. Developing standardised guidance or multistakeholder evaluation processes may help improve consistency. Finally, while feature-based checklists can support systematic assessment, there is a risk that rigid application could lead to superficial compliance rather than meaningful implementation of UCD principles. For this reason, the benchmark should be interpreted as a guiding framework that complements rather than replaces deeper HCD practices and user engagement.

Future work

Future research should focus on empirically validating the proposed benchmark in real-world digital health contexts. One important direction is testing the framework within regulatory or institutional evaluation workflows, such as FDA review processes, IMDRF-informed regulatory assessments or healthcare procurement procedures for SaMD tools. Comparative studies could also examine inter-rater reliability by assessing whether different reviewers or regulatory experts assign consistent scores when applying the benchmark. Additionally, future work may investigate how adoption of such benchmarks influences design practices, user trust, usability and clinical outcomes over time. These efforts would help translate the framework from a conceptual proposal into an operational tool that supports more consistent and user-centred evaluation of digital health technologies.

Conclusion

To ensure that SaMD fulfils its clinical promise while meaningfully addressing patient needs, particularly in mission-critical domains such as cancer care, regulatory frameworks must expand beyond technical validation to include structured, premarket review of usability, accessibility and emotional appropriateness. Current standards, including those from IMDRF and FDA, provide critical safety and performance requirements but do not yet operationalise UCD in measurable and enforceable ways.

This perspective paper proposes a feature-based benchmark as a practical, developer-facing tool that also informs regulatory review before market clearance. By aligning SaMD evaluation with established human factors guidelines, such as the ISO 9241–210 series15 and addressing end-user needs, the benchmark offers a concrete path toward more inclusive, effective and ethical digital health technologies. We envision this benchmark serving as a foundation for collaborative validation studies and consensus-building efforts across oncology and digital health communities. Its adoption could enhance regulatory consistency, reduce postmarket usability failures and ultimately improve patient trust, engagement and outcomes. Future work should pilot the benchmark within regulatory or institutional workflows to support its formal integration as a standard design quality tool for SaMD.
