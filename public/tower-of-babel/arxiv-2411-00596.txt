A mathematical model for inverse freeform design of a point-to-point two-reflector system Journal: opticajournal Publication type: Research Article
P. A. Braam, 1,* J. H. M. ten Thije Boonkkamp, 1 M. J. H. Anthonissen, 1 R. Beltman, 2 and W. L. IJzerman 1,2

Abstract
In this paper, we discuss a mathematical model for inverse freeform design of an optical system with two reflectors in which light transfers from a point source to a point target. In this model, the angular light intensity emitted from the point source and illuminance arriving at the point target are specified by distributions. To determine the optical mapping and the shape of the reflectors, we use the optical path length and take energy conservation into account, through which we obtain a generated Jacobian equation. We express the system in both spherical and stereographic coordinates, and solve it using a sophisticated least-squares algorithm. Several examples illustrate the algorithm’s capabilities to tackle complicated light distributions.

Introduction

The goal in non-imaging optics is to design optical surfaces, such as reflectors and lenses, that convert a source light distribution to a target. To achieve this, many methodologies are used among which forward and inverse methods
[
1
,

However, in complex optical systems, these methods often require manual adjustments of system parameters and rely on iterative techniques to gradually improve performance, whereas inverse methods use principles of geometrical optics and conservation of energy to compute the shapes of optical surfaces directly.

One approach to compute freeform surfaces in optical design is to solve a partial differential equation taking the form of a standard Monge-Ampère equation, a generalized Monge-Ampère equation or a generated Jacobian equation. We give a brief summary of literature on this approach; for a detailed overview, see Romijn
et al.

[

[

]
and Brix

used a collocation method with iteration techniques on a nonlinear solver
[

11

. Moreover, Caboussat
et al.

[

[

[

In non-imaging systems we can distinguish 16 base optical systems that can be combined to create more complex optical configurations
[
16
]
. These systems have two types of sources: sources where light originates from a parallel beam and point sources. The light then traverses through either a reflector or a lens system. Finally, it reaches a desired target domain, which is located in one of four target areas: a region reached by a parallel beam, a point target, a near-field target or a far-field target. The latter two areas indicate that the target area is located relatively close or far from the optical surface, compared to the size of the optical system.

This paper focuses on a specific type of optical system: the point-to-point two-reflector system, which is illustrated in Fig.
1
. In this system, we are interested in finding the shape of the reflectors that transfer light emitted from a point source with a specific angular light distribution to a point target with a desired angular light distribution. Fig.
1

Notably, two reflectors are necessary to both redirect the light rays to the point target and meet the required light distribution in this point. This specific system can for instance be used in single-mode fiber optics to convert the light distribution at a source point to a different one using reflectors. We compute the reflectors in the system by applying a two-stage least-squares algorithm. In the first stage, the algorithm computes the optical ray mapping, defining where each light ray originating from the source will end up at the target, and in the second stage the shapes of the reflectors are determined.

2.2
Optical path length

We aim to describe the reflector system using an equation of the form

\displaystyle v_{1}(\hat{\bm{s}})+v_{2}(\hat{\bm{t}})=c(\hat{\bm{s}},\hat{\bm{t}}),

(2)

to describe how light rays from point source
\mathcal{S}
end up at point target
\mathcal{T}
. In this equation,
v_{1}(\hat{\bm{s}})
is a function related to the shape of the first reflector,
v_{2}(\hat{\bm{t}})
to the second reflector and
c(\hat{\bm{s}},\hat{\bm{t}})
is the cost function
[
17
,
18
]
. To derive Eq. (
2
), we introduce the optical path length

\displaystyle V=u_{1}(\hat{\bm{s}})+d+u_{2}(\hat{\bm{t}}),

(3)

which is constant by the principle of equal optical path length, also known as the theorem of Malus and Dupin
[
19
]
. Here,
d
can be expressed as

\displaystyle d

\displaystyle=|\ell\hat{\bm{e}}_{z}-u_{1}(\hat{\bm{s}})\hat{\bm{s}}-u_{2}(\hat{\bm{t}})\hat{\bm{t}}|.

(4)

By squaring Eq. (
3
), substituting Eq. (
4
), and subsequently simplifying the resulting expression, we obtain

\displaystyle V^{2}=

\displaystyle\;u_{1}^{2}(\hat{\bm{s}})+d^{2}+u_{2}^{2}(\hat{\bm{t}})+2u_{1}(\hat{\bm{s}})u_{2}(\hat{\bm{t}})+2du_{1}({\hat{\bm{s}}})+2du_{2}(\hat{\bm{t}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\;\ell^{2}+2u_{1}(\hat{\bm{s}})(V-\ell\hat{\bm{e}}_{z}\bm{\cdot}\hat{\bm{s}})+2u_{2}(\hat{\bm{t}})(V-\ell\hat{\bm{e}}_{z}\bm{\cdot}\hat{\bm{t}})-2u_{1}(\hat{\bm{s}})u_{2}(\hat{\bm{t}})(1-\hat{\bm{s}}\bm{\cdot}\hat{\bm{t}}),

which can be factorized as

\displaystyle\left(\frac{V^{2}-\ell^{2}}{2u_{1}(\hat{\bm{s}})(V-\ell\hat{\bm{e}}_{z}\bm{\cdot}\hat{\bm{s}})}-1\right)\left(\frac{V^{2}-\ell^{2}}{2u_{2}(\hat{\bm{t}})(V-\ell\hat{\bm{e}}_{z}\bm{\cdot}\hat{\bm{t}})}-1\right)=1-\frac{V^{2}-\ell^{2}}{2}\cdot\frac{1-\hat{\bm{s}}\bm{\cdot}\hat{\bm{t}}}{(V-\ell\hat{\bm{e}}_{z}\bm{\cdot}\hat{\bm{s}})(V-\ell\hat{\bm{e}}_{z}\bm{\cdot}\hat{\bm{t}})}.

(5)

By taking the logarithm on both sides, we obtain Eq. (
2
) with

\displaystyle v_{1}(\hat{\bm{s}})

\displaystyle=\log\left(\frac{V^{2}-\ell^{2}}{2u_{1}(\hat{\bm{s}})(V-\ell\hat{\bm{e}}_{z}\bm{\cdot}\hat{\bm{s}})}-1\right),

(6a)

\displaystyle v_{2}(\hat{\bm{t}})

\displaystyle=\log\left(\frac{V^{2}-\ell^{2}}{2u_{2}(\hat{\bm{t}})(V-\ell\hat{\bm{e}}_{z}\bm{\cdot}\hat{\bm{t}})}-1\right),

(6b)

\displaystyle c(\hat{\bm{s}},\hat{\bm{t}})

\displaystyle=\log\left(1-\frac{V^{2}-\ell^{2}}{2}\cdot\frac{1-\hat{\bm{s}}\bm{\cdot}\hat{\bm{t}}}{(V-\ell\hat{\bm{e}}_{z}\bm{\cdot}\hat{\bm{s}})(V-\ell\hat{\bm{e}}_{z}\bm{\cdot}\hat{\bm{t}})}\right).

(6c)

In Appendix
A
we show that the arguments of the logarithms are strictly positive.

Next, we aim to rewrite Eq. (
2
) in terms of stereographic coordinates. Using the expressions in Eq. (
1c
), the inner product of
\hat{\bm{s}}
and
\hat{\bm{t}}

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{s}}\bm{\cdot}\hat{\bm{t}}

\displaystyle=1-\frac{2|\bm{x}-\bm{y}|^{2}}{(1+|\bm{x}|^{2})(1+|\bm{y}|^{2})}.

Consequently, we can write Eqs. (

\displaystyle v_{1}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\log\left(\frac{(V^{2}-\ell^{2})(1+|\bm{x}|^{2})}{2u_{1}(\bm{x})\kappa_{1}(\bm{x})}-1\right),

\displaystyle v_{2}(\bm{y})

\displaystyle=\log\left(\frac{(V^{2}-\ell^{2})(1+|\bm{y}|^{2})}{2u_{2}(\bm{y})\kappa_{2}(\bm{y})}-1\right),

(7b)

\displaystyle c(\bm{x},\bm{y})

\displaystyle=\log\left(1-\frac{\kappa_{0}(\bm{x},\bm{y})}{\kappa_{1}(\bm{x})\kappa_{2}(\bm{y})}\right),

(7c)

\displaystyle\kappa_{0}(\bm{x},\bm{y})

\displaystyle=(V^{2}-\ell^{2})|\bm{x}-\bm{y}|^{2},

\displaystyle=V-\ell+(V-\ell)|\bm{x}|^{2},

2.3
Energy conservation

We will now derive the energy balance in terms of stereographic coordinates
\bm{x}
and
\bm{y}=\bm{m}(\bm{x})

[
13
,
20
]
, where
\bm{m}=\bm{m}(\bm{x})
is the mapping that specifies how each of the light rays at the point source
\mathcal{S}
will end up at the point target
\mathcal{T}
. We assume that
\mathcal{S}
emits light with distribution
f(\bm{x})
for
\bm{x}
in the stereographic source domain
\mathcal{X}
, and maps this light to
\mathcal{T}
with distribution
g(\bm{y})
for
\bm{y}
in the stereographic target domain
\mathcal{Y}
via the reflector system given in Fig.
2
. Energy conservation then gives

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathcal{A}}f(\bm{x})\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{S}(\bm{x})=\iint_{\bm{m}(\mathcal{A})}g(\bm{y})\;\mathrm{d}\mathcal{S}(\bm{y}),

for arbitrary
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathcal{X}
. We can write this equation as

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathcal{A}}f(\bm{x})\left|\frac{\partial\hat{\bm{s}}}{\partial x_{1}}\bm{\times}\frac{\partial\hat{\bm{s}}}{\partial x_{2}}\right|\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=\iint_{\bm{m}(\mathcal{A})}g(\bm{y})\left|\frac{\partial\hat{\bm{t}}}{\partial y_{1}}\bm{\times}\frac{\partial\hat{\bm{t}}}{\partial y_{2}}\right|\mathrm{d}\bm{y}.

Using Eq. (
1c
), this simplifies to

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathcal{A}}\frac{4f(\bm{x})}{(1+|\bm{x}|^{2})^{2}}\;\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=\iint_{\bm{m}(\mathcal{A})}\frac{4g(\bm{y})}{(1+|\bm{y}|^{2})^{2}}\;\mathrm{d}\bm{y},

and substituting the mapping
\bm{y}=\bm{m}(\bm{x})
on the right-hand side, we find

\displaystyle\iint_{\mathcal{A}}\frac{4f(\bm{x})}{(1+|\bm{x}|^{2})^{2}}\;\mathrm{d}\bm{x}

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathcal{A}}\frac{4g(\bm{m}(\bm{x}))}{(1+|\bm{m}(\bm{x})|^{2})^{2}}\cdot|\text{det}(\text{D}\bm{m}(\bm{x}))|\;\mathrm{d}\bm{x},

where
\text{D}\bm{m}(\bm{x})
denotes the Jacobian of mapping
\bm{m}
. Assuming
\text{det}(\text{D}\bm{m}(\bm{x}))>0
, we obtain the generated Jacobian equation

\displaystyle\text{det}(\text{D}\bm{m}(\bm{x}))

\displaystyle=\frac{(1+|\bm{m}(\bm{x})|^{2})^{2}}{(1+|\bm{x}|^{2})^{2}}\cdot\frac{f(\bm{x})}{g(\bm{m}(\bm{x}))}=:F(\bm{x},\bm{m}(\bm{x})).

(9)

In addition to the energy balance, we impose the transport boundary condition

\displaystyle\bm{m}(\partial\mathcal{X})=\partial\mathcal{Y},

(10)

which ensures that the boundary of the source domain is mapped to the boundary of the target domain, and therefore that all the light from the source will be transferred to the target
[
15
]
.

2.4
Convex solution

Eq. (
8
) gives a relation between the two reflectors and the mapping with infinitely many solutions for
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
. We take a unique solution by enforcing
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
to form a
c
-convex or
c
-concave pair
[
14
,
21
,
22
]
. In the
c
-convex solution pair,
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
are given by

\displaystyle v_{1}(\bm{x})=\max_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}(c(\bm{x},\bm{y})-v_{2}(\bm{y})),

\displaystyle v_{2}(\bm{y})=\max_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}(c(\bm{x},\bm{y})-v_{1}(\bm{x})).

Likewise, the
c
-concave solution pair
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
is defined by

\displaystyle v_{1}(\bm{x})=\min_{\bm{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}(c(\bm{x},\bm{y})-v_{2}(\bm{y})),

\displaystyle v_{2}(\bm{y})=\min_{\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X}}(c(\bm{x},\bm{y})-v_{1}(\bm{x})).

In both cases,
c(\cdot,\bm{y})-v_{1}
has a stationary point and therefore we require

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{x}}c(\bm{x},\bm{y})-\nabla v_{1}(\bm{x})=\bm{0}.

(11)

By the implicit function theorem, this equation provides a mapping
\bm{y}=\bm{m}(\bm{x})
under the condition that the matrix

\displaystyle\bm{C}=\bm{C}(\bm{x},\bm{y})=\text{D}_{\bm{xy}}c=\left(\frac{\partial^{2}c(\bm{x},\bm{y})}{\partial x_{i}\,\partial y_{j}}\right),

is invertible, which is true for Eq. (
7c
). Next, we substitute
\bm{y}=\bm{m}(\bm{x})
into Eq. (
11
) and take the derivative with respect to
\bm{x}
to obtain

\displaystyle\bm{C}\text{D}\bm{m}=\text{D}^{2}v_{1}-\text{D}_{\bm{xx}}c=:\bm{P}.

(12)

Notably, for a
c-
convex pair we require that the matrix
\bm{P}
is symmetric positive definite (SPD)
[
7
]
and by Eq. (
9
) that
\bm{P}
satisfies

\displaystyle\text{det}(\bm{P}(\bm{x}))=F(\bm{x},\bm{m}(\bm{x}))\text{det}(\bm{C}(\bm{x},\bm{m}(\bm{x}))).

(13)

In summary, we have to solve Eq. (
12
) subject to the constraint (
13
) and the transport boundary condition (
10
) for the mapping
\bm{m}(\bm{x})
. Subsequently, we have to solve Eq. (
11
) for
v_{1}(\bm{x})
and finally obtain
u_{1}(\bm{x})
and
u_{2}(\bm{y})
using Eqs. (
7a
) and (
7b
).

3
The least-squares algorithm

In this section we will outline the least-squares algorithm to simulate the point-to-point two-reflector system. In the least-squares algorithm, Eq. (
12
) is solved by minimizing the functional

\displaystyle J_{I}[\bm{m},\bm{P}]=\tfrac{1}{2}\iint_{\mathcal{X}}||\bm{C}\text{D}\bm{m}-\bm{P}||^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\bm{x}

(14)

over
\bm{P}
, where
\bm{m}
is fixed and where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the Frobenius norm. Additionally, the transport boundary condition (
10
) is imposed by minimizing the functional

\displaystyle J_{B}[\bm{m},\bm{b}]=\tfrac{1}{2}\oint_{\partial\mathcal{X}}|\bm{m}-\bm{b}|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\bm{x}

(15)

over
\bm{b}:\partial\mathcal{X}\to\partial\mathcal{Y}
, where
\bm{m}
is fixed. Finally, the least-squares algorithm calculates the mapping
\bm{y}=\bm{m}(\bm{x})
by minimizing the functional

\displaystyle J[\bm{m},\bm{P},\bm{b}]=\alpha J_{I}[\bm{m},\bm{P}]+(1-\alpha)J_{B}[\bm{m},\bm{b}],

(16)

over
\bm{m}
where
\alpha\in(0,1)
is a weighting factor. The algorithm approximates the mapping
\bm{m}
by starting from an initial mapping
\bm{m}^{0}
, which maps the source domain
\mathcal{X}
uniformly to the target domain
\mathcal{Y}
, and then iterates according to

\displaystyle\bm{b}^{n+1}

\displaystyle=\underset{\bm{b}\in\mathcal{B}}{\mathrm{argmin}}\;J_{B}[\bm{m}^{n},\bm{b}],

(17a)

\displaystyle\bm{P}^{n+1}

\displaystyle=\underset{\bm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}{\mathrm{argmin}}\;J_{I}[\bm{m}^{n},\bm{P}],

(17b)

\displaystyle\bm{m}^{n+1}

\displaystyle=\underset{\bm{m}\in\mathcal{M}}{\mathrm{argmin}}\;J[\bm{m},\bm{P}^{n+1},\bm{b}^{n+1}],

(17c)

for
n=0,1,2,..
, where the function spaces are defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}

\displaystyle=\{\bm{b}\in C^{1}(\partial\mathcal{X})^{2}\mid\bm{b}(\bm{x})\in\partial\mathcal{Y}\},

(17d)

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}

\displaystyle=\{\bm{P}\in C^{1}(\mathcal{X})^{2\times 2}\mid\bm{P}\text{ is SPD, }\text{det}(\bm{P}(\bm{x}))=F(\bm{x},\bm{m}(\bm{x}))\text{det}(\bm{C}(\bm{x},\bm{m}(\bm{x})))\},

(17e)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}

\displaystyle=C^{2}(\mathcal{X})^{2}.

(17f)

A more in-depth analysis can be found in
[
7
,
21
,
23
]
.

Upon convergence of (
17
), we compute
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
by substituting
\bm{m}
in Eq. (
11
). A solution for
v_{1}
can then be found by minimizing the functional

\displaystyle I[v_{1}]=\tfrac{1}{2}\int_{\mathcal{X}}|\nabla v_{1}(\bm{x})-\nabla_{\bm{x}}c(\bm{x},\bm{m}(\bm{x}))|^{2}\;\mathrm{d}\bm{x}.

(18)

By setting the first variation of this functional equal to zero, using Gauss’s Theorem and the Fundamental Lemma of Calculus of Variations, we obtain the Neumann boundary value problem

\displaystyle\Delta v_{1}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\nabla\bm{\cdot}\nabla_{\bm{x}}c(\bm{x},\bm{m}),

\displaystyle\bm{x}\in\mathcal{X},

(19a)

\displaystyle\nabla v_{1}(\bm{x})\bm{\cdot}\hat{\bm{n}}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\bm{x}}c(\bm{x},\bm{m})\bm{\cdot}\hat{\bm{n}},

\displaystyle\bm{x}\in\partial\mathcal{X},

(19b)

where
\bm{\hat{n}}
is the unit outward normal on the boundary of the source domain. Note that
v_{1}
is determined up to an additive constant. We discretize this system for
v_{1}
on a grid by using a finite volume method resulting in the linear system
\bm{A}\bm{v}=\bm{b}
. Since the boundary value problem (
19
) has multiple solutions, we enforce a unique solution by setting the average distance from
\mathcal{S}
to
\mathcal{R}_{1}
equal to some
h\in\mathbb{R}
[
7
]
. In Sec.
4.1
we show that we can fix this distance for a specific point instead. Finally, by rewriting Eq. (
7a
) in the form

\displaystyle u_{1}(\bm{x})=\frac{(V^{2}-\ell^{2})(1+|\bm{x}|^{2})}{2\kappa_{1}(\bm{x})(e^{v_{1}(\bm{x})}+1)},

we obtain the distance from the point source
\mathcal{S}
to the first reflector
\mathcal{R}_{1}
at every grid point. Likewise, we have

\displaystyle u_{2}(\bm{y})=\frac{(V^{2}-\ell^{2})(1+|\bm{y}|^{2})}{2\kappa_{2}(\bm{y})(e^{v_{2}(\bm{y})}+1)},

where
v_{2}
is obtained using Eq. (
8
). Thus, for every ray of light with stereographic coordinates
\bm{x}
we know its mapping
\bm{y}=\bm{m}(\bm{x})
, the distance
u_{1}
to the first reflector and the distance
u_{2}
between the second reflector and point target
\mathcal{T}
. Therefore, the shape of the reflectors can be calculated by using the inverse projections (
1c
) and the parameterizations of the reflectors.

5
Conclusion

In this paper we presented an inverse method for a reflector system where light originates from a point source, then propagates via two reflectors and finally ends up at a point target with a desired incoming light intensity. We introduced stereographic coordinates to describe the rays of light at the source and then used the generated Jacobian equation in combination with a least-squares algorithm to calculate the optical mapping and find the shapes of the reflector surfaces. Finally, we discussed how the height of a reflector can be fixed for a specific ray of light and presented numerical examples. Overall, the results advance our understanding of how the shape of the reflectors are obtained using an inverse method. Future research could use the least-squares algorithm to model other base optical systems
[
16
]
and include physical phenomenon such as scattering effects
[
25
]
.

Sign check of the terms in Eq. (
6
)

We show that the arguments of the logarithms in Eqs. (
6

b<d+u_{2}(\hat{\bm{t}})
where
b
denotes the distance between
P_{1}
and
\mathcal{T}

\mathcal{S}P_{1}\mathcal{T}

\displaystyle u_{1}(\hat{\bm{s}})^{2}+\ell^{2}-2u_{1}(\hat{\bm{s}})\ell\hat{\bm{s}}\bm{\cdot}\hat{\bm{e}}=b^{2}<(d+u_{2}(\hat{\bm{t}}))^{2}.

Next, by rewriting Eq. (

d+u_{2}(\hat{\bm{t}})=V-u_{1}(\hat{\bm{s}})

\displaystyle 0<\frac{V^{2}-\ell^{2}}{2Vu_{1}(\hat{\bm{s}})-2u_{1}(\hat{\bm{s}})\ell\hat{\bm{s}}\bm{\cdot}\hat{\bm{e}}}-1.

Therefore, the argument of the logarithm in Eq. (
