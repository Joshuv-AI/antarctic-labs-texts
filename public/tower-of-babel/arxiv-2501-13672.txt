Numerical Analysis of differential equations on weighted Sobolev spaces: beyond classical orthogonal polynomials
Maxime Breden Hugo Chu

Abstract
We lay mathematical foundations for the Numerical Analysis of differential equations on Sobolev spaces weighted by a Gibbs probability measure ν ⁡ ( d ​ x ) = e − V ⁡ ( x ) ​ d ​ x / 𝒵 \nu(\mathrm{d}x)=e^{-V(x)}\mathrm{d}x/\mathcal{Z} on the real line. Over recent decades, the Functional Analysis of these spaces has been thoroughly developed to study Schrödinger-type equations and diffusion processes. While such equations should therefore be amenable to a numerical resolution with respect to orthogonal polynomials, this feat has only ever been achieved with respect to classical bases. We bridge this gap by showing that such equations can be solved with respect to suitable bases by factorising their leading linear component. In particular, we propose a new natural notion of Sobolev orthogonal polynomials, simpler and more tractable than those arising from the usual Sobolev inner product. In the case of V V being an even polynomial, we further establish quantitative estimates for the compactness of the embedding H 1 ​ ( ν ) ↪ L 2 ​ ( ν ) H^{1}(\nu)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\nu) , uncovering a connection with the growth of the Jacobi recurrence coefficients, which are solutions of corresponding Painlevé-type discrete equations. As an application, we rigorously and tightly enclose solutions of the Gross–Pitaevskii equation with sextic potential and rigorously demonstrate the phenomenon of stochastic resonance via a computer-assisted proof.

Introduction

For an even potential
V\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
growing at least linearly at infinity, consider the Gibbs probability measure
\nu(\mathrm{d}x):=e^{-V}\mathrm{d}x/\mathcal{Z}
. It is then well known
[
45
]
that one may construct a Hilbert basis of orthonormal polynomials
\mathcal{P}=\{p_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
on
(L^{2}(\nu),\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
, that is

\langle p_{n},p_{m}\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{n}p_{m}\mathrm{d}\nu=\delta_{mn},\qquad\mathcal{Z}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-V(x)}\mathrm{d}x.

Orthogonal polynomials
[
1
,
40
,
56
,
85
,
88
]
are among the oldest and most fundamental objects in Numerical Analysis, and much is known about their asymptotic behaviour, zeroes, relations with special functions and random matrices, etc. Moreover, they admit a simple construction via a three-term recurrence relation

xp_{n}=a_{n+1}p_{n+1}+a_{n}p_{n-1},\qquad n\geq 1.

(1)

However, somewhat disappointingly, almost all the resolutions of differential equations using orthogonal polynomials rely on the classical families (Fourier, Hermite, Jacobi and Laguerre)
[
44
]
. The main reason is that these classical families have the property that the derivative of a classical orthogonal polynomial is a polynomial in the same family. Therefore, these bases allow for a simple representation of the differentiation operator
\partial_{x}
(typically diagonal, maybe up to a shift), which makes them very convenient for the study of differential equations.

Nevertheless, even if
\nu
is not a classical weight, it is known
[
8
,
5
]
that if
V
grows fast enough at infinity, the Sobolev embedding
H^{1}(\nu)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\nu)
is compact (one actually expects that the faster
V
grows, the better the compactness). In particular, such compactness results have been useful in the pen-and-paper analysis of (possibly nonlinear) Fokker–Planck equations
[
46
]
and Schrödinger type equations
[
68
]
. These equations can be reformulated
[
86
]
as

\mathcal{L}u=f(u),\qquad\mathcal{L}:=V^{\prime}(x)\partial_{x}-\partial_{xx},

(2)

which is then naturally posed in
H^{1}(\nu)
, where
\mathcal{L}
is also a well-studied operator in Probability Theory
[
5
,
67
,
86
]
(as the generator of a diffusion process). In principle, the compactness of the embedding
H^{1}(\nu)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\nu)
also allows one to approximate compact operators like
\mathcal{L}^{-1}
on these spaces with finite-dimensional matrices on the computer, and therefore to numerically solve such equations.
Ideally, for both theoretical and practical purposes, we would like
\mathcal{L}
to be diagonal with respect to the associated orthonormal polynomial basis
\mathcal{P}=\{p_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
. Unfortunately, it is established
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
77
]
that this only occurs if the
p_{n}
’s belong to a classical family, suggesting that working with respect to non-classical weights is quite more challenging.

The goal of this work is to overcome the apparent hurdles of making use of non-classical orthogonal polynomials, and to lay some mathematical foundations for the numerical resolution of problems of the form (
2
) when
V
is not necessarily quadratic. We will show that such bases, because well-adapted to the problem, can be remarkably successful at solving problems of the form (
2
), clearly outperforming some classical bases such as Hermite polynomials.

A Numerical Analysis on weighted Sobolev spaces

Numerical methods for differential equations often require solutions to be sought in function spaces with some regularity, such as
H^{1}(\nu)
, and thus a Galerkin representation with respect to bases of such spaces. When
V(x)=x^{2}/2
, the
p_{n}
’s (which are then Hermite polynomials) enjoy the convenient property that they remain orthogonal with respect to
H^{1}(\nu)
-inner products of the form

\int_{\mathbb{R}}u^{\prime}v^{\prime}\mathrm{d}\nu+\Lambda\int_{\mathbb{R}}uv\mathrm{d}\nu,\qquad\Lambda>0.

(3)

Unfortunately, as soon as
\nu
is not a classical weight, the
p_{n}
’s are no longer orthogonal for such
H^{1}(\nu)
-inner products.
There has therefore been a great effort since the 1960s to construct polynomial bases orthogonal with respect to (
3
), so-called Sobolev orthogonal polynomials, leading to a very rich literature (see
[
62
,
73
,
74
,
75
,
90
]
and references therein). While this is a fundamental question, an immediate problem one encounters is that these polynomials are not easy to construct in practice
[
55
,
90
]
, and that they depend nontrivially on the chosen value of
\Lambda

[
74
]
. This has led to many algebraic studies of these bases, but their use has remained limited and impractical in their original intended field of application: the resolution of differential equations. In 2015, Marcellán and Xu write in their seminal survey
[
74
]
:

“It is now time to go back to the beginning, studying the Fourier expansions and approximation by polynomials in Sobolev spaces, […] to solve problems in other fields. We end this survey with this call of action.”

It seems this call of action has yet to be heeded, presumably in part because these Sobolev orthogonal polynomials prove difficult to work with in practice for studying expansions, getting a priori truncation error estimates, and therefore solving differential equations. We propose in this work a very simple but seemingly unexplored alternative, which is to use a different scalar product on
H^{1}(\nu)
, rooted in the functional analysis of weighted Sobolev spaces. One of the motivations for our construction is to obtain orthogonal polynomials on
H^{1}(\nu)
which relate in a simple way to the orthogonal polynomials
p_{n}
on
L^{2}(\nu)
. A naive but natural approach would therefore be to take
\Lambda=0
in (
3
), because then the antiderivatives of the
p_{n}
’s become orthogonal. However, (
3
) is not a scalar product on
H^{1}(\nu)
when
\Lambda=0
(all the constants would have norm zero), which stems from the fact that there does not hold a classical Poincaré inequality on
H^{1}(\nu)
: the semi-norm
\|\partial_{x}\cdot\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}
does not dominate the
L^{2}(\nu)
-norm
\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}
. Fortunately, for a wide class of potentials
V
(see Proposition
11
) a modified Poincaré inequality holds on
H^{1}(\nu)
:

\mbox{there exists }C_{P}>0,\qquad\int_{\mathbb{R}}u^{2}\mathrm{d}\nu-\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}u\mathrm{d}\nu\right)^{2}\leq C_{P}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(u^{\prime})^{2}\mathrm{d}\nu\quad\mbox{for all }u\in H^{1}(\nu).

(PI)

From now on, we will simply refer to (
PI
) as the Poincaré inequality on
H^{1}(\nu)
, a fundamental tool in the Functional Analysis of weighted Sobolev spaces and their associated equations and stochastic processes
[
18
,
5
,
86
]
. As soon as (
PI
) holds, we can consider the following inner product on
H^{1}(\nu)

(u,v):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}u^{\prime}v^{\prime}\mathrm{d}\nu+\int_{\mathbb{R}}u\mathrm{d}\nu\int_{\mathbb{R}}v\mathrm{d}\nu,

(4)

whose induced norm (henceforth denoted
\|\cdot\|_{H^{1}(\nu)}
) can be shown to be Lipschitz equivalent to the standard norm on
H^{1}(\nu)
; demonstrating that
(H^{1}(\nu),(\cdot,\cdot))
is indeed a Hilbert space.

While this new inner product (
4
) is not of the form (
3
) and may thus appear unusual at first sight, it is actually very natural; for instance, it reduces to the inner product
\langle\partial_{x}\cdot,\partial_{x}\cdot\rangle
on the subspace of
\nu
-mean zero functions of
H^{1}(\nu)
. Moreover, the construction of orthogonal polynomials
\mathcal{Q}=\{q_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
on
H^{1}(\nu)
with respect to
(\cdot,\cdot)
becomes very simple and elegant.

Proposition 1
.

Assume the potential
V
is such that (
PI
) holds. For all
n\geq 1
, the polynomials
\mathcal{Q}=\{q_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
orthonormal with respect to
(\cdot,\cdot)
are uniquely defined
by the properties

q_{n}^{\prime}=p_{n-1}\qquad\textrm{and}\qquad\int_{\mathbb{R}}q_{n}\mathrm{d}\nu=0.

Proof.

The
q_{n}
’s are clearly orthonormal with respect to
(\cdot,\cdot)
, and the uniqueness (up to sign) follows from the Gram–Schmidt algorithm.
∎

While the basis
\mathcal{Q}=\{q_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is not orthogonal with respect to an inner product of the form (
3
), we will from now on refer to the
q_{n}
’s as Sobolev orthogonal polynomials.

Our goal is to use the bases
\mathcal{P}
and/or
\mathcal{Q}
in order to solve problems of the form (
2
). The following properties show that these bases are indeed well suited, both for numerical and for theoretical purposes, even though they do not make
\mathcal{L}
diagonal.

Proposition 2
.

Let
\mathcal{P}^{*}=\mathcal{P}\backslash\{1\}=\{p_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
and
Q^{*}=\mathcal{Q}\backslash\{1\}=\{q_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}
and let
D_{>0}=[\partial_{x}]_{\mathcal{P}^{*}\to\mathcal{P}}
denote the Galerkin representation of
\partial_{x}
with respect to
\mathcal{P}^{*}
and
\mathcal{P}
, which is upper triangular. Then the Galerkin representations of
\mathcal{L}=V^{\prime}\partial_{x}-\partial_{xx}
have the following factorisations

[\mathcal{L}]_{\mathcal{P}^{*}\to\mathcal{P}^{*}}=D_{>0}^{T}D_{>0},\qquad[\mathcal{L}]_{\mathcal{Q}^{*}\to\mathcal{Q}^{*}}=D_{>0}D_{>0}^{T},\qquad\text{and}\qquad[\mathcal{L}]_{\mathcal{Q}^{*}\to\mathcal{P}^{*}}=D_{>0}^{T}.

Furthermore, if
V
is an even polynomial of degree
2k
, then
D_{>0}
is banded with
k
non-zero superdiagonals, and its entries have an explicit dependence on the coefficients
(a_{n})_{n\geq 0}
of the three-term recurrence relation (
1
) (with the
a_{n}
’s themselves satisfying a polynomial recurrence relation).

The first decomposition, which provides us with the Cholesky (or LU) decomposition of
\mathcal{L}
with respect to
\mathcal{P}
, is in fact simply the matrix reformulation of the well-known identity
\langle\mathcal{L}u,v\rangle=\langle\partial_{x}u,\partial_{x}v\rangle
. The second one is more remarkable, as it provides us with a
reverse Cholesky
(or UL) decomposition of
\mathcal{L}
with respect to
\mathcal{Q}
. Moreover, in the case where
V
is an even polynomial of degree
2k
, we show in this work that the banded triangular operator
D_{>0}
can be inverted rather explicitly, provided we are able to quantify the growth of the coefficients
(a_{n})_{n\geq 0}
appearing in the three-term recurrence relation (
1
). This shall then enable the “inversion” of
\mathcal{L}
, estimation of the Poincaré constant
C_{P}
in (
PI
) and more importantly to quantify the compactness of the embedding
H^{1}(\nu)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\nu)
(or equivalently of “
\mathcal{L}^{-1}
”) which is crucial to justify the numerical resolution of PDEs of the form (
2
) on weighted Sobolev spaces. This establishes a
direct connection
between the growth of the sequence
a_{n}
(for which we actually have
a_{n}\asymp n^{1/{2k}}

[
39
]
) and the optimal rate of compactness/approximation estimates on
H^{1}(\nu)
(see Theorem
6
).

In this paper, for clarity, and motivated by the examples driving this work, we mostly focus on the case where
V
is a quartic (or double-well) potential, i.e.

V(x)=\frac{x^{4}}{4}-\kappa\frac{x^{2}}{2},\qquad\kappa\in\mathbb{R}.

(5)

The treatment of this quartic case represents the first step departing from the classical paradigm (for which
D_{>0}
is diagonal). We will show that this quartic case already leads to a rich theory bringing together several ideas in Numerical and Functional Analysis. However, we stress that analogous ideas also enable us to treat the general case of
V
being a polynomial of degree
2k
. We elaborate on this in Section
3.2
, where we show that inverting
D_{>0}
and studying its entries reduces to proving the invertibility of a Toeplitz matrix, which we achieve by proving the positivity of a finite Taylor series.

Our approach to give a thorough and precise Numerical Analysis on the Sobolev space
H^{1}(\nu)
will rely on exploiting the structure of the bidiagonal differentiation operator
D_{>0}
, and some of our estimates are inspired from the related work
[
21
]
on tridiagonal operators. Our framework enables the computation of tight enclosures for the optimal Poincaré constant
C_{P}
on
H^{1}(\nu)
, as stated in the following theorem.

Theorem 3
.

Consider
V(x)=x^{4}/4-\kappa x^{2}/2
with
\kappa=4
. The best Poincaré inequality constant
C_{P}
in (
PI
) is given by

C_{P}=\|D_{>0}^{-1}\|_{\ell^{2}\to\ell^{2}}^{2}=33.58004242\pm 2.3\times 10^{-7}.

(6)

Note that, in general, even giving a non-sharp upper bound on
C_{P}
is not trivial
[
5
,
16
,
18
,
19
,
65
,
95
]
especially when
V
is not convex (which is the case if
\kappa>0
). In contrast, in this particular case, we are able to give a tight enclosure for
C_{P}
.

In fact, we can do much better by considering projections of the operator
D_{>0}^{-1}
, and explicitly quantify the compactness of the embedding
H^{1}(\nu)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\nu)
, a crucial estimate to justify the spectral approach with respect to the basis
\mathcal{Q}=\{q_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.

Theorem 4
.

Consider
V(x)=x^{4}/4-\kappa x^{2}/2
. There exist
C>0
and
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geq N

\|u\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}\leq\frac{C}{n^{3/4}}\|u\|_{H^{1}(\nu)},\qquad\mbox{for all $u\in\overline{\mathrm{Span}\{q_{j}\}_{j>n}}^{H^{1}(\nu)}$}.

(7)

In particular, if
\kappa=4
, the above holds for
C=1.63
and
N=50
.

Remark 5
.

The explicit value of
C
obtained in Theorem
4
, which influences the proofs of some statement to come, like Theorem
8
and
9
, is not sharp in the asymptotic regime
n\to\infty
. If need be, one could obtain a smaller constant
C
, by taking
N
larger.

It is well-established
[
2
,
23
,
28
,
34
,
52
,
83
]
that estimates like Theorem
4
are useful in the numerical resolution of partial differential equations (PDEs), as they are key to determining the required truncation level to approximate a differential equation by its finite Galerkin approximation. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first derivation of such a fully explicit compactness estimate with respect to a non-classical Sobolev orthogonal polynomial basis. Indeed, while in classical polynomial settings, these estimates can be obtained trivially from a diagonal differentiation operator (e.g. see Example
12
), much work is needed to obtain Theorem
4
.

As will be discussed below, our derivation of an explicit constant
C
in Theorem
4
relies on the fact that we focused on a specific potential
V
. However, most of the ideas used in the proof can be generalised for any polynomial potential
V
, allowing us to quantify the fact that the compactness of the embedding
H^{1}(\nu)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\nu)
becomes better as the potential
V
grows faster at infinity.

Theorem 6
.

Consider
V(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}c_{j}\frac{x^{2j}}{2j}
with
c_{k}=1
.
There exists
C>0
such that for all
n\geq 1

\|u\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}\leq\frac{C}{n^{\frac{2k-1}{2k}}}\|u\|_{H^{1}(\nu)},\qquad\mbox{for all $u\in\overline{\mathrm{Span}\{q_{j}\}_{j>n}}^{H^{1}(\nu)}$}.

(8)

We will see in the proof of Theorem
6
that the rate
n^{-\frac{2k-1}{2k}}
is optimal.

All the previous theorems require understanding the growth of the coefficients
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in the three-term recurrence relation (
1
), which themselves solve a polynomial recurrence equation. In the case of
V
being a quartic potential of the form (
5
), the sequence
(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}=(a^{2}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
solves the so-called
discrete Painlevé I equation

[
17
]

\frac{n}{b_{n}}=b_{n-1}+b_{n}+b_{n+1}-\kappa,\qquad n\geq 1.

(9)

A quick heuristic calculation might suggest that
b_{n}\sim\sqrt{n}/\sqrt{3}
regardless of the initial conditions, but it turns out that (
9
) has high sensitivity on initial conditions with a unique positive solution. Therefore, while the coefficients
a_{n}^{2}
do in fact behave asymptotically like
\sqrt{n}/\sqrt{3}
, obtaining quantitative estimates is challenging. We nonetheless give a rigorous computational method to explicitly control the growth of this solution.

Proposition 7
.

Let
(a^{2}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be the unique positive solution to the recurrence relation (
9
). For all
c^{-}<1
and
c^{+}>1
, there exists
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
n\geq N_{0}

c^{-}\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\sqrt{3}}\leq a^{2}_{n}\leq c^{+}\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\sqrt{3}}.

(10)

In particular, if
\kappa=4
,
c^{-}=0.987
and
c^{+}=1.177
, the above holds for
N_{0}=50
.

A computer-assisted proof of stochastic resonance

As a second application, we turn to the study of the dynamics of the stochastic Duffing oscillator

\mathrm{d}x_{t}=(-V^{\prime}(x_{t})+\eta\cos(\omega t))\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathrm{d}B_{t},\qquad V(x)=\frac{x^{4}}{4}-\kappa\frac{x^{2}}{2},

(13)

i.e., a Brownian particle evolving in a double-well potential
V
periodically tilted by the forcing
\eta\cos(\omega t)
. In the absence of noise (
\sigma=0
), if the periodic forcing is weak (
\eta\ll\sqrt{\kappa}
), then the particle is restricted to remain in a single well. However, in their seminal papers
[
10
,
11
]
, Benzi, Parisi, Sutera and Vulpiani observe that for a well-chosen noise strength
\sigma
, the noise can amplify the weak periodic forcing instead of perturbing it, making the signal
(x_{t})_{t\geq 0}
appear almost periodic at an amplitude around
\sqrt{\kappa}\gg\eta
. They therefore named this noise-induced phenomenon
stochastic resonance
.

Stochastic resonance has since been observed in other models, and equation (
13
) has been studied in various areas of application (see
[
78
,
97
]
and the references therein). However, previous investigations of stochastic resonance have mostly relied on numerical simulations and informal perturbative arguments, and mathematically rigorous evidence for this phenomenon is still lacking.

The phenomenon of stochastic resonance is usually characterised via the periodic stationary probability density function
\varrho:(\mathbb{T}/\omega)\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
solving the Fokker–Planck equation

\frac{\partial\varrho}{\partial t}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\Big((-V^{\prime}(x)+\eta\cos\omega t)\varrho\Big)+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}\varrho}{\partial x^{2}}\qquad\mbox{on $(\mathbb{T}/\omega)\times\mathbb{R}\simeq[0,2\pi/\omega)\times\mathbb{R}$},

(14)

which gives the asymptotic periodic statistics of
(x_{t})_{t\geq 0}
. This is well illustrated in Figure
3
which shows that, for a well-chosen value of
\sigma
, almost all of the probability mass of
(x_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is concentrated in the same well at a given time, and switches well periodically.

\sigma_{0}=0.2
.

.

\sigma_{2}=0.8
.

We therefore aim to demonstrate rigorously the occurrence of stochastic resonance in the system (
13
) by computing the density
\varrho
with explicit error bounds. However, the Numerical Analysis of Eq. (
14
) is more intricate than the one of (
12
) given the extra dependence in the time variable: now, we need to derive compactness estimates in space and time simultaneously. Nevertheless, we show that the core ideas behind the proof of Theorem
4
carry over and we obtain:

Theorem 10
.

For
i=0,1,2
, let
\sigma_{i}
be as in Figure
3
, let
\varrho_{i}:(\mathbb{T}/\omega)\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be the unique probability solution of Eq. (
14
) with
\kappa=1
,
\eta=0.12
,
\omega=0.001
,
\sigma=\sigma_{i}
, and let
\bar{\varrho}_{i}:(\mathbb{T}/\omega)\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be the function depicted in Figure
3
(and whose precise description is available at
[
32
]
in the file

). Then, for
i=0,1,2

\sup_{(t,x)\in(\mathbb{T}/\omega)\times\mathbb{R}}|e^{V/2}(\varrho_{i}-\bar{\varrho}_{i})|\leq 3\times 10^{-22}.

In Section
5
, we give a more detailed description of the phenomenon of stochastic resonance, together with a rigorous quantitative statement concerning the range for
\sigma
at which its effect is maximised.

Theorem
10
amounts to a computer-assisted proof of time-periodic solutions of the Fokker–Planck equation (
14
). We note that, while the literature on computer-assisted proofs for PDEs contains several techniques allowing to study time-periodic solutions
[
4
,
29
,
49
,
53
,
91
,
94
,
98
,
99
]
, all these works rely quite heavily on the fact that they only deal with solutions which are also periodic in space (because the spatial domain is either a torus, or a compact interval with boundary conditions and symmetries allowing for a periodic expansion of the solution). In contrast, in this work we deal with a problem posed on the whole real line. In particular, we use a Galerkin representation of the problem with Fourier series in time and the
L^{2}(\nu)
-orthogonal basis
\mathcal{P}=\{p_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in space, rather than using space-time Fourier series as in several of the aforementioned works, which makes the derivation of the estimates required for the computer-assisted proof significantly more challenging.

Organisation of the paper

In Section

, we first recall some results about weighted Sobolev spaces and Poincaré inequalities, and then discuss our construction of Sobolev orthogonal polynomials and the structure of operators with respect to orthogonal polynomial bases. Building on these insights, we focus in Section
3.1
on the case of the quartic potential
V(x)=x^{4}/4-\kappa x^{2}/2
, and prove embedding estimates for
H^{1}(\nu)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\nu)
, i.e. Theorems
3
and
4
, as well as Proposition
7
on which they rely crucially. The case of an arbitrary even polynomial potential is then discussed in Section
3.2
, where we prove Theorem
6
. In Section
4
, we make use of the estimates of Theorems
3
and
4
to solve the Gross–Pitaevskii equation with sextic potential and prove Theorems
8
and
9
. Finally, Section
5
is dedicated to our study of stochastic resonance. It contains some more background on this problem, and several quantitative statements complementing Theorem
10
. Some of the most technical part of the required estimates for the computer-assisted proofs are collected in the Appendix.

Weighted Sobolev spaces

We start by recalling known results about the operator
\mathcal{L}
and the associated weighted Sobolev spaces in Section
2.1
. Then, we construct orthogonal polynomials on
H^{1}(\nu)
in Section
2.2
, and discuss some generalisations and connections with existing works in Section

. Finally, in Section

, we express the differential operator
\partial_{x}
and

in these orthogonal polynomial bases.

The Functional Analytic point of view

The present work is motivated by the study of the overdamped Langevin (or Smoluchowski) dynamics

\mathrm{d}X_{t}=-V^{\prime}(X_{t})\mathrm{d}t+\sqrt{2}\mathrm{d}B_{t},

(15)

for a potential
V\in\mathcal{C}^{2}(\mathbb{R})
which induce a reversible diffusion process
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
with stationary probability (Gibbs) measure

\nu(\mathrm{d}x)=\frac{e^{-V(x)}}{\mathcal{Z}}\mathrm{d}x,\qquad\mathcal{Z}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-V}\mathrm{d}x,

where
V
and
\nu
will always be of this form unless stated otherwise.
The Markov semi-group of
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is generated by
-\mathcal{L}
, where

\mathcal{L}=V^{\prime}(x)\partial_{x}-\partial_{xx}

is a positive self-adjoint operator with respect to the
L^{2}
-inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
. Indeed, by parts, one has that

\langle u,\mathcal{L}v\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}u(\mathcal{L}v)\mathrm{d}\nu=\int_{\mathbb{R}}u^{\prime}v^{\prime}\mathrm{d}\nu=\int_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathcal{L}u)v\mathrm{d}\nu=\langle\mathcal{L}u,v\rangle,\qquad\mbox{for all $u,v\in H^{1}(\nu)$,}

(16)

which can thus also be written as a Dirichlet form. An accessible introduction to this theory can be found in
[
86
, §4]
(see also
[
18
]
for a nice introduction to Poincaré inequalities) but we emphasise that various aspects of this theory can be generalised
[
8
,
5
]
, to higher dimensions and to various geometries.

A central tool in this theory is the Poincaré inequality (
PI
) on
H^{1}(\nu)
.
For instance
[
86
, Theorem 4.4]
, such a Poincaré inequality immediately implies the exponential ergodicity of the process
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
evolving under (
15
). A criterion for the Poincaré inequality (
PI
) to hold and which will be sufficient for our purposes is given by the following proposition. See
[
18
]
for various functional analytic aspects related to this Poincaré inequality.

Proposition 11
.

Suppose that the potential
V
is such that

\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}\frac{(V^{\prime}(x))^{2}}{2}-V^{\prime\prime}(x)=+\infty,

(17)

then there holds a Poincaré inequality of the form (
PI
) on
H^{1}(\nu)=H^{1}(e^{-V}/\mathcal{Z})
.

Proof.

See
[
96
, Theorem A.1]
.
∎

This criterion essentially amounts to
V
having to grow strictly faster than linearly at infinity. Note that a more sophisticated way to verify the Poincaré inequality is via the Muckenhoupt criterion
[
5
, Theorem 4.5.1]
which is actually an equivalent statement and gives (large) bounds on the optimal Poincaré constant
C_{P}
(see also
[
96
, §A.19]
).

Example 12
.

Consider the case of the Gaussian measure, i.e.
V(x)=x^{2}/2
such that
\nu(\mathrm{d}x)=e^{-x^{2}/2}\mathrm{d}x/\sqrt{2\pi}
. Then, we have the following Poincaré inequality, for all
u\in H^{1}(\nu)
,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}u^{2}\mathrm{d}\nu-\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}u\mathrm{d}\nu\right)^{2}\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}(u^{\prime})^{2}\mathrm{d}\nu.

This is easily shown by decomposing
u
in terms of the orthonormal Hermite polynomials
\mathcal{P}=\{p_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

[
1
, §22]
. One can actually improve this further and obtain the compactness estimates

\|u\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\|u\|_{H^{1}(\nu)},

for all
u\in\overline{\mathrm{Span}\{p_{j}\}_{j\geq n}}^{H^{1}(\nu)}
.

The Hermite case is actually one of the few settings for which such fully explicit constructive compactness estimates had so far been proved, thanks to the diagonal nature of the operator
\partial_{x}
when represented with respect to
\mathcal{P}
(see
[
6
,
7
,
77
]
). This is a shame, as from the probability theoretic point of view, the Hermite case (associated to the so-called Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process) is a limiting case in the classification of diffusion semi-groups generated by
-\mathcal{L}
and associated to
\nu
(see
[
5
, §7.7]
for a discussion, and also
[
8
,
67
]
). Nonetheless, the embedding
H^{1}(\nu)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\nu)
is known to be compact in a much broader setting.

Theorem 13
.

If
V
satisfies (
17
),
H^{1}(\nu)=H^{1}(e^{-V}/\mathcal{Z})
is compactly embedded in
L^{2}(\nu)
and
\mathcal{L}
has a discrete spectrum.

Proof.

This is a standard result, although typically not stated this way. Assumption (
17
) implies that the Witten Laplacian

\displaystyle\Delta_{V}:=-\partial_{xx}+\frac{(V^{\prime}(x))^{2}}{2}-V^{\prime\prime}(x)

has compact resolvent on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
(see, e.g.,
[
12
, Theorem 3.1]
), and thus so does
\mathcal{L}=e^{V/2}\Delta_{V}e^{-V/2}
on
L^{2}(\nu)
. The compactness of the embedding
H^{1}(\nu)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\nu)
then follows from (
16
).
∎

As shown in Theorem
6
in the polynomial case, we expect that if
V(x)\sim|x|^{p}
as
x\to\infty
for
p\in(1,\infty)
, then the embeddings should get quantitatively better as
p
increases. We note that in the limiting case
p=1
, while the Poincaré inequality (
PI
) holds, the compactness of the embedding
H^{1}(\nu)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\nu)
does not (see
[
5
, §4.4.1]
).

Remark 14
.

The Poincaré inequality (
PI
) is in fact equivalent to the standard Poincaré inequality on the space of
\nu
-mean-zero functions: Denoting

H^{1}_{0}(\nu):=\left\{u\in H^{1}(\nu)\Big|\int_{\mathbb{R}}u\mathrm{d}\nu=0\right\},

we have

\int_{\mathbb{R}}u^{2}\mathrm{d}\nu\leq C_{P}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(u^{\prime})^{2}\mathrm{d}\nu\quad\text{for all }u\in H^{1}_{0}(\nu)

(18)

if and only if (
PI
) holds.

In summary, once the Poincaré inequality (
PI
) is available, all the Functional Analysis on
H^{1}(\nu)
is reduced to textbook analysis of elliptic PDEs on Hilbert spaces
[
24
,
47
]
.
In particular, the problem
\mathcal{L}u=g
is then well-posed for all
g\in L^{2}(\nu)
with
\nu
-zero mean by the Lax–Milgram Theorem.

Sobolev orthogonal polynomials

A problem naturally arising in Numerical Analysis is the construction of
Sobolev orthonormal polynomials
on the Sobolev space
H^{1}(\nu)
and their use in the resolution of partial differential equations
[
74
]
.

While orthogonal polynomials with respect to “standard” inner products of the form (
3
) (and to variants including discrete measures) have been heavily studied, actually using them for the Numerical Analysis of PDEs seems very challenging (except in the classical case
V(x)=x^{2}/2
). We propose to consider a different inner product, which leads to a very natural construction of orthogonal polynomials on
H^{1}(\nu)
.

Theorem 15
.

Suppose that
\nu
is a probability measure such that the Poincaré inequality (
PI
) holds.
Then the inner product
(\cdot,\cdot)
defined by

(u,v)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}u^{\prime}v^{\prime}\mathrm{d}\nu+\int_{\mathbb{R}}u\mathrm{d}\nu\int_{\mathbb{R}}v\mathrm{d}\nu\qquad u,v\in H^{1}(\nu)

defines a Hilbert structure on
H^{1}(\nu)
inducing its topology. In particular,
H^{1}(\nu)
is complete under the induced norm given by
\|\cdot\|_{H^{1}(\nu)}:=\sqrt{(\cdot,\cdot)}
.

Proof.

It is straightforward to check that
(\cdot,\cdot)
is indeed an inner product on
H^{1}(\nu)
. Moreover, for all
u\in H^{1}(\nu)
,

\displaystyle\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}u\mathrm{d}\nu\right)^{2}\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}u^{2}\mathrm{d}\nu\leq\max(1,C_{P})\left\{\int_{\mathbb{R}}(u^{\prime})^{2}\mathrm{d}\nu+\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}u\mathrm{d}\nu\right)^{2}\right\},

where the second inequality follows from the Poincaré inequality (
PI
), and thus
\|\cdot\|_{H^{1}(\nu)}=\sqrt{(\cdot,\cdot)}
is Lipschitz-equivalent to the standard Sobolev
H^{1}(\nu)
-norm
\sqrt{\langle\partial_{x}\cdot,\partial_{x}\cdot\rangle+\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle}
.
∎

We recall that
\mathcal{Q}=\{q_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
denotes the basis of orthonormal polynomials with respect to
(\cdot,\cdot)
, which are characterised by Proposition
1
. From now on, we refer to them as Sobolev orthogonal polynomials, even though they are not obtained with a scalar product of the form (
3
). We show in Section
2.4
and Section
3
that these Sobolev orthogonal polynomials have valuable properties that make them helpful for obtaining compactness estimates and solving differential equations.

Remark 16
.

Based on Remark
14
, one can think of the construction of the mean-zero Sobolev orthonormal polynomials
\{q_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
as the orthogonalisation of the basis
\{p_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
of
H^{1}_{0}(\nu)
with respect to the inner product

\int_{\mathbb{R}}u^{\prime}v^{\prime}\mathrm{d}\nu,\qquad\mbox{for }u,v\in H^{1}_{0}(\nu),

which is another way to show Proposition
1
.

Remark 17
.

Note that the density of the Sobolev orthogonal polynomials
\{q_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
H^{1}(\nu)
follows directly from the density of the
L^{2}
-orthogonal polynomials
\{p_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
in
L^{2}(\nu)
.

Remark 18
.

Our Sobolev orthogonal polynomials
\mathcal{Q}=\{q_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
can be obtained as the limit as
\Lambda\to 0
of the orthonormal polynomials with respect to the standard inner product (
3
) (since these have
\nu
-zero mean if they are not constant).

Remark 19
.

One could add weights
\Lambda_{1},\Lambda_{2}>0
and consider seemingly more general inner products than
(\cdot,\cdot)
, of the form

\Lambda_{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}u^{\prime}v^{\prime}\mathrm{d}\nu+\Lambda_{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}u\mathrm{d}\nu\int_{\mathbb{R}}v\mathrm{d}\nu\qquad u,v\in H^{1}(\nu).

However, up to normalisation, the obtained
q_{n}
’s do not depend on such weights, which is not the case for inner products of the form (
3
).

Example 20
.

Consider the case of the Gaussian measure, i.e.
V(x)=x^{2}/2
such that
\nu(\mathrm{d}x)=e^{-x^{2}/2}\mathrm{d}x/\sqrt{2\pi}
. Then
\partial_{x}
acts very simply on the basis
\{p_{n}\}
, namely
p_{n}^{\prime}=\sqrt{n}\,p_{n-1}
for all
n\geq 1
and therefore
q_{n}=p_{n}/\sqrt{n}
for
n\geq 1
: In that case, the Sobolev orthonormal polynomials are simply renormalised classical orthogonal polynomials.

We emphasize that the previous example is the exception and not the rule: in general,
q_{n}
need not be proportional to
p_{n}
, and indeed this will not be the case in Section
3
where we work with non-classical weights.

Some generalisations of our Sobolev orthogonal polynomials

The main focus of this paper is on measures of the form
\nu(\mathrm{d}x)=e^{-V}\mathrm{d}x/\mathcal{Z}
supported on the whole real line, with
V
satisfying (
17
). However, even for other measures, our construction of Sobolev orthogonal polynomials can be used as soon as a form of Poincaré inequality is at hand, and in some specific cases it produces back classical orthogonal polynomials.

Example 21

(The Legendre case)
.

Consider
\mathrm{d}\nu=\mathrm{d}x/2
on
[-1,1]
, where the orthonormal polynomials are the Legendre polynomials
[
1
, §22]
. While this example does not sit within the general setting of this paper, it satisfies the assumptions of Theorem
15
. Indeed on
H^{1}(\nu)
, we have the Poincaré–Wirtinger inequality
[
47
, §5.8.1]
(see also
[
5
, §4.5.2]
)

\int_{-1}^{1}u^{2}\mathrm{d}\nu-\left(\int_{-1}^{1}u\mathrm{d}\nu\right)^{2}\leq C_{P}\int_{-1}^{1}(u^{\prime})^{2}\mathrm{d}\nu,\qquad C_{P}=\frac{4}{\pi^{2}}.

The Sobolev orthogonal polynomials
\mathcal{Q}=\{q_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
associated to the scalar product (
4
) are then given by

q_{0}=1,\qquad q_{1}=\frac{p_{1}}{\sqrt{3}},\qquad q_{2}=\frac{p_{2}}{\sqrt{15}},\qquad q_{n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2n-1}}\left(\frac{p_{n}}{\sqrt{2n+1}}-\frac{p_{n-2}}{\sqrt{2n-3}}\right)\quad\mbox{for $n\geq 3$.}

Note that
q_{n}(-1)=q_{n}(1)=0
for all
n\geq 3
,
which furthermore shows that the
q_{n}
’s cannot simply be some (even rescaled) Jacobi polynomials, as those have interlacing zeros in
(-1,1)
. For
n\geq 3
, the
q_{n}
’s in fact coincide with some so-called Legendre associated polynomials
[
1
, §8]
, which are the natural extension of the Jacobi polynomials at the parameters
\alpha=\beta=-1
.

Note that it might sometimes be more natural to construct
H^{1}
-Sobolev orthogonal polynomials
\{q_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
with respect to an inner product with two different measures
\nu
and
\tilde{\nu}

\int_{\mathbb{R}}u^{\prime}v^{\prime}\mathrm{d}\nu+\int_{\mathbb{R}}u\mathrm{d}\tilde{\nu}\int_{\mathbb{R}}v\mathrm{d}\tilde{\nu},

(19)

if there holds a Poincaré inequality of the form

\mathrm{Var}_{\tilde{\nu}}(u)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}u^{2}\mathrm{d}\tilde{\nu}-\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}u\mathrm{d}\tilde{\nu}\right)^{2}\leq C_{P}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(u^{\prime})^{2}\mathrm{d}\nu,\qquad\mbox{for all $u\in L^{2}(\tilde{\nu})\cap H^{1}(\nu)$}.

(20)

Such an inequality can be verified using Muckenhoupt’s criterion (see
[
5
, Chapter 4.5.1]
and
[
81
]
). This naturally extends our construction of Sobolev orthogonal polynomials, which coincide with known orthogonal polynomials if the measures
\nu
and
\tilde{\nu}
belong to classical families. For instance, in the case of beta distributions we have the following.

Example 22

(The Jacobi family)
.

Let
\alpha,\beta>0
and denote
\nu_{\alpha,\beta}(\mathrm{d}x)=(1-x)^{\alpha}(1+x)^{\beta}\mathrm{d}x/Z_{\alpha,\beta}
on
[-1,1]
, for which the
p_{n}
’s are (orthonormal) Jacobi polynomials of parameter
(\alpha,\beta)

[
1
, §22]
. Then, on
H^{1}(\nu_{\alpha,\beta})\cap L^{2}(\nu_{\alpha-1,\beta-1})=H^{1}(\nu_{\alpha,\beta})
, there holds a Poincaré inequality

\int_{-1}^{1}u^{2}\mathrm{d}\nu_{\alpha-1,\beta-1}-\left(\int_{-1}^{1}u\mathrm{d}\nu_{\alpha-1,\beta-1}\right)^{2}\leq C_{P}\int_{-1}^{1}(u^{\prime})^{2}\mathrm{d}\nu_{\alpha,\beta},\qquad C_{P}=\alpha+\beta.

See
[
5
, §2.7.4]
for more details about the associated semi-group. Then, the
q_{n}
’s associated to the inner product (
19
) (with
\nu=\nu_{\alpha,\beta}
and
\tilde{\nu}=\nu_{\alpha-1,\beta-1}
) are nothing but the (rescaled) Jacobi polynomials associated to
\nu_{\alpha-1,\beta-1}
. Observe that the Legendre-Sobolev polynomials on
H^{1}(\nu_{0,0})
(from Example
21
), cannot be constructed via this inner product as we require
\alpha,\beta>0
.

Similarly, concerning the gamma distributions, we have the following.

Example 23

(The Laguerre family)
.

For
\alpha>-1
, denote
\nu_{\alpha}=x^{\alpha}e^{-x}\mathrm{d}x/Z_{\alpha}
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}=[0,\infty)
such that the
p_{n}
’s are orthonormal Laguerre polynomials of parameter
\alpha

[
1
, §22]
.

•

If
\alpha>0
, on
H^{1}(\nu_{\alpha})\cap L^{2}(\nu_{\alpha-1})=H^{1}(\nu_{\alpha})
, we have the Poincaré inequality
[
5
, §4.4.1]

\int_{0}^{\infty}u^{2}\mathrm{d}\nu_{\alpha-1}-\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}u\mathrm{d}\nu_{\alpha-1}\right)^{2}\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}(u^{\prime})^{2}\mathrm{d}\nu_{\alpha},

such that the
q_{n}
’s associated to the inner product (
19
) (with
\nu=\nu_{\alpha}
and
\tilde{\nu}=\nu_{\alpha-1}
) are (rescaled) Laguerre polynomials of parameter
(\alpha-1)
.

•

In the case
\alpha=0
, we have the Poincaré inequality
[
5
, §4.4.1]

\int_{0}^{\infty}u^{2}\mathrm{d}\nu_{0}-\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}u\mathrm{d}\nu_{0}\right)^{2}\leq 4\int_{0}^{\infty}(u^{\prime})^{2}\mathrm{d}\nu_{0}.

In a similar way as in the Legendre case, for
n\geq 2
, the
q_{n}
’s correspond to a natural extension of the Laguerre polynomials at the parameter
\alpha=-1
(which are not orthogonal with respect to a probability measure). They are for instance obtained by generalising the closed form of the Laguerre polynomials.

Remark 24

(On coherent pairs)
.

Let
\{\tilde{p}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
denote the
L^{2}(\tilde{\nu})
-orthonormal polynomials. If for all
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,
p_{n}
is a linear combination of only
\tilde{p}^{\prime}_{n+1}
and
\tilde{p}^{\prime}_{n}
(resp.
\tilde{p}^{\prime}_{n-1}
), the measures
\tilde{\nu}
and
\nu
are said to be
coherent
(resp. symmetrically coherent). This notion was introduced in
[
62
]
and has since been thoroughly studied (see
[
74
, Section 5.1]
for a survey) to give algebraic relations between the usual Sobolev orthogonal polynomials and the
\tilde{p}_{n}
’s. However, it can be directly seen that if
p_{n}
is a linear combination of only
\tilde{p}^{\prime}_{n+1}
and
\tilde{p}^{\prime}_{n}
(resp.
\tilde{p}^{\prime}_{n-1}
), then
q_{n+1}
associated to (
19
) is a linear combination of
\tilde{p}_{n+1}
and
\tilde{p}_{n}
(resp.
\tilde{p}_{n-1}
).

For (
19
) to be inner product on
H^{1}(\nu)
which induces its usual topology, it then suffices for the coherent pair
(\tilde{\nu},\nu)
to satisfy the Poincaré inequality (
20
) or equivalently the Muckenhoupt criterion
[
5
, Chapter 4.5.1]
. Remarkably, it turns out that this property is satisfied by
all
pairs
(\tilde{\nu},\nu)
which are classified to be coherent (see
[
74
, Section 5.2]
and
[
80
]
for this classification).

In the context of coherent pairs, the
q_{n}
’s associated to (
19
) readily appear in the literature in order to study the usual Sobolev orthogonal polynomials
[
74
,
79
]
, but were not identified as being themselves orthogonal with respect to a Sobolev inner product.

The differentiation operator

In preparation for the resolution of equations of the form (
2
), we now write out explicitly the “infinite matrix” representation of
\partial_{x}
and
\mathcal{L}
in the bases
\mathcal{P}
of
L^{2}(\nu)
and
\mathcal{Q}
of
H^{1}(\nu)
. In particular, this section contains the proof of Proposition
2
, which summarizes Proposition
26
and Proposition
27
below.

Notation 25
.

In the rest of this paper, so that all our statements are precise, we denote the
\ell^{2}
- representation of an element
u
of a weighted Sobolev space

•

by
\left[u\right]_{L^{2}}
if we consider its representation with respect to the orthonormal basis
\mathcal{P}=\{p_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
of
L^{2}(\nu)
,

•

by
\left[u\right]_{H^{1}}
if we consider its representation with respect to the orthonormal basis
\mathcal{Q}=\{q_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
of
H^{1}(\nu)
.

That is,
\left[u\right]_{L^{2}}\in\ell^{2}
is the infinite vector of coefficients
\left(\langle p_{n},u\rangle\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, and
\left[u\right]_{H^{1}}\in\ell^{2}
is the infinite vector of coefficients
\left((q_{n},u)\right)_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.
Furthermore, for the sake of clarity, we may shorten some notations by omitting the
\nu
. Henceforth,
\|\cdot\|_{H^{1}}
(resp.
\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}}
) will always denote
\|\cdot\|_{H^{1}(\nu)}
(resp.
\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}
).

Given an operator
\mathcal{A}
acting between two weighted Sobolev spaces, for instance
\mathcal{A}:H^{1}\to L^{2}
, we denote by
\left[\mathcal{A}\right]_{H^{1}\to L^{2}}:\ell^{2}\to\ell^{2}
the operator such that
\left[\mathcal{A}u\right]_{L^{2}}=\left[\mathcal{A}\right]_{H^{1}\to L^{2}}\left[u\right]_{H^{1}}
. In other words,
\left[\mathcal{A}\right]_{H^{1}\to L^{2}}
is the infinite matrix representing the operator
\mathcal{A}
with basis
\mathcal{Q}
for the domain and basis
\mathcal{P}
for the range. We use similar arrow notations for operator norms, i.e.,

\|\mathcal{A}\|_{H^{1}\to L^{2}}=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in H^{1}\\
u\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{\|\mathcal{A}u\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}}{\|u\|_{H^{1}(\nu)}},

and note that

\|\mathcal{A}\|_{H^{1}\to L^{2}}=\left\|\left[\mathcal{A}\right]_{H^{1}\to L^{2}}\right\|_{\ell^{2}\to\ell^{2}}.

When the two spaces are the same, we sometimes write
\left[\mathcal{A}\right]_{L^{2}}
instead of
\left[\mathcal{A}\right]_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}
.

In order to eventually deal with
\mathcal{L}
, we first consider the (unbounded) differentiation operator
\partial_{x}
, which we write with respect to
\mathcal{P}
, i.e.,

\left[\partial_{x}\right]_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}=D=\begin{pNiceMatrix}0&\Block[borders={top,left}]{4-4}{}&&&&\\
\vdots&&D_{>0}&&&\\
&&&&&\\
&&&&&\\
\end{pNiceMatrix}.

As a differentiation operator acting on a polynomial basis,
D
is strictly upper triangular. The submatrix
D_{>0}
, which corresponds to
\partial_{x}
from the subspace of mean zero functions of
L^{2}(\nu)
to
L^{2}(\nu)
, will play a central role in the analysis to come. In particular, the operator
\partial_{x}
(or equivalently
D
) is closely related to the change of basis matrix
P=\left[\mathrm{id}\right]_{L^{2}\to H^{1}}
from
\mathcal{P}
to
\mathcal{Q}
, i.e. the matrix such that

\left[u\right]_{H^{1}}=P\left[u\right]_{L^{2}}
.

Proposition 26
.

The change of basis matrix
P
from
\mathcal{P}
to
\mathcal{Q}
is given by

\displaystyle P=\left[\mathrm{id}\right]_{L^{2}\to H^{1}}=\begin{pNiceMatrix}1&0&\cdots&&&\\
0&\Block[borders={top,left}]{4-4}{}&&&&\\
\vdots&&D_{>0}&&&\\
&&&&&\\
&&&&&\\
\end{pNiceMatrix}.

(21)

Proof.

We normalised the
p_{n}
and
q_{m}
so that
p_{0}=q_{0}=1
, which explains the first column of
P
. The first row of zeros is an immediate consequence of the fact that the
p_{n}
all have mean
0
for
n\geq 1
. Finally, using that the
q_{m}
also all have mean
0
for
m\geq 1
, we get

\displaystyle(q_{m},p_{n})

\displaystyle=\langle q_{m}^{\prime},p^{\prime}_{n}\rangle+\langle q_{m},1\rangle\langle p_{n},1\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle p_{m-1},p^{\prime}_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=D_{m-1,n}.\qed

The following observations are useful for the resolution of differential equations involving the operator
\mathcal{L}
such as (
12
).

Proposition 27
.

We have that

\left[\mathcal{L}\right]_{L^{2}}=D^{T}D\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left[\mathcal{L}\right]_{H^{1}_{0}}=D_{>0}D_{>0}^{T}.

Proof.

According to (
16
), for all
u,v\in H^{1}

\langle u,\mathcal{L}v\rangle=\langle u^{\prime},v^{\prime}\rangle=[\partial_{x}u]_{L^{2}}^{T}[\partial_{x}v]_{L^{2}}=(D[u]_{L^{2}})^{T}(D[v]_{L^{2}})=[u]_{L^{2}}^{T}D^{T}D[v]_{L^{2}},

which yields the first identity. In particular, the restriction of
\mathcal{L}
to the subspace of
\nu
-mean zero of
L^{2}
writes
D_{>0}^{T}D_{>0}
, and, using Proposition
26
,

\left[\mathcal{L}\right]_{H^{1}_{0}}=D_{>0}\left(D_{>0}^{T}D_{>0}\right)D_{>0}^{-1}=D_{>0}D_{>0}^{T}.\qed

Since
D
is upper triangular,
\left[\mathcal{L}\right]_{L^{2}}=D^{T}D
gives a Cholesky factorisation (LU) of
\left[\mathcal{L}\right]_{L^{2}}
, whereas
\left[\mathcal{L}\right]_{H^{1}_{0}}=D_{>0}D_{>0}^{T}
gives a
reverse Cholesky
factorisation (UL) of
\left[\mathcal{L}\right]_{H^{1}_{0}}
. UL decompositions are rather uncommon as they are typically non-unique in infinite dimension, but it seems to be canonical in this case.

Proposition
27
allows for a simple inversion of the operator
\mathcal{L}
. This is particularly the case if the potential
V
is polynomial. Indeed, we have

Lemma 28
.

Let
V(x)=x^{2k}/(2k)+o(x^{2k})
be a polynomial of degree
2k
, and
\nu(\mathrm{d}x)=e^{-V}\mathrm{d}x/\mathcal{Z}
.
Then, for all
m\leq n-2k

D_{m,n}=\langle p_{m},p^{\prime}_{n}\rangle=0,

such that
D
(and
P
) are upper triangular operators on
\ell^{2}
of upper bandwidth
(2k-1)
.

Proof.

Let
m<n
, then

\displaystyle\langle p_{m},p_{n}^{\prime}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}p_{m}(x)p_{n}^{\prime}(x)e^{-V(x)}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}p_{n}(x)\left(p_{m}(x)e^{-V(x)}\right)^{\prime}\mathrm{d}x\qquad\mbox{By parts}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}p_{n}(x)p_{m}(x)V^{\prime}(x)e^{-V(x)}\mathrm{d}x-\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}p_{n}(x)p_{m}^{\prime}(x)e^{-V(x)}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}p_{n}(x)p_{m}(x)V^{\prime}(x)\nu(\mathrm{d}x)\qquad\mbox{By orthogonality since $\deg(p_{m}^{\prime})=m-1<n$}.

Since
V^{\prime}p_{m}
is a polynomial of degree
m+2k-1
, the above term is zero by orthogonality if
m+2k-1<n
, i.e.
m\leq n-2k
.
∎

Remark 29
.

In the case of a polynomial potential
V
, one can think of the basis
\{p_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
as semi-classical in the sense that
[\partial_{x}]_{L^{2}}
is banded with upper bandwidth
(2k-1)
, in contrast with the classical case of Hermite polynomial (
\nu(\mathrm{d}x)=e^{-x^{2}/2}\mathrm{d}x/\mathcal{Z}
) where
[\partial_{x}]_{L^{2}}
is diagonal. This, in turn, implies that
[\mathcal{L}]_{L^{2}}
is banded with bandwidth
(4k-3)
, extending the result of
[
77
]
which classified diffusion operators with a polynomial eigenbasis on
\mathbb{R}
.

Remark 30
.

Of course, if
V
is an even polynomial then for all
m=n\mod 2
,
D_{m,n}=\langle p_{m},p_{n}^{\prime}\rangle=0
.

Remark 31
.

Observe that

\mathcal{L}=-\mathcal{J}\circ\partial_{x}\quad\text{where }\quad\mathcal{J}=-V^{\prime}+\partial_{x}

is the so-called probability flux (or current) operator
[
86
]
. Now, for
u,v\in H^{1}(\nu)
,

\displaystyle\langle u,\partial_{x}v\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}u(\partial_{x}v)e^{-V}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}v\partial_{x}(ue^{-V})\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}v(-V^{\prime}u+\partial_{x}u)e^{-V}\mathrm{d}x

\displaystyle=\langle-\mathcal{J}u,v\rangle,

which shows that
-\mathcal{J}
is the
L^{2}(\nu)
-adjoint of
\partial_{x}
, and provides another proof of the first identity of Proposition
27
. Another consequence of this observation is that

D^{T}=\left[-\mathcal{J}\right]_{L^{2}}=\left[V^{\prime}\cdot\right]_{L^{2}}-D,\qquad\text{and thus}\qquad[V^{\prime}\cdot]_{L^{2}}=D+D^{T},

(22)

where
\left[V^{\prime}\cdot\right]_{L^{2}}
is the multiplication operator by
V^{\prime}
.
This means that the coefficients of
[V^{\prime}\cdot]_{L^{2}}
grow at the same speed as the ones of
D
, which is consistent with the expectation that multiplying by
V^{\prime}
loses as much regularity as differentiating once on a
\nu
-weighted Sobolev space. Furthermore, when
V
is a polynomial, note that one can therefore deduce the differentiation matrix
D
directly from the multiplication by
V^{\prime}
operator, and thus from the multiplication by
x
(Jacobi) operator.

We start in Section

The quartic case

Let us first consider the quartic or double-well potential

V(x)=\frac{x^{4}}{4}-\kappa\frac{x^{2}}{2},

3.1.1
Freud-type orthogonal polynomials

The orthonormal polynomials with respect to
\nu
are the so-called

orthonormal polynomials
\{p_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and constitute a family of
semi-classical
orthonormal polynomials.
These

’s can be constructed recursively via a three-term recurrence formula of the form (
1
), where each
a_{n}>0
, and where there is no
p_{n}
term in the right-hand side since

is even.

Furthermore, in the case of Freud-type polynomials, one can give an explicit recurrence formula for the positive sequence
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}

[
17
]
. Indeed, the sequence
(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}:=(a^{2}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies the so-called discrete Painlevé I equation (
9

b_{0}=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad b_{1}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{2}\nu(\mathrm{d}x)=\frac{\displaystyle{\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}x^{2}\exp\left(-\frac{x^{4}}{4}+\kappa\frac{x^{2}}{2}\right)\mathrm{d}x}}{\displaystyle{\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\exp\left(-\frac{x^{4}}{4}+\kappa\frac{x^{2}}{2}\right)\mathrm{d}x}},

(25)

which can also be computed via Bessel functions (see (

)).

In view of Lemma
28
and Remark
30
,
p_{n}^{\prime}
should be a linear combination of
p_{n-1}
and
p_{n-3}
and indeed have the following:

Proposition 32
.

Let

be of the form (
24
) and
\{p_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be the basis of orthonormal polynomials of

), then

p_{n}^{\prime}=\frac{n}{a_{n}}p_{n-1}+a_{n}a_{n-1}a_{n-2}p_{n-3}.

This can be obtained rather easily from Remark

. Indeed, applying (

) three times to express

yields

\displaystyle V^{\prime}(x)p_{n}

\displaystyle=(x^{3}-\kappa x)p_{n}

\displaystyle=a_{n+1}a_{n+2}a_{n+3}p_{n+3}+a_{n+1}\left(a_{n+2}^{2}+a_{n+1}^{2}+a_{n}^{2}-\kappa\right)p_{n+1}

Using that

and that

\displaystyle p_{n}^{\prime}=a_{n}a_{n-1}a_{n-2}p_{n-3}+a_{n}\left(a_{n+1}^{2}+a_{n}^{2}+a_{n-1}^{2}-\kappa\right)p_{n-1},

and using (

Remark 33
.

Bonan and Nevai
[

]
actually prove that

is bidiagonal if and only if

is of the form (

), up to a translation and scaling (in

).

In order to justify the invertibility of

(or equivalently, of

) and to get explicit compactness estimates for its inverse, we need a precise control on the growths of the
\alpha_{n}
’s and the

’s, and thus on the growth of the

’s. Indeed, using the triangular structure of

one can easily derive a formal inverse

, but to prove that this yields a well-defined and bounded operator, and to then get quantitative compactness estimates, we will need an estimate of the form

\displaystyle\frac{\beta_{n}}{\alpha_{n}}\leq\theta<1\qquad\text{for all }n\geq N_{0},

(29)

with explicit threshold
N_{0}
and constant
\theta
. This condition, which naturally appears in our proof of Theorem

in Section

, was already used in
[

]
in the slightly more general context of tridiagonal operators.

Looking at (
9
), a formal calculation suggests that
b_{n}\sim\sqrt{n}/\sqrt{3}
as

, and this can in fact be established rigorously
[

, §6]
. From the formulae (

) and the fact that

, we get
\beta_{n}/\alpha_{n}\to 1/3
, which shows the existence of a

and of an

large enough such that (

) holds. However, since we require explicit
N_{0}
and
\theta
, we need more quantitative estimates on the
a_{n}
’s (or equivalently on the

’s), like the ones stated in Proposition

, with explicit constants
c^{-}
and

. In order to establish these bounds, we have to overcome the following difficulties. While the recurrence relation (
9
) might seem in appearance very innocent, it turns out that it is extremely unstable and sensitive to initial conditions. More is in fact true
[
3
,
17
]
: the initial condition given by (
25
) is the unique one such that
b_{n}\geq 0
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. In particular, this suggests that one cannot get estimates solely from (

), the positivity condition being crucial. This furthermore constitutes a computational challenge as even calculating a finite number of terms in double precision quickly diverges from the positive solution.

We point out the analysis of the recurrence relation (
9
) has been the topic of a vast literature
[

,

,

,
50
,

,

,

,

,

,

including the celebrated Freud’s conjecture and its proof in the case

[

(see
[

, §2]
for a nice historical review of the problem). However, quantitative bounds like (

Finally, let us emphasise that we do not have access to an explicit form of the

’s and

’s, and nor of the

’s, but we show in this work that these explicit descriptions are in fact not necessary in order to successfully use these bases in practice. This is demonstrated in an unequivocal manner via the establishment of Theorems

3.1.2
A precise analysis of the discrete Painlevé I equation

Proof of Proposition
7
.

The qualitative statement, namely that for all
0<c^{-}<1<c^{+}
, there exists
N_{0}
such that (
10
) holds for all
n\geq N_{0}
, follows directly from the fact that
b_{n}\sim\sqrt{n}/\sqrt{3}
, which is for instance proved nicely in
[
3
, §6]
. However, such asymptotic analysis does not provide quantitative bounds on the
b_{n}
’s. The quantitative statement follows from a computer-assisted proof and adapts a construction first proposed in
[
69
]
(which gives quantitative bounds in the case
\kappa=0
) and greatly simplified in
[
58
]
in the case of this particular recurrence. We first describe below a procedure where, for a given
\kappa
and fixed
c^{-}
and
c^{+}
close enough to

, we find the smallest possible
N_{1}
such that, for
(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfying (
9
) and (
25
), we get

c^{-}\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\sqrt{3}}\leq b_{n}\leq c^{+}\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\sqrt{3}},

(30)

for all
n\geq N_{1}
.

Step 1

(Set-up of a fixed-point problem)
.

Adapting the strategy in
[
58
]
, we introduce

f(x)=-x+\sqrt{1+x^{2}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad g_{n}(x,y)=\frac{x+y-\kappa}{2\sqrt{n}},\ n\geq 1,

and the map
S:\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}^{*}}\to\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}^{*}}
such that, for
b\in\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}^{*}}
,

\displaystyle(Sb)_{n}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{n}f(g(b_{n-1},b_{n+1})),\qquad\text{for }n>1,

\displaystyle(Sb)_{1}

\displaystyle=f(g(0,b_{2})),

where
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}^{*}}
is endowed with the product topology.
It is then straightforward to check that, if
Sb=b
, then, defining
b_{0}=0
,
b
satisfies the recurrence relation (
9
), and we thus seek a fixed point of
S
in
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\mathbb{N}^{*}}
. Note that, by the aforementioned uniqueness of a positive solution of (
9
) (see
[
3
,
17
]
), the solution to (
9
)-(

) is the unique fixed point of
S
in
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\mathbb{N}^{*}}
.

Step 2

(Upper and lower bounds of a fixed point)
.

We keep following the ideas from
[
58
, Theorem 2.1.(a)]
, which relies on a Banach lattice theory argument, and state the following lemma.

Lemma 34
.

If there are sequences
b^{-},b^{+}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\mathbb{N}^{*}}
such that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}

0\leq b^{-}_{n}\leq(Sb^{+})_{n}\leq(Sb^{-})_{n}\leq b^{+}_{n},

(31)

then the unique positive solution
b
of (
9
) satisfies
b^{-}_{n}\leq b_{n}\leq b^{+}_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
.

Proof.

The inequality
(Sb^{+})_{n}\leq(Sb^{-})_{n}
is implied by
b^{-}_{n}\leq b^{+}_{n}
, as from its definition
S
is monotone decreasing with respect to the canonical partial order
\leq
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{\mathbb{N}^{*}}
. Now, for the same reason, we have that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,

(S(K))_{n}\subset[b^{-}_{n},b^{+}_{n}],\quad\text{where}\quad K=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}[b^{-}_{n},b^{+}_{n}]\subset\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}^{*}}.

Thus
S(K)\subset K
, which is convex and compact in
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}^{*}}
by Tychonoff’s theorem. Since
S:\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}^{*}}\to\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}^{*}}
is continuous and
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}^{*}}
is locally convex, by the Schauder–Tychonoff theorem we have that there exists
b\in K
such that
Sb=b
. Since
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}^{*}}
, this fixed point is the positive solution of (

), and the fact that
b
belongs to

finishes the proof.
∎

We are now going to get explicit enclosures of

by constructing explicit sequences
b^{-}
and
b^{+}
satisfying the assumptions of Lemma

.

Step 3

(Criterion for very large

)
.

We henceforth assume that
\kappa>0
(the same procedure, with slightly different conditions, can be used if
\kappa<0
or
\kappa=0
).
Let
c^{+}>1
and
c^{-}<1

\sqrt{12+\left(c^{+}\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{N_{2}}}+c^{+}\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{N_{2}}}-\frac{\sqrt{3}\kappa}{\sqrt{N_{2}}}\right)^{2}}-2c^{+}-2c^{-}\geq 0,

(32)

and

\sqrt{12+\left(2c^{-}\right)^{2}}+\frac{\sqrt{3}\kappa}{\sqrt{N_{2}}}-c^{-}\left(\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{N_{2}}}+\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{N_{2}}}\right)-2c^{+}\leq 0.

(33)

Note that, if
c^{-}
and
c^{+}
are both close enough to

, such an

always exists.
For all

, let

b^{+}_{n}=c^{+}\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\sqrt{3}},\qquad b^{-}_{n}=c^{-}\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\sqrt{3}}.

Then, for

,

\displaystyle(Sb^{+})_{n}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{n}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\left(\frac{c^{+}\sqrt{n-1}}{\sqrt{3}}+\frac{c^{+}\sqrt{n+1}}{\sqrt{3}}-\kappa\right)^{2}}{4n}+1}-\frac{\frac{c^{+}\sqrt{n-1}}{\sqrt{3}}+\frac{c^{+}\sqrt{n+1}}{\sqrt{3}}-\kappa}{2\sqrt{n}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{n}}{2\sqrt{3}}\left(\sqrt{12\left(c^{+}\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{n}}+c^{+}\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{n}}-\frac{\sqrt{3}\kappa}{\sqrt{n}}\right)^{2}}+\frac{\sqrt{3}\kappa}{\sqrt{n}}-c^{+}\left(\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{n}}+\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{n}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{n}{2\sqrt{3}}\left(\sqrt{12+\left(c^{+}\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{N_{2}}}+c^{+}\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{N_{2}}}-\frac{\sqrt{3}\kappa}{\sqrt{N_{2}}}\right)^{2}}-2c^{+}\right),

and similarly, we get that

3.1.3
Compactness estimates

We now show how estimates on the growth of the positive solution of the discrete Painlevé I equation can be leveraged to quantify the compactness of the Sobolev embedding
H^{1}(\nu)\hookrightarrow L^{2}(\nu)
and the Poincaré inequality on
H^{1}(\nu)
, i.e. we give quantitative versions of Proposition
11
and Theorem
13
via Theorems
3
and
4
respectively.
First, using the upper bound on
b_{n}=a_{n}^{2}
from Proposition
7
, we now obtain explicit bounds on the coefficients

\alpha_{n}=\frac{n}{a_{n}}\quad\text{and}\quad\beta_{n}=a_{n}a_{n+1}a_{n+2}

of the (unbounded)
\ell^{2}
-operator

introduced in Section

, which in particular allow us to satisfy (

Corollary 35
.

For all
c^{+}>1
, there exists
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

b_{n}\leq c^{+}\frac{\sqrt{n}}{\sqrt{3}},

(36)

for all
n\geq N_{0}
.
Defining

\displaystyle C_{\alpha}=\frac{\sqrt[4]{3}}{\sqrt{c^{+}}},\qquad\theta=\frac{(c^{+})^{2}}{3}\left(\left(1+\frac{1}{N_{0}}\right)\left(1+\frac{2}{N_{0}}\right)\right)^{1/4},

we get, for all

In the case
\kappa=4
, one can take
c^{+}=1.177
and
N_{0}=50
, which yields

C_{\alpha}\approx 1.213\qquad\text{and}\qquad\theta\approx 0.469.

Remark 36
.

Recall that
b_{n}\sim\sqrt{n/3}
yields
\alpha_{n}\sim\sqrt[4]{3}n^{3/4}
and
\beta_{n}\sim(n/3)^{3/4}
, and therefore
\beta_{n}/\alpha_{n}\sim 1/3
. In particular, up to taking
N_{0}
large enough, one can always get

, which is needed for some subsequent estimates. If need be, one could take

larger in Corollary

, which would allow

to be closer to
1
, and thus
\theta
to be closer to
1/3
.

Note that we only needed the upper-bounds on

from Proposition
7
here, but the lower-bounds will also be used, in Appendix
A
.

Remark 37
.

Of course, these estimates become worse and harder to obtain as
\kappa
gets larger. Furthermore, if
c^{-}
and
c^{+}
were chosen to be further from
1
, then

would be smaller, though the quality of

and

would be worse. However, the crucial feature is for

be strictly less than

.

Notation 38

&&\alpha_{4}&\ddots&\\

&&&&\end{pmatrix}.

Furthermore, for a matrix or operator

3.1.3.2
Proof of Theorem
3

Recalling Remark
14
, the Poincaré constant is the smallest number
C_{P}
such that for all

,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}u^{2}\mathrm{d}\nu\leq C_{P}\int_{\mathbb{R}}(u^{\prime})^{2}\mathrm{d}\nu,

(46)

i.e. such that

\|u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq C_{P}\|u^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}}^{2}=C_{P}\|u\|^{2}_{H^{1}},

which means

C_{P}=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in H^{1}_{0}\\
u\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{\|u\|_{L^{2}}^{2}}{\|u\|_{H^{1}}^{2}}.

Rephrasing this in terms of our operators
P
and
D_{>0}
, we have that the Poincaré constant is the smallest number
C_{P}
such that for all
u\in H^{1}_{0}(\nu)
,

\|[u]_{L^{2}}\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}=\|P^{-1}[u]_{H^{1}}\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}\leq C_{P}\|[u]_{H^{1}}\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}.

Since
u\in H^{1}_{0}(\nu)
, i.e.
(u,q_{0})=\langle u,p_{0}\rangle=0
, and recalling (
21
), we get

C_{P}=\|D_{>0}^{-1}\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}v\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}^{*})\\
v\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{\|D_{>0}^{-1}v\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}}{\|v\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}},

(47)

and we therefore prove Theorem
3
by rigorously enclosing the
\ell^{2}
operator norm of
D_{>0}^{-1}
.

First, since
D_{>0}^{-1}
is upper triangular, using the carving and notations from Section
3.1.3.1
we have that
\|D_{\leq N}^{-1}\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq\|D_{>0}^{-1}\|_{\ell^{2}}
. A lower bound for the Poincaré constant is therefore given by

\bar{C}_{P}:=\|D_{\leq N}^{-1}\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}.

We emphasise that

is a finite matrix, and that rigorous and sharp enclosures of the

operator norm of a finite matrix can easily be obtained. We give below a simple approach based on the Gershgorin circle theorem and interval arithmetic, and also refer to
[
82
, Section 12.4.5]
for further discussion about this question.

•

First, compute rigorously (i.e. in interval arithmetic)
M=D_{\leq N}^{-1}(D_{\leq N}^{-1})^{T}
, so that
\|D_{\leq N}^{-1}\|_{\ell^{2}}=\|M\|_{\ell^{2}}^{1/2}
. Since
M
is symmetric,

is simply the maximum of
|\lambda(M)|
over all the eigenvalues
\lambda(M)
of
M
.

•

Secondly, diagonalise
M
numerically, i.e. find
Q
such that
Q^{-1}MQ
is approximately diagonal.

•

Then, rigorously compute
Q^{-1}
; this can be achieved via the function
inv
in the Julia library

IntervalArithmetic.jl

[
87
]
.

•

Rigorously compute

\bar{\Lambda}
and

have same spectrum.

•

Enclose the spectrum of
\bar{\Lambda}
(and thus
M
, which is a subset of

) via the Gershgorin circle theorem. This gives an upper bound on

.

•

Finally, take

the column of
Q
corresponding to the largest numerical eigenvalue of

\|D_{\leq N}^{-1}v\|_{\ell^{2}}/\|v\|_{\ell^{2}}
, which gives a lower bound on

.

Using the above procedure, we obtain a rigorous enclose of

0&C_{22}N^{-3/4}\end{pmatrix}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2},

where
C_{12}
and

are as in (

) and (

3.1.3.3
Other embedding estimates

In the context of (nonlinear) PDEs, it is often useful to make use of Sobolev inequalities
[
24
, Corollary IX.13]
to deal with nonlinearities. In one dimension, this can be achieved via the derivation of
L^{\infty}
-bounds which are also useful in the proof of the positivity of solutions to elliptic equations in the fashion of
[
20
, §4.3]
. Such bounds can be obtained via the standard inequality
[
24
, Theorem VIII.7]

\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\leq\sqrt{2}\|\varphi\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{1/2}\|\varphi^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{1/2},\qquad\mbox{for all $\varphi\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R})$.}

By setting
\varphi=e^{-V/2}u
, we then obtain

\|e^{-V/2}u\|_{\infty}\leq\sqrt{2}\|e^{-V/2}u\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{1/2}\|(e^{-V/2}u)^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{1/2}.

Now, observe that

\displaystyle e^{V/2}(ue^{-V/2})^{\prime}=-\frac{1}{2}V^{\prime}u+u^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}(-V^{\prime}u+u^{\prime})+\frac{1}{2}u^{\prime}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathcal{J}u+\partial_{x}u\right),

where
\mathcal{J}=-V^{\prime}(x)+\partial_{x}
, such that

\|(e^{-V/2}u)^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=\sqrt{\mathcal{Z}}\|e^{V/2}(e^{-V/2}u)^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}\leq\frac{\sqrt{\mathcal{Z}}}{2}\left(\|\mathcal{J}u\|_{L^{2}}+\|\partial_{x}u\|_{L^{2}}\right).

Note that this calculation does not depend on the choice of
V
. Since
-\mathcal{J}
is the
L^{2}(\nu)
-adjoint of
\partial_{x}
(cf. proof of Proposition
27
, also Remark
31
), it is expected that
\|\mathcal{J}\|_{H^{1}\to L^{2}}
is finite as

\|\mathcal{J}\|_{H^{1}(\nu)\to L^{2}(\nu)}=\|[\mathcal{J}]_{H^{1}\to L^{2}}\|_{\ell^{2}}=\|[\mathcal{J}]_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}P^{-1}\|_{\ell^{2}}=\|-D^{T}P^{-1}\|_{\ell^{2}}=\|D^{T}P^{-1}\|_{\ell^{2}}.

We furthermore need to quantify this bound and have the following lemma, which can be proved using similar methods as in Section
3.1.3.1
.

Lemma 40
.

For
V=x^{4}/4-\kappa x^{2}/2
, if
\mathcal{J}=-V^{\prime}(x)+\partial_{x}
, then there exist an explicit
C_{\mathcal{J}}>0

\|\mathcal{J}u\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}\leq C_{\mathcal{J}}\|u\|_{H^{1}(\nu)}.

Furthermore, for all

Finally, if

Proof.

The general polynomial case

In this section, we explain how the ideas which allowed us to derive Theorem
4
in the case of
V
being quartic can be generalised to
V
being an arbitrary even polynomial of degree
2k
. We first recall
[
35
,
70
]
that in this case, the coefficients
a_{n}
of the three-term recurrence relation (
1
)
can still be obtained as the positive solution of a nonlinear recurrence relation or so-called “string equation”.

Lemma 41
.

There exists a
computable
set of homogeneous polynomials
\left\{R_{j}:\mathbb{R}^{2j-1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\right\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
having positive integer coefficients and with
\deg R_{j}=j-1
such that, for every
k\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and every even polynomial potential
V(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}c_{j}\frac{x^{2j}}{2j}
, the positive sequence
(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}=(a^{2}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
satisfies the recurrence relation

\frac{n}{b_{n}}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}c_{j}R_{j}(b_{n-j},\ldots,b_{n},\ldots,b_{n+j}),

(50)

where
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is the sequence associated to
V
via (
1
). Furthermore, the sum of the monomial coefficients of
R_{j}
is
R_{j}(1,\ldots,1)=\binom{2j-1}{j-1}
.

Proof.

Using (
1
) and the orthogonality of the
p_{n}
’s, we first observe that, for all
n\geq 1
,

\displaystyle a_{n}\langle p_{n-1},p_{n}^{\prime}\rangle=\langle xp_{n},p_{n}^{\prime}\rangle=\langle p_{n},xp_{n}^{\prime}\rangle=\langle p_{n},np_{n}\rangle=n.

We also have from (
22
) that

\langle p_{n-1},V^{\prime}p_{n}\rangle=\langle p_{n-1},p_{n}^{\prime}\rangle,

and thus

\langle p_{n-1},V^{\prime}p_{n}\rangle=\frac{n}{a_{n}}.

(51)

On the other hand, by applying
2j-1
times the recurrence formula (
1
), we can identify the polynomial
R_{j}
(which does not depend on
V
) such that

\langle p_{n-1},x^{2j-1}p_{n}\rangle=a_{n}R_{j}(a^{2}_{n-j+1},\ldots,a_{n}^{2},\ldots,a_{n+j-1}^{2}).

(52)

Therefore, since
V^{\prime}(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}c_{j}x^{2j-1}
, combining (
52
) and (
51
) yields (
50
).
R_{j}(1,\ldots,1)
can then be inferred by applying
2j-1
times the recurrence formula (
1
) with the sequence
(a_{n})_{n\in N}
being constant equal to
1
. This is equivalent to iterating
2j-1
times the corresponding Jacobi operator which is now the adjacency matrix of a symmetric random walk on
\mathbb{Z}
.
R_{j}(1,\ldots,1)=\langle p_{n-1},x^{2j-1}p_{n}\rangle
is thus the number of paths of length
2j-1
from
n-1
to
n
(or equivalently from
0
to
1
) which equals
\binom{2j-1}{j-1}
.
∎

Without loss of generality, assume that
V(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}c_{j}\frac{x^{2j}}{2j}
with
c_{k}=1
. Then, analogously as in the case
k=2
, one can once again expect that for large
n
(if the
b_{n}
’s are positive), the recurrence (
50
) is dominated by the term of highest degree, that is

\frac{n}{b_{n}}\sim R_{k}(b_{n-k},\ldots,b_{n},\ldots,b_{n+k}),

(53)

and therefore that

b_{n}\sim n^{\frac{1}{k}}\binom{2k-1}{k-1}^{-\frac{1}{k}}.

(54)

For a general potential
V
with polynomial growth, the holding of such asymptotics is known as
Freud’s conjecture
and has been the topic of a rich literature (see for instance
[
39
,
70
]
and the references therein). In the case of
V
being polynomial, the conjecture is solved in
[
39
]
which however does not rely on the study of the nonlinear recurrence (
50
). Designing a general validated numerical method akin to the one proposed in Section
3.1.2
to give explicit bounds on the solution of (
50
) would therefore be of great interest.

Proposition 42

(The polynomial case of Freud’s conjecture
[
39
]
)
.

Let
V(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}c_{j}\frac{x^{2j}}{2j}
with
c_{k}=1
, and
(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}=(a^{2}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be the corresponding positive sequence defined by (
1
). Then (
54
) holds.

Proposition
42
is a key ingredient in our proof of Theorem
6
. Before turning to the actual proof, let us give a quick heuristic explanation of how the two are related. On
H^{1}(\nu)
, taking a derivative is essentially equivalent in norm to multiplying by
V^{\prime}
, in the sense that they induce the same loss of regularity (they both send
H^{1}(\nu)
to
L^{2}(\nu)
). This is shown more precisely in the subsequent proof. Since
V^{\prime}
is of degree
2k-1
and equivalent to
x^{2k-1}
at infinity, multiplying by
V^{\prime}
essentially amounts to multiplying by
x

(2k-1)
times, i.e. applying
(2k-1)
times the recurrence relation (
1
). Therefore, using (
54
) and ignoring multiplicative constants, we expect to have

\|p_{n}\|_{H^{1}(\nu)}=\|p_{n}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}\gtrsim\|V^{\prime}p_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}\gtrsim\|x^{2k-1}p_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}\gtrsim\|a_{n}^{2k-1}p_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}\gtrsim n^{\frac{2k-1}{2k}}\|p_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}.

Proof of Theorem
6
.

The proof follows essentially the same lines as the one of Theorem
4
, in a slightly less quantitative way.

Step 1

(Carving of
D_{>0}
)
.

We proceed similarly as in Section
3.1.3.1
, except that we first factor out the expected asymptotic growth in
D_{>0}=[\partial_{x}]_{\mathcal{P}\to\mathcal{P}^{*}}
, and then carve it as follows

D_{>0}=\mathrm{Diag}((j^{1-1/2k})_{j\in\mathbb{N}})M=\mathrm{Diag}((j^{1-1/2k})_{j\in\mathbb{N}})\begin{pNiceMatrix}\Block[borders={bottom,right}]{1-1}{M_{11}}&M_{12}\\
0&\Block[borders={top,left}]{1-1}{M_{22}}\end{pNiceMatrix},

where for some
N
(to be determined later),
M_{11}=\Pi_{\leq N}M\Pi_{\leq N}
,
M_{12}=\Pi_{\leq N}M\Pi_{>N}
and
M_{22}=\Pi_{>N}M\Pi_{>N}
. It is now sufficient to prove that
M
has a bounded inverse
M^{-1}
: indeed, we then have

\|D^{-1}_{>0}\Pi_{>n}\|_{\ell^{2}\to\ell^{2}}=\|M^{-1}\mathrm{Diag}((j^{1/2k-1})_{j\in\mathbb{N}})\Pi_{>n}\|_{\ell^{2}\to\ell^{2}}\leq\frac{\|M^{-1}\|_{\ell^{2}\to\ell^{2}}}{n^{1-1/2k}}.

Step 2

(Boundedness of
M^{-1}
)
.

Again similarly as in Section
3.1.3.1
, we can formally invert
M
as

M^{-1}=\begin{pNiceMatrix}\Block[borders={bottom,right}]{1-1}{M_{11}^{-1}}&-M_{11}^{-1}M_{12}M_{22}^{-1}\\
0&\Block[borders={top,left}]{1-1}{M_{22}^{-1}}\end{pNiceMatrix}.

(55)

First, note that
M_{11}
is indeed invertible, as a finite-dimensional triangular matrix with non-zero coefficients on the diagonal (these are in fact
j^{1/2k}/a_{j}>0
, see the proof of Lemma
41
). Moreover,
M_{12}
is bounded since it has finitely many non-zero entries. The proof that
M
has bounded inverse therefore boils down to showing that the infinite-dimensional banded operator
M_{22}
is boundedly invertible.

Step 3

(Boundedness of
M_{22}^{-1}
)
.

Now recall that
D_{>0}
is the upper triangular part of the multiplication operator
[V^{\prime}\cdot]_{L^{2}\to L^{2}}
(Remark
31
).
Then, proceeding similarly as in Lemma
41
by iterating
2i-1
times the recurrence relation (
1
), one finds that

\langle p_{m-2j-1},x^{2i-1}p_{m}\rangle=a_{m}a_{m-1},\ldots a_{m-2j}R_{i,j}(a_{m-i-j+1}^{2},\ldots,a_{m+i-j-1}^{2}),

where
R_{i,j}
is a homogeneous polynomial of degree
i-j-1
and
R_{i,j}(1,\ldots,1)=\binom{2i-1}{i-j-1}
(by counting the number of paths of length
2i-1
between
m
and
m-2j-1
). Using (
54
), we have that
a_{m+l}\sim a_{m}
for each fixed
l
,
and therefore

\langle p_{m-2j-1},x^{2i-1}p_{m}\rangle\sim\binom{2i-1}{i-1-j}\left(m^{\frac{1}{2k}}\binom{2k-1}{k-1}^{-\frac{1}{k}}\right)^{2i-1}=\binom{2i-1}{i-1-j}m^{\frac{2i-1}{2k}}\binom{2k-1}{k-1}^{-\frac{2i-1}{k}}.

Therefore,

D_{2m-2j-1,m}=\langle p_{m-2j-1},V^{\prime}(x)p_{m}\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}\langle p_{m-2j-1},x^{2i-1}p_{m}\rangle\sim\binom{2k-1}{k-1-j}m^{\frac{2k-1}{2k}}\binom{2k-1}{k-1}^{-\frac{2k-1}{k}},

since
c_{k}=1
.
This show that the matrix
M
introduced in Step 1 is asymptotically Toeplitz. More precisely, letting

\quad m_{2j}=\left\{\begin{tabular}[]{c c}$\binom{2k-1}{k-j-1},$&for $0\leq j\leq k-1,$\\
$0,$&otherwise\end{tabular}\right.\qquad\text{and}\qquad m_{2j+1}=0,

and
\bar{M}_{22}
the upper triangular Toeplitz matrix given by

\bar{M}_{22}=\binom{2k-1}{k-1}^{-\frac{2k-1}{k}}\begin{pmatrix}m_{0}&m_{1}&m_{2}&\ldots&\\
0&m_{0}&m_{1}&m_{2}&\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots\end{pmatrix},

we have that for all
\varepsilon>0
, if
N
is large enough,
\|M_{22}-\bar{M}_{22}\|_{\ell^{2}\to\ell^{2}}<\varepsilon.

Thus, provided
\bar{M}_{22}
is boundedly invertible,
M_{22}
will also be boundedly invertible for all
N
large enough.

Step 4

(Boundedness of
\bar{M}_{22}^{-1}
)
.

By Wiener’s
1/f
Theorem,
\bar{M}_{22}
is boundedly invertible on
\ell^{1}(\mathbb{N})
if and only if the function

\displaystyle P:z\mapsto\sum_{n\geq 0}m_{n}z^{n}=\sum_{n\geq 0}m_{2n}(z^{2})^{n}

does not vanish on the unit disk
\{|z|\leq 1\}
. However, by the Eneström–Kakeya theorem
[
64
]
, if
P(z)=0
, then we must have

|z^{2}|\geq\min_{j=0,\ldots,k-2}\frac{m_{2j}}{m_{2(j+1)}}=\min_{j=0,\ldots,k-2}\frac{\binom{2k-1}{k-1-j}}{\binom{2k-1}{k-2-j}}=\min_{j=0,\ldots,k-2}\frac{k+j+1}{k-1-j}=\frac{k+1}{k-1}>1.

Therefore,
\bar{M}_{22}^{-1}
is indeed bounded on
\ell^{1}
, and because
\bar{M}_{22}^{-1}
is Toeplitz
\|\bar{M}^{-1}_{22}\|_{\ell^{2}\to\ell^{2}}\leq\|\bar{M}^{-1}_{22}\|_{\ell^{1}\to\ell^{1}}<\infty
. ∎

The Gross–Pitaevskii equation with sextic potential

In this section, we prove Theorem
8
and Theorem
9
. As is usual with such computer-assisted proofs
[
93
,
82
]
, the main idea is to study a well-chosen Newton-like operator and prove that it is contracting in a small neighbourhood of the approximate solution. The quantitative compactness estimate of Theorem
4
plays a crucial role in this procedure, and the computable upper-bound of the Poincaré constant
C_{P}
provided by Theorem
3
will also be used.

Notation 44
.

For the rest of this section, we fix some
n\in\mathbb{N}
greater than
N
in Theorem
4
and
we denote by
\Pi_{\leq n}^{L^{2}}
(resp.
\Pi_{\leq n}^{H^{1}}
) the projection onto
\mathrm{Span}\{p_{m}\}_{m=0}^{n}
(resp.
\mathrm{Span}\{q_{m}\}_{m=0}^{n}
) and by
\Pi_{>n}^{L^{2}}
(resp.
\Pi_{>n}^{H^{1}}
) the projection onto
\overline{\mathrm{Span}\{p_{m}\}_{m>n}}^{L^{2}}
(resp.
\overline{\mathrm{Span}\{q_{m}\}_{m>n}}^{H^{1}}

L^{2}(\nu)
and
H^{1}(\nu)
, and all operators are restricted to these subspaces.

We consider the Gross–Pitaevskii equation

-\partial_{xx}\varphi+W(x)\varphi+\varphi^{3}=\omega\varphi,

where
W(x)=x^{6}/4-\kappa x^{4}/2+cx^{2}+d
is a sextic polynomial potential. We look for real even solutions
\varphi
of the above equation such that

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\varphi^{2}W\mathrm{d}x<\infty.

(56)

Then, still with
V(x)=x^{4}/4-\kappa x^{2}/2

\mathcal{L}u+e^{-V}u^{3}-\omega u+r(x)u=0,

and

such that

\mathcal{L}u+e^{-V}u^{3}-\omega u=0,

(57)

which is equivalent to the zero-finding problem

F(u):=u-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{-1}f(u)=u-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{-1}(\omega u+u_{0}-e^{-V}u^{3})=0,\qquad u_{0}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}u\mathrm{d}\nu,

(58)

where we introduced

and work with an invertible operator.

We have the following representations of

on

and

[\tilde{\mathcal{L}}]_{L^{2}}=P^{T}P,\qquad[\tilde{\mathcal{L}}]_{H^{1}}=PP^{T}\qquad\text{and}\qquad[\tilde{\mathcal{L}}]_{H^{1}\to L^{2}}=P^{T}.

(59)

Furthermore,

\|\Pi_{>n}^{H^{1}}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{-1}\Pi_{>n}^{L^{2}}\|_{L^{2}\to H^{1}}\leq\frac{C_{22}}{n^{3/4}},\qquad\|\Pi_{>n}^{H^{1}}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{-1}\|_{L^{2}\to H^{1}}\leq\frac{C}{n^{3/4}},

where

and

are as in Section

and

\Pi_{\leq n}^{H^{1}}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{-1}\Pi_{>n}^{L^{2}}=0\qquad\text{such that}\qquad\Pi_{\leq n}^{H^{1}}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{-1}=\Pi_{\leq n}^{H^{1}}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{-1}\Pi_{\leq n}^{L^{2}}.

(61)

Proof.

The identities in (
59
) follow from Proposition

by adding the action of
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}
on constants. Combining (

, gives (

P^{T}
and thus

is lower triangular. Note that we also have that for

Since

which is a bounded (actually compact from Theorem

)
\ell^{2}

\tilde{\mathcal{L}}^{-1}
is bounded and compact as an operator from

to

. Moreover, by the Sobolev embeddings recalled in Section

,

A computer-assisted proof of stochastic resonance

Description of the problem

In their early 1980s seminal papers
[
10
,
11
]
, Benzi, Parisi, Sutera and Vulpiani introduced the notion of
stochastic resonance
and gave heuristics for its occurrence in the noisy Duffing oscillator

\mathrm{d}x_{t}=(-x^{3}_{t}+\kappa x_{t}+\eta\cos(\omega t))\mathrm{d}t+\sigma\mathrm{d}B_{t},

(SR)

i.e., a Brownian particle evolving in a double-well potential tilted by a periodic forcing. They consider the situation in which this periodic forcing is weak, meaning that, in the absence of noise,
\eta
is too small for the solution to switch periodically between the two wells (that is,
\eta<2\kappa^{3/2}/3\sqrt{3})
. However, the addition of noise can counterintuitively enable the appearance of periodic-like behaviour with large amplitude. Indeed,

•

if
\sigma
is large enough, the dynamics are mostly Brownian and the particle explores both wells at all times;

•

if
\sigma
is small enough, the particle remains in the same well for long time intervals, a behaviour close to the case
\sigma=0
, and switches well irregularly;

•

there exists a “sweet spot” for
\sigma
, as illustrated in Figure
5
and Figure
3
, at which the noise enhances the periodic forcing, making the particle switch well at very regular time intervals, with a “period” corresponding to that of the weak forcing.

Figure 5
:
Sample paths of
(x_{t})_{t\geq 0}
for
\eta=0.12
,
\kappa=1
and
\omega=0.001
and various noise levels
\sigma
.

Stochastic resonance was initially introduced in climate modelling; it has since been relevant in many fields of application such as ecology, engineering, finance, neuroscience, physics etc. (see for instance
[
78
,
97
]
for reviews of applications). Although stochastic resonance is well understood heuristically (e.g. see
[
86
, §7.4]
or
[
59
, §1]
) and has been extensively studied in numerical simulations
[
11
,
31
,
54
]
, there has been no rigorous validation of this phenomenon for the SDE (
SR
). To the best of our knowledge, the only rigorous mathematical analysis of stochastic resonance for (
SR
) has been conducted by Berglund and Gentz
[
13
]
when the system is close to forming a single-well potential at times in
\pi\mathbb{N}
(that is
\eta\approx 2\kappa^{3/2}/3\sqrt{3}
) and
\sigma
is small so that trajectories of the random system can be compared with those of the deterministic one. Let us also mention the work
[
59
]
and references therein which show how systems of the type (
SR
) can be approximated by a two-state continuous-time Markov chain in the small noise limit
\sigma\to 0
.

Since stochastic resonance is characterised by the probability that a particle is located in a particular well at a given time, it can be captured by the probability density function
p(t,x)
of
x_{t}
, which solves the Fokker–Planck equation

\frac{\partial p}{\partial t}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\Big((-x^{3}+\kappa x+\eta\cos\omega t)p\Big)+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}p}{\partial x^{2}}.

(70)

Asymptotically, the statistics of
(x_{t})_{t\geq 0}
follow a so-called
stationary periodic density
function
\varrho_{\sigma}
solving Eq. (
70
) on the time-periodic domain
(\mathbb{T}/\omega)\times\mathbb{R}
. Indeed
[
48
]
, there exists
\lambda>0
such that

\lim_{t\to\infty}\|p(t,\cdot)-\varrho_{\sigma}(t,\cdot)\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}\lesssim e^{-\lambda t}.

Figure
3
illustrates that some kind of local optimum with respect to the noise intensity
\sigma
can also be observed at the level of the stationary periodic densities.

In the literature, several quantities have been introduced with the aim of characterising stochastic resonance
[
11
,
54
]
, sometimes in a much broader context than the one of (
SR
). In this work, we study the indicator proposed in
[
54
]
,

\mathcal{R}(\varrho_{\sigma})=\max_{t\in\mathbb{T}/\omega}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}x\varrho_{\sigma}(t,x)\mathrm{d}x\right|.

(71)

If stochastic resonance occurs, this indicator should be maximised when the periodicity of the dynamics of
(x_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is the most enhanced, and closer to zero when
\sigma
is too large or too small. Using some of the tools introduced in Section
2
and
3
, we prove the existence of
\sigma_{SR}>0
such that
\sigma\mapsto\mathcal{R}(\varrho_{\sigma})
has a local maximum at
\sigma=\sigma_{SR}
, thereby proving that (
SR
) exhibits stochastic resonance. We also give a rigorous enclosure for
\sigma_{SR}
, as well as a rigorous lower bound for
\mathcal{R}(\varrho_{\sigma_{SR}})
.

for each phase

Setting for the computer-assisted proof

Our main task is to find an explicit and rigorous enclosure for a time-periodic solution
\varrho=\varrho(t,x)
, with frequency
\omega
,
of the Fokker-Planck equation (
70
).
In order to reuse the framework introduced in Section
2
and Section
3.1
, we first rescale the problem, using the change of variables

\tilde{\eta}=\frac{2^{3/4}}{\sigma^{3/2}}\eta,\qquad\tilde{\kappa}=\frac{2^{1/2}}{\sigma}\kappa,\qquad\tilde{\omega}=\frac{2^{1/2}}{\sigma}\omega,\qquad s=\omega t,\qquad y=\frac{2^{1/4}}{\sigma^{1/2}}x,

(72)

and the change of unknown

\varrho(t,x)=(1+u(s,y))\frac{\sqrt[4]{2}}{\sqrt{\sigma}}\frac{e^{-y^{4}/4+\tilde{\kappa}y^{2}/2}}{\mathcal{Z}},\qquad\mathcal{Z}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-y^{4}/4+\tilde{\kappa}y^{2}/2}\mathrm{d}y,

(73)

which yields the following equation for
u
:

-\tilde{\omega}\frac{\partial u}{\partial s}+(-y^{3}+\tilde{\kappa}y)\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}+\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial y^{2}}+\tilde{\eta}\cos s\left[(y^{3}-\tilde{\kappa}y)u-\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}\right]=\tilde{\eta}(-y^{3}+\tilde{\kappa}y)\cos s.

(74)

Remark 52
.

The normalisation factor
\mathcal{Z}
, which appears in several estimates to come, can be expressed as

\mathcal{Z}=\frac{\pi}{2}e^{\tilde{\kappa}^{2}/8}\sqrt{\tilde{\kappa}}\left(\mathcal{I}_{-1/4}(\tilde{\kappa}^{2}/8)+\mathcal{I}_{1/4}(\tilde{\kappa}^{2}/8)\right),

where
\mathcal{I}_{\gamma}
denotes the modified Bessel function of the first kind with parameter
\gamma
, and thus can be rigorously computed using
Arb

[
63

.

With the operator

\mathcal{L}=V^{\prime}(y)\frac{\partial}{\partial y}-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial y^{2}},\qquad V(y)=\frac{y^{4}}{4}-\tilde{\kappa}\frac{y^{2}}{2},

and the so-called probability flux (or current) operator

\mathcal{J}=-V^{\prime}(y)+\frac{\partial}{\partial y}=-\mathcal{D}^{T},

both already introduced in Section
2
, the equation satisfied by
u
writes

\tilde{\omega}\frac{\partial u}{\partial s}+\mathcal{L}u+\tilde{\eta}\cos s\mathcal{J}u=\tilde{\eta}V^{\prime}(y)\cos s.

(75)

In order to enforce that
\rho
has mass one, we also impose that

\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}u(s,y)\nu(y)\mathrm{d}y=0\qquad\mbox{for all $s\in\mathbb{T}$},

(76)

where
\nu(\mathrm{d}y)=(e^{-V(y)}/\mathcal{Z})\mathrm{d}y
.
With this zero-mean condition, we instead choose to solve for
v=(\tilde{\omega}\partial_{s}+\mathcal{L})u
such that Eq. (
75

\mathcal{F}v=g,

(77)

where
\mathcal{F}=\mathrm{id}+\tilde{\eta}\cos{s}\mathcal{J}(\tilde{\omega}\partial_{s}+\mathcal{L})^{-1}
, and
g(s,y)=\tilde{\eta}V^{\prime}(y)\cos s
. It is for this problem (

Remark 53
.

Solving for
v
instead of for

can be interpreted as changing the topology in which we solve the problem. This is not mandatory, but leads to slightly less cumbersome calculations in practice, essentially because some of the finer estimates for
\mathcal{J}(\tilde{\omega}\partial_{s}+\mathcal{L})^{-1}
turned out to be somewhat simpler than their counterparts for

We now introduce the Banach space in which we are going to solve (
77

X:=\ell^{1}\left(\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}^{*})\right)\subset\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N}^{*})^{\mathbb{Z}},

that is,

X=\left\{a=\left(a_{m,n}\right)_{m\in\mathbb{Z},n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}},\ \left\|a\right\|_{X}:=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}a_{m,n}^{2}\right)^{1/2}<\infty\right\},

and the corresponding function space, incorporating some symmetries of the problem:

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}:=\left\{u:(s,y)\mapsto\sum_{m\in 2\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}a_{m,n}e^{ims}p_{2n}(y)+\sum_{m\in(2\mathbb{Z}+1)}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}a_{m,n}e^{ims}p_{2n-1}(y)\mid a\in X\right\}\subset\mathcal{C}^{0}(\mathbb{T},L^{2}(\nu)).

The norm on

is the one inherited from

, that is,
\left\|u\right\|_{\mathcal{X}}:=\left\|a\right\|_{X}
. Note that for all

\|u\|_{\mathcal{C}^{0}(\mathbb{T},L^{2}(\nu))}:=\sup_{s\in\mathbb{T}}\|u(s,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}\leq\|u\|_{\mathcal{X}}.

The basis associated to

is given by

\mathcal{E}=\bigcup_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\{e^{ims}\}\otimes\mathcal{P}_{[m]},\qquad\mathcal{P}_{[m]}:=\{p_{[m]+2n-2}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}},

where

[m]:=\begin{cases}1\quad\text{if }m\text{ is odd},\\
2\quad\text{if }m\text{ is even}.\end{cases}

Furthermore, for an element of

we denote by

the projection on its first

components. In particular, if

corresponds to an element of

, and similarly
\Pi_{>N}a_{m,\cdot}=(a_{m,n})_{n>N}
corresponds to an element of
\overline{\mathrm{Span}\{p_{[m]+2n-2}\}_{n>N}}
.

We consider an approximate solution

of (

and the exact solution

Main difficulties and outline of the strategy for obtaining the
Z
bound

As is common in such computer-assisted proofs, our construction of
A
, which will be detailed below, relies on the fact that
\mathcal{F}=\mathrm{id}+\tilde{\eta}\cos{s}(\mathcal{J}(\tilde{\omega}\partial_{s}+\mathcal{L})^{-1})
is a compact perturbation of the identity.
From a theoretical point of view, the main ingredient required to derive the estimate
Z
is then to quantify the compactness of
\mathcal{J}(\tilde{\omega}\partial_{s}+\mathcal{L})^{-1}
. Because we are looking for a periodic solution in time and therefore work with Fourier series, this essentially reduces to quantifying the compactness
on each fibre
\{e^{ims}\}\otimes L^{2}(\nu)
, that is, to study
\mathcal{J}(i\tilde{\omega}m+\mathcal{L})^{-1}
on
L^{2}(\nu)
. Denoting

\displaystyle L_{m}:=\left[i\tilde{\omega}m+\mathcal{L}\right]_{\mathcal{P}_{[m]}}\quad\text{and}\quad J_{[m]}:=\left[\mathcal{J}\right]_{\mathcal{P}_{[m]}\to\mathcal{P}_{[m+1]}},

these compactness estimates for fixed
m
will be obtained by first computing a factorisation of
L_{m}
the form

L_{m}=U_{m}^{T}U_{m},

where
U_{m}
is an upper-triangular infinite matrix. Then, we will prove that
J_{[m]}U_{m}^{-1}:\ell^{2}\to\ell^{2}
is bounded and that
U_{m}^{-1}:\ell^{2}\to\ell^{2}
is compact. We sometimes refer to
U_{m}^{T}U_{m}
as a Cholesky decomposition of
L_{m}
, even though this is a slight abuse of terminology, as
L_{m}
is not self-adjoint (because of the imaginary entries on its diagonal).

Compared to the situation of Section
4
, where we dealt with the
m=0
case, one extra technical difficulty that we have to face here is that we no longer have an explicit expression for the Cholesky factorisation. This will be dealt with by leveraging the tridiagonal structure of
L_{m}
, using ideas introduced in
[
21
]
. For any fixed
m\in\mathbb{Z}
, we will then be able to obtain quantitative compactness estimates for
U_{m}^{-1}
and therefore for
\mathcal{J}(i\tilde{\omega}m+\mathcal{L})^{-1}
, i.e., compactness in space. Obtaining such estimates with an explicit decay in
m
, i.e., also getting quantitative compactness in time, would in principle be possible, but we can get away with only controlling
\mathcal{J}(i\tilde{\omega}m+\mathcal{L})^{-1}
for finitely many
m
’s, and then showing some monotonicity with respect to
m
.

Let us now discuss a bit more the structure of the zero finding map
F
, and the associated construction of its approximate inverse
A
. Denoting

\displaystyle B_{m}:=\frac{\tilde{\eta}}{2}J_{[m]}L_{m}^{-1},

we have

\displaystyle F=\begin{pNiceMatrix}\ddots&\ddots&&&&&\\
\ddots&&\Block[borders={bottom,left,top,right}]{1-1}{B_{-1}}&&&&\\
&\Block[borders={bottom,left,top,right}]{1-1}{B_{-2}}&\mathrm{id}&\Block[borders={bottom,left,top,right}]{1-1}{B_{0}}&&&\\
&&\Block[borders={bottom,left,top,right}]{1-1}{B_{-1}}&\mathrm{id}&\Block[borders={bottom,left,top,right}]{1-1}{B_{1}}&&\\
&&&\Block[borders={bottom,left,top,right}]{1-1}{B_{0}}&\mathrm{id}&\Block[borders={bottom,left,top,right}]{1-1}{B_{2}}&\\
&&&&\Block[borders={bottom,left,top,right}]{1-1}{B_{1}}&&\ddots\\
&&&&&\ddots&\ddots\\
\end{pNiceMatrix}.

That is,
F
is block-tridiagonal, but each
B_{m}
is full. Because we expect each
B_{m}:\ell^{2}\to\ell^{2}
to be compact (compactness in space), and also
\|B_{m}\|_{\ell^{2}}
to become arbitrarily small for large enough
m
(compactness in time), we consider an approximate inverse
A
of
F

The

to equation (

Getting a computable

,

\displaystyle\|T\|_{X}\leq\sup_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\|T_{ij}\|_{\ell^{2}},

which is our starting point for getting a computable

Lemma 56
.

For all
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
\left\|B_{m+2k}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq\left\|B_{m}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}
, and
\left\|B_{-m-2k}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq\left\|B_{-m}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}
.

Proof.

We have that

\displaystyle\|B_{\pm(m+2k)}\|_{\ell^{2}\to\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle=\|J_{[m]}L_{\pm(m+2k)}^{-1}\|_{\ell^{2}\to\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\|J_{[m]}L_{\pm m}^{-1}\|_{\ell^{2}\to\ell^{2}}\|L_{\pm m}L_{\pm(m+2k)}^{-1}\|_{\ell^{2}\to\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle=\|B_{\pm m}\|_{\ell^{2}\to\ell^{2}}\|L_{\pm m}L_{\pm(m+2k)}^{-1}\|_{\ell^{2}\to\ell^{2}}.

Without loss of generality, assume that
m
is even and denote by
(\rho_{j},v_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
the eigenpairs of
L_{0}
, where the
\rho_{j}
’s are real positive and the
v_{j}
’s are orthonormal. Since
L_{m}=\left[i\tilde{\omega}m+\mathcal{L}\right]_{\mathcal{P}_{[m]}}
, we have that
L_{m}v_{j}=(\rho_{j}+i\tilde{\omega}m)v_{j}
for all
j
. Then for any
w=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}w_{j}v_{j}\in\ell^{2}
,

\|L_{\pm m}L^{-1}_{\pm(m+2k)}w\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}=\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\left|\frac{\rho_{j}\pm i\tilde{\omega}m}{\rho_{j}\pm i\tilde{\omega}(m+2k)}\right|^{2}w_{j}^{2}\leq\sup_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{\rho_{j}^{2}+\tilde{\omega}^{2}m^{2}}{\rho_{j}^{2}+\tilde{\omega}^{2}(m+2k)^{2}}\sum_{j\in\mathbb{N}}w_{j}^{2}\leq\|w\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}.\qed

According to (
83
) (or equivalently, to (
84
)), for all
j\geq M+2
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\|T_{ij}\|_{\ell^{2}}=\|T_{j-1,j}\|_{\ell^{2}}+\|T_{j+1,j}\|_{\ell^{2}}=\|B_{j}\|_{\ell^{2}}+\|B_{j+2}\|_{\ell^{2}},

and similarly, for all
j\leq-M-2
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\|T_{ij}\|_{\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle=\|B_{j}\|_{\ell^{2}}+\|B_{j-2}\|_{\ell^{2}}.

Using Lemma
56
, and then once more the structure of
T
(see again (
83
)), we get

\displaystyle\|T\|_{X}\leq\max_{|j|\leq M+3}\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\|T_{ij}\|_{\ell^{2}}=\max_{|j|\leq M+3}\sum_{|i|\leq M+4}\|T_{ij}\|_{\ell^{2}}.

We thus reduced the problem of getting a computable
Z
estimate to that of having to derive finitely many computable estimates
Z_{ij}
such that

\|T_{ij}\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq Z_{ij}\quad\text{for }|i|\leq M+4,\ |j|\leq M+3.

Once such estimates are obtained, we consider

Z:=\max_{|j|\leq M+3}\sum_{|i|\leq M+4}Z_{ij},

(87)

which then satisfies
\|T\|_{X}\leq Z
.

Remark 57
.

Let us briefly explain why we can expect such a
Z
to be less than one, i.e., why we can expect the
\ell^{1}
norm of each “block column” of
T
in (
83
) to be less than one (where each entry of a block column is in fact an operator on
L^{2}
), at least when
M
is taken large enough. For most of the block columns this is clear, as
\|B_{j}\|_{\ell^{2}}\to 0
when
|j|\to\infty
, and
A
was chosen so that
\bar{A}\bar{F}\approx\mathrm{id}
(at least provided the truncation level
N
used for defining
A^{11}
is also taken large enough, this will be discussed more precisely in Remark
59
). For the columns of
T
of index
\pm(M+1)
, this might be less obvious at first glance: even if
\|B_{\pm(M+1)}\|_{\ell^{2}}
decrease with
M
, the number of non-zero terms on these two columns increases with
M
. However, given the block-tridiagonal structure of
\bar{F}^{11}
(see Eq. (
82
)) with the off-block-diagonal elements
\|B_{j}\|_{\ell^{2}}\to 0
as
|j|\to\infty
, we expect
\|A_{ij}\|_{\ell^{2}}
to decay exponentially with
|i-j|
, and
\|A_{jj}\|_{\ell^{2}}
to be of order
1
for large
|j|
. Therefore, we expect the sum
\sum_{|i|\leq M}\|A_{i,\pm M}\|_{\ell^{2}}
to be bounded uniformly in
M
. Using once more that
\|B_{j}\|_{\ell^{2}}\to 0
as
|j|\to\infty
, the norm of the whole column, i.e.,

\sum_{|i|\leq M+4}\|T_{i,\pm(M+1)}\|_{\ell^{2}}=\|B_{\pm(M+1)}\|_{\ell^{2}}+\sum_{|i|\leq M}\|A_{i,\pm M}B_{\pm(M+1)}\|_{\ell^{2}},

should indeed become less than
1
for
M
large enough.

In practice, we may need to take
M
relatively large for this to hold, as
\|B_{j}\|_{\ell^{2}}
is of order
|\tilde{\omega}j|^{-1/2}
, where, for our examples,
\tilde{\omega}=\frac{0.001\sqrt{2}}{\sigma}\ll 1
.

Even if we only have finitely many estimates
\|T_{ij}\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq Z_{ij}
to obtain, each
T_{ij}
itself is still an infinite dimensional operator (acting on
L^{2}(\nu)\simeq\ell^{2}
). Therefore, we further carve the
T_{ij}
’s in four parts

T_{ij}=\begin{pNiceMatrix}\Block[borders={bottom,right}]{2-3}{T^{11}_{ij}}&&&\Block[borders={bottom}]{2-5}{T^{12}_{ij}}&&&&\\
&&&&&&&\\
\Block[borders={}]{3-3}{T^{21}_{ij}}&&&\Block[borders={top,left}]{3-5}{T^{22}_{ij}}&&&&\\
&&&&&&&\\
&&&&&&&\end{pNiceMatrix},

(88)

where
T_{ij}^{11}=\Pi_{\leq N}T_{ij}\Pi_{\leq N-[j]}
,
T_{ij}^{12}=\Pi_{\leq N}T_{ij}\Pi_{>N-[j]}
,
T_{ij}^{21}=\Pi_{>N}T_{ij}\Pi_{\leq N-[j]}
and
T_{ij}^{22}=\Pi_{>N}T_{ij}\Pi_{>N-[j]}
.

We are going to estimate each of the four blocks separately, i.e., find constants
Z_{ij}^{kl}
such that
\|T^{kl}_{ij}\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq Z^{kl}_{ij}
, and then take

Z_{ij}:=\left\|\begin{pmatrix}Z^{11}_{ij}&Z^{12}_{ij}\\
Z^{21}_{ij}&Z^{22}_{ij}\end{pmatrix}\right\|_{\ell^{2}}.

(89)

We emphasise that we did not use the same truncation level on the left (namely
N
) and on the right (namely

) in order to define the four blocks within

. This is not mandatory, but will allow us to make some of the bounds

simpler and sharper. In particular, the fact that the projection on the right depends on the parity of

is related to the fact that

starts with a row of zeros but

does not (see (

), and also Remark

In the next statement, we give computable estimates

. This constitutes the most technical step of our proof of Theorem

Appendix D
Quadrature

In order to evaluate the bounds given in Section
4.2
, we need to compute rigorously many integrals of the form

\frac{1}{\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{m}f_{k}\right)e^{-v}\mathrm{d}x,

where the
f_{k}
’s are polynomials,
m=4
or
m=6
,
v
is a quartic potential of the form
v=mV/2
, and
\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}
is the corresponding normalisation constant. Similarly, we denote the orthonormal polynomials with respect to
e^{-v}
by
\{\tilde{p}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and the coefficients of their recurrence relation (
1
) by
\{\tilde{a}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
. We achieve this integration by essentially following the method employed in
[
20
, §3]
(see also
[
25
]
in the case of unweighted polynomial nonlinearities). Given some
N\in\mathbb{N}
such that
2N-1\geq\sum_{k=1}^{m}\deg{f_{k}}
, we denote by
(\tilde{x}_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
the roots of
\tilde{p}_{N}
. Then, given the weights
[
60
, p. 390]

\tilde{W}_{i}=\frac{\tilde{a}_{N}}{\tilde{a}_{N-1}}\frac{\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}}\int\tilde{p}_{N-1}^{2}e^{-v}\mathrm{d}x}{\tilde{p}^{\prime}_{N}(\tilde{x}_{i})p_{N_{1}}(\tilde{x}_{i})}=\frac{\tilde{a}_{N}}{\tilde{a}_{N-1}}\frac{1}{\displaystyle\left(\frac{N}{\tilde{a}_{N}}p_{N-1}(\tilde{x}_{i})+\tilde{a}_{N}\tilde{a}_{N-1}\tilde{a}_{N-2}p_{N-3}(\tilde{x}_{i})\right)p_{N_{1}}(\tilde{x}_{i})},\qquad i=1,\ldots,N,

we have that

\frac{1}{\tilde{\mathcal{Z}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{m}f_{k}\right)e^{-v}\mathrm{d}x=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\tilde{W}_{i}\left(\prod_{k=1}^{m}f_{k}(\tilde{x}_{i})\right).

The
\tilde{x}_{i}
’s are enclosed rigorously similarly to
[
20
]
by

•

finding approximate roots of
\tilde{p}_{N}
by numerically finding eigenvalues of the
N
-dimensional (tridiagonal) Jacobi matrix constructed from
\{\tilde{a}_{n}\}_{n=1}^{N-1}
,

•

refining these approximate roots using Newton’s method in
BigFloat
arithmetic,

•

and rigorously enclosing these roots via a combination of the Intermediate Value Theorem and the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra (recall that such orthogonal polynomials always have simple real roots).

As in
[
20
]
, in practice we will express a function
u
in
\Pi_{\leq n}^{L^{2}}(L^{2}(\nu))
with respect to the basis
\{p_{n}\}
(orthogonal with respect to
\mathrm{d}\nu=e^{-V}\mathrm{d}x/\mathcal{Z}
now!), i.e.

u=\sum_{j=0}^{n}c_{j}p_{j},\qquad\mathbf{c}:=(c_{0},\ldots,c_{n})^{T},

such that it suffices to evaluate
p_{j}(\tilde{x}_{i})
via the recurrence relation

p_{j+1}(\tilde{x}_{i})=\frac{\tilde{x}_{i}p_{j}(\tilde{x}_{i})-a_{j}p_{j-1}(\tilde{x}_{i})}{a_{j+1}}\qquad i=1,\ldots,N.

This defines a pseudo-Vandermonde matrix
M=(p_{j}(\tilde{x}_{i}))
. As in
[
20
]
, we regularise
M
by replacing it by
\bar{M}=\mathrm{Diag}(W)^{1/m}M
which can be stored in lower precision. For instance, we then have that the finite-dimensional projection of the a cubic nonlinearity can be handled by the matrix

G_{ij}=\langle p_{i},e^{-V}u^{2}p_{j}\rangle,

which can be written as
G=\bar{M}^{T}\mathrm{Diag}(\bar{M}\mathbf{c})^{2}\bar{M}
.

(108)

Proof.

According to Lemma

, for all

\left|\frac{\mu_{n+2k}}{i\tilde{\omega}m+\lambda_{n+2k}}\right|\leq\chi\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left|\frac{\mu_{n+2k}}{i\tilde{\omega}m+\lambda_{n+2k+2}}\right|\leq\chi.

We then obtain the following recurrence inequality

\left|\frac{d_{n+2k+2}^{2}}{i\tilde{\omega}m+\lambda_{n+2k+2}}\right|=\left|1-\frac{\mu_{n+2k}}{i\tilde{\omega}m+\lambda_{n+2k}}\frac{\mu_{n+2k}}{i\tilde{\omega}m+\lambda_{n+2k+2}}\frac{i\tilde{\omega}m+\lambda_{n+2k}}{d_{n+2k}^{2}}\right|\geq 1-\chi^{2}\left|\frac{i\tilde{\omega}m+\lambda_{n+2k}}{d_{n+2k}^{2}}\right|.

Since

, one then readily shows by induction that, for all

the base case being assumption (

Therefore, for all

and the lower bound on

from Lemma

yields (

then directly follow from the lower bounds on

,
\beta_{k}
and
\mu_{k}
from Lemma

Careful construction of the approximate inverse
A^{11}

We give here some details regarding the construction of the approximate inverse
A
, and more precisely about the finite dimensional part
A^{11}
. As explained in Section
5.3
,
A^{11}
should be taken as an approximate inverse of
\bar{F}^{11}
given in (
82
). Indeed, when deriving the
Z
estimate we then have to compute (rigorously, using interval arithmetic)
\mathrm{id}-A^{11}\bar{F}^{11}
, and would like this term to be very small. However, in practice the matrices
A^{11}
and
\bar{F}^{11}
are rather large (of size
(2M+1)(N+1)\times(2M+1)(N+1)
), hence we would like this computation to be done as efficiently as possible. To that end, we will introduce a reformulation of the problem that makes
\bar{F}^{11}
sparser. We note that
\bar{F}^{11}
is already somewhat sparse thanks to its block-tridiagonal structure, but each of the blocks
B^{11}_{m}=\frac{\tilde{\eta}}{2}\Pi_{\leq N}J_{[m]}L_{m}^{-1}\Pi_{\leq N}
is still full, and this is what we are going to improve upon.

Since
J_{[m]}
is lower triangular,
\Pi_{\leq N}J_{[m]}L_{m}^{-1}\Pi_{\leq N}=\Pi_{\leq N}J_{[m]}\Pi_{\leq N}L_{m}^{-1}\Pi_{\leq N}
. Defining
\tilde{L}_{m}^{11}=(\Pi_{\leq N}L_{m}^{-1}\Pi_{\leq N})^{-1}
and
\tilde{L}^{11}=\mathrm{Diag}(\tilde{L}^{11}_{-M},\ldots,\tilde{L}^{11}_{0},\ldots,\tilde{L}^{11}_{M})
, we consider

\tilde{F}^{11}:=\bar{F}^{11}\tilde{L}^{11}=\begin{pNiceMatrix}\tilde{L}^{11}_{-M}&\Block[borders={left,bottom,top,right}]{1-1}{\tfrac{\tilde{\eta}}{2}J^{11}_{[1-M]}}&&&&&&&\\
\Block[borders={bottom,top,right}]{1-1}{\tfrac{\tilde{\eta}}{2}J^{11}_{[-M]}}&\ddots&\ddots&&&&&&\\
&\ddots&\Block[borders={left,top}]{1-1}{\tilde{L}^{11}_{-2}}&\Block[borders={bottom,left,top,right}]{1-1}{\tfrac{\tilde{\eta}}{2}J^{11}_{1}}&&&&&\\
&&\Block[borders={bottom,left,top,right}]{1-1}{\tfrac{\tilde{\eta}}{2}J^{11}_{2}}&\tilde{L}^{11}_{-1}&\Block[borders={bottom,left,top,right}]{1-1}{\tfrac{\tilde{\eta}}{2}J^{11}_{2}}&&&&\\
&&&\Block[borders={bottom,left,top,right}]{1-1}{\tfrac{\tilde{\eta}}{2}J^{11}_{1}}&\tilde{L}^{11}_{0}&\Block[borders={bottom,left,top,right}]{1-1}{\tfrac{\tilde{\eta}}{2}J^{11}_{1}}&&&\\
&&&&\Block[borders={bottom,left,top,right}]{1-1}{\tfrac{\tilde{\eta}}{2}J^{11}_{2}}&\tilde{L}^{11}_{1}&\Block[borders={bottom,left,top,right}]{1-1}{\tfrac{\tilde{\eta}}{2}J^{11}_{2}}&&\\
&&&&&\Block[borders={bottom,left,top,right}]{1-1}{\tfrac{\tilde{\eta}}{2}J^{11}_{1}}&\Block[borders={bottom,left,top,right}]{1-1}{\tilde{L}^{11}_{2}}&\ddots&\\
&&&&&&\ddots&\ddots&\Block[borders={bottom,left,top}]{1-1}{\tfrac{\tilde{\eta}}{2}J^{11}_{[M]}}\\
&&&&&&&\Block[borders={bottom,left,top,right}]{1-1}{\tfrac{\tilde{\eta}}{2}J^{11}_{[M-1]}}&\tilde{L}^{11}_{M}\end{pNiceMatrix}.

We now show that each block
\tilde{L}^{11}_{m}
is at most tridiagonal, hence confirming that
\tilde{F}^{11}
is very sparse. Recalling that
L_{m}=U^{T}\,U
, and using the formula (
101
) for
U^{-1}
, we get that

\displaystyle\Pi_{\leq N}L_{m}^{-1}\Pi_{\leq N}

\displaystyle=U_{\leq N}^{-1}(U_{\leq N}^{-1})^{T}+(\mu_{n}/d_{n})^{2}\left((U_{\leq N}^{-1})_{:,-1}(U_{>N}^{-1})_{1,:}\right)\left(\left(U_{\leq N}^{-1}\right)_{:,-1}\left(U_{>N}^{-1}\right)_{1,:}\right)^{T}

\displaystyle=U_{\leq N}^{-1}(U_{\leq N}^{-1})^{T}+\xi_{N}\left((U_{\leq N}^{-1})_{:,-1}\,((U_{\leq N}^{-1})_{:,-1})^{T}\right),

where

, and the complex number

is given by

\xi_{N}=\left(\frac{\mu_{n}}{d_{n}}\right)^{2}\,(U^{-1}_{>N})_{1,:}\,((U^{-1}_{>N})_{1,:})^{T}=\left(\frac{\mu_{n}}{d_{n}}\right)^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{d_{n+2k}^{2}}\prod_{l=1}^{k-1}\frac{\mu_{n+2l}^{2}}{d_{n+2l}^{4}}.

Therefore, by the Sherman–Morrison formula,

\tilde{L}_{m}^{11}=(\Pi_{\leq N}L_{m}^{-1}\Pi_{\leq N})^{-1}=\Pi_{\leq N}L_{m}\Pi_{\leq N}-\frac{\xi_{N}}{1+\xi_{N}}d_{n}^{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&\cdots&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&&\vdots\\
\vdots&&0&0\\
0&\cdots&0&1\end{pmatrix},

(114)

which is indeed tridiagonal.

We then consider
\tilde{A}^{11}=(\tilde{A}^{11}_{ij})_{-M\leq i,j,\leq M}
a numerically computed inverse of
\tilde{F}^{11}
, and define

. Thanks to this deliberate choice of

\displaystyle\|\tilde{L}_{i}^{11}R_{ij}^{11}(\tilde{L}_{j}^{11})^{-1}\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq\|\tilde{L}_{i}^{11}\|_{\ell^{2}}\|R_{ij}^{11}\|_{\ell^{2}}\|(\tilde{L}_{j}^{11})^{-1}\|_{\ell^{2}}.

Remark 67
.

Even though
\tilde{F}^{11}
itself is very sparse, its (numerical) inverse
\tilde{A}^{11}
is not. In order to alleviate memory constraints, we therefore compute
\tilde{A}^{11}
block-column-wise. That is, we compute
(\tilde{A}_{ij})_{-M\leq i\leq M}
one

at a time. Since the
(Z_{ij})_{-M\leq i\leq M}
depends only on
(\tilde{A}_{i,j-1})_{-M\leq i\leq M}

(\tilde{A}_{ij})_{-M\leq i\leq M}
and
(\tilde{A}_{i,j+1})_{-M\leq i\leq M}
, we then only need to keep in memory three block columns at a time. This allows us to use relatively little memory even if we take
M
to be large. The banded-block-tridiagonal structure of
\tilde{F}^{11}
allows us to make this procedure efficient, as we compute its block-tridiagonal

decomposition once, and then use it

times to solve for
(\tilde{A}_{ij})_{-M\leq i\leq M}
.

Remark 68

We can furthermore roughly halve the cost of the proof by exploiting the “real-preserving” structure of the equation and by choosing

(117)

where we crucially used that the top-right block of

Some embedding constants

The first part of Theorem
54
provides us with a computable upper bound for
\|v-\bar{v}\|_{\mathcal{X}}
. In order to obtain the second part of Theorem
54
(and thus Theorem
10
, Theorem
49
and Theorem
50
), we need to control

e^{V/2}(\rho-\bar{\rho})=\frac{\sqrt[4]{2}}{\sqrt{\sigma}}\frac{e^{-V/2}}{\mathcal{Z}}(\tilde{\omega}\partial_{s}+\mathcal{L})^{-1}(v-\bar{v}),

and
\mathcal{R}(\varrho)-\mathcal{R}(\bar{\varrho})
, which is done thanks to the two following lemmas.

Lemma 72
.

Suppose that
u\in(\tilde{\omega}\partial_{s}+\mathcal{L})^{-1}\mathcal{X}
, then

\left\|\frac{e^{-V/2}}{\mathcal{Z}}u\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{0}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R})}\leq\sqrt{\frac{C_{\mathcal{J}}+1}{\mathcal{Z}}}C_{P}^{3/4}\|(\tilde{\omega}\partial_{s}+\mathcal{L})u\|_{\mathcal{X}}.

Proof.

Writing
u(s,y)=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{ims}u_{m}(y)
and using Lemma
40
, we have that

\left\|\frac{e^{-V/2}}{\mathcal{Z}}u\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{0}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R})}\leq\sqrt{\frac{C_{\mathcal{J}}+1}{\mathcal{Z}}}C_{P}^{1/4}\|u\|_{\mathcal{C}^{0}(H^{1}(\nu))}\leq\sqrt{\frac{C_{\mathcal{J}}+1}{\mathcal{Z}}}C_{P}^{1/4}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\|u_{m}\|_{H^{1}(\nu)},

where we replaced
\max(C_{P},1)
by
C_{P}
in Lemma
40
, because
u
has mean zero (against
\nu
). Since
u_{m}\in H^{1}_{0}(\nu)
, reusing the operators and notations introduced in Section
2.4
, we have

\displaystyle\|u_{m}\|_{H^{1}(\nu)}

\displaystyle=\|[u_{m}]_{\{q_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}}\|_{\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle=\|[u_{m}]_{H^{1}}\|_{\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle=\|(P^{-1})^{T}P^{T}[u_{m}]_{H^{1}}\|_{\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle=\|(D_{>0}^{-1})^{T}D_{>0}^{T}[u_{m}]_{H^{1}}\|_{\ell^{2}}\qquad\mbox{as $(u,q_{0})=\langle u,p_{0}\rangle=0$}

\displaystyle\leq\|(D_{>0}^{-1})^{T}\|_{\ell^{2}}\|P^{T}[u_{m}]_{H^{1}}\|_{\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle=C_{P}^{1/2}\|[\mathcal{L}]_{H^{1}\to L^{2}}[u_{m}]_{H^{1}}\|_{\ell^{2}}\qquad\mbox{thanks to\penalty\ \eqref{eq:CP} and\penalty\ \eqref{eqn:tL-decomp}}

\displaystyle=C_{P}^{1/2}\|[\mathcal{L}u_{m}]_{L^{2}}\|_{\ell^{2}}

\displaystyle=C_{P}^{1/2}\|\mathcal{L}u_{m}\|_{L^{2}(\nu)},

and thus

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{e^{-V/2}}{\mathcal{Z}}u\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{0}(\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R})}\leq\sqrt{\frac{C_{\mathcal{J}}+1}{\mathcal{Z}}}C_{P}^{3/4}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\|\mathcal{L}u_{m}\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}=\sqrt{\frac{C_{\mathcal{J}}+1}{\mathcal{Z}}}C_{P}^{3/4}\|\mathcal{L}u\|_{\mathcal{X}}.

(118)

Finally, note that

\|\mathcal{L}u\|_{\mathcal{X}}\leq\|(\tilde{\omega}\partial_{s}+\mathcal{L})u\|_{\mathcal{X}}.

(119)

Indeed, since
\mathcal{L}
is diagonalisable in an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\nu)
, for all
m\in\mathbb{Z}
we have

\left\|(i\tilde{\omega}m+\mathcal{L})u_{m}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\nu)}=\tilde{\omega}^{2}m^{2}\|u_{m}\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}^{2}+\|\mathcal{L}u_{m}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\nu)}\geq\|\mathcal{L}u_{m}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\nu)},

hence

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{L}u\|_{\mathcal{X}}=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\|\mathcal{L}u_{m}\|_{\mathcal{X}}\leq\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\left\|(i\tilde{\omega}m+\mathcal{L})u_{m}\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\nu)}=\|(\tilde{\omega}\partial_{s}+\mathcal{L})u\|_{\mathcal{X}}.

Combining (
118
) and (
119
) finishes the proof.
∎

Lemma 73
.

Let
u\in(\tilde{\omega}\partial_{s}+\mathcal{L})^{-1}\mathcal{X}
, let
v=(\tilde{\omega}\partial_{s}+\mathcal{L})u
, let
c\in\mathbb{R}
and let

\varrho=\varrho(t,x)=(c+u(s,y))\frac{\sqrt[4]{2}}{\sqrt{\sigma}}\frac{e^{-y^{4}/4+\tilde{\kappa}y^{2}/2}}{\mathcal{Z}},\qquad\mathcal{Z}=\int_{\mathbb{R}}e^{-y^{4}/4+\tilde{\kappa}y^{2}/2}\mathrm{d}y.

(120)

Then, the quantity
\mathcal{R}(\varrho)
defined in (
71
) satisfies

\mathcal{R}(\varrho)\leq C_{P}\frac{\sqrt{\sigma b_{1}}}{\sqrt[4]{2}}\|v\|_{\mathcal{X}}.

Proof.

First, from change of variable (
72
) we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\varrho)

\displaystyle=\sup_{t\in\mathbb{T}/\omega}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}x\varrho(t,x)\mathrm{d}x\right|

\displaystyle=\sup_{s\in\mathbb{T}}\frac{\sqrt{\sigma}}{\sqrt[4]{2}}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}y(c+u(s,y))\frac{e^{-V(s)}}{\mathcal{Z}}\mathrm{d}y\right|\qquad\mbox{where }V(y)=\dfrac{y^{4}}{4}-\tilde{\kappa}\dfrac{y^{2}}{2}

\displaystyle=\sup_{s\in\mathbb{T}}\frac{\sqrt{\sigma}}{\sqrt[4]{2}}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}yu(s,y)\frac{e^{-V(y)}}{\mathcal{Z}}\mathrm{d}y\right|\qquad\mbox{since }y\mbox{ is odd and }e^{-V(y)}\mbox{ even}

\displaystyle=\sup_{s\in\mathbb{T}}\frac{\sqrt{\sigma b_{1}}}{\sqrt[4]{2}}\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{1}(y)u(s,y)\frac{e^{-V(y)}}{\mathcal{Z}}\mathrm{d}y\right|\qquad\mbox{since }\sqrt{b_{1}}=\dfrac{y}{p_{1}(y)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\sigma b_{1}}}{\sqrt[4]{2}}\sup_{s\in\mathbb{T}}|\langle p_{1},u(s,\cdot)\rangle|.

Thus, using the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and the fact that
\|p_{1}\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}=1
, we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\varrho)\leq\frac{\sqrt{\sigma b_{1}}}{\sqrt[4]{2}}\sup_{s\in\mathbb{T}}\|u(s,\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(\nu)}\leq\frac{\sqrt{\sigma b_{1}}}{\sqrt[4]{2}}\|u\|_{\mathcal{X}}.

Then, we reuse (
119
), or more precisely the fact that

\|(\tilde{\omega}\partial_{s}+\mathcal{L})^{-1}\|_{\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}}\leq\|\mathcal{L}^{-1}\|_{L^{2}_{0}(\nu)\to L^{2}_{0}(\nu)},

where
L^{2}_{0}(\nu)=\overline{\mathrm{Span}\{p_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}}}
is the subspace of
L^{2}(\nu)
of zero mean functions with respect to
\nu
(on which
\mathcal{L}
is invertible). Finally, since
[\mathcal{L}]_{L^{2}_{0}(\nu)}=D_{>0}^{T}D_{>0}
(see Proposition

\|\mathcal{L}^{-1}\|_{L^{2}_{0}(\nu)}=\|D_{>0}^{-1}\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}=C_{P}
, and thus

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\mathcal{X}}=\|(\tilde{\omega}\partial_{s}+\mathcal{L})^{-1}v\|_{\mathcal{X}}\leq C_{P}\|v\|_{\mathcal{X}},

which finishes the proof.
∎

Block-wise computation of operator norms

Lemma 75
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
be Banach spaces. For each
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}
, let
M_{ij}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}
be a bounded operator. Consider the operator
M=(M_{ij})_{i,j,\in\mathbb{Z}}
. If
(\|M_{ij}\|_{\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}})_{i,j\in\mathbb{Z}}:\ell^{p}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{R})\to\ell^{q}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{R})
is bounded for some
p,q\in[1,\infty]
, then
M:\ell^{p}(\mathbb{Z},\mathcal{X})\to\ell^{q}(\mathbb{Z},\mathcal{Y})
is bounded.
Furthermore,

\|M\|_{\ell^{p}(\mathcal{X})\to\ell^{q}(\mathcal{Y})}\leq\|(\|M_{ij}\|_{\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}})_{i,j\in\mathbb{Z}}\|_{\ell^{p}\to\ell^{q}}.

Proof.

We prove the statement for
p,q<\infty
, the other cases being similar. Let
y=(y_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\in\ell^{p}(\mathcal{X})
, then

\displaystyle\|My\|_{\ell^{q}(\mathcal{Y})}^{q}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\Big\|\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}M_{ij}y_{j}\Big\|^{q}_{\mathcal{Y}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\Big\|M_{ij}y_{j}\Big\|_{\mathcal{Y}}\right)^{q}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\sum_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\|M_{ij}\|_{\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}}\|y_{j}\|_{\mathcal{X}}\right)^{q}

\displaystyle\leq\Big\|(\|M_{ij}\|_{\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}})_{i,j\in\mathbb{Z}}\Big\|^{q}_{\ell^{p}\to\ell^{q}}\Big\|(\|y_{j}\|_{\mathcal{X}})_{j\in\mathbb{Z}}\Big\|^{q}_{\ell^{p}}

\displaystyle\leq\Big\|(\|M_{ij}\|_{\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{Y}})_{i,j\in\mathbb{Z}}\Big\|^{q}_{\ell^{p}\to\ell^{q}}\|y\|^{q}_{\ell^{p}(\mathcal{X})}.\qed
