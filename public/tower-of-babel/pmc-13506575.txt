ShortSCOPE: An Optical Short-Period Seismic Sensor for Planetary Exploration
Authors: Daniella N DellaGiustina, Bradley D Avenson, Donghwan Kim, Angela G Marusiak, Veronica J Bray, Daniel Hofstadter, Steven Meyer, Scott E Otterbacher, Dathon R Golish, Carina A Bennett, Nicholas C Schmerr, Mehdi Benna, Erin C Pettit, Ronald-Louis Ballouz, Renee C Weber, S “Hop” Bailey
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSeismology offers a window into the formation, structure, and dynamical processes of solar system bodies. Seismic data from Earth, the near side of the Moon, and Elysium Planitia on Mars have transformed our understanding of planetary interiors. Expanding these insights to new targets—such as ocean worlds and asteroids—requires seismometers with enhanced sensitivity, low power consumption, and robustness in extreme environments. We present the performance and environmental qualification of the Short-Period Seismic Characterization using Optics for Planetary Exploration (“ShortSCOPE”) sensor, a novel force-balanced device tailored for future missions. Originally developed under the Europa Lander mission concept, the ShortSCOPE sensor has since been adapted for lunar deployment, achieving Technology Readiness Level 6. The ShortSCOPE sensor operates at low noise (targeting at 10 Hz) with a flat response across a wide frequency band (0.0095 to 150 Hz), and survives shock (up to 500g), random vibration (14.1g RMS), and thermal vacuum cycling from −55 °C to +70 °C, surviving at housing temperatures as low as −170 °C. It is compact (3.2 cm × 4 cm), lightweight (115 grams per sensor), and consumes little power (40 mW per sensor). The design offers exceptional tilt tolerance (at least ± 30° in Europan or lunar gravity), allowing deployment without leveling mechanisms. ShortSCOPE sensors will be integrated into the candidate Artemis–deployed Lunar Environment Monitoring Station, providing the first long-duration seismic observations from the lunar south pole, and new constraints on seismic activity from the Moon at frequencies >20 Hz.Keywords: Seismometer, Europa lander, Lunar environment monitoring station (LEMS), Seismology

Introduction

Seismology is foundational for investigating planetary interiors and dynamics, with a demonstrated legacy across multiple Solar System bodies. On Earth, seismic observations revealed the presence of both an inner and outer core (Oldham 1906; Gutenberg 1914; Lehmann 1936) and played a central role in establishing the theory of plate tectonics (Sykes 1963). The Apollo missions deployed the first extraterrestrial seismic network, yielding insights into the Moon’s internal structure and tidal seismicity (Nakamura 1982; Heffels et al. 2017). More recently, the InSight mission on Mars recorded more than 1,300 seismic events, including several with moment magnitudes Mw ≥ 4.0, providing information about the Martian crust, mantle, and core (Banerdt et al. 2020; Knapmeyer-Endrun et al. 2021; Khan et al. 2021, 2023; Stähler et al. 2021; Samuel et al. 2023). Seismic measurements can resolve the location and focal mechanisms of tectonic events, delineate subsurface layering via body and surface wave propagation, and support geochemical and geodynamic interpretations of planetary interiors.

These capabilities are particularly relevant to future missions targeting the icy moons of the outer Solar System. For example, Europa, Titan, and Enceladus are hypothesized to contain global subsurface oceans in contact with silicate mantles, potentially satisfying the three canonical requirements for life as we know it: liquid water, essential chemical species, and energy sources (Siegel et al. 1979; Marion et al. 2003; McKay and Smith 2005; Hsu et al. 2015). The potential for habitability in these environments depends in part on the exchange of materials—such as surface-derived oxidants—between the ice shell and the ocean. Understanding the mechanisms and spatial distribution of such exchange requires knowledge of ice shell thickness, the depth of underlying liquid reservoirs, and the presence of any intermediate melt layers. Seismometers are uniquely capable of addressing these objectives. Consequently, spacecraft concepts such as the Europa Lander and the upcoming New Frontiers IV mission to Titan, Dragonfly, have included seismic instruments in their proposed payloads (Hand et al. 2017; Turtle et al. 2024).

While the outer Solar System’s icy ocean worlds offer compelling targets for astrobiological exploration, the Moon remains an essential platform for advancing planetary seismology. Despite the successes of the Apollo seismic network, fundamental questions persist regarding the Moon’s internal structure, the frequency and distribution of shallow and deep moonquakes, and the mechanisms driving tectonic activity. These questions are especially relevant at the lunar south pole and far side, regions that remain seismically uncharacterized yet are of strategic interest for both science and human exploration. NASA’s Farside Seismic Suite (FSS), scheduled for delivery to Schrödinger basin via the Commercial Lunar Payload Services (CLPS) program, will mark the first seismic investigation on the far side of the Moon and represents a critical step toward establishing a global lunar seismic network (Panning et al. 2021). In parallel, the Artemis crewed missions (Creech et al. 2022) present upcoming opportunities to deploy modern seismic instrumentation to the lunar south pole, providing continuous, high-fidelity recordings that can refine lunar interior models, inform surface operations, and validate the seismic technologies needed to access other planetary worlds. In this context, the Moon serves not only as a scientific target, but also as a proving ground for developing and maturing the tools that will shape the next generation of planetary geophysics.

Here, we describe the Short-Period Seismic Characterization using Optics for Planetary Exploration (ShortSCOPE) sensor, a flight-candidate technology originally developed under the Europa Lander Pre-Phase A mission concept, now being included for deployment as part of the Lunar Environment Monitoring Station (LEMS) (Benna et al. 2024) slated for a future Artemis lunar surface deployment. We outline the relevant science objectives for a seismic investigation on Europa and discuss how we tailored our sensor design to address them. We then describe the instrument development, its operational characteristics, the environmental testing to which it was subjected, and its readiness for spaceflight. Its deployment on the Moon will significantly enhance the geophysical science return of Artemis, while simultaneously advancing technologies critical for future seismology investigations of ocean worlds, asteroids, and beyond.

Seismic Sensor Design and Performance

A seismic experiment aims to characterize ground motion, typically measured from the displacement of a proof mass. In a conventional geophone, inertial forces deflect the mass from equilibrium, and the proof mass’ velocity is converted into an electrical signal. Modern broadband seismometers instead use a force-balance configuration, in which an electrically generated force counteracts the inertial force, minimizing proof mass motion. Displacement is then measured—often using a capacitive transducer.

Silicon Audio, Inc. (SiA) manufactures commercial-off-the-shelf (COTS) sensors that use a mechanical suspension system akin to a traditional geophone but operate as force-balance accelerometers (Hall et al. 2008; Garcia et al. 2014). In this configuration, motion of the proof mass is counteracted by a coil-magnet actuator within a closed-loop feedback system. Residual displacement is measured using a laser interferometer, providing a highly sensitive optical readout that forms the basis of the sensor’s output (Fig. 1). This design enables a substantially higher dynamic range than conventional geophones. The sensor is well-suited for spaceflight applications, with a compact, low-mass form factor, low power consumption, and ruggedized packaging to withstand extreme thermal and mechanical stresses.

Independent validation in planetary analog environments has benchmarked the SiA COTS seismometers against common seismic instruments (e.g., Trillium compacts, Trillium post-holes, and Sercel 4.5-Hz geophones), demonstrating performance consistent with established systems (Marusiak et al. 2020, 2021).

In the subsequent sections, we detail how we modified the SiA COTS sensor design and performance to support planetary applications by adjusting the proof mass and its suspension system to achieve lower self-noise and greater tilt tolerance. Although initially designed for the seismic environment of Europa, the resulting performance enhancements have broader applicability to other planetary targets—particularly the Moon. The ShortSCOPE sensor was also tested under and modified to withstand launch, vacuum, and thermal environments relevant to robotic planetary missions.

Tailoring for Europa

Europa’s ocean lies beneath a fractured ice shell that exhibits potential evidence of plate-like motion and active surface deformation (Kattenhorn and Prockter 2014; Bland and McKinnon 2017; Collins et al. 2022). In response to the scientific interest in this potentially habitable body, NASA supported development of the Europa Lander mission concept (Hand et al. 2017). The mission’s destination in the outer Solar System necessitated reliance on battery power (the concept did not adopt radioisotope sources), which in turn limited the spacecraft mass and operational lifetime. Payloads would be required to operate within modest resource allocations (Hand et al. 2017). The lander also had to tolerate Europa’s severe particle radiation environment (Paranicas et al. 2007), presenting challenges for electronics durability and shielding.

Seismology was identified as a high-priority investigation for the Europa Lander: A seismometer could constrain the occurrence and spatial distribution of seismic activity within the ice shell, test for plate-like motion, and reveal internal structure, including ice crustal thickness, ocean depth, and stratification within the silicate interior. A 3-axis seismometer was envisioned to be installed inside the spacecraft’s radiation-shielded vault (Hand et al. 2017) and mechanically coupled to the lander structure via a mounting bracket. The instrument would have to meet performance requirements without direct surface deployment. Our Seismometer to Investigate Ice and Ocean Structure (SIIOS) instrument concept supported this configuration: it comprised 3 orthogonal axes, each a single ShortSCOPE sensor, packaged within the 75 mm × 75 mm × 45 mm mission-allocated volume.

The 2017 Europa Lander Concept Study Science Traceability Matrix also identified multiple science objectives that the ShortSCOPE sensor would need to address through its capabilities. Certain seismic phases, such as Crary and Love waves, are expected to exhibit frequency characteristics that vary with ice shell thickness (Crary 1960; Kovach and Chyba 2001; Khurana et al. 2002; Panning et al. 2018). For example, thicker ice shells result in lower characteristic Crary wave frequencies. Likewise, modeling studies have shown that surface wave dispersion can be used to determine mean shell thickness and may even be sensitive to shell topography (Maguire et al. 2021; Marusiak et al. 2023).

The only seismic noise model for Europa published to date estimates ambient seismic noise to be below the self-noise of typical high-frequency geophones, but detectable with lower-noise instrumentation (Panning et al. 2018). However, the model is limited to frequencies ≤0.5 Hz. Observations from terrestrial analogs suggest that high-frequency icequakes—resulting from brittle deformation, crevasse formation, and other cryoseismic processes—commonly occur above 10 Hz, where scattering effects are also more pronounced (Wahr et al. 2009; Podolskiy and Walter 2016). These events are likely to dominate Europa’s ambient noise field and may occur more often than long-period quakes (Lee et al. 2003). Identifying and locating such events will be critical for understanding Europa’s seismicity and for characterizing surface and subsurface mechanical properties.

To address the broad frequency range of anticipated signals, we used the Panning et al. (2018) noise model to set low-frequency design requirements and drew on terrestrial icequake studies to define high-frequency (≥10 Hz) performance targets. For the Europa Lander mission, the COTS SiA sensor required modifications to achieve a self-noise floor of at 10 Hz, enabling detection of long-period signals diagnostic of ocean depth and ice shell structure while retaining bandwidth for high-frequency ice-cracking events. This was accomplished by lowering the resonant frequency from ∼30 Hz to ∼15 Hz and narrowing the bandwidth from 0.005–600 Hz to 0.0095–400 Hz (flat from 0.0095–150 Hz), improving the noise floor with a small trade-off in bandwidth.

Foundational Design Principles

Although specific parameters such as spring geometry, resonant frequency, and proof mass size may vary across designs, all SiA seismic sensors share a common core architecture (Avenson et al. 2017): a coil-wound bobbin serves as the proof mass and is suspended within a magnetic field. The bobbin is constructed from a non-conductive or weakly conductive material, such as polyetherimide, to minimize eddy-current damping. A coil wound around the bobbin enables magnetic actuation, which drives the force-balance mechanism. The bobbin is suspended by springs (e.g., leaf springs) within the housing, allowing it to move relative to the housing in response to ground motion (Fig. 1). This relative motion is measured using a displacement-sensitive Michelson interferometer, which employs an 850-nm vertical-cavity surface-emitting laser (VCSEL) as its light source (Fig. 1). The detected motion is counteracted by the coil-magnet actuator through a closed-loop feedback system, maintaining the proof mass near its equilibrium position. The feedback signal is proportional to the input acceleration and is converted to the sensor’s output.

This architecture is implemented in the ShortSCOPE sensor as two physically distinct but functionally integrated stages (Fig. 1). The mechanical stage houses the actuatable proof mass (coil-wound bobbin) and the grating-mirror assembly of the interferometer. The optical stage consists of a printed circuit board (PCB) and mounting structure that incorporates the lens–VCSEL assembly and photodiode array, which detect intensity variations from the interferometer signal. In the event of an optical stage failure, the feedback damping circuit can remain disengaged, permitting the instrument to operate as a conventional geophone. This configuration provides a graceful failure mode that preserves seismic data acquisition capability even without the optical subsystem.

Noise Performance

Using the SiA COTS sensor as a foundational design, we made several adjustments to achieve the desired self-noise performance. Achieving a lower noise floor was guided by SiA’s proprietary sensor system model representing the instrument’s primary resonance response and feedback system. This model accounts for the electro-mechanical characteristics of the system and subtle differences caused by changes in materials, components, and mechanical design, enabling us to make analytical predictions that guide the design process. A similar process had been used to design five generations of SiA COTS sensors (Models 203-15, 203-60, 213-40, 103-15, and 103-20).

SiA’s sensor system model initially predicted that we could achieve a noise floor approaching at 10 Hz (Fig. 2) by decreasing the overall frequency bandwidth of the ShortSCOPE compared to the SiA COTS device. This could be accomplished by adjusting the spring suspension system to raise the free period T0; that is, lowering the noise while decreasing the resonant frequency, f0.

Modification of the spring-mass system

Refinements to the mechanical suspension system centered on the design, fabrication, and integration of newly developed leaf springs. This effort involved selecting appropriate materials, precisely defining the spring geometry, and ensuring accurate alignment during assembly by monitoring the optical modulation efficiency throughout the process. Design modifications were informed by prior experience with the COTS SiA sensor, for which the mechanical behavior of the suspension and its impact on optical alignment had been previously characterized. Because changes to the suspension geometry directly impact the configuration of the optical interferometer, the design was iterated. Anticipating that new suspension geometries could introduce parasitic resonances not readily captured in simulations, we relied on prototype testing to identify and mitigate these modes. Three prototypes were built and evaluated to verify improved noise performance, and the final configuration was incorporated into the engineering model qualified for environmental testing.

Sensitivity and Digitization

The ShortSCOPE sensor has a sensitivity of 110 V/(m/s2), meaning that with a dynamic range of ±5.5 V, it can record ground accelerations up to ±0.05 m/s2 before the analog output clips. While this sets the intrinsic performance of the sensor, the effective system performance of a seismometer instrument package—such as the observed noise floor and usable dynamic range—also depends on the characteristics of the ADC used to digitize the signal. Ideally, a low-noise ADC is paired with the instrument (e.g., Marusiak et al. 2026). Assuming a seismic sensor with a fixed input-acceleration-referred noise spectral density (NSD, in m/s), increasing the sensitivity (V/m/s2) raises the output-voltage-referred NSD (V/Hz). The output NSD level should be optimized relative to the input-voltage-referred NSD of the paired ADC.

For example, if the sensor and ADC NSDs are equal, the total system noise increases by a factor of 2, resulting in a 41% higher noise floor than that of the sensor alone. In contrast, if the sensor noise is ten times greater than the ADC noise, the ADC contributes only about 0.5% to the overall noise floor. Therefore, to minimize noise added by the ADC, the output-voltage-referred NSD of the sensor must be substantially higher than the input-referred noise of the ADC. However, increasing sensor sensitivity in this way also reduces the clip level for a given ADC input range.

Some high-performance ADCs include a built-in preamplifier, which can be enabled or disabled depending on the science objectives. For applications requiring continuous monitoring of both weak and strong seismic signals, it may be necessary to use two separate ADC channels—with and without the preamplifier—sharing a single seismometer output (i.e., double digitization). This approach improves dynamic range and scientific return but increases power and data demands, presenting a trade-off in resource-limited spaceflight environments.

Frequency Response

The instrument response was estimated using the SiA sensor system model, incorporating component-level selections for the feedback system, and was validated by measurement. The ShortSCOPE maintains a flat response to acceleration from 0.0095 Hz to 150 Hz with −3 dB corner frequencies (Fig. 3).

Frequency response curve of the ShortSCOPE sensor, showing where the curve is flat to within −3 dB

The response of the ShortSCOPE is influenced by temperature-dependent changes in its magnetic, resistive, and capacitive components. These shifts are generally modest but can alter instrument sensitivity and frequency response in cryogenic environments.

The sensor’s magnetic actuation system relies on samarium–cobalt (SmCo) magnets, which exhibit a temperature coefficient of approximately −0.03% per degree Celsius (Stanford Magnets 2024). At lower temperatures, this increase in magnetic strength leads to an increase in feedback force, which in turn reduces output sensitivity. Although the general shape of the instrument’s transfer function remains unchanged under this condition, the amplitude response is diminished at colder temperatures.

Resistors used in the force-feedback loop all have temperature coefficients ≤10 ppm per degree Celsius. At −55 °C, this results in a resistance decrease of ∼0.07% (NASA Electronic Parts and Packaging Program 2025).

Temperature has a more notable impact on the capacitive components of the system. Manufacturer data (WIMA n.d.) indicate that capacitance decreases by approximately 5% at −55 °C, which shifts the system’s corner frequency upward. For the ShortSCOPE sensor, this means a shift from 0.0095 Hz at 20 °C to 0.01 Hz at −55 °C. Although this adjustment is modest, it represents a linear response to temperature and should be considered when modeling instrument performance in cryogenic or high-temperature environments.

Tilt Tolerance and Local Gravity Dependence

In designing the ShortSCOPE, we adopted tilt specifications for the Europa Lander mission concept (Krajewski 2018). These specifications required that all instruments be able to operate while tilted by ±30° relative to the local gravity vector, accounting for a combination of terrain slope and lander tilt. To provide 5° of margin, the ShortSCOPE sensor’s design targeted operation at ±35° of tilt. Other sensor architectures that prioritize displacement sensitivity often exhibit limited tilt tolerance and reduced dynamic range. For example, MEMS-based capacitive seismometers typically increase sensitivity by raising the quality factor Q, but this comes at the expense of displacement range. In these systems, increased capacitance can introduce damping that lowers Q. In contrast, the SiA sensor relies on a high-Q mechanical design paired with the optical readout to achieve greater displacement resolution than capacitive measurements. The interferometer requires only two small, closely spaced optical elements, reducing the risk of damping the proof mass and preserving a high Q—an effect that is maintained without the use of a vacuum enclosure in atmosphere. This configuration supports both displacement sensitivity and tilt tolerance within the expected range of surface conditions on Europa. The design modifications made for the ShortSCOPE preserved the sensor’s low power requirements: (1) the interferometric system maintained its original power consumption, and (2) the electromagnetic force-feedback loop required only microwatts of additional power.

In lower-gravity environments, tilt tolerance will increase further; in a microgravity environment, there is no difference between the horizontal and vertical sensor configurations, as the sensors can operate with omni-tilt capability.

Electro-optical characterization

The actuation or coil constant (i.e., proof mass displacement per unit current) and optical modulation efficiency are determined by applying a current ramp that drives the proof mass back and forth across its full range of motion while monitoring the intensity changes of the diffracted light pattern from the displacement-sensitive interferometer.

The SiA optical displacement measurement system is based on a Michelson interferometer. Relative motion between the grating mounted on the proof mass and a stationary reference reflector produces intensity variations in the diffracted light due to phase differences between the reflected beams (Fig. 5a). As the proof mass moves across its allowable displacement range (typically 200–300 μm), the interferometer produces repeated optical fringes with a period of λ/2, where λ is the wavelength of the light source (λ=850 nm) (Fig. 5b).

The optical sweep, used as a functional checkout and as part of the ShortSCOPE calibration routine, measures coil constant and modulation efficiency of the interferometer by sweeping proof mass across its displacement range. (c) Calibration logic, showing how displacement maps to λ/4 fringe resolution

Calibration begins at the fringe closest to the sensor’s rest position and proceeds with an internal gain adjustment, using the fact that a locked-in single fringe corresponds to a displacement of λ/4 (212.5 nm) (Fig. 5c). The rest position is determined by the sensor’s tilt and fundamental resonance. Once calibration is complete, the sensor operates in closed-loop mode: the coil-magnet actuator applies a counterbalancing force to maintain the proof mass at its operating point, effectively nulling ground-induced displacement. This calibration routine executes automatically upon sensor startup. In the event of clipping, the sensor automatically re-calibrates and resumes normal operation.

Functional Checkouts

A suite of functional checkouts was performed prior to environmental testing to establish baseline ShortSCOPE sensor characterization. These functional tests were also performed during and/or after each environmental test to assess performance changes. These included (i) the resonant frequency (f0) and mechanical quality factor (Q) of the spring-mass system (Fig. 4), (ii) the coil constant, and (iii) the optical modulation efficiency of the interferometer, including how it varies with sensor tilt (±30°) (Fig. 5). In some environments (e.g., the high-vibration conditions of thermal-vacuum testing), not all parameters provided meaningful measurements, but all were monitored and trended to track potential changes in performance.

Environmental Testing and Iterative Redesign

We conducted environmental testing to advance the ShortSCOPE sensor to Technology Readiness Level (TRL) 6. For many of NASA’s flight programs, achieving TRL 6 is a prerequisite for key programmatic milestones such as Preliminary Design Review (e.g., NASA 2023 and NASA 2018a). This designation indicates that a technology has reached sufficient maturity for integration into mission development, reducing technical and programmatic risk.

TRL 6 is defined by the successful operation of a high-fidelity prototype in a relevant environment, with test data that validate analytical performance predictions. Exiting a development program at TRL 6 requires demonstrated engineering feasibility supported by documented environmental test results.

To simulate the conditions anticipated for the Europa Lander mission—including launch, cruise, and surface operations—we performed dynamics, temperature survival, and thermal vacuum testing. Where applicable, test specifications were drawn from the Europa Lander Environmental Requirements (e.g., Kokorowski 2018 and Krajewski 2018, Sect. 3.9.1). Our standard testing campaign to achieve TRL 6 was performed on at least two horizontal single-axis ShortSCOPE sensors, while some exploratory survival testing was conducted on only a single sensor. Horizontal sensors were selected for qualification because their operation can be directly verified in a 1g environment, whereas vertical sensors that are tuned for Europa’s gravity experience proof-mass sag that precludes their operation on Earth. Aside from an approximately 140 μm increase in the mirror–grating gap in the vertical configuration, the two sensors are mechanically identical, making the horizontal axis a high-fidelity prototype representative of both designs.

In the unpowered state, the ShortSCOPE has a Junction Field-Effect Transistor (JFET)-based damping circuit that shorts the geophone coil, lowering the proof-mass quality factor, Q, and thereby suppressing motion during shock-loading, effectively serving as a launch lock. Despite this inherent protection, our environmental test campaign revealed failure modes that prompted iterative redesigns of the sensor. These modifications have resulted in an instrument with significantly improved robustness compared to the original COTS SiA sensor. Here, we describe the test sequences, the failures encountered, and the resulting design changes implemented to achieve TRL 6 qualification.

Dynamics Testing

Dynamics testing is key to validating instrument resilience and overall system reliability after exposure to launch environments. We conducted dynamics survival testing to qualify the ShortSCOPE sensors for most modern launch systems; our standard test campaign included shock testing at 500g followed by random vibration (“vibe”) testing at 14.1g root-mean-square (RMS).

Launch vehicles anticipated for upcoming planetary exploration missions include NASA’s Space Launch System (SLS); SpaceX’s Falcon, Falcon Heavy, and Starship rockets; and United Launch Alliance’s Vulcan Centaur. SLS was initially selected for the Europa Clipper mission and actively considered for the Europa Lander; it remains the only vehicle capable of facilitating a direct trajectory to Jupiter. Ultimately, however, Europa Clipper was assigned to Falcon Heavy, which can also reach Jupiter via gravity assists, resulting in a longer cruise duration. SpaceX’s Starship Human Landing System is under development for future Artemis lunar-landing missions. While not specifically tailored for deep space, Vulcan Centaur is designed to support a broad range of payloads and debuted with the launch of the Peregrine lunar lander in 2024.

When the flight vehicle has not yet been finalized, environmental qualification often adopts the General Environmental Verification Standard (GEVS) for expendable launch vehicles (NASA 2021) as a provisional baseline for vibration, shock and acoustic requirements. The GEVS qualification envelope encompasses the vibration environments associated with all major modern expendable launch vehicles with published launch loads. Shock testing was performed in accordance with GEVS standards.

Shock Environment and Test Conditions

Shock environments arise from events such as stage separation and other transient mechanical loads during launch. We began qualification using the shock profile outlined in the GEVS standard (NASA 2021) for “other hardware” (Fig. 6a). A 500g, 8-millisecond half-sine pulse was applied using a pendulum shock apparatus, with two shocks in both the positive and negative directions of each sensor axis. The selected pulse width corresponds to a frequency of approximately 62.5 Hz, consistent with the expected resonant frequency of the ShortSCOPE sensor as installed (<100 Hz). At this duration, the 500g level provides a conservative representation of the shock expected in typical launch scenarios.

We adopted this 500g level as part of our qualification test to demonstrate survivability under launch and deployment loads.

Sine vibe specifications for SLS and Vulcan Centaur were also reviewed. The SLS environment (NASA 2018b) includes a sweep from 5 to 60 Hz, with sine amplitudes ranging from 0.16g to 0.31g, depending on axis and frequency. For Vulcan, the sine amplitudes range from 0.5g to 0.6g across 5 to 100 Hz (United Launch Alliance 2023). As both profiles are bounded by the GEVS random vibe environment, sensors were not separately subjected to sine testing.

Outcomes of Dynamics Testing

Three dynamics tests were conducted over the course of the qualification campaign, which included a sequence of 500g shocks in the positive and negative direction of each axis, followed by GEVS-level random vibration. The sensors demonstrated survivability in all shock tests. Failures did occur during the first and second vibe tests, prompting mechanical redesigns that ultimately led to a successful qualification relative to the GEVS standard.

In the first test, both sensors survived 500g shock loads, but failed during random vibration, ceasing operation entirely, as noted during post-vibe functional checkouts. Inspection of the test units following random vibration revealed a fracture in the leaf spring arm at its connection to the inner mounting disc, attributed to mechanical fatigue. This failure mode was reproduced in subsequent trials when sensors were oriented radially but was not observed when sensors were mounted axially.

Finite element analysis (FEA) confirmed that the high-aspect-ratio leaf spring design generated localized stress concentrations under lateral excitation, contributing to the observed spring failures. Notably, COTS SiA sensors, which use a different spring geometry, did not exhibit the same stress distribution in FEA. To mitigate this failure mode, the spring anchors were redesigned to redirect stress away from critical junctions. The spring thickness (from 0.003 to 0.004 in.) and aspect ratio and BeCu temper (TD04 to TM08) were also changed to improve mechanical robustness. FEA simulations of the revised geometry showed stress levels comparable to those in the original COTS design. New prototypes incorporating these changes were fabricated and prepared for re-testing.

During the second dynamics test, two additional failure modes were observed only after vibe: one sensor developed a hairline crack in the spring, and another exhibited a top spring fracture. These results indicated that the modified spring design alone was insufficient to prevent fatigue-induced cracking. To address this, twelve set screws (serving as side shock stops) were installed radially—six each on the upper and lower plates at 60° intervals—to constrain in-plane displacement and maintain stresses in the BeCu springs below the material fatigue limit. However, when the gap between the proof mass and the shock stops was not precisely set or maintained under repeated contact, the same failure mode recurred.

To address these issues, five mitigations were implemented: (1) the shock-stop gap was further reduced; (2) gap adjustment precision was improved during assembly; (3) the shock stops were repositioned to interface with more structurally robust regions of the bobbin; (4) the bobbin surface was flattened and polished to minimize stress concentrations at the point of contact; and (5) a Mandatory Inspection Point was introduced during the shock-stop adjustment step of the sensor assembly process.

Following the implementation of these mitigations, two ShortSCOPE sensors (SP-T6A-S06 and S08) successfully passed sequential 500g shock and 14.1g RMS vibration tests.

Thermal Vacuum Cycling

Thermal-Vacuum Environment and Test Conditions

Europa’s long day (85.2 Earth hours) presents challenges for landed spacecraft operations. Near the equator, diurnal surface temperatures vary by approximately 50 °C, with mean temperatures around −167 °C (Spencer et al. 1999). Exact thermal conditions will depend on landing site latitude. Recorded surface temperatures range from −203 °C during polar night to −141 °C at equatorial noon (Spencer et al. 1999), although areas of recent resurfacing or cryovolcanic activity may exhibit localized warming, potentially reaching 0 °C (Abramov and Spencer 2008). These measurements were derived from Galileo mission data and their spatial resolution (80–200 km) is far coarser than the footprint of a landed spacecraft (∼3 m). It is therefore likely that more extreme temperature variations exist within localized terrains, such as permanently shadowed craters. Europa’s atmosphere is extremely tenuous, with pressures near 7.5×10−10 torr (McGrath et al. 2009). Consequently, the primary mechanisms of heat loss from the lander would be conduction at surface contact points and thermal radiation to its surroundings.

To help mitigate Europa’s challenging thermal and particle radiation environment, the Europa Lander concept assumed that most instruments would either be mounted directly to the internal vault walls or thermally and mechanically isolated from them. The allowable flight temperature limits within the vault were specified as −40 °C to +50 °C (Krajewski 2018). For qualification, additional margin requirements extended the testing range to −55 °C to +70 °C (Kokorowski 2018). Payload components were also required to operate under space vacuum conditions of 1×10−14 torr, verifiable through thermal vacuum (TVAC) cycling conducted at ≤10−5 torr (Kokorowski 2018). Thermal ramp rates were limited to |dT/dt| ≤ 5 °C per minute. Additional tolerances and stabilization criteria are provided in Table 1.

Qualification-level thermal vacuum cycling profile as executed, showing the sensor temperature and chamber pressure throughout the test

Testing spanned three full thermal cycles to evaluate sensor performance under repeated thermal stress. The allowable flight temperature range was set to −40 °C to +50 °C, with qualification margins of −15 °C and +20 °C, resulting in a total test range of −55 °C to +70 °C. Each sensor remained operational for 24 hours at the cold limit (−55 °C) and 72 hours at the hot limit (+70 °C). During the first cycle, additional survival testing was conducted with the sensors in a non-operational state for 6 hours at low (−67 °C) and high (+77 °C) temperature extremes.

Pump-down was initiated to a target vacuum level of 10−5 Torr, though this pressure was consistently achieved only during cold-soak periods due to sensor outgassing. The sensors remained within the required thermal limits and functioned throughout testing at an average chamber pressure of 4.7×10−5 Torr (Fig. 8). The optical-stage PCB provided an interface to external electrical ground support equipment (EGSE) via a wire harness, enabling continuous operation and characterization during the test.

The sensors remained operational across the full range of allowable flight temperatures plus qualification margin (−55 °C to +70 °C), satisfying the TRL 6 thermal requirements defined in Kokorowski (2018). Instrument degradation was observed when chamber temperatures exceeded the qualification limits, both above and below the specified range. Performance degradation observed during TVAC testing at temperatures below −50 °C is linked to diminished laser intensity near the operational limits of a 795-nm VCSEL which was used in some TRL 6 prototypes. During testing, this effect was partially mitigated by increasing the drive current to the laser, restoring adequate interferometric signal strength. Upgrading to an 850-nm VCSEL substantially improved low-temperature resilience (Sect. 3.3). Low-temperature performance was further enhanced by implementing an automated drive-current-boosting scheme that compensates for reductions in lasing intensity under cold conditions.

A separate degradation mechanism was also observed at high temperatures beyond the qualification test range (> +70 °C), where thermal expansion of the proof mass (coil-wound bobbin) caused intermittent contact with the side-mounted shock stops. This interference resolves at lower temperature conditions, as the bobbin contracts more than the surrounding housing. Notably, there is a design trade when setting the gap tolerance for the ShortSCOPE’s shock stops: narrower gaps improve robustness to high dynamic loads but increase the risk of mechanical interference due to thermal expansion mismatches at elevated temperatures.

Temperature Survival Testing

Low-Temperature Survivability and Operation

A pilot low-temperature functional and survival test of the ShortSCOPE sensor that incorporated an 850-nm VCSEL was conducted to evaluate its performance under the cold conditions relevant to outer Solar System and lunar south pole environments. Temperature sensors were mounted on both the mechanical and optical stages to monitor their respective thermal responses.

Full functionality was demonstrated in low-temperature vacuum testing from −70 °C to +25 °C, with consistent mechanical and optical performance across the range. However, due to the absence of a thermal break on the copper wire harness connecting the sensor to EGSE, the optical-stage PCB remained warmer than the mechanical stage. As a result, the VCSEL did not reach temperatures low enough to establish a definitive lower operational limit. Minimum recorded temperatures were −70 °C for the optical stage and −90 °C for the mechanical stage.

In a subsequent survival test, the wire harness was removed to eliminate conductive heat flow and allow the optical stage to cool passively. The unpowered sensor was exposed to −130 °C, after which both stages were brought back to ambient conditions and confirmed to be undamaged and fully functional.

The Broadband Seismic Characterization using Optics for Planetary Exploration (“BroadSCOPE”) sensor with an identical optical-stage design (including the same VCSEL and photodiode array) was tested alongside the ShortSCOPE unit. By thermally coupling the harness to the cold plate, the BroadSCOPE sensor’s optical-stage PCB reached −150 °C during unpowered survival testing and was subsequently demonstrated to operate at −130 °C. These results indicate that the ShortSCOPE optical stage exhibits similar cryogenic survivability and operational performance to at least −130 °C (Marusiak et al. 2026). This low temperature operation for the ShortSCOPE sensor has since been demonstrated down to −170 °C as part of the Artemis LEMS instrument suite qualification program to mature the sensor to TRL 8 for lunar polar conditions.

High-Temperature Survivability

Dry heat microbial reduction (DHMR) is a sterilization method required for hardware destined for planetary bodies with potential habitability, such as Europa. It is intended to reduce bioburden to acceptable levels by exposing flight components to elevated temperatures for extended durations. DHMR survival testing was performed on the sensor in accordance with the Europa Lander Preliminary Environmental Requirements Document (Kokorowski 2018).

Two ShortSCOPE sensors (SP-T6A-S06 and SP-T6A-S08) were subjected to three 43-hour cycles of oven heating at 130 °C while powered off. Sensor functional measurements taken before and after DHMR exposure indicated no loss of functionality. Two DHMR test campaigns were conducted in total. The first resulted in mechanical failure due to degradation of the thread-locking compound, which was mitigated in subsequent testing by switching to 3M 2216 epoxy. The second DHMR campaign was completed successfully (Table 3).

Analog-Site Field Testing

Field testing of flight-candidate ShortSCOPE sensors was conducted in a planetary analog environment near Flagstaff, Arizona (DellaGiustina 2021a). The test site, located within the San Francisco Volcanic Field, is a well-established lunar analog due to the presence of lava tubes and other volcanic features (Bleacher et al. 2015; Bell et al. 2019).

We tested a flight-candidate instrument that incorporated three orthogonally oriented ShortSCOPE sensors (one vertical, two horizontals), each with a spring-mass suspension set to operate at 1g (Sect. 2.6), and a gain set to interface with modern field digitizers (132 V/g or 13.5 V/m/s2). It was buried at 1 m depth and connected to an external power supply and digitization system, functioning as a seismic station. A 3-axis 60 V/g SiA COTS instrument (approximately 6 V/m/s2) was also deployed ∼30 cm away from the ShortSCOPE instrument for benchmarking.

The two seismic stations were registered with the Federation of Digital Seismograph Networks (FDSN) as network ZD (2021-2022). The station codes are “COTS” for the SiA COTS instrument and “FLGT” for the flight-ready ShortSCOPE instrument. The ShortSCOPE instrument operated in the field from June 2021 to March 2022, achieving over 6,000 hours of failure-free performance. It was still successfully collecting data when the network was demobilized in 2022.

Comparison of environmental background noise of the vertical component (Fig. 10) demonstrates that the ShortSCOPE has a lower instrument noise floor than the COTS sensor, particularly at low frequencies (0.01–0.1 Hz). In addition to the differences in instrument noise, there are also improvements to the time series. During the field deployment, there was a large MW 8.2 event in Alaska on July 29, 2021. In Fig. 11, we show the response-removed vertical-component seismograms for both the ShortSCOPE and SiA COTS instruments, bandpass filtered from 0.01–0.1 Hz. This 0.01–0.1 Hz bandpass was selected because 10–100 s filtering is commonly used for regional and teleseismic events to preserve body- and surface-wave energy while suppressing higher-frequency local noise and longer-period drift. The ShortSCOPE sensor has higher amplitudes than the COTS sensor, allowing for higher signal-to-noise ratios, thus better detection of global events. We calculate the RMS values of the seismograms shown in Fig. 11 and find that the ShortSCOPE sensor has an RMS 1.31 times that of the COTS sensor. This improvement in signal recovery will translate to reduced uncertainties in arrival times and peak amplitudes. Signals that would fall below the noise floor of the COTS sensor become detectable with the ShortSCOPE, lowering the magnitude of completeness of seismic catalogs. Many events would also exhibit improved signal-to-noise ratios, increasing overall science return. For a single-station planetary seismometer, reduced uncertainty in waveform characteristics directly propagates to improved constraints on event location and source properties (e.g., focal mechanisms), which in turn reduce uncertainties in the inferred seismic structure.

Our field validation demonstrates a successful progression from COTS to flight-candidate performance in a planetary (rather than laboratory) environment over a long duration, highlighting the ShortSCOPE sensor’s operational robustness and improved detection of low-frequency signals. These results support the sensor’s readiness for deployment on future planetary missions.

Mission Readiness and Deployment

Multiple studies indicate that instruments incorporating the ShortSCOPE are deployable across a versatile range of planetary mission scenarios, based on both direct testing with the SiA COTS sensor and analogous demonstrations from prior spacecraft missions. Likewise, the sensor’s high tilt tolerance in low gravity (±30° on Europa or the Moon, and omni-tilt in microgravity) removes the need for leveling mechanisms, offering deployment flexibility while reducing mass and operational complexity.

Laboratory, field, and in situ testing from the InSight mission showed that deck-mounted seismometers can record high-quality data, comparable to ground-deployed sensors outside narrow bands dominated by lander resonances (Panning and Kedar 2019; Panning et al. 2020; Marusiak et al. 2021). These prior studies include field testing of the SiA COTS sensors—identical in size and form factor to the ShortSCOPE—on mock landers at icy ocean world analog sites (Marusiak et al. 2020, 2021; DellaGiustina 2021b) where lander-mounted sensors were benchmarked against buried ones. Although deck resonances must be characterized for each lander design, deck-mounted seismometers can reproduce ground motion with high fidelity over much of the seismic bandwidth of interest so long as deck resonances are characterized in a representative configuration and environment.

Surface deployment of short-period seismic instruments, with direct coupling to the ground, eliminates the amplification effects of lander resonances and has been successfully implemented in previous planetary analog and spacecraft seismology experiments (Marusiak et al. 2022; Yana et al. 2023). Nevertheless, in the presence of an atmosphere, seismic measurements remain susceptible to pressure and wind-related noise.

For applications requiring further noise reduction and thermal stability, burial of the instrument remains the gold standard to improve coupling and reduce susceptibility to diurnal thermal and atmospheric effects (where applicable). On planetary surfaces with low-density, highly scattering surface layers—such as the Moon—burial can also minimize the contribution of scattering noise from these layers. For Artemis, an instrument incorporating the ShortSCOPE sensor will be buried directly by astronauts as part of the LEMS deployment activities, ensuring proper installation and improving temperature stability by placing the sensors below the thermal skin depth of the lunar surface.

Recent validation work has extended these capabilities to include compatibility with autonomous emplacement systems. Testing with the Pneumatic-drill Emplacement of Regolith Sensors for Extraterrestrial Underground Seismology (PERSEUS) device demonstrated that a ShortSCOPE instrument can be readily deployed into a shallow borehole, providing the benefits of burial on a robotic mission (Sanigepalli et al. 2024). Likewise, work is underway to develop ballistic deployment of SiA seismometers onto small bodies via penetrometers (Bray et al. 2025; Saedi et al. 2025).

Taken together, these results indicate that ShortSCOPE-based instruments can be deployed in multiple mission scenarios—including in-lander, on-surface, or through robotic, human, or ballistic emplacement—without fundamental design changes to their seismic sensor technology, enabling a mission to tailor the deployment system to the specific constraints and science objectives of a variety of target bodies.

Summary and Conclusion

Seismology has proven to be one of the most effective tools for probing planetary interiors, offering insights into the internal structure and processes of Solar System bodies. Realizing this capability on future planetary spacecraft demands seismic sensors that integrate low power and mass requirements, high dynamic range, minimal self-noise across a broad bandwidth, and proven resilience to extreme environmental conditions. To meet this need, we adapted a COTS sensor, manufactured by Silicon Audio, Inc., for spaceflight applications—specifically, deployment on Jupiter’s moon Europa, an icy ocean world. We modified the original COTS design to improve low-frequency characteristics by adjusting the resonant frequency and reducing the self-noise floor, particularly below 10 Hz—frequencies critical for characterizing thick ice shells and subsurface oceans, as on Europa. At the same time, we largely preserved much of the sensor’s broad bandwidth to detect high-frequency signals and ensured that it could tolerate a tilt of at least ±30° relative to local gravity, accommodating the rugged topography expected on some surfaces. Environmental qualification included dynamics (shock and vibration), thermal vacuum cycling, and temperature survival tests, each designed to simulate the conditions expected during development, launch, cruise, and surface operations. The sensors achieved TRL 6 through these tests, with early failures informing iterative mechanical redesigns that improved robustness and eliminated critical failure modes. Complementary field deployments further demonstrated the system’s capability to detect seismic signals under a range of planetary conditions, with a noise floor sufficiently low to capture anticipated events on Europa and the Moon.

Although originally developed for Europa and other icy ocean worlds—where seismometers could detect ice shell thickness, ocean depth, and tidally driven seismicity (Schmidt et al. 2011; Panning et al. 2018; Marusiak et al. 2023; Hurford et al. 2020)—the sensor’s performance characteristics make it exceptionally well-suited for lunar deployment. Its first spaceflight application is likely to be as part of a future Artemis lunar surface mission, where ShortSCOPE sensors will comprise each of the three components of two triaxial seismometers as part of LEMS. These observations will provide new constraints on the Moon’s seismicity, particularly at frequencies above 20 Hz (Fig. 12), a spectral region not well covered by the Apollo instruments (Watters et al. 2019; Panning et al. 2021; Nunn et al. 2020). Beyond the Moon and icy ocean worlds, the sensor architecture provides a flight-ready pathway for seismology investigations across a range of planetary bodies. This includes asteroids, which may exhibit tidal seismic signatures (DellaGiustina et al. 2024; Ballouz et al. 2024), and Mars, where a future geophysical network could enable global-scale studies of interior structure and evolution (Dehant et al. 2012). The same architecture can be extended to nearly any solid-surface body, expanding opportunities to probe geophysical properties and assess habitability across the Solar System.
