Development and application of SNP markers to discriminate Korean Perilla (Perilla frutescens) varieties using genomic sequence variations
Authors: Jung-In Kim, Myoung Hee Lee, Sang Woo Kim, Eunsoo Lee, Sungup Kim, Jeongeun Lee, Heungsu Lee, Ki Young Kim, Eunyoung Oh, Min Young Kim, Kwang-Soo Cho, Tae-Hwan Jun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPerilla [Perilla frutescens (L.) Britton] is an annual herbaceous species of the Lamiaceae family native to Northeast Asia, cultivated for both seed oil production and as a leafy vegetable. We developed and validated Kompetitive Allele-Specific PCR (KASP) markers for accurate identification of Korean Perilla cultivars. Whole-genome resequencing of 16 representative cultivars yielded 9686,199 SNPs, with 6183 high-confidence SNPs identified after stringent filtering. From these, 237 KASP markers were designed, and 150 polymorphic markers were validated across 48 cultivars. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) and phylogenetic analyses mostly distinguished seed-type from leaf-type Perilla. Minimal KASP marker sets (five for seed Perilla, six for leaf Perilla) were established, sufficient to distinguish widely cultivated Korean cultivars. These markers, encoded by a binary barcode system, enabled rapid and precise cultivar identification. Application tests demonstrated their utility for evaluating seed purity by quantifying contamination. This work provides substantial genomic resources for cultivar authentication, genetic purity assessment, and molecular breeding. The new KASP system offers a cost-effective, high-throughput, and reliable approach for managing and enhancing Perilla genetic resources, ultimately advancing breeding programs and improving seed industry processes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-33012-8.Keywords: Cultivar identification, KASP markers, Perilla frutescens, Genetic diversity, Seed purity testingSubject terms: Genetics, Plant sciences

Introduction

Perilla [Perilla frutescens (L.) Britton], an annual herbaceous plant in the Lamiaceae family, is native to East Asia and widely cultivated in Korea, Japan, and northeastern China as an oil crop and leafy vegetable1. Genomically, cultivated Perilla is an allotetraploid species (P. frutescens, 2n = 4x = 40), while several diploid wild species (2n = 20) such as P. citriodora, P. hirtella, and P. setoyensis have been reported2. Recent analyses confirm that tetraploid Perilla (P. frutescens) originated from a diploid AA diploid (P. citriodora) progenitor3. Significant progress in Perilla genomics occurred in 2017 with the release of the reference genome of wild Perilla ‘Jeju 17’ (P. citriodora) from Korea, which comprises ten chromosomes and spans 676 Mb4 Subsequently, cultivated Perilla genomes such as ‘PF40’ (P. frutescens) from China5 and ‘Hoko-3 ‘ (P. frutescens) from Japan3 were published. These cultivated forms are characterized by 20 chromosomes and a larger genome size of approximately 1.24 Gb. However, a reference genome for tetraploid Perilla from Korea remains to be established. Further, genomic research on Perilla is still relatively nascent compared to other major global crops due to this delayed genomic characterization.

In Korea, Perilla is extensively used for both seeds and leaves in traditional and contemporary culinary and industrial applications. This dual utility has led to the development of distinct seed-type and leaf-type cultivars, each with pronounced differences in morphology, cultivation methods, and industrial uses. Seed Perilla cultivars, such as ‘Dayu’6 and ‘Deulsam’7, are cultivated for oil and powder production, characterized by soft seed coats, tall stature, wide internode spacing, and flowering from late August to early September. In contrast, leaf Perilla cultivars, such as ‘Donggeul1’8 and ‘Soim’1, are primarily valued for their large, round, heart-shaped leaves for vegetable consumption. These cultivars produce hard seeds, are shorter with narrow internode spacing, and can be grown year-round in greenhouses, flowering from late September to early October.

Recognizing these distinctive utilization patterns, the National Institute of Crop Science (NICS) in Korea has actively bred specialized Perilla cultivars, developing 30 for high seed yield and oil content and 15 for abundant, high-quality leaf production. Despite these breeding achievements, reliable discrimination between cultivars based solely on morphological characteristics remains challenging. This underscores the critical need for molecular marker development through genomic research to establish robust, cultivar-specific identification systems.

The increasing availability of genome information and the acceleration of large-scale sequencing via next-generation sequencing (NGS) are driving forces in modern molecular breeding and the seed industry. Molecular breeding enables the discrimination of desired traits based on genomic information, bypassing the need for phenotypic observation. This makes the development of applicable molecular marker sets crucial for breeding materials and trait-related markers. Single Nucleotide Polymorphisms (SNPs), the most frequent and stable type of genetic variation across the entire genome, are particularly advantageous for this purpose. Consequently, NGS-based genome-wide SNP discovery has rapidly identified numerous molecular markers in various crops. While research on molecular breeding in Perilla is still in its early stages due to the delayed development of an allotetraploid reference genome, studies using Simple Sequence Repeat (SSR) markers have been conducted9–12. However, these efforts are insufficient often focusing on distinguishing cultivated from weedy types or Perilla frutescens var. crispa rather than advanced cultivars. Accordingly, genetic studies of perilla employing SNP markers, which offer high throughput and genome-wide coverage, are essential for advancing molecular research and breeding efforts.

Kompetitive Allele-Specific PCR (KASP) offers a highly cost-effective and high-throughput alternative for detecting SNPs and InDels at specific genomic sites13. Similar to TaqMan probe detection, KASP genotyping relies on terminal fluorescence readings. The KASP system facilitates rapid, high-throughput analysis, as exemplified by its application at KATPA (Korea Agriculture Technology Promotion Agency, Iksan, Korea) using the Soellex™ system (Douglas Scientific LLC, USA). KASP markers have been successfully implemented for genotyping in various crops, including pigeon peas14, chickpeas15, and indica rice16,17. Notably, despite its advantages, no previous studies have utilized KASP markers in Perilla.

This study aimed to address the critical need for a reliable system for distinguishing and classifying Korean Perilla cultivars by developing and validating SNP-based KASP markers using whole-genome sequence information. Our specific objectives were to (1) identify high-confidence SNPs through whole-genome resequencing of representative Perilla cultivars, (2) develop and validate KASP markers for cultivar discrimination, (3) establish minimal marker sets for efficient identification of commercial cultivars, and (4) demonstrate practical applications of these markers in seed purity assessment. This research represents the first comprehensive development and application of KASP markers in Perilla, providing invaluable genomic resources essential for cultivar management and advancing future molecular breeding programs (Table 1).

Results

Analysis of Perilla genome sequence and SNP discovery

Whole-genome sequencing of 16 Korean Perilla cultivars generated approximately 325 Gbp of raw data, which was refined to 225 Gbp after trimming. This yielded an average sequencing depth of 21X for each cultivar relative to the Perilla reference genome size. Reads were mapped to the diploid wild Perilla ‘Jeju 17’ (P. citriodora) reference genome, resulting in an average coverage of 19.6 X (ranging from 11.77 to 28.87 X per cultivar) and covering about 88% (average 560 Mbp) of the reference genome. ‘Bora’ exhibited the highest coverage at 92.43%, while ‘Deulhyang’ had the lowest coverage at 83.21% (Supplementary Table S1).

An integrated SNP matrix comprising 9,686,199 SNPs was generated from the resequenced Perilla cultivars. ‘Bora’ had the highest number of detected SNPs (4,181,878), and ‘Sangyeop’ had the lowest (2,824,720) (Supplementary Table S2). Rigorous filtering steps were applied to these raw SNPs. Initially, the number of polymorphic SNPs with a minimum read depth of three were reduced to 320,433. After additional filtering, a total of 187,788 SNPs were retained when genotypes with no missing data were considered, and 35,173 SNPs were selected when only those uniquely matched to a single genome region were included. Finally, filtering based on Polymorphic Information Content (PIC) values of 0.3 or higher yielded a high-confidence set of 6,183 SNPs (Table 2).

Development and validation of KASP markers

Using the selected 6,183 SNPs, 237 KASP markers were designed to be distributed across all ten Perilla chromosomes based on their mapping to the wild Perilla (P. citriodora) reference genome. From these, 150 polymorphic KASP markers were selected based on their consistent PCR amplification and ability to identify genetic diversity among the 48 Perilla varieties (Supplementary Table S3). Marker distribution varied by chromosome, with chromosome 10 having the most (30 markers) and chromosome 6 having the fewest (six markers).

Genetic differentiation of Perilla cultivars

Principal coordinates analysis

Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) was performed on the 16 Perilla cultivars using the 6,183 high-confidence SNPs. The analysis clearly differentiated the leaf Perilla cultivars (31; Namcheon, 32; Soim, 33; Sangyeup, 35; Pungnyeon, 36; Donggeul1, 37; Donggeul2, 38; Ipdeulkkae1, 41; Bora, 46; Milyang14) and seed Perilla cultivars (1: Dayu, 2: Deulsaem, 3: Sodam, 4: Deulhyang, 5: Deulchan, 17: Daesil) into two distinct clusters based on two principal components (Fig. 1). However, the leaf cultivar ‘Manchu’ (34) clustered more closely with the seed Perilla cultivars in the analysis.

Phylogenetic tree analysis

A KASP genotyping assay was conducted on 48 Perilla varieties using the final set of 150 developed KASP markers. The results from one representative marker (marker 47) are depicted in Fig. 2, where the genotype alleles are divided into red and blue circles, indicating homozygous AA and GG, respectively. Phylogenetic tree analysis using all 150 KASP markers effectively differentiated seed Perilla from leaf Perilla (Fig. 3). Most of the perilla cultivars developed by the Rural Development Administration were classified based on whether they were seed-type or leaf-type. However, among the two shiso accessions, which are well known as traditional medicinal plants, sample No. 47 ‘YCPL177-1’ (P. frutescens Brit. var. acuta Kudo) clustered closely with the seed-type perilla group, whereas sample No. 48 ‘YCPL205-1’ (P. frutescens Brit. var. crispa Hand.-Mazz) was grouped with the leaf-type perilla, indicating clear genetic differentiation.

Selection of minimal KASP marker sets for cultivar distinction

While seed perilla and leaf perilla can be distinguished by their traits, distinguishing cultivars within seed perilla is not straightforward, and similarly, distinguishing cultivars within leaf perilla is also challenging. Therefore, a minimal marker set was developed separately for each.

From the 150 developed KASP markers, a minimal set of five KASP markers (KASP_06, 08, 14, 27, 30) was identified to distinguish nine widely cultivated Korean seed Perilla cultivars: ‘Dayu’, ‘Deulseam’, ‘Sodam’, ‘Deulhyang’, ‘Deulchan’, ‘Daesil’, ‘Anyu’, ‘Baekjin’, and ‘Dami’ (Table 3). For instance, KASP_06 distinguished ‘Dayu’, ‘Deulseam’, ‘Sodam’, ‘Daesil’, ‘Baekjin’ and ‘Dami’ as CC genotypes, and ‘Deulhyang’, ‘Deulchan’ and ‘Anyu’ as TT. The genotypes of other cultivars across different markers are presented in Table 4. A binary barcode system was established by converting marker genotypes. If a cultivar’s genotype matched ‘Dayu’ for a given marker, it was assigned ‘0’; otherwise, it was ‘1’. This resulted in unique binary codes for each seed Perilla cultivar: ‘Dayu’ (00000), ‘Deulseam’ (00001), ‘Sodam’ (00110), ‘Deulhyang’ (11000), ‘Deulchan’ (10111), ‘Daesil’ (01001), ‘Anyu’ (10110), ‘Baekjin’ (01111), and ‘Dami’ (00010) (Table 4). This method effectively differentiated the nine Perilla seed varieties.

Application of developed KASP markers

The utility of the developed KASP markers was further demonstrated through their application in assessing seed purity. Specifically, marker KASP_30, one of the five markers distinguishing seed Perilla cultivars, was used to differentiate between ‘Dayu’ and ‘Deulsaem’, two major seed Perilla cultivars in South Korea. Allelic discrimination plots of mixed seed samples from ‘Dayu’ and ‘Deulsaem’ showed distinct clusters: cluster E (pure ‘Deulsaem’, near X-axis), cluster A (pure ‘Dayu’, near Y-axis), and clusters B, C, and D representing various mixing ratios. The positions of these mixed clusters clearly reflected the respective proportions of each allele, confirming the marker’s efficacy in evaluating seed purity (Fig. 4).

Triplicate samples were used (DNA concentration: 10 ng/ul) (A); 100% Dayu (B); 70%:30% with Dayu: Deulsaem (C); 50%:50% with Dayu: Deulsaem (D); 30%:70% with Dayu: Deulsaem, E; 100% Deulsaem.

Additionally, for high-throughput cultivar testing, 95 plant samples per cultivar were analyzed using three representative KASP markers (KASP_06, 14, and 30) from the seed Perilla set. The three markers were able to differentiate the seed-type perilla varieties based on their genotypes, highlighting their practical application in breeding programs (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Discussion

Genotypic data from next-generation resequencing reads proved invaluable for understanding the evolutionary history and developing molecular resources for Perilla in this study. While using a diploid wild Perilla as the reference genome for SNP generation was sufficient for developing cultivar-differentiating molecular markers, it likely posed some limitations in fully analyzing the genetic diversity and classification of allotetraploid Perilla cultivars. This highlights the ongoing need for further research to comprehensively understand the genetic variation within tetraploid populations. Expanding available genomic resources will refine classification tools and enhance breeding strategies to improve the traits of Perilla cultivars. Fortunately, Perilla genomic research is advancing rapidly, with tetraploid reference genomes recently developed in China ‘PF40’5 and Japan ‘Hoko-3’3. These advancements are expected to mitigate the constraints of diploid-based research, accelerate gene discovery, and expedite Genome-Wide Association Studies (GWAS) and Quantitative Trait Loci (QTL) analyses. Furthermore, the availability of these tetraploid reference genomes will deepen our understanding of Perilla’s complex genetic characteristics and enable more comprehensive genomic studies in the future.

The NICS focuses on Perilla breeding efforts primarily in two directions: developing seed Perilla varieties for high crude fat content and seed production and leaf Perilla varieties for high leaf yield. While seed and leaf Perilla are easily distinguishable due to clear phenotypic differences, differentiating between cultivars within each type proves challenging. For instance, among leaf Perilla cultivars, ‘Soim’, ‘Bora’, and ‘Milyang 14’ are closely related in the PCoA due to ‘Ipdeulkkae1’ being a common parent. Given their lack of distinct phenotypic differences, developing robust molecular markers for cultivar identification is crucial.

In our study, SNP-based PCoA and phylogenetic analyses mostly differentiated seed-type and leaf-type Perilla cultivars. However, the precise genomic regions or functional variants underlying this divergence could not be fully resolved. This limitation is largely attributable to the absence of a high-quality tetraploid reference genome that adequately represents Korean Perilla cultivars, which restricts the ability to accurately identify trait-associated loci. The observed separation between seed and leaf types likely reflects long-term divergent selection driven by their distinct breeding objectives. Therefore, elucidating the exact genetic basis for this differentiation will require future research supported by improved tetraploid reference genome and more comprehensive gene annotations.

Interestingly, our marker analysis revealed that the cultivar Manchu, which has long been distributed by a private seed company as a leaf perilla variety, showed closer genetic similarity to seed perilla accessions. Unfortunately, the parental line of this cultivar is not documented, preventing us from tracing its exact breeding history. However, two plausible scenarios may be considered: first, Manchu might have originated from a mutation selected during a seed perilla breeding program that displayed desirable leaf-type characteristics; second, it may have been developed by selecting leaf-type individuals from a segregating population derived from a cross between leaf and seed perilla. Regardless of the exact process, the case of Manchu highlights the limitations of relying solely on phenotypic traits for varietal classification. Our study demonstrates that the use of molecular markers provides a more comprehensive and reliable tool for varietal evaluation, enabling us to overcome such limitations.

‘YCPL177-1’ (P. frutescens Brit. var. acuta Kudo) and ‘YCPL205-1’ (P. frutescens Brit. var. crispa Hand.-Mazz) are shiso resources related to perilla varieties. Traditionally, perilla has been cultivated as an oilseed crop, whereas shiso is well known as a medicinal plant18. Although perilla and shiso are morphologically distinguishable by traits such as plant size, aroma, leaf and stem color, flower color, seed size, and hardness19, they share the same chromosome number (2n = 40) and are intercrossable through artificial hybridisation20. In our phylogenetic analysis, ‘YCPL177-1’ was clustered with seed-type perilla varieties, whereas ‘YCPL205-1’ was grouped with leaf-type perilla varieties, thereby revealing clear genetic differentiation. These findings suggest that future genetic research on both shiso and perilla, as well as within the shiso species itself, would be of considerable value. The molecular markers developed in this study are expected to serve as useful tools for advancing such research.

KASP markers offer significant advantages for genotyping, including cost-effectiveness, high accuracy flexibility for diverse project scales, rapid turnaround times, and ease of use with standard qPCR instruments21,22. KASP also enables efficient high-throughput genotyping with bi-allelic scoring of SNPs and InDels and boasts a low error rate (0.7–1.6%). KASP markers are widely applied for variety identification, marker-assisted selection, and gene identification in various crops, including rice and cabbage23–25. Crucially, to our knowledge, this study represents the first successful development and application of KASP markers in Perilla, making it a highly significant contribution to Perilla research.

This study established a comprehensive KASP marker system for Perilla. The newly developed 150 KASP markers effectively classified Perilla types and, being rapid and cost-effective, overcame the limitations of conventional purity tests, facilitating reliable varietal purity analysis. Furthermore, these KASP markers enabled the accurate determination of seed contamination rates between cultivars, a critical application demonstrated in other crops, such as sunflower hybrid production26.

Our Perilla barcode cultivar identification system, which encodes KASP marker analysis results in a binary format, significantly facilitates rapid and accurate cultivar identification. Similar barcode systems have proven effective in other crops; for instance, 202 InDels were converted into a 2D barcode format to differentiate 147 soybean cultivars using 27 markers27, and SNP markers achieved 100% accuracy in differentiating 429 wheat cultivars28. Our Perilla barcode system is particularly important for preventing economic losses caused by inadvertently planting seed Perilla and leaf Perilla interchangeably. It underscores the importance of developing discrimination markers for all Perilla cultivars to ensure varietal purity throughout production and distribution. Therefore, the marker-based barcode systems developed in this study can be effectively utilized for diverse applications, including cultivar authentication, seed purity testing, and genetic diversity studies.

Conclusion

This study successfully developed and validated 150 KASP markers for Korean Perilla frutescens cultivars., These markers enable substantially reliable differentiation between seed- and leaf-type varieties, a crucial step for effective breeding and agricultural practices, and facilitate seed purity assessment. We established minimal, cost-effective marker sets that allow for rapid cultivar identification, supporting large-scale breeding programs and quality control efforts within the Perilla industry. The development of a barcode-based identification system further enhances the efficiency of genetic resource management and ensures seed quality assurance. This research not only provides invaluable tools for Perilla but also offers a versatile methodology—integrating genome resequencing, SNP discovery, and KASP marker development—that can be applied to other minor or underutilized crops, thereby bolstering molecular breeding strategies across diverse species.

The implications of this study are far-reaching, promising enhanced reliability in seed distribution, support for the sustainable conservation of Perilla genetic diversity, and increased competitiveness for the Perilla industry and other similar minor crops. While our approach yielded significant results, future research should leverage newly available tetraploid Perilla reference genomes for even more comprehensive genetic analyses and expand the marker sets to encompass a wider global germplasm. This will further solidify the foundation for advanced breeding strategies and the development of superior Perilla varieties.

Methods

Plant materials and DNA extraction

For whole-genome resequencing, 16 Perilla cultivars cultivated in South Korea were selected, encompassing both leaf and seed types. These included: ‘Dayu’, ‘Deulsaem’, ‘Sodam’, ‘Deulhyang’, ‘Deulchan’, ‘Daesil’, ‘Namcheon’, ‘Soim’, ‘Sangyeup’, ‘Manchu’, ‘Pungnyeon’, ‘Donggeul1’, ‘Donggeul2’, ‘Ipdeulkkae1’, ‘Bora’, and ‘Milyang14’ (Table 1). Subsequently, 48 diverse Perilla varieties were used for the development of KASP markers and genetic analysis based on the identified SNP markers (Table 1). Among the 48 perilla resources used in the experiment, 44 were cultivars and breeding lines developed by the NICS, Republic of Korea. No. Two accessions (No. 47 and No. 48) were seed samples collected in Jeju Island, Republic of Korea and maintained by NICS. All accessions are deposited at the National Agrobiodiversity Center (NAC), Republic of Korea, where they are managed under IT and K number systems and are publicly available. Details of these resources are presented in Table 1. As an exception, two cultivars—No. 34 (‘Manchu’) and No. 35 (‘Pungnyeon’) are leaf perilla cultivars developed by private seed companies. Seeds of these two cultivars were purchased from the market and used in this study. All other perilla materials used in this experiment were derived from seeds that had been maintained with high genetic purity in the institute’s own collection at the NICS. The experimental research was conducted in compliance with all relevant domestic and international guidelines and laws.

All Perilla plants were grown in a greenhouse at the Southern Area Crop Science, NICS, Miryang, South Korea. DNA was extracted from the first true leaves, approximately 1 cm in size, using the MACHEREY–NAGEL NucleoSpin Plant II mini kit (Macherey–Nagel, Dueren, Germany).

Resequencing and SNP discovery

NGS was performed to generate genomic data. A 150 bp paired-end library was constructed following Illumina protocols, and DNA was sequenced using the Illumina Hi-seq X equipment. After sequencing, low-quality reads and those shorter than 25 bases were removed using the Solexa QA package v1.13. The cleaned reads were aligned to the reference genome of Perilla ‘Jeju17’(Perilla citriodora)4 with BWA v0.6.1-r10430 using the following parameters: maximum number of gap extensions (− e) = 50, seed length (− l) = 30, maximum differences in the seed (− k) = 1, number of threads (− t) = 64, mismatch penalty (− M) = 6, gap open penalty (− O) = 15, and gap extension penalty (− E) = 8. Reads with high mapping quality were retained for variant calling.

Mapped reads were extracted from the resulting BAM file using SAMtools v.0.1.1631. SNP calling was performed with the SAMtools varFilter command using the following criteria: minimal mapping quality (− Q) = 30, minimum read depth (− d) = 3, maximum read depth (− D) = 100, minimum indel score for nearby-SNP filtering (− G) = 30, distance from gaps to filter SNPs (− w) = 15 bp, and window size for dense SNP filtering (− W) = 30. The minimum and maximum read depths were set to 3 and 100, respectively.

Biallelic loci were selected using an in-house script among the called SNP positions32,33, and the raw SNP matrices were constructed comparing each sample with the draft genome. Non-SNP loci were filled from consensus sequences of each sample. A final SNP matrix was generated by filtering out mis-called SNP loci. SNPs were then classified into three categories based on the proportion of mapped reads at each locus: homozygous SNPs (read depth ≥ 90%), heterozygous SNPs (40% ≤ read depth ≤ 60%), and other SNPs not meeting these criteria.

SNP filtering and marker selection were performed using the following criteria. Polymorphic SNPs were first identified as loci showing sequence variation in at least one sample, with a minimum depth of three reads. A genotype missing filter (0% missing) was then applied to remove loci with missing data in any sample. SNPs showing multiple BLAST hit across the genome were excluded by performing BLAST searches of 150-bp flanking sequences against the tetraploid reference genome. SNP loci with alignments showing ≥ 90% identity and ≥ 50% query coverage in two or more genomic regions were eliminated. Finally, SNP loci with a polymorphic information content (PIC) ≥ 0.3 were retained to ensure high informativeness.

Development of Kompetitive Allele-specific PCR markers

For KASP marker analysis, custom KASP assay mixes were obtained from LGC Genomics (London, UK), and reactions were performed using KASP 2 × Master Mix (KASP 5000 V4.0, LGC Genomics, London, UK). Each 10.14 µL PCR reaction consisted of 0.5 µL template DNA (10 ng/µL), 5 µL KASP Assay mix, 0.14 µL KASP primer and 4.5 µL distilled water. A non-template control (NTC) substituted sterile distilled water for DNA. Reactions were dispensed into a MicroAmpTM Optical 96 Well Reaction Plate (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA).

Gene amplification conditions included an initial hot-start activation at 94 °C for 15 min, followed by ten touchdown cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 20 s, annealing/extension starting at 61 °C for 60 s, decreasing by 0.6 °C per cycle to 55 °C. This was followed by 26 additional cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 20 s and annealing at 55 °C for 60 s. Allele discrimination analysis was conducted using a QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA) and analyzed with SDS software (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA). For fluorescence data acquisition, plates were read at 30 °C for 30 s with data capture enabled, following the standard KASP end-point protocol in which plates were cooled to below 40 °C prior to reading.

Genotyping was performed on all 48 perilla varieties using the developed KASP markers, exhibiting genetic diversity and consistent PCR amplification. In addition, A minimal KASP marker set was selected to discriminate cultivars within each of the leaf-type and seed-type perilla groups. The genotype information obtained from this marker set was converted into a barcode format, represented by binary values (0 or 1). For the selection of the minimal KASP marker set, representative cultivars widely cultivated in South Korea were used. These included nine seed-type perilla cultivars (‘Dayu’, ‘Deulseam’, ‘Sodam’, ‘Deulhyang’, ‘Deulchan’, ‘Daesil’, ‘Anyu’, ‘Baekjin’, and ‘Dami’) and eight leaf-type perilla cultivars (‘Namcheon’, ‘Soim’, ‘Sangyeop’, ‘Donggeul1’, ‘Donggeul2’, ‘Ipdeulkkae1’, ‘Saebora’, and ‘Neulbora’), all of which were developed by the NICS.

To demonstrate the application of KASP markers in cultivars classification and seed purity assessment, various seed mixtures of the representative seed Perilla cultivars ‘Dayu’ and ‘Deulsaem’ were prepared in three replicates: A (100% Dayu), B (70% Dayu:30% Deulsaem), C (50% Dayu:50% Deulsaem), D (30% Dayu:70% Deulsaem), and E (100% Deulsaem). DNA was extracted from these mixtures (10 ng/µL DNA concentration) and analyzed. Additionally, for high-throughput screening, DNA was extracted from 95 individual plants per cultivar for nine seed Perilla cultivars (‘Dayu’, ‘Deulsaem’, ‘Sodam’, ‘Deulhyang’, ‘Deulchan’, ‘Daesil’, ‘Anyu’, ‘Baekjin’, and ‘Dami’), and tested using the SoellexTM system (Douglas Scientific LLC, USA).

Genetic analysis

Principal coordinates analysis

PIC (polymorphic information content) was calculated to evaluate the informativeness of each SNP marker for detecting polymorphism within a population. For biallelic SNPs, PIC was calculated using the formula: PIC = 1 − (p2 + q2) − 2p2q234, where p represents the major allele frequency and q represnets the minor allele frequency. These SNPs were utilized for PCoA, followed by clustering analysis based on Nei’s genetic distance among varieties using the GenAlEx ver. 6.5 program.
