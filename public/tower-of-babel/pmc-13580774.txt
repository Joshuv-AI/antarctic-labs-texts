Evaluation of Sustained Disease Control With Nipocalimab Versus Placebo in the Phase 3 Vivacity‐MG3 Study
Authors: Tuan Vu, Said R. Beydoun, Richard J. Nowak, Hans Katzberg, Maria Ait‐Tihyaty, Kavita Gandhi, John J. Sheehan, Sheryl Pease, Ibrahim Turkoz, Bernie Sattin, Zia Choudhry, Sindhu Ramchandren, Carlo Antozzi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPatients with generalized myasthenia gravis (gMG) frequently have variable and fluctuating symptoms. Treatments providing long‐term, sustained disease control are essential for improving function and health‐related quality of life.MethodsIn the phase 3 Vivacity‐MG3 study, nipocalimab improved clinical outcomes in adults with gMG when added to standard‐of‐care (SOC) therapy. These post hoc analyses further assessed the ability of nipocalimab to produce sustained responses over 24 weeks, using the following response categories for total score improvements in Myasthenia Gravis‐Activities of Daily Living (MG‐ADL) and Quantitative Myasthenia Gravis (QMG), respectively: Meaningful Clinical Improvement (MCI; ≥ 2‐ and ≥ 3‐point), Substantial Improvement (SI; ≥ 3‐ and ≥ 4‐point), Considerable Improvement (CIP; ≥ 4‐ and ≥ 5), Composite Response (CR; MG‐ADL improvement ≥ 2‐points plus QMG improvement ≥ 3‐points), and Minimal Symptom Expression (MSE; MG‐ADL 0 or 1).ResultsSignificantly higher percentages of nipocalimab‐treated than placebo‐treated participants achieved MG‐ADL MCI, SI, and CIP, sustained for ≥ 12 consecutive weeks; achievement of these outcomes occurred as early as week 1 among nipocalimab‐treated participants. Similar results were observed for QMG MCI, SI, and CIP. Significantly higher percentages of nipocalimab‐treated participants achieved CR, sustained for ≥ 8 consecutive weeks versus placebo; the likelihood of achieving sustained CR for ≥ 8 weeks was higher with nipocalimab (odds ratio = 6.3). Nipocalimab‐treated participants were 4‐fold more likely to achieve MSE, sustained for ≥ 8 consecutive weeks versus placebo (p = 0.022). MSE and CR were achieved earlier among nipocalimab‐treated participants.ConclusionsNipocalimab treatment improves gMG symptoms earlier than placebo and provides meaningful and long‐term sustained disease control over 24 weeks among participants with gMG.

Introduction

Patients with generalized myasthenia gravis (gMG) frequently experience a variable disease course, with unpredictable symptom worsening and remission [1, 2]. Unpredictable symptoms and mobility limitations adversely impact activities of daily living, health‐related quality of life, work ability, mental health, and social interactions [2, 3, 4].

Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors, corticosteroids, and non‐steroidal immunosuppressants have been the cornerstones of chronic gMG management, whereas plasma exchange and high dose intravenous immunoglobulin therapy provide transient disease control during acute symptom worsening and myasthenic crises [5, 6, 7, 8]. Traditional therapeutic agents for gMG often provide suboptimal disease control because symptom relief can be delayed, temporary, inconsistent, inadequate, and/or associated with side effects, impacting treatment adherence and general well‐being [9, 10, 11]. Thus, there is an unmet need for gMG treatments with efficacious, consistent, and sustained disease control to foster better health‐related quality of life, reduce healthcare costs, and improve patient satisfaction. Limitations of traditional gMG therapies prompted development of selectively targeted immunotherapies including complement‐5 inhibitors [12, 13, 14], B‐cell directed agents [15], and neonatal fragment crystallizable receptor (FcRn) blockers [16, 17, 18].

Nipocalimab (M281, JNJ‐80202135) is a novel, fully human, aglycosylated monoclonal antibody that targets FcRn with high affinity and specificity, blocking binding of immunoglobulin G (IgG) to FcRn [19, 20, 21]. Blockade of FcRn by nipocalimab selectively causes IgG reductions without impacting other immunoglobulin types [19]. In randomized, placebo‐controlled phase 2 (Vivacity‐MG) and phase 3 (Vivacity‐MG3) trials of nipocalimab plus standard‐of‐care (SOC)‐treated adults with gMG, nipocalimab reduced total IgG and autoantibody levels and resulted in clinically meaningful and statistically significant improvements in the Myasthenia Gravis‐Activities of Daily Living (MG‐ADL) [18, 22] and Quantitative Myasthenia Gravis (QMG) scores [18] compared with placebo plus SOC. In a single‐arm trial of nipocalimab plus SOC in adolescents, there was a reduction in serum IgG and improvements in MG‐ADL and QMG scores [23]. Nipocalimab is approved in several countries for treatment of gMG in adult and pediatric patients (≥ 12 years of age) who have anti‐acetylcholine receptor (AChR) or anti‐muscle‐specific kinase (MuSK) antibodies [24, 25].

This work extends the primary analysis of nipocalimab efficacy during the double‐blind period of Vivacity‐MG3 [18], evaluating the timing of onset and persistence of disease control during the 24‐week double‐blind period. We show that nipocalimab provides rapid onset and sustained disease control versus placebo as measured by proportions of participants achieving response categories of improvement in the MG‐ADL and QMG measures.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

Vivacity‐MG3 (NCT04951622) is an international, multicenter, randomized, double‐blind study designed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of nipocalimab versus placebo, each with SOC in participants with gMG [18]. Vivacity‐MG3 had a screening period (≤ 4 weeks) and a 24‐week double‐blind, placebo‐controlled phase followed by an ongoing open‐label extension (OLE) phase of variable duration per country. Detailed inclusion/exclusion criteria were described [18]. Vivacity‐MG3 enrolled adults (≥ 18 years) with a diagnosis of gMG defined by Myasthenia Gravis Foundation of America Clinical Classification Class II‐IV [26], and MG‐ADL score ≥ 6.

The protocol and amendments were approved by an institutional review board or independent ethics committee at each study site as appropriate (Table S1). Vivacity‐MG3 was conducted in accordance with the protocol/amendments, ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, and Good Clinical Practices guidelines. Written informed consent was provided by participants prior to enrollment.

Treatment

Eligible participants were randomly assigned (1:1) to receive either placebo or nipocalimab (30 mg/kg for first infusion, 15 mg/kg thereafter) once every 2 weeks (Q2W) for 24 weeks, each in combination with SOC [18].

Assessments

The MG‐ADL is an 8‐item clinician‐administered, patient‐reported measure of activities of daily living across four functional domains (ocular, bulbar, respiratory, and limb) [27, 28, 29]. Items are scored on a 4‐point scale (0 [normal] to 3 [severe/unable]) with total scores ranging from 0 to 24; higher scores indicate greater disease severity [27, 28]. A 2‐point improvement from baseline in MG‐ADL total score at the individual patient level is considered meaningful clinical improvement [29]. The MG‐ADL questionnaire was assessed at screening and on day 1, and weeks 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 16, 20, 22, 23, and 24.

The QMG is an objective, physician‐assessed measure of muscle strength [28, 30, 31]. There are 13 items within 4 domains: ocular, bulbar, respiratory, and gross motor. Items are scored on a 4‐point scale (0 [none] to 3 [severe]) [28, 30, 31]. Total scores range from 0 to 39; higher scores indicate greater disease severity. A three‐point improvement from baseline in total score at the individual patient level is considered meaningful clinical improvement [28, 30, 32]. The QMG questionnaire was assessed at screening, on day 1, and at weeks 2, 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 22, and 24.

Statistical Analyses

Analyses were performed using the primary efficacy analysis set defined as all randomized, seropositive (anti‐AChR positive, anti‐MuSK positive, and/or anti‐LRP4 positive) participants who received at least one dose of study intervention in the Vivacity‐MG3 double‐blind phase. In this post hoc analysis, no adjustments for multiplicity were performed. We reported 95% confidence intervals (CI) to aid interpretation and offer a quantitative measure of the precision and clinical relevance of the estimates.

Table S2 summarizes endpoints used in this study. The percentages of participants achieving the following response categories on the MG‐ADL total score at weeks 22, 23, and 24 and for sustained time intervals were calculated: ≥ 2‐points improvement in change from baseline (CFB) (Meaningful Clinical Improvement [MCI]) [29], ≥ 3‐points CFB (Substantial Improvement [SI]), ≥ 4‐points CFB (Considerable Improvement [CIP]), and Minimal Symptom Expression (MSE) (MG‐ADL total score of 0 or 1). While an MG‐ADL of ≥ 2‐points improvement is generally established as clinically meaningful (or MCI) [29] the impact of improvement achieved at the individual patient level also depends on the baseline score at treatment initiation. For those with more severe (higher) MG‐ADL scores, a more substantial absolute mean reduction from baseline may be necessary for them to experience appreciable improvement [33]. Hence, percentages of participants achieving higher improvement thresholds (≥ 3‐ and ≥ 4‐points) were also evaluated. MCI was a pre‐defined key secondary endpoint, and MSE was a pre‐defined secondary objective of the Vivacity‐MG3 study although many of the analyses were post hoc (Table S2). Participants with sustained responses in MCI, SI, CIP, and the Composite Response (see below) measures for at least 8, 12, 16, and 20 weeks were identified as those meeting these criteria consecutively for the respective durations. In addition, participants were considered to have experienced improvement if they met the improvement criteria for at least 75% of the cumulative time within the study for a given category, where each response duration is the number of days between consecutive visits meeting the improvement criteria. The earliest point at which a patient becomes eligible to accrue improvement time is week 1 for MG‐ADL and week 2 for QMG. The cumulative improvement time is divided by the length of the double‐blind phase to determine the proportion of time spent in the improved state. Lastly, the percentages of participants who met MG‐ADL MCI response criteria at weeks 2 and 24 were also calculated.

For MG‐ADL (MCI, SI, and CIP defined above) and QMG (MCI [≥ 3‐point within‐participant CFB], SI [≥ 4‐point within‐participant CFB], and CIP [≥ 5‐point within‐participant CFB]), between‐group differences for response rates were based on a Fisher's exact test or Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel test (CMH) adjusting for autoantibody status (antibody‐positive [for AChR, MuSK or LRP‐4]) and region (East Asia, United States or Rest of World); stratified odds ratios (OR) and corresponding 95% CI were based on logistic regression models similarly adjusting for autoantibody status and region. The median percentage of time in the study during which participants achieved the defined response categories in MG‐ADL and QMG, and MSE were compared between groups using the Wilcoxon test.

Composite response (CR) at the individual patient‐level, defined as meeting both CFB of ≥ 2‐point improvement for MG‐ADL plus ≥ 3‐point improvement for QMG total scores, was calculated at each visit. Improvement over 24 weeks was based on a repeated‐measures effects generalized estimating equation model with region, autoantibody status, treatment, and visit as fixed‐effect factors and interaction of treatment and visit. Correlation of the within‐participant repeated measures was modeled with an autoregressive correlation structure. Between‐group differences for CR of various times were assessed using a stratified CMH test adjusting for autoantibody status and region; stratified ORs were based on logistic regression adjusting for autoantibody status and region. Participants with missing MG‐ADL and/or QMG values were regarded as not meeting improvement criteria.

Meaningful Clinical Improvement

MG‐ADL

Significantly greater percentages of nipocalimab‐treated versus placebo‐treated participants achieved MG‐ADL MCI, sustained for ≥ 12, 16, and 20 consecutive weeks (Figure 1A). The odds of achieving MG‐ADL MCI (≥ 2‐point improvement from baseline in MG‐ADL total score) at weeks 22, 23, and 24 were 2.5‐fold higher with nipocalimab than placebo (Table 2). Likewise, the odds of achieving MG‐ADL MCI, sustained for ≥ 12, 16, and 20 consecutive weeks were greater for the nipocalimab than placebo group (Figure 1B). Additionally, among participants achieving MG‐ADL MCI at double‐blind week 2, 87.8% of nipocalimab‐treated participants (versus 66.7% placebo; p = 0.031) also met MG‐ADL MCI at week 24. At any time post‐baseline, over 90% of participants in each group achieved MG‐ADL MCI.

Achievement of MG‐ADL MCI (MG‐ADL ≥ 2) occurred earlier among nipocalimab‐treated participants (Table 2). The nipocalimab group achieved MG‐ADL MCI more often than placebo, spending > 75% of the study duration with MG‐ADL MCI (p < 0.001) (Table 2). Up to week 24, time spent with MG‐ADL MCI was nominally longer in the nipocalimab group (Table 2).

QMG

QMG MCI occurred earlier in the nipocalimab group (Table 2). The odds of achieving QMG MCI at weeks 22 and 24 were 3.8‐fold higher with nipocalimab than placebo (Table 2). Significantly higher percentages of nipocalimab‐treated vs. placebo participants achieved QMG‐MCI, and sustained ≥ 8, 12, 16, and 20 consecutive weeks (Figure 1C). Odds ratios demonstrate that participants receiving nipocalimab were more likely to sustain QMG MCI for ≥ 8, 12, 16, and 20 consecutive weeks versus placebo (Figure 1D). Significantly more nipocalimab‐treated than placebo participants spent > 75% of study duration with QMG MCI (Table 2). At any time post‐baseline, 75.3% of nipocalimab‐treated participants achieved QMG MCI versus 63.2% for placebo (p = 0.064).

Substantial Improvement

MG‐ADL

The odds of achieving SI at weeks 22, 23, and 24 were 2.9‐fold higher with nipocalimab versus placebo (Table 2). A significantly greater percentage of nipocalimab‐treated versus placebo participants achieved SI (MG‐ADL CFB ≥ 3 points), sustained ≥ 8, 12, 16, and 20 consecutive weeks (Figure 2A). The odds of achieving SI, sustained ≥ 8, 12, 16, and 20 consecutive weeks were ≥ 3.2‐fold higher with nipocalimab than placebo (Figure 2B). At any time post‐baseline, over 80% of participants in each group achieved MG‐ADL SI.

Nipocalimab‐treated participants achieved MG‐ADL SI significantly earlier than placebo participants and spent more time in MG‐ADL SI (Table 2). Likewise, significantly more participants in the nipocalimab group spent ≥ 75% of study time with MG‐ADL SI (Table 2).

QMG

The odds of achieving QMG SI at weeks 22 and 24 were 4.0‐fold higher with nipocalimab versus placebo (Table 2). Achievement of QMG SI occurred significantly earlier among nipocalimab‐treated participants than placebo (Table 2). Significantly more nipocalimab‐treated participants sustained QMG SI for ≥ 8, 12, 16, and 20 consecutive weeks (Figure 2C). Participants receiving nipocalimab were > 3‐fold more likely than placebo to have QMG SI improvement, sustained for ≥ 8, 12, 16, and 20 consecutive weeks (Figure 2D). Significantly more nipocalimab‐treated participants spent ≥ 75% of study time with QMG SI (Table 2). At any time post‐baseline, 70.1% and 46.1% of nipocalimab‐ and placebo‐treated participants, respectively, achieved QMG SI (QMG CFB ≥ 4; p = 0.001).

Considerable Improvement

MG‐ADL

Compared with placebo, significantly more nipocalimab‐treated participants achieved MG‐ADL CIP (MG‐ADL CFB ≥ 4‐points) sustained for ≥ 8, 12, 16, and 20 consecutive weeks (Figure 3A). Likewise, the odds of achieving MG‐ADL CIP, sustained ≥ 8, 12, 16, and 20 consecutive weeks was ≥ 3.8‐fold higher with nipocalimab than placebo treatment (Figure 3B). Achievement of MG‐ADL CIP was significantly earlier in the nipocalimab group (Table 2).

QMG

Significantly more nipocalimab‐treated participants achieved QMG CIP (QMG CFB ≥ 5‐points) sustained for ≥ 8, 12, 16, and 20 consecutive weeks versus placebo (Figure 3C). The odds of achieving MG‐ADL CIP, sustained ≥ 8, 12, 16, and 20 consecutive weeks, were ≥ 3‐fold higher with nipocalimab than placebo treatment (Figure 3D). Achievement of QMG CIP was significantly earlier in the nipocalimab group (Table 2).

Minimal Symptom Expression

The odds of achieving MSE (MG‐ADL score of 0 or 1) at week 22, 23, or 24 were 4.3‐fold higher with nipocalimab than placebo (Table 2). Compared with placebo, significantly more nipocalimab‐treated participants achieved MSE (up to 4‐fold more likely), sustained for ≥ 8 weeks (Figure 4A,B). Discussion

As previously reported, in Vivacity‐MG3, nipocalimab‐treated participants when compared with those treated with placebo experienced significant improvement in mean CFB in MG‐ADL over weeks 22, 23, and 24 and QMG scores over weeks 22 and 24 [18]. Improvement was rapid, as mean CFB differences between treatment groups (eg., separation between curves) favoring nipocalimab were apparent at the first post‐dose assessments (MG‐ADL, week 1; QMG, week 2) [18]. In these new analyses, nipocalimab demonstrated rapid, substantial, and sustained disease control in participants with gMG based on both patient‐reported (MG‐ADL) and clinician‐assessed (QMG) measures of disease activity.

In clinical trials, CFB in MG‐ADL is a commonly used primary endpoint but in recent years, responder status, MSE, and sustenance of treatment effect have gained great interest [34]. While a ≥ 2‐point improvement (MCI) in MG‐ADL is an established threshold for clinical benefit [29], it may not fully capture the entire spectrum of patient responses, particularly those with more pronounced/severe disease at baseline wherein the meaningfulness of change scores depends on the baseline distribution of scores. Not all patients who achieve a ≥ 2‐point improvement experience the same magnitude of benefit. Therefore, higher response thresholds, such as ≥ 3‐ and ≥ 4‐points (substantial improvement [SI] and considerable improvement [CIP]), were also evaluated to provide a more nuanced understanding of treatment effects. These additional categories facilitate differentiation among responders, supporting a more personalized assessment of clinical benefit. Specifically, SI reflects a more substantial change in daily functioning, which may translate into more noticeable and impactful improvements in patients' quality of life, while CIP indicates an even greater benefit, potentially corresponding to significant functional gains. Incorporating these higher thresholds aligns with a patient‐centered approach by emphasizing meaningful and substantial improvements that are more likely to influence daily functioning.

MG‐ADL improvement in the placebo group was quite high (−3.25) in Vivacity‐MG3; [18] thus, it is not surprising that the percentages of placebo‐treated patients who achieved MCI and SI at any time were high. However, these analyses using sustained response thresholds over ≥ 8, ≥ 12, ≥ 16, and ≥ 20 weeks capture clinically meaningful differences in sustenance of improvement between nipocalimab‐ and placebo‐treated groups. The chosen sustained time intervals map to the Vivacity‐MG3 assessment schedule and reduce the chance of transient, short‐lived or chance fluctuations (including placebo responses) that may be observed at any time drive apparent treatment effects. Evaluating progressively longer sustained durations also allows assessment of both initial durability and long‐term maintenance of benefit, which are more relevant to clinical care than a single isolated improvement. To contextualize our results with the literature, any time responses were reported; however, in our view. sustained responses are more meaningful to patients and clinicians.

In these analyses, 53.2% of nipocalimab‐treated participants (vs 27.6% placebo) reported clinically meaningful improvement (2‐point MG‐ADL improvement) sustained for ≥ 20 weeks; over the 24‐week double‐blind period, the median time spent with clinically meaningful, participant‐assessed improvement was longer in the nipocalimab group. Likewise, over the 24‐week double‐blind treatment period of Vivacity‐MG3, the median time spent with SI was longer with nipocalimab. Improvement was rapid in onset; the improvement in MG‐ADL ≥ 2 and MG‐ADL ≥ 3 was reported as early as week 1 (median).

Notably, patients reporting clinically meaningful improvement may still have clinically relevant disease [34]. The concept of MSE (MG‐ADL total score 0 or 1), which was derived from the definition of “remission” [35] was used with eculizumab in REGAIN, [36] efgartigimod in ADAPT, [17] and nipocalimab in Vivacity‐MG3 [18] In Vivacity‐MG3, among nipocalimab‐treated participants, 31% achieved MSE at any time during the double‐blind phase, [18] 23% achieved MSE during consecutive visits, and 18% of participants sustained MSE for ≥ 8 weeks. Achievement of MSE was rapid with the median earliest time of MSE occurring 3 weeks after initiating nipocalimab. Of participants who reached MSE during consecutive visits at any time during the double‐blind phase, the median time spent with sustained MSE was approximately 2‐fold longer with nipocalimab than placebo (101.5 days vs 55 days). Notably, nipocalimab‐treated participants could achieve sustained MCI, substantial improvement, and MSE despite having considerable disease burden with stable background therapy at study baseline.

We also evaluated CR as improvement in both total score CFB of ≥ 2‐points for MG‐ADL and ≥ 3‐points for QMG. Combining both patient and physician perspectives provides a useful assessment of treatment response. Half of nipocalimab‐treated participants achieved CR that was sustained for ≥ 8 weeks, and over 25% of nipocalimab‐treated participants achieved CR response sustained for ≥ 20 weeks. Since QMG was not assessed until 2 weeks post baseline, CR by definition could not be determined until week 2; nonetheless, CR was observed as early as week 2.

It should be noted that two FcRn‐targeting agents in addition to nipocalimab (efgartigimod, rozanolixizumab) are currently approved for treatment of gMG but are administered as symptom‐based cyclic regimen that are repeated when the patient starts to show loss of improvement [16, 17, 37, 38]. During treatment cycles with efgartigimod (ADAPT) or rozanolixizumab (MycarinG), MG‐ADL and QMG responses (mean CFB) improved and occurred at maximal levels while the study drug was administered, but then waned and approached placebo levels after the drug was stopped and before initiating the next cycle [16, 17]. In practice, the duration and degree of improvement can have considerable patient‐to‐patient variability [39, 40]. Cyclic administration may be appropriate for some patients whose disease control is maintained even with longer inter‐cycle duration. However, there are patients with fluctuating disease control and for whom sustained disease control is preferred [2] and more appropriate.

This study has some limitations. These analyses were post hoc/exploratory and involved multiple unadjusted comparisons, so the risk of type I error is increased and nominally significant p‐values may reflect chance occurrences, so the findings should be interpreted cautiously. Although MCI (≥ 2‐points and ≥ 3‐point improvements for MG‐ADL and QMG, respectively) are well‐established, [28] and MSE is increasingly described in clinical trials, [16, 17, 18]. SI, CIP, CR, and MSE have not yet been clinically validated. In clinical practice, the meaningfulness of a given change at a time point is dependent on where in the score continuum a patient started and wherein higher substantial improvement may be perceived clinically meaningful with more severe/worse MG‐ADL scores. Thus, multiple thresholds of change were analyzed—and while not validated, they do provide quantitative measures of improvement that are relevant. Because the first post‐dose MG‐ADL and QMG assessments occurred at week 1 and week 2, respectively, earlier improvement cannot be excluded. Additionally, sample sizes were small, particularly for the MSE analysis, so the clinical significance of some outcomes (eg., MSE) should be guided by the confidence intervals, and need confirmation in a larger and more diverse gMG population to assess generalizability. Finally, future studies would be needed to explore the impact of various patient characteristics on sustained response to nipocalimab.

The strengths of this study include the use of both patient‐reported and clinician‐assessed measures to determine treatment efficacy. The use of a clinician‐administered, patient‐reported measure (MG‐ADL) is consistent with regulatory agencies placing an increased emphasis on the use of patient‐reported outcomes [28, 41]. In conclusion, as measured by the response categories of improvement in MG‐ADL and QMG, nipocalimab treatment effects were rapid, clinically meaningful, and sustained during the 24‐week double‐blind period of the phase 3 Vivacity‐MG3 study.
