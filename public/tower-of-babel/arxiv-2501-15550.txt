Abstract
. We use the geometric reformulation of Markov’s uniqueness conjecture in terms of the simple length spectrum of the modular torus to rewrite the conjecture in combinatorial terms by explicitly describing this set of lengths.

I.  Result

Denote by
\mathscr{N}
the set of primitive necklaces of positive integers with small variation different from
[1]
, i.e. cyclic classes of finite sequences of positive integers such that the sum of any two blocks of the same size has difference at most
1
(see Definition
III.2
). Primitivity refers to not being represented by a power by concatenation of a smaller sequence.

Define the following function on this set of necklaces.

(I.1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\Phi:\mathscr{N}&\to\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\\
[n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}]&\mapsto\frac{1}{10^{k}\cdot 2^{n_{1}+\cdots+n_{k}}}\sum_{S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,k\}}3^{r(S)}2^{k-r(S)}(\xi+2)^{|S|}(\overline{\xi}+2)^{|S^{c}|}\xi^{\sum_{i\in S}n_{i}}\overline{\xi}^{\sum_{i\in S^{c}}n_{i}},\end{split}

for
\xi=3+\sqrt{5}
and
\overline{\xi}=3-\sqrt{5}
, where
r(S)=\sum_{s\in\{\text{runs of }S\text{ and }S^{c}\}}|s|-1
if
S\neq\{1,\ldots,k\}
, and
r(\{1,\ldots,k\})=k
. A
run

s
of
S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,k\}
is a maximal subset
s\subseteq S
such that the numbers are (cyclically) consecutive.

We prove an equivalent statement to Markov’s uniqueness conjecture as follows.

Theorem I.1
.

Markov’s uniqueness conjecture is equivalent to

The function
\Phi
is injective in
\mathscr{N}
.

Previous work in
[
FL24
]
studies the strong rigidity of the set
\mathscr{N}
, giving a parametrization of the set via choosing the consecutive integers appearing in a necklace and the number of occurrences of each, finding the bijection (see
[

),

\mathscr{N}\leftrightarrow\{(x,y,m)\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{3}\mid\gcd(x,y)=1\}\cup\{(0,1,m)\mid m\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\}.

Remark I.2
.

Necklaces in
\mathscr{N}
coincide with the cyclic classes of exponents of the most appearing term in finite Sturmian words or Christoffel words (see e.g.
[
Aig13
, Chapter 7]
), generated by the lower stair sequence of the segment in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
joining
(0,0)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and
(m,n)\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{2}
for
\gcd(m,n)=1
.

II.  Introduction

A triple of positive integers
x,y,z>0
such that

x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}=3xyz

is called a
Markov triple
and were introduced by Markov in the late XIX century. Frobenius conjectured in
[
Fro13
]
that these triples are always determined by their largest number, also called a
Markov number
. This is known as
Markov’s uniqueness conjecture
, see
[
Aig13
]
. Since then, many partial results have been made for Markov numbers of a given form, such as prime powers or particular linear functions of prime powers, see e.g.
[
Bar96
,
Zha07
]
. However, the general statement remains open.

Define the modular torus
\mathscr{M}
as the unique hyperbolic structure on the once-punctured torus such that the isometry group is of maximal order, it being of order
12
. As proved by Cohn in
[
Coh71
]
, there is a map from Markov’s triples to lengths of simple closed geodesics on the modular torus. This allows us to reformulate Markov’s conjecture in geometric terms.

Markov’s conjecture is equivalent to the
geometric Markov’s uniqueness conjecture:

For every two simple closed geodesics on
\mathscr{M}
of the same length, there is an isometry bringing one to the other.
We refer to
[
MP08
]
for further explanation on this equivalence. This has a translation in terms of the multiplicity of the lengths of oriented simple closed geodesics on the modular torus: the first two lengths have multiplicity
6
and all the rest have multiplicity
12
. Here multiplicity refers to the number of geodesics attaining these lengths. This is due to the shortest
12
geodesics being invariant (up to orientation) by one of the isometries, whilst the rest are not. Moreover, one can compute explicitly their lengths, as it will be later done, getting to the expression of the spectrum in
III.1
.

Our main result is a reformulation of the geometric version of the conjecture via explicitly computing the lengths of all simple geodesics in combinatorial terms to find a new combinatorial version of the statement. There is a result of a similar nature in
[
GL21
]
where Gaster and Loustau use McShane’s identity in
[
McS98
]
on the simple closed geodesics of the modular torus to find a reformulation of the conjecture as an identity on Lagrange numbers.

III.  Development

Let
S
be a topological once-punctured torus, i.e. a surface of signature
(1,1)
. Fix
\{a,b\}
canonical generators of
\pi_{1}(S)\cong\mathbb{F}_{a,b}
as in Figure
1
. Define (oriented) closed curves as free homotopy classes of loops on
S
, that are in correspondence with conjugacy classes of
\pi_{1}(S)
. Define the set of closed curves as
\mathscr{C}(S)
. These are thus in correspondence with cyclic shift classes of cyclically reduced words in
\{a,b,a^{-1},b^{-1}\}
. Fix an ideal triangulation in
S
as the colored one in Figure
1
. Consider its dual graph
\Gamma
, a trivalent graph with two vertices embedded in
S
, also pictured in Figure
1
.

Recall that the Teichmüller space of
S
, denoted by
\mathscr{T}(S)
is the space of marked hyperbolic structures on the once-punctured torus
S
. As was first introduced by Thurston in
[
Thu98
]
, it can be parametrized by shear coordinates in the fixed ideal triangulation, measuring the hyperbolic displacement between adjacent ideal triangles. The modular torus is defined as the hyperbolic structure on
S
with zero shear coordinates and will be denoted by
\mathscr{M}
.

Given the dual graph of the fixed triangulation, there is a natural map

\omega:\mathscr{C}(S)\to\mathrm{words}(L,R)/\,\mathrm{cyclic\,\,shift}

by the following procedure. Given a closed curve, retract it to
\Gamma
without backtracking. Fix a startpoint. This gives a finite sequence of Left/Right turns on the graph until reaching the startpoint again. Consider this sequence as a word in "L", and "R". Forgetting the startpoint corresponds to considering cyclic shifts of the words.

Define two matrices,

L=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\text{, and }R=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}.

Let
\ell_{\mathscr{M}}:\mathscr{C}(S)\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
denote the hyperbolic length of a curve in the modular torus via measuring the length of the unique geodesic in the homotopy class. Identifying composition of words with multiplication of matrices, the following trace formula holds for the modular torus, see e.g.
[
MS16
, pp 8]
,

\ell_{\mathscr{M}}(\gamma)=2\cdot\mathrm{acosh}(\mathrm{Trace}(\omega(\gamma))/2).

Recall that a simple curve is a curve without self-intersection, i.e., that it can be represented by a loop on
S
that does not intersect itself. We want to study the simple length spectrum of
\mathscr{M}
,
\mathscr{SS}(\mathscr{M})
, i.e., the set of lengths of oriented simple closed curves with respect to
\ell_{\mathscr{M}}
, with multiplicity. There is a well-known classification of all words in
\{a,b,a^{-1},b^{-1}\}
corresponding to simple closed curves.

Theorem III.1

(
[
BS88
, Theorem 6.2]
)
.

Every simple closed curve on
S
can be represented, after suitably renaming the generators in
\{a,b,a^{-1},b^{-1}\}
, by one of the following words:

(1)

a
,

(2)

aba^{-1}b^{-1}
,

(3)

ab^{n_{1}}ab^{n_{2}}\cdots ab^{n_{r}}
, where
[n_{1},\dots,n_{r}]
is a necklace of positive integers with small variation (see Definition
III.2
).

Conversely, each of these words is homotopic to a power of a simple closed curve.

The only undefined ingredient so far is the following.

Definition III.2

(Necklace with small variation)
.

A
necklace of inegers
is a class of finite sequences of integers with respect to cyclic shifting. It is said to have
small variation
if every two (cyclic) blocks of the same size have sum difference at most
1
, i.e.
[n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}]
with
n_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
has small variation if for any
s\geq 0
, and for any
i_{0},j_{0}\in\{1,\ldots k\}
,

\bigg|\sum_{i=i_{0}}^{i_{0}+s}n_{i}-\sum_{j=j_{0}}^{j_{0}+s}n_{j}\bigg|\leq 1,

where indices are taken modulo
k
.

We want to study the lengths of the curves in Theorem
III.1
with the hyperbolic structure
\mathscr{M}
. The curves of the second type correspond to simple curves bounding the puncture and have zero hyperbolic length, hence we are only interested in the first and third types. Curves of the first type are only
4
, which are
a,b,a^{-1},b^{-1}
, which all correspond to the cyclic word
LR
in the graph
\Gamma
.

For the curves of the third type, note that for a given necklace of positive integers
[n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}]
with small variation different from
[1]
, there are eight closed curves via renaming the generators. These are

ab^{n_{1}}\cdots ab^{n_{k}},ba^{n_{1}}\cdots ba^{n_{k}},ab^{-n_{1}}\cdots ab^{-n_{k}}\text{, and }ba^{-n_{1}}\cdots ba^{-n_{k}},

and their inverses. These correspond to the cyclic words in
\Gamma

R(RL)^{n_{1}-1}L\cdots R(RL)^{n_{k}-1}L,L(LR)^{n_{1}-1}R\cdots L(LR)^{n_{k}-1}R,

R(RL)^{n_{1}}L\cdots R(RL)^{n_{k}}L\text{, and }L(LR)^{n_{1}}R\cdots L(LR)^{n_{k}}R,

respectively. Moreover, the first two will have the same trace and so will the last two. Similarly, to the exceptional necklace
[1]
there are only four closed curves via renaming the generators, which are
ab
,
ab^{-1}
and their inverses, corresponding to the cyclic words in the graph
\Gamma
:
LR
and
LLRR
, respectively.

Let
\omega=[n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}]
be a necklace of positive integers. Asssume
n_{i}\in\{m,m+1\}
for some
m\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
for all
i=1,\ldots,k
, as otherwise the necklace cannot have small variation. Let
|\omega|_{m}
and
|\omega|_{m+1}
be the number of occurrences of
m
and
m+1
. Denote by
\mathscr{N}
the
set of primitive necklaces of positive integers with small variation
different from
[1]
. By
[
FL24
, Proposition 2.8]
, given
m,x,y\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, there is a unique necklace with small variation
[n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}]
with
n_{i}\in\{m,m+1\}
for all
i=1,\dots k
and with
|\omega|_{m}=x
,
|\omega|_{m+1}=y
. Moreover, the necklace is primitive (not represented by a power of a shorter sequence) if and only if
\gcd(x,y)=1
.

Thus, defining the real-valued function
\theta(\_)=2\cdot\mathrm{acosh}(\mathrm{Trace}(\_)/2)
on
SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
,

\displaystyle\mathscr{SS}(\mathscr{M})=6\{\theta(LR)\}\,

\displaystyle\cup\,2\{\theta(LLRR)\}\,\cup\,4\{\theta(L(LR)^{n_{1}}R\cdots L(LR)^{n_{k}}R)\mid[n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}]\in\mathscr{N}\}

\displaystyle\cup\,4\{\theta(L(LR)^{n_{1}-1}R\cdots L(LR)^{n_{k}-1}R)\mid[n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}]\in\mathscr{N}\},

where the scalar multiples denote multiplicity of the set.

The small variation condition is invariant under the choice of the two consecutive integers appearing. Define
\mathscr{N}^{*}
as the set of primitive necklaces with all elements in
\{0,1\}
with small variation, excluding the two simplest necklaces
[0]
and
[1]
. It follows that

(III.1)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathscr{SS}(\mathscr{M})=&8\{\theta(L(LR)^{n_{1}}R\cdots L(LR)^{n_{k}}R)\mid[n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}]\in\mathscr{N}\}\,\cup\\
&4\{\theta(L(LR)^{n_{1}}R\cdots L(LR)^{n_{k}}R)\mid[n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}]\in\mathscr{N}^{*}\}\,\cup\\
&6\{\theta(LR)\}\,\cup\,6\{\theta(LLRR)\}.\end{split}

Lemma III.3
.

Let
\eta=[\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{l}]\in\mathscr{N}^{*}
. Write it uniquely (up to cyclic shifting) as

\eta=[0,\overset{n_{1}-1}{\ldots},0,1,0,\overset{n_{2}-1}{\ldots},0,1,\ldots,0,\overset{n_{k}-1}{\ldots},0,1].

Then,

\mathrm{Trace}(L(LR)^{\eta_{1}}R\cdots L(LR)^{\eta_{l}}R)=\mathrm{Trace}(L(LR)^{n_{1}}R\cdots L(LR)^{n_{k}}R).

Proof.

The lemma follows from elementary properties of the trace. We will write it for convenience of the reader. First of all,

L(LR)^{\eta_{1}}R\cdots L(LR)^{\eta_{l}}R=(LR)^{n_{1}-1}LLRR\cdot(LR)^{n_{2}-1}LLRR\cdot\,\cdots\,\cdot(LR)^{n_{k}-1}LLRR.

Applying a double cyclic shift and regrouping one finds

\displaystyle RR(LR)^{n_{1}-1}LLRR\cdot(LR)^{n_{2}-1}LLRR\cdot\,\cdots\,\cdot

\displaystyle(LR)^{n_{k}-1}LL=

\displaystyle=R(RL)^{n_{1}}L\cdot R(RL)^{n_{2}}L\cdots R(RL)^{n_{k}}L,

which has the same trace as
L(LR)^{n_{1}}R\cdots L(LR)^{n_{k}}R
.
∎

Moreover, note that in the bijection

\displaystyle\Theta:\{\text{necklace of positive integers}\}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\{\text{necklace of integers in }\{0,1\}\}

\displaystyle\eta=[n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}]

\displaystyle\longmapsto[0,\overset{n_{1}-1}{\ldots},0,1,0,\overset{n_{2}-1}{\ldots},0,1,\ldots,0,\overset{n_{k}-1}{\ldots},0,1],

it follows from
[
FL24
, Lemma 2.9]
that a necklace of positive integers
\eta
has small variation if and only if
\Theta(\eta)
has, as
\eta
is the necklace of runs of its image plus
1
, ensuring positivity.
Hence, it follows from
III.1
that the geometric Markov’s uniqueness conjecture is true if and only if

\{[n_{1},\dots,n_{k}]\in\mathscr{N}\mid\mathrm{Trace}(L(LR)^{n_{1}}R\cdots L(LR)^{n_{k}}R)=\ell\}\in\{0,1\}\text{ for all }\ell\in\mathbb{R}.

The last ingredient to prove is the rewriting of this trace in combinatorial terms of the simple length spectrum.

Proposition III.4
.

Let
\xi=3+\sqrt{5}
and
\overline{\xi}=3-\sqrt{5}
. For any
n_{1},\dots n_{k}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Trace}(L(LR)^{n_{1}}

\displaystyle R\cdots L(LR)^{n_{k}}R)=

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{10^{k}\cdot 2^{n_{1}+\cdots+n_{k}}}\sum_{S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,k\}}3^{r(s)}2^{k-r(S)}(\xi+2)^{|S|}(\overline{\xi}+2)^{|S^{c}|}\xi^{\sum_{i\in S}n_{i}}\overline{\xi}^{\sum_{i\in S^{c}}n_{i}},

where
r(S)=\sum_{s\in\{\text{runs of }S\text{ and }S^{c}\}}|s|-1
if
S\neq\{1,\ldots,k\}
, and
r(\{1,\ldots,k\})=k
.

Proof.

Note that

LR=\begin{pmatrix}2&1\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}=S\cdot\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\overline{\xi}&0\\
0&\xi\end{pmatrix}\cdot S^{-1},

where
\xi=3+\sqrt{5}
,
\overline{\xi}=3-\sqrt{5}
,
S=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\overline{\xi}-2&\xi-2\\
2&2\end{pmatrix}
and
S^{-1}=\frac{1}{10}\begin{pmatrix}2(\overline{\xi}-3)&\xi+2\\
2(\xi-3)&\overline{\xi}+2\end{pmatrix}
.

Hence,

L(LR)^{n}R=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\cdot\tilde{S}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}\overline{\xi}^{n}&0\\
0&\xi^{n}\end{pmatrix}\cdot\tilde{\tilde{S}},

where
\tilde{S}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\overline{\xi}&\xi\\
2&2\end{pmatrix}
and
\tilde{\tilde{S}}=\frac{1}{10}\begin{pmatrix}\overline{\xi}+2&\xi+2\\
\xi+2&\overline{\xi}+2\end{pmatrix}
.

Thus,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Trace}(L(LR)^{n_{1}}

\displaystyle R\cdots L(LR)^{n_{k}}R)=

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{10^{k}\cdot 2^{n_{1}+\cdots+n_{k}}}\cdot\mathrm{Trace}\Bigg(M\cdot\begin{pmatrix}\overline{\xi}^{n_{1}}&0\\
0&\xi^{n_{1}}\end{pmatrix}\cdot\,\cdots\,\cdot M\cdot\begin{pmatrix}\overline{\xi}^{n_{k}}&0\\
0&\xi^{n_{k}}\end{pmatrix}\Bigg),

where
M=10\cdot\tilde{\tilde{S}}\cdot\tilde{S}=\begin{pmatrix}3(\overline{\xi}+2)&2(\xi+2)\\
2(\overline{\xi}+2)&3(\xi+2)\end{pmatrix}.

Therefore, it is left to study

\mathrm{Trace}\Bigg(M\cdot\begin{pmatrix}\overline{\xi}^{n_{1}}&0\\
0&\xi^{n_{1}}\end{pmatrix}\cdot\,\cdots\,\cdot M\cdot\begin{pmatrix}\overline{\xi}^{n_{k}}&0\\
0&\xi^{n_{k}}\end{pmatrix}\Bigg)=\mathrm{Trace}(\prod_{l=1}^{k}A_{l}),

with
A_{l}=\begin{pmatrix}3(\overline{\xi}+2)\overline{\xi}^{n_{l}}&2(\xi+2)\xi^{n_{l}}\\
2(\overline{\xi}+2)\overline{\xi}^{n_{l}}&3(\xi+2)\xi^{n_{l}}\end{pmatrix}=(a_{i_{1},i_{2}}^{l})_{i_{1},i_{2}}
.

Write
a_{i_{1},i_{2}}^{l}=c_{i_{1},i_{2}}\cdot d_{i_{2}}^{l}
for
i_{1},i_{2}=1,2
, and
l=1,\ldots k
, where

c_{i_{1},i_{2}}=\begin{cases}3\text{ if }i_{1}=i_{2}\\
3\text{ if }i_{1}\neq i_{2}\end{cases},\text{ and }d_{i_{2}}^{l}=\begin{cases}(\overline{\xi}+2)\overline{\xi}^{n}_{l}\text{ if }i_{2}=1\\
(\xi+2)\xi^{n}_{l}\text{ if }i_{2}=2.\end{cases}

Thus,

\mathrm{Trace}(\prod_{l=1}^{k}A_{l})=\sum_{j_{1}=1}^{2}\cdots\sum_{j_{k}=1}^{2}a_{j_{1},j_{2}}^{1}\cdots a_{j_{k},j_{1}}^{k}=\sum_{S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,k\}}a_{j_{1},j_{2}}^{1}\cdots a_{j_{k},j_{1}}^{k},

where
j_{i}=1
if
i\in S
and
j_{i}=2
otherwise.

Hence,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Trace}(\prod_{l=1}^{k}A_{l})

\displaystyle=\sum_{S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,k\}}c_{j_{1},j_{2}}\cdots c_{j_{k},j_{1}}\cdot d_{j_{2}}^{1}\cdots d_{j_{1}}^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,k\}}3^{r(s)}2^{k-r(S)}(\xi+2)^{|S|}(\overline{\xi}+2)^{|S^{c}|}\xi^{\sum_{i\in S}n_{i}}\overline{\xi}^{\sum_{i\in S^{c}}n_{i}},

as was to be proven.
∎
