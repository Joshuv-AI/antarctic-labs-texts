On importance sampling and independent Metropolis–Hastings with an unbounded weight function Thanks: elmahdi.khribch@essec.edu Thanks: guanyang.wang@rutgers.edu
George Deligiannidis (University of Oxford), Pierre E. Jacob (ESSEC Business School)

Abstract
Importance sampling and independent Metropolis–Hastings are among the fundamental building blocks of Monte Carlo methods. Both require a proposal distribution that globally approximates the target distribution, and pointwise evaluation of the Radon–Nikodym derivative of the target distribution relative to the proposal, also called the weight function. We study the bias of importance sampling and independent Metropolis–Hastings, without assuming that the weight function is bounded. We show that the common random numbers coupling of independent Metropolis–Hastings is maximal. Using that coupling, we derive polynomial bounds on the total variation distance of the chain to its target distribution. We further consider bias removal techniques using couplings, and provide conditions under which the resulting unbiased estimators have finite moments, and under which their efficiency is comparable to that of importance sampling. Experiments illustrate unbiased estimators of the inverse of a normalizing constant, estimators of nested expectations, and combination of importance sampling with robust mean estimation methods.

Introduction

1.1
Context and contributions

1.1.1
Monte Carlo with global proposals

Monte Carlo methods aim to approximate a target distribution
\pi
on a measurable space
(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{X})
, for example
(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
. These techniques are crucial when analytical computation of expectations under
\pi
is infeasible.
The goal is to evaluate integrals of functions
f:\mathbb{X}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
with respect to
\pi
:

\displaystyle\pi(f):=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}[f]=\int_{\mathcal{X}}f(x)\pi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

(1)

Two primary approaches are Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods, that construct a Markov chain with
\pi
as its stationary distribution, and importance sampling (IS) methods, where the target distribution is approximated by weighted samples. Among MCMC methods, the independent Metropolis–Hastings (IMH) algorithm is a specialized form of the Metropolis–Rosenbluth–Teller–Hastings algorithm
(
Metropolis et al., 1953
;
Hastings, 1970
)
, in which proposals are drawn from a proposal distribution
q
, independently of the current state of the chain.
We assume that we can sample from
q
on
(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{X})
, such that

\pi
is absolutely continuous with respect to
q
, and we can evaluate the Radon–Nikodym derivative
\omega(x)=\pi(x)/q(x)
up to a multiplicative constant. We call
\omega
the
weight
function.
The same proposal
q
can be employed in importance sampling
(
Kahn, 1949
, IS,)
, where draws from
q
are weighted by
\omega
.
Rejection sampling could be implemented if
\omega
was bounded by a known constant, but in this article we let
\omega
be unbounded.

1.1.2
Comparing MH with independent proposals and importance sampling

Independent MH and IS are two approaches to account for the discrepancy between a proposal
q
and a target
\pi
, and their comparison is a natural question. The asymptotic variance of self-normalized IS (SNIS) estimators is lower than that of IMH estimators
(
Deligiannidis & Lee, 2018
)
, see (
7
) below. In both cases, the squared bias is negligible relative to the variance as the computing effort goes to infinity, and thus the bias can be ignored from the perspective of the asymptotic mean squared error. Perhaps for this reason,
the comparison of the bias of SNIS and IMH has received little attention, particularly in the general setting where the weight
\omega
may be unbounded.
However, when the resulting biased estimators are used for downstream tasks, the non-asymptotic bias matters and can even become the dominant issue.
We review multiple such settings in Section
1.1.3
.

The question of bias can be rephrased as follows for bounded test functions.
Among
N
independent draws
(X_{n})_{n=1}^{N}
from
q
, consider the selection of an index
I\in[N]=\{1,\ldots,N\}
such that
X_{I}
is as close as possible to
\pi
in total variation distance. Is it preferable to perform
N
steps of IMH and select the terminal state, or to draw
I
from a Categorical distribution with probabilities proportional to
\omega(X_{1}),\ldots,\omega(X_{N})
, as in Sampling-Importance-Resampling (SIR)
(
Rubin, 1988
)
? Our developments lead to the conclusion that, in some generality, IMH leads to a smaller bias than SIR; see Section
4.6
for a summary.
We further explore methods of entirely removing such biases, following
Middleton et al. (2019)
, and study their asymptotic efficiency in Section
5
.

1.1.3
Effect of the bias of Monte Carlo estimators

We review scenarios where the bias of Monte Carlo estimators has noticeable and often detrimental effects on downstream tasks, motivating both its study and its removal. In these settings, the bias interacts with downstream tasks in a different way than the variance, and thus matters even though its square may be small compared to the variance.

Optimization with stochastic gradients

Monte Carlo estimators are often used
in the approximation of gradients within stochastic gradient algorithms. The effect of the bias of gradient estimators in the encompassing optimization has received attention

(
Doucet & Tadic, 2017
;
Hu et al., 2021
;
Demidovich et al., 2023
;
Surendran et al., 2024
, e.g.)
.
The literature shows that the bias can induce a non-vanishing error, affect the convergence rate, or have no significant effect, depending on its magnitude. The case of
IS-based gradient estimators employed in training importance-weighted autoencoders (IWAEs) has received
particular attention: the bias of gradient estimators
is studied in
Daudel & Roueff (2024, Theorem 1)
,
and is shown to propagate
into the encompassing optimization in
Surendran et al. (2024, Corollary 4.5, Theorem 4.6 and Theorem B.1)
,
and in
Surendran et al. (2025, Theorem 3.7)
.
A number of bias-reduction techniques have been proposed
in the context of IWAEs, including jackknife correction
(
Nowozin, 2018
, Proposition 3)
, randomized multilevel Monte Carlo
(
Shi & Cornish, 2021
, Theorems 2 and 3)
, and the iterated SIR
(
Cardoso et al., 2022
, Theorems 3–4)
, assuming bounded weights. Experiments in
Shi & Cornish (2021)
;
Cardoso et al. (2022)
;
Surendran et al. (2024)
show that, relative to SNIS gradient estimators, reducing or removing the bias can be overall beneficial at matched computational cost.

Nested expectations

The bias of Monte Carlo estimators has a particularly clear effect in the context of nested expectations
(
Andradóttir & Glynn, 2016
)
.
The quantity of interest is of the form

\mathbb{E}_{1}\left[\mathbb{E}_{2|1}[f(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})|\Theta_{1}]\right],

(2)

where
\mathbb{E}_{2|1}
denotes a conditional expectation of
\Theta_{2}
given
\Theta_{1}
,
and
f
is a test function. We assume that we are able to sample
\Theta_{1}
from its marginal distribution, and that we can approximate
\mathbb{E}_{2|1}[f(\Theta_{1},\Theta_{2})|\Theta_{1}]
for each
\Theta_{1}
by a numerical method, such as SNIS or IMH.
Such nested expectations arise in modular Bayesian inference
(
Liu et al., 2009
;
Plummer, 2014
)
, Bayesian inference after multiple imputation
(
Zhou & Reiter, 2010
)
or experimental design
(
Rainforth et al., 2018
)
.

Assume that, for each realization
\theta_{1}
of
\Theta_{1}
, the conditional expectation

\mathbb{E}_{2|1}[f(\Theta_{1},\Theta)|\Theta_{1}=\theta_{1}]
is approximated by an estimator
\hat{F}_{N}(\theta_{1})
with cost
N
,
bias of order
N^{-\gamma}
for some
\gamma\in(0,\infty)
, and variance of order
N^{-1}
.
Consider the estimator
M^{-1}\sum_{m=1}^{M}\hat{F}_{N}(\Theta_{1,m})
, where
\Theta_{1,1},\ldots,\Theta_{1,M}
are i.i.d. draws from the marginal distribution of
\Theta_{1}
. The total cost is
C=NM
, and optimizing the budget allocation leads to an overall mean squared error of order
C^{-2\gamma/(2\gamma+1)}

(
Andradóttir & Glynn, 2016
, Remarks 3.5-3.6,)
. Therefore, the magnitude of the bias of the inner estimator directly impacts the overall rate of convergence of the estimator of the nested expectation of interest,
and therefore any reduction in bias brings concrete benefits in the overall mean squared error.
Furthermore, unbiased estimators for the inner expectation recover the canonical Monte Carlo rate for the nested expectation. We illustrate this in Subsection
6.2
.

Plug-and-play property of unbiased estimators

The effect of the bias of a Monte Carlo estimator in an encompassing method is
a priori
unknown, and the magnitude of that bias is typically hard to bound explicitly. Removing the bias offers a plug-and-play solution. We illustrate this in Section
6.3
with the setting of robust mean estimation. Robust mean estimators, such as median-of-means, typically assume access to variables that have expectation equal to the quantity of interest. Biased estimators can be used instead, as done by
Dau (2022)
with SNIS within median-of-means, but not without adjustments in both theory and implementation.

1.1.4
Our contributions

We consider the bias of self-normalized importance sampling and independent Metropolis–Hastings, and methods to remove that bias.
Our key assumption is that the
weight
function
\omega
has
p
finite moments under the proposal
q
. We obtain results on the bias and higher moments of self-normalized importance sampling estimators in Section
2
.
Using a common random numbers coupling studied in Section
3
, we show in Section
4
that the total variation distance between
\pi
and the IMH chain at iteration
t
decays as
t^{p-1}
; this provides polynomial bounds on the bias for bounded test functions. We provide an example for which we obtain matching lower bounds, up to logarithmic terms, suggesting that our working assumption of
p
finite moments of
\omega
under
q
is close to necessary for polynomial convergence rates.
We further obtain explicit dependence in
N
for the particle IMH algorithm
(
Andrieu et al., 2010
)
, a variant of IMH where
N
proposals are sampled at each iteration.
To establish precise bounds that account for both
t
and
N
, we develop novel methods to analyze the average weight,
\hat{Z}_{N}=N^{-1}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\omega(X_{n})
, and thereby control the rejection probability. We first use the
Paley–Zygmund inequality

(
Petrov, 2007
)
(an anticoncentration inequality) to provide a lower bound on the probability of
\hat{Z}_{N}
being small, specifically controlling its behavior when
\hat{Z}_{N}\leq 2
. For larger values of
\hat{Z}_{N}
, we divide the range into an intermediate section
(2,1+t)
and a large section
[1+t,\infty)
. The
Markov inequality
directly handles the large range. For the intermediate range, we employ a
peeling argument
, breaking it down into a union of smaller intervals and applying the Markov inequality to each. Our multiscale analysis, combining both concentration and anticoncentration inequalities, allows us to control the
t
-th moment of the rejection probability, leading to a total-variation bound via the coupling inequality.
In Section
5
we consider the bias removal technique of
Glynn & Rhee (2014)
applied by
Middleton et al. (2019)
to the particle IMH algorithm.
We provide conditions under which these unbiased estimators have finite moments,
and conditions under which their efficiency is asymptotically equivalent to that of self-normalized importance sampling. This distinguishes the bias removal technique of
Middleton et al. (2019)
from other debiasing strategies for IS
(
Blanchet et al., 2015
;
Blanchet & Glynn, 2015
;
Shi & Cornish, 2021
)
.
Section
6
presents numerical illustrations of these unbiased variants of importance sampling, for the estimation of the inverse of the normalizing constant of a distribution, for a nested expectation as in Section
1.1.3
, and in the context of robust mean estimation
as in Section
1.1.3
.

1.2
Importance sampling

Self-normalized importance sampling (IS) is described in Algorithm
1
, see also Chapter 9.2 in
Owen (2013)
.
Central
to IS is the weight function:

\omega:x\mapsto\frac{\pi(x)}{q(x)},\quad\text{so that}\quad q(\omega)=1.

(3)

Since multiplicative constants in
\omega
have no effect on the IS estimator (
5
),
it can be computed as long as the user can evaluate a function proportional to
\omega
in (
3
).
Unless specified otherwise, by IS we refer to the self-normalized version as in Algorithm
1

rather than the more basic estimator
N^{-1}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\omega(X_{n})f(X_{n})

that depends on the multiplicative constant in
\omega
.

1.

Sample
N
particles,
X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}
, independently from
q
.

2.

Compute the importance weights:
\omega(X_{n})=\pi(X_{n})/q(X_{n})
for
n\in[N]=\{1,\cdots,N\}
.

3.

Compute the normalizing constant estimator:

\hat{Z}_{N}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{N})=N^{-1}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\omega(X_{n}).

(4)

4.

For any test function
f
, compute the SNIS estimator

\hat{F}_{N}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{N})=\frac{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\omega(X_{n})f(X_{n})}{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\omega(X_{n})}.

(5)

\mathbf{X}^{N}
for
(X_{1},\ldots,X_{N})
, and
\hat{F}_{N}
for
\hat{F}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N})
– the IS estimator, and
\hat{Z}_{N}

\hat{Z}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N})

.

For any measurable set
A\in\mathcal{X}
, if
q(A)=0
, then
\pi(A)=0
. Furthermore,
\omega(X)
with

.

1.3
Independent Metropolis–Hastings

Independent Metropolis-Hastings (IMH) is an instance of the Metropolis–Rosenbluth–Teller–Hastings algorithm
(
Hastings, 1970
, Section 2.5)
, described in Algorithm
2
.
The IMH chain is aperiodic and
\pi
-invariant by design, whereas under Assumption
1
, it is also
\pi
-irreducible; thus
for
\pi
-almost every
x
,
|P^{t}(x,\cdot)-\pi|_{\mathrm{TV}}\to 0
as
t\to\infty

(
Roberts & Rosenthal, 2004

,
where
P
denotes the transition kernel of IMH,
P^{t}
denotes the
t

|\mu-\nu|_{\text{TV}}=\sup_{A\in\mathcal{X}}\mu(A)-\nu(A)
.

The asymptotic variance of the ergodic average
t^{-1}\sum_{s=0}^{t-1}f(X_{s})
generated by IMH, denoted by
\sigma^{2}_{\textsc{IMH}}
,
is finite if and only if
\pi(f^{2})<\infty
and
q(\omega^{2}\cdot f^{2})<\infty

(
Deligiannidis & Lee, 2018
, Theorem 2 in)
. Furthermore, if
\sigma^{2}_{\textsc{IMH}}
is finite then
Deligiannidis & Lee (2018, Proposition 2,)
provides a general comparison:

\sigma^{2}_{\textsc{IS}}\leq\sigma^{2}_{\textsc{IMH}},

(7)

with
\sigma^{2}_{\textsc{IS}}
as in (
6
). Thus IS outperforms IMH in asymptotic variance. Note that, since IMH defines a Markov transition, it can be used directly as a step within an encompassing Gibbs sampler
(
Skare et al., 2003
)
, and it is commonly used within sequential Monte Carlo samplers
(
Chopin, 2002
;
South et al., 2019
)
; thus IMH has its specific uses irrespective of the performance comparison with importance sampling.

.

1.4
Moment conditions on the weight

Our key assumption is the following.

Assumption 2
.

The weights have a finite
p
-th moment for
p>1
:
q(\omega^{p})<\infty
.

This is a weak assumption in the context of both self-normalized importance sampling and IMH, where the weight is often assumed to be bounded.
For bounded test functions, Assumption
2
with
p\geq 2

is necessary for the asymptotic variance of both self-normalized importance sampling and IMH to be finite.
The case where
p<2
is also of interest, and we consider it in the study of both SNIS
(Proposition
2.1
) and IMH (in Section
4.3
).

Example 1

(Exponential distributions)
.

Let
\pi
be the Exponential(1) distribution and let
q
be the Exponential(
k
) distribution with
q(x)=ke^{-kx}
, both on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
. If
k\leq 1
, the weight
\omega(x)
is upper bounded by
k^{-1}
, and Assumption
2
holds for all
p
. If
k>1
, then
q(\omega^{p})<\infty
holds
with any
p<k/(k-1)
. The example is considered in
Jarner & Roberts (2007)
;
Roberts & Rosenthal (2011)
;
Andrieu et al. (2022)
;
Orenstein (2022)
.

Example 2

(Normal distributions)
.

Let
\pi
be the Normal(0,1) distribution and let

be the Normal
(0,\sigma^{2})

\mathbb{R}
. If
\sigma^{2}\geq 1
, the weight
\omega(x)
is upper bounded by

, and Assumption
2
holds for all

. If
\sigma^{2}<1
, then

Remark 1.1
.

Some of our results do not require

2
Bias and moments of importance sampling

Our first result is a clean statement on the asymptotic bias of self-normalized importance sampling.
Introductory material on importance sampling often notices that the basic importance sampling estimator
N^{-1}\sum_{n=1}^{N}f(X_{n})\omega(X_{n})
is unbiased, but since
\omega
can only be evaluated up to a multiplicative constant, users may need to resort to the
self-normalized estimator
\hat{F}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N})
in (
5
), which is biased:
\mathbb{E}[\hat{F}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N})]\neq\pi(f)
.
The form of the asymptotic bias is well known, e.g. Section 2.5. in
Liu (2008)
.
Formal results with self-contained proofs are however hard to find.
Theorem 2 in
Skare et al. (2003)
emphasizes
on the pointwise relative error of the density of a particle selected from the IS approximation. Their Remark 1 translates this into
a bound on the bias for bounded functions under the assumption of bounded weights.
The asymptotic bias expression appears in
Hesterberg (1988)
, equation (2.63), and is justified by the Edgeworth expansion of the cumulative distribution function of
\sqrt{N}(\hat{F}_{N}-\pi(f))
, using results from
Bhattacharya & Ghosh (1978)
.
However, as noted in
Hesterberg (1988, p. 40)
, the expression is obtained as a first order term in an Edgeworth expansion which does not establish that the actual bias is well-defined or finite.
Moreover, the approach requires third moments of
\omega
and
\omega f
under
q
, as well as Cramér’s condition on the characteristic function of
(\omega,\omega^{2},\omega f,\omega^{2}f^{2})

(
Hesterberg, 1988
, p. 39)
.
Theorem
2.1
below requires fewer positive moments on
\omega
and
\omega f
, but
assumes the existence of an inverse moment for
\omega
. The proof in Appendix
A.2
is elementary.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
f
with
\pi(|f|)<\infty
.
Let
\mathbf{X}^{N}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}){\sim}\bar{q}
as in (
9
) and let
\hat{F}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N})
be as in (
5
).
Assume that
q(|\omega^{2}(f-\pi(f))|^{1+\epsilon})<\infty
for some
\epsilon>0
and
q(\omega^{-\eta})<\infty
for some
\eta>0
. Then

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}N\times\mathbb{E}_{\bar{q}}\left[\hat{F}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N})-\pi(f)\right]

\displaystyle=-\int\left(f(x)-\pi(f)\right)\omega^{2}(x)q(dx).

(16)

Theorem
2.1
relates to the question raised in Section
1.1.2
. Indeed,
consider a draw
X_{I}
obtained by Sampling-Importance Resampling (SIR).
Theorem
2.1
applied to bounded test functions shows that the total variation distance between
X_{I}
and
\pi
is of order
N^{-1}
as
N\to\infty
.
Instead of exact asymptotic order,

Agapiou et al. (2017, Theorem 2.1,)
provides an upper bound on the bias under weaker assumptions.

Agapiou et al. (2017, Theorem 2.3,)
provides upper bounds of order
N^{-1}
also for unbounded test functions,
under moment conditions on
f
and on
f\cdot\omega
. Our Theorem
2.1

establishes that
N^{-1}
is the exact order of the asymptotic bias as a function of
N
, but requires additional conditions.
Theorem
2.1
is similar to results proposed independently in
Daudel & Roueff (2024)
.
Finally, the bias of sequential Monte Carlo estimators is typically also of order
N^{-1}
, where
N
is the number of particles, see
Del Moral et al. (2007)
and
(
Del Moral, 2013
, Section 15.3,)
.

We next provide a result on the
s
-th moments of the error in importance sampling for unbounded test functions. Theorem
2.2
generalizes the MSE part of Theorem 2.3 in
Agapiou et al. (2017)
to arbitrary orders
s\geq 2
, and its assumptions are weaker in the case
s=2
, as discussed below. The proof is in Appendix
A.2
. These bounds are used to obtain the results of Section
5
, and may be of independent interest.

Theorem 2.2
.

Assume that
q(f^{2}\cdot\omega^{2})<\infty
and that there exist
p\in[2,\infty)
and
r\in[2,\infty]
such that
q(\omega^{p})<\infty
and
q(|f|^{r})<\infty
. Then for any
2\leq s\leq pr/(p+r+2)
and any
N\geq 1
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bar{q}}\left[\left\lvert\hat{F}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N})-\pi(f)\right\rvert^{s}\right]\leq CN^{-s/2},

where the constant
C
depends on
r,p,s,q(|f|^{r}),q(\omega^{p}),q(f^{2}\cdot\omega^{2})
.
When
r=\infty
, the statement holds for
f
such that
|f|_{\infty}<\infty
and all
s\leq p
.

A few remarks are in order:

•

The condition
s\leq pr/(p+r+2)
implies
s\leq\min\{p,r\}
.

•

We have
q((f\omega)^{pr/p+r})<\infty
if
q(\omega^{p})<\infty
and
q(f^{r})<\infty
. Indeed, when
r<\infty
,

q((f\omega)^{pr/p+r})\leq q(f^{r})^{p/p+r}q(\omega^{p})^{r/p+r}<\infty
.
When
r=\infty
, the claim remains correct (by understanding
pr/(p+r)
as
p
), since
q((f\omega)^{p})\leq|f|_{\infty}^{p}q(\omega^{p})
.
This observation leads to two facts: 1) If
pr/(p+r)\geq 2
(e.g.
p=r=4
or
p=2,r=\infty
), the assumption
q(f^{2}\cdot\omega^{2})<\infty
in Theorem
2.2
can be derived from the assumptions
q(\omega^{p})<\infty
and
q(f^{r})<\infty
. 2) The basic importance sampling estimator
N^{-1}\sum_{n=1}^{N}f(X_{n})\omega(X_{n})
has a finite
s
-th moment under the same conditions, as it has a finite
pr/(p+r)
-th moment, and
s\leq pr/(p+r+2)\leq pr/(p+r)
.

•

We may be particularly interested in the mean-squared error (MSE) of IS, corresponding to
s=2
.
Theorem
2.2
implies that the MSE is of order
1/N
as long as
2\leq pr/(p+r+2)
. This condition holds, for example, if
\min\{p,r\}\geq 2(1+\sqrt{2})\approx 4.828
, or if
p\geq 3
and
r\geq 10
, or if
p=2
and
r=\infty
.
The case
s=2
can be compared to the MSE part of Theorem 2.3 in
Agapiou et al. (2017)
.
In our notation, they require

q(|f\cdot\omega|^{2d})<\infty
,

q(\omega^{2e})<\infty
,

q(|f|^{2a})<\infty
,

q(\omega^{2b(1+a^{-1})})<\infty
, for
a,b,d,e>1
such that
a^{-1}+b^{-1}=1,d^{-1}+e^{-1}=1
.
Their assumption implies ours, as can be seen by setting
r=2a
and
p=2b(1+a^{-1})
, since then

\displaystyle\frac{pr}{(p+r+2)}=\frac{4b(a+1)}{2a+2b+2ba^{-1}+2}=\frac{2(a+1)}{a(1-a^{-1})+1+a^{-1}+(1-a^{-1})}=\frac{2(a+1)}{a+1},

i.e. our theorem holds with
s=2
under their assumptions.

•

Comparable results are given in
Shi & Cornish (2021)
. For bounded test functions
(
Shi & Cornish, 2021
, Supplementary material, Lemma 5)
, the two results are equivalent. For unbounded test functions
(
Shi & Cornish, 2021
, Supplementary material, Lemma 6)
, Theorem
2.2
yields a bound of
\mathcal{O}(N^{-s/2})
under strictly weaker conditions, whereas Lemma 6 of
Shi & Cornish (2021)
gives only a bound of
\mathcal{O}(N^{-s/2+s/r})
under
q(\omega^{p})<\infty
and
q(|f|^{r})<\infty
.

•

Similar types of results have been obtained for sequential Monte Carlo estimators, e.g. in
Del Moral (2013, Section 15.3)
, under strong conditions.

The following proposition extends Theorem
2.2
to the heavy-tailed regime
p\in(1,2)
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Assume that there exist
p\in(1,2)
and
r\in[1,\infty]
such that
q(\omega^{p})<\infty
,
q(|f|^{r})<\infty
, and such that
\alpha:=pr/(p+r)>1
. Then for any

and any

,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bar{q}}\left[\left\lvert\hat{F}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N})-\pi(f)\right\rvert^{s}\right]\leq C\,N^{-s(\alpha-1)/\alpha},

where the constant

depends on
r,p,s,q(|f|^{r}),q(\omega^{p})
. When

, the statement holds for

such that

and the rate

for all

.

The proof is given in Appendix

.

3
Optimality of coupling IMH with common draws

With a view toward deriving upper bounds on the total variation distance
of IMH to stationarity, we consider the common draws (or
common random numbers
) coupling of a generic IMH algorithm, described in Algorithm
4
and denoted by
\bar{P}
. In this section we denote a state variable by
X
instead of the notation
\mathbf{X}^{N}
employed
in Algorithm
3
,
to simplify the notation, given that the results apply to both IMH and PIMH and for any
N
. We also write
\hat{Z}(X)
instead of
\hat{Z}_{N}(X)
.
The coupling is very simple and was considered in
Liu (1996)
;
Roberts & Rosenthal (2011)
.
It was remarked around Lemma 1 in
Wang et al. (2021)
that this coupling is “one-step maximal”: the meeting probability
\bar{P}((x,y),D)
, with
D=\{(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}):x^{\prime}=y^{\prime}\}
, is maximal over all couplings of
P(x,\cdot)
and
P(y,\cdot)
, and hence equals
1-|P(x,\cdot)-P(y,\cdot)|_{\text{TV}}
. A direct computation gives the identity

\bar{P}((x,y),D)=1-|P(x,\cdot)-P(y,\cdot)|_{\text{TV}}=\int\min\left\{\frac{\hat{Z}(x^{\star})}{\hat{Z}(x)},\;\frac{\hat{Z}(x^{\star})}{\hat{Z}(y)},\;1\right\}\bar{q}(\mathrm{d}x^{\star}).

(17)

1.

Draw
X^{\star}\sim\bar{q}
.

2.

Draw
U
from a Uniform(0,1) distribution, independently of
X^{\star}
.

3.

If
U<{\hat{Z}(X^{\star})}/{\hat{Z}(X)}
, set
X^{\prime}=X^{\star}
,
otherwise set
X^{\prime}=X
.

4.

If
U<{\hat{Z}(X^{\star})}/{\hat{Z}(Y)}
, set
Y^{\prime}=X^{\star}
,
otherwise set
Y^{\prime}=Y
.

5.

Return
(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})

Algorithm 4

Common draws coupling of one step of IMH starting from states
X
and
Y
.

Let
(X_{t},Y_{t})
be a coupled chain started from
(x,y)
at time zero, and evolving according to
\bar{P}
.
Denoting the meeting time by

\tau=\inf\{t\geq 1:X_{t}=Y_{t}\},

(18)

the coupling inequality states that, for
t\geq 1
,

|P^{t}(x,\cdot)-P^{t}(y,\cdot)|_{\text{TV}}\leq\mathbb{P}_{x,y}(\tau>t),

(19)

where the probability
\mathbb{P}_{x,y}
is under the law of
(X_{t},Y_{t})
started from
(x,y)
at time zero.
We will relate the probability
\mathbb{P}_{x,y}(\tau>t)
to the rejection probabilities of IMH from
x
and
y
, and we define

r:x\mapsto\int_{x^{\star}\neq x}\left(1-\alpha(x,x^{\star})\right)\bar{q}(\mathrm{d}x^{\star}),

(20)

where
\alpha(x,x^{\star})
is defined in (
11
).

The meeting time
\tau
is the first time at which both chains accept the proposal simultaneously,
which corresponds to the first time at which
the chain with the highest weight accepts the proposal. Indeed, if
\hat{Z}(x)\geq\hat{Z}(y)
,
then
\alpha(x,x^{\star})\leq\alpha(y,x^{\star})
for all
x,y,x^{\star}
,
and thus
u<\alpha(x,x^{\star})
implies that
u<\alpha(y,x^{\star})
.
Thus, conditionally on
x_{0}=x,y_{0}=y
, the meeting time
\tau
follows
a Geometric distribution with parameter
1-r(x)
, where
r(x)
is defined in (
20
).
Recall that the survival function of a Geometric variable
T
with parameter
\gamma
is given by:

\mathbb{P}(T>t)=(1-\gamma)^{t}
for
t\in\mathbb{N}
.
Still assuming
\hat{Z}(x)\geq\hat{Z}(y)
, we obtain, for
t\geq 1
,

|P^{t}(x,\cdot)-P^{t}(y,\cdot)|_{\text{TV}}\leq\mathbb{P}_{x,y}(\tau>t)=(r(x))^{t}.

(21)

The above upper bound is given in
Roberts & Rosenthal (2011)
. In their remark following Theorem 5, they state that this is also a lower bound without providing a proof.
We do so below, for both discrete and continuous state spaces;
Roberts & Rosenthal (2011)
focus on non-atomic spaces. First, we express

|P^{t}(x,\cdot)-P^{t}(y,\cdot)|_{\text{TV}}=\sup_{A\in\mathcal{X}}|P^{t}(x,A)-P^{t}(y,A)|,

(22)

and we select the set
A=\mathbb{X}\setminus\{x\}
to obtain a lower bound, recalling that
(\mathbb{X},\mathcal{X})
refers to the measurable space on which the Markov chains are defined. Then
P^{t}(y,A)=1
since
x\neq y
, assuming that
q(\{x\})=0
, while
P^{t}(x,A)=1-\left(r(x)\right)^{t}
, i.e. the chain is in
A
at step
t
except if
t
proposals have been rejected. The situation
is slightly more complicated if the proposal has non-zero mass on
\{x\}
and
\{y\}
, e.g. in discrete state spaces, but the following result still holds. The proof is in Appendix
A.3
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
(X_{t},Y_{t})
be a Markov chain evolving according to
\bar{P}
in Algorithm
4
and starting from
X_{0}=x
and
Y_{0}=y
. Let
\tau=\inf\{t\geq 1:X_{t}=Y_{t}\}
, and let
r(x)
be defined as in (
20
).
Then, under Assumption
1
, for all
t\geq 1
,

\displaystyle|P^{t}(x,\cdot)-P^{t}(y,\cdot)|_{\mathrm{TV}}=\mathbb{P}_{x,y}(\tau>t)=\max(r(x),r(y))^{t}.

(23)

Thus, the chain
(X_{t},Y_{t})
generated by the common draws coupling follows a
maximal coupling
, as in
Pitman (1976)
: the coupling inequality is an equality at all times
t
.
To the best of our knowledge, this is the only known case of “all time maximal” couplings of an MCMC algorithm. This peculiarity could prove useful when considering the problem of numerically solving for the optimal coupling between Markov chains, as in the literature on bicausal optimal transport
(
Moulos, 2021
)
and bisimulation metrics
(
Calo et al., 2024
)
. The case of IMH provides a rare case of an explicit solution, which could serve as a benchmark
for algorithmic approaches to the problem of finding optimal couplings.
Note that the upper bound in (
23
) decreases geometrically in
t
; the polynomial rates that come later come from the integration with respect to
x
or
y
.

Remark 4.1
.

We comment on the sharpness of the dependency on

from a state
\mathbf{X}^{N}

\mathbf{X}^{N},\mathbf{X}^{N,\star}
being independent draws from

. Here we show that we cannot improve upon the rate

The central limit theorem implies

\mathbb{P}(\hat{Z}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N})\geq 1+N^{-1/2})\rightarrow p_{0}

occur simultaneously, and thus the acceptance probability is at most

. In turn this means that the rejection probability is at least
cN^{-1/2}
.

We also state a bound for a PIMH chain started at any initial point

Corollary 4.1
.

Under Assumptions

Theorem

Remark 4.3
.

The weight
\omega

4.5
Related results on IMH

The convergence of IMH has garnered significant interest over decades, and in particular the sub-geometric rates have been studied in several works including
Jarner & Roberts (2002)
;
Douc et al. (2007)
;
Roberts & Rosenthal (2011)
;
Andrieu et al. (2022)
.
One approach utilizes drift and minorization techniques
(
Jarner & Roberts, 2002
)
.

Theorem 4.3

(Theorem 5.3 in
Jarner & Roberts (2002)
)
.

Let
P
be the transition kernel of the IMH chain as in Algorithm
2
. Assume that for some
r>0
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\pi(A_{\epsilon})=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{1/r})\quad\text{for}\quad\epsilon\to 0,

(28)

where
A_{\epsilon}=\left\{x\in\mathbb{X}:\omega(x)>1/\epsilon\right\}
, for any
\epsilon>0
. Then, for any
x\in\mathbb{X}
, and any

, we have that

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}(1+t)^{\beta}|P^{t}(x,\cdot)-\pi|_{\mathrm{TV}}=0,

for any

, with

.

The
\mathcal{O}
notation here is such that if

then there exists a constant

such that
|f(x)|\leq M|g(x)|
for all

in the domain of

.
Theorem
4.3
provides a polynomial rate of convergence for the IMH chain in total variation of order
o\left(t^{-1/r+\kappa}\right)
for any

under the assumption that the tail weights satisfy the condition specified in equation (

).
Notably, under the assumption
q(\omega^{p})<\infty
, the condition in (

) is satisfied for

, using Markov’s inequality.
Our Corollary

differs slightly as our bounds are in

instead of

for some arbitrarily small

.
Similar results can be obtained using weak Poincaré inequalities
as described in Remark 29 of

with

which amounts to our Assumption

with

.

Our bounds in Theorem

and Corollary

4.6
Bias comparison and practical recommendations

Results in Sections
2
and
4
enable a comparison of the bias of IS and IMH under Assumptions
1
-
2
, addressing the question raised
in Section
1.1.2
.
Performing
N
steps of IMH to
obtain a sample
X_{N}
, and considering a test function
f
bounded by one,
Corollary
4.3

shows that the bias of
f(X_{N})
is at most
C\,N^{-(p-1)}
.
In terms of variance, however, it is preferable to average over successive states
of the chain. We recommend setting a burn-in period
T=\lceil cN^{\eta}\rceil
with

\eta\in(0,1]
and
c>0
, and averaging over
X_{T+1},\dots,X_{N}
. We can then bound the bias of the average by

\frac{1}{N-T}\sum_{t=T+1}^{N}\left|qP^{t}f-\pi(f)\right|\leq\frac{C}{N-T}\sum_{t=T+1}^{\infty}t^{-(p-1)}=\mathcal{O}\left(N^{-1-\eta(p-2)}\right),

using
\sum_{t>T}t^{-(p-1)}=\mathcal{O}(T^{-(p-2)})
as in (
56
) in the proof of
Theorem
4.1
and
N-T\sim N
.
For
\eta<1
the burn-in phase is negligible in the limit,
and thus the ergodic average has the same asymptotic variance as
if no burn-in were used.
A value
\eta=1
makes the bias decrease to
N^{-(p-1)}
, but the
asymptotic variance is inflated by
1/(1-c)
.

On the other hand, Theorem
2.1
shows that the bias of the SNIS estimator with
N
samples is of the order of
N^{-1}
, under an additional inverse weight assumption. Therefore, as soon as
q(\omega^{p})<\infty
with
p>2
, the bias of IMH
becomes smaller than that of SNIS for a similar budget of weight function evaluations. Our results also show that, in terms of bias and for a fixed total budget, the best choice for the number of proposals per iteration in PIMH is one.
If the weights are bounded, MCMC methods such as PIMH or particle Gibbs
(
Andrieu et al., 2010
)
are geometrically ergodic
(
Wang, 2022
;
Lee et al., 2020
;
Cardoso et al., 2022
, e.g.)
, and thus the bias decays geometrically. The bias comparison is then clearly in favor of MCMC algorithms.

We therefore recommend considering IMH as a potential alternative to SNIS when low-bias estimators are desired, see e.g., Section
1.1.3
for examples of such settings such as gradient estimation or nested expectations.
Importantly,
the user typically does not know the largest value of
p
such that
q(\omega^{p})<\infty
, and thus the exact rate at which the bias of IMH decreases is unknown. This can be problematic: for example, for nested expectations,
optimizing budget allocation requires knowledge of the bias of the inner expectation estimator
(
Andradóttir & Glynn, 2016
)
. To address this, one approach would be to estimate the largest value of
p
such that
q(\omega^{p})<\infty
. This is a tail estimation problem, which has been considered in the context of importance sampling in
Monahan (1993, e.g. )
;
Koopman et al. (2009, e.g. )
;
Vehtari et al. (2024, e.g. )
.
In Section
5
, we pursue
a different approach, which is to remove the bias, without having to estimate the tail behavior of the weight. We will consider a bias removal technique, described in Algorithm
6
, that is applicable whenever SNIS is,
and incurs a moderate and controlled increase in variance.

For completeness, we recall that various bias and variance reduction techniques are available for both IS and IMH. Bias reduction
for IS include the leave-one-out modification proposed in
Skare et al. (2003)
,
or the jackknife
(
Shao & Tu, 2012
;
Nowozin, 2018
)
, both of which can reduce the bias to
N^{-2}
; we implement the former in Section
6.2
. Variance reduction for IS, beyond optimizing the choice of proposal distribution, include
(
Owen, 2025
;
Henmi et al., 2007
)
, and variance reduction for IMH includes Rao-Blackwellization of the acceptance step
(
Casella & Robert, 1996
)
,
averaging over different orderings of the proposed samples
(
Atchadé & Perron, 2005
;
Jacob et al., 2011
)
,
and, depending on the tractability of the proposal distribution, constructing control variates as in
Neal & Pinto (2001)
;
Alexopoulos et al. (2023)
.

5
Bias removal for self-normalized importance sampling

5.1
Motivation

We consider techniques to remove the bias of IS entirely. There exist at least three distinct methods to achieve this. We focus on the approach of
Middleton et al. (2019)
, that follows the pioneering work of
Glynn & Rhee (2014)
, and relies on couplings of PIMH chains as in Algorithm
5
. We refer to this approach as Coupling-based Unbiased Importance Sampling (Coupling-UIS).
Another approach is based on randomly-truncated multilevel Monte Carlo (MLMC)

(
Blanchet & Glynn, 2015
;
Shi & Cornish, 2021
)
. We refer to it as MLMC-UIS.
Finally, any approach that provides unbiased estimators of
1/q(\omega)
can be used to remove the bias of IS, since multiplying an unbiased estimator of
1/q(\omega)
by an independent unbiased estimator of
q(\omega f)
yields an unbiased estimator of
\pi(f)
. An approach using a Taylor expansion of the inverse function was
proposed in
Blanchet et al. (2015)
;
Chopin et al. (2025)
. We refer to that approach as Taylor-UIS. Both MLMC-UIS and Taylor-UIS are described in Appendix
B
. Conversely, using the test function
f:x\mapsto\omega^{-1}(x)
,
the Coupling-UIS estimator of
\pi(f)
provides an unbiased estimator of
1/q(\omega)
; see Section
5.6
and Section
6
.

We propose conditions under which Coupling-UIS has finite moments,
we propose a Rao–Blackwellization of the scheme for increased efficiency,
and we provide conditions under which its asymptotic efficiency matches that of IS as
N\to\infty
(see Theorem
5.6
).
The two other approaches, MLMC-UIS and Taylor-UIS, do not seem to match this asymptotic efficiency, as we observe in Section
6.1
.

5.2
Construction

For bias removal,
Middleton et al. (2019)
employ common random numbers couplings of PIMH within the approach of
Glynn & Rhee (2014)
.
Upon running Algorithm
5
with
N\geq 1
, with
\tau=\inf\{t\geq 1:\mathbf{X}^{N}_{t}=\mathbf{Y}^{N}_{t-1}\}
, one can compute the following unbiased estimator of
\pi(f)
:

\hat{F}_{N}^{u}=\hat{F}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N}_{0})+\sum_{t=1}^{\tau-1}\{\hat{F}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N}_{t})-\hat{F}_{N}(\mathbf{Y}^{N}_{t-1})\},

(29)

where
\hat{F}_{N}
is as in (
5
).
By convention the sum in (
29
) is zero in the event
\{\tau=1\}
, and it is also equal to the infinite sum
\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\{\hat{F}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N}_{t})-\hat{F}_{N}(\mathbf{Y}^{N}_{t-1})\}
since
\hat{F}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N}_{t})=\hat{F}_{N}(\mathbf{Y}^{N}_{t-1})
from time
\tau
onward.
The lack of bias can be seen via a telescopic sum argument, since
\mathbf{X}^{N}_{t}
and
\mathbf{Y}^{N}_{t}
have the same marginal distribution for all
t
, provided that limit and expectation can be swapped, and using (
12
).
Since
\mathbf{X}^{N}_{0}\sim\bar{q}
,
\hat{F}(\mathbf{X}^{N}_{0})
is the (biased) SNIS estimator,
so that the sum in (
29
) can be seen as a bias cancellation term, under some conditions.

Middleton et al. (2019)
consider the case where
\omega
is uniformly upper bounded, and show that (
29
) can have a finite variance. Below we work under much weaker assumptions and bound the moments of these unbiased estimators,
highlighting the effect of the tuning parameter
N
, and comparing its efficiency with that of SNIS.

Remark 5.1
.

The estimator
\hat{F}_{N}^{u}
in (
29
) is an unbiased MCMC estimator whose efficiency is usually compared to that of the underlying MCMC algorithm
(
Jacob et al., 2020
;
Atchadé & Jacob, 2026
)
, i.e. PIMH in the present case. However, it can also be compared to that of SNIS as
N\to\infty
,
which is more compelling, as the asymptotic variance of SNIS is smaller than that of IMH (
7
).

5.3
Moments of Coupling-UIS

We subtract

\hat{F}_{N}^{u}-\pi(f)=\hat{F}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N}_{0})-\pi(f)+\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\{\hat{F}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N}_{t})-\hat{F}_{N}(\mathbf{Y}^{N}_{t-1})\}\mathds{1}(\tau>t).

(30)

We introduce the notation

\displaystyle\Delta_{t}

\displaystyle=\hat{F}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N}_{t})-\hat{F}_{N}(\mathbf{Y}^{N}_{t-1}),\quad\text{BC}=\sum_{t=1}^{\infty}\Delta_{t}\mathds{1}(\tau>t),

,
and the moments of BC.

A first result is that, for bounded test functions

(non-included) under Assumption

5.4
The asymptotic price of bias removal

We consider the price of debiasing self-normalized importance sampling using Coupling-UIS in terms of inefficiency,
defined by the mean squared error multiplied by the average cost
(
Glynn & Whitt, 1992
, see e.g.)
. We start by comparing the mean squared errors
of unbiased and regular IS.
From (
30
), squaring and using Cauchy–Schwarz, we obtain

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\left[(\hat{F}_{N}^{u}-\pi(f))^{2}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[(\hat{F}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N}_{0})-\pi(f))^{2}\right]\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[(\hat{F}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N}_{0})-\pi(f))^{2}\right]\cdot\mathbb{E}[\text{BC}^{2}]}+\mathbb{E}[\text{BC}^{2}],

(32)

with
\text{BC}=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau-1}\{\hat{F}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N}_{t})-\hat{F}_{N}(\mathbf{Y}^{N}_{t-1})\}
.

The mean squared error (MSE) of IS, which is the term
\mathbb{E}[(\hat{F}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N}_{0})-\pi(f))^{2}]
, is of order
N^{-1}
under conditions stated in Theorem
2.2
.
If we can bound
\mathbb{E}[\text{BC}^{2}]
by a term that decreases faster than
N^{-1}
, then the MSE of Coupling-UIS would be asymptotically equivalent to that of IS.
Intuitively, the bias cancellation term BC in (
31
) goes to zero for two reasons: first because
\tau
goes to one as
N\to\infty
, since two independent draws of
\hat{Z}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N})
are more likely to take similar values as
N
increases.
Furthermore, each term
\Delta_{t}=\hat{F}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N}_{t})-\hat{F}_{N}(\mathbf{Y}^{N}_{t-1})
goes to
0
as
N\to\infty
, as described in Proposition
5.2
.
We obtain the following result, proven in Appendix
A.5.4
.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
\hat{F}_{N}^{u}
be the Coupling-UIS estimator (
29
) and
\hat{F}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N})
with
\mathbf{X}^{N}\sim\bar{q}
be the IS estimator. Suppose that the assumptions of Proposition
5.3
are satisfied with
s=2
, that is:
p>2
and
r>2
such that
q(\omega^{p})<\infty
,
q(|f|^{r})<\infty
, and
q(f^{2}\cdot\omega^{2})<\infty
, with
2p+4r+4<r\cdot p
.
Then the mean squared errors of
\hat{F}_{N}^{u}
and
\hat{F}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N})
are asymptotically equivalent:

\lim_{N\to\infty}N\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[(\hat{F}_{N}^{u}-\pi(f))^{2}\right]=\lim_{N\to\infty}N\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\bar{q}}\left[(\hat{F}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N})-\pi(f))^{2}\right].

The assumption
2p+4r+4<r\cdot p
is for example satisfied if
r=\infty
and
p=4+\epsilon
with an arbitrary
\epsilon>0
,
or if
p=5
and
r=15
. However it cannot be satisfied with
p\leq 4
.

The cost of Coupling-UIS is that of running Algorithm
5
. It starts with two draws from
\bar{q}
,
i.e.
2N
draws from
q
, and as many evaluations of the weight function
\omega
. Then either
\tau=1
or the algorithm enters its while loop up to the meeting time
\tau
, drawing
N
new particles at each iterate of the loop. Counting the cost in units of number of evaluations of
\pi
,
Coupling-UIS has an overall cost of
\mathcal{C}=2N+N(\tau-1)
.
If Assumption
2
holds with
p\geq 2
, using Proposition
4.3
then
\mathbb{E}[\tau-1]\leq CN^{-1/2}
for a finite constant
C
.
Thus, as
N\to\infty
,
\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{C}]
is equivalent to
2N
. We can then compare the asymptotic inefficiencies of Coupling-UIS and IS as in the next statement.

Proposition 5.5
.

Denote the cost of the Coupling-UIS estimator
\hat{F}_{N}^{u}
in (
29
) by
\mathcal{C}=2N+N(\tau-1)
. Under the conditions of Proposition
5.4
, the inefficiency, defined as expected cost multiplied by mean squared error, of Coupling-UIS is twice that of IS as
N\to\infty
.

The reason for the efficiency loss in Coupling-UIS is that, in Algorithm
5
the
N
particles in
\mathbf{Y}^{N}_{0}

are required to determine
\tau
but in the event
\{\tau=1\}
, which is increasingly likely as
N\to\infty
, these
N
particles do not participate directly in the estimator
\hat{F}_{N}^{u}
.

5.5
An improved coupling-based unbiased importance sampling estimator

A simple trick provides a remedy, and cuts the asymptotic inefficiency by a half. We can view
\hat{F}_{N}^{u}
as a deterministic function of
initial states
\mathbf{X}^{N}_{0}
and
\mathbf{Y}^{N}_{0}
drawn from
\bar{q}
independently,
as well as additional variables: an arbitrary long sequence of proposals from
\bar{q}
, and a sequence of uniform random variables used to accept or reject these proposals. Denote these additional variables by
\zeta
. Then
\hat{F}_{N}^{u}
can be
written as
\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{X}^{N}_{0},\mathbf{Y}^{N}_{0},\zeta)
, where
\mathcal{A}
is a deterministic function, described by Algorithm
5
and (
29
).
Then we define the estimator
that averages over swapping
\mathbf{X}^{N}_{0}
and
\mathbf{Y}^{N}_{0}
:

\tilde{F}_{N}^{u}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{X}^{N}_{0},\mathbf{Y}^{N}_{0},\zeta)+\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{Y}^{N}_{0},\mathbf{X}^{N}_{0},\zeta)\right).

(33)

Computing (
33
) only requires simple modifications of Algorithm
5
.
Indeed, either
\hat{Z}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N}_{0})\geq\hat{Z}_{N}(\mathbf{Y}^{N}_{0})
or
\hat{Z}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N}_{0})<\hat{Z}_{N}(\mathbf{Y}^{N}_{0})
.
In the first case, we always have
\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{Y}^{N}_{0},\mathbf{X}^{N}_{0},\zeta)=\hat{F}_{N}(\mathbf{Y}^{N}_{0})
, and
\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{X}^{N}_{0},\mathbf{Y}^{N}_{0},\zeta)
can be computed
following Algorithm
5
and (
29
).
In the second case, we always have
\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{X}^{N}_{0},\mathbf{Y}^{N}_{0},\zeta)=\hat{F}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N}_{0})
,
and
\mathcal{A}(\mathbf{Y}^{N}_{0},\mathbf{X}^{N}_{0},\zeta)
can be computed following Algorithm
5
and (
29
) with the role of
\mathbf{X}^{N}_{0}
and
\mathbf{Y}^{N}_{0}
swapped.
The estimator (
33
) amounts to a
Rao–Blackwellized
version of
\hat{F}_{N}^{u}
in (
29
), averaging over the arbitrary specification of which of the two draws from
\bar{q}
is used as
\mathbf{X}^{N}_{0}
or as
\mathbf{Y}^{N}_{0}
.
The following statement is a mild variation of the previous results and is stated without a proof.

Proposition 5.6
.

Consider the Coupling-based UIS estimator
\tilde{F}_{N}^{u}
in (
33
), with cost
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
.
Suppose that the conditions of Proposition

are satisfied.
Then
\tilde{F}_{N}^{u}
is an unbiased estimator of
\pi(f)
for any

, with finite expected cost and
finite variance, and its inefficiency is equivalent to that of IS as
N\to\infty
.

The estimator
\tilde{F}_{N}^{u}
should be preferred to
\hat{F}_{N}^{u}
, as it is asymptotically twice as efficient,
but note that its cost is always larger.
Algorithm
6
provides pseudo-code for the Coupling-UIS estimator, which can be implemented under the same conditions as Algorithm
1
(SNIS) or Algorithm
3
(PIMH).
We recall that
\hat{Z}_{N}
is defined in (
4
),
\hat{F}_{N}
in (
5
),
and

\bar{q}
in (
9
).
The only tuning parameter is
N
, which affects
the cost per estimate and its variance.
The pseudo-code also implements a Rao–Blackwellization argument
(
Casella & Robert, 1996
)

that replaces each
\hat{F}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N}_{t})
by

\displaystyle\left(1-\alpha(\mathbf{X}^{N}_{t-1},\mathbf{X}^{N,\star})\right)\hat{F}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N}_{t-1})+\alpha(\mathbf{X}^{N}_{t-1},\mathbf{X}^{N,\star})\hat{F}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N,\star}),

where
\alpha(\mathbf{X}^{N}_{t-1},\mathbf{X}^{N,\star})=\min(1,{\hat{Z}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N,\star})}/{\hat{Z}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N}_{t-1})})
is the acceptance probability of the PIMH step, as in (

Algorithm 6

Coupling-based unbiased importance sampling (Coupling-UIS).

1.

Sample
\mathbf{X}^{N}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{N})\sim\bar{q}

and
\mathbf{Y}^{N}=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{N})\sim\bar{q}
independently.

2.

If
\hat{Z}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N})<\hat{Z}_{N}(\mathbf{Y}^{N})
, swap
\mathbf{X}^{N}
and
\mathbf{Y}^{N}
, so that

\hat{Z}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N})\geq\hat{Z}_{N}(\mathbf{Y}^{N})
henceforth.

3.

Set
\tau\leftarrow 1
.
Initialize

\tilde{F}_{N}^{u}\leftarrow\frac{1}{2}\hat{F}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N})+\frac{1}{2}\hat{F}_{N}(\mathbf{Y}^{N}).

4.

Update estimate using Rao–Blackwellization over the acceptance step:

\tilde{F}_{N}^{u}\leftarrow\tilde{F}_{N}^{u}+\frac{1}{2}\left(1-\alpha(\mathbf{X}^{N},\mathbf{Y}^{N})\right)\left(\hat{F}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N})-\hat{F}_{N}(\mathbf{Y}^{N})\right).

Draw
U\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)
. If
U\leq\hat{Z}_{N}(\mathbf{Y}^{N})/\hat{Z}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N})
, go to step 6.

5.

Iterate until both conditions in e) and f) are satisfied:

(a)

\tau\leftarrow\tau+1
.

(b)

Sample
\mathbf{X}^{N,\star}=(X^{\star}_{1},\ldots,X^{\star}_{N})\sim\bar{q}
.

(c)

Update estimate using Rao–Blackwellization over the acceptance step:

\displaystyle\tilde{F}_{N}^{u}\leftarrow\tilde{F}_{N}^{u}+\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle\left((1-\alpha(\mathbf{X}^{N},\mathbf{X}^{N,\star}))\hat{F}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N})-(1-\alpha(\mathbf{Y}^{N},\mathbf{X}^{N,\star}))\hat{F}_{N}(\mathbf{Y}^{N})\right.

\displaystyle+\left.(\alpha(\mathbf{X}^{N},\mathbf{X}^{N,\star})-\alpha(\mathbf{Y}^{N},\mathbf{X}^{N,\star}))\hat{F}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N,\star})\right).

(d)

Draw
U\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)
.

(e)

If
U\leq\hat{Z}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N,\star})/\hat{Z}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N})
,
set
\mathbf{X}^{N}\leftarrow\mathbf{X}^{N,\star}
.

(f)

If
U\leq\hat{Z}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N,\star})/\hat{Z}_{N}(\mathbf{Y}^{N})
,
set
\mathbf{Y}^{N}\leftarrow\mathbf{X}^{N,\star}
.

6.

Set

\mathrm{cost}\leftarrow N(\tau+1)
.

7.

Return
\tilde{F}_{N}^{u}
.

5.6
Unbiased estimation of the inverse of the normalizing constant

A particular case is the unbiased estimation of
1/Z
, where
Z=q(\omega_{u})
for an unnormalized weight function
\omega_{u}
proportional to
\omega
. This problem was part of the motivation for
Wang & Wang (2023)
;
Chopin et al. (2025)
, where such unbiased estimators are used for inference in doubly-intractable models.
Coupling-UIS can be directly used for this purpose by setting the test function as
f=1/\omega_{u}
. In this case, IS gives
\hat{F}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N})=1/\hat{Z}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N})
, which is biased. For Coupling-UIS, our previous results apply
with the choice of test function
f=1/\omega_{u}
, but we obtain weaker conditions by leveraging, for example, the identity
\omega_{u}\cdot f=1
. Thus, we rederive Propositions
5.1
,

5.3
and
5.6
,
for that test function in Proposition
5.7
, proven in Appendix

.

Proposition 5.7

(Properties of Coupling-UIS for
1/Z
)
.

Denote by
\hat{I}_{N}
the Coupling-UIS estimator (
29
) with
f=1/\omega_{u}
.
Assume that
q(\omega^{p})<\infty
for some
p\geq 2
and
q(\omega^{-\eta})<\infty
for some
\eta>0
.
Then for any
1\leq s<p
and
N>sp/((p-s)\eta)
,
\hat{I}_{N}
has
s
finite moments:

\mathbb{E}\left[|\hat{I}_{N}-Z^{-1}|^{s}\right]<\infty.

(34)

If furthermore
p>2s

N\geq sp/((p-s)\eta)
there exists
C>0
such that

\mathbb{E}\left[|\hat{I}_{N}-Z^{-1}|^{s}\right]\leq C\,N^{-s/2}.

(35)

For
s=2
:
\hat{I}_{N}
has finite variance for

and
N>2p/((p-2)\eta)
; if

\lim_{N\to\infty}N\cdot\mathbb{E}\left[(\hat{I}_{N}-Z^{-1})^{2}\right]=\lim_{N\to\infty}N\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\bar{q}}\left[\left(\hat{Z}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N})^{-1}-Z^{-1}\right)^{\!2}\right].

The distinctive feature of Coupling-UIS
is the last item of the proposition, i.e. the asymptotic equivalence of the MSE of

and that of the SNIS estimator

Remark 5.3
.

The general Coupling-UIS moment bound (Proposition
5.3
) specialized to
f=1/\omega_{u}
requires
q(\omega^{-r})<\infty
for some

together with the compatibility condition
pr/(p+r+2)>ps/(p-s)
. Proposition
5.7
replaces this by
q(\omega^{-\eta})<\infty
for any
\eta>0
and
p>2s
, with no compatibility condition between
\eta
and

.
When
s=2
, the general compatibility condition reduces to
2p+4r+4<rp
, i.e.

, which forces
p>4
. Both results thus require
p>4

\omega^{-1}

q(\omega^{-r})<\infty
with
r>(2p+4)/(p-4)
, which is very restrictive for moderate

: for instance,
p=5
requires
r>14
,
p=6
requires
r>8
. Proposition
5.7
, on the other hand, requires only
p>4
and
q(\omega^{-\eta})<\infty
for any
\eta>0
.

6.2
Nested expectations

We consider a nested expectation problem, as defined in Subsection

. The setting is that of Bayesian inference after multiple imputation of missing data
(
Zhou & Reiter, 2010
, e.g.)
.
We consider a logistic regression
where the covariates
X
are continuous and contain missing entries. We generate the data by sampling
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
independently from a Normal
(0,1)
distribution,
with
n=100
and
p=10
. The response
Y
is generated given
X
from a logistic regression with parameters
\beta^{\star}
defined as
\beta^{\star}_{i}=i/10
for
i=1,\ldots,5
, and
\beta^{\star}_{i}=0
for
i=6,\ldots,10
. We obtain
\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}=52
. We then remove some entries from
X
, with
X_{ij}
becoming missing with probability
0.4
if
Y_{i}=1
and
0.2
if
Y_{i}=0
, independently across
i
and
j
.
As a result,
285
entries are missing out of
1000
. We use the correct Normal
(0,1)
distribution as the imputation model for each missing entry.

For each completed data set, we approximate the posterior distribution in the logistic regression model, using the Normal
(0,10\cdot I_{p})

as prior on the coefficients. We use importance sampling using as proposal
a multivariate Student distribution with

, the variant of
Skare et al. (2003)
, referred to as SNIS-LOO,

N-1

N^{-2}
,
and finally the Coupling-UIS estimator from Section
5.5
, with no bias.
We consider the test function that maps the parameter to its first component,
so that we are estimating the posterior mean of the first coefficient,
averaged over the distribution of the imputed data. For a total budget
C
,
we need to allocate the budget between the number of imputed data sets
M
and the number of samples
N
per estimator for the inner expectation, so that
C=MN
.
We follow the optimal allocation from
Andradóttir & Glynn (2016)

for SNIS we use
M=C^{2/3}
and
N=C^{1/3}
. For SNIS-LOO, we use
M=C^{4/5}
and
N=C^{1/5}
. For Coupling-UIS, we use a fixed
N=5
, and
M=C/5
. In the latter case the cost is random and we report its average.

6.3
Robust mean estimation

A motivation for eliminating the bias is that
one can then employ any statistical technique that requires unbiased inputs,
without requiring additional assumptions or modifications.
An example is robust mean estimation
(
Lugosi & Mendelson, 2019
)
.
The goal is to estimate the mean
\mu
of a random variable with finite variance
\sigma^{2}
from
n
i.i.d. copies
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
: for a confidence level
\delta\in(0,1)
, we seek
\hat{\mu}_{n}=\hat{\mu}_{n}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n},\delta)
such that
the inequality

\mathbb{P}(|\hat{\mu}_{n}-\mu|>\epsilon)\leq\delta

(36)

holds for the smallest possible
\epsilon=\epsilon(n,\delta)
. The empirical average satisfies (
36
) asymptotically with
\epsilon=\sigma\sqrt{2\log(2/\delta)/n}
via the CLT. On the other hand, Chebyshev’s inequality only yields
\epsilon=\sigma\sqrt{1/(n\delta)}
, with a
\delta
-dependence that cannot be improved under finite variance alone
(
Lugosi & Mendelson, 2019
, Section 2)
. Remarkably, implementable alternatives recover the sub-Gaussian rate.
The Median-of-Means (MoM,
Nemirovskij & Yudin (1983)
) estimator provides a prime example, where the
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
are
partitioned into
K
blocks of size
m
with
n=mK
, and the estimator is obtained as the median of the blockwise means. The following result
shows that the MoM estimator achieves sub-Gaussian performance.

Theorem 6.1

(Theorem
2
in
Lugosi & Mendelson (2019)
)
.

Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be i.i.d. random variables with mean
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
and variance
\sigma^{2}\in(0,\infty)
. Let
\delta\in(0,1)
and

K=\lceil 8\log(1/\delta)\rceil
be a number of blocks, and assume that
n=mK
for some positive integer
m
.
Denote by
\hat{\mu}_{\textsc{MoM},n}
the MoM estimator computed with
K
blocks of size
m
. Then, with probability at most
\delta
,
for all
n\geq\lceil 8\log(1/\delta)\rceil
,

|\hat{\mu}_{\textsc{MoM},n}-\mu|>\sigma\sqrt{\frac{32\log(1/\delta)}{n}}.

The combination of MoM with self-normalized importance sampling (SNIS) was explored in
Dau (2022)
. The proposed estimator, termed MoM-SNIS, is the median of
K
SNIS estimators obtained with
M
draws each, so that the cost is equivalent to SNIS with
N=M\times K
draws. The above result on MoM does not directly apply because SNIS is biased for the quantity of interest.
Dau (2022, Proposition 2,)
shows that
MoM-SNIS achieves sub-Gaussian performance under minimal assumptions on the weight and test function, but only once
N
exceeds a problem-dependent threshold involving the variance of the weights.
Furthermore, as shown in
Dau (2022, Proposition 3)
, it is not possible to obtain a sub-Gaussian concentration inequality for MoM-SNIS that would hold for all
N
larger than a threshold that would depend on
\delta
only.

In contrast, since Coupling-UIS estimators are unbiased,
we can directly use MoM with them, and obtain sub-Gaussian performance for all
n
larger than
\lceil 8\log(1/\delta)\rceil
as in Theorem
6.1
,
where
n
is a number of independent copies of Coupling-UIS.
We could also use any other robust mean estimator, such as those proposed in
Minsker & Ndaoud (2021)
or
Lee & Valiant (2022)
. Drawbacks relative to MoM-SNIS
(
Dau, 2022
)
include the higher variance of Coupling-UIS relative to SNIS for matching costs, and the random nature of the computing cost.

We revisit Example
1
, with
p=2.1
, and the test function
f(x)=x
,
so that
\pi(f)=1
.
We compare the distribution of the errors of self-normalized importance sampling (SNIS)
with
N=4000
, with the MoM-SNIS method of
Dau (2022)
, tuned with
\delta=0.05
, leading to
K=\lceil 8\log(1/\delta)\rceil=24
and
M=192
so that
K\times M=4608
is comparable to
N
.
For MoM-Coupling-UIS, we use
24
samples for each unbiased estimator,
and then we compute the median of
K=24
averages, each over
M=3

unbiased estimators per block. This leads to an average cost of
3856

target density evaluations per MoM-Coupling-UIS estimator. We generate
10^{6}
independent runs of SNIS with
4000
samples,
and then for MoM, of SNIS with
192
samples and Coupling-UIS with
24
samples. This allows us to collect 41,660 MoM-SNIS and 13,880 MoM-Coupling-UIS estimates.

Figure
3
displays the quantiles of the absolute value of the difference between the estimates and
\pi(f)
,
for SNIS, MoM-SNIS, and MoM-Coupling-UIS. The median absolute error is smaller for SNIS than for the robust estimates. However, for quantile of very high order,
the error of SNIS increases faster than the error of the robust methods. From the experiments we observe a small advantage of MoM-SNIS over MoM-Coupling-UIS at comparable costs, so the appeal of the latter appears mostly theoretical.

Write

Lemma A.5
.

Under Assumption
1
and
q(\omega^{p})<\infty
for any
p>1
, the rejection probability (
20

\theta\in[0,1]
and any

\displaystyle r(\mathbf{x}^{N})\leq 1-\min\left\{1,\frac{\theta}{\hat{Z}_{N}(\mathbf{x}^{N})}\right\}c_{p}(\theta),\quad\text{with}\quad c_{p}(\theta)=\frac{(1-\theta)^{p/(p-1)}}{q(\omega^{p})^{1/(p-1)}}\in[0,1].

Proof.

Let

. We start with a

-version of the Paley-Zygmund inequality, as on page 2705, equation (12) of
Petrov (2007)
with
r=1

If
W

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(W>\theta\mathbb{E}[W]\right)\geq\frac{(1-\theta)^{p/(p-1)}\left(\mathbb{E}[W]\right)^{p/(p-1)}}{\left(\mathbb{E}[W^{p}]\right)^{1/(p-1)}}.

(53)

Indeed, for any

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[W]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[W\mathds{1}(W>b)]+\mathbb{E}[W\mathds{1}(W\leq b)]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(W>b)^{(1-1/p)}\mathbb{E}[W^{p}]^{1/p}+b.

Rearranging with

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\bar{q}}\left(\hat{Z}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N})>\theta\right)\geq\frac{(1-\theta)^{p/(p-1)}}{\left(\mathbb{E}_{\bar{q}}\left[\left(\hat{Z}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N})\right)^{p}\right]\right)^{1/(p-1)}}\geq\frac{(1-\theta)^{p/(p-1)}}{q(\omega^{p})^{1/(p-1)}}.

The latter inequality comes from Jensen’s, since

\mathbb{E}_{\bar{q}}\left[\left(\hat{Z}_{N}(\mathbf{X}^{N})\right)^{p}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}_{\bar{q}}\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\omega(X_{n})^{p}\right]=q(\omega^{p}).

\displaystyle\displaystyle\int\min\left\{1,\frac{\hat{Z}_{N}(\mathbf{x}^{N,\star})}{\hat{Z}_{N}(\mathbf{x}^{N})}\right\}\bar{q}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}^{N,\star})

\displaystyle=\int_{\{\mathbf{x}^{N,\star}:\hat{Z}_{N}(\mathbf{x}^{N,\star})\leq\theta\}}\min\left\{1,\frac{\hat{Z}_{N}(\mathbf{x}^{N,\star})}{\hat{Z}_{N}(\mathbf{x}^{N})}\right\}\bar{q}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}^{N,\star})

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\{\mathbf{x}^{N,\star}:\hat{Z}_{N}(\mathbf{x}^{N,\star})>\theta\}}\min\left\{1,\frac{\hat{Z}_{N}(\mathbf{x}^{N,\star})}{\hat{Z}_{N}(\mathbf{x}^{N})}\right\}\bar{q}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}^{N,\star})

\displaystyle\geq 0+\min\left\{1,\frac{\theta}{\hat{Z}_{N}(\mathbf{x}^{N})}\right\}\mathbb{P}_{\bar{q}}\left(\hat{Z}_{N}(\mathbf{x}^{N,\star})>\theta\right)

\displaystyle\geq\min\left\{1,\frac{\theta}{\hat{Z}_{N}(\mathbf{x}^{N})}\right\}\frac{(1-\theta)^{p/(p-1)}}{q(\omega^{p})^{1/(p-1)}}.

, so that

Lemma A.9
.

Under Assumptions
1
-
2
with

We abuse notation slightly and write

for the rejection probability as a function of the value

. Since

is non-decreasing in

Proof of Corollary

Similarly to the proof of Corollary

,
we start from

We next bound the expected rejection probability as follows

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\pi}\left[r(Y)^{t}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\pi}\left[\left(1-\min\left\{\frac{1}{2\omega(Y)},1\right\}c\right)^{t}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\pi}\left[\left(1-\frac{c}{2}\right)^{t}\mathds{1}(\omega(Y)\leq 1)\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\pi}\left[\left(1-\frac{c}{2\omega(Y)}\right)^{t}\mathds{1}(\omega(Y)\in[1,t])\right]+\mathbb{P}\left(\omega(Y)\geq t\right)

The second inequality follows by splitting the weight into
\omega\leq 1,\omega\in[1,t]
and
\omega>t

p>1
. The last inequality uses
\log(1+x)\leq x
with
x=-c/(2\omega(Y))
and
C=c/2
.
Consider the three terms on the last line. The first term decays exponentially fast with

Define
A_{k}:=[t/(k+1),t/k]

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Y\sim\pi}\left[\exp\{-Ct/\omega(Y)\}\mathds{1}(\omega(Y)\in[1,t])\right]

A.4.5
Proof of the result in Example

Given

\pi(x):=Z_{\pi}x^{-p},\qquad q(x):=Z_{q}\log^{k}(x)x^{-(p+1)},\qquad x\in[2,\infty),

with
p>1
and
k>1/(p-1)
. We have

\omega(x)=\frac{\pi(x)}{q(x)}=\frac{Z_{\pi}}{Z_{q}}\,\frac{x}{\log^{k}(x)}.

We first verify that
q(\omega^{p})<\infty
. Since

q(\omega^{p})=Z_{q}\left(\frac{Z_{\pi}}{Z_{q}}\right)^{p}\int_{2}^{\infty}\frac{x^{p}}{\log^{kp}(x)}\frac{\log^{k}(x)}{x^{p+1}}\mathrm{d}x=Z_{q}\left(\frac{Z_{\pi}}{Z_{q}}\right)^{p}\int_{2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{x\log^{k(p-1)}(x)}\mathrm{d}x,

the change of variable

gives

Now we estimate
\mathbb{P}_{X\sim\pi}(X>s)
and
\mathbb{P}_{X\sim q}(X>s)
for any

. For the former,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{X\sim\pi}(X>s)

\displaystyle=Z_{\pi}\int_{s}^{\infty}x^{-p}\mathrm{d}x=\frac{C_{1}}{s^{p-1}},\qquad C_{1}:=\frac{Z_{\pi}}{p-1}.

For the latter,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{X\sim q}(X>s)

\displaystyle=Z_{q}\int_{s}^{\infty}\frac{\log^{k}(x)}{x^{p+1}}\mathrm{d}x=Z_{q}s^{-p}\int_{1}^{\infty}\log^{k}(su)u^{-(p+1)}\mathrm{d}u

\pi

Then the probability of
A_{t}
under
\pi

On the other hand,

(Y_{j})_{j\geq 1}

to get

\displaystyle\int\bar{q}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}^{N}_{0})P(\mathbf{X}^{N}_{0},\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}^{N}_{1})\ldots P(\mathbf{X}^{N}_{t-1},\mathrm{d}\mathbf{X}^{N}_{t})|\hat{F}^{\circ}(\mathbf{X}^{N}_{t})|^{s}\mathds{1}(A_{t})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{t}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}^{N}_{0}\sim\bar{q}}\left[|\hat{F}^{\circ}(\mathbf{X}^{N}_{t})|^{s}\mathds{1}(A_{j,t})\right]=\sum_{j=1}^{t}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}^{N}_{0}\sim\bar{q}}\left[\mathbb{E}\left\{\left.|\hat{F}^{\circ}(\mathbf{X}^{N}_{j})|^{s}\mathds{1}(A_{j,t})\right|\mathbf{X}^{N}_{j-1}\right\}\right].

Conditional on
\mathbf{X}^{N}_{j-1}
,

\displaystyle\int P(\mathbf{X}^{N}_{j-1},d\mathbf{X}^{N}_{j})P(\mathbf{X}^{N}_{j},d\mathbf{X}^{N}_{j+1})\cdots P(\mathbf{X}^{N}_{t-1},d\mathbf{X}^{N}_{t})|\hat{F}^{\circ}(\mathbf{X}^{N}_{j})|^{s}\mathds{1}\{\mathbf{X}^{N}_{j-1}\neq\mathbf{X}^{N}_{j}=\cdots=\mathbf{X}^{N}_{t}\}

\displaystyle=\int P(\mathbf{X}^{N}_{j-1},d\mathbf{X}^{N}_{j})|\hat{F}^{\circ}(\mathbf{X}^{N}_{j})|^{s}\mathds{1}\{\mathbf{X}^{N}_{j-1}\neq\mathbf{X}^{N}_{j}\}

\displaystyle\quad\cdot\int P(\mathbf{X}^{N}_{j},d\mathbf{X}^{N}_{j+1})\cdots P(\mathbf{X}^{N}_{t-1},d\mathbf{X}^{N}_{t})\mathds{1}\{\mathbf{X}^{N}_{j}=\cdots=\mathbf{X}^{N}_{t}\}

\displaystyle=\int P(\mathbf{X}^{N}_{j-1},d\mathbf{X}^{N}_{j})|\hat{F}^{\circ}(\mathbf{X}^{N}_{j})|^{s}\mathds{1}\{\mathbf{X}^{N}_{j-1}\neq\mathbf{X}^{N}_{j}\}r(\mathbf{X}^{N}_{j})^{t-j}

\displaystyle=\int\bar{q}(d\zeta)\alpha(\mathbf{X}^{N}_{j-1},\zeta)|\hat{F}^{\circ}(\zeta)|^{s}r(\zeta)^{t-j}.

We can then upper bound
\alpha
by one, and upper bound
\sum_{j=1}^{t}r(\zeta)^{t-j}
by
(1-r(\zeta))^{-1}
to obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{t}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}^{N}_{0}\sim\bar{q},\zeta\sim\bar{q}}\left[\alpha(\mathbf{X}^{N}_{j-1},\zeta)|\hat{F}^{\circ}(\zeta)|^{s}r(\zeta)^{t-j}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\zeta\sim\bar{q}}\left[|\hat{F}^{\circ}(\zeta)|^{s}\sum_{j=1}^{t}r(\zeta)^{t-j}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\zeta\sim\bar{q}}\left[|\hat{F}^{\circ}(\zeta)|^{s}\frac{1}{1-r(\zeta)}\right].

Next, split the expectation into the cases
\hat{Z}(\zeta)>2
and
\hat{Z}(\zeta)\leq 2
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\zeta\sim\bar{q}}\left[|\hat{F}^{\circ}(\zeta)|^{s}\frac{1}{1-r(\zeta)}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\zeta\sim\bar{q}}\left[\frac{|\hat{F}^{\circ}(\zeta)|^{s}}{1-r(\zeta)}\mathds{1}(\hat{Z}(\zeta)\leq 2)\right]+\mathbb{E}_{\zeta\sim\bar{q}}\left[\frac{|\hat{F}^{\circ}(\zeta)|^{s}}{1-r(\zeta)}\mathds{1}(\hat{Z}(\zeta)>2)\right].

When
\hat{Z}(\zeta)\leq 2
, since
r
is increasing with
\hat{Z}
, we have
r(\zeta)\leq r(2)
and thus
(1-r(\zeta))^{-1}\leq(1-r(2))^{-1}
. This yields:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\zeta\sim\bar{q}}\left[|\hat{F}^{\circ}(\zeta)|^{s}\frac{1}{1-r(\zeta)}\mathds{1}(\hat{Z}(\zeta)\leq 2)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{1-r(2)}\int\bar{q}(\mathrm{d}\zeta)|\hat{F}^{\circ}(\zeta)|^{s}\mathds{1}(\hat{Z}(\zeta)\leq 2)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{1-r(2)}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}_{0}\sim\bar{q}}\left[|\hat{F}^{\circ}(\mathbf{x}_{0})|^{s}\right].

We obtain a bound in
N^{-s/2}
using Theorem
2.2
.

When
\hat{Z}(\zeta)>2
, from Lemma

, we have
r(\zeta)\leq 1-c_{p}(1/2)/(2\hat{Z}(\zeta))
.
Thus

, and
(1-r(\zeta))^{-1}\leq(2/c_{p}(1/2))\hat{Z}(\zeta)
. This yields:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\zeta\sim\bar{q}}\left[|\hat{F}^{\circ}(\zeta)|^{s}\frac{1}{1-r(\zeta)}\mathds{1}(\hat{Z}(\zeta)>2)\right]\leq\frac{2}{c_{p}(1/2)}\int\bar{q}(\mathrm{d}\zeta)|\hat{F}^{\circ}(\zeta)|^{s}\hat{Z}(\zeta)\mathds{1}(\hat{Z}(\zeta)>2).

Since we assume

, we can use the inequality (

\displaystyle|\hat{F}^{\circ}(\zeta)|^{s}\leq\left(\max_{1\leq i\leq N}f(\zeta_{i})+q(\omega f)\right)^{s},

from which we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\zeta\sim\bar{q}}

\displaystyle\left[|\hat{F}^{\circ}(\zeta)|^{s}\frac{1}{1-r(\zeta)}\mathds{1}(\hat{Z}(\zeta)>2)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{c_{p}(1/2)}\mathbb{E}_{\zeta\sim\bar{q}}\left[\left(\max_{1\leq i\leq N}f(\zeta_{i})+q(\omega f)\right)^{s}\cdot\hat{Z}(\zeta)\mathds{1}(\hat{Z}(\zeta)>2)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2^{s}}{c_{p}(1/2)}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\zeta\sim\bar{q}}\left[\left(\max_{1\leq i\leq N}f(\zeta_{i})\right)^{s}\cdot\hat{Z}(\zeta)\mathds{1}(\hat{Z}(\zeta)>2)\right]+q(\omega f)^{s}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\zeta\sim\bar{q}}\left[\hat{Z}(\zeta)\mathds{1}(\hat{Z}(\zeta)>2)\right]\right).

\displaystyle q(\omega f)^{s}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\zeta\sim\bar{q}}\left[\hat{Z}(\zeta)\mathds{1}(\hat{Z}(\zeta)>2)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2M(p)^{p}}{N^{p/2}}q(\omega f)^{s}.

For the remaining term, using Hölder’s inequality yields:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\zeta\sim\bar{q}}

\displaystyle\left[\left(\max_{1\leq i\leq N}f(\zeta_{i})\right)^{s}\cdot\hat{Z}(\zeta)\mathds{1}(\hat{Z}(\zeta)>2)\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}_{\zeta\sim\bar{q}}\left[\left(\max_{1\leq i\leq N}f(\zeta_{i})\right)^{r}\right]^{s/r}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\zeta\sim\bar{q}}\left[\left|\hat{Z}(\zeta)-1\right|^{\frac{r}{r-s}}\mathds{1}(\hat{Z}(\zeta)>2)\right]^{1-s/r}.

Under the assumptions, with

\displaystyle\frac{r}{r-s}\leq\frac{p+r+2}{r+2}=1+\frac{p}{r+2}\leq p,

by assumption.
This gives us:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\zeta\sim\bar{q}}\left[\left|\hat{Z}(\zeta)-1\right|^{\frac{r}{r-s}}\mathds{1}(\hat{Z}(\zeta)>2)\right]^{1-s/r}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{M(p)^{p}}{N^{p/2}}\right)^{1-s/r}.

Finally we use
the fact that

for non-negative

to derive

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\zeta\sim\bar{q}}\left[\left(\max_{1\leq i\leq N}f(\zeta_{i})\right)^{r}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\zeta\sim\bar{q}}\left[\max_{1\leq i\leq N}f(\zeta_{i})^{r}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}_{x\sim q}\left[f(x)^{r}N\right],

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(q(f^{r})N\right)^{s/r}\cdot\left(\frac{M(p)^{p}}{N^{p/2}}\right)^{1-s/r}.

We end up with an exponent of

equal to
s/r-(p/2)(r-s)/r

which under the assumptions is less than
-s/2
, as detailed in the proof of Theorem
2.2
.
Therefore, we obtain an upper bound in

on all terms.

, with
n
even, we write

E(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
for the sample
(x_{2i})_{i=1}^{n/2}
of the even-indexed variables, and
O(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
for the sample
(x_{2i-1})_{i=1}^{n/2}
of the odd-indexed variables.

•

We write
\hat{F}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
for the SNIS estimator of
\pi(f)
based on the sample
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
, and
\hat{F}\circ E(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
for the SNIS estimator of
\pi(f)
based on the even-indexed variables, and
\hat{F}\circ O(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
for the SNIS estimator of
\pi(f)
based on the odd-indexed variables.

The MLMC-UIS estimator is obtained with the following procedure:

1.

Choose an initial level
\ell\geq 0
. You can e.g. take
\ell=1
.

2.

Draw
R\sim\text{Geometric}(\rho)
.

3.

Draw
x_{1},\ldots,x_{2^{\ell+R+1}}
i.i.d. samples from
q
. You may compute once and for all
\omega(x_{i})
and
f(x_{i})
for
i=1,\ldots,2^{\ell+R+1}
, as these will be used in the different SNIS estimators computed below.

4.

Compute

\displaystyle S_{R}

\displaystyle=\hat{F}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{2^{\ell+R+1}}),

\displaystyle S_{R}^{O}

\displaystyle=\hat{F}\circ O(x_{1},\ldots,x_{2^{\ell+R+1}}),

\displaystyle S_{R}^{E}

\displaystyle=\hat{F}\circ E(x_{1},\ldots,x_{2^{\ell+R+1}}).

5.

Compute

\Delta_{R}=S_{R}-\frac{S_{R}^{O}+S_{R}^{E}}{2},

which expectation is equal the bias difference between SNIS estimators with
2^{\ell+R+1}
and
2^{\ell+R}
samples.

6.

Return either the “single sample” or the “Russian roulette” estimator, defined as follows:

\displaystyle\mathrm{SS}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{R+1}}\sum_{n=1}^{2^{R+1}}\hat{F}(x_{(n-1)2^{\ell}+1},\ldots,x_{n2^{\ell}})+\frac{\Delta_{R}}{(1-\rho)^{R}\rho},

\displaystyle\mathrm{RR}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2^{R+1}}\sum_{n=1}^{2^{R+1}}\hat{F}(x_{(n-1)2^{\ell}+1},\ldots,x_{n2^{\ell}})+\sum_{r=0}^{R}\frac{\Delta_{r}}{(1-\rho)^{r}}.

The first term in both the SS and RR estimators represents the average of many SNIS estimator of
\pi(f)

each based on
2^{\ell}
samples. This is biased for
\pi(f)
. The second terms in SS and RR are bias correction terms.

Remark B.1
.

The tuning parameters are
\ell
, which can be set to e.g. one, and the parameter
\rho
of the Geometric distribution, which controls the tail of the distribution of
R
, and thus both the cost and variance of the estimator.

Theorem 3 of
Shi & Cornish (2021)
provides conditions under which the variance of the SS and RR estimators is finite, and the expected cost is finite.
There are two sets of conditions:

•

Assume that
q(\omega^{a}+|f|^{b})<\infty
with
a>4
and
b>2a/(a-4)
.
Then set
\alpha=1-2/b
.

•

Assume that
q(\omega^{a}+|f|^{b})<\infty
with
2<a\leq 4
and
b>(2a+4)/(a-2)
,
or
a>4,b\in((2a+4)/(a-2),2a/(a-4))
. Then set
\alpha=a/2-(a+2)/b-1
.

Then the estimator has finite expected cost and finite variance for
\rho\in(2^{-1},1-2^{-1-\alpha})
. The larger the
\alpha
, the larger the range of
\rho
for which the estimator has finite variance and finite expected cost.

Remark B.2
.

If the test function
f
is chosen to be
x\mapsto\omega(x)^{-1}
, where
Z=\int\omega(x)\,dx
, then the unbiased MLMC estimators are unbiased estimators of
1/Z
.

Remark B.3
.

In the Exponential example considered in Section
6.1
,
with
p=3
, we are in the case
2<a\leq 4
. Considering the function
x\mapsto\omega(x)^{-1}
,
we can take
b
arbitrarily large. Therefore, we select
\alpha=1/2
, define
\rho_{\max}=1-2^{-1-\alpha}=1-2^{-3/2}\approx 0.6464
, and choose
\rho=95\%\rho_{\max}
in the experiments to reflect a slightly conservative but well-informed choice.

B.2
Taylor-UIS

Another approach to removing the bias of SNIS is based on Taylor expansions and randomization, and was proposed in
Blanchet et al. (2015)
, with useful improvements and analyses in
Chopin et al. (2025)
. In these articles,
the goal is to estimate a smooth function of the expectation of a random variable that we can sample from. In the context of importance sampling, the smooth function is the inverse function, and we apply the method of
Chopin et al. (2025)
to estimate
1/Z
without bias. We can then multiply the resulting estimator by an unbiased estimator of
q(f\omega)
to obtain an unbiased estimator of
\pi(f)
. We refer to this approach as Taylor-UIS.

From
g:m\mapsto 1/m
, the starting point is the identity

g(m)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\gamma_{k}\left(\frac{m}{x_{0}}-1\right)^{k},

(71)

where
x_{0}
is a tuning parameter, and the coefficients
\gamma_{k}
are given by

\displaystyle\forall k\geq 1\quad\gamma_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{g^{(k)}(x_{0})}{k!}x_{0}^{k}=\frac{(-1)^{k}}{x_{0}}.

The basic idea is that:

1.

The infinite sum in (
71
) can be estimated without bias with a truncated sum of reweighted terms.
Introduce
R\sim\text{Geometric}(\rho)
, such that
\mathbb{P}(R=k)=(1-\rho)^{k}\rho
for
k\geq 0
, where
\rho\in(0,1)
. Note that
\mathbb{P}(R\geq k)=(1-\rho)^{k}
.

2.

Each term
(m/x_{0}-1)^{k}
in (
71
) can be unbiasedly estimated using draws from
Y
with expectation
m
. The most natural estimator is
(
Blanchet et al. 2015
)
:

\displaystyle U_{r,k}

\displaystyle=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left(\frac{Y_{i}}{x_{0}}-1\right).

(72)

However, a better estimator is as follows: if one has access to
r
random variables
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{r}
with
r>k
, then
one can average over different possible products of
k
distinct variables.
Chopin et al. (2025)

consider averaging over
r
circular shifts of the variables:

\displaystyle U^{C}_{r,k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r}\left\{\sum_{j=0}^{r-1}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left(\frac{Y_{j+i}}{x_{0}}-1\right)\right\},

(73)

where the index
j+i
is taken modulo
r
, i.e.
Y_{r+1}=Y_{1}
,
Y_{r+2}=Y_{2}
, etc.

Combining the above considerations, an unbiased estimator of
1/m
is

R\sim\text{Geometric}(\rho),\quad Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{R}\sim\mathcal{L}(m,\sigma^{2}),\quad\hat{I}:=\sum_{k=0}^{R}\frac{(-1)^{k}U^{C}_{R,k}}{x_{0}\mathbb{P}(R\geq k)}.

(74)

where
\mathcal{L}(m,\sigma^{2})
is the distribution of
Y_{i}
with expectation
m
and variance
\sigma^{2}
.
In our setting each
Y_{i}
would correspond to
\hat{Z}=N^{-1}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\omega(x_{n})
where
x_{n}
are i.i.d. samples from
q
, and thus we need to choose a tuning parameter
N
. The two additional parameters are
x_{0}
and
\rho
.

Chopin et al. (2025)
provide guidance on tuning parameters in their Section 3. Introduce the following notation:

\displaystyle\beta_{0}=|m/x_{0}-1|,\sigma^{2}=\mathbb{V}[Y_{i}],

\displaystyle\hat{m}=\frac{1}{n_{0}}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{0}}Y_{i},\quad\hat{\sigma}^{2}=\frac{1}{n_{0}-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n_{0}}(Y_{i}-\hat{m})^{2},

where
n_{0}
is the length of a pilot sample of
Y_{i}
’s, used to estimate
m
and
\sigma^{2}
.

Chopin et al. (2025)
choose
n_{0}=10
.
Then define

\displaystyle\hat{x_{0}^{\star}}=(\hat{m}^{2}+\hat{\sigma}^{2})/\hat{m},

\displaystyle x_{0,\text{ub}}=\text{bootstrap percentile at level }(1-\alpha)\text{ of }(\hat{m}^{2}+\hat{\sigma}^{2})/(2\hat{m}),

\displaystyle x_{0}=\max(\hat{x_{0}^{\star}},x_{0,\text{ub}}),

where the bootstrap is performed by resampling with replacement from the pilot sample of
Y_{i}
’s.
By default we follow
Chopin et al. (2025)
and use 100 bootstrap samples and
\alpha=0.01
.
Having defined
x_{0}
, we can define

\hat{\beta}^{2}=\frac{\hat{\sigma}^{2}}{x_{0}^{2}}+\left(\frac{\hat{m}}{x_{0}}-1\right)^{2},

and

\rho=\min(1-\hat{\beta}^{2},1/(n_{0}+1)).

See Section 3.2 of
Chopin et al. (2025)
for details on the choice of
\rho
.
Having obtained
\rho
and
x_{0}
from
n_{0}
samples of
X_{i}
, we can then run the
\hat{I}
estimator as in (
74
):

1.

Draw
R\sim\text{Geometric}(\rho)
.

2.

Draw
R
i.i.d. samples
Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{R}
.

3.

Compute
U^{C}_{R,k}
for
k=1,\ldots,R
using (
73
).

4.

Compute
\hat{I}=x_{0}^{-1}+\sum_{k=1}^{R}\frac{(-1)^{k}U^{C}_{R,k}}{x_{0}(1-\rho)^{k}}
, an unbiased estimator of
1/m
.

A natural estimator of
q(\omega\cdot f)
is given by
M^{-1}\sum_{n=1}^{M}\omega(x^{\prime}_{n})f(x^{\prime}_{n})
, where
x^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,x^{\prime}_{M}
are i.i.d. samples from
q
, independent of all the samples used to obtain the unbiased estimator of
Z^{-1}
. Finally we obtain

\hat{\pi}(f)=\hat{I}\times\left(\frac{1}{M}\sum_{n=1}^{M}\omega(x^{\prime}_{n})f(x^{\prime}_{n})\right).

The parameter
M
should be tuned, relative to the tuning of each
Y_{i}
(which itself is a
\hat{Z}_{N}
estimator).
In the application of Section
6.1
,
our interest is in estimating
\pi(f)
for
f(x)=\omega(x)^{-1}
, which is equivalent to estimating
1/Z
,
so we do not need to estimate
q(\omega\cdot f)
, and we can simply use the
\hat{I}
estimator of
1/Z
in (
74
).
We leave the question of how to optimally split the budget between estimating
Z^{-1}
and estimating
q(\omega\cdot f)
for future work.
