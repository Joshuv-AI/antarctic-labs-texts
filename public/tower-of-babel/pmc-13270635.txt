Synthesis of Peptide-Oligonucleotide Conjugates via Condensation of a Lysine Side-Chain with a Thioester
Authors: So Muramoto, Takashi Osawa, Satoshi Obika
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Peptide-oligonucleotide conjugates (POCs) are promising drug delivery tools for oligonucleotide therapeutics. They are conventionally prepared by separately synthesizing peptides and oligonucleotides, introducing reactive functional groups into both molecules, and forming conjugates via click chemistry or maleimide-thiol coupling. However, these approaches generally require prefunctionalization of peptides or rely on the presence of cysteine residues, limiting their applicability to native peptides. Consequently, conjugation of peptides lacking thiol groups on cysteine residues to oligonucleotides remains challenging. Therefore, we aimed to develop a new conjugation method for POCs that does not require chemical modification of native peptides. To achieve this objective, we devised a method to prepare POCs via condensation between the ε-amino group of a lysine side-chain and a thioester-modified oligodeoxynucleotide. In establishing our POC synthesis method, the addition of 2-sulfanylmethyl-4-dimethylaminopyridine, which increases thioester reactivity, enabled rapid and efficient conjugation under mild conditions. Under the optimized conditions, conjugation proceeded efficiently with amino acids and simple trimer peptides having unprotected α-amino groups. Furthermore, conjugation with functional peptides demonstrated that this method is applicable to the synthesis of both linear and cyclic peptide-oligodeoxynucleotide conjugates.

Introduction

Oligonucleotide therapeutics exert pharmacological effects through a mechanism distinct from that of conventional small-molecule drugs as they bind to target RNA and regulate its expression. Owing to this unique action mechanism, they may be applicable to diseases that are otherwise difficult to treat. Oligonucleotide therapeutics are composed of single- or double-stranded oligonucleotides; however, natural DNA and RNA exhibit insufficient in vivo stability and limited affinity for target RNA for therapeutic applications. Chemically modified artificial oligonucleotides are commonly used to overcome these limitations. , Although most oligonucleotide therapeutics act in cells, oligonucleotides exhibit poor membrane permeability and tend to accumulate in the liver, , lacking intrinsic targeting ability. Therefore, despite their therapeutic potential, oligonucleotide therapeutics face significant challenges in delivery. Moreover, chemical modification of the oligonucleotide backbone alone cannot fully resolve these issues, necessitating the development of technologies for efficient intracellular delivery and specific tissue targeting of oligonucleotide therapeutics.

Recently, fundamental research worldwide has actively focused on delivery strategies for oligonucleotide therapeutics. , Specifically, conjugation of oligonucleotide therapeutics with ligand molecules possessing delivery capacity is an effective strategy for targeted drug delivery to specific tissues. Peptides, which are composed of amino acids, exhibit high biocompatibility, and some, known as cell-penetrating peptides (CPPs), can permeate cell membranes. Since the discovery of the trans-activator protein derived from the human immunodeficiency virus in 1988 revealed the existence of CPPs, various CPPs have been investigated as potential tools for intracellular drug delivery. In terms of peptide-oligonucleotide conjugates (POCs), conjugation with cell-penetrating Arg-Gly-Asp (RGD) peptides enhances intracellular uptake of small interfering RNAs. Additionally, receptors highly expressed in specific organs serve as useful targets for peptide-based ligands. For example, a conjugate of bombesin, a G protein-coupled receptor ligand, and an antisense oligonucleotide promotes intracellular uptake of the oligonucleotide by the G protein-coupled receptor-overexpressing PC3 prostate cancer cell line. Furthermore, conjugation with a peptide ligand for the glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor, which is highly expressed in the pancreas, improves pancreatic targeting of oligonucleotide therapeutics. In recent years, POCs have attracted considerable attention as promising drug delivery tools for oligonucleotide therapeutics. However, owing to synthetic limitations, only specific types of functional peptides can be conjugated to oligonucleotides.

Although several reliable methods have been developed for POC synthesis, each approach has inherent limitations. For example, the disulfide exchange reaction, which uses the thiol group on a cysteine side-chain as an anchoring site, − often suffers from poor chemical stability of the resulting POC because the disulfide bond is readily cleaved by intracellular reducing agents such as glutathione. POCs obtained via Michael addition of maleimides to thiols − undergo retro-Michael reactions under physiological conditions, leading to partial decomposition of the conjugates. Native chemical ligation is also a useful approach; − however, it requires prefunctionalization of the peptide with a thioester and the oligonucleotide with a cysteine residue before conjugation. Similarly, click chemistry , requires separate synthesis of peptides and oligonucleotides, followed by the installation of complementary reactive functional groups on both components, precluding the direct use of native peptides. Therefore, many POC synthesis methods rely on multistep prefunctionalization of both partners, increasing synthetic complexity. Focusing on the reactive side-chains of peptides, these conventional methods mainly use the thiol groups of cysteine residues, which exhibit high nucleophilicity. Although maleimide–thiol conjugation enables the utilization of thiol groups present in native peptides, many functional peptides do not contain cysteine residues at appropriate positions. Therefore, a simple conjugation method between oligonucleotides and natural peptides lacking cysteine residues is needed to expand the range of functional peptides applicable to POC synthesis. Among the 20 amino acids constituting proteins, bioactive peptides containing lysine residues (2006 reported peptides) are more numerous than those containing cysteine residues (627 reported peptides). The ε-amino group of a lysine side-chain is nucleophilic and reacts with the thioester structure of acetyl-CoA in the presence of acetyltransferases in vivo. Additionally, lysine and thioesters readily undergo condensation under mild chemical conditions. Therefore, the condensation reaction between lysine and thioesters has been widely used in chemical biology research on peptides and proteins. − In this context, we aimed to establish a POC synthesis method using the ε-amino groups of lysine side-chains in native peptides and thioester-modified oligodeoxynucleotides (ODNs (5′-d­(AGTATTGACCTA)-3′); Scheme ) for the direct conjugation of lysine-containing natural peptides with ODNs.

Results and Discussion

For conjugation of thioester-modified ODNs with lysine ε-amino groups, the introduced functional group must be stable during storage while remaining sufficiently reactive under coupling conditions. Various strategies for controlling thioester reactivity have been reported. For example, increasing steric bulk at the β-carbon, reducing the electrophilicity of the thioester carbonyl carbon via n → π* interactions between the antibonding π orbital of the thioester carbonyl and the lone pair (n) of an adjacent carbonyl oxygen, and the use of alkyl thioesters have been proposed. In this study, we selected alkyl thioester structures, whose reactivity and stability can be readily tuned by changing the alkyl chain length and electronic properties, and used a chemically robust linker to connect the thioester moiety to the ODN. To synthesize an ODN bearing a thioester capable of condensing with the ε-amino group of a peptide lysine side-chain, N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) esters 2A and 2B were prepared in two steps from succinic anhydride 1A and glutaric anhydride 1B, respectively (Scheme ). Moreover, 1-dodecanethiol with a long alkyl chain was selected as the thioester building block because it is a low-toxicity liquid and easy to handle, offering advantages in terms of safety. Next, the 5′-amino linker-modified ODN was reacted with the activated esters 2A and 2B under mild basic conditions to afford thioester-modified ODNs 3A and 3B, respectively. The isolated yield of 3A was lower than that of 3B, and a byproduct with a molecular weight 202 Da smaller than that of 3A was observed during the synthesis of 3A. To identify this byproduct, compound S1 was synthesized by condensing compound 2A with benzylamine and subsequently subjecting it to basic conditions (Scheme S1). The obtained compound S2 was identified as N-benzylsuccinimide, indicating that the byproduct formed during the synthesis of ODN 3A was cyclized compound 4A. In contrast, during the synthesis of ODN 3B, no byproduct corresponding to cyclized compound 4B (202 Da smaller than 3B) was detected. Moreover, an aqueous solution of purified ODN 3B remained stable at −20 °C for over two years, without any noticeable degradation (Figure S8). We further attempted to prepare an ODN bearing a more reactive NHS ester group using disuccinimidyl glutarate; however, the desired NHS ester-modified ODN could not be obtained (Scheme S2 and Figure S9). These results suggest that compound 3B is a chemically stable and easy-to-handle active ester.

Synthesis of Thioester-Modified ODNs 3A and 3B

To evaluate the reactivity of the synthesized thioester-modified ODNs 3A and 3B toward the lysine side-chain, conjugation experiments were conducted with N α-acetyl-l-lysine 5. When 100 equiv of N α-acetyl-l-lysine 5 were reacted with substrates 3A and 3B in aqueous solution at 50 °C for 192 h, the desired conjugates 6A and 6B, respectively, were synthesized (Scheme ). The yield of 6B, in which three carbon atoms are present between two carbonyl groups, was 69%, approximately twice that of 6A. During the synthesis of 6A, byproduct 4A with a molecular weight 18 Da smaller than that of 7A was formed in significant amounts. Based on the mass spectrometry (MS) data and model reactions described above (Scheme S1), this byproduct was identified as cyclized compound 4A. Similarly, during the synthesis of 6B, a minor byproduct with a molecular weight 18 Da smaller than that of 7B was detected, which was presumed to be cyclized compound 4B. These results suggest that compound 3A is prone to intramolecular cyclization to form imide 4A upon conjugation with lysine 5; therefore, compound 3B is a more suitable substrate for this reaction.

Conjugation of N α-Acetyl-l-Lysine 5 with Thioester-Modified ODNs 3A and 3B

In the conjugation reaction, not only was hydrolyzed product 7B formed along with the desired conjugate 6B but the reaction also required 192 h to reach completion (Table , entry 1). To shorten the reaction time, conjugation experiments between the thioester-modified ODN 3B and N α-acetyl-l-lysine were conducted using various buffer solutions (entries 2–4; TEAB, TEAF, MOPS buffer respectively). Among the tested buffers, mildly basic triethylammonium bicarbonate (TEAB) buffer (pH 8.2) accelerated the reaction rate, affording the desired conjugate 6B in 67% yield after 48 h (entry 2). However, the hydrolyzed byproduct 7B was also obtained in 28% yield. To suppress the formation of this hydrolyzed product, a mixed solvent of TEAB buffer (pH 8.2) and N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF; 1:1, v/v) was used. Under these conditions, the formation of 7B was reduced to 4%, whereas the yield of conjugate 6B was increased to 77% (entry 5). The base-promoted hydrolysis of thioesters generally proceeds via a nucleophilic addition-elimination mechanism, in which the rate-determining step is the nucleophilic attack of hydroxide ion on the carbonyl carbon. Therefore, the addition of DMF likely reduces the effective concentrations of water and hydroxide ion in the reaction medium, thereby suppressing hydrolysis. This interpretation is consistent with previous studies demonstrating that increasing hydroxide ion concentration accelerates thioester hydrolysis. However, due to the limited solubility of DNA in organic solvents, increasing the DMF content beyond this ratio was not feasible. Further investigations were conducted by adding various additives (entries 8–15) to activate the thioester in situ, thereby enhancing reactivity toward the ε-amino group of the lysine side-chain (chemical structures of the additives listed in Table are shown in Figure S10). Most additives examined (entries 6–8, 10, and 11) did not improve the yield or shorten the reaction time, and no activated ester intermediates were detected via MS. These findings suggest that the reaction proceeds via direct aminolysis of the thioester, without involvement of activated ester intermediates. The addition of NHS slightly increased the yield of 6B to 85% (entry 12) compared to that recorded under the conditions in entry 5. Notably, the addition of N-hydroxyphthalimide (NHPI) completed the reaction within 24 h but drastically decreased the yield of 6B to 9%, accompanied by extensive hydrolysis of 7B. The addition of NHS markedly increased the reaction rate, indicating that the reaction was possibly promoted via the transient formation of an NHS ester intermediate. The absence of detectable NHS ester intermediates in MS suggests that, once formed, the NHS ester reacts rapidly with the ε-amino group or water. N-hydroxyphthalimide-derived ester intermediates are highly reactive; therefore, they predominantly reacted with the large excess of water in the system, suppressing productive reaction with the lysine side-chain. Remarkably, when 2-sulfanylmethyl-4-dimethylaminopyridine (SMDMAP), known for its high thioester-activating ability, was added, the reaction time was drastically shortened from 48 to 6 h, affording conjugate 6B in 77% yield (entry 15). Moreover, compared with NHS, which exhibited the highest reaction efficiency among the additives, excluding SMDMAP, at the 6 h reaction time point, the use of SMDMAP as an additive resulted in more than a 3-fold higher yield of conjugate 6B. The significant acceleration of the reaction upon addition of SMDMAP is attributed to activation of the thioester via intermolecular S → S acyl transfer from the dodecyl thioester to SMDMAP, followed by intramolecular S → N acyl transfer to form an N-acylpyridinium intermediate. Based on these results, the reaction conditions in entry 15 were determined to be optimal for POC synthesis. Taken together, these findings suggest that the thioester-modified ODN 3B reacts efficiently with lysine only in the presence of a mildly basic buffer and the thioester activator SMDMAP. Furthermore, because ODN 3B can be stored stably without degradation, no preactivation or in situ preparation is necessary, which represents a practical advantage of this conjugation system.

Next, the reactivity between the thioester group and α-amino group of amino acids under the optimized conditions was examined (Figure ). Yields were determined based on the ratio of peak areas observed in the liquid chromatography (LC) spectra. For compounds exhibiting overlapping peaks (9b–h), yields were calculated using a combination of peak area and ion intensity ratios. Detailed procedures are described in the Supporting Information. Conjugation proceeded with over 50% efficiency for all amino acids, indicating that this method can also be used for conjugation via N-terminal amino group of peptides. Among the amino acids examined, glycine exhibited the highest yield (9e). Based on these findings, conjugation reactions were further examined using peptides containing either an amino group on the lysine side-chain or an N-terminal glycine residue. As model peptides, glycine trimer 8f, its analog with the N-terminal glycine replaced by alanine (Ala-Gly-Gly 8g), and a lysine-containing tripeptide (N α-Ac-Lys-Pro-Val-CONH2 8h) were reacted with ODN 3B under the same conditions. The conjugation yield of Ala-Gly-Gly 8g was 30% (9g), whereas Gly-Gly-Gly 8f showed a higher yield of 60% (9f). Additionally, conjugation with the lysine-containing tripeptide N α-Ac-Lys-Pro-Val-CONH2 8h afforded the desired product (9h) in 80% yield, and its isolation yield was also good at 73%.

Using the established method, efficient conjugation was achieved, particularly with short peptides containing amino groups derived from lysine side-chains or N-terminal glycines. Based on the above-mentioned results, conjugations with both linear and cyclic peptides were investigated. As linear and cyclic peptide models, T9 peptide 10, DYKDDDDK peptide 11, gp-41 fusion peptide 12, cyclic RGD (cRGD) peptide 13, and internalizing RGD (iRGD) peptide 14 were selected (Figure ), and their conjugations with ODNs were examined (Table ). First, conjugation of the T9 peptide 10 bearing a single lysine side-chain with ODN 3B was performed. LC–MS analysis of the reaction mixture revealed that the conjugate 15 was obtained in 40% yield (entry 1). Although the yield was lower than those observed for conjugation with the monomer 6B and the trimer 9h, successful conjugation of the 12 mer peptide 10 was achieved. Peptide 10 has two free amino groups derived from the lysine side-chain and N-terminal serine, thus, two possible conjugates can be produced from 10 and 3B. However, it was not clear from the analytical data of product 15 which of the two amino groups in 10 reacted with 3B. Based on the results of conjugations of amino acid monomers, tripeptides, and functional peptides (Figures and ), although the number of conjugation examples remains limited, the reactivity of the N-terminal amino group tends to decrease as the peptide chain length increases. From this consideration, POC 15 might be produced not by the conjugation at the N-terminal serine, but rather at the amino group of the lysine side-chain of 10. DYKDDDDK peptide 11 with two lysine residues was conjugated with ODN 3B. LC-MS analysis of the reaction mixture revealed two separated conjugate-derived peaks in the UV chromatogram (Table S27, retention times = 8.00, 8.42 min (LC-yield; 12%, 6% respectively)), and the combined LC-yield of two POCs 16a and 16b was 18% (entry 2). Masses corresponding to conjugates bearing one ODN molecule per peptide molecule were observed in both peaks by mass analysis. No conjugates bearing multiple ODNs per peptide molecule were observed. In this DYKDDDDK peptide 11, the possible reaction sites are the N-terminal amino group and two lysine side-chains. Since only two conjugate-derived peaks were observed despite the presence of three possible reaction sites in peptide 11, this observation is consistent with preferential conjugation at the amino groups of the two lysine side-chains rather than at the N-terminal amino group. The generated products 16 and 16b were isolated for precise analysis, and the LC-MS/MS analysis was performed to identify the amino group involved in the conjugation in each product (data not shown). However, no definitive fragment information was obtained, which may be due to reduced ionization efficiency arising from the positively charged peptide and negatively charged ODN. Shortening the ODN by 3′-exonuclease treatment of the obtained 16a and 16b to improve the ionization efficiency also did not lead to the identification of the reacted amino group in 11. Based on the above considerations regarding the reactivity of the N-terminal amino group of peptides, POC 16a and 16b are likely formed through conjugation of 3B with the amino group on a lysine side chain in 11, similar to POC 15. Next, the conjugation efficiency of the 17 mer gp-41 fusion peptide 12 bearing an N-terminal glycine was also investigated. to give in 8% LC-yield (entry 3), which is quite lower compared to the conjugate 9f of tripeptide with an N-terminal glycine (Figure ). Although it is difficult to directly compare the results in Table due to differences in peptide stoichiometry resulting from the poor solubility of the gp-41 fusion peptide 12, this result suggests that the reactivity of the N-terminal amino group may decrease as the peptide length increases. Conjugation between cRGD peptide 13 and the ODN proceeded efficiently, affording the desired POC 18 in 70% LC-yield and in 36% isolated yield (entry 4). Finally, conjugation between iRGD peptide 14 and the ODN was examined. In our POC synthesis method, SMDMAP is generated in situ by reducing di-SMDMAP with Tris­(2-carboxyethyl)­phosphine (TCEP); therefore, this system cannot be directly applied to disulfide bond-containing peptides, such as iRGD 14. To overcome this limitation, we focused on the previous observation that the addition of NHS improved the reaction yield (Table , entry 12). Conjugation of iRGD 14 with ODN 3B was performed in the presence of NHS (Figure ). Although the reacted amino group in iRGD 14 could not be identified, the desired POC 19 was successfully obtained in 9% LC-yield after 24 h (entry 5). The lower conjugation efficiency observed for iRGD peptide 14 might be attributable to conformational changes induced by the organic solvent. In aqueous solution, the lysine side-chain of iRGD is exposed to the outside of the cyclic structure through the formation of intramolecular hydrogen bonds. However, under our conjugation conditions using 50% DMF, the strong hydrogen bond-accepting ability of DMF may disrupt these intramolecular hydrogen bonds. In addition, polar organic solvents affect peptide conformation by reducing the hydration shell surrounding the peptide. As a result of these effects, the accessibility of the lysine side-chain may be reduced, leading to decreased reactivity. A similar explanation applies to the low conjugation efficiency observed with linear peptides 11 and 12, suggesting that conformational changes in the peptides may limit ODN access to both the lysine side-chain and the N-terminal glycine. In contrast, the small and more conformationally constrained cRGD peptide is likely to maintain exposure of the lysine side-chain even in the presence of an organic solvent, which may account for its higher conjugation efficiency. Taken together, despite the reduced efficiency observed for certain substrates, all five functional peptides 10–14 successfully yielded their corresponding POCs 15–19, confirming that the established conjugation method is applicable to various functional peptide-ODN conjugate syntheses. While the current reaction is not strictly lysine-selective, these results overall suggest that our approach is particularly effective for cyclic peptide conjugates without disulfide bonds.

Conclusions

In conclusion, a peptide-ODN conjugation method based on condensation between lysine residues and thioesters was developed in this study. During optimization of the reaction conditions, the use of an organic solvent effectively suppressed hydrolysis in the conjugation between thioester-modified ODNs and lysine monomers. In the field of peptide ligation, it has been shown that thioester hydrolysis can be suppressed by diluting with aqueous solvent using an organic solvent. , This study demonstrated that the use of DMF can effectively suppress the hydrolysis of alkyl thioesters even in the POC synthesis of highly water-soluble ODNs. In addition, the thioester-modified ODNs efficiently reacted with lysine side-chain amino groups in the presence of the mildly basic TEAB buffer and SMDMAP, a thioester activator. Additionally, the thioester-modified ODNs remained stable during frozen storage, suggesting that the established method facilitates POC synthesis without preactivation of the ODN substrate. Studies using various amino acids and model tripeptides revealed that, for structurally simple peptides, both lysine side-chain and N-terminal glycine amino groups can serve as effective reaction sites in this system. Furthermore, conjugation reactions with functional peptides were successfully performed. Although some peptides produced POCs with modest yields, all desired POCs were obtained, suggesting that the developed reaction system is applicable to the synthesis of functional POCs. Particularly, conjugation with cRGD peptide proceeded efficiently, indicating that conjugation with cyclic peptides lacking an N-terminal amino group may be particularly efficient. However, in any case, further improvement of lysine reactivity will likely require consideration of factors such as peptide length and solvent-dependent conformational changes. Future studies should apply our method to prepare POCs for the development of drug delivery systems for ODN therapeutics.

Synthesis Methods

Synthesis of 2,5-dioxopyrrolidin-1-yl 4-(Dodecylthio)-4-oxobutanoate (2A)

Under a nitrogen atmosphere, a solution of 1-dodecanethiol (1.3 mL; 5.5 mmol) in anhydrous pyridine (50 mL) was treated with succinic anhydride (0.50 g; 5.0 mmol) at room temperature. The mixture was stirred at 80 °C for 3 h. After completion of the reaction, the solvent was removed under reduced pressure. The residue was treated with toluene and concentrated to obtain an orange solid (1.69 g). The crude intermediate was dissolved in dichloromethane (50 mL) under nitrogen, followed by the addition of NHS (0.72 g; 6.25 mmol) and 1-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-3-ethylcarbodiimide hydrochloride (1.05 g; 5.5 mmol) at room temperature. The mixture was stirred for 3 h. Subsequently, the reaction mixture was diluted with chloroform, washed with water and saturated brine, dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate, and concentrated under reduced pressure. The crude product (1.93 g) was purified via silica gel column chromatography (hexane/ethyl acetate = 3/1) to yield compound 2A as a white solid (1.58 g; 79%).

Synthesis of ODN 3A
