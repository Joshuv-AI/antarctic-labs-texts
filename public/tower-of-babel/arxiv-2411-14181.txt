1.
Introduction

Throughout this paper, let
r
be a large prime.
In a recent breakthrough work of Harper
[
6
]
, it is established that the typical size of Dirichlet character sum
\sum_{1\leqslant n\leqslant x}\chi(n)
is
o(\sqrt{x})
. Precisely, it is proved that if
1\leqslant x\leqslant r
with
\min(x,r/x)\to\infty
, then

(1.1)

\frac{1}{r-1}\sum_{\chi\bmod r}|\sum_{1\leqslant n\leqslant x}\chi(n)|=o(\sqrt{x}),

which would imply that for “typical
\chi
”, the character sum is of size
o(\sqrt{x})
.

In both the random model and the character sum cases, Harper proved a sharp quantitative saving.

This somewhat surprising phenomenon was first proved in an earlier beautiful work of Harper
[
5
]
in the random model case, namely instead of for a character
\chi
, the “better than square-root cancellation” phenomenon holds for a random sum
\sum_{1\leqslant n\leqslant x}f(n)
, where
f(n)
is a random multiplicative function,
2
2

2

f(p)
is defined as an independent identically distributed random variable taking values on complex unit circle for all primes

f(n)
is defined as a completely multiplicative function.

which can be viewed as a random model for Dirichlet characters. In order to connect the deterministic case to the random multiplicative function model, Harper used a derandomization method.

The underlying reason for the “better than square-root cancellation” happening is subtle, which connects to the study of critical multiplicative chaos in probability theory. It is also believed that such a structure is delicate and may easily be destroyed by some perturbation. For example, one can consider general character sums like
\sum_{1\leqslant n\leqslant x}a(n)\chi(n)
(see
[
6
,
14
]
) or random sums
\sum_{1\leqslant n\leqslant x}a(n)f(n)
(see
[
13
,
4

8
]
) with a twist

. It is believed that for some generic coefficients

, such a “better than square-root cancellation” would die out (see
[
16

In this paper, we are interested in
mixed
character sums of the form
\sum_{1\leqslant n\leqslant x}\chi(n)e(n\theta)
, featuring both an additive character
e(n\theta)
and a multiplicative character
\chi(n)
.

For technical reasons it is simpler for us to work with a smooth weight
w\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
.
Throughout this paper,
fix a smooth function
w\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
,
supported on
[0,1]
,
with
\int_{0}^{1}w(t)^{2}\,dt>0
.
Note that by partial summation, (
1.1
) immediately implies

(1.2)

\frac{1}{r-1}\sum_{\chi\bmod r}|\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\chi(n)w(n/x)|=o(\sqrt{x}).

Similarly,
it is also known that for any fixed

\theta

(1.3)

\frac{1}{r-1}\sum_{\chi\bmod r}|\sum_{n\geqslant 1}\chi(n)e(n\theta)w(n/x)|=o(\sqrt{x}).

Our main theorem shows that the typical size will be on the square-root size when

is not “too close to the rationals”.
(The precise condition we impose on

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\theta
denote an irrational real number such that for some positive constant
C=C(\theta)
and all

, the following holds:

(1.4)

\|q\theta\|:=\min_{n\in{\mathbb{Z}}}|q\theta-n|\geqslant C\exp( - q^{\frac{1}{4}}).

If
x

), then we have for all

(1.5)

\sqrt{x}\ll\frac{1}{r-1}\sum_{\chi\bmod r}|\sum_{1\leqslant n\leqslant x}\chi(n)e(n\theta)w(n/x)|\ll\sqrt{x},

where the implied constants are allowed to depend on
\theta
(and
w
).

Henceforth, to simplify the notation
we let
\mathbb{E}_{\chi}S(\chi):=\frac{1}{r-1}\sum_{\chi\bmod r}S(\chi)
.

We give an overview of the proof. The upper bound on the first absolute moment follows easily from the second moment computation together with the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. Indeed,
for
1\leqslant x\leqslant r
, the orthogonality relation implies that

(1.6)

\mathbb{E}_{\chi}[|\sum_{1\leqslant n\leqslant x}\chi(n)e(n\theta)w(n/x)|^{2}]=\sum_{1\leqslant n\leqslant\min(x,r-1)}w(n/x)^{2}\sim x\int_{0}^{1}w(t)^{2}\,dt\asymp x,

provided that
x
is sufficiently large (in terms of

).

A key feature of our result is that the average size is around
\sqrt{x}
uniformly
for all

x\leqslant r
.
However, our methods diverge around

.
In the case

we show the bound without the weight, that is, for

,

(1.7)

\mathbb{E}_{\chi}[|\sum_{1\leqslant n\leqslant x}\chi(n)e(n\theta)|^{4}]\ll x^{2}.

By using partial summation, this would imply

(1.8)

\mathbb{E}_{\chi}[|\sum_{1\leqslant n\leqslant x}\chi(n)e(n\theta)w(n/x)|^{4}]\ll x^{2}.

An application of Hölder’s inequality together with (

) and (

By using the orthogonality, the fourth moment of the mixed character sum in (

(1.9)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1}m_{2}\equiv n_{1}n_{2}\bmod r\\
m_{i},n_{i}\leqslant x\end{subarray}}e((m_{1}+m_{2}-n_{1}-n_{2})\theta).

The congruence condition is irrelevant if

The bulk of the paper focuses on the case

.
This completes the sum over

Recently, Heap and Sahay

Their work is closely tied to partial sums of the form

Remark
.

A restriction like
x\leqslant r
is necessary in Theorem

.
For instance, if

, then

\mathbb{E}_{\chi}\absolutevalue{\sum_{1\le n \le x} \chi(n) e(n\theta)}=\frac{\absolutevalue{1-e((x-r)\theta)}}{\absolutevalue{1-e(r\theta)}}\mathbb{E}_{\chi}\absolutevalue{\sum_{1\le n \le r} \chi(n) e(n\theta)}\ll\frac{1}{\absolutevalue{1-e(r\theta)}}\sqrt{r},

by the easy (

But if

, then for infinitely many primes

the right-hand side is

, not

.

For instance, if

is prime for infinitely many primes

then for some

, we have, by the triangle inequality

\begin{split}\absolutevalue{1-e(r_n\theta)}&=\max(\absolutevalue{1-e(p_n\theta)},\absolutevalue{1-e((p_n+c)\theta)})\\
&\gg\absolutevalue{e(p_n\theta)-e((p_n+c)\theta)}=\absolutevalue{1-e(c\theta)}\gg_{\theta}1.\end{split}

Our main result concerns the size of the mixed character sums for the large range where the two key parameters satisfy
x\leqslant r
. It would be nice if one could determine the distribution. In particular, we raise the following question.

Question 1.2
.

For which ranges of parameters
x,r\to+\infty

is it true that
as
\chi
varies uniformly over the family of Dirichlet characters mod
r
, we have

\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}\sum_{1\leqslant n\leqslant x}\chi(n)e(\sqrt{2}n)\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{CN}(0,1)?

In the above notation,

denotes standard complex Gaussian distribution.
One may also ask about any
\theta
that is not “close to” rationals;
we put
\theta=\sqrt{2}
for concreteness.
Originally, we conjectured that Question

.
However, very recent work of Bober, Klurman, and Shala

[
2
]

shows, in particular, that Question

To attack Question

]
for high moments computation on related problems. It would be even more interesting to determine the limiting distribution for the full range

There has been a line of nice work on studying the character sums with additive twists without averaging over characters, and we refer readers to
[

3

and references therein. We also mention that there is the dual direction of the problem, where one can average over

for the most recent development and reference therein).

2.
Short sum case

In this section, we prove the lower bound in Theorem
1.1
in the case
x\leqslant\sqrt{r}
. As discussed in the introduction, it suffices to prove an upper bound as in (
1.7
).
Expanding the fourth moment and noticing the perfect orthogonality (thanks to
x\leqslant\sqrt{r}
), it is sufficient to show the following proposition.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\theta
satisfy the Diophantine condition (
1.4
). Let
x
be large. Then

(2.1)

\Big|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant m_{1},m_{2},n_{1},n_{2}\leqslant x\\
m_{1}m_{2}=n_{1}n_{2}\\
\{m_{1},n_{1}\}\neq\{m_{2},n_{2}\}\end{subarray}}e((m_{1}+m_{2}-n_{1}-n_{2})\theta)\Big|=o(x^{2}).

The proof of the Proposition closely follows the proof of
[
13
, Theorem 1.6, Theorem 3.1]
.
Our situation is simpler and we give a proof here for completeness.
We remark that the main difference is that the exponential sum in
[
13
]
has multiplicative coefficients, which requires a result of Montgomery–Vaughan
[
10
]
.
Instead, we use the following simple estimate which leads to the weaker Diophantine condition (
1.4
) and thus a better quantitative result.

A simple starting point is that

(2.2)

\sum_{1\leqslant n\leqslant y}e(n\alpha)=\frac{e(\alpha)(1-e(y\alpha))}{1-e(\alpha)}\ll\min\{y,\frac{1}{\|\alpha\|}\}.

Proof of Proposition
2.1
.

We use the same parametrization as in
[
13
]
,
to write
m_{1}=ga
,
m_{2}=hb
,
n_{1}=gb
and
n_{2}=ha
. The constraints on

,
m_{2}
,
n_{1}
,
n_{2}
then become
(a,b)=1
with
a\neq b
,
g\neq h
, and
\max(a,b)\times\max(g,h)\leqslant x
.
Thus the sum we wish to bound becomes

(2.3)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\max(a,b)\times\max(g,h)\leqslant x\\
a\neq b,\ (a,b)=1\\
g\neq h\end{subarray}}e((g-h)(a-b)\theta).

Since
\max(a,b)\times\max(g,h)\leqslant x
, we may break the sum above into the cases (1) when
\max(g,h)\leqslant\sqrt{x}
, (2) when
\max(a,b)\leqslant\sqrt{x}
, taking
care to subtract the terms satisfying (3)
\max(a,b)
and
\max(g,h)
both below
\sqrt{x}
.

Before turning to these cases, we record a preliminary lemma which will be useful in our analysis.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\theta
be an irrational number satisfying the Diophantine condition (
1.4
).
Let
{\mathcal{L}}={\mathcal{L}}(x)
denote the set of all integers
\ell
with
|\ell|\leqslant\sqrt{x}
such that for some

k\leqslant(\log x)^{1+\epsilon}
one has
\|k\ell\theta\|\leqslant x^{-\frac{1}{3}}
, where
\epsilon>0
is small. Then
0
is in
{\mathcal{L}}
, and for any two distinct elements
\ell_{1}
,
\ell_{2}\in{\mathcal{L}}

we have
|\ell_{1}-\ell_{2}|\gg(\log x)^{3/2}
.

Proof.

Evidently
0
is in
{\mathcal{L}}
, and the main point is the spacing condition satisfied by elements of
{\mathcal{L}}
. If
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
are distinct elements of
{\mathcal{L}}
then there exist
k_{1}
,
k_{2}\leqslant(\log x)^{1+\epsilon}
with
\|k_{1}\ell_{1}\theta\|\leqslant x^{-\frac{1}{3}}
and
\|k_{2}\ell_{2}\theta\|\leqslant x^{-\frac{1}{3}}
. It follows that

\|k_{1}k_{2}(\ell_{1}-\ell_{2})\theta\|\leqslant 2(\log x)^{1+\epsilon}x^{-\frac{1}{3}}
. The desired bound on
|\ell_{1}-\ell_{2}|
now follows from the Diophantine property that we required of
\theta
, namely that
\|q\theta\|\gg\exp(-q^{\frac 1{4}})
which implies that
(\log x)^{4}\ll k_{1}k_{2}|\ell_{1}-\ell_{2}|
and the desired estimate follows.
∎

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\leqslant x/\max(g,h)\\
\end{subarray}}\mu(k)\Big|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\leqslant x/(k\max(g,h))\\

3.
Reduction to a counting problem

In this section, we consider the case
\sqrt{r}\leqslant x\leqslant r

and reduce (
1.8
) to
a counting problem for a quadratic Diophantine equation
involving a pair of integers
(k,r)
with
\theta\approx\frac{k}{r}
.
In the next section, we will use the pigeonhole principle to show that this equation does not have too many solutions, provided the Diophantine condition (
1.4
) holds.

The starting point is to apply the Poisson summation formula to flip the character summation in (
1.8
) from “up to
x
” to “up to
r/x
”.
Let
k=\lfloor r\theta\rfloor
.
Write
\theta=k/r+\theta^{\prime}
where
0\leqslant\theta^{\prime}<1/r
.
This is a pragmatic rational approximation of
\theta

that will prevent an increase in the “conductor”
r
for the problem at hand.
3
3

3

This can be thought of as a special case of the “conductor lowering” trick in the delta method.
However, we believe it could be worthwhile to explore more complicated rational approximations of
\theta
, in order to sharpen our analysis.

We define

f_{r,\chi}(n):=\chi(n)e(\frac{kn}{r}),\quad f_{\infty}(n):=w(\frac{n}{x})e(n\theta^{\prime}).

Then since
w(n/x)=0
for all integers
n\leqslant 0
and
n\geqslant x
,
we have

(3.1)

\sum_{1\leqslant n\leqslant x}\chi(n)e(n\theta)w(\frac{n}{x})=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}f_{r,\chi}(n)f_{\infty}(n)=\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{f}_{r,\chi}(\frac{m}{r})\hat{f}_{\infty}(\frac{m}{r})

by Poisson summation in
(\mathbb{Z}/r\mathbb{Z})\times\mathbb{R}
,
where

\hat{f}_{r,\chi}(\frac{m}{r})=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{t\in\mathbb{Z}/r\mathbb{Z}}\chi(t)e\left(\frac{(k+m)t}{r}\right)

and

\hat{f}_{\infty}(\frac{m}{r})=\int_{\mathbb{R}}w(\frac{t}{x})e((\theta^{\prime}-\frac{m}{r})t)dt.

We now estimate the Fourier coefficients
\hat{f}_{r,\chi}(\frac{m}{r})

and
\hat{f}_{\infty}(\frac{m}{r})
.
For a fixed
m
, if
k+m\not\equiv 0\bmod{r}

then by applying standard properties of Gauss sums,

(3.2)

\hat{f}_{r,\chi}(\frac{m}{r})=\chi(k+m)^{-1}\frac{C(\chi)}{r^{1/2}},

where
\absolutevalue{C(\chi)}\leqslant 1
and
C(\chi)
depends only on
\chi
.
Next, we turn to
\hat{f}_{\infty}
.

Lemma 3.1

(Fourier bounds)
.

For all
A\geqslant 0
we have

\hat{f}_{\infty}(\frac{m}{r})\ll_{A}x\big(1+\frac{x\max(\absolutevalue{m}-1,0)}{r}\big)^{-A}.

Proof.

Integrating by parts over
t
,
and recalling that
w
is smooth on
\mathbb{R}

and supported on
[0,1]
,
we get

\hat{f}_{\infty}(\frac{m}{r})\ll_{B}\int_{0}^{x}x^{-B}\absolutevalue{\theta'-\frac{m}{r}}^{-B}\,dt

for all
B\geqslant 0
.
Moreover,
\absolutevalue{\theta'-\frac{m}{r}}\geqslant\max(\frac{\absolutevalue{m}-1}{r},0)
,
since
\absolutevalue{\theta'}\leqslant\frac{1}{r}
.
Thus

\hat{f}_{\infty}(\frac{m}{r})\ll_{B}x\big(x\max(\frac{\absolutevalue{m}-1}{r},0)\big)^{-B}.

Optimizing this bound over
B\in\{0,A\}
gives the desired result.
∎

We first discuss the pesky terms in (
3.1
) with
m\equiv-k\bmod{r}
.
We have

1+\frac{x\max(\absolutevalue{m}-1,0)}{r}\gg\frac{x\max(\absolutevalue{m},r)}{r}

for all
m\equiv-k\bmod{r}
,
because
k\sim\theta r
and we may assume
0<\theta<1
.
Therefore,

(3.3)

\sum_{m\equiv-k\bmod{r}}\hat{f}_{r,\chi}(\frac{m}{r})\hat{f}_{\infty}(\frac{m}{r})=\frac{\bm{1}_{\chi=\chi_{0}}}{r-1}\sum_{m\equiv-k\bmod{r}}\hat{f}_{\infty}(\frac{m}{r})\ll\frac{x^{1-A}}{r-1}

by Lemma
3.1
, provided
A>1
.

Before expanding the fourth moment of (
3.1
), we first reduce to the case where the variables all lie in the same range.
Write
\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}=\sum_{j\geqslant 0}\sum_{m\in I_{j}}
, where

I_{0}=[-2-r/x,2+r/x]

and

I_{j}=\{\absolutevalue{m}\in(2^{j-1}(2+r/x),2^{j}(2+r/x)]\}

for
j\geqslant 1
.
In view of Lemma
3.1
, the ranges
j\ll 1
will morally dominate.
Let
W_{j}=2^{-j\delta}
for some small
\delta>0
to be chosen.
Hölder’s inequality over
j
gives the inequality

(3.4)

\begin{split}&\absolutevalue{\sum_{m\in\Z} \hat{f}_{r,\chi}(\frac{m}{r}) \hat{f}_{\infty}(\frac{m}{r})}^{4}\\
&\ll\absolutevalue{\sum_{m\equiv-k \bmod{r}} \hat{f}_{r,\chi}(\frac{m}{r}) \hat{f}_{\infty}(\frac{m}{r})}^{4}+\mathcal{W}\sum_{j\geqslant 0}W_{j}^{-3}\absolutevalue{\sum_{\substack{m\in I_j \\ m\not\equiv-k \bmod{r}}} \frac{\chi(k+m)^{-1}}{r^{1/2}}
\hat{f}_{\infty}(\frac{m}{r})}^{4}\end{split}

by (
3.2
),
where
\mathcal{W}:=(\sum_{j\geqslant 0}W_{j})^{3}\ll_{\delta}1
.
The first term will be negligible by (
3.3
).

Let
T_{j}:=2^{j}(2+r/x)
.
In particular, if
j\geqslant 1
, then
\absolutevalue{m}-1\asymp T_{j}
for all
m\in I_{j}
.
By orthogonality and Lemma
3.1
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{\chi}\absolutevalue{\sum_{\substack{m\in I_j \\ m\not\equiv-k \bmod{r}}} \frac{\chi(k+m)^{-1}}{r^{1/2}}
\hat{f}_{\infty}(\frac{m}{r})}^{4}\ll\left(\frac{x}{1+(T_{j}x/r)^{A}\bm{1}_{j\geqslant 1}}\right)^{4}\frac{1}{r^{2}}\mathcal{N}_{4}(I_{j}),

where

\mathcal{N}_{4}(I_{j})
is the number of solutions
(m_{1},m_{2},n_{1},n_{2})\in\{m\in I_{j}:m\not\equiv-k\bmod{r}\}^{4}
to

(k+m_{1})(k+m_{2})\equiv(k+n_{1})(k+n_{2})\bmod{r}
, or equivalently to

(3.5)

k(m_{1}+m_{2}-n_{1}-n_{2})\equiv n_{1}n_{2}-m_{1}m_{2}\bmod r.

We write
S=m_{1}+m_{2}-n_{1}-n_{2}
and
P=n_{1}n_{2}-m_{1}m_{2}
.
We have
kS\equiv P\bmod{r}
.

Lemma 3.2

(Injection)
.

For
(m_{1},m_{2},n_{1},n_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}^{4}
, let

\Phi(m_{1},m_{2},n_{1},n_{2}):=(n_{1}-n_{2}+m_{1}-m_{2},n_{1}-n_{2}-m_{1}+m_{2},m_{1}+m_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}.

Let
S,P\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Then
\Phi
maps the set
\mathcal{A}
injectively into the set
\mathcal{B}
, where

\begin{split}\mathcal{A}&:=\{(m_{1},m_{2},n_{1},n_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}^{4}:m_{1}+m_{2}-n_{1}-n_{2}=S,\quad n_{1}n_{2}-m_{1}m_{2}=P\},\\
\mathcal{B}&:=\{(a,b,c)\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}:ab+2cS=S^{2}-4P\}.\end{split}

Proof.

Let
a,b,c
be the linear forms in
m_{1},m_{2},n_{1},n_{2}

such that
\Phi(m_{1},m_{2},n_{1},n_{2})=(a,b,c)
.
Then the following polynomial identity holds:

(n_{1}-n_{2})^{2}-(m_{1}-m_{2})^{2}+c^{2}=(c-S)^{2}-4P.

Therefore,
\Phi
maps
\mathcal{A}
into
\mathcal{B}
.
Moreover, this map is injective,
because the linear forms
a,b,c,S
are linearly independent over
\mathbb{Q}
.
∎

4.
Point counting

We would like to bound
\mathcal{N}_{4}(I_{j})
defined in (
3.5
).
By Lemma
3.2
, we have

(4.1)

\mathcal{N}_{4}(I_{j})\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\ll T_{j},\;P\ll T_{j}^{2}\\
kS\equiv P\bmod{r}\end{subarray}}N_{S,P}(T_{j})

where

(4.2)

N_{S,P}(T):=\#\{a,b,c\ll T:ab+2cS=S^{2}-4P\}.

It will follow from Theorem
4.4

that
\mathcal{N}_{4}(I_{j})\ll T_{j}^{2}+\frac{T_{j}^{4}}{r}
.

We note that the equation
ab+2cS=S^{2}-4P
implies that for
S\ll T
,

ab+4P\equiv 0\bmod{S},\qquad ab+4P\ll TS+S^{2}\ll TS.

Therefore,

N_{S,P}(T)\leqslant\sum_{a,b\ll T}\bm{1}_{S\mid ab+4P}\bm{1}_{ab+4P\ll TS}.

Lemma 4.1
.

Suppose
1\leqslant u,v\leqslant S\ll T
.
Then

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a,b\ll T\\
(a,b)\equiv(u,v)\bmod{S}\end{subarray}}\bm{1}_{ab+4P\ll TS}\ll\frac{T}{S}\log(2 + \frac{T}{S}).

Proof.

There are
O(1)
choices for
\absolutevalue{a}\leqslant S
with
a\equiv u\bmod{S}
.
Thus the total contribution from
\absolutevalue{a}\leqslant S
is

\ll\#\{b\ll T:b\equiv v\bmod{S}\}\ll T/S.

On the other hand, if
A\geqslant S
, then there are
O(A/S)
choices for
\absolutevalue{a}\in(A,2A]
with
a\equiv u\bmod{S}
,
so the total contribution from the dyadic interval
\absolutevalue{a}\in(A,2A]
is

\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\absolutevalue{a}\in(A,2A]\\
a\equiv u\bmod{S}\end{subarray}}\#\{b\equiv v\bmod{S}:ab+4P\ll TS\}\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\absolutevalue{a}\in(A,2A]\\
a\equiv u\bmod{S}\end{subarray}}(1+\frac{TS}{\absolutevalue{aS}})\ll\frac{A}{S}(1+\frac{T}{A}).

On summing over
A\in\{1,2,4,8,\ldots\}
with
S\leqslant A\ll T
,
we get a total bound of

\ll\frac{T}{S}\log(2 + \frac{T}{S}),

as desired.
∎

For any
S\ll T
and
P\ll T^{2}
with
S\neq 0
, we have

(4.3)

N_{S,P}(T)\leqslant\sum_{a,b\ll T}\bm{1}_{S\mid ab+4P}\bm{1}_{ab+4P\ll TS}\ll\frac{T}{S}\log(2 + \frac{T}{S})N(-4P,S),

by Lemma
4.1
, where

N(d,q):=\#\{(a,b)\in(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{2}:ab\equiv d\bmod{q}\}.

Lemma 4.2

(Point counting)
.

Let
d,q\in\mathbb{Z}
with
q\geqslant 1
.
Then
N(d,q)\leqslant\tau(\operatorname{gcd}(d,q))q
, where
\tau(\cdot)
is the divisor function.

Proof.

If
\operatorname{gcd}(q_{1},q_{2})=1
, then
N(d,q_{1}q_{2})=N(d,q_{1})N(d,q_{2})
by the Chinese remainder theorem.
Therefore, it suffices to prove the lemma when
q
is a prime power.
Say
q=p^{t}
and
\operatorname{gcd}(d,q)=p^{m}
.
Then clearly
t\geqslant m\geqslant 0
.
If
m=0
, then
N(d,q)=\phi(q)\leqslant q
.
If
m=1
, then
N(d,q)=2\phi(q)+\bm{1}_{t=1}\leqslant 2q
.
If
m\geqslant 2
, then
N(d,q)=2\phi(q)+p^{2}N(d/p^{2},q/p^{2})
.
By induction on
m
, it follows that
N(d,q)\leqslant(m+1)q
, as desired.
∎

We next count the number of solutions
(S,P)

to the equation
kS\equiv P\bmod{r}

by exploring the Diophantine approximation property of
\theta
.

Lemma 4.3

(Pigeonhole argument)
.

Assume
\absolutevalue{q\theta-a}\gg\Upsilon(q)
for all
(a,q)\in\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{N}
,
where
\Upsilon
is a decreasing, nonnegative function.
For any integer
r
, let
k=\lfloor r\theta\rfloor
.
Then for any
M,N\in\mathbb{R}
with

r/2>M\geqslant N\geqslant 1,

we have

(4.4)

\Upsilon{\left(\frac{N}{\#\{(S,P)\in[1,N]\times[-M,M]:kS\equiv P\bmod{r}\}}\right)}\ll\frac{M}{r}.

For example, if
\Upsilon(q)=\exp(-q^c)
, for some constant
c>0
, then

(4.5)

\left(\frac{N}{\#\{(S,P)\in[1,N]\times[-M,M]:kS\equiv P\bmod{r}\}}\right)^{c}\gg\log{\left(\frac{r}{M}\right)}-O(1),

so

(4.6)

\#\{S\ll N,\;P\ll M:kS\equiv P\bmod{r}\}\ll\frac{N}{(\log(2+r/M))^{1/c}}.

Proof.

By the pigeonhole principle, there exists
(q,d)\in[1,N]\times[-2M,2M]
such that

kq\equiv d\bmod{r},\qquad q\leqslant\frac{N}{\#\{(S,P)\in[1,N]\times[-M,M]:kS\equiv P\bmod{r}\}}.

For such a pair
(q,d)
,
we have
kq=d+ra
for some
a\in\mathbb{Z}
.
But by definition of
k
, we have
\absolutevalue{r\theta-k}<1
.
Therefore,

\absolutevalue{qr\theta-ra}\leqslant\absolutevalue{qr\theta-kq}+\absolutevalue{kq-ra}<q+\absolutevalue{d}\leqslant N+2M\leqslant 3M,

whence
\absolutevalue{q\theta-a}\leqslant 3M/r
.
Yet by assumption,
\absolutevalue{q\theta-a}\gg\Upsilon(q)
.
Since
\Upsilon(q)
is decreasing, we immediately deduce (
4.4
).
Now (
4.5
) follows from (
4.4
).
Next, (
4.6
) follows from (
4.5
) if
r/M
is sufficiently large.
On the other hand, (
4.6
) is trivial if
r\ll M
.
∎

Theorem 4.4
.

Assume
T\gg 1
and let
N_{S,P}(T)
be defined as in (
4.2
).
Then

(4.7)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\ll T,\;P\ll T^{2}\\
kS\equiv P\bmod{r}\end{subarray}}N_{S,P}(T)\ll T^{2}+\frac{T^{4}}{r}.

Proof.

Since
N_{0,0}(T)\ll T^{2}
and
N_{0,P}(T)\ll T\absolutevalue{P}^{\varepsilon}
if
P\neq 0
,
we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}P\ll T^{2}\\
P\equiv 0\bmod{r}\end{subarray}}N_{0,P}(T)\ll_{\varepsilon}T^{2}+\frac{T^{2}}{r}T^{1+2\varepsilon}.

We may henceforth restrict attention to
S\neq 0
.
By (
4.3
) and Lemma
4.2
, we have

\begin{split}\heartsuit:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\ll T,\;P\ll T^{2}\\
kS\equiv P\bmod{r}\end{subarray}}N_{S,P}(T)&\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\ll T,\;P\ll T^{2}\\
kS\equiv P\bmod{r}\end{subarray}}\frac{T}{\absolutevalue{S}}\log(2 + \frac{T}{\abs{S}})N(-4P,S)\\
&\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\ll T,\;P\ll T^{2}\\
kS\equiv P\bmod{r}\end{subarray}}T\log(2 + \frac{T}{\abs{S}})\tau(\operatorname{gcd}(P,S)),\end{split}

where in the last step we use the sub-multiplicativity property
\tau(mn)\leqslant\tau(m)\tau(n)
with
m=4
.
Upon writing
(S,P)=(gS^{\prime},gP^{\prime})
with
g=\operatorname{gcd}(S,P)\geqslant 1
,
and summing
\tau(g)
over dyadic intervals
[G/2,G)
, we get

(4.8)

\begin{split}\heartsuit&\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}G\in\{2,4,8,\ldots\}\\
G\ll T\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\ll T/G,\;P\ll T^{2}/G\\
kS\equiv P\bmod{r}\end{subarray}}T\log(2 + \frac{T}{\abs{GS}})(G\log{G})\\
&+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}G\in\{2r,4r,8r,\ldots\}\\
G\ll T\end{subarray}}\sum_{S\ll T/G,\;P\ll T^{2}/G}T\log(2 + \frac{T}{\abs{GS}})\tau(r)(\frac{G}{r}\log{\frac{G}{r}}),\end{split}

where the last line accounts for the contribution from
g\equiv 0\bmod{r}
.
The last line is

\begin{split}&\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}G\in\{2r,4r,8r,\ldots\}\\
G\ll T\end{subarray}}\sum_{S\ll T/G,\;P\ll T^{2}/G}T(\frac{T}{\absolutevalue{GS}})^{0.1}\tau(r)(\frac{G}{r})^{1.1}\\
&\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}G\in\{2r,4r,8r,\ldots\}\\
G\ll T\end{subarray}}T(T/G)(T^{2}/G)\tau(r)(\frac{G}{r})^{1.1}\\
&\ll T(T/r)(T^{2}/r)\tau(r)\ll\frac{T^{4}}{r}.\end{split}

Let
\mathcal{S}\in\{2,4,8,\ldots\}
with
\mathcal{S}\ll T/G
.
If
T^{2}/G\gg r
, then trivially

(4.9)

\#\{S\asymp\mathcal{S},\;P\ll T^{2}/G:kS\equiv P\bmod{r}\}\ll\mathcal{S}\frac{T^{2}/G}{r},

and otherwise, by Lemma
4.3
with
c=1/3
we have

(4.10)

\#\{S\asymp\mathcal{S},\;P\ll T^{2}/G:kS\equiv P\bmod{r}\}\ll\frac{\mathcal{S}}{(\log(2+rG/T^2))^{3}}.

Upon summing over all possible choices for
\mathcal{S}
, we conclude that
the first line of (
4.8
) is

\begin{split}&\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}G,\mathcal{S}\in\{2,4,8,\ldots\}\\
\mathcal{S}G\ll T\end{subarray}}T\left(\frac{T}{\absolutevalue{G\mcal{S}}}\right)^{0.1}(G\log{G})\mathcal{S}\left(\frac{T^{2}}{rG}+\frac{\bm{1}_{G\gg T^{2}/r}}{(\log(2+rG/T^2))^{3}}\right)\\
&\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}G\in\{2,4,8,\ldots\}\\
G\ll T\end{subarray}}T^{2}(\log{G})\left(\frac{T^{2}}{rG}+\frac{\bm{1}_{G\gg T^{2}/r}}{(\log(2+rG/T^2))^{3}}\right)\\
&\ll\frac{T^{4}}{r}+T^{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}G\in\{2,4,8,\ldots\}\\
G\gg T^{2}/r\end{subarray}}\frac{\log{G}}{(\log(2+rG/T^2))^{3}}\\
&\ll\frac{T^{4}}{r}+T^{2}\log(2+\frac{T^2}{r}),\end{split}

by Lemma
4.5
below, applied with
G=2^{j}
and
2^{s}\asymp T^{2}/r
.
But
\log(2+\frac{T^2}{r})\ll 2+\frac{T^{2}}{r}
, so Theorem
4.4
follows.
∎

Lemma 4.5
.

For any
s\in\mathbb{Z}
, we have

\mathcal{D}(s):=\sum_{j\geqslant\max(1,s)}\frac{j}{\max(1,j-s)^{3}}\ll\max(1,s).

Proof.

If
s\leqslant 1
, then

\mathcal{D}(s)\leqslant\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\frac{j}{\max(1,j-1)^{3}}\ll 1=\max(1,s).

If
s\geqslant 1
, then a change of variables
j\mapsto j+s
gives

\mathcal{D}(s)=\sum_{j\geqslant 0}\frac{j+s}{\max(1,j)^{3}}\ll 1+s\ll\max(1,s).

Each case is satisfactory.
∎

1.1
in the case
\sqrt{r}\leqslant x\leqslant r
.
By (
3.4
),
(
3.3
),

4.4

(4.11)

\mathcal{M}_{4}:=\mathbb{E}_{\chi}\absolutevalue{ \sum_{n\in\Z} \chi(n) e(n\theta) w(\frac{n}{x})}^{4}\ll(x^{1-A})^{4}+\frac{x^{4}}{r^{2}}\sum_{j\geqslant 0}\frac{2^{3j\delta}}{1+(T_{j}x/r)^{4A}\bm{1}_{j\geqslant 1}}\left(T_{j}^{2}+\frac{T_{j}^{4}}{r}\right),

.
