Nonhomogeneous boundary condition for spectral non-local operators
Ivan Biočić, Vanja Wagner

Abstract
We study semilinear non-local elliptic problems driven by spectral-type operators of the form ψ ( − L | D ) \psi(-L_{|D}) in a bounded C 1 , 1 C^{1,1} domain D ⊂ ℝ d D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d} with a nonhomogeneous boundary condition. Here ψ \psi is a Bernstein function satisfying a weak scaling condition at infinity, and L | D L_{|D} is the generator of a killed Lévy process. This general framework covers and extends the theory of the interpolated fractional Laplacian. A key novelty in this setting is the analysis of the nonhomogeneous boundary condition formulated in terms of the Poisson potential with respect to the d − 1 d-1 Hausdorff measure on ∂ D \partial D . We establish sharp boundary estimates for Green and Poisson potentials, introduce a weak L 1 L^{1} trace-like boundary operator, and provide existence results for solutions under quite general nonlinearities, including sign-changing and non-monotone cases. The methodology combines stochastic process techniques, potential theory, and spectral analysis, and expresses the boundary behavior of the solution in terms of the renewal function and the distance to the boundary, suggesting a possible unified treatment of semilinear boundary problems in non-local settings.

Introduction

In this article, we solve the nonhomogeneous non-local semilinear problem of the elliptic type in a bounded
C^{1,1}
domain
D\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{rcll}\psi(-L_{|D})u(x)&=&f(x,u(x)),&\textrm{in $D$},\\
\frac{u}{P^{\psi}_{D}\sigma}&=&\zeta,&\textrm{on $\partial D$}.\end{array}

Here
\psi
is a Bernstein function satisfying a certain weak scaling condition, the operator
L_{|D}
is a non-local operator which is a generator of the Lévy process killed upon exiting the domain
D
, while the function
P^{\psi}_{D}\sigma
is the reference function for the behaviour of the solution at the boundary.

The best-known example of the operator covered by our setting is the so-called interpolated fractional Laplacian
\psi(-L_{|D})=\left((-\Delta)^{\beta/2}_{|D}\right)^{\alpha/2}
where
\psi(\lambda)=\lambda^{\alpha/2}
,
\alpha\in(0,2)
, and where
L_{|D}=-(-\Delta)_{|D}^{\beta/2}
,
\beta\in(0,2)
, is the restricted
\frac{\beta}{2}
-fractional Laplacian. However, our setting covers a much wider class of operators, including the fractional relativistic operators, various tempered operators, etc. For more details, we refer the reader to the condition
(A1)
and the discussion in the paragraph below it.

The goal of this article is to take a step beyond the fractional framework and extend results to more general non-local operators, while at the same time covering the fractional case. The novelty of this article is the nonhomogeneous boundary theory in a quite broad generality, extending the interpolated fractional setting: the function
\psi
is a complete Bernstein function that satisfies a weak scaling condition at infinity, and the operator
L_{|D}
is a generator of a killed subordinate Brownian motion, i.e.
L_{|D}=-\phi(-\Delta)_{|D}
, where the functions
\phi
also satisfies the weak scaling condition at infinity. Moreover, even in the interpolated fractional Laplacian case, our results on the existence of solutions to the semilinear problem with superlinear functions
f
are new.

It is a non-local phenomenon that even the solutions to the linear equations blow up at the boundary - a behaviour which is also exhibited in our setting. By establishing the boundary behaviour of Green and Poisson potentials, we arrive at the abstract boundary condition
\frac{u}{P^{\psi}_{D}\sigma}=\zeta
, which can be considered in a pointwise sense for sufficiently regular solutions, as a limit at the boundary, while in general it can be viewed as a weak
L^{1}
trace-like boundary operator. Here
P^{\psi}_{D}(x,z)
is the Poisson kernel of
\psi(-L_{|D})
(i.e. the generalized normal derivative of the Green function of
\psi(-L_{|D})
) which is the cornerstone of the integral representation of harmonic functions with respect to
\psi(-L_{|D})
, while
P^{\psi}_{D}\sigma
denotes the Poisson potential of the
(d-1)
dimensional Hausdorff measure on
\partial D
– the function that describes the rate at which harmonic functions blow-up at the boundary.

In this setting, we assume that the nonlinearity
f:D\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfies
|f(x,t)|\leq q(x)\Lambda(t)
, for a locally bounded function
q
and a non-decreasing function
\Lambda
. This allows us to deal with positive, negative, sign-changing, monotone, and non-monotone nonlinearities. The main goal of this article is to show the existence and continuity properties of solutions to (
1
) for this wide class of nonlinearities.

The operator
\psi(-L_{|D})
can be written in its spectral form

\displaystyle\psi(-L_{|D})u=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\psi(\lambda_{j})\widehat{u}_{j}\varphi_{j},

where
(\lambda_{j},\varphi_{j})
are the eigenpairs of
L_{|D}=-\phi(-\Delta)_{|D}
. However, this definition requires highly integrable data (
L^{2}
theory), so it is less restrictive to look at the operator in its principal value form:

\displaystyle\psi(-L_{|D})u(x)=\textrm{P.V.}\int_{D}\big(u(y)-u(x)\big)J_{D}(x,y)dy+\kappa(x)u(x).

Here, the jumping kernel exhibits the interior behaviour of the same order as the kernel
j_{\psi\circ\phi}
of the full operator
\psi(-L)=-\psi\circ\phi(-\Delta)
. On the other hand, the jumping kernel
J_{D}
always continuously vanishes at the boundary, with possible different types of nonlinear orders depending on the relationship of
\phi
and
\psi
, see Remark
3.6
. To illustrate this behaviour in the classical case, note that the jumping kernel of the restricted fractional Laplacian
-(-\Delta)_{|D}^{\alpha/2}
does not continuously vanish at the boundary, while the jumping kernel of the spectral fractional Laplacian
-(-\Delta_{|D})^{\alpha/2}
vanishes at the boundary with a linear order. While the jumping kernel of the interpolated fractional Laplacian behaves like the kernel of the restricted (and spectral) fractional Laplacian in the interior, it decays at the boundary with a fractional order. This mixed jumps structure makes the interpolated non-local operators of spectral type particularly relevant for applications in fractional modeling.

In what follows, we briefly present the principal references that are relevant for our work. In
[
23
]
Chan, Gómez-Castro and Vázqez gave a unified approach to the large eigenvalue problem (i.e. for the source term
f(x,t)=\lambda t
) with nonhomogeneous boundary condition for a wide class of fractional-type operators in a domain. The framework included the restricted fractional Laplacian and the interpolated fractional Laplacian as examples, as well as a certain class of Schrödinger operators. Their approach assumed sharp bounds and controlled behavior of the Green function (in terms of fractional powers), both in the interior and on the boundary, as well as the existence of the Poisson/Martin kernel (the hardest assumption to verify, see
[
23
, Remark 2.5]
), from which they develop integrability theory and obtain large eigenvalues. In our approach, by finding the sharp behaviour of the Green potential and proving the non-trivial result on the existence of the Poisson kernel, we are able to extend part of their results to a wider class of interpolated non-local operators and non-linear source terms. Furthermore, the preparatory work has been done which should enable us to move to Schrödinger type problems in future work.

In the work by Huynh and Nguyen
[
42
]
, similarly to
[
23
]
, the authors gave a unified approach to homogeneous semilinear problems for fractional-type operators (including the interpolated fractional Laplacian), again starting from the assumption on the sharp behaviour of the Green function. Although their source term
f
is a quite general non-decreasing function, unlike
[
23
]
, the authors consider only the semilinear problems with homogeneous boundary conditions. This simplifies the theory, since there is no involvement of the Poisson potentials, i.e., harmonic functions. On the other hand, our focused approach covers more general nonlinearities, as well as the nonhomogeneous boundary condition. Moreover, by using the general Dirichlet form theory instead of the fractional-type results, we are able to extend Kato’s inequality from
[
42
]
to our setting.

The influential work by Bonforte, Figalli and Vázquez
[
21
]
also employed a unified approach within the fractional framework, including the case of the interpolated fractional Laplacian. Their results addressed the homogeneous boundary condition, with a power-like nonlinearity
f(x,t)\sim t^{p}
. Furthermore, they studied Hölder regularity of solutions and obtained qualitative boundary estimates, as well as the change of the regularity behaviour in different regimes with respect to the order of the nonlinearity. It is worth noting that obtaining exact regularity up to the boundary, even in the setting of the interpolated fractional Laplacian, would be of significant interest. This is because the standard tool for establishing such results is the corresponding boundary Harnack principle, which in this setting does not have to hold, see
[
47
]
.

Another quite general approach to semilinear non-local problems was introduced by Klimsiak and Rozkosz in
[
52
]
, where they used a probabilistic (martingale) method, and orthogonal projections to solve general semilinear problems in locally compact separable spaces. Although they cover a large class of operators, without the knowledge of the structure of harmonic functions with respect to the operator, their unified theory covers only the homogeneous boundary condition
\zeta=0
in (

A general approach to semilinear problems, covering a wide range of elliptic and parabolic differential operators of second order, as well as integro-differential operators, using balayage techniques has been presented recently by Bogdan and Hansen in
[
40
]
, where they focus on the existence of positive solutions with increasing nonlinearities. Although they cover a wide range of existence results, our focus here lies more on fine properties of the explosion rates of solutions, even without the monotonicity assumption.

To develop our theory for the operator

[

where they proved Hopf’s lemma for

[

[
50

, Kim, Song, and Vondraček investigated potential-theoretic properties of the process generated by

Our approach to studying semilinear problems is motivated by the technique used for the spectral fractional Laplacian in

, and for more general non-local spectral-type operators in

We also note

Main results

To make the exposition clearer, here we state claims only for the special case of the interpolated fractional Laplacian
\psi(-L_{|D})=\left((-\Delta)^{\beta/2}_{|D}\right)^{\alpha/2}
.

Semilinear problems:
The main focus of this article is the existence and uniqueness of weak-dual solutions to semilinear problems in Section
8
. The cornerstone result is a method of sub/supersolutions for the homogeneous problem (Theorem
8.3
).
To investigate the nonhomogeneous problem for non-positive nonlinearities, we use a potential-theoretic approach of harmonic function approximation by an increasing sequence of Green potentials, see Theorem
8.4
. In the fractional setting, Theorem
8.7
shows that for
f(x,t)=-\delta_{D}(x)^{\theta}|t|^{p}
, we can find the solution to the semilinear problem (
1
) if and only if
p<\frac{1+\frac{2\theta}{2+\beta}}{1-\frac{\beta\alpha}{2+\beta}}
. In order to apply this potential-theoretic approach, we established the required representation of harmonic functions and the equivalence with the probabilistic notion of harmonicity, see Theorem
5.9
. Furthermore, the nonhomogeneous problem with non-negative nonlinearities was investigated in its full generality in Theorem
8.5
. This result, translated into the fractional setting in Theorem
8.8
, for
f(x,t)=m\delta_{D}(x)^{\theta}|t|^{p}
, implies the existence of a solution if and only if
p<\frac{1+\frac{2\theta}{2+\beta}}{1-\frac{\beta\alpha}{2+\beta}}
for small enough
m>0
. The general nonhomogeneous problem with a signed nonlinearity was considered in Theorem
8.6
, with its fractional analogue given in Theorem
8.9
.

The boundary condition:

In order to state the nonhomogeneous semilinear problem, it was essential to show that the Poisson kernel
P^{\psi}_{D}(x,z)
, given as the generalised normal derivative of the Green function
-\partial_{V}G_{D}^{\psi}(x,z)
, is well-defined, see Proposition
5.1
. Such results are case-specific and highly non-trivial, and generally represent one of the assumptions in recent works applying a unified approach (e.g.
[
23
,
4
]
). The boundary condition is then given in terms of the reference function
P^{\psi}_{D}\sigma
, whose boundary behaviour is expressed in Proposition
5.2
via the corresponding renewal function
V
and distance to the boundary
\delta_{D}
.

Furthermore, we provide the optimal weighted
L^{1}
space, given in terms of the renewal function
V
, for the distributional formulation of the semilinear problem (
1
), see Subsection
4.1
, (
4.1
) and Definition
7.5
. The aforementioned results, together with the analysis of the weak boundary behaviour of general Poisson and Green potentials in Propositions
6.1
and
6.2
, enabled us to formulate the boundary condition in (

Preliminaries

Let
W=(W_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a Brownian motion in n
\mathbb{R}^{d}

\xi\mapsto|\xi|^{2}
. Let

is a non-negative (i.e. non-decreasing) Lévy process such that

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left[e^{-\lambda S_{t}}\right]

\displaystyle=e^{-\phi(\lambda)t},\quad\lambda\geq 0,\,t\geq 0.

The exponent
\phi
is a Bernstein function of the form

\displaystyle\phi(\lambda)

\displaystyle=b\lambda+\int_{0}^{\infty}(1-e^{-\lambda t})\mu(dt),\quad\lambda\geq 0,

and completely determines the distribution of the subordinator

, for details see
[

. The parameter

is called the drift and the non-negative measure

\int_{0}^{\infty}(1\wedge t)\mu(dt)<\infty

The process

defined by

, called the subordinate Brownian motion, is a Lévy process with the characteristic exponent
\xi\mapsto\phi(|\xi|^{2})

is a pure-jump Lévy process. Mo rover, we assume:

(A1)

The exponent

\displaystyle\underline{a}_{\phi}\lambda^{\underline{\delta}_{\phi}}\phi(t)\leq\phi(\lambda t)\leq\overline{a}_{\phi}\lambda^{\overline{\delta}_{\phi}}\phi(t),\quad\lambda\geq 1,\,t\geq 1.

(2.1)

This assumption drives the small space-time behaviour of

\displaystyle\widetilde{a}_{1}\left(\frac{t}{s}\right)^{\underline{\delta}_{\phi}}\leq\frac{V(t)}{V(s)}\leq\widetilde{a}_{2}\left(\frac{t}{s}\right)^{\overline{\delta}_{\phi}},\quad 0<s\leq t\leq 1.

(2.8)

The large-time sharp bounds for the transition density
p_{D}(t,x,y)
can be given in terms of the smallest eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}>0
of

,

\displaystyle p_{D}(t,x,y)

\displaystyle\asymp e^{-\lambda_{1}t}V(\delta_{D}(x))V(\delta_{D}(y))\quad x,y\in D,

see Section
3
. The killed process
X^{D}
has a

-semigroup given for

,

, and

\displaystyle P^{D}_{t}f(x)=\mathds{E}_{x}[f(X^{D}_{t})]=\mathds{E}_{x}[f(X_{t});t<\tau_{D}]=\int_{D}p_{D}(t,x,y)f(y)dy,

where we naturally extend the definition of a function

to
\partial

has the strong Feller property, i.e.
P_{t}^{D}L^{\infty}(D)\subset C_{b}(D)

. Since

and for

\displaystyle L_{|D}u(x)=Lu(x)=\textrm{P.V.}\int_{D}\big(u(y)-u(x)\big)j_{\phi}(|x-y|)dy-u(x)\kappa(x),

where the function

is the space of all

. However, there is a unique extension of

to a

. Nevertheless, throughout this paper we use the same notation for the semigroups (as well as their infinitesimal generators) in both spaces, when appropriate, since they are equal a.e. on

.

Using the subordination principle, the transition density of

, denoted by

\displaystyle r_{D}(t,x,y)=\mathds{E}[p_{D}(T_{t},x,y)]=\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{D}(s,x,y)\mathds{P}(T_{t}\in ds),\quad x,y\in D,\,t>0.

The semigroup of

Green functions

Since
D
is bounded, the process
X^{D}
is transient and has the Green function

given by

\displaystyle G_{D}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{D}(t,x,y)dt.

The upper bound for

is known: for

it holds that

\displaystyle G_{D}(x,y)\lesssim\frac{1}{|x-y|^{d}V(|x-y|)^{-2}},

(2.11)

where the constant depends on

,
\phi
and

[

\displaystyle G_{D}f(x)\coloneqq\int_{D}G_{D}(x,y)f(y)dy=\mathds{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\tau_{D}}f(X_{t})dt\right],\quad f\geq 0.

\displaystyle G_{D}^{\psi}(x,y)\coloneqq\int_{0}^{\infty}r_{D}(t,x,y)dt=\int_{0}^{\infty}p_{D}(t,x,y){\swabfamily u}(t)dt,

where

denotes the density of the potential measure of
T
, i.e.

U(A)\coloneqq\mathds{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbf{1}_{A}(T_{t})dt\right]=\int_{A}{\swabfamily u}(t)dt
,
A\in\mathcal{B}([0,\infty))
. Note that the existence of a non-increasing potential density
{\swabfamily u}

\psi
is a complete Bernstein function, and in particular a special Bernstein function, see
[
59
, Chapter 11]
.

As before, the probabilistic interpretation of the Green operator

is

\displaystyle G_{D}^{\psi}f(x)\coloneqq\int_{D}G_{D}^{\psi}(x,y)f(y)dy=\mathds{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}f(Y_{t})dt\right],\quad f\geq 0,

and the sharp bounds for

are known for all

\displaystyle G_{D}^{\psi}(x,y)\asymp\left(\frac{V(\delta_{D}(x))}{V(|x-y|)}\wedge 1\right)\left(\frac{V(\delta_{D}(y))}{V(|x-y|)}\wedge 1\right)\frac{1}{|x-y|^{d}\psi(V(|x-y|)^{-2})},

(2.13)

see
[

.
By standard calculation, it is easy to show that

is jointly continuous in the extended sense in

, see Lemma
A.3
.

There is a strong connection between the Green functions of

and

and

. In order to establish this connection, we introduce the conjugate Bernstein function

]
. Since

(A1)
, the function
\psi^{*}
satisfies

with constants
\underline{a}_{\psi^{*}}=1/\overline{a}_{\psi}
,

generates a subordinator

(and the accompanying potential theoretical objects) obtained by subordinating the process

by

,

and

.

Lemma 2.1
.

For every

it holds that

\displaystyle\int_{D}G_{D}^{\psi}(x,\xi)G_{D}^{\psi^{*}}(\xi,y)d\xi=G_{D}(x,y).

3
Operator
\psi(-L_{|D})

In
[
27
, Theorem 2.1]
it was shown that the operators
P^{D}_{t}
are compact for all
t>0
, hence they have a discrete spectrum, which implies that there is an orthonormal basis
(\varphi_{j})_{j}
on
L^{2}(D)
consisting of
eigenfunctions of
L_{|D}
(as well as
P^{D}_{t}
):

\displaystyle L_{|D}\varphi_{j}=-\lambda_{j}\varphi_{j},\quad\text{in $L^{2}(D)$,}

(3.1)

where
0<\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\lambda_{3}\leq\dots
satisfy

\displaystyle\lambda_{j}\asymp\phi(j^{2/d}),\quad j\in\mathbb{N}.

The last assertion holds by
[
26
, Theorem 4.5]
and the fact that the eigenvalues of the Dirichlet Laplacian
\Delta_{|D}
behave like
j^{2/d}
by Weyl’s law, see also
[
30
, Theorem 2.7]
.

In the case of the fractional or classical Laplacian, the eigenfunctions
\varphi_{j}
are bounded in
D
and sufficiently regular in the suitable Hölder space, even up to the boundary, with the bounds given in terms of the eigenvalues. When
L
is the fractional Laplacian, this was proved in
[
35
]
. Following the approach in
[
35
]
, in Appendix
A.2
we prove the following analogous result in our more general non-local setting and for generalized Hölder spaces.

Theorem 3.1
.

For every
j\in\mathbb{N}
we have
\varphi_{j}\in C_{0}(D)\cap C^{V}(D)
and there exist
constants
C_{1},\,C_{2},\,C_{3},\,k,\,\varepsilon>0
, depending only on
d
,
D
and
\phi
, such that

\displaystyle\|\varphi_{j}\|_{L^{\infty}(D)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\lambda_{j}^{k-1},

(3.2)

\displaystyle\|\varphi_{j}\|_{C^{V}(D)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}\lambda_{j}^{k},

(3.3)

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\varphi_{j}}{V(\delta_{D})}\right\|_{C^{\varepsilon}(D)}

\displaystyle\leq C_{3}\lambda_{j}^{k}.

(3.4)

The principal eigenvalue
\varphi_{1}
can be chosen such that
\varphi_{1}>0
, and then by
[
14
, Theorem 3.2 & Theorem 3.3]
it holds that

\displaystyle\varphi_{1}\asymp V(\delta_{D})\quad\text{in $D$}.

(3.5)

Define the operator
\psi(-L_{|D})
by

\displaystyle\psi(-L_{|D})u=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\psi(\lambda_{j})\widehat{u}_{j}\varphi_{j},

where
\widehat{u}_{j}
are the coefficients of
u\in L^{2}(D)
with respect to the orthonormal basis
(\varphi_{j})_{j}
, with domain

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(\psi(-L_{|D}))=\{u=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\widehat{u}_{j}\varphi_{j}\in L^{2}(D):\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\psi(\lambda_{j})^{2}|\widehat{u}_{j}|^{2}<\infty\}.

(3.6)

It can be easily seen that the space
\mathcal{D}(\psi(-L_{|D}))
is a Banach space equipped with the norm
\|u\|^{2}_{\mathcal{D}(\psi(-L_{|D}))}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\psi(\lambda_{j})^{2}|\widehat{u}_{j}|^{2}
, and that
\psi(-L_{|D})
is an unbounded operator in
L^{2}(D)\to L^{2}(D)
. Further,
\psi(-L_{|D})
has a bounded inverse

\displaystyle\psi(-L_{|D})^{-1}f=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\psi(\lambda_{j})}\widehat{f}_{j}\varphi_{j},\quad f\in L^{2}(D).

In the next proposition, we prove that the operator can be seen as the infinitesimal generator of the
L^{2}(D)
semigroup generated by
Y
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
f\in L^{2}(D)
. For a.e.
x\in D
, it holds that

\displaystyle\psi(-L_{|D})^{-1}f(x)=G_{D}^{\psi}f(x).

In particular,
G_{D}^{\psi}f\in L^{1}(D,V(\delta_{D}(x))dx)
.

Proof.

The proof of this claim is very similar to
[
10
, Proposition 2.2]
and
[
3
, Lemma 11]
so we keep the proof concise and put the emphasis on the differences.

First, for
f=\varphi_{1}\geq 0
, by (
2.12
), (
3.1
), and Tonelli’s theorem, we have for a.e.
x\in D

\displaystyle G_{D}^{\psi}\varphi_{1}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\swabfamily u}(t)\int_{D}p^{D}(t,x,y)\varphi_{1}(y)dydt=\int_{0}^{\infty}{\swabfamily u}(t)e^{-\lambda_{1}t}\varphi_{1}(x)dt=\psi(-L_{|D})^{-1}\varphi_{1}(x),

where the last equality follows from the connection between the potential density and the Laplace exponent of the subordinator
T
, see
[
59
, Eq. (5.20)]
.

By (
3.4
) and (
3.5
) we get
|\varphi_{j}|\lesssim V(\delta_{D})
, so by Fubini’s theorem and the previous calculation, we obtain
G_{D}^{\psi}f=\psi(-L_{|D})^{-1}f
a.e. in
D
for all
f\in\textrm{span}\{\varphi_{j}:j\in\mathbb{N}\}
.

The operator
\mathds{G}
on
\textrm{span}\{\varphi_{j}:j\in\mathbb{N}\}
given by
\mathds{G}f=G_{D}^{\psi}f
is an isometry on
\textrm{span}\{\varphi_{j}:j\in\mathbb{N}\}
, with the standard
L^{2}(D)
norm in the domain, and the norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{D}(\psi(-L_{|D}))}
in the codomain. Hence,
\mathds{G}
can be uniquely extended to an isometry on
L^{2}(D)
, which corresponds to
\psi(-L_{|D})^{-1}
since both operators agree on
\textrm{span}\{\varphi_{j}:j\in\mathbb{N}\}
.

Take
f=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\widehat{f}_{j}\varphi_{j}\in L^{2}(D)
and the approximating sequence
f_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{f}_{j}\varphi_{j}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Obviously,
\mathds{G}f_{n}\to\mathds{G}f
in
L^{2}(D)
, and by

\displaystyle\int_{D}\int_{D}G_{D}^{\psi}(x,y)|f(y)-f_{n}(y)|dy\varphi_{1}(x)dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\psi(\lambda_{1})}\int_{D}|f(y)-f_{n}(y)|\varphi_{1}(y)dy

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\|f-f_{n}\|_{L^{2}(D)}}{\psi(\lambda_{1})},

we have that
G_{D}^{\psi}f=\mathds{G}f=\psi(-L_{|D})^{-1}f
a.e. in
D
and that
G_{D}^{\psi}(x,\cdot)f(\cdot)\in L^{1}(D,V(\delta_{D}(x))dx)
since
\varphi_{1}\asymp V(\delta_{D})
by (
3.5
).
∎

Remark 3.3
.

(i)

We defined the operator
\psi(-L_{|D})

semigroup of

of

\displaystyle R^{D}_{t}\varphi_{j}=\int_{0}^{\infty}P^{D}_{s}\varphi_{j}\mathds{P}(T_{t}\in ds)=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda_{j}s}\mathds{P}(T_{t}\in ds)=e^{-\psi(\lambda_{j})t},

has the same spectral form as

in

as in (

), and it can be viewed as the Bochner subordinated operator

. For an overview of the general theory, see e.g.
[

. Further, the relation

for

from Proposition

shows that

is indeed the potential operator of the infinitesimal generator of

in the proper sense in

.

(ii)

Since

can be viewed as the Bochner subordinated
L_{|D}

[

it follows that

is in

, see Lemma

. In the case when

is a local operator, e.g. when

, the spectral coefficients of

with respect to

[

[

for a more general setting. However, the analogous calculation fails in the case when

functions was heavily used in
[

. In this paper, we proceed with an alternative approach, which allows us to obtain the analogous result without assumptions on the decay rate.

Note that analogous results obtained for the operator

hold also for

, since
\psi
and

satisfy the same assumptions. Similarly to Lemma

which connects the Green operators, the following connection between the operators

,

was proved in

Proposition 3.4
.

For every
f\in L^{2}(D)
which is in the
L^{2}(D)
-domain of the operator

L_{|D}
, we have

\displaystyle\psi(-L_{|D})\circ\psi^{*}(-L_{|D})f=-L_{|D}f,\quad\textit{a.e. in $D$.}

In what follows, we obtain the integral representation of the operator

Proposition 3.5
.

For
u\in C^{1,1}(D)\cap\mathcal{D}(L_{|D})
it holds for almost all

\displaystyle\psi(-L_{|D})u(x)=\textrm{P.V.}\int_{D}\big(u(x)-u(y)\big)J_{D}(x,y)dy+\kappa(x)u(x),

\displaystyle p(t-\tau_{D},X_{\tau_{D}},y)\lesssim 1\wedge t,\quad y\in B(x,1\wedge(\delta_{D}(x)/4)),\quad\text{$\mathds{P}_{x}$-a.s.}

4
Green potentials

In this section, we study the functions
u=G_{D}^{\psi}f
, their integrability, and sharp boundary behaviour. The main goal is to prove that
G_{D}^{\psi}f
distributionally solves
\psi(-L_{|D})u=f
in the largest class of functions
f
such that
G_{D}^{\psi}f
is well defined, see Theorem
4.7
.

Lemma 4.1
.

It holds that

G_{D}^{\psi}V(\delta_{D})(x)\asymp V(\delta_{D}(x)),\quad x\in D.

Furthermore,
G_{D}^{\psi}f
is finite a.e. in
D
if and
only if
f\in L^{1}(D,V(\delta_{D}(x))dx)
. Additionally, there exists
C=C(d,D,\phi,\psi)>0
such that

\displaystyle\|G_{D}^{\psi}f\|_{L^{1}(D,V(\delta_{D}(x))dx)}\leq C\|f\|_{L^{1}(D,V(\delta_{D}(x))dx)},\quad f\in L^{1}(D,V(\delta_{D}(x))dx).

Proof.

By Proposition
3.2
we have that
G_{D}^{\psi}\varphi_{1}=\psi(\lambda_{1})^{-1}\varphi_{1}
, so the first claim follows from
(
3.5
). Moreover, the Fubini theorem then implies that if
f\in L^{1}(D,V(\delta_{D}(x))dx)
, then
G_{D}^{\psi}f\in L^{1}(D,V(\delta_{D}(x))dx)
, hence
G_{D}^{\psi}f
is finite a.e. Assume now that
f\geq 0
and
G_{D}^{\psi}f(x)
is finite at some point
x\in D
. Then, by the strong Markov property of
Y
,

\displaystyle G_{D}^{\psi}f(x)

\displaystyle\geq\mathds{E}_{x}\left[\mathds{E}_{Y_{\tau_{B(x,\varepsilon)}}}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}f(Y_{t})dt\right]\right]=\int_{D\setminus B(x,\varepsilon)}G_{D}^{\psi}f(y)\mathds{P}_{x}(Y_{\tau_{B(x,\varepsilon)}}\in dy)

for and
\varepsilon\in(0,\delta_{D}(x))
. Since the distribution of
Y_{\tau_{B(x,\varepsilon)}}
is positive and locally bounded from below for all
y\in D\setminus\overline{B(x,\varepsilon)}
, see
[
47
, p. 146-148]
,
G_{D}^{\psi}f
is finite a.e. on
y\in D\setminus\overline{B(x,\varepsilon)}
. Since
\varepsilon\in(0,\delta_{D}(x))
, we conclude
G_{D}^{\psi}f
is finite a.e. on
D
. Now, take any such
x\in D
, and note that by (
2.13
) we have
G_{D}^{\psi}(x,y)\geq c(\delta_{D}(x),d,D,\phi,\psi)V(\delta_{D}(y))
, for
y\in D\setminus B(x,\delta_{D}(x)/2)
. Hence

\displaystyle\infty>G_{D}^{\psi}f(x)\gtrsim c(\delta_{D}(x),d,D,\phi,\psi)\int_{D\setminus B(x,\delta_{D}(x)/2)}V(\delta_{D}(y))f(y)dy,

(4.1)

and by repeating the same reasoning as in (
4.1
), it follows
f\in L^{1}(D,V(\delta_{D}(x))dx)
. The claim for a general
f
follows by
f=f^{+}-f^{-}
.
∎

Next, we present the sharp behaviour of Green potentials of a large class of functions, which is new even in the interpolated fractional Laplacian case. In classical fractional settings, this property is known in the form
G_{D}\delta_{D}^{\gamma}\asymp\delta_{D}^{\eta}
, see
[

]
and
[

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
U:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
be such that the following conditions hold:

(

)

\int_{0}^{1}U(t)V(t)\,dt<\infty
;

(

(

is bounded from

Then

, and for all

(4.2)

The proof of Theorem

, Proposition 4.1]

(

)

As mentioned before, the claims of Theorem

and Corollary

Corollary 4.4
.

Assume that
\phi(\lambda)=\lambda^{\beta/2}
and

. Let

. Then

\displaystyle G_{D}^{\psi}(\delta_{D}^{\kappa})\asymp\begin{cases}\delta_{D}(x)^{\beta/2},&\kappa>\frac{\beta}{2}(1-\alpha),\\

4.1
Homogeneous solutions to
\psi(-L_{|D})u=f

Our final goal of this section is to show that Green potentials are distributional solutions to the problem
\psi(-L_{|D})u=f
. Recall that a function
u
is a distributional solution to
\psi(-L_{|D})u=f
if

\displaystyle\int_{D}u(x)\psi(-L_{|D})\xi(x)dx=\int_{D}f(x)\xi(x)dx,\quad\xi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(D).

(4.3)

Therefore, to determine the appropriate sharp weighted
L^{1}
space for distributional solutions, we first need to determine the sharp boundary behaviour of
\psi(-L_{|D})\xi
, for
\xi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(D)
, which is governed by
J_{D}
. Indeed, by using the representation (
3.7
), for
\xi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(D)
and
\delta_{D}(x)\leq\frac{1}{2}\textrm{dist}(D^{c},\supp\xi)
we get that

|\psi(-L_{|D})\xi(x)|\lesssim\int_{\supp\xi}J_{D}(x,y)|\xi(y)|dy,

where the equality holds if
\xi
is also non-negative.
Although sharp bounds for
J_{D}(x,y)
are in general not known, see Remark
3.6
, we can apply Proposition
3.5
, (
2.7
) and (
2.9
) to get that for
y\in\supp\xi
and
x\in D
such that
\delta_{D}(x)\leq\frac{1}{2}\textrm{dist}(D^{c},\supp\xi)

\displaystyle J_{D}(x,y)

\displaystyle\asymp\int_{0}^{\textrm{diam}D}\left(\frac{V(\delta_{D}(x))}{\sqrt{t}}\wedge 1\right)\psi(1/t)dt+V(\delta_{D}(x))

\displaystyle\asymp\int_{0}^{V(\delta_{D})^{2}}\psi(1/t)dt+V(\delta_{D})\int_{V(\delta_{D})}^{\textrm{diam}D}\psi(s^{-2})ds+V(\delta_{D})

\displaystyle\asymp V(\delta_{D})^{2}\psi(V(\delta_{D})^{-2})+V(\delta_{D})\int_{V(\delta_{D})}^{1}\psi(s^{-2})ds\eqqcolon\rho(\delta_{D}(x)).

(4.4)

Here, in the first line we used
\nu(t)\asymp\frac{\psi(1/t)}{t}
,
t<1
, see
[
49
, Corollary 2.5 & Proposition 2.6]
, and in the last line we used
V(\delta_{D})\int_{V(\delta_{D})}^{\textrm{diam}D}\psi(s^{-2})ds\gtrsim V(\delta_{D})
. The constant of comparability above depends only on
d
,
D
,
\psi
,
\phi
, and
\supp\xi
.
Therefore, the appropriate choice of the function space for distributional solutions is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{1}\coloneqq L^{1}\big(D,\rho(\delta_{D}(x))dx\big).

Remark 4.5
.

Under suitable assumptions as in Remark
3.6
, it is not hard to get a more explicit behaviour of
\psi(-L_{|D})\xi
. E.g., if

\psi(\lambda)=\left((-\Delta)^{\beta/2}_{|D}\right)^{\alpha/2}
, then as in
[
47
, Example 8.5]

\displaystyle\rho(\delta_{D}(x))\asymp\begin{cases}\delta_{D}(x)^{\beta/2},&\alpha<1,\\
\delta_{D}(x)^{\beta/2}\log\frac{\textrm{diam}D}{\delta_{D}(x)},&\alpha=1,\\
\delta_{D}(x)^{\beta/2-\alpha\beta/2},&\alpha>1,\end{cases}

By applying Corollary
4.3
, we have the following cornerstone result regarding the spaces
\mathcal{L}^{1}
and
L^{1}(D,V(\delta_{D}(x))dx)
which ensures that Green potentials are in the appropriate space for distributional solutions.

Theorem 4.6
.

It holds that
G_{D}^{\psi}\big(\rho(\delta_{D})\big)\asymp V(\delta_{D})
, and the map
G_{D}^{\psi}:L^{1}(D,V(\delta_{D}(x))dx)\to\mathcal{L}^{1}

is continuous.

Proof.

First, assume that
G_{D}^{\psi}\big(\rho(\delta_{D})\big)\asymp V(\delta_{D})
. Then the continuity of
G_{D}^{\psi}
follows by Fubini’s theorem and the symmetry of
G_{D}^{\psi}(x,y)
, since

\displaystyle\|G_{D}^{\psi}f\|_{\mathcal{L}^{1}}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{D}G_{D}^{\psi}|f|(x)\rho(\delta_{D}(x))dx=\int_{D}|f|(x)G_{D}^{\psi}\big(\rho(\delta_{D})\big)(x)dx

\displaystyle\asymp\int_{D}|f|(x)V(\delta_{D}(x))dx=\|f\|_{L^{1}(D,V(\delta_{D}(x))dx)}.

To prove
G_{D}^{\psi}\big(\rho(\delta_{D})\big)\asymp V(\delta_{D})
we treat each term in
\rho(\delta_{D})
separately. By
(A1)
for
\phi
and
\psi
we have that (
U3’
) holds for
t\mapsto V(t)^{2}\psi(V(t)^{-2})
, see Lemma
A.5
. Hence, the formula (
4.2
) implies

\displaystyle G_{D}^{\psi}\big(V(\delta_{D})^{2}\psi(V(\delta_{D})^{-2})\big)

\displaystyle\asymp\frac{1}{\delta_{D}V(\delta_{D})\psi(V(\delta_{D})^{-2})}\int\limits_{0}^{\delta_{D}}V(t)^{3}\psi(V(t)^{-2})\,dt

\displaystyle\hskip-10.00002pt+V(\delta_{D})+V(\delta_{D})\int\limits_{\delta_{D}}^{\textrm{diam}D}\frac{V(t)}{t}\,dt\,.

Since
r\mapsto r^{2}\psi(r^{-2})
is non-decreasing, and
V
satisfies the weak scaling condition, the first term is bounded from above by
\frac{V(\delta_{D})}{\delta_{D}}\int_{0}^{\delta_{D}}V(t)dt\asymp V(\delta_{D})^{2}\lesssim V(\delta_{D}).

For the third term, we can again use the weak scaling property of
V
to get
V(\delta_{D})\int_{\delta_{D}}^{\textrm{diam}D}\frac{V(t)}{t}\,dt\lesssim V(\delta_{D})\int_{0}^{\textrm{diam}D}\frac{V(t)}{t}\,dt\lesssim V(\delta_{D}).

Therefore,
G_{D}^{\psi}\big(V(\delta_{D})^{2}\psi(V(\delta_{D})^{-2})\big)\asymp V(\delta_{D})
.

The second term of
\rho(\delta_{D})
also satisfies (
U3’
), see Lemma
A.5
. Thus, we can apply Corollary
4.3
to get

\displaystyle G_{D}^{\psi}\left(V(\delta_{D})\int_{V(\delta_{D})}^{1}\psi(s^{-2})ds\right)

\displaystyle\asymp\frac{1}{\delta_{D}(x)V(\delta_{D})\psi(V(\delta_{D})^{-2})}\int_{0}^{\delta_{D}(x)}V(t)^{2}\int_{V(t)}^{1}\psi(s^{-2})ds\,dt

\displaystyle\hskip-10.00002pt+V(\delta_{D})+V(\delta_{D})\int_{\delta_{D}}^{\textrm{diam}D}\frac{\int_{V(t)}^{1}\psi(s^{-2})ds}{t\psi(V(t)^{-2})}\,dt\,.

Note that by the weak scaling of
\psi
we have

\displaystyle\int_{V(t)}^{1}\psi(s^{-2})ds\lesssim\begin{cases}V(t)^{2\underline{\delta}_{\psi}}\psi(V(t)^{-2}),&\underline{\delta}_{\psi}<\frac{1}{2},\\
V(t)\psi(V(t)^{-2})\log\big(1/t\big),&\underline{\delta}_{\psi}=\frac{1}{2},\\
V(t)\psi(V(t)^{-2}),&\underline{\delta}_{\psi}\geq\frac{1}{2}.\end{cases}\lesssim V(t)^{\eta}\psi(V(t)^{-2})

(4.5)

for some
\eta\in(0,1)
. Hence, it follows that

\displaystyle G_{D}^{\psi}\left(V(\delta_{D})\int_{V(\delta_{D})}^{1}\psi(s^{-2})ds\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim V(\delta_{D})^{1+\eta}+V(\delta_{D})\left(1+\int_{\delta_{D}}^{\textrm{diam}D}\frac{V(t)^{\eta}}{t}\,dt\,\right)\lesssim V(\delta_{D}),

where for the first term we used that
r\mapsto r^{2}\psi(^{-2})
is non-decreasing, and that
V
is increasing, and for the third we note that
\int_{0}^{\textrm{diam}D}\frac{V(t)^{\eta}}{t}\,dt<\infty
since
V(t)\lesssim t^{\underline{\delta}_{\phi}}
by the weak scaling at zero and
\eta\underline{\delta}_{\phi}<1
.
This finishes the proof of
G_{D}^{\psi}\rho(\delta_{D})\asymp V(\delta_{D})
.
∎

Theorem 4.7
.

For
f\in L^{1}(D,V(\delta_{D}(x))dx)
, the function
u=G_{D}^{\psi}f
solves
\psi(-L_{|D})u=f
in
D

distributionally, i.e. (
4.3
) holds.

Proof.

Let
f\in L^{1}(D,V(\delta_{D}(x))dx)
and
\xi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(D)
. By Theorem
4.6
it follows that
G_{D}^{\psi}f\in\mathcal{L}^{1}
, and by applying (
4.1
) one can easily show that
|\psi(-L_{|D})\xi|\leq C(\phi,\psi,\xi)\rho(\delta_{D})
. Moreover, by Remark
3.3
, we have
\xi\in\mathcal{D}(\psi(-L_{|D}))\subset L^{2}(D)
and
\psi(-L_{|D})\xi\in L^{2}(D)
, so by Proposition
3.2
it holds that
G_{D}^{\psi}\psi(-L_{|D})\xi=\xi
.

Fubini’s theorem, and Propositions
3.2

and
3.4
yield

\displaystyle\int_{D}G_{D}^{\psi}f(x)\psi(-L_{|D})\xi(x)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{D}\int_{D}G_{D}^{\psi}(x,y)\psi(-L_{|D})\xi(x)dxf(y)dy

\displaystyle=\int_{D}G_{D}^{\psi}\big(\psi(-L_{|D})\xi\big)(y)f(y)dy

\displaystyle=\int_{D}\xi(y)f(y)dy,

which proves the claim.
∎

We finish with a regularity property of the Green potentials, which follows from joint continuity of
G_{D}^{\psi}
from Lemma
A.3
and an analogous proof as in

.

Proposition 4.8
.

Let

. Then

\displaystyle\lim_{z\to x\in D}\int_{D}|G_{D}^{\psi}(z,y)-G_{D}^{\psi}(x,y)||f(y)|dy=0,

uniformly on compact subsets of

. In particular,
G_{D}^{\psi}f\in C(D)
.

5
Poisson kernel and harmonic functions

The Poisson kernel of
\psi(-L_{|D})
is defined as a generalised normal derivative of the
Green function:

\displaystyle P^{\psi}_{D}(x,z)\coloneqq-\partial_{V}G_{D}^{\psi}(x,z)=\lim_{D\ni y\to z}\frac{G_{D}^{\psi}(x,y)}{V(\delta_{D}(y))},\quad x\in D,\,z\in\partial D.

Proposition 5.1
.

The function
P^{\psi}_{D}
is well defined, jointly continuous on
D\times\partial D
, and

\displaystyle P^{\psi}_{D}(x,z)\asymp\frac{V(\delta_{D}(x))}{V(|x-z|)^{2}|x-z|^{d}\psi(V(|x-z|)^{-2})},\quad x\in D,\,z\in\partial D.

(5.1)

Further,

\displaystyle\int_{D}G_{D}^{\psi^{*}}(x,\xi)P^{\psi}_{D}(\xi,z)d\xi=K_{D}(x,z),

(5.2)

where
K_{D}(x,z)=\lim\limits_{D\ni y\to z}\frac{G_{D}(x,y)}{V(\delta_{D}(y))}
is the modified Martin kernel in
D
of the Lévy process
X
.

Proof.

The proof follows the lines of
[
10
, Proposition 2.19]
, with the major difference in the calculations arising from the Green function estimate (
2.13
). By the representation (
2.12
) of
G_{D}^{\psi}
, we have that

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{D\ni y\to z}\frac{G_{D}^{\psi}(x,y)}{V(\delta_{D}(y))}=\lim\limits_{D\ni y\to z}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{p_{D}(t,x,y)}{V(\delta_{D}(y))}{\swabfamily u}(t)dt=\int_{0}^{\infty}\lim\limits_{D\ni y\to z}\frac{p_{D}(t,x,y)}{V(\delta_{D}(y))}{\swabfamily u}(t)dt,

(5.3)

where the application of the dominated convergence theorem in (
5.3
) is justified by the estimates (
2.7
) and (
2.9
), integrability of
{\swabfamily u}
near 0, and boundedness of
{\swabfamily u}
away from 0.
Since the limit
\partial_{V}p(t,x,z):=\lim\limits_{D\ni y\to z}{p_{D}(t,x,y)}/{V(\delta_{D}(y))}
exists and is jointly continuous in
(0,\infty)\times D\times\partial D
by Lemma
A.1
, we obtain the existence and joint continuity in
D\times\partial D
of the Poisson kernel. Furthermore, the sharp bounds (
5.1
) for
P^{\psi}_{D}
follow easily from (
2.13
). For (
5.2
), first note that
K_{D}(x,z)
is well defined, see
[
11
, Subsection 4.6]
and Remark
A.2
. By Lemma
2.1
,

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{D\ni y\to z}\frac{1}{V(\delta_{D}(y))}\int_{D}G_{D}^{\psi^{*}}(x,\xi)G_{D}^{\psi}(\xi,y)d\xi=-\partial_{V}G_{D}(x,z)=K_{D}(x,z).

(5.4)

In order to justify the application of the dominated convergence theorem to the left-hand side of (
5.4
), which proves (
5.2
), in what follows we use analogous calculations as in
[
10
, Proposition 2.19]
, accounting for necessary changes based on the Green function estimates (
2.13
). Fix
x\in D
and
z\in\partial D
, and take
\varepsilon>0
such that
\delta_{D}(x)>3\varepsilon
and
y\in D
such that
|z-y|<\varepsilon/2
. First, for
\xi\in D\setminus B(z,\varepsilon)
we have
|\xi-y|\asymp\varepsilon
, and therefore

\displaystyle\frac{G_{D}^{\psi^{*}}(x,\xi)G_{D}^{\psi}(\xi,y)}{V(\delta_{D}(y))}\lesssim\frac{G_{D}^{\psi^{*}}(x,\xi)V(\delta_{D}(\xi))}{V(|\xi-y|)^{2}|\xi-y|^{d}\psi(V(|\xi-y|)^{-2})}\lesssim G_{D}^{\psi^{*}}(x,\xi)V(\delta_{D}(\xi)),

(5.5)

where the right-hand side is an integrable function of
\xi
by Lemma
4.1
. Next, note that for
\xi\in B(z,\varepsilon)\cap D
,
|\xi-x|\asymp\varepsilon
, so by (
2.13
) we have
G_{D}^{\psi^{*}}(x,\xi)\lesssim V(\delta_{D}(\xi))
. This, together with (
2.13
) and
(A1)
, implies that for
\xi\in B(y,\delta_{D}(y)/2)
we have

\displaystyle\frac{G_{D}^{\psi^{*}}(x,\xi)G_{D}^{\psi}(\xi,y)}{V(\delta_{D}(y))}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{V(\delta_{D}(\xi))}{V(\delta_{D}(y))}\frac{1}{|\xi-y|^{d}\psi(V(|\xi-y|)^{-2})}\lesssim\frac{1}{|\xi-y|^{d}\psi(V(|\xi-y|)^{-2})}.

(5.6)

Similarly, for
\xi\in D\cap B(z,\varepsilon)\setminus B(y,\delta_{D}(y)/2)
note that
\delta_{D}(\xi)\leq 3|\xi-y|\leq 2\varepsilon
, so

\displaystyle\frac{G_{D}^{\psi^{*}}(x,\xi)G_{D}^{\psi}(\xi,y)}{V(\delta_{D}(y))}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{V(\delta_{D}(\xi))}{V(\delta_{D}(y))}\frac{V(\delta_{D}(y))}{V(|\xi-y|)|\xi-y|^{d}\psi(V(|\xi-y|)^{-2})}\lesssim\frac{1}{|\xi-y|^{d}\psi(V(|\xi-y|)^{-2})}.

(5.7)

The proof is finished by noting that the constants in (
5.5
), (
5.6
) and (
5.7
) are independent of
y
and that the right-hand sides of the latter two inequalities are integrable functions of
\xi
in
D
by
(A1)
for
\psi
and (
2.8
).
∎

The Poisson potential
P^{\psi}_{D}\zeta
of a measure
\zeta
on
\partial D
is defined by

\displaystyle P^{\psi}_{D}\zeta(x)\coloneqq\int_{\partial D}P^{\psi}_{D}(x,z)\zeta(dz),\quad x\in D.

From the sharp bound (
5.1
) we easily see that
P^{\psi}_{D}\zeta
is finite if and only if
\zeta
is finite.

Proposition 5.2
.

For the
(d-1)
–Hausdorff measure
\sigma
on
\partial D
, it follows that

\displaystyle P^{\psi}_{D}\sigma(x)\asymp\frac{1}{\delta_{D}(x)V(\delta_{D}(x))\psi(V(\delta_{D}(x))^{-2})},\quad x\in D.

(5.8)

Proof.

Let
x\in D
. Since
r\mapsto r^{2}\psi(r^{-2})
and
V
are non-decreasing, and
\delta_{D}(x)\leq|x-z|
for
z\in\partial D
, (
5.1
) implies that

\displaystyle P^{\psi}_{D}\sigma(x)\lesssim\int_{\partial D}\frac{V(\delta_{D}(x))}{V(|x-z|)^{2}|x-z|^{d}\psi(V(|x-z|)^{-2})}\sigma(dz)\lesssim\frac{1}{V(\delta_{D}(x))\psi(V(\delta_{D}(x))^{-2})}\int_{\partial D}\frac{\sigma(dz)}{|x-z|^{d}}.

Set
\Gamma=\{z\in\partial D:|x-z|\leq 2\delta_{D}(x)\}
. The same arguments as above imply that

\displaystyle P^{\psi}_{D}\sigma(x)\gtrsim\int_{\Gamma}\frac{V(\delta_{D}(x))}{V(|x-z|)^{2}|x-z|^{d}\psi(V(|x-z|)^{-2})}\sigma(dz)\gtrsim\frac{1}{V(\delta_{D}(x))\psi(V(\delta_{D}(x))^{-2})}\int_{\Gamma}\frac{\sigma(dz)}{|x-z|^{d}}.

Now (

) follows from
\int_{\Gamma}\frac{\sigma(dz)}{|x-z|^{d}}\asymp\int_{D}\frac{\sigma(dz)}{|x-z|^{d}}\asymp\delta_{D}(x)^{-1}
, as proved in
[
10
, Lemma A.2]
.
∎

Corollary 5.3
.

If
\psi(-L_{|D})=\left((-\Delta)^{\beta/2}_{|D}\right)^{\alpha/2}
, it holds that

\displaystyle P^{\psi}_{D}(x,z)

\displaystyle\asymp\frac{\delta_{D}(x)^{\beta/2}}{|x-z|^{d+\beta-\frac{\alpha\beta}{2}}},\quad x\in D,\,z\in\partial D,

\displaystyle P^{\psi}_{D}\sigma(x)

\displaystyle\asymp\delta_{D}(x)^{-1-\frac{\beta}{2}+\frac{\beta\alpha}{2}},\quad x\in D.

Lemma 5.4
.

If
\zeta
is a finite measure on
\partial D
, then
P^{\psi}_{D}\zeta\in L^{1}(D,V(\delta_{D}(x))dx)\cap\mathcal{L}^{1}
and

\displaystyle\|P^{\psi}_{D}\zeta\|_{L^{1}(D,V(\delta_{D}(x))dx)}+\|P^{\psi}_{D}\zeta\|_{\mathcal{L}^{1}}\leq C|\zeta|(\partial D),

for some constant
C=C(d,D,\psi,\phi)>0
. Moreover,
P^{\psi}_{D}\zeta\in C(D)
.

Proof.

By applying (
5.1
), note that
\|P^{\psi}_{D}\zeta\|_{L^{1}(D,V(\delta_{D}(x))dx)}
is dominated from above by

\displaystyle\int_{\partial D}\int_{D}P^{\psi}_{D}(x,z)V(\delta_{D}(x))dx|\zeta|(dz)\lesssim\int_{\partial D}\int_{D}\frac{dx}{|x-z|^{d}\psi(V(|x-z|)^{-2})}|\zeta|(dz)\lesssim|\zeta|(\partial D),

since the function
r\mapsto(r\psi(V(r)^{-2}))^{-1}
is integrable on
(0,\text{diam}(D))
, by
(A1)
for
\psi
and (
2.8
).
Similarly,
\|P^{\psi}_{D}\zeta\|_{\mathcal{L}^{1}}
is bounded from above by

\int_{D}P^{\psi}_{D}|\zeta|(x)\rho(\delta_{D}(x))dx=\int_{\partial D}\left(\int_{D\cap B(z,1)^{c}}+\int_{D\cap B(z,1)}\right)P^{\psi}_{D}(x,z)\rho(\delta_{D}(x))dx|\zeta|(dz)\eqqcolon I_{1}+I_{2}.

Note that
\rho(\delta_{D})
is bounded and vanishes at the boundary, and that on
D\cap B(z,1)^{c}
we have
P^{\psi}_{D}(x,z)\lesssim V(\delta_{D}(x))
, hence,
I_{1}\lesssim|\zeta|(\partial D)
. By (
5.1
) and (
4.1
), the inner integral in
I_{2}
is split into two parts:

\displaystyle\int_{D\cap B(z,1)}P^{\psi}_{D}(x,z)\rho(\delta_{D}(x))dx\asymp\int_{D\cap B(z,1)}\frac{V(\delta_{D}(x))^{3}\psi(V(\delta_{D}(x))^{-2})dx}{V(|x-z|)^{2}|x-z|^{d}\psi(V(|x-z|)^{-2})}

\displaystyle\hskip 60.00009pt+\int_{D\cap B(z,1)}\frac{V(\delta_{D}(x))^{2}\int_{V(\delta_{D}(x))}^{1}\psi(s^{-2})ds}{V(|x-z|)^{2}|x-z|^{d}\psi(V(|x-z|)^{-2})}dx\eqqcolon J_{1}+J_{2}.

Since
\delta_{D}(x)\leq|x-z|
and
r\mapsto r^{2}\psi(r^{-2})
and
V
are non-decreasing, (
2.8
) implies that

J_{1}\lesssim\int_{B(z,1)}\frac{V(|x-z|)}{|x-z|^{d}}dx\lesssim 1.

Further, by first applying the inequality (
4.5
), and then using the analogous reasoning as for the estimate of
J_{1}
, we also get that

J_{2}\lesssim\int_{B(z,1)}\frac{V(|x-z|)^{\eta}}{|x-z|^{d}}dx\lesssim 1.

Hence
P^{\psi}_{D}\zeta\in\mathcal{L}^{1}
and
\|P^{\psi}_{D}\zeta\|_{\mathcal{L}^{1}}\leq C(d,\psi,\phi,D)|\zeta|(\partial D)
. The continuity of
P^{\psi}_{D}\zeta
follows by the dominated convergence theorem and Proposition
5.1
.
∎

It is known that Poisson kernels are, in general, connected to harmonic functions via their integral representation. Our next goal is to prove this relation, as well as to relate harmonic functions with respect to
\psi(-L_{|D})
to the harmonic functions with respect to
L_{|D}
. We note that we consider harmonic functions in the distributional and probabilistic sense.

Definition 5.5
.

The function
h\in\mathcal{L}^{1}
is harmonic in
D
with respect to
\psi(-L_{|D})
if
\psi(-L_{|D})h=0

in
D
in the distributional sense.

Proposition 5.6
.

The function
h\in\mathcal{L}^{1}
is harmonic in
D
with respect to
\psi(-L_{|D})
if and only if

G_{D}^{\psi^{*}}h
is harmonic in
D
with respect to
L_{|D}
. Further,
for every finite measure
\zeta
on
\partial D
, the function
P^{\psi}_{D}\zeta
is
harmonic in
D
with respect to
\psi(-L_{|D})
.

Proof.

For
h\in\mathcal{L}^{1}
and
\xi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(D)
, we have by Propositions
3.2
and
3.4
that

\displaystyle\int_{D}h(x)\psi(-L_{|D})\xi(x)dx=\int_{D}h(x)G_{D}^{\psi^{*}}\big(-L_{|D}\xi\big)(x)dx=\int_{D}G_{D}^{\psi^{*}}h(x)\big(-L_{|D}\xi\big)(x)dx,

where in the second equality we used Fubini’s theorem. This is justified by noting that
L_{|D}\xi=L\xi\in L^{\infty}(D)
, by
[
9
, Eq. (6)]
, and that
G_{D}^{\psi^{*}}h\in L^{1}(D)
. The latter is a consequence of the estimate
G_{D}^{\psi^{*}}\mathbf{1}\asymp\rho(\delta_{D})
.
Indeed, by Theorem
4.2
for
\psi^{*}
,

\displaystyle G_{D}^{\psi^{*}}\mathbf{1}

\displaystyle\asymp\frac{1}{\delta_{D}(x)V(\delta_{D})\psi^{*}(V(\delta_{D})^{-2})}\!\!\!\int\limits_{0}^{\delta_{D}(x)}\!\!\!V(t)\,dt+V(\delta_{D}(x))+V(\delta_{D}(x))\!\!\!\int\limits_{\delta_{D}(x)}^{\textrm{diam}D}\!\!\!\frac{dt}{tV(t)\psi^{*}(V(t)^{-2})},

which is by (

) and
V^{\prime}(t)\asymp V(t)/t
,
t<1
, comparable to
\rho(\delta_{D})
.

For the second claim, take a non-negative finite measure
\zeta
on
\partial D
. From Proposition
5.1
, it follows that
G_{D}^{\psi^{*}}P^{\psi}_{D}\zeta=K_{D}\zeta
. Therefore, by the first part of the proposition, it suffices to show that

is
L_{|D}
-harmonic. This follows from the Martin representation of the non-negative harmonic functions with respect to

, proved in
[
9
]
(see also Remark

). Indeed, by
[

, Eq. (4.24)]
, we have

, where

,

, while
\widetilde{\zeta}(dz)=K_{D}(x_{0},z)\zeta(dz)
. Further,
M_{D}\widetilde{\zeta}
is

-harmonic by

, Proposition 5.11 & Theorem 3.16]
, hence

is
L_{|D}
-harmonic. For a general finite measure

, we use the decomposition
\zeta=\zeta^{+}-\zeta^{-}
.
∎

Now we prove the Martin integral representation of non-negative
\psi(-L_{|D})
-harmonic functions.

Proposition 5.7
.

A non-negative function
h\in\mathcal{L}^{1}
is
\psi(-L_{|D})
-harmonic if and only if there exists a finite measure
\zeta
on
\partial D

\displaystyle h(x)=P^{\psi}_{D}\zeta(x),\quad\text{for a.e. $x\in D$}.

Proof.

Note that one implication is shown in Proposition
5.6
. Let

be a non-negative
\psi(-L_{|D})
-harmonic function. By Proposition
5.6
it follows that the non-negative function

is

-harmonic. By employing the representation of non-negative

-harmonic functions, see

, there is a finite measure

such that

∎

Next, we turn to the probabilistic definition of a harmonic function and relate it to harmonic functions in Definition

. A function

-harmonic in

\displaystyle h(x)=\mathds{E}_{x}[h(Y_{\tau_{U}})],\quad x\in U,

Remark 5.8

For a regular enough domain

, for

Theorem 5.9
.

The non-negative function
h\in\mathcal{L}^{1}

The proof is based on

6
Boundary behaviour of potential integrals

In this section, we study the boundary behaviour of Poisson and Green potentials relative to
P^{\psi}_{D}\sigma
, which will lead us to the description of the nonhomogeneous boundary condition for
\psi(-L_{|D})
.

First, we study the behaviour of Poisson potentials, where we prove that the explosion rate of the non-negative
\psi(-L_{|D})
-harmonic (i.e.
Y
-harmonic) functions is described by the reference function
P^{\psi}_{D}\sigma
.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let
\zeta\in L^{1}(\partial D)
. It holds that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{t}\int_{\{\delta_{D}(x)\leq t\}}\frac{P^{\psi}_{D}\zeta(x)}{P^{\psi}_{D}\sigma(x)}\varphi(x)dx\xrightarrow{t\downarrow 0}\int_{\partial D}\varphi(y)\zeta(y)d\sigma(y),\quad\varphi\in C(\overline{D}).

Moreover, for a continuity point
z\in\partial D
of
\zeta
, it holds that

\displaystyle\lim_{D\ni x\to z\in\partial D}\frac{P^{\psi}_{D}\zeta(x)}{P^{\psi}_{D}\sigma(x)}=\zeta(z),

(6.1)

with the convergence in (
6.1
) being uniform on
\partial D
if
\zeta\in C(\partial D)
.

Proof.

Let us first prove the second claim, which follows the line of
[
10
, Proposition 3.4]
. By (
5.1
) and (
5.8
), for all
x\in D
and
y\in\partial D
we have

\displaystyle\frac{P^{\psi}_{D}(x,y)}{P^{\psi}_{D}\sigma(x)}\lesssim\frac{\delta_{D}(x)}{|x-y|^{d}}\frac{V(\delta_{D}(x))^{2}\psi(V(\delta_{D}(x))^{-2}}{V(|x-y|)^{2}\psi(V(|x-y|)^{-2}}\leq\frac{\delta_{D}(x)}{|x-y|^{d}}.

(6.2)

Take now a continuity point
z\in\partial D
of
\zeta
,
\varepsilon>0
and choose
\eta>0
such that if
|y-z|<\eta
, then
|\zeta(y)-\zeta(z)|\leq\varepsilon
. Define
\Gamma_{z}=\{y\in\partial D:|y-z|<\eta\}
, so for
|x-z|<\eta/2
, by using (
6.2
) and
[
10
, Lemma A.2]
, we get

\displaystyle\left|\frac{P^{\psi}_{D}\zeta(x)}{P^{\psi}_{D}\sigma(x)}-\zeta(z)\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\delta_{D}(x)\int_{\Gamma_{z}}\frac{|\zeta(y)-\zeta(z)|}{|y-x|^{d}}\sigma(dy)+\delta_{D}(x)\int_{\partial D\setminus\Gamma_{z}}\frac{|\zeta(y)-\zeta(z)|}{|y-x|^{d}}\sigma(dy)

\displaystyle\lesssim\varepsilon+\delta_{D}(x)\left(\frac{\eta}{2}\right)^{-d}\|\zeta\|_{L^{1}(\partial D)},

where the constant of comparability does not depend on
z
. Hence, we obtain (
6.1
). The uniformness, if
\zeta
is continuous, follows by the uniform continuity of
\zeta
since
\partial D
is compact.

The proof of the first claim follows exactly as the proof of
[
3
, Theorem 26]
for the spectral fractional Laplacian, see also
[
10
, Proposition 3.5]
for additional details.
∎

In the next proposition we deal with boundary behaviour of Green potentials with respect to the
P^{\psi}_{D}\sigma
. As expected,
P^{\psi}_{D}\sigma
annihilates Green potentials but only in the weak sense. For a pointwise annihilation, we must assume a special form of functions.

Proposition 6.2
.

Let
\lambda
be a measure on
D
such that
\int_{D}V(\delta_{D}(x))|\lambda|(dx)<\infty
. Then it holds

\displaystyle\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{t}\int_{\{\delta_{D}(x)<t\}}\frac{G_{D}^{\psi}\lambda(x)}{P^{\psi}_{D}\sigma(x)}\varphi(x)dx=0,\quad\varphi\in C(\overline{D}).

If
\lambda
has a density, i.e.
\lambda(dx)=\lambda(x)dx
, such that
\lambda(x)\lesssim U(\delta_{D}(x))
, where
U
satisfies
(U1)
–
(U4)
, then

\displaystyle\lim_{D\ni x\to z\in\partial D}\frac{G_{D}^{\psi}\lambda(x)}{P^{\psi}_{D}\sigma(x)}=0.

(6.3)

Proof.

For the first claim, by Fubini’s theorem we get that

\displaystyle\int_{\{\delta_{D}(x)<t\}}\frac{G_{D}^{\psi}\lambda(x)}{P^{\psi}_{D}\sigma(x)}\varphi(x)dx=\int_{D}\int_{\{\delta_{D}(x)<t\}}\frac{G_{D}^{\psi}(x,y)}{P^{\psi}_{D}\sigma(x)}\varphi(x)dx\,\lambda(dy).

For the inner integral we will show that

\displaystyle\int_{\{\delta_{D}(x)<t\}}\frac{G_{D}^{\psi}(x,y)}{P^{\psi}_{D}\sigma(x)}dx\leq f(t,y),

(6.4)

where
f(t,y)\lesssim tV(\delta_{D}(y))
and
f(t,y)/t\to 0
as
t\to 0
for every fixed
y\in D
.

To this end, for
\delta_{D}(y)<\frac{t}{2}
, we first note that
U(t)=t\,V(t)\psi(V(t)^{-2})
satisfies
(U1)
, (
U3’
), and
(U4)
, and that
U(\delta_{D})\asymp 1/P^{\psi}_{D}\sigma
. By repeating the steps in Lemma
A.6
for
\eta=t
, we get that for
\delta_{D}(y)<\frac{t}{2}

\int_{\{\delta_{D}(x)<t\}}\frac{G_{D}^{\psi}(x,y)}{P^{\psi}_{D}\sigma(x)}dx\leq CtV(\delta_{D}(y))=:f(t,y).

For
\delta_{D}(y)\geq\frac{t}{2}
, we repeat the steps from the proof of
[
10
, Lemma A.5]
. We split the integral in (
6.4
) into four terms, defined below,

\displaystyle\int_{\{\delta_{D}(x)<t\}}\frac{G_{D}^{\psi}(x,y)}{P^{\psi}_{D}\sigma(x)}dx=J_{1}+J_{2}+J_{3}+J_{4}.

By (
2.13
) and (
5.8
) we have that

\displaystyle J_{1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{\{\delta_{D}(x)<t/4\}}\frac{G_{D}^{\psi}(x,y)}{P^{\psi}_{D}\sigma(x)}dx\lesssim V(\delta_{D}(y))\int_{0}^{t/4}\frac{h}{\delta_{D}(y)}dh\leq C_{1}\frac{V(\delta_{D}(y))}{\delta_{D}(y)}\,t^{2}

and

\displaystyle J_{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{\{t/4<\delta_{D}(x)<t\}\cap B(y,t/4)}\frac{G_{D}^{\psi}(x,y)}{P^{\psi}_{D}\sigma(x)}dx\lesssim\int_{B(y,t/4)}\frac{tV(t)\psi(V(t)^{-2})}{|x-y|^{d}\psi(V(|x-y|)^{-2})}dx\leq C_{2}tV(t).

Further, define
D_{3}\coloneqq\{t/4<\delta_{D}(x)<t\}\cap B(y,t/4)^{c}\cap B(y,r_{0})
, for some fixed and small enough
r_{0}>0
dependent on the characteristics of the set
D
, see Appendix
A.5
. We have

\displaystyle J_{3}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{D_{3}}\frac{G_{D}^{\psi}(x,y)}{P^{\psi}_{D}\sigma(x)}dx\asymp\int_{D_{3}}\frac{V(\delta_{D}(x))V(\delta_{D}(y))tV(t)^{2}\psi(V(t)^{-2})}{V(|x-y|)^{2}|x-y|^{d}\psi(V(|x-y|)^{-2})}dx

\displaystyle\leq C_{3}t\,V(\delta_{D}(y))\int_{D_{3}}|x-y|^{-d}dx.

Finally, define
D_{4}\coloneqq\{t/4<\delta_{D}(x)<t\}\cap B(x,r_{0})^{c}
so we have
J_{4}\coloneqq\int_{D_{4}}\frac{G_{D}^{\psi}(x,y)}{P^{\psi}_{D}\sigma(x)}dx\leq C_{4}V(\delta_{D}(y))t\,V(t)^{2}\psi(V(t)^{-2}).

For
\delta_{D}(y)\geq\frac{t}{2}
set

\displaystyle f(t,y)

\displaystyle:=C_{1}\frac{V(\delta_{D}(y))}{\delta_{D}(y)}\,t^{2}+C_{2}tV(t)+C_{3}t\,V(\delta_{D}(y))\int_{D_{3}}|x-y|^{-d}dx

\displaystyle\hskip 50.00008pt+C_{4}V(\delta_{D}(y))t\,V(t)^{2}\psi(V(t)^{-2})

and note that by
[
10
, Eq. (A.30)]
,
\int_{D_{3}}|x-y|^{-d}dx\lesssim\int_{t/4}^{r_{0}}t\,r^{-2}dr\lesssim 1
so by the dominated convergence theorem we have
\lim\limits_{t\to 0}\int_{D_{3}}|x-y|^{-d}dx=0
. Furthermore, since
\lim\limits_{r\to 0}r\psi(r^{-1})=0
we have that
f(t,y)\lesssim tV(\delta_{D}(y))
and

\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{f(t,y)}{t}=0
for all
y\in D
.

For the second claim, assume that
\lambda(x)\lesssim U(\delta_{D}(x))
, where
U
satisfies
(U1)
–
(U4)
. It is enough to show that

\displaystyle\lim_{D\ni x\to z\in\partial D}\frac{G_{D}^{\psi}\big(U(\delta_{D})\big)(x)}{P^{\psi}_{D}\sigma(x)}=0.

We use the sharp bounds for
G_{D}^{\psi}U(\delta_{D})
obtained in (
4.2
) and note that
P^{\psi}_{D}\sigma
annihilates the first and the second term of (

). In the last term, on
\{t\geq\delta_{D}(x)\}
we have
tV(t)\psi(V(t)^{-2})\gtrsim\delta_{D}(x)V(\delta_{D}(x))\psi(V(\delta_{D}(x))^{-2})
and
U(t)V(\delta_{D}(x))\leq U(t)V(t)
. The dominated convergence theorem now implies that as

\displaystyle\frac{V(\delta_{D}(x))\int_{\delta_{D}(x)}^{\textrm{diam}D}\frac{U(t)}{tV(t)\psi(V(t)^{-2})}\,dt\,}{P^{\psi}_{D}\sigma(x)}\lesssim\int_{V(\delta_{D}(x))}^{\textrm{diam}D}U(t)V(\delta_{D}(x))dt\to 0,

which proves the claim.
∎

7
Linear Dirichlet problem for
\psi(-L_{|D})

We begin this section with the definition of a weak-dual solution, a notion first introduced as a very weak solution by Brezis, and later developed and used in many state-of-the-art papers, see e.g.
[
1
,
2
,
4
,
21
,
23
,
11
,
10
]
. The main advantage of this approach is that it eliminates the need to define the operator on a class of test functions. We will later see that essentially the weak-dual solution to (
7.1
) is also a distributional solution.

Definition 7.1
.

Let
\lambda
be a (signed) measure on
D
and
\zeta
a (signed) measure on
\partial D
such that

\displaystyle\int_{D}V(\delta_{D}(x))|\lambda|(dx)+|\zeta|(\partial D)<\infty.

(
I_{V}
)

A function
u\in L^{1}_{loc}(D)
is a weak-dual solution to the problem

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\psi(-L_{|D})u=\lambda,&\textrm{in $D$},\\
\frac{u}{P^{\psi}_{D}\sigma}=\zeta,&\textrm{on $\partial D$},\end{cases}

(7.1)

if for every
\xi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(D)
it holds that

\displaystyle\int_{D}u(x)\xi(x)dx=\int_{D}G_{D}^{\psi}\xi(x)\lambda(dx)-\int_{\partial D}\frac{\partial}{\partial V}G_{D}^{\psi}\xi(z)\zeta(dz).

(7.2)

If we have
\leq
in (
7.2
) instead of the equality and the inequality holds for every non-negative
\xi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(D)
, then we say
u
is a weak-dual subsolution (
7.1
). If the same holds for
\geq
instead of
\leq
, we call the function
u
a weak-dual supersolution.

Remark 7.2
.

(a)

Let
\xi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(D)
. Note that, by calculations as in Proposition
5.1
, the function
\frac{\partial}{\partial V}G_{D}^{\psi}\xi(z)
is well defined and

-\frac{\partial}{\partial V}G_{D}^{\psi}\xi(z)=\int_{D}P^{\psi}_{D}(y,z)\xi(y)dy,\quad z\in\partial D.

Further, (
5.1
) implies
\frac{\partial}{\partial V}G_{D}^{\psi}\xi\in L^{\infty}(\partial D)
, and by Lemma
4.1
we have
|G_{D}^{\psi}\xi(x)|\lesssim V(\delta_{D}(x))
, so the condition (

I
V

) ensures that the integrals in (
7.2
) are finite.

(b)

If
u
is a solution to the linear problem (
7.1
), then
u=G_{D}^{\psi}\lambda+P^{\psi}_{D}\zeta
, a.e. in
D
. Furthermore,
u\in\mathcal{L}^{1}
since both terms are in
\mathcal{L}^{1}
. Conversely, by using Fubini’s theorem, it is easily shown that the function
u=G_{D}^{\psi}\lambda+P^{\psi}_{D}\zeta

is a solution to (
7.1
).

(c)

The relation (
7.2
) can be also rewritten as follows:

\displaystyle\int_{D}u(x)\psi(-L_{|D})\varphi(x)dx=\int_{D}\varphi(x)\lambda(dx)-\int_{\partial D}\frac{\partial}{\partial V}\varphi(z)\zeta(dz),

for all
\varphi\in\{\eta\in C^{V}(D):\exists\xi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(D)\textrm{ such that }\psi(-L_{|D})\eta=\xi\textrm{ in $D$, }\eta_{|\partial D}\equiv 0\}.

This remark, together with Lemma
4.1
and Lemma
5.4
yields the following corollary.

Corollary 7.3
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathcal{M}(D)
and
\zeta\in\mathcal{M}(\partial D)
such that
(

I
V

) holds. Then the linear problem (
7.1
) has a unique weak-dual solution
u
. Furthermore,
u\in L^{1}(D,V(\delta_{D}(x))dx)
,

\displaystyle u(x)=G_{D}^{\psi}\lambda(x)+P^{\psi}_{D}\zeta(x),\quad\text{for a.e. $x\in D$,}

and there is
C=C(d,D,\psi,\phi)>0
such that

\displaystyle\|u\|_{L^{1}(D,V(\delta_{D}(x))dx)}\leq C\left(\int_{D}V(\delta_{D}(x))|\lambda|(dx)+|\zeta|(\partial D)\right).

An elementary observation gives us the following weak maximum principle.

Corollary 7.4

(Weak maximum principle)
.

If
\lambda\geq 0
and
\zeta\geq 0
and they satisfy (

I
V

), then the unique solution
u
to the linear problem
(
7.1
)
satisfies
u\geq 0
a.e. in

7.1
Dirichlet forms and Kato’s inequality

One of the key ingredients in solving semilinear elliptic equations is Kato’s inequality, see
[
3
,
22

25

32

37

for various fractional settings. Here, the standard ingredient in the proof of Kato’s inequality is the use of regularity results for fractional spaces. In this section, we prove Kato’s inequality in the non-local setting of our paper, using a general approach via Dirichlet forms. A similar method has been used in
[
41

, with the major difference being that we do not require an explicit form of the Dirichlet domain corresponding to the operator. As a reference for the results regarding general Dirichlet forms theory in this subsection, we refer to
[
38
]
.

Recall that the operator
\psi(-L_{|D})
, as an
L^{2}(D)
operator, is given by

\displaystyle\psi(-L_{|D})u(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\phi(\lambda_{j})\widehat{u}_{j}\varphi_{j}(x),

for all

\displaystyle\psi(-L_{|D})u(x)=\textrm{P.V.}\int_{D}(u(x)-u(y))J_{D}(x,y)dy+\kappa(x)u(x),\quad u\in C_{c}^{\infty}(D),

and for all

we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}\langle\psi(-L_{|D})u,v\rangle&=\frac{1}{2}\iint_{D\times D}(u(x)-u(y))(v(x)-v(y))J_{D}(x,y)dxdy+\int_{D}\kappa(x)u(x)v(x)dx.\end{split}

It is known that the operator
\psi(-L_{|D})
generates the Dirichlet form

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(u,v)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{D}u(x)(v(x)-P_{s}^{D}v(x))dx\,\nu(s)ds

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\iint_{D\times D}\big(u(x)-u(y)\big)\big(v(x)-v(y)\big)J_{D}(x,y)dxdy+\int_{D}\kappa(x)u(x)v(x)dx

, see for example
[

and
[

\displaystyle\|u\|_{\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{E})}=\|u\|_{L^{2}(D)}+\sqrt{\mathcal{E}(u,u)}.

(7.4)

Since

has positive eigenvalues, for all

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(u,u)=\langle\psi(-L_{|D})u,u\rangle=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\phi(\lambda_{j})|\widehat{u}_{j}|^{2}\geq\phi(\lambda_{1})\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(D)}.

(7.5)

By density of

Theorem 8.3

Let

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\psi(-L_{|D})u(x)=f(x,u(x)),&\textrm{in $D$},\\

We briefly present the proof, since it is standard and essentially the same as the proof of
[
10

Step 1: existence of a solution.
Define
F:D\times\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle F(x,t)=\begin{cases}f(x,\underline{u}(x)),&t<\underline{u}(x),\\

and the operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}v(x)=\int_{D}G_{D}^{\psi}(x,y)F(y,v(y))dy,\quad x\in D,\,v\in L^{1}(D,V(\delta_{D}(x))dx).

By using the Arzelà-Ascoli theorem, it follows that

is a compact operator so, by Schauder’s fixed point theorem, there exists a function

such that

is a weak-dual solution to

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\psi(-L_{|D})u(x)=F(x,u(x)),&\textrm{in $D$},\\

Step 2: finding the minimal and maximal solution.
We use Zorn’s lemma on the family

Step 4: uniqueness of the solution.
When

I_{3}
in Lemma A.3]
, we can prove that

I_{3}\,\asymp\,\frac{1}{\delta_{D}(x)V(\delta_{D}(x))\psi(V(\delta_{D}(x))^{-2})}\int_{0}^{\delta_{D}(x)}U(t)V(t)\,dt.

Estimate of
I_{4}

By applying the similar calculations as in

, it holds that

I_{4}\,\asymp\,V(\delta_{D}(x))\int_{3\delta_{D}(x)/2}^{\eta}\frac{U(t)}{tV(t)\psi(V(t)^{-2})}\,dt\,.

Estimate of

As in

Lemma A.7
.

Let
\eta<\epsilon
and assume that conditions
(U1)
-
(U4)
hold true. There exists
c(\eta)>0
such that for any
x\in D
satisfying
\delta_{D}(x)\geq\eta/2
,

G_{D}^{\psi}\big(U(\delta_{D})\mathbf{1}_{\{\delta_{D}<\eta\}}\big)(x)\leq c(\eta)\,.

Proof.

Here we employ the same strategy as in
[
10
, Lemma A.4]
, so we repeat only the main steps. Take
x\in D
and split the set
\{y:\delta_{D}(y)<\eta\}
into

\displaystyle D_{1}=\{y:\,\delta_{D}(y)<\eta/4\},\qquad D_{2}=\{y:\,\eta/4\leq\delta_{D}(y)<\eta\},

so

G_{D}^{\psi}\big(U(\delta_{D})\mathbf{1}_{(\delta_{D}<\eta)}\big)(x)=\sum_{j=1}^{2}\int_{D_{j}}G_{D}^{\psi}(x,y)U(\delta_{D}(y))\,dy=:\sum_{j=1}^{2}J_{j}.

Estimate of
J_{1}
:

By repeating the steps from
[
10
, Estimate of
J_{1}
in Lemma A.4]
, we get

J_{1}\lesssim\frac{1}{V(\eta)^{2}\psi(V(\eta)^{-2})}\int_{0}^{\eta}U(t)V(t)\,dt.

Estimate of
J_{2}
:

The same calculations as in
[
10
, Estimate of
J_{2}
in Lemma A.4]
yield

J_{2}\lesssim U(\eta/4).

The estimates
J_{1}
and
J_{2}
substantiate the claim.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.2
.

The proof follows the lines of the proof of
[
10
, Theorem 3.6]
. Fix some
\eta<\epsilon
. On
\{\delta_{D}(y)\geq\eta\}
,
U
is bounded by the assumption
(U4)
, so

G_{D}^{\psi}\big(U(\delta_{D})\mathbf{1}_{\{\delta_{D}\geq\eta\}}\big)(x)\lesssim G_{D}^{\psi}V(\delta_{D})(x)\asymp V(\delta_{D}(x))\,,\quad x\in D,

(A.4)

where the last step estimate follows from Lemma
4.1
. On
\{\delta_{D}(x)\geq\eta/2\}
we have

\displaystyle G_{D}^{\psi}\big(U(\delta_{D})\mathbf{1}_{\{\delta_{D}\geq\eta\}}\big)(x)

\displaystyle\gtrsim\int_{B(x,\eta/4)}\frac{1}{|x-y|^{d}\psi(V(|x-y|)^{-2})}dy\asymp\frac{1}{\psi(V(\eta)^{-2})}\gtrsim 1.

On
\{\delta_{D}(x)\leq\eta/2\}
, since
|x-y|\geq\eta/2
, it holds that

\displaystyle G_{D}^{\psi}\big(U(\delta_{D})\mathbf{1}_{\{\delta_{D}\geq\eta\}}\big)(x)

\displaystyle\gtrsim\int_{\{\delta_{D}(y)\geq\eta\}}\frac{V(\delta_{D}(x))V(\delta_{D}(y))}{|x-y|^{d}V(|x-y|)^{2}\psi(V(|x-y|)^{-2})}dy\gtrsim V(\delta_{D}(x)).

Note that,
\delta_{D}(x)\asymp 1
on
\{\delta_{D}(x)\geq\eta/2\}
which yields
G_{D}^{\psi}\big(U(\delta_{D})\mathbf{1}_{\{\delta_{D}\geq\eta\}}\big)(x)\asymp V(\delta_{D}(x))
in
D
. In the case
\delta_{D}(x)\geq\eta/2
, by Lemma
A.7
, we get
that
G_{D}^{\psi}(U(\delta_{D}))(x)\asymp 1
, and the right-hand side of (
4.2
) is also comparable to 1.

Let us deal now with
\delta_{D}(x)<\eta/2
. Since we treat
\eta
as a constant, we can see that
\eta
in (
A.3
) can be replaced by
\textrm{diam}D
. The claim now follows from Lemma
A.6
and (

).
