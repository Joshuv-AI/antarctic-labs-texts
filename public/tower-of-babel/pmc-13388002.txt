Automating Subthalamic Deep Brain Stimulation Programming with Evoked Resonant Neural Activity in Parkinson's Disease
Authors: Kanae J Nagao, Thushara Perera, Matthew Petoe, Wee‐Lih Lee, San San Xu, Kiaran Lawson, Ashton Moorhead, Hugh McDermott, Kristian Bulluss, Wesley Thevathasan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOptimal outcomes from subthalamic nucleus deep brain stimulation (STN‐DBS) for Parkinson's disease (PD) depend on accurate stimulation of an ideal functional target within the dorsolateral STN. Clinical programming is heuristic, and objective methods are needed to improve efficiency and consistency.ObjectivesThis study aimed to investigate the feasibility and acute motor benefit of STN‐DBS programming, guided by intraoperatively recorded evoked resonant neural activity (ERNA) in patients with PD.MethodsWe assessed 12 patients with anatomically well‐placed leads, 4–6 months following STN‐DBS. The worst hemibody was tested off‐medication. Acute motor benefit was double‐blind assessed for three programming configurations: (i) chronic expert clinician settings, (ii) imaging guided, and (iii) an ERNA automated algorithm. We also compared therapeutic and side effect thresholds and the spatial distribution of fractionated current.ResultsERNA programming improved hemibody Movement Disorder Society‐Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale‐Part III scores by 75.7% (median) compared with off‐stimulation. This was not different from imaging (81.6%, P = 0.19) or clinician programming (68.8%, P = 0.33). Therapeutic thresholds (P = 0.90) and side effect thresholds (P = 0.57) did not differ across conditions. ERNA programming was 0.8–1 mm ventral and 0.3 mm posterior to imaging and clinician programming.ConclusionsA programming algorithm based solely on ERNA achieved acute motor efficacy and tolerability equivalent to expert clinical and imaging‐based approaches. ERNA recordings took <1 min, under awake and general anesthetic conditions. These findings suggest that intraoperative ERNA can provide a rapid, objective, and practical starting point for STN‐DBS programming. © 2026 The Author(s). Subthalamic nucleus (STN) deep brain stimulation (DBS) can greatly improve quality of life in Parkinson's disease (PD), 1 , 2 but efficacy depends on accurate stimulation of the dorsolateral STN, making optimal programming essential. 3 , 4

Modern DBS systems provide directional steering and multiple independent current control (MICC) to optimize targeting. 5 Yet, exploiting these features is time‐consuming, expertise‐dependent, and burdensome for patients and clinicians. 6 , 7 Clinical programming remains heuristic, with many effects delayed by hours to days, so that prolonged or frequent programming sessions are usual. Suboptimal results are common, especially in less experienced centers. 8

Objective tools aim to streamline this process. Imaging‐based platforms may shorten programming times compared with traditional monopolar reviews, but motor outcomes are variable, and image processing and specialized software are required. 7 , 9 , 10 Whilst it is uncertain whether functional rather than anatomical localization may be more effective 11 , 12 , 13 , neural biomarkers offer a physiology‐based alternative. Beta‐band local field potentials have been studied, 14 , 15 but are small, greatly attenuated by microlesion effect (even intraoperatively), 16 , 17 , 18 and by general anesthesia. 19 , 20

Evoked resonant neural activity (ERNA) may overcome these limitations: ERNA is a relatively large signal resembling a decaying oscillation that is evoked by DBS pulses, and recordable from the DBS lead. 21 , 22 In the STN, ERNA is minimally attenuated under general anesthesia, appears maximal in the dorsal region, and modulates with motor state and beta band power including during DBS. 19 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 It is postulated that ERNA reflects reciprocal activity between the globus pallidus externa and STN, part of the dysfunctional network in PD. 25 ERNA has also been recorded from the globus pallidus interna 26 and from patients with dystonia, 27 raising potential relevance for other movement disorders. We previously showed that ERNA recorded from STN quadripolar leads in awake PD patients predicts optimal contacts for chronic stimulation. 23 Here, we assess whether such ERNA recorded intraoperatively, both awake and under general anesthesia, can guide programming with directional leads and MICC. We compare ERNA‐based configurations to clinical and imaging‐based approaches, evaluating acute motor benefit and stimulation characteristics.

Patients and Methods

Participants

Participants were patients with PD, implanted with STN‐DBS for motor fluctuations, with ERNA recorded intraoperatively. Postoperative management, including programming, followed standard clinical practice, uninformed by ERNA. After clinical stabilization at 4–6 months, 12 subjects (9 male) (Table S1) were recruited into an acute study comparing three programming conditions.

This was a subset of a larger international, multicenter, prospective observational study ‘ADEPT’ (Assisted Determination of Electrode Positioning and Therapy for Deep Brain Stimulation ACTRN12621001323819), which assesses the reliability, safety, and potential clinical relevance of STN ERNA in patients with PD (including their clinical outcomes).

Twenty‐nine subjects with preoperative Movement Disorder Society‐Unified Parkinson's Disease Rating Scale‐Part III (MDS‐UPDRS‐III) rigidity or bradykinesia score ≥ 2 in the worst affected hemibody and levodopa response ≥40% were eligible. Ten declined participation (Fig. S1). Six were excluded by the treating clinician as they were deemed unlikely to tolerate the study protocol. One was excluded as having insufficient ERNA for operation of the programming algorithm (root‐mean‐square [RMS] recorded across electrodes on the lead <50 μV or corrupted by noise/artefact). Informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was approved by the St. Vincent's Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC‐154/21).

Surgical Procedure and ERNA Recordings

Stereotactic frame‐based DBS implantations (seven general anesthetic, five awake) were performed by the same neurosurgeon (K.B.)‐neurologist (W.T.) team across three centers in Melbourne, Australia. The standard‐of‐care surgical workflow included microelectrode recordings (MER) using Guideline5™ (FHC Inc., Bowdoin, ME, USA) followed by on‐table clinical assessments in response to stimulation. Bilateral eight‐electrode directional leads (Cartesia™ DB‐2022) and a MICC pulse generator (Vercise Genus™, Boston Scientific Corp., Valencia, CA, USA) were implanted. Lead implantation aimed to align the tiers with directional electrodes (tiers 2 and 3) to the dorsal STN, with tier 1 in the ventral STN and tier 4 above the STN border. Anterior orientation of the radio‐opaque marker was intended, and leads were manually rotated under single plane fluoroscopy if orientation correction was needed. Lead position was assessed with co‐registration of preoperative 3‐Tesla magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and either intraoperative computed tomography (CT) (mobile Airo® CT, BrainLab, Munich, Germany) or postoperative CT (Siemens, Munich, Germany) according to the center's usual surgical practice.

ERNA was recorded from the DBS lead (~45 s per lead) immediately following lead fixation to the skull. The recording system comprised a neurostimulator (neuroBi, Bionics Institute, Australia), 28 biosignal amplifier (g.HIamp, g.tec Medical Engineering, Austria), and custom software written in the C# programming language to synchronize stimulation and recording. 19 , 29

ERNA was evoked with constant‐current burst stimulation comprised of 10 symmetric, biphasic pulses (3.36 mA, 130 Hz, 60 μs per phase, negative phase first). Neural activity was recorded on the stimulated electrode following the end of the last stimulation pulse for 100 ms at a sampling rate of 19,200 Hz (24‐bit). This paradigm was repeated 10 times per electrode before addressing the next electrode in sequence. Two patch electrodes on left and right shoulders were used for the amplifier reference and stimulation return, respectively. Recorded signals were re‐referenced to a weighted average of the eight unstimulated electrodes in the contralateral hemisphere and processed using a 130–1000 Hz backwards‐filter. 29 Average power (squared RMS) was calculated from a 3.5–20 ms post‐stimulus window to avoid stimulus artifact.

ERNA: Spatial Distribution Across Leads

The spatial profile of ERNA RMS across leads was characterized to explore its relationship with the optimal stimulation location defined by Dembek et al. 3 Mean ERNA RMS differed across tiers (for directional tiers, average RMS of electrodes was used) (F(2.04, 22.43) = 23.79, P < 0.001, n 2 P = 0.68). ERNA RMS was greater in directional tiers compared with tiers 1 and 4 (tier 1 vs. 2, P < 0.001; tier 1 vs. 3, P = 0.006; tier 4 vs. 2, P < 0.001, tier 4 vs. 3, P < 0.001).

Mean ERNA RMS differed across individual electrodes within directional tiers (F(2.86, 31.44) = 15.14, P < 0.001, n 2 P = 0.58). ERNA RMS was greater in anterior compared with medial electrodes (tier 2 anterior vs. medial, P = 0.006; tier 3 anterior vs. medial, P = 0.013). ERNA RMS was greater in lateral compared with medial electrodes at tier 3 (P = 0.039) but not tier 2 (P = 0.175). Figure 1B shows the grouped mean ERNA RMS recorded from each electrode.

It is possible that the differing geometry of directional and omnidirectional contacts gives rise to differences in the volume of recorded tissue. Any potential effect on ERNA RMS was not systematically addressed but is presumed to be symmetrical around the lead midpoint.

To investigate the influence of general anesthesia, RMS values were normalized to mean RMS within each lead to generate z‐scores (number of standard deviations from the mean) and electrodes were ranked based on these scores. A nested mixed‐effect model with subjects as random effects found that general anesthesia did not alter electrode ranking (β = 0, 95% CI –0.94 to 0.94, P = 1) (Fig. S2).

Euclidean distances from the estimated ideal stimulation location 3 to the center of each recording electrode (surface midpoint for directional electrodes) were calculated. ERNA RMS correlated with a shorter distance between the electrode and ideal location (r = −0.43, P < 0.001) (Fig. 2).

Programming Conditions: Spatial Distribution

Programming conditions were compared by representing current fractionation as a single midpoint in relation to the lead axis, weighted by the relative distribution of current delivered by each electrode. The longitudinal and radial components of each program's weighted midpoint were compared as follows. A three‐dimensional lead model was reconstructed and the percentage of fractionated current at the center of each electrode surface was represented in native space along the longitudinal lead axis (z‐coordinate) and in a plane parallel to the AC‐PC plane (x‐ and y‐coordinates).

The longitudinal component, r z , was calculated by multiplying the z‐coordinate of the center of each electrode surface by its percentage current, then dividing by 100%. r z was positive when dorsal, and negative when ventral, to the lead midpoint (midway along the lead axis between the two directional tiers) (Supporting Information S6). r z differed across conditions (F(2, 22) = 12.25, P < 0.001). Clinical Motor Assessments

Acute clinical assessments were performed in an off‐medication state (withheld >12 hr), 4–6 months after DBS implantation (mean 143 ± 21.2 days). One clinician applied DBS and another, blinded, performed assessments. Subjects were also blinded. DBS was switched off bilaterally for 30–60 min and baseline MDS‐UPDRS‐III was assessed. The three programming conditions were applied to the lead contralateral to the worst affected hemibody in counterbalanced order with a 30‐min stimulation wash‐out between conditions. The other lead remained off‐stimulation throughout. One hemibody (the most affected) was assessed, to minimize patient fatigue (given protocol duration 4–5 hr), and ipsilateral and ceiling effects.

For each programming condition, an optimal stimulation amplitude was determined by increasing current from 1 to 5 mA in 0.5 mA increments, every minute. Concurrently, akinesia, rigidity, and tremor were rated using MDS‐UPDRS‐III subscores (items 3.3, 3.4, 3.6, 3.8, 3.17, total score/20). Stimulation was applied at the same frequency and pulse width as usual clinical programming (7/12 at 130 Hz and 5/12 at either 85, 95, 112, or 128 Hz, all at 60 μs). After reaching a preliminary therapeutic plateau (TP; minimum current that elicited the lowest motor subscore), a 10‐min wash‐in period was followed by further refinements in 0.1 mA steps to establish the optimized TP. Side effect thresholds (SET) were defined by further increasing amplitude until persistent (>1 min) adverse effects or 5 mA, whichever was lower. If no SET was reached, 5.5 mA was used for analysis. Subsequently, a complete MDS‐UPDRS‐III was assessed at the optimized TP. In subjects who failed to reach a TP up to 5 mA (n = 1), or who developed side effects before TP (n = 1), assessment was completed at 5 mA or the highest tolerated amplitude, respectively.

Data and Statistical Analysis

MDS‐UPDRS‐III (worst hemibody) scores (items 3.3–3.8, 3.15–3.17, total score/44) were compared between programming conditions, the postoperative off‐medication/off‐stimulation state, and the preoperative on‐medication state.

Change in MDS‐UPDRS‐III (worst hemibody) for each condition was calculated as a percentage relative to the postoperative off‐medication/off‐stimulation state. This percentage change was also compared with the preoperative levodopa response, to yield the motor response ratio (MRR) as shown below. Statistical analyses were conducted with Python v3.12 (Python Software Foundation, www.python.org). Normally‐distributed data (per Shapiro–Wilk tests) were analyzed using repeated‐measures analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by post‐hoc Tukey's tests or multiple pairwise tests with Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction. Where sphericity assumptions were violated, Greenhouse–Geisser corrections were applied. For nonparametric data, Friedman tests were followed by post‐hoc Conover tests and Holm–Bonferroni corrections. Effect of general anesthesia on ERNA‐P outcomes were assessed using Mann–Whitney U test. Corrected P‐values are reported and P‐values <0.05 were considered significant. Correlations were assessed using repeated‐measures correlation coefficients. Lead orientations were expressed as unit vectors to calculate circular mean, standard deviation, and resultant vector length (r; closer to 1 if all leads orientated in the same direction).

Results

MDS‐UPDRS‐III Hemibody Scores

MDS‐UPDRS‐III hemibody scores differed across conditions (F(2.8, 31.2) = 30.5, P < 0.001).

MDS‐UPDRS‐III hemibody scores were greater in the off‐medication/off‐stimulation state (median 15.5 [interquartile range (IQR) 5.0]) than every programming condition (Clinical‐P 4.0 [4.8], P < 0.001; ERNA‐P 3.5 [2.5], P < 0.001; Imaging‐P 2.0 [4.0], P < 0.001). Clinical‐P scores were greater than Imaging‐P scores (P = 0.006). Scores did not differ between ERNA‐P and Clinical‐P (P = 0.23) or between ERNA‐P and Imaging‐P (P = 0.08).

MDS‐UPDRS‐III hemibody scores for all programming conditions did not differ from the preoperative on‐medication state (F(2.8, 31.2) = 2.3, P = 0.10).

MDS‐UPDRS‐III Hemibody Percentage Change

MDS‐UPDRS‐III hemibody percentage change differed across programming conditions (F(1.8, 20.2) = 3.8, P = 0.04). Percentage improvement was greater for Imaging‐P than Clinical‐P (median 81.6% [IQR 24.9] vs. 68.8% [19.3], P = 0.04) (Fig. 5). Percentage improvement did not differ between ERNA‐P (75.7% [15.4]) and Clinical‐P (P = 0.33) or between ERNA‐P and Imaging‐P (P = 0.19).

Motor Response Ratio

MRR differed across programming conditions (F(1.8, 20.2) = 3.8, P = 0.04). MRR was greater for Imaging‐P than Clinical‐P (median 1.1 [IQR 0.5] vs. 0.9 [0.1], P = 0.04). MRR did not differ between ERNA‐P (1.0 [0.1]) and Clinical‐P (P = 0.33) or between ERNA‐P and Imaging‐P (P = 0.19).

TP did not differ across programming conditions (F(2,22) = 0.11, P = 0.90: mean Clinical‐P 3.2 ± 0.8 mA, ERNA‐P 3.1 ± 0.8 mA, and Imaging‐P 3.2 ± 0.8 mA).

Discussion

In this cohort of 12 patients with Parkinson's disease, DBS leads were anatomically well placed in the STN, within 1.2 mm of estimated sweet spots (well within the error margin for sweet spot localization). The acute hemibody motor benefit for all programming conditions did not differ from the preoperative on‐medication state, consistent with clinically well‐placed leads. ERNA‐P motor outcomes did not differ from Imaging‐P or Clinical‐P. Outcomes of Imaging‐P were better than Clinical‐P. Side effect thresholds did not differ across programming conditions.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The study population was small, with well‐placed leads, and participation bias may have precluded patients who had not achieved their desired clinical outcomes. How the ERNA algorithm would perform with poorly placed leads is uncertain. Acute hemibody outcomes may not fully represent chronic bilateral stimulation, particularly for axial symptoms, delayed side effects, and accounting for ipsilateral effects (which can contribute up to 17% of benefit). 36 As the aim of this study was to isolate the effect of differing stimulation locations (from each condition), we always employed the same stimulation frequency and pulse width used by subjects' chronic clinical programs (with such parameters presumably optimized for chronic bilateral effects).

Nonetheless, chronic clinical programming may itself be constrained by patient fatigue, clinician experience, time limitations, and symptoms that evolve over different latencies. It is infeasible to exhaustively test all electrode combinations and, as expected, clinical programs in this study were less complex (directional in 1/12) than Imaging‐P and ERNA‐P (directional 9/12 for both). The greater configuration complexity may partly explain the advantage of Imaging‐P over Clinical‐P. Prior studies have also reported greater utilization of MICC capabilities with imaging‐based compared with clinical programming, affording similar or better efficacy. 6 , 9 , 37 However, notably in this study, acute deployment of the chronic clinician programs achieved a median 69% hemibody improvement, similar to their 73% preoperative levodopa response (MRR 0.95).

The novel feature of this study was the use of intraoperative ERNA to generate programming configurations. ERNA recordings required only ~45 s per lead, obtained after lead fixation to mitigate any confound of pre‐fixation rotation. The surgical technique aimed to place directional tiers in the dorsal STN, which in all patients corresponded to regions where ERNA was maximal. ERNA profiles followed a consistent pattern across leads, with anterior and lateral electrodes showing the greatest amplitudes, raising the possibility that ERNA may be maximal more antero‐laterally within the dorsolateral STN. This anterior bias is consistent with findings of another small cohort study. 38 Further supporting its potential clinical utility, ERNA RMS correlated with a shorter distance between the recorded electrode and an estimated ideal stimulation location.

Consistent with our previous work, 19 we did not find that general anesthetic meaningfully altered the spatial distribution of ERNA – a major advantage of this signal. Of note, we used ERNA recorded intraoperatively to develop programs for use 4–6 months later. Lead rotation during this period (below our detection limit of ~12°) could therefore have confounded performance.

ERNA‐P configurations slightly differed from Clinical‐P and Imaging‐P configurations, with current fractionation located ~1 mm ventral and ~0.3 mm posterior. This may reflect intrinsic properties of the ERNA generator (as others have reported) 38 , 39 or an unrealized bias in our signal processing. Despite this spatial offset, acute motor outcomes did not differ from the other conditions. Further work is needed to confirm whether this reflects a distinct electrophysiological target, a more optimal stimulation site, or whether algorithmic correction is warranted.

This work raises important questions for future study. Prospective studies in larger cohorts using chronic bilateral ERNA‐P will be needed to determine whether the observed separation of stimulation locations is sustained, to assess algorithm performance in real‐world clinical practice, and to clarify relationships between ERNA features and programming parameters. Recordings from chronically implanted devices are needed to assess durability of the ERNA signal (including from microlesion effects,) whether updating ERNA recordings during programming may provide more effective guidance than those taken 4–6 months prior, and any impacts of medication, diurnal rhythms, as well as DBS itself.

Overall, this study supports the feasibility of ERNA‐based programming. ERNA can be recorded rapidly, under awake or anesthetized conditions, can guide programming without involving postoperative imaging, and has the potential to greatly reduce the health service burden of programming currently required. Utilizing an uncomplicated algorithm, ERNA‐derived configurations produced efficacy and tolerability equivalent to expert clinical and imaging‐based approaches. This suggests ERNA could provide programmers with an immediate, objective, and practical starting point for STN‐DBS in PD.
