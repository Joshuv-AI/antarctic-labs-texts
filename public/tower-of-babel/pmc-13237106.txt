Severity and duration-dependent aortic stiffening in a rabbit coarctation model identifies constitutive material parameters as promising regional biomarkers for hypertension progression
Authors: Arash Ghorbannia, Jamasp Azarnoosh, John F LaDisa Jr
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCoarctation of the aorta (CoA) alters hemodynamics and drives regional remodeling, yet the influence of severity and duration on constitutive properties is undefined. We quantified mechanical responses from CoA to identify constitutive parameters capturing stiffening across clinical severities and durations. Mild (≤ 12 mmHg), intermediate (13–20 mmHg), and severe (> 20 mmHg) CoA was created in rabbits for short, long, and prolonged durations (~ 1, 3, or 22 weeks, respectively). Stress–stretch curves from proximal and distal regions were fit with multiple constitutive models (Linear Elastic, Neo-Hookean, Yeoh, Ogden, Mooney–Rivlin, Holzapfel). Model performance was evaluated by normalized RMSE and R², with reproducibility assessed using bootstrap stability of strain energy–parameter coupling. Deterministic models were compared with multi-parameter hyperelastic formulations. Marked stiffening presented proximally for several CoA severities and durations, but was limited to pronounced sustained CoA distally. Yeoh provided reliable coupling between parameters and strain energy, whereas Holzapfel–Isotropic best captured nonlinear stress–stretch behavior. Within physiological ranges, linear elastic and Yeoh fits approximated stiffness effectively. Severity and duration were significant predictors of stiffening, consistent with findings linking these CoA features to hypertension. Yeoh and linear elastic model parameters are suitable as simplified CoA markers of induced proximal stiffening, while complex formulations capture full nonlinear behavior. These parameters align clinically with proximal remodeling and reduced compliance. They may ultimately inform risk stratification including intervention timing thresholds, as well as growth-and-remodeling and AI-based temporal approaches to forecast irreversible stiffening for personalized intervention with CoA patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47753-7.Keywords: Coarctation of the aorta, Aortic stiffness, Constitutive modeling, Yeoh, Elastic modulus, Regional remodeling, Hypertension, Congenital heart disease, Growth and remodeling, Cardiovascular simulation, Pediatric cardiologySubject terms: Cardiology, Engineering, Medical research, Physiology

Introduction

Coarctation of the aorta (CoA) is a congenital constriction in the proximal descending thoracic aorta that comprises ~ 5–8% of all structural congenital heart lesions1. Surgical intervention can alleviate the blood pressure (BP) gradient presenting as a result of the coarctation and long-term survival has improved dramatically as a result2. Guidelines for intervention include a transcatheter peak-to-peak CoA pressure gradient > 20 mmHg and reports often regard this criterion as hemodynamically significant3. However, long-term evaluation of surgically repaired patients reveals substantial morbidity with hypertension being among the most notable4–6. Importantly, these poor outcomes question the utility of current screening approaches in preventing refractory hypertension that seems to persist post-treatment.

In general, arteries are assumed to have the ability to adapt to mechanical stimuli (perturbation from a normal state) and recover a homeostatic state via smooth muscle (SM) cell synthesis and matrix turnover7–9. In a pathophysiologic setting such as CoA where a deviation from the homeostatic state presents, these mechanisms have been shown to initiate aberrations including arterial thickening, stiffening, and vasoactive dysfunction initially triggered to recover the preferred homeostatic state9,10. Particularly in regions exposed to adversely elevated BP (i.e., proximal to the CoA), an adverse increase in structural stiffness (e.g., from the vascular adaptations to CoA mentioned above) leads to faster forward and reflected traveling pulse pressure waves that are summated, leading to augmented systolic blood pressure (SBP) and an elevated pulse pressure (PP)11 causing further deviation from the hemostatic state. While these changes in BP may subside after successful removal of the CoA, there is a growing body of literature suggesting that aortic tissue material property changes derived from CoA-induced stimuli may be permanent beyond a certain threshold of stimuli that has yet to be elucidated12,13. Precursors such as arterial stiffening may continue to evolve through a positive homeostatic feedback loop ultimately leading to refractory hypertension commonly seen post-treatment.

The mechanisms of hypertension among treated CoA patients are complex and challenging to investigate mechanistically due to heterogeneity and a relatively small number of patients at a given center. BP is consistent among most mammalian species14 making preclinical (animal) models a reasonable choice to mimic CoA within a feasible timeframe and cost. Preclinical models also offer an experimentally controlled condition for longitudinal investigation of underlying mechanisms involved in vascular alterations that persist despite treatment of CoA. Importantly, the use of invasive experiments, such as material testing, allows for the quantification of arterial thickening and stiffening in response to controlled severity and durations of the disease that are important clinically. For example, a rabbit model of CoA was previously used to specifically monitor and characterize the evolution of hypertension precursors in response to the severity and duration of the CoA under experimentally controlled conditions, with predictors then mapped to those from CoA patients13,15. Results showed hypertension precursors, including arterial thickening, stiffening, and dysfunction similar to those reported for patients with CoA11,16 can persist at severities milder than the current putative guideline (i.e., 20 mmHg peak-to-peak CoA gradient) and that the duration of the mechanical stimuli also correlates with quantified vascular impairments10.

To better understand the maladaptive remodeling of aorta responsible for hypertension, it is essential to identify reproducible constitutive markers of arterial stiffness post CoA-repair. Strain energy, quantified as the area under the stress–stretch curve, represents an aggregate measure of tissue mechanics that directly reflects pulse wave propagation17. Importantly, evaluating strain energy within physiologic deformation ranges (e.g., diastolic or systolic stretch) captures the segments of the cardiac cycle most relevant to pulse wave transmission15. Constitutive hyperelastic models offer a complementary approach by parameterizing arterial behavior into interpretable material constants. Yet, while multi-parameter formulations (e.g., Mooney–Rivlin, Holzapfel) reproduce nonlinear mechanics with high fidelity, their parameter estimates often lack uniqueness, undermining their utility as predictive biomarkers7,18. In contrast, simpler deterministic models (e.g., Linear Elastic, Neo-Hookean) sacrifice fidelity but provide uniquely identifiable parameters that can be robustly compared across groups. Here we integrate these approaches in a rabbit model of CoA to disentangle fidelity from determinism and identify biomechanical predictors most strongly associated with refractory hypertension precursors.

The current work aims to identify the characteristics and patterns of pathological arterial thickening and stiffening in response to coarctation at various locations under experimentally controlled conditions and identify reliable constitutive model choices to predict alterations in mechanical properties associated with hypertension progression post-CoA correction. Our expectation is that the results may shed light on the extent of alterations caused by severity and duration of the exposure to CoA-induced stimuli, guiding future guidelines for more accurate assessment of the risks involved in refractory HTN in CoA patients.

Methods

Preclinical model of CoA

All animal procedures were reviewed and approved by the Animal Care and Use Committees of Marquette University and the Medical College of Wisconsin. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (National Research Council, 8th edition) and the Guiding Principles in the Care and Use of Animals of the American Physiological Society compatible with ARRIVE guideline (https://arriveguidelines.org) recommendations. Hypertension precursors, including arterial thickening and stiffening, were quantified following previously published protocols for a rabbit model mimicking CoA in patients19. After approval as mentioned above, CoA was surgically induced under Isoflurane in New Zealand white rabbits (total 48; n = 2–7/group) by tying a suture around the aorta to varying diameters at ~ 10 weeks of age19. CoA severity was assessed via peak-to-peak BP gradient (BPGpp) within the clinical ranges of mild (≤ 12 mmHg), intermediate (13–20 mmHg), and severe (> 20 mmHg) by partially ligating the aorta via suturing against a wire of known diameter (i.e., 2.7, 2.0, or 1.6 mm, respectively) and then removing the wire. Importantly, current treatment guidelines reference a BPGpp >20 mmHg measured, by catheterization, or non-invasively through Doppler echocardiography, anatomic evidence of CoA, and a BPGpp >10 mmHg via catheterization or Doppler with decreased left ventricular systolic function or significant collateral flow20. To further study the ramifications of mechanical stimuli caused by the duration a CoA is present, sutures with different dissolving properties (i.e., rapid dissolvable, dissolvable, and permanent) were used to initiate a stenosis for different durations of ~ 1, 3, and 22 weeks named short, long, and prolonged CoA, respectively. These durations were selected to capture temporally distinct phases of pressure overload–induced remodeling-early, intermediate, and prolonged exposure-when scaled to human developmental stages using established rabbit–human age-mapping frameworks based on growth and life-stage progression21. Importantly, sutures were tied around the proximal descending thoracic aorta (distal to the left subclavian branch) where CoA appears most often. Weekly ultrasound imaging was used, as described elsewhere, to monitor the narrowing and the associated pressure gradient from which the reported durations were derived12,19,22. Together, this combination resulted in 9 study groups (3 severities and 3 durations) as well as a Control group that was used as a baseline. Weekly Doppler ultrasound imaging was performed until BPGpp estimates via simplified Bernoulli Equation no longer changed with body weight. Following the end of the protocol, animals were euthanized by intravenous overdose of pentobarbital sodium (100 mg/kg) as an approved humane endpoint.

Coarctation-induced arterial stiffening was studied by quantifying material properties from within the proximal descending thoracic aorta (between the left subclavian branch and the CoA) and the distal descending thoracic aorta at the diaphragm level. These regions were selected due to exposure to adversely altered BP on the two sides of induced narrowing. To characterize the most pressure-sensitive stress component, material properties were characterized in the circumferential direction using uniaxial extension testing (MTS Criterion Load Frame, MTS, Minneapolis, U.S.A.) at 37 °C in an environmental chamber (MTS Bionix EnviroBath, Minneapolis, U.S.A.). Aortic tissue segments proximal and distal to CoA were excised following protocol completion, and tissues were immediately placed in ice-cold physiological salt solution. Tissue samples were dissected with a length-to-width ratio of ~ 2.6 and preconditioned by stretching to 10% of the gauge length23. Extension testing was performed at 10 mm/min until hyperelastic behavior appeared23,24.

Because strain energy integrates the stress–stretch response over a defined stretch range, it was selected as the primary benchmark of arterial stiffness. This aggregate metric is physiologically meaningful, as it reflects the energy required to deform the vessel wall and directly influences pulse wave propagation. Accordingly, strain energy was computed for each tissue strip as described below. For each strip, the raw mechanical response was obtained as a series of paired stretch–stress data points, (). To enable comparison across samples with different maximum stretches, the global strain energy range obtained across all samples was partitioned into 30 equally spaced intervals between minimum and maximum values. Within each strain energy interval, stress and stretch values were averaged across strips of the same study group. To ensure robustness, only intervals with contributions from at least two independent tissue strips were included in downstream analysis. All strain‑energy values reported in the manuscript were obtained by integrating the stress–stretch curve from the undeformed length (λ = 1.0) up to a common upper stretch limit of λ = 2.0 for every tissue specimen. No specimen was truncated or extrapolated; the same λ‑range (1.0–2.0) was used for all groups.

Constitutive material modeling

The stress-stretch fitting analysis employed Linear Elastic, neo-Hookean, Yeoh, Mooney-Rivlin, Ogden, Fung, and Holzapfel hyperelastic models to characterize the biomechanical behavior of tissues17,26. Arterial tissues exhibit nonlinear, anisotropic stress–stretch behavior, requiring models that can represent both baseline stiffness and higher-order stiffening responses.

We therefore defined two classes of constitutive models:

Deterministic models: simple, low-parameter formulations designed to yield uniquely identifiable and reproducible parameter sets. These included the Linear Elastic model, Neo-Hookean, Fung-exponential, and reduced Ogden formulations with fixed exponent. Deterministic models may sacrifice fidelity at high stretches but provide stable markers of stiffness that can be compared directly.

Complex models: higher-order, multi-parameter formulations capable of reproducing the nonlinear toe region and exponential stiffening observed in aortic tissue. These included the Yeoh model, Mooney–Rivlin variants (2-, 3-, and 5-parameter), and structurally motivated Holzapfel formulations (isotropic and anisotropic). While these models achieve excellent goodness-of-fit, their parameter estimates are prone to non-uniqueness and overfitting, which limits their interpretability.

where and ​ are the predicted and experimental stresses and given stretch , respectively. Parameter bounds were constrained to positive values to avoid unphysical behavior (e.g., decreasing stress with increasing stretch). In addition to NRMSE, model performance was also evaluated in terms of mean squared error (MSE), and coefficient of determination (R²). Optimization was performed via local gradient-based methods (L-BFGS-B) and global evolutionary algorithms (differential evolution).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were designed to evaluate both group-level differences in experimental responses and the reliability of constitutive model parameters as potential biomarkers of arterial stiffening.

Group comparisons of empirical stiffness

Strain energy was used as an aggregate measure of arterial stiffness. For each aortic region (proximal and distal), a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to test for overall differences among experimental groups defined by coarctation severity and duration. When significant main effects were detected, pairwise contrasts were performed using Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) procedure. Tukey’s method was chosen for its control of the familywise error rate in the presence of multiple comparisons, ensuring robust inference across groups. All significance tests were two-sided with multiple pre-specified α = 0.05, 0.01, 0.001, 0.0001 denoted by *, **, ***, **** in results.

Verification of constitutive parameters

To evaluate whether constitutive models replicate strain energy findings, parameter estimates from fitted models were analyzed in parallel using the same ANOVA–Tukey framework. For deterministic formulations (e.g., Linear Elastic, Neo-Hookean, and Reduced Ogden), parameters were uniquely identifiable, allowing direct statistical inference across groups. For complex formulations (e.g., Mooney–Rivlin, Holzapfel), where parameter non-uniqueness may obscure interpretation, statistical tests were coupled with goodness-of-fit metrics (MSE, NRMSE, R²). This dual criterion ensured predictors of stiffening are identified based on both performance and reproducibility.

Reliability and stability analysis

To assess the robustness of parameter–phenotype associations, regression-based models of strain energy were extended to include constitutive parameters as covariates, alongside experimental factors (severity, duration, body weight, sex). Robust standard errors (HC3) were used to mitigate heteroskedasticity, and false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied across families of hypotheses to control for type I error inflation. In addition, bootstrap resampling at the specimen level (n = 1000 iterations) quantified stability of effect estimates, reporting median coefficients, confidence bounds (2.5th − 97.5th percentiles), and consistency of sign/directionality.

Simpler single-invariant models such as Neo-Hookean and 2-parameter Mooney–Rivlin consistently underperformed, with mean R² values of ~ 0.69 (distal) and ~ 0.66 (proximal) and very high MSE (> 2,600 in distal; >11,000 in proximal). The Ogden-1 reduced model also performed well (R² ≈ 0.95 distal; 0.96 proximal). In contrast, the anisotropic Holzapfel-4 model showed reduced stability, with lower average R² (0.86 proximal; 0.91 distal) and high variability across resamples.

Deterministic constitutive markers of arterial stiffness

To evaluate the reproducibility and interpretability of stiffness markers, we contrasted parameter estimates from deterministic single-parameter models with strain energy–based measures, using bootstrapped regression and experimental range analyses.

Proximal aorta

Bootstrapped regressions confirmed high reproducibility of the effects of Severity (Severe) and Duration on proximal stiffening. Severe CoA showed consistent positive associations with stiffness (support 96%, sign consistency 98%, β ≈ 32.1 [1.4–64.3]), while Duration exhibited perfect reproducibility (support 100%, sign consistency 100%, β ≈ 3.1 [1.8–4.4]). By contrast, effects of Body Weight, Sex, and Mild CoA were inconsistent, with wide confidence intervals overlapping zero. Strain energy analysis confirmed these findings, with strong, highly significant effects of Severe CoA (β ≈ 33, p and q < 0.0001) and Duration (β ≈ 3.1, p and q < 0.0001), while Mild CoA and sex were non-significant (q > 0.65).

Distal aorta

Severe CoA and Duration also emerged as reproducible and significant determinants of distal stiffening. Severe CoA showed very strong support (support 99%, sign consistency 100%, β ≈ 20.5 [7.1–36.8]), and Duration again reached high reproducibility (support 91%, sign consistency 94%, β ≈ 0.43 [–0.08–0.95]). Strain energy confirmed robust contributions of Severe CoA (β ≈ 21.2, p and q < 0.0001) and Duration (β ≈ 0.42, p and q < 0.0001). In contrast, Mild CoA effects were inconsistent across bootstraps (support 80%, sign consistency 82%) and lost significance in strain energy analysis (q < 0.0001 but negative effect). Sex showed moderate but inconsistent effects (support 82%, sign consistency 76%; β ≈ 4.1, CI − 8.0–18.0). Body Weight again was non-significant (q > 0.7).

Bootstrap analysis of strain energy–parameter coupling highlighted clear differences in the reproducibility of constitutive models across regions and deformation ranges. In the proximal aorta, parameters from the Yeoh family (particularly p2 in Yeoh-2 and p3 in Yeoh-3) consistently achieved perfect reproducibility across default, systolic, and physiological ranges, while Mooney–Rivlin variants also demonstrated highly stable coupling, especially in the diastolic and systolic ranges. Reduced Ogden reached full reproducibility in the proximal diastolic range but showed diminished reliability in other ranges. Holzapfel formulations, although capable of capturing nonlinear mechanics, exhibited weaker or inconsistent reproducibility of parameter coupling, reflecting greater susceptibility to parameter non-uniqueness.

In the distal aorta, Yeoh-2 and Yeoh-3 again emerged as the most reliable models, with constitutive parameters achieving complete reproducibility in default, systolic, and diastolic ranges. Mooney–Rivlin models similarly produced stable coupling in the systolic range, with C20 parameters repeatedly aligning with strain energy changes in Mooney-Rivlin(5-par). Reduced Ogden parameters showed robust reproducibility in the diastolic range distally but were less stable across other ranges. As in the proximal region, Holzapfel models demonstrated comparatively lower reproducibility, indicating that their additional parameters, while biologically motivated, did not consistently provide deterministic stiffness markers.

Among the models evaluated, the Yeoh-2/3 and Mooney–Rivlin formulations consistently showed high reproducibility across all regions and ranges, with support and sign-consistency near 100%, benchmarks of stiffness. By contrast, the Reduced Ogden model showed selective reliability. In the proximal aorta under diastolic loading, Reduced Ogden parameters were highly stable (100% support, 100% sign consistency), indicating excellent reproducibility. However, in the same region under default and systolic ranges, the reliability dropped markedly, with support values as low as 30–50% and broad confidence intervals, suggesting that parameter estimates became unstable. A similar trend was observed distally: Reduced Ogden showed reproducible results in the diastolic range, but default and physiological fits were more variable, with moderate reliability and inconsistent sign behavior.

Distally, C₂₀ remained significantly higher in Long-Severe and Prolong-Severe groups than in Controls (p ≈ 0.002–0.001). These findings mirror the hierarchical separation of strain-energy across the same groups, confirming that the deterministic stability observed for C₂₀ in bootstrap coupling corresponds to reproducible, physiologically meaningful differences in aortic wall stiffness.

The Yeoh-3 model demonstrated nearly identical patterns. ANOVA confirmed strong group dependence of C₃₀ (proximal p = 2.16 × 10⁻¹⁵; distal p = 0.0013). Proximal post-hoc analyses revealed significant elevations of C₃₀ in Prolong-Severe, Prolong-Mild, and Prolong-Intermediate CoA groups relative to Controls (all p < 0.05), as well as in Long-Mild and Long-Severe subgroups (p < 0.01). Distally, similar trends were observed, with C₃₀ significantly higher in Long-Severe, Long-Mild, and Prolong-Severe groups compared with Controls (p ≈ 0.03–0.05). Collectively, these results show that the higher-order Yeoh-3 parameter captures the same directional increase in stiffness with lesion duration and severity, extending the statistical evidence of determinism beyond C₂₀ to higher polynomial terms.

Discussion

The present study established a systematic mechanical characterization of CoA-induced aortic remodeling, revealing distinct regional, temporal, and severity-dependent patterns of stiffening. Across experimental groups, the proximal aorta exhibited the most pronounced increases in strain energy and constitutive stiffness parameters, while distal regions showed more selective responses confined to the most severe or prolonged constrictions. These findings reinforce that the mechanical consequences of CoA are not uniform along the vessel but depend strongly on both hemodynamic exposure and the duration of abnormal loading.

The main findings are threefold. First, both empirical stress–stretch behavior and hyperelastic fits confirmed marked stiffening in the proximal descending thoracic aorta, especially under prolonged and severe CoA, while distal changes were comparatively muted. Second, reproducible parameter estimates were obtained using Yeoh-2/3 formulations and linear elastic approximations in restricted ranges, supporting their utility as deterministic markers of stiffness. Third, these constitutive indices were strongly coupled to CoA severity and duration, consistent with the role of sustained mechanical loading in driving remodeling.

Regional and duration-dependent remodeling

The progressive elevation of strain energy and constitutive stiffness in the proximal aorta with increasing duration and severity of constriction indicates a cumulative remodeling response to sustained hypertension and altered wall stress. This interpretation is further supported by prior work on hypertension, where severity and duration of CoA were linked to adverse outcomes10–29. Although hypertension was not directly assessed here, the same predictors (CoA severity and duration) shaped arterial stiffening, reinforcing the link between biomechanical remodeling and elevated cardiovascular risk9. Mechanistically, CoA disrupts the stress components that define the vessel’s mechanical state (Fig. 9): intramural wall stress (IWS) is elevated proximally by increased BP, while wall shear stress (WSS) often rises distally due to the CoA velocity jet. In constrained mixture theory of growth and remodeling, rates of changes scale with these deviations as arterial tissue is generally assumed to have the ability to adapt to changes in stress: transient thickening can normalize stress, but persistent loading drives smooth muscle cell phenotypic shift, collagen accumulation, loss of vasoreactivity, and pulse-pressure amplification, forming a positive feedback loop that links maladaptive CoA-induced remodeling to hypertension (Fig. 9).

Mechanically driven feedback loop of maladaptation in response to CoA stimuli. The arterial wall is framed by three principal stresses—IWS in the longitudinal and circumferential directions, and WSS —which regulate homeostasis. CoA perturbs that balance, elevating intramural stress proximally and WSS distally. In constrained mixture theory, remodeling rate scales with deviations from these baseline stresses. For example, the law of Laplace implies arterial thickening could be in response to elevated BP to bring intramural stress gradually back to homeostasis levels. However, persistent loading pushes the vessel into a maladaptive regime: smooth muscle cells shift from contractile to synthetic phenotypes (driven by stretch-field cues30—increasing matrix deposition while weakening contractile machinery and active tone25,30. The return of biodegradable ligatures to near-normal strain energy values further confirms that this stiffening is at least partly reversible once normal hemodynamics are restored quickly. Conversely, when constriction persists beyond the early adaptive window due to the severity and/or duration of CoA, the remodeling transitions from reversible functional adaptation to irreversible structural stiffening (Fig. 1). This outcome is potentially due to permanent collagen accumulation and reduced elastin integrity that prevent full mechanical recovery even after repair30–32. In contrast, distal segments demonstrated more variable behavior: only severe or prolonged CoA produced measurable stiffening, consistent with localized jet impingement and disturbed flow patterns that preferentially load discrete regions of the downstream wall.

Mechanistically, distal vessels often respond to elevated WSS by vasodilation and increased tortuosity, adaptive geometric changes that act to restore near-normal shear levels, as simplified Poiseuille flow predicts that WSS declines approximately with the cube of vessel radius33. Similar compensatory enlargement and tortuosity have been reported not only in CoA34 but in other examples of exposure to adverse WSS like arteriovenous fistula models, where sudden exposure to arterial flow induces outward remodeling to dissipate shear35,36. However, these adaptations are accompanied by wall thinning, which, according to Laplace’s law, elevates IWS33 and predisposes the vessel to structural stiffening. This cascade of shear-driven dilation, wall thinning, and stress amplification provides a mechanistic explanation for the selective distal stiffening observed in the most severe CoA groups in the current and previous studies10,15. This spatial heterogeneity of stiffness and tortuosity is supported by contemporary patient data showing proximal wall thickening/fibrosis and impaired elasticity on histopathology and persistently reduced proximal distensibility even after anatomically successful repair preceding downstream adaptations leading to refractory hypertension37–42.

Constitutive modeling and deterministic markers of stiffness

Comparative model fitting highlighted that while multi-parameter hyperelastic formulations (e.g., Holzapfel–Iso, Yeoh-3) best captured nonlinear stress–stretch responses, simplified deterministic models retained strong diagnostic value. Among these, the Yeoh family of models (particularly Yeoh-2 and Yeoh-3) emerged as the most robust across bootstrap analyses, consistently yielding reproducible and interpretable parameters that scaled with CoA severity and duration. Restricting the fits to physiological ranges further revealed that Yeoh-3’s cubic term enhances sensitivity to subtle nonlinear stiffening not captured by simpler quadratic forms. Within the same stretch intervals, the Linear Elastic model provided a uniquely determined modulus that closely approximated the experimental response, emphasizing its utility as a reproducible stiffness index for comparative or longitudinal analyses.

Together, these results suggest a dual-model framework: nonlinear Yeoh formulations to quantify the full mechanical response and linear elastic fits to provide deterministic stiffness benchmarks. Such pairing allows both mechanistic interpretation and straightforward parameter tracking, aligning with the increasing emphasis on reproducible constitutive metrics in vascular biomechanics.

Mechanistic and translational context

Prior studies of CoA material properties have consistently documented proximal stiffening driven largely by collagen fiber recruitment and reorientation under chronic pressure overload. Our findings extend this knowledge by offering quantitative constitutive parameters that can be directly compared across experimental conditions, bridging the gap between qualitative trends and quantitative modeling. When expressed as percentage changes relative to controls, the observed increases in effective stiffness parameters (approximately 20–40% under severe or prolonged CoA) align closely with remodeling magnitudes reported in both animal models and patient data. These relative differences may thus provide a foundation for developing clinically meaningful thresholds—linking mechanical biomarkers with disease progression and informing postoperative monitoring after CoA repair.

Region-specific comparisons also support the concept that collagen-dominated remodeling dominates proximal responses, while distal regions retain greater elastin-like compliance unless exposed to severe or prolonged hemodynamic disturbance. The reproducible associations of the Yeoh-3 and Yeoh-2 parameters with CoA severity under systolic loading further support a shift toward collagen-driven stiffening, as reflected by the sharper exponential upturn in stress–stretch curves at high deformation. Such mechanistic insights underscore the complementary value of deterministic models for identifying remodeling signatures that are both physiologically interpretable and computationally stable.

Methodological considerations and future directions

While this study evaluated a focused set of constitutive formulations (Yeoh, Neo-Hookean, Ogden, Mooney–Rivlin, and Holzapfel variants), other models incorporating anisotropy, viscoelasticity, or fiber dispersion could yield additional insight into microstructural remodeling. However, such formulations often suffer from parameter non-uniqueness and reduced reproducibility; issues that our deterministic emphasis was designed to minimize17,26. Moreover, anisotropic formulations typically require additional tissue samples for complementary experimental or imaging modalities to identify dominant collagen fiber orientations and distributions, which were not available in the present dataset. Similarly, while biaxial testing enables more complete characterization of arterial anisotropy, uniaxial extension in the circumferential direction was selected based on the dominant pressure-driven loading associated with aortic coarctation and associated pressure gradient. Additionally, this study focused on relative differences in remodeling and stiffening proximal and distal to the coarctation across experimental groups. Consistent uniaxial testing in this principal direction was therefore sufficient to capture comparative stiffness trends, which constituted the primary objective of this work. The current dataset also represents endpoint measurements; future longitudinal analyses combining serial imaging and repeated ex vivo testing could better delineate temporal remodeling trajectories and establish a foundation for the development of data-driven predictive models using the emerging digital twins models43–45. Integrating in vivo hemodynamic metrics (e.g., pressure gradients, pulse-wave velocity) with ex vivo constitutive parameters will further strengthen translational links, allowing personalized prediction of arterial stiffness from clinical data22,29,46. Future work will prioritize tissue acquisition protocols that support more comprehensive mechanical characterization, including biaxial extension, to further quantify anisotropy and identify strategies to deal with non-uniqueness and reproducibility of characterizations when it comes to more complex anisotropic material models.

Several potential limitations should be acknowledged. We focused on a subset of constitutive models (Yeoh, Neo-Hookean, Ogden, Mooney–Rivlin, and Holzapfel variants) chosen for their established relevance in vascular biomechanics. More complex models may capture additional anisotropy or viscoelastic effects but risk parameter non-uniqueness and overfitting. Our choice to emphasize deterministic models ensures reproducibility, though it necessarily omits certain microstructural details. Future work should integrate additional constitutive models, extend to longitudinal remodeling, and link constitutive markers with in vivo hemodynamic indices to strengthen translational potential. An additional limitation relates to the experimental model used to study post-coarctation remodeling. The rabbit model reproduces the hemodynamic environment of CoA but does not recapitulate the developmental or genetic mechanisms responsible for the congenital formation of the lesion in humans. Instead, the model isolates the vascular response to controlled pressure gradients and durations of obstruction in otherwise genetically normal animals. Consequently, the observed maladaptive remodeling and arterial stiffening primarily reflect mechanobiological responses to altered hemodynamics rather than the heterogeneous genetic background present in patients with congenital CoA. While this approach enables mechanistic investigation of pressure-induced vascular remodeling, future studies integrating genetic susceptibility with hemodynamic modeling may provide a more comprehensive representation of disease pathophysiology.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that arterial stiffening due to CoA is regionally heterogeneous, with proximal segments exhibiting the most pronounced remodeling under prolonged and severe constriction. Deterministic constitutive models such as Yeoh-2/3 and linear elastic approximations provided stable, interpretable markers of stiffness, while more complex hyperelastic formulations captured the full nonlinear stress–stretch response with high fidelity. Together, these complementary approaches establish a robust framework for quantifying CoA-induced remodeling and for linking severity and duration of constriction to measurable shifts in material properties.

Clinical perspective

The findings highlight the potential for constitutive parameters to serve as translational biomarkers of vascular remodeling in CoA. Expressed as relative changes compared with normal tissue, the identified markers align with magnitudes reported in patient studies and may inform thresholds for clinical risk stratification. While direct measures of hypertension were not included here, the demonstrated dependence of stiffening on CoA severity and duration parallels prior evidence linking these predictors to adverse outcomes. Ultimately, parameter-based assessments of aortic mechanics could complement current imaging and hemodynamic evaluations, guiding personalized timing and selection of interventions.
