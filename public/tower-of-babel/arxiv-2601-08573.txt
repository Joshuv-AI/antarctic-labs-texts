Non-local singular perturbations of non-convex functionals – recent results
Andrea Braides

Abstract
Singular perturbations have been used to select solutions of (non-convex) variational problems with a multiplicity of minimizers. The prototype of such an approach is the gradient theory of phase transitions by L. Modica, who specialized some earlier Gamma-convergence results by himself and S. Mortola contained in a seminal paper, validating the so-called minimal-interface criterion. I will give an overview of some recent results on perturbations with fractional and higher-order seminorms both in the framework of phase transitions and of free-discontinuity problems, relating these results with the Bourgain-Brezis-Mironescu and Maz’ya-Shaposhnikova limit analysis for fractional Sobolev seminorms, and with the theory of Gamma-expansions. MSC codes: 49J45, 35B25, 82B26, 35R11. Keywords: Γ \Gamma -convergence, non-convex energies, higher-order fractional Sobolev spaces, phase-transition problems, free-discontinuity problems.

Introduction

I begin with some personal recollections about the early times of
\Gamma
-convergence, for which singular perturbation problems were a prototypical issue. When I started my degree thesis work under the guidance of Ennio De Giorgi in 1981, the theoretical foundations of
\Gamma
-convergence had been laid and it was already a stable theory. I got my personal idea of what had been the developments of this theory in the preceding years from those who directly worked with De Giorgi at the first developments, from Tullio Franzoni, to Sergio Spagnolo, to Luciano Modica, who was the first to give a course on
\Gamma
-convergence at Pisa University in the academic year 1982-1983.

An example that De Giorgi liked to quote was (one-dimensional) elliptic homogenization, with an oscillating coefficient taking two values (he called it the ‘Greek function’ as he would freely associate its graph to an hellenic ornamental motive). Indeed,
\Gamma
-convergence had directly stemmed from the interest in homogenization problems for elliptic operators, that De Giorgi and Spagnolo interpreted as a convergence of the energies. Even though
\Gamma
-convergence was then born as a variational counterpart of
G
-convergence, the convergence of Green’s operators for elliptic problems, it was clear to De Giorgi that the flexibility of a purely topological convergence made the new notion not bound to a specific class of problems or structure of the functionals (as had been Mosco convergence for convex functionals). Beside the theoretical developments leading to the paper in collaboration with Franzoni and successive efforts to explore all possible variants of the convergence, De Giorgi was keen on obtaining results showing how
\Gamma
-convergence was not restricted to the usual applications to homogenization, since it needed no regularity, convexity or even coerciveness assumptions. I recall the paper by Buttazzo and Dal Maso
[
13
]
, where an example is given of convergence of non-convex and non-equi-Lipschitz integral functionals with a loss of smoothness, and also my thesis work was about the development of a homogenization theory for non-coercive energies
[
6
]
. In this context the work of Modica and Mortola
[
23
]
was aimed at showing how the type of functional obtained as a
\Gamma
-limit may be completely different from the converging ones. The
functionals they examine have the form

F_{\varepsilon}(u)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}W\Big(\frac{u}{\varepsilon}\Big)dx+\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}dx,

(1)

with the explicit choice
W(z)=\sin^{2}(\pi z)
, but their result is valid for any

-periodic non-negative continuous function

vanishing only on

. The corresponding
\Gamma
-limit in the

-topology is then simply

F_{0}(u)=m_{W}\|Du\|(\Omega),\qquad m_{W}=2\int_{0}^{1}\sqrt{W(s)}ds

(2)

with domain

and with

denoting the total variation of

on
\Omega
. A second result in the paper concerns the functionals

F_{\varepsilon}(u)=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\Omega}W(u)dx+\varepsilon\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}dx,

(3)

Even though Modica and Mortola justify their analysis with possible applications to minimal surfaces, the application of their (second) result to phase-transition problems had to wait for almost a decade.

Singular perturbations are ofter used to obtain approximate solutions to problems which possess no solution or infinitely many ones, or to give a selection criterion among different solutions. One can think of the theory of viscosity solutions of non-linear PDE as an example. In a variational setting, a simple way to obtain solutions is by adding terms with (higher-order) derivatives, whose form is often dictated by modeling considerations. The prototypical example is

\int_{\Omega}W(u)dx+\varepsilon^{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}dx,

with
W
a non-convex potential. The limit of solutions
u_{\varepsilon}
to specific problems obtained using this energies are particular solutions to the corresponding problem with
\varepsilon=0
characterized by additional properties, the type of properties depending on the non-convexity of

, which can be of

.

Potentials of double-well type

with two minimizers. This is the case of the Cahn–Hilliard theory of phase separations
[

was conjectured (e.g. by Gurtin
[
19

using the

-convergence arguments by Modica and Mortola applied to energies (

Singular perturbations of double-well energies

We focus on the one-dimensional case
d=1
.
The double-well potential
W\colon\mathbb{R}\to[0,+\infty)
is a continuous function satisfying

i)
W(z)=0
if and only if
z\in\{-1,1\}
;

ii) there exist the second derivatives
W^{\prime\prime}(-1)
and
W^{\prime\prime}(1)
and are strictly positive;

iii)
\liminf\limits_{|z|\to+\infty}W(z)>0
.

The scaled perturbed functionals take the form

\displaystyle F^{k+s}_{\varepsilon}(u)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle F^{k+s}_{\varepsilon}(u,I)

(11)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{I}W(u)dx+\varepsilon^{2(k+s)-1}\int_{I\times I}\frac{|u^{(k)}(x)-u^{(k)}(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}dx\,dy,

with domain the fractional Sobolev space
H^{k+s}(I)
of the functions
u\in H^{k}(I)
such that the double integral in (
11
) (the square of the corresponding Gagliardo seminorm of
u^{(k)}
) is finite;
I
is a bounded open interval of
\mathbb{R}
. We refer to
[
20
]
for an introduction to these fractional Sobolev spaces.

A key feature of energies (
11
) is their behaviour under scaling. More precisely, if we set
v(t)=u(\varepsilon t)
then we have

F^{k+s}_{\varepsilon}(u)=F^{k+s}_{1}(v,\tfrac{1}{\varepsilon}I)=\int_{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}I}W(v)dx+\int_{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}I\times\frac{1}{\varepsilon}I}\frac{|v^{(k)}(x)-v^{(k)}(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}dx\,dy.

(12)

After this change of variables we can work on functionals depending on
\varepsilon
only through their domains. For convenience of notation, we write
F^{k+s}
in the place of
F^{k+s}_{1}
.

We will show that
F^{k+s}_{\varepsilon}
are equi-coercive with respect to the convergence in measure and their limit is a one-dimensional sharp-interface functional
F^{k+s}
, with domain piecewise-constant functions taking only the values
-1
and
1
, which we can identify with
BV(I;\{-1,1\})
. On such functions, this functional is simply

F^{k+s}(u)=m_{k+s}\#(S(u)),

(13)

determined by the non-zero
surface tension

m_{k+s}
;
S(u)
denotes the set of discontinuity points of
u
.

2.1
Coerciveness in measure

For the classical Modica–Mortola result, coerciveness can be obtained by estimating the number of
transition intervals
; that is, with fixed
\eta\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2})
, intervals
(x_{1},x_{2})
such that
x_{1}=-1+\eta
and
x_{2}=1-\eta
.
This is obtained by noting that the term
\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}|u^{\prime}|^{2}dx
is at least
\tfrac{1}{|x_{2}-x_{1}|}
, so that the energy carried by such an interval is at least
C_{\eta}\tfrac{|x_{2}-x_{1}|}{\varepsilon}+\tfrac{\varepsilon}{|x_{2}-x_{1}|}\geq 2\sqrt{C_{\eta}}>0
. This implies that
|x_{2}-x_{1}|\sim\varepsilon
and that the number of such transitions is equi-bounded. Applying the Bolzano–Weierstrass Theorem we can suppose that actually the transition intervals are independent of
\varepsilon
, and the compactness follows by noting that if
u_{\varepsilon}>1-\eta
(or
u_{\varepsilon}<1-\eta
) on a fixed interval then
u_{\varepsilon}
tends to
1
(or to
-1
, respectively). Note that the optimization of this argument actually proves the lower bound with
c_{W}
.

In the general fractional case, if
k\geq 1
this argument localized on transition intervals is not possible, simply considering functions
u_{\varepsilon}
affine on a transition interval for which the Gagliardo seminorm is
0
. The compactness argument must then use some non-local properties, which, loosely speaking, provide some extra boundary conditions to transition intervals, and is separate from the computation of the lower bound. It relies on the following local interpolation result by Solci
[
27
, Lemma 6]
.

Lemma 1

(local interpolation lemma)
.

If
F^{k+s}_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})\leq S<+\infty
for all
\varepsilon>0
, then there exists
\overline{\eta}>0
such that for all
\eta\in(0,\overline{\eta})
there exists a constant
L
such that for all intervals
J_{\varepsilon}
such that
|J_{\varepsilon}|\geq\varepsilon L
and
||u_{\varepsilon}|-1|\leq\eta
there exists at least a point
t\in J_{\varepsilon}
such that
|u_{\varepsilon}^{(\ell)}(t)|\leq\varepsilon^{-\ell}\eta
for all
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,k-1\}
.

Proof.

The proof follows by interpolation inequalities for fractional Sobolev spaces applied to either the function
u_{\varepsilon}-1
or
u_{\varepsilon}+1
, after noting that by assumption (ii) on
W
there exist positive constants
\alpha,\beta,\overline{\eta}
such that
\alpha(|z|-1)^{2}\leq W(z)\leq\beta(|z|-1)^{2}
if
||z|-1|<\overline{\eta}
.
∎

We have the following compactness theorem
[
27
, Theorem 2]
.

Theorem 2

(Compactness)
.

Let
\{u_{\varepsilon}\}
be a family in
H^{k+s}(I)
such that
F_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})\leq S<+\infty
for all
\varepsilon
. Then, there exist
u\in BV(I;\{-1,1\})
such that, up to subsequences,
u_{\varepsilon}\to u
in measure.
Furthermore, if
W
satisfies
W(z)\geq c_{1}|z|^{p}-c_{2}
for some
c_{1},c_{2}
and
p\geq 1
, then
u_{\varepsilon}\to u
in
L^{p}(I)
.

Proof.

As already observed, it suffices to show that, for fixed
\eta
,
u_{\varepsilon}
has a finite number of transitions between
1-\eta
and
-1+\eta
.
Suppose otherwise, then for all
\varepsilon
small enough there exists an interval
J_{\varepsilon}=(a_{\varepsilon},b_{\varepsilon})
containing a transition and such that
F_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon},J_{\varepsilon})=o(1)
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, and
b_{\varepsilon}-a_{\varepsilon}>kL\varepsilon
.
If
J_{\varepsilon}
contains up to
k
intervals such that
||u_{\varepsilon}|-1|<\eta
then we can apply Lemma
1
.
We deduce that the functions
v_{\varepsilon}(t)=u_{\varepsilon}(a_{\varepsilon}+t(b_{\varepsilon}-a_{\varepsilon}))
tend to a constant in
H^{s+k}(0,1)
, since they have vanishing
H^{k+s}
seminorm and
v^{(\ell)}_{\varepsilon}
is equibounded at a point. This gives a contradiction with the boundary conditions transition.

If
J_{\varepsilon}
contains more such intervals then there exists an interval of length of order
\varepsilon
with a large number of transitions. Applying Rolle’s theorem iteratively, we have the existence of points where
u^{(\ell)}(t)=0
for all
\ell\in\{1,\ldots,k-1\}
, and we can repeat the argument by contradiction.

Finally, if
W(z)\geq c_{1}|z|^{p}-c_{2}
then
\int_{\{|u_{\varepsilon}|>2\}}|u_{\varepsilon}|^{p}dx\to 0
and
(u_{\varepsilon}\vee{-2})\wedge 2
converges to
u
in
L^{p}(I)
, which proves that
u_{\varepsilon}\to u
in
L^{p}(I)
.
∎

Remark 3
.

We note that in the proof of the compactness theorem instead of
-1+\eta
and
1-\eta
we can take any two numbers
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}
such that
[\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}]\cap\{-1,1\}=\emptyset
. In particular, for every

we have that the number of transitions between

and

is equibounded, and analogously for transitions between
-1-2r
and
-1-r
.

2.2
The surface tension

The explicit characterization of the surface tension
m_{1}=c_{W}
(see (
2
)) in the Modica–Mortola result cannot be exported for other perturbations. It is convenient to characterize it in terms of an optimal-profile problem as in
[

\displaystyle m_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf\Big\{\int_{\mathbb{R}}(W(v)+|v^{\prime}|^{2})dt:v(\pm\infty)=\pm 1\Big\}

(14)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf_{T>0}\Big\{\int_{-T}^{T}(W(v)+|v^{\prime}|^{2})dt:v(\pm T)=\pm 1\Big\}.

Note that the second formula also gives an upper bound, suggesting the construction of recovery sequences of the form
u_{\varepsilon}(x)=v(\tfrac{x-\overline{x}}{\varepsilon})
or
u_{\varepsilon}(x)=-v(\tfrac{x-\overline{x}}{\varepsilon})
on intervals
(\overline{x}-\varepsilon T,\overline{x}+\varepsilon T)
with
\overline{x}\in S(u)
for
u\in BV(I;\{-1,1\})
.

This characterization of the surface tension can be exported to the fractional case as in the following proposition.

Proposition 4

(

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and

\displaystyle m_{k+s}=\inf\Big\{\int_{\mathbb{R}}W(v)dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{|v^{(k)}(x)-v^{(k)}(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}dx\,dy:

\displaystyle\hskip 128.0374ptv\in H^{k+s}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}),\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}v(x)=\pm 1\Big\}

(15)

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt=\inf_{T>0}\inf\bigg\{\int_{-T}^{+T}W(v)dt+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{|v^{(k)}(x)-v^{(k)}(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}dx\,dy:

\displaystyle\hskip 113.81102ptv\in H^{k+s}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R}),v(x)={\rm sign}\,x\hbox{ \rm if }|x|\geq T\Big\}.

(16)

In order to prove this result and the lower bound for the
\Gamma
-limit, we preliminarily state a technical cut-off lemma, taken from the proof of

Lemma 5

(A cut-off lemma)
.

Let
\eta,\sigma,T,L>0
, with
\eta<1
,

such that

[\sigma,T]

such that

\displaystyle F^{k+s}(v,\mathbb{R}))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1+\delta)F^{k+s}(u,(-T,T))

\displaystyle+\frac{C_{\delta}}{L^{2s}}\Big(F^{k+s}(u,(T-2L,T))+F^{k+s}(v,(-T,-T+2L))

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\sigma T}+(1+\sigma^{2s}T^{2s})F^{k+s}(u,(-T,T))\Big)+o(1)

The proof relies on a cut-off argument. The function

2.3
The
\Gamma
-limit

By the compactness theorem above, the functionals

are equi-coercive with respect to the convergence in measure. Hence, the corresponding

-limit result can be stated only on

Theorem 6

(sharp-interface limit)
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
s\in(0,1)
with
k+s>\frac{1}{2}
; then
we have

F^{k+s}(u)=m_{k+s}\#(S(u)).

(18)

for all
u\in BV(I;\{-1,1\})
.

Proof.

Let
u_{\varepsilon}\to u
in measure with
F^{k+s}_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})\leq S<+\infty
. Let
t_{0}\in S(u)
, and define

\widetilde{u}_{\varepsilon}(t)=u_{\varepsilon}(t_{0}+\varepsilon t)
. We have
F^{k+s}(\widetilde{u}_{\varepsilon},(-T,T))\leq S
for all
T>0
, and argue similarly to the proof of Proposition
4
. Given
\eta>0
, we fix
x_{0}
such that, up to a change of sign,
|\widetilde{u}_{\varepsilon}(x)-{\rm sign}(x)|\leq\eta
for
|x|\in[x_{0},T]
. We can construct
v_{\varepsilon}\in H^{k+s}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R})
such that
v_{\varepsilon}(x)={\rm sign}(x)
if
|x|\geq\frac{2}{3}T
and for all
\delta>0
there exists
C_{\delta}
such that

\displaystyle F^{k+s}(v_{\varepsilon},\mathbb{R}))\leq(1+\delta)F^{k+s}_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon},(t_{0}-\varepsilon T,t_{0}+\varepsilon T))+\frac{C_{\delta}}{T^{2s}}.

Since
v_{\varepsilon}
is a test function for
m_{k+s}
, summing up for
t_{0}\in S(u)
and taking into account that all
(t_{0}-\varepsilon T,t_{0}+\varepsilon T)
are disjoint for
\varepsilon
small enough, we obtain that

m_{k+s}\,\#(S(u))\leq(1+\delta)\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}F^{k+s}_{\varepsilon}(u_{\varepsilon})+\frac{C_{\delta}}{T^{2s}}.

By the arbitrariness of
T
and
\delta
we obtain the liminf inequality.

The construction of an approximate recovery sequence for a function
u\in BV(I;\{-1,1\})
follows a usual pattern:
given
\eta>0
we find
T>0
and
v\in H^{k+s}_{\rm loc}(\mathbb{R})
such that
v(x)={\rm sign}\,x
if
|x|\geq T
and

F^{k+s}(v,\mathbb{R})\leq m_{k+s}+\eta.

Let
S(u)=\{t_{1},\ldots,t_{N}\}
and define

u_{\varepsilon}(t)=\begin{cases}v\Big(\frac{u(t_{j}^{+})-u(t_{j}^{-})}{2}\cdot\frac{t-t_{j}}{\varepsilon}\Big)&\hbox{ if }|t-t_{j}|\leq\varepsilon\\
u(t)&\hbox{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

We set
x_{0}=\inf I
,
x_{j}=(t_{j+1}+t_{j})/2
for
j\in\{1,\ldots,N-1\}
and
x_{N}=\sup I
.
In order to check that
u_{\varepsilon}
is a recovery sequence, we need to show that the amount of energy due to
the interactions between
x
and
y
belonging to intervals
(x_{j_{1}-1},x_{j_{1}})
and
(x_{j_{2}-1},x_{j_{2}})
with
j_{1}\neq j_{2}
. If
k\geq 1
we note that actually the interaction is only between the intervals
(t_{j_{1}}-\varepsilon T,t_{j_{1}}+\varepsilon T)
and
(t_{j_{2}}-\varepsilon T,t_{j_{2}}+\varepsilon T)
, so that
|x-y|\sim|t_{j_{2}}-t_{j_{1}}|
independently of
\varepsilon
, which gives that these contributions are asymptotically negligible. In the case

one additionally has to note that for
{j_{2}}={j_{1}}+1
there is no interaction between pair of points in
(t_{j_{1}}+\varepsilon T,t_{j_{2}}-\varepsilon T)

F_{\varepsilon}^{k+s}(u_{\varepsilon},I)\leq(m_{k+s}+\eta)\#(S(u))+o(1)

∎

2.4
Analysis at critical exponents

The results in the previous section show that the sharp-interface limit is described by the surface tension, which then defines a function
t\mapsto m_{t}:=m_{k_{t}+s_{t}}
for

[
25
]
and Solci .

2.4.1
Analysis close the exponent

If

The second term in the functional above is scale invariant.
As is often the case for scale-invariant energies, the correct scaling turns out to be logarithmic,

]

Theorem 7
.

The functionals

Theorem 8

The scaled functionals

\displaystyle\hskip-31.2982pt\widetilde{F}^{s}_{\varepsilon}(u):={\frac{2s-1}{1-\varepsilon^{2s-1}}}F^{s}_{\varepsilon}(u)

(interpolation between logarithmic and linear regimes)

The factor

\hskip-5.69046pt\int_{I}W(u(x))dx+\varepsilon^{2s}\int_{I}\int_{I}\frac{|u(x)-u(y)|^{2}}{|x-y|^{1+2s}}dx\,dy\sim\frac{8\varepsilon(1-\varepsilon^{2s-1})}{2s-1}\#(S(u))

2.5
Some notes on the higher-dimensional case

The higher-dimensional case, when we replace the interval

Proposition 15

(equi-coerciveness)
.

Let

The proof follows from the one-dimensional result by a characterization by slicing of higher-order fractional Sobolev spaces

Identifying a function

With respect to the one-dimensional case, the comparison with the local perturbation by the

3
Singular perturbations of energies with non-convexity at infinity

The second type of non-convex potentials
W
are those with a non-convexity at infinity; that is, such that the convex envelope of
W
does not coincide with
W
on one or two half lines. Typical potentials with a well at infinity are the Lennard-Jones potential and the Perona-Malik potential
W(z)=\log(1+z^{2})
. For such potentials the results are still partial and sparse. In this section we analyze the few known results trying to draw some parallel with the previous sections. Moreover, we will mainly consider a one-dimensional environment.

3.1
Perturbations of local type

Up to a translation, it is not restrictive to suppose that
W
has a minimum point in
0
and that
W(0)=0
.
A prototypical
W
is the
truncated quadratic potential

W(z)=\min\{z^{2},1\}
. Since most of the issues are open and independent of the shape of the potential, we will restrict to using this particular potential.

As remarked in the Introduction, it is convenient to use
u^{\prime}
as a variable in the place of
u
, and suitably scale the energies, in order to avoid the necessity of defining the
\Gamma
-limit on spaces of measures.
We begin with the perturbation of minimal (integer) order; that is, with a second derivative. After scaling, the energies we consider have the form

\int_{I}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}W(\sqrt{\varepsilon}u^{\prime})dx+\varepsilon^{3}\int_{I}|u^{\prime\prime}|^{2}dx.

(28)

The power
3
is in accord with the scaling arguments already used for higher-order derivatives, as
3=2k-1
, with
k=2
.
The reason why we consider the scaling in (
28
) for the first integral is that, assuming that
W^{\prime\prime}(0)>0
, we have

\frac{1}{\varepsilon}W(\sqrt{\varepsilon}u^{\prime})=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\Big(\frac{1}{2}W^{\prime\prime}(0)\varepsilon|u^{\prime}|^{2}+o(\varepsilon|u^{\prime}|^{2})\Big)=\frac{1}{2}W^{\prime\prime}(0)|u^{\prime}|^{2}+o(|u^{\prime}|^{2}),

which suggests in a way that
u^{\prime}\in L^{2}(I)
. Note that the scaling is not the unique choice (see e.g.
[
18
]
), but in a sense is the most complete and justified as an expansion by
\Gamma
-convergence
[
9
]
.
For the prototypical quadratic energies

F^{2}_{\varepsilon}(u)=\int_{I}\min\Big\{|u^{\prime}|^{2},\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\Big\}dx+\varepsilon^{3}\int_{I}|u^{\prime\prime}|^{2}dx

(29)

indeed, this turns out to be true for the approximate differential of
u
. Moreover,
sequences with equi-bounded energy are precompact with respect to the
L^{1}
-convergence (up to addition of constants), and the
\Gamma
-limit of
F^{2}_{\varepsilon}
in this topology is

F_{2}(u)=\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}dx+m_{2}\sum_{S(u)}\sqrt{|u^{+}(x)-u^{-}(x)|}

defined in the space
SBV(I)
(see
[
7
]
). This has been proved by Alicandro, Braides, and Gelli
[
2
]
and by Bouchitté, Dubs, and Seppecher
[
11
]
.

The constant
m_{2}
represents the energy necessary to produce a jump discontinuity of size
1
. We reduce to this case by a
1/2
-homogeneity argument. It is important to note that
m_{2}
can be interpreted as given by an optimal-profile problem

m_{2}=\inf_{T}\min\bigg\{T+\int_{0}^{T}|v^{\prime\prime}|^{2}dt:v(0)=0,v(T)=1,v^{\prime}(0)=v^{\prime}(T)=0\bigg\},

where the transition between
0
and
1
is optimized on an interval of length
T
, which is also a variable of the problem.
This minimum problem is obtained after scaling by
1/\varepsilon
an interval where the modulus of the derivative of a function
u_{\varepsilon}
is
above the threshold

1/\sqrt{\varepsilon}
. After defining
v(t)=u(\varepsilon t)
, we obtain the homogeneous boundary conditions on
v^{\prime}
at the endpoints as
\varepsilon\to 0
. This consideration draws a parallel with the phase-transition energies, in which we have observed that boundary conditions in (
14
) are automatically determined in the Modica–Mortola case, while this is not the case for higher-order perturbations.

The general result for perturbations with local higher-order energies is the following theorem by Solci
[
28
]
.

Theorem 16

(integer higher-order perturbations)
.

Let
k\geq 2
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and let

be defined on

. Then the functionals are equi-coercive, up to addition of constants, with respect to the convergence in

, where it takes the form

F_{k}(u)=\int_{\Omega}|\nabla u|^{2}dx+m_{k}\sum_{S(u)}\root k \of{|u^{+}(x)-u^{-}(x)|},

and the constant
m_{k}
is defined by

\displaystyle m_{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\inf_{T}\min\bigg\{T+\int_{0}^{T}|v^{(k)}|^{2}dt:v(0)=0,v(T)=1,

(31)

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274ptv^{(\ell)}(0)=v^{(\ell)}(T)=0\hbox{ for }\ell\in\{1,\ldots,k-1\}\Big\}.

The proof of this result is extremely delicate, and we refer to
[
27
]
for the details of the proof. Again, one issue is, as for phase-transition energies, to enhance the boundary conditions, showing that one can add also conditions on the derivatives of order higher than the first. This can be done using interpolation inequalities, possibly enlarging the intervals where functions have to be considered ‘above the threshold’. Unfortunately, the length of these intervals itself concurs in the determination of
m_{k}
, and the corresponding minimum problems can be extremely sensitive to the change of length if the jump size is small, when the
1/k
-homogeneity argument hinted at above is applied. This second issue can be solved by fine constructions when we have small jumps, and by the use of relaxation results.

Remark 17

(the higher-dimensional case)
.

In
