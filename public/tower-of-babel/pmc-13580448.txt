Neutrophil-Dominant Inflammatory Responses and Inflammation-Coagulation Burden After Extremity Acute Compartment Syndrome: A Retrospective Multi-Cohort Study
Authors: Zihang Zhao, Ruoyu Wang, Liujie Zheng, Zihan Liu, Teng Pan, Shuo Yang, Liang Guo, Chun Chang, Yiran Li, Jialiang Guo, Ruipeng Zhang, Zhiyong Hou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExtremity acute compartment syndrome (ACS) is a pressure-related surgical emergency accompanied by ischemic muscle injury. Whether routinely available inflammatory and coagulation markers characterize outcome-relevant host-response patterns remains unclear.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 852 ACS-related patients, hospitalizations, or critical-care stays across three cohorts. The primary analysis included 227 patients with traumatic extremity ACS and examined early baseline inflammatory and coagulation profiles in relation to limb-specific adverse outcomes. Local inflammatory phenotype and post-baseline trajectory analyses were exploratory. Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV (MIMIC-IV; n=415) and eICU Collaborative Research Database (eICU; n=210) served as supportive critical-care cohorts for systemic outcomes and were not used for external validation of local limb-specific findings.ResultsIn the local cohort, muscle necrosis occurred in 85 patients (37.4%), amputation or muscle necrosis in 87 (38.3%), infection in 35 (15.4%), thrombosis in 29 (12.8%), and any adverse outcome in 109 (48.0%). Higher neutrophil and white blood cell counts were associated with any adverse outcome, muscle necrosis, and amputation or muscle necrosis. These associations remained directionally consistent after additional consideration of American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status, injury-to-hospital time, and, among decompressed patients, injury-to-decompression time. A principal-component-derived inflammation-coagulation axis was associated with adverse limb and thrombotic outcomes. Exploratory analyses identified a neutrophil-dominant inflammatory phenotype and heterogeneous post-baseline trajectories. Supportive MIMIC-IV and eICU analyses showed associations between systemic inflammatory burden and selected systemic outcomes.ConclusionIn the primary local cohort, extremity ACS was accompanied by measurable inflammatory host-response patterns associated with adverse outcomes. Phenotype and trajectory findings were exploratory. Public critical-care cohorts provided supportive systemic-outcome context but did not externally validate the local limb-specific findings. Prospective studies with standardized sampling and clinical covariate ascertainment are needed before these markers can be considered for clinical risk assessment or decision-making.Keywords: acute compartment syndrome, systemic inflammation, neutrophil, inflammation-coagulation, thromboinflammation, muscle necrosis

Introduction

Extremity acute compartment syndrome (ACS) is a limb-threatening emergency in which elevated pressure within a closed fascial compartment compromises tissue perfusion and causes progressive neuromuscular injury.1 Current clinical assessment and management are centered on compartment pressure, injury mechanism, soft-tissue condition, vascular compromise, time to decompression, and surgical treatment.1–4 These factors remain essential, yet clinically important adverse outcomes, including muscle necrosis, infection, thrombosis, amputation, and systemic complications, still occur in a subset of patients.1–4 This suggests that outcome heterogeneity after ACS may not be fully explained by anatomical injury and procedure-related factors alone, and that the inflammatory host response to severe tissue injury may also shape clinical course.

Severe muscle ischemia and tissue necrosis can initiate sterile inflammation, endothelial activation, microvascular injury, and systemic inflammatory signaling.5,6 Neutrophil and monocyte activation, relative lymphocyte suppression, platelet activation, and coagulation abnormalities may therefore reflect different aspects of the injury-related inflammatory host response.7–11 Complete blood count-derived indices, including the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio, systemic immune-inflammation index, systemic inflammation response index, and aggregate index of systemic inflammation, provide clinically accessible summaries of systemic inflammatory status and have been studied in several inflammatory and immune-mediated diseases.12–14 However, these indices remain indirect peripheral blood markers and cannot by themselves define cellular immune mechanisms. Their role in extremity ACS remains insufficiently defined, particularly when considered together with coagulation markers, inflammatory host-response phenotypes, post-baseline trajectories, and limb-specific outcomes.

We therefore aimed primarily to characterize early inflammatory and inflammation-coagulation host-response patterns in traumatic extremity ACS and to examine their associations with clinically relevant limb-specific outcomes in a local cohort. The primary association analyses focused on early baseline inflammatory and coagulation measures in relation to muscle necrosis, amputation or muscle necrosis, infection, thrombosis, and the composite adverse outcome. Inflammatory phenotype structure and post-baseline trajectories were examined as exploratory analyses to describe heterogeneity in inflammatory state and course. MIMIC-IV and eICU were analyzed separately as supportive critical-care cohorts to assess systemic inflammatory burden in relation to organ and systemic outcomes under database-specific definitions, with interpretation limited to supportive systemic-outcome context.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Population

This retrospective multi-source observational study investigated inflammatory host-response burden after acute compartment syndrome (ACS)-related injury and critical illness. The primary local cohort consisted of consecutive patients with traumatic extremity ACS admitted to The Third Hospital of Hebei Medical University between January 1, 2013 and December 31, 2023. Patients were eligible for the local cohort if they had a confirmed diagnosis of traumatic ACS involving an extremity. Operatively and nonoperatively managed patients were both eligible, and treatment strategy was not used as an inclusion criterion.

Patients were excluded from the local cohort if they had chronic compartment syndrome, non-traumatic compartment syndrome, ACS not involving an extremity, incomplete clinical information, unavailable key outcome information, or duplicate hospitalization. For duplicate hospitalizations, the index ACS-related hospitalization was retained. The final local cohort included 227 patients and provided detailed limb-specific outcomes together with baseline and clinically obtained post-baseline laboratory measurements.

Public critical-care support cohorts included 415 ACS-related hospitalizations from MIMIC-IV version 3.1 and 210 ACS-related or ACS-like critical-care stays from the eICU Collaborative Research Database. A strict eICU extremity descriptive subgroup of 100 stays was additionally defined for descriptive and sensitivity analyses. The local cohort was the primary analytic cohort for limb-specific outcome analyses. MIMIC-IV and eICU were analyzed separately as supportive critical-care cohorts to examine systemic inflammatory burden in relation to organ and systemic outcomes under database-specific clinical and coding definitions. Because of differences in case definitions, clinical setting, laboratory timing and availability, and outcome ascertainment, the public cohorts were not considered direct external validation cohorts for the local limb-specific associations, phenotypes, or post-baseline trajectories.

Outcomes

The main local endpoints were muscle necrosis, amputation or muscle necrosis, and any adverse outcome. Infection and thrombosis were analyzed as key secondary endpoints. The any adverse outcome endpoint summarized clinically relevant limb-specific or systemic complications captured in the local cohort and was used as the principal composite clinical endpoint for association screening and domain contribution analyses, with component outcomes reported separately. Outcomes with limited event counts, including amputation alone and selected thrombosis models, were interpreted as exploratory.

In the public critical-care support cohorts, outcomes included in-hospital mortality, acute kidney injury or renal replacement therapy (AKI/RRT), infection or sepsis, thrombosis or embolism, and composite systemic adverse outcomes. These outcomes were used to characterize associations between systemic inflammatory burden and organ or systemic outcomes in ACS-related critical-care hospitalizations.

Laboratory Markers and Inflammatory Indices

Early inflammatory host-response exposures were defined separately according to the prespecified measurement window in each cohort. In the primary local cohort, the prespecified baseline inflammatory and coagulation variables were derived from laboratory fields designated as preoperative in the source dataset. Because treatment strategy was not an inclusion criterion and both operatively and nonoperatively managed patients were eligible for the local cohort, these exposures are referred to as local baseline laboratory measurements throughout the manuscript. MIMIC-IV supportive analyses used laboratory measurements from the first 24 hours of critical-care observation, whereas eICU supportive analyses used database-specific laboratory measurements from the first 24 hours of the ICU stay. These cohort-specific measurement windows were not considered temporally equivalent and were not used for direct cross-database validation of the local baseline associations. The primary leukocyte and platelet domain included white blood cell count, neutrophil count, lymphocyte count, monocyte count, and platelet count. Complete blood count-derived inflammatory indices included neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR), systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), systemic inflammation response index (SIRI), and aggregate index of systemic inflammation (AISI).

NLR was calculated as neutrophil count divided by lymphocyte count; PLR as platelet count divided by lymphocyte count; MLR as monocyte count divided by lymphocyte count; SII as platelet count multiplied by neutrophil count and divided by lymphocyte count; SIRI as neutrophil count multiplied by monocyte count and divided by lymphocyte count; and AISI as neutrophil count multiplied by monocyte count and platelet count and divided by lymphocyte count.

Coagulation markers included prothrombin time, international normalized ratio, activated partial thromboplastin time, fibrinogen, D-dimer, fibrin degradation products, and antithrombin III when available. Additional organ-stress and systemic physiology markers, including albumin, creatinine, potassium, urea or blood urea nitrogen, aspartate aminotransferase, creatine kinase, lactate dehydrogenase, and lactate where available, were used to characterize systemic burden. C-reactive protein, procalcitonin, and interleukin-6 were not used as primary exposures because of limited or inconsistent availability across cohorts. All routine blood-cell and coagulation markers were interpreted as clinically available indicators of inflammatory and inflammation-coagulation burden rather than direct measurements of immune-cell function or molecular immune mechanisms.

Inflammation-Coagulation Axes, Phenotypes, and Post-Baseline Trajectories

Continuous inflammatory markers were standardized before modeling so that effect estimates could be interpreted per standard deviation increase. Axis scores were constructed to summarize related biological domains, including leukocyte activation, coagulation burden, inflammation-coagulation burden, and systemic inflammatory burden. For leukocyte-containing integrated axes, the standardized lymphocyte count was sign-reversed so that lower lymphocyte count represented greater lymphopenic inflammatory burden and higher values of the component variables were directionally aligned with greater inflammatory burden. In the local cohort, a principal component analysis-derived inflammation-coagulation axis was constructed among complete cases with sufficient leukocyte and coagulation marker availability. Component definitions and principal-component loadings are provided in the Supplementary Material.

Exploratory analyses in the local cohort included unsupervised inflammatory phenotype characterization and post-baseline inflammatory trajectory assessment. For phenotype analysis, candidate clustering solutions were evaluated according to separation, stability, cluster size, and biological interpretability, and the selected phenotypes were described using standardized and raw marker profiles together with outcome distributions. Phenotypes were interpreted as clinical patterns of inflammatory burden rather than fixed mechanistic immune subtypes.

For trajectory analysis, repeated post-baseline laboratory measurements obtained during clinical care were used for markers with sufficient longitudinal availability. Patients were classified according to baseline burden and post-baseline change into low-stable, low-escalating, high-resolving, and high-persistent patterns. Because repeat testing was clinically driven rather than obtained according to a standardized sampling schedule, trajectory analyses were considered exploratory descriptions of inflammatory course.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were summarized using means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Logistic regression was used to estimate associations between inflammatory host-response exposures and binary outcomes, with odds ratios for continuous markers and axis scores reported per standard deviation increase. Primary local analyses included age- and sex-adjusted models and clinically adjusted models including age, sex, open injury, and multiple trauma when supported by event counts and model stability. Sensitivity analyses further examined the principal early inflammatory associations after adding American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status and log(1 + injury-to-hospital hours) to the clinical adjustment set. To assess the potential influence of decompression timing, a separate sensitivity analysis was restricted to decompressed patients with available injury-to-decompression time. The core clinical model was first refitted in this identical complete-case population and was then additionally adjusted for log(1 + injury-to-decompression hours); models using untransformed hours were examined as a functional-form sensitivity check. Because injury-to-decompression time was defined for decompressed patients, it was evaluated as a treatment-context sensitivity variable and was not included in the primary model fitted to the overall local cohort. Public-cohort analyses were performed separately using unadjusted and age- and sex-adjusted models. Model stability was assessed using event-per-variable checks.

Primary inference focused on early leukocyte and complete blood count-derived inflammatory measures and inflammation-coagulation burden in relation to the main local outcomes. False-discovery-rate adjustment was applied within biologically and analytically related hypothesis families, and FDR-supported findings were distinguished from nominal associations. Restricted spline analyses were used to examine the functional form of selected continuous associations. Phenotype, post-baseline trajectory, domain-contribution, and machine-learning-assisted feature-priority analyses were treated as exploratory or supportive components of the study, with their findings interpreted according to the corresponding analytic limitations.

Exploratory nested domain models assessed whether leukocyte, coagulation, integrated inflammation-coagulation, phenotype, and post-baseline trajectory domains added explanatory information beyond baseline clinical variables, with changes in area under the receiver operating characteristic curve used to summarize incremental contribution. Machine-learning-assisted analyses used bootstrap stability assessment, penalized regression, random forest, and gradient boosting to characterize recurrent inflammatory feature priority across local outcomes. These analyses were intended to describe explanatory domain contribution and feature stability and were not used to develop or validate a deployable clinical prediction model.

Reporting Guidelines

This study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement and, where applicable to analyses of routinely collected health data, the REporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely-collected health Data (RECORD) statement.15,16

Ethics

The local retrospective cohort study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Third Hospital of Hebei Medical University (approval No. K2025-088-1) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The requirement for informed consent was waived because the study used retrospective de-identified clinical data and involved no direct patient contact. Analyses of MIMIC-IV version 3.1 and eICU were performed using de-identified public critical-care data under their respective database-access and data-use requirements.

Early Leukocyte Signatures and Local Adverse Outcomes

Early leukocyte analyses identified a neutrophil-dominant inflammatory signature associated with local adverse outcomes (Table 2 and Figure 2). After clinical adjustment for age, sex, open injury, and multiple trauma, higher neutrophil count and white blood cell count (WBC) were associated with any adverse outcome, with odds ratios per standard deviation increase of 1.79 (95% CI, 1.29–2.48; q=0.006) and 1.69 (95% CI, 1.23–2.34; q=0.007), respectively. Neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), systemic inflammation response index (SIRI), aggregate index of systemic inflammation (AISI), systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), and monocyte-to-lymphocyte ratio (MLR) also showed associations with the composite adverse outcome in the primary models.

Inflammation-Coagulation Burden and Thrombosis-Related Outcomes

Integrated leukocyte and coagulation analyses supported an inflammation-coagulation burden domain (Table 2 and Figure 2). In local complete cases with sufficient leukocyte and coagulation marker availability, the PCA-derived inflammation-coagulation PC1 was associated with any adverse outcome [OR 1.98 per SD increase (95% CI, 1.34–2.92; q=0.002)], thrombosis [OR 2.06 (95% CI, 1.15–3.69; q=0.039)], muscle necrosis [OR 1.63 (95% CI, 1.12–2.36; q=0.026)], and amputation or muscle necrosis [OR 1.63 (95% CI, 1.12–2.36; q=0.026)].

Domain-level axes showed concordant patterns. The leukocyte activation axis was associated with any adverse outcome, muscle necrosis, and amputation or muscle necrosis, whereas the coagulation axis was most clearly related to thrombosis [OR 1.91 (95% CI, 1.18–3.11; q=0.039)]. The broader inflammation-coagulation axis was associated with any adverse outcome [OR 1.70 (95% CI, 1.23–2.36; q=0.002)] and muscle necrosis [OR 1.40 (95% CI, 1.04–1.89; q=0.048)], with nominal evidence for thrombosis [OR 1.65 (95% CI, 1.04–2.62; q=0.053)]. Full inflammation-coagulation models are reported in Supplementary Table 4, and PCA loadings are shown in Supplementary Figure 2.

Inflammatory Host-Response Phenotype Structure

Exploratory clustering of the local inflammation-coagulation complete-case population identified a two-phenotype solution (Figure 3). A neutrophil-dominant inflammatory phenotype comprised 53 of 162 patients (32.7%) and showed higher inflammatory marker levels and adverse-outcome rates than the lower-burden phenotype. Any adverse outcome occurred in 66.0% versus 37.6% of patients, and infection in 26.4% versus 7.3%. After baseline clinical adjustment, the neutrophil-dominant phenotype was associated with any adverse outcome [OR 3.07 (95% CI, 1.49–6.34; q=0.009)] and infection [OR 4.26 (95% CI, 1.61–11.22; q=0.010)], whereas associations with muscle necrosis and amputation or muscle necrosis were nominal. Post-Baseline Inflammatory Trajectories

Exploratory analyses of repeated post-baseline measurements identified low-stable, low-escalating, high-resolving, and high-persistent inflammatory trajectory patterns. Higher adverse-outcome rates were generally observed among high-activation patterns, particularly high-persistent and high-resolving neutrophil trajectories. However, adjusted trajectory associations were predominantly nominal and did not remain supported after false-discovery-rate correction. Because repeat laboratory testing was clinically driven and longitudinal availability was lower than for the baseline analyses, these findings were interpreted as exploratory descriptions of inflammatory course. Detailed trajectory distributions and outcome models are provided in Supplementary Table 6 and Supplementary Figure 3.

Nonlinear Sensitivity Analyses

Restricted spline analyses showed no false-discovery-rate-supported evidence of nonlinearity for the principal early local inflammatory associations. Neutrophil count and WBC retained approximately graded associations with any adverse outcome, muscle necrosis, and amputation or muscle necrosis, without evidence supporting clinically meaningful threshold cutoffs. Detailed spline analyses are provided in Supplementary Table 7 and Supplementary Figure 4.

Domain Contribution and Machine-Learning-Assisted Inflammatory Feature Priority

Exploratory domain-contribution and machine-learning-assisted analyses were consistent with the primary neutrophil-centered findings. Leukocyte and integrated inflammation-coagulation domains added explanatory information beyond baseline clinical variables for selected local outcomes, while neutrophil count repeatedly emerged as a high-priority feature across resampling and modeling approaches. These analyses were used to assess explanatory domain contribution and feature stability, not to develop or validate a clinical prediction model. Detailed domain-contribution and feature-priority results are provided in Supplementary Tables 8 and 9 and Supplementary Figure 5.

Systemic-Outcome Analyses in Public Critical-Care Support Cohorts

Supportive analyses in MIMIC-IV and eICU showed associations between systemic inflammatory burden and selected organ or systemic outcomes under database-specific definitions. The systemic inflammatory burden axis was associated with acute kidney injury or renal replacement therapy and composite systemic adverse outcomes in both databases and with hospital mortality in eICU; inflammation-coagulation measures also showed associations with selected systemic and thrombotic outcomes. These analyses provided complementary critical-care context and were not interpreted as external validation of the local limb-specific associations. Detailed public-cohort estimates are provided in Supplementary Table 10.

Sensitivity, Clinical-Context, Missingness, and Stability Analyses

Additional clinical-context sensitivity analyses showed stability of the principal raw leukocyte associations while indicating greater attenuation for some derived inflammatory indices. After further adjustment for ASA physical status and injury-to-hospital time, neutrophil count remained associated with any adverse outcome [OR 1.63 (95% CI, 1.17–2.28)], muscle necrosis [OR 1.48 (95% CI, 1.07–2.03)], and amputation or muscle necrosis [OR 1.48 (95% CI, 1.08–2.04)]. WBC showed corresponding associations of 1.55 (95% CI, 1.12–2.15), 1.45 (95% CI, 1.06–1.99), and 1.46 (95% CI, 1.07–1.99), respectively. Several derived indices remained associated with the composite adverse outcome, whereas some estimates for the two limb-specific outcomes were attenuated and their confidence intervals included unity. Among decompressed patients with available timing data, additional adjustment for injury-to-decompression time on an identical complete-case population did not materially alter the overall pattern of associations; results were also qualitatively similar when decompression time was modeled in untransformed hours.

Core local outcomes had complete ascertainment, and baseline leukocyte measures had the highest laboratory availability. WBC and neutrophil count were available in 206 of 227 patients (90.7%), and the principal complete blood count-derived indices in 200 (88.1%). Coagulation complete-case analyses and exploratory phenotype and trajectory analyses involved greater attrition and were therefore interpreted more cautiously. All 105 targeted sensitivity-model fits were successfully estimated, with a minimum event-per-variable ratio of 10.57. False-discovery-rate-supported findings were concentrated in the early leukocyte and inflammation-coagulation analyses, whereas post-baseline trajectory findings were primarily nominal. Detailed clinical-context sensitivity analyses, decompression-timing analyses, missingness assessments, and event-per-variable checks are provided in Supplementary Table 11 and Supplementary Figure 6.

Discussion

Extremity ACS is classically managed as a pressure-related emergency, with clinical attention focused on injury severity, compartment pressure, vascular compromise, decompression, wound condition, and surgical treatment. In the primary local cohort, early baseline neutrophil count and WBC showed the most consistent associations with adverse limb outcomes, and integrated inflammation-coagulation measures provided complementary evidence of broader host-response burden. The principal neutrophil count and WBC associations remained directionally stable after additional consideration of ASA physical status, injury-to-hospital time, and, among decompressed patients, injury-to-decompression time, although several derived inflammatory indices showed greater attenuation. Exploratory phenotype and post-baseline trajectory analyses further suggested heterogeneity in inflammatory state and course, while public critical-care analyses provided supportive systemic-outcome context under database-specific definitions. Taken together, these findings characterize outcome-associated inflammatory host-response patterns in traumatic extremity ACS; assessment of their prospective prognostic performance requires validation in independently sampled cohorts with standardized measurement.

The neutrophil signal requires careful interpretation. Contemporary immunology recognizes neutrophils as heterogeneous cells with context-dependent protective and pathogenic functions.17 Circulating neutrophil levels are shaped by cell kinetics, bone marrow release, tissue redistribution, and emergency granulopoiesis,18 and extensive trauma and surgery may further alter neutrophil distribution between blood and bone marrow compartments.19 In ACS, an elevated circulating neutrophil burden may therefore integrate multiple processes, including the magnitude of tissue injury, ischemia-reperfusion stress, infection susceptibility, and the evolving systemic response to trauma. The present findings are therefore most consistent with neutrophil count as a peripheral marker of a complex acute inflammatory and myeloid response, without resolving the underlying cellular mechanism or direction of effect.

The association between neutrophil-dominant inflammatory burden and muscle necrosis is biologically plausible. Experimental studies of limb and skeletal muscle ischemia-reperfusion injury have implicated neutrophil extracellular traps, extracellular DNA, inflammatory mediators, and neutrophil-related microvascular injury in postischemic tissue damage.20–22 In the present clinical cohort, however, the baseline local laboratory measurements were obtained after the traumatic injury and during the evolving ACS episode. More extensive muscle ischemia or established tissue damage could therefore itself produce a stronger circulating inflammatory response. Conversely, sustained inflammatory activation could plausibly accompany or contribute to secondary microvascular and tissue injury. The retrospective temporal structure cannot distinguish these possibilities. The observed associations should consequently be interpreted as relationships between inflammatory host-response burden and injury outcome, with reverse causation from greater tissue injury remaining an important alternative explanation.

The inflammation-coagulation findings extend the observed host-response pattern beyond leukocyte activation alone. Contemporary coagulation concepts emphasize interactions among innate immunity, platelets, endothelial injury, complement activation, and coagulation signaling.23,24 In trauma, early coagulation abnormalities and venous thromboembolism remain clinically important, while major tissue injury and resuscitation can alter platelet, complement, and coagulation profiles.25–28 ACS provides a clinical setting in which ischemia, swelling, vascular compromise, surgery, immobilization, and systemic inflammation may converge. The associations of coagulation-related and integrated inflammation-coagulation measures with thrombosis and adverse outcomes are consistent with a thromboinflammatory host-response context. These routine laboratory patterns do not identify a specific causal pathway, but they support the coexistence of inflammatory and coagulation disturbances in patients with more complicated clinical courses.

The exploratory phenotype analysis provides a complementary description of inflammatory heterogeneity within the local cohort. The neutrophil-dominant phenotype summarized a multivariable high-burden inflammatory state associated with higher rates of several adverse outcomes, particularly the composite adverse outcome and infection. Similar approaches in trauma research have shown that biologically informed phenotypes can reveal heterogeneity within clinically related injury groups,29 while tissue injury, damage-associated molecular patterns, and post-traumatic immune dysregulation may contribute to such variation.30 The infection association is also biologically plausible because circulating inflammatory biomarkers after major trauma are influenced by both infection and the severity of the underlying injury response.31 Given the complete-case sample size and data-driven clustering process, this phenotype should be considered a hypothesis-generating clinical pattern for future validation.

The derived inflammatory indices and machine-learning-assisted analyses provided supportive evidence for the same general neutrophil-centered pattern, but their results were less uniform than those for raw neutrophil count and WBC. SII, SIRI, NLR, and related indices have shown prognostic associations in other clinical settings.32–34 In the present cohort, several derived indices were associated with the composite adverse outcome, whereas some associations with muscle necrosis and amputation or muscle necrosis attenuated after additional adjustment for ASA physical status and injury-to-hospital timing. This pattern suggests that the composite indices may capture useful inflammatory context but may also be more sensitive to clinical case mix and covariate structure. The machine-learning-assisted analyses similarly identified recurrent inflammatory features across resampling approaches, but were used only to assess feature stability and prioritization. Prospective evaluation would be required to determine whether any individual marker or multivariable combination provides reproducible prognostic information beyond established clinical assessment.

The exploratory post-baseline trajectory analyses add a temporal perspective to the inflammatory patterns observed at baseline. High-resolving profiles may represent a substantial early inflammatory burden followed by decline, whereas high-persistent profiles may accompany ongoing tissue injury, infection, thrombosis, organ dysfunction, or delayed inflammatory resolution. Dynamic inflammatory subphenotypes have also been considered in critical illness research.35 In the present study, however, repeated measurements were obtained according to clinical care rather than a prespecified serial sampling schedule, and patients with repeated testing may have differed systematically from those without it. The trajectory findings therefore describe possible patterns of inflammatory course and provide hypotheses for future longitudinal studies with standardized sampling intervals.

From a clinical perspective, the present findings show that routine leukocyte and coagulation measurements contain information about the inflammatory host-response burden accompanying traumatic extremity ACS. Their principal value at this stage is to identify candidate markers and biological domains for prospective investigation alongside established assessments of injury severity, limb perfusion, compartment pressure, wound condition, and decompression. The current data do not establish clinically actionable thresholds, incremental prognostic performance sufficient for patient-level risk classification, or treatment decisions based on inflammatory markers. Prospective multicenter studies with standardized sampling, prespecified outcomes, and evaluation of added value beyond established clinical variables are needed before these measurements can be incorporated into ACS risk-assessment or monitoring strategies.

Several limitations should be considered. First, the local cohort was retrospective and single-center. The primary clinical adjustment included age, sex, open injury, and multiple trauma, and additional sensitivity analyses incorporated ASA physical status, injury-to-hospital time, and, among decompressed patients, injury-to-decompression time. The models did not include a standardized anatomical injury-severity score; open injury and multiple trauma therefore captured only part of the injury context. The stability of the principal neutrophil and WBC associations across these analyses reduces concern that the findings were entirely attributable to these measured factors, but residual confounding by the extent of muscle and soft-tissue injury, vascular injury, wound severity, transfer processes, treatment selection, transfusion, infection timing, renal function, and other aspects of clinical severity remains possible. Decompression timing is also influenced by injury presentation and clinical decision-making and therefore cannot by itself resolve causal direction. Second, laboratory testing frequency was determined by clinical care. Complete-case phenotype analyses and clinically driven repeated measurements may consequently be subject to selection bias, particularly for the exploratory trajectory analyses. Third, peripheral blood measurements are indirect indicators of host-response burden and do not characterize the immune microenvironment within injured muscle or fascia. The study did not include cytokine profiling, flow-cytometric immune-cell phenotyping, tissue-level assays, transcriptomic profiling, or experimental mechanistic validation. Finally, the public critical-care cohorts differed from the local cohort in case ascertainment, laboratory timing, variable availability, and outcome definitions. Their analyses provide supportive evidence regarding systemic inflammatory burden in critical illness and should not be interpreted as external validation of the local limb-specific associations, phenotypes, or trajectories.

Conclusion

Extremity ACS was accompanied by measurable inflammatory and inflammation-coagulation host-response patterns. In the primary local cohort, early baseline neutrophil count and WBC showed the most consistent associations with adverse limb outcomes, while integrated inflammation-coagulation measures provided complementary evidence and exploratory phenotype and post-baseline analyses suggested additional inflammatory heterogeneity. Public critical-care cohorts provided supportive systemic-outcome context but did not externally validate the local limb-specific findings. These results support prospective multicenter evaluation with standardized sampling and more detailed injury and treatment characterization to determine whether inflammatory markers provide reproducible prognostic value beyond established clinical assessment.
