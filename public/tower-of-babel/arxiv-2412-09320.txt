A simple algorithm to reflect through eigenspaces of unitaries
Baptiste Claudon

Abstract




Abstract

Reflections are omnipresent tools in quantum algorithms. We consider the task of reflecting through the eigenspace of an implementable unitary. Such reflections are generally designed using phase estimation or linear combination of unitaries. These methods have size and depth that scale favorably with the desired precision and the spectral gap of the unitary. However, they require a number of ancilla qubits that grows with both parameters. Here, we present a simple algorithm with the same size and depth scaling but requiring only
3
ancilla qubits for all problem instances. As such, this algorithm is expected to become the reference method to reflect through eigenspaces of unitaries.

I
Introduction

Due to the exponential speedup of quantum algorithms over the best known classical methods in solving certain problems, quantum computing recently gained a lot of attention
Shor 1994
;
Low and Chuang 2019
. Numerous quantum algorithms rely on reflections through well-chosen subspaces. Quantum amplitude estimation
Brassard et al. 2002
, amplitude amplification
Yoder et al. 2014
, search problems
Grover 1996
and Markov chain algorithms
Szegedy 2004
have complexities that are even quantified in terms of the number of uses of these reflection operators. Such reflections were initially constructed using controlled state preparation oracles
Grover and Rudolph 2002
;
Chiang et al. 2009
and controlled phase gates
Claudon et al. 2024
;
Khattar and Gidney 2024
, or the phase estimation algorithm
Kitaev 1995
.

In the present work, we consider the task of constructing a reflection through the eigenspace of an implementable unitary. To the best of our knowledge, the most efficient construction to reflect through one-dimensional eigenspaces is based on linear combination of unitaries
Chowdhury et al. 2018
. It provides the desired reflection up to a spectral norm error
\epsilon
with circuit size and depth complexity
\mathcal{O}\left(\delta^{-1}\log(1/\epsilon)\right)
, where
\delta
is an angular gap separating the target eigenvalue from the others. The construction requires a number of ancilla qubit that increases with both
\epsilon
and
\delta
.

Here, we present a construction based on the quantum signal processing framework
Motlagh and Wiebe 2024
;
Sünderhauf 2023
. Our construction also presents circuit size and depth complexities
\mathcal{O}\left(\delta^{-1}\log(1/\epsilon)\right)
but only requires
3
ancilla qubits, independently of
\epsilon
and
\delta
. Our result is summarized in Proposition
2
.

Because it only uses
3
ancilla qubits, our decomposition is expected to become the routine of choice to construct reflections through eigenspaces of unitaries. It could ultimately improve the efficiency of quantum Monte-Carlo methods
Akhalwaya et al. 2023
, of interest in financial modeling
Glasserman 2004
, or the preparation of physically relevant quantum states
Lin and Tong 2020
, with implications in quantum chemistry
James D. Whitfield and Aspuru-Guzik 2011
;
Feniou et al. 2024
.

;

†

|\cdot|
the modulus.
1

B_{1}(0)

denotes its boundary, the unit circle.
\mathbb{N}=\{0,1,...\}
denotes the set of nonnegative integers.
\log
denotes the logarithm in base
2
.
\ln
denotes the natural logarithm in base
e
.
\sigma(U)
denotes the spectrum of the operator
U
.
I=\ket{0}\bra{0}+\ket{1}\bra{1}
,
X=\ket{0}\bra{1}+\ket{1}\bra{0}
and

II.0.1
Projected unitary encodings and qubitized walk operators

Let us start by introducing (symmetric) projected unitary encodings. They allow to implement normalized operators as the restriction of unitaries to certain input and output subspaces. The following definitions rely on the notion of partial isometries: operators whose restrictions to the space orthogonal to their kernels are isometries. Remark that if
\square
is a partial isometry, then
\square\square^{\dagger}
is an orthogonal projector and
\square^{\dagger}\square=1
.

Definition 1

Let
U
be a unitary and
\square_{L},\square_{R}
be partial isometries. If
\square_{L}^{\dagger}U\square_{R}=A
, for some operator
A
, then
\left(U,\square_{L},\square_{R}\right)
is said to be a Projected Unitary Encoding (PUE) of
A
.

Note that if
\left(U,\square_{L},\square_{R}\right)
is a PUE of
A
, then necessarily
\|A\|\leq 1
.

Definition 2

Let
(U,\square_{L},\square_{R})
be a PUE of
A
. If
\square_{L}=\square_{R}
and
U
is symmetric, then
(U,\square)
is said to be a Symmetric Projected Unitary Encoding (SPUE) of
A
.

Note that if
(U,\square)
is a SPUE of some operator
A
, then
A
is symmetric.

Definition 3

Let
(U,\square)
be a SPUE of
A
. Define the reflection
\mathcal{R}_{\square}=2\square\square^{\dagger}-1
. The operator
\mathcal{W}=\mathcal{R}_{\square}U
is said to be the qubitized walk operator of
(U,\square)
.

SPUE are important because they allow to construct qubitized walk operators. The relationship between the spectra of qubitized walk operators and the encoded symmetric matrices is simple, as stated by Theorem
1
.

Theorem 1

Let
(U,\square)
be a SPUE of
A
. Let
\mathcal{W}
be the qubitized walk operator of
(U,\square)
. If
\lambda\in]-1,1[
is an eigenvalue of
A
with eigenvector
\ket{v}
, then
e^{\pm i\cos^{-1}(\lambda)}
is an eigenvalue of
\mathcal{W}
with normalized eigenvectors:

\ket{\mu_{\pm}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}\sin(\theta)}\left(e^{\pm i\theta}-U\right)\square\ket{v},

(1)

where
\theta=\cos^{-1}(\lambda)
. If
\pm 1
is an eigenvalue of
A
with eigenvector
\ket{v}
, then
\square\ket{v}
is an eigenvector of
\mathcal{W}
with the same eigenvalue. Moreover,

\sigma\left(\mathcal{W}\right)\backslash\{\pm 1\}=\left\{e^{\pm i\cos^{-1}(\lambda)}:\lambda\in\sigma(A)\cap]-1,1[\right\}

(2)

with multiplicities.

II.0.2
Generalized quantum signal processing

Generalized Quantum Signal Processing (GQSP) is a framework to construct PUE of polynomials of unitaries. Every complex polynomial that maps all unitaries to operators of spectral norm less than
1
can be implemented. The construction only requires access to controlled versions of the unitary and a single ancilla qubit. Theorem
2
will be sufficient for our purposes.

Theorem 2

Let
P
be a degree
d\in\mathbb{N}
complex polynomial and
U
be a unitary. If
\|P(V)\|\leq 1
for all unitaries
V
, then we can construct a PUE
(W,\ket{0},\ket{0})
of
P(U)
using
d
controlled-
U
operations,
\mathcal{O}(d)
additional single-qubit gates and
1
ancilla qubit.

II.0.3
Generalized quantum eigenvalue transform

Theorem
1
relates the eigenvalues of symmetric operators to the eigenvalues of their SPUE. Theorem
2
applies polynomials to unitaries. Combining the two results allows to apply polynomials directly to the encoded symmetric operators, as long as they are encoded in a SPUE. The procedure is called Generalized Quantum Eigenvalue Transformation (GQET).

Theorem 3

Let
(U,\square)
be a SPUE of
A
. Consider a degree-
d
complex polynomial
\upsilon(x)=\sum_{n=0}^{d}a_{n}T_{n}(x),
written in the basis of Chebyshev polynomials. Consider its associated signal processing polynomial
\Upsilon(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{d}a_{n}z^{n}
. Assume that
\max_{z\in\partial B_{1}(0)}|\Upsilon(z)|\leq 1
. Let
\mathcal{G}
be the GQSP (Theorem
2
) transformation of the qubitized walk operator of
(U,\square)
applying the polynomial
\Upsilon
. Then,
\left(\mathcal{G},\ket{0}\otimes\square\right)
is a SPUE of
\upsilon(A)
.

Certain polynomials
\upsilon
are such that
\max_{x\in[-1,1]}|\upsilon(x)|\leq 1
but
\max_{z\in\partial B_{1}(0)}|\Upsilon(z)|>1
. In this case, the polynomial
\upsilon(x)/\beta
can still be applied, with
\beta
defined as in Definition
3
.

Definition 4

Let
\upsilon
be a complex-coefficient polynomial and
\Upsilon
its signal processing polynomial, as introduced in Theorem
3
. Define the scaling factor
\beta
of
\upsilon
by:

\beta=\frac{\max_{z\in\partial B_{1}(0)}|\Upsilon(z)|}{\max_{x\in[-1,1]}|\upsilon(x)|}.

(3)

II.0.4
Hermitianization

Theorem
3
allows to apply polynomials to symmetric operators, under the condition that they are encoded in a SPUE. If an operator
A
is only encoded in a PUE, it is still possible to construct a SPUE of an operator whose singular values are the singular values of
A
. The construction is the content of Proposition
1
. The proposition also serves as a definition of the procedure, called hermitianization.

Proposition 1

Let
\left(U,\square_{L},\square_{R}\right)
be a PUE of
A
. Define
\overline{U}=\left(\ket{0}\bra{0}\otimes U+\ket{1}\bra{1}\otimes U^{\dagger}\right)\left(X\otimes 1\right)
and
\overline{\square}=\ket{0}\bra{0}\otimes\square_{L}+\ket{1}\bra{1}\otimes\square_{R}
. Then,
\left(\overline{U},\overline{\square}\right)
is a SPUE of
\ket{0}\bra{1}\otimes A+\ket{1}\bra{0}\otimes A^{\dagger}
. In particular,
\overline{U}
can be constructed from a controlled-
U
gate, its adjoint and an
X
gate.
\left(\overline{U},\overline{\square}\right)
is called the hermitianization of
(U,\square_{L},\square_{R})
.

III
Results

III.0.1
Problem statement and resolution strategy

Let
U
be a unitary acting on a finite set of qubits. Let
e^{i\theta}\in\sigma(U)
be an eigenvalue of
U
and
\Pi
be the orthogonal projection on the corresponding eigenspace. We assume that we know a
\delta\in]0,\pi[
such that
\{\lambda\in]\theta-\delta,\theta+\delta[:e^{i\lambda}\in\sigma(U)\}=\{\theta\}
. We are interested in constructing a SPUE of the reflection
2\Pi-1
. For that purpose, we allow ourselves the use of ancilla qubits, arbitrary two-qubit gates, controlled-
U
and controlled-
U^{\dagger}
operations. Up to considering
e^{-i\theta}U
instead of
U
, we will assume without loss of generality that
\theta=0
. We can then write the spectral decomposition of
U
as follows:

U=\Pi+\sum_{\lambda\neq 0}e^{i\lambda}\ket{v_{\lambda}}\bra{v_{\lambda}}.

(4)

By definition, the set
(\ket{v_{\lambda}})_{\lambda\neq 0}
forms an orthonormal basis for the subspace of orthogonal projector
1-\Pi
. Note that the desired reflection may be written in this basis:

2\Pi-1=\Pi-\sum_{\lambda\neq 0}\ket{v_{\lambda}}\bra{v_{\lambda}}.

(5)

Equation
5
shows that
2\Pi-1
is easy to construct from the qubitized walk operator associated with a SPUE of
\Pi
. To see this, let
(V,\square)
be a SPUE of
\Pi
. Theorem
1
shows that the qubitized walk operator
\mathcal{W}
of
(V,\square)
has eigenvalues
1
and
\pm i
. Using the expression of the eigenvectors with eigenvalues
\pm i
in Equation
1
:

\mathcal{W}^{2}=1-2\square(1-\Pi)\square^{\dagger}-2V\square(1-\Pi)\square^{\dagger}V.

(6)

Restricting the input and output subspaces:

\square^{\dagger}\mathcal{W}^{2}\square=1-2(1-\Pi)-2\Pi(1-\Pi)\Pi=2\Pi-1.

(7)

In particular,
\left(\mathcal{W}^{2},\square\right)
is a SPUE of
2\Pi-1
. Let us therefore focus on constructing a SPUE
(V,\square)
of
\Pi
.

III.0.2
Average out the undesired eigenvalues

The GQSP Theorem
2
allows to construct PUE of polynomials of
U
, as long as these polynomials map unitaries to operators of norm less than
1
. Let
P
be an admissible polynomial.
P(U)
is an operator that shares the same eigenvectors as
U
. Its spectral decomposition may be written:

P(U)=P(1)\Pi+\sum_{\lambda\neq 0}P\left(e^{i\lambda}\right)\ket{v_{\lambda}}\bra{v_{\lambda}}.

(8)

Ideally, we would like a polynomial
P
such that
P(U)=\Pi
, independently of the structure of the spectrum of
U
. As an intermediate step, let us find a polynomial
P
such that
P(U)
has singular value decomposition:

P(U)=\Pi+\sum_{\lambda\neq 0}\left|P\left(e^{i\lambda}\right)\right|\ket{w_{\lambda}}\bra{v_{\lambda}},

(9)

and
\left|P\left(e^{i\lambda}\right)\right|\leq 1/2
for all
e^{i\lambda}\in\sigma(U)\backslash\{1\}
.

Consider the family of polynomials
(P_{t})_{t\geq 1}
defined for each integer
t\geq 1
by:

P_{t}(x)=\frac{1}{t}\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}x^{k}.

(10)

By the triangle inequality,
\left|P_{t}(z)\right|\leq 1
for all
t\geq 1
and
z\in\partial B_{1}(0)
. In particular, each of the polynomials in the family can be implemented by GQSP. Also, they all satisfy
P_{t}(1)=1
. Let now
e^{i\lambda}\neq 1\in\sigma(U)
. By the geometric sum formula,

P_{t}\left(e^{i\lambda}\right)=\frac{1}{t}\frac{e^{i\lambda t}-1}{e^{i\lambda}-1}.

(11)

Since
|e^{i\lambda t}-1|\leq 2
and
\left|e^{i\delta}-1\right|\leq\left|e^{i\lambda}-1\right|
:

\left|P_{t}\left(e^{i\lambda}\right)\right|\leq\frac{1}{t}\frac{2}{\left|e^{i\lambda}-1\right|}\leq\frac{1}{t}\frac{2}{\left|e^{i\delta}-1\right|}.

(12)

Letting
t=\left\lceil 4/{\left|e^{i\delta}-1\right|}\right\rceil
, we have that
\left|P_{t}\left(e^{i\lambda}\right)\right|\leq 1/2
for all eigenvalues
e^{i\lambda}\neq 1
of
U
. Let
\left(\mathcal{G}(U),\ket{0},\ket{0}\right)
be the PUE of
P_{t}(U)
, obtained using the GQSP Theorem
2
. It uses
t
times the controlled-
U
operation and a single ancilla qubit.

III.0.3
Hermitianization

We now have a quantum circuit
\mathcal{G}(U)
such that
\left(\mathcal{G}(U),\ket{0},\ket{0}\right)
is a PUE of the operator
P_{t}(U)
.
P_{t}(U)
is a normal operator with singular values in
[0,1/2]\cup\{1\}
. Moreover, the left and right singular spaces corresponding to the singular value
1
coincide and are defined by the orthogonal projector
\Pi
, as stated by Equation
9
. However, our goal is a SPUE of
\Pi
. We will construct a SPUE of an operator that shares the same singular values as
P_{t}(U)
. Transforming its eigenvalues will ultimately yield the desired SPUE of
\Pi
.

Let
\left(\overline{\mathcal{G}(U)},\overline{\square}\right)
be the hermitianization of
\left(\mathcal{G}(U),\ket{0},\ket{0}\right)
, as defined in Proposition
1
. By definition,
\overline{\square}=\ket{0}\bra{0}\otimes\ket{0}+\ket{1}\bra{1}\otimes\ket{0}
. By construction,
\left(\overline{\mathcal{G}(U)},\overline{\square}\right)
is a SPUE of

\begin{split}&\ket{0}\bra{1}\otimes P(U)+\ket{1}\bra{0}\otimes P(U)^{\dagger}\\
&=X\otimes\Pi+\sum_{\lambda\neq 0}\left[\ket{0}\bra{1}\sigma_{\lambda}e^{i\varphi_{\lambda}}+\ket{1}\bra{0}\sigma_{\lambda}e^{-i\varphi_{\lambda}}\right]\otimes\ket{v_{\lambda}}\bra{v_{\lambda}}\\
&=X\otimes\Pi+\sum_{\lambda\neq 0}\left[\sigma_{\lambda}\ket{\Phi_{\lambda}^{+}}\bra{\Phi_{\lambda}^{+}}-\sigma_{\lambda}\ket{\Phi_{\lambda}^{-}}\bra{\Phi_{\lambda}^{-}}\right]\otimes\ket{v_{\lambda}}\bra{v_{\lambda}},\end{split}

(13)

where by definition

\left\{\begin{aligned} &P\left(e^{i\lambda}\right)=\sigma_{\lambda}e^{i\varphi_{\lambda}},\\
&\ket{\Phi_{\lambda}^{\pm}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}e^{i\varphi_{\lambda}/2}\\
\pm e^{-i\varphi_{\lambda}/2}\end{pmatrix},\end{aligned}\right.

(14)

for
\sigma_{\lambda}\geq 0
and
\varphi_{\lambda}\in[0,2\pi[
. In particular, the spectrum of
\ket{0}\bra{1}\otimes P(U)+\ket{1}\bra{0}\otimes P(U)^{\dagger}
is contained in
[-1/2,1/2]\cup\{\pm 1\}
. Moreover, for any even polynomial
\upsilon
,

\begin{split}&\upsilon\left(\ket{0}\bra{1}\otimes P(U)+\ket{1}\bra{0}\otimes P(U)^{\dagger}\right)\\
&=I\otimes\left[\Pi+\sum_{\lambda\neq 0}\upsilon(\sigma_{\lambda})\ket{v_{\lambda}}\bra{v_{\lambda}}\right].\end{split}

(15)

The procedure requires introducing a second ancilla qubit. Moreover, every use of
\overline{\mathcal{G}(U)}
will require the use of
t
controlled-
U
and
t
controlled-
U^{\dagger}
gates. To be explicit, the GQET also requires to control the reflection through
\ket{0}
. This controlled reflection is simply the two-qubit controlled-
Z
gate.

III.0.4
Exponential convergence towards the desired reflection

Let
0<\epsilon<1
. In light of equation
15
, let us find an even polynomial
\upsilon
of scaling factor
1
and low degree such that
\upsilon(1)=1
and
\upsilon\left([0,1/2]\right)\subset[-\epsilon,\epsilon]
. Indeed, applying the GQET construction
3
to
\overline{\mathcal{G}(U)}
would yield a SPUE of
\upsilon\left(\ket{0}\bra{1}\otimes P(U)+\ket{1}\bra{0}\otimes P(U)^{\dagger}\right)
satisfying

\left\|\upsilon\left(\ket{0}\bra{1}\otimes P(U)+\ket{1}\bra{0}\otimes P(U)^{\dagger}\right)-I\otimes\Pi\right\|\leq\epsilon.

(16)

Arguably, the simplest even polynomial satisfying Equation
16
is a monomial
\upsilon(x)=x^{n}
. Indeed,
n=\lceil\log(1/\epsilon)\rceil
is sufficient for the condition to hold. We only need to show that
\upsilon(x)=x^{n}
has scaling factor
1
. To see this, decompose
x^{n}
in the Chebyshev basis as:

x^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}c_{k}^{(n)}T_{k}(x),

(17)

where the
\left(c_{k}^{(n)}\right)_{k,n\in\mathbb{N}}
are real coefficients. Observe that

\begin{split}&\sum_{k=0}^{n+1}c_{k}^{(n+1)}T_{k}(x)=x^{n+1}=x\sum_{k=0}^{n}c_{k}^{(n)}T_{k}(x)\\
&=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{c_{k}^{(n)}}{2}\left(T_{k+1}(x)+T_{k-1}(x)\right)+c_{0}^{(n)}T_{1}(x).\end{split}

(18)

Identifying the coefficients, we see that each
c^{(n+1)}_{k}
is a positive linear combination of the
\left(c^{(n)}_{l}\right)_{l=0}^{n}
coefficients. Since the coefficients
\left(c^{(0)}_{k}\right)_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
are nonnegative, we get by induction that
c_{k}^{(n)}\geq 0
for all
k,n\in\mathbb{N}
. Hence, for
\Upsilon
the signal processing polynomial of
\upsilon
and all
z\in\mathbb{C}
of modulus
1
:

\left|\Upsilon(z)\right|=\left|\sum_{k=0}^{n}c_{k}^{(n)}z^{k}\right|\leq\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left|c_{k}^{(n)}\right|=\sum_{k=0}^{n}c_{k}^{(n)}=1.

(19)

Taking the maximum over all
z\in\partial B_{1}(0)
, we get that

\max_{z\in\partial B_{1}(0)}\left|\Upsilon(z)\right|\leq 1=\max_{x\in[-1,1]}\left|\upsilon(x)\right|

(20)

and
\beta\leq 1
. Since the reverse inequality always holds,
\beta=1
. The GQET construction implementing the polynomial
x^{\lceil\log(1/\epsilon)\rceil}
uses
\lceil\log(1/\epsilon)\rceil
times the controlled-
\overline{\mathcal{G}(U)}
circuit. The total number of uses of controlled-
U
and controlled-
U^{\dagger}
is therefore
\lceil\log(1/\epsilon)\rceil t
. The construction requires the introduction of a third ancilla qubit. It provides a SPUE
\left(V,\square\right)=\left(V,\ket{0}\otimes\overline{\square}\right)
of an operator
\Pi_{\epsilon}
that is
\epsilon
-close to
\Pi
in spectral norm. Let
\mathcal{W}
be the qubitized walk operator of
(V,\square)
. Using Theorem
1
and
e^{\pm i\cos^{-1}(\epsilon)}=e^{\pm i(\pi/2+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon))}=\pm ie^{i\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)}
,

\left\|\square^{\dagger}\mathcal{W}^{2}\square-(2\Pi-1)\right\|\in\mathcal{O}(\epsilon).

(21)

Let us summarize the main properties of the resulting construction in the following proposition.

Proposition 2

\left(\mathcal{W}^{2},\square\right)
is a SPUE of the reflection
2\Pi-1
through the eigenspace corresponding to the eigenvalue
e^{i\theta}
of
U
, up to a spectral norm error
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
. The circuit
\mathcal{W}^{2}
is constructed from
2\left\lceil 4/{\left|e^{i\delta}-1\right|}\right\rceil\left\lceil\log(1/\epsilon)\right\rceil
controlled-
U
gates, as many controlled-
U^{\dagger}
gates, two-qubit gates and
3
ancilla qubits. In the limit
\delta\to 0
, the number of controlled operations and two-qubit gates is
\mathcal{O}\left(\delta^{-1}\log(1/\epsilon)\right)
.

IV
Discussion

Let us remark that
t=\left\lceil 4/{\left|e^{i\delta}-1\right|}\right\rceil
was chosen for the clarity of the exposition. We could have chosen
t=\left\lceil 2/(c\delta)\right\rceil
, for any
c\in]0,1[
. As a consequence, we would have chosen
n=\lceil\ln(1/\epsilon)/\ln(1/c)\rceil
at the second step. The constant
c=1/e
minimizes the number of uses
2tn
of the controlled-
U
and controlled-
U^{\dagger}
gates. In the limit
\delta\to 0
, the number is reduced from
2\left\lceil 4/{\left|e^{i\delta}-1\right|}\right\rceil\left\lceil\log(1/\epsilon)\right\rceil
to approximately
{2e\log(1/\epsilon)}/(\delta\log(e))\approx 3.8\log(1/\epsilon)/\delta
.

Future work could study a similar problem where the eigenvalue
e^{i\theta}
is only known approximately. One could try to modify the two polynomials used in this work. Ideally, the second polynomial should retain a scaling factor of
1
.
