Harnessing gut microbiota to mitigate Salmonella Dublin: Lessons from S. Typhimurium
Authors: Martin Moran, Nilusha Malmuthuge, Le Luo Guan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe emerging cattle-adapted pathogen, Salmonella Dublin, threatens the global cattle industry by causing high mortality in calves and reduced production efficiency in cows. Due to limited therapeutic options, there is a need for novel interventions to mitigate S. Dublin. In the inflamed gut, Salmonella Typhimurium, and possibly S. Dublin, gain a metabolic advantage by utilizing niche nutrients during anaerobic respiration. S. Dublin invades intestinal epithelial cells using genes encoded by Salmonella pathogenicity island 1 (SPI-1), initiating systemic disease and chronic infection. Propionate, a microbial fermentation product, inhibits SPI-1 transcription, presenting an opportunity to prevent infection. Lactobacilli endogenous to the small intestine of calves may be leveraged to inhibit S. Dublin invasion and growth through propionate synthesis and nutrient blocking, respectively. Here, we discuss critical knowledge gaps of S. Dublin pathogenesis while offering data-driven insights for the development of sustainable microbial-based interventions to mitigate S. Dublin in cattle.

Introduction

Salmonella and Escherichia, both members of the Enterobacteriaceae family, diverged from a common ancestor over 100 million years ago [1]. Salmonella is a diverse genus including over 2,600 serovars, all of which are rod-shaped, gram-negative, facultative anaerobes [2]. Of the two Salmonella species, S. enterica is responsible for 99% of infections and comprises six subspecies: enterica, arizonae, diarizonae, houtenae, indica, and salamae [2–4].

S. enterica subsp. enterica serovar Dublin (S. Dublin) is an emerging cattle-adapted pathogen that has become of increasing concern due to its hindrance to cattle health, welfare, and production efficiency, as well as its zoonotic risk through contaminated dairy products [5,6]. Early descriptions of S. Dublin were from the perspective of human infections; however, this serotype was later found to be more associated with bovine milk and fatal enteritis in young calves [7]. Although zoonotic transmission of S. Dublin from cattle to humans is rare, successful infections can cause severe illness and high fatality rates, especially in immunocompromised individuals [8]. Between 1996–2004 and 2005–2013, the hospitalization rates and fatality rates from S. Dublin infections have increased from 68% to 78%, and from 2.7% to 4.2%, respectively, in the United States [9]. Such increase in incidence and severity in humans is associated with the emergence of multidrug resistance, with over 50% of S. Dublin isolates resistant to over 7 classes of antimicrobials [9].

S. Dublin preferentially infects calves, with reported morbidity, mortality, and lethality rates of 10.55%, 2.79%, and 26.4%, respectively [10]. The primary symptoms differ based on the age of the calf with gastrointestinal symptoms predominating in neonates (0–14 days), and respiratory symptoms predominating in calves/young heifers (0.5–12 months) [11]. A combination of other symptoms may include septicemia, pneumonia, diarrhea, pyrexia, joint swelling, petechiae, and sudden death [11,12]. Alternatively, adult cattle are prone to chronic infections with asymptomatic phenotypes [5]. Although infections are typically non-lethal in chronic carriers, these cattle intermittently shed S. Dublin, propagating discrete pathogen transmission via the fecal-oral route and the milk of lactating cows [5,6]. Additionally, S. Dublin hinders dairy herd production efficiency as infected pregnant cows are prone to abortions and reduced milk yields [13,14]. For instance, S. Dublin infections resulted in an average reduction in energy-corrected milk yield of 1.4 kg per cow in first-parity cows and 3.0 kg per cow in third-parity cows [13], suggesting huge economic losses to the industry.

To date, there is a lack of effective therapeutics to control or prevent S. Dublin outbreaks. Antimicrobials are used for treating bacterial infections; however, this is not recommended for S. Dublin due to its strong ability to acquire multidrug resistance [9]. Additionally, efficacious and robust vaccines are unavailable for Salmonella in cattle [5,15,16]. Vaccines are a critical intervention for the prevention of infectious diseases, but it is difficult to develop vaccines effective at targeting intracellular pathogens like S. enterica, as this requires activation of a cell-mediated immune response (i.e. CD8+ T cell response) [17]. The only available modified live vaccine is in the USA (EnterVene®-d, Boehringer Ingelheim), while inactivated bacterin vaccines are available in the USA (Durvac Salmo D-T®, Colorado Serum Company), Ireland (Bovilis® Bovivac® S, MSD Animal Health), and Australia (Bovilis® S, MSD Animal Health) [18–20]. However, the inactive bacterins available in many countries are suboptimal as these elicit a humoral response, which is more appropriate for extracellular pathogens. Furthermore, the live-modified vaccine is not to be administered to calves younger than two weeks old due to the risk of adverse reactions [15]. Due to these limitations (effectiveness, safety concerns, and limited availability) many calves are left unprotected against S. Dublin. Hence, there is an urgent need for novel prophylactic therapeutics that can effectively prevent infections in calves. This review summarizes the current knowledge of how S. enterica navigates the gut microbiota to induce disease and aims to provide novel and optimized perspectives for selecting probiotics that mitigate S. Dublin in the calf gut.

Metabolic flexibility of Salmonella: Gaining competitive advantage to thrive in the gut

Salmonella are metabolically versatile facultative anaerobes [21], which have been studied extensively due to its highly adaptable and pathogenic nature. As a gastrointestinal pathogen, Salmonella spp. have developed various mechanisms to outcompete the resident commensal microbiota to proliferate in the gut by exploiting byproducts of immune activation. There are several mechanisms that have been reported based on studies conducted in rodent models. In the gut, S. Typhimurium optimizes metabolic efficiency by transitioning from fermentation to anaerobic and aerobic respiration, driving growth blooms that promote competitiveness, invasion, and transmission [22]. In the oxygen-limited gastrointestinal tract (GIT), this process is spearheaded by S. Typhimurium-induced inflammation, which expands pathogens’ nutrient-niche by promoting the availability of alternate electron acceptors. Understanding how S. enterica exploits the intestinal microbiome to outcompete resident microbes and drive gut perturbation is critical for identifying key metabolic pathways that could be targeted in the development of microbiome-based therapeutics. Using S. Typhimurium as a model, we reviewed the metabolic mechanisms that can be targeted to disrupt S. Dublin growth and competition in the calf gut.

To gain an energetic advantage over fermentative gut microbes, S. Typhimurium uses fumarate as an alternate electron acceptor, enabling its expansion in the inflamed gut [23]. Fumarate is synthesized in the gut by the bacterial enzyme aspartate-ammonia lyase (AspA) by converting microbial-derived aspartate (Table 1). Under homeostasis, commensal microbes decrease aspartate availability to inhibit the early colonization of enteric pathogens [43–46]. However, S. Typhimurium induced-mucosal inflammation lyses commensal microbes (i.e. Bacteroides), releasing aspartate into the GIT of gnotobiotic mice, which in return promotes further colonization of Salmonella [23]. Hence, sustained intestinal inflammation leads to the depletion of commensal microbes, creating conditions that favor continuous cycles of infection and a perturbed gut. S. Typhimurium initially uses aspartate for growth and remaining aspartate is converted to fumarate for anaerobic respiration [23]. Notably, increased aspartate was found in the cecum and colon, but not the ileum of S. Typhimurium infected mice [23], suggesting that this pathogen predominantly utilizes aspartate for growth in the small intestine. The small intestine is the primary site of S. Dublin invasion [47], however, there is a lack of understanding of such changes in the calf gut during the early stages of infection. During early life, calves undergo rapid changes in the gut homeostasis including alterations in the microbiome [48], oxidation status, immune regulation [49], and barrier functions [50]. These shifts create a highly dynamic and vulnerable environment in which gut microbiota composition can have profound implications for prevention of pathogen colonization and disease resistance. Therefore, understanding microbial–microbial interactions in this context is critical because commensal microbes provide the first line of defense against enteric pathogens including S. Dublin. In addition to killing beneficial microbiota by the activated immune cells, intestinal inflammation caused by Salmonella promotes the release of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species by neutrophils that are converted through a series of host–microbe interactions to yield the alternate electron acceptors tetrathionate and nitrate [51,52]. Tetrathionate respiration – and in some cases, nitrate respiration – enhances the competitiveness of S. Typhimurium by enabling the efficient utilization of the unique growth-promoting substrates 1,2-propanediol (1,2-PD), ethanolamine, and fructose-asparagine [29,53–55]. S. Typhimurium further advances its metabolic efficiency by favoring nitrate respiration over tetrathionate respiration, as nitrate serves as a more energetically favorable electron acceptor [51]. Accordingly, nitrate respiration is predicted to be relevant for later stages of infection in a streptomycin-treated salmonellosis mouse model [24]. In the perturbed gut, the butyrate-producing commensal Clostridia are diminished, resulting in increased epithelial oxygenation, which promotes S. Typhimurium expansion through its ability to utilize both nitrate and oxygen for respiration [56]. This perturbed state is maintained by nitrate respiring S. Typhimurium that consumes microbially derived butyrate through β-oxidation to promote aerobic conditions and increased colitis in mice (Table 1). Decreased butyrate availability shifts colonocyte metabolism from β-oxidation (butyrate + O2 → CO2) to fermentation (glucose → lactate) [57]. This shift in host-metabolism promotes oxygen concentrations and the release of lactate into the lumen [57], enhancing Salmonella colonization [58]. Additionally, simultaneous nitrate and aerobic respiration is required for fecal-oral transmission of S. Typhimurium from the mouse gut, otherwise, the minimum quantity of bacteria necessary for transmission is not achieved (108 per gram of feces) [56,59]. Overall, S. Typhimurium employs sophisticated metabolic strategies to outcompete the endogenous microbiota, enabling it to accumulate sufficient biomass for effective invasion of host tissues.

By leveraging S. Typhimurium as a model organism, we can gain insights into how S. Dublin navigates the calf intestinal microbiota, revealing key metabolic targets for novel therapeutics aimed at limiting its competitiveness and invasiveness. The presence and functionality of genes responsible for this metabolic flexibility in S. Dublin are poorly characterized (Table 1), and experimental validation of these mechanisms, especially in bovine epithelial models, remains lacking. Future research should prioritize validating the metabolic differences between S. Typhimurium and S. Dublin in the context of bovine-specific models. A thorough understanding of these mechanisms could inform the development of probiotic-based interventions that preserve or restore gut homeostasis by targeting key metabolic pathways. For instance, probiotics capable of anaerobic respiration using fumarate, tetrathionate, or nitrate could not only benefit from these electron acceptors to enhance their own growth, but also reduce their availability to S. Dublin, thereby diminishing its metabolic advantage. Additionally, genomic analysis can identify probiotic strains that resist lysis during intestinal inflammation by employing the same protective mechanisms used by Salmonella, as summarized by [60]. Optimizing such probiotic approaches to disrupt the metabolic processes that enable S. Dublin to thrive presents an effective strategy to prevent invasion in the calf gut.

Pathogenesis and virulence

To develop therapeutics that target S. Dublin, a foundational understanding of how this pathogen moves through the host, induces disease, and evades immune defenses is vital. Although S. Dublin and S. Typhimurium serotypes share high genetic similarity [61], S. Typhimurium is an imperfect representative as it displays a generalist lifestyle, whereas S. Dublin is specific to cattle [3,5]. Nevertheless, the extensive body of research on S. Typhimurium provides a valuable framework for studying commensal-pathogen and host–pathogen interactions to identify therapeutic targets that can be validated and adapted in the context of S. Dublin.

Invasion mechanisms

While some studies have evaluated the invasion of S. Dublin in bovine cells [47,62], work involving S. Typhimurium dominates the literature with clear mechanistic descriptions of invasion processes. S. Typhimurium breaches intestinal epithelial cells (IEC) using four defined mechanisms, which largely rely on the transcription of the highly conserved Salmonella pathogenicity island 1 (SPI-1) [4]. The first invasion route uses either the “trigger” or “zipper” mechanism, by which Salmonella actively induces its own uptake into enterocytes, leading to internalization and subsequent intracellular replication [63–65]. The “trigger” mechanism depends on the secretion of effector proteins through the type-three secretion system encoded by SPI-1 (T3SS-1) [66,67]. Alternatively, the “zipper” mechanism involves the binding of outer membrane adhesin proteins (Rck and PagN) to host cell surface proteins, triggering internalization [65,68]. The second invasion route is through paracellular invasion between IEC following inflammation induced by T3SS-1 effector proteins [69,70]. S. Typhimurium disrupts tight junction integrity by rearranging the F-actin cytoskeleton structure, reducing the barrier function and promoting the occurrence of diarrhea [71]. Recent work validated that S. Dublin interferes with the uniform distribution of the tight junction protein, E-cadherin, using a bovine ileal organoid-derived monolayer [72], suggesting that S. Dublin may also utilize paracellular invasion in the bovine host. The third invasion route is through uptake by CD18+ phagocytes (i.e. macrophages, neutrophils, dendritic cells), which sample the GIT lumen as part of immune surveillance [73]. CD18+ cells are a diverse group of phagocytic leukocytes that transport engulfed material from the gut lumen directly to the circulatory system [73,74]. CD18+ dendritic cells (DCs) are recruited to sites of intestinal inflammation, where they extend dendrites between epithelial cells to sample luminal contents without disrupting epithelial barrier integrity, facilitating the uptake of S. Typhimurium [73]. The final invasion route is through microfold cells (M-cells) [75], which are antigen-delivering cells that use transcytosis to move antigens and pathogens from the luminal surface to the sub-epithelial lymphoid follicles (e.g. Peyer’s patches) [76]. S. Typhimurium attaches to M-cells using pili/fimbriae to induce membrane ruffling and trigger transcytosis [77]. M-cells dominate the bovine ileal mucosa [78], and S. Typhimurium have been reported to invade M-cells of bovine origin [75]. Although it is uncertain whether S. Dublin utilizes each of these invasion routes in bovine cells, it was confirmed that S. Dublin requires the T3SS-1 for invasion in the bovine host [47]. Hence, the expression of T3SS-1 or SPI-1 are critical targets to prevent S. Dublin invasion in the bovine host.

Systemic dissemination and persistent intracellular populations

Once delivered to the Peyer’s patch, S. Typhimurium are taken up by DC, macrophages, and neutrophils that serve as vehicles for systemic dissemination [75,78–80]. Following phagocytosis, Salmonella utilizes T3SS-1 and T3SS-2 (encoded by SPI-2) to rearrange the host phagosome into a Salmonella-containing vacuole (SCV), allowing a persistent state within host cells [81]. DC transport S. Typhimurium from the intestinal lumen to the mesenteric lymph nodes (MLN), facilitating systemic dissemination of the pathogen [82–84]. The MLN help control the systemic spread of S. Typhimurium, as mice lacking these structures show higher quantities of the pathogen at distant sites (e.g. spleen) [85]. Once containing S. Typhimurium, DCs follow a chemotactic gradient through the lymphatic system, promoting the dissemination of the pathogen to remote lymph nodes [86]. Additionally, infected CD18+ phagocytes migrate through the circulatory system of mice to spread this pathogen to the liver and spleen [80]. S. Typhimurium may induce apoptosis of DC through its T3SS-1 causing the bacterium to transfer to macrophages [87]. Once disseminated, persistent and dormant S. Typhimurium populations exist in a variety of host cell types including macrophages, fibroblasts, and epithelial cells at different sites across the body [88–90]. However, S. Typhimurium was not detected in fibroblasts using a bovine-ligated ileal loop model [75], which may be due to the limited lymphatic access in the loop model. This suggests that S. Typhimurium travels through the lymphatic circulation, primarily via immune cells, to reach and potentially invade fibroblasts during systemic dissemination.

In conclusion, the successful invasion of S. enterica is highly problematic as this pathogen can survive, persist, and replicate in many cellular environments within hosts. S. Dublin infections in adult cattle are often subclinical, likely due to the pathogen’s ability to persist within host cells. Intracellular immune populations are central to chronic infections as this provides a protective niche against the host immune response while disseminating the pathogen to distant body sites to provide novel niches for ongoing invasion. This persistence is particularly concerning in herd animals, as it enables S. Dublin to circulate within populations, posing a challenge for controlling transmission. Circulation within cattle herds becomes a greater concern during parturition as neonatal calves are highly susceptible to severe illness. To advance our current understanding, it is crucial to investigate whether S. Dublin shares these cellular survival mechanisms in bovine cells. Identifying these mechanisms could help pinpoint the exact locations within cattle where chronic S. Dublin infections are harbored, improving our ability to manage the disease. Novel therapeutics should target S. Dublin prior to invasion, preventing its persistence in immune cells and limiting both chronic infection and herd-wide transmission.

Probiotics as a targeted therapeutic

Probiotics are “live microorganisms that, when administered in adequate amounts, confer a health benefit to the host” [91]. The commensal microbes are only considered probiotics once they have been isolated, characterized, and proven to provide health benefits to the host [91]. Most probiotic bacteria are defined at the strain level, but some health authorities allow for species-level determination for specific lactic acid bacteria [91].

The efficacy of probiotics is a controversial topic due to instances of adverse host responses in addition to exaggerated health claims [92,93]. However, evidence remains that under certain circumstances, probiotics can reverse dysbiosis to restore gut health [94]. A probiotic therapeutic is particularly applicable to the case of S. Dublin as direct-fed microbes (DFM), including probiotics, significantly influence the small intestinal microbial communities in swine, humans, and calves [95–97]. Therefore, probiotics may prevent the metabolic competitiveness of S. Dublin by maintaining gut homeostasis. Through targeted and optimized probiotic intervention, it may be possible to manipulate the small intestinal microbiome to prevent the expansion and invasion of S. Dublin.

It should be noted that administering probiotics is not entirely without risk, particularly in neonates. In humans, for example, cases of probiotic-associated sepsis have been reported in pre-term infants, with rare but fatal outcomes in some instances [98]. In contrast, there is a striking paucity of information on probiotic safety in calves, and no well-documented adverse case reports are available to date, partly because it is rare to administer microbial interventions to immune-compromised calves. This absence of evidence has led to the general conclusion that probiotic supplementation in calves is low risk [99]. Nevertheless, the theoretical risk of introducing exogenous microbiota to neonatal animals with developing immune systems cannot be discounted [16]. We argue that these uncertain risks are likely outweighed by the well-established threat posed by S. Dublin, which remains a major cause of morbidity and mortality in calves.

Due to its plasticity, the early-life calf gut microbiota represents a strategic timeframe for probiotic manipulation. Resistance is a trait of the gut microbiota and refers to its opposition to disturbance events while the resilience of the gut microbiota refers to how fast the microbiota returns to homeostasis following disturbance events [100,101]. These two qualities of the mature gut microbiota challenge the efficacy of probiotics administered to adults. Hence, the neonatal period of a calves (0–14 days) life provides a “window of opportunity” in which microbial interventions are most effective at improving gut health [102,103]. Additionally, a probiotic that protects neonatal calves against S. Dublin would address the limitation of the preferred modified live vaccine, which, as mentioned earlier, is not to be administered to calves under two weeks of age. The modified live vaccine is preferred over the bacterins because it induces a cell-mediated immune response, which is more effective against intracellular pathogens. For calves over two weeks old, probiotics may be administered alongside this vaccine to provide a multifaceted therapeutic regime that mitigates the intestinal growth and invasion of S. Dublin while simultaneously priming the host’s cell-mediated immune response. Therefore, we suggest that probiotics in calves during the “window of opportunity” increase their effectiveness by maintaining homeostasis and increasing colonization resistance toward Salmonella. However, to elicit such desirable effects, probiotic candidates must be carefully selected and screened to ensure successful, safe, and optimized application against S. Dublin.

Lactobacilli as probiotic candidates to target S. Dublin in calves

The neonatal calf gut microbiome undergoes rapid changes during the pre-weaning period [104]. From birth, bifidogenic oligosaccharides within colostrum selectively promote bifidobacteria growth, with elevated abundance observed upon heat treatment of colostrum [105,106], and timed feeding [107]. Both lactobacilli and bifidobacteria readily ferment lactose, glucose, and galactose made available through dietary milk [32,108], while creating an environment suitable for adult-like obligate anaerobes. Additionally, bifidobacteria and lactobacilli are known to interact in vivo through trophic cross-feeding interactions, promoting stable intestinal populations of these commensal microbes [33]. Due to these biological and nutritional factors, lactobacilli are dominant members of the small intestinal microbiota of 3-week-old calves [109]. For instance, in the ileal digesta, Firmicutes (97.7%) dominated the bacterial community at the phylum level, and lactobacilli (44.5%) was the most abundant genus.

Lactobacilli, which were re-classified into 25 distinct genera [110], are essential members of the healthy gut microbiota. A recent study by Varada and colleagues [111] administered probiotic Limosilactobacillus reuteri and Ligilactobacillus salivarus, which stabilized the gut microbiota by promoting the abundance of Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus while reducing the abundance of the pathobionts E. coli and Clostridium perfringens in buffalo calves from days 4–33 of life. Additionally, probiotic lactobacilli enhanced humoral immunity, lymphocyte proliferation, cell-mediated immunity, and alleviated oxidative stress by promoting activity of the antioxidant catalase and superoxide dismutase [111]. Antioxidant activity is particularly useful for interfering with S. enterica as these serotypes utilize reactive oxygen species for anaerobic respiration, which promotes pathogenic growth in vivo.

The early-life ecological environment of calves supports the selective colonization and dominance of lactobacilli in the small intestine. Lactobacilli contribute to calf gut health by supporting the growth of commensal LAB, suppressing pathogenic microbes, reducing oxidative stress, and modulating the immune response. This natural symbiotic relationship between lactobacilli and the calf host highlights their potential as probiotic candidates. This is particularly relevant in the context of S. Dublin as lactobacilli dominate the GIT region of pathogenic invasion. Given their localization and functional benefits, lactobacilli represent promising candidates for targeted probiotic interventions aimed at mitigating S. Dublin infection in calves.

Host-specificity of lactobacilli

Lactobacilli evolved from free-living ancestors into host-adapted lineages causing lactobacilli to display a variety of ecological lifestyles ranging from free-living to strictly host-adapted [112]. With few exceptions, the genera Lactobacillus, Ligilactobacillus, and Limosilactobacillus are adapted to the vertebrate host [110,112]. Some species of Ligilactobacillus and Limosilactobacillus express urease, an enzyme that enhances bacterial resistance to gastric acid, aiding their survival and colonization in vertebrate hosts [113,114]. Additionally, the conversion of sucrose into exopolysaccharides by some Limosilactobacillus enables biofilm formation on the non-secretory epithelia in the upper intestinal tract, further promoting host adaptation [115,116].

Consideration and validation of lactobacilli lifestyles are essential for probiotic candidate selection since host-adapted isolates adhere, colonize, and establish better in the target host [117]. This was experimentally validated by Zhao and colleagues [96], in which pigs were fed lactobacilli with a range of lifestyles, and the host-adapted lactobacilli were present at higher abundances compared to nomadic or free-living lactobacilli. Since colonization is often a prerequisite for functional effects such as pathogen exclusion and immune modulation, host-specific strains are more likely to deliver consistent probiotic benefits in the target species. This underscores the importance of selecting lactobacilli strains specifically adapted to the bovine gastrointestinal environment, particularly those that naturally colonize calves. Acquisition of a lactobacilli strain from host intestines is insufficient to assume host-adaptation because allochthonous microbes originating from feed or the oral cavity temporarily exist in the GIT [117]. However, for well-studied microbes such as Lm. reuteri, we can infer the host-adaptation of probiotic candidates through subspecies classifications and phylogenomic clustering [118]. For example, herbivore-adapted Lm. reuteri belongs to subsp. reuteri [118,119], and therefore, isolates belonging to this subspecies are the most appropriate Lm. reuteri strains for probiotic application in cattle.

In summary, host-specificity is a key factor in probiotic candidate selection, as it enhances bacterial persistence and colonization in the target environment. Host-adapted lactobacilli strains possess traits such as acid resistance and epithelial adhesion that improve their ecological fitness in the calf GIT. Therefore, we propose the use of bovine-specific lactobacilli, particularly those that stably colonize the calf small intestine, to be targeted probiotics against S. Dublin.

Novel aspects of probiotics to inhibit S. Dublin invasion

Probiotic bacteria can help reduce the host’s susceptibility to pathogenic infections by outcompeting harmful microbes and modulating the host’s immune response, which has been extensively reviewed [120]. In this section, we focus on mechanistic findings from S. Typhimurium to identify probiotic traits in lactobacilli that may prevent S. Dublin growth and invasion in the calf gut.

SCFAs regulate Salmonella invasion

Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) are products of microbial fermentation that serve as an intermediate between the host and the gut microbiome [121], and have been reported to play a vital role in modulating the invasiveness of S. enterica following diffusion into the bacterial cytoplasm [122]. In the preweaned calf hindgut, the most common SCFAs are acetate (~70%), propionate (~20%), and butyrate (~10%) [123] and these metabolites influence many aspects of the calf’s immune system [124]. An in vitro study reported that SCFAs mimicking those of the small intestine and large intestine upregulated and downregulated S. Typhimurium SPI-1 gene expression, respectively [27]. Acetate

Acetate is produced by many microbes through either the acetyl-CoA or the Wood-Ljungdahl pathway [121,132]. As shown in Figure 1, the role of acetate on the virulence of S. enterica is concentration dependent [125]. The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of acetate (pH-unadjusted) was 1875–3750 µg/mL against 12 Salmonella strains belonging to subsp. enterica and arizonae [125]. Subinhibitory concentrations of acetic acid upregulated virulent gene expression in S. Typhimurium str. T7, and to a lesser extent in S. Infantis str. I4 (Figure 1; [125]). Additionally, a SCFA cocktail reflecting that of the small intestine also increased SPI-1 gene expression in S. Typhimurium [27]. SirA and BarA are independent positive regulators of SPI-1 gene expression in S. enterica, but only SirA mediates acetate-induced activation of SPI-1, which requires acetate conversion to acetyl-phosphate via ackA (Figure 1; Table 1; [27]). As depicted in Figure 1, acetyl-phosphate is proposed to activate SirA through phosphorylation, which in turn induces the SPI-1 regulator HilA [27,126]. This is supported by the finding that subinhibitory concentrations of acetic acid upregulated hilA and sipA gene expression in S. Typhimurium T7, both of which are encoded by SPI-1 [125]. Alternatively, when acetate was provided alongside propionate and butyrate at concentrations reflecting the colon, invasive gene expression decreased by 3- to 26-fold, even though acetate was present at 110 mM [27]. In summary, low acetate concentrations promoted invasive phenotypes, but SCFA cocktails including higher amounts of acetate, propionate, and butyrate reduced the invasiveness of S. Typhimurium. Therefore, enhancing acetate yield via targeted probiotic strategies may help mitigate S. Dublin invasion in the calf small intestine.

Butyrate

Butyrate is produced by specialized gut microbiota [121,133] through the phosphotransbutyrylase/butyrate kinase [134] and the butyryl-CoA:acetate CoA-transferase pathways [135]. Butyrate regulates S. enterica virulence through its role as a class IV regulator of SPI-1, allowing it to control all SPI-1 promoters [129]. As depicted in Figure 1, butyrate also exerts a concentration-dependent influence on the invasive gene expression of S. enterica subsp. enterica [125]. The MIC of butyric acid was 3750 µg/mL against 12 Salmonella strains belonging to subsp. enterica and arizonae [125]. In S. Typhimurium T7, subinhibitory concentrations of butyric acid promoted the expression of all invasive genes examined, with the most pronounced increase observed in invA (Figure 1; [125]). However, at concentrations and pH conditions reflecting that of the colon, butyrate reduced SPI-1 gene expression in S. Typhimurium by three to sevenfold (Figure 1; [27]). Interestingly, these effects of butyrate are thought to be specific to virulent gene expression [27]. Phenotypic experiments demonstrated that butyrate reduced S. Enteritidis invasion in avian IEC at pH 6 and 7 [136]. Some lactobacilli can synthesize butyrate in a lipase-mediated manner [133], but these microbes are not major synthesizers of butyrate in the gut [121]. Therefore, in the context of probiotic lactobacilli, butyrate is unlikely to serve as a primary mechanism for mitigating S. Dublin. However, this strategy may be more effectively applied using butyrate-producing probiotics.

Propionate

Propionate is produced by only certain gut microbes through the succinate, acrylate, and propanediol pathways [121]. Lactobacilli are only capable of forming propionate through the propanediol pathway, and this requires that the propanediol utilization (pdu) operon is encoded [32]. Notably, some Salmonella spp. may synthesize 1,2-PD from fucose or rhamnose [38] and subsequently generate propionate through the propanediol pathway [53]. Microbial synthesis of 1,2-PD occurs through the deoxyhexose, methylglyoxal, and lactate pathways, with bacteria and fungi utilizing these pathways under anoxic and aerobic conditions, respectively [16]. One pathway has been validated in vivo, where intestinal 1,2-PD production is initiated by the mucinolytic bacterium Bifidobacterium bifidum, which degrades dietary fibers or host mucin into fucose. Fucose is then converted into 1,2-PD by Bifidobacterium breve using L-fucose permease (fucP), which is converted to propionate by pdu-positive Lm. reuteri [33].

Of the three major SCFA, propionate is the most consistent at reducing S. enterica invasion (Figure 1). Many of the beneficial effects observed by butyrate were simultaneously observed by propionate. For instance, high propionate concentrations reduced invasive gene expression of S. Typhimurium by three to seven-fold, and these effects were specific to invasive genes, including reduced expression of invF and sipC (Figure 1; [27]). Additionally, while subinhibitory concentrations of propionic acid still upregulated invasive gene expression of S. Typhimurium T7, the effect was significantly less compared to that of acetic and butyric acids (Figure 1; [125]). The MIC of propionic acid is 3750 µg/mL [125], and subinhibitory concentrations significantly reduced S. Typhimurium ATCC 14028 motility, fliC expression (required for flagellum synthesis), and altered fimbrial structure, making them brittle and broken [137,138].

Metabolism of propionate via the intermediate propionyl-CoA is necessary for reducing IEC invasion at physiological pH by downregulating SPI-1 gene expression, as shown in (Figure 1; [128]). Propionate exerts this effect by post-translationally destabilizing and degrading HilD, a central regulator of SPI-1 (Figure 1; [128]). By interfering with HilD, SPI-1 invasive genes cannot be upregulated (Figure 1), explaining the consistent observation that propionate inhibits the penetration of epithelial cells. For example, propionate reduced invasion of S. Typhimurium in IPEC-J2 and IPI-2I cells and of S. Enteritidis in DIV-1 cells [136,137,139]. Furthermore, the role of microbially produced propionate was validated in vivo using mouse models, where Bacteroides spp. inhibited S. Typhimurium colonization in the GIT, while mutants unable to synthesize propionate failed to do so [122]. Due to these genetic and phenotypic observations, propionate is a critical inhibitor of Salmonella invasion and therefore represents a strong candidate for therapeutic investigation. However, further research is needed to confirm whether propionate inhibits SPI-1 gene expression in S. Dublin, as this has not yet been demonstrated.

Interestingly, S. Typhimurium catabolizes propionate to pyruvate under nitrate respiration using the prpBCDE operon (Table 1) and does so to overcome the propionate-mediated colonization resistance in the inflamed GIT of mice [39]. This highlights the importance of placing experimental findings in the context of pathogenic metabolism as the incorporation of alternate terminal electron acceptors greatly altered the interpretation of research findings. In conclusion, propionate-producing probiotics, particularly lactobacilli, show strong potential as a preventive strategy against S. Dublin invasion. Furthermore, they may be valuable as a treatment option when used alongside remedies that mitigate oxidative stress as this will reduce anaerobic respiration by S. Dublin.

Nutrient blocking

The endogenous gut microbiota protects from pathogen invasion through competitive exclusion of pathogenic microorganisms, termed colonization resistance. Nutrient blocking – a mechanism that contributes to colonization resistance – occurs when commensal microbes consume nutrients, reducing the availability for pathogen utilization [140]. As mentioned earlier, S. Typhimurium ferments the niche nutrients 1,2-PD, ethanolamine, and fructose-asparagine (F-Asn) under anaerobic respiration, making these strong candidates for nutrient blocking by optimized probiotics. While the genes responsible for the metabolism of these three substrates have been identified in S. Dublin through bioinformatic analyses (Table 1), there is a significant lack of experimental evidence characterizing this serotype’s metabolic phenotypes in response to supplementation with these substrates. In this section, we will explore nutrient blocking as a selection criterion to optimize probiotic lactobacilli intended to mitigate S. Dublin, with Figure 2 providing a conceptual overview. 1,2-propanediol

The niche nutrient 1,2-PD promoted S. Typhimurium competitiveness in the inflamed GIT of mice [53]. This substrate is synthesized in the gut by enteric microbes such as S. enterica, Escherichia coli, and Bifidobacterium breve that ferment host-derived fucose and rhamnose into 1,2-PD [33,38]. Lm. reuteri encoding the pdu operon utilize 1,2-PD for growth promotion, propionate synthesis, and increased acetate yield [33]. These characteristics make pdu-positive lactobacilli optimal candidates for probiotics aimed at blocking 1,2-PD utilization by S. Dublin in calves, as depicted in Figure 2. The therapeutic potential of the pdu operon is enhanced by the fact that it also converts glycerol into the broad-spectrum antimicrobial reuterin (Figure 2; [32]), which inhibits S. Enteritidis growth, especially when in the presence of lactic acid [143,145]. Therefore, the inclusion of pdu-positive lactobacilli strains in probiotic screening is desirable, as it may contribute to colonization resistance through nutrient blocking, the production of reuterin and propionate, and by increasing acetate yield (Figure 2).

Ethanolamine

Ethanolamine is a valuable carbon and nitrogen source for bacteria encoding the ethanolamine utilization (eut) operon [146], including S. Dublin [30,31]. Eukaryotic and prokaryotic cell turnover yields ethanolamine and glycerol from phosphatidylcholine, a component of cell membranes (Figure 2; [141,142]). Consequently, ethanolamine is present in the bovine gut at concentrations exceeding 2 mM [147], making it readily available to S. Dublin in its natural host (Figure 2). In S. Typhimurium, ethanolamine is utilized in the inflamed GIT of mice under tetrathionate respiration, providing a competitive advantage as most commensal microbes cannot metabolize this nutrient [29]. Recent work by Akouris and colleagues [144] determined that Levilactobacillus brevis competitively excluded S. Typhimurium in the presence of ethanolamine and adenosylcobalamin, not through nutrient-blocking, but by upregulating an aggregation-promoting factor surface protein. This protein enhanced L. brevis adhesion to mucin and Caco-2 cells, reducing S. Typhimurium cell surface availability (Figure 2; [144]). Additionally, ethanolamine upregulated propanediol metabolism in L. brevis, potentially aiding nutrient blocking of 1,2-PD. Given these findings, the potential for eut-positive lactobacilli strains to serve as optimized probiotics for targeting S. Dublin in calves warrants further investigation. Although nutrient blocking of ethanolamine was not attributed to conferring colonization resistance by L. brevis, this concept should be explored in the context of S. Dublin and bovine-adapted eut-positive lactobacilli using a bovine-intestinal cellular model.

Conclusion and future perspectives

The cattle industry is challenged by the lack of effective and sustainable therapeutics that mitigate the emerging pathogen S. Dublin. Mechanistic understanding of bacterial–bacterial and host–bacterial interactions suggests that novel microbial-based therapeutics may be an effective method to mitigate S. Dublin in cattle. However, we lack a thorough understanding of the molecular mechanisms employed by S. Dublin, forcing us to use S. Typhimurium as a model for S. Dublin. For instance, most mechanistic insights into metabolic flexibility come from S. Typhimurium studies in murine models. Although genomic comparisons suggest that some of these pathways are conserved in S. Dublin (Table 1), its host adaptation to cattle means that key aspects of pathogenesis and intervention may differ, underscoring the need for direct validation in bovine models. The use of cell types belonging to different host species is appropriate for the investigation of S. Typhimurium due to its generalist lifecycle [148]. However, this introduces limitations when using these research findings to understand the molecular mechanisms of S. Dublin pathogenesis because S. Typhimurium induces dramatically different infections in different animals, with systemic infections in mice and gastrointestinal infections in bovines [15]. Hence, it is unclear whether it is more appropriate to focus on the Typhimurium-mouse experiments, which mimic systemic infection observed by Dublin-bovine infections, or if the Typhimurium-bovine models are more accurate due to the usage of the bovine-host system. Hence, we must be cautious when using a generalist serovar as a model to understand the cattle-adapted S. Dublin. To advance our understanding of host-specific serotypes, it is best to use host-specific cells to maximize the external validity of the research findings. This approach would be best applied to define the niche nutrients that are important for the infectious lifecycle of S. Dublin in the cattle host. The efficacy of probiotics has long been debated, and therefore, the selection of probiotic candidates for targeted applications requires a careful and thorough screening process. The first step is to identify the most reliable taxonomic microbes that are adapted and endogenous to the niche of interest. In the case of mitigating S. Dublin, lactobacilli are a natural choice due to their dominance of the calf’s small intestine, the primary location of intestinal invasion. Since many lactobacilli are host-adapted, it is important to select strains that were not only acquired from the calf host but also demonstrate lineage-specificity for bovine species when possible (e.g. Lm. reuteri). Once the panel of probiotic candidates has been determined, we need to screen for candidates that demonstrate specific and targeted mechanisms that reduce the competitiveness and invasiveness of the target pathogen in vivo. This is challenging in the case of S. Dublin due to the lack of experimental evidence characterizing the invasion and metabolic mechanisms of S. Dublin in cattle. Hence, future work is needed to determine if S. Dublin anaerobically respires using fumarate, tetrathionate, and/or nitrate in the cattle host, and to identify the niche nutrients that promote S. Dublin competitiveness in vivo. Furthermore, research is needed to validate whether propionate also consistently inhibits the invasive gene expression of S. Dublin at physiological pH and whether S. Dublin may utilize propionate as a nutrient in the inflamed gut. By leveraging nutrient blocking and propionate synthesis, probiotics may offer a practical and effective strategy for inhibiting S. Dublin invasion and infection in the bovine gut, highlighting the potential of mechanism-driven probiotic development.
