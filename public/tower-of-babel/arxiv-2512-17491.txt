Abstract
. Chemical reaction networks (CRNs) are foundational models for describing complex biochemical processes. We study noncompetitive CRNs, a class of networks whose static states are rate-independent, and that can implement ReLU neural networks. A central contribution of this work is that noncompetitive CRNs are special instances of Abelian networks (ANs)—a well-established framework for self-organized criticality. CRNs of interest in biochemistry and systems biology are embedded in complex networks so that local CRNs have to respond to internal and environmental cues. We describe the network’s response to such perturbations using a sandpile Markov chain whose state space is the set of CRN’s static states, from where no reaction is possible. The addition of molecules to a static state induces reactions that move the system into a new static state. For noncompetitive CRNs of finite state space, we use AN theory to get that the fraction of static states that are recurrent is in one to one correspondence with the critical group of the AN. Overall, this work establishes a unified algebraic and probabilistic framework for analyzing the long-term behavior of noncompetitive CRNs.



1.
Introduction

Biochemical networks perform complex tasks in small volume and noisy environments, like e.g. transducing signals through signaling networks or controlling gene regulatory networks.
Recent researches in molecular programming have explored mathematical and computational models of such complex biochemical pathways that can be mapped onto architectures which are compatible with the kind of computations that cells perform in natural environments, like for example signaling cascades, see, e.g.,
[
21
]
, where the related biochemical net is similar to a neural network (NN). On the other hand, natural biochemical network dynamics are modeled using chemical reaction network theory (CRN). Chemical implementation of NN architectures as well as the associated learning processes have been proposed
[
21
,
8
,
9
,
25
,
1
]
. We focus here on noncompetitive CRNs
[
26
]
that are rate independent, whose equilibria are robust to reaction rates and kinetic laws, and depend solely on the stoichiometric structure of the CRN. Noncompetitive CRNs can furthermore implement ReLU NN, with continuous input variables which are seen as continuous species concentrations in the CRN implementation. To address the complex computational tasks that cell must perform in small volumes and with low molecular counts, some works have proposed to use discrete NN models like chemical Boltzmann machines
[
25
,
9
]
. We consider here robust discrete noncompetive CRNs.

CRNs of interest in biochemistry and systems biology are embedded in complex networks so that local CRNs have to respond to internal and environmental cues.
We describe the network’s response to such perturbations using a sandpile Markov chain on the set of the static states from where no reaction are possible.
The transition mechanism of the sandpile Markov chain is obtained
by adding molecules at random to static configurations, and then letting reactions occur until a new static state is reached.
A central contribution of the present work is the observation that rate-independent noncompetitive CRNs are special instances of Abelian networks (ANs)—a well-established framework for self-organized criticality in which processors update their internal state and send messages to neighboring processors.
For noncompetitive CRNs of finite state space, we use AN theory to get that the fraction of static states that are recurrent is in one to one correspondence with the critical group of the AN.
Such states
will appear infinitely often along the orbits of the dynamics, and might therefore play a dominant biological role, while the other static states will not persist in the long run.
We further identify structural conditions under which noncompetitive CRNs are execution bounded, meaning that every execution consists of only finitely many reactions, and show that these conditions are equivalent to the halting property of the associated AN. For execution-bounded noncompetitive CRNs with finite state space, one can use algebraic tools from finite ANs theory—including critical groups, production matrices, and burning algorithms—to classify the recurrent states.
We show in particular how generalized toppling networks associated with noncompetitive CRNs can be treated using this approach, and we provide simple examples to illustrate the methodology. Overall, this work establishes a unified algebraic and probabilistic framework for analyzing the long-term behavior of noncompetitive CRNs.

2.2.
Chemical Reaction Networks

In this subsection, we provide a brief introduction to the formalism of chemical reaction networks. For a more detailed study, we refer to
[
17
]
.

Definition 2.1
.

A
Chemical Reaction Network
(CRN) is a triple
\mathcal{H}=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{R})
where
\mathcal{S}
is the set of
species
,
\mathcal{S}=\{1,\cdots,N\}
,
\mathcal{C}
is the set of
complexes
and
\mathcal{R}
is the set of
reactions
,
\mathcal{R}=\{R_{1},\cdots,R_{d}\}
.

The set of
species
is regarded as an orthogonal basis of the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, which means that each species
i
corresponds to the canonical basis vector
e_{i}
of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
A
complex

\nu
is a non-negative integer linear combination of species, represented as a vector in
\mathbb{N}^{N}
, where each component specifies the number of molecules of the corresponding species.
The symbol
i+j
is an abbreviation for
e_{i}+e_{j}
, with a similar interpretation for any complex
\nu\in\mathcal{C}
.

The
reaction vector
associated with the
r^{th}
reaction
\nu_{r}\to\nu^{\prime}_{r}
, is the integer-valued vector
l_{r}\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}
, defined by

l_{r}=\nu^{\prime}_{r}-\nu_{r},

This vector indicates, for each species, the net number of molecules of consumed (reactants) or created (products) during the
r^{th}
reaction. We define the
reactant matrix

\Gamma^{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
and the
product matrix

\Gamma^{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
as follows:
\Gamma^{r}_{ir}
denotes the number of molecules of species
i
appearing as reactants (on the left-hand side) in reaction
r
, while
\Gamma^{p}_{ir}
denotes the number of molecules of species
i
appearing as products (on the right-hand side) in reaction
r
. Notice that
\nu_{r}=\Gamma^{r}_{.,r}
and
\nu^{\prime}_{r}=\Gamma^{p}_{.,r}
.
The
stoichiometric matrix

\Gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
is defined as the difference between the product matrix and the reactant matrix, and has the reaction vectors as its columns, that is,

\Gamma=\Gamma^{p}-\Gamma^{r}=[l_{1}\;\;l_{2}\;\;\dots\;\;l_{d}],

where for
r\in\{1,...,d\}
,
l_{r}=\nu_{r}^{\prime}-\nu_{r}
corresponds to the
r^{\text{th}}
reaction vector.

Definition 2.2
.

Some species only appear as products but never as reactants. They will not influence the dynamics of the system, and will be called the
sink
species.

2.3.
The graph of complexes

The set
{\mathcal{C}}
of chemical complexes

associated with
{\mathcal{S}}
and
{\mathcal{R}}
is defined as
{\mathcal{C}}=\cup_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\{\nu_{r},\nu^{\prime}_{r}\}
. We define
{\mathcal{C}}_{S}
and
{\mathcal{C}}_{P}
to be the sets of source and product complexes, respectively. Note that
{\mathcal{C}}_{S}\cup{\mathcal{C}}_{P}={\mathcal{C}}
, although they are not necessarily disjoint. The set of reactions can then be described using directed edges by writing
{\mathcal{E}}=\{(\nu_{r}\to\nu^{\prime}_{r});\ r\in{\mathcal{R}}\}
. This defines a
directed graph

{\mathcal{G}}=({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{E}})
, with node set
{\mathcal{C}}
and edge set
{\mathcal{E}}
. Let
m:=|{\mathcal{C}}|
and
d=|{\mathcal{E}}|
.
A reaction network of this type is said to be
weakly reversible
if, for each directed edge
(\nu\to\nu^{\prime})\in{\mathcal{E}}
, there exists a sequence of complexes
\nu_{0},\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{k}\in\mathcal{C}
such that
\nu_{0}=\nu^{\prime}
,
\nu_{k}=\nu
and
(\nu_{n}\to\nu_{n+1})\in\mathcal{E}
for all
n=0,\dots,k-1
.
We adopt the notations and definitions from
[
16
]
: a complex
\nu\in{\mathcal{C}}
is said to be

directly linked
to a complex
\nu^{\prime}
, denoted by
\nu\leftrightarrow\nu^{\prime}
, if either
\nu\to\nu^{\prime}
or
\nu^{\prime}\to\nu
.
A complex
\nu
is said to be
linked
to a complex
\nu^{\prime}
, denoted by
\nu\sim\nu^{\prime}
, if either
\nu=\nu^{\prime}
, or there exists a finite sequence of complexes
\nu_{0},\cdots,\nu_{k}\in\mathcal{C}
such that

\nu=\nu_{0}\leftrightarrow\nu_{1}\leftrightarrow\cdots\leftrightarrow\nu_{k}=\nu^{\prime}
.

The relation
\sim
is an equivalence relation, and the corresponding equivalence classes are called
linkage classes
.
Two nodes are said to be
strongly linked
if there exists a directed path from the first to the second and another directed path from the second back to the first.
A
strong-linkage class
of a reaction network is a maximal subset of nodes that are strongly linked to each other.
A
terminal strong-linkage class
is a strong-linkage class in which no node reacts to a node in another strong-linkage class. (A node is said to
react to
another if it lies at the tail of a directed reaction arrow whose head is the latter.)

Nodes belonging to terminal strong-linkage classes are called
terminal
. These notions from graph theory are illustrated in Figure
2
.

A
configuration
or
state
of a CRN is a vector
c\in\mathbb{N}^{N}
. A configuration
c^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}^{N}
is said to be
reachable
from another configuration
c\in\mathbb{N}^{N}
, if there exists a sequence of reactions leading from
c
to
c^{\prime}
i.e. there exist a non-negative vector
u\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
such that
c^{\prime}=c+\Gamma u
. We then write
c\hookrightarrow c^{\prime}
.

In the discrete setting, given a state
c\in\mathbb{N}^{N}
some reactions are possible as long as
c\geq\Gamma^{r}_{.,r}
for some
r\in\{1,...,d\}
, otherwise we say that
no reactions are possible
from
c
.
The support a complex
\nu=\sum_{s=1}^{N}\lambda_{s}e_{s}
is defined by

{\rm supp}(\nu)=\{s\in{\mathcal{S}}:\ \lambda_{s}\neq 0\}.

The
molecularity
of a reaction
\nu\to\nu^{\prime}\in\mathcal{R}
is defined as the number of molecules in the reactant
|\nu|=\sum\limits_{s=1}^{N}\lambda_{s}
. Accordingly, reactions are classified as unimolecular, bimolecular, trimolecular, or, more generally, n-molecular reactions.

Definition 2.3
.

A complex
\nu\in\mathcal{C}
is called
single-species
or
multi-species
if it contains one species or more than one species, respectively.

2.4.
Stoichiometric subspace and the notion of deficiency

Observing that chemical reactions are obtained by adding reaction vectors
l_{r}
,
r=1,\cdots,d
, one naturally defines
the
stoichiometric subspace

{\mathcal{T}}
as the span of the reaction vectors
l_{r}=\nu^{\prime}_{r}-\nu_{r}
.
For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}_{\geq 0}
the sets
x+{\mathcal{T}}
and
(x+{\mathcal{T}})\cap\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{N}
are referred to as
the
stoichiometric compatibility classes
and the
positive stoichiometric compatibility classes
associated with
x
, respectively. Similarly, we define the
discrete stoichiometric subspace

{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}
as the
\mathbb{Z}
-span of the reaction vectors, and for
x\in\mathbb{N}^{N}
,
x+{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}
and
(x+{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}})\cap\mathbb{N}^{N}
are referred as the
discrete stoichiometric compatibility classes
and the
discrete positive stoichiometric compatibility classes
associated with x, respectively. The
deficiency
of a reaction network
is the number
\delta
defined by

\delta=\texttt{\#}\hbox{ complexes}-\texttt{\#}\hbox{ linkage classes }-{\rm dim}({\mathcal{T}}).

The deficiency zero Theorem
[
16
]
states that the o.d.e. associated
with non-weakly reversible CRNs of zero deficiency cannot admit a positive equilibrium, for arbitrary kinetics. For weakly reversible CRNs of zero deficiency and under mass-action kinetics, each compatibility class contains a single positive equilibrium. These results hold in the large volume limit where the input variables represent continuous species concentrations but do not give any particular information for discrete dynamics.

3.
Non competitive CRNs

We focus on a particular class of chemical reaction networks known as noncompetitive CRNs
[
26
]
. This class exhibits favorable properties both in terms of convergence and in their ability to approximate neural networks. These models are motivated by the experimentally observed computational robustness of biochemical CRNs, that are embedded in complex and fluctuating biochemical environments in systems biology.

Definition 3.1
.

A CRN is said to be
noncompetitive
if every species whose molecular count is decreased in a reaction appears as a reactant in that reaction only.

Definition 3.2
.

A configuration
c\in\mathbb{N}^{N}
from which no reactions are possible is called a
static state
. We denote by
\text{Stat }\mathcal{H}
the set of static states of a CRN
\mathcal{H}
.

Noncompetitive CRNs have the property that each positive stoichiometric compatibility class contains exactly one static state for continuous and discrete versions of the CRNs:

Theorem 3.1
.

[Theorem 4 of
[
26
]
]
Assume a stochastic CRN is noncompetitive. If

\textbf{a}\hookrightarrow\textbf{b}
via path
p
with length
\ell_{p}
and
b
is static, then there is no
path from
a
with length longer than
\ell_{p}
, any path with length
\ell_{p}

also ends in
b
, and any path with length shorter than
\ell_{p}
ends in a
state which is not static.

3.1.
Execution boundedness

We now define the notion of an execution and describe the conditions under which an execution is finite

[
14
]
.

Definition 3.3
.

An
execution

\mathcal{E}
is a finite or infinite sequence of configurations
\mathcal{E}=(c_{0},c_{1},...)
, where
c_{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{N}
, such that
c_{n-1}\hookrightarrow c_{n}
and
c_{n-1}\neq c_{n}
for all
n\in\{1,...,|\mathcal{E}|-1\}
.

Definition 3.4
.

A CRN is said to be
execution bounded
at
c\in\mathbb{N}^{N}
if all executions starting at
c
are finite, and a CRN is
entirely execution bounded
if it is execution bounded from every initial configuration.

Intuitively, a CRN will be execution bounded if the total ”energy” of the system decreases with each reaction. We now introduce some tools and give an explicit criterion for deciding whether a CRN is execution bounded.

Definition 3.5
.

A
linear potential function

\psi:\mathbb{R}^{N}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is a non-negative linear function such that, for each reaction
(\nu\to\nu^{\prime})\in\mathcal{E}
,
\psi(\nu^{\prime})-\psi(\nu)<0
.

From definition,

\psi(a)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}v_{i}a_{i}\geq 0,\ \forall a\in\mathbb{R}^{N}_{\geq 0},

and therefore
v_{i}\geq 0
for all
i\in\{1,...,N\}
.

Theorem 3.2
.

(
[
14
]
,Theorem 6.5, and
[
12
]
)
A CRN has a linear potential function if and only if it is entirely execution bounded.

To decide whether a CRN has a linear potential function, one has to determine whether a given system of linear inequalities has a solution using linear programming (see
[
14
]
).

3.2.
Structural description of graphs of complexes for noncompetitive CRNs

Let
U_{0}
be the set of species that are never decreased in any reaction, such as species that are catalysts or enzymes. The noncompetitivity of the CRN then implies that one can partition the set
{\mathcal{S}}\setminus U_{0}
which are decreased in some reaction into a disjoint union of subsets
U_{k}\subset{\mathcal{S}}
, where
U_{k}
,
k=1,\cdots,p
,
p\leq d
.

Definition 3.6
.

Let
\nu\in\mathcal{C}
.
\nu
is a type 1 complex when

{\rm supp}(\nu)\cap U_{k}\neq\emptyset,\ \ {\rm supp}(\nu)\cap U_{k^{\prime}}\neq\emptyset,

for some
k\neq k^{\prime}
and
k,k^{\prime}\neq 0
.
\nu
is a type 2 complex when

{\rm supp}(\nu)\cap({\mathcal{S}}\setminus U_{0})=U_{k},

for a unique subset
U_{k}
,
k\neq 0
.

\nu
is a type 3 complex when

{\rm supp}(\nu)\subset U_{0}
.

\nu
is a complex of type 4 when

{\rm supp}(\nu)\cap({\mathcal{S}}\setminus U_{0})
is strictly included in some
U_{k}
,
k\neq 0
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Assume that the CRN is noncompetitive. Complexes of types 1 and 4 cannot be source complexes.
Let
\nu\in\mathcal{C}_{s}
be a type 2 complex such that
(\nu\to\nu^{\prime})\in\mathcal{E}
for a unique reaction. Then
\nu
and
\nu^{\prime}

are such that

(3.1)

\nu=\sum_{i\in U_{0}}\alpha_{i}e_{i}+\sum_{i\in U_{k}}\beta_{ik}e_{i},

where

is the canonical basis vector of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
associated with species
i
, and

(3.2)

\nu^{\prime}=\sum_{i\in U_{0}}\alpha^{\prime}_{i}e_{i}+\sum_{l=1}^{p}\sum_{i\in U_{l}}\beta^{\prime}_{il}e_{i},

for stoichiometric coefficients
\alpha_{i}
,
\alpha^{\prime}_{i}
,
\alpha^{\prime}_{i}
,
\beta_{ik}

and
\beta^{\prime}_{il}
, that satisfy the constraints
\alpha^{\prime}_{i}\geq\alpha_{i}
, for all
i\in U_{0}
, and

\beta^{\prime}_{il}<\beta_{il}
, for all

,

.

implies that the graphs of complexes of noncompetitive CRNs possess the following generic properties:

•

Complexes of types 1 and 4 cannot be the origin of a directed edge of

.

•

Complexes of type 2 with

•

Complexes of type 3 with

Lemma 3.4
.

In an execution bounded noncompetitive CRN, complexes of type 3 cannot be the origin of a directed edge of
{\mathcal{E}}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
(\nu\to\nu^{\prime})\in\mathcal{E}
, with
\nu=\sum_{s=1}^{N}\lambda_{s}e_{s}
,
\nu^{\prime}=\sum_{s=1}^{N}\lambda^{\prime}_{s}e_{s}
and

. Then
\nu^{\prime}\geq\nu
with a strict inequality for at least one species. For any non-negative linear function
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{N}_{\geq 0}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
,
with
\psi(a)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}v_{i}a_{i}

\forall i

So there is no linear potential function, and the CRN cannot be execution bounded.
∎

.

3.3.
ReLU Neural Networks

Example of a single layer neural network with ReLU activation function.

The authors of
[
26
]
introduced noncompetitive CRNs that can encode ReLU neural networks, see Figure
5
, with activation function

given by the ReLU function
f(y)=\max\{0,y\}
.
It has been observed that such linear rectifier neurons exhibit good learning performances
and can reproduce naturally sparse data, see, e.g.,
[
19
]
.
They also defined so-called RReLU nets that have rational weights with

w_{i}=\pm p_{i}/q_{i}
,
i=1,\cdots,d
, for integers
p_{i}
and
q_{i}
. The input variables
x_{i}
of the single layer neural network of Figure

are usually real numbers,
while the configurations of CRNs are non-negative integer valued vectors.
To allow the rewriting of NN in terms of noncompetitive CRNs, the authors of
[
26
]
adopt the so-called
dual-rail representation
to encode
positive and negative parts of
neural net input variables, by introducing CRN species
X^{+}
and
X^{-}
. When
X=x\in\mathbb{Z}
,
x=x^{+}-x^{-}
is the difference of two states of the related CRN
where both
x^{+}
and
x^{-}
count the number of molecules of species
X^{+}
and
X^{-}
.
Following
[
26
]
, RReLU neural networks can be expressed as a noncompetitive CRN using for all

, the reactions:

q_{i}X_{i}^{+}\longrightarrow p_{i}Y^{+},\ \ q_{i}X_{i}^{-}\longrightarrow p_{i}Y^{-},

when
w_{i}>0
,
and

q_{i}X_{i}^{+}\longrightarrow p_{i}Y^{-},\ \ q_{i}X_{i}^{-}\longrightarrow p_{i}Y^{+},

when
w_{i}<0
. The output of the NN is obtained by applying the ReLU function on
\sum_{i}w_{i}x_{i}^{\pm}(0)
, for an input
x(0)
. The CRN implementation of this sum is obtained, e.g, by repeated executions of the reactions
q_{i}X_{i}^{\pm}\longrightarrow p_{i}Y^{\pm}
starting with
x_{i}^{\pm}(0)
units of species
X_{i}^{\pm}
. During each reaction, stacks of
q_{i}
units of species
X_{i}^{\pm}
are converted into stacks of
p_{i}
units of species
Y^{\pm}
, so that, repeating such reactions as many times as possible, the number of
Y^{\pm}
units is increased by

The conservative ASM

The Abelian sandpile model was originally introduced in physics as a model for avalanches

Most models of ASM use a dissipative version in which, for example, nodes of a

4.
Markov chains associated with CRNs

4.1.
Reminders on Markov chains

We recall here the notion of recurrence for both discrete-time and continuous-time Markov chains.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
(X_{t})_{t\in\mathbb{R}}
be a CTMC (resp.
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a discrete-time Markov chain) on a countable state space
\mathcal{S}
. For
i\in\mathcal{S}
, we define the return probability
m_{i}
as follows:

m_{i}=\mathbb{P}(X_{t}=i\text{ for some }t>0\mid X_{0}=i)\quad\text{(resp. }m_{i}=\mathbb{P}(X_{n}=i\text{ for some }n\geq 1\mid X_{0}=i)\text{)}.

If
m_{i}=1
, the state
i
is said to be
recurrent
; if
m_{i}<1
, the state
i
is said to be
transient
.

This terminology reflects the long-term behavior of the process: If a state is recurrent, then the process is guaranteed to return to it infinitely often, almost surely. In the theory of Markov chains, recurrent states often appear as part of closed communicating classes, which the process cannot escape once entered. This justifies the name
recurrent
, as the system keeps revisiting such states over time.

4.2.
The usual CTMC

In most applications in systems biology and physics, the numbers of molecules of each species in a CRN are random variable whose time evolution is modeled by a Continuous-Time Markov chain (CTMC).
Let

(4.1)

X(t)=(X_{1}(t),\cdots,X_{N}(t))\in\mathbb{N}^{N},

be the random vector representing the molecular counts of the various species at time
t
. The process
(X(t))_{t\geq 0}
is a Markov chain with jumps, for reactions

(\nu\to\nu^{\prime})\in\mathcal{E}
with
l=\nu^{\prime}-\nu

x\longrightarrow x+l,

where
x=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{N})
is a state of the CRN.
This CTMC satisfies the following stochastic equation:

X(t)=X(0)+\sum_{r=1}^{d}Y_{r}\Big(\int_{0}^{t}\lambda_{r}(X(s))\Big)l_{r},

where
\{Y_{1},\dots,Y_{d}\}
is a set of i.i.d. Poisson random variables of unit rates, and, e.g., with mass action transition rates
\lambda_{r}
for each reaction
r=1,\cdots,d
, see, e.g.,
[
15
]
.

4.3.
Sandpile Markov chain for noncompetitive CRNs

The authors of
[
11
]
and
[
26
]
proposed to study the structural properties of CRNs using a simplified nondetermistic dynamical model which was designed to isolate the effect of stoichiometry from the effect of transition rates such as the mass-action rates used in the usual CTMC. The authors of
[
26
]
proved that for noncompetitive CRNs, convergence in this simplified model implies convergence under mass-action kinetics or any other reasonable kinetics. The model was used to explore the set of reachable states using only the CRN’s stoichiometric structure.
In this work, we go further by extending this simplified model and formulating it as a discrete-time Markov chain that is called sandpile Markov chain, which is inspired by the literature on the Abelian sandpile model
[
22
,
3
,
7
,
23
]
; see also the examples given in (
3.4
,
3.4.2
,
3.4.3
).

The state space of this Markov chain is the set of static states
{\rm Stat}\ \mathcal{H}
.
Given the state
q_{n}
at time
n
, the next state of the Markov chain
q_{n+1}
is obtained by randomly adding some molecules
a_{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{N}
to the current state and letting it evolve until equilibrium, that is, letting all possible reactions occur until a static state is reached, which then corresponds to the new configuration
q_{n+1}
.

As stated in the Introduction, CRNs of interest in biochemistry and systems biology are embedded in complex networks where the outputs of submodules feed other local CRNs.
The operation of adding molecules at random to configurations is therefore very natural. The resulting Markov chains have recurrent states that will appear infinitely often along the orbits of the dynamics, while the other static states will not persist in the long run. Besides the relevance of recurrent static states in systems biology, one can also see their importance in looped and recurrent neural networks since, following
[
26
]
, one can translate ReLU neural networks as noncompetitive CRNs, see Section
3.3
. We will give more information on this Markov chain in Section
5.2
.

4.4.
Behavior of usual CTMC associated to noncompetitive execution bounded CRNs

Intuitively, starting from an initial configuration within a given discrete stoichiometric compatibility class, the CTMC chain will be absorbed into that class’s unique static state with probability one. We now formalize this.

Proposition 4.1
.

The usual CTMC associated with an execution bounded CRN
\mathcal{H}
is absorbed into the set of static states. These static states are the only recurrent states of the usual CTMC.

Proof.

Since the CRN
\mathcal{H}
is execution bounded, Theorem
3.2
guarantees the existence of a linear potential function
\Phi
. Let
\text{Stat }\mathcal{H}
be the set of static states of
\mathcal{H}
. We establish absorption of the usual CTMC into
\text{Stat }\mathcal{H}
by showing that
\Phi
satisfies a Foster–Lyapunov drift condition.

By Definition
3.5
:

\Phi(\nu_{r}^{\prime})-\Phi(\nu_{r})<0,\quad\forall r\in\mathcal{R}

Moreover, for any state

x\notin\text{Stat }\mathcal{H}
, there exists at least one reaction
r\in\mathcal{R}
such that
\lambda_{r}(x)>0
. Consequently, for all
x\notin\text{Stat }\mathcal{H}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\Phi(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\lambda_{r}(x)\left(\Phi(x+\nu_{r}^{\prime}-\nu_{r})-\Phi(x)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\lambda_{r}(x)\left(\Phi(\nu_{r}^{\prime})-\Phi(\nu_{r})\right)

\displaystyle<-\epsilon,

for some
\epsilon>0
, where
\mathcal{L}
is the related markovian generator.
Furthermore,
\Phi
is bounded and minimal on
\text{Stat }\mathcal{H}
.

Using the Foster-Lyapunov drift criterion
[
24
]
, this shows that the usual CTMC associated with an execution bounded CRN is absorbed into the set of static states.

Finally these static states are clearly recurrent, because their return probability equals
1
. Since the linear potential function stricly decreases along the trajectories, no other state of the usual CTMC is recurrent.

∎

According to Theorem
3.1
, there is a unique equilibrium per stoichiometric compatibility class. We deduce the following for the stationary distribution of the
usual CTMC
:

Proposition 4.2
.

In each discrete stoichiometric compatibility class, the stationary distribution of the usual CTMC is the Dirac measure concentrated on the unique static state of that class. The total stationary distribution (over the full state space) is a convex combination of these class-wise stationary distributions.

However we will show in the following that the sandpile Markov chain defined in Section (
4.3
), converges to a uniform distribution with support on a fraction of the static states only.

5.1.
Basic mathematical notions from AN theory

Let
G=(V,E)
be a directed graph. A network on
G
is a set of processors
(\mathcal{P}_{v})_{v\in V}
associated with each vertex
v
. Each processor
\mathcal{P}_{v}
contains an input alphabet
\mathcal{A}_{v}
and a state space
\mathcal{Q}_{v}
.

The time evolution of the processor states is governed by transition functions

T_{v}:\mathcal{A}_{v}\times\mathcal{Q}_{v}\rightarrow\mathcal{Q}_{v}
and message-passing functions
T_{(v,v^{\prime})}:\mathcal{A}_{v}\times\mathcal{Q}_{v}\rightarrow\mathcal{A}^{*}_{v^{\prime}}
, for each
(v,v^{\prime})\in E
, where
\mathcal{A}^{*}_{v^{\prime}}
denotes the free monoid of all finite words over the alphabet
\mathcal{A}_{v^{\prime}}
.
Suppose that processor
\mathcal{P}_{v}
receives an input
a\in\mathcal{A}_{v}
while in state
q\in\mathcal{Q}_{v}
. Then it updates its internal state
q

to the new state
T_{v}(a,q)\in\mathcal{Q}_{v}
and, for each edge
(v,v^{\prime})\in E
, sends
T_{(v,v^{\prime})}(a,q)\in\mathcal{A}_{v^{\prime}}
to its neighbouring processors
\mathcal{P}_{v^{\prime}}
.

A processor
\mathcal{P}_{v}
is called abelian if permuting the input letters does not change the resulting state of the processor, nor its outputs, up to permutation.
For a word
w\in\mathcal{A}^{*}_{v}
, we denote by
|w|\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathcal{A}_{v}}
the vector counting the number of occurrences of each letter of
\mathcal{A}_{v}
in
w
. Then the processor
\mathcal{P}_{v}
is abelian if for any words
w,w^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}_{v}^{*}
such that
\lvert w\rvert=\lvert w^{\prime}\rvert
, for all
q\in\mathcal{Q}_{v}
and any
(v,v^{\prime})\in E
, we have:

(5.1)

T_{v}(w,q)=T_{v}(w^{\prime},q)\quad\mbox{ and }\quad\lvert T_{(v,v^{\prime})}(w,q)\rvert=\lvert T_{(v,v^{\prime})}(w^{\prime},q)\rvert

Definition 5.1
.

[
5
]

An Abelian network (AN)
\mathcal{N}
on a graph
G=(V,E)
is a collection of abelian processors
(\mathcal{P}_{v})_{v\in V}
.
The total alphabet and state space are respectively
\mathcal{A}=\cup_{v\in V}\mathcal{A}_{v}
and
\mathcal{Q}=\prod_{v\in V}\mathcal{Q}_{v}
.
The
state
of an Abelian network
\mathcal{N}
is denoted
x.q
, where
q\in\mathcal{Q}
describes the internal state and
x\in\mathbb{N}^{\mathcal{A}}
is a vector counting the number of letters waiting to be processed.

For
v\in V
and
a\in\mathcal{A}_{v}
, consider the map
t^{v}_{a}:\mathcal{Q}\to\mathcal{Q}
:

t^{v}_{a}(q)_{u}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}T_{v}(a,q_{v})&\mbox{if }u=v,\\
q_{u}&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Following
[
6
]
, one can then define mappings
\pi_{a}
,
a\in\mathcal{A}
, that acts on states in three steps : it modifies the internal state
q
to
t^{v}_{a}q

where
v
is the unique node such that
a\in\mathcal{A}_{v}
, decrement
x_{a}
by one and increment each
x_{b}
by the number of
b
letters passed by

T_{(v,v^{\prime})}
with
b\in\mathcal{A}_{v^{\prime}}
.

Definition 5.2
.

An execution is a word
w=a_{1}\ldots a_{r}\in\mathcal{A}^{*}
detailing the order in which the letters are processed.
Let
\pi_{w}(x.q)
be the result of executing
w
starting from
x.q
, with

\pi_{w}=\pi_{a_{1}}\circ\ldots\circ\pi_{a_{r}}
.
The execution is legal if, at each step, the letter to be processed is available, that is, its count is strictly positive at that step. The execution is complete if it removes all letters from the network.

Abelian networks are a generalization of the Abelian sandpile model introduced in
[
5
]
. Further articles on the topic derived general results for Abelian networks, including conditions under which the networks halts for all inputs
[
6
]
, a characterization of the AN critical group that acts on the set of recurrent states, and a burning algorithm to find these states
[
7
]

Example 5.1

Consider the ASM of Section

.

Lemma 6.2
.

For generalized toppling networks, the three following assertions are equivalent:

(1)

The CRN
\mathcal{H}=(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{C},\mathcal{R})
is execution bounded.

(2)

The corresponding Abelian network
\mathcal{N}
halts on all inputs.

(3)

The modulus of the eigenvalues of the associated production matrix
P
are strictly smaller than one.

Proof.

•

(2)\Leftrightarrow(3)
follows from Theorem 5.6 of
[
6
]
.

•

(1)\Rightarrow(3)
: Let
P
be the production matrix of
\mathcal{N}
and
\lambda
its Perron-Frobenius eigenvalue and suppose, for contradiction, that
\lambda\geq 1
. We follow the proof of Theorem 5.6 in
[
6
]
. There exists a vector
y\in\mathbb{Q}^{\mathcal{A}}
such that
x\leq y\leq\lambda x
, and then
Py\geq Px=\lambda x\geq y
, so that
Pny\geq Pnx=\lambda nx\geq ny
for
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
By Lemma 4.5 of
[
6
]
, we can find
n\geq 1
with
z=ny\in K
, so that
Pz
is the state reached by the CRN starting from state
z
, and allowing all possible reactions to occur. If
Pz\geq z
, then for all linear functions with non-negative coefficients
\Phi
, we have
\Phi(Pz)\geq\Phi(z)
. Hence no linear potential function can strictly decrease along all reactions, contradicting execution boundedness. Therefore,
\lambda<1
.

•

(1)\Leftarrow(3)
: Following the proof of Theorem 6.5 of
[
14
]
,it suffices to prove that there is no integer vector
u\geq 0
such that
\Gamma u\geq 0
. Once this is proven, Corollary 6.4 ensures the existence of a linear potential function.
By contradiction, suppose there exists an integer vector
u\geq 0
such that
\Gamma u\geq 0
. We can interpret
u
as the number of times each reaction must fire so that, after these firings, the number of molecules of each species increases. To this vector
u
, we associate a vector
z
, representing the total number of molecules of each species required to carry out the reactions specified by
u
. Clearly,

and since the net change in molecular counts is given by
\Gamma u
and
\Gamma u\geq 0
, the output is at least the input. In particular, this implies that

, and consequently, we deduce that

.

∎

Proposition 6.3
.

The production matrix of a generalized toppling network
\mathcal{N}
doesn’t depend on the choice of the recurrent state

and is given by :

(6.7)

P_{ij}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma^{p}_{ij}}{\Gamma^{r}_{jj}}&\text{if }\Gamma^{p}_{ij}>0,\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases},\quad\forall i,j\in\{1,...,d\}.

Proof.

Let

, and

be the associated unit vector. Then

and for any fixed recurrent state

6.1.1.
The dissipative case

We say that the CRN is
dissipative
when

\sum\limits_{i}\Gamma^{p}_{ij}\leq\Gamma_{jj}^{r}
, for all
j\in\{1,...,d\}
with a strict inequality for at least some
j
.
In this case,

\sum_{i}P_{ij}=\sum_{i}\frac{\Gamma^{p}_{ij}}{\Gamma^{r}_{jj}}=\frac{1}{\Gamma^{r}_{jj}}\sum_{i}\Gamma^{p}_{ij}\leq 1,

so that the matrix
P^{T}
is sub-stochastic. Hence, the spectral radius of
P
is smaller than 1, and strictly smaller than 1 when
\sum\limits_{i}\Gamma^{p}_{ij}<\Gamma_{jj}^{r}
for some
j\in\{1,...,d\}
.
It follows from Lemma
6.2
that the CRN is execution-bounded.

Consider the enlarged state space

\mathcal{W}=\mathcal{S}\cup\{\emptyset\}
, and the
(d+1)\times(d+1)
matrix
S
with coefficients:

(6.8)

S_{ji}=\begin{cases}(P^{T})_{ij}&\text{if }i,j\in\{1,...,d\},\\
1-\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}S_{ji}&\text{if }j\in\{1,...,d\}\text{ and }i=\emptyset,\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

From construction, the restriction of
S
on
\mathcal{S}
is just
P^{T}
.
The matrix
S
is thus stochastic and define a Markov chain
(Y_{n})_{n\geq 0}
of state space

\mathcal{W}
, and absorbing state
\emptyset
. The related fundamental matrix
F
with indexes from
\mathcal{S}

which describes non-absorption in
\{\emptyset\}
is
F=(1-P^{T})^{-1}
, with

F=\sum_{k\geq 0}(P^{T})^{k},

so that, for
j\in\mathcal{S}
,
j\neq i
,

\displaystyle F_{ji}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 1}(P^{T})^{k}_{ji}=\sum_{k\geq 1}S^{k}_{ji}=\sum_{k\geq 1}\mathbb{P}(Y_{l}\neq\emptyset,\ l\leq k;\ Y_{k}=i|Y_{0}=j)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 1}\mathbb{P}(\tau>k;\ Y_{k}=i|Y_{0}=j),

where
\tau=\min\{n\geq 1;\ Y_{n}=\emptyset\}
is the absorption time.

Recall that the odometer
[x.q]
is

|w|
for a complete and legal execution
w
of
x.q
, that is, it gives the number of letters of each type processed during the execution
of
w
. The odomoter
[x.q]
can thus be interpreted as the vector giving, for each molecule type, the number of reactions the CRN takes to move
x.q
to
0.q^{\prime}
where
q^{\prime}
is the final static state.
Theorem
9.9

gives that the mean value of the odometer with respect to uniform random elements
q
from
Rec\ \mathcal{N}
is:

\mathbb{E}[x.q]^{T}=x^{T}(I-P^{T})^{-1}=x^{T}F.

Hence, the mean value of the odometer is related to the behavior of this Markov chain conditioned to non-absorption.

Set
||x||=\sum\limits_{i}x_{i}
, and consider the probability measure
\nu_{x}
on
\mathcal{S}
defined by
\nu_{x}(i)=x_{i}/||x||
.
Standard Markov chain theory
[
20
]
shows then that the average total number of processed letters (molecules) is:

(6.9)

\sum_{i}\mathbb{E}[x.q]^{T}_{i}=||x||\ \nu_{x}F(1,\ldots,1)^{T}=||x||\ \mathbb{E}_{\nu_{x}}(\tau),

where
\nu_{x}
is considered as a line probability vector.
Hence, the total number of iterations to move the random state
x.q
for a uniform
q
of

Rec\ \mathcal{N}
toward the state

is the total number of letters to be processed
||x||

multiplied by the mean absorption time
\mathbb{E}_{\nu_{x}}(\tau)
, when
Y_{0}
is distributed according to the probability measure
\nu_{x}
.

Consider the sandpile Markov chain defined in Section
5.2
, of transition mechanism:

q_{n+1}=1_{a_{n}}\triangleright\triangleright q_{n}=\tau_{a_{n}}(q_{n}).

Theorem
9.6
recalls that the associated stationary measure is the uniform distribution of
\text{Rec }\mathcal{N}
. Formula (
6.9
) shows that
the typical number of reactions involved in a transition

of the stationary chain is given by
\mathbb{E}_{\delta_{a_{n}}}(\tau)
, where

\delta_{a_{n}}
is the point measure centered at

.

6.2.
A special case of multi-species source complexes

As discussed earlier, a general problem with multi-species source complexes, is the accumulation of molecules of some species. This can lead to an infinite state space, and the previous theory of finite Abelian networks doesn’t apply anymore.

In this subsection, we avoid this problem by considering only complexes of type
2
(see Definition
3.6
), and a slightly modified Markov chain, called the batch version of the sandpile Markov chain, see (
6.11
). For this Markov chain to solve the problem of infinite state space, we need all complexes to have the same number of molecules of each source species i.e. for all
k,k^{\prime}\in\{1,...,d\}
,
\nu_{k}=\beta_{k}e_{U_{k}}
and
\nu^{\prime}_{k^{\prime}}=\beta^{\prime}_{k^{\prime}}e_{U_{k^{\prime}}}
for some choice of
\beta_{k}
and
\beta^{\prime}_{k^{\prime}}
. Any reaction is thus of the form:

(6.10)

\beta_{k}e_{U_{k}}\to\beta^{\prime}_{k^{\prime}}e_{U_{k^{\prime}}}

As stated before there is a single reaction having
e_{U_{k}}
as a source complex.

6.2.1.
Batch version of the sandpile Markov chain

One natural objective is to visit the static space as proposed in

[
11
]
and
[
26
]
using a reasonable stochastic process. This can be obtained by using the following
transition mechanism:

(6.11)

q_{n+1}=1_{U_{i_{n}}}\triangleright\triangleright q_{n},

where the sequence
U_{i_{n}}
is i.i.d. in
\{U_{1},...,U_{p}\}.
Set for convenience (see (
5.4
)):

(6.12)

\tau_{U}(q)=1_{U}\triangleright\triangleright q.

Lemma 2.2 of
[
7
]
shows that for all

:

\tau_{U_{k}}=\prod_{j\in U_{k}}\tau_{j}.

We associate to each source complex a processor
\mathcal{P}_{U_{k}}
. The alphabet of this processor is
\mathcal{A}_{k}=\{U_{k}\}
.
The state space of the processor depends on the initial state
\eta\in\mathbb{N}^{N}
, we indeed have that:

\mathcal{Q}^{k}(\eta)=\{\eta-\chi_{k}e_{U_{k}},...,\eta-e_{U_{k}},\eta,\eta+e_{U_{k}},...,\eta+\epsilon_{k}e_{U_{k}}\}

where

Note that two different initial conditions lead to the same state space if and only if they are in the same discrete stoichiometric compatibility class.

For each

\texttt{\#}U_{k}\times(\beta_{k}-1)

(6.13)

\prod\limits_{{k=1,\cdots,p}}\texttt{\#}U_{k}\times(\beta_{k}-1)

6.6

Proposition 6.4
.

Given an initial condition, the collection of processors
\mathcal{N}=(\mathcal{P}_{U_{k}{}})_{k=1,\cdots,p}

{\mathcal{G}}=({\mathcal{C}},{\mathcal{E}})

8.
Discussion

We have established that rate-independent noncompetitive CRNs are special instances of ANs. This connection brings to light several structural and dynamical similarities between these two frameworks, and allows the use of powerful algebraic tools developed for finite ANs—such as critical groups, production matrices, and burning algorithms— for studying execution-bounded noncompetitive CRNs.
However, this theory only applies when the state space of the CRN is finite, which is no longer the case with multi-species source complexes, since nothing prevents the Markov chain from accumulating arbitrarily large quantities of certain molecular species. This phenomenon of unbounded accumulation also appears in non-unary ANs such as, e.g., in the oil-water model considered in
[
10
]
,
which is concerned with
scaling-limit and aggregation behaviors rather than algebraic invariants, and thus addresses a substantially different set of questions than those considered here. Developing these extensions represents an exciting direction for future research.
