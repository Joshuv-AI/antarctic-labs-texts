Experimental study on the mechanical properties of fly ash stabilized bentonite via sodium hydroxide-geopolymerization process
Authors: Tengshen Jing, Shengyang Yuan, Xianfeng Liu, Yulin Liu, Haibin Xu, Weixing Zhou, Gaofeng Pan, Guanlu Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExpansive clay is problematic for transportation infrastructures due to its vulnerability to water induced decrease in strength. Building on existing geopolymer stabilization techniques, this study focuses on optimizing the mix design for expansive soils and elucidating the key physicochemical mechanisms involved in the geopolymerization process to enhance the stabilization performance. To do so, a series of uniaxial compression tests was conducted on a bentonite treated using combination of fly ash and sodium hydroxide (NaOH) while considering different fly ash content, NaOH concentration and initial moisture content. The physical-chemical mechanism of geopolymerization process was investigated using a combination of X-ray diffraction (XRD) and fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis as well as scanning electron microscope (SEM) observation. An emphasis was made on the effect of NaOH concentration. Experimental results show that NaOH concentration has the most significant impact on the strength of treated bentonite, which is followed by moisture content and fly ash content. With increasing the NaOH concentration, an optimum NaOH concentration exhibits and corresponds to the maximum unconfined compression strength (UCS) of the treated bentonite. This behaviour can be explained by a different competition of cation exchange reaction and the formation of geopolymer gel. This means that the formation of geopolymer gel may be hindered in high NaOH concentration.Keywords: Geopolymer, Expansive clay, Stabilization, Mechanical properties, MicrostructureSubject terms: Chemistry, Engineering, Environmental sciences, Materials science

Introduction

Expansive soils are clays exhibiting pronounced swelling–shrinkage behavior induced by moisture variations, which leads to severe strength degradation upon wetting and frequently causes subgrade cracking, slope instability, and embankment settlement. These problems pose persistent challenges to the safety and serviceability of transportation infrastructures in expansive soil regions1,2.

Existing treatment methods for expansive soils generally include pile reinforcement, soil replacement, and soil improvement. Pile reinforcement mitigates structural damage without fundamentally eliminating soil expansiveness, limiting its effectiveness in strongly expansive soils3. Soil replacement can address expansiveness at its source but is often impractical for large-scale projects due to high cost and construction constraints4. Consequently, soil improvement methods are most commonly adopted in engineering practice.

Soil improvement techniques can be categorized as physical, chemical, and biological methods5–8. Physical improvement modifies particle-size distribution through the addition of sand, silt, or gravel9–11, but its effectiveness is often limited in the long term, especially for deep or highly expansive soils. Biological improvement methods alter soil structure through microbial activity and are environmentally friendly; however, their performance is strongly influenced by environmental conditions and may not satisfy engineering strength requirements12–14. Chemical improvement, involving stabilizers such as lime, cement, or fly ash15–17, remains the most effective approach for reducing swelling potential and enhancing strength. Nevertheless, conventional chemical stabilizers, particularly cement, are associated with durability issues (e.g., cracking) and substantial energy consumption and carbon emissions18,19, motivating the development of alternative, sustainable stabilizing materials.

Geopolymers, first proposed by Davidovits20, are alkali-activated aluminosilicate materials synthesized from natural minerals or industrial by-products. By forming stable aluminosilicate networks through reactions with soil minerals, geopolymers have demonstrated superior strength, durability, and environmental performance compared with cement-based stabilization21. Their effectiveness has been reported for soft soils22,23, saline soils24,25, and clays26,27. However, studies on geopolymer stabilization of expansive soils remain limited, and the governing mechanisms have not yet been fully elucidated.

Fly ash is widely used as a geopolymer precursor due to its high Si–Al content and availability21. Although the presence of trace heavy metals may raise environmental concerns, these risks can be mitigated through water or acid washing treatments28,29. Owing to its low early reactivity, fly ash typically requires strong alkaline activation, most commonly using NaOH solutions30. Previous studies have demonstrated that alkaline activator concentration critically affects geopolymer formation and mechanical performance. For example, increasing NaOH concentration within an appropriate range enhances aluminosilicate dissolution and polymerization, whereas excessive alkalinity may lead to premature precipitation of reaction products and strength reduction31–33. Despite these findings, the influence of alkaline activator concentration on the mechanical behavior and microstructural evolution of geopolymer-treated expansive soils has not been systematically clarified.

Objectives of this study are: (1) to investigate the effects of NaOH concentration, fly ash content, and curing age on the mechanical properties of geopolymer-modified bentonite; (2) to quantify the contribution of key factors using the Taguchi orthogonal experimental method; and (3) to elucidate the modification mechanisms through X-ray diffraction (XRD), Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR), and scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The results are expected to provide technical guidance for the design and optimization of geopolymer-based stabilization of expansive soils.

Materials and testing program

Materials

A commercial bentonite was used in this study, with liquid limit of 136.33% and plastic limit of 52.15%. The specific gravity of the bentonite is 2.50. The free swelling rate of the bentonite is 102.5%. The particle size distribution of the bentonite is given in Fig. 1a, which shows that it mainly includes silty and clayey particles. According to the X-ray diffraction (XRD) results of the Bentonite as shown in Fig. 1b, the mainly clay mineral of the bentonite is Ca-montmorillonite.

A commercial fly ash was used as the aluminosilicate precursor for alkali activation. Based on the X-ray diffraction (XRD) results (Fig. 2), the fly ash mainly contains SiO₂, Al₂O₃, and CaO. The oxide composition is approximately SiO₂ (45.1%), Al₂O₃ (24.2%), and CaO (5.6%), obtained from semi-quantitative phase analysis of the XRD results. Owing to its relatively low CaO content and aluminosilicate-dominated composition, the material can be classified as Class F fly ash according to ASTM C618-2234. SiO₂ and Al₂O₃ are identified as the primary source materials involved in forming the aluminosilicate geopolymer network, while CaO is described as contributing to the formation of additional calcium–silicate–hydrate (C–S–H) or calcium–aluminosilicate–hydrate (C–A–S–H) gels, thereby promoting early strength development. The liquid limit and plastic limit of the fly ash are 31.77% and 18.48% respectively, with specific gravity of 2.30. Sodium hydroxide is used as the alkaline activator, and the purity of the used NaOH is larger than 98.5%.

Testing programs

The strength of geopolymer-treated soil is governed by multiple factors, including geopolymer composition (e.g., fly ash content and NaOH concentration), initial water content, and curing time. However, the quantitative degree to which these factors influence the strength of modified bentonite remains unclear.

To address this issue, Taguchi orthogonal experiments were designed to systematically evaluate the contribution of each factor to the strength of geopolymer-treated soil. The Taguchi method has been widely applied in geopolymer research for mixture optimization and performance evaluation, particularly in concrete systems35,36. In this study, this approach is extended to geopolymer-treated expansive soils, where the interactions among soil structure, moisture conditions, and alkali activation are more complex.

Based on the results of the Taguchi orthogonal experiments, the effects of the major factors on the strength of both geopolymer and geopolymer-treated soil were analyzed. Furthermore, the corresponding modification mechanisms were investigated through a series of microstructural tests. The Taguchi method provides an efficient statistical framework for evaluating factor effects through the signal-to-noise (S/N) ratio and contribution analysis, allowing quantitative assessment of the relative importance of each parameter.

For the Taguchi orthogonal tests: fly ash is the precursor of geopolymers and the raw material for the geopolymerization reaction37,38. The amount of fly ash in the system determines whether the reaction can occur or not, so the fly ash content (FA) was set as the first factor, with three levels: 9%, 6%, and 3%. As fly ash has low activity, an alkaline activator is needed to fully activate it, and the concentration of the alkaline activator directly affects the degree of activation. Therefore, the NaOH concentration (c) was set as the second factor, with three levels: 6 mol/L, 4 mol/L, and 2 mol/L. Finally, the water content of soil samples significantly affects their strength, especially for high plasticity clays such as montmorillonite. Thus, the water content (w) was set as the third factor, with three levels: 44%, 35%, and 25%. Water content 44% was used as it was the saturated water content for void ratio of 1.1.

Preliminary tests showed that samples with less than 25% moisture content were difficult to shape, so the lowest water content was selected as 25%. Instead, the aging time (1d, 3d, 7d, 14d, 28d) was set in each experiment group. Specimen preparation

The specimen preparation process in this study was as follows: specific amounts of fly ash, dry soil, solid NaOH particles, and water was weighted first. Then, the fly ash was mixed with the dry soil. After this, the prepared NaOH solution was mixed with the mixture powdered soil via a small mixer. The wetted soil powder was stirring twice for 2 minuter each at with a mixing rate of 0.1 mm/min to ensure the soil powder were thoroughly mixed.

Cylindrical specimens (38 mm × 76 mm) were statically compacted in a stainless-steel mold using a laboratory compaction apparatus (Fig. 3a). Each specimen was prepared in five equal layers. For each layer, a predetermined mass of material was placed and compacted under controlled displacement using a rigid loading cap to achieve the target specimen height and void ratio (e = 1.1). To ensure interlayer uniformity, the surface of each compacted layer was lightly scarified before placing the subsequent layer, thereby improving interlayer bonding and minimizing layering effects.

Test equipments and tested sample. (a) Apparatus for specimen preparation. (b) Testing apparatus.

The compacted specimens were wrapped with cling film and vacuum sealed. They were cured in an environment with a temperature of 22 ± 2℃ and humidity of 70 ± 2% until the designated aging period. During the unconfined compressive strength test, a loading rate of 0.1 mm/min (0.12% strain per minute) was used. Three parallel specimens were prepared for each test condition to ensure repeatability. The results show good consistency among replicates, with variations in peak strength generally within approximately 5%. Minor differences, particularly in post-peak behavior, are attributed to the inherent heterogeneity of geopolymer-treated soils and local variations in the geopolymerization process. The setup for unconfined compression test is shown in Fig. 3b.

To study the effect of each factor on unconfined compression strength, a response index was introduced for analysis. The response index refers to the average unconfined compressive strength at the same level for each factor. For example, for the fly ash content factor, “A1” is the average strength of the test groups with 9% fly ash content, i.e., the average of groups 1 to 3. Thus, the response index for each curing age can be obtained, as shown in Fig. 4.

Relationship between unconfined compression strength with the level of different factors.

For fly ash content, with curing age of 1 day, the strength relationship was A3 > A2> A1, indicating that the sample strength decreased with increasing fly ash content. This is due to that fly ash is non-cohesive, similar to sand mixed with bentonite clay, which reduces the cohesion and strength of modified bentonite39. Figure 4 also shows that the increment of strength from A1 to A2 was greater than that from A2 to A3, which is might due to the effect of fly ash content on treated soil strength decreases as fly ash content below 6%. For NaOH concentration, the strength relationship was B3 > B2 > B1, indicating that within the concentration range of 2–6 mol/L, the sample strength decreased with increasing NaOH concentration. It should be noted that the selected concentration levels in the Taguchi design fall within a range where strength has already entered a post-peak (decreasing) stage. Therefore, the identified “optimal” level reflects a relative comparison within this limited range rather than the true global optimum. For water content, the strength relationship was C2 > C3> C1, with strength initially increasing and then decreasing as water content increased. It indicates that the geopolymerization of montmorillonite-treated soil is closely related to the specimen water content.

With increased curing age, the strength of modified bentonite under all conditions improved. For fly ash content, the strength relationship at 7 days of age remained consistent with that at 1 day. But as the curing age increased to 28 days, the strength of A3 became similar to A2. This phenomenon might be attributed to the initial retardation of strength development by higher fly ash content, which was subsequently compensated by either complete reaction or enhanced water absorption of the fly ash, thereby increasing its pozzolanic activity and resulting in comparable strength to A2 at 28 days. For NaOH concentration, the strength relationship remained consistent across all curing ages. For water content, the strength relationship at 28 days remained consistent with that at 1 day, but at 7 days, C3 strength slightly exceeded C2. At 7 days, the extra water in C3 might have enhanced the hydration or bonding reactions to a degree that temporarily boosted strength. However, at 28 days, the extra water may have led to a reduction in strength due to excessive dilution or poor consolidation, which caused the strength trend to revert to C2.

The effect of NaOH concentration on the unconfined compressive peak strength of geopolymers is shown in Fig. 7. The strength of fly ash samples without alkaline activation (corresponding to NaOH concentration equal to 0) at different water contents is consistent at approximately 0.33 MPa. As the NaOH concentration increases, the unconfined compressive strength first rises to a peak and then begins to decline. In the low concentration range (from 0 to 6 mol/L), the strength of geopolymer significantly increases with concentration. In the medium concentration range (from 6 to 10 mol/L), the strength reaches its peak, representing the optimal concentration range. In the high concentration range (> 10 mol/L), the strength gradually decreases. This indicates that NaOH can effectively enhance the strength of geopolymer within a certain concentration range, but excessive concentration may lead to strength reduction. This phenomenon occurs because of that, in the lower concentration range, the increase in NaOH concentration helps activate the geopolymer material, making the chemical reaction more complete and generating more strength-contributing products (such as C-S-H gel or N-A-S-H gel)40.However, at very high NaOH concentrations, excessive alkalinity leads to structural degradation41, as reflected by the reduction in characteristic crystalline peak intensities in XRD and the attenuation of Si–O–Si and Al–O bands in FTIR (see Sect. 3.2). These semi-quantitative indicators suggest enhanced dissolution and destabilization of the aluminosilicate framework, thereby reducing the unconfined compressive strength.

For all water content conditions, the optimal NaOH concentration range is from 6 to 10 mol/L, where the unconfined compression strength of the geopolymer samples were significantly enhanced. As the moisture content increases from 20% to 30%, the peak unconfined compressive strength gradually decreases. with 4.0 MPa at 20% water content, and which decreases to about 2.0 MPa at 30% water content. This may be due to that high moisture content increases porosity after solidification42, with more voids in the specimen leading to the reduction in compressive strength. Excessive water may prevent some raw material particles from fully participating in the reaction, further weakening the mechanical properties43.

NaOH concentration effect on the modified bentonite clay

Figure 8 present the relationship between axial strain and compression stress for geopolymer treated bentonite with different NaOH concentration and initial water contents. For low water content, w = 25% and 30%, the inclusion of geopolymers significantly enhances soil strength. A typical brittle behaviour can be observed for the modified bentonite. The compression strength increases to a maximum value as NaOH concentration increase to 2 mol/L, then it decreases with the further increase of NaOH concentration. The corresponding peak strain gradually decreases with the NaOH concentration. For samples with water content of 25%, the peak strain is concentrated around 1%, while it slightly increases to 1%~1.5% for samples with water content of 30%, which indicates a stronger plastic deformation capacity. With the further increasing of water content, up to 44% (saturated sample), the improvement in soil strength due to geopolymers is less pronounced compared to lower moisture content, especially when the NaOH concentration is below 1.00 mol/L. In this range, a strain hardening performance is observed for the modified bentonite which is similar to the performance of untreated bentonite. With the increasing of NaOH concentration, the strain hardening performance gradually transfers into strain softening performance, as the NaOH concentrations is larger than 2.75 mol/L. However, when the NaOH concentration is excessively high, 6.00 mol/L, the compression strength reduces sharply to level close to low NaOH concentration.

Overall, geopolymers can enhance soil strength under various water content conditions. But, as water content increase, the effect of NaOH concentration on the compression strength of modified bentonite is delayed, requiring higher concentrations to produce noticeable effects.

The evolution of unconfined compressive peak strength of geopolymer-treated bentonite with NaOH concentration is shown in Fig. 9. In contrast to the monotonic trend observed within the limited Taguchi range, the strength exhibits a nonlinear behavior, characterized by an initial increase followed by a decrease.

In the very low NaOH concentration range (0 ~ 0.5 mol/L), the unconfined compressive peak strength is lower than that of untreated montmorillonite. This is due to the low degree of alkaline activation of fly ash, resulting in insufficient cementitious material formation, and fly ash itself lacks cohesion with a micro-spherical structure, reducing soil cohesion and thereby its macroscopic strength. As NaOH concentration increases to range between 0.5 and 2 mol/L, fly ash can be rapidly activated, and forms cementitious materials that fill the soil pores and bond soil particles. So, the strength of modified bentonite enhanced significantly. However, with further concentration increase (> 2 mol/L), strength begins to decline. It is possibly due to excess NaOH reacting with cations in the clay, which disrupts the soil structure and inhibits gel phase growth, thus reducing soil strength44.

Therefore, the apparent discrepancy between the Taguchi analysis and the detailed experimental results can be attributed to the different concentration ranges considered. The Taguchi design captures the relative trend within a decreasing regime (2–6 mol/L), whereas the detailed experiments reveal the full strength evolution and identify the true optimal NaOH concentration at approximately 2 mol/L.

From an engineering perspective, the obtained unconfined compressive strength values indicate that the geopolymer-treated bentonite has practical potential for expansive soil stabilization. Under favorable conditions, the peak strength reached approximately 1.0–1.8 MPa, which is within or above the typical strength range commonly reported for stabilized expansive soils used in subgrade and foundation improvement. This suggests that the proposed treatment method can provide not only relative strength enhancement, but also strength levels relevant to practical geotechnical applications.

However, the evolution of unconfined compression strength of geopolymer with NaOH concentration is different from that of geopolymer modified bentonite. It might be due to that, for the geopolymer samples, the NaOH concentration range is 6 to 14 mol/L, with an NaOH to fly ash solid mass ratio between 6.0 and 16.8%. But, in the Taguchi orthogonal test, for the geopolymer treated bentonite clay, with NaOH concentrations of 2, 4, and 6 mol/L, the NaOH to fly ash solid mass ratio ranges from 24.22% to 241.71%. The NaOH concentrations all exceed the sample strength inflection point and are in the strength reduction phase. As shown in Fig. 10, at the same NaOH concentration (e.g., 6 mol/L), the NaOH to fly ash solid mass ratio in modified bentonite is much higher than that in geopolymer, which leads to excess NaOH in the modified soil and consequently reduced soil strength.

Modification mechanism of geopolymer for bentonite clay

X-ray diffraction (XRD) spectrum analysis

To investigate the effect of geopolymer incorporation on the mineralogical evolution of bentonite, XRD analyses were conducted on untreated and geopolymer-treated samples. Prior to testing, soil specimens were oven-dried at 105 °C, finely ground to a smooth powder, and evenly spread on sample holders with the surface leveled. XRD measurements were performed using CuKα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å), operated at 45 kV and 40 mA. Scans were collected over a 2θ range of 5–80° with a step size of 0.02° and a scanning rate of 3°/min. The samples used for XRD were prepared at target molding water contents of w = 25% and 44% and subsequently oven-dried at 105 °C to eliminate the influence of free water. The results are shown in Fig. 11.

XRD results of geopolymer treated bentonite under different NaOH concentrations and water content.

Compared with untreated bentonite, the basal reflection of Ca-montmorillonite (d ≈ 15 Å) at approximately 5° remained identifiable at an NaOH concentration of 0.50 mol/L, with no apparent peak shift. This indicates that interlayer cation exchange and structural transformation were still limited at low alkalinity. Nevertheless, the reduced peak intensity suggests that alkaline attack occurred preferentially at the layer edges, inducing partial dissolution of the montmorillonite structure before gel formation45. In addition, no significant change was observed in the 26–28° range, indicating that N–A–S–H gel had not yet formed46. This explains the slight reduction in unconfined compressive strength observed in Fig. 9, as early alkaline corrosion weakened the clay structure in the absence of effective gel bonding.

When the NaOH concentration increased to 2.00 mol/L, the Ca-montmorillonite peak at ~ 5° disappeared and a new basal reflection appeared at ~ 7°, corresponding to Na-montmorillonite (d ≈ 12 Å), indicating pronounced Ca2+–Na+ exchange. Concurrently, diffraction features in the 26–28° region became broadened and diffuse, reflecting the loss of crystalline order and the formation of amorphous N–A–S–H gel47.

At an NaOH concentration of 6.00 mol/L, the Na-montmorillonite peak at ~ 7° weakened markedly, suggesting structural degradation under highly alkaline conditions. Meanwhile, the diffraction intensities of crystalline phases such as quartz, cristobalite, phillipsite, albite, and calcite were further reduced, indicating extensive mineral dissolution47,48. However, excessive alkalinity did not promote additional N–A–S–H gel formation and instead caused partial destruction of the previously formed gel network.

Overall, these mineralogical evolutions explain the “suppression–enhancement–suppression” trend in unconfined compressive strength with increasing NaOH concentration (Fig. 9), with the optimum performance observed at 2.00 mol/L. Moderate alkalinity promotes cation exchange and N–A–S–H gel formation, whereas excessive NaOH leads to mineral dissolution and gel destabilization, ultimately reducing strength. The reduction in diffraction peak intensity at higher NaOH concentrations further indicates increased mineral dissolution and structural degradation. Variations in XRD peak intensity are interpreted qualitatively and comparatively, as all samples were tested under identical preparation and measurement conditions.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis

Oven-dried soil samples (105 °C) were finely ground, mixed with dried KBr, and pressed into pellets with a diameter of 13 mm and a thickness of approximately 1 mm. FTIR spectra were obtained using a Fourier transform infrared spectrometer in transmission mode over a wavenumber range of 400–4000 cm− 1 with a spectral resolution of 0.005 cm− 1, as shown in Fig. 12.

The FTIR results of geopolymer treated bentonite with different NaOH concentrations with the fly ash content of 9% and initial water content of 44%.

FTIR analysis was conducted to elucidate the chemical mechanisms governing the strength evolution of geopolymer-treated bentonite. The attenuation of the Si–O–Si and O–Si–O bending bands below 500 cm− 1 with increasing NaOH concentration indicates progressive depolymerization of aluminosilicate structures and enhanced release of reactive silicate species from fly ash, which are essential for geopolymer gel formation49,50.

The gradual weakening and eventual disappearance of the peaks at 517 cm− 1 (Si–O–Mg bending) and 915 cm− 1 (Al–O–H bending) further confirm disruption of the Al–O octahedral framework and dissolution of Mg, Al, and Si51. In addition, the reduced intensities of the Si–O stretching bands at 795 and 1035 cm− 1, together with the attenuation and slight leftward shift of the characteristic aluminosilicate glass band (from approximately 1092 to 1089 cm⁻¹), suggests depolymerization of the fly ash glass phase and subsequent formation of N-A-S-H gel52–54. It should be noted that the magnitude of this shift is close to the instrumental resolution; therefore, it is interpreted cautiously and considered in conjunction with consistent spectral trends and complementary XRD and SEM evidence, rather than as a standalone indicator. These FTIR observations are consistent with XRD results, which indicate progressive consumption of amorphous aluminosilicate phases.

From a mechanical perspective, the degree of aluminosilicate depolymerization provides a direct explanation for the unconfined compressive strength evolution shown in Fig. 9. At a moderate NaOH concentration of 2.00 mol/L, partial depolymerization facilitates the formation of a continuous N-A-S-H gel that effectively bonds soil particles and fills interparticle voids, thereby enhancing stress transfer while preserving the integrity of the soil skeleton55. This balanced microstructural modification results in peak strength across different initial water contents.

In contrast, excessive alkalinity (6.00 mol/L) causes severe breakdown of the silicate network, as evidenced by the near disappearance of Si–O–Si− and Al–O–H−related bands. The progressive attenuation of these bands with increasing NaOH concentration provides semi-quantitative evidence of aluminosilicate framework degradation at high alkalinity.Such over-activation compromises the continuity of the load-bearing framework, producing a chemically reactive but mechanically inefficient matrix and consequently limiting strength development. Overall, these results demonstrate that the mechanical performance of geopolymer-treated bentonite is governed by the balance between geopolymer gel formation and soil skeleton preservation, rather than by the intensity of chemical activation alone.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was employed to investigate the microstructural evolution of geopolymer-treated bentonite under different NaOH concentrations and initial water contents (w = 25% and 44%). Approximately 0.5 cm³ of each specimen was rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen (− 196 °C) for about 5 min and subsequently freeze-dried under vacuum for 48 h to remove pore water while preserving the internal structure. Rapid freezing was adopted to minimize ice crystal growth and reduce potential microstructural disturbance. The consistency of observed features across specimens and their agreement with XRD and FTIR results indicate that freeze-drying–induced artifacts are negligible. Critical point drying was not employed, as the required solvent exchange process may alter the chemical state of geopolymer gels and clay minerals. Prior to SEM observation, the dried samples were sputter-coated with a ~ 10 nm thick gold layer to improve surface conductivity.

SEM observations were conducted using a tungsten-filament scanning electron microscope (JEOL JSM-IT500, Japan) at an accelerating voltage of 15.0 kV. All micrographs were captured at a magnification of ×2000, corresponding to a scale bar of 10 μm.

At w = 25%, the untreated bentonite (Fig. 13a) shows densely packed clay aggregates formed mainly through mechanical compaction, which provides limited compressive resistance. At a low NaOH concentration (c = 0.50 mol/L; Fig. 13b), partial dissolution of the fly ash glassy phase occurs, generating a small amount of geopolymer gel that locally adheres to bentonite particles. However, the reaction degree remains insufficient to form a continuous binding network. Dispersed unreacted fly ash particles with smooth surfaces are still evident, weakening interparticle friction and load transfer, which explains the reduced unconfined compressive strength compared with untreated bentonite at low alkalinity (Fig. 9).

When the NaOH concentration increases to c = 2.00 mol/L (Fig. 13c), enhanced dissolution of aluminosilicate species promotes extensive geopolymerization. Abundant cementitious gels bridge adjacent particles and consolidate small clusters into dense agglomerates, forming a continuous load-bearing skeleton. This compact microstructure effectively restricts particle displacement and improves stress transmission, corresponding to the peak unconfined compressive strength observed in Fig. 9.

At a higher NaOH concentration (c = 4.00 mol/L; Fig. 13d), the microstructure deteriorates, characterized by the development of microcracks and intra-agglomerate pores. Excessive OH− destabilizes previously formed geopolymer gels and intensifies the dissolution of bentonite minerals, resulting in a weakened binding framework. The loss of structural integrity directly leads to the post-peak reduction in compressive strength.

For samples prepared at w = 44% (Fig. 14), similar trends are observed but with more pronounced degradation at high alkalinity. At c = 2.00 mol/L (Fig. 14a), a relatively dense structure with feather-like gel morphologies is formed, indicating effective interaction among bentonite, fly ash, and geopolymer gels under sufficient moisture availability. In contrast, at c = 6.00 mol/L (Fig. 14b), severe erosion of gel structures, extensive cracking, and fragmented agglomerates are evident. The combined effects of excessive alkalinity and high moisture accelerate gel dissolution and structural collapse, resulting in poor particle interlocking and a marked reduction in unconfined compressive strength (Fig. 9).

SEM results for geopolymer treated bentonite with different NaOH concentration at w = 44%. (a) c = 2.00 mol/L (×2000). (b) c = 6.00 mol/L (×2000).

Overall, SEM observations demonstrate that NaOH concentration governs the balance between geopolymer gel formation and chemical erosion. Moderate alkalinity optimizes gel bonding and skeleton continuity, whereas excessive NaOH induces microstructural degradation, directly controlling the observed strength enhancement–attenuation behavior.

Mechanism analysis of geopolymer treatment of expansive soil

The effect of OH− concentration on the treatment of montmorillonite by geopolymerization is decisive. On one hand, an adequate concentration of OH− chemically erodes the fly ash precursors, releasing Al and Si elements, which then depolymerize in an alkaline environment to form ionic monomers such as [Al(OH)4]− and [SiO2(OH)2]2−. These ionic monomers aggregate through hydroxyl attraction to form NaAlSiO3·xH2O aluminosilicate gel (N-A-S-H), as shown in Fig. 15a.

Schematic of the mechanism of OH- action in geopolymer treatment of soil.

On the other hand, excessive OH− in the solution can break the chemical bonds in montmorillonite, such as Al-O-Si and metal-O bonds. Additionally, minerals like quartz (primarily composed of SiO2) in montmorillonite will react with OH−, forming ions that migrate into the solution, as depicted in Fig. 15b. This process erodes the layered structure of montmorillonite, causing the aggregates to disintegrate and the montmorillonite to dissolve. Furthermore, Ca2+ ions displaced by Na+ in montmorillonite may participate in reactions with dissolved silicate and aluminate species and potentially contribute to the formation of calcium-containing hydrate phases. However, no direct evidence for C-S-H or C-A-S-H was obtained from the XRD or FTIR results in the present study, and these phases therefore cannot be conclusively identified. The experimentally supported mechanisms are mainly the dissolution of montmorillonite and the formation of N-A-S-H-type gel, which together contribute to particle bonding and partial pore filling, as shown in Fig. 15c.

In conclusion, the effect of OH− concentration involves two distinct but competing mechanisms. At moderate alkalinity, OH− promotes the dissolution of reactive aluminosilicate species from fly ash and the subsequent formation of geopolymer gel, which enhances particle bonding and densifies the soil structure. In contrast, at excessive alkalinity, OH− causes over-dissolution and destabilization of the montmorillonite framework, leading to degradation of the clay mineral structure and weakening of the load-bearing skeleton. The observed mechanical behavior therefore reflects the balance between gel formation and alkaline-induced structural degradation.

Conclusions

An experimental study was conducted focusing on the effect of NaOH concentrations on the mechanical properties of geopolymer based modified bentonite. Via XRD, FTIR, and SEM tests, the modification mechanism of geopolymer modified bentonite was analysed. The main conclusions are as follows:

Taguchi orthogonal experiment revealed that NaOH concentration significantly influenced the strength of geopolymer-modified bentonite, followed by moisture content and fly ash content. Strength increased with curing age, indicating ongoing geopolymerization. The optimal conditions (6% fly ash, 6 mol/L NaOH, 30% moisture) achieved the highest strength across all curing ages.

Strength increased with curing age, indicating ongoing geopolymerization. The optimal conditions (6% fly ash, 6 mol/L NaOH, 30% moisture) achieved the highest strength across all curing ages.

Unconfined compressive strength tests demonstrated an initial decrease in strength, followed by an increase, and then a decline with varying NaOH concentrations. At low concentrations (0–0.5 mol/L), NaOH was insufficient to activate the fly ash, resulting in decreased strength. At moderate concentrations (0.5–2 mol/L), NaOH enhanced the geopolymer reaction, significantly improving strength. At high concentrations (> 2 mol/L), excessive NaOH disrupted the geopolymer structure, reducing strength.

XRD, FTIR, and SEM analyses indicated that NaOH concentration affects the microstructure of geopolymer-modified bentonite. During the geopolymerization process bentonite transformed from calcium-montmorillonite to sodium-montmorillonite and formed N-A-S-H gels (shown by the XRD results), with the depolymerization of aluminosilicates according to the FTIR results. This study primarily focuses on the strength behavior and microstructural mechanisms of geopolymer-modified expansive soils. However, key characteristics relevant to engineering application, including swelling behavior and durability under wet–dry cycles, were not investigated in the present work. These limitations constrain the direct practical applicability of the current findings and should be addressed in future studies.
