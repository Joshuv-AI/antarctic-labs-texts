Development and validation of checklists for standardized patients in dental education for special care health needs patients
Authors: Julia Guerrero-Gironés, Irene Mellinas-Martínez, Miguel Ramón Pecci-Lloret, María Gracia Adánez-Martínez, María Pilar Pecci-Lloret
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTraditionally, checklists or evaluation templates have been utilized to assess psychological and health-related issues in fields like psychology and pediatrics. Currently, their use has expanded, particularly in medical research and healthcare evaluation. This study aims to develop and validate checklists for standardized patient use in the subject " Special Care Health Needs patients” within the Dentistry Degree program, ensuring international applicability whenever clinical simulation is employed.Material and methodSix different scenarios were developed for student evaluation. Along with each scenario, a script was also created for the simulated participant or the instructor voicing the simulator. An evaluation template was designed for each clinical scenario. Once the scenarios and checklists were designed, 11 experts in the field of Special Care Health Needs Patients from six different universities were provided with a file for each simulation scenario, which they had to complete. The data collected from the validation of the evaluation templates by the expert panel were sent to the Statistical Support Section (SAE) of the University of Murcia. Data analysis was performed using R version 4.0.3.ResultsThe level of agreement among experts regarding the importance and scoring of items in six clinical evaluation templates was analyzed. Most results were statistically significant (p < 0.05), except for the physical examination dimension in several templates, likely due to the low number of items. Overall, high concordance was observed across competency dimensions, particularly in communication.ConclusionsThe positive statistical outcomes observed in our implementation of these checklists highlight their potential not only to assess students’ clinical behavior with consistency but also to inform and refine educational strategies. The adoption of a standardized, rigorous approach to checklist development—such as the one applied in this study—may contribute significantly to the advancement of performance evaluation in healthcare education, promoting both learner competency and patient-centered care.

Introduction

The global movement towards Competency-Based Medical Education (CBME) demands a robust and heterogeneous assessment system [1]. Clinical competence cannot be evaluated using a single assessment tool, as no single evaluation method can provide the comprehensive information necessary to evaluate something as complex as healthcare delivery. CBME integrates assessment into real-world environments or workplaces, aligning with Miller’s pyramid. Miller’s pyramid defines actions at each level (“knows,” “knows how,” “shows how,” and “does”), which can be observed and measured using assessment instruments. The foundation of any education is knowledge (“knows”), which can be evaluated through written tests (multiple-choice, short-answer questions, essay questions, etc.). Knowledge must be applicable to various situations (“knows how”), assessable through written practical tests, such as clinical case-solving or virtual scenarios [1, 2].

From the third level of the pyramid, “shows how,” practical evaluation in a real or near-realistic environment is required. Objective Structured Clinical Examinations (OSCE), video recordings, and clinical simulations are considered effective methods for evaluating clinical competence, either individually or in combination. Evaluation at the highest pyramid level, “does,” necessitates direct observation of the student in the workplace. This is the ideal form of clinical competency assessment but also the most challenging. Direct observation by one or several specialized instructors during clinical encounters, Clinical Evaluation Exercises (CEX or Mini-CEX), case analysis (360º feedback), and peer assessment are appropriate methods for this level [3–5].

Combining different assessment methods is necessary to guarantee formative and summative evaluations. Clinical competence must be assessed within real clinical contexts, requiring objective, reliable evaluation tools with sufficient validity for student evaluation. Assessment templates can evaluate one or multiple competency components through specific items, designed to allow evaluators to quickly and easily complete them during the clinical scenario. Items must assess a single, clearly defined action or maneuver the student must perform to be scored positively or negatively (completed/not completed) [6].

Traditionally, checklists or evaluation templates have been utilized to assess psychological and health-related issues in fields like psychology and pediatrics. Currently, their use has expanded, particularly in medical research and healthcare evaluation. Checklists are considered objective, efficient, and reliable tools for measuring student skills [7, 8]. Evaluation templates require realistic scenarios replicating real-life events, hence their widespread use in high-fidelity simulation sessions. They enable assessment of procedural skills and socio-emotional competencies—non-technical skills critical in healthcare [9]. Unlike existing checklists primarily developed for general medical or dental contexts, our tool is specifically tailored to the complexities of Special Care Dentistry. This area requires not only clinical decision-making but also the management of behavioral, communication, and systemic health challenges unique to this patient population.

Therefore, this study aims to develop and validate checklists for standardized patient use in the subject " Special Care Health Needs Patients” within the Dentistry Degree program, ensuring international applicability whenever clinical simulation is employed.

Material and method

The research was approved by the Ethics Committee of the University of Murcia (ID: 3554/2021).

Scenario creation

Six different scenarios were developed for student evaluation: a hypertensive patient with hearing impairment, a bronchopathy patient experiencing an asthmatic crisis, a patient with chronic renal insufficiency, a diabetic patient experiencing hypoglycemia, an epileptic and anticoagulated patient having an epileptic seizure, and finally, a patient with cerebral palsy and vision loss.

For the development of the scenarios, JGG, MRPL, and MPPL (instructors of the course) participated with the assistance of the guide directed at professionals and educators for the development of high-fidelity scenarios published by Alinier in 2011 [10]. Along with each scenario, a script was also created for the simulated participant or the instructor voicing the simulator. This script outlined predetermined phrases, information to be provided, the moment when the patient should deteriorate, exhibit aggressive behavior, show a decreased level of consciousness, etc.

Additionally, a script was developed for each scenario to establish the initial parameters at the beginning of the scenario, as well as the possible paths and variations in the patient’s physiological parameters depending on the participants’ actions. This script clearly defines instructions for the instructors in the control room and for the simulated participants regarding the action or actions that could trigger patient deterioration or crisis, waiting times, appropriate actions for patient recovery or further deterioration, etc.

Finally, to facilitate organization and the proper execution of the scenarios, a document was created specifying the personnel and equipment needed for each scenario. This document ensured that all necessary materials were available before each session, including instruments, supporting documentation, and characterization materials, among others.

The six scenarios were selected based on their frequency and complexity in dental care for patients with special health care needs. Each represents a high-risk or medically compromised condition requiring adapted communication, management, or emergency response by dental professionals.

Checklist creation

An evaluation template was designed by JGG and IMM for each clinical scenario. Within each competency component (anamnesis, physical examination, clinical management, and communication), a series of items were included, clearly describing what students had to perform to be rated as completed/not completed (Yes/No). The final score for each checklist was 100 points, assigning each item a score based on its relevance to the clinical scenario.

Checklist validation

Once the scenarios and checklists were designed, 11 experts in the field of Special Care Health Needs Patients from six different universities were contacted via email (Table 2). Among the 11 experts, six were women and six were men, aged between 33 and 60 years, with a minimum of five years of teaching experience in Special Care Health Needs Patients (Table 1). An email invited them to participate in an expert panel for the content validation of six checklists. The experts were provided with a file for each simulation scenario, which they had to complete. They filled out two Likert scales for each evaluation template.

The first 7-point Likert scale was used to assess the importance of each item in the clinical scenario, where:

Column A: values 1 to 3 indicated less important items, values 4 to 6 indicated important items, value 7 indicated a mandatory item.

Additionally, each item has been assigned a score that contributes to a total of 100 points in the student’s evaluation. These values are indicated in parentheses next to each item.

Column B: 1 meant strongly disagree, 2 meant disagree, 3 meant agree, 4 meant strongly agree.

In this phase, the agreement among expert evaluations for each item (importance and item scoring) was analyzed using Kendall’s coefficient of concordance (W). This coefficient ranges from 0 to 1, with stronger agreement as the value approaches 1. Values greater than 0.9 were considered very strong agreement.

Additionally, the p-value of Kendall’s W was used to determine whether to reject the null hypothesis:

H₀ (Null Hypothesis): Expert agreement is due to chance.H₁ (Alternative Hypothesis): Expert agreement is not due to chance.

H₀ (Null Hypothesis): Expert agreement is due to chance.

H₀ (Null Hypothesis): Expert agreement is due to chance.

H₁ (Alternative Hypothesis): Expert agreement is not due to chance.

H₁ (Alternative Hypothesis): Expert agreement is not due to chance.

If the p-value ≤ the significance level (0.05), the null hypothesis is rejected, concluding that expert ratings are associated with each other. Conversely, if the p-value ≥ 0.05, the null hypothesis cannot be rejected, meaning there is insufficient evidence to conclude that expert rankings are associated and not due to chance.

The coefficient of concordance was calculated for each competency dimension as well as overall. In some evaluation templates, the physical examination competency was excluded from the analysis as it contained only one item.

Data analysis

Before sending the data to the statisticians, the Content Validity Index (CVI) was calculated for each evaluation template at the item level (CVI-I) and globally, but only for the experts’ assessment regarding the importance of the item in the evaluation template. Regarding the experts’ assessment of the score assigned to each item, only Kendall’s coefficient of concordance (W) and the p-value were calculated along with the evaluation of the item’s importance. This was due to greater difficulties in reaching a consensus among the experts after several rounds and modifications.

According to commonly accepted guidelines, Kendall’s W values can be interpreted as follows: values above 0.80 indicate strong agreement, values between 0.65 and 0.80 indicate reasonable agreement, and values between 0.50 and 0.65 indicate acceptable agreement agreement [11].

The data collected from the validation of the evaluation templates by the expert panel were sent to the Statistical Support Section (SAE) of the University of Murcia. Data analysis was performed using R version 4.0.3.

Discusion

Evaluation templates are a highly effective tool for assessing clinical performance; however, the literature contains few validated and published templates [12]. The moderate to strong levels of agreement observed in most checklist dimensions, as indicated by Kendall’s W values, support the reliability of our instruments. These results suggest that the checklists are appropriate for evaluating performance in standardized scenarios involving patients with special care needs.The evaluation templates used in our study assessed four competency components: anamnesis, physical examination, clinical management, and communication. They were designed to evaluate each item in a binary manner (YES/NO), where each item had a specific score based on its importance in the clinical scenario. The assigned score was obtained only if the item was correctly performed.

Schmutz et al. [6] define most evaluation templates as being assessed dichotomously (done/not done), ensuring that all important tasks are evaluated by requiring the assessor to focus on predefined actions. However, many of these templates do not properly assess the quality of the performed actions. Authors such as Schmutz et al. [6] and Morgan et al. [13] argue that not all actions within evaluation templates are equally important in assessing a clinical scenario. Therefore, essential actions should be differentiated by assigning higher weightings in the final grading of the instrument, as we have implemented in this study. The scoring system was designed not only to reflect whether a student completed a task but also to weight each action based on its clinical relevance within each scenario. This approach aligns with recommendations in the literature to prioritize essential actions in high-risk patient situations.

However, Schmutz et al. [6] and Jung et al. [14] propose the possibility of adding a third option for items (done/not done/incorrectly done). In this approach, an item receives full credit when marked as done, partial credit when performed incorrectly (i.e., an action performed partially or with delay), and a neutral score of 0 points when marked as not done. Gaba et al. [15] also support the need to identify the importance of actions within evaluation templates. Additionally, they suggest that essential actions should be mandatory, meaning that if a critical action is not performed, the final checklist score should be zero, even if other actions were correctly completed.

A limitation of our study was the lack of an option to rate an item as partially performed, which often forced assessors to decide between marking an item as done or not done, even when the action had been partially executed or there was an attempt to perform it, but the execution was inadequate.

Furthermore, the study did not include an analysis of inter-rater reliability, which is essential to ensure consistency across different evaluators. This omission limits our understanding of how subjectivity may have influenced scoring. Future research should address this aspect to strengthen the robustness of the tool and improve its applicability across settings.

Roy et al. [16], similar to our study, used evaluation templates with assigned scores for each item to assess simulation scenarios, conducting evaluations at two different time points: initially and three months later. Brooks-Buza et al. [17] also used standardized evaluation templates for critical situations, establishing different objectives to determine compliance and allowing observations to be recorded. However, they did not assign a final score, and the clinical simulation assessment was conducted only once.

Another way to assess the simulation itself and obtain a general evaluation of its impact on students is through questionnaires. Rubin et al. [18] and Tanoubi et al. [19] provided participants with a final questionnaire containing a series of questions or items related to different aspects of the simulation. These questionnaires were answered using Likert scales, allowing the researchers to obtain a final rating or evaluation of the simulation.

Similarly, Kalsi et al. [20] and Bartolomé et al. [21] followed the same approach but also administered an initial questionnaire, enabling them to compare initial and final evaluations.

Moreover, regarding internal validity, while the expert consensus and statistical significance across most dimensions support the strength of our evaluation instruments, the lack of an option to score partially performed actions introduces a potential source of measurement bias. Assessors were forced to dichotomize nuanced performances, which could affect the precision of the evaluations. Concerning external validity, our sample of expert evaluators included professionals from six universities and diverse geographic areas, enhancing the generalizability of our results. Nevertheless, further application and validation of these checklists in different educational contexts and with a larger cohort of students are needed to confirm their applicability and reproducibility.

From a practical perspective, the use of standardized checklists in simulation scenarios has proven feasible, requiring minimal additional resources once developed. Initial informal feedback from students indicated a positive reception, highlighting the clarity of expectations and perceived fairness of assessment. These tools can be easily integrated into existing OSCEs or clinical training modules and may serve as a foundation for broader curricular reforms aimed at standardizing competency evaluation in Special Care Dentistry.

Conclusion

The accurate assessment of clinical performance is essential to enhancing patient safety and ensuring the effectiveness of educational interventions. Our study supports that well-constructed, systematically developed checklists may serve as useful tools for evaluating procedural performance in dental education, particularly within the context of Special Care Health Needs patients. However, given the identified limitations—such as the absence of a scoring option for partially performed actions—these instruments should be interpreted as complementary tools within a broader assessment strategy. Future iterations should aim to refine the checklist design to better capture the complexity of clinical performance.
