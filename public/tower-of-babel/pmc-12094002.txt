Association Preoperative Protein Adequacy and Post Elective Laparotomy Surgical Site Infection
Authors: Davie Muhamad, Nurul Ratna Mutu Manikam, Ridho Ardhi Syaiful
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/AimsPostoperative complications tend to occur in high-risk populations, including those undergoing major surgery. Surgical site infections (SSI) are some of the most common postoperative complications in laparotomy procedures. Surgery induces a postoperative stress response, triggering an inflammatory process that increases muscle protein proteolysis. Preoperative protein intake increases muscle protein reserves and supports postoperative wound healing and immunity. This study analyzed the association between preoperative protein adequacy and post-elective laparotomy SSI.MethodsA prospective cohort study was conducted on 93 subjects with an adequate protein group of 48 subjects and an inadequate protein group of 45 subjects undergoing elective laparotomy at Dr. Cipto Mangunkusumo Hospital, Jakarta. The protein adequacy was analyzed using an interview method for seven days preoperatively. Patient monitoring was carried out for 30 days postoperatively to assess the complications in the form of SSIs. The association between protein adequacy and SSI was analyzed using a Chi-Square test, and multivariate analysis was performed to assess the factors most associated with post-elective laparotomy SSI.ResultsAn association was observed between preoperative protein adequacy and post-elective laparotomy SSI (RR 3.413; 95% CI, 1.363–8.549; p=0.004). Multivariate analysis showed that the preoperative protein adequacy and preoperative albumin levels were strongly predictive of the occurrence of SSI.ConclusionsThe preoperative protein adequacy and albumin levels were strongly related to predicting the post-elective laparotomy SSI.Keywords: Laparotomy, Protein, Surgical site infection

INTRODUCTION

Postoperative complications can occur in high-risk populations, including those undergoing major surgery.1 Major abdominal surgery is associated with postoperative complications in approximately 20% of patients.2 Gastrointestinal laparotomy can lead to complications with an incidence of 10.7– 33.5%.3 Surgical site infections (SSI) are the most common postoperative complications in laparotomy procedures, affecting approximately 11.8 cases per 100 surgical procedures in low-to-middle-income countries and approximately 7.8% in Southeast Asia.4-6 Laparotomy procedures have a higher prevalence of postoperative infections compared to laparoscopy, with approximately 35% of laparotomy patients experiencing SSI.7,8 Emergency and elective surgeries carry a risk of infection complications.9 Syaiful et al.10 conducted a study at Dr. Cipto Mangunkusumo Hospital, Jakarta. They reported that 2.8% of 4893 patients who underwent abdominal surgery from 2012 to 2017 experienced SSI.

Patients who undergo surgery will experience a postoperative stress response in the form of an increase in the inflammatory process, which increases muscle protein proteolysis. Therefore, it is imperative to focus on the preoperative protein intake to increase muscle protein reserves and support postoperative wound healing and immunity.11 Sufficient protein intake before surgery can improve the functional capacity, prevent weight loss, and preserve the gut microbiota. All protein intake, whether oral, enteral, or parenteral, should be considered to achieve good postoperative clinical outcomes. In the cases of malnutrition, preoperative parenteral nutrition can reduce the incidence of postoperative infections, including SSI.12 Dijxhoorn et al.13 explained that the increase in preoperative protein by 10% (>1.2 g/kg body weight/day) could reduce the risk of complications (infectious, non-infectious, and decubitus) by 10%. Gastrointestinal surgeries carried a higher risk of developing SSI than non-gastrointestinal surgeries. The prevalence of SSI in patients undergoing gastrointestinal laparotomy can reach 25–40%, with approximately 8% mortality from all nosocomial infections.14 In contrast, patients who are not related to the gastrointestinal tract, such as those in gynecologic oncology undergoing laparotomy, experience an approximately 7% rate of SSI.15

Based on the limited literature on preoperative protein adequacy and its association with infection complications, especially SSI in elective laparotomy in Indonesia, this study examined the association between preoperative protein adequacy and post-elective laparotomy SSI.

SUBJECTS AND METHODS

1. Study population and design

A prospective cohort study was conducted from February to May 2023 on patients undergoing elective laparotomy at Dr. Cipto Mangunkusumo Hospital, Jakarta. The inclusion criteria were as follows: age between 18 to 65 years, subjects planned for elective laparotomy surgery, and subjects willing to participate in the study and provide informed consent. The exclusion criteria were chronic liver disease, chronic kidney disease stages IV and V, and subjects unable to stand or be weighed.

2. Data collection

Permission from the Health Research Ethics Committee was issued by the Faculty of Medicine, Universitas Indonesia (No. KET-112/UN2.F1/ETIK/PPM.00.02/2023 and protocol number 22-12-1529). Data on the characteristics of the study were collected: age, gender, anthropometry, preoperative body mass index (BMI), preoperative nutritional status (Global Leadership Initiative on Malnutrition, GLIM), preoperative energy and macronutrient intake, protein adequacy, duration of surgery, comorbidities, intraoperative blood loss, preoperative albumin level, postoperative albumin level and organ involved in laparotomy. The energy intake and protein adequacy were analyzed using the 24-hour food recall for seven days preoperatively. Furthermore, preoperative protein adequacy was assessed based on ideal body weight (IBW). Patient monitoring was carried out for 30 days postoperatively to assess the complications in the form of SSI.

3. Data analysis

The data collected was processed using the Statistical Program for Social Science (SPSS; IBM Co., Armonk, NY, USA). The categorical scale data are presented as proportions (distribution and frequency). Numeric scale data were tested for normality using a Kolmogorov–Smirnoff test. If the data are normally distributed (p>0.05), it will be presented as the mean and standard deviation. Data not normally distributed (p<0.05) were presented as the median and range (minimum –maximum). The association between protein adequacy and SSI was analyzed using a Chi-Square test. Multivariate analysis assessed the most associated factors with post-elective laparotomy SSI using logistic regression.

RESULTS

This study examined 93 subjects, with an adequate protein group of 48 subjects and an inadequate protein group of 45 subjects undergoing elective laparotomy. Table 1 lists the primary characteristics of the subjects in this study. An association was observed between preoperative protein adequacy and post-elective laparotomy SSI (RR 3.413; 95% CI, 1.363– 8.549; p=0.004), as listed in Table 2. Among the subjects who experienced SSI, there were 16 subjects (35.6%) with a protein intake <1.2 g/kg IBW. An association was observed between the confounding factors (preoperative albumin level, preoperative nutritional status based on GLIM criteria, and organ involved in laparotomy) and post-elective laparotomy SSI (Table 2).

After conducting bivariate analyses (Table 2), multivariate analysis was performed to explore the association between several independent variables and post-elective laparotomy SSI. The independent variables included in the model were the preoperative protein adequacy, preoperative albumin level, preoperative nutritional status based on GLIM criteria, preoperative energy intake, and organ involved in laparotomy. Using these five independent variables, multivariate analysis was performed to determine the variables that most strongly influenced the occurrence of post-elective laparotomy SSI. Multivariate analysis in the initial model showed that not all p-values were less than 0.05. Hence, not all variables significantly affected the occurrence of SSI simultaneously. On the other hand, based on the final model (Table 3), after undergoing four steps, only the preoperative protein adequacy and preoperative albumin level were strongly related to predicting the occurrence of post-elective laparotomy SSI (p<0.05).

DISCUSSION

An association was found between preoperative protein adequacy and post-elective laparotomy SSI (p=0.004). The protein inadequacy, which is defined as less than 1.2 g/kg IBW, was associated with an increased risk of SSI. These findings are consistent with Dijxhoorn et al.13, who reported that a preoperative protein adequacy greater than or equal to 1.2 g/kg body weight reduces the risk of complications, including SSI (p<0.05), in both gastrointestinal and non-gastrointestinal surgical patients. Using the ideal body weight to determine protein adequacy results in a more precise outcome. The protein adequacy will be underestimated or overestimated if a subject is underweight or overweight, respectively, based on the actual body weight.16

The duration of preoperative protein intake monitoring in this study aligns with previous research and ESPEN guidelines, which recommend seven–14 days.17 The decision to use a seven-day preoperative period for this study because the average time subjects spent in preoperative care was seven days. In this study, the analysis of the preoperative protein adequacy was not based solely on oral intake and considered protein obtained through enteral and parenteral. Researchers considered this because patients undergoing treatment might receive enteral or parenteral nutrition as part of the preoperative preparation. Assessing the preoperative protein intake of 47.3% of the subjects was not entirely performed during their hospital stay because these subjects were admitted on average three to five days before the operation. Protein intake analysis before hospital admission was also included to meet the seven-day preoperative requirement.

Previous research on preoperative protein adequacy and the incidence of SSI remains limited, particularly concerning laparotomy procedures. van Venrooij et al.18 focused on patients undergoing cardiac surgery. They reported that patients with good nutritional status but a low protein intake were not at risk of complications and prolonged hospital stays. The study advised against preoperative nutritional interventions for all cardiac surgery patients. Regarding the current research, nutritional status is associated with postoperative complications, particularly SSI. The present study revealed an association between nutritional status and SSIs, where malnutrition increased the risk of SSIs (p=0.009). Adequate protein intake can prevent malnutrition and reduce inflammation, improving the immune system, including neutrophil function and phagocytosis against bacteria, which can prevent the occurrence of SSI.19

Based on the characteristic data, 86.2% of subjects with preoperative albumin levels <3.5 g/dL experienced malnutrition. Hennessey et al.20 reported that preoperative hypoalbuminemia is an independent risk factor for the occurrence of postoperative SSIs. In this study, the preoperative albumin levels were associated with the incidence of SSIs (p=0.001). Alkaaki et al.8 showed similar results, with preoperative albumin levels associated with SSIs (p<0.001). The mechanism behind the occurrence of SSIs due to hypoalbuminemia is related to decreased collagen synthesis and granuloma formation during the wound healing process.20

In this study, the organs involved in laparotomy are significantly associated with the incidence of SSI (p=0.014). The organs involved in laparotomy were categorized into two groups: upper/lower gastrointestinal tract and non-gastrointestinal tract. The non-gastrointestinal tract group refers to gynecological oncology patients. The rationale for selecting these subjects is that they all share the risk of post-elective laparotomy SSIs.15 The causes of SSI vary, one of which is the surgical technique.21 Studies have shown that laparoscopic single-incision procedures are safe and technically feasible for patients undergoing colorectal surgery.22 Furthermore, surgical site contamination with bacteria originating from feces can occur in large bowel surgeries, leading to SSI. On the other hand, not all positive bacterial wound cultures result in SSI, indicating that various other factors contribute to the development of SSI.23

The limitations of this study include the subjective nature of the intake analysis method used, involving interviews, especially when subjects have not yet been admitted to the hospital. This could lead to the research subject not fully recalling the food consumed within the previous 24 hours.

Preoperative protein adequacy and albumin levels were strongly predictive of post-elective laparotomy SSI. Monitoring and ensuring nutritional intake, especially protein adequacy, for at least seven days preoperatively is essential for predicting the occurrence of SSI. Hence, monitoring and optimizing albumin levels for at least seven days preoperatively are necessary.
