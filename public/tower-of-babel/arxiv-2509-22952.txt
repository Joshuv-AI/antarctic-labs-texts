A convergence rate result for front tracking approximations of conservation laws with discontinuous flux
Shyam Sundar Ghoshal, John D. Towers

Abstract
We consider the initial value problem for a scalar conservation law in one space dimension with a single spatial flux discontinuity, the so-called two-flux problem. We prove that a well-known front tracking algorithm has a convergence rate of at least one-half. The fluxes are required to be smooth, but are not required to be convex or concave, monotone, or even unimodal. We require that there are no more than finitely many flux crossings, but we do not require that they satisfy the so-called crossing condition. If both fluxes are strictly increasing or strictly decreasing then our analysis yields a convergence rate of one, in agreement with a recent result. Similarly, if the fluxes are equal, i.e., there is no flux discontinuity, we obtain a convergence rate of one in this case also, in agreement with a classical result. The novelty of this paper is that the class of discontinuous-flux conservation laws for which there is a front tracking error estimate is expanded, and that the method of analysis is new; we do not use the Kuznetsov lemma which is commonly used for this type of analysis.

Introduction

The subject of this paper is approximation via front tracking to solutions
of an initial value problem for a scalar conservation law having a spatially discontinuous flux:

\textrm{Problem $\displaystyle P$:}\quad\left\{\begin{split}&u_{t}+\mathcal{F}(x,u)_{x}=0\quad\textrm{for $\displaystyle(x,t)\in\Pi_{T}:=\mathbb{R}\times(0,T)$,}\\
&\mathcal{F}(x,u):=H(-x)g(u)+H(x)f(u),\\
&u(x,0)=u_{0}(x)\quad\textrm{for $\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{R}$.}\end{split}\right.

(1.1)

Here
\displaystyle H(x)
is the Heaviside function. Thus, the flux
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(x,u)
of this
conservation law has a spatial dependence that is discontinuous at
\displaystyle x=0

if the functions
\displaystyle f
and
\displaystyle g
are different.

The front tracking algorithm
[
32
,
44
]
computes approximate solutions
\displaystyle u^{\delta}
of Problem P by
producing the exact solution to
a simpler problem
\displaystyle P^{\delta}
where the flux
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}
is replaced by a piecewise linear approximation

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\delta}
and the initial data
\displaystyle u_{0}
is replaced by a piecewise constant approximation
\displaystyle u_{0}^{\delta}
:

\textrm{Problem $\displaystyle P^{\delta}$:}\quad\left\{\begin{split}&u^{\delta}_{t}+\mathcal{F}^{\delta}(x,u^{\delta})_{x}=0\quad\textrm{for $\displaystyle(x,t)\in\Pi_{T}$,}\\
&\mathcal{F}^{\delta}(x,u):=H(-x)g^{\delta}(u)+H(x)f^{\delta}(u),\\
&u^{\delta}(x,0)=u^{\delta}_{0}(x)\quad\textrm{for $\displaystyle x\in\mathbb{R}$.}\end{split}\right.

(1.2)

Here
\displaystyle\delta
is a parameter that defines the level of discretization. Our main result (Theorem
3.1
) is that the
front tracking algorithm described below produces approximate solutions that
converge to the so-called vanishing viscosity solution (Definition
1.3
) at a rate of at least
\displaystyle O(\delta^{1/2})
.
If
\displaystyle f
and
\displaystyle g
are both strictly monotone, we obtain
a convergence rate of
\displaystyle O(\delta)
(Theorem
4.2
), in agreement
with a recent result of Ruf
[
45
]
. Finally,
we also obtain
a convergence rate of
\displaystyle O(\delta)

if
\displaystyle f=g
(Theorem
4.3
), in agreement with a classical result due to
Lucier
[
39
]
. Our method of analysis is different from the
methods found in

[
45
]
and
[
39
]
; for example we do not invoke
Kuznetsov’s lemma
[
32
, Theorem 3.11]
.

Front tracking is both a theoretical tool and a practical computational algorithm. It is more
complex to implement than finite difference / finite volume methods, but it is computationally
rapid and devoid of the numerical dissipation produced by finite difference / finite volume methods.

Before specifying the data of the problem we will need the following definition:

Definition 1.1

(flux crossing)
.

We call
\displaystyle u_{\chi}

a flux crossing if
\displaystyle f(u_{\chi})=g(u_{\chi})
, and for some
\displaystyle\epsilon>0
it holds that

\textrm{$\displaystyle\left(g(u_{L})-f(u_{L})\right)\left(g(u_{R})-f(u_{R})\right)<0$ for all
$\displaystyle u_{L}\in(u_{\chi}-\epsilon,u_{\chi})$,
$\displaystyle u_{R}\in(u_{\chi},u_{\chi}+\epsilon)$}.

In less formal terms, a flux crossing
\displaystyle u_{\chi}
is the location of a zero of the function

\displaystyle f(u)-g(u)
where the sign of
\displaystyle f-g
changes.

Assumption 1.1

We make the following assumptions about the data. In what follows

\displaystyle\underline{u},\overline{u}\in\mathbb{R}
with
\displaystyle\underline{u}<\overline{u}
.

(
A
.1)

\displaystyle g(\underline{u})=f(\underline{u})
,
\displaystyle g(\overline{u})=f(\overline{u})
,

(
A

\displaystyle g,f\in C^{2}([\underline{u},\overline{u}])
,

(
A
.3)

\displaystyle f
and
\displaystyle g
are not linear on any nondegenerate interval,

(
A
.4)

\displaystyle f(u)\neq g(u)
for
\displaystyle u\in(\underline{u},\overline{u})
, except for finitely many (possibly zero) flux crossings in the interval
\displaystyle(\underline{u},\overline{u})
,

(

\displaystyle u_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})\cap\textrm{BV}(\mathbb{R})

\displaystyle u_{0}(x)\in[\underline{u},\overline{u}]

,

(

for some

\displaystyle u_{0}(x)=u_{L}

\displaystyle u_{0}(x)=u_{R}

Assumption

Items

The front tracking algorithm.
To describe the front track algorithm we must specify the construction
of
\displaystyle f^{\delta}
,
\displaystyle g^{\delta}
, and
\displaystyle u_{0}^{\delta}
for fixed
\displaystyle\delta>0
.
To specify
\displaystyle f^{\delta}
and
\displaystyle g^{\delta}
, choose a positive integer
\displaystyle K
, and
breakpoints
\displaystyle\mathsf{u}_{0},\mathsf{u}_{1},\ldots,\mathsf{u}_{K}
such that

\underline{u}=\mathsf{u}_{0}<\mathsf{u}_{1}<\cdots<\mathsf{u}_{K}=\overline{u},\quad\textrm{$\displaystyle\mathsf{u}_{k+1}-\mathsf{u}_{k}\leq\delta$ for $\displaystyle k=0,\ldots,K-1$.}

(1.3)

Then
\displaystyle f^{\delta}
and
\displaystyle g^{\delta}
are defined to be the piecewise linear interpolants to
\displaystyle f
and
\displaystyle g

such that
\displaystyle f^{\delta}(\mathsf{u}_{k})=f(\mathsf{u}_{k})
and
\displaystyle g^{\delta}(\mathsf{u}_{k})=g(\mathsf{u}_{k})
for
\displaystyle k=0,\ldots,K
.

The front tracking initial data
\displaystyle u_{0}^{\delta}
is constructed satisfying the following conditions:

\begin{split}&\textrm{1. $\displaystyle u_{0}^{\delta}$ is piecewise constant with
finitely many jumps, and such that}\\
&u_{0}^{\delta}(x)\in[\underline{u},\overline{u}]\,\,\forall x\in\mathbb{R},\quad\TV(u_{0}^{\delta})\leq\TV(u_{0}),\\
&\textrm{2. condition~\ref{init_data_large_x} is satisfied with the same $\displaystyle X$ as for $\displaystyle u_{0}$, and}\\
&\textrm{$\displaystyle\left\|u_{0}^{\delta}-u_{0}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}=O(\delta)$ as $\displaystyle\delta\rightarrow 0$.}\end{split}

(1.4)

Remark 1.3
.

\displaystyle f^{\delta}
and
\displaystyle g^{\delta}
clearly satisfy Assumption
(A.1)
, and for
\displaystyle\delta
sufficiently small,
Assumption
(A.4)
also holds for
\displaystyle f^{\delta}
,
\displaystyle g^{\delta}
.
It follows from Assumption
(A.2)
that
\displaystyle f,g\in\textrm{Lip}([\underline{u},\overline{u}])
. Let
\displaystyle L_{f}
and
\displaystyle L_{g}
denote Lipschitz constants
for
\displaystyle f
and
\displaystyle g
. It is clear that also
\displaystyle f^{\delta},g^{\delta}\in\textrm{Lip}([\underline{u},\overline{u}])
, and

L_{f^{\delta}}\leq L_{f},\quad L_{g^{\delta}}\leq L_{g}.

(1.5)

Moreover, it is clear from (
1.4
) that

\displaystyle u_{0}^{\delta}
satisfies Assumptions
(A.5)
and
(A.6)
.
Thus all parts of Assumption
1.1
are satisfied for the data
associated with the front tracking algorithm, with exception of Assumption
(A.3)
. This assumption
is present to guarantee the existence of one sided traces along the interface at
\displaystyle x=0
.
With the addition of Assumption
1.2

below the required traces of
\displaystyle u^{\delta}
exist without Assumption
(A.3)
.

In view of Remark
1.3
, with
\displaystyle\delta>0
fixed and
\displaystyle f^{\delta}
,
\displaystyle g^{\delta}
, and
\displaystyle u_{0}^{\delta}
in hand, the front tracking algorithm then computes the exact vanishing viscosity solution, defined below (Definition
1.3
), of problem
\displaystyle P^{\delta}
.
The solution of problem
\displaystyle P^{\delta}
is piecewise constant, with discontinuities (fronts), some of which are moving.
The algorithm keeps track of the location of each front as time advances.
Each time two fronts collide, the piecewise constant solution is updated by solving a Riemann problem.
Each such Riemann problem is constructed so that the
\displaystyle\Gamma
condition (Definition
1.2
)
is satisfied, and the resulting front tracking solution is thus a vanishing viscosity solution (Definition
1.3
).

In order for the front tracking algorithm to be well-defined, there cannot be more than finitely many
fronts present in
\displaystyle u^{\delta}
at any finite time
\displaystyle t\in[0,T]
. That this is the case has been proven in
some important cases
[
9
,
17
,
23
,
37
,
43
,
44
]
,
but not in all cases allowed by our assumptions about
\displaystyle f
and
\displaystyle g
. Thus
we add the following assumption:

Assumption 1.2
.

For any
\displaystyle t\in[0,T]
,
\displaystyle u^{\delta}(\cdot,t)
is piecewise constant with finitely many discontinuities.

Vanishing viscosity solutions.

Even in the classical setting where
\displaystyle f=g
, and even if the initial data
\displaystyle u_{0}
is smooth,
solutions develop discontinuities, and so we seek weak solutions. However, weak
solutions are generally not unique. Because of this an additional condition, referred to as an
entropy condition, or admissibility condition, is required. In the case where
\displaystyle f=g
, the
Kružkov entropy solution
[
32
]
is generally sought, and this resolves the uniqueness issue.
In the case of a spatially discontinuous flux, the Kružkov entropy inequalities
do not make sense, and so some other entropy condition is required. The situation is further
complicated by the fact that
there is not just a single notion of entropy solution for the problem (
1.1
), see
[
5
]
. We will
work with the so-called vanishing viscosity solution
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
12
,
36
]
, which we now set out to define.

The notion of traces appears in the definition of vanishing viscosity
solution, by which we mean the following limits:

u(0^{-},t):=\operatorname*{ess\,lim}_{x\uparrow 0}u(x,t),\qquad u(0^{+},t):=\operatorname*{ess\,lim}_{x\downarrow 0}u(x,t).

(1.6)

In the sequel we will sometimes use the abbreviations

\displaystyle u(0^{-},t)=u_{-}
,
\displaystyle u(0^{+},t)=u_{+}
for the traces
appearing in (
1.6
).

Let
\displaystyle\mathrm{co}(a,b)
denote
the interval
\displaystyle[\min(a,b),\max(a,b)]
. The following is Diehl’s
\displaystyle\Gamma

condition
[
5
,
6
,
21
,
22
]
, which will be used below in the definition of vanishing viscosity solutions:

Definition 1.2

(
\displaystyle\Gamma
condition)
.

The pair
\displaystyle(u_{-},u_{+})
satisfies the
\displaystyle\Gamma
condition if

\begin{split}&\textrm{$\displaystyle g(u_{-})=f(u_{+})$, and there exists $\displaystyle u_{\Gamma}\in\mathrm{co}(u_{-},u_{+})$ such that}\\
&\textrm{$\displaystyle(u_{+}-u_{\Gamma})(f(z)-f(u_{+}))\geq 0\,\,\forall z\in\mathrm{co}(u_{+},u_{\Gamma})$ and}\\
&\textrm{$\displaystyle(u_{\Gamma}-u_{-})(g(z)-g(u_{-}))\geq 0\,\,\forall z\in\mathrm{co}(u_{-},u_{\Gamma})$.}\end{split}

Remark 1.4
.

The condition
\displaystyle g(u_{-})=f(u_{+})
appearing in the definition
is the familiar Rankine-Hugoniot condition.

Definition 1.3

(Vanishing viscosity solution,
[
5
,
6
]
)
.

A measurable function
\displaystyle u:\Pi_{T}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a
vanishing viscosity solution of the initial value problem (
1.1
) if it
satisfies the following conditions:

(
D
.1)

\displaystyle u\in L^{\infty}(\Pi_{T})
;
\displaystyle u(x,t)\in[\underline{u},\overline{u}]
for
a.e.
\displaystyle(x,t)\in\Pi_{T}
.

(
D
.2)

For all test functions
\displaystyle\phi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}\times[0,T))
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{split}\iint_{\Pi_{T}}\bigl(u\phi_{t}&+\mathcal{F}(x,u)\phi_{x}\bigr)\,dx\,dt+\int_{\mathbb{R}}u_{0}(x)\phi(x,0)\,dx=0.\end{split}

(1.7)

(
D
.3)

For any test function
\displaystyle 0\leq\phi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}\times[0,T))

which vanishes for
\displaystyle x\geq 0
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{split}&\iint_{\Pi_{T}}\Bigl(|u-c|\phi_{t}+\mathop{\mathrm{}}{sgn}(u-c)\bigl(g(u)-g(c)\bigr)\phi_{x}\Bigr)\,dx\,dt\\
&\qquad+\int_{\mathbb{R}}|u_{0}-c|\phi(x,0)\,dx\geq 0,\quad\forall c\in[\underline{u},\overline{u}],\end{split}

(1.8)

and for any test function
\displaystyle 0\leq\phi\in\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R}\times[0,T))

which vanishes for
\displaystyle x\leq 0
,

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{split}&\iint_{\Pi_{T}}\Bigl(|u-c|\phi_{t}+\mathop{\mathrm{}}{sgn}(u-c)\bigl(f(u)-f(c)\bigr)\phi_{x}\Bigr)\,dx\,dt\\
&\qquad+\int_{\mathbb{R}}|u_{0}-c|\phi(x,0)\,dx\geq 0,\quad\forall c\in[\underline{u},\overline{u}].\end{split}

(1.9)

(
D
.4)

For a.e.
\displaystyle t\in(0,T)
, the traces
\displaystyle u_{-}:=u(0^{-},t)

and
\displaystyle u_{+}:=u(0^{+},t)
satisfy the
\displaystyle\Gamma
condition.

A measurable function
\displaystyle u:\Pi_{T}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfying conditions

(D.1)
and
(D.2)
is called a weak solution of the
initial value problem (
1.1
).

.

Note that condition
(D.4)
assumes the existence of traces.
For the solution
\displaystyle u
of problem

,
existence of traces follows from Assumption

Theorem 1.1

(Uniqueness of vanishing viscosity solutions,
[
5
,
6
]
)

Let
\displaystyle u
,
\displaystyle v
be two vanishing viscosity solutions
in the sense of Definition

\displaystyle u_{0},v_{0}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R};[0,1])

In particular, there exists at most one vanishing viscosity solution

Example 1.1
.

A simple but important example
which has been studied extensively
has the form

u_{t}+(k(x)p(u))_{x}=0,\quad p(u)=u(1-u),\quad\textrm{$\displaystyle\underline{u}=0$, $\displaystyle\overline{u}=1$},

(1.10)

with
\displaystyle k(x)=(1-H(x))k_{L}+H(x)k_{R}
for some
\displaystyle k_{L},k_{R}>0
.
Here
\displaystyle g(u)=k_{L}p(u)
,
\displaystyle f(u)=k_{R}p(u)
.
Our front tracking error estimate applies
to this example, and to our knowledge it is the first such.
The conservation law (
1.10
) can be viewed as a model of vehicle traffic on a unidirectional road (the so-called
Lighthill-Witham-Richards model), where the parameter
\displaystyle k(x)
is a spatially dependent
maximum vehicle speed.
In this case there is no flux crossing, so Assumption
(A.4)
is
trivially satisfied. For this example the vanishing viscosity solution is the
same as many of the other types of solution that have been defined, including the minimal jump solution,
the entropy solution of
[

]
, and the optimal entropy solution.
Although the spatial variation of the solution is not generally bounded, one can obtain
a bound on the spatial variation of the so-called singular mapping
[

,
37
,

,
49
]
.
Since
\displaystyle dp/du
only vanishes at a single point, the singular mapping is invertible, which guarantees
the existence of traces for
\displaystyle u

[
34
]
.
If the break points

\displaystyle\{\mathsf{u}_{k}\}
are
chosen so that
\displaystyle dp^{\delta}/du\neq 0
on any nontrivial interval, then one also has
traces for the front tracking solution
\displaystyle u^{\delta}
,
independent of Assumption
1.2
.

\displaystyle f=g

More recently reference

.

]
,

]

]

[

Reference

A Godunov finite volume algorithm

Although the convergence rate result of this paper is for a front tracking algorithm, a certain finite volume algorithm makes an
appearance as an analytical tool.
Specifically, we use the Godunov-type scheme of
[
21
,
36
]
, which we describe next.

We begin by
discretizing the spatial domain
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}
into cells

I_{j}=\bigl(x_{j}-\Delta x/2,x_{j}+\Delta x/2\bigr]=\bigl(x_{j-\frac{1}{2}},x_{j+\frac{1}{2}}\bigr],

where
\displaystyle x_{j}=j\Delta x
for
\displaystyle j\in\mathbf{Z}
. Similarly, the time
interval
\displaystyle[0,T]
is discretized via
\displaystyle t^{n}=n\Delta t
for
\displaystyle n=0,\dots,N
,
where the integer
\displaystyle N
is such that
\displaystyle N\Delta t\in[T,T+\Delta t)
, resulting
in the time strips

I^{n}=[t^{n},t^{n+1}).

Here
\displaystyle\Delta x>0
and
\displaystyle\Delta t>0
denote the spatial and temporal
discretization parameters respectively.
With
\displaystyle\lambda=\Delta t/\Delta x
, we will assume that the
mesh size
\displaystyle\Delta:=(\Delta x,\Delta t)
approaches
zero with
\displaystyle\lambda
fixed.
We will assume that the following CFL condition holds:

\lambda\cdot\max\left(L_{f},L_{g}\right)\leq 1/2.

(2.1)

We will use
\displaystyle U_{j}^{n}
to denote the resulting finite
difference approximation;
\displaystyle U_{j}^{n}\approx u(x_{j},t^{n})
.
The initial data is discretized via cell averages:

U_{j}^{0}=\frac{1}{\Delta x}\int_{x_{j-\smash{{\frac{1}{2}}}}}^{x_{j+\smash{{\frac{1}{2}}}}}u_{0}(x)\,dx.

(2.2)

Let
\displaystyle\chi_{j}(x)
and
\displaystyle\chi^{n}(t)
denote the characteristic functions for
the intervals
\displaystyle I_{j}
and
\displaystyle I^{n}
, respectively.
The finite difference solution
\displaystyle\left\{U_{j}^{n}\right\}

is extended to all of
\displaystyle\Pi_{T}
by defining

u^{\Delta}(x,t)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\sum_{j\in\mathbf{Z}}\chi_{j}(x)\chi^{n}(t)U_{j}^{n},\qquad(x,t)\in\Pi_{T}.

We use
\displaystyle\Delta_{+}
and
\displaystyle\Delta_{-}
