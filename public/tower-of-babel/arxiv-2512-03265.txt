Large-time behavior in a nonlocal heat equation with absorption. The absorption dominated case with fast decaying initial data
Carmen Cortázar, Fernando Quirós, and Noemí Wolanski

Abstract
. We study the large-time behavior of nonnegative solutions to a nonlocal dispersal equation in ℝ N {\mathbb{R}}^{N} with an absorption term modeled by − u p -u^{p} , with 1 < p < 1 + 2 N 1<p<1+\frac{2}{N} . The initial datum u 0 u_{0} is assumed to be bounded, and to satisfy | x | 2 p − 1 ​ u 0 ​ ( x ) → A ≥ 0 |x|^{\frac{2}{p-1}}u_{0}(x)\to A\geq 0 as | x | → ∞ |x|\to\infty . Under these assumptions, we prove that the decay rate is that of the purely absorbing problem, while the limit profile is a very singular solution to a local diffusion problem with absorption if A = 0 A=0 , and a solution to this same local problem with initial datum A ​ | x | − 2 p − 1 A|x|^{-\frac{2}{p-1}} if A > 0 A>0 .

1.
Introduction

We study the large-time behavior of nonnegative solutions to

(1.1)

\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle u_{t}-{\mathcal{L}}u=-|u|^{p-1}u\quad\mbox{in }{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\times(0,\infty),\qquad u(x,0)=u_{0}(x)\quad\mbox{in }{\mathbb{R}}^{N},\\
\displaystyle\text{where }{\mathcal{L}}u(x)=\int J(x-u)\big(u(y)-u(x)\big)\,{\rm d}y,\end{array}

with
1<p<1+\frac{2}{N}
, and
0\leq u_{0}\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})
such that

(1.2)

|x|^{\frac{2}{p-1}}u_{0}(x)\to A\geq 0\quad\mbox{as }|x|\to\infty.

In particular,
u_{0}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. The assumptions on the kernel
J
are:
J\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})
radially symmetric,
J>0
in
B_{d}
,
J=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}\setminus B_{d}
for some
d>0
, and
\int J(x)\,{\rm d}x=1
. By a solution of the problem we mean a function

u\in C([0,\infty);L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{N}))\cap C^{1}(0,\infty;L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{N}))\cap L^{\infty}(0,\infty;L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{N}))

that satisfies (
1.1
) in a pointwise sense. We define a sub-solution (resp. super-solution) of the problem in a similar way, as a function in these spaces that satisfies (
1.1
) with inequalities
\leq
(resp.
\geq
) instead of equalities.

The operator
{\mathcal{L}}
appears in different contexts to model nonlocal dispersal. In particular, it is used in population dynamics, where
\int J(x-y)u(y,t)\,{\rm d}y
represents the population arriving at
x
at time
t
from every point in space, while
u(x,t)=\int J(x-y)u(x,t)\,{\rm d}y
represents the population leaving the point
x
at time
t
. Thus,
{\mathcal{L}}u(x,t)
is the balance between how much is arriving and how much is leaving
x
at time
t
. Thus, in the absence of sources or sinks,
{\mathcal{L}}u=u_{t}
. If there is absorption, modeled by
-|u|^{p-1}u
, we get (
1.1
); see, for instance,
[
12
,
8
,
5
,
1
,
11
,
6
]
. Equations of this type have also been proposed to model phase transitions
[
3
,
4
]
and image enhancement
[
13
]
.

As in the case
{\mathcal{L}}=\Delta
, the long-time behavior of nonnegative solutions is influenced by two intertwined aspects:

(i)

the competition between the diffusion and the absorption term;

(ii)

the asymptotic behaviour of
u_{0}(x)
as
|x|\to\infty
.

In order to see which mechanism, diffusion or absorption, induces the fastest decay, and hence dominates the evolution, we compare their characteristic decay rates. Solutions to the purely absorbing equation
u_{t}=-u^{p}
decay like
t^{-1/(p-1)}
. The decay of solutions to the purely diffusive equation
u_{t}-{\mathcal{L}}u=0
depends on the size of the initial datum. When the datum is integrable, the decay is of order
t^{-N/2}
, since in this case solutions to this problem behave for large times like solutions to the
local
heat equation

(1.3)

u_{t}=\mathfrak{a}\Delta u,\qquad\mathfrak{a}=\frac{1}{2N}\int J(x)|x|^{2}\,\textup{d}x

with the same initial datum; see
[
9
]
. Thus, diffusion should dominate if
\frac{1}{p-1}<\frac{N}{2}
. This is indeed the case: when
p>1+\frac{2}{N}
and the initial data are integrable (and nonnegative and bounded), then the solution
u
to problem (
1.1
) satisfies

\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{N/2}\|u(\cdot,t)-M_{*}\mathcal{U}(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{K\sqrt{t}})}=0

for every
K>0
, where
\mathcal{U}
is the fundamental solution of (
1.3
) and

M_{*}=\int u_{0}(x)\,\textup{d}x-\int_{0}^{\infty}\int u^{p}(x,t)\,\textup{d}x\,\textup{d}t>0

is the asymptotic (nontrivial) mass of
u
; see
[
18
]
. Notice that, though diffusion is dominant, absorption is still felt: the limit mass, though nontrivial, is not the initial one.

The restriction of this convergence result to the so-called
diffusive scale
,
x\in B_{K\sqrt{t}}
,
K>0
, is not expected to be technical, since, as in the local case (see
[
14
]
), the behavior at infinity of the initial datum should have a strong influence in the long-time behavior of solutions in other scales. The restriction to such scales will appear in all the results that we describe below.

When the initial data are not integrable, the large time behavior of solutions to the heat equation may be very complex if the initial data have a complex behavior at infinity, even in the local case; see, for instance,
[
22
]
. Hence, we restrict ourselves to initial data having a power-like decay at infinity,

(1.4)

|x|^{\alpha}u_{0}(x)\to A>0\quad\mbox{as }|x|\to\infty.

When
\alpha\leq N
, solutions to the purely diffusive nonlocal equation with such data also behave for large times as the solution to the local heat equation (
1.3
) with the same datum. This can be proved, for instance, with scaling techniques, borrowing ideas from
[
20
]
to obtain compactness. The large-time behavior for this local problem is given (see, for instance,
[
14
]
):

•

If
\alpha<N
, by the unique solution
\mathcal{U}_{A,\alpha}
to problem (
1.3
) with initial datum
A|x|^{-\alpha}
. This function has a self-similar form,

\mathcal{U}_{A,\alpha}(x,t)=t^{-\alpha/2}F_{A}(x/t^{1/2}),

where
F_{A}
is a smooth positive profile such that
\lim_{|\xi|\to\infty}|\xi|^{\alpha}F_{A}(\xi)=A
.

•

If
\alpha=N
, by
C_{A,N}\log t\,\mathcal{U}(x,t)
, where
\mathcal{U}
is the fundamental solution to problem (
1.3
) and
C_{A,N}
is a constant that depends only on
A
and
N
, .

Therefore, diffusion is expected to dominate if
\alpha/2>1/(p-1)
. This expectation was confirmed in
[
21
]
(see also
[
20
]
), where it was proved that if
u
is a solution to (
1.1
) with an initial datum
u_{0}
satisfying (
1.4
) with

\alpha>\frac{2}{p-1},

then, for any
K>0
,

\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{\alpha/2}\|u(\cdot,t)-\mathcal{U}_{A,\alpha}(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{K\sqrt{t}})}=0&\text{if }\alpha\in(0,N),\\
\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{t^{N/2}}{\log t}\|u(\cdot,t)-C_{A,N}\log t\,\mathcal{U}(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{K\sqrt{t}})}=0&\text{if }\alpha=N.\end{array}

Notice that in this case absorption has no effect in the large-time behaviour.

Let us take a look now to the region of parameters where we expect the decay rate of solutions to be that corresponding to absorption,
t^{-1/(p-1)}
. This region consists of two parts:
p\in(1,1+\frac{2}{N})
if the initial data are integrable, and
\alpha<2/(p-1)
when they are not and have the decay behavior (
1.4
).

Though the absorption mechanism is dominant in this region of parameters, diffusion is still there, and may play a more or less important role, depending on the behavior of the initial datum
u_{0}
as
|x|\to\infty
. If
u_{0}
has a fast decay, we expect bigger gradients, and hence diffusion will be more significant, while if the decay is slow, solutions may become “flat” in diffusive regions, and then decay there as a solution to the purely absorbing problem.

To identify the expected limits, depending on the behavior of the datum at infinity, we scale the solution, taking into account that we expect to have the decay rate corresponding to absorption. Thus, for each
\lambda>0
we define

(1.5)

u_{\lambda}(x,t)=\lambda^{\frac{2}{p-1}}u(\lambda x,\lambda^{2}t).

If the family
\{u_{\lambda}\}
converges uniformly in compact sets as
\lambda\to\infty
towards some function
U
, taking
t=1
and renaming
\lambda^{2}
as
t
, we will get the convergence of
u
towards
U
in the diffusive scale. But, which function is
U
? Let us explain heuristically how to characterize it.

The scaled function
u_{\lambda}
is a solution to

u_{t}-{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}u=-u^{p}\quad\mbox{in }{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\times(0,\infty),\qquad u(x,0)=\lambda^{\frac{2}{p-1}}u_{0}(\lambda x)\quad\mbox{in }{\mathbb{R}}^{N},

for a scaled operator
{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}
given by

(1.6)

{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}u(x)=\lambda^{2}\int J_{\lambda}(x-y)\big(u(y)-u(x)\big)\,{\rm d}y,\qquad\text{with }J_{\lambda}(z)=\lambda^{N}J(\lambda z).

The operators
{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}
converge, in a certain weak sense, to
\mathfrak{a}\Delta
as
\lambda\to\infty
; see, for instance,
[
2
]
and the references therein. Therefore, the limit function
U
is expected to be a solution to the semilinear local heat equation

(1.7)

U_{t}-\mathfrak{a}\Delta U=-U^{p}\quad\mbox{in }{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\times(0,\infty),\qquad\mathfrak{a}=\frac{1}{2N}\int J(z)|z|^{2}\,\textup{d}z.

On the other hand, we expect the limit function
U
to be related to the initial datum of the original problem, so that the trace of
U
is the limit of the family
\{u_{0,\lambda}\}
,

U(x,0^{+}):=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}U(x,t)=\lim\limits_{\lambda\to\infty}u_{0,\lambda}(x)=\lim\limits_{\lambda\to\infty}\lambda^{\frac{2}{p-1}}u_{0}(\lambda x).

If this is the case and
u_{0}
decays slowly, then

(1.8)

U(x,0^{+})=\lim\limits_{\lambda\to\infty}\lambda^{\frac{2}{p-1}}u_{0}(\lambda x)=|x|^{-\frac{2}{p-1}}\lim_{|\lambda x|\to\infty}|\lambda x|^{\frac{2}{p-1}}u_{0}(\lambda x)=\infty\quad\textup{if }x\neq 0.

This is compatible with
U
begin the
flat
solution
\mathcal{F}(t)=\big((p-1)t\big)^{-\frac{1}{p-1}}
to (
1.7
), and, indeed, if
u_{0}
satisfies (
1.8
), then, for any
K>0
,

\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{\frac{1}{p-1}}\|u(\cdot,t)-\mathcal{F}(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{K\sqrt{t}})}=0;

see
[
19
]
. Notice that for initial data having the power-like behavior at infinity (
1.4
), condition (
1.8
) is equivalent to

\alpha<\frac{2}{p-1}.

This covers the whole absorption dominated region of parameters when the initial data are not integrable.

There is a situation in the absorption dominated region that has not been treated yet, namely, the case
p\in(1,1+\frac{2}{N})
with integrable initial datum such that
|x|^{\frac{2}{p-1}}u_{0}(x)\to A\geq 0
as
|x|\to\infty
. For
u_{0}
satisfying (
1.4
), this would correspond to
\alpha\geq\frac{2}{p-1}>N
. The aim of this paper is to fill in this gap.

When the limit value
A
is 0, then
\lim\limits_{\lambda\to\infty}\lambda^{\frac{2}{p-1}}u_{0}(\lambda x)=0
for all
x\neq 0
, while

\lim\limits_{\lambda\to\infty}\int\lambda^{\frac{2}{p-1}}u_{0}(\lambda x)\,\textup{d}x=\lambda^{\frac{2}{p-1}-N}\int u_{0}(\xi)\,\textup{d}\xi=+\infty,

since
p<1+\frac{2}{N}
. Thus, the expected limit
U
should satisfy

(1.9)

U(x,t)\to 0\quad\textup{as }t\to 0^{+}\textup{ for all }x\neq 0,\quad\int U(x,t)\,{\rm d}x\to\infty\quad\textup{as }t\to 0^{+}.

There is a unique nonnegative solution
V
to the semilinear local heat equation (
1.7
) with this very singular behavior as
t\to 0^{+}
. This nonnegative
very singular solution
to (
1.7
) should then give the large-time behavior in this case. We confirm this expectation in Theorem
4.1
, where we prove that for every
K>0

t^{\frac{1}{p-1}}\|u(\cdot,t)-V(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{K\sqrt{t}})}\to 0\quad\mbox{as }t\to\infty.

When
A>0
, the initial datum of the expected limit is
U(x,0)=A|x|^{-\frac{2}{p-1}}
, and we will prove in Theorem
4.2
that, for every
K>0
,

(1.10)

t^{\frac{1}{p-1}}\|u(\cdot,t)-U_{A}(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{K\sqrt{t}})}\to 0\quad\mbox{as }t\to\infty,

where
U_{A}
is the unique solution to the semilinear local heat equation (
1.7
) such that

(1.11)

U(x,t)\to A|x|^{-\frac{2}{p-1}}\textup{ for all }x\neq 0\quad\mbox{as }t\to 0^{+}.

Our proofs are based on the scaling argument mentioned above. The most important technical difficulties appear, first, when trying to prove that the family
\{u_{\lambda}\}
is uniformly bounded in
t\geq t_{0}>0
, and then, once this is established, showing that the family
\{u_{\lambda}\}
is precompact on compact sets. The second difficulty stems mainly from: (i) the fact that the functions
u_{\lambda}
are solutions of equations involving different operators; and (ii) the lack of a regularizing effect of the diffusion operators themselves. This difficulty was overcome in
[
20
]
with arguments that can be used in our present situation.

In comparison with the available large-time asymptotic results, the main difference is that in this case the initial condition is not attained in the sense of finite measures, so that the characterization of the limit solution needs new and different ideas.

Borderline cases.
If
u_{0}
satisfies (
1.4
) with

\alpha=\frac{2}{p-1}<N,

both decay mechanisms, diffusion and absorption, are balanced. But we may still proceed as in the case
\alpha=\frac{2}{p-1}>N
considered in the present paper, performing the scaling (
1.5
) of the solution to (
1.1
) to obtain a similar result. Indeed, the large-time behavior is given by (
1.10
) for any
K>0
, with
1/(p-1)=\alpha/2
and
U_{A}
the unique solution to the semilinear local heat equation (
1.7
) satisfying (
1.11
); see
[
20
]
. The difference with respect to the case in which
\alpha=\frac{2}{p-1}>N
is that now the absorption decay rate coincides with the diffusion one.

When the initial datum is integrable,
u_{0}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, the borderline balanced case corresponds to
p=1+\frac{2}{N}
. However, the decay rate is not the common one for diffusion and absorption,
t^{-\frac{N}{2}}
, but faster,

\lim_{t\to\infty}t^{N/2}u(\cdot,t)\to 0\quad\mbox{uniformly on compact sets},

due to the combined effects of both mechanisms; see
[
20
]
. Hence, the decay rate needs some correction, as compared to the diffusion/absorption one,
t^{-\frac{N}{2}}
.

There is also a doubly critical case, namely
p=1+\frac{2}{N}
with initial data satisfying (
1.4
) with
\alpha=N
, so that
\alpha=\frac{2}{p-1}
. Notice that for these borderline values of the parameters diffusion and absorption are not balanced, the decay due to the latter being faster (
t^{-\frac{1}{p-1}}
against
t^{-\frac{N}{2}}\log t
). In view of the results for the local case
\mathcal{L}=\Delta
(see
[
14
]
), we expect the same behavior as for the cases
\alpha=\frac{2}{p-1}\neq N
mentioned above. However, the proofs might be more involved, due to the doubly critical character of the problem.

These two remaining cases are expected to require new ideas, and will be considered elsewhere.

Organization of the paper

In Section 2 we first prove existence and uniqueness of a local solution when
u_{0}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
, allowing for sign changes. Next, we obtain a comparison result that implies, in particular, that if the initial datum is nonnegative, then the solution is nonnegative and exists globally. In Section 3 we obtain space and time decay estimates for the solution that are scaling invariant. Section 4 is devoted to the proof of our main result. After choosing the appropriate scaling
u_{\lambda}
for the absorption dominated cases, we use the time estimate from the previous section to show that the family
\{u_{\lambda}\}
is precompact in compact subsets of
t>0
, so that it converges under subsequences to a function
U
. Then, thanks to the space estimate, we identify the limit function
U
. It is here where the main technical difficulties of the paper arise. Finally, we translate the convergence as
\lambda\to\infty
in our large-time behavior results for solutions to (
1.1
).

2.
Existence and comparison

The goal of this section is to prove that problem (
1.1
) has a unique global in time nonnegative solution if the initial datum is nonnegative and belongs to
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{N})\cap L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
.
We begin by proving existence and uniqueness of a
local in time solution
for such initial data, that is a function
u\in C([0,T];L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{N}))\cap C^{1}(0,T;L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{N}))\cap L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{N}))
for some
T>0
that satisfies

(2.1)

\displaystyle u_{t}-{\mathcal{L}}u=-|u|^{p-1}u\quad\mbox{in }{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\times(0,T),\qquad u(x,0)=u_{0}(x)\quad\mbox{in }{\mathbb{R}}^{N}.

Sign changes are allowed. Then, we prove a comparison principle for sub- and super-solutions (in their common existence time) that implies, in particular, that if
u_{0}\geq 0
then
u\geq 0
. This in turn implies, if
u_{0}\geq 0
, that
u\leq u_{\mathcal{L}}
for
t\in[0,T]
, where
u_{\mathcal{L}}
stands for the solution to

(2.2)

u_{t}-{\mathcal{L}}u=0\quad\mbox{in }{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\times(0,\infty),\qquad u(x,0)=u_{0}(x)\quad\mbox{in }{\mathbb{R}}^{N}.

Hence, for every
t\in[0,T]
,

(2.3)

\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})}\leq\|u_{0}\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})},\qquad\|u(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})}\leq\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})}.

Since the time step in the construction of the solution depends only on
\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})}
, we will conclude that the solution exists globally.

We start with the proof of local existence, that uses a fixed point argument.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
p>1
and
u_{0}\in L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})\cap L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})
. There exists
T>0
and a unique function
u\in C([0,T];L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{N}))\cap C^{1}(0,T;L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{N}))\cap L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{N}))
that satisfies (
2.1
).

Proof.

Let

\displaystyle K=\{v

\displaystyle\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{N}))\cap L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{N}))\mbox{ such that }\|v(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})}\leq 2\|u_{0}\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})}

\displaystyle\mbox{ and }\|v(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})}\leq 2\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})}\mbox{ for every }t\in(0,T)\},

and for each
v\in K
let

\displaystyle{\mathcal{T}}v(x,t)

\displaystyle=e^{-t}u_{0}(x)+\int_{0}^{t}\int e^{-(t-s)}J(x-y)v(y,s)\,{\rm d}y{\rm d}s

\displaystyle\quad-\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(t-s)}|v(x,s)|^{p-1}v(x,s)\,{\rm d}s.

It is easy to check that if
u
is a fixed point of
{\mathcal{T}}
in
K
, then
u\in C([0,T];L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{N}))
, and once this is proved, it is immediate that
u\in C^{1}(0,T;L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{N}))
and satisfies (
2.1
). Therefore,
u
is a solution to (
2.1
) if and only if it is a fixed point of
{\mathcal{T}}
in
K
. Hence, let us prove that
{\mathcal{T}}
has a unique fixed point in
K
if we take
T
small enough. We will do this by means of Banach’s fixed-point theorem.

Let us see first that
{\mathcal{T}}:K\to K
if
T
is small, how small depending only on
\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})}
. Indeed, on the one hand,

\displaystyle\|{\mathcal{T}}v(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})}

\displaystyle\leq e^{-t}\|u_{0}\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(t-s)}\|v(\cdot,s)\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})}\,{\rm d}s

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(t-s)}\|v(\cdot,s)\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})}^{p-1}\|v(\cdot,s)\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})}\,{\rm d}s

\displaystyle\leq(1+2t+2^{p}\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})}^{p-1}t)\|u_{0}\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})}\leq 2\|u_{0}\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})}

if

, for some

. Analogously, with the same

\|{\mathcal{T}}v(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})}\leq(1+2t+2^{p}\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})}^{p-1}t)\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})}\leq 2\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})}.

In order to see that
{\mathcal{T}}
it is a contraction in

with the norm

\vvvert v\vvvert=\max\{\|v\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{N}))},\|v\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{N}))}\},

we observe that, if

and

\big||v_{1}(x,s)|^{p-1}v_{1}(x,s)-|v_{2}(x,s)|^{p-1}v_{2}(x,s)\big|\leq p(2\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})})^{p-1}|v_{1}(x,s)-v_{2}(x,s)|,

so that

\displaystyle\|{\mathcal{T}}v_{1}-{\mathcal{T}}v_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{N}))}

\displaystyle\leq(1+p(2\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})})^{p-1})T\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{N}))}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{N}))}

if

\displaystyle\|{\mathcal{T}}v_{1}-{\mathcal{T}}v_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{N}))}

Hence,

is a contraction in

is small, how small depending only on
\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})}
. Therefore,

has a unique fixed point

, which is the unique solution to (

) in

.

Now, we prove a comparison result.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
u_{1}
be a subsolution to (
1.1
) and
u_{2}

{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\times[0,T]

{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
. Then,
u_{1}(x,t)\leq u_{2}(x,t)
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\times[0,T]
.

Proof.

Let
w=u_{1}-u_{2}
. Then,

w_{t}(x,t)\leq{\mathcal{L}}w(x,t)-(|u_{1}(x,t)|^{p-1}u_{1}(x,t)-|u_{2}(x,t)|^{p-1}u_{2}(x,t)).

We multiply this inequality by
\operatorname{sgn}(w)
(where
\operatorname{sgn}(s)=1
if
s>0
and
\operatorname{sgn}(s)=0
if
s\leq 0
) and integrate in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
. Hence, as
w_{t}\operatorname{sgn}(w)=(w^{+})_{t}
, with
w^{+}=\max\{w,0\}
,

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}(w^{+})_{t}

\displaystyle(x,t)\,{\rm d}x\leq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}J(x-y)\big(w(y,t)-w(x,t)\big)\operatorname{sgn}(w(x,t))\,{\rm d}y{\rm d}x

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}(|u_{1}(x,t)|^{p-1}u_{1}(x,t)-|u_{2}(x,t)|^{p-1}u_{2}(x,t))\operatorname{sgn}(u_{1}(x,t)-u_{2}(x,t))\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle\leq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}J(x-y)\big(w(y,t)-w(x,t)\big)\operatorname{sgn}(w(x,t))\,{\rm d}y{\rm d}x

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}J(x-y)\big(w(y,t)-w(x,t)\big)\big(\operatorname{sgn}(w(y,t))-\operatorname{sgn}(w(x,t)\big)\,{\rm d}y{\rm d}x

\displaystyle\leq 0.

Integrating between
0
and
t
,
t\in(0,T]
, we deduce that
\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}w^{+}(x,t)\,{\rm d}x=0
. Hence,
w(\cdot,t)\leq 0
for all
t\in(0,T]
, that is,
u_{1}\leq u_{2}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\times(0,T]
.
∎

As a corollary we get positivity if
u_{0}\geq 0
, since
v\equiv 0
is a solution to the equation.

Corollary 2.1
.

Let
u
be the solution constructed in Theorem
2.1
. Let us assume that

. Then,
u\geq 0
as well.

Thanks to positivity,
u
is a sub-solution to (

). Therefore,

and, in particular, the estimates (

.

As the existence time

in Theorem

depends only on the

Corollary 2.2
.

Let

. Then, there exists a unique maximal solution to (

). This solution is global.

3.
Time and space decay

In this section we prove that if
0\leq u_{0}\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})
,
p\in(1,1+\frac{2}{N})
and
|x|^{\frac{2}{p-1}}u_{0}(x)\to A\geq 0
as
|x|\to\infty
, then there are positive constants
C_{0}
(depending of
p
,
J
and
u_{0}
) and
C_{p}
(depending only on
p
) such that

(3.1)

u(x,t)\leq C_{0}|x|^{-\frac{2}{p-1}},\qquad u(x,t)\leq C_{p}\,t^{-\frac{1}{p-1}}.

Note that this size estimates are invariant under the scaling (
1.5
).

In order to obtain the estimate for the spatial decay, we construct a stationary supersolution.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\phi(x)=(C_{1}+C_{2}|x|^{2})^{-\frac{1}{p-1}}
,
C_{1},C_{2}>0
. If
C_{2}
is small enough, then
{\mathcal{L}}\phi(x)\leq\phi^{p}(x)
for
|x|\geq 2d
. If in addition
C_{1}\leq C_{2}
and
C_{2}
is small enough, then
\phi\geq u_{0}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
and
\phi(x)\geq\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})}\geq u(x,t)
for all
t>0
if
|x|\leq 2d
.

Proof.

As
J
is radially symmetric, a Taylor’s expansion shows that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}\phi(x)

\displaystyle=\int J(x-y)\big(\phi(y)-\phi(x)\big)\,{\rm d}y

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}\int J(x-y)(y_{i}-x_{i})(y_{j}-x_{j})\Big(\int_{0}^{1}\phi_{x_{i}x_{j}}(x+s(y-x))\,{\rm d}s\Big)\,{\rm d}y.

To estimate the right-hand side we observe that, since
C_{2}>0
,

\displaystyle\phi_{x_{i}x_{j}}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{4p}{(p-1)^{2}}C_{2}^{2}x_{i}x_{j}(C_{1}+C_{2}|x|^{2})^{-\frac{p}{p-1}-1}-\delta_{ij}\frac{2C_{2}}{p-1}(C_{1}+C_{2}|x|^{2})^{-\frac{p}{p-1}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4p}{(p-1)^{2}}C_{2}\frac{C_{2}|x|^{2}}{C_{1}+C_{2}|x|^{2}}(C_{1}+C_{2}|x|^{2})^{-\frac{p}{p-1}}\leq\frac{4p}{(p-1)^{2}}C_{2}(C_{1}+C_{2}|x|^{2})^{-\frac{p}{p-1}}.

Besides, if
|x|\geq 2d
and
|x-y|<d
, then
|x+s(y-x)|\geq\frac{|x|}{2}
for every
0<s<1
. Hence, using again the radial symmetry of the kernel, if
|x|\geq 2d
we have

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}\phi(x)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2p}{(p-1)^{2}}C_{2}\Big(\int J(z)|z|^{2}\,{\rm d}z\Big)\Big(C_{1}+\frac{C_{2}}{4}|x|^{2}\Big)^{-\frac{p}{p-1}}

\displaystyle\leq 4^{\frac{p}{p-1}}\frac{2p}{(p-1)^{2}}C_{2}\Big(\int J(z)|z|^{2}\,{\rm d}z\Big)\ \phi(x)^{p}\leq\phi(x)^{p}

if
C_{2}
is small enough, how small depending only on
p
and
J
.

Let now
B>\max\{1,2d\}
be such that
|x|^{\frac{2}{p-1}}u_{0}(x)\leq A+1
if
|x|>B
, and let
C_{2}
be sufficiently small so that
(2C_{2})^{-\frac{1}{p-1}}\geq A+1
. Let
C_{1}\leq C_{2}
. Then

\phi(x)\geq(C_{2}(1+|x|))^{-\frac{1}{p-1}}\geq(2C_{2})^{-\frac{1}{p-1}}>A+1\geq|x|^{\frac{2}{p-1}}u_{0}(x)\geq u_{0}(x)\quad\textup{if }|x|>B.

Taking
C_{2}
even smaller, if needed, so that
(C_{1}+C_{2}B^{2})^{-\frac{1}{p-1}}\geq\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})}
, then

\phi(x)\geq(C_{1}+C_{2}B^{2})^{-\frac{1}{p-1}}\geq\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})}\quad\textup{if }|x|\leq B.

Summarizing, if
C_{1}\leq C_{2}
and
C_{2}
is small enough, then, on the one hand,
\phi(x)\geq u_{0}(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, and on the other hand, as
B>2d
,
\phi(x)\geq\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})}\geq u(x,t)
if
|x|\leq 2d
for all
t>0
.
∎

As a corollary we obtain a spatial decay estimate for
u
by comparison.

Corollary 3.1
.

There is a constant
C_{0}
such that

0\leq|x|^{\frac{2}{p-1}}u(x,t)\leq(1+|x|)^{\frac{2}{p-1}}u(x,t)\leq C_{0}\quad\text{in }{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\times(0,\infty).

Proof.

By Proposition
3.1
,
\phi
is a supersolution in
|x|>2d
to the nonlocal equation in (
1.1
). Moreover,
\phi\geq u_{0}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
and
\phi(x)\geq u(x,t)
in
|x|\leq 2d
,
t>0
. Hence, by comparison (see Remark
2.1
), we have that
u(x,t)\leq\phi(x)
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
t>0
.
∎

Now, we obtain the time decay.

Proposition 3.2
.

There exists a constant
C_{p}
depending only on
p
such that

u(x,t)\leq C_{p}t^{-\frac{1}{p-1}}.

Proof.

Given any
t_{0}>0
, the function
g(t)=C_{p}(t+t_{0})^{-\frac{1}{p-1}}
, where
C_{p}=(p-1)^{-\frac{1}{p-1}}
, is a solution to the ODE
g^{\prime}(t)=-g(t)^{p}
. If
t_{0}
is small enough,
g(0)\geq\|u_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})}
. On the other hand, by Corollary
3.1
, given
T>0
there is some
\beta=\beta(T,p)
such that
g(t)\geq u(x,t)
if
t\in(0,T)
and
|x|\geq\beta
. Hence, by comparison (see Remark
2.1
),
g(t)\geq u(x,t)
for all
t\in(0,T)
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Letting
t_{0}\to 0^{+}
, we deduce that
u(x,t)\leq C_{p}t^{-\frac{1}{p-1}}
for all
t\in(0,T)
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
. As
T>0
is arbitrary, the result is proved. ∎

4.
Large-time behavior

We now prove our large-time behavior results. As announced in the Introduction, the first step is to scale the solution
u
to (
1.1
). The right scaling, given by (
1.5
), is suggested by the time-decay estimate for
u
in the previous section. We then obtain compactness, that gives the existence of convergent subsequences of the family
\{u_{\lambda}\}
. The third step, the most involved, is to identify uniquely the possible limits. The fourth and last step is to translate the convergence in
\lambda
of the functions
u_{\lambda}
into large-time results for the function
u
.

4.1.
Scaling

The first step is to choose the right scaling. Since we want to keep the absorption term and the diffusive character of the equation, we take

u_{\lambda}(x,t)=\lambda^{\frac{2}{p-1}}u(\lambda x,\lambda^{2}t),\quad\lambda>0.

The key point is that, thanks to the estimates (
3.1
) obtained in the previous section, we know that

u_{\lambda}(x,t)\leq C_{p}t^{-\frac{1}{p-1}},\qquad u_{\lambda}(x,t)\leq C_{0}|x|^{-\frac{2}{p-1}}.

In particular, the family
\{u_{\lambda}\}
is locally bounded in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\times(0,\infty)
.

4.2.
Compactness

Once we know that the family
\{u_{\lambda}\}
is locally bounded in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\times(0,\infty)
, its precompactness is obtained with
the same computations
as in
[
20
, Section 3]
. The functions
f
and
F
in that paper correspond here to
f(\lambda)=\lambda^{\frac{2}{p-1}}
and
F(\lambda)=1
. Hence, we have the following result.

Proposition 4.1
.

The family
\{u_{\lambda}\}
has a subsequence
\{u_{\lambda_{k}}\}
that converges uniformly on compact subsets of
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\times(0,\infty)
to a function
U
satisfying the semilinear local heat equation (
1.7
).

4.3.
Identification of the limit function
U

The next step is to characterize the possible limit functions
U
. We will prove that there is only one possible limit, so that convergence is not restricted to a subsequence.

We consider separately the cases
A>0
and
A=0
. When
A=0
we prove that
U=V
, where
V
is the unique nonnegative very singular solution to the semilinear heat equation (
1.7
), that is, the unique nonnegative solution to this equation satisfying (
1.9
). When
A>0
we prove that
U=U_{A}
, where
U_{A}
is the unique solution to (
1.7
) satisfying (
1.11
), that is, with
A|x|^{-\alpha}
as initial trace.

Remark 4.1
.

The existence of solutions
V
and
U_{A}
,
A>0
, to (
1.7
) with the desired behaviour as
t\to 0^{+}
had already been proved in
[
7
]
. A uniqueness proof for
U_{A}
can be found in
[
16
]
. As for the very singular solution
V
, uniqueness was only known within the class of
radially symmetric self-similar
functions; see
[
7
]
. This is not enough for our purposes, since we cannot guarantee
a priori
that the limit
V
has these two properties. However, starting from the uniqueness result in the restricted class, and repeating the arguments used in
[
17
]
to deal with the same issue for the fast diffusion equation, the uniqueness of a nonnegative very singular solution to (
1.7
) without further assumptions follows.

We start with the case
A=0
. We already know that all limit functions
U
are solutions to the semilinear local heat equation with absorption (
1.7
). The next step is then to prove that they have the very singular behaviour (
1.9
) as
t\to 0^{+}
. We start by showing that they go to 0 as
t\to 0^{+}
for all
x\neq 0
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
u_{0}
satisfy (
1.2
) with
A=0
. Let
U=\lim_{j\to\infty}u_{\lambda_{j}}
with
\lambda_{j}\to\infty
. Then,
U(x,t)\to 0
as
t\to 0^{+}
if
x\neq 0
.

Proof.

Let
x_{0}\neq 0
,
a<\frac{|x_{0}|}{2}
and
\lambda>\frac{4d}{|x_{0}|}
. Let
B
be the ball centered at
x_{0}
with radius
a
and
\varepsilon\leq\psi\in C^{2,\alpha}(\bar{B})
such that
\psi\equiv\varepsilon
in
B_{a/4}(x_{0})
,
\psi=M
in
B\setminus B_{a/2}(x_{0})
. Here
M\geq u_{\lambda}(x,t)
if
|x|>\frac{|x_{0}|}{4}
(recall that
u_{\lambda}\leq C_{0}|x|^{-\frac{2}{p-1}}
for every
\lambda>0
). Let
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\varepsilon\big(\frac{|x_{0}|}{2}\big)^{\frac{2}{p-1}}
. By (
1.2
),
u_{\lambda}(x,0)\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}|x|^{-\frac{2}{p-1}}\leq\varepsilon
in
B
if
\lambda
is large enough, so that
u_{\lambda}(x,0)\leq\psi(x)
in
B
.

Let now
v_{\lambda}
be the solution to

(4.1)

\begin{cases}v_{t}-{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}v=0&\mbox{in }B\times(0,\infty),\\
v=M&\mbox{in }({\mathbb{R}}^{N}\setminus B)\times(0,\infty),\\
v(x,0)=\psi(x)&\mbox{in }B.\end{cases}

Then, as
v_{\lambda}
is a supersolution to (
1.1
),
u_{\lambda}\leq v_{\lambda}
in
B\times(0,\infty)
. But, as proved in
[
10
]
,
v_{\lambda}\to V
as
\lambda\to\infty
, where
V
is the solution to

(4.2)

\begin{cases}V_{t}-\mathfrak{a}\Delta V=0&\mbox{in }B\times(0,\infty),\\
V=M&\mbox{in }\partial B\times(0,\infty),\\
V(x,0)=\psi(x)&\mbox{in }B.\end{cases}

Hence,
U\leq V
in
B\times(0,\infty)
, whence
\limsup_{t\to 0^{+}}U(x_{0},t)\leq\psi(x_{0})=\varepsilon
. As
U\geq 0
and
\varepsilon
is arbitrary,
\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}U(x_{0},t)=0
.
∎

Let us prove now that
\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\int U(x,t)\,{\rm d}x=\infty
. To this end, we will find a bound from below for
\int u_{\lambda}(x,t)\,{\rm d}x
. From the definition of
u_{\lambda}
we see that

\int u_{\lambda}(x,t)\,{\rm d}x=\lambda^{\frac{2}{p-1}-N}\int u(x,\lambda^{2}t)\,{\rm d}x.

To find a lower bound for this function, we construct a subsolution to
u
. So, let
L>0
and
v
the solution to

(4.3)

\begin{cases}v_{t}-{\mathcal{L}}v=-v^{p}\quad&\mbox{in }{\mathbb{R}}^{N}\times(0,\infty),\\
v(x,0)=L{\lambda^{N-\frac{2}{p-1}}}h(x)\quad&\mbox{in }{\mathbb{R}}^{N},\end{cases}

where

h(x)=u_{0}(x)\|u_{0}\|_{L^{1}(B_{a})}^{-1}\chi_{B_{a}},

with
a
large so that
\|u_{0}\|_{L^{1}(B_{a})}>0
. If
\lambda
is large, then
v(x,0)\leq u_{0}(x)
, whence
v(x,t)\leq u(x,t)
.

In the next proposition we find a bound of the
L^{1}
-norm of
v(\cdot,t)
.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
v
be the solution to (
4.3
). There exist
C_{0},\gamma>0
such that

\lambda^{\frac{2}{p-1}-N}\int v(x,\lambda^{2}t)\,{\rm d}x\geq L-C_{0}L^{p}\big[\lambda^{-2\gamma}+t^{\gamma}\big].

Proof.

From the equation we see that

\lambda^{\frac{2}{p-1}-N}\int v(x,\lambda^{2}t)\,{\rm d}x=L-\lambda^{\frac{2}{p-1}-N}\int_{0}^{\lambda^{2}t}\int v^{p}(x,s)\,{\rm d}x{\rm d}s.

Therefore, it is enough to get a good bound from above for
\int_{0}^{t}\int v^{p}(x,s)\,{\rm d}x{\rm d}s
. To this end, we use that
v
is a subsolution to the equation without absorption. Hence,

\displaystyle v(x,t)

\displaystyle\leq e^{-t}v(x,0)+\int W(x-y,t)v(y,0)\,{\rm d}y

\displaystyle\leq L{\lambda^{N-\frac{2}{p-1}}}\Big[e^{-t}h(x)+\int W(x-y,t)h(y)\,{\rm d}y\Big],

where
W
is the regular part of the fundamental solution (
\Gamma(\cdot,t)=e^{-t}\delta+W(\cdot,t)
; see
[
15
]
). To estimate the last integral we will use the following bounds for
W
,

(4.4)

W(x,t)\leq Ct,\qquad W(x,t)\leq C\frac{t}{|x|^{N+2}},\qquad W(x,t)\leq Ct^{-\frac{N}{2}}.

The first two were proved in
[
20
]
and the last one in
[
18
]
.

If
|x|<2a
, using the first bound for
W
in (
4.4
) we get

\int W(x-y,t)h(y)\,{\rm d}y\leq Ct.

On the other hand, if
|x|>2a
and
|y|<a
, then
|x-y|>\frac{|x|}{2}
. Notice that
h
vanishes if
|y|>a
. Hence, using the second bound in (
4.4
) we obtain

(4.5)

\int W(x-y,t)h(y)\,{\rm d}y\leq C\frac{t}{|x|^{N+2}}.

Using both integral estimates we conclude that (
4.5
) holds for every
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
. Finally, the third bound in (
4.4
) yields

\int W(x-y,t)h(y)\,{\rm d}y\leq Ct^{-\frac{N}{2}}.

Combining all the above estimates,

v(x,t)\leq L{\lambda^{N-\frac{2}{p-1}}}\Big[e^{-t}h(x)+Ct^{-\frac{N}{2}}\chi_{|x|<\sqrt{t}}+C\frac{t}{|x|^{N+2}}\chi_{|x|>\sqrt{t}}\Big],

whence

\displaystyle\int v^{p}(x,t)\,{\rm d}x

\displaystyle\leq CL^{p}{\lambda^{(N-\frac{2}{p-1})p}}\Big[e^{-pt}\int h^{p}(x)\,{\rm d}x+\int_{|x|<\sqrt{t}}t^{-p\frac{N}{2}}\,{\rm d}x+\int_{|x|>\sqrt{t}}\frac{t^{p}}{|x|^{p(N+2)}}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq CL^{p}{\lambda^{(N-\frac{2}{p-1})p}}\big[e^{-pt}+t^{(1-p)\frac{N}{2}}\big].

Thus, since
(p-1)\frac{N}{2}<1
, we have

\displaystyle\lambda^{\frac{2}{p-1}-N}\int_{0}^{\lambda^{2}t}\int v^{p}(x,s)\,{\rm d}x{\rm d}s

\displaystyle\leq CL^{p}\lambda^{(N-\frac{2}{p-1})(p-1)}\big[1+{(\lambda^{2}t)}^{1-\frac{N}{2}(p-1)}\big]

\displaystyle=CL^{p}\big[\lambda^{-2\gamma}+t^{\gamma}\big],

where
\gamma=1-\frac{N}{2}(p-1)>0
.
∎

As a corollary we get the divergence of the mass of
U
as
t\to 0^{+}
.

Corollary 4.1
.

Let
U=\lim\limits_{j\to\infty}u_{\lambda_{j}}
uniformly on compact sets. Then,

\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\int U(x,t)\,{\rm d}x=\infty.

Proof.

Let
v
be the solution to (
4.3
). By Proposition
4.3
,

\int u_{\lambda_{j}}(x,t)\,{\rm d}x\geq\int v_{\lambda_{j}}\,{\rm d}x\geq L-CL^{p}\big[\lambda_{j}^{-2\gamma}+t^{\gamma}\big].

Therefore, since
u_{\lambda_{j}}(x,t)\leq C|x|^{-\frac{2}{p-1}}
, then

\int_{|x|<R}u_{\lambda}(x,t)\,{\rm d}x\geq\int u_{\lambda}(x,t)\,{\rm d}x-CR^{N-\frac{2}{p-1}}\geq L-C\big[L^{p}t^{\gamma}+R^{N-\frac{2}{p-1}}\big].

Since
p<1+\frac{2}{N}
, letting first
j\to\infty
and then
R\to\infty
we obtain

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}U(x,t)\,{\rm d}x\geq L-CL^{p}t^{\gamma},

whence

\liminf_{t\to 0^{+}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}U(x,t)\,{\rm d}x\geq L.

As
L
is arbitrary, the corollary is proved.
∎

This result allows us to identify
U
as the unique nonnegative very singular solution
V
to the semilinear local heat equation (
1.7
). Therefore, convergence is not restricted to a subsequence.

Corollary 4.2
.

The whole family
\{u_{\lambda}\}
converges uniformly on compact sets to
V
.

Now, we turn to the case
A>0
. We know that all limit functions
U
are solutions to the semilinear local heat equation with absorption (
1.7
). Hence, to identify the limit it is enough to show that they converge towards
A{|x|^{-2/(p-1)}}
for all
x\neq 0
as
t\to 0^{+}
.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
u_{0}
satisfy (
1.2
) with
A>0
. Let
U=\lim_{j\to\infty}u_{\lambda_{j}}
uniformly in compact sets, with
\lambda_{j}\to\infty
. Then, for every
x\neq 0
,

U(x,t)\to A{|x|^{-2/(p-1)}}\quad\mbox{as }t\to 0^{+}.

Proof.

The proof follows the lines of that of Proposition
4.2
. As there, let
x_{0}\neq 0
,
M\geq 4^{3/(p-1)}|x_{0}|^{-2/(p-1)}\max\{A+\varepsilon,C_{0}\}
, where
C_{0}
is such that
u(x,t)\leq C_{0}|x|^{-2/(p-1)}
and
\varepsilon>0
is fixed but arbitrary. Then, if
\lambda
is large there holds that
u_{\lambda}\leq M
if
a-2d\lambda^{-1}<|x|
and
a=\frac{|x_{0}|}{4}
. Now, let
\psi\in C^{2,\alpha}(\overline{B})
with
B=B_{a}(x_{0})
be such that
\psi=M
close to
\partial B
,
\psi=(A+\varepsilon)|x|^{-\frac{2}{p-1}}
in
B_{a/2}(x_{0})
and let
v_{\lambda}
be the solution to (
4.1
). Since
|x|^{\frac{2}{p-1}}u_{0}(x)\to A
as
|x|\to\infty
, then

(A-\varepsilon){|x|^{-\frac{2}{p-1}}}\leq u_{\lambda}(x,0)\leq(A+\varepsilon){|x|^{-\frac{2}{p-1}}}\quad\mbox{in }B

if
\lambda
is large. Hence, by the choice of
M
,
u_{\lambda}\leq v_{\lambda}
if
\lambda
is large. As in Proposition
4.1
, we know that
v_{\lambda}\to V
uniformly in
\bar{B}\times[0,1]
where
V
is the solution to (
4.2
). Hence,
U\leq V
so that

\limsup_{t\to 0^{+}}U(x_{0},t)\leq(A+\varepsilon)|x_{0}|^{-\frac{2}{p-1}}.

Now we prove that

\liminf_{t\to 0^{+}}U(x_{0},t)\geq(A-\varepsilon)|x_{0}|^{-\frac{2}{p-1}}.

To this end, we construct a subsolution. So, this time we let
w_{\lambda}
be the solution to

\begin{cases}w_{t}-{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}w=-w^{p}&\mbox{in }B\times(0,1),\\
w=0&\mbox{in }({\mathbb{R}}^{N}\setminus B)\times(0,1),\\
w(x,0)=\widehat{\psi}(x)&\mbox{in }B,\end{cases}

where
\widehat{\psi}\in C^{2,\alpha}(\overline{B})
with
B=B_{a}(x_{0})
is such that
\widehat{\psi}=0
close to
\partial B
and
\widehat{\psi}=(A-\varepsilon)|x|^{-\frac{2}{p-1}}
in
B_{a/2}(x_{0})
.

In the next lemma, with arguments similar to those in
[
10
]
, we prove that
w_{\lambda}\to W
as
\lambda\to\infty
where
W
is the unique solution to

\begin{cases}W_{t}-\mathfrak{a}\Delta W=-W^{p}&\mbox{in }B\times(0,1),\\
W=0&\mbox{in }\partial B\times(0,1),\\
W=\widehat{\psi}&\mbox{in }B.\end{cases}

As
u_{\lambda}\geq w_{\lambda}
in
B\times(0,1)
if
\lambda
is large, there holds that
U\geq W
and hence,

\liminf_{t\to 0^{+}}U(x_{0},t)\geq(A-\varepsilon)|x_{0}|^{-2/(p-1)}.

As
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary, the proposition is proved. ∎

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
w_{\lambda}
and
W
as in the proof of Proposition
4.4
. Then,
w_{\lambda}\to W
as
\lambda\to\infty
in
B_{a}(x_{0})\times(0,1)
.

Proof.

Let
Z=w_{\lambda}-\bar{W}-\widetilde{C}\lambda^{-1}-\bar{C}\lambda^{-\alpha}t
and
F=w_{\lambda}^{p}-\bar{W}^{p}
, where
\bar{W}
is an extension of
W
as a
C^{2,\alpha}({\mathbb{R}}^{N}\times[0,1])
function. Here,
\widetilde{C}
is such that
|\bar{W}|\leq\widetilde{C}\lambda^{-1}
in
(B_{a+d\lambda^{-1}}(x_{0})\setminus B_{a}(x_{0}))\times[0,1])
and
\bar{C}
is such that
|\mathfrak{a}\Delta\bar{W}-{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}\bar{W}|\leq\bar{C}\lambda^{-\alpha}
in
\bar{B}\times[0,1]
. Then,

Z_{t}-{\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}Z=({\mathcal{L}}_{\lambda}\bar{W}-\mathfrak{a}\Delta\bar{W})-\bar{C}\lambda^{-\alpha}-w^{p}_{\lambda}+\bar{W}^{p}\leq-F(x,t)\quad\mbox{in }B_{a}(x_{0})\times(0,1).

Moreover,
Z=-\bar{W}-\widetilde{C}\lambda^{-1}-\bar{C}\lambda^{2}t\leq 0
in
(B_{a+d\lambda^{-1}}(x_{0})\setminus B_{a}(x_{0}))\times(0,1)
, and
Z(x,0)=-\widetilde{C}\lambda^{-1}\leq 0
in
B_{a}(x_{0})
. Since
Z>0
implies that
w_{\lambda}>\bar{W}
,
Z
is under the assumptions of Proposition
2.2
. Hence,
Z\leq 0
, so that

\limsup_{\lambda\to\infty}(w_{\lambda}-W)\leq 0\quad\mbox{in }B_{a}(x_{0})\times[0,1].

Analogously,
Z=\bar{W}-w_{\lambda}-\widetilde{C}\lambda^{-1}-\bar{C}\lambda^{-\alpha}t
,
F=\bar{W}^{p}-w_{\lambda}^{p}
are under the assumptions of Proposition
2.2
, so that

\limsup_{\lambda\to\infty}(W-w_{\lambda})\leq 0\quad\mbox{in }B_{a}(x_{0})\times[0,1].\qed

This identifies the limit function
U
as the unique nonnegative solution
U_{A}
to the semilinear local heat equation (
1.7
) such that
U(x,t)\to A|x|^{-2/(p-1)}
as
t\to 0^{+}
if
x\neq 0
. Hence, convergence is not restricted to a subsequence.

Corollary 4.3
.

The whole family
\{u_{\lambda}\}
converges uniformly on compact sets to
U_{A}
.

4.4.
Asymptotic results

Finally, using the selfsimilarity of the limit functions
V
and
U_{A}
, which makes them invariant under the scaling, we can translate the convergence of the family
\{u_{\lambda}\}
in compact sets into large-time asymptotic results for the solution to (
1.1
). We begin with the case
A=0
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
p\in(1,1+\frac{2}{N})
and
u_{0}\geq 0
,
u_{0}\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})
, such that
|x|^{\frac{2}{p-1}}u_{0}(x)\to 0
as
|x|\to\infty
. Let
V
be the unique very singular solution to (
1.7
) and
u
the unique solution to (
1.1
). Then, for every
K>0
,

(4.6)

t^{\frac{1}{p-1}}\|u(\cdot,t)-{V}(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{K\sqrt{t}})}\to 0\quad\mbox{as }t\to\infty.

Proof.

Since the very singular solution
V
to (
1.7
) has the selfsimilar form
V(x,t)=t^{-\frac{1}{p-1}}F(|x|t^{-\frac{1}{2}})
, see
[
7
]
, it is invariant under the scaling,
V_{\lambda}(x,t)=V(x,t)
. Hence, if
x=\lambda y
and
t=\lambda^{2}
, then

\displaystyle t^{\frac{1}{p-1}}\big(u(x,t)-V(x,t)\big)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{\frac{2}{p-1}}\big(u(\lambda y,\lambda^{2})-V(\lambda y,\lambda^{2})\big)=u_{\lambda}(y,1)-V_{\lambda}(y,1)

\displaystyle=u_{\lambda}(y,1)-V(y,1).

The uniform convergence

\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\|u_{\lambda}(\cdot,1)-V(y,1)\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{K})}

yields then (
4.6
), since
|x|=|y|\lambda=|y|\sqrt{t}\leq K\sqrt{t}
if
x=\lambda y
,
t=\lambda^{2}
, and
|y|<K
.
∎

The proof for the case
A>0
is similar.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
p\in(1,1+\frac{2}{N})
and
u_{0}\geq 0
,
u_{0}\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})
, such that
|x|^{\frac{2}{p-1}}u_{0}(x)\to A>0
as
|x|\to\infty
. Let
U_{A}
be the unique solution of the semilinear local heat equation (
1.7
) such that
U(x,t)\to A|x|^{-\frac{2}{p-1}}
as
t\to 0^{+}
if
x\neq 0
, and
u
the unique solution to (
1.1
). Then, for every
R>0
,

t^{\frac{1}{p-1}}\|u(x,t)-{U_{A}}(x,t)\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{R\sqrt{t}})}\to 0\quad\mbox{as }t\to\infty.

Proof.

As in the proof of Theorem
4.1
, the uniqueness of the solution
U_{A}
to the heat equation with absorption such that
U(x,t)\to A|x|^{-\frac{2}{p-1}}
as
t\to 0^{+}
if
x\neq 0
implies that
U_{A}
is invariant under the scaling, hence the result.
∎
