Application of a new assignment algorithm based on the minimax difference in earthquake emergency rescue
Authors: Sining Huang, Ying Zhang, Tiantian Qiao, Junwu Dai, Jinlong Liu, Yongqiang Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The primary challenge in earthquake emergency response is the effective dispatch of large-scale rescue teams to disaster areas after an earthquake disaster occurs, especially as this often involves time constraints and road network interruptions. To solve this, we propose a minimax difference submatrix (MDS) algorithm combined with k-means clustering. First, the k-means method is utilized to cluster the rescue locations with similar features, thereby transforming the non-standard assignment problem (rescue team \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ne$$\end{document} rescue location) into a solvable linear assignment problem (LAP). After obtaining the cost matrix, the principle of minimax difference is established; that is, the columns with the largest differences between the maximum and minimum values are selected in sequence for priority assignment. By comparing the results of numerical experiments, it can be seen that the MDS method reduces the computational load effectively compared with the Hungarian algorithm, and can obtain a high-quality approximate optimal solution for the linear assignment problem. Finally, through the case study of simulating the IX-X earthquake intensity scenario in City T, it is demonstrated that the MDS method is an efficient and stable approach for solving the problem of large-scale real-time rescue in earthquake emergencies.

Introduction

Earthquakes rank among the most destructive natural disasters, often resulting in profound loss of life and extensive damage to infrastructure. Their impact is typically manifested through the collapse of buildings, disruption of transportation networks, landslides, and tsunamis. China, due to its complex and diverse geological landscape, is especially susceptible to seismic hazards. Notable examples include the catastrophic 2008 Wenchuan earthquake and the 2013 Ya’an earthquake, both of which led to large-scale urban devastation1,2.In light of these challenges, the development and implementation of an effective emergency response mechanism are of paramount importance. Rapid deployment of rescue personnel and resources, along with the strategic assignment of limited emergency response assets, is vital for reducing casualties and economic losses and ensuring the overall efficacy of post-disaster relief operations.

A scientifically sound, accurate, and dependable emergency response within a dynamic decision-making framework fundamentally relies on the efficient resolution of assignment problems, a core topic in operations research. Assignment problems represent a class of global optimization challenges that are broadly applicable across various domains, including transportation, resource assignment, and the service industry. To address the global optimization of assignment problems, Kuhn introduced the Hungarian algorithm in 19573. This classical approach remains one of the most widely used classical solutions due to its ability to guarantee a globally optimal and unique solution. However, the computational demand associated with the Hungarian algorithm renders it suitable primarily for small-scale problems. Several other exact methods, including Bertsekas’ auction algorithm4,5, the enumeration method, and the branch and bound approach, have been developed to tackle assignment problems. Nevertheless, their practical use becomes constrained when applied to larger-scale instances due to their inherent computational complexity. Karsu and Azizoglu address a variant of the assignment problem aimed at minimizing the sum of squared loads across all agents and proposes a R&B algorithm that integrates a divide-and-conquer strategy with an approach for calculating bounds6. While this method proves effective in certain scenarios, it exhibits limitations when applied to large-scale problems. Specifically, the algorithm lacks effective pruning, which leads to high computational time and complexity.

In order to develop computational efficiency of solving assignment problems, heuristic algorithms employing approximate iterative algorithms have garnered significant attention in recent years. Techniques such as genetic algorithm7,8, simulated annealing algorithm9, objective function value algorithm10, deep greedy switching11,12 are commonly used. These approaches facilitate the attainment of locally optimal solutions, substantially lowering computational effort while broadening the range of practical applications. In addressing disaster rescue team assignment, Yan and Shih developed a mixed integer programming model based on spatio-temporal network technology and introduced a heuristic algorithm suitable for small-scale emergency maintenance problems13. However, this approach becomes computationally intensive and less effective for large-scale and complex-factor problems. Later, Wex et al. proposed a decision-making model for emergency operation centers using a heuristic algorithm to optimize rescue unit assignments, accounting for the dynamic emergence of new incidents14. Similarly, Zhang et al. formulated a multi-stage 0–1 nonlinear programming model that balanced disaster severity with geographical distance15. The NSGA-II evolutionary algorithm was employed to optimize the rescue strategies by balancing team capabilities and disaster severity, aiming for the highest distribution satisfaction under the influence of post-earthquake secondary disasters. Bodaghi et al. proposed a heuristic-based framework for multi-resource scheduling and routing during emergency recovery, effectively allocating both consumable and non-consumable resources16. Zeng et al. proposed an aggregation optimization algorithm based on edge coefficients to transform multi-center vehicle scheduling problems in several rescue centers into multiple single rescue problems17. However, due to rigid assignment constraints, the approach lacks adaptability to dynamic changes, potentially leading to suboptimal resource distribution and a reduced assignment algorithm. Almadi et al. developed a multi-stage mixed-integer programming model incorporating variables, such as time-based demand satisfaction, risk of secondary failure, and resource coordination18. While the model, solved using a commercial solver, aimed to maximize the number of trapped people rescued within a limited time, it failed to account for certain critical situational factors, limiting its practical applicability. Jia A introduced a hierarchical cooperative scheduling framework that integrates multi-level emergency resources and employs heuristic algorithms to optimize real-time data-driven task assignments, enhancing both responsiveness and accuracy19.

In summary, conventional assignment algorithms are often constrained by heavy computational loads and high time complexity. Under large-scale scenarios, they either exhibit low efficiency or fail to produce optimal solutions. Earthquake rescue operations demand rapid and large-scale assignment due to the extensive number of rescue teams and affected sites, all within strict time constraints. Consequently, developing a fast, scalable, and reliable assignment strategy is essential. This study addresses a non-standard assignment problem by incorporating post-earthquake road damage and introducing a clustering-based approach. It proposes an efficient and fast algorithm designed to optimize the dispatch of large-scale emergency rescue teams.

Minimax difference submatrix algorithm

Linear assignment problem

The linear assignment problem (LAP) is one of the fundamental combinatorial optimization problems within the field of operations research. Its objective is to determine an optimal one-to-one assignment between the elements of two sets such that the total cost of all assigned pairs is minimized. Specifically, it involves assigning n jobs in a manner that yields the lowest possible total cost, where the cost of assigning worker i to task j equals j. According to the MDP principle, Algorithm1 effectively mitigates residual large values in the cost matrix and approximates the global optimal solution. When the max–min differences in a few columns are equal, two index selection strategies may be applied. The first method involves randomly selecting one (or a pair) of the few equal maximum or minimum values. The second method considers all such max–min pairs and calculates the next step. In practice, the first method yields results close to the global optimal solution with lower computational cost. The second method generates several solutions, and since the number of equal values is typically small, we could choose the smallest one among a few paths. MDS algorithm

Further, the computational complexity of Algorithm1 can be divided into three parts. First, during the initialization phase, each column of the cost matrix is sorted, and the corresponding indices are recorded to determine the maximum and minimum values of the n-column vector. Second, after selecting the specified element based on the maximum-minimum difference principle, the corresponding column and row are removed from the matrix. Third, the max–min differences in the updated matrix are recalculated to guide the subsequent selection process. To improve efficiency, index tracking is used in place of re-sorting, thereby avoiding redundant computations. Fourth, the sum of the comparisons between the maximum and minimum values for each column of the cost matrix in every iteration was calculated. The computation complexity of Algorithm1 is determined by the sorting workload of n-ary arrays, which is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(n^{2}*\log (n))$$\end{document}, the sum of all cycles of each column element shall not exceed n, which is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(n^{2})$$\end{document}, and the sum of comparison times, which is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(n^{2})$$\end{document}, that is, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(n^{2}*\log (n))$$\end{document}.

The exact solution has large computational complexity, making it suitable only for small-scale LAP. For instance, the Hungarian algorithm has a computational complexity of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(n^{3})$$\end{document}, while both the dynamic programming algorithm and the enumeration method exhibit a complexity of O(n!). The branch definition method is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$O(n^{2}*2^{n})$$\end{document}, depending on the problem scale. Therefore, the overall computational complexity of Algorithm 1 is relatively low by comparison. Although its complexity may not be lower than that of heuristic algorithms, Algorithm 1 demonstrates superior stability and convergence, which are often difficult to achieve with heuristic approaches.

A dataset was randomly generated for n = 100, and the assignment results obtained using the MDS algorithm were compared with those from the classical Hungarian algorithm. The results indicate that when n = 10, the relative error remained around 5%, while for n = 100, it stabilized at approximately 6%. K-means clustering and seismic damage assessment

K-means clustering

Nonstandard assignment problem (m>n) characterized by an unequal matching problem can be transformed into standard assignment problem through a clustering method. This conversion enables the application of the LAP model, as represented by Eq.(1)20,21. K-means algorithm is one of the most widely used methods22–24. K-means clustering algorithm

In the post-earthquake emergency rescue stage, a common challenge involves a non-standard assignment problem arising from a mismatch in the number of personnel and the search and rescue work. To address this issue, the larger group, typically the rescue personnel, can be initially partitioned using the K-means clustering based on predefined rescue standards. For the imbalance of rescue forces caused by uneven cluster sizes resulting from clustering, the MDS algorithm can be re-applied to perform secondary assignment between regions with surplus rescue forces and those with shortages. Given the extremely low time cost of this algorithm, the secondary dispatch will not affect the rescue timeliness.

Seismic damage assessment of traffic components

The effectiveness of a city’s post-earthquake emergency response, such as the resident evacuation and the search and rescue of the injured, largely depends on the extent of damage to the road network components and the overall connectivity of the transportation network system25–27.Assessing earthquake-induced damage to transportation infrastructure allows for the quantification of functional losses within the traffic system. This assessment is a critical component of both pre-disaster planning for earthquake mitigation and post-disaster emergency management28.

As a key project in the transportation system, bridges are often the primary source of traffic disruptions29,30. following an earthquake. To assess their vulnerability, a bridge seismic disaster assessment model, based on the reliable and widely adopted statistical regression method, categorizes the bridge damage into five levels: basically intact, slightly damaged, moderately damaged, severely damaged, and completely damaged. Conclusion

Considering post-earthquake road traffic capacity, the K-means clustering algorithm is employed to convert the non-standard LAP in search and rescue assignment into a standard LAP. To address this issue, the MDS algorithm is proposed, which provides a high-quality, approximate global optimal solution with minimal computational cost. Specifically, for large-scale LAP, the computational workload of the classical exact solution and the balance between the accuracy and iteration count of the heuristic algorithms must be considered. Numerical simulation and case studies fully demonstrate the applicability and high efficiency of the MDS algorithm in large-scale post-earthquake rescue assignments. The MDS algorithm, based on the minimax difference principle, employs an error-reducing strategy to identify the optimal path. While the global optimal solution may not always be achieved for large-scale problems, the algorithm can reliably produce an approximate global optimal solution, and the error margin is about 5% for small-scale problems (n=10) and about 6% for large-scale problems (n=100).Theoretical analysis and experimental comparisons indicate that when addressing the large-scale LAP problems, exact solution methods often suffer from low success rates due to high computational demands. In contrast, the MDS algorithm maintains a high success rate with relatively small errors and demonstrates a clear advantage in computational efficiency.The MDS method is further applied to the actual earthquake search and rescue case, fully considering path passability. It enables rapid generation of effective assignment schemes during the large-scale seismic data information processing. With minimal computational cost, the assignment algorithm broadens its application scope and integrates real-time data efficiently.Despite these merits, the study has several inherent limitations. The algorithm only optimizes for the single objective of rescue distance, ignoring practical factors such as post-earthquake road travel speed and traffic congestion, leading to idealized path calculation results. In addition, the case study is limited to the IX and X seismic intensity scenarios in City T, thus the algorithm’s applicability in low and ultra-high seismic intensity scenarios remains unverified. Future research will focus on constructing a multi-objective optimization model with practical constraints, and expanding case studies across diverse seismic intensity scenarios to further refine the algorithm and clarify its practical application boundaries.
