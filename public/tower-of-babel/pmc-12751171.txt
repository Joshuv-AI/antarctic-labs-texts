Integration of mRNA and MiRNA analysis reveals the molecular mechanisms of testicular development in newborn and adult sheep
Authors: Binpeng Xi, Zengkui Lu, Shengguo Zhao, Rui Zhang, Jianye Li, Xuejiao An, Yaojing Yue
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFecundity limits sheep industry development, but crossbreeding can help. Yet, mechanisms of testis development in Southdown × Hu F1, especially differential mechanisms at key stages, are rarely studied. Recent miRNA-mRNA analyses have enhanced our understanding of testicular sexual development stages. We conducted histological, physiological, and transcriptomic analyses on neonatal (M0) and adult (Y1) testis tis-sues of Southdown × Hu F1. Results showed Y1 testes were fully developed with more germ cells than M0. miRNA-seq identified 405 differentially expressed miRNAs (DEMIs) (43 existing, 212 known from 175 families, and 150 novel), and mRNA-seq found 9794 differentially expressed mRNAs (DEMs). miRNA-mRNA integration analysis yielded 20 miRNA-target gene pairs (20 DEMIs and 145 DEMs). Key pathways like Rap1 signaling, ECM-receptor interaction, and MAPK were identified. miR-29a, miR-544-z, and miR-128-z were found to regulate ITGB1, MAP3K13, and Rap1 respectively, along with key genes KITLG, GATA4, and CDC42 in testicular development, affecting male reproduction. This study clarifies testicular development mechanisms and offers candidate genes for molecular breeding.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12290-6.Keywords: Southdown × Hu F1 sheep, MicroRNA, High-throughput sequencing, Co-expression

Introduction

Ovine species constitute a significant portion of global livestock populations, where reproductive efficiency serves as a critical determinant of economic productivity in animal husbandry systems. The predominant seasonal breeding patterns and single-lambing traits observed in most sheep breeds impose biological constraints on production outcomes [1]. As the primary male reproductive organ in mammals, testes perform dual physiological functions through spermatogenesis and androgen secretion, processes that are fundamentally critical for maintaining male fertility [2]. Therefore, it is of great scientific significance to understand the biological process and molecular mechanism of testicular development.

The testis undergoes coordinated developmental processes involving Sertoli cell proliferation/maturation and spermatogenesis initiation. This multistage differentiation systematically transforms diploid spermatogonia into haploid spermatozoa through precisely regulated proliferative and developmental transitions [3]. Spermatogenesis fundamentally depends on molecular orchestration of gene-hormone net-works, with genetic regulators exerting predominant control over this developmental cascade [4]. Najmeh Salehi et al. showed significant heterogeneity in the testicular somatic and germ cell populations, with the highest diversity observed in Sertoli and myoid cells as well as spermatogonia, spermatocytes, and spermatid germ cells [5]. The production of male gametes is characterized by extensive transcriptional activity, which generates not only messenger RNAs (mRNAs) encoding proteins but also a variety of non-coding RNAs, including microRNAs (miRNAs) [6]. The miRNAs regulate gene expression by degrading or inhibiting target genes through specific binding to the 3’ untranslated region (UTR) of mRNA. They play critical roles in various biological and metabolic processes, including growth, development, and reproduction [7]. Ac-cumulating evidence indicates that miRNAs play a crucial role in testicular development and spermatogenesis. These processes exhibit distinct stage-specific characteristics, and miRNA expression dynamically changes throughout testicular development and the progression of spermatogenic cells [8]. In recent years, advancements in next-generation sequencing (NGS) technologies and bioinformatics have significantly enhanced the identification and understanding of miRNAs [9]. Although miRNAs are small (18-24nt), they play an important role in the regulation of mammalian repro-duction by regulating the expression of their target genes, and miRNAs expressed in Sertoli and germ cells have been shown to regulate their proliferation and differentiation. For example, La et al. studied the expression patterns of small and medium-sized RNA in the testicular tissue of yaks at 6, 18 and 30 months of age and found that miRNAs were differentially expressed at different developmental stages, targeting genes such as BMP2 to regulate spermatogenesis [10]. miR-202 is highly expressed in mouse SSC and is oppositely regulated by GDNF and retinoic acid, which promote self-renewal. miR-202 contributes to the maintenance of SSC because knockdown of miR-202 in SSC decreases stem cell activity, initiates premature differentiation and in-creases mitosis and apoptosis. Knockdown of miR-202 target RNA binding FOX-1 homologue (Rbfox2) blocked meiotic initiation in cultured SSC [11]. These studies have greatly expanded our understanding of the mechanisms underlying testicular phasic sexual development.

Strategic crossbreeding has become pivotal for genetic improvement in animal husbandry, where interspecific hybridization enhances growth performance, carcass traits, and disease resilience-key drivers for modern meat production systems [12]. The Southdown × Hu F1 hybrid demonstrates exceptional agricultural value through early sexual maturity, enhanced fertility, superior muscle development, and optimized slaughter yields, establishing it as an elite hybrid model [13]. Hu sheep [14] is famous in China for its strong fertility and adaptability, excelling as maternal parents in cross-breeding in China [15]. Current evidence demonstrates superior Southdown × Hu F1 hybrid sheep performance in body weight, average daily gain, growth efficiency, and carcass traits compared to Hu sheep [16]. However, the related research on the cross combination of Southdown × Hu F1 sheep is mainly reflected in the production performance, while the research on the potential mechanism of the testicular reproductive performance of Southdown × Hu F1 sheep is relatively few. Therefore, it is important to analyze the mechanism of testicular development in sheep using molecular biological methods.

Our previous research identified significant disparities in mRNA expression, miRNA, and m6A gene regulation between neonatal (M0) and adult (Y1) groups across various developmental stages [17]. In this study, we utilized an integrated miRNA-mRNA analysis combined with bioinformatics approaches to investigate the transcriptome profiles of neonatal and adult sheep testes. Phenotypic changes in testes and germ cells were systematically documented, and reproductive parameters were evaluated in both groups. To uncover the underlying molecular mechanisms, six small RNA sequencing libraries and six mRNA libraries were constructed from three bio-logical replicates, providing novel insights into the regulatory dynamics of miRNA-targeted mRNAs during sheep testicular development and spermatogenesis. This research aims to enhance our understanding of the complex molecular processes governing ovine testicular maturation. Transcriptome analysis revealed a wealth of candidate miRNAs and mRNAs that are potentially critical for the development and genetic improvement of sheep germplasm resources.

Materials and methods

Ethical statementigures

This study has been approved by the Animal Ethics Committee (Permit No. SYXK2019-010) of Lanzhou Institute of Animal Science and Veterinary Medicine, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences (Lanzhou, China).

Animal handling and sample collection

Six Southdown × Hu F1 hybrid rams were surgically castrated under standardized protocols at a commercial breeding facility (Qinghuan Meat Sheep Breeding Co., Gansu, China). Anesthesia was induced through sequential administration of diaze-pam (410 mg, i. m.; Ankang Pharmaceutical) and scopolamine (90.3 mg, i.m.; Cheme Gen), followed by intravenous sodium thiopental (10–20 mg/kg; SPH Xinasia Pharmaceutical). Three neonatal (0-month, designated M0-1/2/3) and three postnatal 1-year-old (Y1-1/2/3) lambs were selected based on verified breeding records. Immediately anesthesia testicular tissues were collected with longitudinal dissection, with the right testes flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen (< 3 min post-excision) for molecular analyses (RNA/protein extraction; stored at −80℃) and left testes fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde (8 h) for histological processing through graded ethanol dehydration, paraffin embedding, and microtome sectioning (5 μm).

Sample measurements were performed with RNA extraction

Frozen samples were subjected to physiological measurements and RNA extraction. The area and length of the samples were quantified using ImageJ software. Total RNA was extracted using the Steady Pure Universal RNA Extraction Kit (TianGen, Beijing, China), quantified with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and assessed for integrity via Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). The RNA integrity number (RIN) was determined to be greater than 8.0.

Small RNA sequencing and analysis

For miRNA sequencing, total RNA was extracted, and 18-30nt RNA fragments were enriched through polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE). Subsequently, a 3’ adapter was ligated to enrich for 36-44nt RNA, followed by the addition of a 5’ adapter. Reverse transcription was then used to amplify PCR products ranging from 140 to 160 bp. Sequencing was carried out on the Illumina Hi-Seq™ 2500 platform by Gene Denovo Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Clean tags were mapped against the Gene Bank (Release 209.0) and R fam (Release 11.0) databases to filter out rRNA, scRNA, snoRNA, snRNA, and tRNA sequences. Tags aligning to repeat sequences or exons/introns were also excluded. Known miRNAs were identified by matching tags to the miRBase database (Release 21) [18], while novel miRNAs were predicted using Mireap_v0.2 software based on their genomic positions and hairpin structures.

mRNA sequencing and analysis

Following total RNA extraction, mRNA was enriched using Oligo(dT) beads, and cDNA was synthesized via reverse transcription with random primers. The cDNA li-brary was constructed using DNA polymerase I, RNase H, dNTPs, and appropriate buffer. cDNA fragments were subsequently purified using the Qia Quick PCR extrac-tion kit, underwent end-repair, were poly(A)-tailed, and ligated to Illumina sequencing adapters. Sequencing was conducted on the Illumina Hi-Seq™ 2500 platform, producing paired-end reads. Clean reads were obtained by filtering out low-quality, adapter-containing, and poly-N sequences. These reads were then mapped to the Oar_rambouillet_v1.0 reference genome [19].

Construction of differentially expressed MiRNAs (DEMIs) -target differentially expressed mRNA (DEMs) network

DEMs were identified using fragments per kilobase per million reads (FPKM) and statistically compared using the IDEG6 web tool. DEMs were defined as those with a false discovery rate (FDR) FDR < 0.01, |log2FC| ≥ 2, based on three biological replicates. For miRNAs, DEMIs (differentially expressed miRNAs) were defined by an FDR < 0.05, |log2FC| ≥ 1. Target genes of DEMIs were predicted using Patmatch (v1.2) software, and negatively correlated DEMI-target gene pairs were visualized using Adobe Illustrator CS6 (San Jose, USA) software.

Functional analysis of DEMs

Gene Ontology (GO) (http://www.geneontology.org) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway (http://www.genome.jp/kegg) databases were utilized for functional enrichment analyses. Differential gene sets were evaluated against a background gene list using Cluster Profiler, with statistical significance determined via the hypergeometric distribution principle. Benjamini-Hochberg correction was applied to the enrichment results to control the false discovery rate (FDR), and pathways/terms with FDR < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) analysis

To authenticate the transcriptome sequencing data, actin and U6 snRNA were employed as reference genes for mRNA and miRNA, respectively. A total of eight DEMs and DEMIs were selected for qRT-PCR validation. Primers were meticulously designed using Primer 5.0 software (Table S1) and synthesized by Sangon Biotechnology. Reverse transcription was performed utilizing Fast King gDNA RT Super Mix for mRNA and the miRNA First-Strand Synthesis Kit for miRNA. Quantitative PCR was executed with the TB Green Premix Ex Taq II kit in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate, and the relative expression levels were calculated employing the 2−ΔΔCt method [20].

Results

Morphological and physiological characteristics of sheep during neonatal and adult periods

The testicular volume and diameter of Y1 group were higher than those of M0 group. The tunica albuginea of Y1 group developed obviously, and so did the connective tissues such as blood vessels. The results of H&E staining are shown in Fig. 1A: Compared with the M0 group, the cross section of seminiferous tubules in the testis tissue of Y1 sheep was significantly larger and formed a large cavity. The testis tissue was mainly composed of seminiferous tubules and the interstitial parts between seminiferous tubules. In the M0 group, the spermatogenic cells in the seminiferous tubules were only composed of germ cells, spermatogonia and Sertoli cells. Compared with the M0 group, in the Y1 group, the seminiferous tubules were significantly differentiated, the Sertoli cells were significantly larger, and a large number of cell types at different stages appeared (spermatogonia, spermatocytes, spermatids and spermatozoa were successively distributed from the basement membrane to the lumen). Compared with the M0 group, the number of layers of spermatogenic cells in the testis of the Y1 group was significantly stratified, and the number of sperm near the lumen was significantly increased. Adult sheep testis diameter of seminiferous tubules, cross-sectional area of seminiferous tubule, spermatogonia area, primary spermatocyte area, number of spermatogonia, the number of primary spermatocytes and other indicators were significantly higher than those in the neonatal period, but the number of Sertoli cells tended to be stable (Fig. 1B).

Overview of RNA sequencing data

Between 75.89% and 81.95% of uniquely mapped clean reads were used for further analyses (Table 1).

Analysis of DEMIs and DEMs

Most of the DEMIs length was 22nt, and the correlation coefficient between the M0 group samples was very high (0.995 ~ 0.976), indicating that the gene expression pattern was highly consistent within the groups. The correlation coefficient of Y1 group was also higher (0.986 ~ 0.985), but slightly lower than that of M0 group, and the correlation coefficient between M0 and Y1 group was lower (for example, M0-1 and Y1-1 were 0.926), but still showed a strong positive correlation, indicating that there were significant differences in expression between the two groups, but the overall trend was consistent. As sheep develop, the number of DEMIs and DEMs continues to increase (Fig. 2B). M0 was significantly separated from the Y1 group on PC1(Fig. 2C), a total of 405 DEMIs were identified, of which 78.7% were up-regulated and 21.3% were down-regulated. There were 9794 DEMs in total, of which 53.79% were up-regulated and 46.21% down-regulated (Fig. 2D and E). The heat map showed significant differences in the expression levels of all miRNAs and mRNAs (Fig. 2H and I).

Gene set enrichment analysis

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) found that the target genes of differential mRNA and differential miRNA in the M0 vs. Y1 comparison group were enriched in pathways related to spermatogenesis. These include germ cell development, male gamete generation, motile cilium, sperm flagellum, spermatid development, and spermatid differentiation, spermatogenesis, and the enrichment trends of these path-ways were similar (Fig. 5C and D).

Differential key pathway analysis

Three important pathways between newborn and adulthood were found, including Rap1 signaling pathway, ECM-receptor interaction and MAPK signaling pathway. In ECM-receptor interaction, Integrin subunit beta 1(ITGB1), KIT pro-to-oncogene (KIT) and KIT ligand (KITLG) were downregulated in adult sheep. This enhances the role of KITLG in Leydig cells of the testis. In addition, the expression pattern of the Leydig cell marker gene Insulin like 3 (INSL3) was positively correlated with the above genes (Fig. 6A). Notably, Occludin (OCLN), a blood-testis barrier marker gene, and sperm function marker genes in the MAPK signaling pathway were consistent with the expression pattern of GATA binding protein 4(GATA4), a differential gene mediated by this pathway (Fig. 6B). In Rap1 signaling pathway and MAPK signaling pathway, Superoxide dismutase 2(SOD2), Cell division cycle 42(CDC42) and VANGL planar cell polarity protein 2(Vangl2) were all up-regulated in adult sheep, these two genes positively regulate spermatid polarity and sperm motility. DEMs such as Histone H3 and TBC1 domain family member 21 (TBC1D21) were positively correlated with the expression of CDC42 (Fig. 6C).

Discussion

Ovine reproductive capacity is fundamentally governed by multi-layered regulatory networks orchestrating testicular maturation and spermatogenesis [21, 22]. Leveraging NGS platforms [23], we systematically characterized developmental transitions in testicular histomorphology, germ cell populations, and associated biochemical parameters between neonatal and adult stages. Integrated miRNA-mRNA transcriptomics of testicular tissues revealed stage-specific regulatory architectures, with computational network modeling identifying functionally validated miRNA-mRNA regulatory axes critical for germline development. Pathway enrichment analysis aligned these molecular signatures with physiological maturation patterns, delineating coordinated endocrine-transcriptional circuits controlling spermatogenic progression. This mechanistic framework advances our understanding of molecular determinants underlying ovine fertility, providing strategic targets for germplasm optimization.

As a critical class of small non-coding RNAs, miRNAs mediate post-transcriptional gene regulation in most eukaryotes and are extensively implicated in the regulatory mechanisms underlying male reproductive processes in animals [24–26]. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the expression and regulatory functions of miRNAs in neonatal and adult ram testes. By high-throughput sequencing, we obtained a large number of small RNA sequences from M0 and Y1 testes. Subsequently, these sequences were compared with those in the miRBase database to identify potential miRNA candidates and explore their roles in testicular development [27], we obtained many existing and known miRNAs. Of these, 787 known miRNAs are known from 493 different miRNA families. Among these miRNA families, miR-506 [28], miR-322 [29], miR-122, miR-221 [30], miR-34 [31], miR-449 [32], miR-290 [6] and miR-21 [33] have been shown to play a role in testicular development or spermatogenesis in other animals in previous studies. Here, we report that the above families may be involved in the mechanism of testicular development in sheep. Additionally, we identified a large number of novel miRNAs, of which 415 were classified as DMEIs. The roles and functions of these miRNAs in testicular development across other species warrant further exploration in future studies (Table S2).

A total of 9794 DEMs including 5268 up-regulated genes and 4526 down-regulated genes were identified by dynamic mRNA expression analysis in the testis of M0 and Y1 sheep. KEGG analysis revealed that up-regulated and down-regulated genes were significantly enriched in certain pathways, these included Rap1 signaling pathway, Hippo signaling pathway, ECM-receptor interaction and MAPK signaling pathway. This result is consistent with our expectations, as differences in relevant biological data suggest that these three pathways may play a role in testicular development. Keisuke Okada et al. reported the key function of RA-GEF-2/Rapgef6, the key factors of the Rap1 signaling pathway, in spermatogenesis [34]. Recent chick spermatogonial stem cell studies have shown that retinoic acid plays a key role in the formation of chick spermatogonial stem cells by regulating ECM-receptor interaction through the transcriptional levels of COL5A1, COL5A2, and COL3A1 [35]. It is worth noting that the expression levels of MST1/2, LATS1/2 and YAP1 in the male reproductive tract and sperm of Hu sheep were significantly in-creased during testicular maturation, which also confirmed the study in this paper [36]. As a classical pathway, MAPK signaling pathway plays an important role in testicular development and spermatogenesis. For example, p38MAPK plays a key role in the regulation of spermatogenesis, the fate determination of pro-Sertoli cells and primordial germ cells during embryonic development, the proliferation of testicular cells after birth, and the function of mature Sertoli cells and Leydig cells [37]. Lokesh Kumar et al. highlighted the important role of mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs) in the management and play of signaling pathways during mammalian male reproduction, including the development of germ cells in the testis and the physiological maturation of sperm in the epididymis [38]. These studies confirm that the Rap1, Hippo, ECM-receptor interaction, and MAPK signaling pathways play key roles in the distinct testicular development observed in newborn versus adult sheep.

In recent studies, the application of integrated miRNA-mRNA analysis has ex-tended the current understanding of the mechanisms of testicular development [39–42]. In this study, we obtained several miRNA-target gene pairs related to spermatogenesis and testicular development, and constructed a regulatory network map by correlation analysis of DEMIs and DEMs. A large number of studies have shown that some miRNA target genes are related to the MAPK signaling pathway [43, 44]. Interestingly, we found that two other important pathways, Rap1 signaling pathway and ECM-receptor interaction, were also enriched for some target genes negatively regulated by miRNAs. The differential expression of these target genes in newborn and adult sheep helps to understand the role of miRNAs in the mechanisms related to animal testicular development.

At present, it is generally believed that the generation of sperm depends on the interaction of various cells and related marker genes in the testicular tissue, and they are also considered to be important candidates to become key factors affecting testicular development through gene interaction [45]. As DEMs, KITLG and c-kit gene ex-pression are of great significance for the normal migration and proliferation of primordial germ cells (PGCs) during mammalian embryonic development [46]. KIT/ITGB1 acts as a precursor of KITLG. KIT is continuously expressed in fetal testicular stromal cells, and the KIT+ region is alternating with the anti-Mullerian hormone positive region, which greatly improves the steroidogenic ability of fetal bovine testicular stromal cells [47]. In this study, the signaling complex KIT/ITGB1 was activated, and the coding protein gene KITLG was down-regulated in adulthood, as was the interstitial cell marker gene INSL3, especially in adult sheep. According to biological data [48], the number of Leydig cells in the testis of sheep reaches a peak in the neonatal period, and its content is positively correlated with gene expression of KITLG, KIT and ITGB1. A large number of studies have shown that the MAPK signaling pathway plays a key role in sperm maturation and the maintenance of testicular microecology [37–49]. In our study, the expression of GATA4 in M0 was significantly higher than that in Y1 mediated by p38MAPK, and the expression of Blood-testis barrier (BTB) marker gene OCLN was positively correlated with GATA4. Anja Schrade [50] et al. Notably, GATA4 conditional knockout in murine SCs significantly compromises BTB integrity and spermatogenic homeostasis, providing experimental validation of our mechanistic observations. Mechanistically, SC polarity emerges as a critical regulator of germ cell development and testicular functional maintenance, with its disruption directly linked to compromised BTB dynamics and aberrant spermatogenic progression. The correct positioning of polarity protein complexes and the organization of the cytoskeleton are used to maintain phase function and ensure the normal progress of spermatogenesis [51]. Rap1 formed a complex with VAV2 and Tian1 to interact with CDC42. It is note-worthy that SOD2, a sperm motility marker, and Vangl2, a testicular structural maintenance gene, were positively correlated with CDC42, suggesting that CDC42 activity is important for the maintenance of spermatogenesis.

Conclusions

In this study, we comprehensively analyzed the changes in mRNA and miRNA profiles in the testis of Southdown × Hu F1 sheep during the neonatal and adult periods. Overall, 9794 mRNAs and 405 miRNAs were differentially expressed at different developmental stages. The overlapping DEMs between the two groups suggest that testicular development can utilize similar strategies, such as MAPK signaling pathway, ECM-receptor interaction and PI3K-Akt signaling pathway play important roles in spermatogenesis. However, our GO and KEGG pathway enrichment results of the top 20 negatively regulated target genes of DEMIs and DEM showed that Rap1 signaling pathway, ECM receptor and MAPK pathway were especially enriched in the comparison group. In addition, miR-29a, miR-544-z and miR-128-z were identified to regulate ITGB1, MAP3K13 and Rap1, respectively, and key genes KITLG, GATA4 and CDC42 in testicular development to regulate male testis reproduction. The results provide basic data for further analysis of the molecular mechanisms in sheep and also provide a theoretical basis for optimizing sheep breeding. Future studies could further investigate the functional roles of the identified miRNAs and signaling pathways in testicular development and fertility regulation.
