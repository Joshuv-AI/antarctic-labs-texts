Permanent Cardiac Pacing in Children: A 30‐Year Follow‐Up Retrospective Study in a Single Pediatric Asian Center
Authors: Jiahui Charmaine Chan, Dyan Zhewei Zhang, Koh Zhi Xuan, Sreekanthan Sundararaghavan, Jonathan Tze Liang Choo, Kah Leng Ho, Eric Tien Siang Lim, Ju Le Tan, Nurhafizah Binte Abdul Aziz, Wan Ting Chan, Monika Kotecha, Jan Hau Lee, Teng Hong Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPermanent cardiac pacing in children is commonly indicated in high‐grade atrioventricular block. These patients require long‐term ventricular pacing (Vp), multiple device changes, and lead revisions.ObjectivesTo describe a single pediatric center's 30‐year experience with permanent pacemaker implantation, focusing on long‐term device‐associated complications (DACs) and pacing‐induced cardiomyopathy (PIC).MethodsWe conducted a retrospective observational study at KK Women's and Children's Hospital, Singapore. Patients with permanent pacemakers followed between January 1, 1994 and December 31, 2024 were included. Primary outcomes were DAC and PIC.ResultsFifty‐three patients underwent 113 pacemaker lead implantations; 51 (96.2%) had epicardial systems. Median follow‐up was 12 years (interquartile range [IQR] 9–19 years). Median age at first pacemaker implantation was 16 months (IQR 2 months to 7.7 years). Congenital complete heart block was the most common indication (26 [49%]), followed by postoperative complete heart block (17 [32%]). Freedom from lead‐associated complications was 85% and 83% at 5 and 10 years, respectively. Eight (15%) patients developed PIC. All patients with PIC had concomitant congenital heart disease (CHD) (8/8). CHD was present in 28.9% (13/45) of patients without PIC. Recovery of ventricular function occurred in two patients following pacing modification, but not in those managed with medical therapy alone.ConclusionIn this pediatric cohort with predominantly epicardial pacing systems, long‐term DAC rates were acceptable. PIC occurred primarily in patients with CHD. These findings support careful pacing strategy selection and long‐term surveillance in children requiring permanent pacing.

Introduction

Permanent cardiac pacing in the pediatric population is commonly indicated in high‐grade or complete atrioventricular block (AVB), which may be congenital or acquired following surgical repair of underlying congenital heart disease (CHD) [1]. Untreated symptomatic bradycardia can result in significant morbidity, including heart failure, failure to thrive, syncope, and sudden death [1].

Although pediatric pacemakers account for less than 1% of all implantations, this population is uniquely important. Children require decades of chronic ventricular pacing (Vp), often necessitating multiple device and lead revisions [2]. They often require an epicardial pacing system, which is associated with higher rates of lead failure compared to transvenous pacing [3]. Additionally, the relatively large size of pulse generators compared to body size may increase the risk of pacemaker pocket infections. Chronic right ventricular (RV) pacing disrupts physiological ventricular activation, causing ventricular dyssynchrony and cardiac dysfunction, especially in patients with CHD [4, 5].

Data on long‐term outcomes of children with permanent pacemakers in South‐East Asia are limited. This study aimed to describe our center's experience with pediatric pacemaker implantation over three decades, focusing on long‐term device‐associated complications (DACs) and pacing‐induced cardiomyopathy (PIC).

Methods

Study Design and Population

This was a retrospective observational study conducted at KK Women's and Children's Hospital, Singapore, a tertiary referral center for pediatric cardiology and cardiothoracic surgery in Singapore, performing approximately 100 pediatric cardiac surgeries annually [6]. At our center, pacemaker implantations are typically performed using epicardial leads. Transvenous implantation is considered in selected patients, with a minimum age of 17 years and weight of 40 kg. Pediatric patients with permanent pacemakers followed between January 1, 1994 and December 31, 2024 were included.

Patient Characteristics

Patient characteristics extracted include: age and weight at first pacemaker implantation, sex, ethnicity, pacing indication, presence of CHD, and preimplantation left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF). EF was assessed using transthoracic echocardiography using motion‐mode imaging and biplane Simpson method where available.

Only cardiac lesions requiring catheter‐based or surgical interventions were classified as CHD; patent ductus arteriosus and patent foramen ovale were excluded. CHD was classified as simple, moderate, and complex CHD according to the 2025 guidelines for management of adults with CHD [7].

Pacemaker Implantation and Device Characteristics

Details collected for pacemaker implantation included lead type (epicardial or transvenous), Vp site, and initial pacing mode. DAC were categorized into lead‐level events (lead fracture, significant threshold rise requiring lead revision, lead infection), analyzed using the total number of implanted leads as the denominator. Patient‐ and procedure‐level events (pacemaker pocket site infection and mediastinitis) [3] were analyzed using the total number of patients as the denominator.

Assessment of Ventricular Function and Cardiac Dysfunction

Ventricular function was assessed using transthoracic echocardiography during clinical follow‐up every 1 or 2 years, earlier if patients developed signs and symptoms of heart failure. Cardiac dysfunction was defined when LVEF was below 50%, with a reduction of more than 10% from baseline. Age at first diagnosis of cardiac dysfunction and duration from first pacemaker implantation to diagnosis were recorded. PIC was diagnosed when no identifiable cause for the cardiac dysfunction was found [8].

Management Following Cardiac Dysfunction

Change in pacing strategy was defined by change in ventricular lead placement, upgrade to cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT), or multisite pacing in patients with single‐ventricle physiology. Medical management included any pharmacological therapy prescribed for treatment of cardiac dysfunction.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were presented as medians with interquartile ranges. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. Comparisons between groups were performed using the Mann–Whitney U and Fisher's exact tests for continuous and categorical variables, respectively.

Time‐to‐event analyses were performed using Kaplan–Meier survival curves to evaluate freedom from DACs on a per‐lead basis and freedom from cardiac dysfunction. For lead‐level analyses, time was measured from lead implantation to first lead complication. Leads were censored at lead revision or replacement, patient death, last follow‐up, or end of study, whichever occurred first.

For analysis comparing freedom from cardiac dysfunction between chronic RV and left Vp, patients were analyzed according to initial ventricular lead placement. Initial biventricular or septal lead placements were excluded from this analysis. Patients with a low burden of Vp (Vp < 70%) were also excluded. Survival curves were compared using the log‐rank test. Patients were censored at last follow‐up, death, or event occurrence, whichever occurred first.

All statistical tests were two‐tailed. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Fifty‐three patients with permanent pacemakers were included. There were 27 male patients (51%). Median follow‐up was 12 years (interquartile range (IQR) 9–19 years). Median age at first pacemaker implantation was 16 months (IQR 2 months to 7.7 years old). CHD was present in 21 patients (39.6%).

The most common indication for pacing was congenital high‐grade AVB (26 patients, 49%), followed by postoperative complete heart block (CHB) (17 patients, 32%). Other indications included sinus node disease (4 patients), long QT syndrome (3), post‐myocarditis CHB (2), and cardiomyopathy (1).

Pacemaker System and Lead Characteristics

51/53 (96.2%) patients had an initial epicardial system. Eleven (21.6%) patients were converted to transvenous pacing subsequently. Median age at first epicardial lead implantation was 1.2 years old (IQR 1.8 months to 7.6 years old), compared with 19 years old (IQR 14.6–20 years old; p < 0.001) for transvenous lead implantation.

A total of 113 pacemaker leads were implanted [100 (88.5%) epicardial and 13 (11.5%) transvenous leads]. All pacemaker leads were steroid‐eluting (Medtronic, USA).

Among the 51 patients with initial epicardial implantation, 37 (72.5%) had leads placed in the RV, 11 (21.6%) in the LV, and 3 (5.9%) received biventricular pacing for CRT. The remaining two patients had initial transvenous septal lead placement.

Thirteen of 37 patients (35.1%) with initial RV epicardial lead placement subsequently underwent a change in lead location. Five required pacing modification due to cardiac dysfunction, while the remaining eight patients underwent elective lead relocation during lead revision, including relocation to the LV in five and conversion to transvenous left bundle branch area pacing (LBBAP) in three. No changes in lead position occurred among patients with initial LV, biventricular, or septal lead placements.

Device‐Associated Complications

Eighteen lead complications occurred among the 113 implanted leads (15.9% event‐per‐lead): lead fracture/impending fracture (n = 12), high pacing threshold (n = 4) and lead infection (n = 2). There were 3 patient‐related complications: pacemaker pocket infection (n = 2) and mediastinitis following epicardial lead placement (n = 1). Freedom from lead‐associated complications was 95% at 1 year, 94% at 2 years, 85% at 5 years, and 83% at 10 years (Figure 1). No complications occurred in transvenous leads during follow‐up. Gender, pacemaker implantation in infancy, and the presence of CHD were not associated with complication rates. Considering epicardial leads only, freedom from epicardial lead failure was 83% and 80% at 5 and 10 years, respectively.

Cardiac Dysfunction and Pacing‐Induced Cardiomyopathy

Cardiac dysfunction occurred in 12 patients (22.6%) during follow‐up. Eight cases were attributed to PIC. Four patients had alternative causes [coronary artery compression caused by calcification of an abandoned epicardial lead (n = 1), progression of underlying dilated cardiomyopathy unrelated to pacing (n = 1), and worsening of underlying CHD (n = 2)].

Baseline characteristics of patients are shown in Table 1. Presence of CHD was the only variable significantly associated in univariate analysis with PIC (100% vs. 28.9%; p < 0.001). Patients with PIC tended to be younger at initial pacemaker implantation (median age 4 months old; IQR 1 month to 1.35 years old); this was not statistically significant (p = 0.075). Seven of eight patients (87.5%) had RV leads. One patient had a single‐ventricle physiology, with the epicardial lead placed in the dominant LV. Median age at diagnosis of PIC was 13.5 years old (IQR 6.5–15.5 years old), following a median of 10.5 years of chronic Vp, although onset ranged from 1 month to 21 years.

Cardiac Dysfunction Based on Ventricular Lead Position

Forty‐five of 53 patients were included in the analysis comparing freedom from pacing‐induced cardiomyopathy (PIC) between right versus left Vp. Eight patients were excluded in total: two had initial septal lead placement, three had initial biventricular lead placement, and three had low Vp burden (< 70%). Among included patients, 35 and 10 had initial right and left Vp, respectively.

Median follow‐up was longer in the RV group (17.0 years, IQR 12–22 years) compared to the LV group (median 9 years, IQR 6–9.5 years; p < 0.001). There was a gradual decline in freedom from PIC in the RV group, while cardiac function of the LV group was relatively preserved in the early follow‐up (Figure 2).

Kaplan–Meier curve showing freedom from pacing‐induced cardiomyopathy in chronic right ventricular (RV) versus left ventricular (LV) pacing.

Freedom from PIC was 90% at 5 years and 87.1% at 10 years for the RV pacing group, while that of the LV pacing group was 100% and 87.5%, respectively.

At later timepoints, estimates for the LV group were imprecise due to small numbers at risk. There was no statistically significant difference between groups (log‐rank χ 2 = 0.030, p = 0.872; Figure 2).

Treatment and Outcomes

Pacing‐Induced Cardiomyopathy

Five out of the eight patients diagnosed with PIC underwent pacing modification. Three patients underwent an upgrade to CRT. One patient had ventricular lead relocation to the LV, while one patient with univentricular physiology underwent dual‐site pacing of the dominant LV. Of these five patients, four also received adjunctive heart failure pharmacotherapy.

Two patients were treated with heart failure medications alone. One patient received no targeted intervention.

Full recovery of LV systolic function was observed in two patients: one following conversion to single‐site left Vp and one following CRT combined with medical therapy. No improvement in ventricular function was observed in patients managed with medical therapy alone or without intervention.

Three deaths occurred among patients with PIC (3/8, 37.5%). Two patients who underwent CRT died from heart failure attributed to PIC. One patient managed with medical therapy alone had an out‐of‐hospital death.

Non‐Pacing‐Induced Cardiac Dysfunction

The patient with coronary artery compression from a calcified abandoned epicardial lead demonstrated complete recovery of cardiac function following coronary artery stenting.

Two patients (one with underlying dilated cardiomyopathy and another with CHD) were managed with addition of heart failure medications to existing transvenous septal pacing systems, without improvement in ventricular function.

The remaining patient, with repaired tetralogy of Fallot and postoperative complete heart block, underwent CRT and initiation of heart failure medications but subsequently died from progressive right heart failure from the underlying CHD.

Discussion

In this study, long‐term device performance was acceptable, but pacing‐associated cardiac dysfunction remained clinically significant, especially among pediatric patients with CHD. These findings support the need for long‐term surveillance and individualized pacing strategies in children requiring chronic pacing.

Device‐Associated Complications

In our cohort, freedom from lead‐associated complications per lead was 85% and 83% at 5 and 10 years, respectively; comparable to reported epicardial steroid‐eluting lead survival of 74%–93% at 5 years and 62%–83% at 10 years [3, 4, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13]. Transvenous lead survival was higher at 91%–98% at 5 years and 73%–90% at 10 years [3, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15]. Most lead‐related complications in our study occurred within the first 5 years post‐implantation, with event rates plateauing thereafter. This early clustering of events may reflect the greater mechanical demands associated with rapid somatic growth in infants and young children, which may contribute to lead stress and fracture, as well as early tissue remodeling at the lead–epicardial interface. The subsequent plateau may reflect the durability of the leads used in our cohort and stabilization of chronic pacing thresholds [12, 13, 16].

Data regarding epicardial versus transvenous lead survival is mixed. Some studies demonstrate the superiority of transvenous leads over epicardial ones, while others report comparable outcomes using modern steroid‐eluting epicardial leads. A nationwide Danish cohort study showed age‐dependent differences in device‐related complications risks, with higher risks in transvenous systems among infants and epicardial systems among older children and teenagers [17]. Other risk factors for lead failure include complex cardiac anatomy and the use of non‐steroid‐eluting leads [3, 10, 17]. In our cohort, all patients had steroid‐eluting epicardial leads and later transitioned to transvenous systems, which may partly explain the relatively low complication rate (Table S1) [17].

Cardiac Dysfunction and Pacing‐Induced Cardiomyopathy

Cardiac dysfunction occurred in approximately one‐fifth of our patients, with pacing‐induced cardiomyopathy accounting for the majority of cases. The observed incidence of 15% in our study falls within the reported range of 6%–19% in pediatric cohorts (Table S1) [18, 19, 20]. Chronic RV pacing disrupts the physiological sequence of ventricular activation and may result in mechanical dyssynchrony, adverse remodeling, and reduced LV systolic function [5]. This negative effect appears to be most pronounced when pacing from the RV free wall or outflow tract. Pacing from the left ventricular apical and lateral wall is associated with preserved function [18].

In our study, pacing‐induced cardiomyopathy occurred only in patients with CHD who also tended to be younger at initial pacemaker implantation. A higher incidence was seen in patients with greater CHD anatomical complexity; however, this was not statistically significant.

Two patients with complex CHD developed very early onset pacing‐induced cardiomyopathy within 3 months of lead placement (double outlet right ventricle and double inlet left ventricle). Both patients did not respond to pacing modification and medical therapy. One subsequently passed away from heart failure attributed to PIC. Children with CHD, especially younger patients and those with complex cardiac anatomy, may be more vulnerable to pacing‐induced dysfunction due to abnormal ventricular geometry, surgical scarring, and altered conduction pathways [5, 21, 22, 23, 24, 25].

Ventricular Pacing Site and Cardiac Outcomes

We did not demonstrate a difference in long‐term freedom from cardiac dysfunction between right and left Vp [5, 18, 21, 22]. This may reflect low statistical power, crossover from RV pacing group to other pacing strategies, shorter follow‐up period in the LV pacing group, and heterogeneous cardiac anatomy.

Despite this, our institution has increasingly adopted epicardial LV pacing, in keeping with existing evidence and expert recommendations [5, 18, 26, 27, 28]. In patients with previous sternotomy, LV epicardial lead implantation via left thoracotomy avoids repeat sternotomy. Biventricular pacing is an established strategy for preventing or treating pacing‐related cardiac dysfunction in selected patients, but is not typically used as the primary pacing mode in our center because of the increased hardware burden, including the need for an additional lead and a larger pulse generator. In our center, biventricular pacing is reserved for selected patients with pre‐existing cardiomyopathy or as an upgrade strategy for patients who develop PIC.

Management and Reversibility of Pacing‐Induced Cardiomyopathy

CRT and single‐site left Vp have been reported to improve ventricular function in selected children with pacing‐induced cardiomyopathy [5, 26, 29]. In our cohort, recovery of ventricular function was seen in a minority of patients following pacing modification, but not in those treated with medical therapy alone. This supports ventricular dyssynchrony as the predominant mechanism for pacing‐induced cardiomyopathy [5, 29, 30], and highlights the importance of regular echocardiographic surveillance, with early consideration of change in pacing strategies where necessary.

Three of five patients in our cohort did not respond to pacing modification, compared with reported non‐responder rates of approximately 17% following CRT in pediatric PIC [18, 31]. Most of these studies evaluated patients with structurally normal hearts. Response to CRT may be less predictable in pediatric CHD due to the heterogeneity of anatomy, and postoperative electromechanical changes. Identification of the “optimal pacing site” with an individualized approach has been proposed (e.g., using electrocardiographic analysis of the QRS complex, speckle tracking echocardiographic monitoring, tissue velocity imaging) to assess for ventricular electromechanical dyssynchrony. However there is a lack of data in pediatric patients with CHD [5, 28, 32].

Conduction System Pacing

Conduction system pacing, including His bundle pacing and LBBAP, has emerged as a strategy to preserve or restore physiological ventricular activation and mitigate ventricular dyssynchrony seen with right Vp [33, 34]. Early data suggest that LBBAP is safe and feasible in selected pediatric patients [33, 35, 36]. In the pediatric population, LBBAP offers several potential advantages over CRT, including fewer lead placements, a smaller hardware burden, and feasibility in patients with coronary sinus anomalies. However, the transvenous approach requires patients to be of appropriate age and size. Furthermore, LBBAP may be technically challenging or unsuitable in patients with complex CHD anatomy, a fibrotic ventricular septum, or surgical patches [37, 38].

In our cohort, three older patients with structurally normal hearts underwent elective change from epicardial to transvenous LBBAP, with no lead‐related complications or cardiac dysfunction during short‐term follow‐up.

Strengths and Limitations

The strengths of this study include its 30‐year follow‐up, providing one of the longest reported experiences of pediatric permanent pacing in Asia, and the relative uniformity of epicardial lead technology used. Limitations include small cohort size, low event rates, unequal pacing group sizes, and follow‐up duration. Furthermore, due to the extended retrospective nature of the study, there was incomplete documentation of anthropometric data, exact epicardial lead locations, and standardized electrocardiographic measurements such as paced QRS duration. Future studies should consider collecting these important clinical parameters [5, 18, 39].

Conclusion

In this single‐center pediatric cohort with predominantly epicardial permanent pacing, long‐term DAC rates were acceptable, while pacing‐associated cardiac dysfunction remained clinically relevant. PIC occurred predominantly in patients with CHD and demonstrated a variable response to intervention. These findings support the need for long‐term echocardiographic surveillance and individualized pacing strategies in children, with careful consideration of the Vp site from the outset.
