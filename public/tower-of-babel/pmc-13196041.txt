Mental health and access to healthcare among racialized and non-racialized migrant sexual and gender minority youth in Canada: findings from a cross-sectional survey conducted during COVID-19
Authors: Amy Rhanim, David Ortiz-Paredes, Kevin L’Espérance, Keven Lee, Olivier Ferlatte
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMigrant sexual and gender minority (M-SGM) youth are vulnerable to pronounced mental health challenges and experience significant barriers to healthcare access due to the intersecting forms of stigma related to sexual and gender minority status, racialization, and migration. However, evidence of these inequities remains limited. This study examined mental health outcomes and healthcare access of M-SGM youth in Canada during the COVID-19 pandemic and assessed differences between racialized and non-racialized youth.MethodsThis cross-sectional study analyzed data from a sub-sample of migrant respondents drawn from a larger national online survey of sexual and gender minority youth. The analytic sample included M-SGM youth aged 15–24 years (N = 291). The survey assessed self-reported changes in mental health during the COVID-19 pandemic, symptoms of anxiety and depression, suicidal ideation and attempts, perceptions of healthcare providers, and unmet mental health needs. Descriptive analyses were conducted and displayed in contingency tables, which allowed stratifying outcomes across racialized and non-racialized respondents.ResultsOverall, 75% of the respondents experienced worsened mental health during the COVID-19 pandemic. Suicidal ideation was prevalent across both groups, with a slightly higher prevalence among racialized respondents (44% vs. 39%). Suicide attempts were reported more frequently among non-racialized youth (35% vs. 11%). Across the sample, respondents frequently reported negative healthcare experiences, including limited provider understanding of sexual and gender minority issues and perceived prejudice. Unmet mental health needs were common, particularly related to anxiety, depression, and self-esteem. Racialized youth more often reported unmet needs related to coming out and suicidal ideation.ConclusionsM-SGM youth in Canada experienced substantial mental health challenges and unmet healthcare needs during the COVID-19 pandemic, with differences observed in terms of race. Findings from this sub-sample highlight the need for public health strategies and culturally responsive mental health services that address intersecting social inequities affecting migrant sexual and gender minority youth.Keywords: Sexual and gender minorities, Migration, Mental health, Healthcare Access, COVID-19, Youth

Introduction

Despite increasing social acceptance in many Western countries, sexual and gender minorities (SGM)—referring to those who identify as lesbian, gay, bisexual, trans, queer, or other non-heteronormative or non-cisgender identities—continue to experience pronounced social and mental health inequities [1]. Although a growing body of research has documented these inequities, SGM populations are frequently treated as homogenous [2], obscuring important within-group diversity and the distinct experiences of subpopulations such as migrant sexual and gender minorities (M-SGM). This lack of differentiation limits the understanding of the specific vulnerabilities faced by M-SGM individuals and constrains the development of appropriate interventions [3, 4].

The term migrant broadly refers to individuals who relocate away from their usual place of residence—either within a country or across international borders—temporarily or permanently for various reasons [5]. Emerging evidence indicates that M-SGM individuals experience higher levels of depression and anxiety than their non-migrant SGM counterparts [3, 6]. These mental health inequities appear to be particularly acute among youth aged 15–24 years, a developmental period characterized by identity formation, exploration, and increasing autonomy, which may heighten susceptibility to mental health challenges [7, 8]. Prior studies have shown that stigma and discrimination related to sexual and gender identities are significant determinants of the poor mental health of SGM youth [9]. However, there is limited research specifically addressing the experiences of youth who are both migrants and members of SGM communities.

In addition to identity-based stressors related to gender and sexuality, M-SGM youth face migration-related challenges such as cultural assimilation, integration difficulties [10] and experiences of racism [11]. In the Canadian context, studies have shown that migrants face racial discrimination within the healthcare system, which negatively affects their mental health and limits their access to appropriate and timely services [12, 13]. These challenges are often compounded by precarious immigration status [14], financial constraints, and a general lack of awareness of their needs among providers [15].

Racialized M-SGM youth – refering to migrant youth who are socially positioned and treated through processes of racialization within systems of power [16] - may be particularly vulnerable to adverse mental health outcomes. While all individuals are racialized, Whiteness is typically constructed as the dominant and normative category, and therefore less frequently associated with the marginalizing effects of racialization in this context [17]. Althought racialized and non-raciliazed migrants may share common challenges related to migration (e.g., adapting to a new society), racialized M-SGM youth often face language barriers, xenophobia, and systemic racism, which can intensify social isolation, anxiety, and depressive symptoms [18]. Discrimination within healthcare settings further restricts access to affirming and culturally responsive care, constituting a significant barrier to mental health support [19]. The intersection of migration status, SGM identity, and racialization thus produces layered and compounding forms of marginalization, positioning racialized M-SGM youth as an especially underserved population in need of targeted mental health interventions.

The COVID-19 pandemic has further exacerbated these preexisting vulnerabilities, disproportionately impacting SGM, migrants, and racialized communities [20]. A national survey conducted by the Mental Health Commission of Canada [21] revealed that anxiety levels among SGM individuals were more than twice as high as those among non-SGM respondents. Within SGM, young and racialized individuals reported particularly elevated rates of anxiety, depression and suicidal ideation. These outcomes were partly attributed to public health measures such as social distancing, which intensified feelings of loneliness and disrupted access to essential services, thereby worsening symptoms of anxiety, depression, posttraumatic stress disorder, and substance use disorders [22, 23]. Migrant and racialized SGM communities also encountered additional healthcare access barriers during the pandemic, including a lack of health insurance, distrust of healthcare providers, and fear of microaggressions and discrimination [24, 25].

Early research on the mental health impacts of the COVID-19 pandemic primarily focused on older adults [26], who were considered at greater risk of severe infection and social isolation. However, subsequent evidence has demonstrated that youth experienced some of the most substantial mental health consequences of the pandemic, driven in part by school closures, social disruption, and prolonged uncertainty [27, 28]. Despite these findings, limited research has examined the mental health impacts of COVID-19 among migrants in Canada [21, 29], particularly those at the intersection of migration, SGM identity and racialization. Consequently, a significant gap remains in the understanding of the mental health experiences and service needs of M-SGM during and after the COVID-19 pandemic [29, 30].

The present cross-sectional study aims to explore mental health status and access to healthcare among M-SGM youth (15–24 years) in Canada during the COVID-19 pandemic, with a focus on differences between racialized and non-racialized groups. Specifically, the study has three objectives: (1) to provide a comprehensive overview of the mental health of M-SGM youth during the pandemic; (2) to examine the perceptions of M-SGM youth regarding mental health providers; and (3) to identify their needs and priorities for mental health services. While grounded in the context of COVID-19, this study addresses structural and intersectional barriers to mental healthcare that persist beyond the pandemic. The findings therefore offer insights to inform equitable, long-term mental health service planning and policymaking for M-SGM youth.

Methods

Study design and population

The I Stand Out Survey was an online survey of SGM youth in Canada conducted between November 2021 and January 2022. The eligibility criteria for participation included (1) identifying as SGM, (2) being between 15 and 24 years old, and (3) living in Canada. Survey respondents were recruited through promotions on social media (i.e., Facebook and Instagram), community groups, and word-of-mouth. The survey questionnaires were completed anonymously, online, and in one of Canada’s two official languages, English or French. The survey collected data from 3,668 respondents on a broad set of constructs related to mental health, including mental health outcomes, minority stress, substance use, resilience, and access to health services. Its development involved collaboration with youth peer researchers from the Jeunes Chercheur·es Queers program, a research capacity-building initiative for queer youth aged 18–29 years. These peer researchers contributed to the survey design, promotion, data analysis, interpretation, and knowledge dissemination. Pilot testing with target population members ensured face validity by gathering feedback on survey content and item relevance. The study received ethical approval from the Comité d’éthique de la Recherche en Sciences de la Santé de l’Université de Montréal.

For the present study, we restricted the analysis to migrant respondents to examine SGM migrants’ mental health and well-being. SGM migrants within the sample were defined as foreign-born and were identified with the following question: “Were you born in Canada? Yes or No.” Respondents who answered “No” (n = 291) were categorized as migrants. Migrants were further stratified into racialized (n = 180) and non-racialized (n = 111) groups on the basis of the following responses: “What racial, ethnic, and geographic backgrounds do you identify with?”. Respondents who identified exclusively as White were classified as non-racialized migrants, whereas all others were classified as racialized migrants.

Data measures, conceptualization, and operationalization

Self-rated mental health

We inquired about self-perceived mental health via the following question: “In general, would you say your mental health is…?”. The respondents answered excellent, very good, good, fair, or poor. Self-rated mental health was dichotomized so that poor and fair health were contrasted with good, very good, and excellent health. This dichotomization fairly correlates with mental health morbidities in Canada [31].

Anxiety

General anxiety was measured via the General Anxiety Disorder-7 (GAD-7), where higher scores indicate greater anxiety severity. Validation studies have shown that the GAD-7 has strong psychometric properties in general populations from different cultures and countries [32, 33]. Anxiety severity was categorized as high for scores of 10 or higher and low for scores below 10, on the basis of established cutoffs that showed high sensitivity and specificity for diagnosing general anxiety disorder [34].

Depression

We measured depression with the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9), a widely used and validated measure where higher scores indicate greater severity of depressive symptoms [35]. Depression severity was categorized into a binary variable, with high severity defined as a score of 15 or higher and low severity defined as a score below 15. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses have demonstrated the reliability and validity of the PHQ-9 across diverse countries, languages, and contexts (e.g., the general population) [36].

Resilience

Resilience was assessed via the Brief Resilience Scale [37]. Resilience was represented by a categorical variable based on the distribution of the individual scores: low (6-11), neutral (12-18), and high (19-30).

Loneliness

We measured loneliness with scores from the UCLA 3-item Loneliness Scale, which was dichotomized into not lonely (0–5) and lonely (6-9) [38].

Suicidality

Suicidality was measured using questions adapted from the Canadian Community Health Survey 2020 [39]. The respondents were asked to report whether they had experienced suicidal thoughts in the past 12 months and whether they had attempted suicide during the same period.

COVID-19 impacts

We created a question to assess respondents’ perceptions of changes in several aspects due to the COVID-19 pandemic compared with the pre-pandemic period. These aspects included physical health, mental health, social life, love life, sexual life, financial situation, access to SGM resources, sense of belonging to the SGM community, and access to health services. The respondents indicated whether these aspects significantly deteriorated, slightly deteriorated, remained the same, slightly improved, or greatly improved. For analysis, we dichotomized the responses into two groups: deteriorated versus remained the same or improved.

Mental health needs

The respondents were asked to report their level of agreement with six statements regarding healthcare providers via a five-point Likert scale (“strongly disagree,” “disagree,” “neither agree nor disagree,” “agree,” and “strongly agree”). The statements addressed their comfort in sharing their sexual orientation and gender identity, their perceptions of healthcare providers’ understanding of SGM health issues, their comfort in discussing physical and mental health concerns, and their views on whether healthcare providers harbor prejudices toward SGM individuals. For analysis, the responses to each statement were categorized into three levels: disagree, neutral (“neither agree nor disagree”), and agree. The respondents were also provided with a list of mental health concerns and asked to identify those for which they wanted help.

Data analysis

The data were analyzed via R (version 4.3.3, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). We computed the variables’ distributions and reported them as numbers (n) and prevalent proportions (%) for racialized and non-racialized SGM migrants. Prevalent proportions were calculated among respondents with available information. Missing values were reported for each variable under study. We refrained from statistical testing, as the purpose of this analysis is descriptive, not inferential. Statistical testing in descriptive analysis is prone to spurious findings and misinterpretation [40, 41].

Results

Table 1 details the characteristics of the survey respondents, comprising 180 racialized M-SGM and 111 non-racialized M-SGM. A greater proportion of non-racialized respondents self-identified as gay (23.4% vs. 13.3%) or lesbian (26.1% vs. 15.6%). A greater proportion of racialized respondents identified as bisexual compared to non-racialized respondents (41.6% vs. 28%). Other sexual orientation identities were distributed similarly across the two groups. Most respondents identified as women, and approximately one-third identified as non-binary in both racialized and non-racialized groups. A total of 28.1% of racialized migrants and 36.9% of non-racialized migrants identified as transgender. Most of the respondents were from Canada’s two most populous provinces, Quebec and Ontario.

Discussion

This study provides novel and timely important insights into the mental health challenges experienced by racialized and non-racialized M-SGM youth during the COVID-19 pandemic in Canada. Addressing a notable gap in the literature, the findings demonstrate substantial mental health challenges within this population and reveal important inequities shaped by the intersection of migration, sexual and gender minority identity, and racialization. Over three-quarters of respondents reported a deterioration in self-perceived mental health, reinforcing previous research documenting the disproportionate psychological impacts of the pandemic on migrant youth and sexual and gender minorities [21, 42]. In addition to confirming these broader trends, this study highlights meaningful differences between racialized and non-racialized M-SGM youth in terms of mental health status, perceived access to care, and specific service needs.

Racialized respondents reported higher levels of anxiety and depression compared to their non-racialized peers, consistent with previous evidence highlighting the disproportionate mental health burden borne by racialized migrant populations [43, 44]. These inequities are likely attributable to intersecting structural and sociocultural stressors. During the COVID-19 pandemic, ethnocultural discrimination intensified globally [45], while public discourse surrounding police violence – particularly following the killing of George Floyd – and racialized narratives attributing blame for the virus contributed to heightened racism toward Black and Asian communities [46, 47]. For racialized M-SGM youth and individuals, exposure to both direct and vicarious racism through media and social networks [48, 49] likely intensified psychological distress, as documented in prior research linking such exposure to increased anxiety and depressive symptoms [50, 51].

Migration-related vulnerabilities likely contributed to the compounding of these stressors. Precarious immigration status is a well-established determinant of poor mental health [52], and the temporary closure of Canada’s borders between April 2020 and November 2021 may have exacerbated emotional strain among migrant youth by prolonging family separation, disrupting transnational support networks, and generating uncertainty regarding legal status. These challenges may have been particularly acute for youth who migrated in response to sexual- or gender-based violence or persecution, for whom displacement and insecurity are already closely tied to trauma and minority stress [53].

Loneliness emerged as a salient concern across the study sample, with more than two-thirds of respondents screening positive for loneliness. While isolation was a widespread consequence of lockdowns and public health restrictions [54], its impact may have been magnified for migrants, who often face additional barriers to social integration, including discrimination, language barriers, financial instability [55], and limited support networks [56]. For M-SGM youth, these challenges intersect with stigma related to sexual and gender identity, potentially restricting access to affirming spaces and communities that are critical for mental well-being. The closure of schools, community organizations, and LGBTQ+ affirming spaces during the pandemic likely further eroded opportunities for connection and belonging, particularly for those who relied on these settings for social support.

Another significant mental health concern reported by the respondents was suicidality. In our survey, racialized M-SGM youth were more likely to report suicidal thoughts compared to their non-racialized counterparts. These results align with current research showing that suicidal ideation is more common in racialized SGM youth [57], especially among racialized females [58, 59]. Our study also revealed that non-racialized M-SGM individuals reported more suicide attempts. This result contrasts with current trends in suicide research, which demonstrate a higher prevalence of suicide attempts among some racialized SGM groups, such as Black and Hispanic youth in the U.S [57, 60]. This apparent discrepancy underscores the complexity of suicidality and may reflect differences in sample composition, reporting practices, or unmeasured contextual factors. Importantly, suicidal ideation and suicide attempts are related but distinct phenomena, influenced by different determinants: attempts are significantly predicted by depression severity, previous hospitalization, hopelessness severity, and past attempts, while hopelessness severity alone significantly predicts ideation [61]. This distinction may help explain the differing patterns observed across groups. Further, protective factors may play a role in shaping these outcomes. Prior research highlights resilience [62] and religious affiliation [63, 64] as potential buffers against suicidality. While religious affiliation was not assessed in this study, we observed differences in resilience, with non-racialized respondents more frequently represented in the lower resilience category. This may point to differential access to protective resources or coping mechanisms across groups. However, these interpretations remain tentative and should be approached with caution. Taken together, these findings highlight the need for more nuanced, intersectional approaches to understanding suicidality among M-SGM youth. As noted by Kernan, Jones [65] there is a need for “more robust measures of race/ethnicity” to avoid “artificial, monolithic categorizations” (p. 5) and better illuminate—rather than reduce—the multifaceted dimension of suicidal experience among racialized SGM youth. Our study contributes to this call for complexity by emphasizing the need to consider migration status when adopting an intersectional lens to fully comprehend these dynamics in the context of suicide. Such research has the potential to offer a more nuanced understanding of suicidality among racialized M-SGM youth to better tailor current and future services to their specific needs.

Our study also revealed important unmet mental health needs among M-SGM. While both groups expressed the need for support related to mental health (e.g., depression, anxiety, and eating disorders), racialized M-SGM youth were more likely to report a need for support related to suicidality, while non-racialized M-SGM youth reported a higher prevalence of suicide attempts. These findings suggest that support for suicidality constitutes a critical need across both groups, underscoring the importance of developing tailored services to address this issue. Additionally, racialized M-SGM youth expressed a greater need for support related to the coming-out process. The internalization of cultural stigma related to mental illness, suicide, and SGM identity [66, 67] may partly explain our results in how racialized individuals can internalize such struggles and influence their help-seeking behaviors. In contrast to Western narratives, which are often seen as the first liberatory step toward living a SGM life, Ghabrial [68] suggest that racialized SGM individuals experience what she calls a “coming out stress” (p. 51). Building on intersectionality and minority stress theories, Ghabrial defines coming out stress as this unique pressure that stems from SGM communities for racialized SGM to disclose their sexuality to their family members, exacerbating the feeling of a cultural disconnect from either culture—the one to which they belong—or the mainstream queer community. Our results thus support the need for culturally adapted support during the coming out process as well as more diverse public health approaches to diversify narratives.

Our study also revealed a decline in respondents’ self-perceived access to health services and SGM resources, regardless of race. Notably, an interesting finding of our study is that non-racialized migrants perceived greater deterioration in their access than did racialized migrants. One hypothesis to partially explain such a result may be that non-racialized M-SGM youth may have had greater awareness of and access to services before the pandemic than their racialized counterparts did. Furthermore, racialized migrants often encounter additional barriers to care upon arrival, including limited knowledge of the healthcare system, difficulties navigating community-based resources, and a lack of culturally adapted services [69]. Our study also noted an important distrust of all M-SGM youth in healthcare providers, with more than half believing that healthcare providers continue to hold biases against SGM individuals. This distrust of healthcare providers often stems from a lack of adequate training and understanding in addressing the specific mental health needs of SGM, racialized individuals, or those living at the intersection of these identities [70–72]. As a result, harmful stereotypes related to sexual orientation, gender identity, culture, race, and migrant experiences are at risk of being perpetuated within care settings [19, 73, 74].

While this study is situated within the context of the COVID-19 pandemic, its implications extend beyond this period. The pandemic functioned as a stress test for mental health systems, revealing and intensifying pre-existing inequities rather than creating entirely new ones [75]. Our findings highlight the urgent need for culturally safe, anti-racist and inclusive care environments, as well as for healthcare providers who are adequately equipped — through both training and resources—to respond to the specific needs of racialized M-SGM. They also underscore the importance of improving access to timely, low-barrier, and multilingual services, as language barriers may hinder the ability of some migrants to communicate about sensitive health issues. In addition, creating dedicated spaces and targeted outreach initiatives for migrant SGM youth is essential to ensure access to care that is affirming and tailored to their needs. In this regard, Clinique Mauve in Montreal (Canada) represents a promising low-barrier model of care for SGM migrants and racialized individuals, offering integrated, culturally responsive services that address longstanding access barriers [76, 77].

Future research is needed to examine the effectiveness and scalability of such integrated initiatives, alongside more comprehensive and longitudinal studies to better capture the complexity of access to care among both racialized and non-racialized M-SGM youth. Future studies should also consider the variability of experiences of different cultural groups [69] regarding access as well as mental health outcomes. Finally, our findings underscore the need for increased public health efforts to support young migrants, racialized and sexual/gender minorities effectively. A more proactive public health approach is needed—one that does not rely on youth to independently seek out services but actively works to remove barriers and build trust within racialized and migrant communities.

Limitations

The descriptive nature and cross-sectional design of this study limit our ability to account for the effect of time on the complexity of mental health issues in pandemic and migration contexts. One limitation of our study is that we did not control for the time of arrival or migration status. Another aspect to consider in our study is the questionnaire’s length, which may have contributed to item nonresponse, thus not providing the full picture of the population that participated in the survey. Additionally, our convenience sampling approach may limit the generalizability of the findings. As the survey was online, anonymous, and recruited through social media, community groups, and word-of-mouth, it may have disproportionately reached youth already connected to relevant networks and with internet access, thereby limiting broader population representation. Furthermore, given the sample size, we were unable to stratify our sample by ethnocultural background or home country. As such, categorizing respondents as racialized/non-racialized masks heterogeneity within ethnoracial and geographic backgrounds [69] and SGM realities. Moreover, as migration was categorized based on foreign-born respondents, this measure does not capture differences in immigration pathways, length of stay, refugee status, or documentation context that may shape respondents’ experiences. Despite these limitations, our study makes significant contributions to the sparse body of literature on mental health among M-SGM populations in a pandemic context. A key strength of our study lies in its participatory approach: by actively involving peer researchers in the development of the questionnaire, its dissemination, and the subsequent analyses, we ensured that our research aligned with the lived experiences and priorities of the SGM youth communities.

Conclusion

This study provides novel insights into the mental health outcomes and healthcare needs of M-SGM youth, both racialized and non-racialized, and fills a critical gap in existing research. While the findings emerged in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic, they remain highly relevant in the post-pandemic period, as they reflect enduring structural and social inequities rather than time-limited crisis effects. Our results underscore the ongoing need for tailored and sustained support services that address the unique experiences of racialized M-SGM youth, particularly in areas related to depression, anxiety, suicide, and coming out support. Continued research is essential to more fully understand the complex, intersectional impacts of migration, race, and sexual and gender identities on mental health. Such evidence is crucial for informing more equitable healthcare policies and interventions and improving preparedness for future public health emergencies.
