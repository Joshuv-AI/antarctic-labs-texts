Abstract
. Large Vision-Language Models (LVLMs) have demonstrated remarkable advancements in numerous areas such as multimedia. However, hallucination issues significantly limit their credibility and application potential. Existing mitigation methods typically rely on external tools or the comparison of multi-round inference, which significantly increase inference time. In this paper, we propose SE lf- E volving D istillation ( SEED ), which identifies hallucinations within the inner knowledge of LVLMs, isolates and purges them, and then distills the purified knowledge back into the model, enabling self-evolution. Furthermore, we identified that traditional distillation methods are prone to inducing void spaces in the output space of LVLMs. To address this issue, we propose a Mode-Seeking Evolving approach, which performs distillation to capture the dominant modes of the purified knowledge distribution, thereby avoiding the chaotic results that could emerge from void spaces. Moreover, we introduce a Hallucination Elimination Adapter, which corrects the dark knowledge of the original model by learning purified knowledge. Extensive experiments on multiple benchmarks validate the superiority of our SEED, demonstrating substantial improvements in mitigating hallucinations for representative LVLM models such as LLaVA-1.5 and InternVL2. Remarkably, the F1 score of LLaVA-1.5 on the hallucination evaluation metric POPE-Random improved from 81.3 to 88.3.

For LVLMs, hallucinations refer to cases where the generated outputs conflict with the visual inputs

In the strawberry example, because the model’s internal knowledge contains the bias that strawberries are typically red, it tends to ignore the visual information and directly responds that the color of the strawberry is red. To mitigate hallucinations in LVLMs, it is crucial to purge this part of knowledge related to hallucinations.

;

, and

;

;

(
Leng et al. 2024b
)
. Therefore, there is a pressing need in the field for hallucination mitigation methods that are
both low-cost in terms of data annotation and inference
. Compared to collecting new and diverse data, comparing multiple responses, or post-processing, a more promising approach is to prune hallucination-related components within the model’s knowledge distribution, thereby addressing hallucination at root.

In this work, we propose

Furthermore, we introduce the Hallucination Elimination Adapter to enhance the stability of the distillation process. The contributions are summarized as follows:

(1)

We propose the self-evolving distillation method, which enables the self-evolution of the LVLM by identifying and purging hallucinations within its dark knowledge, all without introducing additional data sources, models, or inference overhead.

(2)

We propose the Mode-Seeking Evolving method to capture the dominant modes of the purified knowledge distribution while avoiding void spaces in the LVLM output space and the potential chaotic outputs that may result.

(3)

We present Hallucination Elimination Adapter, which isolates the trainable parameters during the SEED process, enhancing the stability of knowledge distillation and correcting the dark knowledge of the original model.

(4)

Extensive experiments on multiple benchmarks demonstrate the superiority of our method in mitigating hallucinations for representative LVLMs, outperforming existing multi-round methods. Notably, on the POPE-Adversarial hallucination benchmark, the F1 score of LLaVA increases from 81.3 to 85.9, while reducing inference cost by 50%.

2.3.
Knowledge Distillation

KD
(
Gou et al. 2021
;
Kim and Rush 2016a
)
aims to transfer knowledge to a smaller model to reduce inference costs while maintaining performance. For LLMs, the standard KD method typically involves minimizing of the forward KLD between the student and teacher’s generative distributions
(
Sanh 2019
)
, or directly training on the text generated by the teacher
(
Kim and Rush 2016b
;
Taori et al. 2023
;
Peng et al. 2023
)
. However, due to the large output distribution space, the commonly used forward KL loss can lead to the student LLM assigning high probabilities to invalid regions
(
Agarwal et al. 2024
)
. As a result, several approaches
(
Wang et al. 2023
;
Gu et al. 2023
;
Kim et al. 2024
)
have proposed using alternative distillation losses, such as Jensen-Shannon divergence, in LLMs. However, the selection of loss functions for distillation LVLMs still requires further exploration.

Pipeline of Our Method.

In hallucination purification, we remove the hallucination distribution from the knowledge distribution according to the purification degree. Finally, we distill the purified knowledge into the hallucination elimination adapter using mode-seeking evolving to refine the internal knowledge distribution of the LVLM.

3.
Self-Evolving Distillation

3.1.
Hallucination Identification

To purge hallucinations within the internal knowledge of LVLMs, we first identify them using a small-scale hallucination probing dataset. For each pair of image and question in this dataset, we feed it into the LVLM to obtain
Logits_{o}
, which contains the model’s dark knowledge:

(1)

\mathcal{L}ogits_{o}=LVLM(\mathcal{I}mage,\mathcal{Q}uestion)

To identify whether hallucinations exist in each piece of dark knowledge, we propose a confidence-based identification method. When LVLM shows low confidence in its own output, it indicates that the knowledge is near or even beyond its capability boundary, making hallucinations more likely
(
Becker and Soatto 2024
)
. Therefore, we convert the hallucination identification problem into assessing confidence.

Logits_{o}
represents the output distribution of this latent knowledge; after applying the Softmax function to this distribution, a sequence of probabilities can be obtained.

(2)

\{\mathcal{P}rob_{0},...,\mathcal{P}rob_{N-1}\}=Softmax(\mathcal{L}ogits_{o})

where
N
represents sequence length.

The higher the overall output probability of an answer sequence, the higher its confidence. The output probability of an answer sequence can be obtained by multiplying the maximum probability values at each position. However, since multiplying several very small probability values may exceed precision limits, we choose the logarithm of the probability as the evaluation metric for confidence.

(3)

\mathcal{C}onf=log(\prod_{i=0}^{N-1}\mathcal{MP}rob_{i})=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1}log(\mathcal{MP}rob_{i})

where
\mathcal{C}onf
represents the estimated confidence,
\mathcal{MP}rob_{i}
denotes the max probability within
\mathcal{P}rob_{i}
.

In this way, we obtain an estimate of the confidence in a piece of dark knowledge, which can serve as an indicator of hallucination. When the confidence is low, we consider it likely that this dark knowledge contains hallucination, which can be supported by
fig.

2
.

3.2.
Hallucination Isolation

After identifying the presence of hallucination in a piece of dark knowledge, the next step is to isolate the hallucinated part of knowledge.

Figure 4.
Two examples where hallucinations in LLaVA-1.5 have been mitigated after SEED.

The hallucinations in an LVLM primarily reside within the LLM component
(
Huang et al. 2023
)
. During the pre-training of the LLM on the text modality, it learns many statistical priors. For example, strawberries are generally red and bananas are generally yellow. During inference, the LVLM might over-rely on the text modality and the biases within the LLM, neglecting visual information. For instance, when asked about the color of a strawberry, the model might respond “red" based on the textual input, even if the provided image depicts a white strawberry
(
Leng et al. 2024a
)
.

To isolate these hallucinations, we aim to weaken the influence of visual information, resulting in an answer predominantly driven by text modality. In this way, the LVLM will inevitably rely on the biases it has learned from the text modality. Therefore, we first add noise to the visual input:

(4)

\mathcal{I}mage^{\prime}=\sqrt{\alpha}\,\mathcal{I}mage+\sqrt{1-\alpha}\,\epsilon,

where
\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
,
\alpha
is the noise strength, and
\mathcal{I}mage^{\prime}
represents the image with noise. In this way, we reduce the amount of visual information received by the LVLM, leading to an answer dominated by text-based information and statistical biases:

(5)

\mathcal{L}ogits_{h}=LVLM(\mathcal{I}mage^{\prime},\mathcal{Q}uestion)

where
\mathcal{L}ogits_{h}
contains isolated hallucinations.

3.3.
Hallucination Purification

To eliminate hallucinations within
Logits_{o}
, we perform a weighted subtraction between these two logits to obtain purified logits:

(6)

\mathcal{L}ogits_{p}=(1+\mathcal{\beta})\mathcal{L}ogits_{o}-\beta\mathcal{L}ogits_{h}

where
\mathcal{L}ogits_{p}
represents purified Logits,
\beta
indicates the purification degree. The larger the value of
\beta
, the stronger the suppression of hallucinations.
\beta
is determined by estimated confidence
\mathcal{C}onf
.

(7)

\beta=\beta_{i},\ if\ \ \mathcal{QT}_{i}<\mathcal{C}onf<\mathcal{QT}{i+1},0<=i<G

where
G
is the number of groups,
\mathcal{QT}_{i}
is the
i/G
quantile of the Conf distribution,
split_{0}
is
-\infty
, and
split_{G}
is
\infty
. The Conf distribution is obtained from the statistics of a subset of the hallucination probing dataset.
\beta_{0}
to
\beta_{G-1}
are predefined constants in descending order.

3.4.
Distilling Knowledge Back

Then, we leverage the purified knowledge to adjust the distribution of the original LVLM, purging its internal hallucinations. Knowledge distillation is a technique that allows a student model to mimic the behavior of a teacher model or system. In this case, we can treat the purified knowledge as the teacher and the original LVLM as the student, removing hallucinations from the internal knowledge of the LVLM. The loss function for the distillation process is as follows:

(8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=

\displaystyle\lambda\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}\left(Softmax(\mathcal{L}ogits_{d})\parallel Softmax(\mathcal{L}ogits_{p})\right)

\displaystyle+(1-\lambda)\,\mathcal{L}_{CE}\left(Softmax(\mathcal{L}ogits_{d}),y_{t}\right)

where
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}
is the distillation loss,
\mathcal{L}ogits_{d}
is the logits to be distilled,
\mathcal{L}_{CE}
is the cross-entropy loss,
y_{t}
denotes the ground truth, and
\lambda
is a constant.

After updating the LVLM based on the gradients of this loss, the model’s hallucinations are reduced.

(9)

\theta_{M{new}}=\theta_{M{old}}-\eta\nabla_{\theta_{M}}\mathcal{L}

where
\theta_{M}
represents parameters of LVLM. By distilling the purified knowledge back into the original model, the LVLM achieves hallucination suppression without adding additional inference overhead.

4.
Mode-Seeking Evolving

In Self-Evolving Distillation, the loss function is crucial as it determines how effectively the distilled LVLM mimics the Logits of purified knowledge. In white-box knowledge distillation, the KL divergence is the most commonly used loss function. KL divergence measures the distance between distribution
Q
and distribution
P
:

(10)

\mathcal{D}_{KL}(\mathcal{P}\parallel\mathcal{Q})=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{P}}\left[\log\frac{\mathcal{P}(X)}{\mathcal{Q}(X)}\right]

KL divergence is asymmetric, meaning
\mathcal{D}_{\text{KL}}(\mathcal{P}\parallel\mathcal{Q})
is not equal to
\mathcal{D}_{\text{KL}}(\mathcal{Q}\parallel\mathcal{P})
. In terms of Self-Evolving Distillation, the forward KL divergence can be written as:

(11)

\mathcal{D}_{KL}(\mathcal{K}\parallel\mathcal{D}_{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{K}}\left[\log\frac{\mathcal{K}(X)}{\mathcal{D}_{\theta}(X)}\right]

where
\mathcal{K}
represents the distribution of the purified knowledge, and
\mathcal{D}_{\theta}
represents the distilled distribution.

By transforming
eq.

11
, the optimization objective can be equivalently expressed as:

(12)

\displaystyle\arg\min_{\theta}\mathcal{D}_{KL}(\mathcal{K}\parallel\mathcal{D}_{\theta})

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{K}}\left[-\log\mathcal{D}_{\theta}(X)\right]-\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K}(X))

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{K}}\left[-\log\mathcal{D}_{\theta}(X)\right]

\displaystyle=\arg\max_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{K}}\left[\log\mathcal{D}_{\theta}(X)\right]

where
\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{K}(X))
is the entropy of the distribution
\mathcal{K}
.

We can find that the objective of the forward KL divergence is forcing
\mathcal{D}_{\theta}
to assign high probability where
\mathcal{K}
has high probability. This leads to
mean-seeking behavior
, meaning that the distilled model’s distribution
\mathcal{D}_{\theta}
tries to cover all high-probability regions (modes) of
\mathcal{K}
, as shown in
fig.

5
. As a result,
\mathcal{D}_{\theta}
’s distribution center tends to lie in intermediate regions between modes of
\mathcal{K}
to cover as many modes as possible simultaneously. The forward KL divergence does not penalize
\mathcal{D}_{\theta}
for assigning high probability to
\mathcal{K}
’s low-probability regions.

This mean-seeking behavior can degrade effectiveness of SEED. Because the output space of large models is vast and
\mathcal{K}
contains numerous modes, mean-seeking behavior causes
\mathcal{D}_{\theta}
to lie in intermediate regions among multiple modes of
\mathcal{K}
. These intermediate regions are typically void spaces in the space of large models
(
Gu et al. 2024
)
. Assigning high probability to void spaces causes the distilled model to produce chaotic results during inference.

In Self-Evolving Distillation, the reverse KL divergence is expressed as:

(13)

\mathcal{D}_{KL}(\mathcal{D}_{\theta}\parallel\mathcal{K})=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}_{\theta}(X)}\left[\log\frac{\mathcal{D}_{\theta}(X)}{\mathcal{K}(X)}\right]

Transforming the expression of the reverse KL divergence, the optimization objective becomes:

(14)

\displaystyle\arg\min_{\theta}\mathcal{D}_{KL}(\mathcal{D}_{\theta}\parallel\mathcal{K})

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}_{\theta}}\left[-\log\mathcal{K}(X)\right]-\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{D}_{\theta}(X))

\displaystyle=\arg\max_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}_{\theta}}\left[\log\mathcal{K}(X)\right]+\mathcal{H}(\mathcal{D}_{\theta}(X))

–

Toy experiment fitting Gaussian Mixture Distribution using Reverse KLD and Forward KLD. (Reverse KLD can fit dominant modes better and avoid the target distribution’s low-probability regions.)

From the equation above, we understand that the reverse KL divergence requires that
\mathcal{K}
assigns high probability where
\mathcal{D}_{\theta}
has high probability. The reverse KL divergence leads to
mode-seeking behavior
: since
\mathcal{D}_{\theta}
must focus on regions where
\mathcal{K}
is high, it will concentrate on the dominant modes of
\mathcal{K}
. In other words,
\mathcal{D}_{\theta}
will attempt to fit dominant modes of purified knowledge distribution
\mathcal{K}
, avoiding void spaces. This can improve the effectiveness of SEED significantly.

Additionally, conventional knowledge distillation is performed on the ground truth token sequences provided by the dataset, with the information solely derived from the logits. In SEED, to better distill the purified knowledge into the model, we propose performing distillation on sequences sampled from the purified knowledge distribution. In SEED, the loss is computed as follows:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}=\mathcal{D}_{KL}\left(Softmax(\mathcal{L}ogits_{d})\parallel Softmax(\mathcal{L}ogits_{p})\right)

(15)

\left\{\begin{aligned} \mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{S}}&=\mathcal{L}_{CE}\left(Softmax(\mathcal{L}ogits_{d}),y_{s}\right),P=\gamma\\
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{S}}&=\mathcal{L}_{CE}\left(Softmax(\mathcal{L}ogits_{d}),y_{t}\right),P=1-\gamma\end{aligned}\right.

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}+\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{S}}

where
y_{s}
represents sampled sequence from purified knowledge distribution,
\gamma
is the probability of distillation applied to the sampled token sequence, and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{D}}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathcal{S}}
represent the distillation loss and supervision loss, respectively.

5.
Hallucination Elimination Adapter

SEED extracts knowledge from an LVLM, purifies it, and then distills it back into the same LVLM, enabling the self-evolution of the LVLM. During this process, the LVLM is continuously updated. Using a continuously updating LVLM as the knowledge source can lead to unstable knowledge extraction. The unstable knowledge is then used for further updates to the LVLM, creating a feedback loop that causes instability in the SEED process. To ensure the stability of the SEED process, the stability of the knowledge source must be guaranteed. However, creating a frozen separate copy of the original LVLM as the knowledge source leads to a significant increase in VRAM consumption during the distillation process.

6.1.
Experimental Settings

Baseline Models.
We selected two representative open-source LVLMs as baselines: LLaVA-1.5
(
Liu et al. 2024a
)
and InternVL2
(
Chen et al. 2024a
)
. LLaVA-1.5 is the most widely used baseline, while InternVL2 offers the most complete range of model scales, allowing us to validate the generalization of our method on larger-scale models. In addition to these original models, we also compared against two advanced LVLM hallucination mitigation methods: Visual Contrast Decoding
(
Leng et al. 2024a
)
, which doubles the inference time, and Caption Rewriting
(
Wang et al. 2024b
)
, which rewrites the training data.

Implementation Details.
For our SEED experiments, we chose the LLaVA 665k instruction tuning dataset as the hallucination probing dataset, and randomly select 1% data in it to estimate the distribution of confidence. In the hyperparameter configuration, we set
\alpha
to 0.3,
\gamma
to 0.1,
G
to 4 and
\lambda
to 0.1. For LLaVA,
\beta_{0}
to
\beta_{3}
are set to 1.1, 0.8, 0.6, 0.4. For InternVL2, they are set to 0.5, 0.2, 0.1, 0.05. During SEED, the Hallucination Elimination Adapter utilizes a rank-128 LoRA
(
Hu et al. 2021
)
with alpha set to 256. The learning rates for LLaVA and InternVL are set to 2e-4 and 4e-5, respectively. The SEED process is conducted for one epoch.

6.2.
Evaluation Benchmarks

In our experiment, we selected three widely used benchmarks for assessing LVLM hallucinations: POPE
(
Li et al. 2023b
)
, MME
(
Fu et al. 2024
)
and MM-Vet
(
Yu et al. 2023
)
.

Figure 6.
Comparison of different hallucination mitigation methods on MME with LLaVA-1.5-7B
(
Liu et al. 2024a
)
.

POPE
(Polling-based Object Probing Evaluation)
(
Li et al. 2023b
)
is a method used to assess object hallucinations in LVLMs. In POPE, the model is required to determine whether a specific object is present in a given image. The MSCOCO
(
Lin et al. 2014
)
dataset is used as the source of test data, and POPE constructs query data with three different settings:
Random setting
, where negative objects are randomly selected;
Popular setting
, where negative objects come from the most frequent objects in the dataset; and
Adversarial setting
, where negative objects are highly related to existing objects in the image but do not actually appear in it. The evaluation results are based on multiple metrics, including accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score, providing a comprehensive assessment of the model’s handling of object hallucinations.

MME

(
Fu et al. 2024
)
aims to provide a comprehensive evaluation of LVLMs on both perceptual and cognitive abilities. The perceptual evaluation focuses on basic information recognition, such as the existence, count, position, and color, while the cognitive evaluation includes tasks such as commonsense reasoning, numerical calculations, text translation, and code reasoning. By employing simple “yes" or “no" instructions, MME is able to quantitatively analyze the model outputs, ensuring the accuracy and objectivity.

MM-Vet

(
Yu et al. 2023
)
is a comprehensive benchmark used to assess six core capabilities of LVLMs. Compared to the single-word responses of POPE and MME, MM-Vet provides natural long-text answers, with the response quality evaluated by GPT-4.

We conducted three types of ablation experiments to explore the effectiveness of various configurations: ablation of the distillation type, ablation of the Hallucination Identification, and ablation of the Hallucination Elimination Adapter.

In the distillation type ablation, we compared forward KLD, forward KLD + reverse KLD, and JSD. The results are shown in

Additionally, we performed an ablation experiment on the Hallucination Elimination Adapter and Hallucination Identification. As shown in

After applying SEED, the model’s responses align with the image input, achieving consistency, which demonstrates the value of our SEED in practical applications.

7.
Conclusion

In this paper, we analyze the primary source of hallucinations in LVLMs: biases within the internal knowledge. Based on this, we propose SEED, a method that can identify hallucination-related knowledge within the model, isolates and purges it, and then distills knowledge without hallucinations back into the model, thereby purifying the model’s output distribution. This approach mitigates hallucinations in LVLMs without increasing inference overhead or requiring any external tools. Through experiments on multiple LVLMs, our SEED demonstrates significant improvements across various hallucination metrics compared to original models and other mitigating methods, validating the effectiveness of SEED.
