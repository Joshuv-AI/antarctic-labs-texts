Nutritional methionine restriction modulates gut microbiota and propionate/GPR41 signaling to mitigate diabetic kidney disease
Authors: Yuyu Zhang, Da Wang, Chuhan Wang, Yiju Li, Jingxi Pang, Xuebo Liu, Zhigang Liu, Tian Yuan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiabetic kidney disease (DKD) is a major diabetes complication lacking effective dietary therapies. While amino-acid-targeted diets modulate metabolic health, their impact on renal outcomes via gut microbiota-derived metabolites remains unclear. Here, we investigated whether a 13-week methionine-restricted (MR) diet improves renal outcomes in db/db mice and alters gut-derived short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) and their receptors. MR improved renal function and histopathology, restored colonic barrier markers (Claudin-1, ZO-1, MUC2), and reduced circulating endotoxin (LPS). 16S rRNA profiling revealed MR-induced gut microbiota remodeling, enriching SCFA-producing taxa like Clostridium spp. This coincided with increased fecal propionate and restored colonic propionate receptor GPR41 expression. Furthermore, fecal propionate and its associated taxa correlated positively with barrier integrity and negatively with systemic LPS, renal TLR4/JNK activation, inflammation, and oxidative stress. Collectively, MR confers renoprotection in db/db mice by modulating the gut–kidney axis via microbiota remodeling and enhanced propionate-GPR41 signaling, highlighting MR as a promising nutritional strategy for DKD management. Subject terms: Diseases, Endocrinology, Microbiology, Nephrology

Introduction

The escalating prevalence of diabetes has consequently led to an increased incidence of age-related chronic diseases like diabetic kidney disease (DKD), which stands as the foremost cause of chronic kidney disease (CKD) worldwide1. Indeed, studies indicate that a substantial proportion of individuals living with diabetes, ranging from 20% to 40%, will eventually develop this debilitating condition2. This high prevalence underscores the urgent need for effective therapeutic and preventative strategies to mitigate the devastating impact of DKD on patient health and healthcare systems.

The gut microbiota, a vast and complex community of microorganisms residing in the gastrointestinal tract, has emerged as a critical modulator of systemic health and plays a significant role in the pathogenesis of various diseases, including kidney disease3. The intricate communication network between the gut microbiota and the kidney, often referred to as the gut–kidney axis, is increasingly recognized as a key factor influencing renal function in both health and disease4. In the context of DKD, alterations in the composition and function of the gut microbiota, termed dysbiosis, are frequently observed5. A hallmark of this dysbiosis is the depletion of short-chain fatty acid (SCFA)-producing bacteria6. This imbalance can disrupt the delicate homeostasis of the gut environment, leading to increased intestinal permeability and the translocation of harmful microbial products, such as lipopolysaccharides (LPS) and various uremic toxins, into the systemic circulation7. These gut-derived factors can trigger systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, and the activation of the innate immune system, all of which contribute to the development and progression of renal injury in diabetic patients. The bidirectional nature of the gut–kidney axis is further underscored by the fact that impaired kidney function in DKD can itself contribute to gut dysbiosis, creating a vicious cycle that exacerbates both gut and kidney pathology7. Understanding the precise mechanisms by which the gut microbiota influences renal health in the context of diabetes is crucial for developing targeted therapeutic interventions.

Dietary interventions have long been considered an integral part of managing chronic kidney disease, including DKD8. Low-protein diets (LPDs) have demonstrated the ability to retard the decline of renal function in advanced stages of CKD and are often recommended in clinical practice9,10. The potential benefits of diets such as vegan, vegetarian, and Mediterranean patterns in managing metabolic health, including diabetes, have also been recognized, and these diets may inherently involve a lower intake of certain amino acids11,12. In this context, methionine restriction (MR), a dietary strategy involving the reduction of the essential amino acid methionine, has garnered significant attention for its broad-spectrum health benefits observed in preclinical studies13–15. MR has been shown to extend lifespan, improve glucose tolerance, reduce adiposity, and attenuate oxidative stress and inflammation in various animal models16. Interestingly, a limited number of studies have reported a potential elevation of methionine levels in patients with DKD10, highlighting the potential relevance of methionine metabolism in the pathogenesis of this condition. Notably, MR has also demonstrated renoprotective effects, delaying the progression of kidney disease and reducing kidney injury in mice. Furthermore, research suggests a potential link between MR and the modulation of the gut microbiota14, indicating a possible mechanism through which MR might exert its beneficial effects on systemic and renal health. However, a critical gap in knowledge persists: it remains unknown how these MR-induced alterations in the gut microbiota mechanistically translate into protection for the diabetic kidney.

Given the compelling preclinical evidence for the benefits of methionine restriction and its potential link to the gut–kidney axis, the current investigation aims to characterize MR-associated renal phenotypes using the db/db mouse model, a well-established model for type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and DKD. Furthermore, this study seeks to explore potential gut–kidney crosstalk pathways that may be associated with observed renal improvements. This study may provide foundational insights for developing dietary strategies for DKD management.

Results

MR alleviates renal injury and suppresses fibrosis in diabetic mice

To define the therapeutic potential of MR in DKD, 8-week-old db/db mice and their wild-type (m/m) littermates were fed either a normal diet (ND) or a methionine-restricted (MR) diet for 13 weeks (Fig. 1A). Previous studies have extensively reported that db/db mice exhibit typical features of DKD, including albuminuria, impaired creatinine clearance, and distinct glomerular histological lesions that align with established benchmarks17–20, making them a robust model for studying DKD. Our subsequent functional and morphological results are highly consistent with these previous reports19, confirming the successful establishment of the DKD model. MR significantly reduced kidney weight in both m/m (p < 0.01) and db/db (p < 0.05) mice compared to their ND counterparts, with db/db mice exhibiting markedly elevated kidney weight under ND conditions (p < 0.01) (Fig. 1B). Serum creatinine level was elevated in db/db+ND mice (p < 0.01), whereas MR intervention substantially lowered serum creatinine (p < 0.01) and urea nitrogen (BUN) levels (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1C, D).

Morphological and functional alterations to renal glomeruli and renal tubule are one of the hallmarks of DKD. Histopathological analysis revealed severe renal injury in db/db+ND mice, including tubular epithelial cell vacuolization, disorganized cell arrangement, necrosis, tubular dilation, and glomerular hypertrophy. These pathological alterations were significantly ameliorated by MR (Fig. 1E). Quantitative analysis of H&E-stained kidney sections revealed that the renal injury rate (%) was significantly higher in the db/db+ND group compared with the m/m+ND group (p < 0.01). MR treatment markedly reduced the renal injury rate in db/db mice (p < 0.01) (Fig. S1). Masson’s trichrome staining demonstrated increased collagen deposition in db/db+ND kidneys compared with m/m+ND group (p < 0.05), which was attenuated by MR (p < 0.01) (Fig. 1F, G). Consistent with the histological findings, fibrogenic genes such as fibronectin (p < 0.05) and Tgf-β (p < 0.01) were significantly upregulated in db/db+ND kidneys. Importantly, MR intervention in db/db mice effectively reversed this, significantly downregulating Tgf-β (p < 0.01), alongside fibronectin and Col3a1 (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1H–J).

These data establish that MR effectively mitigates the structural and functional damage characteristic of DKD.

MR attenuates renal inflammation and oxidative stress in db/db mice

Building on the observed fibrotic improvements, we further investigated renal inflammatory and oxidative stress responses. Proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines-tumor necrosis factor alpha (Tnf-α) (p < 0.01), interleukin 1 beta (Il-1β) (p < 0.05), interleukin-6 (Il-6) (p < 0.01), and C-X-C motif chemokine 10 (Cxcl10) (p < 0.05) were markedly upregulated in db/db+ND kidneys compared to m/m+ND controls. MR intervention significantly suppressed their expression (p < 0.01 for Tnf-α, p < 0.05 for Il-1β, Il-6, and Cxcl10) (Fig. 2A–D).

Concurrently, antioxidant defenses were compromised in db/db+ND mice, evidenced by downregulated superoxide dismutase 1 (Sod1) (p < 0.01) and superoxide dismutase 2 (Sod2) (p < 0.05). And MR elevated their expression (p < 0.01 for Sod1, p = 0.0579 for Sod2) (Fig. 2E, F). NADPH oxidase 2 (Nox2) and NADPH oxidase 4 (Nox4) cooperate with Sod1 and Sod2 to regulate redox homeostasis by generating reactive oxygen species. Conversely, Nox2 (p < 0.05) and Nox4 (p < 0.05) were elevated in db/db+ND kidneys, with MR effectively reducing their expression (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2G, H). Immunohistochemical staining for 8-hydroxy-2’-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG), a marker of oxidative DNA damage, revealed intense nuclear staining in db/db+ND renal glomerular and tubular cells compared with m/m+ND group (p < 0.01), which was attenuated by MR (p < 0.01) (Fig. 2I, J).

These results indicate that MR suppresses renal proinflammatory expression, restores antioxidant capacity, and mitigates oxidative DNA damage in db/db mice.

MR strengthens intestinal barrier integrity in db/db mice

To investigate a potential gut-centric origin for these renal benefits, we assessed intestinal integrity. H&E staining revealed mild colonic architectural abnormalities in both m/m+MR and db/db+ND mice compared to m/m+ND controls (Fig. 3A), characterized by shortened crypt depth (p < 0.01) and reduced muscularis thickness (p < 0.01). MR intervention partially restored these structural parameters in db/db mice (p < 0.01) (Fig. 3B, C). Additionally, immunofluorescence and RT-qPCR analysis demonstrated diminished Claudin-1 expression in m/m+MR (p < 0.01 for protein, p < 0.05 for mRNA) and db/db+ND mice (p < 0.01 for protein, p < 0.05 for mRNA), which MR significantly rescued in db/db mice (p < 0.01 for protein, p < 0.05 for mRNA) (Fig. 3D–F). Similarly, Zona Occludens 1 (Zo-1) mRNA expression, downregulated in db/db+ND mice (p < 0.01), was upregulated by MR (p < 0.05) (Fig. 3G), which are critical for limiting paracellular permeability.

MR stimulates mucin production and reduces intestinal inflammation in db/db mice

Since intestinal barrier integrity depends on both tight junction proteins (Claudin-1 and ZO-1) to seal paracellular spaces and a robust mucus layer to restrict LPS translocation, a process that significantly impacts DKD21, we comprehensively evaluated these representative barrier markers alongside goblet cell function, colonic inflammation, and circulating LPS levels. Alcian blue staining revealed reduced goblet cell density per unit area in m/m+MR (p < 0.01) and db/db+ND mice (p < 0.01) compared to m/m+ND controls, whereas MR restored goblet cell numbers in db/db mice (p < 0.01) (Fig. 4A, B). Consistent with this, mucin 2 (MUC2), a core secretory mucin, was downregulated in db/db+ND mice at both protein (p < 0.01) and mRNA levels (p < 0.05). MR intervention significantly upregulated MUC2 expression in db/db mice (p < 0.01 for protein, p < 0.05 for mRNA) (Fig. 4C–E). Concurrently, MR modulated intestinal inflammatory responses. The mRNA expression of Tnf-α (p = 0.0597) was elevated in db/db+ND colons compared to m/m+ND, while MR suppressed the mRNA expression of Tnf-α (p < 0.01) and Il-6 (p < 0.05) and enhanced anti-inflammatory interleukin-10 (Il-10) (p < 0.05) in db/db mice (Fig. 4F–H). Serum LPS levels, elevated in db/db+ND mice (p < 0.05), were reduced by MR (p < 0.05) (Fig. 4I).

MR modifies gut microbiota composition and enriches SCFAs-producing bacteria in db/db mice

We next hypothesized that MR-associated improvements in gut health might be linked to shifts in the microbiota. 16S rRNA gene sequencing of fecal samples revealed distinct gut microbiota profiles across groups. No significant changes were observed in α-diversity across groups (Fig. 5A). Partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) of the 16S rRNA data revealed clear separation among the four groups (Fig. 5B). Beta diversity analysis based on weighted UniFrac distance matrix revealed a significant overall difference among the four groups (PERMANOVA, p = 4.79639331697061e-7). Pairwise PERMANOVA analysis showed significant differences between m/m+ND and db/db+ND (p = 6.16093022508257e-10), as well as between m/m+ND and m/m+MR (p = 0.0118755819622702). However, no significant difference was observed between db/db+ND and db/db+MR (p = 0.124461591988866) (Fig. 5C). These results suggest that genotype had a strong impact on microbial community structure, whereas MR did not significantly alter beta diversity in db/db mice. A Venn diagram was generated to compare the overlapping and unique operational taxonomic units (OTUs) among the four groups. A total of 359 OTUs were shared across all groups, representing a core microbial community. In addition, each group contained a number of unique OTUs, with 24 in the m/m+ND group, 24 in the m/m+MR group, 22 in the db/db+ND group, and 23 in the db/db+MR group. The number of unique OTUs was comparable between the db/db+ND and db/db+MR groups (Fig. 5D).

Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size (LEfSe) analysis identified Bacteroidetes, Bacteroidia, Bacteroidaceae, Bacteroides, and Porphyromonadaceae (LDA score >4.0, p < 0.001) as the most discriminative taxon enriched in db/db+MR mice (Fig. 5E, F). Volcano plots comparing db/db mice fed a normal diet with those fed an MR diet further revealed that, in the db/db+MR group, several taxa were significantly upregulated, including Clostridium propionicum, Clostridium methylpentosum, members of the Ruminococcaceae family, Clostridium Ⅳ, Mucispirillum schaedleri, and Lachnospiraceae. Conversely, taxa such as those in the Porphyromonadaceae family were significantly downregulated (Fig. 5G, H). Bar charts for individual taxa further quantified the relative abundance changes of significantly up- and downregulated bacteria (Fig. 5I–O). It is worth noting that Ruminococcaceae, Clostridium Ⅳ, and Lachnospiraceae are broadly recognized as butyrate (and acetate) producers22, whereas Clostridium propionicum is known to a well-characterized obligate propionate producer via the propanediol fermentation pathway23.

These results indicate that MR is associated with altered gut microbiota composition, including enrichment of taxa potentially linked to SCFAs production.

MR increases propionate and upregulates colonic GPR41 in db/db mice

Given the observed changes in SCFAs-producing taxa, we quantified fecal SCFAs in experimental groups. SCFAs, primarily acetic acid, propionic acid, and butyric acid (which collectively account for 90% of SCFAs), as well as valeric acid, isobutyric acid, isovaleric acid, and hexanoic acid, are generated through glycolysis by anaerobic bacteria or yeast24. Marked declines were observed in acetate (p < 0.01), propionate (p < 0.01), isobutyrate (p < 0.01), butyrate (p < 0.01), isovalerate (p < 0.05), and valerate (p < 0.01) in db/db+ND mice compared to m/m+ND controls. While MR also reduced acetate (p < 0.01), propionate (p < 0.01), isobutyrate (p < 0.01), butyrate (p < 0.01), and valerate in m/m mice (p < 0.01), it selectively restored propionate (p < 0.05), isobutyrate (p < 0.05), and valerate (p < 0.05) in db/db mice (Fig. 6A–F). To explore SCFA signaling, we analyzed colonic receptors Ffar3 (G-protein coupled receptor 41, GPR41), Ffar2 (GPR43), and Hcar2 (GPR109A). Only Ffar3 was downregulated in db/db+ND (p < 0.01) and m/m+MR mice (p < 0.01) versus m/m+ND group. MR was associated with higher free fatty acid receptor 3 (Ffar3) expression in db/db mice compared with db/db+ND (p < 0.05), whereas free fatty acid receptor 2 (Ffar2) and hydroxycarboxylic acid receptor 2 (Hcar2) remained unchanged (Fig. 6G–I). Immunofluorescence confirmed reduced GPR41 protein expression in db/db+ND (p < 0.01) and m/m+MR mice (p < 0.05), and MR-fed db/db mice showed relatively higher GPR41 staining than db/db+ND (p < 0.01) (Fig. 6J, K).

Association between propionate-GPR41 pathway and renal effects of MR via TLR4/JNK signaling

To determine whether the MR-induced reduction in circulating endotoxins directly mitigates renal injury, we next examined the expression of Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)—the primary receptor for LPS in the kidney25. Tlr4 mRNA expression was significantly upregulated in db/db+ND kidneys compared to m/m+ND controls (p < 0.01), which was lower in MR-fed db/db mice compared with db/db+ND mice (p < 0.01) (Fig. 7A). While butyrate is classically recognized for its critical role in maintaining gut barrier function in general, our study specifically emphasized propionate. This is because the MR intervention selectively restored fecal levels of propionate, alongside isobutyrate and valerate, and specifically upregulated the propionate receptor GPR41 in db/db mice. It is noteworthy that previous studies have similarly linked reduced propionate levels to the progressive loss of renal function in patients with chronic kidney disease26. Furthermore, rather than attributing this metabolic shift to a single species, the MR-induced elevation in propionate likely results from the collective enrichment of a broader consortium of SCFA-producing taxa, including Ruminococcaceae, Lachnospiraceae, and various Clostridium spp.22. Within this network, specific members such as Clostridium propionicum can potently drive propionate synthesis via the well-characterized propanediol fermentation pathway23. This specific restoration of the propionate-GPR41 signaling axis suggests a targeted mechanistic link to the observed renoprotective phenotypes. To further explore this gut–kidney crosstalk, we conducted a comprehensive correlation analysis focusing on the relationships among fecal propionate concentrations, the abundance of SCFA-producing taxa (such as Clostridium spp.), colonic GPR41 expression, and key renal inflammatory and biochemical markers. Correlation analyses revealed a strong positive association between Clostridium spp. (Clostridium propionicum) abundance and fecal propionate levels (Pearson’s r = 0.77, p < 0.01) (Fig. 7B). Notably, fecal propionate inversely correlated with serum LPS (Pearson’s r = −0.74, p < 0.05), while serum LPS exhibited negative correlations with colonic Claudin-1 mRNA (Pearson’s r = −0.8, p < 0.01) and positive correlations with renal Tlr4 (Pearson’s r = 0.85, p < 0.01), Il-1β (Pearson’s r = 0.66, p < 0.05), and Tnf-α (Pearson’s r = 0.84, p < 0.01) (Fig. 7B). Furthermore, serum creatinine and urea nitrogen levels inversely correlated with Clostridium propionicum abundance (Pearson’s r = −0.63 and −0.66, p < 0.05) and fecal propionate (Pearson’s r = −0.81 and −0.71, p < 0.01 and p < 0.05), and positively correlated with serum LPS (Pearson’s r = 0.83 and 0.80, p < 0.01). Western blot analysis confirmed elevated TLR4 and phosphorylated c-Jun N-terminal kinase (p-JNK) protein levels in db/db+ND kidneys (p < 0.05), which were suppressed by MR (p < 0.05) (Fig. 7C–E, Fig. S2).

Taken together, these findings demonstrate that MR is associated with higher fecal propionate levels, increased colonic GPR41 expression, and reduced markers of renal inflammation and dysfunction in db/db mice. These associations suggest a potential link between gut-derived propionate and renal outcomes, which warrants further causal investigation.

Discussion

The key outcomes of this study demonstrate that MR was associated with improvements in renal function and reductions in histopathological abnormalities, as well as lower renal inflammation and oxidative stress. Furthermore, MR appeared to positively influence the gut environment, enhancing intestinal barrier integrity, reducing intestinal inflammation, and reshaping gut microbiota composition, notably increasing the abundance of SCFAs-producing bacteria. This dietary intervention was also linked to elevated fecal propionate levels and increased colonic GPR41 expression. Collectively, these findings suggest a potential association between gut-derived propionate, signaling through GPR41, and the observed renal protective effects, although further causal studies are required to confirm this mechanism.

The db/db mouse model recapitulates key features of human DKD, including glomerulosclerosis, tubular injury, and albuminuria17. Notably, our results show that serum BUN levels were not significantly elevated in db/db+ND mice compared to controls (Fig. 1). This is an expected observation for this model at 21 weeks of age, representing an early-to-moderate stage of DKD18, and is highly consistent with previous reports demonstrating that BUN frequently remains unaltered in db/db mice for up to 20 weeks, despite the presence of elevated creatinine and distinct renal structural lesions19,20,27. Addressing these structural lesions, our histopathological findings-amelioration of tubular vacuolization, necrosis, and glomerular hypertrophy-suggest that MR disrupts structural degeneration at multiple levels (Fig. 1). These effects likely stem from MR’s dual role in mitigating hyperglycemia-induced oxidative stress and suppressing fibrogenic pathways. The downregulation of Tgf-β, fibronectin, and Col3a1 aligns with studies showing that MR reduces mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) production and inhibits profibrotic signaling in renal cells (Figs. 1 and 2)28. Importantly, MR’s renoprotection appears distinct from general protein restriction, as methionine-specific depletion uniquely modulates glutathione synthesis and hydrogen sulfide (H2S) metabolism, both critical for redox homeostasis29. Notably, serum BUN levels were not significantly elevated in db/db+ND mice compared to controls. This is an expected observation for this model at 21 weeks of age, representing an early-to-moderate stage of DKD, and is highly consistent with previous reports demonstrating that BUN frequently remains unaltered in db/db mice for up to 20 weeks, despite the presence of distinct renal structural lesions and elevated creatinine.

Our observation that MR improves renal function and structure (Figs. 1 and 2) aligns with previous preclinical work30,31. For instance, sulfur-containing amino acid (SAA) restriction has outperformed fasting-mimicking and ketogenic diets in rodent models of acute kidney injury, reducing fibrosis and inflammatory infiltration31–33. However, translating these benefits to humans remains challenging, as evidenced by the UNICORN trial, where preoperative SAA restriction failed to reduce postoperative acute kidney injury in cardiac surgery patients despite good tolerability34. This discrepancy underscores the complexity of dietary interventions in heterogeneous clinical populations and emphasizes the need for optimized protocols, such as prolonged MR duration or patient stratification based on metabolic profiles.

A central finding of our study is the restoration of gut barrier integrity by MR (Fig. 3). In the diabetic state, hyperglycemia and inflammation compromise the gut epithelial lining, leading to the “leaky gut” phenomenon35. MR reversed these deleterious changes, suggesting enhanced epithelial renewal and barrier maintenance. Tight junction (TJ) proteins such as Claudin-1, Occludin, and ZO-1 form the paracellular barrier that prevents luminal toxins from accessing the lamina propria36. We observed that MR significantly increased colonic Claudin-1 and ZO-1 expression at both mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 3), correlating with decreased serum LPS levels (Fig. 4). This aligns with work that MR enhances transepithelial electrical resistance and modifies TJ protein composition, including increased Claudin-3 and altered Occludin post-translational modification, in rat colon ex vivo37. Similarly, in aged mice, MR restored diurnal oscillations of TJ gene expression and improved barrier integrity, highlighting its conserved effect across models38. These findings suggest that MR strengthens TJs, limiting paracellular permeability and endotoxin translocation. Goblet cells and mucin secretion represent another essential line of defense. Alcian blue staining revealed that MR restored goblet cell density in db/db colons (Fig. 4). MR also upregulated MUC2 expression, indicating enhanced mucus layer thickness. It is reported that MR balances the redox status via enhanced transsulfuration and H₂S production39, thereby alleviating ER stress in goblet cells and promoting mucin synthesis40. Furthermore, MR attenuated colonic inflammatory markers in db/db mice (Fig. 4), creating a permissive environment for barrier integrity repair41. This finding provides a direct mechanistic link between MR’s effects in the gut and the reduction of inflammatory stimuli reaching the kidney.

This gut barrier enhancement is associated with specific shifts in the gut microbiota. Given that dietary methionine is primarily absorbed in the proximal small intestine with minimal intact amino acids reaching the colon, MR likely influences these distal microbial communities through indirect, host-mediated mechanisms. Specifically, altered host systemic sulfur metabolism42, shifts in bile acid profiles43, or a modified upstream mucosal environment may cascade into structural changes37 in the distal gut microbial community. This spatial remodeling is highly relevant to our findings, as meta-analyses studies consistently report that patients with CKD exhibit reduced fecal SCFA concentrations, accompanied by a depletion of SCFAs-producing bacteria such as Phyla Actinobacteria, Firmicutes and Proteobacteria44. 16S rRNA gene sequencing revealed distinct microbial community shifts with MR, including enrichment of taxa with propionate-producing potential (such as Clostridium spp.) in db/db+MR mice (Fig. 5). LEfSe and volcano plot analyses highlighted significant upregulation of Bacteroidetes, Bacteroidia, Bacteroidaceae, and Porphyromonadaceae in MR-treated mice (Fig. 5). These alterations are consistent with a gut environment enriched in SCFAs-producing and potentially anti-inflammatory taxa, a pattern previously associated with improved metabolic outcomes in obesity and insulin resistance models45. It should be noted that 16S rRNA gene sequencing only provides genus- or OTU-level resolution, and functional inference is limited. The association with SCFA production is putative and requires validation by metagenomic or metabolomic analyses. Reflecting the microbiota changes, MR selectively restored fecal propionate, isobutyrate, and valerate levels in db/db mice (Fig. 6). Acetate, propionate, and butyrate are the three most common SCFAs (which collectively account for 90% of SCFAs), exerting renoprotective effects such as anti-inflammatory, anti-atherosclerotic, and antioxidant functions46. Studies found that GPR41 and GPR43 exhibit the highest responsiveness to propionate, while both receptors are also activated by acetate and butyrate47. While butyrate is classically recognized as a master regulator of gut barrier function48, our study specifically highlighted propionate due to its selective restoration by MR in db/db mice alongside the specific upregulation of its receptor, GPR41. Propionate, acting via its receptor GPR41, enhances barrier integrity and suppresses NF‑κB-driven cytokine release in the gut49. Isobutyric acid activates GPR109A, thereby promoting Claudin-1 expression and enhancing intestinal barrier function50. Valeric acid exhibits higher affinity for GPR43 than for GPR41 in intestinal contexts, as demonstrated by receptor activation assays and functional studies47. We demonstrated that MR restored colonic GPR41 expression in db/db+ND mice, where it had been downregulated; this restoration was specific to GPR41 and did not occur for GPR43 or GPR109A (Fig. 6). Importantly, in rodent models of CKD, propionate supplementation or colonization with propionate-producing taxa ameliorated renal fibrosis, attenuated oxidative stress and inflammation, suggesting a causal role for SCFA depletion in CKD pathophysiology51. This axis links microbial metabolism to host signaling, suggesting that increased propionate-GPR41 engagement may contribute to improved gut barrier function and systemic anti‑inflammatory effects. However, potential beneficial effects of isobutyrate and valerate in db/db mice intestine and kidney cannot be excluded.

Interestingly, the apparent paradoxical effects of MR—where fecal SCFA profiles were reduced in m/m+MR mice but restored in db/db+MR mice, alongside differential impacts on goblet cells and MUC2—likely demonstrate host-microbiota context dependency rather than a uniform dietary response. Dietary MR has been shown to reshape the gut microbial community and metabolite profiles in a host-dependent manner, improving SCFAs production and barrier function in metabolically disturbed models while sometimes reducing certain fermentation products in healthy animals52,53. Importantly, SCFAs such as propionate and butyrate directly stimulate MUC2 expression and goblet-cell function in intestinal epithelium, but their effects are concentration- and context-dependent, so changes in mucin production can differ between healthy and dysbiotic hosts54. Moreover, taxon abundance does not necessarily equate to metabolic output23. In db/db mice, which exhibit baseline dysbiosis and altered substrate flux, MR may selectively favor taxa or metabolic routes that enhance propionate (and related SCFAs) production, potentially contributing to restoration of MUC2 expression and mucus layer integrity. Conversely, in metabolically normal (m/m) mice, MR reduced overall fermentable substrates, lowered several SCFAs, and decreased goblet cell density. This suggests that while MR effectively corrects dysbiosis and barrier dysfunction in a diabetic state, it may exert mild metabolic stress or unintended adverse effects in a healthy host, underscoring that the benefits of such dietary interventions are highly context-dependent and should be carefully modified to baseline metabolic health.

The suppression of renal TLR4 and p-JNK by MR (Fig. 7) offers critical insights into how gut-derived signals exacerbate diabetic nephropathy. TLR4 activation by LPS triggers MyD88-dependent NF-κB and MAPK pathways, fostering renal inflammation and fibrosis55. Concurrently, hyperglycemia-induced JNK activation promotes apoptosis and extracellular matrix deposition via c-Jun phosphorylation56. Our findings suggest that MR disrupts this vicious cycle by reducing LPS translocation (via gut barrier repair) and directly inhibiting TLR4/JNK signaling. Propionate may play a role in this process, potentially by interacting with GPR41 to influence intestinal tight junctions and possibly modulating TLR4 signaling in renal cells, although causality remains to be confirmed, as observed in colitis models where SCFAs block TLR4-NF-κB crosstalk57. Interestingly, MR’s suppression of oxidative DNA damage (8-OHdG) (Fig. 2) may also involve TLR4/JNK signaling modulation. JNK activation exacerbates mitochondrial ROS production, while TLR4 signaling amplifies NADPH oxidase (NOX)-dependent oxidative stress58. The observed downregulation of Nox2 and Nox4 (Fig. 2) by MR may be associated with reduced enzymatic and mitochondrial ROS production, suggesting a potential antioxidant effect.

The robust renoprotection conferred by MR raises the question of whether this benefit is a byproduct of systemic glycemic control or a direct result of gut-microbiota remodeling. These pathways are highly synergistic. Our previous study showed that a 13-week MR intervention significantly lowered fasting blood glucose and HOMA-IR in db/db mice, independent of body weight changes13. While necessary to alleviate systemic glucotoxicity, strict glycemic control alone is insufficient to reverse pre-existing renal damage, largely because epigenetic alterations sustain persistent cellular dysfunction and inflammation (i.e., metabolic memory)59. The MR-mediated recovery of intestinal barrier function, subsequent decline in systemic endotoxemia, and potentiation of propionate-GPR41 signaling create a critical anti-inflammatory and anti-fibrotic buffer. Thus, MR limits DKD progression by pairing broad metabolic improvements with targeted suppression of gut-derived inflammatory signaling in the kidney.

This study has several limitations. First, experiments were conducted only in male mice, so the findings may not necessarily apply to females. Second, the observed gut–kidney associations are correlative rather than causal, and fecal short-chain fatty acid measurements provide only indirect estimates of active concentrations in the colon and systemic circulation. Additionally, the absence of plasma SCFA measurements limits our ability to fully evaluate the systemic availability and direct renal exposure to these metabolites. Furthermore, analyses based solely on 16S rRNA gene sequencing are limited, as they do not capture strain-level variation or actual microbial activity, and thus should be interpreted with caution. Finally, the clinical translatability of these findings remains uncertain. Future studies using germ-free or GPR41-knockout models, targeted microbial colonization, and propionate supplementation will be necessary to validate causality and assess relevance in females and human subjects.

In summary, our 13-week methionine-restricted diet protects against DKD in db/db mice by selectively remodeling the gut microbiota to enrich a consortium of taxa with propionate-producing potential, elevating fecal propionate and restoring colonic GPR41. This propionate-GPR41 signaling is associated with improved intestinal barrier integrity, lower circulating LPS, and reduced markers of renal TLR4/JNK activation, inflammation, oxidative stress, and fibrosis. Together, although these findings are strongly associative, causal links remain to be experimentally validated, they nonetheless provide evidence that MR may provide foundational insights for developing dietary strategies for DKD management. Further mechanistic validation (e.g., germ-free, microbial transfer or receptor-loss models) and clinical evaluation are warranted.

Materials and methods

Animals and diets

Seven-week-old male C57BLKS/JGpt-Leprem2Cd/Gpt (db/db) mice and their m/m littermates were obtained from Gempharmatech Co., Ltd. (China) and housed individually under standard conditions in a temperature-(23 ± 1°C) and humidity-(40%) controlled animal room with a 12-hour light/dark cycle at the Northwest A&F University Experimental Animal Center. After a one-week acclimation period, db/db and m/m mice were fed either a normal diet (ND, containing 0.86% methionine, w/w) or a methionine-restricted diet (MR, containing 0.17% methionine, w/w) without cystine for 13 weeks. All diets were prepared and supplied by Trophic Animal Feed High-Tech Co., Ltd. (China), and the feed formula is detailed in Table S1. The methionine dose used in the feed formula was based on previous studies13. Both diets were formulated using an amino acid premix lacking methionine and cystine. Methionine was added separately to achieve the designated concentrations (0.17% for MR and 0.86% for ND). Cystine was not supplemented to avoid compensatory sulfur amino acid effects under MR60. To maintain isonitrogenous conditions, glutamate was added to the MR diet to compensate for the reduced methionine content, while total caloric density and macronutrient composition were kept identical between diets61. The mice were randomly divided into four groups: (a) m/m+ND (n = 10): m/m mice fed a normal diet, (b) m/m+MR (n = 10): m/m mice fed a methionine-restricted diet, (c) db/db+ND (n = 9): db/db mice fed a normal diet, and (d) db/db+MR (n = 6): db/db mice fed a methionine-restricted diet.

Mice were fasted overnight prior to euthanasia. Animals were deeply anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (100 mg/kg) and xylazine (10 mg/kg), a combination chosen for its rapid induction, reliable depth of anesthesia, and minimal cardiovascular depression. Adequate anesthesia was confirmed by the absence of pedal reflex before further procedures. Mice were then euthanized by cervical dislocation to ensure rapid death. Every effort was made to minimize animal suffering during all procedures. Kidney weights were measured immediately after isolation. All animal procedures were performed in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (Eighth Edition, ISBN-10: 0-309-15396-4) and were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Northwest A&F University (protocol number XN2023-0717).

Serum creatinine and blood urea nitrogen assays

Blood urea nitrogen (BUN) was quantified using a urease method kit (C013-2-1, Jiancheng Technology Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China). Briefly, 20 μL serum was mixed with reagent A (37 °C, 5 min), followed by reagent B (10 min), and absorbance measured at 640 nm. Creatinine (Cr) was assessed via sarcosine oxidase assay (C011-2-1, Jiancheng Technology Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China): 6 μL serum was incubated with working reagent (37 °C, 5 min), and absorbance read at 546 nm. Standard curves were generated using provided calibrators.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), MASSON, and Alcian blue staining

Colon and kidney tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 μm thickness. For H&E staining, deparaffinized sections were stained with hematoxylin for 5 min, differentiated in 1% acid ethanol, counterstained with eosin for 2 min, dehydrated, and mounted (G1120, Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., China). Masson’s trichrome staining (G1340, Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., China) was performed by first staining the sections with Weigert’s iron hematoxylin for 5 min, followed by ponceau magenta for 10 min, phosphomolybdic acid for 2 min, and aniline blue for 2 min to visualize collagen fibers (blue) and muscle/cytoplasm (red). For Alcian blue staining, sections were incubated in Alcian blue solution (pH 2.5) for 30 min to detect acidic mucopolysaccharides, followed by counterstaining with nuclear fast red (G1560, Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., China). All slides were imaged using a microscope (DM5000B, Leica, Germany). Three randomly selected, non-overlapping cortical fields per mouse were examined. A field was defined as abnormal when any histopathological alteration was observed. The renal injury rate (%) was calculated as the percentage of abnormal fields relative to the total fields examined. The crypt depth, muscular thickness, the ratio of collagen-positive area to total area and number of goblet cells per unit area (mm2) were quantified using ImageJ analysis software (National Institutes of Health, Scion Corporation, USA), with thresholds set to exclude background. All analyses were performed in a blinded manner.

Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence

After dewaxing and rehydration, sections intended for immunohistochemistry were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted in 0.01 M PBS. For example, anti-8-OHdG (Abcam, ab48508; 1:200) was used. Subsequently, the sections were washed with PBS and incubated with IgG-biotin-conjugated secondary antibodies for 30 min at room temperature. Next, the sections were incubated in a 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) solution containing 0.03% H₂O₂ for 5–8 min. Hematoxylin was then applied dropwise to counterstain the cell nuclei, and finally, the sections were sealed.

For immunofluorescence, sections were similarly incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies diluted in 0.01 M PBS, including anti-Claudin-1 (Abcam, ab15098; 1:300), anti-MUC2 (Abcam, ab308191; 1:500), and anti-GPR41 (HUABIO, ER63606; 1:500). After primary incubation, the sections were incubated with CY3-conjugated secondary antibodies for 2 h at room temperature. Following washing, the sections were mounted using a medium containing 4,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Sigma-Aldrich, F6057). Microscopic images were captured using a fluorescence microscope (DM5000B, Leica, Germany) and quantitatively analyzed with ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Scion Corporation, USA).

Serum lipopolysaccharide (LPS) assay

Serum LPS levels were measured using a commercial ELISA kit (Jiangsu Meibiao Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Real-time qPCR (RT-qPCR)

Tissue samples were homogenized in AG RNAex Pro reagent (AG21102, ACCURATE BIOTECHNOLOGY(HUNAN) CO.,LTD, ChangSha, China), and total RNA was isolated using chloroform-isopropanol extraction, followed by purification with 75% ethanol. RNA concentration and purity were assessed with a NanoDrop OneC spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Subsequently, 1 µg of RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using an All-in-One RT mix with DNase (R2020L, US Everbright, China). Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) was performed using 2×SYBR Green qPCR Mix with ROX (AH0104-B, Sparkjade Biotechnology, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Gene expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method and normalized to β-actin levels. Primer sequences are provided in Table S2.

16S rRNA microbiota sequencing and analysis

Fecal samples were collected from each group after the 13-week MR intervention and stored at –80 °C until DNA extraction. Sample processing, DNA isolation, and 16S rRNA gene sequencing were conducted by BGI (China). Microbial DNA was extracted from colonic luminal contents using the E.Z.N.A.® Stool DNA Kit (Omega Bio-Tek, USA). The V3–V4 regions of the 16S rRNA gene were amplified with primers 338F and 806R using a thermocycler PCR system. Operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were clustered at 97% sequence similarity from high-quality reads using USEARCH (version 7.0.1090). Downstream analyses, including Venn diagram construction, α-diversity analysis, β-diversity analysis, and visualization of species abundance (heatmaps and bar plots), were performed using QIIME2 (version 2018.11) and the R statistical environment. Partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) was conducted using the mixOmics package in R (version 3.2.1)62. Subsequent data analysis was conducted via the OmicStudio platform (https://www.omicstudio.cn/tool)63.

Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs)

Fecal short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) were quantified by gas chromatography (GC) as previously described64. Briefly, 200 mg of feces was homogenized with 1 mL of Milli-Q water and vortexed for 10 min at room temperature. Then, 150 μL of 50% (w/w) H₂SO₄ was added, and the mixture was thoroughly vortexed. After adding 1.6 mL of diethyl ether as the extraction solvent, samples were incubated on ice for 20 min. The organic phase was collected by centrifugation (8000 rpm, 5 min, 4 °C) and filtered through 0.2μm membranes (Tianjin Branch Billion Lung Experimental Equipment Co., Ltd.). The filtrate was transferred to GC vials for analysis. SCFA concentrations were determined using a GC-2014C gas chromatograph (Shimadzu, Japan) equipped with a DB-FFAP capillary column (30 m × 0.25 μm × 0.25 mm, Agilent, USA). Quantification was performed using external standard curves prepared with known concentrations of acetic, propionic, isobutyric, butyric, isovaleric, and valeric acids (Aladdin Bio-Chem Technology, China). Data acquisition and peak integration were conducted using Shimadzu GC Solution software. SCFA levels are reported as μg/g of feces.

Western blot

Total kidney proteins were extracted from six mice per group using a commercial protein extraction kit (PL001, ZhongHuiHeCai Biotechnology, China). Protein concentrations were determined with the BCA Protein Assay Kit (A55864, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes using a wet transfer system. Membranes were blocked with 5% (w/v) skim milk for 2 h at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-TLR4 (sc-293072, 1:1000), anti-β-actin (sc-47778, 1:2000), anti-phospho-JNK (sc-6254, 1:1000), and anti-JNK (sc-7345, 1:1000) (all from Santa Cruz Biotechnology, USA). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000) for 2 h at room temperature. Immunoreactive bands were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent and visualized with the ChemiDoc™ XRS+ imaging system (Bio-Rad, USA). Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Scion Corporation, USA).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 7.0 (GraphPad Software, USA). Data are presented as mean ± SEM unless otherwise indicated. Two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test was used for multi-group comparisons. Data normality was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For two-group comparisons where the data failed to meet the assumption of normality, the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. β diversity was evaluated using PERMANOVA based on weighted UniFrac distance matrix, and pairwise PERMANOVA was performed to assess differences between individual groups. Differential taxa among groups were identified using Linear Discriminant Analysis Effect Size (LEfSe), applying a nonparametric Kruskal-Wallis test for statistical significance (P < 0.05) and a logarithmic LDA score threshold with an absolute value > 2.0. Differential taxa were determined using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test with Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction (adjusted P < 0.05). Volcano plots were used for visualization of fold changes and statistical significance.
