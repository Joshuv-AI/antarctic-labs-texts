Hannaneh Akrami, Bhaskar Ray Chaudhury, Jugal Garg, Aniket Murhekar

Abstract
The immense success of ML systems relies heavily on large-scale, high-quality data. The high demand for such data has led to several paradigms that involve selling, exchanging, and sharing data. This naturally motivates studying economic processes involving data as an asset. However, data differs from classical economic assets in terms of free duplication – meaning there is no concept of limited supply, as data can be replicated at zero marginal cost. This distinction introduces fundamental differences between economic processes involving data and those concerning other assets. We investigate a parallel to exchange (Arrow-Debreu) markets in settings where data is the asset. In this setup, agents possessing datasets exchange data fairly and voluntarily, aiming for mutual benefit without monetary compensation. This framework is particularly relevant in settings involving non-profit organizations that seek to improve their ML models through data exchange with other organizations, yet are restricted from selling their data for profit. We propose a general framework for data exchange from first principles, focusing on two key properties: (i) fairness , ensuring that each agent receives utility proportional to their contribution to other agent utilities – agent contributions are quantifiable using well-established credit-sharing functions, such as the Shapley value, and (ii) stability , guaranteeing that no coalition of agents can identify an exchange among themselves which they all unanimously prefer to the current exchange. We show that fair and stable exchanges exist for all monotone continuous utility functions. Building on this, we investigate the computational complexity of finding approximate fair and stable exchanges. We present a local search algorithm for instances with monotone submodular utility functions, where each agent’s data contribution is measured using the Shapley value. We prove that this problem belongs to CLS ( = = PPAD ∩ \cap PLS) under mild assumptions. Our framework opens up several intriguing theoretical avenues for future research on data economics.

Differences from Classic Exchange Economies.

Exchange markets, also known as Arrow-Debreu markets
[

]
, have been extensively studied in economics and theoretical computer science. In these settings, agents with endowments of goods engage in trades to maximize their utility. The final allocation is obtained through a

Main Question.

It is trivial to determine an exchange satisfying only one of the foregoing desiderata – an exchange where all agents share nothing is trivially fair, and an exchange where all agents share everything is trivially core-stable, as there exists no exchange where any agent can get strictly higher utility. with polynomially many linear inequalities, and defining a

in

1.4
Technical Overview: Local Search Algorithm and Membership in CLS for Cross-Monotone Share Functions

Recall that modifying the data contribution of any single agent (increasing/ decreasing
x_{ij}
), may potentially change the utility contributions (
\psi_{i^{\prime}j}(\mathbf{x}_{j})
for
i^{\prime}\neq i
) of all agents. Designing an algorithm with no assumption on the structure of these changes seems very challenging – In fact, we suspect that in its most generality, computing an
\varepsilon
-core-stable and
\varepsilon
-reciprocal exchange is PPAD-complete.

In this paper, we consider
cross-montone data share
functions: instances where increasing
x_{ij}
can only decrease
\psi_{i^{\prime}j}(\mathbf{x}_{j})
for all
i^{\prime}\neq i
. This captures instances exhibiting diminishing marginal gains with data– in particular, when
u_{i}(\cdot)
is continuous monotone submodular for all
i
, and
\psi_{ij}(\cdot)
is the Shapley share of
i
’s data in
u_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{j})
, then
\psi_{ij}(\cdot)
satisfies cross-monotonicity. For such instances, we outline a
local-search
algorithm to compute a
\varepsilon
-core-stable and
\varepsilon
-fair exchange. We briefly outline our key ideas behind the local search procedure.

At a high level, our algorithm maintains the invariant that
G(\mathbf{x},\varepsilon/(nL))
is acyclic and reduces the surplus of the agents with high surpluses. Under the
L
-Lipschitzness assumption on the utility functions, it can be shown that every
\mathbf{x}
such that
G(\mathbf{x},\varepsilon/(nL))
is acyclic is
\varepsilon
-core stable. Given an exchange
\mathbf{x}
, define
\Delta(\mathbf{x})=\langle\Delta_{\sigma(1)}(\mathbf{x}),\Delta_{\sigma(2)}(\mathbf{x}),\dots,

\Delta_{\sigma(n)}(\mathbf{x})\rangle
, where
\sigma\colon\mathcal{N}\rightarrow\mathcal{N}
sorts the agents according to the surplus levels, i.e.,
\Delta_{\sigma(1)}(\mathbf{x})\geq\Delta_{\sigma(2)}(\mathbf{x})\geq\dots\geq\Delta_{\sigma(n)}(\mathbf{x})
. Our algorithm maintains
\mathbf{x}\in G(\mathbf{x},\varepsilon/(nL))
, and decreases
\Delta(\mathbf{x})
lexicographically in every iteration. The algorithm terminates when
\max_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\Delta_{i}(\mathbf{x})\leq\varepsilon/n
, as this will ensure that no positive surplus is larger than
\varepsilon/n
, and no negative surplus is smaller than
\varepsilon
: since
\sum_{i}\Delta_{i}(\mathbf{x})=0
, we have
\Delta_{j}(\mathbf{x})\geq-n\max_{i}\Delta_{i}(\mathbf{x})
, implying that
x
is
\varepsilon
-fair.

Given an exchange
x
, with
\max_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\Delta_{i}(\mathbf{x})>\varepsilon/n
, we partition the agents into two sets based on their surpluses by the following procedure: Let
S=\{i_{h}\}
, where
i_{h}
is the agent with the highest surplus. Iteratively, select the agent in
\mathcal{N}\setminus S
with the highest surplus, say
i
, and add
i
to
S
only if the surplus of
i
is at most
\varepsilon/n^{2}
less than the lowest surplus in
S
, i.e.,
\Delta_{i}(\mathbf{x})+\varepsilon/n^{2}\geq\min_{j\in S}\Delta_{j}(\mathbf{x})
. Observe that at termination,
S
will comprise only positive surplus agents as the lowest surplus in
S
is at least
\max_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\Delta_{i}(\mathbf{x})-|S|\cdot(\varepsilon/n^{2})>0
. Intuitively,
S
is the set of agents with high surpluses (they give more than they get). Also note that we have
\min_{i\in S}\Delta_{i}(\mathbf{x})>\max_{j\in\mathcal{N}\setminus S}\Delta_{j}(\mathbf{x})+\varepsilon/n^{2}
. The algorithm’s goal would be to reduce the surplus of agents in
S
(with at least one agent experiencing a strict reduction), and ensure that no surplus in
\mathcal{N}\setminus S
becomes more than the surplus of some agent in
S
, thereby lexicographically reducing
\Delta(\mathbf{x})
. This is achieved (i) by carefully reducing the data flow from
S
to
\mathcal{N}\setminus S

\mathcal{N}\setminus S
to

from

to

Since agents in

1.5
Discussion

In this paper, we introduce a formal model of data exchange. We outline our desiderata, drawing parallels to established theories in classical exchange economies. We show the existence of an exchange that satisfies the desiderata under minimal assumptions. Finally, we outline a local search algorithm for instances where agent utilities exhibit diminishing marginal gains with data. Under mild assumptions, we show that our problem is in CLS. We view this work as a first step in the theoretical exploration of data exchange economies, highlighting several promising avenues for further research. We highlight significant directions that emerge from our current results, as well as mention intriguing possibilities for generalizing our existing framework.

Computational Complexity of Finding a
\varepsilon
-Core-Stable and
\varepsilon
-Fair Exchange under Monotone Utilities and Cross-Monotone Share Functions.

While we show membership in CLS, the complexity of the problem is still open, even when
L/\varepsilon=\textup{poly}(n)
. We outline the main problem that arises when we try to generalize our local search algorithm. This will also highlight the main technical non-triviality in the next major direction.

Observe that our local search algorithm attempts to “balance” the surpluses. When, we decrease the flow from
S
to
\mathcal{N}\setminus S
(when there are no edges from
\mathcal{N}\setminus S
to
S
in
G(\mathbf{x},\varepsilon)
), note that surpluses in
S
decrease or remain unchanged and surpluses in
\mathcal{N}\setminus S
increase or remain unchanged. This creates an optimal balancing scenario: high surpluses in
S
decrease, while low surpluses in
\mathcal{N}\setminus S
increase. Under these conditions, we can leverage more sophisticated balancing potential functions, such as the Euclidean norm of the surpluses
\sum_{i}\Delta^{2}_{i}(\mathbf{x})
, which is polynomially bounded, and improves by an additive factor of
\textup{poly}(\varepsilon,1/n,1/L)
every time we decrease flow from
S
to
\mathcal{N}\setminus S
.

One would hope to adopt a symmetric approach while increasing flow from
\mathcal{N}\setminus S
to
S
(when decreasing flow from
S
to
\mathcal{N}\setminus S
is not feasible). However, this method encounters a major bottleneck: note that increasing a flow from
j\in\mathcal{N}\setminus S
to
i\in S
causes the surplus of
j
to increase (which is good), and all other surpluses to decrease or remain unchanged. While it is ideal for surpluses in
S
to decrease, it could be problematic if surpluses in
\mathcal{N}\setminus S
(low surplus) decrease. A natural solution would be to boost the outflow from these agents in
\mathcal{N}\setminus S
whose surpluses have dropped to
S
.
Unfortunately, the outflows of these agents may already be at maximum capacity (
x_{ji}=1
for all
i\in S
), making this approach infeasible! This situation contrasts sharply with reducing flow from
S
to
\mathcal{N}\setminus S
, where if an agent
i\in S
is at minimum capacity (with
x_{ij}=0
for all
j\in\mathcal{N}\setminus S
), its surplus cannot have increased.

We suspect that without the assumption that
L/\varepsilon=\textup{poly}(n)
, the problem is probably not in CLS, but it is also another question that demands a thorough investigation. Thus, the computational complexity of determining a
\varepsilon
-core-stable and
\varepsilon
-fair exchange is a deeply intriguing direction with many interesting open problems.

Computational Complexity of Finding a
\varepsilon
-Core-Stable and
\varepsilon
-Fair Exchange under Supermodular utilities.

All our computational results discussed so far, apply to instances where agents experience diminishing marginal gains with data. However, there is another interesting subclass of instances, where the datasets are
complimentary
, i.e., the utility gained from a combination of datasets is more than the sum of utilities gained from the individual datasets– for example when data is divided by features
[
LKZ+24
]
, it has been observed that combining datasets turn out to be significantly more valuable
[
CF23
]
. In particular, these are instances where increasing
x_{ij}
can increase or leave unchanged
\psi_{i^{\prime}j}(\mathbf{x}_{j})
for all
i^{\prime}\neq i
. Exploring the computational complexity of these complementary instances presents a promising avenue for future research.

We highlight the challenges encountered when adapting our local search algorithm. Decreasing data flow from an agent
i\in S
to an agent in
j\in\mathcal{N}\setminus S
can only cause the surplus of all agents other than
j
to decrease (non-increase). This behavior aligns well with our potential function, as the high surpluses are decreasing. However, while increasing surplus from
j\in\mathcal{N}\setminus S
to
i\in S
, the surpluses of all agents other than
i
increase, including the high-surplus agents in
S
.

Interestingly, the core issue in both scenarios appears to stem from the need to implement the “
increase-flow
” operation carefully– transferring data from low-surplus agents to high-surplus agents.

Generalizing Current Framework.

Building on the framework outlined in the paper, a wide range of intriguing problems can be explored. Our current model relies on a single trusted central server that facilitates data exchange and allows for the retraining and refinement of machine learning models using data samples collected from multiple agents. However, the concept of
decentralized data exchange
presents a fascinating avenue for investigation, where independent agents attempt to share data without a central authority. This shift opens up several compelling questions: (i) How do agents assess their utility and contribution functions for various data bundles? (ii) What forms of communication are permissible among the agents? (iii) Are there inherent dynamics that could guide us towards a desired solution? Each of these questions offers significant potential for foundational research in this emerging area.

\mathcal{I}=\langle\mathcal{N},\mathcal{D},\mathcal{U}\rangle
, where
\mathcal{N}=[n]
is a set of agents,
\mathcal{D}=(D_{1},\ldots,D_{n})
is a list of agents’ datasets, with
D_{i}
denoting the dataset of agent
i
, and
\mathcal{U}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})
is a list of agent utility functions, where
u_{i}:[0,1]^{n\times n}\rightarrow[0,1]
. The output is an exchange
\mathbf{x}\in[0,1]^{n\times n}
where
x_{ij}
denotes the fraction of dataset
D_{i}
that agent
i
gives to agent
j
.

We define the desiderata for our exchanges, beginning with our notion of fairness.

2.1
Utility sharing functions and reciprocal fairness

Intuitively, we regard an exchange to be fair, or
reciprocal
, if every agent receives as much utility from the exchange as their
contribution
to other agents in the exchange. Formalizing this intuition necessitates defining the contribution of an agent towards the utility of another agent in the exchange, for which we define utility sharing functions.

Share functions.

Conceptually, the utility
u_{j}(\mathbf{x})
of an agent
j
in an exchange
\mathbf{x}
can be viewed as a sum of contributions of the other agents given by
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\psi_{ij}(\mathbf{x}_{j})
. Here,
\psi_{ij}:[0,1]^{n}\rightarrow[0,1]
is a
sharing
rule that measures the contribution of agent
i
towards the utility of agent
j
given that
j
receives the bundle
\mathbf{x}_{j}
; we interchangeably use
\psi_{ij}(\mathbf{x})
and
\psi_{ij}(\mathbf{x}_{j})
.

Definition 2

(Utility sharing function)
.

For an agent
i
, a collection of functions
\{\psi_{ij}\}_{j\in\mathcal{N}}
where
\psi_{ij}:[0,1]^{n}\rightarrow[0,1]
are considered share functions if for any exchange
\mathbf{x}
and agent
i\in\mathcal{N}
, we have

(i)

(Monotonicity)
\psi_{ij}(\mathbf{x}_{j})
is non-decreasing in
x_{ij}
for every
j\in\mathcal{N}
,

(ii)

(Normalization)
\psi_{ij}(\mathbf{x}_{j})=0
if
x_{ij}=0
for every
j\in\mathcal{N}
,

(iii)

(Efficiency)
u_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{j})=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\psi_{ij}(\mathbf{x}_{j})
.

We discuss some examples of sharing functions that have been studied in the literature.

Shapley share.

For a subset
S\subseteq\mathcal{N}
, let
\mathbf{x}_{j}[S]\in[0,1]^{n}
denote the restriction of the bundle
\mathbf{x}_{j}
to the set
S
, i.e.,
(\mathbf{x}_{j}[S])_{k}=x_{kj}
if
k\in S
and
0
otherwise. The Shapley share measures the contribution of an agent
i
to agent
j
in an exchange
\mathbf{x}
as follows. For every possible subset
S\subseteq\mathcal{N}\setminus\{i\}
of agents, we compute the marginal gain in the utility of
j
obtained by adding
i
to the subset
S
, i.e.,
\mu(S)=u_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{i}[S\cup\{i\}])-u_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{j}[S])
. The Shapley share
\psi_{ij}
is then the average marginal gain
\mu(S)
taken over all possible such subsets
S
. Formally,

\psi_{ij}(\mathbf{x}_{j})=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{S\subseteq\mathcal{N}\setminus\{i\}}\binom{n-1}{|S|}^{-1}\cdot(u_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{j}[S\cup\{i\}])-u_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{j}[S])).

The fact that Shapley share functions as defined above are actually share functions as per

Proposition 1

(Shapley shares)
.

For every

u_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{j})=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\psi_{ij}(\mathbf{x}_{j})

Proof.

Consider
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\psi_{ij}(\mathbf{x}_{j})
. Fix a subset
\emptyset\neq S\subsetneq\mathcal{N}
. Let us examine how many times
u_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{j}[S])
occurs as either the first or the second term of the difference in

u_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{j}[S])
occurs as the first term exactly

u_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{j}[S])

is
\frac{1}{n}\cdot\left(|S|\cdot\binom{n-1}{|S|-1}^{-1}-(n-|S|)\cdot\binom{n-1}{|S|}^{-1}\right)

u_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{j}[\emptyset])
is also zero, since

is normalized. Finally, note that

appears only as the first term in the difference, and exactly

Another commonly used share function is the
proportional share
, which attributes agent
i

j
in an exchange

Reciprocity.

Having defined utility sharing functions, we formally define
reciprocal
exchanges. Given an exchange
\mathbf{x}
, we let
\Psi_{i}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}}\psi_{ij}(\mathbf{x}_{j})
denote the total contribution of agent
i
towards other agents in
\mathbf{x}
. Let
\Delta_{i}(\mathbf{x}):=\Psi_{i}(\mathbf{x})-u_{i}(\mathbf{x})
denote the
surplus
of agent
i
in
\mathbf{x}
. By the definition of a sharing rules, we have that
\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\Delta_{i}(\mathbf{x})=0
for any exchange
\mathbf{x}\in[0,1]^{n\times n}
.

Definition 3

(Reciprocal exchange)
.

An exchange
\mathbf{x}
is said to be
\delta
-reciprocal if for all agents
i\in\mathcal{N}
,
|\Delta_{i}(\mathbf{x})|\leq\delta
. A
0
-reciprocal exchange is called a reciprocal exchange.

Note that the exchange
\mathbf{x}=0^{n\times n}
is trivially reciprocal.

2.2
Core-stability

Next, we define another desideratum of exchange called
core-stability
, which captures notions of
stability
and
efficiency
. Intuitively, an exchange is core-stable if no subset of agents can form a coalition and exchange data only among themselves such that every agent in the coalition gets better utility.

Definition 4
.

An exchange
\mathbf{x}
is
\varepsilon
-core-stable if and only if for all
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{N}
, there is no exchange
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
of the instance
\langle\mathcal{N}^{\prime},(D_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{N}^{\prime}},(u_{i})_{i\in\mathcal{N}^{\prime}}\rangle
such that
u_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{i})>u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i})+\varepsilon
for all
i\in\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
. A
0
-core-stable exchange is called a core-stable exchange.

We now define a structure which enables us to capture a subset of core-stable allocations.

Definition 5
.

Given an exchange
\mathbf{x}
for an instance
(\mathcal{N},\mathcal{D},\mathcal{U})
and a parameter
\alpha\in(0,1)
, we define the exchange graph
G(\mathbf{x},\alpha)
as a directed graph with the vertex set
V(G(\mathbf{x},\alpha))=\mathcal{N}
and the edge set
E(G(\mathbf{x},\alpha))=\{(i,j):x_{ij}<1-\alpha\}
.

Intuitively, our goal is ensure that if an exchange
\mathbf{x}
is not
\varepsilon
-core-stable, then for some appropriately chosen parameter
\alpha(\varepsilon)
the exchange graph
G(\mathbf{x},\alpha(\varepsilon))
contains a cycle
C
which represents a coalition of agents who would rather share data among themselves.

It is towards this end that we only record an edge from
i
to
j
in the exchange graph if
i
is not sharing data fully to
j
, and a sufficient increment in the ‘flow’ from
i
to
j
is possible if
(i,j)
appears in
C
. Next, we choose the parameter
\alpha(\varepsilon)
carefully so that by increasing the ‘flow’ along the edges of such a cycle
C
, we obtain an exchange
\mathbf{y}
which each agent in
C
prefers over
\mathbf{x}
by an additive value of
\varepsilon
. Thus, an exchange
\mathbf{x}
whose exchange graph
G(\mathbf{x},\alpha(\varepsilon))
has a cycle
C
is not
\varepsilon
-core stable, since agents in
C
can significantly improve their utility (i.e. at least by
\varepsilon)
) by sharing data among themselves. Conversely, if the exchange graph
G(\mathbf{x},\alpha(\varepsilon))
is acyclic for an exchange
\mathbf{x}
, then
\mathbf{x}
must be
\varepsilon
-core-stable. We now formalize the intuition.

Let
I_{ji}=\{\mathbf{x}\in[0,1]^{n\times n}:x_{ji}=1\}
be the set of exchanges where
j
shares her dataset entirely with
i
. Decreasing
x_{ji}
reduces the utility of
i
. Given an exchange
\mathbf{x}\in I_{ji}
, we are interested in the maximum additive decrease in
x_{ji}
which does not cause a significant drop in the utility of agent
i
. We define this quantity below. Let
\mathbf{e}_{ji}\in[0,1]^{n\times n}
be the exchange where no data is shared among agents, except from
j
to
i
.

Definition 6
.

For
i,j\in\mathcal{N}
, we define
a_{ji}^{\varepsilon}:I_{ji}\rightarrow[0,1]
as the function given by

a_{ji}^{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})=\max\{a\in[0,1]:u_{i}(\mathbf{x}-a\cdot\mathbf{e}_{ji})\geq u_{i}(\mathbf{x})-\varepsilon/n\},

that is,
a_{ji}^{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})
is the maximum additive decrease
a\in[0,1]
in
x_{ji}
which decreases the utility of
i
by additively by
\varepsilon/n
. Define
\alpha(\varepsilon)=\min_{j,i\in\mathcal{N}}\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in{I_{ji}}}a_{ji}^{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})
.

By definition of
\alpha(\varepsilon)
, we observe that:

Observation 1
.

For all
i,j\in[n]
and
\mathbf{x}\in[0,1]^{n\times n}
with
x_{ji}=1
, we have

\displaystyle u_{i}(\mathbf{x})\leq u_{i}(\mathbf{x}-\alpha(\varepsilon)\cdot\mathbf{e}_{ji})+\varepsilon/n.

We now prove that acyclic exchange graphs imply approximately-core-stable exchanges.

Lemma 1
.

If
G(\mathbf{x},\alpha(\varepsilon))
is acyclic for an exchange
\mathbf{x}
, then
\mathbf{x}
is
\varepsilon
-core-stable.

Proof.

Suppose the claim does not hold and there is a subset
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
of agents who can exchange data among themselves and improve their utility additively by
\varepsilon
. Let
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in[0,1]^{n\times n}
be the exchange where agents in
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
fully share data with each other, i.e.,
x^{\prime}_{ij}=1
for all
i,j\in\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
and
x^{\prime}_{ij}=0
otherwise. Then, for every
i\in\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
, we have
u_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})>u_{i}(\mathbf{x})+\varepsilon
.

Since
G(\mathbf{x},\alpha(\varepsilon))
is acyclic, there must be an agent
s\in\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
who is a source in the subgraph of
G(\mathbf{x},\alpha(\varepsilon))
induced by
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
. Hence,
x_{is}\geq 1-\alpha(\varepsilon)
for all
i\in\mathcal{N}^{\prime}\setminus\{s\}
. For simplicity, let us re-index the agents so that
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}=\{1,2,\dots,|\mathcal{N}^{\prime}|\}
. Then by repeatedly using
Observation

1
, we have:

\displaystyle u_{s}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{s})

\displaystyle\leq u_{s}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{s}-\alpha(\varepsilon)\cdot\mathbf{e}_{1s})+\varepsilon/n

(Observation
1
)

\displaystyle\leq u_{s}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{s}-\alpha(\varepsilon)\cdot(\mathbf{e}_{1s}+\mathbf{e}_{2s}))+2\cdot(\varepsilon/n)

(Observation
1
)

\displaystyle\ldots

\displaystyle\leq u_{s}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{s}-\alpha(\varepsilon)\cdot\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}^{\prime}}\mathbf{e}_{is})+|\mathcal{N}^{\prime}|\cdot(\varepsilon/n)

(Observation
1
)

\displaystyle\leq u_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{s})+|\mathcal{N}^{\prime}|\cdot(\varepsilon/n)

(since
x_{is}\geq 1-\alpha(\varepsilon)
for all
i\in\mathcal{N}^{\prime}\setminus\{s\}
)

\displaystyle\leq u_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{s})+\varepsilon,

(since
|\mathcal{N}^{\prime}|\leq n
)

which contradicts the fact that
u_{s}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}_{s})>u_{s}(\mathbf{x}_{s})+\varepsilon
. Thus,
\mathbf{x}
must be
\varepsilon
-core-stable.
∎

3
Existence of Reciprocal and Core-Stable Exchanges

In this section, we prove the existence of exchanges that are reciprocal and core-stable when agent utility functions and share functions are continuous and monotone. First, we show the existence of a reciprocal and
\varepsilon
-core-stable exchange for
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
.

Theorem 3
.

For any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, any data exchange instance
\mathcal{I}
where utility functions and sharing functions are continuous and monotone admits a reciprocal and
\varepsilon
-core-stable exchange.

At a high-level, the proof of
Theorem

3
has two main components: first, we define a compact, convex set
Z
which corresponds to a set of
\varepsilon
-core-stable exchanges, and second, we define a continuous function
g:Z\rightarrow Z
, such that the fixed point of
g
is a reciprocal exchange. Then, Brouwer’s fixed point theorem guarantees that
g
admits a fixed point
z^{*}
, which we show corresponds to an exchange that is reciprocal and
\varepsilon
-core-stable.

3.1
Defining the set
Z
to capture core-stable exchanges

Given an
\varepsilon>0
, let
\alpha:=\alpha(\varepsilon)
. Let
M>cn\log(1/\alpha)
for some large constant
c>1
. We define
Z
to be a subset of
[0,M]^{n\times n}
, and associate each point in
[0,M]^{n\times n}
with an exchange in
[0,1]^{n\times n}
. Concretely, we define a function

as
f(z)_{ij}=1-\exp(-z_{ij})
, which maps the point

to the exchange
f(z)

z_{ij}=\log(\frac{1}{1-f(z)_{ij}})

Recall from

\varepsilon

to algebraically capture the set of acyclic exchange graphs, as follows:

\displaystyle Z=\{z\in[0,M]^{n\times n}:\text{for all }

\displaystyle\text{cyclic permutations }(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})\text{ of }[n],

(2)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{k}z_{i_{j}i_{j+1}}\geq n\log(1/\alpha)\},

where the definition used the convention that

First note that by choice of

of

is non-empty. Since

is defined by a set of linear constraints, and is contained in

Z

Z
is convex and compact and meets the domain requirements for Brouwer’s fixed point theorem.

Observation 2
.

Z
is a convex and compact set.

Next, we show that for every point
z\in Z
,

corresponds to a
\varepsilon

\varepsilon
-core-stable exchanges that is homeomorphic to a convex compact set.

Lemma 2
.

For all

, the exchange

is
\varepsilon
-core-stable.

Proof.

Consider an arbitrary

. We prove that

for all pairs of agents
i,j\in[n]
, as
g(z)=\sum_{i,j}\frac{1}{n^{2}}g^{ij}(z)
; see Figure

for an illustration. We prove below that

indeed maps elements of

to itself, which crucially uses the convexity of set

.

Lemma 4
.

For all
z\in Z
,
g(z)\in Z
.

Proof.

We prove that
g^{ij}(z)\in Z
for all
i,j\in[n]
. By definition of
\delta
, we have
\delta\cdot|\Delta_{j}(f(z))-\Delta_{i}(f(z))|\leq 1
. Then by definition of
\beta^{+}_{ij}(z)
and
\beta^{-}_{ij}
, we have that
g^{ij}(z)\in Z
. Finally, by convexity of
Z
from
Observation

2
, we also have that
g(z)=\sum_{ij}\frac{1}{n^{2}}g^{ij}(z)\in Z
.
∎

Next, we prove that
g
is continuous.

Lemma 5
.

g:Z\rightarrow Z
is a continuous function.

Proof.

Observe that it suffices to prove that for all
i,j\in[n]
,
g^{ij}
is a continuous function. Fix
i,j\in[n]
. Define
h^{ij}:Z\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
as
h^{ij}(z)=\Delta_{j}(f(z))-\Delta_{i}(f(z))
. The continuity of
\phi_{kh}
for all
k,h\in[n]
and the continuity of
f
imply that
\Delta_{i}(f(z))
and
\Delta_{j}(f(z))
are continuous in
z
. Thus,
h^{ij}
is also continuous in
z
.

Let
\mathcal{B}(z,\rho)
denote an open ball of radius
\rho
around
z
. We argue that
g^{ij}
is continuous at every
z\in Z
, by showing that for every
\rho>0
, there exists a
\gamma>0
s.t.
z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}(z,\gamma)
implies
g^{ij}(z^{\prime})\in\mathcal{B}(g^{ij}(z),\rho)
. Fix a point
z\in Z
and
\rho>0
. We consider three cases:

•

h^{ij}(z)=0
. Since
h^{ij}
is continuous, there exists some
\gamma^{\prime}>0
such that
z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}(z,\gamma^{\prime})
implies
h^{ij}(z^{\prime})\in\mathcal{B}(h^{ij}(z),\frac{\rho}{2\delta M})=\mathcal{B}(0,\frac{\rho}{2\delta M})
. For
z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}(z,\gamma^{\prime})
, we define
\beta_{ij}(z^{\prime})=\beta^{+}_{ij}(z^{\prime})
if
h^{ij}(z^{\prime})\geq 0
, and
\beta^{-}_{ij}(z^{\prime})
otherwise. Using the definition of
g^{ij}
from
Eq.

3
, we have for every
z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}(z,\gamma^{\prime})
:

\displaystyle|g^{ij}(z^{\prime})-g^{ij}(z)|

\displaystyle\leq|z^{\prime}-z|+\delta\cdot|\beta_{ij}(z^{\prime})\cdot h^{ij}(z^{\prime})|

\displaystyle\leq|z^{\prime}-z|+\delta\cdot M\cdot\frac{\rho}{2\delta M}

(using
|\beta^{ij}(z^{\prime})|\leq M
)

\displaystyle\leq|z^{\prime}-z|+\frac{\rho}{2}.

We set
\gamma:=\min(\gamma^{\prime},\rho/2)
. Then, the above inequality implies that for all
z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}(z,\gamma)
, we have
g^{ij}(z^{\prime})\in\mathcal{B}(g^{ij}(z),\rho)
. This shows that
g^{ij}
is continuous at
z
.

•

h^{ij}(z)=r>0
. Since
h^{ij}
is continuous, there exists some
\gamma^{\prime}>0
such that
z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}(z,\gamma)
implies
h^{ij}(z^{\prime})\in\mathcal{B}(h^{ij}(z),\frac{r}{2})
. Thus,
|h^{ij}(z^{\prime})-h^{ij}(z)|<r/2
. Since
h^{ij}(z)=r
, we conclude
h^{ij}(z^{\prime})>0
for all
z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}(z,\gamma^{\prime})
. This implies that
g^{ij}(z^{\prime})=z^{\prime}+\mathbf{e}_{ij}\cdot\delta\cdot\beta^{+}_{ij}(z^{\prime})\cdot h^{ij}(z^{\prime})
for all
z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}(z,\gamma^{\prime})
.

Now consider the function
\bar{g}^{ij}:Z\rightarrow Z
given by
\bar{g}^{ij}(z^{\prime}):=z^{\prime}+\mathbf{e}_{ij}\cdot\delta\cdot\beta^{+}_{ij}(z^{\prime})\cdot h^{ij}(z^{\prime})
. We have that
g^{ij}(z^{\prime})=\bar{g}^{ij}(z^{\prime})
for all
z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}(z,\gamma^{\prime})
. Further, since
\beta^{+}_{ij}
and
h^{ij}
are continuous functions,
\bar{g}^{ij}
is also a continuous function. Thus, there exists some
\gamma^{\prime\prime}>0
s.t.
z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}(z,\gamma^{\prime\prime})
implies
\bar{g}^{ij}(z^{\prime})\in\mathcal{B}(\bar{g}^{ij}(z),\rho)
.

We set
\gamma:=\min(\gamma^{\prime},\gamma^{\prime\prime})
. We obtain that
z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}(z,\gamma)
implies
g^{ij}(z^{\prime})\in\mathcal{B}(g^{ij}(z),\rho)
, since
g^{ij}(z^{\prime})=\bar{g}^{ij}(z^{\prime})
for all
z^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}(z,\gamma^{\prime})
. This proves that
g^{ij}
is continuous at
z
.

•

h^{ij}(z)=r<0
. An analysis analogous to the above case shows that
g^{ij}
is continuous at
z
in this case too.

In conclusion, we showed that for all
i,j\in\mathcal{N}
,
g^{ij}
is continuous at all points
z\in Z
. Since
g:=\sum_{i,j\in[n]}\frac{1}{n^{2}}g^{ij}
, we conclude that
g
is a continuous function as well.
∎

We can now prove
Theorem

3
.

Proof of
Theorem

3
.

Since
Z
is a convex, compact set (
Observation

2
), and that the map
g
is a continuous function from
Z
to
Z
in Lemmas
4
and
5
. By Brouwer’s fixed-point theorem,
g
has a fixed-point
z^{*}\in Z
. By definition of

, we know
g(z^{*})_{ij}=z^{*}+\mathbf{e}_{ij}\cdot\frac{\delta}{n^{2}}\cdot\beta_{ij}(z^{*})\cdot h^{ij}(z^{*})
for all
i,j\in\mathcal{N}
, where
\beta_{ij}(z)=\beta^{+}_{ij}(z)
if

, and
\beta^{-}_{ij}(z)
otherwise. Thus

implies that

. This shows that the exchange

∎

3.3
Existence of reciprocal and exact core-stable exchanges

Having proved the existence of reciprocal and
\varepsilon
-core-stable exchanges for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, we next prove the existence of reciprocal and exactly core-stable exchanges. Our proof relies on sequential compactness of the sets of reciprocal and
\varepsilon
-core-stable exchanges. We prove this in the next two lemmas by using the continuity of utility and sharing functions.

Lemma 6
.

The set of reciprocal exchanges is compact.

Proof.

For
i\in\mathcal{N}
, let
\hat{R}_{i}^{+}=\{\mathbf{x}\in[0,1]^{n\times n}:\phi_{i}(\mathbf{x})>u_{i}(\mathbf{x})\}
and
\hat{R}_{i}^{-}=\{\mathbf{x}\in[0,1]^{n\times n}:\phi_{i}(\mathbf{x})<u_{i}(\mathbf{x})\}
. By definition,
\hat{R}_{i}^{+}\cup\hat{R}_{i}^{-}
is the set of exchanges that are not reciprocal for agent
i
. By continuity of
u_{i}
and
\phi_{i}
, we see that both
\hat{R}_{i}^{+}
and
\hat{R}_{i}^{-}
are open sets. Hence the set
\bar{R}=\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal{N}}\hat{R}_{i}^{+}\cup\hat{R}_{i}^{-}
is also an open set. Observe that
\bar{R}
is exactly the set of exchanges that are not reciprocal. Hence, the set of reciprocal exchanges is closed. Since the set of exchanges is also bounded, the compactness of the set of reciprocal exchanges follows.
∎

Lemma 7
.

The set of
\varepsilon
-core-stable exchanges is compact, for any
\varepsilon\in[0,1]
.

Proof.

For a subset
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
of agents and an agent
i\in\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
, consider the set
S(\mathcal{N}^{\prime},i)
of exchanges in which agent
i
will gain significantly if agents in
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
fully exchange data among themselves. Let
\mathbf{y}[\mathcal{N}^{\prime}]\in[0,1]^{n\times n}
be an exchange where
\mathbf{y}[\mathcal{N}^{\prime}]_{kh}=1
for
k,h\in\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
and
0
otherwise. Then
S(\mathcal{N}^{\prime},i)=\{\mathbf{x}\in[0,1]^{n\times n}:u_{i}(\mathbf{y}[\mathcal{N}^{\prime}])>u_{i}(\mathbf{x})+\varepsilon\}
. Note that by continuity of
u_{i}
, the set
S(\mathcal{N}^{\prime},i)
is an open set for all
\mathcal{N}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{N}
and
i\in\mathcal{N}^{\prime}
.

The definition of
S(\mathcal{N}^{\prime},i)
and
\varepsilon
-core-stability implies that the set of exchanges which are
not

\varepsilon
-core-stable is given by
\bigcup_{\mathcal{N}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{N}}\bigcap_{i\in\mathcal{N}^{\prime}}S(\mathcal{N}^{\prime},i)
. Since the intersection of finitely many open sets, and the union of open sets is open, we obtain that the set of exchanges which are not
\varepsilon
-core-stable is open. Thus, the set of
\varepsilon
-core-stable exchanges is closed. Since the set of exchanges is also bounded, the compactness of the set of
\varepsilon
-core-stable exchanges follows.
∎

We now show the existence of reciprocal and exact core-stable exchanges.

Theorem 4
.

Any data exchange instance
\mathcal{I}
where utility functions and sharing functions are continuous and monotone admits a reciprocal and core-stable exchange.

Proof.

Theorem

3
implies the existence of reciprocal and
\varepsilon
-core-stable exchanges for any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
. Thus, for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists an exchange
\mathbf{x}^{k}
that is reciprocal and
\frac{1}{k}
-core-stable. Since the set of reciprocal exchanges is compact (
Lemma

6
), the Bolzano-Weirerstrass theorem implies that the sequence
\{\mathbf{x}^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
has a convergent subsequence
\{\mathbf{x}^{\sigma_{i}}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, which converges to a reciprocal exchange
\mathbf{x}^{*}
.

We argue that
\mathbf{x}^{*}
is also core-stable. Fix an
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
i_{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}
be the smallest integer
i
s.t.
\sigma_{i}\geq\ell
. In other words,
\mathbf{x}^{i_{\ell}}
is smallest-index element of the subsequence
\{\mathbf{x}^{\sigma_{i}}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
which appears no earlier than
\mathbf{x}^{\ell}
in the sequence
\{\mathbf{x}^{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
. Then, the sequence
\{\mathbf{x}^{\sigma_{i}}\}_{i\geq i_{\ell}}
is a subsequence of the convergent subsequence
\{\mathbf{x}^{\sigma_{i}}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, and hence it is also convergent with the same limit point
\mathbf{x}^{*}
.

Moreover, since an
\varepsilon
-core-stable exchange is also
\varepsilon^{\prime}
-core-stable for
\varepsilon^{\prime}>\varepsilon
,
\{\mathbf{x}^{\sigma_{i}}\}_{i\geq i_{\ell}}
is a sequence of
\frac{1}{\ell}
-core-stable exchanges. Since the set of
\frac{1}{\ell}
-core-stable exchanges is compact (
Lemma

7
), we conclude that the limit point
\mathbf{x}^{*}
is also
\frac{1}{\ell}
-core-stable. Thus,
\mathbf{x}^{*}
is
\frac{1}{\ell}
-core-stable for every
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
. Suppose
\mathbf{x}^{*}
is not exactly core-stable. Then, there exists some
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathbf{x}^{*}
is not
\frac{1}{k}
-core-stable, which is a contradiction. Hence,
\mathbf{x}^{*}
is both reciprocal and exactly core-stable.
∎

4
Computational Results

In this section, we focus on the computational aspects of determining a
\varepsilon
-reciprocal and
\varepsilon
-core-stable exchange. It is difficult to make statements on the computational complexity of the problem, without making any assumptions on the utilities or the shares. Therefore, we assume that there is some constant
L>1
such that:

•

The utility functions are
L
-Lipschitz continuous, i.e., for all
i,j\in\mathcal{N}
, exchanges
\mathbf{x}\in[0,1]^{n\times n}
, and
a\in[-1,1]
, we have
|u_{j}(\mathbf{x}+a\cdot\mathbf{e}_{ij})-u_{j}(\mathbf{x})|\leq L\cdot|a|
.

•

The share functions are cross-monotone, i.e., for all
i,j
, i.e.,
\psi_{ij}(\cdot)
is monotone non-increasing in
x_{i^{\prime}j}
for all
i^{\prime}\neq i
, and
\partialderivative{\psi_{ij}}{x_{i'j'}}\leq L
for all
i^{\prime},j^{\prime}
.

In particular, these instances exhibit diminishing marginal gains with data, e.g., we capture all instances where
u_{i}(\cdot)
s are
L
-Lipschitz, continuous monotone submodular, and
\psi_{ij}(x)
is the Shapley share of
x_{ij}
in
u_{j}(x_{j})
. We can make the following stronger observations under these assumptions.

Lemma 8
.

Let
\mathcal{I}
be a data exchange instance with
L
-Lipschitz continuous utility and sharing functions. Then:

(i)

\delta=\frac{1}{\max_{i}u_{i}(1^{n})}\geq\frac{1}{nL}
.

(ii)

\alpha(\varepsilon)\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{nL}
for
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
.

(iii)

For any exchange
\mathbf{x}\in[0,1]^{n\times n}
, if
G(\mathbf{x},\frac{\varepsilon}{nL})
is acyclic, then
\mathbf{x}
is
\varepsilon
-core-stable.

Proof.

For part (i), we note that
|u_{i}(1^{n})-u_{i}(0^{n})|\leq nL
using the Lipschitz continuity of
u_{i}
and since each
u_{i}
is a function of only
x_{ji}
for
j\in\mathcal{N}
. Next, recall that the utility functions
u_{i}
are normalized, i.e.,
u_{i}(0^{n\times n})=0
for all
i\in\mathcal{N}
. Thus, we observe that
\delta=(\max_{i}u_{i}(1^{n\times n}))^{-1}\geq\frac{1}{nL}
.

For part (ii), recall from
Definition

6
that
a_{ji}^{\varepsilon}(\mathbf{x})=\max\{a\in[0,1]:u_{i}(\mathbf{x}-a\cdot\mathbf{e}_{ji})\geq u_{i}(\mathbf{x})-\varepsilon/n\}
, for any
i,j\in[n]
and exchange
\mathbf{x}
with
x_{ji}=1
. If
u_{i}(\mathbf{x})\leq\varepsilon/n
, then
a^{\varepsilon}_{ji}(\mathbf{x})=1
. Otherwise, we have
u_{i}(\mathbf{x}-a^{\varepsilon}_{ji}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\mathbf{e}_{ji})=u_{i}(\mathbf{x})-\varepsilon/n
. By the Lipschitz continuity of
u_{i}
, we get
a^{\varepsilon}_{ji}(\mathbf{x})\geq\varepsilon/nL
. In either case we see that
a^{\varepsilon}_{ji}(\mathbf{x})\geq\varepsilon/nL
for all
i,j\in\mathcal{N}
and
\mathbf{x}\in I_{ji}
. Since
\alpha(\varepsilon)=\min_{i,j\in\mathcal{N}}\inf_{\mathbf{x}\in I_{ji}}a^{\varepsilon}_{ji}
, we obtain
\alpha(\varepsilon)\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{nL}
, proving part (ii).

For part (iii), consider an exchange
\mathbf{x}
such that
G(\mathbf{x},\frac{\varepsilon}{nL})
is acyclic. Then for any cycle
C
in
K_{n}
, there is some edge
e\in C
such that
x_{e}\geq 1-\varepsilon/nL
. Since
\alpha(\varepsilon)\geq\varepsilon/nL
from part (ii), we also get
x_{e}\geq 1-\alpha(\varepsilon)
. Thus the exchange graph
G(\mathbf{x},\alpha(\varepsilon))
is acyclic. By
Lemma

1
,
\mathbf{x}
is an
\varepsilon
-core-stable exchange.
∎

Moreover, we assume oracle access to the utility functions and shares, i.e., given a rational polynomially bounded
x
, we get oracle access to
u_{i}(x_{i})
and
\psi_{ij}(x)
. We remark that all our algorithmic guarantees hold, even when we have access to approximate utilities and shares, where approximations are polynomial in
\varepsilon,1/L
, and
1/n
. However, we stick to oracle access to exact utilities and shares for clarity and ease of presentation.

We first present a local search algorithm, which will later be used to show the membership in PLS. Then, we adapt our fixed point formulation in
Section

3
, to show membership in PPAD, implying that the problem lies in PPAD
\cap
PLS
=
CLS.

Perturbation.

To prove the results presented in this section, we perform a perturbation of the utilities as follows: for each
i\in\mathcal{N}
, we define
\tilde{u}_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i})=u_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{i})+(\varepsilon\cdot\sum_{j}x_{ji})/n
. Also, we introduce a perturbed function
\tilde{\psi}_{ij}(\mathbf{x})=\psi_{ij}(\mathbf{x})+\varepsilon x_{ij}/n
. It can be verified that
\tilde{\psi}_{ij}(x)
satisfies monotonicity, normalization and efficiency and is, therefore, a valid share function. Further, as long as
\psi_{ij}(x)
is cross-monotone, so is
\tilde{\psi}_{ij}(x)
. Therefore, with
\tilde{u}(\cdot)
and
\tilde{\psi}_{ij}(\cdot)
, we now have a perturbed instance with monotone utility functions, and cross monotone share functions.

The primary reason behind the perturbation is to incorporate mild non-satiation for the utilities, i.e., increasing data consumption should cause a strict increase in the utility value at any point. We first claim that any
\varepsilon
-core-stable and
\varepsilon
-reciprocal exchange
x
w.r.t. to the perturbed utilities will give a
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
-core stable and
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon)
-reciprocal exchange w.r.t. the original utilities.

Lemma 9
.

If
x
is
\varepsilon
-core-stable and
\varepsilon
-reciprocal exchange
x
w.r.t. to the perturbed utilities, then
x
is a
3\varepsilon
-core stable and
3\varepsilon
-reciprocal exchange w.r.t. the original utilities.

Proof.

First observe that for any
\mathbf{x}
, we have
|\tilde{u}_{i}(\mathbf{x})-{u}_{i}(\mathbf{x})|\leq{\varepsilon\sum_{j}x_{ji}}/{n}
for all
i
. Also, we have
|\tilde{\psi}_{ij}(\mathbf{x})-\psi_{ij}(\mathbf{x})|=\varepsilon x_{ij}/n
. Finally, let
\tilde{\Delta}_{i}(\mathbf{x})
denote the surplus of agent
i
w.r.t.
\tilde{u}_{i}(\cdot)
. Observe that

\displaystyle|\tilde{\Delta}_{i}(\mathbf{x})-\Delta_{i}(\mathbf{x})|

\displaystyle\leq|\tilde{u}_{i}(\mathbf{x})-{u}_{i}(\mathbf{x})|+\sum_{j}|\tilde{\psi}_{ij}(\mathbf{x})-\psi_{ij}(\mathbf{x})|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon\sum_{j}x_{ji}}{n}+\frac{\varepsilon\sum_{j}x_{ij}}{n}

\displaystyle\leq{2\varepsilon}.

Thus, if
\mathbf{x}
is
\varepsilon
-reciprocal w.r.t. perturbed utilities (means that
|\tilde{\Delta}_{i}(\mathbf{x})|\leq\varepsilon
for all

), then
\mathbf{x}
is
\varepsilon+2\varepsilon=3\varepsilon
reciprocal w.r.t. original utilities (
|\Delta_{i}(\mathbf{x})|\leq|\tilde{\Delta}_{i}(\mathbf{x})|+2\varepsilon\leq 3\varepsilon
). Similarly, if
\mathbf{x}
is
\varepsilon
-core stable w.r.t. perturbed utilities, then
\mathbf{x}
is
\varepsilon+2\varepsilon=3\varepsilon
core-stable w.r.t. original utilities: since
\mathbf{x}
is core-stable, there exists no
S\subseteq[n]
and no exchange
\mathbf{y}
among agents in
S
such that
\tilde{u}_{i}(\mathbf{y})>\tilde{u}_{i}(\mathbf{x})+\varepsilon
. Since
|\tilde{u}_{i}(\mathbf{z})-{u}_{i}(\mathbf{z})|\leq\varepsilon\sum_{j}z_{ji}/n\leq\varepsilon
for
\mathbf{z}\in\{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\}
, we have that there exists no
S\subseteq[n]
and no
\mathbf{y}
among agents in
S
, such that
u_{i}(\mathbf{y})>u_{i}(\mathbf{x})+3\varepsilon
.
∎

Therefore, in what follows, we provide a local search algorithm and prove PLS membership only for perturbed instances. In particular, in all instances we consider from here on, increasing any
x_{ij}
by
\delta
increases
u_{j}(\mathbf{x}_{j})
by at least
\varepsilon\delta/n
.

4.1
Local Search Algorithm

Throughout the algorithm, we maintain the invariant that
G(\mathbf{x},\varepsilon/(nL))
is acyclic. This ensures that our
\mathbf{x}
is always
\varepsilon
-core stable by
Lemma

8
. We will start out the algorithm by setting
x_{ij}=1
for all
i,j
, in which case
G(\mathbf{x},\varepsilon/(nL))
is trivially acyclic.

To achieve reciprocity, our algorithm will reduce the high surpluses in an exchange, while still maintaining
G(\mathbf{x},\varepsilon/(nL))
is acyclic. Given
\mathbf{x}
, let
\Delta(\mathbf{x})=\langle\Delta_{\sigma(1)}(\mathbf{x}),\Delta_{\sigma(2)}(\mathbf{x}),\dots,\Delta_{\sigma(n)}(\mathbf{x})\rangle
be the
sorted surplus profile
of
\mathbf{x}
, where
\sigma
is a permutation of
[n]
s.t.
\Delta_{\sigma(1)}(\mathbf{x})\geq\Delta_{\sigma(2)}(\mathbf{x})\geq\dots\geq\Delta_{\sigma(n)}(\mathbf{x})
. Define
P(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}}(nL/\varepsilon)^{2(n-i)}\cdot\Delta_{\sigma(i)}(\mathbf{x})
. Given an exchange
\mathbf{x}
which is not
\varepsilon
-reciprocal, our algorithm determines an exchange
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
such that
G(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\varepsilon/(nL))
is acyclic, and
P(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})<P(\mathbf{x})-L/2n^{2}
.

Our algorithm first identifies a set
S
of agents with high surplus as follows: let
i
be an agent with maximum surplus. If
\Delta_{i}(\mathbf{x})\leq\varepsilon/n
, terminate and return
\mathbf{x}
as the desired exchange. Otherwise, let
S\leftarrow\{i\}
. Let
i^{\prime}
be the agent in
\mathcal{N}\setminus S
with maximum surplus. While
\Delta_{i^{\prime}}(\mathbf{x})\geq\min_{i\in S}\Delta_{i}(\mathbf{x})-\varepsilon/n^{2}
, add
i^{\prime}
to
S
. Note that at termination,
S
will only comprise of agents with strictly positive surplus, as
\min_{i\in S}\Delta_{i}(\mathbf{x})\geq\max_{i\in S}\Delta_{i}(\mathbf{x})-|S|\cdot\varepsilon/n^{2}>\varepsilon/n-n\cdot\varepsilon/n^{2}=0
. This also implies that
|S|<n
as if there is an agent with strictly positive surplus, there must be at least one agent with strictly negative surplus. This simple claim would be useful later in our analysis.

\mathbf{x}

.

We have

.

Intuitively, set

(i)

\Delta_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\leq\Delta_{i}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}\setminus S}\Delta_{i}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\bigg(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}\setminus S}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}\setminus S}\psi_{ij}(\mathbf{x})+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}\setminus S}\sum_{j\in S}\psi_{ij}(\mathbf{x})\bigg)-\bigg(\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}\setminus S}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{N}\setminus S}\psi_{ji}(\mathbf{x})+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{N}\setminus S}\sum_{j\in S}\psi_{ji}(\mathbf{x})\bigg)

4.2
Membership in PLS

We consider a discrete space of exchanges, where we restrict every

For every

For this, consider any
\mathbf{x}
in

be the output of Algorithm

Lemma 17

\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\in X
.

Proof.

Every

Proof.

Theorem

(6)

4.3
Membership in PPAD

In this section, we show that the problem of computing an
\varepsilon
-reciprocal and
\varepsilon
-core-stable exchange lies in PPAD under the assumption that utility and sharing functions are
L
-Lipschitz continuous for some rational
L
satisfying
L/\varepsilon=\text{poly}(n)
. We rely on the following theorem from
[
EY10
]
which shows that the problem of computing an
almost fixed point
of certain kinds of functions lies in PPAD.

Proposition 2

(
[
EY10
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{F}=\{F_{I}\}
be a family of functions
F_{I}
associated with the instances
I
of a search problem
\Pi
, such that
F_{I}
is a continuous function that maps a convex, compact domain
D_{I}
to itself, and the solutions of
I
are the fixed points of
F_{I}
. The problem of computing an ‘almost fixed point’ is: Given
I
and rational
\varepsilon>0
, compute a rational
x\in D_{I}
such that
|F_{I}(x)-x|<\varepsilon
.

Suppose
\mathcal{F}
satisfies:

(i)

(Polynomial computability) There is a polynomial
q(\cdot)
s.t. for all instances
I
, (a) the domain
D_{I}
is a convex polytope described by a set of inequalities with rational coefficients that can be computed from
I
in time
q(|I|)
, and (b) for any rational
x\in D_{I}
,
F_{I}(x)
is rational and can be computed from
I
and
x
in time
q(|I|+size(x))
.

(ii)

(Polynomial continuity) There is a polynomial
q(\cdot)
s.t. for all instances
I
and rational
\rho>0
, there exists rational
\gamma>0
s.t.
size(\gamma)\leq q(|I|+size(\rho))
and for all
x,y\in D_{I}
,
y\in\mathcal{B}(x,\gamma)
implies
F_{I}(y)\in\mathcal{B}(x,\rho)
.

Then, the problem of computing an almost fixed point for
\mathcal{F}
is in PPAD.

Ideally, we would like to use the function
g:Z\rightarrow Z
from our fixed point formulation from
Section

3
, and prove that the problem of computing an almost fixed point of
g
is in PPAD. However,
g
and
Z
do not directly satisfy the conditions of
Proposition

2
since the formulation suffers from a lack of polynomial computability:

(i)

It is not clear if the constant
\alpha=\alpha(\varepsilon)
appearing in the constraints of
Z
can be efficiently computed.

(ii)

Although the domain
Z
is a convex polytope, it has exponentially many constraints, and hence cannot be directly computed from the instance in polynomial time.

(iii)

The oracle computing the utility and sharing functions returns rational outputs when given a rational exchange
\mathbf{x}
as input. However, for rational
z\in Z
, the exchange
\mathbf{x}=f(z)
need not be rational, as
x_{ij}=f(z)_{ij}=1-\exp(-z_{ij})
.

We address these issues as follows.

(i)

We first re-define the set
Z
. Let
b\geq n\cdot\log(\frac{nL}{\eps})
be a rational number, and let
M=n\cdot b
. Let
Z
be the set given by
Z=\{z\in[0,M]^{n\times n}:\forall\text{ cycles }C\in K_{n},\sum_{e\in C}z_{e}\geq b\}
. By choice of
M
and
b
, the constants appearing in the constraints of
Z
are rational numbers computable in polynomial time and representable using
O(\log n+\log(\frac{nL}{\eps}))
bits.

Further, by definition of
Z
and choice of
b
, for any
z\in Z
, the exchange graph
G(f(z),\frac{\varepsilon}{nL})
is acyclic.
Lemma

8
then implies that the exchange
f(z)
is
\varepsilon
-core-stable for any
z\in Z
. However,
Z
still has exponentially many constraints.

(ii)

Therefore, we embed
Z
in the larger polytope
Z^{\prime}=[0,M]^{n\times n}
, since
Z\subseteq Z^{\prime}
by definition. We define a new function
\tilde{g}:Z^{\prime}\rightarrow Z^{\prime}
whose fixed points are exactly the fixed points of
g
. For this, let
\pi:Z^{\prime}\rightarrow Z
be the
projection
of
Z^{\prime}
onto
Z
, i.e.,
\pi
is the identity on
Z
and maps points in
Z^{\prime}\setminus Z
to their nearest point on the boundary of
Z
. Thus,
\pi(z^{\prime})=\argmin_{z\in Z}{\lVert z^{\prime}-z\rVert}_{2}
for
z^{\prime}\in Z^{\prime}
. Note that
\pi
is a well-defined function since the set
Z
is closed and convex, and the
L_{2}
norm is strictly convex. The function
\tilde{g}
is the composition of
\pi
and
g
, i.e.
\tilde{g}(z)=g(\pi(z))
for
z\in Z^{\prime}
. It is easy to see that any
z\in Z^{\prime}\setminus Z
cannot be a fixed point of
\tilde{g}
, and hence the set of fixed points of
g
and
\tilde{g}
are identical.

(iii)

Finally we address the polynomial computability of
g(z)
for a rational
z\in Z
, given that the utility and sharing oracles only return rational outputs when given rational exchanges
\mathbf{x}
as input. For rational
z\in Z
,
f(z)
need not be rational as
f(z)_{ij}=1-\exp(-z_{ij})
. Therefore, we discuss how to obtain a
continuous, rational approximation

\hat{f}(z)
of
f(z)
through standard techniques
[
EY10
]
. For some small parameter
\kappa
, we partition
[0,M]^{n\times n}
into cubelets of length
\kappa
along cardinal directions, and then construct a simplicization of each cubelet in polynomial time. Given a rational
z\in Z
, we compute
\hat{f}(z)
in polynomial time as follows: identify the simplex to which
z
belongs by binary search, and uniquely express
z
as a convex combination of the
n^{2}+1
vertices
\{z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n^{2}+1}
of the simplex, i.e.,
z=\sum_{i=1}^{n^{2}+1}\lambda_{i}z_{i}
. Compute
\hat{f}(z_{i})
to precision
\kappa
, and then compute
\hat{f}(z)
by linearly interpolation, that is,
\hat{f}(z)=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}\hat{f}(z_{i})
. In this manner, we obtain a continuous, rational approximation function
\hat{f}
for
f
up to an error tolerance of
\kappa
, i.e.,
|\hat{f}(z)-f(z)|\leq O(n^{2}\cdot\kappa)
. Using the rational exchange
\hat{f}(z)
computed from a rational
z\in Z
, we can then approximately compute
g(z)
(and hence also
\tilde{g}(z^{\prime})
for rational
z^{\prime}\in Z^{\prime}
) within a
\text{poly}(\kappa,n)
error. It is then straightforward to see that an
\varepsilon
-almost fixed point of the rational approximation of
\tilde{g}
is an
(\varepsilon+\text{poly}(\kappa,n))
-almost fixed point of
\tilde{g}
. Thus, with the choice of
\kappa=\varepsilon/\text{poly}(n)
, the construction of the continuous rational approximation
\tilde{f}
in polynomial time effectively circumvents the issue of irrational
f(z)
for rational
z
.

In what follows, we assume for simplicity that we can compute a rational value of
f(z)
from rational
z\in Z
.

We are now in a position to prove that
\tilde{g}:Z\rightarrow Z^{\prime}
is polynomially computable, i.e., satisfies condition (i) of
Proposition

2
.

Lemma 19
.

The function
\tilde{g}:Z^{\prime}\rightarrow Z^{\prime}
is polynomially computable.

Proof.

Observe that
Z^{\prime}=[0,M]^{n\times n}
is a convex polytope described by a set of

is rational and polynomial time computable by choice of

. Thus the domain

satisfies property (a) of

2
(i).

Next, we prove property (b) of

2

z^{\prime}\in Z^{\prime}
,
\tilde{g}(z^{\prime})
is rational and can be computed in time
\text{poly}(size(L),size(M),size(z^{\prime}))
. Since
\tilde{g}=g\circ\pi
, it suffices to separately prove this for both
\pi
and

.

For
g
, it suffices to prove the property for each

. Since we are given access to oracles computing the utility and share functions, we only need to argue that

2

is a polytope and can be expressed as

) and

variables.

Let us first note that

, (ii) construct the graph

is acyclic iff

; and if

With the efficient separation oracle for

for

is the solution to the linear program given by

are rational, the LP is one-dimensional, and the constraint set admits an efficient separation oracle, the optimal solution to the LP

for any

. Likewise, we can show that
\beta^{-}_{ij}(z)
is polynomially computable. This shows that the function

is polynomially computable.

Next, we argue that the function
\pi
is polynomially computable. Since
\pi(z)=z
for

, we only need to argue that

is rational and can be computed in polynomial time for rational

. Recall that

. Since we have an efficient separation oracle for the constraint set, that the quadratic program can be solved in polynomial time using the ellipsoid method. Lastly, we argue that for a rational

The quadratic program

\displaystyle\quad\min\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}(z_{ij}-z^{\prime}_{ij})^{2}

\displaystyle\sum_{(i,j)\in C}z_{ij}\geq b,\text{ for all cyclic permutations }C=(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})\text{ of }[n]

\displaystyle 0\leq z_{ij}\leq M\text{ for all }i,j\in[n]

\displaystyle\forall i,j\in[n]:

\displaystyle\ z_{ij}-z^{\prime}_{ij}\leq u_{ij}+\sum_{C:(i,j)\in C}y_{C}

\displaystyle\perp z_{ij}

(7a)

\displaystyle\forall i,j\in[n]:

\displaystyle\ z_{ij}\leq M

\displaystyle\perp\mu_{ij}

\tilde{g}:Z^{\prime}\rightarrow Z^{\prime}

Next, we show that

Theorem 7
.

For every
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, the problem of computing an
\varepsilon
-reciprocal and
\varepsilon
-core-stable exchange is in
PPAD
.

Proof.

We reduce the problem of computing an
\varepsilon
-reciprocal and
\varepsilon
-core-stable exchange to the problem of computing an almost fixed point of
\tilde{g}
, i.e., a point
z^{\prime}\in Z^{\prime}
s.t.
{\lVert\tilde{g}(z^{\prime})-z^{\prime}\rVert}_{2}\leq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2n^{8}L^{3}}
. The theorem follows since the latter problem is in PPAD by
Lemma

21
.

Let
\varepsilon^{\prime}=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2n^{7}L^{2}}
. The reduction maps an almost fixed point
z^{\prime}\in Z^{\prime}
satisfying
{\lVert\tilde{g}(z^{\prime})-z^{\prime}\rVert}_{2}\leq\varepsilon^{\prime}
to the exchange
\mathbf{x}:=f(\pi(z^{\prime}))
. Clearly,
\mathbf{x}
can be computed from
z^{\prime}
in time
\text{poly}(n,\frac{1}{\varepsilon},L)
. We prove that
\mathbf{x}
is
\varepsilon
-reciprocal and
\varepsilon
-core-stable.

First we show that the point
z=\pi(z^{\prime})\in Z
satisfies
{\lVert g(z)-z)\rVert}_{2}\leq 2\varepsilon^{\prime}
. Observe that
\tilde{g}(z^{\prime})=g(z)
since
\tilde{g}(z^{\prime})=g(\pi(z^{\prime}))
. Further, since
z
is the point on
Z
closest to
z^{\prime}
and
g(z)
is another point in
Z
, we have
{\lVert z^{\prime}-z\rVert}_{2}\leq{\lVert z^{\prime}-g(z)\rVert}_{2}
. Thus:

\displaystyle{\lVert g(z)-z\rVert}_{2}

\displaystyle\leq{\lVert g(z)-z^{\prime}\rVert}_{2}+{\lVert z^{\prime}-z\rVert}_{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\cdot{\lVert g(z)-z\rVert}_{2}

(since
{\lVert z^{\prime}-z\rVert}_{2}\leq{\lVert z^{\prime}-g(z)\rVert}_{2}
)

\displaystyle=2\cdot{\lVert\tilde{g}(z^{\prime})-z^{\prime}\rVert}_{2}

(since
\tilde{g}(z^{\prime})=g(z)
)

\displaystyle\leq 2\cdot\varepsilon^{\prime}.

Next we consider the exchange
\mathbf{x}=f(z)
where
x_{ij}=1-\exp(-z_{ij})
. Since
z\in Z
,
Lemma

8
and the definition of
Z
imply that
\mathbf{x}
is
\varepsilon
-core-stable. We claim that:

Claim 3
.

If
\mathbf{x}
is not
\varepsilon
-reciprocal, then
{\lVert g(z)-z\rVert}_{2}>\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{n^{7}L^{2}}
.

However, we showed earlier that
{\lVert g(z)-z\rVert}_{2}\leq 2\cdot\varepsilon^{\prime}=2\cdot\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{2n^{7}L^{2}}=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{n^{7}L^{2}}
. Thus
Claim

3
implies that
\mathbf{x}
must be
\varepsilon
-reciprocal. We now prove the claim.

Proof of
Claim

3
.

If
\mathbf{x}
is not
\varepsilon
-reciprocal, then there exists an agent
i
such that
\Delta_{i}(\mathbf{x})>\varepsilon/n
. We pick the set of high surplus agents
S
by Algorithm
1
. Note that we have
\min_{i\in S}\Delta_{i}(\mathbf{x})>\max_{i\in\mathcal{N}\setminus S}\Delta_{i}(\mathbf{x})+\varepsilon/n^{2}
. By Lemma
10
, we have an agent
j\in\mathcal{N}\setminus S
, such that
\sum_{i\in S}\psi_{ij}(\mathbf{x})\geq\varepsilon/n^{2}
, implying that there exists an
i\in S
, such that
\psi_{ij}(\mathbf{x})\geq\varepsilon/n^{3}
. The Lipschitzness of the utility functions imply that
x_{ij}\geq\varepsilon/(n^{3}L)
, showing that:

\displaystyle z_{ij}=\log(\frac{1}{1-x_{ij}})\geq\log(\frac{1}{1-\varepsilon/(n^3L)})=-\log(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{n^3L})\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{n^{3}L},

where last inequality used the fact that
\log(1+y)\leq y
for all
y>-1
. We now distinguish two cases, depending on the value of
\beta^{-}_{ij}(z)
.

Case 1:
\beta^{-}_{ij}(z)\geq\varepsilon/(n^{3}L)
.

First observe that from the definition of
g(\cdot)
, we have,

\displaystyle(g(z))_{ij}-z_{ij}=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\cdot(g^{ij}(z)-z)_{ij}

(8)

Since
\Delta_{i}(f(z))-\Delta_{j}(f(z))=\Delta_{i}(x)-\Delta_{j}(x)>\varepsilon/n^{3}>0
, we have
(g^{ij}(z)-z)_{ij}=\delta\cdot\beta^{-}_{ij}(\Delta_{i}(f(z))-\Delta_{j}(f(z))
. Since
\beta^{-}_{ij}(z)\geq\varepsilon/(n^{3}L)
, and
\Delta_{i}(f(z))-\Delta_{j}(f(z))=\Delta_{i}(x)-\Delta_{j}(x)>\varepsilon/n^{3}
, and
\delta>1/nL
from
Lemma

8
, we have

\displaystyle|(g(z))_{ij}-z_{ij}|\geq\varepsilon^{2}/(n^{7}L^{2}),

implying that
{\lVert g(z)-z\rVert}_{2}>\varepsilon^{2}/(n^{7}L^{2})
.

Case 2:
\beta^{-}_{ij}(z)<\varepsilon/(n^{3}L)
:

We have already established
z_{ij}\geq\varepsilon/n^{3}L
, meaning that if we reduce
z_{ij}
by
\varepsilon/(n^{3}L)
, then the constraint
z_{ij}\geq 0
will not be violated. Then, the only constraint that can be violated is some constraint of the form
\sum_{\ell\in[k]}z_{i_{\ell}i_{\ell+1}}\geq n\log(nL/\varepsilon)
(indices are modulo
k
), where
i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{k}
is some sequence of agents with
i_{1}=i
and
i_{2}=j
.

This implies that
\sum_{\ell\in[k]}z_{i_{\ell}i_{\ell+1}}<n\log(nL/ \varepsilon)+\varepsilon/(n^{3}L)
, trivially implying that each
z_{i_{\ell}i_{\ell+1}}<n\log(nL/ \varepsilon)+\varepsilon/(n^{3}L)
for
\ell\in[k]
. Observe that since
0\leq z_{i_{\ell}i_{\ell+1}}\leq M
, there is still some slack in increasing
z_{i_{\ell}i_{\ell}}
. In particular, this slack is

\displaystyle M-n\log(nL/ \varepsilon)-\varepsilon/(n^{3}L)

\displaystyle>n^{2}\cdot\log(nL)/\varepsilon)-n\log(nL/ \varepsilon)-\varepsilon/(n^{3}L)

\displaystyle>n\log(nL/\varepsilon)

as
M=n^{2}\log(nL/\varepsilon)
. This suggests that
\beta^{+}_{i_{\ell}i_{\ell+1}}(z)\geq n\log(nL/\varepsilon)
for all
\ell\in[k]
, since no constraint of
Z
gets violated on increasing
z_{ij}
other than
z_{ij}\leq M
. Let
\mathit{gap}_{\ell}=\Delta_{i_{\ell}}(f(z))-\Delta_{i_{\ell+1}}(f(z))
for all
\ell\in[k]
. Note that, we have
\sum_{\ell=2}^{k}\mathit{gap}_{\ell}=\Delta_{j}(f(z))-\Delta_{i}(f(z))<-\varepsilon/n^{2}

and

, where
\mathit{gap}_{\ell}<-\varepsilon/n^{3}
as
k\leq n
. Therefore, there exists agents
i_{\ell}
and
i_{\ell+1}

\Delta_{i_{\ell}}(f(z))-\Delta_{i_{\ell+1}}(f(z))<-\varepsilon/n^{3}

\beta^{+}_{i_{\ell}i_{\ell+1}}(z)\geq n\log(nL/\varepsilon)
. Since

, we have:

\displaystyle|(g^{i_{\ell}i_{\ell+1}}(z)-z)_{i_{\ell}i_{\ell+1}}|

\displaystyle=|\delta\cdot\beta^{+}_{i_{\ell}i_{\ell+1}}(z)\cdot(\Delta_{i_{\ell+1}}(f(z))-\Delta_{i_{\ell}}(f(z)))|

\displaystyle>\frac{1}{nL}\cdot(n\log(nL/\varepsilon))\cdot\frac{\varepsilon}{n^{3}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{n^{4}L^{2}}.

Equation
8

.
