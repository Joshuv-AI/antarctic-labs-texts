Paravertebral muscle quality and adjacent facet joint degeneration as significant predictors of ASDis revision after lumbar fusion
Authors: Dandan Li, Peng Gao, YingJuan Chang, Xing Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPosterior lumbar interbody fusion (PLIF) combined with pedicle screw internal fixation is widely used to treat lumbar degenerative diseases. However, adjacent segment disease (ASDis) is a severe complication and requires revision surgery, and few studies have focused on this complication. Paravertebral muscle quality loss is associated with the occurrence of lumbar diseases and poor postoperative prognosis, but its relationship with ASDis revision remains unclear.PurposeTo explore the relationship between paravertebral muscle quality and ASDis revision after lumbar fusion and identify its independent risk factors.MethodsPatients who underwent revision surgery for ASDis at our hospital were enrolled. To evaluate the risk factors for revision, we selected a control group. Each patient in the control group was matched by age, sex, height, weight, BMI, number of fused segments and follow-up duration with a patient in the revision group. Paravertebral muscle parameters, including the cross-sectional area (CSA), functional cross-sectional area (FCSA), CSA-vertebral index (CSA-VI), FCSA-vertebral index (FCSA-VI), and FCSA/CSA ratio, were measured at the third lumbar (L3) and fourth lumbar (L4) levels via MRI. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and logistic regression were used for analysis.ResultsAt the L3 and L4 levels, the FCSA, FCSA-VI and FCSA/CSA ratio of the paraspinal muscle, erector spinae and multifidus were significantly lower in the revision group (P < 0.05). The L4 paravertebral muscle FCSA/CSA ratio had the highest area under the curve (AUC) value (0.942). Preoperative adjacent facet joint degeneration was greater in the revision group (P < 0.05). Logistic regression revealed that the L4 paravertebral muscle FCSA/CSA ratio (odds ratio = 0.731; P < 0.001) and preoperative adjacent facet joint degeneration (odds ratio = 4.664; P = 0.023) were independent risk factors.ConclusionDecreased paravertebral muscle quality is associated with an increased incidence of ASDis revision. L4 paravertebral muscle FCSA/CSA and preoperative adjacent facet joint degeneration can predict whether revision surgery is needed for patients with ASDis.

Introduction

Lumbar fusion is currently the main treatment for various lumbar pathologies, including degenerative lumbar instability, degenerative disc disease, severe spondylolisthesis, lumbar spinal stenosis, and lumbar deformity [1, 2]. Lumbar fusion can be classified into posterior, anterior, and transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion. Among these approaches, posterior lumbar interbody fusion (PLIF) with pedicle screw fixation is widely utilized clinically because of its high safety profile and minimal invasiveness [3, 4]. While often a successful procedure, lumbar fusion is not without complication ns. One known complication is adjacent segment disease (ASDis) [5]. It is thought that abnormal mechanical loading and increased motion through the proximal or distal segments can accelerate degenerative changes in unfused segments [6–8]. The development of ASDis significantly impairs the quality of life of patients following lumbar fusion surgery, and in severe cases, it necessitates revision surgery on adjacent segments. Therefore, identifying the risk factors for ASDis during revision surgery is highly important.

Paravertebral muscle quality deterioration is characterized primarily by reduced paravertebral muscle mass and increased fat infiltration. Currently, the clinical utility of imaging-based evaluation of paravertebral muscle quality deterioration as a prognostic marker has been increasingly validated across multiple conditions requiring surgery, including metastatic diseases, trauma, and fractures [9–12]. Despite extensive research focused on identifying potential determinants of revision surgery, the association between paravertebral muscle quality and revision surgery incidence remains inadequately understood. Therefore, by comparing paravertebral muscle quality based on preoperative imaging data between patients who underwent revision surgery and those who did not undergo revision surgery because of the absence of clinical surgical indications, this study aimed to explore the relationships between changes in paravertebral muscle quality and the incidence of revision surgery after lumbar fusion and to analyze other imaging-related risk factors for revision surgery.

Materials and methods

Patient selection

The experiments involving human participants were reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Dalian Medical University, and this research was performed in accordance with the ethical standards presented in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards. In this study, the medical records and imaging data of all patients who underwent PLIF with screw fixation for degenerative spinal diseases between January 2007 and January 2023 were retrospectively reviewed. The inclusion criterion was a preoperative diagnosis of degenerative lumbar diseases, including spinal stenosis, degenerative lumbar spondylolisthesis, and lumbar disc herniation. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) a history of other nondegenerative diseases affecting the spinal structure, such as spinal tumors, spinal infections, and spinal fractures; (2) a previous history of paravertebral muscle injury or infection; (3) comorbidities of metabolic diseases that may affect paravertebral muscle metabolism, including diabetes mellitus, thyroid dysfunction, and hyperuricemia; and (4) incomplete lumbar imaging data. During the study period, 957 patients underwent PLIF with screw fixation, among whom 41 patients who met the aforementioned exclusion criteria were excluded. Ultimately, 916 patients were included in the retrospective analysis, 41 of whom underwent secondary revision surgery and were assigned to the revision surgery group. Surgical indications for ASDis revision were defined as severe clinical symptoms (low back pain, radicular pain or numbness, intermittent claudication, etc.) that significantly impaired patients' daily living activities and failed to respond to 3 months of conservative treatment. To minimize selection bias and surgeon-dependent variation, all decisions for revision surgery were made by the same multidisciplinary team using consistent clinical and radiological criteria. All patients were managed under the same clinical pathway at our institution to ensure uniform application of surgical indications. To identify factors associated with revision surgery, 41 patients who underwent single PLIF with pedicle screw fixation were selected as the control group via propensity score matching. These patients were matched to the revision surgery group for age, sex, body mass index (BMI), number of fused segments, and follow-up duration.

MRI equipment and scanning parameters

A GE 3.0T Signa HDxt scanner was used, with a spinal coil for image acquisition. The scanning sequences included sagittal T1-weighted (T1W), sagittal T2-weighted (T2W), sagittal T2W fat suppression, and axial T2W. Axial T2W images were analyzed, with a repetition time/echo time (TR/TE) of 2900–4880 ms/102–120 ms, a field of view (FOV) of 18 × 18–22 × 22 cm, a slice thickness of 4.0 mm, and a 320 × 256 matrix. For the sagittal T1W images, the TR/TE ranged from 300 to 900 ms/7–15 ms, the FOV was 32 × 32 cm, the slice thickness was 4.0 mm, and the matrix width was 320 × 224.

CT and X-ray equipment and parameters

Lumbar CT images were obtained via a SIEMENS Emotion 16-slice spiral CT scanner or a GE Light speed 16-slice spiral CT scanner with a tube voltage of 120 kV, a tube current of 240–320 mAs, a slice thickness of 1 mm, a slice spacing of 1 mm, a rotation speed of 0.6–0.7 s, a pitch of 0.9–1.5 mm, and a matrix of 380 × 380. Lumbar lateral X-ray radiographs were taken via SIEMENS Aristos MX or SIEMENS Aristos VX digital X-ray equipment, with a tube voltage of 81 kV and a tube current of 40 mAs.

Data measurement

Measurements

All measurements were independently conducted by two blinded radiologists with at least 2 years of relevant experience. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus to obtain the final dataset.

Measurement of paravertebral muscle

Using ImageJ software (version 1.53u; National Institutes of Health, USA), the paravertebral muscle was analyzed on preoperative axial T2-weighted MR images at the inferior endplate levels of L3 and L4 [13]. Regions of interest (ROIs) were drawn along the margins of each paravertebral muscle to obtain the total cross-sectional area (CSA) of the psoas major (PM), erector spinae (ES), multifidus (MF), and paraspinal muscle (PSM) (the sum of the erector spinae and multifidus). The functional cross-sectional area (FCSA) of each paravertebral muscle was obtained using threshold-based segmentation [14, 15] (Fig. 1). Statistical analysis

For statistical analysis and model development, SPSS 27.0 and MedCalc software (version 20.022) were used. Interobserver consistency for all the quantitative measurements was evaluated via the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Categorical variables, including sex and the number of fused segments, were analyzed with Pearson’s chi-square test. Normality testing was conducted for all the continuous variables prior to the subsequent analyses. Continuous variables that conformed to a normal distribution are expressed as the means ± standard deviations and were compared via the independent samples t test, whereas those that did not meet the normality assumption were expressed as median (25th percentile, 75th percentile) and analyzed via nonparametric tests. For paravertebral muscle parameters with statistically significant differences between groups, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed to calculate the area under the curve (AUC) values, and the optimal cutoff values, sensitivity, and specificity were determined. The Delong test was applied to compare the AUC values among different parameters. Univariate analyses, including Pearson’s chi-square test and the independent samples t test as appropriate, were performed to assess the differences in facet joint degeneration grades, VBQ values, HU values, preoperative LL values, and postoperative LL values between the experimental group and the control group. Logistic regression analysis was then conducted to incorporate paravertebral muscle parameters and positive results from the univariate analysis to identify independent predictors of ASDis revision. A P value of < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Discussion

The influence of confounding factors such as age, sex, BMI, number of fused segments, and follow-up duration was controlled for in this study via propensity score matching. On the basis of preoperative MR images, we analyzed the relationships between the quality of the PM, ES, MF, and PSM and the incidence of ASDis after PLIF with pedicle screw fixation. We found no significant differences in the preoperative PM CSA, FCSA, CSA-VI, FCSA-VI, or FCSA/CSA ratio between the revision group and the control group.

The PM is the main muscle of the anterior group of lumbar paravertebral muscles and originates from and spans multiple lumbar vertebral segments [20]. The bilateral PM participates in lateral lumbar flexion through axial compression and coordinated interactions but plays a minor role in maintaining lumbar segmental stability. In addition, the PM is involved in hip joint movement [21, 22]. The lack of association between PM-quality deterioration and the occurrence of postlumbar fusion ASDis may be attributed to the fact that the PM is essentially not involved in maintaining the stability of adjacent segments.

This study revealed no significant differences in the CSA or CSA-VI of the MF, ES, or PSM between the ASDis revision group and the control group. However, the FCSA, FCSA-VI, and FCSA/CSA ratios of the MF, ES, and PSM in the ASDis revision group were lower than those in the control group, indicating that fat tissue without contractile function replaced contractile muscle fibers. Previous studies have shown a significant negative correlation between the fat fraction of the paravertebral muscle and muscle strength [23]. The FCSA/CSA ratio reflects the degree of muscle fat infiltration; a lower FCSA/CSA ratio indicates a greater degree of fat infiltration and weaker muscle strength.

The lumbar MF originates from the spinous processes and laminae of the L1 to L5 vertebrae, with its distal attachment to the mammillary processes of the lumbosacral vertebrae and the sacrum, and is adjacent to the ES laterally, which can be divided into superficial and deep layers. The MF is involved in maintaining normal lumbar lordosis and segmental movement [24]. The ES is located lateral to the MF and originates from the dorsal surface of the sacrum, lumbar spinous processes, iliac crest, and lumbodorsal fascia [20]. The ES participates in spinal extension and, together with the MF, counteracts the flexion effect produced by the abdominal muscle during trunk rotation. As the main lumbar extensor muscle, the MF and ES jointly maintain the stability of the lumbar segment. Therefore, preoperative deterioration of MF and ES quality is associated with postlumbar fusion ASDis revision.

Most previous studies on the paravertebral muscle have focused on the L3 and L4 levels. Therefore, paravertebral muscle quality at the L3 and L4 levels was analyzed and compared in this study. The predictive efficacy of paravertebral muscle quality at the L4 level for postlumbar fusion ASDis revision was greater. This may be because L4 belongs to the lower lumbar spine, where the paravertebral muscle is subjected to greater biomechanical changes and trunk loads.

Biomechanical studies on adjacent nonfused segments after PLIF have shown that during lumbar flexion–extension, lateral flexion, and rotation, the pressure on the nucleus pulposus and annulus fibrosus of adjacent nonfused segments increases after PLIF, with the most significant increase occurring during extension. The range of motion (ROM) of adjacent nonfused segments also increases in all directions, especially during flexion and extension. With the progression of ASDis, the intradiscal pressure of adjacent segments increases, whereas the ROM of adjacent segments gradually decreases [25]. Therefore, lumbar flexion–extension movements, especially extension movements, are more closely involved in the development of ASDis.

The MF and ES are the main lumbar extensor muscles involved in lumbar extension movement. Studies have shown that deterioration of ES and MF quality is associated with other adjacent segment pathological changes, such as proximal junctional kyphosis and recurrent adjacent segment fractures after surgery [26]. Together with the results of this study, these findings indicate that the MF and ES can reduce the mechanical stress on adjacent nonfused segments and protect adjacent surgical segments.

Studies using biomechanical models to explore the impact of paravertebral muscle injury on adjacent segments after lumbar fusion have revealed that the axial compression force and shear force of adjacent segments increase after injury to the PSM, suggesting that the PSM plays an important role in the occurrence and development of ASDis [27]. Consistent with the results of this study, the PSM, as the sum of the ES and MF, plays a major role in lumbar flexion–extension movement and is attached to each lumbar segment. It can counteract the increased intradiscal pressure of adjacent segments, stabilize adjacent segments, and reduce the ROM of adjacent segments. Therefore, compared with ES or MF alone, the FCSA/CSA ratio of the PSM is the most effective predictor of ASDis.

Human skeletal muscle fibers can be divided into two main types: type I (slow-twitch oxidative fibers) and type II (fast-twitch fibers). Type II fibers are further divided into Type IIa and Type IIx fibers. Type I fibers have a slow contraction speed but are resistant to fatigue, whereas type II fibers have a fast contraction speed but are more prone to fatigue [23]. According to previous research, the proportion of different muscle fiber types in the PSM is 60% for Type I, 23% for Type IIa, and 15% for Type IIx [28]. Transformation can occur between Type I and Type II fibers: Type I fibers transform into Type II fibers when muscle use decreases, and Type II fibers transform into Type I fibers when muscle use increases.

Studies have shown that in patients with chronic low back pain, the proportion of type I fibers in the MF and ES decreases, the proportion of type II fibers increases, and the number of both types decreases [29–31]. This explains why the FCSA, FCSA-VI, and FCSA/CSA ratios of the MF and ES in the revision group were significantly lower than those in the control group in this study. A biopsy of the MF revealed that the MF contains more fibroadipogenic progenitor cells than the hamstring muscle does, making it more prone to intramuscular fat infiltration and subsequent deterioration of MF quality [32]. These findings indicate that the MF is susceptible to fat infiltration.

In this study, the area under the curve (AUC) value of the FCSA/CSA ratio of the MF was greater than that of the ES. Moreover, since the MF and ES jointly participate in maintaining adjacent segment stability, the FCSA/CSA ratio of the PSM is an independent predictor of the occurrence of revision surgery to correct postlumbar fusion ASDis.

Previous studies [16] have shown that a deterioration of PSM quality is associated with pathological changes in adjacent segments after lumbar fusion. However, these studies did not distinguish between adjacent segment degeneration (ASDeg), which is diagnosed only by changes in imaging parameters, and ASDis, which requires surgical revision. Compared with ASDeg, ASDis requiring surgical revision has a greater impact on patients’ postoperative quality of life. Therefore, this study analyzed the paravertebral muscle quality of patients with ASDis who underwent surgical revision.

Previous studies [9, 33] on the relationship between paravertebral muscle quality and ASDis did not consider the effects of metabolic diseases such as diabetes mellitus and thyroid dysfunction on paravertebral muscle, nor did they consider whether the control group included patients with ASDis who did not undergo surgery. In contrast, the control group included in this study was evaluated by spinal surgeons as not having ASDis surgical indications; thus, cases where ASDis revision was not performed because of personal willingness and other reasons were excluded.

In several studies [34, 35], the paravertebral muscle at the intervertebral disc level was measured and the ratio of the paravertebral muscle CSA to the intervertebral disc CSA was used to control for the influence of body size. However, this method is easily affected by intervertebral disc bulging and herniation. Therefore, in this study, measurements were performed at the lower endplate level, and the FCSA and CSA were normalized to the cross-sectional area of the corresponding vertebral body to calculate the FCSA-VI and CSA-VI to control for body size effects.

The results of this study suggest that incorporating the evaluation of preoperative paravertebral muscle, especially the PSM, into routine preoperative evaluation helps to assess the recovery of lumbar function and the risk of reoperation in patients after lumbar fusion. Patients with poor PSM quality on preoperative MRI before lumbar fusion may be more likely to develop ASDis requiring secondary surgical revision in the future, and appropriate implementation of rehabilitation treatment to strengthen the PSM should be considered.

In addition, regarding surgical approach selection, minimally invasive surgical methods that cause less damage to the paravertebral muscle, such as minimally invasive transforaminal lumbar interbody fusion (MIS-TLIF) under a tubular retractor, should be prioritized over traditional open posterior approaches that require extensive dissection of the paravertebral muscle.

The results of the present study demonstrated that no significant difference in LLC was found between the revision and control groups, and PI-LL mismatch and other spinopelvic sagittal parameters were not significantly associated with the occurrence of revision surgery due to adjacent segment degeneration following lumbar fusion. The possible reasons are as follows. First, all patients received conventional decompression and fusion without intentional aggressive lumbar lordosis reconstruction, and the baseline spinopelvic sagittal parameters were well balanced between groups. Meanwhile, this study enrolled patients who underwent short-segment lumbar fusion, which has a limited effect on the overall spinopelvic sagittal biomechanical environment [36]. Even in the presence of PI-LL mismatch or other sagittal malalignment, the stress on adjacent segments could not be significantly increased; thus, the long-term risk of revision was not clearly elevated. Second, this study adopted revision surgery for clinically symptomatic adjacent segment disease as the endpoint rather than simple radiographic degeneration, and radiographic adjacent segment degeneration is not completely consistent with clinical symptoms or the requirement for reoperation. In conclusion, PI-LL mismatch and other spinopelvic sagittal parameters, together with LLC magnitude, are not key factors affecting long-term revision for adjacent segment disease in patients treated with short-segment lumbar fusion.

This study revealed that degenerative changes in the lumbar facet joint are associated with the occurrence of ASDis revision surgery and are independent predictors of ASDis revision surgery. Previous studies have confirmed that degenerative changes in the lumbar facet joint are associated with ASDeg and manifest only as changes in imaging-related parameters [35]. This may be because the pressure on the facet joints of adjacent segments increases after lumbar fusion, and preoperative facet joint degenerative changes reduce their ability to resist pressure.

Salimi et al. demonstrated that trunk muscle mass measured by bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) is strongly correlated with paravertebral muscle cross‑sectional area and back muscle strength in elderly individuals [14]. For patients with spinal instrumentation, segmental and multi‑frequency BIA represent reliable alternative methods free from metallic interference.

Limitations

The limitations of this study include the following: (1) This was a single-center retrospective study with a relatively small sample size, which may result in residual selection bias and limit the generalizability of the results. Therefore, further validation in multicenter, large-sample, and prospective studies is warranted. (2) The use of the VBQ scores and HU values to reflect BMD may introduce potential measurement errors.(3) Not all enrolled patients underwent routine sarcopenia evaluation via BIA or dual energy X-ray absorptiometry. Therefore, we were unable to adequately assess the potential impact of sarcopenia as a confounding factor on revision surgery outcomes.

Conclusion

This study revealed that deterioration of the quality of the ES, MF, and PSM is associated with the occurrence of postlumbar fusion ASDis revision. The FCSA/CSA ratio of the PSM at the L4 level and the degree of preoperative adjacent segment facet joint degeneration are independent predictive factors for postlumbar fusion ASDis revision. The incorporation of paravertebral muscle quality into routine preoperative evaluations helps predict the risk of reoperation in patients, and implementation of exercise routines that strengthen the paravertebral muscle may reduce the incidence of ASDis revision.
