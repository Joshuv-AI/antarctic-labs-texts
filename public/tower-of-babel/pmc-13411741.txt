Benefit of mixed reality assistance on CT-based ear anatomy spatial recognition: a preliminary study
Authors: Jia Guo, Jianguo Duan, Yandi He, Huilin Cui, Chengyu Zhang, Zhen Yu, Yuqi Jiang, Qinglei Zhang, Haoyue Tan, Huan Jia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo evaluate the benefit of a mixed reality (MR) platform on CT-based ear anatomy spatial recognition and annotation performance by comparing with traditional learning.MethodsA prospective study (N=8) assigned undergraduate medical students to the MR group and traditional group (textbooks and images). Each completed identification exercises of 8 key anatomical structures on 20 temporal bone CT cases over three learning phases. Outcomes included annotation accuracy, task completion time and learning curve. Participants completed a Likert-style questionnaire evaluating the learning experience and the adverse health effects experienced.ResultsThe MR group achieved an earlier learning plateau and better performance than the traditional group (6.0±3.5 vs. 8.0±4.9 cases). Mixed-design ANOVA demonstrated consistent, large-magnitude performance advantages for the MR group in annotation accuracy (η²ₚ=0.605) and task completion time (η²ₚ=0.875). MR teaching method was most advantageous for complex structures such as the ossicular chain and facial nerve. Learning experience scores were higher with MR, though 50% of MR users reported mild visual fatigue. ConclusionsIn this preliminary study, MR-assisted learning shortened the learning curve and improved performance on a CT-based temporal bone annotation task compared with textbook-based learning, with the most pronounced advantages observed for spatially complex structures. These findings indicate the potential of MR as an adjunct in CT-based ear anatomy training and warrant evaluation in larger cohorts. Background

Mixed reality (MR) is a class of visualization technologies that overlay computer-generated three-dimensional content onto the user’s view of the surrounding physical environment while maintaining real-time spatial registration between virtual and real components [1–3]. Within the broader spectrum of extended reality, MR is conceptually distinct from both virtual reality (VR), which fully occludes the physical environment and replaces it with a computer-generated scene, and augmented reality (AR), which superimposes virtual content onto the real world without stable spatial anchoring or interactive coupling [2]. As MR technology has matured in recent years, it has been increasingly applied in medical settings, including orthopedics, neurosurgery, and cardiovascular surgery. These applications typically utilize three-dimensional (3D) projections of patient imaging data as reference objects, providing clinicians with holographic models that accurately reproduce pathological structures at scale. Through precise registration of virtual models with the actual environment, these technologies enable comprehensive overlays on the patient’s physical position, offering powerful support for preoperative planning and intraoperative navigation [4–6].

The concealed nature and complexity of otology anatomical structures, along with the challenging mapping of three-dimensional structures onto two-dimensional imaging, pose severe challenges to specialists’ three-dimensional spatial cognitive abilities. This cognitive difficulty is more prominent in clinical training; for example, endoscopic tympanoplasty requires completing approximately 150 operations to reach a stable level [7], fully reflecting the dual requirements of otology specialty training for spatial cognition and operational skills. Meanwhile, traditional textbook teaching faces practical challenges such as diverse surgical methods, limited training environments, and specimen shortages, with significant room for improvement in both cost-effectiveness and teaching effectiveness [8].

However, the application of MR in otology has not progressed as rapidly as in other specialties due to the diminutive anatomical structures involved. For example, the stapes arches measure approximately 0.3 mm and the round window measures about 1–1.5 mm [9, 10], demanding exceptional precision from MR technology. Multiple robotic prototypes for middle ear surgery have been reported, but only a couple have reached initial clinical use so far [11, 12]. With recent advancements in MR precision capabilities, coupled with the proliferation of telemedicine and remote teaching, novel educational modalities have emerged as subjects of research interest. Unlike clinical applications with stringent demands for precision and real-time performance, MR technology in medical education and research contexts encounters more flexible requirements and tolerance for latency, which lowers the barrier to implementation in otological education.

Several studies have explored the effectiveness of three-dimensional holographic models in teaching ear anatomy [13]. Bosc et al. noted that learning curves for three-dimensional models are more gradual than those for two-dimensional images, highlighting the significance of MR technology in helping junior surgeons learn surgical theory and improve operative techniques [14]. Recent research indicates that the combination of HoloLens 2 with 3D Slicer enables learners to achieve comparable levels of CT structure identification and spatial correspondence with traditional video courses within 30 min of independent training [15].

Current MR systems for ear anatomy primarily focus on inner ear visualization but lack systematic evaluation of learning curves and training efficiency. Future development requires exploring optimal teaching models for anatomical subunits and optimizing curriculum design. Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the efficacy of MR-based training in enhancing learning efficiency for CT-based ear anatomy spatial recognition and annotation, with respect to annotation accuracy, task completion time, learning plateau, learning experience, and adverse health effects.

Methods

Study design

This prospective study was conducted during the development of a surgical robotic imaging-based trajectory planning system. As part of this development process, the educational efficacy of a novel mixed reality (MR) platform (OTOMR) was evaluated in comparison with traditional teaching methods for ear anatomy education.

Dataset

All ear 3D imaging data used in this study were sourced from the otology clinical database of Shanghai Ninth People’s Hospital, Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, and were authorized for teaching research after de-identification. Data inclusion criteria: High-resolution CT (HRCT) data from adult temporal bone scan (18–60 years old) between January 2020 and June 2023; slice thickness 0.625–0.67 mm, matrix 512 × 512, DICOM format raw data. Data exclusion criteria: Cases with pathologies affecting the middle ear or mastoid, including inflammatory conditions (e.g. otitis media) and/or structural abnormalities (e.g. malformations or defects).

Holographic interactive teaching platform

We developed a mixed reality otology holographic interactive teaching platform called OTOMR. The platform is protected by intellectual property rights, including a software copyright (Registration No.2026SR0181455) and a Chinese invention patent (Application No. CN202110335901.6). The platform operates as a Unity application built on the Microsoft HoloLens 2, employing the device’s optical see-through display and spatial mapping to anchor holographic temporal bone models within the user’s real-world field of view; users can physically walk around a world-locked model and observe it from arbitrary viewpoints. The development process incorporated multimodal medical image registration algorithms, utilizing block-normalized mutual information metrics and Powell optimization algorithms for precise registration, with enhanced accuracy achieved through local deformation processing based on spring molecular systems [16].

The platform was developed through a standardized data processing workflow. CT scan data were processed in 3D Slicer 5.6.2 to generate characterized two-dimensional images and segmented three-dimensional models, which were then integrated with corresponding diagnostic information to form comprehensive educational datasets. Using Unity 3D, we constructed anatomically accurate 3D models of ear structures, incorporating color coding and labeling, with interactive functionality implemented through C# scripting.

The anatomical structure interaction module constitutes the platform’s core educational component, leveraging Unity 3D and HoloLens 2 interaction systems to deliver an immersive visualization environment for otological structures. This module efficiently imports clinical data that has been segmented in 3D Slicer through batch STL file processing, presenting high-fidelity ear holographic models that maintain anatomical precision. Users interact with these models through the gesture recognition capabilities of the HoloLens 2 system as shown in Fig. 1, enabling natural and intuitive manipulation.

The platform’s educational utility is delivered through three interconnected functions. It allows for fine structure decomposition, enabling learners to deconstruct composite ear models into discrete units. Furthermore, spatial manipulation and positioning capabilities allow users to dynamically scale, rotate, and reposition models to examine anatomical relationships from multiple angles. Complementing these features, a real-time annotation display automatically identifies and labels key structures, providing immediate visual cues to reinforce the memorization of anatomical terms and spatial relationships.

Study subjects

This study recruited volunteers from comprehensive universities between March and June 2024, enrolling 8 undergraduate students, including 4 clinical medicine students (who had completed the “Systematic Anatomy” course, including 20 min of textbook ear anatomy teaching) and 4 engineering students (with only 20 min of ear anatomy textbook teaching). Inclusion criteria included: (1) Age 18–25 years; (2) Uncorrected or corrected visual acuity ≥ 0.8; (3) No history of severe motion sickness or neuropsychiatric disorders; (4) Demonstrated proficiency in 3D Slicer 4.11 operation. Exclusion criteria included: (1) Previous specialized ear anatomy training; (2) Use of mixed reality (MR) devices in the past three months prior to enrollment; (3) Unwillingness to participate in post-experiment follow-up assessments.

Participants were allocated using stratified randomization by academic background, implemented via a sealed envelope method. The eight participants comprised two strata: four clinical medicine students and four engineering students. For each stratum, four sealed opaque envelopes, two marked ‘MR group’ and two marked ‘Traditional group’, were prepared, shuffled to randomize the order, and placed into a container. Participants within each stratum sequentially drew one envelope each. This procedure ensured that each group contained exactly two medical and two engineering students at baseline, eliminating imbalance in prior academic background. The MR group utilized HoloLens devices to study ear anatomy through holographic representations, while the traditional group learned identical content through standard anatomy textbooks and illustrations. Both groups engaged in self-directed learning for 60 min without instructor interaction. Participants received a 20-minute training session on 3D Slicer 4.11, practicing segmentation on abdominal models with a standardized threshold range of 200–700 HU.

The 60-minute learning session was conducted in a dedicated study environment with a research-team member present to maintain procedural consistency but without providing anatomical instruction. The subsequent 20-case CT annotation tasks were performed during pre-arranged self-study time slots, with participants instructed to work independently and without consulting external anatomical references, discussing case material with peers, or seeking additional instruction.

Evaluation of annotation accuracy, task completion time, learning experience and adverse health effects

The evaluation focused on four metrics: annotation accuracy, task completion time, learning experience and adverse health effects. Participants were required to annotate eight key anatomical structures on 20 clinical temporal bone CT images. The target structures included the external auditory canal, tympanic membrane, ossicular chain, facial nerve, cochlear vestibular membranous labyrinth, cochlear vestibular bony labyrinth, internal auditory canal, and round window membrane. All annotations were exported in the Nii format for subsequent analysis.

For descriptive visualization, three-dimensional reconstructions of MR-group participants’ annotations were subsequently generated in 3D Slicer 4.11 from the exported Nii files. These reconstructions are visualization outputs derived from each participant’s exported annotation data; they are not captures of the HoloLens 2 mixed-reality interface and were not used for quantitative accuracy scoring.

Learning curve assessment

The evaluation protocol was structured across three distinct learning phases: Phase 1 (foundation-building period, cases 1–5) focused on basic operational skill acquisition; Phase 2 (skill breakthrough period, cases 6–10) emphasized plateau achievement and complex structure management; and Phase 3 (mastery-stability period, cases 11–20) concentrated on skill consolidation and efficiency optimization.

The primary metric for evaluating learning progression was the individual learning curve analysis, centered on determining when participants reached stable performance levels. Each participant’s learning plateau was defined using personalized criteria: three consecutive cases achieving accuracy ≥ 85% of their final performance level (calculated from their last five cases), with a coefficient of variation (CV) < 6%. This individualized approach accommodated different baseline capabilities while maintaining standardized stability requirements.

Annotation accuracy assessment

Annotation accuracy was evaluated for eight key anatomical structures: external auditory canal, tympanic membrane, ossicular chain, facial nerve, cochlear vestibular membranous labyrinth, cochlear vestibular bony labyrinth, internal auditory canal, and round window membrane. Reference annotations served as the ‘ground truth’ for accuracy scoring and were established by a senior otologic specialist with subspecialty expertise in temporal bone imaging, following the temporal bone CT–based anatomical reference framework previously defined and validated in our group’s prior work [17]. A standardized 6-point scoring system (0–5) was employed to quantify annotation accuracy: when the subject’s annotation overlapped more than 90% with the actual structure, score was 5 points; when overlap reached 80% with only structural omissions and no misidentification, score was 4 points; when overlap reached 60% with only structural omissions and no misidentification, or 80% overlap with structural misidentification, score was 3 points; when overlap reached 30% with only structural omissions and no misidentification, or 60% overlap with structural misidentification, score was 2 points; when overlap was below 30% with only structural omissions and no misidentification, or 30% overlap with structural misidentification, score was 1 point; when overlap was below 30% with structural misidentification, score was 0 points. For each case, a composite case-level accuracy score (maximum 40 points) was calculated as the sum of the eight individual anatomical structure scores (0–5 points each). These composite scores were used for overall learning-curve analyses and phase-based comparisons.

For structure-specific learning analysis, we established a threshold-based milestone: “good level” (two consecutive cases with accuracy ≥ 4.2 points). This criterion‌ enabled quantitative comparison of learning trajectories across different anatomical structures.

Completion time assessment

Task completion time was measured by recording the time required to complete each annotation task.

Learning experience and adverse health effects assessment

Following completion of all annotation tasks, participants evaluated their learning experience using a questionnaire adapted from Moro et al. [18] addressing four key dimensions: learning process comfort, potential to replace traditional teaching, ease of anatomical understanding, and learning method preference. Responses were recorded on a five-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree). The English version of the questionnaire is provided as Supplementary File 1.

Additionally, participants reported any adverse effects experienced during the learning process, with particular attention to symptoms commonly associated with mixed reality headset usage (eye fatigue, visual blurring, focusing difficulties, and spatial disorientation). This systematic documentation of side effects provided important context for evaluating the practical implementability of MR technology in educational settings.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.1), with statistical significance set at α = 0.05, and results expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). A mixed-design analysis of variance (mixed-design ANOVA) was used to analyze between-group differences in annotation accuracy and task completion time, with Group (MR vs. Traditional) as the between-subjects factor and Phase as the within-subjects factor. Greenhouse-Geisser correction was applied for violations of sphericity assumption. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons between groups at each phase were conducted using contrasts from the ANOVA model, with Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Effect sizes for main ANOVA effects are reported as partial eta-squared (η²ₚ). Effect sizes for the mixed-design ANOVA Group effects are interpreted using Cohen’s benchmarks as applied to partial eta-squared in mixed designs (small ≈ 0.01, medium ≈ 0.06, large ≥ 0.14) [19].

Given the limited sample size, all key mixed-design ANOVA results were verified using non-parametric tests, including the Mann-Whitney U test for between-group comparisons within phases and the Friedman test for within-group comparisons across phases. Mann-Whitney U tests were also conducted to compare overall group performance on cumulative task completion time (sum of all 20 cases per participant) and mean annotation accuracy (average across 20 cases per participant). For non-parametric Mann-Whitney U comparisons, rank-biserial correlation (r) is additionally reported as the effect size measure.

Learning progression was visualized using locally weighted scatterplot smoothing (LOWESS) regression, implemented with the ggplot2 package in R.

Results

Participant characteristics

This study enrolled 8 participants (8 medico-engineering collaborative students), with a mean age of 21.3 ± 1.2 years, GPA range 3.2–4.4 (mean 3.7 ± 0.4), and balanced gender distribution (50% male, 50% female). Clinical medicine and engineering students did not significantly differ in age or GPA. The experimental group (MR group) and control group (traditional group) demonstrated comparable baseline characteristics, with similarity in age, GPA, and gender distribution (Table 1). Across all participants, the cumulative time required for completion of 20 ear structure annotation cases was 34.58 ± 3.77 h, with a mean accuracy score of 35.39 ± 3.50 points across all cases.

Dynamic characteristics of learning curves

Learning curve assessment based on learning plateau

Participants in the mixed reality group reached the learning plateau earlier than those in the traditional group (6.0 ± 3.5 vs. 8.0 ± 4.9 cases, respectively). Although these between-group differences did not achieve conventional statistical significance due to sample size limitations, the 25% improvement in training efficiency represents a small-to-medium effect size with meaningful implications for educational practice as shown in Fig. 2.

Dynamic changes in annotation accuracy

Mixed-design ANOVA of annotation accuracy showed a main effect of group (F(1,6) = 9.17, p = 0.023, η²ₚ = 0.605, indicating a large effect), and phase (F(2,12) = 18.21, p < 0.001, η²ₚ = 0.752). The Group × Phase interaction approached significance (F(2,12) = 3.19, p = 0.078, η²ₚ = 0.347), indicating a large effect size.

Bonferroni-corrected post-hoc comparisons showed that the mixed reality group had higher accuracy than the traditional group in both phase 1 (p = 0.020) and phase 2 (p = 0.002) (Fig. 3).

Dynamic changes in task completion time

For completion time, the mixed-design ANOVA revealed main effects of group (F(1,6) = 41.83, p < 0.001, η²ₚ = 0.875) and phase (F(1.27,7.60) = 30.71, p < 0.001, η²ₚ = 0.837), with no significant Group × Phase interaction (F(1.27,7.60) = 0.60, p = 0.500, η²ₚ = 0.091).

Overall group comparisons

The MR group required substantially less cumulative time to complete all 20 cases than the traditional group, with a mean between-group difference of 398.5 min (MR: 1875.2 ± 66.5 min; Traditional: 2273.8 ± 97.0 min; mean between-group difference = 398.5 min). Mann-Whitney U test showed complete rank separation (U = 0, r = 1.00, p = 0.029).

The MR group demonstrated higher mean annotation accuracy per case than the traditional group, with a mean between-group difference of 3.12 points (MR: 36.95 ± 1.22 points; Traditional: 33.83 ± 1.63 points; mean between-group difference = 3.12 points). Mann-Whitney U test revealed a large effect (U = 1, r = 0.875, p = 0.057).

Adverse health effects and learning experience

During the process of learning ear anatomy models using HoloLens, subjects reported adverse health effects mainly manifesting as eye-related symptoms. At the same time, MR group participants showed higher recognition of material effectiveness in anatomical understanding (e.g. “understanding anatomy became very convenient”), with overall positive trends in learning experience evaluation (Table 3).

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive evaluation of the mixed reality platform’s efficacy in ear anatomy education, i.e. OTOMR. Through a multidimensional assessment framework, we demonstrate that MR technology significantly enhances the learning process compared to traditional methods. Our findings indicate that MR accelerates the learning curve and improves performance in CT-based ear anatomy annotation, as reflected by earlier learning plateau attainment, higher annotation accuracy, and shorter task completion time, while highlighting implementation considerations related to user comfort and visual fatigue.

Systematic assessment of learning effects

Acceleration of learning efficiency

The MR cohort reached the learning plateau after 6.0 cases versus 8.0 cases for the traditional group, despite comparable baseline knowledge levels. This 25% reduction directly informs educational resource allocation [20]. This task-level acceleration is consistent with the mixed-design ANOVA, which demonstrated large-magnitude between-group advantages for the MR group in both annotation accuracy (η²ₚ = 0.605) and task completion time (η²ₚ = 0.875), both effect sizes large by Cohen’s benchmarks, with non-parametric verification yielding directionally consistent results. Together, these results indicate a robust within-task advantage of MR-assisted learning on the CT-based annotation task, the implications of which for broader educational resource allocation merit confirmation in larger cohorts.

The advantage extended to operational efficiency. MR learners saved time across all phases, with the largest gains during mastery-stability (23.1 min per case), suggesting that the spatial understanding facilitated by MR technology enables more efficient information processing and decision-making. This has direct implications for educational practice, as the time savings could be reallocated to other essential competencies within constrained curricula [2].

Notably, the closer alignment between MR training and CT-based evaluation should not be viewed solely as a methodological limitation but also as a reflection of the educational mechanism under investigation. The fundamental challenge in CT-based ear anatomy training is the cognitive translation from two-dimensional learning materials to three-dimensional spatial understanding. Traditional learners must perform this dimensional transformation during evaluation, whereas MR learners have already constructed three-dimensional spatial representations during the training phase. Importantly, the learning and evaluation platforms were entirely separate software systems with OTOMR on HoloLens 2 versus 3D Slicer on a desktop workstation. They differ in interface, interaction modality, and visual presentation, suggesting that software familiarity contributes minimally to the observed differences. The MR group’s superior performance from the earliest cases (Figs. 3 and 4) is consistent with genuine spatial learning transfer rather than gradual adaptation to a familiar format.

Differential impact across anatomical complexity

MR teaching’s benefits were structure-dependent and phase-specific. For complex subunits like the ossicular chain and facial nerve, which require recognition of intricate three-dimensional spatial relationships, MR learners achieved good level 3 cases earlier than traditional learners (Table 2), aligning with neurosurgical training studies showing that 3D manipulation enhances comprehension of challenging anatomical configurations [21]. The facial nerve, in particular, follows a characteristic but tortuous trajectory through the temporal bone, which appears especially well suited to MR-based visualization. Moderate advantages were observed for structures requiring mental reconstruction of curved three-dimensional channels, such as the cochlear vestibular bony labyrinth and internal auditory canal. The cochlear vestibular bony labyrinth presents the additional challenge of defining boundaries with adjacent bony structures on CT, which likely contributes to residual inter-individual variability in both groups. In contrast, structures with simpler geometry showed smaller between-group differences: the external auditory canal, round window membrane, and cochlear vestibular membranous labyrinth each demonstrated 1-case advantages, while the tympanic membrane, which is a relatively planar structure, showed no between-group difference. This structure-specific gradient suggests that MR-based learning confers the greatest pedagogical benefit where traditional two-dimensional resources struggle to convey spatial complexity, while offering diminishing returns for structures adequately represented in conventional formats [13].

These findings advocate for a hybrid curriculum that deploys 2D methods for basics, then integrates MR to cultivate high-fidelity 3D mental models of challenging substructures [3]. Based on the three-phase learning pattern [22], we propose a set of teaching plan optimization suggestions: fully utilize 3D visualization to establish spatial cognition in the foundation-building period, optimize learning strategies through immediate feedback in the skill breakthrough period, and focus on skill deepening and clinical application in the mastery-stability period [23].

Advantages and limitations of MR teaching

Current mixed reality applications in otology teaching are mainly based on the HoloLens platform, a choice with clear technical rationality [24]. HoloLens’s spatial positioning accuracy, stereoscopic presentation capabilities, and natural interaction characteristics meet the special needs of ear fine anatomy learning [20], while effectively addressing specimen shortages and reducing long-term teaching costs through digital content reuse. Using a closed-set forced-choice paradigm, Wu et al. showed that HoloLens 2 with 3D Slicer enabled learners to match traditional video course performance in CT structure identification after just 30 min, while improving interest and confidence [15, 25]. The OTOMR system shifts focus towards customizable content and streamlined clinical data import. It offers greater flexibility for diverse case integration to bridge the gap between education and clinical application, but introduces challenges in technical implementation and ensuring consistent educational quality. Despite user adaptability challenges (50% eye fatigue, 25% blurred vision), consistent with previous studies [26, 27], these symptoms were mild and did not significantly impact learning outcomes. As the underlying head-mounted display platform continues to evolve, OTOMR shows potential as a specialized adjunct for otology teaching; dedicated cost-effectiveness and implementation evaluation will be required to confirm its value in addressing teaching resource bottlenecks.

Study limitations and future plans

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the small sample size (n = 4 per group) substantially limits statistical power and must be considered when interpreting all findings; results should be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Additionally, the inclusion of both clinical medicine and engineering students introduces heterogeneity in prior anatomical knowledge, imaging familiarity, and learning strategies. Although randomization ensured balanced group composition and the two academic subgroups were themselves comparable at baseline, within-group individual variability remains considerable given the small within-stratum sample sizes. This limits the internal validity of between-group comparisons and restricts generalisability to homogeneous learner populations. Furthermore, the large partial eta-squared values observed (η²p = 0.605–0.875) should be interpreted with caution, as this measure tends to be positively biased in small-sample designs and may overestimate the true population effect.

Second, a key methodological consideration is the differential proximity between training modality and evaluation format. The MR group trained with interactive three-dimensional holographic models sharing spatial characteristics with CT-based evaluation tasks, while the traditional group used static two-dimensional materials. This asymmetry may have conferred a near-transfer advantage on the MR group beyond purely anatomical learning gains, and the two contributions cannot be fully disentangled within the current design.

Furthermore, although the learning sessions were conducted under direct supervision, the 20-case annotation tasks took place during pre-arranged self-study time slots rather than under formal examination conditions. Compliance with the instruction to work independently and without external resources was therefore based on participant self-report, and inter-individual variability in adherence to these instructions during the annotation phase cannot be fully excluded as a contributor to the observed performance differences.

Finally, this study mainly focused on anatomical structure annotation as a specific task; the transfer effects of mixed reality technology to other clinical skills (such as surgical operations) require further exploration. It is also necessary to investigate whether mild visual fatigue symptoms accumulate with continuous semester-long use, particularly examining the impact on students with pre-existing vision impairments or binocular vision discrepancies.

Conclusions

In this preliminary study, MR-assisted learning shortened the learning curve on a CT-based temporal bone annotation task compared with textbook-based learning, with earlier attainment of the learning plateau, higher annotation accuracy, and shorter task completion times. The greatest benefits were observed for spatially complex structures such as the ossicular chain and facial nerve. These advantages have direct educational implications: compressed training timelines may allow curricular restructuring, broader access for institutions with limited cadaveric resources, and scalable cross-disciplinary programs. Although some users reported mild adverse effects, learning experience scores favored MR for anatomical comprehension. These findings support MR as a promising adjunct in CT-based ear anatomy training and provide a rationale for adequately powered studies to evaluate whether the observed task-level advantages extend to other learning outcomes, longer-term retention, and clinical performance.
