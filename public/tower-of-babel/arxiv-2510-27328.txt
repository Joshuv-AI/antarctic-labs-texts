A Unified Representation Underlying the Judgment of Large Language Models
Yi-Long Lu, Jiajun Song, Wei Wang

Abstract
A central architectural question for both biological and artificial intelligence is whether judgment relies on specialized modules or a unified, domain-general resource. While the discovery of decodable neural representations for distinct concepts in Large Language Models (LLMs) has suggested a modular architecture, whether these representations are truly independent systems remains an open question. Here we provide evidence for a convergent architecture for evaluative judgment. Across a range of LLMs, we find that diverse evaluative judgments are computed along a dominant dimension, which we term the Valence-Assent Axis (VAA). This axis jointly encodes subjective valence (“what is good”) and the model’s assent to factual claims (“what is true”). Through direct interventions, we demonstrate this axis drives a critical mechanism, which is identified as the subordination of reasoning: the VAA functions as a control signal that steers the generative process to construct a rationale consistent with its evaluative state, even at the cost of factual accuracy. Our discovery offers a mechanistic account for response bias and hallucination, revealing how an architecture that promotes coherent judgment can systematically undermine faithful reasoning.



keywords
large language models, representation engineering, valence-assent axis, evaluative judgment, reasoning hallucination

Introduction

The hallmark of human intelligence lies in its ability to reduce ambiguous, high-dimensional information to tractable, task-relevant judgments. For example, a juror must compress days of conflicting testimony into a single verdict: guilty or not guilty. This process is not a mere tally of evidence but a structured integration into an internal summary
(
marewski2010good
;
sims2018efficient
;
kahneman2011thinking
;
barlow1961possible
;
simon1990invariants
)
. To reach a conclusion, an agent forms an internal representation that filters noise and weighs evidence. Computationally, this information integration is often modeled as projection or accumulation onto a dominant evaluative axis, from which an internal stance and decision emerge
(
polania2019efficient
;
ratcliff2008diffusion
;
bhatia2025computational
)
. Uncovering the algorithmic and representational principles that support this information compression remains a central goal in the study of both biological and artificial intelligence.

How intelligence achieves this information compression is an architectural choice. One possibility is a modular design, composed of domain-specific systems
(
Cosmides2013
;
fodor1983modularity
)
, a mental toolkit often likened to a Swiss army knife.
Cognitive neuroscience provides influential examples of regional functional specialization
(
kanwisher1997fusiform
;
kanwisher2010functional
)
.
An alternative is a domain-general architecture, where diverse judgments are mapped onto a unified internal scale. This perspective is exemplified by the Common Currency Theory, which proposes that the subjective value of disparate rewards is converted into a common neural code that enables direct comparison
(
padoa2006neurons
;
rangel2008framework
;
levy2012root
)
.

This contrast between modular specialization and common currency defines two possible organizational principles for intelligence. Modern
LLM
, now exhibit sophisticated judgmental capabilities, yet the computational mechanisms underlying this ability remain opaque. Recent work in mechanistic interpretability has begun to map the internal representations of LLMs, often revealing what appears to be a modular structure. Recent interpretability research has identified distinct, decodable representations for specific features like sentiment
(
hollinsworth2024
)
, truthfulness
(
burns2024discoveringlatentknowledgelanguage
;
marks2024geometrytruthemergentlinear
)
, risk preference
(
zhu2025steeringriskpreferenceslarge
)
, spatial location
(
gurnee2024languagemodelsrepresentspace
)
, and even concrete entities like the “Golden Gate Bridge”
(
templeton2024scaling
)
.
While the ability to decode such signals can be interpreted as evidence for a modular, “Swiss army knife” architecture, decodability alone does not prove the existence of independent functional modules. A deeper question remains: are these distinct representations truly independent modules, or are they merely different projections of a single, domain-general representation for evaluative judgments?

In this study, we show that LLMs converge on a domain-general architecture for evaluative judgments (Fig.
1
).
We first identify a dominant, convergent dimension using principal component analysis on the model’s (Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct,
qwen25technicalreport
) activation space during value-related judgments. We confirm its general-purpose nature by showing that steering activations along this axis causally controls judgments in a distinct sentiment analysis task. Further analysis reveals its unifying function: this same axis jointly encodes subjective preference (valence, or “what is good”) and factual endorsement (assent, or “what is true”). We therefore term this the
Valence–Assent Axis
(VAA).

Probing the deeper algorithmic consequences of this unified representation, we find that the VAA drives a critical mechanism that we identify as the
subordination of reasoning
(Fig.
1
C). Through targeted steering interventions, we demonstrate that the VAA functions as a powerful control signal that steers the generative process to construct a rationale consistent with its evaluative state, even at the cost of factual accuracy. Finally, we establish that this mechanism is not an artifact of a single model but a general feature across LLMs that vary in architecture and scale. Together, these findings offer a mechanistic account for response bias and hallucination, revealing how an architecture that promotes coherent judgment can systematically undermine faithful reasoning.

2.1
A Common Axis of Value Judgment Generalizes Across Domains

To test whether the model’s judgment relies on domain-specific or domain-general representations, we first examined its internal activations in a
Value Judgment
task. The task presented 175 value statements in two formats: a binary choice between support and oppose, and a continuous rating on a 0–9 scale. The stimuli were sampled to ensure an approximate balance between supportive and opposing judgments.

Our investigation began by applying Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to the model’s hidden states across all 48 layers in both binary and continuous tasks. As shown in (Fig.
2
a), the resulting first principal component (PC1) increasingly correlates with the model’s final decision as layer depth increases, reaching a stable, high correlation in the later layers. We therefore operationally defined the
Judgment Axis
for each layer as its PC1, derived from the balanced task data.

The Judgment Axis showed strong cross-task consistency (binary vs. continuous), particularly within the middle layers. The representational similarity between the axes derived from the two formats peaked at Layer 28 (Pearson’s
r=0.761
; Fig.
2
a), indicating that this is where the model forms a stable, format-independent representation of value judgment.
At the optimal layer (Layer 28), the axis alone explained 26.3% of the activation variance (Fig.
2
c) and strongly predicted the model’s final choice (Fig.
2
b, Pearson’s
r=0.892
), whereas no other components were comparably correlated (Fig.
2
c, all
|r|<0.3
).

Interventions along this axis, derived solely from value statements in binary judgments, produced systematic changes in a distinct
Sentiment Analysis
task, a domain focused on affective tone rather than deep value reasoning. In this task, the model evaluated the sentiment of news headlines using both binary and continuous response formats. In both cases, steering along the axis reliably biased model responses in the direction of the intervention (Fig.
2
d; linear mixed regression, binary:
b=0.734
,
z=36.28
, 95% CI
[0.694,0.773]
,
p<0.001
; continuous:
b=0.839
,
z=63.36
, 95% CI
[0.813,0.865]
,
p<0.001
). A control experiment confirmed the axis’s efficacy within its original Value Judgment domain (Fig.
2
e; linear mixed regression of continuous task,
b=0.839
,
z=47.65
, 95% CI
[0.804,0.874]
,
p<0.001
). These results demonstrate that a single representational axis, learned from value judgments, can generalize to sentiment evaluation, arguing against a modular account and supporting the existence of a shared representation for judgment.

Having shown that the Judgment Axis generalizes across subjective domains (from value judgments to sentiment analysis), we next asked a more critical question: does this same axis bridge subjective preference with objective fact? Evaluative judgment is commonly understood to navigate two distinct domains: subjective valence (assessing what is “good” or preferable) and objective fact (verifying what is “true”). We therefore designed a set of critical tests to determine whether this axis, derived solely from value judgments, also underlies model’s factual evaluation. A positive finding would reveal a strong convergence, implying that the model represents “good” and “true” on a shared dimension.

We first probed the axis’s role in subjective valuation using a
Subjective Preference
task.
Here, the model chose between pairs of words with opposing valence (e.g., “positive” vs “negative”) presented in a semantically neutral context. Intervention along the Judgment Axis exerted strong control over these valenced choices but had no effect on neutral word pairs (Fig.
3
a). A linear mixed-effects model confirmed a significant effect for valenced words (
b=0.795
,
t(200)=64.35
, 95% CI
[0.771,0.820]
,
p<0.001
), while the slope for neutral words was not significant (
b=0.03
,
t(200)=1.29
, 95% CI
[-0.017,0.080]
,
p=0.198
). Representational analysis further revealed that the Judgment Axis was highly aligned with an independently derived Valence Axis, defined from model judgments of different words’ sentiment (Fig.
3
b). The high correlation (Pearson’s
r=0.964
,
p<0.001
) confirms that the Judgment Axis captures a core dimension of semantic value.

Next, to determine if this same value-centric axis generalizes to objective verification, we tested it in a
Single-Letter Order
task, where the model judged simple alphabetical facts such as “‘a’comes before ‘b’.”
We defined an Objective Truth Axis from hidden state activations. The Objective Truth Axis was almost perfectly aligned with the Judgment Axis derived from the Value Judgment task (Fig.
3
c; Pearson’s
r=0.995
,
p<0.001
). This high alignment was further replicated in a distinct task of math expression judgment (e.g., “1+2=4”; see Supplementary Section for details), demonstrating robustness across cognitive domains.

Together, these findings establish a clear convergence of subjective valuation and objective verification onto a shared functional dimension. While this axis is not identical to the specialized vectors derived from individual tasks, their near-perfect alignment points to a shared computational core. To reflect this dual function of encoding both semantic value (Valence) and factual agreement (Assent), we term this fundamental structure the Valence-Assent Axis (VAA).

Having established that the VAA unifies subjective valence and factual endorsement, we next examined its algorithmic consequences for the reasoning process. We sought to determine whether this axis merely biases the final answer or directly influences the underlying step-by-step justification. We tested this in two objective domains: a procedural task (
Alphabetical Order
) and a knowledge-based task (
Factual Judgment
). Because these tasks have verifiable ground-truth answers, we could precisely quantify the intervention’s alignment with the ground-truth. We define this metric as
Alignment Pressure
, where positive values steer the VAA toward the true answer, and negative values create a conflict by steering it toward the false one. To isolate the effect on reasoning, we employed a
think-then-answer
paradigm, prompting the model to externalize its justification before producing a final answer. Using this approach, we found clear evidence for the subordination of reasoning: the VAA systematically directs the reasoning process, guiding the model to construct justifications consistent with the induced directional bias.

In the procedural task, this Alignment Pressure exerted strong control over the model’s final answer (Fig.
4
a; logistic regression,
b=6.77
,
z=9.59
, 95% CI
[5.52,8.30]
,
p<0.001
). A similar effect was observed in the
answer-then-think
condition (
b=4.60
,
z=11.56
, 95% CI
[3.88,5.44]
,
p<0.001
). Negative Alignment Pressure, which pushed the model against the correct answer, triggered a collapse in reasoning integrity. This collapse predominantly manifested as Coherent Hallucinations—logically sound arguments built on a false premise (Fig.
4
b,c). For instance, to justify the incorrect claim that “ice” precedes “house”, the model would falsely reason that “‘i’ comes before ‘h’ in the alphabet,” before reaching the final answer.
A Bayesian multinomial mixed-effects model confirmed this shift: as Alignment Pressure increased, the odds of producing flawed reasoning dropped sharply relative to Sound Reasoning (e.g., Coherent Hallucinations: log-odds
b=-2.37
, 95% CI
[-2.76,-2.00]
).

This subordination mechanism generalized to the factual domain. In the Factual Judgment task, VAA intervention again dictated the model’s final “yes/no” decision (Fig.
4
d;
b=6.01
,
z=7.20
, 95% CI
[4.58,7.89]
,
p<0.001
), and this control was again underpinned by a collapse in reasoning integrity. VAA pressure systematically converted Sound Reasoning into structured failures, most notably Coherent Hallucinations (log-odds
b=-2.97
, 95% CI
[-3.77,-2.28]
) and Contradictory Reasoning, where the reasoning content is factually correct but contradicts the model’s final answer (log-odds
b=-2.72
, 95% CI
[-3.48,-2.05]
) (Fig.
4
e,f). Incoherent Hallucinations—complete reasoning breakdowns—also increased (see Supplementary Table S
6
).

Together, these results demonstrate that the VAA systematically subordinates reasoning across domains. It functions as a control mechanism that establishes a strong judgmental orientation, guiding the model to construct goal-consistent explanations, even when doing so undermines factual accuracy or logical coherence.

Our findings so far indicate that the VAA subordinates reasoning in objective tasks. The question remains whether this mechanism also extends to open-ended, subjective argumentation. To test this possibility, we designed the
Stance-Taking
task to go beyond forced-choice formats. Here, the model was asked to “critically evaluate” a controversial statement and generate a structured, open-ended response that included both reasoning and a one-sentence conclusion. This setup tests whether the VAA can shape not just a final choice, but the entire process of generating a nuanced argument.

The results show that the VAA serves as a consistent control vector for goal-directed argumentation. For a single controversial prompt, varying the Intervention Coefficient (
\alpha
) induced the model to adopt distinct stances, ranging from skepticism to endorsement (Fig.
5
a).
In each case, the model selectively retrieved and organized information to form coherent arguments aligned with the VAA, influencing both the final answer and the reasoning process.

To quantify this control, we analyzed the expressed stance in both the reasoning text and the final answer. The Intervention Coefficient systematically modulated both measures, with the stance shifting progressively from opposition to support as
\alpha
increased (Fig.
5
b; linear mixed-effects model,
b=0.467
,
z=12.23
, 95% CI
[0.392,0.541]
for reasoning;
b=0.785
,
z=25.79
, 95% CI
[0.725,0.844]
for answer, both
p<0.001
). This demonstrates that the VAA does not merely bias a final response, but continuously shapes the entire argumentative trajectory, even in a complex, open-ended generative setting.

Importantly, the model was able to construct these biased arguments while maintaining a high level of argumentative quality. The proportion of Sound Reasoning remained robust across a wide range of interventions. Even at maximum intervention strength, a considerable fraction of the model’s arguments continued to be classified as Sound Reasoning (e.g., 66.7% at
\alpha=-1
and 36.7% at
\alpha=1
). Nonetheless, systematic degradation was evident: as the model was forced to adopt increasingly extreme stances in either direction, the odds of producing Sound Reasoning declined significantly (Fig.
5
c; logistic mixed-effects model,
b=-0.988
,
z=-6.46
, 95% CI
[-1.287,-0.688]
,
p<0.001
). This demonstrates that the model can selectively retrieve factual support to defend a given stance. However, as the stance becomes more extreme, the integrity of the supporting evidence progressively declines.

These findings show that the VAA provides a direct means of controlling an
LLM
’s argumentative output. As the VAA changes, the model selectively organizes reasoning and gathers evidence consistent with the targeted stance, producing arguments that closely follow the VAA’s directive. This directed construction preserves rhetorical coherence but comes at a cost: as the stance becomes more extreme, the quality and reliability of supporting evidence are increasingly compromised.

Finally, we examined the VAA’s generality across eight instruction-tuned LLMs of varying architectures and scales. Our results confirmed that the VAA and its associated effects appear consistently across all tested models.

We first confirmed that every model contains a stable mid-layer axis supporting judgment. Layer-wise representational similarity between binary and continuous Value Judgment tasks revealed a consistent band of high similarity (Fig.
6
a). The optimal layer depth correlated with total layer count (Pearson’s
r=0.967
,
p<0.001
), with relative layer depth ranging from 42% to 74% (mean = 59%). At these layers, the representational similarity between binary and continuous tasks exceeded 0.706 across all models, indicating a robust structural foundation for the VAA.

Next, we tested whether this axis exerts causal influence beyond its native domain. Across all models, VAA intervention significantly controlled outcomes in both Value Judgment and Sentiment Analysis tasks (Fig.
6
b,
p<0.001
for all
LLM
, see Supplementary Table S
2
for full results).
Additional tests on Single-Letter Order and Subjective Preference tasks yielded similar effects. Notably, smaller models (e.g., Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct) exhibited strong alignment between the VAA and their own responses but weaker correspondence with ground-truth labels, suggesting that the VAA primarily reflects models’ internal confidence rather than external correctness (see Supplementary Table S
4
for full results).

Finally, we examined whether reasoning subordination is a general phenomenon. In all models, stronger VAA pressure increased the likelihood of Coherent Hallucinations (Fig.
6
c;
p<0.001
for all). This indicates that models systematically produce flawed reasoning to align with the VAA’s goal. Importantly, this effect did not diminish with model scale, suggesting that the influence of the VAA persists even in larger models. Replications in Alphabetical Order and Stance-Taking tasks confirmed that the VAA consistently shapes both reasoning and conclusions (see Supplementary Information for details).

Together, these results indicate that the VAA is a convergent solution for judgment in tested
LLM
. The VAA’s effects spans different task domains and model scales, revealing a shared mechanism that supports logically coherent but factually inaccurate reasoning.

3
Discussion

Our findings suggest that
LLM
implement a shared representation in judgment tasks. We provide evidence that diverse evaluative tasks, from subjective preferences to objective factual verification, converge onto a dominant representational axis—the VAA. This domain-general representation implies an important algorithmic property: the subordination of reasoning. We find that the VAA establishes a directional bias that guides reasoning, compelling the generative process to construct a rationale that remains consistent with this orientation. This mechanism explains how
LLM
generate coherent, fluent justifications even when factual accuracy is compromised.

The discovery of a domain-general axis for judgments calls into question the implicit “Swiss army knife” view suggested by prior work, which has successfully identified distinct representations for specific concepts like truthfulness, sentiment and risk preference
(
burns2024discoveringlatentknowledgelanguage
;
zhu2025steeringriskpreferenceslarge
;
hollinsworth2024
)
.
Our results suggest that these task-specific representations may not constitute distinct modules but instead share a common higher-order evaluative dimension.
For example, a “truthfulness” axis may correspond to a projection of the VAA onto the subspace of factual verification.
Mapping the geometric relationships among these vectors could help to create a comprehensive overview of how representations are organized within
LLM
.

The effects of the VAA emerge in evaluative settings, suggesting that it functions as a meta-cognitive level control signal for assent, rather than a general regulator of factual answering. It appears to determine whether a model will endorse or reject a proposition, with reasoning serving to justify this decision.
This view predicts that the VAA should have little influence in purely non-evaluative contexts, such as direct numerical computation (e.g.,
3+8=?
), where no stance is explicitly required. It also explains the observed tension when a VAA-directed stance conflicts with high-confidence, retrieved knowledge. This suggests a functional interplay between a rapid, stance-driven process and a more deliberate, knowledge-retrieval process, analogous to human cognitive dissonance; when forced by the VAA, the model generates flawed justifications (i.e., Coherent Hallucinations) to reduce the inconsistency between its internal knowledge and its evaluative stance.

This projection of diverse inputs onto a common evaluative axis resembles functional principles observed in human decision-making, where diverse values are often integrated into a shared scale for choice
(
padoa2006neurons
;
levy2012root
)
. The VAA may play a functionally analogous role, providing a unified scale that aligns subjective preferences with the model’s assessment of factual claims. Furthermore, the VAA-driven subordination of reasoning exhibits patterns similar to human biases such as motivated reasoning and confirmation bias, in which evidence tends to be accumulated toward a preferred conclusion
(
klayman1995varieties
;
kunda1990case
;
ratcliff2008diffusion
)
.
This deep entanglement of the model’s representation of “what is good” (Valence) and “what is true” (Assent) is reminiscent of the Socratic proposition that virtue is knowledge.
The origins of this architecture remain a key open question. It likely emerges during pre-training, as the model learns statistical regularities that link truth, value, and agreement in human language. Alignment tuning (e.g., RLHF) may further refine this latent axis to promote coherent and socially consistent responses. Beyond its theoretical significance, this geometric structure has practical implications. Recent work shows that model behavior can be systematically altered by adding steering vectors to internal activations
(
turner2024
)
. Our findings suggest that these interventions are effective because they exploit the model’s intrinsic representational axis, relying on this emergent internal structure rather than on externally imposed mechanisms.

Our analysis identifies this shared evaluative axis, but it also has some limitations. The VAA likely represents the dominant linear projection of a more complex, potentially non-linear structure. Indeed, our preliminary analysis of the second principal component (PC2) suggests it encodes evaluative intensity (Fig.
2
b). This points toward a more complex representational structure, perhaps encoding second-order evaluations like confidence, similar to value-related confidence signals found in the human ventromedial prefrontal cortex
(
Lebreton2015AutomaticIO
)
.
Furthermore, it remains to be tested whether the VAA also influences other evaluative domains, such as aesthetic judgments (“what is beautiful”), or if it exerts comparable control in extended reasoning tasks, such as multi-step logic or mathematics, in which process integrity is more tightly constrained.

Finally, our study focuses on dense, instruction-tuned models. It is essential to examine how the VAA emerges in base models and evolves through alignment in order to trace its developmental trajectory. Beyond providing a mechanistic understanding, this architecture offers a new perspective on persistent alignment challenges such as sycophancy
(
sharma2025understandingsycophancylanguagemodels
)
. Thus, building truthful systems may require not only regulating factual accuracy
(
casper2023open
)
, but also this core assent mechanism. A promising direction is to use representational editing to decouple the VAA from the model’s knowledge base, thereby encouraging models that can reason coherently while maintaining epistemic independence.

4
Methods

4.1
Models

This study examined a diverse set of instruction-tuned
LLM
to evaluate the generalizability of our findings. The primary model for in-depth analysis and main figure presentation was Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct. To assess the effects of scale and architecture, we further evaluated Qwen2.5-72B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct, Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, Mistral-7B-Instruct-v0.3, and Gemma-2-9B-Instruct
(
qwen25technicalreport
;
grattafiori2024llama3herdmodels
;
jiang2023mistral7b
;
gemmateam2024gemma2improvingopen
)
. All models are dense, text-only architectures. For all generative tasks requiring a chain-of-thought, the decoding temperature was set to 0.2 to ensure near-deterministic and high-quality outputs.

4.2
Causal Intervention via Representational Steering

Our primary method for probing the model’s internal architecture involved intervening in hidden state representations. Given a Transformer-based language model and a sequence of input tokens length
T
, this intervention is defined by the following formula:

\mathbf{h}^{\ell}\leftarrow\mathbf{h}^{\ell}+aV^{\ell},

(1)

where
\mathbf{h}^{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times d}
is the sequence of
d
-dimensional hidden states of layer index
\ell
. We intervene in the forward process of the language model via a steering vector
V^{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a scalar coefficient
a
.

4.2.1
Axis Definition and Layer Selection

The steering vector
V^{\ell}
was obtained from the
Value Judgment
task as the first principal component of the layer-
\ell
activations at the last token position, which contains the model’s final decision state. The axis was oriented so that activations loading positively onto this component correspond to “support” judgments. As in standard PCA, we assume that the sampled data are reasonably balanced across conditions. This data-driven axis serves as a tractable proxy for the hypothesized latent, intrinsic judgmental axis of the model.

To locate layers that encode a stable representation of value judgment independent of response format, we computed representational similarity between the binary and continuous versions of the Value Judgment task for each layer. This similarity was quantified as the Pearson correlation between the PC1 across the two formats. Layers showing higher similarity were considered to share a common representational basis for judgment, regardless of output format.

The resulting similarity profile revealed a continuous band of layers with elevated alignment. For subsequent intervention analyses, we selected representative layers within this band for comparison, as these layers are hypothesized to host stable, abstract representations essential for the task. Preliminary experiments also indicated that these layers tend to produce stronger steering effects. The steering effect of a layer was defined as the average change in the model’s expected responses to the test tasks when the steering vector
V^{\ell}
was applied across the feasible range of the steering coefficient. The selected layers and their alignment values are reported in Supplementary Table
1
.

4.2.2
Intervention Protocol

For each layer
\ell
, we first identified an effective steering range
\mathcal{I}^{\ell}=[a_{\min},a_{\max}]
, where the coefficient
a
spans from a negative to a positive value around zero. This range was defined as the largest continuous interval within which the model’s mean expected response, defined as the average predicted support level across all questions, changed strictly monotonically in both the binary (AB) and continuous (0–9) Value Judgment tasks.

Within this validated range, all interventions used a set of 11 discrete
a
values. To account for asymmetry in the range (
|a_{\min}|\neq a_{\max}
), the set was constructed by sampling the negative and positive sub-intervals independently:

\mathcal{A}=\left\{\tfrac{i}{5}\,a_{\min}\;\middle|\;i\in\{1,\dots,5\}\right\}\;\cup\;\left\{\tfrac{j}{5}\,a_{\max}\;\middle|\;j\in\{0,\dots,5\}\right\}.

(2)

For all subsequent analyses and visualizations, these 11 strengths were linearly mapped onto a normalized intervention coefficient
\alpha\in[-1,1]
to allow for standardized comparison across models and layers.

For objective reasoning tasks with ground truth (i.e.,
Alphabetical Order
and
Factual Judgment
), a unified metric,
Alignment Pressure
(
\alpha_{\text{aligned}}
) was used to represent the intervention’s direction and magnitude relative to the correct answer:

\alpha_{\text{aligned}}=\alpha D_{\text{truth}},

(3)

where
D_{\text{truth}}
is 1 for true statements and
-1
for false statements. Thus, positive
\alpha_{\text{aligned}}
values indicate steering aligned with the truth, while negative values indicate conflict.

4.3
Experimental Paradigms and Stimuli

We used a series of experimental paradigms to systematically characterize the structure of judgment in
LLM
. The tasks were organized into two main groups: the first group aimed to discover and characterize the primary representational axis, while the second group was designed to probe the core mechanism by which this axis influences the reasoning process.

4.3.1
Tasks for Axis Discovery and Characterization

Value Judgment Task

Stimuli consisted of 175 unique value statements (e.g., “Abortion should be a legal option.”) covering 40 distinct topics, adapted from
(
lu2025systematic
;
mooreAreLargeLanguage2024
)
. Models were prompted to indicate their stance (support vs. oppose) in two response formats: Binary Choice and Continuous Rating. In the binary format, models chose between token “A” (support) and “B” (oppose). In the continuous format, models provided a rating from 0 (complete opposition) to 9 (complete support). For defining the judgment Axis, we used an extraction set of 134 statements sampled from 30 of the 40 topics. These 134 were selected to maintain an approximate balance between “support” and “oppose” judgments, ensuring that the resulting principal component captured a general, topic-independent evaluative dimension. For subsequent steering analyses, all 175 statements were included, as pilot analyses showed that the steering effects were similar across the remaining statements, allowing us to combine them without loss of interpretability.

Sentiment Analysis Task

This task served as the primary testbed for cross-domain generalization. Stimuli consisted of 175 unique news headlines sampled from
(
lu2025systematic
;
rathjeGPTEffectiveTool2024
)
. Its structure precisely mirrored the Value Judgment task. To further control for response tokens, the binary format required a choice between option ‘M’ (positive) and ‘N’ (negative), while the continuous format used a 0 (completely negative) to 9 (completely positive) scale.

Subjective Preference Task

This task used a custom lexicon of 100 word pairs (80 valenced, e.g.,“Correct” vs. “Incorrect”; 20 neutral, e.g., “Banana” vs. “Apple”) to serve two goals.
First, it was used for a causal test where the model chose between words in a pair, with the outcome measured by the logit difference as a function of the steering coefficient
\alpha
. The task was presented in a semantically neutral context (“XX…X, option1 or option2?”), with the option order counterbalanced across trials.
Second, its 80 unique valenced words were used as stimuli to define the canonical Valence Axis. To do this, we prompted the model to classify each word’s valence (“Positive” or “Negative”). The Valence Axis was defined as the first principal component (PC1) of the hidden state activations from this task.

Single-Letter Order Task

The purpose of this task was to identify a standard representational axis for simple, objective truth. Stimuli consisted of 100 questions asserting the alphabetical order of 50 unique pairs of single English letters (e.g., “a” comes before “b”). The stimulus set was balanced for correctness, and the letter pairs were distributed across the alphabet. Models were prompted for a direct, single-word answer (“right” or “wrong”). The primary outcome was the alignment between the representational geometry of this task and that of the Value Judgment task, as detailed in the Statistical Analysis section.

4.3.2
Tasks for Probing the Subordination of Reasoning

This set of tasks required the model to generate a chain-of-thought in a structured JSON format containing “think” and “answer” fields.

Alphabetical Order Task

This task assessed model reasoning on a procedural problem with a verifiable ground truth. Stimuli consisted of 30 pairs of common English words, structured into two levels of difficulty (different vs. same initial letters). In each trial, the model was presented with a statement asserting the alphabetical order of a pair (e.g., “‘name’ comes before ‘none’… Is it right or wrong?”). The correctness of these statements was counterbalanced. Two prompt structures were used: a think-then-answer condition and an answer-then-think condition. A screening prerequisite was established: only models achieving
\geq 80\%
accuracy on the think-then-answer condition at baseline (
\alpha=0
) were included in the steering analysis.

Factual Judgment Task

This task tested the Judgment Axis’s influence on reasoning about factual knowledge. Stimuli consisted of 30 questions with clear “Yes/No” answers, selected from the TruthfulQA
(
lin2021truthfulqa
)
dataset (e.g., “Do more than 20% of Americans have passports?”). Models were prompted using the think-then-answer format to provide a reasoning process before the final Yes/No conclusion. A similar screening criterion (
\geq 60\%
baseline accuracy) was applied to ensure models possessed a basic grasp of the facts being queried.

Stance-Taking Task

This task assessed the Judgment Axis’s influence on constructing arguments for complex, open-ended topics. Stimuli consisted of 30 statements covering debatable social issues, conspiracy theories, and pseudoscience (e.g., “Aliens have visited Earth.”). The prompt instructed the model to “critically evaluate” the statement and provide its reasoning (think) and a final one-sentence conclusion (answer). No accuracy-based screening was applied to this task.

4.4
Quantitative Analysis of Reasoning Chains

We developed a three-dimensional evaluation framework to assess the quality of model reasoning using an LLM-as-a-Judge protocol. Each response was rated for
factual correctness
(FC),
logical consistency
(LC), and
reasoning structure
(RS), capturing whether the reasoning was accurate, coherent, and well organized. The protocol showed high reliability against expert annotations (
N=120
; Cohe’s Kappa = 0.87, 0.83, and 0.62, respectively). The primary metrics of factual correctness and logical consistency were further employed for subsequent analyses of model reasoning pattern classification.

For the stance-taking task, we further measured the model’s expressed position of the final answer sentence (
final stance
, 1–7) and the stance reflected in its reasoning content (
reasoning stance
, 1–7). Both measures correlated strongly with human judgments, confirming the validity of the approach.

Among three available judge models
(
guo2025deepseek
;
liu2024deepseek
;
yang2025qwen3
, DeepSeek R1, DeepSeek V3, and Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct, )
, we selected those yielding the highest agreement with human raters: DeepSeek R1 for the main reasoning evaluation, and Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct for stance-related scoring. Full prompts and validation procedures are described in the Supplementary Information.

4.4.1
Reasoning Patterns Classification

To provide a comprehensive assessment of reasoning quality that considers multiple factors, we developed a detailed taxonomy of reasoning patterns based on their factual correctness (FC) and logical consistency (LC) scores.

Primary Reasoning Patterns

These four categories represent unambiguous states of reasoning quality.

•

Sound Reasoning: The reasoning is both factually correct and logically consistent with the answer (FC=+1, LC=+1).

•

Coherent Hallucination: The reasoning is factually incorrect but logically consistent with the answer (FC=
-1
, LC=+1), representing a sound justification for a false conclusion.

•

Contradictory Reasoning: The reasoning is factually correct but logically contradicts the final answer (FC=+1, LC=
-1
), suggesting the model overrode a correct thought process.

•

Incoherent Hallucination: The reasoning is both factually incorrect and logically inconsistent (FC=
-1
, LC=
-1
), representing a complete breakdown.

Nuanced Reasoning Patterns

These categories capture more subtle patterns often observed in complex tasks.

•

Cherry-picking: Logically consistent reasoning that relies on factually mixed or incomplete evidence (FC=0, LC=+1).

•

Ambiguous Logic: Factually correct reasoning that is only weakly compatible with, but does not logically entail, the final answer (FC=+1, LC=0).

•

Mixed: Reasoning with both factually mixed and logically ambiguous components (FC=0, LC=0).

For objective tasks, the analysis focused on the shift from Sound Reasoning to the four primary, unambiguous patterns. Nuanced patterns, being rare, were grouped into a single Mixed/Other category for visual clarity.

4.5
Statistical Analysis

We quantified the alignment among representational axes and assessed how steering interventions along the VAA influenced the model’s behavior and reasoning.

4.5.1
Quantifying Representational Alignment

To examine whether the Judgment Axis also encodes subjective valence and objective truth, we assessed its alignment with two independently derived axes: a Valence Axis and an Objective Truth Axis. Each comparison axis was defined as the PC1 of hidden state activations from its respective task. Specifically, the Valence Axis was derived from activations elicited by 80 valenced words, and the Objective Truth Axis was derived from activations in the Single-Letter Order task.

We quantified the relationship between these axes using three complementary analyses. Our primary measure involved calculating the Pearson correlation between the projections of a shared set of stimuli activations onto both the Judgment Axis and a comparison axis (e.g., the Valence Axis). Next, we assessed direct axis alignment by correlating the PC1 vectors themselves. Finally, to measure the relative importance of the Judgment Axis in other tasks, we calculated the proportion of variance it explained in the activations of the comparison tasks. Results for the latter two analyses are reported in the Supplementary Information.

4.5.2
Modeling Intervention Effects on Behavioral Outcomes

We employed mixed-effects models to assess the causal impact of our steering intervention, controlled by the intervention coefficient (
\alpha
), on the models’ final outputs.

For continuous and probabilistic judgments (i.e., Value Judgment and Sentiment Analysis), we first computed the expected value of the model’s response for each stimulus at each
\alpha
level. For binary choices, this was the probability of selecting the token of “support” or “positive” choices. For continuous ratings, it was the probability-weighted average of the numerical ratings, normalized to a [0,1] scale. We then fitted a linear mixed-effects model (LMM) to predict this expected response, with
\alpha
as a fixed effect and a random intercept for each stimulus to account for item-specific variance.

For the binary outcomes (e.g., answering “right”), we fitted a logistic regression model, using the coefficient of the
\alpha_{\text{aligned}}
term to measure the efficacy of control.

For the Stance-Taking task, the relationship between
\alpha
and the stance score of the reasoning process and answer was modeled using linear mixed-effects models.

4.5.3
Modeling Intervention Effects on Reasoning Patterns

To quantify how VAA intervention reshapes the reasoning process, we fitted a series of mixed-effects models specific to the hypotheses of each task.

For the objective tasks (Alphabetical Order and Factual Judgment), we fitted a Bayesian multinomial logistic mixed-effects model. This model predicts the probability of a response belonging to each primary reasoning category (e.g., Coherent Hallucination), using Sound Reasoning as the reference. The primary predictor was Alignment Pressure (
\alpha_{\text{aligned}}
), and a random intercept was included for each stimulus. Responses classified as Mixed/Other were excluded from this specific analysis to cleanly test the trade-offs between unambiguous reasoning patterns.

For the subjective Stance-Taking task, we examined the effect of stance extremity on reasoning quality. We fitted a logistic mixed-effects model predicting the binary outcome of whether a response was Sound Reasoning or not. The primary predictor was the absolute intervention magnitude
|\alpha|
, which represents the extremity of the induced stance.

For the cross-model analysis, we quantified each model’s susceptibility to a key failure mode. Specifically, we fitted a logistic mixed-effects model predicting the probability of a response being classified as a Coherent Hallucination in the Factual Judgment task, using Alignment Pressure as the predictor. The resulting log-odds coefficient for each model was then used as a standardized measure of effect size.

Regression analyses were performed in R (version 4.3.3) using the packages
brms

(
bürkner2017advancedbayesianmultilevelmodeling
)
and
afex
(based on
lme4
;
JSSv067i01
).

Three-Dimensional Reasoning Evaluation

You are a rigorous and objective research analyst. Your task is to
evaluate a language model’s generated response based on the provided
context. You will provide three independent scores based on the
criteria below.

Important Rules:
- Return ONLY valid JSON (no extra text).
- Your evaluation of each criterion must be independent of the others.

Evaluation Criteria:
1. Factual Correctness (FC):
- Evaluate ONLY the factual soundness of the claims within the
’think’ text against verifiable, real-world knowledge.
- Ignore the logical structure, surface quality, and the final
’answer’. This is purely a fact-checking step.
- Score: 1 if all factual claims in the ’think’ text are correct.
-1 if it contains significant factual errors or clear fabrications.
0 for minor inaccuracies, misleading statements, or a mix of
correct and incorrect facts.

2. Logical Consistency (LC):
- Evaluate ONLY the strict, formal logical relationship between the
premises in the ’think’ text and the final ’answer’.
- Ignore the factual correctness of the premises and the surface
quality. A formal contradiction (e.g., ’think’ states P, while
’answer’ implies NOT P) MUST result in a -1 score.
- Score: 1 if the premises logically entail the ’answer’. -1 if the
premises in ’think’ logically contradict the ’answer’. 0 if the
relationship is compatible but not a strict entailment or
contradiction.

3. Reasoning Structure (RS):
- Evaluate ONLY the structural quality, organization, and clarity
of the ’think’ text as a piece of reasoning.
- You MUST ignore its factual accuracy (handled by FC) and its
logical consistency with the answer (handled by LC). The core
question is: is the form and structure of the reasoning process
clear and well-organized, regardless of its content?
- Score:
- 1 if the reasoning’s structure is exceptionally clear,
direct, and well-organized. It is perfectly concise for
simple tasks and appropriately comprehensive for complex ones.
- 0 if the reasoning’s structure is understandable and serves
its purpose, but may have minor flaws like being slightly
repetitive, verbose, or stylistically awkward. This is the
baseline for a competent response.
- -1 if the reasoning’s structure is severely flawed. This
includes nonsensical repetition, circular logic, irrelevant
tangents, or providing no meaningful reasoning content.
**A flaw should be considered ’severe’ if it makes the
reasoning difficult to follow or demonstrates a clear failure
in the generation process, rather than just being inefficient.**
