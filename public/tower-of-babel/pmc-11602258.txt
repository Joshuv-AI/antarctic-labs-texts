Online transcranial magnetic stimulation reveals dynamic interactions between language control and processing in bilingual language production
Authors: Junjie Wu, Yannan Ji, Chuyao Cai, Xinping Pu, Qiping Wang, Guoli Yan, Qiang Wang, Xin Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractLanguage production in bilinguals relies on the collaborative interaction between two neural systems: the language control system (e.g. the right inferior frontal gyrus) and the language processing system (e.g. the left superior temporal gyrus). However, the causal mapping between these brain systems and their cognitive functions, as well as the temporal dynamics between these two systems during bilingual language production, remain unclear. To investigate this, our study applied online transcranial magnetic stimulation to the right inferior frontal gyrus and left superior temporal gyrus in Chinese–English bilinguals performing a language-switching task. Transcranial magnetic stimulation was administered within seven 100 ms time windows (TW1 to TW7) following the picture onset. We observed time window–specific enhancements of language control, as shown by a significant reduction in switching costs upon the stimulations on both sites relative to the sham stimulation: specifically, the right inferior frontal gyrus at TW1, TW2, TW4, and TW5 and the left superior temporal gyrus at TW2 and TW5. These findings indicate a double-strike top–down control mechanism underpinned by the pathway from the right inferior frontal gyrus to the right inferior frontal gyrus, potentially during both stages during production: the language task schema and the lemma selection. Our study provides the first evidence of the dynamic interactions between language control and processing systems causally underpinning bilingual language production.Keywords: bilingualism, double-strike pathway, lSTG, online TMS, rIFG

Introduction

A proficient bilingual possesses the remarkable ability to speak one of their two languages fluently and switch between them effortlessly. In many tasks, these two languages are activated in parallel and compete with each other, especially during language production (e.g. Finkbeiner et al. 2006; Guo and Peng 2006; Colome and Miozzo 2010), necessitating language control mechanisms to manage the competition (Green 1998; Costa et al. 1999; Abutalebi et al. 2008; Green and Abutalebi 2013; Declerck and Philipp 2015; Abutalebi and Green 2016; Declerck and Koch 2023).

One view holds that language control in bilingual language processing consists of a set of mechanisms involving inhibitory control (Green 1998; Meuter and Allport 1999; Wang 2015; Ma et al. 2016; Kleinman and Gollan 2018; Declerck and Koch 2023; Ivanova et al. 2023). Specifically, Green (1998) proposed the inhibitory control model (ICM), which posits that bilinguals inhibit words in the nontarget language such that words with higher activation levels in the target language are selected for output. These processes may occur at two stages during language switching. The first stage, known as the language task schema stage, involves selecting the appropriate language (i.e. the first language [L1] or the second language [L2]) as required by the task, while the second stage, the lemma selection stage, involves selecting the lexical item in the target language. The ICM provides a theoretical framework for understanding language production mechanisms in bilinguals (Declerck and Philipp 2015; Declerck and Koch 2023).

The language-switching paradigm has been widely used to examine language control in bilinguals (e.g. Meuter and Allport 1999; Costa et al. 2006; Wang et al. 2013; Babcock and Vallesi 2015; Bonfieni et al. 2019; Zheng et al. 2020; Zhu et al. 2022). In this paradigm, bilinguals name pictures or digits in the target language according to cues (e.g. color). Switch trials (L1 → L2, L2 → L1) typically result in longer response times (RTs) and higher error rates (ERs) compared to repeat trials (L1 → L1, L2 → L2), yielding the switching costs (i.e. switching costs = switch − repeat; Meuter and Allport 1999; Christoffels et al. 2007; Guo et al. 2011). Switching costs were also observed to diminish through L2 learning (Liu et al. 2020) or language-switching training (Kang et al. 2017), implying that smaller switching costs are associated with higher efficiency in managing cross-language competition. In other words, switching costs can serve as a quantitative measure of the efficiency of bilingual language control mechanisms (e.g. Linck et al. 2012).

Many studies have employed the language-switching paradigm to investigate the inhibition of nontarget languages during these two stages (i.e. Jackson et al. 2001; Martin et al. 2013; Guo et al. 2013a; Guo et al. 2013b; Liu et al. 2016). In the event-related brain potential (ERP) studies on language switching, the earlier N2 component (occurring around 250 to 350 ms) and the later positive component (LPC, occurring around 450 to 650 ms) are speculated to be closely associated with the language task schema stage and the selection of the lemma in the target language, respectively (e.g. Liu et al. 2016). For instance, Jackson et al. (2001) utilized ERPs to study less proficient bilinguals performing a digit-naming switching task. They discovered that the N2 component (320 ms) was more negative when switching to L2 compared to switching to L1, which can be interpreted as evidence that inhibition may occur at the stage of language task schema competition. Furthermore, Martin et al. (2013) found that the LPC (500 to 650 ms) was larger in switch than repeat trials in the weaker language during picture naming. Liu et al. (2016) tested unbalanced Chinese–English bilinguals in a language-switching task and found that the LPC (450 to 650 ms) amplitude did not differ significantly between L1 and L2 repeat trials but was larger for trials switching to L2 than to L1. These findings suggest that language control might be required at different time windows (TWs) and stages to manage cross-language competition.

With the advent of functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI), researchers have initiated investigations into the neural underpinnings of language control (e.g. Hernandez et al. 2000; Wang et al. 2007; Branzi et al. 2016; Kang et al. 2017; Wu et al. 2019). These studies have revealed cortical and subcortical brain regions involved in language control, including the left prefrontal cortex for response selection, the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (dACC)/presupplementary motor area for conflict monitoring, and the left caudate for translation and language selection (for reviews, see Abutalebi and Green 2008, 2016). Given that regions associated with inhibitory control (e.g. the right inferior frontal gyrus [rIFG]) are part of the language control network, it is assumed that inhibitory control plays a crucial role in bilingual language control (Rodriguez-Fornells et al. 2005; Wang et al. 2007; de Bruin et al. 2014; Iannaccone et al. 2015; Wu et al. 2019, 2020; Tao et al. 2021; Wu et al. 2022). For instance, de Bruin et al. (2014) recruited bilinguals to perform a language-switching task during fMRI scanning. They found increased activation in the rIFG when switching to the nondominant language compared to repeat trials, suggesting the involvement of inhibitory control during language switching in bilinguals.

Furthermore, the interaction between language processing and language control has been investigated in recent neuroimaging studies (e.g. Li et al. 2015; Li et al. 2016; Liu et al. 2020; Li et al. 2022). For example, Li et al. (2015) conducted a study where bimodal bilinguals and monolinguals completed a picture-naming task during fMRI scans. They observed stronger functional connectivity between the language control region (i.e. the dACC) and a typical language processing region, namely, the left superior temporal gyrus (lSTG) in bimodal bilinguals compared to monolinguals. Recently, Li et al. (2022) reported that the dACC exhibited negative effective connectivity with the lSTG. In other words, the stronger activation of the dACC was associated with the weaker activation of the lSTG. These findings suggest a relationship between language control and language processing regions during bilingual language production. However, due to the limited temporal resolution of fMRI, the fine-grained temporal dynamics between these two systems remain unknown. Moreover, the prevalent use of neuroimaging techniques, which are inherently correlational, dominates our insights into the causal relationship between neural activities and distinct cognitive tasks (Silvanto and Pascual-Leone 2012; Siddiqi et al. 2022).

In contrast, transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) can modify cortical excitability, affecting behaviors linked to specific brain regions and providing causal mappings of brain functions (Silvanto and Pascual-Leone 2012; Siddiqi et al. 2022). For example, Wu et al. (2025) employed offline TMS to modulate neural activities in the rIFG of Chinese–English bilinguals and found worse performances in a language-switching task after cTBS compared to those after sham stimulations. Although this study establishes the rIFG as crucial for language control, it’s important to note that offline TMS cannot track the causal dynamics in real time between the language control and the language-processing brain regions.

Thus, online TMS has value in tracking the real-time dynamics of brain functions and has been used to probe the time course of brain function in various studies (Pitcher et al. 2007; Pitcher et al. 2008; Sollmann et al. 2014; Krieg et al. 2016). For instance, Zhao et al. (2021) applied online TMS over the left inferior frontal gyrus or left posterior middle temporal gyrus (pMTG) at different TWs during the presentation of gesture–speech pairs. They observed selective disruption of gesture–speech integration in specific TWs, suggesting a two-stage integration circuit involving the pMTG-to-IFG pathway in the prelexical stage and an IFG-to-pMTG feedback signal in the postlexical stage. Here, we attempt to first use online TMS to modulate language control and language processing regions, providing causal evidence to show their temporal dynamic interactions.

To summarize, neuroimaging studies have revealed that bilingual language production involves both a language control system (typically the rIFG; de Bruin et al. 2014; Sulpizio et al. 2020) and a language processing system (typically the lSTG; Li et al. 2015), as well as their interactions (Li et al. 2015; Blanco-Elorrieta et al. 2018). However, the dynamic interactions between these two systems, as well as the causal or epiphenomenal nature of the brain regions involved, remain to be fully elucidated. To address this gap, we employed online TMS targeting the rIFG and the lSTG across different TWs when bilingual participants were performing the language-switching task. We hypothesize that the fine-grained temporal dynamics between the language control and processing systems play causal (rather than epiphenomenal) roles during bilingual production, predicting changes in switching costs in specific TWs when applying online TMS targeting the rIFG and the lSTG, compared to sham stimulation. Alternatively, if the interactions between language control and processing systems are epiphenomenal, we expect no TMS-induced changes.

Materials and methods

Participants

Twenty-one unbalanced Chinese–English bilinguals (10 females, age = 20.39 ± 0.98) at Tianjin Normal University volunteered to participate in the study. They were native speakers of Chinese and learned English as a foreign language at the age of 8.10 ± 2.32 years through formal education. All of them achieved intermediate proficiency in English as measured by the College English Test Band 4 (CET-41) with a mean score of 472.23 ± 46.46. We further assessed the English proficiency of each participant using the Oxford Placement Test (OPT), which assigns a total score of 50 (Allan 2004), and the average OPT score of our participants was 33.38 ± 6.59. Self-ratings of language proficiency (a 10-point scale: 1 = not fluent, 10 = very fluent) revealed that participants were more proficient in Chinese (8.54 ± 1.17) than English (5.31 ± 1.61), t (20) = 8.80, P < 0.001. All participants were right-handed, aged over 18 years, and had normal or corrected-to-normal vision. They also had no history of brain disease, seizures, or contraindications for TMS following established guidelines (Rossi et al. 2009; Rossi et al. 2021). The Tianjin Normal University Ethics Committee reviewed and approved the experiment. All participants signed informed consent before the experiment.

Materials

Pictures were selected from Van de Putte et al. (2017) and Snodgrass and Vanderwart (1980), consisting of 11 black-and-white line-drawing images of concrete nouns. According to the Corpus of Contemporary American English (COCA; Rafatbakhsh and Ahmadi 2019), available at https://www.english-corpora.org/coca/, the average frequency (per million) of English words was 46.12 ± 42.24. Meanwhile, according to the Chinese Web 2017 (zhTenTen17) Simplified corpus (Gong and Ahrens 2008), accessible at https://app.sketchengine.eu/, the frequency (per million) of Chinese words was 60.84 ± 29.00. There was no significant difference between L1 and L2 in word frequency (t = −0.952, P = 0.352). We also compared the number of syllables in the two groups of words and found no significant difference (English, 1.27 ± 0.47; Chinese, 1.45 ± 0.52; t = 0.86, P = 0.400). Additionally, the average word length of English words was 4.55 ± 1.04, and the average stroke number of Chinese words was 10.73 ± 4.59 (based on the online Xinhua Dictionary, https://mzd.diyifanwen.com/).

Design and procedure

A 2 (Trial type: switch and repeat) × 3 (Stimulation site [Site]: rIFG, lSTG, and sham stimulation [Sham]) × 8 (TW: TW1 to TW7, and No TMS) within-subject design was adopted in our study. The No TMS served as the baseline for the other seven TWs. Online TMS targeted the rIFG and lSTG with Sham (coil reversed) as the control condition in each TW during the task. Specifically, to comprehensively cover the timing for the stages of the language task schema (250 to 350 ms) and lemma selection (450 to 650 ms), we scheduled the TMS to deliver stimulation to the target site starting at 100, 200, 300, 400, 500, 600, and 700 ms following the onset of a picture. Due to an inherent delay of about 100 ms in TMS, the actual stimulations of the first pulse on brain sites were administered at approximately 210 ± 42, 309 ± 31, 409 ± 38, 509 ± 33, 608 ± 39, 700 ± 61, and 750 ± 131 ms, respectively. The corresponding real TWs for TW1 to TW7 are 210 to 310, 309 to 409, 409 to 509, 509 to 609, 608 to 708, 700 to 800, and 750 to 850 ms, respectively. These timings effectively cover the TWs for the language task schema and lemma selection stages.

Participants first visited the Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) Center at Tianjin Normal University for an MRI scan. During this visit, we obtained a high-resolution (1 × 1 × 1 mm) T1-weighted structural scan using a 3.0T Prisma (Siemens Healthcare, Germany) MRI Scanner. During the two additional visits, online TMS was applied to participants, in which sham stimulations were scheduled before real stimulations targeting either the rIFG or lSTG across two separate days to minimize the visits to the TMS laboratory for participants. Specifically, sham stimulations were conducted for half of the total trials on one visit and the other half on the other visit. The session orders of stimulations targeting the rIFG and the lSTG were counterbalanced across participants. MRI-guided TMS neuro-navigation was monitored online throughout the TMS session to determine the actual stimulation site during task execution precisely.

Prior to the first TMS session, participants were presented with pictures in PowerPoint slides and their associated names in both Chinese and English, enabling them to accurately name all pictures in both languages. After a brief practice run, they performed the language-switching task while receiving online Sham or real TMS (see section Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation for the TMS procedure).

Each TMS session consisted of 528 formal trials (176 for Sham, 352 for real TMS) across 8 TWs. The task, programmed by PsychoPy 3.0 (Psychology Software Tools; Peirce et al. 2022), lasted approximately 24 min. Pictures were displayed centrally on a 14-inch computer screen with a resolution of 1024 × 768 pixels.

As shown in Fig. 1a, each trial began with a fixation in the screen center for 200 to 600 ms (in steps of 50 ms), followed by a blank screen of 200 ms. Then, a picture with a blue or red frame was presented for 1,000 ms. Online TMS was applied pseudo-randomly at one of eight different TWs during the picture presentation. Afterward, a blank screen was displayed for 1,200 ms, making the average trial duration 2,750 ms. During the task, we instructed participants to name pictures in the target language according to a pseudorandomized language cue (frame color) as quickly as possible (i.e. red for Chinese and blue for English or red for English and blue for Chinese). The cue–language mappings were counterbalanced across participants. Stimuli were displayed in a pseudo-random order to prevent repetition of the same picture in consecutive trials. Moreover, we ensured that no more than three consecutive trials were conducted in the same condition (L1 repeat, L1 switch, L2 repeat, L2 switch). Each condition across all TWs was assigned an equal number of trials. Additionally, participants completed a language background questionnaire at the end of the second TMS session. Each visit took approximately 1 h to complete all stages.

Transcranial magnetic stimulation

The stimulation site of the rIFG (Montreal Neurological Institutes [MNI] coordinate: x = 51, y = 19, z = 17) was selected based on fMRI studies on inhibitory control and language control (Jahfari et al. 2011; de Bruin et al. 2014). The stimulation site of lSTG (MNI coordinates: x = −54, y = −12, z = 6) was selected based on fMRI studies on language processing in a picture-naming task (Zou et al. 2012; Li et al. 2015). As shown in Fig. 1b, we depict the electric fields induced in the cortex by TMS over the rIFG and lSTG (SimNIBS 4.0, https://simnibs.github.io/simnibs;Conde et al. 2019).

During the experiment, a frameless stereotaxic system (Localite GmbH, Bonn, Germany) was used for accurate stimulation monitoring. MRI images of each participant were loaded into the system and manually registered using anatomical landmarks (anterior and posterior commissures, point on the falx) to ensure precise targeting. Trajectory markers based on the MNI coordinate system defined the participant-specific target region. MRI coregistration was performed during each TMS session to link the 3D model with the participant’s head. To guide coil placement, participants wore a headband with reflective spherical markers tracked by the navigation system. Marker angles were checked and adjusted to be orthogonal to the skull for accurate neuro-navigation.

A TMS stimulator (MagPro X100, MagVenture) was used to deliver the TMS via a 70 mm figure-eight coil (MagVenture MCF-B65). In line with previous studies (O’Shea et al. 2004; Pitcher et al. 2007; Pitcher et al. 2008; Zhao et al. 2021), we employed an online double-pulse TMS at a frequency of 25 Hz and a stimulation intensity of 100% of the resting motor threshold (RMT) in each TW. For example, in TW1, a pulse was delivered approximately 210 ms after the onset of the picture, followed by a second pulse occurring 40 ms later. The Sham was the same as the real TMS except for the coil reversed. We utilized the RMT from the M1 of the ipsilateral hemisphere (e.g. Sollmann et al. 2014; Boux and Pulvermuller 2023). RMT was defined as the intensity that elicited motor-evoked potentials response larger than 50 μV in 5 out of 10 pulses in the first dorsal interosseous muscle (Rossini et al. 1994; Rossini et al. 2015; Wu et al. 2024). The mean stimulation intensity during the experiment was 57.86% ± 8.71% of maximum stimulator output for the lSTG and 60.05% ± 10.10% for the rIFG.

After each TMS session, participants completed a 21-item discomfort survey, rating each item from 0 (absent) to 10 (severe). No differences were reported between the two real stimulation sites (all Ps ≥ 0.226). However, compared to the sham TMS, participants reported higher levels of discomfort with the real TMS, including headache, migraine, scalp pain, face pain, neck pain, numbness or “pins & needles,” problems with vision, nervousness/anxiety, fatigue, dizziness, thinking difficulties, speaking difficulties, hearing difficulties, and change in mood (Ps < 0.032). Moreover, while the real TMS targeting the lSTG did not significantly affect ear pain (P = 0.063), nausea (P = 0.066), or toothache (P = 0.138) compared to the sham TMS, targeting the rIFG resulted in significantly higher levels of these sensations (Ps < 0.004). No participants experienced fainting during the experiment. There were no significant differences across the three sessions for skin rash/burn (P = 0.090) or weepiness/crying (P = 0.353). It is worth noting that discomfort from the real TMS is commonly observed in neuromodulation research (Loo et al. 2008; Lissemore et al. 2024; see also Wu et al. 2025) but tends to lessen quickly (Borckardt et al. 2013). In the current study, no participants withdrew from the study due to discomfort. All left the laboratory without any issues.

Data analysis

RTs were determined by the speech onset time following the picture onset using Praat software (Boersma and Weenink 2018), given that PsychoPy 3.0 (Peirce et al. 2022) automatically generated sound files after picture onset. The voice onset times were manually identified through visual analysis of the waveform intensity (dB) thresholds. Because the noise from TMS interfered with Praat’s automatic recognition of the participants’ vocal naming responses, we manually registered RTs for each trial. Each trial’s RT was evaluated based on the average of two independent raters, and a strong correlation coefficient of 0.948 was found between their ratings.

Trials in TMS malfunction or in which TMS pulses made it impossible to correctly determine word onset (2.59%) were excluded from statistical analyses. We also removed trials with no response (1.31%), trials named in a wrong language (3.51%), trials named after naming in a wrong language (0.94%), trials named in a wrong word (0.56%), trials with hesitations (1.42%), trials of recording failures or absolute outliers (below 300 ms or above 2,200 ms, 0.01%), and relative outliers (2.5 SD above or below individual’s mean, 2.47%). Additionally, due to deviations from a normal distribution in the RT data, we implemented an ordered quantile normalization transformation (Peterson and Cavanaugh 2019) with the bestNormalize package (Peterson 2021).

We used linear mixed-effects models (LMEMs) for RT data and generalized LMEMs for ER data. This analysis was conducted using the lme4 package (Bates et al. 2015), beginning with comprehensive models that included fixed-effects and the maximal random-effects structures (Barr et al. 2013). Our analysis was based on a 2 (Trial type: switch and repeat) × 3 (Site: rIFG, lSTG, and Sham) × 8 (TW: TW1 to TW7, and No TMS) design. Notably, Language was not included as a fixed effect due to the limited number of trials examining the difference in switching costs between L1 and L2. To add to this, the effect of language switching direction has been reported to be inconsistent among studies (e.g. de Bruin et al. 2020; Zheng et al. 2020; Ivanova and Hernandez 2021). Thus, the current analysis focused on the switching costs between two languages without considering language switching direction. Contrast coding was applied to the Trial type (repeat = −0.5, switch = 0.5), while the Site was dummy-coded, with Sham set as the baseline ([0 0]), rIFG coded as [1 0], and lSTG coded as [0 1]. Following the same coding approach, the TW was also dummy-coded with No TMS as the baseline. The models incorporated fixed effects for Trial type, Site, TW, and their interactions, along with random intercepts for participants and items and random slopes for all fixed effects. The full models were specified as follows: RT or ER ~ Trial type * Site * TW + (1 + Trial type * Site * TW | Participant) + (1 + Trial type * Site * TW | Item). In cases where models failed to converge, we utilized a backward-stepping procedure to simplify the random-effects structures. This simplification first involved removing the random slope interactions that accounted for the least variance, and, if necessary, we also removed the main effects from the random structure (Barr et al. 2013). We adhered to the guidelines of Matuschek et al. (2017), simplifying the model only to the extent that it did not significantly affect the fit, with an alpha of 0.2 for the likelihood ratio test to determine significance. Our final model was as follows: RT ~ Trial type * Site * TW + (1 + Trial type + Site | Participant).

To evaluate the overall differences between conditions, we conducted omnibus tests on the model. We used the anova function from the lmerTest package for RTs (Kuznetsova et al. 2017) and Anova function from the car package for ERs (Fox and Weisberg 2019) to assess statistical significance (P-value). These approaches enable us to present the results as the traditional analysis of variance, which can enhance clarity and facilitate comprehension. We focused on the three-way interaction between the Trial type, Site, and TW, which reflects whether switching costs differ between the Sites or TWs.

If the three-way interaction among the Trial type, Site, and TW was significant, we refitted LMEMs in each TW separately. In each TW, the full models were represented as follows: RT or ER ~ Trial type * Site + (1 + Trial type * Site | Participant) + (1 + Trial type * Site | Item). We maintained consistent coding approaches for Trial Type and Site and ensured the model selection process followed the same procedures. We employed the summary function from the lmerTest package (Kuznetsova et al. 2017) to present our results for each TW. Our primary interest in the model was to examine the two-way interaction between the Site and Trial type, particularly comparing switching costs in the rIFG and Sham conditions, as well as those in the lSTG and Sham conditions. To address multiple comparisons, we adjusted the statistical significances for 16 comparisons using the false discovery rate (FDR) method (Benjamini and Hochberg 1995). All these analyses were implemented in the R statistical computing environment (The R Core Team 2023). Our analyses and R codes are available in the Open Science Framework (OSF) repository (https://osf.io/bnceq/; Ji and Wu 2024).

Results

The overall ERs were 3.03%, indicating that our participants performed the task well. Neither the effect of Trial type nor the effect of TW was significant (Trial type: χ2 = 1.60, P = 0.206; TW: χ2 = 2.54, P = 0.111). However, the effect of Site was significant (χ2 = 7.14, P = 0.028). A post hoc test revealed that the ERs in the rIFG (3.63%) condition were higher than those in the Sham (2.65%; z = 3.41, P = 0.002) and the lSTG (2.82%; z = 2.82, P = 0.013) conditions, indicating that stimulation on the rIFG negatively impacted the performance overall. No significant difference was observed between the Sham (2.65%) and lSTG (2.82%; z < 1) conditions. Furthermore, interactions between Trial type and TW (χ2 < 1), Trial type and Site (χ2 < 1), as well as Site and TW (χ2 = 1.38, P = 0.503), were not significant. Similarly, the three-way interaction among the Trial type, Site, and TW did not reach significance (χ2 < 1), suggesting that the TMS effect on switching costs did not vary across different TWs.

Main effects

In the analysis of RTs, as shown in Table 1, the main effect of Trial type was found to be significant (F = 73.13, P < 0.001), indicating that the naming response was slower on switch trials than on repeat trials overall (985 and 946 ms, respectively), yielding the switching costs (39 ms). In addition, we found no significance of Site (F < 1). However, the main effect of TW was significant (F = 70.89, P < 0.001). A post hoc test using the emmeans function (Lenth 2021) showed that RTs were faster in TW1 and TW3 compared to No TMS (938, 949, and 962 ms, respectively; z = −6.62, P < 0.001; z = −3.28, P = 0.023). Conversely, RTs were slower in TW6 and TW7 compared to No TMS (977, 1,021, and 962 ms, respectively; z = 4.69, P < 0.001; z = 13.67, P < 0.001). No significant differences were observed between the remaining TWs and the No TMS (Ps ≥ 0.607).

Two-way interactions

Moreover, the analysis revealed a significant two-way interaction between Site and TW (F = 2.28, P = 0.004). We conducted follow-up simple effect tests using the emmeans function. We found that RTs in the rIFG (1,034 ms) condition were slightly slower than those in the Sham condition (1,005 ms) in TW7 (z = 2.08, P = 0.093). This subtle difference was not observed in other TWs (Ps ≥ 0.288). Additionally, the two-way interaction between the Trial type and TW showed a marginal significance (F = 2.01, P = 0.050). The two-way interaction between the Trial type and Site, which is our main interest, was significant (F = 16.49, P < 0.001). Further analysis using the emmeans function showed that RTs in the switch trials were slower than in the repeat trials across all Sites: Sham (992 and 938 ms, respectively; switching costs = 54 ms; z = 10.26, P < 0.001), rIFG (981 and 953 ms, respectively; switching costs = 28 ms; z = 5.26, P < 0.001), and lSTG (983 and 948 ms, respectively; switching costs = 35 ms; z = 6.38, P < 0.001). These results replicated the classic switch cost effects across all Sites. To explore potential differences in switch costs across Sites, we separately refitted LMEMs for each pair of Sites (rIFG vs. lSTG, rIFG vs. Sham, and lSTG vs. Sham). The results showed that switching costs in Sham were significantly greater than those in the rIFG (54 vs. 28 ms, P < 0.001) and lSTG (54 vs. 35 ms, P < 0.001), with no significant difference between the rIFG and lSTG (28 vs. 35 ms, P = 0.222).

Three-way interactions

Notably, the three-way interaction among the Trial type, Site, and TW was significant (F = 2.04, P = 0.012), indicating that the TMS effect on switching costs varied across different TWs. Subsequently, we separately refitted LMEMs for RTs in each TW. In these models, Trial type (switch and repeat), Site (rIFG, lSTG, and Sham), and their interactions were considered fixed effects. We also incorporated random intercepts for both participants and items, along with random slopes for all the fixed effects. The procedure for simplifying the random effects was followed as mentioned earlier.

The interaction between Trial type and Site [rIFG vs. Sham] was significant in TW1 (t = −2.57, Pfdr = 0.027), TW2 (t = −3.67, Pfdr < 0.001), TW4 (t = −2.66, Pfdr = 0.026), and TW5 (t = −4.71, Pfdr < 0.001), but not the remaining TWs (Psfdr ≥ 0.270). These results indicated that switching costs were significantly smaller when stimulating the rIFG during TW1, TW2, TW4, and TW5 (7, 25, 35, and 19 ms, respectively) compared to Sham (48, 83, 70, and 90 ms, respectively; see Fig. 3). Additionally, the interaction between Trial type and Site [lSTG vs. Sham] was significant in TW2 (t = −3.78, Pfdr < 0.001) and TW5 (t = −4.15, Pfdr P < 0.001), but not in other TWs (Psfdr ≥ 0.103). These results indicated that switching costs were also significantly reduced when stimulating the lSTG in TW2 and TW5 (26 and 30 ms, respectively) compared to the Sham (83 and 90 ms, respectively; see Fig. 3).

In particular, we refitted LMEMs for RTs in No TMS data to examine the interaction between the Trial type (switch and repeat) and Site (rIFG, lSTG, and Sham). Our analysis revealed no significant interaction between Site [rIFG vs. Sham] and Trial type (t < 1), nor between Site [lSTG vs. Sham] and Trial type (t < 1). These results indicated that there was no evidence of a difference in switching costs when no stimulation was applied to either the rIFG or lSTG (53 and 53 ms, respectively) compared to the Sham (39 ms), confirming the null effect of Sham in language switching.

The TMS effect on switch and repeat trials

To further investigate whether the reduction in switching costs resulted from slower RTs in repeat trials or faster RTs in switch trials, we conducted additional analyses of LMEMs based on data from switch trials and repeat trials separately in TW1, TW2, TW4, and TW5. In these models, we considered the Site (rIFG, lSTG, and Sham) as a fixed effect. We also included random intercepts for both participants and items, as well as random slopes for the Site, consistent with our earlier approaches. To present our results for each TW, we employed the summary function from the lmerTest package.

For repeat trials, the effect of TMS was marginally significant when stimulating the rIFG in TW5 (t = 1.70, P = 0.089) but not in TW1, TW2, and TW4 (t < 1; t = 1.38, P = 0.168; t < 1). These indicated that RTs were slightly, but not significantly, slower when stimulating the rIFG in TW5 (969 ms) compared to Sham (931 ms). Moreover, the effect of TMS was marginally significant when stimulating the lSTG in TW2 (t = 1.91, P = 0.057), but not in TW1, TW4, and TW5 (ts < 1). These suggested that RTs for repeat trials were slightly, but not significantly, slower when stimulating the lSTG in TW2 (953 ms) compared to the Sham (924 ms; see Fig. 2). Thus, overall, performance on repeat trials roughly remained consistent across Sites and TWs.

For switch trials, the effect of TMS showed significance when stimulating the rIFG in TW2 (t = −2.98, P = 0.003) and TW5 (t = −2.38, P = 0.018), with marginal significance in TW4 (t = −1.76, P = 0.078) and a nonsignificant trend in TW1 (t = −1.39, P = 0.166). These indicated that RTs were faster (or showed a trend of being faster) when stimulating the rIFG in TW1, TW2, TW4, and TW5 (952, 974, 988, and 988 ms, respectively) compared to Sham (965, 1,007, 999, and 1020 ms, respectively). Additionally, the effect of TMS was significant when stimulating the lSTG in TW5 (t = −2.39, P = 0.017), with marginal significance in TW2 (t = −1.86, P = 0.064) and TW4 (t = −1.78, P = 0.076), but not in TW1 (t = −1.07, P = 0.285). These suggested that RTs were faster when stimulating the lSTG in TW2, TW4, and TW5 (979, 984, and 984 ms, respectively) compared to the Sham (1,007, 999, and 1,020 ms, respectively; see Fig. 2). These results indicated that the reduction in switching costs when targeting rIFG or lSTG is mainly due to the faster RTs in switch trials but with a minor contribution from increased RTs in repeat trials.

Discussion

Online TMS is widely adopted in research for its potential to disrupt cognitive processes, serving as a tool to study neurocognitive functions through “virtual lesions” and to elucidate the causal relationships between brain structure and cognitive functions (e.g. Amassian et al. 1989). Notably, while TMS is often linked to performance disruption, some studies have reported a phenomenon known as “paradoxical enhancement,” where TMS appears to enhance cognitive performance rather than impair it (see Luber and Lisanby 2014, for a review; Beynel et al. 2019, for a meta-analysis). In their meta-analysis, Beynel et al. (2019) found that among studies reporting significant effects, 12% showed improvements in accuracy and 27% in RT using online TMS. It is proposed that online TMS may facilitate cortical processing by triggering excitatory postsynaptic potentials (Luber and Lisanby 2014) or enhance cortical integration by entraining endogenous brain oscillations (Beynel et al. 2020). Although the mechanisms behind this paradoxical enhancement are not fully understood yet, the capacity of TMS to either disrupt or enhance performance provides a valuable tool for exploring causality in neurocognitive functions.

The current study implemented online TMS on Chinese–English bilinguals performing a language-switching task to explore the temporal dynamics of language control and processing. We identified two crucial brain regions involved in language switching based on the existing literature, namely, the rIFG and lSTG. To understand the causal relationships between online TMS stimulations and language-switching performance, TMS pulses targeted the rIFG, lSTG, and sham stimulation (coil-reversed) at one of the TWs (TW1 to TW7) or under the No TMS condition in each trial. We observed significant reductions in switching costs during rIFG stimulation at TW1 (210 to 310 ms), TW2 (309 to 409 ms), TW4 (509 to 609 ms), and TW5 (608 to 708 ms), as well as lSTG stimulation at TW2 (309 to 409 ms) and TW5 (608 to 708 ms), relative to Sham. Additionally, we observed that the reductions in switching costs were primarily due to faster RTs on switch trials following stimulation, with a minor contribution from slower RTs on repeat trials. These findings indicate that online TMS applied to the rIFG and lSTG effectively facilitated language switching, as a result of better inhibition of the nontarget language and reactivation of the target language. Based on the inconsecutive timing of these facilitation effects in switching, we propose a double-strike pathway from the rIFG to the lSTG that underpins language control and processing (see Fig. 4), potentially over the stages of the language task schema and the lemma selection. The role of rIFG in language control

Our results first demonstrate reduced switching costs when stimulating the rIFG during TW1, TW2, TW4, and TW5 compared to sham stimulation. That is, the application of online TMS resulted in reduced switching costs at the time windows of 210 to 409 and 509 to 708 ms after picture onset, indicating an enhancement effect on language control, consistent with previous findings (e.g. Jackson et al. 2001; Verhoef et al. 2009; Misra et al. 2012; Martin et al. 2013; Liu et al. 2016).

The rIFG plays an essential role in suppressing the nontarget language during language switching (e.g. de Bruin et al. 2014; Zhu et al. 2020; Liu et al. 2021; Zhu et al. 2022). For example, de Bruin et al. (2014) observed increased activation in the rIFG when participants switched to a weaker language in picture naming, compared to repeat and blocked trials. This result indicates the rIFG’s involvement in inhibiting the nontarget languages during language switching. In addition, inhibitory control is proposed to function at both stages in bilingual production: the language task schema stage, as reflected by the N2 component (around 250 to 350 ms; e.g. Jackson et al. 2001; Verhoef et al. 2009; Misra et al. 2012), and the lemma selection stage, indexed by the late positive component (LPC, around 500 to 650 ms; e.g. Martin et al. 2013; Liu et al. 2016).

Thus, the TWs where we observed a reduction in switch costs upon the rIFG stimulation are largely consistent with earlier studies showing brain activities of inhibitory control at different time windows after picture onset. Not only do these results support the involvement of inhibitory control across both stages in language switching, namely, the language task schema and the lemma selection, but also demonstrate the facilitation effect of the rIFG online stimulation could last over the two stages in bilingual language production.

The role of lSTG in language processing

We also observed reduced switching costs when stimulating the lSTG in TW2 and TW5 compared to the sham stimulation. That is, the application of online TMS led to reduced switching costs at approximately 309 to 409 and 608 to 708 ms, indicating the facilitation of language processing during these TWs. The two periods are separated by a 200 ms gap.

The lSTG plays a pivotal role in language processing, primarily responsible for the reactivation of the target language within designated TWs (e.g. Indefrey and Bonin 2004; Indefrey 2011; Hut et al. 2017; Jodzio et al. 2023). For instance, Strijkers et al. (2013) recruited Spanish-Catalan bilinguals to name a block of items in one language (L1 or L2) and another block of different items subsequently in the other language (L2 or L1). They observed that ERPs elicited by L2 naming had significantly more positive amplitudes only after naming L1 blocks within the TW of 140 to 350 ms, compared to L1 naming. This observation suggests that lexical access occurred at this time window. In addition, it is widely accepted that lemma selection transpires around 200 to 300 ms after the target language is selected (e.g. Carota et al. 2022; Jodzio et al. 2023). These results may suggest that lemma selection immediately follows the language task schema within the same time frame. In other words, the lemma selection stage may occur 200 to 300 ms after the language task schema stage.

To sum up, in our current study, the observed reduction in switching costs upon stimulating the lSTG during TW2 and TW5 indicates that the online stimulation facilitated the reactivation of the target language task schema in TW2 and the lemma selection in TW5.

The double-strike pathway in language control

Taken together, our research shows earlier facilitation effects upon the rIFG stimulation at TW1, TW2, TW4, and TW5, as well as the later facilitation effects upon the lSTG stimulation at TW2 and TW5. Given the respective neurocognitive functions of these two brain regions in bilingual language switching and production, our results suggest that language control precedes language processing, but they also overlap in timing across both task schema and lemma selection stages as a double strike. Namely, the rIFG (i.e. for language control) and lSTG (i.e. for language processing) operate in the same way twice sequentially, where the rIFG function precedes but also parallels the lSTG function at both stages as a double-strike process. That is, the inhibition of the nontarget language (i.e. language control) not only precedes but also parallels and connects the reactivation of the target language (i.e. language processing), not only at the task scheme but also at the lemma selection stage. This logic is sensible because language switching requires the initial inhibition of the nontarget language used in the previous trial followed and paralleled by the reactivation of the target language (i.e. lexical access). It is conceivable these two processes overlap in timing for processing efficiency. In addition, faster responses in switch trials upon stimulations indicate faster access to the target language and more efficient inhibition of the nontarget language.

This argument is consistent with previous studies showing functional connectivity between regions associated with language control and language processing (e.g. Li et al. 2015; Li et al. 2016; Gullifer et al. 2018; Li et al. 2022). For instance, Li et al. (2022) recruited participants to perform dual-language and spoken-production tasks during an fMRI scan. Using dynamic causal modeling (DCM), they found that the anterior cingulate cortex and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex exhibited effective connectivity with the left STG and left precentral gyrus, suggesting promotive or inhibitory effects of language control on language processing. Likewise, greater functional connectivity during the resting state between the rIFG and left language regions was observed in bilinguals with an earlier age of acquisition (Gullifer et al. 2018) and in comparisons between bilinguals and monolinguals (Gao et al. 2020). Moreover, the rIFG is a crucial region for top–down inhibitory control when suppressing unwanted responses according to a stopping cue (Manuel et al. 2010; Hartmann et al. 2016; Schaum et al. 2021). Therefore, the results of the present study suggest that the rIFG may function as a top–down modulator of language processing in the lSTG. These two brain regions are related and cofunction during bilingual language production.

The sources of switching cost reduction

One of the key findings of the current study shows that the reductions in switching costs were primarily due to faster RTs on switch trials following stimulations, with a minor contribution from slower RTs on repeat trials. That is, our separate analyses on repeat and switch trials across different TWs showed largely consistent RTs to repeat trials but better switching skills upon stimulations on both rIFG and lSTG sites to speed up the RTs during language switching. First, this RT difference attributed to stimulation between switch and repeat trials provides strong evidence of the TMS effects on both sites during language switching. Second, the asymmetry of the TMS effects between the switch and repeat trials indicates the presence of language control when switching, consistent with previous studies that also showed that inhibitory control occurred at switch trials (e.g. Declerck et al. 2015; Zheng et al. 2020). For example, Zheng et al. (2020) recruited participants to name pictures in Dutch or English based on language cues with either short (two or three trials) or long (five or six trials) runs. Their findings highlighted a pronounced switch effect in the N2 component, which was larger after a short run than after a long run. They claimed that the nontarget language is suppressed during a switch but not repeat trial, as the effect of suppressing the nontarget language would reduce given time in the long run but more obvious in the short run. Therefore, given the analyses shown in the section The TMS Effect on Switch and Repeat Trials, the overall reduction in switching costs upon stimulation on both sites can be attributed to the effects observed in TW1, TW2, TW4, and TW5 for the rIFG and in TW2 and TW5 for the lSTG.

TMS effects on naming speed and accuracy

Our findings showed that the TMS impacts picture naming speed and accuracy. Overall, without considering switch/repeat trials or specific stimulation sites, TMS appears to elicit faster RTs during the earlier windows (TW1 and TW3) and slower RTs during the later time windows (TW6 and TW7) compared to the No TMS. These results are consistent with previous studies (Duecker et al. 2013; Jodzio et al. 2023). For instance, Jodzio et al. (2023) used TMS to explore the time course of 3 brain regions (lIFG, pMTG, and lSTG) in picture naming. Their results showed facilitation effects during the earlier time windows and inhibitory effects during the later time windows. The early facilitation effect might be attributed to intersensory facilitation. That is, an additional stimulus, such as the clicking sound of TMS, can speed up response times when it occurs close to the target stimulus (Terao et al. 1997; Sawaki et al. 1999; Duecker et al. 2013; Duecker and Sack 2015). On the other hand, the TMS clicking sound may lead participants to anticipate the TMS pulse, causing them to slow down during the later time windows (de Graaf et al. 2009). Though there was an interaction of TW and Brain Site, the follow-up analyses only revealed marginally significantly slower RTs in the rIFG compared to Sham in TW7, without any significant differences in the other TWs. This indicates that the interpretation of intersensory facilitation or obstruction may still hold in TW1 to TW6. As TW7 corresponds to the late phase of picture naming (750 to 850 ms), we attribute the slightly slower RTs in the rIFG to facial muscle twitches caused by the frontal TMS (e.g. Sundby et al. 2021; Yang et al. 2022).

In addition, our findings showed that the rIFG stimulation negatively impacted the overall naming accuracy, compared to the lSTG stimulation and Sham. As discussed earlier, our main findings showed the neural dynamics of language control and language processing as distinct but highly intertwined processes in language-switching tasks. The overall facilitation in language control upon the rIFG stimulation does not necessarily imply more accurate picture naming. In fact, the meta-analysis by Beynel et al. (2019) showed that online TMS was more likely to impair performance in studies where rTMS had a significant effect, affecting accuracy in 88% of cases. Therefore, we consider these accuracy results perhaps typical in TMS studies but could be further investigated in future work.

Taken together, our findings delineate a double-strike language control–processing pathway that operates sequentially (TW1 vs. TW2, TW4 vs. TW5) and simultaneously (TW2 and TW5) at both stages of language task schema and lemma selection. That is, the rIFG may exert top–down suppression on the lSTG during these two stages in bilingual language switching and production, allowing the initial but lasting inhibition of the nontarget language at both stages, as well as reactivating (i.e. accessing) the target language in any given trial. Some previous studies have posited that language control occurs at the language task schema stage (i.e. Jackson et al. 2001; Verhoef et al. 2009; Guo et al. 2013a), while others have argued for its occurrence at the lemma selection stage (i.e. Verhoef et al. 2010; Martin et al. 2013; Guo et al. 2013b; Liu et al. 2016). Our research corroborates that inhibition transpires across both stages preceding language processing initially, which is also consistent with the ICM (Green 1998). Moreover, the timing difference in the observed effects between these two stimulation sites (i.e. rIFG precedes lSTG) allows us to specify a directional flow of information from language control regions to language processing regions at each stage. So here, we propose a neurocognitive working model of the rIFG-to-lSTG unidirectional and double-strike pathway as a structured framework of the temporal dynamics of bilingual language control and processing (see Fig. 4b). This model illustrates that inhibitory control in bilingual language switching and production is executed in two key stages (i.e. double strikes): language task schema and lemma selection. At each stage, the language control region exerts top–down modulation on the language processing region, such that language control not only precedes but also parallels lexical access.

Limitations and future directions

Last, we acknowledge limitations in this study and raise future research directions based on the current findings. First, our experiment involves fewer data points per condition than suggested by Brysbaert and Stevens (2018), due to the challenge of managing multiple TWs in a relatively complex design and the relatively large effect observed in previous online TMS studies. Contrary to the absolute guidelines set by Brysbaert and Stevens, Meteyard and Davies (2020) argue that power analysis should focus on specific effect sizes. Previous online TMS studies with multiple TWs appear to report effects based on a similar number of trials per condition. For example, Silvanto and Cattaneo (2021) reported 20 trials per condition with 19 participants; Zhao et al. (2021) conducted 13 trials per condition with 26 participants; and Jodzio et al. (2023) included 10 trials per condition with 22 participants. While these examples reflect typical practice, we recommend that future online TMS studies increase data points by focusing on TWs where significant effects are observed in the present study.

Second, given the theoretical scope of the paper, we focused on the rIFG and lSTG, due to the constraint on the number of brain regions targeted in a TMS study. This narrow focus may oversimplify the neural networks involved in bilingual language production, which include areas like the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, the inferior parietal lobule, and the caudate nucleus (Price 2012; Abutalebi and Green 2016; Wu et al. 2019, 2020, 2022). Future research should target additional regions to facilitate meta-analyses that offer a holistic view of these networks.

Finally, our language-switching task utilized only one cue per language, inherently linking language switching with cue switching and confounding these two processes. Heikoop et al. (2016) addressed this by using two cues per language to differentiate between language switching and cue switching. They found greater costs for language switching (82 ms) than cue switching (37 ms). While we attribute most of the switching costs in our study to language switching, cue switching likely contributed as well, as inherent to the design for language switching studies. To elucidate this, future studies should employ two cues per language to better isolate language-switching costs. To take this further addressing the limitations in ecological validity in most existing language switching studies, future research should also employ tasks focusing on single-language production or voluntary language switching to consolidate and expand our findings because bilinguals may not encounter such frequent switching in everyday life but switch between languages in a variety of manners (see Wu et al. 2024).

Conclusion

In the current study, we investigated the effects of online TMS on the inhibitory control brain region (i.e. rIFG) and the language processing brain region (i.e. lSTG) at specific TWs in a bilingual switching task. Our goal is to show the temporal dynamics of the interaction between language control and processing to develop a neurocognitive model. We found enhanced performance due to TMS when targeting the rIFG during TW1, TW2, TW4, and TW5, as well as when targeting the lSTG during TW2 and TW5, compared to sham stimulation. These findings show a double-strike pathway with an rIFG-to-lSTG sequential and simultaneous involvement in bilingual language production. At both stages of language task schema and lemma selection, we propose that the region responsible for language control may exert a top–down modulation over the language processing region. The present study is the first to provide causal evidence for the temporal dynamics of the language control (i.e. rIFG) and processing (i.e. lSTG) brain areas in bilingual language production, as well as a structured framework for the intricate dynamics of bilingual language production.
