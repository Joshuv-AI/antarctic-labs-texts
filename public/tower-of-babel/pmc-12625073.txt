Evaluation of a nurse-led program for the prevention of complications of long-term glucocorticoids (COCORTICO): study protocol for a randomized trial
Authors: Solen Nicol, Kilian Trin, Lori-Anne Simon, Emmanuel Nowak, Ophélie Duval, Claire Martin, Samir Abdelouahad, Sophie Lengagne, Victorine Chinchilla, Valérie Gilet, Antoine Briantais, Alain Saraux, Divi Cornec, Claire De Moreuil, Marie-Laure Tanguy, Dewi Guellec

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLong-term glucocorticoids are widely prescribed for autoimmune and inflammatory diseases, but their use is associated with a wide range of adverse events, some of which can be severe. Patient education on toxicity and preventive strategies is essential, yet many patients do not receive sufficient information. In some areas, nurse-led therapeutic programs have been developed to optimize care. However, no standardized, institutionally supported education program exists to prevent the adverse events of long-term glucocorticoid therapy.MethodsWe are conducting a superiority, open-label, parallel-group, randomized trial, comparing a nurse-led interdisciplinary program with standard of care, across five French hospitals. Eligible participants are adult patients initiating a first sequence of long-term glucocorticoids for a medical condition, excluding onco-hematological diseases, severe chronic renal failure and organ transplantation.The nurse-led prevention program consists of the following: (1) a video explaining glucocorticoids-related adverseevents and appropriate preventive measures, (2) a personal meeting with a trained nurse, (3) a consultation with a dietitian, (4) a personalized individual paper sheet summarizing appropriate non-drug preventive measures for the patient, (5) an information paper sheet summarizing appropriate drug preventive measures for the treating physician, and (6) phone calls at week 12, week 24, and week 36 to ensure that the preventive measures are adequately implemented and understood.Patients in both groups receive an information paper sheet summarizing preventive measures related to glucocorticoid therapy. Patients randomized to the control group receive the standard care provided by their physician.The primary endpoint is the burden of glucocorticoid toxicity, evaluated by a blinded investigator using the cumulative worsening score of the Glucocorticoid Toxicity Index (GTI) at 52 weeks. The secondary endpoints are the occurrence of specific glucocorticoid-related adverse events, quality of life, implementation of preventive measures and cumulative glucocorticoid dosage at 52 weeks.DiscussionWe hypothesize that this therapeutic education program will reduce the burden of long-term glucocorticoid toxicity and improve patients’ quality of life. If so, this program could be implemented in routine care for patients receiving long-term glucocorticoid therapy.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov NCT04909606. Background

Long-term glucocorticoid (GC) therapy is widely prescribed for numerous medical conditions, mainly in rheumatology, internal medicine, and pulmonology [1]. The prevalence of long-term GC use in European countries is estimated at 0.5–0.75% [2]. Especially with long-term use, GC can lead to numerous, sometimes severe, adverse events in different organ systems [3–6]. This represents a substantial burden for healthcare systems [7]. GC exposure varies across patients and conditions. Higher cumulative GC exposure is associated with an increased risk of GC-related adverse events [8–10]. Furthermore, long-term users are typically older and present more comorbidities than short-term users, further increasing their risk of GC-related adverse events [11]. However, these findings do not challenge the relevance of long-term prescription of GC. Long-term GC therapy remains the standard treatment for several conditions for which no validated alternative currently exists. For patients requiring long-term GC, a comprehensive and personalized evaluation by the physician is often challenging due to time constraints. As a result, many patients receiving long-term GC therapy do not obtain the recommended information on potential adverse events and fail to benefit from optimal preventive strategies [12]. Consequently, long-term GC users often request additional information about their treatment and its benefit–risk balance [13].

For the World Health Organization Regional Office for Europe, therapeutic patient education is “a structured person-centred learning process that supports individuals living with chronic conditions to self-manage their own health by drawing on their own resources, supported by their carers and families” [14]. It has been shown to be a cost-effective intervention, improving outcomes and quality of life for people living with medical chronic conditions [15, 16]. Management of patients receiving long-term GC therapy should include an assessment of individual risk for GC-related adverse events, taking into account clinical context and medical history, together with the implementation of appropriate preventive measures. Informing patients and their relatives about the prevention of osteoporosis, infections (including vaccination), adrenal insufficiency, adaptation of physical activity, dietary advice, and smoking cessation are all recommended preventive measures in the context of long-term GC therapy [17]. A therapeutic education program on GC could provide long-term benefits for both patients and the community, leading to a significant reduction in adverse events, improvements in patients’ quality of life, and reduced healthcare costs. In recent years, nurse-led prevention and therapeutic education programs have expanded considerably in the management of chronic conditions, particularly in rheumatology [18]. These nurse-led programs are effective, safe for patients, and also appear to be efficient in rheumatoid arthritis [19].

Our aim is to evaluate whether a nurse-led therapeutic education program can reduce the burden of adverse events attributable to long-term GC in adult patients, by comparing it to usual care.

Methods

Trial design and setting

This study is a superiority, open-label, parallel-group, randomized trial, with a 1:1 allocation ratio. Each participant is followed for approximately 12 months (± 2 months). This study is conducted in five French hospitals: Brest University Hospital, La Rochelle Hospital Centre, Le Mans Hospital Centre, Morlaix Hospital Centre, and Puy-en-Velay Hospital Centre (Additional file 1). The study protocol is reported in accordance with the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) reporting guidelines (Additional file 2) [20, 21].

Study population

Patients are eligible to participate if they meet the following inclusion criteria: (1) aged 18 or over, (2) initiation of GC therapy within the 30 days prior to inclusion, (3) predicted cumulative GC dosage at 52 weeks equal to or greater than 2000-mg prednisone equivalent and (4) affiliated with a social security system. Exclusion criteria are as follows: (1) previous prolonged exposure to GC, (2) GC prescribed as part of the management of a malignant tumor (oncological or hematological), (3) chronic renal failure with creatinine clearance < 30 ml/min (according to each center’s calculation method), (4) history of organ transplantation, (5) pregnancy or breastfeeding, (6) inability to understand the protocol or provide consent and (7) legal guardianship. In protocol version 5.0 (approved on July 25, 2024), inability to communicate by phone was added as an exclusion criterion.

Methods of randomization and bias minimization

After obtaining written informed consent, investigators use the Ennov Clinical software to perform randomization and assign participants to study groups. Randomization is stratified by study centre, age (≤ or > 60) and initial dosage of GC (< or ≥ 30 mg prednisone equivalent daily).

Participants and healthcare professionals (physicians, nurses, dietitians and clinical research technicians) are aware of group allocation. However, the primary outcome is assessed by a trained physician blinded to participants’ allocation [22].

Recruitment

Patients aged 18 years or older, receiving care in one of the participating centres and for whom initiation of long-term GC therapy is indicated, may be invited to participate. Eligible patients are identified by their treating physicians (internal medicine, dermatology, pulmonology, hepato-gastroenterology, or rheumatology) during routine care. Partner physicians and their residents identified in each site receive an information leaflet on the study including the contact details of the local research staff. Informed consent is obtained by one of the study nurses (Additional file 3). In cases of uncertainty requiring medical expertise, study nurses may consult a study physician to validate specific eligibility criteria.

Intervention

In both control and interventional groups, patients receive a summary paper sheet recalling general preventive measures associated with long-term GC therapy, based on the European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology (EULAR) recommendations [17]. All concomitant care or interventions are allowed.

Standard of care or control group

Patients randomized to the control group receive the standard care provided by their physician.

Interventional group

In addition to the standard of care, the therapeutic program begins with an educational video presenting information from the following professionals:

Physicians (rheumatologist, internist, endocrinologist, infectiologist) about GC mechanisms of action, available molecules, indications, common adverse advents, and drug- and non-drug-based preventive measures to reduce the risk of complications

Before the individualized interventions, the research team discusses with the patient to adapt the program to their profile, respect their expectations and learning pace, and personalize the nursing interview. Each participant then attends an individual interview with a trained nurse on drug and non-drug measures to prevent GC toxicity. During this interview, the nurse first checks the patient’s understanding of the video, re-explaining unclear elements if necessary and answering any questions. Physical activity is then assessed using the Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ) [23]. The nurse systematically evaluates skin hygiene, smoking, and alcohol consumption and refers the patient to the appropriate local resources if needed. Concomitant medications are reviewed, considering the patient's medical history and current treatments, potential contraindications identified using checklists, and guidelines from learned societies. The nurse explains the rationale for each measure to the patient. Calcium and vitamin D supplementation are determined based on current treatment, estimated dietary calcium intake, and serum vitamin D levels. Vitamin D supplementation recommendations are based on the publication by Annweiler et al. [24]. Indication for a preventive anti-osteoporotic treatment is determined according to the algorithm of the 2014 French recommendations on the prevention and treatment of GC-induced osteoporosis, from the French Society for Rheumatology and the Osteoporosis Research and Information Group [25]. The nurse systematically screens for contraindications to preventive anti-osteoporotic treatments. Based on biological data and ongoing treatments, the nurse recommends potassium supplementation if needed. The nurse also recommends seasonal influenza vaccination, and pneumococcal vaccination for patients who have not previously received it or for whom prior vaccination is no longer considered effective (Additional file 4). Any reluctance expressed by the patient regarding vaccination is documented by the nurse. Other vaccinations in the French vaccination schedule are reviewed according to the recommendations at the time of the interview, to identify vaccinations requiring updating (Additional file 4) [26]. Depending on the GC dosage, the nurse systematically verifies and explains to the patient any contraindications to live vaccines. The nurse also evaluates the need for preventive treatment of strongyloidiasis by inquiring about any history of residence in endemic areas. For dyslipidemia management, the Heart Score tool is used to define the target LDL level [27]. A specific treatment is initiated if necessary. The nurse explains the risk of adrenal insufficiency and methods for tapering long-term GC.

Each patient also has an interview with a dietitian, including a dietary assessment and detailed guidance on personalized dietary recommendations.

After the end of the interviews, each participant receives a personalized summary paper sheet. It summarizes the non-drug measures considered appropriate at the end of the evaluation with the nurse and the dietitian. A second summary sheet from the nurse is provided to the patient’s referent physician. It contains the drug-related measures considered appropriate at the end of the evaluation. The referent physician may then apply the proposed measures if deemed appropriate.

At 12, 24, and 36 weeks (± 15 days) after inclusion, patients are called by the nurse in order to assess the implementation of the recommendations determined at the end of the initial evaluation, reinforce preventive measures if necessary, answer questions and collect any new element related to the GC therapy (Table 1). When necessary, nurses may seek medical advice.

Outcomes

The primary outcome is the change from baseline to week 52 in the cumulative worsening score of the Glucocorticoid Toxicity Index (GTI) (Table 2) [28]. Secondary outcomes include the following: (1) occurrence (yes/no) of at least one clinically significant GC-related complication during the 52-week follow-up (Table 2), (2) change from baseline to week 52 in the score of each GTI domain, (3) change from baseline to week 52 in quality of life, assessed with the SF-36 score [29], (4) proportion of participants who have implemented each preventive measure (seasonal influenza vaccination, pneumococcal vaccination, other vaccinations, vitamin-calcium supplementation, potassium supplementation, anti-osteoporotic treatment, dyslipidemia treatment), and (5) cumulative GC dosage (in mg of prednisone equivalent) estimated at week 52 from patient adherence logs.

Statistical methods

Sample size

We hypothesize that the mean GTI score at 52 weeks would be 60 in the control group, with a standard deviation of 40 [30]. We hypothesize that the mean score would be 40 in the intervention group. Based on the weighting of the various items of the GTI, we therefore determined that an average reduction of 20 points, i.e. one third of the total score, would demonstrate a meaningful benefit of the program. With an alpha risk of 5% and a power of 90%, each group requires 86 participants. Assuming a 10% loss to follow-up over the study period, this trial requires at least 192 patients. Sample size calculations were performed using SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) with the PROC POWER procedure.

Data analysis

Categorical variables (e.g., occurrence of complications, implementation of preventive measures) will be described as absolute numbers and frequencies. Numerical variables (e.g., GTI and SF-36 scores, cumulative GC dosage) will be described using mean, standard deviation, median, Q1, Q3, minimum and maximum, as appropriate. Numerical variables will remain continuous for primary analyses but may be categorized as supporting information to illustrate clinically meaningful thresholds. Student’s t test or the Mann–Whitney U test will be used to compare continuous variables, depending on their distribution. The χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test will be used to compare categorical variables between the interventional group and the control group, according to the conditions of application. Regression models adjusted for randomization stratification factors will be performed, including a random effect for centre. Analyses will be conducted on an intention-to-treat population, meaning that all randomized participants will be analyzed in the group to which they were assigned, regardless of the intervention received or its completeness. Missing data will be reported in each group but will not be imputed. No additional or interim analyses are planned.

Trial oversight and monitoring

In both groups, participants will be monitored by their referring physician during the one-year follow-up as part of routine care. The research team will document any GC toxicity included in the GTI score and occurring during the participants’ one-year follow-up. To enable this assessment, the research team will prospectively collect medical records and therapeutic modifications throughout the follow-up. No systematic collection of adverse events unrelated to GC toxicity is planned.

Biological samples will be managed through routine care pathways. Participating hospitals or community laboratories may collect and analyse samples. No additional biological collection is planned as part of the study.

The coordinating site, located at Brest University Hospital, will oversee the trial coordination. A clinical research associate appointed by the sponsor will ensure study conduct, data collection, documentation, recording, and reporting in compliance with Standard Operating Procedures at Brest University Hospital, Good Clinical Practice, and applicable legal and regulatory requirements. Only site investigators and their authorized personnel will have access to source documents, securely stored at each participating site in locked offices, and to datasets. Data are entered into an electronic case report form (Ennov) and hosted by Brest University Hospital.

A steering committee (AS, DC, and CDM) will provide oversight and support for the study. The sponsor must approve any substantial modification to the protocol proposed by the investigators and must obtain ethical approval before implementation. The sponsor will update the clinical trial registry and will inform participating sites of any protocol modifications. If necessary, the research teams will collect new consent from patients participating in the trial. Any protocol deviation will be fully documented. As this research concerns low-risk intervention, no data monitoring committee is required.

Dissemination policy

A summary of the study findings will be provided to participants. Results will be disseminated to healthcare professionals through publication in a peer-reviewed journal and presentation at scientific conferences. The final primary publication will adhere to the CONSORT 2010 Statement [31], accounting for the specificities of the study design [31]. Authorship will be determined according to the CRediT taxonomy [32]. No ancillary studies or post-trial care are planned.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first randomized clinical trial to evaluate a therapeutic education program for the prevention of long-term GC-related adverse events. This program may represent a viable and effective tool, well accepted by patients, and able to reduce the burden of long-term GC toxicity.

Therapeutic education is important in the management of chronic conditions because it: (1) increases patient empowerment about their condition [14–16], (2) improves quality of life [14], (3) enhances clinical results [14], and (4) optimizes healthcare resources utilization [14, 33]. More specifically, a program designed to prevent GC toxicity may enhance treatment adherence and strengthen the therapeutic alliance [13, 34].

This project has several strengths. First, this multicenter study is conducted in a university hospital that serves as a referral center for many conditions, including autoimmune rheumatic diseases (Brest University Hospital), and in four general hospitals from different French regions, including rural areas (La Rochelle, Le Mans, Morlaix, and Puy-en-Velay). The patient population will therefore be broad and representative of a substantial proportion of the French population initiating long-term GC therapy in hospital settings. Second, the therapeutic education program will be tailored to each participant, an approach shown to yield better results than general programs [35]. Third, this program is a continuous process, combining initial education sessions with regular follow-up calls to identify difficulties in understanding and applying recommendations. In addition, the program combines several approaches (videos and individual interviews), a strategy shown to be more effective than relying on a single approach [15, 16]. Finally, this program involves nurses, physicians from several disciplines, dietitians and physiotherapists, which enabled the development of an evidence-based intervention [14, 36]. Interdisciplinary therapeutic education programs are associated with improved quality of care, enhanced quality of life, and greater satisfaction for both patients and healthcare professionals [37].

This project also has limitations. First, blinding of participants and medical teams is not feasible. However, the primary outcome will be assessed by a blinded evaluator, limiting observation bias [22]. GC toxicity is difficult to capture in a single outcome, as it may involve multiple organ systems, range from mild to life-threatening, and occur in both the short and long term. In some cases, it is also difficult to attribute adverse events directly to GC treatment [38]. For this study, we chose to use the GTI as the primary outcome because it provides an objective and comprehensive measure of GC toxicity [28]. Although relatively recent, the GTI has already been used in several studies across pulmonary, rheumatologic, and dermatological conditions to assess GC toxicity [38]. Long-term GC therapy is not prescribed exclusively in hospitals: between 10.2% and 13.8% of prescriptions are issued by family physicians [1]. The evaluated intervention is carried out in five hospitals, with a recruitment of patients mainly from these centres. The profile of patients and their care may differ between hospital and other settings, which may impact our results. A city-hospital collaboration, through the communautés professionnelles territoriales de santé (CPTS, territorial professional health communities in France) for example, could help extend this program beyond the hospital setting [39].

Even if this program proves efficacy, complementary strategies for informing patients should also be considered. Long-term GC users often seek information from peers in addition to the information provided by healthcare professionals [13]. Group-based education programs are effective therapeutic approaches [14]. Shared experience and peer discussions are key moments for patients to cope with their condition. Furthermore, a therapeutic education program should be coupled with appropriate drug use, including justified indications, treatment duration, and dosage [40]. This appropriate use should be accompanied, when necessary, by preventive measures, for example, osteoporosis prevention in long-term GC therapy (calcium, vitamin D, bisphosphonates, and/or radiological monitoring) [11].

If the program proves its efficacy, an important but challenging next step will be its implementation in routine care, with continuous efforts toward improvement [14, 16]. At the hospital level, the availability of trained nurses, expanded nursing roles in patient management, strong interprofessional collaboration, and institutional recognition will be key elements for the success of such a program [18, 37]. Concomitantly, at a broader scale, health policies in favor of therapeutic education are necessary to enable the dissemination and sustainability of such programs [14]. Patients should be actively involved in refining, implementing, and adapting the program at each new site [14].

Trial status

This article presents the protocol version 5.0 approved on 25 July 2024. Recruitment began in April 2022, and as of July 3, 2025, 127 patients had been enrolled. Recruitment is expected to be completed by October 2025, with the last patient visit planned for October 2026.
