Novel genetic insights into causal effects of potential metformin targets and immune mediation on sepsis
Authors: Li Xiang, Jiayi Sun, Lu Yang, Yanqin Luo, Yiwen Wang, Keling Chen, Xianli Meng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSepsis remains a major global clinical challenge with limited therapies. Metformin shows promising therapeutic potential, likely through immune modulation, yet mechanistic evidence remains scarce. Hence, our study aimed to investigate the causal effect of metformin on sepsis and explore the role of immune function in mediating this effect.MethodsWe first assessed metformin’s causal effect on sepsis using two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR), then employed two-step MR to evaluate immune mediation of this effect. MR-Egger regression was conducted to detect pleiotropy and heterogeneity. Database-based single-cell RNA-seq analysis and sepsis mouse model further validated the MR findings.ResultsTwo-sample MR indicated that metformin use, via PRKAB1 activation, was associated with a reduced risk of sepsis (OR = 0.9743, 95% CI: 0.9538–0.9951, P = 0.0160). Mediation analysis showed that 10 specific immune cell subsets, including CD39 + CD4+%T cell, CD39 + CD4+AC, CD39 + secreting Treg AC, CD14 + CD16+ monocyte AC, CD86 on myeloid DC, CD14 + CD16+ monocyte% monocyte, CD127 on CD28 + CD4+, CD3-lymphocyte %leukocyte, CD127 on T cell and CD127 on CD45RA-CD4 not Treg mediated metformin’s protective effects. Single-cell RNA-seq analysis revealed reduced T cells and monocytes during sepsis, consistent with MR results that identified elevated T cells and monocytes as major protective factors. PRKAB1 and its homologous genes were highly expressed in the mediation-linked immune cells. In septic mice, metformin attenuated lung injury and inflammatory infiltration, concurrently upregulating PRKAB1 expression significantly. mIHC and IHC assays confirmed metformin pretreatment reversed sepsis-induced CD86 elevation and depletion of CD39, CD4 and CD14.ConclusionOur findings implied that metformin might contribute to sepsis protection, potentially involving PRKAB1-mediated modulation of CD39, CD4, CD14 and CD86. These observations could provide clues for developing targeted therapeutic strategies combining metformin with immune modulation for sepsis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13062-026-00769-5.

Introduction

Sepsis is a severe and systemic inflammatory response syndrome caused by infection. It affects 30 million people worldwide and is one of the leading causes of death in critically ill patients globally. Almost any infection may lead to sepsis. In all hospitalizations, the incidence of sepsis has been reported to be up to 1–2%. If effective interventions are not implemented timely, the mortality rates can rapidly exceed above 30–40% [1–3]. Currently, the treatment for sepsis includes infection control (e.g., antibiotic administration), hemodynamic management (e.g., fluid resuscitation), and the host immune response modulation (e.g., immunosuppression) [4]. Although these therapies significantly improved the survival of sepsis, the mortality rate in patients with sepsis remains persisting high, as heterogeneity in sepsis populations is significant. Therefore, gaining deeper insight into the pathogenesis of sepsis and searching for new therapeutic targets and drugs may make a breakthrough in the dilemma of sepsis therapy [4–5].

Recent studies reported that metformin not only exerted an anti-diabetic effect, but also attenuated the lung injury, inflammatory response, liver and cardiac injury, etc. in septic animals [6–8], indicating metformin may be a promising therapeutic agent for treating sepsis and improving survival rate of patients with sepsis. Previous research showed that sepsis would lead to a dysregulation of the immune system, and the remarkable disorders of immune cells and inflammatory cytokines are one of the major causes of mortality in sepsis [9–10]. For example, patients will present with neutrophil dysfunction, abnormal death of immune effector cells and hyperproliferation of immune suppressor cells during sepsis [9, 11]. Therefore, intervention of immune cells (T cells and lymphocytes) and inflammatory cytokines may be beneficial in the treatment of sepsis [12–14]. However, the detailed mechanism remains unknown. Interestingly, a most recent basic study found that metformin modulated macrophages during sepsis, affecting inflammatory response and immunosuppression [15]. In view of the above factors, we hypothesized that immune cells might mediate the beneficial effect of metformin on sepsis. Nevertheless, the current studies of the pathogenesis of sepsis and metformin rely on observational research, which limits our ability to infer causality [16]. Thus, there is also a need to search for more effective methods to explore the causal connection among metformin, immune cells and sepsis.

Mendelian randomization (MR) is a widely used method for causal inferences, which can infer the causality of one trait (exposure) to another (outcome) by using genetic variants. MR relies on the principle that genetic variants are determined at conception and remain largely unaffected by subsequent disease or outcome, thereby minimizing reverse causality. Furthermore, MR is unaffected by the confounders in exposure-outcome relationships, including sociodemographic factors, lifestyle behaviors or health status, thus the risk of bias can be reduced and the efficacy of causal inference can be enhanced [17–18]. Consequently, MR is increasingly becoming a standard tool for prioritizing and validating novel drug targets [19]. Specifically, drug-target MR analysis can explore the effect of long-term modulation of drug targets on sepsis risk. In this approach, naturally occurring genetic variation in the coding gene of drug target (e.g., metformin’s target) serve as proxies to estimate the effect of the long-term pharmacological treatment on disease outcomes [20–21]. This approach, by exploiting the natural sequence variants of the coding gene of drugs target, can be extended to explore the role of perturbation of a drug target [22]. This facilitates insight into the target-based efficacy and adverse effects of drugs. Therefore, drug-target MR analysis can be applied to mimic the effect of pharmacologically modulating a drug target in clinical trials, which has been previously used to predict clinical benefits and adverse effects of therapy interventions [23–25]. Additionally, the advancement of genome-wide association studies (GWAS) and the elucidation of molecular mechanisms provide a solid foundation for conducting MR study.

In our study, expression quantitative trait loci (eQTLs) of two important pharmacologically actionable target genes (ETFDH and PRKAB1) of metformin along with GWAS datasets of sepsis, 731 immune cells and type 2 diabetes were obtained from the IEU OpenGWAS project (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/), accessed on April 8, 2024. We first performed two-sample MR analysis to evaluate the causal relationship between metformin and sepsis. Second, we conducted a two-step MR analysis to determine the potential mediation pathways for immune cells from metformin to sepsis. Considering that MR study alone may not fully elucidate the causal connection among metformin, immune cells and sepsis, single-cell RNA-seq analysis from public databases and in vivo experiments based on sepsis mouse model were employed to support our MR data. This study would provide novel insight into the mechanism underlying the potential therapeutic effect of metformin against sepsis.

Methods

Ethics and study design

Our MR study used publicly available secondary data for analysis. Informed consent was obtained from all participants as per the original studies, and all ethical approvals were obtained by the original investigators. Therefore, this part of the study was exempt from institutional review board review. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Chengdu University of Traditional Chinese Medicine (2024154). The overall study design is illustrated in Fig. 1. Briefly, we first sourced 2 important pharmacologically actionable target genes (ETFDH and PRKAB1) of metformin from DrugBank (https://go.drugbank.com/drugs/DB00331). Then, we obtained GWAS datasets of the expression quantitative trait loci (eQTLs) of ETFDH and PRKAB1 (eqtl-a-ENSG00000171503 and eqtl-a-ENSG00000111725), as well as sepsis, 731 immunocytes and type 2 diabetes from the IEU OpenGWAS project (https://gwas.mrcieu.ac.uk/). We next performed two-sample MR analysis to evaluate the causal relationship between metformin and sepsis. Additionally, a two-step MR analysis was used to determine the potential mediation pathways for immune cells from metformin to sepsis. The results of the present MR study are reported in accordance with the STROBE-MR guidance.

Selection of genetic instruments for eQTLs of metformin target genes and immunocytes

We generated genetic instruments (eQTLs, single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) with significant effects on biomarkers in the vicinity of the gene encoding) to proxy the effect of metformin targets and the immunocytes in sepsis. To select validated genetic instruments, we applied a conventional instrument selection method that has been used in previous drug target MR studies [26–27]. The four core assumptions of the drug target MR studies: (1) instrumental variables strongly associated with exposure (P < 1 × 10− 5) (association hypothesis). Additionally, weak instruments were excluded using an F-statistic > 10. (2) instrumental variables have no direct correlation with outcome and only influence results through exposure, implying no genetic pleiotropy (excludability assumption). The absence of gene pleiotropy was indicated by a statistically insignificant difference between the MR-Egger regression intercept term and 0 (P > 0.05) and a non-significant result of the MR-PRESSO horizontal pleiotropy test (P > 0.05). (3) instrumental variables must be independent of confounding influences (independent hypothesis). Instruments can be thought of as independent of environmental factors such as socioeconomic and cultural factors because the SNPs selected by MR method are subjected to minimal environmental and acquired life effects since they follow the genetic principle of random assignment of parental alleles to offspring. (4) Targets relevance: instrumental variables are located within ± 300 kb of the cis-acting region of the target gene.

Selection of eQTLs instruments for drug target genes

Within the framework of drug-target MR research, the selection of genetic instrumental variables requires a balance among target relevance, the number of instruments, and statistical stability. The LD parameter settings adopted in this study are based on these considerations and align with prevailing practices in current cis-eQTL-based drug-target analyses. In our study, the SNP selection criteria were a genome-wide significance threshold of P < 1 × 10− 5. To ensure independence among the selected SNPs and to eliminate the influence of linkage disequilibrium (LD) on the outcomes, we further pruned them based on an LD coefficient (r²) threshold of 0.3, a window size of 100 kb, and a minor allele frequency (MAF) greater than 0.01 (MAF > 0.01). PhenoScanner (http://www.phenoscanner.medschl.cam.ac.uk/) was used to remove SNPs associated with confounders and outcomes [hypotheses (2) and (3)]. SNPs were extracted within ± 300 kb of the cis-acting region of the drug target gene [hypothesis (4)]. The relevant instrumental variables listed above were extracted from drug target genes’ eQTL data. The SNPs screened out above were extracted from the GWAS summary statistics of the outcome variables (sepsis, immunocyte, and type 2 diabetes), removing palindromic structure and SNPs with MAF > 0.42. SNPs directly associated with the outcome variables (sepsis, immunocyte, and type 2 diabetes) were also eliminated (P < 1 × 10− 5). Abnormal SNPs were eliminated using MR-PRESSO. With the continuous expansion in scale and resolution of cis-eQTL data, the related analytical strategies are also expected to be further refined based on more detailed characterization of genetic architecture.

Selection of instrumental variables for immunocytes

Selection criteria was set as P < 1 × 10− 5, and the selection hypothesis (1) was: the LD coefficient r2 <0.001 and the LD region width was 10,000 kb, ensuring that the SNPs were independent of each other to eliminate LD’s influence on the outcomes. PhenoScanner was used to eliminate SNPs associated with confounders and outcomes [hypotheses (2) and (3)]. The relevant SNPs screened out above were extracted from the GWAS summary data of the outcome variable (sepsis), removing palindromic structure and SNPs with MAF > 0.42. SNPs (P < 1 × 10− 5) directly associated with the outcome variable (sepsis) were eliminated. Abnormal SNPs were eliminated using MR-PRESSO.

MR mediation analysis of immunocytes in metformin-sepsis improvement

Mediation effect refers to the indirect causal effect of an exposure on an outcome via a specific mediator. Using genetic variants as instrumental variables, this approach minimizes confounding and reverse causation, allowing the separation of direct and indirect effects. The effects of any exposure on an outcome can be divided into direct and indirect (mediation) effects. Both the direct effect of metformin on sepsis and the mediated effect mediated by immune cells were analyzed by two-step MR. The mediating role of immune cells in the effect of metformin on sepsis was obtained by β1 × β2, where β1 is the MR effect of metformin on immunocytes and β2 is the MR effect of immunocytes on sepsis. The proportion (E%) of the mediating effect was calculated using the following formula:

, where β3 is the MR effect of metformin on sepsis (i.e., “total effect”).

Five regression models were employed: MR-Egger regression, random-effect inverse-variance weighted (IVW), weighted median estimator (WME), weighted model and simple model. With SNPs used as instrumental variables, a two-sample MR analysis was applied to evaluate any potential causal relationships between the exposure (metformin) and risk of outcomes (sepsis). First, SNPs related to exposure factors was used to estimate their causal impact on mediating factors. Second, SNPs of mediator variable were utilized to evaluate their causal impact on outcome measures. Then, the direct influence of exposure factors on sepsis was assessed. Finally, the indirect effect of the exposure on the outcome variables and the mediation ratio were estimated through intermediary factors. IVW is currently the most prevalent and is generally used as the main method for causal estimation. When SNPs ≤ 3, the effect of a single SNP on the outcome is evaluated using the Wald ratio, and the rest using the fixed-effect IVW. When > 3 SNPs are included, random-effect IVW was applied. When estimating causal effects using multiple SNPs as instrumental variables, IVW applies the inverse of the variance (R^2) of each locus as weights, and weighted the causal effect estimates of each locus and then summed. The final estimate was used as the causal effect estimates for the IVW method. MR-Egger intrinsically employs a weaker hypothetical premise (InSIDE) to accomplish causal effect estimation based on IVW. To ascertain the causal relationship between exposure and outcome, MR-Egger was used to detect and correct the bias introduced by the pleiotropy of instrumental variables by incorporating a regression intercept. The findings of MR-Egger may be consulted when horizontal pleiotropy is present. We employed IVW to determine the causal relationship between variables and used MR-Egger regression as a supplementary analysis to IVW. By computing Cochran’s Q test, SNP heterogeneity was ascertained. There were heterogenous outcomes when P < 0.05. An additional statistic utilized to quantify heterogeneity is I2 (I-squared), which indicates the proportion of variation attributable to heterogeneity in overall variation. The I2 value lies between 0% and100%. An I2 value > 50% suggests the existence of heterogeneity. The MR-Egger method and MR-PRESSO were used for pleiotropy analysis. Difference of MR-Egger regression intercept and zero were not statistically significant (P > 0.05) and P > 0.05 for MR-PRESSO, indicating that the SNPs were not pleiotropic. We performed a sensitivity analysis using leave-one-out (LOO), gradually removing each SNP, then re-analyzing the remaining SNPs, to observe the effect size of each SNP. All analyses described above were performed using the TwoSample MR package in R 4.1.0 software, with a statistical significance level of α = 0.05.

Evaluation of causal associations between type 2 diabetes and PRKAB1 agonist metformin (positive control)

MR-Egger regression, IVW, WME, weighted model and simple model were employed. The causal association between metformin and positive control (type 2 diabetes) was validated using eQTLs of the metformin target gene PRKAB1 as instrumental variables. The methods of causal effect value estimation, heterogeneity, pleiotropic effects, and sensitivity analysis can be referred to the method described above.

Single cell analysis for validation of MR assumptions

We downloaded the single-cell expression matrices of sepsis from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (accession number: GSE167363). A total of 12 samples of single-cell RNA sequencing data were included, including 10 samples of sepsis patients and 2 samples of healthy subjects. To eliminate low-quality cells, we filtered out cells by the following parameters (nFeature > 200 & nFeature < 5000; mitochondrial genes percentage MT < 20). The total number of cells before filtering was 61,695 (15912 cells from the control, 45783 cells from sepsis group). After QC metric filtering, we retained a total of 59,645 cells for downstream analysis (15740 cells from the control, 43905 cells from sepsis group). All downstream single-cell analysis was carried out using R package Seurat (version 5). Canonical correlation analysis (CCA) was performed to correct for batch effects and to integrate different samples within each dataset. Top 2000 highly variable genes (HVGs) are used for dimensionality reduction by principal component analysis (PCA). Top 30 principal components were selected for tSNE and UMAP visualization. Cluster analysis was performed using “FindNeighbors” and “FindClusters” functions with a resolution of 0.5. Using Wilcoxon-Rank sum test, differential gene expression analysis of each cluster was performed with the “FindAllMarkers” function. Using the obtained differential gene, cell type identification was performed by the “CellMarker 2.0” (http://117.50.127.228/CellMarker/index.html) and classical cell type marker genes. Then major cell types were annotated using SingleR (Human Primary Cell Atlas Data.rds). Dimplot, FeaturePlot, DotPlot were used for visualization of cell atlas and associated genes. Finally, the gene expression distributions of PRKAA1, PRKAA2, PRKAB1, PRKAB2, PRKAG1, and PRKAG2 in sepsis and controls were visualized by VlnPlot and FeaturePlot.

Animals

All animal procedures were conducted under a protocol approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Chengdu University of Traditional Chinese Medicine, and were performed in accordance with the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments (ARRIVE) guidelines and the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. Male C57BL/6 mice (7–8 weeks old) were purchased from SPF (Beijing) Biotechnology Co., Ltd. All mice were group-housed in filtered cages in a temperature-controlled room with a 12:12 light-dark cycle with free access to food and water. Efforts were made to minimize animal suffering and the number of animals used.

Metformin pretreatment and sepsis mouse model

Mice were randomly divided into 3 groups, with five mice in each group. Metformin (dimethylbiguanide hydrochloride, Sigma-Aldrich, 317240, St. Louis, MO, USA) was dissolved in sterile PBS (20 mg/mL). Mice in metformin group received metformin once daily by oral gavage for 35 days at 200 mg/kg body weight, a dose that has been reported to be safe for long-term rodent administration [28]. Mice in both the control and model groups received PBS at 0.1 mL/10 g body weight in the same manner. Mice were monitored daily and body weight measured once a week over 5 weeks.

At the end of the 5-weeks metformin pre-treatment, mice in metformin group and model group were anesthetized with Avertin® (tribromoethanol, 250 mg/kg) and challenged with lipopolysaccharide (LPS, Escherichia coli 055:B5; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA; 10 mg/kg body weight, dissolved in sterile saline, 8 mg/mL) by intratracheal instillation to develop sepsis mouse model, following our published methods [29]. After 24 h, the mice were anesthetized with 1% pentobarbital sodium (50 mg/kg) and sacrificed. Half of the lung tissue collected from mice was fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and the other half was stored at -152 °C until further assays.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted from freshly frozen lung tissues of the mouse using TRIzol (Invitrogen™, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The concentration and purity of total RNA were determined using a NanoDrop 2000 Ultramicro-Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). Reverse transcription of total RNA was performed using the ExonScript® RT SuperMix with dsDNase kit (Exongen Biotechnology Co. Ltd.). qRT-PCR was performed using UltraStart Universal SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix kit (Exongen Biotechnology Co. Ltd.) and detected on the LightCycler® 480 Real-Time PCR System (Roche). The sequence of the forward primer for PRKAB1 is TTGAACAAGGACACGGGCATCTC, and reverse primer sequence is GCACTGAGCACCATCACTCCATC. GAPDH was used as a reference gene (Forward: 5’-GGTTGTCTCCTGCGACTTCA-3’, Reverse: 5’-TGGTCCAGGGTTTCTTACTCC-3’). The cycling parameters were as follows: stage 1, 95 °C for 3 min; stage 2, 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s; stage 3, 95 °C for 5 min, 50 °C for 1 min and 40 °C for 30 s. Fold change of mRNA expression of PRKAB1 were calculated using the 2−ΔΔct method and normalized to GAPDH.

Western blot

Protein lysates were extracted from lung tissues of the mouse using 1 × RIPA lysis buffer (Boster, AR0105, China) supplemented with 1:100 protease inhibitor cocktail (Boster, AR1192, China) and 1:100 phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Boster, AR1183, China). Lysates were incubated on ice for 30 min and cleared by centrifugation at 16,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. Protein concentration was determined by BCA assay (Boster, AR0146, China) with bovine serum albumin as standard. Equal amounts of protein (20 µg) were resolved by SDS‑PAGE on 10% Bis‑tris gradient gels (Biosharp, BL565B, China) under reducing conditions and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore, IPVH00010) using a wet-transfer system (Bio‑Rad). Membranes were blocked with 5% non‑fat dry milk in TBST (Tris‑buffered saline with 0.1% Tween‑20) for 1 h at room temperature, followed by incubation with primary antibodies diluted in blocking buffer overnight at 4 °C. Antibodies used included: anti‑AMPKβ1 (Huabio, SD082-06, rabbit monoclonal, 1:500), anti-phospho-AMPKβ1 (CST, 23021, rabbit monoclonal, 1:1000) and anti‑β‑Tubulin (Servicebio, GB152667, rabbit monoclonal, 1:10000). After three washes with TBST, membranes were incubated with HRP‑conjugated secondary antibodies (Huabio, HA1001, Goat anti-rabbit polyclonal, 1:50000) for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were developed using enhanced chemiluminescence substrate (Supersensitive ECL Kit, Oriscience, PD203-120) and captured on SOPI-SW0002 ChemiStudio imaging system. Band intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.

Histology and immunostaining

Standard protocols were used for histology, conventional immunohistochemistry (IHC), and multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC). Mice were sacrificed 24 h after LPS challenge. The left lung tissues were removed and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight, then embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm. Sections were dewaxed, hydrated and then stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE) for histological evaluation. H&E slides were scanned using Pannoramic MIDI/SCAN 150 (3DHISTECH, Hungary). Section (4 μm) were prepared to perform IHC and mIHC assays. For IHC assays, paraffin sections were dewaxed, hydrated, antigen repaired with sodium citrate for 15 min. Endogenous peroxidase activity was then blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide followed by 3% BSA blocking for 30 min. After incubation with CD86 primary antibody (Proteintech, 13395-1-AP, 1:400) at 4 °C overnight, the sections were incubated with secondary antibody (ab205718, Abcam, 1:2000) for 50 min at room temperature, rinsed with PBS, and then perform DAB staining and hematoxylin counterstaining. Slides were imaged under a light microscope (NIKON). For multiplex immunofluorescence staining, after dewaxing, hydrating, antigen repairing and blocking with BSA, sections were incubated with the specific primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. Following washing three times with PBS, sections were incubated with 480-TSA, 520-TSA, 570-TSA, 620-TSA or 690-TSA-conjugated secondary anti-bodies at room temperature for 1 h, respectively, and then washed with PBS. After five rounds of primary and secondary antibodies incubations, DAPI nuclear staining (Beyotime, C1018, China) was performed at room temperature for 10 min. The following primary antibodies were used to perform immunostaining: anti-CD4 (Abcam, ab183685, rabbit monoclonal, 1:1000), anti-CD14 (CST, #93882, rabbit monoclonal, 1:500), anti-CD16a (S-RMab, S0B2184, rabbit monoclonal, 1:1000), anti-CD39 (CST, #14481, rabbit monoclonal, 1:400), and anti-CD127 (HuaBio, HA721214, rabbit monoclonal, 1:400). As secondary antibodies, peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Abcam, ab205718, 1:2000) were used. IHC and mIHC were each performed with appropriate positive and negative controls in every batch. Pannoramic MIDI imaging system (3D HISTECH, Hungary) was then used to acquire immunofluorescence images. Quantitative analysis of protein expression in whole sample slices was conducted using the multiplex IHC module of the HALO digital pathology software (v4.0, Indica Labs, Albuquerque, NM, USA).

Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA). Results are represented as mean ± SD unless otherwise noted. Comparison between were made using an unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t-test (two-tailed). One‑way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post-hoc test was performed for multigroup comparisons. Differences were considered statistically significant when P < 0.05, and P < 0.01 was highly significant.

Results

Causal association between metformin and sepsis and the mediating role of immune cells

MR analyses of causal association between metformin and sepsis

With eQTLs of metformin target genes used as instrumental variables, the causal association between metformin and sepsis was used for MR analysis based on the sepsis dataset ieu-b-4980. The results from IVW method indicate that metformin, as the agonists of PRKAB1 but not of ETFDH, have been linked to a decreased incidence risk of sepsis (OR = 0.9743, 95% CI: 0.9538–0.9951, P = 0.0160). IVW analysis shows no heterogeneity between PRKAB1 agonists and sepsis-related eQTLs (I² = 0%, Cochran’s Q = 46.0854, P = 0.9761). The difference of MR-Egger regression intercept and zero was not statistically significant (P = 0.9810), and MR-PRESSO did not detect significant horizontal pleiotropy (P = 0.9650). Thus, we concluded that the SNPs do not have horizontal pleiotropy. The results are detailed in Table S5. The leave-one-out analysis, funnel plots, and scatter plots for sensitivity assessment have been provided as supplementary materials (Fig. S2-S4 in Supplementary Material S1). To assess whether the cis-eQTL and sepsis GWAS signals share a causal variant, we also performed a colocalization analysis confined to the gene’s cis-regulatory region. The results were inconclusive (posterior odds ratio between different potential causal configurations = 0.9898), revealing no clearly dominant causal configuration. Consequently, we present this analysis as supplementary information regarding the genetic architecture, rather than as definitive evidence for colocalization (Table S9).

PRKAB1 agonist metformin mediates sepsis pathogenesis via immunocytes

Immune cells may be a mediator of the reduction of sepsis pathogenesis by PRKAB1 agonist. A two-step MR approach was performed to investigate the mediation pathway from PRKAB1 agonists to sepsis via immunocytes in this study. We employed 731 immunophenotypes solely for the initial exploratory screening, aiming to identify immune signals potentially associated with the exposure, rather than reporting all of them as conclusive findings of equal hierarchical significance.

First, we used the metformin target gene PRKAB1’s eQTLs to estimate the causal effect of PRKAB1 agonist metformin on immune cells (731 immunocyte-associated SNPs). The IVW analysis showed the causal associations between PRKAB1 agonists and 191 immunocytes were statistically significant. Among them, PRKAB1 agonists had a positive effect on 82 different immune cells (Table S6). Take CD14+CD16+ monocyte absolute count (AC) as an example, PRKAB1 agonists increased the number of CD14+CD16+ monocyte AC (OR = 1.0527, 95% CI: 1.0080–1.0993, P = 0.0202; Table S6). IVW results show no heterogeneity between PRKAB1 agonists and immunocytes CD14+CD16+ monocyte AC-related SNPs (I² = 0%, Cochran’s Q = 33.0163, P = 0.9997; Table S6). The difference between MR-Egger regression intercept and zero was not statistically significant (P = 0.6518), and MR-PRESSO did not detect significant horizontal pleiotropy (P = 0.9995). Based on this we conclude that the SNPs do not have horizontal pleiotropy. The results of other immune cells positively affected by PRKAB1 agonists are summarized in Table S6.

Second, 731 immunocyte-associated SNPs were used to evaluate the causative influence on the risk of sepsis. The IVW analysis revealed a statistically significant causal association between 59 immunocytes and sepsis. Among them, 32 immunocytes were protective factors against sepsis (Table S7). With CD14+CD16+ monocyte AC as an example, it showed the protection against sepsis from the following data: OR = 0.9390, 95% CI: 0.8949–0.9853, P = 0.0103 (Table S7). No heterogeneity was observed between CD14+CD16+ monocyte AC and sepsis-associated SNPs (I2 =0%, Cochran’s Q = 8.2837, P = 0.8246; Table S7). Differences between MR-Egger regression intercept and zero was not statistically significant (P = 0.2982), and MR-PRESSO did not detect significant horizontal pleiotropy (P = 0.6785). We therefore considered SNPs do not exhibit horizontal pleiotropy. The results of other immune cells causally associated with sepsis are detailed in Table S7.

Combined the results of the first two steps, it can be found that 15 immunocytes have a significant causal relationship with both PRKAB1 agonists and sepsis (Fig. 2).

We then calculated the mediating effect of immune cells under the condition of it as mediators, and the results are shown in Table 2. When the total effect and mediating effect are in the same direction and the mediator accounted for > 5%, the ratio of the mediating effect can be available, that is, the ratio of the mediating effect to the total effect. The negative mediation proportion represents the share of the negative indirect effect in the total effect and occurs when indirect and direct effects oppose each other. Therefore, the top 10 immune cells (CD39 + CD4+%T cell, CD39 + CD4+AC, CD39 + secreting Treg AC, CD14 + CD16+ monocyte AC, CD86 on myeloid DC, CD14 + CD16+ monocyte% monocyte, CD127 on CD28 + CD4+, CD3-lymphocyte %leukocyte, CD127 on T cell and CD127 on CD45RA-CD4 not Treg) could act as mediators of the relationship between PRKAB1 agonists and sepsis from the results in Table 2. The mediation effects of the top 10 immune cells are shown in Fig. 3. It is also clear from our data that the total effect of PRKAB1 agonists on sepsis was estimated to be -0.0261, direction coinciding with the mediating effect of the top 10 immunocytes. Furthermore, the percentage of mediating effect of the top 10 immunocytes were all larger than 5%, and shown in Table 2; Fig. 3. Therefore, CD39 + CD4+%T cell, CD39 + CD4+AC, CD39 + secreting Treg AC, CD14 + CD16+ monocyte AC, CD86 on myeloid DC, CD14 + CD16+ monocyte% monocyte, CD127 on CD28 + CD4+, CD3-lymphocyte %leukocyte, CD127 on T cell and CD127 on CD45RA-CD4 not Treg could be the key mediator of PRKAB1 agonist metformin regulation of sepsis pathogenesis. We also found CD86 on myeloid DC might be a risk factor for sepsis, and the other nine immunocytes were protective factor against sepsis.

It is important to emphasize that for this part of the research, we distinguished between exploratory screening and subsequent focused analyses in terms of both analytical approach and statistical interpretation, and applied multiple testing correction only within the final set of conclusive analyses. We did not uniformly apply FDR correction across all 731 immunophenotypes, as they served an exploratory screening role in our study design. This analytical strategy reflects a statistical balance between sensitivity and false discovery control, the implications of which have been evaluated through subsequent focused analyses and multiple robustness tests. Besides, negative mediation proportions or values exceeding 100% in mediation analysis typically occur when the direction of the mediated effect is inconsistent with that of the total effect, or when effect estimates are statistically unstable. In our study, such results are presented solely as direct outcomes of statistical computation and are not interpreted as reflecting a biologically meaningful negative mediation mechanism. Importantly, our mediation analysis was primarily intended to assess the potential contribution of immune factors to the association between exposure (PRKAB1 agonist metformin) and outcome (sepsis), rather than to draw definitive conclusions about specific mediation mechanisms.

Causation analysis of PRKAB1 agonist metformin and type 2 diabetes

Causation of PRKAB1 agonist and type 2 diabetes (positive control) were analyzed using MR based on the type 2 diabetes dataset. The results of the instrumental variable validation analysis of PRKAB1 agonists were consistent with the meta-analysis of randomized-controlled trials (i.e., direction of action), suggesting that the genetic tools selected for PRKAB1 agonists are rational. According to IVW data, PRKAB1 agonist metformin was associated with a lower incidence of type 2 diabetes (OR = 0.9381, 95% CI: 0.8881–0.9909, P = 0.0381; Table S8). IVW analysis results show no heterogeneity between PRKAB1 agonists and SNPs associated with type 2 diabetes (I²= 0%, Cochran’s Q = 19.2945, P = 0.5663). Difference between MR-Egger regression intercept and zero was not statistically significant (P = 0.1307), and MR-PRESSO did not detect significant horizontal pleiotropy (P = 0.5480). Hence, we concluded that SNPs do not have horizontal pleiotropy. Other results are available with the same description and the results are detailed in Table S8.

Mediator immune cells are enriched in sepsis tissue with higher PRKAB1 expression

Using classical markers for cell type annotation, such as CD79A for B cells; CD3D and CD4 for T cells; CD14 for monocytes; FECR1A for dendritic cells (DCs); PPBP and RAB27B for megakaryocytes; FCGR3B for neutrophils; and PRF1 for natural killer (NK) cells, we identified the major cell types as illustrated in Fig. 4A. A total of 10 cell types were annotated: B cells, T cells, monocytes, NK cells, megakaryocytes, neutrophils, erythroid-like cells, DCs, platelets, and plasma cells (Fig. 4B). Analysis of cell proportions revealed a decrease in the relative abundance of T cells, monocytes, B cells, and DCs in sepsis compared to healthy controls (Fig. 4C), while megakaryocytes, neutrophils, and erythroid-like cells showed an increase relative to the control. Interestingly, the mediation analyses of our MR revealed that elevated T cells and monocytes are major protective factors for sepsis, which means T cells and monocytes were reduced during sepsis. This result was compatible with the results from our single-cell RNA-seq dataset analysis. We also analyzed the gene expression density distribution of PRKAB1 and its homologous genes (PRKAA1, PRKAA2, PRKAB2, PRKAG1, PRKAG2) in the cellular atlas. Metformin upregulates PRKAB1, p-AMPKβ1, CD39, CD4, and CD14 but downregulates CD86 in lung of a sepsis mouse model

To further validate the role of metformin in sepsis, we employed an LPS-induced sepsis mouse model. The survival data show that mice treated with metformin demonstrated a higher survival rate (87.5%) compared to the model group (62.5%) (Fig. S1). We found that lung tissues from septic mice appeared with increased tissue injuries and inflammatory cell infiltration compared with the control group. Metformin was indeed able to decrease LPS-induced lung damage and inhibit extensive infiltration of inflammatory cells (Fig. 5A). We also examined the expression level of the PRKAB1 gene and the activation of its encoded protein, AMPKβ1, in the lung tissues of mice. The results showed that the expression of both PRKAB1 gene and p-AMPKβ1 was decreased in lung tissue of septic mice (P < 0.05), and metformin pretreatment significantly increased the expression levels of both (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5B and C). This is consistent with the results of our previous MR analyses. Furthermore, we detected the expression of CD39, CD4, CD14, CD16a, CD127 and CD86 using mIHC and IHC. The results showed that CD86 is highly expressed in the lung tissues from septic mice compared with control group (P < 0.01). Pretreatment with metformin obviously decreases the level of CD86 compared the model group (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5D, E and F). The multicolor immunofluorescence analysis revealed downregulated CD39, CD4, and CD14 within the lungs of septic mice compared with the lungs of control mice (P < 0.05 or P < 0.01); and metformin significantly elevated the reduced CD39, CD4, and CD14 in septic mice (P < 0.05 or P < 0.001). Besides, CD16a and CD127 levels did not change significantly during sepsis, and metformin pretreatment could significantly up-regulate the CD127 expression of septic mice (Fig. 6). These results largely validated those found through our mediation analysis.

Discussion

The search for potent anti-sepsis drugs remains a significant challenge. While existing clinical studies and laboratory research points to metformin’s therapeutic potential, its causal relationship with sepsis remains unclear. To address this, we first employed a two-sample MR to investigate this potential causality and mediation MR to explore the possible mediating role of the immune cells in the effects of metformin on sepsis. We further combined single-cell RNA-seq analysis based on database and sepsis-mouse-model-based animal experiments to validate our MR results. Our drug-target MR analyses unveiled that metformin, as the agonist of PRKAB1 but not of ETFDH, is causally associated with a decreased rate of sepsis. Subsequently, 15 immune cells types were found to have a significant causal relationship with both PRKAB1 agonist and sepsis by two-step MR analysis. Further analysis of the mediating effect screened the top 10 immune cells (CD39 + CD4+%T cell, CD39 + CD4+AC, CD39 + secreting Treg AC, CD14 + CD16+ monocyte AC, CD86 on myeloid DC, CD14 + CD16+ monocyte% monocyte, CD127 on CD28 + CD4+, CD3-lymphocyte %leukocyte, CD127 on T cell and CD127 on CD45RA-CD4 not Treg), which could act as mediators of the relationship between PRKAB1 agonist (metformin) and sepsis. Finally, the MR findings were validated through single-cell RNA-seq analysis and in vivo experiments. Collectively, our study provides genetic and experimental evidence that the PRKAB1 agonist metformin may causally ameliorate sepsis, potentially by modulating key immune features such as downregulation of CD86 and upregulation of CD39, CD4 and CD14.

Metformin’s role in sepsis treatment

Previous findings demonstrate that metformin intervention is beneficial for the sepsis treatment. For example, some cohort studies have reported that metformin treatment significantly decreased the mortality rate in septic patients or diabetic patients with sepsis [30–31]. A meta-analysis indicated an association between metformin use before admission and lower mortality in septic adult patients with diabetes mellitus [32]. Several animal studies also suggested metformin use effectively increased survival rate and innate immune capacity of septic mice [33–34]. Such studies also found that metformin could protect against sepsis-induced oxidative damage [35], and attenuate sepsis-induced neuronal injury, cognitive impairment [36] and liver injury [7] in septic rats. However, whether metformin has a causal role in sepsis improvement, and what then is the mediator of the metformin-sepsis link, both are interesting questions that remain to be addressed. To further elucidate the causal association of metformin treatment and sepsis and repurpose existing targets and agents for new potential therapeutic uses for sepsis, we performed this drug-target MR analysis and subsequent experimental validation.

AMPK signaling pathway and PRKAB1 in metformin’s action

In the drug-target MR analysis, ETFDH and PRKAB1, 2 pharmacologically actionable target genes of metformin were selected for analysis from DrugBank. From the MR results, only PRKAB1 activation has been found to causally associated with a decreased incidence risk of sepsis. Among the various potential effector targets of metformin identified, AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK), a major cellular energy sensor and master regulator of metabolic homeostasis, has been placed at center stage [37–39]. AMPK is a heterotrimeric complex consisting of catalytic α subunits (AMPKα1 and AMPKα2, encoded by PRKAA1 and PRKAA2, respectively), regulatory β subunits (AMPKβ1 and AMPKβ2, encoded by PRKAB1 and PRKAB2, respectively), and γ subunits (AMPKγ1, AMPKγ2, and AMPKγ3, encoded by PRKAG1, PRKAG2, and PRKAG3, respectively) in mammals. As an encoding gene of the AMPKβ1, PRKAB1 plays an important role in AMPK biological function, such as blood glucose control and insulin resistance regulation. As previously reported, targeted activation of AMPKβ1 suppressed antipsychotic (olanzapine)-induced hyperglycemia in mice [40]. Zheng et al. have found that WW domain-binding protein 2 (WBP2) overexpression prevents diet-induced liver steatosis and insulin resistance through AMPKβ1 [41]. Interestingly, there is evidence that sepsis-induced acute lung injury was associated with downregulation of AMPKα1/α2 and AMPKβ1 phosphorylation reduction, and A769662 treatment ameliorated sepsis-induced acute lung injury in adult mice. This protective effect was associated with nuclear phosphorylation of AMPKα1/α2 and AMPKβ1 [42]. This study indicated that pharmacological AMPK activation might be beneficial in sepsis treatment, which also exactly supports our analysis results. Thus, selecting PRKAB1 as the target gene for our MR analysis would be rational, and our results are also shown to be reliable. Importantly, in the subsequent animal experiments, we found that PRKAB1 gene was downregulated in septic mice, and pretreatment with metformin up-regulated the sepsis-induced PRKAB1 gene reduction. This further demonstrates PRKAB1 gene is indeed a potential therapeutic target for metformin in sepsis treatment in our study.

Identification of key immune cells involved in metformin-mediated sepsis protection

Then what is responsible for the potential mediating role between PRKAB1 gene activator metformin and sepsis improvement? Increasing evidence supports a central role of the immune system in sepsis. For example, some excellent review papers have summarized recent advances in the regulatory effects of immune system in sepsis [43–44]. As studies illustrated, sepsis is characterized by a host immune dysfunction accompanying both pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory (immunosuppressing) response that affect all types of immune cells and their related compartments. Therefore, to explore whether immune cells play an important mediating role in sepsis improvement by metformin, we performed the two-step MR analysis. Our MR results found 10 immune cells could play an important mediating role in linking PRKAB1 agonist metformin to sepsis. Among these, CD39 + CD4+%T cell, CD39 + CD4+AC, CD39 + secreting Treg AC, CD14 + CD16+ monocyte AC, CD14 + CD16+ monocyte% monocyte, CD127 on CD28 + CD4+, CD3-lymphocyte %leukocyte, CD127 on T cell and CD127 on CD45RA-CD4 not Treg could be potential protective factors for sepsis, and CD86 on myeloid DC emerges as a risk factor for sepsis.

These findings are basically consistent with previous studies, which has proved that PRKAB1 activator metformin indeed has important effects on immune system. For instance, researches indicated that metformin reprograms the tumor immune microenvironment by polarizing macrophages toward an M1 phenotype, enhancing DCs and cytotoxic lymphocyte infiltration (including CD8 + T, Vγ9Vδ2 T, and CD20 + B cells), and attenuating CD4 + CD25+ regulatory T cell function [45–51]. These effects are mediated through AMPK activation [46–48], which also triggers PD-L1 degradation, restores metabolic support for T cells (e.g., tryptophan availability), and synergizes with PD-1 blockade to promote vascular normalization and improve response to immunotherapy [48, 49, 52]. Additional studies highlight its role in enhancing NK cell cytotoxicity and modulating chemokines like CXCL1 [53]. Although the immune cells reported in these studies do not fully overlap with those found in our investigation, they collectively support metformin’s immunomodulatory effects, which are indeed closely associated with AMPK activation.

Single-cell RNA-seq analysis supports the role of T cells and monocytes in sepsis protection

While substantial research has documented the immunomodulatory effects of metformin in oncology, evidence regarding its specific mechanisms in sepsis remains limited so far as we know. To address this gap and validate our genetic findings, we further perform a single-cell RNA-seq analysis based on scRNA-seq datasets from patients with sepsis. Results of our analysis indicate that relative abundance of T cells, monocytes, B cells, and DCs in sepsis were reduced compared to healthy controls. The results are, in general terms, in good agreement with our MR data, which indicated that increased T cells and monocytes are major protective factors for sepsis. Importantly, we further found the high expression of PRKAB1 and its homologous genes in monocytes, T cells and NK cells of sepsis. These immune cells happen to be the mediator immune cells obtained from our MR analyses. These results provide more credible evidence for the potential immunomodulatory effects of metformin for sepsis.

Validation of PRKAB1 and immune markers in sepsis via in vivo studies

In the following in vivo studies, we examined the amounts and co-expression of several key immune cell markers by IHC and mIHC of the mediator immune cell identified from the MR analysis. The results revealed that CD86 is highly expressed, and CD39, CD4, and CD14 were downregulated in septic mice, which are consistent with findings from previous studies. As studied by Fu Y, et al., CD86 gene polymorphisms rs1129055 and rs1915087 may increase the risk of sepsis in a Chinese Han population [54]. Khamri W, et al. found high concentrations of soluble CD86 in sera of patients with ALF and mice with liver injury [55]. Some studies also found that CD86 was high expression in cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) induced septic rats [56], in the spleen of lethal mouse CLP model of sepsis [57], and in the DCs isolated from septic mice [58], or in a murine model of septic peritonitis induced by vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus faecium [59]. For CD39, recent clinical studies have suggested that low CD39 expression on monocytes or low level of CD39+ Tregs predicts poor prognosis in adult or paediatric patients with sepsis [60–61]. Low CD4 expression or the depletion and dysfunction of CD4+ T-cell were discovered in sepsis long ago. An observational study has found that decrease of CD4-lymphocytes and CD14-monocytes apoptosis are characteristic alterations in sepsis [62]. Consistently, other studies also found that CD4+ T-cell dysfunction [63], extensive depletion of splenic CD4 cells [64], and diminished autoantigen-specific naive CD4 T cells [65] were key hallmarks in sepsis patients or septic mice. For CD14, there are conflicting reports. Although Yekebas et al. found a down-regulation of CD14 expression on monocytes in porcine pancreatogenic sepsis [66], several groups have reported levels of CD14++CD16+ monocytes positively correlate with disease severity of severe sepsis and septic shock [67], or circulating CD14 were elevated through P2 × 7 receptor activation in sepsis [68]. Another study also showed CD14 inhibition improves survival and attenuates thrombo-inflammation and cardiopulmonary dysfunction in a baboon model of Escherichia coli sepsis [69]. The reason for these contradictory results is currently unclear for us, and more research is needed to explain this discrepancy. Thus, most of the previous findings enumerated above basically fit well with our MR results, which provides evidence that CD86 on myeloid DC is a potential risk factor for sepsis whereas CD39, CD4, and CD14 are protective factors.

Notably, we also found that metformin could decrease the elevated CD86 expression, and increase the reduced expression of CD39, CD4, and CD14 significantly in septic mice. This result also basically matched well with recent studies. Several studies showed that metformin could significantly down-regulate co-stimulatory factors such as CD54, CD80, and CD86 in DCs [70], or inflammatory markers like TLR4, CD80, CD86 in obese mice [71]. Other studies also found that metformin pretreatment inhibited CD86 expression in advanced glycation end products (AGEs) response [72], and suppressed diabetes-induced CD86 and inflammation in rats [73]. At present, the work concerning the role of metformin on CD39, CD4 and CD14 is scarce. But some studies have shown that CD39 is an important target for modulating sepsis, and enhanced expression of CD39 attenuated sepsis-induced liver injury [74] and kidney epithelial cell injury [75]. For CD4, Turpin R et al. found that metformin facilitated DC maturation, increasing the synergistic stimulation of CD4+ T cells and thus promoting antitumor immunity [76]. Other studies also showed metformin increased the levels of CD4+ lymphocytes in triple-negative breast cancer [77] or in a mouse model that resembles some aspects of the human polycystic ovary syndrome [78]. Furthermore, metformin was found to increase the expression of CD14 mRNA in human THP-1 monocyte cells [79]. Together, these lines of findings evidenced that metformin may regulate sepsis outcomes through effects on CD86, CD39, CD4 and CD14.

Strengths and limitations

There are some strengths worth affirmation in the present study. First, this is the first drug target MR study to systematically examined the causal associations between metformin target gene and sepsis, and the potential immune cells that regulate both processes and act as mediators between them. Previous studies have indicated that MR analysis would facilitate the design and interpretation of clinical trials [80–81]. Simulating drug action through naturally occurring genetic variants as instruments can provide us causal estimates with fewer confounders arising from traditional epidemiological researches such as environmental factors and reverse causation, analogous to treatment allocation in RCTs [82]. Second, we mainly focused our MR research on two druggable genes (ETFDH and PRKAB1) of metformin to improve the efficacy, safety, and drug development success in sepsis, and identified PRKAB1 is causally associated with sepsis. Third, our research combined two-sample and mediation MR, single-cell RNA-seq analysis based on database and sepsis-mouse-model-based in vivo experiments in this research, which evaluated and validated our finding from multi-aspects. The consistency of the majority findings obviously strengthens the reliability of our obtained results. Lastly, given the collectively suggested contribution of PRKAB1 activator metformin to sepsis improvement through immune system, our findings alongside prior clinical and pharmacological evidence may therefore indicate potential translational interest in developing PRKAB1-enhancing therapeutic agents.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting our findings. First, the data sets used in this research are publicly available, which might lead to novelty restrictions since it may fail to provide new and unique data resources for the study. However, via meticulously processing and analyzing the data from public databases, new valuable perspectives would emerge from the existing research. Second, our analysis was restricted to only European ancestry to reduce confounding by population stratification, which might limit generalizability of our results to other ethnic populations. Third, by definition, our MR analysis estimates the perturbation effects of metformin targets on the outcomes, as opposed to the direct role of metformin. Fourth, in this study, we just estimated the causal roles of druggable metformin gene targets and sepsis, while the targets and mechanisms of metformin are still under investigation and the genes targets not included in the current analysis could potentially change the results. Fifth, GWAS-based sepsis studies typically rely on ICD codes for case identification, and differences in coding practices and clinical diagnostic criteria across studies or data sources may introduce phenotypic heterogeneity. Although we have leveraged large-scale GWAS data, employed multiple MR methods, and conducted sensitivity analyses to evaluate the robustness of our primary findings under different analytical assumptions—thereby minimizing the influence of random error and systematic bias—further validation and extension of these findings using GWAS datasets based on more stringent clinical definitions or stratified sepsis phenotypes are essential to better elucidate the underlying subtype-specific genetic mechanisms. Sixth, the sepsis mouse model used in this study, based on intratracheal LPS instillation, primarily models an acute pulmonary inflammatory response to endotoxin. While it can reflect sepsis-associated lung injury or early inflammatory responses to some extent, it does not fully replicate the complex pathophysiology of clinical sepsis. Therefore, validating our key findings in a live bacterial infection model represents a necessary next step. Seventh, we assessed the expression of PRKAB1and immune cell marker protein in lung tissue of septic mice but did not assay them in other tissues. Assessing the expression of PRKAB1 and immune cell marker protein from other tissues in sepsis may provide more additional information and evidence. Eighth, we just examined the expression of some common immune cell marker protein by IHC and mIHC of the mediator immune cell from MR results rather than accurately quantify and identify their exact cell types by flow cytometry, which could make the experimental evidence less sufficient. Last but not least, no experiments are performed to validate the anti-sepsis effects of PRKAB1 activator metformin through immune cell-dependent mechanism in sepsis mouse model, which will need to be investigated in future studies.

Conclusions

In summary, our study supports that PRKAB1 activator metformin has some degree of causal association with sepsis improvement, and suggests that the downregulation of CD86 and upregulation of CD39, CD4 and CD14 may serve as an essential intermediary responsible for these effects. This finding provides evidence to consider metformin (PRKAB1 activation) as a potential intervention strategy for sepsis prevention and treatment by regulating immunity. Further mechanistic and clinical studies are needed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms by which metformin improves sepsis through immune system and evaluate the utility and efficacy of metformin in sepsis therapy.
