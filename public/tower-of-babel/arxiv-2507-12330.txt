Forecasting sub-population mortality using credibility theory
Mathias Lindholm, Gabriele Pittarello

Abstract
The focus of the present paper is to forecast mortality rates for small sub-populations that are parts of a larger super-population. In this setting the assumption is that it is possible to produce reliable forecasts for the super-population, but the sub-populations may be too small or lack sufficient history to produce reliable forecasts if modelled separately. This setup is aligned with the ideas that underpin credibility theory, and in the present paper the classical credibility theory approach is extended to be able to handle the situation where future mortality rates are driven by a latent stochastic process, as is the case for, e.g., Lee-Carter type models. This results in sub-population credibility predictors that are weighted averages of expected future super-population mortality rates and expected future sub-population specific mortality rates. Due to the predictor’s simple structure it is possible to derive an explicit expression for the mean squared error of prediction. Moreover, the proposed credibility modelling approach does not depend on the specific form of the super-population model, making it broadly applicable regardless of the chosen forecasting model for the super-population. The performance of the suggested sub-population credibility predictor is illustrated on simulated population data. These illustrations highlight how the credibility predictor serves as a compromise between only using a super-population model, and only using a potentially unreliable sub-population specific model.



Keywords
: Linear credibility estimator, mean squared error of prediction, relative survival, Lee-Carter, small populations

1
Introduction

Human mortality data is often aggregated into age-period tables by populations with common characteristics for a variety of applications ranging from epidemiology and demography to actuarial science.

Modelling the dynamics of the (central) mortality rate of sub-populations can be challenging, especially when the sub-population sizes are small
or the history is insufficient, see e.g.
Millossovich et al. 2014
. In this situation it may be difficult to find reliable estimates for the parameters of a mortality model, which implies difficulties in providing reliable forecasts in this situation.

Further, while these small sub-populations, at least partly, will share global mortality dynamics with the overall super-population to which they belong, the sub-population mortality dynamics are also likely influenced by sub-population specific effects like biological markers, demographic variables or behavioural factors, see e.g.
Dimai 2024
.

1
provides a schematic representation of a setting involving a common super-population, within which two small sub-populations (sub-populations 1 and 2) are explicitly identified. The present paper focuses on modelling and forecasting mortality rates in such small sub-populations—specifically, groups possibly comprising relatively few individuals who are known to share homogeneous characteristics, as exemplified by sub-populations 1 and 2 in the figure. Moreover, in addition it is assumed that

Figure 1
:
Sub-populations 1, and 2 are part of a larger reference super-population.
Sub-population 3 contains the individuals belonging to the reference super-population and that are not part of either of sub-population 1 or 2.
While the mortality trend of the two smaller populations may be influenced by group-specific effects, the literature on this type of problem, which we discuss in our paper, usually assumes that they have some overall mortality trend in common with the super-population.

(i)

it is possible to reliably model and forecast the mortality rates of the super-population, but

(ii)

(some of) the sub-populations are too small to
allow for reliable direct modelling of mortality rates.

Sub-population 3, by contrast, represents a residual group consisting of individuals who are not part of either sub-population 1 or 2, but who still belong to the overall super-population.

Concerning
(i)
, we assume that we have access to reasonable mortality rate forecasts for, e.g., the entire Italian or Swedish population. Examples of commonly used models for mortality forecasting are the Poisson Lee-Carter model from
Brouhns et al. 2002
and the generalised age-period-cohort models from
Hunt & Blake 2021
, see also the survey
Haberman & Renshaw 2011
. More recent data driven approaches are e.g.
Perla et al. 2021
;
Lindholm & Palmborg 2022
;
Robben et al. 2025
. Further, regarding
(ii)
, this suggests that detailed modelling is not guaranteed to be possible at the sub-population level. Instead of directly modelling a simpler model at the sub-population level, an alternative is to consider a credibility theory based approach. This approach is closely connected to modelling the sub-population mortality rates as a random effect type relative survival model
in the fashion of
Buckley 1984
, where the reference model is given by the super-population model. This approach is common to use in so-called claim-frequency modelling in non-life insurance pricing, see e.g.
Bühlmann & Gisler 2005
. Also note that by using a relative risk type model for the sub-population mortality introduces parameter sharing between the super- and sub-population models, which reduces the overall number of parameters. In the current paper the classical credibility theory approach to claim-frequency modelling is adjusted to handle that future mortality rates are modelled as a stochastic process, as in, e.g., the Poisson Lee-Carter model, see again
Brouhns et al. 2002
.

In particular, by adjusting the assumptions that underpin the classical credibility theory approach to claim-frequency modelling it is possible to derive a simple credibility predictor for future expected sub-population mortality rates. This predictor can be expressed as a weighted average between the expected mortality rate estimated on the super-population and the same expected mortality rate corrected by a random effect estimate that can be interpreted as an interaction between sub-population and age. The weights appearing in this averaging, i.e. the credibility weights, provide an automatic calibration of the sub-population mortality as a compromise between a pure super-population model and a sub-population specific relative mortality model. The credibility weights will favour the super-population model if the the sub-population has a too low (effective risk) exposure, or if the super-population provides a sufficiently good fit for the sub-population; and vice versa.

Relating to the current literature on models for mortality forecasting, the suggested credibility approach connects both to multi-population models and credibility theory. In terms of multi-population models, there is a vast literature
that started with the seminal work of
Li & Lee 2005
and the related works on common factor models, see e.g.
Booth et al. 2002
.
These models all use more or less complex parameter sharing setups, generalising the simple relative survival
construction
used in the present paper;
see also the discussion in Section
2
below.

Credibility theory was first applied in the context of multi-population mortality models by
Tsai & Yang 2015
;
Tsai & Lin 2017
.

These papers, however, apply the credibility theory approach only to the latent stochastic process part of the mortality model, whereas in the current paper the credibility modelling also takes the Poisson death count variation into account. A related branch of modelling is the frailty approach taken in
Jarner & Kryger 2011
, which also takes the Poisson variation into account. Furthermore, the credibility theory approach is closely connected to Bayesian modelling: the credibility estimator is known to be the
best linear estimator
that approximates an underlying Bayesian model. In this context
best
refers to its optimality with respect to the quadratic loss function, see e.g. Theorem 2.5 in
Bühlmann & Gisler 2005
and, as we will discuss in this paper, it is
linear
with respect to the past observations.

The modelling approach suggested in the present paper is closely connected to the Bayesian multi-population model discussed in
Van et al. 2017
. The main difference between the current approach and the approach taken in
Van et al. 2017
, is that the linear credibility theory mortality predictor does not rely on explicit distributional assumptions other than with respect to the
first two
moments and dependencies across ages, time periods and (sub-)populations
of the mortality rates. This is particularly useful in situations with small sub-populations having small (effective) size, where a Bayesian approach may rely heavily on prior assumptions due to limited available data.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows: Section
2
starts with a general discussion of modelling of sub-population mortality and mortality forecasting, Section
3
introduces the credibility theory setup, derives the credibility predictor in Proposition
1
together with its mean squared error of prediction in Proposition
2
. This is followed by a discussion on plug-in estimation in Section
3.1.1
. The paper ends with a longer numerical illustration of the suggested credibility predictor and its performance compared to a number of benchmark models in Section
4
.

2
Modelling the mortality of sub-populations

The perhaps most well-known paper on multi-population mortality models is
Li & Lee 2005
, which is an extension of the Lee-Carter model introduced in
Lee & Carter 1992
.
Let us denote age with index
x\in\mathbb{X}
and calendar time with
t\in\mathbb{T}
, with
\mathbb{X},\mathbb{T}\subset\mathbb{N}^{+}
.
The
original formulation of the
Lee-Carter model is a Gaussian model for
the one-year logarithmic difference of central mortality rates
, and was phrased as a Poisson death count model in
Brouhns et al. 2002
according to

\displaystyle D_{x,t}\mid E_{x,t},\mu_{x,t}\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(E_{x,t}\mu_{x,t}),

(1)

where the number of deaths at calendar time
t
and age
x
is denoted with
D_{x,t}
conditionally on the number of individuals exposed to risk at age
x
and time
t
(
E_{x,t}
) and on a mortality trend
\mu_{x,t}
. For more on the Poisson likelihood equivalence, see e.g.
Andersson & Lindholm 2022
. Further, henceforth,
E_{x,t}
will be referred to as “exposure”, following common notation in the literature on human mortality models. Moreover, also to ease the exposition,
\mu_{x,t}
will be referred to as the (central) “mortality rate”.

Continuing, in
Lee & Carter 1992
and
Brouhns et al. 2002
the mortality rates are described by a log-bi linear function given by

\displaystyle\log(\mu_{x,t}):=\alpha_{x}+\beta_{x}\kappa_{t},

(2)

where the
\kappa_{t}
s are modelled as a Gaussian time series, see Section
2.2
below.

Further, models focusing on an overall population as described by (
1
) will be referred to as a “global mortality model”.
Analogously, the multi-population extension of the Lee-Carter model from
Li & Lee 2005
can be phrased as a Poisson death count model as in
Li 2013
according to

D_{x,t}^{i}\mid E_{x,t}^{i},\mu_{x,t}^{i}\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(E_{x,t}^{i}\mu_{x,t}^{i}),

where
i\in\mathbb{I}
with
\mathbb{I}\subset\mathbb{N}^{+}
is the index denoting the sub-population and

\displaystyle\log(\mu_{x,t}^{i}):=

\displaystyle~\alpha_{x}^{i}+\beta_{x}\kappa_{t}+\beta_{x}^{i}\kappa_{t}^{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\log(\mu_{x,t})+\alpha_{x}^{i}-\alpha_{x}+\beta_{x}^{i}\kappa_{t}^{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\log(\mu_{x,t})+\widetilde{\alpha}_{x}^{i}+\beta_{x}^{i}\kappa_{t}^{i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\log(\mu_{x,t})+\log(v_{x,t}^{i}).

(3)

As
discussed in
Van et al. 2017
, the rates from (
2
) can be estimated using a two-step frequentist approach by

1.

treat
ing
the
\mu_{x,t}
s as deterministic and estimating the
\mu_{x,t}
s as
via maximum-likelihood under the model assumption
of the standard Poisson Lee-Carter model given by (
1
) and (
2
),

2.

given the
maximum-likelihood-estimate (MLE)

\widehat{\mu}_{x,t}
of
\mu_{x,t}
, estimate
v_{x,t}^{i}
from (
2
) as the MLE of the subsequent sub-population Poisson death count model using
E_{x,t}^{i}\widehat{\mu}_{x,t}
as log-offset.

This two-step setup is similar to what will be pursued in a credibility estimator context, but where the second step will be replaced by a credibility adjustment.

As an alternative to the above two-step procedure one can view (
2
) directly as a generalised non-linear Poisson model with
E_{x,t}^{i}
as log-offset.

2.1
Relative survival models and modelling small sub-populations

In the current paper focus is on small sub-populations such that the part corresponding to
v_{x,t}^{i}
in (
2
) is not believed to be possible to estimate and forecast reliably, which is the motivation for instead considering models of the type given by

\displaystyle D_{x,t}^{i}\mid E_{x,t}^{i},\Theta_{x,t}^{i},\mu_{x,t}\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(E_{x,t}^{i}\Theta_{x,t}^{i}\mu_{x,t}),

(4)

where
\Theta_{x,t}^{i}
is an
age and population dependent random effect
,
and where
\mu_{x,t}
corresponds to a given global mortality model.

The parametrisation of the mortality rates from (
4
) corresponds to using

\displaystyle\mu_{x,t}^{i}:=\mu_{x,t}\Theta_{x}^{i},

(5)

which can be thought of as a random effects version of a relative survival model, see e.g. p. 413 in
Andersen et al. 2012
. That is, if we replace the random effect
\Theta_{x}^{i}
in (
4
) by a deterministic parameter
\theta_{x}^{i}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\mu_{x,t}^{i}:=\mu_{x,t}\theta_{x}^{i},

(6)

it follows that

\frac{\mu_{x,t}^{i}}{\mu_{x,t}^{j}}=\frac{\theta_{x}^{i}}{\theta_{x}^{j}}.

Further,
\theta_{x}^{i}
corresponds to an adjustment of the intercept of
\mu_{x,t}
on the log-link scale by
\log(\theta_{x}^{i})
, i.e.,
\log(\theta_{x}^{i})
corresponds to
\widetilde{\alpha}_{x}^{i}
in (
2
).

Remark 1
.

(i)

Note that the mortality rate given by (
5
) resembles that of a (stratified) proportional frailty model. This resemblance, however, is faltering, since this would require that the
\Theta_{x}^{i}
s are assigned independently at birth, see e.g.
Vaupel et al. 1979
.
A related well-defined frailty based approach is the so-called SAINT model, see
Jarner & Kryger 2011
.

(ii)

Consider the model

D^{i}_{x,t}\mid E^{i}_{x,t},\mu_{x,t}\sim\text{Poisson}\left(E^{i}_{x,t}\mu_{x,t}\right),

and let

D_{x,t}=\sum_{i}D^{i}_{x,t}\quad\text{and}\quad E_{x,t}=\sum_{i}E^{i}_{x,t}.

Assuming conditional independence across all
(x,t,i)
s, given piece-wise constant
\mu_{x,t}
s over integer ages and years, the likelihood is then given by

\displaystyle\ell((\mu_{x,t})_{x,t})

\displaystyle\propto\sum_{x,t,i}D^{i}_{x,t}\log(E^{i}_{x,t}\mu_{x,t})-E^{i}_{x,t}\mu_{x,t}

\displaystyle\propto\sum_{x,t}D_{x,t}\log(\mu_{x,t})-E_{x,t}\mu_{x,t},

(7)

which allows us to estimate
\mu_{x,t}
using specific parametrisations such as, e.g., the Lee-Carter parametrisation from (
2
). Further, note that (
(ii)
) is likelihood equivalent to the Poisson model given by

D_{x,t}\mid E_{x,t},\mu_{x,t}\sim\text{Poisson}\left(E_{x,t}\mu_{x,t}\right)

as it should.

(iii)

Note that if we replace the random effect
\Theta_{x}^{i}
in (
4
) by a deterministic parameter
\theta_{x}^{i}
, i.e. use (
6
) and assume

\displaystyle D^{i}_{x,t}\mid E^{i}_{x,t},\mu_{x,t}\sim\text{Poisson}\left(E^{i}_{x,t}\theta^{i}_{x}\mu_{x,t}\right),

(8)

and treat
\mu_{x,t}
as known, the MLE of
\theta_{x}^{i}
is then given by

\displaystyle\widehat{\theta}_{x}^{i}:=\frac{\sum_{v}E^{i}_{x,v}F^{i}_{x,v}}{\sum_{v}E^{i}_{x,v}\mu_{x,v}}.

(9)

2.2
Extrapolating a calendar period and cohort component

The sub-population models that are considered in the current paper are of type (
4
) which means that the calendar period effect will be introduced via the
t
-dimension of the global mortality rate model
\mu_{x,t}
. The models that will be used
in the numerical illustrations
are all of Poisson
Generalised Age-Period-Cohort
type,
see Table
1
in Section
4
and
Section

B.1
, which means that all parameters can be estimated using a generalised non-linear Poisson model with log-link
given a set of suitable identification constraints
.

For projecting central mortality rates, it is necessary to extrapolate the calendar period effects for
t>t^{\prime}
and cohorts
t-x>t^{\prime}-1
, where
t^{\prime}:=\max{\mathbb{T}}
and
\mathbb{T}
corresponds to the set of observed calendar times. Following the original Lee-Carter approach from
Lee & Carter 1992
this corresponds to modelling the
\kappa_{t}
s as time series. To be precise, the fitted
\kappa_{t}
s, the
\widehat{\kappa}_{t}
s, are modelled as time series belonging in the ARIMA(
p,q,d
)-class of models, see e.g.
Chapter 3 in
Shumway & Stoffer 2000
. This is in line with the implementation of the procedures used in the
StMoMo

R
-package that later will be used in the numerical illustrations in Section
4
. By using this package, model selection of the time series part of the modelling will be carried out using the Bayesian information criterion (BIC), see
e.g. Chapter 2 in
Shumway & Stoffer 2000
.

Remark 2
.

One can consider
different approaches for extrapolating the calendar time effect
\kappa_{t}
,
for example using a multivariate time-series model to jointly model the calendar time effect of the sub-populations in a similar fashion to the vector auto-regressive moving average models (VARIMA) discussed in
Tiao & Tsay 1989
.
However, the focus of the present paper is rather to illustrate how credibility theory can be used to combine sub-population information. Assessing different choices for extrapolation of calendar period effects goes outside the scope of the paper.

3
Credibility theory for mortality rate forecasting

As discussed in the beginning of
Section

2
, the two-step estimation procedure described for fitting the Poisson Li-Lee model from
Li 2013
will be generalised to the situation when starting from an arbitrary global
\mu_{x,t}
model that will serve as a basis for sub-population mortality models using credibility theory. The focus of this section is to derive such a credibility estimator generalising the setup used for non-life frequency modelling described in Chapter 4 in
Bühlmann & Gisler 2005
. In order to do this, introduce the following assumptions:

Assumption 1
.

Conditionally on a random parameter
\Theta_{x}^{i}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\mu_{x,t}
, we have that for age
x\in\mathbb{X}
, sub-population
i\in\mathbb{I}
and
t\in\mathbb{T}

D^{i}_{x,t}\mid E^{i}_{x,t},\Theta^{i}_{x},\mu_{x,t}\sim\text{Poisson}\left(E^{i}_{x,t}\Theta^{i}_{x}\mu_{x,t}\right).

Assumption 2
.

\Theta_{x}^{i}\perp\!\!\!\perp\mu_{x,t}
, for all
x,t,i
, and
{\mathbb{E}}\left[\Theta_{x}^{i}\right]=1

Assumption 3
.

For
i\in\mathbb{I}
,
x\in\mathbb{X}
and
t,v\in\mathbb{T}
with
v\neq t
, it holds that

{\operatorname{Cov}}(D^{i}_{x,t},D^{i}_{x,v}\mid\Theta^{i}_{x},\mu_{x,t},\mu_{x,v})=0.

Similar to the assumptions in
Bühlmann & Gisler 2005
, Ch. 4
, the above Assumptions
1
–
3
, for
x\in\mathbb{X}
and
t\in\mathbb{T}
, provides us with

\displaystyle\mu_{x,t}\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right):=

\displaystyle~\mu_{x,t}\Theta^{i}_{x}=\mathbb{E}\left[F^{i}_{x,t}\mid\Theta^{i}_{x},\mu_{x,t}\right],

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\mu_{x,t}\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)\mid\mu_{x,t}\right]=

\displaystyle~\mathbb{E}[F_{x,t}^{i}\mid\mu_{x,t}]=\mu_{x,t},

(10)

\displaystyle\Var\left(\mu_{x,t}\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)\mid\mu_{x,t}\right)=

\displaystyle~(\mu_{x,t})^{2}\Var(\Theta^{i}_{x}),

(11)

which is closely aligned with the corresponding results for the standard credibility theory setup in Chapter 4 in
Bühlmann & Gisler 2005
. It is, however, worth pointing out that not only the
\mu_{x,t}
s and the
\Theta_{x,t}^{i}
s are random, but also the exposures
E_{x,t}
. As a consequence of this, it follows by the law of total variance that

\displaystyle\Var\left(F^{i}_{x,t}\mid\mu_{x,t}\right)

\displaystyle=\Var\left(\mathbb{E}\left[F^{i}_{x,t}\mid\Theta^{i}_{x},\mu_{x,t}\right]\mid\mu_{x,t}\right)+\mathbb{E}\left(\Var\left[F^{i}_{x,t}\mid\Theta^{i}_{x},\mu_{x,t}\right]\mid\mu_{x,t}\right)

\displaystyle=(\mu_{x,t})^{2}\Var(\Theta^{i}_{x})+\mu_{x,t}\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{E^{i}_{x,t}}~\Bigg|~\mu_{x,t}\right]

(12)

and similarly, by using
3
, for
v,t\in\mathbb{T}
with
v\neq t
, the covariance between
F^{i}_{x,t}
and
F^{i}_{x,v}
is given by

\displaystyle\text{Cov}(F^{i}_{x,t},F^{i}_{x,v}\mid\mu_{x,t},\mu_{x,v})

\displaystyle=\mu_{x,t}\mu_{x,v}\Var(\Theta^{i}_{x}).

(13)

Remark 3
.

(i)

The interpretation of
\Theta_{x}^{i}
in light of
2
is that it corresponds to a cohort selection effect w.r.t.
x
and
i
, which is thought of as being stable over calendar time. This motivates the assumption that the
\Theta_{x}^{i}
s are independent of the (possibly) stochastic baseline mortality rates
\mu_{x,t}
. Moreover, this assumption ascertains that

\mathbb{E}[F_{x,t}\mid\mu_{x,t}]=\mu_{x,t},

which implies that the sub-population specific mortality projections will not diverge. For more on this, see e.g.
Li & Lee 2005
;
Van et al. 2017
.

(ii)

Note that when later discussing estimation, the quantity
\mathbb{E}[1/E_{x,t}^{i}]
will be estimated using a sample size one estimator. That is,

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{1}{E_{x,t}^{i}}~\Bigg|~\mu_{x,t}\right]:=\frac{1}{E_{x,t}^{i}},

(14)

where the right hand side of (
14
) is equivalent to having considered
\Var(F_{x,t}^{i}\mid E_{x,t}^{i},\mu_{x,t})
from the start.

3.1
Estimation

The objective of this paper is to derive a linear credibility estimator
\widehat{\mu_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)}
of the future central mortality rate
\mu_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)
under Assumptions
1
–
3
over a forecasting horizon
h\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, given the information
\mathcal{G}_{x,t^{\prime}}^{i}:=\{(E_{x,t}^{i},\mu_{x,t}),t\leqslant t^{\prime}\}
.

In credibility theory, estimation can be performed parametrically using a pure Bayesian approach, assuming a distribution for the past losses and a prior distribution over the
group-specific
characteristics. Alternatively, the Bayesian result can be approximated non-parametrically with the so-called linear credibility model, see e.g. p. 401 in
Klugman et al. 2012
. The two approaches are equivalent when the past losses are assumed to follow a distribution from the exponential family and the
group-specific
characteristics are chosen as a conjugate prior, see e.g.
Jewell 1974
.

The linear credibility estimator, given sub-populations
i,i\in\mathbb{I}
and ages
x\in\mathbb{X}
, is expressed in terms of parameters
\bm{\omega}_{x}^{i}:=(\omega^{i}_{x,0},\omega^{i}_{x,1},\ldots,\omega^{i}_{x,t^{\prime}})^{\mathsf{T}}

given by

\bm{\omega}_{x}^{i}=\argmin_{\bm{\omega}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mu_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)-\omega_{0}-\sum_{v\in\mathbb{T}}\omega_{v}F^{i}_{x,v}\right)^{2}~\Bigg|~\mathcal{G}_{x,t^{\prime}}^{i}\right],

(15)

where
\mu_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)
is the future stochastic central mortality rate that we want to estimate as a function of the observed population specific mortality rates. Note that in the formulation of the function to be minimised in (
15
) it is conditioned on
\mathcal{G}_{x,t^{\prime}}^{i}
, which includes knowledge about the
E_{x,t}^{i}
s. This can be relaxed, but from a practical perspective, this will result in quantities that can not be estimated other than with sample size one estimator analogously to (
14
) discussed in
Remark

3
.

By solving (
15
) under Assumptions
1
–
3
provides us with the following result:

Proposition 1
.

Under Assumptions
1
–
3
the linear credibility estimator for the central mortality rate minimising (
15
) is given by

\widehat{\mu_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)}:=(1-Z^{i}_{x})\overline{\mu}_{x,t^{\prime}+h}+Z^{i}_{x}\widehat{\mu}_{x,t^{\prime}+h}^{i},

with

\overline{\mu}_{x,t^{\prime}+h}:=\mathbb{E}[\mu_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\mid\mathcal{G}_{x,t^{\prime}}^{i}]

and where

Z^{i}_{x}:=\frac{\sum_{v\in\mathbb{T}}E^{i}_{x,v}\mu_{x,v}}{\frac{1}{\Var\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)}+\sum_{v\in\mathbb{T}}E^{i}_{x,v}\mu_{x,v}}

(16)

is a credibility weight and where

\widehat{\mu}_{x,t^{\prime}+h}^{i}:=\overline{\mu}_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\widehat{\theta}^{i}_{x}

with
\widehat{\theta}^{i}_{x}
given by (
9
).

The proof of
Proposition

1
is given in
Section

A.1
.

It follows from
Proposition

1
that the credibility estimator can be expressed as a credibility weighted average of a global mortality model,
\overline{\mu}_{x,t^{\prime}+h}
, and a relative survival model,
\widehat{\mu}_{x,t^{\prime}+h}^{i}
, noting that the future stochastic mortality rate
\mu_{x,t^{\prime}+h}
has been replaced by its conditional expected value
\overline{\mu}_{x,t^{\prime}+h}
.
Here one can also note that when using, e.g., a Poisson Lee-Carter model with a time series component as those discussed in Section
2.2
, it follows that

\overline{\mu}_{x,t^{\prime}+h}:=\mathbb{E}[\mu_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\mid\mathcal{G}_{x,t^{\prime}}^{i}]=\mathbb{E}[\mu_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\mid\mu_{x,t},t\leqslant t^{\prime}].

Further, note that in the credibility weights
\mu_{x,t}
s appear, which is natural, since these are known when conditioning on
\mathcal{G}_{x,t^{\prime}}^{i}
.

Continuing,
since the estimator is based on a Poisson assumption and the minimisation of an
L^{2}
-distance, it is sufficient to only specify the first two moments of
\Theta_{x}^{i}
in order to obtain an explicit credibility estimator. This can be compared to the Bayesian analog of (
4
) discussed in
Van et al. 2017
, which relies on a number of additional distributional assumptions
on
\Theta_{x}^{i}
and on the parameters of the global mortality model
.

Further, note that the credibility estimator from
Proposition

1
can be rewritten according to a single relative survival model according to

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)}:=\overline{\mu}_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\widehat{\Theta}_{x}^{i},

(17)

where

\displaystyle\widehat{\Theta}_{x}^{i}:=1+Z^{i}_{x}(\widehat{\theta}_{x}^{i}-1).

(18)

Thus, Equations (
17
) and (
18
) illustrate that one could equivalently define the credibility estimator in terms of the relativities introduced through the random effects
\Theta_{x}^{i}
.

Furthermore, concerning the credibility weights
Z_{x}^{i}
from (
16
) we see that these will be close to 1 if

\sum_{v\in\mathbb{T}}E^{i}_{x,v}\mu_{x,v}\to+\infty,

which means that the credibility estimator collapses to the standard MLE of the relative survival model from (
6
) when having access to large amounts of sub-population
i
data. Continuing, by noting that

\operatorname{CV}(\mu_{x,t}(\Theta_{x}^{i})\mid\mu_{x,t}):=\frac{\sqrt{\Var(\mu_{x,t}(\Theta_{x}^{i})\mid\mu_{x,t})}}{\mathbb{E}[\mu_{x,t}(\Theta_{x}^{i})\mid\mu_{x,t}]}=\frac{\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[(\mu_{x,t}(\Theta_{x}^{i})-\mu_{x,t})^{2}\mid\mu_{x,t}]}}{\mu_{x,t}}=\sqrt{\Var\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)},

it follows that

\frac{1}{\Var\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)}=\frac{1}{\operatorname{CV}(\mu_{x,t}(\Theta_{x}^{i})\mid\mu_{x,t})^{2}}.

Hence, if the coefficient of variation of
\mu_{x,t}(\Theta_{x}^{i})
is large there is a large unexplained source of variation when trying to describe the mean of sub-population
i
using
\mu_{x,t}
. This again motivates collapsing the credibility estimator to the standard MLE of the relative survival model from (
6
).

The model that we present differs from the frequency model for non-life insurance pricing presented in Chapter 4 in
Bühlmann & Gisler 2005
. The main differences can be seen from
1
, where we assume that there is an effect on the central mortality rate that depends on the calendar period and that the (future) mortality rates are stochastic. If we consider an age-only mortality model, i.e.,
\mu_{x,t}:=\mu_{x}=
constant (deterministic), and let
E_{x}^{i}:=\sum_{t\in\mathbb{T}}E_{x,t}^{i}
, which can be thought of as a Lee-Carter model where the calendar period effect has been dropped,
Proposition

1
reduces to the following:

Corollary 1
.

Under Assumptions
1
–
3
, with the additional assumption that
\mu_{x,t}:=\mu_{x}
, the linear credibility estimator for the central mortality rate minimising (
15
) is given by

\widehat{\mu_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)}:=(1-Z^{i}_{x})\mu_{x}+Z^{i}_{x}\mu^{i}_{x},

where the credibility weights are

Z^{i}_{x}:=\frac{E^{i}_{x}}{\frac{1}{\mu_{x}\Var(\Theta^{i}_{x})}+E^{i}_{x}},

and where

\mu^{i}_{x}:=\mu_{x}\widehat{\theta}_{x}^{i},

with
\widehat{\theta}_{x}^{i}
given by

\widehat{\theta}_{x}^{i}:=\frac{1}{\mu_{x}}\sum_{v}\frac{E^{i}_{x,v}}{E^{i}_{x}}F^{i}_{x,v}.

Thus,
Corollary

1
retrieves the credibility estimator from Corollary 4.8 in
Bühlmann & Gisler 2005
.

Concerning the prediction error, we have the following result:

Proposition 2
.

The mean squared error of prediction of the credibility estimator is

\mathbb{E}\left[(\mu_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)-\widehat{\mu_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)})^{2}\mid\mathcal{G}_{x,t^{\prime}}^{i}\right]=\Var\left(\mu_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)\mid\mathcal{G}_{x,t^{\prime}}^{i}\right)+(Z^{i}_{x})^{2}(\overline{\mu}_{x,t^{\prime}+h})^{2}\Var\left(\widehat{\theta}^{i}_{x}\mid\mathcal{G}_{x,t^{\prime}}^{i}\right)

(19)

where

\Var\left(\mu_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)\mid\mathcal{G}_{x,t^{\prime}}^{i}\right)=\overline{\sigma}^{2}_{x,t^{\prime}+h}(\Var\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)+1)+(\overline{\mu}_{x,t^{\prime}+h})^{2}\Var\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)

with
\overline{\sigma}_{x,t^{\prime}+h}^{2}:=\Var(\mu_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\mid\mathcal{G}_{x,t^{\prime}}^{i})=\Var(\mu_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\mid\mu_{x,t},t\in\mathbb{T})
, and where

\Var\left(\widehat{\theta}^{i}_{x}\mid\mathcal{G}_{x,t^{\prime}}^{i}\right)=\Var\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)\frac{\sum_{v}(E^{i}_{x,v}\mu_{x,v})^{2}}{(\sum_{v}E^{i}_{x,v}\mu_{x,v})^{2}}+\frac{1}{\sum_{v}E^{i}_{x,v}\mu_{x,v}},

with
\widehat{\theta}^{i}_{x}
from (
9
).

The proof of Proposition
2
is provided in Appendix
A.2
.

Concerning the MSEP expression from Proposition
2
, this clearly illustrates that by using the suggested credibility theory approach introduces over-dispersion, since even when
Z_{x}^{i}=0
, putting all weight on the super-population mortality model, MSEP will still be influenced by the random effect introduced via
\Theta_{x}^{i}
through its variance.

3.1.1
Plug-in estimation

In order to make
Proposition

1
practically implementable, we need to estimate the global mortality trend
\mu_{x,t^{\prime}+h}
and the variance of the random effect
\Theta^{i}_{x}
,
\Var(\Theta^{i}_{x})
. The global mortality model will be based on the plug-in estimates obtained from applying the methods discussed in
Section
2.2
; see Table
1
in Section
4
for specific models that will be used for illustration purposes. Here one can note that even though Poisson Lee-Carter type models are used to model the global mortality rates in the current paper, one can note that it, of course, is possible to use crude (observed) death rates
F^{i}_{x,t}
for in-sample estimation of
\mu_{x,t^{\prime}+h}
.

For plug-in estimation of
\Var(\Theta^{i}_{x})
we will use a moment based estimator, given estimates of the global mortality
\widehat{\mu}_{x,t}
.

The estimator that will be used is obtained by combining

\displaystyle\widehat{\Var}\left(\sum^{t^{\prime}}_{t=1}F_{x,t}^{i}~\Bigg|~\mathcal{G}_{x,t^{\prime}}^{i}\right)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum^{t^{\prime}}_{t=1}F_{x,t}^{i}-\mathbb{E}\left[\sum^{t^{\prime}}_{t=1}F_{x,t}^{i}~\Bigg|~\mathcal{G}_{x,t^{\prime}}^{i}\right]\right)^{2}~\Bigg|~\mathcal{G}_{x,t^{\prime}}^{i}\right]

\displaystyle\approx\left(\sum^{t^{\prime}}_{t=1}F_{x,t}^{i}-\sum^{t^{\prime}}_{t=1}\widehat{\mu}_{x,t}\right)^{2},

with

\displaystyle\Var\left(\sum^{t^{\prime}}_{t=1}F_{x,t}^{i}~\Bigg|~\mathcal{G}_{x,t^{\prime}}^{i}\right)=\sum^{t^{\prime}}_{t=1}\Var(F_{x,t}^{i}\mid\mathcal{G}_{x,t^{\prime}}^{i})+2\sum_{v>t}{\operatorname{Cov}}(F_{x,v}^{i},F_{x,t}^{i}\mid\mathcal{G}_{x,t^{\prime}}^{i}),

which after straightforward calculations results in the following moment based plug-in variance estimator

\displaystyle{\widehat{\Var_{\text{m}}(\Theta^{i}_{x})}}:=\frac{\left((\sum_{t\in\mathbb{T}}F^{i}_{x,t}-\sum_{t\in\mathbb{T}}\widehat{\mu}_{x,t})^{2}-\sum_{t\in\mathbb{T}}\frac{\widehat{\mu}_{x,t}}{E_{x,t}^{i}}\right)}{(\sum_{t\in\mathbb{T}}\widehat{\mu}_{x,t})^{2}}.

(20)

Further, note that the plug-in estimator

\widehat{\Var}_{\text{m}}(\Theta^{i}_{x})

from (
20
) is not guaranteed to be non-negative due to that the underlying death counts may contain too many zeros, which suggests the following zero-adjusted estimator:

\displaystyle\widehat{\Var}(\Theta^{i}_{x}):=\max\left(\widehat{\Var}_{\text{m}}(\Theta^{i}_{x}),0\right).

(21)

Here one can note that when (
21
) equals 0, it means that the credibility weight is set to 0 and the credibility estimator will place all weight on the global MLE, which is the natural baseline when not having a sufficient amount of data.

To conclude, when the amount of data in sub-population
i
is sufficiently large, or if the fit when describing the mean of sub-population
i
using the global mean is too poor, the credibility estimator from
Proposition

1
reduces to the two-step MLE of the relative survival model (
8
).

4.1
Simulation of (sub-)population data

The synthetic (sub-)population data is based on the crude Italian mortality data by ages and period from the Human Mortality Database
(
Hunt & Blake 2021
)
. The data consists of ages between
0
and
110
and the period
1872
to
2021
.

The observed one-year death probabilities for the Italian population are used as a basis for the simulation model. These probabilities are defined according to

q_{x,t}=\frac{d_{x,t}}{n_{x,t}},

for
x=0,\ldots,110
and
t+x=1872,\ldots,2021
, where
n_{x,t}=e_{x,t}+d_{x,t}/2
denotes the observed initial exposure for age
x
in calendar time
t
. The initial exposure for age and period for the data is estimated using the
central2initial
function from the
StMoMo
package.

The simulated data will be split into three sub-populations with sizes small, medium, and large. These sub-populations will be based on observed log-odds-ratios for the Italian population, where the log-odds-ratio for age
x
and calendar time
t
is given by

\log(q_{x,t}/(1-q_{x,t}))=\delta_{x,t},

with
\delta_{x,t}\in\mathbb{R}
. Given these inital log-odds ratios, the log-odds-ratios for the sub-populations are defined according to

\log(q^{i}_{x,t}/(1-q^{i}_{x,t}))=\log(\Theta_{x}^{i})+\delta_{x,t},\quad i=1,2,3,

where the
\Theta_{x}^{i}
s are non-negative random effects. That is,

\displaystyle q_{x,t}=\frac{\text{e}^{\delta_{x,t}}}{1+\text{e}^{\delta_{x,t}}},

(22)

and

\displaystyle q^{i}_{x,t}=\frac{\Theta_{x,t}^{i}\text{e}^{\delta_{x,t}}}{1+\Theta_{x,t}^{i}\text{e}^{\delta_{x,t}}},~i=1,2,3.

(23)

The above assumptions allow us to simulate the evolution of the different sub-populations using a binomial model based on an initial number of individuals given by
n_{0,t}^{i}
together with the one-year death probabilities
q^{i}_{x,t}
, i.e. raw deaths are simulated according to

D^{i}_{x,t}\mid N_{x,t},q_{x,t}^{i}\sim\text{Binomial}\left(N_{x,t},q^{i}_{x,t}\right).

Further, concerning the
\Theta_{x,t}^{i}
s these are modelled as follows:

•

\Theta_{x}^{1}\sim\text{Uniform}(0.7,0.8)
,

•

\Theta_{x}^{2}\sim\text{Uniform}(1.2,1.3)
,

•

\Theta_{x}^{3}\equiv 1
.

Finally, the super-population, denoted “0”, is constructed according to

D_{x,t}^{0}:=\sum_{i=1}^{3}D_{x,t}^{i},\quad\text{and}~E_{x,t}^{0}:=\sum_{i=1}^{3}E_{x,t}^{i},

where the exposures, i.e. the
E_{x,t}^{i}
s, are defined in
Equation

30
in
Section

B.2
.
Note that by using the above described procedure for generating synthetic data, it is not assumed that the underlying data generating model complies with the model described in
Section

3
. However, for sufficiently small mortality rates the assumed Poisson likelihood equivalence will result in one-year death probabilities that are approximately equal to those used in the Binomial simulation model. For more details, see Appendix
B.2
. As described above, the underlying data that is being used comes from the Italian population, and an illustration of the
q_{x,t}^{i}
s is given in Figure
6
.

The above procedure is implemented by assuming an initial population size of
100~000
individuals for
each cohort of
the super-population 0, and that sub-population 1
cohorts
consist of
5~000
individuals, and sub-population 2
cohorts

500

above consist of

8

In this respect sub-population 2 is of a size that could result in problematic estimates, whereas sub-population 1 is small but not obviously too small for reliable estimation.

A.1
Proof of the credibility estimator

In this section we prove
Proposition

1
under the regularity conditions of
Section

3
.

Given some sub-population given sub-populations
i,i\in\mathbb{I}
and ages
x\in\mathbb{X}
, is expressed in terms of parameters
\bm{\omega}_{x}^{i}:=(\omega^{i}_{x,0},\omega^{i}_{x,1},\ldots,\omega^{i}_{x,t^{\prime}})^{\mathsf{T}}
the credibility estimator of the central mortality rate minimises, conditionally on the filtration
\mathcal{G}_{x,t^{\prime}}^{i}:=\{(E_{x,t}^{i},\mu_{x,t}),t\leqslant t^{\prime}\}
, the following expectation

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{\omega}^{i}_{x}}\;\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mu_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)-\omega^{i}_{x,0}-\sum_{v\in\mathbb{T}}\omega^{i}_{x,v}F^{i}_{x,v}\right)^{2}~\Bigg|~\mathcal{G}_{x,t^{\prime}}^{i}\right],

Here,
\mu_{x,t}\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)
is the central mortality rate in calendar period
t
, age
x
and sub-population
i
that we want to estimate as a function of the observed population specific mortality rates.

For ease of notation, let
\mathcal{E}(\bm{\omega}^{i}_{x}):=\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mu_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)-\omega^{i}_{x,0}-\sum_{v\in\mathbb{T}}\omega^{i}_{x,v}F^{i}_{x,v}\right)^{2}~\Bigg|~\mathcal{G}_{x,t^{\prime}}^{i}\right]
.

Let us consider the first order derivative with respect to
\omega^{i}_{x,0}

\frac{\partial\mathcal{E}(\bm{\omega}^{i}_{x})}{\partial\omega^{i}_{x,0}}=\mathbb{E}\left[-2\left(\mu_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)-\omega^{i}_{x,0}-\sum_{v\in\mathbb{T}}\omega^{i}_{x,v}F^{i}_{x,v}\right)~\Bigg|~\mathcal{G}_{x,t^{\prime}}^{i}\right],

(25)

and with respect to
\omega^{i}_{x,t}
with
t\in\mathbb{T}

\frac{\partial\mathcal{E}(\bm{\omega}^{i}_{x})}{\partial\omega^{i}_{x,t}}=\mathbb{E}\left[-2F^{i}_{x,t}\left(\mu_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)-\omega^{i}_{x,0}-\sum_{v\in\mathbb{T}}\omega^{i}_{x,v}F^{i}_{x,v}\right)~\Bigg|~\mathcal{G}_{x,t^{\prime}}^{i}\right].

(26)

By setting
Equation

25
equal to zero yields

\mu_{x,x,t^{\prime}+h}=\omega^{i}_{x,0}+\sum_{v\in\mathbb{T}}\omega^{i}_{x,v}\mu_{x,v}.

(27)

Analogously, setting
Equation

26
equal to zero and subtracting the product of
Equation

25
and
\mathbb{E}\left[F^{i}_{x,t}~|~\mu_{x,t}\right]
provides us with

\text{Cov}(F^{i}_{x,t},\mu_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)~|~\mathcal{G}_{x,t^{\prime}}^{i})=\sum_{v\neq t}\omega^{i}_{x,v}\text{Cov}(F^{i}_{x,t},F^{i}_{x,v}~|~\mu_{x,t},\mu_{x,v})+\omega^{i}_{x,t}\Var\left(F^{i}_{x,t}~|~\mu_{x,t}\right).

Under
3
, recall (
13
), i.e.

\text{Cov}(F^{i}_{x,t},F^{i}_{x,v}~|~\mu_{x,t},\mu_{x,v})=\mu_{x,t}\mu_{x,v}\Var(\Theta^{i}_{x}),

and, analogously, it holds that

\text{Cov}(F^{i}_{x,t},\mu_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)~|~\mathcal{G}_{x,t^{\prime}}^{i})=\mu_{x,t}\mu_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\Var(\Theta^{i}_{x}).

Using this we obtain

\omega^{i}_{x,t}=(\mu_{x,t^{\prime}+h}-\sum_{v}\omega^{i}_{x,v}\mu_{x,v})(E^{i}_{x,t}\Var(\Theta^{i}_{x})).

(28)

Combining Equations (
27
) and (
28
), we obtain the estimator in
Proposition

1

\widehat{\mu_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)}:=(1-Z^{i}_{x})\overline{\mu}_{x,t^{\prime}+h}+Z^{i}_{x}\widehat{\mu}_{x,t^{\prime}+h}^{i}

where

\overline{\mu}_{x,t^{\prime}+h}:=\mathbb{E}[\mu_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\mid\mathcal{G}_{x,t^{\prime}}^{i}]=\mathbb{E}[\mu_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\mid\mu_{x,t},t\leqslant t^{\prime}],

and where

Z^{i}_{x}:=\frac{\sum_{v\in\mathbb{T}}E^{i}_{x,v}\mu_{x,v}}{\frac{1}{\Var\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)}+\sum_{v\in\mathbb{T}}E^{i}_{x,v}\mu_{x,v}}

(29)

is a credibility weight. This together with that

\widehat{\theta}_{x}^{i}:=\frac{\sum_{v}E^{i}_{x,v}F^{i}_{x,v}}{\sum_{v}E^{i}_{x,v}\mu_{x,v}},

and
\widehat{\mu}_{x,t^{\prime}+h}^{i}=\widehat{\theta}_{x}^{i}\overline{\mu}_{x,t^{\prime}+h}
completes the proof.

A.2
Proof of the Mean Squared Error of Prediction (MSEP)

In this section we prove the MSEP expression from
Proposition

2
.

Let us consider, for some sub-populations
i,i\in\mathbb{I}
, ages
x\in\mathbb{X}
, given the filtration
\mathcal{G}_{x,t^{\prime}}^{i}:=\{(E_{x,t}^{i},\mu_{x,t}),t\leqslant t^{\prime}\}
, the following expectation

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(\mu_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)-\widehat{\mu_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)})^{2}\mid\mathcal{G}_{x,t^{\prime}}^{i}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[(\mu_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)-\left((1-Z^{i}_{x})\overline{\mu}_{x,t^{\prime}+h}+Z^{i}_{x}\widehat{\mu}_{x,t^{\prime}+h}^{i}\right)^{2}\mid\mathcal{G}_{x,t^{\prime}}^{i}\right].

With a few algebraic steps it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(\mu_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)-\left((1-Z^{i}_{x})\overline{\mu}_{x,t^{\prime}+h}+Z^{i}_{x}\widehat{\mu}_{x,t^{\prime}+h}^{i}\right)^{2}\mid\mathcal{G}_{x,t^{\prime}}^{i}\right]

\displaystyle=(Z^{i}_{x})^{2}\;\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mu_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)-\widehat{\theta}_{x}^{i}\overline{\mu}_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\right)^{2}\mid\mathcal{G}_{x,t^{\prime}}^{i}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+2\;Z^{i}_{x}\;(1-Z^{i}_{x})\;\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mu_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)-\widehat{\theta}_{x}^{i}\overline{\mu}_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\right)\left(\mu_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)-\overline{\mu}_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\right)\mid\mathcal{G}_{x,t^{\prime}}^{i}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+(1-Z^{i}_{x})^{2}\;\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mu_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)-\overline{\mu}_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\right)^{2}\mid\mathcal{G}_{x,t^{\prime}}^{i}\right].

Next, note that one can rewrite according to

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mu_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)-\widehat{\theta}_{x}^{i}\overline{\mu}_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\right)^{2}\mid\mathcal{G}_{x,t^{\prime}}^{i}\right]=\Var\left(\mu_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)\mid\mathcal{G}_{x,t^{\prime}}^{i}\right)+\overline{\mu}_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\Var\left(\widehat{\theta}_{x}^{i}\mid\mathcal{G}_{x,t^{\prime}}^{i}\right)

\displaystyle\quad=\overline{\sigma}^{2}_{x,t^{\prime}+h}(\Var\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)+1)+(\overline{\mu}_{x,t^{\prime}+h})^{2}\Var\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\overline{\mu}_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\left(\Var\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)\frac{\sum_{v}(E^{i}_{x,v}\mu_{x,v})^{2}}{(\sum_{v}E^{i}_{x,v}\mu_{x,v})^{2}}+\frac{1}{\sum_{v}E^{i}_{x,v}\mu_{x,v}}\right)

\displaystyle\quad=\Var\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)\;\left(\frac{\sum_{v}(E^{i}_{x,v}\mu_{x,v})^{2}}{(\sum_{v}E^{i}_{x,v}\mu_{x,v})^{2}}+\overline{\sigma}_{x,t^{\prime}+h}^{2}+\overline{\mu}_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\right)+\overline{\sigma}_{x,t^{\prime}+h}^{2}+\frac{1}{\sum_{v}E^{i}_{x,v}\mu_{x,v}},

with
\overline{\sigma}_{x,t^{\prime}+h}^{2}:=\Var(\mu_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\mid\mathcal{G}_{x,t^{\prime}}^{i})=\Var(\mu_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\mid\mu_{x,t},t\in\mathbb{T})
. Further, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mu_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)-\widehat{\theta}_{x}^{i}\overline{\mu}_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\right)\left(\mu_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)-\overline{\mu}_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\right)\mid\mathcal{G}_{x,t^{\prime}}^{i}\right]

\displaystyle\quad=(\overline{\sigma}_{x,t^{\prime}+h}^{2}+\overline{\mu}_{x,t^{\prime}+h}^{2})\Var\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)+\overline{\sigma}_{x,t^{\prime}+h}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad=\Var\left(\mu_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)\mid\mathcal{G}_{x,t^{\prime}}^{i}\right).

Finally, we can write that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mu_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)-\overline{\mu}_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\right)^{2}\mid\mathcal{G}_{x,t^{\prime}}^{i}\right]=(\overline{\sigma}_{x,t^{\prime}+h}^{2}+\overline{\mu}_{x,t^{\prime}+h}^{2})\Var\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)+\overline{\sigma}_{x,t^{\prime}+h}^{2}.

Combining the terms above, we proved that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[(\mu_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)-\widehat{\mu_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)})^{2}\mid\mathcal{G}_{x,t^{\prime}}^{i}\right]

\displaystyle\quad=\Var\left(\mu_{x,t^{\prime}+h}\left(\Theta^{i}_{x}\right)\mid\mathcal{G}_{x,t^{\prime}}^{i}\right)+(Z^{i}_{x})^{2}(\overline{\mu}_{x,t^{\prime}+h})^{2}\Var\left(\widehat{\theta}^{i}_{x}\mid\mathcal{G}_{x,t^{\prime}}^{i}\right).

B.1
Generalised age-period-cohort models

We refer to the generalised age-period-cohort (GAPC) class of models as a class of generalised non-linear models for the central mortality rate that assume the following structure on the predictor
\mu_{x,t},
for some
x\in\mathbb{X}
and
t\in\mathbb{T}
:

\mu_{x,t}=\alpha_{x}+\sum^{K}_{k=1}\beta^{(k)}_{x}\kappa^{(k)}_{t}+\beta^{(0)}_{x}\gamma_{t-x}.

The GAPC models were first discussed in
Hunt & Blake 2021
and allow to model a static age effect
\alpha_{x}
,
K
interaction components between age and time (
\beta^{(k)}_{x}\kappa^{(k)}_{t}
) and interactions between age and cohorts (
\beta^{(0)}_{x}\gamma_{t-x}
). The models that we included in our application and that we reported in
Table

1
(the Lee-Carter, the Age-Period-Cohort, and the Renshaw-Haberman models) belong to the GAPC models class.

B.2
Sub-population simulation

In order to ease the exposition, all sub- and superscripts are dropped. To start off, when using the Poisson assumption, it holds that

\operatorname{P}(D=0\mid E)=\text{e}^{-E\mu},

whereas the corresponding probability under the binomial assumption provides us

\operatorname{P}(D=0\mid N)=(1-q)^{N}.

Further, from the formulation of the binomial model used for simulating the data, it was in addition assumed that

\log\frac{q}{1-q}=\delta,~\text{or equivalently, that}~q=\frac{\text{e}^{\delta}}{1+\text{e}^{\delta}}.

Hence, by assuming that
\delta\ll 0
, i.e. that
q\approx 0
, it follows that

\displaystyle(1-q)^{N}

\displaystyle=\exp\left\{N\log\left(1-\frac{\text{e}^{\delta}}{1+\text{e}^{\delta}}\right)\right\}\approx\text{e}^{-N\text{e}^{\delta}}.

This in turn means that if we replace
\delta
with
\log(\Theta)+\delta
, we arrive at

(1-q)^{N}\approx\text{e}^{-N\Theta\text{e}^{\delta}}.

That is,
\Theta\text{e}^{\delta}
is an approximation of the relative survival model from (
5
) for sufficiently small
q
s.

B.3
Age, period and cohort data classification

While the data from epidemiological studies are tabulated by cohorts, insurance mortality data, human mortality data and registry data are tabulated by calendar period (the year of event). In this Section, we discuss the potential issues with tabulated data and show how to adjust the raw data for modelling central mortality rates.

If the individual mortality data were available, the exact value of the time at risk for each individual in the population would be known. However, as mentioned above, our data consisted of an aggregation of the individual lives by age and period. For each age and period, we only observe the the raw lives (and deaths). Modelling mortality rates directly on the raw lives might lead to biased estimates of the underlying true mortality rates
(
Hunt & Blake 2021
)
.

Let us represent our data in the Lexis diagram in
Figure

6
. The figure is an age-period representation of the tabular data for a period
t
and age
x
. In the picture they are represented represented in the square with vertices A,B,C,E. However, the total individuals who have been exposed to the risk of death in period
t
are those represented with the parallelogram A,C,D,E.

Assuming a uniform distribution of the deaths on yearly parallelograms (A,C,D,E in the picture) we compute the average exposure as follows. For
i\in\left\{0,1,2\right\}
,
x\in\mathbb{X}
and
t\in\mathbb{T}

\displaystyle E^{i}_{x,t}=N^{i}_{x,t}/2+N^{i}_{x+1,t+1}/2.

(30)

The average deaths are computed as
D^{i}_{x,t}=(N^{i}_{x,t}-N^{i}_{x+1,t+1})/2
. For details on the calculation, we refer to
Carstensen & Keiding 2005
. Further adjustments are required for computing the exposure at age
0
and in elder classes
89+
, see, e.g.,
Hunt & Blake 2021
;
Carstensen & Keiding 2005
for details, as modelling youngest and elderly ages goes beyond the scope of this manuscript.

The pre-processing step described in this section enables us to model the central mortality rates that we discussed in this paper.
