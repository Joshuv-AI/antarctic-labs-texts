Blood urea nitrogen as a predictor of 28-day mortality in ICU patients with multiple myeloma: A retrospective cohort study (MIMIC-IV, 2008–2019)
Authors: Tianbi Lan, Xiaoli Wang, Guang Tu, Zhonglan Cai, Ling Xu, Yijun Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Multiple myeloma patients frequently develop acute kidney injury; yet, the prognostic value of early blood urea nitrogen (BUN) in the critical care setting remains unclear. This retrospective cohort study included 304 critically ill adults with multiple myeloma to evaluate BUN as a readily available biomarker for early risk stratification. Using the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care-IV 3.1 database (2008–2019), we analyzed first-time intensive care unit admissions. BUN and all covariates were obtained within 24 hours of admission. Cox regression, restricted cubic splines, and Kaplan–Meier analyses estimated the association between BUN and 28-day all-cause mortality. Mean age was 72.6 ± 10.8 years; 61.5% were male. BUN tertiles were T1 <23 mg/dL, T2 23–44 mg/dL, and T3 >44 mg/dL. After multivariable adjustment, each 1 mg/dL rise in BUN increased mortality risk by 1% (hazard ratio [HR] 1.01, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.01, 1.02). Compared with T1, 28-day mortality risk was higher in T2 (HR 3.47, 95% CI 1.45, 8.30) and T3 (HR 6.96, 95% CI 2.61, 18.61). A threshold effect was observed at 52.57 mg/dL, above which risk plateaued. Survival curves diverged early and remained separated across tertiles (log-rank P < .001); findings were consistent across predefined subgroups. These findings suggest that early BUN measurement can identify high-risk patients who may benefit from intensified monitoring and timely intervention. BUN levels measured within the first 24 hours of intensive care unit admission are independent, dose-dependent predictors of 28-day mortality in critically ill patients with multiple myeloma, supporting the use of BUN for early risk stratification. As an inexpensive and universally available biomarker, BUN holds promise for guiding clinical decision-making and resource allocation in this vulnerable population.

1. Introduction

Multiple myeloma is a plasma-cell malignancy characterized by uncontrolled clonal proliferation, monoclonal immunoglobulin secretion, and progressive bone marrow failure.[1] Beyond skeletal destruction and hematological impairment, the disease frequently disrupts extra-osseous organs[2,3]; acute kidney injury (AKI) is the most common and clinically significant complication, occurring in 30% to 50% of patients at diagnosis and in up to 70% during their disease course.[4] This high incidence of AKI underscores the importance of early prognostic markers, such as blood urea nitrogen (BUN), as demonstrated in recent studies.[5] Our study aims to fill the gap in understanding the role of early BUN measurement in the critical care setting for patients with multiple myeloma. Pathophysiologically, filtered free light chains precipitate with Tamm–Horsfall protein in the distal nephron, forming obstructing casts and inciting an inflammatory cascade that culminates in tubulointerstitial fibrosis.[4,6,7] Hypercalcemia, volume depletion, nephrotoxic antimyeloma agents, and sepsis further amplify renal insult, resulting in rapid rises in BUN and creatinine.[8,9]

In the critical care setting, early prognostic biomarkers are essential for risk stratification and clinical decision-making. Recent studies have demonstrated the prognostic value of BUN in diverse intensive care unit (ICU) populations, including patients with sepsis-associated delirium,[5] acute pulmonary edema,[10] and general critically ill patients.[11] For instance, Zhao et al[5] found that BUN trajectories independently predicted 28-day mortality in sepsis-associated delirium, while Tu et al[10] and Deng et al[11] reported significant associations between admission BUN and mortality in acute pulmonary edema and multicenter ICU cohorts, respectively. These findings highlight BUN as a simple, inexpensive, and universally available biomarker with significant potential for early risk stratification. However, whether these observations extend to patients with multiple myeloma – a population characterized by unique renal pathophysiology involving cast nephropathy and light chain toxicity – remains unexplored.

Whether BUN measured within the first 24 hours of ICU admission provides independent prognostic information for 28-day all-cause mortality among patients with multiple myeloma remains unknown. Recent studies have highlighted the prognostic significance of BUN in various clinical settings, including sepsis and critical illness.[10,11] Our study aims to extend these findings specifically to patients with multiple myeloma, a population with unique pathophysiological challenges. Leveraging the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care-IV (MIMIC-IV) database, we sought to determine if higher BUN concentrations are dose-dependently associated with increased mortality after adjustment for illness severity, comorbidities, and other early laboratory parameters. Our study adds to the growing body of literature on the prognostic value of BUN in critically ill patients, echoing recent findings.[5,10,11]

2. Methods

2.1. Study design and data source

This retrospective cohort study utilized the MIMIC-IV database, version 3.1 (Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center, Boston).[12] The database contains deidentified clinical data on patients admitted to the ICU between 2008 and 2019. Ethical approval was not required for this study because MIMIC-IV is a publicly available, deidentified database for which the Massachusetts Institute of Technology and Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center have already obtained Institutional Review Board approval. The requirement for individual patient consent was waived because all data are fully deidentified in accordance with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act Safe Harbor provisions. Author Guang Tu completed the CITI “Data or Specimens Only Research” course (certification number 65828445), obtained institutional approval, and was responsible for data extraction.

2.2. Patient selection

We identified adults (≥18 years) with an International Classification of Diseases, Ninth or Tenth Revision, diagnosis code for multiple myeloma (20300, 20301, 20302, C9000, C9001, C9002) who had a first-time ICU admission (Fig. 1). Patients were excluded if BUN values within 24 hours of ICU admission were missing. The final analytic cohort comprised 304 patients.

Flowchart of patient inclusion. BUN = blood urea nitrogen, ICU = intensive care unit, MIMIC-IV = Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care-IV.

2.3. Data collection

All variables were extracted within 24 hours of ICU admission. Demographic data included age (years), sex (male/female), and race (White, non-White). Physiological variables comprised heart rate (beats/minute), systolic and diastolic blood pressure (mm Hg), temperature (°C), oxygen saturation measured by pulse oximetry (SpO2, %), hemoglobin concentration (g/dL), platelet count (×109/L), white blood cell count (×109/L), glucose (mg/dL), creatinine (mg/dL), and BUN (mg/dL). Illness severity was quantified with the Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II, range 0–163, higher scores indicate greater severity) and the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score (range 0–24, higher scores indicate greater organ dysfunction). Relevant comorbidities – heart failure, cerebrovascular disease, chronic pulmonary disease, and diabetes mellitus – were identified from the MIMIC-IV comorbidity mapping tables, which are derived from ICD-9/10 diagnosis codes recorded prior to or during the index admission. The selection of these comorbidities was based on their established prognostic significance in critical illness and multiple myeloma, as well as their availability in the MIMIC-IV comorbidity tables. Missing data were present in <5% of observations for all covariates except race (8.2%). We employed multiple imputation by chained equations with 20 imputed datasets using the mice package (version 3.14.0 ) in R (The R Foundation, Vienna, Austria), assuming data were missing at random. Continuous variables were imputed using predictive mean matching, while categorical variables were imputed using logistic regression. Sensitivity analyses comparing complete-case analysis with imputed datasets yielded consistent results.

2.4. Outcomes

The primary outcome was 28-day all-cause mortality, ascertained from the date of ICU admission using the MIMIC-IV in-hospital mortality flags and Social Security Death Index linkage.

2.5. Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics are presented as means ± standard deviation, medians (intertertile range), or counts (percentages) across BUN tertiles. BUN was categorized into tertiles (T1 <23 mg/dL, T2 23–44 mg/dL, T3 >44 mg/dL) based on its distribution in our cohort to ensure equal group sizes and facilitate clinical interpretability, consistent with prior studies examining BUN in critical illness.[5,10,13] Differences were tested using analysis of variance, Kruskal–Wallis or χ2 tests, as appropriate.

Univariate Cox proportional hazards models evaluated the association between each baseline variable and 28-day mortality. Variables for multivariable models were selected a priori based on clinical relevance, biological plausibility, and prior evidence of association with mortality in multiple myeloma and critical illness.[5] We avoided stepwise selection to prevent overfitting and preserve reproducibility. BUN was analyzed both as a continuous variable (per mg/dL) and by tertiles. Multivariable analyses were performed in 3 hierarchical models: model 1 adjusted for age, sex, and race; model 2 added physiological variables, laboratory indices, SAPS II, and SOFA scores; model 3 further adjusted for comorbidities. Variance inflation factors were calculated to assess multicollinearity; all variance inflation factor values were <3.0, indicating no substantial collinearity among covariates. The proportional hazards assumption was verified using Schoenfeld residuals. Global and variable-specific tests showed no significant violations (all P > .05), and visual inspection of scaled residual plots confirmed no systematic time-dependent patterns.

To characterize potential nonlinear effects, restricted cubic splines with 4 knots placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of the BUN distribution (11, 19, 36, and 78 mg/dL) were fitted, with the median BUN (23 mg/dL) as the reference value. A two-piecewise Cox regression model was used to estimate the threshold effect; the turning point (52.57 mg/dL) was identified by maximizing the log-likelihood ratio across candidate values (15–80 mg/dL), with bootstrap resampling (1000 iterations) used to estimate the 95% confidence interval (CI) for the threshold. Kaplan–Meier curves compared survival across BUN tertiles, with differences assessed by the log-rank test. Prespecified subgroup analyses examined consistency of the BUN–mortality association across clinically relevant strata. All analyses were conducted with R Statistical Software (version 4.2.2; The R Foundation) and the Free Statistics Analysis Platform (version 2.2; Beijing FreeClinical Medical Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China).[14] Two-sided P < .05 was considered statistically significant. The events-per-variable ratio in our final model (304 patients, 87 events, 12 covariates) was approximately 7:1, which is generally considered acceptable for multivariable survival analysis.[14]

3. Results

3.1. Study population

Of 3673 patients with multiple myeloma identified in MIMIC-IV, 304 met the inclusion criteria (Fig. 1). The mean age was 72.6 ± 10.8 years, 61.5% were male, and 68.1% were non-White. Median BUN was 23 mg/dL (interquartile range 12–44 mg/dL). Patients in the highest BUN tertile (T3) were older, had higher SAPS II and SOFA scores, and presented more frequently with heart failure and diabetes (Table 1).

Other significant predictors included age, heart rate, SpO2, platelet count, glucose, SAPS II, SOFA score, heart failure, and cerebrovascular disease.

After full adjustment (model 3), BUN remained independently associated with 28-day mortality (per mg/dL; HR 1.01, 95% CI 1.01, 1.02). A significant dose-response relationship was observed (P for trend < .001).

3.4. Nonlinear and threshold effects

The restricted cubic splines curve revealed a steep, nonlinear increase in mortality risk up to approximately 52.57 mg/dL, after which the curve plateaued (P for overall < .001; P for nonlinearity = .001; Fig. 2). Piecewise Cox modeling confirmed a turning point at 52.57 mg/dL (Table 4). Below this threshold, each 1 mg/dL increase in BUN conferred a 7% increase in mortality (HR 1.07, 95% CI 1.03, 1.12), whereas no significant association was observed above 52.57 mg/dL (HR 0.99, 95% CI 0.96, 1.04).

3.5. Survival analysis

Kaplan–Meier curves showed a stepwise decline in 28-day survival across BUN tertiles (Fig. 3). The 28-day survival probabilities were 91% in T1 (<23 mg/dL), 76% in T2 (23–44 mg/dL), and 64% in T3 (>44 mg/dL), corresponding to absolute mortality rates of 9%, 24%, and 36%, respectively (log-rank P < .001). Compared with T1, the absolute risk increase was 15 percentage points for T2 and 27 percentage points for T3. The association between BUN and mortality was consistent across age, sex, race, and comorbidity strata (all P for interaction ≥ .05; Fig. 4).

3.6. Sensitivity analyses

Excluding patients with BUN ≥ 100 mg/dL (n = 24) yielded consistent results, with the highest BUN tertile (T3) showing a 10-fold increased mortality risk compared with the lowest tertile (T1) in the fully adjusted model (HR 10.00, 95% CI 3.66, 27.28). Alternative categorizations using quartiles or clinical cutoffs showed similar dose-response patterns. The likelihood ratio test favored the categorical over the continuous linear model (P = .02), though both approaches demonstrated significant associations (Table 5).

4. Discussion

In this retrospective cohort of 304 critically ill adults with multiple myeloma treated in a single academic ICU, BUN measured within 24 hours of admission was a powerful, dose-dependent predictor of 28-day all-cause mortality. After full adjustment for demographics, severity scores, and comorbidities, each 1 mg/dL rise in BUN increased the hazard of death by 1%, and a clear inflection point at 52.57 mg/dL separated patients at markedly higher risk. The association was consistent across age, sex, race, and comorbidity strata, underscoring the immediate bedside utility of early BUN for risk stratification in this high-risk population.

BUN is not merely a surrogate of glomerular filtration; it integrates nitrogen load, catabolic rate, and neurohormonal activation.[15] Experimental work indicates that high urea concentrations trigger oxidative stress, alter endothelial nitric-oxide metabolism, and perpetuate systemic inflammation, mechanisms that may exacerbate multiorgan dysfunction in the critically ill.[16–18] In myeloma-associated AKI, rapid BUN accumulation reflects not only reduced glomerular clearance but also heightened catabolism driven by tumor burden, systemic inflammation, and frequent precipitants such as sepsis or hypercalcemia.[19–21] Experimental work demonstrates that elevated urea induces oxidative stress, disrupts endothelial nitric-oxide signaling, and up-regulates pro-apoptotic pathways, which may synergize with ICU-specific insults such as vasopressor exposure and mechanical ventilation to accelerate multiorgan failure.[22–24] In addition, elevated BUN correlates with intrarenal ammonia production, which may activate the alternative complement pathway and amplify tubular injury.[25] Our observation that BUN retained prognostic value after adjustment for creatinine and SOFA score supports the notion that BUN conveys additive information reflecting both filtration loss and systemic inflammatory burden. This aligns with recent findings by Tu et al[10] and Deng et al,[11] who similarly noted the independent prognostic value of BUN in their respective cohorts.

Our findings are consistent with and extend prior evidence of BUN’s prognostic utility across diverse ICU populations. In critically ill patients, Deng et al[11] reported that admission BUN predicted 28-day mortality with an adjusted HR of 1.02 per mg/dL, comparable to our estimate (HR 1.01). Similarly, Tu et al[10] identified BUN as an independent predictor in acute pulmonary edema, with the highest quartile showing a 2.3-fold increased mortality risk, substantially lower than the 7-fold risk we observed in the highest BUN tertile of myeloma patients. This disparity likely reflects the unique pathophysiology of cast nephropathy in myeloma, where BUN rises precipitously due to tubular obstruction and catabolism, portending worse outcomes than in other ICU conditions. Notably, Zhao et al[5] demonstrated that BUN trajectories in sepsis-associated delirium provided dynamic prognostic information, suggesting that incorporating serial BUN measurements could further enhance risk prediction in myeloma patients. Collectively, these studies establish BUN as a robust, generalizable prognostic biomarker across ICU settings, with particularly pronounced effects in populations with AKI.

Earlier studies of renal biomarkers in myeloma were generally restricted to outpatient hemo-oncology clinics, chronic dialysis cohorts, or single-center ICU series with fewer than 100 patients.[26–28] Blood samples were typically drawn days after admission or only once dialysis had been initiated, thereby overlooking the decisive early window – usually within the first 24 hours – when rapid pathophysiological changes most influence critical care decisions.[28,29] Moreover, these investigations often treated creatinine as the principal – if not exclusive – surrogate for renal injury, thereby missing the distinct pathophysiology of cast nephropathy,[30,31] in which massive precipitation of free light chains with Tamm–Horsfall protein triggers acute tubular inflammation and accelerated catabolism, allowing BUN to rise steeply while creatinine remains relatively stable.[4,6,7] By leveraging a high-resolution ICU database with minute-level laboratory timestamps, enforcing uniform resuscitation and renal replacement protocols within a single center, and adjusting for contemporaneous illness-severity scores, our study closes these methodological gaps and provides evidence that is more precise, internally consistent, and immediately applicable to bedside decision-making in the ICU.

The identification of a clear threshold at 52.6 mg/dL suggests a readily implementable risk stratification strategy. BUN could be integrated into existing ICU risk scores, such as SOFA or SAPS II, as a dynamic renal component, or used as a standalone trigger for intensified monitoring. For example, patients with admission BUN > 53 mg/dL might automatically trigger nephrology consultation, daily creatinine monitoring, and early consideration of antimyeloma therapy. Future studies should evaluate whether incorporating BUN into machine learning-based prediction models improves discrimination beyond current severity scores. Additionally, the simplicity and universal availability of BUN make it particularly suitable for resource-limited settings where sophisticated biomarkers (e.g., NGAL, cystatin C) are unavailable. Prospective validation of BUN-guided protocols, such as protocolized renal replacement therapy initiation or adjusted antimicrobial dosing, could establish its role in precision medicine for myeloma-associated AKI.

Despite its single-center design, our study benefits from a homogenous ICU protocol, consistent laboratory assays, and complete capture of consecutive admissions, minimizing inter-site variability. BUN is universally available within minutes, incurs no extra cost, and integrates seamlessly into existing workflows. The threshold of ≈53 mg/dL identified here provides a readily recognizable high-risk group for whom earlier nephrology consultation, intensified hemodynamic monitoring, or timely initiation of antimyeloma therapy might translate into survival gains. Future research should build on these findings and explore the potential for BUN-guided interventions, as suggested by recent studies.[10,11]

4.1. Limitations

Our study is subject to several limitations. First, although the single-center design minimized inter-site variability, it limits external validity and may not reflect practices in resource-limited settings or centers with different dialysis policies. Second, selection bias may have occurred because we excluded patients missing BUN values within 24 hours (n = 2), who may have had more severe illness or died before laboratory testing. However, the excluded patients represented a small proportion of the total cohort, and their baseline characteristics were similar to those of the included. Third, residual confounding by unmeasured variables – such as specific antimyeloma regimens, free light chain concentration, or exact timing of renal replacement therapy – cannot be excluded. Additionally, the retrospective design precludes causal inference; prospective interventional trials are required to determine whether BUN-guided management improves outcomes.

Fourth, we measured BUN only at a single time point (within 24 hours of admission), precluding evaluation of dynamic BUN trajectories. Prior studies in sepsis have demonstrated that BUN changes over time may provide additional prognostic information beyond baseline values.[5] Future research should assess whether incorporating serial BUN measurements or BUN clearance rates improves risk prediction in myeloma-associated AKI.

Fifth, our findings require external validation in independent cohorts, particularly in non-ICU settings (e.g., emergency department, general wards) and in patients receiving novel antimyeloma therapies (e.g., daratumumab, carfilzomib) that may alter renal injury patterns. The single-center design and specific patient population (predominantly older adults with high illness severity) limit generalizability to younger patients or those with less aggressive disease.

5. Conclusion

In critically ill patients with multiple myeloma, a single BUN measurement obtained within the first day of ICU stay is a robust, independent predictor of 28-day mortality. As a simple, inexpensive, and universally available biomarker, BUN offers immediate utility for early ICU risk stratification, requiring no specialized equipment or additional cost beyond routine laboratory testing. These data validate early BUN as a clinically meaningful risk indicator that can be readily incorporated into bedside decision-making and provide a rational basis for future interventional trials testing BUN-guided management strategies in this vulnerable population. The identification of a clear risk threshold (≈53 mg/dL) further enhances its practical applicability for identifying high-risk patients who may benefit from intensified monitoring and timely intervention.
