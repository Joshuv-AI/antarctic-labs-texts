Myogenic enhancer snatching promotes adipogenic differentiation during epigenetic reprogramming mediated by lineage-specific transcription factors
Authors: Mingyang Hu, Zhixiang Xu, Jiahao Sun, Linmi Li, Pengxuan Liu, Honghong Zhou, Xiaolong Qi, Weike Ren, Yujia Liu, Shuya Chen, Renzhuo Kuang, Daoyuan Wang, Yaping Guo, Ge Sun, Shuhong Zhao, Mengjin Zhu, Li Li, Yunxia Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe myoblast-to-adipocyte trans-differentiation model is crucial for studying the regulatory mechanisms underlying ectopic fat deposition and cell fate determination. However, the comprehensive dynamics of chromatin accessibility and interactions during this process remain largely unexplored. To address this, we performed an integrative analysis of bulk and single-cell multi-omics data (ATAC-seq, RNA-seq), and Hi-C data on an adipogenesis model in C2C12 myoblasts. Our findings revealed significant reprogramming of chromatin accessibility driven by adipogenic lineage-specific transcription factors (TFs), particularly Cebps and Stats, during trans-differentiation. Notably, 63.46% of the distal open chromatin regions with increased accessibility were shared between myogenesis and adipogenesis. Further joint analysis with Hi-C data suggests that these pre-established enhancers in myoblasts undergo a "regulatory redirection," shifting their influence from myogenic to adipogenic genes—a phenomenon we termed "enhancer snatch." This model was validated by knocking out a "snatched" enhancer (Enhancer-R/L), which was found to be a critical regulator of both Rbl1 and Lbp expression. Overall, our study uncovers a mechanism that pre-established enhancers in original cell lineage are predominantly utilized by lineage-specific TFs during chromatin reprogramming, thereby altering cell's differentiation trajectory.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00018-025-05901-8.Keywords: Trans-differentiation, Cell fate, Chromatin interactions, Enhancer snatch

Introduction

Cell fate is fundamentally linked to organismal development and the onset of various diseases. The intricate interplay among chromatin remodeling, 3D genome architecture, and gene transcription has been established as a pivotal driving force behind cell fate determination [1–3]. Various trans-differentiation models, which involve the direct reprogramming of somatic cells from one lineage to another, have been widely used to elucidate the epigenetic mechanisms underlying cell fate determination. These models include the conversion of fibroblasts to neurons, cardiomyocytes, skeletal muscle cells, hepatocytes, and the trans-differentiation of B cells to macrophages [4–8].

Ectopic fat deposition (EFD) in skeletal muscle cells is positively correlated with insulin resistance and an increased risk of metabolic disorders, including Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD), type 2 diabetes mellitus, obesity, and aging-related sarcopenia [9]. Furthermore, it has been reported that a high-fat diet can promote EFD in skeletal muscle tissue [10], which is closely associated with intramuscular fat content in livestock. Notably, intramuscular fat is essential for enhancing the flavor and palatability of meat [11]. Therefore, understanding the molecular mechanisms governing muscle EFD is important not only for the pathophysiology of obesity-associated disorders in humans but also for improving meat quality in livestock for food industry.

Previous research has identified that EFD in muscle primarily originates from the differentiation of adipocyte precursors in the blood circulation or mesenchymal stem cells in muscle tissue [12, 13]. However, some studies have also demonstrated that dietary interventions, such as those rich in long-chain n-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs), can significantly increase intramuscular fat content [14]. Notably, DHA and EPA, as key n-3 PUFAs, have been demonstrated to directly promote adipogenic differentiation of myoblasts [15, 16]. Furthermore, a class of antidiabetic drugs, thiazolidinediones (TZDs), have been shown to induce adipogenic trans-differentiation in both C2C12 myoblasts and satellite cells [17]. These findings collectively indicate that external stimuli may drive adipocyte differentiation from muscle progenitors, thereby also contributing to EFD. Consequently, the adipogenesis model of C2C12 cells effectively reflects the process of cellular reprogramming and is relevant for studying non-canonical EFD mechanisms under exogenous stimuli.

However, a comprehensive understanding of chromatin accessibility reprogramming and the dynamic changes in chromatin interactions within this myoblast-to-adipocyte trans-differentiation model remains largely unexplored. Insights into muscle-fat differentiation and the underlying gene regulation during cell fate determination can be achieved by integrating analyses of gene expression, chromatin accessibility, and three-dimensional genomic organization during both myogenesis and adipogenesis. To address this gap, we integrated bulk and single-cell multi-omics data (ATAC-seq, RNA-seq), and Hi-C data from the adipogenic trans-differentiation of myoblasts and normal myogenesis and identified changes in chromatin accessibility and enhancer-promoter (E-P) interaction loops that lead to the differential expression of genes critical for adipogenesis. Our study provides a systematic investigation of the epigenetic mechanisms governing chromatin accessibility and chromatin interaction in the adipogenic trans-differentiation model, offering new insights into cell fate determination.

Results

Cocktail inducers cause adipogenesis-related epigenetic changes in myoblasts

To induce adipogenic differentiation in C2C12 myoblasts, we applied an adipogenic differentiation cocktail as described in a previous study [18]. This approach successfully promoted the differentiation of myoblasts toward an adipogenic lineage (Fig. 1A & Fig. S1A). To compare the epigenetic mechanisms involved in adipogenic trans-differentiation with those in normal myogenic differentiation, we selected three representative cellular states for ATAC-seq, RNA-seq, and Hi-C data collection: (1) myoblasts, representing the undifferentiated state of C2C12 cells; (2) myotubes, representing the fully differentiated state during normal myogenesis; and (3) adipocytes derived from the trans-differentiation of myoblasts, representing the fully differentiated state in the adipogenesis model (Fig. 1A & Fig. S1B).

Principal component analysis (PCA) of chromatin accessibility and gene expression levels revealed that adipocytes derived from the trans-differentiation of myoblasts exhibited distinct profiles compared to both myoblasts and myotubes (Fig. 1B). Specifically, the expression of the adipogenic gene Fabp4 and lipid transfer gene Lbp were markedly upregulated in adipocytes derived from trans-differentiated myoblasts, in contrast to the profiles observed in myoblasts and myotubes, and the chromatin accessibility in their surrounding regions showed the same changing trends (Fig. 1C).

Further Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) demonstrated that adipogenesis-related genes were significantly enriched among the upregulated genes when comparing adipocytes with myoblasts and adipocytes with myotubes. However, these genes were not significantly enriched in either the upregulated or downregulated gene sets during myogenic differentiation (Fig. 1D). These findings confirm that the C2C12 cell-to-adipocyte trans-differentiation model was successfully established and underscore the importance of investigating the changes in chromatin accessibility and transcriptome dynamics during adipogenic trans-differentiation.

Comparative analysis of multiple differentiation models reveals core genes regulating the adipogenic trans-differentiation of myoblasts

The extent of gene expression changes often correlates with the degree of phenotypic variation. In our study, we identified 6,294 significantly differentially expressed (DE) genes (|log2FC|≥ 1, FDR < 0.05) during the adipogenic trans-differentiation of myoblasts. This number of DE genes is notably higher than that observed during normal myogenesis (Fig. S2A), likely because the expression changes in these 6,294 genes reflect processes involved in both adipogenic trans-differentiation and myogenic differentiation.

Previous studies have shown that when myoblast cells reach a relatively high density and are in close contact, they are primed to differentiate into myotubes [20]. Therefore, a portion of the myoblasts naturally differentiated into myotubes during the trans-differentiation process, which has an impact on exploration of transcriptional regulation mechanism for adipogenesis. We think that the noise can be effectively reduced by comparing the DE genes of the two processes and retaining the specific DE genes during adipogenic trans-differentiation process. To validate the effectiveness of this strategy, we first compared previously published DE miRNAs identified by miRNA-seq data from adipogenic trans-differentiation of myoblasts [18] and normal myogenesis [21]. This comparison effectively narrowed down the list of adipogenesis-specific DE miRNAs (Fig. S2B), and three of these miRNAs were previously reported to play key roles in adipogenic differentiation [18]. These results suggest that this comparative analysis is effective in removing noise and identifying core DE genes. Consequently, by comparing DE genes during adipogenic trans-differentiation with those from normal myogenesis, we excluded nearly 50% of the DE genes, thereby refining the list to identify adipogenesis-specific DE genes Fig. 2A).

To further pinpoint the core DE genes regulating adipogenesis, we analyzed RNA-seq data from two additional adipogenic models: the differentiation of 3T3-L1 cells to beige adipocytes and the differentiation of pre-adipocytes to adipocytes [22, 23] (Fig. 2B-C). Through the integration of these three sets of adipogenic RNA-seq data, we ultimately identified 219 core DE genes that regulate the adipogenic trans-differentiation of myoblasts (Fig. 2D). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed that the biological processes regulated by these 219 genes are indeed related to adipogenic differentiation (Fig. 2E). These findings confirm that adipogenic genes are actively engaged during the trans-differentiation of myoblasts into adipocytes.

A small fraction of the super enhancers changed during adipogenic trans-differentiation of myoblasts

Given that super enhancers (SEs) largely define the identities of specific cell types [24], we compared SEs among undifferentiated myoblasts, myotubes derived from normal myogenesis, and adipocytes derived from the trans-differentiation of myoblasts. Our results revealed that the number of SEs is similar across the three cellular states (Fig. 3A), and a transformation in the types of SEs was observed during differentiation (Fig. 3B). Further analysis showed that the majority of SEs is shared among the three cellular states (Fig. 3C-D). Specifically, only 6.72% (n = 125) of SEs play a unique regulatory role in adipocytes derived from the trans-differentiation of myoblasts (Fig. 3C-D). Moreover, the genes associated with these adipogenesis-specific SEs exhibit higher expression levels in adipocytes compared to myoblasts and myotubes (Fig. 3E).

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of these 125 adipogenesis-specific SEs revealed that they are closely associated with muscle differentiation-related biological processes, despite being identified only during adipogenic trans-differentiation (Fig. S3A). Based on this analysis, we identified a subset of 32 SEs that are specifically linked to adipogenic biological processes (Fig. 3F-G), and there was an enrichment of adipogenic transcription factors (TFs) in these 32 SEs, such as Stats, Cebps, and Foxk1 (Fig. S3B).

To further investigate the unexpected regulatory nature of these 125 SEs, we performed a statistical analysis of their signal intensities. The comparison revealed that SEs shared among the three cellular states exhibit significantly higher signal intensities in adipocytes than those adipogenesis-specific (Fig. 3H). Additionally, although these adipogenesis-specific SEs were identified as typical enhancers in myotubes, the signal intensities of them in myotubes were comparable to the bottom-ranked 125 SEs of myotubes but were significantly higher than the signal intensities of randomly selected typical enhancers in myotubes (Fig. 3I-J). These findings suggest that the 125 adipogenesis-specific SEs also exhibit strong ATAC-seq signals during normal myogenesis.

Overall, our results indicate that adipogenic transcription factors can induce changes in a small fraction of SEs during the adipogenic trans-differentiation of myoblasts. This results highlight the importance of investigating global changes in chromatin accessibility for a more comprehensive understanding of this process.

Activation of adipogenic transcription factors mediates chromatin accessibility reprogramming during adipogenic trans-differentiation of myoblasts

Our results indicated that super enhancers exhibit minimal changes during the adipogenic trans-differentiation of myoblasts, suggesting that dynamic changes in chromatin accessibility, particularly in non-SE regions, warrant closer attention. To investigate this, we identified 93,169 significantly differentially accessible open chromatin regions (OCRs) (|log2FC|≥ 1, FDR < 0.01) between myoblasts and adipocytes derived from myoblast trans-differentiation (Fig. 4A-B). Cluster analysis of these differentially accessible OCRs based on ATAC-seq signals across the three cellular states revealed that, in contrast to myogenesis, a subset of OCRs specifically changed during adipogenesis (Fig. 4C).

Given the crucial role of transcription factors (TFs) in chromatin accessibility reprogramming, it is essential to consider changes in TF binding when identifying key regulatory elements. Previous studies have demonstrated that DNA sequences bound by TFs are protected from enzymatic cleavage by nucleases such as DNase I and Tn5 transposase [25]. Consequently, these TF-binding sites exhibit a depletion of cleavage signals, a phenomenon referred to as a "footprint," which serves as a marker for TF occupancy and the footprint regions can be identified in whole genome based on ATAC-seq data. So, we first identified 750,443 footprints in the OCRs of adipocytes from trans-differentiation to represent TF binding sites (Fig. S4A). Statistical analysis showed that the number of footprints within OCRs also changed during adipogenic trans-differentiation, and the trend in chromatin accessibility changes mirrored the changes in footprint numbers (Fig. S4B). This finding underscores the regulatory significance of TF binding alterations in chromatin accessibility. Based on this, we identified 60,459 differential OCRs with changes in both ATAC-seq signals and footprint numbers (|log2FC|≥ 0.58 & |changed footprint number|≥ 1) between myoblasts and adipocytes from trans-differentiation (Fig. 4D).

To obtain accurate regulatory relationships between OCR and genes, we generated in situ Hi-C data of myoblasts and adipocytes derived from the trans-differentiation of myoblasts based on the linker-based Hi-C 3.0 method [26–28], separately obtaining 442 million and 534 million valid pairs, and identified 37,781 and 58,591 loops (Fig. S4C-E). Then the OCRs were assigned to the expressed genes utilizing chromatin interaction information. Subsequent associative analysis revealed that alterations in chromatin accessibility or TF binding within these open chromatin regions exert significant regulatory effects on the expression of associated genes (Fig. 4E-F & Fig. S4F). These findings suggest a progressive regulatory relationship between TF binding, chromatin accessibility, and gene expression.

Considering the cell specificity of differentially accessible OCRs as indicated by clustering results (Fig. 4C), we categorized OCRs with increased chromatin accessibility and TF binding into two groups based on whether they were detected exclusively in adipocytes from trans-differentiation. The analysis revealed that 36.64% (14,461) of these OCRs were specifically activated during adipogenesis, while the remaining 63.36% (25,010) were pre-established in myoblasts or myotubes (Fig. 4G & Fig. S4G). Motif enrichment analysis showed that TFs such as Cebps and Stats were significantly enriched in adipogenesis-specific OCRs, whereas TFs such as Ctcf, Myog, and Myf5 were significantly enriched in OCRs with decreased chromatin accessibility and TF binding during adipogenesis (Fig. 4H & Fig. S4H).

To validate the changes in accessibility of these adipogenesis-specific OCRs, we randomly selected four adipogenesis-specific OCRs and conducted a Dual-Luciferase reporter assay. Compared to myoblasts or myotubes, all OCRs showed significantly higher reporter activity in adipocytes from trans-differentiation (Fig. 4I, Supplementary Table 1). This result confirms that these OCRs indeed play a regulatory role specifically during adipogenic trans-differentiation of myoblasts.

Single-cell multi-omics demonstrated the existence of shared enhancers between adipogenesis and myogenesis

To investigate more clearly the accessibility state of enhancers during the trans-differentiation of myoblasts into adipocytes, we performed single-cell multi-omics sequencing (scRNA-seq + scATAC-seq) on the adipocytes derived from trans-differentiation model (Fig. S5A). Based on the sc-multi-omics data, a total of 4,055 cells were captured, of which 7.89% (320 cells) were identified as doublets using the R package scDblFinder and excluded, leaving 3,735 high-quality cells for downstream analysis (Fig. 5A). Quality assessment revealed median values of 16,701 unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) and 37,235 high-quality ATAC fragments per cell (Fig. 5B). Additionally, the fragment length distribution exhibited clear nucleosomal periodicity, further validating ATAC library quality (Fig. S5B).

Unsupervised clustering based on single-cell transcriptomes information classified cells into 7 distinct populations (Fig. 5C). Marker gene analysis confirmed cell identities (Supplementary Table 2), for example, Sned1, Pparg and Myod1 separately characterized adipose stem and progenitor cells (ASPC), mature adipocytes, and muscle cells (Fig. 5D-E). While only ATAC-based clustering showed limited resolution of cellular heterogeneity, integrative analysis with transcriptomic data significantly improved clustering performance, demonstrating the advantage of multi-omics profiling (Fig. S5C). Statistical analysis of cell numbers in each cell type revealed that myogenic and adipogenic lineages (muscle cells, ASPC, and mature adipocytes) dominated the population, with minor contributions from ciliated cells, fibroblasts and endothelial cells.

Across these 7 clusters, we identified 12,754–53,368 OCRs in each cell type, which were categorized as either transcription start site (TSS)-proximal or distal OCRs based on genomic location (Fig. 5F & S5D). Furthermore, the distal OCRs comparison between adipogenesis (ASPC and mature adipocytes) and myogenesis (muscle cells) showed that 64.24% distal OCRs of adipogenesis were also opened during myogenesis (Fig. 5G), consistent with the results based on our bulk data (Fig. 4G). Notably, motif enrichment analysis revealed that adipogenesis-specific distal OCRs were strongly enriched for C/EBP motifs, far exceeding the enrichment of myogenic factors (e.g., MyoD, MyoG). In contrast, co-accessible OCRs showed comparable enrichment for both adipogenic (C/EBPs) and myogenic (MyoD/MyoG) motifs, suggesting a mechanistic basis for their regulatory role switching during trans-differentiation (Fig. 5H).

Collectively, our single-cell multi-omics data demonstrate that a substantial proportion of enhancers remain co-accessible across distinct cell lineages—independent of mixed population averaging effects—with functional divergence driven by lineage-specific transcription factor occupancy.

Chromatin accessibility reprogramming leads to the reallocation of myogenic enhancers during adipogenic trans-differentiation

Our findings indicate that many enhancers with increased signals during adipogenic trans-differentiation, including all super enhancers (SEs) and over 50% of distal OCRs, were also active in myoblasts or myotubes, suggesting these enhancers initially play roles in myogenesis (Fig. 3C & Fig. S4G). We hypothesize that as adipogenic trans-differentiation begins, these pre-established enhancers are repurposed to regulate adipogenesis-related genes through chromatin interaction reprogramming—a process crucial for cell reprogramming that has not been thoroughly explored.

To investigate this hypothesis, it is necessary to identify specifically established loops during adipogenesis by comparing chromatin interactions between adipogenesis and myogenesis. Considering that the adipocytes derived from the adipogenic trans-differentiation of myoblasts contain a large number of muscle cells in addition to adipocytes, there are numerous myogenesis-related interactions in their Hi-C data. To avoid this noise, we used the PCHi-C data [19] of 3T3-L1 adipocytes to identify key interactions during adipogenesis and validated these interactions using Hi-C data from myoblast-to-adipocyte trans-differentiation model. The results showed that the majority (89.89%) of chromatin loops in 3T3-L1 adipocytes were not detected in myoblasts or myotubes, primarily due to the significantly lower interaction strength of these loops during myogenesis (Fig. S6A-C). This suggests that comparing loops between 3T3-L1 adipocytes and myoblasts or myotubes can help identify adipogenesis-specific loops.

Using this approach, we identified 3,872 adipogenesis-specific loops by applying a tighter filtering criterion with the 10 kb contact matrix of myoblasts and myotubes, ensuring that loops in 3T3-L1 adipocytes had contact values below 10 in myoblasts and myotubes. Notably, the majority (2,952, or 76.24%) of these adipogenesis-specific loops were associated with enhancers that were activated in the adipogenesis model of C2C12 myoblasts, confirming that these loops play regulatory roles during adipogenic trans-differentiation (Fig. 6A-B). In addition, the interaction intensity of these adipogenesis-specific loops also increased during adipogenic trans-differentiation of myoblasts (Fig. 6C).

Further analysis revealed that nearly 60% of the newly established loops during adipogenesis were linked to enhancers shared between myogenesis and adipogenesis but were involved in regulating different genes during myogenesis—a phenomenon we termed "enhancer snatching loops" (Fig. 6A-B). This finding aligns with the ratio of distal OCRs that were opened during both myogenesis and adipogenesis among all distal OCRs with increased chromatin accessibility during adipogenesis (Fig. S4D). Additionally, when these enhancers were repurposed to regulate adipogenic genes, the interaction strength of their original loops during myogenesis was significantly weaker than that of the newly established loops in adipocytes (Fig. S6D). Among the DE genes associated with these enhancer-snatching loops, approximately 50% were adipogenesis-specific DE genes involved in key biological processes such as the WNT signaling pathway, carbohydrate metabolism, and fatty acid metabolism (Fig. 6D-E). These findings further support the idea that enhancers active during myogenesis are repurposed to regulate adipogenic genes as trans-differentiation occurs.

To explore the underlying mechanisms driving the formation of enhancer-snatching loops, we performed motif enrichment analysis on the anchors of these loops. The analysis revealed significant enrichment of both myogenic TFs (e.g., Myog, Fos) and adipogenic TFs (e.g., Cebps, Insr, Stats) at these loop anchors (Fig. 6F). In contrast, motif enrichment analysis of loops associated with adipogenesis-specific enhancers only showed significant enrichment of adipogenic TFs, such as Cebps, Stats, and Foxk1 (Fig. S6E). Subsequently, we performed Cebpβ CUT&Tag in adipocytes derived from the myoblast adipogenesis model (Supplementary Table 3) and downloaded published MyoD ChIP-seq data [29] from proliferative (myoblasts) and differentiated (myotubes) C2C12 cells to demonstrate the motif enrichment results. Notably, ~ 40% of enhancer snatching loop harbored Cebpβ peaks in their enhancer anchors (Fig. S6F). Moreover, the TF signal enrichment heatmap reveals that for the repurposed enhancers associated with enhancer snatching loop, they possess the ability to bind both myogenic (MyoD) and adipogenic (Cebpβ) TFs. However, for the adipogenesis specifically opening enhancer, the binding signals of Cebpβ on them are significantly stronger than that of MyoD (Fig. S6G). The above results indicate that the ability of binding by different lineage-specific TFs is the basis of the "regulatory redirection" of "enhancer snatch" model and the emergence of this model is probably related to changes in the binding of lineage-specific TFs.

To functionally validate the role of these enhancer-snatching events, we knocked out a shared enhancer (Enhancer-R/L) between myogenesis and adipogenesis. This enhancer was associated with the gene Rbl1 during C2C12 myogenesis but was repurposed to regulate the gene Lbp during adipogenic trans-differentiation (Fig. 6G). The results showed that the interaction strength between Enhancer-R/L and the myoblast-related gene RBL1 decreases, while its interaction strength with the adipogenesis-related gene Lbp significantly increases during adipogenic trans-differentiation (Fig. S7A). This interaction change is also consistent with the changes in gene expression levels (Fig. S7B). Knockout of Enhancer-R/L resulted in a significant decrease in Rbl1 expression during normal myogenesis without affecting Lbp expression. However, during adipogenic trans-differentiation, knockout of Enhancer-R/L significantly inhibited the upregulation of Lbp, while leaving Rbl1 expression unaffected (Fig. 6H). These results indicate that during chromatin accessibility reprogramming mediated by adipogenic TFs, myogenic enhancers can be repurposed to regulate adipogenesis-related genes, thereby promoting adipogenic differentiation (Fig. 7).

Discussion

In this study, we present novel insights into the chromatin accessibility and enhancer-promoter interactions that govern cell differentiation, with a particular focus on myogenesis and adipogenesis. By analyzing these epigenetic changes, we describe distinct chromatin states and gene expression profiles that highlight the dynamic regulatory landscapes during these processes. Notably, we introduce the concept of "enhancer snatching loops," a novel set of chromatin loops that play a critical role in determining cell fate. This regulatory mechanism, which has been previously underexplored, emphasizes the importance of chromatin architecture in cell fate transitions.

Our investigation also explored the role of super-enhancers, which are recognized as key regulators of cell identity [24]. We found that, compared to the global reprogramming of chromatin accessibility, changes in super-enhancers during adipogenic trans-differentiation of myoblasts were relatively minor. These changes primarily reflect shifts in highly accessible enhancers rather than providing a comprehensive understanding of the epigenetic mechanisms driving adipogenic trans-differentiation. This observation suggests that super-enhancers alone may not fully account for the complexity of cell fate decisions, particularly in the context of trans-differentiation.

Master transcription factors (TFs) are known to play pivotal roles in reprogramming somatic cell fate [3, 4, 7]. Consistent with previous studies, we observed significant motif enrichment of Cebps and Stats in the open chromatin regions (OCRs) undergoing chromatin accessibility reprogramming during adipogenic trans-differentiation. This indicates that the activation of these TFs is central to the chromatin remodeling that occurs during this process. Although both Cebps and Stats are established regulators of adipocyte differentiation [19, 30], our model of adipogenic trans-differentiation did not show the expected synergistic activation of PPARγ, a key TF typically associated with adipogenesis. We speculate that this may be due to the activation sequence of TFs or the influence of myoblasts on adipocyte differentiation. Previous research has shown that PPARγ and Cebpα are activated in sequential waves during adipocyte differentiation, with Cebpβ and Stats being activated first [19, 30]. Moreover, myofibres have been shown to inhibit adipogenesis, which could potentially disrupt the activation of PPARγ [13]. These findings suggest that Cebps may play a more dominant role than PPARγ in driving ectopic adipocyte differentiation within skeletal muscle tissue.

The role of chromatin remodeling and 3D genome architecture in cell fate determination has been increasingly recognized, with studies focusing on enhancer reprogramming, A/B compartment switching, and dynamic changes in topologically associating domains (TADs) and chromatin loops [4–8]. Traditionally, research has emphasized the importance of newly opened enhancers during trans-differentiation, as they often represent new regulatory relationships essential for transitioning cells from one state to another. However, our findings reveal that the majority of enhancers with increased accessibility during adipogenesis are actually shared with myogenesis. This observation challenges the conventional focus on newly activated enhancers, highlighting that pre-established enhancers also play a crucial role in cell fate transitions.

Further analysis using Hi-C data, along with enhancer knockout experiments, suggests that these shared enhancers can switch their interactions with different gene promoters between myogenesis and adipogenesis. Enhancer snatching represents a distinct regulatory mechanism from previous epigenetic phenomena, such as "transcription factor bookmarking", "pre-marking" and "enhancer reuse". "Transcription factor bookmarking" specifically emphasizes genomic positional memory of TF binding rather than enhancer-mediated regulation or cell fate changes [31]. "Pre-marking" emphasizes the promoters region of genes bound by H3K27me3 in embryonic stem (ES) cells are frequent targets for cancer-specific DNA hypermethylation [32]. "Enhancer reuse" refers to a type of injury response enhancer plays a regulatory role during tissue development and can be reactivated to regulate regeneration genes after the tissue is damaged [33]. Unlike "enhancer snatching", "enhancer reuse" mainly focuses on the transcriptional regulation of regeneration at the tissue level and does not involve cell fate determination. Therefore, the enhancer "snatching" mechanism underscores a previously underappreciated aspect of chromatin reprogramming during trans-differentiation, where pre-established enhancers in original cells were predominantly utilized to redirect cell differentiation outcomes, which were strongly correlated with dynamic changes of lineage-specific TF binding. Nevertheless, while our results strongly support the model of enhancer snatching, the precise mechanisms governing enhancer snatching require further functional dissection. Notably, a subset of enhancer snatching loops were not bound by Cebpβ peaks, suggesting potential mediation by other TFs, such as Stats. Furthermore, perturbation experiments and high-resolution chromatin interaction validation, such as acute TF depletion and 4C-seq, will contribute to establishing the direct causal link between lineage-specific TF binding events and the enhancer snatching.

In conclusion, our study uncovers a complex regulatory network involving both new and pre-established enhancers, TF activation, and chromatin architecture reprogramming that drives adipogenic trans-differentiation. These findings not only advance our understanding of the epigenetic mechanisms underlying cell fate determination but also open new avenues for exploring how chromatin dynamics can be manipulated to influence cell identity in complex biological systems.

Methods

Sample collection and cell culture

The mouse C2C12 cell line was obtained from the Cell Bank of Wuhan University. Cells were cultured in 1 × DMEM (Gibco-BRL) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, 10099133) and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2. To induce adipogenic differentiation, C2C12 myoblasts at 90–100% confluence were switched to DMEM containing 0.5 mM IBMX (Sigma, I5879), 0.25 µM dexamethasone (MCE, HY-14648), 5 µg/mL insulin (Sigma, 91077C), and 20 µM rosiglitazone (MCE, HY-17386), and cultured for 7 days. To induce myogenic differentiation, C2C12 myoblasts at 90% confluence were switched to DMEM containing 3% horse serum (Hyclone, SH30074.03) and cultured for 12 h, 24 h, 48 h, 60 h, and 96 h.

RNA-seq library preparation

Triplicate samples of C2C12 cells were collected at various stages: growth medium (GM), differentiation medium of normal myogenesis (12 h, 24 h, 48 h, 60 h, and 96 h), and trans-differentiation medium of adipogenesis (7 days). Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent following the manufacturer’s instructions (Thermo Scientific, 15596026). Strand-specific RNA-seq libraries were constructed according to Illumina's protocol (Illumina, San Diego, CA) and sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq X Ten platform (PE150).

ATAC-seq library construction and sequencing

ATAC-seq was performed largely following established protocols [34, 35]. Briefly, approximately 50,000 C2C12 cells from proliferation, differentiation (60 h), and trans-differentiation (7 days) conditions were lysed in 50 μL of ATAC-seq lysis buffer (10 mmol/L Tris–HCl, pH 7.4; 10 mmol/L NaCl; 3 mmol/L MgCl2; 0.1% NP-40; 0.1% Tween-20; 0.01% digitonin) and incubated on ice for 5 min. Lysis was halted by adding 1 mL of cold ATAC-buffer (10 mmol/L Tris–HCl, pH 7.4; 10 mmol/L NaCl; 3 mmol/L MgCl2; 0.1% Tween-20). Cells were then centrifuged at 500 RCF for 10 min at 4 ℃ to collect the pellet. The transposition reaction was carried out by adding a mixture containing 25 µL of Illumina Tagment DNA buffer (Cat#: 15,027,866), 16.5 µL of 1 × PBS, 0.5 µL of 1% digitonin, 0.5 µL of 10% Tween-20, 2.5 µL of ddH2O, and 5 µL of Illumina Tagment DNA enzyme (Cat#: 15,027,916) to the cell pellet. This was incubated at 37 ℃ for 1 h in a thermocycler. The transposed DNA was purified using the ZYMO RESEARCH DNA Clean & Concentrator-5 Kit (Cat#:D4013). Library preparation was conducted according to previously described methods. Final libraries were resolved on a 2% high-resolution agarose gel, and fragments between 100–1000 bp were excised and sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq X Ten PE150 platform.

Single-cell multi-omics library construction

We performed single-cell multi-omics library construction according to the instructions of the SeekOne® DD Single Cell Mulitiome Amplification Kit (ATAC + RNA) V1.0 (SeekOne, K02901-08). Firstly, single cell suspension was prepared: cell clusters were scraped using a cell scraper and washed once with PBS. The cells were then lysed with 50 μl RSB lysis buffer(ATAC-RSB buffer、0.1% NP-40、0.1% TWeen-20、0.1%digitonin) for 3 min, the reaction was terminated with ATAC-RSB buffer(10 mM Tris–HCl pH7.4、10 mM NaCl) and the supernatant was removed by centrifugation. The cells were resuspended in 500 μl of 1 × reaction buffer, then filtered through a pluriStrainer Mini 20 µm (pluriSelect, 43–10,020-50), and 7500 cells were collected. Tn-5 digestion and single-cell library construction were then performed according to the kit instructions.

In situ Hi-C library construction

We generate high resolution Hi-C data based on the linker-based Hi-C 3.0 method referring to previous research [26–28]. The cells were fixed using a final concentration of 1% formaldehyde (Sigma, 252549) and 2 mM EGS, respectively. Cells were subsequently lysed, DNA digestion using two flat-ended restriction endonucleases, and ligated adjacent using a two-step method with a bridge linker. Hi-C DNA was obtained by uncrosslinking, followed by library construction.

Cebpβ CUT&Tag library construction

Cells were fixed for 5 min at a final concentration of 0.1% formaldehyde (Sigma, 252549) and then terminated for a further 5 min at a final concentration of 0.2 mM glycine (Sigma, V900144). The supernatant was then washed with PBS after centrifugation and discarded. The cells were lysed using RSB lysis buffer (ATAC-RSB buffer, 0.1% NP-40, 0.1% Tween-20, 0.1% digitonin) for 3 min. The reaction was then terminated using 1 ml ATAC-RSB buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl pH7.4, 10 mM NaCl). The supernatant was discarded by centrifugation, after which the cells were resuspended in 500 μl Wash buffer (20 mM HEPES pH7.3, 150 mM NaCl, 0.25 mM Spermidine, 1 × PI). 300,000 cells were used for the preparation of the Cebpβ (proteintech, 23,431–1-AP) and IGG (Abclonal, AC005), respectively. Subsequent processes followed the instructions for the Hyperactive Universal CUT&Tag Assay Kit for Illumina (Vazyme, TD903).

Dual-Luciferase reporter assay

The enhancer regions, including their chromosomal coordinates and primer sequences (Supplementary Table 1), were PCR-amplified from C2C12 genomic DNA and cloned into the pGL3-Promoter plasmid (Promega, E1761). For transfection, C2C12 cells were transfected at three stages: undifferentiated myoblasts, myotubes from normal myogenesis, and adipocytes from trans-differentiation of myoblasts. Transfection was performed using jetPRIME® (Polyplus, 101000046) and Opti-MEM (Gibco, 11058021), and cells were incubated for 48 h. Fluorescence values were determined using the Dual-Luciferase® Reporter Assay System (Promega, E1910) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, with three technical replicates for each construct.

Plasmid cloning

Lenti-CRISPR-V2 plasmids were linearized using BsmBI restriction endonuclease (NEB, E1602S). The sgRNA sequences, which were listed in Supplementary Table 2, were cloned into the linearized Lenti-CRISPR-V2 plasmids using T4 DNA ligase (Servicebio, G3340-50). The reconstructed plasmids were transformed into Tstbl3 Chemically Competent Cells (Tsingke, 1DH24112), cultured for 16 h, and the plasmids were then extracted using the Endo-free Plasmid Mini Kit II (Omega, D6950-01).

Knockout of enhancer

To verify the regulatory role of enhancers, the paired sgRNA CRISPR Cas9 system was used to knock down enhancer sequences in C2C12 cells. The enhancer information and sgRNA sequences were listed in Supplementary Table 4. Lenti-CRISPR-V2 plasmids were linearized with restriction endonuclease BsmBI (NEB, E1602S). The sgRNA was cloned into linearized Lenti-CRISPR-V2 plasmids using T4 DNA ligase (Servicebio, G3340-50). Next, the reconstructed plasmids were transformed into Tstbl3 Chemically Competent Cell (Tsingke 1DH24112) to grow for 16 h and then plasmid were extracted using Endo-free Plasmid Mini Kit II (omega, D6950-01). HEK293T cells were plated at 90% density prior to transfection. Transfection was performed using jetPRIME (polyplus, 101000046), and the medium was refreshed 6 h later. Virus-containing supernatant was collected 60 h post-transfection, filtered through a 0.45 μm disposable syringe filter (MERCK, SLHVR33RB) and stored at -80℃. The C2C12 cells were infected with lentivirus adding 8ug/ml polybrene (Solarbio H8761) and selected with appropriate antibiotics for at least 4 days after 48 h until no surviving cells remained in an uninfected control plate. Finally, we obtained a mixture of cells with a knockout enhancer. We extracted the DNA and performed gel electrophoresis to determine whether the knockout was successful.

RNA isolation, reverse transcription, and qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted using the TransZol Up Plus RNA Kit (ER501-01-V2) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Reverse transcription was carried out with the TaKaRa PrimeScript™ RT kit (RR047A) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (TOYOBO, QPK-201) in a LightCycler 480 (Roche). The RPL4 gene, which is stably expressed in both C2C12 myoblasts and C2C12 cells undergoing adipogenesis, was selected as the internal control for qRT-PCR validation. Relative gene expression levels were analyzed using the 2-ΔΔ Ct method.

RNA-seq data processing

The mouse reference genome and GTF files were downloaded from the GENCODE database version M11 (https://www.gencodegenes.org/mouse/). RNA-seq data from undifferentiated C2C12 cells, myogenesis-differentiated C2C12 cells (12 h, 24 h, 48 h, 60 h, and 96 h), and adipocytes from trans-differentiation of C2C12 cells (with three replicates each) were processed to calculate gene read counts using RSEM v1.3.0 [36]. Additionally, RNA-seq data from studies on the differentiation of 3T3-L1 cells to beige adipocytes, as well as mouse pre-adipocyte and adipocyte cells [22, 23], were analyzed using the same pipeline.

ATAC-seq data processing

Mapping and broad peak calling for ATAC-seq data were performed using the ENCODE ATAC-seq pipeline (https://github.com/kundajelab/atac_dnase_pipelines). ATAC-seq reads were mapped to the mouse M11 reference genome using Bowtie2 v2.2.6 [37]. Pearson correlation coefficients between replicates were calculated based on read coverage of genomic regions, with a bin size of 2 kb using the multiBamSummary bin function in deepTools v2.5.2 [38]. The aligned files (bam and tn5-tagAlign files) of high-correlation replicates (R > 0.9) were merged,which then were used to call broad peaks by MACS2 v2.2.7 [39] with significant peaks defined at P < 10–10. For ATAC narrow peaks, MACS2 v2.2.7 [39] is used to call narrow peaks with each repeated tn5-tagAlign file, and significant narrow peaks (P < 10−5) overlaps of at least 50% between two replicates were identified using BEDTools v2.26.0 [40].

Differential expression analysis of genes

To validate the trans-differentiation model, expression trends of an adipogenesis-related gene set were mapped across different cellular states using GSEA software v3.0 [41]. The adipogenesis-related gene set was obtained from the AmiGO 2 database (https://amigo.geneontology.org/amigo). Differentially expressed genes between different stages were identified using the DESeq2 R package [42] with thresholds set at |log2FoldChange|≥ 1 and FDR < 0.05.

Dynamic change analysis of chromatin accessibility

The transition between super-enhancers (SEs) and typical enhancers during adipogenic trans-differentiation of C2C12 myoblasts were first investigated. SEs in three cellular states were identified using the ROSE algorithm [24] with default parameters. ATAC-seq data were used to identify SEs specific to adipocytes derived from myoblasts. To validate the transcriptional regulatory role of adipogenesis-specific super-enhancers, the gene nearest to the SE within ± 500 kb was assigned to this SE [24] and the expression of these genes associated with adipogenesis-specific super-enhancers among three cellular states were compared.

For dynamic chromatin accessibility analysis of all open chromatin regions, bam files from undifferentiated myoblasts, myotubes, and adipocytes were converted to HOMER-style tag directories using the makeTagDirectory command of HOMER [43]. Then the narrow ATAC-seq peaks of myoblasts and adipocytes were merged with BEDTools v2.26.0 [40]. The merged peaks and HOMER-style tag directory were used to calculate log2FoldChange (log2FC) and P-values for differential accessibility between myoblasts and adipocytes using the annotatePeaks.pl and getDiffExpression.pl commands of HOMER [43]. The merged narrow peaks with |log2FC|≥ 1.5 and P < 0.01 were identified as differentially accessible narrow peaks. The differential accessible narrow peaks and HOMER-style tag directory of three cellular states were used to calculate reads count matrix using annotatePeaks.pl commands of HOMER [43], which was further normalized with edgeR [44] and used to perform kmeans cluster analysis with Pheatmap R package.

To jointly analyze changes in TF binding and chromatin accessibility, footprints within significant broad peaks were identified using the wellington_footprints.py function of pyDNase v0.2.0 [25], with the parameter "-A" and a threshold of P < 10–15. The log2FC of chromatin accessibility and change number of footprints between myoblasts and adipocytes was calculated for merged broad peaks, and differentially accessible peaks were identified based on changes in both chromatin accessibility (|log2FC|≥ 0.58) and footprint number (same direction as the change in ATAC-seq signal). GO enrichment analysis for genes associated with altered SEs and differential peaks was conducted using GREAT v3.0.0 [45]. Motif analysis within footprints of altered SEs and differential peaks was performed using HOMER findMotifsGenome.pl v4.10.3 [43].

Single-cell multi-omics (ATAC + RNA) data processing

The single-cell sequencing data were subjected to quality control using SeekArcTools v1.0 (http://seekarc.seekgene.com/zh/v1.0.0/). All barcodes were clustered into two groups via K-means clustering based on the UMI counts and ATAC fragments per barcode. Barcodes with overall lower UMI counts and ATAC fragments were filtered out, and the remaining barcodes, representing genuine cell data, were retained for downstream analysis. Subsequently, the scDblFinder package [46] was employed to assess the doublet status of these cells. Only cells classified as "singlet" were retained and further analyzed using the Seurat package [47] for cell clustering and identification of cell-specific genes. Cell types for each cluster were annotated based on the CellMarker 2.0 database [48]. Finally, open chromatin regions (OCRs) from myogenic and adipogenic lineages were extracted. OCRs were categorized based on their association with transcription start site (TSS) regions of expressed genes. Distal OCRs between the two cell types were then compared.

Hi-C data processing

To explore 3D genome dynamics during adipogenic trans-differentiation of C2C12 myoblasts, we downloaded Hi-C and promoter capture Hi-C (PCHi-C) data from 3T3-L1 cell differentiation [19]. Paired-end Hi-C reads from 3T3-L1 adipocytes, after 2 days of differentiation induction, were mapped to the mouse genome (mm10) and processed using the HiC-Pro pipeline v2.9.0 [49] to create validPairs files, which were then used to build contact maps with ICE normalization. TAD structures were defined using the insulation method with parameters “-is 480000 -ids 320000 -im iqrMean -ss 160000” [50, 51].

PCHi-C reads [19] from 3T3-L1 adipocytes, after 2 days of differentiation, were mapped to mm10 using the HiCUP pipeline [52]. Significant interactions (Score ≥ 5) were filtered based on published criteria.

Hi-C data from myogenesis (C2C12 myoblast and myotube cells), which was unpublished data from our laboratory, were analyzed using the HiC-Pro pipeline v2.9. 056 [49], generating validPairs files and raw reads matrices at 10 kb resolution. Chromatin loops were analyzed using the HiCCUPS method [53], with resolution set to “-r 25000” with validPairs files from Hi-C reads of myogenesis.

Hi-C data from adipogenic trans-differentiation of myoblasts, which was based on the linker-based Hi-C 3.0 method, were analyzed to identify the linker sequence and align it to the mouse genome (mm10) using CPU v0.1a (https://github.com/cheehongsg/CPU/). Then the bam files were further processed using the HiC-Pro pipeline v2.9. 056 [49], generating validPairs files and raw reads matrices at 10 kb resolution. Chromatin loops were analyzed using the HiCCUPS method [53], with resolution set to “-r 10000” with validPairs files. The interaction strength between the knockout enhancer and the Rbl1, Lbp promoters was calculated using make_viewpoints.py from the HiC-Pro pipeline v2.9. 056 [49].

Analysis of dynamic chromatin interaction

To identify adipogenic differentiation-specific loops in the trans-differentiation model, loops present in 3T3-L1 adipocytes but absent in myogenesis (25 kb) were selected. Then, loops with contact values < 10 in the Hi-C contact matrix (10 kb) of C2C12 myoblasts and myotubes were further filtered. Adipogenic differentiation-specific loops were categorized into two types based on the accessibility of open chromatin regions (OCRs) at enhancer anchors: (I) distal OCRs open only in adipocytes derived from myoblasts, and (II) distal OCRs shared between myogenesis and adipogenesis, indicating regulatory role changes during trans-differentiation.
