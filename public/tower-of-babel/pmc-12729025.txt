Rhizospheric and endophytic bacterial community response to continuous Atractylodes macrocephala cultivation
Authors: Jingyi Xu, Kaohua Liu, Linxin Ye, Qinger Huang, Jiexi Wang, Luping Qin, Bo Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractRhizospheric and endophytic bacteria play key roles in plant health and soil fertility. Atractylodes macrocephala, a traditional East Asia medicinal herb used to treat gastrointestinal disorders, cancer, and other ailments, experiences reduced growth and increased susceptibility to pathogen under continuous cultivation. We investigated the temporal dynamics of bacterial communities associated with A. macrocephala under continuous cultivation at a grassland site in Lin’an, Zhejiang Province, southeast China (119°25’E, 30°14’N). Using Illumina MiSeq sequencing, we analyzed temporal dynamics of bacterial communities associated with A. macrocephala under continuous cultivation. Continuous cultivation led to a significant shift in the structural and diversity patterns of both rhizospheric and endophytic bacterial communities, particularly in diseased roots. Beneficial genera such as Bacillus and Novosphingobium declined, while pathogenic taxa including Ralstonia and Pseudomonas increased, contributing to disease incidence. However, a two-year fallow period after cultivation helped restore microbial balance, suggesting that fallowing can mitigate continuous cultivation effects. Canonical correspondence analysis revealed that soil pH, hydrolyzable nitrogen, and mercury levels were key factors shaping bacterial community composition. Continuous A. macrocephala cultivation caused root-rot, disrupted endophytic and rhizospheric bacterial communities, and degraded soil fertility. A two-year fallow largely restored microbial communities and soil conditions to near-unplanted levels. Beneficial genera (Bacillus, Novosphingobium) declined under continuous cropping, while disease-associated taxa (Ralstonia, Pseudomonas, mainly P. aeruginosa) increased and may serve as indicators of soil health decline. These results clarify microbes’ roles in plant health and suggest mitigation strategies for continuous A. macrocephala cultivation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07776-0.Keywords: Continuous cultivation, Atractylodes macrocephala, Endophytic bacteria, Rhizosphere bacteria

Introduction

Continuous cultivation, defined as repeated cropping of the same crop or crop family in the same soil, has been widely reported in declines of crop yield and medicinal quality [1], deplete soil nutrients [2], disturb root exudate metabolism [3], promote fungal pathogen accumulation [4], and disrupt soil microbial communities [5]. For example, continuous cultivation of Pseudostellaria heterophylla resulted in a disorganized soil microbiome, stunted growth, reduced aboveground biomass, and significantly lower tuber dry weight [6]. These negative effects are well documented in various medicinal plants, including Panax quinquefolius, Pinellia Ternata [7, 8]. Shifts in soil bacterial community composition are a key factor that can either exacerbate or mitigate continuous-cropping disorders [9–11].

A. macrocephala, a perennial traditional Chinese herb commonly utilized in East Asia, particularly China, serves as a traditional remedy for gastrointestinal disorders, cancer, osteoporosis, obesity, and fetal irritability [12]. A. macrocephala is a cornerstone of traditional Chinese medicine, encapsulated by the saying “nine out of ten cases are treated with A. macrocephala,” which underscores its clinical importance. Wild populations occur in at least 14 provinces in China, principally along three mountain ranges: the Tianmu and Dapan mountains in Zhejiang and the Mufu Mountains on the Hunan–Jiangxi border. Estimated yields are about 5,000–7,500 kg per hectare. Historical records—such as the Illustrated Canon of Herbology (Ben Cao Tu Jing) by Su Song (A.D. 1061)—identify the finest Dao‑di A. macrocephala (“Yuzhu”) as originating from Yuqian, Lin’an, Zhejiang. A. macrocephala is typically propagated vegetatively from mother‑plant rhizomes planted in March, with harvest of mature rhizomes in November and immediate replanting to initiate the next cycle. Continuous cultivation has been widely associated with declines in yield and medicinal quality. Proposed contributing factors include deterioration of soil physical and chemical properties, nutrient imbalances, and autotoxic effects of root exudates [13]. Root‑rot disease, frequently observed under continuous cropping, is primarily caused by soilborne fungi such as Fusarium oxysporum, Rhizoctonia solani, and Ceratobasidium spp., and substantially reduces productivity, with average yield losses reported near 40% [14–16].

Rhizospheric and endophytic bacteria are integral to plant health, promoting growth, aiding nutrient acquisition, and protecting against pathogens [17–19]. These microbial communities, particularly bacterial taxa, such as Bradyrhizobium, Rhizobium, and Bacillus, are increasingly used in biocontrol applications. Recent evidence suggests that continuous cultivation disrupts the structure and diversity of rhizosphere and endophytic bacteria, often accelerating pathogen buildup [20]. A stable and beneficial microbial community is likely crucial for sustaining long-term crop cultivation and performance.

Although soil and endophytic bacteria are important, few studies have examined their dynamics in A. macrocephala under continuous cultivation, and reports on rhizospheric and endophytic community responses are scarce. We aim to determine whether changes in A. macrocephala’s bacterial community structure are causally or correlatively linked to increased disease incidence. This study employed molecular approaches to assess bacterial structure and diversity in rhizosphere soil and in root, stem, leaf, and tuber tissues under continuous cultivation and fallow conditions. Our goals were to (1) characterize shifts in bacterial communities associated with continuous cultivation and (2) explore the mechanisms by which these changes impact plant health and soil fertility. This study elucidated the changes of endophytic bacteria and rhizosphere bacteria during the continuous cultivation of A. macrocephala. Some key bacteria such as Bacillus and Novosphingobium were identified. In the future, efforts can be directed towards these bacteria to alleviate the problems caused by continuous cultivation.

Results

There was no significant difference in OTU numbers among disease plants (1241), 1-year-old healthy plants (1208), and 2-year-old healthy plants (1476) (Figure S1, Fig. 1B). Among rhizospheric soils, 2-year healthy samples had the highest OTU count (3,267), whereas 1-year healthy and diseased rhizospheric soils had fewer OTUs (2,620 and 2,361, respectively). 1,663 OTUs were shared among the three rhizospheric soil types out of 3,972 identified (Fig. 1D), while bulk soil samples shared 1,293 OTUs out of 4,011 total (Fig. 1C, D).

In root tissues, 2-year-old healthy plants showed the highest bacterial richness and diversity, while diseased plants had the lowest. In leaf tissues, diseased plants exhibited higher bacterial diversity than healthy plants. Among rhizospheric soils, 2-year healthy soils had the highest microbial richness, while diseased soils had the lowest. For fallow soils, bacterial diversity peaked in the 1-year fallow samples and gradually returned to baseline levels as fallow duration increased (Table S1).

Bacterial community structure

A total of 41 bacterial phyla were identified across all samples (Fig. 2) (Table S2). The bacterial composition varied notably between plant tissues and soil. In healthy plant tissues, Cyanobacteria dominated, comprising 59.49%–99.92% of the bacterial community. In contrast, Proteobacteria were dominant in the stems and tubers of diseased plants (55.78%–96.15%), while Cyanobacteria remained dominant in diseased roots and leaves (50.91%–95.88%) (Fig. 2).

Soil bacterial communities differed markedly from those in plant tissues (Fig. 2). Proteobacteria were the dominant phylum in both rhizospheric and bulk soils (20.12%–41.95%), while the relative level of Chloroflexi rose with the duration of fallow periods.

Diseases significantly influenced both rhizospheric and endophytic bacterial communities. In diseased rhizospheric soil, Chloroflexi (10.70%) and Patescibacteria (3.71%) were more abundant compared to healthy soils, while Proteobacteria levels were slightly lower (37.08%) than in healthy soils (37.70% and 41.95%). In plant tissues, Cyanobacteria abundance was markedly reduced in diseased samples (averaging 47.37%) compared to healthy ones (87.72%), with the lowest level observed in diseased stems (0.90%). Conversely, Proteobacteria and Bacteroidota were enriched in diseased plants, suggesting a potential link to plant disease. Continuous cultivation also altered the rhizospheric bacterial structure, reducing Acidobacteriota (from 13.42% to 9.53%) and increasing Bacteroidota (from 2.12% to 2.70%) (Fig. 2). The microbial profile of continuously cultivated soils resembled that of diseased rhizosphere soils, indicating that continuous cultivation predisposes plants to disease.

Fallowing helped restore bacterial community balance. During fallow recovery, Patescibacteria and Bacteroidota declined (from 2.16% to 1.17% and 2.61% to 0.50%, respectively), returning to levels similar to control soil (1.01% and 0.50%). Meanwhile, Verrucomicrobiota and Planctomycetota increased (from 1.33% to 2.01% and 1.17% to 2.61%, respectively), also approaching levels in control soil (2.49% and 2.13%) (Fig. 2). These changes suggest that fallowing can reverse the microbial imbalances caused by continuous cultivation and disease.

Beta diversity of bacterial communities

Bray-Curtis dissimilarity metrics with PCoA (Principal Coordinates Analysis) were used to examine the impacts of disease and continuous cultivation on bacterial composition (Fig. 3A, B). Soil and endophytic bacterial communities were analyzed separately. The PCoA revealed a clear separation between diseased and healthy plant samples, indicating that diseases significantly altered bacterial community structure. Similarly, continuous cultivation and fallow duration influenced the clustering patterns of both rhizospheric and fallow soil samples, reflecting notable shifts in microbial composition. Moreover, an interaction between disease and continuous cultivation was observed, suggesting that both factors jointly shape bacterial community dynamics.

To better visualize community differences, soil and endophytic bacteria were analyzed separately and compared across groups at the phylum level (Fig. 4A, B). Phylum-level variation was greater among soil samples than among plant endophyte samples. Significant variations were detected in the relative abundances of Firmicutes, WPS-2, and Myxococcota in soils and Cyanobacteria, Proteobacteria, and Bacteroidota in plant tissues. At the genus level, beneficial endophytes such as Bacillus and Novosphingobium showed marked reductions in plant tissues following continuous cultivation and disease. Conversely, harmful genera such as Pseudomonas and Ralstonia were enriched in diseased soils (Fig. 4C). Specifically, Bacillus abundance declined by approximately 37.53% with continuous cultivation and by 57.82% in diseased plants. Novosphingobium showed even sharper declines: 82.37% after continuous cultivation and 75.27% following disease onset. Meanwhile, the abundance of Pseudomonas in plant tissues increased by roughly 28.52-fold after continuous cultivation. However, fallowing reversed these effects: after two years of fallow, Pseudomonas and Ralstonia abundances significantly declined and were statistically indistinguishable from levels in control soils (without A. macrocephala), indicating partial restoration of soil microbial balance (Fig. 4C).

Correlation with soil parameters

Soil physicochemical properties were previously described [24]. CCA revealed significant correlations between bacterial structure (endophytic and rhizospheric) and soil properties (Fig. 5A, B). Heavy metals like Copper (Cu), Chromium (Cr), Plumbum (Pb), and Cadmium (Cd) were positively associated with each other, while organic matter, pH, and hydrolysis N formed another positively correlated group. Moreover, Cr, Arsenic (As), Mercury (Hg), organic matter, and electrical conductivity were significantly associated with bacterial community variation. Among these, Hg had the strongest influence on bacterial communities in diseased plants, while As had the most impact on soil bacterial communities. Changes in the influence of As across treatments illustrated that continuous cultivation and disease significantly shifted soil microbial communities, whereas fallowing gradually restored them toward the structure observed in unplanted (control) soils. Organic matter was particularly important in facilitating microbial recovery during fallow periods (Fig. 5A, B).

Mantel test analysis further identified specific bacterial genera significantly associated with soil properties (Fig. 5C, D). The top ten genera by relative abundance were selected for this analysis. Several bacterial genera (Bradyrhizobium, Candidatus-Udaeobacter, Acidibacter, Acidothermus, Burkholderia-Caballeronia-Paraburkholderia, and Sphingomonas) showed significant associations with soil chemical properties. Bradyrhizobium was positively correlated with hydrolysis N and was abundant in rhizosphere soils of 1-year healthy plants but decreased in 2-year healthy and diseased plants. Candidatus-Udaeobacter showed positive correlations with electrical conductivity and total nitrogen. Acidibacter correlated significantly with total P and available K, being more abundant in 1-year healthy rhizosphere and 2-year fallow soils. Acidothermus was most abundant in control soils and second highest in 1-year healthy rhizosphere soils. Burkholderia-Caballeronia-Paraburkholderia and available P and electrical conductivity contents. The relative level of Burkholderia-Caballeronia-Paraburkholderia showed strong correlations with available phosphorus and electrical conductivity, with the highest abundance in rhizosphere soils of diseased plants. Sphingomonas correlated significantly with total phosphorus and was primarily found in rhizospheres of 2-year healthy and diseased plants (Fig. 5C, D). These genera are closely linked to soil properties and plant health status.

Bacterial predictive function

Rhizospheric and endophytic bacteria of A. macrocephala showed similar functional profiles in metabolism, cellular processes, and tissue system pathways under healthy conditions (Fig. 6A). However, disease caused significant shifts in endophytic bacterial functions. In diseased plants, microbial functions related to diverse environmental metabolism, ABC transporters, carbon metabolism, and two-component systems were elevated in tubers. In stems, increases were observed in metabolic pathways, secondary metabolite biosynthesis, amino acid biosynthesis, and ABC transporters, while photosynthesis and porphyrin/chlorophyll metabolism decreased. Compared to plant tissues, soil bacteria exhibited stronger functional potentials in carbon metabolism, quorum sensing, glyoxylate and dicarboxylate metabolism, fatty acid metabolism, and propanoate metabolism.

Overall, the bacterial communities displayed high genetic diversity, encompassing genes involved in metabolism, environmental adaptation, genetic information processing, and cellular functions, along with some uncharacterized proteins (Fig. 6B). Genes for energy production/conversion and amino acid transport/metabolism were among the most abundant. Many predicted functions warrant further experimental validation.

Discussion

Sustainable cultivation of A. macrocephala requires insights into the effects of continuous cultivation on rhizospheric and endophytic bacteria. This study assessed the influence of continuous cultivation on bacterial diversity and composition in rhizospheric soil, rotation (fallow) soil, and plant endophytes. Our results unveiled that continuous cultivation significantly reshaped the structure and diversity of these communities, increasing A. macrocephala’s susceptibility to disease.

While previous studies have often reported a decline in microbial diversity following continuous cultivation [31–33], we observed an overall increase in bacterial diversity. Considering previous reports of reduced fungal diversity under similar conditions [24], total microbial diversity may have declined, potentially due to expansion of bacteria that favor harmful taxa [34]. Consistent with this, OTU abundance increased in both continuously cultivated and diseased endophytic samples, indicating elevated bacterial richness. The dominant endophytic bacterial phyla, Cyanobacteria, Proteobacteria, and Bacteroidota were consistent with those in the soil microbiota, supporting previous reports that endophytic bacteria primarily originate from the soil and colonize plant tissues via roots, tubers, stems, and leaves [35]. Continuous cultivation also impacted soil physicochemical features. Declines in organic matter, total nitrogen, potassium, and trace elements such as arsenic were observed. Additionally, the decrease in soil pH—below the optimal range for A. macrocephala (5.1–6.6)—may contribute to increased disease susceptibility by altering microbial balance and nutrient availability [24].

Root-rot disease is a key indicator of continuous cultivation disorders, closely linked to alterations in rhizospheric and endophytic bacterial communities [36]. Severe root rot is a longstanding concern in continuous cultivation systems [28]. In this study, root rot significantly reduced bacterial diversity in rhizospheric soils. Interestingly, certain plant tissues—such as leaves—exhibited higher endophytic bacterial diversity under disease conditions than in healthy plants, a phenomenon that warrants further investigation. These shifts suggest that changes in both soil and endophytic bacterial diversity may contribute to disease progress during A. macrocephala continuous cultivation. Continuous cultivation resulted in a decline in beneficial microbes and a rise in detrimental taxa, tipping the microbial balance toward disease susceptibility. However, fallowing helped reverse these effects, reducing harmful bacterial abundance and supporting the recovery of soil microecological health. Among the beneficial bacteria, Bacillus is known for controlling seedling diseases and gray mold in ginseng [37] and has demonstrated growth-promoting and disease-suppressing effects in Pennisetum glaucum [38, 39]. Novosphingobium has also been shown to enhance plant growth [40, 41]. In contrast, Pseudomonas and Ralstonia are widely implicated in continuous cultivation disorders. Ralstonia is a well-documented causal agent of bacterial wilt [42] and may increase Fusarium solani abundance, exacerbating disease [43]. Pseudomonas aeruginosa can kill plants like Arabidopsis and Ocimum basilicum via virulence factor production [44, 45]. However, not all Pseudomonas species are harmful; some strains promote plant growth—for instance, enhancing seedling length, root development, and biomass in Arctium lappa [46]. These findings underscore the importance of monitoring microbial community changes as a potential tool to gauge soil and plant health in continuous cultivation systems.

Fallowing appears to be effective in alleviating continuous cultivation disorders in A. macrocephala. In this study, bacterial diversity in fallowed soils gradually approached that of unplanted control soils, indicating a microbial recovery with increased fallow duration. Notably, the abundance of beneficial biocontrol bacteria such as Verrucomicrobiota simplex augmented over time, eventually returning to levels found in unplanted soils. These bacteria are known to promote plant growth [47]. Fallowing also improved soil health by elevating organic matter content and restoring essential soil elements to baseline levels. This is particularly important because the long-term monoculture of A. macrocephala depletes organic matter and lowers soil pH [24]. Since A. macrocephala grows optimally within a pH range of 5.1–6.6 [24], the pH drop observed during continuous cultivation—to as low as 4.49 in our study—may increase plant susceptibility to disease. These findings highlight the value of soil recovery periods in restoring microbial balance and soil fertility, thereby supporting the sustainable cultivation of A. macrocephala. Based on these analyses, a two‑year fallow appears effective for alleviating continuous‑cultivation problems. Because fallowing can be impractical where land is scarce, crop rotation is a more feasible alternative [48, 49]. Soil physicochemical properties and heavy‑metal concentrations strongly shape rhizosphere and endophytic bacterial communities. For example, elevated soil mercury can reduce overall bacterial abundance and diversity while enriching certain nitrogen‑fixing taxa [50], and higher organic matter is associated with greater abundances of Arenimonas and Gemmatimonas [51]. Plant recruitment of root‑associated microbes is conditional; for instance, Brassica napus selectively recruits Flavobacterium, which regulates fatty acid and lipid metabolic pathways under phosphorus deficiency to mitigate P stress [52].

This study identified several bacterial taxa with potential benefits for the sustainable cultivation of A. macrocephala. Bradyrhizobium species were closely associated with higher hydrolyzed nitrogen and organic matter content, suggesting their role in improving soil structure, promoting nutrient uptake, and promoting plant growth through the secretion of signaling molecules, hormones, and antibiotics [53]. Although Candidatus-Udaeobacter has not been documented in cultivating medicinal plants, it is well documented in environmental studies [54, 55]. This genus is commonly found in grassland and forest soils, exhibits multidrug resistance, and may even utilize antibiotics released into the environment [54]. In our study, Candidatus-Udaeobacter was strongly correlated with soil electrical conductivity and total nitrogen. Burkholderia-Caballeronia-Paraburkholderia, known for its ability to degrade toxic metabolites, also showed strong associations with electrical conductivity and available phosphorus [56]. These bacterial genera may serve as promising candidates for microbial inoculants to help mitigate the negative impacts of continuous cultivation in A. macrocephala cultivation. Next, we will isolate candidate beneficial bacteria and perform validation experiments to confirm their efficacy in alleviating A. macrocephala root‑rot. We also aim to provide reference strategies for mitigating continuous‑cultivation disorders in other medicinal plants.

Conclusions

Continuous cultivation of A. macrocephala led to root-rot disease and significant disruptions in both endophytic and rhizospheric bacteria. These changes were accompanied by declines in soil fertility and key physicochemical properties. However, fallowing allowed for gradual restoration of the bacterial community structure and soil conditions, eventually approaching those of unplanted control soils. This study identified soil pH, organic matter, available phosphorus, electrical conductivity, and mercury content as the most influential factors shaping bacterial communities. Bacillus and Novosphingobium were identified as beneficial strains with the potential to alleviate continuous cultivation disorders, while Ralstonia and Pseudomonas (mainly Pseudomonas aeruginosa) were associated with disease and may serve as microbial indicators of soil health decline. Overall, the findings suggest that a two-year fallow period can effectively restore soil health and allow for the successful replanting of A. macrocephala.

Materials and methods

Study site and experimental design

This study was conducted on a grassland site (119°25’E, 30°14’N) in Lin’an, Zhejiang Province, southeast China, employing a randomized block design with triplicates for 3 years. Soils at the study site were predominantly red‑yellow and yellow‑brown [21]. The plants originated from Guangming Shareholding Economic Cooperative, Yuqian Town, Lin’an District, Hangzhou, Zhejiang Province, China, while the crops maintained for up to two years on a piece of grassland site (119°25’E, 30°14’N) in Lin’an, Zhejiang Province, southeast China. A. macrocephala was propagated vegetatively from mother‑plant rhizomes bearing buds; rhizomes were planted in March and harvested in November. Collected rhizomes were stored at − 80 °C until DNA extraction and were taxonomically confirmed as A. macrocephala by Prof. Qiaoyan Zhang (Zhejiang Chinese Medical University). We selected naturally diseased A. macrocephala plants from consecutive-cultivation fields; root‑rot was diagnosed by characteristic symptoms (root decay, wilting, chlorosis, and stunted growth) [22, 23]. Five treatments were established based on different cultivation practices: no A. macrocephala planting (control), 1-year cultivation, 2-year continuous cultivation (repeated planting of A. macrocephala on the same land for two years), 1-year fallow after 1-year cultivation, and 2-year fallow after 1-year cultivation. Each treatment plot measured 20 × 20 m, totaling 15 plots under the same management. For the 1-year and 2-year cultivation treatments, 15 plant samples were collected per condition, including healthy 1-year plants, healthy 2-year plants, and 2-year plants with root-rot disease. Plant samples were placed in sterile plastic bags, kept on ice, and promptly sent to the laboratory. After washing with tap water and rinsing with distilled water, these samples were dissected into root, stem, leaf, and tuber components and surface-sterilized following a standard protocol. Absence of microbial growth in sterility controls (imprints and sterile water incubations) confirmed successful surface sterilization [24]. Rhizospheric soil was obtained by removing loose soil and retaining soil firmly adhering to the roots. Following sieving (< 2 mm), samples were kept at 4 °C for biochemical tests or frozen at −80 °C for molecular studies, all within two weeks of collection [25]. The experimental conditions included six soil types: 1-year fallow (S_F1), 2-year fallow (S_F2), 1-year healthy rhizospheric (RS_H1), 2-year healthy rhizospheric (RS_H2), 2-year diseased rhizospheric (RS_D), and control soil without A. macrocephala cultivation (S_CK). Plant tissues included 1-year healthy roots, stems, leaves, and tubers (HP_R1, S1, L1, T1), 2-year healthy (HP_R2, S2, L2, T2), and 2-year diseased roots, stems, leaves, and tubers (DP_R, S, L, T). In total, 18 soil samples (6 groups × 3 replicates) and 36 plant samples (3 types × 4 tissues × 3 replicates) were analyzed to assess bacterial diversity and community structure.

DNA extraction and high-throughput sequencing

Genomic DNA was retrieved from soils employing the MoBio-Powersoil™ DNA Isolation Kit (MO BIO Laboratories, Norcross, CA, United States) and from plant tissues employing the Plant Genomic DNA Extraction Kit (MRDNA, Shallowater, TX, United States). DNA quality was checked on a 1% agarose gel, with concentration and purity assessed using a DeNovix DS-11 spectrophotometer (DeNovix, Wilmington, DE, USA). The V3–V4 segment of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene underwent amplification using the barcoded primers 338 F (5’-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3’) and 806R (5’-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3’) in triplicate using an EP Mastercycler Gradient System (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany) in a 20 µL system containing 4 µL of 5× TransStart FastPfu buffer, 2 µL of 2.5 mM dNTPs, 0.8 µL each of forward and reverse primers (5 µM), 0.4 µL of TransStart FastPfu DNA Polymerase, and 10 ng of template with cycling conditions of 3 min at 95 °C; 27 cycles of 30 s at 95 °C, 30 s at 55 °C, and 45 s at 72 °C, and additional 10 min at 72 °C, totaling nine reactions per sample group. The products were resolved on a 2% agarose gel, isolated using the AxyPrep DNA Gel Extraction Kit (Axygen Biosciences, Union City, CA, USA), quantified with a Quantus™ Fluorometer (Promega, USA), pooled in equimolar concentrations, and paired-end sequenced (2 × 300 bp) on the Illumina MiSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) by Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) following standard protocols (https://cloud.majorbio.com/page/project/overview) [26]. Raw sequencing data are available in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) with accession number SRP569808.

Sequencing data analysis

Raw reads were demultiplexed, quality-filtered using Trimmomatic, and merged with FLASH. High-quality reads were clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) with 97% similarity using UPARSE (v7.0.1090; http://drive5.com/uparse/). Taxonomic assignment was performed using the SILVA (Release 138) and Greengenes (Release 13.5) databases. The OTU table was rarefied to assess alpha diversity, with Observed Species, Shannon index, and Chao1 calculated with Mothur [27, 28]. Rarefaction curves and diversity plots were generated in R (v3.3.1).

Statistical and functional analysis

Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) was conducted based on Bray-Curtis distances at the OTU level across all 54 samples. Soil samples (unplanted soil, 1‑year cultivation, 2‑year continuous cultivation, 1‑year fallow after 1‑year cultivation, and 2‑year fallow after 1‑year cultivation) were analyzed together, and plant samples (healthy 1‑year, healthy 2‑year, and 2‑year root‑rot affected) were grouped and compared separately. Differences in bacterial composition were evaluated using the Kruskal-Wallis H test. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (version 26, IBM Corp.). The significance of differences among groups was evaluated by One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by post hoc tests for multiple comparisons using the Least Significant Difference (LSD) and Duncan’s tests. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant [29]. Canonical Correspondence Analysis (CCA) was performed using normalized OTU abundance and soil physicochemical parameters with the vegan package in R (v3.3.1). Soil physicochemical measurements were as described previously [24]. Mantel tests were also performed using Vegan in R (v4.1.2) to assess the association of community structure with environmental factors [30]. To infer microbial functional potentials, KEGG (Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes) and COG (Clusters of Orthologous Groups) pathway analyses were performed using PICRUSt2 (http://huttenhower.sph.harvard.edu/galaxy), which predicts gene content based on the OTU phylogeny and reference databases [30].
