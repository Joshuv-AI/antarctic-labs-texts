Swarm intelligence–optimized deep neural network for intelligent diagnosis of lithium‑ion battery state of health
Authors: Chong Li, Bangjie Yan, Chunxiang Zhu, Xingkai Zhou, Yinghao Jia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Accurate estimation of the State of Health (SOH) for lithium-ion batteries is crucial for the safety and reliability of battery management systems. However, traditional methods often suffer from error accumulation and poor adaptability, while data-driven approaches like Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks are hindered by the challenge of hyperparameter optimization. To address these issues, this paper proposes a swarm intelligence-optimized deep neural network for intelligent SOH diagnosis. A multidimensional indirect health indicator (IHI) extraction mechanism is constructed, deriving three highly correlated features—voltage differential slope, constant-current charging time, and temperature change rate—from the charging process. A residual-corrected LSTM network is designed, and the Sparrow Search Algorithm (SSA) is employed to globally optimize seven critical hyperparameters. Experimental results on the NASA and Oxford datasets show that the proposed method substantially alleviates the burden of hyperparameter tuning and improves SOH prediction accuracy compared with a manually tuned LSTM. Compared to the traditional LSTM model, the mean absolute error (MAE) and root mean square error (RMSE) are reduced by approximately 30.4% and 17.6%, respectively, achieving an MAE of 0.3060% and an RMSE of 0.4641% on the primary test set. The method significantly enhances the accuracy and robustness of SOH estimation, providing a viable technical solution for high-precision intelligent diagnostics in practical applications.

Introduction

With the growing deployment of lithium-ion batteries in large-scale electric bus fleets1 and the increasing complexity of battery health diagnostics using deep learning architectures2, accurate State of Health (SOH) estimation has become critical for Battery Management Systems (BMS). Recent advancements in neuromorphic computing systems offer new technical pathways for rapid battery state estimation in electric vehicles (EVs)3. However, batteries gradually age during charge–discharge cycles, leading to continuous performance degradation. SOH, a key metric reflecting current performance relative to initial conditions, is a core BMS parameter directly affecting system safety and reliability4,5. The accuracy of SOH estimation significantly impacts overall BMS performance and is closely interrelated with other functions like fault diagnosis, forming a comprehensive battery safety monitoring framework6.

Current SOH estimation faces two major challenges: First, traditional methods suffer from limitations in parameter identification error accumulation and poor adaptability to operational conditions. For instance, the ampere-hour integral method is susceptible to current noise, causing capacity calculation deviations; the internal resistance method requires high-precision measurement equipment and is difficult to implement online; and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy is constrained by model mismatch under complex operating conditions. These methods struggle to meet the real-time and robustness requirements for online estimation in dynamic load scenarios7,8. Second, the strong coupling effects between variable operational conditions and battery aging mechanisms make it difficult to accurately represent the nonlinear capacity fade trajectory using conventional models. Factors like temperature fluctuations, charge/discharge rate variations, and historical cycling stress significantly alter internal battery parameters (e.g., SEI film growth rate, lithium-ion diffusion coefficients)9,10.

A comprehensive analysis of current SOH estimation technologies reveals that despite parallel developments in electrochemical modeling11,12, equivalent circuit models, and data-driven approaches, key bottlenecks persist in dynamic condition adaptability, multi-parameter co-optimization, and balancing real-time performance with accuracy. This directly constrains the intelligence level of BMS in complex application scenarios, urgently necessitating new high-precision SOH estimation methods with both theoretical foundation and engineering practicality.

In recent years, data-driven methods based on machine learning have developed rapidly. Approaches such as ICA-Bi-LSTM demonstrate the capability to extract features directly from measurable data and establish mapping relationships for SOH estimation13. Neuromorphic computing-based SOH estimation methods have shown great potential for efficiently processing time-series data14, further propelling data-driven advancements. Parallel feature fusion networks have exhibited exceptional performance in the early prediction of battery remaining useful life, providing important references for multi-feature integration15. Transformer architectures and their variants have excelled in time-series modeling tasks, with cycle-segmentation-based Transformer methods achieving significant results in complex tasks such as internal short-circuit detection in battery packs16. Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks have demonstrated tremendous potential for SOH prediction due to their excellent time-series processing capabilities17,18. However, their performance is highly dependent on hyperparameter selection, and traditional manual tuning methods rarely achieve optimal configurations19,20. Besides LSTM, other neural network architectures, such as memristor-based autoencoders and Gated Recurrent Unit (GRU) networks, have been employed for state of charge (SOC) estimation21–23, showcasing the broad application of neural networks in battery state estimation. Nevertheless, the LSTM hyperparameter space is high-dimensional with interdependent parameters, making traditional grid search and random search methods computationally expensive and prone to local optima, which severely limits their practical effectiveness in battery SOH estimation. In related materials research, hybrid metal matrix composites have been designed to tailor microstructure and mechanical behaviour, illustrating that hybrid structures can effectively enhance overall system performance24.

To address LSTM hyperparameter optimization, researchers have introduced intelligent optimization algorithms for parameter tuning. Although battery SOH estimation technology has evolved over years with some engineering application foundations, three core technical difficulties remain for large-scale commercial adoption in new energy vehicles: (1) global optimization in high-dimensional hyperparameter spaces (7 dimensions) for deep networks like LSTM, where traditional methods often get stuck in local optima leading to significant performance degradation; (2) balancing physical interpretability and engineering simplicity in health indicator extraction, as existing studies frequently rely on complex multimodal data fusion hindering deployment; (3) correcting inherent systematic prediction bias in deep learning models, as single neural networks cannot fully eliminate cumulative modeling errors. Traditional Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) algorithms show some effectiveness but are prone to local optima and slow convergence25. The Sparrow Search Algorithm (SSA), an emerging swarm intelligence algorithm, offers advantages like fast convergence and strong global search capability, providing a new avenue for LSTM optimization26.

Addressing the above challenges, this study proposes a lithium-ion battery health estimation method based on multidimensional Indirect Health Indicators (IHI) and an SSA-LSTM model. The main contributions include:

Constructing a practically deployable IHI set composed of voltage differential slope, constant-current charging time and temperature change rate, all extracted from standard CC-CV charging segments. Instead of proposing entirely new physical indicators, we start from nine candidate IHIs motivated by existing CC-CV and thermal features, then quantitatively screen them using PCC/SRCC/KRCC on the NASA and Oxford datasets. The final three IHIs form a compact, physically interpretable and strongly SOH-correlated feature set suitable for data-driven models;Applying the SSA algorithm to optimise the hyperparameters of the residual-corrected LSTM, which helps avoid poor local configurations in the seven-dimensional search space;Constructing a complete framework integrating multidimensional IHI extraction, SSA optimization, and LSTM prediction, and introducing a residual correction mechanism to enhance prediction accuracy. Experiments on NASA and Oxford public datasets validate the method’s effectiveness and superiority.

Lithium-ion battery IHI extraction

Definition of battery SOH

The SOH of a lithium-ion battery is an important indicator used to measure the current performance relative to its initial performance. From an energy storage perspective, SOH is defined by the degree of capacity degradation of the battery, and the calculation formula is as follows:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$SOH = \frac{{C_{cur} }}{{C_{rat} }} \times 100\%$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_{cur}$$\end{document} is the battery’s current maximum usable capacity, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_{rat}$$\end{document} is the battery’s initial rated capacity.

As the number of charge and discharge cycles increases, mechanisms such as loss of active material, electrolyte decomposition, and thickening of the Solid Electrolyte Interphase (SEI) membrane cause the battery’s capacity to gradually decrease, with SOH showing a nonlinear degradation trend. When SOH drops to 80%, the battery is typically considered to have reached the end of its life. The industry commonly uses 80% as the threshold, but this study adopts NASA’s 1.4Ah termination threshold (approximately 70%) as the EOL to align with the original data labeling.

Datasets

The experimental data used in this study is derived from the NASA open dataset and the Oxford University dataset27,28. The NASA dataset includes cyclic aging data of 18,650-type lithium-ion batteries under different operating conditions, with a data sampling frequency of 1 Hz. The Oxford University dataset contains testing data for the SLBP533459H4-type lithium-ion pouch batteries (manufacturer: BAK Battery) under urban driving cycle conditions, with a sampling frequency of 10 Hz during charging and 1 Hz during discharging.

As shown in Fig. 1, the charging process of the NASA dataset B5 lithium-ion battery clearly reflects typical variation characteristics under different health states in terms of voltage (Fig. 1(a)) and temperature (Fig. 1(b)). During initial charging, Constant Current (CC) mode is used with a charging current maintained at 1.5A, where voltage increases rapidly over time and gradually approaches 4.2V. Subsequently, entering the Constant Voltage (CV) phase, voltage stabilizes at 4.2V while current gradually decreases to approximately 20mA. Throughout the entire charging cycle, the temperature curve shows a trend of first rising then falling. During the CC phase, temperature continuously rises with charging progression, peaking near the transition to the CV phase. As current decreases, temperature slowly drops during the CV phase. Moreover, as the number of charge–discharge cycles increases, aging phenomena gradually appear. From the curves, it can be observed that the voltage rise rate during early charging slows, the CC phase shortens, and the temperature peak may shift. These changes collectively reflect increased internal impedance and variations in thermal effects within the battery. Therefore, by analyzing voltage and temperature variation processes across different charging cycles, key parameters such as CC phase duration, voltage change rate, and temperature peak can be quantified, indirectly extracting feature information characterizing battery health status, providing an effective basis for SOH estimation as supported by recent comprehensive reviews of battery health characterization methods29.Fig. 1Charging curve of lithium battery in NASA dataset.

Principles for selecting IHIs

Effective IHI should possess several key characteristics, such as: showing a significant monotonic relationship with SOH and maintaining a strong correlation with SOH. At the same time, their extraction should be based on standard sensor parameters to ensure ease of access and remain relatively stable under varying operational conditions. State of Charge (SOC) and temperature are crucial factors affecting battery health. Relevant studies have explored SOC and temperature estimation methods based on lumped mass models30. By coupling single-particle dynamics and electro-thermal effects, the impact of SOC and temperature on battery health can be more precisely modeled31, providing theoretical support for IHI selection. Additionally, in-depth analysis of the battery charge and discharge curves reveals that voltage, current, and temperature characteristics exhibit systematic shifts during aging, providing a critical foundation for IHI identification and extraction.

Method for extracting multidimensional indirect health indicators

Unlike many existing studies that rely on complex feature extraction methods such as electrochemical impedance spectroscopy or incremental capacity analysis, this study designs health indicators directly from the intrinsic physical mechanisms of the charging process, avoiding dependence on dedicated and expensive test equipment. The proposed IHIs are constructed using only charging data that can be measured by a conventional BMS, which significantly lowers the implementation barrier while preserving clear physical meaning. It is worth emphasizing that the three finally selected IHIs are not entirely new quantities in the literature. Voltage-slope-based indicators, constant-current (CC) phase duration and temperature-related features have all been reported in previous SOH studies. Our contribution is to cast these physically meaningful quantities into a unified IHI framework, explicitly include them in a larger pool of nine candidates, and quantitatively compare their SOH relevance on two heterogeneous datasets. This systematic screening allows us to retain a minimal yet informative feature set that balances physical interpretability, statistical correlation and ease of acquisition from standard BMS measurements. Based on this framework, nine candidate IHIs are constructed, and the extraction methods for the key IHIs are described below:

From the perspective of electrochemical dynamics, the electrode potential and current density follow the Butler-Volmer equation. This systematic change in heat source distribution results in a noticeable shift in the temperature change rate curve: the peak for healthy batteries occurs at the CC-CV transition point (around 80% charge), while for aging batteries, the peak moves to around 60% charge, and the peak amplitude increases by 20–40%. Moreover, the local hotspot effect caused by increased internal resistance leads to higher temperature non-uniformity, increasing the surface temperature gradient. LSTM model optimized by sparrow search algorithm

LSTM network with residual correction

Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks consist of a memory cell and three nonlinear gates (forget, input and output gates), which control the flow of information and help preserve relevant historical data while filtering out noise. This structure is well suited for modeling the nonlinear and time-varying degradation process of battery SOH. However, in complex SOH estimation tasks a pure LSTM predictor may still exhibit systematic bias with respect to degradation stage or operating condition, leading to residual errors that are not purely random.

To address this issue, this study introduces a residual correction mechanism on top of the LSTM network. After training the base LSTM using the selected IHIs as inputs, the prediction residual sequence is computed as the difference between the true SOH and the LSTM output. Statistical analysis reveals that these residuals still contain structured components correlated with the three selected IHIs (IHI1, IHI3 and IHI5), indicating that a simple linear model using these IHIs as inputs can capture the remaining bias. Therefore, a lightweight linear regression model is constructed to map the three IHIs to the residual, and the final SOH estimate is obtained by adding the estimated residual to the raw LSTM prediction. As illustrated at the bottom of Fig. 4, the three IHIs are fed into the two-layer LSTM backbone to produce a preliminary SOH output, while the same IHIs are also used by a linear residual model whose output is added to the preliminary SOH to obtain the final prediction. In this way, the nonlinear feature learning capability of the LSTM is complemented by the error-compensation ability of linear residual modelling, resulting in a more accurate and interpretable SOH estimator.

Sparrow search algorithm

SSA algorithm principle

SSA proposed by Xue and Shen in 2020, is an emerging swarm intelligence optimization algorithm. It simulates the foraging and anti-predation behaviors of sparrow populations to find the global optimum. Compared to particle swarm optimization (PSO), which is prone to local optima, and genetic algorithms, which converge slowly, SSA achieves a better balance between global exploration and local exploitation through the collaboration mechanism of three roles: discoverers, followers, and vigilants. Additionally, SSA has relatively simple parameter settings, making it particularly suitable for optimizing high-dimensional nonlinear problems such as LSTM hyperparameters.

The SSA algorithm divides the sparrow population into three roles: discoverers, who search for food for the entire population and perform wide-area searches (accounting for 10%-20% of the population); followers, who follow the discoverers to obtain food and compete with them when necessary (accounting for the majority of the population); and vigilants (also referred to as watchdogs in some literature), who scout for dangers and guide the population to safety when threats are detected (accounting for 10%-20% of the population). This division of labor enables SSA to achieve a good balance between global exploration and local exploitation. Compared to traditional optimization algorithms like PSO and genetic algorithms, SSA shows advantages such as faster convergence, stronger global search ability, and simpler parameter settings, particularly when handling high-dimensional nonlinear optimization problems.

In the context of battery SOH estimation, SSA is adopted in this work primarily as a tool for deep network hyperparameter optimization. Unlike previous studies that often relied on empirical tuning or simple grid search, this study combines the global search ability of a bio-inspired optimisation algorithm with the time-series modeling capability of the LSTM network. This bio-inspired optimisation scheme provides an efficient way to explore the 7-dimensional hyperparameter space and helps avoid poor local configurations that can significantly degrade LSTM performance. By simulating the foraging behavior of sparrow populations, the algorithm maintains a balance between exploration and exploitation in the hyperparameter space, which is beneficial for mitigating some of the local-optima issues encountered by traditional optimisation methods.

SSA algorithm process

As shown in Fig. 3, the implementation process of the SSA algorithm comprises the following core steps: First, the algorithm initialization involves setting parameters such as population size (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n$$\end{document}), dimension (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d$$\end{document}), maximum iteration (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$iter_{\max }$$\end{document}), and randomly initializing the sparrow population’s positions within the search space. Then, the fitness of each individual is evaluated and sorted to determine the current best and worst individuals. Next, update the positions of the population using the role-based strategies: the top 20% of individuals are selected as discoverers, the followers are updated based on the discoverers’ positions, and the remaining 20% are selected as vigilants. Boundary handling is performed to ensure that positions remain within the search space. Finally, check for convergence. If the maximum iteration or accuracy requirement is met, the optimal solution is output; otherwise, the process iterates through the fitness evaluation step. The entire algorithm achieves a balance between global exploration, local development, and diversity maintenance, providing strong optimization capabilities for LSTM hyperparameter tuning.Fig. 3SSA algorithm flowchart.

Hyperparameter optimization of SSA-LSTM

Network architecture: The proposed SSA-LSTM model takes three IHIs (IHI1, IHI3 and IHI5) as inputs. As sketched in the lower part of Fig. 4, the backbone network consists of an input layer, two stacked LSTM layers with Dropout, a fully connected layer and a single-neuron output that yields a preliminary SOH estimate. A lightweight linear residual model then maps the same three IHIs to a residual term, which is added to the preliminary SOH as described in Section "LSTM Network with Residual Correction" to obtain the final SOH prediction. SSA is used only to optimise the seven hyperparameters of the LSTM backbone (numbers of units in each layer, dropout rate, learning rate, batch size and the maximum number of epochs), while the residual model is fitted by ordinary least squares without meta-heuristic tuning.Fig. 4Block diagram of lithium-ion battery SOH prediction model.

Block diagram of lithium-ion battery SOH prediction model.

Search space and optimization strategy: The performance of the LSTM-based predictor is highly dependent on its hyperparameter configuration, and traditional manual tuning is random and subjective, making it difficult to obtain a global optimum. In this study, seven key hyperparameters are optimized simultaneously: the number of neurons in the first LSTM layer, the number of neurons in the second LSTM layer, the number of neurons in the fully connected layer, the Dropout rate, learning rate, batch size and the number of training epochs. These seven hyperparameters were selected because preliminary experiments showed that they dominate the trade-off between model capacity, regularization strength and training dynamics, whereas other settings such as activation functions, optimizer type and weight initialization have comparatively smaller impact and are therefore fixed. The resulting search space contains on the order of 1015 possible combinations, so naive grid search or random search would be computationally prohibitive and prone to local optima.

To overcome this difficulty, the Sparrow Search Algorithm (SSA) is employed to perform global optimization in the 7-dimensional hyperparameter space. SSA uses a collaborative search mechanism involving discoverers, followers and vigilants to explore the parameter space efficiently, while the fitness function is defined as the MAE on a validation set obtained by an 80%–20% split of the training data, with early stopping to avoid overfitting. The hyperparameter bounds are set as follows: [16, 128] for the number of neurons in the first LSTM layer, [8, 64] for the second LSTM layer, [8, 32] for the fully connected layer, [0.1, 0.5] for the Dropout rate, [1 × 10–5, 1 × 10–2] for the learning rate, [4, 32] for the batch size and [20, 100] for the number of training epochs.

Training and implementation details: The implementation of the SSA-LSTM model involves four key steps: First, data preprocessing is performed using Min–Max normalization to map the three IHIs to a specific range, and the combined training batteries are further split into training and validation subsets with an 80%–20% ratio. Next, the SSA optimization process is executed, setting the population size to 15, the maximum number of iterations to 30, and the safety threshold. During SSA search, the “epoch” hyperparameter denotes the maximum number of training epochs assigned to each candidate configuration. The actual training can terminate earlier when the validation MAE does not improve for a given patience, due to the early-stopping callback. Hence, SSA effectively tunes the training budget rather than a fixed epoch count. Using approximately 462 charge–discharge cycles from three batteries (B5, B6, B18) in the NASA dataset as training samples, each iteration of SSA requires training and validating the LSTM model 15 times, totaling 450 fitness calculations to perform global optimization in the 7-dimensional hyperparameter space. The discoverers, followers, and vigilants cooperate in searching for the optimal hyperparameters. Afterward, the optimized hyperparameters are used to train the LSTM model, employing ModelCheckpoint, ReduceLROnPlateau, and EarlyStopping callbacks to ensure model quality. Finally, a residual correction mechanism is integrated. After SSA-LSTM is trained on the training set, we compute the residuals on the validation set and fit a linear regression model that maps the three IHIs to the residuals. During inference on the test batteries, this residual model is used to generate a bias estimate, which is added to the original LSTM prediction. This two-stage scheme allows the nonlinear LSTM to capture the main SOH trend, while the lightweight linear model compensates for the remaining systematic bias. This framework effectively combines SSA’s global optimization capability with the time-series modeling ability of LSTM, providing a reliable technical solution for SOH estimation of lithium-ion batteries.

To ensure transparency and reproducibility in the SSA optimization process, a fixed random seed (seed = 42) is used for hyperparameter search. The search space is set as a 7-dimensional parameter space with the following boundaries: the number of neurons in the first LSTM layer [16, 128], the number of neurons in the second LSTM layer [8, 64], the number of neurons in the fully connected layer [8, 32], dropout rate [0.1, 0.5], learning rate [1E-5, 1E-2], batch size [4, 32], and training epochs [20, 100]. The fitness function is based on the MAE loss of the validation set, which is split 80%-20%, and early stopping is implemented to prevent overfitting. The experimental results show that the SSA algorithm converges effectively within 30 iterations, with fitness decreasing steadily from 0.008489 to 0.006942. The convergence speed slows significantly and stabilizes after the 10th iteration, confirming the algorithm’s global search ability and optimization efficiency.

Experimental verification and result analysis

The experiments were conducted on high-performance hardware and software platforms with the following configuration: Intel i7-10750H processor, NVIDIA RTX 2060 GPU, and 16GB RAM. The experimental environment used Anaconda Prompt (Miniconda3) with Jupyter Notebook, and the Keras framework based on TensorFlow 2.0 was used for implementation.

The SSA-LSTM network structure used in the experiments includes an input layer, two LSTM layers, three Dropout layers, a fully connected layer, and an output layer, as described in Chapter 3. The model is trained using the MAE as the loss function and the Adam adaptive optimizer to minimize the loss function. For baseline training without SSA optimization, the initial learning rate is set to 0.001 with a decay strategy: if the validation loss does not improve for 10 consecutive epochs, the learning rate is reduced by a factor of 0.5. When using SSA-optimized hyperparameters, the learning rate is set to 0.01 as determined by the optimization process. The Adam optimizer’s first momentum decay coefficient β1 is set to 0.9, and the second momentum decay coefficient β2 is set to 0.999. The batch size and the number of training epochs are set according to the optimized values from each algorithm, with early stopping implemented to prevent overfitting.

For each dataset group, we adopt a one-battery-out protocol. In the NASA group-1 experiment, batteries B5, B6 and B18 are used for model development: their full life-cycle data are merged and randomly split into 80% for training and 20% for validation. Battery B7 is completely held out as the test set, and its SOH over the entire life cycle is predicted. In group-2, B29–B31 are used for training/validation and B32 for testing. For the Oxford dataset, Cell1–3 serve as training/validation batteries and Cell4 as the test battery. The 80–20 split is applied only within the training batteries for hyperparameter optimisation and early stopping; no test battery samples are involved in SSA or in training the residual model.

Based on the experimental results table, to further validate the advantages of the proposed SSA-LSTM algorithm model, it was compared with ABC-LSTM, CDO-LSTM, GWO-LSTM, WOA-LSTM, LSTM, CNN-LSTM, and Transformer methods. Across three distinct battery datasets, the SSA-LSTM model demonstrated significant advantages in most evaluation metrics.

In the first battery test, the SSA-LSTM model demonstrated significant improvements over the traditional LSTM model: MAE decreased by 30.4% from 0.4394% to 0.3060%, RMSE dropped by 17.6% from 0.5633% to 0.4641%, and MAPE decreased by 32.1% from 0.5300% to 0.3600%. The R2 coefficient increased by 0.189 percentage points, rising from 99.506% to 99.695%. Compared to other optimization algorithms, SSA-LSTM outperformed ABC-LSTM (0.3246%), CDO-LSTM (0.3391%), GWO-LSTM (0.3697%), and WOA-LSTM (0.3169%) by 5.7%,9.8%,17.2%, and 3.4% respectively in MAE metrics. When benchmarked against deep learning models CNN-LSTM (0.5677%) and Transformer (0.5902%), SSA-LSTM showed even more.

remarkable advantages, achieving 46.1% and 48.2% reductions in MAE, respectively, which validates the effectiveness of the Sparrow Search Algorithm.

In the second battery test group, the SSA-LSTM model demonstrated excellent stability, with its MAE decreasing by 5.9% from 0.5500% of the traditional LSTM model to 0.5176%. While CDO-LSTM, GWO-LSTM, and WOA-LSTM showed comparable or slightly superior performance in certain metrics (e.g., CDO-LSTM’s RMSE of 0.6736% was lower than SSA-LSTM’s 0.7088%), SSA-LSTM maintained its competitive edge in overall performance. Notably, CNN-LSTM and Transformer exhibited significant performance degradation under high-temperature conditions, with MAE values reaching 1.2194% and 1.0148% respectively—far exceeding all other models. This indicates that these two deep learning architectures lack sufficient robustness in complex operational environments.

In the third battery test (Oxford dataset), SSA-LSTM demonstrated the best performance with an MAE of 0.0451%, a 25.8% reduction compared to the traditional LSTM’s 0.0608%. It outperformed other optimized algorithms by 83.2%,80.3%,79.6%, and 83.4% respectively against ABC-LSTM (0.2682%), CDO-LSTM (0.2290%), GWO-LSTM (0.2215%), and WOA-LSTM (0.2712%). Interestingly, the Transformer model showed relatively good performance (MAE = 0.1851%) but still lagged 310.4% behind SSA-LSTM, while CNN-LSTM remained the worst performer (MAE = 0.6009%).

A thorough analysis of three battery groups reveals distinct performance characteristics: Within the NASA dataset, the room-temperature group (Group 1) achieves higher accuracy than the high-temperature group (Group 2), with about 68% lower MAE. Surprisingly, the third group based on the Oxford dataset achieved the best performance, highlighting the method’s adaptability to different battery chemistries and operating conditions. Notably, SSA-LSTM’s MAE on the Oxford dataset was merely 0.0451%, significantly outperforming its counterpart on the NASA dataset. This superior performance may be attributed to the more predictable aging patterns observed in Oxford batteries.

Conclusion

This study proposes the SSA-LSTM model for SOH estimation of lithium-ion batteries. By using the Sparrow Search Algorithm to tune the LSTM hyperparameters, the difficulty of manual hyperparameter selection is substantially alleviated in the cases studied. The main contributions of this research include: the use of three easily accessible health indicators—voltage differential slope, constant-current charging time, and temperature change rate—based on the physical characteristics of the charging process; the application of the SSA algorithm for global optimization of LSTM hyperparameters; and the integration of a residual correction mechanism to improve prediction accuracy. Pearson correlation analysis confirmed that the selected health indicators strongly correlate with SOH, providing an effective information source for SOH estimation.

From an engineering practicality perspective, the proposed method has the following features: the online inference time is short, meeting the real-time requirements of BMS systems; the model has a relatively small number of parameters, making it deployable on mainstream vehicle BMS chips; and the health indicator extraction only requires standard sensor data without the need for additional hardware investment.

However, this study has the following limitations: the training dataset is relatively small, which may pose a risk of overfitting; there is multicollinearity among the selected health indicators, reflecting the strong coupling of physical parameters in the charging process; the method relies on complete constant-current constant-voltage charging curves, and its applicability in fast charging or non-standard charging modes needs further validation; the current validation is based only on offline data analysis, lacking real-time deployment testing in an actual BMS system; and the prediction stability under extreme temperature conditions requires further verification. Moreover, the current IHI design assumes that quasi-complete CC-CV charging segments are available. Under fast-charging or highly irregular charging strategies, this assumption may be violated and additional feature engineering (e.g. from partial segments) will be required.

In addition, this study is limited to first-life cells from the NASA and Oxford datasets. The operating profiles and degradation patterns of second-life batteries in stationary storage or repurposed EV applications can be much more diverse. Since the proposed method only relies on standard voltage, current and temperature measurements and uses a relatively compact network, it is in principle suitable for on-board implementation in second-life BMSs. Nevertheless, validating the model on dedicated second-life datasets and incorporating usage-history information will be important topics for future work.

Through experiments on the NASA and Oxford University datasets, the SSA-LSTM model demonstrated significant advantages in evaluation metrics such as MAE, RMSE, MAPE, and R2. Particularly in the first set of battery tests, the MAE was reduced by 30.4% compared to the traditional LSTM model, confirming the effectiveness of the method. This study provides a new technical approach for SOH estimation of lithium-ion batteries and has important implications for improving the intelligence level of battery management systems. Future research will consider constructing a comprehensive battery state estimation framework and explore the application of advanced parameter identification techniques in health indicator extraction to further improve the accuracy and robustness of battery state estimation. In future work, we will also investigate the applicability of the proposed framework to second-life batteries with more diverse usage histories and operating conditions.
