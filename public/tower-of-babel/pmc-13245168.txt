IVUS catheter entrapment and percutaneous retrieval: case report and literature review
Authors: Fintan Corvan, Aaron Peace, Jack Andrews, Dzan Horozic, Joon Heng Tan, Deepti Ranganathan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground2024 ESC Guidelines for the management of chronic coronary syndromes updated their recommendation for intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) guidance of PCI on anatomically complex lesions, particularly left main, bifurcations and long lesions to class 1, level A evidence. With more widespread use complex PCI operators are more likely to encounter complications and require an awareness of potential solutions.Case summaryA 66-year-old man presented with non-ST elevation acute coronary syndrome. Following PCI to the Left Main Stem the unusual complication of a trapped IVUS catheter was encountered. This was successfully retrieved by balloon trapping and pull-back method.DiscussionLiterature review demonstrates that IVUS entrapment during complex PCI is an uncommon complication. Heavily calcified lesions, stents <2.5 mm and third-generation DES are considered risk factors. Several potential options for retrieval such as balloon trapping and use of Guide extension catheters have been described. This is the first reported case of IVUS entrapment in a large stent in Left Main Stem (LMS).Keywords: IVUS Entrapment, Percutaneous coronary intervention, Case report

Introduction

2024 ESC Guidelines updated the recommendation for intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) guided PCI on anatomically complex lesions to class 1A level of evidence.1 This decision was supported by studies such as RENOVATE-Complex PCI2 and IVUS-XPL3 showing evidence IVUS improves outcomes when compared to angiography alone. Analysis of the British Cardiovascular Intervention Society (BCIS) registry showed intravascular imaging for acute coronary syndrome (ACS) patients rose from 1.4% in 2006 to 13.5% in 2019.4

Case presentation

A 60-year-old man presented with increasing typical cardiac chest pain occurring on exertion for 1 month, followed by pain at rest. Past medical history included Non-ST-Elevation Myocardial Infarction (NSTEMI) in 2023 treated with PCI to Ramus Intermediate branch. Blood Pressure on admission was 150/92 mmHg with a heart rate of 56 beats per minute. He examined normally and was pain free in Emergency Department. His Electrocardiogram showed no acute ischaemic changes and peak high sensitivity troponin T was measured at 250 ng/L. An Echocardiogram reported a mildly dilated left ventricle with normal systolic function. There were no valvular abnormalities. Coronary angiography demonstrated a severe calcific stenosis of the distal Left Main Stem (LMS) (Figure 1). The left anterior descending (LAD) had moderate proximal atheroma with eccentric calcification (Figure 1). The Ramus had mild proximal disease with a widely patent stent. The left circumflex (LCx) was a large dominant vessel with severe ostial disease of the first marginal branch. This was classified a Medina 1,0,0 lesion. The right coronary artery (RCA) was recessive with mild plaque only. His Syntax score was 16 and the Heart Team Meeting discussion saw equipoise between PCI and surgical revascularization. This was discussed with the patient and as part of shared decision making the patient had a strong preference for PCI.

Angiography showing guide catheter and calcified left main stem.

He proceeded to intervention and a 7Fr JL3.5 guide catheter was used to engage the LMS. After passing a guidewire the lesion was predilated with a 2.5 mm × 15 mm balloon to 14 atm. IVUS using high-definition 60 MHz rotational imaging catheter, was performed from the LAD to LMS followed by LCx to LMS. This demonstrated significant nodular calcification within the distal vessel and concentric calcification in the proximal LAD. The appearances were similar in the LCx; however, the ostium was free of disease (Figure 2). No calcium modification was carried out. As the LCx ostium was free of significant disease a provisional single stent strategy was chosen. After further pre-dilatation with a 4 mm NC balloon a 4.0 mm × 26 mm third-generation DES was deployed from the proximal LAD back to the ostium of the LMS. Proximal Optimisation Technique (POT) was performed using a 5 mm × 8 mm NC balloon. The LCx was re-wired and struts opened using a 2.0 mm × 12 mm balloon to 10 atm. IVUS was repeated in the LAD with further post dilatation of the stent using a 4 mm NC balloon. The IVUS catheter was then passed into the LCx, through the stent struts with ease, but on trying to remove the IVUS catheter became completely stuck. Due to concerns about snapping the IVUS or avulsing the stent or coronary artery, the proximal end of the IVUS catheter was cut to facilitate passage of a Guide Extension Catheter (GEC). The aim was to trap the stuck IVUS catheter between a balloon and the GEC to facilitate extraction. It was not possible to advance a 2 mm SC balloon inside the 7Fr GEC alongside the cut IVUS catheter due to the thickness of the IVUS catheter. Following removal of the GEC a 2.0 mm × 12 mm balloon was passed into the guide catheter until it sat over the proximal IVUS marker. The balloon was inflated to 12 atm acting as a trapping balloon, with a constant retracting force the IVUS catheter dislodged intact (Figure 3). Angiographically the stent in the LM/LAD had been disrupted, forming a tent like effect, lifting it off the inferior wall of the LMS where it was trapped and pulled. The operators were able to pass small balloons which were sequentially predilated with an improvement in angiographic appearance (Figure 4). The patient remained stable throughout.

Literature review of IVUS entrapment and successful retrieval methods

There are several mechanisms of entrapment proposed. These include where IVUS sheath or catheter tip can catch on a stent strut, especially if passed through a recently deployed or self-expanding stent.10,11 Anatomical features such as tortuous vessel geometry, severe calcification, small-diameter stents ≤2.5 mm and use of modern thin-strut third-generation DES were reported as independent risk factors.5 Device fracture can lead to separated IVUS outer sheath or fractured catheter fragments becoming trapped.12

Potential retrieval methods include the balloon-trapping technique where a balloon ‘traps’ the catheter shaft in the guiding catheter then withdrawing the whole assembly5,13 or using a GEC to cover the catheter exit port and facilitate removal.5,7 A buddy parallel guidewire and balloon technique can open space or push the stent strut aside. Live IVUS guided wiring can identify the same strut space with subsequent balloon modification. Snare or retrieval devices for fractured fragments.5,12 Finally surgical removal was rarely required when percutaneous retrieval failed or there was significant stent deformation or vessel compromise.6,14

Our case demonstrates some recognized risk factors for IVUS entrapment. As the IVUS catheter passed easily into the LCx it is assumed that it had passed through and got stuck on one of the struts of the recently deployed stent. This was a heavily calcified distal LMS lesion and the stent used was a third-generation DES with relatively narrower stent struts. It was larger than the <2.5 mm stents often implicated in the literature. Attempts were made to pass a 7Fr GEC; however, it would not pass over the IVUS catheter alongside trapping balloon due to internal diameter constraints. Passing another balloon to attempt to alter the geometry of the stent was considered; however, it was felt the wire would have invariably gone into an adjacent cell. Ballooning this would jail the IVUS catheter making it impossible to remove. Using live IVUS on an LAD wire could potentially allow wiring of the same cell but is not widely practiced outside experienced centres. Putting a balloon on the same wire as the IVUS to open the cell would not work as the IVUS would not advance to allow the balloon to cross the correct cell. It was fortunate the proximal sensor remained in the guide as it was discovered we could not get additional equipment, such as a balloon, into a 7F GEC. Balloon trapping technique was used successfully to remove the IVUS catheter. It was crucial to trap as close to the location of entrapment therefore if the IVUS catheter fractures the shortest fragment will be left.

Entrapped IVUS is a rare complication. It is important to recognize risk factors when using IVUS such as tortuous anatomy, severe calcification, stent diameter ≤2.5 mm and third-generation DES. To avoid this in the future may require using larger diameter balloons to open the DES struts, calcium modification with IVL or atherectomy in the LM/LAD may also reduce risk.
