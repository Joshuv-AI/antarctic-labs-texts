Texture-aware Intrinsic Image Decomposition with Model- and Learning-based Priors Thanks: * Corresponding author: Xin Yuan.
Xiaodong Wang, Zijun He, Xin Yuan

Abstract
This paper aims to recover the intrinsic reflectance layer and shading layer given a single image. Though this intrinsic image decomposition problem has been studied for decades, it remains a significant challenge in cases of complex scenes, i.e. spatially-varying lighting effect and rich textures. In this paper, we propose a novel method for handling severe lighting and rich textures in intrinsic image decomposition, which enables to produce high-quality intrinsic images for real-world images. Specifically, we observe that previous learning-based methods tend to produce texture-less and over-smoothing intrinsic images, which can be used to infer the lighting and texture information given a RGB image. In this way, we design a texture-guided regularization term and formulate the decomposition problem into an optimization framework, to separate the material textures and lighting effect. We demonstrate that combining the novel texture-aware prior can produce superior results to existing approaches. Code is available at https://github.com/xiaodongwo/Efficient_IID .



I
Introduction

Intrinsic image decomposition is a fundamental mid-level vision problem that aims to separate an image
{\bf I}
into its reflectance layer
{{\bf R}}
and shading layer
{{\bf S}}
as

{\bf I}={{\bf R}}\odot{{\bf S}}
, where “
\odot
” denotes element-wise multiplication. Shading image contains information about illumination conditions and scene geometry, whereas the reflectance image captures the material’s reflectance properties, which remain invariant to lighting conditions and shadow effects. Intrinsic image decomposition has been a focal point of research due to its wide applications in computer vision and computer graphics, such as relighting and material manipulation. Moreover, it plays a crucial role in low-light image enhancement and color correction.

Intrinsic image decomposition is inherently an ill-posed problem since there are infinite pairs of reflectance and shading that lead to the same input. Although considerable advancements have been made, current methods may still fall short when it comes to producing high-quality intrinsic images for real-world scenes. This is because real-world images contain a wide range of materials sometimes with high-frequency textures and unknown complex illumination.

Many efforts have been made to address this ill-posed inverse problem. These methods can be roughly divided into two categories: model-based methods

and learning-based methods

. Model-based methods use hand-crafted priors to regularize the feasible solutions of reflectance and shading, which is flexible but may fail to generalize to natural images in complex scenes such as spatially-varying lighting conditions. Learning-based methods learns the mapping function between an RGB image (input) and their intrinsic image pairs. During the inference stage, the learned network parameters are fixed so the reconstruction speed is fast. Although the performance of these algorithms is superior, the application is hampered by the cost of training such sophisticated networks and computing capability. Moreover, learning-based methods tend to low-frequency structure information rather than high-frequency texture information.

Bearing the above concerns, in this paper, we propose an efficient method to recover high-quality intrinsic images in the wild. Our method is built upon two observations.
i)
Firstly, model-based methods fail to disentangle the spatially-varying lighting and rich textures, which present both features in recovered reflectance and shading layers, as is shown in Fig.
1
(b-c).
ii)
On the other hand, learning-based method is able to separate the lighting effect from reflectance layer but ends up with missing textures in intrinsic images, as shown in Fig.
1
(d). High-frequency textures are hardly learned due to the low-frequency bias of neural network. To address these challenges, we present a novel optimization framework incorporating a pre-extracted learning-based prior to separate the spatially-varying lighting and high-frequency textures in intrinsic images. Specifically, the lighting-free reflectance layer is firstly pre-extracted from an off-the-shelf pretrained intrinsic images network. We then leverage
\mathbf{\ell}_{0}
norm and total variation (TV) prior based on the pre-extracted reflectance layer for ultimate intrinsic image decomposition. This optimization problem is then solved via the alternating direction of multipliers (ADMM) techniques
[
9
]
. Our approach generalizes the traditional optimization framework to complex scenes recovery in intrinsic image decomposition.
The main contributions of this work are summarized as:

•

We design a
learning-based prior
to regularize the reflectance and shading term in traditional optimization framework, which successfully remove the complex illumination in reflectance and preserve rich material textures in shading.

•

We derive an optimization procedure based on the ADMM framework and it leads to excellent results.

•

The proposed method is quite fast. For an image with 512×384 pixels, our method takes less than 3 seconds on average to get good results.

II
Related Work

In this section, we review previous works on intrinsic image decomposition, which can be classified into model-based and learning-based methods.

Model-based methods:
To address the challenges of intrinsic image decomposition, various priors have been employed. These include grayscale shading
[
1
,
2
,
3
]
, smooth shading

11
]

13

1

14

Reflectance sparsity priors are widely used to enhance spatial coherence in images, either globally or locally. Global sparsity assumes that a scene contains only a limited number of distinct materials, leading to similar reflectance values for groups of pixels. This approach often uses clustering techniques to group pixels with similar properties
[
13
,
1
,
14
]
. Local sparsity, on the other hand, assumes that reflectance is piecewise constant within small regions of the image. This method aims to flatten reflectance maps locally by applying a smoothness constraint
[
15
,
11
,
10
]
. By doing so, it helps maintain consistent reflectance within homogeneous areas while allowing for variations at object boundaries. Further, many methods have been proposed that require additional input to solve the problem. With user interaction,
[
16
]
showed that a very high-quality decomposition can be obtained. Researches in
[
17
,
18
]
incorporate additional depth/surface normal input data to provide scene geometry information. The work in
[
4
]
introduced an NIR image to provide shading information. For comprehensive reviews of these methods please refer to surveys by Bonneel et al.
[
19
]
and Garces et al.
[
20
]
.

Learning-based methods:
Data-driven methods have become a popular tool for learning priors and knowledge from training data. Early approaches focused on estimating the relative lightness of reflectance
[
5
]
or ordinal relationships between reflectance values
[
21
]
. Then, various convolutional neural networks have been proposed for dense prediction tasks
[

,
23
]
. Training on real-world datasets such as IIW
[

]
, providing annotations for similar reflectance properties, has been shown to improve the consistency of reflectance predictions

,

. Additionally, several techniques have been demonstrated to enhance local reflectance consistency, including generating labels similar to those in the IIW dataset from dense ground truth

, employing an unsupervised reflectance smoothness loss term
[

, using filters to flatten network predictions
[

, disentangling reflectance into flat surfaces and boundaries, and incorporating priors and classifiers to reduce specularities and shadows
[
8

. Some methods have also explored diffusion models that jointly learn depth and/or normal prediction with intrinsic image decomposition
[
7
,
26
]
. Learning in an unsupervised fashion from multi-illumination datasets has also been developed
[
27
]
. Besides, several works have used transformer to explore the local characteristics of the intrinsic image decomposition problem
[
28
,
29
]
.

III
Proposed Method

In this section, we present our approach for estimating intrinsic images. We begin by reviewing the image formation model, then introduce an efficient solver based on ADMM to address the optimization problem by our model. Finally, we provide details on the implementation and parameter settings.

III-A

Image Formation

Let
{\bf I}
represent an input image with pixel values normalized in the range
[0,1]
, and let
{{\bf R}}
and
{{\bf S}}
denote the reflectance and shading components, respectively. Assuming that the scene captured in the image
{\bf I}
follows Lambertian reflectance, and we can express the intrinsic image decomposition problem as follows:

{\bf I}={{\bf R}}\odot{{\bf S}},

(1)

where
{{\bf S}}
is a gray image, which is also extendable to multi-channel shading (similar idea with
[
6
]
), and
{{\bf R}}
is a multi-channel RGB image. We apply the constraint
{\bf I}\leq{{\bf S}}
. The goal is to estimate
{{\bf S}}
and
{{\bf R}}
from the input image
{\bf I}
.

III-B

Intrinsic Decomposition Model

We formulate the intrinsic image decomposition problem as the minimization of the following objective function:

E({{\bf S}},{{\bf R}})=f_{d}({{\bf S}},{{\bf R}})+\alpha f_{r}({{\bf R}})+\beta f_{s}({{\bf S}})+\gamma f_{a}({{\bf S}}),

(2)

where
\alpha
,
\beta
, and
\gamma
are positive parameters for combining three different objective functions.
f_{d}
is a data-fidelity term,
f_{r}
is the reflectance prior, and
f_{s}
is the shading prior; we further add an absolute scale term
f_{a}
in the energy function.

Data Fidelity:

Similar to many inverse imaging problems, a data-fidelity term is imposed to regularize the solution. We define the following
\mathbf{\ell}_{2}
error metric to quantify the reconstruction error:

f_{d}=\|{\bf I}-{{\bf R}}\odot{{\bf S}}\|_{2}^{2},

(3)

where
|\cdot|_{p}
denotes the
p
-norm operator.

Reflectance Prior:

One key observation about real-world images is that reflectance is generally sparse, exhibiting piecewise constant regions. Previous methods adopt
\mathbf{\ell}_{1}

[
10
]
or
\mathbf{\ell}_{0}

[
30
,
11
]
norm for sparsity. As reported in
[
11
]
,
\mathbf{\ell}_{0}
norm produce more piecewise constant relectance compared to
\mathbf{\ell}_{1}
norm. Moreover, it is less sensitive to nosie and complex shading variations. Therefore we enforce reflectance sparsity by limiting the number of reflectance discontinuities globally, using the
\mathbf{\ell}_{0}
norm defined as:

f_{r}=\|\nabla{{\bf R}}\|_{0},

(4)

where
\nabla
is the gradient operator. This
\mathbf{\ell}_{0}
sparsity term intuitively forces small reflectance discontinuities or noise to zero, while preserving the dominant structures in the reflectance. Further, We introduce a learning-based lighting-free prior into
\mathbf{\ell}_{0}
norm such to handle complex lighting scenes, which is introduced in section Model Solver.

Shading Prior:

Traditional priors involving shading often assume a smooth-varying surface and lighting. While this assumption is effective for simple cases, such as Mondrian-like images with patches of constant reflectance, they often fail in the case of natural images. One key feature of natural images is their complex textures, which can arise from either the reflectance layer (e.g., a flat surface with a dotted pattern) or the shading layer (e.g., a surface with bumps or wrinkles that produce shading patterns). Though researchers have used additional normal map to provide geometry surface hints
[
18
]
, it is still tremendously difficult to tell, even with human annotations.

We assume that existing intrinsic image decomposition networks (IID network) distinguish and recover the reflectance well. This assumption holds since the neural network is generally more photorealistic compared to traditional optimization methods. On this basis, a direct inversion with
{\bf I}\oslash{{\bf R}}^{\prime}
provides enough material textures of shading, where
\oslash
denotes element-wise division. We further impose a Total Variation prior (inspired by
[
31
]
) to enhance the high-frequency shading textures. This total variation-based shading prior term is:

f_{s}=\|\mathbf{TV}({{\bf S}}-{{\bf S}}^{\prime})\|_{1}.

(5)

where
{{\bf S}}^{\prime}
is the texture prior guided shading extracted by the off-the-self pretrained IID neural network, and we employ CRefNet
[
29
]
in this work. In practice, we use
{{\bf R}}^{\prime}
to denote the pre-extracted reflectance layer.

Absolute Scale Term:

In addition to the shading smoothness term, we also incorporate an absolute scale constraint on shading, similar to
[
11
]
, which is formulated as:

f_{a}=\|{{\bf S}}-{{\bf S}}_{0}\|_{2}^{2},

(6)

where
{{\bf S}}_{0}
is a reference shading value, which is a constant value for scale constraint. Adopting this scale constraint has two advantages. First, it helps avoid the ambiguity issue from
{\bf I}={{\bf R}}\odot{{\bf S}}
, since for each decomposition we can enlarge
{{\bf S}}
by any value and reduce
{{\bf R}}
by some value to get a different valid decomposition. Second, it ensures that there are as few extreme values of shading as possible.

III-C

Model Solver

Based on the above discussion, we formulate the ensemble energy function as

\displaystyle E({{\bf S}},{{\bf R}})

\displaystyle=\|{{\bf R}}\odot{{\bf S}}-{\bf I}\|_{2}^{2}+\alpha\|\mathbf{TV}({{\bf S}}-{{\bf S}}^{\prime})\|_{1}

\displaystyle\hskip 9.24994pt+\beta\|\nabla{{\bf R}}\|_{0}+\gamma\|{{\bf S}}-{{\bf S}}_{0}\|_{2}^{2},\hskip 9.24994pt\text{s.t.}\hskip 9.24994pt{\bf I}\leq{{\bf S}},

(7)

we assume
{{\bf R}}\in[0,1]
, therefore
{\bf I}\leq{{\bf S}}
is imposed element-wise. The objective function in Eq. (
7
) is non-convex because of the
\mathbf{\ell}_{0}
and
\mathbf{\ell}_{1}
norm regularization. To solve this problem, we apply alternating projection to split the optimization into two subproblems with respect to
{{\bf R}}
and
{{\bf S}}
. We then iterate between reflectance and shading prior until convergence. In particular, for the
k
-th iteration, the subproblems are as follows:

\arg\!\min_{{{\bf R}}}\ \|{{\bf R}}\odot{{\bf S}}-{\bf I}\|_{2}^{2}+\beta\|\nabla{{\bf R}}\|_{0},

(8)

\arg\!\min_{{{\bf S}}}\ \|{{\bf R}}\odot{{\bf S}}-{\bf I}\|_{2}^{2}+\alpha\|\mathbf{TV}({{\bf S}}-{{\bf S}}^{\prime})\|_{1}+\gamma\|{{\bf S}}-{{\bf S}}_{0}\|_{2}^{2}.

(9)

{{\bf R}}
subproblem:
we introduce an auxiliary variable
{{\bf G}}
and solve for
{{\bf R}}
and
{{\bf G}}
simultaneously by the minimization below:

\arg\!\min_{{{\bf R}},{{\bf G}}}\ \|{{\bf R}}-{\bf I}\oslash{{\bf S}}\|_{2}^{2}+\beta\|{{\bf G}}\|_{0}+\mu\|{{\bf G}}-\nabla{{\bf R}}\|_{2}^{2}.

(10)

As introduced in
[
30
]
, this problem can be further decomposed into two steps estimating
{{\bf R}}
and
{{\bf G}}
respectively.
{{\bf R}}
is computed by diagonalizing operators after Fast Fourier Transform (FFT) for speedup,

({{\bf G}}_{x},{{\bf G}}_{y})=\begin{cases}(0,0)&\text{if }(\nabla_{x}{{\bf R}})^{2}+(\nabla_{y}{{\bf R}})^{2}\leq\beta/\mu\\
(\nabla_{x}{{\bf R}},\nabla_{y}{{\bf R}})&\text{otherwise}\end{cases},

(11)

{{\bf R}}=\mathcal{F}^{-1}\left(\frac{\mathcal{F}({\bf I}\oslash{{\bf S}})+\mu(\mathcal{F}(\nabla_{x})^{*}\mathcal{F}({{\bf G}}_{x})+\mathcal{F}(\nabla_{y})^{*}\mathcal{F}({{\bf G}}_{y}))}{\mathcal{F}(1)+\mu(\mathcal{F}(\nabla_{x})^{*}\mathcal{F}(\nabla_{x})+\mathcal{F}(\nabla_{y})^{*}\mathcal{F}(\nabla_{y}))}\right)

(12)

where
\mathcal{F}
,
\mathcal{F}^{*}
and
\mathcal{F}^{-1}
denote the FFT operator, its complex conjugate and inverse FFT operator respectively.
\mathcal{F}(1)
is the Fourier Transform of the delta function.
\nabla_{x}
and
\nabla_{y}
are the first-order derivative operator along horizontal and vertical direction respectively. In practice, we implement our algorithm by replacing
\mathcal{F}({\bf I}\oslash{{\bf S}})
with a fixed lighting-free reflectance
\mathcal{F}({{\bf R}}^{\prime})
, thereby instantly injecting a lighting-free reflectance prior. We use
\mathbf{\ell}_{0}({{\bf R}},{{\bf R}}^{\prime})
to denote the imposed
\mathbf{\ell}_{0}
norm with pre-extracted prior
{{\bf R}}^{\prime}
. Fig.
2
depicts the general pipeline for our proposed optimization algorithm.

{{\bf S}}
subproblem:
we introduce another auxiliary variable
{{\bf H}}
and solve for
{{\bf S}}
and
{{\bf H}}
simultaneously by the minimization below:

\displaystyle\arg\!\min_{{{\bf S}},{{\bf H}}}\

\displaystyle\|{{\bf S}}-{{\bf I}}\oslash{{{\bf R}}}\|_{2}^{2}+\alpha\|\mathbf{TV}({{\bf H}}-{{\bf S}}^{\prime})\|_{1}

\displaystyle+\sigma\|{{\bf H}}-{{\bf S}}\|_{2}^{2}+\gamma\|{{\bf S}}-{{\bf S}}_{0}\|_{2}^{2},

(13)

where
\sigma
is a constant parameter. We can alternate between
{{\bf S}}
and
{{\bf H}}
to gradually approach the solution. Let us consider the following two subproblems:

\displaystyle\arg\!\min_{{{\bf S}}}

\displaystyle\ \|{{\bf S}}-{{\bf I}}\oslash{{{\bf R}}}\|_{2}^{2}+\sigma\|{{\bf H}}-{{\bf S}}\|_{2}^{2}+\gamma\|{{\bf S}}-{{\bf S}}_{0}\|_{2}^{2},

(14)

\displaystyle\arg\!\min_{{{\bf H}}}

\displaystyle\ \alpha\|\mathbf{TV}({{\bf H}}-{{\bf S}}^{\prime})\|_{1}+\sigma\|{{\bf H}}-{{\bf S}}\|_{2}^{2}.

(15)

Eq. (
14

\textsf{Denoiser}({{\bf S}},{{\bf S}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle{{\bf S}}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\bf I}\oslash{{\bf R}}+\sigma{{\bf H}}+\gamma{{\bf S}}_{0}}{\sigma+\gamma+1},

(16)

\displaystyle{{\bf H}}

\displaystyle=\textsf{Denoiser}({{\bf S}},{{\bf S}}^{\prime}).

(17)

Note that we have incorporated the pre-extracted learning-based prior into Eq. (

Experimental Result

We implement our proposed algorithm using Matlab on a desktop Intel Core i9 CPU and 128GB RAM. We have experimentally set the parameters as follows:
\mu=1
,
\beta=0.01
,
\sigma=1
,
\gamma=1
,
\alpha=2
,
{{\bf S}}_{0}=0.5
. For initialization,
{{\bf R}}
is set to be identical to
{\bf I}
. Meanwhile,
{{\bf S}}
is initialized through summing up the element-wise division of
{\bf I}
by
{{\bf R}}
.
{{\bf R}}^{\prime}
is pre-extracted from CREfNet
[
29
]
, and
{{\bf S}}^{\prime}
is obtained derived by converting the image
({\bf I}\oslash{{\bf R}}^{\prime})
from the RGB color space to the Value component of the HSV color space.
{{\bf S}}
and
{{\bf R}}
are updated until
\varepsilon_{\bf X}=(\|{\bf X}^{k}-{\bf X}^{k-1}\|\oslash\|{\bf X}^{k-1}\|)\leq\varepsilon
, where

could be
{{\bf R}}
or
{{\bf S}}
.

is set to be 0.00001. Surprisingly, we found that it converges within 3 iterations in our experiments.

Applications to low-light enhancement and recoloring:

We apply our method to low-light image enhancement and image recoloring. As shown in Fig.
7

Limitations and future work:

The limitation of our work is that it is challenging to deal with textures with colors. Our method is robust to shadows and spatial-varying lighting but may fail to distinguish color textures in reflectance layer. This is because the
\mathbf{\ell}_{0}
norm tends to oversmooth the reflectance layer and thus mis-distribute the color texture to shading texture layer. Overall our method could be a potential tool to incorporate the learning-based prior to traditional optimization framework and offers a robust way for intrinsic image decomposition, structure-texture decomposition, image editing, low-light image enhancement and so on.

Conclusion

We have presented an efficient method to recover
high-quality intrinsic images for real-world scenes. Our method incorporate a lighting-free prior (pre-extracted from an intrinsic image decomposition network) into a traditional optimization framework, thus enabling intrinsic images with lighting-free and texture enhancement decomposition. We have introduced a
\mathbf{\ell}_{0}
norm to impose the sparsity and lighting-free features of the reflectance, and designed a novel denoising term to enhance the material texture of the shading layer. We have performed experiments on IIW dataset and MIT dataset, thus demonstrating its feasibility. We want to emphasize that our methods are generally suitable for complex scenes with spatially-varying lighting and high-frequency material textures. For spectrally-varying textures it will be challenging to distinguish, and this will be our future work.
