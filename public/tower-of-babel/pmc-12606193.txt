More than half of shoulder vaccinations may end up in the subacromial space
Authors: Halah Kutaish, Pierre-Alban Bouché, Paolo Miguel Rivera, Anne Cornevin, Andres Borgonovo, Shana Mombelli, Rodrigo Brandariz, Benjamin De Pree, Anthony L. Cunningham, Gregory Cunningham
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Shoulder Injury Related to Vaccine Administration (SIRVA) can occur as subacromial bursitis, frozen shoulder, or tendinopathy following deltoid intramuscular immunization. This study evaluated needle overpenetration into the subacromial space using a standard needle in 177 patients undergoing arthroscopic shoulder surgery from 2022 to 2024, with a median age of 55. Findings showed a mean needle length of 25.36 mm was required for subacromial penetration, occurring in 51.9% of cases. Significant associations with needle overpenetration included Body Mass Index (BMI), weight, deltoid thickness, fat thickness, and combined fat and deltoid thickness, all statistically significant (p < 0.05). These results suggest the standard needle length may be too long for most adults, increasing complication risks. To prevent subacromial injections, the study recommends vaccination guidelines to adopt a shorter 15 mm needle for most adults, ensuring proper vaccine delivery into the deltoid muscle and minimizing SIRVA risk.

Introduction

Shoulder dysfunction following vaccine administration was first characterized by Bodor in 20071, who documented two cases of shoulder pain and weakness subsequent to influenza and pneumococcal vaccinations. The author postulated that a periarticular inflammatory response arose from inadvertent vaccination injection into the deltoid bursa or subacromial space. Subsequently, in 2010, Atanasoff coined the term “shoulder injury related to vaccine administration” (SIRVA) in a study detailing 13 cases of shoulder dysfunction and pain linked to deltoid muscle vaccinations2. The authors suggested that an immune-mediated inflammatory reaction could result from the unintended deposition of antigenic material into synovial tissues. The US National Vaccine Injury Compensation Program outlines criteria for diagnosing SIRVA, which includes the emergence of shoulder pain within 48 h post-vaccination, absence of prior shoulder pain or dysfunction, and exclusion of other contributing pathologies, such as brachial neuritis. Subsequent studies have reported various pathologies associated with SIRVA, including adhesive capsulitis, glenohumeral synovitis, traumatic osteonecrosis of the humeral head, chondral injury, and various nerve injuries, including axillary and radial nerve injuries, often accompanied by subdeltoid or subacromial bursitis3–7.

In response to these vaccine-related injuries, best practice recommendations have been established for safe injection techniques. The optimal injection site is defined as the midpoint of a triangular area formed laterally across the deltoid muscle, positioned two to three fingerbreadths below the acromion8. Vaccines should be administered at a 90-degree angle. The Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and according to the Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices General Best Practice Guidelines for Immunization recommends that for adults aged 19 years and older should be vaccinated with needle 22–25-gauge according to weight and sex stratified groups: 130–152 lbs (60–70 kg) use 25 mm needle; men 152–260 lbs (70–118 kg) and women 152–200 lbs (70–90 kg), 25–38 mm needle; and men 260 lbs (118 kg) or more and women 200 lbs (90 kg) or more, 38 mm needle9,10.

Despite these guidelines, reported occurrences of SIRVA have surged alongside the widespread administration of COVID-19 vaccines. As of August 2022, Bass et al. identified 28 cases of SIRVA associated with COVID-19 vaccination in the literature, with the majority presenting as painful shoulder, adhesive capsulitis or bursitis4,5,7. Earlier investigations, including Bodor’s work, highlighted that the deltoid bursa lies between 3 and 6 cm from the lateral acromion, with a skin-to-bursa depth ranging from 0.8 cm to 1.6 cm11–14. Therefore, a needle length of 2.5 cm may be excessive. The authors hypothesized that SIRVA may arise from inadvertent vaccine injection into the subacromial space because of excessive needle length. The aim of this study was thus to quantify the percentage of needle penetration into the subacromial space when adhering to current vaccine administration guidelines, with penetration confirmed through arthroscopic visualization of the subacromial space in a consecutive series of patients undergoing shoulder arthroscopic procedures for various reasons. The authors hypothesized that penetration would be observed in over 50% of cases. The secondary objective was to correlate the measured soft tissue width with demographic patterns and soft tissue width measured on preoperative MRIs to inform optimal needle length recommendations. Our secondary outcome was to identify an optimal threshold for needle length to avoid penetration into the subacromial space using a ROC curve. From this threshold, we calculated the rate of needle penetration into the subacromial space. Finally, we tried to identify which factors were associated with the needle length penetration.

Overpenetration in relation to different factors

The Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis for needle penetration into the deltoid bursa indicates the following key findings: Threshold value was identified at 16.7 mm, which represents the optimal cut-off point for distinguishing between penetration and non-penetration events. Sensitivity at this threshold was 48.0%, while the specificity is relatively high at 81.1% (Fig. 1). When extrapolating the results of needle depth on the different groups for the use of 15 mm needle there was only one penetration 1/172 (0.6%) and no fat only injection 0/172 (0.0%). Based on these results, the recommendation is to use a 15 mm needle. Discussion

In this study, we found that 52% of subjects had subacromial needle penetration using the needle length recommended by the CDC vaccination guidelines (25 mm)10. This may contribute to the occurrence of SIRVA following shoulder vaccination15–17. Another contributing factor of rise of SIRVA during the Covid-19 pandemic was mass vaccination and the lack of experience or inadequate training of the staff administrating the vaccine (e.g. military staff, pharmacy staff, medical personnel from unrelated fields)18.

On the other hand, vaccines administered in the subacromial space could also account for a lower vaccine efficacy, and could explain why a proportion of vaccinated patients during the Covid-19 pandemic still developed infection and disease, in addition to the well-established viral variant immune evasion18–20 In mouse models after immunization with RNA, adjuvanted protein vaccines and adjuvants are taken up by sparse muscle macrophages and dendritic cells but more vaccine flows rapidly in the lymph to draining lymph nodes (LNs), where it is taken up by similar cells and initiates the critical immune response. The muscle myeloid cells flow into the draining lymph node later and modulate the response, which is differential across the lymphoid chain21–23. In humans, the spread of vaccine to the draining axillary LNs has been monitored by PET-CT as inflammatory hypertrophy24–26 No such studies have been conducted to track the fate of vaccines after bursal injection–although the bursa has an excellent lymphatic supply. Future studies of SIRVA should address the adequacy of the immune response to vaccine antigens compared to true intramuscular injection.

The prevalence of SIRVA was rarely documented prior to the mass vaccination during the COVID-19 pandemic. Since then, the literature has reported on this problem with rates varying from 1.5 to 3.3%5,16. A study by Mackenzie et al. found an incidence of 3.7 cases per 100,000 vaccines27. Their findings indicate that SIRVA disproportionately affects women, even when proper injection technique is used. Similarly, Obeidat et al. described 35 cases of post-COVID SIRVA, reinforcing the observed sex disparity and noting subacromial bursitis as the most common MRI finding, present in 89% of cases28. This work shows similar results of women being at significantly higher risk of over penetration of the bursa, which might be a reason of why women seem to have higher cases of SIRVA when using the standard 25 mm needle length.

The significance of SIRVA lies in the widespread use of intramuscular vaccination and the growing number of vaccines now recommended not only during childhood but also throughout adulthood and into old age.

While the work of Mardourian et al. 202329 shows a CT simulation to determine the ideal injection site and needle length they still reported an overpenetration of <15% when using the 2.5 cm needle in the weight groups of all 60–70 kg, females 70–90 kg and males 70–118 kg. This is the first study to document in vivo penetration of the deltoid bursa and demonstrate that more than half of the patients, regardless of their baseline demographics (except for BMI) may suffer an inaccurate vaccine administration. Furthermore, the second main finding of this study was that needle length before subacromial space penetration was strongly correlated with the BMI.

This work shows that a 15 mm needle should be recommended to ensure adequate intra-muscular vaccine delivery to the deltoid muscle without the risk of SIRVA, while keeping in mind the extremely high BMI. However, our MRI measurement findings do not reflect those described by Poland et al.30 evaluating the shoulder fat pad thickness and muscle where they found an average fat pad thickness of 11.7 in women vs 8.3 mm in men30. These differences might be attributed to increased precision with MRI measures or to a change in the soft tissue mass distribution between the supine position for MRI acquisition compared to the sitting position. Caution about too short needle in the high BMI is for the vaccine to be administered in the adipose tissue which can lead to inadequate response31.

The major strengths of the study are the adequately powered size of this prospective cohort, consisting in an adult population with varied age and baseline characteristics in addition to the precise in vivo measurements of soft tissue via direct surgical observation.

This study has several limitations. First, the study population may represent a specific group with conditions affecting the shoulder. Although patients with severely limited shoulder function (such as frozen shoulder or neurological lesions) were excluded, a certain degree of prolonged non-use, leading to muscle atrophy, could be a factor in some of the included diagnoses, such as chronic rotator cuff tear. Nevertheless, multivariate analysis did not identify diagnosis as an independent risk factor, suggesting that this was not a confounding factor. Also, deltoid muscle thickness could have been altered by traction on arm and saline irrigation from the arthroscopic pump. To mitigate these risks, the measurements were done at the beginning of the procedure with low arthroscopic pump pressure with no traction was applied on the arm, to reflect the state of the shoulder during vaccination. Lastly, the study did not assess immunogenicity outcomes following subacromial versus intramuscular injections, and therefore, any implications regarding vaccine efficacy remain warrant further dedicated immunologic research.

These findings suggest that the standard vaccination needle length may be excessive for most adult patients, increasing the risk of complications such as SIRVA and potentially compromising vaccine efficacy. Revising vaccination guidelines to recommend a 15 mm needle for most adults could help prevent subacromial injections and associated complications by ensuring proper vaccine administration into the deltoid.

Methods

Study design and population

This investigation was a monocenter, prospective cohort study conducted at a single facility specialized in shoulder surgery. The design was prospective in nature, encompassing all patients scheduled for arthroscopic shoulder procedures from November 2022 to October 2023. All individuals requiring shoulder arthroscopy were eligible for inclusion in the study. Prior to starting this study, approval was obtained from the local ethics committee (BASEC 2022-02091). This study included all patients aged 18 years and older with indication for a shoulder arthroscopic procedure. Exclusion criteria consisted in patients with a history of previous ipsilateral shoulder surgery, known conditions resulting in local soft tissue atrophy (e.g., neurologic lesion or prolonged joint stiffness leading to muscle atrophy), and patients who were unable to give informed consent for the study. Quantitative data included demographic variables such as age, sex and BMI, with means and standard deviations calculated for each. Additionally, deltoid muscle and total soft tissue thickness were measured on MRI, alongside needle length and the percentage of subacromial penetration.

Recruitment, screening, and informed consent procedure

Convenience sampling was employed for patient recruitment, which occurred continuously via the project leader’s daily practice. Patients indicated for shoulder arthroscopy were included after informed consent was obtained.

Study procedures

Patients were operated under local anesthesia (interscalene block) in the sitting position (beach chair) position with no traction applied on the arm. The arthroscope was inserted into the subacromial space through a standard posterior viewing portal to allow proper visualization of the deep deltoid fascia in every case. A spinal needle was inserted laterally at the appropriate vaccination location, based on CDC guidelines. The needle was inserted at a 90-degree angle, ensuring that the deltoid muscle was not bunched to minimize the distance to the fat pad. The needle was advanced until penetration of the subacromial space was confirmed (Fig. 3). MRI measurements

For all patients with available preoperative MRI scans, quantitative assessment of soft tissue thickness was conducted by two independent observers utilizing OsiriX software (Pixmeo, Geneva, Switzerland). Measurements were performed on 3D-corrected T2-weighted MRI sequences in the coronal plane. Anatomical reference was standardized at the midpoint of the deltoid muscle, precisely located 5 cm distal to the lateral border of the acromion, corresponding anatomically to the typical vaccination injection site. To account for variability, additional measurements were performed at positions 3 mm proximal and 3 mm distal to the defined central reference point. Measurements included the total soft tissue thickness extending from the skin surface down to the deep fascia of the deltoid muscle, as well as the thickness of the deltoid muscle alone.

The interobserver reliability of MRI measurements was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC, ρ), with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) reported in square brackets. The analysis was performed on 149 cases, providing a two-sided 95% CI with a width of less than 0.08 when the ICC estimate exceeds 0.90. Reliability was classified as “excellent” for ρ > 0.90, “good” for 0.75 < ρ ≤ 0.90, “moderate” for 0.50 < ρ ≤ 0.75, and “poor” for ρ ≤ 0.50. Interobserver reliability was good for deltoid thickness (ICC = 0.87 [0.82–0.91]) and also good for the combined measurement of combined fat thickness (ICC = 0.84 [0.78–0.89]).

Statistical analysis

For the descriptive analysis of qualitative variables, we used frequencies and percentages; for continuous variables, we used the mean, standard error and ranges. ROC curves were used to determine the optimal needle length threshold to avoid subacromial penetration. Univariate linear regression was used to determine which factors were associated with the needle length penetration. Variables for which the univariate linear regression had a p-value < 0.2 were included in the multivariate linear model. The multivariate linear regression model used a backward elimination method to identify which factors were associated with the needle length penetration. Coefficients (β) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated. The threshold of significance was 5%, with a power of 80% and a first-species risk of 5%. R software (version 3.5.0) was used for statistical analysis (https://www.r-project.org/).
