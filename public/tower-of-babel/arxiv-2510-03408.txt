Inverse photoacoustic tomography problem in media with fractional attenuation


Abstract
Abstract. We investigate the inverse problem of recovering an initial source for the wave equation with fractional attenuation, motivated by photoacoustic tomography (PAT). The attenuation is modeled by a Caputo fractional derivative of order α ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) \alpha\in(0,1) . We establish uniqueness under a geometric foliation condition via an adaptation of two types of Carleman estimates to the fractional setting, prove stability through continuity inequalities for fractional time-derivatives of wave solutions, and derive a reconstruction scheme based on a Neumann series. While our results apply directly to PAT, we expect that the analytic approach and tools employed might be of broader relevance to the analysis of PDEs with memory effects and to inverse problems for attenuated wave models.

Introduction

For a given open and bounded domain
\Omega
with a smooth boundary
\partial\Omega
and a given relatively open subset
\Gamma\subset\partial\Omega
, this article focuses on the problem of determining an initial condition
u_{0}
supported inside
\Omega
, from observations consisting of the boundary trace at
\Gamma
of a wave field
u
generated by
u_{0}
, and propagating across an acoustically attenuated media. More specifically, for smooth functions
c(x)\geq c_{0}>0
and
a(x)\geq 0
, representing respectively the wave speed and internal damping, we analyze the invertibility —in suitable function spaces— of the observation map

\Lambda_{\alpha}:u_{0}\mapsto u|_{(0,T)\times\Gamma},

with
u
solution to the initial value problem (IVP)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}^{2}u-c^{2}(x)\Delta u+a(x)\partial_{t}^{\alpha}u=0,&(t,x)\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\\[5.0pt]
(u,\partial_{t}u)|_{t=0}=\left(u_{0},-\textstyle\frac{a(x)}{\Gamma(2-\alpha)}u_{0}\right),&x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.\end{array}\right.

(1)

In the previous system,
\Gamma(\alpha)
stands for the Gamma function, while
\partial^{\alpha}_{t}
represents the Caputo fractional derivative of order
\alpha\in(0,1)
defined as

\partial_{t}^{\alpha}f(t)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{f^{\prime}(s)}{(t-s)^{\alpha}}\,ds

for an absolutely continuous function
f
.

Our analysis aims to answer the three main questions related to the inverse problem: uniqueness, stability, and reconstruction; and for this purpose, we adapt an approach previously used by the authors to address the case of a wave equation with memory-type attenuation but involving a regular kernel. The main technical contributions stemming from this work include the refinement of a local unique continuation argument based on a foliation condition and two kinds of Carleman estimates; and specific Sobolev regularity inequalities concerning the Caputo fractional derivative of solutions to the unattenuated wave equation, which are obtained employing a novel formulation of the Caputo fractional derivative in the sense of distributions, the oscillatory integral representation of solutions to the wave equation, and the theory of Fourier Integral Operators (FIO’s).

The motivation behind this work arises from the medical imaging modality of photoacoustic tomography (PAT). The standard mathematical formulation in PAT involves recovering a spatially varying initial source term
u_{0}(x)
, which represents the ultrasound pressure distribution at time
t=0
, from measurements of ultrasonic waves collected at the boundary of the region of interest. This initial pressure distribution is directly linked to the optical energy absorbed by internal tissues following illumination by a laser pulse. It serves as the input for the second stage of PAT, which seeks to infer optical properties of the medium. The recovery of
u_{0}
constitutes what is commonly referred to as the
inverse photoacoustic tomography problem
.

The generation of ultrasound waves in PAT is typically modeled by a singular source term of the form
\delta^{\prime}(t)u_{0}(x)
, where
\delta^{\prime}
denotes the time derivative of the Dirac delta distribution. This representation is inspired by the assumption of instantaneous (or rapid) thermal deposition. It is also physically natural to assume that no acoustic signal exists prior to this, implying that the acoustic field
u(x,t)
vanishes for
t<0
. Therefore, for a wave operator of the form

P[u]=\partial_{t}^{2}u-c^{2}(x)\Delta u+\mathcal{A}[u],

where
c(x)
is the sound speed and
\mathcal{A}
is a linear attenuation operator, the governing model is the inhomogeneous equation
P[u]=\delta^{\prime}(t)u_{0}(x)
, together with the causality condition
u(x,t)=0
for
t<0
.

It is well known that this system is equivalent to an initial value problem (IVP)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}^{2}u-c^{2}(x)\Delta u+\mathcal{A}[u]=0,\\
(u,\partial_{t}u)|_{t=0}=(u_{0},u_{1}),\end{array}\right.

where the initial velocity
u_{1}
is determined by
u_{0}
and the form of
\mathcal{A}
. For example, in the unattenuated case (
\mathcal{A}=0
), one has
u_{1}=0
, while in the damped case, where
\mathcal{A}[u]=a(x)\partial_{t}u
with
a(x)\geq 0
, the initial velocity becomes
u_{1}=-a(x)u_{0}
(see, for instance,
[
18
]
).

In this paper, we study the inverse PAT problem for an attenuation operator defined by a lower order Caputo (fractional) time derivative
\mathcal{A}[u]=a(x)\partial^{\alpha}_{t}u
, with
\alpha\in(0,1)
. The limiting cases,
\alpha=0
and
\alpha=1
, corresponding to the unattenuated and (standard) damped wave equations, respectively, have been previously studied in the literature (see, e.g.,
[
34
,
35
,
18
,
30
,
17
,
31
]
).

We show below that for the fractional attenuation model
\mathcal{A}[u]=a(x)\partial^{\alpha}_{t}u
, the appropriate initial velocity is given by

u_{1}=-\frac{a(x)}{\Gamma(2-\alpha)}u_{0}(x),

which provides a smooth interpolation between the damped and undamped cases.

For uniqueness, we treat the partial data problem under the assumption that the domain admits an
admissible foliation
by strictly convex hypersurfaces. These sorts of conditions are crucial when one aims to obtain global results from local arguments. In our case, the adaptation of a local Carleman estimate methodology forces us to request rather technical conditions over the foliation (see Definition
3
). The main technical result of this section is Theorem
3
, which states the unique continuation principle from where the uniqueness of the inverse problem follows, corresponding to Corollary
1
.

The stability of the inverse problem is stated in Theorem
5
. The strategy adopted in its proof demands us to analyze the continuous dependence of fractional time-derivatives of the solution to the wave equation with respect to the initial data —this is in fact an interesting result by itself. See Lemma
8
. The desired stability inequality then follows by a perturbation argument concerning the analogous estimate for the PAT problem in the absence of attenuation, and previously obtained in the literature.

Finally, for the reconstruction result, we derive a Neumann series expansion under the assumption of full boundary observations (i.e., data available on the entire boundary) and smallness of the damping coefficient. See Theorem
6

1.1
Previous work

The inverse problem in photoacoustic tomography has been extensively studied over the past two decades, both from applied and theoretical perspectives. On the theoretical side, a systematic use of microlocal analysis techniques can be traced back to the influential works of Stefanov and Uhlmann
[
34
,
35
]
. In these papers, the authors provide a comprehensive treatment of the mathematical problem, addressing issues of uniqueness, stability, and reconstruction in both full and partial data settings for the conservative (i.e., unattenuated) wave equation, considering smooth and piecewise smooth wave speeds, respectively. An incomplete list of papers analyzing this inverse problem via microlocal tools is
[
34
,
35
,
18
,
37
,
7
,
30
,
38
,
39
,
3
,
10
,
11
,
31
]
.

Many foundational elements of this approach, however, originate in earlier works, including results on unique continuation and Carleman estimates, as well as insights on the propagation of singularities and time reversal methods. See, for instance,
[
12
,
19
]
.

In the context of attenuated media, the adaptation of such techniques to the damped wave equation (i.e., the
\alpha=1
case) was partially achieved in
[
18
]
, and later extended by the second author in
[
30
,
31
]
. The former of the last two articles focuses primarily on reconstruction, a topic that is also central to
[
17
]
, where multiple reconstruction methods are explored; the second article, on the other hand, incorporates boundary conditions and focuses on partial observations. A different model but somehow related to the damped wave equation is introduced in
[
2
]
, where, in a collaborative project, the first author of this present article employs control-theoretic ideas to study the mathematical inverse photoacoustic tomography problem in the context of thermodynamic attenuation.

Currently, multiple alternatives have been reported in the literature to mathematically represent attenuation effects in biological tissue. This challenge of accurately modeling attenuation in soft tissues is highlighted in
[
24
]
where, in addition, an integral transform relating measurements from the attenuated and unattenuated problems is proposed in the case of homogeneous media (i.e., with constant coefficients). This framework allows for the analysis of various attenuation models, many involving fractional derivatives in time —and in some cases, in space as well (see also
[
4
,
21
,
16
,
33
]
). More recently, an alternative approach has been proposed in
[
22
]
to obtain uniqueness in the inverse problem for homogeneous attenuated media involving time-fractional derivatives.

For heterogeneous media with fractional attenuation,
[
20
]
investigates the inverse problems of determining either a source term or an initial condition for the same equation considered in this work —namely, the wave equation with Caputo fractional attenuation of order
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Stability and uniqueness results are established via global Carleman estimates in the case of constant sound speed. It is now well understood, however, that this approach can be extended to variable sound speeds under specific geometric conditions —specifically, when the domain admits a foliation by concentric, strictly convex Euclidean spheres with respect to the sound speed metric. This can be regarded as a particular instance of our foliation condition (see Definition
3
). We also point out that system (
1
) does not fit into the framework of
[
20
]
, where it is assumed that one of the initial conditions vanishes with the purpose of allowing a symmetrization to negative times. We also point out that the analysis carried out in
[
20
]
allows for the fractional exponent
\alpha
to depend on the space variable. This assumption is not considered in this article (we assume a constant
\alpha
), and we leave the question of whether our techniques can be adapted to that setting for a future work.

This paper aims to extend some of the results obtained in
[
20
]
and provide a more complete treatment of the inverse PAT problem in fractionally attenuated media. Our approach is an adaptation of the methodology introduced by the authors in
[
3
]
, which addresses the inverse photoacoustic tomography problem with attenuation modeled by an integro-differential operator involving a regular kernel. In particular, we introduce a more general (albeit more technical) foliation condition that ensures uniqueness for the inverse problem as a consequence of a local Carleman estimate approach. This is a more restrictive class of foliations than the one introduced in
[
36
]
for the inverse problem of determining a sound speed. The reason for this lies in the fact that attenuation terms break the symmetry of the unattenuated wave equation; thus, standard unique continuation results do not hold, and have to be adapted. Additionally, by analyzing continuity inequalities of solutions to the wave equation —particularly the dependence of time-fractional derivatives with respect to initial data— we derive a stability estimate under sharper assumptions and rather general observation regions.

1.2
Organization of the paper

We begin with a section containing preliminary results that will be used throughout the paper. Specifically, we define a generalization of the Caputo derivative, as introduced in
[
27
]
, and reformulate it as an oscillatory integral using the Fourier and inverse Fourier transforms. We also clarify the motivation behind the particular initial conditions adopted in this work, which arise from formulating the photoacoustic tomography problem as an inhomogeneous wave equation with an instantaneous source at time zero. This preliminary section concludes with results related to the forward problem, notably the energy-dissipative nature of system (
1
), which is established using the positivity of a singular kernel associated with the Caputo derivative. We then demonstrate the well-posedness of the forward problem in fractional Sobolev spaces.

The main part of the paper is divided into three sections, each addressing one of the core aspects of the inverse problem: uniqueness, stability, and reconstruction. Section 3, on uniqueness, presents this property as a corollary of a unique continuation theorem. The main ingredients behind its proof are stated in three separate lemmas, two of them involving different types of local Carleman estimates. Section 4 is dedicated to stability and provides a corresponding theorem derived from a continuity inequality involving the Caputo fractional derivative and the solution to the unattenuated wave equation. This result is of independent interest and uses a microlocal representation (parametrix) of solutions to the wave equation. Finally, Section 5 focuses on reconstruction, providing an explicit formula in the form of a Neumann series, which builds on the main results established in the previous sections.

For completeness, we include several appendices that provide key analytical tools used throughout the paper. These cover interpolation theory and fractional Sobolev spaces, Bochner integration, convexity of Riemannian hypersurfaces, and symbol classes and Fourier integral operators.

2.2
Caputo’s fractional derivative for locally integrable functions

The standard definition of the Caputo fractional derivative
\partial^{\alpha}_{t}u
requires
u
to have an integrable first derivative (see, for instance,
[
32
]
). Based on the fact that the operation of convolution can be applied to two distributions in
\mathbb{R}
with support just bounded from below (see
[
13
]
and also
[
27
]
for an equivalent definition employing partitions of unity), this fractional operator has been recently extended in
[
27
]
to functions belonging to
L^{1}_{loc}[0,T)
—the set of all functions that are absolutely integrable on any compact set
K\subset[0,T)
. For the sake of completeness, we briefly describe the main definitions and properties of the generalized convolution and Caputo derivative, and we refer the reader to the aforementioned paper for more details.

Definition 1

(Definition 2.1 in
[
27
]
)
.

Given
f,g\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
such that there are constants
M_{f},M_{g}\in\mathbb{R}
satisfying
\text{\rm supp}(f)\subset[-M_{f},\infty)
and
\text{\rm supp}(g)\subset[-M_{g},\infty)
, we set

\langle f*g,\varphi\rangle:=\sum_{j}\langle f*(h_{j}g),\varphi\rangle,\quad\forall\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}),

where
\{h_{j}\}_{j\geq 0}
is any locally finite and compactly supported partition of unity in
\mathbb{R}
, i.e.,
h_{j}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
;
0\leq h_{j}\leq 1
;
\sum h_{j}(x)=1
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
; on any compact set
K\subset\mathbb{R}
there are only finitely many
h_{j}
’s with support intersecting
K
.

Remark 1
.

Notice that for a partition of unity as above and
for any test function
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
, the sum in the previous definition is always finite. In fact, the function
t\mapsto\langle(h_{j}g)(s),\varphi(t+s)\rangle
has support contained in

\text{supp}(\varphi)-(\text{supp}(h_{j})\cap[-M_{g},\infty)),

and it is clear that, because of locally finiteness, for all
j
large enough

[-M_{f},\infty)\cap\left(\text{supp}(\varphi)-(\text{supp}(h_{j})\cap[-M_{g},\infty))\right)=\emptyset.

Moreover, for a fixed time
T>0
, if we restrict our test function to those with support in
[0,T]
we can find an integer
N_{T}>0
for which

\langle f*g,\varphi\rangle:=\sum_{j=0}^{N_{T}}\langle f*(h_{j}g),\varphi\rangle,\quad\forall\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}([0,T]).

The extension of the convolution defined above satisfies the following properties. More details can be found in
[
27
]
.

Lemma 1

(Lemma 2.2 in
[
27
]
)
.

(i)

The definition is independent of
\{h_{j}\}
and agrees with the usual definition of convolution between distributions whenever one of the two distributions is compactly supported.

(ii)

For
f,g
as in Definition
1
,
f*g\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
and there is a constant
M\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\text{\rm supp}(f*g)\subset[-M,\infty)
.

(iii)

f*g=g*f
,
f*(g*h)=(f*g)*h
.

(iv)

Denoting by
D_{t}
the distributional derivative, then, for
f,g
as in Definition
1
we have

(D_{t}f)*g=D_{t}(f*g)=f*D_{t}g.

Definition 2

(Definition 3.4 in
[
27
]
)
.

Let
0<\alpha<1
and
f\in L^{1}_{loc}[0,\infty)
such that
t=0
is a Lebesgue point from the right, this is, there is
f_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
for which

\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{t}\int^{t}_{0}|f-f_{0}|dt=0.

The Caputo derivative of order
\alpha
is defined as

\partial^{\alpha}_{t}:f\mapsto\Phi_{-\alpha}*(H(t)(f-f_{0})),

where the kernel
\Phi_{-\alpha}
corresponds to the following tempered distribution

\Phi_{-\alpha}(t)=\frac{-1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\frac{d}{dt}\left(H(t)t^{-\alpha}\right),\quad\text{or equivalently}\quad\mathfrak{F}[\Phi_{-\alpha}](\omega)=(i\omega)^{\alpha}.

Here,
\mathfrak{F}
stands for the Fourier transform in the time variable. The convolution above makes sense in light of Definition
1
since both are distributions supported in
[0,\infty)
.

Following
[
27
]
, we verify that it is indeed an extension of the usual Caputo’s derivative. For
f
as in the previous definition and such that
f^{\prime}\in L^{1}([0,\infty))
, we see that

\displaystyle\Phi_{-\alpha}*(H(t)f)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}H(t)t^{-\alpha}*\frac{d}{dt}(H(t)f)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}H(t)t^{-\alpha}*(H^{\prime}(t)f_{0}+H(t)f^{\prime})

therefore,

\partial^{\alpha}_{t}f=\Phi_{-\alpha}*(H(t)(f-f_{0}))=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}H(t)t^{-\alpha}*H(t)f^{\prime}=\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\int^{t}_{0}\frac{f^{\prime}(s)}{(t-s)^{\alpha}}ds.

In addition, for
f\in L^{1}_{loc}(\mathbb{R})
and since
H(t)h_{j}(f-f_{0})\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
for all functions
h_{j}
in the partition of unity from Definition
1
, by Plancherel’s theorem we have that for any
\varphi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R})
,

\displaystyle\langle\partial^{\alpha}_{t}f,\varphi\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\langle\Phi_{-\alpha}*(h_{j}H(f-f_{0})),\varphi\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\langle(i\omega)^{\alpha}\mathfrak{F}[h_{j}H(f-f_{0})],\mathfrak{F}[\varphi]\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\langle\mathfrak{F}^{-1}\left[(i\omega)^{\alpha}\mathfrak{F}[h_{j}H(f-f_{0})]\right],\varphi\rangle.

Denoting
(\partial^{\alpha}_{t}f)_{j}:=\mathfrak{F}^{-1}\left[(i\omega)^{\alpha}\mathfrak{F}[h_{j}H(f-f_{0})]\right]
we have

\langle\partial^{\alpha}_{t}f,\varphi\rangle=\sum_{j}\langle(\partial^{\alpha}_{t}f)_{j},\varphi\rangle,

(2)

where, formally, we can write

(\partial^{\alpha}_{t}f)_{j}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\int^{\infty}_{0}e^{i(t-s)\omega}(i\omega)^{\alpha}h_{j}(s)(f(s)-f_{0})dsd\omega.

(3)

Remark 2
.

Without loss of generality, and for simplicity, we will assume that the locally finite and compactly supported partition of unity involved in the next computations is such that
h_{0}(0)\neq 0
and
\text{supp}(h_{j})\cap\{0\}=\emptyset
for all

.

Remark 3
.

The integrals in (

such that
\text{\rm supp}(h_{j})\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Furthermore, the function

t\mapsto\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\int^{\infty}_{0}e^{i(t-s)\omega}(i\omega)^{\alpha}h_{j}(s)f_{0}dsd\omega=f_{0}\cdot\mathfrak{F}^{-1}\left[(i\omega)^{\alpha}\mathfrak{F}[h_{j}](\omega)\right](t)

[0,\infty)

(\partial^{\alpha}_{t}f)_{j}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\int^{\infty}_{0}e^{i(t-s)\omega}(i\omega)^{\alpha}h_{j}(s)f(s)dsd\omega\quad\text{mod}\quad C^{\infty}([0,\infty)).

For

(4)

where
\chi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R})
is such that
\chi(0)=1
. We will use the following notation

(\partial^{\alpha}_{t}f)_{0,\epsilon}(t):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int\int^{\infty}_{0}e^{i(t-s)\omega}(i\omega)^{\alpha}h_{0}(s)(f(s)-f_{0})\chi(\epsilon\omega)dsd\omega,

(5)

hence, (
4
) rewrites as

(\partial^{\alpha}_{t}f)_{0}(t):=\overset{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})}{\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}}(\partial^{\alpha}_{t}f)_{0,\epsilon}(t).

(6)

2.3
The initial value problem in PAT with fractional attenuation

We begin with the singular-source formulation of the photoacoustic problem, representing rapid heat deposition of electromagnetic energy and the subsequent ultrasound propagation, which due to causality, we assume no waves are propagating at negative times. In addition, we assume that the attenuation integral runs from
-\infty
to the present time
t>0
, which means that in principle the strength of the attenuation depends on the whole past history of the wave field. We then arrive at the following inhomogeneous system

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}^{2}U-c^{2}\Delta U+\mathcal{A}[U]=\delta^{\prime}(t)\otimes f(x),\\
U|_{t<0}=0.\end{array}\right.

(7)

for an attenuation operator defined in the distributional sense by
\mathcal{A}[U]:=a(x)\left(U(t)*\Phi_{-\alpha}(t)\right)
.
This version of the fractional derivative coincides with the Riemann-Liouville and Caputo derivative from
-\infty
, for functions with sufficient regularity.

The relation between the inhomogeneous problem and the IVP is established in the next lemma.

Lemma 2
.

Let
u
be a smooth solution to the IVP (
1
) in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(0,\infty)
. Then,
U(x,t)=H(t)u(x,t)
solves (
7
) in the sense of distributions.

Proof.

For an arbitrary test function
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})
we have

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{A}[Hu],\phi\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\langle a\Phi_{-\alpha}*(h_{j}Hu),\phi\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\left\langle a(h_{j}Hu)(t),\left\langle\textstyle\frac{-1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\partial_{t}\left(H(t)t^{-\alpha}\right)(s),\phi(t+s)\right\rangle\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\left\langle a(h_{j}Hu)(t),\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int^{\infty}_{t}\frac{\phi^{\prime}(r)dr}{(r-t)^{\alpha}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}a(x)\sum_{j}\int^{\infty}_{0}\int^{\infty}_{t}\frac{h_{j}(t)u(t)\phi^{\prime}(r)}{(r-t)^{\alpha}}drdtdx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}a(x)\int^{\infty}_{0}\phi^{\prime}(r)\left(\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int^{r}_{0}\Big(\sum_{j}h_{j}(t)\Big)u(t)\partial_{t}\left(\frac{(r-t)^{1-\alpha}}{1-\alpha}\right)dt\right)drdx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}a(x)\int^{\infty}_{0}\phi^{\prime}(r)\left(\frac{-1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int^{r}_{0}u^{\prime}(t)\frac{(r-t)^{1-\alpha}}{1-\alpha}dt\right)drdx

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}a(x)\int^{\infty}_{0}\frac{\phi^{\prime}(r)u(0)}{(1-\alpha)\Gamma(1-\alpha)}drdx

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}a(x)\int^{\infty}_{0}\phi(r)\partial_{r}\left(\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int^{r}_{0}u^{\prime}(t)\frac{(r-t)^{1-\alpha}}{1-\alpha}dt\right)drdx

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}a(x)\frac{\phi(0)u(0)}{(1-\alpha)\Gamma(1-\alpha)}dx

\displaystyle=\langle Ha\partial^{\alpha}_{t}u,\phi\rangle+\frac{1}{{(1-\alpha)\Gamma(1-\alpha)}}\langle a\delta u,\phi\rangle.

In consequence, noticing that
(1-\alpha)\Gamma(1-\alpha)=\Gamma(2-\alpha)
and using that
u
solves (
1
), we obtain

\displaystyle\langle(\square_{c}+\mathcal{A})U,\phi\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\delta^{\prime}u+2\delta u^{\prime}+\textstyle\frac{1}{{\Gamma(2-\alpha)}}a\delta u,\phi\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left[-u(0)\phi^{\prime}(0)-u^{\prime}(0)\phi(0)+2u^{\prime}(0)\phi(0)+\textstyle\frac{1}{{\Gamma(2-\alpha)}}au(0)\phi(0)\right]dx

\displaystyle=\langle f(x)\delta^{\prime}(t),\phi\rangle,

this is,
U(x,t)
is a solution to
\square_{c,\alpha}U=f\delta^{\prime}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, and such that
U=0
for
t<0
.
∎

2.4
Some properties of the forward problem

The existence and uniqueness of solutions for the initial boundary value problem (with null Dirichlet conditions), as well as standard regularity and higher regularity results, were proven in
[
20
]
. We complement the analysis of the system (
1
) by establishing finite propagation speed, energy dissipation, and fractional Sobolev regularity.

The finite propagation speed will allow us to treat the IVP in
(0,T)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
as an initial boundary value problem (IBVP) with null Dirichlet conditions by taking a sufficiently large ball as the domain, while the energy dissipation will be an important feature in the study of a reconstruction formula.

We begin with a preliminary result that implies a positivity property for the fractional derivative operator.

Lemma 3
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
and
{\cal{U}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a bounded region with a smooth boundary. The fractional derivative kernel
\frac{t^{-\alpha}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}
defines a positive semidefinite operator in
L^{2}((0,T)\times{\cal{U}})
, meaning that

\left\langle\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int^{t}_{0}\frac{v(x,s)}{(t-s)^{\alpha}}ds,v(x,t)\right\rangle\geq 0,\quad\forall v\in L^{2}((0,T)\times{\cal{U}}),

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
stands for the inner product in
L^{2}((0,T)\times{\cal{U}})
.

Proof.

The
L^{2}(0,T)
–coercivity of the convolution operator with kernel
q(t)=\frac{t^{-\alpha}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}
and
\alpha\in(0,1)
is proven in
[
9
]
. For smooth functions, it is clear from a pointwise application of the previous and Fubini’s theorem that

\left\langle\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int^{t}_{0}\frac{v(x,s)}{(t-s)^{\alpha}}ds,v(x,t)\right\rangle=\int_{\cal{U}}\left(\int^{T}_{0}(q*v)vdt\right)dx\geq 0.

The general case follows by density of smooth function in
L^{2}((0,T)\times U)
and the next inequality which is obtained from Young’s convolution inequality:

\left|\left\langle\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int^{t}_{0}\frac{u(x,s)}{(t-s)^{\alpha}}ds,v(x,t)\right\rangle\right|\leq\frac{T^{1-\alpha}}{\Gamma(2-\alpha)}\|u\|_{L^{2}((0,T)\times{\cal{U}})}\|v\|_{L^{2}((0,T)\times{\cal{U}})}.

∎

Theorem 1
.

The system (
1
) with
\alpha\in(0,1)
has finite propagation speed and is dissipative in the sense that for any
{\cal{U}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
bounded region with a smooth boundary, if
u
solves (
1
) and
u=0
on
(0,T)\times\partial{\cal{U}}
then

E_{\cal{U}}(t)=E_{\cal{U}}(0)-2\langle a\partial^{\alpha}_{t}u,c^{-2}\partial_{t}u\rangle

with

E_{\cal{U}}(t):=\int_{{\cal{U}}}|u_{t}|^{2}c^{-2}dx+\int_{{\cal{U}}}|\nabla u|^{2}dx.

(8)

and
\langle a\partial^{\alpha}_{t}u,c^{-2}\partial_{t}u\rangle\geq 0
.

Proof.

The finite propagation speed is a consequence of the previous and the coarea formula. Indeed, given any smooth function
\phi(x)
such that
c^{2}(x)|\nabla\phi(x)|^{2}\leq 1
, we consider the backward cone

\mathcal{C}=\{(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(0,\tau):\phi(x)<\tau-t\}

whose temporal cross-sections and their boundaries are given respectively by

\mathcal{C}(t)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\phi(x)<\tau-t\}\quad\text{and}\quad\partial\mathcal{C}(t)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\phi(x)=\tau-t\}.

For any smooth solution
u
to (
1
) we denote by
E_{\mathcal{C}(t)}
its energy inside the region
\mathcal{C}(t)
, therefore

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}E_{\mathcal{C}(t)}

\displaystyle=2\int_{\mathcal{C}(t)}\left(c^{-2}u_{t}u_{tt}+\nabla u_{t}\cdot\nabla u\right)dx-\int_{\partial\mathcal{C}(t)}\left(c^{-2}|u_{t}|^{2}+|\nabla u|^{2}\right)|\nabla\phi|^{-1}dS

\displaystyle=2\int_{\mathcal{C}(t)}u_{t}\left(c^{-2}u_{tt}-\Delta u\right)dx+\int_{\partial\mathcal{C}(t)}u_{t}\partial_{\nu}udS

\displaystyle-\int_{\partial\mathcal{C}(t)}\left(c^{-2}|u_{t}|^{2}+|\nabla u|^{2}\right)|\nabla\phi|^{-1}dS

\displaystyle\leq-2\int_{\mathcal{C}(t)}ac^{-2}u_{t}\partial^{\alpha}_{t}udx.

Integrating in time over the interval
(0,\tau)
we get

E_{\mathcal{C}(\tau)}\leq E_{\mathcal{C}(0)}-2\int_{\mathcal{C}(0)}a(x)c^{-2}(x)\left(\int^{\tau(x)}_{0}u_{t}(x,t)\int^{t}_{0}\frac{(t-s)^{-\alpha}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}u_{t}(x,s)dsdt\right)dx,

where
\tau(x)=\sup\{t\in(0,\tau):(x,t)\in\mathcal{C}\}
. Since the last term on the right-hand side is nonpositive, we conclude that
E_{\mathcal{C}(0)}=0
implies
E_{\mathcal{C}(\tau)}=0
, which means there is finite propagation speed.

For the last part of the lemma, we again assume enough regularity and then argue by density. Let’s multiply (
1
) by
c^{-2}u_{t}
and integrate over
{\cal{U}}
, where we recall
u=0
on
(0,T)\times\partial{\cal{U}}
. We then have

\frac{d}{dt}E_{\cal{U}}(t)=2\int_{{\cal{U}}}u_{t}(c^{-2}u_{tt}-\Delta u)dx=-2\int_{\cal{U}}au_{t}(\partial^{\alpha}_{t}u)dx.

Integrating in time and denoting
v=c^{-1}a^{1/2}u_{t}
, we obtain that

E_{\cal{U}}(T)-E_{\cal{U}}(0)=-2\int_{(0,T)\times{\cal{U}}}\left(\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int^{t}_{0}\frac{v(x,s)}{(t-s)^{\alpha}}ds\right)v(x,t)dxdt\leq 0.

∎

Let’s precise now the energy spaces of initial conditions.
Consider
A=c^{2}(x)\Delta
endowed with null Dirichlet boundary conditions at the smooth boundary

of some open and bounded region

We also set

and

D(A^{\theta})=\{f\in L^{2}(\Omega):A^{\theta}f\in L^{2}(\Omega)\},\quad\theta\in(0,1),

the respective domains of the fractional-order operators

. We define the energy spaces of initial conditions as

\mathcal{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega):=D(A^{1/2})\times D(A^{0})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{H}^{2}_{0}(\Omega):=D(A)\times D(A^{1/2}),

ans for
s\in(0,1)
their interpolation spaces

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{1+s}_{0}(\Omega)=D(A^{(1+s)/2})\times D(A^{s/2}),

which are continuously embedded in
H^{1+s}(\Omega)\times H^{s}(\Omega)
. In particular, the space
\mathcal{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
is topologically equivalent to the completion of
C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)\times C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
under the energy norm

\|(f,g)\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)}:=\int_{\Omega}(|\nabla f(x)|^{2}+|g(x)|^{2})dx.

(9)

For more details on interpolation, Sobolev spaces and the defintion of

and

A
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
u
be the unique solution to

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\square_{c,\alpha}u=F,&(0,T)\times\Omega\\
u=0,&(0,T)\times\partial\Omega\\
(u,\partial_{t}u)|_{t=0}=(f,g),&\Omega,\end{array}\right.

(10)

with
(f,g)\in\mathcal{H}^{1+s}_{0}(\Omega)
,

F\in L^{1}([0,T];D(A^{s/2}))

for some

.
Then,

u\in C^{1}([0,T];D(A^{s/2}))\cap C([0,T];D(A^{(1+s)/2})),

and the following continuous dependence holds

\displaystyle\|(u,\partial_{t}u)\|_{H^{1+s}(\Omega)\times H^{s}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\|(f,g)\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1+s}_{0}(\Omega)}+\|F\|_{L^{1}([0,T];D(A^{s/2}))}\right).

Remark 4
.

Notice above that for
(f,g)
and
F
as in the statement of the theorem, they in particular satisfy that
(f,g)\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)
and
F\in L^{1}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))
, therefore, the existence and uniqueness of the solution in the energy space
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)
follows from
[
20
]
.

Proof.

Let’s consider the solution operator for the unattenuated wave equation,

\text{\bf U}(t):(f,g)\mapsto(u(t),\partial_{t}u(t)),\quad\text{for $t>0$}

(11)

where
u
solves

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}^{2}u-c^{2}\Delta u=0,&(0,T)\times\Omega\\
u=0,&(0,T)\times\partial\Omega\\
(u,\partial_{t}u)|_{t=0}=(f,g),&\Omega.\end{array}\right.

(12)

By interpolation, and standard regularity and higher regularity results, we have that the map
t\mapsto\text{\bf U}(t)
belongs to
C([0,T];\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}^{1+s}_{0}(\Omega)))
, where for a given Banach space
X

\mathcal{B}(X)
to denote the Banach space of bounded linear operators from
X
onto itself.

Furthermore, the Duhamel’s principle implies that the inhomogeneous solution operator

\text{\bf V}(t):(f,g,F)\mapsto(v(t),\partial_{t}v(t)),

with

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\partial_{t}^{2}v-c^{2}\Delta v=F,&(0,T)\times\Omega\\
v=0,&(0,T)\times\partial\Omega\\
(v,\partial_{t}v)|_{t=0}=(f,g),&\Omega,\end{array}\right.

(13)

can be written in the form

\text{\bf V}(t)[f,g,F]=\text{\bf U}(t)[f,g]+\int^{t}_{0}\text{\bf U}(t-s)[0,F(s)]ds,

(14)

from where it is clear that for a fixed

F\in L^{1}([0,T],D(A^{s/2}))
,

t\mapsto\text{\bf V}(t)[\cdot,\cdot,F]\in C([0,T];\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}^{1+s}_{0}(\Omega))).

For more details about integration on Banach spaces, we refer the reader to Appendix
B
. Moreover, by interpolation it is clear that

\|\text{\bf V}(\cdot)[f,g,F]\|_{C([0,T];\mathcal{H}^{1+s}_{0}(\Omega))}\leq C\left(\|(f,g)\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1+s}_{0}(\Omega)}+\|F\|_{L^{1}([0,T];D(A^{s/2}))}\right).

Following
[
20
]
, we use the previous two operators along with Duhamel’s principle to write the solution
\text{\bf U}_{\alpha}(t)=(u(t),\partial_{t}u(t))
to (
10

\text{\bf U}_{\alpha}(t)=\text{\bf V}(t)[f,g,F]+\int^{t}_{0}\int^{s}_{0}\text{\bf U}(t-s)Q(s-\tau)\text{\bf U}_{\alpha}(\tau)d\tau ds,

(15)

where
Q(t)
is defined as

Q(t):=-\frac{t^{-\alpha}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\left(\begin{matrix}0&0\\
0&a(x)\end{matrix}\right),\quad\text{for}\quad t\in(0,T].

It follows from Young’s convolution inequality that the operation of convolution against the kernel
Q(t)
maps continuously
L^{1}((0,T);\mathcal{H}^{1+s}_{0}(\Omega))
into itself.

Denoting by
(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}\text{\bf U}_{\alpha})(t)
the double integral on the right-hand side of (
15
) (for
f,g,F
fixed) and
(\mathcal{G}\text{\bf U}_{\alpha})(t):=\text{\bf V}(t)[f,g,F]+(\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}\text{\bf U}_{\alpha})(t)
, equation (
15
) rewrites as
\text{\bf U}_{\alpha}=\mathcal{G}\text{\bf U}_{\alpha}
, which means that we are looking for a fixed point for the operator
\mathcal{G}
in some subspace
X_{0}\subset C([0,T];\mathcal{H}^{1+s}_{0}(\Omega))
.

The Banach fixed point theorem demands to find a contracting iterate
\mathcal{G}^{m}
for some
m\geq 1
. It follows directly from
[
20

that there is a constant
C_{1}>0
such that for all

,

\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{m}\text{\bf V}\|_{C([0,T];\mathcal{H}^{1}_{0})}\leq\frac{C^{m}_{1}T^{m(2-\alpha)-1}}{\Gamma(m(2-\alpha)-1)}\|\text{\bf V}\|_{C([0,T]:\mathcal{H}^{1}_{0})},\quad\forall\text{\bf V}\in C([0,T];\mathcal{H}^{1}_{0}),

and similarly, there is another constant
C_{2}>0
such that

\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{m}\text{\bf V}\|_{C([0,T];\mathcal{H}^{2}_{0})}\leq\frac{C^{m}_{2}T^{m(2-\alpha^{\prime})-1}}{\Gamma(m(2-\alpha^{\prime})-1)}\|\text{\bf V}\|_{C([0,T];\mathcal{H}^{2}_{0})},\quad\forall\text{\bf V}\in C([0,T];\mathcal{H}^{2}_{0}),

with
\alpha^{\prime}=\frac{1+\alpha}{2}
. The constants
C_{i}
,
i=1,2
, depend on
\Omega,T,\alpha
and
a
.

Denoting by
\delta_{1,m},\delta_{2,m}>0
the respective constants on the right-hand side of the previous inequalities, by properties of the Gamma function one deduces that
\delta_{i,m}<1
for
i=1,2
, provided that one takes
m\geq 1
large enough.

Setting

X_{0}=[C([0,T];\mathcal{H}^{2}_{0}),C([0,T];\mathcal{H}^{1}_{0})]_{1-s},

which is a Banach space when equipped with the interpolation norm

(see Appendix
A
for its definition), we get from the previous that

\|\widetilde{\mathcal{G}}^{m}\text{\bf V}\|_{X_{0}}\leq\delta_{2,m}^{s}\delta_{1,m}^{1-s}\|\text{\bf V}\|_{X_{0}},\quad\forall\text{\bf V}\in X_{0}.

This implies that for any pair
\text{\bf V},\text{\bf W}\in X_{0}
,

\|\mathcal{G}^{m}\text{\bf V}-\mathcal{G}^{m}\text{\bf W}\|_{X_{0}}\leq\delta_{2,m}^{s}\delta_{1,m}^{1-s}\|\text{\bf V}-\text{\bf W}\|_{X_{0}},

which means
\mathcal{G}^{m}
is a contraction in
X_{0}
, and consequently, there is a fixed point
\text{\bf U}\in X_{0}
and only remains to show that

X_{0}\subset C([0,T];\mathcal{H}^{1+s}_{0}(\Omega)).

A function
\text{\bf U}(t,x)
belonging to the interpolation space
X_{0}
can be decomposed as
\text{\bf U}=\text{\bf U}_{2}+\text{\bf U}_{1}
with
\text{\bf U}_{i}\in C([0,T];\mathcal{H}^{i}_{0}(\Omega))
,
i=1,2
. Moreover, for all
t\in[0,T]
and
\rho>0
we have

\displaystyle\|\text{\bf U}_{2}(t)\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}^{2}_{0}(\Omega)}+\rho^{2}\|\text{\bf U}_{1}(t)\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}\leq\|\text{\bf U}_{2}\|^{2}_{C([0,T];\mathcal{H}^{2}_{0}(\Omega))}+\rho^{2}\|\text{\bf U}_{1}\|^{2}_{C([0,T];\mathcal{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega))},

thus, taking square root and infimum with respect to the decomposition
\text{\bf U}=\text{\bf U}_{2}+\text{\bf U}_{1}
we deduce the following inequality between the
K
-functionals for the respective pairs of spaces
(\mathcal{H}^{2}_{0}(\Omega),\mathcal{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)))
and
(C([0,T];\mathcal{H}^{2}_{0}(\Omega)),C([0,T];\mathcal{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)))

K(\rho,\text{\bf U}(t))\leq K(\rho,\text{\bf U}),\quad\forall t\in[0,T].

The definitions of the K-functional and the interpolation norm are included in Appendix
A
.

It follows directly then that
\|\text{\bf U}(t)\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1+s}_{0}(\Omega)}\leq\|\text{\bf U}\|_{X_{0}}
for all

and subsequently,

\|\text{\bf U}\|_{C([0,T];\mathcal{H}^{1+s}_{0}(\Omega))}\leq\|\text{\bf U}\|_{X_{0}}.

The continuity inequality follows from the previous, the fixed-point formulation of equation (
10
), the continuous embedding of
\mathcal{H}^{1+s}_{0}(\Omega)
into
H^{1+s}(\Omega)\times H^{s}(\Omega)
, and the fact that for
m
large enough,
\|\tilde{\mathcal{G}}^{m}\|_{\mathcal{B}(X_{0})}\leq\delta_{m}<1
with
\delta_{m}\to 0
as

. Indeed,

\displaystyle\|\text{\bf U}\|_{X_{0}}=\|\mathcal{G}^{m}\text{\bf U}\|_{X_{0}}

\displaystyle\leq\sum^{m-1}_{k=0}\|\tilde{\mathcal{G}}\text{\bf V}(\cdot)[f,g,F]\|_{X_{0}}+\|\tilde{\mathcal{G}}^{m}\text{\bf U}\|_{X_{0}}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\|\text{\bf V}(\cdot)[f,g,F]\|_{X_{0}}+\delta_{m}\|\text{\bf U}\|_{X_{0}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\|\text{\bf V}(\cdot)[f,g,F]\|_{C([0,T];\mathcal{H}^{1+s}_{0}(\Omega))}+\delta_{m}\|\text{\bf U}\|_{X_{0}}\right),

thus, by taking

large enough we can absorb the last term on the right and and obtain

\displaystyle\|\text{\bf U}\|_{H^{1+s}(\Omega)\times H^{s}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\text{\bf U}\|_{X_{0}}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\text{\bf V}(\cdot)[f,g,F]\|_{C([0,T];\mathcal{H}^{1+s}_{0}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\|(f,g)\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1+s}_{0}(\Omega)}+\|F\|_{L^{1}([0,T];D(A^{s/2}))}\right).

∎

3
Uniqueness

In this section, we generalize the uniqueness result presented in
[
20
]
by allowing more general foliations by strictly convex surfaces. In
[
20
]
, the authors address the constant sound speed case, and it is clear, as pointed out there, that the argument can be extended to variable speeds subject to the existence of a function with strictly convex level sets (for the sound speed metric
c^{-2}dx^{2}
). A common particular instance of this requirement consists in imposing the sound speed to satisfy an inequality of the form

\frac{(x-x_{0})\cdot\partial c(x)}{c(x)}<1,\quad\forall x\in\Omega.

for some fixed
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In geometric terms, the previous inequality is equivalent to requiring the Euclidean spheres
\{x:|x-x_{0}|=r\}
, with
r>0
, to be strictly convex under the sound speed metric.

The main result of this section states that provided the existence of an admissible foliation by strictly convex hypersurfaces, null boundary observations at
(0,T)\times\Gamma
imply a vanishing initial condition
u_{0}
in a domain of influence associated to
(0,T)\times\Gamma
and the foliation.

Below, we define what we mean by an admissible foliation of
\Omega
with respect to the observation region
\Gamma
. We point out that this is a more restrictive type of foliation than those considered previously in
[
36
]
. The reason for considering this family of foliations lies in the fact that, due to the time-convolution term, we do not have a John’s (or double-cone) unique continuation-type result at our disposal, and we are forced to move in small increments and via local unique continuation results, perpendicular to the foliation. This contrasts with standard unique continuation results where the zero-set typically advances across balls for the sound speed metric, which might not be strictly convex for large radius.

Definition 3
.

Let
\Omega_{1}\Supset\Omega
be a larger domain with a smooth boundary.
We say that a one-parameter family of smooth, compact, and oriented hypersurfaces
\Sigma(\Omega,\Gamma)=\{\Sigma_{s}\}_{s\in[\underline{s},\overline{s}]}
is
an
admissible foliation of
(\Omega,\Gamma)

if the following conditions are met:

(a)

(uniformly continuous family) for every
\epsilon>0
there is
\delta>0
such that the Hausdorff distance
\text{dist}_{H}(\Sigma_{s},\Sigma_{s^{\prime}})<\epsilon
whenever
|s-s^{\prime}|<\delta
;

(b)

each
\Sigma_{s}
divides
\Omega_{1}
into two open connected parts which we denote as
\Sigma_{s}^{\text{int}}
and
\Sigma_{s}^{\text{ext}}
, with the later region satisfying that its closure contains
\partial\Omega_{1}
. Moreover,
\Omega\subset\Sigma^{\text{int}}_{\underline{s}}
,
\overline{\Sigma_{s}^{\text{int}}}\subset\Sigma_{s^{\prime}}^{\text{int}}
for all
s>s^{\prime}
, and
\Sigma_{s}\cap(\partial\Omega\setminus\Gamma)=\emptyset
for all
s
;

(c)

(normal vector field and its integral curves) for any
s
and
x\in\Sigma_{s}
we let
\nu(x)
be the interior unit normal vector, this is, pointing towards
\Sigma_{s}^{\text{int}}
. This gives to each leaf
\Sigma_{s}
a global orientation. The resulting vector field
\nu
is assumed to be
C^{2}
and we require that its integrals curves passing through
\Omega\cap\Sigma_{\overline{s}}^{\text{ext}}
intersect the observation region
\Gamma
only once;

(d)

(flat coordinates) for every
s\in[\underline{s},\overline{s}]
and at each point
x\in\Sigma_{s}
there is a flat chart
(U,\varphi)
containing
x
. In other words, there is a continuous function
\zeta(s^{\prime})
such that each leaf
\Sigma_{s^{\prime}}
intersecting
U
takes the local form
\{x=(x^{\prime},x^{n}):x^{n}=\zeta(s^{\prime})\}
.

(e)

(boundary normal coordinates) for every
s\in[\underline{s},\overline{s}]
there is an
\epsilon_{s}>0
such that boundary normal coordinates can be prescribed in a collar neighborhood of
\Sigma_{s}
of the form
\{x\in\Omega_{1}:\text{\rm dist}(x,\Sigma_{s})<\epsilon_{s}\}
;

(f)

(strict convexity) for all
s
, the surfaces
\Sigma_{s}
are strictly convex with respect to the sound speed metric
c^{-2}dx^{2}
(
c=1
outside
\Omega
).
In other words, for every
s\in[\underline{s},\overline{s}]
there is
\kappa_{s}
(principal curvature) such that in boundary normal coordinates, where the metric takes the form
g_{\alpha\beta}(x^{\prime},x^{n})dx^{\alpha}dx^{\beta}+(dx^{n})^{2}
,

\partial_{x^{n}}g^{ij}\xi_{i}\xi_{j}\geq\kappa_{s}|\xi|^{2}_{g},\quad\forall\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

More details about convexity of hypersurfaces can be found in Appendix
C
.

We denote by
\text{\rm dist}(x,y)
the distance function for the sound speed metric
c^{-2}dx^{2}
and by
\text{\rm dist}_{\nu}(x,y)
the length (in the sound speed metric) of the integral curve segment of
\nu
connecting
x
and

4
Stability

For a fixed compact subdomain
\mathcal{K}\subset\Omega
with smooth boundary, we consider initial conditions in

H^{1}_{0}(\mathcal{K}):=\{f\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):\text{\rm supp}(f)\subseteq\mathcal{K}\}.

The technical result upon which the main part of the stability theorem follows consists in showing that for

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}^{2}v-c^{2}\Delta v=0,\\
(v,\partial_{t}v)|_{t=0}=(v_{0},0),\end{array}\right.

(26)

the fractional derivative
\partial^{\alpha}_{t}v
depends continuously on
v_{0}
, in terms of its

-norm. This is the content of Theorem
4
below.
The stability of the inverse problem, stated in Theorem
5
, is then a consequence of the well-posedness of the fractional forward problem and a standard compactness-uniqueness argument.

Below, we denote by
C^{1-\alpha}([0,T];L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
the Banach space of all
(1-\alpha)-
Hölder continuous functions with respect to time, with values in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and equipped with the norm

\|v\|_{C^{1-\alpha}([0,T];L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}:=\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|v(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}+\sup_{t,\tau\in[0,T],t\neq\tau}\frac{\|v(\cdot,t)-v(\cdot,\tau)\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}}{|t-\tau|^{1-\alpha}}.

Theorem 4
.

Let

be the solution to (
26

has compact support in

. Moreover, for each
\delta\in(0,T)

\|\partial^{\alpha}_{t}v\|_{L^{1}((0,T);H^{1-\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}+\|\partial^{\alpha}_{t}v\|_{C((\delta,T);H^{1-\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}\leq C\|v_{0}\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},\quad\forall v_{0}\in H^{1}_{0}(\mathcal{K}).

Proof of Lemma

1. According to Minkowski’s integral inequality we obtain

\begin{aligned} \|{\cal{I}}_{1-\alpha}u(\cdot,t)\|_{L^{2}}&\leq\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int^{t}_{0}\frac{\|u(\cdot,s)\|_{L^{2}}}{(t-s)^{\alpha}}ds\\

\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\|{\cal{I}}_{1-\alpha}u(t)\|_{L^{2}}\leq\frac{T^{1-\alpha}}{\Gamma(2-\alpha)}\|u\|_{C^{0}(L^{2})}.

Similarly, noticing first that for

,

\displaystyle|{\cal{I}}_{1-\alpha}u(x,t)-{\cal{I}}_{1-\alpha}u(x,\tau)|

\displaystyle\leq\int^{t}_{\tau}\frac{(t-s)^{-\alpha}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}|u(x,s)|ds+\int^{\tau}_{0}\frac{(t-s)^{-\alpha}-(\tau-s)^{-\alpha}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}|u(x,s)|ds,

then, by Minkowski’s integral inequality and the triangle inequality, we get

\displaystyle\|{\cal{I}}_{1-\alpha}u(\cdot,t)-{\cal{I}}_{1-\alpha}u(\cdot,\tau)\|_{L^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\|u\|_{C^{0}(L^{2})}\left(\int^{t}_{\tau}\frac{(t-s)^{-\alpha}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}ds+\int^{\tau}_{0}\frac{|(t-s)^{-\alpha}-(\tau-s)^{-\alpha}|}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}ds\right),

and subsequently,

\displaystyle\|{\cal{I}}_{1-\alpha}u(\cdot,t)-{\cal{I}}_{1-\alpha}u(\cdot,\tau)\|_{L^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2\|u\|^{2}_{C^{0}(L^{2})}}{\Gamma(2-\alpha)^{2}}\left((t-\tau)^{2(1-\alpha)}+\left((t-\tau)^{(1-\alpha)}+\tau^{1-\alpha}-t^{1-\alpha}\right)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4(t-\tau)^{2(1-\alpha)}}{\Gamma(2-\alpha)^{2}}\|u\|^{2}_{C^{0}(L^{2})}.

Taking supremum with respect to
t,\tau\in[0,T]
, with
t\neq\tau
, we deduce there is a constant
C_{T,\alpha>0}
such that

\|{\cal{I}}_{1-\alpha}u\|_{C^{1-\alpha}(L^{2})}\leq C_{T,\alpha}\|u\|_{C^{0}(L^{2})}.

2. Applying Fubini to pass the Fourier Transform inside the fractional integral, and also Minkowski’s integral inequality and Youngs’s convolution inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle\|{\cal{I}}_{1-\alpha}u\|_{L^{1}(H^{r})}

\displaystyle=\int^{T}_{0}\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(1+|\xi|^{2})^{r}\left(\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int^{t}_{0}\frac{\hat{u}(\xi,s)}{(t-s)^{1-\alpha}}ds\right)^{2}d\xi\right)^{1/2}dt

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int^{T}_{0}\int^{t}_{0}\frac{\|u(\cdot,s)\|_{H^{r}}}{(t-s)^{\alpha}}dsdt

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\int^{t}_{0}\frac{(t-s)^{-\alpha}}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}ds\right)\|u\|_{L^{1}(H^{r})}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{T^{1-\alpha}}{\Gamma(2-\alpha)}\|u\|_{L^{1}(H^{r})}.

∎

Proof of Lemma

Given the initial data
v_{0}
in (
26
), it is well known that
v
can be represented
by a Fourier Integral Operator (FIO) acting on
v_{0}
, this is, up to an smooth function we can write

as a finite sum of Fourier integrals of the form

\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\int e^{i\phi(t,x,\xi)}A(t,x,\xi)\hat{v}_{0}(\xi)d\xi,

with
\phi
a phase function which is smooth and positively homogeneous of order one in the Fourier variable
\xi

a classical symbol of order zero, this is, it has an asymptotic expansion
\sum_{j\geq 0}A_{j}(t,x,\xi)
with each

\xi

[
44

15

Since we are interested in local regularity, we will work near some arbitrary
(t_{1},x_{1})\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
and microlocalize —i.e., we localize in phase-space— around a null-bicharacteristic for the symbol
p(t,x,\tau,\xi)=c^{2}(x)|\xi|^{2}-\tau^{2}
and passing through
(t_{1},x_{1},\tau_{1},\xi_{1})\in T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{n+1}

with a microlocalization of it, this is, we apply a zero-order pseudodifferential operator with symbol
\chi(x,\xi)

\xi

(x_{0},\xi_{0})\in T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{n}

(0,x_{0},\tau_{0},\xi_{0})\in p^{-1}(\{0\})

(t_{1},x_{1},\tau_{1},\xi_{1})

v_{0}
is contained in a conic neighborhood of
(x_{0},\xi_{0})\in T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then,

(0,x_{0};\tau_{0},\xi_{0})\in T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{n+1}

(t_{1},x_{1})

It is known that for
v_{0}\in H^{m}_{comp}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

-functions—

We denote by
v_{+}
and
v_{-}

are associated with the positive and negative sound speed in the sense they respectively solve the eikonal equations

\partial_{t}\phi_{\pm}=\pm c(x)|\partial_{x}\phi_{\pm}|.

In general, these equations can not be solved globally in the whole interval
[0,T]

[
44

. At

\phi_{\pm}=x\cdot\xi,\quad A_{0}^{\pm}=1/2,\quad A_{j}^{\pm}=0\quad\text{for}\quad j\geq 1.

\phi

t_{2}

The intervals where the eikonal equation is solvable form a finite covering of
[0,T]
and we complete it to a covering of all
\mathbb{R}
. Recalling Remark
2

In addition, we will perform the computations solely on the component

We are interested only in the leading order terms, or in other words, the most singular component of

\displaystyle=e^{ix\cdot\xi}\sum_{l=0}^{N-1}\lambda^{\alpha-l}\int e^{i\lambda t\theta}(i\theta)^{\alpha-1}(\chi_{2}(\theta)-1)\frac{B_{l}(0,\theta)}{i(\theta-\psi^{\prime}(0))}d\theta

(30)

\displaystyle+\lambda^{\alpha-N}\int\int^{0}_{-\infty}e^{i\lambda((t-s)\theta+\psi(s))}(i\theta)^{\alpha-1}(1-\chi_{2}(\theta))B_{N}(s,\theta)dsd\theta.

Let’s rewrite the previous in the following form

e^{-ix\cdot\xi}j_{1,1}(t,x,\xi)=\sum_{l=1}^{N-1}a_{l}(t,x,\xi)+S_{N}(t,x,\xi)

(31)

where

a_{l}(t,x,\xi)=|\xi|^{\alpha-l}\int e^{i|\xi|t\theta}(i\theta)^{\alpha-1}(\chi_{2}(\theta)-1)\frac{B_{l}(0,\theta,x,\xi)}{i(\theta-\psi^{\prime}(0,x,\xi))}d\theta

and

S_{N}(x,\xi)=|\xi|^{\alpha-N}\int\int^{0}_{-\infty}e^{i(|\xi|(t-s)\theta+\phi(s,x,\xi)-x\cdot\xi)}(i\theta)^{\alpha-1}(1-\chi_{2}(\theta))B_{N}(s,\theta,x,\xi)dsd\theta.

It is clear from (

) with

that, since the phase functions involved are homogeneous of order 1 in
\xi
, all the derivatives
\partial^{\beta}_{x}\partial^{\gamma}_{\xi}j_{1,1}(t,x,\xi)
are of tempered growth. Moreover, for any compact
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
there is a constant
C_{K,N}>0
such that

|S_{N}(t,x,\xi)|\leq C_{K,N}(1+|\xi|)^{\alpha-N},\quad\forall(t,x,\xi)\in[0,T]\times K\times\mathbb{R}^{n}.

To conclude that (
31
) is indeed an asymptotic sum of symbols (depending smoothly on
t
), we verify that
a_{l}\in S^{\alpha-l}
, for all
l\geq 0
(this is true after adding a cut-off supported away from
\xi=0
). This follows from the fact that
\psi

(x,\hat{\xi})
, with
\hat{\xi}=\xi/|\xi|
smooth away from the origin and homogeneous of order zero, and also because the functions
B_{l}
involve the symbol
A\in S^{0}
. For a complete statement of the property used here, we refer the reader, for instance, to
[
15
, §1]
.

We have shown that
e^{-ix\cdot\xi}j_{1,1}(t,\cdot,\cdot)\in S^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{x}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\xi})
depending smoothly on
t\geq 0
, and moreover, there is a
\Psi
DO of order
\alpha
, say
Q_{1}
, such that

\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n+1}}\int j_{\epsilon,1,1}(t,x,\xi)\hat{v}_{0}(\xi)d\xi=Q_{1}[v_{0}].

We now estimate
j_{\epsilon,1,2}
. Since the function
\chi_{2}(\theta)
is compactly supported it makes the integral without
\chi(\epsilon\lambda\theta)
to be absolutely convergent, thus, we can take the limit as
\epsilon\to 0
and work with

j_{1,2}:=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}j_{\epsilon,1,2}=i\lambda^{\alpha+1}\int\int^{0}_{-\infty}e^{i\lambda((t-s)\theta+\psi(s))}B_{0}(s)(i\theta)^{\alpha-1}\chi_{2}(\theta)dsd\theta.

For
s<0<t
, we use

e^{i\lambda((t-s)\theta+\psi)}=\frac{1}{i\lambda(t-s)}\partial_{\theta}\left(e^{i\lambda((t-s)\theta+\psi)}\right)

and integrate by parts once to get

j_{1,2}=-i\lambda^{\alpha}\int\int^{0}_{-\infty}e^{i\lambda((t-s)\theta+\psi(s))}\frac{B_{0}(s)}{i(t-s)}\partial_{\theta}\left((i\theta)^{\alpha-1}\chi_{2}(\theta)\right)dsd\theta.

Let’s denote
g(\theta)=\partial_{\theta}\left((i\theta)^{\alpha-1}\chi_{2}(\theta)\right)
. By using (
28

with

We notice that the previous integrals are absolutely convergent and integrate over compact regions. Since

and
\Psi(t,s,x,\xi)

\xi

d_{1,2}(t,\cdot,\cdot)\in S^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{x}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\xi})
and depends smoothly on
t>0
, with an integrable singularity in
t=0
.

Similar to what was previously done, in the limit as
\epsilon\to 0
, the integral

j_{\epsilon,2}=i\lambda^{\alpha}\int\int^{0}_{-\infty}e^{i\lambda((t-s)\theta+\psi(s))}(i\theta)^{\alpha-1}C_{0}(s)\chi(\epsilon\lambda\theta)dsd\theta

can be expressed as

e^{ix\cdot\xi}d_{2,1}(t,x,\xi)+e^{i\phi(t,x,\xi)}d_{2,2}(t,x,\xi)

for symbols
d_{2,1},d_{2,2}\in S^{\alpha-1}
, the first one smooth for
t\geq 0
, while the second one smooth for
t>0
and with an integrable singularity at
t=0
.

We conclude that

\mathcal{J}_{2}(0)=\lim_{\epsilon\to 0}\mathcal{J}_{2}(\epsilon)=Q[v_{0}](t,x)+\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n+1}}\int e^{i\phi(t,x,\xi)}\gamma_{2}(t,x,\xi)\hat{v}_{0}(\xi)d\xi

for some
\Psi
DO
Q
of order
\alpha
and
\gamma_{2}(t,\cdot,\cdot)\in S^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n}_{x}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}_{\xi})
depending smoothly for all

and with an integrable singularity at

. Moreover, for
T>0
such that
\text{\rm supp}(h_{0})\subset[-T,T]
, there is some constant
C>0
for which the next inequality holds,

\|\mathcal{J}_{2}(0)\|_{L^{1}([0,T];H^{m-\alpha}(\Omega))}\leq C\|v_{0}\|_{H^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\left(1+\int^{T}_{0}\int^{0}_{-T}\frac{1}{(t-s)}ds\right).

with last integral bounded because

\int^{T}_{0}\int^{0}_{-T}\frac{1}{(t-s)}dsdt=\int^{T}_{0}\ln\left(1+\frac{T}{t}\right)dt=T\int^{\infty}_{2}\frac{\ln(z)}{(z-1)^{2}}dz<\infty.

Notice the second equality above is obtained after the change of variables

.

4.
Summarizing all the previous computations, we have proven that there are symbols

only depending smoothly on

, such that, for any

,

(\partial^{\alpha}_{t}v)_{j}=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\int e^{i\phi(t,x,\xi)}\beta_{j}(t,x,\xi)\hat{v}_{0}(\xi)d\xi\quad\text{for}\quad j\geq 1,

and

\displaystyle(\partial^{\alpha}_{t}v)_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\int e^{i\phi(t,x,\xi)}\beta_{0}(t,x,\xi)\hat{v}_{0}(\xi)d\xi

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\int e^{ix\cdot\xi}q(t,x,\xi)\hat{v}_{0}(\xi)d\xi.

The mapping properties of FIO’s and
\Psi

and

,

. We also deduce from Remark

\|(\partial^{\alpha}_{t}v)_{0}\|_{L^{1}([0,T];H^{m-\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}+\sum_{j=1}^{N_{T}}\|(\partial^{\alpha}_{t}v)_{j}\|_{C([0,T];H^{m-\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))}\leq C_{T}\|v_{0}\|_{H^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

∎

The next lemma gives microlocal stability for the inverse problem, since the unattenuated observation map is known to be stable (under appropriate geometric conditions). The stability property will then follow by requiring the injectivity of the (attenuated) observation map.

Lemma 9
.

Let
\Lambda_{0}:H^{1}_{0}(\mathcal{K})\to H^{1}((0,T)\times\Gamma)
be the measurement operator for the unattenuated case (i.e.,
a\equiv 0
). Then, there is a constant
C>0
such that

\|\Lambda_{0}u_{0}\|_{H^{1}((0,T)\times\Gamma)}\leq C\left(\|\Lambda_{\alpha}u_{0}\|_{H^{1}((0,T)\times\Gamma)}+\|u_{0}\|_{H^{\alpha}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\right),\quad\forall u_{0}\in H^{1}_{0}(\mathcal{K}).

Proof.

Let
u
be the solution to (
1
) and
v
to (
26
) with initial condition
v_{0}=u_{0}
. We see that
w=v-u
satisfies

\square_{c,\alpha}w=a\partial^{\alpha}_{t}v,\quad(w,\partial_{t}w)|_{t=0}=(0,\frac{a}{\Gamma(2-\alpha)}u_{0}),

(32)

therefore, the well-posedness of the forward problem in Theorem
2
gives us the inequality

\|(w,\partial_{t}w)\|_{L^{\infty}([0,T];\mathcal{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega))}\leq C\left(\|u_{0}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|a\partial^{\alpha}_{t}v\|_{L^{1}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega)))}\right),

but because
a(x)
is compactly supported within
\Omega
,
we can replace the
L^{1}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))
-norm by the norm in
L^{1}([0,T];L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
.
From Theorem
4
, we then deduce the inequality

\|(w,\partial_{t}w)\|_{L^{\infty}([0,T];\mathcal{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega))}\leq C\|u_{0}\|_{H^{\alpha}(\Omega)}.

(33)

We now focus on the trace of the error function
w
, in particular, in determining the regularity of
Rw=w|_{(0,T)\times\partial\Omega}
where we denote by
R
the boundary trace operator that is well defined since
w\in H^{1}(\Omega)
.

Let’s recall the fixed point formulation of equation
\square_{c,\alpha}w=a\partial^{\alpha}_{t}v
from section
2.4
,

\displaystyle w(t)=

\displaystyle U_{1}(t)[0,\frac{a}{\Gamma(2-\alpha)}u_{0}]+\int^{t}_{0}U_{1}(t-s)[0,a\partial^{\alpha}_{t}v(s)]ds

\displaystyle+\int^{t}_{0}\int^{s}_{0}U_{1}(t-s)Q(s-r)(w(r),\partial_{r}w(r))^{t}drds,

where
U_{1}=\Pi_{1}\text{\bf U}
, with
\Pi_{1}
the projection to the first component and
U
the semigroup associated with the (unattenuated) wave equation, i.e. the initial source-to-solution map. The integral above is understood in the Bochner integral sense (more details of this can be found in Appendix
B
).

We apply the trace operators and obtain

\displaystyle Rw(t)=

\displaystyle RU_{1}(t)[0,\frac{a}{\Gamma(2-\alpha)}u_{0}]+\int^{t}_{0}RU_{1}(t-s)[0,a\partial^{\alpha}_{t}v(s)]ds

(34)

\displaystyle+\int^{t}_{0}RU_{1}(t-s)\int^{s}_{0}Q(s-r)(w(r),\partial_{r}w(r))^{t}drds,

where we notice that, due to finite propagation speed, there is
\delta>0
for which
RU_{1}(t)[f,g]=0
for all
t\in(0,\delta)
and any pair of initial conditions in
H^{1}_{0}(\mathcal{K})\times L^{2}(\mathcal{K})
.

Following computations from Appendix
B
, the
H^{1}((0,T)\times\partial\Omega)
-norms of the integral terms are bounded by

\displaystyle\|F_{1}\|_{L^{1}((0,T);L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|F_{1}\|_{L^{\infty}((\delta,T);L^{2}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle+\|F_{2}\|_{L^{1}((0,T);L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|F_{2}\|_{L^{\infty}((\delta,T);L^{2}(\Omega))}

with
F_{1}(s)=a\partial^{\alpha}_{t}v(s)
and

F_{2}(s)=\int^{s}_{0}Q(s-r)(w(r),\partial_{r}w(r))^{t}dr,

and further bounded from above by a positive constant times

\displaystyle\|\partial^{\alpha}_{t}v\|_{L^{1}((0,T);L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|\partial^{\alpha}_{t}v\|_{L^{\infty}((\delta,T);L^{2}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle+\|\partial_{t}w\|_{L^{1}((0,T);L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|\partial_{t}w\|_{L^{\infty}((\delta,T);L^{2}(\Omega))}.

By recalling the estimate from Theorem
4
and (
33
), we obtain a final upper bound of a positive constant times
\|u_{0}\|_{H^{\alpha}(\Omega)}
.
In other words, by denoting
Ku_{0}=Rw|_{(0,T)\times\Gamma}

, for any
u_{0}\in H^{1}_{0}(\mathcal{K})
we have

\displaystyle\|Ku_{0}\|_{H^{1}((0,T)\times\Gamma)}\leq C\|u_{0}\|_{H^{\alpha}(\Omega)}.

The proof is then complete by noticing that
\Lambda_{\alpha}u_{0}=Ru
,
\Lambda_{0}u_{0}=Rv
and
v=u+w
, hence
Ku_{0}=\Lambda_{0}u_{0}-\Lambda_{\alpha}u_{0}
.
∎

Definition 5
.

For a compact
\mathcal{K}\subset\Omega
and
\Gamma\subset\partial\Omega
relatively open. We say the pair
(\mathcal{K},\Gamma)
satisfies the visibility condition of singularities if all unit speed geodesics issued from
\mathcal{K}
reach
\Gamma
non-tangentially and in finite time.
We also define
T_{1}(\mathcal{K},\Gamma)
as the maximum of the exit times of unit speed geodesic issued from
\mathcal{K}
and exiting
\Omega
through
\Gamma
.

Remark 9
.

In the case of complete data, i.e.
\Gamma=\partial\Omega
, we might replace the previous condition with the manifold
(\Omega,c^{-2}dx^{2})
being non-trapping and
\partial\Omega
strictly convex. In this case, the observation time
T
has to be larger than
T_{1}(\Omega)
defined as half the length of the longest geodesic segment contained in
\Omega
. We also take initial conditions in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
.

Theorem 5
.

Under the visibility condition of singularities and the foliation condition of theorem
3
, if
T>T_{1}(\mathcal{K},\Gamma)
, then there is
C>0
such that

\|u_{0}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq C\|\Lambda_{\alpha}u_{0}\|_{H^{1}((0,T)\times\Gamma)},\quad\forall u_{0}\in H^{1}_{0}(\mathcal{K}).

Proof.

We start from the result of lemma
9
,

\|\Lambda_{0}u_{0}\|_{H^{1}((0,T)\times\Gamma)}\leq C\left(\|\Lambda_{\alpha}u_{0}\|_{H^{1}((0,T)\times\Gamma)}+\|u_{0}\|_{H^{\alpha}(\Omega)}\right).

The visibility condition implies stability for the unattenuated inverse problem, thus
\|u_{0}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq C\|\Lambda_{0}u_{0}\|_{H^{1}((0,T)\times\Gamma)}
for some constant
C>0
, which gives

\|u_{0}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq C\left(\|\Lambda_{\alpha}u_{0}\|_{H^{1}((0,T)\times\Gamma)}+\|u_{0}\|_{H^{\alpha}(\Omega)}\right).

for a possibly different constant
C>0
.

Finally, the foliation condition implies the injectivity of
\Lambda_{\alpha}
(as stated in Theorem
3
), and added to the compactness of the injection
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\to H^{\alpha}_{0}(\Omega)
, a standard compactness-uniqueness argument leads to the desired inequality.
∎

5
Reconstruction

We adapt the dissipative sharp time reversal approach introduced by the authors in
[
3
]
to the setting of fractional memory terms. Let’s begin by defining the time-reversed system and operator based on the following observation.

Lemma 10
.

For any pair of functions
f,g
such that
\partial_{t}f,\partial_{t}g\in L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{2}(\Omega))
, then

\langle a\partial^{\alpha}_{t}f,c^{-2}\partial_{t}g\rangle=\langle c^{-2}\partial_{t}f,aI^{1-\alpha}_{T}\partial_{t}g\rangle

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the inner product in
L^{2}((0,T)\times\Omega)
and
I^{1-\alpha}_{T}
is a time-reversed fractional integral operator defined as

I^{1-\alpha}_{T}u(t):=\frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int^{T}_{t}\frac{u(s)}{(s-t)^{\alpha}}ds.

Proof.

Direct computations employing Fubini’s theorem give

\displaystyle\langle a\partial^{\alpha}_{t}f,c^{-2}\partial_{t}g\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\int^{T}_{0}c^{-2}\overline{g^{\prime}(x,t)}\left(\frac{a(x)}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int^{t}_{0}\frac{f^{\prime}(x,s)}{(t-s)^{\alpha}}ds\right)dtdx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\int^{T}_{0}\int^{T}_{s}\frac{a(x)c^{-2}\overline{g^{\prime}(x,t)}f^{\prime}(x,s)}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)(t-s)^{\alpha}}dtdsdx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\int^{T}_{0}c^{-2}f^{\prime}(x,s)\left(\frac{a(x)}{\Gamma(1-\alpha)}\int^{T}_{s}\frac{\overline{g^{\prime}(x,t)}}{(t-s)^{\alpha}}dt\right)dsdx.

∎

We define the Time-Reversal operator as the map
A_{\alpha}:h\mapsto(v,\partial_{t}v)|_{t=0}
, that takes the boundary data
h\in H^{1}((0,T)\times\partial\Omega)
to the restriction at time
t=0
of the solution
v
to the final boundary value system:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}^{2}v-c^{2}\Delta v-a(x)I^{1-\alpha}_{T}\partial_{t}v=0,\\
(v,\partial_{t}v)|_{t=T}=(\phi,0),\\
v|_{(0,T)\times\partial\Omega}=h.\end{array}\right.

Here
\phi
is the harmonic extension of
h(\cdot,T)
to
\Omega
.

For any
u_{0}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)

we take
h=\Lambda_{\alpha}u_{0}
. The error function
w=u-v
then solves

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\partial_{t}^{2}w-c^{2}\Delta w=-a(x)\partial^{\alpha}_{t}u-a(x)I^{1-\alpha}_{T}\partial_{t}v,\\
(\bar{u},\partial_{t}\bar{u})|_{t=T}=(u(T)-\phi,\partial_{t}u(T)),\\
w|_{(0,T)\times\partial\Omega}=0,\end{array}\right.

therefore, standard energy computations along with Lemma
10
for the real-valued functions
u,v
give

\displaystyle E_{\Omega}(w,T)-E_{\Omega}(w,0)

\displaystyle=-2\langle a\partial^{\alpha}_{t}u,c^{-2}\partial_{t}w\rangle-2\langle aI^{1-\alpha}_{T}\partial_{t}v,c^{-2}\partial_{t}w\rangle

\displaystyle=-2\langle a\partial^{\alpha}_{t}u,c^{-2}\partial_{t}u\rangle+2\langle a\partial^{\alpha}_{t}u,c^{-2}\partial_{t}v\rangle

\displaystyle-2\langle aI^{1-\alpha}_{T}\partial_{t}v,c^{-2}\partial_{t}u\rangle+2\langle aI^{1-\alpha}_{T}\partial_{t}v,c^{-2}\partial_{t}v\rangle

\displaystyle=-2\langle a\partial^{\alpha}_{t}u,c^{-2}\partial_{t}u\rangle+2\langle a\partial^{\alpha}_{t}v,c^{-2}\partial_{t}v\rangle.

Recalling the definition of the energy norm (
9
), we see that

\displaystyle E_{\Omega}(w,0)

\displaystyle=\|(w(0),\partial_{t}w(0))\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)}\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle E_{\Omega}(w,T)

\displaystyle=\|(u(T)-\phi,\partial_{t}u(T))\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)},

therefore, it follows from Lemma
3
and the identity

\|f-\phi\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}=\|f\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}-\|\phi\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)},\quad\forall f\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),

that

\displaystyle\|(w(0),\partial_{t}w(0))\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=E_{\Omega}(w,T)+2\langle a\partial^{\alpha}_{t}u,c^{-2}\partial_{t}u\rangle-2\langle a\partial^{\alpha}_{t}v,c^{-2}\partial_{t}v\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\|(u(T)-\phi,\partial_{t}u(T))\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)}+2\langle a\partial^{\alpha}_{t}u,c^{-2}\partial_{t}u\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\|(u(T),\partial_{t}u(T))\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)}+2\langle a\partial^{\alpha}_{t}u,c^{-2}\partial_{t}u\rangle

\displaystyle=E_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(u,T)-E_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega}(u,T)+2\langle a\partial^{\alpha}_{t}u,c^{-2}\partial_{t}u\rangle

\displaystyle=E_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(u,0)-E_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega}(u,T)

\displaystyle=\|(u_{0},-\textstyle\frac{a}{\Gamma(2-\alpha)}u_{0}))\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)}-E_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega}(u,T),

where we have used Theorem
1
(dissipation of energy). Then, by Poincare’s inequality we obtain

\|w(0)\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}\leq\left(1+\left(\textstyle\frac{C_{P}\|a\|_{C(\mathbb{R}^{n})}}{\Gamma(2-\alpha)}\right)^{2}\right)\|u_{0}\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}-E_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega}(u,T),

(35)

for some positive constant
C_{P}
depending on
\Omega
.
Defining the error operator

K:=\text{Id}-\Pi_{1}A_{\alpha}\Lambda_{\alpha},\quad Ku_{0}=w(0),

where
\Pi_{1}
stands for the projection to the first component, the reconstruction formula is a consequence of
K
being a contraction in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
. Assuming the damping coefficient is small enough, the previous statement will follow from the next observability-type inequality.

Lemma 11
.

Under the same hypothesis of the stability theorem and assuming
\Gamma=\partial\Omega
, there is a constant
C_{0}>0
such that for all
u_{0}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
,

\|u_{0}\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}\leq C_{0}E_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega}(u,T),

with
u
solution to (
1
). The constant
C_{0}>0
is uniform with respect to the damping coefficient
a(x)
in a small ball in
C_{c}(\Omega)
centered at zero.

Proof.

Let
v
be the solution to the unattenuated problem (
26
) with initial condition
(u_{0},0)
(do not confuse it with the function
v
above, solution to a time-reversed system). We know the exterior observability inequality holds for
v
as it was proven in
[
30
]
thus, by letting
w=v-u
, we get

\|u_{0}\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}\leq CE_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega}(v,T)\leq CE_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega}(u,T)+CE_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega}(w,T).

Recalling that
w
solves the system (
32
), we can recast it in integral form as

(w(t),\partial_{t}w(t))=\text{\bf V}(t)[0,-\textstyle\frac{au_{0}}{\Gamma(2-\alpha)},a\partial^{\alpha}_{t}v]

with
V
the solution operator for the inhomogeneous wave equation, with null Dirichlet conditions on a sufficiently large domain
\Omega^{\prime}\supset\Omega
(see (
11
)-(
14
)) depending on the final time
T>0
. It is clear that the map
a\mapsto(w(t),\partial_{t}w(t))
is continuous from
C(\mathbb{R}^{n})
to
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and moreover, by the inequalities proven in Theorem
4
we have

\|(w(T),\partial_{t}w(T))\|_{H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq C\|a\|_{C(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\|u_{0}\|_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}.

In consequence, we obtain the inequality

\|u_{0}\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}\leq CE_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega}(u,T)+C\|a\|^{2}_{C(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\|u_{0}\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}.

By restricting the damping coefficient to a ball in
C_{c}(\Omega)
centered at zero and of sufficiently small radius
\delta_{0}>0
, we can absorb the last term with the left-hand side and conclude that

\|u_{0}\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}\leq C_{0}E_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega}(u,T),\quad\forall u_{0}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),

for a constant
C_{0}>0
, uniform with respect to the damping coefficients within the ball.
∎

Bringing (
35
) and the previous lemma, we deduce there is
\delta_{0}>0
such that for any damping coefficient
a\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
with
\|a\|_{C(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\delta_{0}
, the next inequality holds

\|Ku_{0}\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)}\leq\left(1+\left(\textstyle\frac{C_{P}\|a\|_{C(\mathbb{R}^{n})}}{\Gamma(2-\alpha)}\right)^{2}-C_{0}^{-1}\right)\|u_{0}\|^{2}_{H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)},\quad\forall u_{0}\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),

with
C_{0}>0
the constant from Lemma
11
. It is clear then that by restricting the previous to damping coefficients
\|a\|_{C(\mathbb{R}^{n})}<\delta
, such that
C_{0}^{-1/2}>\frac{C_{P}\delta}{\Gamma(2-\alpha)}
, we get that
K
is a strict contraction in
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
. We have proven the following theorem.

Theorem 6
.

Let
(\Omega,c^{-2}dx)
be non-trapping and
\partial\Omega
strictly convex. Assume there is an admissible foliation
\Sigma(\Omega,\partial\Omega)
, and the observation time
T>0
is such that
T>\max\{T_{0}(\Omega,\Sigma),T_{1}(\Omega)\}
(see Remark
9
). Then, there is
\delta>0
so that for any damping coefficient
a\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
with
\|a\|_{C(\mathbb{R}^{n})}<\delta
,
the error operator
K=\text{\rm Id}-\Pi_{1}A_{\alpha}\Lambda_{\alpha}
is a strict contraction in
H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
and we have the following Neumann series reconstruction formula for the fractionally attenuated photoacoustic problem:

u_{0}=\sum^{\infty}_{m=0}K^{m}A_{\alpha}h,\quad h=\Lambda_{\alpha}u_{0},\quad\forall u_{0}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega).

Appendix A
Interpolation and Sobolev Spaces

We mainly follow
[
28
]
(Chapters 1.7, 1.9, 1.11 and 1.15) and
[
40
]
, but other useful references are
[
5
,
29
]
. With regard to intermediate derivatives, we follow
[
28
]
Chapter 1.2.

Real interpolation.
Let’s consider two separable Hilbert spaces
X\subset Y
with dense and continuous injection. The
K
-functional associated with the pair
(X,Y)
is defined as

K(\rho,u;X,Y):=\inf_{u_{0}+u_{1}=u}\left(\|u_{0}\|_{X}^{2}+\rho^{2}\|u_{1}\|_{Y}^{2}\right)^{1/2},\quad u_{0}\in X,\;u_{1}\in Y,

for every
u\in X+Y
and for all
\rho>0
. We define the interpolation space
[X,Y]_{\theta}
as

[X,Y]_{\theta}=\{u\;|\;u\in Y,\;\rho^{-(\theta+1/2)}K(\rho,u;X,Y)\in L^{2}(0,\infty)\}

with norm

u\mapsto\left(\|u\|_{X}^{2}+\int^{\infty}_{0}\rho^{-(2\theta+1)}K(\rho,u;X,Y)^{2}d\rho\right)^{1/2}.

(36)

Sobolev Spaces.
For any
s\in\mathbb{R}
, the Sobolev spaces
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
are defined via the Fourier transform as follows:

H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})=\{u\in\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):\|u\|^{2}_{H^{s}}:=\int(1+|\xi|^{2})^{s}|\hat{u}|^{2}d\xi<\infty\}

with
\mathcal{S}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
the Fréchet space of tempered distributions.

Given a bounded open subset
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with smooth boundary and
m
a positive integer, we define

H^{m}(\Omega)=\{u|_{\Omega}:\in H^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\}

with the norm

\||u|\|_{H^{m}(\Omega)}:=\inf\{\|U\|_{H^{m}}:u=U|_{\Omega},\;U\in H^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\}.

It can be characterized as the space of functions
u\in L^{2}(\Omega)
with derivatives
\partial^{\alpha}_{x}u\in L^{2}(\Omega)
for all multi-index
|\alpha|\leq m
, and with equivalent norm

\|u\|_{H^{m}(\Omega)}:=\left(\sum_{|\alpha|\leq m}\|\partial^{\alpha}_{x}u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\right)^{1/2}.

For any
s\in(0,1)
we define the fractional order Sobolev spaces
H^{s}(\Omega)
as the following interpolation space (for instance, following the K-method). Writing
H^{0}(\Omega)=L^{2}(\Omega)
, then

H^{s}(\Omega):=[H^{1}(\Omega),H^{0}(\Omega)]_{\theta},\quad\theta=1-s,

with its respective interpolation norms. They can be characterized as

H^{s}(\Omega)=\{u|_{\Omega}:u\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\}

with the equivalent norm

\||u|\|_{H^{s}(\Omega)}:=\inf\{\|U\|_{H^{s}}:u=U|_{\Omega},\;U\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\}.

We will also need the spaces

H^{1+s}(\Omega):=[H^{2}(\Omega),H^{0}(\Omega)]_{\frac{1-s}{2}}=[H^{2}(\Omega),H^{1}(\Omega)]_{1-s},\quad s\in(0,1).

The subspaces
H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
are defined as the completions of
C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega)
under the
H^{s}(\Omega)
-norms. For
0\leq s\leq 1/2
,
H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)=H^{s}(\Omega)
, while for
1/2<s\leq 1
,
H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)
is strictly contained in
H^{s}(\Omega)
and can be characterized as

H^{s}_{0}(\Omega)=\{u\in H^{s}(\Omega):u|_{\partial\Omega}=0\},\quad 1/2<s\leq 1.

It is also possible to obtain them as interpolation spaces, except for the case
s=1/2
. In fact,

[H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),L^{2}(\Omega)]_{1-s}=H^{s}_{0}(\Omega),\quad\text{for }s\in(0,1),\;s\neq 1/2,

with equivalent norms,
while for
s=1/2
one has

[H^{1}_{0}(\Omega),L^{2}(\Omega)]_{1/2}=:H^{1/2}_{00}(\Omega),

which is a strict subset of
H^{1/2}_{0}(\Omega)
with continuous inclusion.

For a bounded open set with smooth boundary,
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
T>0
, the space
H^{1}([0,T]\times\partial\Omega)
is defined as

\{u\in L^{2}([0,T];H^{1}(\partial\Omega)):\partial_{t}u\in L^{2}([0,T];L^{2}(\partial\Omega))\},

where the trace spaces
H^{s}(\partial\Omega)
for any
s\in\mathbb{R}
are defined via finite partitions of unity (due to the compactness of
\partial\Omega
).

Interpolation spaces of intermediate derivatives.
Set
A=c^{2}(x)\Delta
—with
c(x)
a smooth function bounded from below by a positive constant— endowed with null Dirichlet boundary conditions at
\partial\Omega
, thus, its domain is

D(A):=H^{2}(\Omega)\cap H^{1}_{0}(\Omega).

Using its spectral decomposition, we define for
\theta\in(0,1)
the operators
A^{\theta}f=\sum_{j\geq 0}\lambda_{j}^{\theta}(f,\phi_{j})_{L^{2}}
where
\{\lambda_{j}\}
corresponds to the spectrum of
A
, and
\{\phi_{j}\}
respective eigenfunctions forming an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\Omega)
. We will use the notation
D(A^{0}):=L^{2}(\Omega)
.

Remark 10
.

For two separable Hilbert spaces
X\subset Y
, with
X
dense and continuously injected in
Y
, there is always a self-adjoint and positive operator
A
in
Y
with domain
X
, hence
D(A^{0})=Y
and
D(A)=X
.

Instead of using the K-method, it is possible to alternatively define interpolation spaces of intermediate derivatives as

[D(A),D(A^{0})]_{\theta}:=D(A^{1-\theta}),

with
D(A^{1-\theta})
corresponding to the domain of
A^{1-\theta}
, this is,

D(A^{1-\theta}):=\{f\in L^{2}(\Omega):A^{1-\theta}f\in L^{2}(\Omega)\},

and they are endowed with the norm

f\mapsto\left(\|f\|^{2}_{D(A^{0})}+\|A^{1-\theta}f\|_{D(A^{0})}^{2}\right)^{1/2},

which turns out to be equivalent to the one defined in (
36
). In particular, we have that
[D(A),D(A^{0})]_{1/2}=D(A^{1/2})
is topologically equivalent to
H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
.

For evolution equations one might consider energy spaces of initial conditions given by

\mathcal{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega):=D(A^{1/2})\times D(A^{0})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{H}^{2}_{0}(\Omega):=D(A)\times D(A^{1/2}).

An application of the Reiteration Theorem (see
[
28
, Chapter 1, Theorem 6.1]
) gives, for
s\in(0,1)
, that their interpolation spaces are

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{1+s}_{0}(\Omega)

\displaystyle:=[\mathcal{H}^{2}_{0}(\Omega),\mathcal{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)]_{1-s}

\displaystyle=[D(A),D(A^{0})]_{(1-s)/2}\times[D(A),D(A^{0})]_{1-s/2}

\displaystyle=D(A^{(1+s)/2})\times D(A^{s/2}).

Recalling the definition of fractional Sobolev spaces as interpolation spaces, namely

H^{s}(\Omega)=[H^{2}(\Omega),H^{0}(\Omega)]_{\theta},\quad s=2(1-\theta),

because
D(A)
is continuously embedded in
H^{2}(\Omega)
and
D(A^{0})=H^{0}(\Omega)
, by the interpolation Theorem (see
[
28
, Chapter 1, Theorem 5.1]
) we find that
\mathcal{H}^{1+s}_{0}(\Omega)
is continuously embedded in
H^{1+s}(\Omega)\times H^{s}(\Omega)
.

Appendix B
Integration on Banach spaces

We present a brief introduction to the Bochner integral for vector-valued functions.
More details can be found in
[
8
]

Let
X
be an abstract Banach space and
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
a finite measure space. A vector-valued function
F:\Omega\to X
is said to be
(strongly)
\mu
-measurable
if there exists a sequence of
simple functions
, this is, functions of the form
f_{n}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\chi_{E_{i}}(t)u_{i}
, with
u_{i}\in X
,
E_{i}\in\Sigma
measurable subsets of
\Omega
, and where
\chi_{E_{i}}(t)
stands for the characteristic function in
E_{i}
, such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|f_{n}(t)-f(t)\|_{X}\ =0\quad\text{for almost every $t\in\Omega$.}

A
\mu
-measurable function
f:\Omega\to X
is said to be
Bochner integrable
if there exists a sequence of simple functions
(f_{n})
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}\|f_{n}-f\|_{X}d\mu=0,

in which case we define the integral of
f
over some
E\in\Sigma
as

\int_{E}fd\mu:=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{E}f_{n}d\mu,

where for
f_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\chi_{E_{i}}(t)u_{i}
, its integral is given by

\int_{\Omega}f_{n}d\mu=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mu(E_{i}\cap E)u_{i}.

We have the following characterization of integrability.

Theorem 7
.

Given a
\mu
-measurable function
f
, we have that
f
is Bochner integrable if and only if
\int_{\Omega}\|f\|_{X}d\mu<\infty
.

Some properties of the Bochner integral that will be useful for us are the following.

Theorem 8
.

If
f
is
\mu
-Bochner integrable, then

(i)

\lim_{\mu(E)\to 0}\int_{E}fd\mu=0;

(ii)

\left\|\int_{E}fd\mu\right\|_{X}\leq\int_{E}\left\|f\right\|_{X}d\mu
for all
E\in\Sigma
;

(iii)

if
(E_{n})
is a sequence of pairwise disjoint members of
\Sigma
and
E=\bigcup^{\infty}_{n=1}E_{n}
, then

\int_{E}fd\mu=\sum^{\infty}_{n=1}\int_{E_{n}}fd\mu,

where the sum on the right is absolutely convergent.

We denote by
\mathcal{B}(X)
the Banach space of bounded linear operators from
X
to itself. Take
R\in\mathcal{B}(X)
. By definition of integrability, it is clear that for
f:\Omega\to X
integrable,
Rf
is integrable as well and moreover

R\int_{E}fd\mu=\int_{E}Rfd\mu.

In fact, the same property holds for
R
a closed linear operator from
X
to some other Banach space
Y
, provided
Rf
is integrable. See
[
8
]
for a proof of this. A particularly useful example is the closed operator
\partial_{x}:H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)
.

For the sake of this paper, we are interested in
\Omega=[0,T]\subset\mathbb{R}
and
\mu
the Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{R}
. Let’s consider also a continuous one-parameter family linear operators
R(s):X\to X
for
s\in[0,T]
(e.g. the semigroup associated to the wave equation). By continuity of
R(t)
and the definition of measurability,
R(t)x
is
\mu
-measurable for any
x\in X
, and if
f:[0,T]\to X
is measurable then
R(t)f(t)
is as well.

To preserve integrability we need, for instance, uniform boundedness, this is, we assume there is
M>0
such that their operator norms satisfy
\|R(s)\|<M
for all
s
. Then,
R(t)f(t)
is also Bochner integrable (in fact, we only need
\|R(t)\|\|f\|_{X}
to be integrable in
[0,T]
).

Let
\text{\bf U}(t)
be a uniformly bounded strongly continuous semigroup in
X
(for instance, take the semigroup associated to the wave equation in a domain
\Omega
with null Dirichlet condition and
X=H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\times L^{2}(\Omega)
). For any
\text{\bf F}(t)\in L^{1}([0,T];X)
(i.e. Bochner integrable) the following integral is well defined for every
t\in[0,T]
and

t\mapsto w(t)=\int^{t}_{0}\text{\bf U}(t-s)\text{\bf F}(s)ds\in C([0,T];X).

Let’s consider a bounded subdomain
\omega
, strictly contained in
\Omega
and with smooth boundary
\partial\omega
. We call
R
the trace operator on
\partial\omega
which is known to be continuous from
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
to
H^{1/2}(\partial\omega)
.
Denoting by
U_{i}(t)=\pi_{i}\text{\bf U}(t)
,
i=1,2
, the projection to the first and second component respectively, and taking
\text{\bf F}(t)=[0,F(t)]
with
F(t)\in L^{1}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega))
and
\text{\rm supp}(F)\subset\omega
, we have for each
t\in[0,T]
,

Rw(t)=\int^{t}_{0}RU_{1}(t-s)\text{\bf F}(s)ds,

and moreover
Rw(t)\in C([0,T];H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega))
(we also used that the projection operator
\pi_{1}:X\to H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
is continuous). Via a Fourier integral representation of solutions to the wave equations, it can be proven that
RU_{1}(t-s)\text{\bf F}(s)\in H^{1}((0,T)\times\partial\omega)
. See appendix
A
for a definition of this space.

Let’s notice that for any
t\in(0,T)
and
h
sufficiently small,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{h}(Rw(t+h)-Rw(t))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{h}\int^{t+h}_{0}RU_{1}(t+h-s)\text{\bf F}(s)ds-\frac{1}{h}\int^{t}_{0}RU_{1}(t-s)\text{\bf F}(s)ds

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{h}\int^{t+h}_{t}RU_{1}(t-s)\text{\bf F}(s)ds+\int^{t}_{0}R\frac{1}{h}\big(U_{1}(t+h-s)\text{\bf F}(s)-U_{1}(t-s)\text{\bf F}(s)\big)ds.

Taking the limit as
h\to 0
, by
[
8
, Theorem 9 in §II.2]
, we obtain

\displaystyle\partial_{t}Rw(t)

\displaystyle=RU_{1}(0)\text{\bf F}(t)+\int^{t}_{0}RU_{2}(t-s)\text{\bf F}(s)ds

\displaystyle=\int^{t}_{0}RU_{2}(t-s)\text{\bf F}(s)ds,

where we used that
\text{\bf U}(0)=\text{Id}
and
\text{\rm supp}(F)=\omega_{0}\subset\omega
.

We next show that for some
\delta>0
and
M>0
, the map
F\mapsto Rw
is continuous from

\mathcal{L}^{1}_{\delta,M}:=L^{1}((0,T);L^{2}(\omega_{0}))\cap L^{\infty}((\delta,T);L^{2}(\omega_{0}))\to H^{1}((0,T)\times\partial\omega).

Instead of using Lebesgue integration in manifolds we follow a Bochner integral approach. Let’s introduce

g_{1}(t)=\int^{T}_{0}RU_{1}(t-s)\text{\bf F}(s)ds,

and notice that
s\mapsto RU_{1}(\cdot-s)\text{\bf F}(s)\in H^{1}((0,T)\times\partial\omega)
is Bochner integrable in
[0,T]
, hence,

\displaystyle\|g_{1}\|_{H^{1}((0,T)\times\partial\omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\int^{T}_{0}\|RU_{1}(\cdot-s)\text{\bf F}(s)\|_{H^{1}((0,T)\times\partial\omega)}ds

\displaystyle\leq C\int^{T}_{0}\|\text{\bf F}(s)\|_{H^{1}_{0}(\omega)\times L^{2}(\omega))}ds

\displaystyle=C\|\text{\bf F}\|_{L^{1}((0,T);H^{1}_{0}(\omega)\times L^{2}(\omega))}.

It remains to estimate

g_{2}(t)=\int^{T}_{t}RU_{1}(t-s)\text{\bf F}(s)ds.

since
Rw=g_{1}-g_{2}
. Let’s first notice that

\displaystyle\partial_{t}g_{2}(t)

\displaystyle=-RU_{1}(0)\text{\bf F}(t)+\int^{T}_{t}RU_{2}(t-s)\text{\bf F}(s)ds=\int^{T}_{t}RU_{2}(t-s)\text{\bf F}(s)ds.

Secondly, we see that thanks to the finite propagation speed property there is
\delta>0
such that
R\text{\bf U}(t-s)\text{\bf F}(s)=0
for
s\in[t,t+\delta]
, thus

g_{2}(t)=\int^{T}_{t+\delta}RU_{1}(t-s)\text{\bf F}(s)ds\quad\text{and}\quad\partial_{t}g_{2}(t)=\int^{T}_{t+\delta}RU_{2}(t-s)\text{\bf F}(s)ds.

We have

\displaystyle\|g_{2}\|^{2}_{H^{1}((0,T)\times\partial\omega)}

\displaystyle=\|g_{2}\|^{2}_{L^{2}((0,T)\times\partial\omega)}+\|\partial_{t}g_{2}\|^{2}_{L^{2}((0,T)\times\partial\omega)}+\|\partial_{x}g_{2}\|^{2}_{L^{2}((0,T)\times\partial\omega)},

thus, by Theorem
8
and Jensen’s inequality

\displaystyle\|g_{2}\|^{2}_{H^{1}((0,T)\times\partial\omega)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int^{T}_{0}\left(\int^{T}_{t+\delta}\|RU_{1}(t-s)\text{\bf F}(s)\|_{L^{2}(\partial\omega)}ds\right)^{2}dt

\displaystyle+\int^{T}_{0}\left(\int^{T}_{t+\delta}\|RU_{2}(t-s)\text{\bf F}(s)\|_{L^{2}(\partial\omega)}ds\right)^{2}dt

\displaystyle+\int^{T}_{0}\left(\int^{T}_{t+\delta}\|\partial_{x}RU_{1}(t-s)\text{\bf F}(s)\|_{L^{2}(\partial\omega)}ds\right)^{2}dt

\displaystyle\lesssim\int^{T}_{0}\int^{T}_{t+\delta}\|RU_{1}(t-s)\text{\bf F}(s)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\partial\omega)}dsdt

\displaystyle+\int^{T}_{0}\int^{T}_{t+\delta}\|RU_{2}(t-s)\text{\bf F}(s)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\partial\omega)}dsdt

\displaystyle+\int^{T}_{0}\int^{T}_{t+\delta}\|\partial_{x}RU_{1}(t-s)\text{\bf F}(s)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\partial\omega)}dsdt

By Fubini’s theorem and then the change of variables
r=s-t
, we get

\displaystyle\|g_{2}\|^{2}_{H^{1}((0,T)\times\partial\omega)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int^{T}_{\delta}\int^{s-\delta}_{0}\|RU_{1}(t-s)\text{\bf F}(s)\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\partial\omega)}dtds

\displaystyle+\int^{T}_{\delta}\int^{s-\delta}_{0}\|RU_{2}(t-s)\text{\bf F}(s)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\partial\omega)}dtds

\displaystyle\lesssim\int^{T}_{\delta}\int^{s}_{\delta}\|RU_{1}(-r)\text{\bf F}(s)\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\partial\omega)}drds

\displaystyle+\int^{T}_{\delta}\int^{s}_{\delta}\|RU_{2}(-r)\text{\bf F}(s)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\partial\omega)}drds

\displaystyle\lesssim\int^{T}_{\delta}\int^{T}_{0}\|RU_{1}(-r)\text{\bf F}(s)\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\partial\omega)}drds

\displaystyle+\int^{T}_{\delta}\int^{T}_{0}\|RU_{2}(-r)\text{\bf F}(s)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\partial\omega)}drds.

We now use the time-symmetry of the (unattenuated) wave semigroup for initial conditions of the form
[0,F]
, namely

U_{1}(-t)[0,F]=-U_{1}(t)[0,F]\quad\text{and}\quad U_{2}(-t)[0,F]=U_{2}(t)[0,F],

which implies that

\displaystyle\|g_{2}\|^{2}_{H^{1}((0,T)\times\partial\omega)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\int^{T}_{\delta}\int^{T}_{0}\|RU_{1}(r)\text{\bf F}(s)\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\partial\omega)}drds

\displaystyle+\int^{T}_{\delta}\int^{T}_{0}\|RU_{2}(r)\text{\bf F}(s)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\partial\omega)}drds

\displaystyle=\int^{T}_{\delta}\|RU_{1}(\cdot)\text{\bf F}(s)\|^{2}_{H^{1}((0,T)\times\partial\omega)}ds

\displaystyle\lesssim\|\text{\bf F}\|^{2}_{L^{2}((\delta,T);H^{1}_{0}(\omega)\times L^{2}(\omega))}.

We have proven the inequality

\|Rw\|_{H^{1}((0,T)\times\partial\omega)}\lesssim\|\text{\bf F}\|_{L^{1}((0,T);H^{1}_{0}(\omega)\times L^{2}(\omega))}+\|\text{\bf F}\|_{L^{2}((\delta,T);H^{1}_{0}(\omega)\times L^{2}(\omega))}.

In the particular case of
\text{\bf F}=(0,F)
with
F\in\mathcal{L}^{1}_{\delta}
, then

\|g_{1}\|_{H^{1}((0,T)\times\partial\omega)}\lesssim\|F\|_{L^{1}((0,T);L^{2}(\omega))}

and

\|g_{2}\|^{2}_{H^{1}((0,T)\times\partial\omega)}\lesssim C\|F\|_{L^{2}((\delta,T);L^{2}(\omega))}\lesssim C\|F\|_{L^{\infty}((\delta,T);L^{2}(\omega))}\|F\|_{L^{1}((0,T);L^{2}(\omega))},

for a constant depending on the parameter
M
. Consequently, for some other constant
C_{\delta}>0
depending on
\delta
and
M
,

\displaystyle\|Rw\|_{H^{1}((0,T)\times\partial\omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\|g_{1}\|_{H^{1}((0,T)\times\partial\omega)}+\|g_{2}\|_{H^{1}((0,T)\times\partial\omega)}

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\|F\|^{1/2}_{L^{1}((0,T);L^{2}(\omega))}+\|F\|^{1/2}_{L^{\infty}((\delta,T);L^{2}(\omega))}\right)\|F\|^{1/2}_{L^{1}((0,T);L^{2}(\omega))}

\displaystyle\lesssim\|F\|_{L^{1}((0,T);L^{2}(\omega))}+\|F\|_{L^{\infty}((\delta,T);L^{2}(\omega))}.

One can take
\delta=0
if we apriori know that
F\in L^{\infty}((0,T);L^{2}(\omega))
.

Appendix C
Strict convexity of Riemannian hypersurfaces

We give a brief summary of relevant ideas concerning the strict convexity of Riemannian hypersurfaces, and provide a derivation of the convexity condition in terms of cotangent vectors. For more details, we refer the reader to the book
[
25
]
.

Let us recall that a Riemannian hypersurface
(M,g)\subset(\widetilde{M},\widetilde{g})
—with the latter the ambient manifold of dimension
n
— is strictly convex with respect to a unit normal vector field
N
along
M
, if its scalar second fundamental form satisfies
h(X,X)<0
, for all
X\in TM
.

The scalar second fundamental form
h
is the covariant symmetric 2-tensor field defined by

h(X,Y)=\langle\Pi(X,Y),N\rangle

for all smooth vector fields
X,Y\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
, where
\Pi(X,Y)
stands for the second fundamental form of
M
. Equivalently we might write

\Pi(X,Y)=h(X,Y)N.

By homogeneity of
h
, we have
h(X,X)=|X|_{g}^{2}h(\hat{X},\hat{X})
with
\hat{X}=X/|X|_{g}\in SM
, the sphere bundle of
M
, where
-h(\hat{X},\hat{X})
reaches a minimum. Consequently, the condition
h(X,X)<0
is equivalent to the existence of
\kappa>0
such that
-h(X,X)\geq\kappa|X|_{g}
.

Denoting by
\widetilde{\nabla}
the Levi-Civita connection of
\widetilde{M}
, we have that

\Pi(X,Y)=\left(\widetilde{\nabla}_{X}Y\right)^{\perp},

where
⟂
stands for the normal projection of
T\widetilde{M}|_{M}
onto
NM
, the normal bundle of
M
, and on the right hand side
X,Y
represent any extension of
X,Y\in\mathfrak{X}(M)
to an open set of
\widetilde{M}
. We recall that the local representation of
\widetilde{\nabla}_{X}Y
is

\widetilde{\nabla}_{X}Y=(X(Y^{k})+X^{i}Y^{j}\Gamma^{k}_{ij})E_{k}

for a given smooth local frame
(E_{i})
. The connection coefficients
\Gamma^{k}_{ij}
are the Christoffel symbols of the metric
\widetilde{g}
.

Let’s assume now that
M
is orientable. In boundary normal coordinates on
M
, this is, in a collar neighborhood of
M
(in
\widetilde{M}
) we write
x=(x^{\prime},x^{n})
with
x^{\prime}=(x^{1},\dots,x^{n-1})
local coordinates on
M
and
x^{n}
representing the signed distance to
M
, the ambient metric
\widetilde{g}
takes the form

g_{\alpha,\beta}dx^{\alpha}dx^{\beta}+(dx^{n})^{2}

with
1\leq\alpha,\beta\leq n-1
. We take
N=(0,\dots,0,-1)
as the exterior unit normal vector field, thus, for any smooth vector field
X
in
M
(understood as
X=X^{\alpha}E_{\alpha}+0\cdot E_{n}
in
T\widetilde{M}
),

\Pi(X,X)=h(X,X)N=X^{\alpha}X^{\beta}\Gamma^{n}_{\alpha\beta}E_{n},

where in our coordinates
E_{n}=-N
. Then,
h(X,X)=-X^{\alpha}X^{\beta}\Gamma^{n}_{\alpha\beta}
.
According to our choice of normal vector field,
M
is strictly convex if there is
\kappa>0
such that

X^{\alpha}X^{\beta}\Gamma^{n}_{\alpha\beta}\geq\kappa|X|^{2}_{g}.

In boundary normal coordinates the Christoffel symbols satisfy
\Gamma^{n}_{\alpha,\beta}=-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{n}g_{\alpha\beta}
, therefore, the previous conditions rewrites as

-\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{n}g_{\alpha\beta})X^{\alpha}X^{\beta}\geq\kappa|X|^{2}_{g}.

In terms of covectors and the inverse of the metric,
\tilde{g}^{-1}=g^{\alpha,\beta}dx^{\alpha}dx^{\beta}+(dx^{n})^{2}
, we obtain the equivalent condition

-\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{n}g^{\alpha\beta}(x))\xi_{\alpha}\xi_{\beta}\geq\kappa|\xi|^{2}_{g},\quad\forall(x,\xi)\in T^{*}M.

Indeed, let’s recall the natural isomorphism between tangent and cotangent bundles,
X^{\alpha}=g^{\alpha\beta}\xi_{\beta}
. On one hand, we have that
|X|^{2}_{g}=g^{\alpha\beta}\xi_{\alpha}\xi_{\beta}=|\xi|^{2}_{g}
, and on the other,

\displaystyle\Pi(X,X)

\displaystyle=\Pi(g^{-1}\xi,g^{-1}\xi)

\displaystyle=g^{\alpha\gamma^{\prime}}\xi_{\gamma^{\prime}}g^{\beta\gamma}\xi_{\gamma}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{n}g_{\alpha\beta}\right).

By differentiating the relation
g_{\alpha\beta}g^{\beta\gamma}=\delta_{\alpha\gamma}
we get

(\partial_{n}g_{\alpha\beta})g^{\beta\gamma}=-g_{\alpha\beta}(\partial_{n}g^{\beta\gamma}),

from where it follows

\displaystyle\Pi(X,X)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}g^{\alpha\gamma^{\prime}}\xi_{\gamma^{\prime}}\left(-g_{\alpha\beta}(\partial_{n}g^{\beta\gamma})\right)\xi_{\gamma}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(g^{\alpha\gamma^{\prime}}g_{\alpha\beta}(\partial_{n}g^{\beta\gamma})\right)\xi_{\gamma}\xi_{\gamma^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{n}g^{\gamma^{\prime}\gamma})\xi_{\gamma}\xi_{\gamma^{\prime}}.

Appendix D
Symbol classes, FIO’s and
\Psi
DO’s

For any
m\in\mathbb{R}
, and
n\geq 1
integer,
we define the
symbols class

S^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
as the set of all smooth functions
a(x,\xi)
satisfying that for any compact
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and any multi-indices
\alpha,\beta
, there is a constant
C_{K,\alpha,\beta}>0
such that

|\partial^{\alpha}_{x}\partial^{\beta}_{\xi}a(x,\xi)|\leq C_{K,\alpha,\beta}(1+|\xi|)^{m-|\beta|},\quad\forall(x,\xi)\in K\times\mathbb{R}^{n}.

We say that
a(x,\xi)
is a
symbol of order
m
. The set
S^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
becomes a Frechét space when augmented with the seminorms corresponding to the best constants in the inequalities above.

We say that the symbol
a(x,\xi)
is
classical
if it has an asymptotic expansion of the form

a(x,\xi)\sim\sum^{\infty}_{j=0}a_{m-j}(x,\xi),

where
a_{m-j}
are smooth and positively homogeneous in
\xi
of order
m-j
for
|\xi|>1
(i.e.,
a(x,\lambda\xi)=\lambda^{m}a(x,\xi)
for
\lambda>0
), thus elements of
S^{m-j}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
; the sign
\sim
means that

a(x,\xi)-\sum^{M}_{j=0}a_{m-j}(x,\xi)\in S^{m-M-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),\quad\forall M\geq 0.

A real-valued function
\phi(x,\xi)\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\dot{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
(with
\dot{\mathbb{R}}^{n}:=\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\{0\}
) is called a phase function if it is positively homogeneous in
\xi
of order 1, and
(\partial_{x}\phi,\partial_{\xi}\phi)\neq 0
for all
x,\xi
. An operator
A
defined by

Af(x)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\int e^{i\phi(x,\xi)}a(x,\xi)\hat{f}(\xi)d\xi,\quad\forall f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

with
a(x,\xi)
a symbol of order
m
, is said to be a Fourier Integral Operator of order
m
. If the symbol is classical, we call
a_{m}(x,\xi)
the principal symbol of the operator. In the particular case of
\phi(x,\xi)=x\cdot\xi
we say that
A
(or equivalently
a(x,D)
) is a Pseudodifferential Operator (
\Psi
DO) of order
m
.

By interpreting the previous oscillatory integral in the sense of distributions, an FIO extends to a continuous map from
\mathcal{E}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
to
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
—the spaces of compactly supported distributions and distributions, respectively. If in addition, the phase function is nondegenerate and the FIO is associated to a local canonical graph, it continuously maps
H^{s}_{comp}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
to
H^{s-m}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. This is, for instance, the case of all
\Psi
DO’s. For definitions of nondegeneracy and local cannonical graph we refer the reader to
[
44
]
.
