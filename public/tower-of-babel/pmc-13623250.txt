Magnetic seizure therapy for severe depressive episodes: An open-label case series
Authors: Aromal Shibu, Harsh Pathak, Vanteemar S. Sreeraj, Urvakhsh Meherwan Mehta, Preeti Sinha, Shyam Sundar Arumugham, Jagadisha Thirthalli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Magnetic seizure therapy (MST) is a potential alternative to electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) with a favorable cognitive adverse effect profile.Cases:We present the first report of MST administration in India on six patients with severe depressive episodes referred for convulsive therapy. MST was administered thrice weekly under general anesthesia. Four of six patients achieved remission following MST (mean 8.5 sessions per patient, range 7–13). No clinically meaningful cognitive decline occurred; reorientation was consistently rapid with mild self-limiting adverse effects.Conclusion:MST was feasible and safe within the existing ECT infrastructure, with meaningful clinical effectiveness, and preserved cognitive function.

INTRODUCTION

Depression is a major contributor to the global burden of disease, resulting in substantial morbidity alongside significant loss of functioning and productivity.[1] A substantial proportion of patients do not respond to first-line treatments, including antidepressants and psychotherapies.[2] Although electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) remains the most effective biological intervention with remission rates of 50–75%,[3] its use is limited by cognitive adverse effects (AEs).[34] The high resistance of the scalp and skull necessitates diffuse electrical stimulation to induce a therapeutic seizure, contributing to cognitive impairment.[15]

Magnetic seizure therapy (MST) was developed as a potential cognitive-sparing alternative to ECT [Table 1]. MST uses high-intensity transcranial magnetic stimulation to focally stimulate superficial cortical regions to induce therapeutic seizures. The relative sparing of deeper temporal structures may preserve memory functions while maintaining antidepressant efficacy.[467]

The administration of MST mirrors that of ECT, requiring general anesthesia and a muscle relaxant. MST utilizes biphasic pulses delivered at frequencies ranging from 25 to 100 Hz. Magnetic stimulation is applied via a twin or circular coil centered over the vertex or the frontal cortex.[1]

The evidence on MST’s efficacy has grown through several key trials over the last two decades. Lisanby et al.[7] first demonstrated the feasibility of MST in a randomized within-subject comparison trial with 10 patients. Following this, other randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have shown comparable efficacy of MST and ECT with better cognitive AE profiles.[689] Importantly, Deng et al.[6] found that MST required approximately 2.3 additional sessions compared to ECT. Most recently, the CREST-MST trial,[10] involving 239 participants with depression, demonstrated that MST had noninferior efficacy compared to RUL ultrabrief ECT while exhibiting a significantly more favorable cognitive safety profile.

Reorientation following MST is consistently faster, typically occurring within 2–8 minutes, compared to 8–26 minutes after ECT.[3] Studies have demonstrated the preservation of autobiographical memory following MST.[69] Common noncognitive AEs are mild and self-limiting, including headache, nausea, and myalgia.[6711]

There are currently no reports on MST from India. We present the first case series evaluating the feasibility, effectiveness, and safety of MST in an Indian setup.

METHODS

Six patients with severe depressive episodes received MST at our tertiary care center in South India between September 2025 and April 2026. Patients with a clinical indication for convulsive therapy were considered for MST when minimization of cognitive AEs was a priority. MST was initiated after obtaining informed consent. Patients with inadequate response were offered ECT.

MST was administered thrice weekly using the Magpro XP MST Device with a bifrontal twin coil (Magventure, Denmark) at 100% machine output and 100 Hz frequency. Stimulation was initiated at 200 pulses; pulse count was titrated upward in multiples of 200 based on seizure quality and adequacy. Seizure threshold was estimated, and the treatment dose was delivered at up to twice the estimated threshold, with a maximum of 1,200 pulses per session. Anesthesia was induced with either thiopentone (1.5–3 mg/kg IV) or etomidate (0.2–0.3 mg/kg IV), selected based on anticipated seizure threshold, with succinylcholine (0.5–1 mg/kg IV) as the muscle relaxant. EEG was acquired for monitoring seizure quality in five patients (EEG was not feasible for one patient). Acute postictal reorientation time was recorded after each session as part of the routine clinical recovery assessment. Depressive symptom severity was assessed using the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAM-D). Response was defined as ≥50% reduction in HAM-D from baseline, and remission as HAM-D ≤7. Suicidality was assessed clinically at baseline and during treatment; no standardized suicidality scale was administered. Cognitive side effects were monitored using the Battery for ECT-Related Cognitive Deficits (B4ECT ReCoDe)[11] after every third session. Noncognitive AEs were monitored after each session using the Short-term Adverse Effects of electroconvulsive therapy (SAVE) checklist[12] as there are no MST-specific adverse effect monitoring tools. Further, the noncognitive AEs of ECT are often attributed to anesthetic procedures and seizures,[13] which are common to both MST and ECT.

Cases

Six patients (5 men, 1 woman; age range 21–37 years, mean 26.8 years) with severe depressive episodes received MST. MST was preferred over ECT across all cases to minimize cognitive risk, particularly in cognitively demanding occupational or academic roles, with prior neurosurgical history or with pre-existing memory complaints. A total of 51 sessions were administered across six patients (range: 7–13 per patient). Concomitant psychotropic medications varied across patients and were continued or adjusted according to individual clinical requirements, which might have impacted seizure induction, cognition, and confounded treatment effects. Effectiveness

Four of six patients achieved remission (HAM-D ≤7) [Supplementary Table 1]. P1, who presented with treatment-resistant RDD and dissociative amnesia, required anesthetic optimization (thiopentone to etomidate) and preprocedure aminophylline augmentation to achieve adequate seizure generalization; remission was attained by week 3. P2, with multiple failed pharmacotherapy trials, ketamine infusions, and a high-lethality suicide attempt, achieved the fastest remission – seven sessions. P4 demonstrated the most robust antidepressant response, achieving remission by week 2. P6, with first-episode depression and high suicidality, attained remission by week 3.

Two patients did not respond. P3, with chronic psychotic depression, complex trauma, and a history of right frontotemporal craniotomy, showed minimal symptomatic change across 13 sessions, but clinically appeared to have improved insight into auditory hallucinations. However, this was not confirmed using a structured instrument for assessing psychosis or insight. P5 showed partial improvement (38%) without achieving a response; he subsequently transitioned to ECT and demonstrated rapid improvement within 2 sessions.

Cognitive safety

B4ECT ReCoDe battery assessments did not demonstrate a clinically meaningful decline in any domain [Supplementary Table 2]. Visual memory retention and autobiographical memory domains, classically vulnerable to ECT, remained stable throughout. Subjective memory was variable: One patient demonstrated marked improvement; it remained stable for most patients. One patient (P6) showed mild decline despite preserved objective cognitive performance. All patients regained full orientation within 2–5 minutes after each session.

Noncognitive AEs

The AEs were infrequent and mild [Supplementary Table 3]. Two patients reported transient headache during earlier sessions, which resolved spontaneously. Mild myalgia with spontaneous resolution was reported in the first two sessions by P6. One patient experienced transient oxygen desaturation, managed with bag-mask ventilation (P5). Neck pain following the first session in P4 resolved by the following day, prompting an increase in succinylcholine dose. No serious AEs were observed. No additional AEs were identified on systematic assessment using the SAVE checklist.[12]

DISCUSSION

This is the first report on MSTs from India. Our findings suggest that MST is feasible and safe in a setting that uses the same anesthetic and procedural infrastructure as ECT. Four of six patients achieved remission, with no clinically meaningful cognitive deficits.

Earlier reviews[3] reported remission rates of roughly 15% to 30%, while the largest recent double-blind RCT[10] reported a remission rate of 22.5%. Our cohort received a mean of 8.5 sessions, compared with a mean of 14.2 sessions reported in the recent RCT.[10]

Prior work has also shown variable outcomes, although the response was similar to that observed in ECT.[89] This may reflect the clinical population studied. A trial on adolescent treatment-resistant depression[14] reported very high response rates (91.1%), suggesting that younger patients may be particularly responsive. The relatively high remission observed in our cohort may partly reflect the clinical profile, with a relatively young population, not all of whom met criteria for treatment resistance. This finding should be interpreted with caution, given the small, selected sample.

Individual cases highlight both the promise and the limits of MST. One patient remitted despite failing seven medications and nine ketamine infusions. Another patient with first-episode depression and suicidality remitted by week 3, although residual symptoms persisted after the acute episode resolved.

The two nonresponders are equally informative. One patient with chronic psychotic depression, complex trauma, and prior right frontotemporal craniotomy showed virtually no improvement. The clinical complexity and prior neurosurgical structural disruption potentially could have interfered with neural circuits mediating seizure-induced therapeutic effects. Another patient had a partial response but improved rapidly after transitioning to ECT. This raises the possibility of either a priming or carryover effect of prior MST; however, this remains an untested hypothesis requiring prospective investigation.

Cognitive safety is the standout finding of this series. Serial assessments showed no clinically meaningful decline in immediate/delayed recall, visual retention, or category/letter fluency. One patient reported a decline in subjective memory despite stable or improved objective performance. ECT is often associated with anterograde and retrograde memory impairment as well as prolonged postictal disorientation.[69] MST appears to spare deeper temporal structures involved in memory processing, which can explain its cognitive advantage.[5] Acute clinical reorientation was rapid, occurring within 2 to 5 minutes after MST sessions.[6] Time to orientation is one of the strongest predictors of cognitive AEs following ECT.[15] Comparative studies consistently report faster postictal reorientation with MST than ECT, although the magnitude of this difference varies substantially across studies.[16]

MST may represent a cognitive-sparing alternative to ECT for selected patients in whom preservation of cognitive function is an important consideration. However, MST remains an emerging and investigational treatment and is not currently approved for routine clinical use in India. We administered MST as an off-label treatment. MST uses the same anesthesia and monitoring framework as ECT, which makes deployment feasible, but the equipment is expensive. Operational issues such as coil heating, high power demands, and the need for separate power backup add practical complexity in resource-limited settings. Regulatory pathways and training standards must be established before MST can move to a routine clinical option.

This report has several intrinsic limitations, including a small sample size, an open-label assessment, and a lack of controls. The heterogeneous diagnostic profile of the cohort limits the generalizability. There may be a selection bias as the patients were treated in a tertiary care center. The relatively young age of the cohort may also have contributed to the favorable cognitive outcomes. In addition, concomitant psychotropic medications were not standardized and may have influenced both seizure characteristics and clinical outcomes. The variable and nonstandardized follow-up periods preclude conclusions regarding the durability of treatment response.

Despite these limitations, the series provides an important first step toward establishing MST in India. Future studies should include larger, multicenter RCTs with systematic comparisons of stimulation parameters. There is a need for the development of clear regulatory and training pathways to establish MST as a potential, cognition-sparing convulsive treatment.
