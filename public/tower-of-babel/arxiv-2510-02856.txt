Local Routing on a Convex Polytope in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3}
Sreehari Chandran, R. Inkulu

Abstract
Given a convex polytope P P defined with n n vertices in ℝ 3 \mathbb{R}^{3} , this paper presents an algorithm to preprocess P P to compute routing tables at every vertex of P P so that a data packet can be routed on P P from any vertex of P P to any other vertex of P P . At every vertex v v of P P along the routing path, until the packet reaches its destination, the next hop is determined using the routing tables at v v and the information stored in the packet header. In O ⁡ ( n + min ⁡ ( n 3 , 1 ϵ 7 ) ) O(n+\min(n^{3},\frac{1}{\epsilon^{7}})) time, the preprocessing algorithm computes a routing table at every vertex of P P of amortized size O ⁡ ( min ⁡ ( n , 1 ϵ 3 / 2 ) ) O(\min(n,\frac{1}{\epsilon^{3/2}})) bits. If the optimal shortest distance between s s and t t on P P is d ⁡ ( s , t ) d(s,t) , then the routing path produced by this algorithm has length at most 8 + ϵ sin ⁡ θ m ​ ( D + d ​ ( s , t ) ) \frac{8+\epsilon}{\sin{\theta_{m}}}(D+d(s,t)) . Here, ϵ ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) \epsilon\in(0,1) is an input parameter, D D is the maximum length of the diagonal of any cell when ∂ P \partial P is partitioned into 1 ϵ 3 \frac{1}{\epsilon^{3}} geodesic cells of equal size, and θ m \theta_{m} is half the minimum angle between any two neighbouring edges of P P on ∂ P \partial P .

For any plane

The intersection of a finite set of closed half-spaces in

The convex hull of a finite set of points in

The

For any data packet originating at any (source) vertex of
P

At every vertex

This requires preprocessing

Naturally, the routing information in the packet header includes the destination vertex identifier.
The typical objectives of a routing problem include minimizing the following:

to

(ii) the maximum space required for routing tables at any vertex of

(iii) the time to preprocess

to compute routing tables at the vertices of

(iv) the maximum size of the label associated with any vertex of

The local routing algorithms determine the next hop based on the location of

at which the packet is residing, and the neighbourhood of

Specifically, in the case of local routing on
\partial P

\partial P

That is, there must exist an edge of

A simple polygon in the plane having pairwise disjoint simple polygonal holes is known as a polygonal domain.

We denote the input convex polytope located in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
by

.
The boundary of

(defined earlier) is denoted by

Without loss of generality, we assume each face of the input convex polytope

We call two vertices

To distinguish from the vertices of

For any point

For any face

The Euclidean distance between any two points

For an edge-weighted graph

Our preprocessing algorithm approximates

As part of this, following Dudley

]

[

]

For each

Next, as described below, for every

For computing

to its corresponding face

For every

If no vertices of

A vertex

At

With a stays ahead argument, we show that the routing path produced by our greedy algorithm performs better than any routing path that zig-zags between the vertices of

located on the left and right sides of

Once the packet arrives at

in the packet header with the plane corresponding to routing the packet to

And the rest of the details of the greedy algorithm for this case are the same as in the previous case.

To our knowledge, this is the first algorithm for routing on a convex polytope in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
With this research, we explored several ideas in the context of geometric routing.
These include
computing a sketch
P^{\prime}
of input convex polytope
P
, selecting vertices as landmarks via deterministic sampling by setting up a grid over
P^{\prime}
, setting up a two-level hierarchy over the vertices of
P
and designing respective routing schemes, computing a Steiner spanner on each face of
P^{\prime}
, taking the help of spanner to compute routing tables at the vertices of
P^{\prime}
, enhancing those tables to build corresponding routing tables at the sampled vertices of
P
, and the greedy routing scheme to ensure local routing.
The following theorem summarizes our result:

Theorem
.

Given a convex polytope
P
with
n
vertices and an input parameter
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, the preprocessing algorithm assigns a unique label of size
O((\lg{(\min(n,\frac{1}{\epsilon}))})^{2})

and it computes routing table at every vertex of

of amortized size

\partial P
such that the length of the routing path from any vertex

of

to any vertex

of

is upper bounded by

.

Routing Algorithm

Given a source vertex
s
of
P
and a destination vertex
t
of
P
, with the help of routing tables computed at the vertices of
P
, the routing algorithm described in this section routes a packet from
s
to
t
.
As detailed, the routing algorithm needs to handle routing in the following cases:
routing from a non-representative vertex to its representative,
routing from a representative vertex
r
to a vertex whose representative is
r
,
routing between two representative vertices while both of them belong to the same patch, and
routing between two representative vertices while the patches to which they belong are distinct.
Since in the first three cases routing is between two vertices that belong to the same patch, there are essentially two cases: routing from
s
to
t
when both
s
and
t
are located on the same patch, and routing from
s
to
t
when
s
and
t
are located on two distinct patches, say
\delta_{i}
and
\delta_{j}
respectively.

Since the routing algorithm is a local routing algorithm, any vertex
v_{1}
forwards the packet to another vertex
v_{2}
only when there is an edge of
P
with endpoints
v_{1}
and
v_{2}
.
Considering this, given two vertices of
P
and a geodesic path
\pi(s,t)
from
s
to
t
located on
\partial P
, we propose a greedy algorithm that routes from
s
to
t
along a
routing path

\pi_{r}(s,t)
such that every two successive vertices along
P
are joined by an edge of
P
.
Note that any two successive intermediate vertices in
\pi(s,t)
need not be joined by an edge of
P
whereas this is not the case with
\pi_{r}(s,t)
.
Upon a packet reaching to a
pseudo-destination
, say a vertex
v
of
P
, two vectors originating at
v
stored in the routing table at
v
which together define a plane
\eta
, the intersection of
\eta
with
\partial P
between
v
and
w
defines a path
\pi(v,w)
.
The concatenation of ordered sequence of
\pi(v_{i},v_{i+1})
for
v_{0},v_{1},\ldots,v_{k-1}
, with
v_{0}=s
and
v_{k}=t
, is
\pi(s,t)
.
Naturally,
|\pi(s,t)|=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}|\pi(v_{i},v_{i+1})|
.
Each of these subpaths is approximated with a routing path, that is, for every
i
, corresponding to
\pi(v_{i},v_{i+1})
, there is a
\pi_{r}(v_{i},v_{i+1})
.
The stretch factor due to our routing algorithm is an upper bound the worst-case ratio of
|\pi_{r}(s,t)|
to
|\pi_{opt}(s,t)|
, where
\pi_{opt}(s,t)
is an optimal shortest path between
s
and
t
on
P
.
Essentially, the objective of the local routing algorithm is to route the packet from one pseudo-destination (or source of the packet) to another pseudo-destination until the packet arrives at its destination.

Stretch of routing path

First, we state a few lemmas that help in proving the stretch of the routing path produced by the algorithm.

The proof of the following is due to elementary trigonometry, and this lemma is useful in proving propositions mentioned later.

Lemma 6

In any triangle
ABC
,
|AB|+|BC|\leq\frac{|AC|}{\sin{(\frac{B}{2})}}
.

Proof.

Let
R
be the circumradius of triangle
ABC
.
Then,

|AB|+|BC|\leq 2R((\sin{C})+(\sin{A}))=

4R\cos{(\frac{B}{2})}\cos{(\frac{A-C}{2})}\leq 4R\cos{(\frac{B}{2})}

=\frac{(2R)(2\sin{(\frac{B}{2})}\cos{(\frac{B}{2})})}{\sin{(\frac{B}{2})}}

=\frac{2R\sin{B}}{\sin{(\frac{B}{2})}}=\frac{|AC|}{\sin{(\frac{B}{2})}}
.

4
Conclusions

The algorithm presented in this paper devised a geodesic local routing scheme for routing between the vertices of a convex polytope
P
.
For the input parameter
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, the preprocessing time, sizes of routing tables at the vertices of
P
, size of labels associated to vertices, and the size of the header of the packet are upper bounded in terms of the number of vertices of
P
and
\epsilon
.
The stretch of the routing path in the devised scheme depends on
D
, which relies on the local geometry of
P
.
This research approximates
P
with another convex polytope
P^{\prime}
whose complexity is a function of
\epsilon
, and computes a geodesic spanner with vertices of
P
projected on the faces of
P^{\prime}
to help in computing routing tables at the vertices of
P
.
In addition, for computing routing tables at the vertices of
P^{\prime}
, we used a routing scheme designed for abstract graphs.
The local routing in the routing phase is ensured by carefully choosing the next hop at every intermediate vertex.
To our knowledge, this is the first result for local routing on polytopes.
The future work could include computing patches of fixed diameter so that the influence of the parameter
D
could be further reduced.
Besides, future research could aim to improve the sizes of routing tables and the preprocessing time.
Especially, in parallel to landmarks found for abstract graphs as in
[
12
,
34
]
, it may be possible to cluster the vertices of
P
in each patch using geometric techniques for finding representative vertices.
It would be interesting to extend this work to routing between the vertices of a polytope which is not necessarily convex.
Besides, when
\mathbb{R}^{3}
consists of obstacles that are convex polytopes, routing between the vertices of those polytopes so that the routing path does not intersect the interior of any of those obstacles could also be an interesting problem to explore.
