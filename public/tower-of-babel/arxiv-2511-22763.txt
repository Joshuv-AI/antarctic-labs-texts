Saturation effects in exclusive vector meson production in DIS
Oscar Garcia-Montero, Yannik Hoffmann, Sören Schlichting

Abstract
We investigate saturation effects in exclusive vector meson production in deep inelastic scattering (DIS), where we model fluctuations within the target protons as localized color-charge hotspots. Based on the Color Glass Condensate (CGC) framework and the dipole picture for vector meson production, we examine the dependencies of coherent and incoherent scattering cross sections on the momentum transfer. We draw conclusions on the effectiveness of our hot spot model and the strength of the suppression of the scattering cross sections caused by saturation effects. We find that saturation has mild effects in the given energy and charge-density ranges, but can also show that suppression becomes more prominent as the color-charge density inside the proton increases.



I
Introduction

Exclusive vector meson production in electron-proton (
\mathrm{e}\mathrm{p}
) scattering allows for unique insights into the internal structure of the proton and the underlying structure of Quantum Chromodynamics (QCD). Traditionally, research has focused on the proton’s structure functions, which reveal properties of the parton distribution functions (PDFs)
34
. Substantial experimental and theoretical work has been done in the past decades to extract such functions
31
;
8
.
Nevertheless, recent evidence gained from experiments on collective flow in proton-proton and proton-nucleus collisions indicates that the transverse profile of partons is also highly significant to describe observed spectra and particle correlations in hadronic collisions
14
;
54
;
6
;
4
;
46
;
55
. This is especially true at high energies, where a significant fraction of the hadron’s momentum is carried by small-
x
gluons, leading to a rapid growth in gluon density. In this high-density regime, gluon recombination and other non-linear QCD effects are expected to play an important role
25
.

High-energy deep inelastic scattering (DIS) processes, found in electron-hadron collisions and in ultra-peripheral collisions (UPC) at the Large Hadron Collider (LHC) and the Relativistic Heavy-Ion Collider (RHIC)
35
;
9
, offer a theoretically robust framework for investigating the internal partonic structure of hadronic states. Furthermore, due to the intrinsic sensitivity of DIS processes to low-
x
degrees of freedom in the target, these processes let us probe the transverse distributions to gain further understanding of the behavior of quarks and gluons in hadronic bound states in different energy regimes.

In exclusive vector meson production, no net color charge transfer occurs between the dipole probe and the target hadron. Thus, the only correlations in transverse momentum and rapidity between the vector meson and the outgoing scattering products, or decay products of the dissociated target, are due to direct momentum transfer, which probes the color-charge distributions inside the hadron
30
.

In the asymptotic high-energy regime, this interaction is most effectively described within the dipole formalism
36
, wherein the electron radiates a virtual photon, that subsequently undergoes quantum fluctuations into a quark-antiquark (
\mathrm{q}\bar{\mathrm{q}}
) pair.

A computational advantage of the dipole picture is the separation of the whole process into three distinct and statistically independent sub-processes: An electron initially emits a virtual photon,
which dissociates under quark-antiquark pair creation with a certain probability. This probability depends on the correlation of the wave functions of the initial and final states (photon and
\mathrm{q}\bar{\mathrm{q}}
pair respectively), which in turn depend on the virtuality and polarization of the photon and the resulting dipole. The color dipole then interacts with the color charges present in the proton, transferring energy and momentum to the target. This interaction is usually represented as a two-gluon ladder in perturbative QCD. In scattering experiments, the decay products of the vector meson are detected and their energy and momentum analyzed
7
. The target proton either remains intact in an entirely elastic scattering (coherent scattering) or dissociates (incoherent scattering) due to the energy imparted to it.

In this work, we investigate saturation effects in the proton using the dipole picture of deep inelastic scattering. We model the interaction between the dipole and the target with the Color Glass Condensate (CGC), an effective field theory of QCD that naturally incorporates multiple scattering and coherence effects, which become increasingly important at high gluon densities
27
. We compute both the coherent and incoherent cross sections for exclusive vector meson production using the fully non-relativistic expression for the overlap function, and compare our results to the analytically tractable dilute (leading-twist) limit
16
;
17
. Following the methodology of previous studies, we exploit the hierarchy of relevant scales to disentangle the different sources of fluctuations, distinguishing between color charge fluctuations and geometric fluctuations of the proton structure, included phenomenologically successful hotspot models of the proton structure
22
;
2
;
55
;
47
;
6
. This framework allows us to systematically explore not only the impact on the coherent cross section, but also how high gluon densities affect different sources of incoherent fluctuations.

II.1
Cross sections and scattering amplitude of the process

In this work, we compute cross sections for the fully diffractive (coherent) process,
\gamma^{*}\mathrm{p}\rightarrow\mathrm{V}\mathrm{p}
, and the incoherent process,
\gamma^{*}\mathrm{p}\rightarrow\mathrm{V}\mathrm{p}^{*}
. In the Good-Walker picture
30
, the coherent cross section is given by the square of the average of the scattering amplitude as
38

\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\mathrm{\rm coh}}_{\mathrm{T,L}}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{1}{16\pi}\big|\big\langle\big\langle A^{\mathrm{\gamma^{*}p\rightarrow Vp}}_{\mathrm{T,L}}\big\rangle\big\rangle\big|^{2}

(1)

and the incoherent cross section computes via the variance of the amplitude:

\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\mathrm{\rm inc}}_{\mathrm{T,L}}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\frac{1}{16\pi}\Bigl(\big\langle\big\langle\big|A^{\mathrm{\gamma^{*}p\longrightarrow Vp}}_{\mathrm{T,L}}\big|^{2}\big\rangle\big\rangle-\big|\big\langle\big\langle A^{\mathrm{\gamma^{*}p\longrightarrow Vp}}_{\mathrm{T,L}}\big\rangle\big\rangle\big|^{2}\Bigr)\,,

(2)

where
t=-\mathbf{\Delta}^{2}
is the Mandelstam variable describing momentum transfer from the dipole to the proton. The double averaging process is introduced here since, in what follows, we will include two different sources of fluctuations in our computations. On one hand, color fluctuations in the target will arise at scales
Q_{\mathrm{s}}^{-1}
, the saturation scale of the target. On the other hand, we include geometrical fluctuations, which arise at scales similar to the nucleon radii. The double average, which we define with the compact notation of

\langle\langle O\rangle\rangle\equiv\langle\langle O\rangle_{\mathrm{c}}\rangle_{\mathrm{h}}\,,

(3)

denotes averaging of color fluctuations in the target, labeled as
\mathrm{c}
, and the averaging of event-by-event geometrical fluctuations, labeled as
\mathrm{h}
.

Experiments have shown that the total cross section is accurately given by the direct sum of the cross sections from virtual photons with transverse and longitudinal polarization
38

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma}{\mathrm{d}t}\approx\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\mathrm{T}}}{\mathrm{d}t}+\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\mathrm{L}}}{\mathrm{d}t}\,.

(4)

We will henceforth not show the subscripts
\mathrm{T}
or
\mathrm{L}
, and the reader should consider all given cross sections to be the sum over polarizations, unless stated otherwise.

To better extract how the incoherent production process is affected by the fluctuations in the initial wavefunction of the proton target, we can separate the
\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\mathrm{inc}}/\mathrm{d}t
into two different parts as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma_{\mathrm{inc}}}{\mathrm{d}t}

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\frac{1}{16\pi}\big\langle\langle\mathinner{\!\left\lvert A\right\rvert}^{2}\rangle_{\mathrm{c}}-\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\langle A\rangle_{\mathrm{c}}\right\rvert}^{2}\big\rangle_{\mathrm{h}}}_{\mathrm{color\penalty\ fluc.}}

\displaystyle\quad+\underbrace{\frac{1}{16\pi}\big\langle\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\langle A\rangle_{\mathrm{c}}\right\rvert}^{2}\big\rangle_{\mathrm{h}}-\big|\big\langle\langle A\rangle_{\mathrm{c}}\big\rangle_{\mathrm{h}}\big|^{2}}_{\mathrm{hotspot\penalty\ fluc.}}\,,

(5)

which clearly shows that color fluctuations are given by the hotspot average of the color variance, while the hotspot fluctuations are calculated as the hotspot variance of color averaged amplitudes.

II.2
Scattering amplitude in the dipole picture

In the dipole picture of DIS, the scattering amplitude for exclusive vector meson production can be split into three distinct sub-processes. The incoming photon splits into a
\mathrm{q}\bar{\mathrm{q}}
pair, which interacts with the target and is eventually projected into a meson state. The amplitude for this tripartite process can then be given, at leading order

\displaystyle A^{\mathrm{\gamma^{*}p\rightarrow Vp}}_{\mathrm{T,L}}

\displaystyle(Q^{2},\mathbf{\Delta})=\mathrm{i}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{r}\penalty\ \int\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{b}\penalty\ \int\dfrac{\mathrm{d}z}{4\pi}\,\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\mathrm{p}}_{\mathrm{dip}}(\mathbf{r})}{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{b}}

(6)

where the properties of the probe itself are given by

, the wave-function overlap for the virtual photon splitting into a
\mathrm{q}\bar{\mathrm{q}}
and subsequent vector meson production. On the other hand, the interaction of the
\mathrm{q}\bar{\mathrm{q}}
pair with the strongly interacting target is described by the dipole cross section,
\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\mathrm{p}}_{\mathrm{dip}}(\mathbf{r})}{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{b}}
, where
\mathbf{r}
denotes the transverse dipole size and
\mathbf{b}
the impact parameter of the dipole relative to the target’s center of mass, as depicted in Fig.
1

\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
, respectively), by

\displaystyle\mathbf{b}=\frac{\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y}}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}\,.

The photon virtuality is given by

\displaystyle\Gamma(\mathrm{q}\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathrm{q}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu\rightarrow\mathrm{e}^{+}\mathrm{e}^{-})=A_{\mathrm{q}}^{2}\frac{4\pi e_{\mathrm{q}}^{2}\alpha_{\mathrm{em}}}{m_{\mathrm{q}}^{2}}\,,

(10)

where

\displaystyle\varepsilon=\sqrt{Q^{2}z(1-z)+m_{\mathrm{q}}^{2}}\,.

(11)

In the non-relativistic limit, the produced quarks both carry the same momentum and energy, making the impact parameter,
\mathbf{b}

\mathbf{\Delta}

\displaystyle A^{\mathrm{\gamma^{*}p\rightarrow Vp}}_{\mathrm{T,L}}

\displaystyle(Q^{2},\mathbf{\Delta})=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{4\pi}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{r}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{b}\penalty\ \mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\mathbf{b}\mathbf{\Delta}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\mathrm{p}}_{\mathrm{dip}}(\mathbf{r})}{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{b}}

II.3
Dipole cross section and correlations

All information about the interaction of the
\mathrm{q}\bar{\mathrm{q}}
-pair with the target is contained in the dipole cross section,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\mathrm{p}}_{\mathrm{dip}}}{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{r})=2\left(1-D(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{r})\right)\,,

(13)

which, for a single configuration of color charges, can be written using the dipole correlator
D
, namely,

\displaystyle D(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{r})=\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{c}}}\mathrm{tr}\left[V\left(\mathbf{b}+\tfrac{\mathbf{r}}{2}\right)V^{\dagger}\left(\mathbf{b}-\tfrac{\mathbf{r}}{2}\right)\right]\,.

(14)

In this trace,
V(\mathbf{x})
corresponds to a light-like Wilson line operator, which describes the interaction of the passing quark with the target color fields. It is explicitly written as a resummation of an infinite number of insertions of classical color fields:

\displaystyle V(\mathbf{x})=P_{+}\exp

\displaystyle\left\{\mathrm{i}g\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\mathrm{d}z^{+}A^{-}_{a}(z^{+},\mathbf{x})t^{a}\right\}

(15)

Integration along
z^{+}
, which is the light-cone coordinate of a source moving along the longitudinal axis of collision, describes the quarks passing through the target.
Additionally, the gauge field
A_{a}^{-}
and its fundamental generator
t^{a}
constitute the gluon field at transverse position
\mathbf{x}

49
;
50

A^{-}_{a}(z^{+},\mathbf{x})=\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{z}\,G(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z})\rho_{a}(z^{+},\mathbf{z})\,.

(16)

The function
G(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z})
is the Green’s function associated with the propagation of a free tranverse gluon, namely
51
;
37

\displaystyle G(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z})=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{z})}}{\mathbf{k}^{2}+m^{2}}\,,

(17)

where
m
has been introduced as an IR-regulator.

We will compute the vector-meson production cross section in two limits, namely for a dense and for a dilute target. When considering a dense target, the dipole cross section is taken to be the trace of the full Wilson line, while considering a dilute target (also commonly known as leading-twist approximation) corresponds to an expansion of the dipole operator only up to the first non-trivial order in the gluon fields (and thus, the color-charge density). The leading-twist vector meson cross section was studied in previous work
17

We note that in both cases, we need the color average, i.e. the average over color-charge configurations, of the dipole cross section

Proton hotspot model

\displaystyle\mu_{\mathrm{p}}^{2}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{h}}}\mu_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}\left(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}_{i}\right)\,.

(29)

Event-by-event spatial distributions in the transverse plane are modelled by assuming a Gaussian profile for the color charge density of each hotspot, namely

\displaystyle\mu^{2}_{\mathrm{h}}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\mu_{0}^{2}}{2\pi r_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}\exp\left\{-\frac{\mathbf{x}^{2}}{2r_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}\right\},

(30)

where the effective width of these distributions is
r_{\mathrm{h}}
. The dimensionless parameter
\mu_{0}
determines the total normalization of color charge present in the proton target and
R
its effective radial width. Generally, we expect the color-charge normalization parameter,
\mu_{0}
, to be dependent on the center-of-mass energy of the process, as a higher
\sqrt{s}
will induce greater charge densities in the target.

We also note in passing, that the double average procedure induces a non-trivial color charge density distribution on average. In particular, at the leading-twist limit, the emerging distribution can be understood at the analytical level, where the induced density is given by
\langle\langle\rho_{a}(\mathbf{x})\rho_{b}(\mathbf{y})\rangle_{\mathrm{c}}\rangle_{\mathrm{h}}=\delta_{ab}\delta^{(2)}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})\bar{\mu}^{2}(\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{B})
. The new effective color correlation, also Gaussian, is explicitly given by
17

\bar{\mu}^{2}(\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{B})=\frac{\mu_{0}^{2}}{2\pi R_{\mathrm{C}}^{2}}\exp\left\{-\frac{(\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{B})^{2}}{2R_{\mathrm{C}}^{2}}\right\}\,,

(31)

where the
coherent radius
,
R_{\mathrm{C}}=r_{\mathrm{h}}+R^{2}(N_{\mathrm{h}}-1)/N_{\mathrm{h}}
, corresponds to the new effective width of the proton due to the hotspot average
17
.

Based on the hot-spot model, we can now explicitly compute the correlation function
G_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}
. Since, in accordance with the discussion in Appendix
A
, the correlation function
G_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}
is linear in the color-charge density correlator
\rho_{a}\rho_{b}
, individual hot-spots contribute additively to
G_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}
and it is instructive to first consider the contribution of a single hot-spot located at a position
\mathbf{b}=0
. We can then express
G_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}
semi-analytically (see
Appendix

A
), which is given by

\begin{split}G_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y},r_{\mathrm{h}}}=\frac{g^{2}\mu_{0}^{2}C_{\mathrm{F}}}{(4\pi)^{2}}&\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u\penalty\ \int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}v\penalty\ \frac{\mathrm{e}^{-m^{2}(u+v)}}{uv+\frac{r_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}{2}(u+v)}\quad\Biggl[\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{4}\frac{u\,\mathbf{y}^{2}+v\,\mathbf{x}^{2}+\frac{r_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}{2}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})^{2}}{uv+\frac{r_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}{2}(u+v)}\right\}\\
&-\frac{1}{2}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{4}\frac{(u+v)\,\mathbf{x}^{2}}{uv+\frac{r_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}{2}(u+v)}\right\}-\frac{1}{2}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{4}\frac{(u+v)\,\mathbf{y}^{2}}{uv+\frac{r_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}{2}(u+v)}\right\}\Biggr]\,,\end{split}

(32)

where
C_{\mathrm{F}}=\frac{N_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}-1}{2N_{\mathrm{c}}}
is the fundamental Casimir and
m
is the IR-regulator introduced in Eq. (
17
).

By considering the contributions from all hotspots, the total function
G_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}
can then simply be computed using

\begin{split}G_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}=&\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{h}}}G_{\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}_{i},\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{b}_{i},r_{\mathrm{h}}}\,,\end{split}

(33)

where
G_{\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}_{i},\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{b}_{i},r_{\mathrm{h}}}
is given by shifting
G_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y},r_{\mathrm{h}}}
, as defined by
Eq.

32
, to the center of the hotspot,
\mathbf{b}_{i}
.

Clearly, the formulation of a model in terms of microscopic charges, where Wilson lines are obtained by solving the YM field equations, has the advantage that it automatically ensures the consistency of all
N
-point functions. Notably, this is not the case for many phenomenological parametrizations of the dipole cross section, which are not based on microscopic considerations, but instead provide an explicit parametrization of the dipole cross-section
D
directly as a function of
\mathbf{r}
and
\mathbf{b}
. We found that inserting popular parametrizations such as the GBW model
28
into higher correlations of the dipole operator,
Eq.

14
, leads to inconsistencies for higher-order
N
-point functions, where e.g. the absence of cancellatiosn in the quadrupole correlation leads to a non-vanishing constant even for
|\mathbf{b}|\to\infty
, resulting in divergent amplitudes. For more information, the reader is referred to Appendix
A
.

IV
Conclusions

In this study we compute exclusive vector meson production cross sections in the dipole picture, using a Monte-Carlo sampling approach to account, event-by-event, for the geometrical subnuclear fluctuations of the proton, as given by the successful hotspot model. We benchmarked our numerical Monte-Carlo setup against analytical leading-twist results presented in previous work
17
, and compared the leading- and full-twist limits to study saturation effects. For the relevant observables, we computed the diffractive coherent and incoherent cross sections of charmed and bottom vector mesons (
\mathrm{J}/\psi
and
\Upsilon
), where we made use of the non-relativistic approximation of the wave-function overlap for the relevant vector meson. We find good agreement to coherent
\mathrm{J}/\psi
production data from H1
3
;
7
, as well as the shape of incoherent data. However, we confirm the trend observed in previous studies which indicated that an extra prefactor,
K=3

17
, is needed to account for the mismatch of the relative normalization of coherent and incoherent cross-sections. Since saturation effects fail to explain this mismatch, the only remaining possibility is that a more accurate description of the vector meson wave-function
43
beyond the strict non-relativistic limit considered in this work as well as in the leading-twist analysis of
17
is required, and we intend to pursue such an analysis in the future.

We also undertook a systematic study, exploring how the overall normalization of charge density, as well as the magnitude of the incumbent quark masses affect the cross sections in both the dense and dilute limits. We observe a consistent trend, where larger charge densities and smaller masses are better suited to distinguish the linear and non-linear effects.

We also present a consistent parametrization of the dipole amplitude, where the color fields inside the proton are created by individual, localized charge hotspots. This is necessary to obtain a physical incoherent cross section, since simpler models with no infra-red regulation present pathological behavior for higher correlation functions (e.g. quadrupole) and large dipole sizes, especially when
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\mathbf{r}\right\rvert}>2R_{\mathrm{p}}
. The formulas we present here have been cured of this pathological behavior, giving a finite incoherent cross section for all geometry configurations.

Just as expected, we find that saturation effects lead to a suppression of the overall scattering cross section relative to the leading-twist limit. While being quite mild in
\mathrm{e}\mathrm{p}
collisions, we expect, naturally, that these saturation effects are more significant in systems with higher charge densities, such as those with greater center-of-mass energies or
\mathrm{e}\mathrm{A}
collisions. As an outlook, in future work we plan to tackle the question of how system size and nuclear structure affect the relations between the nonlinear and linear limits of vector meson production both in Ultra-peripheral collisions in RHIC and the LHC
35
;
9
and the upcoming EIC
1
.

Appendix A
Dipole cross section and color correlations

Beginning with the definition of the dipole correlator

\displaystyle D(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{r})=\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{c}}}\mathrm{tr}\left[V(\mathbf{x})V^{\dagger}(\mathbf{y})\right]\,,

(34)

first the color average is applied and then exchanged with the trace, which makes use of the linearity of the trace, as

\displaystyle\langle D(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{r})\rangle=\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{c}}}\mathrm{tr}\left[\big\langle V(\mathbf{x})V^{\dagger}(\mathbf{y})\big\rangle\right]\,.

(35)

For ease of notation, the color field operator
A_{\mathbf{x}}
is defined such that the Wilson line operator takes the form

\displaystyle V(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=P_{+}\exp\left\{ig\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\mathrm{d}z^{+}A^{-}_{a}(z^{+},\mathbf{x})t^{a}\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{igA_{\mathbf{x}}}\,.

(36)

The Wilson lines in the correlator are now expanded to second order, which is sufficient for a full solution with the highly Lorentz-contracted target
27
;
16
, yielding

\displaystyle\langle D(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{r})\rangle

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{c}}}\mathrm{tr}

\displaystyle\left[\bigg\langle\left(1+igA_{\mathbf{x}}-\frac{g^{2}}{2}A_{\mathbf{x}}^{2}\right)\left(1-igA_{\mathbf{y}}-\frac{g^{2}}{2}A_{\mathbf{y}}^{2}\right)\bigg\rangle\right]\,.

(37)

Multiplying out the terms and using that odd order color fluctuations average to
0
, we get:

\displaystyle\langle D(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{r})\rangle\approx\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{c}}}\mathrm{tr}\left[1+g^{2}\langle A_{\mathbf{x}}A_{\mathbf{y}}\rangle-\frac{g^{2}}{2}\langle A_{\mathbf{x}}^{2}\rangle-\frac{g^{2}}{2}\langle A_{\mathbf{y}}^{2}\rangle\right]

(38)

Since the dilute model assumes only one scattering of the dipole on the target, we can, without loss of generality, generalize that interaction to occur at a single longitudinal
z^{+}
. The integration then becomes trivial and we substitute the color field operator
A_{\mathbf{x}}
with its generator
t^{a}
(with color
a
) and corresponding stochastic variable
\xi^{a}_{\mathbf{x}}
, which is now independent of
z^{+}
:

\displaystyle\langle D(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{r})\rangle

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{c}}}\mathrm{tr}\biggl[1+g^{2}t^{a}t^{b}\big\langle\xi^{a}_{\mathbf{x}}\xi^{b}_{\mathbf{y}}\big\rangle-\frac{g^{2}}{2}t^{a}t^{b}\big\langle\xi^{a}_{\mathbf{x}}\xi^{b}_{\mathbf{x}}\big\rangle

\displaystyle\quad\quad-\frac{g^{2}}{2}t^{a}t^{b}\big\langle\xi^{a}_{\mathbf{y}}\xi^{b}_{\mathbf{y}}\big\rangle\biggr]

(39)

The stochastic variables, of course, have the same average and correlation relation as the color field operators, namely

\displaystyle\big\langle\xi_{\mathbf{x}}^{a}\big\rangle=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\big\langle\xi_{\mathbf{x}}^{a}\xi_{\mathbf{y}}^{b}\big\rangle=\frac{\lambda_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}}{g^{2}C_{\mathrm{F}}}\delta^{ab}\,.

(40)

Here,
\lambda
is a short-hand for

\displaystyle\lambda_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}=g^{2}C_{\mathrm{F}}\big\langle A_{a}^{-}(z^{+}=0,\mathbf{x})A_{a}^{-}(\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muz\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{+}=0,\mathbf{y})\big\rangle\,,

(41)

where, to solve the
z^{+}
and
\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5muz\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu^{+}
integrals, we used that the interaction occurs at a single
z^{+}=0
. From the Fierz identity of the Lie algebra generators
t^{a}
in adjoint color space, it follows that
52

\displaystyle t^{a}_{ij}t^{a}_{kl}=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{il}\delta_{jk}-\frac{1}{2N_{\mathrm{c}}}\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}\,.

(42)

Thus, the trace over two generators of the same color is
\mathrm{tr}\left[t^{a}t^{a}\right]=t^{a}_{ij}t^{a}_{ji}=\frac{1}{2}(N_{\mathrm{c}}^{2}-1)
. Evaluating the trace and averages, we find the color averaged dipole correlator to be

\displaystyle\langle D(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{r})\rangle\approx 1+G_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}\,,

(43)

where the dipole correlation function

\displaystyle G_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}=\lambda_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}-\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}}-\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{\mathbf{y}\mathbf{y}}

(44)

was defined. This will be calculated in
Section

A.4
.

This leads to the dipole cross section, in the dilute limit, taking the form

\displaystyle\Bigg\langle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\mathrm{p,dilute}}_{\mathrm{dip}}}{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{r})\Bigg\rangle=2\left(1-\langle D(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{r})\rangle\right)\approx-2G_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}\,,

(45)

where, as before,
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}+\frac{\mathbf{r}}{2}
and
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{b}-\frac{\mathbf{r}}{2}
.

A.1
Dipole cross section in the dense limit

We again start from the trace over the color average of the dipole correlator. In the dense limit, interactions between probe and target are assumed to occur all the way along the path through the target, and the integral will not become trivial. Instead, we again introduce stochastic variables
\xi
, but this time carry out the Gaussian averaging of the Wilson line correlators by representing the standard Gaussian integral over color charges as a discrete stochastic process in the evolution variable
z
. Wilson lines are initialized such that
V(z=0)=0
, and in each evolution step, we expand the individual Wilson lines as follows:

\displaystyle V_{\mathbf{x}}

\displaystyle(z+\delta z)

\displaystyle=V_{\mathbf{x}}(z)\left(1+igt^{a}\xi^{a}_{\mathbf{x}}(\delta z)^{\tfrac{1}{2}}-\frac{g^{2}}{2}t^{a}\xi^{a}_{\mathbf{x}}t^{b}\xi^{b}_{\mathbf{x}}\delta z+...\right)

(46)

Here,
\delta z
is size of each step. Expanding both Wilson lines in this way yields

\displaystyle V_{\mathbf{x}}(z

\displaystyle+\delta z)V^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{y}}(z+\delta z)

\displaystyle=V_{\mathbf{x}}(z)\left(1+igt^{a}\xi^{a}_{\mathbf{x}}(\delta z)^{\tfrac{1}{2}}-\frac{g^{2}}{2}t^{a}\xi^{a}_{\mathbf{x}}t^{b}\xi^{b}_{\mathbf{x}}\delta z+...\right)

\displaystyle\quad\left(1+igt^{c}\xi^{c}_{\mathbf{y}}(\delta z)^{\tfrac{1}{2}}-\frac{g^{2}}{2}t^{c}\xi^{c}_{\mathbf{y}}t^{d}\xi^{d}_{\mathbf{y}}\delta z+...\right)^{\dagger}V^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{y}}(z)

(47)

\displaystyle=V_{\mathbf{x}}(z)\Bigl(1+g^{2}t^{a}\xi_{\mathbf{x}}^{a}t^{c}\xi_{\mathbf{y}}^{c}\delta z

\displaystyle\quad-\frac{g^{2}}{2}t^{a}\xi_{\mathbf{x}}^{a}t^{b}\xi_{\mathbf{x}}^{b}\delta z-\frac{g^{2}}{2}t^{c}\xi_{\mathbf{y}}^{c}t^{d}\xi_{\mathbf{y}}^{d}\delta z

\displaystyle\quad+O(g^{1})+O(g^{3})+...\Bigr)V^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{y}}(z)\,.

(48)

All odd orders and orders above the second are disregarded. When now applying the color average and trace, as well as equivalently rearranging the equation and dividing by
\delta z
, we get a finite-differences differential equation:

\displaystyle\partial_{z}

\displaystyle\left(V_{\mathbf{x}}(z)V^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{y}}(z)\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{\delta z\rightarrow 0}\frac{V_{\mathbf{x}}(z+\delta z)V^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{y}}(z+\delta z)-V_{\mathbf{x}}(z)V^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{y}}(z)}{\delta z}

(49)

\displaystyle=V_{\mathbf{x}}(z)\Bigl(g^{2}t^{a}\xi_{\mathbf{x}}^{a}t^{c}\xi_{\mathbf{y}}^{c}-\frac{g^{2}}{2}t^{a}\xi_{\mathbf{x}}^{a}t^{b}\xi_{\mathbf{x}}^{b}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad-\frac{g^{2}}{2}t^{c}\xi_{\mathbf{y}}^{c}t^{d}\xi_{\mathbf{y}}^{d}\Bigr)V^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{y}}(z)

(50)

We continue by calculating the
z

Using the Fierz identity for the Lie-group generators, as is shown above for the calculation of the dilute-limit correlator, and applying the average with Wick’s theorem, yields the differential equation

\displaystyle\partial_{z}\langle D(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{r})\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{\mathrm{c}}}\mathrm{tr}\left[\Big\langle\partial_{z}\left(V_{\mathbf{x}}(z)V^{\dagger}_{\mathbf{y}}(z)\right)\Big\rangle\right]

(51)

\displaystyle=G_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}\langle D(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{r})\rangle\,,

(52)

which is solved by

\displaystyle\langle D(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{r})\rangle=\mathrm{e}^{G_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}}\,.

(53)

Here, the initial condition of

was chosen, as

is already normalized in such a way that the dipole cross section is

before interaction with the target.
Thus, the dipole cross section in the dense limit is given by

\displaystyle\Bigg\langle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\mathrm{p,dense}}_{\mathrm{dip}}}{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{r})\Bigg\rangle=2\left(1-\mathrm{e}^{G_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}}\right)\,.

(55)

\displaystyle=g^{2}C_{\mathrm{F}}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{z}\penalty\ \mathrm{d}^{2}{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{z}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}\penalty\ \int\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{k}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\penalty\ \frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{k}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\penalty\ \big\langle\rho_{\mathbf{z}}\rho_{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{z}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}\big\rangle

\displaystyle\quad\times\frac{1}{(\mathbf{k}^{2}+m^{2})({\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{k}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}^{2}+m^{2})}\biggl[\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{x})}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{k}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}({\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{z}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}-\mathbf{y})}

\displaystyle\quad\quad-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{x})}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{k}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}({\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{z}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}-\mathbf{x})}-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{z}-\mathbf{y})}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{k}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}({\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{z}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}-\mathbf{y})}\biggr]

(72)

As above,
\langle\rho_{\mathbf{x}}\rho_{\mathbf{y}}\rangle
is the color-averaged correlated hotspot color-charge density, which is given by

\displaystyle\langle\rho_{\mathbf{x}}\rho_{\mathbf{y}}\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathrm{h}}}\mu^{2}\left(\frac{\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y}}{2}-\mathbf{b}_{i}\right)\delta^{(2)}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})

(73)

with

\displaystyle\mu^{2}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\mu_{0}^{2}}{2\pi r_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{\mathbf{x}^{2}}{2r_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}}\,.

(74)

Performing the
\mathbf{z}
and
{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{z}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}
integration, which makes use of the
\delta
distribution after exchanging the sum over hotspots and the integrals, yields

\displaystyle G_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}

\displaystyle=\frac{g^{2}\mu_{0}^{2}C_{\mathrm{F}}}{(2\pi)^{4}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\mathrm{h}}}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{k}\penalty\ \mathrm{d}^{2}{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{k}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}\penalty\ \frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{r_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}{2}\left(\mathbf{k}+{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{k}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}\right)^{2}}\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\mathbf{k}+{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{k}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu})\mathbf{b}_{j}}}{(\mathbf{k}^{2}+m^{2})({\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{k}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}^{2}+m^{2})}

\displaystyle\times\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\mathbf{k}\mathbf{x}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{k}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}\mathbf{y}}-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\mathbf{k}\mathbf{x}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{k}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}\mathbf{x}}-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\mathbf{k}\mathbf{y}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{k}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}\mathbf{y}}\right]\,.

(75)

The phase
\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}(\mathbf{k}+{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{k}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu})\mathbf{b}_{j}}
is part of the result of the Fourier transformation. Factorizing this phase with the other exponentials results in

\displaystyle G_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}

\displaystyle=\frac{g^{2}\mu_{0}^{2}C_{\mathrm{F}}}{(2\pi)^{4}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\mathrm{h}}}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{k}\penalty\ \mathrm{d}^{2}{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{k}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}\penalty\ \frac{\exp\left\{-\frac{r_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}{2}\left(\mathbf{k}+{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{k}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}\right)^{2}\right\}}{(\mathbf{k}^{2}+m^{2})({\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{k}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}^{2}+m^{2})}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\left[\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(\mathbf{k}\mathbf{x}_{j}+{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{k}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}\mathbf{y}_{j})}-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(\mathbf{k}+{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{k}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu})\mathbf{x}_{j}}-\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(\mathbf{k}+{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mu\mathbf{k}\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu})\mathbf{y}_{j}}\right]\,,

(76)

where we have defined
\mathbf{x}_{i}=\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{b}_{i}
and
\mathbf{y}_{i}=\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{b}_{i}
.
Thus, with multiple hotspots, the position of each hotspot is simply subtracted from the quark positions
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
, which are integrated over in the scattering amplitude. Using Feynman’s integration trick, namely

\frac{1}{(\mathbf{k}^{2}+m^{2})}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u\penalty\ \mathrm{e}^{-u(\mathbf{k}^{2}+m^{2})}\,,

(77)

the remaining integral is a complex Gaussian integration, which can performed to give

\displaystyle G_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}

\displaystyle=\frac{g^{2}\mu_{0}^{2}C_{\mathrm{F}}}{(4\pi)^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\mathrm{h}}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}u\penalty\ \int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}v\penalty\ \frac{\mathrm{e}^{-m^{2}(u+v)}}{uv+\frac{r_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}{2}(u+v)}

\displaystyle\quad\times\Biggl[\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{4}\frac{u\mathbf{y}_{j}^{2}+v\mathbf{x}_{j}^{2}+\frac{r_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}{2}(\mathbf{x}_{j}-\mathbf{y}_{j})^{2}}{uv+\frac{r_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}{2}(u+v)}\right\}

\displaystyle\quad\quad-\frac{1}{2}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{4}\frac{(u+v)\,\mathbf{x}_{j}^{2}}{uv+\frac{r_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}{2}(u+v)}\right\}

\displaystyle\quad\quad-\frac{1}{2}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{4}\frac{(u+v)\,\mathbf{y}_{j}^{2}}{uv+\frac{r_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}}{2}(u+v)}\right\}\Biggr]\,.

(78)

The reader can note that
G_{\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}}
only depends on the magnitudes
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\mathbf{x}_{i}\right\rvert}
and
\mathinner{\!\left\lvert\mathbf{y}_{i}\right\rvert}
, and the angle between
\mathbf{x}_{i}
and
\mathbf{y}_{i}
,
\phi_{\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{i}}
, since

\begin{split}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})^{2}&=(\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{y}_{i})^{2}\\
&=\mathbf{x}_{i}^{2}+\mathbf{y}_{i}^{2}-2\sqrt{\mathbf{x}_{i}^{2}\mathbf{y}_{i}^{2}}\cos\phi_{\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{i}}\,.\end{split}

(79)

Because the numeric scattering amplitude integrals already have four and eight dimensions respectively, this two-dimensional integration cannot realistically be included in every integration point. An interpolation method was chosen to replace the two integrals giving
G
. This tricubic interpolation uses the magnitudes of the vectors and the angle between them as inputs to interpolate over precalculated results, achieving a significant speedup for each calculation of
G
, compared to numeric integration methods, with only small losses in accuracy (relative error
<10^{-4}
).

Appendix B
Numerics

B.1
Numerical integration

Numeric integrators
33
were used to solve the four- and eight-dimensional integrals required for the amplitudes in the coherent and incoherent cross sections.

All proper numeric calculations before analysis were performed on high-performance computers at the Paderborn Center for Parallel Computing (PC2)
10
.

B.2
Hotspot sampling

Generating a hotspot configuration consists of first sampling
N_{\mathrm{h}}
independent positions in transverse 2d space according to the spatial density distribution of the proton (see
Eq.

26
). Then, the spatial average of these positions is calculated and subtracted from each position, which aligns the center of mass with the desired center of the proton. This process causes the actual size of the spatial distribution of hotspots to be slightly smaller than the size of the distribution on which they were sampled initially. Previous work
17
found that, with Gaussian hotspot and proton profiles, as are in use here as well, the resulting distribution is still Gaussian, and calculated the new size parameter, the "coherent radius", to be
17

\displaystyle R_{\mathrm{C}}^{2}=r_{\mathrm{h}}^{2}+\frac{N_{\mathrm{h}}-1}{N_{\mathrm{h}}}R^{2}\,.

(80)

The Monte Carlo-sampling approach outlined in this work does not need the analytical solution for the resulting distribution.

B.3
Energy transfer orientation average

Averaging over the polar angle
\phi_{\mathbf{\Delta}}
of
\mathbf{\Delta}
as

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma}{\mathrm{d}t}=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\phi_{\mathbf{\Delta}}}{2\pi}\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma}{\mathrm{d}(-\mathbf{\Delta}^{2})}

(81)

yields the desired, direction independent cross section.
For the the color fluctuations this can be done semi-analytically:

\begin{split}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\phi_{\mathbf{\Delta}}}{2\pi}\left(\big\langle|A(e)|^{2}\big\rangle_{\mathrm{c}}-\big|\langle A(e)\rangle_{\mathrm{c}}\big|^{2}\right)&=\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\phi_{\mathbf{\Delta}}}{2\pi}\frac{1}{16\pi^{2}}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{r}\penalty\ \mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mur\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{b}\penalty\ \mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mub\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}\penalty\ \left(\Psi_{\gamma}^{*}\Psi_{\mathrm{V}}\right)(Q^{2},\mathbf{r})\penalty\ \left(\Psi_{\gamma}^{*}\Psi_{\mathrm{V}}\right)(Q^{2},\mathbf{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mur\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu})\\
&\quad\times\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}(\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mub\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu})\mathbf{\Delta}}\Biggl(\Bigg\langle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\mathrm{p}}_{\mathrm{dip}}}{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{r})\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\mathrm{p}}_{\mathrm{dip}}}{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mub\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}(\mathbf{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mub\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu},\mathbf{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mur\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu})\Bigg\rangle-\Bigg\langle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\mathrm{p}}_{\mathrm{dip}}}{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{r})\Bigg\rangle\Bigg\langle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\mathrm{p}}_{\mathrm{dip}}}{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mub\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}(\mathbf{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mub\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu},\mathbf{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mur\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu})\Bigg\rangle\Biggr)\end{split}

(82)

Here, the first term inside the parentheses of the reduced dipole cross section is the color-averaged dipole-dipole correlator as shown above. The second term is the trivial product of two single color-averaged dipole correlators arising from the square of the amplitude before the hotspot averaging. Performing the
\phi_{\mathbf{\Delta}}
integral yields the following result:

\begin{split}\int\frac{\mathrm{d}\phi_{\mathbf{\Delta}}}{2\pi}\left(\big\langle|A(e)|^{2}\big\rangle_{\mathrm{c}}-\big|\langle A(e)\rangle_{\mathrm{c}}\big|^{2}\right)&=\frac{1}{16\pi^{2}}\int\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{r}\penalty\ \mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mur\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}\penalty\ \int\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{b}\penalty\ \mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mub\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}\penalty\ \,J_{0}\left(|\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mub\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}||\mathbf{\Delta}|\right)\left(\Psi_{\gamma}^{*}\Psi_{\mathrm{V}}\right)(Q^{2},\mathbf{r})\penalty\ \left(\Psi_{\gamma}^{*}\Psi_{\mathrm{V}}\right)(Q^{2},\mathbf{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mur\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu})\\
&\qquad\qquad\times\Biggl(\Bigg\langle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\mathrm{p}}_{\mathrm{dip}}}{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{r})\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\mathrm{p}}_{\mathrm{dip}}}{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mub\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}(\mathbf{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mub\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu},\mathbf{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mur\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu})\Bigg\rangle-\Bigg\langle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\mathrm{p}}_{\mathrm{dip}}}{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{b}}(\mathbf{b},\mathbf{r})\Bigg\rangle\Bigg\langle\frac{\mathrm{d}\sigma^{\mathrm{p}}_{\mathrm{dip}}}{\mathrm{d}^{2}\mathbf{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mub\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu}}(\mathbf{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mub\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu},\mathbf{\mkern 1.5mu\overline{\mkern-1.5mur\mkern-1.5mu}\mkern 1.5mu})\Bigg\rangle\Biggr)\end{split}

(83)

J_{0}
denotes the Bessel function of the first kind. The scattering cross section now only depends on the magnitude of
\mathbf{\Delta}
.
