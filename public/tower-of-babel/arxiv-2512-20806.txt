Safety Alignment of LMs via Non-cooperative Games
Anselm Paulus, Ilia Kulikov, Brandon Amos, Rémi Munos, Ivan Evtimov, Kamalika Chaudhuri, Arman Zharmagambetov

Abstract
Ensuring the safety of language models (LMs) while maintaining their usefulness remains a critical challenge in AI alignment. Current approaches rely on sequential adversarial training: generating adversarial prompts and fine-tuning LMs to defend against them. We introduce a different paradigm: framing safety alignment as a non-zero-sum game between an Attacker LM and a Defender LM trained jointly via online reinforcement learning. Each LM continuously adapts to the other’s evolving strategies, driving iterative improvement. Our method uses a preference-based reward signal derived from pairwise comparisons instead of point-wise scores, providing more robust supervision and potentially reducing reward hacking. Our RL recipe, AdvGame , shifts the Pareto frontier of safety and utility, yielding a Defender LM that is simultaneously more helpful and more resilient to adversarial attacks. In addition, the resulting Attacker LM converges into a strong, general-purpose red-teaming agent that can be directly deployed to probe arbitrary target models.

Introduction

Language models (LMs) have become ubiquitous across numerous applications, from conversational assistants to content generation and decision support systems. As these models grow in capability and deployment scale, ensuring their safety has emerged as a critical challenge. Modern LM developers face a fundamental tension: balancing the usefulness and helpfulness of model responses against the need to prevent harmful, inappropriate, or unsafe outputs. Achieving this balance is essential for responsible deployment of LMs in real-world settings.

Safety alignment of LMs is not solved.
Despite undergoing careful safety post-training (
e.g.
, as in
Meta Platforms, Inc. 2024a
), state-of-the-art models remain far from robust when placed against adaptive attacks, exhibiting nontrivial jailbreak success rates on standard safety benchmarks, as shown in
fig.

1
. We conjecture that this is due to sub-optimality of the existing methods for safety alignment of LMs. Current approaches generally fall into two complementary categories. The first approach involves manually collecting sets of harmful input prompts across topics of interest and performing fine-tuning to teach models to refuse or respond appropriately to such input
(
Ganguli et al. 2022
)
. The second approach leverages automatic prompt optimization methods to
jailbreak
LMs
(
Guo et al. 2021
;
Zou et al. 2023
)
, followed by adversarial training (via RL or SFT) on the collected prompts. Increasingly, such jailbreaking attacks are crafted via specialized Attacker LMs designed to generate adversarial prompts
(
Paulus et al. 2025
)
.

In practice, these approaches are typically applied iteratively in a sequential process. For instance, when using an Attacker LM, the standard procedure,
e.g.
, as in
Paulus et al. 2025
involves: (1) training the Attacker to generate adversarial prompts, (2) using those prompts to train the Defender LM (the main model being aligned) to resist such attacks, and (3) repeating this alternation until some convergence criterion is met. This sequential cat-and-mouse game, while effective to some degree, suffers from inefficiencies and potential instabilities inherent in alternating optimization
(
Nocedal and Wright 2018
)
.

This paper: safety alignment via attacker-defender games.

In this paper, we introduce a different paradigm: jointly training the Attacker and Defender LMs by formulating the safety alignment problem as an adversarial game, which we call
AdvGame
. Rather than alternating updates,
AdvGame
trains both agents concurrently, allowing each side to adapt online to the other’s current capabilities. This joint view better matches the operational reality of safety alignment: attack surfaces shift as models change and this encourages equilibria that reflect the long-run interaction between Attacker and Defender.

One possible approach to such formulations employs a two-player
zero-sum
game, which under mild conditions admits a minimax equilibrium
(
Nash 1950
)
. However, we argue that adversarial safety alignment games are inherently non-zero-sum and asymmetric. In a true zero-sum game, one player’s gain is exactly another player’s loss. But in safety alignment, the Attacker and Defender have distinct, not perfectly opposed objectives. We describe this in-detail in
section

4.1
. Recent work has explored such zero-sum game direction in
self-play
settings, where the same one model serves as both Attacker and Defender
(
Liu et al. 2025a
)
(
i.e.
, shared parameters). Such self-play has gained some popularity in recent years for training models to solve math, logic and other problems
(
Silver et al. 2017
;
Zhao et al. 2025
)
. While self-play formulations may be sensible for scenarios such as “an LLM teacher proposes challenging math problems for a student LLM to solve”, where collaborative improvement is the goal, we contend that self-play is problematic for adversarial safety alignment: sharing a single set of parameters to serve both Attacker and Defender can entangle objectives, leak gradients across roles, and bias exploration, undermining the diversity and hardness of attacks or encouraging degenerate “solutions” (
e.g.
, trivial refusals or Attacker becoming too safe).

Our proposal.

AdvGame
formalizes Attacker–Defender training as non-cooperative game in which each agent optimizes its own reward under evolving opponent behavior (without any parameter sharing, see
section

4
). The Defender’s reward promotes safe, policy-compliant, and useful responses; the Attacker’s reward promotes eliciting failures (denial of service) or policy violations while penalizing trivial or uninformative prompts. To robustly train both sides, we instantiate online reinforcement learning objectives that can use
preference signals
rather than point-wise scores, enabling richer supervision when absolute reward calibration is difficult (
section

4.1
).
In safety alignment specifically, point-wise judges are difficult to verify due to the absence of ground truth, subjective nature of safety violations, and inconsistent score calibration across contexts, all of which make them vulnerable to reward hacking. Preference-based signals address these issues by requiring only reliable relative comparisons rather than fragile absolute metrics.
Furthermore, our formalization allows using off-policy samples, which we show significantly improves training stability in comparison to pure on-policy learning (
section

4.2
).
We provide a comprehensive empirical evaluation of safety post-training methods, yielding a Defender LM that achieves state-of-the-art performance on HarmBench and exhibits greater robustness to strong adaptive attacks than prior defended models (
section

5
). Finally, in addition to Defender LM, the trained Attacker LM becomes a strong, general-purpose red-teaming agent that can be directly used to probe arbitrary models (
section

5.4
).

Related Work

2.1
Attacks and Defenses on LMs

Red teaming began as a manual methodology to surface safety gaps and operationalize mitigations, establishing core practices and scaling lessons for aligning LMs
(
Ganguli et al. 2022
)
. Automation quickly expanded the attack surface: gradient-based prompt optimization exposes highly transferable prompts that circumvent alignment
(
Guo et al. 2021
;
Zou et al. 2023
)
, fast adaptive adversarial prompting accelerates search and adaptation
(
Paulus et al. 2025
)
, multi-round evolutionary methods cultivate diverse red-team agents at scale
(
Ma et al. 2024
;
Pavlova et al. 2024
)
. There are works that show that multi-shot demonstrations might also be effective in jailbreaking—
Anil et al. 2024
demonstrate that many (30-200) violating examples enable successful attacks across all major LLMs following power law effectiveness.

Another attack vector is
prompt injection
, where attackers exploit LMs’ instruction-following nature by embedding malicious instructions in untrusted data (
e.g.
, tool output). Here, the static manual attacks are still dominant
(
Evtimov et al. 2025
;
Debenedetti et al. 2024
)
, but jailbreaking attack methods can also be used with some adaptation
(
Chen et al. 2025
)
. Recent work explores training an Attacker LM via online RL under fixed Defender LM
(
Wen et al. 2025
)
. In terms of defense, SecAlign
(
Chen et al. 2025
)
represents a breakthrough defense using preference optimization with specially constructed datasets, achieving near-zero success rates against several attacks while maintaining utility. It is worth mentioning that both system-level defenses and various guardrails are widely used against jailbreaking and prompt injection attacks
(
Meta Platforms, Inc. 2024b
)
; however, they typically face challenges from simple adversarial transformations
(
Wen et al. 2025
)
.

2.2
Nash Equilibria and Game-theoretic Methods in RLHF

A growing body of work frames alignment as a game between a policy (main LM being aligned) and a preference model (or judge), seeking stable fixed points in co-adaptation via Nash-equilibrium and no-regret lenses
(
Nash 1950
)
, inspired by self-play advances in RL
(
Silver et al. 2017
)
. IPO by
Azar et al. 2023
introduced a foundational framework that formulates preference optimization as a game between a response model and another static response model. Their work presented a general objective that can recover DPO
(
Rafailov et al. 2024
)
. Building on this foundation, Nash-MD by
Munos et al. 2024
extended IPO to non-static opponents by phrasing preference optimization as a maxmin game between two LLMs.
Further, IPO-MD by
Calandriello et al. 2024
established important theoretical connections by showing the equivalence between online IPO and self-play.
Section

3
and
Section

A.2.1
provide further background information on DPO, IPO, IPO-MD and Nash-MD which we directly build on.
SPPO
(
Wu et al. 2024
)
adapted the multiplicative weights update algorithm to solve Nash equilibria in unregularized preference games.
Robust Preference Optimization
(
Faye et al. 2025
)
extends the partition function approximation idea from SPPO to general rewards, potentially offering an improved alternative to GRPO
(
Shao et al. 2024
)
.
SRPO
(
Choi et al. 2025
)
introduced an asymmetric formulation where preference learning is phrased as a minmax game between two response policies, with one player privileged over the other by receiving the first player’s output as input.
Optimistic Nash Policy Optimization
(
Zhang et al. 2025c
)
employed optimistic online mirror descent with two substeps, achieving improved theoretical convergence rates.

Parallel to these algorithmic developments, game-theoretic preference optimization has found important applications in safety and security for LMs.

Li et al. 2024a
introduce a black-box jailbreak framework that leverages preference optimization against static Defender LM. Their approach computes a scalar harmfulness response score and converts it into preferences by comparing rewards across different responses.

Liu et al. 2025a
expand this to self-play between two contradictory modes of the same LM: Defender and Attacker, and consider a zero-sum setting.

Zhang et al. 2025a
propose a collaborative game in which the safety judge and the Defender are trained together through the point-wise judge. While these are all valid approaches, our proposed
AdvGame
considers non-cooperative game (non-self-play) by jointly training Attacker and Defender, and directly uses a pair-wise judge.

Background & Notation

We first establish some notation and review background on preference optimization below.
We denote by
\mathcal{T}
the space of all possible tokens of a language model vocabulary; and by
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{T}^{*}
the space of token sequences of arbitrary length, where
T^{*}=\bigcup_{n=0}^{\infty}T^{n}
is the Kleene closure.
We define a preference model as a function that is given a seed query
s\in\mathcal{L}
and two responses
y,y^{\prime}\in\mathcal{L}
and selects the winning response, where we denote the probability of
y
being the winner as

\text{Pr}(y_{w}=y|s,y,y^{\prime})=P(y\succ y^{\prime}|s)
.
Moreover, we define a distribution for randomly sampling the winner/loser as

\displaystyle(y_{w},y_{l})\sim\lambda(y,y^{\prime}|s)=\begin{cases}(y,y^{\prime})&\text{with probability $P(y\succ y^{\prime}|s)$},\\
(y^{\prime},y)&\text{with probability $P(y^{\prime}\succ y|s)$}.\end{cases}

(1)

Direct preference optimization.

In Direct preference optimization (DPO)
(
Rafailov et al. 2024
)
, a major assumption is that the preference model follows the Bradley-Terry (BT) model
(
Bradley and Terry 1952
)

\displaystyle P(y\succ y^{\prime}|s):=\frac{\exp(R(y|s))}{\exp(R(y|s))+\exp(R(y^{\prime}|s))}=\sigma\bigl(R(y|s)-R(y^{\prime}|s)\bigr),

(2)

where
\sigma
denotes the sigmoid function.
This implies, that the preferences are governed by an underlying implicit point-wise reward model
R
.
The goal in DPO is to maximize this implicit reward,
i.e.

\displaystyle\max_{\pi}R(\pi|\xi)-\beta D_{\text{KL}}(\pi\|\pi_{\text{ref}}|\xi):=\max_{\pi}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\sim\xi\\
y\sim\pi(\cdot|s)\end{subarray}}\Biggl[R(y|x)-\beta\log\frac{\pi(y|s)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|s)}\Biggr],

(3)

where
\pi_{\text{ref}}
denotes a reference model and
\xi
denotes a distribution over queries
s\in\mathcal{L}
.
Exploiting the existence of a closed-form solution to the above optimization problem, DPO derives the loss function

\displaystyle L_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_{\theta})=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{(s,y_{w},y_{l})\sim\mathcal{D}}\Biggl[-\log\sigma\Biggl(\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}|s)\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}|s)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}|s)\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}|s)}\Biggr)\Biggr],

(4)

where
\pi_{\theta}
is a parameterized model and
\mathcal{D}
is an offline dataset of queries, winning and losing responses.

DPO has also been used in an online training setting
(
Guo et al. 2024
;
Lanchantin et al. 2025
)
, where the responses are generated in an online fashion from the trained model, and the preferences are generated online from a judge model.
The loss then takes the form

\displaystyle L_{\text{online-DPO}}(\pi_{\theta})=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\sim\zeta\\
y,y^{\prime}\sim\text{SG}[\pi_{\theta}](\cdot|s)\\
y_{w},y_{l}\sim\lambda(y,y^{\prime}|s)\end{subarray}}\Biggl[-\log\sigma\Biggl(\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}|s)\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}|s)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}|s)\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}|s)}\Biggr)\Biggr],

(5)

where
\text{SG}[\cdot]
denotes a stop-gradient operation, meaning that we do not compute gradients through the sampling procedure.

4
Method

Figure 2:
AdvGame DPO variant overview.
An Attacker LM proposes two prompt modifications from a seed prompt, which a Defender LM responds to with the goal of safety. The Attacker and Defender are optimized with preference objectives defined by faithfulness, compliance, and deflection judges.

Our final goal is to produce a safe and robust (Defender) model
\pi
:
Given a harmful query
s_{h}\in\mathcal{L}
, it should produce a safe response
y\sim\pi(\cdot|s_{h})
. Safety here can be defined in multiple ways,
e.g.
, it is safe to reject answering a harmful query. However, in line with other recent works
(
Rachidy et al. 2025
)
, we instead resort to
deflection
, that is, providing the user with safe semantically adjacent information.
When instead given a benign query
s_{b}
, the model should comply and produce a helpful and direct response
y\sim\pi(\cdot|s_{b})
.

The standard safety training approach is to train the model on a fixed preference dataset.
However, this leaves the model vulnerable to jailbreaking or prompt injection attacks which are not present in the training data, the model is
not robust
.
Similar to previous works
(
Liu et al. 2025a
)
, our approach to improving robustness is to formulate the problem as an adversarial training problem.
This means that we directly model an adversarial Attacker in the training procedure of our Defender model (see
fig.

2
).
Ideally, this results in the trained Defender being robust against any Attacker.
This kind of adversarial training has a long history in machine learning,
e.g.
, in training robust classifiers
(
Madry et al. 2018
)
.

Specifically, we model the Attacker as another language model
\rho
.
Its task is to rephrase the original (seed) query
s\in\mathcal{L}
belonging to class
c\in\{\text{harmful},\text{benign}\}
, into another (attack) query
x\sim\rho(\cdot|c,s)
that preserves the original intent, but fools the Defender into generating an undesired output
y\sim\pi(\cdot|x)
.
We can now formulate the game between Attacker and Defender as the coupled optimization problems

\displaystyle\max_{\rho}R_{\text{att}}(\xi,\rho,\pi)-\beta D_{\text{KL}}(\rho\|\rho_{\text{ref}}|\xi)

\displaystyle:=\max_{\rho}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}(c,s)\sim\xi\\
x\sim\rho(\cdot|c,s)\\
y\sim\pi(\cdot|x)\end{subarray}}\Biggl[R_{\text{att}}(c,s,x,y)-\beta\log\frac{\rho(x|c,s)}{\rho_{\text{ref}}(x|c,s)}\Biggr],

(6)

\displaystyle\max_{\pi}R_{\text{def}}(\xi,\rho,\pi)-\beta D_{\text{KL}}(\pi\|\pi_{\text{ref}}|\xi,\rho)

\displaystyle:=\max_{\pi}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}(c,s)\sim\xi\\
x\sim\rho(\cdot|c,s)\\
y\sim\pi(\cdot|x)\end{subarray}}\Biggl[R_{\text{def}}(c,s,x,y)-\beta\log\frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}\Biggr],

(7)

where
R_{\text{att}}
and
R_{\text{def}}
are some reward functions judging the success of the Attacker and Defender, respectively.
Note that unless
R_{\text{att}}=-R_{\text{def}}
, this game is non-zero-sum, in contrast to our main baseline Self-RedTeam
(
Liu et al. 2025a
)
.
Another difference to Self-RedTeam is that we do not share the model parameters between Attacker and Defender model.
We argue that this is important, as a model capable of generating harmful attacks with the correct prompting will necessarily be unsafe when used as a Defender model.

The reason for including the KL-divergences in the objectives is that both models should produce human readable text, which can be achieved by staying close to the respective reference models.
For training the Defender, this is standard practice in all of LM fine-tuning, as the final model should be able to interact with humans.
For the Attacker, this choice is more subtle, as the Attacker model is only used during training.
Arguably, the more exhaustively it explores the space of attack queries, the more robust the Defender will be to out-of-distribution attacks.
However, unfortunately exhaustively exploring the exponential search space of possible attack sequences is impossible.
Regularizing the Attacker model to stay close to the reference model means staying on the comparably low-dimensional subspace of human-readable text, which makes the optimization problem more tractable.
1
1

1

This means that we do not aim to produce a model robust against attacks such as GCG
(
Zou et al. 2023
)
which produce non-readable attack sequences.
We argue that such attacks should be defended against using perplexity-based classifiers
(
Alon and Kamfonas 2023
)
, whereas robustness to human-readable attacks should be implemented on the model-level via fine-tuning.

4.1
Preference Judge

Our formulation requires computing rewards in an online fashion.
One option is to directly prompt a judge language model to provide a scalar score
R(c,s,y,x)\in\mathbb{R}
and apply an online RL algorithm like GRPO
(
Shao et al. 2024
)
, which is the approach taken in Self-RedTeam.

However, as shown in
Lanchantin et al. 2025
, LM judges have difficulty providing an accurate scalar score on a global scale, but perform better at making a binary preference decision between two responses.
We therefore resort to a pairwise preference judge, which provides samples from a winner/loser distribution

\displaystyle(y_{w},y_{l})

\displaystyle\sim\lambda_{\text{def}}(y,y^{\prime}|c,s,x):=\begin{cases}(y,y^{\prime})&\text{with probability $P_{\text{def}}\bigl(y\succ y^{\prime}|c,s,x\bigr)$},\\
(y^{\prime},y)&\text{with probability $P_{\text{def}}\bigl(y^{\prime}\succ y|c,s,x\bigr)$},\end{cases}

(8)

\displaystyle(x_{w},x_{l})

\displaystyle\sim\lambda_{\text{att}}\bigl((x,y),(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})|c,s\bigr):=\begin{cases}(x,x^{\prime})&\text{with probability $P_{\text{att}}\bigl((x,y)\succ(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})|c,s\bigr)$},\\
(x^{\prime},x)&\text{with probability $P_{\text{att}}\bigl((x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\succ(x,y)|c,s\bigr)$}.\end{cases}

(9)

Under the assumption of the Bradley-Terry model, these preferences can be related back to implicit rewards via

\displaystyle P_{\text{def}}\bigl(y\succ y^{\prime}|c,s,x\bigr)

\displaystyle=\sigma\bigl(R_{\text{def}}(c,s,x,y)-R_{\text{def}}(c,s,x,y^{\prime})\bigr),

(10)

\displaystyle P_{\text{att}}\bigl((x,y)\succ(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})|c,s\bigr)

\displaystyle=\sigma\bigl(R_{\text{att}}(c,s,x,y)-R_{\text{att}}(c,s,x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\bigr).

(11)

Note that the Defender preference model compares two responses conditioned on the same attack query
x
, whereas the Attacker preference model compares two attack queries along with their respective responses.

We can now view
eq.

6
as a problem of online preference optimization, which has been an active area of research in recent years
(
Rafailov et al. 2024
;
Azar et al. 2023
;
Munos et al. 2024
)
.
However, before we derive the loss functions, we first specify how exactly the pairwise judges form their binary decisions.

Defender judge.

We design the Defender pairwise safety judge such that it prefers outputs that align with our desired Defender model:
on benign seed queries, the LLM should prefer the response which is more compliant and useful.
On harmful seed queries the LLM should prefer the response that better deflects from the query.
The specific judge prompts contain a detailed description of these notions along with examples, see
section

C.2
for the templates.

\displaystyle P_{\text{def}}\bigl(y\succ y^{\prime}|c,s,x\bigr)

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}P_{\text{deflection}}(y\succ y^{\prime}|s)&c=\text{harmful},\\
P_{\text{compliance}}(y\succ y^{\prime}|s)&c=\text{benign}.\end{cases}

(12)

Note that the judges do not have access to the attack queries
x
and
x^{\prime}
.
This is on purpose, as the judge should make its decision purely based on how the responses relate to the original seed query.
Allowing the judge to access the attack queries would open up additional opportunities for the Attacker to reward-hack the judge model.

Attacker judge.

Choosing the objective of the Attacker is critical. One simple option is to consider a zero-sum game as in Self-RedTeam by negating the rewards
or equivalently inverting the preferences

\displaystyle P_{\text{att}}\bigl((x,y)\succ(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})|c,s\bigr)

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}P_{\text{deflection}}(y^{\prime}\succ y|s)&c=\text{harmful},\\
P_{\text{compliance}}(y^{\prime}\succ y|s)&c=\text{benign}.\end{cases}

(13)

However, we argue that this
introduces an imbalance by making the task too simple for the Attacker
: For both harmful and benign seed queries, if the Attacker can cause the Defender to generate gibberish, it succeeds according to the judge, as gibberish is neither a good deflection nor a compliant response.
The Attacker will therefore simply resort to confusing the Defender into producing nonsense, rather than tricking the Defender into compliance.
In practice, such a game formulation will not find a good approximation of the equilibrium, and the resulting Defender will not be robust.

Instead of inverting the rewards/preferences, we simply
swap the rewards of harmful and benign queries
for the Attacker: On a benign query, the Attacker gets rewarded for deflection of the final response, and the Defender gets rewarded for compliance of the final response, and vice versa for harmful queries,
i.e.

\displaystyle P_{\text{att}}\bigl((x,y)\succ(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})|c,s\bigr)

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}P_{\text{compliance}}(y\succ y^{\prime}|s)&c=\text{harmful},\\
P_{\text{deflection}}(y\succ y^{\prime}|s)&c=\text{benign}.\end{cases}

(14)

This ensures that the Attacker will try to fool the Defender into thinking that the query belongs to a different category, a task that is much more well-behaved.

Faithfulness judge.

Besides the compliance/deflection judge discussed above, we need to add one additional judge to ensure that the Attacker actually rephrases the seed query, and does not completely change the subject.
Following
Liu et al. 2025a
, we add a binary pointwise faithfulness judge, which classifies attack queries as faithful or not faithful.
2
2

2

Note that in practice all our judge models are instantiated as a single judge model with varying templates, see
section

C.2
.

If we would not include such a signal, the Attacker would be able to exploit the facts that 1) the Defender does not know about the original seed query and 2) the judge does not observe the attack query directly.
For example, when the Attacker simply changes the subject of a benign query to another benign topic, the Defender will comply as it should, but the response will appear like a deflection to the judge.

In terms of rewards, non-faithful responses are treated as having large negative rewards
which equivalently for Attacker preferences means that faithful attacks are always preferred over non-faithful ones

\displaystyle P_{\text{att}}\bigl((x,y)\succ(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})|c,s\bigr)

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}P_{\text{compliance}}(y\succ y^{\prime}|s)&\text{$x$ and $x^{\prime}$ faithful to $s$ and $c=\text{harmful}$},\\
P_{\text{deflection}}(y\succ y^{\prime}|s)&\text{$x$ and $x^{\prime}$ faithful to $s$ and $c=\text{benign}$},\\
1&\text{only $x$ faithful to $s$},\\
0&\text{only $x^{\prime}$ faithful to $s$},\\
0.5&\text{$x$ and $x^{\prime}$ not faithful to $s$},\end{cases}

(15)

whereas the Defender preferences are adjusted to have no preference when the attack query is not faithful to the seed

\displaystyle P_{\text{def}}\bigl(y\succ y^{\prime}|c,s,x\bigr)

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}P_{\text{compliance}}(y\succ y^{\prime}|s)&\text{$x$ faithful to $s$ and $c=\text{harmful}$},\\
P_{\text{deflection}}(y\succ y^{\prime}|s)&\text{$x$ faithful to $s$ and $c=\text{benign}$},\\
0.5&\text{$x$ not faithful to $s$}.\end{cases}

(16)

4.2
AdvGame-DPO

We now discuss how
eq.

6
can be optimized when the reward is implicitly given by a pairwise preference model.
In the main text, we build on the work of DPO, which we describe in
section

3
and is illustrated in
fig.

2
.
In
appendix

A
, we discuss an alternative formulation based on Nash-MD
(
Munos et al. 2024
)
and IPO-MD
(
Calandriello et al. 2024
)
, which does not rely on the BT-model assumption and leads to the alternative
AdvGame-IPO-MD
losses.

Nash equilibrium.

First, we characterize the existence of an equilibrium at which both the Attacker and Defender objective are maximized.
We observe that the Defender problem is independent of
\rho
and decouples over
x
as

\displaystyle\pi^{*}(\cdot|c,s,x)

\displaystyle=\argmax_{\pi}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\sim\pi(\cdot|x,s)\end{subarray}}\bigl[R_{\text{def}}(c,s,x,y)\bigr]-\beta D_{\text{KL}}(\pi\|\pi_{\text{ref}}|x).

(17)

This problem is strongly concave in the tabular
\pi
, therefore a unique solution exists and we denote it as
\pi^{*}
.
Next, the Attacker problem can be decomposed over seeds

\displaystyle\rho^{*}(\cdot|c,s)

\displaystyle=\argmax_{\rho}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\sim\rho(\cdot|c,s)\\
y\sim\pi^{*}(\cdot|c,s,x)\end{subarray}}\bigl[R_{\text{att}}(c,s,x,y)\bigr]-\beta D_{\text{KL}}(\rho\|\rho_{\text{ref}}|c,s),

(18)

which is again strongly-concave, and we denote the unique optimal solution as
\rho^{*}
.
The unique Nash equilibrium is therefore given by
(\rho^{*},\pi^{*})
.

AdvGame-DPO
Losses.

Using a derivation similar to the DPO loss derivation
(
Rafailov et al. 2024
)
, we derive in
section

A.1
loss functions for estimating the Nash equilibrium
(\rho^{*},\pi^{*})
.
When optimizing a parameterized attacker
\rho_{\phi}
and defender
\pi_{\theta}
, the loss for the defender reads as

\displaystyle L_{\text{DPO-def}}(\pi_{\theta}):=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}(c,s)\sim\zeta\\
x\sim\text{SG}[\rho_{\phi}](\cdot|c,s)\\
y_{1},y_{2}\sim\text{SG}[\pi_{\theta}](\cdot|x)\\
y_{w},y_{l}\sim\lambda_{\text{def}}(y_{1},y_{2}|c,s,x)\end{subarray}}\Biggl[-\log\sigma\Biggl(\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}|x)\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}|x)\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}|x)}\Biggr)\Biggr],

(19)

while the attacker optimizes the loss

\displaystyle L_{\text{DPO-att}}(\rho_{\phi}):=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}(c,s)\sim\zeta\\
x_{1},x_{2}\sim\text{SG}[\rho_{\phi}](\cdot|s)\\
y_{1}\sim\text{SG}[\pi_{\theta}](\cdot|x_{1})\\
y_{2}\sim\text{SG}[\pi_{\theta}](\cdot|x_{2})\\
x_{w},x_{l}\sim\lambda_{\text{att}}\bigl((x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2})|c,s\bigr)\end{subarray}}\Biggl[-\log\sigma\biggl(\beta\log\frac{\rho_{\phi}(x_{w}|c,s)\rho_{\text{ref}}(x_{l}|c,s)}{\rho_{\text{ref}}(x_{w}|c,s)\rho_{\phi}(x_{l}|c,s)}\biggr)\Biggr].

(20)

AdvGame-DPO-MD
Losses.

We also experiment with modifying the models that generate the online training data.
We find in our discussion of the alternative formulation
AdvGame-IPO-MD
in
section

A.2
, that instead of generating the online data on-policy, it can be beneficial to use a geometric mixture model, see
eq.

52
.
We therefore define the corresponding
AdvGame-DPO-MD
losses by replacing the on-policy generator models
\text{SG}[\rho_{t},\pi_{\theta}]
with geometric mixture models
\text{SG}[\rho_{t}^{\alpha},\pi_{\theta}^{\alpha}]
,
i.e.

\displaystyle L_{\text{DPO-MD-def}}(\pi_{\theta}):=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}(c,s)\sim\zeta\\
x\sim\text{SG}[\rho_{t}^{\alpha}](\cdot|c,s)\\
y_{1},y_{2}\sim\text{SG}[\pi_{\theta}^{\alpha}](\cdot|x)\\
y_{w},y_{l}\sim\lambda_{\text{def}}(y_{1},y_{2}|c,s,x)\end{subarray}}\Biggl[-\log\sigma\Biggl(\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}|x)\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}|x)\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}|x)}\Biggr)\Biggr]

(21)

and

\displaystyle L_{\text{DPO-MD-att}}(\rho_{\phi}):=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}(c,s)\sim\zeta\\
x_{1},x_{2}\sim\text{SG}[\rho_{t}^{\alpha}](\cdot|c,s)\\
y_{1}\sim\text{SG}[\pi_{\theta}^{\alpha}](\cdot|x_{1})\\
y_{2}\sim\text{SG}[\pi_{\theta}^{\alpha}](\cdot|x_{2})\\
x_{w},x_{l}\sim\lambda_{\text{att}}\bigl((x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2})|c,s\bigr)\end{subarray}}\Biggl[-\log\sigma\biggl(\beta\log\frac{\rho_{\phi}(x_{w}|c,s)\rho_{\text{ref}}(x_{l}|c,s)}{\rho_{\text{ref}}(x_{w}|c,s)\rho_{\phi}(x_{l}|c,s)}\biggr)\Biggr].

(22)

In practice, following
Munos et al. 2024
, we approximate the geometric mixture by an EMA

\displaystyle\pi_{t+1}^{\gamma}

\displaystyle=(1-\gamma)\,\pi_{t}^{\gamma}+\gamma\,\pi_{\theta},

(23)

\displaystyle\rho_{t+1}^{\gamma}

\displaystyle=(1-\gamma)\,\rho_{t}^{\gamma}+\gamma\,\rho_{\phi}.

(24)

Note that preference optimization algorithms like DPO and IPO are inherently designed to be off-policy, thereby gracefully handling this modification.
This is an important advantage over prevalent on-policy RL methods like GRPO, as we will later see that the ability to use off-policy data can strongly stabilize the training dynamics in adversarial training.

4.3
Algorithm

Given the
AdvGame-DPO-MD
loss functions, we next explain the algorithm step by step.
We first initialize the Attacker and Defender models as well as the EMA models with the reference models.

Attacker rollouts.

Then, for each training step, we first sample a batch of seed queries, consisting of both harmful and benign seeds.
For each seed
s
the Attacker generates two prompts
x_{1},x_{2}
with a task template depending on whether the seed is harmful or benign, see
section

C.1
.

Faithfulness judge.

Then the seed and each of the prompts are passed to the faithfulness judge model, which classifies each prompt as faithful or not faithful to the seed.

Defender rollouts.

For all faithful prompts, the Defender now generates two responses
y_{1},y_{2}
given only the prompt and no additional task description.
This results in a tree-like structure of game rollouts.

Defender judge.

After this, the Defender judge is called, conditioned on the seed and the two responses, returning a binary preference determining the winning and losing response in
eq.

21
.
If the seed is benign, the Defender judge is prompted to make its decision based on compliance, whereas if the seed is harmful, the Defender judge is prompted to make its decision based on deflection, see the respective templates in
section

C.2
.

Attacker judge.

Next, we look at the prompts generated by the Attacker. According to
eq.

15
, if both prompts are not faithful, we have no preference and ignore the seed.
If only one of them is faithful, the faithful prompt is set as the winning prompt and the other one as the losing prompt in
eq.

22
.
If both are faithful, the Attacker judge is called conditioned on the seed and
the winning response for each prompt according to the Defender judge
.
This is inspired by optimistic optimization algorithms for computing Nash equilibria
(
Zhang et al. 2025c
)
, with the idea being that the Attacker plays against an improved Defender, by optimizing against the preferred response.
The judge then returns a binary preference determining the winning and losing prompt in
eq.

22
.
The decision criterion is flipped in comparison to the Defender judge: For benign seeds we judge deflection, for harmful seeds we judge compliance.

Update.

Once the winner/loser pairs for prompts and responses are determined, we calculate the
AdvGame-DPO-MD
losses, and compute the gradients.
After applying the updates, we update the Attacker and Defender EMA.
Finally, we return the EMA models as the final trained attacker and defender models.

algorithm

Experiments

The primary goal of this section is to conduct an extensive evaluation of
AdvGame

•

To what extent can
AdvGame
advance the Pareto frontier of utility and safety?

•

How effective is our Attacker for generic model safety red teaming?

•

What are the critical factors contributing to the success of
AdvGame
(pairwise vs. point-wise judge, EMA, etc.)?

5.1
Setup

5.1.1
Models

We finetune two base models: Llama3.1-8B
4
4

4

We use the Instruct versions of all models by default, unless explicitly specified otherwise

(
Meta Platforms, Inc. 2024a
)
and Qwen2.5-7B
(
Qwen et al. 2025

https://huggingface.co/mlabonne/Meta-Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct-abliterated

For the judge model, we employ Llama3.3-70B-Abliterated for evaluating Llama experiments and Qwen2.5-32B for Qwen experiments. 5.1.2
Training

We use the WildJailbreak dataset
(
Jiang et al. 2024
)
for training. Our training data consists of all vanilla harmful and vanilla benign subsets, which we split 80/20 for training and validation. Additionally, also for validation purposes, we randomly sample 256 prompts from both the adversarial harmful and adversarial benign subsets of WildJailbreak.

After extensive hyperparameter tuning, we set the learning rate to 1e-6 and use a batch size of 4. For all variations of
AdvGame
(DPO/IPO/GRPO), we set
\beta=0.1
and apply exponential moving average (EMA) with a decay rate of
\gamma=0.95
. We use default generation configurations for each model for vLLM sampling during training and do not apply length normalization. All experiments were conducted on 16 NVIDIA H200 GPUs: 8 GPUs for ray actors used to sampling rollouts (a.k.a. generators/samplers) and 8 GPUs for trainers. All training runs were given the budget of 48h (or max_steps=1000, whichever comes first). We use fairseq2
(
Balioglu et al. 2023
)
library to implement this recipe.

5.1.3
Baseline methods

We compare our approach against three baselines: (1) the original Llama3.1-8B and Qwen2.5-7B instruction tuned models, (2) Llama3.1-8B-Abliterated, an uncensored version with removed safety guardrails, and (3) SelfRedTeam
(
Liu et al. 2025a
)
, where we use author-provided checkpoints for Qwen-2.5 and train our own checkpoint for Llama using their publicly available code.

Utility

The utility scores, which measure general knowledge and instruction-following ability, presented in
table

show that the Original models (instruct versions of Qwen-2.5-7B and Llama-3-8B) generally achieve the highest performance across most benchmarks. Then the goal of the adversarial training is to preserve or improve these utility metrics while improving safety metrics. The Self-RedTeam baseline often results in a noticeable drop in utility, with several scores highlighted in red indicating a significant decrease compared to the Original. Among the

and

sometimes show larger reductions, especially on more challenging tasks like IFBench and ArenaHard (v0.1). Overall, DPO-MD and IPO-MD versions of
AdvGame
appear to strike the best balance between maintaining utility and introducing safety improvements.

Safety and Compliance

Safety is measured by the reduction in harmful behavior, while compliance reflects the model’s ability to avoid over-refusing benign prompts. The Original models exhibit higher rates of harmful behavior, especially on (fixed) adversarial prompts. Self-RedTeam reduces harmful outputs but at the cost of higher over-refusal (or alternatively lower compliance).
AdvGame
variants, particularly
AdvGame-DPO-MD
and
AdvGame-IPO-MD
, strike the best balance between all three axis: safety, compliance and utility.

Pair-wise judge vs point-wise judge

At first glance, the curves suggest that the point-wise judge leads to better scores for both the Attacker and Defender. When looking at the table, there is a clear drop in both safety and utility metrics. This supports the idea that point-wise judging makes it easier for models to exploit the reward hacking. EMA helps smooth out updates, making training more stable, which seems to be important for overall performance.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we introduced
AdvGame
, a novel framework for adversarial safety alignment that jointly trains Attacker and Defender language models in a non-zero-sum, non-cooperative game, departing from traditional sequential alternation or self-play approaches. Our experimental results demonstrate that
AdvGame
variants, particularly
AdvGame-DPO-MD
and
AdvGame-IPO-MD
, achieve superior balance across safety, compliance, and utility metrics while exhibiting significantly greater robustness against adaptive attacks, especially black-box methods generating human-readable adversarial prompts (
e.g.
, PAIR and TAP). Through comprehensive ablation studies, we established that several design choices are critical for effective adversarial training: (1) actively training a separate Attacker model (without parameter sharing) to generate diverse and challenging attacks, (2) employing pair-wise preference judging rather than point-wise scoring, and (3) utilizing off-policy sampling with EMA for improved training stability.

In terms of limitations, we acknowledge that our approach focuses exclusively on natural-language attacks that humans can understand, deliberately excluding non-readable attack sequences (
e.g.
, GCG). Also, while our experiments show limitations with GRPO-based training, we do not claim this universally holds: better hyperparameter search and better reward design might improve its performance.

We believe that future work on improved reward model design and extensions to other attack modalities could further enhance the robustness and applicability of game-theoretic approaches to LM safety alignment.

Derivation of

We aim to derive losses for optimizing the defender optimization problem
eq.

17

\displaystyle\pi^{*}(\cdot|c,s,x)

\displaystyle=\argmax_{\pi}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\sim\pi(\cdot|x,s)\end{subarray}}\bigl[R_{\text{def}}(c,s,x,y)\bigr]-\beta D_{\text{KL}}(\pi\|\pi_{\text{ref}}|x),

(27)

and the attacker optimization problem
eq.

18

\displaystyle\rho^{*}(\cdot|c,s)

\displaystyle=\argmax_{\rho}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\sim\rho(\cdot|c,s)\\
y\sim\pi^{*}(\cdot|c,s,x)\end{subarray}}\bigl[R_{\text{att}}(c,s,x,y)\bigr]-\beta D_{\text{KL}}(\rho\|\rho_{\text{ref}}|c,s).

(28)

Defender loss.

Equation

27
is essentially the same problem as in DPO, therefore we simply restate the derivation of the loss here.
We first exploit the well-known closed-form solution of the KL-regularized linear program, which gives

\displaystyle\pi^{*}(y|c,s,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z(x,s)}\exp\bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}R_{\text{def}}(c,s,x,y)\bigr)\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x),

(29)

\displaystyle Z(c,s,x)

\displaystyle:=\sum_{y^{\prime}}\exp\bigl(-\tfrac{1}{\beta}R_{\text{def}}(c,s,x,y^{\prime})\bigr)\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^{\prime}|x).

(30)

Rearranging yields for any
y\in\mathcal{L}
that

\displaystyle\log Z(c,s,x)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{\beta}R_{\text{def}}(c,s,x,y)-\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y|c,s,x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}.

(31)

Next, we subtract the expression for two possible values of
y
to eliminate the log-partition function

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{\beta}R_{\text{def}}(c,s,x,y_{1})-\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_{1}|c,s,x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{1}|x)}

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{\beta}R_{\text{def}}(c,s,x,y_{2})-\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_{2}|c,s,x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{2}|x)}

(32)

and rearranging

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{\beta}\bigl(R_{\text{def}}(c,s,x,y_{1})-R_{\text{def}}(c,s,x,y_{2})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_{1}|c,s,x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{1}|x)}-\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_{2}|c,s,x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{2}|x)}.

(33)

Now we exploit that due to the BT-model assumption, the difference of rewards can be expressed in terms of the preferences,
i.e.

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{\beta}\sigma^{-1}\Bigl(P_{\text{def}}(y_{1}\succ y_{2}|c,s,x)\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_{1}|c,s,x)\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{2}|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{1}|x)\pi^{*}(y_{2}|c,s,x)},

(34)

\displaystyle P_{\text{def}}\bigl(y_{1}\succ y_{2}|s\bigr)

\displaystyle=\sigma\Biggl(\beta\log\frac{\pi^{*}(y_{1}|c,s,x)\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{2}|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{1}|x)\pi^{*}(y_{2}|c,s,x)}\Biggr).

(35)

To train the Defender, we can now replace
\pi^{*}
with the trainable policy
\pi_{\theta}
, and the task is to match the probabilities on both sides of the equation

\displaystyle P_{\text{def}}\bigl(y_{1}\succ y_{2}|c,s,x\bigr)

\displaystyle=\sigma\Biggl(\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{1}|x)\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{2}|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{1}|x)\pi_{\theta}(y_{2}|x)}\Biggr).

(36)

Crucially,
\pi_{\theta}
is only conditioned on
x
and not
c
and
s
, therefore it has to implicitly infer the relevant information on
c
and
s
from
x
, including the information whether it is harmful or benign.

Using a cross-entropy loss to match the distributions on both sides gives the online DPO loss for the Defender as

\displaystyle L_{\text{DPO-def}}(\pi_{\theta}\mid c,s,x)=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}y_{1},y_{2}\sim\text{SG}[\pi_{\theta}](\cdot|x)\\
y_{w},y_{l}\sim\lambda_{\text{def}}(y_{1},y_{2}|c,s,x)\end{subarray}}\Biggl[-\log\sigma\Biggl(\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}|x)\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}|x)\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}|x)}\Biggr)\Biggr].

(37)

Sampling the queries for the Defender loss online from the trained Attacker
\rho_{\phi}
gives the final
AdvGame-DPO
Defender loss

\displaystyle L_{\text{DPO-def}}(\pi_{\theta})=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}(c,s)\sim\zeta\\
x\sim\text{SG}[\rho_{\phi}](\cdot|c,s)\\
y_{1},y_{2}\sim\text{SG}[\pi_{\theta}](\cdot|x)\\
y_{w},y_{l}\sim\lambda_{\text{def}}(y_{1},y_{2}|c,s,x)\end{subarray}}\Biggl[-\log\sigma\Biggl(\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}|x)\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}|x)\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}|x)}\Biggr)\Biggr].

(38)

Attacker loss.

We now tackle the Attacker problem
eq.

28
.
Once again, we exploit the availability of the closed-form solution

\displaystyle\rho^{*}(x|c,s)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{Z(c,s)}\exp\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{\beta}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{y\sim\pi^{*}(\cdot|x)}\bigl[R_{\text{att}}(c,s,x,y)\bigr]\Bigr)\rho_{\text{ref}}(x|c,s),

(39)

\displaystyle\log Z(c,s)

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{\beta}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{y\sim\pi^{*}(\cdot|x)}\bigl[R_{\text{att}}(c,s,x,y)\bigr]-\log\frac{\rho^{*}(x|c,s)}{\rho_{\text{ref}}(x|c,s)}.

(40)

Eliminating the log partition function by subtracting for two values of
x
gives

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{\beta}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{y_{1}\sim\pi^{*}(\cdot|x_{1})}\bigl[R_{\text{att}}(c,s,x_{1},y_{1})\bigr]-\log\frac{\rho^{*}(x_{1}|c,s)}{\rho_{\text{ref}}(x_{1}|c,s)}=\tfrac{1}{\beta}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{y_{2}\sim\pi^{*}(\cdot|x_{2})}\bigl[R_{\text{att}}(c,s,x_{2},y_{2})\bigr]-\log\frac{\rho^{*}(x_{2}|c,s)}{\rho_{\text{ref}}(x_{2}|c,s)}.

(41)

Rearranging and observing that the difference in
R
can be expressed in terms of preferences yields

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{\beta}\Bigl(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{y_{1}\sim\pi^{*}(\cdot|x_{1})}\bigl[R_{\text{att}}(c,s,x_{1},y_{1})\bigr]-\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{y_{2}\sim\pi^{*}(\cdot|x_{2})}\bigl[R_{\text{att}}(c,s,x_{2},y_{2})\bigr]\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\log\frac{\rho^{*}(x_{1}|c,s)\rho_{\text{ref}}(x_{2}|c,s)}{\rho_{\text{ref}}(x_{1}|c,s)\rho^{*}(x_{2}|c,s)},

(42)

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{\beta}\Bigl(\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}y_{1}\sim\pi^{*}(\cdot|x_{1})\\
y_{2}\sim\pi^{*}(\cdot|x_{2})\end{subarray}}\bigl[R_{\text{att}}(c,s,x_{1},y_{1})-R_{\text{att}}(c,s,x_{2},y_{2})\bigr]\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\log\frac{\rho^{*}(x_{1}|c,s)\rho_{\text{ref}}(x_{2}|c,s)}{\rho_{\text{ref}}(x_{1}|c,s)\rho^{*}(x_{2}|c,s)},

(43)

\displaystyle\tfrac{1}{\beta}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}y_{1}\sim\pi^{*}(\cdot|x_{1})\\
y_{2}\sim\pi^{*}(\cdot|x_{2})\end{subarray}}\bigl[\sigma^{-1}P\bigl((x_{1},y_{1})\succ(x_{2},y_{2})|c,s\bigr)\bigr]

\displaystyle=\log\frac{\rho^{*}(x_{1}|c,s)\rho_{\text{ref}}(x_{2}|c,s)}{\rho_{\text{ref}}(x_{1}|c,s)\rho^{*}(x_{2}|c,s)}.

(44)

Since the inverse sigmoid
\sigma^{-1}(P)
appears inside the expectation, we cannot directly isolate a closed-form expression for the preference probability
P
due to the nonlinearity of
\sigma
.
However, because the Bradley-Terry preference model satisfies

\sigma^{-1}(P((x_{1},y_{1})\succ(x_{2},y_{2})|c,s))=R_{\text{att}}(c,s,x_{1},y_{1})-R_{\text{att}}(c,s,x_{2},y_{2})
,
taking expectations over
y_{1}\sim\pi^{*}(\cdot|x_{1})
and
y_{2}\sim\pi^{*}(\cdot|x_{2})

distributes linearly over the reward difference by linearity of expectation.
We therefore define the
marginalized preference
as

\bar{P}(x_{1}\succ x_{2}|c,s)\triangleq\sigma\bigl(\mathbb{E}_{y_{1},y_{2}}[\sigma^{-1}(P((x_{1},y_{1})\succ(x_{2},y_{2})|c,s))]\bigr)
,
which aggregates pairwise preferences in log-odds space—the natural parameterization of Bradley-Terry.
Applying the sigmoid to both sides of
eq.

44

\bar{P}(x_{1}\succ x_{2}|c,s)=\sigma\left(\beta\log\frac{\rho^{*}(x_{1}|c,s)\,\rho_{\text{ref}}(x_{2}|c,s)}{\rho_{\text{ref}}(x_{1}|c,s)\,\rho^{*}(x_{2}|c,s)}\right).

(45)

To train our policies, we can now replace
(\rho^{*},\pi^{*})
with the trainable policies, and the task is to match the probabilities on both sides of the equation.
The online DPO-style loss now reads as the cross-entropy loss

\displaystyle L_{\text{DPO-att}}(\rho_{\phi}\mid c,s)=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{1},x_{2}\sim\text{SG}[\rho_{\phi}](\cdot|c,s)\\
y_{1}\sim\pi^{*}(\cdot|x_{1})\\
y_{2}\sim\pi^{*}(\cdot|x_{2})\\
x_{w},x_{l}\sim\lambda_{\text{att}}\bigl((x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2})|c,s\bigr)\end{subarray}}\Biggl[-\log\sigma\biggl(\beta\log\frac{\rho_{\phi}(x_{w}|c,s)\rho_{\text{ref}}(x_{l}|c,s)}{\rho_{\text{ref}}(x_{w}|c,s)\rho_{\phi}(x_{l}|c,s)}\biggr)\Biggr].

(46)

Approximating
\pi^{*}
by the trained Defender policy
\pi_{\theta}
gives the final
AdvGame-DPO
Attacker loss

\displaystyle L_{\text{DPO-att}}(\rho_{\phi})=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}(c,s)\sim\zeta\\
x_{1},x_{2}\sim\text{SG}[\rho_{\phi}](\cdot|s)\\
y_{1}\sim\text{SG}[\pi_{\theta}](\cdot|x_{1})\\
y_{2}\sim\text{SG}[\pi_{\theta}](\cdot|x_{2})\\
x_{w},x_{l}\sim\lambda_{\text{att}}\bigl((x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2})|c,s\bigr)\end{subarray}}\Biggl[-\log\sigma\biggl(\beta\log\frac{\rho_{\phi}(x_{w}|c,s)\rho_{\text{ref}}(x_{l}|c,s)}{\rho_{\text{ref}}(x_{w}|c,s)\rho_{\phi}(x_{l}|c,s)}\biggr)\Biggr].

(47)

A.2
AdvGame-IPO-MD

A.2.1
Background

IPO.

Identity preference optimization (IPO)
(
Azar et al. 2023
)
is an alternative formulation of preference optimization, which does not assume an underlying BT model for the preferences.
Instead, it directly optimizes the regularized preference of the trained model over a fixed behavioral model from which a dataset is sampled,
i.e.

\displaystyle\max_{\pi_{\theta}}P(\pi_{\theta}\succ\mu|\xi)-\beta D_{\text{KL}}(\pi\|\pi_{\text{ref}}|\xi):=\max_{\pi_{\theta}}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\sim\xi\\
y\sim\pi_{\theta}(\cdot|s)\\
y^{\prime}\sim\mu(\cdot|s)\end{subarray}}\Biggl[P(y\succ y^{\prime}|s)-\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y|s)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|s)}\Biggr].

(48)

This allows modeling non-transitive preferences, and also resolves issues of over-optimization in DPO.
This can intuitively be seen directly from the respective optimization problems: In DPO the implicit reward is unbounded and can therefore dominate the KL term, leading to over-optimization, whereas in IPO the preferences are bounded.

When given an offline dataset
\mathcal{D}
of queries
s\sim\xi
and responses
y\sim\mu
, with responses judged as winning/losing according to a preference model, IPO derives the following offline loss

\displaystyle L_{\text{IPO}}(\pi_{\theta})=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{(s,y_{w},y_{l})\sim\mathcal{D}}\Biggl[\Biggl(\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}|s)\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}|s)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}|s)\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}|s)}-\frac{1}{2\beta}\Biggr)^{2}\Biggr].

(49)

Similar to DPO, it is possible to use IPO in an online fashion
(
Calandriello et al. 2024
)
, leading to the loss

\displaystyle L_{\text{online-IPO}}(\pi_{\theta})=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\sim\zeta\\
y,y^{\prime}\sim\text{SG}[\pi_{\theta}](\cdot|s)\\
y_{w},y_{l}\sim\lambda(y,y^{\prime}|s)\end{subarray}}\Biggl[\Biggl(\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}|s)\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}|s)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}|s)\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}|s)}-\frac{1}{2\beta}\Biggr)^{2}\Biggr].

(50)

Nash-MD.

Nash Mirror-Descent (Nash-MD)
(
Munos et al. 2024
)
takes a step beyond DPO and IPO, as it is inherently designed to be an online algorithm.
The main idea is to replace the behavioral policy
\mu
in the IPO formulation with another model that is trained simultaneously, leading to the saddle-point problem

\displaystyle\max_{\pi}\min_{\pi^{\prime}}P_{\text{def}}(\pi\succ\pi^{\prime}|\xi)-\beta D_{\text{KL}}(\pi\|\pi_{\text{ref}}|\xi)+\beta D_{\text{KL}}(\pi^{\prime}\|\pi_{\text{ref}}|\xi).

(51)

While from a game-theoretical perspective the optimal solution in IPO is the best response against the behavioral policy, the Nash equilibrium of the above saddle-point problem is by definition robust against any other possible policy.

Exploiting the symmetry between the two players, the authors derive a new algorithm called Nash Mirror Descent (Nash-MD), for which it is possible to prove last iterate convergence to the Nash equilibrium in a tabular setting.

The idea of Nash-MD is to iteratively play the best response against a geometric mixture between the last iterate and the reference model, defined as

\displaystyle\pi_{t}^{\alpha}(y|s):\propto\pi_{t}(y|s)^{\alpha}\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|s)^{1-\alpha}

(52)

with the mixture parameter
\alpha\in[0,1]
.
The Nash-MD iteration is now defined as

\displaystyle\pi^{t+1}

\displaystyle=\argmax_{\pi}P(\pi\succ\pi_{t}^{\alpha}|\xi)-\beta D_{\text{KL}}(\pi\|\pi_{t}^{\alpha}|\xi).

(53)

In practice, this optimization problem can only be solved approximately.
The authors of Nash-MD propose to use a policy-gradient based approach in which they only take a single inner optimization step.

IPO-MD.

Another approach, which has also been used in
(
Calandriello et al. 2024
;
Zhang et al. 2025b
)
, is to apply the online IPO loss to
eq.

53
, leading to

\displaystyle L_{\text{IPO-MD}}(\pi_{\theta})=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\sim\zeta\\
y,y^{\prime}\sim\text{SG}[\pi_{\theta}^{\alpha}](\cdot|s)\\
y_{w},y_{l}\sim\lambda(y,y^{\prime}|s)\end{subarray}}\Biggl[\Biggl(\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}|s)\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}|s)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}|s)\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}|s)}-\frac{1}{2\beta}\Biggr)^{2}\Biggr].

(54)

Another practical consideration is that sampling from the geometric mixture is computationally intractable for large-scale language models.
The authors of Nash-MD propose two approximations: Using a token-wise geometric mixture or using an exponential moving average (EMA).

A.2.2
Loss Derivation

We now discuss an alternative formulation to the one in
eq.

6
, which is tailored to the approaches in IPO-MD and Nash-MD.

As the defining criterion of IPO, we directly optimize the preferences instead of optimizing the implicit rewards of Attacker and Defender.
As the preference model requires samples from two separate policies, this in turn requires to formulate both the attacker and the defender problem as individual min-max games between two players, which we define as

\displaystyle\max_{\pi}\min_{\pi^{\prime}}

\displaystyle P_{\text{def}}(\pi\succ\pi^{\prime}|\xi,\rho)-\beta D_{\text{KL}}(\pi\|\pi_{\text{ref}}|\xi,\rho)+\beta D_{\text{KL}}(\pi^{\prime}\|\pi_{\text{ref}}|\xi,\rho),

(55)

\displaystyle\max_{\rho}\min_{\rho^{\prime}}

\displaystyle P_{\text{att}}\bigl((\rho,\pi)\succ(\rho^{\prime},\pi^{\prime})|\xi\bigr)-\beta D_{\text{KL}}(\rho\|\rho_{\text{ref}}|\xi)+\beta D_{\text{KL}}(\rho^{\prime}\|\rho_{\text{ref}}|\xi),

(56)

or equivalently

\displaystyle\max_{\pi}\min_{\pi^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}(c,s)\sim\xi\\
x\sim\rho(\cdot|c,s)\\
y\sim\pi(\cdot|x)\\
y^{\prime}\sim\pi^{\prime}(\cdot|x)\end{subarray}}\Biggl[P_{\text{def}}(y\succ y^{\prime}|c,s,x)-\beta\log\frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}+\beta\log\frac{\pi^{\prime}(y^{\prime}|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^{\prime}|x)}\Biggr],

(57)

\displaystyle\max_{\rho}\min_{\rho^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}(c,s)\sim\xi\\
x\sim\rho(\cdot|c,s)\\
x^{\prime}\sim\rho^{\prime}(\cdot|c,s)\\
y\sim\pi(\cdot|x)\\
y^{\prime}\sim\pi(\cdot|x^{\prime})\end{subarray}}\Biggl[P_{\text{att}}\bigl((x,y)\succ(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})|c,s\bigr)-\beta\log\frac{\rho(x|c,s)}{\rho_{\text{ref}}(x|c,s)}+\beta\log\frac{\rho^{\prime}(x^{\prime}|c,s)}{\rho_{\text{ref}}(x^{\prime}|c,s)}\Biggr].

(58)

The preference model is the same as in the main text, so we directly continue by deriving losses for solving these optimization problems.

As in the DPO case, the Defender optimization problem decouples over seeds and queries

\displaystyle\pi^{*}(\cdot|c,s,x):=\max_{\pi}\min_{\pi^{\prime}}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\sim\pi(\cdot|x)\\
y^{\prime}\sim\pi^{\prime}(\cdot|x)\end{subarray}}\Biggl[P_{\text{def}}(y\succ y^{\prime}|c,s,x)-\beta\log\frac{\pi(y|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)}+\beta\log\frac{\pi^{\prime}(y^{\prime}|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y^{\prime}|x)}\Biggr].

(59)

This problem is strongly convex-concave in the tabular
\pi,\pi^{\prime}
and symmetric, hence by Sion’s minimax theorem
(
Sion 1958
)
the unique Nash equilibrium exists and we denote it by
\pi^{*}(\cdot|c,s,x)
.
Note that while the original policy
\pi(\cdot|x)
did not depend on the seed, the equilibrium
\pi^{*}(\cdot|c,s,x)
does depend on it.
Intuitively, the optimal behavior of the Defender requires knowledge of the original seed without the intervention of the Attacker.

Plugging this in to the Attacker problem and decoupling over seeds yields

\displaystyle\rho^{*}(\cdot|c,s):=\max_{\rho}\min_{\rho^{\prime}}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\sim\rho(\cdot|c,s)\\
x^{\prime}\sim\rho^{\prime}(\cdot|c,s)\\
y\sim\pi(\cdot|x)\\
y^{\prime}\sim\pi(\cdot|x^{\prime})\end{subarray}}\Biggl[P_{\text{def}}\bigl(\pi^{*}(\cdot|c,s,x)\succ\pi^{*}(\cdot|c,s,x^{\prime})|c,s\bigr)-\beta\log\frac{\rho(x|c,s)}{\rho_{\text{ref}}(x|c,s)}+\beta\log\frac{\rho^{\prime}(x^{\prime}|c,s)}{\rho_{\text{ref}}(x^{\prime}|c,s)}\Biggr].

(60)

This problem is again strongly convex-concave and symmetric, hence the unique Nash equilibrium exists and we denote it by
\rho^{*}(\cdot|c,s)
.

To solve the two coupled saddle-point problems, we use a variant of the Nash-MD/IPO-MD algorithm, see
section

A.2.1
.
As in Nash-MD, the idea is to iteratively play the best response against the geometric mixture (
eq.

52
) of the previous iterate and the reference policy,
i.e.

\displaystyle\rho_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\argmax_{\rho}P_{\text{att}}\bigl((\rho,\pi^{*})\succ(\rho_{t}^{\alpha},\pi^{*})|\xi\bigr)-\beta D_{\text{KL}}(\rho\|\rho_{t}^{\alpha}|\xi)

(61)

\displaystyle\pi_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\argmax_{\pi}P_{\text{def}}(\pi\succ\pi_{t}^{\alpha}|\xi,\rho^{*})-\beta D_{\text{KL}}(\pi\|\pi_{t}^{\alpha}|\xi,\rho^{*}).

(62)

Unfortunately we do not have access to the equilibrium policies
(\rho^{*},\pi^{*})
, therefore we plug in the current geometric mixtures
(\rho_{t}^{\alpha},\pi_{t}^{\alpha})
as our best guess, this yields

\displaystyle\rho_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\argmax_{\rho}P_{\text{att}}\bigl((\rho,\pi_{t}^{\alpha})\succ(\rho_{t}^{\alpha},\pi_{t}^{\alpha})|\xi)-\beta D_{\text{KL}}(\rho\|\rho_{t}^{\alpha}|\xi)

(63)

\displaystyle\pi_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\argmax_{\pi}P_{\text{def}}(\pi\succ\pi_{t}^{\alpha}|\xi,\rho_{t}^{\alpha})-\beta D_{\text{KL}}(\pi\|\pi_{t}^{\alpha}|\xi,\rho_{t}^{\alpha}).

(64)

These are now instances of the IPO preference optimization problem discussed in
section

A.2.1
.
Therefore, we can straight-forwardly state the corresponding online IPO losses of
AdvGame-IPO-MD
as

\displaystyle L_{\text{IPO-MD-def}}(\pi_{\theta})=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}(c,s)\sim\zeta\\
x\sim\text{SG}[\rho^{\alpha}_{\theta}](\cdot|c,s)\\
y_{1},y_{2}\sim\text{SG}[\pi^{\alpha}_{\theta}](\cdot|x)\\
y_{w},y_{l}\sim\lambda_{\text{def}}(y_{1},y_{2}|c,s,x)\end{subarray}}\Biggl[\Biggl(\beta\log\frac{\pi_{\theta}(y_{w}|x)\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{l}|x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_{w}|x)\pi_{\theta}(y_{l}|x)}-\frac{1}{2\beta}\Biggr)^{2}\Biggr]

(65)

and

\displaystyle L_{\text{IPO-MD-att}}(\rho_{\phi})=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\begin{subarray}{c}s\sim\zeta\\
x_{1}\sim\text{SG}[\rho^{\alpha}_{\theta}](\cdot|s)\\
x_{2}\sim\text{SG}[\rho^{\alpha}_{\theta}](\cdot|s)\\
y_{1}\sim\text{SG}[\pi^{\alpha}_{\theta}](\cdot|x_{1})\\
y_{2}\sim\text{SG}[\pi^{\alpha}_{\theta}](\cdot|x_{2})\\
x_{w},x_{l}\sim\lambda_{\text{att}}\bigl((x_{1},y_{1}),(x_{2},y_{2})|s\bigr)\end{subarray}}\Biggl[\Biggl(\beta\log\frac{\rho_{\phi}(x_{w}|c,s)\rho_{\text{ref}}(x_{l}|c,s)}{\rho_{\text{ref}}(x_{w}|c,s)\rho_{\phi}(x_{l}|c,s)}-\frac{1}{2\beta}\Biggr)^{2}\Biggr].

(66)

As discussed in Nash-MD, sampling from the geometric mixture is intractable, as it requires normalizing by a sum over every possible response.
In Nash-MD, two approximations are proposed: A per-token geometric mixture, and an EMA model.
We resort to the latter choice, as sampling from a per-token geometric mixture is not supported by current inference frameworks like vLLM, whereas the EMA model is easy to implement.

Finally, for the sake comparison, we also define a simplified version
AdvGame-IPO
where the geometric mixtures
(\rho_{\theta}^{\alpha},\pi_{\theta}^{\alpha})
are replaced with the online policies
(\rho_{\theta},\pi_{\theta})
.
These can be interpreted as applying IPO to the sub-problems of fictitious play
(
Brown 1951
)
instead of Nash-MD.

The results in

6
provide valuable insights into the effects of different training strategies for GRPO. The top panel compares training with a point-wise judge versus a pair-wise judge. The pair-wise judge approach yields improved safety metrics. However, this comes at the cost of a noticeable drop in utility, meaning the model’s overall usefulness or task performance is reduced. The top panel of
fig.

6
explores the impact of on-policy versus off-policy generators during training. Similarly to DPO, in the on-policy setting, the vLLM generator for online RL uses the current model to generate texts, synchronizing after each training step. Off-policy training, on the other hand, is implemented via an Exponential Moving Average (EMA) of the model parameters (see
eq.

23
), and it is also synced after each step. Notably, on-policy training leads to better safety metrics. This improvement can be attributed to the fact that on-policy generation ensures the model is always evaluated and updated based on its most recent behavior, which seems to be important for GRPO. This is in stark contrast to our findings for
AdvGame-DPO
and
AdvGame-IPO
, where lagging behind ensures stability yielding noticeably better overall performance compared to on-policy RL.
