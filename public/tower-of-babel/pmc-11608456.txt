Effects of immersion in various beverages, polishing and bleaching systems on surface roughness and microhardness of CAD/CAM restorative materials
Authors: Kerem Yılmaz, Erdem Özdemir, Fehmi Gönüldaş
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe aim of this study was to investigate the effect of an immune-boosting beverage with Sambucus nigra (SAM), an energy beverage (ENE), an in-office bleaching agent with a novel composition, and a fine-grain professional dental prophylaxis (PDP) paste on the surface roughness (Ra) and microhardness (MH) of CAD/CAM restorative materials over time.MethodsFeldspathic ceramic (FC) and polymer infiltrated ceramic network (PICN) specimens were prepared (N = 126). Glazing, polishing, bleaching and immersion in beverages groups were formed. Bleaching gel containing 25% Hydrogen Peroxide Superior was applied to the specimens with a bleaching pen. Ra and MH were measured at five time points of baseline, days 1, 14, 28 and after PDP. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images were taken after PDP. Statistics were performed by ANOVA and Bonferroni tests (α = 0.05).ResultsRa increased over time regardless of material type, polishing method, beverage type and bleaching (p < 0.0001). Glazed and polished FCs caused higher roughness than PICN, SAM and ENE than distilled water, and bleaching than no bleaching (p < 0.0001). For glazed or polished FCs, MH decreased slightly lower at all time points, but for PICN, MH increased until day 28, not changing after PDP. Beverages decreased the MH of ceramics at all time points, but PDP did not lead to a change for PICN (p < 0.0001). The MH decreased more in bleached specimens than in non-bleached specimens up to day 28 (p < 0.0001). According to the SEM images after PDP, the glazed FC had numerous crack marks, pores and detached particles. The polished FC had traces of mechanical polishing. The polymer part of the PICN had voids, but the ceramic part was better preserved.ConclusionsBoth energy beverages and immune-boosting beverages can increase roughness on FCs compared to PICNs. There was no significant difference between glazing and polishing in terms of lower roughness in FCs. PDP tended to slightly increase the roughness for ceramics, while it tended not to change it for PICN. Beverages decreased the microhardness of ceramics. PDP further decreased the microhardness in glazed ceramics, but increased it in polished ones. In PICN the microhardness increased over time, but decreased slightly after PDP.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-024-05256-9.Keywords: Bleaching, Ceramics, Surface roughness, Microhardness, Hydrogen peroxide superior, Professional dental prophylaxis, Scanning electron microscopy

Background

Dental restorations made in regions where appearance is very important need to keep its physical properties for a long time. Restorative materials produced by computer-aided design and computer-aided manufacturing (CAD/CAM) are frequently preferred for this purpose today [1]. These types of materials can be microstructurally composed of only ceramics or can have a structure containing resin and ceramic. CAD/CAM feldspathic ceramics (FC), which are widely used to date, have a glassy structure containing a crystalline phase. Polymer infiltrated ceramic network materials (PICN), in which the filler particle distribution is significantly different compared to conventional resin composites (RC), are other widely used materials today. PICNs are produced by infiltration of pre-sintered glass ceramic network with secondarily polymerized urethane dimethacrylate (UDMA) and triethylene glycol methacrylate (TEGDMA) monomers [2]. While the surface smoothness of CAD/CAM ceramic materials can be achieved by both glazing and mechanical polishing, glazing is not possible in resin-containing ceramics due to the degradation of the organic matrix during firing [3].

Regardless of the polishing method used for a material, a smooth surface should be obtained as a result. On rough surfaces, plaque accumulation occurs, and as a result, a suitable environment for gingival inflammation is caused [4]. Another important physical property of the material surface is surface microhardness (MH). It is expected that the initial MH of material is not to deteriorate over time due to environmental conditions [5].

Energy beverages can degrade the surfaces of dental materials due to the acid content. The acidic environment degrades the surface of resin-containing materials through weakening of resin bonds, erosion of filler materials and degradation of the matrix [6], whereas it degrades the surface of ceramics due to selective leaching of alkali ions and aqueous corrosion of ceramic glasses [7]. The number of studies on the effect of beverages with acidic characteristic such as energy beverages on the surface roughness (Ra) and MH of CAD/CAM restorative materials is insufficient [8].

The potential to degrade the surface of CAD/CAM materials also applies to bleaching (BLE) agents used for teeth bleaching. BLE is accomplished by in-office treatment of high concentrations of hydrogen peroxide (HP) by the clinician or at-home of low concentrations of carbamide peroxide (CP) by the patient [9]. It has been reported that when the BLE agent comes into contact with the restorative material, it will dissolve in the material and disrupt the surface integrity of the material [10]. Today, new BLE agents with different components have been developed and new studies need to be conducted to investigate the performance of these agents. One of these is an office BLE agent containing 25% HP superior (HPS). HPS is the only system in which the BLE agent is applied with a pen-shaped brush, and the manufacturer claims to have a unique ingredient with poloxamer that allows for a fast starting and high power of the effect [11].

Dental prophylaxis, which is used to remove stains from the surfaces of teeth and restorative materials, can nowadays be performed with new professional dental prophylaxis (PDP) pastes with low relative dentin abrasiveness (RDA). However, the number of studies on the effect of these pastes on Ra and MH of CAD/CAM restorative materials is insufficient [12].

Immune-boosting beverages with Sambucus Nigra (black elderberry) used to support treatment during the COVID-19 pandemic are also likely to disrupt the surface of dental materials due to their acidic character, however, the number of studies examining the effect of such beverages on Ra and MH of CAD/CAM restorative materials is also insufficient [13]. One study [14] examined the effect of black elderberry beverage on Ra and MH, however glass ionomer and giomer materials were used. It is a critical necessity to conduct research examining the effects of immune-boosting beverages, which are increasingly used in viral pandemics, on the physical properties of dental restorative materials.

The majority of studies on Ra and MH of dental restorative materials have focused on acidic or alcoholic beverages, tea or coffee, acids such as gastric acid, hydrochloric acid, bleaching agents, tooth brushing or polishing methods [9, 15, 16]. However, no study has yet been conducted to examine the effect of immune boosting beverages with black elderberry, energy beverages, polishing methods, BLE and PDP on Ra and MH of CAD/CAM restorative materials. Therefore, the aim of this study was to investigate the effect of immune-boosting beverage with black elderberry, energy beverage, polishing method, in-office BLE with HPS and PDP on Ra and MH of CAD/CAM FC and PICN materials. The first null hypothesis was that the polishing method, BLE, beverages and PDP would not change the Ra of the tested materials, and the second null hypothesis was that they would not change the MH.

Methods

The materials used and the study design are displayed in Table 1; Fig. 1, respectively. Using a diamond saw cutting machine (Micracut 201, Metkon, Turkey), 2 mm thick rectangular specimens were obtained from FC and PICN blocks randomly selected from their boxes (N = 126). Glazing (GLA) and mechanically polishing (POL) methods were used in this study. A total of 84 FC specimens were randomly divided into two equal groups. One group was planned to be glazed (FC-GLA) and the other to be mechanically polished (FC-POL) (n = 42). In each group, half of the specimens were bleached and the other half were not bleached (n = 21). Specimens with and without bleaching were randomly selected. The PICN specimens were only mechanically polished due to the microstructural limitation of containing resin content. Half of the total 42 PICN specimens were randomly selected and BLE application was planned. The other half was determined as the non-BLE group (n = 21).

Materials used in the study

The study design. BAS: Baseline measurements before immersion in beverages, FC-GLA: Glazed feldspathic ceramic specimens, FC-POL: Mechanically polished feldspathic ceramic specimens, PICN: Mechanically polished hybrid ceramic specimens, FC-GLA-BLE: Bleached glazed feldspathic ceramic specimens, FC-POL-BLE: Bleached mechanically polished feldspathic ceramic specimens, PICN: Mechanically polished hybrid ceramic specimens, SAM: Immune-boosting beverage with black elderberry, ENE: Energy beverage, DIS: Distilled water used as control group

Specimen size calculation was performed using a statistical power analysis program (G*Power ver. 3.1.9.7, Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf, Germany) to determine a significant group effect. 18 treatment combinations were considered in the analysis. With the significance level (alpha) set at 0.05, power set at 0.80 and effect size set at 0.26 to correspond to a medium effect, the minimum sample size was set at 119. Considering the equal distribution of the number of subgroups, the total sample size was planned as 126 (the lowest number of subgroups was n = 7) [17, 18].

Procedures such as preparation of the specimens, taking surface measurements after removal from the beverages, bleaching and PDP application were carried out by the researchers involved in the study. For this purpose, the researchers randomly selected the specimens and carried out the procedures. As far as possible, the researchers carried out the work in parts. The allocation of tasks and the sequence of workers in successive sessions were planned randomly.

The specimens in the FC-GLA group were pre-finished by 8 μm diamond finishing bur and glazed by glaze powder and liquid (Vita Akzent Plus, VITA Zahnfabrik, Germany). The specimens in the FC-POL and PICN groups were mechanically polished by ceramic polishing set (Diasynt Plus HP, EVE Ernst Vetter, Germany) and paste (Eve Diaopl HP, Eve Ernst Vetter, Germany). After verifying the thickness of all specimens with a digital micrometer (Absolute Digimatic Caliper, Mitutoyo, Japan), the specimens were placed in distilled water in a 37 °C incubator (UM 400, Memmert, Germany) for 24 h to standardize the initial environmental conditions.

After baseline (BAS) Ra and MH measurements were taken on all specimens, in-office BLE agent with HPS (Cavex Bite&White In-Office, Cavex Holland BV, Netherlands) was applied on the specimens in FC-GLA-BLE, FC-POL-BLE, PICN-BLE groups. The gel was applied with a whitening pen at a thickness of approximately 1 mm and left on the surface for 45 min in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. After the surfaces were washed and dried, Ra and MH measurements were taken.

The specimens were stored in containers marked with specific codes, and then subjected to immersing process in beverages. The beverages tested in this study were immune boosting beverage with black elderberry (Sambucol Plus Effervescent, Pharmacare Europe, UK) (SAM) and an energy beverage (Monster, Coca Cola Company, USA) (ENE). Distilled water (Galenik Distilled Water, Galenik Pharmacy, Turkey) (DIS) was used as control group. To simulate intraoral conditions and to standardize environmental conditions, the immersion of the specimens in the beverages was carried out at 37 °C during the whole process.

According to the manufacturers, the average consumption time for beverages such as tea or coffee are 15 min. In this case, 24 h of storage in the beverage is roughly 1 month of consumption. To simulate a 2.5-year period, a 28-day immersion period was applied and Ra and MH measurements were taken on days 1, 14 and 28 [19]. At the end of the immersion, the specimens were washed, dried and polished with a fine-grained (RDA = 7) PDP paste (Proxyt Fine, Ivoclar Vivadent, Germany) at 2500 rpm using a dental contra-angle angldruva (CrossPro, Anthogyr SA, France) with a latex-free rubber brush (Pro-Cup light blue, Kerr, Germany) mounted. Ra and MH measurements were taken again after PDP.

A profilometer device (Perthometer M2, Mahr, Germany) was used for Ra measurements. The measurement range was set to 5.6 mm and the cut-off value to 0.25 mm. NHT-6 type scanner tip with 100 μm measuring range was used. The measuring force was 5 mN. For one specimen, measurements were taken at three points near the centre and the average of these measurements was calculated and recorded. A Vicker’s MH instrument (HVS 1000 MH Tester, Bulut Makine, Turkey) was used for MH measurements. Each measurement was performed for 15 s and under a load of 300 g.

The surfaces of one randomly selected specimen each from FC-GLA, FC-POL and PICN groups were evaluated by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) after PDP. For this purpose, a device (Phenom™ XL G2 Desktop SEM, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) with a high resolution emission field was used. The specimens were coated by sputtering gold on a coating machine (Agar Sputter Coater, Agar Scientific, UK). The surfaces were examined at different magnifications using an accelerating voltage of 15 kV.

Statistical analysis was performed with a statistical software package (Statistical Analysis Software SAS 9.4, SAS Institute, USA). A Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was applied to test whether the distribution of each variable was normally distributed. A 4-way ANOVA was used to evaluate the 2-, 3- and 4-way interactions of 4 independent variables (time, material, beverage and BLE) and the effects of each variable on Ra and MH values, and the significance between means was analyzed by Bonferroni test. Cohen’s d test was applied to determine the effect size (α = 0.05).

Discussion

The first null hypothesis that polishing method, BLE and beverages would not change the Ra of the tested materials was rejected. The surfaces of dental materials need to be as smooth as possible to prevent surface staining, plaque accumulation and improve the appearance properties of teeth [5]. Ra thresholds have been reported as 0.2 μm to prevent bacterial retention [20], 0.5 μm for the perceptibility of rough surface with the tongue and the finished restoration surface [21]. The average roughness value of natural tooth enamel was reported to be 0.64 μm [22]. According to the Ra results, after 28 days the Ra value is higher than the threshold of perceptibility by tongue for FC-GLA and FC-POL immersed in DIS, ENE or SAM and lower for PICN.

In this study, the number of beverages tested was set at three, in agreement with a previous study [23]. One of them was distilled water, which was the control group. The other was a treatment-supportive beverage that was frequently consumed during the COVID-19 pandemic. The third was to be another beverage frequently consumed with COVID-19. Energy beverage was one such beverage and the number of studies testing it was low. Therefore, in agreement with previous studies [24, 25], energy beverage was selected as the third beverage.

This study is in agreement with previous studies [26–28] reporting that energy beverages increase the Ra of restorative materials. Citric acid contained in energy beverages may deteriorate the surface features of dental materials. Al-Thoibity et al. [28] reported that in dental ceramics, citric acid ions bind to metal oxides to maintain electrical neutrality, resulting in dissolution of oxide ions and thus surface degradation.

DIS was also reported to affect the Ra of dental materials [29]. However, in this study, the effect of DIS on Ra was at the lowest value than the other beverages. According to the results, Ra was unchanged for FC-GLA than BAS, while an increase of only 0.1 μm was found for FC-POL. For PICN, the Ra value obtained after 28 days was 0.4 μm, which was higher than that of CAD/CAM ceramics. Although the rough surface perceptibility threshold was not exceeded, the increase in PICN showed that PICN was more influenced by DIS than FC. The effect of DIS on PICN could be explained by the attack of hydroxyl ions on the matrix as a result of the self-catalytic reaction of water [30]. In a previous study [31] with similar findings to the present study, it was found that CAD/CAM FC was less affected by water than PICN, and it was reported that the feldspathic particles and porous structure of the ceramic surface may be responsible for the effect of water. This study indicates that it would be beneficial to perform further studies investigating the effect of water on the Ra and MH of CAD/CAM ceramic materials on a microstructural basis.

It is surprising that only one study [14] was found in the literature on the effect of immune-boosting beverages with Sambucus Nigra, which is recommended as a supporting treatment due to its anti-viral features in the COVID-19 pandemic, on the surface features of dental materials. In the aforementioned study, unlike this study, glass ionomer and giomer were tested and softening of the material surface and displacement of filler particles on the outer surface were reported as the cause of deterioration of both Ra and MH. In the current study, when SAM-BLE pair was evaluated regardless of material, the Ra value exceeded the rough surface perceptibility threshold on day 1. This may be related to the surface degrading properties of citric acid as reported in previous studies [27–29].

In this study, at the end of 28 days, PICN had a smoother surface than FC, a finding consistent with previous studies. PICN contains a feldspathic ceramic matrix (86% by weight) infiltrated with low viscosity copolymers (UDMA and TEGDMA) [32, 33]. In a previous study [34], it was reported that after acid dissolution of feldspathic matrix, the boundaries between the ceramic and polymer parts became apparent, which was influential for less roughness. Miyashita-Kobayashi et al. [35] reported that increasing the amount of inorganic filler in the resin matrix increases the resistance to roughening. In this study, the roughness value of PICN never exceeded the threshold value of 0.5 μm, however FC roughened even higher than the roughness value of tooth enamel in After PDP. FCs contain feldspathic crystal particles embedded in a glass matrix [36]. Possibly the ceramic glass matrix structure of material, which makes it difficult to polish well, may have caused its roughness.

In this study, BAS Ra values of all materials, regardless of beverage, were 0.3 μm, similar to those of Alencar et al. [37]. In this study, at the end of day 28, glazed or mechanically polished FC groups, with or without prior bleaching, showed similar roughness and reached the roughness threshold of tooth enamel. Unlike this result, the PICN specimens without BLE exhibited a more favorable Ra of 0.4 μm.

For ceramics, glazed surfaces were reported to be the gold standard for smoothness [37]. However, in this study, mechanically polished CAD/CAM FCs exhibited as good smoothness as glazed ones, in agreement with other studies [38, 39]. It has been reported that polishing CAD/CAM FC with rubber as the final tool before glazing makes the surface smoother [40]. In this study, the lack of a major difference in the effect of polishing and glazing on Ra may be due to the use of an 8 μm diamond bur as the final tool of the finishing process. Moreover, Ludovichetti et al. [33] reported that finishing before glazing or mechanical polishing creates a rough topography on the surface of material, moreover, CAD/CAM FCs are more affected by the quality of finishing due to their higher surface hardness than PICNs, and consequently, PICNs may be easier to polish.

It has been reported that BLE agent that provides whitening of tooth enamel has the potential to degrade surface when it comes into contact with the surface of the restorative material [41]. In this study, a new HP formula (25% HPS) was used for in-office BLE. Conventional in-office BLE gels mostly contain 30–35% HP. In contrast to conventional BLE gels, HPS contains CP and vinyl pyrolidone peroxide in combination, as well as heat-reversible poloxamer to adjust the viscosity. When the gel comes into contact with the tooth surface, an exothermic reaction cycle occurs, resulting in an increase in the magnitude of the effect. This agent, which can be used with a pen-like brush, can be applied for a maximum of 45 min, according to the user instructions catalog [11, 42].

In this study, exposure to BLE agent prior to immersion in beverages resulted in a small increase in Ra after 1 day for PICN specimens (from 0.3 μm to 0.4 μm), however, for FC-GLA and FC-POL, the increase was of significant magnitude (up to two times). PICNs may be a preferable alternative to glazed or mechanically polished CAD/CAM FCs, as the surfaces are less damaged by BLE.

In a previous study [9] on the effect of BLE on Ra, which obtained results compatible with this study, it was reported that as a result of the potential diffusion of H+ or H3O+ free radicals produced by the BLE agent to the surface of the ceramic material, alkaline ions were selectively leached and thus dissolution occurred in the glass network of the ceramic. In another study [43], it was reported that free radicals produced by peroxides could lead to weakening of matrix of the RC, breaking the filler-matrix bond and consequently increasing the surface roughness. The dual, interwoven ceramic and polymer network in PICNs [39] and the homogeneous filler mixture [6] may be one of the reasons for the less roughening of BLE.

MH is a property that relates to polishability and the magnitude of wear on opposing teeth [16]. It was reported that higher MH of the material, the more resistant it was to surface penetration and groove formation [5]. The second null hypothesis that polishing method, BLE, beverages and PDP would not affect the MH of the tested materials was rejected. According to the results, regardless of beverage or BLE, the MH value was FC-GLA, FC-POL, PICN from largest to smallest. With FC-GLA and FC-POL, a decrease in MH was exhibited over time compared to MH in BAS, whereas with PICN, an increase was exhibited over time. BLE caused a significant decrease in the MH of FC-GLA and FC-POL specimens, however, it tended to increase the MH of PICN specimens.

In this study, the results regarding the effect of polishing method or BLE on MH were consistent with previous studies [44, 45]. Beverages have been reported to cause loss of main components such as potassium, aluminum and silicon in ceramics [45]. Çakmak et al. [16] reported that MH in PICNs did not degrade over time and that the cause was the material structure, not the polishing method. In this study, the decrease in MH in FC specimens over time can be explained by the decrease in SiO2 from the glassy structure due to low pH or oxidizing solutions, while the increase in MH in PICN specimens can be explained can be explained by the degradation of the polymer network and the increase in the presence of hard ceramic component on the surface.

The MH of dental materials, including saliva, may decrease over time due to beverages [25]. In previous studies [46–48] in parallel with this study, it was reported that the acid of energy beverages ranging from 1.5 to 3.4 depending on the brand, and Choi et al. [49] reported that hydrolysis between the resin matrix and filler as a result of the polymer absorbing water decreases the hardness of RCs. Monster energy beverage was used in this study. Silva et al. [46] reported that the surface degrading effect of Monster energy beverage, which has a pH of 3.41, may increase with time and frequency of use. Since MH did not decrease compared to BAS in both bleached and unbleached PICN specimens, the MH specimens were protected from the detrimental effects of both BLE and beverages over time. In FC-GLA and FC-POL, however, the beverages caused a significant decrease in MH compared to BAS, and this decrease was similar in the bleached and unbleached groups, and despite the significant increase in MH with PDP, the new values did not reach the level in BAS (p < 0.0001).

In the SEM image of FC-GLA after PDP, there were a large number of crack marks parallel to each other and a smaller number of pores scattered in a diffuse manner. In FC-POL, the structure was not as homogeneous as in FC-GLA. There were many separated particles in and around the holes. The striations were in contact with each other and there were shallow striations dividing the cracks at different angles. These shallow striations were probably caused by the impact marks of the tools used for POL. However, as the profilometer result did not show a significant difference in Ra between FC-GLA and FC-POL, the additional striations caused by the POL tools do not appear to be a significant problem that would justify to choice of GLA. The main problem with FC was better understood as a result of the SEM analysis of PICN. In this study, the SEM results for PICN supported the profilometer results and concluded that PICN is preferable to glazed or mechanically polished FC.

The SEM image of the PICN showed that the ceramic and polymer sections were generally uniformly distributed. However, in agreement with a previous study [50], it was observed that the polymer matrix was lost and peeled particles were formed. However, the ceramic section was largely preserved. The number of feldspathic particles separated from the main structure by protrusion was small. The innovative microstructure of PICN, consisting of a polymer-infiltrated ceramic network, may produce a different damage mode than conventional FCs [51]. As a result of the delamination of the polymer section, the inorganic particle separates from the structure without fracture. Therefore, the tendency to crack is low. As FCs do not contain a polymer section, external factors increase the possibility of surface cracking. In addition, PICN consists of an interwoven, three-dimensional, intersecting ceramic network. Cracks mostly propagate along the ceramic network, but these cracks are deflected and eliminated along the polymer-ceramic interface [52].

The hardness values of the materials are also effective in breaking particles from the material surface. High hardness values increase the brittleness of the material. Cracks lead to fractures and the formation of seperated particles over time [53]. In this study, the lower hardness of PICN compared to FC may have contributed to the formation of fewer cracks and consequently fewer broken particles, resulting in a smoother surface.

GLA is a longer process than POL. However, faster completion of the process can provide more time for the dentist and a more comfortable treatment experience for the patient. This study showed that the surface roughness of glazed and mechanically polished specimens in conventional FCs was higher than desired. Preferring PICN material for restoration, which was found to be more successful than FC due to its advanced micromechanical properties, may pave the way for both the dentist and the patient to benefit from the aforementioned advantage. The limitation of this study is that it was performed in an in vitro environment. The buffering system of saliva, the pH level of the oral cavity, the complexity of the oral cavity environment, including the dilution effect on acidic foods and beverages, microbial film formation and the mechanical effect of chewing and other similar forces were not considered. The fact that an immersion period of days was used to simulate a period of years may have prevented an accurate replication of the effect of the oral environment over time. However, there are no universally accepted definitive standards for in vitro studies examining various surface properties of restorative materials such as color, roughness and microhardness. There were major financial and methodological difficulties in establishing an in vivo study environment for many years. Therefore, this study was planned as an in vitro study with a 28-day immersion period.

The increasing consumption of energy beverages due to social isolation caused by pandemics such as COVID-19, which have become a global health threat, as well as the increasing consumption of immune-boosting beverages used as a treatment support, created a necessity to determine the effect of such beverages on the surface properties of dental restorative materials. Future studies are needed to investigate the effect of immune-boosting beverages with Sambucus Nigra, energy beverages, new bleaching agents with different technologies, polishing methods or PDP paste on the surface properties of CAD/CAM restorative materials in both in vitro and in vivo environments.

Conclusions

Within the limitations of this study, the results obtained were as follows:

Glazed or mechanically polished FC specimens immersed in energy beverage or immune-boosting beverage exceeded the finished restoration acceptability threshold specified in this study for surface roughness at all time points except baseline, but PICN did not.

The microhardness of glazed FC specimens decreased over time, regardless of the beverage in which they were immersed. In contrast, in mechanically polished FC specimens, the microhardness decreased until day 28 and increased after PDP. Unlike ceramics, PICN specimens exhibited increasing tendency in microhardness from baseline to day 28, with a slight decreasing tendency after PDP.

The microhardness values at the baseline stage were similar for specimens immersed in the immune-boosting beverage and the energy beverage, however, over time, the energy beverage led to a higher decrease in microhardness compared to the other.
