Randomization Tests for Conditional Group Symmetry
Kenny Chiu, Alex Sharp, Benjamin Bloem-Reddy

Abstract
Symmetry plays a central role in the sciences, machine learning, and statistics. While statistical tests for the presence of distributional invariance with respect to groups have a long history, tests for conditional symmetry in the form of equivariance or conditional invariance are absent from the literature. This work initiates the study of nonparametric randomization tests for symmetry (invariance or equivariance) of a conditional distribution under the action of a specified locally compact group. We develop a general framework for randomization tests with finite-sample Type I error control and, using kernel methods, implement tests with finite-sample power lower bounds. We also describe and implement approximate versions of the tests, which are asymptotically consistent. We study their properties empirically using synthetic examples and applications to testing for symmetry in two problems from high-energy particle physics.

A secondary motivation is the prominence of symmetry in certain prediction problems from machine learning;

Whereas symmetry of a probability distribution manifests as invariance under a group of transformations, a conditional distribution may exhibit a form of symmetry that only appears in relationships between variables, known as
equivariance
: a transformation of
X
induces a corresponding transformation of
Y
.
Under certain conditions, detecting equivariance can be reduced to a problem for which existing group-invariance tests are applicable, but those conditions are not satisfied in many settings.
Tests for (marginal) distributional group symmetry are based on the fact that an invariant distribution factors into the independent product of the group’s “uniform distribution” and a distribution over group-induced equivalence classes known as orbits—a characterization that follows from the fact that marginal invariance can only hold with respect to compact groups. The independence between group transformation and orbit is key to the computational and statistical simplicity of tests for marginal invariance.
In contrast, conditional symmetries do not have such a convenient characterization, in part because they may hold with respect to a non-compact group, for which a uniform distribution does not exist.
As a consequence, designing tests for conditional symmetry requires overcoming new technical and statistical challenges.

Contributions

The present work initiates the study of hypothesis testing for conditional group symmetry and formulates a general framework for such tests based on a single independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) sample.
In order to do so, we
develop a number of novel characterizations of the invariance and equivariance of a conditional distribution under the action of a locally compact group.
The tests we develop can be used to detect the presence or absence of a particular conditional symmetry in data, with that symmetry specified by hypothesis.
The tests also may be used as model-checking criteria for models meant to exhibit a symmetry.

In addition to a general formulation of symmetry-based conditional randomization tests for which we prove Type I error control properties, we provide specific kernel-based instantiations of our tests, which allow the tests to be used on any data type for which a kernel can be defined. The testing procedure approximates the conditional distribution of the estimated maximum mean discrepancy between two samples from a suitable null hypothesis distribution, conditioned on certain symmetry-induced statistics of the observations. Using this test, we derive a finite-sample lower bound on the test’s power against non-symmetric alternatives, which also implies that the test is asymptotically consistent as the number of observations
n\to\infty
.
We explore the properties of our tests empirically on synthetic data and demonstrate their application to two problems from high-energy particle physics.

Related work

To our knowledge, general-purpose tests for conditional symmetry have not appeared in the existing literature.
The focus of this paper is on tests for conditional group symmetry, which should not be confused with the literature that tests conditional distributions for symmetry about zero (or some other value) for every value of the conditioning variable
(
Zheng, 1998
;
Bai and Ng, 2001
;
Su, 2006
, e.g.,)
.
In contrast to the present work, those tests do not consider symmetry in the relationship between the variables and are applicable to a disjoint set of problems from those considered here.
All mentions of conditional symmetry in this work will refer to the group invariance or equivariance of a conditional distribution, as we define in
Section

2.3
.

Some recent methods in machine learning aim to estimate symmetry groups from data
(
Krippendorf and
Syvaeri, 2020
;
Zhou, Knowles and
Finn, 2021
;
Desai, Nachman and Thaler, 2022
;
Dehmamy et al., 2021
;
Yang et al., 2023
)
.
They do not address the corresponding inferential questions, and the present work can be viewed as a complement to those estimation methods.

The only work that we are aware of that addresses any notion of testing for conditional symmetry is that of
Christie and Aston (2025)
, who proposed tests for the invariance of the conditional expectation
f(x)=\mathbb{E}[Y|X=x]
,
f\colon\mathbf{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, under the action of a specified group.
1
1

1

We note that the primary aim of
Christie and Aston (2025)
is to estimate the
largest
group under which
f
is invariant, which amounts to performing a collection of tests over a subgroup lattice of some candidate largest group.
In principle, our tests could be substituted into their procedure, though we do not address that problem here.

Testing for the invariance of the conditional expectation is not the same as testing the invariance of the conditional distribution, and those authors’ focus on the former objective leads them to formulate tests that rely on certain regularity properties of
f
(e.g., smoothness), for which there are no distributional analogues.
In contrast, the tests that we develop here are applicable to a different set of problems: testing for distributional symmetry, and with theoretical properties that do not rely on regularity of the conditional moments.
Moreover, the statistical properties of the tests in
(
Christie and Aston, 2025
)
are sensitive to the choice of a number of user-specified parameters. In particular, a sampling distribution on the group is chosen by heuristic. In the tests we develop, the required sampling distribution over the group and other test parameters are estimated from data in such a way that preserves the validity of the test while improving power against alternatives.

The methods we develop rely on group-theoretic inversion probability kernels, which have not been used widely in statistics or machine learning, likely owing to their relatively recent appearance in probability
(
Kallenberg, 2011
)
.
Since their appearance in a preliminary version of this work
(
Chiu and
Bloem-Reddy, 2023
)
, inversion kernels have been used to solve problems involving symmetry in machine learning
(
Cornish, 2024
;
Lawrence et al., 2025
;
Zhang et al., 2025
)
.
Using inversion kernels, we characterize conditional symmetry through a particular conditional independence relation, generalizing a result of
Bloem-Reddy and Teh (2020)
that holds in a much more limited setting. Those authors used the result to study equivariant neural networks and did not consider testing for conditional symmetry.
Deterministic inversions have been used more widely in the machine learning literature
(
Bloem-Reddy and Teh, 2020
;
Winter et al., 2022
;
Kaba et al., 2023
, e.g.,)
, and have counterparts in classical statistical problems involving groups
(
Fraser, 1961
;
Eaton, 1989
;
Eaton and
Sudderth, 1999
)
.

2.1
Groups and group actions

This work focuses on symmetries that can be encoded in the mathematics of group theory, which comprise many of the symmetries of interest in modern statistical, computational, and scientific problems. We assume a basic familiarity with groups and group actions; see
Section

A

Thorough treatments of classical group theoretic applications in statistics, along with relevant mathematical details, can be found in

We denote a group by

2.2
Testing for distributional invariance

The literature on hypothesis tests for group invariance of a probability measure is extensive, going back at least to
Hoeffding (1952)
, and even earlier for special cases. A textbook treatment of such tests when
\mathbf{G}
is finite can be found in
(
Lehmann and
Romano, 2005
, Ch. 15.2)
; although the technical details for infinite compact groups are more involved, the basic structure is similar.
As a reference point for the methods we will develop for testing conditional symmetry, we briefly review the basics of testing for distributional invariance, highlighting particular aspects that are important in the sequel.

A function
f
with domain
\mathbf{X}
is
\mathbf{G}
-invariant if
f(gx)=f(x)
, all
x\in\mathbf{X},g\in\mathbf{G}
. Observe that a
\mathbf{G}
-invariant function is constant on each orbit.
This specializes to probability measures.

Definition 2.1
.

A probability measure

-invariant
if
g_{*}P(A)=P(A)

,
A\in\mathbf{S}_{\mathbf{X}}
.

When the group action is continuous,
\mathbf{G}
-invariance of a probability measure is possible only when
\mathbf{G}
is compact (a non-compact group acting continuously would induce non-compact orbits, which would not admit invariant probability measures).
For a specified compact group
\mathbf{G}
, consider the hypotheses

\displaystyle H_{0}\colon P\text{ is $\mathbf{G}$-invariant}\quad\text{versus}\quad H_{1}\colon P\text{ is not $\mathbf{G}$-invariant.}

(1)

\displaystyle\phi_{\alpha}(X)=\mathbbm{1}\{T(X)>q_{T(GX)}(1-\alpha)\}\;,

2.3
Symmetry of conditional distributions

The primary focus of this paper is on a different notion of probabilistic symmetry, pertaining to conditional distributions. Here, we review the basic concepts needed to describe the problem.
Suppose
\mathbf{G}
acts on
\mathbf{X}
and also on another set
\mathbf{Y}
; the group action may be different on each. A function
f\colon\mathbf{X}\to\mathbf{Y}
is
\mathbf{G}
-equivariant
if
f(gx)=gf(x)
, for all
x\in\mathbf{X},\ g\in\mathbf{G}
. In the case that
\mathbf{G}
acts trivially on
\mathbf{Y}
, equivariance specializes to invariance.

We say that
P_{X,Y}
is jointly
\mathbf{G}
-invariant if it is invariant in the sense of
Definition

2.1
extended to
\mathbf{G}
acting on
\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{Y}
.
In addition to joint invariance, we may define symmetry for a conditional distribution. We write the disintegration of
P_{X,Y}
as
P_{X,Y}=P_{X}\otimes P_{Y|X}
, where
P_{Y|X}
denotes a regular conditional probability of
Y
given
X
(i.e., a Markov probability kernel from
\mathbf{X}
to
\mathbf{Y}
).

Definition 2.2
.

The conditional distribution
P_{Y|X}
is
\mathbf{G}
-equivariant
if

\displaystyle P_{Y|X}(B\mid x)=P_{Y|X}(gB\mid gx)\;,\quad x\in\mathbf{X},\ B\in\mathbf{S}_{\mathbf{Y}},\ g\in\mathbf{G}\;.

P_{Y|X}
is
\mathbf{G}
-invariant
if

\displaystyle P_{Y|X}(B\mid gx)=P_{Y|X}(B\mid x)\;,\quad x\in\mathbf{X},\ B\in\mathbf{S}_{\mathbf{Y}},\ g\in\mathbf{G}\;.

We use the term “
\mathbf{G}
-symmetric” as a catch-all for either of the conditional symmetries in
Definition

2.2
.
As a simple example of equivariance, consider a case in which
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is conditionally Gaussian-distributed and is acted on by
\mathbb{T}_{d}
, the locally compact group of
d
-dimensional translations.

Example 1
.

For random vectors
X
,
Y
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, suppose that
Y|X\sim\mathsf{N}(X,\Sigma)
, where
\Sigma
is some fixed covariance matrix.
It is straightforward to check that
P_{Y|X}
is
\mathbb{T}_{d}
-equivariant.
If
\varepsilon\sim\mathsf{N}(0,\Sigma)
with
\varepsilon{\;\perp\!\!\!\perp\;}X
so that
Y\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm d}}}{{=}}X+\varepsilon
, then for any
g_{v}\in\mathbb{T}_{d}
where
g_{v}x\coloneqq v+x
for
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle P_{Y|X}(g_{v}Y\in A\mid X)=P_{Y|X}(v+X+\varepsilon\in A\mid X)=P_{Y|X}(Y\in A\mid g_{v}X)\;.

If, instead,
Y|X\sim\mathsf{N}(1_{d},\Sigma)
, where
1_{d}
is the
d
-dimensional vector of ones, then
P_{Y|X}
is
\mathbb{T}_{d}
-invariant. In this example, conditional invariance is equivalent to independence. That is not a coincidence, but as we establish in
Theorem

3.1
, is a consequence of the transitivity of
\mathbb{T}_{d}
acting on
\mathbf{X}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Similar to the hypotheses (
1
) in testing distributional invariance, the primary aim of this paper is to test the hypotheses

\displaystyle\begin{split}H_{0}\colon P_{Y|X}\text{ is $\mathbf{G}$-symmetric}\qquad\text{versus}\qquad H_{1}\colon P_{Y|X}\text{ is not $\mathbf{G}$-symmetric.}\end{split}

(3)

In the setting of distributional invariance from
Section

2.2
, the test properties stem from the decomposition
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm d}}}{{=}}G\rho(X)
with
G{\;\perp\!\!\!\perp\;}X
and
G\sim\lambda
. To test for conditional symmetry in a similar fashion, we require an analogous decomposition of the joint distribution of
(X,Y)
in terms of a random element
\Gamma\in\mathbf{G}
, to be shared between
X
and
Y
.
The conditional setting presents new challenges, however.
Conditional symmetries can hold with respect to non-compact groups, as in
Example

1
. Hence, in contrast to tests for distributional invariance, tests for conditional symmetry cannot simply rely on the sampling from a normalized Haar measure, which does not exist for non-compact groups.
Moreover, because equivariance encodes systematic regularities in the relationship between variables, the required random group element
\Gamma
cannot be independent from
X
and
Y
.
In short, whereas the test for distributional invariance required only a maximal invariant and a uniformly random group element, tests for conditional symmetry must make use of additional structure arising from the group actions.

3
Characterizations of conditional symmetry

In addition to orbit selectors, the tests we will develop rely on a technique known as
group inversion
.
3
3

3

For technical details on measurability issues that can arise, the interested reader may refer to
Section

A.1
. In particular, measurable versions of the objects defined below may not exist. Throughout, we assume that the group action is proper so that conditions needed for measurability are satisfied.

Recall from
Section

2.2
that an orbit selector
\rho
maps an element
x
to its orbit representative,
\rho(x)=r_{x}
. This is well-defined even for non-compact groups, subject to the regularity condition of proper action.
The properties that we describe below do not depend on which elements are chosen as the representatives, but the specific subsequent functions and kernels will be relative to that choice.

Recall that the stabilizer subgroup of
x
is
\mathbf{G}_{x}=\{g\in\mathbf{G}:gx=x\}
, and that the action is free if
\mathbf{G}_{x}=\{\textrm{id}\}
for all
x\in\mathbf{X}
.
A
\mathbf{G}
-equivariant function
{\psi^{\circ}}\colon\mathbf{X}\to\mathbf{G}
is called a
representative inversion
if
{\psi^{\circ}}(x)\rho(x)=x
for all
x\in\mathbf{X}
.
As the name suggests,
{\psi^{\circ}}
returns the element of
\mathbf{G}
that moves, or “inverts”, the representative
\rho(x)
to
x
; the inverse group element
{\psi^{\circ}}(x)^{-1}
moves
x
to
\rho(x)
.
In order for
{\psi^{\circ}}
to be uniquely defined, the group action must be free.
If it is not, an equivariant
inversion probability kernel
(inversion kernel for short)
P_{\Psi|X}\colon\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{S}_{\mathbf{G}}\to[0,1]
can be used in place of
{\psi^{\circ}}
.
If
\Psi|X\sim P_{\Psi|X}
, then
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm a.s.}}}{{=}}\Psi\rho(X)
.
At a high level, one may think of the inversion kernel
P_{\Psi|X}
as the uniform distribution on the left coset
g\mathbf{G}_{\rho(X)}
, where
g\rho(X)=X
. Assuming that the group action is proper (see
Section

A.1
) ensures that the left coset is compact and a uniform distribution is well-defined.
When the action is free, the inversion kernel simplifies to
P_{\Psi|X}({\,\vbox{\hbox{$\bullet$}}\,}|x)=\delta_{{\psi^{\circ}}(x)}({\,\vbox{\hbox{$\bullet$}}\,})
.

Figure

2
visualizes these structures for
\mathrm{SO}(2)
and
\mathrm{SO}(3)
. The main difference is that the stabilizer subgroup of
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is trivial while that of
x\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is not. In some cases, a representative inversion can still be (non-uniquely) defined when the action is not free (see
Example

2
), in which case an equivalent inversion kernel can be defined as
P_{\Psi|X}^{\prime}({\,\vbox{\hbox{$\bullet$}}\,}|x)\coloneqq P_{\Psi|X}({\psi^{\circ}}(x)^{-1}{\,\vbox{\hbox{$\bullet$}}\,}\mid\rho(x))
.

The restricted Lorentz group is a non-compact, non-commutative, connected real Lie group (a differentiable manifold) that consists of transformations

For

3.1
Equivariance as conditional independence

Equivariant Markov kernels arise naturally in the disintegration of jointly invariant measures.
Under general conditions, a measure is jointly invariant if and only if it disintegrates into an invariant marginal measure and an equivariant Markov kernel.
A measure-theoretic proof of this fact is provided by
Kallenberg (2017, Theorem 7.6)
.

Bloem-Reddy and Teh (2020)
connected this to conditional independence, showing that for compact
\mathbf{G}
acting on
\mathbf{X}
such that a measurable representative inversion
{\psi^{\circ}}
exists, and
\mathbf{G}
-invariant
P_{X}
,
P_{Y|X}
is
\mathbf{G}
-equivariant if and only if

\displaystyle X{\;\perp\!\!\!\perp\;}{\psi^{\circ}}(X)^{-1}Y\mid M(X)\;.

(5)

Informally, the relation in (
5
) says that given knowledge of the orbit through a maximal invariant
M(X)
, the distribution of the transformed variable
{\psi^{\circ}}(X)^{-1}Y
does not depend on the particular value
X
takes on its orbit.

Figure

3
illustrates this intuition for the simple example of 2D rotations: the aligned conditional distributions of
Y|X=x
for different values of
x
on the same orbit should be the same. If they are not, additional dependence between
Y
and
X
is indicated.

Fig 3:
Three random samples of
Y\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
(triangle) drawn conditionally on
X\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
(circle). If the conditional distribution
P_{Y|X}
is
\mathrm{SO}(2)
-equivariant, then
P_{Y|X}({\psi^{\circ}}(x)^{-1}{\,\vbox{\hbox{$\bullet$}}\,}|x)
is the same for all samples.

The assumptions for (
5
) in
(
Bloem-Reddy and Teh, 2020
)

are somewhat restrictive, as they require
\mathbf{G}
to be compact and
P_{X}
to be invariant. In many situations, one or both of those assumptions will not hold.
The following theorem substantially generalizes (
5
), making it applicable to non-compact
\mathbf{G}
and non-invariant
P_{X}
. Moreover, it does not require the existence of a representative inversion
{\psi^{\circ}}(x)
, so that the action of
\mathbf{G}
on
\mathbf{X}
may not be free.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\mathbf{G}
be a lcscH group acting on each of
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
, the action on
\mathbf{X}
proper, with measurable inversion kernel
P_{\Psi|X}
.
The conditional distribution
P_{Y|X}
is
\mathbf{G}
-equivariant if and only if,

\displaystyle(\Psi,X){\;\perp\!\!\!\perp\;}\Psi^{-1}Y\mid M(X)\;,\quad\text{with}\quad\Psi\mid X\sim P_{\Psi|X}\;.

(6)

The proof can be found in
Section

C.1
. We note the following special cases. If there exists a representative inversion function
{\psi^{\circ}}\colon\mathbf{X}\to\mathbf{G}
, then condition (
6
) reduces to (
5
).
If the action of
\mathbf{G}
on
\mathbf{Y}
is trivial, then (
6
) reduces to
X{\;\perp\!\!\!\perp\;}Y\mid M(X)
.
Finally, if the action of
\mathbf{G}
on
\mathbf{X}
is transitive, as in
Example

1
, then (
6
) specializes to the (unconditional) independence.

Theorem

3.1
implies that a test for the hypotheses (
3
)
can be performed using a test for the conditional independence in (
6
).
If the action of
\mathbf{G}
on
\mathbf{X}
is transitive, then
\mathbf{X}
has a single orbit and the test simplifies to a test for (unconditional) independence.
In principle, any general-purpose test for conditional independence may be used.
However, it is known that any test of level
\alpha
over the entire null hypothesis class has no power against any alternative
(
Shah and Peters, 2020
)
.
The high-level solution is to restrict the null hypothesis class, which is commonly accomplished through problem-specific assumptions
(
Shah and Peters, 2020
;
Kim et al., 2022
)
or modeling some parts of the relevant conditional distributions
(
Zhang et al., 2011
;
Pogodin et al., 2024
)
.

3.2
Distributional identities for conditional randomization

One broadly useful set of tools for testing conditional independence are conditional randomization tests
(
Candès et al., 2018
;
Berrett et al., 2019
;
Liu et al., 2022
)
, in which a suitable randomization of the data is used to generate a test that is similar in spirit to
Algorithm

1
.
This motivates a refinement of the characterization of conditional symmetry in
Theorem

3.1
, on which we will build a conditional randomization test in the next section. The basic idea is that for a pair
(X,Y)
, the “centered” variables
\rho(X)
and
\tilde{Y}\coloneqq\Psi^{-1}Y
,
with
\Psi|X\sim P_{\Psi|X}
,
can be used to obtain a randomized pair
(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm d}}}{{=}}(X,Y)
. The randomization involves sampling one or both of

\displaystyle\tilde{Y}\mid\rho(X)\sim P_{\tilde{Y}|R}\;,\quad\Gamma\mid\rho(X)\sim P_{\Gamma|R}\;,

(7)

with
\tilde{Y}{\;\perp\!\!\!\perp\;}\Gamma\mid\rho(X)
.
Here,
\Gamma
denotes the random variable with conditional distribution

\displaystyle P_{\Gamma|R}({\,\vbox{\hbox{$\bullet$}}\,}\mid r)=\int_{\mathbf{X}}P_{\Psi|X}({\,\vbox{\hbox{$\bullet$}}\,}\mid x^{\prime})P_{X|R}(dx^{\prime}\mid r)\;,

(8)

where
P_{X|R}
is the conditional distribution of
X
given an orbit, represented by the random representative
R
(or more generally, any maximal invariant
M
).
We refer to
P_{\Gamma|R}
as the
group-orbit kernel
as it is the distribution on
\mathbf{G}
induced by restricting
P_{X}
to the orbit of
R
.
The group-orbit kernel has the property that if
\Gamma|\rho(X)\sim P_{\Gamma|R}
, then
(\rho(X),X)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm d}}}{{=}}(\rho(X),\Gamma\rho(X))
. (See
Lemma

A.2
for a proof.)

In
Section

C.2
, we prove a number of distributional identities involving these randomizations and that underpin the tests we construct in
Section

4
.
In particular, with
\Gamma
sampled as in (
7
),
P_{Y|X}
is
\mathbf{G}
-equivariant if and only if

\displaystyle(\rho(X),\tilde{Y},X,Y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm d}}}{{=}}(\rho(X),\tilde{Y},\Gamma\rho(X),\Gamma\tilde{Y})\;.

(9)

This identity says that if we are able to sample
\Gamma
from the correct distribution conditional on
\rho(X)
, then we can test for
\mathbf{G}
-equivariance by testing for equality in distribution between the data and the randomization
(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\coloneq(\Gamma\rho(X),\Gamma\tilde{Y})
.
Crucially, the randomization (and therefore the test) can be conditional on part of the observed data:
\rho(X)
and
\tilde{Y}
.
Conditioning restricts the null hypothesis set to agree with that part of the data, and therefore sidesteps the result of
Shah and Peters (2020)
.
Moreover, the identity (
9
) specializes to

\displaystyle(\rho(X),\tilde{Y},Y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm d}}}{{=}}(\rho(X),\tilde{Y},\Gamma\tilde{Y})\;,

(10)

which implies that as long as
\mathbf{G}
acts non-trivially on
\mathbf{Y}
, the distributional comparison can be carried out using only observed
Y
and randomized
Y^{\prime}=\Gamma\tilde{Y}
.

In
Section

4
, we describe how valid tests for conditional symmetry can be implemented for each of these cases.

Consider random vectors

and

\Gamma\rho(X)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm d}}}{{=}}X

Example 5

(Continuation of

.

The Standard Model of physics is invariant under the Lorentz group, and violations of

High-energy quarks produced in collisions such as those at the LHC quickly decay through a cascading process of gluon emission into collimated sprays of stable hadrons, which are subatomic particles that can be detected and measured

. Observe that this is not distributional invariance, because
gX\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm d}}}{{\neq}}X

describes how to sample from the inversion kernel, so if samples can be generated from

4
Randomization tests for conditional group symmetry

The distributional identities in
Section

3.2
provide the foundation for tests of conditional symmetry, in the form of conditional randomization tests (CRTs).
The basic idea is as follows. With a set of observations
(X,Y)_{1:n}
, generate
B
randomization sets
(X,Y)_{1:n}^{(b)}
as

\displaystyle(X,Y)_{i}^{(b)}\coloneq(\mathsf{\Gamma}_{i}^{(b)}\rho(X_{i}),\mathsf{\Gamma}_{i}^{(b)}\mathsf{\tilde{Y}}_{i}^{(b)})\;,\quad b=1,\dotsc,B,\ \ i=1,\dotsc,n.

The distributional identities in

Proposition

C.1
indicate that the randomization can be implemented in three distinct ways by conditioning on observed data differently:

R1.

Condition on
\rho(X)_{1:n}
and
\tilde{Y}_{1:n}
, with
\tilde{Y}_{i}=\Psi^{-1}_{i}Y_{i}
, and where
\Psi_{i}
is a representative inversion if one exists or is otherwise sampled from the inversion kernel and fixed. Set
\mathsf{\tilde{Y}}_{i}^{(b)}=\tilde{Y}_{i}
and sample
\mathsf{\Gamma}_{i}^{(b)}|\rho(X_{i})\sim P_{\Gamma|R}
.

R2.

Condition on
\rho(X)_{1:n}
and
\Psi_{1:n}
, where the latter is a representative inversion if one exists or is otherwise sampled from the inversion kernel and fixed. Set
\mathsf{\Gamma}_{i}^{(b)}=\Psi_{i}
and sample
\mathsf{\tilde{Y}}_{i}^{(b)}|\rho(X_{i})\sim P_{\tilde{Y}|R}
.

R3.

Condition on
\rho(X)_{1:n}
. Sample both
\mathsf{\Gamma}_{i}^{(b)}|\rho(X_{i})\sim P_{\Gamma|R}
and
\mathsf{\tilde{Y}}_{i}^{(b)}|\rho(X_{i})\sim P_{\tilde{Y}|R}
.

In
Section

D.1
, we show that in settings where a representative inversion
{\psi^{\circ}}
can be defined despite the group action not being free, randomizing to
\Psi
is not necessary in order for the tests to have the appropriate level.

Each of the randomization procedures above yields a different test; we differentiate between them by indicating what statistic of
(X,Y)_{1:n}
is conditioned on, denoted
S_{1:n}\coloneq S(X,Y)_{1:n}
. For example, in
R1
,
S_{i}=(\rho(X_{i}),\tilde{Y}_{i})
. Carrying out the appropriate randomization for each
i=1,\dotsc,n
and repeating
B
times, the CRT with test statistic
T
estimates a conditional
p
-value as

\displaystyle p_{B}\coloneqq\frac{1+\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathbbm{1}\left\{T\left((X,Y)_{1:n}^{(b)}\right)\geq T\left((X,Y)_{1:n}\right)\right\}}{B+1}\;.

(11)

The decision to reject the null hypothesis is taken if
p_{B}
is less than or equal to a specified significance
\alpha\in[0,1]
. The test statistic
T
should be some signal of distributional equality, with small values constituting evidence in favor of the null hypothesis.
For example, we implement our tests in the subsequent sections using kernel maximum mean discrepancy (MMD).

It is straightforward to show via
Proposition

C.1
and its corollaries that the resulting CRT based on
B
randomization sets is a level-
\alpha
test.
Moreover, as
B\to\infty
, the CRT produces a consistent conditional
p
-value, in the following sense. Under the null hypothesis that
P_{Y|X}
is
\mathbf{G}
-equivariant, if
(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm d}}}{{=}}(X,Y)
, then for
P
-almost every sequence
s_{1:n}
, as
B\to\infty
,

\displaystyle p_{B}\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm a.s.}}}{{\to}}\;\mathbb{P}\left\{T\left((X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})_{1:n}\right))\geq T\left((X,Y)_{1:n}\right)\mid S^{\prime}_{1:n}=S_{1:n}=s_{1:n}\right\}\;.

(12)

Proposition

4.1
formalizes the result generally in terms of a test based on the identity (
9

4.1
Approximate conditional randomization

In

As a simple alternative to the conditional randomization tests described in the previous sections, consider generating

5
Kernel-based hypothesis tests for conditional group symmetry

In the experiments reported in
Section

6
, we test for conditional symmetry using a kernel-based test statistic of observations denoted for convenience by
Z_{1:n}
. For tests based on identity (
9
) (
Proposition

C.1
),
Z_{i}=(X,Y)_{i}
, with a kernel defined on the product space
\mathbf{Z}=\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{Y}
. For tests based on (
10
) (
Corollary

C.2
),
Z_{i}=Y_{i}
, with a kernel defined on
\mathbf{Z}=\mathbf{Y}
. We provide a brief overview of kernel-based hypothesis testing and other related ideas in this section.
See
Christmann and
Steinwart (2008)
for a thorough treatment of the theory of kernels, and
Muandet et al. (2017)
for a review of their uses in statistics and machine learning.

Let
k_{\sigma}\colon\mathbf{Z}\times\mathbf{Z}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a positive semidefinite kernel with parameter
\sigma
, corresponding to the Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS)
\mathcal{H}
. If the kernel is measurable and bounded, then the
kernel mean embedding
(KME) of a probability measure
P
on
\mathbf{Z}
is the unique element
\mu_{P}({\,\vbox{\hbox{$\bullet$}}\,})\coloneqq\int_{\mathbf{Z}}k_{\sigma}(z,{\,\vbox{\hbox{$\bullet$}}\,})P(dz)\in\mathcal{H}
such that
\mathbb{E}_{P}[f(Z)]=\langle f,\mu_{P}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
for all
f\in\mathcal{H}

(
Gretton et al., 2012
)
.
A kernel is said to be
characteristic
if the map
P\mapsto\mu_{P}
from
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{Z})
into
\mathcal{H}
is injective, meaning that the embedding for each probability measure is unique
(
Sriperumbudur
et al., 2010
)
.
KME-based hypothesis tests compare distributions through their KMEs
(
Gretton et al., 2007
;
Gretton et al., 2012
)
.
The test statistic in KME-based tests is an estimator of the squared
maximum mean discrepancy
(MMD),

\mathrm{MMD}_{\sigma}^{2}(P_{1},P_{2})\coloneqq\left\|\mu_{P_{1}}-\mu_{P_{2}}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}
.
If the kernel
k_{\sigma}
is characteristic,
\mathrm{MMD}_{\sigma}^{2}
is a metric on
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{Z})
; hence
\mathrm{MMD}_{\sigma}(P,Q)=0
if and only if
P=Q
.
If a non-characteristic kernel is used instead,
\mathrm{MMD}_{\sigma}^{2}
may be unable to separate some distinct elements of
\mathcal{P}(\mathbf{Z})
, which would not affect the level of the test, but may reduce the power under some alternatives.

5.1
Adaptive kernel test statistics

It is known that the power of kernel MMD-based tests is highly dependent on the chosen kernel parameters, such as the bandwidth
\sigma

(
Ramdas et al., 2015
)
.
Several solutions have been proposed, in the form of data-based heuristics
(
Gretton et al., 2012
;
Garreau, Jitkrittum and
Kanagawa, 2017
)
and, more recently, adaptive methods
(
Schrab et al., 2023
;
Biggs, Schrab and Gretton, 2024
;
Cárcamo, Cuevas and
Rodríguez, 2024
)
.
In our experiments, when using a kernel with a parameter (e.g., the Gaussian kernel), we use two test statistics based on solutions proposed in the adaptive kernel literature.
The first adapts the unnormalized FUSE statistic

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathrm{FUSE}}_{\omega}(Z_{1:n},Z_{1:n}^{\prime})=\frac{1}{\omega}\log\left(\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\exp\left(\omega\widehat{\text{MMD}}^{2}_{\sigma_{\ell}}(Z_{1:n},Z_{1:n}^{\prime})\right)\right)\;,

where, following
Biggs, Schrab and Gretton (2024)
,
\omega=\sqrt{n(n-1)}
. The statistic can be interpreted as taking a softmax across estimated MMD
2
values computed using different kernels
k_{\sigma_{\ell}}
.
The bandwidth values
\{\sigma_{1},\dotsc,\sigma_{L}\}
in the original work
(
Biggs, Schrab and Gretton, 2024
)
are quantiles of the pairwise Euclidean distances in the data. In the context of a permutation test, the empirical measure of the data is a maximal invariant, so any function of it (such as quantiles) can be used in the context of a test for permutation-invariance, as in
Section

2.2
.
Our adaptation to CRTs for conditional group symmetry, which satisfies the requirements of
Proposition

4.1
, is obtained by replacing the quantiles of the observation with quantiles of variables contained in the conditioning set
S_{1:n}
, such as the pairwise Euclidean distances in
\tilde{Y}_{1},\dotsc,\tilde{Y}_{n}
.

The second test statistic we use is an adaptation of the
supremum kernel distance
(SK,
Cárcamo, Cuevas and
Rodríguez 2024
) given by

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathrm{SK}}(Z_{1:n},Z_{1:n}^{\prime})=\sup_{\ell\in\{1,\dotsc,L\}}\widehat{\text{MMD}}^{2}_{\sigma_{\ell}}(Z_{1:n},Z_{1:n}^{\prime})\;.

Similar to the FUSE statistic above,
\sigma_{\ell}
can depend on
S_{1:n}
, and using this procedure remains valid due to the conditioning argument in
Proposition

4.1
.

Analogous versions of the power lower bound in
Theorem

5.1
hold for the FUSE and SK statistics. For brevity, their statements and proofs can be found in
Section

C.4
.

6
Experiments

We evaluate our proposed tests on synthetic examples and on realistic state-of-the-art simulated data used in particle physics.
In all of our experiments, we sample
n
data points from a distribution or dataset and perform a test for conditional symmetry with respect to a specified group.
We repeat this procedure over
N=1000
simulations for each test setting and report the proportion of simulations in which the test rejected, which is an estimate of the test size (resp. power) when the distribution has (resp. does not have) the specified conditional symmetry.
With
N=1000
simulations, estimates are precise up to approximately
\pm 0.016
.
We set the test level to be
\alpha=0.05
in all of our experiments.
Except where specified otherwise, we use a single randomized comparison set (reuse = true) for estimating the
p
-value in our tests.

We default to aggregating Gaussian and Laplace kernels via the FUSE and SK test statistics for continuous data.
To compute the bandwidths
\{\sigma_{1},\dotsc,\sigma_{L}\}
, we follow a procedure similar to the one described by
Biggs, Schrab and Gretton (2024)
.
We first compute the distances
\{\|\tilde{Y}_{i}-\tilde{Y}_{j}^{\prime}\|_{2}\}_{i,j}
and
\{\|\tilde{Y}_{i}-\tilde{Y}_{j}^{\prime}\|_{1}\}_{i,j}
for the Gaussian and Laplace kernels, respectively.
We then compute half of the 5% quantile and two times the 95% quantile for each set, and take the
L=10
bandwidth values to be the discrete uniform interpolation of those two values. All estimates of MMD use the U-statistic.

All experiments were implemented in Julia (version 1.6.1) and run on a high-performance computing allocation with 4 CPU cores and 16 GB of RAM.
Additional experiments and experimental details can be found in
Section

E
, including an experiment showing how our tests for conditional symmetry can be used for checking learned symmetry in predictive models (
Section

E.9
).
Associated computer code can be found on GitHub.

A
Additional background on groups

A
group

\mathbf{G}
is a set with a binary operation
\cdot
that satisfies the associativity, identity, and inverse axioms. We denote the identity element by
id
.
For notational convenience, we write
g_{1}g_{2}=g_{1}\cdot g_{2}
for
g_{1},g_{2}\in\mathbf{G}
.
A group
\mathbf{G}
is said to be measurable if the group operations
g\mapsto g^{-1}
and
(g_{1},g_{2})\mapsto g_{1}g_{2}
are
\mathbf{S}_{\mathbf{G}}
-measurable, where
\mathbf{S}_{\mathbf{G}}
is a
\sigma
-algebra of subsets of
\mathbf{G}
.
For
A\subseteq\mathbf{G}
and
g\in\mathbf{G}
, we write
gA=\{gh:h\in A\}
and
Ag=\{hg:h\in A\}
. A measure
\nu
on
\mathbf{G}
is said to be
left-invariant
if
\nu(gA)=\nu(A)
for all
A\in\mathbf{S}_{\mathbf{G}}
, and
right-invariant
if
\nu(Ag)=\nu(A)
.
When
\mathbf{G}
is lcscH, there exist left- and right-invariant
\sigma
-finite measures
\lambda_{\mathbf{G}}
and
\tilde{\lambda}_{\mathbf{G}}
, known as
left-
and
right-Haar measures
, respectively, that are unique up to scaling
(
Folland, 2016
, Ch. 2.2)
.

A
group action
is a function
\Phi\colon\mathbf{G}\times\mathbf{X}\to\mathbf{X}
that satisfies
\Phi(\textrm{id},x)=x
and
\Phi(g_{1}g_{2},x)=\Phi(g_{1},\Phi(g_{2},x))
for all
x\in\mathbf{X},g_{1},g_{2}\in\mathbf{G}
.
A group
\mathbf{G}
acts measurably on a set
\mathbf{X}
if the group action
(g,x)\mapsto gx
is measurable relative to
\mathbf{S}_{\mathbf{G}}\otimes\mathbf{S}_{\mathbf{X}}
and
\mathbf{S}_{\mathbf{X}}
.
Throughout, all actions are assumed to be continuous (and therefore measurable), and for convenience we simply say that
\mathbf{G}
acts on
\mathbf{X}
, writing
gx=\Phi(g,x)
.
This standard notation hides the particular action
\Phi
associated with
gx
, and it is important not to lose track of that fact.

The
stabilizer subgroup
of
x\in\mathbf{X}
is the subset of group actions that leave
x
invariant, denoted
\mathbf{G}_{x}=\{g\in\mathbf{G}:gx=x\}
.
The action is
free
(or
exact
) if
gx=x
implies that
g=\textrm{id}
, in which case
\mathbf{G}_{x}=\{\textrm{id}\}
for all
x\in\mathbf{X}
.
The
\mathbf{G}
-orbit
of
x\in\mathbf{X}
is the subset of all points in
\mathbf{X}
reachable through a group action in
\mathbf{G}
, denoted
O(x)=\{gx:g\in\mathbf{G}\}
.
Each orbit is an equivalence class of
\mathbf{X}
, where two points are equivalent if and only if they belong to the same orbit.
The stabilizer subgroups of the elements of an orbit are all conjugate; that is, if
gx=x^{\prime}
for
x\neq x^{\prime}
, then
g\mathbf{G}_{x}g^{-1}=\mathbf{G}_{x^{\prime}}
.
If
\mathbf{X}
has only one orbit, then the action is said to be
transitive
.

A.1
Proper group actions

In the analysis of probabilistic aspects of group actions, measurability issues can arise without regularity conditions.
The key regularity condition that we assume is that the group action is
proper
: there exists a strictly positive measurable function
h\colon\mathbf{X}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that
\int_{\mathbf{G}}h(gx)\lambda(dg)<\infty
for every
x\in\mathbf{X}

(
Kallenberg, 2007
)
.
This definition of proper group action is a non-topological version of the definition commonly used in previous work in the statistics literature
(
Eaton, 1989
;
Wijsman, 1990
;
McCormack and Hoff, 2023
, e.g.,)
, and only requires the existence of Haar measure. The common topological version defines
(g,x)\mapsto(gx,x)
as a proper map, i.e., the inverse image of each compact set in
\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{X}
is a compact set in
\mathbf{G}\times\mathbf{X}
. That definition implies the one we use; see
Kallenberg (2007)
for details.

A sufficient condition for proper group action is that
\mathbf{G}
is compact and acts continuously on
\mathbf{X}
.
When
\mathbf{G}
is non-compact, a group action can fail to be proper if
\mathbf{G}
is “too large” for
\mathbf{X}
in the sense that the stabilizer subgroups are non-compact. A class of non-compact group actions known to be proper are those of the isometry group of a non-compact Riemannian manifold.
In this work, we rely on the assumption of proper group actions to guarantee the existence of measurable orbit selectors and inversion kernels. The proposition below compiles some known useful properties of these objects.

To state the proposition, let
\nu
be any bounded measure on
(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{S}_{\mathbf{X}})
and let
\bar{\mathbf{S}}^{\nu}_{\mathbf{X}}
be the completion of
\mathbf{S}_{\mathbf{X}}
to include all subsets of
\nu
-null sets, and denote by
\bar{\nu}
the extension of
\nu
to
\bar{\mathbf{S}}^{\nu}_{\mathbf{X}}

(see, e.g.
Çinlar, 2011
, Proposition 1.3.10)
.
All statements of
\bar{\nu}
-measurability in the following proposition are with respect to
\bar{\mathbf{S}}^{\nu}_{\mathbf{X}}
, so that a set
A\subseteq\mathbf{X}
is
\bar{\nu}
-measurable if
A\in\bar{\mathbf{S}}^{\nu}_{\mathbf{X}}
.
Moreover, a function defined by a particular property is
\bar{\nu}
-measurable if it is measurable in the usual sense with respect to
\bar{\mathbf{S}}^{\nu}_{\mathbf{X}}
, and if the defining property holds with the possible exception of a
\bar{\nu}
-null set.
Such a function would also be
\bar{\upsilon}
-measurable for any measure
\upsilon\ll\nu
.

Proposition A.1
.

Let
\mathbf{G}
be a lcscH group acting continuously and properly on
\mathbf{X}
, and
\nu
any bounded measure on
\mathbf{X}
.
Then the following hold:

1.

The canonical projection
\pi\colon\mathbf{X}\to\mathbf{X}/\mathbf{G}
is a measurable maximal invariant. Any measurable
\mathbf{G}
-invariant function
f\colon\mathbf{X}\to\mathbf{Y}
can be written as
f=f^{*}\circ\pi
, for some measurable
f^{*}\colon\mathbf{X}/\mathbf{G}\to\mathbf{Y}
, and
f^{*}
is bijective if and only if
f^{*}\circ\pi
is a measurable maximal invariant.

2.

There exists a
\bar{\nu}
-measurable orbit selector
\rho\colon\mathbf{X}\to\mathbf{R}
, which is a maximal invariant statistic, and it induces a
\bar{\nu}
-measurable cross-section
\mathbf{R}
.

3.

For a fixed
\bar{\nu}
-measurable orbit selector
\rho
, there exists a unique
\bar{\nu}
-measurable inversion probability kernel
P_{\Psi|X}\colon\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{S}_{\mathbf{G}}\to[0,1]
with the following properties:

(a)

P_{\Psi|X}
is
\mathbf{G}
-equivariant: For all
g\in\mathbf{G},x\in\mathbf{X},B\in\mathbf{S}_{\mathbf{G}}
,
P_{\Psi|X}(B|gx)=P_{\Psi|X}(g^{-1}B|x)
.

(b)

For each
x\in\mathbf{X}
,
P_{\Psi|X}({\,\vbox{\hbox{$\bullet$}}\,}|\rho(x))
is normalized Haar measure on the stabilizer subgroup
\mathbf{G}_{\rho(x)}
.

(c)

For each
x\in\mathbf{X}
, if
\Psi|X=x\sim P_{\Psi|X}
, then
\Psi\rho(x)=x
with probability one.

(d)

If there is a
\bar{\nu}
-measurable representative inversion
{\psi^{\circ}}\colon\mathbf{X}\to\mathbf{G}
associated with
\rho
such that it satisfies
{\psi^{\circ}}(x)\rho(x)=x
and
{\psi^{\circ}}(gx)=g{\psi^{\circ}}(x)
for each
x\in\mathbf{X},g\in\mathbf{G}
, then
P_{\Psi|X}^{\prime}(B|x)=P_{\Psi|X}({\psi^{\circ}}(x)^{-1}B|\rho(x))
is an equivalent inversion kernel.
In particular, this holds when the action of
\mathbf{G}
on
\mathbf{X}
is free, in which case
\mathbf{G}_{\rho(x)}=\{\textrm{id}\}
and the inversion kernel is
\delta_{{\psi^{\circ}}(x)}
.

The measurability of the canonical projection is a result from functional analysis; see
Eaton (1989, Theorem 5.4)
for an extended statement and references. One implication is that
\pi
generates the invariant
\sigma
-algebra on
\mathbf{X}
, so that every invariant function can be written as a measurable function of it.
Items 2–3c follow directly from results of
Kallenberg (2011)
;
Kallenberg (2017)
on the existence of universally measurable versions of
\rho
and
P_{\Psi|X}
.
Item 3d follows from 3a and 3b.

We rely heavily on the group-orbit kernel defined in (
8
), where the following is claimed.

Lemma A.2
.

Let
\mathbf{G}
act properly on
\mathbf{X}
,
P_{\Gamma|R}
be as defined in (
8
), and
P_{X}
be any probability measure on
\mathbf{X}
. If
X\sim P_{X}
, then
(\rho(X),X)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm d}}}{{=}}(\rho(X),\Gamma\rho(X))
.

Proof.

For any measurable
f\colon\mathbf{R}\times\mathbf{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, by 3(c) of
Proposition

A.1
, standard disintegration of measure, and Fubini’s theorem,

\displaystyle\int f(\rho(x),x)P_{X}(dx)

\displaystyle=\int f(\rho(x),\Psi\rho(x))P_{\Psi|X}(d\Psi\mid x)P_{X}(dx)

\displaystyle=\int f(r,\Psi r)P_{\Psi|X}(d\Psi\mid x^{\prime})P_{X\mid R}(dx^{\prime}\mid r)(\rho_{*}P_{X})(dr)

\displaystyle=\int f(r,\gamma r)P_{\Gamma|R}(d\gamma\mid r)(\rho_{*}P_{X})(dr)

\displaystyle=\int f(\rho(x),\gamma\rho(x))P_{\Gamma|R}(d\gamma\mid\rho(x))P_{X}(dx)\;.

∎

B
Further background on testing for distributional invariance

As noted in
Section

2.2
, the literature appears to be missing a proof of the conditional level for the randomization test in the most general setting of a compact group acting properly, with possible non-trivial stabilizer subgroups.

For simplicity, we assume that the probability of ties is zero. If that is not the case (i.e., if
T
is supported on a discrete set), then a modified version with randomized tie-breaking yields a level-
\alpha
test
(
Hemerik and Goeman, 2018
, e.g.,)
.

Theorem B.1
.

Let
\mathbf{G}
be a compact group that acts on
\mathbf{X}
. Let
M
be any maximal invariant. Assume that
X_{1:n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm iid}}}{{\sim}}P
.
For test statistic
T
and the randomization test in
Algorithm

1
, assume that
\mathbb{P}\{T(X^{(b)}_{1:n})=T(X^{(b^{\prime})}_{1:n})\}=0
for
b\neq b^{\prime}
.
For any fixed
B\in\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha\in[0,1]
, if
P
is
\mathbf{G}
-invariant then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(p_{B}\leq\alpha\mid M(X)_{1:n}\right)=\frac{\lfloor\alpha(B+1)\rfloor}{B+1}\leq\alpha\;,\quad P\text{-a.s.}

The high-level idea of the proof is as follows.
It is known that any maximal invariant is a sufficient statistic for
\mathcal{P}^{\circ}(\mathbf{X})
, the set of
\mathbf{G}
-invariant probability distributions on
\mathbf{X}

(
Farrell, 1962
;
Dawid, 1985
;
Bloem-Reddy and Teh, 2020
)
. The use of sufficiency greatly simplifies the subsequent technical details, allowing for the application of standard results for Monte Carlo tests, and also exposing randomization tests for invariance to be co-sufficient sampling methods
(
Barber and Janson, 2022
)
.

The proof of
Theorem

B.1
relies on two auxiliary results. The first is a characterization of distributional invariance, which is well-studied in probability
(
Kallenberg, 2005
)
. See
Eaton (1989)
for an accessible treatment of the basic results and some of their applications in classical statistics. We summarize two characterizations of invariance that are needed below.

Lemma B.2
.

Let
\mathbf{G}
be a compact group acting on
\mathbf{X}
and
P
a probability measure on
\mathbf{X}
.
Let
\rho
be a measurable orbit selector.
With
X\sim P
, the following are equivalent.

I0.

P
is
\mathbf{G}
-invariant.

I1.

If
G\sim\lambda
with
G{\;\perp\!\!\!\perp\;}X
, then
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm d}}}{{=}}GX
.

I2.

If
G\sim\lambda
and
R\sim\rho_{*}P
with
G{\;\perp\!\!\!\perp\;}R
, then
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm d}}}{{=}}GR
.

Proof.

Suppose that
P
is
\mathbf{G}
-invariant. Then for any measurable
f\colon\mathbf{X}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{G}}f(gx)P(dx)\lambda(dg)=\int_{\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{G}}f(x)P(dx)\lambda(dg)=\int_{\mathbf{X}}f(x)P(dx)\;,

which shows that
Property

I0
implies
Property

I1
. For the converse, assume that
X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm d}}}{{=}}GX
. Then for any
h\in\mathbf{G}
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{X}}f(hx)P(dx)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{G}}f(hgx)P(dx)\lambda(dg)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbf{X}\times\mathbf{G}}f(gx)P(dx)\lambda(dg)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbf{X}}f(x)P(dx)\;,

where the second equality follows from the invariance of Haar measure.

The proof that
Property

I2
is equivalent to
Property

I0
is more involved. An accessible proof can be found in
Eaton (1989, Theorems 4.3–4.4)
; see also
Kallenberg (2017, Theorem 7.15)
.
∎

We note that
Property

I2
holds even conditionally on
\rho(X)
. That is,

\displaystyle(\rho(X),X,G)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm d}}}{{=}}(\rho(X),G\rho(X),G)\;,

which implies that
{X|\rho(X)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm d}}}{{=}}G\rho(X)|\rho(X)}
.

One implication of this is that
\rho(X)
is a sufficient statistic for the set of
\mathbf{G}
-invariant probability measures on
\mathbf{X}

(
Farrell, 1962
;
Dawid, 1985
;
Bloem-Reddy and Teh, 2020
)
. (In fact, any maximal invariant is sufficient.) To state the result precisely, for each
x\in\mathbf{X}
, let
\Phi_{\rho(x)*}\lambda
denote the pushforward of Haar measure by the group action applied to
\rho(x)\in\mathbf{X}
.

Corollary B.3
.

Let
\mathbf{G}
be a compact group acting on
\mathbf{X}
, and
\mathcal{P}^{\circ}(\mathbf{X})
be the set of
\mathbf{G}
-invariant probability measures on
\mathbf{X}
. Then
\rho(X)
is a sufficient statistic for
\mathcal{P}^{\circ}(\mathbf{X})
. That is, for each
P\in\mathcal{P}^{\circ}(\mathbf{X})
,

\displaystyle P(X\in A\mid\rho(X))=\Phi_{\rho(X)*}\lambda(A)\;,\quad A\in\mathbf{S}_{\mathbf{X}}\;,\quad P\text{-a.s.}

The second auxiliary result establishes a useful identity for exchangeable sequences of random variables, used extensively in the literature on randomization tests.
A proof can be found in, e.g.,
Dufour (2006)
.
For simplicity, we assume that the probability of ties is zero, but that case can be handled with a randomized tie-breaking procedure described by
Dufour (2006)
;
Hemerik and Goeman (2018)
.

Lemma B.4

(
Dufour (2006)
, Proposition 2.2)
.

Let
S_{0},S_{1},\dotsc,S_{B}
be an exchangeable sequence of
\mathbb{R}
-valued random variables such that
\mathbb{P}(S_{i}=S_{j})=0
for
i\neq j
,
i,j\in\{0,\dotsc,B\}
. Define

\displaystyle p_{B}=\frac{1+\sum_{b=1}^{B}\mathbbm{1}\{S_{b}\geq S_{0}\}}{B+1}\;.

Then for any
\alpha\in[0,1]
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(p_{B}\leq\alpha)=\frac{\lfloor\alpha(B+1)\rfloor}{B+1}\;.

Proof of
Theorem

B.1
.

Due to the sufficiency of
\rho(X)
for
\mathcal{P}^{\circ}(\mathbf{X})
, the samples
X^{(b)}_{1:n}=(G^{(b)}_{1}X_{1},\dotsc,G^{(b)}_{n}X_{n})
are conditionally i.i.d. (and therefore conditionally exchangeable) given
\rho(X)_{1:n}
, with the same conditional distribution as
X_{1:n}
under
H_{0}
.
Consequently,
\left(T(X^{(b)}_{1:n})\right)_{b=0}^{B}
are conditionally i.i.d. given
\rho(X)_{1:n}
, with the same conditional distribution as
T(X_{1:n})
under the null.
It then follows from
Lemma

B.4
that (
2
) holds.

Proof of

The proof of
Theorem

5.1
builds on the analysis of
Sriperumbudur
et al. (2012)
on the empirical estimation of MMD. In particular, for RKHS
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{H}|_{1}=\{f\in\mathcal{H}:\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq 1\}
, the MMD for probability measures
P,Q
on
\mathbf{X}
can be written as

\displaystyle\mathrm{MMD}_{\sigma}^{2}

\displaystyle(P,Q)=\left(\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}|_{1}}\left|\int f(x)P(dx)-\int f(x)Q(dx)\right|\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}[k_{\sigma}(X,\tilde{X})]+\mathbb{E}[k_{\sigma}(X^{\prime},\tilde{X}^{\prime})]-2\mathbb{E}[k_{\sigma}(X,X^{\prime})]\;,\quad X,\tilde{X}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm iid}}}{{\sim}}P\;,X^{\prime},\tilde{X^{\prime}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm iid}}}{{\sim}}Q\;,

with empirical estimator

\displaystyle\mathrm{MMD}_{\sigma}^{2}(X_{1:n},X_{1:n}^{\prime})=\left(\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}|_{1}}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(f(X_{i})-f(X^{\prime}_{i}))\right|\right)^{2}\;,\quad X_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm iid}}}{{\sim}}P\;,\ X^{\prime}_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm iid}}}{{\sim}}Q\;.

The proof of
Theorem

5.1
relies on the following lemma, which is a special case of Proposition B.1 from
(
Sriperumbudur
et al., 2012
)
, which itself extends a version of Talagrand’s inequality
(
Christmann and
Steinwart, 2008
, Thm. 7.5 and A.9.1)
to apply to sequences of independent but not necessarily identically distributed random variables.

Lemma C.6
.

Let
M\geq 0
,
n\geq 1
. For each
i\in[n]
, let
(\mathbf{X},\mathcal{A},P_{i})
be a probability space. Let
\mathcal{F}
be a set of
\mathcal{A}
-measurable
\mathbb{R}
-valued functions. Let
\theta_{i}\colon\mathcal{F}\times\mathbf{X}\to\mathbb{R}
be bounded measurable functions, such that for each
f\in\mathcal{F}
,
i\in[n]
,

•

\int_{\mathbf{X}}\theta_{i}(f,x)P_{i}(dx)=0
;

•

\int_{\mathbf{X}}\theta_{i}(f,x)^{2}P_{i}(dx)\leq\varphi^{2}_{i}<\infty
; and

•

\|\theta_{i}(f,{\,\vbox{\hbox{$\bullet$}}\,})\|_{\infty}\leq M<\infty
.

Define
\mathbf{W}=\mathbf{X}^{n}
,
P=P_{1}\times\dotsb\times P_{n}
, and
H\colon\mathbf{W}\to\mathbb{R}
by

\displaystyle H(w)=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{n}\theta_{i}(f,x_{i})\right|\;,\quad w=(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{n})\;.

Then for all
\epsilon>0
,

\displaystyle P\left(w\in\mathbf{W}:H(w)-\mathbb{E}[H(W)]\geq\epsilon\right)\leq e^{-\frac{3}{2}\frac{\epsilon^{2}}{3a+\epsilon}}\;,

with
a=2M\mathbb{E}[H(W)]+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi^{2}_{i}
. Written differently, for all
\eta>0
,

\displaystyle P\left(w\in\mathbf{W}:H(w)\geq\mathbb{E}[H(W)]+\sqrt{2a\eta}+2\eta M/3\right)\leq e^{-\eta}\;.

In order to apply the lemma to our setting, observe that our MMD-based test statistic evaluated on two (conditionally) independent randomization samples
Z_{i}\sim P_{i}
and
Z_{n+i}\sim Q_{i}
can be written as, with
b_{i}=(-1)^{\mathbbm{1}\{n+1\leq i\leq 2n\}}
,

\displaystyle T(Z_{1:n},Z_{(n+1):2n})=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}|_{1}}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{2n}b_{i}f(Z_{i})\right|=\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1,j=1}^{2n}b_{i}b_{j}k_{\sigma}(Z_{i},Z_{j})\right)^{1/2}\;.

Since there is a different distribution for each
i
, consider the “population” version of the statistic to be

\displaystyle T(P_{1:n},Q_{1:n})=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}|_{1}}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{2n}b_{i}\mathbb{E}[f(Z_{i})]\right|=\left(\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1,j=1}^{2n}b_{i}b_{j}\mathbb{E}[k_{\sigma}(Z_{i},Z_{j})]\right)^{1/2}\;,

which is equal to
0
if
P_{i}=Q_{i}
for each
i\in[n]
.
Finally, observe that with

\displaystyle\theta_{i}(f,z)=(-1)^{\mathbbm{1}\{n+1\leq i\leq 2n\}}\frac{1}{n}\bigg(f(z)-\int f(z^{\prime})P_{i}(dz^{\prime})\bigg)\;,\quad f\in\mathcal{H}|_{1}\;,

(20)

the conditions of
Lemma

C.6
are satisfied, with
\varphi_{i}^{2}\leq\kappa^{2}/n^{2}
and
M\leq 4\kappa/n
.

We will use it to bound

\displaystyle\left|T(Z_{1:n},Z_{(n+1):2n})-T(P_{1:n},Q_{1:n})\right|

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{1}{n}\left|\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}\mid_{1}}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{2n}(-1)^{\mathbbm{1}\{n+1\leq i\leq 2n\}}f(Z_{i})\right|-\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}\mid_{1}}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{2n}(-1)^{\mathbbm{1}\{n+1\leq i\leq 2n\}}\mathbb{E}[f(Z_{i})]\right|\right|

\displaystyle\quad\leq\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}\mid_{1}}\frac{1}{n}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{2n}(-1)^{\mathbbm{1}\{n+1\leq i\leq 2n\}}\left(f(Z_{i})-\mathbb{E}[f(Z_{i})]\right)\right|

(21)

\displaystyle\quad=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}\mid_{1}}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{2n}\theta_{i}(f,Z_{i})\right|\;.

We will make repeated use of the following upper bound on the expectation of
H(W)
, the proof of which is standard, for example,
(
Sriperumbudur
et al., 2012
, Appendix D)
.

Lemma C.7
.

Let
\theta_{i}
be as defined in (
20
) and
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{H}|_{1}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[H(W)]\leq 2\kappa\sqrt{\frac{2}{n}}\;.

Proof of
Lemma

C.7
.

For independent
Z_{i},Z_{i}^{\prime}\sim P_{i}
and
Z_{n+i},Z_{n+i}^{\prime}\sim Q_{i}
, and independent Rademacher random variables
R_{i}
,
i\in[n]
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[H(W)]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}|_{1}}\left|\sum_{i=1}^{2n}\theta_{i}(f,Z_{i})\right|\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}|_{1}}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{2n}(-1)^{\mathbbm{1}\{n+1\leq i\leq 2n\}}\left(f(Z_{i})-\mathbb{E}[f(Z_{i})]\right)\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}|_{1}}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{2n}(-1)^{\mathbbm{1}\{n+1\leq i\leq 2n\}}\left(f(Z_{i})-f(Z_{i}^{\prime})\right)\right|\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}|_{1}}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{2n}R_{i}\left(f(Z_{i})-f(Z_{i}^{\prime})\right)\right|\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}|_{1}}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{2n}R_{i}f(Z_{i})\right|\right]\;,

where the first inequality follows from Jensen’s inequality.
Using RKHS identities and the boundedness of the kernel
(
Sriperumbudur
et al., 2012
, see Appendix D,)
yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}|_{1}}\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{2n}R_{i}f(Z_{i})\right|\right]\leq\kappa\sqrt{\frac{2n}{n^{2}}}=\kappa\sqrt{\frac{2}{n}}\;.

∎

Proof of
Theorem

5.1
.

The proof proceeds in two stages.
We first derive a high-probability upper bound (over observations
Z_{1:n}
and randomization samples
Z_{1:n}^{(0)}
) for the conditional probability that a randomized test statistic is greater than the observed test statistic, conditional on
\mathcal{A}_{1:n}\coloneqq(S_{1:n},Z_{1:n},Z_{1:n}^{(0)})
.
We then use the probability upper bound to lower bound the test power in terms of a binomial cumulative distribution function.

Let
P^{0}_{1:n}
be the conditional distribution of
Z_{1:n}^{(0)}
conditioned on
S_{1:n}
under
H_{0}
.
For
b\in[B]
, let
Z_{1:n}^{(b)}
and
Z_{1:n}^{\prime(b)}
denote further independent randomization samples from
P^{0}_{1:n}
.
Let
\hat{\Delta}_{n}\coloneqq|T(Z_{1:n},Z^{(0)}_{1:n})|
denote the absolute value of the observed test statistic, and let
\xi_{n}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[H(W)]
.
Observe that

\displaystyle p(\hat{\Delta}_{n})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\left|T(Z^{(b)}_{1:n},Z^{\prime(b)}_{1:n})\right|\geq\hat{\Delta}_{n}\;\right|\;\mathcal{A}_{1:n}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\left|T(Z^{(b)}_{1:n},Z^{\prime(b)}_{1:n})\right|\geq\hat{\Delta}_{n}\;\right|\;\mathcal{A}_{1:n},\hat{\Delta}_{n}>\xi_{n}\right)\mathbbm{1}\left\{\hat{\Delta}_{n}>\xi_{n}\right\}

\displaystyle\quad+\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\left|T(Z^{(b)}_{1:n},Z^{\prime(b)}_{1:n})\right|\geq\hat{\Delta}_{n}\;\right|\;\mathcal{A}_{1:n},\hat{\Delta}_{n}\leq\xi_{n}\right)\mathbbm{1}\left\{\hat{\Delta}_{n}\leq\xi_{n}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\min\left\{1,\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\left|T(Z^{(b)}_{1:n},Z^{\prime(b)}_{1:n})\right|\geq\hat{\Delta}_{n}\;\right|\;\mathcal{A}_{1:n},\hat{\Delta}_{n}>\xi_{n}\right)+\mathbbm{1}\left\{\hat{\Delta}_{n}\leq\xi_{n}\right\}\right\}\;.

Focusing on the first term in the upper bound, we have

\displaystyle p^{*}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\left|T(Z^{(b)}_{1:n},Z^{\prime(b)}_{1:n})\right|\geq\hat{\Delta}_{n}\;\right|\;\mathcal{A}_{1:n},\hat{\Delta}_{n}>\xi_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\left|T(Z^{(b)}_{1:n},Z^{\prime(b)}_{1:n})\right|-\xi_{n}\geq\hat{\Delta}_{n}-\xi_{n}\;\right|\;\mathcal{A}_{1:n},\hat{\Delta}_{n}>\xi_{n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{3}{2}\frac{(\hat{\Delta}_{n}-\xi_{n})^{2}}{3a+\hat{\Delta}_{n}-\xi_{n}}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{3}{2}\left(\hat{\Delta}_{n}-\xi_{n}-3a+\frac{9a^{2}}{3a+\hat{\Delta}_{n}-\xi_{n}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\min\left\{1,\exp\left(-\frac{3}{2}\hat{\Delta}_{n}+\frac{3}{2}\xi_{n}+\frac{9}{2}a\right)\right\}

(22)

\displaystyle\leq\min\left\{1,\exp\left(-\frac{3}{2}\hat{\Delta}_{n}+3\kappa\sqrt{\frac{2}{n}}+72\kappa^{2}\sqrt{\frac{2}{n^{3}}}+\frac{9}{n}\kappa^{2}\right)\right\}\;.

The first inequality follows from
Lemma

C.6
and the fact that the conditional distributions of
Z_{1:n}^{(b)}
and
Z_{1:n}^{\prime(b)}
are the same.
The third inequality follows from the bound
a\leq\frac{2\kappa^{2}}{n}\left(1+8\sqrt{\frac{2}{n}}\right)
, which comes from
Lemma

C.7
and the bounds on
\varphi_{i}^{2}
and
M
.

From
Lemma

C.6
conditioned on
S_{1:n}
, for
\eta>0
, we have with probability over
Z_{1:n}
and
Z_{1:n}^{(0)}
,

\displaystyle 1-e^{-\eta}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\left.H(Z_{1:2n})\leq\xi_{n}+\sqrt{2a\eta}+\frac{2M\eta}{3}\;\right|\;S_{1:n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\left|T(P_{1:n},P^{0}_{1:n})-T(Z_{1:n},Z^{(0)}_{1:n})\right|\leq\xi_{n}+\sqrt{2a\eta}+\frac{2M\eta}{3}\;\right|\;S_{1:n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\left.T(P_{1:n},P^{0}_{1:n})-\left|T(Z_{1:n},Z^{(0)}_{1:n})\right|\leq\xi_{n}+\sqrt{2a\eta}+\frac{2M\eta}{3}\;\right|\;S_{1:n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\Delta_{n}-\hat{\Delta}_{n}\leq 2\kappa\sqrt{\frac{2}{n}}+\sqrt{2\eta\left(\frac{2\kappa^{2}}{n}\left(1+8\sqrt{\frac{2}{n}}\right)\right)}+\frac{8\kappa\eta}{3n}\;\right|\;S_{1:n}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\Delta_{n}-2\kappa\left(\sqrt{\frac{2}{n}}+\sqrt{\eta}\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}+8\sqrt{\frac{2}{n^{3}}}}+\frac{4\eta}{3n}\right)\leq\hat{\Delta}_{n}\;\right|\;S_{1:n}\right)\;,

(23)

where the second inequality follows from (
21
), the third inequality from the fact
T(P_{1:n},P^{0}_{1:n})\geq 0
, and the fourth inequality from applying the previous bounds on
\xi_{n}
,
a
, and
M
.
Applying the probabilistic bound in (
23
) to the upper bound on
p^{*}
, we find that with probability at least
1-e^{-\eta}
over
Z_{1:n}
and
Z_{1:n}^{(0)}
,

\displaystyle p^{*}

\displaystyle\leq\min\left\{1,\exp\left(-\frac{3}{2}\left(\Delta_{n}-2\kappa\left(\sqrt{\frac{2}{n}}+\sqrt{\eta}\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}+8\sqrt{\frac{2}{n^{3}}}}+\frac{4\eta}{3n}\right)\right)\right.\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\left.\left.+\;3\kappa\sqrt{\frac{2}{n}}+72\kappa^{2}\sqrt{\frac{2}{n^{3}}}+\frac{9}{n}\kappa^{2}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\min\left\{1,\exp\left(-\frac{3}{2}\Delta_{n}+C_{n}(\eta)\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=:p_{\eta}(\Delta_{n})\;,

where
C_{n}(\eta)
is defined as in (
14
).
Therefore,

\displaystyle p(\hat{\Delta}_{n})

\displaystyle\leq\min\left\{1,p_{\eta}(\Delta_{n})+\mathbbm{1}\left\{\hat{\Delta}_{n}\leq\xi_{n}\right\}\right\}\;.

By (
23
) and
Lemma

C.7
, with the same probability,

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}\left\{\hat{\Delta}_{n}\leq\xi_{n}\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbbm{1}\left\{\Delta_{n}-2\kappa\left(\sqrt{\frac{2}{n}}+\sqrt{\eta}\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}+8\sqrt{\frac{2}{n^{3}}}}+\frac{4\eta}{3n}\right)\leq 2\kappa\sqrt{\frac{2}{n}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathbbm{1}\left\{\Delta_{n}\leq 2\kappa\left(2\sqrt{\frac{2}{n}}+\sqrt{\eta}\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}+8\sqrt{\frac{2}{n^{3}}}}+\frac{4\eta}{3n}\right)\right\}\;,

which equals zero under assumption (
15
).
Hence,
p(\hat{\Delta}_{n})\leq p_{\eta}(\Delta_{n})
with probability at least
1-e^{-\eta}
.

We now argue that the test power is lower bounded by a binomial CDF using the upper bound on
p(\hat{\Delta}_{n})
.
Conditioned on
S_{1:n}
,
N_{B}\coloneqq p_{B}(B+1)-1
is a Binomial
(B,p(\hat{\Delta}_{n}))
random variable with

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(p_{B}\leq\alpha\mid S_{1:n})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(N_{B}\leq\alpha(B+1)-1\mid S_{1:n})

\displaystyle=F_{B,p(\hat{\Delta}_{n})}(\lfloor\alpha(B+1)-1\rfloor)\;,

where
F_{B,p(\hat{\Delta}_{n})}
is the CDF of the Binomial
(B,p(\hat{\Delta}_{n}))
distribution.
It is known that for
q\geq p
, a Binomial
(B,q)
-distributed random variable
X_{B,q}
stochastically dominates a Binomial
(B,p)
-distributed random variable
X_{B,p}
in the sense that for all
\ell\in\{0,1,\dotsc,B\}
,

\mathbb{P}(X_{B,q}\geq\ell)\geq\mathbb{P}(X_{B,p}\geq\ell)\;.

The inequality is reversed for the CDFs, so that the bound
p(\hat{\Delta}_{n})\leq p_{\eta}(\Delta_{n})
implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(p_{B}\leq\alpha\mid S_{1:n})

\displaystyle\geq F_{B,p_{\eta}(\Delta_{n})}(\lfloor\alpha(B+1)-1\rfloor)\;.

Thus, we have showed that with probability at least
1-e^{-\eta}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(p_{B}\leq\alpha\mid S_{1:n})\geq\sum_{\ell=0}^{\lfloor\alpha(B+1)-1\rfloor}\binom{B}{\ell}p_{\eta}(\Delta_{n})^{\ell}(1-p_{\eta}(\Delta_{n}))^{B-\ell}\;.

∎

C.4.2
Lower bound for high-probability rejection of alternatives

Using the Chernoff bound on the upper tail of deviations of a binomial random variable, we can obtain an explicit lower bound on values of
\Delta_{n}
that correspond to alternatives for which the test (with high probability) rejects the null with probability at least
1-\beta
for
\beta\in(0,1)
.
For convenience, let
B_{\alpha}\coloneq\lfloor\alpha(B+1)-1\rfloor
.

Corollary C.8
.

Assume the conditions of
Theorem

5.1
and that
B_{\alpha}\geq Bp_{\eta}(\Delta_{n})
. Fix any
\beta\in(0,1)
. For each
\eta>0
, if

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}\geq\frac{2}{3}\left(C_{n}(\eta)-\ln\frac{B_{\alpha}}{B}-\ln\left(1-\sqrt{\frac{-2\ln\beta}{B_{\alpha}}}\right)\right)\;,

then for almost every sequence
(S_{i})_{i\geq 1}
, conditionally on
S_{1:n}
, we have that
\mathbb{P}(p_{B}\leq\alpha|S_{1:n})\geq 1-\beta
with probability over
(Z_{1:n},{Z}^{(0)}_{1:n})
at least
1-e^{-\eta}
.

In the limit of
B,n\to\infty
, we see that if
\text{MMD}(P,P^{(0)})\geq-\frac{2}{3}\ln\alpha
, then the test will reject with probability at least
1-\beta
.

Proof of
Corollary

C.8
.

Let
B_{\eta}(\Delta_{n})\coloneqq Bp_{\eta}(\Delta_{n})
.
Applying a Chernoff bound on the upper tail of deviations of a binomial random variable to
N_{B}\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(B,p_{\eta}(\Delta_{n}))
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(N_{B}\geq B_{\alpha}\mid S_{1:n})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\left.N_{B}\geq\left(\frac{B_{\alpha}}{B_{\eta}(\Delta_{n})}\right)B_{\eta}(\Delta_{n})\;\right|\;S_{1:n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left\{-B_{\eta}(\Delta_{n})\left(\frac{B_{\alpha}}{B_{\eta}(\Delta_{n})}\ln\left(\frac{B_{\alpha}}{B_{\eta}(\Delta_{n})}\right)-\frac{B_{\alpha}}{B_{\eta}(\Delta_{n})}+1\right)\right\}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-B_{\alpha}\ln\left(\frac{B_{\alpha}}{B_{\eta}(\Delta_{n})}\right)+B_{\alpha}-B_{\eta}(\Delta_{n})\right)\;.

This bound is at most
\beta
if and only if

\displaystyle B_{\alpha}\ln\left(\frac{B_{\alpha}}{B_{\eta}(\Delta_{n})}\right)-(B_{\alpha}-B_{\eta}(\Delta_{n}))\geq-\ln\beta\;.

Note that

\displaystyle\ln\left(\frac{B_{\alpha}}{B_{\eta}(\Delta_{n})}\right)=-\ln\left(1-\frac{B_{\alpha}-B_{\eta}(\Delta_{n})}{B_{\alpha}}\right)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\ell}\left(\frac{B_{\alpha}-B_{\eta}(\Delta_{n})}{B_{\alpha}}\right)^{\ell}\;,

where the last equality follows from the Taylor expansion of
-\ln(1-x)
for
|x|<1
, and so an equivalent condition is

\displaystyle B_{\alpha}\sum_{\ell=2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\ell}\left(\frac{B_{\alpha}-B_{\eta}(\Delta_{n})}{B_{\alpha}}\right)^{\ell}\geq-\ln\beta\;.

The condition is satisfied if

\displaystyle B_{\alpha}\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{B_{\alpha}-B_{\eta}(\Delta_{n})}{B_{\alpha}}\right)^{2}\geq-\ln\beta\;,

or equivalently,

\displaystyle B_{\eta}(\Delta_{n})\leq B_{\alpha}\left(1-\sqrt{-\frac{2\ln\beta}{B_{\alpha}}}\right)\;.

Substituting in the definition for
B_{\eta}(\Delta_{n})
and rearranging for
\Delta_{n}
yields

\displaystyle\Delta_{n}\geq\frac{2}{3}\left(C_{n}(\eta)-\ln\left(\frac{B_{\alpha}}{B}\right)-\ln\left(1-\sqrt{-\frac{2\ln\beta}{B_{\alpha}}}\right)\right)\;.

∎

C.5
Power lower bounds for adaptive statistics

As with
Theorem

5.1
, the conditional probability of rejecting when using the FUSE statistic can be lower bounded in terms of

\displaystyle\bar{\Delta}_{n}\coloneqq\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\left[\left.\widehat{\textrm{MMD}}^{2}_{v,\sigma_{\ell}}(Z_{1:n},Z^{(0)}_{1:n})\;\right|\;S_{1:n}\right]}\;.

For convenience, let
C_{n}^{\vee}(\eta)
denote the maximum over
\ell\in[L]
of
C_{n,\ell}(\eta)
, which is obtained by substituting
\kappa_{\ell}
for
\kappa
into (
14
).
Define
\kappa_{\lor}\coloneqq\sup_{\ell}\kappa_{\ell}
.

Proposition C.9
.

Let
T
be the FUSE statistic using
\widehat{\textrm{MMD}}^{2}_{v,\sigma_{\ell}}
and exact randomization in the CRT.
For any
\eta>0
, if

\displaystyle\bar{\Delta}_{n}>2\kappa_{\lor}\left(2\sqrt{\frac{2}{n}}+\sqrt{\eta}\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}+8\sqrt{\frac{2}{n^{3}}}}+\frac{4\eta}{3n}\right)\;,

S_{1:n}

S_{1:n}
, with probability at least
\max\{0,1-Le^{-\eta}\}
over

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(p_{B}\leq\alpha\mid S_{1:n})\geq F_{B,p_{\eta}(\bar{\Delta}_{n})}(\lfloor\alpha(B+1)-1\rfloor)\;,

where
F_{B,p_{\eta}(\Delta_{n}^{\vee})}
is the distribution function for the binomial distribution of
B
trials with success probability

\displaystyle p_{\eta}(\bar{\Delta}_{n})=\min\left\{1,L\exp\left(-\frac{3}{2}\bar{\Delta}_{n}+C_{n}^{\vee}(\eta)\right)\right\}\;.

Using this, a lower bound for
\bar{\Delta}_{n}
that guarantees power
1-\beta
with high probability, analogous to the one in

C.8
, can be obtained.

For the following proof, define

\displaystyle a^{\lor}\coloneqq\sup_{\ell\in[L]}a_{\ell}\leq\kappa_{\lor}^{2}\left(\frac{2}{n}+16\sqrt{\frac{2}{n^{3}}}\right)

(25)

and similarly

\displaystyle\xi_{n}^{\lor}\coloneqq\sup_{\ell\in[L]}\xi_{n,\ell}\leq 2\kappa_{\lor}\sqrt{\frac{2}{n}}\;.

(26)

Proof of
Proposition

C.9
.

Let

\displaystyle T(Z_{1:n},Z_{1:n}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\widehat{\mathrm{FUSE}}_{\omega}(Z_{1:n},Z_{1:n}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\omega}\log\left(\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\exp\left(\omega\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}^{2}_{v,\sigma_{\ell}}(Z_{1:n},Z_{1:n}^{\prime})\right)\right)\;,

\widehat{T}\coloneqq T(Z_{1:n},Z_{1:n}^{(0)})
denote the observed test statistic.
Denote
\mathcal{A}_{1:n}\coloneqq(S_{1:n},Z_{1:n},Z_{1:n}^{(0)})
.
Observe that

\displaystyle p(\widehat{T})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbb{P}\left(\left.T(Z_{1:n}^{(b)},Z_{1:n}^{\prime(b)})\geq\widehat{T}\;\right|\;\mathcal{A}_{1:n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left\{\left.\frac{1}{\omega}\log\left(\sup_{\ell\in[L]}\exp\left(\omega\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}^{2}_{v,\sigma_{\ell}}(Z_{1:n}^{(b)},Z_{1:n}^{\prime(b)})\right)\right)\geq\widehat{T}\;\right|\;\mathcal{A}_{1:n}\right\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\sup_{\ell\in[L]}\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}^{2}_{v,\sigma_{\ell}}(Z_{1:n}^{(b)},Z_{1:n}^{\prime(b)})\geq\widehat{T}\;\right|\;\mathcal{A}_{1:n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\sup_{\ell\in[L]}\left|\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}_{v,\sigma_{\ell}}(Z_{1:n}^{(b)},Z_{1:n}^{\prime(b)})\right|\geq\sqrt{\widehat{T}}\;\right|\;\mathcal{A}_{1:n}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\sup_{\ell\in[L]}\left|\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}_{v,\sigma_{\ell}}(Z_{1:n}^{(b)},Z_{1:n}^{\prime(b)})\right|-\xi_{n}^{\lor}\geq\sqrt{\widehat{T}}-\xi_{n}^{\lor}\;\right|\;\mathcal{A}_{1:n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\min\left\{1,\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\sup_{\ell\in[L]}\left|\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}_{v,\sigma_{\ell}}(Z_{1:n}^{(b)},Z_{1:n}^{\prime(b)})\right|-\xi_{n}^{\lor}\geq\sqrt{\widehat{T}}-\xi_{n}^{\lor}\;\right|\;\mathcal{A}_{1:n},\sqrt{\widehat{T}}>\xi_{n}^{\lor}\right)\right.

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\mathbbm{1}\left\{\sqrt{\widehat{T}}\leq\xi_{n}^{\lor}\right\}\Bigg\}\;,

where the last inequality follows from the same argument as that in
Theorem

5.1
.
We first derive a high-probability upper bound for the first term.
Applying a Fréchet inequality (first inequality),
Lemma

C.6
(third inequality), the same factoring trick as in (
22
) (fourth inequality), and Jensen’s inequality (last two inequalities), we obtain

\displaystyle p^{*}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\sup_{\ell\in[L]}\left|\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}_{v,\sigma_{\ell}}(Z_{1:n}^{(b)},Z_{1:n}^{\prime(b)})\right|-\xi_{n}^{\lor}\geq\sqrt{\widehat{T}}-\xi_{n}^{\lor}\;\right|\;\mathcal{A}_{1:n},\sqrt{\widehat{T}}>\xi_{n}^{\lor}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\min\left\{1,\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\left|\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}_{v,\sigma_{\ell}}(Z_{1:n}^{(b)},Z_{1:n}^{\prime(b)})\right|-\xi_{n}^{\lor}\geq\sqrt{\widehat{T}}-\xi_{n}^{\lor}\;\right|\;\mathcal{A}_{1:n},\sqrt{\widehat{T}}>\xi_{n}^{\lor}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\min\left\{1,\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\mathbb{P}\left(\left.\left|\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}_{v,\sigma_{\ell}}(Z_{1:n}^{(b)},Z_{1:n}^{\prime(b)})\right|-\xi_{n,\ell}\geq\sqrt{\widehat{T}}-\xi_{n}^{\lor}\;\right|\;\mathcal{A}_{1:n},\sqrt{\widehat{T}}>\xi_{n}^{\lor}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\min\left\{1,\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\exp\left(-\frac{3}{2}\frac{\left(\sqrt{\widehat{T}}-\xi_{n}^{\lor}\right)^{2}}{3a_{\ell}+\sqrt{\widehat{T}}-\xi_{n}^{\lor}}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\min\left\{1,\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\exp\left(-\frac{3}{2}\sqrt{\widehat{T}}+\frac{3}{2}\xi_{n}^{\lor}+\frac{9}{2}a_{\ell}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\min\left\{1,L\exp\left(-\frac{3}{2}\sqrt{\widehat{T}}+\frac{3}{2}\xi_{n}^{\lor}+\frac{9}{2}a^{\lor}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\min\left\{1,L\exp\left(-\frac{3}{2}\sqrt{\frac{1}{L}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}^{2}_{v,\sigma_{\ell}}(Z_{1:n},Z_{1:n}^{(0)})}+\frac{3}{2}\xi_{n}^{\lor}+\frac{9}{2}a^{\lor}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\min\left\{1,L\exp\left(-\frac{3}{2L}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\left|\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}_{v,\sigma_{\ell}}(Z_{1:n},Z_{1:n}^{(0)})\right|+\frac{3}{2}\xi_{n}^{\lor}+\frac{9}{2}a^{\lor}\right)\right\}\;.

Applying a union bound with (
23
) and the bounds on
a^{\lor}
(
25
) and
\xi_{n}^{\lor}
(
26
), for any
\eta>0
, with probability at least
\max\{0,1-Le^{-\eta}\}
over
Z_{1:n}
and
Z_{1:n}^{(0)}
,

\displaystyle\exp\left(-\frac{3}{2L}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\left|\widehat{\mathrm{MMD}}_{v,\sigma_{\ell}}(Z_{1:n},Z_{1:n}^{(0)})\right|+\frac{3}{2}\xi_{n}^{\lor}+\frac{9}{2}a^{\lor}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\exp\left\{-\frac{3}{2L}\sum_{\ell=1}^{L}\left(\Delta_{n,\ell}-2\kappa_{\ell}\left(\sqrt{\frac{2}{n}}+\sqrt{\eta}\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}+8\sqrt{\frac{2}{n^{3}}}}+\frac{4\eta}{3n}\right)\right)\right.

\displaystyle\quad\qquad+\left.\frac{3}{2}\left(2\kappa_{\lor}\sqrt{\frac{2}{n}}\right)+\frac{9}{2}\kappa_{\lor}^{2}\left(\frac{2}{n}+16\sqrt{\frac{2}{n^{3}}}\right)\right\}

D
Further considerations for randomization tests for conditional symmetry

D.1
Randomization using representative inversions with non-free group actions

As demonstrated in
Example

2
, a representative inversion
{\psi^{\circ}}
may be (non-uniquely) defined even when the group action is not free in some cases.
In these cases, a test for conditional symmetry that uses the deterministic representative inversion is still a valid level-
\alpha
test under the null.
This follows from the observation that for a random action
\Psi|X\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\rm d}}}{{=}}{\psi^{\circ}}(X)H|X
, where
H
is a random element of the stabilizer subgroup
\mathbf{G}_{X}
of
X
, assuming that
Y|X
is
\mathbf{G}
-equivariant,

\displaystyle P_{Y|X}(\Psi^{-1}Y\in B\mid x)

\displaystyle=P_{Y|X}(({\psi^{\circ}}(x)H)^{-1}Y\in B\mid x)

\displaystyle=P_{Y|X}({\psi^{\circ}}(x)^{-1}Y\in B\mid H^{-1}x)=P_{Y|X}({\psi^{\circ}}(x)^{-1}Y\in B\mid x)\;.

That is, the conditional distributions of
\Psi^{-1}Y|X
and
{\psi^{\circ}}(X)^{-1}Y|X
are the same.
A test that uses the representative inversion may be more computationally efficient than one that samples from the inversion kernel.
However, tests will generally have different properties under alternatives.
We explore such differences empirically in
Section

E.4
.

Training an invariant MNIST classifier

As an application under a different context, we illustrate how our tests for symmetry of a conditional distribution
P_{Y|X}
can be adapted as tests for symmetry of a function
f:\mathbf{X}\to\mathbf{Y}
by taking
P_{Y|X}=\delta_{f(X)}
.
This means that our tests could be used as, for example, validation tools for assessing whether a prediction model has learned symmetry after training, requiring only input data and model-predicted output.
To demonstrate this utility, consider a setting where an MNIST classifier is trained to be invariant with respect to image rotations via data augmentation
(
Chen, Dobriban and Lee, 2020
;
Huang, Orbanz and
Austern, 2022
)
.
Unlike methods that encode symmetry as inductive biases, data augmentation may not always succeed in producing a symmetric model.

The MNIST dataset is a collection of
28\times 28
pixel images of handwritten digits.
The training set consists of
20000
images; the test set consists of
45000
augmented images, from which we uniformly sample to use in our tests.
We train two identical LeNet convolutional neural networks for predicting the digit in the image, where one model is trained on images augmented with rotations and the other without.
Before and during training, we pause the training process after every three epochs to test the invariance properties of each model.
To generate the data used in each test, a sample of
n=100
augmented images is sampled from the test set and fed into the model.
The data
Y
are then taken as the digit probabilities predicted by the model, and so each observation is a vector on a simplex of dimension
d=10
(or
d=9
in the setting where the “
9
” images are removed from the data).
In our tests, we take the maximal invariant to be the unaugmented image to avoid complications with orienting images.

As augmentation ensures that the marginal distribution of the images will be invariant, we test for conditional invariance using tests for invariance via
Property

E8
.
For the test statistic, we use the
information diffusion kernel
for simplex data
(
Lebanon and Lafferty, 2002
)
, given by

k(y,y^{\prime})\approx(4\pi\sigma)^{-\frac{d+1}{2}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{\sigma}\arccos^{2}\left(\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sqrt{y_{i}y_{i}^{\prime}}\right)\right)\;,

where
\sigma
is the kernel parameter.
To perform randomization in these tests, we apply random rotations to the images and then predict their label probabilities using the model trained until that point in the training process.
We repeat this procedure for
N=1000
instances at each testing point to estimate the rejection rate of the test, which can be interpreted as a measure of how invariant the model is at that point during training.

In both settings, the rejection rate for the model trained with augmentations is approximately
\alpha=0.05
after the 30th epoch, whereas it is unclear if the rejection rate for the model trained without augmentations is converging to any value.

Here, we used the estimated test rejection rate as a measure of model invariance.
It may be tempting to determine whether symmetry has been learned at a particular time point by whether a single test rejects or not, but conducting a suite of tests at multiple points during training would be subject to multiple testing considerations.
A potential direction for future work is anytime-valid tests for conditional symmetry, which would allow for the continual evaluation of symmetry-learning based on a single test at each time point during training.
Anytime-valid tests for distributional invariance have recently appeared in the literature
(
Koning, 2023
;
Ramdas et al., 2023b
;
Lardy and Pérez-Ortiz, 2025
)
.
