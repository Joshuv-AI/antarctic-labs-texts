Activated Backstepping with Control Barrier Functions for the Safe Navigation of Automated Vehicles
Laszlo Gacsi, Max H. Cohen, Tamas G. Molnar

Abstract
This paper introduces a novel safety-critical control method through the synthesis of control barrier functions (CBFs) for systems with high-relative-degree safety constraints. By extending the procedure of CBF backstepping, we propose activated backstepping —a constructive method to synthesize valid CBFs. The novelty of our method is the incorporation of an activation function into the CBF, which offers less conservative safe sets in the state space than standard CBF backstepping. We demonstrate the proposed method on an inverted pendulum example, where we explain the underlying geometric meaning in the state space and provide comparisons with existing CBF synthesis techniques. Finally, we implement our method to achieve collision-free navigation for automated vehicles using a kinematic bicycle model in simulation.

A promising way to achieve provably safe behavior for these systems is through the use of control barrier functions (CBFs)

CBFs

To achieve collision-free navigation, AVs must satisfy

There exist methods to address such high-relative-degree constraints using CBFs.
The first method is

Another constructive method is

II-B

Safety Constraints with High Relative Degree

We revisit techniques to generate CBFs for higher relative degree cases.
We focus on
relative degree two
, although these methods can be recursively applied to relative degrees higher than two.
Relative degree two often occurs in mechanical systems where the safety constraints are on position (or configuration) and the inputs are forces (or torques) affecting the second derivative of position due to Newton’s second law.

Consider a smooth output
{\mathbf{y}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}}
that selects the states (e.g., position) relevant to safety, and a
constraint set

\mathcal{C}
defined by a smooth
constraint function

{\psi:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}}
:

\mathcal{C}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\psi\big(\mathbf{y}(\mathbf{x})\big)\geq 0\}.

(11)

Assumption 1
.

The output
\mathbf{y}
satisfies:

L_{\mathbf{g}}\mathbf{y}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{0},\quad{\rm rank}\big(L_{\mathbf{g}}L_{\mathbf{f}}\mathbf{y}(\mathbf{x})\big)=p,\quad\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{E}.

(12)

Assumption
1
implies that the output
\mathbf{y}
has relative degree two: the control input
\mathbf{u}
shows up in its second time derivative.
We use the following notation for first time derivatives:

\dot{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x})=L_{\mathbf{f}}\mathbf{y}(\mathbf{x}),\quad\!\!\dot{\psi}\big(\mathbf{y}(\mathbf{x}),\dot{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x})\big)=\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{y}(\mathbf{x}))\cdot\dot{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x}).

(13)

Since
\dot{\psi}
is independent of the input
\mathbf{u}
, the constraint function
\psi
is not a CBF, in general.
The following approaches generate a candidate CBF
h
from the constraint function
\psi
.

1

The most popular tool to address high-relative-degree safety constraints is

The limitation of HOCBFs is that

2
Rectified CBF

To overcome the limitations of HOCBFs,

Backstepping CBF

An alternative approach for systematically constructing a valid CBF is

III
ACTIVATED BACKSTEPPING

Although backstepping provides a constructive way to generate valid CBFs, it is often conservative in that the resulting safe set
\mathcal{S}
may be much smaller than the constraint set
\mathcal{C}
.
To remedy this, we propose
activated backstepping
, an approach that yields a larger safe set, and builds on
the framework of ReCBFs
[
17
]
.
Specifically,

[
17
, Ex. 4]

introduced
the main idea behind
activated backstepping
using a simple example, but did not provide a full characterization of such an approach.
Here, we fill in these gaps by formally establishing the underlying properties of this approach and providing more detailed examples.
The advantage of ABCs over ReCBFs is that they obviate the need to choose
\varepsilon
, producing a valid CBF with chatter-free inputs by construction.

To enlarge the safe set
\mathcal{S}
, we make an observation.
If

{\dot{\psi}\big(\mathbf{y}(\mathbf{x}),\dot{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x})\big)\geq\dot{\psi}\big(\mathbf{y}(\mathbf{x}),\boldsymbol{\kappa}(\mathbf{y}(\mathbf{x}))\big)}
, then the output
\mathbf{y}
evolves safer than the single integrator with controller
\boldsymbol{\kappa}
in (
19
), implying safety w.r.t.
\mathcal{C}
.
Hence, at these points
\psi(\mathbf{y}(\mathbf{x}))
is a perfectly valid CBF.
Thus, we propose the
activated backstepping CBF (ABC)
:

\psi(\mathbf{y}(\mathbf{x}))-\gamma(-s(\mathbf{x}))&\text{if }s(\mathbf{x})<0,\end{cases}

(20)

where
{\gamma\in\mathcal{K}^{\rm e}}
is smooth with
{\gamma^{\prime}(s)=0\iff s=0}

and:

\displaystyle s(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\dot{\psi}\big(\mathbf{y}(\mathbf{x}),\dot{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x})\big)-\dot{\psi}\big(\mathbf{y}(\mathbf{x}),\boldsymbol{\kappa}(\mathbf{y}(\mathbf{x}))\big)

(21)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{y}(\mathbf{x}))\cdot\big(\dot{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x})-\boldsymbol{\kappa}(\mathbf{y}(\mathbf{x}))\big).

Note that
s(\mathbf{x})
in (

in (

The term

indicates that the proposed CBF (

For

L_{\mathbf{g}}h=\mathbf{0}\iff\Theta^{\prime}(-s)=0\iff s\geq 0,

(27)

where we also used (
23
).
If
{\Theta^{\prime}(-s)=0}
and
{s\geq 0}
:

\displaystyle\!\!L_{\mathbf{f}}h=\dot{\psi}(\mathbf{y},\dot{\mathbf{y}})\geq\dot{\psi}(\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\kappa}(\mathbf{y}))>-\alpha(\psi(\mathbf{y}))=-\alpha(h),

(28)

where we substituted
{\Theta^{\prime}(-s)=0}
into
L_{\mathbf{f}}h
in (
24
);
we used
{s\geq 0}
and (
21
);
we applied (
19
);
and we used
{s\geq 0}
and (
20
).
Thus, (
10
) holds and Lemma
1
completes the proof.
∎

) for all
{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{S}}

Remark 1
.

The advantage of activated backstepping over the standard backstepping is twofold.
First, because
h
differs from
\psi
in (
20
) only at safety-critical states (when
{s(\mathbf{x})<0}
), the size of the resulting safe set
\mathcal{S}
becomes larger
(see the example below).
Second, when
h
matches
\psi
(when
{s(\mathbf{x})\geq 0}
), significant computations can be saved.
Specially, one does not need to calculate
\dot{h}
and the derivative of
\boldsymbol{\kappa}
, as the safety filter in (
7
)-(
8
) gives
{\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{k}_{\rm d}(\mathbf{x})}
.
The ABC in (
22
) also offers an advantage over the ReCBF in (
15
): it strictly satisfies (
4
), i.e.,
{\sup_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\dot{h}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})>-\alpha(h(\mathbf{x}))}
, rather than
{\sup_{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}\dot{h}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})\geq-\alpha(h(\mathbf{x}))}
that holds for ReCBFs when
{\varepsilon=0}

[
17
]
.
This subtlety is important for ensuring the Lipschitz continuity of safe controllers like (
6
)
[
18
]
.
For
{\varepsilon=0}
, ReCBFs could yield discontinuous inputs with chatter (frequent jumps), and for small
{\varepsilon>0}
, the inputs may change rapidly (see the example below).
Thus, carefully selecting
\varepsilon
is important.
ABCs eliminate the challenge of tuning
\varepsilon
, at the price of additional complexity of designing controller
\boldsymbol{\kappa}
.

III-A

Illustrative Example

We first demonstrate the utility of activated backstepping using an inverted pendulum example, as in
[
4
]
, and compare it with the other methods.
The equations of motion are:

\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\dot{\varphi}\\
\dot{\omega}\end{bmatrix}}_{\dot{\mathbf{x}}}=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\omega\\
\sin(\varphi)\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})}+\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}0\\
1\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x})}u,

(29)

where the state
\mathbf{x}=(\varphi,\omega)
includes the angle
\varphi
measured from the upright position and the angular velocity
\omega
, while the input
u
is a torque.
For safety, we keep the pendulum above the horizontal position so that
{-\pi/2\leq\varphi\leq\pi/2}
.
This is captured by the output
{y(\mathbf{x})=\varphi}
and constraint function:

\psi(\varphi)=\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}-\varphi^{2}.

(30)

Since

{\dot{y}(\mathbf{x})=L_{\mathbf{f}}y(\mathbf{x})=\omega}
,

{L_{\mathbf{g}}y(\mathbf{x})=0}
,
and
{L_{\mathbf{g}}L_{\mathbf{f}}y(\mathbf{x})=1}
,
y
has relative degree two and satisfies Assumption
1
.
Furthermore,
\psi
satisfies Assumption
2
for
{\mathcal{E}=\mathbb{R}^{2}}
, because
{\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial y}(\varphi)=-2\varphi=0}
holds for
{\varphi=0}
and
{\psi(0)=\pi^{2}/4>0}
.

III-A
1
High-order CBF

The HOCBF in (
14
) becomes:

h(\mathbf{x})=-2\varphi\omega+\alpha\bigg(\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}-\varphi^{2}\bigg),

(31)

with
{L_{\mathbf{f}}h(\mathbf{x})=-2\omega^{2}-2\varphi\sin(\varphi)-2\alpha^{\prime}(\pi^{2}/4-\varphi^{2})\varphi\omega}
and

{L_{\mathbf{g}}h(\mathbf{x})=-2\varphi}
. If
{L_{\mathbf{g}}h(\mathbf{x})=0}
, we have
{\varphi=0}
, leading to
{L_{\mathbf{f}}h(\mathbf{x})=-2\omega^{2}}
, and
{h(\mathbf{x})=\alpha(\pi^{2}/4)}
.
Thus, (
10
) does not hold for states where
{\omega^{2}\geq\alpha(\alpha(\pi^{2}/4))/2}
.
Therefore, based on Lemma
1
, this choice of
h
is not a CBF.

III-A
2
Rectified CBF

The ReCBF in (
15
) takes the form:

h(\mathbf{x})\!=\!\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}-\varphi^{2}-\frac{1}{2\mu}{\rm ReQU}\bigg(\!2\varphi\omega-\alpha\bigg(\!\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}-\varphi^{2}\!\bigg)+\varepsilon\!\bigg)\!,

(32)

with the choice
{\gamma(s)=-s^{2}/(2\mu)}
for
{s<0}
.
Based on (
16
), this is a CBF if
{\alpha(\pi^{2}/4)\geq\varepsilon}
, putting an upper bound on
\varepsilon
.

III-A
3
Backstepping CBF

The first step of this method is to design the virtual controller
\kappa
so as to ensure (
19
):

-2\varphi\kappa(\varphi)>-\alpha\bigg(\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}-\varphi^{2}\bigg).

(33)

For example, we can choose
{\kappa(\varphi)=-K\varphi}
and
{\alpha(r)=\alpha_{\rm c}r}
with
{2K\geq\alpha_{\rm c}>0}
.
With this, the backstepping CBF (
18
) is:

h(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}-\varphi^{2}-\frac{1}{2\mu}\big(\omega+K\varphi\big)^{2}.

(34)

The corresponding safe set
\mathcal{S}
is a rotated ellipse in the state space.
Elliptical safe sets have previously been constructed by intuition for the inverted pendulum
[
4
]
and also for lane keeping control
[
9
]
—these can also be viewed as a special case of backstepping with a linear virtual controller
\kappa
.

III-A
4
Activated Backstepping CBF

We use the
ABC in (
20
) with
{\gamma(s)=s^{2}/(2\mu)}
for
{s>0}
:

\displaystyle h(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}-\varphi^{2}&\text{if }s(\mathbf{x})\geq 0,\\
\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}-\varphi^{2}-\frac{s(\mathbf{x})^{2}}{2\mu}&\text{if }s(\mathbf{x})<0,\\
\end{cases}

(35)

\displaystyle s(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=-2\varphi(\omega+K\varphi),

or, more compactly, as in (
22
), with
{\Theta(s)={\rm ReQU}(s)/(2\mu)}
:

h(\mathbf{x})=\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}-\varphi^{2}-\frac{1}{2\mu}{\rm ReQU}\big(2\varphi(\omega+K\varphi)\big).

(36)

Notice the difference between the standard backstepping in (
34
) and the activated version in (
35
): we incorporated the switching function
s
that includes a factor
\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial y}(y(\mathbf{x}))=-2\varphi
.

) with the safety-critical controller (

), (b,g) ReCBF (

), (c,h) backstepping CBF (

), and
(d,i) ABC (

).
The pendulum starts moving from the same initial condition in each case, the desired input is
{k_{\rm d}(\mathbf{x})=0}
, and the CBF must keep the angle within
{-\pi/2\leq\varphi\leq\pi/2}
.
The extended class-
\mathcal{K}
function of each CBF is linear,
{\alpha(r)=\alpha_{\rm c}r}
, and the parameters are:

{\alpha_{\rm c}=1\,{\rm s^{-1}}}
and

for all CBFs,

{\varepsilon=2\,{\rm s^{-1}}}

(a,b,c,d) show the phase plane with the constraint set
\mathcal{C}
(red), safe set
\mathcal{S}

All four cases are safe: the trajectories stay inside
{\mathcal{S}\cap\mathcal{C}}

The backstepping input (purple) has a larger magnitude than that of the HOCBF (blue), while the activated backstepping input (orange) is slightly larger and changes more sharply.
The input of the ReCBF with
{\varepsilon=2\,{\rm s^{-1}}}

However, selecting
\varepsilon
too large, such as
{\varepsilon=4\,{\rm s^{-1}}}

) (i.e.,
{\alpha(\pi^{2}/4)<\varepsilon}
), thus

If one chooses a too small

{t\in[3,4]\,{\rm s}}

.

Figure

{L_{\mathbf{g}}h(\mathbf{x})\!=\!0}
(thin black) and switching line
{L_{\mathbf{f}}h(\mathbf{x})+\alpha(h(\mathbf{x}))=0}
where the controller starts to deviate from the desired
{k_{\rm d}(\mathbf{x})=0}
(dash-dot magenta); cf. (
7

A CBF is valid if the singular region
{L_{\mathbf{g}}h(\mathbf{x})=0}
lies between the switching lines where
{L_{\mathbf{f}}h(\mathbf{x})>-\alpha(h(\mathbf{x}))}
; see (

\mathcal{S}

{s(\mathbf{x})\geq 0}

and also to
{L_{\mathbf{g}}h(\mathbf{x})=0}
based on (

).

Overall, activated backstepping yields a valid CBF
under weaker conditions than
the HOCBF method.
The ABC aims to minimize the difference between the safe set and the constraint set, yielding a larger safe set than traditional backstepping, at the price of a slightly larger and sharper input.
Furthermore, the ABC generates a Lipschitz continuous controller without jumps or chatter, a phenomenon that may occur for ReCBFs if
\varepsilon
is not chosen carefully.

IV
APPLICATION TO SAFE VEHICLE CONTROL

Finally, we implement activated backstepping for the safe navigation of automated vehicles.
We design safe controllers for obstacle avoidance using a kinematic bicycle model
[
7
]
:

\displaystyle\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}\dot{\xi}\\
\dot{\eta}\\
\dot{\vartheta}\\
\dot{v}\end{bmatrix}}_{\dot{\mathbf{x}}}=\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}v\cos(\vartheta)\\
v\sin(\vartheta)\\
0\\
0\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{x})}+\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\
0&0\\
v/L&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{g}(\mathbf{x})}\underbrace{\begin{bmatrix}u_{1}\\
u_{2}\end{bmatrix}}_{\mathbf{u}},

(37)

where
L
is the wheelbase.
The vehicle has four states: rear wheel position (
\xi
,
\eta
), yaw angle
\vartheta
, and rear wheel speed
v
; while it has two control inputs: tangent of steering angle
u_{1}
and longitudinal acceleration
u_{2}
.
We assume that
{v\neq 0}
.

As shown in Fig.
3
(a), our goal is to drive the vehicle with desired velocity
{v_{\rm d}>0}
along a lane at
{\eta=0}
, and then safely bypass a circular obstacle of radius
R_{\rm O}
centered at (
\xi_{\rm O}
,
\eta_{\rm O}
).
Collision-free behavior is captured by the output coordinates
\mathbf{y}(\mathbf{x})=(\xi,\eta)
and constraint function:

\psi(\mathbf{y}(\mathbf{x}))=(\xi-\xi_{\rm O})^{2}+(\eta-\eta_{\rm O})^{2}-R_{\rm O}^{2},

(38)

which satisfy Assumption
1
if
{v\neq 0}
and Assumption
2
for
{\mathcal{E}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:(\xi,\eta)\neq(\xi_{\rm O},\eta_{\rm O})\}}
.

We design a safe controller based on the
ABC (
22
).
First, we construct a virtual controller
\boldsymbol{\kappa}
satisfying (
19
).
While this is more challenging than in the previous example, it can be done algorithmically using the smooth safety filter in (
7
) and (
9
) for the single integrator (with weights
{\hat{\Gamma}_{1}\!=\!\hat{\Gamma}_{2}\!=\!1}
):

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\kappa}(\mathbf{y})=\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{\rm d}(\mathbf{y})+\lambda\big(a(\mathbf{y}),\|\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{y})\|^{2}\big)\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{y}),

(39)

\displaystyle a(\mathbf{y})=\dot{\psi}\big(\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{\rm d}(\mathbf{y})\big)+\hat{\alpha}_{\rm c}\psi(\mathbf{y}),\quad\mathbf{b}(\mathbf{y})=\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{y}),

where
{\boldsymbol{\kappa}_{\rm d}(\mathbf{y})=(\hat{v}_{\rm d},0)}
is chosen to be a constant velocity with
{\hat{v}_{\rm d}>0}
.
Then, we use the proposed CBF (
22
) with
{\Theta(s)={\rm ReQU}(s)/(2\mu)}
, and synthesize a safe controller via the safety filter (
7
)-(
8
) with
{\alpha(r)=\alpha_{\rm c}r}
.
We set the desired controller to be a lane-keeping controller inspired by
[
9
]
:

\mathbf{k}_{\rm d}(\mathbf{x})=\begin{bmatrix}-K_{\eta}\eta-K_{\vartheta}\sin(\vartheta)\\
K_{v}(v_{\rm d}-v)\end{bmatrix}.

(40)

with gains
{K_{\eta},K_{\vartheta},K_{v}>0}
.

.

Initially, the desired controller is used (see Fig.

(b,e)), the yaw angle is zero while the speed increases towards the desired value
v_{\rm d}
(Fig.

h
matches the constraint function
\psi
(Fig.

As the vehicle approaches the obstacle,

Then, the vehicle responds to the obstacle and the input differs from the desired value (Fig.

After passing the obstacle, the desired input is used again.
Driving away from the obstacle,
h
matches
\psi
again because the vehicle’s velocity is safe.
Finally, the yaw angle converges to zero and the speed approaches
v_{\rm d}
(Fig.

(c,f)) as the vehicle continues to travel in its lane as desired.

V
CONCLUSION

We proposed
activated backstepping
to constructively generate control barrier functions (CBFs) for safety-critical control.
We showed that activated backstepping CBFs (ABCs) are valid CBFs that provide advantages over existing high-order, rectified, or backstepping CBFs in certain scenarios.
We highlighted these advantages in a comparative example of an inverted pendulum.
Finally, we demonstrated the efficacy of the proposed ABC in safety-critical vehicle control for collision-free driving.
