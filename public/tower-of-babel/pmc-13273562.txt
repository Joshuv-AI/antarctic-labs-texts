Comparative analysis of excessive daytime sleepiness and sleep apnea symptoms in renal transplant recipients and a historical dialysis cohort
Authors: Turki Alharbi, Salman Alahmadi, Turki Almejaish, Amer Alburodi, Mohammed Alsaif, Mohammed Albader, Abdulrahman Altheaby, Ziad AL-Jahdali, Rajkumar Rajendram, Hamdan AL-Jahdali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesSleep disorders are prevalent in end-stage renal failure (ESRF) patients on dialysis. Renal transplantation may enhance the quality of sleep but comparative data are limited. The aim of our study was to assess the prevalence and factors linked to self-reported obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) and excessive daytime sleepiness (EDS) in kidney transplant recipients compared with dialysis patients.MethodsThis cross-sectional study analyzed 159 renal transplant recipients using the STOP-BANG questionnaire, Berlin Questionnaire, and Epworth Sleepiness Scale. These results were compared to those for a historical cohort of 227 dialysis patients. Multinomial logistic regression models adjusted for demographic and clinical confounders were used to explore associations.ResultsIn the combined cohort, 27.5% reported EDS (44.1% in dialysis vs. 3.8% in transplant patients, P < 0.001) and 30.6% reported OSA risk (41.9% in dialysis vs. 14.5% in transplant patients, P < 0.001). After adjusting for age, sex, body mass index, neck size, smoking, coffee intake, and comorbidities, renal transplantation was significantly associated with lower adjusted odds of EDS (odds ratio (OR): 0.014), OSA (OR: 0.422), and co-occurrence (OR: 0.092). No polysomnography data were available.ConclusionRenal transplant patients reported fewer symptoms of EDS and OSA risk compared with dialysis patients, indicating potential benefits of transplantation beyond renal function.Keywords: Obstructive Sleep Apnoea (OSA), Excessive Daytime Sleepiness (EDS), Sleep quality Dialysis, Renal transplant, Chronic renal disease

Introduction

Sleep disorders are prevalent in patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD). Approximately two-thirds of individuals with end-stage renal failure (ESRF) on dialysis experience sleep impairments, where conditions such as insomnia, excessive daytime sleepiness (EDS), and obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) are often diagnosed.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 These conditions are frequently linked to factors like dialysis, metabolic imbalances, uremia, medications, and mood disorders such as anxiety and depression.1,7 In this population, poor sleep is distressing but also associated with increased morbidity and mortality.8

Kidney transplantation restores renal function and improves several physiological stressors that contribute to sleep disturbances.9, 10, 11, 12 Improvements in insomnia and OSA have been reported.9, 10, 11, 12 The estimated prevalence of OSA ranges from 6% to 13% in the general population, whereas it varies between 30% and 80% among patients with ESRF on dialysis.12,13 Studies have indicated notable decreases in both the prevalence and severity of OSA after transplantation.12,14 Other studies found that the prevalence of OSA remains considerable in kidney transplant recipients, with rates similar to those found in dialysis patients15,16 Nevertheless, polysomnography studies reported post-transplant reductions in both severity and prevalence, but OSA persists in ∼25–45% of recipients,10,14,17, 18, 19, 20 highlighting the need for ongoing surveillance.

EDS affects 40–68% of patients with ESRF on dialysis and is influenced by factors such as comorbidities, medication, poor sleep hygiene, cardiometabolic derangements, and concomitant sleep disorders.3,21 Transplantation significantly reduces EDS in some patients, but the prevalence remains clinically significant.22 Contributing factors include immunosuppressive medications, chronic pain, and psychological stress related to recovery and lifestyle adjustments.23 Importantly, the sleep quality in transplant recipients does not match the levels reported in the general population.24

Polysomnography is the gold standard for diagnosing sleep disorders12 but its feasibility is limited in large-scale clinical settings. Validated patient-reported outcome measures, including self-reported questionnaires, provide a practical alternative for assessing symptoms such as EDS, estimating the OSA risk, and evaluating the patient burden. These tools are especially valuable in large transplant populations where scalable approaches are essential. The primary aim of this cross-sectional study was to compare the prevalence of self-reported EDS and the OSA risk between kidney transplant recipients and in a historical cohort of dialysis patients. A secondary aim was to identify the association between the self-reported OSA risk and EDS within the transplant population.

Materials and Methods

This observational, cross-sectional survey study was conducted at King Abdulaziz Medical City (KAMC), Riyadh, KSA from May 2024 to October 2024. The study was conducted in adherence to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and approval obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at the King Abdullah International Medical Research Center (KAIMRC) in Riyadh, KSA (IRB/0340/24). In accordance with regulations governing research involving human participants, informed consent before enrollment in the study was provided.

The study enrolled adult renal transplant recipients (aged 18 years or older) who attended routine follow-up at outpatient clinics. We included all renal transplant patients who attended the ambulatory outpatient clinic, unless they chose not to participate. The inclusion and exclusion criteria for dialysis patients were detailed in our previous study.3 Data were collected in person using validated, structured sleep questionnaires, which were administered by trained personnel to ensure data integrity and address participant queries.

The historical comparison group consisted of 227 patients with ESRF on dialysis, and their data were obtained from a previous study conducted at the same institution.3 Importantly, both studies employed a similar methodology and identical instruments to assess sleep symptoms, ensuring methodological consistency across cohorts, including factors such as age, sex, comorbidities, and medication usage.

Three validated Arabic-language tools were used to assess sleep symptoms. The Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS) was used, where scores greater than 10 are considered to indicate EDS.25,26

The STOP-BANG questionnaire is a widely used screening tool for identifying individuals at risk for OSA.27 The validated Arabic STOP-BANG questionnaire was used to evaluate the risk of OSA based on eight binary (Yes/No) items related to common risk factors, including snoring, daytime tiredness, observed apneas, high blood pressure, body mass index (BMI), age, neck circumference measured at the cricoid cartilage (males: 40 cm; females: 38 cm), and gender. Higher STOP-BANG scores are associated with a greater likelihood of moderate to severe OSA (0–2: low risk of OSA; 3–4: intermediate risk of OSA; 5–8: high risk of OSA).27,28

The Berlin Questionnaire (BQ) was developed in 1996, and details of the questionnaire and its validity and accuracy in the Arabic language have been reported previously.3,29,30 A high-risk BQ score predicts a respiratory disturbance index (RDI) >5 with moderate sensitivity (86%) and specificity (77%).29 Three categories are defined in BQ based on the information collected. In Category 1, a positive response is defined as frequent symptoms (>3 times per week) for the questions about snoring and witnessed apneas. In Category 2, a positive response is defined as frequent symptoms for two or more questions about fatigue, sleepiness, and/or drowsy driving. In Category 3, a positive response is defined as a self-report of hypertension and/or a BMI >30 kg/m2. Based on the data collected, patients were stratified into high-risk for OSA and low-risk for OSA groups according to their responses. Positive scores for at least two of the three categories were scored as high risk for OSA. We screened depression using an Arabic validated self-administered depression questionnaire.31

Abbreviations

ESRD: End-stage renal disease; OSA: Obstructive Sleep Apnoea; BQ: Berlin questionnaires; EDS: Excessive Daytime Sleepiness.

Data analysis

Sample size was estimated to detect a 20% absolute difference in sleep disorder prevalence between groups (e.g., 40% in dialysis vs. 20% in transplant), with 80% power and α = 0.05, where the requirement was estimated at 194 participants per group. To offset non-response, all eligible patients who attended outpatient clinics during the study period were invited. The final sample comprised 159 transplant recipients and 227 historical dialysis patients, approximating the targeted numbers.

Descriptive statistics were calculated for demographics, comorbidities, and sleep disorder symptoms, and expressed as counts, percentages, means, and standard deviations. The primary outcome was categorized into four mutually exclusive groups: no symptoms (reference category), EDS only, OSA only, and both. Associations between renal status and sleep disorder symptoms were analyzed using multinomial logistic regression adjusted for age, sex, BMI, neck size, smoking, coffee intake, and relevant comorbidities. Results were reported as odds ratios (OR) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs), and statistically significant differences were accepted at P < 0.05. We employed the Hosmer–Lemeshow test to assess the goodness of fit for the model. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results

In total, 159 patients with renal transplants participated, as well as 227 dialysis patients from the historical comparator group.3 As shown in Table 1, renal transplant patients were younger, with a lower BMI, and lower prevalence of diabetes and smoking, and had substantially lower rates of EDS and OSA compared with the historical dialysis cohort. Differences in sleepiness and OSA were probably influenced by the younger age and lower BMI in the transplant group. The combined cohort comprised 386 patients with CKD (mean age 51.0 ± 12.5 years, 61.4% male), and their demographic and clinical characteristics are shown in Table 1. Overall, 27.5% reported EDS and 30.6% exhibited OSA symptoms (Table 1). When grouped into mutually exclusive categories, 11.9% had EDS only, 15.0% had OSA only, and 15.5% had both OSA and EDS.

Discussion

Our findings based on this single-center, cross-sectional study indicate that renal transplant recipients reported significantly fewer symptoms of sleep disorders (i.e., EDS, OSA risk, or both) compared with a historical cohort of patients on dialysis, even after adjusting for potential confounders (age, sex, BMI, and neck circumference). Validated Arabic-language instruments were used to assess symptoms across both groups, where this consistent methodology reduced the measurement bias. Thus, these data add to the existing literature suggesting that improvements in renal function post-transplantation may mitigate selected factors that contribute to sleep-related breathing disorders and daytime somnolence in patients with CKD.32, 33, 34 The mechanism that underlies this effect may be related to metabolic changes and improvements in the regulation of fluid balance.10

EDS affects 40–68% of patients with ESRF on dialysis and is influenced by factors such as comorbidities, medication, poor sleep hygiene, cardiometabolic derangements, and concomitant sleep disorders.3,21 EDS is one of the main clinical manifestations of OSA and is a treatment target. Approximately 20–50% of patients with OSA report EDS.35,36 The presence of EDS with OSA indicates symptomatic OSA. In patients with OSA, EDS is associated with a worse quality of life, worse functional status, and more severe driving impairment.37

In the present study, we found a significant decrease in symptomatic OSA following renal transplantation, as assessed based on EDS and either the BQ or STOP-BANG questionnaires. However, its occurrence was significantly reduced in post-renal transplant patients.14 Post-renal transplant EDS has several potential etiologies, including the effects of immunosuppressant medications, obesity, comorbidities, and OSA14 In the present study, we observed significantly less self-reported EDS (3.8%) in the transplant cohort than the dialysis cohort (44.1%). Improvement in EDS is probably due to decreases in metabolic derangement and sleep disorders, including sleep quality, insomnia, OSA, and RLS9,12,38,39

However, some previous studies found no changes in sleep quality after renal transplantation, whereas others observed improvements that did not reach levels comparable to those in the general population23,24,34,40,41 Indeed, in the present cohort, OSA and EDS symptoms were not eliminated, and their prevalence post-transplant remained clinically significant, as evidenced by the ESS, BQ, and STOP-BANG criteria. Our observations are consistent with other studies that reported persistent OSA symptoms in 25–45% of transplant recipients.10,14,17, 18, 19, 20,24 The improvement in OSA following transplantation is associated with enhancement of metabolic balance, the resolution of metabolic derangements in the form of uremia, acidosis, and hypervolemia, and alleviation of mood disorders such as anxiety and depression.1,7 However, these improvements can be offset by the weight gain that often occurs after transplantation, which may result in the persistence of OSA in ∼25–45% of recipients.10,14,17, 18, 19, 20 The variable improvement in sleep quality is probably multifactorial, reflecting the tools used for assessing the sleep quality, age, education, marital condition, home environment, employment, and psychosocial issues of transplant recipients.33,34,42

Sleep disorders can negatively impact kidney allograft function and recipient outcomes, highlighting the critical importance of systematic screening for sleep disorders and their management to promote the health and longevity of both the graft and the recipient,43,44 while also improving the quality of life post-transplant. Thus, reducing the EDS and OSA risk post-renal transplant is clinically important. Untreated OSA can cause cardiometabolic complications, and recurrent hypoxia during sleep may compromise graft survival.17,33,45

Limitations, strengths, and weaknesses

The present study had several limitations that should be acknowledged. The single-center design limits the generalizability of the findings, and the use of a historical control group introduces the possibility of bias due to variations in clinical practice and unmeasured confounders that may have changed over time. Another limitation is that recruitment was limited to clinic visitors who agreed to participate over a 6-month period. Extending recruitment to one year or longer would probably have increased the sample size and study power. Crucially, the reliance on questionnaire-based screening tools (BQ and STOP-BANG) rather than polysomnography, which is the gold standard for OSA diagnosis, was a major methodological limitation. Questionnaires are practical for large-scale studies, but are less sensitive and specific than objective measures of sleep apnea. Thus, the assessment of OSA risk was presumptive rather than diagnostic. Finally, in the present study, we did not specifically analyze the potential influence of different immunosuppressant regimens on sleep disorders, which could be an important area for future investigation. However, this study also had several strengths. The use of validated Arabic versions of the ESS and the STOP-BANG questionnaire increases the applicability of the findings within the study population. The inclusion of a historical control group of ESRF patients on dialysis provided a valuable comparison to help understand the symptom burden in a similar patient population. Furthermore, the adjustment for multiple potential confounders in the regression analyses strengthened the confidence in the associations observed between renal transplantation and self-reported sleep disorders.

Conclusion

This single-center, cross-sectional study suggested that renal transplantation was significantly associated with a reduced likelihood of self-reported sleep disorder symptoms and improved sleep quality compared with a historical cohort of dialysis patients. These patient-reported symptomatic improvements highlight the potential benefits of transplantation for addressing sleep health in patients with chronic renal failure and improving their quality of life. Our results are relevant to screening-based identification of sleep disorders, rather than a formal clinical diagnosis. However, definitive conclusions regarding the prevalence of sleep disorders and direct causation require prospective longitudinal studies utilizing polysomnography as the diagnostic gold standard. The present study provides a strong foundation for future research, emphasizing the need for comprehensive sleep assessment in this vulnerable population.
