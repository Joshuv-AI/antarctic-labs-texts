Federated learning with LSTM and error correcting codes for secure and private identification of IoT devices
Authors: Shaya A Alshaya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rapid proliferation of Internet-of-Things (IoT) devices has enabled seamless automation and data-driven intelligence across diverse domains, including smart homes, healthcare, and industrial systems. However, the resulting hyper-connectivity also expands the attack surface, raising critical concerns regarding privacy, device identification, and network security. This paper introduces FL-HDECOC, a novel federated learning-based framework for privacy-preserving device identification in heterogeneous IoT environments. The proposed model combines the temporal modeling capabilities of Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks with the robustness of Error-Correcting Output Codes (DECOC) for multi-class classification, integrated within a federated architecture that ensures data locality and user privacy. Differential privacy is further employed to formally protect shared model updates against inference attacks. Experimental evaluations demonstrate that FL-HDECOC outperforms baseline models, achieving 96.2% accuracy and 95.5% F1-score, while reducing communication rounds by 25% under privacy budget ε = 1.0. These results highlight its promise for secure and scalable IoT deployments.Keywords: Federated learning, Privacy-preserving machine learning, IoT device identification, LSTM, DECOCSubject terms: Electrical and electronic engineering, Information theory and computation

Introduction

In the era of pervasive connectivity and automation, a vast array of household and industrial devices is increasingly interconnected via the Internet. Machine learning (ML) applications that leverage data streams from heterogeneous Internet-of-Things (IoT) environments, such as smart homes, intelligent transportation systems, industrial automation, and digital healthcare, demonstrate substantial potential1–3. The ongoing transition to Industry 5.0, which marks a significant technological evolution, combines artificial intelligence (AI) with advanced network infrastructures to develop intelligent, robust, and scalable systems that contribute meaningfully to human progress4,5. Nevertheless, this hyperconnectivity introduces substantial risks to the confidentiality and integrity of data transmitted across interconnected devices. Consequently, safeguarding communication within IoT ecosystems is a central concern. Traditional security measures, such as cryptographic mechanisms (e.g., encryption, hashing, digital signatures), firewalls, and intrusion detection/prevention systems (IDS/IPS), remain fundamental in protecting networks against a wide range of attack vectors6–8.

Recent studies have explored the integration of emerging technologies, including ML, deep learning (DL), federated learning (FL), and privacy-preserving computation, with IDS frameworks to design decentralized and privacy-conscious detection mechanisms9,10. Despite these advances, there remains a noticeable gap in unified frameworks that combine temporal modeling, multiclass classification, and federated privacy guarantees tailored specifically for IoT environments.

The proliferation of IoT devices has been significant in recent years. Projections from Finances Online estimate that the number of connected devices will increase to approximately 25.44 billion by 203011. Likewise, forecasts by IoT Analytics suggest that global IoT device installations will reach 29.70 billion by 202912. This massive expansion not only increases the heterogeneity of the ecosystem, but also broadens the attack surface, exposing devices to a wider range of vulnerabilities. The diversity and complexity of potential attack scenarios necessitate robust, multilayered defenses to prevent the infiltration of malware, worms, and other malicious software.

In this context, we propose a novel hybrid architecture called Federated Learning-Hybrid DECOC (FL-HDECOC)for privacy-preserving device identification in IoT networks. The proposed model combines the sequence modeling capabilities of Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks with the robust, error-tolerant classification mechanism of Error-Correcting Output Codes (DECOC), embedded within a federated learning framework. The rationale behind this integration is threefold: (1) LSTM effectively captures temporal behavioral patterns from device performance metrics; (2) DECOC enables scalable and noise-resilient multiclass classification; and (3) federated learning ensures that raw device data remain local, thereby preserving privacy. Differential privacy is further incorporated to safeguard against inference attacks from shared-model updates.

The primary contributions of this study are threefold. First, we propose a novel hybrid federated architecture, FL-HDECOC, which integrates Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks with Error-Correcting Output Codes (DECOC) to enable the effective and privacy-preserving identification of IoT devices. This hybrid approach leverages the temporal modeling capabilities of LSTM and the robustness of DECOC in multiclass classification to address the challenges of heterogeneity and data sensitivity in decentralized environments. Second, we incorporate differential privacy into the federated learning framework to provide formal privacy guarantees during model training, ensuring that sensitive information remains protected even in the presence of potentially adversarial aggregation. Third, we validate the effectiveness of the proposed model through extensive experiments, comparing FL-HDECOC against several baseline architectures, including Federated LSTM (FedLSTM), Transformer-based FL, and centralized models. The results demonstrated that FL-HDECOC outperformed these baselines in terms of classification accuracy, training convergence, and resilience under privacy-preserving constraints.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section II presents related works on privacy-preserving federated learning and classification. Section III details the proposed FL-HDECOC methodology. Section IV discusses the implementation and evaluation of the results. Section V concludes the study and outlines future research directions.

Related works

The rise of data-centric technologies and the proliferation of Internet of Things (IoT) devices have intensified the demand for robust privacy-preserving mechanisms. These devices generate large quantities of data that are often processed using machine learning (ML) and artificial intelligence (AI) techniques. Although such applications offer significant advantages across smart homes, healthcare, transportation, and industrial automation, they simultaneously increase the potential for privacy breaches. The sensitive nature of the collected data necessitates the implementation of privacy-preserving technologies (PPTs) that can ensure data confidentiality without compromising analytical utility13. Modern privacy-preserving mechanisms (PPMs) aim to minimize the collection and exposure of sensitive user data, while enabling organizations and institutions to extract meaningful insights. With the integration of AI and data-sharing protocols across IoT ecosystems, the challenge of privacy maintenance has become increasingly complex.

Federated Learning (FL), for example, offers a decentralized learning model in which individual devices train models locally and share only model updates rather than raw data with a central server. This strategy significantly mitigates the risk of privacy leakage during training14. Numerous legal frameworks have been established to ensure compliance with global privacy regulations. The General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) in the European Union and the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) in the United States are prominent examples of stringent data-protection standards. In alignment with such frameworks, technologies such as anonymization, secure multi-party computation (SMPC), homomorphic encryption (HE), differential privacy (DP), blockchain, and trusted execution environments (TEEs) have been developed and adopted in various IoT and data-processing scenarios15,16.

Differential Privacy (DP) is a mathematically grounded framework designed to protect individual data entries by introducing controlled random noise into statistical outputs. A mechanism is said to satisfy ε-differential privacy if the inclusion or exclusion of a single record in a dataset does not significantly alter the outcome of any analysis, thereby minimizing the risk of revealing sensitive information. Two main models of DP are commonly applied: local differential privacy (LDP), in which users perturb their data before sharing it, and global differential privacy (GDP), in which a trusted data curator applies noise after aggregation17,18. Homomorphic Encryption (HE) offers another promising privacy-preserving approach that allows computations to be performed directly on encrypted data. This ensures that sensitive information remains confidential throughout the processing pipeline. Fully Homomorphic Encryption (FHE) enables arbitrary computations on ciphertexts, while Somewhat Homomorphic Encryption (SHE) supports only a limited set of operations. Despite its strong theoretical foundations, HE is constrained by high computational overhead, which limits its practical adoption in real-time or resource-constrained environments, such as IoT systems19. Secure Multi-Party computation (SMPC) enables multiple participants to collaboratively compute a function over their inputs without disclosing them to one another. This is achieved through cryptographic techniques, such as secret sharing and oblivious transfer, ensuring that privacy is maintained even during collaborative processing. SMPC is particularly valuable in contexts where data confidentiality is paramount, such as healthcare analytics, secure voting, and financial transactions20. Trusted Execution Environments (TEEs) represent hardware-based solutions that isolate sensitive computations from the main operating system and software stack. By executing code within a secure enclave protected by hardware-enforced boundaries, TEEs provide robust defense against software-level attacks. One significant advancement in this domain is Verifiable Hardware (Ve-H), which extends the TEE functionality to support secure notarization and lineage tracking using monotonic counters and trusted timestamps21.

Blockchain technology, which is characterized by its decentralized structure and immutable ledger, offers tamper-resistant record-keeping and cryptographic integrity. Although it is widely adopted in applications such as digital identity verification, healthcare record management, and financial systems, permissioned blockchains often suffer from limitations such as high latency, low throughput, and storage inefficiency. To address these challenges, Alibaba Cloud developed LedgerDB, a centralized ledger that emulates blockchain properties such as tamper-evidence and non-repudiation, while improving auditability and performance through timestamp authorities and Dasein Verification22. Complementing this, the Verifiable Database (VeDB) has emerged as a high-performance alternative, integrating advanced data structures and verification mechanisms to ensure efficient and scalable auditability21. Nonetheless, existing blockchain and ledger-based solutions still grapple with verification overhead, immutable data management, and evolving privacy threats, particularly in large-scale distributed systems.

Recent advancements in edge computing and federated learning have been applied to UAV systems, demonstrating the feasibility of privacy-aware frameworks for distributed intelligence. For instance, Yao et al.23 proposed a biometric privacy protection framework in UAV delivery systems, while other efforts have addressed secure identity management24, location privacy25, and edge-based security frameworks26. FedShufde27 introduced a privacy-preserving FL framework tailored to UAV environments, and MoCFL28,29 demonstrated the potential of mobile clustering for dynamic edge networks. These works collectively emphasize the importance of decentralized architectures, privacy guarantees, and edge intelligence, which align with the motivations of FL-HDECOC but are largely focused on UAV and mobility contexts rather than heterogeneous IoT environments. Our work complements these studies by introducing a behavior-driven, privacy-preserving federated framework specifically for general-purpose IoT device identification, incorporating error-correcting classification and differential privacy.

These existing approaches lack a unified solution that simultaneously addresses temporal dynamics, scalable multiclass classification, and privacy in federated settings. Our FL-HDECOC framework directly tackles this triad by integrating LSTM and DECOC with differential privacy guarantees, specifically tailored for heterogeneous IoT environments.

Proposed methodology

In this section, we present the design and underlying rationale of the proposed Federated Learning-Hybrid DECOC (FL-HDECOC) framework for privately preserving IoT device identification. The exponential increase in the deployment of resource-constrained heterogeneous IoT devices poses significant challenges in achieving secure, scalable, and privacy-aware identification, especially under decentralized data distributions. The FL-HDECOC framework is specifically engineered to address three core challenges: (1) temporal heterogeneity in device behavior, (2) scalability of multiclass identification, and (3) preservation of data privacy under strict regulatory and architectural constraints.

As illustrated in Fig. 1, FL-HDECOC integrates the temporal sequence modeling capabilities of Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks with a robust multi-class classification mechanism of Error-Correcting Output Codes (DECOC). This integration occurs within a federated learning (FL) paradigm in which models are collaboratively trained without sharing raw data across the network. Each client device, such as a Raspberry Pi or edge processor, collects behavioral data consisting of low-level hardware telemetry, including CPU cycles, GPU utilization, RAM access patterns, and thermal readings, sampled at 1-second intervals over a fixed window (e.g., 60 s). These metrics are indicative of the hardware’s real-time operational signature and serve as the basis for behavioral fingerprinting.

FL-HDECOC framework architecture. The diagram includes key components: real-time telemetry collection, LSTM sequence modeling, DECOC ensemble classifiers, differential privacy (DP) perturbation module, and global aggregation server.

The collected data underwent local preprocessing, including min-max normalization, noise filtering, and transformation into sliding windows to preserve the temporal structure. Each window was represented as a multivariate time-series input to a two-layer LSTM network configured with 64 hidden units per layer and ReLU activation. LSTM compresses the temporal sequence into a fixed-size embedding vector (typically of size 128), which encapsulates the behavioral dynamics of the device over the observation window.

This embedding vector is then input to the DECOC classification module, which employs a 7-bit binary code matrix to decompose the multiclass identification task into a set of seven binary classification subtasks. The 7-bit matrix was chosen based on the minimal code length required for 5-class classification with fault tolerance. It allows correction of single-bit errors and simplifies model complexity, striking a balance between computational efficiency and classification robustness. Each binary task was handled by a separate lightweight multilayer perceptron (MLP) consisting of one hidden layer with 32 neurons and a sigmoid output. The outputs from these binary classifiers form a predicted binary code, which is decoded using Hamming distance minimization to infer the final device label.

Unlike conventional softmax-based classifiers or one-vs-rest approaches, DECOC improves multiclass generalization by encoding label redundancy through binary subproblems. This encoding offers enhanced fault tolerance, especially useful in federated settings where partial model drift and noisy updates are common. This error-tolerant decoding mechanism makes DECOC particularly well suited for federated scenarios with noisy updates or partial model drift.

The local LSTM-DECOC model was trained on the device using cross-entropy loss for each binary classifier, with adaptive learning rates governed by the Adam optimizer. To preserve data confidentiality, the federated learning protocol strictly restricts data access at the device level. During each communication round, only model parameter updates (e.g., gradients or weights) are shared with the central aggregator server. To further mitigate the risk of information leakage through model updates, we employ differential privacy (DP) using the Gaussian mechanism with a noise multiplier calibrated to maintain ε-differential privacy per client update.

The central server receives DP-protected updates from a subset of participating devices and performs weighted model aggregation using a Federated Averaging (FedAvg) algorithm. This iterative process continued over multiple rounds (typically 50–100) until model convergence was achieved. Importantly, the design supports asynchronous client participation, accounting for the intermittent connectivity and computational diversity typical of edge devices in real-world IoT deployment. Asynchronous participation allows clients to upload model updates at irregular intervals, which reflects real-world device availability. Our experiments confirm that FL-HDECOC maintains stable convergence under such conditions due to the error-correcting properties of DECOC, which buffer inconsistencies in updates.

Despite its modular structure, FL-HDECOC remains computationally lightweight: each LSTM-DECOC model requires approximately 2.1 MB of memory, enabling efficient training even on Raspberry Pi 4 devices. Moreover, the 7-bit DECOC design reduces multiclass complexity to binary submodels, which improves scalability across large device pools.

Figure 1 visually outlines the system architecture, showing the flow of information from real-time telemetry capture at the device level to federated model refinement at the global level. The key modules include the on-device LSTM for feature extraction, DECOC-based binary classification ensemble, differential privacy engine that perturbs updates, and federated server responsible for secure aggregation and model distribution. These components work in concert to enable the high-accuracy, scalable, and privacy-compliant identification of IoT devices.

In conclusion, the FL-HDECOC framework distinguishes itself through algorithmic hybridization, temporal modeling capacity, and privacy-by-design integration. Unlike broadly defined federated models, FL-HDECOC targets the specific challenge of behavior-based device identification with formal privacy guarantees and a modular architecture that is adaptable to heterogeneous and constrained IoT environments.

Implementation and results

The proposed FL-HDECOC framework was implemented using Python with the TensorFlow and PyTorch libraries. The federated learning component was simulated using the flower framework, which allowed the orchestration of distributed training across simulated IoT clients. Experiments were conducted on a dataset derived from LwHBench that featured behavioral metrics from Raspberry Pi devices under varying workloads.

Each local device instance was allocated 60% of the data for training, 20% for validation, and 20% for testing. The LSTM network used two layers, with 64 hidden units each, followed by a dropout rate of 0.3. Training used a learning rate of 0.001, batch size of 32, and weight decay of 1e-5. The optimizer was Adam with default β₁=0.9 and β₂=0.999. Training was performed for 10 epochs per communication round. To simulate non-IID distributions, each client was assigned data from a distinct subset of device types and operating conditions. This reflects realistic heterogeneity where each IoT device encounters unique workload patterns. The DECOC classifier was constructed with a 7-bit coding matrix for a 5-class device identification task employing shallow MLPs as binary learners. Federated training was conducted over 50 rounds, with ten clients participating asynchronously in each round. Differential privacy was enforced by using a Gaussian mechanism with ε = 1.0.

The third baseline employs a one-dimensional convolutional neural network (1D-CNN) trained locally on each device without federated aggregation or differential privacy. This configuration emphasizes the local feature extraction capabilities without collaborative learning. Additionally, a traditional Random Forest classifier was included as a non-deep learning baseline to represent the ensemble methods commonly used in device fingerprinting tasks. Finally, a lightweight transformer-based federated learning model is evaluated to explore the performance of attention-based architectures in distributed settings.

Additionally, qualitative inspection of the misclassification patterns revealed that DECOC-based decoding prevents frequent class confusion among devices with similar behavior profiles, which is a limitation in softmax-based federated models.

We also evaluate the impact of varying ε values on differential privacy. As shown in Fig. 2, FL-HDECOC maintained a robust performance for ε values between 0.5 and 2.0, demonstrating its ability to balance privacy and accuracy.

Accuracy vs. Privacy Budget (ε) in FL-HDECOC.

Figure 2 illustrates the impact of the differential privacy budget (ε) on the classification performance of the proposed FL-HDECOC framework. The privacy budget ε governs the magnitude of the noise injected into the local model updates during the federated training. Smaller ε values correspond to stronger privacy guarantees and greater noise, whereas larger values reduce privacy protection but preserve more accurate model gradients. The results revealed a non-linear relationship between ε and classification accuracy. When ε is set to 0.1, the framework achieves an accuracy of 91.2%, which reflects the degradation in model performance owing to heavy noise injection. This result confirms that excessive perturbation of model updates adversely affects the convergence of the local models and the overall learning process.

As ε increases from 0.1 to 1.0, the accuracy improves significantly, peaking at 96.2% for ε = 1.0. This indicates that moderate noise levels offer an optimal tradeoff between privacy and model utility. In this range, the injected noise is sufficient to ensure reasonable privacy guarantees, while still allowing the model to learn effectively from the underlying data distribution. Beyond ε = 1.0, the accuracy experiences a slight decline, with values of 95.7% and 95.1% recorded for ε = 1.5 and ε = 2.0, respectively. Although the privacy guarantees weaken in this range, the marginal performance loss may stem from overfitting or the reduced regularization effect provided by noise. These results suggest that, while loosening the privacy constraint reduces the risk of noise-related degradation, it does not necessarily yield further performance gains. Even under strong privacy constraints (ε ≤ 1), the framework maintains a high accuracy, which underscores the strength of its hybrid architecture. The combination of temporal modeling via LSTM and error-tolerant classification via DECOC enables the model to generalize effectively despite the noise perturbations in the federated setting. These findings have practical implications for privacy-aware IoT deployments. System designers can select an appropriate ε value based on application requirements: lower ε values for high-sensitivity environments, such as healthcare or finance, and higher ε values for scenarios that prioritize accuracy, such as smart homes or industrial automation. The model’s graceful performance degradation across privacy levels also opens opportunities for adaptive privacy-control mechanisms, where the privacy budget can be tuned dynamically according to contextual sensitivity or user preferences.

To evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed FL-HDECOC framework rigorously, a series of comparative experiments were conducted using several well-established baseline models. The first baseline, Centralized LSTM-DECOC, uses the same underlying architecture as FL-HDECOC, but is trained in a centralized setting, allowing access to the complete dataset without privacy constraints. This model served as an upper-bound performance reference. The second model, FedLSTM, adopts a standard federated learning approach utilizing only LSTM layers, thereby lacking the error-resilient structure of DECOC. This offers a meaningful comparison for assessing the contribution of the DECOC within the federated setting.

Under balanced (1:1) data distribution, the model rapidly achieves convergence, surpassing 96% accuracy within 20 communication rounds. As the heterogeneity increases to moderate (1:5) and strong (1:10) levels, convergence slows slightly, with final accuracies of 94.8% and 92.4% reached after approximately 23 and 27 rounds, respectively. The error-tolerant DECOC component contributes to this resilience by mitigating the effects of non-representative local datasets, enabling effective multiclass generalization despite the challenges introduced by non-IID and imbalanced distributions.

The communication efficiency of the FL-HDECOC was compared with that of the FedLSTM and Transformer-FL models. Figure 4 shows that the proposed model achieves faster convergence owing to the lightweight DECOC classifiers.

Training convergence of FL-HDECOC vs. baseline models.

Figure 4 presents the training convergence behavior of the proposed FL-HDECOC model in comparison with two baseline federated learning architectures: FedLSTM and Transformer-FL. The FL-HDECOC framework demonstrated superior convergence characteristics in terms of both speed and final performance. Notably, it achieved a validation accuracy of over 90% within the first 10 communication rounds and converged to a peak accuracy exceeding 96% by round 20. This rapid convergence is attributed to the hybrid model architecture, where the LSTM layer effectively captures temporal dependencies in device behavior, and the DECOC module introduces redundancy that stabilizes multiclass classification.

In terms of communication efficiency, FL-HDECOC achieved convergence approximately 25% faster than Transformer-FL, completing training in 20 rounds. Each communication round required approximately 320 KB of data transmission per client, a bandwidth demand well within the capabilities of typical IoT edge uplinks (~ 1 Mbps).

To evaluate computational overhead, we profiled the model on Raspberry Pi 4 clients. Training consumed approximately 41 MB of RAM and peaked at 24% CPU usage per round. Network latency remained below 200 ms per communication round under simulated LTE conditions, demonstrating the model’s suitability for resource-constrained edge environments.

In contrast, the FedLSTM model converged more slowly, reaching approximately 93% accuracy after 20 rounds. Although it leverages temporal modeling, its monolithic structure lacks the error correction and redundancy advantages of DECOC, making it more susceptible to local overfitting and slower generalization across clients. The Transformer-FL model exhibited the slowest convergence, stabilizing at approximately 91% in the final round. Although transformer-based architectures are powerful for sequence modeling, their training complexity and sensitivity to parameter initialization can hinder performance in federated environments with non-independent and identically distributed (IID) data. Transformers typically require more data and computational resources, making them less efficient for edge-device scenarios. These results demonstrated the effectiveness of the FL-HDECOC design in federated learning contexts. The use of lightweight binary classifiers in the DECOC framework combined with robust LSTM-based feature extraction enables efficient and stable convergence with fewer communication rounds. This advantage is particularly critical in IoT applications, where communication cost and device heterogeneity are major constraints.

The proposed FL-HDECOC framework outperformed existing federated methods in terms of accuracy and training efficiency, while providing strong privacy guarantees. Its hybrid design allows for effective multiclass classification with a minimal communication burden. Moreover, the integration of LSTM enables robust temporal modeling, which is crucial for behavioral-based device identification.

These results validate the effectiveness of combining DECOC and LSTM in federated environments and open avenues for further research on cross-device generalization and adaptive code matrix optimization.

Conclusion

This paper introduced FL-HDECOC, a novel federated learning framework for privacy-preserving IoT device identification that combines the temporal modeling power of Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks with the robustness of Error-Correcting Output Codes (DECOC) for multiclass classification. The proposed approach addresses the pressing challenges of data privacy, device heterogeneity, and scalability in decentralized IoT environments. By integrating LSTM and DECOC within a federated learning paradigm, FL-HDECOC effectively captured behavioral patterns from time-series device data while preserving the classification accuracy under strong privacy constraints. Differential privacy mechanisms further enhance data protection by ensuring that no sensitive information is revealed during model updates. The experimental results demonstrate that the FL-HDECOC framework outperforms conventional federated approaches, such as FedLSTM and Transformer-FL, in terms of classification accuracy, convergence speed, and resilience to privacy perturbations. The convergence behavior and accuracy-privacy trade-off analyses highlight the practical applicability of FL-HDECOC for real-world deployments where privacy preservation and communication efficiency are critical. These findings suggest that FL-HDECOC is a promising solution for next-generation smart environments such as industrial IoT, healthcare monitoring systems, and smart city infrastructures.

Future research will focus on advancing the proposed framework by incorporating adaptive privacy control mechanisms that dynamically adjust privacy budgets based on contextual sensitivity. Additionally, efforts will be directed toward enabling robust cross-device generalization and validating the framework through real-world deployments across heterogeneous IoT hardware platforms. To further assess scalability and practical applicability, future work will also involve extending the classification scope to encompass a broader range of device types and evaluating the model’s performance under more complex non-IID data distributions, including highly imbalanced scenarios representative of production-scale environments.
