Host immunological response of Ross 308 broilers fed an anti-IL-10 antibody to Eimeria and Clostridium perfringens challenge
Authors: Michael Carroll, Krysten Fries-Craft, Yuyang Cao, Melissa Monson, Stephan Schmitz-Esser, Elizabeth A. Bobeck
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Understanding host response mechanisms may lead to improved management of poultry diseases such as necrotic enteritis. Ross 308 broilers (20 birds/cage, 32 cages) received diets ± anti-interleukin-10 (IL-10) antibody, an intervention to improve immune activation, in three experimental replicates (R1, R2, R3). R1 and R2 received 1 × 108 colony forming units (CFU) Salmonella Typhimurium on day (d) 1. On d 14, birds were assigned to unchallenged control or 15,000 sporulated Eimeria maxima oocyst gavage challenge, with a subset receiving E. maxima + Clostridium perfringens (1 × 10⁸ CFU d 18–19) to induce necrotic enteritis. Intestinal tissue and digesta samples were collected from 6 birds/treatment at baseline (d 14), peak infection (d 21), and post-peak infection (d 25) to measure luminal IL-10 (ELISA), interferon gamma (IFNγ) via immunohistochemistry, and IL-10, CD4, CD8, CD25, and KUL01 via RNAscope. Data were analyzed via two-way ANOVA (P ≤ 0.05). In R3, baseline duodenal IL-10 was 458 ng/mg protein greater in birds fed the control diet, while E. maxima + C. perfringens inoculation increased jejunal and ileal IL-10 content by 528 and 649 ng/mg protein at peak infection (P < 0.05). In R2, peak jejunal tissue % IL-10 area increased during E. maxima + C. perfringens challenge (P < 0.05). Baseline and peak jejunal R3 RNA was sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000, mapped to the chicken genome, and differential expression identified with DESeq2 (|log₂ fold change| > 1; FDR ≤ 0.05). At peak, E. maxima challenge overrepresented genes involved in cell cycle, lipid metabolism, and B cell proliferation (P < 0.002), while E. maxima + C. perfringens overrepresented defense response and catabolic process terms (P < 0.0001). Sex altered the host immune response to challenge (P < 0.05), while E. maxima ± C. perfringens induced a similar host immune response regardless of dietary anti-IL-10. Overall, pathogenic challenge status was the primary driver of host immune response with the effect of anti-IL-10 antibody influenced by S. Typhimurium.

Introduction

Coccidiosis and necrotic enteritis continue to be prevalent immune challenges for broiler production (Blake et al., 2020). Current practices for necrotic enteritis prevention and treatment are not sufficient for control of Eimeria and Clostridium perfringens challenges in broilers. For example, Eimeria vaccination programs are strain and species specific, leading to incomplete coverage (Dalloul and Lillehoj, 2006). As the broiler industry continues to maintain or increase the overall percentages of flocks in antibiotic free production, antibiotics are not available as a tool to reduce or prevent necrotic enteritis (Noack et al., 2019). Furthermore, there is an increased concern for Eimeria resistance to available prevention programs (Arabkhazaeli et al., 2013). Therefore, there is a need to further understand the host immune response to necrotic enteritis challenge to develop additional interventions for control of Eimeria and C. perfringens.

An anti-interleukin-10 (IL-10) antibody has previously been proposed as an intervention for necrotic enteritis through IL-10 neutralization and thereby improving the host immune response (Rasheed et al., 2020a, 2020b; Sand et al., 2016). IL-10 shifts the host immune response to a more anti-inflammatory state, decreasing interferon gamma (IFNγ)-producing T helper 1 (TH1) cells and macrophage activity (Couper et al., 2008). Downregulation of tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) from IL-10 signaling reduces the pro-inflammatory response (Trifunovic et al., 2015). Stimulation of host IL-10 production by Eimeria spp. impacts the ability of the host to mount an effective immune response leading to reduced clearance of intracellular pathogens (Berger, 2000). Hong et al. (2006) has shown an upregulation of IL10 mRNA up to 9 d post-infection with Eimeria. Therefore, the evasion mechanism of Eimeria spp. via increased host IL-10 production provides a potential target for dietary intervention.

Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium has been utilized previously to generate a repeatable necrotic enteritis research model (Coles et al., 2021; Hernandez-Patlan et al., 2019). Salmonella Typhimurium produces a more favorable environment for Eimeria and C. perfringens challenge through an IL-10 mediated pathway suppressing local immune responses (Kogut and Arsenault, 2017; Shivaramaiah et al., 2011). Saturation of IL-10 in the luminal environment from a preexisting S. Typhimurium infection could potentially reduce the efficacy of the anti-IL-10 supplementation. Previously published data from this study showed S. Typhimurium influences performance and immunometabolic response during an Eimeria maxima and C. perfringens challenge (Fries-Craft and Bobeck, 2024a, 2024b).

Although impacts on certain components of a systemic immune response have been investigated (Fries-Craft and Bobeck, 2024b), it is equally important to understand the host-specific responses to the anti-IL-10 antibody in the intestine. Both the site of action for the antibody provided through the diet and the site of interaction between the host and these pathogens provide context for the host immune response to anti-IL-10 antibody. Luminal IL-10 content quantification and immunohistochemistry can offer insight into the host cytokine production at the tissue level. Additionally, a complete transcriptional analysis of intestinal samples provides perspective on the host immune response and other cellular responses, including potential involvement of novel genes and pathways. Previous transcriptome analysis of white leghorns during an Eimeria acervulina infection has shown differences in carbohydrate metabolism, lipid metabolism, and immune response/defense transcripts in duodenal tissue (Kim et al., 2009).

Transcriptome analysis allows for the determination of sex based on sex-linked chromosomal genes allowing for an additional layer of analysis considering the effect of sex. There has been little work done to understand the impact of sex on the broiler response to Eimeria or necrotic enteritis challenge. Zhu et al. (2000) found significantly higher serum IFNγ in female broilers compared to males under challenge with E. maxima. A meta-analysis of Eimeria challenge models in broilers showed females were more susceptible to performance depression (Kipper et al., 2013). While sex chromosome-linked genes have been investigated in poultry for embryonic development, with particular interest in mono-sex culturing of layer hens (Sun et al., 2023), and sex of the bird can influence transcriptomic expression patterns during infectious disease challenge (Deist et al., 2017), there has not been significant investigation into the effect of sex on transcriptional regulation during a health challenge with Eimeria or necrotic enteritis. Evaluation of anti-IL-10 antibody for Eimeria infections have not previously separated the host responses by sex (Rasheed et al., 2020a, 2020b; Sand et al., 2016). Therefore, the objective of the current study was to investigate the intestinal immunological response of Ross 308 broilers to E. maxima and E. maxima + C. perfringens challenge when fed anti-IL-10 antibody.

Materials and methods

Animal care and handling

The Iowa State University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee approved all live animal procedures (IACUC #19-156). Samples used to conduct the host immunological response analyses herein were collected as part of previously published research investigating the mechanism of anti-IL-10 antibody during coccidiosis and necrotic enteritis in broiler chicks (Fries-Craft and Bobeck, 2024a, 2024b). Animal husbandry, sample collection timelines, feed formulation, and challenge inoculum preparation have been described in-depth by Fries-Craft and Bobeck (2024a). Briefly, 3 replicate studies were performed using straight run Ross 308 broilers with a challenge period from day 0-25; replicate 1 (R1), replicate 2 (R2), and replicate 3 (R3) will be referred to as such henceforth. Each replicate study was designed in a 2 × 3 factorial with anti-IL-10 antibody diet and challenge as the main effects. Dietary supplementation of the anti-IL-10 antibody was selected as a practical daily route of continuous administration any time the bird consumed feed, which could be readily adopted in commercial settings with a much higher bird census. The experiment consisted of 3 replicates over time, with two model challenge designs: 2 replicates with Salmonella Typhimurium and a 3rd replicate without Salmonella Typhimurium. The logic for R1 and R2 followed a broiler chick Necrotic Enteritis model previously developed that found the addition of S. Typhimurium improved model impact and repeatability (Shivaramaiah et al., 2011).

The first two replicates (R1 and R2) inoculated chicks on day (d) 1 with 1 × 108 colony forming units (CFU) S. Typhimurium for birds in the E. maxima or E. maxima + C. perfringens challenge group (Shivaramaiah et al., 2011; Table 1). Because Salmonella is known to upregulate IL-10 production as a host evasion mechanism, the logic for the 3rd replicate (R3) excluding IL-10 was to assess the impact without an additional source of host IL-10 upregulation. Therefore, R3 was conducted without S. Typhimurium inoculation on d1, with the rest of the R3 challenge model identical to R1 and R2.

Three challenge treatments were applied across models, regardless of d1 S. Typhimurium inoculation: 1) unchallenged (negative control), 2) E. maxima challenge, consisting of 15,000 sporulated oocysts field strain (M6; Martin et al., 1997) orally gavaged on d 14, or 3) E. maxima + C. perfringens challenge, where birds received the E. maxima challenge on d 14 plus 1 × 108 CFU C. perfringens TAMU strain on d 18 and 19 (Ausland et al., 2020; Table 1).

Luminal IL-10 quantification

Luminal contents were collected from the duodenum, jejunum, ileum, and cecal portions of the gastrointestinal tract from 6 chicks/group at baseline (d 14), peak infection (d 21), and post-peak infection (d 25) for all three replicate experiments. Digesta samples were diluted 1:5 in sterile PBS, vortexed, and centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 15 min. Supernatants were collected, protein concentration determined by bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (Thermo Scientific, Rockford, IL), adjusted to 2.5 mg/mL in PBS, and stored at −80 °C. Luminal IL-10 was measured by capture ELISA (Arendt et al., 2016). Briefly, 96-well plates (Corning Inc. Corning, NY) were coated overnight at 4°C with rabbit anti-IL-10 polyclonal antibody (bs-0698R, Bioss Inc., Woburn, MA; 1:2,000 in carbonate buffer, pH 9.6). Plates were washed with PBS + 0.05% Tween-20, blocked for 1 h with 1% nonfat milk, and incubated with recombinant chicken IL-10 standards (Kingfisher Biotech, St. Paul, MN; 4,000-16 ng/mL, twofold serial dilutions) or diluted samples in duplicate. After overnight incubation at room temperature with gentle shaking, plates were washed and probed with affinity-purified anti-IL-10 egg antibody produced in-house and purified commercially (1:1,000) for 4 h, followed by rabbit anti-chicken IgY-horseradish peroxidase (Polyclonal, Cat# A150-102, Fortis Life Sciences, Waltham, MA; 1:10,000) for 45 min. After six washes, plates were developed with 1-Step Ultra TMB-ELISA substrate (Thermo Scientific, Rockford, IL), stopped with 0.5 M sulfuric acid, and read at 450 nm (Biotek Software v3.03.14). Blank-corrected values for IL-10 concentrations were calculated as ng IL-10/mg protein based on the standard curve, with the intercept constrained to zero.

Immunohistochemistry Slide Preparation and Jejunal IFNγ Staining

Tissue samples from the distal jejunum were collected at baseline, peak, and post-peak infection timepoints (6 samples/treatment group) in all three replicate experiments. Tissue was fixed in 10% formaldehyde for 24 h before being transferred to 70% ethanol until slide preparation. Fixed tissue samples were sent to the Iowa State University Veterinary Diagnostic Lab (Ames, IA) for slicing and slide preparation.

Tissue slides for immunofluorescence staining were first deparaffinized and rehydrated by sequential immersion in xylene (3 changes, 10 minutes each), followed by a graded ethanol series (100%, 95%, and 75% ethanol for 1 minute each), and rinsed in distilled water (dH2O). Heat-induced epitope retrieval (HIER) was conducted using a pressure cooker (Instant Pot, Instant Brands Inc., Ottawa, ON, Canada) by placing slides in a round glass staining dish with Tris-Urea buffer (pH 9.5) and cooking under low pressure for 1 minute. After pressure release and a 5-minute room temperature cooldown, slides were washed three times with Tris-buffered saline (TBS), and excess liquid was removed. A hydrophobic barrier was drawn around the tissue using a PAP Pen (ImmEdge Pen, Vector Laboratories, Burlingame, CA) to prevent reagent overflow. Slides were blocked in Pierce Protein-Free TBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) for 4 hours at 4°C in a humidified chamber. Primary antibodies (1:100 Rabbit anti-IFNγ, polyclonal, MSB1488897, MyBioSource, San Diego, CA) were diluted in blocking buffer and applied overnight at 4°C in a humidified chamber. After rinsing with TBS, fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies (1:400 Donkey Anti-Rabbit IgG Dylight 594, polyclonal, A30-107P, Bethyl Laboratories Inc., Montgomery, TX) were applied and incubated for 2 hours at 4°C in the dark. Slides were washed three times with TBS and 1:250 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, Electron Microscopy Science, Hatfield, PA) in Fluoro-Gel with tris buffer was applied. Coverslips were carefully applied to avoid air bubbles, and slides were stored at −20°C in the dark for long-term preservation.

Co-expression of IL-10 and CD4, CD8, CD25, and KUL01 in Jejunal Tissue by RNAscope

Distal jejunal tissue samples were collected at peak infection timepoint (6 samples/treatment) and fixed in 10% formaldehyde before being transferred to 70% ethanol. Samples were sent to Iowa State University Veterinary Diagnostic Laboratory (Ames, IA) for slide preparation and staining. Briefly, tissue was embedded in paraffin, cut to 5 µm thickness, and mounted on Superfrost® Plus Slides (Fisher Scientific, Hampton, NH). Slides were baked for 1 h at 60°C then deparaffinized. Visualization of RNA content was done with the ACDbio RNAscope 2.5 HD Assay-Red kit (ACDbio, Newark, CA, catalog #322430). The tissue sections were pretreated with hydrogen peroxide for 15 min, then boiled in target retrieval buffer for 30 min. The protease plus pretreatment was applied for 30 min. The tissue sections were hybridized with custom species-specific RNAscope probe combinations for 2 h. The probes included Gg-CD14-C1 (alias for KUL01; Entrez Gene ID 100194427; ACDbio, Newark, CA), Gg-IL2RA-C1 (alias for CD25; Entrez Gene ID 395294; ACDbio, Newark, CA), Gg-CD4-C1 (ACDbio, Newark, CA), Gg-CD8A-C1 (ACDbio, Newark, CA), and Gg-IL10-C2 (ACDbio, Newark, CA). Bound probes were amplified six times before detection of red signal (C2 signal for IL-10). Followed by four more rounds of amplification and then green signal detection (C1 signal for CD4, CD8, KUL01, and CD25 immune cell markers). Slides were counterstained with hematoxylin, and coverslips were applied with VectraMount mounting medium (Vector Laboratories, Newark, CA).

Immunohistochemistry and RNAscope slide imaging

Images for both IHC and RNAscope were acquired using Olympus CellSense software (v.1.18, Evident, Waltham, MA) paired with a microscope equipped with a 20x objective lens. Illumination was manually adjusted until the image was clearly visible. Exposure time was set to 2.222 ms, and the resolution was conFig.d to 1360 × 1024 for both “Live/Movie” and “Snapshot/Process” modes. Fluorescent images were acquired under dark field conditions, eliminating the need for external light during imaging. Signal quantification was performed using the HALO Area Quantification FL module (Indica Labs, Albuquerque, NM), which allows for precise selection of jejunum tissue regions.

For IHC, ten images were captured per slide from distinct regions of the jejunum, and this procedure was repeated for all samples. IHC results are reported as fluorescence quantification by pixel in the jejunal tissue slide images. Area analyzed represents the total pixel area with any positive fluorescence; conversely, % negative area represents the amount of the image not displaying any fluorescence. With appropriate configuration, the module enabled effective visualization and quantification of TXred (IFNγ) signals, which were classified into three intensity categories based on signal strength. The thresholds for TXred weak were below 1485 RFU, TXred moderate was between 1485 and 3921 RFU, and TXred strong was above 3921 RFU. Total, weak, moderate, and strong area for IFNγ is representative of the number of pixels for those markers depending on fluorescence intensity. The % for all measures were calculated from the total area analyzed.

For RNAscope, an image of the whole slide was captured with the module enabled effective visualization and quantification of IL-10, CD4, CD8, CD25, and KUL01 signals. Slides were first analyzed for CD4, CD8, CD25, and KUL01 expression and IL-10 individually. Then the co-expression of IL-10 with each immune marker was analyzed. Total area is defined as the area of the image with tissue present, quantified by pixels. The % Negative area is defined as the % of total area not displaying either fluorescent signal. The % IL-10/Immune marker area is defined as the % of total area displaying IL-10/Immune marker signal. The IL-10/Immune marker positive intensity is the average relative fluorescence units for area positive for IL-10/Immune marker. The Dual Positive area is defined as the number of pixels displaying a fluorescent signal for both IL-10 and an immune marker. The % Dual Positive area is the Dual Positive area as a % of Total Area. The Dual Positive IL-10/Immune marker Intensity is the average relative fluorescent signal for either IL-10 or the immune marker in areas of dual positive fluorescence.

Statistical analysis for ELISA, IHC and RNAscope

Data from luminal IL-10 content, immunohistochemistry, and RNAscope were analyzed using SAS v9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). For baseline, peak, or post-peak in each replicate, a separate linear model in Proc Mixed analyzed data with diet and challenge as fixed effects.yijk=μ+Di+Cj+(DxC)ij+eijkWhere yijk denotes the response variable, Di is the main effect of diet, Cj is the main effect of challenge, (DxC)ij is the interactive effect of diet and challenge, and eijk denotes individual error. The challenge term in R1 and R2 baseline data encompassed the effects of early S. Typhimurium inoculation (in the groups to be later challenged with E. maxima ± C. perfringens) while baseline analysis for R3 omitted model terms involving the challenge effect. Post hoc analysis of Tukey’s Honest Significant test was performed for further separation of means when appropriate. Significance for luminal IL-10 content, immunohistochemistry, and RNAscope was set at p-value (P) ≤ 0.05.

RNA extraction and transcriptome sequencing

RNA extraction and transcriptome sequencing of snap-frozen jejunal tissue samples from 6 birds per treatment group at baseline and peak timepoints from R3 only is described by Fries-Craft, et al. (2024b). Briefly, samples were thawed on ice with RNAlater (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA) before RNA extraction from ≤ 0.2 g of tissue using the Invitrogen PureLink RNA Mini Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) following manufacturer’s instructions. A Nanodrop 2000 Spectrophotometer (BioTek, Winooski, VT) was used to quantify the extracted RNA. SUPERase∙In RNase Inhibitor (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) was added prior to removal of contaminating DNA with Invitrogen TURBO™ DNase kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) according to manufacturer recommendations. All samples had an RNA integrity number > 8 using the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer with Eukaryote Total RNA Nano chip (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA) and were submitted to the Iowa State University DNA facility (Ames, IA). Prior to RNA sequencing, both eukaryotic and prokaryotic ribosomal RNA were removed using NEBNext rRNA depletion kits (human/mouse/rat and bacteria; New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA). Equal volumes of each depletion solution were combined before being added to the RNA samples. Libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Ultra II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA), followed by 100-cycle paired-end total RNA sequencing on the Illumina NovaSeq6000 (using a S4 flow cell on 4 lanes with 2.5 billion reads per lane; Illumina, San Diego, CA). Library preparation and sequencing was performed at the Iowa State University DNA facility. Raw sequences were uploaded to the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) sequencing read archive (SRA) and are publicly available (Bioproject ID: PRJNA1455178).

Transcriptome bioinformatic analysis

FastQC v0.11.9 was used to evaluate each fastq file for quality and the absence of sequencing adapters (Andrews, 2010). Reads were trimmed with Trimmomatic v0.39 using a sliding window of 3 base pairs averaging a quality score of 15, and an average overall read quality of 4 (Bolger et al., 2014). A minimum read length of 36 base pairs removed short reads. RNA reads were aligned to the Red Jungle Fowl genome GRCg6a from Ensembl (GCA_000002315.5) with STAR v2.7.6 (Dobin et al., 2013). featureCounts from Subread v2.0.2 was utilized to count reads and output data table for analysis (Liao et al., 2014).

Read counts for transcriptome analysis were analyzed with the package DESeq2 v1.46.0 in R v.4.4.1 (Love et al., 2014). Sex was assigned to the samples based on expression of W chromosome linked genes. Due to differences in experimental structure before and after challenge, the baseline and peak timepoints were analyzed separately. Baseline model included animal room and diet (control or anti-IL-10). One outlier was removed from the control diet baseline data based on principal component analysis (PCA) plot and uneven variance compared to the rest of the samples. Peak model included sex of the bird, diet (control or anti-IL-10), and challenge (unchallenged, E. maxima, or E. maxima + C. perfringens) as main effects and the interaction of sex and challenge. Based on Wald’s tests comparing groups, significant differentially expressed genes were identified with a false discovery rate adjusted p-value (FDR) ≤ 0.05 and |log2 fold change| >1. Overrepresentation of PANTHER Gene Ontology Slim (GO-Slim) Biological Process terms by the differentially expressed genes was assessed with PANTHER Classification System v19.0 utilizing Fisher’s Exact test with FDR correction (Mi et al., 2021).

Results

Luminal IL-10 protein production in the intestine

Baseline. Baseline levels of luminal IL-10 in the R1 duodenum were 752 ng IL-10/mg protein for unchallenged birds, which was significantly greater compared to Salmonella challenged birds with 294 ng IL-10/mg protein (Fig. 1A; main effect; P = 0.05). In R2, luminal IL-10 in the duodenum at baseline was significantly higher for anti-IL-10 without a Salmonella challenge at 1,600 ng IL-10/mg protein compared to all other groups (Fig. 2A; P = 0.007). Diet significantly changed luminal IL-10 in baseline duodenum during R3; the control diet produced 953 ng IL-10/mg protein, significantly more than the anti-IL-10 diet at 495 ng IL-10/mg protein (Fig. 3A; main effect, P = 0.007). Baseline. Comparison of read counts between diets at baseline resulted in 29 differentially expressed (DE) genes (Supplemental Table 3). The results of diet at baseline are confounded by sex because randomly selecting birds from pens produced all females from the control diet and all males from the anti-IL-10 diet (Supplemental Fig. 2). Notably, a 12 log2 fold change increase in the control group was observed for the gene encoding histidine triad nucleotide-binding protein, HINTW, a female-specific derivative of HINT in avian species. The gene encoding heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein K like, HNRNPKL, also showed a significant log2 fold change of 8.4 for the control diet compared to the anti-IL-10 diet. Additional differentially expressed genes at baseline included a 1.6 and 1.0 log2 fold change for cysteine dioxygenase type 1 (CDO1) and EGF like, fibronectin type III and laminin G domains (EGFLAM) in the anti-IL-10 diet compared to the control diet. The gene encoding mitochondrial ribosomal protein S30 (MRPS30) showed a −1.2 log2 fold change decrease in the anti-IL-10 diet compared to the control diet at baseline. As all the above genes are located on one of the sex chromosomes in chicken, their differential expression is attributable to the unintended separation of sexes at baseline. Investigation of biological process terms enriched for these genes resulted in no significant GO-Slim terms at baseline.

Biological Process terms relating to the cell cycle of chromosome organization, DNA recombination, chromosome segregation, mitotic sister chromatid segregation, and mitotic chromosome condensation were overrepresented by the DE genes in both E. maxima and E. maxima + C. perfringens compared to unchallenged at peak infection (Figs. 11; 12; Supplemental Table 4). Related to these processes, E. maxima compared to unchallenged at peak infection uniquely overrepresented Biological Process terms of DNA replication initiation, DNA replication, and DNA metabolic process (Fig. 11), while E. maxima + C. perfringens compared to unchallenged uniquely overrepresented Biological Process terms of kinetochore assembly and kinetochore organization (Fig. 12).

Only a few GO Biological Process terms related to immune functions were overrepresented for E. maxima and E. maxima + C. perfringens compared to unchallenged at peak infection (Figs. 11; 12; Supplemental Table 4). E. maxima overrepresented terms associated with blood coagulation, driven by DE genes including a 4.0, 1.9, 1.5, and 1.4 log2 fold change in kallikrein B1 (KLKB1), coagulation factor VII (F7), fibrinogen beta chain (FGB), and sonic hedgehog (SHH), respectively. E. maxima + C. perfringens overrepresented terms associated with the humoral immune response and the defense response to other organisms (Fig. 12). E. maxima and E. maxima + C. perfringens both had a −3.2 log2 fold change decrease in interferon gamma (IFNG) compared to the unchallenged at peak infection.

Although not associated with an overrepresentation of a GO Biological Process term, there were other significant DE genes in E. maxima and E. maxima + C. perfringens with known functions relating to the immune response (Supplementary Table 3). Certain pro-inflammatory interleukins and their receptors had increased expression in E. maxima and E. maxima + C. perfringens compared to unchallenged, such as interleukin-2 receptor (IL2RA; both 1.1 log2 fold change), interleukin-17A (IL17A; 5.7 and 4.1 log2 fold change), interleukin-17F (IL17F; 3.1 and 2.3 log2 fold change), and interleukin-17 receptor D (IL17RD; 1.6 and 1.7 log2 fold change). Conversely, interleukin-4 induced 1 (IL4I1; −2.6 and −2.9 log2 fold change), interleukin-12B subunit (IL12B; −2.6 and −1.8 log2 fold change), interleukin-21 (IL21; −2.2 and −2.0 log2 fold change), and interleukin-21 receptor (IL21R; both −1.1 log2 fold change) were pro-inflammatory cytokines decreased by E. maxima and E. maxima + C. perfringens. The anti-inflammatory cytokine interleukin-10 (IL10) was reduced by −2.8 and −3.1 log2 fold changes for E. maxima and E. maxima + C. perfringens compared to unchallenged, respectively.

Genes responsible for the complement components which partake in the membrane attack complex were differentially expressed for E. maxima and E. maxima + C. perfringens compared to unchallenged at peak infection in jejunal tissue. Complement component 6 (C6) and complement components 7 (C7) were downregulated by E. maxima with −2.3 and −1.5 log2 fold changes and by E. maxima + C. perfringens with −1.6 and −1.3 log2 fold changes. Conversely, complement component 8 (C8) was upregulated with 1.5 and 1.2 log2 fold changes by E. maxima and E. maxima + C. perfringens compared to unchallenged, respectively.

Two major histocompatibility complex genes central to antigen processing and presentation had significantly increased expression in E. maxima and E. maxima + C. perfringens compared to unchallenged at peak infection. There was a 1.4 and 1.2 log2 fold change increase in major histocompatibility complex B class II beta chain DMB1 (DMB1) for E. maxima and E. maxima + C. perfringens, respectively. E. maxima and E. maxima + C. perfringens had a 1.1 and 1.0 log2 fold change increase in major histocompatibility complex class II beta chain BLB2 (BLB2). Challenges with E. maxima or E. maxima + C. perfringens also impacted a few genes encoding immune regulating cell surface proteins, such as cluster of differentiation 14 molecule (CD14), which had significantly reduced (both −1.0 log2 fold change) expression in E. maxima and E. maxima + C. perfringens compared to unchallenged birds during peak infection. E. maxima uniquely decreased CD72 molecule like 1 (CD72L1) and CD72 molecule like 2 (CD72L2) by −1.2 and −3.4 log2 fold changes compared to unchallenged. Alternatively, E. maxima + C. perfringens increased the CD226 molecule by 1.2 log2 fold change compared to unchallenged.

Other notable immune related DE genes were secreted frizzled-related protein 1 (SFRP1), secreted phosphoprotein 1 (SPP1), chicken mononuclear phagocyte marker (TIMD4) with log2 fold changes of 1.0, −3.2, and 1.5 for E. maxima compared to unchallenged. E. maxima + C. perfringens showed −3.1 and 1.4 log2 fold changes in SPP1 and TIMD4 compared to unchallenged, respectively. Additionally, E. maxima increased avian beta-defensin 1 (AvBD1) with a 2.9 log2 fold change compared to unchallenged at peak infection in jejunal tissue.

Peak – Sex Main Effect. The main comparison of the sexes, cumulatively across the other factors, produced 119 DE genes (Supplemental Table 3). Males compared to females at each level of challenge status produced 103, 113, and 151 DE genes for unchallenged, E. maxima, and E. maxima + C. perfringens, respectively (Fig. 13; Supplemental Table 3). Many of the significant DE genes with large expression changes in males compared to females were located on the W and Z chromosomes and had expression patterns consistent with their expected sex linkage. W chromosome genes of HINTW and ATP synthase subunit alpha (ATP5F1AW), that should not be present in males, had a −14.8 and −9.8 log2 fold change decrease for unchallenged males compared to females. Males compared to females challenged with E. maxima also showed this expected decrease in HINTW (−13.1 log2 fold change), while the sex linkage of ATP5F1AW (−11.0 log2 fold change) was identified again in males compared to females under challenge with E. maxima + C. perfringens. Conversely, on the Z chromosome, SMC2 had a 1.5 log2 fold change increase for unchallenged males compared to females. Males significantly upregulated GRAM domain containing 3 (GRAMD3) and leucine rich repeat containing 19 (LRRC19) with log2 fold changes increases of 1.1 and 1.0, respectively for E. maxima, and log2 fold changes increases of 1.2 and 1.3, respectively for E. maxima + C. perfringens. Within E. maxima + C. perfringens challenged birds, males also upregulated cholinergic receptor nicotinic alpha 6 subunit (CHRNA6) and iduronidase, alpha-L (IDUA) with log2 fold changes increases of 3.5, and 1.1, respectively, when compared to females.

Peak – Sex by Challenge Interaction. Unlike at baseline, random distribution of the male and female chickens at peak infection provided mixed groups for each challenge status, making it possible to investigate the differential impact of challenge on each sex (Fig. 9). The interaction of sex and challenge compared males and females within the E. maxima and E. maxima + C. perfringens challenge groups to unchallenged birds of the same sex. The comparison of E. maxima males, E. maxima females, E. maxima + C. perfringens males, and E. maxima + C. perfringens females yielded 1251, 1815, 1033, and 1494 DE genes compared to unchallenged birds of the same sex (Supplemental Table 3). There were 529 DE genes shared between all four comparisons of the 2607 unique DE genes identified; this is a 20.3% overlap between the four groups (Fig. 14). The exclusion of either E. maxima challenged males or females resulted in a low number of DE genes shared between the remaining three comparisons (58 and 20, respectively; Fig. 14). However, the exclusion of either E. maxima + C. perfringens challenged males or females resulted in a greater number of DE genes shared between the remaining three comparisons (169 and 116, respectively; Fig. 14). These groups downregulated numerous genes encoding centromere proteins (Supplemental Table 3).

Discussion

Examining localized host responses to biological disease pressures can guide decisions to better manage outcomes in rapidly changing commercial broiler house settings. The use of luminal IL-10 quantification, IHC, RNAscope, and transcriptional analysis allow for the examination of two different model systems to study necrotic enteritis in broilers, one using S. Typhimurium inoculation (R1 and R2) prior to an E. maxima and C. perfringens challenge, and one without (R3). The employment of a dietary anti-IL-10 antibody to understand impacts of localized IL-10 during infection was also studied. The changes in host intestinal immunological outcomes can further explain the impact of feeding anti-IL-10 antibody and provide perspective on the use of S. Typhimurium in future research challenge models.

The bird performance, pathogen clearance, immunometabolism, and jejunal microbial community composition from this study have been previously published (Fries-Craft and Bobeck, 2024a, 2024b; Fries-Craft et al., 2024a, 2024b). Briefly, challenge was shown to be the significant driver in performance depression and lesion scores not significantly improving with anti-IL-10 antibody supplementation (Fries-Craft and Bobeck, 2024a). Additionally, differences in immunometabolism of peripheral blood mononuclear cells were driven by challenge, while neither challenge nor antibody supplementation impacted plasma IL-10 or IFN-γ (Fries-Craft and Bobeck, 2024b). It should be noted that the biological pathogen challenges were significant, and a higher anti-IL-10 antibody inclusion rate may have resulted in improvements to suppression of luminal IL-10 and improving responses across time replicates. However, the objective of the work was not an antibody dose response, and therefore this would be an avenue for future work in this disease model.

Feeding anti-IL-10 has been proposed to mitigate performance depression and aid in clearance of Eimeria (Sand et al., 2016). As an immune evasion mechanism, Eimeria promotes host IL-10 production, therefore reducing host pro-inflammatory cytokine production (Sand et al., 2016). The expected effect of feeding anti-IL-10 of decreasing luminal IL-10 content was observed in the duodenum at baseline in R3 when S. Typhimurium inoculation was not included in the model. However, the luminal IL-10 content increased for the anti-IL-10 diet without S. Typhimurium in the duodenum at baseline in R2. IL-10 luminal concentrations in other portions of the gastrointestinal tract (jejunum, ileum, ceca) were unaffected by the supplementation of anti-IL-10 in the diet at baseline for R1, R2, and R3. The duodenal results of R3 are comparable to previous research supporting the use of anti-IL-10 supplementation for an Eimeria challenge (Rasheed et al., 2020a, 2020b; Sand et al., 2016). Although there were a few significant differences in jejunal tissue concentrations of IFNγ due to diet; these appear in R3 with no S. Typhimurium inoculation present in the model.

There is insufficient evidence from IHC sampling to support a change in IFNγ due to diet when S. Typhimurium is included in the challenge model. The co-expression of IL-10 with immune markers follows the same trend of diet having little effect when birds were fed anti-IL-10 at approximately 0.03% inclusion. The biggest differences due to diet were in R3, without a pre-inoculation with S. Typhimurium, on the dual positive area for IL-10 and CD4. The predisposing S. Typhimurium inoculation could be confounding the dietary anti-IL-10 and necrotic enteritis challenge effects on IL-10 concentrations throughout the small intestine, since Salmonella is known to increase host IL-10 production to induce immune tolerance and establish itself within the gut microbiota (Kogut and Arsenault, 2017). The luminal IL-10 contents in the duodenum of R1 at baseline indicated lower IL-10 production with a S. Typhimurium inoculation. However, the duodenal IL-10 content with S. Typhimurium at baseline in R2 was not different from the unchallenged control birds. Significant differences in cytokines in R3, without a predisposing S. Typhimurium inoculation, indicate a difference in host immune response to anti-IL-10 dietary intervention dependent upon preexisting colonization by S. Typhimurium. Additionally, the minimal number of significant differences due to diet in the present study do not provide sufficient evidence of a change in cytokine response during E. maxima and C. perfringens challenge due to anti-IL-10 supplementation at the current antibody inclusion rate. It could be possible that with several elements of the model impacting host IL-10 production and previous research supporting anti-IL-10′s efficacy occurring during subclinical challenge that a different inclusion rate was needed (Arendt et al., 2016; Sand et al., 2016).

The differences in luminal IL-10 content of the duodenum at peak infection in R2 and post-peak infection in R1 coincide more with the E. maxima and C. perfringens challenge groups than those given S. Typhimurium. Furthermore, the response of luminal IL-10 to E. maxima and C. perfringens in R3, without a predisposing S. Typhimurium inoculation, is significant in the jejunum and ileum at peak infection, while R1 and R2 show no significant treatment effect on luminal IL-10 in the jejunum, ileum, and ceca. In R3, without a predisposing S. Typhimurium inoculation, the jejunal and ileum luminal IL-10 content is significantly greater for E. maxima + C. perfringens at peak infection compared to unchallenged. The luminal IL-10 content of the ceca in R3 post-peak infection is lower for E. maxima compared to unchallenged, but similar to E. maxima + C. perfringens. However, the difference in challenge type in R3 is not supported by differences in jejunal tissue expression of IL-10 at peak infection. The luminal IL-10 contents of R1 in the duodenum at post-peak infection showed challenge having a significant interaction with diet, but most of the challenge groups responded similarly. Furthermore, R2 showed significantly greater luminal IL-10 content in the duodenum at peak infection for E. maxima + C. perfringens compared to E. maxima. While R1 did show significant differences in jejunal tissue concentrations of IFNγ from IHC at peak infection based on challenge type; there were negligible differences in jejunal tissue concentrations of IFNγ from IHC at peak infection in R2 and R3. The IL-10 area, % IL-10 area, and IL-10 average positive intensity of jejunal tissue in R2 was significantly greater for E. maxima + C. perfringens compared to unchallenged. While there were no significant differences in IL-10 concentrations in jejunal tissue during R1, the pattern of E. maxima + C. perfringens having a greater IL-10 average positive intensity remained true for R3. Notably, the only immune cell marker to be significantly different in jejunal tissue was KUL01 in R2; where E. maxima had significantly greater KUL01 area and % KUL01 area than E. maxima + C. perfringens. KUL01 is a reliable marker of monocytes and macrophages in avian species (Mast et al., 1998); however, the significant difference in R2 alone is insufficient to conclude differences between treatment groups without further investigation.

These data provide evidence to show key cytokines and immune cell markers associated with host immune response are not drastically altered by the addition of C. perfringens to the challenge model when S. Typhimurium is present. The removal of S. Typhimurium from the challenge model shows a response in IL-10 to E. maxima and C. perfringens challenge at peak infection.

A transcriptomic analysis was performed for jejunal tissue in R3 to further investigate host responses to diet and challenge without S. Typhimurium. Birds were randomly divided across groups and at baseline, sex of the bird was fully confounded with diet and thus the effect of diet was not differentiable from the effect of sex at this timepoint. Very few genes (29) were DE at baseline and the nearly 60% of these DE genes were sex-linked. For example, HINTW is a multicopy gene located on the female-specific avian W chromosome and has been clearly associated with early sex differentiation in chickens (Sun et al., 2023; Backström et al., 2005) and its expression in the current study is more likely attributable to sex than diet effect.

The transcriptome analysis at peak infection in R3 offers more insight into the effect of diet and challenge on the host response. A decrease in SLC25A48 transcription was the only difference in jejunal transcription due to diet in R3 at peak infection. Thus, dietary treatment of anti-IL-10 had minimal impact on the transcriptional changes at peak infection. However, both E. maxima and E. maxima + C. perfringens challenge altered host jejunal mRNA expression of genes associated with lipid metabolism at peak infection. The upregulation of APOB, the main ligand for low density lipoprotein receptor (Chen et al., 2025), ACOT12, involved in acetyl-CoA metabolism (Wang et al., 2025), LPL, involved in triglyceride hydrolase (Song et al., 2025), and LIPI, a phospholipase, for both E. maxima and E. maxima + C. perfringens groups demonstrate an increase in lipid metabolism. Additionally, E. maxima upregulated jejunal transcripts from genes associated with cholesterol metabolism including: SOAT1, an acyltransferase responsible for catalyzation of fatty acid-cholesterol esters (Yang et al., 2019), and APOA1, promotes cholesterol efflux from tissue (Yamamoto et al., 2025). The increase in lipid metabolism may be to compensate for malabsorption of nutrients during Eimeria challenge, due to a decrease in oxidation of glucose and α-ketoglutaric acid (Allen and Danforth, 1984; Ruff and Allen, 1982). Lipids involved in pro- and anti-inflammatory processes in protective immunity have been previously described as a reason for lipid metabolism changes during an Eimeria infection (Kim et al., 2009). E. maxima and E. maxima + C. perfringens induced similar responses in the host relating to lipid metabolism, however, only the E. maxima challenge group increased genes linked to cholesterol metabolism. The role of cholesterol as a cell membrane component, energy storage molecule, and hormone precursor is relevant to the bird’s response to Eimeria challenge (Kim et al., 2009). Further investigation is warranted to determine how and why the addition of C. perfringens to the challenge model reverted cholesterol metabolism back to comparable levels to the unchallenged group.

The E. maxima and E. maxima + C. perfringens challenge groups decreased expression of genes associated with cell replication. Genes responsible for chromosome condensation, SMC2 and SMC4, passenger proteins important for chromosome migration during mitosis, INCENP, CENPW, CENPH, and CENPA, and the stabilization and replication of DNA, CDC6, CDC45, DNA2, and TOP2A, were downregulated in the challenge groups. An infection-driven decrease in healthy cells able to proceed through the cell cycle and decrease in available nutrients likely limit the ability of E. maxima and E. maxima + C. perfringens challenged birds to exhibit similar transcriptional levels for genes relating to the cell cycle as the unchallenged birds.

The DE genes relating to immune functions were similar for E. maxima and E. maxima + C. perfringens compared to unchallenged. The need for antigen-presentation increases with both challenge groups with an increase in DMB1 and BLB2, genes encoding molecules central to the major histocompatibility complex class II pathway of antigen processing and presentation. The membrane attack complex is a useful mechanism for lysing pathogens during an infection, particularly Eimeria (Tretiak et al., 2025). E. maxima and E. maxima + C. perfringens challenges increased expression of genes encoding complement proteins, C6 and C7, responsible for the membrane attack complex; however, the challenged groups also decreased expression of C8.

There were considerable changes in transcripts of genes coding for cytokines and their receptors in both the E. maxima and E. maxima + C. perfringens challenge compared to unchallenged. As expected for a proinflammatory response, multiple IL17 genes and IL2RA were increased in expression for E. maxima and E. maxima + C. perfringens groups. The downregulation of IL4I1, IL12B, IL21, and IL21R by E. maxima and E. maxima + C. perfringens challenge can further explain the immune response. IL-2 promotes proliferation and differentiation of T-cells and B-cells, while IL-21 has a similar function but has been shown to be more responsive in an antiviral or anti-tumor response, and inhibits dendritic cell activation which would be essential in response to protozoic and bacterial challenge (Spolski and Leonard, 2008). Additionally, the suppression of IL4I1 expressed by macrophages and granulocytes can control the inflammatory response and signal to T and B lymphocytes (Elsheimer-Matulova et al., 2020). The increase in IL17 provides benefit in defensive response to protozoan infection (Min et al., 2013). Both E. maxima and E. maxima + C. perfringens challenge groups downregulated the major pro-inflammatory cytokine, IFNG. Decreased IFNG expression could be a response to an increase in IL-10 as predicted from the previously discussed evasion mechanism of Eimeria, but only in combination with C. perfringens in this study as luminal content of IL-10 in jejunum increased for birds challenged with E. maxima + C. perfringens but not E. maxima alone. However, the transcriptional response of IL10 expression was not reflective of luminal IL-10 or IHC tissue concentrations within the jejunum at peak infection; IL10 transcription decreased for both E. maxima and E. maxima + C. perfringens challenged birds compared to the unchallenged. These changes in gene expression and luminal contents of IL-10 did not coincide with significant changes in IHC jejunal tissue concentrations of IL-10. There is a cascade of downregulation for IFNG expression, starting with the downregulation of SPP1, secreted phosphoprotein 1, responsible for upregulating IL-12 and IFNγ (Maeno et al., 2006). Jejunal tissue IHC concentrations decrease in IFNγ coincided with the transcriptional downregulation of IFNG and SPP1 in E. maxima + C. perfringens challenged birds. The host response to E. maxima and E. maxima + C. perfringens challenges appears to signal for T-cell response and phagocytic cell activity.

The E. maxima and E. maxima + C. perfringens challenged groups shared a similar transcriptional response to each other compared to the unchallenged. However, when the challenge groups are separated by sex and compared to their counterparts from the unchallenged group, there are unique differences. These differences were not expected to be found and perhaps highlight one of the most interesting outcomes within this analysis. The males and females challenged with E. maxima + C. perfringens share similar DE genes to E. maxima challenged birds of both sexes, but less to each other. Males challenged with E. maxima shared the downregulation of cell cycle transcripts with female challenged birds. The males challenged with E. maxima + C. perfringens appear to maintain cell proliferation; this may provide benefit for intestinal barrier integrity. No significant differences were found in lesion scores between E. maxima and E. maxima + C. perfringens challenge groups (Fries-Craft et al., 2024a), this analysis would have benefited from including sex in the model to validate transcriptions associated with the cell cycle contribute to improved intestinal barrier integrity.

The similarity in cytokine signaling between males challenged with E. maxima and females challenged with E. maxima ± C. perfringens shows a commonality in response for female E. maxima + C. perfringens to the E. maxima challenged groups. The males challenged with E. maxima + C. perfringens showed a lower IL17A and higher IL21R transcription, responsible for heterophil attraction and B cell maturation for antibody production, respectively. Along with increased cell cycle transcripts, these transcriptional changes suggest males challenged with E. maxima + C. perfringens move through the inflammation to resolution process faster than males challenged with E. maxima and females of either challenge group. The male E. maxima + C. perfringens challenge shares different transcriptional regulation relating to hemoglobin and secreted phosphoproteins to the E. maxima challenged groups. Ghasemi et al. (2025) found hemoglobin was reduced during an Eimeria acervulina, Eimeria maxima, and Eimeria tenella infection suggesting an increase in oxygen transport would reduce systemic stress during challenge. The sex differences in the present study for hemoglobin subunit transcripts highlight the host ability to maintain oxygen transport during challenge differs between hens and roosters.

The hens of both challenge groups differentially expressed a significant number of genes from their unchallenged counterparts. Interestingly, the females challenged with E. maxima + C. perfringens had an altered cytokine signaling response compared to males challenged with E. maxima + C. perfringens, demonstrating the addition of C. perfringens to the challenge model amplified sex differences in host immune response. Female E. maxima + C. perfringens at peak infection showed a heightened inflammatory response compared to male E. maxima + C. perfringens birds. Females increased transcription of IL2RA and decreased transcription of SOCS1 and SOCS3. The reduction in suppression of cytokine signaling 1 and 3 in combination with increased IL2RA suggest a greater proinflammatory cytokine response from females.

The addition of C. perfringens to the challenge model changes the transcriptional response of males and females differently, but still comparable to E. maxima challenged birds regardless of sex. A sex-based approach to necrotic enteritis treatment may be more beneficial with males and females responding differently to C. perfringens challenge on top of E. maxima. Necrotic enteritis is a costly health challenge in the broiler industry (Collier et al., 2008); a more precise treatment approach for each sex is a factor worth consideration for split sex grow out for broilers.

Overall, anti-IL-10 antibody supplementation did not have a significant effect on intestinal immune responses to E. maxima or E. maxima + C. perfringens challenge in broilers, likely due to a need for a higher inclusion rate to obtain more consistent host responses. The host response to E. maxima and E. maxima + C. perfringens was comparable; thus, either challenge model could be utilized to elicit an immune response for future research models. Future research utilizing a E. maxima + C. perfringens challenge model should consider sex as a factor for determining host response. The effect of sex on the response to E. maxima + C. perfringens challenge should be further investigated to determine how current interventions for necrotic enteritis affect each sex. Furthermore, the existence of a preexisting S. Typhimurium colonization will alter immune responses to E. maxima and C. perfringens.
