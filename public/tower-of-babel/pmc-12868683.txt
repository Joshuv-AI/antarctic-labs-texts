Photoenzymatic catalysis enables enantioselective radical dearomative spirocyclization
Authors: Changtong Zhu, Yan Zhang, Zhiwei Deng, Luyun Ji, Xiaorui Chen, Dejing Yin, Changmei Liu, Zhengshan Luo, Yijian Rao, Zhenbo Yuan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDearomatization offers a powerful route towards three-dimensional spirocyclic architectures, which are pivotal in bioactive molecules and pharmaceuticals, particularly for enantioselective construction of congested quaternary spirocenters. However, biocatalytic dearomatization towards these attractive spirocyclic frameworks under mild and green conditions remain underexplored. Here, we report a photoenzymatic platform synergizing visible-light activation with engineered nicotinamide-dependent ketoreductase (KRED) catalysis to achieve highly enantioselective dearomative spirocyclization of N-hydroxyphthalimide esters. Semi-rational engineering of KRED P2-D12 enables the synthesis of diverse chiral spirocyclohexadienones, also including the challenging ortho-cyclohexadienones, with exceptional functional group tolerance and stereocontrol (up to >99% ee). The utility of this methodology is underscored significantly by the enantioselective total synthesis of the cytotoxic natural product (+)-denobilone A (93% ee). Overall, this work establishes a chemobiocatalytic platform for the synthesis of complex and chiral spiroarchitectures with high enantioselectivity, expanding the biocatalytic toolbox in sustainable synthetic chemistry.Subject terms: Biocatalysis, Synthetic biology

Introduction

Spirocyclic scaffolds are ubiquitous in natural products, pharmaceuticals, bioactive molecules, catalysts, ligands and advanced materials, particularly occupying a privileged position in pharmaceuticals with profound significance due to their unique three-dimensional architectures and structural novelty (Fig. 1a)1–4. Consequently, conventional methodologies, including gem-alkylation of cyclic substrates5,6, ring closure of gem-disubstituted precursors7,8, intramolecular cyclizations9,10, rearrangement11, and cycloadditions12–16, have historically enabled access to these intricate frameworks17,18. Moreover, dearomatization has alternatively emerged as a particularly attractive and well-developed strategy in chemical synthesis, transforming simple and readily available planar arenes into three-dimensional spirocycles with remarkable efficiency19,20, especially those chiral spirocycles with architectural complexity and versatile bioactivities21,22. In contrast, biocatalytic dearomatization for spirocycle formation remains comparatively underexplored, presenting a conspicuous gap in sustainable green synthetic methodology (Fig. 1b)23. Despite the abundance of chiral spiro motifs in natural products, only a handful of native dearomative spirocyclases have been identified and characterized, hindered by the inherent complexity of biosynthetic pathways, which could only be employed in the biosynthesis of parts of specific natural products24–28. To address this void, innovative efforts have been exploited by biologists and chemists based on the enzyme promiscuity to repurpose non-natural biotransformations for the synthesis of a variety of spiroindoles, spiropyrroles, spirolactones and their derivatives, propelling significant advances in synthetic chemistry29. However, this biocatalytic toolbox remains predominantly confined to only heme-dependent enzymes, notably cytochrome P450 monooxygenases29 and chloroperoxidase (CPO)30. Such a narrow enzyme repertoire imposes inherent limitations on the structural diversity and functional complexity of biologically accessible products, particularly for stereochemically intricate chiral spirocyclic scaffolds. Therefore, to transcend these constraints, the deliberate design and development of biocatalytic paradigms with other non-heme biocatalysts are imperative, which could unlock unprecedented routes to enantioselective spirocyclization, thereby expanding the synthetic horizon for high-value chiral building blocks in drug discovery and fine chemical synthesis.

Photobiocatalysis, strategically integrating the unrivaled stereoselectivity of enzymes with the activation power of light, has emerged as a powerful and sustainable platform for asymmetric synthesis, enabling precise stereocontrol under mild conditions31–41. Within the burgeoning field of photobiocatalysis, the strategy of visible-light-excited enzymes has aroused considerable interest from chemists and biologists, as it could successfully realize the unnatural transformations with excellent enantioselectivity through repurposing natural enzymes with direct excitation, even under radical-mediated conditions that are traditionally challenging for enantioinduction42,43. As this field has continually matured, the scope of such methods has progressively expanded from less hindered chiral acyclic44–58 and monocyclic architectures59–63 to the more sterically demanding fused-ring systems with high selectivity64,65. Despite these advances, the stereoselective synthesis of more challenging spirocyclic compounds, particularly those featuring highly congested chiral spiro centers, remains conspicuously unexplored using this powerful strategy of visible-light-excited enzymes, which might be addressed through the powerful dearomative spirocyclization strategy.

Beyond the prevalent use of flavin-dependent enzymes in photobiocatalysis42,44,45,48,50–53,56–65, nicotinamide-dependent enzymes have emerged as a complementary toolkit for asymmetric synthesis, inaugurated in 2016 by Hyster’s group to report a nicotinamide-dependent ketoreductase-catalyzed debromination reaction66. Following this pioneering work, Zhao’s group also developed a photoenzymatic radical addition using a nicotinamide-dependent ketoreductase P2-D1249. Recently, the scope was further expanded to nicotinamide-dependent imine reductases by Huang’s group, enabling the photoenzymatic hydroalkylation for chiral amines54.

In this work, inspired by the powerful strategy of dearomative spirocyclization and photobiocatalysis, we develop a photoenzymatic platform with nicotinamide-dependent ketoreductase (KRED) as a non-heme photobiocatalyst to realize the stereoselective dearomatization for the synthesis of chiral spirocycles (Fig. 1c). The KRED variant P2-D12 from Lactobacillus kefir67 affords targeted versatile spirocyclic products with great functional group tolerance in excellent yields and enantioselectivities after protein engineering through rational design. The established method exhibits highly practical potential for the asymmetric preparation of natural product (+)-denobilone A, providing a biocatalytic approach for the enantioselective synthesis of spirocycles that may be applied in the development of bioactive natural products and medicinal agents.

Results

Establishment of the enantioselective photoenzymatic dearomative spirocyclization reaction

In our initial studies, model substrate 1a with N-hydroxyphthalimide (NHPI) ester as a radical precursor46,49 was prepared, and tested in the dearomatization reaction catalyzed by nicotinamide-dependent KREDs or flavin-dependent “ene”-reductases (EREDs)51,66, which have been proven as visible-light activable enzymes with highly promiscuous. Under the irradiation by blue LEDs (440 nm), several enzymes were pleasingly found to catalyze the dearomative spirocyclization but in unsatisfactory yields (Table 1, entries 1–3 and Supplementary Table 1), among which KREDs variant P2-D12 provided the highest yield (33%) with excellent enantioselectivity (>99% ee value) for product 2a (Table 1, entry 2). After validating that the substrate 1a with NHPI ester was capable of forming the desired product, the reaction parameters were optimized to improve the catalytic efficiency. With the optimization of the light source, blue light (410 nm) provided product 2a with improved yield (37%) with comparable enantioselectivity (>99% ee value), and higher λmax of light source than 440 nm would diminish the yield or enantioselectivity (Table 1, entries 4–5 and Supplementary Table 2). Further decreasing the enzyme loading (Supplementary Table 3) and replacement of the cosolvent DMSO by other aprotic or protic solvents (Table 1, entries 6–7 and Supplementary Table 4) exhibited worse efficiency for this reaction. Buffer optimization revealed that buffer and its pH had a great influence on the reaction performance, and the employment of citrate phosphate (pH 5) could enhance the dearomative spirocyclization reaction yield to 56% (Table 1, entries 8–9 and Supplementary Table 5). Temperature is also an important influencing factor for the photoenzymatic reaction, which finally improved the yield to 79% without the loss of enantioselectivity (Table 1, entry 10 and Supplementary Table 6). Moreover, no reactivity was observed in the control experiments lacking P2-D12 (Table 1, entry 11), blue LED (Table 1, entry 12), glucose dehydrogenase (GDH) (Table 1, entry 13) or oxidized form of nicotinamide-adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADP+) (Table 1, entry 14). These results suggest the importance of NHPI ester, KRED, visible-light, and NADPH (reduced form of nicotinamide-adenine dinucleotide phosphate) regeneration system in the developed photoenzymatic dearomative spirocyclization reaction.

Semi-rational engineering of P2-D12 for efficient synthesis of 2a

To further improve the yield of desired product 2a, a structure-guided semi-rational mutagenesis strategy was utilized. Firstly, the substrate 1a was docked into crystal structure of P2-D12 (PDB: 7EJJ)49 to reveal the possible interactions between the substrate and protein (Fig. 2a). Docking results showed that interactions included strong hydrogen bonding interactions between the substrate and Q153, carbon hydrogen bonding and π-anion interactions between the substrate and D150, π-alkyl interactions between the benzene rings of 1a and A94, P151, L195 and M205, as well as alkyl-alkyl interactions at methyl on the substrate with M205 and M206 (Fig. 2b). In addition, relatively small active pocket and the significant steric hindrance between substrate and cofactor NADPH were observed (Fig. 2a), which might result in the poor result of this reaction. After alanine-scanning of the surrounding amino acids, the mutation of residue L195 between substrate and cofactor NADPH to alanine manifested the highest yield of 2a (91%) still with excellent ee value (>99%) (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Table 7). Further mutagenesis of the candidate residue L195 unfortunately failed to provide better result (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Table 8). Thus, P2-D12-L195A affording the desired product 2a in 91% yield with >99% ee value was used for the following studies, which was explained by structural analysis and molecular dynamics (MD) simulations for the best performance. The active site volume of P2-D12-L195A increased to 499 Å3 from 440 Å3 in P2-D12 (Fig. 2e, f), which might result in the closer distance between the substrate 1a and NADPH to accelerate electron transfer in the stable EDA complex intermediate49. To further elucidate the reaction process, MD simulations for five example replicas68 revealed a shorter EDA complex distance (4.71 Å) in P2-D12-L195A than that (5.72 Å) in P2-D12 (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Fig. 1), also supporting the hypothesis of increased active site volume to facilitate closer substrate-NADPH proximity.

Substrate scope investigations

To investigate the substrate tolerance of the photoenzymatic dearomative spirocyclization reaction, substrate scope survey was conducted. As shown in Fig. 3, substrates bearing various substituents on the benzene ring A were subjected to the catalytic reaction. Substrates with hydrogen atom (1a), electron-donating methyl group (1b) and electron-withdrawing -F and -Cl atoms (1c–d) on the ring A exhibited 63–95% yields and excellent enantioselectivities (98–> 99% ee). In addition, the great steric hindrance in dimethyl-substituted 1e and naphthyl-substituted 1f did not hamper the enantioselectivities in the photoenzymatic dearomative spirocyclization (96–99% ee). Next, electron-donating group (-OMe) or moderate electron-withdrawing groups (-F, -Cl) on benzene ring B of the substrates were all compatible in the reaction, delivering the desired products 2g–i in 31–93% yields with 93–97% ee values. In addition, the chiral structure of 2i was unambiguously confirmed by X-ray crystallography (CCDC 2432288, Supplementary Data 1). Moreover, the strong electron-withdrawing group (-CF3) would decrease the yield of the corresponding product 2j significantly to 28%, but maintained the enantioselectivity (>99% ee). The sterically congested substrates with dimethyl groups substituted at different positions of benzene ring B or a fused naphthyl group replaced for benzene ring B could also proceed smoothly to give the desired products 2k–m in 90–96% ee values. What is more, the more challenging spirocyclic ortho-cyclohexadienones69 2n and 2o could also be obtained in enantioselectivity (at least 99% ee), albeit the relatively lower yields (40–47%) compared to their para-counterparts. Finally, structural modifications of the linker between biaryl moiety and NHPI ester were also explored. Replacing the oxygen linker with sulfur or carbon and elongating the linker length yielded structurally diverse frameworks including spiroindane 2p, spirobenzothiophene 2q, spirochromane 2r and spirobenzoxepine 2 s in 91–96% ee values, showcasing the broad substrate scope of this transformation.

Synthetic utility study

To investigate the synthetic utility of this photoenzymatic dearomative spirocyclization reaction, preparative scale reaction was conducted on a 0.5 mmol scale, producing spirocyclic para-cyclohexadienone 2a (70 mg) in 69% isolated yield with 92% ee value (Fig. 4a). Encouraged by the result, we applied the method to the enantioselective total synthesis of (+)-denobilone A, a naturally occurring spirocyclic ortho-cyclohexadienone isolated from the stems of Dendrobium nobile with cytotoxic activity on cancer cell70, although previous synthetic efforts have already yielded the racemic form69. We synthesized the key intermediate 3a according to the literature, and subsequently installed the NHPI ester to produce 3b, which served as the substrate of the established photoenzymatic dearomative spirocyclization. After screening several mutants (Supplementary Table 9 and Supplementary Fig. 2), (+)-denobilone A (3c) could be obtained with 93% ee value delightfully (Fig. 4b), the stereochemistry of which was confirmed by its specific rotation based on the previous characterization data70. Despite the suboptimal yield compared with that of the racemic photocatalytic reaction (33% yield), this biocatalytic route represents a significant advancement towards the sustainable and enantioselective synthesis of denobilone A, providing a valuable pattern for the construction of pharmaceutically relevant molecules.

Mechanistic experiments

To gain better insight into the reaction mechanism, ultraviolet-visible spectroscopy experiments were conducted to probe the visible-light-excited species. As shown in Fig. 5a, only after the addition of substrate 1a to the mixture of NADPH and P2-D12-L195A, a broad absorption band ranging from 400 to 550 nm was observed, suggesting the formation of an electron donor-acceptor (EDA) complex66,71. Then, we used 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidinyloxy (TEMPO) to conduct the radical capture experiment. The photoenzymatic reaction was inhibited in the presence of TEMPO, and the TEMPO adduct derived from substrate 1a could be detected by high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. 3), indicating the involvement of radical mechanism. Then, the labeling experiment with H218O was carried out to investigate the source of the oxygen atom of the carbonyl in spirocyclohexadienone. The corresponding 18O-labeled product (2a-18O) was detected, indicating the carbonyl oxygen originated from H2O in buffer (Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. 4). The amount of NADPH (instead of GDH turnover system) and glucose were tested to prove their catalytic performance in the reaction. The results showed that a catalytic amount of NADPH or glucose was enough to furnish the reaction with high yields, and with the increasement of the amount of NADPH or glucose, the reaction yields slightly increased (Supplementary Fig. 5). These results indicate that the presence of NADPH or its regeneration system is sufficient for the reaction to proceed, and the amount has minimal impact on the reaction outcome. What is more, the origin of enantioselectivity was also interpreted by the docking energies (Supplementary Fig. 6), which was consistent with the stereo outcome determined by the X-ray single crystal structure. Finally, based on the above results and previous literature46,69,72,73, a plausible mechanism for this photoenzymatic reaction was proposed in Fig. 5c. First, the substrate binds into enzyme pocket to form the EDA complex with the cofactor NADPH, which is generated by the glucose dehydrogenase (GDH) system. Upon visible-light excitation, the complex undergoes photoinduced single electron transfer, generating the active radical I bound in P2-D12 with radical cation NADPH•+, along with liberation of phthalimidyl anion (PhthN-) and carbon dioxide (CO2). Induced by the chiral enzyme pocket, enantioselective dearomative spirocyclization of radical I produces the chiral pentadienyl radical II, followed by the electron transfer from radical II to NADPH•+ to form the pentadienyl cation III and NADPH. Then, the hydration of cation III with H2O from buffer, and the subsequent elimination of methanol, result in the final chiral spirocyclic product 2a. Finally, the product is replaced by another substrate for the next catalytic cycle.

Discussion

This study successfully established a photobiocatalytic dearomative platform for the enantioselective synthesis of spirocyclic compounds, addressing a significant void in sustainable synthetic methodologies. By repurposing and rationally engineering a nicotinamide-dependent ketoreductase (P2-D12-L195A) as a visible-light-excited photobiocatalyst, we have developed a highly efficient platform for dearomative spirocyclization. This system overcomes the formidable challenges associated with stereocontrol at sterically congested all-carbon quaternary spirocenters, delivering a broad spectrum of chiral spirocyclohexadienones, including the asymmetric synthesis of (+)-denobilone A. Moreover, the excellent enantioselectivities and moderate to excellent yields under mild and aqueous conditions, demonstrate powerful synthetic practicality and remarkable tolerance towards diverse electronic perturbations and significant steric bulk across multiple positions. Crucially, mechanistic investigations elucidate critical roles of the enzyme-substrate complex in both radical generation and stereocontrol, as well as the effectiveness of using only a catalytic amount of NADPH. This work not only opens horizons for a practical and green solution to construct pharmacologically privileged spirocyclic motifs through photobiocatalysis, but also suggests that visible-light-excited enzymes may be broadly repurposed to unlock hitherto inaccessible radical transformations with exquisite stereochemical fidelity to fundamentally advance the frontiers of synthetic chemistry.

Methods

General information

Commercial reagents were used as received unless otherwise stated. Dry methanol, dry ethanol, dry tetrahydrofuran, and dry N, N-dimethylformamide were obtained from Energy Chemical (Shanghai, China). The analytical grade reagent of acetonitrile, methylene chloride, sodium sulfate, sodium hydroxide and hydrochloric acid were obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent (Beijing, China). NMR spectra were recorded on Bruker AV400 (400/600 MHz) spectrometer. Ultra-performance liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (UPLC-MS) was recorded by Waters Maldi Synapt. EtOAc refers to ethyl acetate, DMSO refers to dimethyl sulfoxide, DMF refers to N, N-dimethylformamide.

Expression, purification and concentration of enzymes51,66

For the expression of enzymes, an E. coli BL21(DE3) colony transformed with plasmids were inoculated in 20 mL Luria-Bertani (LB) media with antibiotics (100 mg/L ampicillin) and grown overnight at 37 °C. The seed culture was then inoculated into 500 mL of 2×YT medium containing antibiotics, which were grown at 37 °C (120 rpm) until the OD600 reached 0.6–0.8. Then, 0.1 mM isopropyl β-D-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) was added to induce the expression of proteins at 18 °C and 120 rpm for 10 h. For protein purification, the cells were harvested and collected by centrifugation (8000 × g for 10 min), and subsequently resuspended with Tris-HCl buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, 300 mM NaCl, 1 mM MgCl2, 20 mM imidazole, pH 8). Then, the mixtures were lysed by high pressure homogenizer (800 bar, 5 °C), and centrifuged (40,000 × g) at 4 °C for 30 min. The proteins were purified by a nickel-NTA column and concentrated to 10 mg/mL with Tris-HCl buffer (50 mM, 1 mM MgCl2, pH 8) using an Amicon Ultra concentration tube. After that, the proteins were stored in Tris-HCl buffer (50 mM, 1 mM MgCl2, pH 8) containing 10% glycerol at −80 °C for further use.

General procedure for biocatalytic reaction

A reaction mixture containing substrates 1 (1 mM), enzyme (1 mol%), GDH (2 mol%), NADP⁺ (5 mol%), and glucose (5 mM) in citrate phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 5, 10% glycerol) with DMSO (10% v/v) was prepared in a final volume of 1 mL, which was stirred at 20 °C for 16 h under inert atmosphere and the irradiation of LEDs (45 W, λmax = 410 nm). Upon completion of the reaction, acetonitrile (2 mL) was added to quench the reaction. The yields of reaction mixture were determined by HPLC, and enantioselectivities were analyzed via HPLC on a chiral stationary phase.

General procedure for large scale reaction

A reaction mixture containing substrate 1a (0.48 mmol), P2-D12-L195A (1 mol%), GDH (2 mol%), NADP⁺ (5 mol%), and glucose (5 equiv.) in citrate-phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 5, 10% glycerol) with DMSO (10% v/v) was prepared in a final volume of 200 mL, which was stirred at 20 °C for 16 h under inert atmosphere and the irradiation of LEDs (45 W, λmax = 410 nm). Upon completion of the reaction, the mixture was extracted with EtOAc for 3 times, washed with brine, dried with Na2SO4, and concentrated in vacuo. The crude product was purified by column chromatography to obtain the pure product 2a. Isolated yield of product 2a was reported, and enantioselectivity was analyzed via HPLC on a chiral stationary phase.
