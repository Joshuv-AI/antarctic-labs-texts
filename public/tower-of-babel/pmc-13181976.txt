Translation, cultural adaptation, and psychometric validation of the Chinese versions of the PROMIS + HF-27 and PROMIS + HF-10 profiles in patients with heart failure
Authors: Wanhui Li, Zhiyi Pei, Wenpei Liu, Chen Zhang, Jiayi Lin, Meihua Piao, Xiaofeng Kang, Yin Zhou, Yingying Guo, Xiaoxuan Zhou, Dingyang Gao, Xiaoyin Yang, Yingying Hu, Hui Ma, Minhong Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHeart failure (HF) is a prevalent clinical syndrome characterized by dyspnea, fatigue, exercise intolerance, and oedema, significantly impairing patients’ health-related quality of life (HRQoL). The PROMIS + HF profile is a targeted instrument designed to evaluate novel interventions, enhance clinical care and shared decision-making, and assess quality of care. However, a linguistically validated Chinese version is currently lacking. This study aimed to translate and culturally adapt the PROMIS + HF into Chinese and evaluate its psychometric properties among Chinese patients with HF.MethodsFollowing a standardized cross-cultural adaptation process, the psychometric properties of the Chinese PROMIS + HF profiles (PROMIS + HF-27 and PROMIS + HF-10) were evaluated. A cross-sectional study with a longitudinal component was conducted using convenience sampling to recruit eligible patients from tertiary hospitals in China between September 2022 and February 2023. Participants completed a sociodemographic questionnaire, the PROMIS + HF profiles, the Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire (KCCQ), and the Minnesota Living with Heart Failure Questionnaire (MLHFQ). Psychometric evaluation included floor/ceiling effects, construct validity, criterion-related validity, convergent validity, known-groups validity, reliability and responsiveness.ResultsA total of 420 patients (mean age 57.68 years) were included. Both profiles exhibited an absence of floor and ceiling effects. Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) supported the construct validity of both profiles, demonstrating acceptable fit indices after modification (PROMIS + HF-27: χ2/df = 3.874, CFI = 0.869, TLI = 0.855, SRMR = 0.055, RMSEA = 0.08; PROMIS + HF-10: χ2/df = 3.408, CFI = 0.958, TLI = 0.940, SRMR = 0.038, RMSEA = 0.073). Criterion-related validity was supported by significant correlations between overall, physical health scores and the 6-Minute Walk Test (6MWT). Convergent validity was demonstrated through strong correlations with KCCQ and MLHFQ scores, and known-groups validity was confirmed by the instruments’ ability to distinguish between New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional classes. Internal consistency was good to excellent for the PROMIS + HF-27 (Cronbach’s α: 0.87–0.95) and acceptable to good for PROMIS + HF-10 (Cronbach’ α/Spearman-Brown: 0.71–0.88). Test-retest reliability was excellent for both instruments (ICC: 0.89–0.97). Responsiveness was demonstrated in the longitudinal subsample, with medium to large effect sizes (Cohen’s d = 0.41–0.66) observed for most domains, except for the psychological and social scores in the PROMIS + HF-10, which showed small change (Cohen’s d = 0.26–0.29).ConclusionsThe Chinese versions of the PROMIS + HF-27 and PROMIS + HF-10 profiles demonstrate satisfactory psychometric properties, supporting their use for the comprehensive evaluation of HRQoL in Chinese HF patients. The PROMIS + HF-27 is recommended for academic research requiring high precision, whereas the PROMIS + HF-10 offers a time-efficient alternative for routine screening in clinical practice. Future research should further evaluate their predictive validity for long-term prognosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12955-026-02527-7.

Background

Heart failure (HF) is a complex clinical syndrome characterized by structural or functional impairments of ventricular filling or ejection [1, 2]. According to the 2022 Work Report of the China Heart Failure Center, the prevalence of HF among adults aged 25 and older is estimated at 1.1%, with nearly 3 million new cases reported annually [3–5]. This condition presents a significant public health challenge, profoundly impairing patients’ physical, psychological, and social well-being. Because HF severely compromises patients’ health across multiple domains, the comprehensive collection of data to identify patient-specific distress is critical for optimizing treatment and care [6, 7]. However, standard objective measures often fail to fully capture the patient’s health status, particularly regarding subjective experiences like pain, dyspnea, fatigue, and anxiety [8]. To address this limitation, Patient-Reported Outcomes (PROs) are increasingly utilized in clinical assessment [9].

PROs are defined by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) as information regarding a patient’s health status reported directly by the patient without interpretation by a clinician [10, 11]. By quantifying multidimensional domains such as functional status and health-related quality of life (HRQoL) [12], PRO measures provide unique insights that inform patient-centered decision-making [13]. Recognizing their value, organizations including the FDA and the International Society for Pharmacoeconomics and Outcomes Research (ISPOR) strongly endorse incorporating PROs into routine practice and healthcare evaluation [14, 15].

The selection of appropriate measurement tools is critical for the effective implementation of PROs [8]. Currently, assessing HRQoL in Chinese HF patients relies heavily on instruments including the Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire (KCCQ), the Minnesota Living with Heart Failure Questionnaire (MLHFQ), and the Short Form Health Survey (SF-36) [16]. While these tools are widely validated, their application faces significant challenges. First, there is a lack of a standardized metric across different conditions. Disease-specific tools (e.g., KCCQ) preclude comparisons with other chronic disease populations, while generic tools (e.g., SF-36) may lack sensitivity to HF-specific changes. Second, the response burden is often high. Researchers frequently administer multiple overlapping scales to ensure comprehensive coverage, resulting in redundant measurement and survey fatigue for patients [16].

To address the fragmentation of existing measures, the National Institutes of Health (NIH) established the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) [11]. Grounded in the World Health Organization (WHO) framework of health, the PROMIS system utilizes Item Response Theory (IRT) to create a standardized, precise, and flexible assessment system covering physical, mental, and social health domains [17]. A key advantage of PROMIS is its common metric, which facilitates efficient comparisons across diverse chronic conditions, including cancer and cardiovascular diseases.

Despite its strengths, the generic PROMIS domains may not fully capture the nuance of HF-specific symptoms. To bridge this gap, Faraz et al. developed the PROMIS + HF profiles (including the PROMIS + HF-27 and PROMIS + HF-10 versions) [18, 19]. These profiles combine the universality of the PROMIS core domains with HF-specific items to provide international and cross-disease comparability alongside disease-specific sensitivity. While the PROMIS + HF has demonstrated excellent psychometric properties internationally, a linguistically validated Chinese version is currently unavailable.

Therefore, this study aimed to perform the translation, cross-cultural adaptation, and psychometric evaluation of the PROMIS + HF profiles in Chinese patients with HF. By introducing this instrument, we aim to provide a standardized, internationally aligned assessment tool to facilitate high-quality clinical practice and research in China.

Methods

Design

The study comprised three sequential phases: translation, cross-cultural adaptation and psychometric evaluation. Following the initial translation, the pre-final version underwent cognitive interviews to ensure conceptual clarity, linguistic equivalence, and cultural relevance for Chinese patients. Subsequently, a cross-sectional study was conducted to rigorously assess the psychometric properties of the Chinese PROMIS + HF-27 and PROMIS + HF-10 profiles. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fuwai Hospital (Approval No. 2022 − 1669).

Setting and sample

A convenience sampling strategy was employed to recruit participants from the HF outpatient clinic and inpatient wards of Fuwai Hospital in Beijing between August 2022 and February 2023. All participants were informed of the study purpose and procedures, and written informed consent was obtained prior to enrollment. Inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 18 years; and (2) a clinical diagnosis of HF according to the Chinese Medical Association guidelines [20]. Exclusion criteria included cognitive impairment, mental disorders, language barriers, or any condition precluding the comprehension or completion of the questionnaire. These criteria were applied consistently across both the cross-cultural adaptation and psychometric validation phases.

For the cognitive interviews, the PROMIS National Center recommends 3 to 10 respondents per item set [21]. Accordingly, five patients with HF were enrolled for this phase. For the psychometric evaluation, the ratio of participants to variables exceeded the rule of thumb (5–10:1), thereby ensuring adequate statistical power and stable parameter estimation for confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) [22]. Additionally, a total of 50 and 75 patients were recruited for the test-retest reliability and responsiveness assessments.

Measures

Demographic and clinical characteristics

A questionnaire was developed to collect sociodemographic and clinical data. Sociodemographic variables, including age, gender, occupation, income and education level, were self-reported by participants. Clinical data, including admission diagnosis, New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional class, left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), and comorbidities, were extracted from electronic medical records (EMR).

PROMIS + HF-27 and PROMIS + HF-10

This study developed the Chinese version of PROMIS + HF profiles following a standardized process of translation and cross-cultural adaptation. The PROMIS + HF-27 evaluates 12 domains across three health dimensions: physical health (e.g., dyspnea, fatigue, physical function), mental health (e.g., anxiety, depression, illness burden) and social health (e.g., social roles, independence). The PROMIS + HF-10 is a short form consisting of 10 key items. Both versions generate subscale scores for physical, mental and social health, as well as an overall summary score. Raw scores are linearly transformed to a standardized 0-100 scale, with higher scores representing better health status.

Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire (KCCQ)

The KCCQ is a disease-specific instrument comprising 23 items across seven domains: physical limitation, symptom frequency, symptom stability, symptom burden, self-efficacy, social function, and quality of life [23]. It utilizes a 2-week recall period. Scores are transformed to a 0-100 scale, where higher scores indicate better health status [24]. Thresholds of 5, 10, and 20 points represent small, moderate, and large clinical changes, respectively [24]. The Chinese version has demonstrated excellent internal consistency (Cronbach’s α: 0.81–0.93) [25]. In this study, the KCCQ served as a criterion measure for convergent validity.

Minnesota Living with Heart Failure Questionnaire (MLHFQ)

The MLHFQ is a 21-item instrument assessing HF-related quality of life over a 4-week recall period [26]. Items are rated on a 6-point Likert scale (0–5). The instrument yields physical, emotional, and total scores, with lower scores reflecting better health status. The Chinese version, validated by Zhu et al. [27], has shown good reliability and validity. The MLHFQ served as an additional criterion measure for convergent validity.

Phase 1: Translation

Permission for translation was granted by the PROMIS Health Organization. The translation procedure strictly adhered to the Functional Assessment of Chronic Illness Therapy Translation (FACIT-trans) guidelines [21, 28].

Step 1: Forward translation

Two bilingual translators (Researchers A and B), both native Chinese speakers, independently translated the items from English to Chinese. One translator was based in China and the other in the United States to ensure broader linguistic and cultural coverage.

Step 2: Reconciliation

A third bilingual researcher (Researcher C), also a native Chinese speaker, reviewed both forward translations in conjunction with the PROMIS translation guidelines. Researcher C developed a reconciled version by selecting the most appropriate wording, integrating elements from both translations or generating new phrasings when necessary. All decisions were documented with detailed rationales.

Step 3: Back translation

A bilingual translator (Researcher D), a native English speaker fluent in Chinese, performed a back-translation of the reconciled version. To ensure objectivity, Researcher D was blinded to the original English source. The back-translation was required to be literal, reflecting the direct semantic meaning of the Chinese wording without stylistic refinement.

Step 4: Expert review

Three native Chinese-speaking experts (one cardiologist and two cardiovascular nursing specialists) independently evaluated the preliminary translations, reconciliation notes and back-translation results. For each item, they selected the most accurate and culturally appropriate option or proposed new translations when necessary.

Step 5: Finalization review

The Translation Project Manager summarized all issues identified during the expert review stage. The Language Coordinator integrated all materials and developed the preliminary test version by refining item wording and resolving discrepancies. The complete translation package was then submitted to the PROMIS Center for final methodological review to ensure alignment with validated PROMIS instruments.

Step 6: Formatting and proofreading

The pre-final version underwent independent formatting and proofreading by two reviewers to confirm that all previous issues had been addressed and that the final instrument met PROMIS formatting standards.

Phase 2: Cross-cultural adaptation (Cognitive interviews)

In August 2022, cognitive interviews were conducted with five HF patients to evaluate comprehension and the response process. The interview guide followed the framework proposed by Willis (see Supplement 1). Feedback was analyzed by the Lead Translator and Language Coordinator to identify items requiring revision. Revisions were incorporated to produce the final Chinese PROMIS + HF profiles.

Phase 3: Psychometric evaluation

Structural validity

CFA was performed using the robust Maximum Likelihood Estimator (MLR) to evaluate the factor structure of the PROMIS + HF-27 and PROMIS + HF-10 profiles. Model fit was assessed using the Chi-square statistic (χ2), Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis index (TLI), Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR), and Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA). An acceptable CFA model is generally expected to meet the following criteria: χ2/df < 3, CFI and TLI > 0.9, SRMR and RMSEA < 0.08 [29, 30]. Items with a factor loading equal to or higher than the criterion of 0.4 were retained [31]. Given that this study performed a three-factor model validation directly on the three dimensions (physical, mental, and social health) of the PROMIS + HF-27 and PROMIS + HF-10, without further refining factors for specific sub-domains, the criteria for fit indices were relaxed to accommodate potential estimation errors. Consequently, model fit was considered acceptable when χ2/df < 5, CFI and TLI approached 0.9, SRMR and RMSEA were < 0.08 [32].

Criterion-related validity

The 6-Minute Walk Test (6MWT) distance served as an external criterion for physical health. The test was conducted in a 30-meter indoor corridor, allowing patients to rest if symptoms such as severe dyspnea occurred [33]. Pearson or Spearman correlation coefficients were calculated between PROMIS + HF physical and overall Health scores and 6MWT, with coefficients interpreted as moderate (r or rho > 0.3) or strong (r or rho > 0.6) [34].

Convergent validity

Convergent validity was examined by correlating PROMIS + HF scores with established instruments, including the KCCQ and MLHFQ. Correlation coefficients were interpreted using the same thresholds (r > 0.30 = moderate; r > 0.60 = strong).

Known-group validity

Known-group validity was assessed by examining the ability of PROMIS + HF scores to discriminate between patients with different NYHA functional classes (I-IV). Differences in physical and overall health scores were evaluated and visualized using box plots.

Reliability

Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s α for domains with > 2 items and the Spearman-Brown split-half coefficient for 2-item domains. Values ≥ 0.70 were considered acceptable, and ≥ 0.90 indicated excellent reliability. Test-retest reliability was evaluated in a subgroup of 50 stable patients who reported “no change” in symptoms on KCCQ item 3 and completed the retest after 7 days. Intraclass Correlation Coefficients (ICC) were calculated, with values of 0.61–0.80 indicating good reliability and > 0.80 indicating excellent reliability [35].

Responsiveness

Responsiveness was evaluated in a subset of 75 patients hospitalized for acute HF and reassessed one month post-treatment. Changes in scores were analyzed using paired t-tests and quantified using Cohen’s d effect sizes (small: 0.2; medium: 0.5; large: 0.8) [36].

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 27.0 and Mplus 8.0. Descriptive statistics summarized demographic and clinical characteristics. Structural validity was assessed via CFA. Pearson or Spearman correlations were used to evaluate criterion-related and convergent validity. Known-group validity was evaluated using box plot analysis. Internal consistency was evaluated using Cronbach’s α and the Spearman-Brown coefficient, whereas test-retest reliability was assessed using ICC. Responsiveness was examined via paired t-tests and Cohen’s d.

Results

Translation and cross-cultural adaptation

The translation of the 4 domain labels and 11 items of the PROMIS + HF was conducted in strict accordance with the FACIT-trans method. Demographic characteristics of the translators are detailed in Supplement 2. While the translations provided by different experts were largely consistent, adjustments to phrasing and syntax were made for a small number of items to accommodate linguistic differences between English and Chinese. During the translation process, the previously translated instructions (e.g., “In the past 7 days”) were not included in the same file as the items being translated. Consequently, translators occasionally overlooked the implied past tense context, leading to tense discrepancies between the back-translated version and the original English text. However, since the Chinese language does not utilize explicit grammatical tense markers, this did not affect the accuracy of the final translation results.

In the cognitive interview phase, five patients with HF were recruited to evaluate content validity. The sample was diverse in terms of age, gender, and education (Table 1). Participants completed the scale in 3–8 min, followed by a 10–15 min interview. Data saturation was achieved by the fourth respondent.

Cognitive interviews indicated that the majority of PROMIS + HF content was clear and highly relevant to respondents’ heart failure symptoms. Participants confirmed the feasibility of the “past 7 days” recall period. Notably, three respondents suggested that extending this period to 30 days might introduce memory bias, a finding that supports the validity of the original 7-day timeframe. One respondent appreciated the quantitative definitions provided for frequency-based options (e.g., defining “Rarely” as once and “Sometimes” as 2 to 3 times) and suggested applying similar annotations to intensity-based options (e.g., “A little bit”). This proposal was not adopted after consultation with the PROMIS Center to preserve the subjective nature of the intensity scales. However, modifications were necessary for item phrasing to address linguistic ambiguity. Three respondents interpreted the term “daily work” in the item “How often did you have to rest to finish your daily work?” as referring exclusively to professional employment. After the original developers clarified that the term encompasses all daily life activities, the translation team revised the term to “daily activities” in order to ensure conceptual equivalence in the Chinese context.

Psychometric evaluation

A total of 450 patients were recruited for the cross-sectional survey. Of these, 446 returned completed questionnaires. Following a quality control review by two independent researchers, 26 questionnaires were excluded due to invariant response patterns or logical inconsistencies. Consequently, 420 valid questionnaires were included in the final analysis. Furthermore, 50 patients were enrolled in the test-retest reliability assessment. For the responsiveness assessment, 100 patients were initially enrolled, and 75 completed the follow-up evaluation.

Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics

The mean age of the 420 participants was 57.68 years (SD = 15.37). For the test-retest (n = 50) and responsiveness (n = 75) cohorts, the mean ages were 59.44 (SD = 15.05) and 57.75 (SD = 16.59) years respectively. Detailed characteristics of the study population are presented in Table 2.

Validity

Construct validity

CFA was conducted to verify the three-factor structure (physical, mental, and social health) of both the PROMIS + HF-27 and PROMIS + HF-10. For the PROMIS + HF-27, as shown in Table 4, the fit indices for the initial model (Model 1) did not meet the recommended criteria. Based on modification indices (MIs) and content similarity, error terms were allowed to correlate for five pairs of items: (1) item a9 (“My sleep quality was…”) and a10 (“I had difficulty falling asleep”) within the Sleep Disturbance domain; (2) item a12 (“Did you have chest pain”) and a14 (“How much did pain interfere with your ability to participate in leisure activities”) within the Pain domain; (3) item b3 (“I have had to work really hard to pay attention or I would make a mistake”) and b4 (“I have been upset about my problems with memory, concentration, or making mental mistakes”) within the Cognitive domain; (4) item b5 (“I felt that I wanted to give up on everything”) and b7 (“I felt hopeless”) within the Depression domain; and (5) item c2 (“I am limited in doing my work”) and c4 (“Does your health now limit you in doing things that make you feel independent”). The revised model (Model 2) demonstrated substantially improved fit indices, all of which met the acceptable standards. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the standardized factor loadings for the revised 27-item model ranged from 0.514 to 0.886, indicating good construct validity.

Criterion-related validity

Using the 6-minute walk distance as an external criterion, we analyzed its correlations with the physical and overall health scores of the PROMIS + HF-27 and PROMIS + HF-10. In total, 143 patients completed the 6MWT. The results indicated that the physical health scores of both the PROMIS + HF-27 and PROMIS + HF-10 exhibited strong correlations with the 6MWT (r = 0.653, P < 0.01 and r = 0.628, P < 0.01). The correlations for overall health scores were moderate-to-strong (r = 0.580, P < 0.01 and r = 0.576, P < 0.01), with all correlation coefficients exceeding 0.50.

Convergent validity

Regarding convergent validity, consistent with our hypotheses, the PROMIS + HF profiles demonstrated significant correlations with both the KCCQ and MLHFQ. Specifically, the physical, mental, and overall health scores of both the PROMIS + HF-27 and PROMIS + HF-10 showed strong correlations (r > 0.7) with the KCCQ clinical summary, quality of life, and overall summary scores. The social health score exhibited a slightly lower but still substantial correlation (r > 0.6) with the KCCQ social limitation score. Regarding the MLHFQ, the physical, mental and overall health scores of both versions generally demonstrated strong correlations (r > 0.70) with the MLHFQ physical, emotional, and total scores, although the correlation between the PROMIS + HF-10 and the MLHFQ emotional dimension was slightly lower (r > 0.60) (see Tables 6 and 7).

Known-group validity

To assess known-groups validity, patients were stratified into four groups based on the NYHA functional classification. We investigated whether the physical and overall health scores of the PROMIS + HF-27 and PROMIS + HF-10 could discriminate between patients with different NYHA classes. The results are illustrated in Figs. 3 and 4. The analysis revealed that mean scores for both physical and overall health significantly decreased as NYHA class increased (P < 0.001). These findings indicate that both the PROMIS + HF-27 and PROMIS + HF-10 can effectively distinguish between patients with varying levels of cardiac function.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to translate, culturally adapt, and validate the PROMIS + HF profiles in a Chinese clinical setting. Following a rigorous translation process compliant with FACIT-trans guidelines, the Chinese versions of the instrument demonstrated satisfactory psychometric properties. These findings strongly confirm the utility of PROMIS + HF as a precise and standardized instrument for assessing HRQoL in Chinese patients with HF.

Developing a CFA model for PROMIS + HF posed a challenge because several sub-domains contain fewer than three items, which can lead to unstable parameter estimation in standard CFA. To address this, we evaluated a higher-order three-factor structure (physical, mental, and social health) for both the 27-item and 10-item versions. The model fit was optimized by allowing for residual correlations among items belonging to the same original sub-domain, guided by MIs. This approach yielded acceptable fit indices, thereby confirming that both instruments align well with the theoretical WHO health framework. Notably, the PROMIS + HF-10 demonstrated superior fit indices compared to the PROMIS + HF-27. This is likely attributed to the short-form structure, which involves a simpler factor configuration and reduced measurement error variance relative to the comprehensive 27-item profile.

Our hypotheses regarding criterion-related and convergent validity were largely supported by the data. Theoretically, a shorter 6MWT distance correlates with impaired physical function and reduced capacity for daily activities in HF patients. We therefore hypothesized a positive correlation between 6MWT distance and the physical and overall health scores of the PROMIS + HF-27 and PROMIS + HF-10 versions. The results demonstrated acceptable correlations, with all coefficients exceeding 0.5 (P < 0.01). Notably, the physical health scores of both versions showed stronger correlations with 6MWT distance (r > 0.6, P < 0.01) compared to the overall health scores. This outcome is likely attributed to the fact that the overall health score integrates physical, mental, and social health dimensions. Given that the 6MWT specifically measures exercise tolerance, which is a core component of physical health, the aggregation of the three dimensions in the overall score is likely to reduce this correlation. These findings further support the construct validity of the PROMIS + HF-27 and PROMIS + HF-10. However, it is important to note that the correlation coefficients did not reach the threshold for a strong correlation (r > 0.8). This may be due to the fact that the physical health dimension encompasses domains such as sleep, which are not directly related to physical activity capacity, thus influencing the overall correlation.

As hypothesized, the PROMIS + HF physical, mental, social, and overall health scores demonstrated robust correlations with the corresponding domains of the KCCQ (Clinical Summary, Quality of Life, Social Limitation, Overall Summary) and the MLHFQ (Physical, Emotional, Total). The results confirmed these expectations, with all tested domains showing strong correlations. Notably, the PROMIS + HF-27 consistently exhibited stronger associations with these legacy instruments compared to the PROMIS + HF-10. While the PROMIS + HF-10 offers greater efficiency and lower respondent burden, it necessarily entails a slight reduction in measurement precision and increased error variance compared to the comprehensive 27-item profile. Our findings are consistent with those of Faraz et al. [19], who also analyzed the correlation between PROMIS + HF and KCCQ. Both the PROMIS + HF-27 and the PROMIS + HF-10 scales demonstrated stronger correlations with physical and overall health scores, while correlations with mental and social health scores were slightly weaker. This discrepancy may be indicative of disparities in the specific domains of mental and social health encompassed by the respective instruments, or differences in the item frameworks. In this study, the PROMIS + HF social health dimension demonstrated the lowest correlations (r < 0.70) in comparison to other dimensions. This may be attributable to the exclusion of items pertaining to sexual function from PROMIS + HF, in contrast to the inclusion of such items in KCCQ. During the survey process, we observed that patients perceived the impact of heart failure on sexual function as more severe than other social health-related items. This strong impact on sexual function is likely to influence the correlation between PROMIS + HF social health scores and KCCQ social limitations scores. Furthermore, social health scores in the PROMIS + HF were consistently lower than those for physical and mental health. This decline is initially driven by a severe symptom burden such as dyspnea, fatigue and sleep disorders, which reduces the enthusiasm and physical capacity to engage in social activities. Beyond these physical barriers, the discrepancy reflects the profound impact of social stigma on the social well-being of Chinese HF patients. Many patients strictly regulate their lifestyle and dietary habits, often avoiding social gatherings for fear that their specific requirements might burden others or lead to social marginalization. Within Chinese culture, patients frequently experience “losing face” (diu mian zi) due to functional limitations and a perceived inability to fulfill traditional social or familial roles. This cultural nuance often leads patients to internalize a sense of being a “burden” (lei zhui), leading them to withdraw from social interactions to avoid potential judgment. Given that high levels of perceived social isolation are established predictors of adverse clinical outcomes, including increasing risks of death, hospitalization and emergency department visits [37], these lower social health scores underscore a critical need for integrated social care to improve patient prognosis.

We hypothesized that PROMIS + HF physical and overall health scores would differ significantly across HF patients with varying NYHA functional classes. As illustrated by the box plots, the mean physical and overall health scores for both the PROMIS + HF-27 and PROMIS + HF-10 versions exhibited a significant stepwise decrease corresponding to higher NYHA functional class. These findings are consistent with those reported by Faraz et al. [19], demonstrating that the PROMIS + HF instruments possess robust known-groups validity. However, a notable discrepancy was observed regarding the separation of classes. While the box plots in the study by Faraz et al. [19] showed a lack of clear differentiation between scores for NYHA Class II and III patients, our study demonstrated a more uniform distribution of score increments across all four NYHA classes. This variance is likely attributed to the method of NYHA assessment. Faraz et al. [19] relied on patient self-reporting, which carries risks of bias and error, our study utilized clinical assessments performed by expert clinicians, thereby ensuring higher accuracy and reliability in the determination of functional class.

Both versions of the PROMIS + HF demonstrated strong internal consistency. Cronbach’s α and Spearman-Brown coefficients ranged from 0.71 to 0.95, indicating good to excellent reliability without redundancy. Test-retest reliability was also high (ICC = 0.89–0.98), supporting temporal stability. In longitudinal analysis, PROMIS + HF-27 and PROMIS + HF-10 were able to detect significant improvements in health status one month following treatment for acute decompensated heart failure. The largest changes were seen in physical and overall health domains, consistent with the rapid clinical improvement typically observed following stabilization. Although PROMIS + HF-10 demonstrated good responsiveness, its effect sizes were smaller than those of PROMIS + HF-27, particularly in the mental health domain. This may be explained by the reduced content coverage in PROMIS + HF-10, which retains only anxiety and depression items while omitting cognition, life satisfaction, and illness burden items included in the longer version.

The two instruments possess distinct strengths and limitations tailored to different needs. The PROMIS + HF-27 is primarily designed for academic research, characterized by its comprehensive coverage of diverse health domains. Its larger number of items ensures higher sensitivity in detecting changes in patient health status, although this is accompanied by a heavier respondent burden. Conversely, the PROMIS + HF-10 captures the most critical health domains for HF patients using a concise set of items, thereby minimizing respondent burden and facilitating flexible utility in clinical settings. As the medical paradigm shifts toward holistic health, the need for extensive PRO data collection has increased, resulting in a documented surge in data volume [38]. This trend underscores the critical need, highlighted by the American Heart Association [39], to prioritize both precision and the minimization of item count in future PRO instrument development. The stepwise screening approach proposed by Kroenke et al. [40] during the development of the Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ) offers a valuable framework for achieving this goal. A preliminary screening can be conducted using the PHQ-2 with fewer items. If the patient’s symptoms are significantly positive, it is recommended to complete the longer PHQ-9. Clinicians can adapt this logic by utilizing the PROMIS + HF-10 for initial screening, followed by the PROMIS + HF-27 for a refined, targeted assessment of identified high-risk domains. This tiered strategy effectively mitigates the burden on low-risk patients while ensuring that high-risk patients receive a more sensitive evaluation, ultimately providing clinicians with actionable information characterized by high diagnostic accuracy.

Among the numerous heart failure-specific PROs developed over the last two decades, the KCCQ and MLHFQ remain the most widely applied and regulatory-approved instruments [2]. The PROMIS + HF-27 shares significant structural similarities with these legacy tools, offering comparable item counts and broad coverage of physical, mental, and social health. Our analysis confirmed high convergent validity between PROMIS + HF scores and the corresponding domains of these established measures. Additionally, like the KCCQ-12, the PROMIS + HF-10 provides a viable short-form alternative for multidimensional assessment. However, content focus diverges notably. While the KCCQ prioritizes HF-specific symptoms and the MLHFQ addresses treatment side effects, PROMIS + HF uniquely includes items assessing anxiety, independence, and cognition. A critical advantage of PROMIS + HF is its modular structure, which explicitly categorizes domains to enable distinct scoring for physical, mental, social and overall health score. In contrast, the KCCQ and MLHFQ are mainly focused on physical health with less distinct item categorization, resulting in scoring gaps. Specifically, neither the KCCQ nor the MLHFQ allows for the derivation of the mental health score or the social health score, a critical limitation overcome by the PROMIS + HF framework.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, as a single-center study conducted in a specialized hospital, the recruited patients often presented with relatively severe symptoms. Multi-center studies are required to validate these findings across diverse populations. Second, the sample size used for criterion-related validity and responsiveness analysis was relatively small. These constraints may affect the statistical power and the generalizability of the findings related to objective functional capacity and longitudinal change. Consequently, the current study did not estimate the Minimal Important Difference (MID). Future longitudinal studies with larger, more diverse cohorts are warranted to establish the MID using both anchor-based and distribution-based methods, thereby enhancing the clinical interpretability of the Chinese PROMIS + HF profiles. Third, we did not analyze patients by HF etiology, subtypes or medication regimens. Future research should investigate these subgroups and employ longer follow-up periods to evaluate the prognostic predictive value of the instrument.

Conclusion

The Chinese versions of the PROMIS + HF-27 and PROMIS + HF-10 are valid, reliable, and responsive instruments for assessing HRQoL in HF patients. The PROMIS + HF-27 is recommended for research requiring high precision, while the PROMIS + HF-10 is ideal for routine clinical screening. Their introduction provides Chinese clinicians and researchers with a standardized tool that enables international data comparison and patient-centered care optimization.
