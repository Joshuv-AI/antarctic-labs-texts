Assessment of heavy metals and radon gas in the urine samples: a comparative study between workers of car mechanics and non-car mechanics
Authors: Iman Sabah Obai, Dhuha Jalil Lawi, Ali Abid Abojassim, Abdulhussein A. Alkufi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Urine is the best method for monitoring internal exposure to environmental pollutants such as heavy metals and radon gas. This may lead to human exposure to these environmental pollutants, which is associated with potential health risks. Therefore, this study aims to determine concentrations of heavy metals chromium (Cr), cadmium (Cd), and lead (Pb), and radon (222Rn) concentrations in 20 urine samples collected from male volunteer workers of car mechanics from Al-Najaf governorate, Iraq. Similarly, 5 urine samples from the non-car mechanics group were collected from several regions in the Al-Najaf governorate. Heavy metals and radon gas were measured using an atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS) and a nuclear track detector type CR-39, respectively. The mean value of Cr, Cd, Pb, and Rn concentrations for workers was 0.018 ± 0.011 mg/L, 0.083 ± 0.067 mg/L, 0.004 ± 0.002 mg/L, and 24.300 ± 20.906 Bq/m3, respectively. In non-car mechanic individuals, the mean values of Cr, Cd, Pb, and Rn concentrations were 0.010 ± 0.006 mg/L, 0.002 ± 0.001 mg/L, 0.002 ± 0.0007 mg/L, and 4.246 ± 0.489 Bq/m³, respectively. Statistical analysis revealed that cadmium levels were significantly higher in car mechanics (0.083 ± 0.067 mg/L) compared to non-car mechanics (0.002 ± 0.001 mg/L, p < 0.001), while lead concentrations were also elevated in workers (0.004 ± 0.002 mg/L vs. 0.002 ± 0.0007 mg/L, p = 0.010). Radon levels showed a marked increase in car mechanics (24.30 ± 20.91 Bq/m³) compared to controls (4.25 ± 0.49 Bq/m³, p = 0.003). Spearman’s correlation analysis demonstrated significant positive correlations between radon and lead (ρ = 0.511, p = 0.021), as well as a strong correlation between chromium and cadmium (ρ = 0.692, p = 0.001). Accordingly, the mean values of heavy metals and radon concentrations in the urine samples of workers were generally higher than those in the non-car mechanic group. Additionally, no statistically significant correlations were observed between Rn and Cr, Rn and Cd, Cr and Pb, or Cd and Pb among workers. Overall, this study indicates that Cr concentrations were within the commonly reported reference ranges, while Rn concentrations in urine lack well-established standard reference limits. Therefore, radon results should be interpreted with caution in terms of safety assessment, while Cd and Pb concentrations in most urine samples of workers exceeded the permissible limits. The limitations of the study are described, as the findings do not establish causality between radon and heavy metals exposure and the health of car mechanics and non-car mechanics. Due to the cross-sectional design of this study, the results reflect associations rather than causal relationships and cannot be generalized to populations with different environmental conditions and exposure levels.

Introduction

Radiological and toxicological pollutants can cause cancer and other changes in the health of people and the biosphere, and therefore contribute greatly to environmental changes1. Cadmium (Cd), Chromium (Cr), Manganese (Mn), Nickel (Ni), and Lead (Pb) are important metals for use in various parts of the metal industry, such as the welding process and alloy smelting. The potential for environmental and organismal contamination of heavy metals from sources of occupation is a major concern, as it could lead to adverse effects over the long-term2. Heavy elements are toxic even at low concentrations and have a high density for metallic compounds. Heavy components are those metallic components with a density of about 4–5 times that of water, expressed in grams per cubic centimeter (g/cm³)3. The presence of increased levels of some metals may lead to health problems. Measuring the number of heavy metals within the body of a human being can help in assessing their potential for toxicity4. Chromium and cadmium are classified as cancer-causing to humans by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), a specialized agency of the World Health Organization (WHO), while lead is neurotoxic and causes concern to the public. Chromium, cadmium, and lead are responsible for pulmonary and renal disorders, osteoporosis, and anemia5,6. Measurement of urinary heavy metals has been the main interest of the biological and environmental sciences since. Since then, measurement of heavy metals in urine has been the main interest of both the biological and environmental sciences. Urine is commonly chosen as a preferred matrix because it is easily collected, stored, and prepared for analysis of exposure. This characteristic makes urine an important matrix in metal analysis. The analysis of a urine sample is more truly representative of the whole-body consumption of a particular component than any other biological fluid7. Radionuclides (uranium and radon) can be ingested by humans through various routes such as eating meat, fish, plants, vegetables, smoking, using soil, drinking water, and breathing air3. Most of the external exposure of radionuclides occurs when people come into contact with soil and thereby may come into contact with the radioactive material in their blood and urine8. Further analysis shows that inhaled alpha particles have the ability to penetrate the respiratory system, namely the lungs. Consequently, the cells within the lungs are involved and may cause the development of some illnesses, including leukemia or lung cancer9.

222Rn is a naturally occurring radioactive inert gas. It is produced as a result of the decay of radium-226. The nuclear decay of all nuclei of 222Rn eventually leads to nuclei of 210Pb. A radioactive isotope, radon-222, decays to a radioactive isotope of polonium with an alpha decay half-life of 3.824 days. It is generally thought that the body rapidly removes radon after inhalation and ingestion. Radon that is consumed is eliminated from the body via the process of breathing. However, the uptake of radon into the body is through dissolution of the gas in blood within the GIT and other tissues10. The noble gases, and especially radon, are most soluble in adipose tissue compared to blood, which means that radon will be stored in adipose tissue inside the body to a much larger extent than in the blood. Moreover, the largest internal radiation dose comes from the inhalation of the radon progeny11. Radiation emitters and radon nuclides can enter the body directly upon breathing or consumption, and penetrate tissues inside. Each type of radiation can damage different tissues, and all ionizing radiation can cause cancer. The main difference between the health effects caused by alpha particles, beta particles, gamma rays, and X-rays is their energy. They are powered by their tissue penetration depth, which affects the amount of energy transferred to tissues and the damage they cause to tissues12. The radioactive elements are absorbed into the body, where they are carried by the blood to spread throughout the body. The majority of it is excreted in the urine in a few days, with a portion remaining in the kidneys and bones13. Radon can be measured mainly by air measurements, but some studies have indicated that it is possible to use the presence of radon in biological fluids, such as urine, as an indirect biomarker of internal exposure. Once inhaled or ingested, radon enters the bloodstream and is eliminated in part via urine, which indicates recent exposures. Thus, urine analysis can offer additional information to environmental data, especially in occupational exposure settings where exposure is continuous. Radon or its progeny is detectable in biological samples like blood and urine, and this makes it possible to use it for exposure assessment, as reported previously8,10,13. The analysis of urine samples is one of the most important biomarkers for assessing radiological and toxicological pollutants inside the human organism, and can be used as a reliable parameter for internal exposure14. The sources to which Auto Mechanics might be exposed to heavy metals and radon gas include oil and fuel, paints and coatings, soil and buildings15. Auto mechanics can be exposed to hazardous pollutants like heavy metals (chromium, cadmium, lead), among others. The inhalation or dermal contact with these toxic materials is caused by an occupational activity. They are manufactured by welding, spray painting, and battery recycling. These pollutants have been associated with several health effects such as kidney damage, nerve damage, and cancer15. The other pollutant that is a health concern for auto mechanics is radon gas, which is a recognized lung cancer-causing agent and is usually due to inadequate ventilation in auto mechanics’ shops. New cancer and kidney disease statistics and other health issues that include exposure to heavy metals and radon gas are alarming. The World Health Organization (WHO) estimates that radon causes 3%–14% of lung cancer deaths around the world16. Exposure to radioactive materials, like radon and uranium gas, and to other heavy metals, has also been linked to health issues, such as cancer and kidney disease. There have been other studies suggesting a possible association between kidney cancer and radon exposure17. The number of cancer cases has increased in Iraq, and some studies have studied the impact of radioactive and toxic contamination. Studies have been conducted to determine the natural radioactivity and radon gas levels in certain regions of Iraq and the implications for human health18. Several studies have examined exposure to either heavy metals or radon, but most of these have been environmental studies only, and these might not provide good estimates of exposure to an individual. Also, most studies have focused on one contaminant at a time and ignored the possible combined effects of multiple contaminants. Moreover, very little has been done to highlight high-risk occupations like car mechanics, especially in developing countries, including Iraq, where occupational risk and environmental monitoring may be weak. Thus, the need still exists for a simultaneous assessment of radon exposure along with other heavy metals in biological samples for a more complete assessment of occupational exposure and related health effects. The novelty of this research is that it brings together the AAS and CR-39 technique to comprehensively evaluate occupational exposure to heavy metals and radon-222 and to compare the results with the control group. The present study aims to investigate the level of 222Rn, Cr, Cd, and Pb contamination in urine samples of car mechanics and non-mechanics from Al-Najaf Governorate, Iraq, in order to evaluate the potential occupational exposure and to compare the level of contamination between the group of exposed workers and the control group. Based on this objective, it is hypothesized that occupational exposure in car mechanics leads to significantly elevated levels of heavy metals (Cr, Cd, and Pb) and radon (222Rn) in urine samples compared to non-exposed individuals.

Materials and methods

Sampling

Only males were included in the current study and the groups (exposed and non-exposed) were chosen according to certain criteria such as no history of drug or alcohol addiction, no previous illnesses and non-smoking. Although there was a very strict set of inclusion criteria, other potentially confounding variables were not strictly controlled such as diet and environment. The data in Table 1 are summarized in detail for all the donors involved in the research who were divided into two groups: (car mechanics workers and non-car mechanics workers (control). The table distinguishes between four major variables: sample code, age, weight and years of employment. The first group of workers, car mechanics, is made up of 20 persons ranging in age from 18 to 61 years, weight from 54 to 133 kg, and working experience from 1 to 33 years indicating a wide range of exposure. The second group is non-car mechanic (Control) of 5 persons aged from 20 to 60 years, with a weight range of 50 to 85 kg, and a zero occupational exposure (years of job), as illustrated. This table compares the demographic and occupational factors of the research group and serves as the basis to look at the effect of work-related exposures on health outcomes. The average age of the exposed group was 37.5 years and that of the non-exposed group was 40 years, which was average for ages. These samples were taken in August of 2024. These samples were taken from the industrial area of the Governorate of Najaf. A survey of car mechanics found that they work an average of eight hours a day, except on Fridays. Finally, all participants in this study provided written consent, and various data and information were gathered, including age, occupation, marital status, education level, history of work, and work duration. Due to recruitment constraints, the sample size was limited and unequal between groups. This study was designed as a preliminary exploratory investigation; therefore, no formal sample size calculation was performed. However, strict inclusion criteria were applied to ensure participant homogeneity, and non-parametric statistical analysis (Kruskal–Wallis test) was used to account for the small and imbalanced sample size.

Sample preparation

The laboratory containers were thoroughly cleaned with detergents and distilled water to ensure they were completely free of impurities or contamination before the metal concentrations in the urine were measured. 10 mL of urine was obtained from both the car mechanics workers’ and non-working individuals. 1 mL of urine was taken from each sample and placed in a different test tube for the acid digestion procedure. Each test tube was filled with 1 mL of concentrated nitric acid (HNO3) from Merck Company (England). After that, the test tubes were left at room temperature for 24 h. Nitric acid and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) from Merck Company (Germany) are combined in a 1:2 ratio to create the solution. After that, the mixture is heated to 70 °C and allowed to settle for 1–2 h until the solution becomes clear. The materials were allowed to cool to room temperature after the digestion process. Deionized water was then used to dilute the samples until they reached a final volume of 10 mL. Nitrocellulose filters with 0.45 μm pore sizes were used to filter the produced samples further19.

After cleaning specialized containers with detergents and distilled water, identical urine samples from the same individuals were obtained to ensure radon concentration was measured without contamination. Car mechanics workers’ and non-car mechanics provided 1 mL urine samples. After labeling Eppendorf tubes with symbols, urine samples were chilled in the fridge until analysis20.

Measuring of heavy metals components

Thereafter, the processed samples underwent heavy metal analysis using an atomic absorption spectrophotometer (AAS), namely the Buck Scientific model 210VGP. The concentrations of heavy metals in urine were measured in parts per million (ppm) or milligrams per liter (mg/L). Four standard solutions were used for each element to establish calibration curves for determining the concentrations of heavy metals in the examined samples. The analytical reliability of the AAS method for urine samples was supported through calibration using standard solutions, application of blank measurements, and comparison with previously published analytical procedures for biological matrices. In addition, validation of the analytical method for heavy metal determination in human urine samples was based on previously reported approaches for biological matrices21–23. Cadmium, chromium, and lead calibration working solutions were prepared at the prescribed concentrations. Before measurement, the standard concentrations (ppm) used for each element were entered into the associated software, and the standard solutions were placed in the device atomizer24,25.

Measuring of radon concentration

Although radon is commonly measured in air, previous studies have demonstrated the feasibility of detecting radon in biological samples, including urine, using solid-state nuclear track detectors such as CR-3920. Urine samples (size 3 mL) were collected and analysed in terms of radon concentration with a CR-39 nuclear track detector (Analysis Systems Ltd, UK) of thickness 1 mm and dimensions 1 × 1 cm2. In the present study, the dimensions of the cup container is 7 cm in height and 2 cm in diameter. The CR-39 detectors were attached to the upper part of the cup container with double-sided adhesive tape in a way to have the sensitive side of the detector facing up, 5 cm from a sample for 3 months26. The detectors were then collected and chemically etched for 1 h at 98 0 C in 6.25 N NaOH. An optical microscope of magnification (10 × 40)27 was used to count the number of tracks on each detector.

Quality assurance and quality control (QA/QC) measures taken were pre-cleaned containers, high-purity reagents, and controlled acid digestion and dilution. Calibrated AAS was performed for heavy metals, and pre-cleaned containers, CR-39 detectors, standard etching, and background were used for radon measurements. Results were reliable as the volume and conditions of the samples were maintained at constant values. Heavy metal measurements and radon measurements both had quality assurance and quality control. To assure accuracy and detect contamination, calibration with a standard solution and the use of blanks were used for heavy metals. The handling, exposure conditions, and etching procedures of the detectors were standardized in order to assure reliability of the measurements, and background track density was taken into account.

Statistical analysis

The IBM SPSS Statistics software, especially version 27 for the Windows operating system, was used to conduct the statistical analysis of the data. Graphing was also done as part of the analysis. The Spearman’s correlation coefficient, mean, and standard deviation are among the analyses. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare mean Cr, Cd, Pb, and radon-222 values between car mechanics workers and non-car mechanics. This non-parametric test compares two independent groups when the data are not normally distributed. It is used to determine whether the two groups have statistically significant differences. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Because multiple statistical comparisons were performed, the results should be interpreted cautiously because of the possible increase in type I error. Since the present study was designed as a preliminary exploratory investigation with a limited sample size, no formal correction for multiple comparisons was applied. Due to the limited sample size, the multivariate regression analyses were considered exploratory and were interpreted cautiously to avoid overestimation of associations.

Results and discussion

Table 2 delineates the concentrations of chromium, cadmium, and lead in urine samples from research participants, categorized into automotive workers and non-car mechanics (control), with a comparison to the American Conference of Governmental Industrial Hygienists (ACGIH) permissible levels (0.025 mg/L for Cr, 0.005 mg/L for Cd, and 0.05 mg/L for Pb)31. In automotive workers, the concentration range of chromium was found to be (0.001 to 0.074) mg/L, with three samples (U10, U12, U14) exceeding the permissible limit, indicating that occupational exposure may have occurred to the workers due to chromium-containing substances. The cadmium concentration of this sample set was in the range (0.004–0.206) mg/L, and 14/20 samples exceeded the concentration limit; one of the samples, U8 (0.206 mg/L), might be a chronic source of exposure (e.g., welding fumes, battery). Lead concentration was found to be in the acceptable range (0.0021–0.0089) mg/L. The concentrations of chromium (0.001 and 0.019) mg/L, cadmium (0.001 and 0.0045) mg/L, and lead (0.001 and 0.0029) mg/L were all below the limit and thus indicate limited exposure for the non-worker group. The results suggest that there is a lot of occupational exposure to cadmium and chromium in the automobile industry, and health risks of the workers include renal impairment, respiratory problems, and potential carcinogenic effects, which highlight the importance of implementing strict occupational safety measures and regular monitoring of the workers’ environment, as well as the use of personal protective equipment to limit metal exposure. The findings are consistent with those of other workers31,32 who reported health risks for automotive workers exposed at work to heavy metals.

The data in Table 3 shows the concentration of radon-222 in urine of car mechanics and non-car mechanics as radionuclides per cubic meter (Bq/m³) along with the safety limits recommended by the International Commission on Radiological Protection (ICRP)33, the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA)34, the World Health Organization (WHO)35 and Code of Federal Regulations (CFR)36(39–300 Bq/m³). The level of radon among car mechanics rose from (3.82–89.13) Bq/m3, with most of the cases being below the ICRP upper limit of 300 Bq/m3. Nonetheless, U12 exceeds the limitations set by the WHO (100 Bq/m³), EPA (74–184 Bq/m³), and CFR (39 Bq/m³). Samples U3, U8, U9, and U15, however, all violated the CFR limit, possibly indicating radon exposure related to occupational factors, including poor ventilation and radon-emitting materials. The findings suggest that there is an increased risk of exposure to radon for workers in the automotive industry, which may lead to higher rates of adverse health effects like lung cancer, highlighting the importance of improving ventilation systems, regular testing, and mitigation measures in these facilities.

The concentrations of Cr, Cd, Pb, and 222Rn in urine samples of workers and non-car mechanics are compared in Table 4. The mean concentration of chromium in workers was higher (0.018 ± 0.011 mg/L) than that of non-car mechanics (0.010 ± 0.006 mg/L), but the difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.784). Workers (0.083 ± 0.067 mg/L) had markedly elevated levels of cadmium compared to non-car mechanics (0.002 ± 0.001 mg/L), with a very significant difference (P < 0.001). The lead concentrations for workers (0.004 ± 0.002 mg/L) were higher than those of non-car mechanics (0.002 ± 0.0007 mg/L), and there was a significant difference (P = 0.010). The workers had significantly high concentrations of radon-222 (24.300 ± 20.906 Bq/m³) compared with non-car mechanics (4.246 ± 0.489 Bq/m³) (P = 0.003). The findings support the concerns about occupational exposure to workers, particularly cadmium and lead, and the need for improved occupational safety and regular health monitoring. The concentration of urine lead, chromium, and cadmium for car mechanics and non-mechanics is compared in Fig. 1. The findings reveal higher levels of lead among car mechanics, suggesting that they are exposed to a variety of substances, including lubricants, fuels, and pollutants from vehicle exhaust. In contrast, the concentrations of chromium remained within the limits of the normal range, while the cadmium concentrations were significantly higher in the car mechanics group, suggesting significant occupational exposure of workers. The graph (Fig. 2) shows the comparison of the urinary radon levels of car mechanics and non-mechanics. The data show that radon levels in car mechanics are significantly higher, indicating possible occupational exposure to higher radon levels. This is possibly because the workplaces do not have proper ventilation, or because the cause of the radiation is not known. Long-term exposure to higher radon levels may have health risks, such as respiratory issues and an increased risk of radiation-related cancers.

Table 5 shows the Spearman correlation coefficients between 222Rn, Cr, Cd, and Pb for workers, highlighting the strength and significance of their relationships. There is a significant positive correlation between Rn and Pb (ρ = 0.511; P = 0.021), which may mean that there is a connection in the way of exposure or absorption. There was a highly significant correlation between Cr and Cd (ρ = 0.692, P = 0.001), which suggests a possible common origin or contamination source for these two elements. There was no strong or significant association between other correlations, such as Rn and Cr, Rn and Cd, Cr and Pb, and Cd and Pb, and these are considered weak or no correlation. The results draw attention to the necessity for more studies on combined exposures and the possible health effects of combined exposure to workers.

The possible association between long-term exposure to the job and the increase in radon concentration was observed from the significant positive correlation between age (ρ = 0.693, p < 0.01) and radon concentration (Fig. 3). Overall, the data in Fig. 3 indicate that links may exist between exposure-related variables and the concentrations of some pollutants, especially radon and lead.

The findings from this study regarding the impact of radon and heavy metal exposure in car mechanics can be extrapolated to other occupations, including repair shops and industrial work facilities, and to regions of similar exposure and environment. There are several recommendations to limit the potential exposure of car mechanics to radon and heavy metals in the workplace: enhance the ventilation, provide personal protective equipment, provide training and education, and have periodic medical examinations.

The small sample size used is one of the major drawbacks of our study. The sampling process was very rigorous since the study aimed at selecting statistically representative individuals, that is, free from chronic diseases, non-smokers, no drug or alcohol abuse, similar environment, and different ages. I would therefore suggest a thorough study, including other variables like smoking, disease, and age difference in order to get a more generalized study.

This study is one of the few studies conducted in Iraq to measure the levels of both heavy metal and radon exposure among car mechanics by the combined use of two complementary methods (AAS and CR-39) and a control group to provide a reliable comparison. Its results are, however, restricted to the one-governorate sample, and other toxic metals and radioactive elements were not measured, as well as a cross-sectional design that cannot evaluate long-term health effects. Furthermore, several statistical comparisons were made without formal procedures for adjustment, so there is a possibility of an increase in the likelihood of a Type I statistical error. Moreover, the multivariate regression was conducted based on a small sample size, which might result in the risk of model over-fitting and the instability and generalizability of the estimated associations. In the present study, the urinary concentration of the heavy metal was reported as raw concentration (mg/L), without correction for creatinine. Thus, caution should be used when comparing with international biomonitoring reference values, as the dilution of urine may affect the concentration measured in the sample, and future studies should incorporate creatinine-corrected measurements to better assess exposure. These findings are novel because only a few studies in the literature have examined the level of heavy metals in car mechanics, and none of the studies examined the level of radon concentration in the urine of car mechanics. Likewise, reported urinary biomonitoring results for both Nigerian car workers and controls had significantly higher concentrations of both Pb and Cd than have been reported elsewhere38,39.

Conclusion

Heavy metal (Cr, Cd, and Pb) as well as radon (222Rn) concentrations measured in the urine samples of car mechanics in Al-Najaf governorate were generally above the levels found in non-car mechanics. This distinction identifies possible occupational exposures to the car repair worker, mostly via inhalation and dermal exposure. Compared to the control group, cadmium levels were much higher in the automotive workers than were the levels of chromium and lead. Lead levels were within the allowable limits, but the high levels of cadmium in the workers suggest possible occupational and/or environmental contamination on the job. High levels of cadmium and radon found in car mechanics could be due to high exposure in their profession. Long-term exposure to these pollutants has been linked to harmful renal, respiratory, and carcinogenic effects from previous studies. The present study, however, was only a biomonitoring assessment and did not involve clinical or diagnostic assessments, and so direct health effects cannot be confirmed. Although most of the workers had radon-222 levels in the higher recommended safety limit of 300 Bq/m³, a few radon-222 concentrations were found above lower reference levels specified by WHO, EPA, and CFR guidelines, indicating that there is a need for more improvement in radon-222 monitoring and ventilation practices in the workplace. A combination of AAS and solid-state nuclear track detectors (CR-39) enabled the detection of both chemical and radioactive occupational exposures. Occupational biomonitoring and better ventilation in the workplace are recommended, especially for those exposed for extended periods, based on high levels of cadmium and radon that have been found in car mechanics. The present study can only be considered to have investigated the sample from Al-Najaf governorate. They should not be extrapolated to other populations or work settings without further large-scale studies of broader geographic areas and larger populations. However, the results suggest laboratory-based potential concerns regarding laboratory exposure that need further exploration in other environmental/occupational settings. Future studies are recommended to include larger populations from multiple Iraqi governorates and to investigate additional heavy metals (e.g., nickel and manganese) and naturally occurring radioactive elements (e.g., uranium and thorium). Longitudinal studies are also recommended to better evaluate the possible long-term combined health effects of heavy metal and radioactive exposure among car mechanics.
