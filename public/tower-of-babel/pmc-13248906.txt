Retrospective cohort study of mRNA COVID-19 vaccine-associated enhanced disease in the Vaccine Safety Datalink, 2021–23
Authors: Edward A. Belongia, Burney A. Kieke, Kayla E. Hanson, David L. McClure, Thomas G. Boyce, Matthew F. Daley, Malini B. DeSilva, Laura P. Hurley, Stephanie A. Irving, Lisa A. Jackson, Jonathan Duffy, Michael M. McNeil, Maria E. Sundaram
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTVaccine-associated enhanced disease (VAED) was recognized as a theoretical risk when COVID-19 vaccines were authorized. Epidemiologic data can identify a signal for VAED if vaccine effectiveness (VE) is negative for preventing serious illness. In 2021, we initiated a cohort study to examine VAED indirectly by estimating VE against COVID-19 hospitalization and severe illness in the Vaccine Safety Datalink. The study cohort included VSD enrollees who were age-eligible to receive mRNA COVID-19 vaccine from February 2021-March 2023. Outcomes were initial COVID-19-associated hospital admission and severe in-hospital complications (acute respiratory distress or failure). VE was estimated by dose, variant period, and for early (14–119 d) and late (≥120 d) post-vaccination periods. VE was defined as 100*(1- adjusted hazard ratio). Relative VE (rVE) was estimated for bivalent vaccine versus ≥2 monovalent vaccine doses. The hospitalization analysis included all 16,027 enrollees with COVID-19 hospitalization and a 5% random sample of non-hospitalized enrollees who contributed person-time (n = 138,238). Two-dose original monovalent VE against hospitalization exceeded 90% in most age groups during the pre-Delta and Delta periods. VE was lower during the Omicron period. During the Omicron BA.2/BA.4/BA.5/XBB period, a fourth monovalent dose or bivalent dose generated VE of 59%–78% across age groups with modest waning. rVE was 48%–60% across age groups for bivalent vaccine vs. ≥2 original monovalent doses. Overall, the results were similar for VE against severe in-hospital complication. In conclusion, there was no signal for VAED during any variant period or for any specific dose of monovalent or bivalent vaccine.

Introduction

Vaccine-associated enhanced disease (VAED) is a rare phenomenon that occurs when a vaccine triggers an immune response that results in greater illness severity during subsequent infection with the target pathogen. Only a few instances of VAED have been identified over the past seven decades despite widespread adoption of many new vaccines and technologies. VAED has been observed in three human vaccine trials. In the mid-1960s, a formalin-inactivated RSV vaccine was associated with hospital admission among infants who subsequently acquired RSV infection.1 Also during the 1960s, a cluster of severe atypical measles cases was identified among children who had received a formalin-inactivated measles vaccine several years earlier.2 More recently, a Phase 3 trial of a tetravalent dengue vaccine in children aged 2–5 y found that vaccination was associated with increased risk for severe dengue, but only in children who were seronegative when vaccinated.3 The immunologic mechanisms of VAED are not well-understood and may vary by antigen and manufacturing platform. At least two immunologic abnormalities appear to contribute. The first is antibody-dependent enhancement (ADE) of disease, which occurred in some children after receipt of the dengue vaccine. ADE can occur when binding (i.e., non-neutralizing) antibodies allow viruses to enter phagocytic cells, where virus replication can continue and lead to more severe disease.4 Immune complex deposition may also contribute to ADE. The second major mechanism is enhanced disease, characterized by a T helper cell 2 (Th2)-biased CD4+ response. There was evidence for this phenomenon in children who received the inactivated RSV and measles vaccines. The source of the Th2-biased response is uncertain, but contributing factors may include weak stimulation of T cells by the vaccine antigen, elevated ratio of binding antibodies to neutralizing antibodies, or formalin disruption of vaccine epitopes.5

The severe acute respiratory syndrome (SARS) outbreak of 2003 stimulated efforts to develop vaccines against SARS coronavirus (SARS-CoV-1), and later, the Middle East Respiratory Syndrome (MERS) coronavirus. In some cases, vaccinated animals developed immunologic abnormalities suggestive of VAED pathology.5 Prior to emergency authorization of SARS-CoV-2 vaccines, there was justifiable concern about the potential for VAED. However, studies in nonhuman primates and phase 2/3 human trials did not identify any immunologic abnormalities suggesting VAED, and post-authorization studies have consistently demonstrated high levels of protection against serious illness with rapid waning.

VAED can be difficult to distinguish from vaccine failure, i.e., the absence of protection without causing enhanced pathology. Immunologic markers are needed to make this distinction, including the ratio of binding antibodies to neutralizing antibodies and measures of Th2-biased response.4 Using epidemiologic data only, VAED would be expected to generate significantly negative absolute vaccine effectiveness against severe disease. In late 2020, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) requested a retrospective cohort study to examine VAED indirectly through vaccine effectiveness against coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) hospitalization in the Vaccine Safety Datalink (VSD). Here we report vaccine effectiveness during distinct viral variant periods for original formula mRNA vaccine doses and the bivalent booster that was authorized in September 2022.

Methods

Study design and data sources

This retrospective cohort study was conducted within the VSD. The VSD was established in 1990 as a collaboration between the CDC and multiple integrated health care organizations.6 Currently, the VSD includes 13 collaborating organizations and contains data representing approximately 4% of the US population. Each participating organization maintains comprehensive electronic health records with data on immunizations, outpatient and inpatient diagnoses, and procedures. This study included data from six VSD sites: Denver Health (Colorado), HealthPartners (Minnesota), Kaiser Permanente Colorado, Kaiser Permanente Northwest (Oregon), Kaiser Permanente Washington, and Marshfield Clinic Health System (Wisconsin).

COVID-19 vaccination data at participating sites were sourced from electronic health records and state immunization registries, where applicable. VSD sites have established linkages with state immunization information systems to ensure near-complete capture of COVID-19 vaccinations, including doses administered in pharmacies and other locations not affiliated with the health care organization.7

Ethical oversight and approval

This was a minimal risk observational study conducted in accordance with federal law and CDC policy (45 CFR part 46.114; 21 CFR part 56.114); as such, direct patient consent was not deemed to be necessary. This study was reviewed and approved by all participating sites’ institutional review boards (IRBs).

Study cohort

The study period was February 1, 2021, through March 31, 2023. VSD enrollees were eligible for cohort inclusion if they were age-eligible for receipt of mRNA COVID-19 vaccine during this interval. Person-time follow-up for persons ≥16 y of age began on February 1, 2021 or when an individual met the age-eligibility criteria; follow-up for persons 12–15 y of age began on May 10, 2021 or when an individual met the age eligibility criteria; follow-up for persons 5–11 y of age began on October 29, 2021 or when an individual met the age eligibility criteria; and follow-up for persons 6 months through 4 y of age began on June 17, 2022 or when an individual met the age eligibility criteria. Individual follow-up was censored at the time of death, COVID-19 hospitalization, disenrollment from VSD, receipt of a non-mRNA COVID-19 vaccine, or end of the study period. A minimum of 12 months of continuous enrollment in VSD was required at the beginning of follow-up (or since birth if age <12 months). Individuals with a non-valid pattern of COVID-19 vaccine receipt (based on FDA authorization), as well as those receiving mixed-product mRNA primary series, were excluded from the cohort.

Vaccine doses and variant periods

mRNA COVID-19 vaccines were identified by CVX (vaccine administered) codes (www.cdc.gov/iis/code-sets/index.html). Vaccination status was a time-varying variable in the analysis. At each time point, vaccination status was categorized by the total number of monovalent doses received (ranging 0–4) or receipt of the 2022–23 bivalent vaccine. All monovalent doses received as part of this analysis represented the original COVID-19 vaccine formulation (“original monovalent”). Vaccinated follow-up time began 14 d after receipt of each dose. Receipt of two mRNA vaccine doses from the same manufacturer constituted completion of the primary series with one exception: 3 doses were required for children under five years of age who received the Pfizer vaccine. For this analysis, we did not identify immunocompromised individuals who were recommended to receive additional doses. Moderna and Pfizer doses were considered interchangeable for subsequent doses after completion of the primary series. To account for the role of waning immunity in changes of vaccine effectiveness over time, we categorized vaccination status based on the interval since receipt of the most recent dose. The interval 14–119 d after vaccine receipt was defined as the early post-vaccination period. The late post-vaccination period was defined as 120 d or more after receipt of the vaccine.

This analysis included hospitalizations during four periods of variant circulation: Pre-Delta (February 1 – July 2, 2021), Delta (July 3 – December 18, 2021), Omicron BA.1 (December 19, 2021 – March 19, 2022), and Omicron BA.2/BA.4/BA.5/XBB (March 20, 2022 – March 31, 2023). For brevity, we refer to the latter period as ‘Omicron BA.2.’ The variant time periods were determined by the predominant circulating variant/subvariant in the United States.8,9

Outcomes

The study outcomes were COVID-19 hospital admission and severe in-hospital complication. Hospital admissions due to COVID-19 were identified by diagnoses coded according to the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10). A COVID-19 hospitalization was defined as any admission associated with ICD-10 diagnosis code U07.1 (COVID-19) or J12.82 (pneumonia due to COVID-19). Severe in-hospital complication was defined as the presence of ICD-10 code J80 (acute respiratory distress syndrome) or J96.0 (acute respiratory failure) among patients hospitalized for COVID-19.

Statistical analysis

We estimated hazard ratios (HR) for COVID-19 hospitalization and severe in-hospital complication using Cox regression models stratified by VSD site. Absolute vaccine effectiveness (VE) was defined as 100*(1-HR) for each dose relative to unvaccinated person-time. For the bivalent vaccine, relative VE (rVE) was estimated using monovalent vaccinated person-time (2, 3, or 4 original monovalent dose recipients, both separately and combined) as the comparator. The time scale for the regression models was calendar time in days (February 1, 2021 – March 31, 2023). Follow-up time ended with the initial COVID-19 hospital admission, disenrollment from VSD, death, receipt of a non-mRNA COVID-19 vaccine, or end of study period.

For computational efficiency, we employed a sampling approach due to the cohort size and large number of model parameters. The sampling process was applied separately for the hospitalization and severe in-hospital complication endpoints. Briefly, the cohort was divided into two groups: those who experienced the outcome during their follow-up and those who did not. The former were included in the modeling dataset and were assigned an analytic weight of 1.0 (i.e., all individuals with the outcome were included in the analysis). A 5% simple random sample was drawn from the latter group and included in the modeling dataset. Each person in the latter group had an analytic weight equal to the inverse of the sampling probability (1/.05 = 20). The regression analyses incorporated the analytic weights and used robust variance estimation.10 Since the sampling procedure was performed independently for each outcome, the person-time estimates for the two outcomes differ slightly.

Time-invariant independent variables included in the regression models were sex and race/ethnicity (White, non-Hispanic; Hispanic/Latino; unknown; multiple races/other race, non-Hispanic). Time-dependent variables included vaccine dose, age group during each variant period (a categorical variable with levels for each combination of age group and variant period), dose*age group-variant (interaction term), and presence of ≥1 high-risk condition associated with severe COVID-19 (assessed on January 1 of each study year). High-risk status was defined as the presence of a chronic disease associated with increased risk of severe COVID-19; a list of specific diagnosis codes is available in Supplemental Table S1. Prior receipt of influenza vaccine (since July 1, 2019), prior receipt of pneumococcal vaccine (since 65th birthday), and prior receipt of herpes zoster vaccine (since 50th birthday) were included as covariates to minimize potential confounding related to preventive care utilization and health behaviors. The models included six COVID-19 vaccine exposure categories (0, 1, 2, 3, or 4 original monovalent doses, 1 bivalent dose), with the latter four subdivided into 14–119 and ≥120 d after receipt of the corresponding dose. Estimates for partial completion of the primary series (i.e., 1 dose) are not reported. We estimated absolute VE for each age group (6 months-4 y, 5–11, 12–17, 18–49, 50–64, 65–79, and ≥80 y) within each variant period accounting for vaccine availability by age group; rVE was estimated for the bivalent booster during the Omicron BA.2 period. To account for sparse data, we only report VE estimates for subgroups with five or more outcome events; due to this restriction, no VE estimates for the 6 months through 4-y age group are reported.

Results

The final study cohort comprised 2,780,786 people eligible to contribute person-time during the study period; approximately 1.7–2.0 million people were at risk for COVID-19 hospitalization at any point in time. The analytic cohort for the hospitalization outcome included all 16,027 cohort members who were hospitalized with COVID-19 (Table 1) and a 5% random sample of those who were not hospitalized (n = 138,238). For severe in-hospital complication, there were 8,145 events (Table 2), and the 5% random sample included 138,633 individuals. The sampled non-hospitalized cohorts were closely matched (<1% absolute difference) to the corresponding full cohorts on all model covariates (data not shown). Follow-up person time was greatest during the Omicron BA.2 period and lowest during the Omicron BA.1 period (Table 3). Two-dose (Primary Series) effectiveness (original monovalent vaccine)

During the pre-Delta variant period and within 14–119 d after vaccination (early post-vaccination period), two-dose VE against COVID-19 hospitalization exceeded 95% among adults less than 80 y old (Figure 1). VE was 89% (95% confidence interval [CI], 84–93%) among adults ≥80 y of age. During the early post-vaccination period, two-dose VE against severe in-hospital complication was 98% (95% CI, 97–99%) for ages 65–79, and 93% (95% CI, 89–96%) among individuals ≥80 y of age. The number of hospitalizations was insufficient to estimate two-dose VE ≥ 120 d after vaccination during the pre-Delta period. Two-dose VE against hospitalization remained high during the Delta variant period. During the early post-vaccination period, VE was 93% to 95% in every adult age group <80 y. Two-dose VE was 89% (95% CI, 81–94%) among adults aged ≥80 y. During the late post-vaccination period, two-dose VE against hospitalization ranged from 80% in adolescents 12–17 y old to approximately 95% in persons 18-64 y old. Two-dose VE was slightly lower among persons 65–79 y old (91%; 95% CI, 88–93%) and ≥80 y (84%; 95% CI, 79–87%). Two-dose VE against severe in-hospital complication in adults was 92%–98% in the early post-vaccination period and 85%–97% in the late post-vaccination period.

During the Omicron BA.1 variant period, two-dose VE against hospitalization in adults was lower compared to the Delta period. VE in adult age groups ranged from 46%–78% during the early post-vaccination period, and 60%–71% during the late post-vaccination period. VE was 50% (95% CI, 26–67%) among adolescents 12–17 y old during the late post-vaccination period. For severe in-hospital complication, two-dose VE was 62% to 81% among adults <80 y old during the early post-vaccination period; VE was 72% to 86% in adult age groups during the late post-vaccination period.

Three-dose effectiveness (original monovalent vaccine)

During the Delta variant period, the number of outcome events was sufficient for three-dose VE estimation only in the early post-vaccination period among those aged ≥50 y for the hospitalization outcome, and ≥65 y for severe in-hospital complication. All of these estimates were ≥98% (Figure 2). During the Omicron BA.1 variant period, three-dose VE against hospitalization was very high for adult age groups, ranging from 86%–95% during the early post-vaccination period. In the late post-vaccination period, three-dose VE was 89% or higher among adults 50 y of age and older. For severe in-hospital complication, three-dose VE in adults was 94%–98% during the early post-vaccination period. Only two age groups had enough hospitalizations with severe in-hospital complications following dose 3 in the late post-vaccination period to generate VE estimates: persons aged 65–79 y (VE 93%; 95% CI, 89–96%) and persons aged ≥80 y (VE 93%; 95% CI, 90–94%).

During the Omicron BA.2 variant period, three-dose VE against hospitalization during the early post-vaccination period was 44%–60% across adult age groups. VE was modestly lower during the late post-vaccination period, ranging from 42% (95% CI, 33–50%) among people 65–79 y of age to 53% (95% CI, 41–63%) among those 18–49 y of age. Three-dose VE against severe in-hospital complication in the early post-vaccination period ranged from 46% in people ≥80 y of age to 76% in people 65–79 y of age. In the late post-vaccination period, estimates ranged from 44% in people ≥80 y of age to 65% in people 50–64 y of age.

Four-dose effectiveness during Omicron BA.2 period (original monovalent vaccine)

During the Omicron BA.2 period, four dose early VE against hospitalization ranged from 59% (95% CI, 41–71%) in persons 50–64 y of age to 75% (95% CI, 70–79%) among those ≥ 80 y of age (Figure 3). VE during the late vaccination period was 51%–65% across age groups, with significant waning observed in the 65–79 age group. VE against severe in-hospital complication was similar to VE against hospitalization, ranging from 72% to 80% during the early pre-vaccination period and 62% to 66% in the late post-vaccination period. Bivalent vaccine effectiveness during Omicron BA.2 period

For COVID-19 hospitalization, absolute VE of the bivalent booster during the early post-vaccination period ranged from 69% (95% CI, 53–80%) in people aged 50–64 y to 78% (95% CI, 63–87%) in people aged 18–49 y (Figure 4). VE estimates were modestly reduced in most age groups during the late post-vaccination period, with absolute VE reduction ≤10% compared to the early post-vaccination period. For severe in-hospital complication, absolute VE during the early post-vaccination period ranged from 72% (95% CI, 65–78%) in people ≥80 y to 80% (95% CI, 66–88%) in people 50–64 y of age. There was a modest reduction in VE during the late post-vaccination period, with absolute VE reduction <10% for ages 65–79 y and ≥80 y. Discussion

This study was conceived early in the COVID-19 pandemic to identify a potential statistical signal for vaccine-associated enhanced disease using immunization records and electronic health records. No such signal was found in any age group during the early postvaccination period or during the late postvaccination period. We separately examined each monovalent dose and the bivalent dose, as applicable, during different variant periods. Absolute VE estimates and the lower bound of the 95% confidence interval were positive for nearly all age groups, doses, and time periods, indicating vaccination was protective rather than triggering more severe COVID-19 disease. Relative VE against hospitalization was high for the bivalent vaccine, although rVE was negative among persons ≥80 y of age when bivalent vaccine was compared to receipt of four monovalent doses. This finding is not a biologically-plausible manifestation of VAED, and it is likely due to bias or random effect. The bivalent vaccine and fourth monovalent dose were highly protective (absolute VE > 60%) against hospitalization and severe in-hospital complication. The bivalent vaccine had an rVE of 48% compared to ≥2 monovalent doses (i.e., it performed well); and the findings of negative rVE were not consistent across age groups or comparison groups (2, 3, or 4 monovalent doses), nor were they consistent across outcomes (hospitalization vs. severe complication). Most importantly, a VAED signal requires absolute VE to be negative, and rVE is not informative for VAED assessment. Absolute VE was positive for the bivalent vaccine.

The absence of a signal for vaccine-enhanced disease in this study is consistent with published VE studies showing that mRNA vaccines are protective against severe COVID-19.11–16 The findings from this study complement those of a prior cross-sectional study in the VSD.17 In that study, vaccination status was not associated with severity of illness among patients hospitalized for COVID-19. Strengths of the current study include a large and geographically diverse study population, the study period exceeding two years, inclusion of vaccine status as a time-varying covariate, and assessment of both early and late VE for monovalent or bivalent vaccine doses during different variant periods. There are also several limitations. The analysis included all licensed or authorized mRNA vaccines, and we did not stratify VE by product. The VSD population largely represents insured populations, and Medicaid recipients are underrepresented. Outcome misclassification may have occurred since COVID-19 hospitalizations were identified by ICD-10 codes without direct laboratory confirmation. This was unlikely to be an important source of bias as the positive predictive value of ICD-10 code U07.1 has been reported to be 93% for integrated delivery systems.18 Misclassification was also possible for patients who were admitted for an unrelated diagnosis but tested positive for COVID-19 during admission screening. In addition, the effects of prior SARS-CoV-2 infection could not be assessed. Finally, this analysis relied on national viral surveillance data to estimate the variant periods; however, SARS-CoV-2 variants were not known for the study participants.

In conclusion, this study found no epidemiologic evidence for the occurrence of vaccine-associated enhanced disease in any age group or variant period.
