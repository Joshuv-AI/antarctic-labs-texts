Surgical decision-making in splenic hydatid cyst management via successful total splenectomy: a case report
Authors: Andrei-Antonio Caracioni, Sebastian Happ, Natmir Mena, Thomas Simon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEchinococcosis classifies as a helminthic zoonosis affecting more than 3 million people. The outcome depends on the affected organ and implicated structures, with a lack of treatment in the long-term resulting in up to 90% of patients dying; even after treatment, the overall lifespan is reduced by 1–2 years. Postoperative death can reach as high as 2.2% if E. granulosum cysts are involved with organs other than the liver and lungs, which are considered very rare. This case aims to document this unique and personalized surgical approach for this rare presentation of splenic hydatid cysts.Case presentationWe present the case of a 43-year-old white female patient of Central European (Swiss) ethnic background who presented with an Echinococcus infection of the spleen. Following a biking accident, a chest x-ray incidentally revealed a solitary hydatid cyst in the spleen, characterized by extensive plate-like wall calcifications and measuring over 8-cm in length. The unilocular characteristics with a thick wall presence in combination with a high degree of calcification, absence of daughter cysts, positive serum markers are characteristic of a type V hydatid cyst (Gharbi classification)/CE5m (World Health Organization–Informal Working Group on Echinococcosis classification).With guidelines lacking a diagnostic and preoperative investigation pathway for splenic involvement, we conducted a full series with three dimensional reconstruction in computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging of the anatomical relationship between the hydatid cyst and surrounding parenchymatous organs.Owing to chronic inflammation, extensive adhesions, and a cyst twice the size of the spleen with close localization to the hilum, an open surgical approach was conducted. The risk of spontaneous rupture and anaphylactic shock, especially with likely prior hemorrhage, further supported this decision. To minimize recurrence and complications, total pericystectomy with splenectomy was performed. Perioperative immunization was ensured to prevent overwhelming postsplenectomy infection.After 6 weeks of preoperative albendazole therapy, total splenectomy in hydatid cyst infestation offers the advantage of reduced intraoperative risk and lower recurrence rate.A follow-up 1 month, and 4 month postintervention including clinical evaluation, imaging, and laboratory findings were inconspicuous, revealing no need for further intervention.ConclusionThus far, only consensus among experts can be retrieved when making decisions regarding the treatment of splenic involvement of hydatid cysts. This unique case of successful cyst removal via total splenectomy, with an uncomplicated postoperative course, contributes to the development of treatment guidelines for splenic hydatid cysts. The Valais region should be recognized as an additional risk factor for echinococcal infection, particularly owing to the consumption of wild berries and contaminated food or water. Given the chronic inflammation, potential adhesions, and cyst size—twice the size of the spleen—an open surgical approach was deemed necessary. The risk of hemorrhage and spontaneous rupture further justified surgical excision to minimize recurrence and complications. Adequate perioperative immunization was implemented to prevent overwhelming postsplenectomy infection. Introduction

Echinococcosis is classified as a helminthic zoonosis and is considered by the World Health Organization (WHO) as one of the 17 neglected tropical diseases, affecting more than 3 million people over all continents with Northern predominance, except for Antarctica [1–3]. The global impact is certainly not to be underestimated, with an incidence in endemic areas of up to 50 per 100,000 person-years and a resulting prevalence of up to 10% in humans and 95% in livestock. Mortality depends on the species within the Echinococcus genus. Even after treatment, the overall lifespan is reduced by 1–2 years [4, 5]. Various disease patterns can be attributed to different species [4]. Humans become so-called accidental-intermediate hosts in the lifecycle of dog-sheep-dog [6, 7].

If patients undergo surgical treatment, postoperative death can reach as high as 2.2%, and relapse may occur in 6.5% of cases after intervention [3]. E. granulosum cysts primarily affect the liver and less often the lungs. The involvement of other organs is considered very rare [7, 8].

Two forms of Echinococcus must be distinguished, as they originate from the same genus but present differently and have distinct prognoses. Echinococcus granulosus causes cystic echinococcosis, mainly in agricultural regions, with dogs as definitive hosts and livestock, such as sheep, as intermediate hosts. It forms unilocular cysts that grow slowly and generally have a favorable prognosis. In contrast, Echinococcus multilocularis causes alveolar echinococcosis, primarily in the Northern Hemisphere, with foxes as definitive hosts and rodents as intermediates. Its multilocular cysts are highly invasive, infiltrating liver tissue and metastasizing to the lungs and brain, leading to a poor prognosis if untreated. Early diagnosis and prevention are crucial for managing these infections [4, 7].

Most splenic hydatid cysts present with chronic mild abdominal pain in the left hypochondrium, but may vary from dyspepsia and constipation to being completely asymptomatic. Clinical examination can reveal a palpable mass [9, 10]. However, our patient displayed no clinical signs or symptoms of echinococcal infection of the spleen. In our case, it was an incidental finding on x-ray.

Preoperative imaging included a computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction of the abdominal parenchymatous organs to determine the precise anatomical neighboring involvement. Upon sonographic imaging, the internal pattern is highly heterogeneous, with areas where the fluid appears thickened and even sedimented—likely a past hemorrhage.

Owing to the scarcity of guidelines regarding splenic cyst management, we share our step-by-step management, from imaging to diagnostic measures, as well as intra- and postoperative management with follow-ups.

Case description and clinical findings

We present the case of a 43-year-old white female patient of Central European (Swiss) ethnic background who, during a chest x-ray following a bicycle accident, incidentally revealed a calcified cystic mass in the upper left abdominal quadrant. She denied a history of abdominal pain, dysphagia, dyspepsia, constipation, or any other symptom suspicious for abdominal hydatid cyst. Clinical examination did not reveal signs, such as palpable masses. Notable travels included Switzerland, particularly Ticino, and northern regions of Italy. The cyst was solitary, with no other organ involvement, and was an unusual finding in Switzerland, which is not yet considered a highly endemic area for Echinococcus granulosus infections. The Valais, a region in southern Switzerland, is considered a possible echinococcal risk factor zone among local farmers. The Swiss Federal Office of Public Health highlights collecting berries, and other low-hanging or bush-growing fruits as significant exposure for echinococcal infection [11].

Differential splenic diagnosis may reach from pseudocyst, hematoma, abscess, even to neoplastic changes [12, 13].

Subsequent serological investigations detected positivity for Echinococcus granulosus hydatid fluid antigen (EgHF-Ag). Other etiologies of splenic cysts have been ruled out through typical sono- and radiographic findings, such as cap-like calcifications and unilocular hypodense cysts with lack of internal septation, in combination with serological markers. Tuberculoma was ruled out through laboratory analysis.

The approximately 8-cm large, characteristically thick wall with high degree of calcification, no daughter-cysts being present, accompanied by cap-like calcification, and positive serum markers were characteristic for a hydatid cyst type V (Gharbi classification)/CE5m (World Health Organization–Informal Working Group on Echinococcosis classification [WHO-IWGE classification)]. The suspected sonographic findings were confirmed intraoperatively by the discovery of a thick, highly calcified cyst wall. Owing to the possibility of trauma-associated bleeding, we concluded that the sonographic heterogeneity originated from a past hemorrhage.

Guideline-compliant we treated the patient for probable hydatid cyst [7].

Indications for operative treatment

Preoperative discussions with multiple university hospitals focused on surgical access and resection strategies. Given the chronic inflammation caused by echinococcosis [14–16], the large cyst volume (~467.5 mL, about twice the normal splenic volume), and expected adhesions to the omental bursa, an open surgical approach was chosen. These adhesions were confirmed intraoperatively. “Cap-like calcifications” were identified both visually and by palpation.

Furthermore, the sonographic imaging revealed a highly varied internal pattern, with areas where the fluid seems to be thickened and even has sediment. Another possible explanation for this is a previous hemorrhage, although it is currently impossible to determine when it occurred. The partially sedimented mass did not appear to have internal blood flow.

Large cysts always carry the risk of spontaneous rupture due to traumatic external impact, potentially leading to anaphylactic shock [17]. Based on all these present findings, we decided on surgical removal to obtain immediate and total cure [12].

Studies, along with consensus among experts, have indicated that compared with a partial splenectomy, the complete splenectomy in hydatid cyst infestation confers the advantage of a lower recurrence rate and fewer complications. Surgical removal displayed an intraoperative mortality risk of 0.5–4% [12, 18, 19]. The postoperative mortality risk of up to 1.9% in adults, owing to postoperative overwhelming postsplenectomy infection [20–24]. Furthermore, based on retrospective studies, a total splenectomy is recommended in cases of large, central hydatid cysts of the spleen [12, 21, 23]. Moreover, cyst localization near the splenic hilum is a significant risk factor for worse outcomes and should favor total splenectomy [14, 15]. Atmatzidis et al. also advocated for splenectomy over spleen-preserving surgery in the presence of centrally or near the hilum located large cysts [12, 25].

Spillage of protoscolex-rich fluid during surgical removal of a soft tissue organ cyst may occur in up to 11.3% of cases [26].

Step-by-step case management

Owing to a cycling accident, sonographic extended focused assessment with sonography for trauma (eFAST) and thoraco-abdominal CT ruled out osseous lesions, pneumothorax, and hemothorax.

Sonographically, we observed large, heterogeneous, and complicated splenic cyst with an ultrasonic diameter of approximately 7 × 7.7 cm. The lesion exhibits distinct peripheral calcifications with shadowing (Fig. 2a, b, c). The internal pattern is highly heterogeneous, with areas where the fluid appears thickened and even sedimented (Fig. 2a, c). Therefore, a differential diagnosis includes a past hemorrhage, the timing of which cannot be currently assessed. The partially sedimented mass appears to lack internal vascularity (Fig. 2c). In addition, there is no sonographic evidence of free fluid in the peritoneal, Morrison, or Douglas spaces. The liver appears unremarkable, and both kidneys are normal. Prior to the surgical intervention, a minimum of a 6-week preoperative therapy was administered using a benzimidazole, specifically albendazole. Albendazole 400 mg (2 × daily) was chosen owing to its superior oral bioavailability among the available therapeutic agents. It necessitates laboratory monitoring of liver function at 2, 4, and 12 weeks after the therapy initiation [27]. The minimum 1-month preoperative treatment with albendazole aims to achieve a substantial to complete elimination of protoscoleces within the hydatid cyst. Consequently, the cysts are rendered so-called sterilized, potentially reducing the risk of recurrence [28]. Even though E. granulosum can suppress the inflammatory system by inhibiting macrophages [29], an anaphylactic shock may occur in the context of a cyst rupture [30]. We have also consensually agreed to focus on preventing a potential perioperative systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) and its risk reduction through perioperative medication.

An MRI was conducted for two essential reasons: first, to portray the various implicated structures for a comprehensive preoperative analysis. Second, to identify minor involvement with hydatid cysts of the liver and other visceral organs. Literature supports MRI being highly effective in diagnosing and assessing the progression of focal lesions in the spleen [31]. In other settings, it is also used to diagnose cystic changes in the abdominal region with high sensitivity and specificity [32–35].

Analogous to the CT examination, in the MRI, a cystic lesion was observed at the upper margin of the spleen, displaying typical characteristics in all sequences, with a diameter of approximately 7.5 × 7.2 cm and a cranio-caudal extension of about 8.4 cm. No diffusion abnormalities suggestive of malignancy were noted in diffusion-weighted sequences. Calcifications along the cyst wall were identified. In addition, a cap-like ventral covering of the cyst, is present with a maximum thickness of approximately 9 mm. This alteration appears hypointense to the surrounding liver parenchyma in all sequences without contrast enhancement. A cyst wall is discernible, and there is no evidence of perifocal inflammation. No contrast uptake is observed in this region. The spleen parenchyma itself exhibits a homogeneous structure. Mesentery, retroperitoneum, and peritoneum show no pathological lymphadenopathy. No masses were observed, and there was no free fluid. The employed curative strategy involved total pericystectomy through splenectomy.

We performed surgery in the supine position via a midline laparotomy. The laparoscopic approach was deemed unsuitable owing to the cyst’s size and the risk of an anaphylactic shock associated with inadvertent cyst content release into the abdominal cavity. No ascites was observed, and the overall abdominal situation, except for the enlarged spleen due to cyst involvement, appeared unremarkable. Further dissection included the opening of the omental bursa after careful adhesiolysis, with entry occurring above the transverse colon. The bursa, along with the surrounding tissue, showed signs of inflammation and adhesions. Dissection continued to expose the pancreas, revealing the splenic vein and artery. Owing to the cyst’s size, anatomical displacement occurred, with the splenic hilum now resting on the tail of the pancreas. Anatomical conditions were very dense. Further mobilization proceeded along the greater curvature of the stomach. Ligasure© (Medtronic, MN, USA) was used for sealing vessels of the short gastric arteries. We successfully removed the spleen, along with the cyst, after release from the splenic ligaments. Following thorough irrigation, dry conditions were observed. A drainage tube was inserted, followed by the placement of an omental flap to fill the tissue defect in the former splenic site and prevent serous fluid accumulation. Abdominal closure was performed using small-bites and small-stitch techniques, culminating in cutaneous closure via intracutaneous sutures.

Histopathological report

The splenic specimen weighed 363 g and measured 17.5 × 11 × 4.5 cm. The splenic parenchyma appeared within normal limits. A cyst was characterized by a fibrotic wall, mostly devoid of epithelium, but in some areas, covered by a nonkeratinizing squamous epithelium. Within the cyst, focal areas of blood and fibrin deposits were observed, confirming the hypothesis of possible trauma-induced hemorrhage. The fibrotic cyst wall also exhibited focal chronic inflammation and numerous calcifications. Periodic Acid–Schiff (PAS) staining showed no evidence of remaining parasites, granulomatous, or giant cell reactions. The adjacent splenic parenchyma was within normal limits, and the hilar lymph nodes were unremarkable.

We assumed that the albendazole pretreatment had effectively addressed all viable forms of echinococcosis. The purpose of this pretreatment was to result in a sterile cyst.

Postoperative management/treatment

A 24-hour surveillance period in the intensive care unit passed without complications, and thromboprophylaxis was first initiated 6 hours postintervention. On the first postoperative day, the drainage was removed due to minimal serous discharge. Albendazole 400 mg (twice daily) was continued for 4 more weeks postoperatively to reduce the risk of recurrence [7].

The patient experienced an uncomplicated recovery and was discharged home on the third postoperative day, in good general condition and free of complaints.

Follow-up and outcomes

A follow-up 1 month, and 4 month postintervention including clinical evaluation, imaging, and laboratory findings were inconspicuous, revealing no need for further intervention.

Discussion

Optimal management of splenic hydatid cysts requires a balance between surgical risk and long-term disease control, necessitating an individualized, multidisciplinary approach.

The minimally invasive percutaneous treatment using the puncture, aspiration, injection, and re-aspiration principle (PAIR) was rejected owing to the risks of anaphylactic shock, local spread leading to secondary echinococcosis, and complications from the size of the cyst and its calcifications. The risk of infection or recurrence of echinococcosis is to be considered in up to 3% of cases [7, 36, 37].

Even though complex liver cysts have been removed laparoscopically [38], those insights cannot be extrapolated to splenic involvement, as cyst-organ-size-ratio vary. Case series and single reports on isolated primary splenic hydatid cysts have generally favored total splenectomy for large cysts, with diameters of up to 16 cm [14]. Partial splenectomy is a realistic approach when the hydatid cyst is confined to one pole or a peripheral location within the spleen [39]. The WHO Echinococcosis Working Group guidelines indicate that while more radical interventions carry a higher surgical risk, they are associated with a lower relapse rate. Treatment decisions should be tailored to each individual patient [40].

These considerations were discussed with multiple university centers before proceeding. Despite the preoperative WHO classification as a CE5m cyst, we performed an open total pericystectomy via complete splenectomy.

The final decision was made on the basis of several factors: adhesion from chronic echinococcosis [14–16], possible intra-cystic bleeding, the large cyst size with rupture risk from trauma, and the patient’s strong preference for surgery over long-term antibiotic therapy or watchful waiting. We consulted multiple university centers before proceeding.

Owing to the limited availability of antiparasitic drugs, albendazole was ordered from abroad and administered in compliance with guidelines at a dose of 10–15 mg/kg/day, divided into two doses, resulting in 400 mg twice daily [7]. A meta-analysis from 2018 highlighted the effectiveness of albendazole as a first-line treatment [41].

Moreover, a multicenter consensus is needed, as current guidelines lack clear recommendations for large splenic cysts with the characteristic findings presented in this case [40].

After the removal of the spleen, the crucial role in filtering blood and immune response, with particular regard to encapsulated bacteria, is lost. Splenectomy strictly includes pre- or postoperative management with vaccinations protecting against Streptococcus pneumoniae (13-valent conjugate), Haemophilus influenzae type b, and Neisseria meningitidis. A follow-up is required 2 months later for additional doses targeting Streptococcus pneumoniae (polysaccharide) and Neisseria meningitidis. For long-term protection, repeat vaccinations for Streptococcus pneumoniae and Neisseria meningitidis every 5 years, with annual vaccinations for influenza. No further Haemophilus influenzae type b vaccinations are needed. Adhering to postsplenectomy protocols with vaccinations helped us reduce the risk of overwhelming postsplenectomy infection (OPSI) [42–44].

To our knowledge, only limited cases have been described in recent literature presenting a previously asymptomatic significant splenic hydatid cyst and our patient’s characteristic findings. We needed to extrapolate information from previous case reports and expert consensus, as guidelines focused primarily on hepatic and pulmonary E. granulosus involvement [8, 14, 45, 46]. The intention to pursue a curative approach for the cyst necessitated precise CT and MRI diagnostic imaging methods, coupled with interdisciplinary collaboration among gastroenterology, radiology, and surgery. This approach considers and involves a thorough discussion of the patient’s case, aiming to eradicate the cyst entirely.

Conclusions

An essential advancement would be a prospective analysis of splenic echinococcosis, comparing and stratifying median laparotomy versus minimally invasive approaches on the basis of cyst size, findings, and surgical urgency. In addition, a precise pre- and postoperative duration and antiparasitic medication protocol are crucial for future cases with histopathological results. The necessity of larger randomized studies and the pooling of scientific research in a contemporary medical setting is indispensable, especially considering that a non-negligible portion of evidence-based medicine still relies on literature ranging from the early 2000s back to the 1980s.
