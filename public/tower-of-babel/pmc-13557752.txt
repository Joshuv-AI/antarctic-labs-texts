Enhancing Precision in Pelvic Tumor Reconstruction: Early Experience and Iterative Design of Custom 3D-Printed Implants
Authors: Jan Lesensky, Michal Benes, Zdenek Cejka, Ana C. Belzarena
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Pelvic tumor resections with custom 3D-printed implant reconstruction have become the benchmark in limb-salvage surgery. However, evidence regarding reconstructive precision is limited, and adaptive prosthetic design is rarely discussed. This study reports our early experience, highlighting iterative design modifications and lessons learned to optimize fit and accuracy. We also evaluated implant positioning comparing preoperative plans with postoperative imaging.Methods:We retrospectively reviewed seven patients with primary bone malignancy who underwent periacetabular resection and reconstruction with custom 3D-printed implants. Patient-specific plans and postoperative imaging were analyzed. Implant design was iteratively refined based on early intraoperative challenges, with modifications implemented in later cases. Outcomes were assessed descriptively, and statistical analyses were conducted to compare implant version, acetabular version, and implant frame overlap.Results:Early challenges, including open-book pelvic migration, joint instability, and implant malpositioning, prompted design modifications, such as modular implant splitting, angle-stable locking screws, novel constrained cup, and clamp-like pubic fixation plates, which improved fit and stability. Minor discrepancies between planned and actual implant positions were observed overall, with median differences in implant version, acetabular version, and implant frame overlap of 1.6° (IQR 1.2-2.4), 2.7° (IQR 2.4-6.3), and 87.0% (IQR 80.0-88.0), respectively. Comparisons between initial and modified implants demonstrated markedly improved positioning, and learning curves showed progressive improvements in placement and intraoperative performance.Conclusion:Patient-specific instruments combined with iterative prosthetic design enable precise pelvic reconstructions and enhance short-term results. Early experience demonstrates how adaptive modifications and surgical learning curves can address challenges, laying the foundation for improved long-term functional outcomes.

The pelvis is a frequent site of neoplasms, comprising approximately 10 to 15% of all newly diagnosed primary malignant bone tumors.1,2 Historically, most pelvic tumors were treated with external hemipelvectomy, leaving a poor functional and psychological outcome.3 Recently, the availability of advanced imaging modalities, adoption of modern systemic and local therapy, improved surgical techniques, and in particular the development of reconstructive options made limb-sparing procedures the preferred strategy.4 Hand in hand with patients' prolonged life expectancy because of evolving treatment possibilities, the durability and functional outcomes of pelvic reconstructions stand as one of the main contemporary challenges in oncology orthopaedics.5

Pelvic reconstruction alternatives after resection of the periacetabular segment include resection without reconstruction; arthrodesis; allograft reconstruction; and endoprosthetic replacement with saddle prosthesis, pedestal prosthesis, or a custom-made implant.6 In recent years, there has been an increasing trend toward reconstructing the pelvic defects because restoration of the pelvic anatomy is essential to re-establish pelvic ring stability, preserve function, and optimize gait.5 Therefore, 3D-printed custom-made implants are being increasingly used to restore the native pelvic anatomy, as has already been successfully done for other anatomical regions.7 To improve the outcomes, several tools, such as CT-assisted navigation, optical navigation, and patient-specific cutting guides and instruments, are available.8,9 Although strong evidence is limited, custom 3D cutting guides are believed to have the same precision as navigation systems but with shorter surgical time and potentially lower cost.8

While previous clinical studies assessed the accuracy of implant positioning using intraoperative navigation systems, precision of the custom-made cutting-guide aids has not been investigated10 and continues to be an area where additional research is needed.11 Furthermore, advances in prosthetic design is one of the pieces to the puzzle for extended implant survivorship.12,13 Unfortunately, the current literature does not provide detailed guidance on how to enhance implant modeling based on direct clinical experience. Therefore, the aim of this study was to evaluate the accuracy of implant positioning with the assistance of custom-made cutting guides between preoperative plans and postoperative in vivo conditions, in patients undergoing 3D-printed pelvic reconstruction after tumor resection. In addition, the study sought to determine whether iterative improvements in implant design and accumulated surgical experience contributed to increased precision in implant placement.

Methods

Patient Cohort

A chart review was conducted on patients who underwent pelvic reconstruction in our institution from January 2019 to June 2025. We identified 49 patients diagnosed with a primary pelvic malignant bone tumor, of whom 27 underwent pelvic tumor resection, and of those, seven had reconstructions with custom-made 3D-printed endoprostheses performed at a single orthopaedic oncology reference center. Only patients in whom the acetabular segment was reconstructed (i.e., type II according to Enneking and Dunham,14 1978, with variable extension into segments I and III) were included in the study. Seven patients were identified. Three patients were women and four men, with a mean age of 43 years (range, 24-70). Detailed patient and tumor characteristics are described in Table 1. All surgical procedures were performed by a team led by a fellowship-trained orthopaedic oncologist. Study approval was granted by the local institutional review board.

Personalized Implant Planning and Fabrication

Once the patient was diagnosed with a pelvic tumor indicated for resection, individual implant designing process began (Figure 1). Initially, high-resolution CT scans with 1-mm slices and MRI were fused to create an interactive 3D model through a software program (Materialise Mimics Medical version 26.0.576) to aid in planning the resection. A preliminary software model was then created and underwent further rigorous evaluation by a multidisciplinary team for the reconstructive feasibility, biomechanical integrity, and surgical strategy. In some cases, a plastic dummy model was fabricated to help with planning and visualization (Figure 1B). The implant was designed with the intent of a 10-mm margin from the tumor. Upon finalizing the prosthetic design, definitive implant production took place. The final implant was manufactured within a 6-week time frame, using advanced additive manufacturing techniques. The main implant scaffold was produced with laser sintering of titanium alloy Ti6Al4V, and its surface was coated with diamond-like carbon (DLC). The contact surfaces interfacing with the surrounding bone were engineered with a trabecular titanium layer to promote osseointegration and, hence, support stable fixation. In addition, screw and plate fixation implants were designed based on individual tumor extent. The inlay of the acetabular articulating surface was made of polyetheretherketone (PEEK) polymer. A constrained hip mechanism was used in all cases. Along with the implant, patient-specific cutting guides were fabricated to ensure precise resection and implant fit. The entire process of implant designing and manufacturing was provided by the same company and one engineer in all cases (ProSpon Company, Czech Republic).

The implant design was progressively refined based on insights gained from surgical experience and clinical follow-up because certain limitations became evident over time. The first three implants served as the baseline for subsequent improvements, and the impact of these design modifications was assessed by comparing the original (initial three patients) and refined (subsequent four patients) implants across the following outcomes: implant version, acetabular version, and implant frame overlap, defined as the subtraction of the original plan and the final postoperative image.

Surgical Technique

Surgery was performed under general anesthesia with the patient in a lateral decubitus position. A single-incision lateral “question-mark approach,” staring on the iliac crest and toward the anterosuperior iliac spine and extending distally to the lateral proximal thigh, was implemented in all cases (Figure 1E). Soft-tissue dissection respected the extent of the tumor to achieve negative oncologic margins. Once the bone was sufficiently exposed around the tumor, the patient-specific cutting guides (PSCGs) were mounted to perform precise osteotomies. The depth of the osteotomies was assessed by the length of the blade with respect to the edge of the PSCG. In all cases, an extra-articular resection of the hip joint was performed, and the femoral head and neck were resected en bloc with the pelvic tumor mass. The gluteal muscles were never detached from the femur but were partially resected off the pelvic bone as dictated by the extent of the tumor. The custom-made implant was fitted into the defect and secured following the preoperative plan. Subsequently, the femoral stem was introduced, and the implant head was reduced into the acetabular constrained liner. Dissolvable calcium phosphate beads with vancomycin were placed around the massive implant as a local antibiotic carrier to maintain higher local concentrations in the early postoperative period. Finally, soft-tissue reconstruction was performed with the remaining postresection soft tissues, followed by wound closure. Patients were kept bedridden for 3 to 5 days after the surgery. Then physical and occupational therapies were initiated. After discharge, the patients were periodically followed up. Initial control CT scan was obtained 6 to 10 weeks after the surgery with consecutive imaging as required by the oncological follow-up protocol.

Comparative Measurements

For each case, preoperative plans and postoperative CT scans were uploaded into a 3D image segmentation software (Materialise Mimics Medical version 26.0.576). Subsequently, the preoperative plan was overlapped with the actual postoperative model. The images were perfectly aligned using surface matching, with the unaffected bony landmarks serving as a merging landmark reference. The overlapped model underwent semiautomatic calculation of the implant position and orientation between the planned and final postoperative models. For comparative measurements, three parameters were defined (Figure 2). First, we measured the implant version, which was defined as the deviation of the implant with respect to the sagittal plane and center of the sacrum (S2/S3 transverse line). Second, the acetabular version was measured, which was represented as the deviation from the planned acetabular plane. Finally, the implant frame overlap, defined as the subtraction of the preoperative plan created by the software and the final postoperative image without the fixation plates and screws, was calculated. Additional intraoperative recorded variables included estimated blood loss and surgical time. Furthermore, functional outcomes were evaluated using the Musculoskeletal Tumor Score (MSTS) and Harris Hip Score (HHS).

Statistical Analysis

The normal distribution of the data was assessed with the Shapiro-Wilk test. Owing to the non-normal distribution of our data, continuous variables are presented as median and interquartile ranges (IQRs). Given the limited sample size, groups were compared using Mann-Whitney U test. Learning curves were constructed with position measurements, estimated blood loss, and surgical time as variables. The level of statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. The statistical analysis was conducted using Prism v. 10.4.0 (GraphPad Software, USA).

Results

Design Improvement

During the first three reconstructive procedures, we identified four critical areas for design improvement (Figure 3). First was intraoperative open-book migration of the pelvis, which was a logical consequence of disrupting the pelvic ring in the lateral decubitus position. This required increased force to reduce the bones toward the implant, which often resulted in poor purchase of the screws. Also securing and fixing the posterior segment first often resulted in poor fit in the anterior segment, which was more difficult to correct because of a smaller area for adjustment. This shortcoming was solved by splitting the implant into two parts that are independently fixed to the bone and later connected. The added anatomic plates allowed the precise securing of the pubic bone, and the main implant was secured to the posterior pelvic segment. Subsequent reconnection of both implant parts was done with conical screws. This way the positional forces were moved from the “bone-to-metal” interface to “metal-to-metal” interface after all the screws were secured in the bone with minimal force.

Second, during follow-up, we encountered proximal migration of the implant and resultant rotational instability of the pelvis. Therefore, to achieve increased resistance to translational forces, dynamic angle-stable locking screws were added, resulting in stronger implant stability that still allowed for compression on the implant-bone interface.

One case of hip instability was observed, resulting from failure of the femoral head locking mechanism. The patient did not recall any incident that might have caused the failure and has continued to walk independently. The original constrain mechanism seemed to be weak in restricting the forces of the artificial hip joint. Thus, a novel constrained cup design was developed, moving the more robust locking mechanism behind the head and securing it with a solid screw.

Finally, the inferior pubic screws attaching the implant to the remaining pubic bone were insufficient because the bone stock in this area is usually limited. We enhanced this fixation with a clamp-like anatomic plate design, which performed excellent in terms of implant fixation and also serving as an additional reduction tool for the aforementioned intraoperative open-book migration problem.

None of the above-listed problems occurred in any of the subsequent cases using the improved design. These innovations in the prosthetic design led to significantly superior implant positioning, which reached statistical significance in implant version (P = 0.029), acetabular version (P = 0.024), and implant frame overlap (P = 0.029) (Table 2).

Cutting Guides and Margins

Final pathology reports demonstrated negative surgical margins in all but one case (Table 1). In addition, in one patient, a close soft-tissue margin of 1 mm was observed. The one case of positive bony margin was identified in a patient with grade 3 chondrosarcoma who also had the longest interval from indication to surgery (11 weeks), presumably allowing the tumor to overgrow the planned cutting guide‑assisted resections. Across the cohort, the interval from indication to surgery decreased from a median of 10 weeks (IQR 8-11) in the initial three cases to 7 weeks (IQR 6-8) after the implementation the improved prosthetic design, although this did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.200). At the time of writing this article, 1 patient had died of disease, two patients had no evidence of disease, and four were alive with metastatic disease. No patient developed local recurrence, including those with a positive margin and 1-mm margin (Table 1).

Learning Curves

With the gained experience, all position measurements demonstrated a consistent improvement (i.e., decrease in implant version and acetabular version and increase in implant frame overlap) (Figure 4, A–C). Similarly, estimated blood loss featured a decreasing trend across the seven cases (Figure 4D). Surgical duration remained relatively constant throughout all procedures, suggesting that transition from monolithic implant design to modular design did not increase the surgical time (Figure 4E).

Implant Positioning

In all cases, a small discrepancy between the preoperative plan and postoperative CT scans was noted (Figure 2). Implant version differed with a median of 1.6° (IQR 1.2-2.4). The median difference in acetabular version was 2.7° (IQR 2.4-6.3). Finally, the planned model's and actual implant's frame featured a median overlap of 87% (IQR 80.0-88.0). All results of the position analysis are presented in Table 1.

Functional Outcomes

Functional outcomes were recorded in all but one patient who died of the disease before the assessment during final follow-up. Median MSTS was 21 (IQR 19.8-21.5) without any significant difference (P = 0.700) between the patients who received old design (21; IQR 19-21) compared with the novel implant group (21; IQR 20-23). Median HSS was 82.2% (IQR 73.8-86.1). Similarly, there was no statistically significant difference (P > 0.999) between the old (83.4%; IQR 58.3-85.6) and improved (81.0%; IQR 79.0-87.7) design groups. No patients had postoperative surgical site infections. All patients were independent ambulators, and all required at least 1 crutch for longer walks.

Discussion

Surgical interventions involving the pelvis are particularly demanding because of its intricate and irregular bony architecture, presence of multiple muscular attachments, and close proximity of critical neurovascular structures and visceral organs.15 This makes the classic free-hand pelvic limb-sparing surgery more prone to achieving positive margins.16 Therefore, resections with custom-made cutting guides or intraoperative navigation, based on thorough preoperative planning, and subsequent reconstructions with individual 3D-printed implants are becoming the leading treatment option. Precise osteotomies and implant positioning are paramount in achieving good long-term outcomes. Based on our results, patient-specific instruments combined with improved design of the prosthesis yield more precise implant positioning, as statistically significant improvements in the studied parameters were found compared with the original prosthetic design.

Precise implant placement is deemed crucial because malpositioning can compromise the biomechanical function and reconstruction longevity, emphasizing the importance of both meticulous preoperative planning and instrument-guided intraoperative execution. The accuracy of custom-made implant positioning has been previously assessed in both musculoskeletal tumor and revision arthroplasty surgery. Overall, previous studies have found a favorable concordance between the preoperative plan and the final implant position.10 Moreover, the aid of intraoperative navigation seemed to be beneficial, although this was not statistically significant.10 The precision of custom-made cutting guides has mostly been tested only in cadaver studies, showcasing the need for thorough understanding and recognition of the shape of the targeted bone because precision is highly dependent on osseous homogeneity and curvature.17 Our findings demonstrated an overall satisfactory level of precision in implant positioning. Moreover, the combination with design improvements resulted in markedly enhanced accuracy. However, direct comparison of our results with previous studies is limited by differences in methodology and assessed parameters. In particular, the methodology established in this study—which has not been previously reported—precludes the use of standardized thresholds for malpositioning because none currently exist for comparable analyses.

The key findings of this study highlight the iterative refinement of the prosthetic design, guided by observations from early surgical outcomes. Initial procedures demonstrated that intraoperative pelvic open-book displacement compromised the accuracy of implant-to-bone reduction. Consequently, the design evolved from a monolithic construct to a modular configuration featuring independent fixation plates for the anterior pelvic segment. This modification allowed greater control during implant placement and reduced intraoperative pelvic tension, while preserving the inherent biomechanical advantages of 3D-printed prostheses—namely, physiological stress distribution and optimal prosthesis-to-host bone conformity.5 Proximal migration and rotational instability prompted the addition of dynamic angle-stable locking screws, which markedly enhanced pelvic stability while still allowing compression on the implant-bone interface. Locking screw technology is widely known to improve fixation strength.18 Hip instability observed in one case—attributable to failure of the femoral implant locking mechanism observed incidentally on follow-up radiograph—prompted the development of a novel constrained cup design. Incorporating a robust screw, which allowed tightening of the acetabular cup to secure the femoral head and thus provided superior joint stability, is consistent with prior innovations in constrained acetabular implants for complex hip reconstructions.19 In addition, loosening of the inferior pubic screws was mitigated by introducing clamp-like anatomic plates, which offered improved fixation and facilitated reduction. Notably, as demonstrated along the learning curve, dividing the implant into modular components did not increase surgical time compared with the initial monolithic cases. This design evolution therefore reflects a successful application of implant modularity principles.

Patient-specific cutting guides assist surgeons in achieving safe margins and optimizing osteotomies for fitting custom 3D-printed implants in complex tumor resections. In our initial cases, the guides were relatively rudimentary and consisted of separate templates for each osteotomy, referenced to fixed anatomic landmarks such as the sacroiliac joint or pubic symphysis. With iterative experience, the guides evolved into integrated, multiplanar constructs that reproduced the exact geometry of the planned implant, allowing simultaneous execution of all osteotomies with predefined angles and improved control of the saw trajectory. In the most recent cases, depth control was further enhanced by calibrating the visible length of the saw blade relative to the guide, providing a reproducible intraoperative reference for osteotomy completion. This progressive refinement improved the accuracy of bone cuts, facilitated implant seating, and reduced the need for intraoperative adjustments. Beyond the pelvis, such patient-specific instrumentation has broader potential applications in complex oncologic resections, including margin optimization, protection of critical structures, and standardization of technically demanding multiplanar osteotomies, thereby contributing to both oncologic safety and reconstructive precision. Previous studies have demonstrated that these tools increase the rate of negative oncologic margins and may contribute to improved disease-free survival.5,8,20 Individualized implants with patient-specific instrumentation are particularly valuable in challenging scenarios, such as in pediatric patients, where no off-the-shelf implants are available.20 However, only a limited number of reports focus specifically on the instruments themselves.5,20-22 A key limitation of this approach in pelvic tumor surgery is the substantial up-front planning and design required to produce both the custom prosthesis and the corresponding instruments, as well as the associated financial burden on healthcare systems.21 Nevertheless, with the growing accessibility of additive manufacturing, patient-specific implants and instruments have become increasingly feasible and may ultimately be more cost-effective and widely available than intraoperative CT navigation.23 Our early experience emphasizes the importance of minimizing the interval between the surgical indication for a patient-specific instrument-assisted resection and the operation itself. This is illustrated by the single case in which a positive margin occurred in a patient with a high-grade tumor who did not receive neoadjuvant therapy; the 11-week interval likely allowed tumor progression beyond the planned resection.

The choice of pelvic reconstruction depends on factors such as patient activity level, tumor extent, and the balance between durability and complication risk, with newer technologies offering the potential for improved outcomes in complex oncologic cases. External hemipelvectomies are currently reserved for a minority of patients with otherwise unresectable tumors.24 Iliofemoral and ischiofemoral arthrodeses provide durable solutions with generally favorable outcomes but are associated with limb shortening, gait impairment, and high nonunion rates.24,25 However, the bony defect after tumor resection often renders fusion impossible. Pseudoarthroses represent less complex, though functionally limited, alternatives for less active patients.24 Reconstruction using saddle or pedestal prostheses and massive pelvic allografts is well established but often limited by high complication rates, including dislocation, infection, or implant failure due to loosening.5,24 By contrast, custom-designed prostheses, surgical navigation tools, and novel biomaterials that promote bone ingrowth currently occupy a central role in modern orthopaedic oncology, offering potentially more durable reconstructions with complication profiles comparable with standard approaches.5

Our findings suggest improved implant positioning with increasing experience in prosthetic design, accompanied by incremental improvements in the other outcomes analyzed. The process of implant design and selection of the reconstructive approach involves close collaboration among multiple specialties within the sarcoma team, engineers, and company coordinators. It has been recognized that centralization of sarcoma care in tertiary centers may enhance the quality of treatment, ensuring patients receive comprehensive, coordinated management tailored to their complex needs.26 The precision observed in this early series, although preliminary, highlights the potential benefits of performing such technically demanding procedures in specialized centers experienced with 3D-printed implants.

This study is not free of limitations. Its primary focus was on comparing implant positioning between preoperative plans and postoperative outcomes, with additional emphasis on the impact of iterative prosthetic design refinements. As a result, the analysis was centered on quantitative accuracy rather than functional or patient-reported outcomes. Moreover, the proposed design improvements were derived from short-term follow-up, and further challenges may emerge with longer observation. Despite the small sample size, this early experience provides meaningful insight into the precision achievable with patient-specific cutting guides and the translational value of adaptive implant design. These findings lay the groundwork for future multicenter studies with larger cohorts and long-term follow-up to validate their functional and oncologic implications.

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that, despite minor deviations between preoperative plans and final postoperative positioning, the integration of patient-specific surgical guides and prosthetic designs enhances reconstructive precision and short-term stability in pelvic tumor surgery. These iterative refinements not only can improve surgical comfort, immediate implant fit, and alignment but may also contribute to superior long-term functional outcomes and implant longevity. The evolution from early intraoperative challenges to optimized design solutions highlights the critical role of continuous feedback and experience-driven innovation in complex pelvic reconstructions. Although these early results are encouraging, validation in larger cohorts with extended follow-up is necessary to confirm their durability and generalizability. Overall, this study underscores the growing value of personalized, technology-assisted approaches in advancing limb-salvage surgery and improving oncologic reconstruction outcomes.
