Single-molecule insights into SAM-I riboswitch ligand binding using scaled magnetic force spectroscopy with comparison to conventional biophysical methods
Authors: Zhen Wang, Henning Labuhn, Rémi Moulinas, David Salthouse, Akifumi Nakamura, Yoshihiro Akahori, Airi Kon, Shohei Kawasaki, Adeline Feri, Pascale Beurdeley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBiophysical methods are widely used for drug discovery, with surface plasmon resonance (SPR) and isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) being amongst the most used techniques. However, these types of bulk or ensemble methods do not provide single-molecule resolution or structural and mechanistic insights, so that researchers are forced to rely upon a battery of complementary techniques. Here, we used a scaled magnetic force spectroscopy (MFS) instrument system capable of analysing hundreds of single molecules in parallel and obtaining dynamic conformational insights. Taking the S-adenosylmethionine-I (SAM-I) riboswitch as a model system, we demonstrated that MFS can be used to characterize the RNA structure in conjunction with the published crystal structures. Next, we compared the performance of MFS to SPR and ITC for measuring binding affinities of two ligands with highly different affinities, SAM and SAH in both high and low magnesium concentrations. MFS gave similar binding affinities to SPR and ITC but was more closely aligned to ITC. We then demonstrated the power of MFS to study the molecular dynamics of SAM-I and the mechanisms of SAM and S-adenosylhomocysteine (SAH) binding. By subjecting the RNA to constant forces for extended periods, we were able to observe that the conformation dynamics of SAM-I are impacted differently by SAM and SAH binding, pointing to different stabilization mechanisms between the two ligands.

Introduction

Riboswitches are structured, non-coding RNA elements typically located in the 5′ untranslated regions of bacterial messenger RNAs, where they serve as key regulators of gene expression [1,2]. These RNA sensors respond directly to specific small molecules or ions, undergoing conformational changes that modulate the expression of downstream genes. Each riboswitch consists of two, often overlapping, modular domains: an aptamer, which acts as the sensor by binding the ligand, and an expression platform (EP), which translates the aptamer’s structural state into a transcriptional or translational response. With the increase in the antibiotic resistance and multiple drug resistance, riboswitches present novel antibacterial drug targets [3–5]. Small molecules mimicking the active riboswitch ligand have been developed to inhibit bacterial growth, such as Ribocil for the flavin mononucleotide (FMN) riboswitch [6,7]. Therefore, understanding the structure of riboswitches and the mechanisms by which ligand binding affects their activity is important for drug discovery and design.

SAM riboswitches, comprising at least six distinct families, regulate the biosynthesis and transport of methionine, cysteine and S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) [8,9]. Among these, the SAM-I riboswitch family controls methionine biosynthesis through a negative feedback mechanism that represses gene expression in response to elevated intracellular SAM levels [10]. The aptamer domain includes a relatively stable region that forms the ligand-binding site and a more flexible segment that competes with the EP for a shared RNA strand. In the absence of SAM, the shared strand is captured by the EP, and the aptamer domain adopts an antiterminator conformation, allowing transcription of the downstream gene. Upon SAM binding, the aptamer sequesters the shared strand, enabling the remaining portion of the EP to fold into a terminator helix, thereby halting transcription [1,10,11].

Structurally, the ligand-bound SAM-I aptamer comprises four helices (P1–P4) connected by unpaired regions [12,13]. Helices P2, P3 and P4 form consecutive hairpin structures, while P1 arises from long-range base pairing of segments at the ends of the aptamer (Figure 1(A)) [11,14,15]. Crystallographic studies reveal that the ligand-bound aptamer adopts a compact architecture stabilized by two sets of helical stacks between P1–P4 and P2–P3. A characteristic kink-turn (K-turn) motif in the P2 stem facilitates a pseudoknot formation between the P2 stem-loop and the single-stranded J3/4 junction [12,13,16]. The SAM binding site is a pocket formed by the minor grooves of P1 and P3, with ligand binding inducing a compaction that brings these helices into close proximity [14,17–22]. Magnesium ions are now recognized as essential for SAM binding and are believed to act by pre-organizing the aptamer into a conformation that includes the K-turn and pseudoknot, the stacking of stems P2 and P3, and close juxtaposition of P1 and P3 (Figure 1(B)) [15,16,18,23]. To quantify binding affinities between biomolecules, several biophysical techniques are commonly employed. Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) remains a key method for probing molecular interactions, as it directly measures the heat change upon binding without the need for labelling [24]. Surface plasmon resonance (SPR) has emerged as a powerful alternative [25] that detects binding events through changes in the refractive index at a functionalized gold surface, where the target molecule is immobilized. Although these conventional methods offer many strengths, they do not inherently reveal information about target structure. However, orthogonal techniques such as magnetic force spectroscopy (MFS) can provide additional insights. MFS is a single-molecule technique in which macromolecules of interest (RNA in the present study) are tethered between a surface and a paramagnetic bead. A controllable magnetic force is applied, while molecular extension is measured by tracking the vertical bead position (Figure 2(A)). Various force protocols can then be implemented, including ramped, stepped or constant-force protocols (Figure 2(B)). In force ramp experiments, the molecules are subjected to cycles of gradually increasing, then decreasing force during which unfolding and refolding can be detected by sudden changes in vertical bead position. The effect of ligands on the unfolding and folding forces allows their impact on the stability of the RNA structures to be measured. In stepped force experiments, the molecules are subjected for a fixed amount of time at a constant force which is then increased in a stepwise manner. This allows the transition between different folded-states to be observed, and the equilibrium force at which the RNA spends equal time in each state can be determined for each transition. Finally, in constant-force experiments, the RNA is subjected to an equilibrium force for an extended period, to allow the impact of ligand binding on folding and unfolding dynamics to be explored. In the present study, we investigated the binding of SAM to the SAM-I riboswitch, compared with a low-affinity binder (S-adenosylhomocysteine, SAH) and a non-binder (Ribocil-C) under two different magnesium concentrations. We initially characterized the SAM-I riboswitch aptamer behaviour using MFS and compared MFS to ITC and SPR methods to demonstrate that MFS is an effective technique for assessing binding affinities. We then used a range of MFS approaches for a deeper investigation of the molecular dynamics of the apo- and ligand-bound riboswitch. Our results confirm that SAM binding stabilizes the SAM-I structure by inducing a long-lifetime folded-state. We also demonstrate that SAH binding to the SAM-I RNA structure in high magnesium stabilizes the SAM-I aptamer through a different mechanism and does not induce the long-lifetime stable folded-state observed with SAM binding.

Materials and methods

Preparation of SAM and SAH

SAM (S-(5’-adenosyl)-l-methionine chloride (hydrochloride)), SAH (S-(5’-adenosyl)-l-homocysteine) and Ribocil-C were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (catalogue numbers A7007, A9384 and SML1685). SAM solution used for MFS was purchased from NEB (catalogue number B9003).

Preparation of SAM-I riboswitch RNAs

pEX-A2J2 plasmids, containing Bacillus subtilis yitJ SAM-I riboswitch sequences with additional bases (underlined below) flanked by the T7 polymerase promoter sequence at 5’-end and a BsaI recognition site at 3’-end, were prepared at Eurofins Genomics (Japan) and amplified by Escherichia coli DH5α competent cells followed by purification with NucleoBond Xtra Maxi EF (Takara Bio, Japan, catalogue number 740424.50).

The plasmids were linearized by BsaI followed by treatment of DNase and ethanol precipitation. The crude products were directly used for in-vitro transcription using T7 RiboMAX Express Large Scale RNA Production System (Promega). The obtained RNAs were extracted by Monarch RNA Cleanup Kit (NEB) and further purified on NAP-25 (Cytiva) and Amicon Ultra (10 kDa, Merck Millipore) with nuclease-free water. The concentration and purity of the products were confirmed by UV/vis spectrophotometry (Lunatic, Unchained Labs) and automated electrophoresis (TapeStation, Agilent). The RNA sequences in this study are summarized in Table S1.

Preparation of 5’-biotinylated SAM-I riboswitch RNAs

Stable version

The linearized stable SAM-I riboswitch plasmid described above was used for in vitro transcription using MEGAshortscript T7 Transcription Kit (Invitrogen) with the following components: 7.5 mM ATP, 7.5 mM CTP, 7.5 mM UTP, 3 mM GTP and 4.5 mM 5’-biotin-GMP (Trilink Biotechnologies). The resulting solution was serially washed with phenol/chloroform/isoamyl alcohol (125:24:1 v/v/v) and chloroform/isoamyl alcohol (24:1 v/v). Transcribed RNA in the final aqueous phase was precipitated by ethanol and further purified on NAP-25 and Amicon Ultra (10 kDa) with nuclease-free water. The concentration and purity of the purified RNA was assessed by spectrophotometry (Lunatic, Unchained Labs) and microfluidic capillary electrophoretic separation (LabChip, Revvity).

Native version

The linearized native SAM-I riboswitch plasmid described above was used for in-vitro transcription using MEGAshortscript T7 Transcription Kit (Invitrogen) with the following components: 7.5 mM ATP, 7.5 mM CTP, 7.5 mM UTP, 3 mM GTP and 4.5 mM 5’-biotin-GMP (Trilink Biotechnologies). The resulting RNA was extracted by Monarch RNA Cleanup Kit (NEB), further purified using denaturing 6% PAGE with 1× TBE buffer and finally desalted using Microspin G-25 (Cytiva). The concentration and purity of the purified RNA was assessed by UV/vis spectrophotometry (Lunatic, Unchained Labs) and automated electrophoresis (TapeStation, Agilent).

Isothermal titration calorimetry studies of SAM-I riboswitch RNAs and SAM/SAH

RNA samples in low Mg2+ or high Mg2+ buffer (50 mM HEPES-KOH pH 7.5, 100 mM KCl, 1 mM or 10 mM MgCl2, 1% DMSO) were prepared by heating at 80°C for 3 min in the presence of KCl followed by snap-cold at 0°C for at least 5 min and then mixed with HEPES-KOH, MgCl2 and DMSO. The RNAs (10 µM) were set in the sample cell and SAM or SAH (200 µM in the same buffer) were added to the syringe of a MicroCal PEAQ-ITC Automated ITC instrument (Malvern Panalytical). The titration was carried out at a reference power of 5 µcal/s and temperature of 25°C with 19 injections at an injection rate of 2 µL per 4 s duration at 150 s intervals. Data collected were fit to a single-site binding model after subtracting control data without RNA.

Surface plasmon resonance studies of SAM-I riboswitch RNAs and SAM/SAH

SPR experiments were performed using a Biacore T200 (Cytiva) instrument. Neutravidin (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was immobilized on a Sensor Chip CM5 (Cytiva) by the amine coupling method. Subsequently, 5’-terminus biotinylated SAM-I riboswitch RNA was captured. RNA was heated at 95°C for 2 min, then immediately chilled on ice before applying for immobilization. The analyses of the ligand binding were performed with a running buffer (50 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 100 mM KCl, 1 mM MgCl2 and 2% DMSO) at a flow rate of 30 µL/min. Ligands were injected with association and dissociation times of 60 and 300 s, respectively. All experiments were performed at 25°C.

NA sequences and preparation for MFS analysis

The two B. subtilis yitJ SAM-I riboswitch sequences were flanked by 6 nucleotides of single-stranded RNA, and a sequence that is used to allow the annealing to the DNA handles for surface (3’ DNA splint) and bead (5’ DNA splint) attachment [26]. The sequences used are shown in Table S1. The RNA was synthesized by Genewiz from Azenta Life Sciences (Germany), and the DNA handles were synthesized by Eurofins (France). The RNA was annealed to the two DNA handles (10 µM) in annealing buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.4, 50 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA). Once annealed, the structures were mixed with an equal volume of GenTegraRNA (GenTegra LLC) and stored at − 20°C until use. The annealed RNA structure (0.07 fmol) was then attached to 3 µL of MyOne T1 streptavidin beads (Invitrogen) in Oligo Buffer (OB) (1× PBS, 0.2% BSA, 0.1% NaAz) for 10 min before being washed and resuspended in 5 µL OB for loading in the instrument.

MFS instrument use and measurement

Flow cell surfaces were functionalized with an oligonucleotide and then passivated with OB buffer after flow cell assembly. The RNA bound to the MyOne beads was injected into the flow cell and allowed to hybridize to the surface-bound oligonucleotide for 30 min in OB. After hybridization, the buffer was changed to the testing buffer (50 mM HEPES, pH 7.4, 100 mM KCl, 1 mM or 10 mM MgCl2, 1% DMSO). Unbound beads were removed by washing and any non-specifically bound beads were removed by increasing the magnetic force to ~25–30 pN. Subsequent experiments were performed at 22°C, with recording of bead vertical (Z) positions at 30 Hz.

MFS was performed using Depixus’ prototype instrument [27,28]. A force ramp experiment was first recorded for each structure in test buffer containing 1% DMSO, with a constant magnet rate of 0.125 mm/s moving between 1.2 mm and 0.12 mm above the flow-cell surface (approximately 1–32 pN force). For each cycle, the RNA structure was held at the highest force for 1–3 s before returning to low force. For each experiment, 50 ramp cycles were recorded with a 3-s waiting time at low force between each cycle. This experiment was used as a control for normalization as well as to identify functional structures. Next, ligand (SAM, SAH, Ribocil-C or Mock) was added at increasing concentrations in test buffer. Stepped force experiments were also performed. To do so, the magnet was held at 0.8 mm above the flow-cell surface (approximately 4 pN) for 30 s, before lowering it stepwise by 0.01 mm every 30 s until it was 0.25 mm above the surface (approximately 22 pN). For constant-force experiments, the beads were submitted to the same force for 30–90 min. The force used was determined using the stepped force experiment of the same molecule to calculate the equilibrium force of the first transition (between the folded- and partially unfolded-states) (see below for more details).

Analysis of force ramp experiment data

Only beads with measured signal variance equal to the noise expected from Brownian motion were analysed. Beads with excessive tracking noise or detached beads were thus excluded. For each structure, each cycle was analysed separately and the force at which the abrupt jumps occurred due to unfolding of the RNA structure was detected using the ruptures open-source change-point detection library, with the window-based search method [29]. We then selected parameters to define analysable RNA structures: (1) In the control (ligand-free) condition, the proportion of cycles with unfolding events >50% at a force between 10 and 30 pN and with an unfolding size between 20 and 90 nm. (2) For each experimental condition, the total number of analysable cycles was >20. (3) Structures remained attached to the flow cell in more than 40% of the tested conditions. Structures were removed from analysis when all analysable cycles contained no unfolding or refolding events.

For each analysable structure, the median forces at which unfolding occurred in the absence of ligand were used to normalize all cycles of all other conditions. The distributions of normalized unfolding force for each cycle of all analysable beads were plotted to show the change upon ligand addition. The median of normalized median force for all beads was plotted for each ligand’s concentration. The dissociation constant (Kd) was then calculated from fitting the plot using the following formula: y = (u + b × x/KD)/(1 + x/KD), to model the fractional occupancy of binding ligands [30] where u and b are free-scaling parameters. To analyse the unfolding probability, the fractions of cycles that contained 0, 1 or 2 unfolding events were calculated for each bead and the median fraction with standard error of the mean (SEM) was plotted against ligand concentration as a bar graph.

To convert the SAM-I aptamer’s end-to-end extension from nm to nucleotides at a given force, we used the Worm-Like-Chain (WLC) model, with a persistence length lp = 0.80 nm or lp = 0.75 nm and a contour length per nucleotide (nt) of lc = 0.72 nm or lc = 0.68 nm for 1 mM Mg2+ or 10 mM Mg2+, respectively [31]. The contour length was estimated by taking the mean change in extension of the stable SAM-I aptamer of all force cycles in which the RNA fully unfolded, defined as release of the full length of the stable SAM-I aptamer (130 nt). The density histograms of all identified unfolding events of all molecules in each experiment were plotted and fitted with a mixture of four or three Gaussian distributions for 0, 0.5 and 5 µM of SAM binding to both native and stable SAM-I aptamers in 1 mM or 10 mM Mg2+ conditions, respectively.

Analysis of stepped force experiments

Data were excluded from RNA structures showing high noise (i.e. the variation in extension exceeding twice the expected Brownian motion within a 1-s data acquisition window) or structures that did not fold and unfold as expected in response to the applied magnetic force. Two separate transitions were identified: the first transition at lower force was assigned as transition 1 and the second at higher force was assigned as transition 2. For each force step, a simple two-state heuristic hidden-Markov-model (HMM) was then used to attribute the most likely state (folded or partially unfolded for transition 1 and partially unfolded or unfolded for transition 2) to each data point with the data being presented as a trace of bead position versus time. This allowed measurement of the lifetime the structure spent consecutively in either state before refolding/unfolding.

Attributed states were used to compute the fraction of time that the structure spent in each state for each force-step. For both transitions, a sigmoid function was fitted to the second state population (partially unfolded-state for transition 1 and unfolded-state for transition 2) against the force to obtain each structure’s equilibrium force, at which it spent exactly half the time in each of the two states. To study the effect of ligand binding on transition dynamics, the median equilibrium force for each transition for all beads was plotted against ligand concentration with SEM, and the data were fitted with the same formula as above.

Analysis of constant-force experiments

The data were cleaned and fitted with an HMM model as described above. Only transition 1 was studied with the two states attributed to the folded and partially unfolded-states. For the folded-state, two populations of lifetimes were observed when the SAM ligand was present. To distinguish the populations, we used a threshold time of 5 s. An event was classified as a long folded-state event if the lifetime was above this threshold, otherwise it was classified as a short folded-state event. To assess ligand concentration dependency, we computed the median observed lifetime of the partially unfolded, long and short folded-states for the DMSO control and different concentrations of the ligands for each structure individually. We then computed the median observed lifetime of all structures for each concentration. The probability of occurrence of the long folded-state was computed by dividing the number of observed long folded-state events by the total number of observed folded-state events.

Results

SAM-I RNA dynamics studied by MFS

Using B. subtilis yitJ SAM-I riboswitch aptamer as a model system, we first aimed to demonstrate that MFS can be used to study the binding affinity of SAM ligand to the SAM-I riboswitch. Two versions of the SAM-I aptamer were used, the native version and a stable version with an additional six bases at both ends which extends the length of the P1 stem, favouring the formation of the P1 in solution (Figure 1(A)). In addition, the aptamer sequences were flanked by a 6-nucleotide single-stranded sequence, acting as spacers to allow free rotation of the molecules for folding, plus sequences used for annealing to DNA handles at both ends (Figure 2(A), Table S1). The biotinylated SAM-I aptamer was bound to a streptavidin paramagnetic bead and attached to a flow-cell floor via hybridization to a surface-bound oligonucleotide (Figure 2(A)). Force ramp experiments were performed to first characterize the unfolding signature of both the native and stable SAM-I aptamers.

Analysis of the single-molecule traces showed that native SAM-I aptamer unfolded in one step (Figure 3(A)) and that sometimes the RNA did not appear to refold properly at the 1 mM Mg2+ concentration (based on 25% of force cycles not showing unfolding, Figure S1A). In contrast, under the same experimental conditions, stable SAM-I aptamer unfolded in two steps and refolded back after every force cycle (Figure 3(B)). Since the stable SAM-I aptamer contains additional base pairs at the end of the P1 stem, it is likely that the additional unfolding step was due to the opening of the stabilized P1 stem or even P1/P4. The unfolding occurred across a wide range of forces for native SAM-I aptamer, with the variability suggesting that the conformations differ with each refolding. The extension size of each unfolding event was used to estimate the number of nucleotides released upon unfolding (unfolding size) [31,32]. The broad distribution of unfolding sizes across force cycles (Figure S1B) revealed substantial structural heterogeneity, consistent with molecular simulation studies showing that the apo-SAM-I aptamer adopts multiple conformations [33]. The highest peak of the unfolding size distribution was at approximately 70 nt (Figure 3(A), S1). Given the known structure of the SAM-I riboswitch [12,13] and its binding dynamics [14,18,21,22], this indicates that the most frequently observed unfolding event is due to the P2/P3 stem. Under the same conditions, the stable SAM-I aptamer showed much less variation in unfolding force between different force cycles (Figure 3(B)), signifying that it refolds faster into a more uniform conformation. A broad distribution of unfolding sizes was again observed, with some events corresponding to the entire stable SAM-I aptamer unfolding in a single transition (Figure S1C). Magnesium ions are required for SAM ligand binding as well as promoting the SAM-I riboswitch to fold into a structure ready for ligand binding [12,18,20–22,34]. Indeed, higher Mg2+ stabilized the folded forms for both apo-aptamers as expected (Figure 3(A,B)), whereas without Mg2+ the native SAM-I aptamers did not fold into any structures stable enough to be detected by MFS force ramp experiments (Figure S2). In 10 mM Mg2+, unfolding occurred at higher forces, but the number of events remained unchanged for both native and stable SAM-I aptamers (Figure 3(A,B)). The overall stabilizing effect of Mg2+ could also be observed by the drastic decrease in the proportion of force cycles where the molecules did not refold (0 unfolding) compared to the low Mg2+ condition for the native SAM-I aptamer (Figure S1A). It has been previously shown that Mg2+ stabilizes both the P1 stem and the pseudoknot formation of the P2 stem [18,34]. At 10 mM Mg2+, the native SAM-I aptamer still unfolded in a single step, indicating that this higher Mg2+ concentration did not stabilize the P1 stem sufficiently for unfolding of the different stems to be distinguished separately. The unfolding size estimation showed that even though the unfolding forces remained relatively widespread, the majority of the unfolding events corresponded to the P2/P3 stem (Figure S1B), suggesting that Mg2+ stabilizes the formation of P2/P3 stems. For the stable SAM-I structure, the overall unfolding force increased with higher Mg2+ concentration, but the force differences between the two unfolding steps were also greater (Figure 3(B)). This is consistent with the decrease in the single-step unfolding size of the full stable SAM-I aptamer (Figure S1C). Therefore, it is likely that magnesium ions stabilize the P2/P3 stems more than the P1/P4 stems. Alternatively, the stabilization effect of the additional six-base pair of the P1 stem is less sensitive to Mg2+ compared with the P2/P3 stems. Overall, the SAM-I aptamer unfolding behaviour observed during our MFS force ramp experiments is consistent with the current understanding of the SAM-I riboswitch and additionally suggests greater stabilization of the P2/P3 structure by magnesium.

Using MFS to estimate ligand-binding affinities to SAM-I riboswitch

The SAM-I aptamer undergoes a conformational change upon SAM ligand binding, which involves the stabilization of joining regions between helices and decreased P1 dynamics [12,13,18–20,33–36]. Both Mg2+ and SAM ligand are required for successful ligand binding, enhancing each other’s binding affinity cooperatively [20]. In addition, the SAM-I riboswitch is known to discriminate against SAM’s demethylated analogue, SAH, which has been shown to bind with a 100-fold lower binding affinity and not induce conformational switching [8,9,37]. Here, force ramp experiments were performed to estimate the binding affinity of SAM and SAH to both SAM-I aptamer in low (1 mM) and high (10 mM) magnesium concentrations (Figure 3(C–F), S3). A non-ligand, Ribocil-C, as well as a no-ligand (Mock) condition was used as a control (Figure S4).

MFS force ramp experiments showed that the natural ligand SAM binding stabilizes the structure of the native RNA aptamer (Figure 3(A,C)). In the presence of SAM ligand, an additional (second) unfolding event was observed at a lower force (Figure 3(A)), with a concentration-dependent increase in the proportion of force cycles showing two events and a decrease in cycles in which the molecule did not refold (Figure S1A). We postulated that this additional unfolding event could be due to ligand-induced stabilization of the P1 stem, which is already present in the stable SAM-I aptamer (Figure 3(B)). Indeed, in the presence of SAM ligand, the unfolding size distribution showed an increase of around 48 nt, corresponding to the unfolding of P1/P4 stem together with the single-stranded sequence (Figure 3(A), S1B). SAM binding also increased the force of the original unfolding event attributed to the P2/P3 stem, resulting in an overall increase in the unfolding force (Figure 3(C)). To quantify the effect of SAM ligand binding to the native SAM-I aptamer, the unfolding force of each event for a single molecule was normalized to the median unfolding force of the same molecule in the no-ligand control condition. The result showed that SAM ligand increased the overall unfolding force in a concentration-dependent manner (Figure 3(C)). Our Kd estimation confirmed the high-affinity binding of SAM ligand to the native SAM-I aptamer at low Mg2+ concentration (~100 nM in 1 mM Mg2+). Increased magnesium concentration induced a similar additional unfolding event in the presence of SAM. However, the original unfolding event was not stabilized further by SAM binding, resulting in an overall decrease in the unfolding force (Figure 3(C)). The binding affinity increased in higher Mg2+ concentration (~90 nM in 10 mM Mg2+), consistent with the hypothesis that Mg2+ pre-organizes the structure into a conformation that is ready for ligand binding [16].

For the stable SAM-I aptamer, SAM binding increased the force of both unfolding steps (P1/P4 stem at lower force and P2/P3 at higher force) in both low and high magnesium concentrations (Figure 3(B,D)). At high SAM ligand concentration (5 µM), the two unfolding steps occurred at similar forces, sometimes appearing as a single event in 1 mM Mg2+ (Figure 2(B), S1C), indicating that SAM ligand binding to the stable aptamer stabilizes the P1/P4 structure more than the P2/P3. Mg2+ concentration again increased the binding affinity of SAM ligand for the stable SAM-I aptamer (Figure 3(D)). At low magnesium, the stable form showed a lower binding affinity for SAM than the native aptamer (Table 1, Figure 3(C,D)). This demonstrates that the additional P1 stem length reduces the affinity, perhaps by restricting the conformational change or by slightly changing the pre-organized conformation so that it is less accessible for SAM ligand. The effect of SAH, a weak binder, was also assessed by MFS for both the native and stable SAM-I aptamers at low and high Mg2+ concentration (Figure 3(E,F) S3). At low Mg2+ concentration, only one unfolding event was observed even at the highest SAH concentration (1 mM) for the native SAM-I aptamer, with only a slight decrease in the unfolding force at very high SAH concentration (Figure 3(E), S3). SAH did not change the unfolding force of the stable aptamer, consistent with the current understanding that SAH binding does not induce conformational switching of the SAM-I riboswitch [9,37]. At high Mg2+ concentration, SAH binding to the native SAM-I aptamer caused an additional unfolding step similar to SAM binding, albeit at a much higher concentration, confirming the much lower binding affinity of SAH compared with SAM ligand (Kd ~15.4 µM, Table 1, Figure 3(E), S3). The higher Mg2+ concentration would increase the proportion of pre-organized ligand-binding-ready molecules, allowing the detection of P1 stabilization at high SAH concentration. For stable SAM-I aptamer, a small effect of SAH binding on structural stability could also be observed (Figure 3(F)), primarily due to an increase in the unfolding force of the first unfolding step (P1/P4 stem). This indicates that SAH binding primarily stabilized the P1 stem. The effect of SAH binding could be differentiated from the Ribocil-C and no ligand (mock) negative controls (Figure S4). Interestingly, at high magnesium concentration, SAH binding showed an increase in binding affinity for the stable form (~0.83 µM) compared to the native form (~15.4 µM). This observation suggests that the additional stem length and the high magnesium concentration stabilize the structure into a conformation that is more favourable for SAH binding.

For both methods, only the aptamer sequences shown in Figure 1(A) without additional sequences were used. The results were summarized in Table 1. No results could be obtained for SPR in the high-magnesium condition, which was greater than the concentrations typically used in SPR. In low Mg2+ concentration (1 mM), both methods detected high-affinity binding of SAM to both forms of the aptamer within the nanomolar range (Figure 4 for native SAM-I aptamer, S5A for stable SAM-I aptamer). Similarly to MFS, the binding affinities were lower for the stable SAM-I aptamer compared to the native aptamer which was attributed to the additional P1 stem bases. Consistent with the MSF findings, SAH binding to either aptamer was not detected by SPR or ITC. Significantly, at the higher Mg2+ concentration (10 mM), ITC also detected weak binding of SAH to both the native and stable SAM-I aptamers (Figure 4(B) for native SAM-I aptamer; Figure S5B for stable SAM-I aptamer) which was clearly differentiated from the no-RNA negative controls (Figure S5D, S5E).

The dissociation constant values obtained were reasonably comparable between MFS, ITC and SPR, showing similar nanomolar ranges (Table 1). For SAM binding, the ITC Kd values were higher than the SPR values (roughly 3–6-fold), with the MFS Kd values more aligned to the ITC results. At 10 mM Mg2+, ITC measured a Kd value of ~7.3 nM, which was similar to the published Kd of SAM being around 19 nM [13,35]. Overall, this comparative study supports the use of MFS to determine RNA-ligand binding affinities and highlights differences between the techniques, including the failure of SPR under high magnesium conditions. At 10 mM, the high magnesium concentration tested was not outside the intracellular levels that can occur in bacteria, so this observation could be considered a limitation of SPR.

The impact on SAM binding on the conformation dynamics of SAM-I

To further study the observed stabilization of SAM-I aptamers by SAM ligand binding, we performed MFS stepped force experiments using the native and stable SAM-I aptamers across a range of SAM concentrations. The aptamers were subjected to a constant force for a fixed period before it was increased in a regular step-wise manner (Figures 2(B) and 5(A)). With this approach, the RNA is mostly folded at low forces, and only Brownian motion is recorded. With increasing force, the less stable parts of the structures unfold and transition between the folded and unfolded states. At higher force steps, the structures spend an increasing proportion of time in the unfolded state, eventually remaining fully unfolded. The proportion of time a molecule spends in the unfolded state can be calculated for each force, and the equilibrium force at which the structure spends 50% of the time at the unfolded state (Figure S7A) can be determined. When the RNA structure unfolds via an intermediate state (such as SAM-I riboswitch), the equilibrium forces are calculated separately for each transition. For the native SAM-I aptamer, stepped force experiments detected three states (named folded, partially unfolded and unfolded states) with two separate transition force ranges, corresponding to the successive unfolding of different stems (Figure 5(A), S6A). While the force ramp showed only a single unfolding event at around 20 pN (transition 2, corresponding to P2/P3 unfolding), the observed lower force transition (transition 1) at an estimated 5 pN is consistent with unfolding of the P1/P4 stem. Both transitions showed fast transition rates, demonstrating that the molecule is highly dynamic. At higher Mg2+, the forces at which all the transitions occurred increased, whereas the frequencies of transition decreased due to general stabilization of the native SAM-I structure (Figure 5(A)). The stable SAM-I aptamer did not show any transition between different states (Figure S6B), due to the P1 stem being too stable. Instead, the whole structure was observed to unfold at once and refolding did not occur once the molecule has been fully unfolded. Therefore, only the native SAM-I aptamer was used for further analysis.

The lower force transition (transition 1) was stabilized upon SAM addition, which was observed by less frequent transitions at higher force steps, with the molecule sometimes remaining in the lower state over multiple force steps (Figure S7A). The observed effect was similar for both the 1 and 10 mM Mg2+ conditions, except that in 10 mM Mg2+, a much lower concentration of SAM ligand was required (Figure S7A). The higher force unfolding corresponding to the second transition (transition 2) was not affected by the SAM ligand (Figure S7A, S8A), which fits with the force ramp experiments in which the second/original unfolding showed no change in the presence of SAM, except in 1 mM Mg2+. The negative control Ribocil-C caused no change in either transition (Figure S7C, S8C).

To further assess the stabilization of the lower force transition by SAM ligand, we performed long constant-force experiments at the equilibrium force of this transition (~4.3 pN for 1 mM Mg2+ and ~7.5 pN for 10 mM Mg2+) for 30–90 min. The RNA transitioned rapidly between two states (named folded- and partially unfolded-states), corresponding to the fully folded and intermediate form with P1/P4 unfolded (Figure 5(B)). The data were fitted with a HMM and the mean lifetime of each molecule was calculated for both states. In the absence of ligand, the native SAM-I aptamer spent a similar amount of time in both folded- and partially unfolded-states (Figure 5(B), S9A), with a mean lifetime of 0.308 ± 0.015 s for the folded state and 0.275 ± 0.012 s for the partially unfolded-state. Addition of SAM caused the appearance of a long-duration folded-state (Figure 5(B), S9A, S9B, S10A). While SAM ligand did not change the overall lifetime of the partially unfolded-state (Figure 5(C)), the folded-state lifetime could be observed as two populations: short and long stable lifetimes (Figures S10A, S10B). SAM binding did not change the lifetime of the short folded-state, whereas the long folded-state lifetime increased with ligand concentration (Figure 5(D), S10A, S10B). Therefore, we attributed the long folded-state to SAM ligand binding and stabilization of the riboswitch. The short lifetime folded-state also occurred in the ligand-free control condition. Surprisingly, the lifetime of the long stable folded-state increased with SAM ligand concentration. While this could be caused by a cooperative stabilization effect by SAM molecules, this is not consistent with the 1:1 stoichiometry indicated by ITC. However, at high SAM concentrations, some SAM molecules could bind transiently outside of the folded structure and stabilize it. As expected, SAM ligand also increased the occurrence of the long stable folded-state in a concentration-dependent manner, due to increased probability of the ligand binding to the riboswitch (Figure 5(E)). The apparent low occurrence is due to the frequent transition of SAM-I aptamers when not bound by SAM ligand, even though the time between each SAM ligand binding decreases (kon increases with SAM ligand concentration).

Higher Mg2+ further increased the effect of SAM ligand binding and decreased the concentration at which this occurred (Figure 5, S9B). The lifetime of the partially unfolded-state lengthened with higher Mg2+ concentration (1.373 ± 0.057 s), due to a general stabilization of the RNA structure and reduced transition frequency (Figure 5(C)). The short folded-state lifetime remained largely unchanged, and the long stable folded-state lifetimes also increased with increasing SAM concentration (Figure 5(D)). As observed with the 1 mM concentration, in high Mg2+ the occurrence of the long stable folded-state also increased in a SAM-ligand dependent manner (Figure 5(E)). Overall, the effect observed in high Mg2+ confirms that magnesium increases the structural stabilization of the SAM-I aptamer induced by SAM ligand binding.

Long constant-force experiments were used to study in detail the effect of SAH binding on the dynamics of the first transition of native SAM-I aptamer. SAH binding was observed to stabilize the P1 stem by increasing the lifetime of the folded-state in a concentration-dependent manner (Figure 6(B,C), S9D). However, the lifetime was significantly shorter compared with SAM ligand and represented a continuous increase rather than two distinct populations (Figure S10C compared with S10B). Discussion

Here we compared three biophysical techniques to assess binding of the SAM ligand and SAH to the SAM-I riboswitch aptamer: ITC and SPR, which are conventional bulk techniques used in RNA drug discovery, and a scaled single-molecule MFS platform. While all three methods detected SAM binding with comparable ranges (Table 1), the dissociation constants obtained by SPR were lower compared to ITC and MFS. This is consistent with observations that the values derived from SPR and ITC often differ within a 2–5-fold range [38], with SPR giving lower values that are partly attributed to surface immobilization effects [39]. Other contributing factors include mass transport and rebinding in SPR or differing sensitivity of the methods to buffer conditions. Furthermore, SPR failed at high magnesium concentrations (10 mM), revealing a constraint of this method, considering that high intracellular magnesium conditions can be encountered and are sometimes required for assessing RNA binding. The MFS Kd values were closer to those obtained with ITC, probably reflecting the fact that although the RNA is immobilized in MFS, DNA adapters ensure freedom of movement. Both MFS and ITC also detected SAH binding to the aptamer in high magnesium. Overall, the comparison supports the use of MFS to determine RNA-ligand binding affinities. However, each method has its own constraints, strengths and drawbacks. While SPR offers high-throughput kinetic screening of drug or ligand binding due to its capability of detecting a wide range of affinities with a fast turnover time and a low requirement of starting material, the sensorgram may be difficult to interpret. ITC provides more accurate label-free thermodynamic measurements as well as the binding stoichiometry but requires a much higher amount of starting material. MFS pairs low sample requirement with a broad dynamic range of binding affinities, but adaptors need to be integrated during RNA production, and the technique takes longer to perform than ITC or SPR. However, it provides additional information on conformation dynamics, binding mechanisms and, as a scaled single-molecule technique, it can open up a new understanding of heterogeneity, and rare or transient events that are missed in bulk ensemble methods.

By monitoring mechanical unfolding and refolding transitions at single-molecule resolution, MFS revealed the unfolding landscape of the SAM-I riboswitch aptamer. Stepped force experiments showed the folded molecules have two thermodynamically distinct substructures with different stabilities (Figure 5(A)). In light of the published crystal structure [12,13] and the dynamics [18,20–23,33,34,36], the more stable secondary-structure element likely corresponds to the P2/P3 stem. The more mobile substructure corresponds to the P1/P4 stem, which exhibited lower stability but underwent robust stabilization upon SAM binding [21,33]. Both Mg2+ and SAM are required for the conformational changes, and they act cooperatively [20–23]. Mg2+ shifts the RNA into a pre-organized conformation that is ready for ligand binding, which can be seen in the stabilization of the whole structure and a decrease in the ligand-binding affinity. The additional base pairs of the P1 in the stable SAM-I aptamer also favour a shift towards the pre-formed expression platform state even in the absence of ligand [22,40]. This structural pre-organization could explain why the stable SAM-I aptamer binds SAH with significantly higher affinity, since the energy barrier required to transition in the bound conformation has already been bypassed.

Constant-force experiments revealed that SAM binding induces a SAM-dependent long-lived folded state, with the lifetime of the unfolded state not altered (Figure 5(D)). This strongly supports a ‘conformational selection’ model, where a small fraction of the RNA ensemble pre-folds into a native-like state that directly captures and is stabilized by the SAM ligand, locking the conformation for minutes [21]. In contrast, the short-lived states were also present in the absence of SAM ligand and could represent partially misfolded or highly dynamic conformations of the SAM-I aptamer that rely on an induced-fit mechanism. In these states, structural rearrangement occurs too slowly to successfully trap the small molecule before its rapid dissociation [41].

MFS also provided insights into the structural dynamics for the weak binder SAH, reflecting the chemical and structural differences between SAM and SAH target engagement. Structurally, SAM possesses a positively charged sulphonium methyl group that stabilizes the three-way junction and drives the compaction of the aptamer [1,13,20]. SAH binds through different mechanisms, similar to the MFS finding for, lacking the methyl group, cannot provide the same electrostatic interactions in the binding pocket [37]. Consequently, while MFS confirmed that SAH can bind to the pre-organized aptamer under high magnesium conditions, it does not induce the same degree of structural stabilization, presumably because it is unable to induce the same conformational changes as SAM ligand. The absence of the long stable folded-state during SAH binding demonstrates that although both ligands occupy the same pocket, they modulate the RNA energy landscape through distinct thermodynamic pathways. This finding is similar to the MFS study of different ligands binding to the PreQ1 riboswitch [26]. In this reported study, both the natural PreQ1 ligand and a synthetic ligand bind to the PreQ1 riboswitch with detectable binding affinities. While natural PreQ1 ligand binding induces a stable pseudoknot by conformational changes, the synthetic ligand decreases the rate of unfolding through a kinetic mechanism, revealing that different small molecules can have the same impact on gene expression through distinct mechanisms.

Given the rapid escalation of global antibiotic resistance, riboswitches represent a novel class of anti-bacterial therapeutic targets [3–5]. Here, we used the B. subtilis SAM-I riboswitch as a model system to validate our single-molecule platform. However, study of the same riboswitch from pathogenic bacteria is warranted [42], since the ligand-binding properties can be different [43]. In addition, MFS can provide insights into the binding mechanisms of other riboswitches, such as TPP, FMN, and even other structured RNAs to understand small-molecule and RNA interactions and to validate in silico RNA-targeted drug design. With RNA in general offering a largely unexplored avenue for therapeutic intervention [44,45], the detailed molecular understanding gained from MFS will facilitate future drug discovery.
