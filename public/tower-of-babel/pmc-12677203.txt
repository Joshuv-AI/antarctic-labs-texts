Pacemaker-induced apnoea in a patient with cervical medullary trauma: a case report

Introduction

Cardiac electronic device implantation can lead to several known complications, including lead dislodgement, infection, and unintended nerve stimulation. Among these, phrenic nerve (PN) capture occurs in 0.5%–5% of cases, causing involuntary diaphragmatic contractions. A lateral position of the atrial lead is more prone to PN capture than placement in the right atrial appendage (RAA), which is the preferred site.1 In contrast, vagus nerve (VN) stimulation resulting in apnoea has not been previously described. We report a unique case of right atrial (RA) pacing-induced apnoea, likely mediated by stimulation of vagal afferents, without evidence of PN capture.

Case presentation

A 65-year-old man without prior medical history was admitted to the neurology department after a syncopal episode and fall, resulting in a C3–C4 spinal cord injury with tetraplegia and complete anaesthesia below C4. While under monitoring, he developed electrocardiographic (ECG) abnormalities.

The initial ECG showed sinus rhythm with a right bundle branch block, 1 mm ST elevation in leads V1–V3, and inverted T waves in lateral leads (I, aVL, V4–V6). Urgent coronary angiography revealed no obstructive coronary disease. A diagnosis of Takotsubo syndrome secondary to spinal cord injury was suspected. Shortly thereafter, the patient developed complete third-degree heart block followed by asystolic cardiac arrest, complicated by ventricular tachycardia during cardiopulmonary resuscitation. After return of spontaneous circulation, a temporary single-chamber pacemaker (PM) with a screw-in lead was inserted without complication.

On Day 2, transthoracic echocardiography showed moderate left ventricular systolic dysfunction (ejection fraction 41%) with global hypokinesia. There was a 15 mm pericardial effusion adjacent to the right ventricle and left ventricular apex, without signs of tamponade. Inflammatory cardiomyopathy was excluded with metabolic PET-CT and an immunologic workup. The aetiology of the initial syncope remained unexplained, although a primary arrhythmia (e.g. idiopathic AV block) could not be ruled out. Additionally, considering the development of ventricular tachycardia and LV dysfunction, possibly secondary to autonomic dysfunction, a decision was made to implant a permanent dual-chamber defibrillator.

The procedure was performed on Day 7. The patient was conscious, intubated, and on pressure-support ventilation due to altered ventilatory mechanics following cervical spinal cord injury. Mild sedation was used.

The patient was in sinus rhythm at 50 b.p.m. The right ventricular lead was placed at the apex and the RA lead at the RAA (Figure 1A). The leads were tested in DDD mode at 60 b.p.m. Immediately upon initiation of RA capture, a sudden drop in respiratory rate followed by apnoea was observed (Supplementary material).

Pacemaker-induced apnoea was suspected. To clarify the underlying mechanism, we sought to determine whether apnoea was triggered by the electrical stimulus alone, regardless of atrial capture, or whether effective atrial capture was required.

Therefore, a stepwise approach was applied. Output and impulse width were initially set to their lowest values: 0.2 V and 0.03 ms, respectively. The output was gradually increased to 1 V while maintaining the impulse width at 0.03 ms, triggering neither atrial capture nor apnoea. The impulse width was then progressively increased while keeping the output at 1 V. At an impulse width of 0.1 ms, two simultaneous phenomena occurred: intermittent atrial capture and bradypnoea. Further increases in impulse width led to sustained atrial capture and a progressive decrease in respiratory rate (Table 1). These findings demonstrated that apnoea was not related to the electrical impulse alone but rather required atrial capture.

No pacing configuration that allowed both reliable atrial capture and normal respiration could be achieved. Consequently, the atrial lead was repositioned to the lateral RA wall (Figure 1B) and output was programmed to 3.5 V with an impulse width of 0.4 ms. Following repositioning, no further episodes of apnoea occurred.

The post-procedural course was uneventful. Device interrogation on the following day confirmed normal PM function. The patient was subsequently transferred to a spinal cord injury rehabilitation centre.

Discussion

Ventilatory control involves a complex network of brainstem and spinal structures. The diaphragm, the primary respiratory muscle, is innervated by the PN, which originates from the C3–C5 spinal cord segments. The PN receives input from inspiratory neurons located within the dorsal respiratory group (DRG) of the medulla oblongata. The DRG interacts with the apneustic centre (AC) in the pons, which helps regulate inspiratory activity. The AC, in turn, receives inhibitory input from vagal afferents, contributing to respiratory rhythm regulation.2

Phrenic nerve stimulation is used to stimulate breathing in patients with respiratory insufficiency, including those with central sleep apnoea or neuromuscular disorders.3,4 Conversely, injury to the PN can cause diaphragmatic paresis or paralysis, potentially leading to respiratory failure.5

The VN plays a key regulatory role in the autonomic nervous system, influencing gastrointestinal, cardiovascular, respiratory, and endocrine functions. Vagal nerve stimulation (VNS) is used in various conditions, for example, drug-resistant epilepsy.6 Interestingly, obstructive and central sleep apnoea are potential complications of VNS. These respiratory disturbances have been reported in patients with epilepsy who had no prior history of sleep apnoea and often resolved upon deactivation of the stimulator, highlighting the role of vagal afferents in breathing control.6,7

The VN carries afferent fibres from the airway and lungs to respiratory centres in the brainstem, where they can stimulate or inhibit respiration depending on the receptors activated. Less is known about cardiac vagal afferents and their effects on breathing. The heart is richly innervated by the VN through pericardial fat pads that contain autonomic ganglia, which convey ascending input to the brainstem and may exert an inhibitory influence on breathing.8–10 Atrial pacing has also been reported to reduce central and obstructive sleep apnoea events, possibly by decreasing nocturnal vagal tone via sympathetic activation.10,11 These findings underscore the interplay between cardiac and respiratory vagal pathways. Interconnections between cardiac and respiratory vagal afferent fibres have been described.12

Considering that atrial pacing resulted in complete apnoea without hemidiaphragmatic contraction or paralysis, the findings do not support PN capture as the underlying mechanism, instead suggesting a more complex neurological circuit involving the VN. We hypothesize that the RAA lead most likely activated a vagal afferent, leading to brainstem-mediated inhibition through the DRG and AC. Additionally, this patient’s C3–C5 spinal cord injury may have impaired PN motor output. The combination of VN-mediated inhibition of the AC and an already weakened PN motor output (Summary figure) may have predisposed him to pacing-induced apnoea.

Conclusion

In summary, this case describes dual-chamber defibrillator implantation in a patient with cervical spinal cord trauma who developed sudden apnoea during atrial capture when the atrial lead was positioned in the RAA. This unusual phenomenon was most likely mediated by an inhibitory VN afferent signal processed at the brainstem. Repositioning the lead to the lateral RA wall was successful and did not result in further apnoea. However, the lateral RA lead placement cannot be recommended as a universal solution, as it may increase the risk of PN capture. Clinicians should carefully monitor for changes in respiratory rhythm, including not only PN capture but also apnoea, when implanting PMs in patients with cervical spine trauma.
