DATA-DRIVEN PRONTO : a Model-free Solution for Numerical Optimal Control
Marco Borghesi, Lorenzo Sforni, Giuseppe Notarstefano

Abstract
This article addresses the problem of data-driven numerical optimal control for unknown nonlinear systems. In our scenario, we suppose to have the possibility of performing multiple experiments (or simulations) on the system. Experiments are performed by relying on a data-driven tracking controller able to steer the system towards a desired reference. Our proposed DATA-DRIVEN PRONTO algorithm iteratively refines a tentative solution of the optimal control problem by computing an approximate descent direction via a local trajectory perturbation. At each iteration, multiple trajectories are gathered by perturbing the current trajectory with a suitable dither signal, and then used to obtain a data-driven, time-varying linearization. The exploration is guided by the tracking controller, so that perturbed trajectories are obtained in closed loop. We show local convergence of DATA-DRIVEN PRONTO to a ball about an isolated optimal solution, whose radius depends on the amplitude of the dither signal. We corroborate the theoretical results by applying it to an underactuated robot.

Nonlinear optimal control

In this paper, we propose a combination of identification techniques together with

Retrieving an optimal trajectory for this kind of setups is important, for example, in several industrial systems, where optimized execution of a repeated task results in significant savings.

Article Contribution

The main contribution given in this paper is
DATA-DRIVEN PRONTO
, an algorithm for nonlinear optimal control extending the applicability of
PRONTO

[
35
]

to the model-free scenario.
In particular,
DATA-DRIVEN PRONTO
leverages the possibility to probe the system dynamics in a safe way via the application of an independently-designed control law.
At each algorithm iteration, we perform
L
experiments (simulations) in which we use the given controller plus an exploration dither to gather data in the neighbourhood of the current trajectory. These data are then used to identify the dynamics linearizations about the current trajectory, substituting the need for a model in the optimization process.
The proposed algorithm achieves the following desirable features:
i) avoiding parametrization errors:
DATA-DRIVEN PRONTO
uses data to identify local first-order approximations of the dynamics, thus avoiding errors due to a global, inexact parametrization of the nonlinear dynamics or optimal policy;
ii) being model-free:
DATA-DRIVEN PRONTO
itself does not require any knowledge of the system; however, partial system knowledge can be
used to enhance the numerical stability of the algorithm, e.g., improving the design of a closed-loop control law;
iii) being data-efficient: thanks to the efficient algorithm structure, the number of required experiments (simulations) needed is comparable, at each algorithm iteration, with the system dimension.
We provide theoretical guarantees on the algorithm convergence properties, together with insights on how to choose its parameters. The proposed algorithm is proved to be locally uniformly ultimately bounded to a ball about the optimal solution whose radius depends on the amplitude of the dither used to achieve successful identification.

Problem Setup and Preliminaries

In this first section, we introduce the problem setup together with some preliminaries on model-based optimal control of nonlinear systems.

II-A

Model-free Nonlinear Optimal Control

In this article, we consider nonlinear systems described by the discrete-time dynamics

x_{t+1}=f(x_{t},u_{t}),\qquad x_{0}={x_{\text{init}}}

(1)

where
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is the dynamics and
x_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n},

u_{t}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
are, respectively, the state of the system and the control input at time
t\in{\mathbb{N}}
. The initial condition is fixed to be
{x_{\text{init}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
We assume dynamics (
1
) to be
unknown
, i.e., we do not have access to an explicit form of
f
.
Nevertheless, we assume to be able to actuate the system with given input sequence
u_{0},\ldots,u_{T-1}
and to measure the noiseless states
x_{0},\ldots,x_{T}
.
Following the notation introduced in Section
I
, we define those sequences as

\begin{split}\bm{u}&\coloneqq(u_{0},\ldots,u_{T-1})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{{{m}T}}\\
\bm{x}&\coloneqq(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{T})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{{n}(T+1)}.\end{split}

(2)

More formally, we assume we can measure
trajectories
of system (
1
), i.e.,
state-input sequences satisfying the following definition.

Definition 1
(System trajectory)

The pair
(\bm{x},\bm{u})

is a trajectory of system (
1
) if it satisfies

x_{t+1}=f(x_{t},u_{t})

(3)

for all
t=0,\ldots,T-1
with
x_{0}={x_{\text{init}}}

Compactly, we denote a trajectory as

Definition 2
(Trajectory manifold)

We denote as

the set of all the trajectories of (
1
) as per Definition

Notice that, by defining

\vdots\\
x_{T}-f(x_{T-1},u_{T-1})\end{bmatrix},

it holds that

Conversely, a generic element of

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bm{\alpha}&\coloneqq(\alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{T})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{s_{x}},\\
\bm{\mu}&\coloneqq(\mu_{0},\ldots,\mu_{T-1})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{s_{u}}.\end{split}

(5)

Our objective is to design input sequences
\bm{u}
for the unknown system (
1
)
such that the resulting trajectory
(\bm{x},\bm{u})
minimizes a nonlinear performance index

\displaystyle\ell(\bm{x},\bm{u})\coloneqq\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\ell_{t}(x_{t},u_{t})+\ell_{T}(x_{T}),

(6)

where
\ell_{t}:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{\geq 0}

Compactly, we aim at solving the following optimal control problem

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{x},\bm{u}}\;\;\;

\displaystyle\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\ell_{t}(x_{t},u_{t})+\ell_{T}(x_{T})

(7)

\displaystyle\subj\;\;\;

\displaystyle x_{t+1}=f(x_{t},u_{t}),\;\;\;t=0,\ldots,T-1,

\displaystyle x_{0}={x_{\text{init}}}.

We introduce the following hypothesis on the smoothness of the dynamics and the cost function.

Assumption 1
(Smooth Cost and Dynamics)

The dynamics
f
in (
1
) and the cost function
\ell
in (
6
) are twice continuously differentiable in their arguments, i.e., they belong to class
C^{2}
.

As it will be clearer further in the paper,
we also introduce the following assumptions regarding the second-order derivatives of the cost function (
6
).

Assumption 2
(Positive Definite Cost Hessian)

For all state and input sequences
(\bm{x},\bm{u})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{s}
, it holds that
{\nabla}^{2}\ell(\bm{x},\bm{u})>0
.

Assumption
2
guarantees the possibility
of finding a valid descent direction.
Furthermore, since the cost
\ell
is tipically user-defined, it is possible to readily satisfy Assumption
2

We highlight that, in this paper, the key challenge is that dynamics (
1
) and its derivatives are not accessible in their explicit form, i.e., they are not available to the solver.

VI
Conclusions

In this paper we proposed a novel data-driven optimal control algorithm called
DATA-DRIVEN PRONTO
. This algorithm extends the applicability of
PRONTO
by removing the knowledge of the system model, relying on the ability to explore system trajectories.
The main advantage of this algorithm is the possibility to overcome the suboptimality of model-based solution by estimating the linearizations about a trajectory instead of computing them from the model. Theoretical guarantees on the convergence of the algorithm have been given, together with insight on how to tune the design parameters of the algorithm.

, and define
\epsilon_{F}=(M-\kappa(FF^{\top}))/2
. By continuity, we can always find
\delta_{F}>0
such that

|F^{\prime}-F|<\delta_{F}\implies|\kappa(F^{\prime}F^{\prime\top})-\kappa(FF^{\top})|<\epsilon_{F},

(76)

namely,
\kappa(F^{\prime}F^{\prime\top})<M
, and thus
F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}_{M}
. In other words, we can find an open ball about any point
F\in\mathcal{F}_{M}
(whose radius depends on
F
) which is contained in the set
\mathcal{F}_{M}
.

Next, we show
\mathcal{F}_{M}
is a cone.
This follows from the fact that if
F\in\mathcal{F}_{M}
, then also
\lambda F\in\mathcal{F}_{M}
, since

\begin{split}\kappa(\lambda^{2}FF^{\top})&=|\lambda_{\text{max}}(\lambda^{2}FF^{\top})/\lambda_{\text{min}}(\lambda^{2}FF^{\top})|\\
&=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}\kappa(FF^{\top})\\
&=\kappa(FF^{\top})<M.\end{split}

(77)

VII-E

Proof of Lemma
5

The proof goes through three main steps.
First, we find a closed-form expression for the errors (
33
) on the Jacobians at each
t
.
Second, we show that this expression is a differentiable function of the entries of
\Delta X_{t},\Delta U_{t}
.
Finally, we define a local linear bound on the norm of
\Delta_{AB}
.

I) Closed-form expression for
\Delta A_{t}
and
\Delta B_{t}
.

Define

\begin{split}\Delta x^{i}_{t}&=\hat{x}^{i}_{t}-x_{t},\;\;\;\Delta u^{i}_{t}=\hat{u}^{i}_{t}-u_{t},\\
\Delta x_{t}^{+,i}&=f(\hat{x}^{i}_{t},\hat{u}^{i}_{t})-f(x_{t},u_{t})\end{split}

(78)

where the apex
i
denotes the
i
-th perturbation of the current trajectory
\eta=(\bm{x},\bm{u})
.
By expanding in Taylor series the dynamics
f
about
x_{t},u_{t}
, we obtain:

\Delta x^{+,i}_{t}=A_{t}\Delta x^{i}_{t}+B_{t}\Delta u^{i}_{t}+o_{\eta}(\Delta x^{i}_{t},\Delta u^{i}_{t}),

(79)

for
\Delta x_{t}^{i}\rightarrow 0,\Delta u_{t}^{i}\rightarrow 0
. The pedex
\eta
in
o_{\eta}(\cdot)
highlights the fact that the little-o term depends on the trajectory
\eta
.
We then build
\Delta X_{t},\Delta U_{t},\Delta X^{+}_{t}
as in (
20
).
It holds:

\Delta X^{+}_{t}=\begin{bmatrix}A_{t}&B_{t}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Delta X_{t}\\
\Delta U_{t}\end{bmatrix}+o_{\eta}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\Delta X_{t}\\
\Delta U_{t}\end{bmatrix}\right),

(80)

for
\Delta x_{t}^{i}\rightarrow 0,\Delta u_{t}^{i}\rightarrow 0
for all
i=1,\ldots,L
, from which, under Assumption
4
, we obtain:

\begin{bmatrix}A_{t}&B_{t}\end{bmatrix}=\left(\Delta X_{t}^{+}-o_{\eta}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\Delta X_{t}\\
\Delta U_{t}\end{bmatrix}\right)\right)\begin{bmatrix}\Delta X_{t}\\
\Delta U_{t}\end{bmatrix}^{\dagger}.

(81)

Recall that we estimate the Jacobians with

\begin{bmatrix}\hat{A}_{t}&\hat{B}_{t}\end{bmatrix}=\Delta X_{t}^{+}\begin{bmatrix}\Delta X_{t}\\
\Delta U_{t}\end{bmatrix}^{\dagger}.

(82)

We can then subtract (
81
) from (
82
) to obtain

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}\Delta A_{t}&\Delta B_{t}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=o_{\eta}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\Delta X_{t}\\
\Delta U_{t}\end{bmatrix}\right)\begin{bmatrix}\Delta X_{t}\\
\Delta U_{t}\end{bmatrix}^{\dagger},

(83)

for all
t=0,\ldots,T-1
.

II) Continuous differentiability of
[\Delta A_{t},\Delta B_{t}]
in (
83
)

Notice that
o_{\eta}(\cdot)
in (
83
) must be a continuously differentiable function of
\eta,\Delta X_{t},\Delta U_{t}
by Assumption
1
(since it is a stack of the
o_{\eta}(\cdot)
in (
79
), which in turn must be
C^{1}
in their argument being the remainder of a first-order approximation of a
C^{2}
function). Furthermore, if
(\Delta X_{t},\Delta U_{t})\in\mathcal{F}_{M}
for all
t
, then also the pseudoinverse in (
83
) is a
C^{1}
function of the the entries of
\Delta X_{t},\Delta U_{t}
, since it can be calculated as

\begin{bmatrix}\Delta X_{t}\\
\Delta U_{t}\end{bmatrix}^{\dagger}=\begin{bmatrix}\Delta X_{t}\\
\Delta U_{t}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\Delta X_{t}\\
\Delta U_{t}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\Delta X_{t}\\
\Delta U_{t}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}\right)^{-1},

(84)

and this is a product of
C^{1}
functions of the entries of
\Delta X_{t},\Delta U_{t}
in the considered domain.
This proves that, given
(\Delta X_{t},\Delta U_{t})\in\mathcal{F}_{M}
, for all
t=1,\ldots,T-1
the error matrix
[\Delta A_{t},\Delta B_{t}]
in (
83
) is a
C^{1}
function of
\Delta X_{t},\Delta U_{t}
.
In turn, this implies that the function
\Delta_{AB}(\eta,\bm{\Delta X},\bm{\Delta U})
is a
C^{1}
function of
\bm{\Delta X},\bm{\Delta U}
, since it is the stack of all
[\Delta A_{t},\Delta B_{t}]
.

III) Local linear bound on
\|\Delta_{AB}\|

Denote, for simplicity,

\Delta_{t}\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}\Delta X_{t}\\
\Delta U_{t}\end{bmatrix}.

(85)

By substituting (
84
) in (
83
), we obtain

\begin{split}\begin{bmatrix}\Delta A_{t}&\Delta B_{t}\end{bmatrix}&=o_{\eta}(\Delta_{t})\Delta_{t}^{\top}(\Delta_{t}\Delta_{t}^{\top})^{-1}\\
&=o_{\eta}(\Delta_{t}\Delta_{t}^{\top})(\Delta_{t}\Delta_{t}^{\top})^{-1},\end{split}

(86)

and by taking the norms and using Assumption
4
,

\begin{split}\left\|\!\begin{bmatrix}\Delta A_{t}&\Delta B_{t}\end{bmatrix}\!\right\|&\!\leq\!\|o_{\eta}(\Delta_{t}\Delta_{t}^{\top})\|\|(\Delta_{t}\Delta_{t}^{\top})^{-1}\|\\
&\!\leq\!\|o_{\eta}(\Delta_{t}\Delta_{t}^{\top})\|\|\Delta_{t}\Delta_{t}^{\top}\|^{-1}\kappa(\Delta_{t}\Delta_{t}^{\top})\\
&\!\leq\!\|o_{\eta}(\Delta_{t}\Delta_{t}^{\top})\|\|\Delta_{t}\Delta_{t}^{\top}\|^{-1}M\end{split}

(87)

Notice that by definition,
o_{\eta}(\cdot)
goes to zero faster than its argument, i.e., for any
\epsilon>0
we can find
\delta>0
such that, for all
t=0,\ldots,T-1
, it holds

\|\Delta_{t}\Delta_{t}^{\top}\|\leq\delta\implies\|o(\Delta_{t}\Delta_{t}^{\top})\|\|\Delta_{t}\Delta_{t}^{\top}\|^{-1}\leq\epsilon.

(88)

Being
[\Delta A_{t}\;\Delta B_{t}]
continuously differentiable in
\eta,\Delta X_{t},\Delta U_{t}
in the considered domain, we know that for any compact set
\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{K}\subset{\mathcal{T}}\times\mathcal{F}_{M}
, for all fixed
\eta\in\mathcal{H}
and
\Delta_{t}\coloneqq(\Delta X_{t},\Delta U_{t})
,
\Delta^{\prime}_{t}\coloneqq(\Delta X_{t}^{\prime},\Delta U^{\prime}_{t})\in\mathcal{K}
there exist
g(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K})>0
such that

\begin{split}&\left\|\begin{bmatrix}\Delta A_{t}&\Delta B_{t}\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}\Delta A^{\prime}_{t}&\Delta B^{\prime}_{t}\end{bmatrix}\right\|\leq g(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K})\left\|\Delta_{t}-\Delta_{t}^{\prime}\right\|\\
&\left\|\!\begin{bmatrix}\Delta A_{t}&\Delta B_{t}\end{bmatrix}\!\right\|\!\leq\!\left\|\!\begin{bmatrix}\Delta A^{\prime}_{t}&\Delta B^{\prime}_{t}\end{bmatrix}\!\right\|\!+\!g(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K})(\|\Delta_{t}\|\!+\!\|\Delta_{t}^{\prime}\|)\\
\vphantom{\int}\end{split}

(89)

Using (
88
), given any
\epsilon>0
, there exists
\delta>0
for which, by picking
\|\Delta^{\prime}\|\leq\delta
, we can ensure
\|[\Delta A_{t}^{\prime}\;\Delta B_{t}^{\prime}]\|\leq\epsilon M
, from which

\begin{split}\left\|\!\begin{bmatrix}\Delta A_{t}&\Delta B_{t}\end{bmatrix}\right\|\leq&g(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K})\|\Delta_{t}\|+g(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K})\delta+\epsilon M.\end{split}

(90)

Since
\epsilon
can be picked arbitrarily small, we obtain it must hold

\begin{split}\left\|\!\begin{bmatrix}\Delta A_{t}&\Delta B_{t}\end{bmatrix}\!\right\|\leq&g(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K})\|\Delta_{t}\|,\end{split}

(91)

and since this holds for all
t=0,\ldots,T-1
, we have

\begin{split}\|\Delta_{AB}(\eta,\bm{\Delta X},\bm{\Delta U})\|\leq&g(\mathcal{H},\mathcal{K})T\|(\bm{\Delta X},\bm{\Delta U})\|\end{split}

(92)

for all
(\eta,\bm{\Delta X},\bm{\Delta U})\in\mathcal{H}\times\mathcal{K}
.

VII-F

Proof of Lemma
6

Consider the exact problem (
3
). We reformulate it as

\displaystyle\min_{\zeta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{s}}\;\;\;

\displaystyle\zeta^{\top}{\nabla}^{2}\ell(\eta)\zeta+{\nabla}\ell(\eta)^{\top}\zeta

(93)

s.t.

\displaystyle H(\eta)\zeta=0,

with
\zeta=(\bm{\Delta}\bm{x},\bm{\Delta}\bm{u})
and

\begin{split}H(\eta)&\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}H_{x}(\eta)&H_{u}(\eta)\end{bmatrix},\\
H_{x}(\eta)&:=\begin{bmatrix}I&0&0&0\\
-A_{0}&I&0&0\\
0&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
0&0&-A_{T-1}&I\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{s_{x}\times s_{x}}\\
H_{u}(\eta)&:=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0\\
-B_{0}&0&0\\
0&\ddots&0\\
0&0&-B_{T-1}\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{s_{x}\times s_{u}}.\end{split}

(94)

The estimated problem (
7
) differs from this only in the constraints, i.e., it can be written as

\displaystyle\min_{\zeta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{s}}\;\;\;

\displaystyle\zeta^{\top}{\nabla}^{2}\ell(\eta)\zeta+{\nabla}\ell(\eta)^{\top}\zeta

(95)

s.t.

\displaystyle\hat{H}(\eta,\bm{\Delta A},\bm{\Delta B})\zeta=0,

where
\hat{H}(\eta,\bm{\Delta A},\bm{\Delta B})=H(\eta)+\tilde{H}(\bm{\Delta A},\bm{\Delta B})
given by

\begin{split}\tilde{H}(\bm{\Delta A},\bm{\Delta B})&:=\begin{bmatrix}\tilde{H}_{x}(\bm{\Delta A},\bm{\Delta B})&\tilde{H}_{u}(\bm{\Delta A},\bm{\Delta B})\end{bmatrix},\\
\tilde{H}_{x}(\bm{\Delta A},\bm{\Delta B})&\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0&0\\
-\Delta A_{0}&0&0&0\\
0&\ddots&\ddots&0\\
0&0&-\Delta A_{T-1}&0\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{s_{x}\times s_{x}}\\
\tilde{H}_{u}(\bm{\Delta A},\bm{\Delta B})&\coloneqq\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0\\
-\Delta B_{0}&0&0\\
0&\ddots&0\\
0&0&-\Delta B_{T-1}\end{bmatrix}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{s_{x}\times s_{u}}.\end{split}

(96)

By Assumption (
2
), (
93
) is a strictly convex program and has a unique minimizer
\zeta^{\star}(\eta)
. Furthermore, given the structure of
H(\eta)
, Linear Independence Constraint Qualification (LICQ) holds for any
\eta\in{\mathbb{R}}^{s}
. This means that KKT conditions hold for the solution
\zeta^{\star}(\eta)
of problem (
93
)
[
1
, Thm. 3.14]
. By
[
1
, Thm. 3.18]
, having only equality constraints, also SOSC holds for
\zeta^{\star}(\eta)
. We can apply
[
1
, Thm. 3.19]
and obtain that there exists a
\delta_{AB}(\eta)>0
such that, for all
\|\tilde{H}(\bm{\Delta A},\bm{\Delta B})\|\leq\delta_{AB}(\eta)
, there exists a unique solution
\hat{\zeta}^{\star}(\eta,\bm{\Delta A},\bm{\Delta B})
to (
95
) and it depends differentiably on
\tilde{H}(\eta,\bm{\Delta A},\bm{\Delta B})
.
Furthermore,
\delta_{AB}(\eta)
depends continuously on
\eta
, since
{\nabla}^{2}\ell(\eta),{\nabla}\ell(\eta),H(\eta)
describing the QP (
93
) are all continuous in
\eta
by Assumption
1
.

Next, as
\tilde{H}(\bm{\Delta A},\bm{\Delta B})
is linear in
\eta,\bm{\Delta A},\bm{\Delta B}
, also
\hat{\zeta}^{\star}(\eta,\bm{\Delta A},\bm{\Delta B})
is differentiable in
\eta,\bm{\Delta A},\bm{\Delta B}
. This means that
\zeta^{\star}(\eta)-\hat{\zeta}^{\star}(\eta,\bm{\Delta A},\bm{\Delta B})
is smooth in its arguments.

To conclude the statement, notice that if
\bm{\Delta A},\bm{\Delta B}=0
then
\tilde{H}(\bm{\Delta A},\bm{\Delta B})=0
, and thus, since problems (
93
) and (
95
) become identical,
\zeta^{\star}(\eta)-\hat{\zeta}^{\star}(\eta,0,0)=0
.
