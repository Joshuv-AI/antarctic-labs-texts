Dose-response, net clinical benefit and optimal dose ranges of stimulants in children and adolescents with ADHD: a network meta-analysis
Authors: Yu Chang, Po-Chung Ju, Ming-Hong Hsieh, Yi-Chun Liu, Cheng-Chen Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStimulant treatment with methylphenidate (MPH) and amphetamines (AMP) is first-line for children and adolescents with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), but quantitative guidance on comparative efficacy and dose selection within licensed dose ranges remains limited. This study aimed to compare dose-response relationships for MPH and AMP and identify dose ranges that balance symptom improvement and adverse event-related discontinuation across benefit-risk preferences. CENTRAL, PubMed, and Embase were searched from inception to August 12, 2025 for double-blind randomized trials of fixed-dose MPH or AMP in patients aged 5–18 years with ADHD. A Bayesian random-effects dose-response network meta-analysis estimated symptom change and discontinuation due to adverse events. Benefits and harms were integrated using net clinical benefit (NCB) across a range of risk-preference values to reflect real-world variation in benefit-harm trade-offs. Forty-eight trials (4,964 participants) were included. For both drugs, efficacy increased with dose and then plateaued (approximately 45 mg/day for MPH and 25 mg/day for AMP), while adverse event-related discontinuation rose steadily with higher doses. Across plausible preferences, AMP generally achieved higher maximal NCB than MPH. Under neutral preferences, population-level NCB was highest at approximately 18–46 mg/day for MPH (peak 30–39 mg/day) and 12–26 mg/day for AMP (peak 18–20 mg/day). By combining dose-response modeling with a net clinical benefit translation, we identified preference-sensitive, comparative dose ranges for MPH and AMP to support more transparent stimulant selection and dose titration in routine care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00787-026-03025-3.Keywords: ADHD, Methylphenidate, Amphetamine, Dose-response relationship, Drug, Network meta-analysis, Patient preference

Introduction

Pharmacotherapy with methylphenidate (MPH) and amphetamines (AMP) is the first-line treatment for children and adolescents with ADHD [1]. Guidelines recommend individualized titration: start at a low dose and increase gradually to achieve maximum benefit with tolerable side effects [2, 3]. However, the target endpoint of titration remains unclear [4, 5]. In practice, stimulant titration often relies on trial and error rather than quantitative guidance [6].

A previous dose-response meta-analysis of MPH and AMP in children and adolescents reported greater symptom reduction at higher doses, accompanied by more discontinuations due to adverse events [7]. In adults, a subsequent network meta-analysis comparing licensed and unlicensed doses of MPH and AMP suggested small additional efficacy but higher dropout rates above licensed ranges [8]. Building on these findings, evidence that supports dosing decisions within the licensed dose ranges used in practice remains limited in two ways [4, 9]. First, direct comparative evidence across the full therapeutic dose ranges of MPH and AMP is limited. Second, efficacy and safety are typically analyzed as separate outcomes [10]. Analyzing these endpoints in isolation makes it difficult to identify dose ranges that offer a clinically acceptable trade-off.

To address this, we adopted a decision-analytic framework using net clinical benefit (NCB), which integrates benefits and harms by weighting efficacy against adverse events according to clinical values and risk preferences [11–13]. Applying NCB to the dose-response estimates allows us to identify dose ranges that may offer favorable benefit-harm trade-offs under different value judgments.

We conducted a Bayesian dose-response network meta-analysis to jointly model efficacy and adverse event-related discontinuation in fixed-dose methylphenidate and amphetamine trials in children and adolescents with ADHD [14, 15], enabling a unified comparison across therapeutic dose ranges. We then applied a net clinical benefit framework to translate these estimates into preference-sensitive target dose ranges. Our goal is to provide practical quantitative anchors to guide switching and titrating in routine care.

Methods

This study was preregistered in PROSPERO (CRD420251123663) on August 10, 2025. Any deviations from the registered protocol are detailed in eMethod 1. This review adheres to the PRISMA statement (eMethod 2).

Eligibility criteria

The eligibility criteria were adapted from the dose-response meta-analysis [7], and were updated through a new systematic review. We included double-blind randomized controlled trials (RCTs) investigating the treatment of ADHD in children and adolescents aged 5–18 years, where the ADHD or hyperkinetic disorder diagnosis was established using standardized diagnostic criteria (i.e., DSM-III or later versions and ICD-9 or later versions). Trials were required to include at least one fixed-dose treatment arm with MPH or AMP; trials using flexible-dose titration only or combining MPH/AMP with other ADHD medications were excluded. The intervention duration had to be at least five days, thereby excluding studies with very short-term outcomes (e.g., single-day classroom observation trials). For crossover trials, only data from the period before the crossover was included. Included studies had to report outcomes using a standardized ADHD symptom scale, such as the ADHD Rating Scale [16]. Studies that were open-label from inception were excluded. No restrictions were placed on the language or year of publication.

Search strategy, selection and data extraction

We conducted a systematic search of Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), PubMed, and Embase. The search terms are provided in eMethod 3. Additionally, we conducted citation chasing using the web-based Citationchaser platform for the key prior meta-analyses [7, 10, 17]. All searches were completed on August 12, 2025.

The screening and data extraction processes were managed using the Rayyan platform. An artificial intelligence (AI) system was employed to assist the screening process; after every 100 manually screened articles, the AI provided suggestions for categorization. However, all final inclusion and exclusion decisions were made manually. The Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool was used to assess the methodological quality of the included studies.

The following data were extracted from each study: author names, publication year, mean age with standard deviation, sex ratio, trial design (crossover or parallel), medication type and dosage (fixed specified dose or weight-based), treatment duration, outcome measures, information source (clinician, teacher, or parent), co-administration of psychotherapy, and funding sources. For each active treatment arm, we extracted the daily dose specified in the trial. When doses were reported only in mg/kg/day, we converted them to mg/day using the trial-specific mean weight when available; otherwise, we imputed mean weights from the U.S. CDC growth charts [18]. We prioritized outcomes from multiple sources hierarchically: clinician ratings first, followed by teacher ratings, then parent ratings. We extracted outcome data from the timepoint closest to 12 weeks, including means and standard deviations of scale scores, the number of participants in each treatment arm, and the number of dropouts due to side effects.

Data collected in the previous meta-analysis were incorporated into this study after validation [7]. Two board-certified child and adolescent psychiatrists (YC and YCL) independently performed the screening, data extraction, and risk of bias assessments. If a consensus could not be reached, a third board-certified psychiatrist (CCC) was consulted for a final decision.

Dose equivalence conversion

To ensure comparability across different stimulant formulations, all MPH and AMP doses were converted to standardized daily equivalents, following the approach used in the previous dose-response meta-analysis [7]. The conversion factors were derived from established pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic principles, including differences in active isomers, chemical forms (e.g., base vs. salt), and relative potencies in the FDA Approved Drug Products database [19]. A detailed description of the conversion factors for each specific formulation is provided in the eMethod 4.

Outcome

The primary outcomes were treatment efficacy, measured as the change in ADHD symptom severity on standardized rating scales, and rate of discontinuation due to adverse events.

Meta-analytic process

We first generated network plots to visualize the evidence base for different doses of MPH and AMP. The dose-response network meta-analysis was conducted within a Bayesian framework using a random-effects model. This model-based NMA used weakly informative default priors as described in eMethod 5. Dose-response relationships were modelled using restricted cubic (natural) splines with three knots placed according to Harrell’s recommended percentiles (0.10, 0.50, 0.90) [20]. The rationale for modeling choices is detailed in eMethod 6. For the dropout outcome, given the lower number of events and the approximately monotonic pattern of dose-response, we specified a simpler linear dose-effect function and compared it with a spline-based model using the deviance information criterion (DIC) in sensitivity analysis.

Efficacy outcomes were expressed as standardized mean differences (SMD), while safety outcomes were modeled as binary events. We estimated the dose-response curves for both efficacy and discontinuation probability for MPH and AMP within a unified network model, calculating 95% credible intervals (CrIs). To facilitate clinical interpretation, we extracted and ranked the estimated efficacy and discontinuation probabilities at 5-mg dose increments.

To evaluate incoherence (inconsistency), we compared our network meta-analysis results with an unrelated mean effects (UME) model and generated residual deviance plots. Between-study heterogeneity was assessed using the between-study standard deviation (τ) of relative effects. Because no universally accepted thresholds for τ exist, we interpreted its magnitude using empirical distributions from a large set of Cochrane reviews, in which τ ≈ 0.17 is approximately the median value for continuous outcomes [21].

We assessed the robustness of primary efficacy findings through sensitivity analyses: (1) restricting the analysis to studies with a low risk of bias, and (2) excluding trials with co-administered psychotherapy to isolate pure pharmacological effects. To investigate sources of heterogeneity and effect modification, we performed univariable network meta-regressions on the primary efficacy outcome. This approach was chosen because the limited number of studies precluded multivariable modeling. We examined the following study-level covariates: trial design, study duration (mean-centered), and information source (rater). Analyses were conducted using the MBNMAdose package in R [22, 23].

Benefit-risk trade-off analysis

We balanced efficacy against risk using the likelihood to be helped or harmed (LHH) framework [24–26], and operationalized this balance by defining treatment acceptability as the maximization of net clinical benefit (NCB), calculated as:

where absolute risk reductions (ARR) and absolute risk increase (ARI) represent the probabilities of clinical benefit and harm respectively, and λ denotes the maximum acceptable risk-benefit trade-off threshold.

For efficacy, SMDs were converted to response probabilities and ARRs by applying a probit link to the pooled placebo response rate from control arms. A clinically important response was defined as a ≥ 0.5 SD improvement [27, 28], with 0.4 and 0.6 SD thresholds examined in sensitivity analyses (eMethod 7). For risk, ARI was defined as discontinuation due to adverse events, quantified as risk differences (eMethod 8). In this setting, the LHH metric indicates how many patients benefit for every one patient who discontinues due to adverse events.

To determine the λ thresholds, we aligned our analysis with patient preferences reported in the literature. Although discrete choice experiments (DCEs) measure stated preferences (via the marginal rate of substitution, MRS) and LHH assesses clinical outcomes, they are intrinsically linked through the NCB framework [29]. We treated the MRS as an operational proxy for the threshold λ (eMethod 9). Guided by empirical DCE findings, we mapped reported trade-off ratios to our λ values. Because the trade-off ratio for avoiding severe side effects (~ 3.7) is closest to our definition of treatment-limiting harm [30], we treated values around as risk-neutral and set a corresponding range of λ = 3–5. Lower trade-off ratios for general adverse events (~ 2.14) motivated a risk-tolerant range of λ = 1–3 [31], whereas λ > 5 (e.g., 5–10) was classified as risk-averse.

For each posterior draw and each value of λ, we recorded the dose at which the dose-net clinical benefit curve was maximal (argmax). The empirical distribution of these argmax doses defined the posterior for the optimal dose and its 95% highest posterior density (HPD) interval, which we interpreted as the optimal dose range. We then mapped these estimates by selecting, for each λ, the integer MPH and AMP doses with the highest posterior net benefit. Graphically, we summarized these results using three plots: (1) dose-net benefit curves displaying the posterior mean and uncertainty bands across doses; (2) trajectories of the HPD-based optimal dose ranges as a function of λ, illustrating how preference weights shift the location of the optimal dose; and (3) probability heatmaps showing, for each combination of dose and λ, the posterior probability that that dose is optimal.

Results

The study selection process is shown in the PRISMA 2020 flow diagram (eResult 1). Studies identified after 16 June 2020 but excluded at full-text review, together with reasons for exclusion, are listed in eResult 2. The updated search did not identify any additional eligible fixed-dose trials beyond those captured in the prior review, suggesting that the fixed-dose randomized evidence base has remained largely unchanged since June 2020.

Study selection and characteristics

A total of 48 RCTs, comprising 4,964 participants, were included in the analysis. Individual study characteristics are summarized in eResult 3. The mean age of participants was 11.0 years (SD = 2.3), and the median publication year was 2002. A majority of the trials (n = 38, 79%) were conducted in the United States, and 26% of the participants were female. Regarding the medications studied, 37 trials (77%) investigated MPH only, 7 (15%) investigated AMP only, and 4 (8%) included both. The trial designs included 31 crossover and 17 parallel-group trials. The average trial duration was 3.1 weeks. Overall, 16 trials (33%) were assessed as having a low risk of bias. Seven (15%) trials combined pharmacotherapy with psychotherapy, and in 12 trials (25%) the outcome assessor was a clinician. The analysis included a total of 152 treatment arms. Of these, 150 provided data on efficacy, and 48 on dropouts due to side effects. Network plots for efficacy and dropouts due to side effects, are presented in eResult 4–5.

Efficacy and dropout analysis

Analysis of efficacy outcomes revealed an inverted U-shaped dose-response curve for both medications (Fig. 1). Efficacy for MPH peaked at approximately 45 mg/day, while for AMP the peak was observed near 25 mg/day; beyond these thresholds, the efficacy curves trended downwards. In contrast, the probability of dropout due to adverse events increased monotonically with dose for both MPH and AMP (Fig. 2), anchored to the observed pooled dropout rate of 1% in the placebo arms. Regarding rank order, MPH 45 mg showed the highest estimated efficacy, followed by AMP 25 mg and MPH 50 mg (Table 1). Conversely, the highest rates of side effect-related dropout were estimated for AMP 40 mg, followed by MPH 60 mg and AMP 35 mg (Table 1). Model selection for the dropout outcome indicated that the simpler linear model was sufficient (eResult 6). Complete arm-level outcomes are reported in eResult 7.

Meta-regression and sensitivity analysis

Studies using weight-based dosing were associated with a significantly larger effect size compared with fixed-dose strategies (coefficient = 0.24; 95% CrI, 0.06 to 0.41). Teacher ratings were associated with greater treatment effects than clinician ratings (coefficient = 0.26; 95% CrI, 0.08 to 0.44). Conversely, study duration showed a negative association with treatment efficacy; for every additional week of follow-up, the SMD decreased by 0.05 (95% CrI, − 0.08 to − 0.03). Full parameter estimates are provided in eResult 10. Sensitivity analyses that (1) restricted the sample to studies with a low risk of bias and (2) excluded trials with co-administered psychotherapy both yielded dose-response estimates consistent with the primary analysis (eResult 11–13).

Net clinical benefit analysis

Net clinical benefit curves for the evaluated risk-preference scenarios were all inverted U-shaped (Fig. 3). Detailed curves for absolute risk reduction and absolute risk increase are provided in eResult 14–15. Across all plausible risk preferences, amphetamines generally achieved a higher maximal net clinical benefit than methylphenidate (eResult 16).

As risk aversion increased, the corresponding optimal dose ranges decreased accordingly (Fig. 4 and eResult 17). For individuals with neutral risk preferences (λ = 3–5), the optimal dose ranges were 18–46 mg/day for MPH (peak benefit at 30–39 mg/day) and 12–26 mg/day for AMP (peak at 18–20 mg/day). For those with more risk-tolerant preferences (λ = 1–3), the optimal ranges shifted upward to 30–49 mg/day for MPH (peak at 40–44 mg/day) and 16–37 mg/day for AMP (peak at 20–23 mg/day). Conservative preferences (λ > 5) yielded ranges of 12–41 mg/day for MPH (peak at 18–29 mg/day) and 8–23 mg/day for AMP (peak at 14–18 mg/day). Heatmap visualization revealed the probability of being the optimal dose was more widely dispersed for MPH, whereas it was more concentrated within a narrow range for AMP (eResult 18). The pattern of higher maximal net clinical benefit for AMP, along with the estimated optimal dosing ranges, remained robust across alternative response definitions (eResult 19–22).

Discussion

This study combined dose-response network meta-analysis with NCB framework for children and adolescents with ADHD. By synthesizing direct and indirect evidence, we quantified the trade-offs between symptom reduction and tolerability for MPH and AMP across their therapeutic dose ranges.

Our findings refine the comparative effectiveness profile of stimulants. Previous network meta-analyses have suggested that amphetamines are, on average, slightly more efficacious than methylphenidate but less well tolerated [10, 32]. Our analysis extends this evidence by showing how these benefit-risk trade-offs change across the dose range and under different assumptions about risk tolerance in trial data. Across the clinically relevant dose range and within the explored values of λ, net clinical benefit tended to favor AMP over MPH, driven by higher efficacy in the AMP dose range where adverse-event-related dropout remained relatively low. We also found that efficacy did not plateau as early as previously reported (around 20 mg/day for AMP and 30 mg/day for MPH) [7], but continued to increase up to approximately 25 mg/day for AMP and 45 mg/day for MPH, suggesting that the ceiling for potential benefit may be higher than previously estimated.

Meta-regression suggested that trials using weight-based dosing regimens showed larger effect sizes, supporting the view that adjusting doses for body weight improves exposure and symptomatic benefit [32–35]. Longer study duration was associated with smaller effects, consistent with previous reports of waning treatment benefit [36]. Trials using teacher ratings also showed larger effects than those using clinician ratings, suggesting that the assessment context may influence observed effect sizes [37].

These quantitative estimates can support communication and shared decision-making [38, 39]. By visualizing the trade-offs, clinicians can align dosing strategies with family preferences regarding risk tolerance. We propose that the identified optimal ranges may serve as Bayesian anchors for titration rather than rigid targets. The lower bound of the optimal range could be considered as an evidence-informed initial target dose, and the dose at the peak of the net benefit curve as a reference for maximal expected benefit. Our results also inform medication management. The comparative dose-response curves may offer a quantitative starting point for approximating equipotent doses when switching between stimulants.

Limitations

First, the dose equivalency conversions are based on pharmacokinetic, pharmacodynamic, and trial data, but significant inter-individual variability may exist. Second, net clinical benefit depends on the risk-preference parameter λ, which represents value judgments rather than a fixed biological constant. This is a feature of decision-analytic synthesis, because benefit-harm trade-offs vary across real-world settings. We therefore specified a clinically plausible range of λ values, reported how results change across this spectrum. Third, the network was restricted to short-term fixed-dose monotherapy trials, most of which were conducted in the United States, which may limit generalizability to routine clinical practice that relies on flexible titration and combination regimens. Finally, we were unable to formally quantify reporting biases or grade the certainty of the evidence using standard frameworks, because these approaches are not yet well validated for this type of analysis. Future trials should collect individual-level longitudinal data across dose levels, ideally under standardized titration algorithms and with prospective assessment of treatment preferences.

Conclusions

By combining dose-response network meta-analysis with a net clinical benefit framework, this study integrates efficacy and tolerability across stimulant treatment options for children and adolescents with ADHD into a preference-sensitive metric. Our findings translate trial data into clinically interpretable dose ranges and estimate of equipotent doses that can serve as quantitative anchors for titration and switching. These tools may help move stimulant prescribing from trial-and-error toward more transparent, patient-centered, and evidence-informed decision making.
