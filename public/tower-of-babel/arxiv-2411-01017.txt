Definability and Scott rank in Separable Metric Structures
Diego Bejarano

Abstract
. We give a notion of Scott rank for separable metric structures based on the definability of the (metric closures of) automorphism orbits in continuous infinitary logic. This is a continuous analogue of work of Montalbán for countable structures. In the process, we prove some results concerning definability, type omitting, and back-and-forth for metric structures.



1.
Introduction

In
[
Sco65
]
, Scott shows that the infinitary logic
\mathcal{L}_{\omega_{1},\omega}
is strong enough to classify countable structures in a particular language up to isomorphism. In particular, for every countable structure
\mathcal{A}
, there is an
\mathcal{L}_{\omega_{1},\omega}
sentence
\varphi
such that for any other countable structure
\mathcal{B}
in the same language,
\mathcal{A}\cong\mathcal{B}
if, and only if,
\mathcal{B}\models\varphi
. In relation to this result, many notions of Scott rank have been proposed, which aim to give a notion of complexity to countable structures. Of particular note are the notions introduced by Montalbán in
[
Mon15
]
, but best reference in Montalbán’s book
[
Mon21
]
: the Scott rank of a countable structure
\mathcal{A}
is the least ordinal
\alpha
such that the automorphism orbit of every finite tuple of elements of
\mathcal{A}
is
\Sigma_{\alpha}
in
\mathcal{L}_{\omega_{1},\omega}
without parameters. Equivalently, it is the least ordinal
\alpha
such that
\mathcal{A}
has a
\Pi_{\alpha+1}
Scott sentence. We refer the reader to
[
Mon15
, Section 3.1]
for a brief summary of the history of Scott rank.

Scott’s original result was generalized to separable metric structures in
[
Ben+17
]
using a continuous analogue of Scott’s back-and-forth relations, which focuses on how complex are the formulas that define automorphism orbits inside a countable structure. The purpose of this work is to generalize Montalbán’s notion of Scott rank to separable metric structures. To do this, we investigate the definable sets (without parameters) in separable metric structures to obtain the following results:

Theorem
.

Let
{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}
be a separable metric structure,
n
a positive integer, and
\bar{a}\in{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}^{n}
:

(1)

The (metric closure of) the automorphism orbit of
\bar{a}
in
{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}
is definable (without parameters) by an infinitary continuous formula (
Theorem

5.2
).

(2)

Therefore, a nonempty subset of
A^{n}
is definable (without parameters) by an infinitary continuous formula if, and only if, it is metric-closed and automorphism-invariant (
Corollary

5.3
).

(3)

Moreover, under some mild assumptions, the Scott sentence of
{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}
can be constructed in a uniform way from these definitions (
Theorem

5.5
).

The main tool used in these results is a metric version of the back-and-forth process, which we develop in Section 4 to characterize isomorphisms of separable metric structures. Using a notion of quantifier complexity defined in Section 2, we define a notion of Scott rank in Section 6 and show that this version of Scott rank satisfies a notion of Robustness similar to the discrete case. However, due to some subtleties with our type-omitting theorem in Section 3, we can only show that the existence of a Scott sentence bounds the Scott rank at limit ordinals (the details are found in Section 6).

This paper is organizes as follows: Section 2 introduces the main definition of continuous infinitary logic, and we show basic results about definability and types. Section 3 proves uses a continuous analogue of Makkai’s consistency properties to show a Model Existence Theory and a weaker analogue of Montalbán’s type omitting theorem. Section 4 defines back-and-forth sets and shows that they corresponds to isomorphisms. Section 5 combines the work of the previous sections to show (the metric closures of) automorphism orbits are definable and builds a Scott predicate using these definitions. In Section 6 we put everything together to show some properties of the Scott rank.

2.
Continuous Infinitary Logic

In this section we introduce the basic notions of continuous infinitary logic, first defined by Ben Yaacov and Iovino in
[
BI09
]
. Further work on the topic can be found in
[
Eag14
]
,
[
Ben+17
]
and
[
HMT23
]
. This logic is ‘continuous’ in the sense that our formulas will be interpreted as uniformly continuous functions into the real numbers, and ‘infinitary’ in that we will add the connectives
\sup_{n}
and
\inf_{n}
, which are interpreted as taking the supremum (resp. infimum) of a countable set of formulas when certain conditions are met. Our definitions of the logic are essentially those of
[
HMT23
]
.

Throughout most of this paper,
\sup_{x}
acts as the continuous analogue of the universal quantifier, and
\sup_{n}
acts as the analogue of conjunction. Similarly,
\inf_{x}
plays the role of the existential quantifier, and
\inf_{n}
the role of countable disjunction. This is because we will be interested primarily in conditions of the form
``\varphi<r,"
where
\sup
and
\inf
do take these prescribed roles. However, this convention no longer holds if we look at expressions of the form
``\varphi>r"
. Therefore, to avoid confusion, we will not use
\land
,
\lor
,
\bigwedge
or
\bigvee
going forward. We begin with the notion of a vocabulary (sometimes also called a signature):

Definition 2.1
.

By a continuous vocabulary
\tau
, we mean a family of formal symbols. For each symbol
S\in\tau
we specify:

(1)

its kind: either predicate or function. This determines what kind of object its interpretation should be;

(2)

its arity: a number
n_{S}
, which determines how many arguments
S
takes;

(3)

a function
\Delta_{S}
, which is a modulus of continuity of arity
n_{S}
, and we require that any interpretation of
S
respects this modulus. We will elaborate on this point below;

(4)

If
S
is a predicate symbol, we also specify a compact subset of
I_{S}
of
\mathbb{R}
. We require that that any interpretation of
S
has its image contained in this subset.

In this setup, a constant will be a zero-ary function. We assume that
\tau
always includes a binary predicate symbol
d
, with
\Delta_{d}(r_{1},r_{2})=r_{1}+r_{2}
.

The modulus of continuity is meant as a guarantee from the language that the interpretation of the symbol
S
will be continuous (and how continuous). For predicates,
\Delta_{S}
and
I_{S}
together guarantee that the interpretation of
S
will be a uniformly continuous function. We now give the formal definition:

Definition 2.2
.

(1)

A modulus of continuity of arity
n
is a continuous function
\Delta:[0,\infty)^{n}\to[0,\infty)
such that
\Delta(0)=0
and

\Delta(\overline{r})\leq\Delta(\overline{r}+\overline{s})\leq\Delta(\overline{r})+\Delta(\overline{s}),

for all
\overline{r},\overline{s}\in[0,\infty)^{n}
. We say that
\Delta
is faithful if
\Delta(\overline{r})=0
implies
\overline{r}=\overline{0}
.

(2)

Given a metric space
(X,d_{X})
, and
\Delta
a modulus of continuity of arity
n
. We define a pseudo-distance
d_{\Delta}
on
X^{n}
by letting
d_{\Delta}(\overline{x},\overline{y})=\Delta(d_{X}(x_{i},y_{i})\mid i<n)
. If
\Delta
is faithful,
d_{\Delta}
is a distance function on
X^{n}
.

(3)

We say that a function
S:(X,d_{X})^{n}\to(Z,d_{Z})
between metric spaces respects
\Delta
if for every
\overline{x},\overline{y}\in X^{n}
we have that
d_{Z}(S(\overline{x}),S(\overline{y}))\leq d_{\Delta}(\overline{x},\overline{y})
.

We can now define the notion of a metric
\tau
-structure:

Definition 2.3
.

A metric
\tau
-structure
{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}
consists of a set
A
, known as the domain of the structure, alongside interpretations of all the symbols in
\tau
such that:

(1)

each predicate symbol
P\in\tau
interprets a bounded, uniformly continuous function
P:A^{n_{P}}\to\mathbb{R}
that respects
\Delta_{P}
and whose image is contained in
I_{P}
;

(2)

d
interprets a distance on
A
such that
(A,d)
is a complete, bounded metric space;

(3)

each function symbol
f\in\tau
interprets a continuous function
f:A^{n_{f}}\to A
that respects the modulus
\Delta_{F}
.

Example 2.4
.

a
)

If
B
is the unit ball of a Banach space
X
over
\mathbb{R}
or
\mathbb{C}
. We can name the point
0_{X}\in B
, and the norm
||\cdot||:B\to[0,1]
as a predicate. We can also add functions
f_{\alpha,\beta}(x,y)=\alpha x+\beta y
for each pair of scalars with
|\alpha|+|\beta|\leq 1
. Alternatively, there is way of treating all of
X
as a metric structure by using countably many sorts to name the ball of radius
n
centered at
0_{X}\in B
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
. To capture the structure of a
C^{*}
algebra, we can include multiplication and the
∗
-map as functions.

b
)

Given a probability space
(X,\mathcal{B},\mu)
, we can construct a metric structure as follows: let
A
be the result of quietening
\mathcal{B}
by all the sets of measure zero, and we take the distance
d
to be the measure of the symmetric difference between two sets. We can also add functions for the Boolean operations
\land
(meet),
\lor
(join), and complement. As distinguished elements we can name
0
and
1
in
A
.

c
)

Metric structures also extend all classical first-order structures. Given a first-order structure
M
, we can turn it into a metric structure by considering the discrete metric (
d(a,b)=1
if
a\neq b
and zero otherwise). Any function is uniformly continuous with respect to this metric, and we capture the relations on
M
by thinking about them as maps
R:M^{n}\to[0,1]
.

The notions of substructure, extension, reduct, etc., are defined in the natural way. We now define the language of continuous infinitary logic:

Definition 2.5
.

Suppose that
\tau
is a continuous vocabulary, we define the language
L^{\mathbb{R}}_{\omega_{1},\omega}(\tau)
recursively as follows. We start by fixing a set
\{x_{i}\mid i\in\omega\}
of distinct variable symbols, then:

(1)

each
x_{i}
is a
\tau
-term that respects the
\mathbb{N}
-modulus
\Delta_{x_{i}}(r_{0},r_{1},\dotsc)=r_{i}
(we could instead ask that it respects the
n
-ary modulus
\Delta_{x_{i},n}(r_{0},r_{1},\dotsc,r_{n})=r_{i}
for every
n\geq i
,);

(2)

if
t_{0},\dotsc,t_{n-1}
are terms and
f\in\tau
is a function symbol with
n_{f}=n
, then
f(t_{0},\dotsc,t_{n-1})
is a
\tau
-term that respects the modulus
\Delta_{f}\circ(\Delta_{t_{i}}\mid i<n)
;

(3)

a
\tau
-atomic formula is an expression of the form
P(t_{1},\dotsc,t_{n_{P}})
where
P\in\tau
is a predicate symbol and
t_{1},\dotsc,t_{n_{P}}
are
\tau
-terms. A
\tau
-atomic formula respects the bound
I_{p}
and the modulus
\Delta_{P}\circ(\Delta_{t_{i}}\mid i<n)
;

(4)

the zero-ary functions
\mathbf{0}
and
\mathbf{1}
are
\tau
-formulas with bounds
I_{\mathbf{0}}=\{0\}
and
I_{\mathbf{1}}=\{1\}
;

(5)

If
\varphi
and
\psi
are
\tau
-formulas, then so are
\varphi+\psi
,
\max(\varphi,\psi)
,
\min(\varphi,\psi)
, and
q\varphi
for all
q\in\mathbb{Q}
. The corresponding bounds and moduli are easily computable from those of
\varphi
and
\psi
. These are the only connectives we allow;

(6)

If
\varphi(x_{0},\dotsc,x_{n-1})
is a
\tau
-formula, then
\sup_{x_{i}}\varphi(x_{0},\dotsc,x_{n-1})
and
\inf_{x_{i}}\varphi(x_{0},\dotsc,x_{n-1})
are also
\tau
-formulas for
i<n
. These formulas respect
I_{\varphi}
and the
(n-1)
-ary modulus

\hat{\Delta}_{\varphi}(r_{0},\dotsc,r_{n-2})=\Delta_{\varphi}(r_{0},\dotsc,r_{i-1},0,r_{i+1},\dotsc,r_{n-2});

(7)

If
\{\varphi_{n}\mid n\in\omega\}
is a countable set of
\tau
-formulas all of whose free variables are contained in a finite set of variables
\overline{x}
, and there is a modulus
\Delta
and a compact set
I\subset\mathbb{R}
such that every
\varphi_{i}
respects both
\Delta
and
I
. Then
\sup_{n}\varphi_{n}
and
\inf_{n}\varphi_{n}
are
\tau
-formulas with free variables
\overline{x}
respecting
\Delta
and
I
;

Then
L^{\mathbb{R}}_{\omega_{1},\omega}(\tau)
is the set of all
\tau
-formulas. A
\tau
formula is quantifier free if it is built using only steps (1)-(5). Similarly, a formula is finitary or
L^{\mathbb{R}}_{\omega,\omega}(\tau)
if it is built using only steps (1)-(6). We can also give the natural definitions of free variable and sentence. There is also a natural notion of equality for formulas:

Definition 2.6
.

We say that two
\tau
-formulas
\varphi(\bar{x})
and
\psi(\bar{x})
are logically equivalent, and write
\varphi(\bar{x})=\psi(\bar{x})
, if
\varphi^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}(\bar{a})=\psi^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}(\bar{a})
for every
\tau
-structure
{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}
and every
\bar{a}\in{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}^{|\bar{x}|}
.

Remark 2.7
.

(1)

In
L^{\mathbb{R}}_{\omega_{1},\omega}(\tau)
,
\mathbb{R}
refers to the fact that we are in a continuous setting,
\omega
is a bound on the number of free variables in any formula (as in every formula has less than
\omega
many free variables), and
\omega_{1}
is a strict bound on the number of formulas we can
\sup
or
\inf
at once.

(2)

Note the additional requirements in point (7) of
Definition

2.5
. In particular, we can only take a countable supremum or infimum of a set of formulas if all the formulas satisfy the same modulus of continuity. We will deal with this requirement in the next subsection.

(3)

The lattice version of the Stone-Weierstrass Theorem shows that our connectives are dense in the set of all continuous connectives as defined in
[
Ben+17
]
. Moreover, as we have access to countable supremum and infimum, we also have access to
\limsup
and
\liminf
, so any continuous connective is expressible in our language using two infinitary qualifiers.

(4)

We will often use the connectives

\varphi\ \dot{-}\ \psi=\max(\varphi-\psi,0)

|\varphi|=\max(\varphi,0)+(-1)\min(\varphi,0);

(5)

We should also mention a peculiar fact about continuous infinitary logic mentioned in Remark 2.4 of
[
HMT23
]
: continuous infinitary logic is not closed under substituting constants for variables.

Another feature of this language is that every formula is equivalent to one in prenex normal form, i.e. a formula obtained from a quantifier-free formula by only using quantifiers:

Lemma 2.8
.

Every
\tau
-formula is equivalent to a
\tau
-formula in prenex normal form.

Proof.

Fix
\tau
-formulas
\varphi(\overline{x},y)
and
\psi(\overline{x})
be
\tau
-formulas. Without loss of generality, assume that
y
does not occur in
\psi
. Then

\displaystyle(\sup_{y}\varphi)+\psi

\displaystyle=\sup_{y}(\varphi+\psi)

\displaystyle\max(\sup_{y}\varphi,\psi)

\displaystyle=\sup_{y}\max(\varphi,\psi)

\displaystyle\min(\sup_{y}\varphi,\psi)

\displaystyle=\sup_{y}\min(\varphi,\psi)

Multiplication by rationals is a bit more complicated. For
q\in\mathbb{Q}
:

q(\sup_{y}\varphi)=\begin{cases}\sup_{y}q\varphi&\text{if $q>0$}\\
\inf_{y}q\varphi&\text{if $q<0$}\\
\mathbf{0}&\text{if $q=0$}\\
\end{cases}

The situation for
\inf
is analogous. Thus, by induction on the complexity of formulas, we can always write an equivalent formula in prenex normal form. This completes the proof.
∎

As in
[
Mar16
]
, we use
\sim\varphi
as a shorthand for applying the continuous and infinitary versions of DeMorgan’s Laws to a formula in prenex normal form. That is, moving a factor of
-1
through all the quantifiers, turning
\sup
into
\inf
and vise-versa in the process.

Definition 2.9

(Truth in a Model)
.

An easy induction argument shows that if
{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}
is a metric
\tau
-structure, and
\varphi(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{n})
is a
\tau
-formula, then
\varphi
is interpreted in
{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}
as a uniformly continuous function
\varphi^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}:A^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
. If
\varphi
is a sentence (i.e. it has no free variables), we consider the value of
\varphi^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}\in\mathbb{R}
as a measure of how true (or really false)
\varphi
is in
{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}
. We will follow the convention that
0
(or more generally
\min I_{\varphi}
) corresponds to true.

Finally, we note the following result from
[
Ben+17
]
:

Lemma 2.10

(
[
Ben+17
]
Lemma 2.4)
.

(1)

We can define a seminorm on the set of formulas given by

\lVert\varphi(\bar{x})\rVert=\sup\{|\varphi^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}(\bar{a})|\mid{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}\ \text{is a metric $\tau$-structure and $\bar{a}\in A^{|\bar{x}|}$}\}

and
\lVert\varphi(\bar{x})\rVert\in I_{\varphi}
;

(2)

If
|\tau|\leq\kappa
, for some infinite cardinal
\kappa
, then the space of
L^{\mathbb{R}}_{\omega,\omega}(\tau)
formulas equipped with this norm has density character
\leq\kappa
.

2.1.
Weak Modulus of Continuity

First introduced in
[
Ben+17
]
, weak moduli of continuity allow us to specify a large and rich enough subset of formulas from the language where we may take countable suprema and infima freely without worrying about the continuity, without losing expressive power.

Definition 2.11
.

A
weak modulus of continuity
is a function
\Omega:[0,\infty)^{\omega}\to[0,\infty]
that satisfies:
\Omega(\overline{0})=0
;

\Omega(\overline{r})\leq\Omega(\overline{r}+\overline{s})\leq\Omega(\overline{r})+\Delta(\overline{s})

for all
\overline{r},\overline{s}\in[0,\infty)^{\omega}
;
\Omega
is lower-semicontinuous in the product topology (i.e. the premiages of rays
(y,\infty]
are open), and continuous in each argument.

We say that a
\tau
-formula
\varphi
of arity
n
respects
\Omega
if it respects
\Omega|_{n}
as a modulus of continuity.

Fixing a weak modulus of continuity
\Omega
is equivalent to fixing a coherent sequence of moduli
\Omega|_{n}
for each arity:

Lemma 2.12

(
[
Ben+17
]
Lemma 2.3)
.

Let
\Omega:[0,\infty)^{\omega}\to[0,\infty]
be a weak modulus of continuity, then, for every
n\geq 1
, the truncations
\Omega|_{n}
is an
n
-ary modulus of continuity and
\Omega(x_{0},x_{1}\dotsc)=\sup_{n}\Omega|_{n}(x_{0},\dotsc,x_{n-1})
.

As in Section 5 of
[
Ben+17
]
, our focus in the later sections will be on universal weak-moduli: a weak-modulus such that every quantifier-free formula satisfies
\Omega
after adding some number of initial dummy variables.

Definition 2.13

(c.f.
[
Ben+17
]
Definition 5.1)
.

Suppose
\tau
is a countable continuous vocabulary. A weak modulus
\Omega
is universal for
L=L^{\mathbb{R}}_{\omega_{1},\omega}(\tau)
if it satisfies the following conditions:

(i)

For every atomic formula
\varphi(x_{0},\dotsc,x_{k-1})
, there is an
N<\omega
such that

\Delta_{\varphi}(r_{0},\dotsc,r_{k-1})\leq\Omega|_{N}(0,\dotsc,0,r_{0},\dotsc,r_{k-1});

(ii)

For every
k<\omega
and every
M>0
, there is an
N<\omega
such that

M\cdot\Omega|_{k}(r_{0},\dotsc,r_{k-1})\leq\Omega|_{N}(0,\dotsc,0,r_{0},\dotsc,r_{k-1});

(iii)

For every increasing sequence
i_{0}<i_{1}<\dotsc
, and every
\overline{r}\in[0,\infty)^{\omega}
, let
\overline{s}(i_{k})=\overline{r}(k)
and
\overline{s}(k)=0
otherwise. Then
\Omega(\overline{r})\leq\Omega(\overline{s});

(iv)

For every
k,n<\omega
,

\Omega|_{k}(r_{0},\dotsc,r_{k-1})+\Omega|_{n}(s_{0},\dotsc,s_{n-1})\leq\Omega|_{k+n}(r_{0},\dotsc,r_{k-1},s_{0},\dotsc,s_{n-1}).

(v)

For every
k<\omega
, let
d_{k}(x_{0},\dotsc,x_{2k-1})=\max_{i<k}d(x_{i},x_{k+i})
. Then
\Delta_{d_{k}}\leq\Omega|_{2k}
.

Lemma 2.14

(c.f.
[
Ben+17
]
Propositions 5.2)
.

Let
\Omega
be a universal weak modulus for
L
, then

(i)

For every atomic formula
\varphi(x_{0},\dotsc,x_{k-1})
, there is an
N<\omega
such that

\psi(x_{0},\dotsc,x_{N-1})=\varphi(x_{N-k},\dotsc,x_{N-1})
respects
\Omega
;

(ii)

For every formula
\varphi(x_{0},\dotsc,x_{k-1})
that respects
\Omega
and every
M>0
, there is an
N<\omega
such that
\psi(x_{0},\dotsc,x_{N-1})=M\varphi(x_{N-k},\dotsc,x_{N-1})
respects
\Omega
;

(iii)

For every
M>0
and every atomic formula
\varphi(x_{0},\dotsc,x_{k-1})
, there is an

such that for every increasing sequence
\sigma\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
with

,
\psi(x_{0},\dotsc,x_{\max\sigma})=M\cdot\varphi(x_{\sigma(0)},\dotsc,x_{\sigma(k-1)})
is a quantifier-free

\Omega
;

(iv)

For all tuples
i_{0}<\dotsc<i_{n-1}<j_{0}<\dotsc<j_{n-1}

respects
\Omega
. Moreover,

\max_{k<n}d(x_{i_{k}},x_{j_{k}})\leq d^{\Omega|_{n}}((x_{i_{0}},\dotsc,x_{i_{n-1}}),(x_{j_{0}},\dotsc,x_{j_{n-1}}))

.

Our definition of a universal weak modulus of continuity is largely the same as in
[
Ben+17
]
. We add
(v)
to
Definition

2.13
, which is compatible with the original definition, and allows for some compatibility with the sup metric.

Lemma 2.15

(c.f.
[
Ben+17
]
Proposition 5.3)
.

Suppose
\tau
is a countable continuous vocabulary. Then there exists a weak modulus
\Omega
that is universal for
L=L^{\mathbb{R}}_{\omega_{1},\omega}(\tau)
.

Proof.

Fix an enumeration
\{\varphi_{2k+1}\}_{k\in\omega}
of all atomic formulas in
L
, and let

\varphi_{2k}=d_{k}(x_{0},\dotsc,x_{2k-1})=\max_{i<k}d(x_{i},x_{k+i}).

Then, with
\Delta_{\varphi_{0}}=id
, we write

\Omega_{U}(L)(r_{0},r_{1},r_{2},\dotsc)=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}(i+1)\cdot\sup_{k\leq i}\Delta_{\varphi_{k}}(r_{i},\dotsc,r_{i})

is a universal modulus. Note that
\Omega
has range in
[0,\infty]
, so the infinite sum might not converge.
∎

Remark 2.16
.

There is one important caveat not present in
[
Ben+17
]
: since we put a restriction on the connectives we are allowed to use, it is possible that the
d_{\Omega|_{n}}
are no longer quantifier-free, but instead are the uniform limit of quantifier-free formulas. However, as these formulas will be our distances for the Cartesian powers, we will treat them as quantifier free formulas. One way to do this formally is to expand the language by adding a predicate for each of the distances
d_{\Omega|_{n}}
. Each
\tau
-structure has a unique expansion to this new language and
\Omega
is a universal weak-modulus for the new language.

2.2.
Quantifier complexity

Definition 2.17
.

Let
\varphi(x_{1},\dotsc,x_{n})
be a
\tau
-formula,
\Omega
a weak-modulus of continuity, and
I\subset\mathbb{R}
compact. We say that
\varphi
is a weak-
(\Omega,I)
-formula if it respects
\Omega|_{n}
as a uniform modulus of continuity and its image is contained in
I
.

This definition is a less restrictive class than that defined in Section 2.2 of
[
Ben+17
]
. However, we believe it is the correct notion to analyze definability.

Having a notion of prenex normal form allows us to define the quantifier complexity of a formula as follows:

Definition 2.18
.

Let
\alpha
be an ordinal. We recursively define the quantifier complexity of a
\tau
-formula
\varphi
as follows:

(1)

If
\varphi(\overline{x})
is a quantifier-free
\tau
-formula, then
\varphi(\overline{x})
is a
\sup^{0}=\inf^{0}
formula.

(2)

\varphi(\overline{x})
is an
\inf^{\alpha}
formula if it is logically equivalent to one of the form
\inf_{n}\inf_{\overline{y}_{n}}\psi_{n}(\overline{x},\overline{y}_{n})
where each
\psi_{n}(\overline{x},\overline{y}_{n})
is
\sup^{\beta}
formula for some
\beta<\alpha
.

(3)

\varphi(\overline{x})
is a
\sup^{\alpha}
formula if is logically equivalent to one of the form
\sup_{n}\sup_{\overline{y}_{n}}\psi_{n}(\overline{x},\overline{y}_{n})
where each
\psi_{n}(\overline{x},\overline{y}_{n})
is
\inf^{\beta}
formula for some
\beta<\alpha
.

By induction on complexity, we get that the quantifier complexity of every
\tau
-formula is a countable ordinal.

Lemma 2.19
.

For any weak-
(\Omega,I)
-formulas
\varphi_{n}(\bar{x},\bar{y})
we have

\psi(\bar{x})=\inf_{\bar{y}}\inf_{n}\varphi_{n}(\bar{x},\bar{y})=\inf_{n}\inf_{\bar{y}}\varphi_{n}(\bar{x},\bar{y}).

(i.e. we can change the order of the quantifiers up to logical equivalence). Moreover,

(1)

the formulas
\varphi^{\bar{y}}_{n}(\bar{x})=\inf_{\bar{y}}\varphi_{n}(\bar{x},\bar{y})
are weak
(\Omega,I)
-formulas, and thus so is
\psi(\bar{x})
;

(2)

if
\varphi_{n}(\bar{x},\bar{y})
is
\inf^{\alpha}
, for
\alpha>0
, then so is
\varphi^{\bar{y}}_{n}(\bar{x})
. In particular, if every
\varphi_{n}(\bar{x},\bar{y})
is
\inf^{\alpha}
, for
\alpha>0
, then so is
\psi(\bar{x})
.

A similar result holds when
\inf
is replaced for
\sup
everywhere.

Proof.

Fix a metric
\tau
-structure
{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}
,
\bar{a}\in{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}^{|\bar{x}|}
, and some real
t\in I
. Suppose that

(\inf_{\bar{y}}\inf_{n}\varphi_{n}(\bar{a},\bar{y}))^{{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}}<t.

Then, there is some
\bar{b}\in{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}^{|\bar{y}|}
and some
n<\omega
such that
\varphi_{n}(\bar{a},\bar{b})^{{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}}<t
. Therefore,

(\inf_{n}\inf_{\bar{y}}\varphi_{n}(\bar{a},\bar{y}))^{{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}}\leq\varphi_{n}(\bar{a},\bar{b})^{{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}}<t.

The other direction follows with the same proof.

The first moreover statement follows from the rules for constructing formulas, and the fact that the countable infimum of weak-
(\Omega,I)
-formulas is a weak-
(\Omega,I)
-formula. The second moreover statement follows from the definition of
\inf^{\alpha}
formulas.
∎

2.3.
Definable Predicates

Definition 2.20
.

Let
{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}
be a metric
\tau
-structure. A
definable predicate
for
{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}
is a function
P:A^{n}\to I
, for some closed interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
, such that
P
is the uniform limit of
\tau
-formulas. We say that
P
is definable over
B\subset A
if
P
is the uniform limit of
\tau
-formulas with parameters from
B
. Furthermore, we say that
P
is a weakly-
(\Omega,I)
if it satisfies
\Omega
as a weak modulus of continuity, and its image is contained in
I
.

We want to extend our notions of complexity to definable predicates. Doing so necessitates the following lemma:

Lemma 2.21
.

Fix a weak modulus of continuity
\Omega
, and a closed interval
I=[I_{\min},I_{\max}]\subset\mathbb{R}
. Let
P:A^{n}\to I
be a weakly-
(\Omega,I)
definable predicate, and
\alpha>0
. Further suppose that
P
is the uniform limit of
\inf^{\alpha}
-
\tau
-formulas
\{\varphi_{n}(\bar{x})\}_{n<\omega}
. Then
P
is the uniform limit of weak-
(\Omega,I)
-
\inf^{\alpha}
-
\tau
-formulas.

A similar result holds when
\inf
is replaced for
\sup
everywhere.

Proof.

Write

\varphi_{n}^{I}(\bar{x})=\max(I_{\min},\min(I_{\max},\varphi_{n}(\bar{x})))

and

\varphi_{n}^{(\Omega,I)}(\bar{x})=\inf_{\bar{y}}\left(\varphi_{n}^{I}(\bar{y})+d^{\Omega}(\bar{x},\bar{y})\right)

It is clear from construction that
\varphi_{n}^{(\Omega,I)}
has its image contained in
I
, and it is an
\inf^{\alpha}
formula by
Lemma

2.8
and
Lemma

2.19
.

We now show that
\varphi_{n}^{(\Omega,I)}(\bar{x})
satisfies
\Omega
. Indeed, fix
\bar{a},\bar{b}\in A^{\bar{x}}
, and assume without loss of generality that
\varphi_{n}^{(\Omega,I)}(\bar{a})^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}\geq\varphi_{n}^{(\Omega,I)}(\bar{b})^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}
. Thus, it is enough to show that

\varphi_{n}^{(\Omega,I)}(\bar{a})^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}-\varphi_{n}^{(\Omega,I)}(\bar{b})^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}\leq d^{\Omega}(\bar{a},\bar{b})^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}.

Fix
\epsilon>0
, and
\bar{z}\in A^{\bar{x}}
such that

\varphi_{n}^{(\Omega,I)}(\bar{b})^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}\geq\varphi_{n}^{I}(\bar{z})^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}+d^{\Omega}(\bar{b},\bar{z})^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}-\epsilon

which is possible by the definition of
\varphi_{n}^{(\Omega,I)}
. Note that we also have

\varphi_{n}^{(\Omega,I)}(\bar{a})^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}\leq\varphi_{n}^{I}(\bar{z})^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}+d^{\Omega}(\bar{a},\bar{z})^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}

by definition. Now

\displaystyle\varphi_{n}^{(\Omega,I)}(\bar{a})^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}-\varphi_{n}^{(\Omega,I)}(\bar{b})^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}

\displaystyle\leq\varphi_{n}^{(\Omega,I)}(\bar{a})^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}-\varphi_{n}^{I}(\bar{z})^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}-d^{\Omega}(\bar{b},\bar{z})^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}+\epsilon

\displaystyle\leq\varphi_{n}^{I}(\bar{z})^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}+d^{\Omega}(\bar{a},\bar{z})^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}-\varphi_{n}^{I}(\bar{z})^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}-d^{\Omega}(\bar{b},\bar{z})^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}+\epsilon

\displaystyle=d^{\Omega}(\bar{a},\bar{z})^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}-d^{\Omega}(\bar{b},\bar{z})^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}+\epsilon

\displaystyle\leq d^{\Omega}(\bar{a},\bar{b})^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}+d^{\Omega}(\bar{z},\bar{b})^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}-d^{\Omega}(\bar{b},\bar{z})^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}+\epsilon

\displaystyle\leq d^{\Omega}(\bar{a},\bar{b})^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}+\epsilon

and taking
\epsilon\to 0
gives the desired result.

It remains to show that
\varphi_{n}^{(\Omega,I)}(\bar{x})\to P(\bar{x})
uniformly. Fix
\epsilon>0
, and
n<\omega
such that
\left\lVert P-\varphi_{n}\right\rVert_{\infty}<\epsilon
. Note that since the image of
P
is contained in
I
, and
\varphi_{n}
approaches
P
everywhere, we also get that
\left\lVert P-\varphi^{I}_{n}\right\rVert_{\infty}<\epsilon
. The definition of
\varphi_{n}^{(\Omega,I)}
readily gives us that

\varphi_{n}^{(\Omega,I)}(\bar{a})^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}-P(\bar{a})^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}\leq\varphi_{n}^{I}(\bar{a})^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}-P(\bar{a})^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}<\epsilon

for any
\bar{a}\in A^{|\bar{x}|}
.

For the other inequality, we use the same idea as in the previous part of this proof. Take
\bar{z}\in A^{\bar{x}}
such that

\varphi_{n}^{(\Omega,I)}(\bar{a})^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}\geq\varphi_{n}^{I}(\bar{z})^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}+d^{\Omega}(\bar{a},\bar{z})^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}-\epsilon.

Then, using the fact that
P
respects
\Omega
as a modulus of continuity we can reason that

\displaystyle P(\bar{a})^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}-\varphi_{n}^{(\Omega,I)}(\bar{a})^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}

\displaystyle\leq P(\bar{a})^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}-\varphi_{n}^{I}(\bar{z})^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}-d^{\Omega}(\bar{a},\bar{z})^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}+\epsilon

\displaystyle\leq P(\bar{z})^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}+d^{\Omega}(\bar{a},\bar{z})^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}-\varphi_{n}^{I}(\bar{z})^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}-d^{\Omega}(\bar{a},\bar{z})^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}+\epsilon

\displaystyle=P(\bar{z})^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}-\varphi_{n}^{I}(\bar{z})^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}+\epsilon

\displaystyle\leq 2\epsilon

This completes the proof. For the
\sup
case we use

\varphi_{n}^{(\Omega,I)}(\bar{x})=\sup_{\bar{y}}\left(\varphi_{n}^{I}(\bar{y})-d^{\Omega}(\bar{x},\bar{y})\right)

and proceed in a similar manner.
∎

The case
\alpha=0
is a bit more subtle: if a weakly-

\tau
-formulas, then it is both the uniform limit of weak-

-
\inf^{1}
-

-formulas and the uniform limit of weak-
(\Omega,I)
-
\sup^{1}
-
\tau
-formulas. Thus, we could see these predicates as the continuous analogue of
\Delta^{0}_{1}
.

.

For
\alpha>0
, we say that a definable predicate

is weakly-

-

if it is the uniform limit of weak-

-

2.4.
Definable Sets

Starting in Section 4, we will be exploring the quantifier complexity of formulas that define automorphism orbits inside a metric structure. Hence, here we recall the definition of definability in continuous logic, as it is a bit more subtle than what one would expect:

Definition 2.23

(
[
Ben+08
]
)
.

Let
{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}
be a metric
\tau
-structure,
D
a metrically closed subset of
A^{n}
, and
\partial(\bar{x},\bar{y})
a
\tau
-formula that defines a complete, bounded distance on
A^{n}
. We say that
D
is
\partial
-definable with parameters from
B\subset A
if the function

\partial^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}(\overline{x},D):\bar{x}\mapsto\inf_{\bar{a}\in D}\partial(\bar{x},\bar{a})

is an
L(B)
-definable predicate. That is, there are
L(B)
-formulas
\varphi_{n}(\overline{x})
such that
\varphi^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}_{n}(\overline{x})\to\partial^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}(\overline{x},X)
uniformly. Furthermore, for
\alpha
an ordinal, we say that
D
is
(\Omega,\inf^{\alpha})
-definable if the function
d^{\Omega}(\bar{x},D)
is the uniform limit of weak-
(\Omega,\inf^{\alpha})
-
\tau
-formulas.

This notion of definability is stronger than asking that
X
be the zero-set of some formula, but it has a number of natural advantages. We refer the reader to Chapter 9 of
[
Ben+08
]
for the details, but we record the following results as we will use them later:

Lemma 2.24

(Proposition 2.10 of
[
Ben+08
]
)
.

Let
F,G:X\to[0,1]
be arbitrary functions such that

\forall\epsilon>0\ \exists\delta>0\ \forall x\in X\ \left(F(x)\leq\delta\Rightarrow G(x)\leq\epsilon\right).

Then, there exists a continuous, increasing function
\alpha:[0,1]\to[0,1]
such that
\alpha(0)=0
and

\forall x\in X\left(G(x)\leq\alpha(F(X))\right)

Proof.

Let
g:[0,1]\to[0,1]
be a (possibly discontinuous) function defined by

g(t)=\sup\{G(x)\mid F(x)\leq t\}

for
t\in[0,1]
. Note that
g
is an increasing function and that
G(x)\leq g(F(x))
. Also, the assumption on
F
and
G
implies that
g(0)=0
and that
g(t)
converges to
0
as
t\to 0
.

Now we construct an increasing, continuous function
\alpha:[0,1]\to[0,1]
with
\alpha(0)=0
and
g(t)\leq\alpha(t)
as follows: let
(t_{n})_{n\in\omega}
be a decreasing sequence in
[0,1]
with
t_{0}=1
and
\lim_{n\to\infty}t_{n}=0
. Set
\alpha(0)=0
,
\alpha(1)=1
, and
\alpha(t_{n})=g(t_{n-1})
. Finally, take
\alpha
to be linear on each segment of the form
[t_{n},t_{n-1}]
.

By construction,
\alpha
is continuous, as
g
is continuous at
0
. Moreover,
g(t)\leq\alpha(t)
: to see this, note that for all
t\in[0,1]
there is an
n\geq 1
such that
t_{n+1}\leq t\leq t_{n}
, so
\alpha(t)
is a convex combination of
g(t_{n})
and
g(t_{n-1})
, with
g(t)\leq g(t_{n})\leq g(t_{n-1})
. Thus,
g(t)\leq g(t_{n})\leq\alpha(t)
.
∎

This lemma allows us to prove the main result regarding definability:

Theorem 2.25

(Proposition 9.19 of
[
Ben+08
]
)
.

Let
{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}
be a metric
\tau
-structure,
D\subset A^{n}
be a closed, nonempty subset, and
\partial(\bar{x},\bar{y})
a
\tau
-formula that defines a complete, bounded distance on
A^{n}
. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

D
is a
\partial
-definable set;

(2)

There is a definable predicate
P(\bar{x})
that vanishes on
D
, and for every
\epsilon>0
there is a
\delta>0
such that if
P^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}(\bar{x})<\delta
, then
\partial^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}(\bar{x},D)\leq\epsilon
;

(3)

There is a sequence
\{\varphi_{m}\mid m\geq 1\}
of
\tau
-formulas and a sequence
\{\delta_{m}\mid m\geq 1\}
of positive reals such that for all
m\geq 1
and all
\bar{x}\in A^{n}
:

\bar{x}\in D\Rightarrow\varphi_{n}^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}(\bar{x})=0

\varphi_{n}^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}(\bar{x})\leq\delta_{m}\Rightarrow\partial^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}(\bar{x},D)\leq 2^{-m};

(4)

Quantification over
D
is possible: for every
\tau
-formula
\psi(\overline{z},\overline{x})
with
k=|\overline{z}|
and
n=|\overline{x}|
, if the map

\psi(\overline{z},\overline{x}):(A^{k},\rho)\times(A^{n},\partial)\to(\mathbb{R},d_{\text{euclidean}})

is uniformly continuous for some
\tau
-formula
\rho(\overline{z},\overline{w})
that defines a complete, bounded distance on
A^{k}
. Then, there is a definable predicate
Q(\overline{y})
such that for all
\overline{a}\in A^{k}

Q^{{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}}(\overline{a})=\inf_{\overline{b}\in D}\psi^{{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}}(\overline{a},\overline{b})

and similarly for
\sup
.

The same statement holds over parameters.

Proof.

(1\Rightarrow 3)
As
F(\bar{x})=\partial(\bar{x},D)
is a definable predicate, there are
\tau
-formulas
\{\psi_{m}\mid m\geq 1\}
converging uniformly to
P
. We may assume that

|F^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}(\bar{x})-\psi_{m}^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}(\bar{x})|\leq\frac{1}{3}\cdot 2^{-m}

for
\bar{x}\in A^{n}
. Note that if
\bar{x}\in D
, then
F^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}(\bar{x})=0
and thus
\psi_{m}^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}(\bar{x})\leq\frac{1}{3}\cdot 2^{-m}
. Also, if
\psi_{m}^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}(\bar{x})\leq\frac{2}{3}\cdot 2^{-m}
, then

F^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}(\bar{x})\leq\psi_{m}^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}+|P^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}(\bar{x})-\psi_{m}^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}(\bar{x})|\leq\frac{2}{3}\cdot 2^{-m}+\frac{1}{3}\cdot 2^{-m}=2^{-m}.

Thus, the formulas
\varphi_{m}(\bar{x})=\psi_{m}(\bar{x})\ \dot{-}\ \frac{2^{-m}}{3}
satisfy (3) with
\delta_{m}=\frac{2}{3}\cdot 2^{-m}
.

(3\Rightarrow 2)
Let
P(\bar{x})=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}2^{-m}\varphi_{m}(\bar{x})
, which is the uniform limit of the partial sums.

(2\Rightarrow 1)
Without loss of generality, both
P(\bar{x})
and
\partial(\bar{x},D)
have range
[0,1]
. We use
Lemma

2.24
with
F(\bar{x})=P^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}(\bar{x})
and
G(\bar{x})=\partial^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}(\bar{x},D)
. Thus, we get a continuous, increasing function
\alpha:[0,1]\to[0,1]
and
\partial^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}(\bar{x},D)\leq\alpha(P^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}(\bar{x}))
.

Write

H(\bar{x})=\inf_{y}\min(\alpha(P(\bar{x}))+\partial(\bar{x},\bar{y}),1)

and observe that
H(\bar{x})
is a definable predicate. Indeed, if
\{\varphi_{m}\}_{m\in\omega}
is a sequence converging uniformly to
P(\bar{x})
, then the formulas

\psi_{m}(\bar{x})=\inf_{y}\min(\alpha(\varphi_{m}(\bar{x}))+\partial(\bar{x},\bar{y}),1)

converge uniformly to
H(\bar{x})
. We now claim that
H^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}(\bar{x})=\partial^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}(\bar{x},D)
for all
\bar{x}\in A^{n}
.

First, if
\bar{y}\in D
, then
P^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}(\bar{y})=0
and

H^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}(\bar{x})\leq\min(\alpha(0)+\partial(\bar{x},\bar{y}),1)=d^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}(\bar{x},\bar{y})

and so
H^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}(\bar{x})\leq d^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}(\bar{x},D)
. Conversely, recall that
\partial^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}(\bar{x},D)\leq\alpha(P^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}(\bar{x}))
, so we have

\displaystyle H(\bar{x})

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{y}\min(\partial(\bar{x},D)+\partial(\bar{x},\bar{y}),1)

\displaystyle\geq\min(d(\bar{x},D),1)

\displaystyle=\partial(\bar{x},D)

by our assumption on the range of the metric.

(1\Rightarrow 4)
Without loss of generality, the distances
\partial
and
\rho
have range in
[0,1]
(i.e. the bound is 1). Fist, note that
\psi(\bar{x},\bar{y})
obeys some modulus of continuity

\Delta_{\psi}:[0,\infty)^{2}\to[0,\infty)

so that for every
\bar{z}\in A^{k}
and every
\bar{x},\bar{y}\in A^{n}
we have

|\psi(\bar{z},\bar{x})-\psi(\bar{z},\bar{y})|\leq\Delta_{\psi}(\rho(\bar{z},\bar{z}),\partial(\bar{x},\bar{y}))=\Delta_{\psi}(0,\partial(\bar{x},\bar{y})).

Moreover,
\Delta_{\psi}(0,r)
is an increasing function with range
[0,\Delta_{\psi}(0,1)]
. Therefore,
\Delta_{\psi}(0,r)
is a uniformly continuous function, so it is the uniform limit of the basic connective. Thus,

Q_{d}(\bar{z})=\inf_{\bar{x}}\psi(\bar{z},\bar{x})+\Delta_{\psi}(0,\partial(\bar{x},D))

is a definable predicate. We claim that this is the desired predicate, meaning that
Q_{d}=Q
.

To see this, recall that

\psi(\bar{z},\bar{y})\leq\psi(\bar{z},\bar{x})+\Delta_{\psi}(0,\partial(\bar{x},\bar{y}))

taking the infimum over all
\bar{y}\in D
on both sides, and using the fact that
\Delta_{\psi}(0,r)
is continous and increasing, we get

Q(\bar{z})\leq\psi(\bar{z},\bar{x})+\Delta_{\psi}(0,\partial(\bar{x},D))

for all
\bar{z}\in A^{k}
and
\bar{x}\in A^{n}
. Thus, we can take the infimum over all
\bar{x}\in A^{n}
on the right-hand side and get that
Q\leq Q_{d}
. The other direction is clear since
Q_{d}
is an infimum over a strictly larger collection.

(4\Rightarrow 1)

\partial^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}(\bar{x},D)=\inf_{\bar{a}\in D}\partial^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}(\bar{x},\bar{a})
.
∎

Note the alternation of strict and non-strict inequalities in the second condition. Finally, we record the following characterization of definability communicated to us by Itaï Ben Yaacov:

Lemma 2.26
.

Let
P:A^{n}\to I
be a definable predicate with
\min I=0
. Then
P
defines the
\partial
-distance to a closed, non-empty, set
D\subset A^{n}
(its zero-set) if and only if the following two conditions hold in
{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}
(meaning that they evaluate to 0):

\sup_{\bar{x},\bar{y}}\left(\left(P(\bar{x})\ \dot{-}\ P(\bar{y})\right)\ \dot{-}\ \partial(\bar{x},\bar{y})\right)

\sup_{\bar{x}}\inf_{\bar{y}}\left(P(\bar{y})+(\partial(\bar{x},\bar{y})\ \dot{-}\ P(\bar{x}))\right).

The same statement is true when
P(\bar{x})
has parameters from
{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}
.

Proof.

The first conditions says that

\forall x\forall y\ P(\bar{x})\leq P(\bar{y})+\partial(\bar{x},\bar{y})

meaning that
P
is 1-Lipschitz with respect to
\partial
. The second condition attempts to express that

\forall x\exists y\ P(\bar{y})=0\land\partial(\bar{y},\bar{x})\leq P(\bar{x})

but can only do so approximately: it only requires that for every
\bar{x}\in A^{n}
and every
\epsilon>0
there is a
\bar{y}\in A^{n}
such that
P(\bar{y})<\epsilon
and
\partial(\bar{x},\bar{y})\leq P(\bar{y})+\epsilon
. Nonetheless, if
P(\bar{x})=\partial(\bar{x},D)
for some nonempty set
D\subset A^{n}
, then both conditions are satisfied.

For the converse, suppose
P
satisfies both conditions and let
D
be its zero-set. Fix some
\bar{a}\in A^{n}
and some
\epsilon>0
; we use the second condition inductively to define a sequence
\{\bar{a}_{n}\}_{n\in\omega}\subset A^{n}
such that
\bar{a}_{0}=\bar{a}
,
P(\bar{a}_{n+1})<\epsilon/2^{n}
and
\partial(\bar{a}_{n+1},\bar{a}_{n})\leq P(\bar{a}_{n})+\epsilon/2^{n}
.

Note that by construction
\partial(\bar{a}_{n+1},\bar{a}_{n})\leq(3\epsilon)/2^{n}
for
n\geq 1
, and so the sequence is Cauchy and has a limit
\bar{b}\in A^{n}
. By construction,
P(\bar{b})=\lim P(\bar{a}_{n})=0
, so
\bar{b}\in D
and
D
is nonempty. Moreover,

\partial(\bar{a},D)\leq\partial(\bar{a},\bar{b})\leq P(\bar{a})+\partial(\bar{a},\bar{a}_{1})+\partial(\bar{a}_{1},\bar{b})\leq P(\bar{a})+4\epsilon.

Thus, we have that
\partial(\bar{x},D)\leq P(\bar{x})
, and the converse inequality follows from the first condition in the lemma. This complete the proof.
∎

2.5.
Types

We begin by summarizing the basic definitions and facts about types in infinitary continuous logic:

Definition 2.27
.

Let
\Omega
be a weak modulus of continuity,
{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}
a metric
\tau
-structure, and
\bar{a}\in{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}
. Then

(\Omega,\mathcal{L})-\operatorname{tp}(\bar{a})=\{(\varphi,r)\mid\text{$\varphi$ satisfies $\Omega$ and }\varphi^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}(\bar{a})\leq r\}

Note that, up to logical equivalence,
(\Omega,\mathcal{L})-\operatorname{tp}(\bar{a})
is equal to
\{\varphi\mid\varphi^{\bar{\mathcal{A}}}(\bar{a})=0\}
after doing some routine modifications to the formulas. 5.

We now use back-and-forth sets to show that the closures of automorphism orbits in separable metric structure are definable, and in turn use this fact to construct Scott sentences, and Scott predicates, for separable metric structures.
