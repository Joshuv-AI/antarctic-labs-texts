A note on the recovery sequence in the double gradient model for phase transitions
Jakob Deutsch

Abstract
. We investigate the lim sup \limsup inequality in the double gradient model for phase transitions governed by a Modica–Mortola functional with a double-well potential in two dimensions. Specifically, we consider energy functionals of the form E ε ​ ( u , Ω ) = ∫ Ω ( 1 ε ​ W ​ ( ∇ u ) + ε ​ | ∇ 2 u | 2 ) ​ 𝑑 x E_{\varepsilon}(u,\Omega)=\int_{\Omega}\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}W(\nabla u)+\varepsilon|\nabla^{2}u|^{2}\right)dx for maps u ∈ H 2 ​ ( Ω , ℝ 2 ) u\in H^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{2}) , where W W vanishes only at two wells. 1.
Introduction

The study of diffuse interface models for phase transitions has been central to the calculus of variations and mathematical materials science for decades. The seminal Modica–Mortola functional

F_{\varepsilon}(u,\Omega):=\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}W(u)+\varepsilon|\nabla u|^{2}\,dx,

and its variants provide a classical variational description of liquid-liquid phase separation. The sharp interface limit of such functionals, obtained via
\Gamma
-convergence, yields surface energies that concentrate on
(n-1)
-dimensional interfaces and is now a standard cornerstone of the field
[
Mod87
,
MM77
,
Gur87
]
. There is a vast literature connecting singularly perturbed energies to geometric variational problems that has inspired numerous generalisations, among which we mention some to the vectorial
[
FT89
,
Bal90
,
Bou90
,
CG21
]
and higher-order settings
[
FM00
,
Che+11
,
BDS25
]
.

Considering analogous variational models dealing with phase transformations in solids, the deformations are vectorial, and thus the relevant variables are matrix valued. A prototypical model in this direction is the double-gradient Modica–Mortola functional

E_{\varepsilon}(u,\Omega)=\int_{\Omega}\!\Big(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\,W(\nabla u)+\varepsilon\,|\nabla^{2}u|^{2}\Big)\,dx,

with a simply connected Lipschitz domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
, a deformation
u\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
, a non-negative potential
W\colon\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}\to[0,\infty)
vanishing on a finite set of possibly set-valued wells, and a parameter
\varepsilon>0
representing the thickness of transitional interfaces. The authors
S. Conti
,
I. Fonseca
and
G. Leoni

[
CFL02
]
established a
\Gamma
-convergence result for this class of functionals and derived a cell formula based on periodic functions for the limiting interfacial energy density for two single point wells. While the
\Gamma
-
\liminf
inequality was proven in a very general setting, the construction of the recovery sequence requires making certain restrictive assumptions on the potential. We remark that there has also been an extensive effort to incorporate frame-indifference into the potential. In the celebrated results of
[
CS06
,
CS06b
,
CS06a
]
the
\Gamma
-limit was computed in two dimensions (cf. also
[
Sti21
]
). The problem in higher dimensions in its full generality remains open, although significant advances have recently been made in
[
DF20
,
DF25
]
.

A central object in studying the
\Gamma
-limit is the optimal profile constant, which is computed by taking the
\Gamma
-
\liminf
on the unit cube with respect to a single jump interface, i.e.,

\displaystyle K^{*}=\Gamma(L^{1})\text{-}\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}E_{\varepsilon}(u_{0},(1/2,1/2)^{2})

(1)

where the interface normal is taken to be
e_{2}
and

u_{0}(x)=\begin{cases}Ax&\text{if }x_{2}\geq 0,\\
Bx&\text{if }x_{2}<0,\end{cases}

for two wells
A=-B=a\otimes e_{2}
and
a\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
as a simplification. This constant appears as the surface energy density in the
\Gamma
-limit and governs the cost per unit of an interface separating two wells of an optimal configuration in the limiting space
BV(\Omega;\{A,B\})
. In practice, this formulation is too abstract to employ in actual problems. One would thus like to express it in terms of a simpler structure. This has proven to be considerably difficult and has only been achieved in the setting of two single point wells where certain symmetry assumptions are placed on the potential
W
, see
[
CFL02
]
. We note that in the case of fluid-fluid phase transitions, the optimal profile constant (
1
) reduces to a one-dimensional geodesic problem (cf.
[
FT89
]
).

The main contribution of this paper is the extension of the result by
[
CFL02
]
in two dimensions under the assumption of a certain bound on the optimal profile constant in terms of the geodesic distance of the two wells. More precisely, we introduce an a priori bound in terms of a geodesic distance between the wells
A
and
B
(cf. Definition
8
)

\displaystyle K^{*}<3\,d_{W}(A,B).

(2)

We prove that, if (
2
) holds in conjunction with standard regularity and (quadratic) growth assumptions for the potential
W
, then
K^{*}
can be expressed as a minimisation problem over periodic gradients on the unit cube. More precisely, we show that

\displaystyle K^{*}=\inf\Bigg\{

\displaystyle\int_{(-1/2,1/2)^{2}}LW(\nabla u)+\frac{1}{L}|\nabla^{2}u|^{2}\,dx:\,L>0,\,u\in H^{2}((-1/2,1/2)^{2};\mathbb{R}^{2}),\,

\displaystyle\nabla u\text{ is 1-periodic in }x_{1},\,\nabla u(x)=\nabla u_{0}(x)\text{ for $x_{1}\in(-1/2,1/2),\,|x_{2}|\in(1/4,1/2)$}\Bigg\}

(3)

holds. This characterisation of the optimal profile constant
K^{*}
has already been suggested in
[
CFL02
]
under the symmetry condition

W(m_{1},m_{2})=W(-m_{1},m_{2}).

Under this assumption, the equality (
1
) can be shown, essentially, by reflecting and glueing optimal profiles. Although this approach is certainly viable in the presence of symmetry, it fails in its absence. We circumvent this difficulty by introducing a novel approach of glueing together optimal profiles in two dimensions based on a careful analysis of their traces. We show that the bound on the optimal profile constant translates to a bound on the energy of suitable traces of carefully selected one-dimensional line segments. This bound, in turn, implies that said traces are separated in exactly two connected regions such that the gradient along these lines is close to the wells in these regions. This makes an optimal glueing easier.

The paper is organised as follows. In Section 2, we give an overview of the preliminaries needed. More precisely, in Subsection
2.1
we introduce the notation used throughout this paper. In Subsection
2.2
, we recall certain important properties of the geodesic distance and curves fulfilling the geodesic length bound of
3d_{W}(A,B)
. The main contribution of the paper is presented in Section 3. This section begins with a discussion on modifications of optimal profiles in Subsection
3.1
, where we gather certain statements from
[
CFL02
]
and improve them in a suitable way. Subsection
3.2
is devoted to a glueing procedure of curves which are subject to certain energy bounds. This is, in a sense, the heart of this paper: We introduce a method here which will allow us to create transitional maps to pass from one trace to another. In Subsection
3.3
, we prove our main result Theorem
3.1
. Lastly, in Subsection
3.4
we will introduce a class of potentials
W
which fulfil the assumption (
2
). This class is essentially comprised of all perturbations (in a suitable sense) of quadratic double well potentials.

2.
Setting and Preliminaries

2.1.
Notation & Setup

In the following, we denote the unit cubes in 1D and 2D with
q=(-1/2,1/2)
resp.
Q=(-1/2,1/2)^{2}
. We will use
C
for a generic changing positive constant when writing estimates. We denote important dependencies on parameters by subscripts (
C_{\sigma}
,
C_{\tau}
, etc.). For a matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
, we sometimes write
(m_{1},m_{2})
where
m_{i}
is the
i
-th column. Furthermore, for
r>0
and a set
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
we write
B_{r}(M)=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\dist(M,x)<r\}
. We use the usual notation
B_{r}(x)
for open balls at a point
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
with radius
r>0
. For
h>0
and
\omega\subset\mathbb{R}
we define the cylinders
\omega_{h}:=\omega\times(-h,h)
. Furthermore, we set
\omega_{*}:=\omega_{1/2}=\omega\times(-1/2,1/2)
. We consider the Modica–Mortola type functional

E_{\varepsilon}(u,\Omega):=\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}W(\nabla u)+\varepsilon|\nabla^{2}u|^{2}\,dx

for
u\in H^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{2})
,
W\colon\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
,
\varepsilon>0
and a Lipschitz domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
. We select two matrices
A,B\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
which we will call wells. Similarly to the model in
[
CFL02
]
, we restrict ourselves to

A=-B=a\otimes e_{2}=(0,a)

with
a\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
and
e_{2}
being the second unit vector. We further denote for
(m_{1},m_{2})=M\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}

W_{0}(M):=|m_{1}|^{2}+\min|m_{2}\pm a|^{2}=\min\{|M-A|^{2},|M-B|^{2}\}.

For brevity, we use notations of the type
\min|M\pm A|^{2}:=\min\{|M-A|^{2},|M-B|^{2}\}
. Generally, we use
\pm
as a placeholder for the two cases with
+
and
-
.
We assume that
W
is a double well potential, i.e., for all
M\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
we have
W(M)=0
if and only if
M\in\{A,B\}
. Moreover, we impose the following conditions on the potential
W
:

(H1)

W
is continuous.

(H2)

We assume that
W
has global quadratic growth around the wells, i.e., there exist
C>0
such that for
(m_{1},m_{2})=M\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}

\frac{1}{C}W_{0}(M)\leq W(M)\leq CW_{0}(M).

Throughout the paper, for all statements we will assume
(H1)
and
(H2)
.

Remark 2.1
.

It has been shown in
[
CFL02
, Remark 6.1]
that the combination of
(H1)
and
(H2)
implies the existence of a constant
C>0
such that for all
M,N\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}

\displaystyle W(M)\leq C(W(N)+|M-N|^{2}).

(4)

Moreover,
(H2)
implies

\displaystyle|m_{1}|^{2}\leq CW(M)

(5)

for all
(m_{1},m_{2})=M\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
. Furthermore, we note that, for all
\alpha>0
, condition
(H2)
implies the existence of a constant
C_{\alpha}>0
such that for all
M\in B_{\alpha}(\{\pm A\})^{c}
we have

\displaystyle\max|M\pm A|^{2}\leq C_{\alpha}\min|M\pm A|^{2}\leq C_{\alpha}W(M).

(6)

Indeed, we observe that

\lim_{|M|\to\infty}\frac{|M+A|}{|M-A|}=1

holds and we have for any
R>0
and for
M\in B_{R}(0)\setminus B_{\alpha}(A)^{c}

\frac{|M+A|}{|M-A|}\leq\frac{R+|A|}{\alpha}.

Now, we choose
R>0
such that for all
M\in B_{R}(0)^{c}
we have

\frac{|M+A|}{|M-A|}<2.

We consequently have for a large
C>0
(independent of
\alpha
)

|M+A|\leq\max\left\{2,\frac{C}{\alpha}\right\}|M-A|

for all
M\in M\in B_{\alpha}(\{\pm A\})^{c}
.
By symmetry, (
6
) holds with
C_{\alpha}=\max\left\{2,C/\alpha\right\}
. We note here that for small
\alpha>0
we have

C_{\alpha}=\frac{C}{\alpha}.

We now recall the definition of an optimal profile energy:

Definition 2.2
.

For
h>0
and an open
\omega\subset\mathbb{R}
we define the optimal profile energy by

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}(\omega,h):=\inf\left\{\liminf_{n\to\infty}E_{\varepsilon_{n}}(u_{n},\omega_{h}):\varepsilon_{n}\to 0^{+},u_{n}\to u_{0}\text{ in }L^{1}(\omega_{h},\mathbb{R}^{2})\right\}

(7)

where

u_{0}(x):=Ax\chi_{\{x_{2}>0\}}(x)+Bx\chi_{\{x_{2}\leq 0\}}(x).

Remark 2.3
.

In
[
CFL02
, Lemma 4.3]
it has been shown that
\mathcal{F}
is independent of
h
and that if
(H1)
holds, we have

\mathcal{F}(\omega):=\mathcal{F}(\omega,h)=\mathcal{H}^{1}(\omega)K^{*},

where the constant
K^{*}
is defined as
K^{*}:=\mathcal{F}(q)
. Therefore,
\mathcal{F}(\cdot)
is a multiple of the Hausdorff measure.
Moreover, if
(H2)
holds, we have

\displaystyle K^{*}=\inf\bigg\{\liminf_{n\to\infty}E_{\varepsilon_{n}}(u_{n},q_{h}):

\displaystyle~\varepsilon_{n},c^{+}_{n},c^{-}_{n}\to 0^{+},u_{n}\to u_{0}\text{ in }L^{1}(\omega_{h},\mathbb{R}^{2})

\displaystyle~u_{n}(x)=u_{0}(x)+c^{\pm}_{n}\quad\text{ for }x\in(\omega\times((-h,-2/3h)\cup(2/3h,h)))\bigg\}.

This was shown in
[
CFL02
, Proposition 6.2]
for
h=1
. The proof of this proposition can be directly adapted for any
h>0
. The information about vertical boundary conditions is crucial for glueing.

Next, we will introduce the concept of an optimal profile sequence:

Definition 2.4
.

Let
h>0
. We say that a pair of sequences
(u_{n},\varepsilon_{n})\subset H^{2}(\omega_{h},\mathbb{R}^{2})\times(0,1)
(which are admissible in taking the infimum in (
7
)) is an optimal profile sequence with respect to
\omega
if it attains the minimum, i.e., if

\lim_{n\to\infty}E_{\varepsilon_{n}}(u_{n},\omega_{h})=\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\omega)K^{*}.

Via a diagonalization argument, it can be shown that the infimum in the definition of the optimal profile energy is in fact a minimum. Therefore, the existence of an optimal profile sequence is always guaranteed. One crucial property of optimal profile sequences is the local optimality:

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
h>0
. Suppose
(u_{n},\varepsilon_{n})\subset H^{2}(\omega_{h},\mathbb{R}^{2})\times(0,1)
is an optimal profile sequence with respect to
\omega\subset\mathbb{R}
. Then, it is also an optimal profile sequence with respect to any open set
\tilde{\omega}\subset\omega
with
|\partial\tilde{\omega}|=0
.

Proof.

We just note that by Definition
2.2

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}E_{\varepsilon_{n}}(u_{n},\tilde{\omega}_{h})

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}E_{\varepsilon_{n}}(u_{n},\omega_{h})-\liminf_{n\to\infty}E_{\varepsilon_{n}}(u_{n},(\omega\setminus\tilde{\omega})_{h})

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{F}(\omega)-\mathcal{F}(\omega\setminus\overline{\tilde{\omega}})=\mathcal{F}(\tilde{\omega}).

Since, by the definition of
\mathcal{F}
, we have

\mathcal{F}(\tilde{\omega})\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}E_{\varepsilon_{n}}(u_{n},\tilde{\omega}_{h}),

we conclude with

\mathcal{F}(\tilde{\omega})=\lim_{n\to\infty}E_{\varepsilon_{n}}(u_{n},\tilde{\omega}_{h}).

∎

2.2.
Discussion on geodesic distances

In the theory of liquid-liquid phase transitions, i.e., the classical vectorial Modica–Mortola functional, the geodesic distance with respect to
W
plays a crucial role in the explicit computation of the
\Gamma
-limit (cf.
[
FT89
]
). For the theory of solid-solid phase transitions, the geodesic distance function is generally not used since the
\Gamma
-limit is computed via a cell formula over periodic functions. However, in our analysis we will still use a certain property of curves which have a bound on their geodesic length. Here, we recap the definition and some of the basic properties of geodesic distances.

Definition 2.6
.

Let
I
be any closed interval and
\varphi\in W^{1,1}(I;\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2})
. We call

L_{W}(\varphi):=2\int_{I}\sqrt{W(\varphi(s))}|\varphi^{\prime}(s)|\,ds

the length of
\varphi
with respect to
W
. We further denote the geodesic distance with respect to
W
between the two matrices
M,N\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
by

\displaystyle d_{W}(M,N):=\inf\{L_{W}(\varphi):\,\varphi\in W^{1,1}(I;\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}),\,\varphi(-1)=M,\varphi(1)=N\}.

(8)

To simplify the analysis that follows, we will use
I=[-1,1]
and note that the above quantities are invariant under reparametrization of
I
.

Lemma 2.7
.

The geodesic distance function
d_{W}\colon\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
defined in (
8
) is locally Lipschitz continuous.

Proof.

Let
R>0
and
A,\tilde{A},B\in B_{R}(0)
. Let
\varphi\in W^{1,1}([-1,1];\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}),\,\varphi(-1)=A,\varphi(1)=B
such that for
\varepsilon>0
we have

L_{W}(\varphi)\leq d_{W}(A,B)+\varepsilon.

Let
\zeta\colon[-1,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
and
\tilde{\zeta}\colon[-1,0]\to\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
be the linear interpolation of
\tilde{A}
and
A
on the respective intervals,
\tilde{\varphi}\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
be the reparametrization of
\varphi
defined by
\tilde{\varphi}(s):=\varphi(-1+2s)
and
\psi\colon[-1,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
defined by

\psi(s):=\begin{cases}\tilde{\zeta},&\text{in }[-1,0],\\
\tilde{\varphi},&\text{in }[0,1].\end{cases}

Now, since

\mathcal{L}_{W}(\psi)=L_{W}(\zeta)+L_{W}(\varphi)

we can derive from the definition of the geodesic distance

\displaystyle d_{W}(\tilde{A},B)-d_{W}(A,B)

\displaystyle\leq L_{W}(\psi)-L_{W}(\varphi)+\varepsilon

\displaystyle=L_{W}(\zeta)+\varepsilon

\displaystyle\leq C\sup_{M\in B_{R}(0)}\sqrt{W(M)}|A-\tilde{A}|+\varepsilon.

Since
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary and by symmetry, we infer for any
\tilde{A},A,B\in B_{R}(0)\times B_{R}(0)

|d_{W}(\tilde{A},B)-d_{W}(A,B)|\leq C\sup_{M\in B_{R}(0)}\sqrt{W(M)}|A-\tilde{A}|

By
(H1)
we know that
\sup_{M\in B_{R}(0)}\sqrt{W(M)}
is finite. We have shown that
d_{W}
is Lipschitz in the first variable in
B_{R}(0)\times B_{R}(0)
. By the symmetry of the geodesic distance function,
d_{W}
is also Lipschitz in the second variable in
B_{R}(0)\times B_{R}(0)
. From this we can conclude that
d_{W}
is locally Lipschitz.
∎

To motivate condition (
2
) we make the following observation:

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
\varphi\in W^{1,1}([-1,1];\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2})
with
\varphi(-1)=M\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2},\varphi(1)=N\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}
that fulfils
L_{W}(\varphi)<3d_{W}(M,N)
. Then, for each
\alpha>0
with

\displaystyle\alpha<\gamma(M,N,W,\varphi):=\min\left\{\frac{|M-N|}{2},\frac{3d_{W}(M,N)-L_{W}(\varphi)}{8L}\right\}

(9)

where
L>0
is the Lipschitz constant of
d_{W}
on the region
B_{R}(0)\times B_{R}(0)
with
R=2\max\{|M|,|N|\}
we have

\sup\varphi^{-1}(B_{\alpha}(M))<\inf\varphi^{-1}(B_{\alpha}(N)).

Proof.

We first assume
\alpha<|M-N|/2
. Note that this implies that
\sup\varphi^{-1}(B_{\alpha}(M))=\inf\varphi^{-1}(B_{\alpha}(N))
is not possible since
\varphi
is continuous. We prove the statement by contradiction. Assume that the reverse inequality

\sup\varphi^{-1}(B_{\alpha}(M))>\inf\varphi^{-1}(B_{\alpha}(N))

holds.
If this is the case, we could find
s_{M}^{\alpha}\in\varphi^{-1}(B_{\alpha}(M))
and
s_{N}^{\alpha}\in\varphi^{-1}(B_{\alpha}(N))
with
s_{N}^{\alpha}<s_{M}^{\alpha}
. By Lemma
2.7

d_{W}
is Lipschitz continuous on
(B_{R}(M)\cup B_{R}(N))\times(B_{R}(M)\cup B_{R}(N))
with Lipschitz constant
L>0
. By our choice of
\alpha
we have
\varphi(s_{M}^{\alpha}),\varphi(s_{N}^{\alpha})\in B_{R}(0)
. We derive

|d_{W}(M,N)-d_{W}(\varphi(s_{M}^{\alpha})),\varphi(s_{N}^{\alpha}))|\leq L(|M-\varphi(s_{M}^{\alpha})|+|N-\varphi(s_{N}^{\alpha})|)\leq 2L\alpha.

Analogously, we have

|d_{W}(M,N)-d_{W}(M,\varphi(s_{N}^{\alpha}))|\leq L\alpha

and

|d_{W}(M,N)-d_{W}(\varphi(s_{M}^{\alpha}),N)|\leq L\alpha.

From these estimates, we can derive

\displaystyle 3d_{W}(M,N)\leq d_{W}(M,\varphi(s_{N}^{\alpha}))+d_{W}(\varphi(s_{N}^{\alpha}),\varphi(s_{M}^{\alpha}))+d_{W}(\varphi(s_{M}^{\alpha}),N)+4L\alpha\leq L_{W}(\varphi)+4L\alpha.

So for

\displaystyle\alpha<\frac{3d_{W}(M,N)-L_{W}(\varphi)}{8L}

(10)

we derive a contradiction.
∎

Now, we introduce the concept of an admissible curve. These are essentially those curves where we transition from one phase to another only once which means we can set a point which separates the phases, i.e., above this point we are close to one phase and below we are close to the other one.

Definition 2.9
.

Let
\varphi\in W^{1,1}([-1,1];\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2})
with
\varphi(-1)=M,\varphi(1)=N
. Then, we call the pair
(\varphi,\alpha)
admissible if
\varphi
fulfils the assumptions of Lemma
2.8
and
\alpha<\gamma(M,N,W,\varphi)
where
\gamma
is explicitly defined in (
9
). Moreover, we define the phase separating point for an admissible pair
(\varphi,\alpha)
by

s_{\varphi}^{\alpha}:=\frac{1}{2}(\sup\varphi^{-1}(B_{\alpha}(M))+\inf\varphi^{-1}(B_{\alpha}(N))).

Next, we show that admissible curves enjoy certain properties.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
\varphi,\psi\in W^{1,1}([-1,1],\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2})
with equal endpoints
\varphi(-1)=\psi(-1)=A
and
\varphi(1)=\psi(1)=B
. Then, the following holds:

(i)

Suppose that
(\varphi,\alpha)
and
(\psi,\alpha)
are admissible pairs for some
\alpha>0
and the phase separating points coincide
s_{\varphi}^{\alpha}=s_{\psi}^{\alpha}
. Then, we have for every
s\in[-1,1]
the estimate

|\varphi(s)-\psi(s)|^{2}\leq C_{\alpha}(W(\varphi(s))+W(\psi(s))).

(ii)

Let
K>0
. Suppose that
\varphi
fulfils

L_{W}(\varphi)<K<3d_{W}(A,B).

Then,
(\varphi,\alpha_{K})
is admissible for

\displaystyle\alpha_{K}:=\min\{(3d_{W}(A,B)-K)/(12L),|A-B|/8\}.

(11)

Here,
L>0
is the Lipschitz constant of
d_{W}
on the region
B_{R}(0)\times B_{R}(0)
with
R=2\max\{|A|,|B|\}
.

Proof.

We start by proving
(i)
. We have for
s\in\varphi^{-1}(B_{\alpha}(A))
and
\alpha<R

|\varphi(s)-\psi(s)|^{2}\leq C(|\varphi(s)-A|^{2}+|\psi(s)-A|^{2})\leq(W(\varphi(s))+|\psi(s)-A|^{2}).

If
\psi(s)\in B_{\alpha}(B)
we would have by the definition of the phase separating points

s_{\psi}^{\alpha}>\sup\psi^{-1}(B_{\alpha}(B))>s>s_{\varphi}^{\alpha}

which contradicts our assumption. So we must have
\psi(s)\in B_{\alpha}(A)
or
\psi(s)\in B_{\alpha}(\{A,B\})^{c}
. In the first case, we apply
(H2)
and, in the second case, (
6
) to derive

|\psi(s)-A|^{2}\leq C_{\alpha}W(\psi(s)).

Analogous estimates can be made for
s\in\varphi^{-1}(B_{\alpha}(B))
and
s\in\varphi^{-1}(B_{\alpha}(\{A,B\})^{c})
. In conclusion, we derive for every
s\in[-1,1]
the pointwise estimate

\displaystyle|\varphi(s)-\psi(s)|^{2}\leq C_{\alpha}(W(\varphi(s))+W(\psi(s))).

For
(ii)
, we just observe that

\alpha_{K}<\gamma(A,B,W,\varphi)

by comparing
\alpha_{K}
to the definition of
\gamma
(cf. (
9
)). By definition, this means that
(\varphi,\alpha_{K})
is admissible.
∎

3.
Main Result

We are now in a position to state the main result of this paper. We recall the definition of the optimal profile constant
K^{*}
from Definition
2.4
, and of the geodesic distance
d_{W}
from Definition
2.6
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose that
(H1)
,
(H2)
and

K^{*}<3d_{W}(A,B)

holds. Then,

\displaystyle K^{*}=K^{*}_{\mathrm{per}}:=\inf\Bigg\{

\displaystyle\int_{Q}LW(\nabla u)+\frac{1}{L}|\nabla^{2}u|^{2}\,dx:\,L>0,\,u\in H^{2}(Q;\mathbb{R}^{2}),\,\nabla u\text{ is 1-periodic in }x_{1},

\displaystyle\nabla u(x)=\nabla u_{0}(x)\text{ for $x_{1}\in(-1/2,1/2),\,|x_{2}|\in(1/4,1/2)$}\Bigg\},

where
Q
is the unit cube
(-1/2,1/2)^{2}
and
u_{0}
is as in Definition
2.2
.

It was already shown in the proof of
[
CFL02
, Proposition 6.4]
that under
(H1)
we have
K^{*}\leq K^{*}_{\mathrm{per}}
. We highlight that only the continuity of
W
is needed so that one can apply the Riemann-Lebesgue Lemma. For convenience, we recall this fact with a sketch of its proof.

Proposition 3.2

(
[
CFL02
, Proposition 6.4]
)
.

Suppose that
(H1)
holds. Then, we have

K^{*}\leq K^{*}_{\mathrm{per}}.

Proof.

Let
\delta>0
. First, let
u\in H^{2}(Q;\mathbb{R}^{2})
which almost minimizes
K^{*}_{\mathrm{per}}
, i.e., there exists an
L>0
such that

K^{*}_{\mathrm{per}}+\delta>\int_{Q}LW(\nabla u)+\frac{1}{L}|\nabla^{2}u|^{2}\,dx.

Then, for any sequence of positive numbers
\{\varepsilon_{n}\}
with
\varepsilon_{n}\to 0
we define a rescaled sequence of maps
z_{\varepsilon_{n}}\in H^{2}(Q;\mathbb{R}^{2})
(cf.
[
CFL02
, (6.20)]
) such that

\nabla z_{\varepsilon_{n}}(x)=\begin{cases}a\otimes e_{2}&\text{if }x_{2}>\frac{\varepsilon L}{2}\\
\nabla u\left(\frac{x}{\varepsilon L}\right)&\text{if }|x_{2}|\leq\frac{\varepsilon L}{2}\\
-a\otimes e_{2}&\text{if }x_{2}<-\frac{\varepsilon L}{2}.\end{cases}

Here, we used the fact that we can extend
\nabla u
periodically in
x^{\prime}
. We note that

E_{\varepsilon_{n}}(z_{\varepsilon_{n}})=\int_{Q}LW\left(\nabla u\left(\frac{x^{\prime}}{\varepsilon L},t\right)\right)+\frac{1}{L}\left|\nabla^{2}u\left(\frac{x^{\prime}}{\varepsilon L},t\right)\right|^{2}\,d(x^{\prime},t).

Now, we apply the Riemann Lebesgue Lemma to infer

\liminf_{n\to\infty}E_{\varepsilon_{n}}(z_{\varepsilon_{n}})=\int_{Q}LW(\nabla u)+\frac{1}{L}|\nabla^{2}u|^{2}\,dx

and, consequently,

K^{*}\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}E_{\varepsilon_{n}}(z_{\varepsilon_{n}})\leq K^{*}_{\mathrm{per}}+\delta.

Since
\delta>0
was arbitrary we conclude.
∎

For completeness, we also recall that the equality
K^{*}=K^{*}_{\mathrm{per}}
paired with
(H1)
and
(H2)
implies

\Gamma(L^{1})-\lim_{n\to\infty}E_{\varepsilon_{n}}(u,\Omega)=K^{*}_{\mathrm{per}}\mathcal{H}^{1}(J_{\nabla u}\cap\Omega)

(cf.
[
CFL02
, Theorem 6.6 & 6.7]
) for any bounded, simply connected Lipschitz domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

The remainder of this section is devoted to showing the inequality
K^{*}\geq K^{*}_{\mathrm{per}}
.
We will start the discussion with Lemma
3.4
, where we show that optimal profile sequences can be modified suitably at the horizontal boundary. This idea was originally used in
[
CFL02
]
, but we refined it here, so that the trace of the gradient satisfies a suitable energy bound. Afterwards, in Lemmas
3.5
–
3.7
, we are concerned with glueing together traces with certain energy bounds. In Theorem
3.9
, we combine these auxiliary steps to derive
K^{*}_{\mathrm{per}}\leq K^{*}
. Lastly, in Theorem
3.10
we will show that the assumptions of the main result are fulfilled as long as the potential
W
is a suitable perturbation of a quadratic potential.

3.2.
Optimal interpolation of traces

For
\varepsilon>0
we define the energy of a curve
\varphi\in W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2})
by

I_{\varepsilon}(\varphi):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}W(\varphi)+\varepsilon|\varphi^{\prime}|^{2}\,ds.

We note that by applying the standard Modica–Mortola trick (Young inequality) we have

L_{W}(\varphi)\leq I_{\varepsilon}(\varphi).

where
L_{W}
is given in Definition
8
. Now, we will begin the discussion about the procedure to glue two traces together in an optimal way. For this, we will derive energy bounds of certain transitional maps. We start with an observation on vertical translations of traces.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\varphi\in W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2})
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
with
I_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)<\infty
. Then, for each
\sigma>0
and
\beta\in(-1,1)
there exists a map
\tilde{v}\in H^{2}((-\sigma,\sigma)_{*},\mathbb{R}^{2})
and a constant
C_{\sigma}>0
with the following properties:

(i)

\partial_{2}\tilde{v}(x_{1},x_{2})=\varphi_{2}(x_{2})
near
x_{1}=-\sigma
for all
x_{2}
,

(ii)

\partial_{2}\tilde{v}(x_{1},x_{2})=\varphi_{2}(x_{2}+\beta)
near
x_{1}=\sigma
for all
x_{2}
,

(iii)

\partial_{1}\tilde{v}=0
near
x_{1}=\pm\sigma
,

(iv)

We have the energy estimate

E_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{v},(-\sigma,\sigma)_{*})\leq C\left(\sigma+C_{\sigma}\frac{\beta^{2}}{\varepsilon}\right)(1+I_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)).

Proof.

Let
\beta\in(-1,1)
and
\sigma>0
. We define

\tilde{v}(x_{1},x_{2}):=\int_{-1/2}^{x_{2}+\rho(x_{1})}\varphi_{2}(s)\,ds

for
(x_{1},x_{2})\in(-\sigma,\sigma)_{*}
, where
\rho\in C^{\infty}(-\sigma,\sigma)
with

•

\rho(-\sigma)=0
,
\rho(\sigma)=\beta
, and
\rho^{\prime}=0
near
\pm\sigma
,

•

|\rho^{\prime}|\leq C\beta/\sigma
and
|\rho^{\prime\prime}|\leq C\beta/\sigma^{2}
.

Notice that

\partial_{2}\tilde{v}(x_{1},x_{2})=\varphi_{2}(x_{2}+\rho(x_{1}))

and

\partial_{22}\tilde{v}(x_{1},x_{2})=\varphi_{2}^{\prime}(x_{2}+\rho(x_{1})).

Therefore, by the choice of
\rho
we know that
\tilde{v}
fulfils (i) and (ii). By
(H2)
we have

\displaystyle\int_{(-\sigma,\sigma)_{*}}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\min\{|\partial_{2}\tilde{v}\pm a|^{2}\}+\varepsilon|\partial_{22}\tilde{v}|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{(-\sigma,\sigma)_{*}}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\min\{|\varphi_{2}(x_{2}+\rho(x_{1}))\pm a|\}+\varepsilon|\varphi_{2}^{\prime}(x_{2}+\rho(x_{1}))|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\sigma\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\min\{|\varphi_{2}(x_{2})\pm a|\}+\varepsilon|\varphi_{2}^{\prime}(x_{2})|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle=\sigma I_{\varepsilon}(\varphi).

For the remaining partial derivatives, we find

\partial_{1}\tilde{v}(x_{1},x_{2})=\varphi_{2}(x_{2}+\rho(x_{1}))\rho^{\prime}(x_{1}),

\partial_{11}\tilde{v}(x_{1},x_{2})=\varphi_{2}^{\prime}(x_{2}+\rho(x_{1}))(\rho^{\prime}(x_{1}))^{2}+\varphi_{2}(x_{2}+\rho(x_{1}))\rho^{\prime\prime}(x_{1}),

as well as

\partial_{12}\tilde{v}(x_{1},x_{2})=\varphi_{2}^{\prime}(x_{2}+\rho(x_{1}))\rho(x_{1}).

We now estimate the first derivative

\displaystyle\int_{-\sigma}^{\sigma}\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}|\partial_{1}\tilde{v}|^{2}\,dx_{2}\,dx_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C\beta^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\int_{-\sigma}^{\sigma}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}|\varphi_{2}|^{2}\,dx_{2}\,dx_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C\beta^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\int_{-\sigma}^{\sigma}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(\min|\varphi_{2}\pm a|^{2}+1)\,dx_{2}\,dx_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C\beta^{2}}{\sigma}\left(I_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)+\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right),

and the remaining second derivatives where we repeatedly use
|\rho^{k}|\leq\beta/(\sigma^{k})
for
k\in\{1,2\}
,
|\beta|\leq 1
, (
4
) and (
5
)

\displaystyle\int_{-\sigma}^{\sigma}\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\varepsilon|\partial_{11}\tilde{v}|^{2}\,dx_{2}\,dx_{1}

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{-\sigma}^{\sigma}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\varphi_{2}^{\prime}(x_{2}+\rho(x_{1}))(\rho^{\prime}(x_{1}))^{2}|^{2}+|\varphi_{2}(x_{2}+\rho(x_{1}))\rho^{\prime\prime}(x_{1})|^{2}\,dx_{2}\,dx_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C\varepsilon(\beta^{4}+\beta^{2})}{\sigma^{4}}\int_{-\sigma}^{\sigma}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\varphi_{2}^{\prime}|^{2}+|\varphi_{2}|^{2}\,dx_{2}\,dx_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C\beta^{2}}{\sigma^{3}}\left(I_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)+\varepsilon\right),

and

\displaystyle\int_{-\sigma}^{\sigma}\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\varepsilon|\partial_{12}\tilde{v}|^{2}\,dx_{2}\,dx_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C\varepsilon\beta^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\int_{-\sigma}^{\sigma}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\varphi_{2}^{\prime}|^{2}\,dx_{2}\,dx_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C\beta^{2}}{\sigma}I_{\varepsilon}(\varphi).

Putting these estimates together, we derive

\displaystyle E_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{v},(-\sigma,\sigma)_{*})

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{(-\sigma,\sigma)_{*}}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}(|\partial_{1}\tilde{v}|^{2}+\min|\partial_{2}\tilde{v}\pm a|^{2})+\varepsilon|\nabla^{2}\tilde{v}|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq C\left(C_{\sigma}\left(\frac{\beta^{2}}{\varepsilon}+I_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)\right)+\sigma I_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\sigma+C_{\sigma}\frac{\beta^{2}}{\varepsilon}\right)(1+I_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)).

This concludes the proof.
∎

In the next lemma, we will observe that, as long as phase separating points (cf. Definition
2.9
) coincide, we can transition between traces of gradients such that the energy of the transitional map is controlled in terms of the width of the transitional region and the height of the interface.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\varphi,\psi\in W^{1,1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2})
,
K>0
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
with
I_{\varepsilon}(\varphi),I_{\varepsilon}(\psi)<K<3d_{W}(A,B)
.
Furthermore, let
s_{\varphi}=s_{\varphi}^{\alpha_{K}},\,s_{\psi}=s_{\psi}^{\alpha_{K}}
as in Lemma
2.10
. Suppose that

•

s_{\varphi}=s_{\psi}
, and

•

there exists
h>\varepsilon>0
such that
\varphi(s)=\psi(s)=\pm A
for
\pm s\geq\pm h
.

Then, for each
\sigma>\varepsilon>0
there exists a map
\tilde{w}=\tilde{w}_{\sigma}\in H^{2}((-\sigma,\sigma)_{*},\mathbb{R}^{2})
with the following properties:

(1)

\partial_{2}\tilde{w}(x_{1},.)=\varphi_{2}
near
x_{1}=-\sigma
,

(2)

\partial_{2}\tilde{w}(x_{1},.)=\psi_{2}
near
x_{1}=\sigma
,

(3)

\partial_{1}\tilde{w}
= 0 near
x_{1}=\pm\sigma
,

(4)

We have the energy estimate:

E_{\varepsilon}(\tilde{w})\leq C_{K}\left(\frac{h}{\sigma}+\sigma\right)(I_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)+I_{\varepsilon}(\psi)).

Proof.

To start with, we note that
s_{\varphi}
and
s_{\psi}
are well defined since by the Modica–Mortola trick

L_{W}(\varphi)\leq I_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)<K\leq 3d_{W}(A,B)

holds.
By Lemma
2.10

(ii)
we know that
(\varphi,\alpha_{K})
is admissible in the sense of Definition
2.9
, i.e.,
s_{\varphi}^{\alpha_{K}}
is well defined (and analogously
s_{\psi}^{\alpha_{K}}
).
Now, we directly define

\tilde{w}(x_{1},x_{2})=\tilde{\rho}(x_{1})\int_{-1/2}^{x_{2}}\varphi_{2}(s)\,ds+(1-\tilde{\rho}(x_{1}))\int_{-1/2}^{x_{2}}\psi_{2}(s)\,ds,

where
\tilde{\rho}\in C^{\infty}((-\sigma,\sigma);[0,1])
with
\tilde{\rho}(-\sigma)=0
,
\tilde{\rho}(\sigma)=1
and
\tilde{\rho}^{\prime}=0
near
\pm\sigma
,
|\tilde{\rho}^{\prime}|\leq C/\sigma
, and
|\tilde{\rho}^{\prime\prime}|\leq C/\sigma^{2}
.
We notice that

\partial_{1}\tilde{w}(x_{1},x_{2})=\tilde{\rho}^{\prime}(x_{1})\left(\int_{-1/2}^{x_{2}}\varphi_{2}(s)-\psi_{2}(s)\,ds\right),

and

\partial_{2}\tilde{w}(x_{1},x_{2})=\tilde{\rho}(x_{1})\varphi_{2}(x_{2})+(1-\tilde{\rho}(x_{1}))\psi_{2}(x_{2}).

Using the quadratic growth assumption
(H2)
, we see that

\int_{-\sigma}^{\sigma}\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}W(\nabla\tilde{w})\,dx_{2}\,dx_{1}\leq C\left(\int_{-\sigma}^{\sigma}\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}|\partial_{1}\tilde{w}|^{2}+\min|\partial_{2}\tilde{w}\pm a|^{2}\,dx_{2}\,dx_{1}\right).

We estimate the two terms separately. For the first term, we apply Hölder’s inequality to derive

\displaystyle\int_{(-\sigma,\sigma)_{*}}|\partial_{1}\tilde{w}|^{2}\,dx\leq\frac{1}{\sigma}\left(\int_{-h}^{h}|\varphi_{2}(s)-\psi_{2}(s)|\,ds\right)^{2}\leq\frac{h}{\sigma}\int_{-h}^{h}|\varphi_{2}-\psi_{2}|^{2}\,ds.

(16)

Since
s_{\varphi}=s_{\psi}
we can apply Lemma
2.10

(i)
to derive

\displaystyle\int_{-h}^{h}|\varphi_{2}-\psi_{2}|^{2}\,ds\leq C_{K}\varepsilon(I_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)+I_{\varepsilon}(\psi)).

The second term is estimated in a similar spirit:

\displaystyle\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\min|\partial_{2}\tilde{w}\pm a|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\min|\tilde{\rho}(x_{1})\varphi_{2}(x_{2})+(1-\tilde{\rho}(x_{1}))\psi_{2}(x_{2})\pm a|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\min|\psi_{2}\pm a|^{2}+|\psi_{2}-\varphi_{2}|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq C_{K}\varepsilon(I_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)+I_{\varepsilon}(\psi)).

Therefore, we observe

\displaystyle\int_{-\sigma}^{\sigma}\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}\min|\partial_{2}\tilde{w}\pm a|^{2}\,dx_{2}\,dx_{1}\leq C_{K}\sigma\varepsilon(I_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)+I_{\varepsilon}(\psi)),

and, consequently,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{(-\sigma,\sigma)_{*}}W(\nabla\tilde{w})\,dx\leq C_{K}\left(\frac{h}{\sigma}+\sigma\right)(I_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)+I_{\varepsilon}(\psi))

(17)

Now, we compute the second derivatives and get

\partial_{11}\tilde{w}(x_{1},x_{2})=\tilde{\rho}^{\prime\prime}(x_{1})\left(\int_{-1/2}^{x_{2}}\varphi_{2}(s)-\psi_{2}(s)\,ds\right)

\partial_{12}\tilde{w}(x_{1},x_{2})=\tilde{\rho}^{\prime}(x_{1})(\varphi_{2}(x_{2})-\psi_{2}(x_{2}))

and

\partial_{22}\tilde{w}(x_{1},x_{2})=\tilde{\rho}(x_{1})\varphi_{2}^{\prime}(x_{2})+(1-\tilde{\rho}(x_{1}))\psi_{2}^{\prime}(x_{2}).

Similar to (
16
) we have

\displaystyle\varepsilon\int_{(-\sigma,\sigma)_{*}}|\partial_{11}\tilde{w}(x_{1},x_{2})|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq\frac{C\varepsilon h}{\sigma^{3}}\int_{-1/2}^{1/2}|\varphi_{2}-\psi_{2}|^{2}\,ds

and, therefore, we infer

\varepsilon\int_{(-\sigma,\sigma)_{*}}|\partial_{11}\tilde{w}(x_{1},x_{2})|^{2}\,dx\leq C_{K}\frac{\varepsilon^{2}h}{\sigma^{3}}(I_{\varepsilon}(\varphi)+I_{\varepsilon}(\psi)).

Moreover,

Lemma 3.8

3.4.
The geodesic distance bound

Here, we show that any continuous perturbation (in a certain sense) of the quadratic potential

Theorem 3.10
.

Suppose that
W
is continuous and

\displaystyle|\sqrt{W}-\sqrt{W_{0}}|\leq\sigma\sqrt{W}_{0}

We first note that

K_{*}=\inf\left\{\liminf_{n\to\infty}\int_{(-1/2,1/2)}\frac{1}{\varepsilon_{n}}\tilde{W}(u_{n}^{\prime}(s))+\varepsilon_{n}|u_{n}^{\prime\prime}(s)|^{2}\,ds:\,\varepsilon_{n}\to 0^{+},\,u_{n}\in H^{2}(q;\mathbb{R}^{2}),\,u_{n}\to u_{0}\text{ in }L^{1}(q,\mathbb{R}^{2})\right\}.

(26)

\displaystyle d_{\tilde{W}_{0}}(A,B)=d_{W_{0}}(A,B).

27

\tau>1
we find a
k_{0}\in\{1,..,n\}
and
C_{\tau}>0
such that

a_{k_{0}}\leq\frac{\tau C_{a}}{n}\qquad b_{k_{0}}\leq\frac{2\tau C_{b}}{(\tau-1)n}\qquad c_{k_{0}}\leq\frac{2\tau C_{c}}{(\tau-1)n}.

Proof.

We just note that

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{\tau C_{a}}a_{k}+\frac{(\tau-1)}{2\tau C_{b}}b_{k}+\frac{\tau-1}{2\tau C_{c}}c_{k}\leq 1.

Therefore, we can find a
k_{0}\in\{1,..,n\}
such that

\frac{1}{\tau C_{a}}a_{k_{0}}+\frac{(\tau-1)}{2\tau C_{b}}b_{k_{0}}+\frac{\tau-1}{2\tau C_{c}}c_{k_{0}}\leq\frac{1}{n}.

Since the coefficients are all non-negative the statement follows.
∎

Here, we present a short auxiliary lemma which is used for modifying optimal profiles at their boundary.

Lemma A.2
.

Let
I=I_{0}\cup I_{1}
with intervals
I_{0}=(0,\delta/m),I_{1}=[\delta/m,2\delta/m)
for
\delta>0
and
m\in\mathbb{N}
. Moreover, let
u\in H^{2}(I_{*},\mathbb{R}^{2})
,
\varepsilon>0
and
M>0
such that there exists
s\in I_{0}
with

\displaystyle\int_{(-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})}\frac{1}{\varepsilon}W(\nabla u(s,t))+\varepsilon|\nabla^{2}u(s,t)|^{2}+\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}|(\nabla u-\nabla u_{0})(s,t)|^{2}+|(u-u_{0})(s,t)|\,dt<M,

\displaystyle E_{\varepsilon}(u,I)+\int_{(I_{1})_{*}}\frac{1}{\delta^{2}}|\nabla u-\nabla u_{0}|^{2}+|u-u_{0}|\,dx<\frac{\delta}{m}M,

and
\varphi\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R};[0,1])
with
\varphi(0)=1
,
\varphi(1)=0
and

|\varphi^{\prime}|\leq M\frac{\delta}{m}\text{ and }|\varphi^{\prime\prime}|\leq M\frac{\delta^{2}}{m^{2}}.

Then there exists a constant
C_{M}>0
such that the map

w(x)=\varphi(x_{1})u(x_{1},x_{2})+(1-\varphi(x_{1}))u(s,x_{2})

fulfils

E_{\varepsilon}(w,I)\leq C_{M}\left(\delta+\frac{\delta}{\varepsilon m}\right),

and, as a consequence, if
\varepsilon m\geq 1/M
we have

E_{\varepsilon}(w,I)\leq 2C_{M}\delta.

Proof.

We first compute the first derivative of
w
:

\displaystyle\nabla w(x_{1},x_{2})=(\varphi^{\prime}(x_{1})(u(x_{1},x_{2})-u(s,x_{2})),0)+\varphi(x_{1})\nabla u(x_{1},x_{2})+(1-\varphi(x_{1}))(0,\partial_{2}u(s,x_{2})).

By our growth assumptions
(H2)
, we have

\displaystyle W(\nabla w)\leq C\left(1+\frac{m^{2}}{\delta^{2}}|u-\bar{u}|^{2}+|\nabla u-\nabla u_{0}|^{2}+|\overline{\nabla u}-\nabla u_{0}|^{2}\right),

where we have used the short notation
\overline{u}(x_{1},x_{2})=u(s,x_{2})
and
\overline{\nabla u}(x_{1},x_{2})=\nabla u(s,x_{2})
.
To estimate the difference
u-\bar{u}
, we apply Poincaré inequality, and we observe

\displaystyle\int_{I_{*}}|u-\bar{u}|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{\delta^{2}}{m^{2}}\int_{I_{*}}|\partial_{1}(u-\bar{u})|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{\delta^{2}}{m^{2}}\int_{I_{*}}|\nabla(u-u_{0})|^{2}+|\overline{\nabla u}-\nabla u_{0}|^{2}\,dx

\displaystyle\leq C_{M}\frac{\delta^{3}}{m^{3}}.

For the other terms, we only need to observe

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon}|I_{*}|\leq C\frac{\delta}{\varepsilon m}.

By our assumptions and the last two inequalities, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{I_{*}}W(\nabla w)\,dx\leq C_{M}\frac{\delta}{m\varepsilon}.

Similarly, we can estimate

\displaystyle|\nabla^{2}w|^{2}\leq C\left(|\nabla^{2}u|^{2}+|\nabla^{2}\bar{u}|^{2}+\frac{m^{4}}{\delta^{4}}|u-\bar{u}|^{2}+\frac{m^{2}}{\delta^{2}}|\nabla u-\nabla\bar{u}|^{2}\right).

In the exact same fashion as before, the last two terms can be estimated by a Poincaré inequality, with which we can derive

\displaystyle\varepsilon\int_{I_{*}}|\nabla^{2}w|^{2}\,dx\leq C_{M}\left(\frac{\delta}{m}+\frac{\delta}{m\varepsilon}\right).

Since the as a consequence’ part of the Lemma is an immediate deduction, we conclude.
∎
