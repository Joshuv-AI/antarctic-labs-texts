Abstract
The electricity system becomes more complex, connecting massive numbers of end-users and distributed generators. Adding or removing grid connections requires expert studies to align technical constraints with user requests. In times of labour shortages, carrying out these studies represents a significant amount of time that engineers at system operators spend in planning departments. As time is limited, only standard block connectivity contracts can be offered to end-users, or the requests pile up. Even if offers are made, these often do not perfectly match the user’s requirements, leading to overpaying or underusing the grid capacity. This paper investigates whether end-users can negotiate individual, flexible time-of-use contracts directly with the grid using Large Language Models (LLMs) in chats at scale. This work addresses system-level technical challenges in automating contract design under grid constraints, integrating LLMs with power system models, and ensuring secure, reliable interaction. We develop a chat system using functional programs for power system analysis, enabling users to request customised, technically feasible contracts at scale. We demonstrate high accuracy in executing engineering studies, robustness to user input variations, self-assessment of connection requests by small and medium enterprises, and potential for secure, chat-enabled maintenance planning. This initial study paves the way toward developing a tailored LLM system, resulting in possible high-efficiency gains for grid planning and customer management. The code is available at: https://github.com/TU-Delft-AI-Energy-Lab/LLM-Electricity-Contracts

Introduction

The energy transition requires many engineers to study renewable energy systems to improve design, feasibility, or stability. In conventional power system studies, the electrical engineer modifies a grid model, simulates the model, analyses the result, and iterates until the study’s objective is met. These manual steps, simulation, and analysis, take time, are subject to the engineer’s interpretation, and do not scale well with the size of the grid as the number of connected devices, the number of analyses, the study objectives, and others. An additional challenge for society is the shortage of engineers, limiting the pace of the energy transition. This research enhances engineering capabilities and customer management to speed up studies related to realising renewable grids.

In the past, connecting devices to a single power system required standardisation of this connection to automate the processing, modelling and analysis at scale. However, the devices connected to the grid are heterogeneous in their technical settings and frequently change operating patterns. Standardisation misses out on the potential to leverage the hidden flexibility in the heterogeneity of these devices. As we need to realise the renewable grid, here we investigate the opposite of standardisation: the customisation of connections at scale, building upon the heterogeneity of devices to realise more flexibility for transmission and distribution grids. This research investigates using a chatbot to automate customising contracts with a large number of heterogeneous owners of energy devices at scale, for very little human effort and costs. These heterogeneous owners vary in knowledge and technical settings, rendering the use of
LLMs

We investigate initial use cases in low voltage, medium voltage, and transmission systems while studying properties distinctive for such an LLM-based system related to spelling mistakes, security issues, personalisation, expected efficiency increase, and the consistency of responses.
This work does not propose new optimisation algorithms or faster solvers for grid studies. Instead, it targets the administrative and interaction bottleneck in electricity contracting by enabling non-expert users to negotiate technically feasible contracts through natural language, while relying on established power-system analysis tools for validation.

Natural Language for Grid Connections

II-A

Problem statement

A power grid connects a heterogeneous population of entities
\mathcal{K}
, including households, SME, Distributed Energy Resources (DERs), large loads or generators. Each entity
\kappa\in\mathcal{K}
has a time-dependent complex power net injection
P_{\kappa,t}
. The ongoing electrification and the energy transition have both increased the magnitude and variability of
P_{\kappa,t}
. This increase introduces new grid capacity constraints due to rising uncertainty and reduced predictability. The population
\mathcal{K}
is structurally heterogeneous, differing in type (consumer, producer), scale (household, industry), flexibility, and operating patterns. The statistical properties of
P_{\kappa,t}
vary greatly across entities in terms of mean load level, variance, temporal correlation, and peak-to-average ratio. Each connected entity
\kappa
typically holds a contract with an energy supplier, distribution system operator (DSO), or transmission system operator (TSO). The contract defines allowable consumption or injection levels, connection capacities, and other technical parameters. As the number of entities
|\mathcal{K}|
is extremely large, contracts are typically standardised, leaving little room for individual specification. A standard contract may impose capacity limits

P^{L}_{t}\leq P_{\kappa,t}\leq P^{U}_{t}

(1)

and define the price of net injection
C_{t}
, where
C_{t}
is often determined through market mechanisms. In a low-voltage system,
P^{U}_{t}=P^{U}
is typically fixed and specifies the allowed connected capacity to the grid. However, the heterogeneity in
P_{\kappa,t}^{U}
across entities and time is typically unknown, and collecting this information would incur prohibitive transaction costs. In standard contracts, a uniform upper bound

P^{U}\geq P_{\kappa,t}^{U}\qquad\forall\kappa\in\mathcal{K}_{h},\,\forall t

(2)

is applied for all entities of the same type
\mathcal{K}_{h}\subset\mathcal{K}
and all times. This unification leads to systematic overestimation of aggregate capacity needs and significant underutilization of grid infrastructure. The existing contracting process in power systems has two issues:

1.

Assumption of homogeneity leads to grid inefficiency: The heterogeneous distribution of information prevents effective customisation of contracts using existing rule-based forms or questionnaires. This limitation forces operators to offer only standard contracts, leading to underutilization of the grid or rejection of new connection requests in congested areas. We study residence requesting LV connections as an example.

2.

High complexity of customisation leads to long processing times: Custom contracts require extensive expert involvement and take months to design, hence, they are used primarily at the transmission level. For example, specifying time intervals

t=[t_{s},t_{e}]

(3)

with
P^{U}_{t}=0
corresponds to planned outages of large generators as coordinated with the TSO. This complexity represents a barrier to (dis-)connecting large loads or generators. We study MV connection planning of SMEs and outage planning in the HV as examples.

II-B

Approach

The proposed workflow Fig.
1
uses language models in a chat to customise electricity contracts.
This workflow has the potential to reduce contracting complexity, accelerate customisation, and lead to an efficiency increase of grids
. The end-user who owns the grid device(s) interacts with the chat via language. The chat is powered by an LLM and interfaces with the user and symbolic functions, also called APIs. These APIs can call various grid studies, applying pre-defined rules and providing the LLM with additional input. These studies may have access to grid models, specialist software, and databases, for example, a list of grid components or existing contracts. In various ways, engineers can oversee such an automated process, e.g., in real-time or after the chat, before finalising the contract.

III-C

Validation, Error Handling, and Reliability Guarantees

The proposed approach explicitly separates natural-language interaction from grid validation: the LLM is limited to dialogue management and parameter extraction, while physical feasibility and contract admissibility are enforced exclusively through deterministic functional programs
h(\alpha)\rightarrow\beta
implementing established power-system analysis methods. As a result, physical feasibility and grid-safety are enforced by the deterministic functional programs, independently of the LLM’s correctness, provided that all admissibility constraints are implemented in this function. LLM errors may still lead to preference mismatch, misleading explanations, or additional interaction rounds; however, under the proposed approach, contracts cannot be issued or executed by the system unless they satisfy all deterministic validation constraints, assuming these constraints are correctly encoded in the deterministic functions. Potential LLM failure modes include:

(i)

incorrect parameter extraction
g
,

(ii)

inconsistent response generation
f
,

(iii)

miscommunication of solver outputs

These failure modes are detected when the deterministic function returns a warning or when generated outputs are inconsistent with constraints stored in the chat context. Upon detection, the system, as instructed, triggers corrective feedback, such as requesting clarification, recomputing parameters, or proposing alternative feasible contracts.

Use cases of connection requests

IV-A

Residential households in low-voltage grids

Using the flexibility behind the smart meter to support the grid remains challenging. To use residential flexibility would require communicating with each household
as the knowledge of the customer, their technical settings and energy requirements is highly heterogeneous (the first technical issue in Sec.
II-A
). This communication would involve
several employees at an energy supplier, from customer service over engineers to the legal department. In this hypothetical scenario, as Fig.

V-H

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, the proposed approach is the first to apply LLMs to electricity contract design, managing end-users of the grid, system operators, or energy suppliers. The key benefit of the LLM is to coach the user through the contract design, where the user has heterogeneous knowledge about this process. This makes the contract design scalable to a large number, where the state-of-the-art contracts have to be standardised and typically are supported with long guidelines, rules, and FAQs. This paper presented the initial idea and studied some algorithmic properties (like temperature), sensitivity to user input, privacy, and the impact this system can have within three power system use cases.

While the case studies present a promising impact, the methods and study have several limitations. LLMs have vulnerabilities that need to be studied in detail before deploying this system. The vulnerabilities typically involve security (e.g., prompt hacking and adversarial attacks) and privacy attacks
[
33
]
, and various methods for system design can be applied (e.g., API hardening or paraphrasing). Sec.
V-G
studied one privacy attack on grid data with a basic brute force strategy. Beyond the proposed measures to secure privacy, the hosting setup and data policies primarily determine privacy. For example, the LLM system can be either hosted in a cloud environment or in a private environment, where the data stays in this environment, ensuring high privacy. In either environment, regular chats are typically not used to retrain models live, ensuring the privacy of entered information. When self-hosted, the developer can specify the type of information recorded from the user, and the chat is not stored on the server. Beyond these future needs on privacy and security, this work has not yet explored fine-tuning, a wider variety of the intent of users, exhaustive robustness to very high error rates, real-world variety beyond typographical errors, or evolving model behaviours. No standard benchmarks exist yet to compare this model against others. This study also did not focus on a variety of baselines and improved evaluation frameworks that go beyond the accuracy of responses and parameter correctness to exhaustively compare different models and settings. The real-world applicability needs to be studied further, for example, to consider dynamic operating envelopes that can be defined through contracted flexibility, the expected cost reduction, and customer adoption of the system. While the methodological framework is independent of a specific interface, designing a dedicated User Interface for deployment is a natural next step. All the aforementioned limitations need to be addressed to develop this system further for production readiness.

VI
Conclusion

This paper introduced a new interaction layer between end-users and system operators that has not existed in current practice, where such studies are carried out manually and only by experts. Applying this layer via LLM-based
chats to customise contracts can drastically reduce the transaction costs of interacting with electricity users. Electrical grids conventionally standardise energy supply contracts, not considering the hidden flexibility in the diversity of preferences and devices. The reduction of transaction costs can make the customisation of supply contracts profitable, reduce outage planning durations, and enable new tooling for SMEs to directly test their electricity connections. For example, we demonstrate a
7
-fold increase of EVs that can connect to the grid, exploiting the heterogeneity of devices and preferences. The chat system shows low error rates, robustness to typos, and adaptability. We believe this approach can enhance the digitalisation of the electricity system and
enable
the energy transition.
