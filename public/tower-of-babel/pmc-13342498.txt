Dissecting genetic insights and combining abilities into growth and productivity of bread wheat under rainfed environments
Authors: Kashif Ahmed, Mukhtar Ahmed, Ghulam Rasool, Attaullah Lashari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bread wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) production faces increasing instability under rainfed and semi-arid conditions, necessitating the development of cultivars adapted to these environments. However, the genetic basis of physiological growth dynamics and their connection to yield under water-limited conditions remains poorly understood, limiting breeding progress. To address this, six genetically diverse parental lines were crossed in a full diallel mating design (Method I, Model I) and evaluated with their F₁ hybrids using a randomized complete block design with three replications across seven phenological stages (Zadok scale Z-20 to Z-92). Analysis of variance showed highly significant (P < 0.01) genetic variation among genotypes for all growth, yield, and phenological traits. Among parental lines, Pakistan-2013 displayed superior performance, with early heading (110.5 days), longer grain-filling duration (46.1 days), the highest thousand-grain weight (47.3 g), and the maximum grain yield (27.6 g plant⁻¹). In contrast, NR-514 was the weakest parent, with the lowest thousand-grain weight (32.0 g) and grain yield (12.2 g plant⁻¹). Among direct crosses, Pakistan-2013 × NR-499 showed superiority, with the maximum number of tillers (16.1 plant⁻¹), the highest grains per spike (65.1), the highest thousand-grain weight (51.2 g), and the highest grain yield (31.5 g plant⁻¹). Reciprocal effects were most notable in NR-499 × Pakistan-2013, with significant positive RCA effects for thousand-grain weight (4.933**) and grain yield (4.929**), indicating substantial maternal contributions. General combining ability (GCA) effects identified Pakistan-2013 as the best general combiner for yield-related traits (GCA = 3.785** for TGW; 3.556** for GYPP), suggesting dominant additive gene action. Specific combining ability (SCA) effects were highest in Borlaug-2016 × NR-516 for thousand-grain weight (5.626**) and grain yield (7.471**). Additionally, Pakistan-2013 × NR-499 showed significant positive SCA for grain yield (2.835*), indicating strong non-additive gene action (dominance/epistasis) and heterotic potential in these elite hybrids. Stage-dependent gene action was observed for growth traits, with additive effects prevailing during early stages and non-additive effects influencing reproductive stages. Baker’s ratio (> 0.70) and GCA: SCA ratios (> 1) confirmed the predominance of additive gene action for most traits, supporting early-generation selection. These findings establish a genetic framework that links growth dynamics, phenology, and yield components, offering practical strategies for developing climate-adapted wheat ideotypes for rainfed ecosystems. Future research should focus on multi-environment trials to confirm the stability of combining ability and utilize molecular marker-assisted selection, thereby speeding up the introgression of favorable alleles into breeding programs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47633-0.

Introduction

Wheat (Triticum aestivum L.) is the leading cereal crop worldwide in terms of cultivated area and production, covering over 215 million hectares and yielding approximately 805.3 million tons in 2024–251. Its adaptability across various agro-ecological zones, spanning latitudes from 30° to 60° N and 27° to 40° S, and annual rainfall from 500 to 1200 mm, supports its role as a staple food for nearly 40% of the global population2, 3. Hexaploid wheat accounts for about 95% of worldwide production and provides a vital source of carbohydrates, proteins, fibers, lipids, and essential minerals, accounting for roughly 20% of global caloric intake4. Because of its essential role in global food security and economies, improving wheat yield and resilience is crucial for supporting the growing population2, 5.

Wheat is the primary staple, feeding over 200 million people in Pakistan. It is grown on 9.1 million hectares, yielding around 28.98 million tons with an average productivity of 3,193 Kg/ha⁻¹ as reported6. However, more than 20% of wheat cultivation occurs under rainfed conditions, especially in the Pothwar plateau, where average yields are about half the national average (~ 1.5 t ha⁻¹) due to limited water availability and suboptimal farming conditions5, 7. Additionally, climate change poses increasing challenges, including rising temperatures, unpredictable rainfall, and more severe droughts, which significantly threaten wheat production worldwide and have already caused yield reductions of up to 70% in some cereal-growing regions². These issues highlight the urgent need to develop high-yielding, drought-tolerant wheat cultivars that are suited to changing environments.

Advancing wheat breeding requires a detailed understanding of the genetic architecture controlling yield and growth traits8. Diallel mating designs offer an efficient way to analyze genetic effects, including additive, dominance, and epistatic interactions that influence key agronomic traits9. General combining ability (GCA) indicates additive gene effects and their heritability, aiding in the identification of superior parental lines with consistent performance across environments. Specific combining ability (SCA), on the other hand, captures non-additive genetic interactions responsible for heterosis and hybrid vigor10. Understanding the relative roles of GCA and SCA is crucial for strategic parental selection and hybrid development. Notably, superior hybrids can result not only from parents with high GCA but also from combinations involving parents with contrasting combining abilities, emphasizing the complexity of gene action in controlling yield11.

Plant growth analysis is a common method used in plant physiology, breeding, and ecology to evaluate dry matter accumulation and leaf development, thereby identifying traits that improve plant productivity12. The genetic mechanisms and gene actions that control plant growth parameters provide a theoretical basis for understanding how genotype × environment interactions affect plant development. Traits that adapt to environmental constraints and maintain a steady growth rate (RGR) can be identified through growth analysis across various environments. RGR measures the increase in biomass per unit of above-ground biomass per day (g g⁻¹ day⁻¹); faster dry matter production enhances carbon availability for shoot and root growth, boosting biomass accumulation13. Crop growth rate (CGR) indicates changes in dry weight over time, rising during early development, peaking from booting to heading, and then declining because maximum dry matter production occurs earlier14.

Leaf area index (LAI) is another essential dimensionless measure that describes the total leaf area per unit ground surface area, representing the crop’s ability to absorb light energy15. Achieving a high LAI is a key requirement for high grain yield, as it helps intercept and efficiently convert radiation into dry matter, which is then translocated into grains16. Growth parameters like LAI and CGR at the flowering stage have been identified as crucial factors influencing grain yield. The net assimilation rate (NAR) specifies the net increase in total dry matter per unit of leaf area over time, reflecting photosynthetic efficiency during both light and dark periods14. Ultimately, higher CGR, increased RGR, larger leaf area, and substantial dry matter accumulation during the vegetative stage lead to higher grain yields17. These plant growth parameters work together to enhance yield more effectively than any individual trait alone.

Phenological traits, including days to heading (DTH), days to maturity (DTM), and grain filling duration (GFD), directly influence yield potential in water-limited environments18, 19. Early-heading genotypes escape terminal drought stress, while a longer grain filling period allows more assimilate to accumulate in grains, thereby increasing thousand-grain weight (TGW) and final grain yield16, 17, 20, 21. Likewise, yield components such as plant height (PH), number of tillers per plant (NTPP), spike length (SPL), number of spikelets per spike (NSPS), number of grains per spike (NGPS), and thousand-grain weight (TGW) show complex inheritance patterns controlled by both additive and non-additive gene actions13, 22, 23. Identifying parental lines with advantageous combining abilities for these traits, and understanding their gene action patterns, is essential for developing high-yielding wheat cultivars suited to rainfed conditions24.

Although a variety of wheat germplasm is available, there is limited information on the combining ability and gene action of physiological growth parameters across different phenological stages in rainfed environments in Pakistan. Most prior studies have either focused solely on yield components or on growth parameters at a single time point25, 18, 26, without capturing the dynamic changes in crop growth throughout the growing season. Additionally, the roles of reciprocal effects and maternal inheritance in influencing growth efficiency and yield potential under moisture-stress conditions remain largely unexplored. Filling these knowledge gaps is vital for developing effective breeding strategies that aim to improve both high-yield potential and drought tolerance.

Therefore, the present study was conducted with the following objectives: to clarify the genetic basis of yield-related traits and physiological growth parameters across seven phenological stages (Zadok scale Z-20 to Z-92) through detailed diallel mating design analysis; to describe key growth parameters (CGR, RGR, LAI, NAR) that support biomass accumulation and grain yield in various wheat genotypes under rainfed conditions; to estimate general combining ability (GCA), specific combining ability (SCA), and reciprocal combining ability (RCA) for identifying superior parental lines and hybrid combinations; and to understand the nature and extent of gene action governing these traits to offer useful insights for wheat breeding programs aimed at increasing productivity and adaptation to rainfed environments.

Materials and methods

The study was carried out in two phases. In the first phase (Rabi 2018-19), population development was performed using Griffing’s9 diallel mating design. In the second phase (Rabi 2019-20), genetic analysis of F1 hybrids and parents was conducted to assess morphological, growth, and yield-related traits. A complete diallel mating design was used to estimate inheritance patterns and gene action for growth parameters at various stages and yield traits in bread wheat.

Population development (griffing diallel mating design)

Six wheat genotypes (Pakistan-2013, NARC-2009, NR-499, Borlaug-2016, NR-514, NR-516) obtained from NARC-Islamabad were grown as parental lines in a 6 × 6 complete diallel mating design (Method I, Model I) following Griffing9. The parental lines were selected for their diverse genetic backgrounds and contrasting performance in key agronomic and physiological traits under rainfed conditions in preliminary trials conducted at NARC-Islamabad. This diversity was crucial for capturing a broad range of genetic variation in the diallel analysis. Crosses were made by emasculation and pollination at the reproductive stage, including 15 direct, 15 reciprocal crosses, and 6 parental selfs (Table 1). About 50 crosses per parental combination were performed to produce enough F₀ seed. Emasculation was done carefully before anther dehiscence, and panicles were bagged immediately after pollination to prevent contamination with foreign pollen. At harvest, F₀ seeds from confirmed crosses were identified by their position in the bagged spike. They were visually screened for hybrid vigor and intermediate morphological traits (e.g., glume color and shape) compared with the selfed parents. Any seed lot showing signs of selfing was discarded. The true F₀ seeds were harvested at physiological maturity.

Experimental site

The study was carried out at two crossing blocks: Wheat Program (Crop Science Institute, NARC, Islamabad) and PMAS Arid Agriculture University, Rawalpindi, during Rabi 2018-19. Sowing took place in late October 2018, with harvest in April 2019. Sowing dates were staggered by 14 days between sites to ensure pollen availability.

Experimental description

The F₀ wheat seeds from the 2018-19 crossing program were planted using a dibbler in late November 2019. Each genotype was sown in a single row per replication. Row length was 3 m with a row spacing of 30 cm and a plant spacing of 15 cm. Nitrogen fertilizer was applied in two splits: two-thirds during land preparation and one-third 30 days after sowing, at a rate of 120 kg ha⁻¹. Recommended agronomic practices were followed. The crop was grown under purely rainfed conditions throughout the growing season.

Quantification of growth parameters and yield traits

Growth analysis assesses plant development by measuring biomass accumulation, leaf area expansion, and related physiological parameters under rainfed conditions. To explore the genetic basis of morphological, growth, and yield-related traits, four key physiological growth parameters, i.e., crop growth rate (CGR), relative growth rate (RGR), leaf area index (LAI), and net assimilation rate (NAR), were recorded across seven phenological stages based on Zadok’s scale: Z-20 (Tillering), Z-31 (Stem Elongation), Z-39 (Flag Leaf Ligule), Z-47 (Flag Leaf), Z-60 (Anthesis), Z-81 (Dough), and Z-92 (Maturity). Additionally, agronomic traits including plant height, number of tillers per plant, spike length, number of spikelets per spike, number of grains per spike, thousand-grain weight, grain yield per plant, days to heading, days to maturity, and grain filling duration were evaluated.

At each growth stage, destructive sampling was conducted by randomly selecting five plants per row from each replication. Plants were separated into leaves, stems, and spikes, then oven-dried at 70–80 °C for 72 h. Dry weights were recorded and used to calculate the following growth parameters. A randomized complete block design (RCBD) with three replications was used, and all parental lines, along with their direct and reciprocal F₁ hybrids, were included in the analysis. Traits showing significant genotypic variation (P < 0.01) were analyzed using diallel analysis based on Griffing’s Method-I Model-I9 to estimate combining ability and gene action dynamics.

Phenological and yield-related traits

Other yield-related traits include Plant height (cm), Number of tillers plant− 1, Spike length (cm), Number of spikelet spike− 1 (No.), Number of grains spike− 1 (No.), Thousand grain weight (g), Grain yield plant− 1 (g), Days to heading (days), Days to maturity (days), Grain filling duration (days). Statistical analysis

General statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using ANOVA under a General Linear Model for a RCBD, followed by LSD post hoc testing in IBM SPSS-20 software30.

Combining ability and genetic components

Combining abilities (GCA, SCA, RCA) were estimated using Griffing’s Method-I, Model-I9 in SAS University Edition. Genetic components were analyzed by calculating the GCA: SCA ratio31, to assess the nature of gene action. A full diallel mating design (6 × 6) was used with six genetically diverse bread wheat parental lines. Crosses were made to produce F₁ hybrids, and healthy F₀ seeds were collected for further evaluation.

Genetic diversity

Mean performance and combining ability analysis

Advancing wheat yield under rainfed conditions necessitates a thorough understanding of the genetic mechanisms underlying trait inheritance, particularly the combining ability of potential parental lines. This study estimated general combining ability (GCA), specific combining ability (SCA), and reciprocal combining ability (RCA) to assess additive, non-additive, and maternal effects for key morpho-physiological and yield traits across six diverse parental lines and their F1 hybrids, using Griffing’s Diallel Method I, Model.

ANOVA of combining ability showed highly significant (P < 0.01) mean squares for GCA, SCA, and RCA across all traits and phenological stages, emphasizing the role of both additive and non-additive gene actions (Table 4). Some exceptions were seen in certain crop growth rate (CGR) stages (Z-20 to Z-92), where RCA effects were significant at P < 0.05, suggesting limited maternal influence at those stages. The Baker’s General Predictability Ratio (GPR), which indicates additive gene control, was ≥ 0.70 for most traits, confirming the dominance of additive gene action (Table 4).

Growth parameters

Crop growth rate (CGR) [g (crop) m⁻² day⁻¹]

Analysis of variance showed highly significant differences (P < 0.01) among parental lines, direct crosses, and reciprocal crosses for CGR across all seven phenological stages (Table 3). The Baker’s general predictability ratio (GPR) for CGR was 0.90 at tillering (Z-20), indicating additive gene action, while values ranged from 0.53 to 0.65 at later stages, suggesting non-additive gene action. The GCA: SCA ratio was 4.38:1 at Z-20, confirming additive control at the early stage, and ranged from 0.55:1 to 0.91:1 at later stages, indicating non-additive control (Table 4).

Among parental lines, Pakistan-2013 recorded the highest CGR values across all stages, ranging from 1.86 g m⁻² day⁻¹ at tillering (Z-20) to 11.17 g m⁻² day⁻¹ at anthesis (Z-60). NR-516 showed lower CGR values at early stages (0.92 g m⁻² day⁻¹ at Z-20), while NR-514 had the lowest at maturity (3.21 g m⁻² day⁻¹ at Z-92) (Fig. 1). Among direct F₁ hybrids, Pakistan-2013 × NR-499 exhibited the highest CGR at most growth stages. Borlaug-2016 × NR-516 and Pakistan-2013 × NARC-2009 recorded the lowest CGR values. For reciprocal crosses, Borlaug-2016 × NARC-2009 showed higher CGR throughout the vegetative and reproductive phases, while NR-516 × NR-514 consistently had the lowest values.

Combining ability analysis displayed highly significant (P ≤ 0.01) GCA, SCA, and RCA effects for CGR across different growth stages (Table S1). Among the parents, Pakistan-2013 consistently exhibited significantly positive GCA effects throughout the crop cycle, ranging from 0.169** at the tillering stage (Z-20) to a peak of 0.504** at the flag leaf stage (Z-47), followed by 0.503** at anthesis (Z-60), 0.501** at the dough stage (Z-81), and 0.202** at maturity (Z-92). In contrast, NR-514 showed highly significant negative GCA effects across all stages, ranging from − 0.186** at Z-20 to − 0.502** at maturity (Z-92).

Among direct crosses, several combinations exhibited strong specific combining ability. The cross Borlaug-2016 × NR-516 showed highly significant positive SCA effects, ranging from 0.655** at Z-39 to 0.676** at anthesis (Z-60), and 0.642** at Z-81. In contrast, the cross Pakistan-2013 × NARC-2009 displayed significant negative SCA effects across most stages, ranging from − 0.476* at Z-31 to − 0.472* at Z-60 and − 0.467* at Z-81.

Reciprocal effects further emphasize the importance of maternal influence on CGR expression. The cross NR-499 × Pakistan-2013 showed highly significant positive RCA effects across multiple stages, ranging from 0.808** at Z-31 to Z-47 and increasing to 0.822** at Z-60 and Z-81. In contrast, significant negative reciprocal effects were observed in NR-514 × NR-499, with values ranging from − 0.346* at Z-31 to −0.319ns at later stages, indicating an adverse maternal influence.

Relative growth rate (RGR) [g g⁻¹ day⁻¹]

Analysis of variance revealed highly significant differences (P < 0.01) among parental lines, direct crosses, and reciprocal crosses for RGR across all seven phenological stages (Table 3). The Baker’s GPR for RGR ranged from 0.91 to 0.92 across stages Z-20 to Z-81, indicating strong additive gene action, while at maturity (Z-92) it was 0.69, indicating non-additive control. The GCA: SCA ratios ranged from 5.17:1 to 5.81:1 across vegetative stages, confirming the dominance of additive gene action (Table 4).

Among parental lines, Pakistan-2013 exhibited the highest RGR values during early and mid-growth stages, with 0.089 g g⁻¹ day⁻¹ at tillering (Z-20) and 0.065 g g⁻¹ day⁻¹ at anthesis (Z-60), then declined to −0.021 g g⁻¹ day⁻¹ at maturity (Z-92). NR-514 had lower RGR values, recording 0.045 g g⁻¹ day⁻¹ at Z-20 and − 0.042 g g⁻¹ day⁻¹ at Z-92 (Fig. 2). Among direct F₁ hybrids, Pakistan-2013 × NR-499 showed the highest RGR across most growth stages. Pakistan-2013 × NARC-2009 had the highest RGR at maturity (0.018 g g⁻¹ day⁻¹ at Z-92). NR-514 × NR-516 had the lowest RGR during early stages (0.032 g g⁻¹ day⁻¹ at Z-20), while Pakistan-2013 × Borlaug-2016 showed the lowest at maturity (−0.09 g g⁻¹ day⁻¹ at Z-92). For reciprocal crosses, NR-514 × Pakistan-2013 (0.088 g g⁻¹ day⁻¹ at Z-20) and NR-514 × Borlaug-2016 maintained higher RGR values, whereas NR-516 × NARC-2009 consistently had the lowest values.

Combining ability analysis showed highly significant (P ≤ 0.01) GCA, SCA, and RCA effects for RGR across different growth stages (Table S2). Among the parents, Pakistan-2013 consistently had significant positive GCA effects throughout the crop cycle, ranging from 0.003** at the tillering stage (Z-20) to 0.003** at the flag leaf stage (Z-47), then 0.003** at anthesis (Z-60) and dough stage (Z-81), and a significant negative value of − 0.006** at maturity (Z-92). In contrast, NR-516 displayed significant negative GCA effects during most growth stages, ranging from − 0.005** at Z-20 to Z-81, followed by a positive significant value of 0.002** at maturity (Z-92).

Among direct crosses, several combinations demonstrated strong specific combining ability. The cross Borlaug-2016 × NR-516 showed highly significant positive SCA effects, ranging from 0.004** at the tillering stage (Z-20) to 0.005** at stem elongation (Z-31) and anthesis (Z-60), and reaching 0.008** at maturity (Z-92). In contrast, the cross Pakistan-2013 × NARC-2009 exhibited significant negative SCA effects across most stages, ranging from − 0.003** at Z-20 to − 0.003** at Z-60 and − 0.003* at Z-81, followed by a positive significant effect of 0.009** at maturity (Z-92).

Reciprocal effects further emphasized the significance of maternal influence on RGR expression. The cross NR-516 × Pakistan-2013 showed highly significant positive RCA effects at multiple stages, from 0.007** at Z-20 to Z-81 and 0.004** at maturity (Z-92). Conversely, significant negative reciprocal effects were observed in Borlaug-2016 × Pakistan-2013 (− 0.037** at Z-92) and in NR-514 × Pakistan-2013 (− 0.031** at Z-92).

Leaf area index (LAI) [m² leaf m⁻² ground]

Analysis of variance revealed highly significant differences (P < 0.01) among parental lines, direct crosses, and reciprocal crosses for LAI across all seven phenological stages (Table 3). Among parental lines, Pakistan-2013 showed the highest LAI values at all stages, with readings of 2.45 at tillering (Z-20), 4.32 at stem elongation (Z-31), 5.67 at flag leaf ligule (Z-39), peaking at 6.32 at flag leaf (Z-47), followed by 6.54 at anthesis (Z-60), 4.21 at dough (Z-81), and 1.23 at maturity (Z-92). The crosses NR-514 × NR-516 and Borlaug-2016 × NR-514 exhibited the lowest LAI values. For reciprocal crosses, NR-499 × Pakistan-2013 demonstrated strong positive effects, while NR-516 × NR-514 showed consistently negative values.

Combining ability analysis revealed highly significant (P ≤ 0.01) GCA, SCA, and RCA effects for LAI across different growth stages (Table S3). Among the parents, Pakistan-2013 consistently showed significant positive GCA effects throughout the crop cycle, ranging from 0.120** at the tillering stage (Z-20) to 0.120** at the flag leaf stage (Z-47), followed by 0.122** at anthesis (Z-60), 0.104** at the dough stage (Z-81), and 0.038** at maturity (Z-92). In contrast, the cross Pakistan-2013 × NR-516 displayed significant negative SCA effects at all stages, ranging from − 0.086** at Z-20 to − 0.094** at Z-81 and − 0.030* at maturity (Z-92).

Discussion

The genetic analysis of growth parameters and yield-related traits in bread wheat provides a detailed framework for understanding plant development across different phenological stages and their effects on yield potential under rainfed conditions. This study combines combining ability analysis with multi-stage growth dynamics to reveal the genetic structure influencing productivity in water-limited environments, identifying superior parents and cross combinations while explaining the physiological basis for yield improvement.

Genetic architecture and combining ability

The study found highly significant (P < 0.01) genetic variation among genotypes for all 38 traits (Table 3), confirming considerable diversity within the wheat germplasm examined. This diversity offers avenues to select parent lines with beneficial alleles for rainfed adaptation. Using Griffing’s Diallel Method-I Model-I9, combining ability analysis indicated that both additive and non-additive gene actions influence trait inheritance, with their importance differing across traits and developmental stages. Similar genetic variability has also been observed in wheat33, 34, underscoring its importance for developing stress-tolerant cultivars.

The dominance of additive gene action for most traits, indicated by Baker’s General Predictability Ratio (GPR) ≥ 0.70 and GCA: SCA ratios > 1 (Table 4), suggests that early-generation selection would be effective for improving these traits. Baker first established that GPR values approaching unity enable reliable selection in early segregating generations32, a principle later validated by subsequent studies35, 36. Stage-dependent gene action was evident across traits: additive effects predominated during early developmental stages, while non-additive effects governed later reproductive stages (Table 4). This shift over time has important breeding implications, early-generation pedigree selection would be effective for improving initial vigor, while heterosis breeding may be more beneficial for enhancing reproductive-stage performance.

High GCA effects arise from additive gene effects and additive × additive interactions, making lines with significant positive GCA valuable for improving specific traits through selection. Pakistan-2013 consistently showed the highest positive GCA effects across multiple traits, establishing it as the most valuable parental line for rainfed wheat improvement. SCA effects are essential for identifying superior cross combinations and estimating non-additive gene action17. In self-pollinated crops like wheat, significant SCA in homozygous lines can indicate potential for transgressive segregation in advanced generations37. Significant RCA effects highlight the importance of maternal and cytoplasmic influences on agronomic performance38, 39, underscoring the need for careful selection of the female parent in crossing programs. The strong reciprocal effects observed, especially in crosses involving NR-499 as the female parent, demonstrate that cytoplasmic inheritance plays a significant role in determining adaptive performance under rainfed conditions.

Growth dynamics and physiological basis of yield

Plant growth parameters (CGR, RGR, LAI, NAR) collectively govern biomass accumulation and dry matter partitioning in wheat canopies14, 29. Under rainfed conditions, these physiological traits directly affect radiation interception, carbon fixation, and assimilate allocation to reproductive structures, making them critical criteria for yield enhancement34, 40, 41, 42. The multi-stage analysis showed dynamic genetic control throughout crop development, with mean performance gradually increasing from early stages (Z-20) to a peak at reproductive stages (Z-60 to Z-81), then declining toward maturity (Z-92)14, 43, 44, 45.

Crop growth rate (CGR) measures biomass production per land area and time, reflecting physiological efficiency in wheat canopies14, 29, 46. Consistent CGR throughout the growing season directly supports reproductive development and grain filling, making it a vital trait for yield enhancement34, 40, 41, 42. Pakistan-2013 maintained higher CGR across all stages (1.86–11.17 g m⁻² day⁻¹), with GCA effects ranging from 0.169** to 0.504**, highlighting it as an excellent donor for biomass improvement. The positive values of GCA, SCA, and RCA effects collectively suggest that additive, non-additive, and maternal gene actions govern CGR inheritance47, with the gradual decline in dry matter accumulation throughout the season supporting increased thousand-grain weight and overall productivity22, 48, 49.

The cross Borlaug-2016 × NR-516 exhibited the highest positive SCA effects during reproductive stages (0.655** to 0.676), indicating strong heterotic potential for post-anthesis biomass accumulation. This suggests that specific parental combinations can generate superior CGR through complementary gene action even when individual parents show moderate GCA. Reciprocal effects were strongest in NR-499 × Pakistan-2013 (0.808 to 0.822**), highlighting maternal influence on CGR expression and emphasizing the importance of female parent selection38, 39, 50. Stage-dependent gene action was particularly evident for CGR: at tillering (Z-20), GCA: SCA = 4.38:1 and Baker’s GPR = 0.90 indicated strong additive control, enabling early-generation selection32, 36, 51. However, from stem elongation onward, ratios decreased below one (0.55:1 to 0.91:1), with Baker’s GPR less than 0.70, indicating a transition to non-additive control during reproductive stages. This temporal shift suggests that while early vigor can be improved through pedigree selection, enhancing post-anthesis biomass may require exploiting heterosis through hybrid breeding or recurrent selection, with selection postponed until F₄ or F₅ generations to allow the fixation of additive variance.

Relative growth rate (RGR) measures biomass increase per unit of existing dry matter, reflecting growth efficiency and resource allocation. Sustained RGR during early vegetative stages ensures rapid canopy establishment, providing a foundation for subsequent reproductive development and grain filling. Pakistan-2013 exhibited the highest RGR during early and mid-stages (0.089 to 0.065 g g⁻¹ day⁻¹), with significant positive GCA effects across vegetative stages (0.003). The shift to negative GCA at maturity (−0.006) reflects the physiological transition toward senescence and assimilate remobilization, a desirable trait for efficient grain filling. NR-516 showed significant negative GCA throughout vegetative development (−0.005), confirming its limited potential for early growth efficiency, followed by positive GCA at maturity (0.002), indicating a different physiological strategy focused on late-season remobilization.

The decline in RGR with advancing growth stages, reaching negative values at maturity (Fig. 2), reflects canopy self-shading, assimilate diversion to reproductive structures, and accelerated senescence52, 53. For SCA effects, Borlaug-2016 × NR-516 showed significant positive values across stages (0.004** to 0.008), with the highest effect at maturity, indicating strong heterotic potential for growth efficiency during the critical grain-filling period. Reciprocal effects were most noticeable in NR-516 × Pakistan-2013 (0.007 to 0.004**), highlighting maternal contributions to growth efficiency and emphasizing the importance of reciprocal crossing strategies to capture cytoplasmic influences16. Across vegetative stages, GCA: SCA ratios ranged from 5.17:1 to 5.81:1 with Baker’s GPR of 0.91–0.92, confirming strong additive control and high heritability17. At maturity, the GCA: SCA ratio of 1.10:1 and Baker’s GPR of 0.69 indicate a shift toward greater non-additive influence, though additive effects remain significant.

Leaf area index (LAI) determines light interception capacity and photosynthetic potential, directly influencing biomass accumulation15. Sustained LAI during reproductive stages ensures efficient radiation interception and dry matter transformation, making it a key determinant of yield potential20. Pakistan-2013 exhibited the highest LAI across all stages (2.45 to 6.32), with GCA effects ranging from 0.038** to 0.122**, establishing it as the leading donor for enhancing canopy development. The progressive increase during vegetative growth, peaking at flag leaf emergence (Z-47) followed by decline toward maturity (Fig. 3), aligns with typical canopy development patterns in cereals38. The post-flag leaf decline reflects leaf senescence, aging, and thermal stress, physiological processes that influence the duration of photosynthetic activity during grain filling.

For SCA effects, NR-514 × NR-516 showed significant positive values across stages (0.190** to 0.197), with the highest at anthesis. This demonstrates that even poor general combiners can produce transgressive segregants with increased LAI when combined properly, indicating that heterosis or complementary gene action can generate superior canopy development beyond parental expectations54. Reciprocal effects were strongest in NR-499 × Pakistan-2013 (0.192 to 0.128**), highlighting cytoplasmic factors in leaf area development and emphasizing the importance of female parent selection for optimal canopy architecture. Across all stages, GCA: SCA ratios ranged from 2.19:1 to 4.03:1, with Baker’s GPR of 0.81–0.89, confirming strong additive control throughout canopy development32. The heritable nature of additive variance suggests that favorable alleles for increased LAI can be effectively accumulated through recurrent selection, leading to significant genetic gains in later generations42.

Net assimilation rate (NAR) measures photosynthetic efficiency and radiation-use efficiency, reflecting the plant’s ability to convert intercepted radiation into biomass34, 55. High NAR enables efficient carbon fixation and sustained growth, making it a key factor in stress tolerance and yield stability55. Pakistan-2013 showed superior NAR across most stages (1.23 to 1.72 g m⁻² day⁻¹), with GCA effects ranging from 0.074** to 0.145**, establishing it as an excellent donor for improving photosynthetic efficiency. Peak values at the flag leaf stage (Z-39) coincide with maximum photosynthetic activity and increased leaf area (Fig. 4), reflecting the enhanced photosynthetic capability of superior genotypes.

For SCA effects, NR-514 × NR-516 showed significant positive values across stages (0.130** to 0.190), with the highest at anthesis and dough stages. This suggests that heterosis can be used to improve NAR through specific parental combinations, even in cases of low general combining ability. Reciprocal effects were strongest in NR-499 × Pakistan-2013 (0.192), confirming maternal contributions to photosynthetic performance via cytoplasmic inheritance (chloroplast and mitochondrial genomes). Across all stages, GCA: SCA ratios ranged from 2.28:1 to 5.36:1, with Baker’s GPR of 0.82–0.91, indicating strong additive control throughout the crop cycle. The consistent additive control suggests that pedigree selection would be very effective for improving NAR and photosynthetic efficiency under rainfed conditions. Additionally, significant SCA and RCA effects show that specific cross combinations and maternal selection can further enhance carbon fixation capacity during critical reproductive stages22.

Genetic control of agronomic and yield traits

Plant height (PH) influences crop architecture, lodging resistance, and resource allocation. Under rainfed conditions, optimal height balances enough biomass accumulation with lower lodging risk in wind-prone areas7, 56, 57. While taller plants produce more vegetative biomass and are better at capturing light, excessive height raises lodging risk, making semi-dwarf types desirable for rainfed conditions. NARC-2009 had the shortest height (102.5 cm), and NR-516 the tallest (113.9 cm). GCA effects identified NARC-2009 as the best donor for reducing height (−3.310), followed by Pakistan-2013 (−1.029). For SCA effects, NARC-2009 × NR-499 showed the largest negative value (−5.163), indicating transgressive segregation for shorter height. Reciprocal effects were significant, with NR-499 × NARC-2009 (−10.50), showing strong maternal influence38, 39. The GCA: SCA ratio (1.69:1) and Baker’s GPR (0.77) confirmed additive inheritance, supporting early-generation pedigree selection58.

The number of tillers per plant (NTPP) directly affects spike-bearing capacity and final grain yield22, 23, 59. Efficient and fertile tillers directly contribute to grain production, making NTPP an important selection criterion. NARC-2009 had the highest tiller count (13.2), while NR-514 had the lowest (8.2). Among crosses, Pakistan-2013 × NR-499 produced the most tillers (16.1). GCA effects identified Pakistan-2013 as the best general combiner (1.399), followed by NR-499 (0.520). For SCA effects, Borlaug-2016 × NR-516 (1.458) and Pakistan-2013 × NR-499 (1.428) showed strong positive values, indicating complementary gene action for tiller initiation and survival. Reciprocal effects were strongest in NR-514 × Pakistan-2013 (2.500**). The GCA: SCA ratio (6.52:1) and Baker’s GPR (0.93) confirmed high heritability and additive effects, enabling effective early-generation pedigree selection.

Spike length (SPL) determines spikelet number and potential grain set23, 60. Longer spikes can hold more spikelets, leading to higher grain numbers and increased final yield. NARC-2009 had the longest spikes (15.7 cm), while NR-514 had the shortest (11.2 cm). Among crosses, Pakistan-2013 × NR-499 produced the longest spikes (19.0 cm). GCA effects identified Pakistan-2013 as the best general combiner (1.425), followed by NR-499 (0.517). For SCA effects, Borlaug-2016 × NR-516 (1.269) and Pakistan-2013 × NR-499 (1.431) showed strong positive values, indicating that non-additive effects can be exploited to further increase spike length. Reciprocal effects were most prominent in NR-514 × Pakistan-2013 (2.500**). The GCA: SCA ratio (6.11:1) and Baker’s GPR (0.92) confirmed high heritability and the dominance of additive effects36, 61. Spike length is influenced by rachis internode extension, suggesting that pedigree selection for elongated rachises could improve spike architecture20.

The number of spikelets per spike (NSPS) directly influences floret count and potential grain yield13, 23, 42. Pakistan-2013 had the highest NSPS (22.6), while NR-514 had the lowest (16.0). GCA effects identified Pakistan-2013 as the top general performer (1.090). Regarding SCA effects, Borlaug-2016 × NR-516 (1.458) indicated that non-additive effects (dominance or epistasis) could produce additional gains beyond parental expectations42. Reciprocal effects confirmed maternal influences, with NR-499 × Pakistan-2013 showing the highest positive RCA (3.00*). Spikelet number is determined by meristem activity and assimilate allocation during early reproductive development62, 63. The GCA: SCA ratio (1.48:1) and Baker’s GPR (0.75) suggest that while additive effects are significant, non-additive effects also play a key role in spikelet development23.

The number of grains per spike (NGPS) is the most direct factor influencing yield potential, showing a positive correlation with economic yield13, 42. Pakistan-2013 had the highest NGPS at 61.1, while NR-514 had the lowest at 45.7. Among crosses, Pakistan-2013 × NR-499 exhibited the highest NGPS at 65.1. GCA effects identified Pakistan-2013 as the top general combiner (3.693), followed by NR-499 (1.384). For SCA effects, Borlaug-2016 × NR-516 showed the highest positive value at 7.627, indicating one of the strongest heterotic responses observed and highlighting strong non-additive interactions. Grain number depends on floret survival and assimilate partitioning during the critical period around anthesis62, 63. Reciprocal effects confirmed maternal contributions, with NR-499 × Pakistan-2013 displaying the highest positive RCA at 4.933 and NR-499 × NARC-2009 showing the largest negative at −6.267**, consistent with previous reports38, 39. The GCA: SCA ratio of 3.84:1 and Baker’s GPR of 0.88 indicated high heritability and dominance of additive effects37, 64.

Thousand grain weight (TGW) reflects kernel size and sink strength during grain filling, directly influencing final grain yield23, 65. Even with the same number of grains per spike, variations in kernel weight can significantly affect productivity. Pakistan-2013 had the heaviest grains (47.3 g), while NR-514 had the lightest (32.0 g). The cross between Pakistan-2013 and NR-499 showed the highest TGW (51.2 g). GCA effects identified Pakistan-2013 as the top general combiner (3.785), followed by NR-499 (1.255). Among SCA effects, Borlaug-2016 × NR-516 (5.626) displayed the highest positive value, indicating that heterosis can be used to improve TGW through specific parental combinations. Crosses between complementary parents often produce progeny with higher kernel weight through complementary gene action affecting grain development65. Reciprocal effects confirmed maternal contributions, with NR-499 × Pakistan-2013 showing the highest positive RCA (4.933) and NR-499 × NARC-2009 the largest negative (−6.267**), highlighting maternal influence on kernel development via cytoplasmic factors that affect grain filling and assimilate transport38, 39. The GCA: SCA ratio (4.39:1) and Baker’s GPR (0.90) suggest high heritability and the dominance of additive gene effects37, 64.

Grain yield per plant (GYPP) consolidates all yield components and physiological processes, making it the ultimate indicator of breeding success13, 22, 23, 66. Under rainfed conditions, yield stability relies on the combined expression of multiple traits throughout the growing season. Pakistan-2013 had the highest GYPP (27.6 g), while NR-514 had the lowest (12.2 g). The cross Pakistan-2013 × NR-499 showed the highest yield among the crosses (31.5 g). GCA effects identified Pakistan-2013 as the best general combiner (3.556), followed by NR-499 (1.326). For SCA effects, Borlaug-2016 × NR-516 (7.471) recorded the highest positive value, indicating strong heterotic potential and suggesting that these parents carry complementary favorable alleles affecting multiple physiological pathways67. Crosses between genetically diverse parents often produce transgressive segregants through complementary gene action and favorable epistatic interactions12. Reciprocal crosses confirmed significant maternal effects, with NR-499 × Pakistan-2013 showing the highest positive RCA (4.929) and NR-499 × NARC-2009 exhibiting the largest negative value (−6.267**), aligning with several studies demonstrating cytoplasmic contributions to yield potential16, 38. The GCA: SCA ratio (3.26:1) and Baker’s GPR (0.87) confirmed high heritability and the predominance of additive effects36, 37, 68.

Phenological traits and environmental adaptation

Days to heading (DTH) is a vital phenological trait that influences wheat adaptation and yield potential in specific environments16, 17. Early heading genotypes avoid terminal drought stress and have longer post-heading periods, allowing extended grain filling and better assimilate accumulation12. In rainfed conditions, the optimal heading date balances vegetative growth with reproductive development to maximize yield. NARC-2009 had the earliest heading at 108.9 days, while NR-516 was the latest at 124.1 days. Negative GCA effects, which favor earliness, were observed in Pakistan-2013 (−2.811) and NARC-2009 (−2.191). NR-516 displayed a significant positive GCA (3.420), indicating a tendency toward late heading, which is disadvantageous under terminal stress. Regarding SCA effects, NARC-2009 × NR-516 showed a favorable negative SCA (−2.685), promoting earliness through non-additive gene action. Reciprocal effects were notable in NR-516 × Borlaug-2016 (6.333), highlighting maternal influences on flowering time20. The GCA: SCA ratio (4.29:1) and Baker’s GPR (0.90) confirmed high heritability and the predominance of additive effects16, 69, supporting effective early-generation pedigree selection for optimal heading date.

Days to maturity (DTM) determines the length of the reproductive phase and directly influences grain filling, yield potential, and adaptation across different environments21, 66. Early maturity combined with a prolonged post-anthesis period allows for extended grain filling, which increases yield under stress-prone conditions10, 58. NARC-2009 had the earliest maturity at 148.2 days, while NR-514 was the latest at 163.5 days. Negative GCA effects were observed in Pakistan-2013 (−2.970) and NARC-2009 (−1.940). For SCA effects, NR-499 × Borlaug-2016 showed a favorable negative SCA (−2.834), suggesting early maturity. Reciprocal effects were significant in NR-516 × Borlaug-2016 (7.633**), indicating maternal contributions58, 70. The GCA: SCA ratio (3.70:1) and Baker’s GPR (0.88) confirmed high heritability and predominantly additive effects48, 60, 66, supporting effective early-generation pedigree selection for optimal maturity duration.

Grain filling duration (GFD), the period from anthesis to physiological maturity, directly affects kernel development and final grain weight16, 66. A longer GFD allows for more translocation and accumulation of photo-assimilates in grains, enhancing yield potential, especially under stress conditions where rapid grain filling may be limited71. Pakistan-2013 had the longest GFD at 46.1 days, while NR-514 had the shortest at 31.4 days. The cross between Pakistan-2013 and NR-499 showed the longest duration at 47.1 days. Positive GCA effects were observed for NARC-2009 (1.373), NR-499 (1.255), and Pakistan-2013 (0.719*), indicating their potential to extend the grain filling period through additive genetic effects. For SCA effects, Borlaug-2016 × NR-516 (5.838) showed the highest positive value, followed by NR-514 × NR-516 (3.657), highlighting strong heterotic potential for extending GFD via complementary gene action affecting senescence and assimilate transport. Reciprocal effects were strongest in NR-499 × Pakistan-2013 (5.725), while NR-516 × Borlaug-2016 (−4.283) showed the most negative effect, emphasizing maternal influence on grain filling dynamics16, 71. The GCA: SCA ratio (1.22:1) and Baker’s GPR (0.71) suggest moderate to high heritability with significant non-additive contributions. While additive effects support early-generation pedigree selection72, 25, the presence of substantial non-additive variance indicates that hybrid breeding strategies may also be effective for optimizing GFD through heterosis exploitation71.

Breeding implications and future directions

Pakistan-2013 and Borlaug-2016, with consistent positive GCA effects across multiple traits, are invaluable genetic resources for rainfed wheat improvement. Pakistan-2013 demonstrated superior combining ability for all growth parameters (CGR, RGR, LAI, NAR), yield components, and phenological traits, making it the most valuable parent for multi-trait enhancement. Borlaug-2016 contributed strong GCA for yield-related traits and, when combined with NR-516, produced the highest SCA effects observed (5.626** for TGW, 7.471** for GYPP), among the strongest heterotic responses documented in wheat. Notable crosses, particularly Pakistan-2013 × NR-499 and Borlaug-2016 × NR-516, should be advanced for variety development. Pakistan-2013 × NR-499 excelled across all growth parameters and yield components, resulting in maximum grain yield (31.5 g plant⁻¹), showing that combining superior physiological traits directly boosts productivity. Borlaug-2016 × NR-516 should be prioritized for hybrid development or population improvement programs given its exceptional SCA effects. The predominant additive gene action for most traits supports early-generation pedigree selection7, 48, 58, 69, while significant non-additive effects on reproductive-stage traits indicate that hybrid breeding could be effective for exploiting heterosis42. Strong reciprocal effects emphasize the importance of maternal parent selection and cytoplasmic inheritance38, 39, with crosses using NR-499 as the female parent showing superior adaptive performance under rainfed conditions.

Integrating growth parameters with yield component analysis provides a comprehensive understanding of yield physiology34, 42. The superiority of Pakistan-2013 × NR-499 across all growth parameters and its highest grain yield demonstrate that sustained biomass accumulation, efficient radiation interception, and high photosynthetic efficiency collectively determine yield potential under rainfed conditions73, 74. This is achieved through maintained CGR for continuous assimilate supply, sustained RGR reflecting efficient resource use, high LAI optimizing light capture, and superior NAR indicating efficient radiation conversion. Future research should focus on QTL mapping or genome-wide association studies (GWAS) in populations derived from superior parents, especially Pakistan-2013, to facilitate marker-assisted selection. The most promising crosses (Pakistan-2013 × NR-499 and Borlaug-2016 × NR-516) should be advanced to F₂ and subsequent generations to evaluate transgressive segregation and develop improved varieties suited for rainfed environments.

Conclusion

This study revealed the dominance of additive gene action affecting growth and yield traits in bread wheat under rainfed conditions, with GCA: SCA ratios exceeding one and Baker’s GPR values ≥ 0.70 for most traits, confirming the viability of early-generation selection. Pakistan-2013 and Borlaug-2016 were identified as superior parents based on their consistent positive GCA effects across multiple traits. Crosses such as Pakistan-2013 × NR-499 and Borlaug-2016 × NARC-2009 performed well across growth efficiency, spike fertility, grain weight, and yield, demonstrating strong SCA effects and heterosis potential. Reciprocal effects, especially in NR-499 × Pakistan-2013, highlighted the importance of maternal inheritance in adaptive performance under rainfed conditions. By combining key physiological traits with yield components, this work provides a genetic framework for developing ideotypes that prioritize productivity and resilience in water-scarce environments. These findings provide a foundation for breeding programs to improve climate resilience in wheat.
