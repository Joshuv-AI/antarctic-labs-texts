Mindfulness enhancement through logotherapy in patients with mastectomized breast cancer: a randomized controlled clinical trial
Authors: Mahgol Nasr-Esfahani, Fatemeh Ghaedi-Heidari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMindfulness, a psychological capacity, is often diminished in patients with breast cancer. This decline can negatively impact mental and physical health, behavioral regulation, and interpersonal relationships. Consequently, interventions to enhance mindfulness are crucial. This study aimed to evaluate the effect of logotherapy on mindfulness levels in patients with mastectomized breast cancer in Isfahan, Iran. This randomized controlled trial (IRCT20240310061242N1| 24/03/2024) investigated the effects of logotherapy on mindfulness in 64 patients with mastectomized breast cancer at Seyyed-al-Shohada Hospital, Isfahan, Iran. Participants were recruited via convenience sampling and randomly assigned to either the intervention (n = 30) or control (n = 34) group. The intervention group received eight logotherapy sessions (twice a week, 45–60 min each) over four weeks. Data were collected using a demographic questionnaire and the Five Facet Mindfulness Questionnaire (FFMQ-39). SPSS 22 was used for data analysis. Descriptive statistics and Chi-square test, t-test, ANOVA, Pearson correlation, and ANCOVA were used. The level of statistical siginificance was determined at α = 0.05. After the intervention, the control group’s mean (SD) mindfulness score was 121.17 (13.78), and the intervention group’s mean (SD) score was 129.76 (17.05). Data analysis using ANCOVA showed a significant difference in mindfulness between the control and intervention groups after the intervention (p = 0.003). Additionally, a significant relationship was found between occupation and mindfulness only in the intervention group (p = 0.01). Given the significant impact of logotherapy on enhancing mindfulness in patients with breast cancer, it is recommended that logotherapy be used as an effective method to improve patients’ well-being.Keywords: Breast cancer, Mindfulness, Logotherapy, PatientsSubject terms: Cancer, Psychology

Introduction

Breast cancer remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality among women globally. In 2022, it accounted for approximately 670,000 deaths worldwide, and was the most prevalent cancer among women in 157 of 185 countries, according to the World Health Organization (WHO)1. Projections indicate a significant increase in incidence, with estimates reaching 3 million new cases annually by 2040, and a corresponding mortality rate of 1 million2. Notably, Iran has also experienced a substantial rise in breast cancer incidence and mortality3. Data from the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) reveal 15,492 new cases and 5,305 deaths in Iranian women in 2022, making it the most common cancer in the country4.

Treatment strategies for breast cancer, tailored to individual patient characteristics and disease stage, typically involve a combination of surgery, radiation therapy, and chemotherapy1. Mastectomy, a common surgical intervention, can lead to significant physical and psychological problems, including body image disturbances, depression, anxiety, existential loneliness, death anxiety, demoralization, and diminished mindfulness5,6. These psychological challenges are associated with reduced quality of life and survival rates7.

Mindfulness, a psychological capacity, is frequently altered in patients with breast cancer. Defined as the moment-to-moment, focused, and non-judgmental awareness and acceptance of internal and external experiences, mindfulness plays a crucial role in mitigating negative affect and promoting positive outcomes across various domains, including mental and physical health, behavioral regulation, and interpersonal relationships8. Consistent with these findings, a 2018 study in Israel reported low mindfulness levels in lung cancer patients, with older participants exhibiting lower levels9.

To address the psychological challenges faced by patients with breast cancer, various interventions are employed, including logotherapy, meaning-centered psychotherapy, acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT), cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT), and exercise programs like diaphragmatic breathing training. These interventions aim to enhance patients’ coping mechanisms and improve their quality of life10,11.

Logotherapy, an existential approach developed by Viktor Frankl, aims to enhance psychological well-being by facilitating the discovery of meaning in life12,13.This therapeutic modality assists individuals in finding purpose, even amidst adversity, by emphasizing personal strengths, values, responsibility, and choice14. A core principle of logotherapy is that meaning is derived from awareness of life goals and fundamental motivations15. A central tenet of logotherapy is that meaning is unconditional and can be found in any circumstance, irrespective of suffering or illness16. This approach teaches individuals to transform negative experiences into opportunities for achievement through adopting constructive attitudes17.

Logotherapy, by focusing on the discovery of meaning in life, holds promise for mitigating psychological distress18. Research demonstrates its efficacy in reducing depression, anxiety, and posttraumatic stress, as well as enhancing meaning and quality of life in patients with breast and uterine cancer19. Furthermore, logotherapy has been shown to alleviate death anxiety and existential loneliness in patients with advanced cancer20. In previous studies, limited attention has been given to mindfulness as an outcome of logotherapy. Moreover, most of the existing research has been conducted in Western contexts, often with heterogeneous cancer populations rather than specifically with mastectomized women21,22. These studies are further constrained by small sample sizes and limited cultural sensitivity, which restrict the generalizability of their findings. Considering that women in Iran may face unique cultural, social, and healthcare-related challenges following mastectomy—such as strong family involvement in decision-making, limited access to psycho-oncology services, and cultural perceptions of femininity and body image—it is important to investigate the effect of logotherapy within this specific context. Given the significant physical and psychological burden of cancer, interventions to address these challenges are crucial. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the effect of logotherapy on mindfulness in this population. If effective, this intervention could empower patients to find meaning amidst illness, foster a positive outlook, and cultivate mindful acceptance of present moment experiences.

Methods

Study design and setting

A two-group non-blinded randomized controlled trial (RCT) with a pretest–posttest design was conducted at Seyyed-al-Shohada Hospital affiliated with Isfahan University of Medical Sciences, Iran (IRCT20240310061242N1| http://www.irct.ir/| 24/03/2024).

Sample size and sampling

This study recruited 64 patients with mastectomized breast cancer from Seyyed Al-Shohada Hospital, Isfahan University of Medical Sciences, Iran, via convenience due to the limited availability of eligible mastectomized breast cancer patients. Sampling was carried out between June and November 2024. An a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power (version 3.1) for a repeated-measures ANOVA examining the group × time interaction (two groups × two measurements: pretest and posttest). Assuming a medium effect size (f = 0.28), an alpha level of 0.05 (two-tailed), statistical power of 0.80, and an expected correlation of 0.50 between repeated measures, the required sample size was estimated to be 64 participants in total (32 per group). Inclusion criteria were willingness to participate, literacy, Breast cancer as initial diagnosis, completion of post-mastectomy recovery, absence of psychiatric disorders, and no significant life stressors (e.g., death of a loved one, divorce) within the past six months. Exclusion criteria included incomplete questionnaires and concurrent participation in other counseling programs.

Data were collected using two instruments:1 a demographic questionnaire assessing age, marital status, occupation, and chronic disease history, and (2) the 39-item Five Facet Mindfulness Questionnaire (FFMQ-39). The FFMQ-39 utilizes a 5-point Likert scale (1 = never/rarely correct, 5 = always correct) and measures five facets of mindfulness: observing (items 1, 6, 11, 15, 20, 26, 31, 36), describing (items 2, 7, 12, 16, 22, 27, 32, 37), acting with awareness (items 5, 8, 13, 18, 23, 28, 34, 38), non-judging (items 3, 10, 14, 17, 25, 30, 35, 39), and non-reactivity (items 4, 9, 19, 21, 24, 29, 33). Items for acting with awareness and non-judging are reverse-scored.

The FFMQ-39 yields scores ranging from 39 to 195, with higher scores indicating greater mindfulness. Tamanayifar et al. (2015) validated this instrument in a student population at Tehran University. Confirmatory factor analysis confirmed the five-factor structure, demonstrating acceptable model fit. Test–retest reliability, assessed via correlation coefficients, ranged from 0.76 to 0.86 for individual facets and was 0.89 for the total score. Internal consistency, measured by Cronbach’s alpha, ranged from 0.63 for non-reactivity to 0.87 for acting with awareness, with an overall alpha of 0.8523.

Following ethical approval, 71 patients with mastectomized breast cancer were recruited from oncology departments using convenience sampling. Three patients were excluded for failing to meet inclusion criteria, and one declined participation. The remaining 67 participants were randomly assigned to a control group (n = 35) or an intervention group (n = 36) using Kitset random number generator. Both groups completed baseline questionnaires. The intervention group then received logotherapy sessions. Post-intervention questionnaires were administered to both groups. One participant in the control group did not complete the posttest, and six participants from the intervention group were excluded due to missing more than two sessions. Consequently, data from 34 participants in the control group and 30 participants in the intervention group were analyzed (Fig. 1).

Consort flow diagram of the participants.

The intervention group received eight logotherapy sessions, delivered twice weekly for four weeks (45–60 min per session), as detailed in Table 1. Sessions were conducted face-to-face by M.N., a psychiatric nurse practitioner with logotherapy expertise. Due to hospitalization constraints, some sessions were held during inpatient stays, while others were scheduled post-discharge. Prior to the intervention, a contact list of participants in the intervention group was created to facilitate post-discharge session coordination. A multidisciplinary team of 10 experts, including psychiatric nurses, psychiatrists, and clinical psychologists, validated the logotherapy content. The control group received routine care and education. To minimize information contamination, the control group was recruited before the intervention group.

Ethical consideration

The Ethics Committee of Isfahan University of Medical Sciences approved this study ethically (ethical code: IR.MUI.NUREMA.REC.1402.188). After receiving the ethical code, the research setting, including the oncology department of Seyyed-al-Shohada Hospital affiliated with Isfahan University of Medical Sciences, Iran, was visited and sampling was done among the patients based on the inclusion criteria. Following explanation of the research objectives and assurance of confidentiality, patients provided informed consent. In accordance with ethical guidelines, the control group received the intervention content upon study completion.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed by SPSS 22. Descriptive statistics (frequency distribution tables, percentage, mean and standard deviation) was applied to describe sample demographic characteristics. Chi-square test, t-test, ANOVA, Pearson correlation, and ANCOVA were used. P value of 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Result

The mean (SD) age was 48.06 (9.03) in the control group and 43.57 (9.073) in the intervention group. No significant between-group differences were observed in age, marital status, occupation, or chronic disease history. However, a significant difference was found in education level (χ2 = 11.2, p = 0.01) (Table 2).

Discussion

This study investigated the impact of logotherapy on mindfulness in patients with mastectomized breast cancer. The findings demonstrated a significant increase in mean mindfulness scores within the intervention group, indicating that logotherapy effectively enhanced mindfulness in this population. This result aligns with Dadras’s study (2023), which showed a significant post-intervention increase in mindfulness scores among older participants receiving Rumi-based meaning therapy. Baseline analysis revealed no statistically significant difference in mindfulness scores between the control and intervention groups. Following the intervention, the intervention group demonstrated a significant increase in mean mindfulness scores24. Although Dadras’s intervention differed with the one used in this study, both studies incorporated concepts of life meaning, suffering, and finding meaning. This suggests that logotherapy, like other meaning-centered interventions, can address the unique psychological challenges faced by cancer patients. From diagnosis onwards, these individuals encounter a novel and uncertain life context, often accompanied by physical and psychological symptoms such as depression, anxiety, existential loneliness, death anxiety, and diminished mindfulness. These challenges significantly impair daily functioning and restrict social participation6,25.

These stressful conditions can impede an individual’s acceptance of illness as a universal human experience26. Diminished mindfulness exacerbates this by fostering rumination on past negativity or future anxieties, intensifying negative emotions27. Logotherapy suggests that the presence of purpose and meaning enables individuals to overcome psychological adversity and assume responsibility for their existence by leveraging their freedom of choice28. Logotherapy aims to assist individuals in discovering meaning within adversity, grounded in Viktor Frankl’s belief that meaning in life provides profound resilience, even in extreme circumstances. It encourages a shift from focusing on illness to finding purpose and meaning amidst it29. Consequently, educational interventions emphasizing meaning, purpose, values, responsibility, and peer support can facilitate acceptance and promote proactive coping.

Logotherapy facilitates the discovery of meaning, guiding individuals to identify inherent meaning within unavoidable challenges and suffering18. Emphasizing individual uniqueness, it rejects a universal meaning of life, instead proposing that meaning is contextual and personally constructed17,30. Regardless of life stage or circumstance, including severe conditions like cancer, finding meaning remains attainable. This pursuit of personal meaning empowers individuals to endure suffering and effectively manage adversity30.

Group logotherapy in the present study allowed individuals to empathize with each other and provided them with an opportunity to share their experiences. Interacting with peers helped patients understand that they are not alone in facing illness and may have been effective in increasing their mindfulness. Additionall, it is important to note that there is a lack of studies directly examining the effect of logotherapy on mindfulness in this specific population. To date, only one study has directly examined the effect of logotherapy on mindfulnesss, and it was conducted in older adults rather than in mastectomized breast cancer patients.

This study found no significant correlation between mean mindfulness scores and demographic variables in the control group. However, in the intervention group, occupation was significantly associated with mindfulness. Specifically, unemployed individuals exhibited higher mindfulness compared to employed participants. This suggests that occupational stress may negatively impact mindfulness. This finding is consistent with Bartlett et al.31, who reported an inverse relationship between perceived stress and mindfulness31. Furthermore, this result may reflect contextual or psychosocial factors rather than a causal relationship. Further studies are needed to confirm this finding.

This study was limited by individual differences in learning, motivation, adherence to intervention techniques, and exercise compliance. Participants’ motivation levels are influenced by psychological, physical, and social factors and can affect the extent to which they actively participate in sessions. Also differences in regular attendance at sessions, completion of exercises, and interaction with others can lead to meaningful differences in final outcomes.

Furthermore, inherent to human subject research, controlling for all participant psychological, social, and economic factors was not feasible, and these factors were beyond the control of the research team. Another limitation of this study was the relatively small sample size, which may make it harder to detect smaller effects and limits how widely the results can be applied. In addition, participants were recruited through convenience sampling because of the limited availability of eligible mastectomized breast cancer patients. This approach may result in selection bias. For future research, it would be valuable to include larger samples and use random sampling methods to make the findings more generalizable. Expanding recruitment across multiple centers could also help ensure that results are relevant to a more diverse group of patients. Another limitation of this study was the lack of detailed analysis of disease stage and type of mastectomy. Although all participants were within stages I–III, the specific type of mastectomy (e.g., radical or modified radical) was not controlled for in the analyses. These factors could have influenced psychological outcomes and should be considered in future research. It should be noted that some sessions were held during the patients’ hospitalization and others after discharge and when the participants returned to the hospital. Therefore, the different clinical status of the participants at the time of receiving the intervention is considered one of the study’s implementation limitations that can be considered in the design of future studies.

Additionally, future research should incorporate longer follow-up periods to assess the sustained effects of logotherapy on mindfulness, as this study only examined immediate post-intervention outcomes.

One important limitation of this study is its non-blinded design. Both participants and the intervention provider were aware of the group assignments, which might have introduced performance bias (influence of participants’ expectations) and detection bias (influence of researcher expectations in assessing outcomes). Although the use of a standardized self-report measure (FFMQ questionnaire) may have reduced detection bias to some extent, the possibility of expectancy effects cannot be completely ruled out.

These findings have practical implications for clinical practice. Logotherapy can be integrated into psycho-oncology care programs to support women’ psychological adaptation after mastectomy, improve mental well-being, and foster resilience. Healthcare providers, including psychiatric nurses can implement logotherapy techniques in individual or group therapy sessions to enhance mindfulness.

Conclusion

The results of the present study showed that logotherapy can be an effective intervention in increasing mindfulness in breast cancer patients; therefore, it is recommended to use logotherapy as an effective method to increase mindfulness in these patients in physical, psychiatric, and other centers. Empowering psychiatric nurses through logotherapy training workshops and in-service programs could expand their caregiving role in enhancing mindfulness among patients with breast cancer.
