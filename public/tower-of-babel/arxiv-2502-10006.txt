Characterization of quasispheres via smooth approximation
Dimitrios Ntalampekos

Abstract
. We prove that every two-dimensional quasisphere is the limit of a sequence of smooth spheres that are uniform quasispheres. In the case of metric spheres of finite area we provide necessary and sufficient geometric conditions for a quasisphere, involving the doubling property, linear local connectivity, the Loewner property, conformal modulus, and reciprocity. In particular, although an arbitrary quasisphere does not satisfy necessarily all of those geometric conditions, we prove that every quasisphere can be approximated by uniform quasispheres that are uniformly doubling, linearly locally connected, 2 2 -Loewner, reciprocal and satisfy a uniform bound for the modulus of annuli.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background

In this paper we provide characterizations of quasispheres involving geometric conditions and approximation by smooth spaces. For
n\geq 2
, an
n
-dimensional quasisphere is a metric space that can be mapped to the Euclidean
n
-sphere with a quasisymmetric homeomorphism (see Section
2.1
for the definition). The class of quasisymmetric maps generalizes conformal homeomorphisms between Euclidean domains and can be defined on arbitrary metric spaces, even without the presence of a smooth structure.

Quasispheres appear naturally in the areas of Analysis on Metric Spaces and Geometric Function Theory, but also in adjacent areas. For example, in Complex Dynamics they appear in the study of expanding Thurston maps. Specifically, it is shown in
[
5
]
that for an expanding Thurston map
f
of the
2
-sphere, the associated visual metric on the
2
-sphere is quasisymmetric to the Euclidean metric if and only if the map
f
is topologically conjugate to a rational map. Furthermore, a major open conjecture in Geometric Group Theory, namely Cannon’s conjecture
[
33
,
9
,
3
]
, asserts that the boundary at infinity of a Gromov hyperbolic group is always a quasisphere if it is topologically a
2
-sphere. Hence, there is a considerable amount of interest in understanding completely these objects, at least in the
2
-dimensional setting.

The most significant work on quasispheres so far is due to Bonk and Kleiner
[
2
]
, who characterized Ahlfors
2
-regular quasispheres as
linearly locally connected
, or else
\llc
, spheres (see Section
5.1
). Roughly speaking, this condition prevents cusps and dense wrinkles on a surface. The Bonk–Kleiner theorem has been an object of intense study over the past two decades and has been extended to other topologies
[
37
,
21
,
12
,
15
,
13
]
.

While the
\llc
condition is necessary for a quasisphere, Ahlfors
2
-regularity is too restrictive. In fact, quasispheres can even have infinite area. Currently, we have very limited knowledge about quasispheres that are not Ahlfors
2
-regular or have infinite area, such as the aforementioned instances in Complex Dynamics and Geometric Group Theory.

We start our investigation from smooth quasispheres, which are very well understood from a quantitative point of view. With the current techniques it is not hard to establish the following result (actually we prove a more general result, Theorem
1.4
below). We refer the reader to Section
5.1
for the definitions of the metric notions appearing in the statement.

Theorem 1.1

(Smooth quasispheres)
.

Let
X
be a Riemannian
2
-sphere. The following statements are quantitatively equivalent.

(A)

X
is a quasisphere.

(B)

(1)

X
is a doubling and linearly locally connected space and

(2)

there exist constants

\Mod_{2}\Gamma(\overline{B}(a,r),X\setminus B(a,Lr);X)<M.

(C)

2

Of course a smooth sphere satisfies

Question 1.2
.

To what extent does the conclusion of Theorem
1.1
remain valid for arbitrary, non-smooth, metric
2

We explore answers to this question in two different directions. First, we discuss the case of spheres of finite area and attempt to provide a characterization in the spirit of Theorem
1.1
. Second, and in fact as our main theorem, we prove that every quasisphere of dimension
2
can be approximated by smooth uniform quasispheres. We conclude that, although an arbitrary
2

1.3.
Approximation by smooth quasispheres

A typical example of a quasisphere that is not Ahlfors
2
-regular and has infinite area is the
snowsphere
of Meyer
[
22
]
; see Figure
1
. The snowsphere is constructed as follows. Consider the surface of the unit cube in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Each face is a unit square and we subdivide it into nine squares of side length
1/3
. Then we replace the middle square with a cubical cap, consisting of five faces of side length
1/3
. We repeat this subdivision and replacement process in each square of side length
1/3
, etc. The space that we obtain in each stage of the construction is a polyhedral surface with its intrinsic metric and it is actually a quasisphere with uniform parameters. The snowsphere is the Gromov–Hausdorff limit of that sequence of spaces. Motivated by the example of the snowpshere we pose the following question.

Question 1.7
.

Does every quasisphere arise as the limit of a sequence of polyhedral or smooth spheres that are uniform quasispheres?

As the main result of the paper, we provide an affirmative answer to the question.

Theorem 1.8

(Smooth approximation of quasisymmetric
2
-manifolds)
.

Let
X
be a compact Riemannian
2
-manifold (with boundary), equipped with a Riemannian metric
g
and with the corresponding intrinsic metric
d_{g}
. Let
d_{X}
be another metric on
X
that induces its topology. The following are quantitatively equivalent.

(1)

The metric space
(X,d_{g})
is quasisymmetric to
(X,d_{X})
.

(2)

For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
there exists a Riemannian metric
g_{k}
on
X
and a metric
d_{k}
that is locally isometric to the intrinsic metric
d_{g_{k}}
such that

(X,d_{k})
converges to
(X,d_{X})
in the Gromov–Hausdorff metric as
k\to\infty
and

(X,d_{k})
is uniformly quasisymmetric to
(X,d_{g})
for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

In this case there exists an approximately isometric sequence
f_{k}\colon(X,d_{k})\to(X,d_{X})
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, of uniform quasisymmetries.

The implication from
(2)
to
(1)
is quite standard. We reformulate the reverse implication, which is the most technical, in a way that the quantitative dependence is more clear. Let
X
be a smooth manifold. We define
R_{\loc}(X)
to be the collection of metrics
d
on
X
that are locally isometric to the intrinsic metric arising from a Riemannian metric on
X
. For a metric
d
on
X
and a homeomorphism
\eta\colon[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
we denote by
QS(X,d,\eta)
the collection of metric spaces
Y
with the property that there exists an
\eta
-quasisymmetric map from
(X,d)
onto
Y
.
The implication from
(1)
to
(2)
in our main theorem can be restated as follows. Note that the closure refers to the Gromov–Hausdorff distance.

Theorem 1.9
.

For each distortion function
\eta
there exists a distortion function
\eta^{\prime}
such that for each compact Riemannian
2
-manifold
X
with intrinsic metric
d_{g}
we have

QS(X,d_{g},\eta)\subset\overline{R_{\loc}(X)\cap QS(X,d_{g},\eta^{\prime})}.

We remark that Bonk and Kleiner, in their main theorem
[
2
]
*Theorem 11.1, prove that each
2
-dimensional quasisphere admits good graph approximations that satisfy condition
(B)
(B)(2)
with discrete modulus in place of conformal modulus. The definition of those graph approximations as well as the discrete modulus condition are technical to state and handle. In contrast, conformal modulus can be defined directly on a surface of finite area using the Hausdorff
2
-measure or equivalently (if the surface is smooth) in local coordinates and is perhaps a more tangible object than discrete modulus on graph approximations of a space. We remark that conformal modulus and discrete modulus are not comparable in general, even in the setting of quasispheres of finite area. This is illustrated by the fact that not every quasisphere of finite area satisfies
(B)
(B)(2)
, as already discussed, but as a consequence of the result of Bonk and Kleiner the discrete analogue of that condition is always satisfied. Hence, our main theorem is not a consequence of
[
2
]
*Theorem 11.1.

On the other hand, as a corollary of Theorem
1.8
and Theorem
1.4
, one can always approximate a quasisphere by smooth quasispheres that satisfy the conformal modulus analogue
(B)
(B)(2)
of the condition of Bonk–Kleiner.

Corollary 1.10
.

Every
2
-dimensional quasisphere is the Gromov–Hausdorff limit of a sequence of
2
-dimensional quasispheres that are doubling, linearly locally connected,
2
-Loewner, reciprocal, and satisfy condition
(B)
(B)(2)
, quantitatively.

Theorem
1.8
is similar in spirit to the main results in
[
25
,
26
]
, which assert that every metric surface of locally finite area can be approximated in the Gromov–Hausdorff sense by polyhedral surfaces of controlled geometry. This result has been generalized to higher dimensions by Marti and Soultanis under some additional geometric assumptions
[
18
]
. Our argument for Theorem
1.8
is very robust and we expect that if one imposes further properties on a quasisphere, such as Ahlfors
2
-regularity or finiteness of area, then the smooth approximating surfaces will also have these properties.

1.4.
Bi-Lipschitz surfaces

We include a discussion on metric surfaces that are bi-Lipschitz to smooth surfaces. The analogues of Theorem
1.8
and Theorem
1.9
remain valid if one replaces quasisymmetric with bi-Lipschitz maps. Let
(X,d)
be a metric space. For
\lambda\geq 1
we denote by
BL(X,d,\lambda)
the collection of metric spaces
Y
with the property that there exists a
\lambda
-bi-Lipschitz map from
(X,d)
onto
Y
.

Theorem 1.11
.

For each
\lambda\geq 1
there exists
\lambda^{\prime}\geq 1
such that for each compact Riemannian
2
-manifold
X
with intrinsic metric
d_{g}
we have

BL(X,d_{g},\lambda)\subset\overline{R_{\loc}(X)\cap BL(X,d_{g},\lambda^{\prime})}.

The proofs of Theorems
1.8
and
1.11
follow exactly the same scheme, but the verification of the bi-Lipschitz property is far more elementary than the quasisymmetric property.

Although a quantitative characterization of smooth quasispheres is available thanks to Theorem
1.1
, this is not the case with smooth bi-Lipschitz spheres, i.e., Riemannian
2
-spheres that are bi-Lipschitz equivalent to the Euclidean
2
-sphere. So far there are some sufficient conditions so that a smooth sphere or plane is bi-Lipschitz equivalent to the Euclidean sphere or plane, respectively. For example, Fu
[
11
]
and Bonk–Lang
[
4
]
prove that certain bounds on the integral Gaussian curvature provide a sufficient condition for quantitative bi-Lipschitz parametrization. The result of Fu strengthens an earlier result of Toro

[
34
,
35
]
, implying that graphs of functions in the Sobolev space
W^{2,2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
admit local bi-Lipschitz parametrizations, quantitatively, depending on the
W^{2,2}
norm. See also the related works
[
32
,
23
]
.

2.
Approximations of metric spaces and quasisymmetries

In this section we introduce a variant of an approximation of a metric space, as defined by Bonk and Kleiner
[
2
]
*Section 4. Roughly speaking, an approximation of a metric space is a graph on the space with controlled combinatorial and metric properties. The main result of the section is Theorem
2.8
and it provides sufficient conditions so that a homeomorphism between metric spaces that respects a pair of approximations of those spaces is quasisymmetric, quantitatively. This section can be read independently of the other parts of the paper.

2.1.
Preliminaries

For quantities
A
and
B
we write
A\lesssim B
if there exists a constant
c>0
such that
A\leq cB
. If the constant
c
depends on another quantity
H
that we wish to emphasize, then we write instead
A\leq c(H)B
or
A\lesssim_{H}B
. Moreover, we use the notation
A\simeq B
if
A\lesssim B
and
B\lesssim A
. As previously, we write
A\simeq_{H}B
to emphasize the dependence of the implicit constants on the quantity
H
. All constants in the statements are assumed to be positive even if this is not stated explicitly and the same letter may be used in different statements or within the same proof to denote a different constant.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space. We denote by
B_{d}(x,r)
(resp.
\overline{B_{d}}(x,r)
) the open (resp. closed) ball centered at
x
with radius
r>0
. For a set
A\subset X
and
r>0
we denote by
N_{d}(A,r)
the open
r
-neighborhood of
A
and by
\diam_{d}(A)
the diameter of
A
. Often we drop the subscript
d
from the above notation when this does not lead to a confusion.

Let
(X,d_{X})
and
(Y,d_{Y})
be metric spaces and
f\colon X\to Y
be a homeomorphism. We say that
f
is
quasisymmetric
if there exists a homeomorphism
\eta\colon[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
such that for every triple of distinct points
x,y,z\in X
we have

\frac{d_{Y}(f(x),f(y))}{d_{Y}(f(x),f(z))}\leq\eta\left(\frac{d_{X}(x,y)}{d_{X}(x,z)}\right).

In that case we say that
f
is
\eta
-quasisymmetric. A homeomorphism
\eta\colon[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
as above is called a
distortion function
. A homeomorphism
f\colon X\to Y
is
bi-Lipschitz
if there exists
\lambda\geq 1
such that

\lambda^{-1}d_{X}(x,y)\leq d_{Y}(f(x),f(y))\leq\lambda d_{X}(x,y)

for every
x,y\in X
. A metric space
X
is called an
n
-dimensional quasisphere
, where

, if there exists a quasisymmetric map
f\colon\mathbb{S}^{n}\to X
, where
\mathbb{S}^{n}
denotes the Euclidean
n
-dimensional unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
.

(
[

]
*Proposition 10.8)
.

Let

be metric spaces,

\lambda\geq 1
, then
\lambda^{-1}d_{Y}\leq\widetilde{d}\leq d_{Y}
.

We say that
\widetilde{d}
is the
metric arising from gluing
d_{Y}
with
d_{S}
.

Proof.

Note that
\widetilde{d}
is clearly symmetric. If
\widetilde{d}(x,y)=0
, then either
d_{Y}(x,y)=0
, so
x=y
, or there exist points
u_{n},v_{n}\in S
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, such that
d_{Y}(x,u_{n})+d_{S}(u_{n},v_{n})+d_{Y}(v_{n},y)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. The assumption that
S
is closed in
(Y,d_{Y})
implies that
x,y\in S
. Also, since
d_{S}\leq d_{Y}
, we have
u_{n}\to x
in
d_{S}
and
v_{n}\to y
in
d_{S}
. Since
d_{S}(u_{n},v_{n})\to 0
, we conclude that
d_{S}(x,y)=0
, so
x=y
. Finally, for the triangle inequality, let
x,y,z\in Y
and
s,t,u,v\in S
. Since
d_{S}\leq d_{Y}
on
S\times S
, we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{d}(x,y)

\displaystyle\leq d_{Y}(x,u)+d_{S}(u,v)+d_{Y}(v,y)

\displaystyle\leq d_{Y}(x,u)+d_{S}(u,s)+d_{S}(s,t)+d_{S}(t,v)+d_{Y}(v,y)

\displaystyle\leq(d_{Y}(x,u)+d_{S}(u,s)+d_{Y}(s,z))+(d_{Y}(z,t)+d_{S}(t,v)+d_{Y}(v,y)).

Also, by the definition of
\widetilde{d}
, one obtains immediately the estimates

\displaystyle\widetilde{d}(x,y)

\displaystyle\leq d_{Y}(x,z)+(d_{Y}(z,t)+d_{S}(t,v)+d_{Y}(v,y)),

\displaystyle\widetilde{d}(x,y)

\displaystyle\leq(d_{Y}(x,u)+d_{S}(u,s)+d_{Y}(s,z))+d_{Y}(z,y),

\displaystyle\widetilde{d}(x,y)

\displaystyle\leq d_{Y}(x,y)\leq d_{Y}(x,z)+d_{Y}(z,y)

Taking infimum over all
s,t,u,v
gives the triangle inequality, completing the proof of
(1)
.

Now, we prove
(2)
. If
x,y\in S
, then for every
u,v\in S
we have

\displaystyle d_{S}(x,y)\leq d_{S}(x,u)+d_{S}(u,v)+d_{S}(v,y)\leq d_{Y}(x,u)+d_{S}(u,v)+d_{Y}(v,y),

because
d_{S}\leq d_{Y}
. This shows that
\widetilde{d}(x,y)\geq d_{S}(x,y)
. Moreover, equality holds for
u=x
and
v=y
. Thus,
\widetilde{d}(x,y)=d_{S}(x,y)
. Now, let
x\in Y\setminus S
and
y\in S
, as in the second part of
(2)
. It is clear, by the definition of the infimum, that

\displaystyle d_{Y}(x,y)

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{u\in S}\{d_{Y}(x,u)+d_{S}(u,y)\}

\displaystyle\geq\inf_{u,v\in S}\{d_{Y}(x,u)+d_{S}(u,v)+d_{Y}(v,y)\}=\widetilde{d}(x,y).

On the other hand, for every
u,v\in S
we have

d_{Y}(x,u)+d_{S}(u,v)+d_{Y}(v,y)\geq d_{Y}(x,u)+d_{S}(u,v)+d_{S}(v,y)\geq d_{Y}(x,u)+d_{S}(u,y).

This shows that

\widetilde{d}(x,y)\geq\inf_{u\in S}\{d_{Y}(x,u)+d_{S}(u,y)\},

which completes the proof of
(2)
.

Next, we establish
(3)
. If
x\in Y\setminus S
, let
r=\dist_{\widetilde{d}}(x,S)=\dist_{\widetilde{d}}(x,S\setminus\{x\})
. Let
z\in S
. By
(2)
, for each
\varepsilon>0
there exists
u\in S
such that

\widetilde{d}(x,z)\geq d_{Y}(x,u)+d_{S}(u,z)-\varepsilon\geq\dist_{d_{Y}}(x,S)-\varepsilon.

This implies that
r\geq\dist_{d_{Y}}(x,S)>0
. Let
y\in Y
with
\widetilde{d}(x,y)<r
. For
u,v\in S
we have

d_{Y}(x,u)+d_{S}(u,v)+d_{Y}(v,y)\geq\widetilde{d}(x,u)\geq r>\widetilde{d}(x,y).

The definition of
\widetilde{d}
implies that
\widetilde{d}(x,y)=d_{Y}(x,y)
. Now, if
y,z\in B_{\widetilde{d}}(x,r/3)
, then
\dist_{\widetilde{d}}(y,S)\geq 2r/3>\widetilde{d}(y,z)
. Hence, by what we have proved,
\widetilde{d}(y,z)=d_{Y}(y,z)
, which implies that
y,z\in B_{d_{Y}}(x,r/3)
. Also,
B_{d_{Y}}(x,r/3)\subset B_{\widetilde{d}}(x,r/3)
trivially.

If
x\in S
, let
r=\dist_{\widetilde{d}}(x,S\setminus\{x\})
. Note that
r
might be zero, in which case we have nothing to prove. Suppose that
r>0
and let
y\in Y
with
0<\widetilde{d}(x,y)<r
. In particular,
y\notin S
. For
u\in S\setminus\{x\}
, by
(2)
, we have

\displaystyle d_{S}(x,u)+d_{Y}(u,y)\geq d_{S}(x,u)=\widetilde{d}(x,u)\geq r>\widetilde{d}(x,y).

The definition of
\widetilde{d}
in the second part of
(2)
implies that
\widetilde{d}(x,y)=d_{Y}(x,y)
. If
y,z\in B_{\widetilde{d}}(x,r/3)
, then for
u,v\in S\setminus\{x\}
, we have

\displaystyle d_{Y}(y,u)+d_{S}(u,v)+d_{Y}(v,z)\geq\widetilde{d}(y,u)+\widetilde{d}(v,z)\geq 2r/3>\widetilde{d}(y,z).

If
u=v=x
, then the left-hand side is at least
d_{Y}(x,y)
by the triangle inequality. Now, the definition of
\widetilde{d}
implies that
\widetilde{d}(y,z)=d_{Y}(y,z)
and
y,z\in B_{d_{Y}}(x,r/3)
. Also,
B_{d_{Y}}(x,r/3)\subset B_{\widetilde{d}}(x,r/3)
trivially. This completes the proof of
(3)
.

For
(4)
, note that the identity map from
(Y,d_{Y})
to
(Y,\widetilde{d})
is trivially
1
-Lipschitz. We show the continuity of the inverse map. By
(3)
we see that the identity map is a local homeomorphism in
Y\setminus S
. Let
x\in S
and
x_{n}\in Y
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, be a sequence with
\widetilde{d}(x_{n},x)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. By
(2)
, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
there exists
v_{n}\in S
such that
d_{S}(x,v_{n})+d_{Y}(v_{n},x_{n})\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. Since
d_{S}
is topologically equivalent to
d_{Y}
, we conclude that
d_{Y}(x,v_{n})\to 0
. Since
d_{Y}(v_{n},x_{n})\to 0
, we conclude that
d_{Y}(x_{n},x)\to 0
, as desired.

Suppose that
(S,d_{S})
is a discrete metric space, as in
(5)
. Then
(S,d_{Y})
is also discrete since
d_{S}\leq d_{Y}
. As a consequence, by
(4)
, the identity map is a homeomorphism from
(Y,d_{Y})
onto
(Y,\widetilde{d})
. This implies that for each
x\in Y
we have
r=\dist_{\widetilde{d}}(x,S\setminus\{x\})>0
, since
\dist_{d_{Y}}(x,S\setminus\{x\})>0
. By
(3)
we conclude that the identity map restricts to an isometric homeomorphism between
B_{d_{Y}}(x,r/3)
and
B_{\widetilde{d}}(x,r/3)
.

Finally, suppose that
d_{S}\geq\lambda^{-1}d_{Y}
as in
(6)
. For
x,y\in Y
and
u,v\in S
we have

d_{Y}(x,u)+d_{S}(u,v)+d_{Y}(v,y)\geq\lambda^{-1}(d_{Y}(x,u)+d_{Y}(u,v)+d_{Y}(v,y))\geq\lambda^{-1}d_{Y}(x,y).

The definition of
\widetilde{d}
gives immediately that
\widetilde{d}\geq\lambda^{-1}d_{Y}
.
∎

2.3.
Approximations of metric spaces

Following
[
2
]
*Section 4 we define the notion of an approximation of a metric space as follows. Given a graph
G=(V,\sim)
we denote by
k_{G}(u,v)
, or simply by
k(u,v)
when the graph
G
is implicitly understood, the combinatorial distance of vertices
u,v\in V
; that is, the minimum number of edges in a chain connecting the two vertices. Note that
k(u,v)
is understood to be
\infty
if there is no chain of edges connecting
u
and
v
. Let
(X,d_{X})
be a metric space. We consider quadruples
\mathcal{A}=(G,\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{r},\mathcal{U})
, where
G=(V,\sim)
is a graph with vertex set
V
,
\mathfrak{p}
is a map
\mathfrak{p}\colon V\to X
,
\mathfrak{r}
is a map
\mathfrak{r}\colon V\to(0,\infty)
, and
\mathcal{U}=\{\mathcal{U}(v):v\in V\}
is an open cover of
X
. We let
\mathfrak{p}(v)=p_{v}
,
\mathfrak{r}(v)=r_{v}
, and
\mathcal{U}(v)=U_{v}
for
v\in V
. For
K>0
we define the
K
-star
of a vertex
v\in V
with respect to
\mathcal{A}
as

\mathcal{A}\text{-}\st_{K}(v)=\bigcup\{U_{u}:u\in V,\,k(u,v)<K\}.

We will drop the letter
\mathcal{A}
and denote the
K
-star by
\st_{K}(v)
, whenever this does not lead to a confusion. For
K,L\geq 1
, we call the quadruple
\mathcal{A}
a
(K,L)
-approximation of
X
if the following four conditions are satisfied.

(A1)

Every vertex of
G
has valence at most
K
.

(A2)

B(p_{v},r_{v})\subset U_{v}\subset B(p_{v},Lr_{v})
for every
v\in V
.

(A3)

Let
u,v\in V
. If
u\sim v
, then
U_{u}\cap U_{v}\neq\emptyset
and
L^{-1}r_{u}\leq r_{v}\leq Lr_{u}
. Conversely, if
U_{u}\cap U_{v}\neq\emptyset
, then
k(u,v)<K
.

(A4)

N(U_{v},{r_{v}/L})\subset\st_{K}(v)
for every
v\in V
.

Recall that
N(A,r)
denotes the open
r
-neighborhood of the set
A
. The
(K,L)
-approximation
\mathcal{A}
of
X
is called
fine
if
U_{v}\neq X
for all
v\in V
.

Observe that in the above definition the constant
K
encodes combinatorial information, which therefore remains invariant under homeomorphisms, and the constant
L
encodes metric information. Note that if
\mathcal{A}
is a
(K,L)
-approximation of
X
and
K^{\prime}\geq K
,
L^{\prime}\geq L
, then
\mathcal{A}
is also an
(K^{\prime},L^{\prime})
-approximation of
X
. We record some immediate further properties of
(K,L)
-approximations.

(A5)

If
X
is connected, then
G
is connected.

Proof.

This follows from
(A3)
.
∎

(A6)

Let
u,v\in V
. If
k(u,v)\geq 2K
then
U_{u}\cap N(U_{v},{r_{v}/L})=\emptyset
.

Proof.

Suppose that
U_{u}\cap N(U_{v},{r_{v}/L})\neq\emptyset
, so
U_{u}\cap\st_{K}(v)\neq\emptyset
by
(A4)
. Hence, there exists
w\in V
such that
U_{u}\cap U_{w}\neq\emptyset
and
k(w,v)<K
. By
(A3)
, we have
k(u,v)\leq k(u,w)+k(w,v)<2K
, a contradiction.
∎

(A7)

Let
u,v\in V
. If
k(u,v)\geq 2K
then for each
x\in U_{u}
and
y\in U_{v}
we have

C(L)^{-1}d_{X}(p_{u},p_{v})\leq d_{X}(x,y)\leq C(L)d_{X}(p_{u},p_{v}).

Proof.

By
(A6)
,
d_{X}(x,y)\geq r_{u}/L
and
d_{X}(x,y)\geq r_{v}/L
. Therefore, by
(A2)
,

d_{X}(p_{u},p_{v})\leq Lr_{u}+d_{X}(x,y)+Lr_{v}\leq(2L^{2}+1)d_{X}(x,y).

On the other hand, we have
U_{u}\cap U_{v}=\emptyset
by
(A3)
. By
(A2)
we conclude that
d_{X}(p_{u},p_{v})\geq\max\{r_{u},r_{v}\}
. Therefore,

d_{X}(x,y)\leq Lr_{u}+d_{X}(p_{u},p_{v})+Lr_{v}\leq(2L+1)d_{X}(p_{u},p_{v}).\qed

2.4.
Approximations and connectedness assumptions

We say that a metric space
X
has
bounded turning
if there exists a constant
L\geq 1
such that for each pair
x,y\in X
there exists a connected set
E\subset X
that contains
x
and
y
with
\diam(E)\leq Ld_{X}(x,y)
. In this case we say that
X
has
L
-bounded turning.

Lemma 2.3

(Connected sets and chains of vertices)
.

Let
X
be a metric space,
K,L\geq 1
, and
\mathcal{A}=((V,\sim),\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{r},\mathcal{U})
be a
(K,L)
-approximation of
X
. Let
u,v\in V
and
E\subset X
be a connected set with
p_{u},p_{v}\in E
. Then there exist vertices
u=w_{0},w_{1},\dots,w_{n}=v
,
n\geq 0
, with
k(w_{i-1},w_{i})<K
for each
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, such that

\diam(\{p_{w_{0}},\dots,p_{w_{n}}\})\leq C(L)\diam(E).

In particular, if
X
has
L
-bounded turning, then for every pair of points
p_{u},p_{v}\in\mathfrak{p}(V)
there exist points
p_{u}=p_{w_{0}},p_{w_{1}},\dots,p_{w_{n}}=p_{v}\in\mathfrak{p}(V)
with
k(w_{i-1},w_{i})<K
for each
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, such that

\diam(\{p_{w_{0}},\dots,p_{w_{n}}\})\leq C^{\prime}(L)d_{X}(p_{u},p_{v}).

Proof.

By
(A2)
we have
p_{u}\in U_{u}
and
p_{v}\in U_{v}
. If
E\subset U_{u}
, then
U_{v}\cap U_{u}\neq\emptyset
so
k(u,v)<K
by
(A3)
. We have
\diam(\{p_{u},p_{v}\})\leq\diam(E)
, so the conclusion follows in this case. Suppose that
E\not\subset U_{u}
. By
(A2)
,
\diam(E)\geq r_{u}
. The collection
\{U_{w}:w\in V,\,U_{w}\cap E\neq\emptyset\}
is an open cover of
E
. The connectedness of
E
implies that there exist vertices
u=w_{0},w_{1},\dots,w_{n}=v
, where
n\geq 0
, such that
U_{w_{i}}\cap E\neq\emptyset
for
i\in\{0,\dots,n\}
and
U_{w_{i-1}}\cap U_{w_{i}}\neq\emptyset
for
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. By
(A3)
, we have
k(w_{i-1},w_{i})<K
for
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
.

If
E\subset\st_{K}(w_{i})
for some
i\in\{0,\dots,n\}
then
U_{w_{j}}\cap\st_{K}(w_{i})\neq\emptyset
for all
j\in\{0,\dots,n\}
. Property
(A3)
implies that
k(w_{i},w_{j})<2K
and
C(L)^{-1}r_{w_{i}}\leq r_{w_{j}}\leq C(L)r_{w_{i}}
for all
j\in\{0,\dots,n\}
. In particular,
r_{w_{j}}\leq C_{1}(L)r_{u}\leq C_{1}(L)\diam(E)
for all
j\in\{0,\dots,n\}
. By
(A2)
, we have
\diam(U_{w_{j}})\leq 2Lr_{w_{j}}\leq C_{2}(L)\diam(E)
for
j\in\{0,\dots,n\}
.

Next, suppose that
E\not\subset\st_{K}(w_{i})
for all
i\in\{0,\dots,n\}
. By
(A4)
, since
E\cap U_{w_{i}}\neq\emptyset
, we have
\diam(E)\geq r_{w_{i}}/L
. We conclude that
\diam(U_{w_{i}})\leq 2Lr_{w_{i}}\leq 2L^{2}\diam(E)
for all
i\in\{0,\dots,n\}
.

Combining both cases, we arrive at the conclusion
\diam(U_{w_{i}})\leq C_{3}(L)\diam(E)
for all
i\in\{0,\dots,n\}
. If
F=\{p_{w_{0}},\dots,p_{w_{n}}\}
, then there exist
i,j\in\{0,\dots,n\}
such that

\diam(F)\leq\diam(U_{w_{i}})+\diam(U_{w_{j}})+\diam(E)\leq C_{4}(L)\diam(E).

This completes the proof.
∎

We say that a metric space
X
is
quasiconvex
if there exists
L\geq 1
such that for every pair of points
x,y\in X
there exists a curve
\gamma
connecting
x
and
y
with
\ell(\gamma)\leq Ld_{X}(x,y)
. In this case we say that
X
is
L
-quasiconvex.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
X
be a metric space,
K,L\geq 1
, and
\mathcal{A}=((V,\sim),\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{r},\mathcal{U})
be a
(K,L)
-approximation of
X
. Suppose that
X
is
L
-quasiconvex. Then for every pair of vertices
u,v\in V
with
k(u,v)\geq K
there exist vertices
u=w_{0},w_{1},\dots,w_{n}=v
,
n\geq 0
, with
w_{i-1}\sim w_{i}
for each
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, such that

(2.1)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{n}r_{w_{i}}\leq C(K,L)d_{X}(p_{u},p_{v}).

Proof.

Let
u,v\in V
be distinct vertices. Let
\gamma
be a curve connecting
p_{u}
and
p_{v}
with
\ell(\gamma)\leq Ld_{X}(p_{u},p_{v})
. Note that
U_{u}\cap U_{v}=\emptyset
by
(A3)
, so by
(A2)
we have

(2.2)

\displaystyle r_{u}+r_{v}\leq\ell(\gamma)\leq Ld_{X}(p_{u},p_{v}).

Suppose, first, that
|\gamma|
is contained in
N(U_{w},r_{w}/L)
for some
w\in V
. Then
U_{u}\cap N(U_{w},r_{w}/L)\neq\emptyset
so by
(A6)
we conclude that
k(u,w)<2K
and similarly
k(v,w)<2K
, so
k(u,v)<4K
. We consider a chain
u=w_{0},\dots,w_{n}=v
of length
n=k(u,v)
with
w_{i-1}\sim w_{i}
for
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. By
(A3)
we have
r_{w_{i}}\leq C(K,L)r_{u}
for each
i\in\{0,\dots,n\}
. Thus, in combination with (
2.2
) we have

\sum_{i=0}^{n}r_{w_{i}}\leq(n+1)C(K,L)r_{u}\leq(4K+1)C(K,L)\ell(\gamma).

This completes the proof in that case.

Next, suppose that
|\gamma|
is not contained in
N(U_{w},r_{w}/L)
for any
w\in V
. The collection
\{U_{w}:w\in V,\,U_{w}\cap|\gamma|\neq\emptyset\}
is an open cover of
|\gamma|
. The connectedness of
|\gamma|
implies that there exist vertices
u=w_{0},w_{1},\dots,w_{n}=v
, where
n\geq 0
, such that
U_{w_{i}}\cap|\gamma|\neq\emptyset
for
i\in\{0,\dots,n\}
and
U_{w_{i-1}}\cap U_{w_{i}}\neq\emptyset
for
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. By
(A3)
, we have
k(w_{i-1},w_{i})<K
for
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. Since
|\gamma|
is not contained in
N(U_{w_{i}},r_{w_{i}}/L)
, by
(A4)
we have

(2.3)

\displaystyle\int_{\gamma}\chi_{\st_{K}(w_{i})}\,ds\geq\int_{\gamma}\chi_{N(U_{w_{i}},r_{w_{i}}/L)}\,ds\geq L^{-1}r_{w_{i}}

for each
i\in\{0,\dots,n\}
.

We claim that for each
x\in X
we have

(2.4)

\displaystyle\sum_{w\in V}\chi_{\st_{K}(w)}(x)\leq C(K).

We fix
w\in V
. Suppose that
x\in\st_{K}(w)\cap\st_{K}(w^{\prime})
for some
w^{\prime}\in V
,
x\in X
. Then there exist
s,s^{\prime}\in V
such that
k(w,s)<K
,
x\in U_{s}
,
k(w^{\prime},s^{\prime})<K
, and
x\in U_{s^{\prime}}
. Since
U_{s}\cap U_{s^{\prime}}\neq\emptyset
, by
(A3)
we have
k(s,s^{\prime})<K
, so
k(w,w^{\prime})<3K
. By
(A1)
we conclude that there are at most
C(K)
vertices
w^{\prime}
such that
\st_{K}(w^{\prime})
intersects
\st_{K}(w)
. This proves the claim. We now integrate both sides of (
2.4
) and by (
2.3
) we obtain

\displaystyle C(K)\ell(\gamma)\geq\sum_{i=0}^{n}\int_{\gamma}\chi_{\st_{K}(w_{i})}\,ds\geq L^{-1}\sum_{i=0}^{n}r_{w_{i}}.

It only remains to satisfy the requirement that
w_{i-1}\sim w_{i}
. This can be easily achieved by adding, for each
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, at most
K
vertices in a combinatorial path from
w_{i-1}
to
w_{i}
. For each added vertex
w
the value of
r_{w}
is comparable to
r_{w_{i}}
, thanks to
(A3)
. This completes the proof.
∎

2.5.
Images of approximations of metric spaces

In the statements below, such as in Lemma
2.6
, if
Y
is a metric space, then the metric of
Y
is implicitly understood to be
d_{Y}
, whenever this is not explicitly stated. In some cases, such as in Lemma
2.7
, where we consider different metrics on the same underlying set
Y
, we will explicitly state the metric that we are using each time.

Definition 2.5

(Image of approximation under a homeomorphism)
.

Let
X,Y
be connected metric spaces,
f\colon X\to Y
be a homeomorphism, and
K,L\geq 1
. Suppose that
\mathcal{A}=((V,\sim),\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{r},\mathcal{U})
is a fine
(K,L)
-approximation of
X
. Define
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}=((V,\sim),\mathfrak{p}^{\prime},\mathfrak{r}^{\prime},\mathcal{U}^{\prime})
, where for
v\in V
we set
\mathfrak{p}^{\prime}(v)={p}^{\prime}_{v}=f(p_{v})
,
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}(v)=U_{v}^{\prime}=f(U_{v})
, and

r_{v}^{\prime}=\inf\{d_{Y}(f(x),p_{v}^{\prime}):x\in X,d_{X}(x,p_{v})\geq r_{v}\}.

We say that
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
is the

Lemma 2.6

(Image of approximation under a quasisymmetry)

(2.5)

if

\displaystyle\diam(U_{v}^{\prime})\simeq_{K,L,\eta}r_{v}^{\prime}.

Next, we show

Lemma 2.7

(Changing the metric on vertices)
.

Let
(Y,d_{Y})
be a metric space,
K,L,M\geq 1
, and
\mathcal{A}=((V,\sim),\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{r},\mathcal{U})
be a fine
(K,L)
-approximation of
Y
. Let
S=\mathfrak{p}(V)
and

be a metric on
S
, and suppose that the following conditions hold.

(1)

(Y,d_{Y})
has
L
-bounded turning.

(2)

d_{S}\leq d_{Y}
on
S\times S
.

(3)

For every pair of distinct vertices
u,v\in V
we have
d_{S}(p_{u},p_{v})\geq r_{v}/L
.

(4)

For every pair of vertices
u,v\in V
there exist vertices
u=w_{0},w_{1},\dots,w_{n}=v
,
n\geq 0
, with
k(w_{i-1},w_{i})<K
for each

, such that

\diam_{d_{S}}(\{p_{w_{0}},\dots,p_{w_{n}}\})\leq Ld_{S}(p_{u},p_{v}).

Consider the metric
\widetilde{d}
on
Y
arising by gluing
d_{Y}
with
d_{S}
, as in Lemma
2.2
. Then the following statements are true.

(i)

Let
u,v\in V
and
x\in U_{u}
,

. Then

\min\{r_{u}+r_{v},d_{Y}(x,y)\}\leq C(K,L)\widetilde{d}(x,y).

In particular, if

, then

\widetilde{d}(x,y)\leq d_{Y}(x,y)\leq C(K,L,M)\widetilde{d}(x,y).

(ii)

(Y,\widetilde{d})
has

-bounded turning.

(iii)

Let
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}
be the image of
\mathcal{A}
under the identity map from

onto

. Then
\widetilde{\mathcal{A}}
is a fine
(K,\widetilde{L})
-approximation of
(Y,\widetilde{d})
, where
\widetilde{L}=C(K,L)
.

Proof.

First, we show
(i)
. Let
x,y\in X
and
u,v\in V
such that
x\in U_{u}
and
y\in U_{v}
. Let
s,t\in V
. If
s=t
, then

d_{Y}(x,p_{s})+d_{S}(p_{s},p_{t})+d_{Y}(p_{t},y)\geq d_{Y}(x,y).

Suppose that
s\neq t
. If
k(u,s)<2K
and
k(v,t)<2K
, then by assumption
(3)
and
(A3)
, we have

d_{Y}(x,p_{s})+d_{S}(p_{s},p_{t})+d_{Y}(p_{t},y)\geq d_{S}(p_{s},p_{t})\geq L^{-1}\max\{r_{s},r_{t}\}\gtrsim_{K,L}(r_{u}+r_{v}).

If
k(u,s)<2K
and
k(v,t)\geq 2K
, then by assumption
(3)
,
(A3)
, and
(A6)
, we have

\displaystyle d_{Y}(x,p_{s})+d_{S}(p_{s},p_{t})+d_{Y}(p_{t},y)\geq L^{-1}r_{s}+L^{-1}r_{v}\gtrsim_{K,L}(r_{u}+r_{v}).

The same is true when
k(u,s)\geq 2K
and
k(v,t)<2K
. Finally, suppose that
k(u,s)\geq 2K
and

. Then by

we have

d_{Y}(x,p_{s})+d_{S}(p_{s},p_{t})+d_{Y}(p_{t},y)\geq L^{-1}(r_{u}+r_{v}).

Combining all cases, we see that

\inf_{s,t\in V}\{d_{Y}(x,p_{s})+d_{S}(p_{s},p_{t})+d_{Y}(p_{t},y)\}\gtrsim_{K,L}\min\{r_{u}+r_{v},d_{Y}(x,y)\}.

The definition of
\widetilde{d}
gives the first part of

. For the second part, we note that if

, then by
(A2)
and
(A3)

.

Next, we show
(ii)
. Since

, the identity map from

onto

. Let

and

. We consider two cases.

Case 1:

. Then by the fact that

Case 2:

. In this case, by

. Combining this with conclusion

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \widetilde{d}(x,y)&\gtrsim_{K,L}(r_{u}+r_{v})\gtrsim_{K,L}(d_{Y}(p_{u},x)+d_{Y}(p_{v},y))\\
&\gtrsim_{K,L}(\widetilde{d}(p_{u},x)+\widetilde{d}(p_{v},y)).\end{aligned}

Also, by Case 1, there exist a connected set

containing

\displaystyle\diam_{\widetilde{d}}(E_{x})\lesssim_{K,L}\widetilde{d}(x,p_{u})\quad\text{and}\quad\diam_{\widetilde{d}}(E_{y})\lesssim_{K,L}\widetilde{d}(y,p_{v}).

By assumption

, there exist vertices
u=w_{0},w_{1},\dots,w_{n}=v
,
n\geq 0
, with
k(w_{i-1},w_{i})<K
for each

\displaystyle\diam_{d_{S}}(\{p_{w_{0}},\dots,p_{w_{n}}\})\leq Ld_{S}(p_{u},p_{v}).

2.6.
Approximations and quasisymmetric homeomorphisms

We state the main result of the section. Essentially it allows us to upgrade a local quasisymmetry between metric spaces
X
,
Y
to a global one and even provides the flexibility of changing the metric in large scales of the target space
Y
.

Theorem 2.8
.

Let
(X,d_{X}),(Y,d_{Y})
be connected metric spaces,
f\colon X\to Y
be a homeomorphism,
\eta\colon[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
be a homeomorphism, and
K,L\geq 1
. Let
\mathcal{A}=((V,\sim),\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{r},\mathcal{U})
be a fine
(K,L)
-approximation of
(X,d_{X})
and suppose that the following conditions are satisfied.

(1)

(X,d_{X})
and
(Y,d_{Y})
have
L
-bounded turning.

(2)

For every pair of distinct vertices
u,v\in V
we have
d_{X}(p_{u},p_{v})\geq r_{u}/L
.

(3)

For each
v\in V
,
f|_{\st_{2K+1}(v)}\colon(\st_{2K+1}(v),d_{X})\to(f(\st_{2K+1}(v)),d_{Y})
is
\eta
-quasisymmetric.

(4)

For
S=f(\mathfrak{p}(V))
there exists a metric
d_{S}\colon S\times S\to[0,\infty)
such that
f\colon(\mathfrak{p}(V),d_{X})\to(S,d_{S})
is
\eta
-quasisymmetric and
d_{S}\leq d_{Y}
on
S\times S
. Moreover, if
u,v\in V
and
u\sim v
, then
d_{S}(f(p_{u}),f(p_{v}))\geq L^{-1}d_{Y}(f(p_{u}),f(p_{v}))
.

Consider the metric
\widetilde{d}
on
Y
arising by gluing
d_{Y}
with
d_{S}
, as in Lemma
2.2
. Then there exists a homeomorphism
\eta^{\prime}\colon[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
depending only on
K,L
, and
\eta
, such that
f\colon(X,d_{X})\to(Y,\widetilde{d})
is
\eta^{\prime}
-quasisymmetric.

We establish a preliminary statement before giving the proof of Theorem
2.8
. We believe that the constant
2K+1
appearing in assumption
(3)
above and also in the lemma below can be replaced with
K
, but we do not include a proof for the sake of brevity.

Lemma 2.9

(Local to global quasisymmetry)
.

Let
X,Y
be connected metric spaces,
f\colon X\to Y
be a homeomorphism,
\eta\colon[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
be a homeomorphism, and
K,L\geq 1
. Suppose that
\mathcal{A}=((V,\sim),\mathfrak{p},\mathfrak{r},\mathcal{U})
is a fine
(K,L)
-approximation of
X
and the following conditions hold.

(1)

\mathcal{A}^{\prime}=f(\mathcal{A})
is a
(K,L)
-approximation of
Y
.

(2)

For each
v\in V
,
f|_{\st_{2K+1}(v)}
is
\eta
-quasisymmetric.

(3)

f|_{\mathfrak{p}(V)}
is
\eta
-quasisymmetric.

Then there exists a homeomorphism
\eta^{\prime}\colon[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
depending only on
K,L
, and
\eta
such that
f\colon X\to Y
is
\eta^{\prime}
-quasisymmetric.

Proof.

For
x\in X
, we use the notation
x^{\prime}=f(x)
. Let
x,y,z\in X
such that
d_{X}(x,y)=td_{X}(x,z)
for some
t>0
. Our goal is to show that the images
x^{\prime},y^{\prime},z^{\prime}
satisfy

d_{Y}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\leq\eta^{\prime}(t)\cdot d_{Y}(x^{\prime},z^{\prime})

for some distortion function
\eta^{\prime}
that depends only on
K,L
, and
\eta
. Consider vertices
u,v,w\in V
such that
x\in U_{u}
,
y\in U_{v}
, and
z\in U_{w}
. We consider several cases for the relative positions of these vertices.

Case 1:

k(u,v)<2K
and
k(u,w)<2K
. Then
x,y,z\in\st_{2K}(u)
. By assumption
(2)
,
f
is an
\eta
-quasisymmetry on
\st_{2K+1}(u)
, so
d_{Y}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\leq\eta(t)d_{Y}(x^{\prime},z^{\prime})
.

Case 2:

k(u,v)\geq 2K
and
k(u,w)\geq 2K
. By
(A7)
, we have

\displaystyle d_{X}(p_{u},p_{v})\simeq_{L}d_{X}(x,y)\quad\text{and}\quad d_{Y}(p_{u}^{\prime},p_{v}^{\prime})\simeq_{L}d_{Y}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime}),

and

\displaystyle d_{X}(p_{u},p_{w})\simeq_{L}d_{X}(x,z)\quad\text{and}\quad d_{Y}(p_{u}^{\prime},p_{w}^{\prime})\simeq_{L}d_{Y}(x^{\prime},z^{\prime})

Thus, we have
d_{X}(p_{u},p_{v})\leq C(L)\cdot t\cdot d_{X}(p_{u},p_{w})
. Since
f|_{\mathfrak{p}(V)}
is
\eta
-quasisymmetric, we conclude that
d_{Y}(p_{u}^{\prime},p_{v}^{\prime})\leq\eta(C(L)\cdot t)d_{Y}(p_{u}^{\prime},p_{w}^{\prime})
, from which we obtain

d_{Y}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\leq C(L)\eta(C(L)\cdot t)d_{Y}(x^{\prime},z^{\prime}).

Case 3:

k(u,v)\geq 2K
and
k(u,w)<2K
. Let
s\in V
be such that
2K\leq k(u,s)<2K+1
; such
s
exists because
k(u,v)\geq 2K
and the graph
(V,\sim)
is connected by
(A5)
. Property
(A7)
implies that
d_{X}(p_{u},p_{v})\simeq_{L}d_{X}(x,y)
and the latter equals
t\cdot d_{X}(z,z)
by assumption. Since
x,z\in\st_{2K}(u)
, we have
d_{X}(x,z)\lesssim_{K,L}r_{u}
, by
(A2)
and
(A3)
. Finally, by
(A6)
we have
r_{u}\lesssim_{L}d_{X}(p_{u},p_{s})
. Combining these estimates, we have

d_{X}(p_{u},p_{v})\lesssim_{K,L}t\cdot d_{X}(p_{u},p_{s}).

Since
f
is
\eta
-quasisymmetric on
\mathfrak{p}(V)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\ d_{Y}(p_{u}^{\prime},p_{v}^{\prime})\leq\eta(C(K,L)\cdot t)d_{Y}(p_{u}^{\prime},p_{s}^{\prime}).

Invoking again
(A7)
, we obtain
d_{Y}(p_{u}^{\prime},p_{v}^{\prime})\simeq_{L}d_{Y}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
, so

(2.10)

\displaystyle d_{Y}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\leq C(L)\eta(C(K,L)\cdot t)d_{Y}(p_{u}^{\prime},p_{s}^{\prime}).

It remains to bound the term
d_{Y}(p_{u}^{\prime},p_{s}^{\prime})
in the right-hand side by a certain multiple of
d_{Y}(x^{\prime},z^{\prime})
.

By the condition
k(u,s)<2K+1
,
(A2)
, and
(A3)
, we have
d_{X}(x,p_{s})\lesssim_{K,L}r_{u}
. By
(A3)
, we have
U_{u}\cap U_{v}=\emptyset
, so
r_{u}\leq d_{X}(p_{u},p_{v})
. Therefore, by the above,

d_{X}(x,p_{s})\lesssim_{K,L}r_{u}\lesssim_{K,L}d_{X}(p_{u},p_{v})\simeq_{K,L}d_{X}(x,y)\simeq_{K,L}t\cdot d_{X}(x,z).

Note that
x,p_{s},z\in\st_{2K+1}(u)
. Since
f
is
\eta
-quasisymmetric in
\st_{2K+1}(u)
, we conclude that

(2.11)

\displaystyle d_{Y}(x^{\prime},p_{s}^{\prime})\leq\eta(C(K,L)\cdot t)d_{Y}(x^{\prime},z^{\prime}).

By
(A2)
and
(A3)
,
d_{Y}(p_{u}^{\prime},p_{s}^{\prime})\lesssim_{K,L}r_{u}^{\prime}
. By
(A6)
we have
r_{u}^{\prime}\lesssim_{L}d_{Y}(x^{\prime},p_{s}^{\prime})
. These two estimates and (
2.11
) give

d_{Y}(p_{u}^{\prime},p_{s}^{\prime})\leq C(K,L)\eta(C(K,L)\cdot t)d_{Y}(x^{\prime},z^{\prime}).

This estimate, combined with (
2.10
) gives the desired

d_{Y}(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})\leq C(K,L)\eta(C(K,L)\cdot t)^{2}d_{Y}(x^{\prime},z^{\prime}).

Case 4:

k(u,v)<2K

3.
Simplicial complexes

In this section we introduce metric and quasiconformal simplicial complexes. Then we discuss approximations of those complexes in the sense of Section
2
.

3.1.
Metric simplicial complexes

We provide some definitions. We direct the reader to
[
7
]
*Chapter I.7 for further details.

Let
n,m\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
with
n\leq m
. An
n
-simplex

S\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
is the convex hull of
n+1
points in general position, i.e., not lying in the same
n
-dimensional plane. The points are called the
vertices
of
S
. If the mutual distance of vertices is
1
, then
S
is called the
standard
n
-simplex
(which is unique up to isometry). A
face

T\subset S
is the convex hull of a non-empty subset of vertices of
S
. Each face
T
of
S
is a
k
-simplex for some
k\leq n
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\{S_{\lambda}\}_{\lambda\in\Lambda}
be a family of simplices. Let
Z=\bigcup_{\lambda\in\Lambda}(S_{\lambda}\times\{\lambda\})
and
\sim
be an equivalence relation on
Z
. The space
X=Z/\sim
is a
simplicial complex
if the natural projection
p\colon Z\to X
satisfies the following conditions.

(1)

For every
\lambda\in\Lambda
the map
p_{\lambda}=p(\cdot,\lambda)\colon S_{\lambda}\to X
is injective.

(2)

If
p_{\lambda}(S_{\lambda})\cap p_{\lambda^{\prime}}(S_{\lambda^{\prime}})\neq\emptyset
, then for each point
q\in p_{\lambda}(S_{\lambda})\cap p_{\lambda^{\prime}}(S_{\lambda^{\prime}})
there exists a face
T_{\lambda}
of
S_{\lambda}
, a face
T_{\lambda^{\prime}}
of
S_{\lambda^{\prime}}
, and an isometry
h_{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}\colon T_{\lambda}\to T_{\lambda^{\prime}}
such that
q\in p_{\lambda}(T_{\lambda})\cap p_{\lambda^{\prime}}(T_{\lambda^{\prime}})
and

p(x,\lambda)=p(x^{\prime},\lambda^{\prime})
if and only if
x^{\prime}=h_{\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}}(x)
.

By the above definition, the vertices and faces of
S_{\lambda}
,
\lambda\in\Lambda
, yield naturally the notions of vertices and faces of
X
. As a consequence, if a face of
X
is contained in a union of faces, then it must be contained in one of them. Let
S\subset p_{\lambda}(S_{\lambda})
be a face of
X
. We define
d_{S}(p_{\lambda}(x),p_{\lambda}(y))
to be the Euclidean distance of the points
x
and
y
. This gives a length metric on
S
.

Let
x,y\in X
. A
string
from
x
to
y
is a finite sequence
\Sigma=(z_{0},z_{1},\dots,z_{m})
, where
x=z_{0},z_{m}=y
,
m\in\mathbb{N}
, such that
z_{i-1},z_{i}
lie in the same face
S(i)\subset X
for each
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
. The length of
\Sigma
is defined as

\displaystyle\ell(\Sigma)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}d_{S(i)}(z_{i-1},z_{i}).

The
trace
of
\Sigma
is the union of the line segments from
z_{i-1}
to
z_{i}
,
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
, and is denoted by
|\Sigma|
. The
intrinsic pseudometric
on
X
is defined by

\displaystyle d_{X}(x,y)\coloneqq\inf\{\ell(\Sigma):\text{$\Sigma$ is a string from $x$ to $y$}\}.

If there is no such string, then
d_{X}(x,y)=\infty
. If
d_{X}
is a metric, i.e., for every pair of distinct points
x,y
we have
0<d_{X}(x,y)<\infty
, then
(X,d_{X})
is called a
metric simplicial complex
. In that case,
(X,d_{X})
is a length space.

Let
X,Y
be simplicial complexes. A map
f\colon X\to Y
is
simplicial
if it maps each simplex of
X
linearly onto a simplex of
Y
. We say that
f
is a simplicial isomorphism if
f
is in addition bijective. In that case,
f^{-1}
is also a simplicial map.

Let
X
be a simplicial complex. For a point
x\in X
, let
S(x)
be the collection of all simplices containing
x
. For
S\in S(x)
, define
\varepsilon(x,S)
to be the distance in the length metric of
S
from
x
to the union of faces of
S
that do not contain
x
. Also, let
\varepsilon(x)=\inf\varepsilon(x,S)
where the infimum is over all simplices
S\in S(x)
. Then

(3.1)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &B_{d_{X}}(x,\varepsilon(x))\subset\bigcup S(x)\quad\text{and}\\
&d_{X}(x,y)=d_{S}(x,y)\,\,\,\text{for}\,\,\,y\in B_{d_{X}}(x,\varepsilon(x))\cap S,\,\,\,\text{where}\,\,\,S\in S(x).\end{aligned}

See
[
7
]
*Lemma I.7.9 for an argument. We list some elementary properties of simplicial complexes.

(SC1)

If
\varepsilon(x)>0
for each
x\in X
then
(X,d_{X})
is a length space
[
7
]
*Corollary I.7.10.

(SC2)

Let
x\in X
. If
S(x)
contains finitely many simplices, then
\varepsilon(x)>0
. In that case, we say that
X
is
locally finite
at
x
. If
X
is locally finite at each point, then we say that
X
is
locally finite
. By
(SC1)
, a locally finite simplicial complex is a length space.

(SC3)

Let
f
be a simplicial map from
X
onto a simplicial complex
Y
. If
X
is locally finite, it is a consequence of (
3.1
) that
f
is continuous. If
f
is a simplicial isomorphism, then
f
is a homeomorphism.

3.2.
Quasiconformal simplicial complexes

We introduce the notion of a quasiconformal simplicial complex, by requiring that the simplices have uniform geometry.

Definition 3.2
.

Let
X
be a simplicial complex and
M\geq 1
. We say that
X
is an
M
-
quasiconformal simplicial complex
if the following conditions hold.

(QC-SC1)

For each
x\in X
,
\#S(x)\leq M
. That is,
x
belongs to at most
M
simplices.

(QC-SC2)

For each
n
-simplex
S\subset X
, where
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a linear map
\tau
from
S
onto the standard
n
-simplex in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

M^{-1}\frac{d_{S}(x,y)}{\diam_{d_{S}}(S)}\leq|\tau(x)-\tau(y)|\leq M\frac{d_{S}(x,y)}{\diam_{d_{S}}(S)}

for all
x,y\in S
.

(QC-SC3)

For each
x\in X
, if
S,T\in S(x)
are non-degenerate simplices, then

M^{-1}\leq\frac{\diam_{d_{S}}(S)}{\diam_{d_{T}}(T)}\leq M.

Remark 3.3
.

The bound
\#S(x)\leq M
implies that
X
does not have any
n
-simplices for
n>M
.

Our goal is to prove that a simplicial isomorphism between quasiconformal simplicial complexes is locally bi-Lipschitz in a quantitative sense; see Lemma
3.7
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
X
be a simplicial complex and
x,y\in X
be distinct vertices. Then
d_{X}(x,y)\geq\varepsilon(x)
. If, in addition, there exists
M\geq 1
such that
X
is an
M
-quasiconformal simplicial complex and the vertices
x,y
lie in a simplex
S\subset X
, then

\varepsilon(x)\leq d_{X}(x,y)\leq\diam_{d_{X}}(S)\leq\diam_{d_{S}}(S)\leq c(M)\varepsilon(x).

Proof.

Let
x,y\in X
be distinct vertices. We show the first inequality. If
d_{X}(x,y)<\varepsilon(x)
, then, by (
3.1
),
y\in B_{d_{X}}(x,\varepsilon(x))\subset\bigcup S(x)
, so
y\in S
for some
S\in S(x)
, and
d_{X}(x,y)=d_{S}(x,y)
. By the definition of
\varepsilon(x,S)
, we have
d_{S}(x,y)\geq\varepsilon(x,S)\geq\varepsilon(x)
. This is a contradiction.

Next, observe that the height of the standard
n
-simplex is
\sqrt{(n+1)/(2n)}\geq\sqrt{2}/2
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Thus the distance of each vertex of the standard simplex to the faces that do not contain it is at least
\sqrt{2}/2
. This implies that if
X
is
M
-quasiconformal and
x
is a vertex, then by condition
(QC-SC2)
, for each
S\in S(x)
we have

\varepsilon(x,S)\geq M^{-1}\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\diam_{d_{S}}(S).

If
T\in S(x)
is any other simplex, then by the above and
(QC-SC3)
we have

\varepsilon(x,T)\geq M^{-2}\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\diam_{d_{S}}(S).

Thus,
\varepsilon(x)\geq c(M)\diam_{d_{S}}(S)
. The remaining inequalities in the statement of the lemma are trivial.
∎

4.1.
Gromov–Hausdorff convergence

Let

be a metric space and let

. A map

|d_{X}(x,y)-d_{Y}(f(x),f(y))|<\varepsilon

We define the

and

. A sequence of sets

to a set

and

and

, respectively, and

. This is denoted by

. We note that if there exists an
\varepsilon
-isometry from

to
Y
for some
\varepsilon>0
, then
d_{GH}(X,Y)<2\varepsilon

[
8
]
*Corollary 7.3.28. We say that a sequence of metric spaces

,

,
converges in the Gromov–Hausdorff sense
to a metric space

if

. By
[

]
*Corollary 7.3.28, this is equivalent to the requirement that there exists a sequence of

-isometries

. See

]
*Section 7 and

5.1.
Geometric notions

Let
(X,d,\mu)
be a metric measure space. For a set
G\subset X
and disjoint sets
E,F\subset G
we define
\Gamma(E,F;G)
to be the family of curves in
G
joining
E
and
F
. Let
\Gamma
be a family of curves in
X
. A Borel function
\rho\colon X\to[0,\infty]
is
admissible
for
\Gamma
if
\int_{\gamma}\rho\,ds\geq 1
for all rectifiable paths
\gamma\in\Gamma
. Let
Q\geq 1
. We define the
Q
-modulus
of
\Gamma
as

\Mod_{Q}\Gamma=\inf_{\rho}\int_{X}\rho^{Q}\,d\mu,

where the infimum is taken over all admissible functions
\rho
for
\Gamma
. We say that
X
is a
Q
-Loewner space
if there exists a decreasing function
\phi\colon(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
such that for every pair of disjoint non-degenerate continua
E,F\subset X
we have

\Mod_{Q}\Gamma(E,F;X)\geq\phi(\Delta(E,F)),

where
\Delta(E,F)
is the
relative distance
of
E
and
F
, which is defined by

\frac{\dist(E,F)}{\min\{\diam(E),\diam(F)\}}.

We say that
X
is
Ahlfors
Q
-regular
if there exists a constant
M\geq 1
such that

M^{-1}r^{Q}\leq\mu(B(x,r))\leq Mr^{Q}

for each
x\in X
and
0<r<\diam(X)
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space. We say that
X
is
linearly locally connected
(
\llc
) if there exists a constant
M\geq 1
such that for each ball
B(a,r)
in
X
the following two conditions hold.

(LLC
1
)

For every
x,y\in B(a,r)
there exists a connected set
E\subset B(a,Mr)
that contains
x
and
y
.

(LLC
2
)

For every
x,y\in X\setminus B(a,r)
there exists a connected set
E\subset X\setminus B(a,r/M)
that contains
x
and
y
.

In that case we say that
X
is
M
-LLC. We say that
X
is
doubling
if there exists a constant
M\geq 1
such that for every
R>0
, every ball of radius
R
can be covered by at most
M
balls of radius radius
R/2
. In that case we say that
X
is
M
-doubling.

5.2.
Quasiconformal maps

A

is a
2
-dimensional topological manifold with a metric inducing its topology. In this section, metric surfaces are always considered as metric measure spaces endowed with the Hausdorff
2
-measure (or else area measure). Let

be metric surfaces of locally finite Hausdorff

-measure. A homeomorphism

is

if there exists

such that

K^{-1}\Mod_{2}\Gamma\leq\Mod_{2}h(\Gamma)\leq K\Mod_{2}\Gamma

for every curve family
\Gamma
in

. In this case we say that

is
K
-quasiconformal. We say that a map

is

if

is the uniform limit of a sequence of homeomorphisms and there exists

such that for every curve family

in

\displaystyle\Mod_{2}\Gamma\leq K\Mod_{2}h(\Gamma).

In this case we say that

Rajala

introduced the notion of a

More generally, it was shown in

When the target surface is reciprocal, a weakly quasiconformal map is upgraded to a genuine quasiconformal map, according to the next lemma.
