Recovery of iron from iron tailings by co-pyrolysis with coal gangue: process optimization and economic evaluation
Authors: Zheng Liu, Fengxing Zhang, Guanling Zheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIron tailings and coal gangue are voluminous solid waste byproducts. However, traditional disposal methods and conventional reduction roasting often are hindered by high costs and a reliance on external fossil fuels as reductants. To overcome these challenges, this study introduces a novel co-pyrolysis process utilizing coal gangue as the sole reductant source for recovering iron from tailings. Key process parameters, including co-pyrolysis temperature (600 – 900 °C), co-pyrolysis time (20 – 60 min), coal gangue ratio (10 – 50 wt%), and magnetic separation intensity (60 – 140 mT), were optimized. In optimal conditions (800 °C, 30 min, 20% wt%, 100 mT), an iron concentrate with 59.10% total Fe (TFe) and 75.4% recovery rate was obtained. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), X-ray diffraction (XRD), and vibrating sample magnetometer (VSM) revealed that the pyrolysis of coal gangue generated reducing gases (CO, H2, CH4) and fixed carbon. These reductants were capable of converting the weakly magnetic hematite (Fe2O3) to the strongly magnetic magnetite (Fe3O4), facilitating efficient low-intensity magnetic separation. An economic evaluation of a production scale of 250,000 t/y showed a net cost of 86.79 USD/t after accounting for byproduct revenue. Importantly, aiming for a zero-waste process, the residual waste was successfully transformed into high-strength non-fired bricks, providing a technically viable and economically superior closed-loop solution compared to existing single-stream utilization methods.Keywords: Iron tailings, Coal gangue, Iron recovery, Magnetic separation, Economic evaluationSubject terms: Chemistry, Energy science and technology, Engineering, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Steel is one of the most important materials, ubiquitous in our daily lives. The World Steel Association reported that global crude steel production was 1,885 million tons in 20241. The steady increase in steel production has stimulated demand for iron ore, inevitably leading to the generation of large amounts of iron tailings. Iron tailings are solid wastes produced during the mining, crushing, and sorting processes of iron ore. It has been reported that in the production of iron concentrate, the generation of iron tailings is approximately 1.5 tons per ton of finished product2. In 2024, China’s iron tailings reached 540 million tons, accounting for more than 50% of the total tailings production3. However, the utilization rate of iron tailings remains at 20%, which is inadequate4. The issue of the accumulation of large quantities of iron tailings is of particular environmental concern, due to the significant area of land it occupies and the resultant pollution it causes. In addition, it represents a considerable waste of resources. Therefore, efficiently achieving the resource utilization of iron tailings has become a research hotspot.

There are already numerous reports on the resource utilization of iron tailings. For example, iron tailings can be used as construction materials5–7, backfill in underground goafs8,9, to prepare functional materials10–12, or to recover useful elements13,14. Two challenges characterize the present situation. Firstly, there is the issue of the increasing scarcity of high-grade iron ore deposits. Secondly, and concomitantly, there is the steady rise in steel demand, with iron tailings possessing substantial iron content. Researchers have investigated the prospect of recovering iron from these tailings. A variety of recovery methods are available, including reduction roasting15–17, acid leaching18–20, and bioleaching21,22. Reduction roasting has been identified as a viable method for the effective utilization of iron ores with a low magnetic susceptibility. The process entails the reduction of weakly magnetic iron ore to strongly magnetic magnetite through carbon monoxide (CO) and hydrogen (H2). Subsequently, magnetic separation is employed to obtain iron concentrate23. The utilization of fossil fuels as the primary source of heating and reducing gases for reduction roasting is inherently limited by cost and environmental concerns. Previous studies have used waste sawdust24, nutshell 25, and cow dung26 as reductants. But it’s hard to collect them, and there aren’t many. Consequently, there is an urgent need to identify abundant waste carbon sources that do not present collection challenges.

Coal gangue, as the primary by-product of coal mining and washing processes, contains a certain amount of residual carbon and volatile matter. In the presence of elevated temperatures and an absence of oxygen, coal gangue has been observed to generate reducing gases in addition to reducing fixed carbon27. This characteristic provides a novel approach for the recovery of iron from iron tailings. It has been demonstrated that the magnetism of iron minerals is significantly enhanced through reduction magnetization roasting 28. This process allows for efficient separation of iron from gangue minerals using low-intensity magnetic separation. This method achieves “treating waste with waste” and offers a collaborative disposal solution for two major bulk solid wastes.

This study proposes a technical scheme for the recovery of iron through the process of co-pyrolysis of coal gangue and iron tailings. An investigation was conducted into the effects of key process parameters, including co-pyrolysis temperature, co-pyrolysis time, mixing ratio, and magnetic separation intensity, on iron recovery rate and concentrate grade, with the aim of ascertaining the optimal process conditions. In addition, the reaction mechanisms and phase transformation rules during the co-pyrolysis process were investigated through a range of analytic methods, including thermogravimetric analysis (TGA), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), X-ray diffraction (XRD), and vibrating sample magnetometer (VSM). A final economic evaluation of the process was conducted, and an investigation was made into the potential utilization of the residual waste slag in building materials. Ultimately, the study aims to propose a technically viable and economically sound solution for the collaborative high-value utilization of iron tailings and coal gangue.

Experimental procedure

The co-pyrolysis experiment of coal gangue and iron tailings was conducted using a high-temperature rotary tube furnace (KJ-G12, KJLY). The coal gangue and iron tailings were vigorously shaken in a conical flask at a specific ratio to achieve uniform mixing. Subsequently, they were positioned at the core of a quartz tube, with N2 (99.9% purity, 100 mL/min) introduced to purge the air. The initiation of the nitrogen flow was halted once the tube furnace attained the predetermined temperature. Throughout the process, the valve at one end of the furnace remained closed, while the other end was connected to the external environment via an insulated plug. Following the attainment of the predetermined reaction time, the heating process was terminated, and N2 (99.9% purity, 100 mL/min) was reintroduced to cool the mixture to room temperature before the removal of the pyrolyzed mixture. During the process of pyrolysis and cooling, the furnace tube rotated at a speed of 1 r/min. Based on the thermodynamic study and preliminary experiments on coal gangue pyrolysis, the temperature at which the process of co-pyrolysis was conducted was set at 600 – 900 °C. The duration of the co-pyrolysis process ranged from 20 to 60 min. The coal gangue mixing ratio was set at 10 – 50% wt. A magnetic separator (DTCXG-ZN50, DTEE) was used for low-intensity magnetic separation of the pyrolyzed mixture to obtain iron concentrate and residual waste. The intensity of the magnetic field was set at a range of 60 – 140 mT. The experiment was repeated three times, and the average result was recorded. The experimental setup and process flow are illustrated in Fig. 1. The pyrolysis tail gas is absorbed and treated using a gas-washing bottle containing an alkaline solution.

Analytical methods

The microstructure was observed employing a field emission scanning electron microscope (Regulus 8100) and a field emission transmission electron microscope (FEI Tecnai G2 F20). The phase structural analysis was conducted by means of an X-ray diffractometer (XRD, D8 Advance, Bruker). The thermal reaction characteristics were analyzed using thermal analysis instrumentation (TGA/DSC 3+, METTLER TOLEDO). The magnetic hysteresis loop testing was performed utilizing a physical property measurement system (PPMS DynaCool, Quantum Design). The volume concentration of pyrolysis gases from coal gangue was measured using gas chromatography (GC-2014, Shimadzu). The compressive strength of non-fired bricks was determined using an electronic pressure testing machine (RGM-6300Y, Regal).

The iron grade is determined by the potassium dichromate titration method. Weigh out 0.2 g of sample powder and transfer it into a conical flask. Add 10 mL of concentrated HCl, after which the mixture is heated gently until the sample is fully dissolved. The titration process is conducted using a saturated SnCl2 solution until the yellow colouration disappears. Following this, the cooling process is initiated, and 5 mL of sulfuric-phosphoric acid mixture is added (where sulfuric acid, phosphoric acid, and water are measured out at a ratio of 1:1:2). The addition of three drops of a 0.2% sodium diphenylamine sulfonate solution serves as the indicator. The next step is to titrate with a 0.02 M K2Cr2O7 solution. During the initial stage, the addition is made drop-by-drop. As the endpoint is approached, the addition of drops is undertaken at 5-second intervals. Results and discussion

Iron recovery using fine grinding-magnetic separation

To improve the TFe of the concentrate and lower the SiO2 content, an initial attempt was made to recover iron from iron tailings through fine grinding-magnetic separation. The iron tailings were ground in a vertical roller mill to a fineness of -0.02 mm, accounting for 80% of the mass. As shown in Fig. 2, a low-intensity magnetic separation (LIMS) was first performed at a magnetic field intensity of 90 kA/m, producing an LIMS iron concentrate with 61.83% TFe, 3.02% SiO2, and 7.45% iron recovery. The remaining ore powder was then subjected to a high-intensity magnetic separation (HIMS) at 700 kA/m, producing an HIMS iron concentrate with 49.77% TFe, 8.91% SiO2, and 58.28% iron recovery. After combining the LIMS and HIMS concentrates, the TFe was determined to be 51.50%, with an SiO2 content of 8.33%. It was evident that the iron recovery from HIMS exhibited a significantly higher value in comparison to that from LIMS. However, it was observed that the SiO2 present within the HIMS iron concentrate was comparatively high. This indicated that the high-intensity magnetic separator exhibited suboptimal separation efficiency for the ore powder. The findings suggested that even with fine grinding, the conventional fine grinding-magnetic separation process resulted in a low TFe recovery and was not the optimal solution for iron recovery.

Thermodynamic study on the reduction of iron oxide

To assess the thermodynamic spontaneity of iron recovery from iron tailings and coal gangue through pyrolysis, the reduction of iron oxide was investigated from a thermodynamic perspective. The primary reducing gases that were produced during the pyrolysis of coal gangue, namely H2, CH4, and CO, were regarded as reductants, in addition to the inherent carbon present within the coal gangue and the carbon produced from the pyrolysis29. To facilitate comparison, the stoichiometric coefficient of Fe2O3 in the reaction was standardized to 2, and only one reductant was considered to participate in the reaction. The standard Gibbs free energy change (ΔrG°), in relation to temperature, for the potential reactions between Fe2O3 and the four reductants was calculated using HSC Chemistry 6.0, as illustrated in Fig. 3(a) – (d). It was observed that under standard conditions and at lower temperatures, the reaction’s ΔrG° was negative, indicating the easy reduction of Fe2O3 to Fe3O430. However, the reduction of Fe2O3 to FeO and Fe required higher temperatures. Specifically, when H2, CH4, and C were used as reductants, respectively, the temperature required for Fe2O3 to be reduced to FeO needed to be above 552 K, 677 K, and 703 K. Furthermore, the temperature necessary for the reduction of Fe2O3 to Fe was above 783 K, 831 K, and 863 K. When CO was used as a reductant, within the temperature range of 300 – 1300 K, the ΔrG° for the reduction of Fe2O3 to Fe3O4, FeO, and Fe were all negative, indicating a spontaneous tendency for Fe2O3 to be reduced by CO over a wide temperature range.

In summary, Fe2O3 is readily reduced to Fe3O4. However, due to the difficulty of separating FeO by magnetic separation, the reduction temperature must be controlled above 863 K (approximately 590 °C). It was therefore proposed that the co-pyrolysis temperature in the ensuing experiments be initiated at 600 °C.

Optimization of process conditions

The efficiency and completeness of the reduction reaction, as well as the separability of the final products, are directly or indirectly controlled by process conditions such as co-pyrolysis temperature, co-pyrolysis time, coal gangue to iron tailings ratio, and magnetic separation intensity. Consequently, investigating the influence of these parameters on the iron recovery rate and TFe is crucial for efficient and economical iron recovery from iron tailings.

Effect of co-pyrolysis temperature

The co-pyrolysis temperature exerts a direct influence on iron recovery31. This occurrence can be ascribed to the elevated temperature of co-pyrolysis, which has been demonstrated to enhance the process of heat and mass transfer between iron tailings and reductants. However, the composition of reductants produced from coal gangue pyrolysis varies at different temperatures, and the products of Fe2O3 reduction also differ32. The effect of co-pyrolysis temperature on iron recovery was studied under conditions of 30 min co-pyrolysis time, 20% coal gangue ratio, and 100 mT magnetic separation intensity.

As demonstrated in Fig. 4(a), an elevation in co-pyrolysis temperature was associated with a minimal change in TFe. As the co-pyrolysis temperature rose from 600 °C to 900 °C, the TFe in the magnetic separation concentrate fluctuated between 57.5% and 59.5%. TFe did not manifest a discernible trend in relation to the co-pyrolysis temperature. It was posited that elevated pyrolysis temperatures could enhance reaction spontaneity; however, the influence of elevated temperatures on the type and concentration of reducing agents was also highlighted. This finding suggests that the effect of pyrolysis temperature on TFe is not straightforward. The iron recovery rate initially showed an upward trend with rising pyrolysis temperature. At 800 °C, the iron recovery rate exhibited a notable peak of 75.4%. It was hypothesised that during the pyrolysis process, the weakly magnetic hematite might gradually transform into the strongly magnetic magnetite. At lower pyrolysis temperatures, the iron recovery rate was comparatively low because hematite was not fully reduced to magnetite. Consequently, in order to enhance the iron recovery rate, it was imperative to elevate the pyrolysis temperature.

Influence of (a) co-pyrolysis temperature, (b) co-pyrolysis time, (c) coal gangue to iron tailings ratio, and (d) magnetic separation intensity on iron recovery rate and TFe.

However, at temperatures exceeding 900 °C, the hematite particles on the surface of the iron tailings may be over-reduced, and the generated FeO reacted with Al2O3 and SiO2, leading to the formation of weakly magnetic iron-containing compounds such as FeAl2O4 and Fe2SiO433. This process facilitated the introduction of Al, Si, and other elements into the magnetic concentrate34. These iron-containing compounds encapsulated the particle surface, hindering the further reduction of internal iron oxides, and resulting in a decrease in the iron recovery rate. At a co-pyrolysis temperature of 800 °C, both TFe and the iron recovery rate of the iron concentrate achieved relatively favourable results. Therefore, the pyrolysis temperature was set at 800 °C.

Effect of co-pyrolysis time

Co-pyrolysis time is also a significant factor in determining the level of iron recovery26,35. The effect of co-pyrolysis time on iron recovery was studied under conditions of 800 °C co-pyrolysis temperature, 20% coal gangue ratio, and 100 mT magnetic separation intensity. As demonstrated in Fig. 4(b), an elevation in co-pyrolysis time resulted in a corresponding rise in TFe. An increase in TFe from 54.35% to 59.10% was observed when the co-pyrolysis time was extended from 20 min to 30 min. As the reaction time continued to increase, the TFe showed only a marginal improvement. The effect of co-pyrolysis time on iron recovery rate was even more significant. When the co-pyrolysis time was prolonged to 30 min, the iron recovery rate increased significantly to 75.4%, which was notably higher than the 39.97% achieved at 20 min of co-pyrolysis time. Subsequently, the iron recovery rate exhibited a gradual decrease in conjunction with an increase in reaction time.

It was hypothesised that when the co-pyrolysis time was too long, the resultant Fe3O4 could undergo further reactions with the excess reducing gases produced by coal gangue pyrolysis or the carbon present in coal gangue. This process was believed to result in the formation of FeO. Conversely, an overly long co-pyrolysis time could also result in the exhaustion of reducing gases and carbon, thereby causing the generated Fe3O4 to undergo reoxidation. These factors resulted in a reduction in the minerals that could be separated by magnetic separation, thereby lowering the iron recovery rate. The results indicated that a co-pyrolysis time of 30 min yielded optimal TFe and iron recovery rate. This favorable kinetic condition was conducive to subsequent scaling up and practical application. The study demonstrated that there was a limited effect on improving the TFe of the iron concentrate when the co-pyrolysis time was extended. Given the direct correlation between co-pyrolysis time and energy consumption, it was recommended to optimize the co-pyrolysis time to 30 min.

Effect of coal gangue to iron tailings ratio

The mixing ratio of coal gangue and iron tailings has a significant impact on iron recovery. This is because the proportion of coal gangue has a substantial effect on the amount of reducing agent required during the reaction process. The effect of coal gangue ratio (wt%) on iron recovery was studied under conditions of 800 °C co-pyrolysis temperature, 30 min co-pyrolysis time, and 100 mT magnetic separation intensity.

As demonstrated in Fig. 4(c), an increase in the coal gangue ratio from 10% to 20% resulted in a simultaneous improvement in both TFe and the iron recovery rate. When the coal gangue ratio continued to increase, the TFe remained essentially unchanged, while the iron recovery rate showed a gradual downward trend. When the coal gangue ratio was 50%, the iron recovery rate decreased to 61.2%. This phenomenon was attributed to the observation that an elevated coal gangue ratio could provide a greater supply of reductants, thereby promoting the conversion of hematite to magnetite. However, an excessively high coal gangue ratio could generate excessive reducing gases during pyrolysis, leading to the over-reduction of hematite in iron tailings to FeO15. A significant proportion of the formed FeO, which was characterized by a weak magnetic susceptibility, proved to be difficult to separate effectively through magnetic separation processes. This phenomenon led to a decline in the efficiency of iron recovery. Consequently, a mixing ratio of 20% of coal gangue to iron tailings was identified as the optimal proportion for the mixture.

Effect of magnetic separation intensity

The magnetism exhibited by iron-containing products during the co-pyrolysis process is characterized by its variability and heterogeneity. The efficacy of magnetic separation depends on the magnetic field force’s capacity to overcome existing resistances, thereby facilitating the separation of the target magnetic material from non-magnetic materials25. The magnetic field intensity is a key adjustable parameter in the generation of magnetic force. Therefore, experiments on magnetic separation intensity were conducted on the pyrolysis slag under the following conditions: 800 °C co-pyrolysis temperature, 30 min co-pyrolysis time, and 20% coal gangue ratio.

Figure 4(d) showed that the TFe exhibited an initial upward trend, followed by a subsequent decrease. As the magnetic field intensity increased from 60 mT to 100 mT, a slight increase in the grade was observed, reaching a peak of 59.10% at 100 mT. Subsequently, as the magnetic field intensity further increased to 120 mT and 140 mT, the grade underwent a significant decrease. This was because, at lower magnetic field intensity, the magnetic force was insufficient to firmly adsorb all target strongly magnetic particles.

It was posited that an increase in magnetic field intensity would result in enhanced adsorption of high-grade iron particles, which might lead to a slight improvement in concentrate grade. However, when the magnetic field intensity exceeded the optimal selectivity point (100 mT), the magnetic force became excessively strong, and this began to have a negative impact. A stronger magnetic force would select iron oxides with a lower grade of magnetism that were not previously adsorbed into the concentrate. It is important to note that a high magnetic field intensity would significantly adsorb intergrown magnetic minerals and non-magnetic gangue. The presence of gangue minerals in the concentrate would serve to dilute the iron content.

As the magnetic field intensity increased from 60 mT to 140 mT, the iron recovery rate exhibited a continuous upward trend from 67.4% to 76.5%. This was attributed to the higher magnetic field intensity, which had the capacity to overcome the weaker magnetic response exhibited by FeO. This resulted in the adsorption of FeO into the magnetic concentrate. Therefore, to achieve maximum selectivity for strongly magnetic particles, it was necessary to set the magnetic separator’s magnetic field intensity to 100 mT.

Mechanism of the co-pyrolysis process

Analysis of the pyrolysis products from coal gangue

Coal gangue is a pivotal raw material in the provision of reductants. It is imperative to comprehend the temperature range and rate at which these reducing gases are produced, and to quantify the types, yields, and proportions of these reductants. This is of fundamental and crucial significance for elucidating the co-pyrolysis mechanism.

As demonstrated in Fig. 5(a), the TG curve exhibited the mass loss of coal gangue during the heating process. The most rapid mass loss was observed to occur at a temperature range of approximately 350 °C to 650 °C. This process was understood to involve the thermal decomposition of organic matter (coal components) within coal gangue, yielding volatile matter (gases and liquid oils). As the temperature rose from approximately 650 °C to 800 °C, the mass of coal gangue decreased steadily. This phenomenon might be attributed related to the decomposition of more stable organic matter at higher temperatures or the decomposition of certain minerals, such as dehydroxylation of kaolinite or a minor amount of carbonate decomposition. At temperatures exceeding 800 °C during the pyrolysis process, the mass of coal gangue exhibited minimal fluctuations. The residual products primarily comprised ash and fixed carbon. Meanwhile, Fig. 5(a) showed that the peak of the DTG curve was indicative of the temperature at which the most rapid mass loss occurred. A peak of considerable significance manifested at a temperature ranging from approximately 530 °C to 550 °C. This observation indicated that this specific temperature range was conducive to the most pronounced decomposition of organic matter within the coal gangue. A smaller, broader peak or shoulder peak was observed around 700 – 750 °C, corresponding to a secondary weight loss stage. This phenomenon, when considered in conjunction with Fig. 5(c), suggested a potential correlation with decomposition reactions releasing CO.

(a) TG-DTG of coal gangue pyrolysis; (b) Mass yield of coal gangue pyrolysis products; (c) Mass fraction of main components in coal gangue pyrolysis gas.

Figure 5(b) presents the mass yields of solid phase (char), liquid phase (liquid oil), and gas phase (gas) products obtained from coal gangue pyrolysis in the temperature range of 600 °C to 900 °C. The principal product of coal gangue pyrolysis was solid residue, with a yield of 88 – 89%. Despite the relatively low mass percentage of gaseous products (approximately 3%), their composition was of paramount importance when considering their application as reductants. Temperature exhibited a negligible impact on total gas yield. However, it was hypothesised that temperature might influence the conversion between gas and liquid oil.

As illustrated in Fig. 5(c), the mass percentage of the primary components (H2, CH4, CO2, CO) in the coal gangue pyrolysis gas was examined within the temperature range of 600 °C to 900 °C. As the temperature elevated, a significant increase was observed in the proportion of strong reductants, CO and H2, whilst the proportion of CH4 and CO2 gradually decreased. Methane also exhibited reducing properties; however, it was susceptible to cracking at elevated temperatures. The role of carbon dioxide (CO2) as an oxidising agent in this reaction was well-documented, and its decrease had been shown to be beneficial for the reduction reaction. The formation of carbon monoxide (CO) was attributed to the cracking of methane (CH4) at elevated temperatures, the Boudouard reaction, or the water-gas shift reaction. The decline in CH4 and CO2, as demonstrated in the figure, confirmed the pathways of CH4 cracking and the Boudouard reaction producing CO.

From the perspective of gas composition, the original gases produced solely during the main pyrolysis stage (~ 400 – 550 °C) might not be sufficiently strong reductants. The gases generated at 800 °C and 900 °C exhibited the strongest reducing power. In consideration of energy consumption and the feasibility of the process, it was determined that an operative pyrolysis temperature of 800 °C would be conducive to the effective production of reducing gases.

Iron phase transformation during co-pyrolysis

In order to clarify the iron phase transformation, the raw materials and products were characterized. As shown in Fig. 6(a), coal gangue particles were arranged in layers, and clear layered structures were occasionally discernible at the edges, which was a characteristic morphology for clay minerals (e.g., kaolinite, illite, montmorillonite, etc.). Irregularly shaped angular blocky particles were also observed. These particles might be detrital minerals such as quartz and feldspar, or they might be rock fragments that had not yet undergone complete decomposition. The size distribution of these particles was uneven, ranging from submicrometer to tens of micrometers. This suggested that the coal gangue sample exhibited a broad particle size distribution and heterogeneity.

(a) SEM, (b) EDS, and (c) XRD of coal gangue; (d) SEM, (e) EDS, and (f) XRD of iron tailings; (g) SEM, (h) EDS, and (i) XRD of iron concentrate.

As demonstrated in Fig. 6(b), the EDS analysis indicated a high abundance of Al, Si, C, and O elements. The elevated levels of silicon and aluminium indicated that the sample was rich in aluminosilicate minerals. The presence of the C element was indicative of the presence of residual organic carbon (coal) or carbonate minerals within the coal gangue. XRD analysis of Fig. 6(c) revealed that the sample predominantly comprised quartz, kaolinite, and muscovite.

As demonstrated in Fig. 6(d), the predominant particle morphology of iron tailings was found to be either flaky or scaly. This was a typical morphological characteristic of many-layered silicate minerals (such as mica group, chlorite, kaolinite, and other clay minerals) after crushing or grinding. The majority of the flaky particles exhibited a size range of 1 – 5 micrometers. The EDS analysis in Fig. 6(e) revealed the presence of major elements such as Si, O, Fe, Al, Mg, and K. Among the distinct characteristic peaks of Fe, the L-series was observed at approximately 0.7 keV in the low-energy region, a stronger Kα peak was detected at approximately 6.4 keV, and the Kβ peak was at approximately 7.06 keV. This finding confirmed the presence of iron tailings with a high iron content. XRD analysis of Fig. 6(f) revealed that the sample predominantly comprised quartz and hematite.

As demonstrated in Fig. 6(g), the iron concentrate particles exhibited a predominantly irregular granular or blocky morphology, accompanied by relatively rounded edges. A significant proportion of particles coalesced to form agglomerates, with a predominance of sizes ranging from sub-micron to several microns. The overall structure appeared to be relatively dense. About the composition of the specimen, Fig. 6(h) demonstrates that Fe and O were the most prevalent elements, thus indicating that the predominant minerals were iron oxides. In comparison with iron tailings, the relative content of elements representing silicate gangue minerals, such as Si, Al, Mg, K, and Na, had significantly decreased. This finding indicates that deoxygenation-reduction reactions of hematite had occurred. This finding indicated that the iron concentrate still contained a small amount of gangue mineral impurities. This was because the separation efficiency of the magnetic separation process was not 100%, with some gangue minerals being mechanically entrained within the agglomerates of iron mineral particles. XRD analysis of Fig. 6(i) demonstrated that the sample predominantly comprised quartz and magnetite. This suggested that the hematite present in the iron tailings had transformed into magnetite.

The phase transformation was subjected to further verification through TEM characterization. Figure 7(a) showed the TEM of iron tailings exhibited distinct lattice fringes, substantiating the crystalline nature of the substance within this region. The lattice spacings of 0.269 nm and 0.251 nm correspond to the (1 0 4) and (1 1 0) crystal planes of hexagonal hematite crystals, respectively. Figure 7(b) showed the TEM of the iron concentrate exhibited magnetite crystal plane fringes. The 0.223 nm lattice fringe was found to correspond to the (3 2 1) crystal plane of cubic magnetite crystals. Saturation magnetization is a method of evaluating the intensity of magnetism and the separation potential of iron-containing minerals. Higher saturation magnetization signifies a greater quantity of magnetic minerals or stronger magnetism, thereby facilitating effective separation through magnetic separation. The saturation magnetizations of iron tailings, co-pyrolysis products, and concentrate were analyzed by VSM. As illustrated in Fig. 7(c), the saturation magnetization of iron tailings was found to be a mere 0.30 emu/g, indicating a concomitant weak magnetism and consequent difficulty in their isolation through magnetic separation processes. This observation was consistent with the findings derived from earlier analyses. The saturation magnetization of pyrolysis products exhibited a substantial increase, reaching 27.3 emu/g. This finding suggested that the transformation of certain weakly magnetic minerals into strongly magnetic minerals occurred during the co-pyrolysis process. Following the process of magnetic separation, the saturation magnetization of the concentrate exhibited a further increase, reaching 32.1 emu/g. Consequently, the process of co-pyrolysis rendered the iron tailings highly magnetic, a property that proved advantageous in the subsequent separation procedure by LIMS.

Consequently, a cost-benefit analysis of the process was conducted to assess production costs. The estimated results were then compared with the market price of iron concentrate in order to ascertain the application potential of recycled iron. Considering fluctuations in transportation costs or energy prices, the project would only incur losses if transportation costs increased by 13.2% or electricity prices rose by 617%. This demonstrated the strong resilience of the process’s profitability.

Utilization of residual waste

Given the substantial quantity of residual waste, an investigation was initiated to ascertain its utilization potential. Specifically, residual waste was evaluated for its suitability as a building material, with a particular focus on its potential for use in the production of non-fired bricks. Non-fired bricks were prepared by using slag micro-powder (S95 grade) as a substitute for Portland cement and using water glass (modulus 2.5), granular NaOH (AR), and tap water as raw materials. The bricks were prepared with varying mixing ratios of residual waste to slag micro-powder. The compressive strengths at 3d, 7d, and 28d were measured, as demonstrated in Table 5. Conclusion

The present study successfully verified a cost-effective and sustainable “waste-treating-waste” strategy for the synergistic utilization of iron tailings and coal gangue, offering distinct advantages over traditional reduction roasting methods. Through systematic optimization, the process achieved a high iron recovery rate of 75.4% and a concentrate TFe of 59.10% under conditions of 800 °C pyrolysis temperature, 30 min duration, 20% coal gangue ratio, and 100 mT magnetic intensity. Mechanistic insights confirmed that pyrolysis-derived reducing agents facilitated the transformation of hematite to magnetite, enhancing magnetic separability without the need for external reductants or high-energy inputs. Preliminary economic analysis demonstrated the process was highly competitive. The net production cost of the commodity was determined to be 86.79 USD/t. Beyond iron extraction, the process ensured complete resource utilization. The residual waste was engineered into non-fired bricks, achieving a compressive strength of 43.8 MPa, exceeding industry standards (MU25). By integrating iron recovery with construction material production, this work provided a scalable, circular economy model. It offered a promising pathway to resolve the environmental burden of dual solid wastes while generating economic value, warranting further pilot-scale investigations.
