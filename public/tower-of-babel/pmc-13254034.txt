Biosensor Compared With Standard Care to Identify Extravasation Injuries in Paediatric Intensive Care: A Randomised Controlled Trial
Authors: Craig A. McBride, Sarfaraz Rahiman, Melanie Kennedy, Luregn J. Schlapbach, Jessica A. Schults, Tricia M. Kleidon, Thim Baveas, Rebecca Paterson, Joshua Byrnes, Robert S. Ware, Amanda J. Ullman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo evaluate whether, in children treated in paediatric intensive care units (PICU) with peripheral venous access devices (PVADs) receiving intermediate‐ or high‐risk peripheral intravenous medications, the use of a tissue‐infiltration biosensor (ivWatch), compared to standard care, reduces extravasation injuries.MethodsA superiority, parallel group randomised controlled trial conducted in a single Queensland quaternary hospital PICU from September 2020 to July 2022. Children with PVADs inserted in the distal half of the limb and receiving intermediate‐ to high‐risk infusates for ≥ 24 h were randomly allocated in a 1:1 ratio to biosensor (ivWatch monitor applied within 10–15 mm of the PVAD tip) plus standard clinical observation, or standard observation alone. Primary outcome was extravasation severity on the 4‐point Cincinnati Children's Hospital Medical Centre Extravasation Harm Scale. Injuries were compared between treatment groups using ordinal logistic regression.ResultsA total of 175 children were enrolled, 88 to biosensor and 87 to standard care. Extravasation severity was similar between groups, with no, mild and moderate injury occurring in 56 (69%), 25 (30%) and 2 (2%) participants in the biosensor group and 61 (70%), 24 (28%) and 2 (2%) in the standard care group (proportional odds ratio = 1.13; 95% CI: 0.59, 2.15, p = 0.71). No statistically significant between‐group differences in extravasation severity, volume, treatment sequelae, PVADs used or dwell time were observed. The biosensor was acceptable to nurses.ConclusionsIn this PICU population, biosensor monitoring in addition to standard care did not significantly reduce extravasation injuries. The technology was acceptable to bedside nursing staff. Trial Registration: ACTRN12620000317998

Introduction

Reliable access via peripheral venous access devices (PVADs) is essential for patients in Paediatric Intensive Care Units (PICUs) and paediatric wards. PVADs are primarily used for short‐term administration of peripherally compatible medications (e.g., antibiotics) and hydration [1, 2]. PVADs can be subdivided into short peripheral catheters (SPC), long peripheral catheters (LPC) and midline catheters (MC) [3]. In addition to PVADs, children in PICU commonly have a central venous access device (CVAD) and may also have other lines (such as arterial lines). PVADs are used primarily as an adjunct to care rather than a primary device. In urgent and critical situations, PVADs can be used to administer vesicant solutions, such as inotropes and electrolytes until such time as a CVAD can be sited [4, 5]. The nature of PVADs in children, across mobile areas into small veins, means extravasation injuries are common.

Extravasation is the accidental injection or leakage of potentially injurious solutions, such as a medication, fluid or parenteral nutrition, into the tissue surrounding the PVAD [6, 7]. Depending on their location extravasations sometimes lead to skin loss, tissue necrosis, tendon and nerve damage, or even limb amputation—with major costs to healthcare systems and affected patients [8, 9, 10, 11]. Injuries from extravasation are more common within the PICU when compared to other specialties and wards and occur in up to 11% of admitted children, likely as a result of higher patient complexity with multiple drug requirements as well as increased patient vulnerability due to factors such as sedation and compromised perfusion [1, 12]. Young children are inherently more vulnerable due to their inability to verbalise or localise pain/discomfort, their skin and vein fragility and difficulty inspecting PVAD insertion sites [1, 10, 13]. Regular assessment of a PVAD site for signs of injury (e.g., extravasation) and device dysfunction (e.g., blockage), along with prompt recognition and management, is the backbone of severe injury prevention [14, 15]. Such regular assessment—typically hourly for continuous infusions, at the commencement and completion of intermittent infusions, and every 5–10 min during vesicant administration—can be difficult in busy PICUs, as other high‐value tasks are prioritised [15, 16]. Sedation/intubation, vesicant or vasoactive medications, all increase the risk and potential severity of these injuries [17, 18].

The difficulty of accurately and consistently recognising PVAD extravasation in a timely manner highlights a critical treatment gap. Emerging technologies, such as biosensors, aim to address this gap by providing continuous, automated monitoring. One such biosensor is ivWatch (ivWatch LLC; Newport News; VA; USA; Figure 1), which uses visible and near‐infrared lights [19]. The ivWatch sensor continuously monitors the optical properties of tissue near the PVAD site and provides audible and visual notifications when tissue fluid volume changes. The manufacturer's product information states that the device can detect extravasations of as little as 0.22 mL of infused fluid [19, 20]. Although the technology shows considerable potential, there are few studies, and no randomised trials to date, exploring its effectiveness to reduce harm and thereby support its application into the PICU [21, 22, 23]. Given the manner in which this biosensor works, it is not expected it will decrease the event rate of extravasation injuries. It may, however, alert staff more rapidly to an extravasation event and therefore decrease the severity of any such injury.

The aim of this study was to evaluate, in critically ill children admitted to PICU and treated with intermediate‐ or high‐risk intravenous medications, whether the ivWatch biosensor in conjunction with standard clinical observation, compared with standard clinical observation alone, reduced extravasation severity. Secondarily, we aimed to compare the biosensor to standard clinical observation in terms of extravasation volume, quality of life impacts, healthcare costs and the sequelae of extravasation injuries during PICU admission. We also aimed to investigate the biosensor's acceptability to healthcare staff and the feasibility of conducting such research in a busy PICU.

Materials and Methods

Design

A single centre, parallel group, superiority, randomised controlled trial was conducted in the PICU of the Queensland Children's Hospital, a quaternary specialist paediatric hospital in Brisbane, Australia, from September 2020 to July 2022. Approximately 2000 critically ill children (neonates to 17 years old) are admitted to the multidisciplinary 36‐bed PICU annually. The trial was prospectively registered with the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry (ACTRN12620000317998) before recruitment started. The trial protocol and analysis plan has been published before recruitment completion [19]. The trial is reported in accordance with the CONSORT guidelines [24].

Population

Children with PVADs inserted in the distal half of any limb, receiving intermediate‐risk to high‐risk infusates for ≥ 24 h were approached for recruitment. Children on an end‐of‐life care pathway, palliative care involvement, no consent, non‐English speaking parents or caregivers requiring an interpreter were excluded [19]. Midline catheters were excluded, as the tip is distant from, and deeper to, the insertion site when compared with short and LPC.

Randomisation, Allocation Concealment and Masking

Following prospective informed consent, and immediately prior to PVAD insertion, participants were randomised by PICU clinical Research Nurses using a concealed web‐based randomisation software in a 1:1 ratio, with randomly selected block sizes of 8 or 10, ensuring allocation concealment.

Clinical staff, patients and caregivers were unable to be masked to their treatment allocation, as the PVAD with biosensor can be visually observed. Assessment and treatment of extravasation injuries for participants allocated standard care versus biosensor plus standard care were measured using objective and validated measures and were assigned routinely to clinical staff who were not study investigators. The biostatistician assessing the final dataset was masked to allocation.

Interventions

Participants were allocated to receive either: Standard care: clinical observation; orBiosensor plus standard care: clinical observation alongside the ivWatch monitor, applied within 10–15 mm of the PVAD cannula tip.

Observation continued until PVAD removal or until discharge from PICU.

Using visible and near infra‐red light, the biosensor continuously monitored the participant's PVAD tip and surrounding tissue. The biosensor can detect fluid accumulation in the subcutaneous tissue and subsequent changes in the scattering of infra‐red light, generating notifications of potential extravasation. The biosensor was used in accordance with the manufacturer's guidelines. For participants allocated to the biosensor group, a corner of the PVAD dressing was lifted to appropriately place the ivWatch sensor on the skin. All other aspects of PVAD insertion, management and removal were conducted in accordance with standard site‐specific policies and clinical practice guidelines. PVADs were usually inserted by a medical officer using palpation and visualisation, or image guidance such as ultrasound when indicated. Following assessment and selection of the most appropriate insertion site and PVAD (gauge and length), 2% chlorhexidine and alcohol were used for skin antisepsis. Following confirmation of successful PVAD insertion, a 3‐way tap with needleless connectors was attached. Dressing and securement typically consisted of a bordered transparent dressing, with or without tissue adhesive at the insertion site, which was at the discretion of the inserting clinician. Nursing staff performed regular PVAD site and patency assessments reflecting usual care to maintain pragmatic, generalisable study conditions.

Training was provided prior to use of the biosensor. The biosensor has two alarm settings determined by the manufacturer—yellow and red—indicating possible and presumptive extravasation injuries. Nursing staff were prompted by any yellow alarm to clinically assess the PVAD but could elect to leave it in situ. A red alarm prompted removal of the PVAD and assessment for a presumptive extravasation injury. Normal cares and checks were provided in both arms, as our intention was to assess ivWatch as a possible adjunct to normal cares rather than a replacement for them.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was extravasation severity, measured using the 4‐point Cincinnati Children's Hospital Medical Centre Extravasation Harm Scale (see Supporting Information) [10, 25, 26]. Secondary outcomes were (1) extravasation volume, as determined by surface to limb‐length ratio and two‐dimensional and three‐dimensional camera imaging; (2) active treatment sequelae, measured using number and types of dressings utilised, skin grafting and/or scar management requirement (censored at 6 months); (3) quantity of PVADs used per participant; (4) PVAD dwell time, measured in hours; (5) participant quality of life, measured using the Brisbane Burns Scar Impact Profile (BBSIP) [27, 28, 29] at 1, 3 and 6 months; (6) healthcare costs (cost per reduction in extravasation injury severity); and (7) feasibility based on eligibility, recruitment, retention, protocol fidelity, missing data and clinician acceptability within treatment groups.

Data Collection Methods

Patients were screened for inclusion eligibility by PICU Research Nurses and informed consent obtained from patients or caregivers. Research Nurses were responsible for educating PICU staff and ensuring protocol compliance throughout the trial. Quantitative data were collected from the electronic medical record (Cerner Corporation, North Kansas City, MI, USA) by the Research Nurses and entered into Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) [30]. Participants were followed up daily by Research Nurses until PIVC removal or patient discharge from PICU, and again at 7 and 28 days post‐discharge. If evidence of extravasation was observed or diagnosed, then participants were referred to the paediatric burns inpatient medical and nursing team. Discharged patients had follow‐up data collected by telephone. Acceptability data were collected via multi‐methods cross‐sectional survey at two time points (early and end of study), exploring acceptability via open text, numeric rating scales (0–10) and Likert responses (see Table S1). The study manager was responsible for conducting quality checks and monitoring 100% of the source data verification for all data associated with the first participant, all consent forms, all primary endpoints as well as a random 5% of data for all other patients.

Sample Size

Published local and international data report approximately 40% of intermediate‐ to high‐risk infusates result in extravasation injury within the paediatric inpatient population [7, 31]. Based on cohort data, we anticipated a clinically important 78% relative decrease in severity, which is equivalent to a proportional odds ratio (POR) of 0.32 [26, 32]. Consequently, to achieve 90% power (α = 0.05) to detect a difference of at least this magnitude we aimed to recruit 96 participants in each group.

Data Analysis

All results were analysed descriptively according to their characteristics and distribution. Continuous variables are described as mean (standard deviation; SD) or median (25th to 75th percentile) values. Categorical data are described as frequency (percentage). The association between treatment group and extravasation severity was assessed using ordinal logistic regression, with treatment group included as the main effect. The effect estimate is presented as POR and 95% confidence interval (95% CI). The association between treatment group and secondary outcomes measured on interval scales was assessed using linear regression, with treatment group included as the main effect. Effect estimates are presented as mean difference (MD) and 95% CI. Analysis follows the intention‐to‐treat principle with participants analysed in the group they were assigned to. A per‐protocol analysis examined the sensitivity of the primary outcome to treatment compliance. All analyses were conducted using Stata v13.1 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA).

Ethical Considerations

Ethics approval was obtained from the Children's Health Queensland Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC, reference number: HREC/20/QCHQ/60867), Griffith University HREC (reference number: 2020/310) and The University of Queensland HREC (2021/HE000581) before the study commenced. A statistical analysis plan was in place prior to commencement. Trial registration number: ACTRN12620000317998. This trial was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki's Note for Guidance on Good Clinical Practice (CPMP/ICH‐135/95) and the Australian National Health and Medical Research Council's National Statement on Ethical Conduct in Research Involving Humans. The authors declare the research presented in this manuscript adheres to the ethical principles outlined by the above‐named bodies. All procedures involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institution and the Declaration of Helsinki (1964), as revised in 2013.

Primary Outcome—Extravasation Severity

Extravasation injuries occurred in 27 (32.5%) and 26 (29.9%) of participants in the biosensor and standard care groups respectively, when assessed using the Cincinnati Children's Extravasation Harm four‐point scale (0–3, increasing severity). Extravasation severity distributions were similar between groups: no injury (67.5% vs. 70.1%), mild injury (30.1% vs. 27.6%) and moderate injury (2.4% vs. 2.3%) in the biosensor and standard care groups, respectively. There were no severe injuries in either group. There was no statistically significant difference observed between groups in extravasation severity (POR = 1.13; 95% CI: 0.59, 2.15; p = 0.71) (Table 3). Findings were similar in the per protocol analysis, with no injury (66.7% vs. 70.1%), mild injury (30.7% vs. 30.7%) and moderate injury (2.7% vs. 2.3%) in the biosensor and standard groups, respectively (POR = 1.17; 95% CI: 0.61, 2.27; p = 0.64).

Secondary Outcomes

There was not strong observed association between group allocation and any difference in extravasation volume (MD = −0.2 mL; 95% CI: −1.2, 0.7), number of PVADs used (MD = −0.1; 95% CI: −0.5, 0.4) or dwell time (MD = −0.1 days; 95% CI: −0.9, 0.6).

Four patients only (two in each group) had their extravasation injury formally dressed with a silver‐containing dressing (all four assessed as moderate injuries, Cincinnati Harm Scale 3). Each Biosensor has a cost of $AUD80.00 (excluding additional staff time for application) in addition to Standard care. The one‐off cost of each ivWatch Model 400 Patient Monitor, is $AUD5500.00. The costs of treating a major extravasation injury are in dressings, possible surgery and subsequent scar management. No patient required more than one dressing to their wound, as all were healed without sequelae at change of first dressing. This and the absence of major injury, with its predicted attendant long‐term sequelae, meant a full economic evaluation was futile and therefore abandoned. The system has one reusable component (the Monitor) and one single‐use component (the Sensor applied to the patient). Consequently, the Biosensor is considered to increase healthcare costs with no additional benefit in terms of reduced extravasation injury severity in this setting.

Similarly, given the paucity of injuries and subsequent requirement for treatment, quality of life analysis was futile and therefore not performed.

Acceptability and Feasibility Outcomes

Of families approached for the trial, 182 agreed to participate while 52 declined (22.2% decline rate). Protocol violations are outlined in the above tables.

Thirteen nurses completed the acceptability survey in the early part of the study, and 12 towards the end of recruitment (see Table S2). Median acceptability scores (11‐point numeric rating scale) of the biosensor at early and late stages of the study were 9 (IQR 8–10) and 9 (IQR 7–10) respectively. Most nursing staff completing the survey reported the biosensor ‘never’ or ‘rarely’ influenced their ability to do PVAD site checks.

Discussion

In this single‐centre RCT, adding continuous biosensor monitoring to standard clinical observation did not reduce the severity of PVAD‐related extravasation injuries in children in PICU. The study did demonstrate the technology can be feasibly integrated into routine PICU care, and it provides insights into the contexts in which such monitoring may offer clinical benefit.

This is the first RCT evaluating the efficacy of a biosensor using visible and near‐infrared light (ivWatch), in comparison to standard care (clinical observation alone), to decrease significant paediatric extravasation injuries. A major strength of the study is its pragmatic design, conducted in a busy quaternary PICU, demonstrating feasibility in a real‐world, high acuity environment. The biosensor was found to be acceptable to bedside nursing staff, an essential consideration for the integration of novel technologies into routine clinical care. The extravasation event rate observed aligned with estimates used in the power calculation, supporting the validity of recruitment and trial procedures.

Several factors may explain these findings. The study was conducted in a quaternary children's hospital where institutional awareness of PVAD extravasation risk is high, prompting a multi‐pronged effort to decrease this healthcare associated injury, including proactive management of PVADs through the introduction of a care bundle [33]. In addition, nurse to patient ratios in Australian PICUs (typically 1:1) may allow more time to monitor skin perfusion, and we cannot rule out device‐related benefit in settings with different standard care. Frequent use of central venous access in the intensive care environment may have reduced PVAD exposure in terms of number, concentration and duration of medium to high‐risk medications. While PVADs remain in use in PICU, they may be less commonly used as a primary intravascular device, particularly for vesicant medications. The expected event rate, but lower than expected moderate and severe injury rates, imply that extravasation was detected rapidly following its onset and before enough time had elapsed for a volume sufficient to cause more severe injury to extravasate into tissue. In an environment where children are less likely to have central venous access, but still require intravenous medications, a biosensor may still have utility, although robust supporting data are lacking. To explore this effectiveness in non‐PICU settings, a multi‐site randomised study in general inpatient wards and neonatal intensive care environments is underway [34].

It is reasonable to assume increased movement of the PVAD will decrease its lifespan and increase extravasation risk. Dressings for PVADs continue to evolve, and a combination of simple and more complex securement techniques was used. No child in this study had tissue adhesive as a part of their PVAD dressing. There are insufficient data from this study to make a dressing recommendation which might mitigate extravasation risk.

Protocol violation rates were comparable with other randomised controlled trials in intensive care settings, indicating overall high feasibility of the intervention. However, Research Nurse coverage, particularly towards the end of the COVID‐19 pandemic, was less extensive than anticipated, resulting in some missed opportunities for eligibility assessment, recruitment, randomisation, or allocation.

Feasibility and acceptability are relevant to families as well as to clinicians. Of the families approached, 182 agreed to participate while 52 declined (22.2% decline rate). The consent rate was comparable to other randomised controlled trials in critically ill children in Australia, indicating most parents are willing to participate in a trial including a biosensor in this setting [35, 36, 37]. We take this as an indication of a collaborative, rather than a coercive, approach to research at a time of high stress and uncertainty for families. Survey‐based staff assessment of acceptability was limited by low response rates. In those staff who did complete the survey, there was a high degree of acceptability, but firm conclusions cannot be drawn from such low numbers.

Limitations

Several limitations must be acknowledged. The study was conducted in a large single site in Australia with 1:1 nursing care, and findings may not be generalisable to all settings. The incidence of extravasation was similar to the expected rate but the rate of moderate to severe injury was less than expected, possibly due to the high rate of central venous access in this cohort which reduced peripheral administration of vesicant medications. Future studies could focus on populations with higher peripheral vesicant exposure, such as general paediatric wards or neonatal units, to better capture clinically meaningful events.

Some losses to follow‐up and deviations from protocol occurred, reflecting the challenges of conducting research in a 24/7 intensive care environment with limited research staff coverage, particularly outside regular hours. The COVID pandemic may also have contributed to this. Weekend and overnight admissions may have different characteristics to the more elective, planned daytime admissions captured in this study, introducing potential bias. We also excluded patients and families who had difficulty communicating in English, as we did not have access to interpreter services at all hours. We were not comfortable with this exclusion, as it further contributes to known disparities in access to healthcare [38].

Standardisation of PVAD insertion (site, device size [length and gauge] or inserter expertise) was not feasible, which may have contributed to variability in outcomes. Prioritisation of other cares may have meant the sickest children, on the most medications, were the least likely to have their PVAD sites checked regularly as other cares took priority. This study is only applicable to SPC, as these were used in all but one of the patients in the trial. Extrapolation to other types of PVAD (LPC and/or MC) or to central venous access devices is not appropriate.

Allocation concealment to clinical staff was not possible since the biosensor is visible and must be managed. This limitation was mitigated by blinding the outcome assessors (burns surgeons and nurses assigned extravasation severity) and assigning outcomes according to standardised definitions. The data analysis was performed by the statistician using a masked dataset, ensuring unbiased evaluation of extravasation severity.

Some of our planned analyses, such as quality of life and economic outcomes, could not be meaningfully assessed due to the rapid healing of all extravasation injuries and the absence of severe injuries.

This study was performed in a high‐resource quaternary PICU with high nurse‐to‐patient ratios and frequent use of central venous access devices. These factors limit the generalisability of this study to other settings, such as general, paediatric wards, regional hospitals or adult populations, where peripheral administration of vesicant medications is more common and staffing ratios lower, and extravasation rates and injury severity may differ. Future studies should consider multi‐centre designs including settings with varying staffing levels, patient acuity and vascular access practices to enhance external validity [34].

The approach to extravasation prevention, surveillance, management varies across and within hospitals. Surveys of practice report only 65% of Australian and New Zealand neonatal intensive care units have written prevention and recognition policies for extravasation injuries [11, 39]. More than three‐quarters of units reported a policy for regular nursing observations and PVAD site visibility, as originally described by Millan [11, 40]. Current best practice recommendations for vesicant medication administration are for site monitoring every 5–10 min [15]. It is difficult to imagine this being done consistently in all environments. If the event rate and sequelae are higher in a particular environment, then a biosensor may have utility.

Conclusion

In this PICU population the use of a tissue‐infiltration biosensor (ivWatch) did not reduce the incidence or severity of extravasation injuries compared to standard care. The study nonetheless demonstrated the feasibility and staff acceptability of biosensor use in a busy PICU environment. These findings provide a foundation for future research in settings with higher rates of peripheral vesicant administration, where the technology may have greater clinical impact. We are currently trialling the same device in neonatal intensive care and general paediatric wards in a multicentre trial to see if differences in patients, nursing ratios and CVAD use may impact the utility of a biosensor in reducing the severity of extravasation injuries [34].
