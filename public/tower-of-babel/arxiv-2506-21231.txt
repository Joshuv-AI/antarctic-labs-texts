Block Coordinate Descent Network Simplex for Optimal Transport
Lingrui Li, Nobuo Yamashita

Abstract
We propose the Block Coordinate Descent Network Simplex (BCDNS) method for solving large-scale discrete Optimal Transport (OT) problems. BCDNS integrates the Network Simplex (NS) algorithm with a block coordinate descent (BCD) strategy, decomposing the full problem into smaller subproblems per iteration and reusing basis variables to ensure feasibility. We prove that BCDNS terminates in a finite number of iterations with an exact optimal solution, and we characterize its per-iteration complexity as O ⁡ ( s ​ N ) O(sN) , where s ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) s\in(0,1) is a user-defined parameter and N N is the total number of variables. Numerical experiments demonstrate that BCDNS matches the classical NS method in solution accuracy, reduces memory footprint compared to Sinkhorn algorithm, achieves speed-ups of up to tens of times over the classical NS method, and exhibits runtime comparable to high-precision Sinkhorn algorithm.



Keywords:
Optimal Transport, Network Simplex, Block Coordinate Descent, Sinkhorn, Wasserstein Distance

1.  Introduction

The Optimal Transport (OT) problem is to find the most efficient way to
transport resources between a finite number of locations while minimizing transportation costs. It is rooted in mathematical optimization and has a rich history dating back to the 18th century. Gaspard Monge
[
11
]
first proposed it in 1781. Then in the mid-20th century, Leonid Kantorovich
[
8
]
extended Monge’s framework to encompass continuous distributions, reformulating it as a linear programming model and significantly broadening its theoretical foundation and practical applications. In this thesis, we focus specifically on solving the discrete OT problem.

The OT problem has found applications in various disciplines. In machine learning, the OT problem has been used for tasks such as domain adaptation
[
3
]
and generative modeling
[
2
]
. In economics, the OT problem has provided insights into resource allocation, market equilibrium, and pricing mechanisms
[
6
]
. In biology, the OT problem has been applied to analyze genetic expression patterns
[
12
]
.

[

. In geophysics and signal processing, the OT problem has been employed for seismic waveform comparison and inversion.
[

.

Several methods have been developed to solve the OT problem. Each method has distinct strengths and limitations.
The Hungarian method is a classical approach for solving linear assignment problems and can also be applied to OT problems
[
9
]
. It achieves exact optimal solutions by constructing a cost matrix and iteratively reducing reducing dimensions of the matrix. However, its time complexity is
O(N^{2})
, where
N
is the number of variables in OT problem, and hence it is not applicable for the large-scale OT problem.
The Sinkhorn algorithm is a popular method for the large-scale OT problem due to its computational efficiency
[
4
]
. It solves the OT problems with regularization of entropy, which is an approximation of the OT problem. Therefore it provides approximate solutions of the OT problem, and it may not be suitable for applications that require exact solutions. In recent years, many improvements based on the Sinkhorn algorithm have been proposed, such as the Greenkhorn algorithm
[
altschuler2017Near
]
and the Adaptive Primal–Dual Accelerated Gradient Descent (APDAGD) algorithm
[
dvurechensky2018Computational
]
. However, these methods still operate within the Sinkhorn framework and do not overcome the limitation of producing only approximate OT solutions.
In contrast, the Network Simplex (NS) method, a specialized variant of the simplex method, is well known to yield exact solutions. Michael D. Grigoriadis
[
7
]
reported that the NS method is reliable among several algorithms. However, the NS method is not a polynomial time algorithm and it takes
O(N)
time in each iteration, which leads to slow computational performance when applied to large-scale problems.
We note that the above three methods needs at least
O(N)
space complexity.
To overcome the difficulty of large space complexity for the OT problem, Yue Xie et al.
[
16
]
proposed the Block Coordinate Descent (BCD) method for solving the OT problem.
Their method constructs a small subproblem by randomly selecting variables based on predefined rules and solves it in each iteration. Since the subproblem is small, the BCD method does not require the O(N) space complexity. However, since the BCD method proposed by Yue Xie et al. adapts the random variable selection, it is not guaranteed to find an optimal solution within a finite number of iterations. Moreover, it sometimes takes a large number of iterations to get a reasonable approximate solution.

The motivation behind this research lies in the growing demand for efficient methods capable of solving large-scale OT problems with highly accurate solutions. As data sizes continue to grow, we recognize the increasing importance of solving large-scale OT problems with both speed and accuracy. To address these challenges, we aim to develop a method that enables fast computation of exact solutions for large-scale OT problems.

To achieve the goal of efficiently solving large-scale OT problems, we introduce the idea of the BCD method. The BCD method divides a large-scale OT problem into smaller subproblems, enabling a reduction in computational complexity. Although the BCD method is generally considered slow when exact solutions are required, we observe that the optimal solutions of the OT problem are typically sparse—due to the relatively small number of equality constraints compared to the number of variables—which significantly improves the efficiency of BCD. By employing the NS method to solve the subproblems, we guarantee that the final solution is also exact.

In this paper, we propose the
Block Coordinate Descent Network Simplex (BCDNS)
method, a novel algorithm that integrates the BCD and NS methods to achieve computational efficiency and solution accuracy. Our approach reduces computational complexity by decomposing large-scale OT problems into smaller subproblems, while the NS method ensures that each subproblem is solved with precision. Numerical experiments demonstrate significant performance gains of the BCDNS method over traditional approaches, addressing key limitations of existing methods.

In the BCD method, the selection of the block coordinate is crucial. Compared to existing BCD method
[
16
]
that randomly selects variables based on matrix properties, we propose that the BCDNS method first selects the basis variables from the previous subproblem as the initial solution for the next subproblem. This strategy ensures that the subproblem always has a feasible and an optimal solution. Moreover, by integrating the principles of the NS method, we ensure that the BCDNS method converges to the exact optimal solution within a finite number of computations.
Furthermore, we categorize variables into two groups based on their likelihood of becoming optimal basis variables. This targeted selection process prioritizes variables with a higher probability of being optimal basis variables, thereby improving the efficiency of finding the optimal solution.

The outline of this paper is as follows. In Section 2, we introduce the foundational concepts of the OT problem and review several existing methods for solving it. In Section 3, we describe the core principles of the proposed BCDNS method. We first present its prototype along with the corresponding theoretical proofs. Then we develop a concrete implementation based on the prototype. Section 4 reports numerical experiments, including parameter estimation for the BCDNS method and a comparative performance analysis against existing approaches. Finally, Section 5 concludes the paper by summarizing the key contributions and discussing potential directions for future research.

2.  Discrete Optimal Transport

We consider a discrete OT problem
[
15
]
between
n
supply locations and
m
demand locations. Let
\{1,2,\dots,n\}
denote the index set of supply points, and
\{1,2,\dots,m\}
denote that of demand points.

Each supply point
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
provides a non-negative amount of mass
p_{i}\geq 0
, and each demand point
j\in\{1,\dots,m\}
requires an amount
q_{j}\geq 0
. The total supply and demand are assumed to be balanced:

\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}q_{j}.

Let
C=\{c_{ij}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
be the cost matrix, where each entry
c_{ij}\geq 0
denotes the cost of transporting one unit of mass from supply point
i
to demand point
j
. A transport plan is represented by a matrix
X=\{x_{ij}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
, where
x_{ij}
is the amount of mass transported from
i
to
j
.

The discrete OT problem can then be formulated as the following linear program:

\displaystyle\min_{X\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n\times m}}\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}c_{ij}x_{ij}

(1)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}\quad

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{m}x_{ij}=p_{i},\quad\forall i=1,\dots,n,

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{ij}=q_{j},\quad\forall j=1,\dots,m,

\displaystyle x_{ij}\geq 0,\quad\forall i=1,\dots,n,\;j=1,\dots,m.

To express this in standard matrix-vector form, we define the following notation. Let
N=nm
be the total number of transport variables, and let
M=n+m
be the number of equality constraints. We vectorize the matrix
X
into a column vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
by stacking its columns, i.e.,

\mathbf{x}=\operatorname{vec}(X)=[x_{11},x_{21},\dots,x_{n1},x_{12},\dots,x_{nm}]^{\top}.

We also define:

•

the cost vector
\mathbf{c}=\operatorname{vec}(C)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
,

•

the supply vector
\mathbf{p}=[p_{1},\dots,p_{n}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

•

the demand vector
\mathbf{q}=[q_{1},\dots,q_{m}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,

•

the constraint vector
\mathbf{b}=[\mathbf{p}^{\top},\,-\mathbf{q}^{\top}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
.

•

The constraint matrix

A=\begin{bmatrix}I_{n}&I_{n}&\cdots&I_{n}\\
-1_{n}^{\top}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&-1_{n}^{\top}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&-1_{n}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N},

where
I_{n}
denotes the
n\times n
identity matrix, and
1_{n}
is an
n
-dimensional column vector of ones.

With this notation, we can rewrite the OT problem as:

\displaystyle\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\quad

\displaystyle\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{x}

(2)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}\quad

\displaystyle A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b},

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}\geq 0.

3.  Existing Methods for Optimal Transport

In this section, we review several representative algorithms for solving the discrete OT problem. These include the classical
NS
method, the
Sinkhorn algorithm
based on entropy regularization which is widely used for large-scale problems, and the recently proposed
BCD
method.

3.1
Network Simplex Method for Optimal Transport

As shown in Appendix
A
, the constraint matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N}
in the OT problem has rank
M-1
. This allows us to partition the set of variable indices
\mathcal{N}=\{1,\dots,N\}
into two subsets: a basis index set
\mathcal{B}\subseteq\mathcal{N}
with
|\mathcal{B}|=M-1
, and its complement
\mathcal{N}\setminus\mathcal{B}
. The columns of
A
corresponding to
\mathcal{B}
, denoted
A_{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times(M-1)}
, form a full-rank submatrix. The basic variables
\mathbf{x}_{B}
can then be uniquely determined by the system
A_{B}\mathbf{x}_{B}=\mathbf{b}
, while the non-basic variables
\mathbf{x}_{N}
are fixed at zero. A basic feasible solution further satisfies the non-negativity constraint
\mathbf{x}_{B}\geq 0
.

To determine whether a given basic solution is optimal, we examine its reduced-cost vector. Define the dual potential vector
\boldsymbol{\pi}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}
via the dual feasibility condition
\boldsymbol{\pi}=(A_{B}^{\top})^{-1}\mathbf{c}_{B}
, where
\mathbf{c}_{B}
is the cost subvector corresponding to the basis
B
. Then, for any index
k\in N\setminus B
, the reduced cost is computed as
r_{k}=c_{k}-\boldsymbol{\pi}^{\top}A_{k}
. Due to the structure of the OT problem, each column
A_{ij}
has exactly one
+1
and one
-1
, so the reduced cost simplifies to the closed-form expression

r_{ij}=c_{ij}-(\pi_{i}-\pi_{n+j}).

Optimality of the current basic solution is certified if all reduced costs are non-negative, i.e.,
r_{ij}\geq 0
for all
i,j
. If a variable
x_{i^{*}j^{*}}
has
r_{i^{*}j^{*}}<0
, it indicates a direction of descent in the objective, and that variable is introduced into the basis.

To perform a pivot, we add the index
(i^{*},j^{*})
to the basis
B
, inducing a unique cycle in the associated flow graph. Then determine the step size
\Delta
by the minimum flow along the negative edges of the cycle, ensuring feasibility. Subsequently, update the flow:
x_{i^{*}j^{*}}\leftarrow\Delta
, and for each edge
(u,v)
along the cycle, the value of
x_{uv}
is incremented or decremented by
\Delta
depending on the cycle direction. Finally, the variable that reaches zero and remove it from the basis, completing the pivot step
[
14
]
.

Each iteration of the NS method involves computing the dual potential vector
\boldsymbol{\pi}
in
O(M)
time, evaluating all reduced costs in
O(N)
, and performing pivoting operations in
O(M)
. Since each pivot strictly improves the objective function by at least
-r_{i^{*}j^{*}}>0
, and the number of possible basic feasible solutions is finite, the NS method terminates in a finite number of iterations.

3.2
Block Coordinate Descent Method for Optimal Transport

In this subsection we introduce the BCD method for the OT problem in the LP form (
2
).

The BCD method iteratively selects a subset of coordinates
\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathcal{N}=\{1,2,\dots,N\}
. For any feasible solution
\mathbf{x}
, we define a subproblem of the BCD method

D(\mathbf{x};\mathcal{H})\triangleq\arg\min_{\mathbf{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left\{\mathbf{c}^{T}(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{d})\mid\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{d}\geq 0,\;A\mathbf{d}=0,\;d_{i}=0,\;\forall i\in\mathcal{N}\backslash\mathcal{H}\right\}.

(3)

q(\mathbf{x};\mathcal{H})\triangleq\min_{\mathbf{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\left\{\mathbf{c}^{T}(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{d})\mid\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{d}\geq 0,\;A\mathbf{d}=0,\;d_{i}=0,\;\forall i\in\mathcal{N}\backslash\mathcal{H}\right\}.

(4)

Here,
D(\mathbf{x};\mathcal{H})
represents the set of optimal solutions for the subproblem with
\mathcal{H}
, and
q(\mathbf{x};\mathcal{H})
denotes the optimal objective value corresponding to
d\in D(\mathbf{x};\mathcal{H})

[
13
]
.

The iterative BCD algorithm proceeds as follows:

•

Initialization
: Start with a feasible solution
\mathbf{x_{0}}
and an initial block
\mathcal{H}_{0}\subseteq\{1,\dots,N\}
.

•

Subproblem Optimization
: For the current solution
\mathbf{x^{k}}
, solve the subproblem restricted to the working set
\mathcal{H}_{k}
to find the descent direction
\mathbf{d^{k}}\in D(\mathbf{x^{k}};\mathcal{H}_{k})
. Update the solution as:

\mathbf{x^{k+1}}=\mathbf{x^{k}}+\mathbf{d^{k}}.

•

Optimality Check
: If optimality is achieved, terminate the algorithm. Else adjust the block
\mathcal{H}_{k}
for the next iteration using predefined rules or adaptive heuristics.

The existing BCD method
[
16
]
, randomly selects block coordinates based on certain properties of specific matrices.
In
[
16
]
, the authors propose the Accelerated Random Block Coordinate Descent algorithm and establish the following convergence theorem.

Theorem 1
.

The gap between the current objective function value and the optimal objective function value converges with rate
1-v
, i.e.,

\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{x}^{k+1}-\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{x}^{*}\leq(1-v)(\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{x}^{k}-\mathbf{c}^{\top}\mathbf{x}^{*}).

Here,
v
satisfies

v\geq\zeta\frac{n(\tilde{p}-2)}{(n^{2}-3)(n!)^{2}},

where
\zeta
is a predefined probability parameter in the range
(0,1)
, and
\tilde{p}
is a parameter representing the properties of the matrix in the range
[3,n]
.

These approaches provide a simple mechanism for constructing subproblems by selecting block coordinates. However, when the size n of the OT problems is large,
v
becomes extremely small, which leads to slow convergence.
Furthermore, these approaches do not account for the sparsity of the solution of the OT problem. Many variables that ultimately take zero values continue to be included in the block coordinates at each iteration. Moreover, these approaches have an inherent limitation: they cannot guarantee convergence to the optimal solution within a finite number of iterations. This limitation arises from the possibility of extreme cases where the randomly selected block of coordinates rare includes the optimal basis variables.

3.3
Sinkhorn Algorithm for Optimal Transport

Instead of solving the classical OT problem defined in (1), the Sinkhorn algorithm
[
4
]
introduces an entropy regularization term

\min_{X\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n\times m}}\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}c_{ij}x_{ij}-\varepsilon H(X),

where
\varepsilon>0
is the regularization parameter, and
H(X)
is the entropy function defined as

H(X)=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}x_{ij}\log x_{ij}.

The parameter
\varepsilon
controls the strength of the entropy regularization, balancing the trade-off between the classical transport cost and the entropy of the transport plan.

Introduce Lagrange multipliers
\alpha=(\alpha_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
and
\beta=(\beta_{j})_{j=1}^{m}
for the supply and demand constraints, respectively. The Lagrangian function of the regularized OT problem is

\mathcal{L}(X,\alpha,\beta)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}c_{ij}x_{ij}-\varepsilon H(X)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}\left(p_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{m}x_{ij}\right)+\sum_{j=1}^{m}\beta_{j}\left(q_{j}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{ij}\right).

Taking the partial derivative of
\mathcal{L}
with respect to
x_{ij}
and setting it to zero, we obtain the optimality condition

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial x_{ij}}=c_{ij}+\varepsilon(\log x_{ij}+1)-\alpha_{i}-\beta_{j}=0.

Solving for
x_{ij}
, we find

x_{ij}=\exp\left(\frac{\alpha_{i}+\beta_{j}-c_{ij}}{\varepsilon}-1\right).

Let
u_{i}=e^{\alpha_{i}/\varepsilon}
and
v_{j}=e^{\beta_{j}/\varepsilon}
, then the optimal transport plan can be expressed as

x_{ij}=u_{i}e^{-c_{ij}/\varepsilon}v_{j}.

Define
K=e^{-C/\varepsilon}
as the matrix obtained by element-wise exponentiating the cost matrix
C=\{c_{ij}\}
. The transport plan
X
can then be written as:

X=\text{diag}(\mathbf{u})K\text{diag}(\mathbf{v}),

where
\text{diag}(\mathbf{u})
and
\text{diag}(\mathbf{v})
are diagonal matrices whose diagonal entries are the elements of the vectors
\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{v}
, respectively.

From the equality constraints
X\mathbf{1}_{m}=\mathbf{p}
and
X^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{n}=\mathbf{q}
, we derive the following update rules

\mathbf{u}^{(t+1)}=\frac{\mathbf{p}}{K\mathbf{v}^{(t)}},

\mathbf{v}^{(t+1)}=\frac{\mathbf{q}}{K^{\top}\mathbf{u}^{(t+1)}}.

Here, the division is performed element-wise.

The Sinkhorn algorithm terminates when the change in the scaling factors
\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{v}
becomes sufficiently small

\|\mathbf{u}^{(t)}-\mathbf{u}^{(t-1)}\|<\delta,\quad\|\mathbf{v}^{(t)}-\mathbf{v}^{(t-1)}\|<\delta,

where
\delta>0
is a small predefined tolerance.

The regularization parameter
\varepsilon
introduces a smoothing effect in the optimal transport problem. As
\varepsilon
decreases, the solution becomes closer to the exact OT plan, but the algorithm may converge more slowly. Conversely, larger values of
\varepsilon
result in faster computations, but with a higher approximation error in the transport plan.

4.  Block Coordinate Network Simplex Methods with Basis Variable Succession

The goal of the NS method is to identify the optimal basis variables through iterative pivoting. While each NS iteration efficiently updates the basis structure, computing reduced costs for all variables incurs a cost of
O(N)
per iteration.

To reduce this per-iteration cost, we adopt the BCD strategy: at each iteration, we restrict attention to a selected subset
\mathcal{H}_{k}\subseteq\mathcal{N}
of variables and solve a corresponding subproblem using the NS method.

The OT problem is particularly well-suited to such block decomposition. In general LPs with
M
constraints, computing the reduced cost for a single variable requires
O(M)
operations. However, due to the special network structure of the OT problem, each reduced cost can be computed in
O(1)
time via the relation

r_{ij}=c_{ij}-(\pi_{i}-\pi_{n+j}),

We now summarize the notation and assumptions used throughout this section:

•

\mathbf{x}^{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
: the basic feasible solution at iteration
k
,

•

\mathbf{x}^{*}
:the optimal solution to the full OT problem (
2
),

•

\mathcal{B}_{k}\subseteq\{1,\dots,N\}
: the set of basis indices at iteration
k
,

•

\mathcal{B}^{\ast}\subseteq\{1,\dots,N\}
: the basis of a globally optimal solution,

•

\mathcal{H}_{k}\subseteq\{1,\dots,N\}
: the block of variable indices selected at iteration
k
,

•

\bar{c}^{k}:=\bar{c}(\mathbf{x}^{k},\mathcal{B}_{k})
: the reduced cost vector at
\mathbf{x}^{k}
.

4.1
Basis Variables Succession

In BCD schemes for the OT problem, the convergence behaviour hinges on the construction of the working set
\mathcal{H}_{k}
at each iteration
k
.

The following theorem identifies the precise conditions under which a restricted NS step on a block
\mathcal{H}_{k}
yields a globally optimal solution. It also shows when global optimality cannot be achieved.

Theorem 2
.

Apply the network–simplex method to the subproblem restricted to
\mathcal{H}_{k}
, and set

d^{k}\in\mathcal{D}\bigl(\mathbf{x}^{k};\mathcal{H}_{k}\bigr),\qquad\mathbf{x}^{k+1}=\mathbf{x}^{k}+d^{k}.

Then:

(i)

If
\mathcal{B}_{k}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{k}
and

\mathcal{B}^{\ast}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{k}
, the iterate

\mathbf{x}^{k+1}
is globally optimal.

(ii)

If
\mathcal{B}_{k}\not\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{k}
, then—even when

\mathcal{B}^{\ast}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{k}
—the iterate

\mathbf{x}^{k+1}
cannot be globally optimal.

Proof.

(i)

Because
\mathcal{B}_{k}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{k}
, the NS subproblem starts
with basis
\mathcal{B}_{k}
. Pivoting within
\mathcal{H}_{k}
yields a
basis
\widehat{\mathcal{B}}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{k}
satisfying

\bar{c}\bigl(\mathbf{x}^{k+1},\widehat{\mathcal{B}}\bigr)_{i}\;\geq\;0,\quad\forall\,i\in\mathcal{H}_{k}.

(5)

Since
\mathcal{B}^{\ast}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{k}
, the subproblem contains an
optimal basis of the full problem; NS therefore stops only when no negative
reduced costs remain in
\mathcal{H}_{k}
.
As dual variables depend solely on the basis,

\widehat{\mathcal{B}}\cap\mathcal{H}_{k}=\mathcal{B}^{\ast},

so condition (
5
) extends to all indices
i=1,\dots,N
:

\bar{c}\bigl(\mathbf{x}^{k+1},\widehat{\mathcal{B}}\bigr)_{i}\;\geq\;0,\quad\forall\,i.

By complementary slackness,
\mathbf{x}^{k+1}
is globally optimal.

(ii)

Assume
j\in\mathcal{B}_{k}\setminus\mathcal{H}_{k}
with

\mathbf{x}^{k}_{j}>0
. Because
j\notin\mathcal{H}_{k}
, the subproblem
cannot pivot
j
or alter its value, hence

\mathbf{x}^{k+1}_{j}=\mathbf{x}^{k}_{j}>0.

In any optimal OT solution all non–basic variables are zero.
Since
j\notin\mathcal{B}^{\ast}
(because

\mathcal{B}^{\ast}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{k}
), optimality would require

x^{\ast}_{j}=0
, contradicting
\mathbf{x}^{k+1}_{j}>0
.
Therefore
\mathbf{x}^{k+1}
cannot be optimal.
∎

The above analysis motivates a practical strategy, which we call
basis variable succession
: at each iteration, we selecte the block
\mathcal{H}_{k}
includes the basis variables
\mathcal{B}_{k}
obtained from the previous subproblem. This design contrasts with existing BCD approaches
[
16
]
that select blocks randomly.

we can solve the subproblem in iteration
k
with complexity
O(L_{k}|\mathcal{H}_{k}|)
, which is significantly lower than the
O(LN)
complexity of solving the full problem directly, where
L_{k}
and
L
denote the number of NS iterations for the subproblem and the full problem, respectively.

This strategy offers two practical advantages:

1.

The subproblem always includes all constraints and at least
M-1
variables, ensuring feasibility.

2.

Reusing the previous basis variables as the initialization eliminates the need for recomputing a starting basis in the NS method.

4.2
A Prototype of Block Coordinate Descent Network Simplex Methods and its Finite Termination

Following the strategy of §4.1, choose a block

\mathcal{H}_{k}\supseteq\mathcal{B}_{k},\qquad\lvert\mathcal{H}_{k}\rvert\geq M-1,

and split it into

\mathcal{B}_{k},\qquad\mathcal{I}_{k}=\mathcal{H}_{k}\setminus\mathcal{B}_{k}.

If
\mathcal{I}_{k}
is picked arbitrarily, the subproblem

\mathcal{D}\!\bigl(\mathbf{x}^{k};\mathcal{H}_{k}\bigr)

might admit only the zero direction, yielding the same basis

\mathcal{B}_{k+1}=\mathcal{B}_{k}
and
\mathbf{x}^{k}=\mathbf{x}^{k+1}
.

Define the index set of negative reduced costs

\mathcal{N}_{k}\;=\;\bigl\{\,\ell\in\{1,\dots,nm\}:\;\bar{c}^{\,k}_{\ell}<0\bigr\}.

From iteration
k
on we impose the rule

\mathcal{I}_{k}\cap\mathcal{N}_{k}\;\neq\;\varnothing.

(6)

If
\mathcal{N}_{k}=\varnothing
, then

\bar{c}^{\,k}\geq 0
and, by complementary slackness,

\mathbf{x}^{k}
is already globally optimal.

Under condition (
6
) each subproblem produces a strictly improving search direction, ensuring monotone decrease of the objective value. The next theorem establishes finite termination and global optimality of the resulting sequence
\{\mathbf{x}^{k}\}
.

Theorem 3
.

The proposed prototype of BCDNS method satisfies the following properties:

1.

Monotonicity and Convergence:
The objective function decreases monotonically at each iteration, and the method converges to the global optimal solution.

2.

Finite Termination:
The method terminates within a finite number of iterations.

Proof of Part 1.

Monotonicity:
For any iteration
k
, based on the selection rule for
\mathcal{I}_{k}
, there exists at least one
l\in\mathcal{I}_{k}
such that the reduced cost of
x_{l}
is negative. By the properties of the reduced cost, adding
x_{l}
to the basis variables decreases the total cost, i.e.,

q(\mathbf{x}^{k};\mathcal{B}_{k}\cup\{l\})<q(\mathbf{x}^{k};\mathcal{B}_{k}).

where
q(\mathbf{x}^{k};\mathcal{I}_{k})
is defined by (
4
) as the optimal value of the subproblem associated with the index set
\mathcal{I}_{k}
. Since
l\in\mathcal{I}_{k}
, it follows that:

q(\mathbf{x}^{k};\mathcal{H}_{k})=q(\mathbf{x}^{k};\mathcal{B}_{k}\cup\mathcal{I}_{k})\leq q(\mathbf{x}^{k};\mathcal{B}_{k}\cup\{l\})<q(\mathbf{x}^{k};\mathcal{B}_{k}).

Furthermore, since
\mathcal{J}_{k}
is the optimal basis variables of the previous subproblem, we have:

q(\mathbf{x}^{k};\mathcal{B}_{k})=q(\mathbf{x}^{k-1};\mathcal{H}_{k-1}).

Combining the above, it follows that:

q(\mathbf{x}^{k};\mathcal{H}_{k})<q(\mathbf{x}^{k-1};\mathcal{H}_{k-1}).

Thus, the cost function decreases monotonically at each iteration.

Global Optimality:
After solving each subproblem
\mathcal{D}(\mathbf{x}^{k};\mathcal{H}_{k})
, we compute the reduced costs of all variables. If all reduced costs satisfy
r_{ij}\geq 0
, the algorithm terminates; otherwise, the next iteration begins. By the properties of the reduced cost, this condition guarantees that the final solution is globally optimal.
∎

Proof of Part 2.

The total number of possible combinations of the basis variables among
N
variables is at most
\binom{N}{M-1}
. Based on the selection rule, in each iteration, the set
\mathcal{I}_{k}
always contains at least one variable with a negative reduced cost. Denote the variable with the smallest reduced cost as
x_{l}
. When solving the subproblem using the NS method,
x_{l}
will be added to the basis variables, while one of the current basis variables
\mathcal{B}_{k}
will leave the basis. This implies that each iteration examines at least one new combination of the basis variables. Consequently, after at most
\binom{N}{M-1}
iterations, all possible combinations of the basis variables will have been examined. Therefore, the algorithm is guaranteed to terminate within a finite number of iterations.
∎

4.3
Grouped Selection Block Coordinate Descent Network Simplex method

Since an optimal solution to the OT problem is typically sparse, most variables take value zero and do not belong to the optimal basis. Consequently, selecting
\mathcal{I}_{k}
uniformly at random often results in the inclusion of irrelevant variables, slowing convergence.

To address this issue, we propose a grouped block coordinate selection strategy that partitions the variable index set
\{1,\dots,N\}
into two disjoint subsets:

•

\mathcal{R}
: indices of variables deemed likely to enter the optimal basis
\mathcal{B}^{*}
,

•

\mathcal{S}
: indices of variables with low likelihood of being part of the optimal basis
\mathcal{B}^{*}
.

Let
0<s<t\leq 1
be fixed sampling parameters. At each iteration, the reduced costs are computed for a randomly selected subset of size
tN
from
\mathcal{R}
, together with a small subset from
\mathcal{S}
for exploration. Among these, we select the
sN
variables with the smallest reduced costs to form the block
\mathcal{I}_{k}
, as these variables are most promising for descent directions.

If all selected variables have non-negative reduced costs, the algorithm infers that none are useful for further improvement and moves them to set
\mathcal{S}
. The selection process is then repeated until condition (
6
) is satisfied.

This strategy guides the search toward structurally relevant directions and improves computational efficiency. Based on this mechanism, we introduce the
Grouped Selection Block Coordinate Descent Network Simplex (GS-BCDNS)
method. The full algorithm is presented below.

for

6.  Conclusion

In this paper, we review the existing methods for solving the OT problems, including the NS method, the BCD method, and the Sinkhorn algorithm. Then we propose the BCDNS methods and explain thier theoretical foundations and the basis variable succession strategy. Furthermore, we provide a detailed algorithmic framework and established theoretical guarantees for finite termination and convergence to the exact optimal solution.

Extensive numerical experiments demonstrate that the BCDNS method achieves superior performance in terms of runtime and computational cost compared to the NS method. While the BCDNS method converges slightly slower than the Sinkhorn algorithm with large values of
\epsilon
, it has the advantage of guaranteeing convergence to an exact solution. Moreover, compared to the Sinkhorn algorithm with small values of
\epsilon
, the BCDNS method exhibits even faster convergence. This makes the BCDNS method particularly valuable for large-scale OT problems that require exact solutions.

Future work could explore several directions. First, improving the selection strategy for block coordinates may further enhance convergence speed. Second, extending the BCDNS method to more general network flow problems could broaden its applicability.
