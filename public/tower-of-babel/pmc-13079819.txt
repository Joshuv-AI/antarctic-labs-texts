Simultaneous Pi2 pulsation detected by CSES-01, Swarm, RBSP and Arase satellites
Authors: Essam Ghamry, Kazuhiro Yamamoto, Dedalo Marchetti, Kazue Takahashi, Islam Hawash, Mohamed Khalifa, Jayashree Bulusu, Mohamed Metwaly, Yoshizumi Miyoshi, Zeren Zhima, Ayako Matsuoka, Mariko Teramoto, Yoshiya Kasahara, Fuminori Tsuchiya, Atsushi Kumamoto, Atsuki Shinbori, Iku Shinohara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this study, we present simultaneous multi-point observations of Pi2 magnetic pulsations studied, for the first time, through joined measurements of multiple missions: the CSES-01 and Swarm (A and C), in the topside ionosphere, Van Allen Probe (or Radiation Belt Storm Probes, RBSP) (A and B) and Arase in the magnetosphere. We focused on the compressional component of the satellites and the horizontal component of magnetic field from Kakioka (KAK) ground station in Japan. The Pi2 event occurred from 12:40 to 12:56 UT on January 12, 2019, where CSES-01, RBSP-A, and KAK were on the night side; Swarm-A/C, were on the day side while RBSP-B and Arase were in the dusk sector. We observed 90-degree phase delay between RBSP-A-Bz and RBSP-A-Ey which can be interpreted as a radially trapped fast mode for the compressional oscillation. Both the wavelet transforms and the Hilbert–Huang transform (HHT) were applied for signal analysis, revealing wave-like structures and strong coherence among all data sets and confirming the Pi2 pulsation nature. The compressional component in the topside ionosphere and in the magnetosphere seem very similar with the horizontal component of KAK station. During 12:43–12:45 UT, CSES-01 and Swarm-A/C exhibited an in-phase variation while both were in the Southern Hemisphere. However, as CSES-01 transitioned to the Northern Hemisphere between 12:47 and 12:56 UT, the corresponding signals became out of phase. During the selected Pi2 event, RBSP-B was located very close to Arase in the dusk sector and detected compressional oscillations with a waveform nearly identical to that observed by RBSP-A, suggesting that the observed Pi2 exhibited cavity resonance characteristics. To understand their propagation mechanism, we conduct further analysis of the duskside Pi2 pulsations in this event. We found that the penetration/propagation speed of the low-frequency Pi2 pulsations is high (|m|~ 0.3) and much larger than the average Alfven speed in the plasmasphere, while the high-frequency Pi2 pulsations have a finite m number (m ~ -1.7) and their phase speed is comparable to the average Alfven speed. We suggest that the nightside Pi2 pulsations propagate sunward through a waveguide-like mode, consistent with the high-frequency Pi2 signatures detected on the duskside in the magnetosphere.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46510-0.

Introduction

Pi2 pulsations, with frequencies ranging from 6.6 to 25 mHz (corresponding to periods of 40–150 s), are geomagnetic phenomena associated with substorm onsets. The excitation and propagation mechanisms of Pi2 pulsations remain among the key open topics in the study of ultra-low-frequency (ULF) waves in the magnetosphere and ionosphere. Pi2 pulsations exhibit several important characteristics: (1) they can be observed at almost all local times, predominantly on the nightside but occasionally on the dayside1,2; (2) they are primarily compressional wave events3; and (3) they are frequently detected in low-latitude regions (L < 5) by both ground-based and satellite measurements3. Yeoman4 presented several possible scenarios for the generation mechanisms of Pi2 pulsations. The first involves a surface wave excited at the plasmapause by incoming compressional waves originating from a tailward source5. The second mechanism corresponds to plasmaspheric cavity modes6–9, while the third suggests that mid- and low-latitude Pi2 pulsations can be driven by fast-mode pulses generated by periodic bursty bulk flows breaking in the near-Earth magnetotail10. Kepko and Ghamry et al.11,12 provided evidence of the plasmaspheric virtual resonance (PVR) mode by showing observations of the Pi2 pulsations using two satellites, one in the inner plasmasphere and another one outside plasmasphere.

It was demonstrated that also polar‐orbiting satellites in upper ionosphere can detect Pi2 pulsations. These ionospheric Pi2 had high coherence with the events on the low‐latitude ground stations suggested that these Pi2 pulsations are associated with the plasmaspheric resonance mode13–17. Takahashi13 found that Pi2 pulsations observed simultaneously by the Upper Atmosphere Research Satellite (∼580 km altitude) and low-latitude ground stations exhibited in-phase radial and compressional magnetic field components in the Northern Hemisphere. Han et al.14 conducted a statistical analysis of Pi2 pulsations observed by the Ørsted satellite in low-Earth orbit (LEO) and at a low-latitude ground station. The compressional oscillations at Ørsted correlated strongly with the low-latitude Pi2 in the horizontal component, while the radial component showed only weak correspondence with ground observations. Sutcliffe15 identified three Pi2 events using magnetic field data obtained from the CHAMP satellite in low-Earth orbit (LEO) and from a low-latitude ground station. They found that the CHAMP observations showed that the radial and compressional components were strongly correlated with the ground-based H-component of Pi2 pulsations. Park et al.16 statistically examine the latitudinal structure of the Pi2 pulsations. Their analysis revealed that both the relative amplitude and the cross-phase between the radial and compressional magnetic field components exhibit a strong dependence on the satellite’s geomagnetic latitude. Thomas17 found that 15 high-coherence compressional Pi2 events observed simultaneously by Swarm and low-latitude ground stations were driven by oscillating field-aligned currents. Recently,18 studied a Pi2 event observed, for the first time, using China Seismo-Electromagnetic Satellite (CSES-01) satellite. They showed that Pi2 pulsation can be generated by the field-aligned currents (FACs) in the pulses of substorm current wedge (SCW). Ghamry and Nosé19,20 demonstrated that the cavity mode can exist on the morning side; however, satellite observations on the dusk side remain very limited.

Previous studies investigated Pi2 pulsations using either magnetospheric or ionospheric satellites. Only one study18 used a single satellite mission from each region (in magnetosphere and ionosphere). We present the first simultaneous observations of Pi2 pulsations across both regions by integrating data from multiple missions: the Chinese mission (CSES-01), European Space Agency mission (Swarm), National Aeronautics and Space Administration mission (Van Allen Probes), and Japanese mission (Arase). The event, observed between 12:40 and 12:56 UT on January 12, 2019, was also recorded at the Kakioka (KAK, Japan) ground station, which serves as the reference for our analysis. One of the most notable aspects of this case study is that the Pi2 event is clearly observed on the dusk side (RBSP-B and Arase), confirming that a cavity mode can be excited across dusk sector. To understand their propagation mechanism, we conduct deeper analysis of the duskside Pi2 pulsations in this event. Another important feature revealed in this study is the first comparable wave signature detected simultaneously by CSES-01 and Swarm in the upper ionosphere.

The organization of this paper is as follows. In Sect. "Data sets", we describe the data sets. In Sect. "Observations", we present observations. In Sect. "Discussion and conclusion", we discuss the results of our observations and provide our conclusions.

Data sets

Ionospheric satellites

CSES-01 mission

China Seismo-Electromagnetic Satellite (CSES) was launched successfully on 2 February 2018 and it’s still in orbit and in good operational conditions, exceeding the planned life. Recently (June 2025) a second satellite (CSES-02) was launched in order to integrate and continue the mission. The satellite is also known as ZhangHeng-01 in honor of the Chinese seismologist (Zhang Heng, 78– 139 A.D.) who built the first ever seismoscope in the world. The main goal of CSES is to study and monitor the ionospheric environment to search for possible earthquake-induced phenomena21. For this purpose, CSES is equipped with a variety of payloads which measure the orientation and intensity of the magnetic and electrical fields (HPM = High Precision Magnetometer and EFD = Electrical Field Detector), the density and composition of the ionospheric plasma (LAP = Langmuir Probe and PAP = Plasma Analyser Package), two complementary particle detectors (HEPP = High Energy Particle Package and HEPD = High Energy Particle Detector) a Tri-Band Beacon (TBB) used above China and GNSS Radio Occultation (GNRO-RO) receiver which also provides vertical profiles of electron density. HPM, which is the instrument of most interest for this paper is composed of two fluxgate magnetometers (FGM) and one scalar Coupled Dark State Magnetometer (CDSM). Fluxgates instruments (FGM-S1 and FGM-S2) are placed on a boom about 3.9 m far from the satellite body and CDSM 4.7 m far in order to attenuate the possible electromagnetic contamination of the satellite and cross-talk with the other payloads. CSES-01’s orbit has been selected to optimize its goals, and it is a quasi-polar (inclination of 97.4°) Sun-synchronous orbit with a revisiting time of 5 days. Consequently, the local time is fixed at 02:00 and 14:00 at the equator for ascending and descending semi-orbits. Several studies on CSES-01 data tried to identify and better understand the preparation phase of the earthquakes by detecting ionospheric disturbances and discussing their relationship with the seismic events (e.g.,22–24). It is clear that CSES-01 is a precious space platform not only to study earthquakes but also to understand better several geophysical topics. In fact, with the magnetic data of CSES-01, it was possible to build a new geomagnetic model for the equatorial region25, which even contributed to the new IGRF-13 global model26. Other important contributions are related to the investigation of geomagnetic storms, such as the one on 26 August 201827 or volcano eruptions, such as the 2021 La Palma eruption28 and the Hunga Tonga–Hunga Ha’apai eruption29,30. In particular, as already mentioned above, CSES-01 detected successfully and well in agreement with the ground measurement of a Pi2 pulsation, as reported by18. In this paper, we are going to investigate the data from the Fluxgate magnetometers (FG1) onboard CSES-01 resampled at 1 Hz from the originally acquisition at 60 Hz. Swarm mission

Swarm is a European Space Agency (ESA) mission composed of three identical satellites (Alpha A, Bravo B and Charlie C) launched in November 2013 and still in orbit and in good condition. The special quasi-polar orbital configuration of the Swarm mission is configured to place two satellites (Swarm A and Swarm C) at a lower orbit and Swarm B at a higher orbit and at different local times and precession speeds. All three satellites, in fact, have precessing orbits, which means that the local time of each orbit is slowly evolving over time. This is an important difference from CSES-01, which is Sun-synchronous orbit, i.e., it always measures at the same local time. Such difference makes the two missions complementary each other and their integration is potentially crucial to understanding better the complex ionospheric environment.

The Swarm spacecraft are equipped with the “state of art” magnetometers for space-based measurements with the goal of measuring and monitor the evolution of Earth’s magnetic field and, in general, the ionospheric environment31. Each Swarm satellite is equipped with a Vector Field Magnetometer (VFM) and Absolute Scalar Magnetometer (ASM) placed on half and end of its boom respectively. One of the unique features that guarantees higher precision in the Swarm VFM measurements is the co-location of three-star cameras on the same optical bench as the VFM. This allows a very precise rotation of the measurements from the instrumental frame to NEC (North-East-Center) frame. In analogy to CSES satellite data, we selected the Swarm magnetic product at 1 Hz time resolution (MAGx_LR, where x is the first letter of the satellite). As the CSES-01 and Swarm measure the total magnetic field (including the internal field, crustal field, and external field), the CHAOS-8 geomagnetic field model32 was applied to remove internal and crustal contributions from both CSES-01 and Swarm magnetic field data. The CHAOS-8 model describes the near-Earth geomagnetic field variability from 1999 to 2025 using satellite observations (Swarm, MSS-1, CSES, CryoSat-2, CHAMP, SAC-C, Ørsted) and annual differences of monthly mean ground-based measurements. The residuals were then converted to mean-field-aligned (MFA) coordinate system.

Magnetospheric satellites

ERG (Arase) satellite

The ERG probe, also known by its Japanese name Arase, is a vital research asset of the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency (JAXA). Launched on December 20, 2016, the probe is an integral part of the Exploration of Energization and Radiation Ionization in Geospace program33. The main goal of this mission is to reveal the secrets behind the formation and evolution of Van Allen radiation belts, which contain high-energy electrons with energies exceeding one mega-electron-volt (MeV), as well as to understand how these belts respond to perturbations caused by solar winds and space storms. We analyzed magnetic field data obtained by the Magnetic Field Experiment (MGF)34 and electron density data from Arase-PWE/HFA35,36.

Van Allen Probe (radiation belt storm probes, RBSP)

The twin RBSP spacecraft, launched on 30 August 2012, undergo into a near-equatorial orbit with apogee near L ~ 6 and contain a comprehensive suite of particle and field sensors designed to study the Earth’s radiation environment37. RBSP consists of two spacecraft (RBSP-A and RBSP-B). Both have identical triaxial fluxgate magnetometers capable of measuring 64 vectors per second38. Their ∼9 h orbits are nearly identical, and their slight differences allow them to lap each other, varying the distance between the two spacecraft. We used Electric and Magnetic Field Instrument Suite and Integrated Science (EMFISIS)38 to get magnetic field data.

The Arase and RBSP magnetic field data are rotated from the GSM coordinate system into MFA coordinate system. In MFA system, ez is in the direction of the mean magnetic field, ey (eastward) is parallel to ez x r, where r is the position vector of the satellite relative to the center of the Earth; and ex (radial) is given by ex = ey x ez. This decomposition allows the dominant magnetic field wave polarization to be determined as toroidal (azimuthal), poloidal (radial), or compressional (parallel). The electric field data were provided in the Modified Geocentric Solar Ecliptic (MGSE) coordinates. Using the E·B = 0 assumption, MGSE coordinates transferred to MFA coordinates. This assumption is good when the angle between the spacecraft spin plane and the magnetic field (θSP-B) is larger than 15°.

Kakioka geomagnetic ground station

We used magnetic field measurements from the Kakioka (KAK) in Japan at magnetic latitude 29.38° (L = 1.23), geographic latitude 36.23°, and geographic longitude 140.18°.

Spectral analysis

Spectral analysis in the frequency domain was conducted to confirm the similarity of Pi2 pulsations observed simultaneously at KAK and by the satellite missions.

The Hilbert–Huang transform (HHT)

The Hilbert–Huang Transform (HHT) is a powerful signal processing technique specifically designed for analyzing non-linear and non-stationary data, making it widely used in geophysical applications. The HHT represents a significant advancement over traditional Fourier-based methods, which assume data stationarity and linearity—assumptions that are often violated in real-world geophysical signals44. The HHT consists of two main components: Empirical Mode Decomposition (EMD) and Hilbert Spectral Analysis (HSA). The EMD process is the foundation of the method, serving as an adaptive filter that decomposes any complex signal into a finite number of Intrinsic Mode Functions (IMFs)45. The Hilbert transform is subsequently applied to each IMF to derive instantaneous amplitude and frequency, from which the time–frequency energy distribution is constructed. Unlike traditional decomposition methods that use predetermined basis functions, EMD is entirely data-driven, meaning the basic functions are derived directly from the signal itself. This adaptive nature allows the method to capture the local characteristics of the data without making prior assumptions about its structure.

Wavelet transform

The wavelet transform represents a powerful mathematical tool for analyzing signals that contain features varying in both time and frequency domains. Unlike the traditional Fourier transform, which provides frequency information averaged over the entire time series, the wavelet transform offers a time-localized frequency analysis that is particularly well-suited for studying non-stationary signals common in geophysical and space physics applications46,47. The fundamental principle of wavelet analysis lies in the decomposition of a signal using a family of functions called wavelets, which are localized in both time and frequency. These wavelets are derived from a single prototype function called the mother wavelet through scaling and translation operations. The scaling parameter controls the frequency resolution, while the translation parameter determines the temporal position of the analysis window48. This dual localization capability makes wavelets exceptionally effective for detecting transient events, periodicities, and structural changes in time series data46.

Spectrograms of the HHT reveal time–frequency-rich structures that are difficult to discern from the time series alone. Color-coded intensity maps show energy concentrations in specific frequency bands and time intervals, with colors (black, red, and orange) indicating higher spectral energy density. All data show a significant concentration of energy of higher (~ 13 mHz) and lower (~ 8 mHz) frequency. With HHT it’s possible to denote different components of the signals, especially for RSPB-B, Swarm C and CSES-01. The second (in order of intensity) component seems to be a lower frequency as small variations or oscillations of the baseline field. However, the most intense signal well depicted by both the HHT and continuous wavelets is in within 5 mHz and 10 mHz for all investigated satellites. This appears as a bright horizontal band across all spectrograms in this period, indicating a globally synchronized oscillation that confirms the coherent nature of the Pi2 events.

Discussion and conclusion

Using multipoint satellite observations (CSES-01, Swarm, RBSPand Arase), we detected a clear Pi2 pulsation simultaneously observed in the ionosphere and magnetosphere. This represents, to the best of our knowledge, the first coordinated Pi2 study combining ionospheric and magnetospheric multi-satellite data, with the KAK ground station used as a reference. One of the most notable aspects of this case study is that the Pi2 event is clearly observed by both RBSP-B and the Arase satellites on the duskside (17:30–18:40 MLT). So, we will focus on this duskside Pi2 event with detailed analysis of high (~ 13 mHz) and low (~ 8 mHz) frequency Pi2 pulsations. While Pi2 pulsations are sometimes (~ 5% according to49) detected by magnetospheric satellites on the dawnside and duskside in the plasmasphere as shown in many statistical studies (e.g.,18,50), few studies20,21 provide a detailed analysis on Pi2 pulsations on the flank sides observed in the magnetosphere. To understand their excitation mechanism, we conduct further analysis of the duskside Pi2 pulsations in this event. One of the clues for understanding the excitation mechanism is the phase relation between ground and magnetospheric Pi2 pulsations around the plasmapause. Figure 7a shows the L-shell distribution of the electron density at the RBSP-B (bule line) and Arase (green line) satellites on their outbound orbit during the Pi2 event. The region of the Pi2 pulsations is shaded in gray. Although the location of the plasmapause during this event was unclear due to the density irregularities at L > 5, the electron density decreased down to ~ 10 cm-3 at the dips of the density irregularities, which was much lower than the density of the empirical model inside the plasmasphere (dashed line51). Therefore, we consider that the RBSP-B and Arase satellite were near the plasmapause and sometimes entered the plasmatrough region. Because the spatial separation between RBSP-B and Arase is large in the azimuthal direction (Fig. 3), the discrepancy of the electron density in Fig. 7a can be attributed to azimuthal mesoscale structure of the density irregularity like the plasmaspheric undulation52. If the Pi2 pulsations in this event are cavity mode waves, such a density irregularity can affect the activity of Pi2 pulsations. These Pi2 pulsations were observed in both the high- and low-density regions of the density irregularity near the plasmapause (Fig. 7b). The low-frequency Pi2 pulsations can be considered as the fundamental cavity mode because the frequency matches with the fundamental mode frequency reported in previous studies49,53. Thus, the high-frequency Pi2 pulsations can be considered as the second harmonic cavity mode. In the low-density region during 12:27–12:41 UT, both the low- and high-frequency (~ 14 mHz) Pi2 pulsations were observed. The confinement of the high-frequency Pi2 pulsations in the low-density region can be attributed to the radial nodal structure of the second harmonic cavity model. According to the MHD simulation of54, the phase difference between the ground H-component and magnetospheric parallel component of the second harmonic cavity mode changes from 0° to 180° slightly inward the plasmapause at most MLTs except for the noon sector. The second harmonic cavity mode has its node of the parallel magnetic field oscillation at the point of this phase shift. Therefore, the second harmonic high cavity mode waves with a high frequency were detected when RBSP-B was away from the plasmapause, that is, in the low-density region during 12:27–12:41 UT and they showed the 180° phase difference. In the high-density region during 12:23–12:27 UT and after 12:41 UT, RBSP-B was probably close the node of the magnetic field oscillations and observed no high-frequency Pi2 waves or weak high-frequency Pi2 waves with an intermediate phase shift (~ 90°) around 12:40 UT. If it is the case, this study first reports that the density irregularity near the plasmapause controls the local activity of Pi2 pulsations.54 statistically showed that the phase difference of the high-frequency waves observed by RBSPs is intermediate (~ − 45°) rather than out of phase on the duskside. The phase difference in our result is close to that of the simulation result in54. What causes the discrepancy between our case study and the observational statistics is an unsolved question. One possible reason is that the source region of the Pi2 pulsations was on the duskside and phase difference usually seen in the midnight sector (~ 180°) was observed on the duskside.9,54 suggested that fundamental cavity mode excited on the nightside propagate through the plasmaspheric cavity like a waveguide mode and they reach the flankside. In this hypothesis, the phase difference from the midnight gradually increases as the guided waves propagate sunward at the Alfvén speed. In the recent statistical study of49, however, they revealed a small-time delay (~ 3 s) between Pi2 pulsations in the magnetosphere around the midnight (22–02 MLT) and dayside ground Pi2 pulsations and they concluded that the waveguide mode model cannot account for the small delay because this model takes 10 s for the propagation from midnight to noon.

Here we discuss that the waveguide model can explain the duskside Pi2 pulsations in this event. Figure 8a shows Bz at RBSP-B and Arase filtered with a 2–20 mHz for the Pi2 event during 12:40–12:56 UT. To examine a small phase difference between two spacecraft, we switched to using 1-s magnetic field data of RBSP-B and Arase. The 1-s magnetic field data of Arase was calculated by averaging the MGF Level-2 64-Hz data34. Since RBSP-B and Arase was close to each other, highly coherent oscillations were observed by two spacecraft around 8 mHz (Fig. 8d, e). The phase difference was ~ 3°, corresponding to ~ 1 s for 8 mHz waves. Thus, the phase difference was very small and comparable to the time resolution of the data. Assuming that Pi2 pulsations are global mode waves and neglect the difference in L-shell, we can estimate the azimuthal wave number (m number) in the magnetosphere. The estimated value is |m|~ 0.3. This is comparable to the result of49 (|m|~ 0.1). For an 8.3 mHz wave, the phase velocity at RBSP-B and Arase (L ~ 5.8 RE) is 10,061.2 km/s. This is much higher than the Alfvén velocity in the magnetosphere. From the radial distribution of the electron density measured by Arase (Fig. 7a), the averaged Alfvén speed inside the plasmapause at L = 5.7 was 1,694.0 km/s for the proton plasma. In this case, the phase velocity of the 13.5 mHz wave at L ~ 5.8 RE is 1,843.8 km/s. This is close to the averaged Alfvén speed. Therefore, we consider that the propagation of the waveguide mode is still possible for the high-frequency Pi2 pulsations. In this event, the high-frequency Pi2 pulsations show a 180° phase difference even on the duskside. This implies that a favorable condition for the cavity mode resonance is maintained in a wide MLT range and this is also favorable for the propagation of the waveguide mode in the dusk sector. It should be noted that the 1-s time resolution data that we analyzed in this study may introduce a timing error of < 0.5 s in the lag of 3.6 s, corresponding < 14% (= 0.5/3.6) of the estimated m number and the phase velocity. We do not consider that this error significantly affect the conclusion.

In conclusion, the duskside Pi2 pulsation can be regarded as a characteristic feature of the ionosphere–magnetosphere coupling process. The nightside Pi2 pulsations could propagate sunward as a waveguide-type mode, similar to the high-frequency Pi2 observed on the duskside. The results show that the joint use of CSES-01 and Swarm satellite data enables detailed investigations of ULF wave propagation and coupling processes in the upper ionosphere.
