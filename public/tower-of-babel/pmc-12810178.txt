Introduction

In contemporary practice, dentists aim to meet patients' desire for a beautiful, natural white smile, recognizing that esthetics significantly influences oral health-related quality of life.

The most frequent side effect of endodontically treated teeth is discolored teeth. It has been associated with the duration of the substance's contact with the structure of the tooth and whether or not the components employed in the treatment are chromogenic.1

Coronal tooth discoloration can occur in certain clinical settings due to intra- and/or postendodontic procedural errors. This is mostly due to a lack of knowledge about the possibility of discoloration of materials used during and after endodontic treatment, which can be related to nonendodontic etiological variables.2

One of the primary goals of an endodontic procedure is to eradicate microorganisms from the root canal structure, since these bacteria are the source of periapical disorders and contribute to their persistence. Although the microbial count can be decreased by cleaning and shaping the system of root canals, the microbes that are trapped in the accessory canals cannot be totally removed. To lower the amount of infection in the canal system of the tooth, it is therefore recommended to utilize intracanal medications between treatment sessions.3

Several researchers have suggested using formocresol, antibiotic combinations, and calcium hydroxide to successfully eradicate contamination from the root canal.4

Ca(OH)2 is the most commonly used and most popular endodontic medicament in the world. However, its low solubility and diffusibility could limit its ability to rapidly increase the pH to the level necessary to eliminate bacteria within the dentinal tubules. As a result, researchers have been looking into next-generation intracanal medications, and one of the most promising ones to come out is triple antibiotic paste (TAP).5,6

The infection affecting the root canal system is considered a polymicrobial disease since it consists of both anaerobic and aerobic microbes. It is doubtful that any one antibiotic could effectively sterilize the canal due to the intricacy of the canal system infection. More likely, to deal with the varied flora encountered, a combination would be required. The most promising combination appears to be metronidazole, ciprofloxacin, and minocycline (MINO).7

Triple antibiotic paste's superior antibacterial capabilities, both inside the dentinal tubules and on the dentinal surface, account for its rising popularity.2,7 Crown discoloration is an unwanted side effect of using TAP.2

Minocycline, an aminocyclitol derivative, has been blamed for the discoloration. It can create calcium ion complexes, which can color teeth in a variety of ways. When the antibiotic-containing paste enters the tubular structures of dentin, tooth discoloration starts. It is crucial to remember that MINO can start to discolor teeth 24 hours after coming into contact with it.2

According to Torabinejad et al., crown darkening is observed in 40% of teeth that have received TAP treatment.4 This elevated proportion signifies a significant clinical issue.4 To overcome this, it has also been proposed that crown discoloration can be reduced by substituting another combination of antibiotics, such as cephalosporin, amoxicillin, or clindamycin (CLIN), for MINO.2,8 Nevertheless, there aren't many prior researches that contrast the outcomes of TAP with double antibiotic paste (DAP) with doxycycline, amoxicillin, or CLIN.9

Treatment options for reducing or eliminating tooth discoloration following regenerative endodontic procedures (REPs) include internal bleaching or masking the discoloration with resin composite veneer. The therapy of choice should be simple, practical, and minimally invasive methods.10

Inherently discolored teeth can be fixed in a variety of ways, but bleaching is a safe substitute that takes less time to complete.5 If done properly, the internal bleaching procedure is a low-risk, straightforward, and inexpensive operation that requires little downtime.11

Sodium perborate combined with distilled water works as an intracoronal bleach just as effectively as hydrogen peroxide because there is minimal possibility of external resorption.5

As far as we are aware, not many researches have examined the impact of various antibiotic formulations used as intracanal medications on crown discoloration. Thus, the aim of this research was to determine the possibility of coronal discoloration caused by various antibiotic combinations as well as the effectiveness of bleaching agents to treat discolored crowns caused by antibiotic pastes.

Materials and Methods

Sample Collection and Storage

A total of 55 freshly extracted (not more than 2 months) sound human single-rooted maxillary central incisors were collected. Teeth were cleaned with an ultrasonic scaler and stored in distilled water until usage.

Base Color Assessment

Pretreatment color readings were taken of the entire labial surface of all teeth, and these values served as a baseline against which all post-treatment data were compared. To evaluate color, a PerkinElmer LAMBDA 750 UV/Vis Spectrophotometer was utilized. Every specimen underwent three measurements, with the mean value being noted.

Sample Preparation

A standardized root length of 10 mm below the facial cementoenamel junction (CEJ) was obtained by sectioning each sample (Fig. 1). To avoid disrupting intact crown and preventing coronal microleakage, all procedures, including the removal of pulp tissue, irrigation, application of antibiotic pastes, and placement of cotton pellet and temporary filling material, were carried out from the apical aspect.

Decoronated single-rooted tooth

After removing the pulp tissue, the root canal was irrigated with a 3% sodium hypochlorite solution; then, to simulate clinical irrigation, a 17% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) solution (Prime Dental RC Help) and distilled water were used.

The antibiotic pastes were mixed and placed into the pulp chamber via apical access. Internal axial walls are coated with lentulospiral filler (Fig. 2). All samples were kept for 1 month at 100% humidity in an incubator at 37°C after the apical apertures were sealed with sticky wax.

Tooth Color Assessment

On the buccal surfaces of the crown, spectrophotometric measurements were performed (Fig. 4). To guarantee that color measurement was done on the same area at every turn with a vertical angle, a standardized circular strip with a diameter of 6 mm was attached to the buccal surface of the crown 2 mm above the CEJ. One month later, color variations (ΔE) were noted.

Results

Microsoft Excel 2007 was used to enter the data for this investigation, and SPSS statistical software, version 23.0, was used for analysis. Depending on the data's normality, the one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for the intergroup comparison before moving on to post hoc analysis. The homogeneity of the variables was examined using Levene's test, and the distribution of the data was examined using the Shapiro–Wilk test. The groups were compared within the different time intervals using the paired t-test.

Intergroup Comparison between Different Groups in Terms of Mean Color Change after Bleaching

The pretreatment mean values were 57.28 for the control group, 57.28 for the MINO group, 56.52 for the doxycycline group, 56.93 for the amoxicillin group, and 55.17 for the CLIN group. The post-treatment values were 51.11 for the control group, 52.61 for the MINO group, 53.27 for the doxycycline group, 53.10 for the amoxicillin group, and 55.57 for the CLIN group. The control group experienced the largest mean color change, followed by the MINO group and the CLIN group with the lowest. One-way ANOVA analysis revealed that the intergroup comparison between the various groups was statistically significant (Table 1).

Discussion

Coronal discoloration remains a challenge for clinicians to avoid and treat, particularly when the anterior esthetic zone is involved.12 In endodontics, intracanal medications are used in a variety of methods.13,14

The most widely employed and well-liked endodontic medication worldwide is Ca(OH)2 for its limited diffusibility and solubility. TAP is one of the most promising new generation intracanal medications that have been studied as a result.5

Triple antibiotic paste, used in regenerative endodontic therapy, combines ciprofloxacin, metronidazole, and MINO. However, it can cause tooth darkening, especially in anterior teeth, posing esthetic issues.

Minocycline in TAP causes tooth crown staining by binding to calcium ions and forming an insoluble compound. Doxycycline, amoxicillin, cefaclor, and CLIN can act similarly as substitutes.15,16

Iriboz et al.5 chose 85 extracted human incisors for the study because the maxillary incisors are in the esthetic zone and provide the best esthetic results for discoloration and whitening effect. They studied the effectiveness of sodium perborate bleaching using different activation techniques on crowns stained by two distinct antibiotic pastes.

The objective of this research was to assess the bleaching effect on crown discoloration caused by various antibiotic combinations, such as MINO plus doxycycline, amoxicillin, and CLIN.

Similar to the Yasa et al.'s11 study, where the access cavity was prepared apically and the pulp tissue was removed, canals were irrigated and dried apically, and specimens were then apically filled using a syringe with antibiotic pastes, all of the samples in our study had their access cavities prepared apically to prevent disruption of the entire crown and to prevent coronal microleakage.

In order to simulate an immature tooth, Betancourt et al.17 employed 40 single-rooted maxillary incisors. The apical portion of each specimen's root was sectioned to yield a standard root length of 10 mm below the labial CEJ.

In order to standardize all the samples in our investigation, the roots were sectioned 10 mm below the CEJ using a diamond disc, and the apical access was sealed with sticky wax in order to replicate the oral environment.

In our methodology, we coated the sealers in the root canals by coating, inserting, and spinning a lentulospiral file anticlockwise so that it would reach the prepared working length and not bind to the prepared canal walls. For 20 seconds, the lentulospiral was revolved slowly (300 rpm).

All samples underwent final irrigation with 3% sodium hypochlorite solution, and 17% EDTA solution, and samples were maintained at 100% humidity in an incubator at 37°C for a period of 4 weeks.

The study assessed coronal discoloration risks from different antibiotic combinations. TAP with CLIN did not cause noticeable darkening, supporting the null hypothesis. TAP with doxycycline and MINO caused discoloration within a day, rejecting the null hypothesis. TAP with amoxicillin led to significant color changes from the 1st week, while TAP with CLIN caused discoloration after 27 days.18

Thus, the discoloration effect of the antibiotic paste was determined at 1-month intervals in the current investigation to determine the prompt staining effect caused by the medicament.

To assess tooth discoloration in endodontics, we used a spectrophotometer, addressing the limitations of other methods such as shade tabs, digital photo analysis, and colorimetry, to ensure uniformity in our research.

In this study, tooth discoloration was measured using a PerkinElmer LAMBDA 750 Spectrophotometer. Using a spectrophotometer, we assessed the effect of bleaching agent on teeth colored with TAP in three different time periods: Baseline (preshade), 1 month after TAP was applied, and 1 week after the bleaching agent was applied.

Therefore, the MINO group showed the most discoloration in the study we conducted. MINO had the greatest mean color change, followed by amoxycillin, doxycycline, and CLIN, which had the least.

The discoloration is likely due to MINO forming an insoluble compound with tooth calcium through chelation, which causes most of the staining in TAP. As MINO integrates into the tooth matrix, the discoloration worsens over time.

In our investigation, TAP combined with MINO caused crown discoloration that started to show on day 1 following paste placement and got worse over time. This is consistent with the research mentioned above. Beginning in week 1, TAP combined with doxycycline and amoxicillin caused appreciable color changes. At all tested times, TAP with CLIN did not cause any noticeable discoloration.

These results align with those of Yasa et al.,11 Venkataraman et al.,15 and Akcay et al.9

Our research also showed that coronal discoloration resulting from TAP containing doxycycline and amoxicillin was marginally greater than that resulting from CLIN combination. Chromogenic precipitates may be the cause of discoloration when amoxyclav is present.15

These results are in line with studies conducted by Chen et al.,19 Venkataraman et al.,15 and Betancourt et al.,17 which found no statistically significant difference in coronal discoloration between antibiotic combinations containing amoxicillin, doxycycline, and CLIN.

Internal bleaching represents a conservative management option for coronal discoloration in nonvital teeth, including trauma- associated cases.20,21

Oxidizing chemicals can be used in the pulp chamber to reduce discoloration. The walking bleach technique involves sealing an oxidizing agent, such as sodium perborate, inside the pulp chamber.20,21

In our study, sodium perborate was used to bleach crowns discolored by various antibiotic combinations. Spectrophotometry measured the color change after 7 days. TAP with MINO showed more whitening than taps with doxycycline, amoxicillin, and CLIN. The control group had the greatest color change, followed by the MINO group, with the CLIN group showing the least.

Antibiotic combinations in endodontic therapy can cause significant staining over time, influenced by the TAP's composition. Esthetic considerations are crucial when choosing intracanal medication.

The significance of this result for the clinical situation is that, in order to prevent coronal discoloration, every attempt should be made to completely remove disinfectant (antibiotic pastes) from treated pulp chambers after root canal obturation before placing permanent restorations. This is particularly crucial in the area of esthetics, including the anterior region.

This study provides an introduction to the role of antibiotic paste combinations in coronal discoloration and whether the discoloration of teeth treated with TAP can be improved by bleaching procedures.

To satisfy esthetic standards, greater investigation and comprehension of the chromogenic potential of endodontic medications are necessary, and the development of nonstaining intracanal medicaments is evident.
