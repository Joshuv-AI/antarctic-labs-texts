Tackling activity-stability paradox of reconstructed NiIrOx electrocatalysts by bridged W-O moiety
Authors: Muhammad Imran Abdullah, Yusheng Fang, Xiaobing Wu, Meiqi Hu, Jing Shao, Youkun Tao, Haijiang Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
One challenge remaining in the development of Ir-based electrocatalyst is the activity-stability paradox during acidic oxygen evolution reaction (OER), especially for the surface reconstructed IrOx catalyst with high efficiency. To address this, a phase selective Ir-based electrocatalyst is constructed by forming bridged W-O moiety in NiIrOx electrocatalyst. Through an electrochemical dealloying process, an nano-porous structure with surface-hydroxylated rutile NiWIrOx electrocatalyst is engineered via Ni as a sacrificial element. Despite low Ir content, NiWIrOx demonstrates a minimal overpotential of 180 mV for the OER at 10 mA·cm−2. It maintains a stable 300 mA·cm−2 current density during an approximately 300 h OER at 1.8 VRHE and shows a stability number of 3.9 × 105 noxygen · nIr−1. The resulting W – O–Ir bridging motif proves pivotal for enhancing the efficacy of OER catalysis by facilitating deprotonation of OER intermediates and promoting a thermodynamically favorable dual-site adsorbent evolution mechanism. Besides, the phase selective insertion of W-O in NiIrOx enabling charge balance through the W-O-Ir bridging motif, effectively counteracting lattice oxygen loss by regulating Ir-O co-valency.

Introduction

Photovoltaic-driven (electro) transformation of H2O and CO2 to valuable solar fuels is an intriguing possibility to rectify the world’s carbon footprint and offer an environmentally friendly substitute to traditional petroleum and coal so as to accomplish renewable energy generation1. The anodic reaction, also referred as the oxygen evolution reaction (OER), which serves as an essential half-cell reaction for catalytically splitting of H2O or reduction of CO2. The main challenge remaining in the development of OER is to obtain active, stable, and cost-effective electrocatalysts, especially in acidic media. At present, iridium oxides are generally considered as one of the most appropriate catalysts, owing to the superiority in balanced performance compared to other noble metals (Pt, Ru)2 and non-noble metal (Ni, Fe, Co, Mn etc.) materials3–6. However, the scarcity of Ir raises extremely high requirements of activity and durability as to minimize the amount of usage.

As a result, multiple research efforts have concentrated on building Ir-based electrocatalysts with optimal activity and durability. In recent years, several Ir-based oxides, comprising doped IrO2 (Sr, Ca, Co, Fe, Mo, etc.)7–13, heterostructure IrO2 (Ru@IrOx, and IrRuNiCoFe)13,14, and perovskite precursors15 were investigated to improve the activity and stability of Ir sites by regulating the electronic structure, creating the surface strain or triggering the surface reconstruction16. The IrOx nanomaterials generated by the surface reconstruction often demonstrate greater performance than the rutile IrO2 due to the increased surface area, Ir-O covalency and sufficient binding abilities with oxygenated intermediates (*OH, *O, and *OOH) on the surface sites15,17. The inherent activity of IrOx towards the OER could be increased through atomic-level doping or alloying with transition metals8,12,18–20.

The 3 d transition metal orchestrates the O* and OH* intermediates adsorption energy on the catalyst surface through meticulous modulation of the local electronic environment19,21. The transition metals (Ni, Fe) within the aforementioned materials had been reported to be specifically dissolved by an electrochemical pretreatment, which lead to a surface reconstruction and generate the active electrocatalyst phase. Continuing along this line of thought, recent investigations on IrNi oxides9,11,22, and IrOx/SrIrO310, also showed that the surface reconstruction by depletion of non-noble metals leads to a large boost in OER performance.

Despite the activities of surface-reconstructed IrOx catalysts are appealing candidates for acid OER, fewer electrocatalysts has been reported to operate at large potential (>1.6 VRHE) over long periods of time and evident deterioration of performance has been observed23. The capability to operate at higher potential stands out as a distinct advantage for acid OER, which plays a key role in cost reduction for the practical implementation. Iridates like NiIrOx form amorphous IrOxHy active phases during the acidic OER. The microstructures of these amorphous phases vary depending on factors such as the initial iridate’s crystal structure, defects, and preparation22. The electrocatalysts derived from iridates through surface reconstruction exhibit significantly higher activity but suffer from reduced structural stability and increased iridium leaching compared to crystalline IrO2 nanocatalysts22.

To concurrently enhance the catalytic stability and performance, acumens into OER mechanism and structure-performance association are highly desired. The primary factor identified as the root cause of the degradation is the over-oxidation of exposed iridium (Ir), which leads to the dissolution of Ir in the forms of Ir3+ and IrO42-10,23. Over-oxidation of NiIrOx exposed Ir in acid electrolytes is primarily triggered by the lattice oxygen participation, which results in oxygen vacancies (VO) formation during OER24. The formation of VO further increases the explosion of Ir atoms on the surface that are then overly oxidized into solvable higher-valence IrO42− ions. Inevitably, excessive Ir oxidation in IrOx causes the crystalline lattice collapse, thereby impairing stability. Consequently, the NiIrOx, that utilizes a LOM mechanism, is highly unstable and could only operate at 100 mA cm−2 for less than 24 h10,23.

Up to now, the most assumed way to stabilize lattice oxygen of IrOx is to build bulk protonated IrO2, which, conversely, is attained by forfeiting activity25. Moreover, at excessive proton concentrations in an acid electrolyte, effective surface deprotonation of key intermediates is fairly challenging. A potential drawback of adding more surface proton acceptors is that they could cover up pristine active sites while improving the deprotonation route for OER intermediates26,27. For transferring protons, bridged oxygen (M-Obri-M) may serve to be proton acceptors while sustaining oxo intermediates20. Intense proton adsorption, on the other hand, is shown by highly reactive and poorly coordinated Ir-O(II-δ)-Ir species, impeding ongoing deprotonation and OER kinetics.

Though activating and stabilizing of catalysts are often accomplished at the expense of one another, it has been suggested that enhancing the connection between Ir metal and supports by adding acid-stable metal oxides as supports is a workable solution for balancing both factors28. Among them, Ta2O5, or WO3 support is widely investigated12,18,19 Multi-valent metals (Ta, Mo, and W etc.) can realize charge compensation for the increase in oxidation state due to the leaching of transition metal during surface reconstruction or the loss of Ir during LOM governed OER process. Prior research efforts have demonstrated that WO3 support plays a key role in OER, either through strain or electron feeding processes. It is possible to construct adequate Ir-O-W moieties by putting Ir into WO3. However, stability is increased in Ir:WO3/Ir and Ir@WOx at the expense of activity as compared to IrOx, because IrOx and WO3 have a poor miscibility29,30. It is technically challenging to accomplish optimum activity and stability by atomic-scale controlled W-O doping in Ir-O-Ir. The real effect of W, i.e., to function via atomic level doping is still veiled and requires accurate electrocatalytic structure identification down to atomic level.

Herein, we have employed a dual modulation approach to tackle the activity-stability paradox of surface reconstructed IrOx catalysts by forming bridged W-O moiety in NiIrOx electrocatalyst. Considering adoption in the NiIrOx crystal structure, the multi-valent characteristics and the intonation of Brønsted acidity by W as well as the acid stability of tungsten oxide31,32, it is anticipated that replacing Ir in NiIrOx with W will enable controlled W-O doping in the Ir-O-Ir framework. This substitution aims to generate the desired Ir-O-W active species, thereby reducing Ir consumption and optimizing the electronic configuration. We synthesized a self-supported, phase-selective NiWIrOx nanoparticles-based electrode through electrodeposition and subsequent electrochemical de-alloying, as shown in Fig. 1a, b. In contrast to those recently reported Ni iridate (pre-)electrocatalysts with dense structures (e.g., NiIr), electrodeposited NiWIr can be protonated in acid, and keep its structure intact; the protonated NiWIr undergoes a crystalline-to-crystalline phase transition during dealloying, with rutile nanocrystallites instead of amorphous IrOxHy. The small quantities of Ni and W are integrated atomically into IrOx lattice to concurrently boost the OER performance and stability. The introduction of the variable valence states of W is to counteract the charge imbalance originating from the intense dissolution of Ni during de alloying of NiIrOx. Similar to solid Bronsted acid catalysts33, the integration of W-O in Ir-O(II-δ)-Ir may regulate Obri’s proton adsorption and desorption. Concurrently, the inherent deprotonation capacity of the W-O-Ir moiety is incorporated into the NiIrOx framework. These combined effects hold the potential to attenuate lattice oxygen participation and facilitate effective deprotonation during OER. Consequently, this synergistic modification may not only elevate the efficiency of the OER but also confers heightened stability to the overall catalyst performance. It is found that the obtained NiWIrOx electrocatalyst deliver a low overpotential of 180 mV to attain 10 mA cm−2 current density complemented with a 312 h stability in 0.5 M H2SO4 electrolyte at 1.8 VRHE potential under approximately 300 mA·cm−2 current density. Results

Electrochemical synthesis and de-alloying

The NiWIr-ED is synthesized by electrodeposition in a typical three-electrode setup with Ti fiber felt as the working electrode. Fig. S1 shows the optical images of the precursor solution and the working electrodes during the process. The optimized composition of NiIrOx was investigated by varying the concentration of Ni, W and Ir as depicted in Fig. S2. The NiIr-ED, IrW-ED and Ir-ED were also synthesized using a similar procedure without W, Ni and Ni-W precursors, respectively, as a control samples.

The electrodeposited NiWIr-ED was subjected to electrochemical de-alloying, resulting in the production of highly nanoporous NiWIrOx as illustrated in Fig. 1a. This process was carried out using the chrono potentiometric method and took place over a span of 2 h, with a solution consisting of 0.5 M H2SO4. Following the de-alloying process, the resulting material was repeatedly washed with deionized (DI) water and subsequently subjected to vacuum drying. The structure of NiWIr-ED nanosheets is a result of concurrent electrodeposition of iridium (Ir) tungsten (W) and nickel (Ni) on the surface of titanium felt (Ti-F), specifically on the hydrogen-adsorbed Ni surface. Subsequent dealloying removes a significant portion of the nickel from the structure (Fig. 1a, c). The resulting NiWIrOx nanoparticles exhibit a composition primarily comprising porous Ir-O-WCUS (coordinately unsaturated active sites (CUS, every W and Ir atom coordinate with three O atom) frameworks, with only trace amounts of nickel remaining. During the initial 35 min of the dealloying process, the potential remains at approximately 0.2 VRHE (Fig. S2). This period marks the dissolution of the exposed nickel on the surface, resulting in the formation of nanoporous metallic IrW (Fig. S2). The potential experiences a sudden increase to around 1.47 VRHE, ensuring complete oxidation over the following 80 min, as depicted in Fig. S2. This approach differs from conventional electrochemical dealloying methods, which involve potential cycling within the range of 0.05–1.5 VRHE, where the dissolution and oxidation of iridium (Ir) and tungsten (W) take place simultaneously. As illustrated in Fig. S2, the process of nickel leaching begins at a lower potential for NiWIr-ED when compared to NiIr-ED at a current density of 20 mA·cm⁻². The potential for OER increases progressively within the first 40 min. However, after the 40 min, when more time is allowed for oxidation (specifically, between 40 and 120 min), the OER potential remains constant. Extended leaching is suggested to be responsible for the increased formation of Ir-O-WCUS in NiWIrOx, which in turn enhances the OER activity (Fig. S3a). Nevertheless, it’s likely that the rate of Ir-O-WCUS generation stabilizes once most of the near-surface nickel has been leached, resulting in a consistent OER activity after 2 h of oxidation. The distinctive fingerprint in potential-current prompted by methanol oxidation reaction (MOR) could also be used to uncover surface nickel leaching in acidic medium (Fig. S3b). The different reaction of the NiWIrOx for MOR proves that the reaction strongly depends on the generation of reactive intermediates on catalyst surface. Ni is more active for MOR than Ir in alkaline medium because the surface Ni strongly adsorbed OH*, which is essential for MOR34–36. In acidic medium, surface Ni leached which in turn decreases the MOR activity. The results of long-term MOR and OER in acidic medium showed a high MOR current on NiWIrOx at start due to a high surface coverage of OH*, which can be explained by the higher surface Ni content on the catalyst in the beginning. The surface Ni reduced with the passage of time which subsequently decreased the MOR current rather than the OER current. These results are in line with that of the HADDF-TEM elemental mapping and XPS tests as depicted in Fig. S3c–e.

As nickel migrates out, the iridium (Ir) tends to be in a higher oxidation state. Meanwhile, tungsten (W) possesses the capacity to stabilize both higher and lower oxidation states of Ir, depending on the applied potential, through charge compensation. The reduced leaching of iridium (Ir) in the electrolyte during the dealloying process of NiWIr-ED serves as additional confirmation of the presence of stable Ir-O-WCUS, in contrast to NiIr-ED, as depicted in Fig. 1c.

Formation of NiWIrOx nanocrystalline

Before creating the binary NiWIr-ED structure, an analysis of the electrodeposition characteristics of pure metals were conducted on Ti fibers through Ir-ED, IrW-ED, and Ni-ED depositions. Fig. S4 shows the morphology of Ni-ED, which exhibits a highly porous hierarchical structure. This structure is a result of the restricted mass transport of Ni2+ ions and the influence of hydrogen bubble templating, attributed to an excess negative deposition potential of −2.7 VAg/AgCl. Conversely, the deposition of Ir and W in the absence of Ni2+proved to be challenging.

The reduction of [IrCl6]2- and WO42- could be catalyzed by absorbed hydrogen (H). However, due to the excessively strong adsorption of hydrogen on the Ti surface37, the reduction of [IrCl6]3- and WO42- hardly occurred on the Ti fiber, resulting in no obvious changes in the morphology after the deposition (Fig. S4). This problem can be solved by the aid of Ni-H, and the step-by-step procedures are illustrated in Fig. 1. The standard Gibbs free energy of hydrogen adsorption (ΔG Hads) on nickel falls within the range of −0.27 eV to −0.23 eV, suggesting a more favorable adsorption of hydrogen for catalyzing the deposition of W and Ir compared to the titanium substrate38. Once the suitable environment for Hads is established, the deposition of Ir and W takes place, concurrently with the ongoing deposition of Ni, resulting in the formation of NiWIr-ED.

Examination of the structural changes was conducted on the NiWIr-ED nanosheets during the reconstruction process by electrochemical dealloying (Fig. 2 and Fig. S5). After a 2-hour dealloying process, it is observed with the complete disappearance of the nanosheets, transforming into small nanoparticles ranging from 3 to 1.5 nm in size (Fig. 2a–c, Fig. 2e–g and Fig. S5). For NiWIr-ED, a uniform and relatively dense coating is observed on the surface of the Ti-felt. In the case of NiWIrOx, a nanoscale porous layer extends over the Ti fibers due to the leaching of Ni species from the NiWIr-ED. This porous structure may facilitate the transport and removal of gas bubbles from the catalyst layer. The cross-sectional SEM images shows that the thickness of the catalyst layer is approximately 2 µm for both NiWIr-ED and NiWIrOx samples, remaining consistent before and after dealloying (Fig. 2b, f).

The HRTEM images, as illustrated in Fig. 2d reveal that the NiWIr-ED catalysts exhibit a polycrystalline nanosheet structure spanning several nanometers. Furthermore, the measured interplanar distances of 0.215 nm and 0.209 nm in Fig. 2d, confirm a strong alignment with the Ir and Ni rich surfaces of NiWIr alloy, corresponding to the (111) plane in the face-centered cubic structure after electrodeposition. In the case of NiWIrOx and NiIrOx, the structure consists of nanoparticles featuring the (200) plane of IrO2 after dealloying, corresponding to a lattice fringe with a spacing of 0.225 nm (Fig. 2h and Fig. S5). HRTEM elemental mapping images of NiWIrOx reveal a lower Ni content compared to NiWIr-ED in Fig. 2i, j.

The (200) facet exhibits a higher surface energy of 2.29 J·cm−2 compared to the (110) facet with 1.84 J·cm−2 surface energy (Fig. S6)39. This observation suggests that reconstruction of NiIr-ED and NiWIr-ED iridate by nickel leaching can yield an IrO2 nanocatalyst with high-energy (200) facets. It’s worth noting that the crystal-to-crystal phase transition observed in NiWIrOx and NiIrOx stands in contrast to the conventional behavior observed in previously reported Ni iridate catalysts22,30. Typically, these catalysts tend to undergo a reconstruction into an amorphous IrOxHy phase during the OER, as documented in prior studies22,40,41.

Acid corrosion significantly impacts the structure of electrocatalysts used in acidic OER. For previously reported iridates with dense crystal structures, acid corrosion under non-catalytic conditions typically causes the dissolution of non-iridium elements and leads to uncontrollable surface amorphization22,40,41.

To investigate the different behavior of NiWIr catalyst developed in this study, an acid etching experiment was performed on the NiWIr-ED by treating the catalyst in 3 M HCl solution for 28 h. After acidic treatment, Ni2+ ions in NiWIr-ED are found to be replaced with H+ ions, resulting in a protonated form named as H-NiWIr. The XRD pattern of H-NiWIr (Fig. S7a) closely matches that of NiWIr-ED, except for a shift to smaller diffraction angles, indicating a slight lattice expansion while maintaining the overall structure, which is further corroborated by the HR-TEM image (Fig. S7c, d). The lattice expansion is most likely due to the incorporation of the hydrated hydronium ions (H3O+) and water molecules into the NiWIr structures during the ion exchange process. The cross-sectional SEM images reveal that the catalyst layer has a thickness of approximately 2 µm for both the NiWIr-ED and H-NiWIr samples. This thickness remains consistent before and after acid etching (Fig. S8).

TEM elemental mapping images of H-NiWIr (Fig. S7) reveal a lower Ni content compared to NiWIr-ED. The Ni:Ir atomic ratio for H-NiWIr is approximately 29% Ni to 4.86% Ir, which is significantly lower than the ratio of 13% Ni to 76.05% Ir in NiWIr-ED. From the Ni 2p XPS results (Fig. S7b), it is found that the Ni 2p XPS peak of H-NiWIr is less intense and shifts to lower energy compared to NiWIr-ED. Overall, these characterization results demonstrate that proton exchange in NiWIr-ED iridate is feasible without causing structural amorphization.

The bulk composition assessed through EDS indicates a composition of Ni54W0.1Ir2.89 for NiWIr-ED. After dealloy, the composition of NiWIrOx, as shown in Table S1, undergoes a significant alteration, due to the substantial dissolution of Ni. Consequently, the initially smooth surface of NiWIr-ED, as shown in Fig. 2a–c, undergoes a transformation into a more rugged morphology with the development of surface cracks and nanoscale pores during the dealloying process (Fig. 2e–g and Fig. S9). In the X-ray Powder Diffraction (XRD) patterns (as shown in Fig. 2k), the peaks associated with NiWIr-ED at 44.271 (111) and 51.541 (200) exhibit a slight deviation towards lower angles compared to that with pure Ni (JCPDS No. 04-0850) and Ir (JCPDS No. 06-0598). This deviation suggests the formation of an alloy. Accurately determining the phase of materials such as NiWIrOx and NiIrOx after dealloying through XRD analysis presents a formidable challenge due to nano-broadening effect of particle sizes (1.5–3 nm) and the insufficient detect limitation. This is substantiated by the findings obtained from EDS and inductively coupled plasma (ICP) emission spectroscopy, which collectively affirm the incorporation of W (ICP: 8.8 at.%, EDS: 8.7 at.%) into the NiIrOx framework (detailed in Table S1). By employing NiWIr-ED as the sacrificial template, a dual purpose is achieved: not only is the nanostructure of the catalysts adjusted, but also W-O is effectively integrated into the NiIrOx.

Identification of W-O-Ir Sites in NiWIrOx

To elucidate the integration of W atoms for W-O sites within NiWIrOx at atomic level, we subsequently performed a Raman test, a highly sensitive technique for discerning alterations in the coordination environment. The NiIrOx manifests two distinct Raman-active modes, namely Eg (546 cm−1) and B2g band (719 cm−1)42, as illustrated in Fig. 2l. The distinctive Raman peaks (546/719 cm−1Eg/B2g) in NiIrOx resemble those found in NiWIrOx, yet they exhibit a shift towards higher frequencies in NiWIrOx (549/721 cm−1 Eg/B2g). Given that the former relates to the stretching of Ir-O bonds within the octahedral (IrO6) units42,43, we can reasonably deduce that W atoms exhibit a preference for occupying the position of Ir in the octahedral structure. Furthermore, peak intensity of the Eg/B2g bands in NiWIrOx is reduced compared to NiIrOx, which implies a decrease in the proportion of Ir in octahedral coordination possibly due to the substitution of W in the form of W-O-Ir.

To provide an in-depth analysis, high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) was utilized, along with elemental mapping. The HAADF-STEM images depicted in Fig. 2m, n corroborate that the aggregated particle structures of NiWIrOx consist of nanocrystals. It was observed that only crystalline planes corresponding to Ir (200) were visible in the fast Fourier transform image after the dealloying process as shown in Fig. 2n. Lattice patterns indicative of W-O related with tungsten oxides were not observed. It should be noted that determining the presence of isolated W in the crystalline structure of NiWIrOx through Z contrast analysis is inconclusive. This is because the signal intensity of a particular element is directly proportional to its atomic number39. In this case, the heavier Ir and W atoms appear equally bright compared to the lighter Ni atoms. HAADF-STEM-EDS also established the uniform W-O incorporation into the NiIrOx structure (Fig. 2o).

Electrocatalytic performance for acid OER

The electrochemical characteristics of NiWIrOx, NiIrOx, and IrOx-Com catalysts were assessed in a 0.5 M sulfuric acid (H2SO4) electrolyte. Initially, the NiWIrOx and NiIrOx catalysts underwent an electrochemical dealloying process at 20 mA·cm−2 for 2 h to establish a stable polarization curve (Fig. 3a and Fig. S2). Afterwards, the NiWIrOx composite demonstrated significantly enhanced performance in the OER, displaying an overpotential of 180 mV at 10 mA·cm−2, which is approximately 50 and 60 mV lower than that observed for NiIrOx and the commercial IrOx catalysts, respectively (Fig. 3a). At a potential 1.5 VRHE, the OER current density for NiWIrOx measures 270 mA·cm−2, which is 4.5 and 9 times higher than those of NiIrOx (60 mA·cm−2) and Com. IrOx (30 mA·cm−2), respectively. The Tafel slope of 35 mV·dec-1 exhibited on NiWIrOx is significantly lower compared to that on NiIrOx, IrOx-Com, and Ir-ED, as illustrated in the Fig. 3b. Although the intricacies of the multiple OER steps make it challenging to deduce the mechanism solely from the Tafel slope, the low value may imply a favorable electrocatalytic kinetics. A comparison of the OER performance and Tafel slope with previously reported Ir-based catalysts is depicted in Fig. 3c and summarized in Table S2, which confirm the superior performance and kinetics of NiWIrOx catalyst for acid OER. The activity of the NiWIrOx electrode was also examined using sample current voltammetry (SCV) to eliminate the potential exaggeration of activity measured with potentiodynamic polarization LSV curves, which may be influenced by double-layer charging current (Fig. S10)44. Fig. S10 displays the comparative OER activity obtained for NiWIrOx using both LSV (at 1 and 5 mV·s−1) and SCV techniques. The scan rate shows a clear effect on the current density in the potential widow from 1.2 to 1.4 VRHE, which is most likely due to the double-layer charging. Above 1.4 V, where the OER starts, negligible difference was observed using difference scan rates and testing methods.

We also examined the electrochemical behavior of NiWIrOx in 1 M HClO4, which has the same pH as that of 0.5 M H2SO4 (Fig. S11). No significant electrolyte effect was observed, indicating that the surface chemistry of Ir was not interfered by the sulfate adsorption.

To gain insights into the intrinsic activity of the catalysts, we calculated the electrochemically active surface area by measuring the electrochemical double-layer capacitance (Cdl) (see Fig. S12). The Cdl values for NiWIrOx, NiIrOx, IrOx-Com, Ir-ED and IrW-ED are as follows: 657, 464, 167, 9.5, and 1.5 mF·cm−2, respectively. With a typical specific capacitance (Cs) of 35 mF·cm−2 for metals and 60 mF·cm−2 for metal oxides, we can calculate the roughness factor (Rf) values for NiWIrOx, NiIrOx, IrOx-Com, Ir-ED and IrW-ED, which turn out to be 10950, 7733, 2783, and 158 and 25 respectively (Fig. S12). Notably, the Rf value of the NiWIrOx catalyst is 1.41 times larger than that of NiIrOx and exceeds that of IrOx-Com nanoparticles. This suggests a significantly enlarged active surface area probably due to nanopore formation through dealloying. The intrinsic activity, which is determined by normalizing the geometric area activity to the roughness factor (Rf) and volumetric charge (Q) as shown in Fig. 3d and Fig. S13, unveils a jspecific and jQ of 24.5 µA·cm−2 and 377 µA ·mC−1 for NiWIrOx. This specific activity is 4.3 and 3 times greater than that of NiIrOx (5.68 µA·cm−2 and 122 µA·mC−1) and IrOx-Com (8.4 µA·cm−2 and 106 µA ·mC−1), respectively (Fig. 3d and Fig. S13). The Nyquist plot obtained from Electrochemical Impedance Spectroscopy (EIS) in Fig. 3e reveals variations in the resistance components during the OER at 1.50 VRHE. The charge transfer resistance (RCT) of NiWIrOx measures approximately 0.39 ohms·cm2, suggesting a faster electron transfer rate during the OER in comparison to NiIrOx (1.2 ohms·cm2) and IrOx-Com (1.47 ohms·cm2). Moreover, the NiWIrOx exhibits slightly lower ohmic resistance (1.11 ohms·cm2) than both NiIrOx (1.15 ohms·cm2) and IrOx-Com (2.79 ohms·cm2). This can be attributed to lower contact resistance between the substrate and the catalyst layer, which underscores one of the advantages of the deposition method over spray coating.

This heightened catalytic activity is further confirmed through mass activity calculations, with a peak value of 450 A·gIr−1 achieved at an overpotential of η = 270 mV. This figure stands at 6.83 and 35 times higher than that of NiIrOx (63 A·gIr−1) and the commercial IrOx-Com (12 A·gIr−1), respectively, as demonstrated in Fig. 3f and Fig. S13. In the meantime, TOF (Turnover Frequency) values are calculated by considering the current density at η = 270 mV, the NiWIrOx exhibits the highest TOF at 0.213 s−1, surpassing NiIrOx (0.031 s−1) and IrOx-Com (0.006 s−1) by a factor of 6.87 and 35.5, respectively, as depicted in Fig. 3f and Fig. S13. Thus, the experiment results demonstrate the enhanced site-specific activity of Ir due to the presence of W-O-Ir motifs.

A small resurgence of Ir0 was detected on NiIrOx, and NiWIrOx (Fig. 5a, b). This observation suggests the presence of metallic Ir species in the proximity of the surface11. Analyzing the O 1 s spectra before and after OER stability tests, we identify the presence of three distinct oxygen species on the catalyst. The peaks observed at 533 and 531.5 eV are indicative of adsorbed water, and M-OH, respectively. The peak detected at 530.5 eV is corresponds to M-O, as illustrated in Fig. 5c11. The shift to higher binding energies in the O 1 s in NiWIrOx as compared to NiIrOx signifies a electronic interaction among Ir and W11. This shift suggests electron density transfer from W to Ir, thus indicating a charge redistribution occurring within NiWIrOx due to the doping of W-O into NiIrOx.

The normalized Q activity and OH-surface concentration was calculated and compared for the catalysts. NiWIrOx shows a much higher Q activity with a 91% of OH/O + OH% surface concentration, compared to NiIrOx with a low Q activity and a 97% of OH/O + OH% surface concentration (Fig. 5d). This observation aligns well with the research conducted by Reier et al.49, which states an optimal OH/O + OH% surface concentration of approximately 90-95% is crucial and a decrease in activity is observed when the OH/O + OH% surface concentration surpasses 95%. It is suggested that the OH surface groups on iridium play a pivotal role as precursors for the formation of active (OII-δ) active sites50. Within the optimal range, a higher OH/O + OH% surface concentration was associated with an increased OER activity. The optimal value as high as 90-95% for OH/O + OH% surface concentration indicates the significance of a minor contribution from lattice oxygen. However, if all oxygen species were to occupy the surface as OH groups like the case with a OH/O + OH% surface concentration of 100%, it would lead to the absence of lattice oxygen connecting the individual IrOx clusters (Fig. 5d and Fig. S11)49. The lack of interconnection would impede electron transport across the catalyst layer. Therefore, the relatively low specific activity of NiIrOx in comparison to NiWIrOx could potentially be attributed to the absence of flexible structural motifs within the high OH/O + OH% surface concentration range (Fig. S11)51. The presence of W-O in NiWIrOx facilitates the generation of the optimal amount of hydroxide groups in comparison to NiIrOx (Fig. 5d, e). This characteristic helps prevent the amorphization and loss of lattice oxygen during the OER.

Theoretical calculations conducted by Ping et al. have indicated that, in OER conditions, the surface OH sites undergo deprotonation, transforming into the active sites on IrO2 surfaces52. Furthermore, an in-operando X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) investigation by Pedersen et al. revealed that an IrOx surface becomes entirely coated with deprotonated oxy species (Ir-O and W-O) at large OER potentials53. Identical to solid Bronsted acid catalysts33, the integration of W-O in NiIrOx can successfully regulate Obri’s and OLatt proton adsorption and desorption. The oxy species (Ir-O and W-O) was linearly concurrent with charge transfer, implying generation through an deprotonation/oxidation of surface hydroxyl groups54,55.

The creation of the intermediate Ir3+ can follow two pathways: the metallic Ir0 can directly dissolve, resulting in the formation of soluble Ir3+. Alternatively, it can transform into Ir3+ through the decomposition of Ir4+O2OH, leading to the release of O2 and the formation of surface H Ir3+O2 intermediates (Fig. S16). The detection of Ir3+ and Ir0 in the ex-situ state could potentially act as a precursor for the active species (e.g. Ir4+) predominantly observed in the in-situ state. Consequently, despite the differences between the ex-situ and in situ states, it seems reasonable to establish a correlation between the surface concentration of Ir3+ and Ir0 (Fig. 5e–f) and the observed surface concentration of OH/O + OH% (Fig. 5d). It was observed that following the dealloying, NiWIrOx exhibited a slightly lower presence of low-valence Ir species (Ir3+ and Ir0) compared to NiIrOx, During the extensive 312-h OER test at 1.8 VRHE, it is observed no significant alterations in the peak position and concentration of the Ir4+, Ir3+, Ir0 and lattice species (M-O) on the NiWIrOx surface as compared to that of NiIrOx (Fig. 5a, c, f). This indicates that the Ir-O-W moiety developed on the NiWIrOx surface exhibit considerable stability and activity over the long-term OER assessment (Fig. 5e–f). The stability is likely attributable to the electron-donating and charge balancing function of W, while in NiIrOx during 24 h OER the charge balancing was done by loss of Ir3+, Ir0 and lattice oxygen (Fig. 5c, f and Fig. S16). These findings also align with ICP-OES data presented in Fig. 4g. Additionally, we can draw insights from the work of Kasian and colleagues56 who highlighted the relationship between Ir dissolution and electrode potential. Specifically, at sufficiently high potentials, the preferable pathway involves the further oxidation of Ir5+ or Ir6+ (Fig. S17 and Fig. 4g). The pathways proposed in the literature for OER-induced Ir dissolution primarily involve the formation of soluble Ir3+ or Ir > 6+ species, as illustrated in Figs. S16, S17. To ensure the sustained durability of the catalyst, it is crucial to establish a robust structure that impedes the generation of unstable Ir³⁺ or Ir⁶⁺ species, as depicted in Fig. S17. In our scenario, the W-O presence in NiWIrOx self-supported layer with (200) crystalline plane represents an optimal configuration to attain this goal.

From the W 4 f spectra observed during the OER (Fig. 5b), the differentiation of peaks uncovers the presence of W+6 states46. The hypervalent oxidation of W has the capability to adsorb excess oxygen atoms during electrocatalysis, serving to prevent further oxidation of the active Ir oxides to higher valences, such as the unstable Ir6+ species. This preventative action helps in preventing the dissolution of Ir oxides in acidic environments46. The W with lower electronegativity is anticipated to initially bind oxygen intermediates. Subsequently, the in situ-formed W-O-Ir species effectively curtail further electron loss and the oxidation of surface-active Ir oxides into soluble Ir. This, in turn, mitigates a crucial intermediate responsible for catalytic degradation. All the above results indicate that the formation of W-O-Ir in NiIrOx balanced the charge triggered by caustically nickel removing and enhanced the activity and stability by stabilizing the intermediates formed from deprotonation of hydroxylated Ir3+ and Ir4+ precursors.

The X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) spectra of the catalysts in Fig. 5g exhibit a consistent spectral shape and edge position, indicating that the oxidation state of iridium in the bulk phase is nearly identical and nominally at +439. When consider the XPS results shown in Fig. 5a, it becomes evident that the surface iridium in both NiIrOx and NiWIrOx primarily exists in oxidation states of +3 and +4, while the bulk iridium remains in the +4 oxidation state. The extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) spectra in k2 weighted R-space and K-space, as well as the fitting results, are presented in Fig. 5i, j, Fig. S18 and Fig. S19, respectively, where their corresponding fitting parameters are listed in Table S3.

The fitted EXAFS parameters presented in Table S3 reveal a first Ir-O shell distance of 1.98 Å and 1.97 Å within the [IrO6] octahedron for NiIrOx and NiWIrOx, respectively. The fitted EXAFS data reveal shorter Ir–O bond lengths (1.97 Å) in NiWIrOx compared to NiIrOx (1.98 Å) and IrO2 (2.0 Å) (Table S3). This reduced Ir–O bond length aligns with the concept of covalency contraction, as originally defined by Shannon and Vincent57,58. This concept is quantitatively represented by a parameter, Rd, which is defined for transition metal halogens and chalcogens. Rd measures the cube of the mean M–X (X = F, N, O etc.) bond length relative to that of MgmXn57 and accounts for the bond shortening resulting from increased covalency. Rd is inversely proportional to the covalency of the M–X bond.

The Rd values for NiIrOx and NiWIrOx, after 2 h of dealloying, are 0.849 and 0.825, respectively. Both values are lower than that of rutile-type IrO2 (0.869), indicating an increase in the covalency of the Ir–O bond in the following order: rutile-type IrO2 < NiIrOx < NiWIrOx. Both NiIrOx and NiWIrOx exhibit shortened surface Ir–O bond lengths, as depicted in Fig. 5h and Fig. 5i. Specifically, the Ir–O distances in NiIrOx are contracted by 0.02 Å, and those in NiWIrOx are reduced by 0.03 Å in comparison to IrO2 (Table S3). Hence, the remarkably short Ir–O distance observed in NiIrOx and NiWIrOx strongly suggests a iridium oxidation state greater than IrO2 (Fig. 5g, i).

The observed loss of nickel is not surprising, as there is a strong thermodynamic driving force for the dissolution of nickel in both NiIrOx and NiWIrOx. The NiWIrOx exhibits better charge neutrality when nickel dissolves compared to NiIrOx (Fig. S11). In the absence of a charge mediator, charge compensation occurs through a change in the oxidation state of iridium, along with the loss of lattice oxygen39. After a 2-hour dealloying process, the Ir L3-edge of NiWIrOx (Fig. 5g) reveals a reduction in white-line intensity, indicating a decrease in the oxidation state of iridium when compared to NiIrOx39. The reduced white line intensity observed at the Ir L3-edge indicates that additional electrons are introduced into the Ir 5 d states of NiWIrOx during electrochemical oxidation via W-O-Ir. This leads to an increase in the electron density around Ir, as illustrated in Fig. 5g. This outcome suggests that NiWIrOx is protected from overoxidation during the OER process, and the incorporation of W-O in NiIrOx effectively stabilities the charge imbalance resulting from Ni leaching (Fig. S16).

On the other hand, the white line intensity for NiIrOx increases following dealloying at the same current density, which suggesting the raising of the oxidation state and reduction of the electron density around the Ir atom (Fig. 5g). This increase serves to counterbalance the loss of Ni and maintain overall charge neutrality. Concurrently, the oxide undergoes a gradual restoration of its lattice oxygen through the filling of oxygen vacancies, which in turn leads to the re-establishment of Ir-O coordination. The loss of Ni has a destabilizing effect on the structure rich in defects, causing Ir to undergo reoxidation and give rise to a mixture of octahedral clusters featuring Ir3+ and Ir4+ states, as illustrated in Fig. 5a for NiIrOx. Consequently, the final structure manifests as a disordered amalgamation of these Ir3+/Ir4+ octahedra, a result of the coordinated diffusion of anions (O) and cations (Ni) during the dealloying process.

Before the OER, the Ir white-line intensity of NiWIrOx was slightly higher than that of IrO2. After OER, the Ir white-line intensity of NiWIrOx was slightly lower than that of IrO2, with the peak position shifting 0.09 eV to lower energy (Fig. S20). This shift and lowering of Ir white-line intensity in NiWIrOx suggests electron density transfer from W to Ir site during prolonged OER. Consequently, a significant amount of Ir0 and Ir3+ remained within the NiWIrOx structure post OER, indicating an increased electron density around the Ir atoms. In addition, Fig. 5h shows an inverse trend for the W L3-edge compared to that of the Ir L3-edge, which also indicates a substantial electron transfer from W to Ir sites. The doping of W resulted in a higher W white-line intensity in NiWIrOx compared to WO3 before OER, indicating a decrease in electron density of W sites. After OER, the W white-line intensity of NiWIrOx further increased, indicating a further decrease of electron density around W due to the electron transfer to Ir sites. The XAS results before and after OER supports the conclusion that W donates electrons to Ir, which probably prevents over-oxidation of Ir active sites during OER process (Fig. 5h). Conversely, the Ir white-line peak intensity of NiIrOx was slightly lower than that of IrO2, but its peak position shifted 0.11 eV to higher energy after OER, indicating a reduction of the electron density of Ir sites due to the lack of transfer from W during prolonged OER (Fig. S20). As a result, a significant amount of Ir0 and Ir3+ leached from the NiIrOx structure post-OER (Fig. 4g).

These XAS findings regarding electron transfer from W to Ir are in good agreement with the XPS results related with the variation of Ir0 and Ir3+ content (Fig. 5a, c). This observation signifies that within the NiWIrOx system, charge balance is efficiently maintained through the process of W-O insertion. Notably, this charge balance is achieved primarily through the electron-donation effect of W-O rather than through the depletion of lattice oxygen or the elevation of the oxidation state of Ir in the near-surface region (Fig. 5a, c). In comparison to IrO2, the valence states of Ir in the near-surface region show an increase in NiWIrOx and NiIrOx. However, when the same current density is applied for dealloying, the oxidation states of Ir is lower on NiWIrOx than that on NiIrOx, as illustrated in Fig. 5g. This outcome proves that the incorporation of W-O into NiIrOx has a protective effect on the Ir sites, preventing them from undergoing excessive oxidation and subsequent dissolution, which is in alignment with the results obtained from ICP-OES, as depicted in Fig. 4g.

The average length of the Ir–O bond varies between 1.91 and 2.02 Å, depending on the number of deprotonated OI- entities present. For instance, the stoichiometry HIr3O8 exhibits an average bond length of 1.91 Å, while H4Ir3O8 has an average bond length of 2.02 Å22. Additionally, the Ir-O peak in NiWIrOx shifted negatively and shortened by 0.01 Å (to 1.96 Å) after OER, as shown in Fig. S18 and Table S3. In addition, Fig. S19 illustrates an opposite trend for the W-O peak position compared to the Ir-O peak position. The incorporation of W into NiIrOx led to a positive 0.01 Å shift in the W-O peak in NiWIrOx after OER. Conversely, the Ir-O peak in NiIrOx shifted positively and lengthened by 0.02 Å (to 2.02 Å) after OER, as depicted in Fig. S18 and Table S3. The shortening of Ir-O bond induced by W-O insertion may enhance efficiency of deprotonating OII-H to OI- during the OER process. This makes OI- more readily available for water attack, leading to the formation of *OOH as compared to NiIrOx and IrO2. The effectiveness of deprotonation in NiWIrOx is further substantiated by the MOR and XPS studies, as shown in Fig. 5 and Fig. S23. This phenomenon may be correlated with the higher OER activity and stability of NiWIrOx in comparison to NiIrOx.

Identification of acidic OER mechanism of the NiWIrOx

In the realm of electrocatalysts containing Ir, there are generally two catalytic mechanisms at play for OER: the adsorbate evolution mechanism (AEM) and the lattice oxygen participation mechanism (LOM) (Fig. 6a–c). Under the AEM, the catalysts primarily offer suitable sites on the Ir surface for the adsorption of crucial reaction intermediates, such as HO*, O*, and HOO*. Conversely, in the LOM, these catalysts contribute lattice oxygen atoms to facilitate the generation of O2. To elucidate the primary mechanism governing OER, we carried out in situ analysis with isotope labeling. The experiments involved in situ Raman spectroscopy testing using 16O and 18O labeled H2O electrolytes. For NiIrOx and NiWIrOx, we observed two bands at 540–550 and 710–720 cm−1 (see Fig. 6d–f), which are attributed to the Eg and B2g modes of Ir–O vibration, respectively42. When relating Raman spectra taken in H216O and H218O electrolytes at the OER potential, the Eg and B2g peaks in NiIrOx were negatively shifted from 546.6 to 541.6 cm−1 and 719.9 to 710.2 cm−1, while no significant change was observed in the Eg and B2g peaks in NiWIrOx. Furthermore, when relating Raman spectra recorded in a mixed H216O + H218O electrolyte at the OER potential, NiIrOx exhibited a broadened B2g peak ranging from 690.3 to 719.9 cm−1. Under the same condition, NiWIrOx showed no evident changes. Replacement of lattice Ir–16O with Ir–18O resulted in frequency shifts in vibration modes59. The shift in the peak frequency in NiIrOx indicated the participation of lattice oxygen during the OER process, while the participation is counteracted by the introduction of W as no evident shift was observed in NiWIrOx. These observations provide compelling evidence that LOM is not the predominant factor governing OER catalysis facilitated by the NiWIrOx catalyst. Therefore, the LOM pathway can be excluded for NiWIrOx catalyst for OER process.

Recent research has shown that other transition metal oxides, such as Co-Ru catalysts60, exhibit heterogeneous dual sites (DS)-AEM reaction pathways rather than conventional single site (SS)-AEM for OER. The previous studies suggest a degree of universality in the strategy, indicating that the DS-AEM mechanism may be applicable to a range of transition metal catalysts. However, the specific applicability and effectiveness of this strategy may vary depending on the catalyst composition, structure, and reaction conditions. Density functional theory (DFT) calculation is performed to elucidate the catalytic properties and mechanisms underlying the improved OER performance of the NiIrOx system with bridged W-O moiety. The presence of dual W-O-Ir and single Ir site are assessed on NiWIrOx catalyst, respectively, to compare the SS-AEM and DS-AEM pathway during OER process.

Unlike previous studies where surface construction normally induced the formation of amorphous layer of IrxOyH, this research indicates that nickel leaching did not lead to such structural changes in this system. Instead, a crystal-to-crystal phase transition is observed. Given the unique nature and the specific objectives of this study, the focus of DFT calculations is not on surface construction or amorphization. For the models used for DFT calculation in this study, it is to mimic the real surface of the catalyst after the reconstruction by introducing the W-O moieties and trace amount of Ni in the IrO2 crystal structure. The illustration figures and details of the structure models for NiWIrOx, NiIrOx, and IrO2 catalysts are shown as Fig. S22 and Supplementary Data 1.

Consequently, it is conducted a theoretical examination of the catalytic mechanisms occurring on the IrO2 (200) facet during the OER. The IrO2 (200) facet presents a higher concentration of active sites (7 Ir active sites per nm2), compared to the IrO2 (110) facet (5 Ir active sites per nm2) as depicted in Fig. S639. Notably, the bridge site on the (110) surface exhibits no catalytic activity during the OER process, primarily because oxygen molecules binding to the bridge sites (considered inert sites) possess a stronger bond compared to their attachment to the CUS recognized as active sites (Fig. S6)39.

Considering the surface of NiWIrOx is hydroxylated, the structural model for DFT calculation is constructed with 50%-tOH termination on the surface. This percentage is chosen to model the surface state under OER operation conditions. According to the previous theoretical studies52, the surface of IrO2 with 50%-tOH termination is the most stable one at the OER working potential of 1.4 ~ 1.8 V. The illustration of the structural models with OH termination can be found in Fig. S22a–c. Besides, the solvent effect, which may influence the adsorption free energies and theoretical overpotentials, is also considered during the DFT calculation.

Fig. 6g compares the results of DFT calculation between DS-AEM and SS-AEM reaction pathway on NiWIrOx catalyst. In both theory and practice, Ir sites exhibit considerably higher activity when compared to W sites. The results in Fig. 6g indicate that the OER process is primarily impeded by the conversion of *O to *OOH intermediates, which is the potential determining steps (PDSs). The cooperative W-O-Ir sites lower the thermodynamic energies barrier associated with PDSs from 2.14 to 1.92 eV in comparison with the single Ir sites. The conversion of *OH intermediates to *O on cooperative W-O-Ir sites (0.39 eV) proceed more readily through the subsequent dehydrogenation when compared to single sites (0.68 eV). Consequently, the NiWIrOx are prone to follow DS-AEM instead of SS-AEM pathway, and the thermodynamic OER overpotential is decreased by 0.22 V with W-O-Ir dual site (Fig. 6g).

Furthermore, the free energies associated with the adsorption of HO* decreases in the presence of Ni, indicating a stronger binding energy for HO* on NiIrOx compared to IrO2 following the conventional AEM pathway (Fig. S22d). The introduction of W mitigates the strong adsorption for HO* and facilitates the subsequent deprotonation step. NiWIrOx exhibits a balanced difference between the formation of O* and OH* when compared to NiIrOx and IrO2 in the context of the acidic OER. The thermodynamic energies barrier of PSD has been reduced to 2.14 eV for NiWIrOx compared to 2.55 eV in NiIrOx and 2.61 eV in IrO2 following the conventional AEM pathway.

Taking into consideration that XPS and XANES results identifying the overarching changes in the electronic structure of NiIrOx by W substitution, a Bader charge analysis is conducted to examine the partial alterations in the elemental charge distribution on the catalysts near surface during *OOH adsorption (Fig. 7a). The substituted W atom in NiIrOx affects the positive charge of the oxygen bridging Ir atom, reducing it from +1.75|e| to +1.63|e | , and making slight adjustments to the charge distribution of neighboring Ir sites. Furthermore, the Bader charge (+1.27|e | ) of the substituted W atom in NiWIrOx is noticeably higher than that of the original Ir atom in IrO2 (+0.99|e | ), yet it remains lower than the charge of Ir atoms in NiIrOx (+1.52|e | ). This observation is in good agreement with the findings from the Ir XPS and Ir XANES analysis. Besides, an analysis of the bond lengths and bond angles within the W-O-Ir and Ir-O-Ir units is conducted by theoretical calculation as depicted in Fig. 7a. Both the structure characteristics of symmetry and bond length play key roles in the collaborative dual-site OER pathway61. A high symmetry along with a reduced atomic distance of 2.67 Å between W and Ir is observed in the dual-site adsorption of *OOH. Owing to the symmetry and collaborative coordination nature of the W-O-Ir, the DS-AEM reaction pathway is facilitated with a notably low thermodynamic energy barrier on the NiWIrOx catalyst.

The above findings suggest that the OER over the NiWIrOx catalyst may proceed via an unconventional pathway driven by the presence of heteroatomic W-O-Ir units. This unique reaction mechanism involves the adsorption of oxygen intermediates on dual-site W-O-Ir units. In this context, the binuclear W-O-Ir structure is responsible for providing redox flexibility, which is expected to be highly advantageous for OER reactions involving multiple electron transfers. The presence of dual-metal sites with specific atomic distances is crucial for facilitating the adsorption and desorption of oxygen, as well as promoting efficient deprotonation and minimizing energy barriers.

To explore the adsorption and desorption of reaction intermediates, cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements were conducted using a N2-saturated 0.5 M H2SO4 electrolyte. Fig. 7b illustrates the CV curves for the freshly prepared electrocatalysts, including NiWIrOx, NiIrOx, and IrO2. There are two opinions about the *OOH formation on Ir active center before O2 evolution. One is through the Ir4+ oxidized to Ir5+ just before the OER onset potential. The other suggests that it is energetically favorable for the oxide to accommodate electrophilic OII- rather than further oxidize Ir4+ to Ir5+ due to the diffuse nature of the Ir 5 d orbitals and the metal’s high oxidation state (+4) in IrO250,62. But in both cases the Ir4+ need to be deprotonated before reaction with H2O to form OOH on Ir active site. Based on the L3-edge white line peak positions, it can be observed that NiIrOx, NiWIrOx, and IrO2 exhibit a consistent +4 bulk oxidation state.

The intensity of the white line peak may indicate the oxidation state and electron density around the Ir, as discussed earlier. Consistent with the XPS peak positions and XAS data for the L3-edge white line peak, NiIrOx, NiWIrOx, and IrO2 show a preference for the incorporation of OII- rather than further oxidizing Ir4+ to Ir5+ (Fig. 5g). As Ni departs from NiIrOx and NiWIrOx, water reacts with the resulting nickel vacancy, leading to the formation of protonated species, namely Ir-OII-H-Ir and Ir-OII-H-W (Fig. S16)49. These species subsequently undergo deprotonation, resulting in the creation of Ir-OI-Ir and Ir-OI-W at higher potentials, approximately around 1.4 VRHE, prior to the onset of the OER, as illustrated in the Fig. 7b.

The deprotonation process of the iridium active site is investigated, which is driven by the potential. This process holds significant importance as it represents a crucial step in the formation of the key intermediate O* and can be expressed as follows: Ir-OH → Ir-O* + H + + e-. Comparing the NiIrOx and Com-IrOx catalysts to the NiWIrOx sample, it becomes apparent that their surfaces exhibit a greater affinity for OH-group coating. This observation is supported by the results obtained from O 1 s XPS analyses, as illustrated in Fig. 5c. Furthermore, the deprotonation capacity of the hydroxyl groups can be assessed by conducting cyclic voltammetry measurements, which measures the oxidative charge stored on the catalyst surface and offers insights into the deprotonation ability63,64.

Through the integration of the anodic peak response to voltage, the amount of stored oxidative charges can be determined, as illustrated in Fig. 7c, d and Fig. S21. Notably, it is evident that NiWIrOx exhibits a higher charge storage capacity (760 mC·cm−2) compared to NiIrOx (360 mC·cm−2) and Com-IrOx (220 mC·cm−2). The integrated charge was employed to calculate the electrochemically assessable active sites (ECSA). ECSA offers valuable insights into the electron transitions associated with metallic Ir, as exemplified by cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves (Fig. 7b and Fig. S21). This metric directly correlates with the number of OER-active sites present on the electrode’s surface. NiWIrOx exhibits a significantly higher ECSA value (2200 cmecs²·mgIr⁻¹) compared to both NiIrOx (877 cmecs²·mgIr−1) and IrOx (187 cmecs²·mgIr−1) (Fig. 7d).

To gain a deeper understanding of the deprotonation capacity, we conducted MOR studies for NiWIrOx and NiIrOx in the presence and absence of 0.5 M H2SO4 + 1 M methanol34. It was observed that OH-terminated surfaces exhibit the highest stability for both NiWIrOx and NiIrOx, particularly under low potentials ranging from 1 VRHE to 1.4 VRHE, with deprotonation occurring in the voltage range of 1.4 VRHE to 1.8 VRHE (Fig. 7b and Fig. S23). Notably, the preference for the deprotonation process was evident on the surface of NiWIrOx and NiIrOx, specifically with Ir sites being covered by O* at higher potentials within the 1.4 to 1.8 VRHE range (as indicated in Fig. S23). The NiWIrOx catalyst rapidly converted *OH to *O before reaching 1.7 VRHE, as shown by the area under the MOR LSV curve. While NiIrOx retained both *OH and O* after reaching 1.7 VRHE (Fig. S23). The NiWIrOx surface demonstrated a greater capability to generate O*, owing to its higher OER activity compared to MOR in the voltage range of 1.4 to 1.8 VRHE. This suggests an efficient deprotonation of *OH to *O at a lower potential than NiIrOx, as illustrated in the corresponding Fig. S23.

Moreover, a significant quantity of hydroxide ions is absorbed onto the Ir-enriched surface, where the valence states of Ir3+/Ir4+ coexist as depicted in Fig. 5. The cathodic peak positions in the CV correspond to the potential for hydroxide desorption, as shown in Fig. 7b. These peaks occur in the following order: NiWIrOx (0.81 VRHE) > NiIrOx (0.78 VRHE) > IrOx (0.77 VRHE). The correlation between OH desorption potential and the anodic and cathodic peak current density for the Ir3+/Ir4+ redox process demonstrates that greater OH- desorption potential corresponds to enhanced OER activity (Fig. 7c). This positive shift in OH desorption potential suggests a weaker binding affinity for oxygen intermediates, thereby expediting the release of O2, as illustrated in Fig. 7c65.

MOR tests are also utilized to probe *OH and unveil the inherent energy profiles that dictate the kinetics of OER on different catalysts (Figs S23a–d). If *OH adsorption serves as the RDS in the OER process, it follows that the catalyst surface should remain devoid of *OH since the subsequent steps occur rapidly34. The surface *OH coverage, θ[*OH], can be calculated by taking the ratio of the integrated area under the MOR current density curve to that under the OER current density curve. Because the OER current is almost zero below 1.4 VRHE, the potential range of 1.4–1.8 VRHE is used to calculate the value of θ[*OH]. The OH* formation dominates under low potential (1.4 VRHE to 1.6 VRHE), while the OH* conversion tends to dominate the surface of NiWIrOx and NiIrOx at high potentials (1.6 VRHE to 1.8 VRHE) as illustrated in Fig. S23c, d. To probe the OH* coverage, we integrated area for MOR and OER in low and high potential regions, respectively. The values of θ[*OH] follow the trend: NiIrOx (2.48) > NiWIrOx (1.82) in the range of 1.4–1.6 VRHE (Fig. S23e–j) and NiIrOx (1.16) > NiWIrOx (1.03) in the range of 1.6–1.8 VRHE (Fig. S23g–l). The high [OH*] on NiIrOx suggests the stronger bonding of *OH and slow conversation of *OH to *O by deprotonations and subsequent breakage of M-*OH bond as compared to NiWIrOx, whereas the adsorption of *OH on NiWIrOx is relatively balanced. Furthermore, the high difference between the MOR and OER current densities (7 mA·cm−2@1.8 VRHE) for NiIrOx than NiWIrOx (−8 mA·cm−2 @1.8 VRHE) also showed that there is still *OH present on the surface of NiIrOx even at higher 1.8 VRHE potential (Fig. S23d, j, i), which demonstrates the strong affinity of NiIrOx for *OH. The higher OER activity of NiWIrOx than NiIrOx may originate from the relatively balanced bonding energy of *OH. Overall, the results of MOR test probe that the introduction of W-O in NiIrOx system could optimize the adsorption, desorption, and deprotonation of *OH on NiWIrOx surface.

The corrosion of metals is intricately linked to both the cohesive energy of the among metal (Ecoh) and the adsorption energy of oxygen intermediates (ΔEO)66. Transition metals from the 3d series with lower Ecoh values exhibit a reduced activation barrier for breaking bonds among the metals. Notably, Ecoh is contingent on the d-band filling and shells of the metal. Therefore, the following relationship is suggested: Ecoh decreases as the d-band filling increases, whereas Ecoh increases with a greater number of d-shells58. Consequently, W exhibits a higher Ecoh compared to Ni, leading to the suppression of element dissolution. In the case of Ir, characterized by a high Ecoh, the dissolution of Ir is strongly influenced by ΔEO. A metal with a high ΔEO can readily form thicker oxides during the adsorption process and can leave uncoordinated metal sites during the desorption process, resulting in increased dissolution of the metal species66. As a result, the inclusion of W-O in the NiIrOx may weaken the adsorption of oxygen intermediates (ΔEO) at the Ir site. This weakening of adsorption suppresses the dissolution of Ir during the adsorption and desorption processes of oxygen intermediates.

In addition, the Pourbaix diagram of Ni/W/IrO2 mixtures (Fig. 7e) are mapped based on the database from Materials Project. Please noted that the Pourbaix diagram included the components of W, Ni, and Ir oxides, but did not include the NiWIrOx structure. The Pourbaix diagram of this simplified system can provide supplementary information of the phase stability for Ni-W-IrOx system. It can be found that under acidic condition (pH = 0 ~ 4) and OER work potential (1.4 ~ 2.0 V), W element is present in the form of high valence oxide, which valence state (6 + ) is the same as that of NiWIrOx measured by XPS and XAS. Besides, the crystal phase of W solid remains identical under these conditions. At the same time, Ni exists in an ionic state, and IrO2 dissolves at high oxidation potentials. For instance, when potential is higher than 1.8 V at pH value of 1.0, IrO2 is oxidized to IrO4-. It is shown in the diagram that WO3 is much more stable than NiO and IrO2 at high oxidation potential and acidic condition. Therefore, it is theoretically reasonable to infer that W-O incorporation can enhance the phase stability of NiIrOx under the working condition of OER. This conclusion is also corroborated by the results of post characterization on the catalysts. The HR-TEM analysis shows that the crystal structure of NiWIrOx remains the same as that before the test (Fig. S15). The HAAD-STEM/EDS analysis indicates a similar microstructure and element distribution after the durability test (Fig. S3). The ICP measurements of the electrolyte reveals a much lower Ir dissolution after the introduction of W (Fig. S16). Combined with the theoretical analysis, the phase stability of NiWIrOx under acidic condition and OER work potential can be confirmed.

Discussion

Nanoporous NiWIrOx electrocatalyst was constructed through a simple electrochemical method to tackle the activity-stability paradox of surface reconstructed IrOx by creating a bridged W-O moiety in NiIrOₓ. Unlike other nickel iridates-derived catalysts that typically result in the amorphous IrOxHy phase, the NiWIr nanoalloy transforms into an surface-hydroxylated rutile nanocatalyst with a (200) crystal plane orientation after electrochemical dealloying, which shows a high density of active Ir sites and a high catalytic activity for the OER under acidic conditions. Moreover, the NiWIrOx catalyst significantly reduces the Ir dissolution during OER, allowing for operational stability exceeding 300-h at a high current density of 300 mA·cm−2. It is shown by characterizations and calculations that incorporating W into NiIrOx could effectively customizes the electronic structure. The modified electronic structure of NiWIrOx might weaken the adsorption energy with reaction intermediates, which are beneficial for catalytic reactions and enhances stability in Ir-based electrocatalysts. Both experimental and theoretical results indicate that the collaborative coordination of W – O–Ir, along with a strong electron coupling effect, promotes the heterogeneous DS-AEM OER pathway. Simultaneously, this collaborative coordination allows the gain of electrons from surrounding electron-enriched W sites via bridging oxygen atoms, suppressing the overoxidation of active Ir centers into soluble acidic IrO42- species. The engineering of the composition in NiWIrOx significantly enhances electrochemical performance and the lifespan of the electrocatalyst by optimizing the Ecoh and EO. In conclusion, our study highlights the potential of the NiWIrOx platform as an effective strategy for tackling the challenge of the activity-stability paradox for Ir-based electrocatalyst, which also provides theoretical insight that may further inspire research effort to design cost-efficient and high-performing electrocatalysts for acid water electrolysis.

Methods

Materials

Chloroiridic acid (H2IrCl6·6H2O) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Shanghai, China). Nickel chloride hexahydrate (NiCl2·6H2O) oxalic acid ((COOH)2·2H2O), ammonium chloride (NH4Cl), boric acid (H3BO3) and trisodium citrate (C6H5O7Na3) were purchased from Macklin. 40 wt.% platinum on graphite carbon (Pt/C) was purchased from Johnson Matthey. Deionized water (DI) with a resistance of 18.25 MΩ cm−1 was used in all the experiments. All the above chemicals were of analytical grade and used as received without further purification.

Characterizations

The crystal structure of the materials was examined using X-ray diffraction (XRD) on a PANalytical Empyrean with Cu Kα irradiation (λ = 1.5406 Å). The structure and morphology of the catalysts were characterized by field emission scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Thermo Fisher/Apreo 2S), transmission electron microscope (TEM, JEOL-F200), high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) JEOL-F200 respectively. Surface electronic properties determined by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Fisher K-Alpha + ) with monochromatized Al Ka X-ray and all spectra corrected by C 1 s 284.8 eV. Analysis of Ir loading amount and element metal content using an inductively coupled plasma spectrometer (ICP-OES, Pekin Elmer Avio 200). We used the ultrasonication to collect samples for HRTEM and STEM after electrodeposition and electrochemical dealloying.

Preparation of catalysts

An electrochemical workstation (CHI760e) was used to accomplish NiWIr-ED electrodeposition in the typical three-electrode setup. The working electrode was a Ti fiber felt, the reference electrode was a saturated Ag/AgCl, and the counter electrode was a Pt-mesh. The Ti fiber felt surface had been treated using a 5 wt% oxalic acid (COOH)2·2H2O solution at 95 °C for 30 min to get rid of surfaces oxide, subsequently rinsed with deionized water, vacuum dried, and installed on a 1 cm2 home-made Teflon cell. The solution for electrodeposition was prepared by dissolving 0.1 M NH4Cl, trisodium citrate 0.441 g, 0.3 M H3BO3, 0.045 M NiCl2·6H2O, 0.009 M Na2WO4 and 0.005 M H2IrCl6·6H2O in deionized water (DI water, 18.2 mΩ), and stirring for 30 min. The salt solution was thereafter evacuated for 30 min with bubbling N2 gas to eliminate the dissolved O2. The electrodeposition was performed under ambient conditions using a steady voltage of −2.7 VAg/AgCl. The NiWIrOx was optimizied by varying the composition of Ni W and Ir. The NiIr-ED, IrW-ED and Ir-ED were also synthesized using the similar procedure without W, Ni and NiW precursors respectively as a control samples.

Electrodeposition of catalysts

The electrochemical deposition is a versatile and controllable process for depositing metals onto substrates, offering advantages such as high efficiency, uniform coating thickness, and the ability to coat complex shapes. When a voltage is applied between the anode and the cathode, a potential difference is established. This potential difference drives the migration of ions from the solution to the surface of the cathode. As metal ions are reduced at the cathode, they form a layer of solid metal that adheres to the substrate surface. The thickness and quality of the deposited metal layer depend on factors such as the current density applied potentials, deposition time, electrolyte composition, and temperature.

The reduction of [IrCl6]2− and WO42− ions could be catalyzed by absorbed hydrogen (H). However, due to the excessively strong adsorption of hydrogen on the Ti surface37, the reduction of [IrCl6]3− and WO42− hardly occurred on the Ti fiber, resulting in no obvious changes in the morphology after the deposition (Fig. S4). PEM Electrolyzer Measurements

To evaluate the application feasibility, we assessed the performance of the developed NiWIrOx catalyst under the environment mimicking industrial operations. In a PEMWE single cell, the NiWIrOx electrode was applied as the anode. The membrane electrode assembly (MEA) was sandwiching the anode and cathode (6.25 cm2 area per side) on both sides of the Nafion 212 (DuPont) membrane. For commercial MEA, the anode catalyst is IrO2 (2 mg cm−2) and the cathode catalyst is Pt/C (1 mg cm−2). Polarization curves were produced for PEMWE, injecting preheated DI water as a reactant into the anode while retaining the cell temperature at 80 °C. The ohmic resistance of the cell was measured to be 206.6 ± 1.8 mΩ⋅cm2 with a cell area of 6.25 cm2.

Operando Raman spectroscopy analysis

We performed in situ electrochemical Raman spectroscopy testing with isotope labeling to explore the involvement of lattice oxygen in the OER process. The spectroscopic measurements were conducted using a Renishaw inVia Raman spectrometer with a testing wavelength of 532 nm. In our setup, a custom-made electrolytic cell was utilized, with a saturated Ag/AgCl electrode serving as the reference electrode and a Pt wire as the counter electrode. The working electrode consisted of the catalyst loaded directly onto a titanium felt substrate after dealloying. Raman spectra were collected using chronoamperometry with an applied potential of 1.6 V. Three different electrolytes were employed for testing: standard 0.5 M sulfuric acid solution in H216O, 0.5 M sulfuric acid solution in H218O, and 0.5 M sulfuric acid solution in mixture of H216O and H218O.

XPS fitting and data evaluation

The Ti-O contributions to the O 1 s spectra were fitted in analogy to an uncoated Titanium fiber felt (TFL) that underwent the same electrochemical protocol (TiOx-OER) and subsequently excluded when calculating the OH concentration. This is plausible as TiOx does not show any OER-activity in the investigated potential range68. All other samples show TiOx contributions below 0.3% and thus, Ti-O contributions in the O 1 s spectra were neglected on those samples. The Ir3+-surface concentration depicts the amount of reduced iridium in the surface. The Ir3+-surface concentration was calculated by dividing the Ir3+- contribution of the Ir4f spectra by the sum of Ir3+, Ir4+ and Ir0 respectively. The M-OH surface concentration was calculated by dividing the OH contribution of the O1s spectra by the sum of -OH, -H2O and -O lattice oxygen contribution.

XAS analysis

All XAFS data were analyzed using the program Demeter. For all samples, the EXAFS oscillations were extracted from the normalized XAS spectra by subtracting the atomic background using a quadratic spline fit to k2-weighted data, where k is the photoelectron wave number. The χ(k) functions were then Fourier transformed into R-space. The Fourier-transform window was in the k range 3–12 Å−1.

Theoretical calculations

The IrO2 (200) surfaces were modeled with a slab of four atomic layers (Fig. S22a–c) in which the bottom one layer was frozen, and a vacuum layer of about 20 Å along the z-axis was built. The (200) plane was chosen according to the testing results of HR-TEM, which indicated the preferential exposing of (200) plane in the catalysts. For W and Ni-doped IrO2, the Ir sites were substituted with one Ni and two W atoms (Fig. S22a). The concentration of the dopants was chosen to be 4.2% for Ni and 8.3% for W, respectively, which values are close to the testing results of ICP and EDS. For Ni doping, the Ni atom was placed in the second layer instead of the first layer, because the content of Ni element was much lower in the surface edge than that in the inner parts as shown in the EDS mapping results. For W doing, one W atom was placed in the first layer and the other one in the third layer, as shown in the EDS mapping that the dispersion of W is relatively uniform in the catalyst. The surface termination in the structural model included 50%-tOH termination which position were marked with yellow circles in Fig. S22a–c. The percentage of 50% was chosen to model the surface state under OER operation conditions. According to the previous theoretical studies52, the surface of IrO2 with 50%-tOH termination is most stable at the OER working potential.

All spin-polarized density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed with the plane-wave basis set as implemented in the Vienna Ab Initio Simulation Package (VASP)69, and the electrons and ions interactions were described by the projector augmented wave (PAW) potential70,71. The exchange–correlation interactions were determined by the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA)72,73. The plane wave energy cutoff of 500 eV, and the convergence criterion for the residual force and energy was set to 0.05 eV Å−1 and 10−5 eV, respectively. The empirical correction in Grimme’s method (DFT + D3) was used to describe the van der Waals (vdW) interactions74. The Brillouin region was sampled by the Monkhorst-Pack method with a 2 × 2 × 1 k-point mesh. The PBEsol functional was used by setting GGA = PS in INCAR. The effect of solvation effects on catalytic activity was considered by using the VASPsol implicit solvation model since the reaction takes place in an aqueous solution74–76. Besides, PH = 0 is assumed in the acidic medium in this study77.

Probing of oxygen intermediates by methanol oxidation reaction

Probing the *OH species via MOR provides valuable insight into the energy profiles that govern OER kinetics. The first step in the OER catalytic cycle is the formation of *OH. In this step, “M” refers to the active site on the activated surface, which may either be an oxidized metal cation center or an electrophilic lattice oxygen, as depicted in Fig. S23a, b. The second step is often considered the rate-limiting step in alcohol oxidation. In this step, the electrophilic *OH is attacked nucleophilically by methanol or water molecules, losing an electron to form either an aldehyde or the oxygen intermediate (*O), as shown in Fig. S23a, b.

If the adsorption of *OH is the rate-determining step (RDS) in OER, the catalyst surface should remain free of *OH because the subsequent steps are fast, and thus, no response to alcohol molecules would occur. However, if *OH adsorption is rapid while the subsequent step is the RDS, the discrepancy in reaction rates between the two elementary steps leads to the accumulation of *OH on the catalyst surface, contributing to the MOR current.

The kinetic behavior of MOR after the onset of OER can be attributed to the faster deprotonation of *OH (Step ii in OER, Figs S23a, b), which is dependent on the applied potential. The surface coverage of *OH (θ[*OH]) is governed by the relative rates of *OH formation and consumption (r(1)/r(2)). θ[*OH] can be calculated by comparing the integrated areas (IA) under the MOR and OER current density curves, as follows: θ[*OH] = MORIA / OERIA. Since the OER current is negligible below 1.4 VRHE, the potential range of 1.4–1.8 VRHE was used to estimate θ[*OH]. To probe *OH coverage, the integrated areas of MOR and OER current densities in two potential ranges were compared: 1.4 VRHE to 1.6 VRHE and 1.6 VRHE to 1.8 VRHE.
