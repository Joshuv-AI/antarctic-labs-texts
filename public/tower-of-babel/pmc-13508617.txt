Magnitude of the placebo response across non-surgical treatment modalities used for knee osteoarthritis: A meta-analysis with meta-regression
Authors: Robin Holtedahl, Alexandra Christine Hott, Inger Johanne Widding Hansen, John Bjørneboe, Jens Ivar Brox
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo quantify pain-related placebo responses across treatment modalities in placebo-controlled clinical trials of non-surgical osteoarthritis interventions.DesignA systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) published since January 2008 was conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines. Searches of the PubMed and Embase databases were conducted through May 2025. Hedges’ g was calculated as the effect size for pre-to-post change in pain outcomes using a random-effects model. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic, and sources of variance were analyzed with multivariate meta-regression.ResultsOne hundred and seventy-nine RCTs were included. Four trials also included a no-treatment arm. A Hedges’ g of 0.5 or greater for improvement from baseline was observed in the placebo arm of 109 (60%) trials, while 15 (8%) reported worsening. Substantial heterogeneity was present (I2 = 88%, p < 0.0001), with a wide prediction interval (−0.31 to 1.60). Multivariate meta-regression identified four covariates that together accounted for 33% of the explained variance: baseline pain, funding source, effect size in the active treatment arm, and geographical region. In subgroup analysis, outcomes were not significantly affected by treatment modality. Trials conducted in North America reported the highest effect sizes.ConclusionMost trials reported moderate to large effect sizes for improvement in the placebo group. Treatment modality did not significantly affect outcomes and most of the variance in the placebo response remained unexplained. Important limitations include substantial statistical heterogeneity, the lack of data on psychological variables and few trials with a no-treatment group.

Introduction

Knee osteoarthritis (KOA) guidelines recommend patient-centered, biopsychosocial care that emphasizes education, exercise, and weight loss [1]. Despite the introduction of numerous pharmacological and non-pharmacological treatments, consistent supporting evidence remains limited for many interventions [2].

The benefits of any treatment reflect both its specific therapeutic effect and placebo responses [3]. Placebo responses comprise the total change observed after placebo administration, including the natural course of the condition, regression to the mean, and the placebo effect itself. Substantial placebo responses have been reported in trials of osteoarthritis and other painful musculoskeletal conditions, particularly for subjective outcomes [[4], [5], [6]].

Given the challenges of blinding patients and providers in exercise trials, this meta-analysis focused on placebo responses in trials of interventions more amenable to blinding, such as pharmacological treatments. Even in these trials, commercial sponsorship may influence placebo responses, as sponsored trials may be more likely to test novel agents and create stronger expectations of efficacy among participants and outcome assessors. Other factors believed to influence placebo responses include baseline pain scores [7], sample size [[7], [8], [9]], disease duration [7] and publication year [7,10]. Also, larger placebo responses have been linked to interventions that are more invasive, complex, or time-consuming [8,9,[11], [12], [13]]. Conversely, a previous systematic review of osteoarthritis trials found lower placebo responses in developed countries than in developing countries [9].

This meta-analysis quantitatively examined methodological, demographic, and clinical variables that may influence placebo responses in randomized controlled trials (RCTs) of non-surgical, non-exercise-based KOA interventions. To test the assumption that more invasive and intensive interventions have stronger placebo responses we also assessed placebo response magnitude across five treatment modalities: physical, oral, intra-articular, topical, and intravenous, subcutaneous or acupuncture-based interventions.

Materials and methods

The protocol for this review was registered in PROSPERO (CRD 420251113096), and the review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses reporting checklist [14].

Data sources and eligibility criteria

Following protocol registration, PubMed and EMBASE were systematically searched for eligible trials published from January 2008 through May 2025. The search used the terms “KOA” AND (placebo OR sham), with filters for RCTs and English-language publications. Two authors (RH and AH) assessed eligibility in two stages: title-and-abstract screening followed by full-text review of potentially eligible articles. Reasons for exclusion were recorded by each reviewer, and disagreements were resolved by consensus. Trials were eligible if they were 1) randomized, parallel-group studies of KOA treatments that were double- or triple-blinded; 2) included a placebo or sham arm, with or without a no-treatment arm; 3) evaluated treatments other than psychological or exercise-based interventions or anatomy-altering surgery; 4) reported a pain-related, knee-specific continuous outcome; and 5) provided sufficient data to calculate effect sizes, i.e. SDs, SEs, or 95% CIs. Detailed eligibility criteria are provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Data extraction

The first author recorded key trial characteristics in a standardized Microsoft Access v.2412 data extraction form; the full item list is provided in Supplementary Table S2. Outcome values for the active treatment, placebo, and no-treatment arms, along with trial registration and funding source, were extracted by the first author and verified by two independent authors (AH, JB). The WOMAC pain subscale was used as the preferred outcome measure; when unavailable, total WOMAC or another knee-specific pain measure was used. Radiological OA severity and disease duration were not extracted because these variables were inconsistently reported or omitted in many trials.

Pre-to-post change values were checked to ensure the correct direction of effect. Sixteen clinically relevant covariates were extracted for the meta-regression analysis (Table 1). For trials of ongoing treatments, the end-of-trial outcome value was extracted. For trials reporting later follow-up outcomes, including single-intervention trials, the latest available outcome was extracted, with follow-up capped at 2 years. Placebo administration was classified into five groups: 1) physical interventions (e.g., short-wave ultrasound, cryotherapy, taping), 2) oral formulations (tablet, capsule, sachet, syrup), 3) intra-articular injections, usually normal saline, 4) topical preparations (gel, ointment, liniment), and 5) intravenous, subcutaneous, or acupuncture procedures. The final category was grouped together because these interventions penetrate the skin and/or soft tissue, but not the joint capsule. The grouping into five categories was intended to capture differences in the perceived invasiveness or complexity of the interventions while also ensuring sufficient numbers in each category for regression analysis.

Risk of bias assessment

Four of the authors (RH, JIB, AH, IJH)) independently and pairwise assessed risk of bias using the revised Cochrane Risk of Bias tool for randomized trials [15]. Judgments were classified as low risk, some concerns, or high risk. Selective reporting bias was assessed from trial registration status and whether protocols were submitted before trial initiation. Trial size (>49 patients) and loss to follow-up (>15%) were also considered. Disagreements were resolved by consensus. Sensitivity analysis excluded trials judged to be at high risk of bias in any domain.

Statistical analyses

Analyses were conducted using Comprehensive Meta-Analysis version 4.0.000, MedCalc version 22.021 (MedCalc Software Ltd, Ostend, Belgium), and Microsoft Excel version 2503 (Microsoft, Redmond, WA, USA). Hedges' g, which adjusts for small sample sizes, was used to estimate change from baseline to follow-up. Effect sizes of 0.8, 0.5, and 0.2 were interpreted as large, moderate, and small, respectively. Calculations were based on each trial's SD of change, or alternatively on the SE, 95% CI, or p-value when available [16]. When these data were unavailable, a recommended formula was applied using a pre-to-posttest correlation of 0.5 [17]. The same approach was used for the treatment and no-treatment arms. Two-sided p-values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

A random-effects model with the DerSimonian-Laird method was applied because the trials were drawn from multiple populations. The 95% CIs were calculated using the inverse-variance approach, accounting for both within- and between-trial variance. Heterogeneity was assessed with I2, which estimates the proportion of variability not attributable to sampling error; values ≥ 50% were considered high [18]. Prediction intervals were calculated to assess whether similar future trials would be expected to yield comparable results [19]. Univariate meta-regression was used to examine effect sizes across predefined covariate subgroups. A multivariate regression model was then developed by stepwise removal of covariates with p-values ≥0.10. R2 values were estimated for each retained covariate and for the overall model.

To test the robustness of our findings, we performed two ad hoc sensitivity analyses. First, we excluded trials with very large treatment-arm effect sizes (>3). The inclusion of such trials have been shown to influence results from metaanalysis in trials evaluating the effectiveness of psychological treatments [20]. Second, because the published values in this subgroup may have been SEs rather than SDs, we recalculated SDs by multiplying each reported value by the square root of the trial sample size and then recomputed the effect sizes. The same recalculation was applied to the placebo arms. We then assessed the impact of these analyses on the pooled placebo-group effect size and meta-regression results.

Results

After removing duplicates, searches identified 549 trials. Of these, 295 were excluded after the first screening and 75 after the second, leaving 179 trials available for analysis. Four trials, involving 141 participants in total, also included a no-treatment arm. The average follow-up was 20 weeks (range 10–26) for single-procedure trials (n = 20) and 20 weeks (range 4–104) for ongoing-treatment trials (n = 160). Risk of bias

Of the 179 trials, 168 (93%) were judged to be at unclear or high risk of bias in at least one domain; 132 (73%) adequately generated their randomization sequence, 112 (62%) appropriately concealed allocation, 84 (47%) adequately blinded patients and caregivers, and 88 (49%) adequately blinded outcome assessors (because only patient-reported outcome measures were extracted, the participant was designated as the assessor in all trials). Only nine (5%) trials assessed the success of participant blinding. In 53 trials (29%), the total sample size was <50, while 53 trials (29%) reported ≥15% missing outcome data. High risk or some concerns of bias due to selective reporting were found in 68 (38%) and 54 (30%) trials, respectively. Calculation of effect sizes

Analysis of effect sizes (Hedges' g) for clinical outcomes in the placebo arms showed substantial heterogeneity, with an overall I2 of 88% (p < 0.0001), indicating that 88% of the observed variance reflected true effects rather than sampling error. Effect sizes ranged from −0.94 to 3.80 (interquartile range, IQR, 0.33 to 0.97), and the pooled effect size was 0.63 (95% CI 0.56 to 0.71). The prediction interval was −0.29 to 1.55, meaning the true effect size would fall within this interval in 95% of comparable populations. Moderate or large effect sizes (Hedges’ g ≥ 0.5) indicating improvement were reported in 109 (60%) trials, while fifteen (8%) trials reported worsened pain compared to baseline. The pooled effect size in the no-treatment arms of four trials was 0.14 (95% CI = −0.03 to 0.31, I2 = 56%).

There were 29 trials with an effect size ≥3 in the treatment arm. In sensitivity analysis, after converting SEs to SDs in these trials, the pooled effect size in the placebo arm was reduced to 0.58 (95% CI 0.51 to 1.39), with I2 virtually unchanged (84%). When these 29 trials were excluded from the analysis, pooled effect size in the placebo arm was reduced to 0.57 (95% CI 0.50 to 0.64, I2 79%).

Subgroup analysis by treatment modality (Fig. 3) did not reveal significant differences in pooled effect size (p = 0.73, τ2 = 0.22). Hedges' g values for each category were: topical 0.76 (95% CI, −0.42 to 1.91; I2 = 79.15%); oral 0.61 (95% CI, −0.27 to 1.45; I2 = 85.45%); intra-articular 0.71 (95% CI, −0.25 to 1.64; I2 = 88.99%); physical 0.56 (95% CI, −0.31 to 1.52; I2 = 76.99%); subcutaneous/intravenous/acupuncture 0.74 (95% CI, −0.84 to 2.27; I2 = 94.18%). Forest plots (Supplementary Fig. S2a–e) show effect sizes for individual trials within each subgroup. Meta-regression analysis

In univariate meta-regression, six predefined covariates were significantly associated with effect size (Supplementary Table S4). Effect sizes differed significantly by geographic region (p = 0.012), with the highest pooled estimate observed in North American trials, all but two of which were conducted in the USA, and the lowest in Australian trials (Hedges’ g 0.84 and 0.50, respectively).

In the multivariate analysis, active-treatment effect size, geographic region, baseline pain level, and funding source together explained 33% of the variance (Table 3, Supplementary Fig. 3a–d). Excluding the 29 trials with treatment-arm effect sizes of ≥3 removed geographic region as a contributor to explained variance. Subgroup analyses showed no significant differences in pooled effect size across the seven risk-of-bias domains. The pooled estimate was essentially unchanged after excluding trials with high risk of bias in at least one domain (0.65, 95% CI 0.52 to 0.78; Supplementary Table S6a and b).

Discussion

This meta-analysis reviewed placebo responses in 179 RCTs of non-surgical treatments for KOA published since 2008. Using meta-regression, we examined covariates that might explain heterogeneity and quantified effect sizes in the placebo group across treatment modalities.

Overall findings

More than 90% of trials reported pain reduction, and 60% showed moderate or large effect sizes. This is consistent with previous systematic reviews, reporting placebo responses accounting for a substantial proportion of the overall efficacy observed with active treatments [9,20,21]. Because of substantial heterogeneity across patients, settings, and treatment modalities, we considered pooling of effect estimates unreliable and prioritized multivariate regression to examine sources of variance. Four covariates—sponsorship type, baseline pain severity, active-treatment effect size, and geographic region—explained roughly one-third of the observed variance.

Although estimating the pooled effect size was not the primary aim of this analysis, we believe the observed value of 0.64 is likely an overestimate. We identified 29 trials with active-treatment Hedges’ g values of 3 or higher, which is highly implausible because it implies mean posttest values at least three standard deviations above pretest values [23]. Our concern is supported by the study by Zou et al. that reported a mean overall treatment effect size of 1.09 (95% CI 1.03 to 1.14), with the largest effect size for topical NSAIDs (1.37) [9]. In our study, 87 (48%) treatment arms exceeded this value. However, rather than excluding trials with implausibly large effect sizes, we followed guidelines for meta-epidemiological research and conducted sensitivity analyses to assess their influence [16]. These sensitivity analyses reduced the estimated pooled placebo response by approximately 10%.

Substantial heterogeneity may raise concerns about the validity of treatment-effect estimates, as seen in research on NSAID efficacy. The variability across placebo groups is expected because trials differ in demographic characteristics (e.g., age and sex distribution), design features (e.g., outcome measures and follow-up duration), and clinical factors (e.g., participant expectations and disease duration) and because patients are not responding to placebo itself but to the meaning of the placebo [24].

Comparison with other trials and reviews

Only two meta-analyses, to our knowledge, have examined the influence of funding on placebo responses, with differing conclusions [25,26]. Commercially sponsored trials may more often be associated with highly standardized, professional clinical settings and test novel agents or interventions, thereby increasing patient expectations [26,27]. Our finding of an association between placebo responses and baseline pain levels aligns with previous reviews, including of musculoskeletal conditions [7,28,29]. This association may indicate that individuals with extreme baseline scores have a stronger tendency to move toward average values at follow-up due to regression to the mean [30,31]. The association between placebo responses and active-treatment effect size was expected and likely reflects contextual and nonspecific factors shared by both groups. Given balanced visit schedules, adequate blinding, and proper randomization, we had expected a stronger association, as reported in previous reviews [[32], [33], [34]].

There was significant variation across geographic regions, with trials in North America reporting the highest effect sizes and those in Asia and Australia the lowest both in uni- and multivariate analysis; however, this finding was not consistent in the sensitivity analysis and should be critically interpreted. A prior systematic review of OA trials reported lower placebo responses in developed countries than in developing countries; however, the specific criteria used in that analysis were not described [9]. We are unaware of other reviews of OA trials that have specifically examined the influence of geographic region on placebo responses.

Longer duration of treatment or follow-up has been associated with larger placebo responses in some previous reviews [7,9,35]. We extracted only one outcome time point per trial and therefore cannot exclude within-trial longitudinal changes. In a meta-analysis of 41 surgical trials the placebo response was time-invariant throughout blinded follow-up [36]. Subsequent published RCTs have reported sustained effects on pain and function after sham surgery [[37], [38], [39], [40]] and intra-articular injections [41,42]. Similarly, patients with advanced Parkinson's disease who underwent sham surgery reported persistent improvements in physical function 12 months after the procedure [43].

Role of non-specific factors

KOA symptoms fluctuate naturally, and disease flares may increase the likelihood of trial recruitment [44]. Consistent with this, a prospective KOA cohort found that regression to the mean accounted for up to 1 point on a 0–10 pain scale [30]. Thus, between-trial differences in spontaneous remission and regression to the mean may have contributed to the observed heterogeneity. Although the pooled effect size in the four trials with a no-treatment arm was small (Hedges’ g 0.14), wide confidence intervals limit certainty about the magnitude of non-specific effects. A previous review identified spontaneous improvement as an important contributor to placebo responses across conditions [45]. A Cochrane review of placebo-controlled trials with no-treatment arms included six trials of physical interventions for pain conditions [4]. In our analysis of published data from these trials, the pooled effect size was 0.18 (95% CI -0.59 to 0.94) in no-treatment groups and 0.60 (95% CI 0.08 to 1.12) in corresponding placebo groups. These findings are consistent with the present analysis and provide indirect support for placebo effects.

Placebo responses across treatment modalities

Subgroup analysis found no significant association between treatment modality and outcome, challenging the view that more “intensive” interventions produce larger placebo effects [8,9,11,12,46]. Our findings are in line with the understanding of the placebo response as a meaning response, according to which patients are not responding to the placebo itself, but to the meaning of the placebo for the individual [24]. In addition, the previous reviews, like the present analysis, relied on comparisons by pooling heterogeneous trials rather than directly comparing groups randomized to different placebos within the same trial. This design is vulnerable to bias because it cannot adequately control for confounding.

The optimal trial design for comparing placebo delivery methods is a single four-arm trial that includes two active treatments and their respective placebos. A meta-analysis of twelve trials using this design found no consistent evidence that more “intensive” placebos produce larger placebo effects [47]. Of the included RCTs, only two focused on musculoskeletal disorders. One trial found that sham acupuncture was more effective than a placebo pill for reducing pain but not for functional improvement [48]. Another trial showed no difference between parenteral and oral placebos for acute trauma-related pain [49]. A later published trial reported greater improvements on pain with a topical placebo compared to an oral placebo [50]. Given the scarcity of well-designed multi-placebo RCTs, we believe there is currently insufficient evidence linking treatment modality to the magnitude of placebo effects.

Strengths and limitations

Strengths of this meta-analysis include a rigorous, comprehensive search strategy, clearly defined eligibility criteria, and the use of meta-regression models to assess the effects of moderating covariates. By calculating effect sizes, the study accommodated trials with diverse outcome measures, thereby broadening the scope of eligible research.

Some trials failed to provide information about radiological stage of KOA or duration of the disease. We were therefore unable to assess the potential impact of these covariates on our analysis, though a previous review reported increased placebo responses with longer disease duration [42].

An important limitation is the inability to reliably distinguish placebo effects from overall placebo responses. We were also unable to evaluate psychological factors widely acknowledged as critical contributors to placebo effects. It is reasonable to believe that contextual differences—such as those in the patient-provider relationship—substantially contributed to between-trial heterogeneity. While an in-depth discussion of these underlying components is beyond the scope of this review, factors such as positive beliefs, expectations, and the perceived competence and warmth of healthcare providers may yield clinically substantial and enduring benefits [[51], [52], [53]]. Interestingly, these factors have been shown to affect neurobiological processes, as demonstrated in a sham-controlled surgical trial for advanced Parkinson's disease, where perceived treatment was a stronger predictor of 12-month outcomes than the actual intervention [43]. Therefore, accounting for patient expectations when designing placebo-controlled trials could improve their assay sensitivity [54,55].

Conclusion

In 60% of trials, placebo-group participants reported pain reduction consistent with a moderate or large effect size. However, considerable statistical heterogeneity was observed across trials. Four covariates—type of sponsorship, baseline pain level, effect size in the active treatment arm, and geographic region—explained approximately one-third of the observed variance. The sources of the remaining unexplained variance are unclear, but psychological factors, such as patient-provider interactions and patient expectations, are likely contributors. The analysis did not reveal a significant association between treatment modalities and outcomes. Including psychological evaluations and a no-treatment control group in future OA intervention trials may help clarify the mechanisms underlying placebo effects.
