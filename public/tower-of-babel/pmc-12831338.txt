Differentiating tumor recurrence and pseudoprogression in postoperative gliomas using pseudo-continuous arterial spin labeling (pCASL) technique
Authors: Peiquan Liu, Yujie Chen, Xudong Ai, Weiyin Vivian Liu, Hongquan Zhu, Yujie Ding, Dong Liu, Tengfei Chao, Jiaxuan Zhang, Wenzhen Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo assess whether the pCASL technique and its radiomics features can enhance the differentiation between tumor recurrence (TR) and pseudoprogression (PsP) in postoperative glioma patients.MethodsA retrospective study of 120 postoperative glioma patients (WHO Grade 2–4) from Tongji Hospital, Wuhan, was conducted. MRI data, including T1WI, T2WI, T2FLAIR, contrast-enhanced T1WI, and pCASL, were analyzed. Final diagnoses of TR or PsP were confirmed through pathology or follow-up. Among the patients, 65 had recurrence, and 55 had PsP. Process the pCASL images to generate the CBF parameter map, then perform N4 bias correction and Z- score standardization to obtain the standardized CBF parameter map for group analysis. The lesion areas were outlined, and mean values for ROI were calculated. Statistical analysis included the Mann-Whitney U test and ROC curve analysis. Radiomics features were extracted from the CBF maps. These features were then further selected and divided into training and testing sets. Machine learning models, including Support Vector Machine (SVM), logistic regression, random forest, and Gaussian Naive Bayes, were developed and subsequently validated.ResultsThe Mann-Whitney U test showed a significant difference in mean CBF values between TR and PsP groups (p < 0.001). ROC analysis revealed an AUC of 0.879 (95% CI: 0.817–0.941), sensitivity of 0.846, specificity of 0.836, PPV of 0.859, and NPV of 0.821. After feature selection, seven radiomics features were retained. SVM yielded the best performance with an AUC of 0.971, sensitivity of 0.950, specificity of 0.813, PPV of 0.864, and NPV of 0.929.ConclusionThe pCASL technique, combined with radiomics features, effectively differentiates TR from PsP in postoperative glioma patients. The pCASL provides reliable diagnostic information, with radiomics further improving classification accuracy. The SVM model demonstrated the best performance, highlighting the potential of combining pCASL and radiomics for accurate, non-invasive differentiation of TR and PsP. This approach could enhance clinical decision-making and patient management.Keywords: Pseudo-continuous arterial spin labeling, Pseudoprogression, Radiomics, Machine learning

Introduction

Gliomas originate from glial cells and are the most common primary malignant tumors of the central nervous system in adults, with an annual incidence of approximately 6.4 per 100,000 people. Gliomas account for about 81% of intracranial malignant tumors and are characterized by high heterogeneity and poor prognosis [1–3]. Currently, the main treatment for gliomas is surgical resection followed by adjuvant chemoradiotherapy, with six cycles of temozolomide (TMZ) as part of the regimen. However, achieving complete resection while preserving patient function and quality of life remains a dilemma for neurosurgeons [4].

In recent years, during the follow-up of glioma patients after chemoradiotherapy or immunotherapy, many patients have been observed to develop areas of enhancement on MRI images that resemble tumor recurrence. These enhanced areas appear as patchy or nodular lesions, often associated with surrounding cerebral edema. Additionally, patients may exhibit clinical signs such as intracranial hypertension or neurological deficits, which are similar to those seen in tumor recurrence. However, over time, both the imaging and clinical manifestations improve. This phenomenon is referred to as pseudoprogression (PsP) [5, 6]. PsP commonly occurs 2 to 6 months after radiotherapy in glioma patients. The biological mechanisms behind PsP are not fully understood, but possible explanations include:(1) TMZ inhibits cell replication at the G2/M phase of the cell cycle and increases the number of DNA breaks. This combined effect may lead to an exaggerated cellular response in the tumor bed (the original location of the tumor before treatment), resulting in endothelial cell injury, blood-brain barrier (BBB) disruption, and oligodendrocyte damage [7], (2) In patients with diffuse gliomas carrying isocitrate dehydrogenase 1 (IDH1) (R132H) mutations, a specific therapeutic T-helper cell response may be induced. PsP represents an inflammatory response within the tumor, which is associated with an increase in peripheral T-cell responses induced by vaccines [8, 9]. Pseudoprogression is not a true tumor recurrence; it is a benign change that typically does not require special treatment, apart from symptomatic management to alleviate symptoms. In contrast, recurrent gliomas, which progress rapidly and have a poor prognosis, may require repeat surgeries. Therefore, the treatment strategies and prognoses for PsP and recurrent gliomas are entirely different. Early and accurate differentiation between the two is crucial, as it directly impacts clinical evaluation of the efficacy of prior treatments and the formulation of subsequent therapeutic plans.

The basic principle of ASL is to apply a radiofrequency pulse to label the hydrogen protons in the arterial blood. The labeled arterial blood then flows into the imaging plane for perfusion imaging. The resulting image is referred to as the “label image,” which includes information from both the blood flowing into the tissue and the static tissue. In addition, non-labeled blood imaging of the imaging region is called the “control image,” which includes static background tissue information. The difference between these two images is the perfusion image [10]. 3D-pCASL is a recently introduced whole-brain perfusion imaging technique based on the Fast Spin Echo (FSE) sequence, which has relatively low image artifacts [11].

Previous studies have found that, in the assessment of brain tumor perfusion, the diagnostic performance of 3D-pCASL is comparable to Dynamic Susceptibility Contrast (DSC) imaging, particularly for patients who are not suitable for DSC due to renal failure [12, 13]. Patients with PsP often exhibit lower CBF compared to patients with true TR [14–16].

Radiomics extracts a large number of image features through high-throughput techniques, which help to reveal disease heterogeneity information that is not visible to the naked eye. These features, when compared and analyzed with clinical data, can be used for disease diagnosis, treatment planning, efficacy prediction, and prognosis assessment, among other applications [17–21]. Although arterial spin labeling has demonstrated potential in differentiating tumor recurrence from pseudoprogression [22–24], few studies have further explored its discriminative ability through radiomics features and evaluated it using different machine learning methods [25–28].

This study aims to explore whether pCASL technology and its radiomics features can improve the differentiation between pseudo-progression and tumor recurrence in glioma patients, with the goal of providing clinical reference for diagnosis and treatment decisions.

Materials and methods

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of our hospital, and the requirement for written informed consent was waived, in accordance with the basic principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (approval date: July 29, 2024, approval number: TJ-IRB202407107).

This study retrospectively collected data from 163 patients diagnosed with diffuse gliomas of grades 2 to 4 based on the 2021 WHO classification of CNS tumors, with data collected from September 2020 to January 2025. Tumor progression was defined by the radiological features of the Response Assessment in Neuro-Oncology (RANO) criteria [29, 30]: an increase in lesion volume on T2 FLAIR images, or the appearance of new enhancing lesions or an increase in the volume of contrast-enhancing components > 25% on contrast-enhanced T1-weighted images. The study defined pseudoprogression as newly emerging or enlarging measurable enhancing lesions that showed stability or regression on consecutive MRIs over at least 6 months, TR was defined as the progression of the lesion on T2 FLAIR or T1WI+C on two consecutive MRIs (with at least an 8-week interval) showing continuous enlargement ( > 25%), with worsening clinical symptoms, or confirmed tumor recurrence by pathology after a second surgery. Exclusion criteria were as follows: incomplete postoperative pathology, the maximum diameter of enhancing lesions < 1 cm, loss to follow-up after surgery, poor image quality, or failure of post-processing of cerebral blood flow (CBF) images. Finally, 120 eligible cases were included, consisting of 65 patients with tumor recurrence and 55 with pseudoprogression. The inclusion flow is shown in Fig. 1.

Scanning protocol

All MRIs were performed using a 3.0T MRI scanner (Discovery MR750, GE Medical Systems, Milwaukee, Wisconsin, USA) equipped with a 32-channel head coil. The scanning protocol included the following MRI sequences: T1-weighted MRI, T2-weighted MRI, T2-weighted FLAIR MRI, contrast-enhanced T1-weighted (T1WI+C) MRI, and pseudo-continuous arterial spin labeling (pCASL) MRI.

T1WI based on the 3D T1 BRAVO sequence, the detailed parameters for routine sequences were as follows:

Data processing

We used the head motion correction module provided by the MR750 for motion correction, the CBF parameter maps were post-processed using the ASL module of the MR750 workstation, followed by N4 bias correction and Z-score standardization to obtain the standardized CBF parameter map for group analysis. Region of interest (ROI)

The delineation of the ROI was performed by two neuroimaging specialists (with 5 and 8 years of imaging diagnosis experience, respectively) who were blinded to the patients’ clinical outcomes. The CBF images were linearly registered to either T1WI+C or T2FLAIR using ITK_SNAP 4.0. Lesion delineation was based on the contrast-enhanced regions, cystic, necrotic, or hemorrhagic areas were excluded, and care was taken to avoid including perifocal edema. The average values of the lesion areas were calculated and the ROI was preserved (Fig. 2). Finally, intra-class correlation (ICC) analysis was performed on the results from the two specialists.

Schematic diagram of ROI delineation

Radiomics feature extraction

Radiomic features were extracted from the CBF parameter maps using the PyRadiomics library in Python 3.7.6. The resampled pixel size was set to 1x1x1. A total of 851 radiomic features were extracted, including 14 shape features, 18 first-order features, 75 texture features, and 744 wavelet transform features.

Radiomics feature selection and model development

The ROI-derived features from both specialists were analyzed, and features with an ICC value lower than 0.75 were excluded. The data were then standardized using Z-score normalization. The patients were divided into training and testing sets in a 7:3 ratio. In the training set, features with a Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) greater than 0.99 were removed, and redundant variables were eliminated using Lasso regression.

Machine learning models were developed using support vector machine (SVM), logistic regression (LR), random forest (RF), and Gaussian naive Bayes classifiers (NB). Model hyperparameters were adjusted using 10-fold cross-validation, and the performance of the models was evaluated in the independent testing set. SHAP (Shapley Additive exPlanations) was used for visualization and interpretability analysis of the best-performing model (Fig. 3).

Radiomics research pipeline: the first step is region of interest (ROI) delineation and segmentation; the second step involves feature extraction, including shape and volume features, first-order features, texture analysis, and wavelet features; the third step is feature selection; the fourth step is to analyze diagnostic performance using the selected radiomic features. Data analysis

Statistical analysis for the ICC(3,1) of the ROI average values, utilizing a two-way mixed-effects model, was performed with the Pingouin library in Python 3.7.6. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The Mann-Whitney U test and chi-square test were used to assess the differences in variables between the TR and PsP groups in the training and independent validation sets. Based on the training set, prediction models were developed using four machine learning methods (SVM, LR, RF, NB), and their diagnostic performance was compared using ROC analysis in the independent testing set.

Result

Demographic characteristics of patients

A total of 120 patients were included in the study. Following subsequent surgery and a minimum of six months of follow-up, 65 patients were classified as TR and 55 as PsP. An independent testing set comprised 36 cases, including 20 TR and 16 PsP patients. The demographic and clinical characteristics of the patient cohort are presented in Table 1. No statistically significant differences were found between the TR and PsP groups in terms of age, gender, IDH status and WHO classification in both the internal comparison within the training and independent testing sets.

Comparison of mean values

The ICC value of the mean CBF measurements between the two physicians was 0.910, indicating good consistency. The Mann-Whitney test showed a statistically significant difference in the distribution of mean CBF values between the two groups (p < 0.001). The ROC curve analysis based on the CBF variable yielded an AUC of 0.879 (95% CI: 0.817–0.941), sensitivity of 0.846, specificity of 0.836, PPV of 0.859, and NPV of 0.821.

Radiomics feature extraction and ICC analysis

A total of 851 features were extracted from the CBF images. All radiomic features derived from the ROI delineated by two radiologists were analyzed for ICC. The results showed that among the 851 radiomic features, 459 features had an ICC greater than 0.75, indicating good consistency. The results from the more senior rater were selected for further analysis.

Radiomics feature selection and model development

A further analysis was conducted on the 459 features with an ICC value greater than 0.75. After removing features with a PCC > 0.99, 325 features remained. Following Lasso feature selection, 7 features were retained: CBF_original_firstorder_10 percentile, CBF_original_glszm_GrayLevelNonUniformity, CBF_wavelet-HHH_glszm_SmallAreaEmphasis, CBF_wavelet-HLL_glcm_ClusterProminence, CBF_wavelet-LHL_firstorder_Mean, CBF_wavelet-LHL_glcm_ClusterShade, CBF_wavelet-LLH_glcm_ClusterShade. Among the four machine learning models tested, the SVM model achieved the optimal performance, with an AUC of 0.971 (95% CI: 0.907–1.000), sensitivity: 0.950, specificity:0.813, PPV: 0.864, NPV: 0.929 (Table 2).

Interpretability analysis of SVM models

SHAP is a method derived from game theory, designed to enhance the interpretability of machine learning models and eliminate the so-called “black-box effect.” By calculating Shapley values, which represent the average marginal contribution of each feature to the model’s prediction after considering all possible combinations, the features are ranked by their importance, with higher-ranked features contributing more significantly to the model’s outcome [31]. Based on the Shapley values, in the SVM model, the feature with the greatest impact on the prediction is CBF_original_firstorder_10 percentile, followed by CBF wavelet-LHL firstorder Mean, CBF_wavelet-HLL_glcm_ClusterProminence and others in order of importance (Figs. 4–6).

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the discriminative value of pCASL and its radiomics features for distinguishing between PsP and TR. Both the direct measurement of mean CBF and the machine learning model established using radiomics features demonstrated good diagnostic performance in differentiating between the two groups, confirming the clinical application value of pCASL technology. We used the N4 bias field correction algorithm to correct MRI intensity inhomogeneities and normalize image intensity across all patients [32, 33], and LASSO regression was applied to avoid overfitting in small sample sizes [34].

The optimal feature subset identified in this study consisted of 7 radiomics features, including those derived from wavelet-transformed features, with 2 first-order features and 5 texture features. We conducted an interpretability analysis of the model based on Shapley values and found that CBF_original_firstorder_10 percentile contributed the most to the model. This suggests that, compared to the mean, the 10th percentile may have a better ability to distinguish between TR and PsP. Original_firstorder_10%ile reflects the lower end of the grayscale intensity distribution. We speculate that this is due to internal liquefaction, necrosis, and other pathological changes within malignant tumors, which lead to a more dispersed pixel intensity distribution, thereby lowering the values in the lower grayscale range [35, 36]. Previous studies on gliomas have shown that first-order features such as mean, 10th percentile, 90th percentile, and energy exhibit the highest reproducibility and have been proven to be associated with tumor cell structure [37, 38]. Among the texture features, the most significant contributor is the Gray Level Co-occurrence Matrix (GLCM), which is derived by analyzing the spatial relationship of pixel pairs at specific distances and orientations within the image. It is used to describe the granularity of the texture and the directionality of repetition [39]. The irregular growth of malignant tumor cells and liquefactive necrosis lead to disordered cell arrangement, thereby causing disturbances in the texture features of the image [40]. GLCM has been shown to be associated with tumor heterogeneity [41].

A systematic review and meta-analysis indicated that previous studies investigating the use of ASL for distinguishing TR from PsP reported an AUC of 0.88 (95% CI: 0.85–0.91) [42], which is consistent with the findings of this study. However, this study further explores the value of radiomics features in differentiation, with the SVM model demonstrating exceptional performance in the test set: AUC = 0.971 (95% CI: 0.907–1.000). Its stability and accuracy surpass those of other machine learning models, highlighting the model’s significant potential.

Previous studies on postoperative differentiation often utilized DSC sequences. Kim et al [25] incorporated perfusion-weighted imaging, represented by DSC, into a radiomics model, demonstrating superior diagnostic performance compared to single ADC, CBV, or conventional MRI. However, there have been few reports on the radiomics study of ASL and the analysis of radiomics models using ASL. Another study [43] showed that DSC achieved an AUC of 0.912 (95% CI: 0.837–0.988) in distinguishing between TR and PsP. This result lies between the performance of using the mean CBF and radiomic features. However, compared with contrast agent perfusion imaging, non-invasive ASL is more suitable for populations with renal insufficiency and difficulty with intravenous injections. Its primary advantage lies in being unaffected by the leakage effect from the blood-brain barrier disruption [44, 45]. ASL measures blood flow perfusion by tagging water molecules in the blood, enabling non-invasive assessment of regional blood flow differences across various tissues. This is particularly valuable in pathological or tumor tissues, where blood flow often exhibits significant heterogeneity. Such heterogeneity is typically associated with factors like tumor angiogenesis, oxygen demand, and metabolic activity [46, 47], Consequently, some studies suggest that ASL could serve as an independent predictive marker and has the potential to replace Dynamic Contrast-Enhanced MRI (DSC) in certain clinical applications [16, 22, 24, 48].

Among the various ASL sequences, pCASL offers a higher signal-to-noise ratio, reduced susceptibility artifacts, and simpler clinical operation [44]. Following glioma treatment, both the structure and function of the tissue undergo changes. Radiation therapy can lead to cell necrosis, granulation tissue formation, edema, and degeneration. TR results in increased cell density and reduced extracellular space [49]. TR shows increased microvascular density and abundant collateral vessels, which leads to high perfusion in perfusion-weighted imaging, whereas PsP presents as low perfusion (Fig. 6).

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, in terms of imaging modalities and technical consistency, we only employed arterial spin labeling (ASL) MRI, with all images acquired on a GE scanner. While we minimized technical variability through standardized protocols and centralized data processing by a single team, potential variability in technical implementation may still have influenced results. Incorporating equipment from other manufacturers (e.g., Siemens, Philips) is also recommended to enhance the robustness of conclusions. Second, our predictive models were exclusively built using machine learning approaches. Exploring deep learning and ensemble learning methods in future work could help identify the optimal MRI radiomics model and improve predictive performance. Third, the sample size is relatively small and limited to a specific cohort, restricting the generalizability of our findings. A multi-center study with an expanded, more representative sample is therefore necessary to validate our results and improve the model’s stability and validity.

Conclusion

The pCASL technique, combined with radiomics features, effectively differentiates TR from PsP in postoperative glioma patients. The pCASL provides reliable diagnostic information, with radiomics further improving classification accuracy. The SVM model demonstrated the best performance, highlighting the potential of combining pCASL and radiomics for accurate, non-invasive differentiation of TR and PsP. This approach could enhance clinical decision-making and patient management.
