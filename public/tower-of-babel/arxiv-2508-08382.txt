UrzaGPT : LoRA-Tuned Large Language Models for Card Selection in Collectible Card Games
Timo Bertram

Abstract
Collectible card games (CCGs) are a difficult genre for AI due to their partial observability, long-term decision-making, and evolving card sets. Due to this, current AI models perform vastly worse than human players at CCG tasks such as deckbuilding and gameplay. In this work, we introduce UrzaGPT , a domain-adapted large language model that recommends real-time drafting decisions in Magic: The Gathering . Starting from an open-weight LLM, we use Low-Rank Adaptation fine-tuning on a dataset of annotated draft logs. With this, we leverage the language modeling capabilities of LLM, and can quickly adapt to different expansions of the game. We benchmark UrzaGPT in comparison to zero-shot LLMs and the state-of-the-art domain-specific model. Untuned, small LLMs like Llama-3-8B are completely unable to draft, but the larger GPT-4o achieves a zero-shot performance of 43%. Using UrzaGPT to fine-tune smaller models, we achieve an accuracy of 66.2% using only 10,000 steps. Despite this not reaching the capability of domain-specific models, we show that solely using LLMs to draft is possible and conclude that using LLMs can enable performant, general, and update-friendly drafting AIs in the future.

Introduction

For decades, novel developments in artificial intelligence have been tested on games. Games provide structure, and games allow for easy comparison, but games can also be incredibly difficult. In the past, abstract board games were the prime target for AI
(
Brown and Sandholm 2019
;
Silver et al. 2018
)
. More recently, video games and strategy games also have been explored as test environments
(
Vinyals et al. 2019
;
OpenAI et al. 2019
)
. Modern games are varied in their rules, which can even change in expansions or updates. Video games require many actions per second, fine-grained control, and efficient image processing
(
Pearce and Zhu 2022
;
Vinyals et al. 2019
)
. Strategy games often require language processing and long-horizon planning
(
Hoover et al. 2020
)
. Despite AI having achieved expert-level or even superhuman performance in games like chess and Go
(
Schaeffer et al. 2007
;
Schrittwieser et al. 2020
)
, only weak game agents exist for many contemporary games. Collectible card games (CCGs) are one of those genres for which currently, no agents exist that perform on the level of human experts.

The recent explosion of language modelling due to transformers
(
Vaswani et al. 2017
)
and large-scale pre-training
(
Radford and Narasimhan 2018
)
may provide a solution for this. Large Language Models (LLMs) compress a vast amount of information into one model and thus learn about games and strategy. LLMs are inherently language-based, which makes it simple to describe rules and abilities. This is especially crucial for CCGs, where a large part of their complexity comes from the natural language used to describe the functions of cards (see Figure
1
).

In this work, we introduce
UrzaGPT
, a domain-adapted LLM for drafting
Magic: The Gathering
, and explore its utility. Specifically, we contribute the following:

•

An evaluation of the zero-shot drafting performance of popular LLMs.

•

We fine-tune LLMs to drafting
Magic: The Gathering
and show how models can quickly be adapted to this domain.

•

An ablation on the impact of fine-tuning parameters on the performance.

We begin by discussing related work (Section
2
) and briefly explaining the most critical game concepts (Section
3
) before outlining the specifics of our method in Section
4
and discussing the results in Section
7
. All code will be released upon acceptance.

Related Work

Artificial Intelligence in Competitive Games.

In the past, artificial intelligence for games was often restricted to structured board and card games such as chess
(
Silver et al. 2018
;
Campbell, Hoane, and Hsu 2002
)
, Go
(
Silver et al. 2016
;

, checkers

The majority of agents are completely domain-specific, offering no way to adapt to a similar environment and require complete retraining on a new task.

LLM-based GameAI.

The recent advancements in natural language processing have led to a large interest in using language models as game agents

. Game environments have been used to benchmark LLM and VLM reasoning

)
, and

. Additionally, work in LLMs for games has tackled Pokémon
(

)
, Slay the Spire
(

)
, Werewolf
(

. Despite all of this work, the performance of LLM-based agents is often subpar when not making further adjustments to their actions. For example, modern LLMs still fail to play TicTacToe perfectly, with the exact prompt used and model choice having a large influence
(
Topsakal and Harper 2024
)
. In the BALROG benchmark, many models achieve some progress on the easier games, but more complicated environments like NetHack
(
Küttler et al. 2020
)
are currently out of reach.

Drafting and Deckbuilding in Collectible Card Games.
Research on game agents for collectible card games is mostly focused on using reinforcement learning or genetic algorithms
(
Dockhorn and Mostaghim 2019
;
Kowalski and Miernik 2023
)
. In both cases, heuristic agents are used to evaluate decks, while decks are either created sequentially

;

)

(

;

. Lastly, some work directly learns card selection from human data
(

Ward et al. 2021

. The current state-of-the-art in drafting prediction uses a contrastive model that projects cards and decks into a learned space to make selections
(

)
.

Our work focuses on drafting as a pure next-token prediction task. Previous work uses language embeddings to represent cards but still trains a classification model from scratch
(
Bertram, Fürnkranz, and Müller 2024
)
. We skip the classification model and directly fine-tune an LLM to predict the chosen cards.

Magic: The Gathering

Collectible card games like
Magic: The Gathering
,
Hearthstone
, or
Lorcana
, are competitive, turn-taking games. As card games, they feature a large amount of stochasticity and imperfect information. Additionally, they use thousands of unique, highly complex cards, which makes representation and adaptation difficult. In
Magic: The Gathering
, the goal of gameplay typically is to reduce the opponent’s life points to zero. To achieve that, players have to first build their decks, i.e., decide which cards they want to play with, and later use the chosen cards to win the game. This reveals two connected, but also distinct, challenges of the game:
deckbuilding
and
gameplay
. Deckbuilding is concerned with which cards to use, while gameplay regards how to use them. In this work, we solely focus on deckbuilding and disregard the gameplay. However, LLMs might also enable gameplay in the future.

Drafting

Deckbuilding in
Magic: The Gathering
exists in different forms. Before the game, players decide the format they want to use:

Constructed
allows players to freely choose from all available cards. Decks have to contain at least 60 cards, and every card can be put into a deck up to 4 times. Which cards are available depends on the chosen restrictions, but the total number of unique cards to choose from currently ranges from 4,095 when playing
Standard
to 29,084 in
Vintage
.

Drafting
significantly restricts the deckbuilding process by turning it into a sequential process. Instead of freely choosing from a large pool, players are repeatedly tasked with selecting a single card out of a
pack
of initially 15 pseudo-randomly distributed cards. Players simultaneously select one card out of the 15 and, after choosing one, pass all unchosen cards to the player next to them, who has to select from the 14 remaining cards. Thus, throughout a draft, the number of possible options reduces until all 15 cards are chosen and the whole process is repeated twice more, leading to a total of 45 per player. As a side note, drafts are typically played with 8 players. This means that every player sees every pack of cards
twice
, once with
n
cards and once with
n-8
cards, from which small amounts of information about the opponents’ preferences can be deduced. Most of the time, players draft with packs from a single
expansion
of the game, which means that only about 200-300 unique cards can appear in packs. Expansions are highly unique, with new expansions often containing mostly completely new cards. This makes it difficult to adapt to the new expansion.

In this work, we only regard drafting here and do not yet aim to model constructed deckbuilding.

Colors

While we will not explain the rules of
Magic: The Gathering
in detail here, we briefly want to explain a concept that is important for later explanations;
colors
.

In MTG, cards either require mana (
spells
) or produce mana (
lands
). Spells and mana are colored, i.e., red spells will require red mana produced by a

. Due to this restriction, it is possible to draw combinations of lands and spells that do work together, i.e., red mana and blue spells. Therefore, most decks will only contain 1 to 2 out of the 5 colors in the game. This provides an easy baseline for sensible drafting: if a model frequently picks cards not in the colors of the deck, it does not understand drafting. We will later refer back to this concept and investigate whether LLMs adhere to it.

4
Method

Our hypothesis is straightforward: LLMs excel at processing language, and
Magic: The Gathering
cards are largely language-based, so LLMs should be able to evaluate and draft cards. Since there are no fast simulators for the full game available, it is not possible to test the drafted decks at scale. Rather, we resort to human data as the ground truth and check whether the LLMs’ actions line up with the human players’.

Data

We use open-access datasets available at
17lands.com
. The datasets contain step-by-step drafts of human players, which allow us to reconstruct which card was picked at every decision point. Data is collected solely from users of the
17lands.com
website who downloaded a tool that records their games. This in itself selects for a subset of proficient players, who are invested enough into the game such that they would go through this process. Additionally, data contains information about the rating of players, allowing for additional refinement to high-ranked players if necessary.

We preprocess the datasets by transforming them into a language-only format, noting the cards currently in the player’s deck, the cards in the pack from which the player was allowed to choose, and the chosen card. From this, the task is to predict the chosen card, given the pack and the deck. Note that this is a noisy problem, as there is no objectively correct answer, but rather a prediction of human decisions. It is possible that another player would make a different decision, but we simply regard the chosen card as the ground truth.

There are multiple datasets with different
expansions
of the game available, but we restrict this study to the
NEO
dataset. The
NEO
dataset contains 5,122,921 pick decisions, although we find that we did not require all of them. We used a subset of 10,000 samples as the test set for later experiments. The data contains a mixture of drafts from
Premier draft
and
Traditional draft
, the majority of which is
Premier
. These two variations have slight differences, e.g., in
Traditional
, sideboard cards can become important, but we regard them all the same. The data contains information about the players’ proficiency, such as their rank and results, but we found no significant difference in results when filtering for high-ranked players.

Card Representation

There are two ways to describe cards in the prompt for the LLMs. One can either simply use the cards’ names, or one can use the full card text.

Names
are an easy identifier and vastly reduce the size of the prompt, which leads to increased throughput. However, only using card names makes the strong assumption that models have seen plenty of data on these cards in pre-training, such that they are able to associate a card’s name with their abilities. Including the full card
text
with all values and categories on the card seems intuitively more useful, as it provides more information, but there are issues with it. Firstly, card texts are long and thus the prompt grows immensely compared to just using card names. In our experiments, a single card required 100-300 tokens. With up to 44 cards in the deck and packs, we reach the maximum context length for some standard models, e.g., Llama-3-8B’s maximum of 8,000 tokens. In addition, it has been shown that large context sizes can lead to information retrieval issues
(
Machlab and Battle 2024
)
. In the following section, we provide a small ablation with both versions and compare their performance.

LoRA Fine-Tuning

While large language models (LLMs) possess extensive general knowledge, they are not optimized for specific downstream tasks. Fine-tuning is a common approach to adapt these models to new domains or tasks. In full fine-tuning, all model parameters are updated using a task-specific dataset. Although this can achieve strong performance, it requires significant computational resources and storage. To address these limitations,
parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT)
techniques have been proposed, which reduce the number of trainable parameters while maintaining performance.

In this work, we use
Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)

(
Hu et al. 2022
)
to fine-tune LLMs. LoRA freezes the original model weights and introduces trainable
low-rank matrices
into the attention layers. Specifically, given a weight matrix
W\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
in the transformer (e.g., a query or value projection matrix), LoRA reparameterizes it as:

W^{\prime}=W+\Delta W=W+AB

where
A\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times k}
are the low-rank matrices with rank
r\ll\min(d,k)
, and only
A
and
B
are trained during fine-tuning. This approach significantly reduces the number of trainable parameters and memory footprint.

Importantly, LoRA introduces these updates in a way that maintains the original model’s pretraining knowledge, and it can be implemented without modifying the model architecture. It has been shown to reduce the number of trainable parameters by up to 10,000
\times
compared to full fine-tuning whileachieving comparable performance on many tasks.

5
Experiments

We begin by checking the zero-shot performance of popular LLMs without using domain-specific data. Then, we move towards fine-tuning them to improve upon that baseline. All experiments use the same prompt (Appendix
B
). The prompt was iterated on several times in the zero-shot setting until we achieved decent results. Notably, the prompt only regards the current decision without a history of previous pick options, thus omitting signals that players can pick up on while drafting. This decision was made to allow for a clear comparison of architectures to prior work.

Zero-Shot Drafting with LLMs

For the zero-shot drafting experiment, we selected multiple models of similar parameter sizes (7 to 8 billion) and queried them for drafting selections out of the box.

Our prompt includes “Respond with the card name only.” but we did not negatively score models that did not adhere to it. We found that many models provided long outputs going through iterative thinking processes, so we manually evaluated whether the final decision of the model lined up with the truth in the data. Because this experiment required manual extraction of the card selection, we restricted it to 100 samples per model. Figure
3
shows the result of this.

Figure 3:
Zero-shot selection accuracy and illegal selections of different models. A correct selection is counted when the output clearly selects the true chosen card in the test data. Illegal selections are when the model provides an unclear output or selects a card that was not offered. The larger GPT-4o models perform vastly better than the smaller and distilled models. We also find that using the full card text slightly harms performance (43% vs 38%).

We see that the smaller 7-8 billion parameter models are unable to follow the prompt, which leads to illegal actions. Illegal actions are defined as either not making a concrete selection in the response of the model or by picking a card that was not available for selection. However, the large GPT-4o models both follow the prompt strictly, always answering with the card name only and always choosing a possible card. Although both of these perform better than the random baseline of 0.221, we find that including the complete card text in the prompt slightly decreases the accuracy from 0.43 to 0.38. This suggests that the models already possess knowledge of the cards, that only using card names provides enough information, and that including the full text dilutes the prompt. This is validated with knowledge prompts (Appendix
C
), which show that the small Llama model is unable to understand the card and makes errors in the description of the drafting process, while the large GPT-4o perfectly replicates the ability of the card.

Fine-Tuning

The results from the zero-shot experiment show promising results for the large models, but the 7B and 8B models are unable to draft. In this experiment, we investigate how supervised fine-tuning influences the performance.

For this, we train the
Mistral-7B-Instruct
and the
Llama-3-8B

(
Touvron et al. 2023
)
models on the aforementioned datasets. We train on 1 million picks and evaluate on a held-out test set.

Setup
We train the models on prompt completion, i.e., we give in the complete prompt (Figure
2
) and use the cards in the dataset as the target completion. To make fine-tuning more computationally efficient, we use LoRA
(
Hu et al. 2022
)
with a rank of 8 and an alpha of 16 (Appendix
A
). For training, we use a batch size of 8 with 4 gradient accumulation steps, leading to an effective batch size of 32. Training was done on 3 Nvidia A100 GPUs and took 6 hours per model.

Figure 4:
Accuracy of predicted selections of the
Llama-3-8b
and
Mistral-7B-Instruct
models when supervised fine-tuning. Accuracy quickly rises at the start and makes further improvements up to a peak of 66.2% after 10,000 steps for
Llama-3-8B
.
Mistral-7B-Instruct
reaches a lower peak accuracy of 64.3%. This performance almost reaches the accuracy of domain-specific models.

We measure two metrics; the accuracy of predictions and the adherence of the model to the primary colors of the deck (see Section
3
). For this, we simply check whether the chosen card’s color is in the two primary colors of the current deck, as given by the sum of cards of each color. This is a slightly inaccurate metric, due to staying on color not always being the correct choice, but nonetheless gives insight into the decisions.

We find that fine-tuning quickly increases the accuracy of the models, reaching an accuracy of 60% after roughly 1,000 steps (see Figure
4
). Further training increases the accuracy more, with
Llama-3-8B
reaching a peak accuracy of 66.2%.
Mistral-7B-Instruct
performs slightly worse with an accuracy of 64.3%.

Although 66.2% is a vastly higher accuracy than the initial zero-shot performance of 13%, and outperforms the zero-shot performance of the large GPT-4o models (43%), this result still falls short of the performance of domain-specific drafting models (68%). Despite this, the gap between the fine-tuned LLMs and domain-specific models is small.

Influence of Rank on Performance

We validate how the chosen parameters of the fine-tuning process influence the resulting models. Most crucially, the
rank
of the LoRA adapters controls how many parameters can be changed in the fine-tuning process, offering better adaptability when using more computation.

The influence of LoRA rank on the performance of
Llama-3-8B
. We find that increasing the rank yields better performance, both in accuracy of predictions and adherence to the color of the deck, but diminishing returns kick in at a rank of 16. This shows a clear trade-off between the parameter count of the fine-tuning process and the resulting performance.

Figure

Llama-3-8B

6
Discussion

Our results show that LLMs possess the capabilities to select cards in a drafting context with minimal exposure to domain-specific data. However, several important observations and open questions arise from our findings.

Zero-shot capabilities.
The performance gap between small and large LLMs in the zero-shot setting suggests that model scale plays a crucial role in encoding niche domains like
Magic: The Gathering
. While GPT-4o achieves reasonable zero-shot performance (43% accuracy), smaller models like Llama-3-8B struggle without additional tuning. This highlights that mere exposure to natural language isn’t sufficient to learn drafting heuristics unless the model has seen significant domain-relevant data.

Data efficiency of fine-tuning.
Remarkably, we find that with just 1,000 steps of LoRA fine-tuning, models can exceed 60% accuracy—outperforming larger, untuned models. This demonstrates strong transfer potential and the utility of supervised data, especially for structured decision-making tasks. However, performance plateaus rather quickly, indicating a ceiling imposed either by model capacity or the limits of the training objective.

Representation trade-offs.
Including the full card text in prompts surprisingly reduced performance in the zero-shot setting. This suggests that longer contexts introduce unnecessary noise or exceed the model’s ability to retrieve key information. It may also reflect pretraining limitations, where the model’s understanding of card names is learnt but full-text parsing of novel cards is not.

Limitations of accuracy metrics.
While accuracy is a straightforward measure for predicting card picks, it fails to capture the quality of full drafts or synergy across picks. Two picks may be equally valid depending on downstream deck strategy and personal preferences influence decisions. Thus, future work should consider reinforcement learning from outcomes or incorporate gameplay simulations to better evaluate utility.

Transferability and generalisation.
Our study focused on a single expansion (NEO), leaving open the question of generalisation. Can models trained on one set adapt to others with similar mechanics? Are their representations reusable across expansions? NEO was chosen to compare to prior work, but is ultimatively an arbitrary choice, thus performance should translate to other sets, but more work is required to investigate this in depth.

LoRA parameter count.
We find that introducing more trainable parameters has a positive correlation with the fine-tuned model’s performance. Thus, an open question is how far one can push this performance. Would more parameters increase accuracy further? Would full fine-tuning exceed the PEFT setting? More work is required to investigate this in depth.

Overall, our findings support the viability of LLM-based drafting agents, but also reveal limitations in LLMs’ general capability for highly strategic tasks.

7
Conclusion

This work introduces
UrzaGPT
and takes the first steps towards fully LLM-based drafting in the collectible card game
Magic: The Gathering
. We frame drafting, i.e., selecting cards, as a pure language task, prompting an LLM with the current cards in the deck and the available options.

Out of the box, smaller LLMs (7 or 8 billion parameters) do not perform well on this task. They largely do not adhere to the prompt and due to this perform worse than random. Large LLMs (GPT-4o) perform much better and achieve a 43% zero-shot accuracy. This clearly shows the general game-playing capabilities of LLMs, which seem to have been trained on
Magic: The Gathering
data. GPT-4o is able to draft solely from card names. However, we find that including the cards’ abilities in the prompt diminishes performance, likely due to diluting the prompt.

Small amounts of fine-tuning allow even the 7 billion parameter models to draft competitively. Fine-tuning the whole model is unfeasible even for the smaller models, so we use LoRA to adapt the models to the task. After only 1,000 steps with LoRA-tuning, the LLMs reach an accuracy of 60%, only 8 percentage points behind the state-of-the-art drafting model, underlining the potential of LLMs for gameplaying tasks. Further training yields increasingly better performance, but the LLMs show a lower ceiling than that of domain-specific models (66.2% vs 68%).

Altogether, we show that quick adaptation of small-scale LLMs to drafting is possible. In the future, this can enable low-cost agents and player aids that can quickly adapt to new expansions of the game.

8
Limitations and Future Work

This work is limited by its reliance on historical data. We assume that cards selected by players form an objective ground truth, despite this not being generally true. Rather than regarding each pick as an independent decision, it would be more truthful to the domain to evaluate the final deck after a complete draft. In the future, we aim to bring
UrzaGPT
to the real world, using simulators to validate the final decks and to plug into real players’ drafts.

We also find that larger models provide better results. These models might not be computationally feasible without large resources.

9
Ethical Statement

The existence of a super-human drafting agent could have an impact on the online versions of the game as it provides an advantage to players who use them. However, drafting companions already exist and are an accepted tool. Addtionally, current models are not on a human level and should only be used as auxiliary helpers and should not be followed without validation.

Appendix C
LLM Knowledge Questions

Here we ask some general
Magic: The Gathering
and drafting questions to pre-trained models. This serves to underpin their selections and investigate whether they know cards well.

Q1: How does drafting work in Magic: The Gathering and what do you have to pay attention to?

GPT-4o:
Great question! Drafting is one of the most skill-testing and fun ways to play Magic: The Gathering. It combines both deckbuilding and gameplay, and it’s a popular format for tournaments and casual play alike. Here’s how it works and what you should pay attention to:

How Drafting Works (Booster Draft Format)

1.

Players & Packs:

•

Usually 8 players sit in a pod.

•

Each player gets 3 booster packs (often from the same set or compatible sets).

2.

The Draft Process:

•

Everyone opens their first pack, picks one card, then passes the remaining cards to the left.

•

You receive a pack from the player to your right, select a card, and repeat.

•

Once all cards are drafted from the first pack, open the second pack, but pass to the right this time.

•

Third pack passes to the left again.

3.

Deck Construction:

•

After drafting, build a minimum 40-card deck, including lands.

•

Typically, you’ll have around 23 spells and 17 lands.

•

You can use as many basic lands as you want, provided by the event organizer or store.

Key Things to Pay Attention To

1.

Signaling and Reading the Table

•

Watch what colors or strategies seem open.

•

If strong cards of a color keep coming late, that color is likely under-drafted.

•

Conversely, if the color you’re drafting dries up early, others at the table may be in the same color.

2.

Color Commitment

•

Ideally, settle into two main colors, but stay flexible early on.

•

Don’t over-commit in the first few picks unless you open a powerful “bomb” worth building around.

3.

Card Evaluation

•

Prioritize:

–

Bombs:
Game-winning cards, especially rares or mythics.

–

Efficient Removal:
Cards that can deal with your opponent’s threats.

–

Efficient Creatures:
Good stats for cost, especially with abilities like Evasion (Flying, Menace).

•

Lower priority:

–

Situational cards or narrow build-around cards (unless your deck supports them).

4.

Curve Considerations

•

Pay attention to your mana curve:

–

Early drops (1-2 mana creatures) are important for pressure.

–

Midrange creatures (3-5 mana) fill your deck’s core.

–

A few high-end bombs or big creatures can finish games.

5.

Synergy vs Raw Power

•
