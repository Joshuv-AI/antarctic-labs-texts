A framework for training graduate students and campus communities in inclusive teaching
Authors: Berit E. Batterton, C. Melman Neill, Christopher R. Biggs, Hannah S. Rempel
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWhile graduate student teaching assistants (TAs) contribute significantly to university education, many graduate programs across diverse disciplines offer limited formal pedagogical training. In turn, many researchers informally develop teaching and mentoring skills as they advance to faculty positions or related careers. This can perpetuate a lag in the implementation of inclusive educational environments despite the clear benefits demonstrated by recent pedagogical research. For instance, the integration of inclusive teaching strategies like universal design for learning, growth mindset feedback, and the use of relatable role models in curricula may help increase the persistence, success, and self-efficacy of traditionally underrepresented groups in the sciences. Additionally, research indicates that training graduate TAs in evidence-based practices may have benefits beyond teaching efficacy, such as greater confidence in research preparedness and science communication—skills applicable to any scientific field or career path. Here, we developed and implemented an inclusive teaching series for a marine science department that included: (i) campus-wide pedagogical journal article discussions and knowledge-sharing, (ii) expert-led interactive workshops on evidence-based teaching strategies, and (iii) a graduate TA professional development module on inclusive lesson planning with opportunities to teach and receive feedback. Based on our experiences, we share a framework and resources to facilitate a broader adoption of formalized TA training in inclusive teaching practices within graduate programs across a variety of fields.

INTRODUCTION

While graduate student teaching assistants (TAs) play a critical role in university education (1–3), most programs across a variety of disciplines, from sciences to humanities, offer minimal formal TA training in modern pedagogy (4–7). For example, 52% of surveyed US biology departments required 10 or fewer hours of annual graduate teaching training (1), and fewer than 55% of North American sociology departments offered formal TA instruction (4). Interest in teaching is one of the leading factors motivating graduate students to pursue faculty careers (8); however, students are often left to informally develop teaching and mentoring skills as they advance to faculty positions or related careers. Despite the devaluation of pedagogical training in many programs (9), it has clear benefits, such as increasing understanding of evidence-based learning and confidence in instructional abilities (10). Importantly, hands-on teaching practice can also improve perceived socio-emotional support among undergraduates in graduate TA-taught classes (11). Moreover, greater pedagogical knowledge has broadly applicable benefits for students interested in a variety of careers, such as increased perceived research preparedness and communication ability (12–16). By providing TA training, universities cannot only enhance present and future undergraduate education, but also foster the development of teaching, mentorship, and communication skills applicable across a wide range of careers.

Modern pedagogical research emphasizes the importance of incorporating inclusive teaching strategies in university classrooms for improving undergraduate education and graduate professional development (PD) (7, 11, 17–31). Inclusive pedagogy fosters greater belonging, persistence, and self-efficacy for underrepresented groups, reducing barriers to equitable student success (18, 32, 33). Evidence-based inclusive practices include adopting universal design for learning frameworks (19, 34), with instructors recognizing their own identities and biases (35), integrating diverse perspectives and content (33, 36, 37), centering student agency and active learning (38–41), and using free, open educational resources (17, 31). Despite the demonstrated importance of these approaches, establishment of inclusive curricula and campus environments remains inconsistent in higher education (29, 42–44).

Here, we provide a framework for institutions to train graduate TAs, faculty, and staff in inclusive teaching. We share our experiences in developing and implementing a departmental inclusive teaching series that included: (i) discussions of modern pedagogical literature and community knowledge-sharing, (ii) interactive workshops led by educational experts, and (iii) a graduate PD course module whereby students design lessons and practice teaching (Fig. 1). We believe that this framework can be readily implemented in programs across a variety of disciplines to promote instructional efficacy, student success, and inclusive campus climates.

PROCEDURE

Journal article discussions

To expose our campus community to discipline-based education research (DBER) and inclusive teaching, we led two journal article discussions. The first article used quantitative approaches to assess the efficacy of strategies, such as reducing the stakes of individual exams, to address gender-based achievement gaps in undergraduate science courses (32). We chose this article because it highlights actionable inclusive teaching strategies and provides a model for translating quantitative skills to DBER. The second article described real-world college-level instructional practices that support students from diverse backgrounds and identities (31). Discussions began with a summary of the article’s focal points, followed by a lightly moderated discussion where attendees shared their reflections on the article, personal inclusive teaching experiences, and ideas for future implementation.

Educational expert workshops

To further train our campus community in evidence-based pedagogy, we hosted workshops led by educational experts, who were compensated for their time. The first focused on the importance of belonging in classroom environments, presented by an expert in the development of instructional content for socio-emotional, science, and math education. Attendees learned about how belonging can impact achievement (18, 45), as well as intervention strategies to foster self-efficacy (e.g., wise feedback; 46, 47), build supportive student–teacher relationships (e.g., banking time; 48), and mitigate stereotype threat (e.g., values affirmation; 49). The second workshop led by an expert in STEM DBER focused on humanizing biology to promote equitable classrooms. Attendees learned the importance of including relatable, counter-stereotypic role models (37, 47), diversifying curriculum materials (48, 49), and promoting ideological awareness (50). We provided a workshop summary sheet for the participants to reference in subsequent curriculum development and teaching (Appendix S2).

Curriculum development and implementation

We created a several-week module on inclusive lesson plan development and implementation for a graduate PD course. Students read an article on backward design (41), engaged in an interactive lecture (Appendix S1; S2), and explored an example 4–5th grade lesson plan (51) based on the 5E Instructional Model (52). They then incorporated inclusive teaching strategies into a lesson plan on a topic of their choice using a provided 5E template (Appendix S3; S4) and presented it for peer feedback. Our institution offers limited undergraduate courses as we host only a small cohort of spring semester students. Therefore, we utilized long-standing partnerships with local K-12 schools to provide reliable opportunities for students to gain first-hand experience in real classrooms and improve their confidence through teaching practice. In future years, we plan to integrate graduate student-led teaching (i.e., guest lectures) into undergraduate courses so that students may have the option to draft and practice college-level lessons. Offering multiple avenues for teaching practice, if possible, affords students the autonomy to develop education and communication skills at the level they deem most beneficial for their individual career goals.

CONCLUSION

Our framework for PD in inclusive teaching includes three main pathways: (i) departmental discussions of pedagogical journal articles and lived experiences, (ii) hands-on workshops led by experts in inclusive instructional strategies, and (iii) a graduate PD course module, in which students developed and practiced lesson plans. This method provided formalized training for graduate TAs while also opening discussions and workshops to the greater department—facilitating growth, knowledge sharing, and engagement throughout our community. Prior to this program, less than a third of participants had received training in inclusive teaching or outreach. Multiple faculty and postdoc participants expressed plans to incorporate strategies they learned into upcoming courses. Graduate students in the PD course reflected that “creating a lesson plan was one of the most effective aspects in helping [them] learn”, particularly when paired with peer feedback and resources, such as the “extremely helpful” sample lesson plan (Appendix S3). By extending our framework into an annual series via a graduate core course, we seek to build our campus community’s knowledge of inclusive teaching and provide students with broadly applicable education and mentorship skills to support their career success. We believe this framework can be readily adopted to facilitate PD in inclusive pedagogy for graduate TAs and broader campus communities across a variety of disciplines.
