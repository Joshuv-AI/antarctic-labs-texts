Effects of Normocalcemic Hyperparathyroidism on Structural Bone Compromise: A Prospective Observational Cohort Study
Authors: John Pietro, Amr Kaadan, Travis Cotton, Jon Olansen, Janine Molino, Roy Aaron
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNormocalcemic hyperparathyroidism (NCHPTH) is characterized by elevated serum parathyroid hormone (PTH) levels in the presence of normal serum calcium levels, with the exclusion of secondary causes. NCHPTH remains underdiagnosed and is often associated with structural compromise of bone, namely osteoporosis and fragility fractures. The purpose of this study was to perform a prospective observational cohort study investigating NCHPTH in our Metabolic Bone Disease Service (MBDS).MethodsBetween January 2022 and June 2024, 815 patients were referred to our MBDS with an International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision diagnosis code of osteoporosis. Each patient underwent dual energy X-ray absorptiometry, and when indicated, assays for serum calcium, ionized calcium, 25-hydroxy-vitamin D (25[OH]D), and PTH.ResultsNCHPTH was identified in 25 patients (3%), approximating one patient per month. Skeletal involvement was frequent in the NCHPTH cohort, with 72% presenting with a history of fragility fractures. Mean PTH levels were 130.8 pg/mL, while serum calcium and 25(OH)D levels remained within the normal range. We compared the prevalence of NCHPTH in our MBDS to other referral populations, finding an increased prevalence in our service, likely due to the high frequency of skeletal compromise in this population.ConclusionsThese results indicate that NCHPTH is not merely a biochemical phenomenon but is rather frequently associated with skeletal pathology. We conclude that reliance on serum calcium alone likely leads to underdiagnosis of NCHPTH, whereas routine PTH testing can facilitate earlier detection, allow for preventative interventions, and enhance secondary fracture prevention efforts.Keywords: Hyperparathyroidism, Orthopedics, Osteoporosis, Osteoporotic fractures, Parathyroid diseases

GRAPHICAL ABSTRACT

INTRODUCTION

A variant of primary hyperparathyroidism, normocalcemic hyperparathyroidism (NCHPTH) is defined by high parathyroid hormone (PTH) levels and normal serum calcium levels in the absence of causes of secondary hyperparathyroidism such as vitamin D deficiency, intestinal malabsorption, chronic kidney disease, hypercalciuria, and medications that interfere with calcium homeostasis.[1–3] There is uncertainty as to whether NCHPTH is a unique disease unto itself, a forme fruste of primary hyperparathyroidism, or a diagnosis of exclusion of secondary hyperparathyroidism. A number of studies have shown progression over time to hypercalcemia in some patients, whereas other studies have not.[4,5] Regardless, NCHPTH is often associated with structural damage to bone consisting of decreased bone density, hyper-resorption, increased porosity and often culminating in fractures, as presenting clinical findings.[6–9]

This is a study of NCHPTH in a Metabolic Bone Disease Service (MBDS) that functions in large part as a fracture liaison service (FLS). One of the clinical features of interest in this study is the presence of structural bone disease, separating NCHPTH from what has been described as “just a biochemical signature” in patients without bone or kidney complications.[5,10,11] Furthermore, other studies investigating NCHPTH have reported a high prevalence of fragility fractures, cortical bone loss, and impaired trabecular microarchitecture in affected populations.[12,13] Several reports have indicated that 50% to 57% of patients with NCHPTH had osteoporosis, and 46% had a fracture in adulthood.[2,10,14] Fragility fractures have been reported to occur in 11% to 21% of patients with NCHPTH.[15] Recent population-based data indicate that even modest and sustained elevations of PTH levels may attenuate trabecular bone microarchitecture. Moreover, a major study of data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) affirmed that isolated fasting PTH elevation impairs lower lumbar spine trabecular bone scores (TBS) and that this may predispose to fracture.[6]

While prevalence rates have been reported to vary in the general population from 0.4% to 0.6%,[10,16–18] those rates are higher in patients within referral centers, both MBDS and FLS, with prevalence rates of 5.2% to 7.8% being reported.[2,19] This is likely due to a concentration of the disease, given the patient population of such centers, and the detailed methodology employed for diagnosis. Unlike in primary hyperparathyroidism, serum calcium concentration cannot be relied upon as a surrogate marker to diagnose NCHPTH due to its persistently normal levels. Accurate diagnosis therefore depends on documenting persistent PTH elevation with normal serum calcium levels, alongside strict exclusion of secondary causes.[20–23] Therefore, it is particularly important that MBDS and FLS clinicians have a high index of suspicion for NCHPTH so that appropriate diagnosis and treatment can be carried out.[7] The purpose of this report is to characterize the prevalence and clinical features of NCHPTH in an MBDS. We assess the utility of routine PTH measurements to screen for NCHPTH. We suspected that NCHPTH occurs with some frequency in an MBDS, that it can be occult without recommended laboratory studies, and that it is associated with structural compromise of bone, notably osteoporosis and fractures as presenting clinical findings. The diagnosis of NCHPTH may be missed when serum calcium concentration is relied upon as a surrogate marker because it is routinely included in automated basic metabolic panels, whereas PTH measurement is often omitted from routine screening.

METHODS

After review, the Institutional Review Board determined that the study did not require ethical review. This is a prospective observational cohort study in an MBDS of patients with osteoporosis. Between 01/01/2022 and 06/01/2024, 815 new patients were seen with an International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision diagnosis code of osteoporosis. All patients underwent bone density assessment by dual energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) using a Hologic Horizon W QRB Series scanner. The initial diagnosis of osteoporosis was made by a T-score of <−2.5 at spine, hip, and/or forearm. All patients were evaluated, where appropriate, with a standard battery of laboratory tests, including PTH (Table 1). Indications for laboratory testing included osteoporosis, atypical osteopenia, and atypical fractures or fracture mechanisms. Eligibility criteria consisted of two sets of laboratory tests measuring serum PTH, serum and ionized calcium, and 25-hydroxy-vitamin D (25[OH]D). The diagnostic criteria for NCHPTH in the MBDS were elevated serum PTH, normal serum and ionized calcium, and normal 25(OH)D, on duplicate examinations, and the absence of renal or gastrointestinal dysfunction.[2,3,5,6] Exclusion criteria included suspected secondary hyperparathyroidism, other metabolic bone disease, clinical suggestion of malabsorption (e.g., bariatric surgery, celiac disease, etc.), abnormal age-adjusted estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), hypercalciuria >300 mg/24 hr, and medications that interfere with calcium homeostasis, such as thiazide diuretics, lithium, and anticonvulsants.[1,2,5,7,10,21, 24] To minimize selection bias, all patients meeting eligibility criteria during the study period were included. Laboratory tests were performed at our institutional laboratories or at a community-based laboratory. PTH concentration was determined by a two-step immunoassay. Both serum and urine calcium levels were determined colorimetrically with Arsenazo III dye. Ionized calcium levels were measured by an ion-specific electrode. Serum 25(OH)D concentrations were determined by a one-step immunoassay.

Patients with elevated PTH and normal serum calcium concentration without secondary causes, were suspected of having NCHPTH. When needed, vitamin D supplementation was used to bring the serum 25(OH)D to about 40 ng/mL. Because PTH levels are susceptible to fluctuation due to dietary calcium intake, medication, hydration levels at time of testing, and assay variability, the protocol minimized these effects to provide an accurate PTH level by conducting two PTH tests per patient spread apart over 3 months and ensured vitamin D levels were within the normal range prior to diagnosis. Primary hyperparathyroidism was identified by elevated serum calcium and PTH and those patients were referred to endocrine surgery for further evaluation and treatment. In our community, the upper limits of normal serum PTH vary by laboratory (65–91 pg/mL). For comparative purposes, PTH levels are expressed as both absolute values and as the ratio of the observed patient values (Pt) to the upper limit of normal for their respective laboratory (Lab) indicating normalized values (Pt/Lab) (Table 2). In this context, elevated PTH Pt/Lab would be >1.0. Patients with NCHPTH often underwent PTH ultrasound or 99mTc Sestamibi PTH scan (Fig. 1). If surgery was indicated, these scans were used to assist with operative planning. It is important to note that imaging plays no role in the diagnosis of NCHPTH or the indication for surgery. Treatment, including osteoporosis medications, was withheld until the final diagnosis was established.

Statistical analysis and study design

This is an observational study, and statistics are largely descriptive. Mean, median, standard deviation (SD), range, and confidence interval are reported for the continuous variables while frequency and percentage are reported for the categorical variables and compared by χ2 analysis. The sample size was based upon the primary dependent variable in the study, serum PTH concentration and was estimated with the intent to detect a difference of 2 SDs in PTH between the NCHPTH and normal cohorts. This effect size is a conservative estimate based on pilot data, which found a mean difference of 86, a pooled SD of 35.6, and a Cohen’s d of 2.4. Given these parameters, we found that a sample size of N=24 per group maintained a power of at least 90% for the comparisons we intend to examine. Statistical significance was defined as a P value less than 0.05.

RESULTS

The base cohort of 815 osteoporotic patients consisted of 676 females (83%) and 139 males (17%) of mean age 71 (range, 14–95 years). Of the 815 patients, 26 were excluded due to secondary hyperparathyroidism or failure to complete follow-up laboratory tests. Twenty-five patients (3%), or approximately one patient per month, from our osteoporotic cohort were identified as having NCHPTH based on the clinical and biochemical criteria above. Of these patients, 23 (92%) were female and 2 (8%) were male, with a mean age of 73 years (range, 58–89) consistent with the general distribution of patients in our MBDS. Other symptoms associated with primary hyperparathyroidism, such as stone formation, were not observed in this group. Of the 25 patients, 10 (40%) underwent parathyroidectomy.

NCHPTH patients presented to our MBDS with a history of structural bone compromise, including fractures and osteoporosis. Eighteen of 25 patients (72%) presented with fractures and 7/25 patients (28%) had a history of multiple fractures over time. The fracture diagnoses are presented in Table 3. No characteristic fracture patterns were identified in the population. The observed fractures were all fragility fractures, and the mean age of patients with fractures was 71.8 years. By comparison, in a random osteoporotic cohort matched for age and sex, the fracture incidence was 80%. The point is not that NCHPTH creates a greater incidence of fractures than osteoporosis, but rather that it is not protective against fractures and should be taken seriously as a condition associated with a high incidence of both metabolic and structural bone failure. NCHPTH is not simply a biochemical finding with no consequence, but rather a condition with substantial structural morbidity and functional disability, mandating accurate diagnosis and appropriate treatment intervention. The bone densities of the NCHPTH population are presented in Table 4. Mean bone densities were osteoporotic in the femoral neck and forearm, and osteopenic in the lumbar spine and total hip. Four of 25 patients (16%) had pre-existing lumbar spine surgery with implants, and no spinal DXA scores were available.

DISCUSSION

Virtually all populations of NCHPTH have been described in metabolic bone referral and FLS environments indicating not only a concentration of prevalence but also the special attention of these environments to diagnose NCHPTH given an appropriate index of suspicion and recommended diagnostic studies, particularly serum PTH.[16] MBDS and FLS provide a structured framework for the evaluation and management of fragility fractures and, thereby, an understanding of their underlying pathological causes, including osteoporosis.[25] Several studies have shown that an FLS is an effective organizational approach to secondary fracture prevention.[17,18,26–30]

Recent evidence has indicated that NCHPTH is an uncommon phenomenon, particularly when stringent contemporary diagnostic criteria are applied. In the largest rigorously screened metabolic bone referral cohort to date, 6,280 adults were referred for evaluation and only 11 individuals met the case definition requiring ≥2 normal serum calcium values, repeated elevation of PTH levels, and exclusion of secondary causes, corresponding to a prevalence of 0.18%.[20] Community-based surveys, which relied on a single calcium measurement and low normal vitamin D levels (<20 ng/mL), have yielded higher prevalence rates of 0.2% to 0.7%, however these estimates may overstate the prevalence due to the lack of strict diagnostic criteria.[21] In our MBDS, we observed an NCHPTH prevalence of 3%, approximating 1 case/month in the 29-month observation period, with the majority of patients being women, consistent with our broader MBDS demographic. A compendium of data from several endocrine referral services (N=11,957 patients) reported an average prevalence of 1.38% (range, 0.4%–8.9%).[16] In our study, the NCHPTH patients were drawn from a baseline comparison population that also had osteoporosis and fractures, and our observed prevalence should be seen in that context. The variability in estimates of prevalence reflect differences in diagnostic criteria, techniques, exclusion criteria, and definitions of normality which make it difficult to establish consistency in clinical definition.[10,31] The prevalence of structural bone failure in NCHPTH will also vary substantially depending upon the population of patients being seen. MBDS referral populations, including ours, specifically consist of patients with osteoporosis and/or fractures, rather than those presenting for screening, and NCHPTH is found in the evaluation of osteoporosis.

Regardless of its precise definition, structural compromise of bone is associated with NCHPTH, defined by either osteoporosis, fragility fractures, or both. Both magnitude and the frequency of low bone density and the degree of bone loss were found to be greatest at the distal 1/3 radius followed by the femoral neck reflecting a greater sensitivity of cortical bone sites to PTH-mediated bone turnover. In our MBDS cohort, 18/25 (72%) of our patients experienced an osteoporotic fracture at various sites. Reports from 5 bone centers of 109 patients with NCHPTH reported a prevalence of osteoporosis of 44% (range, 25%–57%).[16] By comparison, multiple reports comprising 92 NCHPTH patients showcased that 13% to 46% of them experienced fractures.[7,16] These results emphasize that NCHPTH is associated with structural bone failure,[7] providing the impetus for evaluation and therapy. Several studies have shown that isolated PTH can have a negative effect on the structure of bone. One study of 3,516 subjects derived from the 2005–2006 NHANES demonstrated a negative association between PTH and TBS of −0.0034 (95% CI, −0.0050 to 0.0017; P<0.0001).[6] In this study, serum calcium concentration averaged 9.5 mg/dL and 25(OH)D averaged 57 mg/dL indicating the isolated effect of PTH on TBS. TBS reflects bone microstructure and is related to the mechanical strength of bone.[32] Low TBS has substantial predictive value for fracture risk in prospective and cross-sectional studies.[6,33,34] Other studies, using quantitative computed tomography also demonstrated an association between serum PTH levels and bone microarchitecture.[35,36]

Occult NCHPTH can be identified by the utilization of recommended diagnostic laboratory guidelines. For many years, stakeholder individuals and institutions have recommended a biochemical evaluation of serum and urine of osteoporotic individuals and those with fragility fractures including prominently, serum PTH.[14,37,38] While constituents of the laboratory evaluation may vary somewhat among centers, between 2001 and 2020, the American Society for Bone and Mineral Research, American Association of Orthopedic Surgeons, the American Orthopedic Association, and many other societies have published consensus guidelines for secondary fracture prevention.[26,39,40] Subsequent studies have pointed out that these are often not followed in the initial work-up of osteoporosis with or without fractures.[16,41–44] The experience in our MBDS confirms that patients who have been referred to us from the community have often started on medication, usually bisphosphonates, without the recommended diagnostic evaluation. With serum calcium being an unreliable marker of NCHPTH, it is important to consider NCHPTH and to follow recommended laboratory testing guidelines as part of the initial workup, especially for fragility fractures and osteoporosis, before starting any medical therapy. PTH testing should be performed prior to instituting, or after withholding, medical therapy for osteoporosis as many of these therapies secondarily elevate the PTH levels, thus precluding an accurate diagnosis. Without appropriate biochemical testing, the prevalence of NCHPTH can be underestimated as a cause of reduced bone density and fractures. [21,44]

This study has some limitations and some advantages. This was an observational study and association but not causation can be determined. Following recommended laboratory guidelines strengthens the associations. Secondly, referral bias could lead to higher detection rates of NCHPTH due to the concentration of NCHPTH patients in the MBDS environment.

Because data is reported from an MBDS with a relative concentration of NCHTPH patients, the prevalence of the condition is not comparable to that in the general population. It is, however, consistent with other reports from referral populations and highlights the opportunity for clinicians to identify NCHPTH patients in those environments. Our population, even in an MBDS, may be too small to establish a definitive incidence or to describe stereotypical fracture patterns. Additionally, the overall fracture rate of the base population was unavailable, therefore the fracture rates of the base and NCHPTH populations could not be compared. However, we observed that all of our NCHPTH population exhibited structural compromise of bone, which should be considered a skeletal manifestation of NCHPTH and provides a justification for treatment intervention, which is supported by the literature.[6]

Our observations have led to several conclusions. (1) NCHPTH remains a diagnostically elusive condition that may be underrecognized in osteoporotic patients, or those with a history of fragility fractures, particularly when depending upon serum calcium concentration as the sole screening marker; (2) The clinical and radiological aspects of NCHPTH are difficult to quantify because of the differing evaluative methods used to rule out secondary hyperparathyroidism and by the small sample sizes in most studies leading to under-recognition of NCHPTH in osteoporotic patients, or those with a history of fragility fractures [1]; (3) In our MBDS, we identified NCHPTH in 3% of our osteoporotic patients, most of whom had a fracture history. Osteoporosis and fractures should be considered as “presenting symptoms” that are skeletal manifestations of elevated PTH and should be included in treatment decisions; (4) These findings support the recommendation that a structured diagnostic approach, which incorporates guideline-driven serum PTH screenings, can enable early detection of NCHPTH that would be otherwise missed and that coexisting structural bone damage, either osteoporosis or fractures, indicates treatment interventions or detailed follow-up. Future studies are warranted to investigate the long-term outcomes of NCHPTH-focused interventions and to assess the cost-effectiveness and broader integrations of routine PTH screenings in MBDS clinics.
