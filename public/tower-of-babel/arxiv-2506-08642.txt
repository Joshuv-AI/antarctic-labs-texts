Contact temporal network during motility induced phase separation
Italo Salas, Francisca Guzmán-Lastra, Denisse Pastén, Ariel Norambuena

Abstract
Motility-induced phase separation (MIPS) is a paradigmatic non-equilibrium transition in active matter, determined by the Péclet number and packing fraction. We investigate the single-phase and phase-separated regimes of MIPS using a complex network approach, where networks are constructed from particle interactions over finite time windows. In the single-phase (gas-like) regime, the degree distributions P ⁡ ( k ) P(k) exhibit Gaussian behavior and resemble those of random graphs. Plotting the location and height of the P ⁡ ( k ) P(k) peak reveals a universal curve across different Péclet numbers at fixed packing fraction. In the phase-separated regime, we analyze the dense and dilute phases independently. The P ⁡ ( k ) P(k) distributions unveil distinct collective dynamics, including caging in the dense phase and the emergence of active solid-like structures at longer times. Clustering coefficients and average path lengths in both phases stabilize rapidly, indicating that short simulations are sufficient to capture essential network features. Overall, our results show that network metrics expose both universal and phase-specific aspects of active matter dynamics. Notably, we identify distinct and previously unreported topological structures arising in the dense and dilute phases within the MIPS regime.



I
Introduction

Active Brownian particles (ABP) serve as a valuable framework for investigating the non-equilibrium dynamics and statistical properties of self-propelled agents, whether they are living organisms or artificial entities
Romanczuk et al. 2012
;
Bechinger et al. 2016
;
Cates and Nardini 2024
;
Tan et al. 2022
. In contrast to passive Brownian particles studied in random diffusive systems, ABP offers a new dimension of complexity because of their inherent particle self-propulsion mechanisms. In particular, these active particles undergo a distinctive phase transition known as Motility-Induced Phase Separation (MIPS).

In this phenomenon, purely repulsive interactions and persistent self-propulsion spontaneously drive the system into coexisting dense and dilute phases
Fily and Marchetti 2012
;
Redner et al. 2013
;
Stenhammar et al. 2014
;
Theers et al. 2018
;
Van Der Linden et al. 2019
. MIPS highlights how activity alone can trigger phase transitions without the need for attractive forces, emphasizing fundamental distinctions between equilibrium and active systems. Despite significant advances in theoretical, computational, and experimental investigations
Stenhammar et al. 2014
;
de Castro et al. 2023
;
de Castro et al. 2021
;
Cates and Nardini 2024
;
Gompper et al. 2020
, critical aspects of MIPS, at high densities, such as microscopic dynamics, structural complexities, and the temporal evolution of these phase-separated states, remain not yet understood..

The rich out-of-equilibrium ABP dynamics motivates a topological understanding of emergent phenomena using the language of complex networks. In particular, self-organization and phase transitions can be understood in terms of the temporal connections between particles. The contact particle-particle interactions within this system are still a significant topic of interest, where the density effects on self-diffusive dynamics, contact time, and effective velocity were recently studied from the kinetic theory lens
Soto et al. 2024
;
Soto 2025
. Phase separation and clustering have also been analyzed in terms of particle shape
Theers et al. 2018
, inertia
Caprini et al. 2022
, chirality
Levis and Liebchen 2019
;
Ma and Ni 2022
, hydrodynamic interactions
Zöttl and Stark 2023
;
Theers et al. 2018
;
Tan et al. 2022
;
Petroff et al. 2015
, and quorum sensing
Ridgway et al. 2023
. In recent years, agent-based models such as ABP have been used to model contagion dynamics on top of compartmental ecological models
Norambuena et al. 2020
;
de Castro et al. 2023
;
Forgács et al. 2022
, and temporal networks have been used to track burstiness or clusterization in this type of system
Richardson and Gorochowski 2015
;
Gorochowski and Richardson 2017
;
Zhong et al. 2023
;
Bhaskar et al. 2021
.

In recent years, studies conducted by complex networks have been applied to many natural systems such as social interactions
Newman 2001
;
Kertész et al. 2021
;
Gonzalez et al. 2008
, seismology
Abe and Suzuki 2006
;
Telesca and Lovallo 2012
;
Martin and Pastén 2021
;
Pastén et al. 2018
, space plasmas
Suyal et al. 2014
;
Mohammadi et al. 2021
;
Saldivia et al. 2024
, or biological interactions
Thiery and Sleeman 2006
;
Barabási et al. 2011
;
Scabini et al. 2021
;
Riley 2007
;
Stockmaier et al. 2021
, among others. The formalism of complex networks can reveal nontrivial behavior in systems due to their geometrical and topological foundations
Newman 2003
;
Albert and Barabási 2002
. Through complex network analysis, it is possible to characterize and describe a physical system based on its topological properties, as well as to identify the type of network according to the structural organization of connections between nodes, for example, a random network
P. and Rényi 1960
, a scale-free network
Barabási and Albert 1999
, or a small-world network
Watts and Strogatz 1998
. By representing systems as networks of interconnected nodes, this approach enables a quantitative and qualitative analysis of the underlying interaction patterns and their temporal evolution. However, applying complex network analysis to active matter, particularly ABP systems undergoing phase transitions such as MIPS, remains largely unexplored. In this work, we bridge this gap by introducing a novel approach that leverages complex network analysis to characterize the temporal complex networks formed by ABPs during motility-induced phase separation.

We systematically construct networks based on particle interactions over varying time windows to investigate how local particle contacts evolve into global structural properties. Our analysis reveals that the network structure resembles a random graph with scale-free behavior in the single-phase regime, capturing features analogous to an active gas. Conversely, we identify distinct and previously unreported network topologies for the dense and dilute phases in the MIPS regime, uncovering nontrivial correlations and structural complexity.

III
Complex Networks for Active Brownian Particles

A network is defined as a group of nodes connected by edges, where the connections can be either directed or undirected. In our model, each node corresponds to an individual ABP, and their interactions define the connections: two nodes are connected if
r_{ij}\leq C_{r}
within a given time window. A time window is defined as several consecutive time steps, expressed in terms of
t_{r}
, as schematically shown in Fig.
2
. Based on this criterion, we constructed a time-dependent network by tracking the dynamics of each particle across the regimes previously described, using time windows of increasing widths ranging from very short intervals (
W=0.75t_{r}
) to much longer ones (
W=30t_{r}
). However, the phase-separated (MIPS) case requires a slightly different approach to analyze each phase separately.

In the MIPS regime, we use the DBSCAN algorithm
Ester et al. 1996
, which relies on two parameters:
\epsilon_{s}
, the maximum distance between two particles for one to be considered within the neighborhood of the other, and
m_{s}
, the minimum number of neighboring particles required to define a cluster. We set
\epsilon_{s}=C_{r}
and
m_{s}=4
throughout simulations. Using the DBSCAN method, it is possible to identify each particle as belonging either to the large cluster (dense phase) or not (dilute phase) at each time step. This classification allowed us to analyze the properties of each phase separately.

III.1
Adjacency matrix

The adjacency matrix
A
is the building block for representing a complex network since it encapsulates information about node connections. Temporal connections between ABPs are considered if
r_{ij}\leq C_{r}
, then
A_{ij}=A_{ji}=1
; otherwise,
A_{ij}=0
. Thus, we obtain an undirected network in a given time window. Specifically, if
r_{ij}\leq C_{r}
, then
A_{ij}=A_{ji}=1
; otherwise,
A_{ij}=0
, thus we obtain an undirected network.

In the MIPS regime, a distinct procedure is used to compute
A_{ij}
. When analyzing each phase separately, we impose an additional condition: since a particle may transition between phases within a single time window (though not at the same time step), particles
i
and
j
are considered connected only if they belong to the same phase at a given time. As a result, a single particle may contribute to the adjacency matrices of both the dense and dilute phases. We compute key network metrics from these matrices, including the degree distribution, global clustering coefficient, and average path length. These quantities provide valuable insight into the structure and organization of the underlying interaction network.

III.2
Degree distribution
P(k)

To characterize the structure of a complex network, we begin by analyzing the degree distribution. The degree
k_{i}
of a node
i
is defined as the number of unique encounters the corresponding particle experiences within a given time window, and is defined as

k_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}A_{ij}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}A_{ji},

(5)

where the adjacency matrix elements
A_{ij}
are computed as explained in Sec.
III.1
. The degree distribution
P(k)
read as

P(k)=\frac{n_{k}}{N},

(6)

with
n_{k}
representing the total number of particles with degree
k
, and
N
the total number of particles in the system. The above metric can capture the statistical properties useful to classify the resulting networks, for instance, as random (Erdős-Rényi
P. and Rényi 1960
), scale-free (Barabási-Albert
Barabási and Albert 1999
), or of another type. More importantly, changes in
P(k)
can signal structural transitions in the network
pasten2017time
, which will be useful to characterize different ABP regimes explored in this work. From an active matter perspective,
P(k)
encodes how particles interact over time and reflects the evolving nature of connectivity in the system regarding the Péclet number and packing fraction. Depending on the topology of a network, the degree distribution could show different behaviors
Erdős and Rényi 1959
;
Barabási and Albert 1999
;
Albert and Barabási 2002
;
Newman 2003
.

III.3
Network Global clustering coefficient

Another key property of complex networks is the small-world phenomenon
Watts and Strogatz 1998
. Watts and Strogatz introduced a network model that exhibits small-world characteristics, namely a low average path length combined with a high clustering coefficient. These features distinguish small-world networks from random graphs and regular lattices, which lack a balance of local and global connectivity. The local clustering coefficient (defined for each node) and the global clustering coefficient (defined for the entire network) can be analyzed to quantify this behavior. The clustering coefficient
Newman et al. 2000
;
Newman 2003
measures the tendency of nodes to form tightly connected groups, with higher values indicating a greater propensity for clustering. The global coefficient, in particular, captures the overall tendency of the network to form such clusters, and is defined as:

C=\frac{\displaystyle{\sum_{i,j,k}A_{ij}A_{jk}A_{ki}}}{\frac{1}{2}\displaystyle{\sum_{i}k_{i}(k_{i}-1)}},\quad\quad 0\leq C\leq 1.

(7)

Note that the clustering coefficient is a quantity accumulated over time windows; therefore, in this context, a clustering coefficient close to 0 represents a complex network without triplets, and a clustering coefficient similar to 1 represents a fully connected complex network.

V
Conclusions

In this work, we studied the behavior of Active Brownian Particles (ABPs) by constructing and analyzing complex networks based on particle contacts over finite-time windows. We explored how the network structure evolves as the time window increases, both in the single-phase regime (before phase separation) and the Motility-Induced Phase Separation (MIPS) region. By tracking key network metrics such as degree distribution, clustering coefficient, and average path length, we uncovered how time-accumulated interactions give rise to qualitatively distinct network topologies in each regime. Moreover, complex networks provide a universal language for characterizing interaction structures and allow for cross-comparison between different systems. Our analysis found that degree distribution, clustering coefficient, and average path length encode meaningful information about local confinement, correlations, and connectivity among particles.

In the single-phase region, we combined numerical simulations with phenomenological analysis to explain the emergence of Gaussian degree distributions consistent with random contact networks. By computing the mean free path, we derived an expression for the critical packing fraction from dilute (single-phase) to the dense regime. One of the main advantages of a network-based framework for studying phase transitions is that in the single-phase (gas-like) regime, the network structure collapses onto a universal form for the maximum value of
P(k)
, largely independent of the Péclet number and packing fraction. This regime is characterized by Gaussian degree distributions, low clustering coefficient
C
, and small average path length
l_{G}
, consistent with random interactions.

The contact networks exhibited broader degree distributions and higher clustering in the MIPS regime, where ABP spontaneously separated into dense and dilute regions because of persistent motion and excluded volume interactions. We constructed the complex networks separately for the dilute (fluid-like) and dense (hexagonal) phases to gain deeper insight. Although the dilute phase is often treated as a gas in the literature, our analysis revealed key differences from the single-phase gas, which is evident from its complex network structure. Specifically, the degree distribution in the dilute phase remains unchanged, mainly with an increasing time window, with pairwise encounters being more probable than interactions involving more than ten particles. Notably, we observed a plateau in the degree distribution for
k\sim 10-100
, corresponding to particles that travel along the contact wall bordering the dense phase.

In the dense phase, the network structure in short time windows is dominated by local hexagonal configurations, where about
k\sim 6
neighbors typically cage particles. However, as the time window increases, the degree distribution broadens, reflecting the growing probability of large-scale collective rearrangements. These include dislocations, bubbles, and dynamic reconfigurations that resemble an active solid
Digregorio et al. 2018
;
Goswami et al. 2025
, with particles showing connections up to
k\approx 100
.

Our results highlight the power of complex network analysis in characterizing dynamic phase transitions in out-of-equilibrium systems. Furthermore, this approach facilitates the recognition of similar transitions in related systems, such as granular media or collective animal behavior, by framing the phase in terms of complex network metrics like degree distribution, clustering coefficient, and average path length. This framework opens up new avenues for studying collective behavior in a wide range of contexts, from bacterial colonies and ant swarms to human crowds, through the lens of their contact networks. Moreover, the network-based approach offers tools for tracking and potentially controlling clustering in both living and synthetic active matter, with implications for understanding processes such as disease transmission. Finally, the methodology developed here lays the groundwork for future studies involving more complex interactions, including polarization, alignment, hydrodynamic effects, taxis, confined geometries, and control protocols.
