Peliosis Hepatis Complicated by Scleroderma and Rapidly Progressing to Liver Failure
Authors: Satoru Hagiwara, Hiroshi Ida, Takaaki Chikugo, Yoriaki Komeda, Naoshi Nishida, Akihiro Yoshida, Tomoki Yamamoto, Takuya Matsubara, Masatoshi Kudo
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
In November 2019, a 76-year-old woman was diagnosed with limited cutaneous scleroderma caused by Raynaud’s phenomenon and skin hardening on the periphery of the extremities. In October 2022, blood tests and abdominal ultrasonography revealed liver dysfunction and multiple liver masses, respectively. In November 2022, a percutaneous liver mass biopsy revealed peliosis hepatitis, so the patient was referred to Kindai University Hospital, and signs of liver failure were observed. Considering her age, the patient was ineligible for liver transplantation, and she died in September 2023. Peliosis hepatis complicated by scleroderma has not been previously reported, so we report this as a valuable case.

Introduction

Peliosis hepatis is a rare disease characterized by dilated sinusoids and an irregularly shaped blood-retention cavity approximately 1-10 mm in length within the liver (1,2). Cases range from being localized in one part of the liver to occurring diffusely throughout the liver, and in some cases, differentiating them from tumors is difficult. They are thus categorized as tumor-like lesions according to the World Health Organization's histological classification of liver tumors. Various causes of peliosis hepatis have been reported, and a relationship with collagen disease has been suggested (3-6).

We herein report a case of peliosis hepatis complicated by limited cutaneous scleroderma that rapidly progressed to liver failure.

Case Report

A 76-year-old woman presented to our hospital for the comprehensive treatment of a liver mass. She had a history of scleroderma and no history of alcohol consumption or smoking.

The history of the current illness

In November 2019, the patient presented with Raynaud's phenomenon and skin hardening on the peripheral parts of the extremities and was diagnosed with limited cutaneous scleroderma. The patient was followed up without any medication. In October 2022, a blood sample revealed liver dysfunction, and abdominal ultrasonography revealed multiple liver masses. The patient was referred to a nearby hospital in November 2022 for the examination and treatment of the liver mass. A percutaneous liver mass biopsy was performed, which resulted in a diagnosis of peliosis hepatis. Abdominal magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) in February 2023 showed an enlarged liver mass; however, malignant disease could not be ruled out. Therefore, the patient was referred to Kinki University Hospital.

Presenting symptoms during hospitalization

The following parameters were recorded on presentation: height 155 cm, weight 75.0 kg, body temperature 36.7°C, blood pressure 149/78 mmHg, pulse rate 76 beats/min, and respiratory rate 16 breaths/min. Furthermore, clear consciousness, no palpebral conjunctival anemia, no yellowing of the bulbar conjunctiva, a flat/soft liver, and a nonpalpable spleen were reported. No oral medications were administered during presentation.

Blood test findings at referral admission

Although hepatobiliary enzymes were slightly elevated, no decrease was reported in prothrombin time (PT), albumin, or platelet count, and the liver reserve was maintained. In addition, no increase in the ammonia concentration was observed. Hepatitis virus markers were negative. Immune function tests revealed no increase in immunoglobulin (Ig) G; however, antinuclear antibodies were 640, and centromere antibodies were significantly elevated at 500 U/mL. All other antibodies, including double-stranded DNA and ribonucleoprotein antibodies, yielded negative findings (Table).

Contrast-enhanced CT revealed multiple low-density areas, measuring up to 3 cm, in both lobes of the liver. The lesion was considered ischemic in the arterial-dominant and portal vein-dominant phases, although some blood vessels running inside the mass were observed in the portal vein-dominant phase. Contrast-enhanced MRI findings during the diagnosis at another hospital

The liver mass showed low and high signal intensities on T1- and T2-weighted imaging, respectively. Although blood vessels were partially present inside the mass in the portal vein-dominant phase, the lesion was considered ischemic in the arterial and portal vein-dominant phases. In the equilibrium phase, it was considered a bloody lesion with blood vessels inside the mass. In the hepatobiliary phase, where vascular images were examined, the ethoxybenzyl uptake was reduced. Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) showed isointensity. In addition, the apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) map showed isointensity similar to that of DWI (Fig. 2).

Abdominal contrast-enhanced ultrasound findings

Irregular, faint hyperechoic activity was observed diffusely in both liver lobes in the B-mode. No deep staining was observed in the early vascular phase, and a hypovascular area was detected. Numerous hypovascular areas were found in the portal phase, and no delayed staining was observed, even five minutes after contrast imaging. In the postvascular phase, the mass showed a defect. In addition, the blood vessels penetrated this defect without deviation or stenosis (Fig. 4).

Abdominal contrast ultrasound findings. (a) In B-mode, irregularly shaped pale hyperechoic waves were observed diffusely in both liver lobes. (b) In the early vascular phase, no deep staining was observed, and many hypovascular areas were seen. (c) Even in the portal phase, hypovascular areas were observed. (d) The post-vascular phase indicated a defect, and the existing blood vessels penetrated through the defect without deviation or stenosis.

Contrast-enhanced CT findings at seven months after referral

Contrast-enhanced CT revealed a diffuse high-density area within the liver. The low-density mass observed on earlier CT was no longer detectable. In the hepatic arterial, portal venous, and equilibrium phases, the contrast effect persisted in both lobes of the liver, suggesting multiple blood retention cavities due to peliosis hepatis. Furthermore, ascites retention was observed, and a gastrorenal shunt was formed, suggesting portal hypertension (Fig. 5).

Clinical course after referral

The patient was referred to Kinki University Hospital because the liver mass had rapidly increased in size, and malignant disease was suspected. We suspected an increase in peliosis hepatis because a biopsy of the liver mass at another hospital showed a typical pathological pattern of peliosis hepatis. Furthermore, upper and lower endoscopy revealed no malignant disease that could be a primary tumor, and positron emission tomography-CT showed no accumulation of fluorodeoxyglucose, ruling out the possibility of angiosarcoma. Therefore, we followed the case to monitor the increase in peliosis hepatis. A percutaneous liver mass biopsy is contraindicated in patients with peliosis hepatis due to the risk of bleeding; therefore, a re-biopsy was not performed. The hepatobiliary enzyme levels worsened over time, and in September 2023, the total bilirubin level had decreased to 19.0 mg/dL and the PT value to 34.7%. The patient ultimately died due of liver failure (Fig. 7).

Discussion

Various causative agents have been reported for the etiology of peliosis hepatis, including anabolic steroids, azathioprine, oxaliplatin, oral contraceptives, glucocorticoids, tamoxifen, and danazol (7-11). Infectious diseases, such as tuberculosis and acquired immunodeficiency syndrome, as well as toxic substances, including polyvinyl chloride and arsenic, are also known to be contributing factors (12,13). Furthermore, a relationship with malignant tumors has been suggested, with many reports describing the involvement of blood diseases, such as malignant lymphoma and multiple myeloma (14).

Recently, numerous reports have suggested a relationship with collagen disease, particularly in patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (3-6). However, glucocorticoids and immunosuppressants are occasionally used in patients with collagen disease, possibly contributing to peliosis hepatis. Although the present patient had scleroderma, no pulmonary or renal lesions were observed, and the diagnosis was localized scleroderma. Localized scleroderma is less frequently associated with organ damage than systemic scleroderma (15,16). Therefore, the relationship between scleroderma and hepatic purpura in this case was unclear. However, no studies have previously reported the combination of scleroderma and hepatic purpura, so this is a valuable case, prompting the publication of this report.

The definitive diagnosis of peliosis hepatis is based on pathological examinations. Peliosis hepatis is a vascular lesion that has been reported to pose a high risk when performing a percutaneous liver biopsy (17-19). Therefore, a laparoscopic liver biopsy should be performed under direct visualization (20). In this case, liver angiosarcoma was suspected at another hospital, so a percutaneous liver biopsy was performed. Although bleeding was absent, peliosis hepatis should be considered as a differential diagnosis, and a laparoscopic liver biopsy is required. If the trigger is clear during peliosis healing, removing it will reduce or eliminate the condition in many cases. The course of peliosis can be improved by discontinuing triggering drugs, preventing exposure to chemicals, and treating infections. However, 20-50% of cases are idiopathic, and some of these patients have a progressive course. Portal hypertension, such as hepatomegaly and ascites, can be fatal in the short term due to liver failure and intra-abdominal hemorrhaging (21-25). Although this case was complicated by scleroderma, the condition was stable, and the cause of the peliosis was unknown. No effective treatment was found, and the patient ultimately died of liver failure approximately 11 months after the diagnosis of peliosis hepatitis. In patients with idiopathic peliosis hepatis, the type of cases in which the condition rapidly progresses is unclear; thus, further elucidation of the pathology of peliosis hepatis is required.

A limitation of this report is that no pathological autopsy was performed; therefore, whether or not exacerbation of peliosis hepatis was the cause of liver failure cannot be confirmed. However, CT and MRI performed seven months after the patient was introduced revealed findings (pooling) that suggested significant sinusoidal dilatation compared to the previous CT and MRI findings. Therefore, it is speculated that sinusoidal disorder caused by peliosis hepatis was the cause of liver failure.

In conclusion, we encountered a case of peliosis hepatis complicated by scleroderma, which rapidly progressed to liver failure. There have been no reports of peliosis hepatis complicated by scleroderma, and since it rapidly progressed to liver failure, this case is of significance when considering the etiology and pathology of peliosis hepatis. The pathogenesis of peliosis should be further elucidated in future studies.
