Root-oriented replantation with socket reconstruction as a regenerative strategy for periodontally hopeless teeth: a pilot feasibility report
Authors: Yingman Liu, Haoning Tang, Changjian Li, Xin Zhang, Jing Wang, Yuwei Zhan, Chunhao Yang, Jing Cao, Huan Bian, Yanfeng Li
Published: 2026

Background

Periodontitis, a clinically prevalent chronic infectious disease, is characterized by the progressive destruction of periodontal supporting tissues [1]. Severe periodontal tissue loss that exceeds beyond the apical third of the root results in critically compromised physiological tooth function. Subsequently, the teeth often exhibit clinical manifestations, such as degree III mobility, pathological migration, and occlusal trauma. Even with non-surgical and surgical periodontal treatment, long-term survival and treatment success remain unpredictable. Generally, the teeth are diagnosed as periodontally hopeless teeth (PHT) [2]. In most cases, these teeth would be extracted during the initial therapy.

Advances in periodontal biology establish intentional replantation (IR) as a viable option for natural tooth preservation. Currently, IR has been clinically applied for preserving teeth with refractory periapical periodontitis or severe periodontal destruction [3–5]. The IR procedure involves multiple steps: atraumatic tooth extraction, root surface debridement, endodontic treatment, and subsequent reinsertion of the tooth into its original socket. Current evidence indicates that IR is contraindicated for cases with inadequate supporting bone due to buccal or lingual alveolar bone defects, or because of insufficient alveolar bone in the furcation area, among other reasons [3, 6].

High-level evidence regarding IR in teeth without prior root canal treatment remains limited. The majority of clinicians advocate performing endodontic treatment either preoperatively or concurrently if the pulp is infected. Otherwise, root canal therapy should be considered 2 weeks post-replantation [7, 8]. Preliminary findings indicated that alveolar bone loss extends to the apical region of the root, and pulpal inflammation or necrosis was frequently observed in the affected teeth [9]. Several studies suggested that inflammation in the apical pulp could be affected by periodontitis, even when the coronal pulp remains unaffected [10]. Endodontic intervention is often warranted for such teeth in IR. The first postoperative month represents a critical phase for periodontal healing in replanted teeth. The risk of replantation failure will inevitably increase due to potential pulp necrosis if endodontic treatment is delayed. Conventional root canal therapy has a reported success rate exceeding 90% [11]. Nevertheless, endodontic retreatment or periradicular surgery may be required, especially in cases in which there is pre-existing periapical pathosis. During the replanting procedure, the extraction enables direct extraoral visualization of apical pathology. In this situation, the treatment strategy warrants re-evaluation: should conventional root canal treatment, despite its well-documented failure rates, remain the standard of care? Is the coronal access still the optimal solution? These questions compel us to explore potentially superior alternative therapeutic strategies.

Robotic systems are increasingly being applied in medicine, and they have demonstrated exceptional accuracy and procedural stability in implantology [12]. Our research is the first to propose a therapeutic strategy utilizing a robotic system to reconstruct the alveolar socket based on the root morphology, followed by root-oriented replantation (ROR) to regenerate periodontal support tissues. This approach aims to preserve PHT and offers the potential to broaden the clinical application of conventional tooth replantation. Furthermore, ROR reduces the clinical crown height of replanted teeth and effectively prevents occlusal trauma during the initial healing phase. The procedure incorporates extraoral retrograde root canal therapy (retro-RCT) that completes pulp tissue removal and three-dimensional (3D) apical sealing. These technical advantages enhance the success rate of ROR.

Preoperative preparation and surgical protocol

Initial periodontal therapy and oral hygiene instructions were provided before surgery. Patients underwent pulp vitality testing using an electric pulp test (SybronEndo elements™ diagnostic, SybronEndo, Mexico) and cold test. The affected tooth exhibited vital pulp with an electric pulp tester reading of 33 (positive response comparable to control teeth), along with a positive response to the cold test. Cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) scans were acquired and stored in the Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) format. The morphology of the dentition was captured using an intraoral scanner (TRIOS 5, 3Shape, Denmark) and was exported as a standard tessellation language (STL) file. Then, the STL and DICOM datasets were imported into the robotic planning software (DentalNavi, YakeRobot Technology Ltd, China) for 3D reconstruction. In the software, precise surgical planning, the socket reconstruction procedure, and the design of the surgical guide were performed.

Subsequently, the workflow for socket reconstruction was planned. Within the planning software, the 3D morphology of the tooth root was precisely segmented using a combination of manual and semi-automatic segmentation tools. A uniform 0.3 mm virtual layer was added to this 3D root model to define the morphology of the future alveolar socket. The definitive post-replantation position of the tooth was determined based on the apical alveolar bone condition, the positions of adjacent teeth, and the surrounding tissues. Based on this target morphology, the precise preparation path, depth, and angulation for the surgical drills were planned. The socket reconstruction procedure (approximately 30 min) and tooth preparation (15 min) were performed concurrently by separate surgical teams to minimize the total extra-oral time. Postoperative evaluation

Postoperative CBCT scans were immediately acquired to assess the positional accuracy of the replanted tooth. Patients were instructed to mouthrinse with 0.12% chlorhexidine twice daily for two weeks and avoid hard-food mastication for three months. Sutures were removed at two weeks. The follow-ups were scheduled at one, three, and six months post-surgery.

Results

The procedure was completed without any intraoperative complications. The accuracy of the robot-assisted replant placement was evaluated by superimposing postoperative CBCT images onto preoperative virtual planning data using 3D deviation analysis software (DentalNavi, YakeRobot Technology Ltd, China). The accuracy demonstrated coronal deviation of 1.10 mm, apical deviation of 1.13 mm, and angular deviation of 1.658° relative to the preoperatively planned position. Postoperative pain responses were evaluated using the Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) scoring system [12]. Pain VAS scores decreased from 6 preoperatively to 1 on the day of surgery and 0 at 48 h postoperatively. The clinical improvements were observed 3 months after treatment. The teeth achieved complete stability. The following clinical parameters were recorded (Fig. 3): (1) healthy gingiva, (2) probing depth: 3 mm, (3) absence of bleeding on probing and suppuration, (4) Compared to the immediate post-surgical period, the gingival margin shifted apically by approximately 1 mm, and (5) During the 5-month follow-up, no signs of ankylosis were detected (absence of metallic percussion sound), and the tooth maintained physiological mobility. Radiographically, alveolar bone height increased to the coronal third of the root, with no periapical radiolucency observed. Compared with the immediate postoperative period, the position of the alveolar ridge crest showed no significant change (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Preserving natural teeth remains the objective in the field of dentistry. Our research has innovatively introduced ROR, a technique designed to preserve PHT (anterior or posterior) by utilizing the apical alveolar bone. The progressive integration of robotic systems into clinical practice has enabled the precise reconstruction of alveolar sockets lost to periodontal destruction. This advancement broadens the clinical scope of tooth replantation and provides a new therapeutic strategy for preserving PHT.

Intentional apical repositioning of the clinical crown is a deliberate design feature of ROR, serving multiple biological and restorative objectives. First, this positioning reduces the crown-to-root ratio and eliminates occlusal trauma during the critical initial healing phase. Second, it accommodates the biological reality that periodontal fibers cannot attach to enamel. Thus, a controlled apical migration of the gingival margin toward the cementoenamel junction is anticipated postoperatively. Supracrestal tissue attachment (biologic width) served as a critical reference during preoperative virtual planning of the replanted tooth position. The definitive restoration (veneer) was delayed until soft tissue stabilization was achieved (≥ 3 months postoperatively), allowing for physiologic tissue remodeling. The restorative margin was subsequently placed at the stabilized gingival level. This strategy, combined with the precisely established alveolar bone level achieved through robotic preparation, ensures a healthy and stable periodontal environment. The favorable probing depths (3 mm) and gingival health observed at follow-up support this approach, indicating successful re-establishment of the biologic width and harmonious relationship with adjacent teeth.

Dental plaque remains the primary etiological factor in periodontal disease. Plaque control is the key to periodontal therapy [14]. However, periapical microbial invasion is frequently present in PHT. Conventional periodontal surgery often fails to eradicate such infection due to limited access and restricted instrumentation. In this study, ROR offered a distinct advantage. It enables thorough removal of root-surface calculus and plaque via combined mechanical and chemical methods under direct visual guidance, which generated conditions for the preservation of teeth. Proper periodontal ligament regeneration remains the therapeutic goal [15]. In teeth with periodontitis, IR rarely achieves true periodontal ligament (PDL) healing, with most clinical outcomes dominated by ankylosis. Nevertheless, the pursuit of strategies to promote PDL regeneration holds undeniable significance [16, 17]. Clinically, while conventional surgery or IR may address vertical bone resorption, horizontal bone defects, particularly extending to the apex, remain a therapeutic challenge [3]. Accordingly, our research group has developed the ROR technique to maximize the preservation and leverage the apical alveolar bone to achieve periodontal remodelling. Periodontal regeneration primarily relies on the survival of PDL stem cells on the root surface, within the alveolar socket, or in adjacent granulation tissue [18–20]. Intraoperatively, to preserve the biologically active granulation tissue of the root surface, magnification-assisted ultrasonic root debridement under magnification was meticulously employed. The extracted teeth were continuously immersed in saline to maintain cellular viability. Compared with scaling and root planning using hand curettes, ultrasonic instrumentation reduced cementum loss. Cementum takes effect in promoting periodontal reattachment and minimizes root resorption due to its greater resistance to resorptive processes, as compared with dentin [21–24].

The precise 3D reconstruction of the socket constituted a determinant. It aimed to maximize alveolar bone preservation and to substantially improve the tooth’s long-term prognosis. In the field of autologous tooth transplantation, full-guidance templates and 3D tooth models are applied to assist in socket preparation [25, 26]. Undoubtedly, the root presents a highly complex 3D anatomy. In current clinical practice, surgical templates and 3D-printed models are employed by clinicians to visualize the general contours of the alveolar socket. However, they demonstrate limitations in micro-scale 3D morphological reconstruction, and they fail to achieve a precise reproduction of the alveolar socket that fully matches the root. Our study addressed these limitations by incorporating task-autonomous robotic technology into the socket reconstruction process. For PHT, the original socket morphology is often lost, and the surrounding bone is typically insufficient. In such cases, the robotic system enables a customised 3D reconstruction that precisely conforms to the anatomical contours of the root. In implant dentistry, robotic-assisted surgery has shown superior precision as compared with manual approaches, with reported mean deviations of 0.69 mm at the coronal entry point, 0.75 mm at the apex, and an angular deviation of 1.51° [27]. By leveraging this technological advantage, this study enhanced the fit between the reconstructed socket and the root.

The pulp tissue represents an additional potential source of infection. Preoperative vitality testing in this case revealed vital pulp, indicating that immediate extraoral endodontic treatment was required to prevent subsequent necrosis during the healing phase. Hence, comprehensive root canal disinfection is essential to ensure long-term teeth retention. Traditionally, root canal treatment is performed via coronal access and filled with gutta-percha and sealer. In challenging cases, retrograde fillings may be placed during surgery to improve the apical seal. The evidence indicates that calcium silicate-based bioceramics (iRoot SP/BP) exhibit excellent root canal sealing efficacy, effective antimicrobial properties, and favorable biocompatibility [28–32]. Building upon these advances, this study was the first to propose retro-RCT performed intraoperatively during ROR. All procedures, including root-end resection, pulp extirpation, canal shaping, and filling, were completed through the apical foramen under extraoral conditions. The canals were sealed with iRoot SP and iRoot BP to achieve 3D obturation and effective retrofilling. This approach offered multiple benefits. First, it enabled thorough apical debridement. Second, it simplified the clinical procedure by eliminating the need for delayed coronal access. Third, it supported minimally invasive restorative strategies. Collectively, these features embody current endodontic principles emphasising precision and minimal intervention.

The success rate of dental implants is approximately 95%, which exceeds that of intentional replantation (88%). However, implants lack the proprioceptive function inherent to natural teeth, which is important for neurosensory feedback and for occlusal regulation. Compared to single-tooth implants, tooth replantation offers superior cost-effectiveness for specific indications. More importantly, even in cases in which replantation fails, dental implants remain a viable secondary option [33, 34]. ROR combined with extraoral retro-RCT represents a promising treatment approach for PHT. This pilot study has several limitations. First, the follow-up period is limited to 3–5 months. Long-term assessment for late complications (e.g., ankylosis, root resorption) is not yet available. Second, the sample size is small, and larger randomized controlled trials with extended observation periods are required to validate the efficacy of this technique.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that the integration of robotic-assisted socket reconstruction and ROR represents a paradigm-shifting therapeutic breakthrough for preserving PHT. In this case, the periodontal tissue of the affected tooth was successfully reconstructed with robot-assisted 3D anatomically matched alveolar socket reconstruction, which fully utilized the subjacent alveolar bone. Furthermore, this study pioneered a novel minimally invasive endodontic concept: immediate retro-RCT during replantation surgery that effectively eliminates the conventional delayed coronal access approach. The traditional method carries multiple drawbacks: (1) compromised tooth integrity due to coronal destruction; (2) technical challenges; (3) interference with periodontal healing; (4) risk of apical reinfection. At the three-month follow-up, the replanted teeth exhibited stable periodontal support, absence of mobility, and restoration of clinical function. These findings indicate successful clinical outcomes and the effective integration of the tooth within the alveolar environment. This technological advancement overcomes the limitation of conventional tooth replantation, to preserve the PHT. This innovative approach improves the retention rate of natural dentition and establishes a new technical pathway for minimally invasive regenerative therapy in dentistry. The technique demonstrates substantial clinical value.
