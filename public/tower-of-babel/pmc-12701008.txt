Multi-resolution dataset of electricity consumption in Chinese cities
Authors: Kaile Zhou, Rong Hu, Xinhui Lu, Ziwei Yang, Yaxuan Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Timely and spatially refined electricity consumption data are essential for supporting urban energy transition, electricity demand response, and supply-demand balancing strategies. However, publicly accessible datasets with high temporal resolution and city-level granularity remain scarce, particularly in developing countries such as China. In this study, we generate electricity consumption estimate for 296 Chinese cities in 2022 at both daily and monthly resolutions. In addition, this study presents a top-down framework for estimating city-level daily and monthly electricity consumption by integrating open-access multi-source data, including nighttime light imagery and high-resolution meteorological variables. The validation results demonstrate a strong alignment between the estimated multi-resolution values and officially reported annual statistics. This dataset addresses critical data gaps in urban-scale electricity studies by providing a publicly accessible, lower-cost, and scalable proxy for capturing electricity consumption dynamics at both daily and monthly scales. It is particularly valuable for academic research, exploratory analysis, and understanding spatiotemporal patterns of urban electricity consumption.

Background & Summary

Electricity consumption is regarded as a supplementary indicator indirectly reflecting residents’ economic conditions and quality of life, due to its indispensability in industrialization and modern living1. The widespread availability of electricity has a positive impact on the livelihoods of impoverished populations2. The quality of life and lifestyle of urban residents also can be reflected by their patterns of electricity consumption3,4. The electricity consumption dataset with multi-resolution can help decision-makers identify the electricity usage characters during different time periods, thereby providing a basis for optimizing supply-demand balance5,6. In addition, the analysis of urban electricity consumption characteristics is an important step in establishing a model to enhance the resilience of energy system7,8. The research suggests that extreme events, such as high temperatures and droughts, may have an impact on the structure of electricity demand and could potentially lead to outage9–12. These outages may further exacerbate the existing vulnerabilities of low-income or marginalized communities, thereby triggering a public health crisis13,14. In addition, measures to mitigate and adapt to climate change have further triggered a shift in energy demand15. Therefore, understanding the local electricity demand is crucial in ensuring the provision of key services when facing future climate changes in demand. However, it is challenging to obtain the required electricity consumption data for simulating the impact of extreme events on electricity demand, especially at high-resolution temporal scales.

In 2022, the electricity sector was responsible for approximately 40% of global carbon dioxide emissions, making it a central target in global decarbonization efforts16. In response, countries worldwide are undertaking electricity system reforms to promote sustainable development and reduce dependence on traditional fossil energy sources17–19. Among them, China stands out as the world’s largest electricity consumer, accounting for an estimated 31% of global electricity demand in 202220. Projections suggest that this share will increase from one-quarter in 2015 to one-third by 2025, further highlighting China’s critical role in the global energy transition20. Despite the electricity sector’s importance, the availability of multi-resolution, such as monthly and daily, electricity consumption data in China remains limited. The National Bureau of Statistics publishes electricity data only at annual level21. Such coarse temporal granularity restricts detailed analysis of spatiotemporal dynamics in electricity consumption patterns22. Although the National Energy Administration releases monthly national-level reports, they do not provide data at city-level23. Moreover, while grid companies may possess high-frequency and high-resolution consumption records, data access is often restricted due to privacy concerns, data protection policies, and commercial sensitivities24. A publicly accessible, temporally resolved, and cross-city compatible framework and dataset for city-level electricity consumption estimation is currently lacking. This has significantly hindered progress in related field aimed at comprehensively assessing urban energy efficiency and the transitions of low-carbon development. Therefore, it is essential to explore alternative approaches, such as utilizing indirect data sources or modeling techniques, to construct large scale electricity consumption datasets, thereby promoting broader stakeholder engagement in energy research and analysis.

Major urban lighting activities, such as residential lighting, commercial advertising, traffic signals, and industrial operations, are all heavily dependent on electricity supply. Therefore, there exists a strong correlation between nighttime light (NTL) intensity and electricity consumption25. Existing studies have demonstrated that NTL intensity are consistently correlated with electricity usage, carbon emissions, and human activity intensity26–28. In addition, NTL intensity data have been widely used to estimate macro-level variables such as regional Gross Domestic Product (GDP) and population distribution, both of which are closely associated with urban electricity demand29. Although NTL intensity cannot directly capture the detailed structure of final consumption of electricity, the spatial characteristics they represent, such as economic activity intensity and population density, can indirectly support their validity as a proxy for urban electricity usage. Compared with conventional electricity statistics, NTL intensity offer advantages such as low acquisition cost and high update frequency, providing a long-term, stable, and reproducible remote sensing source for urban scale energy dynamics research. NTL images have advantages such as temporal and spatial continuity, independence, and objectivity, which are applied in the academic field for electricity demand30,31. Previous studies further confirm that NTL intensity can serve as a reliable spatiotemporal proxy for electricity consumption, particularly in region where fine-grained statistical data are lacking30,32. Therefore, in the context of missing high-resolution electricity data at the city-level, this study proposes a multi-resolution urban electricity consumption estimation framework based on NTL remote sensing data, aiming to offer a feasible inference pathway and theoretical support to bridge the data gap.

Methods

According to the availability of NTL data, this study has constructed a framework for estimating city-level monthly and daily electricity consumption data. This is the first time that Chinese daily electricity consumption at the city-level has been clearly reported. The framework of estimating multi-resolution electricity consumption data of Chinese cities is illustrated in Fig. 1.Fig. 1Framework of estimating the electricity consumption data.

Framework of estimating the electricity consumption data.

Firstly, as shown in Fig. 1, the daily NTL intensity data of the city were extracted from the satellite images. Secondly, the monthly urban electricity consumption data was allocated from national monthly data and province annual data. Thirdly, the daily urban electricity consumption data was estimated with machine learning model. Finally, the effectiveness of the data was validated. The estimation framework enables the generation of city-level electricity consumption data at multi-resolution, supporting dynamic analysis of energy demand trends in both temporal and spatial dimensions. The model’s flexibility and interpretability also allow it to be extended to other geographical regions or time periods with similar input availability.

This study estimated the daily and monthly electricity consumption of 296 Chinese cities in 2022. In this study, city refers to the prefecture-level region above the county level and below the provincial level in China. The four municipalities directly under the Central Government, Beijing, Tianjin, Shanghai, and Chongqing, were also included. Daily NTL intensity calculation

To estimate daily electricity consumption at the city-level, this study constructed a time series of NTL intensity for each city in China throughout 2022. The NTL images were derived from Visible Infrared Imaging Radiometer Suite (VIIRS)/Polar-orbiting Partnership (NPP) Daily Gridded Day Night Band 15 arc-second Linear Lat Lon Grid Night (VNP46A1) product provided by National Aeronautics and Space Administration’s Black Marble suite41,42. The VNP46A1 product provides daily global nighttime radiance data at a spatial resolution of 15 arc seconds, corresponding to approximately 500 meters on the ground. The VNP46A1 product provides radiance data that have been atmospherically and lunar-calibrated by NASA’s Black Marble suit and includes a quality band for cloud detection. In this study, pixels flagged as cloud-contaminated were excluded based in the cloud mask provided in the product. The NTL images are obtained by cutting the vector data of the administrative area. Geospatial aggregation was conducted in two steps. Firstly, the national VIIRS nighttime light imagery was clipped into city-level subsets using Python. Secondly, the subsets were processed in ArcGIS by applying city-level administrative boundary masks, reprojecting the NTL images into an equal-area projection, and then calculating the area-weighted average radiance of valid pixels within each city. For each of the 296 Chinese cities, the city administrative boundary was used as a spatial mask. Daily urban electricity consumption estimation

This study developed a robust modeling framework that combines daily satellite observed NTL intensity with daily meteorological data to estimate daily electricity consumption for 296 Chinese cities in 2022. The explanatory variables include daily average temperature, daily minimum temperature, daily average wind speed, and calibrated daily average NTL intensity. The 90% of the data were used as training data and 10% as test sample. Prior to modeling, all input variables were normalized to ensure comparability. To map the nonlinear relationships between the explanatory variables and electricity consumption, we adopt the random forest regression model. The random forest is an ensemble learning method that builds multiple decision trees during training and outputs the average of the individual predictions to improve generalization and reduce overfitting. Its robustness, interpretability, and ability to handle high-dimensional nonlinear data make it particularly well-suited for electricity demand estimation tasks. Technical Validation

The technical validation of this study includes four parts: (1) Internal consistency validation; (2) COVID-19 impacts in 2022; (3) Evaluation of model performances; (4) Spatial validation.

Internal consistency validation

To verify the consistency of the constructed dataset during the process of time aggregation and spatial aggregation, this study conducted consistency tests for yearly, monthly, and daily data, as well as for city and provincial data. The consistency test results of the two resolutions of city-level electricity consumption data provided by this study are shown in Fig. 4.Fig. 4Compare monthly top-down estimates of statistical electricity data with estimated electricity data in log-log scale. (a–i) January to December.

Compare monthly top-down estimates of statistical electricity data with estimated electricity data in log-log scale. (a–i) January to December.

As shown in Fig. 4, to align the resolution of the two types of data, we summed up the estimated daily electricity consumption data for each city monthly and compared it with the statistic monthly electricity consumption for that city as calculated from the statistical data. The city-level electricity consumption datasets at both monthly and daily resolutions estimated by the top-down framework exhibit a high degree of consistency. In the scatter plot of Fig. 4, the data points are mainly distributed along the 45° diagonal line, indicating a significant positive correlation between the estimated and statistic values across months. The regression fits are statistically significant, and the relatively narrow confidence intervals suggest that the proposed framework captures meaningful variance in electricity consumption. Although, the results of internal consistency verification vary by season. For instance, the R2 values fluctuate between 0.621 and 0.728, with lower accuracy in months such as February and June. Additionally, there is a notable negative bias over several months, indicating that the estimates tend to underestimate electricity consumption. However, the dataset provided by this study is not intended to replace official statistics and should not be used as a direct decision-making tool for high-risk applications such as grid management or infrastructure planning. These results suggest that the dataset can still serve as a useful resource for research and exploratory analysis, providing valuable insights into the spatiotemporal patterns of electricity consumption at the urban level.

COVID-19 impacts in 2022

In 2022, a part of cities in China implemented COVID-19 lockdown measures. To illustrate the sensitivity of the dataset in evaluating urban responses to COVID-19 lockdown, we selected tree representative cities, Shenzhen, Changchun, and Shanghai, for analysis. As shown in Fig. 6, estimated daily electricity consumption was plotted, with lockdown periods indicated by shaded regions, and 7-day rolling averages were calculated to highlight trends.Fig. 6Estimated daily electricity sensitivity to COVID-19 lockdown. (a) Shenzhen. (b) Changchun. (c) Shanghai.

Estimated daily electricity sensitivity to COVID-19 lockdown. (a) Shenzhen. (b) Changchun. (c) Shanghai.

Figure 6 presents the changes in the 7-day rolling averages of estimated electricity consumption before, during, and after lockdowns for the three cities. The results indicate that both Changchun and Shenzhen experienced pronounced declines in electricity consumption during their respective lockdowns, whereas Shanghai’s consumption remained fluctuating. This difference can be attributed to the prolonged, multi-stage natura of Shanghai’s lockdown and its more complex socio-economic structure, which mitigated immediate reductions in electricity consumption. These observations indicate that the estimation framework has a certain degree of sensitivity to sudden social events.

Model performance evaluation

To systematically evaluate the model performance and provide methodological reference for related studies, this study compares estimation accuracy of four regression models for daily urban electricity consumption, including random forest, K-nearest neighbors, linear regression, and decision tree. Prior to train, all input variables were standardized. The dataset was divided into training and testing sets in a ratio of 90% and 10%, respectively. The model performance was assessed using 10-fold cross-validation. As shown in Fig. 7, three evaluation metrics were employed, including coefficient of determination R-Square (R2), mean absolute error (MAE), and root mean square error (RMSE).Fig. 7Performance comparison of the electricity consumption estimation models. (a) R2. (b) MAE. (c) RMSE.

Performance comparison of the electricity consumption estimation models. (a) R2. (b) MAE. (c) RMSE.

Figure 7(a) shows that the random forest model achieves the highest R2 values, with a median exceeding 0.85 and a narrow interquartile range, indicating both high predictive accuracy and stability. In contrast, the R2 value for the other models remain consistently below 0.5, suggesting limited explanatory power. Figure 7(b) presents the MAE values, where the random forest model again outperforms others, with lower average prediction errors. The results reveal that random forest produces MAE values below 0.2 × 105 MWh while linear and K-nearest neighbors models tend to exceed 0.3 × 105 MWh. Figure 7(c) further confirms these findings through RMSE analysis, which penalizes large deviations more heavily. The random forest model demonstrates both the lowest RMSE reinforcing its robustness in handing complex nonlinear relationships in input variables, including meteorological features and NTL intensity. Overall, the random forest model clearly outperforms the alternative models across all metrics. Its ensemble learning structure and resistance to overfitting make it particularly well-suited for this electricity consumption estimation task. These results justify the choice of random forest model as the core predictive engine in the proposed data estimation framework.

As shown in Fig. 8(d), high temperature correlation becomes negligible or even slightly negative, with is expected due to diverse heating energy sources. In contrast, daily average wind speed slightly decreases model error by 3.99% when permuted, suggesting it contributes little to the predictive capability in this context. The relatively low standard deviations across repeated permutations confirm that these feature importance estimates are stable and reliable. Overall, these findings provide insight into the key drivers of urban electricity demand and enhance the interpretability of the random forest model.

Limitations

Although the dataset constructed in this study can reflect the general characteristics and spatiotemporal variations of electricity consumption in Chinese cities, it is inevitably subject to several limitation. First, the accuracy of the city-level estimates, while showing significant correlations with official statistics, remains imperfect. Some uncertainties limit the dataset’s applicability for critical real-world decision-making scenarios such as grid management, infrastructure investment, and policy implementation. Instead, the dataset is more appropriate for academic research, exploratory analysis, and the study of electricity consumption patterns under different conditions. Secondly, the estimated framework is mainly based on correlations between remotely sensed and meteorological variables with historical statistical data. While we have considered as many variables with wide coverage and availability as possible, some relevant influencing factors are not yet included. For example, due to difference in heating and cooling demand between northern and southern China, electricity consumption also varies. Northern cities require more heating during winter while southern cites require more cooling during summer45. Future research should consider more geographically related variables such as heating degree days and cooling degree days. Finally, the temporal coverage of the current dataset is limited to the year 2022 at daily and monthly resolutions. We will continue monitoring the availability of relevant data sources and integrate more valuable datasets to optimize the proposed framework, expand temporal coverage, and dynamically update the dataset at multiple spatiotemporal scales.

Usage Notes

The dataset presented in this study is stored in the widely supported.xlsx format, making it readily accessible in common data analysis environments such as Python, R, MATLAB, and JAVA. To facilitate reproducibility, the data are organized by multi-resolution (daily and monthly) and region (province and city), and file naming conventions are standardized to ensure clarity and ease of automated processing. This structure allows researchers to efficiently load, manipulate, and analyze the data in a consistent and transparent manner. The dataset provides multi-resolution electricity consumption data at both daily and monthly scales, offering valuable temporal granularity for diverse research. It holds significant utility for researchers, supporting studies such as urban electricity load estimation, evaluation of demand response mechanisms, exploration of market dynamics, analysis of energy equity and accessibility, and assessment of low-carbon transition pathways.

While the dataset curtly spans the year 2022, the methodological framework is fully generalizable to other time periods and geographic contexts. Researchers can replicate the data construction process using publicly available NTL and meteorological datasets in combination with local electricity statistics, allowing for extension to different years or additional countries and regions. This flexibility makes the dataset a scalable foundation for longitudinal studies and comparative energy research in both data-rich and data-scarce environments.
