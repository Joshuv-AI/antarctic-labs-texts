Comparative analysis of select 3D human mesenchymal stromal cell-derived spheroid models for adipogenic differentiation
Authors: Bianca Vezzani, Elisa Tamburini, Anna Terenziani, Nadia Ilaria Oliverio, Simonetta Croci, Alessandro Bartolomucci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn vitro modelling of adipose tissue (AT) commonly employs 2D systems, which do not fully capture the complexity of AT, lacking cell-cell interactions and extracellular matrix (ECM) remodelling. Despite the innovations in 3D culture systems offering physiologically relevant AT platforms, the field lacks consensus and cross-protocol method comparison. We performed a comparative analysis of three scaffold-free spheroid-generation techniques: Ultra Low Attachment plates (ULA), Hanging Drops (HD), and Microwell Culture Plates (MCP), utilizing human AT-derived mesenchymal stromal cells. Each method produced viable spheroids of similar size and morphology within 48 hours. However, MCP demonstrated greater uniformity and a significant increase in mass density when compared to ULA and HD. Adipogenic media increased spheroid size in ULA and MCP, but not in HD spheroids. Notably, the diameter of ULA spheroids was 2- and 6-times larger compared to HD and MCP ones, respectively, regardless of adipogenic induction. Gene expression analysis indicated that all techniques led to PPARγ and ADIPOQ expression during adipogenesis. All methods supported adipocyte differentiation, as evidenced by lipid droplet accumulation, PLIN1, CD36, and HSL expression, and ECM rearrangement. These findings suggest that all the described techniques constitute a robust platform for producing adipogenesis-competent spheroids, while method-specific differences suggest an application-dependent choice.KEYWORDS: 3D culture, spheroids, adipose tissue, MSC, in vitro modelling

Introduction

Obesity, a disease characterized by excessive accumulation of adipose tissue (AT), represents a significant health concern that has reached pandemic proportions globally, with approximately one in eight individuals classified as obese [1]. As the prevalence of obesity continues to rise, it becomes increasingly crucial to understand the functional mechanisms of this tissue, examine its pathological features, and identify novel markers of obesity and associated diseases to facilitate the development of innovative therapeutic interventions and treatments. The AT is the primary site of energy storage within the human body and plays an essential role in maintaining metabolic balance through its endocrine functions, both under physiological and pathological conditions associated with obesity [2]. AT is engaged in a diverse array of processes, including metabolic regulation, cellular signalling, and hormonal activity, underscoring its extensive role in preserving homoeostasis [3]. While conventional model systems of AT include the use of 2D primary adipocytes or mesenchymal stromal cell (MSC) derived adipocytes cultures [4,5], recent advancements in three-dimensional (3D) culture systems offer a more physiologically relevant model of the in vivo environment. In fact, 3D models, unlike two-dimensional systems, more effectively replicate cell-cell and cell-matrix interactions, signalling pathways, and tissue architecture observed in vivo [6]. For instance, the AT undergoes substantial remodelling including production of extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins such as collagen, fibronectin, and laminin [7], by a variety of cells including adipocytes, cells of the stromal vascular fraction containing preadipocytes, capillary endothelial cells, infiltrated monocytes/macrophages, and mesenchymal stem cells [8: 9]. The more broadly used primary cell line for studying human adipogenesis in vitro are MSC derived from culture of the stromal vascular fraction (SVF) isolated from AT [4], here referred to as ADSC]. Notably, MSC have been shown to self-organize into 3D spheroids, which exhibit enhanced multilineage differentiation potential and increased secretion of therapeutic factors compared to conventional 2D monolayer cultures of MSC [10,11]. Several methodologies have been developed to generate spheroids from MSC, as outlined in Table 1. These methodologies can be categorized into two primary groups: one utilizes matrix or scaffolds (such as hydrogels, Matrigel, decellularized ECM, etc.), and the other relies solely on cellular arrangements. Among the latter, three principal techniques for spheroid formation are identifiable: i) direct seeding on Ultra Low Attachment (ULA) plates, ii) seeding on Hanging Drops (HD) placed on top of a plate cover, and iii) seeding on specialized Microwell Culture Plates (MCP) designed to provide physical restraints that promote cell aggregation (e.g. V-shape plates, microwell plates). New methods are constantly and rapidly evolving in 3D cultures, aiming to develop more complex structures, composed of different cell types, such as endothelial or immune cells, thus resembling AT complexity, emphasizing the importance of standardization and cross-method comparison. To date, most studies on 3D models of AT used a single technique to generate spheroids and primarily focus on their function, whereas systematic cross-method comparisons for differentiation remain rare. To address this need, we conducted a comparative analysis of three common approaches for generating AT-differentiated spheroids starting from human AT-derived MSC, namely ULA, HD, and Corning ElplasiaTM plates as MCP, focusing on spheroids morphometric characterization, and adipogenesis/ECM markers expression.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and spheroids generation

Human MSC isolated from subcutaneous adipose tissue (RoosterVial-hAD-1 M-XF) were procured from RoosterBio Inc. (Frederick, MD, USA) and expanded in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines. In brief, the cells were thawed on vitronectin-coated plates (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and cultured in RoosterNourish™-MSC-XF medium (RoosterBio, hereafter referred to as MSC medium). Upon reaching 80–90% confluence, the cells were dissociated using TrypLE (ThermoFisher Scientific) and subsequently replated at a density of 3,000 cells/cm2 on plastic surfaces. All experiments were performed on MSC at passage 6. To generate spheroids, cells were seeded in MSC culture medium using the following techniques specifically validated for MSC:

Ultra Low Attachment (ULA) method: 20,000 cells per well in 100 μL of medium for each well (surface area 0.32 cm2) on the flat-bottom ULA Nunclon Sphera Microplate (ThermoFisher Scientific). Cell seeding density: 200 cells/μL. Adapted from [15].

Ultra Low Attachment (ULA) method: 20,000 cells per well in 100 μL of medium for each well (surface area 0.32 cm2) on the flat-bottom ULA Nunclon Sphera Microplate (ThermoFisher Scientific). Cell seeding density: 200 cells/μL. Adapted from [15].

Hanging Drop (HD) method: 7,000 cells per drop in 20 μL of medium placed on the lid of a 96-well plate. The lid was subsequently positioned over the 96-well plate filled with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to maintain consistent humidity levels. After 48 h of culture, 3 drops of HD, which contained a total of 21,000 cells at seeding, were transferred into 1 well of the ULA plate to proceed to the induction of adipogenesis. Cell seeding density: 350 cells/μL. Adapted from [20].

Hanging Drop (HD) method: 7,000 cells per drop in 20 μL of medium placed on the lid of a 96-well plate. The lid was subsequently positioned over the 96-well plate filled with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to maintain consistent humidity levels. After 48 h of culture, 3 drops of HD, which contained a total of 21,000 cells at seeding, were transferred into 1 well of the ULA plate to proceed to the induction of adipogenesis. Cell seeding density: 350 cells/μL. Adapted from [20].

Microwell Culture Plates (MCP) method: 20,000 cells per well in 50 μL of medium transferred into ElplasiaTM Spheroid Microplates with 79 500 μm wide and 400 μm deep microcavity dimensions located at the bottom of each well of a 96-well plate (Corning, New York, NY, USA). Cells were permitted to sediment for one hour, after which an additional 50 μL of medium was added to each well. Cell seeding density: 200 cells/μL.

Microwell Culture Plates (MCP) method: 20,000 cells per well in 50 μL of medium transferred into ElplasiaTM Spheroid Microplates with 79 500 μm wide and 400 μm deep microcavity dimensions located at the bottom of each well of a 96-well plate (Corning, New York, NY, USA). Cells were permitted to sediment for one hour, after which an additional 50 μL of medium was added to each well. Cell seeding density: 200 cells/μL.

Cells and spheroids were cultured in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37°C. Spheroids were generated starting from the same cell line in 3 independent experimental replicates for each tested generative technique.

Biophysical and morphological analysis

To perform biophysical and morphological analysis of the spheroids, we utilized the W8 system (CellDynamics ISRL, Bologna, Italy) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, after 48 hours from seeding, the spheroids were carefully collected in a 15 mL tube, washed with PBS, and centrifuged at 0.3 rpm for 5 minutes to gently pellet the spheroids without disrupting their structure. The spheroids were then suspended in W8 Analysis solution (CellDynamics) and loaded into the W8 analysis system. Only spheroids with a diameter exceeding 75 µm were selected for subsequent analysis. When feasible, 20 replicates were collected for each study. The total number of spheroids analysed for each generative technique used is reported in the figure legend and refers to three independent experimental replicates.

Brightfield imaging

Brightfield images were acquired using Epi-Fluorescence Eclipse TE300 microscope, equipped with a 10x objective (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan), and analysed with ImageJ Fiji software (National Institutes of Health, USA) to quantify spheroid size and morphology.

For this purpose, we designed the macro SPHEROID_DIM.ijm, which enables batch processing of images and integrates three alternative segmentation pipelines, among which the user can select the most appropriate one, with the option to preview Regions of Interest (ROIs) and manually refine them if necessary. The three segmentation modes differ in their level of robustness: method 1, based on a fixed threshold, is suitable for images with stable contrast; method 2, which combines fixed thresholding, inversion, and hole filling, improves detection in spheroids with blurred or hollow central regions; and method 3, employing adaptive thresholding, compensates for heterogeneous intensity or uneven illumination. The macro also allows the quantification of several morphometric parameters, including area, perimeter, and circularity, with particular attention to the Feret diameter, defined as the distance between two parallel tangents on opposite sides of an object’s contour along a specific direction. The selected morphometric parameters are exported for each image and subsequently summarized across the entire dataset, enabling comprehensive statistical analysis.

The spheroid analysis plugin (Spheroid2D_ROITool deposited in a publicly accessible GitHub repository) was developed with the help of OpenAI artificial intelligence [ChatGPT (GPT-5.1). 2025, https://chat.openai.com] and subsequently reviewed and validated.

Viability assay

To evaluate cell viability, spheroids were transferred into a well of a multi96 plate and incubated for 30 minutes at 37°C with 5% CO2 in a solution containing 5 μg/mL Propidium Iodide (PI, Sigma-Aldrich, Saint Louis, MO, USA) and 4 μM calcein (Merk-Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA). After incubation, the spheroids were washed twice with PBS and imaged using Epi-Fluorescence Eclipse TE300 microscope, equipped with a 10x objective (Nikon). The quantification was performed by measuring the percentage of area covered by PI-positive nuclei and calcein-positive cells using ImageJ Fiji software (National Institutes of Health). Viability is expressed as the ratio between PI and calcein area.

Adipogenic differentiation

After 48 hours of spheroid formation, the MSC medium was removed, and adipogenesis was induced by adding 100 μL of StemPro Adipogenesis Differentiation Medium (ThermoFisher Scientific) to each well of both ULA and ElplasiaTM. For the HD-generated spheroids, because the seeding density for each drop was 350 cells/μL and the total number of cells/HD was 7,000, to have the same density and number of cells as the starting point for the differentiation, 3 HD were transferred into each well of ULA plate, and adipogenesis was induced as described above. Control spheroids were kept in 100 μL of MSC medium. During the 14-day differentiation period, the media were changed every other day by replacing 50 μL of the old media with fresh media. Adipogenic differentiation was performed in three independent experimental replicates.

Spheroid fixation and immunofluorescence

To conduct immunofluorescence experiments, we adapted the protocol described by Dekkers and colleagues [28]. All reagents were purchased from Merk-Millipore unless otherwise specified. Spheroids were collected and delicately washed twice with PBS. Subsequently, they were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 45 minutes at 4°C, with gentle vortexing performed after 20 minutes. After fixation, spheroids were washed twice for 5 minutes in a 1% PBS/Tween solution and then stored in the same solution at 4°C for a maximum duration of three days. For staining procedures, spheroids were transferred to a 96-well microplate and incubated in a blocking and permeabilizing solution comprising 0.2% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and 0.1% Triton in PBS (spheroids buffer, SB) for 15 minutes at 4°C under gentle agitation. Following permeabilization and blocking, spheroids were incubated with primary antibodies as specified in Table 2, diluted in 100 μL of SB, and incubated overnight at 4°C. The following day, the spheroids were washed three times with SB for 1 hour and 30 minutes at room temperature under gentle agitation. Following the final wash, spheroids were incubated overnight at 4°C under gentle agitation with the secondary antibodies diluted in SB, as detailed in Table 2, and protected from light. Spheroids were washed once with SB for 1 hour and 30 minutes at room temperature under gentle agitation and then incubated with fluorescent-labelled antibodies/markers as detailed in Table 2. After one washing step with SB for 1 hour and 30 minutes at room temperature, the nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1 mg/mL, Thermo Fisher, 1:500) for 15 minutes at room temperature in agitation. A final wash was performed with SB for 1 hour at room temperature under gentle shaking. The spheroids were then mounted in a clearing/mounting solution consisting of 2.5 M Fructose and 60% glycerol in H2O on a glass slide pre-disposed with a supporting spacer to prevent spheroid flattening. The mounted samples were imaged using the high-resolution confocal laser microscope Stellaris 5, equipped with a DMI8 microscope and a 20X objective (NA 0.7) (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). The 3D reconstruction was performed using the software LAS X Office (Leica).

Data are reported as 2^-dCT [30].

Single nuclei extraction

Spheroids were collected and centrifuged at 700 rcf for 5 minutes at 4°C and resuspended in 1 mL of PBS 1X + 0.04% BSA. Following a second centrifugation at 700 rcf for 5 minutes at 4°C, the supernatant was removed, and the spheroids were incubated with 100 μL of Lysis Buffer composed of Nuclei prep buffer (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA) and RNAse Inhibitor (1 U/ul, Sigma-Aldrich) on ice for 20 minutes, with gentle pipetting every 2 minutes. After the lysis, 100 μL of chilled Nuclei Wash buffer (10 mM TRIS HCl Ph 7.4, 10 mM NaCl, 3 mM MgCl2, 1% BSA, 0,10% Tween 20, 1 mM DTT, 1 U/ul RNAse Inhibitor in dH2O) was added, and the samples were centrifuged at 500 rcf for 5 minutes at 4°C. After another washing step in Nuclei Buffer (1% BSA, 1 U/ul RNAse Inhibitor in dH2O), the pellet was resuspended in 50 μL of chilled Nuclei Buffer, and the nuclei were counted using trypan blue and Burker-Chamber.

Western blot

Proteins were isolated using RIPA Buffer (Cell Signalling Technologies, Danvers, MA, USA). Spheroids were incubated in 50 μL of chilled RIPA Buffer on ice for 20 minutes, vortexing every 2 minutes. Samples were then centrifuged at 11,750 rcf for 10 minutes at 4°C, and the supernatant was collected. Protein concentration was established using the Pierce Dilution-Free Rapid Gold BCA Protein Assay Kit (ThermoFisher Scientific) following manufacturers’ instruction. 5 μg of protein were loaded on Bolt™ 4 to 12%, Bis-Tris Plus WedgeWell™ polyacrylamide gels (ThermoFisher Scientific) and run in MOPS SDS Running buffer (ThermoFisher Scientific) at 100 V for 1 hour and 30 minutes. Proteins were transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane at a constant 300 mA at 4°C for 2 hours and 30 minutes. The membrane was blocked with Western Blocking Reagent (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) for 1 hour at room temperature and incubated with the primary antibodies diluted in 5% BSA/TBS-Tween™ (ThermoFisher Scientific) listed in Table 2. The following day, after three washes with TBS-Tween™ solution, the membranes were incubated with the secondary antibodies diluted in Western Blocking Reagent. Proteins were visualized using the iBright™ Imaging System (ThermoFisher Scientific) following 1 minute incubation with the ImmobilonⓇ Western chemiluminescent HRP substrate (Merk-Millipore). Membranes were stripped using the Restore PLUS Western Blot Stripping Buffer (ThermoFisher Scientific) solution following the manufacturer’s instructions. After stripping, the membranes were blocked and incubated with the antibodies as described above. Protein quantification was performed using ImageJ Fiji software (National Institutes of Health), and expressed as a ratio on the housekeeping β-tubulin or actin. The reported data refer to 3 experimental replicates.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 10.0.2) and RStudio environment 2026.07.0 Build 139, R version 4.5.0 (2025–04-11 ucrt). Data distribution normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Comparisons were performed using one-way ANOVA, or Analysis of Variance of Aligned Rank Transformed Data when appropriate, followed by Tukey’s, Šídák’s or Holm-Šídák’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons. In case of non-normal distribution, comparisons between two groups were performed using the two-tailed Kruskal-Wallis Test followed by Dunn’s test for multiple comparisons. All experiments were performed in triplicate, and each replicate comprised a different number of spheroids, as indicated in the figure legends. Since the replicates were not statistically different, statistical analyses were performed on the values from individual spheroids. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant. Data are graphed as truncated violin plots with the dotted line highlighting the median and the point lines the quartiles, or as bars ± SEM.

Results

To evaluate the impact of ULA, HD, and MCP methodologies on spheroid formation, a four-phase approach was employed: i) spheroid formation, ii) characterization after 48 hours in culture in order to homogenize the cell density, and iii) assessment of the spheroids’ differentiation status after 5, 7, and 14 days based on structural and early adipogenic features (Figure 1(A)). The initial seeding density was the same for ULA and MCP (200 cells/μL), with the same total cell number (20,000 cells/well), while following the protocol developed by Robledo and colleagues [20], the HD had an initial seeding density of 350 cells/μL (total number of 7,000 cells per drop). To reduce potential confounding due to seeding variability, we pooled 3 drops of HD spheroids per well of a ULA plate to equalize seeding density (20,000 cells/well for ULA and MCP; 7,000 cells/drop for HD), prior to adipogenic induction.

Spheroid generation and characterization

After seeding, MSC were allowed 48 h to aggregate into spheroids, as previously reported (Taylor et al., [15]; [20]), before starting the biophysical evaluation. Spheroids created using ULA, HD, and MCP techniques were similar in size and vitality, exhibiting good roundness and minimal cell death in the core (Figure 1(B–D)). Biophysical and morphological analysis showed no differences in diameter or weight among spheroids produced by ULA, HD, or MCP (Figure 1(D,E)). However, we observed a significantly higher mass density in the MCP spheroids compared to ULA and HD spheroids (Figure 1(F)). Consequently, the MCP methodology resulted in less variation in diameter and mass density (Figure 1(G)). Thus, despite similar general characteristics of spontaneous 3D aggregation under baseline conditions, some biophysical differences emerged, with MCP manifesting improved homogeneity.

Effects of adipogenic differentiation on spheroid diameter

Next, we investigated the effect of the different spheroid formation methods on differentiation in response to standard adipogenic media (ADIPO; see method). To assess size variation during adipogenic differentiation, images were evaluated using a novel ImageJ plugin, named Spheroid2D_ROITool, designed to ensure robustness and adaptability, enabling faster and reproducible processing of spheroid images acquired under varying illumination conditions and across a wide range of spheroid sizes. Spheroids generated with the ULA technique exhibited an increase in diameter during adipogenesis compared to their undifferentiated counterparts, starting from the first evaluation point at day 5 and continuing until day 7 (Figure 2(A)). Similar to the ULA, the MCP spheroids also exhibited a significant increase in diameter during adipogenic differentiation, which reached statistical significance only at day 14 (Figure 2(C)). Conversely, HD-generated spheroids did not display a significant increase in diameter during adipogenic differentiation (Figure 2(B)).

The comparison of spheroid sizes during CTRL and ADIPO revealed that, at all examined time points, the ULA spheroids had an average diameter 2- and 6-times larger than that of HD and MCP spheroids, respectively, with the latter being the smallest in all conditions and time points (Figure 2 (D–F)). Interestingly, analysing size changes revealed that HD spheroids progressively increased in size over the 14-day culture period, regardless of the culture medium used, indicating that the size of the spheroids was unaffected by the differentiative process. Conversely, the ULA spheroids display a gradual, albeit insignificant, decrease in diameter during differentiation (Figure 2(G)). The total number of nuclei did not change during adipogenesis (Supp Figure S1A), suggesting that the trend in spheroid’s size reduction may result from structural reorganization. The MCP spheroids maintained stable size throughout all evaluated time points, regardless of the culture medium (Figure 3(G)), indicating that the physical confinement typical of the microwell plate used for this approach limited the physical growth of the spheroids. The evaluation of the number of spheroids per well in the ULA and HD techniques, revealed the absence of differences between techniques used, differentiation or days in culture (Supp. Figure S1B). The number of spheroids/well was not evaluated in the MCP condition since the plate is specifically designed to physically separate the single spheroids within the same well by allocating them into microcavities, while still allowing to share the same medium. Interestingly, we did not observe differences in cell death among CTRL and ADIPO, or due to the generation technique used (Supp. Figure S1C). Overall, adipogenic induction promoted an increase in size in ULA and MCP, but not in HD spheroids.

Impact of the generation method on spheroids’ adipogenic differentiation

Gene expression analysis revealed that key genes involved in adipogenesis such as peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ) and adiponectin (ADIPOQ) increased in all spheroids subjected to adipogenic media, with no significant differences between the different techniques used (Figure 3(A,B)). Adipogenesis was confirmed by the increased expression of the fatty acid translocase CD36 and the hormone-sensitive lipase (HSL) (Figure 3(C)). Structural analysis through confocal imaging revealed that after 14 days of adipogenic induction under all conditions examined, cells were characterized by lipid droplets formation within the cytoplasm, surrounded by perilipin (PLIN1) (Figure 3(D,F,H)). Additionally, smaller, disorganized lipid droplets were observed in undifferentiated spheroids, encased within an intricate network of fibronectin (FN) (Figure 3(E,G,I)).

Effects of adipogenic differentiation on ECM organization

Further investigation of the extracellular matrix component revealed a tendency for reduced expression of genes encoding for ECM markers, such as FN and collagen 1a (COL1A), during adipogenic differentiation, with a significant decrease in COL1A expression only in ULA-generated spheroids (Figure 4(A,B)). This effect was confirmed also by measuring COL1A protein level (Figure 4(C)). The differences in ECM organization were highlighted by structural analysis through confocal imaging, showing that undifferentiated spheroids are surrounded by a dense FN net (Figure 4(D,F,H) and Supp. Figure S2), while after adipogenesis, adipocytes emerge from the FN net, as shown in Fig. 4(F,G,I) Notably, the FN organization was not affected by the method used for spheroid creation.

Discussion

In this study, we systematically compared three among the most commonly used techniques for AT spheroid generation starting from human-AT-derived MSC, namely ULA, HD, and MCP, to assess their effects on spheroid morphology, biophysical properties, and adipogenic differentiation. To focus on the spheroids’ generation technique and avoid donor-dependent variation, we used a commercial primary human adipose tissue-derived MSC cell line, as detailed in the Materials and Methods section. It has been reported that adipose tissue-derived MSC could present differentiation variability across different donors [31,32], thus potentially impacting spheroid formation. Although initial appearances in terms of morphology and vitality were similar, we identified differences in mass density and size dynamics, indicating that the generation methods could influence the outcome of 3D cultures. These differences must be taken into account when planning experiments to select the most appropriate generative technique for functional studies of adipose spheroids or the differentiation of adipose organoids. Several studies, as summarized in Table 1, have reported MSC ability to self-aggregate into spheroids without requiring a physical constraint. Based on this evidence, we decided to use the flat-bottom ULA, instead of the V- or U-shaped ones, to have a ‘constraint-free’ method to be compared to the others. In fact, HD technique, was developed to mimic the U-shape of the commercial ULA plates, using gravity as the driving force to promote cellular aggregation. On the other hand, MCP methods rely on physical barriers and U-shaped micro-wells to obtain size-comparable spheroids. With the growing interest in spheroid generation, the range of commercially available ULA plates, as well as microwell culture plates, is expanding. We decided to use the NunclonTM Sphera Microplate following the protocol published by Taylor and colleagues [15]. As different plastic types might impact cell culture, this might also be reflected in their ability to promote spheroid formation. As suggested by the presented data, human adipose tissue-derived MSC can form spheroids and undergo adipogenic differentiation in this specific type of plate.

Interestingly, differences in the initial seeding density (200 cells/μL for ULA and MCP; 350 cells/μL for HD) did not affect HD spheroid size or cell density, which were comparable to those obtained with ULA. Despite the higher seeding density used for the HD, spheroids generated with the MCP exhibited higher mass density and reduced size variability compared to both ULA and HD, indicating that MCP supports more reproducible and compact cell aggregation. This may be due to the optimized well structure, which enhances cell-cell interactions and provides physical boundaries to promote the formation of size-controlled spheroids. Notably, in the absence of restraint present in the MCP, the spheroids in the ULA and HD aggregate to form a large spheroid, thus further explaining the lack of an increase in diameter in the MCP spheroids.

Previous studies have highlighted that spheroid size, compactness, and uniformity are key parameters affecting nutrient diffusion, oxygen gradients, and the overall differentiation microenvironment [33–36]. Once maintained in culture, the spheroids displayed differences in size modulation depending on the technique used. Compared to HD and MCP, ULA spheroids were the largest at every time point, regardless of the culturing medium. When subjected to adipogenic induction, spheroids demonstrated method-dependent differences in size evolution. MCP and ULA spheroids induced to differentiate exhibited increased diameters compared to undifferentiated spheroids, as determined using the same methods, consistent with lipid accumulation and cellular hypertrophy characteristic of mature adipocytes [37,38]. This result suggests that adipocyte differentiation contributes to spheroid enlargement. This condition was observed from the early stages of adipogenesis and continued throughout differentiation. However, while HD spheroids progressively increased in size in culture, both during adipogenesis and in the CTRL condition, MCP and ULA maintained a stable diameter throughout the culture period under the same differentiation conditions. In contrast, we saw a non-significant trend to decrease in diameter at day 14 of adipogenesis in ULA spheroids. These findings suggest that the ULA technique promotes the formation of larger spheroids that reach their maximum size after 5 days in culture, whereas the HD method supports gradual assembly, resulting in a steady increase in size. The lack of size changes in MCP spheroids during differentiation indicates a probable structural constraint inherent to this technique, preventing large size variation and spontaneous growth.

Despite the differences observed in spheroid size, gene expression analysis revealed that the spheroids undergoing adipogenesis upregulated the key adipogenic regulators PPARγ and ADIPOQ [39] after 14 days of induction, with no significant differences in terms of relative expression. Western blot and confocal imaging data further confirmed successful adipogenic differentiation, consisting of CD36 and HSL expression and lipid droplet accumulation surrounded by PLIN1. Notably, undifferentiated spheroids exhibited smaller, disorganized lipid structures intertwined within a fibronectin-rich matrix, which was progressively remodelled during differentiation. The decrease in ECM gene expression, especially COL1A, in ULA spheroids supports the notion that adipogenesis involves ECM loosening and remodelling, which facilitates adipocyte hypertrophy, a phenomenon also observed in 3D cultures of SVF-derived human adipocytes [8,40]. It is well known that ECM plays a regulatory role during adipocyte differentiation, modulating the plasticity and function of the diverse adipose tissue-resident cells [41–43]. We could speculate that the decrease in ECM synthesis could reflect the size reduction observed in ULA spheroids during adipogenesis, since the progressive decline in ECM components might lead to an alteration of the spheroid structure with a consequent diameter reduction. However, this hypothesis remains to be confirmed by future studies performing ultrastructural analysis of the different spheroids. Notably, the preservation of fibronectin organization across different generation methods indicates that, although ECM composition change during differentiation, the overall structural framework remains mainly governed by cellular function rather than by the spheroid formation platform [44,45]. It is important to underline that loosening of ECM components in the late stages of adipogenic differentiation might affect the long-term maintenance of the spheroids. We have shown that MSC are able to provide ECM components allowing spheroid assembly. However, their differentiation into adipocytes might reduce the overall ECM abundance, indicating the necessity of providing additional matrix (i.e. Matrigel or other ECM scaffold, see Table 1) in case of long term in vitro studies.

Collectively, these findings reveal that all three methods can produce viable spheroids suitable for adipogenic differentiation, with comparable reproducibility, compactness, and improved differentiation efficiency. The characteristics, pros and cons, and suggested applications of the three methods analysed are summarized in Figure 5. The differences in size confer to ULA and HD spheroids a superior quality in terms of handling by the operator, enabling easier media exchange during both culture and immunofluorescence experiments, resulting in improved confocal imaging. Conversely, MCP spheroids, despite smaller size affecting their handling, are characterized by a more homogenous spheroids’ size and mass-density homogeneity. Thus, our study suggests that the technique for generating spheroids should be selected based on the specific application required. For instance, if an experiment requires frequent spheroid handling, or is aimed at assessing AT morphological changes during specific treatment with imaging techniques, the ULA or HD methods could be the optimal choice. Furthermore, the removal of physical restraint could also be useful in assessing 3D adipogenesis in human AT-MSCs isolated from different donors. On the other hand, if less variability in terms of size and cell density is required or reduced culturing time is ideal, as in high-throughput setups, MCP represents a valid alternative, while preserving the ability of adipogenic differentiation.

This study also has limitations. We focused on structural properties and basic adipogenic markers indicative of early differentiation, while we did not assess the impact of these properties on metabolic functions. Thus, future work should explore how the observed differences in spheroid size translate into functional metabolic outcomes, such as lipid metabolism, insulin sensitivity, or cytokine secretion, and whether these can be adjusted through matrix modification or dynamic culture conditions. For example, we predict that spheroids characterized by a smallerand constant size, such as MCP, would be best suited for large-scale screening, due also to the high number of spheroids per well, to test the effects of specific molecules on adipogenesis processes or on lipolysis due to a more homogeneous outcome. Conversely, spheroids characterized by a more complex structure due to increased ECM content and volume, as observed in ULA and HD, would be best suited for developing more complex AT-organoids, possibly embodying immune cells recruitment/differentiation and vasculature. Moreover, integrating biophysical characterization with single-cell and spatially resolved transcriptomics is likely to uncover cell-type heterogeneity and microenvironmental gradients that could influence the distinct size profiles seen across different generation techniques.
