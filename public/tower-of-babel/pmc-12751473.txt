Teacher engagement in health profession education: a scoping review
Authors: Ramya Rathan, Salah Eldin Kassab, Henk G Schmidt, Marloes Nederhand, Andrea Woltman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTeacher engagement (TE) is crucial since it substantially impacts students' academic achievement, personal growth, and general well-being. While extensive research has explored TE in various university settings, there is a noticeable gap in studies focusing on the context of health professions education (HPE). In HPE, TE is key in fostering/developing clinical competencies, critical thinking, communication, and teamwork skills essential for effective healthcare delivery. However, the literature on TE in HPE is fragmented and controversial, with conflicting findings regarding its relationship to student outcomes and professional satisfaction. Our scoping review aims to explore and address these gaps.MethodsThis study followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) methodology. Seven online databases were used to identify relevant literature. Out of 249 articles, 18 were selected for analysis.ResultsStudies on TE used varied dimensions and measurement tools, making it challenging to understand the concept consistently. Also, five studies employed the Utrecht Work Engagement Scale (UWES-9), two used the Engaged Teacher Scale (ETS), another two used a Chinese adaptation of the work engagement scale, and one used both the UWES-17 and a faculty engagement questionnaire. Qualitative studies highlighted key factors such as job satisfaction, feedback, autonomy, and professional development opportunities. The outcomes were categorized into personal satisfaction, teaching practices, student engagement, self-efficacy, job satisfaction, retention, and teaching performance.ConclusionThe analysis demonstrates a growing interest in TE within HPE, though there is a notable lack of consistency in how it is defined and measured. Further research is essential to deepen this understanding and to develop targeted strategies that cultivate a mutually beneficial and sustainable teaching and learning environment.Keywords: Teacher engagement, Health Profession Education

Background

Engagement is recognized as a multidimensional construct, comprising the cognitive, emotional and behavioural aspects, often studied from a student perspective [1, 2]. In the context of teachers, engagement is understood as a positive work-related state of mind characterized by vigor, dedication and absorption that reflects the degree of cognitive, behavioral, emotional, and social aspects in their professional roles [3]. In this review, we adopt a broader perspective, acknowledging both the established theoretical dimensions from educational psychology and the “state of mind” perspective derived from work engagement theory to capture the complexity of TE in HPE [4]. More engaged teachers are likelier to create a learning environment that results in better student learning. This is because TE plays a crucial role in student engagement and success [3] and is closely linked to improved working conditions and productivity, as well as effective communication and classroom management [5]. Teachers who are profoundly immersed in their work demonstrate greater dedication and job satisfaction, lower turnover rates, and perform better [6]. As a result, promoting TE has grown significantly in educational innovations [7, 8].

In HPE, a teacher is an essential figure who is responsible for imparting knowledge and skills to develop competent healthcare professionals. These educators are often drawn from diverse backgrounds, including physicians, nurses, scientists, and other health professionals, reflecting healthcare education’s interdisciplinary–but challenging–nature [9]. Unlike other fields, healthcare education demands that students be fully competent and practice-ready upon graduation, as their skills and knowledge directly affect patient outcomes [10].

However, in HPE, TE can also have an impact because of the curriculum itself. Competency-based curriculum, clinical simulations, and internships shape teaching practices and opportunities for engagement, which in turn influence how teachers experience their professional roles by interacting with students. Curriculum should be understood as a contextual factor when examining the determinants and outcomes of TE. Engaged teachers play a vital role in ensuring that students gain hands-on experience and are prepared to handle the ethical and professional responsibilities unique to healthcare [11]. Additionally, HPE programs must meet rigorous accreditation standards, which rely on committed faculty to uphold high-quality education, continuous improvement, and patient safety [12].

TE has gained significant attention in HPE in recent years, underscoring its potential critical role in enhancing educational outcomes and teacher well-being for several reasons. First, TE has been demonstrated to correlate with teacher effectiveness, which is crucial for student achievement [13]. Second, engaged educators are less likely to face burnout and the health problems that are associated with it, lowering teacher attrition [13]. Third, teachers who actively invest in their work are typically more productive, take on more duties, and are involved in the larger school community [14]. Therefore, understanding TE may enhance teachers’ productivity and well-being in the classroom, making it a promising avenue for research aimed at enhancing medical education.

There are varied definitions of TE, which have led to a diverse conceptualisation, because some studies focus on emotional and psychological aspects, whereas others focus on the behavioural dimensions. Furthermore, the Utrecht Work Engagement Scale (UWES) and the Engaged Teacher Scale (ETS), which are used as measurement tools, as well as other observational methods, differ in focus, further complicating the comparison. The various factors influencing engagement are inconsistently prioritised across studies [15–17]. Also, the outcomes range from teacher well-being and reduced burnout to improved student performance and school environment [18, 19], reflecting various research agendas. These discrepancies highlight the need for a framework that is theoretically grounded and draws on established psychological models which will incorporate the educational, clinical, and curricular conditions of HPE. The utility of such a framework lies in its ability to organize existing psychological dimensions of engagement while adapting them to the distinctive clinical, curricular and institutional conditions in which HPE teachers work.

This review provides a careful examination of the current research on TE in HPE. It identifies the significant gaps and proposes a comprehensive research plan to establish a unified, context-adaptable framework for measuring and enhancing TE in diverse educational and cultural settings. While there are various review articles on TE published within higher educational institutions [20–22], the concept of TE in HPE is still underexplored. The absence of a unified framework in HPE creates significant challenges. For example, it is difficult to compare the findings across the studies due to Inconsistent definitions and dimensions. Additionally, the development and validation of measurement instruments remain inconsistent, which limits their applicability in the context of HPE. This review identifies conceptual and methodological gaps and proposes the development of a theoretically informed framework for understanding TE in HPE. Such a framework should build upon established psychological models of engagement while integrating the contextual factors specific to HPE.

Methodology

Our scoping review included five steps based on Arksey and O’Malley’s protocol [23].

Step 1: To identify the research question

The questions included in this review are: (1) What are the key dimensions of TE in HPE? (2) What are the existing instruments used to measure TE in HPE? (3) What factors influence TE in HPE? (4) What are the outcomes of TE in HPE?

These questions were formulated to capture the existing research while highlighting the gaps that needed to be addressed. Identifying the dimensions of TE helps to clarify its conceptual boundaries. Examining the different measurement instruments is important to determine whether the tools are valid, reliable and appropriate for HPE contexts. Exploring the factors responsible for TE will provide an idea into what supports or hinders engagement, while mapping the outcomes will highlight the potential implications of TE for teacher well beong and educational quality.

Step 2: Identifying relevant articles

Key terms were selected to search for studies related to research questions. The key terms used were as follows: [‘Faculty Engagement’ OR ‘Teacher Engagement’ OR ‘Employee Engagement’] AND [‘Health Profession Education/Medical Education’] AND [‘Clinical’ OR ‘Medicine’ OR ‘Nursing’ OR ‘Dentistry’ OR ‘Pharmacy’ OR ‘Physiotherapy’]. These particular disciplines were selected because they are most commonly represented in the HPE literature, and preliminary scoping indicated that they had sufficient published work on TE. We also searched for studies in the bibliography of the key articles that address the subject of TE. We selected studies published from Jan 1, 2000, until Oct 10 2024. Seven databases were used: ERIC, SCOPUS, MEDLINE, ProQuest, PubMed, EBESCO and Science Direct.

Step 3: Study selection

The Inclusion criteria were peer-reviewed articles with precise data collection, published in English, the study population being HPE teachers in both undergraduate and postgraduate programs, and research mainly focusing on dimensions of engagement, measuring TE, factors affecting engagement, or the outcomes.

The exclusion criteria consisted of articles not published in English, studies related to physician engagement, TE in higher education fields not related to HPE, and letters to editors, conference papers, books, chapters, conference abstracts, editorials, commentaries, white papers, dissertations or theses.

All titles and abstracts were independently screened by 2 reviewers(RR and SK) based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Any disagreements were discussed and resolved by consensus. Full text screening followed the same procedure to ensure consistency and reduce bias.

Step 4: Charting the data

Microsoft Excel was used for the data charting. A data charting template was prepared, which consisted of the following headings such as title, author, name of the journal, year when the article was published, the country where the study was conducted, study population used in the study, the methodology used, the study design, measurement of engagement, dimensions of engagement, method used for data collection, outcome measures, results, and conclusions. The template was shared on Google Drive and was accessible to all the authors.

Step 5: Collating, summarizing, and reporting the results

This step comprised of organizing the relevant findings using the charting template. A descriptive numerical summary was analyzed for the quantitative data, including a descriptive numerical summary of the study’s characteristics. For the qualitative Analysis, RR and SK, the first two authors reviewed the articles and classified them based on themes. The third, fourth and fifth authors (HS, MN and AW) reviewed the identified themes.

Definitions of TE

Out of the 18 identified articles [24–41],, 10 studies clearly defined TE, with seven studies using the definition that was given by Schaufeli in which work engagement is defined as a “positive, work-related state of mind, involving dedication (i.e. enthusiasm), vigour (i.e. energy) and absorption (i.e. concentration)” [28–30, 33, 35, 39, 40]. A study in 2024 also defined TE as “the teachers’ effort and attention to teaching professional tasks, adequate emotions and commitment to relationships with students and colleagues in classroom and school” [34]. Another study in 2018 used a definition that was derived by Khan and defined engagement as “the harnessing of organization member selves to their work, roles; in engagement people employee and express themselves physically, cognitively, and emotionally, during role performances“ [37]. Wang, in 2023, defined work engagement as a “cognitive and affective state related to work, which is generally positive and lasting” [41]. The remaining eight studies had no precise definitions.

Dimensions of TE

TE encompasses several dimensions contributing to educators’ overall effectiveness and satisfaction in their professional roles. A study conducted in the year 2024 included four dimensions of TE: cognitive engagement, emotional engagement, social engagement with students, and social engagement with colleagues [34]. As mentioned in Table 2 below, 5 out of the 18 studies reported these dimensions [25, 29, 30, 35, 39]. Some of the studies also used terminologies such as concentration/focus instead of absorption and vitality/energy instead of vigor [33, 40, 41]. Also, a study in 2019 adopted a psychological perspective to understand engagement, focusing on how teacher values and behaviours influence learners’ emotional and cognitive engagement [24].

Methods used for measuring TE

Out of the 18 studies selected for this review, nine of them were quantitative [25, 28, 30, 33, 34, 37, 39–41], five were qualitative [26, 27, 31, 35, 36] and four were mixed in nature [24, 29, 32, 38].

Five studies used the UWES 9 [25, 28–30, 39], two studies used the ETS [34, 38], two studies used the Chinese Version of the Work Engagement Scale [33, 40] which is similar to the UWES, and one study used the UWES-17 and the Faculty engagement questionnaire based on the classic 1990 William Kahn model of employee engagement to measure TE [37].

Additionally, five studies were based on semi-structured interviews [24, 29, 31, 35, 36], one was a focus group interview [26] and one was based on a written reflection [27].

Driving factors for TE

TE results from several extrinsic and intrinsic factors. The factors identified from the selected papers for this study were broadly categorized into individual, institutional, student, and relationship factors, as detailed in Table 3. Within the individual factors category, intrinsic motivation [25, 29, 30, 32, 36, 38] and teachers’ beliefs and attitudes [24, 35, 38] were frequently observed. In the institutional factors category, mentorship and administrative support [27, 30, 31, 36] along with the teaching conditions and facilities [29, 31, 32] was the most frequently reported. For the student factors, feedback and evaluation [28, 29, 31, 35] was identified as strong corelates of TE across multiple studies. However it is important to note that most of the reviews studies report associations other than relationships. While these findings suggest that student input can meaningfully influence TE, more research is required to determine the extent to which feedback and evaluation is the true cause. Lastly, regarding the relationship factors, both social support [29, 33] and the work environment and relationships [32, 35]were identified in the existing literature.

Outcomes of TE in HPE

The concept of TE or work engagement, whether in clinical or academic settings, highlighted on the importance of both personal satisfaction and student outcomes [24, 28, 36]. Some of the studies also mentioned how engagement positively influenced teaching practices and student learning experience [27, 28, 31]. There is a clear link connecting job satisfaction and work engagement. Higher job satisfaction tends to correlate with greater work engagement, suggesting that when teachers feel satisfied in their roles, they are more engaged in their work [29, 30, 32]. Some studies also pointed out that external factors such as role stress and lack of recognition can negatively impact TE [32, 40]. Another observation we made was that some studies reported that cognitive engagement was higher when compared to emotional or social engagement, which showed that while teachers may be mentally engaged, there could be a lack of emotional or social involvement [37–39].

Discussion

TE is critical in an educational institution since it influences both the quality of teaching and potentially student outcomes. The studies reviewed in this study provide strong support for the TE role in enhancing teaching quality and teacher well-being. Since most of the studies have focused on teacher-centred variables without measuring learning results, direct evidence linking TE to improved student outcomes remains limited. Therefore, the assumption that higher TE leads to improved student outcomes should be interpreted cautiously.

A major theoretical limitation identified in this review is the limited integration of frameworks from educational psychology. The inconsistencies we observed across definitions, dimensions and driving factors in HPE reflect deeper psychological shortcomings in how engagement has been conceptualized in this field. Unlike the broader educational psychology literature, which clearly distinguishes between antecedents, components and outcomes of engagement, many HPE studies conflate motivational factors with the construct itself. Situating our findings within established engagement frameworks from higher education [2, 42] clarifies these inconsistencies.

In educational psychology, engagement is understood as a multidimensional construct encompassing cognitive, emotional and behavioural dimensions [1, 2, 42]. Most HPE studies relies on occupational models such as the UWES, without significantly grounding them within broader psychological theories of engagement. Integrating models such as the Job Demands-Resources (JD-R) framework [43], Self-determination theory(SDT) [44], and Social Cognitive Theory(SCT) [45] would provide stronger conceptual clarity: autonomy and intrinsic motivation aligns with SDT, while the influence of workload and institutional resources aligns with JD-R model. Likewise, the role of feedback and teacher confidence echoes SCT. Interpreting the findings through these frameworks reveals that many of these variations across studies arise from different psychological assumptions rather than actual conceptual disagreements.

Cross-cultural differences observed in the reviewed studies also illustrate the situated nature of engagement. Institutional culture, clinical workload and teaching norms influence how TE is expressed and sustained, reinforcing the need for context-sensitive frameworks rather than universal models [34, 38, 39]. In this respect, engagement should be viewed as a construct shaped by local conditions, resources and expectations.

The ETS and the UWES are both important tools used to measure engagement in HPE. The ETS, developed by Klassen [46]; appears more situated in educational contexts because it captures cognitive, emotional and social engagement with students and colleagues, aligning with a sociocultural framework that emphasizes interaction and context in teaching [47]. In contrast, the UWES measures general work engagement, which focuses on vigor, dedication, and absorption and is used across different occupational groups [29] and may not be able to capture teaching-specific dimensions such as the interaction between students and colleagues.

TE is shaped by individual, institutional, student and relationship factors. In HPE, where teachers are often balancing clinical and teaching duties, institutional factors such as a supportive culture [26, 27, 30, 31, 36, 39], job demand and resources [28, 35], and the facilities and teaching conditions [29, 31, 32, 41] are critical to their sustained engagement and effectiveness.

Curricular design also mediates TE by shaping teachers’ autonomy, motivation and workload. Problem-based learning (PBL) exemplifies this relationship, as it encourages facilitation and collaboration but may also increase the cognitive and emotional demands. Prior work has highlighted such curricular influences on both teacher and Student engagement [48, 49] emphasising the need for further investigation.

Student motivation [29] and feedback [28, 29, 31, 35] reinforce a teacher’s dedication. Motivated and more responsive students contribute significantly [50]; validating the teacher’s efforts and providing a sense of purpose. Because most available studies were cross-sectional or descriptive, these associations should be interpreted as correlational and more longitudinal research is needed to confirm these causal pathways. Furthermore, the students’ varied learning needs require teachers to adapt and innovate, which in turn fosters growth and engagement among teachers. Relationship factors further strengthen TE, as supportive relationships with colleagues, mentors, and students promote collaboration, emotional resilience and continuous improvement while helping to reduce burnout [32, 35].

TE enhances teaching performance, job satisfaction and retention as engaged teachers are more effective role models and are more likely to remain committed to their institutions [28, 30]. These outcomes highlight the crucial role of TE in fostering a productive educational environment, thereby enhancing both the teacher’s and the student’s experiences.

To address our research questions, the results showed that (i) TE in HPE has been conceptualized using varied dimensions, with limited consensus, (ii) measurement tools related to TE are UWES and ETS, but often without consideration of their contextual suitability, iii)multiple individual, institutional, and student factors influence TE, though evidence remains limited and iv) outcomes are most consistently linked to teacher wellbeing and teacher quality, with less evidence of direct impact on student outcomes. These findings directly support our argument that TE in HPE lacks a unified, psychologically grounded framework.

Conclusion

The analysis of the current study reveals an increasing focus on the topic of TE within HPE. There is still an inconsistency in its definition, framework, and the tools required to measure it. The answers to our research questions highlight the importance of developing an HPE-specific framework grounded in existing psychological causal models, rather than seeking a single unified model. Such a framework would enable more consistent definitions, valid measurements, and interventions that could enhance both teacher well-being and educational quality.

Framework for future research

The topic of TE in HPE needs more research to deepen our understanding and improve outcomes. Firstly, a comprehensive framework specific to TE in HPE is lacking. Rather than aiming for a single unified framework, future research should focus on developing and validating an HPE-specific framework grounded in established psychological causal models. Such a framework would integrate psychological dimensions of engagement with the contextual variables distinctive to teaching and learning in HPE. Further research is required to understand the adoption and effectiveness of the ETS within HPE, as many educators still depend on the UWES to measure TE.

Another clear drawback is the fuzziness of the construct, which mixes the factors and consequences with the dimensions of TE. For instance, motivational aspects, feedback or social support are more accurately considered as factors influencing TE but are sometimes considered as dimensions of engagement itself. Similarly, outcomes such as job satisfaction, reduced burnout or improved teaching performance are occasionally embedded within the conceptualisation of TE rather than treated as consequences. This blending of factors, dimensions and outcomes complicates the synthesis of the findings and contributes to the lack of conceptual clarity in HPE literature.

Additionally, it is crucial to determine whether TE should be regarded as a stable trait or a fluctuating state. The current literature in HPE continues to debate this issue. In this scoping review, most studies have conceptualized TE as a stable construct, i.e. a trait. Engagement involves a more profound psychological connection to one’s work, encompassing cognitive, emotional, and behavioural dimensions, which indicate a trait-like quality [37]. Additionally, the emotional aspect of engagement, which includes positive thoughts about the institution and work environment, further supports the notion of engagement as a trait, as it reflects a consistent emotional response rather than a fluctuating one. On the other hand, the concept of engagement has been conceptualised in different ways. Some frameworks view engagement as a relatively stable trait or construct that reflects enduring psychological investment in work. Others, however, conceptualise engagement as a more dynamic and context-dependent state that can fluctuate based on situational factors and work environments. This ongoing debate in educational psychology has not yet been fully incorporated into the HPE Literature, which limits the conceptual clarity of TE in this field. Understanding the concept of TE within the specific context of HPE is the most significant contribution of this review. This review will contribute to a contextually grounded understanding of how educators experience and sustain engagement in complex educational environments by understanding TE through established psychological theories.

Limitations

Despite a comprehensive search strategy, certain studies in HPE may have been missed if they had been published in databases that were not included in our search and we also acknowledge that other health professions, such as occupational therapy or psychiatry, were not included in the search, and this may have excluded potentially relevant studies.

While focusing only on studies from HPE offered a clear picture of TE in this field, this can be a limitation. Engagement, motivation and learning are fundamentally psychological constructs whose theoretical foundations lie in educational psychology. By restricting our dataset exclusively to HPE studies, our ability to interpret these constructs through robust psychological models was limited. Future research would benefit from integrating the wider literature to provide a more comprehensive and theoretically grounded interpretation of TE in HPE.

Secondly, this review may not have included relevant research published in languages other than English. Lastly, the review findings are based on data available up to the time of our search. Given the rapidly evolving interest in HPE, it is likely that new relevant studies have been published since our data collection, which is not reflected in our analysis.
