Quality assessment of synthetic data in healthcare: a critical appraisal with a focus on subgroup bias
Authors: Christos Chatzichristos, Dimitris Katsimpokis, Congting Lai, Lore Van Santvliet, Flavio Camarrone, Daan Knoors, Gijs Geleijnse, Michel Van Speybroek, Martine Lewi, Bart Vannieuwenhuyse, Maarten De Vos
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe creation of synthetic data in healthcare research has emerged as a compelling solution to address challenges related to data privacy, in case of data-sharing and data scarceness for training machine learning models.ObjectiveThe current paper aims to provide a critical landscape analysis of synthetic data quality metrics used in healthcare, mapping the state of the field, examining how current fidelity and utility scores often fail to capture weaknesses that appear in specific patient subgroups, and illustrating these blind spots with a lung-cancer case study. We specifically set out to highlight the need for assessing synthetic datasets not only at the aggregate level but also across clinically meaningful subgroups, to ensure reliability and fairness in downstream applications.MethodsBy examining the landscape of synthetic data adoption in healthcare, we highlight the methodological and utility considerations that shape its integration into the research ecosystem. We elucidate how synthetic data can expedite research initiatives, support data-driven decision-making, and facilitate innovative methodologies while safeguarding sensitive patient information. Conversely, while many papers focus solely on the advantages of synthetic data, we aim to highlight also the potential constraints of using synthetic data in a real-world case involving lung cancer patient level data. Our analysis centers on identifying situations where synthetic data might introduce biases or inaccuracies that hinder the generation of meaningful clinical insights.ResultsWe demonstrate the limitations of using synthetic data in a lung cancer case study, especially when dealing with small subgroups from the original dataset. While high values for fidelity and utility metrics are achieved when considering the entire synthetic dataset, significant discrepancies arise in cross-classification performance when examining subsets. Additionally, visual inspection reveals spurious correlations in the synthetic data that are not present in the real data.ConclusionGlobal realism scores can give a false sense of security: in our case study, metrics that rate the full dataset as “high quality” overlook errors which appear once we zoom in on specific subgroups. We argue that future work must (i) design subgroup-aware fidelity and utility metrics, (ii) favor conditional generators that model rare strata explicitly, and (iii) report metric panels alongside qualitative, clinical sanity checks. Until such standards mature, our analysis highlights that researchers and regulators should treat synthetic data with caution.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12911-026-03719-3.

Introduction

Back in 2017, The Economist published a story titled, “The world’s most valuable resource is no longer oil, but data” [1]. Since then, data has continued to be one of the most critical and valuable assets in industry, where we see innovative and complex machine learning (ML) algorithms and architectures, which depend on a plethora of data for training, testing, mining, and designing ‘data-centric’ statistical algorithms [1]. The rapid advancement of computing power, storage capabilities, sensor technology, and advanced data processing techniques has ushered us into an era of unprecedented data availability across various domains. This proliferation of data has paved the way for the analysis of big data, enabling us to harness its immense potential. The synergy between big data and ML has proven to be transformative. As datasets expand in size and richness, more complex ML models can be utilized resulting in improved performance. ML empowers us to extract valuable insights from vast, diverse, and disjoint data sources with remarkable speed and scalability, often with minimal human intervention.

However, the healthcare sector faces unique challenges in embracing this data-driven revolution, resulting in a noticeable lag in harnessing the power of big data and ML compared to other industries. Access to high quality, findable, accessible, interoperable, and reusable (FAIR) [2] patient level data is one of the main challenges for advancing innovation in ML and big data for life sciences and healthcare. Several measures are put in place to protect the patient, such as data privacy regulations and security laws. Along with organizational guidelines, those measures limit access to patient level data for care optimization and secondary data use, such as scientific research. Data anonymization can play a key role in striking a balance between safeguarding data privacy and allowing reuse of patient level data for scientific research. The process of anonymizing patient level data should ensure that patient re-identification is impossible, mitigating the risk of privacy breaches. Effective anonymization techniques can pave the way for privacy-preserving access to patient level data at scale, enabling the use of real-world data as a foundational resource for healthcare studies. In this manuscript, we explore the significance of synthetic data in healthcare, focusing on its potential to address these challenges and foster innovation in the field of data science for healthcare and life sciences.

In the quest to address the challenge of securely sharing healthcare data while preserving privacy, two distinct avenues have emerged:i.Data management approaches such as federated machine learning (FML) [3], Multi-Party Computation (MPC) [4], and Distributed Learning (DL) [5] utilize advanced ML techniques to train models across a multitude of decentralized edge devices or servers that retain local data samples. Importantly, these approaches sidestep the need for direct data exchange, addressing some of the core privacy concerns. However, these approaches entail certain limitations and constraints, such as communication and computation overhead, scalability and limited expressiveness.ii.Alternatively, methods and tools are being designed to generate synthetic health records at scale, eliminating the risk of accidental data disclosure [6, 7]. As the ML community’s interest in synthetic data continues to grow, the synthetic datasets generated are becoming increasingly comprehensive, detailed, and representational over time.

In an ideal scenario, synthetic data closely mimics the characteristics of the original dataset, enabling analysis as if it were real data. However, its significant advantage lies in its ability to effectively address privacy concerns. When synthetic datasets are created, they ideally sever any direct ties to individual patients, while retaining the statistical patterns of the original data. As a result, researchers from various institutions can exchange synthetic derivatives (when privacy metrics assure that the risk of sensitive data exposure is minimal) without encountering the same data privacy concerns associated with genuine patient level data [8, 9]. Moreover, the time required to derive valuable insights from synthetic data can be significantly shorter thanks to accelerated approval of use for scientific research, compared to real patient level data. Once a generator has been trained, synthetic records can be produced very fast and in virtually unlimited numbers, so researchers can spin up millions of synthetic data samples, oversample rare events, or create dozens of balanced test-and-validation splits without the years of recruitment and curation that real-world cohorts demand. This mass-production ability unlocks analyses that observational data might not be able to support, such as Monte-Carlo power studies, systematic sensitivity checks, counterfactual “what-if” scenarios, and the training of machine-learning models on otherwise vanishingly small subgroups.

Several studies suggest that synthetic data can be used as substitute for real data in controlled environments or specific use cases [10]. The use of synthetic data can be considered especially beneficial when access to real data is constrained due to privacy regulations or data scarcity. Synthetic data allows model training to proceed without relying on potentially sensitive or unavailable information, making it a valuable tool in situations where data privacy is paramount, as in healthcare.

We review quality metrics for synthetic data, with a specific focus on realism metrics and we also discuss pitfalls in data generation methods, the smoothing effect, and the disagreement between different metrics. In the Results section, where we present a real-life use case involving lung cancer patients, we illustrate how heterogeneity in underlying statistics can lead to divergent outcomes and evaluation metrics when synthetic data is analyzed at the subgroup level. This underscores the importance of assessing synthetic data quality not only using global metrics but also in relation to the specific analytic tasks and populations under investigation. Methods

Several studies across many domains propose alternative methods for generating synthetic data [11]. The methods used for generation of synthetic data in healthcare research can be separated based on the type of data to synthesize. Tabular data (Electronic Health Records (EHRs) [12, 13]), time-series (e.g. disease progression [14], Electrocardiograph (ECG) [15–17]etc.), medical images [18, 19] and gene expression data [20] are the main subcategories of health data for which synthetic counterparts are computed. Despite the fact, as often stated in publications, that the primary goal of synthetic data is to act as suitable replacement for real data [21] or comprise a realistic substitute for existing data [22, 23], it has been shown that there are serious limitations of synthetic data that must be considered [6, 10]. Models for synthetic data generation predominantly capture common patterns and central tendencies in the original dataset, but they often fail to adequately represent outlier, or rare cases present in authentic data. These outliers, while infrequent, can be clinically or operationally crucial, capturing atypical but important variations such as rare disease phenotypes, exceptional responses to treatments, or unexpected interactions between variables. Their omission can lead to systematic bias, reducing representativeness and masking potential edge cases that are critical for robust model performance. In certain contexts, limited reproduction of outliers can be beneficial, as minority or unique records might carry higher risks for re-identification and privacy leakage. However, in most training and validation scenarios, especially in health or safety-critical applications, this gap severely constrains model generalization, diminishes predictive accuracy, and undermines the trustworthiness of synthetic datasets. Ultimately, the fidelity and inclusiveness of synthetic data remain tightly bound to the capacity of the generative model to balance privacy protection with comprehensive data representation.

As mentioned in several studies, there is no single fit-for-all approach that can generate the optimal synthetic data in all scenarios, and as will be further explained in the metrics section, one must consider the problem at hand and possibly carry out a tentative analysis before choosing the suitable data generating method [24–26]. A detailed overview of the generative models is out of the scope of the current manuscript and we refer the interested reader to relevant reviews [6, 7, 27–30].

Quality assessment of the generated dataset

There is a warranted need for quality assessment of synthetic data but is intrinsically difficult and very closely tied to the application for which they will be used. In the most general case, one hopes that the synthetic dataset is a perfect substitute for the real data for all applications. This would mean that the distributions of the data are identical in all dimensions and all correlations between variables are retained [31]. However, when strong privacy guarantees are required, the noise added or suppression of information needed to protect individuals inevitably removes some higher-order structure, so certain relationships will not be preserved. By contrast, when synthetic data are generated purely for internal data-augmentation (e.g., to balance classes during model training without ever leaving the secure environment) privacy demands are much lower; the emphasis shifts toward retaining as much correlation and rare-pattern fidelity as possible, even if that means relaxing or forgoing formal privacy budgets. Hence, a thought-provoking paradox emerges about the trade-off between patient privacy and data utility in synthetic data mapping [32].

For every new synthetic dataset, a well-suited quality check must be developed. Robust evaluation of synthetic datasets demands metrics that are generic enough to let different generators be compared and cover all aspects of data quality, yet adaptable enough to reflect the clinical question at hand. An one-size-fits-all metric cannot do this: fidelity and privacy requirements differ radically between, say, an arrhythmia-detection model that needs realistic millisecond-level ECG morphology and a health-economics study concerned only with annual billing totals. For that reason, multiple domain-specific and complementary metrics are typically used that fall into two groups: a) data realism, and b) privacy (or information disclosure) [6, 7] (Fig. 1). The first family, data-realism metrics, is tuned to the analytic task and asks whether the synthetic records preserve those statistical patterns of the Real World Data (RWD) that the task relies on. The second family, privacy-disclosure metrics, measures how difficult it would be to re-identify individuals or sensitive attributes from the synthetic dataset, regardless of how realistic it otherwise appears.Fig. 1Different types of metrics for quality assessment of synthetic data

Different types of metrics for quality assessment of synthetic data

There are three broad classes of privacy (information) disclosure metrics: membership, identity and attribute (feature) disclosure. Membership disclosure asks only whether a particular individual’s real record was part of the confidential training set at all. If an adversary already knows partial demographic or clinical details, repeated probing of the synthetic dataset (or the generator) may allow them to decide whether that target appears in the original data, an outcome that can itself be sensitive (e.g., confirming inclusion in a cancer registry) [33]. Identity disclosure goes a step further: by systematically combining auxiliary information (such as age, postcode and diagnosis dates) with similarity searches or queries on the synthetic data, an attacker may eventually link a synthetic record to the actual person it most closely reflects [8, 34]. Finally, attribute disclosure refers to the risk of an attacker inferring sensitive features from guessing the original value of the synthesized attributes of an individual whose information is contained in the confidential dataset based on a subset of attributes known to the attacker.

The data realism metrics are further divided into Fidelity (or resemblance) and Utility metrics (Fig. 1). Various fidelity metrics are utilized and usually divided into qualitative metrics and statistics (univariate and multivariate, although this discrimination is not always clear since multiple methods can be used for both). Qualitative metrics consist of the visual inspection of the generated synthetic data (or visualizations of properties of it) by experts. Usually, qualitative checks by experts are implemented as final check after the assessment of the data with privacy metrics and quantitative realism metrics. Keep in mind that the visual inspection of the whole generated dataset is usually not possible, due to time constraints, hence, qualitative assessment of a subset is performed. The main categories of quantitative fidelity metrics are statistic tests (e.g., Kolmogorov-Smirnov test, t-test, chi-squared test, etc.) and distance metrics between the Probability Distribution Functions of the real and synthetic data (e.g., Kullback-Leibler divergence, Jensen-Shannon distance, Wasserstein distance, etc.). Different types of realism metrics. Fidelity and utility

Utility metrics, and in particular cross-classification metrics, are central to this study. Cross-classification metrics give a task-oriented view of utility: they tell us whether a model trained on one version of the data generalizes to another. In practice, four complementary train–test configurations are explored: training on real data and testing on a real hold-out set (TRTR, the upper-bound benchmark); training on real data and testing on synthetic data (TRTS); training on synthetic data and testing on real data (TSTR); and training and testing solely on synthetic data (TSTS). Standard performance statistics (accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, F1-score, etc.) are reported for each configuration, and the gaps between them reveal how much predictive signal the generator has preserved or distorted. If a model trained on synthetic data out-performs the real-to-real baseline, the jump usually reflects leakage, the generator has memorized parts of the training set or has encoded label information in a way that the task becomes artificially easy. Conversely, if synthetic-trained models perform well below the real-to-real benchmark, the gap suggests that the generator has over-smoothed or obscured clinically important structure, so the synthetic set will not support reliable downstream prediction. In healthcare, these gaps carry heightened stakes: an inflated synthetic-to-real performance could lead clinicians or regulators into deploying models whose apparent accuracy is an artifact of data leakage, jeopardizing patient safety. A diminished gap may lead to the premature rejection of potentially life-improving tools and waste already limited clinical data resources. A weakness of single-model assessments is their sensitivity to the specific learning algorithm.

Disagreements among metrics

Even after defining various metrics for assessing the realism and privacy of synthetic data, the actual evaluation of synthetic data remains a significant challenge, primarily due to the conflicting outcomes often reported by different metrics. These metrics, each designed to measure a specific aspect of the data, such as privacy, utility, or statistical fidelity, can lead to divergent conclusions about the quality and usability of the synthetic datasets. The disagreements among evaluation metrics primarily stem from their different sensitivities and objectives [34, 47]. Metrics designed to assess statistical fidelity focus on how well the synthetic data mimics the statistical properties of the original dataset. They measure aspects like distributional similarity and correlation structures without necessarily considering the privacy implications. In contrast, privacy metrics evaluate how effectively the synthetic data protects against the identification of individuals; achieving a high score generally requires the generation process to inject noise or to modify data structures, which can inadvertently reduce the data’s utility for analytical purposes [6]. Another layer of complexity is added by the inherent characteristics of the data itself. Real-world data often exhibits high dimensionality and complex interdependencies among variables, making it difficult to create synthetic versions that maintain all properties simultaneously. The more complex the dataset, the harder it is to achieve a good balance across different metrics.

Statistical smoothing and sub-groups

The restricted dimensionality inherent in synthetic data modeling imposes fundamental constraints on its ability to accurately reproduce the complex, high-dimensional dependencies present in the original data. Prior work has shown that many generative models for high-dimensional biomedical data tend to prioritize dominant patterns while under-representing rare or outlier cases, particularly when training objectives emphasize global divergence or reconstruction losses. Most generative approaches, whether based on variational autoencoders (VAEs) [48], generative adversarial networks (GANs) [49], or diffusion models [50], operate by mapping the high-dimensional data distribution into a lower-dimensional latent space. This mapping acts as a form of dimensionality compression, whereby only dominant variance components are preserved, often corresponding to the primary sources of variation in the data. As a result, the model’s learned manifold tends to emphasize global structure while smoothing over weak or rare dependencies within the correlation network of the data. Architectures such as VAEs, GANs and related models map complex patient distributions into a lower-dimensional latent or dependency space, which effectively performs statistical smoothing: major modes and correlations are preserved, while weak or infrequent dependencies that characterize small clinical subgroups are attenuated [51, 52].

This smoothing has particularly important implications for sub-group representation. In many biomedical datasets, such as patient cohorts or -omics profiles, there exist small but clinically critical sub-populations, patients with rare mutational signatures, atypical response patterns, or specific demographic characteristics. Because these groups are underrepresented in the training distribution, the synthetic generator often fails to allocate sufficient probability mass to their regions in feature space. When loss functions are optimized for overall distributional similarity (e.g., minimizing Wasserstein distance or KL divergence), the contribution of rare events is statistically minor, leading to gradient under-updating for these subspaces. Consequently, the synthetic data may reproduce the dominant population structure while erasing or blending the fine-grained details that define rare sub-groups.

For example, consider a scenario where the marginal distribution of a gene expression variable exhibits a long-tailed distribution reflecting rare, biologically meaningful overexpression events. A generative model trained to minimize global reconstruction loss might truncate or attenuate the tail, leading to the loss of these biologically important outlier cases. In the context of oncology or -omics applications, this could mean that minor yet relevant molecular profiles, such as those correlating with resistance to targeted therapy, are inadequately captured in the synthetic data, thereby limiting its use in downstream analyses that rely on population diversity and subgroup fidelity. Such an example has been reported with pediatric COVID-19 models with synthetic records led to increased performance drops in 57.7% of the minority subgroups examined, even though the overall AUC metrics appeared to improve. This highlights how aggregate performance measures can mask deteriorations in performance for underrepresented groups [53]. Furthermore, another study showed that iteratively retraining on synthetic outputs creates “fairness feedback loops” that progressively erase minoritized representations, starting from an otherwise unbiased dataset [54]. Our lung-cancer use case should therefore be read as an empirical illustration of this hypothesized limitation: despite good global fidelity and utility metrics on the full 50 000-patient synthetic cohort, we observe clear degradation in cross-classification performance and altered correlation structure once the analysis is restricted to the 992-patient stage IV sub-cohort. This pattern is consistent with the idea that current generators reproduce the dominant structure of the registry reasonably well, but smooth over or distort the fine-grained dependencies that define under-represented patient strata.

To examine this systematically, we next introduce a lung cancer registry dataset, and an experimental design aimed at testing how well current generative models and evaluation metrics perform when analyses focus on underrepresented patient strata. This will allow an assessment not only of global statistical fidelity but also of the local reconstruction fidelity of rare signals and sub-group structures that underpin clinical and molecular heterogeneity.

Case‑Study design: Lung‑Cancer registry dataset

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) is a type of lung cancer that comprises several subtypes, including adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and large cell carcinoma. Unlike small cell lung cancer, NSCLC cells are characterized by larger cell size and distinct histological features. NSCLC is the most common form of lung cancer, accounting for approximately 85% of all cases. The prognosis and treatment options for NSCLC depend on factors such as the stage of the cancer, the specific subtype, and the patient’s overall health. Advances in medical research and treatment modalities have contributed to improved outcomes for individuals diagnosed with NSCLS. Lung cancer is both prevalent and highly heterogeneous, making it an ideal testbed for subgroup-aware evaluation of synthetic data. At the same time, the high sensitivity and strict privacy regulations surrounding clinical and genomic data in oncology make direct access to real patient-level datasets nearly impossible, highlighting the need to explore synthetic data as a privacy-preserving alternative for such analyses.

The Lancelot project [55] was a public-private funded research project implemented between 2020 and 2022. Lancelot aimed to contribute to reducing the impact of cancer by enabling the reuse of sensitive health data while preserving patients’ privacy. Lancelot focused on enabling privacy-preserving analyses of so-called vertically partitioned data, where data of a group of individuals is distributed among multiple data sources. In this project, open-source privacy-preserving solutions have been developed that are generically applicable on vertically partitioned datasets by making use of Multi-Party-Computation (MPC). These solutions have been integrated into an open-source infrastructure and tested in an experimental setting on realistic synthetic data, based on IKNLs Netherlands Cancer Registry (NCR) data. The patient cohort, utilized during the Lancelot project, consists of patients with NSCLC that are registered in the NCR from 2014 to 2019 (50.000 NSCLC patients). This data contains features from pathological data (anatomo-path features) and data corresponding to treatment (a complete list of the 37 distinct features can be viewed in Table 2). PrivBayes [57] is a privacy-preserving generative model that has been used to create synthetic tabular data while preserving the privacy of individuals and is based on the principle of probabilistic modeling, specifically Bayesian networks. PrivBayes is trained on a de-identified dataset to learn the conditional dependencies and structure of the original data. The algorithm infers the probabilistic relationships between different attributes in the dataset and estimates the parameters of the learned Bayesian network based on the training data. This involves determining the probabilities associated with each variable given its parent variables in the network. PrivBayes ensures that synthetic data preserves the conditional dependencies between variables, making it suitable for analysis while protecting individual privacy and providing a privacy guarantee by introducing noise to the probability distributions in the Bayesian network. The PrivBayes generator is chosen because it mirrors a real-world scenario: a real dataset that is not accessible to us and a PrivBayes-generated release from a completed project. Our aim is to evaluate whether that dataset would have been deemed acceptable had reviewers looked beyond global fidelity metrics and examined subgroup utility. We retain the implementation’s default privacy budget of ε = 1.0 (with θ = 20 as the maximum number of parents per node). An ε of 1 limits any single person’s influence on the output probabilities to a factor of e1 ≈ 2.7, a level widely regarded as providing a balanced, “moderate” privacy guarantee while preserving useful analytical signal [58]. In other words, PrivBayes represents the class of classical, privacy-budgeted Bayesian network generators that are already deployed in real healthcare data-release workflows, making it a natural anchor for our case study.

To complement our primary synthesis approach, we also generate data with Gaussian Copula Synthesizer (GCS) [59] and CTGAN [60] so we could test whether the findings we observed, generalize beyond a single generator. The GCS model fits marginal distributions with kernel-density estimates and captures cross-feature dependence through a Gaussian copula; we used the Synthetic Data Vault (SDV)-library defaults (copula-correlation estimated on the full real training set, 10 Monte-Carlo samples per synthetic record, and no privacy constraints) to keep the procedure reproducible [59]. For CTGAN, a conditional tabular GAN that models mixed discrete/continuous columns, we train for 100 epochs with a batch size of 500, pac = 10, learning rate 2 × 10− 4, and we use conditional vectors on all categorical fields to encourage balanced generation of minority categories. Including these two structurally different generators, which were not part of our original shortlist, we aim to test whether the limitation in the representation of subgroups exists in all methods. Including these two structurally different generators allows us to cover three major design families for tabular health data (privacy‑budgeted Bayesian networks, copula‑based models and deep GANs) and to test whether the subgroup‑representation limitations we observe are specific to one architecture or persist across paradigms.

As explained above, we can compute general metrics for the whole dataset. Additionally, we want to focus here what happens in clinically relevant sub-cohorts. Therefore, we work at two distinct levels. First, we analyze the entire generated synthetic population (50 000 records). Next, we extract a focused subset: 992 patients with stage IV NSCLC, who are ineligible for surgery or radiotherapy and instead receive systemic chemo- or immunotherapy. This sub-cohort is a clinically defined stratum: stage IV NSCLC patients who are ineligible for surgery or radiotherapy and instead receive systemic chemo- or immunotherapy. It was selected a priori because it represents a small (under 2% of the cohort) but clinically critical population for pharmacological research purposes. This subset is sampled from the full synthetic cohort (no separate model is trained) so any divergences we observe reflect the generator’s smoothing effect. A similar subset with exact same criteria was extracted from the real data.

We retained 22 manually chosen predictors after excluding administrative IDs, incidence year, redundant TNM components, anatomically granular or low-information variables, and features with clearly non-random missingness. Preprocessing comprised renaming registry codes to intuitive English labels, applying simple recoding/encoding (binary gender and treatment indicators, ordinal TNM encodings, categorical typing of clinical factors), and training models on real data with standardization via scikit-learn [61] StandardScaler, reusing the same fitted scalers when evaluating the models on the corresponding synthetic cohorts. All analyses were performed at patient level (one record per patient, no duplicates), without additional stratification of train/test splits. To make the procedure explicit for all four configurations: in every case the StandardScaler is fitted only on the training data of that configuration and then applied unchanged to the corresponding test set, and hyperparameters are tuned by inner cross-validation only within the training domain. Thus for TRTR and TRTS the scaler is fitted on the real training split (and reused on the real test split and on the synthetic set, respectively), with tuning on the real training split; for TSTR and TSTS the scaler is fitted on the synthetic training split (and reused on the real test set and on the held-out synthetic split, respectively), with tuning on the synthetic training split. No information from any test set is used at training time.

Comparing utility and fidelity metrics across these two scales quantifies how much detail is lost in the sub-cohort relative to the full dataset. We evaluate different fidelity and utility metrics for both cohorts. For fidelity we focus mainly on various distances between the feature distributions. Statistical tests were also considered but due to the size of the dataset the values were inconclusive. For the computation of those metrics, the github code provided in [35] was used and modified. However, in this manuscript, we contend that there are limitations in the synthetic dataset which have been overlooked. Despite its strong performance in standard fidelity metrics, there is a critical need for comprehensive qualitative measurements and utility metrics, as we will demonstrate. Most importantly, we will illustrate that while the dataset may appear adequately realistic when considered as a whole, focusing on specific subsets of the cohort reveals significant discrepancies in outcomes of interest and utility metrics. As it can be noted with PrivBayes in the sub-cohort some of the features (Age, Lateralization, Lymph nodes examined, Therapy and Targeted Therapy) exhibit higher distance from the real data (higher than 0.15) which is by itself a “concerning” finding for the realism of the dataset. If we examine the sub-cohort generated from the other two generators, we can see also suboptimal fidelity, mainly in the extreme difference in the percentage of missing values for some variables. GCS in the sub-cohort generates data with missing values above 50% in Tumor Size, Brain Diagnostics and Time to OS, showing inability to capture correctly numerical values. CTGAN, although it generates data with higher fidelity, yields data with a big distance in cm (values above 0.6 in Cosine and JS distance are considered very big) and increased difference in missing values in Brain Diagnostics and Positive Lymph node.

Moving to utility metrics, we observe that models trained on synthetic data exhibit a marked reduction in the variability of AUC values when tested across multiple iterations (Fig. 3). Although model training involved cross-validation for hyperparameter tuning, the reported AUC per fold reflects test performance. The reduced variability in these test results suggests that the synthetic datasets produce more homogeneous training samples, pointing toward the smoothing effect introduced during generation (an effect observed consistently across all generators). In contrast, models trained on real data show greater variability in AUC across folds, reflecting the richer heterogeneity and complexity of the original dataset. Discussion

The integration of synthetic data in healthcare research offers a compelling pathway to address the challenges of data privacy and accessibility that accompany the use of real patient data. Our analysis delves into the utility, limitations, and inherent biases of synthetic data, especially when considered in a health research context. The fundamental role of synthetic data in augmenting healthcare research, despite certain pitfalls, underscores its growing significance in the field. While our study primarily focuses on structured data, it is important to note that synthetic data generation for unstructured modalities, such as language and imaging, represents a rapidly expanding yet comparatively less regulated and less studied domain.

As our results demonstrate, a detailed, subset-specific analysis of synthetic data use in healthcare needs a more careful consideration. Discrepancies in outcomes between models, trained on real versus synthetic data, may become particularly evident within smaller, specialized sub-cohorts. For instance, our investigations uncovered significant differences in model accuracy, especially when employing non-linear SVMs/ GBc in a sub-cohort of our population (accounting for less than 2% of the total cohort). This provides, to our knowledge, one of the first concrete, end-to-end demonstrations in a real oncology registry that synthetic datasets can simultaneously achieve strong global fidelity yet fail to support reliable utility in a clearly defined, clinically relevant minority subgroup. These findings illustrate that while synthetic data proficiently replicates the general characteristics of large datasets, it often falls short in capturing the nuanced, subset-specific dynamics crucial for precise clinical research and informed decision-making. When evaluating synthetic data, researchers typically rely on a limited subset of metrics, often due to practical constraints such as computational resources, high dimensionality, or data access restrictions [65]. This subset is frequently treated as a faithful proxy for the dataset’s overall quality. However, evaluations based on marginal statistics, pairwise comparisons, or performance on a single predictive task can overlook crucial aspects such as subgroup interactions, higher-order correlations, and temporal dependencies. Our contribution here is to show explicitly, within a single case study, how a standard “panel” of realism and cross-classification metrics can look reassuring at the full-cohort level while masking substantial degradation once the analysis is restricted to a well-defined sub-cohort (stage IV NSCLC) [66]. Although future methodological advances may reduce this limitation, today’s synthetic datasets should therefore be used with caution for hypothesis-free risk-factor discovery.

Synthetic data’s intrinsic smoothing of high-dimensional detail is tolerable for routine descriptive analyses but becomes critical (both ethically and statistically), when the goal is for example extreme-phenotype discovery (e.g., identifying molecular drivers of the rare patients who achieve exceptional benefit or suffer severe toxicity in immuno-oncology [67]). Because generators compress complex correlation structures into lower-rank representations, the low-frequency signals that define these outliers can be damped or lost, leading investigators (and, downstream, clinicians or regulators) to draw false-negative conclusions. That risk is not merely technical: publishing or relying on smoothed-over synthetic data could amplify health-equity gaps, misallocate research funding, or misinform therapeutic decisions.

For tasks that rely on understanding or predicting outcomes for these subsets, synthetic data that is skewed towards the general statistics of the whole dataset may prove inadequate. This inadequacy can manifest in several ways:Predictive Modelling: Models trained on synthetic data that lacks subset-specific fidelity might show overall high accuracy but underperform significantly when predicting outcomes for the underrepresented groups [65, 68].Bias Amplification: If synthetic data does not accurately reflect the diversity within the original dataset, there is a risk of perpetuating or even amplifying biases present in the data, leading to unfair or unethical outcomes [69].Research and Policymaking: Decisions based on insights drawn from inadequately representing synthetic data might lead to scientific conclusions, and possibly policies that do not effectively address the needs or characteristics of all groups within the dataset [70].

These aspects are important in healthcare applications, where the precision and accuracy of data are paramount due to the direct impact on patient outcomes and treatment efficacy. The healthcare sector often deals with highly heterogeneous populations where specific subsets of patients may exhibit unique health characteristics or respond differently to treatments. The evaluation of synthetic data, therefore, gains an extra layer of importance, as failing to accurately model these subsets can lead to significant disparities in healthcare delivery and research outcomes. Practically, our results suggest that any deployment of synthetic data in oncology and similar domains should include an explicit subgroup-aware evaluation step, using the same clinically meaningful strata that would be used for real-world reporting and regulatory submissions, rather than relying solely on aggregate metrics. Compared to other recent methods of insights sharing, such as FML, this subgroup-aware evaluation step is possible since it can be achieved by the data provider before the delivery of the synthetic data.

For health-care systems, synthetic data are useful only if each clinically relevant sub-cohort is faithfully reproduced. This requires generation pipelines to go beyond “one-shot, one-size-fits-all” synthesis: at training time they must stratify the input so that minority cohorts (or variables of interest) appear with their true epidemiological weight and condition the model on subgroup markers or external knowledge bases. Recent work on ontology-guided and reinforcement-tuned generators shows that explicitly targeting rare-disease codes can close large fidelity gaps associated with generic GANs [71], and open-source simulators illustrate how hierarchical, statistics-driven stratification can deliver balanced synthetic sets [72]. Yet such cohort-tailored approaches imply that the data steward already knows which subpopulation matters and is willing to regenerate the dataset whenever a new question arises, a practice still at odds with the “generate once, use anywhere” marketing of most commercial platforms.

Nevertheless, no finite clinical decision or action shall be solely based on synthetic data but a later confirmation in real data must always be sought. There are some limitations that must be considered in our study. First, our evaluation palette, while broader than many prior studies, still covers only a tractable subset of utility metrics; high-order interactions, rare-event behavior and longitudinal dependencies remain largely untested because of computational and data-access constraints. Second, the cross-classification experiments rely on a finite panel of off-the-shelf algorithms; the findings are consistent across logistic regression, a polynomial-kernel SVM and GBc, but different model families, hyperparameter searches or resampling schemes could still change the observed results. Third, all results are based on a single trained instance per generator, consistent with the single-release setting in which synthetic healthcare data are realistically disseminated; synthetic-data generators can be sensitive to initialization and random seeds, and the complementary MIMIC-IV analysis (Supplementary Note S1) indicates that the subgroup-level failure is stable across independent runs, but this could not be verified on the registry data itself. Fourth, the empirical demonstration rests on a limited number of case studies and subgroup definitions; while a second, independent dataset (MIMIC-IV AKI) with its own subgroup is examined in the Supplementary Material, the broader generalizability of the subgroup-related findings remains to be established. Fifth, the synthetic-generation techniques assessed are representative of today’s state of the art but cannot yet guarantee full preservation of the high-dimensional correlation structure needed for hypothesis-free risk-factor discovery, so our findings may not generalize to future, more expressive generators. y Collectively, these limitations highlight the need for richer, resource-aware validation frameworks and for continual reevaluation as generation and attack. At the same time, by explicitly contrasting full-cohort and subgroup-level performance across three very different generators, our work provides a concrete template for such future validation studies and clarifies where existing metric panels are most likely to mislead.
