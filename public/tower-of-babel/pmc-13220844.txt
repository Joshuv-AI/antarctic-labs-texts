The impact of gut microbiota and metabolite-driven immune cell spatiotemporal dynamics on tumors
Authors: Jun-Bo Ding, Mei-Xi Lin, Dan Zang, Jun Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCancer poses a significant threat to human health. The immune response plays a pivotal role in tumor cell elimination, with increasing attention given to the spatiotemporal dynamics of immune cells across temporal, spatial, and functional dimensions. The gut microbiota, which constitutes the majority of the human microbiome, contributes to tumor initiation and progression. The tumor microenvironment (TME) serves as the primary site for immune cell-mediated anti-tumor responses, with the gut microbiota and its metabolites driving immune cell activation and spatiotemporal remodeling. This review explores the mechanisms by which the gut microbiota and its metabolites influence anti-tumor immunity, alongside the impact of immune cell dynamics on tumors. Furthermore, it proposes novel research strategies, including the “gut microbiota-immune cell dynamic monitoring model” and the “organoid-microbe co-culture platform.” This work lays a theoretical foundation for advancing our understanding of the underlying mechanisms and developing innovative therapeutic approaches.Keywords: Gut microbiota, metabolites, immune cells, tumors, tumor microenvironment

Introduction

Cancer has become one of the leading threats to human health, driven primarily by the abnormal proliferation and invasiveness of tumor cells. In 2024, new cancer cases in China reached 3,246,625, with lung cancer being the most prevalent malignancy. 1 The host immune response plays a critical role in tumor cell elimination, and recent advances in cancer research have increasingly focused on immunotherapy and the host immune system. However, despite the promising efficacy of targeted anti-PD-1/PD-L1 therapies, 2-8 a subset of patients remains unresponsive to immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) treatments in clinical practice. 9

The microbiota consists of a diverse array of microorganisms, including bacteria, fungi, viruses, archaea, and protists. These microbes colonize the human skin, gut, and respiratory tract, forming complex symbiotic relationships with the host, and are often referred to as the "second genome” of humans. 10 The gut microbiota, which comprises over 97% of the total human microbiota, plays a pivotal role in maintaining systemic homeostasis and regulating physiological functions. 11 , 12 Mounting evidence has established the gut microbiota’s involvement in metabolism, aging, inflammatory diseases, and immunity, 12-17 highlighting its significance in host health. Dysbiosis of the gut microbiota, as a core component of the human microecological system, is intricately linked to tumor initiation and progression, influencing the entire pathological course of cancer development.

Pathogenic bacteria can induce chronic inflammation, and their metabolites, such as secondary bile acids, promote abnormal proliferation and malignant transformation of intestinal epithelial cells. Additionally, a reduction in short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) compromises intestinal barrier integrity and immune regulatory functions. Conversely, beneficial bacteria (e.g., Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus) exert anti-carcinogenic effects by regulating microbial homeostasis, degrading carcinogenic substances, and enhancing anti-tumor immunity. 10 For instance, the enrichment of pathogenic bacteria such as Escherichia coli and Bacteroides fragilis (enterotoxin-producing strains) has been associated with chronic inflammation and the release of pro-inflammatory and carcinogenic mediators. 18 Additionally, beneficial bacteria like Bifidobacterium have been shown to improve chemotherapy outcomes by alleviating inflammation and boosting mucosal immunity in cancer patients. 19 Existing studies demonstrate that the gut microbiota contributes to tumor initiation and progression by driving the spatiotemporal dynamics and functional plasticity of immune cells, thereby exerting anti-tumor immune effects within the tumor microenvironment (TME). 20-28

Immune cells are essential in anti-tumor therapy. Notably, immune cell exhaustion, marked by the upregulation of immune checkpoint proteins (e.g., PD-1, CTLA-4, Tim-3, and Lag-3) on T cells, leads to T-cell dysfunction 29 and is a key mechanism driving the development and progression of malignant tumors. 30 , 31 Recent research has increasingly focused on the spatiotemporal dynamics of immune cells, considering their temporal, spatial, and functional characteristics. During ICI treatment, the clonal expansion of T cells is recognized as a hallmark of enhanced anti-tumor activity. With the advent of single-cell sequencing technologies, the distribution and cellular states of lymphocytes within the TME have been shown to correlate closely with cancer immune responses, exhibiting features of tumor-specific reactive T cells. 32 However, only a small fraction of tumor-infiltrating T cells are tumor-specific; the majority are “bystander T cells” that recognize non-tumor-related antigens, lacking activation or exhaustion markers, and thus fail to exert direct anti-tumor activity. 33 In addition, natural killer (NK) cells, central to innate immunity, play an irreplaceable role in non-specific anti-tumor immunity. Their functional activity is maintained in dynamic equilibrium through the regulation of multiple immune checkpoints on both the cell membrane and within the cytoplasm. The TME, as a complex and dynamic ecosystem, modulates NK cell functionality through factors such as hypoxia, metabolic reprogramming, and the secretion of immunosuppressive molecules, often inducing an exhausted phenotype. However, targeted interventions can restore their anti-tumor functions effectively. For instance, single-checkpoint blockade therapies, multi-checkpoint blockade therapies, and combination therapies targeting checkpoints such as NKG2A, TIGIT, and PD-1/PD-L1 have been developed and investigated, which hold great potential in reversing NK cell exhaustion and enhancing their anti-tumor efficacy. 34

This review focuses on the mechanisms by which the gut microbiota and its metabolites modulate the TME by regulating the spatiotemporal dynamics of immune cells, including their temporal changes, spatial distribution, and functional plasticity. Additionally, this review examines the positive effects of beneficial microbial taxa and their metabolites in enhancing anti-tumor immunity and improving the efficacy of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, while highlighting the detrimental impacts of pathogenic bacteria, gut microbiota dysbiosis, and antibiotic use, which can induce immunosuppression and hinder therapeutic outcomes. Furthermore, this review proposes innovative research strategies, including the “gut microbiota-immune cell dynamic monitoring model,” the “organoid-microbe co-culture platform,” and an artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted multi-omics data analysis approach. These strategies offer a theoretical and technical foundation for overcoming resistance to immunotherapy and facilitating precision interventions. Finally, this study addresses the challenges in understanding the molecular mechanisms of microbiota-mediated regulation and its clinical translation, identifying key priorities for future research into the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) and microbiota-targeted therapeutic strategies.

Dynamic changes of immune cells

Temporal dimension

Investigating the dynamic changes in immune cell parameters—such as cell number, surface molecule diversity, and molecular phenotype—at different time points 32 , 33 is essential for identifying optimal therapeutic windows for anti-tumor therapies and discovering potential biomarkers to predict treatment efficacy (Figure 1). Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients experience markedly different clinical outcomes when treated with ICIs at varying time points. 35 , 36 In the circulatory system, hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells (HSPCs), along with most mature leukocytes (except effector CD8+ T cells), peak during the resting phase (nighttime in humans, daytime in rodents) and decrease during the active phase. 37 In resectable NSCLC patients receiving neoadjuvant PD-1 inhibitor (nivolumab) therapy, clonal expansion of peripheral blood T cells within 2–4 weeks of treatment was significantly associated with pathological remission. 38 A previous study demonstrated that NSCLC patients with high T cell receptor diversity in PD-1+ CD8+ T cells in baseline peripheral blood exhibited a better response to PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, as well as longer progression-free survival (PFS) durations (6.4 months vs. 2.5 months; hazard ratio [HR] = 0.39; 95% confidence interval [CI] = 0.17–0.94; P = 0.021). 39 These findings suggest that early temporal dynamics of T cells may serve as a potential indicator for evaluating anti-tumor immune responses.

Temporal dynamics of immune cells. Immune cell populations in the human body exhibit circadian fluctuations, which can influence the effectiveness of conventional treatments for patients with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC).

Additionally, tumorigenesis and progression are closely linked to persistent chronic stimulation and inflammation. Inflammatory cells modulate the TME and immune cells, playing an essential role throughout tumor development. 40 During inflammation-driven tumor progression, naive T cells differentiate into effector T cells following antigen activation. After antigen clearance or resolution of inflammation, a subset of effector T cells can differentiate into memory T cells, which possess antigen-independent self-renewal capacity and serve as the core mediators of long-term anti-tumor immunity. However, under persistent chronic inflammation, T cells undergo progressive functional decline due to sustained antigen stimulation and inflammatory signaling, characterized by the loss of effector functions and high expression of inhibitory receptors (e.g., PD-1), ultimately entering an exhausted state. In addition, sustained antigen stimulation and elevated levels of inflammation disrupt the normal differentiation program of memory T cells, preventing the maintenance of a stable developmental state. 41 Chronic inflammation also significantly influences the developmental maturation and functional translation of NK cells. The development and maturation of NK cells are regulated by key molecules, including γc chain cytokines (e.g., IL-15), transcription factors (e.g., T-bet, Eomes), and the mTOR signaling pathway. The inflammatory microenvironment can alter NK cell maturation and functional transformation by modulating metabolic processes (e.g., mTOR inhibition) and mediating cytokine competition. 42 As immunotherapy progresses, exhausted CD8+ tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) expand over time, exhibiting a time-dependent exhaustion profile. Simultaneously, a large population of newly recruited tumor-specific T cells display fewer exhaustion characteristics and stronger effector functions, emerging as key mediators of anti-tumor immunotherapy. 43

Recent research has highlighted the gut microbiota and its metabolites as key regulators in modulating immune cell abundance and function, alleviating T-cell exhaustion, and shaping immune memory. One study demonstrated that mice transplanted with fecal samples from healthy individuals and supplemented with butyrate showed a significant increase in the proportions of dendritic cells (DCs), CD4+ T cells, and CD8+ T cells over a 52-week observation period (–2 to +52 weeks). 44 In contrast, a study on patients with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease-hepatocellular carcinoma (NAFLD-HCC) found that their microbiota induced the expansion of regulatory T cells (Tregs) producing interleukin-10 (IL-10+ Tregs) while inhibiting the expansion of cytotoxic CD8+ T cells compared to baseline levels. 45 Furthermore, indole-3-propionic acid (IPA), a tryptophan-derived metabolite produced synergistically by Lactobacillus johnsonii and Clostridium sporogenes, can modulate the stem cell program of CD8+ T cells by enhancing H3K27 acetylation at the Tcf7 super-enhancer region, promoting the generation of precursor exhausted CD8+ T cells. IPA helps maintain CD8+ T cell subset homeostasis, preventing excessive exhaustion. 46 Additionally, a study demonstrated that a fasting-mimicking diet (FMD) enriched Bifidobacterium pseudolongum in the gut. The functional metabolite L-arginine produced by this bacterium binds to the SLC7A1 receptor on CD8+ T cells, inducing the differentiation and infiltration of tissue-resident memory CD8+ T cells (TRM). This process preserves immune memory throughout the treatment cycle, ultimately suppressing the progression of colorectal cancer (CRC). 47 These findings highlight the close relationship between the dynamic changes of immune cells during circadian rhythms, chronic inflammatory processes, and immunotherapy cycles, and the regulatory effects of the gut microbiota and its metabolites. These microbial interactions play a pivotal role in mediating anti-tumor immune responses.

Spatial distribution

The spatial distribution of immune cells is essential for mediating anti-tumor immune responses (Figure 2). 30 , 31 , 48-51 As early as 2005, Schwab et al. proposed mechanisms for immune cell migration, demonstrating that T cells exit lymph nodes and enter the circulation through CCR7 and sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P) receptor signaling. 52 Subsequent studies showed that CXCL9/10-expressing DCs promote the infiltration of immune cells, such as CD8+ T cells, into tumors, enhancing anti-tumor activity compared to control groups. 50 Tregs are particularly enriched in perilymphatic regions of the tumor stroma, where they interact with mature regulatory DCs to create an immunosuppressive microenvironment. 51 Recent findings have further revealed that CD8+ T cells located within 20 μm of tumor cells (N20-CD8+ T cells) are strongly correlated with improved pathological complete response (pCR), disease-free survival (DFS), and overall survival (OS). The positive correlation between CXCL9 expression and N20-CD8+ T cell abundance suggests that CXCL9 plays a key role in recruiting CD8+ T cells to tumor cells. 53 Additionally, from a chronobiological perspective, BMAL1, a core transcription factor in circadian rhythms, has been shown to influence immune cell dynamics. BMAL1 deficiency results in the complete loss of circadian rhythms. 54 Lineage-specific deletion of BMAL1 in endothelial cells leads to circadian oscillations in the number and function of CD8+ T cells, impairing immune cell recruitment. 55 The circadian regulation of immune cell infiltration into tumors by BMAL1 profoundly impacts immunotherapy efficacy. Specifically, BMAL1 modulates the rhythmic expression of ICAM-1, influencing CD8+ T cell infiltration and activation. Furthermore, BMAL1 governs the circadian expression of PD-1, characterized by high expression in the morning (ZT1) and low expression in the evening (ZT13). This rhythmic regulation affects the therapeutic efficacy of CAR-T cell therapy and anti-PD-1 immune checkpoint blockade.

Spatial distribution of immune cells. The infiltration, migration, and recruitment of immune cells lead to inter-individual variability in their spatial distribution, which can serve as a predictive indicator of the efficacy of conventional tumor therapies. N20-CD8+ T cells: CD8+ T cells located within 20 μm of tumor cells.

Notably, the gut microbiota can activate intestinal immune cells, which then migrate to the lungs via the bloodstream, thereby indirectly enhancing pulmonary immune responses. 56 , 57 The gut microbiota modulates immune cell migration and distribution by regulating immune cell development and function, 58 potentially through effects on the expression and activity of homing receptors, such as CCR7. 52 , 59 Specifically, the gut microbiota and its metabolites can drive immune cell migration through distinct signal transduction pathways, while also promoting immune cell infiltration into tumors via epigenetic modifications. For instance, the gut microbe Akkermansia muciniphila (AKK) interacts with the intestinal mucosa to induce DCs to secrete interleukin-12 (IL-12), which upregulates the expression of chemokine receptors CCR9+ and CXCR3+ in mice. This leads to the recruitment of CD4+ central memory T cells from mesenteric and tumor-draining lymph nodes to the tumor site, thereby enhancing the enrichment of TILs and improving the efficacy of PD-1 checkpoint inhibitors. 60 However, the effect of AKK on the efficacy of ICIs is not linear and is tightly regulated within a specific range of relative abundance. When Akkermansia levels fall within the normal range of 0–4.799%, it enhances the response rate to ICIs and is associated with longer OS and PFS in patients. In contrast, when the relative abundance of Akkermansia exceeds 4.8% (high abundance) or is absent, it exacerbates intestinal dysbiosis, induces an immunosuppressive TME, and reduces the efficacy of anti-PD-1 inhibitors. This regulatory pattern follows a normal distribution, distinct from the linear relationship observed in most bacterial taxa, where higher abundance is consistently linked to greater therapeutic benefits. 61

Additionally, TREM2, a core biomarker of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), plays a pivotal role in the efficacy of immunotherapy. Blockade of TREM2, in combination with anti-PD-1 therapy, alleviates the inhibition of the macrophage TLR-MAPK pathway, inducing macrophage polarization toward a pro-inflammatory phenotype. This process is accompanied by a significant enrichment of the core gut microbe Ruminococcus gnavus and its metabolite dextran, which activates T cells through the TLR4-TNF signaling pathway and upregulates the expression of CXCL9 in TAM populations. This chemokine specifically binds to CXCR3, which is highly expressed on the surface of TNF-producing CD4+ T cells, thereby facilitating the precise directional migration of immune cells to the tumor site. 62 Indole-3-lactic acid (ILA), a metabolite derived from Lactobacillus plantarum L168 (LP-L168), exerts its effects through dual epigenetic modifications. In DCs, ILA enhances the binding of H3K27ac to the enhancer region of the IL12a gene, promoting IL-12 secretion. In CD8+ T cells, ILA recruits CTCF and increases H3K27me3 modification of the Saa3 gene, thereby suppressing its expression. These modifications facilitate the infiltration and migration of CD8+ T cells from lymphoid organs and the intestinal mucosa to tumor tissues, enhancing the anti-tumor immune response. 63 Butyrate increases chromatin accessibility and induces histone acetylation at H3K27ac and H3K9ac, activating the STAT4 signaling pathway. This significantly upregulates the expression of CXCL11 in tumor cells, further promoting NK cell infiltration into tumors via the CXCL11-CXCR3 axis. This process enhances NK cell cytotoxicity, as evidenced by upregulated expression of CD107a and IFN-γ, ultimately driving an anti-tumor immune response. 64

Functional plasticity

The functional plasticity of immune cells plays a critical role in determining their anti-tumor activity and is regulated by various factors (Figure 3). 48 , 58 , 65-67 Based on PD-1 expression levels, Thommen DS et al. classified CD8+ TILs in NSCLC patients into three categories: PD-1-negative CD8+ TILs (PD-1−), CD8+ TILs with PD-1 expression similar to healthy donors (PD-1N), and CD8+ TILs with PD-1 expression exceeding that of healthy donors (PD-1T). 68 They found that the PD-1T subset exhibited unique transcriptomic and metabolic profiles, highly expressed CXCL13 to recruit immune cells, and had the greatest tumor reactivity. Macrophage polarization also influences tumor progression. TAMs polarized toward the M2 phenotype suppress anti-tumor immunity and promote tumor progression through various mechanisms, including secretion of immunosuppressive molecules, interaction with myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), modulation of chemokine profiles, and upregulation of immune checkpoint proteins. 69

Functional plasticity of immune cells. Immune cell functionality is regulated by various factors (e.g., intratumoral microbiota, gut microbiota, and metabolites). Immune cells in distinct functional states contribute differently to anti-tumor responses.

The gut microbiota and its metabolites are key regulators of immune cell function. Gut microbiota-derived metabolites, such as SCFAs, cross the intestinal epithelial barrier via passive diffusion or active transport mediated by monocarboxylate transporter 1 (MCT1) and sodium-coupled monocarboxylate transporter 1 (SMCT1). 70 In contrast, bile acids are absorbed across the intestinal epithelium primarily through the apical sodium-dependent bile acid transporter (ASBT), subsequently entering the peripheral circulation and infiltrating the tumor microenvironment. 71 These metabolites modulate the functional phenotype and metabolic activity of immune cells, particularly CD8+ T cells and macrophages, through epigenetic modifications or cell-specific signaling pathways. SCFAs, for instance, play essential roles in numerous biological processes. 72 They contribute not only to maintaining normal physiological functions but also exert significant effects on the pathogenesis and progression of diseases, including immune disorders, inflammation, metabolic diseases, and cancer. 70 , 72-76 One study demonstrated that SCFAs have distinct effects on Tregs from different sources, leading to variations in their differentiation potential. 77 As natural histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitors, SCFAs markedly suppress the activity of class I and class II HDACs, induce hyperacetylation of histones on a genome-wide scale, thereby opening chromatin regions and reshaping the transcriptional programs of immune cells. Furthermore, SCFAs can be converted into acyl-CoA and act as acyl donors to mediate novel epigenetic modifications such as histone acetylation, propionylation, and butyrylation, directly regulating the expression of effector genes, metabolism-related genes, and immune checkpoint genes. 78 Macrophages treated with the microbial metabolite butyrate exhibit a downregulation of lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced pro-inflammatory mediators (including nitric oxide, IL-6, and IL-12) and undergo functional modulation through inhibition of HDACs. 79

In oncology, butyrate is widely recognized for its role in enhancing anti-tumor activity. 80-84 For example, Fusobacterium nucleatum, a bacterium found intratumorally, produces abundant butyrate, which inhibits HDACs in CD8+ T cells, induces H3K27 acetylation, and promotes expression of the Tbx21 promoter. This downregulates PD-1 expression, alleviates CD8+ T cell exhaustion, and enhances effector functions. 83 Besides non-specifically inhibiting histone deacetylases, short-chain fatty acids also regulate immune cell functions through cell-specific signaling pathways. Short-chain fatty acids, including butyrate and propionate, induce chromatin decondensation and DNA damage through the inhibition of HDACs, thereby activating the cytosolic DNA-sensing cGAS-STING pathway. This activation upregulates the expression of major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class I antigen presentation-related genes, such as NLRC5, Tap1/2, and Lmp2/7, as well as interferon-stimulated genes including CCL5 and CXCL10. Furthermore, SCFAs sustain and amplify immune activation through an IFN-γ-mediated positive feedback loop, which potently drives the activation of CD8⁺ T cells and elicits robust antitumor immune responses. 85 Butyrate can remodel the metabolic program of antigen-activated CD8⁺ T cells through both GPR41/GPR43 receptor-dependent and receptor-independent pathways. It relieves the dependence of glycolysis on the tricarboxylic acid cycle, enhances oxidative phosphorylation and fatty acid oxidation, and upregulates memory-associated transcriptional programs such as FoxO1, ultimately promoting the differentiation of CD8⁺ T cells into long-lived memory phenotypes. 86 In addition, secondary bile acids derived from the gut microbiota can directly bind to PMCA4 on the surface of T cells, enhance its ATPase activity, and accelerate the efflux of intracellular Ca2+. This subsequently blocks the Ca2+-NFAT2 signaling axis, inhibits NFAT2 nuclear translocation and the transcription of downstream effector genes (Ifng, Tnf, Gzmb), ultimately impairing the activation, proliferation, and antitumor cytotoxic function of CD8⁺ T cells. 48 Indole-3-carboxylic acid (ICA), a tryptophan metabolite, exerts its effects by regulating the IDO1/Kyn/AHR axis. On the one hand, it suppresses IDO1 expression in tumor cells and reduces kynurenine production. On the other hand, it competitively antagonizes the binding of Kyn to the aryl hydrocarbon receptor (AHR), thereby reducing tumor infiltration of Tregs and enhancing the antitumor function of CD8⁺ effector T cells. 87 Additionally, gut microbiota from the Bacteroides genus exhibits anti-tumor properties by inducing T helper 1 (TH1) immune responses and promoting the maturation of intratumoral DCs, significantly enhancing the anti-tumor efficacy of CTLA-4 antibodies. 88 Other microbial taxa, including Lactobacillus rhamnosus, Bifidobacterium, Firmicutes, and Actinobacteria, have also been shown to promote anti-tumor effects. 89 Specifically, Bifidobacterium enhances DC activation under homeostatic conditions and improves the effector functions of tumor-specific CD8+ T cells. 90

In conclusion, the spatiotemporal dynamics and functional plasticity of immune cells are pivotal to anti-tumor immunity. Temporally, immune cell dynamics provide a basis for identifying optimal therapeutic windows and predictive biomarkers for efficacy. The gut microbiota and its metabolites enhance anti-tumor activity by modulating immune cells (including NK cells, DCs, CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and their subsets). Spatially, immune cell distribution influences anti-tumor outcomes, with various factors (e.g., chemokines, endothelial cell-derived molecules, and gut microbiota) regulating immune cell migration and recruitment. Cross-organ migration of immune cells further supports the “gut-lung axis” theory. Functionally, the gut microbiota and its metabolites modulate macrophage and CD8+ T cell functions, with various microbial taxa enhancing anti-tumor responses through immune activation and cell maturation.

Immune cells in the tumor microenvironment: mediating anti-tumor immune responses

Despite the remarkable progress achieved with immune checkpoint therapies, particularly PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors, most patients still encounter the challenge of immune resistance. As a predictive biomarker for immunotherapy, PD-L1 expression provides some indication of treatment responsiveness. However, a previous clinical trial demonstrated that although the greatest relative benefit was observed in patients with a PD-L1 tumor proportion score (TPS) of 50% or higher, a subset of these patients still failed to respond to treatment. 4 This suggests that additional factors may influence the anti-tumor immune response. In recent years, the TME has gained significant attention from researchers. Comprising tumor cells, immune cells, extracellular matrix (ECM), blood vessels, and signaling molecules, the TME exhibits high heterogeneity and complexity, and its influence on immunotherapy is driven by various components. 91 The TIME has become a central focus of research. TILs are considered a hallmark of the TME and can serve as predictive markers for patient survival and immunotherapy response. 92 , 93 Furthermore, the density, composition, and functional status of tumor-infiltrating leukocytes are critical for prognostic assessment and treatment prediction. 94 Recent classifications of tumors based on immune cell infiltration have introduced the concepts of “hot” (highly infiltrated) and “cold” (non-infiltrated) tumors, with immune cell infiltration now recognized as dynamic and modifiable. 95

A study demonstrated that circNDUFB2 enhances the interaction between TRIM25 and IGF2BPs, forming a ternary complex (TRIM25/circNDUFB2/IGF2BPs) that promotes the ubiquitination and degradation of IGF2BPs. This activation of the RIG-I-MAVS signaling cascade recruits CD8+ T cells and DCs into the TME. 96 Additionally, the spatial distribution of immune cells within the TME—such as the central margin versus invasive margin—can significantly influence cancer prognosis and immunotherapy efficacy. 97 In lung cancer, the presence of immunosuppressive cells such as Tregs, MDSCs, and M2-polarized TAMs has been shown to promote tumor progression and impair prognosis. 92 , 93 , 98 NK cells, as core effectors of innate immunity, play an essential role in anti-tumor immune responses. NK cells recognize and eliminate tumor cells with low MHC-I expression through the balance between activating and inhibitory receptors, secrete cytokines like IFN-γ to regulate adaptive immunity, and generate NKm cells that provide long-term anti-tumor memory. However, in the TME, tumor cells, stromal cells, and metabolic disturbances such as hypoxia and nutrient deprivation contribute to NK cell dysfunction, leading to tumor immune escape. 99 The immunosuppressive TME hampers NK cell infiltration, disrupts immune synapse formation, and suppresses NK cell cytotoxicity through biophysical properties (e.g., increased ECM stiffness and reduced porosity) and ECM components. These factors represent major obstacles to NK cell-based therapies for solid tumors. 100

Researchers have recently proposed that targeting the TIME offers distinct advantages over traditional ICI therapies. 101 These advantages include the stimulation of neoantigen-specific T cell responses, induction of immunogenic cell death, the use of oncolytic viruses, cytokine therapy, anti-angiogenic treatments, and targeting cancer-associated fibroblasts and the ECM. Although the combination of TIME-targeted therapies with ICIs has not yet been widely implemented, this therapeutic strategy has garnered considerable attention in the research community. For instance, the combination of NKTR-214, a controlled interleukin-2 receptor agonist, with nivolumab demonstrated promising responses in patients with advanced solid tumors, including melanoma, renal cell carcinoma, and NSCLC. 102 Similarly, the combination of talimogene laherparepvec with ipilimumab showed higher responsiveness and prolonged survival in patients with advanced melanoma. 103 As a result, research into the TME, especially the TIME, is gaining increasing interest from investigators.

Gut microbiota-mediated remodeling of the tumor microenvironment.

Tumor microenvironment remodeling. Gut microbiota, microbial metabolites, prebiotics, postbiotics, and traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) extracts (including Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG (LGG), Bifidobacterium, butyrate, acetate, urolithin A (UA), Ganoderma lucidum polysaccharides (GLP), MS-20, and Quxie Capsule (QX)) can modulate tumor initiation and progression by influencing immune cell activity and cytokine signaling.

Positive regulatory axis

The diversity and abundance of “beneficial” gut microbiota, such as Ruminococcaceae and Faecalibacterium, enhance anti-tumor immune responses in patients by improving the function of effector T cells both peripherally and within the TME. 119 A combined therapy of Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG (LGG) and PD-1 antibodies increases the expression levels of IL-6, IFN-γ, CXCL9, and CXCL10, induces IFN-β production via the cGAS/STING/TBK1/IRF7 axis, and enhances intratumoral DC infiltration. These effects activate the cytotoxic function of CD8+ T cells and increase the number of tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells. 104

The combination of Bifidobacterium strains and oxaliplatin reduces Treg populations while enhancing the infiltration of CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, effector CD8+ T cells, and NK cells in lung cancer. It also induces the expression of IFN-γ and IL-2 while downregulating TNF-α and IL-10. 105 In this context, highly infiltrating TILs serve as positive drivers for reducing tumor burden. Furthermore, Lactobacillus casei BL23 exerts immunomodulatory effects by downregulating intratumoral IL-22 and upregulating the expression of caspase-7, caspase-9, and Bik genes in tumors, thereby protecting mice from CRC development. 106

In addition to microbial species, microbial metabolites also play a significant role in regulating the TME. Butyrate, a metabolite with HDAC inhibitory activity, promotes the intratumoral accumulation of CD8+ T cells and IFN-γ production by inducing ID2 expression. It also modulates CD8+ T cell function through IL-12 signaling, mediating anti-tumor responses. 107 Acetate, derived from Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron, enhances H3K27 acetylation at the ACC1 promoter region, thereby promoting M1 pro-inflammatory polarization of macrophages. This action further enhances the cytotoxic function of CD8+ T cells and regulates the expression of immune-related genes (CD86, NOS2, CD163, and ARG1) in mouse tumor tissues, influencing the immune microenvironment of HCC and tumor progression. 108 Urolithin A (UA) significantly increases the accumulation of primary CD8+ cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) in tumors induced by the EG7 cell line (ovalbumin-expressing lymphoma EL4) via the UA-ERK1/2-ULK1 axis, thereby enhancing their anti-tumor activity. 109

Building on the positive regulatory effects of the gut microbiota and its metabolites on the TME, oral administration of prebiotics, postbiotics, traditional Chinese medicine (TCM), and TCM extracts is emerging as a promising therapeutic strategy. Castalagin, a natural polyphenol, has been shown to modulate the gut microbiome composition and improve the CD8+/FOXP3+ CD4+ T cell ratio within the TME. 110 Ganoderma lucidum polysaccharides (GLP) can regulate gut dysbiosis and reshape the TME by increasing intratumoral cytotoxic CD8+ T cells and Th1 helper cells while reducing immunosuppressive Tregs, thereby enhancing the efficacy of anti-PD-1 immunotherapy. 111 As a postbiotic, MS-20, a fermented soybean milk product, promotes tumor infiltration by CD8+ T cells, significantly reduces intratumoral CD4+ PD1+ and CD8+ PD1+ T cells, and modulates anti-tumor immune responses by converting exhausted T cells in mice. 49 Additionally, Quxie Capsule (QX) downregulates pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α) while upregulating the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10. It reduces the proportion of MDSCs and increases CD8+ T cell infiltration, thereby inhibiting the occurrence and progression of colitis-associated colorectal cancer (CAC) and significantly altering the gut fungal microbiota in CAC mice. 112 Oral administration of ginseng polysaccharides (GPs) increases the expression of functional cytokines (IFN-γ and TNF-α) in CD8+ T cells, expands the cytotoxic CD8+ T cell subset, and reduces Treg populations, thereby enhancing immunotherapeutic efficacy. 23 Resveratrol (RSV) enhances anti-tumor immune responses by reducing intratumoral G-MDSC accumulation, impairing their ability to suppress CD8+ T cells, and promoting the differentiation of M-MDSCs into CD11c+ and F4/80+ cells, thereby inhibiting tumor development. 113

Moreover, narrow-spectrum antibiotics only target and eliminate specific pathogenic bacteria and exert milder disturbance on the commensal gut microbiota, resulting in relatively limited impacts on tumor therapeutic progression; they may even enhance the antitumor efficacy of immunotherapy. Clinical studies have confirmed that the risk of CRC is significantly elevated in individuals treated with broad-spectrum antibiotics, whereas no clear association has been observed between narrow-spectrum antibiotic use and CRC incidence. 120 As a narrow-spectrum antibiotic, colistin improves antitumor outcomes in male mice by downregulating testosterone levels, thereby enhancing the antitumor efficacy of anti-PD-L1 therapy. 121 Another study revealed that Fusobacterium nucleatum and its metabolite succinate are key drivers of resistance to anti-PD-1 immunotherapy in CRC. Succinate inhibits the cGAS-IFN-β pathway in tumor cells via the SUCNR1-HIF-1α-EZH2 axis, downregulates the expression of chemokines CCL5 and CXCL10, and consequently reduces the infiltration and cytotoxic function of CD8⁺ T cells in tumor tissues. In contrast, metronidazole effectively eradicates Fusobacterium nucleatum and reduces systemic succinate levels, thereby restoring the sensitivity of tumor cells to immunotherapy. This provides a precise target and intervention strategy for clinically reversing immunotherapy resistance in CRC. 122

In conclusion, the gut microbiota and its metabolites are pivotal in regulating tumor immune responses. “Beneficial” microbiota and their metabolites mediate the spatiotemporal dynamics and functional plasticity of various immune cell types via cytokine and chemokine signaling, thereby regulating the TME and enhancing anti-tumor activity, whereas specific antibiotics can restore tumor sensitivity to immunotherapy.

Negative regulatory axis

While “beneficial” gut microbiota demonstrates considerable potential for enhancing tumor immunotherapy, the impact of “harmful” gut microbiota and dysbiosis on cancer patients warrants equal attention. Lung cancer patients, compared to healthy individuals, exhibit reduced gut microbiota diversity, with increased abundance of specific taxa such as Enterococcus, Veillonella, Agathobacter, Megasphaera, and Coriobacteriaceae, which may contribute to tumor initiation and progression. 123 Furthermore, broad-spectrum antibiotics exhibit a wide antimicrobial spectrum and exert severe disruptive effects on gut microbial structure, representing the primary drug class responsible for conferring resistance to immunotherapy. In 2018, Routy B et al. first reported that antibiotic use disrupts the gut microbiota, leading to a marked reduction in the clinical efficacy of PD-1 inhibitor therapy in NSCLC patients. 60 Previous studies demonstrated that mice undergoing fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) had a higher percentage of activated CD8+ T cell subsets, such as CD56+CD8+ T cells, compared to those treated with broad-spectrum antibiotics. 49 , 83 , 124 Recent studies have shown that re-colonization of Intestinibacter following antibiotic treatment induces Treg17 cell migration to tumors by downregulating intestinal mucosal addressin cell adhesion molecule 1 (MAdCAM-1), thus worsening the TME and diminishing the efficacy of immunotherapy in a mouse lung cancer model. 114 Notably, the impact of antibiotics on immunotherapy efficacy is closely associated with the critical time window of exposure, potential dose-response relationship, and type of antimicrobial spectrum. A subsequent meta-analysis indicated that antibiotic use at the onset of ICI treatment significantly adversely affects patient PFS and OS; under similar conditions, antibiotic-exposed patients showed a reduction of 1.2 months in median PFS and 6.7 months in median OS. 125 Researchers have demonstrated that this effect depends on the time window of antibiotic exposure, with the period from 60 d before to 60 d after ICI initiation (–60 d, +60 d) representing the most sensitive window during which antibiotics compromise the efficacy of immunotherapy. This can be attributed to antibiotic-induced gut dysbiosis, which delays CD8+ T cell activation and misaligns it with the peak plasma concentration of PD-1 inhibitors. By contrast, antibiotic exposure occurring 90 d before ICI initiation exerts no significant impact on ICI efficacy. The antibiotic-immunotherapy exposure ratio (AIER) serves as a vital indicator for evaluating the dose response effect. Researchers observed that an AIER ≥ 4.2% during the whole immunotherapy period (WIOP) was associated with markedly reduced PFS and OS in patients. 126 These findings suggest that high dose, long course, or repeated administration of antibiotics persistently disrupts the commensal structure of the gut microbiota, thereby continuously suppressing antitumor immune responses and impairing the therapeutic efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors.

Due to tumor invasiveness, the gut microbiota can spread to tumors via hematogenous dissemination, 127 with the intratumoral microbiota playing a pivotal role in TME modification and tumor progression. However, the intratumoral microbiota does not exhibit a uniform distribution, but instead demonstrates significant heterogeneity, which profoundly impacts its regulatory effects on tumor development. This heterogeneity is reflected not only in structural differences across various tumor types, within the same tumor, and between tumor and adjacent normal tissues, but also in its functional diversity, which significantly influences tumorigenesis and progression. Structurally, microbiota colonization varies between tumor types: the dominant phyla in CRC are Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes; Ascomycota fungi are enriched in pancreatic cancer; and Stenotrophomonas maltophilia proliferates selectively in HCC tissues. These differences are closely linked to the oxygen partial pressure, nutritional metabolic characteristics, and vascular permeability of tumor tissues. Even within the same tumor, microbial composition varies significantly between the hypoxic core region and the peripheral vascular-rich area. 128 Functionally, this heterogeneity manifests in bidirectional effects—both tumor-promoting and tumor-suppressive. For example, Aspergillus sydowii in lung cancer secretes IL-1β via the β-glucan/Dectin-1/CARD9 pathway, recruiting and expanding MDSCs, infiltrating Tregs, and PD-1+ CD8+ T cells to create a locally immunosuppressive environment, thereby promoting tumor progression. 115 Similarly, lactic acid secreted by Escherichia coli within tumors mediates M2 macrophage polarization via the RIG-I-MAVS-NF-κB signaling pathway. This process inhibits Nlrp3 promoter transcription, regulates IL-2 and IFN-γ secretion, modulates mTOR activity, promotes PD-1 expression in Tregs, and impairs CD8+ T cell activation, ultimately facilitating CRC liver metastasis. 116 In contrast, Streptococcus enriched in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) upregulates the expression of chemokines such as CCL3, CCL4, CCL5, CXCL10, and CXCL11, which facilitates the infiltration of CD8+ effector memory T cells, reduces CD4+ Treg accumulation, and enhances sensitivity to anti-PD-1 therapy. 129

While most studies have emphasized the positive roles of SCFAs in anti-tumor therapy and elucidated their molecular mechanisms, some SCFAs have been shown to promote tumor progression. For instance, formate secreted by Fusobacterium nucleatum promotes Th17 cell expansion through AhR signaling, exacerbating CRC progression. 117 Acetate, one of the most abundant SCFAs in human NSCLC, reprograms tumor metabolism by upregulating and inducing acetylation of c-Myc. It also promotes lung cancer growth by increasing PD-L1 expression and inhibiting CD8+ T cell infiltration. 118

In summary, the core mechanism of gut microbiota-mediated anti-tumor immune responses lies in its ability to mediate immune cell activation and spatiotemporal remodeling, which in turn regulates TME remodeling. On one hand, specific microbiota can reverse the immunosuppressive state through metabolite-immune axes; on the other hand, pathogenic bacteria, dysbiosis, hematogenously disseminated intratumoral bacteria, and related metabolites can further exacerbate the immunosuppressive TME.

Challenges and perspectives

Despite significant progress in understanding the gut microbiota-immune axis, several challenges remain. First, while certain gut microbial taxa, such as Bifidobacterium and LGG, have been shown to improve the TME and regulate anti-tumor immune responses—by activating DCs or enhancing CD8+ T cell function 104 , 105 —the precise molecular signaling pathways underlying these effects are yet to be fully elucidated. For example, elevated expression of the Wnt/β‑catenin pathway in gastric cancer tissues is negatively correlated with CD8⁺ T cell infiltration, and blockade of this pathway enhances the sensitivity of gastric cancer cells to PD‑1 antibody therapy. 130 As a core oncogenic signaling pathway most frequently aberrantly activated in tumors, the PI3K/AKT/mTOR pathway suppresses the infiltration, proliferation, and cytotoxic activity of CD8⁺ T cells and NK cells. Meanwhile, it promotes the polarization of MDSCs and TAMs toward the M2 phenotype as well as the recruitment of regulatory T cells, ultimately generating an immune infiltration profile unfavorable for immune surveillance. 131 Furthermore, gut dysbiosis can activate multiple oncogenic signaling pathways including TLR‑MyD88‑NF‑κB, β‑catenin/Wnt, and STAT3. By disrupting intestinal barrier integrity, triggering chronic inflammation, downregulating antigen presentation, and upregulating PD‑L1 expression in tumor cells, these pathways collectively mediate immunosuppression and therapeutic resistance. 132 Second, broad-spectrum antibiotics may impair the efficacy of immunotherapy, while the elimination of specific pathogenic bacteria could potentially benefit patients. However, no conclusive studies have determined the optimal dosage or timing for antibiotic administration in this context. Finally, clinical translation remains a significant hurdle. Most current clinical trials are small-scale, resulting in high variability in outcomes. Additionally, practical challenges such as ethical concerns and patient compliance further complicate clinical implementation. Ongoing research is essential to address safety issues, such as autoimmunity or inflammation, induced by gut dysbiosis.

The comprehensive establishment of personalized models offers a promising strategy for overcoming the barriers to clinical translation. One highly promising direction for future research is the development of organoid-microorganism co-culture platforms. Jens Puschhof et al. outlined a series of protocols for constructing these models, including microorganism screening, microinjection techniques, the establishment of both 2D and 3D co-culture systems, and methods to assess co-culture efficacy. Researchers dissociated organoids into single cells and seeded them onto culture plates to form a confluent monolayer of cells. Microorganisms can then be directly administered to either the apical or basolateral compartments of this monolayer, establishing a 2D co-culture system. In contrast, the 3D co-culture system uses cystic organoids as culture carriers. Microorganisms are precisely delivered into the organoid lumen via microinjection, effectively recapitulating intestinal epithelial polarity and the native luminal microenvironment. This model supports the viability of anaerobic bacteria, prevents excessive bacterial overgrowth, and maintains high physiological relevance, making it a powerful tool for future studies of the gut microbiome. 133 Recent studies utilizing the organoid model’s precise simulation capabilities have shown that tumor-resident Staphylococcus can drive lung cancer metastasis by activating host pseudohypoxic signaling pathways. 134 However, human intestinal organoids (HIOs) derived from conventional pluripotent stem cells lack immune components, limiting their ability to fully replicate the complexity of human intestinal biology. To address this, Carine Bouffi et al. transplanted HIOs differentiated from pluripotent stem cells under the renal capsule of immune-humanized NSGS mice. At 12, 16, and 20 weeks post-transplantation, mass cytometry and immunostaining techniques demonstrated that human immune cells migrated into the mucosal layer of HIOs, forming lymphoid follicles that resembled gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT). Additionally, microbial exposure led to an increased number of microfold cells (M cells) in the HIO epithelium, which, in turn, activated immune cells through the secretion of IgA antibodies. 135 This model not only preserves the structural and functional characteristics of human intestinal tissue but also provides precise control over microbial exposure, offering valuable insights for future research.

The gut microbiota plays a pivotal role in the dynamic regulation of human immune cells. 136 Therefore, developing dynamic monitoring models to study the gut microbiota-immune cell crosstalk not only enables in-depth exploration of the gut microbiota-immune axis but also aids in determining the optimal timing for microbial interventions. A notable advancement in this area is the establishment of a 3D collagen hydrogel intestinal model (InTRIC), which incorporates tissue-resident immune cells (THP-1 macrophages). This model features an innovative real-time dynamic monitoring module that allows precise tracking of the migratory and invasive trajectories of macrophages across the epithelial layer when stimulated with commensal bacteria (Lactobacillus rhamnosus) and pathogenic bacteria (Pseudomonas aeruginosa), while concurrently monitoring the secretion profiles of key inflammatory cytokines, such as Interleukin-8 (IL-8). Compared to conventional static models, InTRIC more accurately reflects the dynamic regulatory characteristics of the intestinal microenvironment in vivo, providing a robust tool for investigating the molecular mechanisms underlying the regulation of immune cell functions by the gut microbiota. 137 Additionally, Silvia Galván-Peña and colleagues used photoconvertible Kaede mice in combination with single-cell genomics to create dynamic migration atlases of colonic immune cells under baseline and inflammatory conditions. Their work uncovered immune cell migration pathways influenced by the gut microbiota, offering novel methodologies for tracking cell migration and laying the groundwork for the development of in vivo dynamic monitoring models. 59

Pharmacological research on the clinical translation of gut microbiota and its derivatives into targeted or adjuvant therapeutics remains to be systematically deepened. Exploring the potential of microbiota as a core resource for small‑molecule drug discovery, defining druggable translation pathways, and evaluating their practical application value in overcoming immunotherapy resistance represent key directions urgently requiring breakthroughs in this field. Bioactive small molecules such as short‑chain fatty acids, bile acid derivatives, inosine, and tryptophan metabolites exhibit high host compatibility, low off target effects, and mechanisms of action closely linked to the tumor immune microenvironment, making them “ideal candidates” for small molecule drug development. 138 These bioactive small molecules modulate immune cells and the tumor microenvironment, thereby influencing the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy and the occurrence of immune‑related adverse events. 139 Preclinical and early clinical studies have confirmed that exogenous supplementation with bioactive small‑molecule microbial metabolites such as inosine, urolithin A, and TMAO, or elevation of endogenous metabolite levels via prebiotic or dietary interventions, can restore immune cell infiltration, reverse the immunosuppressive microenvironment, and enhance CD8⁺ T cell activity, thus significantly improving immunotherapy response rates and patient PFS. 140 Furthermore, Lactobacillus reuteri and its tryptophan metabolite indole‑3‑carboxaldehyde can enhance the proliferation and cytotoxic function of cytotoxic CD8⁺ T cells by activating the AhR‑CREB pathway in CD8⁺ T cells, markedly boosting the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors and reversing tumor unresponsiveness to ICIs. 141 Therefore, small molecule development strategies centered on microbial metabolites possess clear clinical value in overcoming immunotherapy resistance, establishing personalized combination regimens, and expanding the eligible population for immunotherapy, and are expected to become an important direction for next generation combination tumor immunotherapy.

Moreover, the integration of AI algorithms and machine learning models with multi-omics gut microbiota data further enhances the comprehensiveness of research strategies. This integration facilitates data mining, correlation analysis, and functional prediction, 142 , 143 supporting the creation of intelligent systems for dynamic monitoring models. AI algorithms play a critical role in database construction and application. They integrate statistical correction logic and unsupervised learning techniques, such as Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP), to standardize heterogeneous data from different sequencing platforms. Additionally, AI-driven multi-omics integration strategies, including early-stage concatenation and late-stage model fusion, along with supervised learning models like Random Forest and XGBoost, enable the identification of cross-omics feature clusters and biomarker discovery. Federated learning further supports data integration from multiple institutions without direct data sharing, while time series models help analyze longitudinal multi-omics data to capture temporal microbial community patterns. By incorporating new clinical cohort data, federated learning can optimize diagnostic, predictive models, and databases. 144

Conclusion

Cancer remains one of the primary threats to human health in the modern era. Immunotherapy, particularly ICIs, has made remarkable strides in cancer treatment by harnessing the body’s anti-tumor immune response. However, not all patients benefit from immunotherapy, and some experience immune-related adverse events (irAEs). As a result, identifying reliable biomarkers to predict immune responses has become a key research focus.

Accumulating evidence highlights the role of the gut microbiota in modulating the TME by influencing the spatiotemporal dynamics and functional plasticity of immune cells, thereby regulating the host immune system and anti-tumor responses. Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of the crosstalk between the gut microbiota and the immune system is critical for advancing our knowledge of the entire anti-tumor process. This review systematically explores the relationship between anti-tumor immune responses and the spatiotemporal dynamics of immune cells from three dimensions. It also examines the gut microbiota-mediated dynamic changes in host immune cells, including proliferation, migration, recruitment, and functional transformation, while analyzing the effects of TME remodeling on tumor progression from multiple perspectives. However, the functional effects of one or more gut microbial taxa and their metabolites exhibit striking interhost heterogeneity. A case in point is Akkermansia muciniphila as discussed in this review, whose anti-tumor immune modulation is tightly associated with its relative abundance and features a dual regulatory role. From the optimization of tumor therapeutic strategies to the implementation of personalized precision interventions, the gut microbiota holds immense translational potential in oncology. Nevertheless, its clinical translation is plagued by multiple bottlenecks, which stands as one of the critical challenges demanding urgent breakthroughs at present. The comprehensive construction of personalized predictive and interventional models, coupled with the application of artificial intelligence and machine learning algorithms to the in-depth integration and analysis of multi-omics data of the gut microbiota, will become the core direction of our future research.

In conclusion, the future holds promising potential for precisely modulating the host’s anti-tumor immune response through the gut microbiota, thus enhancing therapeutic efficacy while minimizing adverse effects.
