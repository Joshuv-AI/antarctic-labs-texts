Do Theory of Mind Benchmarks Need Explicit Human-like Reasoning in Language Models?
Yi-Long Lu, Chunhui Zhang, Jiajun Song, Lifeng Fan, Wei Wang

Abstract
Theory of Mind (ToM), the ability to attribute mental states to others, is fundamental for human social intelligence and a critical capability for advanced Artificial Intelligence. Recent advancements in Large Language Models (LLMs) have shown promising performance on ToM benchmarks, raising the question: Do these benchmarks necessitate explicit human-like reasoning processes, or can models succeed through alternative strategies? We investigate this question empirically by applying Reinforcement Learning (RL) and Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) to LLMs of varying scales (0.5B to 7B parameters) and evaluating them across multiple ToM datasets. Our results reveal a scale-dependent impact of RL: while RL significantly improves accuracy and fosters high-quality, interpretable, and transferable belief-tracking reasoning in larger models (7B), it leads to "reasoning collapse" in smaller models ( ≤ \leq 3B), where high accuracy and generalization ability are achieved via drastically shortened, less meaningful responses. Surprisingly, further SFT achieves competitive and generalizable performance across these benchmarks, often matching or exceeding RL models in accuracy, despite not being explicitly trained to produce structured reasoning traces. These findings highlight a critical discrepancy between benchmark accuracy and the nature of learned reasoning. Our work suggests that current ToM benchmarks may be solvable without requiring the explicit, human-like simulation of mental states they were designed to probe. LLMs, particularly when scale is limited or training signals focus solely on output correctness, may leverage alternative rules effective for benchmark data structures. Background and Related Work

2.1
Theory of Mind in Psychology

tom
is a core concept in psychology referring to the capacity to understand and attribute mental states—such as beliefs, desires, intentions, and knowledge—to oneself and others. This ability is crucial for predicting behavior, navigating social interactions, and understanding communication. A classic method for assessing
tom
, particularly in developmental psychology, is the false-belief task. These tasks probe whether an individual can understand that another person may hold a belief that is contrary to reality, and predict their actions based on that false belief.

Consider a classic
tom
scenario:
Sally places her marble in a basket and leaves; Anne moves it to a box. Where will Sally search for her marble when she returns?
The correct answer depends not on physical laws, but on understanding Sally’s false belief. This kind of ability is critical to human social interactions
[
9
,
20
]
. The complexity of these tasks can be increased by nesting beliefs, moving from first-order (e.g., "Where does Anne think the marble is?") to higher-orders (e.g., "Where does Anne think Sally thinks the marble is?").

2.2
ToM in Artificial Intelligence

Early AI approaches to ToM involved neural networks designed to track agents’ beliefs in controlled environments
[
6
,
13
,
10
]
. With the advent of large language models (LLMs), research has shifted to exploring whether these models can inherently perform ToM-like reasoning. Recent studies have systematically examined LLMs’ performance on various ToM tasks, including false-belief scenarios
[
9
,
20
,
21
,
18
]
. Benchmarks such as Hi-ToM
[
8
]
, Explore-ToM
[
16
]
, and ToMi
[
10
]
have been developed to assess LLMs on tasks ranging from simple first-order beliefs to complex higher-order reasoning. While large models like GPT-4 have shown proficiency in standard false-belief tasks
[
9
]
, performance tends to degrade significantly on higher-order problems
[
8
]
. Recent efforts have also focused on building larger ToM datasets and fine-tuning smaller LLMs to improve social cognition reasoning in more efficient models
[
16
]
.

2.3
Rule-based reinforcement learning in LLMs

Rule-based reinforcement learning (RL) has proven effective in enhancing large language models (LLMs) beyond standard supervised fine-tuning. This approach uses structured reward signals to guide model behavior without requiring explicit step-by-step supervision. Notably, it enables the emergence of reasoning-like behavior through relatively simple feedback mechanisms. Recent work has shown that such rewards can encourage models to internalize structured thinking. For instance, DeepSeek-R1
[
7
]
demonstrated that answer-level rewards led to gradual increases in both response length and accuracy, suggesting the emergence of reasoning dynamics. Logic-RL
[
24
]
applied format-constrained rewards to synthetic logic puzzles, training a 7B model that learned to reflect and verify its answers—skills that transferred to real-world math benchmarks such as AIME. Similarly, SWE-RL
[
22
]
applied similar principles to software engineering, achieving state-of-the-art performance on coding tasks and exhibiting cross-domain generalization. These successes highlight RL’s potential to activate latent reasoning skills through reward design. However, prior successes have largely occurred in formal domains, where rules and ground truths are well-defined. It remains an open question whether such methods generalize to social reasoning tasks, which require interpreting mental states and hidden commonsense. The extent to which rule-based RL can elicit human-like mental state inference in LLMs remains underexplored.

Experimental Methods

3.1
Theory of Mind Benchmarks

To assess whether RL enables LLMs to acquire explicit mind reasoning, we curated a diverse set of
tom
benchmarks. Guided by prior work
[
24
]
, we prioritized datasets with clean structure, well-defined and verifiable answers, and non-trivial reasoning requirements. These benchmarks span varying levels of difficulty and include diverse question formats and narrative styles. This design allows for controlled analysis across model sizes and training methods, isolating the impact of reinforcement learning on belief modeling.

3.1.1
Training data

We generated an initial Hi-ToM-style dataset of 3,000 samples using the data generation script from
He et al. 2023
. This dataset was evenly distributed across ToM orders: 0 (factual questions), 1, 2, 3, and 4, with 600 samples per level. To assess out-of-distribution (OOD) performance,
all 600 fourth-order samples were reserved for testing
. From the remaining 2,400 samples, we randomly selected 2,000 for training and 400 for validation.

To further enhance the diversity of the training set, we incorporated 1,200 samples from ExploreToM’s dataset
[
16
]
, covering a range of story types. We excluded all binary-response questions (e.g., "Yes" or "No") from training, as they were highly biased in the ExploreToM dataset
1
1

1

Our preliminary results indicated that such biases significantly impaired the model’s ability to learn correct reasoning patterns.
. As a result, the final training dataset comprised 3,200 samples across 900 unique stories. During training, we relied solely on the structured story formats provided by ExploreToM.

3.1.2
Evaluation data

For evaluation, we used multiple datasets to assess both in-distribution performance and generalization capabilities. In addition to the 600 4th-order OOD ToM samples generated by the script, we also incorporated the original Hi-ToM dataset
[
8
]
, comprising 600 examples across ToM orders 0 to 4. We also evaluated model performance on the ToMi dataset
[
10
]
, which covers unexpected transfers as well as first- and second-order false belief tasks. To further examine generalization, we tested on the ExploreToM benchmark
[
16
]
, using 1,066 questions with raw story structures. We further tested robustness to surface variation using an infilled version, where stories were enriched with contextual details and rewritten into natural narratives. (Appendix Table
2
).

The final evaluation has 5 different datasets: the original Hi-ToM, 4th-order ToM (OOD), ToMi dataset, 1066 ExploreToM dataset with raw story structures, and infilled story version.

Rule-based reinforcement learning

Format reward

Answer reward

To check and reinforce the correct answer, we used a regular expression-based approach to evaluate answer accuracy. This method accounts for variations in response style, as the standard answers in the dataset are typically single words or phrases, whereas model-generated responses often take the form of complete sentences.

R_{\text{answer}}=\begin{cases}2,&\text{if the format is correct and the answer matches the ground truth}\\
-2,&\text{if the format is incorrect or the answer cannot match the ground truth}\end{cases}

We summed

RL algorithm

We adopt GRPO, the reinforcement learning baseline algorithm proposed in DeepSeekMath
[
17
]
, as our RL foundation. GRPO has already demonstrated strong potential in DeepSeek-R1
[
7
]
and has recently been shown to be stable and reproducible across a series of follow-up works
[
12
,
22
]
. To reduce the overhead of training an additional value function as in PPO
[
15
]

The training process was conducted using the veRL framework
[
19
]
. We set the training batch size to 8 and the number of rollout generations to 16. A consistent learning rate of 5e-7 and a temperature parameter of 1 were used to encourage greater model exploration. The model was trained for 2 epochs on 3,200 samples, resulting in a total of 800 optimization steps. All experiments were performed on 4 A100 (80GB) GPUs.

3.3.2
Supervised Fine-Tuning

SFT adapts pre-trained LLMs to specific tasks via input-output training. It provides a baseline for evaluating whether complex methods like RL offer real gains. On ToM tasks, the comparisons between SFT and RL help assess if models rely on true reasoning or surface-level patterns. We fine-tuned Qwen models using the veRL framework with full-parameter updates on ground truth answers. Training used a batch size of 256 and AdamW (lr=1e-5) on the same 3,200 samples.

3.4
Evaluation metrics

All model outputs are scored via regex-based answer matching to compute the accuracy. Each input follows a standard format:

Story: {story}\n Question: {question}

Baseline models use Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting with thinking in JSON responses (See Appendix
B.1
). RL models use DeepSeek-R1-style prompts (Appendix
B.1
). The SFT model is evaluated with default prompting.

Thinking quality

To assess the reasoning quality for RL-trained models, we use GPT-4.1 to rate model-generated reasoning on 600 fourth-order ToM questions, which are considered challenging even for humans. Two criteria are scored on a 0–10 scale:
logical coherence
(is the reasoning internally consistent?) and
factual alignment
(does it reflect the story’s content accurately?). The final thinking quality score is the average of these two ratings, normalized to the [0, 1] range. Prompt details are in the Appendix
B.2
.

Knowledge transfer performance

To further examine the quality of model-generated reasoning, we use the thinking content produced by the 7B-1M and 3B models on fourth-order ToM questions as input to GPT-4o-mini, prompted with CoT. We evaluate GPT-4o-mini’s answer accuracy using both pre- and post-RL thinking traces. Since the final sentence often summarizes the reasoning and contains the answer, we also removed this conclusion before passing the content, ensuring that GPT-4o-mini relies only on the reasoning process itself.

SFT on ToM Benchmarks

To isolate the effects of reinforcement learning, we compared SFT-only models with their RL-finetuned counterparts across multiple ToM benchmarks. Table

Surprisingly, SFT alone achieved near-identical performance to RL in several cases. For the 7B-1M model, SFT even outperformed RL slightly on Hi-ToM (86.5% vs 84.5%). 5
Discussion

Our empirical investigation reveals a complex interplay between model scale, training methodology, and the acquisition of Theory of Mind capabilities in Large Language Models. The findings highlight both the potential of reinforcement learning and the limitations of current evaluation paradigms for social reasoning tasks.

RL’s success in inducing explicit reasoning in larger models

We demonstrated that RL significantly boosts performance on a range of
tom
benchmarks across all tested Qwen models. Larger models (7B-1M) trained with RL not only achieved state-of-the-art accuracy on complex 4th-order
tom
tasks, surpassing significantly larger models like GPT-4o and DeepSeek-v3 on certain benchmarks, but also developed some interpretable and transferable reasoning processes. Our analysis using GPT-4.1 for thinking quality assessment and the knowledge transfer experiment with GPT-4o-mini provides converging evidence that RL can induce more structured belief tracking in models with sufficient capacity. This aligns with prior work showing RL’s ability to foster emergent reasoning in domains like mathematics and coding
[
7
,
24
,
22
]
, suggesting that sophisticated reasoning skills can be reinforced through task-specific rewards.

Reasoning collapse: the pitfall of RL in smaller models

The beneficial effect of RL on reasoning quality was not uniform across all model sizes. The reasoning collapse emerged in smaller models (
\leq
3B). Despite achieving substantial accuracy gains comparable to the larger models on many benchmarks after RL training, these models failed to generate interpretable, structured reasoning traces. Instead, they appeared to rely on shorter, potentially memorized patterns or rules optimized directly for the final answer, rather than explicitly tracking agents’ mental states. This phenomenon underscores a crucial mismatch between achieving high accuracy on benchmark questions and possessing genuine, human-like reasoning capabilities. The simple rule-based rewards, while effective at optimizing for correctness, may inadvertently encourage shortcut learning in models with limited capacity relative to the complexity of the underlying task logic.

Effectiveness of SFT and implications for benchmark validity

The surprisingly strong performance of models trained solely with SFT further supports the notion that existing
tom
benchmarks, such as Hi-ToM and ExploreToM, while valuable, may not be sufficiently challenging to exclusively probe deep mental state inference. SFT models achieved accuracy comparable to or even slightly better than their RL-trained counterparts on several datasets, including those designed to test generalization (4th-order ToM, infilled stories). One possible explanation is that the datasets might contain exploitable patterns, such as surface-level correlations between narrative elements and answers, possibly introduced by templated generation. For example, in ExploreToM
[
16
]
, 22% of questions have “yes” as the correct answer, while only 4% are “no,” introducing a strong prior. Additionally, general pretraining might equip models with reasoning skills that can be activated by SFT. The generalization seen with SFT also implies that simply increasing the complexity or naturalism of the stories (as in infilled ExploreToM) might not be enough to overcome potential implicit strategies if the underlying logical structure remains predictable.

Our findings have important implications for the development and evaluation of social intelligence in AI. First, achieving high accuracy on current
tom
benchmarks should not be taken as definitive proof of human-level understanding of mental states. Evaluation methods must evolve to directly assess the quality and robustness of the reasoning process, perhaps through counterfactual questioning, requiring explanations for belief states, or using adversarial generation techniques specifically targeting reasoning chains rather than just final answers
[
18
,
21
]
. Second, training methodologies, including RL, need to be carefully designed and potentially adapted based on model scale. For smaller models, simpler rewards focused solely on output correctness might inadvertently lead to non-interpretable, brittle solutions. Future work could explore curriculum learning, auxiliary losses that encourage intermediate state tracking, or reward functions derived from desired reasoning properties (e.g., consistency of belief attribution).

5.1
Limitations

Our study has several limitations. In our experiments, we did not evaluate recent reasoning models such as o3 and DeepSeek-R1. However, our goal was not to achieve state-of-the-art performance but to assess whether current ToM datasets can elicit explicit mind reasoning via RL. The evaluation of thinking quality relies on GPT-4.1, which is itself an LLM, and its judgment may not perfectly capture human assessment of reasoning or be free from its own biases. The knowledge transfer experiment using GPT-4o-mini similarly depends on the capabilities of another LLM. What exactly is being transferred needs further investigation. While these methods offer valuable proxies for reasoning structure, human evaluation would provide a stronger gold standard. We focused on a specific family of models (Qwen 2.5) and a particular RL setup (GRPO with simple rewards); results may vary with different architectures, model families, or RL algorithms and reward designs. Furthermore, our training data was generated from synthetic sources; evaluating performance on more naturalistic or human-annotated
tom
scenarios would be a valuable direction for future work.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we investigated the effectiveness of reinforcement learning for enhancing Large Language Models’ ability to reason about mental states, a core aspect of Theory of Mind. We trained Qwen 2.5 models of varying sizes using rule-based RL on synthetic
tom
datasets and evaluated their performance and reasoning processes across multiple benchmarks.

Our results demonstrate that while RL effectively improves accuracy on
tom
tasks across model scales, its impact on the quality of reasoning is scale-dependent. In larger models, RL fostered structured, interpretable belief tracking that generalized to higher-order reasoning and transferred knowledge effectively to another model. In contrast, smaller models (
\leq
3B) exhibited reasoning collapse, achieving high accuracy via drastically shortened, less interpretable outputs. Furthermore, we found that simple
sft
(
sft
) alone yields surprisingly competitive and generalizable performance on current benchmarks.

These findings underscore a critical challenge in the pursuit of artificial social intelligence: achieving high performance metrics on existing benchmarks does not guarantee robust, interpretable reasoning, particularly in smaller models. The discrepancy between
tom
accuracy and mind reasoning quality revealed by our analysis suggests that current evaluation methods may be insufficient to probe the underlying cognitive processes models employ.

Moving forward, it is crucial to:

•

Develop training methodologies, potentially involving more sophisticated reward designs or architectural innovations, that encourage the learning of interpretable
tom
reasoning.

•

Create
tom
benchmarks that go beyond measuring final answer correctness to explicitly evaluate the quality, consistency, and interpretability of models’ belief attribution and reasoning processes.

By focusing on both performance and the underlying mechanisms, we can pave the way towards building LLMs that not only perform well on social tasks but also genuinely understand and reason about the minds of others, fostering more natural and trustworthy human-AI interaction.

Appendix A
Datasets Description

Hi-ToM dataset

The Hi-ToM dataset
[
8
]
is specifically designed to systematically evaluate LLMs’
tom
reasoning across varying orders of complexity. It comprises multi-agent stories set within an object-finding game, where five agents interact in one or more rooms containing objects and containers. The dataset is automatically generated using scripts. The synthetic generation process involves fitting randomly chosen story components (rooms, objects, containers, agents) into pre-defined chapter templates, which can include multiple actions like object movement and agent communication. Each story is coupled with five progressively higher-order questions, as exemplified by: "Where is O really?" (0th-order), "Where does A1 think O is?" (1st-order), and extending up to 4th-order nested beliefs.

Explore-ToM dataset

This dataset
[
16
]
employs an A*-guided adversarial generation process to create complex stories that challenge models’
tom
reasoning. Instead of sampling different elements independently, this dataset uses LLM to sample plausible story contexts (characters, locations, objects, etc.). Subsequently, an A* search algorithm explores sequences of actions defined in a domain-specific language, such as object movements, to construct story structures that are particularly challenging for target LLMs. Each story is paired with questions probing first-order, second-order, and state-tracking abilities. These structured stories are optionally rewritten with language models to produce naturalistic narratives. The whole dataset consists of 13309 questions with 619 unique raw stories.

ToMi dataset

The ToMi dataset
[
10
]
is a ToM benchmark generated by scripts. The current ToMi dataset consists of 5994 questions covering unexpected transfers as well as first- and second-order false belief tasks.

System prompt for RL training

You are a helpful assistant. The assistant first thinks about the reasoning process in the mind and then provides the user with the answer. The reasoning process and answer are enclosed within <think> </think> and <answer> </answer> tags, respectively, i.e., <think> reasoning process here </think><answer> answer here </answer>. Now the user asks you to solve a
tom
reasoning problem. After thinking, when you finally reach a conclusion, clearly state your answer within <answer> </answer> tags.\nNote: You should assume the following.\n(1) An agent witnesses everything and every movement before exiting a room.\n(2) An agent A can infer another agent B’s mental state only if A and B have been in the same room, or have private or public interactions.

CoT system prompt for baseline evaluation

Read the following story and answer the question. Think step-by-step. Provide the thinking first, and then the answer. Answer in the following JSON format:

{

"thinking": "step by step thinking",

"answer": "answer text"

}

Prompts for knowledge transfer

For knowledge transfer experiment, we used the CoT system prompt and simply added the models’ RL thinking content to user message: “
Story:
{
story
}
\n Question:
{
question
} \n <think>{thinking}</think>”.
For baseline models, the reasoning content is extracted from the "thinking" field in the JSON response. For RL models, it corresponds to the text enclosed within the <think>…</think> tags. We evaluated both the original reasoning content and a version that removed the final summarizing statement.

Assumptions in prompts

In the original HI-TOM
[
8
]
dataset, they attached the following assumption list to each story:

Note: You should assume the following.
(1) An agent witnesses everything and every movement before exiting a room.
(2) An agent A can infer another agent B’s mental state only if A and B have
been in the same room, or have private or public interactions.
(3) Note that every agent tends to lie. What a character tells others doesn’t
affect their actual belief. An agent tends to trust an agent that exited the
room later than themselves. The exit order is known to all agents.
(4) Agents in private communications know that others won’t hear them, but
they know that anyone can hear any public claims.

We only kept the first 2 assumptions
for RL training. When evaluating the baseline models, we found that these assumptions did not improve performance, and in some cases even degraded it. Therefore, we excluded them during evaluation.
