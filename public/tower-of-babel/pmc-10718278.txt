Rare median and musculocutaneous nerve fusion with intraoperative electrical confirmation: illustrative case
Authors: Audrey Huang, Sima Vazquez, Jose Dominguez, Avinash Mohan, Jin Li, Jared M Pisapia
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDNerve transfer is a surgical technique in which a redundant or expendable fascicle is transferred or coapted to an injured nerve distal to the site of injury for the purpose of reinnervation. Successful nerve transfer is dependent on correct intraoperative identification of donor and recipient nerves.OBSERVATIONSAn 8-year-old male was recommended for ulnar nerve fascicle to biceps branch of musculocutaneous nerve transfer to restore elbow flexion weakness after a demyelinating spinal cord injury. The biceps branch was identified approximately midway along the upper arm. Proximal musculocutaneous nerve stimulation induced hand movement and electromyography activity in the median nerve muscles. Neurolysis of the thickened proximal structure revealed fusion of the musculocutaneous and median nerves. Because of the proximity of the median and musculocutaneous nerves, median rather than ulnar nerve fascicles were used as donors for transfer.LESSONSThe authors provide the first reported intraoperative finding of an anatomical variant in which the musculocutaneous nerve and median nerve were fused in the upper arm, confirmed through intraoperative electrical stimulation. Surgeons should be aware of this rare anatomical variant to ensure correct nerve identification when performing nerve transfers in the proximal upper extremity.Keywords: musculocutaneous nerve, median nerve, anatomic variant, nerve fusion, nerve transferABBREVIATIONS: EMG = electromyography, MCN = musculocutaneous nerve, MN = median nerve, MRI = magnetic resonance imaging

Nerve transfers have gained increasing popularity in the treatment of peripheral nerve injury.1,2 Compared to nerve grafting, nerve transfer can be used in preganglionic brachial plexus injuries. Additional benefits of nerve transfer include the avoidance of dissection in a zone of more proximal injury, a single coaptation site, and a shorter distance from the coaptation site to muscle motor endplates.2,3 Motor nerve transfers involve rerouting of the distal end of a healthy redundant or expendable nerve fascicle into an injured nerve downstream from the nerve injury. Nerve transfers specific for restoration of elbow flexion include the Oberlin technique, in which a fascicle from the ulnar nerve is coapted to a damaged musculocutaneous nerve (MCN), or double fascicular nerve transfer, in which a fascicle of the median nerve (MN) to the branch to the brachialis muscle is also included.4,5

Critical to the success of these procedures is the identification of the appropriate donor and recipient nerves. Local anatomy, branching pattern, and intraoperative nerve stimulation are used as adjuncts in the initial exposure. The hand and extremity can be prepped into the field, and a handheld nerve stimulator can be applied to look for specific muscle movements. In addition, neuromonitoring and electromyography (EMG) can be used to identify activation of specific muscles following direct electrical stimulation. However, because of preoperative nerve injury, electrical stimulation is not always possible, and a reliance on nerve anatomy is essential. Recognizing and understanding the anatomical variants are helpful toward this end. In this report, we present the first intraoperative identification of a rare anatomical variant of MCN and MN fusion and confirm findings using internal neurolysis and electrical stimulation.

Illustrative Case

Clinical Presentation

An 8-year-old male was referred for elbow flexion weakness after an acute demyelinating process of the spinal cord, likely acute disseminated encephalomyelitis, 5 years prior to presentation. He initially presented with quadriparesis that improved after plasmapheresis and over time. However, at his most recent follow-up, the patient lacked antigravity strength in the right biceps, deltoid, and triceps. His most recent cervical magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed a reduction of prior spinal cord signal abnormality with 1- to 2-mm diameter T2 signal extending from the mid-cervical to upper thoracic spinal cord. EMG showed the presence of motor unit potentials in the weakened arm muscles suggesting an upper, rather than lower, motor neuron injury. To restore elbow flexion, nerve transfer was recommended, with an ulnar nerve fascicle as a donor to the biceps branch of the MCN.

Intraoperative Findings

After the induction of general anesthesia, the entire extremity was prepped into the field such that arm and hand movements after nerve stimulation could be directly observed. Sterile neuromonitoring leads were placed to record intraoperative EMG in ulnar- and MN-innervated muscles. A linear incision was made in the groove between the biceps and triceps. The incision extended from approximately three fingerbreadths from the axilla to beyond the midpoint of the proximal arm. The medial antebrachial cutaneous nerve was identified and protected. Dissection proceeded through the fascia, and a neural structure was identified deep to the biceps muscle with a branch originating from it approximately midway along the proximal arm. In the presence of an upper motor neuron injury, direct stimulation was applied. A current of 0.2 mA resulted in weak biceps contraction. In addition, hand movement and EMG activity in median nerve-innervated muscles were unexpectedly noted. Given concerns for the possible spread of current, the dissection was continued, and the brachial artery was identified. A separate structure consistent with the MN was not noted close to the brachial artery. Exploration deep to the brachial artery was performed, and electrical stimulation in the region was applied, but no additional neural structure was found. Dissection proceeded toward the medial aspect of the arm, and the ulnar nerve was identified. Its identity was confirmed using direct electrical stimulation.

Further dissection under the biceps proximally and distally did not show additional separate neural structures. On closer observation of the initially identified neural structure under the biceps muscle, the neural structure appeared to have two separate elements. Under the microscope, an internal neurolysis was performed to separate the medial and lateral components. The anatomy and subsequent nerve stimulation findings suggested an anatomical variant in which the MCN was fused with the MN (Fig. 1). After internal neurolysis, direct stimulation did not result in spread of the current. Direct stimulation along the lateral neural element distal to the takeoff of the biceps branch showed weak elbow flexion consistent with the brachialis branch of the MCN. Stimulation of the medial portion of the neural structure showed finger and wrist flexion consistent with the MN.

Surgical Intervention

Given the proximity of the MN to the MCN, two fascicles from the MN were used as a donor rather than a fascicle from the more distant ulnar nerve. Nerve coaptation was performed as follows. Microsurgical techniques were used to perform an internal neurolysis of the MN. Direct stimulation was applied to several individual fascicles, identifying fascicles on the medial aspect of the nerve to have the most flexor carpi radialis function with no finger and thumb flexion or forearm pronation. Two fascicles were isolated and cut sharply. The proximal aspect of the biceps branch of the MCN was cut sharply. The two ends were placed adjacent to each other (Fig. 2). The elbow was flexed and extended through its full range, and no movement was noted between the cut nerve ends. The recipient biceps branch was slightly larger in cross-sectional area than that of the two donor MN fascicles, so a nerve connector was used for coaptation. Sutures were placed to secure the nerves to the connector, and fibrin glue was applied. The wound was closed in a standard fashion and the arm was immobilized. The patient tolerated the procedure well and reported preservation of preoperative hand and wrist strength after surgery.

Discussion

Observations

In this illustrative case, we present a unique anatomical variant of MCN and MN fusion noted during a peripheral nerve transfer for the restoration of elbow flexion. Although several cadaveric studies have reported anatomical variants between the MCN and MN, fusion of the two nerves is rare. In early reports, connections between the MCN and MN were classified into three types based on whether an anastomotic branch between the two nerves was proximal or distal to the coracobrachialis muscle.6 Given the absence of morphological details in this schema, Choi et al.7 proposed a new classification system that consisted of three patterns. The first pattern referred to fusion of the MCN and MN, which was consistent with intraoperative findings in the present case. This pattern was noted in approximately 5% of the 276 arms studied among 138 cadavers. Intraoperative findings in the current case are consistent with anatomical studies by Clarke et al.8 in which intraneural fascicular dissection was performed in cases of MCN and MN fusion. The authors noted that the nerves were surrounded by common epineurium, and an internal neurolysis was performed such that the nerves could be separated. The same observations of a fused epineurium and the ability to separate the two trunks with no evidence of communicating branches were noted in our case. Although the nerves appeared fused throughout their visualized course in the operative exposure, in cadaveric studies, the length of the fused segment ranged from approximately 2 to 14 cm.7 Another variant was noted in a cadaveric study in which the MCN and MN were separate proximally and then fused together more distally in the upper arm.9

Rather than fusion, some studies have documented the absence of the MCN.10 In a systematic review examining nearly 500 limbs, the absence of the MCN occurred in approximately 1.7% to 13% of cases. In these cadaveric studies, the MN supplied the biceps brachii and brachialis muscles.7,11 Such reports may over- or underestimate the reported incidence of MCN and MN fusion. Other studies have reported various degrees of MN and MCN fusion observed during ultrasound-guided nerve blocks.12,13 A limitation described by authors of those studies was that some of the common trunks identified on imaging might have been separable if an anatomical dissection had been performed. They addressed this limitation by using electrical stimulation to help identify neural structures. Given the open nature of our study, both intraneural dissection and electrical stimulation were utilized.

Compared to fusion of the MCN and MN, other patterns of connection between the two nerves are more frequent. One anatomical study reported a communicating branch in 21 of 106 upper limbs (20%).14 In the classification scheme put forth by Choi et al.,7 pattern 2 refers to a connecting branch between the MCN and MN. It was the most common pattern, occurring in 53 (73%) of the 73 variant arms, and the category was further subdivided based on the number of branches. In pattern 3, two connecting branches between the MCN and MN were present, which occurred in 5 (6.8%) of the variant arms.7 In comparative anatomical studies, such connections between the MCN and MN nerve have been noted in monkeys and apes and may represent a primitive MN supply of the anterior arm muscles.15 Similarly, a single nerve trunk in the anterior aspect of the arm is noted in animals such as amphibians, reptiles, and birds. These anatomical variants likely have an embryologic basis.7

A limitation of our study is that an extended exposure along the entire course of the anomalous nerves, as in cadaveric studies, was not performed. For instance, dissection was not performed more proximally to the coracobrachialis muscle or the brachial plexus. Only the minimum exposure required from a clinical standpoint to safely identify neural structures and to perform the nerve transfer was done in an effort to avoid harm. However, electrical testing was performed with real-time feedback of muscle contraction, which is not possible in cadaveric studies.

Lessons

Fusion of the MCN and MN can occur in the upper extremity. Intraneural dissection and electrical stimulation can be used to help identify variant nerve anatomy. Knowledge of this variant and others increases the ability to correctly identify donor and recipient nerves in proximal upper-extremity nerve transfer surgeries.
