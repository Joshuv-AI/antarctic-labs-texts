Protoplasting and Transformation Using CRISPR/Cas9 in Coccidioides
Authors: Katrina M Jackson, Matthew M Morales, Edyta Szewczyk, Christina M Homer, Ashley N Itogawa, Rachel B Brem, Anita Sil, Bridget M Barker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract Coccidioides posadasii and C. immitis are human fungal pathogens endemic to the American Southwest. C. posadasii and C. immitis are the causative agents of coccidioidomycosis, or Valley fever. They are pathogens of growing concern, as reported cases of coccidioidomycosis have increased 20‐fold in the last two decades. Despite their importance as pathogens, Coccidioides spp. are understudied, especially compared to other human fungal pathogens with similar infectious burdens. The reasons Coccidioides spp. are understudied are multifactorial, including requirement of high biocontainment, technical difficulties in lab‐based culture growth, and a historic lack of genetic tools. Previous methods of genetic manipulation have been technically challenging, time consuming, and inefficient. Here, we present protocols for designing gene deletion constructs, generating protoplasts from both genetically engineered biosafety level 2 (BSL2) and wildtype biosafety level 3 (BSL3) strains, and performing transformations with CRISPR/Cas9. The protoplasting protocol described here uses a cell wall digestion enzyme employed in the wine‐making industry and results in high‐quality protoplasts that have the potential to be used for applications beyond transformations. We also present a high‐efficiency transformation method using CRISPR/Cas9. The protocols described here will allow for genetic manipulation of Coccidioides, using both BSL2 and BSL3 strains. This resource can be applied to expand research done in Coccidioides spp., build molecular tools, and expand overall knowledge of these important pathogens. © 2026 The Author(s). INTRODUCTION

Coccidioides posadasii and C. immitis are human fungal pathogens endemic to arid and semi‐arid regions of the American continents. Coccidioides spp. are thermally dimorphic fungi, growing as arthroconidia‐producing mycelia under environmental conditions and as endospore‐filled spherules during infection (Jackson et al., 2024; Mead et al., 2019). Disease is caused by the inhalation of arthroconidia, typically following aerosolization due to ground disturbance or dust storms (Kollath et al., 2019), and total annual cases of coccidioidomycosis in the United States are estimated to be 206,000 to 360,000 (Chiller, 2019; Centers for Disease Control, 2022; Williams et al., 2025). Despite the medical importance of Coccidioides spp., they are understudied due to technical challenges in culturing and biocontainment and a historic lack of genetic tools. The C. posadasii Silveira reference strain was first sequenced in 2010 (Neafsey et al., 2010), and 49% of the genome consists of hypothetical genes of unknown function (Mead, Roe, et al., 2020). Recent transcriptomic analyses have begun to identify gene transcripts upregulated specifically in spherule morphology and associated with the dimorphic switch (Carlin et al., 2021; Duttke et al., 2022; Homer et al., 2025; Mead, Roe, et al., 2020; Whiston et al., 2012). Some of these genes do not have orthologs outside of Coccidioides spp. and would benefit from characterization through reverse genetics, which can directly link specific genes to phenotypes. Isolation of hypovirulent or avirulent mutants through this strategy would speed development of a protective vaccine. For example, the deletion of CPS1 from the C. posadasii Silveira wildtype reference strain resulted in an avirulent strain that provides protection against infection with virulent Coccidioides strains and is being developed as a potential vaccine (Galgiani et al., 2022; Narra et al., 2016; Powell et al., 2021; Shubitz et al., 2018; Shubitz et al., 2021). Similarly, an avirulent Triple Knock Out (TKO, or ΔT) strain lacking three genes has also been used in vaccine studies and serves as a biosafety level 2 (BSL2) lab strain that can be used as a tool for labs without biosafety level 3 (BSL3) containment capabilities (Reichard et al., 2000; Xue et al., 2009). The challenges in genetic editing of Coccidioides spp. have also delayed access to other vital molecular tools, such as strains expressing fluorescent markers.

When genetically manipulating filamentous fungi, the first step often involves developing transformation‐ready protoplasts, or competent cells, with the cell wall enzymatically removed (Peberdy & Ferenczy, 1985). Protoplasting methods for Coccidioides have been developed, published, and successfully used for transformations (Homer et al., 2025; Hung et al., 2012; Reichard et al., 2000; Xue et al., 2009). However, the digestion of fungal cell walls has historically required various commercial enzyme mixes that included β‐glucanase and chitinase activity (Rodriguez‐Iglesias & Schmoll, 2015), which are no longer commercially available (see Current Protocols article: Wilson et al., 2025), resulting in challenges in making Coccidioides protoplasts. To solve the issues with enzyme availability, protoplasting protocols have had to be reworked using expensive, individually designed enzyme mixes. It was recently shown that enzymes used in the wine‐making industry are effective at digesting fungal cell walls (Oakley et al., 2012; Roux & Chooi, 2022; Syme et al., 2018). These enzymes are affordable and are anticipated to remain commercially available. They are also very effective at digesting the fungal cell wall across a wide range of fungal organisms (see Current Protocols article: Wilson et al., 2025), including, as we show here, C. posadasii.

The first successful gene deletion in Coccidioides occurred in 2000 and used a transformation method adapted from the Aspergillus field (Reichard et al., 2000). In short, cell wall–digesting enzymes were used to make protoplasts, which were then combined with polyethylene glycol (PEG), CaCl2, and a DNA repair construct that included a hygromycin resistance gene targeted to insert in the middle of the target gene sequence. Selective pressure was applied with hygromycin, and PCR was used to screen potentially successful transformants. This method resulted in 8% of hygromycin‐resistant colonies being successful transformants disrupting the target gene (Reichard et al., 2000). The previously discussed Δcps1 mutant was developed through Agrobacterium‐mediated transformation; the creation of the DNA repair cassette used overlap PCR and cloning with restriction enzymes (Narra et al., 2016). However, challenges such as difficulty in making the DNA repair template, low‐efficiency transformations, and high rates of ectopic integrations have delayed characterizing unknown genes.

In 2017, a CRISPR‐Cas9 transformation protocol was developed for Aspergillus fumigatus that used in vitro assembled Cas9–guide RNA (gRNA) ribonucleoproteins (RNPs) combined with protoplasts to result in high‐efficiency transformations (Al Abdallah et al., 2017). This method relied on a Cas9 enzyme and gRNAs targeting a double‐stranded cut to the specific gene being deleted and homology‐directed repair (HDR). Microhomology arms of 35 to 59 bp adjacent to the Cas9 cut sites were sufficient for a high number of transformants. CRISPR was first applied to the dimorphic pathogen Blastomyces dermatitidis in 2020 (see Current Protocols article: Kujoth et al., 2020). Although HDR was not successful in B. dermatitidis, HDR in the absence of CRISPR has been shown to be effective in Coccidioides with homology arms of 500 to 1000 bp in length (Hung et al., 2012). In the absence of CRISPR to direct the repair machinery to cut sites, this protocol resulted in 20 to 100 transformants, of which only 5% to 10% had homologous recombination at the target location (Hung et al., 2012). Leveraging HDR in the presence of CRISPR combines techniques that can lead to higher‐efficiency, precise transformations with a lower number of ectopic integrations.

Therefore, we sought to improve transformation methods across multiple laboratories (Homer et al., 2025; Hung et al., 2012). Here, we present a high‐efficiency CRISPR/Cas9 method for transforming C. posadasii. We describe the process to design DNA repair templates and CRISPR gRNAs (Basic Protocol 1 and Alternate Protocol 1), generate Coccidioides protoplasts from both BSL3 and BSL2 strains using commercially available enzymes (Basic Protocol 2 and Alternate Protocol 2), transform BSL3 and BSL2 strains of Coccidioides with CRISPR/Cas9 guides and DNA repair templates to trigger HDR (Basic Protocol 3 and Alternate Protocol 3), and passage potential mutants to increase transformation efficiency (Support Protocol). We have found that 90% of hygromycin‐resistant colonies have a hygromycin gene and 60% of those show evidence of gene deletion and integration of the DNA repair template in the targeted location. We also describe common issues, pitfalls, and troubleshooting tips for the protocols (see Critical Parameters and Troubleshooting sections).

CAUTION: C. posadasii and C. immitis are risk group 3 (RG3) pathogens that need to be worked with in BSL3 laboratory facilities. Follow all appropriate guidelines and regulations for the use and handling of pathogenic microorganisms. Researchers in the United States should comply with requirements outlined in the NIH Guidelines for Research Involving Recombinant or Synthetic Nucleic Acid Molecules and follow recommendations for BSL3 from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Biosafety in Microbiological and Biomedical Laboratories (BMBL 6th Edition) (Meechan & Potts, 2020). Consult with local and national regulatory entities for other regions. Note that the protocols presented here do not delineate all the standard aspects of biocontainment, i.e., disinfecting samples prior to removal from the biosafety cabinet (BSC). Those steps should be performed according to each institution's specific risk assessments, protocols, and guidelines.

CONSTRUCTION OF GENE DELETION CONSTRUCT

The design and construction of the gene deletion construct involve two parts: generating gRNAs to mediate double‐stranded breaks at the 5’ and 3’ ends of the target gene and generating a DNA repair template with the resistance marker that will replace the deleted gene. Methods for accomplishing these steps in Coccidioides were adapted from CRISPR/Cas9 methods in A. fumigatus (Al Abdallah et al., 2017). For HDR in Coccidioides, DNA repair templates designed with homology regions of 50 bp and 100 bp exhibit lower transformation success (see Supporting Information, Table 1). Weighing the higher efficiency of canonical HDR with longer homology regions against the increased cost of longer homology regions, 200‐bp homology regions on both ends of the gene of interest are recommended for the DNA repair template. The laboratory strain C. posadasii Silveira was used as the reference genome to design the repair construct for targeting genes of interest in this protocol. We recommend that the corresponding genome be assessed if gene deletions are pursued in C. immitis or other C. posadasii strain backgrounds, although other work has shown that single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) within homology regions are tolerated (Al Abdallah et al., 2017). When transforming already genetically modified isolates, such as TKO, it is worth remembering that genetically manipulated isolates often have resistance to hygromycin and phleomycin, so other resistance markers should be used. Designing the gene deletion construct includes using publicly available resources like FungiDB (Alvarez‐Jarreta et al., 2024) and the UCSC CRISPOR tool (Concordet & Haeussler, 2018). To decrease cost, this protocol uses the Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT) CRISPR RNA (crRNA)/trans‐activating crRNA (tracrRNA) system to design CRISPR gRNAs (where only the short crRNA must be changed and synthesized for each new gene targeted). Full single‐guide RNAs (sgRNAs) could also be synthesized or expressed if preferred, as described in Alternate Protocol 3. Gene deletion constructs can be designed either to delete outside the stop and start codons or to span only the start to stop codon. The advantage of the first method (described in this protocol) is that the PAM sites and crRNA sites will be specific to that region of the genome, therefore decreasing the chances of ectopic integrations. The second method (described in Alternate Protocol 1), however, has a lower likelihood of interfering with the upstream and downstream regions of surrounding genes or causing other unwanted impacts on the genome.

CONSTRUCTION OF GENE DELETION CONSTRUCT

In certain fungi, it is common to produce gene deletion mutants lacking the gene sequence spanning from the start codon to the stop codon. If genes are in close proximity and there is concern that the deletion template will interfere with the promoter or terminator regions, a deletion from start codon to stop codon is recommended. This can be accomplished with appropriate design of the gene repair construct, as described below.

CREATION OF PROTOPLASTS IN BSL3

The protoplasting methods described here are adapted from those published by Hung et al. (2012). Coccidioides protoplasting requires a specific life stage for successful cell wall digestion. Based on observational data from our protoplast optimization efforts (data not shown), half or more of the arthroconidia need to have formed a germ tube but cannot have formed branched or very long (i.e., >200 µm) hyphae. If the cell wall digestion occurs too early or too late during germination, there will not be successful recovery of protoplasts. The exact amount of time required for arthroconidia to reach this stage of germination is heavily dependent on location and environmental conditions. For example, in San Francisco, California, arthroconidia reach this stage after 18.5 hr. In Flagstaff, Arizona, there is a seasonal range, with arthroconidia germinating after 16.5 to 17.5 hr in the winter and spring but after 18.5 to 19.5 hr in the summer and fall. The cell wall structure is also relevant: for the BSL3 strain C. posadasii Silveira, protoplasts can only be made from germ tubes. For the avirulent BSL2 deletion strain TKO, which lacks multiple chitin‐producing genes and likely has an altered cell wall (Xue et al., 2009), protoplasts can be made from mycelia/hyphae, which are present 24+ hr after arthroconidia inoculation (Alternate Protocol 2). Mycelia are not effective starting points for protoplasting the C. posadasii Silveira strain; the digestion enzyme mixture cuts the mycelia into fragments without releasing protoplasts. Overall, when attempting this protocol, it is very important to optimize it to the specific location, facility, and strain of Coccidioides and to be aware that changing conditions may impact overall timing and success.

CREATION OF PROTOPLASTS IN BSL2

The C. posadasii mutant TKO strain has deletions of genes in the cell wall that impact virulence; thus, TKO is avirulent in mice (Reichard et al., 2000; Xue et al., 2009). As such, TKO can be safely grown and manipulated in BSL2 laboratory conditions, simplifying the transformation process and offering a lower barrier to entry. There are substantial differences between TKO and Silveira, likely due to impacts on the cell wall following the deletion of genes in the cell wall. TKO is also on the C735 strain background, rather than the Silveira background, possibly accounting for other growth or cell wall variations. Growth rate, cell wall digestion timing, and other protoplasting‐related methods should be expected to differ between TKO and Silveira. For instance, unlike Silveira, TKO protoplasts can be released from 24‐hr‐old, branched hyphae rather than germlings, thus increasing the number of recovered protoplasts. Similar to Silveira, the protoplasts are still very fragile and should still be treated with care, avoiding excessive vortexing or pipetting.

CRISPR/Cas9 TRANSFORMATION IN BSL3

CRISPR/Cas9 is a genetic tool that has been effective in fungal pathogens for genetic alterations, including gene deletions. Using protoplasts as the starting material, the protocol described here results in efficient transformations. There are multiple steps that can be taken to maintain high transformation efficiency. The crRNAs and tracrRNAs should not undergo multiple freeze‐thaw cycles, and the RNP complexes should be kept on ice until transformation. Furthermore, protoplasts should be treated as fragile, and anything leading to potential rupture (vortexing, pipetting) should be avoided as much as possible. It is also important for colonies to establish growth post‐transformation before the selective overlay is applied, so that cells are not shocked upon experiencing selection. It is typically helpful to visually confirm the presence of colonies before adding the overlay.

CRISPR/Cas9 TRANSFORMATION IN BSL2

CRISPR/Cas9 can be used to genetically manipulate the TKO BSL2 Coccidioides strain, though with marked differences from wild‐type strains. TKO has a resistance marker specific to hygromycin and phleomycin, so engineered selection cassettes should allow selection using nourseothricin or other antifungals. sgRNA can optionally be used instead of the crRNA/tracrRNA combination. sgRNA is more stable than crRNA/tracrRNA but also more expensive. Note that there are also differences in medium in this protocol, with sorbitol used in place of sucrose in the GYES plates. Finally, only 24 hr may be required to establish colonies post‐transformation before adding the selective overlay in some geographic regions (e.g., Berkeley, California), though 48 hr is required in others (e.g., Flagstaff, Arizona).

PASSAGING OF PUTATIVE MUTANTS AND PCR CONFIRMATION

Passaging potential transformants on selective medium is important to obtain homokaryons in this multinucleate fungus and decreases the chances of having colonies that derived resistance through unstable genetic mechanisms: colonies that temporarily derive resistance often fail to propagate after the first passage. Ectopic integration of the repair cassette is less frequent in Basic Protocol 3 and Alternate Protocol 3 compared to non‐CRISPR‐guided methods, with ∼40% of colonies showing ectopic integration, compared to 80% to 90%. However, ectopic integration is still common, so colonies should always be carefully screened to confirm genomic integration, regardless of ability to grow on selective medium or presence of the resistance cassette. PCR can be used as a first pass to confirm the presence of selection marker, genomic integration in the correct location, and lack of amplification of the gene targeted for knockout. Whole‐genome sequencing or Southern blotting should be used to confirm the transformation.

COMMENTARY

Background Information

The protocols described here were developed in collaboration with three different laboratory groups, with each group modifying protocols independently. This accounts for the variation in certain steps and processes: we noted when certain steps or incubation lengths can be adjusted and the potential outcomes of those adjustments, when relevant. However, users should keep in mind that not all outcomes have been specifically tested. Users may want to adjust non‐critical incubation lengths or other non‐critical aspects of the protocols to best fit their laboratory, environmental, and institutional requirements.

The BSL2 (Support Protocols 1 and 2) and BSL3 (Basic Protocols 2 and 3) protocols were largely developed in separate laboratories. As such, certain variations between the BSL2 and BSL3 protocols can be disregarded and adjusted to best fit the user's current laboratory setup. For example, although the BSL2 protocols were optimized for the VinoTaste enzyme, we have shown that Laffort's Extralyse is just as effective. Transformations with TKO were optimized using sgRNA and transformations with Silveira use crRNA and tracrRNA, but sgRNA should be just as effective with Silveira, and crRNA and tracrRNA have been shown to work with TKO. Other variations (dry block versus water bath, 15‐ml conical tubes versus 2‐ml microcentrifuge tubes, adding the transformation reaction to the molten GYES versus adding the molten GYES to the transformation reaction) are laboratory specific and can be adjusted to best fit the user's needs. The differences between TKO and Silveira that are critically important are indicated throughout the protocols. For example, TKO protoplasts can be made from mycelia; Silveira protoplasts cannot. (Note that TKO protoplasts can also be made using germlings, but users will have to determine the TKO germination timeline for their specific laboratory environment.) Mycelia cannot be collected via centrifugation, so TKO mycelia are collected using cell strainers or Miracloth. TKO also grows better on GYES with sorbitol, whereas Silveira grows very poorly on medium made with sorbitol. TKO can grow on GYES made with sucrose but grows more quickly when the GYES is made with sorbitol. As such, attention should be paid to the medium recipes to ensure the medium correctly matches the isolate being manipulated. Overall, although users should pay close attention to the critical parameters outlined in these methods, there are adjustments that can be made to best fit these protocols to each laboratory setting and user's needs.

Critical Parameters

Protoplasts

The first and most important stage in transforming Coccidioides is having high quality and quantity of protoplasts (Basic Protocol 2 and Alternate Protocol 2). There are many factors that influence this: a protoplast stock containing debris such as unfiltered digestion enzymes and cell wall fragments can lead to reduced transformation efficiency, poorly digested protoplasts with residual cell wall are also ineffective as a starting point for transformations, and protoplasts are very fragile and can be lost during the preparation protocol (any step with protoplasts should limit pipetting steps and vortexing, as well as unnecessary freeze‐thaw cycles). When working with protoplasts, the centrifuge brake should be always set to a gentle setting (4) to avoid rupture. Finally, it should be noted that we have found the efficiency of transformation is tied to the age of the protoplasts if frozen in certain buffers. We found a marked reduction in transformations when using Silveira protoplasts that are stored at –80°C in STC buffer for >4 months (Fig. 7). Although TKO protoplasts do not lose viability or transformation efficiency during long‐term storage in DMSO and PEG, most of those protoplasts do not survive the freeze‐thaw process. Silveira protoplasts seem to be more hardy and more capable of surviving the freeze‐thaw process in general. Although long‐term storage and transformation efficiency for Silveira protoplasts stored in DMSO and PEG have not yet been tested in our hands, Silveira protoplasts can be successfully revived from DMSO and PEG after 2 weeks of storage, with comparable viability to protoplasts stored in STC.

RNP complexes

Freeze‐thaw cycles for the tracrRNAs and crRNAs (Basic Protocol 3 and Alternate Protocol 3) should be avoided as much as possible, as too many freeze‐thaw cycles can decrease the overall transformation efficiency. The RNP complexes should be made immediately before the transformation and kept on ice until being combined with the protoplasts. If there are institution‐specific concerns with bringing significant quantities of ice into the BSL3 facility, RNPs can be kept in 50‐ml conical tubes filled with ice or on chilled beads that can then be disinfected.

Additional optional controls for protoplasting and transformations

There are several steps in the protoplasting and gene deletion protocols where optional controls may be added, especially if troubleshooting is needed. The protoplasts, for instance, cannot survive osmotic shock when combined with water. At the completion of protoplasting, a 100‐µl aliquot of protoplasts may be combined with water and plated in tandem with protoplasts in osmotic buffer to determine the rate of colonies formed from arthroconidia or hyphae compared to protoplasts. Freezing protoplasts leads to some loss of viability. Thawing and plating an aliquot of protoplasts can assist with determining viability. Protoplasts may rupture during the transformation process, and a control plate of protoplasts without DNA or antifungal may be used to determine overall viability. Development of resistance to the selective marker in the absence of genomic integration of the construct

Coccidioides can develop derived selective marker resistance while growing on selective medium. This occurs more frequently when the drug concentration is lower than recommended, which primarily happens when the selective marker (hygromycin, for example) degrades after being in the incubator for >2 weeks. Therefore, colonies collected from the initial transformation plates after 2 weeks should be considered suspect. The no‐DNA control plate (Table 5) should also be monitored; after colonies appear on that plate, new colonies growing on other plates are likely either to have evolved resistance to the selective marker or to be an indicator that the selective agent is breaking down or ineffective. Transient resistance to the selection marker can occur; those colonies typically do not survive passaging or additional stressors. For example, we have found that colonies with transient resistance do not survive when being grown from glycerol stock or for long‐term storage at room temperature. We have found that 90% of the colonies that have grown out to the fourth passage have the hygromycin gene, with 60% of those showing evidence of correct genomic integration. Although the mechanisms behind failed transformants that passage successfully are not fully understood, in some cases, it is due to transient gene presence, and in other cases, it appears to be due to tandem integration or ectopic integration.

Importance of assessing phenotypic concordance of multiple independently generated mutants

As of now, there is not a well‐validated system for genetic complementation in Coccidioides. As CRIPSR/Cas9‐mediated transformation procedures are known to cause off‐target mutations in microorganisms, including fungi, it is important to generate at least two independently generated gene deletion mutants for each target gene. Only phenotypes that are concordant between the independent mutants can be ascribed to the gene that was intended to be deleted, as it is improbable that the same off‐target mutation would occur in both independent mutants. Independent mutants should be from separate colonies on the initial transformation plate that successfully survive to passage 4 and show evidence of correct genomic integration.

Understanding Results

The purpose of the set of protocols presented here is to make transformants of Coccidioides for both BSL2 and BSL3 strains. These protocols provide methods to make Coccidioides protoplasts by using an enzyme to digest the cell wall, as well as to recover the protoplasts following the enzymatic digestion. The result of these protocols should be a pure solution of protoplasts, with an absence of cell debris and cell wall digestion enzymes. The anticipated result of these protocols is successfully transformed cells that have the gene of interest deleted in all nuclei (as Coccidioides cells are multinucleate) and replaced with a selective marker. Therefore, a successful result is a true transformant containing the selective marker and with the deletion construct genomically integrated, as verified by PCRs (see Support Protocol) and genomic sequencing.

Time Considerations

Basic Protocol 1: Construction of deletion gene construct

Researchers should plan for 15 to 30 min to design the gene construct.

Alternate Protocol 1: Construction of deletion gene construct

Researchers should plan for 15 to 30 min to design the gene construct.

Basic Protocol 2: Protoplasting in BSL3

The initial overnight growth required to make germinated conidia is highly dependent on geographical location and environmental conditions. Growth typically occurs over 16.5 to 19.5 hr, but researchers should expect some variation at this step and optimize for the conditions within their facility. The hands‐on part of the protoplasting takes 9 to 12 hr, with the enzyme digestion lasting approximately 2 to 3 hr. However, after protoplasts have been resuspended in STC, typically after 7 to 8 hr of active work, the protoplast solution can be stored in the refrigerator overnight. Protoplasts stored at –80°C in STC have decreased transformation efficiency after 4 months. Researchers should assume extra time will be needed when working under BSL3 biocontainment.

Alternate Protocol 2: Protoplasting in BSL2

The initial overnight growth typically takes 24 to 25 hr. The hands‐on part of the protoplasting takes 9 to 12 hr, with the enzyme digestion lasting ∼3.5 hr. However, after protoplasts have been resuspended in STC, typically after 7 to 8 hr of active work, the protoplast solution can be stored in the refrigerator overnight or frozen at –80°C in PEG and DMSO solutions. So far, no loss of transformation efficiency has occurred after storing frozen protoplasts for 5.5 months.

Basic Protocol 3: CRISPR/Cas9 transformation in BSL3

The transformation protocol takes about 5 to 6 hr. One hour should be dedicated to assembling the RNP complexes and aliquoting DNA repair templates. Of the 4 to 5 hr for the transformation, 2 to 3 hr is for incubations, and only 1 to 2 hr is active work. After the RNPs are added to the protoplasts, that transformation step can occur for a minimum of 1 hr up to 3 hr, though other researchers have achieved successful transformations in <15 min. There are 48 hr between the initial transformation and the selective overlay; the selective overlay takes 1 to 2 hr. Researchers should assume extra time will be needed when working under BSL3 biocontainment.

Alternate Protocol 3: CRISPR/Cas9 transformation in BSL2

The transformation protocol takes about 4 to 5 hr. One hour should be dedicated to assembling the RNP complexes and aliquoting DNA repair templates. Of the 3 to 4 hr for the transformation, 1 hr is for incubations and 2 to 3 hr is active work. After the RNPs are added to the protoplasts, the transformation step can occur for 15 to 20 min. There are 20 to 48 hr between the initial transformation and the selective overlay, depending on geographic region; the selective overlay takes 1 to 2 hr.

Support Protocol: Passaging mutants and PCR confirmation

There are 5 to 7 days between each passage to allow for Coccidioides growth. Overall, passaging and PCR confirmation take 4 to 5 weeks to complete. Researchers should assume extra time will be needed when working under BSL3 biocontainment.
