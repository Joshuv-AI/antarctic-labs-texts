Extensive handlebar narrow pedicle penile degloving injury in a child: A case report
Authors: Humaid Alzaabi, Iftikhar Jan Suleman, Mohammad Ilyas Bader
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bicycle handlebar trauma rarely causes extensive perineogenital degloving in children. A 13-year-old boy sustained an 11 × 11 cm degloving injury involving the suprapubic, penile, and perineal regions, with narrow-based skin flaps and disruption of superficial penile vessels. He underwent wound exploration, washout, debridement, and layered closure. The suprapubic flap subsequently necrosed, while the penile skin remained viable, and the wound healed progressively by secondary intention. Follow-up revealed scar-related acquired cryptorchidism and mild dorsal chordee. Prompt surgical management and long-term follow-up are essential to identify and manage delayed complications.

Introduction

Bicycle accidents are a major cause of unintentional traumatic injury in childhood, accounting for approximately 5–14% of blunt abdominal trauma in children.1,2 Handle bar injuries are known to cause significant abdominal trauma. Shear injuries caused by bicycle handlebars represent an uncommon subset of handlebar-related trauma, with only rare cases resulting in extensive degloving injuries of the penis and groin. Because of the rarity of this mechanism, the full spectrum of injury patterns, clinical outcomes, and long-term complications remains poorly defined in literature.3 Surgical management can be challenging, and the choice of operative approach may significantly influence wound healing, cosmetic outcome, and long-term functional recovery.

Case report

A 13-year-old boy sustained a bicycle handlebar injury resulting in an extensive degloving of the suprapubic, penile, and perineal regions. Tangential force from craniocaudal direction in the suprapubic area, resulted in extensive soft-tissue injury involving the suprapubic, penile, and perineal regions.

Child was taken to theatre for exploration of the wound. Two large lacerations were identified. One was large flap of degloving injury of 11 × 11 cm in the suprapubic area with very narrow pedicles on both sides of penis in the form of double U-shaped flaps was noted (Fig. 1). This extended on both sides of scrotum to perineum. There was a complete loss of subcutaneous fat from the middle third area of the wound. Then a second laceration at the base of penis was identified which involved degloving of the proximal two third of the penis. Deep penile vessels were intact but superficial vessels were severed due to force of injury. Ventral aspect of the penile shaft was unharmed including urethra. The wound was carefully inspected for foreign bodies. Deep sutures were placed to approximate the subcutaneous fat and recreate the penopubic angle. Skin flaps were then debrided at the edges and advanced to achieve wound closure. Wound suction drain was placed, and a urinary catheter was placed.

On postoperative day 2, the distal end of large flap started looking ischemic which was due to poor vascularization from narrow pedicles from both sides (Fig. 3). This was managed conservatively. The drain was removed on postoperative day 5, and he was discharged home with oral antibiotics and instructions for daily wound cleansing with chlorhexidine or povidone-iodine solution.Fig. 3Wound appearance before discharge, showing areas of skin compromise/necrosis, green arrows showing neck of the glans swelling.

At the one-week follow-up appointment, the distal third of the large flap was necrosed. Although, wound around the scrotum, proximal flap and skin on the penile shaft was healthy (Fig. 4). On subsequent follow-ups, there was complete ischemia and necrosis of the distal flap. This was treated conservatively. Gradually the wound healed and scar contracted. On the subsequent examination at one-month, right testis was not palpable. There as mild dorsal chordee was witnessed as well. The child was reviewed at three months and chordee improved significantly and scar thinned out to a single line. Discussion

Management of degloving injuries is challenging, particularly in the pediatric population, where the extent of injury may not be immediately apparent in the emergency setting and long-term outcomes can be difficult to predict. In the present case, although primary wound closure was achieved and progressive healing was observed, the patient developed delayed complications due to poor blood supply to a large flap from very narrow pedicles. Child also developed acquired cryptorchidism which was possibly due to entrapment of cord structures in the scar tissue.

Bicycle handlebar injuries are potentially serious and may be life-threatening. Their presentation can be subtle or delayed, and the history may initially appear deceptively minor, leading to underestimation of the severity of injury.4 Therefore, physicians should maintain a high index of suspicion when evaluating children with a history of handlebar trauma, particularly when the injury involves the abdomen, pelvis, or perineogenital region.

The mechanism of handlebar injury itself should be considered an indicator of potentially significant trauma. Children presenting after handlebar impact may require careful clinical assessment, serial examinations, and prompt imaging where indicated, including computed tomography, to exclude hollow viscus or solid organ injuries.4

Severe blunt trauma and even impalement injuries can occur because the small surface area at the end of the handlebar may act in a spear-like manner, concentrating force over a limited area.3,5,6 In a prospective study of 813 bicycle-related injuries in children, 21 were handlebar-related injuries, and 10 of these involved life-threatening intra-abdominal trauma. Notably, in all 10 cases, the bicycle handlebars lacked protective plastic covering at the end of the handle.3,7

The typical mechanism of pediatric bicycle handlebar injury involves loss of control of the bicycle, followed by sudden rotation of the front wheel and direct impact of the handlebar end against the rider's neck, chest, abdomen, pelvis, or perineal region.3,5,8 Children may be particularly vulnerable to these injuries because of immature decision-making, limited ability to react during sudden braking or collision, and reduced muscular strength to counteract the force of impact.3,9,10

Although abdominal injuries following pediatric bicycle handlebar trauma are more commonly described in literature, reports of extensive perineogenital degloving injuries remain scarce. Consequently, there is limited evidence to guide the optimal surgical approach, timing of reconstruction, and follow-up strategy for these patients. In our case there was almost complete loss of suprapubic skin including subcutaneous tissue. The flap skin had narrow pedicles but as there was decent blood supply to the pedicle at the time of exploration, hence the decision was made to reconstruct the flap and bridge the lost subcutaneous fat by approximation of the surrounding tissue. Although, there was distal necrosis but the wound healed well eventually through secondary intention. In this context, studies of similar handlebar-related injuries are still valuable, as they help explain the mechanism of trauma, the potential severity of injury, and the importance of prevention. The present case contributes to this limited literature by demonstrating that the same mechanism of concentrated force from a bicycle handlebar can produce extensive soft-tissue injury involving the suprapubic region, penis, and perineum, with delayed complications including acquired cryptorchidism and penile scarring. There is a possibility of worsening of dorsal chordee which may require correction. Hence, a long term follow up is valuable.

The general principles of managing degloving injuries are similar in pediatric and adult patients, including allowing the wound and scar to mature before definitive revision when clinically appropriate. In our case, the subsequent development of an acquired undescended testis represented a case-specific complication that necessitated surgical intervention with orchiopexy, which was combined with scar revision at approximately six months after the initial injury.

Preventive strategies are essential in reducing the risk of pediatric handlebar injuries. Suggested measures include modifying the design and shape of handlebar ends, ensuring protective end coverings are present, limiting excessive side-to-side rotation of the front wheel assembly, promoting appropriate protective equipment, and increasing awareness of safe cycling practices among children, parents, and caregivers.3

Conclusion

Bicycle handlebar injuries in children can cause rare but severe perineogenital degloving injuries. This case highlights the importance of early recognition, prompt surgical exploration, careful reconstruction, and long-term follow-up for wound healing, cosmetic outcome, and delayed complications. Prevention should focus on safe cycling practices, protective equipment, handlebar safety modifications, and caregiver awareness.
