Effectiveness of a capacity-building program for adolescent obesity prevention on perceived skills, professional attitudes, and challenges among school nurses in a lower-middle-income community: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Radhia Chaieb, Maha Dardouri, Jihen Sahli, Mohamed Ben Rejeb, Souad Chelbi, Nabiha Missaoui, Ghada Saad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesChildhood obesity prevention is a public health priority to reduce the burden of chronic diseases. This study assessed the impact of a capacity-building program designed to enhance school nurses’ competencies in adolescent obesity prevention in the Sousse region of Tunisia.Trial DesignA randomized controlled trial was conducted among all school nurses in the region for nine months, from January to September 2024, in the Sousse region health district. Eligible participants were stratified by the school district and randomly assigned to either the intervention (IG) or control group (CG) using a random number generator.MethodsThe target population was school nurses actively engaged in school health services, including health promotion and obesity prevention, with a minimum of six months of professional experience. Ninety-five participants were allocated to the intervention. The intervention was multidisciplinary, including expert-led trainers on nutrition, communication, physical activity, and obesity. The primary outcome was perceived skills, and the secondary outcomes were professional attitudes and challenges regarding childhood obesity prevention. Blinding was not used in this study.ResultsA total of 116 nurses participated, predominantly female, with one-third aged 40–49 years. Forty-nine participants were randomised to the IG and 46 to the CG (n = 46). At baseline, the intervention group reported significantly lower self-perceived competency in obesity management than the control group (p = 10−3). After six months, there is a significant difference between the intervention and the control group in terms of perceived skills, including significant improvements in educating adolescents about physical activity, promoting healthy eating habits, and addressing obesity-related concerns with confidence (p = 10−3). Regarding perceived challenges, the IG expressed an increased need for further training, particularly in counseling and motivational interviewing (p = 10−3), higher than the CG.ConclusionThis capacity-building program significantly improved school nurses’ skills, confidence, and motivation in adolescent obesity prevention. Nevertheless, skills related to family dynamics and behavioral management require more targeted interventions. Sustained professional training is crucial for addressing structural barriers and ensuring the long-term effectiveness of the program.Trial registrationThis clinical trial was registered with the Pan African Clinical Trial Registry on April 7, 2025, under reference number PACTR202504907833519.Keywords: Capacity-building, Skills, Attitudes, Nurses, Adolescent, Obesity, Prevention, Intervention, Professional development

Introduction

Obesity is a rapidly growing global issue, particularly among children and adolescents [1]. It is defined as a “chronic, complex, progressive, and recurrent disease characterized by an excessive accumulation of body fat that harms health” [2]. Between 1975 and 2016, the prevalence of childhood obesity among children and adolescents aged 5 to 19 years more than quadrupled, increasing from 4% to 18% worldwide [2]. If current trends continue, projections suggest that by 2030, 2.16 billion adults will be classified as overweight, while 1.12 billion will be obese [3].

Childhood obesity is a significant risk factor for various complications, including cardiovascular, respiratory, orthopedic, metabolic, and liver diseases later in life [4]. Additionally, it poses a serious psychological burden as adolescents with obesity are more vulnerable to discrimination and bullying, which can lead to low self-esteem, reduced social interactions, behavioral disorders, and academic difficulties [5]. Notably, evidence suggests that obesity is a risk factor for suicidal thoughts. Notably, studies have identified obesity as a risk factor for suicidal ideation [6, 7]

In Tunisia, obesity was the second-leading risk factor contributing to the total number of Disability-Adjusted Life Years (DALYs) in 2019. The prevalence of obesity among Tunisian children and adolescents aged 5 to 19 years increased significantly from 3.4% in 2000 to 8.5% in 2016 [8]. Similarly, the prevalence of overweight in this age group rose from 15.2% in 2000 to 25% in 2016 [8]. Despite multiple national programs implemented as part of nutrition policies and strategies to prevent obesity, the prevalence continues to increase at an alarming rate [9]. This suggests that the obesity prevention action plan executed in Tunisia between 2013 and 2017 may have been insufficient.

These alarming trends highlight the urgent need to manage and prevent childhood and adolescent obesity, especially regarding community health worldwide and in Tunisia. In particular, school nurses play a crucial role in obesity prevention and management and are considered a cornerstone of public health efforts [10, 11]. However, they face several barriers that hinder them from effectively carrying out this role [11]. Key challenges include limited knowledge and inadequate training, particularly in modern assessment methods, counseling techniques, and behavioral management strategies for obesity [12–15]. Additionally, access to reliable information, as well as information-sharing and collaboration, have been identified as major of the main challenges faced by school nurses working within the field of childhood obesity, as shown in a study in North Sweden [16].

Given these barriers, there is an urgent need for further research to develop innovative programs that empower school nurses to take a more active role in childhood obesity prevention efforts [11]. Consequently, obesity prevention action plans must prioritize strengthening nurses’ skills and knowledge, especially in managing adolescent obesity.

The present study aimed to assess the effect of a capacity-building training program for school nurses in the Sousse region in Tunisia, with a focus on enhancing their knowledge, skills, and professional attitudes toward the prevention of obesity in adolescents.

Methods

Patient and public involvement

This study involved school nurses practicing in the health districts of the Sousse region. Nurses were involved in participants’ enrolment, allocation, intervention, and data collection.

Trial design

The current randomized controlled trial was conducted over nine months, from January to September 2024, in the Sousse region. This study adheres to CONSORT 2025 guidelines for reporting clinical trials. The conceptual framework of this trial is the superiority of the intervention (capacity-building training program and usual professional development) over the comparator (the usual professional development). The unit of randomisation was the individual participant with an allocation ratio (1:1). Figure 1 shows the trial process.

The schematic diagram of the study process

Changes to trial protocol

The randomization technique was changed from sealed opaque envelopes in the protocol to a random allocation sequence during the study for applicability. The primary outcome was identified as the perceived skills of nurses regarding obesity prevention in adolescents.

Trial settings

The trial was carried out within the Medicine School Unit of the Sousse region, Tunisia. This unit oversees 12 district health centers providing medical services to schools in both urban and rural areas.

Eligibility criteria

The target population was school nurses in the Sousse region who provided informed consent and were actively engaged in school health services, including health promotion and obesity prevention, with a minimum of six months of professional experience. Trainers eligible to deliver the intervention are experts in nutrition, endocrinology and obesity, psychology, and physical education.

Intervention and comparator

The intervention, adapted from a University of Manitoba program [17], was implemented as a structured Train‑the‑Trainees program intended to enhance school nurses’ competencies in adolescent obesity prevention. It comprised four face-to-face sessions of six hours with a break of one hour. Intervention sessions were delivered every Saturday for 4 consecutive weeks from February 17th to March 9th, 2024. IG was divided into 4 sub-groups. Sessions were delivered in rotation by a psychologist, a nutritionist, a physical education professor, and an endocrinologist. Training sessions were conducted in a training center at the university.

The psychologist trained nurses in positive communication techniques to assess motivation and encourage behavior change. The nutritionist provided guidance on balanced nutrition, risks of processed foods, and healthy meal planning. The physical education professor emphasized regular exercise and strategies for integrating physical activity into adolescents’ routines. The endocrinologist addressed the medical causes, consequences, and clinical signs of secondary obesity, as well as the referral criteria.

Each session concluded with interactive discussions to reinforce learning. The program was reviewed and approved by the Regional Health Directorate and the Ministry of Health.

The comparator is the usual professional development provided by the Ministry of Health, including yearly conferences and specific meetings as needed. Both groups were eligible for the usual professional development.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the perceived professional skills of nurses, and the secondary outcomes were the attitudes and perceived challenges regarding adolescent obesity management and prevention. These were assessed using the Perceived Skills, Professional Attitudes, and Challenges Scale developed by SanchezRamirez et al. [18], which evaluates obesity management through perceived competencies, professional attitudes, and challenges. Responses were captured on a single 5-point Likert-type item scale ranging from 1 (“strongly disagree”) to 5 (“strongly agree”). It also collected demographic data and used a unique identifier to track responses across three time points (baseline (T0), immediately post-intervention (T1), and six months after the intervention (T2). Since the CG did not receive intervention, it was assessed at baseline and six months. Each item was analyzed separately by computing the mean response across participants.

Originally in English, the questionnaire was translated into French using Werner and Campbell’s back-translation method [19]. Content validation was conducted with 11 nurses, and expert feedback ensured a Content Validity Index (CVI) greater than 0.7 for all items. Reliability testing resulted in a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.828, confirming good internal consistency. Self-administered questionnaires were utilized at all study time points to evaluate changes over time.

Sociodemographic and professional information were recorded using a data collection sheet. Information included gender, age, years of experience, and weight according to BMI classification (Normal, overweight, or obese).

Harms

This trial did not present any potential tangible or intangible harm.

Sample size

Sample size was determined using Biostat TGV software, based on a similar study that assessed perceived skills, attitudes, and challenges in obesity management [18]. With an estimated effect size of 0.69 in perceived skills, a power of 90% (1-β = 0.9), and a significance level of 5% (α = 0.05), the required sample size was calculated to be 86 participants (43 per group).

Randomization

Eligible participants were stratified by the school district and randomly assigned to either the intervention or control group (CG) using a random number generator. The second investigator (M.D) performed the randomization technique. The unit of randomization was the individual participant with an allocation ratio (1:1).

Implementation

The principal investigator (R.C.) enrolled and assigned participants to the interventions. The second investigator (M.D.) and the supervisor of the trial (G.S.) had access to the random allocation sequence. The allocation list was stored in a shared Excel file.

Blinding

In this trial, blinding techniques were not used.

Statistical methods

Data were entered and analyzed using the SPSS Statistics software version 25.0. Qualitative variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, while quantitative variables were expressed as means ± standard deviations. Normal distribution of continuous variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. The Likert-type scales were evaluated using mean and SD analysis. According to empirical research, parametric methods are robust to violations of the assumptions of interval-level measurement and normality and can be appropriately applied to Likert-type data. Norman [20] showed that parametric statistics, including the use of means, standard deviations, and ANOVA, are highly robust even when data are skewed or ordinal in nature. Carifio and Perla [21] argued that the longstanding debate about the misuse of Likert scales has been largely resolved, supporting the legitimacy of summarizing such data with parametric methods. Sullivan and Artino [22] also highlight that reporting means and standard deviations for Likert-type items is acceptable and informative.

In line with this well-established methodological guidance, and consistent with the analytic strategy employed in the original study of Sanchez-Ramirez et al. [18] from which the instrument was adapted in this study, the authors chose to present the mean and standard deviation for each item rather than computing a composite score.

Comparisons between groups were performed using the chi-square test for categorical variables and Student’s t-test for continuous variables, depending on the independence of the samples. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 with a 95% confidence interval. No missing data were reported.

Results

Participant flow

One hundred sixteen nurses were enrolled. After assessing eligibility, twenty-one nurses were excluded before the intervention (11 participated in pre-testing, four were on long-term leave, and six declined to participate). At last, 95 participants were randomly allocated to the IG (n = 49) or CG (n = 46) (in a 1:1 ratio). Six participants were lost to follow-up after six months (4 in the IG and 2 in the CG) (Fig. 2).

Recruitment

After approval from health authorities, participant recruitment started on January 7th, 2024, and finished on February 9th, 2024. The follow-up took place from March to the end of August 2024, spanning a period of six months. Assessment after follow-up was conducted in the first two weeks of September 2024.

Intervention and comparator delivery

Intervention was delivered in rotation by a psychologist, a nutritionist, a physical education professor, and an endocrinologist. Training sessions were conducted in a training center at the university.

Baseline data

In both groups, most of the participants were female (IG: 83.7%, CG: 84.8%). Approximately one-third were aged 40–49 years (38.8% vs. 32.6%). Among participants, 28.6% in the IG and 28.3% in the CG had more than 21 years of experience. No significant baseline differences in sociodemographic characteristics were observed (Table 1).

Numbers analysed, outcomes, and estimation

In the IG, 45 participants were analysed in comparison to 44 participants in the CG. Immediately after the intervention, nurses in the IG reported significantly greater confidence than those in the CG in promoting physical activity (2.84 ± 0.367 vs. 2.02 ± 0.614, p = 0.001), encouraging healthy eating (2.76 ± 0.435 vs. 2.07 ± 0.712, p = 0.001), and delivering obesity interventions (2.53 ± 0.505 vs. 2.09 ± 0.626, p = 0.001). They demonstrated higher perceived competence (1.58 ± 0.499 vs. 3.11 ± 1.159, p = 0.001) and comfort in discussing weight with patients (1.56 ± 0.546 vs. 2.78 ± 1.228, p = 0.001). Persistent challenges were also identified, including inadequate training (4.29 ± 0.695 vs. 3.54 ± 1.048, p = 0.001), insufficient compensation (4.47 ± 0.505 vs. 3.41 ± 0.979, p = 0.001), and limited referral pathways (4.11 ± 0.959 vs. 3.57 ± 1.167, p = 0.017). A strong need for support handling sensitive conversations (4.56 ± 0.624 vs. 3.96 ± 0.759, p = 0.001) and motivational interviewing skills (4.53 ± 0.625 vs. 4.02 ± 0.802, p = 0.001) were also reported (Table 3).

After six months, the IG showed significant improvements in perceived competence, managing weight-related discussions (1.60 ± 0.618 to 2.31 ± 0.514, p = 0.001), promoting physical activity (1.27 ± 0.539 to 2.53 ± 0.548, p = 0.001), and encouraging healthy eating (1.24 ± 0.529 to 2.47 ± 0.548, p = 0.001). Participants were less likely to view obesity care as beyond their professional scope (2.33 ± 1.225 to 1.76 ± 1.004, p = 0.020) and reported better time management for obesity-related care (2.60 ± 1.053 to 1.62 ± 0.614, p = 0.001). However, perceived barriers increased, including limited referral options (3.60 ± 1.053 to 4.27 ± 0.863, p = 0.003) and insufficient patient education resources (3.73 ± 1.176 to 4.36 ± 0.802, p = 0.004). In contrast, the CG showed no significant changes in competencies, attitudes, or perceived barriers over the same period (Table 4).

Discussion

Interpretation

The current study assessed the impact of an interprofessional educational intervention on school nurses’ competencies, attitudes, and perceived barriers in managing adolescent obesity. Our findings revealed significant improvements across all assessed domains in the IG compared to the CG. Notably, sustained gains were observed in perceived competencies, particularly in nurses’ ability to educate and motivate adolescents to engage in physical activity and adopt healthier eating habits. These outcomes align with prior studies demonstrating the effectiveness of educational interventions in enhancing obesity management skills among healthcare providers [12–15, 18]. Specifically, Sanchez-Ramirez et al. [18] reported similar improvements using a comparable pre-post design, including enhanced competencies in weight assessment, patient counseling, family engagement, and behavior strategies, with some effects persisting for six months post-intervention.

However, our findings also indicated that improvements in managing family-related issues and behavior modification techniques diminished over time, suggesting the need for targeted and continuous training to support long-term skill retention. These findings are consistent with Thea’s study on the 5AsT-MD program, which showed that multi-method training enhanced residents’ ability to overcome obesity-related barriers [23]. The 5AsT-MD course draws on the “5As of Obesity Management (ASK, ASSESS, ADVISE, AGREE, ASSIST), which is an evidence-based framework to guide practitioners’ obesity counseling [23]. Furthermore, Ogunleye et al. [14] highlighted that many behavioral interventions lack long-term efficacy, reinforcing the importance of addressing non-weight-related outcomes in obesity care. In our study, participants identified challenges such as caregiver fatigue, relapse prevention, emotional eating, and mental health concerns, emphasizing the need for a holistic and person-centered approach to obesity management [14].

Significant shifts in professional attitudes were also observed. Nurses in the IG were less likely to perceive obesity care as beyond their professional scope and viewed it as less burdensome and time-consuming. These changes suggest that the intervention enhanced their engagement and responsibility toward obesity management, echoing findings from Sanchez-Ramirez, who reported that training can reduce weight-related bias and improve provider attitudes [18].

Furthermore, nurses’ confidence in delivering obesity care improved markedly, with reduced concerns about being overwhelmed and increased preparedness to apply learned strategies. This mirrors a Canadian study where confidence gains among primary care providers were sustained from one to twelve months post-training [24]. Confidence remains a critical barrier to implementing preventive interventions in practice [25, 26]. Thus, our results underscore the importance of structured, skill-focused training.

This view is further supported by Baillargeon et al., who found that multimodal training reduced provider bias and enhanced care delivery confidence [24]. Despite this potential, persistent challenges were noted. Prior research has identified barriers such as limited time [27], knowledge gaps [28, 29], and insufficient training [27, 30, 31], which constrain providers from developing personalized, evidence-based care plans [32].

In practice, healthcare professionals often default to generic advice on diet and activity, lacking individualized approaches or structured follow-up. These issues contribute to suboptimal obesity care and highlight the need for comprehensive, ongoing training [33–36].

Bleich et al. reported that recent medical graduates were more likely to consider social determinants of obesity, while those with longer tenure showed greater skepticism, possibly due to repeated failures in intervention outcomes. Nevertheless, most physicians advocate for enhanced training and system-level changes to improve obesity care [37]. The literature consistently supports obesity education as a cornerstone of improving care quality, emphasizing its integration at undergraduate and postgraduate levels [24, 38]

Six months post-intervention, nurses in the IG reported increased awareness of persistent barriers, particularly around initial training deficiencies, including limited professional development opportunities, insufficient guidance on motivational interviewing, inadequate compensation, and restricted referral pathways. These findings highlight the systemic challenges in delivering effective obesity care and underscore the need for institutional support and policy reforms. Notably, these barriers were more frequently reported by the IG, likely due to heightened awareness following training.

Additionally, while improvements in skills, attitudes, and perceived barriers were maintained at six months, they were less pronounced than immediately post-intervention, indicating a partial decline in impact over time. This underscores the importance of ongoing training and reinforcement to sustain progress.

This study offers important contributions to the literature on obesity management in low- and middle-income settings. The “Train-the-Trainees” program effectively realigned school nurses’ roles with current clinical guidelines and emphasized an interprofessional approach, integrating expertise from endocrinologists, dietitians, physical educators, and psychologists. This multidisciplinary model enabled nurses to address obesity as a complex condition shaped by physical, behavioral, psychological, and social factors.

Furthermore, the program promoted sustainable practices, encouraging the use of motivational interviewing, individualized care plans, and a focus on non-weight-related outcomes such as self-esteem and quality of life. By bridging training gaps and improving confidence, the intervention highlighted the pivotal role of school nurses, particularly in resource-constrained environments. These findings call for system-level reforms to enhance training infrastructure and establish school nurses as key actors in obesity prevention and management.

Nonetheless, challenges remain—particularly in integrating family support and behavior modification strategies, which are critical for long-term success. Future interventions should adopt a more holistic model that incorporates psychosocial and environmental factors alongside clinical competencies. Sustained professional development and institutional backing are essential to ensure lasting impact.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the intervention did not cover family engagement or behavioral modification strategies for adolescents and their families. Second, despite using a relevant sampling strategy, sample size and participation rates were limited, which may affect generalizability within the Tunisian context. Given the key role of pediatric nurses in adolescent care, future research should involve both school and pediatric nurses to increase sample size and scope.

Conclusion

This study underscores the positive impact of a comprehensive “Train-the-Trainees” program in enhancing school nurses’ competence, motivation, and confidence in managing adolescent obesity. The findings demonstrate both short- and medium-term benefits, reinforcing the importance of structured, interprofessional education in tackling complex public health issues.

Ultimately, this research highlights the critical role of well-designed training programs in empowering healthcare professionals to combat adolescent obesity effectively. To optimize outcomes, future strategies must bridge the gap between individual skill development and systemic barriers, fostering interdisciplinary collaboration and integrated policies. This approach offers a more sustainable and impactful path forward in adolescent obesity prevention and care.
