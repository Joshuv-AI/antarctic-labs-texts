New drug targets for acute myeloid leukemia identified through a comprehensive analysis of the plasma protein
Authors: Yaonan Hong, Xu Wang, Jingjie Dong, Qi Liu, Yingying Shen, Xiawan Yang, Peicheng Wang, Wenjie Li, Baodong Ye, Keding Shao, Ya-Huei Kuo, Dijiong Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveAcute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a malignant blood disease with a poor prognosis. With the development of targeted therapies, many refractory/relapsed or unfit AML patients have achieved better survival rates. However, primary or acquired resistance frequently occurs, and some targets become unavailable. This study aimed to identify and verify novel targets for AML using Mendelian Randomization (MR) analysis.MethodThe association between plasma protein quantitative trait loci (pQTL), expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL), and AML was examined via MR. Bayesian co-localization analysis was used to detect common segments of TCL1A and AML. Bioinformatics technology was used to explore the relationship between TCL1A and AML, and quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis was used to detect the expression of TCL1A in various cell lines. Furthermore, high-throughput virtual screening (HTVS) and molecular dynamics (MD) simulation was used to identify potential TCL1A inhibitors. Further verification was carried out by AML cell experiments.ResultsOur study found a strong association between plasma TCL1A protein levels and AML (OR: 1.66; 95% CI: 1.46–1.88), and rs78986913 was the same variant in both TCL1A and AML. High TCL1A expression was associated with low overall survival rates in AML patients (P < 0.001), especially in the FAB M0 and M2 subgroups. Additionally, TCL1A was abnormally highly expressed in many AML cell lines (including MOLM-13, MV4-11, THP-1) compared to normal bone marrow stromal cells (HS-5) and other solid tumor cells. We also identified 19 small molecules, finding that Bletilloside A (id number: 2292159-89-6) is one of the most fitting small molecules for the TCL1A protein pocket. MD simulation confirmed the good stability of the Bletilloside A-TCL1A complex. And Bletilloside A could inhibit the proliferation of AML by inhibiting TCL1A.ConclusionThere is a causal relationship between TCL1A and AML. The expression level of TCL1A deserves further monitoring in clinical settings, and the benefits of targeting TCL1A should be further validated.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12885-025-15438-5.Keywords: Acute myeloid leukemia, TCL1 family AKT coactivator A, Mendelian randomization, High-throughput virtual screening, Bletilloside A

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) accounts for about 70% of adult acute leukemia cases, with a 5-year overall survival rate around 30% [1–3]. With a deeper understanding of leukemia mechanisms and the development of structural pharmacology, many small molecule targeted therapies have been developed. Among them, BCL-2 inhibitors, IDH1 inhibitors, and FLT3 inhibitors are the most popular in clinical practice, dramatically changing the prognosis of refractory AML [4–6]. However, many AML patients still have a poor prognosis due to the lack of available targets or primary/acquired resistance to targeted therapy. It is clinically significant to further understand leukemia development and explore new treatment targets, with the hope that AML can become a theoretically curable disease without stem cell transplantation.

With recent technological advances, protein quantitative trait loci (pQTL) and expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) datasets have become crucial for studying human biology and pathophysiology. These datasets help bridge the gap between the genome and diseases [7]. Mendelian randomization (MR) analysis has been widely used for drug target development and drug repurposing in recent years [8]. As a genetic instrumental variable analysis, MR usually uses single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) from genome-wide association studies (GWAS) as genetic instruments to estimate the causal effect of an exposure on an outcome [9].

Our study aimed to identify a novel potential drug target for AML through MR analysis, in accordance with bioinformatics technology and experimental validation. We found that TCL1A, which is known to be highly expressed in T-cell prolymphocytic leukemia (T-PLL) [10], has a causal relationship with the development of AML based on plasma protein.

Materials, patients and methods

Ethical approval and consent to participate

The large-scale GWAS data used in our analysis were publicly available and reported by human participants collected from several previous studies. In all original research, informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the data were approved by the institutional review boards of the respective studies.

Study design

This study aimed to investigate the causal relationships among pQTL, eQTL, and AML using MR analysis. MR analysis is a research method that infers the one-way causal effect of an exposure on an outcome by leveraging the random allocation of genetic variation. Effective instrumental variables (IVs) in causal inference must satisfy three key assumptions: (1) the SNP is significantly associated with the exposure; (2) the SNP is not associated with the outcome through confounding factors; (3) the SNP influences the outcome only through the exposure (Fig. 1). In order to eliminate potential confounders, Phenoscanner V2 was used to detect SNPs associated with both exposure and outcomes [11]. There were no missing data in this study.

GWAS data source

GWAS summary statistics of pQTL

The GWAS summary statistics for plasma proteins were derived from data released by Ferkingstad et al. [7]. This study measured 4,907 plasma proteins in 35,559 subjects. GWAS summary statistics of eQTL

The TCL1A-eQTL dataset was obtained from eQTLGen (https://eqtlgen.org/) [12]. This study conducted a GWAS on 30,935 European individuals, analyzing approximately 20,000 SNPs. Genome-wide significant associations (P < 1 × 10−8) were selected as the instrumental variables.

GWAS summary statistics of AML

The GWAS summary statistics for AML were derived from data released by the FinnGen study [13]. This study conducted a GWAS on 314,436 European individuals (Ncase = 244, Ncontrol = 314,192), analyzing approximately 21 million SNPs. The diagnosis of AML follows the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10).

MR analysis

We conducted a two-sample bidirectional MR to assess the reciprocal causal relationships between plasma protein and AML, designated as the total effect. The primary statistical analysis approach was the inverse-variance weighted (IVW) method under a random-effects model [14]. Additionally, we employed four complementary methods (MR-Egger [15], weighted median [16], weighted mode [16], and simple mode) to validate the effectiveness of the IVW results. The IVW approach assumes that all SNPs satisfy the three Mendelian Randomization assumptions. MR-Egger analysis can identify horizontal pleiotropy through the intercept. Weighted median and weighted mode were used to assess the existence of a causal relationship.

Sensitivity analysis

Heterogeneity among SNPs was evaluated using Cochran’s Q statistic and funnel plots [17]. Horizontal pleiotropy was detected using the MR-Egger intercept method [14] and the MR-PRESSO approach [18]. If outliers were detected, they were removed, and the MR causal estimates were reassessed. If heterogeneity remained high after outlier removal, the stability of the results was evaluated using a random-effects model, which is less susceptible to weaker SNP-exposure associations. Finally, a leave-one-out analysis was conducted to assess the impact of each SNP on the overall causal estimates. P > 0.05 indicates no heterogeneity or multiple effects.

Bayesian co-localization analysis

The probability of the causal variable in the “coloc” package with default parameters was used to assess whether the two traits share the same causal variant. Bayesian co-localization offers posterior probabilities for five hypotheses regarding whether a single variant is shared between two traits [19]. In this study, we examined the posterior probabilities of hypothesis 3 (PPH3), which assumes that both eQTL and AML are associated with the region through distinct variants, and posterior probabilities of hypothesis 4 (PPH4), in which both eQTL and AML are linked to the region via shared variants. We used coloc.abf to define a gene as having evidence of co-localization based on a gene-based PPH4 > 95%, as determined by at least one algorithm [20, 21].

Bioinformatics data sources

RNA-seq data and corresponding clinical information of LAML patients were obtained from the TCGA database (https://www.cancer.gov/ccg/research/genomesequencing/tcga) [22]. This dataset included gender, age, white blood cell (WBC) count, bone marrow (BM) blasts, and peripheral blood (PB) blasts. Additionally, RNA-seq data from healthy donors were downloaded from the GTEx database (https://gtexportal.org/). We assessed the sensitivity and specificity of the clinical features compared to TCL1A using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. Kaplan–Meier survival curves of the AML patients were generated using kmplot.com (http://kmplot.com/analysis/) [23].

Data mining of the distribution of TCL1A

cBioPortal (http://www.cbioportal.org) is a comprehensive cancer genomics database that facilitates the identification of molecular data in cancer tissues and provides insights into relevant genetics, epigenetics, gene expression, and proteomics [24, 25]. In our study, we used cBioPortal to investigate the mutational landscape of TCL1A in the genome of AML. Additionally, we utilized the Human Protein Atlas (HPA; www.proteinatlas.org) database [26] to observe the distribution of TCL1A protein across various tissues and cell lines. Furthermore, we employed the HPA database to examine the immunohistochemistry (IHC) results of TCL1A in different tumor types and analyzed its subcellular localization.

Virtual screening of the TCL1A inhibitors

High-throughput virtual screening (HTVS) was conducted to identify potential TCL1A inhibitors. The X-ray crystal structure of human TCL1A, retrieved from the PDB database (PDB ID: 1JSG), was refined using the Protein Preparation Wizard module. The processed protein was analyzed with the Sitemap module to predict potential binding sites, focusing on the same binding pocket as the template protein substrate. A grid file was generated centered around this pocket, with a box size of 20 Å × 20 Å × 20 Å. A total of 1,620,219 compounds in 2D format from the Chemdiv (D001) and Targetmol (T001) databases were processed through the LigPrep Module in Schrodinger, which included hydrogenation and energy minimization, ultimately yielding 3D structures for virtual screening. The HTVS mode was used to screen the prepared small-molecule compounds, and the top 10% scoring compounds were selected for a second round of screening using the Standard Precision (SP) mode. The top 10% scoring compounds from the second round were further filtered through the Extra Precision (XP) mode, producing a ranked list of molecule candidates. To assess the drug-likeness of the virtually screened compounds, we utilized quantitative estimation of drug-likeness (QED) analysis, selecting compounds with QED values greater than 0.4. Additionally, all pan-assay interference compounds (PAINS) were excluded to minimize the risk of false positives. Finally, protein–ligand interaction diagrams were generated using PyMOL. The protein was represented as a navy-blue cartoon model, the ligands were displayed as cyan sticks, and the binding interfaces were depicted as magenta sticks. Non-polar hydrogen atoms were omitted, while hydrogen bonds, ionic interactions, and hydrophobic interactions were visualized as yellow, magenta, and green dashed lines, respectively.

Molecular Dynamics (MD) simulation

MD simulations were conducted using Gromacs 2022. The force field parameters were obtained via the pdb2gmx tool in Gromacs and the AutoFF web server. During the simulation, the amber14sb force field was employed for the molecular parameters of the receptor protein, while the GAFF2 force field was used for those of the ligand. The system was solvated by surrounding it with a 1 nm cubic water box of the TIP3P type. Using the gmx genion tool, ions were added to the system to achieve electrical neutrality. Long-range electrostatic interactions were handled using the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method, with a cutoff distance set at 1 nm. Constraints on all bonds were implemented using the SHAKE algorithm, and the integration time step in the MD simulation was set to 1 fs using the Verlet leapfrog algorithm. Prior to the MD simulation, the system underwent energy optimization. The energy minimization process consisted of 3000 steps of steepest descent optimization followed by 2000 steps of conjugate gradient optimization. The simulation was run under NPT system conditions at a temperature of 310 K and constant pressure, with a simulation time of 100 ns. During the simulation, tools such as g-rmsd, g-rmsf, g-hbond, g-Rg, and g-sasa were used to calculate the root mean square deviation (RMSD), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), hydrogen bonds (HBonds), radius of gyration (Rg), and solvent accessible surface area (SASA), respectively.

Validation in cell lines

Cell culture

The human bone marrow stromal cell line (HS-5 (CVCL_3720)), leukemic cell lines (MOLM-13 (CVCL_2119), MV4-11 (CVCL_0064), THP-1 (CVCL_0006), KG-1a (CVCL_1824), SKM-1 (CVCL_0098), HL-60 (CVCL_0002), NB4 (CVCL_0005), SKNO-1 (CVCL_2196), NOMO-1 (CVCL_1609), and MONO-MAC-1 (CVCL_1425)), colon cancer cell lines (HT29 (CVCL_A8EZ), SW480 (CVCL_0546), HCT116 (CVCL_0291)), breast cancer cell line (MDA-MB-231 (CVCL_0062)), and lung cancer cell line (A-549 (CVCL_0023)) were preserved in our laboratory. Cells were seeded at an initial concentration of 1 × 105/mL in 1640 medium (or DMEM medium or McCoy’s 5 A medium) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 incubator. The media were replaced every 48 h, and cells were observed and photographed to document their growth status.

Quantitative Real-Time PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis

Human bone marrow stromal cell line (HS-5) was used as the control group, and each sample was repeated six times. Samples from cells were collected, and total RNA was extracted according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The concentration and purity of the extracted RNA were measured, and cDNA was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit. The reaction conditions were as follows: incubation at 25 °C for 10 min, incubation at 42 °C for 15 min, and heat inactivation at 85 °C for 5 s. The cDNA was then stored at −20 °C. A reaction volume of 20 μL was prepared, and the PCR cycling conditions were as follows: 94 °C for 30 s (pre-denaturation), followed by 40 cycles of 94 °C for 5 s (denaturation) and 61 °C for 35 s (annealing). Three independent replicates were performed for each experiment, and GAPDH was used as the reference gene for quantification. The expression levels of TCL1A were quantitatively measured. Differences in mRNA expression levels between groups were calculated using the 2-ΔΔCT method. Primer sequences are presented in Table 1.

Proliferation detection

Cells in the logarithmic growth phase were collected and seeded into a 96-well plate at 1 × 104 cell per well. Three replicate wells were set up for each sample. Following the addition of the corresponding concentrations of drugs, the cells were incubated in a constant-temperature incubator for 48 h, respectively. 10 μL of CCK-8 solution (Shanghai Biosharp, QX1210) was added, and further incubated for 2 h. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader, and the relative inhibition rate of the cells was calculated.

Western Blot (WB) analysis

Reference to previous protocol in our laboratory for WB analysis [27]. The membranes were incubated overnight with primary antibodies against TCL1A (Proteintech, #10,475–1-AP), β-actin (Cell Signaling Technology (CST), #4970). Immunoreactive bands were visualized using anti-mouse and anti-rabbit secondary antibodies and an ECL chemiluminescent substrate, followed by autoradiography. The band intensity was analyzed using ImageJ.

Statistical analysis

R (version 4.2.1) packages TwoSampleMR (version 0.5.6) were used for MR analyses. A MR-PRESSO analysis was performed using the MR-PRESSO package. The phenoscanner V2 package was used to remove confounders.

The experimental data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (±S) and analyzed using SPSS 23.0 software. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Graphical representations of the results were generated using GraphPad Prism 8 software.

Results

Identification of genetic instruments

The number of SNPs utilized for each pqtl and eqtl ranged from 1 to 68. All the SNPs had a minimum F-statistic value greater than 10, indicating the absence of weak instrumental variants. Table S1, S5 showed the SNPs for exposure that are causally related to the outcome measures.

Mendelian randomization showed a strong correlation between TCL1A and AML

A total of 88 plasma proteins have been found to be associated with AML. Among these, the top five proteins showing the most significant correlation with AML (P < 5 × 10−3) include TCL1 family AKT coactivator A (TCL1A), Adhesion G protein-coupled receptor F5 (ADGRF5), Fibronectin leucine-rich transmembrane protein 2 (FLRT2), GRB2-related adaptor protein 2 (GRAP2), and Peptidylprolyl isomerase C (PPIC) (Tables S1-S4). Specifically, elevated levels of TCL1A (OR: 1.66; 95% CI: 1.46–1.88 per 1-SD higher concentration), FLRT2 (OR: 2.47; 95% CI: 1.41–4.32), GRAP2 (OR: 8.70; 95% CI: 2.28–33.28), and PPIC (OR: 1.96; 95% CI: 1.27–3.03) increased the risk of AML, while increased levels of ADGRF5 (OR: 0.71; 95% CI: 0.58–0.87) decreased the risk of AML (Fig. 2A, B). Among these, TCL1A was the most strongly associated with AML (Fig. 2C).

TCL1A-eqtl showed a significant positive association with AML

TCL1A is located on chromosome 14, at position 96176284–96180462 (Fig. 3A). We extracted 19 genome-wide significant SNPs as IVs after removing palindromic and ambiguous SNPs, SNPs without proxies, and SNPs in the wrong causal direction identified by the MR Steiger filter. MR-PRESSO analysis did not detect any SNP outliers (Table S5). The intercept term of the MR-Egger regression was 0.074, with a corresponding P value of 0.172, indicating no pleiotropy among the included SNPs (Table S6). The IVW analysis revealed a positive correlation between TCL1A-eQTL and AML (P = 1.699 × 10−6, OR = 2.080, 95% CI: 1.541–2.808). The effect estimates from the four models (MR-Egger, weighted median, weighted mode, and simple mode) were consistent with this finding (Fig. 3B, C, Table S7). When applying the leave-one-out method to remove each SNP individually, all tests demonstrated effect sizes and their 95% confidence intervals to the right of the null line at 0, closely resembling the estimates obtained from the random-effects inverse-variance weighted method (Fig. 3D). These results suggest that excluding any single SNP does not significantly impact the overall findings, indicating the robustness of our Mendelian randomization study. Additionally, the funnel plot demonstrated substantial symmetry among all included SNPs. Both IVW and MR-Egger regression analyses yielded Cochran’s Q statistics of 23.099 (P = 0.146) and 25.857 (P = 0.103), respectively, indicating no heterogeneity among the SNPs (Fig. 3E, Table S8). Bayesian co-localization strongly suggested that TCL1A (coloc.abf-PPH4 = 0.999) shared the same variant (rs78986913) with AML (Fig. 3F, Table S9).

High TCL1A expression implies a poor prognosis in AML

Our study revealed that ROC analysis showed TCL1A had an AUC ROC of 0.985 (95% CI: 0.973–0.996), indicating excellent diagnostic potential (Fig. 4A). The expression levels of TCL1A were significantly elevated in AML patients compared to the normal group (Fig. 4B). Furthermore, within the AML cohort, males exhibited significantly lower TCL1A expression levels compared to females. The expression of TCL1A in patients was significantly correlated with their WBC count, BM blasts and FLT3 mutation. There were no statistically significant differences in age, PB blasts, Cytogenetic risk, FAB classifications, Cytogenetics, RAS mutation, IDH1 R132 mutation, or NPM1 mutation in AML patients (Fig. 4C-O).

The TCL1A expression is highly tissue-specific

We observed that mutations in TCL1A are present in a minority of AML patient samples, with no significant predominance of any major genetic alterations (Fig. 6A). Data from the HPA database showed that TCL1A exhibits high tissue specificity, almost exclusively expressed in lymph nodes, tonsils, spleen, appendix, bone marrow, and other related tissues (Fig. 6B). Furthermore, cell lines enriched with TCL1A RNA primarily included leukemia and lymphoma cells (Fig. 6C). TCL1A localization in cells is evident in the endoplasmic reticulum, cytoplasm, and nuclear matrix, with no overlap with microtubules. Notably, the expression of TCL1A in REH cells (leukemic cells) is significantly higher compared to A-431 (human skin cancer cells) and U2OS (human osteosarcoma cells) (Fig. 6D).

Novel discovery of potential therapeutic agents for TCL1A

A total of 1,620,219 small molecules were generated through conformational sampling, resulting in 2,585,846 distinct conformations. Following HTVS, the top 10% of conformers were selected, yielding 257,291 small molecules. Further refinement using SP docking narrowed the selection to the top 10%, resulting in 24,199 candidates. Finally, XP docking was employed, and the top 5% were selected, leading to 1,267 small molecules, which were then ranked based on their docking scores (Fig. 7A, B). Among the virtually screened compounds, 133 small molecules exhibited docking scores below −7, 1,149 had no PAINS structures, and 806 had QED scores greater than 0.4. A total of 14 compounds satisfied all three criteria (Fig. 7C).

Comparative TCL1A expression in AML and other cell lines and the molecular dynamics and biological effects of Bletilloside A

Our study revealed that all of the human leukemia cell lines MOLM-13 (2.02 ± 0.28), MV4-11 (23.73 ± 1.45), THP-1 (35.98 ± 5.16), KG-1a (12.77 ± 4.88), SKM-1 (12.48 ± 1.18), HL-60 (2.65 ± 0.46), NB4 (2.25 ± 0.46), SKNO-1 (239.38 ± 30.83), NOMO-1 (41.13 ± 11.30), and MONO-MAC-1 (22.32 ± 3.94) showed higher TCL1A levels compared with the human bone marrow stromal cell line HS-5 (1.03 ± 0.25) (Fig. 6A). Notably, most of the human leukemia cell lines (including MV4-11, THP-1, KG-1a, SKM-1, SKNO-1, NOMO-1, and MONO-MAC-1) showed higher TCL1A levels compared with solid tumor cell lines HT29 (0.18 ± 0.05), SW480 (1.08 ± 0.31), HCT116 (2.82 ± 0.53), MDA-MB-231 (2.70 ± 0.41), and A-549 (0.65 ± 0.11) (Fig. 9A).

Bletilloside A (id number: 2292159–89–6), a novel polysaccharide first discovered in the tubers of Bletilla striata [28], has various health benefits, including antitumor effects [29]. To elucidate the stability of protein–ligand interactions and the flexibility of the protein structure within the Bletilloside A-TCL1A complex, a 50 ns MD simulation was conducted using GROMACS 2022. The RMSD represents the sum of all atomic deviations of the target conformation at a specific time and serves as a key indicator of system stability. The RMSD results indicate that smaller deviations throughout the simulation process suggest better stability (Fig. 9B). The Rg predicts the compactness of the receptor structure after simulation, demonstrating that both the Bletilloside A-TCL1A complexes maintain a compact protein structure (Fig. 9C). The SASA reflects the area of the protein surface exposed to the solvent, and the results indicate that the contact area between Bletilloside A and TCL1A remains relatively stable (Fig. 9D). The Hbond results show that, in most cases, the complex has at least two hydrogen bonds, indicating favorable hydrogen bond interactions between Bletilloside A and TCL1A (Fig. 9E). The RMSF can represent the flexibility magnitude of amino acid residues in the protein. The RMSF values for Bletilloside A and TCL1A are relatively low (mostly below 2 Å), suggesting low flexibility and high stability (Fig. 9F).

In addition, we performed a CCK-8 assay to evaluate cell viability and found that Bletilloside A significantly inhibited the proliferation of AML cells, with IC60 values of 29.906 μM, 36.410 μM, and 89.395 μM for SKNO-1, MV4-11, and HL60 cells, respectively (Fig. 9G-I). Furthermore, we observed that Bletilloside A effectively inhibited the expression of the TCL1A protein in SKNO-1 and HL60 cells (Fig. 9J, K).

Discussion

AML is the most common leukemia in both adults and children [30]. Over the past decades, intensive chemotherapy and hematopoietic stem cell transplant (HSCT) have been the main treatments, with allo-HSCT being considered the only curative method. With the advent of targeted and precision treatments, the prognosis of acute promyelocytic leukemia (APL) and chronic myeloid leukemia (CML) has improved dramatically [31, 32]. In recent years, several targeted therapies have been approved for AML, and some are still under investigation, but none have provided a complete cure [33–35]. Many clinical issues remain, including the need to explore more specific targets and inhibitors.

In this study, we found that TCL1A expression showed a strong positive correlation with AML in both the pQTL and TCL1A-eQTL datasets. The TCL1 gene family is known to bind and enhance AKT kinase activity, promoting cell proliferation. TCL1 genes are expressed during early embryogenesis, in specific tissues, and in immature T and B lymphocytes [36]. Chromosome rearrangements leading to abnormal activation of TCL1A are considered major genomic abnormalities [10]. TCL1A acts as an AKT kinase co-activator, promoting its nuclear transport, which leads to dysregulated cell metabolism, proliferation, and apoptosis, provoking various neoplasms such as lymphoma [37, 38]. TCL1A is an important oncogene in the initiation of T-prolymphocytic leukemia due to rearrangements in the TCL1 region [39]. However, few studies have explored the role of TCL1A in AML. Bayesian co-localization analysis showed that rs78986913 was the same variant in both TCL1A and AML, indicating a strong association. Previous studies have also found that higher levels of TCL1A in plasma are associated with clonal hematopoiesis (CH), and the eQTL and pQTL related to CH may be limited to myeloid cells, which is consistent with our research. However, rs78986913 did not show an independent signal for the susceptibility to CH (Padj = 0.78) [40]. This SNP may not function independently but rather act as a genetic modifier, whose functional effects at this locus are either exposed or amplified within the specific bone marrow microenvironment, thereby contributing to the pathogenesis of AML. Bioinformatics analysis revealed that TCL1A expression levels were significantly different in the bone marrow of healthy individuals compared to AML patients. In patients with FLT3 mutations, TCL1A expression is significantly lower than that in the wild-type group. A possible explanation is that FLT3-ITD mutations lead to ligand-independent autophosphorylation of the FLT3 receptor, subsequently activating the downstream PI3K/AKT signaling pathway [41, 42]. This activation results in negative feedback inhibition of TCL1A expression. High TCL1A expression correlates with more aggressive disease characteristics and poor prognosis. Moreover, we found that TCL1A expression, whether high or low, was consistent with OS when AML patients were stratified by karyotype or molecular risk classification. These results suggest that TCL1A expression can predict AML progression and OS. Notably, in patients with FAB subtypes M0, M1 and M2, high expression of TCL1A was associated with worse prognosis in AML patients. Previous articles have found that TCL1A was not expressed in normal hematopoietic stem cells [43]. In poorly differentiated subtypes (M0/M1/M2), TCL1A may promote the maintenance of leukemia stem cell quiescence through certain pathways (such as AKT), thereby preventing differentiation and enhancing self-renewal. Although TCL1A mutations were rare in AML patient samples, matching the low mutation frequency typically seen in AML [44], this differs from T-cell prolymphocytic leukemia, which is highly associated with TCL1A rearrangements [39, 45].

These findings prompted further investigation using the HPA database and cell samples. We found that TCL1A is specifically expressed in leukemia and lymphoma cells. IHC results showed significantly higher TCL1A expression in leukemic cell lines compared to other tumors. RT-qPCR analysis also showed abnormally elevated TCL1A levels in AML cell lines compared to HS-5 and other cancer cell lines, suggesting that TCL1A is a potential therapeutic target for AML.

Currently, there are no commercially available TCL1A inhibitors. We conducted a structure-based HTVS of 1,620,219 compounds to identify potential TCL1A inhibitors. From this screening, 10 small molecule compounds emerged as potential drug targets. After extensive literature research, we found that Bletilloside A (id number: 2292159–89–6) was the most likely compound to bind with TCL1A. Bletilloside A has various health benefits, including hemostatic [46], immunomodulatory [47, 48], anti-inflammatory [49], antibacterial [50], and antitumor effects [29]. The functions of Bletilla striata polysaccharides (BSPs) align with the symptoms of AML. Moreover, BSPs have shown effectiveness in treating lung cancer [51] and hepatocellular carcinoma by enhancing immune cell activity and lymphocyte subset proportions [29]. Previous studies also found that Bletilla striata and its extracts can induce apoptosis in AML cells and exhibit antileukemic effects [52]. In this study, we used MD simulations to elucidate the stability of protein–ligand interactions and the flexibility of protein structure in the Bletilloside A-TCL1A complex. Furthermore, we found that Bletilloside A could significantly inhibit the proliferation of AML cells by suppressing the expression of TCL1A. Given its symptom-relief capabilities in AML and potential to suppress TCL1A, Bletilloside A is a promising candidate for drug development.

However, our study has several limitations. First, our analysis was performed using the European population, which lacks gender classification. Second, this study did not conduct a more in-depth study of TCL1A downstream Third, our study need to further expand the sample size for clinical verification.

In conclusion, our study has established a causal relationship between TCL1A and AML, revealing that abnormal TCL1A levels directly influence AML progression and overall survival rates, suggesting its potential as a diagnostic criterion for AML. Clinical data analyses indicate that AML patients with increased TCL1A levels should receive closer monitoring and additional care to delay disease progression. We believe that TCL1A could be a significant biomarker for prognostic evaluation in AML patients and a promising therapeutic target for improving AML clinical outcomes. Furthermore, Bletilloside A appears to be a promising therapeutic drug, warranting further research to elucidate its mechanism of action.
