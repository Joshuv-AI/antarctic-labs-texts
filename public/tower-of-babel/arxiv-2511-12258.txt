Transitional Bell Correlation from Dirac Wavepackets
Ju Gao, Fang Shen

Abstract
We derive a closed-form expression for the Bell–CHSH correlation of entangled, counter-propagating electrons using realistic Dirac wavepackets and localized detection. In contrast to the conventional distance-independent result, the Bell parameter evolves continuously from the quantum bound 2 ​ 2 2\sqrt{2} to the classical limit 2 2 as the spatial overlap of the two waves decreases. The quantum enhancement arises entirely from transverse overlap, showing that the Bell violation reflects the local overlap of propagating Dirac waves rather than any action at a distance.



I
Introduction

Bell’s theorem quantifies the limits of correlations allowed by any local realistic theory
1
;
5
. Its experimental violation has long been interpreted as evidence that nature cannot be both local and realistic
2
;
7
. In standard analyses, however, the problem is formulated entirely in a Hilbert space of spin states: the spatial structure and dynamical evolution of the quantum state are excluded by construction. As a result, the predicted correlations are independent of distance and the Bell parameter remains fixed at the quantum maximum
|\mathcal{B}|=2\sqrt{2}

4
, even for arbitrarily large detector separations.

This abstraction removes the physical ingredients—wavepacket propagation, finite detector size, and spatial overlap—that govern how real measurements take place. A detector interacts only with the portion of the quantum field that actually reaches it in space and time, so measurement is a local sampling process rather than a global projection on idealized subsystems. To reflect this physical structure, we describe each electron as a spatially extended Dirac wavepacket
6
;
9
;
8
;
11
whose charge, current, and spin densities evolve continuously in space. The entangled pair is then represented by their full joint wavefunction, and detection is modeled through window functions
W_{A}(\mathbf{r}_{1})
and
W_{B}(\mathbf{r}_{2})
that restrict observables to each detector’s active region, yielding the measurable correlator

C(\hat{\bm{a}},\hat{\bm{b}};t)=\frac{\displaystyle\iint W_{A}W_{B}\,\Psi^{\dagger}(\hat{\bm{a}}\!\cdot\!\bm{\Sigma}_{1})(\hat{\bm{b}}\!\cdot\!\bm{\Sigma}_{2})\Psi\,\mathrm{d}^{3}r_{1}\,\mathrm{d}^{3}r_{2}}{\displaystyle\iint W_{A}W_{B}\,\Psi^{\dagger}\Psi\,\mathrm{d}^{3}r_{1}\,\mathrm{d}^{3}r_{2}}.

(1)

This formulation retains the standard spin operators but restores the physical space in which the entangled state propagates. The resulting Bell correlation becomes a continuous function of the spatial overlap of the two electrons, rather than a fixed constant. In the limit of full overlap, the Bell–CHSH parameter approaches
2\sqrt{2}
, reproducing the conventional quantum prediction; when the overlap vanishes, it returns to the classical bound
2
.

By restoring the spatial structure of the entangled electrons and the locality of detection, the present analysis shows how the Bell correlation is governed by the physical traits of entanglement itself. In this setting, the correlation arises locally and continuously from the overlap of the two spatially extended waves, without invoking any nonlocal mechanism. A similar spatially resolved wave–entity description was recently applied to the Aharonov–Bohm effect, yielding the observable energy shift within the same local framework
10
. This spatially resolved treatment also complements efforts to model solid-state spin qubits
14
, where locality and decoherence hinge on the spatial structure of the underlying wavefunctions.

II
Construction of the Dirac Wavepacket

The free Dirac equation in vacuum is

i\hbar\,\partial_{t}\Psi(\bm{r},t)=\left[-\,i\hbar c\,\bm{\alpha}\!\cdot\!\nabla+mc^{2}\gamma^{0}\right]\Psi(\bm{r},t),

(2)

with
\bm{\alpha}\!\cdot\!\nabla=\alpha_{x}\partial_{x}+\alpha_{y}\partial_{y}+\alpha_{z}\partial_{z}
.
Its positive–energy plane–wave eigenstates take the form

\Psi_{\bm{P},s}(\bm{r},t)=e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}(\bm{P}\cdot\bm{r}-\mathcal{E}t)}\,u_{s}(\bm{P}),

(3)

where
s=\uparrow,\downarrow
labels the spin projection and
\mathcal{E}=\sqrt{m^{2}c^{4}+P^{2}c^{2}}\simeq mc^{2}+P^{2}/(2m)
for
P\!\ll\!mc
. Explicit expressions for the four–component spinors
u_{s}(\bm{P})
are standard and given in the Supplemental Material.

To obtain a localized electron in real space, we superpose these plane waves with a Gaussian momentum distribution centered at
\bm{P}_{0}
,

F(\bm{P},\bm{P}_{0})=(2\pi d^{2})^{3/2}\exp\!\left[-\frac{d^{2}|\bm{P}-\bm{P}_{0}|^{2}}{2\hbar^{2}}\right],

(4)

normalized so that
(2\pi\hbar)^{-3}\!\int d^{3}P\,F(\bm{P},\bm{P}_{0})=1.

The parameter
d
sets the rms spatial width at
t=0
. The corresponding wavepacket is

\Psi_{s}(\bm{r},t;\bm{P}_{0})=(2\pi\hbar)^{-\frac{3}{2}}\!\int d^{3}P\,F(\bm{P},\bm{P}_{0})\,u_{s}(\bm{P})\,e^{\frac{i}{\hbar}(\bm{P}\cdot\bm{r}-\mathcal{E}t)}.

(5)

Carrying out the Gaussian momentum integral yields the closed expression

\begin{split}\Psi_{s}(\bm{r},t;\bm{P}_{0})=\;\mathcal{N}\,e^{-imc^{2}t/\hbar}\exp\!\left[\frac{-r^{2}+2id^{2}\bm{P}_{0}\!\cdot\!\bm{r}/\hbar}{2(d^{2}+iL^{2})}\right]\\[2.0pt]
\times\exp\!\left[-\,i\frac{d^{2}P_{0}^{2}L^{2}}{2\hbar^{2}(d^{2}+iL^{2})}\right]u_{s}(\bm{r};\bm{P}_{0}),\end{split}

(6)

with normalization

\mathcal{N}=\left(\frac{d}{\sqrt{\pi}(d^{2}+iL^{2})}\right)^{3/2},\qquad L^{2}=\frac{\hbar t}{m}.

The parameter
L
is the quantum diffusion length: the spatial width expands from
d
at
t=0
to
\sqrt{d^{2}+L^{2}}
at time
t
. The position–dependent spinor
u_{s}(\bm{r};\bm{P}_{0})
results from the Gaussian
integration over momentum of
u_{s}(\bm{P})
and is listed explicitly in the Supplemental Material.

III
Longitudinal Entanglement of the Dirac Wavepackets

To analyze locality using spatially extended electrons, we adopt the
longitudinal configuration
, where the spin quantization axis is chosen along the propagation direction. For free Dirac wavepackets in this geometry, the intrinsic circulating current that encodes the
z
–spin lies entirely in the transverse
(x,y)
plane and is carried forward rigidly along
\hat{\bm{z}}
. Consequently the spin orientation remains stable throughout propagation, and the spin degree of freedom is well defined for each extended wavepacket in this setting.

Each electron is described by the normalized Dirac wavepacket constructed in Sec.
II
, with momentum spectra centered at
\bm{P}_{0}=\pm P\,\hat{\bm{z}}
. The antisymmetric (singlet) entangled state is

\Psi(\bm{r}_{1},\bm{r}_{2},t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\!\left[\Psi_{\uparrow}(\bm{r}_{1},t;P\hat{\bm{z}})\,\Psi_{\downarrow}(\bm{r}_{2},t;-P\hat{\bm{z}})-(1\!\leftrightarrow\!2)\right],

(7)

which satisfies the fermionic exchange condition

\Psi(\bm{r}_{1},\bm{r}_{2},t)=-\Psi(\bm{r}_{2},\bm{r}_{1},t)
.

Detectors are modeled as finite spatial regions rather than idealized global projectors. A detector located at longitudinal position
\pm Z
samples the portion of the wavefunction that reaches that location, represented by planar window functions

\displaystyle W_{A}(\bm{r}_{1})

\displaystyle=W_{A}(x_{1},y_{1})\,\delta(z_{1}-Z),

(8)

\displaystyle W_{B}(\bm{r}_{2})

\displaystyle=W_{B}(x_{2},y_{2})\,\delta(z_{2}+Z),

where
W_{A,B}(x,y)
are broad apodization functions describing the active detection areas. No assumption is made about their detailed transverse shape; the only requirement is that the detectors are finite and localized
15
. The delta functions idealize the detectors as infinitesimally thin in the longitudinal direction; any finite thickness would contribute an overall factor that cancels in the normalized correlator, so the
\delta(z\!\mp\!Z)
form is sufficient for describing the measurable correlation. We work in the laboratory frame for clarity. The detector spacing
2Z
is always understood relative to the initial wavepacket width
d
, and the overlap–controlled correlation has the same physical meaning in any inertial frame.

The detection time
T
is defined by the arrival of the wavepacket peaks at each detector,
T=Z/v_{g}
, with
v_{g}=P/m
the group velocity. Thus the quantum diffusion length satisfies
L^{2}=\hbar Z/P
.

Evaluated at
t=T
, the two–electron wavefunction becomes

\displaystyle\Psi(\bm{r}_{1},\bm{r}_{2};T)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,\mathcal{N}^{2}\,e^{-\,i\frac{2\hbar Z}{\lambda_{c}^{2}P}}e^{\frac{-(x_{1}^{2}+y_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+y_{2}^{2}+2Z^{2})+2id^{2}PZ/\hbar}{2\left(d^{2}+i\,\hbar Z/P\right)}}

\displaystyle\times\Big[u_{\uparrow}(\bm{r}_{1})\,u_{\downarrow}(\bm{r}_{2})-e^{\frac{-4i\,d^{2}PZ/\hbar}{d^{2}+i\,\hbar Z/P}}u_{\uparrow}(\bm{r}_{2})\,u_{\downarrow}(\bm{r}_{1})\Big],

(9)

with

\mathcal{N}=\left(\frac{d}{\sqrt{\pi}\,\bigl(d^{2}+i\,\hbar Z/P\bigr)}\right)^{3/2}.

The wavepacket construction produces position–dependent four–component spinors,

\displaystyle u_{\uparrow}(\bm{r}_{j})

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1\\[2.0pt]
0\\[2.0pt]
e^{i\tfrac{\pi}{4}}\tfrac{\lambda_{c}}{2}\tfrac{iz_{j}+d^{2}P/\hbar}{d^{2}+i\,\hbar Z/P}\\[6.0pt]
e^{i\tfrac{\pi}{4}}\tfrac{\lambda_{c}}{2}\tfrac{ix_{j}-y_{j}}{d^{2}+i\,\hbar Z/P}\end{pmatrix},

(10)

\displaystyle u_{\downarrow}(\bm{r}_{j})

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0\\[2.0pt]
1\\[2.0pt]
e^{i\tfrac{\pi}{4}}\tfrac{\lambda_{c}}{2}\tfrac{ix_{j}+y_{j}}{d^{2}+i\,\hbar Z/P}\\[6.0pt]
e^{i\tfrac{\pi}{4}}\tfrac{\lambda_{c}}{2}\tfrac{-iz_{j}-d^{2}P/\hbar}{d^{2}+i\,\hbar Z/P}\end{pmatrix},

(11)

where
\lambda_{c}=\hbar/(mc)
is the Compton wavelength.

Thus, the entangled singlet state of Eq.
III
is obtained entirely within the Dirac framework, using spatially extended wavepackets together with finite, localized detectors.

IV
Bell Correlation of the Entangled Dirac Wavepackets

We evaluate the conventional Bell spin–spin correlator for the entangled singlet of realistic Dirac wavepackets, Eq. (
III
), together with the detector windows of Eq. (
8
), allowing direct comparison with standard results
(
3
)
. Although our planar
detectors do not implement a full Stern–Gerlach interaction along arbitrary transverse axes, the correlator

C(\hat{\bm{a}},\hat{\bm{b}};T)=\frac{\displaystyle\iint W_{A}W_{B}\,\Psi^{\dagger}(\hat{\bm{a}}\!\cdot\!\bm{\Sigma}_{1})(\hat{\bm{b}}\!\cdot\!\bm{\Sigma}_{2})\Psi\,d^{3}\bm{r}_{1}\,d^{3}\bm{r}_{2}}{\displaystyle\iint W_{A}W_{B}\,\Psi^{\dagger}\Psi\,d^{3}\bm{r}_{1}\,d^{3}\bm{r}_{2}}

(12)

serves as the baseline formula within the standard spin–operator framework. Here
(\hat{\bm{a}},\hat{\bm{b}})
denote the nominal analyzer directions, introduced through the local coupling to the spin field. The denominator of
Eq. (
12
) provides the proper normalization of the wave amplitude weighted by the same window functions.

Inserting the longitudinal singlet of
Eq. (
III
) and performing the Gaussian integrals (see
Supplemental Material) yields

\displaystyle C(\hat{\bm{a}},\hat{\bm{b}};Z)=-a_{z}b_{z}

\displaystyle-\frac{2e^{-\tfrac{4d^{2}Z^{2}}{d^{4}+\hbar^{2}Z^{2}/P^{2}}}}{1+e^{-\tfrac{8d^{2}Z^{2}}{d^{4}+\hbar^{2}Z^{2}/P^{2}}}}\Bigl[\cos\!\Bigl(\tfrac{4d^{4}PZ/\hbar}{d^{4}+\hbar^{2}Z^{2}/P^{2}}\Bigr)(a_{x}b_{x}+a_{y}b_{y})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\,\,\,\,+\sin\!\Bigl(\tfrac{4d^{4}PZ/\hbar}{d^{4}+\hbar^{2}Z^{2}/P^{2}}\Bigr)(a_{x}b_{y}-a_{y}b_{x})\Bigr].

(13)

Introducing the dimensionless variables

\zeta=\frac{Z}{d},\qquad\kappa=\frac{Pd}{\hbar},

(14)

where
\zeta
measures detector separation in units of
d
and
\kappa
compares directed propagation (
P
) to quantum diffusion (
\hbar/d
), gives

\displaystyle C(\hat{\bm{a}},\hat{\bm{b}};\zeta)=-a_{z}b_{z}-\frac{2e^{-\tfrac{4\kappa^{2}\zeta^{2}}{\kappa^{2}+\zeta^{2}}}}{1+e^{-\,\tfrac{8\kappa^{2}\zeta^{2}}{\kappa^{2}+\zeta^{2}}}}

\displaystyle\times\Bigl[\cos\!\Bigl(\tfrac{4\kappa^{3}\zeta}{\kappa^{2}+\zeta^{2}}\Bigr)(a_{x}b_{x}+a_{y}b_{y})+\sin\!\Bigl(\tfrac{4\kappa^{3}\zeta}{\kappa^{2}+\zeta^{2}}\Bigr)(a_{x}b_{y}-a_{y}b_{x})\Bigr],

(15)

with limiting behavior

C(\hat{\bm{a}},\hat{\bm{b}};\zeta)=\begin{cases}-\,a_{x}b_{x}-a_{y}b_{y}-a_{z}b_{z},&\zeta=0,\\[4.0pt]
-\,a_{z}b_{z}-\sech(4\kappa^{2})(a_{x}b_{x}+a_{y}b_{y}),&\zeta\to\infty.\end{cases}

(16)

To quantify the transition in Eq. (
IV
), we evaluate the CHSH parameter

\mathcal{B}(\zeta)=C(\hat{\bm{a}},\hat{\bm{b}};\zeta)+C(\hat{\bm{a}},\hat{\bm{b}}^{\prime};\zeta)+C(\hat{\bm{a}}^{\prime},\hat{\bm{b}};\zeta)-C(\hat{\bm{a}}^{\prime},\hat{\bm{b}}^{\prime};\zeta),

(17)

using the standard analyzer settings
13

\hat{\bm{a}}=(0,0,1),\;\hat{\bm{a}}^{\prime}=(1,0,0),\;\hat{\bm{b}}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1,0,1),\;\hat{\bm{b}}^{\prime}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(-1,0,1).

The resulting
transitional Bell parameter
is

\mathcal{B}(\zeta;\kappa)=-\sqrt{2}\!\left[1+\sech\!\Bigl(\tfrac{4\kappa^{2}\zeta^{2}}{\kappa^{2}+\zeta^{2}}\Bigr)\cos\!\Bigl(\tfrac{4\kappa^{3}\zeta}{\kappa^{2}+\zeta^{2}}\Bigr)\right],

(18)

which encapsulates the continuous transition from the quantum to the classical correlation regime.

We next introduce two quantities that separate the geometric and dynamical structure of the Bell correlator: the transverse overlap factor

F_{\perp}(\zeta,\kappa)=\sech\!\Bigl(\tfrac{4\kappa^{2}\zeta^{2}}{\kappa^{2}+\zeta^{2}}\Bigr),

(19)

which quantifies the diminishing transverse overlap, and the longitudinal cross–phase

\Phi_{\parallel}(\zeta,\kappa)=\frac{4\kappa^{3}\zeta}{\kappa^{2}+\zeta^{2}},

(20)

which encodes the longitudinal phase difference between the two counter–propagating components of the antisymmetric singlet.

With these definitions,

\mathcal{B}(\zeta;\kappa)=-\sqrt{2}\,\bigl[1+F_{\perp}(\zeta,\kappa)\cos\Phi_{\parallel}(\zeta,\kappa)\bigr].

At full spatial overlap,

|\mathcal{B}(0;\kappa)|=2\sqrt{2},

(21)

recovering the maximal quantum violation.

As the packets separate, the longitudinal cross–phase tends to zero and the transverse overlap approaches a finite residue. In the limit

\zeta\to\infty
,

|\mathcal{B}(\infty;\kappa)|=\sqrt{2}\,\bigl[1+\sech(4\kappa^{2})\bigr].

(22)

A critical threshold value,

\kappa_{\ast}=\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{\operatorname{arcosh}\!\bigl((\sqrt{2}-1)^{-1}\bigr)}\approx 0.618,

(23)

marks two distinct dynamical regimes. For
\kappa\geq\kappa_{\ast}
, directed propagation dominates; overlap decays rapidly and
|\mathcal{B}(\zeta;\kappa)|
drops into the classical range once the spatial
separation exceeds the initial wavepacket width. For
\kappa<\kappa_{\ast}
, diffusion maintains substantial overlap over a broader range of separations, yielding a reduced but persistent violation with
|\mathcal{B}(\zeta;\kappa)|>2
.

V
Conclusion

We have derived a closed–form expression for the transitional Bell parameter
\mathcal{B}(\zeta;\kappa)
of entangled electrons using realistic Dirac wavepackets and finite detection windows. Unlike the conventional,
distance–independent Bell parameter
4
,

\mathcal{B}(\zeta;\kappa)
evolves continuously from the quantum bound to the classical limit as the spatial overlap of the two electron waves diminishes.
A threshold
\kappa_{\ast}\!\approx\!0.618
emerges naturally: for
\kappa\!\geq\!\kappa_{\ast}
, Bell violation with
|\mathcal{B}(\zeta;\kappa)|>2
is confined to a narrow region of strong overlap and then relaxes rapidly to the classical value as the electrons separate. In contrast, for
\kappa<\kappa_{\ast}
quantum diffusion maintains substantial overlap over a broader range of separations, yielding a reduced but persistent violation with
|\mathcal{B}(\zeta;\kappa)|>2
.

Moreover, the measurable correlation is not unique: its magnitude depends on which physical quantity the detector couples to. If detection is sensitive to the physical current density rather than to spin operators, the Bell violation can be strongly suppressed or even vanish. This measurement dependence will be explored in a companion study, where current–based detection is shown to yield
purely local correlations even within the quantum regime.

Finally, we note that a similar wave overlap-based mechanism may arise in photonic Bell tests, where narrowband sources generate extremely long coherence lengths
12
. Assessing this possibility requires a full Maxwell-wavepacket analysis, which lies beyond the scope of the present work.
