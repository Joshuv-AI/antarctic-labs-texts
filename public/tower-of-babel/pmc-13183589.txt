Exploring sex differences in bvFTD through clinician insights
Authors: Sterre C M de Boer, Xulin Liu, Carmela Tartaglia
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

To complement recent findings related to sex differences in frontotemporal dementia (FTD), 1 , 2 , 3 we conducted an online survey among clinicians who regularly diagnose or care for patients with behavioral variant FTD (bvFTD). We gathered their insights on three topics: (1) Why an unequal sex distribution is observed in sporadic bvFTD, 4 (2) whether there are any sex differences in symptom presentation in bvFTD, and (3) exploring potential reasons for a drop in the percentage of female participants in familial FTD studies once they convert from the presymptomatic to symptomatic stage.

The survey was designed using Google Forms and was distributed among members of numerous FTD‐related consortia, including Genetic Frontotemporal dementia Initiative (GENFI), The ARTFL‐LEFFTDS Longitudinal Frontotemporal Lobar Degeneration (ALLFTD), and Neuropsychiatric International Consortium (NIC‐FTD) between February 2024 and February 2025. A total of 38 clinicians (across 19 countries) responded, making an average of 23 bvFTD diagnoses a year.

First, clinicians were asked to estimate the percentage of genetic versus sporadic male patients diagnosed with bvFTD. Clinicians estimated that 50.0% of genetic patients are male, whereas 59.5% of sporadic bvFTD are male.

Next, we examined whether clinicians perceive differences in symptom presentation between sexes within the genetic group. Of the respondents, 18% responded “Yes.” Several clinicians hypothesized that symptoms may vary between sexes; answers provided included: “More aggression in male group” and “… women have more social cognitive reserve, and hence are for a longer time able to have “social small talk,” this may lead to a delay in diagnosis.” Another suggested that gender may affect caregiver roles: “husbands support wives in a different way to wives supporting husbands, but this is diluted by family experience of FTD.”

In cases of sporadic bvFTD, 41.0% of clinicians reported perceiving differences in symptom presentation between sex. Symptomatic differences included: “more woman get caught in online romantic scams,” “women show more compulsivity, (verbal) repetition, eating and hyperorality whereas men show more aggression, more compulsivity in physical exercise and more motor symptoms,” “more criminal behavior in men,” “more obsessions in females,” “more aggressive behavior in men,” and “female patients are more depressed and anxious.” Three clinicians reported more prevalent sexual‐related impulse control issues in males. One clinician noted that the degree to which anti‐social behaviors are considered, “medical illness” versus “cultural variation” differs between sexes, and, the degree to which wives support husbands versus husbands support wives differs between cultures. Similarly, one clinician responded that there may be a difference in types of executive dysfunction between sexes, which could be societal and culturally related. Another clinician reported that more males are referred to the clinic and more likely to have externalizing type of behaviors, whereas females with sporadic bvFTD present with a more psychiatric presentation. Finally, one clinician mentioned that the quality of the symptoms is similar between sexes but that the severity is greater in males than in females.

Clinicians were also asked an open‐ended question about any additional observations they had regarding sex differences in bvFTD. Of the 15 who responded, 4 mentioned a possible caregiver sex/gender effect when reporting complaints, illustrated by comments such as: “male caregivers may underreport behavioral symptoms” and “higher level of worry in female spouse about their husband than vice versa leads to earlier consult.” Likewise, the clinical interpretation of the complaints given and symptoms observed might also differ between sexes. Two clinicians hypothesized that females get more often misdiagnosed as a primary psychiatric disease and that attitude and risks are culturally modified for males versus females.

For the final aim of our survey, we asked clinicians why they believe the percentage of female carriers drops once symptoms emerge in familial studies like GENFI and ALLFTD. Of the 31 clinicians, the majority pointed to higher research participation motivation among females in general and the influence of caregiving roles as potential factors. Research participation is often facilitated by caregivers, and because male patients are frequently cared for by female spouses (and vice versa), this dynamic may contribute to the observed distribution shift. Three clinicians speculated there might be sex‐related protective effects or sex effects in mutation penetrance that contribute to this skewness in distribution. Finally, some clinicians raised concerns about possible biases in diagnostic criteria or clinical severity rating scales being more applicable to male bvFTD patients.

In summary, our findings highlight the importance of combining clinical observations with research on both biological and social determinants in bvFTD. Further studies are necessary to investigate this emerging and evolving topic.
