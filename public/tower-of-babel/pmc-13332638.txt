Mechanical Circulatory Support as a Bridge to Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement in Aortic Stenosis–Related Cardiogenic Shock

Patients presenting with cardiogenic shock due to severe native or bioprosthetic aortic valve disease undergo early multidisciplinary Heart Team evaluation. Temporary mechanical circulatory support is considered to stabilize hemodynamics, facilitate end-organ recovery, and enable definitive aortic valve intervention, resulting in myocardial recovery and clinical stabilization.

Cardiogenic shock complicating severe aortic stenosis represents a critical clinical challenge with limited therapeutic options and high short-term mortality. Fixed left ventricular outflow obstruction, impaired forward flow, and secondary myocardial dysfunction often preclude immediate definitive valve intervention, while delays in treatment are associated with poor outcomes.1,2

Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) has emerged as a viable therapy in high-risk and inoperable patients; however, the optimal management strategy for patients presenting in active cardiogenic shock remains uncertain.3,4 Temporary mechanical circulatory support (MCS) may provide hemodynamic stabilization, improve end-organ perfusion, and allow recovery of myocardial function, thereby facilitating definitive valve intervention in selected patients.2,5

Case Presentations

Case 1

A 64-year-old woman with atrial fibrillation, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, polysubstance use disorder, and prior infective endocarditis status post surgical aortic valve replacement with a 25-mm Magna Ease (Edwards Lifesciences) bioprosthetic valve 10 years earlier presented with progressive dyspnea. She was hypotensive on presentation and required intravenous inotropic support.

Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated severely reduced left ventricular systolic function with an estimated ejection fraction (LVEF) of 15% and severe bioprosthetic aortic valve stenosis with a mean gradient of 46 mm Hg, a peak gradient of 81 mm Hg, and a valve area of 0.6 cm2. Coronary angiography revealed nonobstructive disease. Right heart catheterization showed a cardiac output (CO) of 2.8 L/min with a cardiac index (CI) of 1.5 L/min/m2. Laboratory testing revealed elevated serum lactate consistent with cardiogenic shock.

After multidisciplinary Heart Team discussion, temporary MCS was initiated with TandemHeart (LivaNova) under conscious sedation and use of intracardiac echocardiography for placement of the inflow cannula in the left atrium. The patient demonstrated rapid hemodynamic improvement with normalization of end-organ perfusion.

Three days later, she underwent a successful valve-in-valve TAVR using a 26-mm Sapien S3 Resilia valve (Edwards Lifesciences), with bioprosthetic valve fracture performed using a 26-mm True Balloon (BARD Peripheral Vascular Inc)6. Mechanical support was discontinued the following day. Intracardiac echocardiography during decannulation revealed a small iatrogenic atrial septal defect with left-to-right shunting, which did not require closure.

The patient was ambulated within 4 hours of decannulation and discharged home on guideline-directed medical therapy. Follow-up echocardiography demonstrated improvement in LVEF to 40% and a mean transvalvular gradient of 5 mm Hg.

Case 2

A 44-year-old woman with severe Takayasu arteritis on chronic corticosteroids, systemic lupus erythematosus, recurrent pericardial effusions status post pericardial window, a dilated aortic root (4.6 cm), atrial fibrillation, and prior bioprosthetic aortic valve replacement with a 23-mm Perimount valve (Edwards Lifesciences) 13 years earlier presented with progressive dyspnea, hypotension, and decompensated heart failure.

Initial laboratory testing demonstrated acute kidney injury and mild lactate elevation. Right heart catheterization showed markedly elevated filling pressures with a CO of 2.5 L/min and a CI of 1.6 L/min/m2. Echocardiography demonstrated an LVEF of 20% with severe bioprosthetic aortic regurgitation and moderate stenosis (mean gradient 38 mm Hg and peak gradient 74 mm Hg).

After Heart Team discussion, MCS was initiated with the use of TandemHeart (LivaNova) under conscious sedation and the use of intracardiac echocardiography for inflow cannula placement in the left atrium. The patient's hemodynamics and end-organ function improved significantly. Two days later, she underwent TAVR with a 26-mm Evolut FX valve (Medtronic), followed by postdilation using a 24-mm True Balloon (BARD Peripheral Vascular Inc).

During the procedure, retrograde valve crossing was unsuccessful. An antegrade approach was performed by advancing a guide catheter and wire from the left femoral vein through the transseptal access, across the mitral valve and aortic valve, which was then snared in the ascending aorta to establish an arteriovenous rail (Figure 1). This was followed by successful valve deployment. Mechanical support was weaned during the procedure with continuous hemodynamic monitoring.

Gore and Associates). The patient was discharged home 7 days later on guideline-directed medical therapy. At 1-year follow-up, echocardiography demonstrated LVEF recovery to 50% with a mean transvalvular gradient of 8 mm Hg.

Case 3

A 77-year-old man with hypertension and heparin-induced thrombocytopenia presented with acute respiratory distress in the setting of an active gastrointestinal bleed. He was hypotensive on arrival, requiring multiple vasopressors. Laboratory evaluation revealed severe anemia with a hemoglobin level of 6.3 g/dL, prompting transfusion of multiple units of packed red blood cells.

Despite resuscitation, he developed progressive respiratory failure requiring endotracheal intubation. Echocardiography demonstrated an LVEF of 20% with low-flow, low-gradient severe aortic stenosis (mean gradient 27 mm Hg and valve area 0.47 cm2). Coronary angiography showed nonobstructive disease. Right heart catheterization revealed a CO of 3.8 L/min and a CI of 2.1 L/min/m2 while on dobutamine.

Given active bleeding and a history of heparin-induced thrombocytopenia, transseptal support requiring high-intensity anticoagulation was avoided. An Impella CP device (Abiomed; Johnson & Johnson MedTech) was placed using argatroban for anticoagulation, resulting in significant hemodynamic improvement. After 2 days of stabilization, the Impella device was removed, and TAVR was performed using a 26-mm Sapien S3 Ultra valve (Edwards Lifesciences).

Postprocedure echocardiography demonstrated improvement in LVEF to 40% with a mean transvalvular gradient of 10 mm Hg. The patient was discharged home 4 days later.

Discussion

This case series demonstrates the feasibility of using temporary MCS as a bridge to TVAR in patients presenting with cardiogenic shock due to severe native or bioprosthetic aortic valve disease (Table 1). Cardiogenic shock in this setting is driven by fixed afterload obstruction and impaired forward flow, often resulting in profound myocardial depression and multiorgan dysfunction.1,3 Contemporary observational data provide important context for this high-risk population. In a large multicenter study, Goel et al7 demonstrated that patients undergoing TAVR in the setting of cardiogenic shock experience significantly higher early mortality compared with stable TAVR cohorts, although procedural success remains favorable. These findings suggest that although definitive valve intervention is feasible, outcomes are strongly influenced by the severity of shock physiology at presentation. In this context, temporary MCS may serve as a strategy to mitigate hemodynamic instability and transition patients to valve intervention under more controlled physiologic conditions.

Device selection was individualized based on valve pathology, shock phenotype, bleeding risk, and anticoagulation considerations. Transseptal left atrial-to-arterial support provided effective unloading without traversing the stenotic aortic valve, thereby avoiding further increases in left ventricular afterload in patients with severe stenosis or mixed valve disease.5,8 In contrast, axial-flow support with Impella was favored in patients with active bleeding or heparin intolerance, as it allows stabilization with lower anticoagulation requirements and alternative anticoagulation strategies.9,10

In this series, anticoagulation protocols were tailored to device platform and patient-specific bleeding and thrombotic risk profiles. For transseptal left atrial-to-arterial support, systemic anticoagulation with intravenous unfractionated heparin was administered, targeting therapeutic activated clotting times consistent with institutional MCS protocols, with serial monitoring to mitigate thrombotic and hemorrhagic complications. In the patient with active gastrointestinal bleeding and prior heparin-induced thrombocytopenia, argatroban infusion was used during axial-flow (ie, Impella) support with dose titration guided by activated partial thromboplastin time. Device-related surveillance included serial assessment for access-site complications, limb ischemia, hemolysis (including plasma-free hemoglobin and lactate dehydrogenase levels), and adequacy of ventricular unloading. After transseptal decannulation, intracardiac echocardiography was used to evaluate residual iatrogenic atrial septal defects; small left-to-right shunts without right-sided volume overload were managed conservatively, whereas larger or bidirectional defects were closed percutaneously.

Beyond hemodynamic stabilization, MCS may facilitate complex procedural strategies. In patients with degenerated surgical bioprostheses or hostile anatomy, transseptal access can enable antegrade valve crossing and creation of stable arteriovenous rails when retrograde approaches fail.8 In addition, short-term support may permit myocardial recovery before definitive valve intervention, particularly in patients with low-flow, low-gradient aortic stenosis or shock-mediated cardiomyopathy.3,11

Alternative stabilization strategies warrant consideration. Balloon aortic valvuloplasty may provide temporary gradient reduction; however, its durability is limited, and restenosis or worsening regurgitation may occur, particularly in degenerated bioprosthetic valves. Venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation offers full cardiopulmonary support but may increase left ventricular afterload and exacerbate ventricular distension in severe aortic stenosis unless adjunctive unloading strategies are used. These considerations further underscore the importance of individualized device selection based on valve pathology and shock phenotype.

In this series, valve intervention was performed after brief periods of stabilization, allowing improvement in end-organ perfusion and reduction in vasoactive support. Timing of definitive TAVR was guided by objective markers of physiologic stabilization, including downward lactate trends or normalization, recovery of renal function, reduction or discontinuation of vasoactive agents, and improvement in CI and filling pressures on invasive monitoring. All patients demonstrated meaningful recovery of left ventricular systolic function and were discharged home, with durable improvements observed on follow-up echocardiography. These findings support the concept that myocardial dysfunction in aortic stenosis–related cardiogenic shock may be at least partially reversible when afterload obstruction is addressed in a controlled and timely manner.2,4

This report is limited by its small sample size and observational design. Larger prospective studies are needed to better define optimal patient selection, device choice, anticoagulation strategies, and timing of transcatheter valve intervention in this high-risk population.
