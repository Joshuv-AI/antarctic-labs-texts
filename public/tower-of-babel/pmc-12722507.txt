Complications during lumbar selective nerve root block: a scoping review
Authors: Anna Frøbert Welinder, David Kocemba, Viktor Hartmann Kuang, Mikkel Mylius Rasmussen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSelective Nerve Root Block (SNRB) is widely used for managing radicular pain, serving both therapeutic and diagnostic purposes. A complete list of all reported complications associated with SNRB has never been reported.AimTo map the existing evidence on complications associated with lumbar SNRB.MethodsA scoping review was conducted of peer-reviewed studies on complications associated with transforaminal or foraminal lumbar SNRB in adults with degenerative spine conditions. A systematic search was performed across six databases on 9th of September 2024. Two independent reviewers screened studies for eligibility.ResultsOf the 6,939 screened studies, 118 met the inclusion criteria, comprising a total of 15,817 patients and 72,518 SNRB procedures. A total of 145 distinct complication types and 4,439 complications were identified. Of these, 5.2% were classified as mild/moderate and 0.2% as severe according to the CTCAE grading system. The rate of complications for each study varied substantially, ranging from 0.2% to 73% calculated per procedure. Across all studies, the overall adjusted complication rate was 5.4% per procedure and 5.8% per patient. The most frequent were intravascular injection, vasovagal reactions, and central steroid effect. Severe events, including infection and paralysis, were rare (≤ 0.03%).Discussion and conclusionThe SNRB procedure has a low complication rate, with serious complications being rare. This scoping review highlights the heterogeneous complication data in publications. Standardized guidelines for SNRB complication reporting are warranted.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00701-025-06747-6.Keywords: Lumbar selective nerve root block (SRNB), Complications, CTCAE classification, Scoping review

Introduction

Radiculopathy is a common degenerative spine condition [49]. Due to its close correlation with age, it is expected to increase over the coming decades in the Western world [44]. Conservative management is typically recommended as the initial approach [48] with selective nerve root block (SNRB) as both a therapeutic option during the subacute phase [25] and as a diagnostic adjunct prior to surgery [11, 12, 33, 37]. SNRB is an image-guided interventional procedure that involves the targeted injection of local anesthetics and/or corticosteroids near the affected nerve root to reduce inflammation, alleviate radicular symptoms, and potentially provide sustained pain relief [22, 25, 36, 56]. SNRB is often used interchangeably with the term transforaminal epidural steroid injection (TFESI), although some authors distinguish between the two based on intent and injection volume. SNRB typically employs a smaller volume to precisely target a single nerve root for diagnostic purposes, whereas TFESI often uses a larger volume to achieve a broader therapeutic effect [5]. Despite these distinctions, the terminology remains inconsistently applied across the literature [19, 30, 47, 51]. As the frequency of SNRB procedures remains high, there is a growing need for a comprehensive evaluation of the associated complications. Previous reviews [2, 7, 18] have focused on only a few types of complications, and while case reports of rare complications exist, no comprehensive synthesis of complication types, frequencies, or severities has been undertaken to date.

The aim of this scoping review is to systematically map the existing evidence on complications associated with lumbar SNRB procedures in patients with degenerative spine conditions. Specifically, the review aims to characterize complications in terms of their rate and severity, the types of pharmaceuticals administered and the imaging modalities used.

Methods

This review was conducted using the Joanna Briggs Institute methodology and reported in accordance with PRISMA-ScR [41, 50]. A scoping approach was selected to allow for comprehensive mapping of the literature on complications associated with lumbar SNRB [50].

An a priori protocol was registered on the Open Science Framework [16].

Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they were peer-reviewed, reported complications associated with lumbar selective nerve root block (SNRB) procedures performed via a transforaminal, foraminal, or periradicular approach in adult patients (≥ 18 years) with degenerative spinal conditions and published in English. Eligible study designs included case reports, case series, prospective and retrospective cohort studies, cross-sectional studies, and randomized controlled trials. All types of injectates and imaging modalities were accepted. There were no restrictions based on patient sex, race, or ethnicity.

For the purposes of this review, the term “complication” was defined broadly to include any complications described by the included studies. No distinction was made between events if they were explicitly identified as complications in the original publication.

Studies were excluded if they were animal or cadaveric studies, commentaries, letters to the editor, grey literature, or did not report any complications.

Information sources and search strategy

A comprehensive search was developed in collaboration with an information specialist and conducted in the following databases: PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane Library, Web of Science, Google Scholar, and Scopus. The search covered all studies from the inception of each database through 9 September 2024. To identify additional studies, both backward and forward citation tracking were performed using Scopus. The search strategy included terms related to “lumbar,” “selective nerve root block,” “SNRB,” and “complications.” The complete search strings for all databases are provided in the appendix.

Study selection

All identified studies were imported into Covidence [23]. Two independent reviewers screened titles and abstracts for eligibility, followed by full-text review of potentially relevant articles. Disagreements were resolved through discussion and consultation with a third reviewer.

Data extraction

A standardized data extraction form was developed and pilot-tested prior to use. The following data items were collected: author(s), year of publication, country, study design, sample size, intent of SNRB, spinal level of injection, type and volume of injectate, use of contrast agent, imaging modality employed, and reported complications. Missing data were recorded as “not specified” (NS). The terminology used to describe complications was retained from the original publications where possible, with adjustments made only in cases of direct synonymy for consistency.

Data synthesis

Data were synthesized using descriptive and narrative methods, with a focus on mapping the types, frequencies, and severities of complications across studies. Four overall complication rates were calculated. Two were calculated based on the number of SNRB procedures, and two were calculated based on the number of patients.

Complication rates based on SNRB procedures

A crude complication rate was calculated based on the studies that reported the number of SNRB procedures. In this calculation, studies not reporting the number of SNRB procedures performed were excluded.

Several studies, n = 51, did not report the number of procedures performed, but only the number of patients and complications. As a sensitivity analysis, a one-to-one ratio of SNRB procedures per patient was assumed. Based on this number, an adjusted complication rate was calculated.

Complication rates based on number of patients

A crude complication rate was also calculated based on the studies that reported the number of patients. In this calculation, studies not reporting number of patients were excluded.

As a sensitivity analysis, a one-to-one ratio of patients per SNRB procedure was assumed. Based on this number, an adjusted complication rate was calculated.

Reported complications were categorized into nine groups based on the classification system CTCAE (Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events): (1) nervous system disorders; (2) injury, poisoning, and procedural complications; (3) musculoskeletal and connective tissue disorders; (4) general disorders and administration site conditions; (5) infections and infestations; (6) psychiatric disorders; (7) vascular and cardiac disorders; (8) endocrine, metabolism, and nutrition disorders; and (9) renal and urinary disorders. Complications that did not fit into these categories were classified as “other organ system disorders”. In addition, complications were classified into mild/moderate, corresponding to grades 1 and 2 on the CTCAE scale. Complications corresponding to grade 3 trough 5 on the CTCAE scale are classified as severe [17].

To provide an overview of the study designs, each study was assigned to one of three evidence tiers according to conventional hierarchies of methodological rigor [39].

The rate of severe complications was calculated as the total number of complications per SNRB.

No framework exists for reporting lumbar SNRB-related complications. Therefore a comprehensive classification scheme was developed for this review to facilitate consistent reporting. The proposed framework builds on the CTCAE system and organizes all identified complications by organ system and severity (mild/moderate vs. severe).

Results

Study selection

The initial search yielded 7,502 studies. After removal of 1,665 duplicates and addition of 1,102 studies through citation searching, 6,939 unique studies were screened by title and abstract. Of these, 329 studies were selected for full-text review.

Following full-text assessment, 211 studies were excluded for the following reasons: no information on complications (n = 112), grey literature (n = 35), unclear or missing data on complications (n = 15), ineligible patient population (n = 14), use of a different injection technique (n = 14), study design (n = 12), and language other than English (n = 9).

A total of 118 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the review. The study selection process is detailed in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Study design classification

Included studies were classified into evidence tiers based on conventional hierarchies of study design strength [39]. As shown in Fig. 4, 29 studies (26%) were categorized as Tier A, representing randomized controlled trials. Forty-five studies (38%) were classified as Tier B, comprising prospective and retrospective cohort studies. The remaining 44 studies (37%) were considered Tier C, including case reports, case series, and cross-sectional or case–control designs. The distribution of studies across tiers is illustrated in Fig. 4.

Complications

Complication rate per procedure

A total of 145 distinct types of complications were identified in the 118 included studies, amounting to 4,439 reported complications among 15,817 patients from 72,518 SNRB procedures.

Several studies did not report the number of SNRB procedures performed, but only the number of patients and complications. As a sensitivity analysis we assumed a one-to-one ratio of SNRB procedures per patient. This assumption was based on the fact that all included patients underwent a minimum of 1 SNRB to be included in any study. Based on this assumption, the total number of procedures was estimated at 81,558. This resulted in an overall adjusted complication rate of 5.4% per procedure.

When the complication rate per procedure was calculated for each study, complication rates ranged from 0.2% to 73% [24, 40]. Complication rate per patient

Reported complications varied in severity, ranging from transient increases in pain to permanent neurological injury and death. The rate of complications per patient ranged widely across studies, from 0.7% to 119% [39, 47]. Values exceeding 100% reflect studies where patients experienced multiple complications. However, only a small number of patients (0.3%, n = 45) were from studies reporting frequencies above 100%. The exact number of patients experiencing ≥ 1 complication was not possible to extract from the included studies. Six studies did not report the number of patients included, but only the number of SNRB procedures and complications. Frequency of complications

The most frequently reported complications were intravascular injection (n = 939), and vasovagal reactions (n = 552). Other commonly reported complications included central steroid effect (n = 364), increased pain (n = 351), transient motor deficit (n = 227), nerve root tingling/irritation (n = 125), increased radicular pain (n = 103), symptom aggravation (n = 102), and headache (n = 96).

Some complications types were concentrated within a small number of studies. One study accounted for all reported cases of central steroid effects [14], while another contributed 90% of all symptom aggravation cases [31]. The distribution of the most frequently reported complications is illustrated in Fig. 2.

Injection purpose and spinal level

Among the included studies, only one utilized SNRB as a diagnostic tool, whereas all others used SNRB therapeutically. The spinal levels targeted ranged from L1 to S1. However, 23% of studies (n = 27) did not specify the spinal level injected.

Number of procedures and levels per patient

There was considerable variation in the number of SNRB procedures administered per patient. For studies reporting both the number of patients and the number of SNRB performed, thirty percent of studies (n = 35) reported a single injection per patient, while 22% (n = 26) reported multiple injections per patient. A large proportion (48%, n = 57) did not provide information on the number of injections per patient. Similarly, the number of spinal levels injected varied. In 38% of studies (n = 45), the procedure was administered at two or fewer spinal levels, whereas 39% (n = 46) of studies reported injections at three or more levels. In 23% of studies (n = 27), the number of levels injected was not specified.

Injectates used

Most studies (77% of total studies, n = 91) reported the use of a combined injectate consisting of both a corticosteroid and a local anesthetic. A smaller subset (6%, n = 7) used corticosteroids alone, while 17% (n = 20) did not report the type of injectate used.

Regarding corticosteroid type, dexamethasone was the most frequently used (23%, n = 27), followed by methylprednisolone (19%, n = 22), triamcinolone (16%, n = 19), and betamethasone (8%, n = 10). In 14% of studies (n = 17), two or more types of corticosteroids were used, and in 20% (n = 23), the specific steroid type was not reported. In terms of corticosteroid formulation, both particulate and non-particulate preparations were reported. Betamethasone and methylprednisolone were reported in both forms. However, reporting on particulate status was inconsistent, and the number of studies specifying this detail was too limited to allow for subgroup analysis of an association between injectate type and complications.

In terms of local anesthetics, bupivacaine and lidocaine were equally represented, each appearing in 31% of studies (n = 36). Ropivacaine and mepivacaine were used in 3% of studies (n = 4 and n = 3, respectively), while levobupivacaine was used in one study (1%). In 6% of studies (n = 7), more than one local anesthetic was used. Eight percent of studies (n = 10) did not include a local anesthetic, and 17% (n = 20) did not specify the anesthetic agent used.

Other injectates

Several studies reported the use of additional substances in the injectate. Fourteen studies included saline as a diluent or flush, two studies added clonidine, one used hyaluronic acid, and another used vitamin B12.

Injectate volumes

Reported injectate volumes ranged from 1 to 18 mL. Among studies using only local anesthetics, the median injectate volume was 3.0 mL (interquartile range [IQR]: 2.44–5.00 mL). For steroid-only injections, the median was also 3.0 mL (IQR: 2.50–3.50 mL), and for studies using a combination of steroid and anesthetic, the median volume remained 3.0 mL (IQR: 2.44–5.25 mL).

Imaging guidance and contrast use

Three primary imaging modalities were used to guide the SNRB procedure. Fluoroscopy was the most employed method, used in 77% of studies (n = 91), followed by computed tomography (CT) in 7% (n = 8) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) in 1% (n = 1). A small number of studies (3%, n = 4) employed a combination of imaging modalities, while 1% (n = 2) did not utilize any imaging guidance. No studies utilized ultrasound as imaging modality. In 11% of studies (n = 13), the imaging modality was not reported.

All studies using CT guidance employed contrast. Among studies using fluoroscopy, 95% (n = 86) used contrast agents, 2% (n = 2) did not, and 3% (n = 3) did not report contrast use. Of the two studies using MRI, one employed contrast, while the other did not specify whether it used contrast. Across all imaging modalities, 15% of studies (n = 18) did not report whether contrast was used.

Discussion

A total of 145 different complications were identified among 15,817 patients. This corresponded to 72,518 SNRB procedures when multiple procedures per patient were included, and 81,558 SNRB procedures when assuming one procedure per patient for studies with missing SNRB count. This review found an overall adjusted complication rate was 5.4% per procedure and 5.8% per patient. Based on CTCAE classification, 5.2% of procedures resulted in mild or moderate events, while severe complications occurred in only 0.2%. The most common complications were intravascular injection, vasovagal reactions, and central steroid effects, whereas severe events such as infection, paralysis, or permanent motor deficit were rare (< 0.03%). Considerable heterogeneity was observed in both reported complication frequencies and study methodologies. Furthermore, the pharmaceuticals used for injections and the imaging guidance used during the procedures varied.

In this review 145 different complication types were reported. When complications were reported, the definitions and terminology differed significantly between studies. An example is the inconsistent use of terms such as increased pain, back pain, radicular pain, and worsening of pain without clear definitions or explanations [8], Further, it was often unclear whether the reported pain was transient or persistent. A systematic review and meta-analysis [9] found similar inconsistent complication reports in epidural corticosteroid injections. It was concluded that the assessment and reporting of complications were “suboptimal” and that methods for assessing complications were not well reported. Currently, there are between 14 and 30 complication types described in the literature [2, 8, 18]. These earlier publications reported 45 unique complications in total. Our review adds to the current literature by identifying more types of complications. All 45 previous reported types of complications were also included in this review.

Substantial variation in the number of complications reported was observed in the included studies. Some studies reported many different complications with high incidence rates [21, 46] while other studies, even with large sample sizes, reported very few [19, 38]. The lack of consistent data might be explained by missing standardized guidelines for reporting complications. Previous reviews have not consistently reported individual complication rates for each included study [2, 7].

The calculation of complication rates was limited by incomplete and heterogeneous reporting across studies. Crude rates were based on reported data, whereas adjusted rates required assumptions to include studies lacking either patient or procedure counts. Assuming a 1:1 ratio of SNRB per patient as a sensitivity analysis enabled broader inclusion but could have introduced bias, as some patients may have undergone multiple procedures. Large studies reporting only procedure numbers may have skewed estimates by outweighing smaller studies with detailed patient-level data. Adjusted rates likely underestimate the true complication risk.

Furthermore, a high number of studies which examined SNRB and did not mention complications and explicitly stated zero complications was excluded from this review. This approach could have resulted in an overestimation of the complication rate. This approach was chosen as this review primarily focused on covering the types of complications associated with SNRB procedures. Other reviews did not include overall complication rates per SNRB or patient, limiting comparability [8, 9, 18].

A strength of this study is its identification of 118 studies. In comparison, previous reviews on complications included between 7 and 66 studies, with some not reporting the number of studies included [2–4, 8, 9, 15, 18, 20, 33, 43] Case reports, case series, or uncontrolled studies were not excluded in this review to include all complications associated with lumbar SNRB procedures.

One previous review included 63 studies but focused on all epidural steroid injection approaches, such as caudal, interlaminar and transforaminal approach. They partially addressed complications and reviewed 287 cases of complications [2]. Another included 7054 injections [8]. This review is the first to systematically report on psychological complications, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage, and visual impairments in lumbar SNRB.

The most frequent complications highlight challenges in reporting, as three out of the eight most common complications are predominantly driven by a single study, accounting for over 80% of the studies. These findings on frequent complications align with previous reviews [2, 8, 18]. The most common complications were intravascular injection. The rate of intravascular injections in this review was 1.2%, n = 939 out of 81,558 SNRB. Earlier studies found rates between 1.9% and 11.2% [18] and notably transforaminal epidural steroid injections at the S1 level had a higher rate of 21.3%. Our study included both lumbar and sacral injection levels without discriminating spine level. For instance, one study specifically focused on the risk of intravascular uptake during S1 transforaminal epidural steroid injections [46]. Therefore, this review expected higher complication rate than 1.2%. However, this review included studies with very large numbers of SNRB procedures. One study alone performed 22,298 SNRB but did not report any intravascular injections [31]. As the number of SNRB procedures were high it would be expected that common complications such as intravascular injections accidently occurred during the study. This underreporting could lead to an underestimation of the rate of intravascular injections. Furthermore, caution is warranted when interpreting estimates due to different terminology used, e.g. vascular penetration was used instead of intravascular injection [8].

Although rare, the most severe complications indicate SNRB can result in serious outcomes. Severe complications such as permanent motor deficit, paralysis, meningitis, visual impairment, and death were reported. The incidence rate per SNRB for all severe complications was 0.2% and underlining the rarity. These findings are consistent with earlier findings. For instance, an incidence rate of epidural abscess post-spinal intervention of 0.2–2.8 per 10,000 cases and an incidence of epidural hematomas of 1 in 150,000 epidurals is reported [8]. In this review, a rate of 6 epidural abscesses per 81,558 SNRB is found. This was similar to rates found in the literature. Similarly severe infections (meningitis, epidural abscess, osteomyelitis, and discitis) are rare, with an incidence of 0.1–0.01% of all spinal injections [18]. This aligns with our findings confirming the rarity of these events.

There was considerable variation in the composition of injectate used in the included studies, i.e., 4 types of steroids and 5 types of local anesthetics, injection volumes ranging from 1 mL—18 mL, and various imaging techniques. These variations could potentially influence complication rates [43]. Our data were not sufficiently powered or detailed to perform sub-analysis for comparison of complications rates for particulate and non-particulate injections.

Both particulate and nonparticulate Betamethasone and Methylprednisolone was used in the included studies. Triamcinolone was exclusively reported used in particulate form. Concerns have been raised regarding the use of particulate steroids. In cervical SNRB procedures, the use of particulate steroids has been linked to increased thromboembolic risk compared to non-particulate steroids [10, 33, 42], and therefore non-particulate steroids are recommended in the literature. In lumbar SNRB procedures similar recommendations preferencing non-particulate steroids can be found [26], yet evidence remains inconclusive and no clear consensus has been established [6, 26]. Evidence regarding a possible association between particulate steroids and thromboembolic risk in lumbar SNRB is limited and we did not find studies investigating complications using particulate steroids specifically linked to thromboembolic events.

Most studies focused on therapeutic SNRB. In these cases, limiting injectate volume to avoid spread to adjacent nerve roots is of less concern. However, studies using up to 18 mL raise concern for tissue disruption and complications. Despite large variations across injectate composition, the median volume was 3 mL, with minor differences in interquartile range. This suggested a volume preference and has not been systematically explored in other reviews.

Most studies (62%) used either Bupivacaine or Lidocaine. Although this paints a picture of preference, there are studies that suggest that the clinical difference in pain levels and duration is minimal [6].

Most studies in this review used fluoroscopy. This has been reported with several advantages such as being cheaper than traditional CT or MRI and offering lower doses of ionizing radiation than CT [45]. The second most used imaging modality in our review was CT (7%), and although relatively expensive, it is still less expensive than MRI and takes less time [29, 34]. It is reported that CT provides better visualisation and precise anatomical resolution compared to standard two-dimensional fluoroscopy [32, 52].

None of the studies included in this review used ultrasound guidance. Ultrasound is a newer alternative technology with minimized radiation exposure and the use of contrast [53]. Recent literature suggest that the degree of pain relief of patients were similar to those of fluoroscopy guidance in lumbar SNRB [28, 53]. The absence of ultrasound-guided studies in this review show that the method is still relatively new. This might be attributed to the high level of expertise required and technical challenges in deep lumbar visualization [55].

Almost all studies included in our review used contrast. The two studies that did not use contrast both employed fluoroscopy and were case reports describing rare complications such as paraplegia [1] and generalized pruritus [13, 35

To enhance comparability and clinical interpretability, complications in this review were categorized using the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE). This system was chosen because it permits structured grading across multiple organ systems. This is relevant in lumbar SNRB where complications may affect neurological, vascular, infectious, or endocrine systems. CTCAE provided a framework to distinguish mild/moderate (grades 1–2) from severe (grades 3–5) events and enabled consistent rate calculations across heterogeneous studies. Previous reviews of lumbar SNRB or transforaminal epidural steroid injections did not apply the CTCAE or any other formal severity grading system; instead, they described complications as “minor”, “major” or “serious harms” [2, 7, 9]. Consequently, previous data are difficult to compare due to the absence of standardized severity grading. Future use of established grading systems such as CTCAE may improve complications reporting.

The absence of a uniform reporting framework complicates comparison of complication data across studies. To address this we propose a structured classification scheme (Table 16) combining CTCAE severity grading with organ-based grouping. This approach may serve as a future reporting standard to improve consistency, transparency, and comparability of complication data in lumbar SNRB research. Table 16 summarizes the currently reported complications but also illustrates the limited consistency across studies and may provide a foundation for future research to refine and validate complication classification.

In this review a wide range of study designs were included consistent with scoping review methodology. However, this heterogeneity of included studies can influence complication reporting. Most SNRB procedures are based on retrospective cohort studies. This provides valuable information on complications over time in real-world settings. However, retrospective designs are susceptible to incomplete or biased data. For instance, a large retrospective study included 22,298 SNRB and did report common complications such as intravascular injection but only had a total of 117 complications [31]. This is natural as the study design is not intended for intense follow-up and reporting of minor complications. Several case reports and case series were also included. While anecdotal, they are often the first to highlight rare or unexpected complications. For example, two case reports included in this review described two instances of death following SNRB procedures [27, 54]. Other reviews have also included both case reports and cohort studies [8], or solely reported randomized single-blind or double-blind trials in comparison [2]. Randomized controlled trials provide the highest-quality evidence for the efficacy of treatments. The high level of hospital visits and intense follow-up increases the chances of minor complications being reported. However, their controlled settings and relatively small sample sizes arguably limit the detection of complications.

Our study was limited by the large variation in terminology, heterogeneous complication rate and the inconsistent reporting of complications in the included studies. Excluding non-English language studies could have introduced language bias. Furthermore, several studies had missing data, which can represent both reporting- and selection bias. Assuming a 1:1 ratio to estimate patient- and procedure-based complication rates could have introduced bias in the calculated rates. Overall, the heterogeneity across studies and missing data reduced the precision and should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Conclusion

The complications rate of lumbar SNRB procedure is low, and serious complications are very rare. We found 145 types of complications associated with SNRB, significantly more than previously reported. Clinicians should be aware of more complications than previously reported. We found the CTCAE classification system to be useful for grouping and grading complications. There is currently no official guideline for reporting complications associated with SNRB. Inconsistent reporting indicate a need for standardized reporting of complications.
