Point-of-care echocardiography training pathways: a global perspective and the need for standardisation
Authors: Marcus Abbawy, Finlay Holden, Tom Fisher, Prashant Nasa, Akshay Hiryur Manjunathasawamy, Suresh Renukappa, Subashini Suresh, Nanda Kumar, Tom Ingram, Sandeep Hothi, James M Cotton, Aruna Maharaj, Cyril Chacko, Tonny Veenith
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPoint-of-care ultrasound echocardiography has become an indispensable tool for rapid clinical assessment in emergency medicine. This review article examines the heterogeneity in training pathways globally, outlining the advantages and presenting the challenges to standardisation, documentation practices and quality assurance. By reviewing current curricula, training methods and international models, we aim to identify the positive elements and propose a framework for an ideal national training programme, including the potential role of an examination and higher education institutions.Keywords: Echocardiography, Ultrasonography, Cardiac Imaging Techniques

Introduction

Echocardiography, the use of ultrasonography to investigate cardiac function, has evolved from a specialised cardiology investigation to a readily accessible tool for bedside assessment. Point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS) echocardiography offers rapid, non-invasive, qualitative insights into cardiac function. This can be invaluable in the management of patients in emergency and intensive care medicine, complementing and extending the traditional clinical examination. It is most useful for answering a specific clinical question to help rule in or out a diagnosis rather than performing a full echocardiographic investigation. This is done by looking at valvular or ventricular abnormalities, haemodynamic status and assessment of preload responsiveness during resuscitation and pericardial effusion. The two most common forms of classical echocardiography are transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) and transoesophageal echocardiography (TOE) each with their own advantages.1 Most used in POCUS echocardiography is TTE, which is non-invasive, in contrast to TOE. POCUS echocardiography (also referred to as focused cardiac ultrasound) in this study describes the scope and intent of the examination. POCUS as an imaging modality has been shown to be 96.6% accurate in diagnosing common conditions that present to the emergency department, where diagnostic accuracy is crucial and can significantly impact patient management.2 This includes cardiac, thoracic, abdominal and proximal lower limb POCUS looking for pleural effusion, reduced ejection fraction, abdominal aortic aneurysm and other acutely presenting conditions. The range of practice extends from basic critical care echocardiography (BCCE) to advanced critical care echocardiography (ACE). BCCE is confined to two-dimensional (2D) and M-mode and is used to define the type of shock and major haemodynamic abnormalities. ACE indicates the level of expertise expected of an advanced echocardiographer, using colour, spectral Doppler, tissue Doppler and speckle tracking, alongside other advanced modalities, in addition to the basic modalities.

In the UK, the number of high-quality studies conducted regarding the use of POCUS echocardiography does not adequately meet the current demand arising from increased practice.3 This is concerning, given that the unregulated adoption of POCUS by untrained operators can have profound implications and is ranked among the top 10 technological hazards for patient safety.4 Additionally, the absence of a standardised training programme for POCUS echocardiography leads to significant variability in practice and interpretation. Establishing a national training framework is crucial to ensure consistent quality and patient safety. There are many training pathways for intensive care echocardiography. These include fellowship programmes, university programmes, accredited workshops, short commercial courses, conferences, university courses and diplomas; however, there is a lack of standardisation in education goals. By reviewing and evaluating the different approaches in the UK, USA, Canadian, European and Australasian systems, it is possible to identify best practice and propose an optimal approach which mitigates shortcomings and encompasses the strengths of each in a coherent model appropriate to the UK healthcare environment. This review will focus solely on point-of-care training pathways, setting aside formal comprehensive echocardiography training. While POCUS echocardiography is increasingly being adopted in resource limited settings and will be highlighted later, this is not the focus of this review. Instead, this review is directed towards an audience of doctors specialising in critical care, emergency medicine, acute medicine and anaesthesia.

Methods

For this structured narrative review, a literature search was conducted to identify studies which examine current point of care echocardiography training pathways, their scope and methods of implementation. The systemic search was limited to studies published from inception to 28 March 2026 and limited to English language articles. Earlier studies were included where necessary to provide theoretical background and context. The search was carried out across PubMed, Google Scholar and ScienceDirect.

The search strategy incorporated four primary conceptual domains: (1) POCUS echocardiography terms (“point-of-care ultrasound”, “echocardiography”, “POCUS”, “cardiac ultrasound”, “focused echocardiography”), (2) training-related terms (“training”, “certification”, “education”, “curriculum”, “assessment”, “competency”, “pathway”, “fees”), (3) healthcare-related terms (“healthcare professional”, “doctor”, “clinician”, “physician”, “hospital”, “medicine”, “clinical”) and (4) location-related terms (“United Kingdom”, “Europe”, “United States”, “Australia”, “New Zealand”, “Canada”, “Africa”). Boolean operators (AND/OR) were used to combine these terms with adjustments made to reflect the requirements of each search engine.

Studies were included if they focused on point-of-care ultrasound, described training programmes and curricula and included relevant healthcare professionals in predetermined locations. Additional criteria for inclusion were publication in peer-reviewed journals, available in English and falling within the predefined publication period. Studies were excluded if they solely focused on comprehensive echocardiography training or did not include mention of the training components.

From the studies identified title and abstract screening was used to establish relevant articles based on the inclusion and exclusion criteria. The full texts of qualifying studies were finally reviewed to assess for inclusion. The list of references from relevant studies was also examined to identify any further articles missed in the initial search. Specific training programmes were selected for analysis if they contained a structured training pathway, included defined curriculum content, assessment methods and a target audience.

Comparison of current POCUS echocardiography training programmes

Echocardiography training in the UK

The Echocardiography Training Programme, established by the British Society of Echocardiography (BSE) and the National School of Healthcare Science, is the leading echocardiography training scheme in the UK.5 Piloted in 2019,6 this 18-month full-time programme integrates a 30-credit physics-focused module from the BSE syllabus into the pre-existing 30-credit module of the Scientific Training Programme. This template was used successfully for the training of advanced critical care echocardiographers in cardiology and could also be used to develop a gold standard POCUS echocardiography programme within the UK. Traditional echocardiography training placed significant emphasis on ventricular function and valvular pathology, whereas POCUS echocardiography emphasised the diagnosis of shock and haemodynamic assessments of patients. Through higher-quality immersive training in a shortened timescale compared with current training models, this model will increase the number of trained echocardiographers in the workforce. International comparisons with training in the USA, Europe, Australia, Canada and New Zealand

Australia

Australia has developed structured POCUS training programmes, often integrated into specialty training curricula. These programmes emphasise practical skills and competency-based assessment. The key bodies associated with POCUS training include the Australasian College of Emergency Medicine (ACEM),7 the Australasian Society for Ultrasound in Medicine (ASUM), the Royal Australian College of General Practitioners (RACGP) and the College of Intensive Care Medicine of Australia and New Zealand (CICM). ASUM provides a pathway to becoming certified in POCUS, awarding a Certificate in Clinician-Performed Ultrasound (CCPU), enabling medical practitioners to use ultrasound for diagnosis at the point of care.8 Finally, the RACGP offers a non-certified course to help general practitioners develop their ultrasound skills.9

The CICM provides a structured pathway for trainees to become competent.10 BCCE has been included since the 2014 curriculum for intensive care training. The CICM has established an ultrasound special interest group, which has made recommendations about the standardisation and levels of training in critical care echocardiography.11

Various providers across Australia run ACEM-approved short courses, such as the Australian Institute of Ultrasound, which must meet the minimum requirements delineated by the ACEM.12 Requirements include the length, content and type of assessment of the course. Many courses are also ASUM-accredited, meaning they count towards receiving the CCPU certificate. On completing the course, candidates must submit a logbook of their ultrasound scans and undergo an examination by an ASUM assessor to become CCPU certified. Variability in current training curricula

Current POCUS echocardiography training varies significantly across institutions and specialties. Many rely on informal mentorship or short courses, leading to inconsistent skill acquisition. This variability in training risks variability in competence, which can lead to misinterpretation and potentially compromising patient care. The absence of a standardised curriculum across specialties also hinders the assessment of competency and the assurance of quality. The introduction of POCUS echocardiography into various training curricula, such as those of the Royal College of Emergency Medicine, the Faculty of Intensive Care Medicine is a significant step forward in encouraging training.

Observational studies have identified inconsistencies in the training of core POCUS echocardiography skills across different regions in the UK, with senior doctors not engaging in the programme in the same way across regional training programmes, highlighting the need for a formal training programme.18 Inadequate uptake of POCUS echocardiography training may be attributed to organisational barriers such as a lack of echocardiography trainers and limited time constraints due to an increased workload. These issues may stem from insufficient staffing levels, meaning that workforce priorities are directed elsewhere, as training does not take precedence over patient care.19,21 The variation in the curriculum across different specialties leads to inconsistent levels of competency across clinical areas.

Importance of standardised documentation

Standardised documentation is essential for maintaining a record of findings and ensuring continuity of care. A structured reporting template, including agreed key measurements and qualitative assessments, should be incorporated so that reporting templates and acquisition of those measurements become a key component of the training programmes. This ensures that findings are accurately recorded and communicated, facilitating informed clinical decisions. A good storage archive allows for review of previous scans, permitting comparison and identifying any changes. The Faculty of Intensive Care Medicine outline in their standards that all images need to be stored using appropriate data governance to ensure quality assurance. Furthermore, when a scan has been performed to inform a clinical decision, a subsequent structured report must be generated and stored in the patient’s records.22

Unfortunately, documentation falls short of the standard required for safe and effective practice, with one survey finding that total POCUS documentation in Emergency Departments was as low as 38% in some cases, possibly due to the time-pressured setting of an emergency department.23 Furthermore, in a recent survey of UK intensive care, the appropriate documentation and storage of reports was found to be only 25%.24 Other potential issues arising from incomplete documentation include medicolegal complications or poor handover of care with a diagnosis being unclear.

Responsibility and implementation

The responsibility for implementing a national POCUS echocardiography training programme should be shared among professional bodies, higher education institutions and healthcare institutions. A collaborative approach is the most likely to succeed in developing, overseeing and implementing the programme, while higher education institutions (HEIs) could contribute to curriculum development, assessment and research. If implemented, a standardised examination should be developed and administered by a recognised body in collaboration with HEIs, with uniformity in standards of practice across specialties.

A recent collaboration paper (National EvaluATion of the provision of Critical Care ECHOcardiography in Shock, NEAT ECHO) on the use of echo in critical care shock management24 revealed that only 54% of those with shock received an echocardiogram within 72 hours. Significantly, the echocardiogram altered management or reduced ambiguity in diagnosis in 54% of patients. Furthermore, just 25% of these echocardiograms complied with the national guidance. Analysing the credentials of the clinicians performing the echocardiography, only 56% were accredited. Most of these accreditations were from the FUSIC course, followed by the BSE courses. The principal conclusion drawn from these findings is the need for a structured, easily accessible, country-wide standard to help increase the number of clinicians trained in sonography and provide a pathway to help streamline echocardiogram technique and guideline compliance. This also reinforces the requirement for a good governance framework for the use of POCUS. Additionally, obstacles to echocardiography accreditation should be identified and addressed.

Effectiveness of training

The effectiveness of POCUS echocardiography training was analysed in a systematic review by Gibson et al who found that short training interventions (6 hours of didactics and hands-on training) allowed medical students and physician trainees to achieve near perfect agreement with expert echocardiographers when detecting left ventricular systolic dysfunction and pericardial effusion.25 There is a steep early learning curve whereby core binary pathologies can be learnt quickly, with subsequent improvement through deliberate repeated practice. This review highlights the importance of sufficient POCUS echocardiography training within the acute setting, enabling faster, more accurate diagnosis which helps to improve patient outcomes through earlier treatment. Furthermore, a structured training programme ensures safe and consistent utilisation, reducing the likelihood of diagnostic error.

Key priorities and recommendations for improved training

Drawing from international best practices influenced by the NEAT study, we have determined that a robust POCUS echocardiography training programme should be comprehensive, practical and tailored to the specific needs of different healthcare professionals, while incorporating the following:

Standardised curriculum and institutional oversight: A national curriculum outlining core competencies and learning objectives should be developed, with the curriculum highlighting the requirements of intensive care patients. While established UK pathways currently exist, a collaboration between different societies could help facilitate this by unifying programmes and outlining a minimum level of standardisation required among their trainees. Additionally, a national programme should be led by a designated professional body. The BSE is well-positioned to lead in this capacity, in collaboration with other stakeholder organisations, such as the Intensive Care Society, the Faculty of Intensive Care Medicine and the Royal College of Emergency Medicine (RCEM). However, acknowledgement of the differing clinical needs of different specialties and the level of competence clinicians in these fields require is also crucial in a standardised curriculum. This highlights the importance of involving the Royal Colleges and Societies in decision-making to ensure standardisation can be balanced but flexible to meet each trainee’s needs.

Further considerations

Use of POCUS echocardiography in low-resource settings

With the benefits of POCUS echocardiography evident, there is an increasing need to implement training pathways in resource-limited settings to improve clinical care. As of 2025, sub-Saharan African countries rank highly worldwide with the highest rates of heart disease per hundred thousand people.32 With limitations such as equipment shortages, lack of consistent internet connection and an absence of infrastructure for POCUS training, there should be a focus on tackling these issues and therefore establishing the groundwork needed to allow training programmes in resource-poor environments.33 Médecins Sans Frontières offers a POCUS training of facilitators course that focuses on teaching participants to provide POCUS training, while also imparting the basic knowledge behind POCUS echocardiography in resource-limited settings.34 Additionally, resource-poor environments are not limited to lower income countries; higher income countries may also experience resource constraints such as in remote settings or during natural disasters or pandemics. So, while the initial POCUS echocardiography objective should be the development of an effective standardised nationwide training pathway, future considerations should involve implementation under harsher circumstances and looking to other resource-poor environments to understand how they have overcome these issues. Looking to the future, research focused on sub-Saharan Africa, published by Huson et al, suggests that a five-question protocol for cardiac ultrasound in resource-limited settings could have a significant impact on the treatment of cardiac disease, particularly heart failure, in these settings.35

Potential role of AI and technological advancements in training

Artificial intelligence (AI) provides a platform for significant development not only in diagnostic accuracy36 but also in reducing assessment times.37 Quicker investigative results and documentation could lead to a decreased workload for emergency clinicians, alongside a swifter time from admission to treatment, improving patient outcomes. AI may also allow for continuous real-time monitoring, providing the patient with constant surveillance and allowing for rapid detection of deterioration in the acute setting. By analysing vast datasets of echocardiograms, AI algorithms can identify subtle patterns that may escape the human eye, potentially leading to earlier diagnosis of rare or emerging conditions. This is akin to how AI-powered ECG analysis can detect structural heart disease, as highlighted in the recent Nature article,38 by identifying subtle cardiac abnormalities from routine heart rhythm recordings, expanding access to early screening and personalised treatment strategies. However, at this stage, AI still fails to overcome some of the challenges POCUS currently faces such as the significant capital requirement needed to implement the technology, alongside the time investment required to train workers in AI use, rather than ultrasound interpretation. AI can also support education through training and competency development. First, as discussed in the study, AI-powered platforms can offer simulated environments and real-time guidance for training, enabling novices to acquire POCUS skills more efficiently and safely in an environment that uses mistakes for learning. Second, AI-guided reporting can automate measurements, identify anatomical structures and generate preliminary reports, significantly reducing the workload for clinicians and ensuring standardised, accurate documentation.39 AI-guided reporting can improve workflow efficiency and integrate data into electronic health records. Telemedicine and AI are poised to revolutionise POCU in transformative ways. Telemedicine is the remote delivery of healthcare services using telecommunication technology. In Brazil, the Telehealth Network of Minas Gerais have developed a sustainable framework for telehealth, allowing vulnerable populations with greater health inequalities to access healthcare professionals remotely so they can use tools such as tele-ECG, tele-consultations and more recently the use of tele-echocardiography.40 These features have been shown to reduce diagnostic delays in cardiac disease, reduce the costs associated with diagnosis and lower rates of hospitalisation. This synergy of telemedicine for remote access and AI for enhanced analysis makes POCUS an even more powerful tool for diagnostics and patient management.

Elsewhere, other notable advancements are arising in the fields of three-dimensional (3D) echocardiography, contrast echocardiography41 and portable echocardiographic devices. Studies have shown that the accuracy of 3D echocardiography is greater than that of traditional 2D imaging when assessing left ventricular volumes, to the extent that some European guidelines now recommend 3D imaging over 2D for this purpose.42 Combining 3D imaging with advancements in AI may eventually enable instant, precise volumetric measurements, further aiding rapid diagnosis and prognostic assessments. Meanwhile, portable devices are being trialled, with the primary benefit being their accessibility and speed. While they may not be as consistently accurate as conventional echocardiography, their increasing availability can help reduce delays in management.41

Conclusion

Standardising POCUS echocardiography training is crucial for ensuring quality and patient safety. By leveraging international best practices and implementing a standardised training programme, we can enhance the skills and confidence of healthcare professionals, ultimately improving patient outcomes. The collaboration between professional bodies and HEIs will be crucial in achieving this goal. Training pathways across the UK and worldwide are constantly evolving, and awarding bodies regularly update the structure of their pathways. However, our recommendations for a better programme ensure that healthcare providers are proficient in delivering POCUS across front-line specialties with better continuity of care and mutual exchange. The key conclusions from our study are that mentorship, competency-based assessment, standardised documentation and oversight from higher education institutions can play a pivotal role in the future of POCUS echocardiography training.

The trajectory of POCUS is positive, with substantial research demonstrating the significant benefits it provides. Its importance is clear, and ideally, a national training programme can facilitate accurate implementation, optimising promising outcomes and ensuring correct practice. An ideal approach would incorporate international, multisociety and larger echocardiography organisations including the American Society of Echocardiography, European Association of Cardiovascular Imaging, BSE and relevant medical societies. Finally, consideration should be given to commence basic POCUS in medical schools as an extension of the physical examination.
