Conflict and uneven development in the multidecade distributed solar energy transition in the United States
Authors: David J. Hess
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SignificanceA central research challenge for sustainability science is the following question: why is there so little progress in meeting decarbonization goals? This study addresses the problem of stalled and uneven development, that is, the failure of sustainable innovations to scale up and fulfill their potential. The explanatory framework includes technical and economic factors as well as strategic action by challengers and incumbents in an industry, broader societal and global changes, and shifts in policy signals. This comprehensive and longitudinal perspective from transition studies helps to identify policy opportunities that would not otherwise be evident and to envision new policies and outcomes, such as the use of local solar generation to meet grid stability and peak load challenges. These opportunities are discussed in the conclusion.

Sustainability science researchers have already recognized the value of integrating natural science and engineering perspectives with a range of social science fields (1). Sustainability transition studies research contributes to this integration in multiple ways, among which is attention to multidecade changes (or transitions) in consumption-production systems such as food, energy, mobility, buildings, and water (2). Transition studies research also embeds system change in broader contexts of institutional change and global processes. These perspectives are valuable because they have the potential to contribute to one of the most fundamental problems in sustainability science: developing technically and politically feasible solutions that can help accelerate systematic transitions, especially in countries where there are governance conflicts over the pace and form of the transitions (3).

With respect to the problem of how to achieve more rapid and complete system change, one of the important problems is the puzzle of nonlinearity. The concept of nonlinearity in transition studies refers to patterns of change that do not always conform to a gradual, linear model of growth of innovations and that may include periods of failure and stalled development as well as rapid acceleration (4, 5). This study uses the case of small-scale solar generation in the United States to demonstrate nonlinear processes in a multidecade transition. Because this form of more sustainable energy underwent periods of collapse and slow growth, it provides a good example of nonlinearity in transitions. In recent years, the cost of solar energy has declined, and solar generation has become an increasingly important part of the mix of low-carbon electricity sources that will be necessary to achieve both sustainability and resilience goals in the electricity sector. The specific case can be used to demonstrate how transition studies can help us understand the general problem of why transitions stall. Understanding the problem of stalled transitions and developing creative solutions to accelerate the pace of sustainability transitions are increasingly important with each passing year of failure to meet environmental goals such as reduced greenhouse gas emissions.

The field of transition studies is large and contains diverse conceptual frameworks, of which multilevel perspective is one of the most prominent. This framework uses the concepts of regime, niche, and landscape to explain transition patterns and nonlinearity. With respect to the regime concept, existing systems of consumption-production are stabilized by “alignments of technologies, policies, user patterns, infrastructures, and cultural discourses that have been created in previous decades”; are maintained by incumbent actors (such as the utility companies in the electricity industry); and are shaped by a regime of shared institutions and rules (6). Niches are sites of radical innovation that generally emerge in spaces that are protected from market competition and are often developed by new entrants, which can include networks of entrepreneurs, research and development organizations, and incumbents from other industries. Relations between new entrants and incumbent regime actors are shaped by landscape changes, such as global political events, natural disasters, and economic and demographic trends (6).

An important factor that contributes to stalled development is the threat, real or perceived, that radical niche innovations and associated actors (such as solar industry developers) can pose to incumbent actors (such as utility companies). Incumbents that benefit from an existing configuration of a system (such as an electricity system based on centralized, baseload power from fossil fuels and nuclear energy) tend to mobilize to slow, control, or even block the momentum for the growth of niche innovations that are perceived as a threat (7–9). In some cases, incumbents also attempt to select from among the different niches to accept forms that are most compatible with the existing configuration of the system (e.g., large-scale solar farms rather than small-scale photovoltaics). Thus, the consumption-production system (sometimes also referred to as the technological system) can undergo some diversification and reconfiguration as it partially integrates innovations, but it can also do that by marginalizing innovations. In some cases, there is a more complete change in which the incumbents are replaced or displaced in a new configuration of the system.

Researchers in both sustainability science and transition studies have recognized that when a conflict emerges, it is not restricted to the economic field of marketplace competition and technical innovation. Although an analysis should include issues of economic and technological feasibility, it should also include attention to institutional and political conflict and broader landscape factors (1, 7, 10). The longitudinal analysis presented here develops this approach in transition studies and sustainability science by examining transitions as political processes that involve coalitions of actors who have conflicting views of system change (11, 12). These coalitions involve strategic action in diverse social fields (e.g., the market, civil society, science, the states, and the media) that comprise aspects of the broader societal landscape in which transition dynamics occur (13). Their strategic action also affects and is affected by governance structures and policy outcomes. This approach is used to point to novel policy interventions and system solutions that might not otherwise be noticed by an approach to changes in the consumption-production system that focus only on economic and technical dimensions.

Research Design

The following analysis adopts the longitudinal, extended case study method that is widely used in transition studies and related fields that study industrial and technological change. The method involves “reconstructing sequences of events, cumulative trends, and evolving contexts with attention to their temporal ordering and the steps along the way” (5). It enables a multidecade, comprehensive approach to the analysis of nonlinearity. Landscape events and trends, together with major policy and governance changes, are identified as one set of causal factors, and they are used to introduce and distinguish periods of a transition and shifts in the institutionalization of niche innovations.

The approach to longitudinal transitions developed here is multicausal: it includes landscape events and trends, state-oriented policy signals, and an analysis of actors and their strategic actions. The latter includes the relationships between new entrants (or challengers) and incumbents, the coalition partners of both, and the various tactics and targets of their actions (13). Our analysis also includes the more technical and economic aspects of the transition, such as the declining cost of the niche technology and the relationship of economic competitiveness to policy conflicts (14).

The analysis focuses on one category of solar energy: distributed solar energy (DSE). The category of DSE can be interpreted broadly, and solar hot water heating will be mentioned during the review of the first period. However, throughout the analysis, DSE will be used in the narrow sense to refer to small-scale photovoltaics used for homes, commercial facilities, and industrial sites. Although the distinction with utility-scale solar is fuzzy, one widely used cutoff point is the US Energy Information Administration’s current definition of utility-scale solar as starting at 1 megawatt of generation capacity (15).

The study design focuses on the national political and policy level in the United States and also includes some state-level analysis. Examples of policy signals and support for DSE include tax incentives, standards for interconnection to the grid, favorable pricing schemes (such as net metering, which is common in the United States and pays excess local generation at the retail rate), a legal framework for a range of innovative financing instruments, a renewable portfolio standard (a policy that requires a percentage of generation from renewable energy), and support for research and development. Other countries share some of the support policies and the structural features of US electricity markets, and they also have incumbent opposition to DSE. Thus, research on the US case can also be relevant for other countries.

The analysis has four parts that correspond with three different periods of conflicts and an emerging fourth period. The time periods of 1970–1990, 1991–2010, 2011–2020, and 2021 going forward have been selected for analytic purposes. The boundaries between periods are fuzzy, and to some degree, the relationships described for each period can be found in other periods. Data for the analysis are from historical primary sources (e.g., the news media sources described in the section “Market-Enabling Policies”), peer-reviewed secondary sources (as cited), and publicly available data sets from the US government (as cited). Data sources for the two figures are given in Materials, and more details are available in the SI Appendix.

Transition periods and changing coalitions

Intra-Niche Conflict and Niche Selection (1970–1990).

During the 1970s, the issue salience of national energy policy became elevated because of changes in the broader landscape trends and events. The growth of air conditioning and urban demand led to electricity system brownouts in some cities, and during the winter of 1969–1970, there were shortages of heating oil (16). The oil spill off the coast of Santa Barbara, California, also raised public awareness of the environmental risks of oil imports and contributed to the massive public demonstrations on the first Earth Day in 1970. In 1973, countries associated with the Organization of Petroleum Exporting States launched an oil embargo on countries that had supported Israel during the Yom Kippur War. Although petroleum represented only 17% of the electricity generation mix in 1973 (17), the rise in oil prices and consumer shortages at the filling stations elevated the broader salience of the energy issue, including for the electricity sector. Moreover, during the 1970s, the movement to oppose nuclear energy grew and was strengthened by the Three Mile Island disaster in 1979.

With respect to energy governance and policy, solar energy became part of the mix of proposals for a new national energy policy. There were competing solar niche technologies and associated actors, with different relationships to the existing configuration of the system. During the ferment of energy policy change of the 1970s, solar advocates were divided between those in favor of utility-scale solar, generally led by large technology companies, and ecological advocates who supported DSE and entrepreneurial businesses (16). The strategic decision of the ecological advocates to align with the anti-nuclear energy movement may have won popular support. However, their coalition decision also weakened opportunities for the solar advocates to gain support from government funders and the utilities, which favored the existing configuration of the electricity system with baseload power from nuclear energy and coal (16). At the time, the coal-gas competition among incumbent actors was less salient than in later periods. The tension in the solar niche continued during the next half century, with utility-scale forms gaining dominance. One might argue that the dominance of utility-scale solar over DSE was a strictly economic outcome because of its lower cost than DSE under most circumstances, but the strategic decision to align with the anti-nuclear and ecological movements also weakened the potential for DSE to receive support from industrial incumbents and their allies in government positions.

Policy support was crucial for developing any of the emerging renewable energy technologies at the time. During the administration of President Jimmy Carter (1976–1980), a Democrat, opportunities opened somewhat for DSE. The Energy Tax Act of 1978 and some state government policies provided tax credits and other financial incentives for solar installations (18). Research and development funding helped to reduce the cost of photovoltaic energy fivefold during the 1970s, and the federal government’s Block Buy Program helped to create initial demand (19).

However, incumbents associated with the utility industry developed various tactics for limiting the development of the solar niches. During the time when some funding was flowing toward solar, one tactic was niche selection, that is, an attempt to steer government funding for niche development in incumbent-compatible directions. Thus, where government funding for solar research became available, it followed the utilities’ preference of support for demonstration projects for utility-scale solar (20). In addition, utilities and allies in the government also developed tactics to support niche containment for solar heating systems. For example, the incumbent actors developed demonstration projects and advertising campaigns that portrayed the technology as prohibitively expensive, claims that advocates argued were exaggerated (20).

A more important incumbent tactic was to support political leaders who would reverse or weaken any energy transition policies. Thus, crucial political support waned during the subsequent administration of Republican President Ronald Reagan (1981–1989), when the federal government cut budgets for renewable energy research and failed to renew the federal tax credit for residential solar. The Reagan administration also cut the Block Buy commercialization program and redirected the remaining funding for solar research toward basic research (19). Likewise, in California under Republican California Governor George Deukmejian (1983–1991), the California solar credit was also ended. The loss of government support was facilitated by landscape changes in global energy markets, in which lower petroleum prices contributed to a reduction in the salience of national energy policy reform. The loss of government support caused the collapse of the solar hot water heating industry after 1985, and it also weakened opportunities for photovoltaic commercialization (19, 21). In summary, the utility strategy was to contain all solar niches and to capture any solar development funding by favoring the utility-scale niche. During this period, small-scale solar installers tended to be confined to a specialty market of off-grid applications for which they continued to improve the technology.

Market-Enabling Policies, Niche Expansion, and Coalition Development (1991–2010).

Although the United States failed to enact a national policy for DSE or renewable energy more broadly during the period before 1990, there were gradual changes that supported policies, especially after 1990 (22). Several landscape changes reopened political opportunities for incremental change that had closed down under the Reagan and Deukmejian administrations. At the federal government level, the invasion of Kuwait in 1990 and the ensuing Gulf War contributed to the return of energy policy to national salience. The National Energy Policy Act of 1992 supported all forms of energy and included important tax credits for renewable energy. The credits were renewed multiple times after 1992, but they sometimes expired before being renewed, and there were reductions of renewable energy growth during the lapses (22). The lack of political support for continuous federal credits contributed to the nonlinear pattern of development of DSE and other forms of renewable energy. However, the 1992 law also built on the initial step toward the creation of the wholesale generation market (established in the 1978 Public Utilities Regulatory Policy Act) by authorizing the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) to order wheeling of power by transmission utilities. (Wheeling is the transmission of electricity over wires for one system by another.) This aspect of the 1992 law facilitated the wave of state-government restructuring laws that provided some opportunities for both utility-scale and small-scale photovoltaic solar.

During the 1990s, the publication of reports by the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change and the negotiation of the Kyoto Protocol agreement were indications of a global landscape change of increased awareness of the need for a long-term energy transition. The control of the US presidency by a Democrat from 1993 to 2000 also opened some policy opportunities. For example, in 1997, President Bill Clinton announced the goal of installing one million solar systems on the roofs of buildings across the United States. However, Congress did not approve major reform legislation, and in 1999, the US Senate failed to ratify the Kyoto Protocol. With the election of President George W. Bush (a Republican from the fossil-fuel-oriented state of Texas) in 2000, the development of energy transition policy shifted mostly to the state level.

Even before 2000, state governments had begun to experiment with policies that supported DSE and renewable energy in general. The first renewable portfolio standard was enacted in 1983, and during the 1990s, seven states implemented such policies, some of which included support for solar energy (often both utility-scale and small-scale photovoltaics). Of more direct relevance and importance to small-scale photovoltaics were the state-level net metering policies. Net metering was not the only policy driver for DSE; however, it made small-scale photovoltaics more affordable because it paid the net of excess solar exported to the grid at the generous retail rate. A few states had such policies before 1990, but the main wave occurred during the late 1990s, when restructuring reforms were also being implemented. (See Fig. 1; the exact count of a state’s first net metering policy depends on the definition of the policy.) The Energy Policy Act of 2005, which required state governments that had not implemented net metering programs to consider implementation within three years, led to another wave of policies after 2005. An analysis of diffusion dynamics indicated that the passage of laws followed a regional pattern and may have been facilitated by information flows from regional offices of the Environmental Protection Agency (23).

Net metering policies, in combination with declining prices and additional policy support such as financial incentives, helped to bring about the gradual growth of small-scale photovoltaics but in a geographically uneven pattern. However, rapid growth in DSE was not evident until after 2005–2007. The US Energy Information Administration does not provide disaggregated data for the different solar niches during the period, but its aggregate solar data (including utility-scale solar) shows that the annual growth rate in solar capacity was low (under 3%) until 2007, when the growth rate went above 10% in some years (24). In California (the largest market), statistics are available for DSE growth starting in 2005; they indicate corresponding rapid growth in net metering capacity from 92 MW in 2005 to 642 MW in 2010 (25). By 2009, some areas of the state were reaching the previous limit of 2.5% of peak demand, and in 2010 the state legislature doubled the limit (26).

It is sometimes assumed that during the initial phase of net metering legislation, there was little or no opposition by the utilities because it was so unimportant. To better understand the extent of opposition by the utility industry, a search was conducted of media reports in the ProQuest News and Newspapers Database from January 1, 1990, through December 31, 2010, under the keyword string “net metering” and (“bill” or “law”). From 2,992 media reports, 44 cases of utility opposition to net metering in 23 states were identified, and the review indicates opposition to net metering even before DSE began to grow rapidly. The main reasons given by utilities and their supporters in government for their opposition were the cross-subsidy from non-solar customers, risks to workers, and the potential for system instability. Although the utilities attempted to block net metering laws or expansion plans in some cases, they often focused instead on having an unfavorable payment structure and a cap on the volume allowed. One reason is that net metering was still a novelty and was expensive for customers. The utilities also introduced barriers to implementation, such as high insurance levels. In several states, there were fewer than a dozen cases of homes or businesses that had pursued DSE by the year 2000.

During this period, coalitions were generally limited. The solar industry tended to find support among environmentalists, who had expanded their reasons for support from the anti-nuclear movement of the 1970s to general concern with climate change. Both utilities and the solar industry found allies in state legislatures. The utility industry often did not openly oppose initial legislation that enabled DSE, but its opposition was expressed in the details of the enabling legislation. For example, utilities often found enough allies to configure DSE with capacity limits and, in some cases, a payment structure below the retail rate. In some of the more conservative states in the South, the utility industry successfully blocked enabling legislation. There was also geographic variation in the composition of the coalition, with differences over the type of policy reform supported between conservative (“red”) and progressive (“blue”) states (27).

At the end of this period (e.g., 2009 and 2010), there were battles over the expansion of capacity limits. A record of support for and opposition to the capacity expansion bills in California in 2009 and 2010 indicates that the supporter coalition included city governments, the solar industry, environmental organizations, and the building industry. The building and construction industry saw advantages in broader energy-transition measures, such as financing for improvements in energy efficiency and solar generation. City governments were responding to voter support for decarbonization policies, their experiences with energy-efficiency and solarization measures, and in some states (e.g., California) to local-level electricity reform known as community choice.

Opposition to one version of the California bill to raise capacity limits included the main utilities, associated unions, and a consumer organization concerned with cross subsidies from non-solar customers (28). This development showed how utilities were expanding their coalition to include labor and consumer perspectives as articulated by industry-affiliated unions and consumer groups, and the expansion of frames and coalition composition continued in the next decade (29). The potential transfer of resources from non-solar-DSE to solar-DSE customers had been raised well before 2010 in some of the legislation that enabled net metering, but the issue was not important when DSE was so marginal. As DSE underwent more rapid growth in some states toward the end of this period, and as attempts to lift capacity constraints grew, the framing shift from safety and reliability to effects on ratepayers became increasingly salient.

Transition Acceleration and Policy Rollbacks (2011–2020).

By 2011, the cost of solar energy was decreasing for several reasons. The ongoing innovation of the technology and economies of scale involved a global process. The shifts in the locus of innovation from one country to another led to continual improvements and the ability for US installers to use less expensive products from Asian suppliers (19). Furthermore, the growth of third-party vendors offered new access to financing in the form of leases or power purchase agreements (payments to the building owner for on-site solar generation). New market entrants included solar leasing firms that obtained partnerships with large banks to provide a new, much more affordable model of financing for small-scale photovoltaics. For commercial DSE, financing based on property taxes was also spurring development. Utility-scale (non-DSE) solar was also becoming much more attractive financially. There was rapid growth of total solar photovoltaic energy, which by 2020 had reached 6.5% of the country’s total net summer electricity generation capacity and had also become an important part of total annual added electricity capacity (30; Table 4.2.A).

Between 2010 and 2012, the number of net metering photovoltaic customers doubled from 150,740 to 323,365, and it doubled again between 2012 and 2014 (30; Table 4.10). In response to the rapid growth of DSE, utilities mobilized in a more concerted way to contain the expansion of the niche. In 2012, the Edison Electric Institute, an organization that serves utilities, held a conference that included a presentation called “Facing the Challenges of a Distribution System in Transition.” The presentation drew critical attention to state government policies that were providing subsidies that were “accelerating this transition” (31). In addition to net metering, the presentation also drew attention to other types of state government policy support for solar energy.

This meeting showed a shift in the utility coalition strategy. First, the coalitions became more coordinated nationally and more integrated into conservative politics. After the 2012 meeting, utility executives met with public service commissioners and consumer groups in the Critical Consumer Issues Forum to discuss the problems with distributed generation. Second, the tactics expanded from opposing new net metering laws (which by 2012 were in more than 80% of the states) or capacity expansions to rolling back existing laws. An example of the shift in strategy was support from the American Legislative Exchange Council in 2014, when this conservative advocacy organization developed model legislation designed to weaken net metering (32).

During this period, the utilities also developed a broader strategy of policy interventions to contain small-scale photovoltaics. A review of policy conflicts between 2013 and 2015 identified 40 significant conflicts and found that the most frequent goal was to seek a rate reduction in compensation to a level much lower than the retail rate, or to gain approval for a monthly fee under net metering (7). Subsequent research indicated that conflicts continued through the end of the decade, and they included attempts to roll back broader renewable energy policies (9). With respect to small-scale photovoltaics, the utilities also attempted to block third-party solar (e.g., leasing), to secure a monopoly over solar installations, and to enter the solar leasing market directly. To enhance their legitimacy, the utilities often strengthened coalitions with consumer organizations and electricity-oriented labor unions. In addition, there were coalitions with individuals or groups that claimed to represent marginalized social constituencies (and some of the groups were funded by utilities). These expansions helped to portray the rollbacks not only as a consumer issue but also as a broader social justice issue. The incumbent coalition also attempted to gain rollbacks at the federal government level, for example, by requesting that FERC assert control over state government net metering policies and roll back the provisions of the 1978 Public Utility Regulatory Policy Act.

Coalitions in favor of distributed energy also underwent shifts in tactics and composition. One reason for the change is that solar customers and potential customers could now be mobilized for protests and petitions. Furthermore, the solar industry had grown and built alliances with other business sectors and industries. Thus, the pro-solar coalitions expanded to include a growing number of solar customers (both residential and industrial), third-party financing companies, their partners in the financial and technology sectors, and small businesses and community organizations that wanted access to solar energy. In some cases, such as in Colorado, there were organized public protests, but the pro-solar coalitions also adopted institutionalized tactics such as lobbying and litigation (7). To support their claims in the media and courts, the pro-solar coalitions developed studies that contested the utilities’ claims of cost shifting, and they pointed out the benefits that small-scale solar offered to the grid that were ignored in utility studies. Solar advocates also worked with local and state governments to develop resolutions and policies in support of 100% renewable energy or net-zero energy by 2035–2050.

The battles contributed to and drew on the broader landscape change in national politics toward increasing polarization. Before 2010, multiple Republican governors supported renewable portfolio standards, and the 2008 Republican presidential candidate recognized the need to address climate change. However, after 2010, support by Republican officials for energy-transition policies could trigger a primary challenge and attacks from party leadership, even for otherwise conservative candidates. Conversely, support for small-scale solar and, more broadly, for decarbonization policies continued to be favored in states controlled by the Democratic party. In conservative states, solar advocates also found some support under the frame of the property rights of building owners to have access to solar if they wanted it.

In some states, the utility coalitions achieved their goals of roll-backs of renewable portfolio standards and net metering, but other outcomes were more favorable to DSE. For example, a few states authorized new net metering programs after 2012 (e.g., Mississippi and South Carolina). However, the programs in the late-adopting conservative states came with significant restrictions, including compensation below the retail rate in Mississippi and a capacity cap in South Carolina. In a crucial battle over the large DSE market in California, the state’s utilities commission approved a successor tariff for net metering after capacity limits were reached, but a more substantially revised policy under discussion in 2021–2022 included potentially significant retrenchment for small-scale solar.

The Emerging Potential for a Settlement (from 2021 onward).

Although the policy battles for the 2011–2020 decade continue into the 2020s, there are some signs of an emerging institutional settlement, and these changes are characterized here as an emerging fourth phase. One significant development is the potential for compromises on net metering policy. The solar industry developed principles that recognize that at high levels of adoption of small-scale solar photovoltaics (e.g., 10% of system capacity), a “fair value of solar” tariff can be introduced if the value estimation includes short- and long-term benefits of distributed solar (34). Thus, the solar industry has opened the door for negotiation under some conditions, even though it maintains that net metering is still in the public interest at lower rates of adoption.

A more fundamental settlement is also beginning to emerge with respect to the integration of small-scale photovoltaics, battery storage, and other distributed energy resources into the existing system configuration, albeit with fundamental changes in the configuration. Some utilities are experimenting with new ways of using small-scale photovoltaics to improve system efficiency, resilience, and profitability. One landscape pressure is growing recognition of the need for net-zero carbon goals in response to climate change, including both government-driven policy and customer demand. Another change is growing concern with resilience in the face of climate change. This concern is contributing to the growth of microgrids, which have the capacity to operate in island mode during power outages.

Although natural-gas peaker plants remain an important source of flexibility for electricity supply, the combination of customer-owned photovoltaics and battery storage with demand management is becoming a source of both supply-side and demand-side flexibility. These combinations of distributed resources have the added advantage of reducing greenhouse gas emissions and increasing electricity resilience. One study concluded that distributed photovoltaics, hot water heating, on-site storage, and electric vehicle charging could be combined with traditional demand management (thermostat control) to add a projected 387 GW of capacity to the US electricity system by 2025 in a national system with total capacity of 1,200 GW in 2021 (35).

The realization of the projections depends in part on policy rules, but a decision by FERC in 2020 (Order 2222, followed by Order 2222-A) required system operators to develop a framework for distributed energy participation in wholesale markets. The framework can include virtual power plants, that is, a network of local generation, storage, and efficiency that could have enough capacity to replace a conventional power plant (such as a natural-gas peaker plant) and to improve resilience through localized generation. The extent to which batteries and distributed generation resources will be aggregated and integrated into wholesale markets remains uncertain, and there are ongoing conflicts over this new set of rules. However, the general capacity for these resources to solve utilities’ load management, decarbonization, and resilience goals has the potential to soften the oppositional view that utilities have adopted toward small-scale photovoltaic solar during the previous half century. The integration of DSE and related technologies into the grid provides the basis for a partial reconfiguration of the electricity system that may reduce the tensions prominent in the “solar wars” that were especially intense during the 2010 decade. This change would require ongoing policy support, but it would be of a different type than the traditional forms of support such as net metering.

Discussion and Conclusions

As early as the 1970s, the utilities recognized that on-site, small-scale generation of solar energy represented a threat not only to the historical mix of energy sources but also to the stabilized system configuration of centralized generation and transmission. In other words, the incumbent actors viewed the transition as a potential long-term substitution, and they mobilized to contain it and to select the more compatible form of grid-controlled solar farms. Five decades later, the convergence of renewable energy, distributed generation, microgrids, digitized systems, and energy storage makes it increasingly possible, both technically and economically, for customers to generate and store their own electricity. The changes carry significant risks of stranded assets for long-distance generation and transmission infrastructure, and they also create the potential for distribution organizations to develop competing local generation and energy services (36). Moreover, the original division of the niche into incumbent-compatible and incumbent-threatening forms may be shifting toward a system reconfiguration that includes virtual power plants and microgrids with small-scale solar. The new configuration may not end the decades-old conflict, but it will certainly reposition it, and it provides a pathway for reducing the conflict that has led to the decades-long efforts by incumbents to contain the growth of small-scale solar energy.

With respect to sustainability science, the analysis demonstrates the value of viewing transitions through the lens of a multicausal framework that goes beyond a focus on technology and economics to include the interaction of landscape changes, policy signals, and strategic action, which together explain nonlinearity during a multidecade period. For small-scale solar energy, there was a nonlinear pattern of openings and growth followed by closed opportunities. During the first phase of the 1970s and 1980s, a coalition of utilities and Republican lawmakers effectively shut down the emerging solar heating industry and captured funding that could have gone toward the development of small-scale photovoltaics. But during the 1990s and early 2000s, actions in multiple state governments through incremental policy changes, coupled with reduction in the price of photovoltaics, set the stage for rapid growth after 2005. This second phase of growth led to a third phase of a newly mobilized and more sophisticated national coalition in opposition to small-scale solar (and often all renewables) which, especially after 2011, actively mobilized to roll back supportive policies. However, the new wave of opposition by incumbent actors was only partially successful, and growth in all segments of the solar industry has occurred, albeit in an uneven pattern across states, which reflects variation in policy signals, climate, political ideology, consumer demand, and outcomes of policy conflicts. In summary, the transition studies perspective, as demonstrated here, can help sustainability science to develop an understanding of transition nonlinearity and stalling as emerging from tactics, goals, and coalitions of actors in addition to broader structural, economic, technological, and policy changes.

Although the analysis presented here is specific to one industry and one type of conflict within the industry, the broader framework of transition studies approaches to nonlinearity is applicable across countries and technological systems. With respect to countries, an example of similar dynamics appeared in Germany, where the coalition of the Social Democratic and Green Parties (1998–2005) led to policy shifts at the federal government level that favored the growth of small-scale solar development. However, by 2012, the high cost of electricity in Germany, driven in part by renewable-energy developments and subsidies, led to policy reforms that resulted in the slowing of the growth of solar photovoltaics (19, 37). These changes occurred amid major landscape shocks, such as the Fukushima disaster and its effects on German nuclear industry policy, and amid strategic action of coalitions associated with new entrants and incumbents. With respect to other consumption-production systems, similar dynamics involving incumbent opposition occur in numerous industries, such as efforts by the automotive industry to build highways and weaken rail and mass transportation; attempts by the natural gas industry to limit electric lighting in the early twentieth century and building electrification in the twenty-first century; by the agrifood industry to conventionalize and industrialize local and organic food innovations; and by the pharmaceutical industry to restrict the growth of the therapeutic use of nutritional interventions.

In general, incumbent responses to the challenges posed by radical innovations focus on blocking and capturing early-stage research and funding for the development of niche innovations, weakening or ending financial incentives, controlling standards, and maintaining or strengthening regulatory hurdles. The responses also include the selection of regime-compatible niche innovations, the redesign of innovations in directions compatible with the existing configuration of the industry and technological system, and claims that niche developments pose existential threats to incumbents or hardships to consumers. However, if the niche innovations do manage to scale up on the basis of a combination of structural, techno-economic, policy, and strategic factors, the incumbents may be forced to undergo more systematic reconfiguration to incorporate the changes. The analysis presented here suggests some indications of this process of incorporation of the niche and transformation of the system in the United States.

The comprehensive and longitudinal approach to sociotechnical change may also help point the way to policy solutions that are not constrained by thinking informed by historical battles. As we described earlier, there are current openings for policy to support the growth of small-scale photovoltaics that have emerged from a potential system reconfiguration that includes virtual power plants and renewable-energy microgrids. Although the historical policies remain important, there are also opportunities for new types of policy support that can be identified with integrated analyses such as the one presented here.
