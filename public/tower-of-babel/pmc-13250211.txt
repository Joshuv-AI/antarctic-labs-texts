Implications of relying on manufacturer patient support programs for access to high-cost specialty and biologic medicines: a critical qualitative study
Authors: Quinn Grundy, Dana Hart, Joel Lexchin, Navindra Persaud, Mina Tadrous, Marc-André Gagnon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesPharmaceutical manufacturers routinely launch high-cost, specialty medicines, including biologics and biosimilars, with an accompanying patient support program (PSP). PSPs offer patients financial support, case management and clinical services to facilitate access to the promoted medicine. Because these programs are proprietary, there is little publicly available information, thus, this study aimed to generate in-depth understandings of the patient, health system and policy-level implications of relying on manufacturer PSPs for affordable access to high-cost medicines.DesignQualitative, critical ethnographic study conducted from November 2023 to April 2025. We conducted an interpretive, thematic analysis, triangulating fieldnotes (40 hours observations at public events), semistructured in-depth interview transcripts (n=48 interviews with 52 participants) and documents (ie, policies, media, reports).SettingThis study examined direct experiences with manufacturer PSPs across Canada. PSPs have proliferated in Canada as the universal public health insurance scheme does not cover outpatient prescription drugs or infusion services.ParticipantsA purposive sample of 52 participants with direct experience of pharmaceutical industry PSPs, including patients prescribed specialty medicines, clinicians, pharmaceutical industry and PSP provider employees, payers and policymakers.ResultsManufacturer PSPs are the default pathway for people to afford and access high-cost specialty and biologic prescription medicines in Canada. Though they are the only care pathway available, participants experienced support as variably helpful, stressful and superfluous; variable over time and across patients’ experiences; and available at the discretion of the PSP. Across participants, four core themes were identified: (1) patients are required to engage with PSPs in exchange for access; (2) outsourcing supports for access to medicines gives rise to a parallel, private health system, generating additional health system complexity; (3) programs are inherently designed for physicians, thus adoption is prioritised over access; and (4) this results in calculated policy trade-offs and health system and patient risks when depending on manufacturer PSPs for affordable access to medicines.ConclusionsPatients and health systems are precariously dependent on manufacturers to afford and access high-cost, specialty medicines. As a parallel, private care system, there remains little transparency nor patient accountability, with access at the discretion of manufacturers through physician intermediaries. Thus, reliance on pharmaceutical industry resources for affordable access to medicines may incur greater costs for patients and health systems in the longer term. Policymakers need to consider how to design patient-centred, equitable, accountable systems that ensure affordable access to important medicines.Keywords: Drug Therapy, Patients, Health policy, QUALITATIVE RESEARCH, Health Services Accessibility

Introduction

As high-cost, specialty medicines dominate pharmaceutical spending, countries globally are grappling with how to provide access to new therapies while ensuring the sustainability of private and public drug spending. Many specialty medicines are manufactured in lab-grown cells, hence are known as ‘biologics,’ and have complicated handling, storage, administration (ie, via injection or infusion) and monitoring regimens.1 To enable faster adoption and remove barriers to access, when biologics first came to market in the early 2000s, pharmaceutical manufacturers created accompanying ‘patient support programs’ (PSPs).2 A PSP, which exclusively enrols patients prescribed the branded drug, provides financial assistance or free drugs, reimbursement navigation and case management and clinical services including self-injection training, infusion clinic booking, vaccinations, lab testing or drug delivery.2,4 While the services vary with the brand of medicine and the health system context, manufacturer PSPs are now an established commercial strategy evident worldwide.4,9

With a shift towards personalised medicine and the growing burden of chronic disease, pharmaceutical promotion seeks both to expand the market for the promoted medicine and to maximise the length of time a patient stays on a drug by ensuring a patient can both fill and re-fill their prescription and preventing switching to competitor products or cost-effective alternatives such as biosimilars.6 10 The published research on PSPs has largely been conducted by pharmaceutical company employees or researchers with company ties and is focused on the outcomes of nursing care in relation to enhanced medication adherence.511,14 Further, with growing policy scrutiny of high drug prices, the pharmaceutical industry justifies high drug prices not in terms of affordability but, in part, due to their role in facilitating access through manufacturer patient financial assistance and PSPs.15 However, it is not well understood to what extent patient access is facilitated and in what way, nor the broader health system or policy implications of relying on pharmaceutical companies for affordable access to high-cost drugs. Because these programs are proprietary, there is little publicly available information or independent research about key access considerations including the availability and functioning of PSP operations, costs and resource use, what services are provided and to whom, and how they interface with the broader healthcare system.

Canada offers a uniquely rich context for studying this phenomenon. Canada’s universal, public health insurance covers hospital and physician services, but not outpatient prescription drugs nor services required for their administration (eg, outpatient infusion therapy).16 Manufacturer PSPs have proliferated in Canada: 1 in 10 prescription medicines have a PSP, which are concentrated among high-cost biologics and biosimilars for autoimmune conditions, cancer treatment and rare diseases.3 In Canada, PSP operations are concentrated between a few third-party companies, including Innomar Strategies (a subsidiary of Cencora) and McKesson Canada, which are funded through exclusive contracts with manufacturers; these third-party companies also own and operate specialty pharmacies, infusion clinics and drug distributors.3 17

Given the opacity around PSPs,3 6 the aim of this study was to generate in-depth understandings of the patient, health system and policy-level implications of manufacturer PSPs as they relate to patients’ access to medicines. We conducted a critical policy ethnography18 to generate an in-depth, nuanced account of manufacturer PSPs as a now-established form of pharmaceutical marketing and a major development in the privatisation of healthcare, situated within broader Canadian social, economic and political contexts. Grounded in a feminist political economy approach,19 20 we examined primarily the experiences of those receiving, providing, navigating or coordinating care in the context of manufacturer PSPs, and triangulated these data with interviews with health system and industry leaders, policymakers, observations at public events and documents. In this paper, we analyse key themes expressed across participant experiences that suggest an uneasy status quo and discuss the implications of relying on commercial entities for affordable access to medicines.

Methods

Study design

Since the currently available research for the impact of PSPs is almost exclusively conducted by pharmaceutical companies,13 14 21 we undertook a critical approach to question these dominant accounts of the nature, necessity and value of PSPs within the context of a publicly funded healthcare system and relevant policy arenas. Because PSPs provide a variety of supports, including case management, pharmacy and nursing care, and are funded and/or operated by pharmaceutical companies or contracted third parties,3 we adopted a feminist political economy approach as the theoretical framework.19 20 Specifically, we sought to examine how PSPs work to create access to branded, high-cost medicines by examining the experiences and perspectives of those giving and receiving care (both formally and informally, and within and between the public and private health systems). We further situated these experiences within corporate incentive structures, neoliberal health policies, material conditions and professional mandates that shape conditions of care, asking who benefits and who is placed at risk within these contexts.20 22 The methods are reported according to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research guidelines (COREQ) (online supplemental file 1).23

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were involved in the design, or conduct, and dissemination of this research. We collaborated with two national patient associations who advised on patient recruitment strategies and disseminated recruitment materials through their social networks. We further worked with the leadership of one of these patient associations to plan for project validation and dissemination, sharing and receiving feedback on project findings with leadership and in a webinar to members. We also collaborated with two clinician organisations—a national network of nursing unions and a pharmacy continuing education network—who advised on the project direction, aims and recruitment and dissemination strategies among clinicians. Throughout the project, we presented preliminary analyses to public audiences, including policymakers and health systems leaders, employees of pharmaceutical companies and PSP providers and patients, which provided the opportunity to gain feedback on the project’s direction and gaps and to validate lines of inquiry and emerging themes.

Data sources, sampling and data collection

We triangulated three methods of data collection:18 individual interviews, observation at public events and document analysis. The lead author (QG), who is a registered nurse and qualitative researcher, conducted all interviews and fieldwork between November 2023 and April 2025; the broader research team, including physicians, a pharmacist, pharmaceutical policy and political economy experts, met regularly through data collection and analysis to incorporate multiple perspectives, challenge and question emerging interpretations and to ensure inquiry remained open-ended. QG purposively recruited people over age 18 with direct experience with manufacturer PSPs, aiming to sample a diverse range of experiences in terms of geographic location (ie, across Canada and urban, rural and remote areas) and role including: patients prescribed specialty medicines and enrolled currently or previously in a PSP (P); clinicians working in both the publicly-funded health system (eg, specialist physicians, pharmacists, health system administrators) and the private health system (eg, private infusion clinic nurses, PSP nurses and case managers) (CLIN); pharmaceutical industry and private insurance company employees (IND); and policymakers including public payers (POL). Study recruitment flyers and emails were circulated through the authors’ professional networks, at public events, through direct email contact and snowball sampling. We aimed to recruit approximately 15 participants per category to achieve sufficient information power; we arrived at this sample size by balancing the exploratory study aim and the lower sample specificity (accounting for multiple different roles and experiences) with the use of theory, and the inclusion of multiple forms of data, including in-depth, narrative interviews.24

Prospective participants then contacted QG directly to express interest. Following brief eligibility screening via email, QG scheduled a 60-minute interview and emailed all eligible participants a study information and consent form and requested that they read, review and sign prior to the interview, with the option to ask any questions prior to signing. At the outset of the interviews, QG introduced the research team’s purpose: to understand people’s experiences and the health system and policy implications of medication-related care occurring outside the public health system (online supplemental file 2). QG answered participants’ questions about the study, confirmed written consent and asked for verbal assent to proceed; in some cases, participants elected to provide verbal assent only to further protect their anonymity.

Consistent with a feminist, interpretive approach, we sought to generate in-depth understandings of the development and policy impact of industry PSPs that were grounded in the experiences of those that provide and receive care.19 20 QG conducted in-depth interviews guided by open-ended questions, which explicitly prompted participants to share concrete examples of a range of experiences with PSPs and tailored to the participant’s role (see online supplemental file 2). Most interviews were conducted virtually or via telephone, with two conducted in-person at workplaces. With participant written and verbal consent, interviews were audio-recorded; participants were also given the option to speak ‘off record’ or to pause the recording at any time. As a token of appreciation, at the end of the interview, all participants except for government employees (who are prohibited from accepting gifts or payments) were offered a CAD$50 gift card. All recordings were transcribed, then checked for accuracy by QG or DH, and de-identified.

QG also conducted targeted participant observations (~40 hours) at conferences, webinars and professional meetings (open to the public or for registered participants) where PSPs were an active topic of discussion, taking field notes and conducting informal interviews during these events. Documents shared during public events (eg, conference programs) or by participants (eg, patient brochures, pharmacy receipts, policies) were collected for analysis.

Data analysis

The text for analysis included all transcripts, field notes and documents. Prioritising patient experiences, QG wrote an interpretive summary for each patient participant and developed an initial coding scheme; a second author, DH, who was familiar with the data, validated the coding scheme through an in-depth review and revision in discussion with QG. Interpretive analysis of participants’ accounts allowed for examining the day-to-day, practical activities and experiences, and analysis of participants’ location and orientation within social structures and relationships.25 Within each code, QG and DH inductively identified key themes and further developed the coding scheme to enable analysis across experiences.25 QG then coded all interviews, field notes and documents iteratively and inductively in Word, writing interpretive memos to develop a thematic analysis. The full team met at regular intervals throughout data collection and analysis to review emerging lines of inquiry, preliminary analyses and to question and discuss emerging themes and insights.

Results

We recruited 52 participants who participated in 48 interviews (lasting 25 to 87 min). We attribute findings to participants using a unique study ID indicating their primary role (ie, P=patients (n=18); CLIN=clinicians and health system administrators (n=19); IND=industry employees (n=5); POL=policymakers (n=10)). Within each category, we continued recruiting participants until recurring patterns across experiences arose, then, explicitly sought to recruit people who might have a different experience based on their social identities or disease or treatment experience (for patients), or clinical specialty, employer or professional role. We stopped recruiting when we determined we had sufficient information power to address the study aim.24

Participants across roles described biologic medicines as transformative for people living with severe and often debilitating autoimmune conditions, describing the time ‘before’ and ‘after’ biologics came to market and contrasting how biologics helped to restore function, prevent hospitalisation, and promote quality of life. In the time between the ‘before’ and the ‘after,’ pharmaceutical manufacturers sought to promote their rapid adoption, implementing a suite of “solutions to barriers” (IND2). Now an established and expected status quo, participants described reliance on manufacturer PSPs for affordable access to biologics and other specialty medicines and expressed uncertainty about depending on manufacturers for these supports.

Our analysis identified four themes that collectively illustrate how reliance on pharmaceutical manufacturers to provide necessary medication-related care results in an uneasy status quo with a shifting of risks and burdens onto patients and the public, in the longer term. We found that (1) engagement with the PSP was a requirement for access and served as the only way to afford and access high-cost specialty medicines; (2) patients’ experience of precarious dependence is a direct, structural consequence of outsourcing PSP services to a complex, closed, parallel, private health system, funded by manufacturers and operated by their contractors; (3) because PSPs are designed largely for physicians to enable the rapid adoption of promoted medicines, patients’ care is often fragmented and complex and requires that patients take on coordination work and (4) health system dependence on manufacturers exists within an uneasy status quo, as PSP design and delivery is shaped by the commercial drug lifecycle and competition dynamics. Engagement with the PSP as a requirement for access

Once their physician decided to prescribe the specialty medicine, the PSP was the only way for the patient to access it—the PSP enrollment form and the prescription were the same document, which was faxed directly to the company operating the PSP. Within a day or so, the patient received a call from the PSP case manager, a moment which they described as feeling “overwhelmed” (P8, P15) and “in a cloud” (P10), often unsure who was calling or why, while also experiencing severe symptoms and, often, disability, and the stress of starting a ‘heavy-duty’ treatment with uncertainty around whether they had coverage or could afford such an expensive medicine.

During this initial period, the PSP case manager collected detailed information from the patient about their insurance coverage. Then, patients received another call from the PSP—this time, a nurse—to obtain their medical history, which was required to complete insurance forms such as prior or special authorisation. Given the enormous volume and complexity of insurance paperwork and the need to coordinate between the pharmacy, infusion clinic, and lab, patients largely characterised the PSP case management as helpful. The PSP, however, was the default and only care pathway; patients could not access services such as infusion care for their specialty medicines through the publicly funded system. However, patients also received brochures and branded symptom journals, sharps containers, travel kits and other gifts by mail and gained access to dedicated websites, which outlined the promised supports far beyond assistance completing insurance forms. The PSPs typically outlined a “comprehensive”26 support “journey,”27 with supports encompassing education about the treatment, help accessing the medication, and provision of ongoing support28 “at every stage of your treatment …not just at the start of therapy, but for as long as you need us.”28

Crucially, participants underscored that PSPs provided a lifeline in terms of offering free drugs while awaiting coverage decisions, or financial assistance to offset the considerable financial burden of deductibles or co-pays for their high-cost drugs. “Realistically,” explained a PSP nurse, without the PSP’s financial assistance, “I don’t think anyone would be able to afford it flat out” (CLIN1). Industry participants described the financial assistance as built into PSP design: “Making sure cost is never a barrier to treatment” (IND2). However, this lifeline was fraught with uncertainty. Patients in a PSP had no way to know whether, or for how long, they could access financial assistance or free drug supply. As one patient articulated: “If the company decides, ‘It’s not worth it for us to do this,’ there’s just that uncertainty. There’s no contract… Then I’d be out-of-pocket another $5,000 a year or something” (P6). Patients realised that whether and to what extent they had access to these supports, especially financial support, was at the sole discretion of the PSP.

Over time, patients found their experiences variable, both within individual experiences and across multiple experiences, which contrasted with the personalised, comprehensive, and tailored support initially advertised. Characterised as “sometimes helpful, sometimes not” (P2), patients sensed that the PSP was working “in the background.” The PSP case manager telephoned periodically, often to administer standardised symptom questionnaires, which patients thought likely pertained to their insurance coverage; patients acknowledged this was necessary and helpful, but at times found it invasive or a duplication of assessments conducted by their clinicians. Most characterised contact with PSPs as “impersonal,” noting that staff turnover was high, and was largely restricted to securing initial coverage then dropped off over time. However, patients underscored their need for greater support, and when helpful, patients knew their PSP case manager by name and relied on them heavily. In a few cases, the PSP served as a patient’s main point of contact with the health system, helping to navigate drug shortages, or connecting patients with providers when they experienced disruptions in care (eg, their physician retired or relocated).

Consequently, participants across roles, but especially patients, sensed they should not ‘rock the boat’ to avoid jeopardising access to these medicines. Those receiving financial assistance explained that they simply “didn’t ask why” (P4), hoping that if they did not call attention to it, the PSP would continue sending free drugs or paying their co-pay. Others, however, experienced being “cut-off” (P3, P11, P17), and in some cases, had to advocate for themselves, negotiating with the PSP, sometimes over text message, to negotiate co-pay support. Ultimately, some participants had to incur large costs out-of-pocket or had to change medications. A nurse summed up this uncertainty as, “some secret lie that’s kind of known but not known,” where patients who were told they needed this drug to “be healthy” were left with “this kind of wrangling, the figuring out of the drug company, how much are they going to charge today or will the insurance system pay for the drug or not?” (CLIN3)

Outsourcing access creates a parallel private health system

Reliance on pharmaceutical manufacturers has resulted in patients being referred to and receiving care within a parallel and, increasingly, closed and unaccountable private health system. Figure 1 provides an overview of the number of different entities that patients typically encountered in accessing their specialty medication. Most manufacturers had exclusive agreements with a third-party PSP provider, which in turn might have exclusive agreements with an infusion clinic network or specialty pharmacy; some manufacturers worked with an array of vendors providing PSP services, infusion or specialty pharmacy services to tailor PSP delivery to geographic area, prescriber preferences or indication. Most PSP providers, however, were vertically integrated, with infusion clinics, specialty pharmacy, and even drug distribution provided within the same company. Because the PSP is brand-exclusive, patients prescribed multiple specialty medicines dealt with multiple PSPs, pharmacies and infusion clinics simultaneously, or had to re-enrol with a different PSP, and thus often a different infusion clinic and specialty pharmacy, when changing medicines. The proliferation of brand-specific PSPs also created complexity for providers: some specialist practices had hired a coordinator to manage all the PSPs in their therapeutic area. The PSP pathway excluded the patient’s primary care provider, and often, their community pharmacy, where patients accessed non-specialty medicines for other health conditions.

The result of pharmaceutical companies’ outsourcing strategies, exclusivity within contractual relationships, and vertical integration of PSP providers with private, for-profit clinical services was that patients and clinicians experienced a high degree of complexity and fragmentation of the care they received or delivered. Sometimes, patients’ care occurred seamlessly along this pathway, but often patients experienced challenges stemming from role confusion, miscommunication, and discoordination of care given the number of entities and people involved:

It gets to be very confusing for families. They get confused about, “I just don’t know who to call.” And then you’ve got a program [for the insurer] who also has nurses there. Then you’ve got their clinic nurse, and you’ve got their infusion nurse. (CLIN8)

Consequently, patients described having to take on case management of their own PSP case manager and care providers to ensure no one “dropped the ball” (P10). Participants noted that there was no one with oversight of the entire care pathway and thus questioned, “Who’s checking? What is the [PSP’s] role? Are they in the circle of care? What are their responsibilities?” (CLIN5) Patients and clinicians described a lack of care coordination mechanisms—sometimes the PSP referred patients back to their provider or reported adverse events by fax, but sometimes this did not occur, or the providers did not find it helpful. Infusion clinic nurses described reaching the “end of line,” with a patient in the infusion chair, and only then identifying critical gaps in patient education and communication that had occurred and needing to bypass the PSP to deal directly with prescribers or pharmacists to safely infuse the drug. As patients became established on the treatment, many characterised the PSP as “another cook in the kitchen that’s kind of disrupting things” (P2).

Access to promote adoption

At an industry event, industry participants explained that the commercial rationale for a PSP is “an enabling function,” meaning that PSPs are designed to overcome barriers to prescribing to promote more rapid adoption and access to the medicine. Participants noted that prescribers remained the focal point for PSP services, explaining that “sometimes the patient is not the closest to the decision, so you really have to be in tune with where you need to support based on that” (IND2). Physician participants explained that up to a quarter of patients in their busy specialist clinics (roughly 10 patients/day) were prescribed a specialty medicine, and that dealing with the corresponding paperwork was “one of the most painful parts of my job” (CLIN10). Patients typically went through weeks of delay before receiving a coverage decision; if coverage was denied, patients and their physicians faced beginning the process anew, completing numerous insurance forms for an alternative medication. Patients, too, appreciated this burden on physicians, with some believing that delegating this work to the PSPs might create greater capacity within the health system.

Multiple participants acknowledged that PSP effectively stood for ‘physician support program’ and physicians affirmed that PSPs, in taking on the administrative aspects of access to specialty medicines, allowed them to focus on medical concerns and not paperwork. However, the line between administrative and medical was sometimes difficult to delineate: while physicians characterised processes such as filling out prior authorisation forms for insurance purposes as ‘administrative,’ nurses working for the PSPs conducted in-depth clinical assessments over the phone to document treatment and symptom histories, administered standardised symptom assessments to document change over time, coordinated diagnostic testing, advocated for patients around payer’s criteria, and coordinated with the specialist’s practice. Apart from signing off, patient and payer participants expressed concern about the minimal degree to which physicians may be involved in patients’ ongoing management. Consequently, patients sometimes experienced similar issues with fragmentation and lack of coordination, finding it difficult to identify or follow-up with the person responsible for the process. In some cases, patients and their caregivers again took on the work of managing their PSP case managers, asserting their right to be included in communications between the PSP and their physician’s office (P2, P9, P15).

Physician practices depended on PSP support and acknowledged that this in turn would “funnel prescriptions toward their medication and obviously brought some profits” (CLIN14). Industry participants explained that the level of service provided by a PSP was critical to “getting a physician’s trust, and therefore the prescription, and therefore the sales. There’s a straight line there” (IND2). Sales representatives dropped off PSP enrolment forms and facilitated opportunities for physicians to meet the PSP nurse at sponsored dinner events (CLIN11). However, participants across roles were concerned about the degree to which physicians now relied on these services, as there was not an “obligation, but an expectation’” (IND5) that companies provide these services, and the extent to which they influenced treatment decision-making.

A precarious status quo

Participants across roles described an uneasy status quo. Participants questioned to what extent the proliferation of PSPs represented “calculated trade-offs” (P17): for both originator and biosimilar or generics manufacturers, launching with a PSP seemed necessary to compete in the market, but operating a health service like infusion care was costly due to the need for clinical staffing; for policymakers, PSPs provided early access to new therapies, but worked against the introduction of cost-effective alternatives down the line; and for the public health system, relying on a default system of privatised care reduced system readiness and capacity.

The PSP model had evolved, driven by commercial interests, to fill health system gaps and remove prescribing barriers—then proliferated. Proliferation occurred in terms of the number of different medications accompanied by a PSP, but also in terms of the services offered as each sought “the competitive edge” (CLIN14), precipitating a cascade of service additions in therapeutic areas with multiple options: “If one’s offering mental health, the other one will offer mental health. If one’s offering some other advantage, fecal calprotectin four times a year, the other ones will follow suit” (CLIN14). However, both clinician and industry participants questioned whether a PSP always met a “validated” need in the therapeutic area (IND5), or, whether manufacturers launched a medication with a PSP by default because of the need to compete within the market: “if you don’t provide it, are you disadvantaged commercially?” (IND5)

Now over 20 years since the first originator biologics and their PSPs were introduced to the Canadian market, many have come off-patent and faced biosimilar or generic competition. At industry conferences and within interviews, employees of both originator and biosimilar pharmaceutical companies questioned whether it remained commercially strategic to continue providing this level of support, in particular, case management and clinical services, and sought opportunities to shift clinical service delivery onto the publicly funded system. The impetus for potentially withdrawing or scaling back PSP services, however, differed depending on whether the medication was an originator, biosimilar or generic and its commercial lifecycle stage. For example, as products “matured,” came off patent and faced biosimilar or generic competition, employees of originator manufacturers at industry conferences discussed the tensions among maintaining the prescriber and patient experience and brand loyalty, while decreasing costs or shifting resources to the company’s new on-patent products. For originator manufacturers, the PSP could also serve to defend against biosimilar competition through brand loyalty2 29 and extending the drug’s life cycle; for example, pharmaceutical industry leaders and consultants emphasised the potential for PSPs to generate commercially valuable data, which could support the application and launch of new indications for existing biologics.30 31 In contrast, for biosimilar or generic manufacturers, participants described the expectation that they provide equivalent PSP services on market entry as risking ‘failure to launch’ or market withdrawal given the costs of operating an equivalent PSP at a lower medicine price point.

Participants across roles, however, questioned how to maintain the continuity of necessary services, framing it as a practical and ethical question. One sales representative questioned, once a company provides support, whether free drug or nursing services, “how do you justify the stopping of it?” (IND4) Policymakers described the challenges of trying to introduce policies to incentivise or require switching to cost-effective alternatives, such as biosimilars, to their health systems because prescribers would question whether patients would have continued access to the same level of supports provided by the originator manufacturer. They noted that biosimilar companies sometimes struggled to provide the same level of infusion support, which could delay or deter market entry. Further, the existence of exclusive agreements among originator manufacturers, PSP providers and specialty pharmacies meant that the patients remained on the originator or brand drug (often with financial assistance from the PSP) and substitution for cost-effective alternatives never occurred, working against incursion of biosimilars or generics into market share. One policymaker characterised this as a “double hit that is quite problematic” (POL3), noting that from a long-term perspective, the existence of this parallel, private and vertically integrated health system for high-cost specialty and biologic medicines undermined competition in the market and would ultimately lead to even higher drug pricing. Given these tensions, policymakers noted that industry did not “brag” about their PSPs, despite spending “a lot of money doing all this work,” instead choosing “to talk about gaps in the system rather than the fact that the manufacturers fill the gaps” (POL5).

Discussion

When patients are prescribed high-cost specialty and biologic medicines in Canada, they are referred to a private, parallel, fragmented, and opaque care system as the default mechanism by which to afford and access their medication. While providing some patients early and affordable access to medicines, the abdication of medication-related care for people living with serious, chronic conditions to pharmaceutical manufacturers and their vendors results in a precarious dependence on commercial interests and a shifting of longer-term risk and burdens on patients, caregivers and the public.

These findings raise concerns about relying on pharmaceutical manufacturers for sustainable, affordable access to high-cost therapies. Because PSP services are designed to generate and maintain prescriptions for the promoted medicine, the continuity of care is dependent on the company’s commercial interest. With shifting commercial incentives and competition dynamics over the life of a patented drug, PSPs are designed to promote rapid adoption and uptake of new, high-cost and on-patent drugs and defend against biosimilar or generic competition. However, these findings suggest that the industry is also actively looking to scale back or discontinue services or shift services to the publicly funded system, especially for medicines whose patent protections have expired. Relying on manufacturers for affordable access to new medicines may mean that the payers pay three times in incurring, first, the high costs of drugs which included the cost of the PSP up front, second, the additional costs reflected in even higher drug prices should cost-effective alternatives fail to launch or withdraw from the market, and third, in taking on service delivery should manufacturers discontinue their PSP.

Reliance on industry for affordable access has created a parallel private health system with no clear minimum standards for access, continuity or comprehensiveness of services and little public oversight. When physician practices shifted the work involved in securing and maintaining drug coverage and access to the PSP provider, this represented a form of privatisation as publicly funded physician practices were no longer primarily responsible for these processes. The precarious dependence and high degree of variability that patients experience in working with PSPs is, thus, a direct, structural consequence of outsourcing essential medication-related care to private, commercial entities and their network of exclusive relationships. Through exclusive distribution networks, contractual agreements and affiliated services, PSP providers can increasingly steer patients toward their own services, creating a closed care system that may limit patient choice and autonomy.32 Running counter to the goals of health equity, these findings are consistent with recent studies that suggest PSPs allow pharmaceutical companies to opaquely and differentially discriminate in terms of who is granted financial support, while gleaning a large amount of personal and health information in the process.15

Thus, patients and the health system remain in a precarious dependence. While some question the extent to which the proliferation of PSPs reflects true clinical need, these findings also point to the necessity of case management, nursing and pharmacy care for people living with complex, chronic diseases. Because PSPs provide branded care that is exclusive to the drug, only patients prescribed medicines with a PSP are eligible for this clinically necessary, highly valued care and only for the duration of their treatment.

Strengths and limitations

This study is the first in-depth investigation of PSPs, triangulating multiple forms of data and multiple perspectives among those who directly receive, deliver, design, coordinate or administrate care within the context of PSPs. However, this study has limitations. Because the sample was purposive, it is therefore not representative; though it included participants who had direct experience with PSPs, the focus of purposive sampling was on PSPs for a few conditions for which biologics are well-established. There may be important differences in PSP operations or impact in other therapeutic areas such as oncology or rare diseases. We were limited in our ability to recruit specialist physicians and employees of brand or originator pharmaceutical companies; however, despite the smaller sample sizes within some clinician and industry subgroups, the in-depth, narrative nature of the interviews and incorporation of published documents from these sources meant that we achieved a high degree of information power with a small number of interviews.24 This study was conducted in Canada, which offered a uniquely rich context for study. Whether and how these insights transfer to other health system contexts is an important area for future work.

Conclusions

Manufacturer PSPs are the default care pathway for people prescribed specialty medicines, meaning that patients must choose between engaging with the PSP or not taking the medicine. As an established commercial strategy, PSPs are a form of branded care, engendering prescriber loyalty and factoring into treatment decision-making, and may generate barriers to utilisation of cost-effective treatment alternatives. PSPs provide access to essential clinical care for people with complex, chronic disease, in community settings and within nursing and pharmacy scopes of practice; however, because it occurs within a parallel, private system and is drug-specific, the care pathway remains fragmented and lacks coordination. To build health system capacity and readiness towards equitable care of people with complex and chronic diseases, public health systems need to invest in community health and case management services that care for people safely, equitably and holistically.
