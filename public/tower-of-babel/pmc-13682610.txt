Methionine supplementation during late gestation in March calving Angus crossbred beef heifers and cows
Authors: Dempster M Christenson, Landon F Tadich, Rick N Funston
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe objectives of these two studies were to evaluate the impact of excess Met (methionine) supplementation during late gestation on intake, body weight (BW), average daily gain (ADG), reproductive performance, and subsequent calf performance in artificially inseminated heifers and naturally bred cows. In Exp. 1, a 3-yr study used 38 pregnant Black Angus crossbred heifers/yr (n = 113) in a Calan gate feeding system. There was a 20-d acclimation period before the 93 ± 6-d trial began 161 ± 6 d after artificial insemination. Heifers were assigned to one of three treatments and fed ad libitum hay with either no supplement (HAY1), 0.9 kg/d dried distillers’ grain (DDG), or 0.9 kg/d dried distillers’ grain with 30 g/d of a methionine hydroxy analog (MET1). In Exp. 2, a 3-yr study used 145 pregnant Red Angus crossbred cows/yr (n = 436) on upland winter range fed one of five treatments: no supplement (NS), ad libitum hay (HAY2), 0.45 kg/d of a distillers’-based cube (CAKE1), 0.9 kg/d of a distillers-based cube (CAKE2), or 0.9 kg/d of a distillers’-based cube plus 30 g/d of a methionine hydroxy analog (MET2). In Exp. 1, hay dry matter intake during the trial period was not different (P = 0.38) but final BW and ADG were greater (P < 0.01) in DDG and MET1 compared to HAY1 heifers. In Exp 2, NS cows had significantly lower body condition score (BCS) at the end of the trial period than other treatments (P < 0.01). Similarly, pre-calving and pre-breeding BW were also less in NS cows than other treatments (P < 0.01). Pregnancy rates for HAY2 (94%), CAKE1 (95%), CAKE2 (94%), and MET2 (94%) were significantly greater (P < 0.01) than NS (80%) cows. There was a tendency (P = 0.09) for calves from HAY2 cows to have greater marbling compared to NS. There were no other differences in calving performance or progeny carcass characteristics in either experiment. While late gestational protein supplementation impacted BW and ADG in heifers, and BW and BCS in cows, there were no significant differences in the dam or progeny due to Met supplementation.

Introduction

Gestational nutrition is crucial to both dam and progeny during pregnancy, and the success that they have following parturition (Short et al. 1990; Paradis et al. 2017). Undernutrition during mid to late gestation decreases secondary muscle fibers and intramuscular adipocytes of the conceptus affecting future skeletal muscle growth (Du et al. 2010). The likely outcome of poor gestational nutrition may include poor calf performance (Funston et al. 2010), and dams that have reduced body weight, pregnancy rate, and delayed time of pregnancy (Houghton et al. 1990; Short et al. 1990; Bohnert et al. 2013). Conversely, supplementation of crude protein (CP) and metabolizable energy (ME) in excess of requirements decreased calf body weight (BW) at weaning but increased post weaning average daily gain (ADG) and carcass quality (Zago et al. 2020). While supplemental nutrient requirements may change drastically across production systems, it is important to both fulfill requirements while not over supplementing. During winter months in the Nebraska Sandhills, CP and total digestible nutrients (TDN) of forage is inadequate for late-gestation cows (DelCurto et al. 2000) and could lead to amino acid (AA) deficiencies in utero-placental fluids and maternal serum if gestating cows are not supplemented (Crouse et al. 2019). A study supplementing dams with 3.5 to 3.7 g/d bypass Met during the periconception period reported no changes in BW or body condition score (BCS), but cows tended to produce greater milk protein concentrations and steer progeny had greater neutral detergent fiber and acid detergent fiber digestion than control or a fishmeal treatment (Silva et al. 2021). Another study reported greater fetal longissimus muscle weight at 63 d gestation in Met supplemented dams compared to Met deficient dams (Hauxwell et al. 2023).

Epigenetics can be defined as heritable changes in gene expression resulting from alterations in chromatin structure without changes in DNA sequence and is influential at each stage of gestation (Funston and Summers 2013). There are three main processes that can affect a nutrient’s ability to promote epigenetic changes: deoxyribonucleic acid methylation, histone modifications, and non-coding micro ribonucleic acid. When Met is restricted, hypomethylation is expected to occur, reducing the transcription ability, which reduces metabolism and growth potential. Although Met is often the first limiting AA (Richardson and Hatfield 1978), there are other naturally occurring nutrients involved with one-carbon metabolism such as folate, choline, vitamin B12 and betaine, which are used to synthesize each other when concentrations of these compounds are low (Crouse et al. 2025). Excess Met can be the substrate for these compounds when these compounds are limited, and any remaining excess can follow the methyl donor pathway, which has been shown to increase deoxyribonucleic acid methylation in the gestating calf (Liu et al. 2020; Crouse et al. 2025). We hypothesized excess rumen protected Met supplementation during late gestation may benefit heifer growth and feed efficiency, cow BW gain, their reproductive performance, and their progeny performance.

Materials and methods

Exp. 1

The University of Nebraska Animal Care and Use Committee approved the procedures and facilities used in this experiment (Protocol #1692). Heifers were purchased from a local producer at weaning in October and developed at West Central Research, Extension, and Education Center in North Platte, NE. In Exp. 1, a three-year study from 2017 to 2019 used March calving artificially inseminated (AI) pregnant Black Angus crossbred heifers (n = 113). Heifers were stratified by BW and placed in a Calan Broadbent (American Calan, Northwood, NH) individual feeding system. An open 3-sided barn containing 10 headgates per pen had 4 pens extending outside. Starting in October, 40 pregnant heifers/yr were acclimated over 20 d before beginning the 93 ± 6-d trial at 161 ± 6 d of gestation. Following the acclimation period, heifers were assigned to one of 4 pens blocked by BW and assigned to one of three treatments stratified by age, AI sire, and BW. From late October to early February heifers were provided: ad libitum hay with either no supplement (HAY1; n = 37; Table 1), 0.9 kg/d dried distillers’ grains (DDG; n = 38; Table 1), or 0.9 kg/d dried distillers’ grains with 30 g/d of a methionine hydroxy analog (MET1; n = 38; MFP feed supplement, Novus International Inc.; supplied 10.6 g/d Met). Both HAY1 and DDG treatments are likely supplementation plans for March calving late gestation heifers in this region that provide adequate or excess CP. Other studies supplemented 5.3 g/d Met or less to meet the needs of heifers (Hersom et al. 2009; Clements et al. 2017; Silva et al. 2021), but this study supplied excess. Hay was offered twice daily with supplementation top dressed once daily in the morning. The weight of hay offered was recorded daily. Feed refusals were collected once weekly and recorded. Total hay intake minus orts and nutrient analysis conducted at Ward Laboratories (Kearney, NE) was used to calculate average daily dry matter intake. Data for heifers that failed to acclimate to the Calan Broadbent feeding system or failed to calve during the first calving season were removed from the study. Removal from the study did not correspond with any treatment. The number of heifers that completed the study varied by year and treatment (yr 1 = 38, yr 2 = 40, yr 3 = 35; HAY1 = 37, DDG = 38, MET1 = 38).

The feeding trial ended at 254 ± 0.6 d of gestation. All heifers entered a drylot for calving and were offered 8.6 kg/d grass hay, 1.25 kg/d corn, and 1.34 kg/d wet corn gluten fiber (dry matter basis). Calving began 16 ± 1 d after the trial period ended. Calves were individually weighed within 24 h of birth. An experienced technician scored calving difficulty (1 = no assistance, 2 = easy assist, 3 = difficult assist, 4 = caesarian, 5 = breech/abnormal presentation) and calf vigor (1 = nursed immediately, 2 = nursed on their own after some time, 3 = required assistant to suckle, 4 = died shortly after birth, 5 = dead on arrival). All values for calving difficulty less than ‘2’ were changed to ‘0’ and all others were assigned a score of ‘1’ for binomial analysis. All values for calf vigor greater than ‘1’ were changed to ‘0’ for binomial analysis.

First-calf heifers and calves were immediately placed on upland range after calving in the Nebraska Sandhills where the same feed was provided until the end of April when grass growth was adequate to sustain pairs without supplementation. Cows were administered prostaglandin F2а (Lutalyse High-Con, Zoetis) in late May and 2 bulls were introduced for 55 d. Pregnancy status was determined in late September by an experienced technician using ultrasound. First-calf heifers grazed pasture until calving season began mid-February when they began calving season feeding management in drylot. Calving difficulty and calf vigor data were collected in the same manor. Three-year-old cows and their second calf were then sold.

First parturition calves were administered vaccines for bovine rhinotracheitis, parainfluenza-3 virus, bovine respiratory syncytial virus, and bovine viral diarrhea type I and II (BoviShield 5; Zoetis, Florham Park, NJ) at approximately 2 mo of age. At this time, calves were also branded, weighed, and bull calves were castrated. One month before weaning in November, calves were administered Bovishield 5 (Zoetis). At weaning, calves were vaccinated against bovine rotavirus-coronavirus, clostridium perfringens type C and D, and Escherichia coli bacterin-toxoid (Guardian; Intervet, Millsboro, DE); and topical endectocide was applied (Ivermectin; Aspen Veterinary, Liberty, MO). Calves received a Synovex Choice implant (Zoetis; 200 mg of trenbolone acetate and 14 mg of estradiol benzoate) at weaning and were reimplanted with a Synovex Plus (Zoetis; 200 mg trenbolone acetate and 24 mg of estradiol benzoate) halfway between the first implant and slaughter. Calves were transitioned to a calf feedlot diet over 14 d (Table 2) in drylot. The calf feedlot diet was maintained for 69 ± 8 d until calves were approximately eleven months of age when they transitioned to the finishing diet. The finishing diet was maintained until slaughter at approximately 14 months of age (Tyson Foods, Lexington, NE; 221 ± 3 d on feed). Slaughter date was chosen based on days-on-feed and historical end points for genetically similar cattle targeted for between 1.3 and 1.5 cm of 12th rib fat. Slaughter BW was calculated by adjusting hot carcass weight to a common dressing percentage of 63%. Carcass data were collected by camera 24 h later and included hot carcass weight, marbling score (300 to 399 = slight; 400 to 499 = small; 500 to 599 = modest; 600 to 699 = moderate; 700 to 799 = slightly abundant; 800 to 899 = moderately abundant), 12th rib fat thickness, longissimus muscle (LM) area, and yield grade (YG).

Exp. 2

The University of Nebraska Animal Care and Use Committee approved the procedures and facilities used in this experiment (Protocol #1474). In Exp. 2, a three-year study from 2016 to 2018 used March calving Red Angus crossbred primiparous and multiparous cows (n = 436; 3/4 Red Angus, 1/4 Simmental) on upland winter range at the Gudmundsen Sandhills Laboratory, Whitman, NE. Cow age ranged from 2 to 8 years at breeding time each year. The average cow age at breeding time increased from year to year (yr 1 = 3.2 ± 0.1, yr 2 = 3.9 ± 0.1, yr 3 = 4.6 ± 0.1). At the start of the breeding season each year in early June, all cows were administered prostaglandin F2а (Lutalyse Highcon, Zoetis, Florham Park, NJ) 5 d after introduction to summer range with a bull to cow ratio of 1:17 for 45 days. Pregnancy status was determined using transrectal ultrasonography at weaning October 30th each year, which determined enrollment in the study. Cows were assigned to a treatment and pasture stratified by age and previous BW. During late gestation, cows were fed 1 of 5 treatments: no supplement (NS; n = 2 pastures/yr), ad libitum bale processed hay (HAY2; n = 2 pastures/yr), 0.45 kg/cow per d of a distillers’-based cube (CAKE1; n = 2 pastures/yr), 0.9 kg/cow per d of a distillers’-based cube (CAKE2; n = 2 pastures/yr), or 0.9 kg/cow per d of a distillers’-based cube plus 30 g/cow per d methionine hydroxy analog (MET2; n = 2 pastures/yr; MFP feed supplement, Novus International Inc.; supplied 10.6 g/cow per d Met;). The NS, HAY2, CAKE1, and CAKE2 are all control treatments commonly fed to March calving late gestation cows from this region and supplementation should provide adequate or excess CP. Other studies supplemented 5.3 g/d Met or less to meet the needs of cows (Clements et al. 2017; Leão et al. 2024), but this study supplied excess. The 82 ± 4-d treatment period began each year on December 7th ± 3 d and ended prior to calving on February 27th ± 1 d. Each year, each treatment was provided in two of ten dormant upland range pastures, which were considered equivalent in size and forage quality. All pastures were formed along the same hillside and ranged from 35 to 41 ha in size. Forage biomass in August varied by year (yr 1 = 2330 kg/ha, yr 2 = 1830 kg/ha, yr 3 = 2369 kg/ha) but would be more than enough for the number of cattle during the treatment period (yr 1 = 67 to 78 kg/cow/d, yr 2 = 52 to 61 kg/cow/d, yr 3 = 68 to 80 kg/cow/d). Before the study began each year in December, esophageal canulated steers grazed three adjacent pastures and the masticated samples were analyzed (Ward Laboratories, Kearney, NE; CP = 5.5 ± 0.5%; TDN = 55 ± 2%). Springman et al. (2017) describes the forages found in the Nebraska Sandhills. Lardy (1997) describes forage sampling on native upland range and meadow pasture found in the Nebraska Sandhills. Hay used for the HAY2 treatment and during calving averaged 7.6 ± 1.4% CP and 56 ± 2% TDN (Ward Laboratories, Kearney, NE). Fifteen pregnant cows were allotted to each pasture every year, but cows were only retained in the data set if they calved (yr 1 = 147 cows, yr 2 = 147 cows, yr 3 = 142 cows; NS = 85 cows, HAY2 = 84 cows, CAKE1 = 89 cows, CAKE2 = 89 cows, MET2 = 89 cows). Cow individual BW and body condition score (BCS) were recorded prior to the start of the treatment period in December, at the end of the treatment period prior to calving in late February, prior to breeding in May, and at weaning the first of November each year. Cow BCS was assessed by an experienced technician using the 1 to 9 BCS scale (Wagner et al. 1988; Rasby et al. 2000).

Treatment on pastures rotated annually. Cows previously assigned a treatment rotated as a unit to a new pasture while repeating the treatment each year. New primiparous cows were added to subsequent years of the study to replace cows that failed to become pregnant or calve. Cows were managed as a common group from calving until weaning each year.

Two weeks prior to calving, cows were vaccinated against Clostridium perfringens type C, Escherichia Coli bacteria toxoid, bovine rotavirus, and bovine coronavirus (ScourGuard 4KC, Zoetis). Cows were provided hay in drylot until they calved. Calving began 6 ± 3 d after treatment ended each year. Calves were weighed within 24 h of birth. At birth, calves were administered a 7- way clostridial vaccine (Alpha 7, Boehringer/Ingelheim). An experienced technician scored calving difficulty (1 = no assistance, 2 = easy assist, 3 = difficult assist, 4 = caesarian, 5 = breech/abnormal presentation) and calf vigor (1 = nursed immediately, 2 = nursed on their own after some time, 3 = required assistant to suckle, 4 = died shortly after birth, 5 = dead on arrival). All values for calving difficulty less than ‘2’ were changed to ‘0’ and all others were assigned a score of ‘1’ for binomial analysis. All values for calf vigor greater than ‘1’ were changed to ‘0’ for binomial analysis.

After calving, pairs were provided hay and 0.45 kg/d per cow dried distillers’-based cube (Table 3) on dormant meadow pasture until grass growth was adequate to sustain pairs without supplementation. Pairs were moved to upland range pasture in June until weaning. Range pasture quality was sampled using canulated steers in June, July, and September each year (Ward Laboratories, Kearney, NE; June CP = 11.2 ± 0.8%, July CP = 8.5 ± 1.9%, September CP = 7.6 ± 1.4%; June TDN = 62 ± 0.3%, July TDN = 58 ± 1.8%, September TDN = 60 ± 2.4%). At breeding time in May, calves and cows were weighed, calves were vaccinated, and bull calves were castrated. Vaccinations included: infectious bovine rhinotracheitis, bovine viral diarrhea type I and II, bovine parainfluenza virus-3, bovine respiratory syncytial virus, Mannheimia haemolytica, Pasteurella multocida (Vista Once SQ, Merck), and a 7-way clostridial (Vision 7, Merck). At weaning on October 30th, calves were weighed and steers received 2 doses of Vista Once SQ 14 d apart and a 7-way clostridial with somnus (Vision 7 Somnus, Merck). Following weaning, steer calves (yr 1, n = 63; yr 2, n = 73; yr 3, n = 48) remained in a drylot with ad libitum hay for 2 weeks before being shipped 167 km to a feedlot at West Central Research, Extension, and Education Center in North Platte, Nebraska. Steer calves were developed in drylot for slaughter using the same method as Exp. 1 for 213 ± 5 d on feed. Data was not collected on heifer calves after weaning.

Experimental design and statistical analysis

For Exp. 1, data were analyzed with a mixed-model analysis of variance using the PROC GLIMMIX procedure in SAS 9.4 (Cary, NC, USA) with quantities expressed as least-squares mean (LSM) and SEM. Each heifer was considered the experimental unit in a randomized complete block design where pen assignment was the block. Heifer and calf response variables were analyzed using a linear model that included treatment as a fixed effect, calf sex as a covariate, and random effects of pen, AI sire, and the intercept within year. Response variables included Julian birth date of the heifer, AI technician, heifer BW, heifer ADG, average dry matter intake (DMI), gain to feed ratio, gestation length, calf BW, calf ADG, calving difficulty, calf vigor, first-calf heifer pregnancy rate after treatment, hot carcass BW, marbling score, ribeye area, yield grade, and backfat. Pre-treatment BW was used as a covariate when analyzing post-treatment BW and ADG. Binomial variables were analyzed using an adaptive Gauss-Hermite quadrature method with three quadrature points and a binomial (BIN) distribution option. The intercept within year random effect was removed for binomial variable analysis. First-calf heifer pregnancy rate was further analyzed to investigate a Type II error. This included an odds ratio (OR) option and analysis of HAY1 treatment compared to the combined treatments of DDG and MET1. Analysis of second-calf birth BW used the same model with the exception of AI sire within year as a random effect. Only the fixed effect of treatment was included in the analysis of pregnancy status, calving difficulty, and calf vigor of the second calf due to failure to converge.

For Exp. 2, data were first averaged within each experimental unit using the PROC MEANS procedure and then analyzed with a mixed-model analysis of variance using the PROC GLIMMIX procedure in SAS 9.4 (Cary, NC USA) with quantities expressed as LSM and SEM. Supplement treatment × pasture each year was considered the experimental unit in a randomized design. Although cows retained their treatment assignments every year of the study, longitudinal repeated measures analysis of cow BW for all three years in sequence was rejected because results for BW, BCS, and pregnancy rate suggest cows did not retain the effects of treatment from year to year. The fixed effects were year and treatment because the treatment by year effect was not significant. Response variables included cow age, cow BW, cow BCS, BCS change, cow ADG, calf Julian birth date, calf BW, calf ADG, calving difficulty, calf vigor, cow pregnancy rate after treatment, marbling score, ribeye area, yield grade, and backfat. Cow and calf BW were analyzed using a repeated measures linear model with denominator degrees of freedom determined using the Kenward-Roger approximation. In addition to the fixed effects of treatment and year, cow BW analysis also included the number of months after treatment began each year and its interaction with treatment at each weigh period (start of treatment, end of treatment, breeding time, and weaning time). In addition to the fixed effects of treatment and year, calf BW analysis also included the calf age in months and its interaction with treatment at each weigh period (birth, breeding time, weaning, feedlot entry, and slaughter). Due to generation of the lowest Akaike and Bayesian information criterion values, compound symmetry was selected for the covariance structure of cow BW and ante-dependence correlation was selected for calf BW. Experiment 1 and 2 data normality and variance assumptions were checked using standardized residual plots. Pairwise differences were evaluated using the LSMEANS option using a Tukey’s multiple comparison adjustment. Data were considered significant if P ≤ 0.05 and a tendency when P ≤ 0.10 but P > 0.05.

Results and discussion

Exp. 1

Heifer BW, ADG, intake, and calving performance are summarized in Table 4. There were no interactions between treatment and calf gender on heifer responses (P ≥ 0.15). Heifer age was not significantly different between treatments (P = 0.34). Initial heifer BW did not significantly differ among treatments (P = 0.77). Heifer end treatment BW and ADG were greater in DDG (536 ± 15 kg) and MET1 (537 ± 15 kg) compared to HAY1 (516 ± 15 kg; P < 0.01), but there were no significant differences between DDG and MET1 (P ≥ 0.98). There were also no differences observed in hay average daily DMI among heifers during the treatment period (P ≥ 0.30). Due to differences in ADG and the supplementation treatment, DDG and MET1 heifers had greater ratio of gain to hay feed than HAY1 heifers (P < 0.01), but DDG and MET1 did not significantly differ (P = 0.87). A recent study by Redifer et al. (2023) supplemented 0, 15, and 30 g/d per heifer methionine hydroxy analog for 45 d in late gestation and found no significant differences in BW or BCS. The current study provided additional energy and protein to a forage diet in the form of dried distillers’ grains combined with excess Met supplementation. The added energy and rumen degradable (RDP) and rumen undegradable protein (RUP) provided by the distillers’ grain in DDG and MET1 treatments promoted greater BW gain than HAY. Small differences in response to RUP across several studies suggest mild AA deficiency in most forage-based diets and illustrate that adequate levels of RDP may reduce the need for Met supplementation (Titgemeyer and Löest, 2001). Dried distiller’s grains have greater RUP than many supplements, which protects AA like Met from ruminal breakdown (Kleinschmit et al. 2007; Kelzer et al. 2010). In the current study, Met supplementation had no detectable impact on heifer ADG and BW during the prepartum treatment period, which suggests the protein and energy provided in the DDG treatment met any limitations there may have been in the HAY treatment and additional AA supplementation did not impact growth or intake. Length of gestation, calving difficulty, and calf vigor were not significantly different between treatments (P ≥ 0.22). First-calf birth weight was similar among treatment groups (P = 0.30). Pregnancy rate during the subsequent breeding period of the heifers that produced a calf was not significantly different (HAY1 82%, DDG = 97%, MET1 = 100%; P = 0.22). The odds of HAY1 first-calf heifers becoming pregnant was 7.1 (95% confidence interval: 0.8, 64.0) times the odds of DDG first-calf heifers becoming pregnant, but HAY1 and MET1 cannot be adequately compared for odds ratio because 100% of MET1 were pregnant. When an 80% power analysis of a binomial variable is simulated to have an 18% difference with values between 79% and 82% for population one and 97% and 100% for population two, the number of experimental units required to find a significant difference is 50 units (P = 0.05) and a tendency requires 40 units (P = 0.10). An odds ratio above 4 suggests a strong association between exposure and outcome while the power analysis indicates this study did not have enough experimental units to see a significant difference at 18%. A difference of 18% is likely a significant effect for producers suggesting this could be a Type II error. Comparing the pregnancy rate of HAY1 to the combined experimental units of both supplemented treatments (DDG and MET1) reports a significant difference (P = 0.02). This suggests supplementation is important for late-gestation heifers on a forage diet to become pregnant as first-calf heifers. Second-calf calving difficulty, calf vigor, and birth BW were not significantly different between treatments (P ≥ 0.45).

First-calf performance and carcass data is shown in Table 5. There were no interactions between treatment and calf gender on calf response (P ≥ 0.18). There was a tendency for BW differences at the start of the finishing period (P = 0.09), but BW did not significantly differ between any two treatments (P ≥ 0.12). Similar studies have reported no differences in progeny growth when Met is supplemented before or after calving (Clements et al. 2017; Silva et al. 2021; Redifer et al. 2023). No significant differences were observed in ADG during the finishing period (P ≥ 0.43) or final BW at time of harvest (P = 0.54). Carcass characteristics were not significantly different among treatments (P ≥ 0.25). Given that no performance variables point to MET1 calves underperforming, there are three possible explanations for this response: (1) there are no differences between treatment groups, (2) dams maintained fetal growth by compensating for Met deficiency using one-carbon metabolism (Crouse et al. 2019; Syring et al. 2022), or (3) excess Met led to hypermethylation and reduced efficiency (Zhang, 2018) that did not significantly differ from heifers with small AA deficits or adequate nutrition. The third point implies a more accurate assessment of nutritional needs is required before supplementation to individuals with deficits.

Cow BW and BCS were not adequately stratified among treatment groups at the start of the study causing some Type I errors. Cow BW and BCS were less in MET2 than HAY2 (P = 0.07 and P = 0.02, respectively). There were no other significant BW differences between the treatments at the start of the study (P ≥ 0.41), but BCS was less in MET2 than some other treatments (NS: P = 0.06; CAKE1: P = 0.12; CAKE2: P = 0.03). All other BCS differences at the start of the study were not significantly different between treatments (P ≥ 0.88). At the end of treatment, prior to calving, NS cows had significantly less BW and BCS scores than all other treatments (P < 0.01 and P < 0.01, respectively). At this time, HAY2 cows had significantly greater BW and BCS than CAKE1 cows (P < 0.01, P = 0.02, respectively) and significantly greater BW than all other treatments (CAKE2: P < 0.01; MET2: P < 0.01). Also, CAKE1 cows had significantly less BCS than CAKE2 (P = 0.02), but BW was not significantly different (P = 0.91), which suggests supplementation was adequate for weight gain in CAKE1 cows and additional supplementation can increase body condition. All other BW and BCS were not significantly different at this time (P ≥ 0.92 and P ≥ 0.10, respectively). During the treatment period, ADG and BCS gain mirrored the BW and BCS differences at the end of treatment except CAKE1 tended to have lesser BCS gain than MET2 (P = 0.05), which is likely a reflection of the starting BCS in MET2 cows rather than a functional change based on treatment. Despite the potential for initial differences in MET2 impacting future BW and BCS, it appears the differences in energy, protein, and their availability to the supplemented groups allowed greater weight gain, but there was no added benefit to cow weight gain when supplemented with Met.

Cow BW and BCS continued to differ at breeding time with HAY2 cows having significantly greater BW and BCS than all other treatments (P ≤ 0.04 and P ≤ 0.02, respectively). There were no other significant differences in cow BW between treatments at breeding time (P ≥ 0.25), but NS cows had significantly less BCS than CAKE1 (P = 0.03) and CAKE2 cows (P < 0.01) and NS cows tended to have less BCS than MET2 (P = 0.08). No other significant differences in BCS between treatments were detected at breeding time (P ≥ 0.34). Given the normal environmental challenges during winter, low protein quality of dormant forages, and demands during early lactation, BW and BCS loss is not surprising. Between calving and breeding time, BCS loss was significantly less in NS cows than CAKE1, CAKE2, and MET2 cows (P < 0.01) and tended to be less than HAY2 cows (P = 0.10). Cows in the HAY2 group also had significantly less BCS loss than CAKE2 (P = 0.03) and tended to have less BCS loss than MET2 (P = 0.10). No other differences in BCS loss between treatments were significantly different (P ≥ 0.28). Average daily gain was significantly less in NS cows than all other treatments (P < 0.01), but there were no other significant differences between treatments (P ≥ 0.13). The differences in BCS loss and ADG illustrate the effect of differing supplementation strategies supporting greater energy storage during gestation and greater energy storage expenditure when energy was needed for lactation. Cow BW and BCS typically follow similar seasonal patterns, however, this study reports greater pairwise differences in BCS than in BW. This is likely because lipid storage mobilization during early lactation is more apparent in the body regions used to assess BCS (Singh and Bhakat 2022). A study on dairy cattle reported moderate to weak correlation between BCS and BW at this time (Roche et al. 2007). Although there was a tendency for MET2 cows to differ in BCS loss compared to NS cows, this may be attributed to BCS differences at the start of the trial. The residual effects of three months of treatment followed by supplementation during calving show BCS loss between the start of treatment and breeding time was least in HAY2 cows (P ≤ 0.04) and greatest in NS cows (P ≤ 0.01) with no other significant differences by treatment (P ≥ 0.49). Hay most likely provided a better source of RDP and energy that were lacking in NS cows and exceeded the adequate sources in CAKE1, CAKE2, and MET2 cows.

Cow BW and BCS at weaning were not significantly different between most treatment groups (P ≥ 0.31; P ≥ 0.26). The exceptions were significantly greater HAY2 cow BW than MET2 (P = 0.02) and a tendency for higher NS cow BCS than MET2 (P = 0.06), which can be attributed to BW and BCS differences at the start of the study. Cow ADG from breeding to weaning was significantly greater in NS cows than HAY2, CAKE2, and MET2 cows (P < 0.01) and tended to be greater than CAKE1 cows (P = 0.10). Cow ADG was also significantly greater in CAKE1 cows than HAY2 and MET2 cows (P = 0.01), but there were no other significant differences in ADG between treatments (P ≥ 0.10). Weaning BW and BCS suggest dams were able to recover their pre-treatment BW and BCS while grazing summer upland range pasture.

Cow calving difficulty and calf vigor were not significantly different by treatment (P ≥ 0.66). Julian birth date did not significantly differ by treatment (P = 0.59). Dam pregnancy rate tended to be less in NS dams compared to all other treatments (0.05 ≤ P ≤ 0.07). Cow pregnancy rate was not significantly different for the interaction of treatment and year (P = 0.97) or by year (P = 0.54), which would indicate repeated NS treatment did not have a compounding effect from year to year. However, managing an NS cow herd would result in a greater number of open dams annually than other treatments. Houghton et al. (1990) and Freetly et al. (2000) observed similar pregnancy rates among gestationally feed-restricted cows and unrestricted cows when cows were supplemented a high metabolizable energy diet between calving and breeding. The current study did not supply supplement postpartum and did not observe the BCS gain or BW gain seen in the previously mentioned studies. The current findings do not suggest that Met supplementation was the driving factor for differences in cow BW and BCS. The variety of the current studies treatment groups, especially pertaining to cows receiving NS, embody nutritional differences that implicate RDP and RUP rather than Met specifically (Titgemeyer and Löest 2001). Although BW differences had decreased by breeding time between NS cows and other treatments, the BCS of NS cows at the start of breeding time likely decreased fertility. Similar studies have shown no differences in reproductive performance due to Met supplementation (Clements et al. 2017; Silva et al. 2021; Redifer et al. 2023), but this study contrasts inadequate nutritional inputs during gestation with adequate or excess nutrition resulting in reduced calf growth during gestation and decreased future pregnancy rate.

Calf birth BW tended to be less in NS cows than HAY2 cows (P = 0.10), but there were no other tendencies to differ between treatments (P ≥ 0.14; Table 7). Nutrient partitioning shifts towards fetal needs during late gestation (Ferrell et al. 1976; Reynolds et al. 1986). Low nutrient intake by NS cows compared to other treatments led to 36 to 75 ± 9 kg lesser BW and 0.5 to 0.8 ± 0.1 lower BCS after treatment ended and decreased calf growth during late gestation (Freetly et al. 2000; Du et al. 2010; Funston et al. 2010; Bohnert et al. 2013). Calf BW at breeding time was significantly less in NS calves compared to HAY2, CAKE1, and CAKE2 calves (P < 0.01), but there were no other significant differences between treatments at breeding time (P ≥ 0.19). At weaning time, NS calves had significantly less BW than HAY2 (P < 0.01) and CAKE2 calves (P = 0.01) with a tendency for less BW than CAKE1 calves (P = 0.07). Calf weaning BW was also significantly less in MET2 calves compared to HAY2 calves (P = 0.02). There were no other significant differences in calf BW between treatments at weaning time (P ≥ 0.13). There is probably a Type I error for calf weaning BW between MET2 and HAY2. If there were a calf BW difference between MET2 and HAY2 there should also be a difference between HAY2, CAKE1, and CAKE2 or between MET2, CAKE1, and CAKE2 at any time point between birth and slaughter. The differences between HAY2 and MET2 at weaning could be related to genetic differences among cows retained on treatment despite random sire genetics. Between birth and weaning time, calf ADG was significantly greater in HAY2 calves than NS, CAKE1, and MET2 calves (P ≤ 0.03). Also, NS calves had significantly less ADG than CAKE2 calves (P < 0.01). Between breeding time and weaning time, the effect of no supplementation in NS cows became more noticeable on the dam and calf. The NS dam’s initial BCS and BW losses during treatment reversed, but calf growth was less than their counterparts. This suggests NS dams reduced energy transfer to their offspring to retain energy for themselves (Freetly et al. 2000). Although HAY2 dams had significantly greater BW at breeding time, the greater energy reserves apparent in BCS did not significantly increase calf weaning BW compared to adequately conditioned treatment groups.

When steer calves entered feedlot there were no significant differences in BW based on dam treatments (P ≥ 0.14). There were no significant differences in BW at slaughter or feedlot ADG (P ≥ 0.47). This agrees with a meta-analysis by Zago et al. (2020), which reported BW differences between progeny of dams treated with above and below normal CP and ME diets during late gestation were minimized during the finishing period. In this study, there was a tendency for greater marbling in HAY2 steer calves than MET2 steer calves (P = 0.09), but other carcass characteristics were not significantly different (P ≥ 0.18). This is probably another Type I error caused by initial stratification of treatment groups. Zago et al. (2020) reported greater marbling score in progeny of dams treated with above normal CP and ME diets. A recent study on calves genetically similar to those in this study reported greater marbling score in calves of dams fed ad libitum hay pre-partum in comparison to grazing corn residue pre-partum (Musgrave et al. 2022). The marbling scores of the current study are unexpected because the diet highest in CP and ME (MET2) was not significantly different than the diet lowest in CP and ME but tended to be less than a diet with less CP and ME.

Conclusions

Winter supplementation strategies change drastically with environment and heifer or cow requirements during each stage of the production system. Late gestation Met supplementation had no detectable impact on dam and subsequent progeny performance. Based on the results of the first experiment, the addition of Met to late-gestation rations in primiparous heifers had no impact on DMI, but heifers receiving distillers-based supplement gained more BW throughout the treatment period than those that didn’t. Likewise, in the second experiment, cows receiving greater supplementation while grazing winter range had greater BW, BCS, and fertility at critical timepoints throughout the production year whether that supplementation was in the form of hay, distillers’ grains, or with the addition of methionine hydroxy analogue. This suggests a supplementation strategy is warranted in March calving females grazing dormant pasture. Methionine found in distillers grains has been reported to withstand breakdown in the rumen and be highly absorbed by the intestines. It is possible that Met levels within distillers’ grains may have been enough to aid in performance, and additional Met had negligible impact despite being provided in excess. These findings support the common belief that gestational nutrition does have some impact on progeny performance, but more research on limiting AA availability is needed to better understand all the factors influencing gestating dam and progeny needs.
