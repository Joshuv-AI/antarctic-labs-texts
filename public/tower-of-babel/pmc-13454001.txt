When AL amyloidosis meets the liver: integrating clinical insight and current understanding
Authors: Yuan Xia, Ting Yang, Hui Zhu, Qingyang Zhu, Junling Han, Ruixin Wu, Mengying Yu, Jianfeng Zhu, Kai Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Light chain (AL) amyloidosis is a plasma cell-derived protein misfolding disorder in which structurally unstable monoclonal light chains aggregate into amyloid fibrils and deposit in target organs. Beyond simple disease burden, emerging evidence indicates that intrinsic light chain sequence and clonal properties actively shape organ tropism and clinical phenotypes. Hepatic involvement represents a distinctive but under-recognized manifestation, characterized by marked hepatomegaly and disproportionate alkaline phosphatase elevation, often preceding overt hepatocellular dysfunction. While usually occurring within multiorgan disease, hepatic-predominant presentations show enrichment of kappa-restricted clones, suggesting selective deposition driven by light chain biophysical features. Advances in plasma cell–directed therapies have transformed survival; however, delayed hepatic recovery despite rapid hematologic remission highlights dissociation between clonal suppression and amyloid clearance. Understanding how clonal biology governs organ-specific deposition and response kinetics may enable improved risk stratification and the development of mechanism-based strategies to accelerate organ recovery and optimize long-term outcomes.

Introduction

Light chain (AL) amyloidosis is a rare systemic plasma cell disorder characterized by the misfolding of monoclonal immunoglobulin light chains into insoluble amyloid fibrils that deposit in tissues and progressively impair organ function. The incidence is estimated at 8–12 cases per million person-years and it accounts for approximately 0.8% of end-stage renal disease. The median age at diagnosis is around 76 years, with a male predominance [1]. Clinical manifestations are highly heterogeneous and determined by the pattern and extent of organ involvement, most commonly affecting the heart and kidneys, which largely drive prognosis. Because early symptoms are often non-specific and mimic more common conditions, diagnosis is frequently delayed, and significant organ damage may already be present at recognition. The median diagnostic delay from symptom onset is 2.7 years, and half of patients consult at least five physicians before a correct diagnosis is established [2]. Despite therapeutic advances, AL amyloidosis remains associated with substantial morbidity and mortality, underscoring the need for improved recognition of less typical organ presentations.

Hepatic involvement represents one such under-recognized manifestation. Although amyloid deposition in the liver is often identified histologically in systemic AL amyloidosis, clinically significant hepatic disease is less common and typically presents with marked hepatomegaly and disproportionate cholestatic enzyme elevation, while transaminases and bilirubin may remain relatively preserved in early stages. This biochemical pattern can lead to misclassification as primary hepatobiliary disease and delay appropriate hematologic evaluation. Furthermore, the clinical behavior, organ response kinetics, and outcomes of hepatic-predominant AL amyloidosis differ in important respects from non-hepatic presentations. In this review, we integrate a representative case from our center with evidence from major referral cohorts to synthesize current understanding of the clinical features, diagnostic challenges, and distinguishing characteristics of hepatic AL amyloidosis in comparison with both non-hepatic AL disease and other forms of hepatic amyloidosis.

A representative clinical case of hepatic AL amyloidosis

A 59-year-old male was admitted to our center on March 15, 2024, with a 2-month history of abdominal distension, epigastric discomfort, intermittent right upper quadrant pain, and 10-kg weight loss. Physical examination revealed marked hepatomegaly (approximately 5 cm below the right costal margin and 15 cm below the xiphoid process) with a firm liver on palpation, without splenomegaly, ascites, or peripheral oedema.

Laboratory testing showed a cholestatic pattern of liver injury with markedly elevated alkaline phosphatase (ALP 1072 U/L) and γ-glutamyl transferase (γ-GT 1045 U/L), mild aminotransferase elevation (ALT 54 U/L, AST 74 U/L), and near-normal bilirubin. Platelet count (461 × 10⁹/L) and serum vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF 431 pg/mL) were mildly elevated. Cardiac evaluation, including cardiac enzymes, troponin I, BNP, NT-proBNP, echocardiography, and cardiac MRI, was unremarkable. Renal assessment, including renal ultrasonography and quantitative urinary protein testing, showed no abnormalities. Screening for autoimmune, metabolic, endocrine, coagulation, infectious, and malignant causes was negative.

Monoclonal protein evaluation showed elevated serum IgG at 31.80 g/L, while IgA, IgM, and IgG4 levels were within normal ranges. Serum protein electrophoresis demonstrated an M-spike of 17.2% (13.5 g/L), and immunofixation confirmed IgG-κ monoclonality. Serum free light chain (FLC) analysis revealed κ 137 mg/L and λ 29.7 mg/L (dFLC 107 mg/L), with a κ/λ ratio of 4.61. Urine electrophoresis detected 23% M-protein (25 mg/L). Twenty-four-hour urinary light chain excretion was at the upper limit of normal, and urine immunofixation was positive for κ light chains.

Contrast-enhanced chest and abdominal CT demonstrated emphysema with pulmonary bullae, fatty liver, and hepatomegaly (Fig. 1A). Abdominal MRI confirmed marked hepatomegaly with hepatic steatosis, hepatic cysts, gallstones, and minimal ascites (Fig. 1B). Whole-body MRI showed no skeletal lesions. Liver biopsy revealed diffuse amyloid deposition within the hepatic sinusoids, hepatocellular interstitium, and portal vascular walls, accompanied by architectural distortion and hepatocellular hydropic degeneration (Fig. 2B). Immunohistochemical staining was positive for CD38, CD138, kappa, lambda, and Congo red, and scattered positivity for CD56, Mum-1, CD19, and CD22. The pathological diagnosis was hepatic amyloidosis.

On further questioning, the patient denied limb paraesthesias, pain, or sexual dysfunction. Examination revealed macroglossia and mild facial telangiectasia. There was no hyperpigmentation, acrocyanosis, Raynaud phenomenon, hirsutism, leukonychia, purpura, palpable superficial lymphadenopathy, gynecomastia, or peripheral oedema.

The patient was diagnosed with AL amyloidosis (Mayo 2004/2012 Stage I). Autologous stem cell transplantation and daratumumab-based therapy were declined, and six cycles of CyBorD were administered, resulting in complete hematologic remission with hepatic response. Overall survival at the last follow-up was 26 months.

Definition and diagnosis of hepatic AL amyloidosis

Hepatic involvement in AL amyloidosis is defined by the presence of amyloid deposits on liver biopsy; alternatively, in patients with biopsy-proven amyloidosis at another site, hepatomegaly (liver span greater than 15 cm in the absence of heart failure) or a serum alkaline phosphatase (ALP) level exceeding 1.5 × the upper limit of normal (ULN) is considered supportive of liver involvement [3]. However, investigators from the Mayo Clinic have suggested that the current ALP threshold of 1.5×ULN may be too low, as hepatic congestion secondary to cardiac dysfunction, particularly right-sided heart failure, can also lead to mild ALP elevation. Their findings further demonstrated that patients with isolated hepatic involvement had a significantly higher ALP/ULN ratio than those with concurrent multiorgan involvement (4.4 vs. 2.82) [4], indicating that a higher diagnostic cutoff for ALP may be warranted in the future.

Given the multiorgan nature of AL amyloidosis, target-organ biopsies (e.g., myocardium, kidney, and liver) typically yield high diagnostic sensitivity. Prior to the 21 st century, and particularly before publication of the “Consensus Opinion From the 10th International Symposium on Amyloid and Amyloidosis” in 2005 [3], the diagnosis of hepatic involvement largely relied on liver biopsy. The historically high frequency of organ biopsies reflects the limited clinical awareness of the disease at that time [5], and such procedures carry non-negligible procedural risks and practical challenges. In contrast, the broad involvement of surrogate tissues, including abdominal fat, salivary glands, and bone marrow, has enabled safer and less invasive diagnostic alternatives with acceptable sensitivities. These surrogate biopsies are now recommended as first-line diagnostic approaches. Consequently, non-invasive assessments, including serum ALP measurement and liver imaging, have become the preferred methods for evaluating hepatic involvement in AL amyloidosis.

Clinical manifestations of hepatic AL amyloidosis

The predominant clinical features of hepatic amyloidosis include fatigue, anorexia, abdominal distension, nausea, weight loss, and edema, with the majority of patients presenting with one or more of these symptoms (Table 1). In rare cases, spontaneous hepatic rupture may occur [6]. However, the nonspecific nature of these manifestations often makes early diagnosis challenging. In a natural history study, clinicians considered amyloidosis in the differential diagnosis in only 26% of patients prior to liver biopsy [7], and this proportion is likely to be even lower in non-plasma cell disease centers or departments. Notably, a recent report from the Amyloidosis Forum Multi-organ System Working Group highlighted that the presence of gastrointestinal symptoms may facilitate earlier recognition and diagnosis of the disease [8]. Therefore, unexplained hepatomegaly should prompt the consideration of AL amyloidosis. Clinical characteristics of hepatic AL amyloidosis

Pathophysiology and histopathology

AL amyloidosis, like other plasma cell disorders such as multiple myeloma, is driven by abnormal plasma cell proliferation; however, its underlying pathogenic mechanisms are distinct. AL amyloidosis is caused by monoclonal immunoglobulin light chains produced by the abnormal plasma cell clone. These light chains, particularly the variable light-chain (VL) region, exhibit an increased propensity for partial unfolding and misfolding due to patient-specific sequence characteristics and reduced kinetic stability. Increasing evidence suggests that the primary structure of amyloidogenic light chains renders them susceptible to proteolytic cleavage, which may facilitate or accelerate misfolding and fibrillogenesis. In this context, proteolytic processing is now considered an important contributor to AL light-chain amyloidogenesis. Following abnormal proteolytic cleavage, some light chains generate VL-containing fragments with greater amyloidogenic potential than the intact protein [9, 10]. Misfolded light chains or their fragments subsequently aggregate into soluble oligomers and ultimately assemble into highly ordered β-sheet-rich amyloid fibrils, which deposit within the interstitial spaces of target organs, including the heart, kidneys, liver, and peripheral nerves [11–13].

A retrospective study from Portugal that analyzed 1,773 liver biopsies found that only 0.4% were diagnosed with hepatic amyloidosis, underscoring its rarity among liver diseases [14]. Hepatic amyloidosis may also result from other amyloid types including apolipoprotein A1, lysozyme, and AA amyloidosis [3]. In a recent pathological analysis at the Mayo Clinic involving 58 cases of hepatic amyloid, 55.2% were AL amyloidosis, while transthyretin, serum amyloid A, and apolipoprotein A1 accounted for 25.9%, 6.9%, and 6.9% of cases, respectively [15].

Histopathological evaluation of hepatic AL amyloidosis at the Mayo Clinic revealed that amyloid deposition was confined to the sinusoids in 66% of patients, vascular structures in 13%, and both compartments in 21% [7]. In a separate study from the same center, amyloid predominantly accumulated in the sinusoids (62.5%), followed by the hepatic arteries (25%). The deposits were strongly Congophilic and birefringent and were commonly observed within the connective tissue of larger portal tracts or the walls of major hilar veins and nerves [15, 16]. The histopathological patterns were similar between kappa and lambda subtypes [15].

Laboratory findings

Hepatic AL amyloidosis is often characterized by a distinctive “disassociation” pattern in liver function tests, with marked elevations in ALP and γ-GT levels, whereas aminotransferases (AST and ALT) and bilirubin levels are typically normal or only mildly elevated in the early stages. This pattern reflects the preferential deposition of amyloid within the hepatic sinusoids and arteries, rather than direct hepatocellular injury. ALP elevations are often pronounced; in studies from the Mayo Clinic [4] and China [17], baseline ALP levels in patients with hepatic AL amyloidosis were 2.8-fold and 2.41-fold above the ULN, respectively. Hyperbilirubinemia is less common; in a Chinese cohort, 32.4% of patients had elevated total bilirubin (TBIL) [18], which typically indicates advanced-stage disease [4] and predicts poorer progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS) [18].

Hepatomegaly is highly prevalent in hepatic AL amyloidosis and has been reported in 55.7% [17] to 67.6% [18] of cases in recent studies. This enlargement is also attributable to amyloid deposition, as amyloid fibrils preferentially accumulate around hepatocytes, particularly within the sinusoids and portal regions, leading to hepatocellular compression and architectural distortion; these changes can further contribute to hepatic congestion and increased liver stiffness [15, 16]. In clinical practice, some patients may also present with a firm or stiff liver on palpation, and recent studies suggest that increased liver stiffness may serve as a useful marker of hepatic involvement in AL amyloidosis [19]. However, hepatomegaly is not a specific finding. A Dutch study found that many patients with a liver span > 15 cm on CT or ultrasound did not demonstrate hepatic involvement on serum amyloid P (SAP) scintigraphy or elevated ALP [20]. Additionally, a large population-based study of 2,080 individuals showed that 26% had a liver span > 15 cm, as measured by transabdominal ultrasound, indicating that hepatomegaly is present in approximately one-quarter of the general population [21]. Since amyloidosis patients frequently have cardiac or renal involvement resulting in congestive heart failure and subsequent passive hepatic congestion, a liver span > 15 cm should be interpreted cautiously and only considered indicative of hepatic amyloidosis after exclusion of congestive heart failure [3].

Isolated hepatic involvement versus multi-organ involvement

Hepatic involvement occurs in approximately 15–20% of patients with AL amyloidosis. In a large European cohort study (n = 4,480), liver involvement was observed in 14.3% of patients, with rates of 16.3% and 13.3% before and after 2010, respectively [22]. In a Mayo Clinic study (n = 1,551), the prevalence of hepatic involvement was 21%, with rates of 27%, 22%, and 16% in 2000–2004, 2005–2009, and 2010–2014, respectively [23]. The reasons for this temporal decline remain unclear and should be interpreted with caution. Earlier diagnosis due to improved techniques could theoretically reduce organ involvement, similar trends have not been observed in other commonly affected organs, including the heart, kidneys, and nervous system. Moreover, cardiac and renal involvement are supported by validated biomarker-based criteria, whereas hepatic involvement remains less clearly defined. We did not identify studies showing a temporal decline in median ALP levels among AL patients. Therefore, the reported decrease in hepatic involvement may partly reflect changes in diagnostic ascertainment and organ-involvement criteria, rather than a true reduction in hepatic disease burden.

AL amyloidosis is predominantly a multi-organ disease, with the involvement of two or more organs observed in 64.5% of patients [23], and three or more organs affected in 20% [23] to 23.3% [22]. Among all AL amyloidosis cases, isolated hepatic amyloidosis accounts for only 5% [8]. According to our analysis, truly isolated liver involvement, defined as no evidence of involvement of other organs, is present in approximately 3.9% [18] to 6% [4] of hepatic AL amyloidosis cases. Compared to patients with concurrent multi-organ involvement, those with isolated hepatic involvement were recently reported to be younger (median age, 52 vs. 61 years), have a higher kappa isotype proportion (75% vs. 38%), higher baseline ALP levels (4.4×ULN vs. 2.82×ULN), and a superior 5-year OS rate (100% vs. 64%) [4].

Thus, isolated hepatic AL amyloidosis is uncommon, yet clinically distinct, characterized by a younger patient profile, a predominance of the kappa isotype, and a more favorable prognosis compared to its presentation as part of a multi-organ process.

Light chain isotype

In AL amyloidosis, the lambda isotype accounts for approximately 79.2% of the cases [24], a finding consistent with a Chinese scoping review (n = 1,366) that reported a similar prevalence of 81.0% [25]. However, lambda isotype is less common in patients with hepatic AL amyloidosis. The patient described in this study exhibited a kappa isotype.

We reviewed four studies published since 2010 and found lambda isotype frequencies of 46.1% (China), 60% (USA), 67% (Italy), and 78.4% (China) (Table 1). A retrospective study (n = 557) by the Mayo Clinic comparing AL amyloidosis patients undergoing autologous stem cell transplantation (ASCT) found that kappa isotype was more frequently associated with hepatic involvement than lambda (18% vs. 7%), whereas renal (57% vs. 69%) and neurological involvement (9% vs. 16%) were less common, and kappa isotype patients demonstrated longer PFS and OS [26]. Another study reported that patients with isolated hepatic involvement had a markedly higher proportion of kappa isotypes than those with concurrent multiorgan involvement (75% vs. 38%) [4]. Differences in the immunoglobulin light-chain variable region contribute to the distinct organ tropism observed in AL amyloidosis; for instance, KV1-33 has been associated with liver tropism. Structural differences between kappa and lambda light chains are thought to result in differential amyloidogenic potential and susceptibility to proteolysis and tissue clearance [27]. Whether κ-restricted AL amyloidosis is more frequently associated with secretion of intact immunoglobulin molecules, in addition to excess free light chains, remains unclear. Although monoclonal proteins in AL amyloidosis may be present as either intact immunoglobulins or free light chains, amyloid deposits are composed predominantly of light chains or light-chain fragments. Current data do not clearly establish a κ- versus λ-specific difference in the frequency of intact H2L2 immunoglobulin secretion, and this issue warrants further investigation, particularly in hepatic-predominant AL amyloidosis, where κ-restricted clones appear to be enriched.

Treatment outcomes indicate that patients with hepatic AL amyloidosis and kappa isotype achieve higher rates of hepatic responses [4]. Another study reported that among patients achieving organ-specific responses, those with hepatic response had a higher prevalence of kappa isotype (44%) than renal (23%) or cardiac (27%) responders [28]. Survival analyses showed that hepatic involvement did not affect OS in kappa light chain patients (median OS: 4.8 years with liver involvement vs. 3.7 years without, P = 0.56), whereas in lambda light chain patients, liver involvement was associated with significantly shorter survival (median OS: 1.4 years vs. 4.9 years, P = 0.001) [29].

Taken together, kappa isotype is more prevalent in hepatic AL amyloidosis, particularly in patients with isolated liver involvement. Patients with either isolated hepatic involvement or kappa isotype are more likely to achieve an organ response and have a favorable prognosis. In contrast, lambda light-chain patients with hepatic involvement exhibit worse overall outcomes, likely due to the additive burden of multi-organ disease, given the higher frequency of cardiac and renal involvement in this subtype.

Cytogenetics

Approximately 80% of patients with AL amyloidosis harbor cytogenetic abnormalities. t(11;14) translocation is the most frequent, occurring in 40–60% of patients, and has historically been considered a poor prognostic factor [30, 31]. However, recent studies indicate that in the era of CD38-targeted therapy, t(11;14) no longer predicts adverse outcomes [32]. Another common abnormality is gain(1q21), observed in 15–20% of patients and is still associated with poor prognosis even with CD38-directed therapies [32, 33]. Del(17p) is found in 3–5% of patients; although it portends poor prognosis in other plasma cell disorders and hematologic malignancies, it appears to have no significant impact on survival in AL amyloidosis [32, 34].

Cytogenetic data on hepatic AL amyloidosis are limited. Among the six studies included in our review, only one reported cytogenetic findings. In this Mayo Clinic cohort, 65% of patients harbored t(11;14), and 5% had high-risk cytogenetic abnormalities (HRCA) including del(17p), t(4;14), t(14;16), and t(14;20) [4]. The patient described in this report did not carry t(11;14), but exhibited gain(1q21) with four copies (1q21 amplification), a cytogenetic alteration associated with poor prognosis. The patient was followed up for 20 months, with detailed outcomes.

Prognosis

Historically, hepatic involvement in AL amyloidosis has been associated with a poor prognosis. An Italian study (1986–2007) reported worse survival in patients with hepatic involvement than in those without hepatic involvement (median OS 1.7 vs. 4.4 years, P < 0.001) [29]. Similarly, an early Mayo Clinic study (1975–1997, n = 98) reported a median OS of only 8.5 months [7]. In contrast, a recent Mayo Clinic study (2000–2021, n = 130) demonstrated a markedly improved median OS of 10.8 years [4]. The substantial differences within the same center can be attributed to several factors: (1) different inclusion criteria, as the earlier study included only patients with liver biopsy-confirmed involvement, whereas the latter defined hepatic involvement as ALP > 1.5×ULN; (2) the more recent study included only patients with multiple ALP measurements, excluding early deaths and lost-to-follow-up cases; and (3) the temporal gap, with the earlier cohort reflecting a pre-novel therapy era. Substantial discrepancies were observed between these two Chinese studies. The National Clinical Research Center for Kidney Diseases (2004–2019, n = 88) reported a median OS of only 9 months [17], while Peking Union Medical College Hospital (2007–2018, n = 102) reported a median OS of 41 months [18]. This pronounced disparity may be explained by differences in baseline characteristics between the two cohorts; the former is one of China’s largest referral centers for kidney diseases, including patients with a very high prevalence of renal (98.9%) and cardiac (75%) involvement, while the latter cohort presented with lower rates of renal (71.6%) and cardiac (68.6%) involvement, and included 3.9% of patients with isolated hepatic involvement. These findings highlight the need for larger multicenter studies to better define the survival characteristics of hepatic AL amyloidosis.

Multiple factors influence the prognosis of AL amyloidosis. In addition to cytogenetics, several other clinical parameters have been associated with poor outcomes. Elevated TBIL levels have been consistently reported as an adverse prognostic factor in hepatic AL amyloidosis [7, 17, 18, 35]; similarly, AST > 2×ULN and ALP > 4×ULN levels have been linked to inferior survival [35]. Although elevated transaminases and bilirubin levels lack sufficient sensitivity and specificity for diagnostic purposes in hepatic AL amyloidosis and typically appear only in advanced stages of the disease [8], their elevation may nevertheless indicate more severe disease and poorer prognosis.

Cardiac involvement is another critical adverse event. Parameters such as congestive heart failure [7], NT-proBNP level ≥ 1800 pg/mL [17], higher cardiac stage [4], and Mayo 2004 staging [18] are all associated with poor survival. More recently, the AL International Staging System (AL-ISS) incorporated echocardiographic longitudinal strain into biomarker-based staging with NT-proBNP and high-sensitivity troponin T, enabling more refined risk stratification. However, its prognostic value in hepatic AL amyloidosis has not yet been specifically validated and warrants further study [36]. Additional negative prognostic indicators include age ≥ 60 years [4] and the lambda isotype [4, 29]. Conversely, favorable factors include isolated hepatic involvement and achieving ≥ hematologic VGPR within six months [4].

Interestingly, thrombocytosis (platelet count > 500 × 109/L) has been identified as an independent adverse prognostic factor for hepatic AL amyloidosis [7]. A Polish study found that patients with elevated blood cell counts had a higher incidence of hepatic involvement than those with normal counts (34.8% vs. 10.3%) [37]. This association appears paradoxical, as cytopenia, rather than cytosis, is typically observed in advanced liver disease accompanied by hypersplenism. However, it should be noted that in AL amyloidosis, amyloid deposition can also cause splenomegaly and splenic hypofunction [38], a notion supported by the presence of Howell-Jolly bodies in the peripheral blood of 28% of patients with hepatic involvement [7]. Therefore, thrombocytosis may indicate splenic impairment, which in turn contributes to a poor prognosis through mechanisms that remain incompletely understood.

In summary, hepatic involvement generally indicates poor prognosis. However, the introduction of novel therapies, particularly CD38-targeted monoclonal antibodies and ASCT, has improved outcomes and partially mitigated the historically adverse impact [4]. High-risk patients, especially those with multiorgan involvement, cardiac involvement, or lambda isotype, should receive these potentially beneficial therapies whenever feasible.

Hepatic response to therapy

According to the Consensus Opinion from the 10th International Symposium on Amyloid and Amyloidosis in 2005, hepatic response was defined as either a ≥ 50% decrease in abnormal ALP (defined as the difference between the measured value and the ULN) or a ≥ 2 cm reduction in liver span on imaging, while an increase of > 50% from the nadir was considered disease progression [3]. However, this definition lacked granularity, particularly in light of subsequent refinements in the response criteria for cardiac (2012) [39] and renal (2014) [40] AL amyloidosis. Therefore, in 2018, the Mayo Clinic introduced a more detailed hepatic response assessment, categorizing patients into four tiers based on the extent of ALP reduction from baseline: no response (≤ 30% reduction), partial response (PR, 31–60% reduction), very good partial response (VGPR, > 60% reduction from baseline to nadir > 2×lower limit of normal)), and complete response (CR, nadir ALP ≤ 2× lower limit of normal). Validation in this cohort revealed distinct prognostic stratification, with corresponding 10-year survival rates of 29%, 24%, 46%, and 80% [28]. Thus, deeper hepatic responses were associated with improved overall survival.

In a Mayo Clinic cohort, hepatic response rates at 6, 12, and 24 months were 28%, 36%, and 48%, respectively, with a median time to hepatic response of 21.5 months from treatment initiation [4]. Consistent with these findings, two Chinese studies reported hepatic response rates of 35.7% (median, 10 months) [17] and 30.8% (median, 12 months) [18]. For hematologic response, the Mayo cohort demonstrated CR, VGPR/dFLC-PR, and PR rates of 29%, 37%, and 20%, respectively, at 6 months [4]. The corresponding CR/VGPR/PR rates from the two aforementioned Chinese studies were 41%, 17%, and 3% (median 1 month) [17] and 23%, 36%, and 20% (median 4 months) [18] respectively. These findings indicate that hepatic responses occur later than hematological responses.

Current treatments for AL amyloidosis primarily target plasma cells. Once a significant hematologic response is achieved, the levels of toxic amyloidogenic light chains are presumably reduced to a degree sufficient to promote clearance from amyloid deposits and/or minimize direct tissue injury. Consequently, organ responses consistently lag behind hematologic responses [11]. In a Mayo Clinic study (n = 313), the median time from initiation of therapy to normal FLC ratio (nFLCr), often representing hematologic CR, was a median of 3.8 months. Organ responses followed nFLCr, with a median interval of 2.1 months from the documented nFLCr to the first organ response. The hepatic response rates at 6 and 12 months after nFLCr were 71% and 87%, respectively, approaching the response rates observed for cardiac and renal involvement. Furthermore, within one year of achieving nFLCr, 80% of surviving patients demonstrated organ responses; those achieving early organ response within this period exhibited significantly superior OS compared with patients who achieved nFLCr but not early organ response [41].

In summary, hepatic responses in AL amyloidosis are markedly delayed relative to hematologic responses, often requiring up to one year to manifest. Nevertheless, achieving a hepatic response remains the principal therapeutic goal in patients with hepatic AL amyloidosis.

Treatment and recent advances

The treatment goals for AL amyloidosis are to reduce FLC levels, prevent further amyloid deposition in vital organs, and alleviate or reverse organ dysfunction caused by amyloid deposits. The primary strategy to achieve these goals is anti-plasma cell therapy to halt the production of abnormal light chains [1]. Currently, the treatment of hepatic AL amyloidosis largely follows standard AL amyloidosis regimens, utilizing drugs similar to those used for multiple myeloma. These include proteasome inhibitors (e.g., bortezomib and ixazomib), immunomodulatory drugs (e.g., thalidomide, lenalidomide, and pomalidomide), anti-CD38 monoclonal antibody (daratumumab) [1], and BCMA-targeted therapies (e.g., anti-BCMA immunoconjugates, bispecific antibodies, CAR-T) [42]. According to the latest mSMART.org guidelines (v10 April 2023), daratumumab-CyBorD (daratumumab, cyclophosphamide, bortezomib, dexamethasone) ±ASCT is the standard first-line therapy for newly diagnosed AL amyloidosis [43].

ASCT can improve survival and organ responses. Prior studies in patients with hepatic AL amyloidosis have shown that those receiving ASCT experience greater reductions in ALP and deeper hepatic responses [28]. Unfortunately, ASCT is only feasible for 10–20% of newly diagnosed AL amyloidosis patients owing to strict eligibility criteria and the availability of alternative treatments [13]. Liver transplantation has previously shown efficacy in certain forms of hepatic amyloidosis, such as ATTR, and has historically been explored in a limited number of AL amyloidosis cases. However, owing to its substantial complication rate and associated mortality, it is rarely used in AL amyloidosis [44].

Summary

Hepatic involvement in AL amyloidosis represents a distinct yet under-recognized phenotype within a systemic plasma cell disorder, typically characterized by a cholestatic biochemical profile and frequent diagnostic delay. Although isolated liver disease is uncommon, hepatic amyloid deposition usually reflects broader light chain-driven organ tropism and commonly coexists with cardiac and renal involvement, which largely determine prognosis. Emerging evidence that kappa-restricted clones are enriched in hepatic-predominant cases supports a role for intrinsic light chain properties in organ-selective deposition and differential response kinetics. The advent of modern plasma cell-directed therapies, particularly CD38 monoclonal antibodies and autologous stem cell transplantation, has substantially improved survival; however, hepatic responses often lag behind hematologic remission, highlighting the disconnect between clonal control and tissue recovery.

Looking ahead, further progress will require recognizing liver involvement not merely as a passive manifestation of systemic disease, but as a clinically relevant component of diagnosis, risk stratification, and treatment assessment. Refining diagnostic thresholds, particularly for alkaline phosphatase elevation in the setting of cardiac dysfunction, may improve assessment of hepatic disease burden, while standardized amyloid typing and longitudinal organ response measures could strengthen comparability across studies. Mechanistic insights into the interplay between light chain structure, clonal biology, and hepatic tropism may also improve prediction of liver involvement at diagnosis. As deeper hematologic responses become increasingly achievable, future strategies should extend beyond suppressing amyloid production to promoting amyloid clearance and accelerating hepatic recovery, with the goal of improving long-term outcomes.
