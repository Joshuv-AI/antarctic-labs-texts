Genome-wide development of genomic SSR markers, polymorphism analysis, and dosage-aware SSR fingerprinting in hexaploid Camellia Oleifera
Authors: Chengcai Zhang, Ping Lin, Xiaohua Yao, Huadong Ren, Kailiang Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOil-Camellia is an important woody oil crop, and most cultivated cultivars are hexaploid. Unresolved kinship among major cultivars and the lack of reliable identification methods have hindered both the development of new cultivars and the deployment of elite ones. An important reason is the limited availability of molecular markers suitable for genetic analysis.ResultsUsing five published oil-Camellia genomes, we surveyed genome-wide SSRs and found densities of one SSR per 4.22–4.67 kb across species. Based on the C. oleifera var. Nanyongensis reference genome, we designed 14,854 primer pairs and validated 132 new SSR markers. Genetic diversity and relationships among 44 hexaploid cultivars were assessed with a 30-marker subset, yielding 3–21 alleles per locus; polymorphism information content ranged from 0.07 to 0.91. Phylogenetic analyses using dosage-resolved SSR genotypes revealed the genetic relationships between 44 cultivars. We evaluated the discriminatory power of each marker and identified a minimal set (Coz09127 and Coz14197) that distinguishes all cultivars; using these two core markers, we constructed—for the first time—fingerprints for 44 hexaploid C. oleifera cultivars.ConclusionsWe surveyed SSR distribution in oil-Camellia genomes and developed 132 SSR markers. Using 30 loci, we characterized genetic diversity and relationships among 44 hexaploid cultivars, and identified a minimal two-marker set that distinguishes all tested cultivars, enabling construction of cultivar DA-SSR fingerprints. These results strengthen the genetic toolkit for polyploid C. oleifera and support reliable cultivar identification and deployment in breeding and production.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07601-8.Keywords: Oil-Camellia, Genetic diversity, Polymorphism, Fingerprint, Cultivar identification

Background

Oil-Camellia generally refers to species within the Camellia genus of the Theaceae family that are rich in oil, such as Camellia oleifera, C. vietnamensis, C. chekiangoleosa, and C. yuhsienensis, and have been cultivated in China as traditional oil crops for more than 2300 years [1, 2]. Among these, hexaploid C. oleifera (2n = 6x = 90) is the primary cultivated species and is characterized by a high seed oil content (>30%), high oleic acid content (>80%), and enrichment in physiologically active components [3–5]. Because cultivars differ markedly in yield, quality, resistance, and regional adaptability [2, 6], reliable identification and a clear understanding of genetic relationships among cultivars have become essential for cultivar deployment and targeted breeding. Phenotypic traits are prone to instability and are influenced by environmental factors, cultivation practices and growth phases (Fig. 1). Consequently, phenotypic DUS (distinctness, uniformity and stability) testing and image-based machine-learning approaches remain limited in their utility for cultivar identification [7]. Therefore, unclear cultivar identities not only obscure pedigree information and lead to arbitrary parent choices in breeding, but also create confusion in cultivar deployment and promotion. Recently, molecular marker techniques have gained widespread use in genetic diversity studies, molecular fingerprinting, and variety identification because of their stability and reproducibility [8–10]. Among the different types of molecular markers, simple sequence repeats (SSRs) present highly polymorphism informative content, high allelic variation, and good reproducibility [11] and have been widely used in the studies mentioned above [12–15].

Adult tree of C. oleifera and its grafted seedlings

In recent years, a limited number of SSRs have been developed in oil-Camellia [16–18]. Chen et al. [9] and Yan et al. [10] determined genetic relationships and constructed molecular fingerprints for varieties via 10 to 24 SSR markers without determining allele dosage [9, 10]. As alleles in polyploid species can occur in multiple copies at a locus, accurate determination of allele dosage through conventional electrophoretic methods is difficult [11]. Moreover, methods such as peak-ratio counting or the coding of genotypes as “allelic phenotypes” leave dosage ambiguous [19, 20]. Consequently, applying SSR genotyping approaches capable of accurately resolving allele dosage is essential for improving the reliability of genetic analyses in polyploid oil-Camellia. Therefore, the application of SSRs in polyploid oil-Camellia still suffers from a limited number of loci and difficulty in determining allele dosages [9, 10], which in turn restricts their use in high-resolution genetic studies and in the production of accurate cultivar fingerprints. Although single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) have become the dominant marker type in genomic studies, SSRs remain valuable because they are multi-allelic and highly informative for individual identification and parentage analysis [21]. Moreover, sequencing-based SSR genotyping that resolves allele dosage provides a practical complement to SNPs, particularly in polyploid crops such as oil-Camellia. Recent genome assemblies of C. oleifera [1, 22], C. lanceoleosa [23], C. chekiangoleosa [24] and C. crapnelliana [25] have laid the foundation for large-scale marker development. Recently, a high-throughput sequencing-based SSR genotyping technique, SSRSeq, has provided a new avenue for genetic studies on polyploid species via SSR markers [26, 27]; however, SSRSeq has not been used for fingerprinting or to refine the genetic relationships among the main oil-Camellia cultivars. Sequencing-based detection of SSR allele dosage offers a feasible solution to overcome the inherent ambiguity of traditional SSR genotyping in polyploids, and thus holds potential to improve the precision of genetic analyses in oil-Camellia. Expanding the set of dosage-resolved SSRs would clarify cultivar relatedness, verify cultivar identity, and increase the marker density required for breeding studies.

This study analyzed five published oil-Camellia genome datasets, systematically searched for SSR loci across the whole genome, and elucidated the frequency and distribution patterns of SSR motifs. Genomic SSR markers were developed following PCR amplification and validated by polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE). A set of polymorphic genomic SSR loci was genotyped via SSRSeq and used to analyze the genetic relationships of the test cultivars. We next evaluated the single-marker discriminatory power of each marker. Finally, we defined a core SSR panel and generated an allelic dosage–aware SSR (DA-SSR) fingerprint for each cultivar. We believe that this work will substantially advance genetic research and breeding in C. oleifera and enable reliable verification of major cultivars.

Results

SSR distribution characteristics

Genome data were downloaded and analyzed using the MISA program for five genomes: C. oleifera var. Nanyongensis, C. lanceoleosa, C. chekiangoleosa, C. crapnelliana, and C. oleifera var. ‘Changlin 40’. A total of 640,983, 694,090, 608,668, 615,332 and 1,884,438 SSRs were identified, with occurrence frequencies of one SSR per 4.40 kb, 4.22 kb, 4.38 kb, 4.67 kb, and 4.56 kb, respectively (Table 1). The frequencies of C. lanceoleosa and C. crapnelliana were the highest and lowest, respectively. Dinucleotide repeats were the predominant type, accounting for approximately 74.03% to 76.02% of all SSRs, followed by trinucleotide repeats. Hexanucleotide repeats were the least common, comprising only 0.98–1.28% of the SSRs (Table 1). Among the dinucleotide repeats, AG/CT was the most abundant, ranging from 46.61% to 49.46%, followed by AT/AT, which accounted for 40.76% to 43.16%, whereas CG/CG was the least abundant, ranging from 0.14% to 0.37%. Among the trinucleotide types, AAT/ATT was the most common, accounting for 35.75% to 38.81%, followed by AAG/CTT and ACC/GGT (Fig. 2). The occurrence of the same repeat motif varied among species, such as AACAGT/ACTGTT, which accounted for 3.18% and 3.62% of the hexanucleotide repeats in C. oleifera and C. chekiangoleosa, respectively, whereas it accounted for only 0.21% in C. lanceoleosa. Conversely, AGGCCC/CCTGGG accounted for 0.04% and 0.06% of C. oleifera and C. chekiangoleosa, respectively, but was notably greater in C. lanceoleosa (4.12%).

Development and validation of genome SSR primers

SSR primers were developed based on the genomic information from C. oleifera var. Nanyongensis. For the 55,207 candidate loci, primers were successfully designed for 14,854 loci via Primer 3 software (Additional Table 1). Subsequently, 225 primer pairs were randomly selected for synthesis. PCR amplification was conducted via the use of genomic DNA from eight samples, and the products were analyzed via 10% polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (Additional Fig. 1). The results revealed that 132 SSR loci (58.67%) presented good amplification results, 15 SSRs (6.67%) did not amplify the target bands, and 78 SSRs (34.67%) produced non-specific amplification or had poor amplification efficiency (Additional Table 2). Among the 132 markers, 107 SSR loci presented polymorphisms in eight samples. The 132 SSR loci were mapped onto 15 chromosomes of C. oleifera var. Nanyongensis, revealing a distribution of 5–13 loci per chromosome (Fig. 3).

Genetic relationships among the 44 cultivars

On the basis of the distribution of 107 polymorphic SSR loci across 15 chromosomes, two loci per chromosome were selected to perform the genetic study among 44 cultivars via SSRSeq (Table 2; Fig. 4). The analysis revealed that the Na and Ae values of the markers ranged from 3 to 21 and from 1.08 to 10.98, respectively; the Ho and He values ranged from 0.04 to 0.98 and from 0.07 to 0.91, respectively (Table 3). The PIC values ranged from 0.07 to 0.91, and 21 markers (70.0%) presented greater polymorphism (PIC ≥ 0.5). To compare the effects of allele dosage on phylogenetic inference, two phylogenetic trees were constructed with allele dosage-aware versus dominant marker approaches (Fig. 5). A striking discordance was observed between the two trees. The RF distance reached 72 (the theoretical maximum divergence was 82, normalized RF = 0.878), indicating that 87.8% of the bipartitions conflicted between the phylogenies, suggesting fundamentally distinct evolutionary interpretations. The dosage-aware tree retained more supported clades (≥ 50%: 4 vs. 3; ≥70%: 3 vs. 2), indicating slightly stronger support at well-resolved splits (Fig. 5). Only five clades were consistent in both clustering trees, highlighting the influence of allele dosage on genetic clustering (Fig. 5). In the dosage-aware phylogeny, the clustering of several cultivars matched their documented geographic origins. For example, CR2 and CR24, both from Guangxi Province, formed a short subclade (Table 2; Fig. 5). In the Jiangxi material, the following pairs clustered closely: G70 with GZY3, G16 with CL27, GZY9 with GF20, GZY1 with CL166, and GZY4 with GW1. Two Zhejiang samples (ZL2 and CL40) also grouped together (Table 2; Fig. 5). In contrast, in the phylogenetic tree constructed without allele-dosage information, only two of these pairs, CR24 with CR2 and GZY1 with CL166, remained closely related. These results indicate that incorporating allele-dosage information improves clustering accuracy and better reflects true genetic relationships (Table 3).

Construction of DA-SSR fingerprints

To assess the impact of allele dosage on cultivar discrimination, we genotyped the same 30 SSR loci under two scoring schemes—dosage-aware and dosage-unaware (Fig. 7a). Across the 44 cultivars, the number of cultivars that a single locus could uniquely distinguish ranged from 3 to 42 with dosage and 1 to 42 without dosage. Notably, 22 markers discriminated 1–14 additional cultivars when dosage was incorporated, and no marker discriminated more cultivars in the absence of dosage information than in its presence. Based on PIC values, we selected eight SSR markers (PIC ≥ 0.80) and evaluated their single-marker capacity for cultivar discrimination with allele-dosage information considered (Fig. 7b). Coz09127 exhibited the greatest resolving power, distinguishing 42 cultivars (excluding CL166 and CR22); Coz12178 and Coz06083 each discriminated 40 cultivars; and Coz14197 and Coz08117 each resolved more than 35 cultivars. Notably, the two-marker set Coz09127 + Coz14197, which are located on chromosomes 9 and 14, respectively, and belong to independent segregation, could be used to achieve effective differentiation of all the participating varieties. Finally, fingerprints unique to each variety were created via two core markers (Coz09127 and Coz14197), allowing accurate variety differentiation (Fig. 8). We term this approach the dosage-aware SSR (DA-SSR) fingerprint, a molecular fingerprint that—unlike typical SSR fingerprinting—simultaneously encodes band size, band presence/absence, and allele dosage.

Discussion

Distribution characteristics of SSRs in the oil-Camellia genomes

In recent years, SSR marker development and applications in genetic diversity assessment and germplasm fingerprinting remain active topics, despite the dominance of SNPs. SSRs are still useful because their multi-allelic nature yields high discriminatory power for individual identification and parentage with relatively few loci, enabling cost-effective assays [21]. SSR markers have been developed for C. oleifera [16, 18]; however, the number of markers remains insufficient, especially those lacking loci that are evenly distributed across the genome. Recently, five oil-Camellia reference genomes have been published, which provides opportunities for large-scale SSR locus identification. In this study, we analyzed the genome data of C. oleifera, C. lanceoleosa, C. chekiangoleosa, and C. crapnelliana and revealed that the genomic SSR occurrence ranged from one SSR per 4.22 kb to 4.67 kb (Table 1). Although the observed differences in SSR densities among species appear substantial, statistical comparisons were not applicable here because only one genome assembly was analyzed per species. These frequencies are higher than those reported for C. sinensis, which has 216.88 SSRs/Mb [28]; other species such as bamboo, which has one SSR per 16 kb [29]; and Carya illinoinensis at one SSR per 4.55 kb [15]. However, the frequency is lower than that report for C. nitidissima Chi (Theaceae), which has one SSR per 3.84 kb (excluding mononucleotide repeats) [30]; walnut, which has one SSR per 2.3 kb [31]; and Chinese jujube, which has one SSR per 1.17 kb [32]. Moreover, the SSR density in the five oil-Camellia species was dramatically greater than that in C. oleifera var Cenruan No. 3 (48.34 SSRs/Mbp) [33]. The reason for this discrepancy may be that only a portion of the genome (578.26 Mbp) of the hexaploid C. oleifera was analyzed by Wang et al. [33]. Among the dinucleotide repeats, AG/TC was the most common (Fig. 2), followed by AT/TA, which is consistent with findings from the transcriptomes of C. oleifera [16] and C. nitidissima Chi (Theaceae) [30] but opposite to Diospyros oleifera, in which AT/TA was more abundant than AG/TC [10]. The predominance of AG/CT repeats may reflect underlying mutational biases and genome composition characteristics. Previous studies in plants have suggested that AG/CT repeats are relatively stable and prone to expansion in noncoding regions, whereas AT-rich repeats may be more susceptible to replication slippage or other mutational processes and can be constrained by structural or functional factors [21, 34]. In terms of trinucleotide types, AAT/ATT (35.96–38.81%), AAG/CTT (19.72–20.32%), and ACC/GGT (19.12–20.69%) were predominant, whereas in the transcriptomes, ACC/GGT, AAG/CTT, and ATC/ATG were the main types [16]. Similarly, different predominant motifs between genomic and transcriptome SSRs have been reported in Rosa hybrida [35]. This may be due to base preferences in the coding and noncoding regions of the gene [36].

Development and genetic polymorphisms of SSRs

A total of 14,854 primer pairs in C. oleifera var. Nanyongensis was designed. In total, 225 primer pairs were randomly synthesized and 132 pairs were successfully validated using the genomic DNA of C. oleifera varieties (Fig. 3, Additional Fig. 1), achieving a success rate of 58.67%, which is higher than that of C. sinensis (32.00%) [28], and the transcriptome SSR in C. oleifera (49%) [16] but lower than that of Cyamopsis tetragonoloba (86.4%) [37] and Exbucklandia tonkinensis (76.26%) [38]. The 107 polymorphic SSRs were located on the chromosomes of C. oleifera var. Nanyongensis, with each chromosome featuring 3 to 10 loci. Furthermore, two SSR loci from each chromosome were selected, yielding 30 SSRs for genotyping 44 samples using SSRSeq technology. The analysis revealed that 21 loci (70.00%) displaying higher PIC values (≥ 0.5). These markers are suitable for studies on the genetic diversity of C. oleifera, the construction of genetic maps, the mapping of quantitative trait loci (QTLs), pedigree assessment, and evolutionary studies [15, 25].

Phylogenetic tree construction and genetic structure analysis

Accurate determination of SSR allele dosage reduces uncertainty and markedly enhances genetic diversity and differentiation estimates in polyploid species [25]. Recently, researchers have used high-throughput sequencing to determine SSR dosages, facilitating analyses of genetic diversity, demographic history, and evolutionary patterns in wild C. oleifera populations [25, 39, 40]. However, research using dosage-aware SSR data to characterize the genetic diversity and relatedness of major cultivars remains limited. Because major cultivars anchor breeding and production, focusing on them yields insights that are immediately useful for developing new cultivars and guiding deployment. To assess the impact of allele dosage on the genetic analysis of C. oleifera, we constructed phylogenetic trees using these 30 SSRs with and without dosage information. The two trees presented high RF distance, with 87.8% of the bipartitions conflicting between the phylogenies (Fig. 5). This result emphasizes the importance of allele dosage in the genetic analysis of polyploid C. oleifera [39, 40].

The dosage-aware tree recapitulated geographic origin, with a Guangxi pair (CR2 with CR24), multiple Jiangxi pairs, and a Zhejiang group clustering as short subclades (Table 2; Fig. 5). Chen et al. used 10 SSRs to study C. oleifera varieties and reported through cluster analysis that CL21 and CL40 might be synonymous [9]. CL40 originated from Zhejiang Province, whereas CL21 originated from Jiangxi Province [2]. In the present study, CL40 clustered together with ZL2, GZY3 and G70, whereas CL21 clustered together with XL183, GZY7, and ZL10, indicating that CL21 and CL40 are distinct varieties (Fig. 5). Additionally, varieties from provinces, including Guangdong, Guangxi, Hunan, and Hubei, were dispersed in the cluster diagram, a phenomenon also reported in previous studies [5]. The results of cluster analysis are influenced by the original geographical distribution and pedigree background. A possible reason is that C. oleifera, a perennial cross-pollinated tree species with over 2300 years of cultivation history [1], has undergone an exchange of resources between provinces during its long cultivation history. In 1974, the national oil-Camellia breeding programme conducted nationwide plus-tree selection. Over 11,000 candidate plus trees were initially identified, and >1,000 were confirmed in the final selection, most of which originated from Guangxi, Jiangxi, Hunan, and Zhejiang Provinces. Ultimately, breeding institutions across multiple provinces advanced subsets of the finally confirmed plus trees into subsequent breeding and, following their respective naming conventions, produced most of the cultivars used in this paper [2]. The geographic distribution of these cultivars has been strongly influenced by human activities, resulting in many accessions originating from the same region being dispersed across the clustering tree. Thus, cultivars developed by breeding programs in different provinces may derive from closely related germplasm pools. These findings also suggest that the use of SSR markers to resolve diversity and shared ancestry in C. oleifera populations can clarify their spread from origins to production areas and guide hybrid crosses that minimize inbreeding depression.

The individual inbreeding coefficients (Fi) indicate mild inbreeding in the combined sample. A small subset of accessions (GZY12, E465, CR3, CL21, GW1, and CL3) presented high Fi values (≥ 0.25). The high Fi values might be due to crosses between nearby relatives in wild stands. In addition, trees that come from small patches where closely related individuals mate are often selected, whereas long-term self-pollination is unlikely because C. oleifera is self-incompatible [23]. A similar high Fi phenomenon was also reported in C. hainanica populations [39]. Because Fi here is based on only 30 SSR loci in a polyploid context, these values should be viewed as indicative rather than definitive. Potential biases from locus number, null alleles, and microsatellite homoplasy remain, even with dosage-aware genotyping; therefore, ‘high-Fi’ individuals are highlighted as putative cases and warrant confirmation with denser marker sets. For breeding practices, these putative high-Fi cultivars should not be paired with each other; instead, they should be crossed with genetically distant parents to minimize the risk of inbreeding depression.

SSR fingerprint construction

C. oleifera is usually hexaploid with a complex genetic background [1, 26]. We employed SSR-Seq for SSR genotyping, whereby sequencing, assembly, and alignment confirm amplicon identity, reduce nonspecific amplification, and, importantly, allow allele-dosage quantification [7]. For example, at Coz01008, both CRO156 and GZY4 carried alleles with 3 and 5 GAAA repeats, but their allele-dosage ratios were 1:5 in CRO156 and 2:4 in GZY4. At Coz11161, XL27 and ZL2 harbored TAG-repeat alleles of 6, 7, and 8 units, with dosage ratios of 3:2:1 in XL27 and 4:1:1 in ZL2 (Additional Fig. 3). Therefore, even when different varieties have the same bands, SSR-seq can detect each allelic dose, thereby distinguishing the corresponding varieties, which is difficult to achieve via traditional polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and fluorescent SSR technology. Conventional SSR detection provides only two pieces of information (band size and presence/absence), whereas SSR-Seq adds a third dimension: allele dosage.

The authenticity and purity of nursery stock varieties of fruit trees are fundamental for ensuring long-term high quality and yield in orchards. However, relying solely on phenotype makes it difficult to distinguish which variety the nursery stock belongs to, often resulting in losses by the time results are obtained three to five years later. Molecular fingerprint profiles can be used to identify varieties at the seedling stage, thus providing references for growers and seedling management agencies [41]. Extensive research has been conducted on a wide range of agricultural and forestry crops [11]. In polyploid species, such as oil-Camellia [9, 10] and Salix psammophila (tetraploid) [42], alleles can be present in more than one copy and allele dosage is difficult to estimate correctly. The MAC-PR and “allelic phenotype” methods were adopted to determine the genotype of SSRs in some polyploid species [20]. However, traditional approaches often fail to assess allelic dosages accurately. In oil-camellia, 10 and 24 fluorescent SSR markers have been used to construct molecular fingerprints of varieties and germplasms that do not incorporate allele-dosage information [9, 10]. In this study, we constructed DA-SSR fingerprints of 44 varieties using two core loci (Coz09127 and Coz14197). The two core loci effectively differentiated among all the tested varieties (Fig. 7). This approach uses only two primer pairs, one multiplex PCR, and a single SSRSeq run, thereby improving the convenience, reproducibility, and efficiency of varietal identification. Therefore, the DA-SSR fingerprint presented here might play significant roles in the variety identification and genetic study of C. oleifera. These loci, selected for high PIC and clear amplification, yielded consistent dosage-aware genotypes in the tested germplasm. Validation in other populations will be valuable and is planned for future work. On the other hand, dosage-aware SSR fingerprints can be integrated into breeding pipelines to support QTL mapping and marker-assisted selection for traits such as fruit size, oil content, and pest/disease resistance in polyploid C. oleifera.

Conclusion

We conducted genome-wide SSR discovery across five oil-Camellia genomes, designing 14,854 primer pairs in C. oleifera and validating 132 new markers. From these, 30 informative loci were used to genotype 44 cultivars, and incorporating allele dosage consistently sharpened clustering and improved cultivar discrimination compared with dosage-unaware scoring. Building on this, we established a two-locus, dosage-aware fingerprint that uniquely distinguishes cultivars. The results can be used to infer dissemination routes and genetic relatedness among cultivars, providing practical guidance for cross breeding. These findings will advance genetic research in C. oleifera, enable more informed selection of hybrid combinations, facilitate the authentication of cultivar seedlings, QTL mapping, and marker-assisted selection in C. oleifera.

Methods

Experimental materials and DNA extraction

This study utilized 44 C. oleifera cultivars (Table 2). These cultivars encompass regionally adapted varieties across 14 of the 16 oil-Camellia-growing provinces in China. All materials were sourced from the oil-Camellia germplasm resource garden in Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China (E119°57′, N30°03′). Young leaves collected in the spring were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80 °C. DNA was extracted using a Plant DNA Extraction Kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China). The integrity of the DNA was assessed through 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, and the DNA concentration was determined using a Quawell Q5000 spectrophotometer (Quawell Technology, Inc., United States), diluted to 50 ng/µL, and stored at −80 °C until use.

Genomic SSR locus mining

The genomes of one hexaploid C. oleifera [22] and four diploid oil-Camellia species, C. oleifera var. Nanyongensis [1], C. lanceoleosa [23], C. chekiangoleosa [24], and C. crapnelliana [25] were downloaded from NCBI Bioproject (PRJNA732216, PRJNA1105714, PRJNA780224, and PRJNA993816) and China National Center for Bioinformation (CNCB, GWHBGBN00000000). The sequences were analyzed using the MISA program (http://pgrc.ipk-gatersleben.de/misa/misa.html). The criteria for SSR identification included a minimum of 10 dinucleotide repeats and at least five tri- to hexanucleotide repeats, with two simple repeat sequences less than 100 bp apart, classified as composite SSRs. The primer design was automated using scripts p3_in.pl, primer3_core, and p3_out.pl [43].

Development and validation of SSR markers

The 2-nt repeats of SSRs might result in stuttering and non-specific amplification, affecting the accuracy of the testing results [11]. Therefore, SSR loci containing tri- and tetranucleotide repeats were selected for primer design to ensure both representativeness and amplification stability. A total of 225 such loci were randomly chosen for validation. PCR amplification was performed via the use of genomic DNA from eight samples (CR2, GZY3, XG, XL210, CRO156, CL4, E102, CL53). PCRs were conducted in a 20 µL mixture containing 1 µL of template DNA, 0.4 µL of each primer, 0.4 µL of dNTPs, 2 µL of 10× buffer, and 0.5 U of Taq enzyme, which was adjusted to 20 µL with ddH2O. The PCR protocol included initial denaturation at 94 °C for 3 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, annealing at 60 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s, with a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. The target bands were resolved via 10% nondenaturing polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and visualized via silver staining. The gel was stained with a 1 g/L AgNO3 solution for 10–15 min, developed with a developing solution (20 g/L NaOH with 10 mL formaldehyde) for 5–8 min, and washed twice with purified water before imaging. The SSR loci that yielded the target bands in the eight samples were selected for further experiments. These markers were subsequently aligned with C. oleifera var. Nanyongensis, and chromosome distribution maps of each marker were created via TBtools-II software [44]. Two polymorphic SSR loci were selected on each chromosome, yielding a 30-marker panel for downstream analyses.

SSR genotyping via SSRSeq

Genomic DNA from 44 samples was amplified via 30 SSR loci. The PCR products were detected via 1% agarose gel electrophoresis. Clear single-band primers obtained from the amplification were used for subsequent experiments. SSR-seq was performed by Genesky Biotechnologies, Inc. (Shanghai, China). PCR amplification, high-throughput sequencing of the PCR products, and SSR genotyping were performed as described by Cui et al. [26]. Each locus was first amplified on an AB 2720 Thermal Cycler (Life Technologies Corporation, USA) in a 10 µL reaction containing 1× PCR buffer (Takara, Dalian, China), 2.0 mM MgCl₂, 0.2 mM each dNTP, 0.2 µM forward primer, 0.2 µM reverse primer, 1 U Hot-Start Taq DNA polymerase (Takara, Dalian, China), and 1 µL template DNA (10 ng µL⁻¹). Cycling: 95 °C 2 min; 11 cycles of 95 °C 20 s, 63→58 °C annealing (− 0.5 °C per cycle) 40 s, 72 °C 60 s; then 24 cycles of 95 °C 20 s, 65 °C 30 s, 72 °C 60 s; final extension 72 °C 2 min. A multiplex PCR primer panel was prepared according to a standard of 15 primer pairs per panel and amplified via multiplex PCR [26]. Each multiplex used 20 µL with 1× buffer, 2.0 mM MgCl₂, 0.2 mM each dNTP, 1 U Hot-Start Taq, 2 µL DNA (~ 20 ng total), and primers at 0.10 µM each as a starting concentration. Primer amounts were fine-tuned to 0.06–0.15 µM per assay to balance signal across loci. Cycling followed the same touchdown profile adapted for multiplexing: 95 °C 2 min; 11 cycles of 94 °C 20 s, 63→58 °C (− 0.5 °C per cycle) 40 s, 72 °C 60 s; then 24 cycles of 94 °C 20 s, 65 °C 30 s, 72 °C 60 s; final extension 72 °C 2 min.

The specificity and amplification efficiency of each primer pair in the multiplex PCR system were assessed by sequencing, and the primer mixture in the PCR panel was adjusted and optimized. An optimized multiplex PCR primer panel was used to amplify all the samples. Postquality control and amplification products from all multiplex PCR primer panels, using the same sample DNA, were combined to ensure equivalent amounts of each locus primer amplification product. Index sequences were introduced into the library ends compatible with the Illumina platform through PCR amplification. The reaction used an 11-cycle PCR program to minimize bias. All the sample index PCR amplification products were mixed in equal amounts. The final FastTarget™ sequencing library was purified via gel extraction, and the fragment size distribution was verified via an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. The molar concentration of the library was precisely quantified before high-throughput sequencing was conducted on an Illumina HiSeq platform in a 2 × 150 bp paired-end sequencing format to obtain FastQ data. Sequencing data were merged via FLASH software [45], and successful merges were aligned to reference sequences via BLAST+ (http://ftp://ftp.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/blast/executables/blast+/LATEST) to filter effective reads. After alignment with the reference genome via BLAST+, the enrichment efficiencies of the target segments were calculated. SSR motifs were identified from each read, and reads corresponding to different repeat numbers of the motif were quantified.

We followed the sequencing-based SSR genotyping workflow for polyploids described by Cui et al. [26]. In brief, paired-end reads were quality-filtered, merged, and assigned to loci; within each locus, reads were collapsed to haplotypes and tallied by repeat number to obtain per-allele read counts. Candidate alleles were retained using a frequency gate proportional to ploidy (hexaploid threshold ≈ 0.5 × 1/6). Read counts were then corrected for PCR stutter (locus-specific slip ratios) and for allele-specific amplification bias (estimated from high-depth samples). Corrected allele frequencies were renormalized (sum = 1), and allele dosage was obtained as ploidy × corrected frequency and rounded to an integer partition summing to six. Implementation followed Cui et al., using the Perl scripts str_count.pl (https://github.com/ccoo22/SSRseq_count) and str_type.pl (https://github.com/ccoo22/SSRSeq), as well as the online SSRSeq v1.1 typing service for consistency checks (http://bioinfo.geneskybiotech.com/software/ssrseq_type/v1.1/en/).

Statistical analysis

Data for each marker, including observed heterozygosity (Ho), expected heterozygosity (He), number of alleles (Na), number of effective alleles (Ae), allele frequency and polymorphism information content (https://github.com/huangkang1987/polygene). Neighbor-joining (NJ) trees were. The resulting trees were visualized or compared with the iTOL (https://itol.embl.de). To assess topological congruence, we computed the Robinson–Foulds (RF) distance between the dosage-aware and dosage-unaware NJ trees (built in RStudio using ape package); lower RF (or normalized RF) indicates more similar inferred evolutionary relationships [46]. Population structure analysis and determination of the inbreeding coefficient (Fi) of each cultivar were also performed in PolyGene v1.7 and visualized in R. Population structure was inferred with STRUCTURE (via PolyGene v1.7) under the admixture model with correlated allele frequencies. SSR genotypes were encoded as dominant presence/absence (allele presence = 1, absence = 0). We tested K = 2–10 with 10 independent replicates per K (burn-in = 50,000; MCMC = 100,000; thinning = 1). The optimal K was determined using the Evanno ΔK criterion, with the mean log-likelihood (LnP(K)) trend examined for consistency. For each K, replicate Q-matrices were aligned across runs and averaged prior to visualization. Individuals with max q ≥ 0.80 were classified as pure, otherwise admixed; Q barplots were drawn with individuals ordered identically to the SSR-dosage NJ tree to facilitate comparison. Analyses were conducted in R using pophelper (Evanno summaries and run alignment), and LEA (sNMF cross-entropy).

Construction of the fingerprint

We compared dosage-aware vs. dosage-unaware scoring across 30 SSR loci and tallied each locus’s unique discrimination count, then ranked loci by PIC and resolving power. A minimal, located on different chromosomes two-locus panel (Coz09127 and Coz14197) separated all cultivars. Cultivars were identified by an allele size-dosage fingerprint; exact two-locus matches were considered the same cultivar, whereas any mismatch indicated distinct cultivars. Plots were produced in R.
