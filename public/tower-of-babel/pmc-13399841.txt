A Single‐Bundle Anterior Cruciate Ligament Reconstruction Technique Using Quadrupled Semitendinosus Tendon With Tibial Double Knotless Anchor Fixation And Femoral Suspensory Fixation
Authors: Zhenlong Liu, Defu Liu, Jianli Gao, Tianying Tang, Xintong Jiang, Xi Gong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAnterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injury is a common sports injury and has a significant effect on knee function. Its injury can seriously affect athletic ability and quality of life and may lead to complications such as secondary osteoarthritis. ACL reconstruction is an important means of treating ACL injury, which is usually accomplished under arthroscopy by using autologous grafts of bone‐tendon‐bone or hamstring tendon. This article describes a modified single‐bundle ACL reconstruction technique based on autologous grafts of the semitendinosus tendon. By adopting the strategy of only a single tendon transplantation fixed by double knotless anchors on tibia and suspension on femur, ligament function reconstruction can be completed and the gracilis tendon can be preserved, better preserving the function of the gracilis muscle.

The video shows the use of the semitendinosus tendon, combined with the tibial double knotless anchors fixation and femoral suspensory fixation, for the reconstruction of the anterior cruciate ligament. This approach enables the functional reconstruction of the anterior cruciate ligament while minimizing the impact on the original anatomical structure of the knee joint. The anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) is an important structure for maintaining the stability of the knee joint. It is approximately 30 mm long and 10 mm wide. 1 , 2 ACL injury is a common sports injury, affecting many age groups, including young, active athletes, and older patients. 3 This injury can lead to secondary traumatic osteoarthritis, meniscus injury, and ACL injury on the healthy side. 4

Anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction is the most common approach for treating ACL injuries. 5 Autologous grafts of bone‐tendon‐bone (BTB) and hamstring tendon (HT) are the 2 most common grafts performed in ACL construction. 6 , 7 , 8 BTB grafts have several postoperative drawbacks, including anterior knee pain (AKP), quadriceps weakness, possible patellar fractures, patellar tendon rupture, and subpatellar contracture. There are also problems with the use of HT grafts, such as the increasing risk of joint laxity during medium‐ and long‐term follow‐up, hamstring tissue weakness, delayed graft‐tunnel healing, and reduced strength during deep flexion. 9 , 10 Nevertheless, there are very few differences that have been clearly shown between these 2 reconstruction techniques, and the graft choice comes down to the surgeon's discretion. 7

We describe a modified ACL reconstruction technique that can be accomplished by using autologous grafts of the semitendinosus tendon in a quadrupled arrangement. At the same time, it combines the methods of tibial double knotless anchor fixation and femoral suspensory fixation, enabling the tendon grafts to have better contact with the bone tunnel and thereby promoting recovery. Compared with the traditional HT reconstruction method using 2 tendons, the semitendinosus and gracilis tendons, it has less impact on the original anatomical structure of the knee joint.

Preoperative Evaluation

Physical examinations, radiographs, and magnetic resonance imaging are conducted before the operation to evaluate anterior cruciate ligament injuries.

Preparation

The patient is placed in the supine position. Aseptic preparation and anesthesia is performed, and the corresponding anatomical positions of the knee joint are marked.

Surgical Procedure

A 3‐cm longitudinal incision is made on the medial side of the tibial tubercle and subcutaneous fat is cleared to reveal the pes anserinus. The semitendinosus tendon is harvested by standard technique and cleared of any muscular tissue. The appropriate length of the tendon is 28 cm or longer, and it is folded into 4 strands to produce a quadrupled semitendinosus tendon graft. The graft diameter is then measured and is usually 7‐9 mm. The graft is prepared on both ends with suture (Ethibond) to prevent it from falling off (Figure 1).

Superior view of the graft (black arrows), fully prepared and tensioned. It is folded into 4 strands, with a length of 7 cm after folding. Both ends are firmly woven with knitting thread.

An arthroscope is inserted through the conventional approach (anterolateral and anteromedial approaches) to explore the internal structure of the knee joint and evaluate the stump of ACL, as well as the combined injury of the meniscus and cartilage (Figure 2). The ACL stump and lateral wall of the intercondylar notch are cleared. Subsequently, the posterior edge of the lateral wall is exposed, and an intercondylar notchplasty is then performed. Finally, fat pads and anterior synovial folds between the condyles are cleared to expose the field of vision.

Arthroscopic image viewing from the anterolateral approach with the patient in the supine position (left knee). Observe the remnant of the anterior cruciate ligament (black arrow) under arthroscopy.

The entry point of the femoral tunnel is located via the anteromedial approach, and the midpoint is 6 mm away from the posterior edge of the lateral condyle of the femur. The femoral tunnel is drilled by a 4.5‐mm drill (Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA) at the same size as the graft (Figure 3). The length of the wide bone tunnel is 25 mm, and the total length of the superior bone tunnel is 35 mm (Figure 4). The 2 bone tunnels are concentric and a long needle is used to guide a No. 5 suture into the superior bone tunnel, in preparation for subsequent passage of the graft.

Arthroscopic image viewing from the anteromedial approaches with the patient in the supine position (left knee). The black arrow represents the 4.5‐mm drill for drilling the femoral tunnel.

Arthroscopic image viewing from the anteromedial approach with the patient in the supine position (left knee). The black arrow represents the established femoral bone tunnel.

The tibial footprint position is located by the Acufex point‐to‐point tibial tunnel guide (Smith & Nephew) (Figure 5). The positioning device was set at 55°, and an 8‐mm‐diameter drill is used for drilling, until the size of the graft is achieved. The tibial tunnel entry point is cleared using shaver (Smith & Nephew). The tibial footprint of the ACL lies in the intercondylar area, slightly medial to the midline.

Arthroscopic image viewing from the anterolateral approach with the patient in the supine position (left knee). The blue arrow represents the drill for drilling the tibial bone tunnel and the black arrow represents the needle for guiding the graft into the femoral tunnel.

One TightRope (Smith & Nephew) and a long guide Kirschner wire (Smith & Nephew) are used to guide the graft into the bone tunnel (Figure 6). After passing through the femoral bone tunnel, it is flipped and fixed. The femoral footprint is fixed with a suspensory bone button. Check that the graft does not move during flexion and extension and that there is no impact in the front and outside during straightening. Tighten the graft at 10° and reconstruct the ligament insertion point to the tibial bone tunnel under the posterior push stress. Two 4.75‐mm bone tunnels are drilled for tibial fixation (Figure 5). After the tendon graft passes through the tibial bone tunnel (Figure 7), two 4.5‐mm PUSHLOCK knotless anchors (Arthrex) are fixed to the 2 newly built bone tunnels. One knotless anchor tightens the tension reduction wire, and the other knotless anchor fixes the braided wire (Figure 8). Rinse, suture each layer, apply pressure bandaging, and fix the splint in a straight position.

DISCUSSION

The ACL reconstruction method with autologous grafts (such as BTB, HT) has better biocompatibility and promotes bone‐bone or tendon‐bone healing, because the grafts are derived from the patient's own tissues. Meanwhile, the autologous graft method also has the advantages of lower re‐fracture rate and revision risk, reduced infection risk, better knee joint stability, and lower risk of anterior knee pain after surgery. 11 The guidelines of the American College of Orthopaedic Surgeons mention that surgeons may tend to choose BTB grafts to reduce the risk of transplant failure or infection, or choose HT to reduce the risk of forward or kneeling pain. 12 Compared with BTB autologous transplantation, HT autologous transplantation has less anterior knee pain and a lower incidence of patella‐related complications after surgery. Moreover, HT grafts do not require bone block removal. Therefore, there is less limitation in knee joint movement after the operation, and the early recovery of mobility ability is faster. 11 , 12

The traditional autologous transplantation method for HT requires the simultaneous extraction of semitendinosus tendons and gracilis tendons, which has a certain impact on the original knee flexion ability of the knee joint. This method improves upon the traditional HT autologous transplantation method. The advantages and disadvantages are summarized in Table 2. This method requires only a semitendinosus tendon (with a length of at least 28 cm) and then folds it into 4 strands. The reconstruction of ACL function can be completed without using the gracilis tendon, better preserving its function. Meanwhile, this method uses a suspended bone button instead of a compression button. The tendons can have a larger contact area with the 4 walls of the bone tunnels and thus a larger healing area. Because the semitendinosus tendon needs to be folded into 4 strands, its length must be at least 28 cm to conform to the original structure of the ACL.
