Development and explanation of a machine learning model for identifying non-localized early-onset T1 colorectal cancer
Authors: Yin Zhang, Fuzhou Han, Mingyu Zheng, Duo Xu, Nan Yao, Wenqiang Li, Jun Qu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFor patients with early-onset T1 colorectal cancer (CRC), it is crucial to perform radical resection with minimal tissue damage. This study aims to develop and elucidate a machine learning model for the identification of non-located early-onset T1 CRC.MethodsWe extracted relevant data from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database and randomly allocated patients into a training set and a validation set. Five machine learning models were utilized: extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), support vector machine (SVM), random forest (RF), decision tree (DT), and logistic regression (LR) were developed for the tumor metastasis status prediction. Area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) and confusion matrix were used to evaluate the model. The Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) method was employed as the primary technique for model explanation.ResultsA cohort of 1,878 patients was selected according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. The XGBoost model was selected for its favorable performance, with an AUC of 0.755 and accuracy of 0.833 in the validation cohort. Significant risk factors, identified by SHAP values greater than 0.1 for non-localized T1 tumors, included carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) levels exceeding 5 ng/mL, tumor size greater than 5 cm, mucinous adenocarcinoma, signet-ring cell adenocarcinoma, tumor location in the left colon, and poorly differentiated or undifferentiated histological grades. Additionally, black race, a diagnostic-to-surgical interval exceeding one month and PNI may represent potential risk factors.ConclusionThe XGBoost model holds the potential to assist clinicians in evaluating and selecting optimal treatment strategies for patients with early-onset T1 CRC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03998-8.Keywords: T1 stage colorectal cancer, Early-onset colorectal cancer, Machine learning, Lymph node metastasis, Distant metastasis

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) represents the third most commonly diagnosed malignancy, accounting for 9.6% of all cancer incidences. Additionally, it is the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality, comprising 9.3% of all cancer deaths [1]. Early-onset colorectal cancer (EO-CRC) is defined as CRC occurring in individuals younger than 50 years of age and the global burden of EO-CRC is rising [2]. Despite a general decline in the overall incidence of CRC in certain high-income regions, the incidence of EO-CRC has increased by 64.3% over the past two decades [3]. Early-onset colorectal cancer (EO-CRC) has emerged as the leading cause of cancer-related mortality in young men and ranks second in young women, following breast cancer [4]. By 2030, the incidence rate of EO-CRC is projected to increase by 131.1% [5]. Currently, the most commonly recommended age for routine screening is 50 years, with some countries advocating for initiation at 45 years. The absence of routine screening in younger populations often results in diagnoses at more advanced stages. Consequently, healthcare systems face the significant challenge of balancing the financial burden on medical insurance with the necessity for early screening in younger patients. Furthermore, the prognosis for patients with EO-CRC may be less favorable compared to those with late-onset CRC, as EO-CRC is associated with a higher risk of local or distant metastasis [6–8]. Consequently, EO-CRC is hypothesized to exhibit more aggressive biological behavior.

T1 stage CRC is characterized by a primary tumor that invades only the submucosa. While the degree of invasion may reflect an early stage of development, approximately 10% of T1 stage CRC are accompanied by lymph node metastasis, and 3% by distant metastasis [9–12]. Endoscopic resection is frequently considered for T1 stage CRC, especially in the absence of adverse histological features. However, research suggests that specific histological characteristics, such as lymphovascular invasion and poor differentiation, are correlated with an increased risk of lymph node metastasis, potentially necessitating surgical intervention [13–15]. In instances where endoscopic resection yields noncurative results, further surgical intervention may be required to achieve complete excision of malignant tissue and reduce the risk of metastasis. Therefore, although endoscopic techniques provide a minimally invasive approach for the treatment of T1 stage CRC, the possibility of non-localized disease and the associated risk of lymph node metastasis necessitate a comprehensive evaluation of histopathological factors to inform treatment decisions effectively.

For patients diagnosed with early-onset T1 CRC, it is imperative to undertake radical resection while minimizing tissue damage, as both short-term outcomes and long-term quality of life must be taken into account. Traditional predictive models for early-onset T1 CRC primarily rely on histological evaluations and linear statistical methods such as logistic regression. However, histological assessments alone cannot provide accurate risk stratification. Logistic regression fundamentally assumes additive relationships between variables, which inherently limits its capacity to model complex nonlinear interactions [16]. Machine learning, with its capacity to handle imbalanced data and mitigate overfitting, presents a promising alternative. Moreover, machine learning models can be demonstrated through web-based interfaces and interpreted via multiple methods. Our objective is to evaluate multiple machine learning models for predicting non-localized early-onset T1 CRC and to identify the most effective model using data from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database.

Materials and methods

Study design and patient selection

Clinical data was obtained from the SEER program using SEER*Stat 8.4.0.1 software in the period of 2010 to 2020. International Classification of Cancer Diseases Edition III (ICD-O-3) were used to searching pathological evidence. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age < 50 years old; (2) diagnosed with CRC (excluding appendix); (3) pathological T1 stage. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) accepted radiation and/or systemic therapy before and/or during surgery; (2) unknown N, M stage; (3) unknown size; (4) unknown differentiation degree; (5) unknown location; (6) multiple primary cancer. This study was retrospectively designed and the related data were retreived from an open database, informed consent was not used. The workflow was shown as Fig. 1.

The analysis flowchart of this study. Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results, SEER; extreme gradient boosting, XGBoost; support vector machine, SVM; random forest, RF; decision tree, DT; logistic regression, LR

Variables

In this study, age, sex, race, tumor site, pathology type, tumor size, differentiation degree, carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) level, perineural invasion (PNI), months from diagnoses to surgery, N stage and M stage were selected from SEER database. The tumor site was grouped into right colon (cecum, ascending colon, hepatic flexure and transverse colon), left colon (splenic flexure, descending colon, sigmoid colon and rectosigmoid junction colon) and rectum. The histology variable was classified as adenocarcinoma with adenoma background, adenocarcinoma, mucinous adenocarcinoma and signet-ring cell carcinoma. CEA levels over 5 ng/mL were defined as positive.

Model development and evaluation

Localized tumor was defined as T1N0M0 and non-localized tumor were defined as T1N + Mx or T1NxM1. The entire cohort was divided into training and test sets at a 7:3 ratio. During model development, the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) was applied to preprocess the training set, followed by 10-fold cross-validation for robustness assessment. Five Machine learning models including extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost) model, support vector machine (SVM), random forest (RF), decision tree (DT) and logistic regression (LR) were developed for the tumor metastasis status prediction in this study. Hyperparameter tuning was performed using grid research method.

Area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), F1-score, precision, sensitivity, specificity, accuracy and confusion matrix were used to evaluate the model.

The Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) plot was performed for explaining the model for decision making which was a game-theoretic approach to explain the output of the model, demonstrated the contribution of the variables to the outcome. Permutation feature Importance (PFI) was performed to evaluate a feature’s importance by randomly shuffling its values and measuring the resulting degradation in model performance. The greater the performance drop, the more critical the feature is to the model’s predictions.

Statistical analysis and software

All data analysis, data visualization, and statistical analysis in this manuscript were performed in R Studio (version 4.1.2). Categorical variables were expressed in counts and percentages, and continuous variables were presented as means ± standard deviations (SD). The t-test was used for continuous variables, while categorical variables were compared using the Chi-Squared test. The complete code has been updated in the supplementary material 1.

A cohort of 1,878 patients was selected based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Of these, 1,315 patients were allocated to the training set and 563 to the validation set, adhering to a 7:3 ratio (Table 1). Within the training set, the median age was 39.3 years with a SD of 7.8 years, and 391 patients (29.7%) were classified as non-localized. In the validation set, the median age was 39.9 years with a SD of 7.6 years, and 125 patients (22.2%) were identified as non-localized. After applying the SMOTE method for data oversampling and performing 10-fold cross-validation on the training set, the models were evaluated on the validation set.

Evaluation of the best machine learning model

The primary metric utilized for model evaluation is the AUC. The AUC values for the models are as follows: 0.755 for the XGBoost model (Fig. 2A), 0.700 for the SVM model (Fig. 2B), 0.719 for the RF model (Fig. 2C), 0.739 for the DT model (Fig. 2D), and 0.752 for the LR model (Fig. 2E). Confusion matrix was performed to evaluate the correctness of the models (Fig. 3A-E). Accuracy, F1-score, precision, sensitivity and specificity were performed for comprehensively assessing model performance (Fig. 3F-J).

Given its superior performance on the validation set, the XGBoost model was deemed the most effective predictive model and was subsequently selected for further research.

Model interpretation and feature importance visualization

A SHAP plot was employed to illustrate the significance of each feature within the XGBoost model (Fig. 4A-B). CEA, tumor size, pathology, tumor site, and histological grade were identified as significant contributors to the model, as indicated by their high mean SHAP values (> 0.1). To complement SHAP analysis. We have incorporated the explanation tools provided by XGBoost, along with PFI method, for global model interpretation (Fig. 4C-D). The results were consistent with those obtained using SHAP, confirming the accuracy of the SHAP findings. In the XGBoost explanation tool and PFI method, sex seems to be a potential significant contributor. To further investigate the model’s performance across patient subgroups, we conducted sex-specific analyses based on AUC scores and confusion matrix. The results demonstrate consistent predictive performance in male cohorts (AUC = 0.76) and female cohorts (AUC = 0.73), confirming the model’s robustness across genders (Supplementary material 2).

Discussion

T1 stage CRC poses a distinct challenge in treatment decision-making, as it may be addressed through either endoscopic techniques or surgical intervention. Poor differentiation grade, lymphovascular invasion, and positive margins are considered unfavorable histologic features in T1 stage CRC cases that have undergone endoscopic en-bloc resection. Patients exhibiting these adverse histologic characteristics require a salvage operation, as these features are associated with an elevated risk of local recurrence and lymph node or distant metastasis [17]. EO-CRC may exhibit more aggressive characteristics, necessitating a more thorough evaluation of the clinicopathological features associated with early-onset T1 CRC. Identifying which clinicopathological features are indicative of lymph node involvement or distant metastasis in early-onset T1 CRC patients is crucial for developing an effective treatment strategy. Consequently, constructing a predictive model to identify the risk factors associated with non-localized early-onset T1 CRC is vital for the clinical management of EO-CRC patients. In this study, we constructed five machine learning models to predict lymph node or distant metastasis in early-onset T1 CRC, utilizing data from the SEER database. Following evaluation, the XGBoost model was selected as the optimal model. The SHAP method was employed to interpret the model’s predictions. The analysis identified CEA >5 ng/mL, tumor size >5 cm, mucinous adenocarcinoma, signet-ring cell carcinoma (SRCC), left side located, poorly differentiated and undifferentiated tumors as significant risk factors for lymph node or distant metastasis in early-onset T1 CRC. Diagnostic-to-surgical interval exceeding one month, PNI and black race are potential risk factors. Nevertheless, the results must be approached with caution. We must recognize that a diagnostic-to-surgical interval exceeding one month could be a consequence of the disease’s complexity, leading to delays in surgery, rather than a true causal relationship. Finally, we deployed our model using a Shiny-based web interface. For patients undergoing endoscopic resection, the model assists in determining whether additional surgery is needed and the optimal frequency for follow-up care. In low-risk cases, regular surveillance without the need for surgery can be recommended to maintain bowel function. In high-risk cases, we recommend performing radical surgery. Our machine learning model demonstrates strong practicality in resource-limited settings. The model is built exclusively on readily available clinicopathological parameters, requiring neither immunohistochemical staining nor molecular diagnostics, thus ensuring accessibility in regions with constrained medical resources.

Previous studies have predominantly concentrated on the metastasis status of T1 CRC rather than specifically addressing early-onset T1 CRC. In the context of T1 CRC, the assessment of lymph node metastasis primarily depends on four clinicopathological factors: lymphovascular invasion, histological grade, depth of submucosal invasion, and tumor budding. Among these, lymphovascular invasion is a particularly strong predictor of lymph node metastasis in T1 CRC. A meta-analysis encompassing 21 studies that included lymphovascular invasion demonstrated a significant association between lymphovascular invasion and lymph node metastasis [18]. Furthermore, lymphovascular invasion may serve as a risk factor for recurrence [19, 20]. Therefore, assessing the status of lymphovascular invasion is crucial in cases of T1 CRC, and additional staining techniques may be necessary in certain scenarios, such as endoscopic resection. A high histological grade is correlated with an increased risk of lymph node metastasis in T1 CRC. Caputo et al. identified poor differentiation as the sole independent significant predictor of nodal involvement in pT1 colorectal tumors [21]. Sun et al. demonstrated that, in comparison to well or moderately differentiated tumors, poorly differentiated tumors are associated with a higher risk of lymph node metastasis in patients with T1 CRC [11]. However, poor differentiation does not constitute an independent risk factor for 5-year overall survival in these patients. Notably, when a lesion exhibits multiple levels of histological differentiation, the criteria for evaluation differ between the World Health Organization (WHO) classification and the Japanese guidelines. According to the WHO classification, tumor grading is determined by the least differentiated component, whereas the Japanese guidelines classify tumors based on the predominant differentiation [22, 23]. A study demonstrated that the least differentiation analysis exhibited increased sensitivity, thereby decreasing the likelihood of incorrectly classifying patients with lymph node metastasis as negative in cases of T1 CRC [24]. The depth of submucosal invasion remains a contentious factor in the context of lymph node metastasis. According to Japanese guidelines, a depth of submucosal invasion exceeding 1000 μm following endoscopic therapy warrants consideration of a salvage operation [25]. However, some studies have indicated that the depth of submucosal invasion is not an independent risk factor for lymph node metastasis in T1 CRC [26, 27]. In certain instances, accurately assessing the depth of submucosal invasion presents challenges, and variations in diagnostic methodologies may lead to reduced interobserver agreement. The application of immunohistochemical staining has been shown to enhance the precision of evaluating submucosal invasion depth [18]. A retrospective study with a limited sample size identified tumor budding as a significant risk factor for lymph node metastasis in T1 CRC, demonstrating a high sensitivity of 83.3% [28]. Research conducted by Lee et al. indicated that an average of more than 3.5 tumor budding foci per ×200 field was associated with an increased likelihood of lymph node metastasis in T1 CRC [29]. In a meta-analysis, tumor budding demonstrated a significant association with lymph node metastases, exhibiting minimal statistical heterogeneity (Odds ratio: 4.00, 95% confidence interval: 3.36 to 4.76; P < 0.001) [13]. In contrast, distant metastasis is infrequently observed in T1 CRC compared to lymph node metastasis. The CEA level may serve as the most robust indicator of distant metastasis [9, 12, 30].

The predictive models for T1 colorectal cancer (CRC) have primarily been developed using multivariable logistic regression (LR) methods. Oh et al. constructed a nomogram based on histologic grade, depth of submucosal invasion, vascular invasion, and tumor budding [31]. They found that vascular invasion and high-grade histology were the strongest risk factors. Although our database does not include vascular invasion, our model suggests that high-grade histology is also a significant risk factor for early-onset T1 CRC. Kang et al. developed a predictive model based on tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes combined with histology, using the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression method [32]. Some studies have incorporated preoperative imaging results to develop predictive models [33, 34]. There are also machine learning models designed to assess the risk of metastasis in T1 CRC, most of which rely on methods such as liquid biopsy, proteomics, or whole-slide imaging [35–38]. However, due to economic and technical constraints, these methods may not be feasible in regions with limited resources. In contrast to the aforementioned models, the unique features of our model include: (1) focusing specifically on the early-onset CRC group; (2) using easily accessible clinical and pathological features for modeling; (3) employing multiple machine learning methods to select the optimal model, which is deployed on a website, making it user-friendly and clinically applicable; and (4) utilizing the SEER database, which ensures a large sample size and enhances the model’s stability. Compared to late-onset CRC, early-onset CRC is more likely to develop lymph node metastasis [39–41]. CEA levels may be closely associated with distant metastasis [42]. Additionally, the log odds of positive lymph nodes might be an effective predictive factor for the prognosis of early-onset colorectal cancer [43]. Demb et al. demonstrated that racial and ethnic disparities in early-onset CRC mortality were evident [44]. However, due to the racial imbalance in the SEER cohort, we did not observe a significant impact of race on early-onset T1 CRC metastasis. Jeri-Yabar et al. found that, compared to late-onset CRC, early-onset CRC is more prone to distant metastasis. Xie et al. suggested that the lymph node metastasis rate was higher in early-onset CRC patients with right colon involvement than in those with left colon involvement [39]. This finding differs from ours, which may be due to differences in definitions of the right and left colon, age stratification, and tumor staging. Further validation is needed in future studies.

There are some limitations in our study. SEER lacks detailed histopathological variables such as lymphovascular invasion, tumor budding and depth of submucosal invasion—all established predictors of metastasis in T1 CRC. This may lead to underestimation of high-risk subgroups. Secondly, this study lacks external validation. Due to the limited sample size of early-onset T1 CRC patients in our single-center cohort, external validation on an independent dataset was not feasible. Future validation through multi-center collaborations is warranted. Finally, our model was developed based on the U.S. population, and we acknowledge the potential limitations in its applicability to diverse regions with varying healthcare standards. Its underrepresentation of Asian and African populations limits generalizability to global settings. Future studies integrating multi-ethnic cohorts and advanced pathological annotations are essential to refine risk prediction. Therefore, it is essential to conduct a multi-ethnic clinical implementation study in the future.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we developed a machine learning model utilizing the XGBoost algorithm to identify non-localized early-onset T1 CRC patients. Our analysis identified several significant risk factors for lymph node or distant metastasis in this patient population, including CEA > 5 ng/mL, tumor size > 5 cm, mucinous adenocarcinoma, SRCC, left-sided tumor location, poor or undifferentiated histological grade. The model was deployed using a Shiny-based web interface, providing valuable assistance in clinical decision-making(https://asch721.shinyapps.io/asch721/).
