A Novel PTPN2 Isoform Differentially Regulates Immune Response
Authors: Xiaoguang Li, Fedik Rahimov, Rui Wang, Lei Gao, Renze Ma, Tiankai Han, Yingtao Bi, Freidrich Cruz, Jing Wang, Shaun Grosskurth, Elizabeth Asque, Jesus Paez‐Cortez, Marc C. Levesque, Nancy E. Crosbie, Timothy Radstake, Amlan Biswas, Kanstantsin Katlinski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGenome‐wide association studies implicate the PTPN2 gene locus (18p11.21) in risk for several autoimmune diseases, including inflammatory bowel disease. Through genetic fine mapping, we identified the single‐nucleotide polymorphism rs80262450 in the PTPN2 gene as the putative causal variant. Analysis of GTEx tissue samples and genetically engineered myeloid cell lines carrying risk and nonrisk alleles of rs80262450 demonstrated increased expression of the PTPN2 splice isoform 4 (PTPN2.4), suggesting that the rs80262450 enhances disease susceptibility by favoring production of PTPN2.4. Furthermore, we found that PTPN2.4 contains a nuclear export sequence (NES) that leads to its retention in the cytoplasm. Differential localization of PTPN2.4 isoform results in a distinct protein binding profile revealed by mass‐spectrometry analysis, and its overexpression increased TNF‐α. PTPN2.4 knockdown reduced pro‐inflammatory cytokines in human macrophages. Mutations within the NES motif abolished the unique localization and function of PTPN2.4. Lastly, increased expression of PTPN2.4 was found in Crohn's disease tissues, demonstrating its involvement in the disease. Together, we identified the pathogenic isoform PTPN2.4 as a novel driver of intestinal inflammation and a potential target to attenuate inflammation in IBD.

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), which includes Crohn's disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC), is a chronic inflammatory condition characterized by immune dysregulation and the aberrant pro‐inflammatory response in the gastrointestinal tract. Protein tyrosine phosphatase nonreceptor type 2 (PTPN2) plays a crucial role as an intracellular enzyme that modulates the signaling network governing inflammatory cytokines. Its capacity to attenuate key signaling pathways makes it a central regulator in immune homeostasis, particularly within the IBD milieu [1, 2, 3]. Genome‐wide association studies (GWAS) have linked the PTPN2 gene region to multiple autoimmune diseases, including type 1 diabetes [4], rheumatoid arthritis [5], and inflammatory bowel disease [6]. Genetic variation in PTPN2 can disrupt its regulatory function, thereby fostering an environment in which aberrant immune responses are more likely to occur. Alternative splicing expands the functional capacity of the genome by generating multiple proteins with diverse intracellular localizations and functions [7]. PTPN2 exhibits a complex splicing pattern, resulting in multiple isoforms with distinct structural and functional properties. The presence of multiple PTPN2 isoforms underscores its versatility in fine‐tuning signaling events [8]. T‐cell protein tyrosine phosphatase (TCPTP), the PTPN2 isoform 2 (PTPN2.2), is a well‐studied variant that is predominantly expressed in immune cells and has specific regulatory effects on immune receptor signaling mediated by its unique N‐terminal sequence [3]. However, the functions of other isoforms remain unclear.

In this study, we revealed the presence of a novel isoform, PTPN2.4, in human blood, immune cells, spleen, and gut, with increased expression observed in patients with CD compared with non‐CD disease individuals. We also demonstrated that a common genetic variant regulates preferential splicing of PTPN2.4 and its role in inflammatory cytokines owing to its unique subcellular localization and substrate specificity. Our data highlight the potential association between dysregulated PTPN2 expression, particularly the increased exon 6a‐containing alternatively spliced transcript, and pathological inflammatory responses. Therefore, inhibiting PTPN2.4 has the potential to attenuate pathogenic inflammatory responses in patients with IBD.

Results

Fine Mapping Prioritizes rs80262450 as the Causal Variant in IBD GWAS

The 18p11.21 (chromosome 18, p arm, G‐band 11.21) locus that harbors the PTPN2 gene has been linked to multiple autoimmune disorders by GWAS. However, the causal variant(s) and the effector gene(s) at this locus have not yet been definitively elucidated. To identify the putative causal variant, we first obtained summary statistics from one of the largest GWAS meta‐analyses in IBD [6], in which the PTPN2 gene locus showed a strong association (Figure 1A) and performed a conditional analysis, conditioning on the lead SNP rs80262450 in that region. This analysis suggested that rs80262450 is also the probable, and only, causal variant in this locus, as no other variant in the associated haplotype block remained statistically significant. rs80262450 is located inside the PTPN2 intron (Chr18:12,818,923), 290 base‐pairs downstream of the canonical splice‐donor site, directly after the PTPN2.4 isoform‐specific exon 6a. Notably, the intron that harbors the risk allele A demonstrates a greater excision ratio in various tissues in GTEx [9], compared with the nonrisk allele‐containing intron (data for whole blood are shown in Figure 1B,C, normalized effect size [NES] = 0.59, p = 2.5 × 10−15; additional tissues are shown in Figure S1). Multiple SNPs in strong LD with rs80262450 show statistically significant association with the splicing phenotype, also known as splicing quantitative locus or sQTL. A similar conditional analysis using summary statistics data from the GTEx catalog indicated that the A allele of rs80262450 is the probable functional allele that mediates the preferential excision of the intron. Combined, these observations suggested that the risk allele increases disease risk by favoring splicing of the PTPN2.4 isoform from the pre‐mRNA. Finally, colocalization analysis between the GTEx sQTL GWAS summary statistics and the IBD GWAS data demonstrated a strong shared effect for spicing and IBD‐risk (posterior probability of colocalization 99% in whole blood), suggesting that autoimmune disease risk is likely driven by PTPN2 isoforms that contain exon 6a.

PTPN2 Isoform 4 Is Expressed in Human

We next tested whether this novel exon 6a is present in PTPN2 transcripts in humans. RT‐PCR was carried out to specifically amplify the DNA segment covering exon 6a in human peripheral blood, spleen, and primary immune cells, with the primer pairs shown in Table S1. Sanger sequencing confirmed the exon 6a product in human blood samples, and in various immune cell types, including primary monocytes, human monocyte‐derived macrophages (MDMs), and monocyte‐derived dendritic cells (moDCs) (Figure S2A,B). Thus, the human PTPN2 gene is alternatively spliced to generate PTPN2.4, which is expressed in human tissues.

The rs80262450 Variant Leads to Increased Expression of the PTPN2.4 Isoform

To test whether rs80262450 enhances expression of the PTPN2 isoform 4, we performed CRISPR‐Cas9 homology‐directed repair (HDR)‐mediated intronic SNP knock‐in to generate mutant, human monocytic lines U937. We achieved efficient HDR editing, with approximately 54% of cells exhibiting indels. We then randomly selected clones for sequencing, harboring correct insertion of the nonrisk (i.e., GG) and risk allele genotypes (GA and AA) for analysis. Subsequently, we assessed expression of PTPN2.4 by qRT‐PCR and demonstrated a trend toward higher PTPN2.4 expression in the AA haplotype lines when compared with the GG lines. An additional analysis using a dominant genetic model, in which GA and AA clones were combined and compared with GG clones [10, 11, 12]. demonstrated a significant difference between GA+AA and GG clones (p < 0.05) (Figure 1D–G). These results, consistent with data from human tissue samples in the GTEx project, indicate that the A allele of rs80262450 increases preferential splicing of the PTPN2.4 isoform.

Nuclear Export Signal Sequence in PTPN2.4 Leads to Its Distinct Cytosolic Localization

The PTPN2 isoform 4 protein contains predicted catalytic, coiled coil, and nuclear localization signal (NLS) domains and an uncharacterized region encoded by exon 6a (Figure S2C). [13]. Using the bioinformatic tool LocNES, we identified a nuclear export signal (NES) sequence within the exon 6a region with a high probability score (Figure S3), which prompted us to experimentally investigate the subcellular localization and distribution of this protein isoform. We generated PTPN2.2 or PTPN2.4 overexpressing lines on a PTPN2 null background, where all isoforms were completely inactivated (Figure S4A–D). In the parental cell line, we did not observe distinct bands for the two isoforms by western blot, and overexpression of PTPN2.2 and PTPN2.4 showed no difference in electrophoretic mobility. We used isoform‐specific constructs and functional assays to determine effects specific to PTPN2.4. Confocal microscopy imaging and biochemical assays of subcellular localization demonstrated distinct localization patterns for PTPN2.2 and PTPN2.4. Interestingly, PTPN2.4 was found to be excluded from the nucleus, while PTPN2.2 was detected in both the nucleus and cytoplasm (Figure 2A,B; Figure S4A–D). To validate whether the cytosolic localization of PTPN2.4 is determined by NES, we mutated the bulky hydrophobic amino acids present in the NES motif into alanine. These mutated NES were then incorporated into plasmids and transduced into THP‐1 cells. Examination of the localization of these NES mutants suggested that the PTPN2.4 NES was responsible for its unique subcellular localization (Figure 2C,D).

Unique and Shared Interacting Proteins of the PTPN2.4 and PTPN2.2 Isoforms

To determine whether the NES motif in PTPN2.4 alters its protein interaction patterns, which may result in diverse substrate selectivity and functions, we conducted proteomic analysis to identify PTPN2.4‐specific binding partners. Protein complexes were immunoprecipitated with a pan‐specific PTPN2 antibody and analyzed by mass spectrometry. Immunoprecipitated proteins were then compiled and sorted to categorize isoform‐specific interacting proteins. (Figure 3). Consistent with the distinct subcellular localization, we identified shared binders, such as STAT1, that were precipitated with both isoforms; while (Figure 2) the majority of PTPN2.4 binders were cytosolic proteins, nuclear proteins were found to be enriched in immunocomplexes with PTPN2.2. Next, we analyzed the biological processes that these interacting proteins are involved in using WebGestalt. Several PTPN2.4 interacting proteins were found to be involved in neutrophil‐mediated immunity, exocytosis, regulation of binding, granulocyte activation, and localized cytoplasmic, suggesting involvement of PTPN2.4 in these processes.

Differential Effects of PTPN2 Isoform 4 in Regulating Immune Responses

PTPN2 is a negative regulator of several cytokine signaling pathways and T cell receptor (TCR) signaling and therefore acts as a crucial checkpoint of inflammation [14]. To determine the role of PTPN2.4 in the regulation of inflammation, we used cell lines overexpressing PTPN2 isoforms. We first performed the Clariom D microarray assay to identify differentially expressed genes in response to lipopolysaccharide (LPS) stimulation. Pathway enrichment analysis revealed upregulation of genes involved in TNF‐α signal pathway (TNF, ICAM1, IL1B, and IL6) in cell lines overexpressing PTPN2.4 compared with cell lines overexpressing PTPN2.2 (Figure 4A–C). To further validate our findings, we showed that overexpression of PTPN2.4 in either the parental cell line or a PTPN2 knockout background led to a significant increase in TNF‐α production. In contrast, overexpression of PTPN2.2 resulted in decreased TNF‐α levels. Overexpression of isoforms 1 or 3 did not affect inflammatory cytokines such as TNF‐ α (Figure S4E; Figure 4D). Furthermore, we observed that PTPN2.4 enhanced the levels of ICAM‐1 and p‐STAT5 (Figure 4D), while having no significant effect on p‐STAT1 or p‐STAT3 (Figure S4F,G). The overexpression of both isoforms led to decreased levels of p‐STAT1, indicating that the enzymatic activity of PTPN2.4 was preserved (Figure S4F). NES motif mutations abolished the function of PTPN2.4 in regulating TNF‐α (Figure 4E). To confirm these findings in primary cells, we performed ribonucleoprotein (RNP)‐mediated CRISPR‐Cas9 KO of PTPN2.4 in human monocyte‐derived macrophages (MDMs) and monocytic cells. Efficient KO was validated by ICE analysis, confirming INDEL formation at the exon 6a. There was a significant reduction in the production of proinflammatory cytokines TNF‐α and IL‐6 (Figure 4F; Figure S4H–J1). Taken together, these results suggest a distinct role of PTPN2.4 in regulating inflammatory responses.

Previous reports have suggested that the dendritic cell (DC)‐intrinsic activity of PTPN2 explains its central role in maintaining immune tolerance within the immune system [15]. To investigate the impact of PTPN2.4 in dendritic cell‐mediated T cell activation and immune tolerance disruption, we generated PTPN2.4‐deficient moDCs. Following maturation in the presence of LPS, these cells were co‐cultured with allogeneic carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE)‐labeled pan T cells. Silencing of PTPN2.4 in moDCs did not affect T cell proliferation or cytokine production in the mixed lymphocyte reaction (MLR) assay. However, silencing the positive control gene CD86 [16] significantly reduced T cell proliferation and activation. Expression levels of HLA‐DR, CD83, CD80, and CD86 were not altered in PTPN2.4‐deficient moDCs (Figure 5A–D; Figure S5A–F). Additionally, previous studies showed that PTPN2 deficiency leads to augmented T cell expansion and survival capacity of activated T cells [17] and enhancement of T cell signaling [18]. We utilized CRISPR RNP to knock out PTPN2.4 in T cells and co‐cultured them with moDC cells. PTPN2.4 silencing in T cells did not affect T cell proliferation or T cell cytokine production, while knocking down the positive control PD‐1 led to a significant increase in immune tolerance (Figure 5E,F; Figure S5G).

PTPN2 Isoform 4 Is Upregulated in CD Patients

The single‐nucleotide variant rs80262450 appears to be strongly associated with IBD disease risk through favoring splicing of the PTPN2.4 isoform from the pre‐mRNA. Using qRT‐PCR, we observed a significant upregulation of PTPN2 Isoform 4 expression in human PBMC following LPS stimulation (Figure 6A). To better understand PTPN2.4 expression in disease tissues, we performed RNA‐seq and transcriptomic analyses on fresh clinical resection samples from patients with Crohn's disease. We found a significantly increased expression of PTPN2.4 (ENST00000591115.5) in inflamed human colon tissue samples compared with noninflamed counterparts (Figure 6B,C; Figure S6), with isoform 4 exhibiting a greater fold change compared with isoform 2 (ENST00000327283.7) or PTPN2 gene expression (ENSG00000175354) (Figure 6D,E). Our data suggest that both PTPN2.2 and PTPN2.4 are consistently detected in the colon, supporting the biological relevance of both isoforms in disease tissue. Taken together, our results indicated that increased expression of isoform 4 of the PTPN2 in CD patients likely contributes to gut inflammation.

Discussion

PTPN2 phosphatase plays a critical role in the regulation of immune responses. Inhibition of the PTPN2 catalytic activity with a small‐molecule compound leads to increased inflammation and antitumor immune response [19]. Furthermore, GWAS has linked genetic variation at the PTPN2 gene locus with numerous autoimmune diseases, including IBD. Several rare, putative loss‐of‐function variants in PTPN2 were found in families affected with very early‐onset IBD [20, 21] and primary immunodeficiency [22]. Previous independent studies identified multiple PTPN2 locus variants, including rs1893217, rs2292758 [23], rs2542151, and rs7234029 [24] in IBD and other autoimmune disorders. For example, rs1893217 was found to be associated with IBD. It mechanistically leads to reduced PTPN2 phosphatase activity without altering overall PTPN2 transcript or protein levels and was functionally validated in patient‐derived immune and epithelial cells and in vitro systems [25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30]. These findings suggest that PTPN2 is not only critical for immune regulation but also an important determinant of genetic predisposition to both common complex and rare inherited autoimmune diseases. However, the association signals at PTPN2 have not yet been clearly delineated due to the well‐recognized challenges inherent in GWAS, such as the presence of noncoding disease‐associated variants in high linkage disequilibrium and a lack of causal coding variants. The risk allele of the IBD‐associated lead SNP rs80262450 was predicted as an expression quantitative locus (eQTL) for PTPN2, and the two neighboring long noncoding RNA genes, RP11‐973H7.1 and LINC01882, in cultured fibroblast cells (p = 1.6 × 10−8) in the GTEx database. But it was also found to be a stronger sQTL (p = 2.5 × 10−15) regulating excision of the intron downstream to exon 6a, specific to the PTPN2 isoform 4, from pre‐mRNA in more than 10 different tissue types. Most importantly, the lead SNPs for the IBD GWAS signal and the sQTL GWAS signal, rs80262450, colocalize with one another with high probability, suggesting that the disease risk is likely affected by aberrant splicing of the gene products. PTPN2 possesses different splicing isoforms, among which the well‐studied isoform PTPN2.2 has been demonstrated as a physiological suppressor of immune responses. However, the functions of other isoforms mostly remain unknown.

Analysis of sQTL data from tissue samples from the GTEx database suggested a dose‐dependent effect of the rs80262450 variant on the expression of PTPN2.4. More specifically, the A allele was associated with a higher excision rate of the intronic sequence directly downstream of exon 6a, suggesting that it enhances the expression of the PTPN2.4 isoform. In CRISPR‐engineered cell lines expressing GG, GA, and AA genotypes, the disease‐associated allele A was associated with increased levels of PTPN2.4 (Figure 1), which links the disease‐associated intronic variant at PTPN2 to the expression of PTPN2.4. Although the exact mechanism of this regulation is unclear, we propose that rs80262450 alters a deep intronic splicing silencer or enhancer element, which regulates the alternative splicing of exon 6a by binding to various splicing regulatory proteins. However, such elements and, in particular, the impact that single‐nucleotide changes have on these elements, have proven difficult to define and measure. A few examples of disease‐associated common SNPs that impact splicing regulatory regions exist in the literature [31]. Given that PTPN2 plays a protective role in inflammation, we further hypothesized that aberrant splicing of PTPN2 may result in the generation of different functional isoforms and shifting the balance of these isoforms, or the abundance of individual isoforms, determines the outcome of immunological response and consequently the risk for disease development. Genetic variants that affect splicing play a significant role in complex disease risk, comparable in impact to variants influencing gene expression levels, also known as eQTLs [32]. Here, we characterize an underlying molecular mechanism of a splicing‐driven disease risk at one of the pleiotropic GWAS loci, whereby a disease‐associated splicing regulatory SNP enhances the expression of a rarer isoform with an activity opposite to its canonical form, resulting in an increase in inflammatory responses. Since the previously published rs1893217 and rs80262450 are in strong linkage disequilibrium, it is difficult to fully distinguish their individual biological effects, and we cannot exclude that both contribute independently to disease pathogenesis.

PTPN2.4 is generated by alternative splicing of PTPN2 pre‐mRNA, and in contrast to the canonical isoform PTPN2.2, carries an additional exon, exon 6a, encoding a 23 amino acid long structural motif with unknown function. To rule out its potential effect on the catalytic activity of PTPN2.4 isoform, we measured levels of the phosphorylated STAT1 protein (p‐STAT1), a key PTPN2 substrate. Overexpression of either PTPN2.2 or PTPN2.4 resulted in a comparable decrease in p‐STAT1 levels, suggesting that NES, the protein domain encoded by exon 6a, does not interfere with the catalytic activity of PTPN2.4 (Figure S4D).

An important mechanism that has been involved in the isoform‐specific functions of PTPN2 is the difference in cellular localization. It was shown that the PTPN2.1 variant resides in the endoplasmic reticulum due to a hydrophobic C‐terminus that conceals the nuclear localization sequence. While both PTPN2.2 and PTPN2.4 isoforms have bipartite nuclear localization signals (residues 350–358 and 377–381 in PTPN2.2, residues 373–381 and 400–404 in PTPN2.4) only PTPN2.4 has a predicted nuclear export signal, or NES, within exon 6a. And consistent with the previous findings, isoform 2 was detected in the nuclei, while isoform 4 was preferentially retained in the cytosol, indicating that the export signal played a critical role in PTPN2.4 subcellular localization (Figure 2).

Differential distribution of phosphatases may differentially affect the repertoire of binding substrates and signaling pathways, which may result in differential functional activities [33]. Using mass‐spectrometry analysis of immunoprecipitated complexes from cell lines overexpressing isoforms 2 or 4, we validated PTPN2‐associated proteins (e.g., STAT1, INSR, XPO1) and identified differential protein binding profiles for PTPN2.2 and 4. The absence of other known substrates likely reflects technical limitations inherent to co‐IP MS–based interactome profiling rather than the true absence of interaction. These include transient and low‐affinity interactions, limited peptide detectability, and differences in protein abundance or accessibility in our experimental system. In addition, overexpression of individual PTPN2 isoforms in a knockout background may not fully recapitulate endogenous expression levels or subcellular stoichiometry, which can further affect capture efficiency. While the cytosolic protein STAT1 was coprecipitated with both isoforms, nuclear proteins showed differential binding and were enriched in immunocomplexes with PTPN2.2. Although we cannot exclude that these two isoforms may have different substrate specificities, a differential substrate binding profile may suggest differential functional activities for the two isoforms (Figure 3). Previous studies have shown that the catalytic activity of the PTPN2 isoforms (PTPN2.1 and PTPN2.2) without distinction is increased by stimuli such as IFNγ [34], adhesion [35], and VEGFR, demonstrating its engagement in inflammation. To understand the contribution of individual isoforms to inflammatory response, we used a gain‐of‐function approach and evaluated gene expression profiles in myeloid cell lines overexpressing either PTPN2.4 or PTPN2.2 upon stimulation with LPS. Notably, overexpression of the PTPN2.4 isoform was associated with increased activity of the TNF pathway (IL6, IL1B, ICAM1, and TNF) compared with PTPN2.2 overexpression under inflammatory conditions, which suggests a pro‐inflammatory role of the PTPN2.4 isoform. Conversely, inactivation of PTPN2.4 resulted in decreased TNF‐α production in MDMs and myeloid lines, confirming the opposing role of PTPN2.4 in regulation of inflammatory responses in these cells (Figure 4). Previous studies have shown that the PTPN2 rs1893217 SNP or PTPN2 knockdown reduces TNFα secretion following MDP stimulation [25]. Our findings complement these observations, demonstrating that PTPN2 isoform‐4 specifically regulates TNFα production in response to LPS, revealing that both subcellular localization and isoform identity contribute to the regulation of inflammatory responses. Previous reports established an important role for PTPN2 in inflammatory responses; however, the contribution of individual PTPN2 isoforms was not specifically examined, making it difficult to assess isoform‐specific mechanisms, including potential contributions from alternative splicing or selective loss of PTPN2 isoform‐4. In addition, PTPN2 is widely recognized as a negative regulator of inflammation, and several studies report increased TNFα production following global PTPN2 loss. Differences in experimental context, including the nature of the stimulus (MDP versus LPS) and the lack of isoform‐level resolution in prior work, may contribute to the apparent differences between studies. In this context, our findings highlight the role of alternative splicing and the function of individual isoforms. Despite the evidence of differential regulation of TNF‐α production by PTPN2 isoforms, the precise mechanism remains unknown. Thie et al. demonstrated that PTPN2 may positively regulate LPS response in Raw264.7 cells by targeting inhibitory Tyr527 phosphorylation of Src to enhance the activating phosphorylation of Tyr416 residue [36]. Although our data could not reveal differential binding of PTPN2 isoforms to Src, one limitation of this study is that mice lack PTPN2.4, which makes murine models irrelevant for understanding its function. Given a very well‐established role of proinflammatory mediators in the regulation of immune tolerance, we tested how PTPN2 isoforms may affect DC cell functions. Since we did not observe any differential effect of isoforms on DC cells mediated T cell activation, our data also suggest that the function of PTPN2 isoforms may be context dependent.

To demonstrate the clinical relevance of our hypothesis, we assessed the expression of PTPN2 isoforms in samples from patients with IBD and observed elevated levels of the PTPN2 gene (all isoforms) in the mucosa and intestinal wall in CD patients compared with healthy individuals. When we examined isoform‐specific expression patterns in the inflamed mucosa, we observed that both isoforms were upregulated, although the upregulation of PTPN2.4 was significantly higher compared with PTPN2.2 (Figure 6; Figure S6). In IBD patients, PTPN2.2 and PTPN2.4 are both upregulated, suggesting that levels of PTPN2.2 might not be sufficient to alleviate the disease. This points to a pathogenic role for PTPN2.4 that outweighs the protective role of PTPN2.2 in disease contexts. Our results indicated that the dysregulation of isoform expression—particularly the increased level of the pathogenic isoform 4— acts as a novel driver of intestinal inflammation, rather than a nonisoform‐specific compensatory negative‐feedback response to inflammation. Although the precise mechanism that leads to PTPN2.4 generation in disease settings is elusive and could involve genetic variants or inflammation‐mediated alternative splicing, these results collectively indicate that PTPN2.4 has a nonredundant role in augmenting sensitivity to inflammation and disease susceptibility and represents a novel therapeutic target.

Although IBD has been linked to the common variant rs80262450 in PTPN2, which causes elevated expression of the alternate splice variant, PTPN2.4, further investigations are necessary to elucidate the underlying mechanisms by which rs80262450 regulates the alternative splicing of PTPN2.4. Additionally, gaining a deeper understanding of the inflammatory conditions that give rise to PTPN2.4 generation and how PTPN2.4 regulates the inflammation will be a subject of future research. Our data, together with evidence of increased PTPN2 signaling in a large fraction of IBD patients, suggest that PTPN2.4 is an upstream driver of IBD and a pathogenic isoform. The finding that inactivation of PTPN2.4 attenuated production of proinflammatory cytokines offers the opportunity for a therapeutic blockade of PTPN2.4 in inflammatory disease. Consequently, targeted therapies specifically aimed at blocking PTPN2.4 may hold promise for effectively treating IBD patients exhibiting elevated levels of this isoform.

Materials and Methods

Conditional and Colocalization Analyses of GWAS Data

Approximate conditional analysis was performed using the COJO method [37] implemented in the GCTA software tool (v1.93.2) [38]. For this analysis, we used the IBD summary statistics from the de Lange et al., GWAS, downloaded from the GWAS catalog (GCST004132), and the EUR genotype data from the 1000 Genomes Project [39]. The regional plots were made using the topr R package (v2.02) [40]. Variant correlation values (r2) were estimated from high‐coverage whole‐genome sequences of over 13,000 individuals of European ancestry [41, 42]. Colocalization analysis was performed using the coloc R package (v5.2.3) using the Bayesian methods described previously [43].

Monocytic Line Cell Culture, Differentiation, Stimulation, and PTPN2 Constructs and Transfection

The THP‐1, U937, and HEK293T cell lines were obtained from the ATCC (Catalog# TIB‐202, CRL‐1593.2, and CRL‐3216). HEK293T cells were maintained in DMEM (Catalog# 10‐017‐CV, Corning) supplemented with 10% FBS (Catalog# 10082–147, Gibco), 5 mM L‐glutamine (Catalog# 25030–081, Gibco), and 1% penicillin streptomycin (Catalog# 15140‐122, GibcoTHP‐1 and U937 cells were from ATCC and maintained in RPMI 1640 (Catalog# A10491‐01, Gibco) supplemented with 10% heat‐inactivated FBS (Catalog# 10082–147, Gibco, USA), 5 mM L‐glutamine (Catalog# 25030–081, Gibco), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Catalog# 15140‐122, Gibco). Cells were cultured in an incubator at 37°C in 5% CO2. THP‐1 or U937 cells were differentiated by 5 ng/mL PMA (Catalog# tlrl‐pma, Invitrogen, USA) stimulation for 2 days, washed, and rested for 2 days without PMA, and treated with 100 ng/mL of LPS‐EB Ultrapure (Catalog# tlrl‐3pelps, InvivoGen, USA) for 24 h. PTPN2 isoforms 2 (NM_080422.2), or isoform 4 (NM_001207013.1), or NES mutants (LWITLYLKLLML → LWITLYAKAAAA, LWITLYLKLLML → AWITAYAKAAAA) were subcloned into the LV165 with EF1a promoter, IRES2‐eGFP or LV225 vector or LV117 vector (from GeneCopoeia, USA). Insert and vector were ligated, and product identity was confirmed by sequencing.

CRISPR HDR

CRISPR HDR: Alt‐R CRISPR HDR Design Tool from Integrated DNA Technology (IDT) was used to design a CRISPR RNA (crRNA) located in proximity to the SNP variant. The sequence of this crRNA (TGCACCTCTATGCCCAGATA) was submitted for synthesis and used with a transactivating crRNA (tracrRNA) and a single‐strand oligonucleotide (Ultramer, IDT: 5′‐ CAGGAGTTCGAGACCAACCTGACCAACATAGAGAAACCCCGTCTCTACTAAAAATACAAAGGTTATCTGGGCATAGAGGTGCATGCCTGTAATCCCAGCTACTTGG‐3′) serving as a homology‐directed repair template carrying the SNP variant (includes the SNP and a second mutation to disrupt the PAM motif).

Ribonucleoprotein Assembly and Electroporation

Ribonucleoprotein (RNP) complex, including (crRNA/tracrRNA/Cas9) and ssODN (50 ng/µL), was electroporated into the monocytic lines U937 using Lonza SF Cell Line 96‐well Nucleofector Kit (Catalog# V4SC‐2096, Lonza, USA) and optimized program FS‐100 in an Amaxa 96‐well Shuttle system (Catalog# AAF‐1002B, Lonza). Each nucleofection reaction consisted of 0.5–1 × 106 cells and mixed with RNP. After electroporation, warm growth media were added to the well to transfer the cells to tissue culture plates. Cells were then collected in complete medium and plated into 96‐well plates for single‐cell cloning. After 2–3 weeks, clones were isolated and verified for genotyping, which is performed by PCR and Sanger sequencing. The CRISPR‐induced insertions and deletions (indels) were analyzed by the ICE (Inference of CRISPR Edits) tool, developed by Synthego.

Genomic DNA Extraction, PCR, and Sanger Sequencing

gDNA was extracted using QuickExtract DNA Extraction Solution (Catalog# SS000035‐D2, Biosearch Technologies). Primers (Azenta) for human DNA sequencing were used at 10 µM (primer sequences are listed in Table S1). PCR amplification was carried out using Platinum PCR SuperMix High Fidelity (Catalog# 12532‐016, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and under the conditions recommended by the manufacturer. PCR was performed using the C1000 Touch Thermal Cycler (Catalog# 1851196, Bio‐Rad). PCR amplicons were electrophoresed and sent to Sanger sequencing (Azenta).

Reverse Transcription Quantitative Real‐Time PCR and RT‐PCR

RNAs were collected using RNeasy Plus Kits (Catalog# 74134, Qiagen) following the manufacturer's instructions. For both cell line and human patient sample experiments, cDNA was synthesized from RNA using the SuperScript IV VILO Master Mix (Catalog# 11756050, Thermo Fisher Scientific) following the manufacturer's protocol. Real‐time PCR was performed using the CFX96 Touch Real‐Time PCR qPCR system (Catalog# 1845097, Bio‐Rad) and the PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (Catalog# A25741, Applied Biosystems, USA). Data are presented as relative levels using the 2ΔCT method with GAPDH serving as the reference gene (Hs.PT.39a.22214836, IDT). Primer sequences used in the RT‐qPCR experiments are listed in Table S1. RT‐PCR was performed using SuperScript IV One‐Step RT‐PCR System and C1000 Touch Thermal Cycler. PCR amplicons were electrophoresed and sent for sequencing.

Lentivirus Packaging and Transduction

Lentiviral particles encoding PTPN2 or GFP were produced by co‐transfecting HEK293T cells with lentiviral vector and packaging plasmids (psPAX2, pMD2.G) using FuGENE. Viral supernatants were filtered, concentrated via sucrose gradient ultracentrifugation (107,000g, 2 h, 4°C), resuspended in PBS/1%BSA, and stored at −80°C. THP‐1 cells were transduced with the lentivirus, and then selected by antibiotics or cell sorting (BD Aria) 72 h posttransduction. GFP‐positive cells were single‐cell cloned in 96‐well plates, and clones were verified by flow cytometry (BD LSRFortessa).

Immunofluorescence and Confocal Microscopy

Cells were seeded in glass‐bottom chambers, stimulated with PMA for 15 min, rinsed with PBS, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min. After three PBS washes, cells were blocked (3% BSA, 0.1% Triton X‐100) for 30 min, then incubated overnight at 4°C with rabbit anti‐PTPN2 antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, 58935) in PBS/3% BSA. After three PBS washes, samples were incubated 1 h at room temperature (dark) with Goat anti‐Rabbit IgG Alexa Fluor Plus 555 (Invitrogen, A‐21428) in PBS/3% BSA. DAPI (Invitrogen, D1306) was added before imaging with a Zeiss LSM880 confocal microscope.

Immunochemistry and Western Blotting

Cytoplasmic proteins and nuclear extraction were prepared from 1–10 million THP‐1 cells using the Subcellular Protein Fractionation Kit and centrifuged. Protein lysates were collected and resolved on 4%–12% gradient gels (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The gels were then transferred by the iBind Western device with iBind Cards and the iBind Solution Kit according to the manufacturer's instructions (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Membrane was probed with the relevant primary rabbit anti‐PTPN2 antibody (Catalog# 58935, Cell Signaling Technology) and with secondary anti‐rabbit IgG, HRP‐linked Antibody (Catalog# 7074S, Cell Signaling Technology), the β‐Actin (13E5) Rabbit mAb (HRP Conjugate) (Catalog# 5125, Cell Signaling Technology) was used at 1:1000. Membranes were developed with SuperSignal West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate (Catalog# 34580, Thermo Scientific).

Coimmunoprecipitation

Cells were collected and frozen in −80 freezer until use. The coimmunoprecipitation (Co‐IP) experiment was performed using a Pierce MS‐Compatible Magnetic IP Kit (Catalog# 90409, Thermo Scientific), following the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, the cells were lysed with ice‐cold IP‐MS cell lysis buffer provided with the kit. After incubating for 10 min on ice, the cell lysate samples were centrifuged at 14,000g for 10 min to remove cell debris. The cell lysates were combined with PTPN2 antibody (Catalog# 58935, Cell Signaling Technology) and incubated overnight at 4°C. Following the antibody incubation, the samples were combined with Pierce protein A/G magnetic beads and washed according to the manufacturer's guidelines. The proteins bound to the beads were eluted and subjected to bottom‐up proteomics sample preparations and LC‐MS/MS analysis.

LC‐MS/MS Analysis and Data Interpretation

The samples were analyzed with a ThermoFisher Scientific Easy nLC 1200 and a Thermo Fisher Scientific Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer. Peptides were eluted with a linear gradient from 6% ACN to 35% ACN over 106 min, followed by 14 min of washing the column at 90% ACN. The Lumos mass spectrometer was operated in DDA mode, with an MS1 scan range from 300–800 m/z at 120,000 resolving power. Peptides from charge state 2–7 were considered for fragmentation, and a dynamic exclusion set to 25 s. Peptides were selected with a 0.8 m/z isolation window and an HCD normalized collision energy of 34%. Tandem mass spectra were analyzed in the Orbitrap at 50,000 resolving power, a 100,000 AGC target, and 120 ms maximum injection time. Data were analyzed using Proteome Discoverer (version 2.2, ThermoFisher). A fold change value was calculated for each protein identified.

MSD Assay

Cells were plated in 96‐well plates. At the end of the assays, supernatant and whole cell lysate were collected, and cytokine concentration was measured using the U‐PLEX TH1/TH2 Combo (human) kit (IFN‐γ, IL‐1β, IL‐2, IL‐4, IL‐5, IL‐8, IL‐10, IL‐12p70, IL‐13, and TNF‐α) (Catalog# K15010K‐4, Meso Scale Discovery). The MSD assay was performed on the Meso Sector S 600 MM instrument (Catalog# IC1AA‐0, Meso Scale Discovery), and samples were analyzed using the XLfit software (IDBS, UK).

Clariom D Human Assay Microarrays and Analysis

Gene array was conducted using Clariom D Assay (ThermoFisher) with 100 ng RNA added to the target preparation protocol, which followed the manufacturer's instruction through completion of Array Hybridization (16 h). After hybridization, the GeneChip arrays were washed and stained using the Affymetrix GeneChip Fluidics Station 450. Data were collected on the GeneChip Scanner 3000 7G Instrument and then analyzed using the Transcriptome Analysis Console (TAC version 4.0.1) for technical quality control (QC) metrics. Affymetrix CEL files were SCAN‐normalized using the SCAN.UPC package [44, 45] and the custom ClariomD ENSG cdf definitions provided by BrainArray [46] (University of Michigan) using the R coding language. The ENSG gene annotation was obtained using the biomaRt package in R [47, 48]. Differential genes between comparisons were identified under |log2FC|>log2(1.5) and FDR < 0.05 by the limma R package.

Preparation of Human Peripheral Blood Mononuclear Cells (PBMC) and Monocytes

Human Whole Blood & PBMCs (Research Blood Components, USA) was layered on Ficoll‐Paque PLUS (Catalog# 17144003, Cytiva), and human PBMC was separated from plasma and red blood cells through gradient centrifugation. The buffy coat was collected and was treated with eBioscience 1X RBC Lysis Buffer (Catalog# 00‐4333‐57, Invitrogen) twice for 5 min to lyse remaining red blood cells. Isolated human PBMC were frozen in BAMBANKER Serum‐Free Cell Freezing Medium (Catalog# BB01, Bulldog Bio).

Differentiation of Macrophages and Dendritic Cells

Frozen PBMCs were thawed and stained with StraightFrom Whole Blood CD14 MicroBeads human (Catalog# 130‐090‐879, Miltenyi Biotec), sorted with autoMACS Pro Separator (Catalog# 130‐092‐545, Miltenyi Biotec), and cultured in complete RPMI 1640 medium (Catalog# A10491‐01, Gibco) with penicillin streptomycin, 2 mM GlutaMAX (Catalog# 35050–061, Gibco), 1 mM Sodium Pyruvate (Catalog# 11306‐070, Gibco), 0.1 mM MEM NEAA (Catalog# 11140‐050, Gibco), 25 mM HEPES (Catalog# 15630‐080, Gibco), 55 mM β‐mercaptoethanol (Catalog# 21985‐023, Gibco), and 10% FBS (Catalog# 10082–147, Gibco) at 37°C, 5% CO2. Monocytes were differentiated to moDCs with 20 ng/mL IL‐4 (Catalog# 200‐04‐50UG, PeproTech) and 50 ng/mL GM‐CSF (Catalog# 300‐03‐250UG, PeproTech), or to macrophages with 50 ng/mL M‐CSF (Catalog# 300‐25‐250UG, PeproTech). CRISPR‐Cas9 editing was performed on day 3 (moDCs) or day 5 (MDMs). Fresh medium was added on day 6. moDCs were stimulated with 200 ng/mL LPS‐EB Ultrapure (Catalog# tlrl‐3pelps, InvivoGen) for 24 h on day 8; MDMs were stimulated with LPS on day 5 or 10. Supernatants were collected for cytokine analysis, and cells were detached with UltraPure 0.5 M EDTA, pH 8.0 (Invitrogen), for flow cytometry.

CRISPR RNP‐Based Genome Editing in Primary Immune Cells

sgRNAs targeting PTPN2, CD86, and PD‐L1 were designed using online tools, purchased, and assembled with Cas9; sequences are in Table S2. RNPs were electroporated into immune cells using optimized Lonza protocols (CM‐137 for moDCs/MDMs, EO‐115 for pan T cells). Each nucleofection used 0.5–1.5 × 106 cells in supplemented buffer with RNP and 10 µM Cas9 Electroporation Enhancer. After electroporation, cells were cultured in RPMI medium with differentiation cytokines for 4–6 days before LPS treatment.

T Cell Culture and CFSE Staining and MLR Assay

Frozen PBMC were thawed and stained with Pan T Cell Isolation Kit Human (Catalog# 130‐096‐535, Miltenyi Biotec). Cells were sorted using the autoMACS Pro Separator (Catalog# 130‐092‐545, Miltenyi Biotec) according to the manufacturer's instructions, and the negative fraction was collected. Isolated T cells were labelled with CellTrace CFSE Cell Proliferation Kit (Catalog# C34554, Invitrogen). moDCs and allogenic pan T cells were co‐cultured for 5–7 days in complete RPMI medium with 1% penicillin streptomycin, 2 mM GlutaMAX, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 0.1 mM MEM nonessential amino acids, 25 mM HEPES, 55 mM β‐mercaptoethanol, and 10% heat‐inactivated FBS at 37 °C in 5% CO2. After 5 days, the supernatant was collected to analyze secreted cytokine levels. The cells were blocked and stained with staining antibodies and the L/D dye.

Flow Cytometry

DAPI (Catalog# D1306, Invitrogen) or LIVE/DEAD Fixable Aqua Dead Cell Stain Kit (Catalog# L34957, Invitrogen) was used for detecting dead cells. Cells were first blocked with Human TruStain FcX (Fc Receptor Blocking Solution) (Catalog# 422302, BioLegend) using a 1:100 dilution and then stained for 30 min at 4°C with staining antibodies. moDCs were stained with FITC anti‐human CD83 Antibody (Catalog# 305306, Clone HB15e, BioLegend), PE anti‐human CD80 Recombinant Antibody (Catalog# 370612, Clone QA18A16, BioLegend), PE/Cyanine5 anti‐human CD86 Antibody (Catalog# 305408, Clone IT2.2, BioLegend), PE/Cyanine7 anti‐human HLA‐DR Antibody (Catalog# 307616, Clone L243, BioLegend), APC anti‐human CD274 (B7‐H1, PD‐L1) Antibody (Catalog# 329708, Clone 29E.2A3, BioLegend), APC/Fire 750 anti‐human CD209 (DC‐SIGN) Antibody (Catalog# 330116, Clone 9E9A8, BioLegend), Brilliant Violet 510 anti‐human CD40 Antibody (Catalog# 334330, Clone 5C3, BioLegend) using 1:200 dilution. PTPN2 Gene/Isoform RNA‐seq Data Analysis

Sample collection and RNA‐Seq analysis were previously described [49, 50, 51]. Quality control of raw reads was done using FastQC (Andrews S. (2010), which is available online at: http://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc). Reads were then aligned to the human reference sequence Ensembl GRCh38 release 110 using Kallisto [52]. Next, the derived pseudo counts were normalized into log2 transcripts per million (TPM), and the tximport R package [51] was used to summarize transcript‐level abundance estimates for gene‐level analysis. PTPN2 data were analyzed with one‐way ANOVA for multiple comparisons and an unpaired two‐tailed Student's t‐test when comparing involved/uninvolved to the Normal group. An additional paired t‐test was performed when comparing involved to uninvolved samples.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism. Differences were examined by one‐way ANOVA or two‐tailed Student's t‐test. The p‐values were indicated (∗ p < 0.05, ∗∗ p < 0.01, ∗∗∗ p < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗ p < 0.0001).
