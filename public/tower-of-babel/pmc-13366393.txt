Revision total elbow arthroplasty for aseptic and septic nonunion of distal humerus fractures: a case series
Authors: Alyssa A. Federico, Adina Tarcea, Sara Kayvani, Kevin A. Hildebrand, Neil J. White
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDistal humerus fractures can be difficult to manage in older patients with poor bone quality and those with risk factors for failure of open reduction internal fixation. When nonunion occurs, there are limited repeat fixation options and conversion to total elbow arthroplasty (TEA) can be considered. The objective of this descriptive, retrospective case series is to compare the outcomes of TEA for aseptic vs. septic nonunion of distal humerus fractures.MethodsSeven cases were identified by 2 orthopedic surgeons that specialize in TEA. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (a) adult patients (≥18 years of age), (b) underwent TEA after distal humerus open reduction internal fixation complicated by aseptic or septic nonunion, (c) had clinical follow-up data ≥1 year after TEA, and (d) completed patient-reported outcome surveys ≥1 year after TEA. Charts were reviewed to extract the relevant data, and outcomes between aseptic and septic nonunion groups compared using range of motion, grip strength, x-rays, and patient-reported outcome measures.ResultsAfter TEA, all average patient-reported outcome scores were better in the aseptic nonunion group compared to the septic nonunion group, indicating a more favorable recovery trajectory (Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand score: 39.5 ± 9.3 vs. 58.4 ± 21.9; visual analog pain scale: 3.1 ± 2.1 vs. 4.4 ± 2.4). In addition, range of motion was better in the aseptic nonunion group compared to the septic nonunion group for most parameters (flexion: 127.3 ± 17.3 vs. 106.7 ± 18.8; supination: 73.3 ± 20.5 vs. 58.3 ± 28.4; pronation: 80.5 ± 9.9 vs. 69.0 ± 21.5), except elbow extension (27.5 ± 43.7 vs. −2.3 ± 8.7). Two patients in the aseptic nonunion group developed post-operative complications (ulnar neuropathy and periprosthetic fracture with osteolysis) compared to one patient in the septic nonunion group (periprosthetic infection).ConclusionTEA is a viable treatment option for patients with aseptic and septic nonunion of distal humerus fractures, although patient outcomes in septic nonunion tend to be worse. Conversion to TEA is best done in 2 stages to ensure infection is identified and treated appropriately.

Distal humerus fractures occur in 7.71 per 100,000 people per year, mainly affecting young males involved in high energy trauma and older females with osteoporosis.34 These fractures can be difficult to manage in older patients due to osteoporosis, metaphyseal comminution, short end segments, and poor soft tissue quality.37 Nonoperative management is often favored in nondisplaced fractures, low demand patients, and patients with high surgical risk.24,37 Comminuted, intra-articular, and displaced fractures often benefit from open reduction internal fixation (ORIF), which has a 44% risk of complications.24 Mechanical complications are the most common, including malunion and nonunion.24,32

The most widely accepted definition of nonunion is a fracture that persists for nine months without evidence of healing for at least three months.4 Patients who suffer nonunion often require a revision surgery, which can be quite complex due to bone loss and limited fixation options.24,28 The management options are limited and invasive, including revision ORIF, elbow fusion, excision arthroplasty, elbow hemiarthroplasty, and total elbow arthroplasty (TEA). The risk of failure following repeat ORIF for humerus fractures is high, and patient-reported outcomes are poor following elbow fusion and excision arthroplasty.11,15,29,31,38

Studies evaluating TEA as a salvage procedure for distal humerus fracture nonunion have demonstrated improvements in both pain and function.2,19,27 While pain is a common presenting symptom in cases of nonunion, 74%-100% of patients undergoing salvage TEA report no or mild pain post-operatively.2,27 Range of motion and scores on patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) also improve, with 86%-90% of patients reporting good or excellent arm function after surgery.2,27 Although TEA is a viable option to manage complications after distal humerus fractures, these surgeries become even more complex in the setting of infection.28

Septic nonunion is when infection occurs in association with impaired bone healing, and the diagnostic criteria include presence of a fistula, purulent drainage, and 2 positive tissue cultures.9,23 The risk of septic nonunion is higher in patients with open fractures and in those who undergo initial surgical fixation compared with patients managed nonoperatively.39 It can be difficult to confirm the presence of infection, especially in low-grade infections without clinical symptoms.9,36 However, the distinction between aseptic and septic nonunion becomes extremely important when considering conversion to TEA.6,36

In the setting of septic nonunion, conversion to a TEA is often done in 2 stages to improve the chance of infection eradication.1,28 The first operation involves removal of all hardware, thorough irrigation and debridement, and placement of an antibiotic cement spacer. After resolution of infectious serology and symptoms, a second operation can be performed for TEA instrumentation.28 In the setting of suspected aseptic nonunion, a one-stage operation can be considered for removal of hardware and TEA under the same anesthetic. Although a single operation carries less risk than 2 separate operations, there remains a 28% chance of uncovering an indolent infection in cases of presumed aseptic nonunion.36

The inherent risk of infection in patients with nonunions who have had previous surgery raises the following question: is TEA a viable salvage option in cases of septic nonunion? Furthermore, should every distal humerus fracture nonunion conversion to TEA be performed in a two-stage manner? To answer these questions, we present a small descriptive case series of 7 patients (4 aseptic nonunion, 3 septic nonunion) who suffered this devastating complication and underwent conversion to TEA. We review these cases to compare the important clinical features that led to an end-stage operation, approaches to management in aseptic and septic nonunion, and overall patient outcomes.

Methods

Case identification

This retrospective descriptive case series was performed at 2 tertiary hospitals in Calgary, Alberta, Canada, and received institutional ethics approval (REB21-1851). A total of 11 cases of distal humerus nonunion were identified by 2 orthopedic surgeons that specialize in TEA (K.A.H., N.J.W.). The inclusion criteria were as follows: (a) adult patients (≥18 years of age), (b) underwent TEA after distal humerus ORIF complicated by aseptic or septic nonunion, (c) had clinical follow-up data ≥1 year after TEA, and (d) completed patient-reported outcome surveys ≥1 year after TEA. Four patients were subsequently excluded because they were managed nonoperatively for their distal humerus fracture or did not have the required follow-up data. Seven patients were included in the final analysis. Cases of septic nonunion were defined as those with one or more positive intraoperative cultures after the original distal humerus ORIF and prior to or during implantation of the TEA components, prompting the initiation of an antibiotic treatment plan.

Data extraction

Chart reviews were conducted for the 7 patients included in the study. Demographic and clinical data were extracted using a spreadsheet developed a priori. The data extracted included date of birth, sex, body mass index, past medical history, nicotine use, handedness, injury side, injury date, open or closed injury, fracture classification, associated injuries, pre- and post-operative neurovascular status, ORIF date, arthroplasty indication, infectious lab markers before TEA, one- or two-stage arthroplasty, TEA date, TEA implant system, antibiotic cement use, intraoperative bacterial culture results, details about any additional operations, date of most recent follow-up, clinical update, elbow range of motion parameters, and implant stability most recent x-ray. A one-stage operation was defined as hardware removal and TEA performed under the same anesthetic. Two-stage operations were defined as hardware removal under one anesthetic and TEA instrumentation under a second anesthetic. Intraoperative culture results were unavailable at the time of surgery due to the incubation period necessary to identify bacteria and therefore did not inform intraoperative decision-making. These results were reviewed postoperatively and used to inform subsequent treatment.

Outcome assessment

Outcomes after TEA were assessed using a combination of physical exam data, x-rays, and PROMs collected prospectively during routine follow-up. Physical exam data collected included bilateral arm flexion, extension, supination, pronation, and grip strength. Measurements from the injured and uninjured sides were compared for grip strength. The PROMs used were Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand (DASH), Patient-Rated Elbow Evaluation (PREE), Oxford Elbow Score, and Mayo Elbow Performance Score (MEPS). In addition, patients were to complete a visual analog pain scale.

The DASH questionnaire contains 30 items to score symptoms and level of disability from 0 to 100, with a higher score indicating more severe disability.10 The PREE is a 20-item questionnaire with pain and function subscales. The total score is graded from 0 to 100, with a higher score indicating more pain and disability.20 The Oxford Elbow Score contains 12 items that are grouped into elbow function, pain, and social–psychological domains. Each domain is scored out of 16 for a total score is out of 48, with a lower number indicating more impairment.13 Lastly, the MEPS rates patient function on a scale of 0 to 100 based on 4 variables: pain, arc of motion, stability, and daily function. A lower score indicates worse outcomes.5

All radiographs from final follow-up were reviewed by 2 orthopedic surgeons with expertise in TEA (K.A.H., N.J.W.). Implant loosening was assessed using a previously described 0-4 grading scale.14 The grades are defined as follows: type 0 is a <1 mm radiolucent line involving <50% of the bone–implant interface; type 1 is a 1 mm radiolucent line involving <50% of the interface; type 2 is a >1 mm radiolucent line involving >50% of the interface; type 3 is a >2 mm radiolucent line involving the whole interface; and type 4 is gross loosening.14

Statistical analysis

Summary statistics were used to synthetize patient demographics and outcomes data from all 7 patients. Means and standard deviations were calculated for continuous data including age, time from injury to ORIF, time from ORIF to TEA, time between 2 stages for TEA, number of operations per patient, time from injury to final follow-up, range of motion measures, percent grip strength, DASH scores, PREE scores, Oxford Elbow scores, MEPS Scores, and visual analog pain scale scores. Proportions were used to describe categorical data including sex, medical comorbidities, surgical complications, subsequent surgeries, nerve injuries, and implant loosening on final x-rays was calculated. The small sample size limited our ability to assess statistical significance between groups.

Results

Patient characteristics

The mean age at time of injury was 68.9 year old, and there was a strong female predominance (85.7% female, 14.3% male) (Table I). Six out of 7 patients (85.7%) had comorbidities that can predispose to nonunion, including smoking, obesity, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, hypertension, dyslipidemia, chronic obstructive pulmonary disorder, congestive heart failure, and malignancy history. All patients in the septic nonunion group had relevant medical comorbidities, compared to 3 out of 4 in the aseptic nonunion group. There was only 1 open fracture (14.3%) and 2 patients (28.6%) with concurrent injuries, including a distal radius fracture and radial head fracture. There were 4 cases of aseptic nonunion (57.1%) and 3 cases of septic nonunion (42.9%) (Table II). Two of the septic nonunion patients had positive cultures return from an irrigation and débridement procedure performed prior to the TEA. The third patient with septic nonunion was not suspected to have an infection; however, one of the intraoperative cultures taken during the TEA procedure returned positive for methicillin-resistant S. aureus. The average time from ORIF to TEA was 18.7 ± 16.1 months. Two-stage operations were performed in 5 patients (71.4%), and one-stage operations performed in 2 patients (28.6%), one from each of the aseptic and septic nonunion groups. Patient outcomes

The aseptic nonunion group demonstrated a large standard deviation in elbow extension, reflecting considerable variability across individual measurements (−10°, 0°, 33°, and 87°). The percent grip strength compared to the uninjured side was nearly equal in both groups (67.7% aseptic nonunion, 67.0% septic nonunion). Complications

Three out of 7 patients (42.9%) developed complications secondary to the TEA procedure, including ulnar neuropathy, implant osteolysis with periprosthetic fracture, and periprosthetic infection (Table II). Two out of 7 patients (28.6%) required subsequent surgical procedures to manage these complications. The patient with implant osteolysis and periprosthetic fracture underwent a single stage revision TEA with ORIF, as detailed in a recent case report.8 The periprosthetic infection developed in the patient who was not suspected to have a septic nonunion but had positive cultures from the TEA procedure. This patient required 3 revision surgeries including an irrigation and débridement and bearing change, excision of the TEA components and insertion of an antibiotic spacer, and revision TEA.

The overall rate of nerve injury throughout the clinical course was 85.7%. There were 2 cases of radial nerve injury and 4 cases of ulnar nerve injury. Three nerve injuries occurred after initial ORIF (50%), 1 after TEA (16.7%), and 2 after revision surgeries for complications related to the TEA (33.3%). Notably, both radial nerve palsies fully recovered, whereas all ulnar nerve palsies persisted, resulting in a 66.7% rate of nerve injury at final follow-up.

At final follow-up, there were 2 patients with evidence of implant osteolysis on x-ray (28.6%). One case demonstrated radiolucency graded as type 4 around the humeral component and type 1 around the ulnar component, while the other showed type 1 radiolucency around the humeral component and type 4 around the ulnar component (Fig. 2). Discussion

Overall, this small, descriptive case series suggests that TEA is a viable treatment option for both aseptic and septic nonunion of distal humerus fractures. However, septic nonunion is a more complicated problem associated with poorer patient reported outcomes. In addition, patients with aseptic nonunion have better elbow range of motion in nearly all planes except for extension. The aseptic nonunion group averaged 27.5 ± 43.7° of extension compared to −2.3 ± 8.7° in the septic nonunion group. The slight degree of hyperextension observed in the septic nonunion group may be a result of more aggressive bone resection and soft tissue débridement to ensure clear infection margins. It is important to temper patient expectations prior to performing these high risk and complicated procedures, especially in the setting of septic nonunion. The goal is to improve patient function compared to their pre-operative status, not to have them return to normal function.

There was substantial variation in the amount of time between ORIF and TEA (18.7 ± 16.1 months), which raises questions about whether there should be a lower threshold for performing TEA. Although each patient's clinical course is unique, providers may consider discussing TEA with patients who have multiple risk factors for nonunion when the first signs of failure present. Notably, 6 out of 7 patients (85.7%) in our case series had risk factors for nonunion including older age, smoking, obesity, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, hypertension, dyslipidemia, chronic obstructive pulmonary disorder, congestive heart failure, and malignancy history.33 TEA is typically considered an end-stage operation. However, if a patient's function is significantly impaired from a nonunion, early conversion to TEA could improve their quality of life and potentially decrease the total number of operations a patient receives. Interestingly, many studies support TEA as a first line treatment strategy for severely comminuted distal humerus fractures in older patients with poor bone quality.3,7,17, 18, 19,21,22,25,27,35 A recent systematic review comparing primary TEA in acute fractures and secondary TEA to manage nonunions showed that secondary TEA is associated with higher complication rates (odds ratio of 2.5) and worse functional outcomes.30

Another key consideration when converting a nonunion to TEA is whether to perform a one-stage or two-stage operation. Two-stage revision remains the recommended treatment strategy for all periprosthetic joint infections.12,16 Even in cases where infection is not suspected, there is a small risk that intraoperative cultures unexpectedly return positive.36 Infection after TEA can be devastating, resulting in poor patient outcomes and multiple revision surgeries, as demonstrated by one of the presented cases. By performing a two-stage operation, surgeons can identify indolent infection and treat with appropriate antibiotics prior to TEA implant insertion. In addition, the population most affected by distal humerus fractures is older individuals, many of whom have multiple medical comorbidities.34 Removing all the hardware and preparing the joint for TEA is already a large operation that can be difficult for patients and surgeons to withstand. Two-stage operations can also be beneficial for surgeons to avoid fatigue from a prolonged operation and allow for in-depth case review and planning prior to TEA.

TEA is known to have a higher complication rate than other total joint arthroplasties, with a cumulative complication rate of 60.7% reported in a recent systematic review.26 In our case series, 2 patients in the aseptic nonunion group developed post-operative complications, with one requiring a revision surgery. There was one patient in the septic nonunion group who had a post-operative complication related to indolent infection. They ultimately required 3 revision surgeries and had the lowest PROM scores out of all patients. These findings reaffirm the increased complexity of septic nonunion cases, and importance of diagnosing infection prior to implanting TEA components.

The rate of nerve injury in this case series was notably high at 85.7%, with 57.1% persisting at final follow-up. Nerve injuries occurred at different stages including after ORIF, TEA, and revision surgeries for complications. Importantly, only one nerve injury occurred after TEA, compared to higher rates after other types of operations. Reducing the number of subsequent operations may help lower the overall risk of nerve injury. To further protect against nerve injury, when performing the conversion to TEA, the authors prefer the lateral paraolecranon approach. The ulnar nerve is often transposed from previous operations, and all tissue can safely be peeled off the lateral window without need to dissect out the ulnar nerve.

A strength of this study is the division of patients into aseptic and septic distal humerus nonunion groups. The distinction between type of nonunion, along with 1 year follow-up data, provides important information about long-term recovery trajectories. In addition, our patient population was very representative of individuals at high risk of distal humerus fractures and nonunion, making the findings clinically relevant.34 A major limitation of this study is the small sample size, which is due to the rarity of cases. The small sample size limited our ability to perform statistical comparison between groups and draw definitive conclusions. However, this case series still provides important clinical insight into a devastating surgical complication and the range of management options. In addition, no PROMs were obtained pre-operatively, limiting our ability to determine if TEA improved function compared to pre-operative status. We plan to continue monitoring differences in recovery between aseptic and septic nonunion patients in the future, adding to the existing data.

Conclusion

TEA represents a viable treatment option for patients with both aseptic and septic nonunion of distal humerus fractures. However, patient outcomes in septic nonunion tend to be worse, which is aligned with the more challenging clinical course they face. Missed indolent infections may further compromise patient outcomes, often requiring multiple revision operations. Although limited by the small sample size and descriptive design, this case series highlights the need for larger studies to develop evidence-based management recommendations for this challenging clinical scenario.
