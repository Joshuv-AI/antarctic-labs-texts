The Sacro-Femoral-Pubic Angle May Estimate Pelvic Tilt in Patients with Severe Developmental Dysplasia of the Hip and Leg Length Discrepancy Undergoing Total Hip Arthroplasty
Authors: Özgür Avci, Alpaslan Öztürk, Yavuz Akalın, Nazan Çevik, Oğuz Çetin, Furkan Yeşilbursalı
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSpinopelvic movement disorders are associated with instability after total hip arthroplasty (THA). In patients with Crowe type 3–4 developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH) and leg length discrepancy (LLD), sagittal radiographs may not allow accurate assessment of spinopelvic motion. This study aimed to evaluate the sacro-femoral-pubic (SFP) angle, a coronal plane parameter, as a predictor of calculated pelvic tilt (PT).MethodsThirty-two patients with neglected unilateral Crowe type 3–4 DDH who underwent THA were retrospectively analyzed. Preoperative SFP angle and sacral slope (SS) were measured, and preoperative PT was calculated using postoperative pelvic incidence and preoperative SS values.ResultsThe SFP angle showed a strong negative correlation with calculated PT (r = −0.77, p < 0.001). Linear regression analysis demonstrated that the SFP angle was a significant predictor of PT (β = −1.02, 95% CI, −1.32 to −0.72; adjusted R2 = 0.58). The regression model demonstrated a root mean square error of 5.46°.ConclusionsThe SFP angle may serve as an indirect surrogate for calculated PT in patients with Crowe type 3–4 DDH and LLD when preoperative sagittal spinopelvic assessment is not feasible prior to THA or spinal deformity surgery.

Lewinnek et al.1) reported that the acetabular cup inclination should be 40° ± 10° and the anteversion should be 15° ± 10° to prevent dislocation, defining these angle values as the safe zone. However, recent studies have reported cases of total hip arthroplasty (THA) that either dislocated despite being within the safe zone or remained stable despite being outside the safe zone.2) The cause of dislocations in these THA cases remains unclear, and spinopelvic motion disorders have been implicated, leading to a shift from the definition of “Lewinnek’s safe zone” to a “patientspecific safe zone” concept. Spinopelvic motion disorders have been reported as the fourth cause of dislocations after THA.3) The dislocation rate was 7.2 times higher in Crowe type 3 and 4 developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH) patients with leg length discrepancy (LLD).4) However, how to perform preoperative spinopelvic assessment in this patient group remains controversial.56)

Pelvic incidence (PI), sacral slope (SS), and pelvic tilt (PT) are parameters first described by Legaye et al.7) from France, and their measurements are performed in the sagittal plane. PI simply indicates the size of the pelvis in the lateral plane. PT, on the other hand, indicates the position of the pelvis relative to the body axis in the sagittal plane, either forward or backward. It has also been shown that PT correlates with health-related quality of life in patients with deformities.89) Therefore, the importance of measuring the forward or backward tilt of the pelvis and planning surgery accordingly is increasingly being recognized.3) These parameters, PT and SS, are dynamic and take different values in relation to each other in positions such as lying down, standing, and sitting.78) Additionally, the total value of PT and SS is always equal to PI, regardless of position.7) PI and PT measurements are defined using the centers of the femoral heads in the acetabulum as reference points. However, in neglected Crowe type 3 and 4 DDH cases with LLD, the femoral heads are not located in the true acetabulum but are completely displaced to a more proximal region.10) In these cases, how PI and PT should be measured and how preoperative spinopelvic assessment should be performed remains undefined, leaving this issue unclear. Additionally, there are few publications on cases in which subtrochanteric shortening osteotomy was performed and the prosthesis was placed in the true acetabulum in Crowe type 3 and 4 patients. There is no consensus on the feasibility of PI and PT measurements before surgery.56) In one of these publications, PI and PT measurements were performed using the center of the femoral head, which was dislocated and located in the pseudoacetabulum, as a reference.6) In contrast, another publication stated that the dislocated femoral head should not be used as a reference for PI and PT measurements and did not record these values preoperatively.5)

Although PT is defined as a sagittal plane parameter, Blondel et al.11) claimed that PT can also be measured using only anteroposterior (AP) radiographs and defined the sacro-femoral-pubic (SFP) angle. Subsequent studies have also reported that the SFP angle correlates with PT.111213) Therefore, it was thought that PT could be calculated from AP pelvic radiographs to facilitate preoperative spinopelvic assessment. However, to our knowledge, there is no study evaluating the predictive value of the SFP angle for PT in Crowe type 3 and 4 DDH patients with LLD.

Therefore, in our study, we aimed to evaluate whether the SFP angle measured on AP pelvic radiographs is sufficient to predict PT in the sagittal plane in Crowe type 3 and 4 DDH patients with LLD. We hypothesized that the SFP angle shows a strong correlation with calculated PT values in Crowe type 3–4 DDH patients with LLD.

METHODS

This study was performed in line with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Approval for this research project was received from the local Ethics Committee of Health Sciences University, Bursa Yüksek Ihtisas Research and Training Hospital on 2025/07-01-2024 TBEK. Written informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Patients who underwent THA for neglected unilateral Crowe type 3 and 4 DDH at our clinic between 2016 and 2024 were retrospectively evaluated, provided they had adequate follow-up and appropriate sagittal plane radiographs before and after surgery. Cases without sagittal plane radiographs prior to THA and those with bilateral Crowe type 3 and 4 dysplasia were excluded from the study. The degree of proximal migration of the femoral head according to the Crowe classification was determined by dividing the distance between the inferior aspect of the femoral head-neck junction and the teardrop by the pelvic height on the pelvic AP radiograph.10) In this classification, type 1 and 2 hip dislocations are considered subluxations, while type 3 and 4 hip dislocations are considered high hip dislocations. According to the exclusion criteria mentioned above, a total of 32 unilateral DDH patients were included in our study: 19 Crowe type 4 (59.38%) and 13 Crowe type 3 (40.63%) (Fig. 1).

Radiographic Measurement

Radiographs were evaluated using Synapse Mobility Picture Archiving and Communication System software (FUJIFILM Healthcare). Preoperative and postoperative standing AP pelvic radiographs and standing lateral spinopelvic radiographs including the pelvis and femoral heads were evaluated. All radiographs were obtained with the patient in a standardized standing position and at a source-to-image distance of 115 cm.14) We attempted to obtain AP pelvic radiographs with neutral pelvic rotation whenever possible. The SFP angle was determined by measuring the angle between the midpoint of the S1 vertebra, the midpoint of the femoral head, and the upper border of the midpoint of the pubic symphysis on the AP pelvic films.11) When measuring the SFP angle on the side with high hip dislocation, the true acetabulum was used as a reference. Since pelvic asymmetry and rotation may occur in DDH patients, the SFP angle was also measured from the normally positioned hip, and the average of the 2 measured SFP values was taken to determine the angle (Figs. 2A and 3A). In this way, we aimed to reduce the effect of pelvic asymmetry and rotation on the measurement values. Representative examples of the radiographic measurements are shown in Figs. 2 and 3. The SS angle was measured as the angle between the sacral endplate and the horizontal plane on lateral radiographs. While the SS angle could be measured on preoperative lateral radiographs, PI and PT measurements could not be obtained on preoperative lateral radiographs due to the high position of the hip (Figs. 2B and 3B).

Since the prosthesis was placed in the true acetabulum after surgery, the PT and PI values were measured and recorded on the lateral pelvic films taken after surgery. PI was measured by taking the midpoint of the femoral heads that overlapped with the perpendicular line drawn from the midpoint of the superior endplate of the S1 vertebra as a reference7) (Figs. 2C and 3C). Postoperative PI and PT measurements were calculated using the artificial femoral head placed in the true acetabulum as a reference.5) Postoperative PI was used to calculate preoperative PT values according to the formula PI = SS + PT.715) The correlation between the calculated PT value and the SFP angle was evaluated using the Pearson correlation test and linear regression analysis. All radiological measurements were independently performed by 2 orthopedic surgeons (ÖA and AÖ) experienced in musculoskeletal radiology, each repeated 3 times at different time points, and the mean values were used for analysis. Inter- and intraobserver reliability was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) based on a 2-way random-effects model with absolute agreement (mean rating, k = 2).

Statistical Analysis

Average values were reported as mean ± standard deviation. Pearson’s correlation analysis and linear regression analysis were used to assess the relationship between preoperative calculated PT and the SFP angle. Data were analyzed using the IBM SPSS version 23.0 (IBM Corp.), and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. A post hoc power analysis was performed for the Pearson correlation between SFP angle and PT. With a sample size of 32, a correlation coefficient of r = 0.788, and α = 0.05 (2-tailed), the achieved statistical power (1–β) was calculated as 0.97 using G*Power software version 3.1.9.2 (Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf).

RESULTS

Of the patients included in the study, 11 (34.38%) were male and 21 (65.62%) were female, with a mean age of 50.25 ± 6.21 years (range, 38–65 years) at the time of surgery. The average LLD in the patients was 54.41 ± 12.39 mm. The average PI value of our patients was found to be 60.91° ± 9.65°, while the average SS in the standing x-rays taken before surgery was 46.34° ± 9.50°, and the SFP angle, calculated by taking the average of the right and left SFP values, was found to be 59.66° ± 6.46°. The preoperative calculated PT value, obtained by subtracting the SS value from the PI value, was found to be 14.53° ± 8.53° on average.

A strong negative correlation was observed between the SFP angle and calculated PT angle (Pearson’s r = −0.77, p < 0.001). In linear regression analysis, the SFP angle was a significant predictor of calculated PT (β = −1.02; 95% CI, −1.32 to −0.72; p < 0.001), explaining 58% of the variance in calculated PT (adjusted R2 = 0.58) (Fig. 4). The regression model demonstrated a root mean square error of 5.46°. The regression equation was: calculated PT = 75.34 − 1.02 × (SFP angle). Interobserver ICC values ranged from 0.84 to 0.91, and intraobserver ICC values ranged from 0.82 to 0.89, indicating excellent reliability for all radiographic measurements.

DISCUSSION

In this study, a strong correlation was found between preoperative calculated PT values using the SFP angle measured from preoperative AP pelvic radiographs in Crowe type 3 and 4 DDH patients with unilateral LLD and PI values calculated using the femoral head in the true acetabulum after surgery as a reference. This allows PT values, which cannot be reliably measured preoperatively in Crowe type 3 and 4 patients, to be estimated using SFP angle measurements, thereby facilitating preoperative spinopelvic assessment and planning for post-THA instability. We found that estimation of PT values using the SFP angle may represent a suitable approach for assessing sagittal pelvic position in this patient population. To our knowledge, this is the first study to specifically evaluate the applicability of the SFP angle in patients with Crowe type 3–4 DDH and LLD.

In patients undergoing THA, assessment of spinopelvic motion and particularly PT prior to surgery is very important to identify patients with spinopelvic motion disorders, to adjust prosthesis versions to the “patient-specific safe zone,” and to determine the need for restrictive prostheses prior to surgery.16) However, due to the inability to identify femoral heads on lateral radiographs in patients with advanced DDH, PI and PT values used in spinopelvic motion assessment are often unmeasurable in these patients. Nevertheless, the calculated PT value can be determined using the SFP angle, which is easily measurable on AP pelvic radiographs and has proven interobserver and intraobserver reliability,17) and the PI value of patients can also be obtained using the PI = PT + SS formula. Therefore, we consider the use of the SFP angle to be particularly valuable in advanced-stage DDH patients prior to THA, as it provides a practical means of preoperative sagittal pelvic assessment when conventional spinopelvic measurements are not feasible. In this context, estimation of PT using the SFP angle may help identify patients with abnormal pelvic alignment and potential spinopelvic imbalance before surgery, thereby contributing to preoperative risk assessment and surgical planning. Nevertheless, the SFP angle should be considered an adjunctive parameter rather than a substitute for conventional sagittal spinopelvic evaluation. Coronal PT, which is commonly observed in patients with severe DDH and LLD, may influence radiographic measurements; therefore, bilateral SFP measurements were averaged to minimize this potential bias.

A cadaver study conducted to predict sagittal PT values from AP radiographs using the SFP angle showed a high degree of correlation.18) While some publications report that the SFP angle is reliable in predicting PT values, others argue the opposite.19) In a recent meta-analysis, Chai et al.20) demonstrated a moderate correlation between the SFP angle and the sagittal PT angle, leading several authors to conclude that sagittal PT cannot be reliably assessed on AP pelvic radiographs.19) However, as previously reported, this meta-analysis included only 9 observational studies with a limited number of patients, and it would be incorrect to draw definitive conclusions that SFP does not provide accurate results.21) Additionally, it is known that patients with an abnormally increased standing PT (≥ 19°) are at risk for instability.22) Instead of taking sagittal spinopelvic radiographs in the standing, sitting, and deep sitting positions for all patients to identify these high-risk patients, it has been reported that AP radiographs can be easily used for screening high-risk patients, thereby reducing patients’ exposure to x-ray radiation.23)

Several techniques for measuring PT from pelvic AP radiographs have been described in the literature.24) Tannast et al.25) evaluated 6 different parameters described for assessing pelvic position in AP pelvic films and reported that measurements based on the vertical distance between the upper edge of the pubic symphysis and the sacrococcygeal joint showed the highest correlation. Although this measurement is easy to perform, the complexity of the calculation equation makes it impractical for use. Furthermore, the highest Pearson correlation coefficient in this study was 0.68 in men and 0.63 in women. In the study by Blondel et al.,11) the correlation coefficient between the SFP angle and PT was found to be 0.87 on average in men and 0.67 in women. In another study evaluating the SFP angle in patients with ankylosing spondylitis, the Pearson correlation coefficient between the SFP angle and PT was found to be 0.852. In our study, the average Pearson correlation value was 0.788, which is within the range reported in previous clinical studies and suggests a strong association between the SFP angle and calculated PT in this specific patient population. However, due to the small number of patients in our study, we did not evaluate factors such as sex and age that could influence this result.

Blondel et al.11) found average PT and SFP values of 18.9° ± 11.5° and 59.2° ± 8.9°, respectively, while Hu et al.26) evaluated 115 patients with ankylosing spondylitis and thoracic kyphosis and found PT and SFP values of 32.9° ± 13.1° and 47.5° ± 12.4°, respectively. In our study, the mean calculated PT and SFP values were 14.53° ± 8.93° and 59.83° ± 6.49°, respectively. We believe that these differences in the studies may be due to the presence of dysplasia, sex, age, and ethnic differences. Blondel et al.11) reported in their study on the normal population that PT and SFP had a higher correlation in men than in women. In contrast, there are also studies reporting a higher correlation in women.1317) Furthermore, since it is known that PT varies with age and ethnic origin, we consider these differences to be normal.27)

There is limited research on preoperative PI measurements in advanced DDH patients, and there is no consensus on the matter.56) The measurements in the study stating that PI and PT values can be measured using the dislocated and high-positioned femoral head as a reference do not, in our opinion, provide very accurate results.6) Although this study reported a change in PI values in the postoperative period compared to the preoperative period, PI is generally regarded as a constant anatomical parameter in adulthood and is not substantially influenced by posture or pelvic position.71528) Therefore, in the present study, postoperative PI was used as a surrogate for preoperative PI. In another study focusing on spinopelvic parameters in advanced DDH patients, it was suggested that PI values be measured based on the femur placed in the actual acetabulum postoperatively.5) In our study, following this approach, we chose to use the PI value measured postoperatively as a reference for preoperative measurements as well, given that it is a constant parameter.

The SRS-Schwab adult spinal deformity classification incorporates PT as one of its sagittal modifiers.2930) Because PT assessment may be challenging in patients with advanced DDH and high-riding femoral heads, estimation of PT using the SFP angle may facilitate spinopelvic evaluation in this patient population. Nevertheless, further studies are required before SFP-based assessment can be incorporated into routine spinal deformity classification systems. Alternative methods for assessing PT on lateral radiographs have been described. However, identification of the required anatomical landmarks may be challenging in neglected DDH cases with altered pelvic anatomy. Therefore, SFP measurement from AP pelvic radiographs may represent a practical alternative in selected patients.

Our study had certain limitations. The retrospective nature of the study and the relatively small sample size are among its main limitations. However, we included a highly specific patient population consisting of individuals with unilateral high-grade DDH who had adequate follow-up and appropriate preoperative sagittal radiographs. Furthermore, because our study included only patients with unilateral Crowe type 3–4 DDH and LLD, the generalizability of these findings to other DDH subgroups or routine THA populations may be limited. Additional studies involving larger and more heterogeneous patient populations are needed to validate our findings. Another limitation of our study is that we were unable to evaluate sex, age, and ethnic origin in relation to SFP values due to the small number of patients. Another major concern in radiographic evaluations is the possibility of measurement errors. However, the fact that the inter- and intraobserver reliability in our study was above 0.8 suggests that errors were minimal. We did not differentiate between intragluteal and pseudoacetabular dislocation patterns in Crowe type 4 patients, which may influence spinopelvic parameters and represents a limitation of this study. Another important limitation is that preoperative PT was not directly measured but calculated using postoperative PI and preoperative SS values. Although PI is generally considered a constant anatomical parameter in adulthood, the use of a calculated PT value may introduce cumulative measurement error originating from both PI and SS measurements. Therefore, the observed association between the SFP angle and PT should be interpreted with the understanding that PT was a derived rather than independently measured parameter.

In conclusion, in our study, which is the first study to evaluate the SFP angle in Crowe type 3 and 4 DDH patients with LLD, we found a strong correlation between the SFP angle and PT. Therefore, the SFP angle may serve as an adjunctive parameter for estimating PT in patients with severe DDH when direct sagittal spinopelvic assessment is not feasible. However, it should not be considered a substitute for conventional sagittal spinopelvic measurements when these can be obtained.
