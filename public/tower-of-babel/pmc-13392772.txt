Functional Chain Engineering in MOFs: Balancing Pore Size and Affinity for Noble Gas Separation
Authors: Kyu‐Min Ryoum, Jihyun Park, Kwang Hyun Oh, Hoi Ri Moon, Youn‐Sang Bae
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe efficient separation of Xe from Kr requires adsorbents with optimized pore environments that maximize van der Waals interactions with Xe atoms. To achieve this, a series of five metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) are synthesized using a mixed‐ligand strategy that systematically incorporates bulky C8 alkoxy substituents into the IRMOF‐1 framework through varying ratios of terephthalic acid (BDC) and 2,5‐bis(octyloxy)‐1,4‐benzenedicarboxylic acid (C8BDC). Increasing the C8BDC content progressively reduces the pore size, and ML‐80C8 (20% BDC and 80% C8BDC) provides the optimal pore environment for selective Xe adsorption. ML‐80C8 exhibits the best Xe/Kr separation performance, achieving a high Xe uptake of 0.96 mmol g− 1 and a markedly elevated Xe/Kr selectivity of 12.1, comparable to that of high‐performing benchmark MOFs. It demonstrates excellent Xe/Kr separation performance under dynamic mixture conditions, outstanding stability, and easy regenerability. These results highlight the potential of mixed‐ligand MOFs for tailored pore designs in noble gas separation.

Introduction

Xe and Kr are high‐value noble gases with growing demand owing to their applications in the lighting, medicine, aerospace, and semiconductor industries [1, 2]. However, both gases exist only in trace amounts in the Earth's atmosphere (0.087 ppm for Xe; 1.14 ppm for Kr), and their chemical inertness makes atmospheric recovery particularly challenging [3, 4]. Currently, Xe and Kr are recovered as by‐products of cryogenic air separation processes designed primarily for nitrogen and oxygen production [5]. Further purification requires additional condensation and cryogenic distillation steps, which are highly energy‐intensive, and contribute to their high market prices ($60/L for Xe and $1/L for Kr by 2023) [6, 7].

Adsorption‐based separation is a cost‐effective alternative for Xe and Kr recovery. Unlike conventional cryogenic distillation, adsorption‐based separation can be performed under mild conditions, typically at ambient temperature, which significantly reduces energy consumption [1]. This principle relies on differences in the gas–solid interaction strength; however, distinguishing the adsorption strength of the adsorbent toward the two gases is challenging because of their similar kinetic diameters and the absence of permanent dipole or quadrupole moments [8, 9, 10]. Excluding very few examples of Kr‐selective adsorption [11, 12, 13], current strategies focus on amplifying the slightly higher polarizability of Xe compared to Kr, which originates from the higher molecular weight of the former, by preferentially enhancing the van der Waals interactions with Xe. This approach improves both Xe uptake and Xe/Kr selectivity [14, 15, 16]. Furthermore, adsorbents intended for Xe/Kr separation should exhibit rapid adsorption kinetics, easy regeneration, and high structural stability. Various materials, including zeolites, activated carbon, and metal–organic frameworks (MOFs), have been explored to meet these requirements [17, 18, 19, 20].

MOFs are emerging as promising candidates for many industrial applications, including gas storage [21, 22], separation [23, 24], catalysis [25, 26], sensing [27, 28], and drug delivery [29], because of their high surface area, high crystallinity, and tunable pore structures [30, 31]. Composed of metal nodes and organic linkers, MOFs allow precise control over pore size and surface chemistry through the judicious choice of metals, ligands, and functional groups [32]. This versatility enables targeted design for challenging separations such as Xe/Kr [33, 34], CO/CO2 [35, 36], C2H6/C2H4 [37, 38], hydrogen isotopes [39, 40], xylene isomers [41, 42], and N2/CH4 [43, 44]. Large MOF databases encompassing both experimental and hypothetical structures have enabled high‐throughput computational screening of materials suitable for Xe/Kr separation. Sikora et al. screened 137 000 hypothetical MOFs and found that optimal Xe/Kr separation occurs in structures with the largest cavity diameters (LCD) of 4.1−8.2 Å, uniform pore morphology, and LCD‐to‐pore limiting diameter (PLD) ratios of 1−2 [45]. Banerjee et al. screened 125,000 MOFs and identified SBMOF‐1 (pore size of 4.2 Å) as the top‐performing, synthesizable candidate with a Xe/Kr selectivity of 16 [20]. Recently, Kurisingal et al. suggested that optimal pore sizes for Xe/Kr separation lie between 3.8 and 5.2 Å [8]. The pore sizes of high‐performing MOFs such as SBMOF‐1 (4.2 Å), MOF‐Cu‐H (4.4 Å), NU‐200 (4.5, 4.7 Å), and Th‐BPYDC‐I (4.05 Å) indeed fall within this range [20, 46, 47, 48]. Because open metal sites (OMSs) can enhance the adsorption strength even for nonpolar gases, MOFs with OMSs have also been employed for Xe/Kr separation [49, 50, 51]. Functionalization strategies have likewise shown promise. For example, UiO‐66 derivatives with electron‐donating groups, such as methoxy (─OCH3) and amino (─NH2) groups, exhibit improved Xe/Kr selectivity [33], while UiO‐68‐F4 shows the highest selectivity among the four functionalized variants (─F4, ─Me2, ─TMS2, and ─I2) [52]. More recently, introducing methyl groups into MOFs has been found to improve Xe/Kr separation performance by fine‐tuning the pore size [53, 54]. Kang et al. employed amino functionalization in the MOF, resulting in enhanced structural stability through increased hydrogen bonding between the framework and the functional groups, as well as improved Xe/Kr separation performance due to rearrangement of the pore structure [55].

Building on these insights, we aimed to design a MOF adsorbent with a pore size of 3.8–5.2 Å and a high density of methyl groups to maximize Xe/Kr selectivity. To this end, we developed mixed‐ligand MOFs (MLMOFs) based on a cubic IRMOF‐1 (MOF‐5) framework by combining its native linker, 1,4‐benzenedicarboxylic acid (BDC), with a methyl‐rich linker, 2,5‐bis(octyloxy)‐1,4‐benzenedicarboxylic acid (C8BDC). An IRMOF constructed with C8BDC has been reported to adopt a rhombohedral framework owing to framework distortion induced by alkoxy–aromatic interactions [56]. Given the bulkiness of C8BDC, we anticipated that a MOF composed entirely of C8BDC would not provide sufficient pore space for Xe/Kr adsorption. Therefore, we hypothesized that partial substitution of BDC with C8BDC would preserve the cubic structure rather than the rhombohedral structure, maintain pore accessibility, and create a methyl‐rich environment to enhance Xe/Kr selectivity (Scheme 1). Accordingly, we synthesized a series of MLMOFs, denoted ML‐xC8 (x = 20, 50, and 80), where x represents the molar percentage of C8BDC among the total linkers. ML‐0C8 corresponds to IRMOF‐1, which was constructed solely with BDC linkers, while ML‐100C8 refers to IRMOF‐C8, which was composed entirely of C8BDC linkers. We systematically investigated the effect of C8BDC content on the surface area, pore size, Xe and Kr adsorption capacities, Xe/Kr selectivity, and isosteric heats of adsorption. The best‐performing sample was further evaluated through breakthrough experiments under Xe/Kr mixture conditions, and cyclic adsorption–desorption tests were conducted to assess the stability and performance under more realistic conditions.

Results and Discussion

Syntheses and Characterizations of ML‐xC8 Materials

A series of ML‐xC8 materials with varying molar ratios of C8BDC and BDC linkers were synthesized via a one‐pot solvothermal method (Scheme 1). Optical microscopy (OM) images (Figure 1a and Figure S1) revealed that all materials formed uniformly sized crystals with particle sizes of 200–300 µm. The powder x‐ray diffraction (PXRD) pattern of IRMOF‐C8 exhibits peak splitting owing to its rhombohedrally distorted phase [56]. All ML‐xC8 samples containing both linkers showed diffraction patterns consistent with those of IRMOF‐1 and no significant peak splitting, suggesting the formation of a cubic structure (Figure S2).

1H‐Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) analyses were performed to determine the ratios of the two linkers (Figure 1b and Figure S5). Peaks corresponding to BDC were observed at approximately 8.00 ppm, whereas those for C8BDC were observed at 7.19, 3.91, 1.61, 1.33, 1.19, and 0.79 ppm. The ratio of the two linkers was calculated based on the integrated peak areas near 8.00 and 3.91 ppm. The measured C8BDC contents of ML‐20C8, ML‐50C8, and ML‐80C8 were 23%, 47%, and 80%, respectively (Table 1), confirming that the intended linker ratios were successfully incorporated into the frameworks.

To confirm complete incorporation of the ligands into the MOFs and the absence of encapsulated free ligands, Fourier‐transform infrared (FT‐IR) spectra were analyzed (Figure S10). In the spectra of the BDC and C8BDC ligands, a peak at 1,677 cm−1 corresponding to the carboxylic acid group is observed; however, this peak is absent in all three MOFs. Additionally, 1H NMR spectra (Figure S5) were compared with that of MOF prior to removal of N,N‐Diethylformamide (DEF) solvent (Figure S11). No peaks corresponding DEF were observed after dichloromethane (DCM) solvent exchange. Therefore, the FT‐IR spectra and 1H NMR results indicate that neither DEF nor unreacted ligands remain in the pores of ML‐xC8 materials.

To further understand how this compositional variation influences the pore structure, the LCD and PLD of IRMOF‐1 and IRMOF‐C8 were calculated using Zeo++. IRMOF‐1 exhibited LCD and PLD values of 15.1 and 8.2 Å, respectively, whereas IRMOF‐C8 showed significantly reduced values of 4.8 and 1.1 Å (Table S2). These results indicate that the incorporation of bulky C8BDC linkers can substantially reduce the pore size and suggest that pore structure within the framework can be tuned by adjusting the linker composition.

The porosity and internal pore structure were further characterized from the N2 adsorption isotherms obtained at 77 K (Figure 1c). As the C8BDC content increased, the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) surface area and total pore volume gradually decreased, likely owing to the bulky alkoxy chains of the C8BDC linker. Pore size distribution (PSD) analysis revealed that all ML‐xC8 samples exhibited broad peaks in the smaller‐pore region compared to IRMOF‐1 (Figure 1d). Furthermore, as the C8BDC content increased from 20% to 80%, the pore size decreased from 7.1–7.6 Å to 5.1 Å. For IRMOF‐C8, the N2 adsorption isotherm at 77 K was not measurable because of the narrow pores. Instead, a CO2 adsorption isotherm was obtained at 195 K, yielding a BET surface area of 256 m2 g−1 (Figure S4a). These results suggest that IRMOF‐C8 has extremely small pore sizes, which are smaller than those of ML‐xC8 samples.

NMR analyses of samples collected at different reaction times revealed a gradual increase in the C8BDC proportion during the crystal growth of ML‐xC8 (Figure S3). This behavior can be attributed to the difference in the crystallization rates between the two linkers. The bulky nature of C8BDC results in a significantly slower crystallization rate for IRMOF‐C8 than that for IRMOF‐1. Therefore, even at a high initial concentration of C8BDC in the mother liquor, BDC was preferentially incorporated into the early crystal nuclei. As the reaction proceeded and BDC was consumed, the C8BDC ratio gradually increased. This hypothesis was further supported by the observation that the rate of increase was more pronounced at higher initial C8BDC contents, with the highest rate observed for ML‐80C8.

Effect of C8BDC Content on Xe and Kr Adsorption

Xe and Kr adsorption isotherms were acquired at 298 K for IRMOF‐1, IRMOF‐C8, and the three ML‐xC8 materials to evaluate the effect of the C8BDC content on Xe and Kr adsorption (Figure 2a,b). In all five MOFs, Xe showed a higher uptake than Kr, a trend commonly observed in most adsorbents [8]. This behavior can be attributed to the higher polarizability of Xe (4.0 × 10−24 cm3) compared with Kr (2.48 × 10−24 cm3), resulting in stronger van der Waals interactions. Examination of the isotherm shapes further indicates stronger Xe–MOF interactions than Kr–MOF interactions: for the weakly adsorbing Kr, all materials displayed nearly linear isotherms up to 100 kPa, whereas Xe isotherms (except for IRMOF‐1) exhibited a Type I profile with a steeper initial slope at low pressure as the proportion of C8BDC increased.

Single‐component adsorption isotherms of (a) Xe and (b) Kr at 298 K, gas uptakes per unit surface area (SA) for (c) Xe and (d) Kr at 298 K, and isosteric heats of adsorption (Qst ) for (e) Xe and (f) Kr.

Xe uptakes at 100 kPa (in mmol g−1) increased in the order ML‐50C8 (1.01) > ML‐80C8 (0.96) > IRMOF‐1 (0.75) > ML‐20C8 (0.62) > IRMOF‐C8 (0.41). In contrast, Kr uptake at 100 kPa (in mmol g−1) exhibited a different sequence: ML‐50C8 (0.25) > IRMOF‐1 (0.24) > ML‐20C8 (0.18) ≈ ML‐80C8 (0.18) > IRMOF‐C8 (0.12). For both Xe and Kr, the adsorption capacity did not depend solely on the C8BDC content. Increasing the C8BDC fraction enhanced gas–solid interactions, improving Xe and Kr adsorption; however, the bulky alkoxy chains also reduced the surface area of the MOF frameworks, which negatively impacted adsorption. These opposing effects balanced each other and collectively governed the Xe and Kr uptake.

To isolate the effect of the enhanced gas–solid interactions from surface area contributions, the uptake values were normalized by the BET surface area. For both Xe and Kr, the uptake per unit surface area followed the order IRMOF‐C8> ML‐80C8> ML‐50C8> ML‐20C8> IRMOF‐1 (Figure 2c,d), which is consistent with the order of the C8BDC content. This clearly demonstrates that the incorporation of the C8 alkoxy groups strengthens the interaction between the adsorbent and both noble gases.

Further insights into these interactions were obtained through the isosteric heat of adsorption (Q st) calculations (Figure 2e,f). All five MOFs exhibited higher Q st values for Xe than for Kr, confirming Xe‐selective adsorption in all materials. For both gases, the Q st order mirrored the uptake‐per‐surface‐area trend, indicating that C8BDC incorporation enhanced gas–solid interactions. These results suggest that the van der Waals forces between the C8 alkoxy chains and Xe/Kr molecules play a key role in increasing the adsorption affinity.

To assess the adsorption behavior of the five MOFs within a single pore, the number of Xe and Kr atoms adsorbed per unit cell was calculated from the gas uptakes at 298 K and 1 bar (Table S4). The PXRD patterns confirmed that the cubic structure was preserved in ML‐xC8 even after the introduction of the C8 alkoxy chains. In addition, IRMOF‐C8, previously referred to as MOF‐5‐C8 in the literature [56], showed only a 2%–3% reduction in cell volume owing to the distortion from a cubic to a rhombohedral structure. Considering these factors, it can be concluded that the cell volume does not significantly affect Xe and Kr adsorption performance. Therefore, ignoring the cell volume, the gas uptake per unit cell was calculated to evaluate the effect of the number of alkoxy chains. The number of adsorbed gas atoms per unit cell of the cubic IRMOF‐1 structure was estimated by dividing the adsorbed amount by the formula weight of Zn4O(BDC)3; the same method was applied to the other four MOFs. ML‐80C8 exhibited the highest number of Xe atoms per unit cell (1.33), whereas ML‐50C8 adsorbed the highest number of Kr atoms per unit cell (0.29). In IRMOF‐C8, which exhibited the strongest interactions with both Xe and Kr, all ligands were functionalized with alkoxy groups, yielding an average of six alkoxy groups per unit cell. However, the bulky nature of these groups reduces the PLD to only 1.1 Å, as noted above. Consequently, many pores become inaccessible, leading to a comparatively lower number of adsorbed Xe and Kr atoms per unit cell.

A detailed examination of the Xe and Kr isotherms at 298 K (Figure 2a,b) revealed several noteworthy facts. IRMOF‐1, which possessed the largest surface area, exhibited linear isotherms for both Xe and Kr, indicating weak gas–solid interactions. Consequently, both Xe and Kr uptakes were moderate, and the Xe/Kr selectivity was expected to be low. In contrast, IRMOF‐C8, which has the highest density of C8 alkoxy groups, showed the lowest adsorption capacity for both gases, primarily because of its low surface area. These results suggest that relying solely on either a large surface area or a high C8 alkoxy group density is unfavorable for effective Xe and Kr adsorption. However, ML‐50C8 and ML‐80C8 demonstrated high Xe uptake, indicating that a balanced combination of surface area and C8 alkoxy group density enhances the adsorption performance. While ML‐50C8 exhibited a slightly higher Xe uptake at 100 kPa, ML‐80C8 achieved superior Xe uptake at lower pressures (<60 kPa), making both materials the most effective Xe adsorbents among the five MOFs studied. Notably, although ML‐50C8 showed the highest Kr uptake, ML‐80C8 displayed significantly lower Kr adsorption, indicating that ML‐80C8 is likely to deliver the highest Xe/Kr selectivity.

Effect of C8BDC Content on Xe/Kr IAST Selectivity

To evaluate the selective adsorption performance of Xe from Xe/Kr gas mixtures, the Xe/Kr selectivity was calculated using the ideal adsorbed solution theory (IAST) [57], a well‐established method for predicting the adsorption behavior of gas mixtures in an adsorbent from single‐component isotherms [38, 44, 58]. Under 100 kPa of equimolar Xe/Kr mixture, ML‐80C8 exhibits the highest selectivity (12.1), followed by IRMOF‐C8 (7.1), ML‐50C8 (5.3), ML‐20C8 (4.3), and IRMOF‐1 (3.4) (Figure 3a). This performance surpasses that of benchmark MOFs such as Co‐MOF‐74 (9.8), Mg‐MOF‐74 (7.0), Zn‐MOF‐74 (5.5) [50], and CALF‐20 (11.4) [59]. Given that the typical industrial Xe/Kr composition is 20:80, the selectivity was also evaluated under this ratio (Figure 3b). Although the selectivity of ML‐80C8 decreased slightly to ∼9.5, it remained the highest among the five MOFs and was comparable to those of several high‐performance MOFs, including SBMOF‐1 (16) [20], Cu‐CDC (11.5) [60], SBMOF‐2 (10) [61], MIL‐120 (9.6) [62], and NU‐200 (9.3) [47].

IAST‐predicted Xe/Kr selectivities at 298 K for (a) equimolar (50:50 v/v) and (b) industrially relevant (20:80 v/v) Xe/Kr gas mixtures.

Henry's constants were calculated using a virial equation to further verify the preferential adsorption of Xe over Kr [63, 64]. Henry's constant is a useful indicator of the affinity between an adsorbent and an adsorbate [33]. As shown in Table 2, the ratio of Henry's constants for Xe and Kr followed the same trend as the IAST selectivity values. The agreement between the IAST selectivity and Henry's constant confirms that ML‐80C8 exhibits strong preferential adsorption of xenon in the Xe/Kr mixtures.

Among the series of MOFs, ML‐80C8 achieved the highest IAST selectivity because its pore size (5.1 Å) falls within the ideal range of 3.8–5.2 Å for Xe/Kr separation [8]. ML‐20C8 (7.1–7.6 Å) and ML‐50C8 (5.9–6.9 Å) exhibited lower selectivities than ML‐80C8, which can be attributed to their larger pore sizes compared to the ideal range. Meanwhile, although IRMOF‐C8 exhibits the highest Xe Q st, it shows lower Xe/Kr selectivity than that of ML‐80C8, possibly due to the significantly small pore size, which favors the adsorption of the smaller Kr molecules. Overall, these results indicate that ML‐80C8 provides an optimal pore environment for effective Xe/Kr separation. With the second‐highest Xe uptake and the highest Xe/Kr selectivity among the five MOFs studied, ML‐80C8 is identified as the best candidate for efficient Xe/Kr separation.

Potential of ML‐80C8 for Xe/Kr Separation Processes

Because the IAST selectivity is derived solely from adsorption equilibrium data, it does not account for kinetic effects or the dynamic flow of gas mixtures [50]. As a result, IAST‐based predictions can be incomplete or inaccurate when assessing the actual separation performance under practical operating conditions. To more accurately evaluate the separation performance of ML‐80C8, the most promising candidate identified from the isotherms and IAST selectivity, dynamic breakthrough experiments were conducted under actual mixing conditions. The first breakthrough experiment was performed using a 50:50 Xe/Kr mixture (Figure 4a). In this test, Kr began to elute after approximately 5 s, whereas Xe was detected only after 48 s. This produced a separation window of approximately 43 s, during which high‐purity krypton (>99.99%) was collected. The Xe/Kr selectivity calculated from the breakthrough experiment was 9.11, which was slightly lower than the IAST‐predicted value. A second breakthrough experiment was conducted using an industrially relevant 20:80 Xe/Kr ratio (Figure 4b). Under these conditions, Kr was detected at the outlet after 4 s, whereas Xe appeared after 15 s, yielding a breakthrough‐based Xe/Kr selectivity of 6.97. These results confirm that ML‐80C8 can effectively separate Xe from Xe/Kr mixtures under realistic conditions.

Breakthrough curves of ML‐80C8 for (a) equimolar (50:50 v/v) and (b) industrially relevant (20:80 v/v) Xe/Kr mixtures over three cycles and (c) repeated Xe adsorption isotherms on ML‐80C8 at 298 K over five cycles.

For practical applications in separation processes, it is crucial to ensure that the adsorbent performance remains stable over multiple adsorption–desorption cycles. Cyclic adsorption–desorption experiments were carried out to assess the stability and regenerability. Five consecutive single‐component adsorption–desorption cycles were performed for ML‐80C8 (Figure 4c). Under mild desorption conditions in vacuum (∼20 min), near‐complete desorption was achieved, and the adsorption capacity remained virtually unchanged across all five cycles, demonstrating excellent recyclability. Additionally, breakthrough experiments were repeated over three consecutive cycles using Xe/Kr mixtures with molar ratios of 50:50 and 20:80. After each cycle, simple helium purging at ambient temperature and pressure was sufficient for complete regeneration, and the separation performance remained consistent across all three cycles. ML‐80C8 was characterized before and after the breakthrough experiment (Figure S9). Both PXRD patterns and N2 adsorption isotherms demonstrate that ML‐80C8 almost unchanged after 3 breakthrough cycles. These results confirmed the excellent stability and easy regenerability of ML‐80C8 under practical operating conditions.

Conclusions

In this study, we demonstrated that progressively increasing the number of C8 alkoxy functional groups in the IRMOF‐1 framework allows systematic tuning of the pore structure, thereby enhancing the Xe/Kr separation performance. Q st calculations revealed that a higher C8 alkoxy group content strengthened the interactions with Xe. However, excessive incorporation of bulky C8 alkoxy groups significantly reduced the pore size, favoring the adsorption of smaller Kr molecules and producing a countereffect. Consequently, ML‐80C8, in which 80% of the original BDC ligands were substituted with C8BDC, exhibited optimal Xe/Kr separation performance. This was validated through IAST selectivity, Henry's constant, and dynamic breakthrough experiments. These findings underscore the critical role of pore structure control in adsorptive gas separation and demonstrate that a mixed‐ligand strategy enables modulation of pore architecture, leading to improved Xe/Kr separation. Furthermore, the excellent stability and regenerability of ML‐80C8, as evidenced by repeated adsorption–desorption cycles, highlight the potential of this approach for industrial applications.

Experimental Section

Materials

Dimethyl‐2,5‐dihydroxy terephthalate and C8BDC were prepared by reported methods with minor modifications [65, 66]. DEF was purified by distillation prior to use. All other reagents were purchased from commercial vendors and used without further purification.

Syntheses of Ligands and MOFs

Synthesis of Dimethyl‐2,5‐Dihydroxy Terephthalate

Dimethyl succinylsuccinate (41.4 g, 182 mmol) was dissolved in acetic acid (128 mL) and heated to 353 K with stirring. Upon reaching 353 K, N‐chlorosuccinimide (25.0 g, 187 mmol) was slowly added, and the mixture was stirred for 1 h at the same temperature. The resulting precipitate was collected by filtration and washed sequentially with acetic acid, deionized (DI) water, and diethyl ether. The yellow crystalline solid was yielded after drying under vacuum at 403 K. 1H NMR (400 MHz, CDCl3) δ (ppm): 10.05 (s, 2H), 7.47 (s, 2H), 3.97 (s, 6H).

Synthesis of C8BDC

Dimethyl‐2,5‐dihydroxy terephthalate (4.00 g, 17.9 mmol) and K2CO3 (7.32 g, 53.0 mmol) were suspended in N,N‐dimethylformamide (120 mL). Under an Ar atmosphere, 1‐bromooctane (9.15 mL, 53.0 mmol) was added, and the mixture was stirred at 358 K for 30 h. After filtration, the filtrate was concentrated using rotary evaporation. DI water (140 mL), KOH (7 g, 125 mmol), and tetrahydrofuran (140 mL) were added, and the mixture was refluxed at 383 K overnight. The organic solvent was removed by rotary evaporation, and the residue was filtered and acidified with HCl to pH < 2. The resulting precipitate was collected via filtration, washed with DI water, and dried under vacuum at 383 K to yield a beige solid. 1H NMR (400 MHz, CDCl3) δ (ppm): 10.99 (s, 2H), 7.88 (s, 2H), 4.30 (t, 4H), 1.92 (m, 4H), 1.57–1.44 (m, 4H), 1.42–1.25 (m, 16H), 0.88 (t, 6H).

Synthesis of IRMOF‐1

Zinc nitrate hexahydrate (0.224 g, 0.753 mmol) and BDC (0.040 g, 0.241 mmol) were dissolved in DEF (10 mL) in a glass jar. The jar was sealed and placed in an oven at 373 K for 24 h. The mother liquor was decanted, and the resulting cubic crystals were washed with fresh DEF.

Synthesis of IRMOF‐C8

Zinc nitrate hexahydrate (0.075 g, 0.252 mmol) and C8BDC (0.08 g, 0.189 mmol) were dissolved in DEF (10 mL) in a glass jar. The jar was sealed and placed in an oven at 373 K for 48 h. The mother liquor was decanted, and the cubic crystals were washed with fresh DEF.

Syntheses of ML‐xC8 (x = 20, 50, and 80)

ML‐xC8 materials were prepared by a reported method with minor modifications [37, 67]. A 0.1 м BDC solution, 0.1 м C8BDC solution, and 0.3 м zinc nitrate hexahydrate solution were added to a 20 mL vial according to Table S1. DEF (6 mL) was then added, and the vial was placed in an oven at 373 K for 48 h. After 48 h, the mother liquor was decanted, and the MLMOF crystals were washed with fresh DEF.

Characterization

OM images were recorded using a Nikon SMZ745T microscope equipped with a TrueChrome Metrics camera. PXRD patterns were collected on a Bruker D2 Phaser diffractometer (Cu Kα radiation, λ = 1.54050 Å) operating at 30 kV and 10 mA, with a step size of 0.02° over a 2θ range of 3°–40°. N2 and CO2 adsorption isotherms were obtained at 77 and 195 K, respectively, using a BELSORP‐max analyzer (Microtrac BEL Corp., Japan). Before the adsorption measurements, the samples were washed with DCM three times per day for one week, and subsequently degassed under vacuum (p < 10−5 mbar) at 393 K for 12 h. The specific surface areas were calculated from the linear region of the BET equation, and the micropore volumes were determined using the Horváth–Kawazoe (HK) method. 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) spectra were recorded using a Bruker Avance III 300 spectrometer. Prior to NMR analysis, MOF samples were digested in DCl and dimethyl sulfoxide‐d6. The Bruker Avance III 300 spectrometer was conducted at the National Research Facilities and Equipment Center (NanoBio‐Energy Materials Center) at Ewha Womans University. Fourier‐transform infrared (FT‐IR) spectra were measured using a ThermoFisher Scientific iS10 FT‐IR spectrometer.

Xe and Kr Adsorption Measurements

Xe and Kr adsorption isotherms, as well as cyclic Xe adsorption isotherms, were obtained using a TriStar II gas adsorption analyzer (Micrometrics Instruments Co.) equipped with an isothermal bath for temperature control. Samples were degassed at 403 K for 12 h under vacuum (< 1.0 × 10−5 mmHg) before measurement. Isotherms were collected at 273 and 298 K up to a pressure of 100 kPa. To test regeneration performance, Xe adsorption isotherms were measured over five cycles, with mild vacuum regeneration (20 min) between each cycle.

Dynamic Breakthrough Experiments

Dynamic breakthrough experiments were conducted using a custom‐built fixed‐bed adsorption setup (Figure S7). The system was equipped with three mass‐flow controllers (MFCs; 0–100 mL min−1, Bronkhorst). Two MFCs supplied pure Xe and Kr gases, which were mixed using a gas mixer, whereas the third MFC controlled the helium flow for column purging and regeneration. The fixed‐bed column was placed in a ventilated, temperature‐controlled oven (298 K). Pressure regulators were installed at both the inlet and outlet to monitor the pressure drop within the column. The outlet gas composition was analyzed using a MAX300‐LG mass spectrometer (Extrel). The samples were pelletized and sieved to obtain binderless pellets (500–850 𝜇m) using a YLJ‐15 hydraulic pellet press (MTI Corporation) to minimize pressure drop. Before packing, the samples were degassed under vacuum at 403 K for 12 h. A stainless‐steel column (150 mm × 4.4 mm) was packed with the adsorbent, with void spaces filled using glass beads (750 𝜇m) and glass wool. After packing, the column was degassed at 403 K for at least 1 h under helium flow to remove any impurities adsorbed during packing. Breakthrough experiments were performed using Xe/Kr gas mixtures (50:50 and 20:80 v/v) at total flow rates of 10 mL min−1 (i.e., 5 mL min−1 each for Xe and Kr) and 20 mL min−1 (i.e., 4 mL min−1 for Xe and 16 mL min−1 for Kr), respectively. At t = 0, the feed gas was switched from He to the Xe/Kr gas mixture. Once the adsorbent reached saturation, the feed gas was switched back to He for regeneration (3 min). Adsorption–desorption cycles were repeated three times for each condition.
