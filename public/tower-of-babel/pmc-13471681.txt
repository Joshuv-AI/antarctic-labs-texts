Dose-dependent γ-radiation effects on seed vigor and genomic mutation patterns in Shuidong mustard (Brassica juncea var. multiceps)
Authors: Yuxing Huang, Yongguo Zhao, Xiaoyang Ou, Guangyuan Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundShuidong mustard (Brassica juncea var. multiceps) is an important leafy vegetable in South China, yet its production is frequently constrained by seasonal drought stress during autumn sowing. Gamma (γ) radiation can induce genetic variation to potentially improve seed vigor and stress resilience. However, the dose-dependent effects of γ-radiation on seed physiological traits and genome-wide mutations in this crop remain unclear, limiting its effective application in mutation breeding.ResultsShuidong mustard seeds exhibited a biphasic response to 60Co-γ irradiation (0–4000 Gy). Doses lower than 971 Gy (LD50) induced manageable genomic stress, primarily generating single‑nucleotide substitutions, with only moderate effects on germination and root length. In contrast, high doses (≥ 2000 Gy) severely compromised viability, reducing fresh weight by 51.5% and disrupting genomic integrity—evident in elevated GC content (up to 41.44%) and a high frequency of large‑fragment deletions. Mutation analysis revealed dose‑stable hotspots, notably on chromosome AA_Chr09, and significant enrichment of mutations in genes related to DNA repair, ubiquitin‑mediated proteolysis, and central metabolism.ConclusionThis study delineates the dose‑dependent physiological and genomic responses of Shuidong mustard to γ‑radiation. A dose of 971 Gy (LD₅₀) is proposed as a practical benchmark for mutation breeding, balancing the introduction of genetic diversity with acceptable physiological impact. Our results provide a genomic foundation for optimizing radiation‑based breeding strategies to enhance stress resilience, while also advancing the mechanistic understanding of DNA damage response and genome stability in plants.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-13025-x.Keywords: Shuidong mustard (Brassica juncea var. multiceps), Gamma radiation, Seed vigor, Genomic variation

Background

Gamma (γ) radiation is a widely used physical mutagen in plant breeding, valued for its high penetration depth and precise dose control, which enable the induction of a broad spectrum of mutations from single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) to chromosomal rearrangements [1–4]. The practical utility of ⁶⁰Co-γ rays is demonstrated by their successful application in the improvement of diverse crops, including rice [3–5], Linum usitatissimum [6], Lavatera arborea [7], Medicago sativa [8], and Syringa [9]. In Brassica species, γ-irradiation has also been effectively employed to develop improved varieties with traits such as enhanced yield, altered maturity, and increased disease resistance. For example, mutagenesis in rapeseed (B. napus) [10] and mustard [11] has successfully altered plant architecture and selected for desirable agronomic traits. Specific achievements include the development of the high-yielding, disease-resistant B. napus cv. ABASIN-95 [12], early-maturing, high-yielding mutants in B. juncea [13, 14], and the official release of the superior mustard mutants ‘Binasarisha-7’ and ‘Binasarisha-8’ in Bangladesh [15]. Studies also indicate that the genetic response to γ-irradiation is genotype-dependent, with optimal doses varying across cultivars [16–17].

The application of genomics has advanced the systematic characterization of radiation-induced mutational profiles in crops. Whole-genome re-sequencing, for instance, has proven effective in identifying genome-wide variants and elucidating dose-dependent mutation patterns [18–21]. This approach has enabled insights into specific genomic outcomes of radiation treatment. In rapeseed, genome re-sequencing of a large M2 population revealed that high doses of gamma radiation (2000 Gy) are tolerable and can generate heritable structural variations, including deletions that alter gene copy number and affect key seed quality traits such as fatty acid composition [22]. Similarly, studies in mustard have employed radiation to break linkage drag in the M3 generation of B. juncea‑B. fruticulose introgression lines, with treatments leading to the reduction or loss of introgressed chromatin segments and the development of lines with improved agronomy and pest resistance [23]. Despite these advances, a detailed understanding of the dose–response relationship—particularly its impact on critical early-stage traits like seed vigor in the M1 generation, and the associated genome-wide mutation spectrum—remains limited for many vegetable Brassica crops.

Shuidong mustard (B. juncea var. multiceps, AABB, 2n = 36) is a high‑quality leafy vegetable indigenous to South China and has been recognized as a National Geographical Indication product [24], with an estimated annual market value reaching 1.98 billion yuan [25]. Its cultivation, however, is constrained by susceptibility to abiotic stresses such as drought, which negatively impacts seed germination and seedling establishment [26]. Improving seed vigor and stress resilience is therefore a key breeding objective for this crop [27]. While mutation breeding offers a viable strategy, the precise effects of γ-radiation on Shuidong mustard—from physiological damage to genomic alterations—are not well-defined, making the rational selection of optimal mutagenic doses challenging.

To address this gap, we treated Shuidong mustard seeds with a gradient of ⁶⁰Co-γ radiation doses (200–4000 Gy). We then integrated phenotypic assessment with whole-genome re-sequencing to achieve the following specific objectives: (1) to quantify the dose-dependent impact of γ-radiation on seed vigor and growth traits; (2) to characterize the genome-wide mutations (SNPs and InDels) induced across different doses; and (3) to identify genomic regions and functional pathways preferentially disrupted by irradiation. This study provides a comprehensive, dose-resolved analysis of γ-radiation effects, linking physiological outcomes to genomic changes, to inform efficient mutagenesis strategies for the breeding of Shuidong mustard.

Methods

Plant materials and radiation treatment

Freshly harvested seeds (designated M0) of Shuidong mustard were provided by Dianbai Shuidong Mustard Development Co., Ltd. in 2025. Before treatment, a subsample was assessed for germination under standardized conditions (25 °C with sufficient moisture), resulting in a germination rate exceeding 90%, thereby validating its suitability for the experiment. The seed lot was then divided into six equal aliquots, with approximately 10,000 seeds (~ 20 g) per aliquot. These dry seeds were subjected to 60Co-γ irradiation at the Institute of Crop and Nuclear Technology Utilization, Zhejiang Academy of Agricultural Sciences, using a dose rate of approximately 1 Gy/min. Six radiation doses were applied: 0 (control), 200, 500, 1000, 2000, and 4000 Gy. The resulting irradiated seeds were designated as M1. A portion of each M1 seed batch was immediately used for germination assays. The remaining seeds were wrapped in aluminum foil, placed in airtight containers, and stored at -20 °C for subsequent study.

Seed germination test in the laboratory

To investigate the effects of gamma radiation on seed vigor, a standard germination assay was conducted. For each radiation dose, 150 uniformly plump and intact M1 seeds were selected and equally divided into three biological replicates. Before the germination experiment, seeds were surface-sterilized in 75% ethanol for 5 min and rinsed thoroughly with distilled water. For each replicate, seeds were placed in a 10 cm × 10 cm × 1.5 cm germination box lined with two layers of moist filter paper. The boxes were incubated in a growth chamber maintained at 25 °C, 80% relative humidity, and a 12/12 h light/dark cycle with a light intensity of 15,000 lx for 7 d. Germination was monitored three times daily (at 8:00, 14:00, and 20:00), with seeds scored as germinated when the radicle protruded more than 1.0 mm. On the 7th day, all seedlings per replicate were randomly sampled to measure root length, shoot length, and fresh weight. After measurement, five individual plants per treatment were harvested, frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at -80 °C for subsequent DNA extraction and sequencing to assess radiation-induced genetic variation.

Seed performance was assessed using key physiological indices. The final germination percentage, indicative of overall seed viability, was calculated as the proportion of seeds that germinated by day 7. Additionally, individual seedlings (50 per treatment for 6 doses with three replicates, totaling 900 if all germinated) were measured for shoot length, root length, and fresh weight per plant. Germination vigor, which reflects the speed and uniformity of germination, was determined by counting the percentage of germinated seeds by day 3. The seedling growth vigor index, serving as a measure of seedling robustness, was derived from the product of the final germination rate and mean root length, given that root growth is highly responsive to stress. Germination kinetics were analyzed by fitting cumulative germination data to an exponential growth curve using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA), from which the time to reach 50% of maximum germination (T₅₀) was estimated. All statistical analyses were conducted using one-way ANOVA with the Generalized Linear Model (GLM), treating dose as a fixed effect and replication as a random effect, followed by post-hoc tests to evaluate the mean effects of dose using IBM SPSS V22 Statistics software (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA).

Seedling emergence test in soil

To evaluate seed viability under field-simulated conditions, a seedling emergence test was performed. For each radiation treatment, 150 seeds (50 seeds per replicate × 3 replicates) were sown in horticultural substrate. A commercially available nutrient soil (Wangdu Jiansheng Nutritious Soil Production Co., Ltd.) designed for germination assays was used. Seeds were placed in trays filled with substrate and maintained in a greenhouse under controlled conditions. Sufficient moisture was provided by regular watering to ensure optimal germination conditions. Seedling emergence was recorded when cotyledons were fully expanded and visible above the substrate surface. The emergence rate was determined as the percentage of seeds that successfully reached this stage. The dose required for 50% seedling mortality (LD₅₀) was estimated using probit regression in R.

Seed viability assessment by TTC staining

Seed viability was assessed using the TTC (2,3,5-triphenyltetrazolium chloride) assay according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Beijing Biolab Technology Co., Ltd.). For each radiation dose (0, 200, 500, 1,000, 2,000, and 4,000 Gy) and the negative control, five healthy, uniformly sized M1 seeds were selected. The experiment included three biological replicates, resulting in a total of 15 seeds per treatment. As the negative control, seeds were heat-treated at 90 °C for 10 min to eliminate dehydrogenase activity, thereby preventing TTC reduction and ensuring the absence of red staining. All seeds were surface-sterilized with 75% ethanol for 5 min, rinsed thoroughly with distilled water, and imbibed in 1 mL of distilled water at 30 °C for 24 h to soften the seed coats. After imbibition, the seed coats were carefully removed to expose the embryos. Embryos from each treatment group were placed in 5 mL centrifuge tubes, immersed in 1 mL of TTC solution, and incubated at 30 °C for 1 h. Viable seeds developed a distinct red formazan precipitate, whereas non-viable seeds remained unstained. Following incubation, all seeds were rinsed twice with distilled water to remove residual dye and arranged in a single row under standardized lighting conditions to ensure consistent imaging.

To quantify metabolic activity, the red color intensity of TTC-stained seeds was measured using CIELAB (L*, a*, b*) color space analysis in Photoshop CC 2019 (Adobe Systems Incorporated, San Jose, CA, USA). The TTC assay relies on the reduction of soluble, colorless TTC to insoluble red triphenylformazan (TTF) by dehydrogenase enzymes in metabolically active tissues, where staining intensity correlates with seed viability. The analysis focused on the a-value, which represents the green–red axis in the device-independent CIELAB color model. A higher positive a-value indicates stronger red coloration, reflecting greater formazan production and thus higher seed viability, whereas lower or negative values indicate reduced or absent metabolic activity. All seeds were imaged under uniform lighting to minimize variations in luminance (L*). A circular sampling tool with a fixed diameter of 31 pixels was used to measure the average a-value in the central region of each seed. This sampling area was smaller than the total seed area (> 100 × 100 pixels) to minimize errors caused by color heterogeneity and ensure that measurements reflected stained embryonic tissue. This approach provided an objective and quantitative assessment of seed vigor, replacing subjective visual scoring. The dose-response relationship between radiation dose and TTC redness (a-value) was analyzed using nonlinear regression in GraphPad Prism 9.0, with model parameters optimized to fit the observed data and assess the goodness-of-fit.

Detection of radiation-induced genetic variation via high-throughput DNA sequencing and bioinformatics analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted from M1 seedling samples exposed to different doses of gamma radiation using a modified CTAB method. The DNA was sheared into 200–500 bp fragments by ultrasonication (Covaris, USA), followed by end repair, adenylation, and ligation of Illumina adapters to construct sequencing libraries. Library quality was validated by evaluating fragment size distribution and adapter ligation efficiency. After purification with magnetic beads to remove unligated adapters and non-target fragments, the libraries were amplified via PCR and quantified. Paired-end sequencing (100 bp) was performed on the Illumina HiSeq 2000 platform (Novogene, Beijing, China).

Raw sequencing reads underwent preprocessing to eliminate adapter sequences, trim low-quality bases (quality score ≤ 5), and discard reads with over 10% ambiguous bases (Ns). The cleaned reads were aligned to the reference genome of Brassica juncea cv. ‘Tumida T84-66’ V2.0 (accessed on 20 April 2025) [28] using BWA V0.5.9. SNPs and small InDels were identified employing the Genome Analysis Toolkit (GATK) under best practices guidelines. Initially, aligned reads were processed with Picard to mark PCR duplicates, thereby mitigating artifacts resulting from amplification bias. Variant calling was conducted using the GATK HaplotypeCaller algorithm, which facilitates local de novo assembly of haplotypes in active regions to enhance the accuracy of SNP and small InDel identification. For population-level analysis, genomic VCF (gVCF) files were generated for each sample, followed by a joint genotyping step across all samples to create a comprehensive set of raw variant calls.

These raw variants underwent stringent filtering to ensure reliability, based on the following criteria: (1) removal of SNPs within a 5 bp window of an InDel and filtering of adjacent InDels within a 10 bp window using vcfutils.pl (varFilter -w 5 -W 10) in BCFtools; (2) exclusion of clusters containing more than two variants within a 5 bp window (clusterSize 2, clusterWindowSize 5); (3) application of a quality score threshold of QUAL < 30; (4) imposition of a threshold for Quality by Depth (QD) < 2.0; (5) requirement for a root-mean-square mapping quality (MQ) ≥ 40; (6) filtration based on Fisher Strand Bias (FS) > 60.0 to eliminate variants exhibiting significant strand specificity. All other filtration parameters adhered to the default values recommended in the GATK best practices documentation.

Genetic functional annotation of variants was conducted using ANNOVAR and SnpEff, classifying variant effects into high (e.g., frameshift, nonsense), moderate (e.g., missense), low (synonymous), and modifier categories. Genes containing high- or moderate-impact variants were utilized for functional enrichment analysis through homology searches against the Swiss-Prot, COG, Pfam, and GO databases. All sequencing data are deposited at the National Genomics Data Center of China (accession number: CRA032398).

Identification of irritation-responsive mutations

To characterize genomic alterations induced by γ‑radiation in B. juncea, we performed whole‑genome re-sequencing of the cultivar ‘Shuidong’ (including materials irradiated with 200–4000 Gy and a non‑irradiated control) and aligned the reads to the reference genome of a closely related cultivar, ‘Tumida T84‑66’. To accurately identify radiation‑associated variants, the following filtering pipeline was applied: (1) polymorphisms (SNPs and InDels) between the ‘Shuidong’ and ‘Tumida’ genomes were excluded as inter‑cultivar variation; (2) background genetic variants present in the non‑irradiated ‘Shuidong’ control were removed; (3) variants shared across different irradiated samples were retained, as these shared mutations may indicate radiation‑sensitive genomic regions or recurrent mutation sites. The resulting set was defined as putative radiation‑induced variants for downstream analysis.

Mutation hotspot scoring and threshold stratification

We systematically scanned the entire genome using a sliding window approach (100 kb window size with a 50 kb step). The hotspot score for each window was calculated by integrating five characteristic indicators, each assigned fixed weighting coefficients, according to the formula: Hotspot score = Density score + AF score + InDel score + Large InDel score + Functional score. Specifically, the scores were defined as follows: Density score = Variant count per 100 kb × 2; AF score = Average allele frequency × 100 × 1.5; InDel score = InDel variant ratio × 100 × 2; Large InDel score = Ratio of InDels (> 5 bp) × 100 × 3; Functional score = Ratio of high/moderate impact variants × 100 × 2.

The statistical threshold for defining hotspots is established as follows: the overall hotspot score is the arithmetic sum of the five individual scores. Hotspot regions are classified into four categories based on empirically predefined cutoff thresholds: > 100 for Extreme Hotspot, 50–100 for Strong Hotspot, 20–50 for Moderate Hotspot, and ≤ 20 for normal genomic regions. These cutoff values were determined based on the frequency distribution of genome-wide hotspot scores, effectively stratifying mutation enrichment levels.

Results

Effect of γ-radiation on M1 seed germination

Seed germination kinetics were significantly influenced by γ-radiation in a clear dose-dependent manner (Fig. 1). Under control conditions (0 Gy) and at the low dose of 200 Gy, seeds germinated rapidly, reaching near-maximum germination percentage by approximately 110 h, with a time to 50% germination (t₅₀) ranging from 26.41 to 27.32 h. The high goodness-of-fit values (R² > 0.97) confirmed the suitability of the sigmoidal model for describing germination progress under these conditions. At 500 Gy, germination was delayed, as indicated by an increased t₅₀ of 31.02 h, although the final germination percentage remained comparable to that of the control. A more pronounced inhibition was observed at doses above 1000 Gy: germination onset was markedly delayed, the slope of the cumulative curve was flattened, and the final germination percentage declined. At a dose of 2000 Gy, the inhibitory effect became more pronounced, with the t₅₀ surpassing 34 h and the final germination percentage declining to 81%. At the highest dose of 4000 Gy, germination was strongly inhibited throughout the observation period, resulting in a greatly prolonged t₅₀ of over 33 h and a final germination rate of only 62%. These results demonstrate that increasing radiation doses progressively delay germination initiation, reduce germination rate, extend t₅₀, and lower the final germination percentage. The effect shifted from mild at lower doses (≤ 500 Gy) to severe at higher doses (≥ 2000 Gy).

Germination vigor and seedling vitality index were quantitatively evaluated to assess the physiological impact of γ-radiation. Germination vigor, which reflects the speed and uniformity of germination, remained largely unchanged at doses from 0 to 2000 Gy (Fig. 2). However, a significant reduction of approximately 14% was observed at 4000 Gy compared to the maximum value at the 500 Gy group (p < 0.05). In contrast, the seedling growth vitality index—a composite measure of germination percentage and subsequent seedling growth—exhibited a more pronounced and dose-sensitive decline. A statistically significant decrease was first detected at 1000 Gy. The most pronounced effects were noted at higher doses: at 2000 Gy, the vitality index decreased to approximately 50% of the value observed at 500 Gy, while at 4000 Gy, it declined to a mere 15% (p < 0.05) (Figs. 2 and 3).

Radiation dose exerted a significant and differential influence on seedling growth parameters (Fig. 4). Statistical analysis with GLM confirmed a highly significant effect of dose, which explained 38.7–66.4% of the total variation in shoot length, root length, and fresh weight (Additional file 1). The specific responses varied by trait: root length showed a transient, non-significant increase at lower doses (200 and 500 Gy) before declining sharply at higher doses, reaching only 62.1% and 21.7% of the control value at 2000 Gy and 4000 Gy, respectively. In contrast, shoot length remained relatively stable up to 1000 Gy, with significant reductions occurring only at 2000 and 4000 Gy. Fresh weight exhibited a progressive, dose-dependent decrease, declining to approximately 56.4% and 48.5% of the control value at 2000 Gy and 4000 Gy, respectively.

Survival rate of M1 seedlings

The γ-radiation dose produced a significant dose-dependent inhibitory effect on seedling emergence, with marked variations in emergence rates across different dose groups (Fig. 5a). Seeds in the control group (0 Gy) achieved optimal germination, with a final emergence rate of 58.3%. In the low-dose treatment group (200 Gy), the emergence rate was 47.9%, reflecting a modest decrease relative to the control while still maintaining a relatively high germination level. However, as the irradiation dose increased, emergence rates continued to decline: in the 500 Gy and 1000 Gy treatment groups, rates fell to 27.1% and 29.6%, respectively. According to the probit regression model, the estimated LD₅₀ for seedling survival was determined to be 971 Gy (Fig. 5b), at which the predicted relative survival rate dropped to 50% compared to the control group. At higher doses of 2000 Gy and 4000 Gy, germination was severely compromised, yielding emergence rates of only 4.1% and 2.1%, respectively, with the majority of seeds losing their capacity to germinate.

Effects of γ-radiation on seed vitality

Seed metabolic activity following γ-irradiation was assessed using TTC (2,3,5-triphenyltetrazolium chloride) staining, where the intensity of red formazan formation correlates with dehydrogenase activity and seed vigor (Fig. 6a). A clear dose-dependent reduction in staining intensity was observed. Non-irradiated control seeds (0 Gy) showed the strongest red color, indicating high metabolic activity, whereas heat-killed seeds remained nearly colorless. With increasing radiation dose, the staining progressively faded: from dark red at 200 Gy to lighter red/orange at 500–1000 Gy and to pale tan or nearly colorless at 2000–4000 Gy. This visual decline was quantified by measuring the TTC redness (a-value). The redness value decreased exponentially with dose and was best described by the model y = 27.72e–0.0007068x. The value dropped from ~ 62 in the control to below 40 at 1000 Gy, reflecting a pronounced loss of reducing capacity at low-to-medium doses. Beyond 1000 Gy, the decrease slowed and plateaued near 35 at 4000 Gy.

The effect of different doses of 60Co-γ radiation on the staining degree of Shuidong mustard seeds. a, dose-response relationship between γ-radiation dose and TTC staining intensity (indicating dehydrogenase activity) in Shuidong mustard seeds. b, the data points represent observed redness intensity, and the curve shows the best-fit model

Furthermore, a significant positive correlation was observed between the TTC redness value and seed germination rate (Spearman’s r = 0.812, P < 0.05), confirming the reliability of TTC staining as a rapid indicator of radiation-induced loss of seed vigor. Together, these results demonstrate that γ-irradiation causes an exponential, dose-dependent reduction in seed metabolic activity, evident both visually as fading TTC staining and quantitatively as decreased redox capacity, most markedly within the 0–1000 Gy range.

Genomic sequencing quality and GC content

Genomic DNA was extracted from five individual seedlings per treatment for whole‑genome sequencing, yielding 442.55 Gbp of high‑quality clean data with a Q30 score of 95.45%. On average, 94.21% of reads were successfully mapped to the reference genome, producing a mean sequencing depth of 13 × and a genome coverage of 90.84% (Additional file 2), confirming the high quality and completeness of the dataset.

Compared with the non‑irradiated control, irradiated samples displayed a significantly elevated genomic GC content overall (P < 0.01; Fig. 7a). This increase followed a clear dose‑dependent trend (Fig. 7b): at lower doses (200–1000 Gy), GC content remained stable within a narrow range (38.0–38.8%), whereas a marked rise was observed at doses ≥ 2000 Gy, reaching 40.0–41.4% (Additional file 2).

Sequence variation analysis of SNPs and InDels

Whole‑genome re-sequencing of γ‑irradiated and control plants of Shuidong mustard identified approximately 1.40–1.56 million SNPs and 0.46–0.51 million InDels across the 30 analyzed samples. Based on the reference genome size of 904.4 Mb, this corresponds to an overall mutation density of 2.1–2.3 × 10⁻⁶ per base pair. The transition/transversion (Ti/Tv) ratio ranged from 1.38 to 1.40, indicating that transition mutations were prevalent. The nonsynonymous/synonymous (Nonsyn/Syn) mutation ratio was approximately 0.82, suggesting that nonsynonymous substitutions were less frequent than synonymous ones (Additional file 3).

To discriminate radiation‑induced mutations from pre‑existing variation, sequencing reads from the cultivar ‘Shuidong’ were aligned to the reference genome of a divergent cultivar (‘Tumida’). This approach enabled the stepwise removal of two major sources of non‑induced variation: (1) inter‑cultivar polymorphisms, and (2) background variants present in the non‑irradiated control. The resulting high‑confidence set of putative radiation‑induced variants was used for all subsequent analyses (Table 1).

Radiation-induced variation statistics at different doses

We analyzed the dose–response relationship across five radiation doses (200–4000 Gy) by quantifying the number of putative radiation‑induced variants (SNPs and InDels), mutation hotspots (cluster score > 50), and other mutational characteristics (Table 1). The total number of variants did not increase linearly with dose. Instead, it peaked at 2000 Gy (~ 71,430 variants) and then declined to ~ 62,613 variants at 4000 Gy, corresponding to approximately 12,522–14,286 variants per individual plant (n = 5). Mutation hotspots followed a similar non‑linear trend, being most numerous at 2000 Gy (n = 6,748) and fewest at 4000 Gy (n = 6,345).

SNPs accounted for ∼99% of all variants, whereas InDels constituted ∼1%, of which only ∼0.03% were large‑fragment InDels (> 5 bp); this composition was stable across doses. Other mutational traits also varied with dose: the mean allele frequency of variants decreased from 0.76% at 200 Gy to 0.49% at 2000 Gy, then rose slightly to 0.59% at 4000 Gy. The proportion of InDels among all variants increased gradually from 1.03% to 1.12% with increasing dose, while the fraction of large‑fragment InDels decreased from 0.05% (200 Gy) to 0.03% (2000 Gy), suggesting a relative shift toward smaller InDels at higher doses. Functional annotation revealed that the proportion of variants predicted to have moderate‑ or high‑impact effects (e.g., nonsynonymous, stop‑gain, splice‑site) was highest at 1000 Gy (1.08%) and lowest at 4000 Gy (0.76%) (Table 1). Taken together, radiation‑induced mutagenesis in B. juncea showed a complex dose‑dependence: variant number and hotspots peaked at 2000 Gy, whereas the highest frequency of putatively functional variants occurred at 1000 Gy.

Non‑uniform distribution of variants and mutation hotspots

Analysis of SNP and InDel distribution across the 18 chromosomes of B. juncea revealed a markedly non‑uniform genomic landscape following γ‑irradiation (200–4000 Gy) (Fig. 8; Additional file 4). AA_Chr09 consistently exhibited the highest density of variants, accumulating up to 6,483 variants at 200 Gy, suggesting elevated radiosensitivity of this chromosome. Genome‑wide, InDels constituted on average 1.06% of all variants, but this proportion was notably higher in AA_Chr09 and AA_Chr10 (up to 2.17%), indicating chromosome‑specific susceptibility to structural alterations (Fig. 8b; Additional file 4).

Genome-wide distribution of 200 Gy radiation-induced variants across chromosomes in Shuidong mustard. a, total number of variants (SNPs and InDels) per chromosome, with AA_Chr09 showing the highest mutational burden. b, percentage of InDels relative to total variants per chromosome, highlighting structural variation rates against the genomic average (dashed line)

Given the uneven chromosomal distribution, we next asked whether mutations clustered at specific genomic regions. Mutation hotspot analysis identified loci with significantly elevated concentrations of radiation‑induced variants. Hotspot scores at 200 Gy (mean ≈ 1.6) were highly skewed, with a distinct subset of loci showing pronounced clustering against a background of low mutational density—a pattern consistent across all doses (Fig. 9a; Additional file 5). Variant density and InDel proportion were positively correlated in all dose groups (Fig. 9b; Additional file 5), indicating that mutation‑rich regions are also enriched for structural variants. The 25 highest‑scoring hotspots were non‑randomly distributed and recurrently mapped to specific chromosomal coordinates (Fig. 9c), supporting the existence of genomic “fragile sites” preferentially targeted by γ‑radiation. Classification of the genome by mutation density confirmed that hotspot regions dominated the landscape: “Moderate_Hotspots” accounted for 42–43% of the genome, “Strong_Hotspots” for 23–27%, and “Extreme_Hotspots” for 22–27%, while “Normal” regions represented only about 7.5% (Fig. 9d).

To understand the functional implications of these recurrently mutated regions, we performed gene enrichment analysis within the identified hotspots. This revealed a dose‑stable, non‑random pattern, with a conserved core set of genes being significantly overrepresented (Fig. 10a; Additional file 6). The most frequently mutated locus was BjuVB07G20900.1 (enrichment score ~ 132), a homolog of the DNA repair protein REV1. Other top-enriched genes encoded an F‑box/WD‑40 repeat protein (BjuVA01G42840.1), a chaperonin GroEL homolog (BjuVB01G41140.1), and a thymidine kinase (BjuVB02G62730.1), suggesting a coordinated impact on protein homeostasis and genome maintenance (Additional file 7). Notably, all top‑enriched genes were located exclusively within designated hotspot regions and were absent from non‑hotspot segments (Fig. 10b). These results indicate that γ‑radiation preferentially targets a specific set of functionally coherent genomic loci.

Functional enrichment of genes within mutation hotspots

To elucidate the functional implications of the observed mutational landscape, we performed a KEGG pathway enrichment analysis on the genes recurrently mutated across all radiation doses (Fig. 11; Additional file 8). This analysis revealed a highly significant overrepresentation of genes associated with DNA repair, signal transduction, and core metabolic processes. Notably, genes encoding components of DNA repair mechanisms were prominently enriched (Fig. 11). The most significantly affected pathways included ‘Homologous recombination’ (33 genes), ‘Nucleotide excision repair’ (32 genes), and ‘Mismatch repair’ (29 genes), corroborating the finding that γ-radiation imposed a strong selective pressure on maintaining genomic stability. Furthermore, signaling and metabolic networks were substantially disrupted. The ‘Plant MAPK signaling pathway’ (111 mutated genes) and ‘Plant hormone signal transduction’ (80 genes) represented the most extensively perturbed categories, suggesting a targeted disruption of environmental sensing and intracellular communication. Similarly, core metabolic processes such as ‘Carbon metabolism’ (37 genes), ‘Amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism’ (37 genes), and ‘Starch and sucrose metabolism’ (31 genes) exhibited widespread mutational saturation, indicating a broad impact on primary metabolic functions (Fig. 11).

Discussion

This study provides the first integrated phenotypic and genomic analysis of the dose‑dependent effects of γ‑radiation on Shuidong mustard, a regionally important allotetraploid vegetable crop. We observed a biphasic response: doses ≥ 971 Gy (LD₅₀) progressively inhibited seedling development, reducing germination to 62% and fresh weight by 51.5% at 4000 Gy (Figs. 1 and 4), while lower doses (200–500 Gy) were associated with a non‑significant trend toward increased root elongation. This physiological suppression at high doses was paralleled by distinct, dose‑dependent shifts in the spectrum and distribution of genome‑wide mutations, offering a mechanistic perspective on radiation‑induced mutagenesis in this species. The calculated LD₅₀ for seedlings in Shuidong mustard (971 Gy) is notably higher than values reported for some other species, such as wheat (287.80 Gy) [29] and snake gourd (181.85 Gy) [30]. This may reflect a greater innate tolerance to γ‑radiation within the Brassica genus, a notion supported by the documented tolerance of rapeseed to doses as high as 2000 Gy [22]. The varying radiosensitivity among different crops emphasizes the need for specific dose-response relationships for each species and cultivar to ensure successful mutation breeding.

Our genomic findings both align with and refine the established model of radiation mutagenesis. Consistent with prior studies in crops such as rice, where single‑base substitutions (SBSs) constitute the majority of induced variants [31], SBSs were also overwhelmingly predominant in irradiated Shuidong mustard, accounting for ~ 99% of all identified mutations (Table 1). However, unlike the relatively even distribution reported in some systems, mutations in our study were markedly non‑random. We observed a pronounced chromosomal bias, with AA_Chr09 consistently exhibiting the highest variant load (Fig. 8), and identified clear mutation hotspots—a pattern of regional vulnerability also reported in gamma‑irradiated wheat [32]. The dose‑response for total variant number was non‑linear, peaking at 2000 Gy before declining at 4000 Gy. This pattern differs from the monotonic increase with dose reported in some other Brassica studies [22] and may reflect the severe genomic stress and reduced cell viability induced by 4000 Gy in our system, which could limit the recovery of mutant lineages. Furthermore, the spectrum of structural variations shifted with dose: the proportion of small InDels increased gradually, whereas the ratio of large‑fragment InDels (> 5 bp) was highest at the lowest dose (200 Gy) and decreased thereafter. This suggests that the mechanisms generating small insertions/deletions (e.g., polymerase slippage, microhomology‑mediated repair) may be differentially engaged across doses compared to those creating larger deletions (e.g., from mis‑repair of complex double‑strand breaks).

Gene enrichment analysis within mutation hotspots identified a conserved set of genes recurrently affected across all radiation doses. These include a homolog of the DNA repair translesion polymerase REV1 (BjuVB07G20900.1), as well as genes involved in ubiquitin‑mediated proteolysis (e.g., F‑box proteins) and chaperonin function—consistent with the recurrent targeting of protein homeostasis components reported in other irradiated crops. For instance, gamma‑irradiated wheat also showed mutation enrichment in the “protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum” pathway, which is heavily populated by heat‑shock protein genes [32]. Similarly, radiation‑induced structural variations in rapeseed, such as copy‑number changes in Bna.FAE1 and a deleterious deletion in Bna.FAD2.A5, have been shown to directly alter seed‑quality traits [22]. The high mutation frequency observed in these genes in our study (Additional file 7) points to loci that are either functionally important or genomically vulnerable. It should be noted, however, that mutation frequency alone does not establish functional significance; recurrently mutated genes may simply reside in regions that are structurally prone to radiation‑induced damage due to chromatin conformation, replication timing, or other genomic features. Therefore, while the observed pattern is notable, it remains unclear whether mutations in these specific genes mechanistically contribute to phenotypic adaptation or merely mark susceptible genomic territories. Future studies integrating transcriptomics, detailed variant annotation, and functional validation in advanced generations will be essential to determine the biological relevance of these mutations.

The physiological and genomic data are coherently linked by the seed viability assay in our study for the first time. The TTC assay showed an exponential, dose‑dependent decline in metabolic activity (Fig. 6), which correlated strongly with germination rate. This indicates that the physiological decline in seed vigor is a direct reflection of diminishing cellular redox capacity. At doses ≤ 500 Gy, metabolic function was largely maintained, coinciding with a mutational profile dominated by SBSs and small InDels. In contrast, high doses (≥ 2000 Gy) triggered a collapse in viability and were associated with elevated genomic GC content, a potential indicator of widespread DNA fragmentation and selective loss of AT‑rich sequences, alongside a saturation of mutations across critical pathways.

Methodologically, a key strength of this study lies in the experimental design for variant calling. By using a distinct cultivar (‘Tumida’) as the reference genome for the re-sequenced ‘Shuidong’ material, we established a clear baseline that allowed for the effective filtering of inter‑cultivar polymorphisms. This strategy enhances the specificity for identifying putative radiation‑induced variants compared to analyses that rely solely on within‑cultivar comparisons, where background and induced variation are more confounded. Our approach, employing an average sequencing depth of 13 ×, enabled the genome‑wide detection of SNPs and InDels, which is consistent with the scale of variant profiling performed in other polyploid Brassica studies, such as the re-sequencing of M2 rapeseed populations at 12 × coverage to link mutations to seed quality traits [22]. Nevertheless, as noted for complex plant genomes, a higher sequencing depth (e.g., ≥ 30 ×) is often recommended to achieve optimal accuracy and to resolve low‑frequency mosaic mutations [33]. A further limitation is the absence of transcriptomic data, which precludes direct linkage between the observed mutations and alterations in gene expression for key candidate genes (e.g., REV1 homologs). Future integrative genomics studies combining whole‑genome re-sequencing with transcriptome profiling would be valuable to functionally interpret the mutated gene set identified here.

Conclusions

This study delineates the dose‑dependent continuum of γ‑radiation effects in Shuidong mustard, from a low‑dose regime associated with a manageable mutational load to a high‑dose regime characterized by severe physiological injury and widespread genomic disruption. The recurrent targeting of specific genomic regions and functional pathways provides a prioritized list of candidates for investigating mechanisms of radiation sensitivity. For mutation breeding applications, our data suggest that doses of 971 Gy may offer an optimal balance, generating sufficient genetic diversity for selection while preserving seed viability and minimizing detrimental chromosomal alterations.
