From technique mastery to scientific thinking: how open-ended experiments shape medical students' perception of laboratory course roles in China
Authors: Qi Lu, Huan Dou, Sunan Shen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBasic science education is universally acknowledged as a pivotal component of medical education. In China, medical students undergo significant preclinical training but often lack scientific thinking. As China transitions to a knowledge-intensive nation, addressing this gap is crucial. This research aims to qualitatively and quantitatively examine how sophomore medical students perceive the roles of basic laboratory courses, assess whether open-ended experiments can change these perceptions, and qualitatively determine their feelings towards experienced open-ended experiments.MethodsThis study has an interventional design, surveying students before and after an open-ended experiment to evaluate its impact on their perceptions of the roles of the basic laboratory course. Quantitative methods, including frequency analysis, Kendall's W test, the chi-square test, and an independent t test, were used to analyse the rankings of the roles of basic laboratory courses thoroughly. Moreover, qualitative thematic analysis provides insights into students' experiences with the open-ended experiments.ResultsThe study revealed that the students in the historical control groups considered learning to master experimental operation techniques as the most important role of the laboratory courses, whereas the students in the post-intervention groups regarded cultivating scientific thinking as the most crucial role. Compared with the historical control groups, the post-intervention groups attached greater importance to the role of cultivating scientific thinking, which was statistically significant. The main concerns and experiences of the open-ended experiments discussed by participants from the historical control groups were summarized into two themes: “negatively biased feelings of open-ended experiments” and “inappropriate for open-ended experiments at present”. However, the two major themes that emerged from the post-intervention groups are “positively biased feelings of open-ended experiments” and “moderate and appropriate open-ended experiments at present”.ConclusionsThis study revealed that medical students’ perceptions of the roles of laboratory courses were influenced by experimental teaching methods. Open-ended experiments have emerged as effective tools for fostering scientific thinking. This research emphasizes the importance of refining teaching methods in the preclinical phase to foster an environment conducive for enhancing the scientific thinking of Chinese students and the overall effectiveness of medical education.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-024-06325-x.Keywords: Open-ended experiments, Students’ perceptions, Laboratory course, Basic medical education, Scientific thinking

Introduction

Education in basic sciences, such as chemistry, biochemistry, and physiology, is universally acknowledged as a pivotal component of medical education and is deemed essential for fostering a comprehensive understanding of the underlying principles and mechanisms that govern human health and disease. Since the publication of the Flexner Report [1], the role and value of basic science in medical education have been increasingly affirmed by scholars and medical educators. There is a common perspective that the contribution of the basic sciences to the development of medical practitioners goes beyond factual knowledge as preparation for clinical application and serves to develop the thinking skills necessary for successful clinical reasoning and decision-making [2]. Through scientific cultivation, clinicians (such as physicians) can be distinguished from mere technicians. Clinical scientists possess the ability to address poorly structured problems, whereas technicians are limited to following procedures [3]. In this way, students pursuing a clinical medicine major are mandated to embark on their medical training with deep immersion in the fundamental sciences. Typically, they devote half of their undergraduate years to preclinical training, primarily centred on acquiring a solid foundation in basic science knowledge.

This educational model is implicitly based on the hypothesis that medical decision-making on the basis of an understanding of the biomedical sciences results in improved clinical care. Woods and colleagues confirmed this hypothesis, pointing out that this educational model gives future doctors an advantage in medical decision-making, especially in dealing with abnormal cases [4]. Although this point has not been proven beyond doubt [5], the importance of basic science in medical education is unquestionable. However, there is currently a tendency to compress the time for basic science education, suggesting that the Flexner model is not practical enough [6, 7]. Researchers have warned that this is a very short-sighted approach. While it may lead to short-term efficiency gains, it sacrifices the unique cognitive skills that doctors should possess, ultimately leading to stagnation in professional development and social poverty [8].

The unique cognitive skills that doctors should possess are related to the scientific thinking skills used in scientific inquiry [9]. Given the importance of scientific thinking for many domains, scientific thinking has been considered a major goal in science education at all educational levels [10]. Klarh et al. reported that “scientific thinking is a particular form of human problem-solving that involves mental representations of (1) hypotheses about the structure and processes of the natural world and (2) various methods of inquiry used to determine the extent to which those hypotheses are consistent with phenomena” [11]. Scientific thinking involves both conceptual and procedural thinking in science, which can be nurtured through the development of conceptual knowledge and the processes or activities fostering knowledge acquisition [12]. Science educators now recognize that students should be exposed to authentic scientific inquiry to develop students’ scientific thinking styles. However, in many commonplace learning experiences in science education, information is merely presented, leading students to respond in rote manners that bypass genuine scientific thinking [13].

The phenomenon of rote learning is relatively common in the Chinese education system [14] and has a negative effect on science education. Although China attaches great importance to science education, effective science education that can cultivate students' scientific thinking is rare. As a developing country, China has long paid close attention to the educational concepts of developed Western countries. Flexner's thoughts also have a profound influence on medical education in China. Chinese students majoring in clinical medicine embark on their training journey by immersion in the basic sciences, typically through a minimum of 2 years of preclinical training, predominantly focused on the acquisition of basic scientific knowledge. However, biomedical science education in China often focuses more on teaching practical skills rather than fostering a scientific way of thinking and acting. While this issue may exist in other countries as well [15], it is of particular concern for developing China. As China currently faces the transition from a labour-intensive to a knowledge-intensive nation, the role of science education is self-evident. In addition, the Chinese educational environment may possess certain unique characteristics, as modern science did not originate in East China, and traditional culture has had a certain negative impact on science education [16]. A study even revealed that after university education, Chinese students' critical thinking ability and academic skill level declined [17]. Furthermore, a large-scale study on the training and development of medical students in China revealed that from 2019 to 2021, over ten thousand students each year conducted satisfaction assessments of medical education curricula. In this series of three consecutive years of surveys, satisfaction with the basic science courses consistently ranked the lowest among all the courses [18].

With respect to the pedagogy of basic sciences, Flexner advocated for laboratory-based training experiences in medical education over didactic instruction [19]. Indeed, the importance of science laboratories as distinctive learning environments conducive to meaningful student learning has been emphasized in higher education over the years, particularly in the science disciplines [20, 21]. Many researchers have noted that an experimental teaching environment enables students to explore in a scientist-like manner. From the point of doing research, laboratory work is intrinsic to science in general and to the scientist in particular. However, the question of how this same laboratory can be optimally utilized in instructing future scientists, including clinical scientists, remains unsettled and, at times, fiercely debated [20].

In fact, there are conflicting claims about the effects of laboratory activities in science education due to the widespread use of the term “experiment” [22]. Generally, there are two types of laboratory activities. One is formal laboratory, for which alternative names are traditional, structured, convergent, confirmatory, or cookbook laboratories. The other is called the experimental laboratory. Other names for such an approach are open ended, inductive, discovery oriented, unstructured project, or undergraduate research laboratories. These activities could have a positive or negative impact on student learning according to the specifics of the particular activity [23]. In recent years, some studies in the field of life sciences have focused on and revealed the impact of different experimental teaching methods on student learning outcomes. An intriguing finding is that students' objectives for laboratory activities may not align with those of the instructors, which could affect the effectiveness of experimental teaching [24].How to improve teaching methods to correct students' inappropriate views on objectives has become a very valuable direction for research. However, relatively few studies have focused on this topic in the field of medical education in China.

The aim of this study is to explore how Chinese medical students in their second year perceive the roles of basic laboratory classes and their relationships with teaching models, especially that of open-ended experiments. The basic laboratory classes mainly refer to chemistry laboratory classes, biochemistry laboratory classes, and biomedical laboratory classes in Chinese medical schools.

Methods

Context

Pathogen Identification and Immune Cell Analysis Technique (PIICAT)

This study took place from March 2024 to May 2024 in a laboratory course named Pathogen Identification and Immune Cell Analysis Technique (PIICAT) at a top-tier comprehensive university medical school. Microorganisms have the potential to trigger various diseases, leading to alterations in immune organs, cells, and molecules during disease onset. Understanding the correlation between these changes and diseases necessitates the application of cytological and immunological techniques. To enhance students' scientific and cultural acumen, foster innovation, and cultivate creative skills, this experimental course aims to equip students with proficiency in modern medical technology and prepare them for the evolving landscape of medicine. The course will commence with the identification and analysis of pathogens responsible for inducing diseases, followed by an examination of the cellular and molecular immunological functions. Throughout the experimental process, the instructor will provide overarching experimental concepts, primarily serving as a guide to facilitate specific experimental skills training and encourage students to develop independent and critical thinking skills for problem solving. Students will be responsible for designing detailed experimental procedures by researching relevant information. The experimental design emphasized a research-oriented, comprehensive, practical, and innovative approach.

This laboratory course is primarily composed of eight laboratory tasks, with the first seven being confirmatory experiments, and the last one being an open-ended experiment (OEE). In this laboratory course, students conduct experiments in groups of three.

The open-ended experiment (OEE)

This is an exploratory experiment on the unknown “X disease”, which is called “Is ‘Disease X’ definitely coming?” This study is an investigation into the pathogenesis and immunology of emerging novel infectious diseases.

In the molecular medicine laboratory, students are engaged in practical activities as part of the PIICAT course. Specifically, they are conducting experiments related to pathogen molecular biology identification, flow cytometry, and tissue immunofluorescence. By integrating theoretical knowledge, students are tasked with independently designing and executing an investigative experiment aimed at elucidating the pathogenic features and immunological pathogenic mechanisms associated with a specific disease, denoted as “X disease”.

Study design

This study adopts a historical control design, with students randomly selected for comparison before and after the intervention of OEE. Firstly, before the intervention, historical control groups were randomly selected, and data was collected by interviewing them. Following the intervention, additional groups were randomly selected as post-intervention observation groups. To avoid potential test effects, different participants were recruited for interviews before and after the intervention. Furthermore, considering educational equity and ethics, all participants, including those in the historical control groups, were given the opportunity to participate in the OEE course.

This research approach draws on the idea of historical control trial (HCT) methods used in clinical practice. HCTs are favoured due to considerations of feasibility, ethics, and economics. Although historical control design is prone to sample selection bias, outcome evaluation bias, and nondefinitive conclusions, the use of HCTs provides at least a tentative comparison of the experimental intervention with the control [25]. This type of research design, which reveals the effects of educational interventions by comparing historical control groups with post-intervention groups, has also been applied in medical education research [26].

After completing the seven confirmatory experiments, and before the commencement of the open-ended experiment, five groups of students, A1-A5 (as divided for the course), were invited to participate in a survey study as historical control groups. Additionally, upon completion of the open-ended experiment, another five groups of students, B1-B5, were invited to participate in the survey study as post-intervention groups (Table 1). To ensure that the two groups of students were essentially equivalent at baseline, we compared their GPAs prior to their participation in the course and found no significant differences (T = −1.05,p > 0.05)in the average GPA between the historical control groups(M = 4.10,SD = 0.23) and post-intervention groups(M = 4.20,SD = 0.25). In this research design, the open-ended experiment is considered an intervening variable.

Data collection

Compared with the traditional class environment, the lab environment is more complex and typically involves flexible classroom arrangements, contextualized learning activities, and opportunities for collaborative work. In this case, mixed methods of quantitative and qualitative paradigms may be more suitable. We initially employed a quantitative ranking survey to gather participants' perceptions of the roles of laboratory courses, followed by qualitative interviews to offer additional explanations and deeper insights from students regarding their experiences in laboratory classes. In this study, focus group interviews were chosen as the main method of data collection. During the interviews, an interview guide was used (Appendix 1) to help call attention to, and focus on, relevant themes of discussion. The interview guide was validated via a pilot interview, and slight changes were made in the phrasing of the ranking part. The interviews lasted on average one hour, and were conducted by the first and second authors according to the following structure:

Introduction and presentations

During this session, the researchers and participants introduced themselves to each other. There were two researchers: the second author of this study, who is the instructor of the course, and the first author, who was unknown to the participants, thus making self-introduction essential. Before the official start of the survey, the first author clarified to the participants that the purpose of this research was to facilitate curriculum and teaching reforms, thanked them for their contributions, and distributed participation gifts (USB drives worth 100 RMB). The researchers also assured the participants that the research data would not be disclosed externally for any purposes other than research, and that their responses would not be linked to their academic performance. The study commenced in a relaxed atmosphere, with coffee, milk tea, drinks, and fruits made available.

Focusing exercise

A ranking task appeared on the interview guide as a focusing exercise to concentrate the group’s attention and interactions on a particular topic, which was also the only quantitative data used in this study. In this case, this was done by asking the students to rank the importance of the nine roles played by the laboratory courses they experienced. The statements were inspired by Gyllenpalm and Wickman’s work [22]. The participants were first asked to rank the items independently, and then they engaged in discussions with each other. This approach allows every participant's perspective to be presented, and by comparing the similarities and differences of the rankings, it can effectively stimulate discussion.

Discussion session

After ranking, the participants were asked to explain their ranking and whether these roles had been achieved. During the discussion, the researchers facilitated discussions among the students about the experimental teaching models they had experienced and their relationships with the roles of the laboratory courses. If students mentioned open-ended experiments, the researchers would further inquire about their experiences during the open experiments and ask if the students wished to increase the proportion of open-ended experiments.

The interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed verbatim, and anonymised before analysis by the moderator. The assessment of data saturation indicated that it was sufficient, based on the strength of the information power derived from the data [27].

Data analysis

About language

This study conducted interviews in Chinese, taking into consideration the fact that the utilization of one's mother tongue enables participants to articulate their thoughts with greater precision, aligning with the growing trend of employing native languages in qualitative research [28]. The analysis was also performed in the original Chinese language, and the authors approved the translation. We invited the participants to review the text to ensure that the translated version accurately reflected their intended meaning.

Quantitative approach

For the ranking question, the respondents independently ranked a set of choices. We also asked them to add other open-ended responses, but no other choices were generated. We conducted a frequency analysis on the rankings and calculated the average score for each choice. Inter-rater reliability for the ranking question was measured via Kendall’s W [29]. Kendall's W is a statistical measure used to assess the degree of agreement among multiple raters evaluating the same set of items. Its value ranges from 0 to 1, with values closer to 1 indicating a higher level of agreement among the raters. The statistical significance was measured via a follow-up chi-square test. If the chi-square test shows a significance level (p value) lower than 0.05, it indicates that the inter-rater agreement is significant. An independent samples t test was used to determine whether there was a significant difference before and after experiencing an open-ended experiment for each choice. Before conducting the independent samples t test, we performed a test for homoscedasticity to ensure that the variances of the two groups were equal. Statistical computations were performed with SPSS 16.0.

Qualitative approach

We employed an inductive approach to analyse our qualitative data in a bottom-up manner. The data collected in the interviews were analysed via a thematic analysis [30]. A thematic analysis involves systematically identifying patterns of meaning across a dataset through the process of coding. These codes are then used to develop themes, which are the central organizing concepts that capture shared meanings within the data. Ultimately, these themes are contextualized and applied to address the research questions.

Before the analysis, the authors discussed the topics associated with the research interest and then analysed the transcripts independently. After the independent analysis, the authors resolved coding discrepancies and chose more appropriate coding and themes. The participants were asked to read and provide their comments on the analysis while also verifying or disputing its accuracy and authenticity in reflecting their experiences, views, or practices. The transcripts were organized and analysed using Nvivo 12.

Results

Medical students’ perceptions of the roles of basic laboratory courses

Historical control groups

The participants in the historical control groups were asked to separately rank the roles of basic laboratory courses. A moderate level of agreement among these 15 respondents was found to be statistically significant (Kendall's W = 0.54, χ2(8, N = 15) = 64, p < 0.001). Among these roles, mastering experimental operation techniques, handling processes and methods used in scientific research, and learning about the science behind technical and natural phenomena in our everyday life, were ranked as the top three choices (Table 3). Learning about the nature of science, reproducing the discovery process of scientific knowledge, and learning about the historical development of science, were deemed less important roles.

Independent samples T test results

To compare whether there was any change in the participants' choices before and after they participated in the open-ended experiment in this course, an independent sample t test was conducted. Negatively biased feelings of open-ended experiments

In this study, students from historical control groups were interviewed prior to their participation in the open-ended experiment of this course. The interviews revealed that all of them indicated that they had experienced only one open-ended experiment, which they encountered during the biochemistry laboratory course. Students' primary feelings towards open-ended experiments were that they are time-consuming and laborious and disappointing, yet they also leave a profound impression.

Time-consuming and laborious

In the interviews, the students reported that they felt that open-ended experiments were quite demanding in terms of time and energy. This was mainly because they needed to design the experiments independently, and the continuous trial and error process would incur significant time costs.

"We all arrived at the lab at 1:30 pm, but some students felt that the experiment hadn't even started by 6 pm. The main reason was that there were issues with their initial experimental design. Then, halfway through, they suddenly realized there was a problem with the design, so what they had done before was not valid and they had to redo it. They didn't even have time for dinner. " [A3-2]

Disappointing

There were also students who expressed disappointment with this open-ended experiment. They stated that since it was their first time encountering such an experiment, quite a few of them chose to make only minor modifications to the original experimental cases. This made the open-ended experiment not significantly different from the previous confirmatory experiments in form, failing to spark much interest among the students.

"The biochemistry experiment last semester included a slightly more open-ended experiment, but I felt that perhaps most people were not very familiar with this part. Therefore, the changes made to the original experiment were relatively minor, such as switching experimental materials. And there were probably no significant changes in the experimental methods. " [A1-1]

"The experiment in our textbook originally used catalase from potatoes, and then the teacher asked us to design our own experiments based on this original experiment. Many groups just changed the materials, for example, using pig liver or carrots, which did not show much innovation. " [A1-3]

"I feel like I didn't learn much from that open-ended experiment." [A4-1]

Impressive

The open-ended experiments left a deep impression on the students. They compared the experience of open-ended experiments with that of confirmatory experiments to express their feelings about the different experiences.

"Compared to previous confirmatory experiments, where teachers would give us all the steps and we would just follow them, the open-ended experiments left a more profound impression, though the entire process could be quite challenging and painful. " [A3-2]

Inappropriate for open-ended experiments at present

However, when researchers inquired about whether it is necessary to introduce and increase the number of open-ended experiments in the basic medical science curriculum, students generally responded negatively.

Lack of professional proficiency

Some students believed that they were not yet equipped with the necessary abilities to conduct open-ended experiments.

"We may need to master the most basic techniques first and then conduct open-ended experiments later. I think it's not yet the time for us to design experiments independently. Our level of expertise might not be sufficient yet." [A5-3]

"Just like someone who can't swim diving straight into a river might drown, we might not know what to do at all if we try to conduct an open-ended experiment without the necessary skills." [A2-3]

Open-ended experiments are not suitable for lab courses

Some students believed that open-ended experiments should be genuine scientific research experiments, which are more suitable for advanced undergraduate or graduate stages and are not suitable for basic laboratory courses.

"I feel that this arrangement is too rushed. It's not appropriate to engage in these open-ended experiments before we have learned enough. At this stage, we should eliminate all open-ended experiments." [A4-3]

"I believe that experiments conducted in a laboratory setting are still different from the authentic ones. The main purpose of a lab course is to master experimental techniques, and accordingly, the teaching approach should involve following predetermined steps in an orderly manner. This allows students to properly learn and master the techniques. " [A2-3]

Confirmatory experiments as priorities

After conducting the interviews, the students generally believed that the current stage should focus primarily on confirmatory experiments. Confirmatory experiments are beneficial for understanding experimental operation techniques, laying a solid foundation for their future.

"I agree that confirmatory experiments are quite important, because I believe that mastering the techniques and skills of operation is crucial and fundamental to the success of an experiment. Without these skills, even if one has ideas for an experiment, it may not be feasible in practice. " [A5-2]

Positively biased feelings of open-ended experiments

The students from the post-intervention groups were interviewed after completing the open-ended experiments in their immunology lab classes. They had participated in two main open-ended experiments, one being the open-ended experiment in the biochemistry lab class mentioned earlier, and the other being the open-ended experiment in the immunology class, which is the focus of this study. Owing to their recent participation in the open-ended experiment in the immunology class, the students from the post-intervention groups primarily focused their discussion on this experiment when sharing their experiences and feelings about open-ended experiments. Their main feelings about open-ended experiments differed from those of the students in the historical control groups, and they found it thought-provoking, challenging, and somewhat uncertain.

Thought-provoking

In the interviews, the students mentioned that the open-ended experiments in the immunology class allowed them to be actively involved in the experimental design process. During this process, they engaged in extensive thinking, which helped cultivate their scientific thinking. This experimental format fostered a deeper understanding of the scientific method and encouraged them to think critically about experimental design and outcomes.

"I feel that in the past, confirmatory experiments mainly involved the teacher explaining what to do at each step, and then the students would simply follow those instructions. There was no opportunity for students to think independently. However, with the open-ended experiments this semester, we actively research and read relevant literature, and then design the experiments ourselves. I feel that this approach is more effective in cultivating our scientific thinking." [B4-3]

"The open-ended experiment has allowed me to be part of the experimental design process, which is incredibly interesting. I've truly experienced the beauty of thinking. " [B1-2]

Challenging

The students who participated in the interviews generally reflected that open-ended experiments were more challenging, requiring them to actively engage in the process, independently design experiments, and participate in the inquiry process, which gave them a sense of difficulty and pressure.

"With confirmatory experiments, even if we didn't fully understand the logic of the experiment during class, we could still follow the steps and complete the experiment, and then gain understanding afterwards. However, with open-ended experiments, from the first step to the last, we need to have a clear understanding of what we are doing at each step and what our objectives are. I find this to be quite challenging. " [B5-3]

Uncertain

Through interviews, we found that students are still influenced by their previous mindset when conducting open-ended experiments, always seeking a correct answer. Students tend to feel anxious about the uncertainty of the results of open-ended experiments.

"When it's a confirmatory experiment, the result is essentially predetermined, so you know for sure what outcome you'll get. However, with open-ended experiments, even after you obtain a result, you might still have doubts and consider whether your result is accurate or not. " [B5-3]

"If my result turns out to be different from everyone else's, I would definitely doubt if there's something wrong with my approach. " [B5-1]

"I wouldn't assume that I'm right; I'd inevitably assume that I'm wrong. " [B5-2]

Moderate and appropriate open-ended experiments at present

Compared with the generally negative attitudes towards open-ended experiments among the students in the historical control groups, the students in the post-intervention groups showed a certain degree of acceptance towards them. When researchers inquired about the current setup of open-ended experiments, students generally believed that an appropriate proportion of open-ended experiments could be set, but they should be arranged after confirmatory experiments, and attention should be given to the degree of openness.

Need to control the quantity

In the interviews, the students expressed that although open-ended experiments are helpful for cultivating scientific thinking, they are challenging and time-consuming. Therefore, they suggested that it would be sufficient to incorporate 1–2 open-ended experiments in each experimental course during the basic medical science stage.

"I think having one or two open-ended experiments in a course is enough, which is within our capacity." [B4-3]

"Certainly, having all experiments as open-ended ones would not be feasible, as they can be quite challenging. " [B1-1]

Need to be presented later

The students noted that open-ended experiments should be set after the confirmatory experiments. Confirmatory experiments aid in mastering experimental operation techniques and laying a solid foundation, whereas open-ended experiments serve as a more advanced level of training on the basis of that foundation.

"I believe it would be relatively reasonable to arrange such open-ended experiments towards the end of the semester. In the beginning, we should mainly focus on confirmatory experiments to cultivate our operational skills, and then the final open-ended experiments can help us develop our experimental design capabilities. It's important to lay a solid foundation first, as the difficulty from 0 to 1 is different from that of advancing from 1 to 2. " [B1-3]

Need to pay attention to the degree of openness

Some students noted that fully open-ended experiments can make them feel overwhelmed. Therefore, the degree of openness needs to be considered, and sufficient guidance from teachers is required during the experimental process.

"Fully open-ended experiments may be too challenging for some groups to complete. Providing semi-open-ended experiments would reduce the pressure on students and make the tasks more manageable. " [B3-2]

"With our current skill level, fully open-ended experiments can indeed be quite challenging and confusing. We really need the guidance of our teachers to help us through the process and ensure we are on the right track. " [B2-3]

Discussion

This study investigated the impact of open-ended experiments on students' perceptions of the roles of laboratory courses in the basic medical science stage. The research revealed that there were differences in the ranking of the importance of laboratory course roles between the student groups surveyed before they participated in the open-ended experiment (historical control groups) and the student groups surveyed after they participated (post-intervention groups). The historical control groups believed that mastering experimental operation techniques was the most important role of the basic-stage experimental course, whereas the post-intervention groups considered cultivating scientific thinking to be the most crucial role. Compared with the historical control groups, the post-intervention groups significantly perceived cultivating scientific thinking as more important. Additionally, this study also examined participants' experiences and attitudes towards open-ended experiments. The study revealed that students in the post-intervention groups significantly improved their experience with open-ended experiments, and their acceptance of open-ended experiments also increased remarkably.

Chinese medical students’ scientific thinking: deficiency and development

The research revealed that, prior to the intervention, mastering experimental operation techniques received the most emphasis from the students. This discovery aligns with some previous research findings, suggesting that students tend to prioritize specific and tangible benefits [34]. The reason why students may place more emphasis on technical goals rather than scientific goals could be related to China's cultural traditions. Although China has a brilliant history of ancient science and technology, it is primarily a history of technology, and has not naturally evolved to the modern scientific stage. For a long time, the Chinese cultural tradition has lacked objective conditions for modern scientific elements, and our technological development and educational tradition are deeply marked by technological thinking, which is determined by our traditional agricultural civilization and handicraft economic form. In essence, “science” is a discipline (or several branches of disciplines), whereas technology is not. Technology concerns itself primarily with the application of scientific principles to meet human needs, creating tangible products and tools rather than epistemological questions. To a large extent, technology belongs to the realm of “tacit knowledge” that comes from repeated practice and proficiency, lacking the unique logical structure and other essential components of a discipline [35].

These findings also reflect the naive thoughts that students have in terms of cognition, as well as their lack of scientific thinking. This may be related to the exam-oriented education environment, as existing research has confirmed the negative impact of such an environment on scientific inquiry activities [36]. China's education system focuses on exam-oriented education, with a widespread phenomenon of overreliance on memorization and test-taking skills. This educational model emphasizes standardized testing and competition rather than fostering students' creativity and critical thinking abilities. Chinese students have been educated primarily in an exam-oriented system for decades during their primary and secondary school years, where the mindset of seeking standard answers is deeply ingrained. The uncertain results of open-ended experiments make them feel uneasy, resulting in psychological insecurity. As a result, Chinese students may lack the ability to independently learn and inquire, which is a significant obstacle to pursuing scientific creation.

However, on the basis of the findings of this study, students' scientific thinking can be developed. By being exposed to open-ended experimental environments, students' perceptions of laboratory courses can change, and their cognitive abilities can be enhanced. The results of this study echo those of previous studies on epistemological beliefs among medical students, which revealed that sophomores' epistemological beliefs may be undergoing a transition from certainty to uncertainty [37, 38]. This transition towards a more sophisticated epistemological stance can be supported and accelerated through educational practices that encourage reflection on the nature of knowledge, the process of scientific discovery, and the role of uncertainty in scientific inquiry. Medical educators are responsible for creating an environment that fosters the development of scientific thinking for undergraduate students in their early years. As Flexner once explicitly noted, “He does not have to be a passive learner, just because it is too early for him to be an original explorer. He can actively master and securely fix scientific technique and method in the process of acquiring the already known [39].”

Transformation of teaching methods: effectiveness and necessity

This study revealed that laboratory teaching methods can influence students' perceptions of the roles of laboratory courses. Students tended to associate open-ended experimental teaching methods with the cultivation of scientific thinking. By virtue of their design, open-ended experiments encourage students to actively engage in the experimental design process. The students are presented with the opportunity to explore, hypothesize, test, and refine their understanding, thus gaining a more comprehensive grasp of the scientific method. This finding is consistent with previous research findings, confirming the advantages of open-ended experiments [40]. However, we also discovered that the majority of experiments in the basic medical education stage are primarily verification-based, which is consistent with the findings of previous studies [41].This teaching method has strengthened students' emphasis on technical gains while weakening the scientific inquiry aspect of laboratory courses. This current situation indicates that the improvement of teaching methods is imperative.

Flexner has stated that “(Scientific) Investigation and (medical) practice are thus one in spirit, method, and object…for the hospital is itself in the fullest sense a laboratory [42].” The transfer of learning theory of Perkins and Salomon also suggests that transfer occurs when learning in one context, or with one set of materials, impacts performance in another context, or with other related materials [43]. Although the transfer of learning from academic to practical settings is a complex process, some studies have shown that when students are exposed to problem-based learning and active learning strategies in the preclinical years, they demonstrate improved clinical reasoning and decision-making skills later in their medical training [44]. Flexner also emphasized that “An active apperceptive relation must be established and maintained between laboratory and clinical experience. Such a relation cannot be one-sided; it will not spontaneously set itself up in the last two years if it is deliberately suppressed in the first two” [45].Therefore, it is necessary to create an environment for students to cultivate scientific thinking during the basic medical stage so that they are more likely to transfer these thinking skills to a clinical context in later years.

Limitations

This study employed a historical control design, which has certain limitations, particularly the potential for sample bias. Although this study attempted to minimize possible biases through random sampling, the same experiment should be conducted in similar classes in the future to improve the validity of the research findings. Additionally, this study adopted a mixed-methods approach that combines both qualitative and quantitative research methods and was conducted with 30 students. However, the sample size could be expanded in the quantitative research section to include all the students participating in this course. Future improvements should be made in this area to enhance the persuasiveness of the research results.

Conclusions

The perceptions of medical students towards the roles of laboratory courses are crucial, as they reflect the influence of experimental teaching in medical education. This study revealed that experimental teaching methods influence medical students' perceptions of laboratory course roles in medical education. Open-ended experiments are effective in increasing students' emphasis on the achievement of "cultivating scientific thinking". The teaching method of open-ended experiments is highly important for improving the quality of science education for Chinese medical students. It is essential to refine teaching methods during the preclinical phase of medical education to create an environment that fosters the development of scientific thinking in students, which will, in turn, assist them in their clinical practice.
