Studying COVID-19 transmission in US state prisons using an agent-based modelling approach: a simulation study
Authors: Allison Lydia Owens, Mike Fliss, Lauren Brinkley-Rubinstein
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesWe aim to use an agent-based model to accurately predict the spread of COVID-19 within multiple US state prisons.DesignWe developed a semistochastic transmission model of COVID-19.SettingFive regional state-owned prisons within North Carolina.ParticipantsSeveral thousand incarcerated individuals.Primary and secondary outcome measuresWe measured (1) the observed and simulated average daily infection rate of COVID-19 for each prison studied in 30-day intervals, (2) the observed and simulated average daily recovery rate from COVID-19 for each prison studied in 30-day intervals, (3) the mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) of each prison’s summary statistics and the simulated results and (4) the parameter estimates of key predictors used in the model.IntroductionThe COVID-19 pandemic disparately affected incarcerated populations in the USA, with severe morbidity and infection rates across the country. In response, many predictive models were developed to help mitigate risk. However, these models did not feature the systemic factors of prisons, such as vaccination rates, populations and capacities (to determine overcrowding) and design and were not generalisable to other prisons.MethodsAn agent-based model that used geospatial contact networks and compartmental transmission dynamics was built to create predictive microsimulations that simulated COVID-19 outbreaks within five North Carolinian regional prisons between July 2020 and June 2021. The model used the characteristics of an outbreak’s initial case size, a given facility’s capacity and its incarcerated vaccination rate as additional parameters alongside traditional susceptible-exposed-infected-recovered transmission dynamics. By fitting the model to each prison’s data using approximate Bayesian computation methods, we derived parameter estimates that reasonably modelled real-world results. These individualised estimates were then averaged to produce generalised parameter estimates for North Carolina state prisons overall.ResultsOur model had a mean average MAPE score of 23.0 across all facilities, meaning that it reasonably forecasted facilities’ average daily positive and recovery rates of COVID-19. Our model estimated an average incarcerated vaccination rate of 54% across all prisons (with a 95% CI of ±0.12). In addition, the prisons of this study were estimated to be operating at 90% of their capacity on average (95% CI ±0.16). Given the high levels of COVID-19 observed in these prisons, which averaged over one-third positive tests on respective 1-day maxima, we conclude that vaccination levels were not sufficient in curbing COVID-19 outbreaks, and high occupancy levels likely exacerbated the spread of COVID-19 within prisons.In addition, data gaps in facilities without recorded daily testing resulted in poor spread predictions, demonstrating how important consistent data release practices are in incarcerated settings for accurate tracking and prediction of outbreaks.ConclusionThe findings of this study better quantify how spatial contact networks and facility-level characteristics unique to congregate living facilities can be used to predict infectious disease spread. Our approach also highlights the need for increased vaccination efforts and potential capacity reductions to mitigate COVID-19 transmission in prisons.Keywords: COVID-19, Prisons, Epidemiology, STATISTICS & RESEARCH METHODS

Introduction

During the first year of the COVID-19 pandemic, incarcerated populations in the USA were extremely impacted, with infection rates that were 5–6 times higher and mortality rates that were 2–3 times higher than the general population.1

Solitary confinement was an often-used mitigation strategy, and the use of solitary confinement increased by 500% to impact 300 000 people during the COVID-19 pandemic.2 A US-based study of those in state prisons found that 86% of incarcerated respondents spent more than 20 hours under lockdown a day and that 50% were in facility-instituted lockdowns for over 3 months.3 Overcrowding, a condition of confinement that can contribute to disease spread, was also common, with 26%–44% of people who were incarcerated being housed in prisons that were overcrowded.4

With overcrowding making social distancing impossible in many carceral settings,3 the North Carolina (NC) Department of Adult Correction suspended visitation, work release and educational programmes early into the pandemic.5 Movement was also further limited by the quarantine of all incarcerated people for 14 days on admittance within state-run facilities.5 These instituted lockdowns were comparable to mass solitary confinement due to their length and restrictive quality. In NC specifically, incarcerated people were transferred to other carceral facilities, increasing the strain on capacity, after occurrences of large outbreaks of COVID-19 happened.5 Many incarcerated people were otherwise kept within overcrowded dormitories, with the NC Sentencing and Policy Advisory Commission predicting that 3000 incarcerated people were held over the state’s Standard Operating Capacity in 2020.6 Considering these factors, prisons and jails as congregate living facilities presented unique challenges in mitigating the spread of COVID-19, including vaccination, overcrowding and solitary confinement concerns.

Infectious disease modelling of pandemics is traditionally done through compartmental models, which split populations into categories based on biological states of infection. The Kermack-McKendrick SIR model7 has been used to model communicable diseases, in which the population is split into susceptible, infected and recovered individuals. Variations of this model have been applied to specific large urban jail populations, finding reproduction ratios to decrease with the simulation of various health interventions.8 However, most of these studies only use dynamic transmission models, which assume homogeneous mixing within their populations, which is an inaccurate reflection of the incarcerated population during the COVID-19 pandemic. In addition, with the sole use of biological transmission models, facility-level characteristics, such as a facility’s geographical layout, capacity rate and interaction between staff and incarcerated people, cannot be included.9 These characteristics and their impact on COVID-19 transmission cannot be well explained without a spatial understanding of the facility being incorporated into modelling efforts.

Studies have been done to categorise prisons by these characteristics and their COVID-19 outcomes,10 revealing low-outbreak, high-outbreak and high-death profiles. However, the current modelling techniques of prisons cannot be highly generalised, especially to non-urban or less populous jails,9 meaning they can serve less populations. This issue is further pronounced when considering how county jails, state facilities and federal prisons differ in leadership and policy implementation, including health interventions and surveillance.

Using agent-based modelling as an alternative to traditional transmission models can generalise model capabilities while including facility-level characteristics. Agent-based modelling integrates compartmental transmission models with stochastic simulated interactions of individuals within a larger population to predict disease spread. In this study, we develop an agent-based prediction model for COVID-19 spread within five NC prisons of various populations and profiles and calculate average jail parameters for these predictions. With the aim of providing useful predictions to a wider range of policy makers and carceral staff, we focus on the facility-level characteristics of overcrowding, vaccination and jail layouts. We intend for this model to be used by stakeholders in carceral settings, including carceral staff, physicians and others involved in the decision-making process of mitigating disease risk.

Methods

Overview of methods

We built an agent-based, simulation-based model in RStudio to predict COVID-19 transmission in a general prison setting. We used transmission dynamic equations to calculate the spread of disease from incarcerated people, referred to as ‘agents’, that were geographically spaced in dormitory settings throughout the simulated prison setting. We then used the following parameters to predict disease transmission: the initial number of people exposed to COVID-19 (defined as the ‘initial case size’), the facility’s capacity percentage (the ‘overcrowding’ parameter), the facility’s vaccination percentage of incarcerated individuals, the latent period (‘time of incubation’) and the time to recovery of an individual agent. The latent period and time to recovery were predictors adapted from previous models;8 the vaccination and overcrowding parameters were created using various data sources,11 12 and initial case size parameters were determined from relevant literature.13

The COVID Prison Project was a registry data project started to increase public awareness of COVID-19 in prisons through the collection and aggregation of COVID-19 testing data from various state and federal carceral systems. We used incarcerated infection-related data collected by the COVID Prison Project11 to calibrate the model’s parameters for each prison and conduct sensitivity analysis. We created a Latin hypercube space for parameter identification and used approximate Bayesian computing to identify the best parameters for each facility. We then divided the facilities’ data into training and testing sets and evaluated the model’s efficacy at predicting the average daily infection rate and recovery rate for each facility in 30-day increments.

Patient and Public Involvement

Incarcerated individuals were included in this study through de-identified data. The public will be involved in the dissemenation of this study’s findings to correctional facilities, incarcerated health scholars, and incarcerated individuals and impacted communities.

Facility selection

For this study’s purpose, we examined the data of 63 prisons, jails and carceral facilities in NC collected between 14 July 2020 and 21 June 2021. As demographics were not reported in the data collected by the COVID Prison Project, we processed all facility data equally across sociodemographic groups. 53 of 63 facilities reported model-required population counts. We found a daily average of 28% of incarcerated residents testing positive for COVID-19 (n=53 facilities) over a 1-year period. We standardised the population for each facility across time, so days where populations were unreported could still be included. We then created a testing average metric, μT= ∑ t1p+t2p+…+tip, where μT is the average percentage of incarcerated people tested daily at the facility across time, p is the population of the facility, and ti is the number of tests administered to incarcerated people on a given day. We then disqualified facilities that had a testing average metric of >1, as the model assumed single testing for each agent once a day, and we did not want to complicate analysis with facilities that had unequal amounts of tests for incarcerated people per day. This left 43 facilities, with the mean testing average metric of the remaining facilities being 68%.

We then selected a sample of facilities (n=5 facilities) with the highest testing average metrics of various sizes and capabilities. These facilities’ data were used to fit and calibrate the model. There was a weak correlation (R2= 0.37) between the testing average metric and the amount of positive COVID-19 tests, so we did not select facilities with abnormally high levels of COVID-19 transmission. There was no specific eligibility criterion in the reporting of participant data; all incarcerated people tested for COVID-19 within these facilities were included in our analysis. The selected facilities had various populations, days of recorded data and incarcerated testing averages. On average, the testing average metric of these facilities was high (85±3%), with a wide range of population sizes (mean 583.2, IQR 314 people). The days of available data greatly varied by over a month (mean 277.8, IQR 39 days), which was a factor in our decision to run shorter microsimulations to account for the lack of data.

We ultimately selected the following prisons to be analysed by our model: Alexander Carceral Institution, Craggy Carceral Institution, Foothills Carceral Institute, Pamlico Carceral Institution and Wilkes Carceral Center. All facilities were state prisons located in NC and varied in population size (263 to 1100). Since official reporting aggregates by state-wide population, direct comparison of the demographic composition of these facilities was not possible. However, the NC prison population at the time was reported to be 95% male, with the age distribution mainly between 26 years and 50 years, and race demographics reported to be 50% Black, 40% white and 8%–10% other minorities.14 15

Although the sample of facilities is not guaranteed to be statistically representative of the entire NC prison system, important dimensions of facility diversity are captured. Alexander Correctional Institution is a large, close-custody prison in western NC,16 while Craggy represents a medium-security facility in the Asheville area.17 Foothills Correctional Institution is distinct for housing youthful offenders, producing a younger demographic profile.18 Pamlico Correctional Institution reflects a medium-security prison in the eastern coastal region,19 and Wilkes Correctional Center represents a smaller, minimum-security institution in the west.20 Together, these facilities span different custody levels, regions and population profiles. Such variation likely corresponds to differences in demographic composition and operational constraints, enhancing the generalisability of our findings.

Model description

We modified a traditional susceptible-exposed-infected-recovered (SEIR) framework in which all agents were initialised as susceptible or exposed to COVID-19 and could then move to the exposed, infected and recovered categories based on the model’s parameters in a semistochastic way to represent infection.

For each simulation, we initialised 100 arbitrary agents and created a grid space for all agents to exist within. Simulated agents represented different-sized clusters of incarcerated people depending on the population of the facility being modelled, with one simulated agent representing the transmission behaviour of two incarcerated people in the least populous facility and 11 incarcerated people in the most populous facility.

Figure 1 outlines the transmission dynamics for a simulated agent. Each agent was initialised with the following attributes: grid coordinates, a transmission state, a list of other agents as possible infection contacts, and their date of entering the facility. All agents were assumed to enter the facility simultaneously (day 0). Agents were then either designated as initial cases, meaning they started the simulation as exposed to COVID-19, or otherwise initialised as susceptible. During the pandemic, asymptomatic testing was not reported to be widely implemented,13 and unknown amounts of population fluctuation and staff interactions occurred, so the initial case size parameter was created to capture some of the variable COVID-19 exposure. We also implemented barriers within the simulation to mimic dormitory settings, decreasing transmission. The simulation would then run for 30 ‘cycles’ or days. After each simulation ran, two outcomes were calculated:

the average daily infected rate of the facility, μI = ∑ i1+i2+….+inn where in is the number of infected agents divided by the total number of agents within the facility on the nth cycle iteration.

the average daily infected rate of the facility, μI = ∑ i1+i2+….+inn where in is the number of infected agents divided by the total number of agents within the facility on the nth cycle iteration.

the average daily recovery rate of the facility, μR = ∑ r1+r2+…+rnn where rn is the number of recovered agents divided by the total number of agents within the facility on the nth cycle iteration.

the average daily recovery rate of the facility, μR = ∑ r1+r2+…+rnn where rn is the number of recovered agents divided by the total number of agents within the facility on the nth cycle iteration.

We then used these outcomes to compare the model results to the observed results for each facility to assess the model’s effectiveness in evaluating the outcome of COVID-19 transmission through these variables. We chose both the infection and recovery rates to represent the outcome of COVID-19 transmission to create a more holistic picture and use as much available data as possible. From each facility, a randomly selected 30-day interval of data was used as a training dataset to calculate an observed average daily infection rate and recovery rate to be comparable to the model. The training set data was randomly selected to not introduce possible bias from selecting earlier or later COVID-19 outbreaks. As the training datasets only included consecutive days, they did not represent all seasonal changes or outbreaks; however, all data used in the development of the model showed a clear increase in COVID-19 prevalence, which was representative of the trend observed in the testing sets. 11% of all available data was used in the development of the model. The model results were predictive of 30-day observed intervals occurring after the dates of the respective training dataset for each facility.

To do this, we set a threshold (0.01), so parameter combinations that yielded simulations with equal or lower scores were then kept for each facility. The parameter combination that yielded the simulation with the median score underneath the threshold was designated as the best calibration of the model for the given facility. Model testing

With the model calibrated for each facility, we took the remaining data and split it into testing datasets of 30 consecutive days to then test the model’s accuracy in predicting COVID-19 transmission using the parameters based on facility-level characteristics. There were 46 testing datasets in total for the five facilities. We also noted whether a major outbreak, which we defined as an increase of 20 or more infections within a 30-day period, occurred during each testing dataset’s duration. We believe the testing sets are representative of the spread of COVID-19, as all facilities had a minimum of 60 days of reported data, and this data included different seasonal changes and many outbreak fluctuations. There was no difference in the collection, eligibility criteria, outcomes or predictors of the testing data and training data for this study.

For each testing set, we calculated the absolute percentage error of the average daily infection rate and the average daily recovery rate. We then averaged these absolute percentage errors with equal weighting to create a mean absolute percentage error (MAPE), as derived below: μAPE = 0.5⋅μIM-μI0μIO⋅100+0.5⋅μRM-μROμRO⋅100. We also calculated the average MAPE for each facility to understand how well the facility was modelled overall.

Results

Average MAPE values were compared with standard ranges established in the literature.26 For MAPE scores, highly accurate forecasting is shown by MAPE <10, good forecasting by a score between 10 and 20 and reasonable forecasting by a score between 20 and 50.26 The model provided highly accurate forecasting for Alexander and Pamlico Correctional Institution (CI), good forecasting for Foothills CI, reasonable forecasting for Wilkes Correctional Center (CC) and inaccurate forecasting for Craggy CC. The model had a mean average MAPE score of 23.0 across all facilities, meaning that it reasonably forecasted facilities’ average daily positive and recovery rates of COVID-19(figure 6). The inaccurately modelled facility Craggy Carceral Center had significantly higher MAPE values for testing sets with and without major outbreaks. We hypothesise this is due to a significant difference in repeated entries, with over 100 days of 0 reported COVID-19 positive tests and 100 days of 200 plus reported COVID-19 positive tests. We included a facility with missing data to understand the importance of the data structure and quality on the model.

The average MAPE value for Wilkes CC is especially high due to one of the facility’s testing sets containing a large outbreak compared with steady transmission in the other sets. However, there is little variation in MAPE scores of the facility’s testing sets, emphasising the model’s robustness. Overall, Foothills CI performed most consistently in testing due to having the least variation between its testing sets. Facilities with more available data, such as Alexander CI and Pamlico CI, had more variation but overall better MAPE scores. Craggy CC’s MAPE scores were significantly higher, as this facility had the least amount of data available.

Discussion

Using agent-based modelling, we found that the incorporation of facility-level characteristics in modelling COVID-19 transmission within prisons yielded predictions similar to the observed COVID-19 spread within multiple prison facilities. We also found that creating a model generalisable to multiple facilities created important parameter estimates to help better understand the conditions under which the COVID-19 epidemic happened. Our key finding was that the model reasonably forecasted the average infection and recovery rates of COVID-19 outbreaks in multiple facilities. Using multiple calibrated parameters, we found the most important factors in model performance to be the vaccination rate, latent period and recovery period. Out of non-biological parameters, vaccination rate had a dominating importance over initial case size or overcrowding. While the latent period and recovery period could be standardised for future models, clear evidence showed that increasing the vaccination rate and decreasing the initial case size and overcrowding would mitigate COVID-19 transmission. In addition, these data suggest that further work should investigate the weight of various facility-level characteristics in modelling diseases within multiple facilities.

This model provides insight into how to produce short-term predictions of the spread of COVID-19 within individual or multiple prisons, which opens opportunities in the use of infectious disease modelling to make faster, targeted decisions for specific population groups. In future works, more sophisticated models with different variables (such as staffing or population fluctuation) could be produced, and interventions based on the insights of models like this could be designed to help mitigate disease spread within prison populations.

Policy implications

There are many policy implications of this study, including the importance of data availability, insights into the best practices for outbreak data tracking and overall policy inferences to limit the human toll of outbreaks. Consistent, thoroughly defined data collection is necessary for evidence-based approaches to be designed for incarcerated populations during epidemics. The most important facility-level variables to be collected during epidemics are population counts and capacities for analysis of overcrowding, in addition to how and when a facility deems a person as ‘recovered’. Many facilities did not have the transparency and consistency of data collection and reporting necessary to create models to help make facility-specific predictions of COVID-19 spread, limiting the possible effectiveness of this study.

More accurate models can be created to help mitigate the spread of COVID-19 and other diseases by expanding the type of data collected by facilities and improving outbreak data tracking. This includes but is not limited to official counts of those exposed to COVID-19 (or future communicable and/or infectious diseases), those who entered facilities without contact tracing, and asynchronous testing of all individuals within the facility.8

In addition, as previous studies have shown, depopulation is an important effort to stop the spread of COVID-19, and transparent, daily reporting of prison populations could help this factor be used in future studies and health-related decisions. Our study is also consistent with the understandings of the importance of vaccinations in mitigating the spread of COVID-19. Ultimately, we hypothesise increasing vaccination of incarcerated populations and depopulating at or near-capacity facilities will help decrease the spread of COVID-19.

Limitations

This study has modelling, data and scope limitations. We used a singular type of model (agent-based model with five main parameters) on multiple facilities and assumed heterogeneous mixing of the population. We also used a limited scope of facilities due to data concerns and a lack of facilities with high percentages of daily testing for incarcerated persons. While the exact demographic and characteristic representativeness of the included facilities in NC could not be determined, the included facilities were diverse in facility type, geographic location, population size and security levels. Future work could strengthen representativeness by analysing a stratified sample or incorporating state-wide facility-level data if made available. The sample size of the training sets used in the development of the model may not be enough to be representative of the facilities used. In addition, the model assumes lockdown conditions, which do not allow for movement within the facility, and does not explicitly consider staff COVID-19 prevalence or staff mobility. The model did not account for individuals in solitary confinement for a quarantine of 14 days or other reasons, which was used at some of the facilities modelled for an indefinite amount of time. We also did not account for false-positive tests or misdiagnoses of COVID-19 being present in the data. Finally, the model does not address population fluctuation and assumes all agents (representing incarcerated people) enter the facility at the same time, which is unrealistic in real-life situations.

These limitations then directly influence our prediction of COVID-19 within these facilities and the accuracy of the daily predicted levels of COVID-19. Therefore, the conclusions of our analysis should focus on the implications of using a semicompartmental model to model multiple facilities under one system, increasing the use capabilities of COVID-19 models for incarcerated populations from previous studies.

Conclusions

Despite the limitations of our model, we conclude that carceral facility-level characteristics (especially overcrowding and vaccination rates) improve traditional compartmental SEIR transmission models; their availability will support outbreak tracking and control within prisons. By using information on the overcrowding, vaccination rate and exposure level to COVID-19 of a given facility, predictive insights can be made for said facility over 30-day intervals to help make short-term decisions about relevant health interventions. In addition, flexible modelling is possible for multiple facilities using the outlined methods, simultaneously making the modelling of various incarcerated populations possible. As state and federal governments responded swiftly to the peak of the COVID-19 epidemic, more measures could be put in place to proactively mitigate risk related to COVID-19. Selecting and tracking the effect of intervention measures is aided by more accurate outbreak modelling. Transparent and consistent data sharing is an integral part of this process to create better prediction models and make important decisions for the health of incarcerated people.
