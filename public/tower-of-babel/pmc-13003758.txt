Preliminary determination of a priori information for forensic lubricant analysis in sexual assault cases
Authors: Candice Bridge, Brooke Baumgarten, Liansheng Tang, Ngoc Ty Nguyen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhile mass production of materials provides many benefits to the community, it hinders the potential to source unknown samples in a forensic context. To circumvent this issue, the forensic community has been exploring the use of likelihood ratios to compare two probabilities with the goal of deciding if it is more likely that the unknown sample is from a known source or a different source. To aid in this calculation, it is helpful to understand the prevalence of that item in the local community, which is a determination that can be hindered by mass production. This study aimed to determine the prevalence, including rarity and commonality, of sexual lubricant products in the local area surrounding the University of Central Florida (UNIVERSITY). This information would provide a baseline on the number of products available for purchase in the area that can be used in acts of consensual intercourse or sexual assault. Understanding the products available would also provide insight into the type of chemical compounds that are present within sexual lubricants, which can be used for sourcing purposes. The results of this study revealed several major findings: (i) the physical marketplace around the UNIVERSITY is dominated by water-based lubricants, (ii) lubricant brands may be directly associated with a particular retail store, and (iii) specific chemical compositions of a lubricant product may also be associated with a particular retail store. Providing frequency data and odds information about the lubricant products can assist investigators and forensic scientists in understanding the layout of the local sexual lubricant marketplace, which can assist in determining the uniqueness of the unknown sample recovered in a sexual assault case and potentially provide an investigative lead. Introduction

Mass production of products within the marketplace has made modern life easy by guaranteeing availability whenever the commodity is desired (i.e., supply meeting demand). Additionally, mass production of products ensures that the item’s functions (e.g., feel, taste, function) remain the same each time they are purchased. In the sexual lubricant marketplace, the consistency of the final product, especially over time, is a primary target. However, the two advantages of mass production to the common consumer, i.e., “consistency in the resulting product” and “availability,” make analytical differentiation and source determination difficult for the forensic community. Even if forensic scientists could source an unknown sexual lubricant sample to a particular product (i.e., brand and type), the next question is, “how prevalent is this lubricant in the community?” This idea of product prevalence is an important consideration that is not often discussed until after the analysis has been completed. Understanding where a commercial manufactured product stands in the community will help the scientists determine the rarity of a product, which can be a lead for investigators looking to narrow the field of suspects. Considering the interest in using likelihood ratio (LR) calculations in forensic science, understanding the rarity of a feature (i.e., chemical composition, type of sample, brand) may increase the strength of the LR [1, 2].

There are a few studies in the literature that provide a similar exploration of the prevalence of manufactured products, i.e., frequency of occurrence studies.

Ryland et al. [3] conducted a frequency of occurrence study on automotive paint evidence, particularly the topcoat paint colour. Based on their collection of reference data, they had the statistics to support examiner statements of sourcing determination. The researchers provided an example of the type of statement that could be presented in court based on their statistical numbers, “‘At least 97% of the vehicles on the road can be eliminated as possible sources of the questioned paint on the basis of general topcoat colour alone’ can give the court a better scale for evaluating the ‘could have’ conclusion”. When this research was published in 1981, there were a few calls [4, 5] describing the need for generating statistical terms to understand the meaning and significance of the data in different forensic disciplines; however, to date, there are a limited number of papers published. Since the Ryland paper was published, there were several documents presenting a frequency of occurrence study in different disciplines including: handwritten numeral characteristics [6], bullet lead compositions [7], quantification of textural elements [8, 9] and style markers [10–12], forensic genetics [13], gun ammunition [14], fibres [15, 16], and glass [17, 18]. There are even fewer papers discussing the frequency of occurrence in the forensic lubricant discipline. There is one paper on forensic lubricants in which the authors conducted a market survey of condom lubricants and personal hygiene products. Burnier et al. [19] investigated the chemical differences of sexual lubricants on an international scale using attenuated total reflection-Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (ATR-FTIR). They observed that regardless of the international location from which the samples were purchased, 96% of the condom lubricants and personal hygiene product samples contained silicones as lubricants.

With regard to lubricant analysis, two ways in which rarity can be determined are: the presence of a particular chemical compound and the product itself. This research group has evaluated the rarity of individual chemical compounds, which aids in determining sexual lubricant classifications that are differentiated based on unique chemical feature(s) or combinations thereof [20–25]. The next exploration necessary to continue advancing the forensic discipline is to determine the rarity of lubricant types in the community (i.e., combinations of individual components). The determination of the rarity of a product within the community is especially important since the presence of a product makes it available to be used in sexual acts and/or sexual assaults and this knowledge can then inform examiners and/or investigators on the type of lubricants that could have been a source for the recovered lubricant. This information may narrow down the suspect pool by limiting it to potential buyers of the lubricant product. It also may inform the jury of the a priori information on the prevalence of a product used in a criminal case.

The absence of information regarding the prevalence of lubricants in the community may adversely affect the decision-making process when looking for investigative leads. In the absence of information regarding the prevalence of a lubricant product in the marketplace relative to those used in a sexual assault, the investigation team may falsely elevate or diminish potential evidence sources within the criminal investigation. Therefore, the authors conducted an occurrence frequency study to determine the baseline of sexual lubricant/lubrication products available around the University of Central Florida (UNIVERSITY). After collecting the survey of lubricants for sale around the UNIVERSITY, the authors used the information to provide baseline knowledge to the community with regards to several questions that may be helpful to a criminal investigation. The goal of this research was to determine the frequency of lubricant products under different scenarios.

This study aimed to quantify the frequency and calculate the odds of finding specific types of lubricant samples based on the products available around the UNIVERSITY. These values can provide insight into the prevalence of these lubricant products and add context to the information about the rarity or commonality of lubricant components that may be recovered after a sexual assault.

Methods

The authors deemed it was appropriate to provide a first attempt in determining the population frequencies and prior odds of lubricant types in forensic lubricant cases by understanding the commercial lubricant market around the UNIVERSITY.

Baseline survey acquisition

The lubricant marketing survey was performed at all stores within a five-mile (~8.0 km) radius of the UNIVERSITY that could potentially sell lubricant products (Figure 1). The collection area was divided into five sections: a one-mile (~1.6 km) radius around the center (i.e., the UNIVERSITY area but not including the UNIVERSITY), followed by diagonal cuts across the map that divided the remaining area into North, South, East, and West (Figure 1).

Depiction of geographic separation of the location of stores investigated within the survey.

A team of 11 people surveyed the retail stores within the survey area. The collection of information was performed in teams of two people per location. Each team was provided with a letter from the National Center for Forensic Science that was presented to the store owner(s) detailing the purpose of the survey and what actions the survey team would take in their store. Teams were requested to collect pictures of both the store as well as the products on the store shelves for future reference and archiving, if allowed by the store’s supervisor. The teams were also provided with a survey tally sheet to take an on-site count of the number of lubricants that fit into one of 23 potential marketing categories. A copy of the survey sheet is provided in the Supplemental Information (Supplementary Figure S1).

The goal was to survey how many types of lubricated products were sold, how many different manufacturers sold samples, and the number of samples that contain a chemical additive (e.g., one that induced warming or cooling sensation to the user). The determination of the presence of additives was based on the ingredient list or the information present on the front of the product’s container (i.e., marketing information). This information aided in binning the data into a marketing group and provided a viable start to understanding the chemical compounds present in each sexual lubricant. While retail markets sell more than one item of each product (e.g., box of Lelo condoms), it was decided that equal weight would be assigned to the purchasing of one item amongst the types of items that were sold. Therefore, it was irrelevant how many individual items of a product were available for purchase; only the number of different products available was recorded. As an example, Trojan condoms were listed as a different product from Trojan Sensitive condoms.

A total of 101 stores were surveyed to determine the brands and the lubricant available for sale within each store, thereby providing a baseline of lubricants in the UNIVERSITY area. Of the retail stores that could potentially sell sexual lubricant products, the stores fell into five categories: gas stations, grocery stores, pharmacies, smoke shops, and supermarkets (Table 1). Supermarkets, such as Walmart Supercenter and Target, have multiple in-store departments including personal care products and groceries. As a result, supermarkets were placed in a separate category from stores that sold mainly groceries. Of the 101 stores surveyed, there were 55 gas stations, 19 grocery stores, 23 pharmacies, two smoke shops, and two supermarkets. There were no adult stores located within this five-mile radius and, as a result, were not included in this study.

Data analysis

Data analysis of the baseline results was conducted in two ways: brand identity and how the lubricant was marketed (i.e., the primary lubricant chemical). Each sample was initially categorised based on the label (marketed-type): water-based, oil-based, organic-based, silicone-based, condom, and other. Subsequently, samples were further placed into the following subcategories: none, anaesthetic, edible, flavour, sensation, and one flavour sensation combination.

While the authors have analysed and determined the composition of many condoms, not all lubricated condoms encountered in this survey have been analysed for the Sexual Lubricant Database (SLD). Considering that condom packaging does not generally contain information about the lubricant used, for the purposes of this study, online resources were used to identify if the lubricant present was silicone-based, water-based, or oil-based, including the manufacturer’s website and the Condom Depot website (http://www.condomdepot.com/).

There were some stores in which a particular type of lubricant product was out of stock, and therefore, the type of lubricant sold was based on the store’s label of what should have been on the shelf, which was cross-referenced with other stores in the area, since multiple stores stocked their shelves in a similar fashion to provide the consumer with an identical presentation.

Results and discussion

At each store, the team tallied the total number of items for sale that fit into one of the several categories, based on the label information and/or indicated ingredients (Table 2). In this study, only different products were tallied and not the number of samples on the shelf per product. While the number of samples available per product and the products’ sales can influence the baseline population, it was more important to determine the potential of a particular type of lubricant being used in a sexual assault based on the available products in the market. This would assign equal weight of selection to each lubricant item in the store, which would be comparable to consumer purchasing practice. Generally, consumers purchase one lubricant product when they go to the store.

Breakdown by retail establishment

One way the data were examined was by determining the types of lubricants that are commonly sold at the different retail location types. It was immediately apparent that gas stations, regardless of brand, exclusively sold only condoms. Conversely, the smoke shops that were surveyed primarily sold bottled lubricants (Table 1).

While pharmacies sold the most items out of any of the retail stores that were surveyed (on average, 63.9 products per store), the breakdown of types was comparable to grocery stores and supermarkets. The breakdown between condom and bottled products were nearly equal, 49.4% and 49.8%, respectively. There were two products sold at a pharmacy that were categorised as others—vaginal moisturizers that contained water-based lubricants such as glycerine and hydrogenated palm oil glyceride.

Breakdown by marketing type

When examining the data, based on lubricant marketing types, the largest share of the surveyed retail stores belonged to the water-based condom lubricants without additives, at 21.4%. The next major product was the water-based bottled lubricant at 15.3%. Next were the silicone-based condoms that did not contain additives (10.7%), which were closely followed by condom lubricant with a base that could not be identified from the packaging information or their safety data sheet, if one was published (10.4%); refer to Figure 2 and Table 2. Therefore, nearly 60% of the lubricant market fell into one of these four marketing types.

Percentage market share of lubricant types on the retail market.

The next step was to determine the brand presence of a particular manufacturer in the market. During data exploration, the degree of representation of each manufacturer in the UNIVERSITY market was evaluated. The goal was to determine if there was one manufacturer/brand that had a higher probability of being purchased, due to the sheer volume of products in the market, and thus has a higher potential of being used in a sexual assault. This was considered a function of whether the product was a condom or a bottled lubricant.

Lubricated condoms

There were seven different manufacturers represented in the 101 stores, with a total of 1 535 condom products identified. Eighteen products were non lubricanted condoms and thus would not leave a lubricant residue of its own. As such, these samples were removed from the remaining calculations. Of the 1 517 products identified, 1 060 products were made by Trojan (69.9%), 281 were Lifestyles products (18.5%), 156 were Durex products (10.3%), and the remaining four manufacturers represented <2% of the products (20 products) within the retail stores (i.e., Good Clean Love, Sustain, One, and Lelo). A complete list of the manufacturers and their market share is provided in Supplemental Table S1.

Bottled lubricants

The bottled lubricants were less prevalent in the marketplace. Twenty-five different manufacturers were observed in the local UNIVERSITY marketplace, with a total of 1 098 bottled lubricant products. Twenty-two products were not identified by the manufacturer or ingredients because of the absence of a photograph and thus were categorised by only the marking information on the front of the package. Of the 1 076 products remaining, 350 were made by KY (32.5%), 195 were Astroglide products (18.1%), 105 were made by Trojan (9.8%), 98 CVS products were observed (9.1%), and 82 products were manufactured by Wet (7.6%). The remaining 20 manufacturers had <6% of the market share each, which together totaled 22.9% (246 products). A complete list of the manufacturers and their market share is provided in Supplemental Table S2.

Other Products

The other products observed were only available for purchase in 12 of the 23 pharmacy locations. There were 11 feminine moisturizers and one contraceptive gel. Of the 12 products, 10 were Replens products (83.4%), one products was manufactured by CVS (8.3%) and one product was a VCF product (8.3%).

Use of information for likelihood calculations

Likelihood calculations have received great interest in the forensic science community. Particularly in the areas of DNA and latent fingerprint analysis, likelihood calculations have been accepted and even incorporated into the daily operations of many forensic laboratories [26–28]. While there is a wealth of information in the literature about LR, a short synopsis is provided here.

The common form of the LR originates from the odd form of the Bayes Rule [13].

Here, DM refers to the decision maker, which is the jury, and EX refers to the expert witness who calculates the LR of a “match” between the known and unknown samples to address the two hypotheses below [29]. The resulting value will provide support towards one of the following two hypotheses for common sources or for different sources. If the result is larger than 1 (e.g., 1, 10, 100), then the results will support the first hypothesis; however, if the result is smaller than 1 (e.g., 0.1, 0.01, 0.001), then LR supports the second hypothesis. Additional information on the calculation and utility of LR and the Bayes theorem is described by Lund and Iyer [29], Dawid [30], and Kaye and Freedman [31].

H1: Does the unknown sample come from the same source as the known sample?

H2: Does the unknown sample come from a source different from the known sample?

In this case, the LR involves the hypotheses, with one stating that two pieces of evidence (i.e., E for evidence from the known samples, and S for evidence from the unknown samples) are from the same source (H1), and the other hypothesis stating that the two pieces of evidence are from different sources (H2).

Under the first hypothesis of same sources, the numerator of the LR is one. Also, under the second hypothesis of different sources, the two pieces of evidence are independent. With the data collected from the stores surveyed, several questions that could be important when determining the denominator of the LR based on population frequencies and odds in a case were considered. To do this, the conditional probabilities based on the frequencies of a type of item were calculated.

The decision maker also must determine the prior and posterior odds based on what they know about the case and the information that they drew from the testimonies of the expert witnesses in the case. While they are related, the two odds deal with different aspects of the case [29]:

Prior odds: the consideration of the evidence before knowledge of the examination results.

Posterior odds: the odds when the lubricant evidence is included in the analysis by the decision maker.

However, the prior odds are usually difficult to ascertain for the DM because they must rely on their knowledge of the individual type of evidence (i.e., lubricant, glass, or something else). As an example, if the evidence in question was a lubricant, then the DM would have to rely on their understanding of the lubricant market (such as the percentage of silicone-based lubricants vs. water-based lubricants available for purchase). However, most condom purchases are based on personal preference and not market observations. Therefore, jurors may not be aware of the other types of products available when they are making a decision about the strength of the evidence presented by the forensic expert. Therefore, to bypass this, many forensic statisticians will simply use a ratio of 1:1 in place of the prior odds [27], to indicate that there is a 50% chance of the prior odds leaning towards one of the two questions presented above.

Calculation of the prior odds

In a criminal event, if it were determined that a raspberry-bottled lubricant was identified by the forensic crime laboratory, based on the presence of specific components, the next question by the investigators could be, “what is the rarity of this lubricant in the vicinity of the criminal event?” This is considered to be the odds that a particular lubricant was used in the crime. If every supermarket, gas station, and/or pharmacy sold a type of water-based bottled lubricant that is flavoured raspberry, then this opens up the suspect pool. If, however, this lubricant was not sold in any store in the vicinity (rarity), but was found in the possession of one potential suspect, then this provides an investigative lead for the detective. Additionally, these odds provide context for the jury during the judicial proceedings. The odds can be employed in several ways to understand the abundance of specific products, a particular lubricant chemical formula, or even a particular manufacturer. While some of these odds cannot be determined through chemical analysis of the samples, the research team can utilise the chemical composition determination made from the manufacturer’s ingredient list. Illustration 6: prior odds for base formulation types

The prior odds of encountering a lubricant based on the class of the lubricant (bottled or condom) in the surrounding area can be calculated.

Based on the count of condoms in the five-mile (~8.0 km) radius of the UNIVERSITY, the probability of finding a given condom type is nearly 58.4% compared with the other forms of lubricated products. Therefore, the prior odds of finding and purchasing a condom are 0.584/(1 − 0.584) = 1.404, which is nearly 1.4 to 1, which means that there is a higher likelihood of someone purchasing a condom versus the other product types (Table 8).

This finding was a bit surprising to the authors because discussions of forensic lubricant residues largely centre around the detection of silicone-based lubricants. The combined odds (bottle + condom) of finding a water-based lubricant without additives were significantly larger than the odds of finding a silicone-based lubricant without additives, at 0.586 to 0.181, respectively. This difference in probability gives indications that more discussion and research are needed regarding the detection and identification of water-based lubricants, especially since the base lubricant component of water-based lubricants, e.g., glycerol, polyethylene glycol (PEG), polypropylene glycol, or polybutylene glycol, can be found in additional commercially available products [4, 6].

How common is it to find lubricants in different retail stores?

In the event that a laboratory was able to determine that the unknown lubricant residue was a condom lubricant based on the presence of talc [32] or a spermicide [33] (which are common components), in the presence of more another unique component, such as pulegone [22], it may be possible to generate an investigative lead with the assistance of this survey’s information. If the unique composition of the lubricant is only available in one type of product, which is only sold at one or two types of retail markets or a specific store chain, there can then be a prediction of the potential store from which the sample was purchased. This lead may eventually result in the identification of which customer(s) purchased that type of lubricant product from that store(s) in the vicinity of where the crime occurred. Prevalence of lubricant types

Prevalence, as defined by the medical community, is the number of subjects with a disease at one time point “versus” the total number of subjects in the study [34]. However, as defined in this study, prevalence in the number of lubricants of one lubricant type (base component, additive, etc.) vs. the total number of individual items surveyed in the study (Equation (6)).

To make this calculation, the team assessed the primary lubricant base from examination of the ingredient list. It should be noted that lubricant products that contain a mixture of lubricant bases, such as one that contains PEG (PEG) and decamethylcyclopentasiloxane (D5), would be included in both calculations for PEG and siloxane.

The prevalence of siloxanes in the surveyed area can now be considered. Based on the ingredient list, 21.5% of the 2 627 lubricated products surveyed contained a type of polydimethylsiloxane. The presence of any siloxane inclusive product was different based on the store in which the product was sold. In the two supermarkets, the prevalence of silicone-based lubricants was 17.6% and 26.2%, respectively. In the 23 pharmacies, the prevalence ranged from 0% to 31.7%, but the average prevalence was 26.3%. Similar results were observed for the grocery stores; however, the average prevalence was lower at 18.4%. Interestingly, gas stations had locations with as much as 42.9% prevalence of silicone-inclusive products. However, there were some locations that did not contain any and, as a result, the average prevalence of silicone-containing products for this type of retail location was 12.4% (refer to Table 13). The prevalence of products that contained water-based formulations was also calculated, and there were significantly more products that had this type of formulation than those that contained silicone at all stores surveyed (Table 13). The average prevalence rate of inclusive water-based lubricants in the marketplace was 68%, whereas for silicone-based lubricants, it was only 18%. Oil-based and organic-based products had a significantly lower prevalence in the marketplace, with an average value across all retail markets of 2.2%. It is important to note that Table 13 does not include the products where the base lubricant could not be determined from the package (i.e., condom items). Future calculations will be conducted based on the detection of individual components in the local market to determine their prevalence.

Application to forensic lubricant casework

Having knowledge of the local retail market of lubricants can be beneficial for the criminal investigation. The use of the classification of the lubricant residue to indicate potential sources where the assailant may have purchased the sexual lubricant product could help narrow down the suspect pool, especially in cases where the assailant is unknown.

The local retail market should be based on the area of responsibility of the forensic crime laboratory. If the laboratory serves two counties adjacent to each other, then the retail stores in both counties should be included in their personal survey until a country-wide survey can be conducted. One thing that was observed during this study was that the major commercial stores (e.g., CVS, Walgreens, Walmart) aim to provide a similar purchasing experience for their customers at each of their stores. Therefore, they stock the same sexual products on their shelves in generally the same location. This would make it easier to develop a survey of local stores because it may not be necessary to visit every single CVS. Although this survey was conducted on brick-and-mortar retail locations, ~20% of lubricant sales are purchased this way. Nearly 40% are sold in the online retail space, and 20% are distributed through direct-to-consumer channels [35–37]. Therefore, until there is some evidence that sexual assailants purchase their products in brick-and-mortar or online, it would be necessary to include both spaces in the local survey of the crime laboratory. Inclusion of personal hygiene products would also be beneficial to be included; although these are less likely to be purchased by sexual assailants, most of these products are much harder to conceal than condoms.

With the use of the National Center of Forensic Science’s SLD [38] and a local survey of retail stores, a forensic crime laboratory may provide some investigative leads to law enforcement officials. The SLD would help laboratories classify unknown lubricant stains or residues that would aid in identifying manufacturers or lubricant products that meet that classification. The identification of individual compounds can narrow down the type of lubricant and/or purpose of the lubricant. Chemical information can identify if the lubricant is silicone-based, water-based, or oil-based, and it can also indicate if the lubricant is a condom, bottle lubricant, or possibly another type of sexual product. Additionally, some lubricant products have unique chemical compositions and the identification of unique compounds, e.g., pulegone, can be attributed to one specific lubricant product. Narrowing down the source of the lubricant residue/stain based on its chemical composition can associate it with a brick-and-mortar retail market. This is especially true for bottled lubricants and less popular/marketed condom products. While Trojan and Durex condoms were present at most of the markets surveyed, products like Lelo condoms were only present at one pharmacy location. If the unknown lubricant is linked to the Lelo condom based on the chemical composition, this could be a strong investigative lead. For unidentified assailants, this information allows investigators to query the sales records of relevant retailers to generate suspect leads. Conclusion

This study attempted to assess the prevalence of lubricated products, their chemical compositions, and manufacturer representation in the local UNIVERSITY area. Collating the types of lubricants and determining the frequency and odds of encountering one of these items in the local community provides an invaluable resource to the local forensic lubricant community that may aid in developing investigative leads and/or narrowing the potential sources of lubricants. A set of baseline numbers has been calculated for this area which aids in determining the rarity of a lubricant composition that may aid the forensic scientist in their a priori information. This type of information also provides a valuable resource to the trier of fact to determine or to estimate the rarity of particular products which would be most important if a residue collected from the victim, or the crime scene, has a similar chemical composition.

What is interesting about these findings is that there should be more focus on researching water-based lubricant products and the persistence of their components. The majority of the research and many forensic analytical protocols for sexual lubricants are focused on detecting and identifying the presence of siloxane products, which is understandable since siloxanes are easier to detect using a variety of instrumental techniques, and these components persist for longer time periods. However, it appears that the lubricant marketplace is largely comprised of water-based lubricants, which contain components like PEG, polybutylene glycol, glycerol and hydroxyethylcellulose. The marketplace around the UNIVERSITY is dominated with water-based products by a near 2:1 ratio. However, it must be noted that the inclusion of online retailers could shift the ratio of water- to silicone-based products. Based on the 2022 Personal Lubricant Market Forecast report by Grand View Research which included E-commerce, water-based lubricants dominated the market with 52.25% of the overall revenue when compared to silicone-based or oil-based lubricants [39]. Similarly, if a lubricant marketing survey were conducted in another major city or rural area, the results could also differ. Thus, these baseline surveys could be unique to the area of responsibility for different forensic laboratories. Additional studies are needed to determine if other locales have a similar or different prevalence of lubricant products.

Additional work will be conducted to determine the prevalence and/or rarity of individual chemical compounds in lubricant products present in the UNIVERSITY area. This information will aid in potentially linking an unknown lubricant residue to a particular brand, especially for rarer components identified during the forensic analysis. It may also become possible to source a lubricant or its residue to a particular brand, manufacturer, and/or retail market. The latter accomplishment of linking a rare component to a particular retail market would be dependent on whether a particular lubricant brand is sold at a given type of retail establishment. This would provide investigative leads for sexual assault investigations in which biological evidence, such as DNA, has not been recovered. Making connections between the suspect and the victim using lubricant evidence may provide continued progress in these types of cases, many of which may be dismissed due to the lack of evidence [40].
