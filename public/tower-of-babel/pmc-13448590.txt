Infant feeding practice and associated factors among hiv-positive mothers attending anti-retroviral therapy services in health facilities of Bahir Dar City
Authors: Zinash Mekonen, Hunegnaw Almaw, Beselam Gizachew, Getnet Sintayehu, Reta Tesera Abawa, Etsegenet Kindie Tiruneh, Addisu Gasheneit Ferede, Getasew Mulat Bantie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Infant feeding practices among Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV)-positive mothers are vital for child survival, nutrition, and prevention of mother-to-child HIV transmission. Despite the World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines promoting optimal infant feeding practices, adherence remains inconsistent in Ethiopia. This study aimed to assess infant feeding practices and associated factors among HIV-positive mothers attending ART services in health facilities of Bahir Dar City, Ethiopia, 2023. An institution-based cross-sectional study was conducted from March 15, 2023, to April 15, 2023. A systematic random sampling technique was used. A survey questionnaire was used to collect data on feeding practices. The data were entered and analyzed by SPSS software version 23. A binary logistic regression was performed to assess any relationship between each independent variable and outcome variables. An adjusted odds ratio (AOR) with a 95% confidence interval (CI) was used for any association between the dependent and predictor variables, while significance was determined using 95% confidence intervals. This study included 198 participants with a response rate of 94.7%. About 40% of mothers and 18% of fathers were unable to read and write. Approximately 12% of mothers were divorced About 34% of mothers had a monthly income of 1500–3000 birr. The magnitude of appropriate infant feeding practice was 80.8% (95% CI: 75–86%). The identified determinants of infant feeding practices were counseling during antenatal care (AOR = 2.79, 95% CI: 1.12, 6.92) and knowing the HIV status of the infant (AOR = 2.94, 95% CI: 1.14, 7.61). Appropriate infant feeding practices were 80.8%, which is below the WHO target. Counselling during antenatal care and knowledge of the infant’s HIV status were significantly associated with appropriate infant feeding practices. Thus, there is still a need to strengthen maternal counseling and support programs to improve adherence to recommended practices.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53353-2.

Introduction

Infant feeding practices are parents’ efforts to regulate or alter the infant’s diet and eating habits. It is crucial for child growth, development, and survival, particularly during the first two years of life, as it supports immune function, prevents malnutrition, and reduces morbidity and mortality from common childhood illnesses1–4. Infants born to HIV-positive mothers face increased risks of malnutrition, HIV infection, and mortality5–12. The WHO recommends infants born to HIV-positive mothers start exclusive breastfeeding at birth, continue it for six months, and add complementary foods at six months when the infants are not HIV infected or have unknown HIV status13,14. Replacement feeding is also recommended only when the AFASS (acceptable, feasible, affordable, sustainable, and safe) criteria are fulfilled15–18. Moreover, HIV-positive mothers need to take antiretroviral therapy (ART) consistently throughout the breast-feeding period so that the risk of transmitting HIV to their children remains extremely low13.

About one-half of HIV transmissions were attributed to breastfeeding in Africa. In Ethiopia, HIV remains a public health concern, and infant feeding practices among HIV-positive mothers are strongly shaped by socio-economic disadvantage, stigma, and limited access to consistent, high-quality counseling services7,19.

Although national guidelines align with WHO recommendations, evidence suggests there are gaps in the appropriate infant feeding practices among HIV-positive mothers in Ethiopia20,21. There is also limited context-specific data on infant feeding practices and their determinants in the Amhara region, and there is a need for localized evidence to inform interventions. Thus, this study aimed to determine infant feeding practices and identify the determinants among HIV-positive mothers in Bahir Dar City.

Methods

Study design, setting, and period

This facility-based quantitative cross-sectional study was conducted in Bahir Dar City health facilities from March 15 to April 15, 2023. Bahir Dar city is the capital of the Amhara region and is located 564 km from Addis Ababa, the capital of Ethiopia. In the city, there were six subcities; there were three public hospitals, five health centers, and five private health facilities, and there were 393 infants with mothers as ART service attendants.

Population

All mothers with infants who have followed up for ART services were the source population.

Eligibility criteria

Reproductive-age HIV-positive mothers with infants who had follow-up for ART services in Bahir Dar city during the data collection period were included. While seriously ill mothers were excluded.

Sample size determination

The sample size was determined using Epi-Info version 7 software, with the stat calc Fleiss W/CC model with assumptions of a 1:1 ratio, 80% power, and a 10% nonresponse rate. Attitudes about exclusive breastfeeding, HIV disclosure to a partner, and breast problems were the predictors for infant feeding practices22. Finally, a determinant with the largest sample size was chosen, and the final sample was 209 (Table 1).

Study Variables

Dependent variable

✓ Infant feeding practices (appropriate = 1, inappropriate = 0).

Independent variables

Maternal-related characteristics: age, marital status, occupation, educational status, religion, monthly income, HIV disclosure status, place and mode of delivery, antenatal follow-up, nutritional counseling, postnatal services, stage of HIV, CD4 count, breast problems, and chronic illnesses.

Infant-related characteristics: Age, sex, mouth ulcer, HIV status, or any diseases.

Operational and term definitions

Infant feeding practice

A mother had appropriate infant feedings, exclusive breast or replacement feeding for the first six months, and introduced complementary foods at six months; otherwise, she had inappropriate practices23.

Exclusive breastfeeding

Infant receives only breast milk for the first six months of life, with no other liquids or solids except for vitamins, minerals, or medicines24.

Exclusive replacement feeding

A commercial or home-prepared non-breast milk during the first six months until the child is fully fed on family foods.

Mixed breastfeeding

Taking of fluids, solid foods, or non-human milks in addition to breastfeeding in the first six months of age or starting complementary feeding under six years of age.

Data collection tool and procedure

A survey questionnaire was prepared in English by reviewing different literature (Supplementary materials). The questionnaire was initially prepared in English, then translated to the local Amharic language by independent translators, and then back to English to check for consistency. Finally, an Amharic version of the questionnaire was used to collect the data. Twelve nurses with bachelor’s degrees were recruited, a one-day training was provided, and the survey was conducted at the exit after mothers received the services.

Data quality assurance

Data quality was ensured using a standardized questionnaire pretested on 5% of the sample at Merawi Hospital to assess clarity and completeness. After revisions, data collectors received an additional briefing and one day of training. Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha (α = 0.82). Questionnaires were checked daily for completeness, and missing data were addressed through on-site verification, and incomplete questionnaires were excluded from the final analysis.

Data processing and analysis

Data were coded and entered into EpiData v4.12 and analyzed using SPSS v23. Descriptive statistics were computed and presented in tables and figures. Continuous variables were assessed for distribution and categorized using standard literature-based and context-specific cut-offs to improve interpretability. A binary logistic regression was applied, as the outcome variable was dichotomous. Model fitness was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test (p > 0.05), and multicollinearity was checked using the variance inflation factor (VIF = 1.67). Following common epidemiological practice, variables with p < 0.2 in bivariate analysis were entered into multivariable models to avoid potential confounders. Adjusted odds ratios (AORs) with 95% confidence intervals were reported, and variables with p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Ethical Consideration

Ethical clearance was obtained from the research ethical review committee of GAMBY Medical and Business College with reference number G/C-09/78/2025. All the methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. All the experimental protocols were approved by an ethical review committee of GAMBY Medical and Business College. Accordingly, a support letter was obtained from the Amhara Public Health Institute and respective health facilities. Before collecting the data, written informed consent was secured. Participants’ full rights to refuse the study were preserved. Personal identifiers were secured. The names of the respondents were not used to ensure anonymity and confidentiality. All information obtained from the respondents was kept confidential.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of the participants

This study included 198 participants with a response rate of 94.7%. The mean age of the respondents was 31.5 (± 4.75) years. About 55% and 58% of the infants were under six months of age and males, respectively. About 40% of mothers and 18% of fathers were unable to read and write. About 99% of the mothers were Orthodox Christians. Nearly 98% of the respondents were of Amhara ethnicity. Approximately 12% and 17% of mothers were divorced and had no social support. About 34% of mothers had a monthly income of 1500–3000 birr (Table 3).

Obstetric characteristics of the respondents

In this study, most of the respondents (67.7%) had one to two children; 98% attended antenatal care (ANC), and 89% received infant feeding counseling. All of the mothers gave birth in the health facility. Nearly 90% of the mothers got postnatal care (PNC) services. About 71% of the mothers knew their HIV status before pregnancy, and 73% of them disclosed their status to their husbands (Annex 1).

Infant feeding practices of HIV-positive mothers

Most respondents (90.4%) had ever breastfed; 84.3% initiated breastfeeding within one hour of birth, and 28.3% of infants received additional foods/fluids within 6 months. About 9% of the infants were given the expressed breast milk. Nearly 9% of infants were on exclusive replacement feeding, and 14.6% practiced mixed feeding (Table 4).

The health conditions of the mothers and infants

About 98% of the mothers were on HIV stage I. 3% of mothers had experienced breastfeeding problems, and 4% had chronic comorbidities. About 48% of the mothers knew the infant’s HIV status, and 1.5% were HIV-positive. Nearly 10% of the infants had mouth ulcers (Annex 2).

The nutritional counseling practices of health care workers

Nearly 70% of mothers were counseled on infant feeding options, 80% on the benefits of breastfeeding, 70% on the benefits of replacement feeding, and 37.5% on the risk of mixed feeding (Annex 3).

The magnitude of infant feeding practices

In this study, 80.8% of the study participants had appropriate infant feeding practices (95% CI: 75%, 86%) (Fig. 1).

Factors associated with infant feeding practices

A binary logistic regression analysis was done to identify factors associated with infant feeding practices. In a bivariate logistic regression, counseling on feeding options, HIV disclosure, knowing the HIV status of the infant, the sickness of the infant, and counseling on the benefits of breastfeeding were the factors associated with infant feeding practice at 20% significance. However, in the multivariable logistic regression, only receiving counseling on feeding options during ANC and knowing the HIV status of the infant were significantly associated with infant feeding practice at a p-value of 0.05.

Accordingly, for those mothers who got infant feeding counseling during the ANC follow-up, the odds of appropriate infant feeding practice were about 2.8 (AOR = 2.79, 95% CI: 1.12, 6.92) times higher compared to mothers who didn’t receive infant feeding counseling during the ANC follow-up. Similarly, for mothers who knew the HIV status of their infant, the odds of appropriate infant feeding practice were 2.9 (AOR = 2.94, 95% CI: 1.14, 7.61) times higher compared to mothers who didn’t know the HIV status of their infant (Table 5).

Discussion

In this study, the magnitude of appropriate infant feeding practices was 80.8% (95% CI: 75–86%), and receiving antenatal care counseling and knowing the infant’s HIV status were the identified significant factors.

The magnitude of appropriate infant feeding practice was 80.8% (95% CI: 75, 86%). This finding was higher than the study findings of Shashemene Woreda, 32.1%25; Debrelibanos District, 65.8%26; Afar Region, 63.8%27; and Assayita District, Afar Region, 9.2%28. The variations could be due to differences in study period, setting, and sample size. Additionally, Bahir Dar City, as the capital of the Amhara Region, has access to health facilities, trained health professionals, and maternal and child health services that are substantially better than in rural or pastoralist areas such as Afar. Urban mothers are more likely to attend antenatal and postnatal care, receive repeated counseling on infant feeding, and be exposed to health information through mass media and community-based health programs. In contrast, pastoralist and rural communities often face limited access to health services, lower female literacy, cultural feeding practices, and mobility-related barriers that can negatively influence optimal infant feeding.

The current study revealed that mothers receiving counseling during antenatal care follow-up had a significant positive effect on appropriate infant feeding practice. For those mothers who received infant feeding counseling during the ANC follow-up, the odds of appropriate infant feeding practice were about 2.8 times higher compared to mothers who didn’t receive infant feeding counseling during the ANC follow-up. This finding was supported by study findings in the Dabat Health and Demographic Surveillance System (DHDSS) site29 in Oromia30 in Shashemene town, Ethiopia25, and in the Debrelibanos district, North Showa Zone26. These could be linked with antenatal counseling and nutrition advice, which are associated with improved infant and young child feeding knowledge and practices among women attending ANC services31.

Another significant factor influencing infant feeding practice was knowing the infant’s HIV status. Mothers who knew their infant’s HIV status had higher odds of practicing appropriate infant feeding, possibly because this knowledge may increase maternal attention to safe feeding and adherence to recommended practices. It is supported by evidence showing that HIV status disclosure and related knowledge are associated with better infant feeding practices among HIV-positive mothers, where mothers who disclosed their HIV status were significantly more likely to follow recommended feeding practices18,31.

The current study revealed no statistically significant association between feeding options counseling and the benefits of breastfeeding and infant feeding practices. However, previous studies revealed a significant association between feeding option counseling, breastfeeding benefits, and appropriate infant feeding practices32,33. This discrepancy may be explained by the relatively small sample size of the present study, which could have reduced the statistical power to detect a significant association18,32,34. Similarly, infant feeding practices were not associated with illness outcomes in infants. Moreover, HIV disclosure status and infant illness were not associated with infant feeding practices35,36. This discrepancy may be explained by the protective effect of early initiation of breastfeeding and exclusive breastfeeding, which enhance infant immunity and reduce the risk of illness.

Limitations of the study

This study has potential limitations, including chicken egg dilemma due to thre design, social desirability bias (mothers may over-report recommended feeding practices), recall bias (self-reported feeding practices), and selection bias (only mothers attending ART services were included). Additionally, this study did not assess the frequency of breastfeeding during the first six months or the frequency of complementary feeding according to age-specific recommendations. Moreover, the sample size we used was small and could limit the power to detect the potential predictor and statistical significance.

Conclusion

Appropriate infant feeding practices were 80.8%, which is below the WHO target. Counselling during antenatal care and knowledge of the infant’s HIV status were significantly associated with appropriate infant feeding practices. The current study findings underscored that the appropriate infant feeding practices were low as per the WHO target and required extensive work to enhance infant feeding practices. The significant association between antenatal care counseling and appropriate infant feeding highlights the importance of strengthening and sustaining comprehensive infant feeding counseling during routine ANC visits. Moreover, the influence of maternal awareness of the infant’s HIV status underscores the need to integrate infant feeding guidance with PMTCT and early infant diagnosis services. These results imply that coordinated ANC-based counseling and HIV-related services can improve infant feeding practices. Therefore, strengthening maternal counseling and support programs to further improve adherence to recommended practices.
