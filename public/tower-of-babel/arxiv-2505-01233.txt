Security Metrics for Uncertain Interconnected Systems under Stealthy Data Injection Attacks footnoteinfo
Anh Tung Nguyen, Sribalaji C. Anand, André M. H. Teixeira

Abstract
This paper quantifies the security of uncertain interconnected systems under stealthy data injection attacks. In particular, we consider a large-scale system composed of a certain subsystem interconnected with an uncertain subsystem, where only the input-output channels are accessible. An adversary is assumed to inject false data to maximize the performance loss of the certain subsystem while remaining undetected. By abstracting the uncertain subsystem as a class of admissible systems satisfying an ℒ 2 {\mathcal{L}}_{2} gain constraint, the worst-case performance loss is obtained as the solution to a convex semi-definite program depending only on the certain subsystem dynamics and such an ℒ 2 {\mathcal{L}}_{2} gain constraint. This solution is proved to serve as an upper bound for the actual worst-case performance loss when the model of the entire system is fully certain. The results are demonstrated through numerical simulations of the power transmission grid spanning Sweden and Northern Denmark.

Introduction

Networked control systems (NCSs) are a ubiquitous part of many critical infrastructures such as power grids
(
Singh et al., 2014
)
, water distribution systems
(
Amin et al., 2012
)
, and transportation systems
(
Park et al., 2014
)
. Owing to the use of insecure communication protocols in such systems, an increasing number of cyber-attacks are deployed through the communication channels
(
Hemsley and Fisher, 2018
)
.
This motivates research on the security of NCSs, particularly from a control theoretic viewpoint over the last decade
(
Dibaji et al., 2019
)
. Alongside this progress, one open question is to address the security of uncertain NCSs, which is the objective of this paper.

Some related studies have explored this area. For instance,
Park et al. (2019)
;
Zhang et al. (2022)
construct attack policies when the plant model is uncertain for the adversary.
Russo and Proutiere (2021)
develops a data-driven attack policy against data-driven controllers.
Harshbarger et al. (2020)
studies the role of an uncertain plant model on the stealthiness of an attack.
Anand et al. (2024)
;
Anand and Teixeira (2023)
provide a metric to quantify the security against stealthy attacks on uncertain systems. This paper extends the work
(
Anand et al., 2024
)
to consider interconnected deterministic and uncertain subsystems.

In this paper, we consider an interconnected continuous-time NCS, which consists of two subsystems
\Sigma_{c}
and
\Sigma_{u}
as represented in Fig.
1
. The model of
\Sigma_{c}
is certain to the adversary and the defender while the model of
\Sigma_{u}
is not available to the defender. An adversary is assumed to inject false data into
\Sigma_{c}
to increase its performance loss while staying stealthy. Under the above setup, we present the following contributions.

1.

An optimization problem is proposed to quantify the worst-case performance loss caused by a stealthy adversary. We observe the problem to be non-convex.

2.

This non-convex optimization problem is shown to be equivalent to a semi-definite program (SDP) problem.

3.

We show that the solution to the aforementioned SDP problem serves as an upper bound of the actual performance loss caused by stealthy adversaries.

The results obtained are demonstrated through numerical simulations of the power transmission grid spanning Sweden and Northern Denmark.

\displaystyle x_{c}(0)=0,~~x_{u}(0)=0.

(4)

\displaystyle x_{c}(\infty)=0,~~x_{u}(\infty)=0.

(5)

2.2
Adversarial purpose & security metric

We define that the adversary is detected if the energy of the residual signal
y_{r}

[0,H]

\displaystyle\left\lVert y_{r}\right\rVert^{2}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{2}[0,H]}>\delta.

(6)

Adversaries, who are detected, can be mitigated quickly and efficiently. Therefore, we are interested in studying stealthy attacks that always guarantee the energy of the residual signal

\displaystyle\left\lVert y_{r}\right\rVert^{2}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{2}[0,H]}\leq\delta.

(7)

At the same time, the adversary aims to design an attack signal that maximizes the performance output of the system
\Sigma_{c}
. The two aforementioned adversarial purposes (stealthiness and maximum performance loss) are formulated in the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle Q\triangleq\sup_{a}~~

\displaystyle\left\lVert y_{p}\right\rVert_{{\mathcal{L}}_{2}}^{2}

(8)

s.t.

\displaystyle\eqref{model_S1},\eqref{model_S2},x_{c}(0)=0,x_{u}(0)=0,

\displaystyle x_{c}(\infty)=0,x_{u}(\infty)=0,

\displaystyle\left\lVert y_{r}\right\rVert^{2}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{2}}\leq\delta,\left\lVert a\right\rVert^{2}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{2}}\leq E.

This metric was utilized for designing defense and mitigation strategies in our previous work such as
Nguyen et al. (2025)

Anand et al. (2024)

\displaystyle Q\leq\underline{Q}.

Remark 1

In this section, we recall several existing methods in the literature on how to obtain

4.1
Model-based estimation

We first provide an estimation of
\gamma_{u}
in (
26
) based on system matrices (
2
) in the following proposition.

Proposition 1

(Model-based estimation of
\gamma_{u}
)

Given the system (
2
) and the relationship (
26
), the value of
\gamma_{u}
in (
26
) is computed by solving the following SDP problem:

\displaystyle\min_{P_{u}\in{\mathbb{S}}^{n_{u}},\,\gamma_{u}>0}~~\gamma_{u}

(41)

s.t.

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}A_{u}^{\top}P_{u}+P_{u}A_{u}&~P_{u}B_{u}\\
B_{u}^{\top}P_{u}&-\gamma_{u}I\\
\end{array}\right]+\left[\begin{array}[]{c}C_{u}^{\top}\\
D_{u}^{\top}\\
\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{c}C_{u}^{\top}\\
D_{u}^{\top}\\
\end{array}\right]^{\top}\preceq 0.

\triangleleft

{pf}

The proof is straightforward by applying the cyclo-dissipative system theory once again with the storage function
V_{u}(x_{u}(t))\triangleq x_{u}(t)^{\top}P_{u}x_{u}(t)
and the supply rate
s_{u}(u_{c}(t),u_{u}(t))\triangleq\gamma_{u}\left\lVert u_{c}\right\rVert^{2}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{2}}-\left\lVert u_{u}\right\rVert^{2}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{2}}
.
\blacksquare

It is worth noting that
\gamma_{u}
is computed by solving (
41
) if all the system matrices (
2
) are certain, which does not hold true under Assumption
1
.
However, the value of
\gamma_{u}
obtained by solving (
41
) can be used as the ground truth for the data-based method that considers both Assumptions
1
-
2
in the next subsection.

Remark 2

In the case of full system knowledge, the system can also be decomposed into two parts where
\Sigma_{u}

in Proposition
1
is used to proceed with the security quantification in Theorem

4.2
Extremum-seeking estimation

Assumption
2
enables us to inject input
u_{c}
into the uncertain system
\Sigma_{u}
and measure the output
u_{u}
with the aim of approximating the value of
\gamma_{u}
. From a system theory perspective, the maximum value of
\gamma_{u}
corresponds to the maximum singular value of
\Sigma_{u}
, a fixed input frequency (says
\omega_{u}^{\star}
).
By exploiting this property,
we adopt the extremum-seeking feedback
(
Krstic and Wang, 2000
)
to approximate
\gamma_{u}
in (
26
) in the following procedure for a single-input-single-output system:

\displaystyle u_{c}(t)

\displaystyle=\sin(t\omega_{u}(t)),

(48)

\displaystyle\omega_{u}(t)

\displaystyle=\omega_{uo}+\chi(t)+\zeta(t),\,\chi(t)=\alpha_{p}\sin(\omega_{p}t+\phi_{p}),

(49)

\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}_{u}(t)

\displaystyle={\left\lVert u_{u}\right\rVert^{2}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{2}[0,t]}}/{\left\lVert u_{c}\right\rVert^{2}_{{\mathcal{L}}_{2}[0,t]}},

(50)

\displaystyle\dot{\eta}(t)

\displaystyle=\!\!-\omega_{h}\eta(t)+\omega_{h}\tilde{\gamma}_{u}(t),

(51)

\displaystyle\dot{\xi}(t)

\displaystyle=\!\!-\omega_{l}\xi(t)+\omega_{l}(\tilde{\gamma}_{u}(t)-\eta(t))\chi(t),\dot{\zeta}(t)=k\xi(t),

(52)

where
\omega_{uo}
is a base frequency,
\chi(t)
is the harmonic perturbation,
\zeta(t)
is the extremum seeking direction,
\omega_{u}(t)
is the time-varying approximate of
\omega_{u}^{\star}
,
\tilde{\gamma}_{u}(t)
is the time-varying approximate of
\gamma_{u}
in (
26
),
\eta(t)
is the output of a high pass filter, and
\xi(t)
is the output of a low pass filter. Parameters
\alpha_{p}
,
\omega_{p}
, and
\phi_{p}
of the harmonic perturbation,
\omega_{h}
of the high pass filter,
\omega_{l}
of the low pass filter, and
k
of the extremum-seeking direction are designed.
How to choose these parameters to guarantee stability can be found in
Krstic and Wang (2000)
.
It is worth noting that the system parameters
(A_{u},B_{u},C_{u},D_{u})
in (
2
) are not needed since we only need to measure the output
u_{u}
to proceed with (
48
)-(
52
), which is depicted in Fig.

)-(

) is reported in Fig.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed a metric to quantify the security of NCS when a part of the system is uncertain. We provided a computationally efficient method to determine the value of the metric when the maximum
{\mathcal{L}}_{2}
gain of the uncertain part is available. Moreover, we discussed model-based and data-based methods to obtain such a maximum
{\mathcal{L}}_{2}
gain.
The results were numerically illustrated on a power transmission grid spanning Sweden and Northern Denmark.
Future work includes addressing the scalability aspects and extension to nonlinear systems.
