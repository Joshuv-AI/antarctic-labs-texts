Immune checkpoint inhibitor–related eruptive keratoacanthoma
Authors: Kazuki Watanabe, Mitsuhito Ota
Published: 2026

An 86-year-old man presented with a 3-month history of pruritic rashes, which had gradually increased in size and number on his hands and forearms. He reported frequent exposure to ultraviolet (UV) light during outdoor work. He had been treated with pembrolizumab (programmed cell death protein 1 inhibitor) for lung adenocarcinoma for 15 months, stopping 2 months before the presentation. We identified discrete erythematous hard papules of up to 8 mm in diameter on the patient’s forearms and dorsal hands (Figure 1A). Some papules showed crateriform appearances with hyperkeratosis (Figure 1B). He had no oral mucosal lesions. We performed a skin biopsy, which showed a keratin-filled crater and irregular epidermal proliferations with mild atypical keratinocytes (Appendix 1, available at www.cmaj.ca/lookup/doi/10.1503/cmaj.252150/tab-related-content). We diagnosed eruptive keratoacanthoma attributed to pembrolizumab. We treated the patient with cryotherapy and topical corticosteroids, with complete resolution within 2 months.

Clinical presentation of immune checkpoint inhibitor–related eruptive keratoacanthoma in an 86-year-old man. (A) Discrete erythematous papules on the forearms and dorsal hands. (B) Hyperkeratotic and crateriform appearance of the tumour.

Keratoacanthoma is classified as a subtype of well-differentiated cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma. In most cases, it presents as a solitary lesion; however, multiple eruptive keratoacanthomas can develop idiopathically (Grzybowski syndrome) or in association with immune checkpoint inhibitors. Immune checkpoint inhibitor–related eruptive keratoacanthoma has been reported as a rare adverse event, developing up to 18 months after the first infusion or up to 19 weeks after discontinuation of the drugs.1,2 The pathogenesis remains unclear; however, it has been proposed that immune checkpoint inhibitors may unleash T cell–mediated inflammatory responses, leading to potentially reversible hyperplasia in the UV-induced dysplastic keratinocytes.1,3 Eruptive keratoacanthomas appear as multiple, erythematous, hyperkeratotic crateriform papules and nodules, mainly on the UV-exposed extremities, sometimes with scaling or lichenification with pruritus. Lesion biopsy with pathology confirms the diagnosis.1,2 Differential diagnoses include viral warts and molluscum contagiosum. Treatment options, such as topical corticosteroids, topical 5-fluorouracil, intralesional corticosteroids, oral retinoids, cyclosporine, methotrexate, cryotherapy, and curettage are often curative. 3 Continuation of immunotherapy may be feasible.2
