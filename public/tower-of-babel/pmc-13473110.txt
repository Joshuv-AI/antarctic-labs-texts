Species-specific assessment of climate change vulnerability in Himalayan Pikas and identification of at-risk elevational and latitudinal zones
Authors: Nishma Dahal, Pritam Chhetri, Sunil Kumar, Rajat Nayak, Vineet K Dubey, Aashna Sharma, Barry R Noon, Uma Ramakrishnan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractClimate change is rapidly transforming high-altitude ecosystems, placing cold-adapted species at risk. We assessed the vulnerability of four Himalayan Pika species (Ochotona macrotis, O. roylei, O. nubrica, and O. sikimaria) by integrating species distribution models with landscape metrics and sensitivity indices. Using verified occurrence records and bioclimatic variables, we projected habitat suitability under moderate (SSP 2-4.5) and extreme (SSP 5-8.5) emission scenarios for 2050 and 2070. We compared eight ecological and anthropogenic indices for two dispersal scenarios to identify species most at risk. Species responses varied, but overall exposure and fragmentation were estimated to decline, with concurrent decline in Protected Area (PA) coverage and increase Human Footprint (HFP). HFP is predicted to increase for most species under a highly restricted dispersal scenario (constrained to the current species’ range). Overall, we observed habitat loss, gain and stability within the same elevation bands for three species - O. macrotis and O. nubrica above 4000 m, and for O. sikimaria above 3500 m. Ochotona roylei is predicted to follow upslope range tracking, with habitat loss between 3000 – 4000 m, stable habitat at 4000 - 4500m and gain above 4500 m. Models predicted vulnerability at higher elevations (>4500 m) and latitudes (33–34°N), for O. nubrica, contrasting with the typical trailing-edge loss seen in other pikas. Projections for O. macrotis suggested loss at high latitudes (34–35°N) of its sampled range, indicative of vulnerability driven by internal range fragmentation rather than latitudinal tracking. Based on habitat specialization, sensitivity to climate drivers, and projected range change, our study identified O. roylei as the most vulnerable species. Diverse species-specific habitat and climate drivers challenge the assumption of uniform upward elevational shifts driven solely by climate change. Introduction

Climate warming is predicted to drive both shifts to higher elevations and latitudes as organisms track dynamic thermal niches across montane and temperate ecosystems1–4. Global evidence suggests that many species have already shifted their elevation range by ~11 meters per decade and poleward by ~17 km per decade5. With dynamic ranges driven by climate change, species are expected to be on the move, emphasizing the importance of species’ ecology for implementing effective conservation measures. However, range shifts are not uniform across taxa or regions and are often constrained by habitat availability, landscape structure and species-specific traits like dispersal and adaptive capacity.

Pikas (Mammalia: Ochotonidae: Ochotona) are highly sensitive to climate change and serve as ideal model species for studying the ecological impacts of environmental change6. Range-restricted montane species like pikas particularly are informative model systems for examining how ecological specialization and limited dispersal mediate responses to climate-driven environmental change. Insights from well-studied American pikas indicate that climate driven range shifts are often constrained by habitat availability at higher elevations (e.g., presence of talus patch)7,8. These findings highlight that projected climatic suitability does not necessarily translate into realised range expansion. In contrast, for lesser known Asian pikas, species distribution models (SDM) often lack the resolution needed to identify suitable habitats and assess potential range shifts under climate change9.

The Himalayan mountains support six species of pikas each with distinct habitat associations and elevational distributions10. Given the lack of data on the current status of most pika species of the Himalayas, existing IUCN assessments are speculative and species’ susceptibility to future environmental changes poorly known. Of additional concern, all Himalayan species exhibit low genomic diversity11 suggesting potentially reduced genetic adaptation to future climate change. Elevations in the Himalayas above 4000 m have warmed ~ 0.5°C per decade - more than double the regional average of 0.2°C. Projections indicate a total rise of 2.6 to 4.6°C across the Hindu Kush Himalayan region by the end of this century12. Rapid warming could significantly alter the current distribution of pikas, to cooler habitats at higher elevations and latitudes. The Himalayan arc spans roughly 10 degrees of latitude (~26–36°N) and transitions sharply from monsoon-dominated Southeast (Eastern Himalaya) to the cold, arid northwest (Western) Himalaya. The wetter Eastern Himalaya usually have shallower lapse rate compared to the drier Western Himalaya13. A shallower lapse rate implies a weaker altitudinal temperature gradient, suggesting that elevation-dependent warming (i.e., the tendency for stronger warming at a higher elevation) is less pronounced in Eastern Himalaya compared to Western Himalaya, affecting temperature seasonality, snow persistence, growing season length, etc. While temperature is directly known to affect pikas, snowpack persistence and growing season impact their winter survival14,15. Elevation is often recognized as the primary driver of ecological zonation in the Himalaya, while latitudinal zonation has received comparatively less attention in regional climatology literature (e.g. Bahuguna et al.16. Considering both elevation and the latitudinal gradient is important for understanding species’ responses to climate change. In the Himalaya, its hourglass shape can initially increase the suitable area during upslope range shift, with range expansion up to ~5000m; however, beyond this elevation threshold, the area decreases, resulting in range contraction3.

A common approach to assess climate impacts is to estimate the amount and distribution of currently suitable habitat and project its availability under future scenarios, assuming niche conservatism17. However, changes in species’ range alone may not fully capture vulnerability to climate change, as both species and regions differ in their responses to environmental changes18, leading to variation in climate sensitivity. Climate change may further move suitable habitats beyond the bounds of current PAs19, undermining conservation efforts20. We assessed the vulnerability of two narrow-ranging species (Ochotona nubrica and O. sikimaria) and two wide-ranging species (O. roylei and O. macrotis), differing in elevational and latitudinal distributions as well as habitat associations10. In addition to assessing distributional changes, we evaluated complementary metrics including exposure, fragmentation (Fr), protected area coverage (PA), Human Footprint Index (HFP), sensitivity, and specialization to provide a comprehensive vulnerability assessment21. Using SDM and vulnerability indices, we: (a) looked for evidence of upslope and poleward shifts, (b) identified elevational and latitudinal zones posing risks to persistence, and (c) compared species-level vulnerabilities to inform conservation priorities.

Methodology

Species distribution modeling

Target species and study site

Asian pikas are morphologically similar with overlapping ranges, making field identification difficult22. To address this challenge, we only used occurrences that were either genetically and/or photographically verified by experts. Our study focused on four (O. sikimaria, O. nubrica, and O. roylei, and O. macrotis) out of the six pika species found in Himalaya. Of the two species not included in the study, O. curzoniae have only a small part of their range within our study area and we did not detect the presence of O. forresti along any of the 11 elevation transects sampled across the Himalayas (for details, see Dahal et al., [10]). For O. macrotis, whose IUCN red listed range23 is extremely large compared to our sampled occurrence range, we estimated its distribution only within the Himalayan extent to avoid model extrapolation. For all the other species, distributions were predicted on the basis of geographic ranges delineated by the IUCN Red List24,25 (Fig. 1).

Occurrence data and environmental variables

Most of the occurrence data used in the current study are from Dahal et al.10, based on systematically surveyed elevation transects which covered the elevation range of each species. To avoid spatial autocorrelation and reduce model overfitting to training data27, spatial filtering was done using the Spatially Rarefy Occurrence Data tool implemented in SDMtoolbox in ArcGIS version 10.8.2. Occurrence points were thinned by retaining only one record within 1 km radius. This thinning distance was explicitly selected to match the spatial resolution of the environmental predictors and to reduce the influence of clustered samples on model performance. Additionally, we plotted occurrences on a multivariate heterogeneity surface derived using all the bioclimatic and topographic variables and selected non-overlapping occurrences in environmental space to ensure an even representation of environmental conditions across study area. The final set of occurrences, after correcting for spatial autocorrelation, is presented in Table 1. Climatic and other environmental variables (downloaded from the WorldClim version 2.1 at 30 arc-second spatial resolution) were selected based on their biological relevance and the thermal sensitivities of different pika species6,8,10,11.

We downloaded the Digital Elevation Model (http://srtm.csi.cgiar.org/SELECTION/inputCoord.asp) and derived seven topographical variables: (1) Elevation difference DEM (SDDEM)- standard deviation of elevation from the mean of elevation around 2 km sq radius was used as a proxy for topographic heterogeneity (2) mean DEM (average elevation within 2 km distance), (3) topographic exposure (TE)28 - represents the degree of the shelter afforded to a location which can affect surface temperature, and evaporation (derived by subtracting DEM with mean DEM within 2 km distance), (4) slope - measures terrain steepness and its influences on water runoff, soil erosion, and vegetation structure (5) slope difference (standard deviation of slope)- captures spatial variability in slope, representing surface roughness and terrain heterogeneity, (6) northness, and (7) eastness - represent slope orientation, affecting microclimate (north-facing slopes are cooler and wetter, while east-facing slopes receive early sunlight).

Prior to identifying the variables that best defined a species’ climatic niche, we removed highly correlated variables (Pearson’s correlation coefficient, r ≥0.70), as spatial autocorrelation of variables can lead to inflated variable importance29. Amongst correlated variables, we selected those we hypothesized to be most ecologically relevant to the species. Empirical studies from the Himalaya and Tibetan Plateau demonstrate strong associations between pika occurrence and thermal regimes, precipitation seasonality, talus structure, and forage availability. One, or more, precipitation and temperature related variables were evaluated during model fitting for all the species and for the species dependent on talus, we included variables that reflect terrain heterogeneity and habitat structure. The vifstep R function was used to calculate the variance inflation factor (VIF), and variables with VIF<1030 were available for selection when estimating the climate niche of a species. The final variables selected for each species are presented in Table 1.

Model development and projection

We developed SDMs by correlating species occurrences with bioclimatic and topographic variables. Pseudo-absence records were generated using the gRandom function available in the R package SDM (version 4.2.2) within a calibration area defined by each species’ known Himalayan range, delimited using current IUCN distribution polygons. Following the recommendations of Barbet-Massin et al.31, we selected 1000 pseudo absences for model calibration, a sampling strategy that has also been applied in recent species distribution modelling studies32,33. We then used a 70% random sample of initial data (presence only) as training data and evaluated the training set against the 30% test set. We repeated split sampling regime five times to account for the uncertainty associated with data partition34. We subsequently used the R package ‘SDM’30 to develop an ensemble species distribution model. The ensemble approach was chosen because it combines predictions from a set of models, which may include different spatial predictors, creating a more robust and reliable prediction35.

The predictive performance of the models was evaluated using subsampling and bootstrap procedures with three repetitions each and assessed using area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) and True Skill Statistics (TSS)36. The AUC measures the overall discrimination capacity of the model37 and the TSS balances the capacity to correctly predict presences and pseudo-absences38. Overall, our final ensembled models were based on seven distinct models: Multiple Adaptive Regression Splines (MARS)39, (Generalized Additive Models (GAM),40, Mixture Discriminant Analysis (MDA)41, Random forests (RF)42, Boosted Regression Trees (BRT)43, Generalized Linear Models (GLM)44, and Flexible Discriminant Analysis (FDA)45 for the ensemble model.

To reduce the influence of outliers and provide a robust consensus prediction46, the final suitability estimate for each pixel was based on its median value across all seven models. Ensemble models were projected to future periods (2050 and 2070) using two Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs): SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5. SSP2-4.5 represents a moderate climate change scenario, where greenhouse gas emissions peak around mid-century and then decline47. SSP5-8.5 represents an extreme emissions scenario characterised by high fossil fuel use and minimal climate policy48. These pathways were selected to capture a range of alternative future climate outcomes. To account for uncertainty in climate projections, five widely used General Circulation Models (GCMs) from Phase 6 of the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project (CMIP)49: ACCESS-CM2, CNRM-ESM2-1, HadGEM3-GC31-LL, MPI-ESM1-2-HR, and MRI-ESM2-0 were used. To minimize model-specific biases, a pixel-wise median was calculated across the five raster layers derived from different GCMs for each bioclimatic variable. All models, both current and future, were converted to binary predictions by thresholding continuous suitability values using the Minimum Training Presence (MTP). The MTP approach uses the lowest predicted suitability value across all training occurrences, ensuring that all observed presences are included within the predicted suitable area (i.e., omission error = 0 for the training data)50. The spatial distribution and habitat suitability model for four Pika species under current and future climatic scenarios are presented in Supplementary Figure S1.

Dispersal assumptions

We considered two dispersal scenarios – Scenario 1: species were restricted to their present suitable habitat as predicted by the model, with no ability to colonize newly available suitable areas, Scenario 2: species where dispersal was allowed in available suitable habitats within trait-informed buffer zones (see Mancini et al.51). The maximum dispersal (buffer) distance was similar to Mancini et al.51 but we adopted a more conservative scaling approach using generation length in place of age at the first reproduction, as most Himalayan Pikas have limited dispersal ability (Table S7).

Buffer distance = (*Horizon / Generation length) × Dispersal distance per reproductive event.

* Horizon = future time - present time

Given the strong site fidelity and unique life-history constraints of high-altitude specialized taxa like pikas, we parameterized dispersal distance using direct empirical field observations from closely related Ochotona species (summarized in Supplementary Table S7). Drawing from mark-recapture data and long-term field studies in natural populations (e.g., O. curzoniae52, O. roylei53, we applied a conservative generation time of 1 year and an age at first reproduction of 10 months across our focal species. Pikas exhibit restricted movement54, therefore, dispersal distance per reproductive event was capped at 60 m. Using a baseline year of 2000, our projection horizons were defined as 50 years for the 2050 scenarios and 70 years for the 2070 scenarios. To define accessible areas under future climate change scenarios, binary suitability maps (for both 2050 and 2070) were clipped using the current suitable habitat polygon to represent no dispersal (Scenario 1) and the buffered polygon to represent maximum dispersal (Scenario 2). These clipped rasters defined the future suitable habitat under each dispersal scenario for all subsequent spatial analyses.

Overall vulnerability index

Species vulnerability was assessed using eight indices (representing climatic sensitivity, landscape structure, and anthropogenic pressure) – namely, specialization, marginality, sensitivity, fragmentation, range change, human footprint, protected area coverage, and exposure. Specialization and marginality were derived from the climatic variables used in the species distribution models following Hirzel et al.55, and sensitivity was calculated as the mean of specialization and marginality following Cianfrani et al.21. Habitat fragmentation was quantified using landscape metrics calculated from suitable habitat patches. Anthropogenic pressure was assessed using the Global Human Footprint dataset, and PA coverage was estimated by overlaying suitable habitats with national 56 and global 57 PA layers. Similarly, we also measured the percentage of suitable habitat falling within PAs to understand if future shifts may extend beyond current PA networks58 and assessed human footprint, which represents the average anthropogenic pressure within each species’ suitable range. Range change was calculated as the percentage difference between current and projected suitable habitats under future climate scenarios. A summary of all indices, their descriptions, formulas, and data sources is provided in Table 2, while the vulnerability component conversion used to standardize these indices is provided in Supplementary Table S8, adapted from Cianfrani et al.21.

Identifying vulnerable geographic regions

Median values from the binary maps were taken and results were grouped under medium (SSP 2-4.5) and extreme (SSP 5-8.5) climate change scenarios for the years 2050 and 2070. Results were then considered for two dispersal scenarios (Scenario 1 < 2). We then compared current and future binary maps under each dispersal assumption. Range change maps were overlaid on elevation and latitudinal layers to identify regions associated with highest loss, stability and gain. The number of pixels were classified as loss (present in current but absent in future), gain (absent in current but present in future) and stable (present in both time periods) were grouped in elevation and latitudinal bands. These pixels were grouped into predefined elevation and latitudinal bands (summarized in Table 4).

Results

Niche model performance and variable importance

We only retained models with TSS ≥ 0.7 36 and AUC > 0.9 (Swets, 1988); Random Forest (AUC range 0.91 - 0.98 &TSS range 0.75 - 0.92), followed by GAM (AUC range 0.84 - 0.92 & TSS range 0.71 - 0.88) and BRT (AUC range 0.87 – 0.95, TSS range 0.73 - 0.85). Detailed evaluation metrics for all algorithms are provided in Supplementary Table S2. In terms of variable importance, precipitation seasonality had the highest relative importance for three pika species (O. sikimaria, O. nubrica, and O. roylei) (Supplementary Table S3), and the mean elevation was the most important variable for O. macrotis. Response curves (Figure S4) revealed clear, species-specific, and often nonlinear relationships between habitat suitability and key climatic predictors. For O. sikimaria, O. nubrica, and O. roylei, suitability showed a pronounced unimodal response to precipitation seasonality, with optimal suitability occurring at intermediate values and declining sharply at higher seasonality levels, indicating sensitivity to intra-annual precipitation variability. In contrast, O. macrotis exhibited a strong positive response to elevation, with suitability increasing consistently with mean elevation, reflecting its dependence on high-elevation environments. However, given the restricted geographic sampling of O. macrotis (see Fig. 1), this pattern reflects the environmental conditions of only the sampled area and not of the entire geographic range of O. macrotis. Across species, response curves also indicated thresholds beyond which suitability declined rapidly, suggesting potential climatic limits to persistence under future warming conditions.

Species exposure to climate change: range change, fragmentation, protection, and human disturbance

Projected range shifts indicated species-specific responses to climate change. Ochotona macrotis is estimated to exhibit the most severe contraction in suitable habitat within the modelled extent, particularly under dispersal scenario 2. Similarly, O. roylei is estimated to experience a significant decrease in range. In contrast, O. nubrica is expected to demonstrate the highest potential for expansion under favourable dispersal scenarios. Our models inferred relatively greater range expansion for O. sikimaria in dispersal scenario 2 compared to scenario 1. Patterns of habitat structure and anthropogenic pressure were also estimated to vary among species. Ochotona nubrica and O. macrotis are predicted to maintain higher habitat connectivity, whereas the highest fragmentation risk is estimated for O. roylei. Species sensitivity to climate change: niche specialization and marginality

Climatic niche indices revealed clear interspecific differences in vulnerability. O. roylei was estimated to show the strongest specialization and sensitivity, particularly under restrictive dispersal conditions, indicating a highly restricted climatic niche. In contrast, O. sikimaria was projected to exhibit scenario dependent variation, with higher specialization and marginality under buffered conditions but lower values when restricted to its current suitable habitat. Both O. macrotis and O. nubrica were estimated to show comparatively low specialization, marginality, and sensitivity, suggesting broader climatic tolerance. These patterns indicate O. roylei as the most climatically constrained species among the four taxa. Results are summarized in Table 3 and Fig. 2, with detailed values provided in Table S5.

Overall vulnerability index (VI)

The estimated VI varied across species, with an overall worsening effect as dispersal constraints became more restrictive. However, for a small range, O. sikimaria, both dispersal scenarios produced similar outcomes. Exposure and fragmentation consistently show improvements in future, but this improvement did not translate in PA coverage for O. sikimaria. Overall, O. roylei appeared to be the most at risk as the majority of indices fall on the negative side (also reflected in Fig. 2) and core area was also estimated to show reduction (Fig. 3). However, as dispersal constraint is decreased, the risk declines significantly, indicating that even moderate dispersal capacity can buffer projected risks. Other species show mixed responses. The HFP and PA coverage appear to remain the same across timeframes (Fig. 2, Table S5).

Latitudinal and elevational trends in habitat change

Among the four species, O. roylei is predicted to display the expected climate-consistent pattern of range tracking, both elevational and poleward. Along the latitudinal gradient, O. roylei showed habitat loss at lower latitudes (28–29° N), stability toward higher latitudes (34–36° N), and gains at intermediate bands. Along the elevational gradient, the models predicted habitat loss at lower elevations (3000–4000 m), stability at mid elevations (4000–4500 m), and habitat gains above 4500 m (refer to Table 4 & Table S6). The results were consistent across the two dispersal scenarios. Hence, for O. roylei, lower altitudes and latitudes appear to be regions of highest risk.

Patterns for the other three species – O. macrotis, O. nubrica, and O. sikimaria were non- directional with simultaneous habitat loss, gain, and stability within the same elevational and latitudinal bands (Table 4 & Table S6). Overall, for these three species, risk zones appeared to overlap with zones of stability and gain. For O. macrotis, risk was predicted at elevations above 4000 m and mid-latitudes (34–35°N) of its sampled range, O. nubrica showed the highest risk at higher elevations (above 4500 m) and mid-latitudes (33–34°N). Ochotona sikimaria displayed loss above 3500m, however stability was also predicted for the same elevation zone. Latitudinal risk could not be robustly inferred for O. sikimaria due to limited spatial extent (for detailed latitudinal and elevational pattern of loss, gain and stable habitat ranges, refer to Supplementary Table S6).

Discussion

Niche Shifts Under Climate Scenario SSP 2-4.5: Globally, multiple species are experiencing significant range shifts presumably driven by climate-induced changes to their environments60. In the Himalaya, steep elevation gain creates sharp climatic zonation, potentially leading to rapid shifts in distributional ranges. The cold-adapted species, therefore, may deviate from the gradual, conventional, poleward or elevational upslope movement in response to abrupt climatic changes. Based on our niche models, we predict O. macrotis to expand its distribution toward lower latitudes (28–29°N) while also shifting upward in elevation (up to 5000 m), likely tracking colder habitats at higher elevations near the equator. However, given the restricted occurrence data available for O. macrotis, these projections likely represent only a subset of its realized niche and should be interpreted cautiously. Similar patterns have been observed in riverine ecotones, where latitude and elevation interact to shape the distributions of cold-adapted species61. This suggests that for the high-altitude specialists, upward shifts can compensate for latitudinal movement, maintaining thermal niche conditions even near the equator. The fate of O. macrotis (vulnerable at 4000–5000 m and 34–35°N latitude), is similar to O. princeps in North America, with local extinction risks at high elevations7 although such comparisons remain speculative given data limitations. In contrast, O. roylei is estimated to continue its occupancy of stable habitats and expand its range to include higher latitudes (33–34°N). Overall, this species is projected to experience upward gains (4500–5000 m) with habitat losses at lower elevations (3000–4000 m) and latitudes (28–29°), reinforcing the standard predictions of poleward and higher elevation movement62,63. However, O. roylei is vulnerable at lower elevations (3000–4000 m) and latitudes (28°–29°N), where their densities are high64. A more complex pattern is predicted for the narrow-ranging O. nubrica – with habitat gains at the lower latitudes (28–29°N) and mid-elevations (4000–5000 m), while retreating from higher latitudes (33–34°N) and high elevational bands (4500–5000 m), suggesting a response shaped by localized environmental pressures rather than a uniform poleward advance65. Despite having the broadest niche breadth10, O. nubrica relies on cold-adapted shrub-steppe microhabitats sensitive to warming and precipitation changes22,66. For O. sikimaria, which already occupies a small range, stability is predicted at higher elevations (4000-5000 m) and loss at low to mid-elevations (3500–4500 m) suggesting a loss in the extent of its suitable habitat. Further, O. sikimaria shows limited adaptive ability and high overlap with human settlements (e.g., experiencing conflicts with farmers due to their foraging habits). The current PA network overlaps O. sikimaria’s habitat, suggesting existing PAs may provide some climate refugia.

All studied species are predicted to experience range expansion under unrestricted dispersal (scenario 2) but are expected to experience range contraction under restricted dispersal (scenario 1). This finding highlights the critical role of dispersal in tracking changes in the distribution of suitable climate. Although PAs offer partial buffering against climate change, their effectiveness is dependent on landscape connectivity to facilitate dispersal and persistence under future climate conditions. Moreover, increasing human use of Himalayan landscapes may undermine the effectiveness of PAs in ensuring long-term persistence despite declines in fragmentation and greater PA coverage. Across species, the mismatch in exposure and fragmentation with PA coverage highlight improving PA coverage, connectivity and decreasing human footprint index (HFP) should be the conservation priorities for conservation of Himalayan Pikas.

Niche Shifts Under Climate Scenario SSP 5-8.5: In contrast to scenario 2-4.5, niche shifts under more extreme climatic changes projected declines in geographic range and niche breadth (high specialization). Declines in species’ marginality, specialization, and sensitivity indices indicate that dispersing from previously suitable habitats and ranges presupposes the existence of newly suitable habitats that are accessible via dispersal. However, our niche models projected that species would be forced into constrained, specialized, and unstable environmental niches. These spatially constrained niche and range shifts stand in contrast to the relatively minor shifts in niche breadth (low specialization) and distribution projected under scenario 2-4.5. Projected range change metrics illustrated the necessity of movement—scenario 5-8.5 predicted consistent habitat loss, whereas scenario 2-4.5 predicted a generalized adaptive response to climate change. Even in the case of O. sikimaria which was projected to expand to a larger protected under 5-8.5, this apparent adaptive ability came at the cost of extreme niche specialization.

Among the four species, O. roylei was predicted to be most vulnerable to future climate change. This species exhibited the highest levels of climatic specialization and sensitivity and showed consistent declines in suitable habitat across scenarios, particularly under the restricted dispersal scenario. Its dependence on dispersal to colonize suitable habitat, combined with reduced PA coverage under restricted dispersal, suggests limited resilience to climate change. Although PA overlap improves slightly when dispersal is enabled under the buffered scenario, the species is constrained by its narrow ecological niche and declining habitat availability. Its core elevational range, estimated between 3500 and 4500 m, further increases vulnerability due to potential human disturbance and conflict with local farmers. O. nubrica was predicted to experience minimal habitat fragmentation and potential range expansion under dispersal-enabled conditions. However, its future distribution may be compromised by declining PA coverage. O. macrotis is estimated to display moderate sensitivity and marginality within its sampled range but may benefit from reduced fragmentation and human pressure and expand its range under conditions that permit dispersal. O. sikimaria’s future niche is projected to be influenced by trade-offs between declines in habitat connectivity and anthropogenic pressure, and dispersal abilities which enables movement into less disturbed and better protected habitats. However, dispersal may be to alpine environments which are more naturally fragmented. Overall, our results suggest that O. roylei may be the most climatically constrained and vulnerable species among the four taxa. This is a consequence of the species’ requirement for highly specialized talus habitat with adjacent vegetation in its elevational limits67.

Overall, the persistence of local populations in stable core areas, where fragmentation is not projected to increase (Fig. 2, Table S5), could help maintain the species in the Himalayas’ limited habitats. Notably, all Himalayan pikas exhibit low overall genomic diversity11, which may constrain their adaptive potential under climate change. This genetic limitation, combined with the ecological sensitivity highlighted by this study, suggests that maintaining landscape connectivity in the context of climate change is essential. Corridors that facilitate gene flow across major physiographic barriers should be prioritized to prevent isolation and inbreeding. Conservation planning must focus on securing mid- to high-elevation refugia, mitigating habitat fragmentation, expanding PAs, and maintaining climate-resilient corridors. These steps are essential for enhancing both ecological and evolutionary resilience of Himalayan Pikas in a rapidly changing environment.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrates that cold-specialist pikas in the Himalayas exhibit species-specific patterns of elevational and poleward migration in response to anthropogenic-induced climate change. By evaluating eight vulnerability parameters across two dispersal scenarios, we identified O. roylei as the taxon at highest risk. Restricted dispersal ability, small range size, and limited colonization potential significantly elevated extinction risks for this species. Our research identified the maintenance and extension of landscape connectivity as the primary factor in mitigating climate-driven range contraction. While our study provide essential insights into habitat shifts under varying dispersal assumptions, they do not incorporate future land-use and land-cover (LULC) changes, such as agricultural expansion and livestock grazing, which may physically obstruct the corridors essential for dispersal under climate change. In addition, for O. macrotis, our result is based on a limited portion of its range within the Himalaya, so future studies should incorporate occurrence data across the entire range of O. macrotis to improve model generality and inference. Future research should prioritize the integration of high-resolution remote-sensing LULC data and circuit-theory-based connectivity models to refine these species-specific dispersal kernels. Further, given the importance of genomic diversity in a species adaptive capacity, future SDMs should include the adaptive capacity of species as dictated by their genomic diversity. This is particularly relevant to Himalayan pikas which have exceptionally low genetic diversity11.
