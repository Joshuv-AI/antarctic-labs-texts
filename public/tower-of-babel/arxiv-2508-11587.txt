Inversions in parking functions
Kyle Celano, Jennifer Elder, Kimberly P. Hadaway, Pamela E. Harris, Amanda Priestley, Gabe Udell

Abstract
. In this paper, we obtain a q q -exponential generating function for inversions on parking functions via symmetric function theory and also through a direct bijection to rooted labeled forests. We then apply these techniques to unit interval parking functions to give analogous results. We conclude by introducing a probabilistic approach through which we obtain formulas for the total number of inversions and several other statistics across all parking functions and other sets of words closed under rearrangement.



1.
Introduction

Throughout, let
\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,\dots\}
, and if
n\in\mathbb{N}
, then
[n]=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
.
Also, let
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
denote the set of permutations of
[n]
.
Konheim and Weiss
[
30
]
defined parking functions as follows: consider a one-way street with
n
parking spots (labeled in increasing order) and
n
cars, each of which has a preferred spot.
As each car parks, it drives to its preferred spot and parks there if it is unoccupied.
If that spot is occupied, then the car continues driving and parks in the next available spot, if any exists.
We encode the information of the preferred spots as a positive integer-valued
preference list

\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{n})\in[n]^{n}
, where for each
i\in[n]
, the positive integer
\alpha_{i}
indicates the preferred spot of car
i
.
We say the preference list
\alpha
is a
parking function
if all cars are able to park using the aforementioned parking rule.
The
outcome permutation
of
\alpha
, denoted
\pi(\alpha)
, is defined by setting
\pi(\alpha)(i)=j
if car
j
parks in spot
i
for each
i\in[n]
.
We let
\PF_{n}
be the set of all parking functions of length
n
.
Konheim and Weiss establish
that the number of parking functions of length
n
is

(n+1)^{n-1}
, see
[
30
, Lemma 1]
.
A second proof of this count was given by
Pollak by parking the cars on a circle with one new parking spot, see
[
16
]
.

Introduced by Hadaway
[
25
]
, a
unit interval parking function
of length
n
is a parking function
\alpha\in\PF_{n}
such that
\pi(\alpha)^{-1}(i)-\alpha_{i}\leq 1
for all
i\in[n]
.
In other words, these are parking functions in which, for all
i\in[n]
, car
i
parks either in its preference
a_{i}
or in the spot after its preference
a_{i}+1
.
Let
\UPF_{n}
denote the set of unit interval parking functions of length
n
.
Hadaway
[
25
]
proves that
|\UPF_{n}|=\Fub_{n}
, which is the
n^{th}
Fubini number
and counts the number of ordered set partitions of
[n]

[
29
,
A000670
]
.
Unit interval parking functions have been studied in connection to enumerating Boolean intervals of the weak Bruhat order of the symmetric group and in connection to the faces of the permutohedron
[
9
,
13
]
.

We are interested in the study of inversions of parking functions and unit interval parking functions.
Recall that for a word
w\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, an
inversion
is a pair
(i,j)
of integers in
[n]
such that
i<j
and
w_{i}>w_{j}
.
We denote the set of inversions of a word
w
by
\Inv(w)
and let
\inv(w)=|\Inv(w)|
.
Treating parking functions as words, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define the polynomials

\PF_{n}(q)=\sum_{\alpha\in\PF_{n}}q^{\inv(\alpha)}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\UPF_{n}(q)=\sum_{\alpha\in\UPF_{n}}q^{\inv(\alpha)},

and set
\PF_{0}(q)=\UPF_{0}(q)=1
.
The inversion generating function
\PF_{n}(q)
appeared incidentally in
[
37
, Section 8]
in their study of remixed Eulerian numbers and as we explain in
Section

3
, it appears as a specialization of certain symmetric functions studied in
[
28
]
. Beyond this, little has been said about inversions in parking functions directly.

Define

\Exp_{q}(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}q^{\binom{n}{2}}\frac{z^{n}}{[n]_{q}!}\quad\mbox{and }\quad\exp_{q}(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{z^{n}}{[n]_{q}!},

where
[n]_{q}=1+q+\cdots+q^{n-1}
and
[n]_{q}!=[n]_{q}[n-1]_{q}\cdots[1]_{q}
.
With these definitions, using combinatorial techniques, we derive the following generating functions.

Theorem 1.1
.

The
q
-exponential generating functions for
\PF_{n}(q)
and
\UPF_{n}(q)
are

\sum_{n\geq 0}\PF_{n}(q)\frac{z^{n+1}}{[n]_{q}!}=(z\Exp_{q}(-z))^{\langle-1\rangle}\quad\mbox{and }\quad\sum_{n\geq 0}\UPF_{n}(q)\frac{z^{n}}{[n]_{q}!}=\frac{1}{2-\exp_{q}(z)},

where
F(z)^{\langle-1\rangle}
denotes the compositional inverse of
F(z)
.

Theorem

1.1
gives
q
-analogues of classical results of Konheim and Weiss
[
30
]
and Cayley
[
7
]
, respectively:

(1)

\sum_{n\geq 0}|\PF_{n}|\frac{z^{n+1}}{n!}=(ze^{-z})^{\langle-1\rangle}\quad\mbox{and }\quad\sum_{n\geq 0}|\UPF_{n}|\frac{z^{n}}{n!}=\frac{1}{2-e^{z}},

where the latter result follows from the bijection between unit interval parking functions and Cayley permutations (see
[
25
]
).
We also show that the equations in (
1

Theorem 1.2
.

There are
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-modules
\mathbb{C}[\PF_{n}]
and
\mathbb{C}[\UPF_{n}]
such that

(2)

\sum_{n\geq 0}\ch\mathbb{C}[\PF_{n}]z^{n+1}=(zE(-z))^{\langle-1\rangle}\quad\mbox{and }\quad\sum_{n\geq 0}\ch\mathbb{C}[\UPF_{n}]z^{n}=\frac{1}{2-H(z)}\ ,

where
\ch
denotes the Frobenius characteristic map from
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-modules to symmetric functions of degree
n
.
Here,

E(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}e_{n}z^{n}
and
H(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}h_{n}z^{n}

are generating functions for the elementary symmetric functions and the complete homogeneous symmetric functions, respectively.

The Frobenius image in (
2

[
28

. In
Section

3

Section

2

Theorem

1.2
gives a second proof of
Theorem

1.1
. For unit interval parking functions, we provide two ways of computing its Frobenius characteristic in
Section

2.
Labeled rooted forests

The main objective of this section is to describe a statistic on rooted forests, which by way of a bijection to parking functions, maps to inversions of parking functions.
We use standard definitions from graph theory for trees, forests, rooted trees, and rooted forests, for more on those topics we recommend
[
11
]
.
To begin, recall that parking functions are in bijection with combinatorial objects called
labeled rooted forests

[
30
]
, where a rooted forest is made up of rooted trees in which every vertex is given a unique integer.
We denote the set of labeled rooted forests on vertex set
[n]
by
\F_{n}
.
If
T
is a rooted labeled tree and
v
is a nonroot vertex of
T
, define the
parent
of
v

v
to the root of
T

We say that
v
is a
child
of

p(v)=u
.
For a labeled rooted tree
T

r(T)
denote its root.
The
subtrees
of a labeled rooted tree
T
are labeled rooted subtrees
T_{1},T_{2},\dots,T_{k}
such that
r(T_{i})
is adjacent in
T
to
r(T)
for each
i\in[k]
, where we order the trees so that
r(T_{1})<r(T_{2})<\cdots<r(T_{k})

An unlabeled rooted tree is called a
rooted plane tree
provided the children of each internal vertex are given a total ordering.
A forest of rooted planar trees is a
rooted plane forest

if the forests are given a total ordering.
Next, we introduce technical definitions used in our proofs.

Let

3.
Inversions in parking functions

In this section, we investigate inversions for parking functions by combining the bijection
\rho:\F_{n}\to\PF_{n}
with the classical parking function symmetric function of Haiman
[
28
]
.
We assume a basic understanding of symmetric functions and
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-representation theory e.g.
[
51
, Chapter 7]
and
[
43
]
.

We set the following notation for this and subsequent sections. A
weak composition
of
n
with
k
parts is a sequence
(c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{k})
of nonnegative integers satisfying
\sum_{i=1}^{k}c_{i}=n
.
A
composition
is a weak composition with only positive parts.
We let
\ell(c)
be the number of parts of a weak composition
c
.
Let
\Comp(n,k)
and
\WComp(n,k)
to be the set of compositions and weak compositions, respectively, of
n
with
k
parts.
Also, let
\Comp(n)=\bigcup_{k}\Comp(n,k)
and likewise
\WComp(n)=\bigcup_{k}\WComp(n,k)
.
Given a word
w\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
with largest value
m
, the
content
of
w
, denoted by
\con(w)=(c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{m})\in\WComp(n)
is defined by setting
c_{i}
to be the number of times the value
i
appears in
w
for each
i\in[m]
.

Recall that the
complete homogeneous symmetric function

h_{c}
is defined by
h_{c}=h_{c_{1}}h_{c_{2}}\cdots
where for all positive integers
k
, we have

h_{k}\coloneqq\sum_{1\leq i_{1}\leq i_{2}\leq\cdots\leq i_{k}}x_{i_{1}}x_{i_{2}}\cdots x_{i_{k}},

and
h_{0}\coloneqq 1
.
Similarly, the
elementary symmetric function

e_{c}
is defined by
e_{c}=e_{c_{1}}e_{c_{2}}\cdots
, where for all positive integers
k
, we have

e_{k}\coloneqq\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{k}}x_{i_{1}}x_{i_{2}}\cdots x_{i_{k}},

and
e_{0}\coloneqq 1
.
Finally, the
power-sum symmetric functions

is defined by
p_{c}=p_{c_{1}}p_{c_{2}}\cdots
, where for all positive integers
k
, we have

p_{k}\coloneqq\sum_{i\geq 1}x_{i}^{k},

and
p_{0}\coloneqq 1
.

If
X
is a finite set, let
\mathbb{C}[X]
denote the vector space over
\mathbb{C}
with basis
X
.
If a group
G
acts on
X
, we abuse notation and let
\mathbb{C}[X]
also denote the standard
G
-module defined by the action of
G
on the basis
X
(thus extending by linearity to the entire vector space).
For an
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-module
V
with character
\chi
, the
Frobenius image
is

\ch(V)=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\chi(\sigma)p_{cyc(\sigma)},

where
cyc(\sigma)
denotes the cycle type of
\sigma
. The map
\ch
from the set of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-modules to symmetric functions is called the
Frobenius characteristic map
.

3.1.
On the
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-module of parking functions and rooted labeled forests

The symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
acts on
\PF_{n}
via

\sigma\cdot\alpha=(\alpha_{\sigma^{-1}(1)},\alpha_{\sigma^{-1}(2)},\dots,\alpha_{\sigma^{-1}(n)})\quad\text{for all $\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}$, $\alpha\in\PF_{n}$,}

making the module
\mathbb{C}[\PF_{n}]
. The set
\F_{n}
can also be made into an
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-module
\mathbb{C}[\F_{n}]
as follows.
For
F\in\F_{n}
and an index
i\in[n-1]
, define
s_{i}(F)
to be the rooted labeled forest obtained from
F
by applying the simple transposition
(i,i+1)
to the labels and swapping the labels of
i
and
i+1
. Then define the
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-action by setting

(5)

(i,i+1)\cdot F=\begin{cases}s_{i}(F)&\mbox{if }p(i)\neq p(i+1)\\
F&\mbox{if }p(i)=p(i+1)\end{cases}

for each
i\in[n-1]
and
F\in\F_{n}
.
We can show for all
i\in[n-1]
that

(1)

(i,i+1)\cdot((i,i+1)\cdot F)=F
,

(2)

(i,i+1)\cdot((i+1,i+2)\cdot((i,i+1)\cdot F))=(i+1,i+2)\cdot((i,i+1)\cdot((i+1,i+2)\cdot F))
, and

(3)

(i,i+1)\cdot((j,j+1)\cdot F)=(j,j+1)\cdot((i,i+1)\cdot F)
if
|j-i|>1
.

We establish the above items in the following technical result.

Lemma 3.1
.

Equation

5
defines an
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-action on
\F_{n}
.

Proof.

This follows from checking that all relations of the generators
(i,i+1)
of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
are satisfied by the action; specifically:

(1)

(i,i+1)\cdot((i,i+1)\cdot F)=F
,

(2)

(i,i+1)\cdot((i+1,i+2)\cdot((i,i+1)\cdot F))=(i+1,i+2)\cdot((i,i+1)\cdot((i+1,i+2)\cdot F))
, and

(3)

(i,i+1)\cdot((j,j+1)\cdot F)=(j,j+1)\cdot((i,i+1)\cdot F)
if
|j-i|>1
.

We verify each:

(1)

Since
s_{i}(s_{i}(F))=F
, this is true regardless of the relation of
p(i)
and
p(i+1)
.

(2)

(a)

p(i)=p(i+1)=p(i+2)
: This is clear since everything is fixed.

(b)

p(i)=p(i+1)\neq p(i+2)
:

\displaystyle(i,i+1)\cdot((i+1,i+2)\cdot((i,i+1)\cdot F))

\displaystyle=(i,i+1)\cdot((i+1,i+2)\cdot F)

\displaystyle=(i,i+1)\cdot(s_{i+1}(F))

\displaystyle=s_{i}s_{i+1}(F),

and

\displaystyle(i+1,i+2)\cdot((i,i+1)\cdot((i+1,i+2)\cdot F)

\displaystyle=(i+1,i+2)\cdot((i,i+1)\cdot s_{i+1}(F))

\displaystyle=(i+1,i+2)\cdot s_{i}s_{i+1}(F))

\displaystyle=s_{i}s_{i+1}(F).

(c)

p(i)\neq p(i+1)=p(i+2)
: This is similar to the previous case.

(d)

p(i)\neq p(i+1)\neq p(i+2)
:

3.2.
Generating function formula for inversions on parking functions

The next ingredient in understanding inversions for parking functions is a general statement about words.
For this, recall that the

Next, for all nonnegative integers

It is known
[

(q;q)_{n}\ps(h_{\alpha})=\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{\alpha}_{q}.

Recall that if

has content

Corollary 3.6

We note that Cayley permutations have also been viewed as ordered set partitions, and descent generating functions for Cayley permutations have been studied in

First, we need an important characterization of unit interval parking functions.

and let
\alpha^{\uparrow}=(a^{\prime}_{1},a_{2}^{\prime},\dots,a^{\prime}_{n})
be the weakly increasing rearrangement of
\alpha
.
Then:

(1)

a^{\prime}_{i}\in\{i,i+1\}
for all
i
.

(2)

Place a separator in
\alpha^{\uparrow}
before each element
a^{\prime}_{i}
satisfying
a^{\prime}_{i}=i
, we obtain a decomposition
\alpha^{\uparrow}=\pi_{1}\mid\pi_{2}\mid\cdots\mid\pi_{m}
, where each
\pi_{j}
is of the form
(i,i,i+1,i+2,\dots,i+j)
. The words
\pi_{j}
are called the
blocks
of
\alpha
.

(3)

The sizes of the blocks determine
\alpha^{\uparrow}
uniquely.

(4)

The entries in each block appear in increasing order in
\alpha
. Equivalently, a rearrangement
\sigma
of
\alpha
is in
\UPF_{n}
if and only if the entries in
\sigma
respect the relative order of the entries in each block
\pi_{j}
.

(5)

In particular, the number of UPFs with increasing rearrangement
\alpha^{\uparrow}
is
\binom{n}{|\pi_{1}|,|\pi_{2}|,\ldots,|\pi_{m}|}
.

Next, we utilize
Theorem

4.1
to establish a bijection between unit interval parking functions and Cayley permutations.

Lemma 4.2
.

Define
\psi:\UPF_{n}\to\C_{n}
as follows: suppose
\alpha\in\UPF_{n}
has block-structure
\pi_{1}\mid\pi_{2}\mid\cdots\mid\pi_{k}
.
Let
\alpha_{i}
denote the
i
th entry of
\alpha
, and
\psi(\alpha)_{i}
denote the
i
th entry of
\psi(\alpha)
.
Set

\psi(\alpha)_{i}=j\quad\text{ if $\alpha_{i}$ appears in $\pi_{j}$}

for each
i\in n
. Then
\psi
is a bijection such that for all
\alpha\in\UPF_{n}
,
\Inv(\psi(\alpha))=\Inv(\alpha)
.

Proof.

First, the image of
\psi
is indeed a Cayley permutation since the numbers
1,2,\dots,k
appear at least once each in
\psi(\alpha)
if
\alpha
has
k
blocks.

Suppose
\alpha\in\UPF_{n}
and
\alpha_{i}>\alpha_{j}
for some
i<j
. By
Theorem

4.1
part (4),
\alpha_{i}
is in a strictly larger (indexed) block than
\alpha_{j}
. Hence,
\psi(\alpha)_{i}>\psi(\alpha)_{j}
. On the other hand, if
\alpha_{i}\leq\alpha_{j}
for some
i<j
, then
\alpha_{i}
and
\alpha_{j}
are either in the same block, or
\alpha_{i}
is in a smaller (indexed) block than
\alpha_{j}
. Hence,
\psi(\alpha)_{i}\leq\psi(\alpha)_{j}
. Thus,
\Inv(\psi(\alpha))=\Inv(\alpha)
.

Suppose
\psi(\alpha)=\psi(\beta)
. Then, we can see that
\alpha
and
\beta
must have the same block structure since the contents of
\psi(\alpha)
and
\psi(\beta)
are the same. By definition,
\psi(\alpha)_{i}=\psi(\beta)_{i}=j
if an element of block
\pi_{j}
appears in position
i
of
\alpha
and
\beta
. By
Theorem

4.1
part (4), there is only one way to arrange the elements of each block
\pi_{j}
such that an element of
\pi_{j}
appears in the positions
i
such that
\psi(\alpha)_{i}=j
. Hence,
\alpha=\beta
.

Given a Cayley permutation
w\in\C_{n}
, let
c=\con(w)=(c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{k})
be the content of
w
.
For each
j\in[k]
, if
i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{c_{j}}
are the positions of the
j
’s in
w
, then replace the
j^{\prime}s
(from left to right) by
a,a,a+1,a+2,\dots,a+c_{j}-1
, where
a=c_{1}+c_{2}+\cdots+c_{j-1}+1
to construct a sequence
\alpha
(where we set
c_{0}=0
).
The sequence
\alpha
is proven to be a unit interval parking function in the proof of
[
5
, Theorem 2.5]
.
Moreover,
\psi(\alpha)=w
since the block structure of
\alpha
is
c
.
∎

4.2.
Inversion generating function for unit interval parking functions

For
\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, let
\asc(\pi)
denote the number of
ascents
of
\pi=\pi_{1}\pi_{2}\cdots\pi_{n}
i.e., the number of positions
i\in[n-1]
such that
\pi_{i}<\pi_{i+1}
.
Define for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the polynomials

(19)

A_{n}^{\inv,\asc}(q,t)=\sum_{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}q^{\inv(\pi)}t^{\asc(\pi)}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\UPF_{n}(q)=\sum_{\alpha\in\UPF_{n}}q^{\inv(\alpha)}.

We provide two methods for obtaining a
q
-exponential generating function for
\UPF_{n}(q)
: (1) by showing that
\UPF_{n}(q)
is a specialization of

and applying a theorem of Stanley
[
49
]
; and (2) a direct computation. We start with the former.

Proposition 4.3
.

For
n\geq 1
,
A_{n}^{\inv,\asc}(q,2)=\UPF_{n}(q)
.

Proof.

By
Lemma

4.2
, we have

\UPF_{n}(q)=\sum_{w\in\C_{n}}q^{\inv(w)}.

Let
\Asc(\sigma)=\{i\in[n-1]:\sigma_{i}<\sigma_{i+1}\}
denote the set of ascents of a permutation
\sigma
. Define a set

\mathcal{P}_{n}=\{(\sigma,S)\mid\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n},\ S\subseteq\Asc(\sigma^{-1})\}.

Define a map
\eta:\mathcal{P}_{n}\to\C_{n}
as follows: suppose
[n]\setminus S=\{s_{1}<\cdots<s_{r}\}
. Define a partition
B=(B_{1},\dots,B_{r})
of

by

B_{k}=\{s_{k-1}+1,s_{k-1}+2,\dots,s_{k}\}

for each
k\in[r]
, where
s_{0}=0
. For example, if
(\sigma,S)=(236841759,\{2,3,6,8\})
, then
\sigma^{-1}=612583749
and
\Asc(\sigma^{-1})=\{2,3,4,6,8\}
. Note
S\subseteq\Asc(\sigma^{-1})
. So
[9]\setminus S=\{s_{1}=1,s_{2}=4,s_{3}=5,s_{4}=7,s_{5}=9\}
, and

B=(\{1\},\{2,3,4\},\{5\},\{6,7\},\{8,9\}).

Then, set
w_{i}=k
if
\sigma_{i}\in B_{k}
and set
\eta(\sigma,S)=w=w_{1}w_{2}\cdots w_{n}
. Continuing the example,

\eta(236841759,\{2,3,6,8\})=224521435.

Since each block of the partition is nonempty,
w\in\C_{n}
. Suppose
(i,j)\in\Inv(\sigma)
. Then
\sigma_{i}
must be in a larger indexed block than the one containing
\sigma_{j}
by construction. Hence,
w_{i}>w_{j}
, so
(i,j)\in\Inv(w)
.

On the other hand, suppose we are given
w\in\C_{n}
with
r
distinct values and set
B_{k}=\{i\in[n]\mid w_{i}=k\}
for each
k\in[r]
.

Suppose the content of
w
is
c=(c_{1},\dots,c_{r})
. Then for each
k\in[r]
, set
s_{k}=c_{1}+\cdots+c_{k}
and then define

B_{k}=\{s_{k-1}+1,s_{k-1}+2,\dots,s_{k}\},

where
s_{0}=0
. Then we set
\sigma
to be obtained by replacing the
j
-th leftmost
k
in
w
by
s_{k-1}+j
for each
k\in[r]
and
j\in[c_{k}]
.
For example, let
w=224521435
. The content of
w
is
c=(1,3,1,2,2)
, so
s_{1}=1,s_{2}=4,s_{3}=5,s_{4}=7,s_{5}=9
and
(B_{1},\dots,B_{5})=(\{1\},\{2,3,4\},\{5\},\{6,7\},\{8,9\}).
Moreover,
\sigma=236841759
. Now, set

S\coloneqq\bigcup_{k=1}^{r}B_{k}\setminus\{\max(B_{k})\}.

Suppose
m\in S
, say
m\in B_{k}\setminus\max(B_{k})

because
B_{k}
contains only consecutive numbers. Hence,
m=s_{k-1}+j
and
m+1=s_{k-1}+j+1
for some

\sigma

\sigma_{i}=m

is the

in

(j+1)

From here, one can show that

|\C_{n}|=\Fub_{n}=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}2^{\asc(\sigma)},

(see

\displaystyle\UPF_{n}(q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{w\in\C_{n}}q^{\inv(w)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{(\sigma,S)\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}q^{\inv(\sigma)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}q^{\inv(\sigma)}\cdot|\{S\subseteq[n]\mid S\subseteq\Asc(\sigma^{-1})\}|

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}q^{\inv(\sigma)}2^{\asc(\sigma^{-1})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}q^{\inv(\sigma)}2^{\asc(\sigma)}

We provide two proofs of the following result.

Theorem 4.4
.

We have

\sum_{n\geq 0}\UPF_{n}(q)\frac{z^{n}}{[n]_{q}!}=\frac{1}{2-\exp_{q}(z)}\ .

Proof 1: Specialization.

Stanley
[
49
, Corollary 3.6]
proved that
A_{n}^{\inv,\asc}(q,t)
satisfies

(20)

1+\sum_{n\geq 1}tA_{n}^{\inv,\asc}(q,t)\frac{z^{n}}{[n]_{q}!}=\frac{1-t}{1-t\Exp_{q}(z(1-t))}\ .

Set
t=2
in (
20
)
to get

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 1}2\UPF_{n}(q)\frac{z^{n}}{[n]_{q}!}=\sum_{n\geq 1}2A_{n}^{\inv,\asc}(q,2)\frac{z^{n}}{[n]_{q}!}=\frac{-1}{1-2\Exp_{q}(-z)}+1=\frac{\exp_{q}(z)}{2-\exp_{q}(z)}+1

where the first equality comes from
Proposition

4.3
, while the third follows from the identity
\exp_{q}(-z)=1/\Exp_{q}(z)
. From there, the proof follows by algebraic manipulations.
∎

Proof 2: Elementary.

This result can be found directly without relying on
Equation

20
. Indeed,
Lemma

4.2
implies that

(21)

\UPF_{n}(q)=\sum_{w\in\C_{n}}q^{\inv(w)}=\sum_{c\in\Comp(n)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\in[n]^{n}\\
\con(w)=c\end{subarray}}q^{\inv(w)}=\sum_{c\in\Comp(n)}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{c_1,c_2,\dots,c_{\ell(c)}}_{q},

where the final equality comes from MacMahon
[
34
]
.
Starting with the right-hand side of
Theorem

4.4
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2-\exp_{q}(z)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{1-\frac{\exp_{q}(z)}{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{1}{2^{k}}\exp_{q}(z)^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{1}{2^{k+1}}\left(\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{z^{n}}{[n]_{q}!}\right)^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{1}{2^{k+1}}\sum_{n\geq 0}z^{n}\sum_{c\in\WComp(n,k)}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{[c_{i}]_{q}!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{1}{2^{k+1}}\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{z^{n}}{[n]_{q}!}\sum_{c\in\WComp(n,k)}\frac{[n]_{q}!}{[c_{1}]_{q}!\cdots[c_{k}]_{q}!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{1}{2^{k+1}}\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{z^{n}}{[n]_{q}!}\sum_{c\in\WComp(n,k)}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{c_1,c_2,\dots,c_{k}}_{q}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{z^{n}}{[n]_{q}!}\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{1}{2^{k+1}}\sum_{c\in\WComp(n,k)}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{c_1,c_2,\dots,c_{k}}_{q}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{z^{n}}{[n]_{q}!}\sum_{c\in\WComp(n)}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{c_1,c_2,\dots,c_{\ell(c)}}_{q}\cdot\frac{1}{2^{\ell(c)+1}},

where we recall that
\ell(c)
denotes the number of parts of a weak composition
c
.
Since every weak composition can be made by adding 0’s to a (strong) composition, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{c\in\WComp(n)}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{c_1,c_2,\dots,c_{\ell(c)}}_{q}\cdot\frac{1}{2^{\ell(c)}}=\sum_{c\in\Comp(n)}\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{c_1,c_2,\dots,c_{\ell(c)}}_{q}\sum_{j\geq 0}\binom{\ell(c)+j}{j}\frac{1}{2^{\ell(c)+j}}.

Expanding
2^{-(m+1)}(1-x)^{-(m+1)}
with Newton’s generalized binomial theorem and then substituting
x=1/2
, we have that for all positive integers
m
,

\sum_{j\geq 0}\binom{m+j}{j}\frac{1}{2^{m+j+1}}=1.

Hence, the claim follows.
∎

Remark 4.5
.

The definition of
\UPF_{n}(q)
along with the closed form in
Theorem

4.4
make it a compelling
q
-analog of
\Fub_{n}
. However, there is a different
q
-analog of
\Fub_{n}
, which has received much more attention in the literature. Originally studied under the name
\operatorname{ROS}
by Steíngrimsson
[
55
]
, an “inversion,” as defined by Remmel and Wilson
[
40
]
, of a ordered set partition
B=(B_{1},\dots,B_{k})
is pair
(i,B_{j})
such that
i
appears in an earlier block than
B_{j}
and
i
is greater than something in
B_{j}
. This kind of inversion has been related to the Delta Conjecture, an important conjecture in the theory of symmetric functions
[
26
]
. This “inversion” statistic has been shown to be equidistributed with a number of other statistics, and the resulting
q
-analog has many wonderful properties
[
26
,
41
,
27
]
. The Delta Conjecture context is also closely related to parking functions, leading us to wonder whether it is coincidence that ordered set partitions can be viewed as parking functions themselves.

4.3.
Unit interval parking function symmetric function

To generalize to a result on symmetric functions, we require an
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-action on
\UPF_{n}
. Suppose
\alpha\in\UPF_{n}
has block structure
\pi_{1}\mid\pi_{2}\mid\cdots\mid\pi_{k}
, as described in
Theorem

4.1
.
For
i\in[n-1]
, define

(i,i+1)\cdot\alpha=\begin{cases}\alpha&\text{if $\alpha_{i},\alpha_{i+1}$ are in the same block}\\
(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\dots,\alpha_{i-1},\alpha_{i+1},\alpha_{i},\alpha_{i+2},\dots,\alpha_{n})&\text{if $\alpha_{i},\alpha_{i+1}$ are in different blocks.}\end{cases}

One can check that this defines an
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-action on
\UPF_{n}
, as noted in
[
9
, pg. 11]
.
The
content
of
\alpha
is the composition

\con(\alpha)=(|\pi_{1}|,|\pi_{2}|,\dots,|\pi_{k}|).

In
[
5
, Theorem 2.9]
, the authors show that two unit interval parking functions have the same content if and only if they are rearrangements of each other.
Moreover, they show that for each composition
c\in\Comp(n)
there is a unit interval parking function
\alpha
with
\con(\alpha)=c
.
Thus,

\UPF_{n}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{c\in\Comp(n)}h_{c}.

Since
\UPF_{n}
and
\C_{n}
have the same Frobenius image, they are isomorphic as
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-modules.
In particular, we show that the map
\psi
, defined in
Lemma

4.2
,
is an explicit isomorphism.

Proposition 4.6
.

Let
\psi:\UPF_{n}\to\C_{n}
be as in
Lemma

4.2
.
Then, for all
\alpha\in\UPF_{n}
,
\con(\psi(\alpha))=\con(\alpha)
.
Moreover,
\psi
extends to an
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-module isomorphism.

Proof.

For
\alpha\in\UPF_{n}
with block structure
\pi_{1}\mid\pi_{2}\mid\cdots\mid\pi_{k}
, The map
\psi
replaces each element of block
\pi_{j}
with a
j
for each
j\in[k]
. Hence, entry
j
of
\con(\alpha)=(|\pi_{1}|,|\pi_{2}|,\dots,|\pi_{k}|)
is exactly the number of
j
’s in
\psi(\alpha)
for each
j\in[k]
, so
\con(\alpha)=\con(\psi(\alpha))
.

Suppose
i\in[n-1]
. If
\alpha_{i}
and
\alpha_{i+1}
are in the same block, then
\psi(\alpha)_{i}=\psi(\alpha)_{i+1}
. Hence,
(i,i+1)\cdot\psi(\alpha)=\psi(\alpha)=\psi((i,i+1)\cdot\alpha)
. On the other hand, if
\alpha_{i}
and
\alpha_{i+1}
are in different blocks, then
\psi(\alpha)_{i}\neq\psi(\alpha)_{i+1}
, so

(i,i+1)\cdot\psi(\alpha)=(\psi(\alpha)_{1},\psi(\alpha)_{2},\dots,\psi(\alpha)_{i-1},\psi(\alpha)_{i+1},\psi(\alpha)_{i},\psi(\alpha)_{i+2},\dots,\psi(\alpha)_{n})=\psi((i,i+1)\cdot\alpha).

Hence,
\psi
is an isomorphism of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-actions.
∎

Theorem 4.7
.

We have

\sum_{n\geq 0}\UPF_{n}(\mathbf{x})z^{n}=\frac{1}{2-H(z)}
, where
H(z)
is defined in
Equation

8
.

Proof.

The result follows by writing

\frac{1}{2-H(z)}=\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{1}{2^{k+1}}\left(\sum_{n\geq 0}h_{n}z^{n}\right)^{k}=\sum_{n\geq 0}z^{n}\sum_{c\in\Comp(n)}h_{c}\sum_{j\geq 0}\binom{\ell(c)+j}{j}\frac{1}{2^{\ell(c)+j+1}}

and recalling that
\sum_{j\geq 0}\binom{\ell(c)+j}{j}\frac{1}{2^{\ell(c)+j+1}}=1
.
∎

While setting
t=2
makes sense for polynomials, it is not clear what the representation theoretic meaning is of this evaluation. In particular, we do not know how to derive
Theorem

4.7
from a known
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-representation on
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
.

Just like
\mathbb{C}[\PF_{n}]
,
\mathbb{C}[\UPF_{n}]
is a graded
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-module, with the grading coming from the area function, the function

\UPF_{n}(\mathbf{x},t)\coloneqq\sum_{\alpha\in\UPF_{n}^{\uparrow}}t^{\area(\alpha)}h_{\con(\alpha)}

is the graded Frobenius image of
\mathbb{C}[\UPF_{n}]
. This means that

\UPF_{n}(\mathbf{x},t)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\ch\left(\mathbb{C}\{\alpha\in\UPF_{n}\mid\area(\alpha)=k\}\right)t^{k}.

We can likewise get a nice expression for the generating function of
\UPF_{n}(\mathbf{x},t)
.

Theorem 4.8
.

The generating function of
\UPF_{n}(\mathbf{x},t)
is

\sum_{n\geq 0}\UPF_{n}(\mathbf{x},t)z^{n}=\frac{t}{[2]_{t}-H(tz)}

Proof.

We first rewrite the right-hand side as

\frac{t}{[2]_{t}-H(tz)}=\frac{t}{(t+1)-H(tz)}=\frac{1}{1-\frac{H(tz)-1}{t}}

and note that

H(tz)-1=\sum_{n\geq 1}h_{n}z^{n}t^{n}.

Then, we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-\frac{H(tz)-1}{t}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}\frac{1}{t^{k}}\left(\sum_{n\geq 1}h_{n}z^{n}t^{n}\right)^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 0}z^{n}\sum_{c\in\Comp(n)}t^{n-\ell(c)}h_{c}.

Now we just have to show that

\UPF_{n}(\mathbf{x},t)=\sum_{c\in\Comp(n)}t^{n-\ell(c)}h_{c}.

Indeed, observe that if
\alpha\in\UPF_{n}^{\uparrow}
and
\con(\alpha)=(c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{n-k})
, then for each
i\in[n-k]
, cars
2+\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}c_{j},3+\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}c_{j},\dots,c_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}c_{j}
each park exactly 1 spot away from their preference, and thus contribute
c_{i}-1
in total to the area. Thus,
\area(\alpha)=c_{1}+c_{2}+\dots+c_{n-k}-(n-k)=k
, and so we have the desired expression.
∎

5.
Expectations of
k
-transitive statistics

In this section, we continue our discussion on
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-invariant sets of words by connecting the total number of inversions on the set to the total number of descents in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-invariant set of words. The parking function motivation comes from Schumacher’s
[
46
, Theorem 10]
which establishes

(22)

\sum_{\alpha\in\PF_{n}}\des(\alpha)=\binom{n}{2}(n+1)^{n-2}.

Using probabilistic techniques,
we obtain an analogous formula for inversions on parking functions, which we state and prove in
Corollary

5.4
. Moreover, we extend this to a larger class of statistics on
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-invariant set of words, namely those defined via
k
-transitive functions
.

For a random variable
f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
on a finite set
\Omega
, define the
expectation
of
f
with respect to the uniform measure on
\Omega
to be

(23)

\E_{\Omega}[f]=\frac{1}{|\Omega|}\sum_{x\in\Omega}f(x)=\sum_{y\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}_{\Omega}(f=y)\cdot y,

where we denote the discrete uniform probability distribution by

\mathbb{P}_{\Omega}(f=y)=\frac{|\{x\in\Omega\mid f(x)=y\}|}{|\Omega|}.

For further definitions and background in probability, see
[
12
,
15
,
24
]
. When we know the size of a set and our random variables are chosen appropriately, computing the expectation of a statistic (
23
) is equivalent to computing the sum of the statistic over the elements of the set.
We use the language of expected value to clarify combinatorial arguments which would otherwise be much more cumbersome. Linearity of expectation and symmetry often lie at the heart of these arguments. In the same way that descents are inversions that happen next to each other, we can consider ‘adjacent’ and ‘non-adjacent’ versions of other statistics. In
Theorem

5.2
, we prove a general result relating expectations (and therefore counts) of such pairs of statistics. Our first application is to obtain the total number of inversions of parking functions from Schumacher’s count of the total number of descents. In
Section

5.1
, we explore numerous other applications of this.

For positive integers
k\leq n
, define the set

I_{n,k}=\{(i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{k})\mid 1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{k}\leq n\}.

A function
\chi:\mathbb{N}^{n}\times I_{n,k}\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be
k
-transitive
provided there is a function
\theta:\mathbb{N}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

(24)

\chi(w,(i_{1},i_{2}\dots,i_{k}))=\theta(w_{i_{1}},w_{i_{2}},\dots,w_{i_{k}})

for each
w\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
and
(i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{k})\in I_{n,k}
. In other words,
\chi
depends only on the ordered list of values
w_{i_{1}},w_{i_{2}},\dots,w_{i_{k}}
and not on the positions. The name comes from the fact that the symmetric group
\mathfrak{S}_{n}

acts
k
-transitively
on
[n]
for any
k\in[n]
, meaning if
\textbf{p}=(i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{k})
and
\textbf{q}=(j_{1},j_{2},\dots,j_{k})
are a vector of distinct values of
[n]
, then there is a permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
such that
\sigma(i_{s})=j_{s}
for all
s\in[k]
. Moreover, if
\chi
is
k
-transitive, then

(25)

\chi(w,\textbf{p})=\chi(\sigma\cdot w,\textbf{q})

for any
w\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
.

Let
W
be a finite subset of
\mathbb{N}^{n}
and let
\chi
be a
k
-transitive function. Define random variables on
W
by setting

(26)

f_{\chi}(w)=\sum_{\textbf{p}\in I_{n,k}}\chi(w,\textbf{p})\quad\text{and}\quad g_{\chi}(w)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-k}\chi(w,(1+i,2+i,\dots,k+i))

for all
w\in W
.
We start by giving an example of a transitive function that both serves as a motivation as well as being of interest in what follows.

Example 5.1
.

Let
W
be a finite subset of
\mathbb{N}^{n}
. For
w\in W
and integers
1\leq i<j\leq n
, define

\chi^{\inv}(w,(i,j))=\begin{cases}1&w_{i}>w_{j}\\
0&w_{i}\leq w_{j}.\end{cases}

We can see that
\chi^{\inv}
is a
2
-transitive function with
\theta(a,b)=1
if
a>b
, and
0
if
a\leq b
.
In the language of probability,
\chi^{\inv}
is a classical example of an indicator random variable.
Moreover,
f_{\chi^{\inv}}
is the classical inversion statistic
\inv
, while
g_{\chi^{\inv}}
is the classical descent statistic
\des
.

For
W=\PF_{n}
, we can rephrase (
22
) as a statement about the expectation
\E_{\PF_{n}}\left[\des\right]=\E_{\PF_{n}}\left[g_{\chi^{\inv}}\right]
of the random variable
g_{\chi^{\inv}}
on
\PF_{n}
.
Analogously, we want to compute the expectation
\E_{\PF_{n}}\left[\inv\right]
of
\inv=f_{\chi^{\inv}}
.
Note that the probabilistic interpretation of these computations is to determine the expected number of decents (or inversions) in a uniformly random parking function of length
n
.

For a
k
-transitive function
\chi
, define a restriction

\chi_{1}(w)\coloneqq\chi(w,(1,2,\dots,k))

for
w\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
. For instance, if
\chi=\chi^{\inv}
, then
\chi^{\inv}_{1}(w)=1
if
w
has a descent in position 1 and is 0 otherwise.

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
k\leq n
be positive integers.
Let
\chi:\mathbb{N}^{n}\times I_{n,k}\to\mathbb{R}
be a
k
-transitive function and let
W\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{n}
be an
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-invariant set of words. With
f_{\chi}
and
g_{\chi}
as in
Equation

26
, we have

(1)

\E_{W}[f_{\chi}]=\binom{n}{k}\E_{W}[\chi_{1}]
,

(2)

\E_{W}[g_{\chi}]=(n-k+1)\E_{W}[\chi_{1}]
, and

(3)

\E_{W}[f_{\chi}]=\frac{1}{k}\binom{n}{k-1}\E_{W}[g_{\chi}]
.

Proof.

For a fixed element
\textbf{p}=(i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{k})\in I_{n,k}
, fix
\sigma
to be a permutation such that
\sigma(i_{s})=s
for each
s\in[k]
. Since
W
is
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-invariant, for all
w\in W
we have

\sigma\cdot w=(w_{\sigma^{-1}(1)},w_{\sigma^{-1}(2)},\dots,w_{\sigma^{-1}(n)})\in W.

Since
\chi
is
k
-transitive, we can use
Equation

25
with
\textbf{q}=(1,2,\dots,k)
to get that for any
y\in\mathbb{R}

\mathbb{P}_{W}(\chi(w,\textbf{p})=y)=\mathbb{P}_{W}(\chi_{1}(\sigma\cdot w)=y)=\mathbb{P}_{W}(\chi_{1}(u)=y),

where
\sigma
is the permutation such that
\sigma(i_{s})=s
for all
s\in[k]
.
Therefore, by linearity of expectation, we have

(27)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{W}\left[f_{\chi}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\textbf{p}\in I_{n,k}}\mathbb{E}_{W}\left[\chi(w,\textbf{p})\right]=\sum_{\textbf{p}\in I_{n,k}}\E_{W}[\chi_{1}]=\binom{n}{k}\E_{W}[\chi_{1}],

since
|I_{n,k}|=\binom{n}{k}
, proving (a).

For part (b), we proved in part (a) that
\mathbb{P}_{W}\left(\chi(w,(i,i+1,\dots,i+k-1)=y\right)=\mathbb{P}_{W}\left(\chi_{1}(w)=y\right)
for all
i\in[n-k]
.
Hence,

\E_{W}\left[g_{\chi}\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{n-k+1}\E_{W}\left[\chi(w,(i,i+1,\dots,i+k-1))\right]=(n-k+1)\E_{W}[\chi_{1}],

as desired.

Finally, part (c) follows from combining (a) and (b).
∎

By selecting
\chi
to be the 2-transitive function
\chi^{\inv}
and by the definition of expectation applied to indicator random variables, we immediately obtain the following.

Corollary 5.3
.

Let
W\subset\mathbb{N}^{n}
be an
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-invariant set of words of positive integers.
Then,

(a)

\E_{W}\left[\inv\right]=\binom{n}{2}\mathbb{P}_{W}\left(\chi^{\inv}_{1}=1\right)
,

(b)

\E_{W}\left[\des\right]=(n-1)\mathbb{P}_{W}\left(\chi^{\inv}_{1}=1\right)
, and

(c)

\E_{W}\left[\inv\right]=\frac{n}{2}\E_{W}\left[\des\right]
.

Note that part (c) of
Corollary

5.3
is equivalent to

(28)

\sum_{w\in W}\inv(w)=\frac{n}{2}\sum_{w\in W}\des(w).

From this, we can therefore derive the desired analogue of Schumacher’s result (
22
) for inversions since
\PF_{n}
is
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-invariant.

Corollary 5.4
.

For
n\geq 1
, the total number of inversions across all parking functions is

\sum_{\alpha\in\PF_{n}}\inv(\alpha)=\frac{n(n+1)^{n-2}}{2}\binom{n}{2}.

Let
\Fub_{n}=|\C_{n}|
be the
n
-th Fubini number defined by

\Fub_{n}=\sum_{{k=0}}^{n}\sum_{{j=0}}^{k}(-1)^{{k-j}}{\binom{k}{j}}j^{n}.

The sequence of Fubini numbers is OEIS sequence
A000670
. We obtain similar results for unit interval parking functions.

Corollary 5.5
.

Let
n\geq 1
.

(1)

The total number of descents across all unit interval parking functions, (or equivalently all Cayley permutations) is

\sum_{\alpha\in\UPF_{n}}\des(\alpha)=\frac{n-1}{2}(\Fub_{n}-\Fub_{n-1}).

(2)

The total number of inversions across all unit interval parking functions, (or equivalently all Cayley permutations) is

\sum_{\alpha\in\UPF_{n}}\inv(\alpha)=\frac{n(n-1)}{4}(\Fub_{n}-\Fub_{n-1}).

Proof.

By
Lemma

4.2
, we can work with Cayley permutations.

First observe that

\mathbb{P}_{\C_{n}}(\chi_{1}^{\inv}=1)=\mathbb{P}_{\C_{n}}(\alpha_{1}>\alpha_{2})=\mathbb{P}_{\C_{n}}(\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2})=\frac{1}{2}(1-\mathbb{P}_{\C_{n}}(\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2})).

The map

\{\alpha\in\C_{n}\mid\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}\}\rightarrow\C_{n-1},

defined by
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3},\dots,\alpha_{n})\mapsto(\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3},\dots,\alpha_{n})
is a bijection, so we have

(29)

\mathbb{P}_{\C_{n}}(\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2})=\frac{|\C_{n-1}|}{|\C_{n}|}=\frac{\Fub_{n-1}}{\Fub_{n}},

and thus

(30)

\E_{\C_{n}}(\chi_{1}^{\inv})=\mathbb{P}_{\C_{n}}(\chi_{1}^{\inv}=1)=\frac{\Fub_{n}-\Fub_{n-1}}{2\Fub_{n}}.

Applying
Corollary

5.3
part (b) to (
30
) we get

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha\in\UPF_{n}}\des(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\Fub_{n}\E_{\C_{n}}[\des]

\displaystyle=\Fub_{n}(n-1)\mathbb{P}_{\C_{n}}(\chi_{1}^{\inv}=1)

(31)

\displaystyle=\frac{n-1}{2}(\Fub_{n}-\Fub_{n-1}).

Applying
Corollary

5.3
part (c) to (
31
) yields

\sum_{\alpha\in\UPF_{n}}\inv(\alpha)=\frac{n(n-1)}{4}(\Fub_{n}-\Fub_{n-1}).\qed

Remark 5.6
.

Theorem

5.2
is a statement about any finite
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-invariant set of words
W\subset\mathbb{N}^{n}
, so perhaps it is of interest to view the result as a statement about the
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-module
\mathbb{C}[W]
or about its Frobenius image.
However, it is unclear at this time what the representation theoretic significance is.

The only property of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
used in
Theorem

5.2
is that it acts
k
-transitively on
[n]
. Thus, we have the following generalization.

Proposition 5.7
.

Let
k\leq n
be positive integers. Let
G
be a group that acts
k
-transitively on
[n]
and let
W\subset\mathbb{N}^{n}
be a
G
-invariant set of words of positive integers. For any
k
-transitive function
\chi:\mathbb{N}^{n}\times I_{n,k}\to\mathbb{R}
, we have

(1)

\E_{W}[f_{\chi}]=\binom{n}{k}\E_{W}(\chi_{1})
,

(2)

\E_{W}[g_{\chi}]=(n-k+1)\E_{W}(\chi_{1})
, and

(3)

\E_{W}[f_{\chi}]=\frac{1}{k}\binom{n}{k-1}\E_{W}(g_{\chi})
.

Example 5.8
.

Let
\mathfrak{S}_{n}^{+}
denote the alternating subgroup of
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. Then using the work of Désarménien and Foata
[
10
]
, Fulman et al. in
[
21
, Eqn. (7)]
) note that if
A_{n}(q)=\sum_{w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}q^{\des(w)}
, then

\sum_{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{2n}^{+}}q^{\des(\pi)}=2A_{2n}(q)+(1-q)^{n}A_{n}(q)\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\sum_{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{2n+1}^{+}}q^{\des(\pi)}=2A_{2n+1}(q)+(1-q)^{n}A_{n+1}(q).

Considering both equations separately, we take a derivative with respect to
q
of both sides and set
q=1
, to arrive at

(32)

\sum_{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{2n}^{+}}\des(\pi)=2\sum_{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{2n}}\des(\pi)\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\sum_{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{2n+1}^{+}}\des(\pi)=2\sum_{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{2n+1}}\des(\pi).

We give more details on the history and counts of descents and inversions in permutations at the start of the next section, and use those counts along with the equations in (
32
) to determine that

(33)

\sum_{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}^{+}_{n}}\des(\pi)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\des(\pi)=\frac{n!(n-1)}{4}.

Since the alternating group
\mathfrak{S}_{n}^{+}
acts 2-transitively on
[n]
for

, we can conclude by
Proposition

5.7
that

(34)

\sum_{\pi\in\mathfrak{S}^{+}_{n}}\inv(\pi)=\frac{n!n(n-1)}{8}.

As a consequence, one can also see that total number of descents or inversions is the same in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}\setminus\mathfrak{S}_{n}^{+}
as there is in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}^{+}
. The equality of the total number of descents can be shown combinatorially via the involution of Wachs
[
60
]
.

Applications

The method of enumerating statistics by way of computing the expectation of a carefully chosen
k

Theorem

5.2
straightforwardly in the following scenarios to obtain results, both old and new.

5.1.1.
Inversions and descents in permutations

Counting the total number of inversions
[

,
A001809
]
is a very old problem from 1838 posed by Stern
[

]
and solved by Terquem
[
59
]
.
The sequence enumerating the total number of descents is known as the
Lah numbers

[
29
,
A001286
]
. Note we used these results in

, now we prove them independently by using
Corollary

5.3

Corollary 5.9
.

The number of descents and inversions in
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
is given by

\sum_{w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\des(w)=\frac{n!(n-1)}{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\inv(w)=\frac{n}{2}\frac{n!(n-1)}{2}=\frac{n!n(n-1)}{4},

respectively.

Proof.

Recall that
\chi^{\inv}:\mathbb{N}^{n}\times I_{n,2}\to\{0,1\}
is defined by

\chi^{\inv}(w,(i,j))=\begin{cases}1&w_{i}>w_{j}\\
0&w_{i}\leq w_{j}\,,\\
\end{cases}

and that this has
f_{\chi^{\inv}}=\inv
and
g_{\chi^{\inv}}=\des
.
For
w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, the probability that
\chi^{\inv}(w,(1,2))=1
is exactly
\frac{1}{2}
. Hence, by
Corollary

5.3
part (b), we have that

\E_{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}[\des]=(n-1)\frac{1}{2}\,.

Thus, we have

\sum_{w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\des(w)=\frac{n!(n-1)}{2}.

In an analogous fashion,
applying
Corollary

5.3
part (c) yields

\sum_{w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\inv(w)=\frac{n}{2}\frac{n!(n-1)}{2}=\frac{n!n(n-1)}{4}.

∎

5.1.2.
Ties in words

A
tie
in a word
w\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
is a position
i
such that
w_{i}=w_{i+1}
. The number of ties of
w
is denoted
\tie(w)
. We have the following application of the aforementioned technique.

Corollary 5.10
.

Let
w
be a word with content
(c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{\ell})
. Then

\sum_{u\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}\cdot w}\tie(u)=\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\binom{n}{c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{i-1},c_{i}-2,2,c_{i+1},\dots,c_{\ell}}\,.

Proof.

Define

\chi^{\tie}(w,(i,j))=\begin{cases}1&w_{i}=w_{j}\\
0&w_{i}\neq w_{j}.\end{cases}

Then,
\tie(w)=g_{\chi^{\tie}}(w)
. If
\con(w)=(c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{\ell})
, then

f_{\chi^{\tie}}(w)=\binom{c_{1}}{2}+\binom{c_{2}}{2}+\cdots+\binom{c_{\ell}}{2}

as this function simply selects pairs of numbers which are the same. If
W=\mathfrak{S}_{n}\cdot w
, then
|W|=\binom{n}{c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{\ell}}
and
f_{\chi^{\tie}}(u)=f_{\chi^{\tie}}(w)
for all
u\in W
, so

\sum_{u\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}\cdot w}f_{\chi^{\tie}}(u)=\binom{n}{c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{\ell}}\left(\binom{c_{1}}{2}+\cdots+\binom{c_{\ell}}{2}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\binom{n}{c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{i-1},c_{i}-2,2,c_{i+1},\dots,c_{\ell}}.

Thus, the total number of ties is

\sum_{u\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}\cdot w}\tie(u)=\frac{2}{n}\sum_{u\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}\cdot w}f_{\chi^{\tie}}(u)=\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\binom{n}{c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{i-1},c_{i}-2,2,c_{i+1},\dots,c_{\ell}}.\qed

For some particular choices of
W
,
Corollary

5.10
has interesting interpretations. In particular, we are able to derive nicer formulas for the total number of ties across all Cayley permutations (
Proposition

5.11
) and across all parking functions (
Corollary

5.14
), leading us to wonder if there exist bijective proofs that the formulas we can derive from
Corollary

5.10
are equal to these simpler expressions.

Proposition 5.11
.

The total number of ties across all Cayley permutations of length
n
is

\sum_{w\in\C_{n}}\tie(w)=(n-1)\Fub_{n-1}.

Proof.

This follows from
Equation

29
, which says that

\E_{\C_{n}}[\chi_{1}^{\tie}]=\frac{|\C_{n-1}|}{|\C_{n}|}=\frac{\Fub_{n-1}}{\Fub_{n}},

where
\Fub_{n}
denotes the
n
-th Fubini number.
∎

Combining
Proposition

5.11
with
Corollary

5.10
, yields the identity

\frac{2}{n}\sum_{c\in\Comp(n)}\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\binom{n}{c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{i-1},c_{i}-2,2,c_{i+1},\dots,c_{\ell}}=(n-1)\Fub_{n-1}\,.

Finding a bijective proof of this fact remains an open problem; see
Section

6
for a formal statement.

Remark 5.12
.

If
\Fub(z)=\sum_{n\geq 0}\Fub_{n}z^{n}/n!
, then recall that
\Fub(z)=\frac{1}{2-e^{z}}
and thus

\Fub(z)^{\prime}=\sum_{n\geq 1}\Fub_{n}\frac{z^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}=\sum_{n\geq 2}(n-1)\Fub_{n-1}\frac{z^{n-2}}{(n-1)!}=\sum_{n\geq 1}\left(\sum_{\alpha\in\C_{n}}\tie(\alpha)\right)\frac{z^{n-2}}{(n-1)!}

Hence,

\sum_{n\geq 0}\left(\sum_{w\in\C_{n+1}}\tie(w)\right)\frac{z^{n}}{n!}=\frac{ze^{z}}{(2-e^{z})^{2}}.

Lemma 5.13
.

The number of parking functions of length
n
having a tie in position 1 is given by

(n+1)^{n-2}\,.

Proof.

We begin by recalling the map of Pollak
[
42
]

(w_{1},w_{2},\dots,w_{n})\in[n+1]^{n}
, we can imagine cars parking on a circular street with
n+1
spots with preference list
w
. Since there are
n
cars and
n+1
spots, there is empty spot. If
i
is the empty spot, set

\alpha=(w_{1}-i,w_{2}-i,\dots,w_{n}-i),

where all entries are taken modulo
n+1
. This defines a parking function
\alpha\in\PF_{n}
and hence an
(n+1)
-to-1 map

\psi:[n+1]^{n}\to\PF_{n}
as proven in
[
16
]
. Then we can see that
\psi(w)
is a parking function with a tie in position 1 if and only if
w
has a tie in position
1
. Since there are
(n+1)^{n-1}
sequences
w\in[n+1]^{n}
such that
w
has a tie in position 1, and since
\psi
is
(n+1)
-to-1, we have

|\{\alpha\in\PF_{n}\mid\alpha_{1}=\alpha_{2}\}|=\frac{(n+1)^{n-1}}{n+1}=(n+1)^{n-2}.\qed

As a consequence of
Theorem

5.2
, we have the following.

For

\sum_{\alpha\in\PF_{n}}\tie(\alpha)=(n-1)(n+1)^{n-2}.

.

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha\in\PF_{n}}\tie(\alpha)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}i\binom{n-1}{i}n^{n-1-i}=(n-1)\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}\binom{n-2}{i-1}n^{n-1-i}=(n-1)(n+1)^{n-2},

By Schumacher’s result on the number of ties, we can write

Thus, we can apply

5.1.3.
Descent tops and inversion tops

Recall that the
major index
of a permutation
w
is
\maj(w)=\sum_{i\in\Des(w)}i
, or the sum of the indices where a decent occurs in
w
. This can be thought of as summing over descents weighted by the position they occur in. Instead of weighting by position, we can weight by its value and arrive at the
descent top statistic
defined by

\dtop(w)=\sum_{i\in\Des(w)}w_{i}.

For sequence data on the descent top statistic see
A263753
. For a word
w\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, define

\chi^{\dtop}(w,(i,j))=\begin{cases}w_{i}&w_{i}>w_{j}\\
0&\text{else,}\end{cases}

The function
\chi^{\dtop}
is a
2
-transitive function and
\dtop=g_{\chi^{\dtop}}
.
We can also define the
inversion top statistic
as

f_{\chi^{\dtop}}(w)=\itop(w)=\sum_{i<j}\chi^{\dtop}(w,(i,j)).

For
r\in[n]
, the probability that
\chi^{\dtop}((w,(1,2))=r
is
\frac{1}{n}\frac{r-1}{n-1}
because you need to select a permutation with
w_{1}=r
and
w_{2}<r
. Hence, we have

\displaystyle\E_{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}[\chi_{1}^{\dtop}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=1}^{n}\frac{r-1}{n(n-1)}\cdot r

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\sum_{r=1}^{n}r^{2}-r

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n(n-1)}\left(\frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6}-\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{n+1}{3}.

Thus, we have

(37)

\sum_{w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\dtop(w)=\frac{(n+1)!(n-1)}{3}\qquad\sum_{w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\itop(w)=\frac{(n+1)!n(n-1)}{6}=\binom{n+1}{3}n!.

5.1.4.
Graphical inversions

Let
D
be a directed graph on vertex set
[n]
and directed edge set
E
, the elements of which we represent using arrows
i\to j\in E
. For a word
w\in[n]^{n}
, define

\chi^{D}(w,(i,j))=\begin{cases}1&w_{i}\to w_{j}\in E\\
0&w_{i}\to w_{j}\not\in E.\end{cases}

If we define
\theta(x,y)=1
if
x\to y\in E
and
0
otherwise for
x,y\in[n]
, then
\chi^{D}(w,(i,j))=\theta(w_{i},w_{j})
for all
w\in[n]^{n}
, so this is a
2
-transitive function. The function
f_{\chi^{D}}
is the
graphical inversion statistic
, denoted
\inv_{D}^{\prime}
, studied by Foata and Zeilberger in
[
18
]
, where it was studied alongside the analogously defined
graphical major index
We have

(38)

\E_{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}[\chi_{1}^{D}]=\frac{|E|}{n(n-1)}

since
\chi^{D}_{1}(w)=1
if and only if
w_{1}\to w_{2}\in E
for each
w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. Defining the
graphical descent statistic
as
\des_{D}^{\prime}=g_{\chi^{D}}
, we have

(39)

\sum_{w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\inv_{D}^{\prime}(w)=n!\binom{n}{2}\frac{|E|}{n(n-1)}=\frac{n!|E|}{2}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\sum_{w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\des_{D}^{\prime}(w)=n!(n-1)\frac{|E|}{n(n-1)}=(n-1)!|E|.

by combining
Equation

38
with
Theorem

5.2
.

If we take
D
to be the complete graph with edges of the form
i\to j
whenever
i>j
, we have
\chi^{D}=\chi^{\inv}
and
|E|=\binom{n}{2}
is the number of edges, so
Equation

39
yield the formulas in
Corollary

5.9
.

5.1.5.
Descents, big and small

Instead of looking at all inversions, we can restrict our attention to inversions of a particular form. For a word
w\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, indices
i
and
j
define

\chi^{\sdes}(w,(i,j))=\begin{cases}1&w(i)=w(j)+1\\
0&\text{else.}\end{cases}

Then we define
\sdes=g_{\chi^{\sdes}}

which counts the number of
small descents
of
w
, i.e., positions
i
where
w(i)=w(i+1)+1
, and likewise
\sinv=f_{\chi^{\sdes}}
counts
small inversions
, i.e., positions
i<j
where
w(i)=w(j)+1
. Observe that if
\sigma
is a permutation, then

(40)

\sinv(\sigma)=\des(\sigma^{-1})

and so

(41)

\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\sinv(\sigma)=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\des(\sigma)=\frac{n!(n-1)}{2}.

One advantage of using
\sinv(\sigma)
over
\des(\sigma^{-1})
is that the former is more easily generalizable to words.

We can also define
big descents
and
big inversions
as
\bdes=g_{\chi^{\bdes}}
and
\binv=f_{\chi^{\bdes}}
, respectively, where

\chi^{\bdes}(w,(i,j))=\begin{cases}1&w(i)>w(j)+1\\
0&\text{else.}\end{cases}

for a given word
w\in\mathbb{N}^{n}

and pair
1\leq i<j\leq n
. Note that
\chi^{\inv}=\chi^{\sdes}+\chi^{\bdes}
.
Big descents correspond to
1
-descents of Foata and Schutzenberger
[
17
]
. These were more recently studied by Shareshian and Wachs in a quasisymmetric function generalization of Eulerian polynomials
[
47
,
48
]
and by Elizalde, Rivera, Zhuang on sets of pattern avoiding permutations
[
14
]
.
To compute expectations, note that a permutation starting with a small descent starts with a number
i
bigger than
1
and then
i-1
. Thus, it follows that

(42)

\E_{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}[\chi_{1}^{\sdes}]=\frac{(n-1)\cdot 1}{n(n-1)}=\frac{1}{n}=1-\E_{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}[\chi_{1}^{\bdes}].

Thus, combining
Equation

42
and
Theorem

5.2
(or equivalently
Equation

41
,
Theorem

5.2
, and
Corollary

5.9
), we have the following.

Proposition 5.17
.

Suppose
n\geq 1
.

(1)

\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\sdes(w)=(n-1)(n-1)!
.

(2)

\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\sinv(w)=\binom{n}{2}(n-1)!=\frac{n!(n-1)}{2}
.

(3)

\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\bdes(w)=\frac{(n-1)(n-2)(n-1)!}{2}
.

(4)

\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\binv(w)=\frac{(n-1)(n-2)n!}{4}
.

Items (1) and (3) appear to be well-known; see
A001563
and
A001804
, respectively.

For parking functions, the statistic
\sinv
counts the number of pairs of cars with adjacent preferences
i
and
i+1
so that the second car has the smaller preference. Similarly,
\sdes
counts the number of pairs of adjacent cars, car
i
and car
i+1
, with adjacent preferences
j
and
j-1
, respectively.

The Pollak map
[n+1]^{n}\to\PF_{n}
(discussed in
Lemma

5.13
) sends
w=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
to a parking function
\alpha
of the form
(a_{1}-i,\dots,a_{n}-i)
for some
i
, where the subtraction is computed modulo
n+1
. If
\chi_{1}^{\sdes}(\alpha)=1
, then
a_{1}-i=a_{2}-i+1\pmod{n+1}
, so we must have that
a_{1}-a_{2}=1\pmod{n+1}
On the other hand, If
w_{1}=w_{2}+1
or
w_{1}=1
and
w_{2}=n
, then
i\not\in\{w_{1},w_{2}\}
since both
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
must be occupied, so
\chi_{1}^{\sdes}(\alpha)=1
. Hence,
\alpha
has
\chi_{1}^{\sdes}(\alpha)=1
if and only if
a_{1}-a_{2}=1\pmod{n+1}
. For
w=(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n})\in W=[n+1]^{n},
the probability
a_{1}-a_{2}=1\pmod{n+1}
is
\frac{1}{n+1}
since the first letter can be freely chosen and the second letter is uniquely determined by the first, so

(43)

\E_{\PF_{n}}[\chi_{1}^{\sdes}]=\frac{1}{n+1}.

Thus, combining
Equation

43
and
Theorem

5.2
, we have the following.

Proposition 5.18
.

Let
n\geq 1
.

(1)

\sum_{w\in\PF_{n}}\sdes(w)=(n-1)(n+1)^{n-2}
.

(2)

\sum_{w\in\PF_{n}}\sinv(w)=\binom{n}{2}(n+1)^{n-2}
.

(3)

\sum_{w\in\PF_{n}}\bdes(w)=\binom{n-1}{2}(n+1)^{n-2}
.

(4)

\sum_{w\in\PF_{n}}\binv(w)=\frac{n(n-1)(n-2)(n+1)^{n-2}}{4}
.

5.1.6.
Permutation patterns

We finish our probabilistic discussion with a closer look
at when
W=\mathfrak{S}_{n}
. The archetypal example of
k
-transitive function on
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
is a test for the existence of a “pattern” of length
k
in a permutation.
For instance, a
132
-pattern of a permutation
w
is any triple of increasing indices
(i_{1},i_{2},i_{3})
such that
w_{i_{1}}<w_{i_{3}}<w_{i_{2}}
and
w_{i_{1}}<w_{i_{2}}
.
In this light, an inversion is a
21
-pattern.
More generally, for fixed
k
, we can define a
pattern
with a permutation
\rho\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}
, namely, a function
\chi^{\rho}:\mathbb{N}^{n}\times I_{n,k}
by setting

\chi^{\rho}(w,(i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{k}))=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }w_{i_{\rho^{-1}(1)}}<w_{i_{\rho^{-1}(2)}}<\cdots<w_{i_{\rho^{-1}(k)}}\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

If
\chi^{\rho}(w,\textbf{p})=1
, we say that the pattern
\rho

occurs
in
w
in the positions indicated by the entries in
p
.
Then
f_{\chi^{\rho}}(w)
is the number of occurrences of the pattern
\rho
in
w
and
g_{\chi^{\rho}}(w)
is the number of occurrences of
\rho
in adjacent positions.
Hence, by
Theorem

5.2
, we have

(44)

\sum_{w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}f_{\chi^{\rho}}(w)=\frac{1}{k}\binom{n}{k-1}\sum_{w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}g_{\chi^{\rho}}(w).

Here are two simple observations about any pattern
\rho\in\mathfrak{S}_{k}
: First,

(45)

\displaystyle\chi^{\rho}(w,\textbf{p})

\displaystyle=\chi^{id}(u,\textbf{p}),

where
u
is the permutation obtained by rearranging letters of
w
in the positions
p
in increasing order. Second,

(46)

\displaystyle\sum_{w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}f_{\chi^{\rho}}(w)

\displaystyle=n!\binom{n}{k}\frac{1}{k!}\,.

For this, we can see that
\mathbb{P}_{\mathfrak{S}_{n}}(\chi^{\rho}_{1}=1)=\frac{1}{k!}
since there is one way to arrange the first
k
letters to form the pattern
\rho
.
Since there are
n!
permutations, the claim follows from
Theorem

5.2
part (a).
Combining
Equation

44
and
Equation

46
, we have

(47)

\sum_{w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}g_{\chi^{\rho}}(w)=\frac{(n-k+1)n!}{k!}=(n-k+1)!\binom{n}{k},

which can also be directly seen from
Theorem

5.2
part (b).

Some classical permutation statistics can be written as sums of pattern enumerators. Let
R\subseteq\mathfrak{S}_{k}
.
One can see that

(48)

\chi^{R}\coloneqq\sum_{\rho\in R}\chi^{\rho},

is
k
-transitive, since a permutation
w
can only have a single pattern occur on a given
k
-tuple of positions. Hence, from
Equation

45
,
Equation

46
, and
Equation

47
, we have that

(49)

\E[f_{\chi^{R}}]=|R|\cdot\E[f_{\chi^{id}}]=|R|\cdot n!\binom{n}{k}\frac{1}{k!}\quad\text{and}\quad\E[g_{\chi^{R}}]=|R|\cdot\E[g_{\chi^{id}}]=|R|\cdot\frac{(n-k+1)n!}{k!}.

Example 5.19
.

The classical
peak
number of a permutation
w
is defined as

\operatorname{pk}(w)=|\{i\in[n-2]\mid w_{i}<w_{i+1}>w_{i+2}\}|.

Peaks of permutations are a classic topic in combinatorics and have been studied by a great many authors. See for example
[
1
,
2
,
4
,
20
,
58
,
3
,
33
,
38
,
39
,
45
,
56
]
.
We define the
horizon
number of a permutation
w
to be

\operatorname{hz}(w)=\#\{1\leq i_{1}<i_{2}<i_{3}\leq n\mid w_{i_{1}}<w_{i_{2}}>w_{i_{3}}\}.

Note the horizon number of a permutation is simply the number of
132
and
231
patterns.
Namely, if
R=\{132,231\}
, then

\operatorname{hz}=f_{\chi^{R}}
and
\operatorname{pk}=g_{\chi^{R}}
. Hence,

(50)

\sum_{w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\operatorname{pk}(w)=2\sum_{w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}f_{\chi^{132}}=\frac{(n-2)n!}{3}

and

(51)

\sum_{w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\operatorname{hz}(w)=\frac{1}{9}\binom{n}{2}(n-2)n!=\frac{1}{3}\binom{n}{3}n!.

Remark 5.20
.

Comparing
Equation

37
and
Equation

50
reveals that

(52)

\sum_{w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}}\dtop(w)=\sum_{w\in\mathfrak{S}_{n+1}}\operatorname{pk}(w).

We leave finding a combinatorial proof as an open problem. See
Section

.

5.2.
Generating functions

Given a family
\{W_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
of words so that
W_{n}\subseteq\mathbb{Z}_{>0}^{n}
and
W_{n}
is
\mathfrak{S}_{n}
-invariant for all
n
, and given a
k
-transitive function
\chi
, define the exponential generating functions

(53)

\displaystyle F(z)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\left(\sum_{w\in W_{n}}f_{\chi}(w)\right)\frac{z^{n}}{n!},\quad G(z)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\left(\sum_{w\in W_{n}}g_{\chi}(w)\right)\frac{z^{n}}{n!},\quad\text{and}\quad H(z)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\left(\sum_{w\in W_{n}}\chi_{1}(w)\right)\frac{z^{n}}{n!}.

For example, if
\chi=\chi^{\inv}
and
W_{n}=\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, then
F(z)
is the exponential generating function for the total number of inversions,
G(z)
is that for descents, and
H(z)
is that for descents in the first position.

Proposition 5.21
.

With
F(z)
,
G(z)
, and
H(z)
as in
Equation

53
,
we have the following relations:

(54)

\displaystyle F(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{z^{k-1}}{k!}\dfrac{d^{k-1}}{dz^{k-1}}[G(z)],

(55)

\displaystyle G(z)

\displaystyle=zH^{\prime}(z)-(k-1)H(z),

(56)

\displaystyle\dfrac{d^{k-1}}{dz^{k-1}}\left[G(z)\right]

\displaystyle=z\dfrac{d^{k}}{dz^{k}}\left[H(z)\right],\text{and}

(57)

\displaystyle F(z)

\displaystyle=\frac{z^{k}}{k!}\dfrac{d^{k}}{dz^{k}}\left[H(z)\right].

Proof.

These are routine computations following from
Theorem

5.2
. To establish
Equation

54
, we have

F(z)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{k}\binom{n}{k-1}\left(\sum_{w\in W_{n}}g_{\chi}(w)\right)\frac{z^{n}}{n!}=\frac{z^{k-1}}{k!}\sum_{n\geq 1}\left(\sum_{w\in W_{n}}g_{\chi}(w)\right)\frac{z^{n-k+1}}{(n-k+1)!}=\frac{z^{k-1}}{k!}\dfrac{d^{k-1}}{dz^{k-1}}[G(z)].

Recall from
Theorem

5.2
(b) that
\E_{W}[g_{\chi}]=(n-k+1)\E_{W}[\chi_{1}]=n\E_{W}[\chi_{1}]-(k-1)\E_{W}[\chi_{1}]
. Hence, we have

\displaystyle G(z)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\left(\sum_{w\in W_{n}}g_{\chi}(w)\right)\frac{z^{n}}{n!}=\sum_{n\geq 1}\left(\sum_{w\in W_{n}}\chi_{1}(w)\right)\frac{z^{n}}{(n-1)!}-(k-1)\sum_{n\geq 1}\left(\sum_{w\in W_{n}}\chi_{1}(w)\right)\frac{z^{n}}{n!},

and therefore
Equation

55
follows.

Equation

56
follows from using the product rule applied to the
Equation

55
. Lastly,
Equation

57
follows from combining
Equation

54
and
Equation

56
.
∎

Proposition

5.21
allows one to quickly get exponential generating functions for
\sum f_{\chi}
and
\sum g_{\chi}
given an expression for
H(z)
, which is usually the simplest to compute.
Note that as far as exponential generating functions are concerned, it does not matter if one computes

G(z)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\left(\sum_{w\in W_{n}}g_{\chi}(w)\right)\frac{z^{n}}{n!}\quad\text{or }\quad\dfrac{d^{k-1}}{dz^{k-1}}[G(z)]=\sum_{n\geq 1}\left(\sum_{w\in W_{n}}g_{\chi}(w)\right)\frac{z^{n-k+1}}{(n-k+1)!}.

since both “record” the examined sequence in essentially the same way.

Example 5.22
.

Consider
W_{n}=\UPF_{n}
and
\chi=\chi^{\inv}
.
It is slightly more straightforward to compute
G^{\prime}(z)=zH^{\prime\prime}(z)
.
Recall that

\sum_{n\geq 0}\Fub_{n}\frac{z^{n}}{n!}=\frac{1}{2-e^{z}}.

Combining this and
Equation

30
yields

\displaystyle 2H^{\prime}(z)

\displaystyle=2\sum_{n\geq 1}\left(\sum_{w\in\UPF_{n}}\chi^{\inv}_{1}(w)\right)\frac{z^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 1}(\Fub_{n}-\Fub_{n-1})\frac{z^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\geq 1}\Fub_{n}\frac{z^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}-\sum_{n\geq 1}\Fub_{n-1}\frac{z^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{2-e^{z}}\right)^{\prime}-\frac{1}{2-e^{z}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2(e^{z}-1)}{(2-e^{z})^{2}}.

Thus, from
Corollary

5.5
,

(58)

G^{\prime}(z)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\left(\sum_{\alpha\in\UPF_{n}}\des(w)\right)\frac{z^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}=z\left(\frac{e^{z}-1}{(2-e^{z})^{2}}\right)^{\prime}=\frac{ze^{2z}}{(2-e^{z})^{3}},

and from
Proposition

5.21
,

(59)

F(z)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\left(\sum_{\alpha\in\UPF_{n}}\inv(w)\right)\frac{z^{n}}{n!}=\frac{z}{2!}G^{\prime}(z)=\frac{z^{2}e^{2z}}{2(2-e^{z})^{3}}.

Example 5.23
.

Consider
\chi=\chi^{\tie}
on
W_{n}=\C_{n}
. From
Equation

29
we have

H^{\prime}(z)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\left(\sum_{\alpha\in\C_{n}}\chi_{1}^{\tie}(\alpha)\right)\frac{z^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}=\sum_{n\geq 1}\Fub_{n-1}\frac{z^{n-1}}{(n-1)!}=\frac{1}{2-e^{z}}.

(60)

Future work

We conclude with some directions for further study.
One can study inversions of parking functions for other variations and special subsets of parking functions, including

.

For other families of parking functions, find nice expressions for their inversion generating functions. Furthermore, determine if there is a natural

Inversions are just one of many word statistics to consider on parking functions. Hence, one research agenda is as follows:

q
-analogues can be explained via representation theoretic formulas. We would like fully representation theoretic explanations of all of the results in this paper. In particular, we pose the following.

Determine the representation theoretic interpretation of

5.2
and of the equality

given in

5.8
.
