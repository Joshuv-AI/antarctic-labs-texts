Quantitative Aortic Arch Calcification Is Associated with Non-ipsilateral Ischemic Lesions after Transfemoral Carotid Artery Stenting
Authors: Koji SHIMONAGA, Yoshito HIRATA, Masakazu MITSUNOBU, Ryo OGAMI, Yoshihiro KIURA, Atsushi TOMINAGA, Nobutaka HORIE
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Non-ipsilateral ischemic lesions after carotid artery stenting are considered to arise from emboli originating in the aortic arch, but the quantitative contribution of aortic arch calcification to post-carotid artery stenting ischemic lesions remains unclear. This study aimed to investigate the association between volumetric aortic arch calcification and non-ipsilateral diffusion-weighted imaging hyperintense lesions after transfemoral carotid artery stenting. We retrospectively analyzed 99 consecutive transfemoral carotid artery stenting procedures performed between January 2018 and December 2023. Aortic arch calcification volume was quantified using preoperative computed tomography angiography using threshold-based segmentation. Postprocedural diffusion-weighted imaging performed within 48 h was assessed for hyperintense lesions in the contralateral hemisphere or cerebellum. Variables were evaluated using univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses. Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis was used to determine the optimal cut-off value for predicting non-ipsilateral lesions. Non-ipsilateral high-intensity diffusion-weighted imaging lesions occurred in 14 patients (14.1%), who showed significantly higher aortic arch calcification volumes than did those without lesions (median 2.2 vs. 1.0 mL, p = 0.005). Receiver operating characteristic analysis identified an optimal cutoff of 1.3 mL (sensitivity 78.6%, specificity 62.3%, area under the curve 0.73). Both age (odds ratio 1.1 per year, p = 0.039) and aortic arch calcification volume (odds ratio 1.3 per mL, p = 0.040) were independently associated with non-ipsilateral lesions. Aortic arch type and guiding catheter size showed no significant associations. Quantitative aortic arch calcification on preoperative computed tomography angiography was independently associated with non-ipsilateral ischemic lesions after transfemoral carotid artery stenting. Incorporating calcification volume into preprocedural assessments may assist in selecting alternative access routes or protective strategies for patients at high risk.

Introduction

Carotid artery stenting (CAS) has become an established alternative to carotid endarterectomy, particularly in patients with high surgical risk.1-3) However, periprocedural ischemic complications, most frequently detected as new lesions on diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), remain a major concern. Although most lesions occur in the ipsilateral hemisphere, non-ipsilateral DWI lesions are also observed and are presumed to originate from emboli dislodged from the aortic arch during catheter manipulation.4,5) These lesions are clinically relevant because they cannot be prevented by conventional distal embolic protection devices and may be associated with cognitive decrease and silent cerebral infarction.

In addition, qualitative assessment of aortic arch calcification has revealed an association with peri-procedural embolic events. However, previous studies were based on subjective grading or thickness measurements,5-7) and no studies have quantitatively evaluated the calcification burden using volumetric analysis. Furthermore, it is unclear whether the calcification volume is independently associated with non-ipsilateral ischemic lesions after transfemoral CAS, beyond age-related atherosclerotic changes or carotid plaque morphology.

Recent advances in 3-dimensional computed tomography angiography (CTA) and semi-automated plaque analysis software have enabled objective measurement of aortic calcification volume. Quantitative volumetric assessment provides an objective and reproducible measure of the overall calcified plaque burden, which may better reflect embolic risk during catheter manipulation than conventional qualitative grading or thickness-based evaluation. This approach may provide a reproducible imaging biomarker to better stratify patients at risk of embolic complications, and may potentially guide procedural strategies, including the selection of alternative access routes such as transcarotid artery revascularization.

Therefore, we hypothesized that the aortic arch calcification volume would be associated with the occurrence of non-ipsilateral DWI high lesions after transfemoral CAS. This study aimed to evaluate this association and determine a clinically applicable cut-off value for risk stratification, thereby contributing to more appropriate therapeutic decision-making for carotid stenosis.

Materials and Methods

Study design and patient population

This retrospective observational study was approved by the Hiroshima Prefectural Hospital Ethics Review Committee (R5-13-1) on June 27, 2023, and an opt-out consent process was implemented. A total of 115 consecutive CAS procedures performed between January 2018 and December 2023 were reviewed.

To ensure a homogeneous cohort undergoing transfemoral navigation across the aortic arch, we included only cases treated through the transfemoral approach, with available preoperative CTA and postoperative DWI obtained within 48 h. Of the 115 procedures initially reviewed, 16 cases treated through alternative access routes (transbrachial approach) were excluded. The remaining 99 transfemoral CAS cases were included in the final analysis.

CTA acquisition and quantitative aortic arch calcification analysis

All patients underwent contrast-enhanced CTA from the aortic arch to the intracranial artery. Calcified plaques of the aortic arch (from the ascending aorta to the origin of the left subclavian artery), representing the aortic segment potentially involved in embolic events during transfemoral CAS, were identified using a window width of 500-900 HU and window level of 100-250 HU.

Quantification of calcification volume was performed using threshold-based automated segmentation with SYNAPSE VINCENT (FUJIFILM Medical systems, Tokyo, Japan). Volume data were recorded in milliliters (mL). This semi-automated method has been validated previously, showing high intra- and interobserver reproducibility for thoracic aortic plaque quantification (intraclass correlation coefficient >0.9) (Figure 1).

Magnetic resonance imaging and DWI lesion assessment

Postoperative brain magnetic resonance imaging, including DWI, was obtained within 48 h post-CAS. Two board-certified neurologists, blinded to the aortic plaque data, assessed the DWI lesions. Only non-ipsilateral lesions (in the contralateral hemisphere or cerebellum) were considered, to isolate embolic sources from the aortic arch. The primary end point of this study was the occurrence of non-ipsilateral DWI high-intensity lesions (contralateral hemisphere or cerebellum) within 48 h after transfemoral CAS.

Clinical, carotid imaging, and procedural variables

The clinical data included age, sex, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, smoking status, coronary artery disease, chronic kidney disease, and symptomatic carotid artery disease.

Carotid imaging findings comprised the degree of stenosis rate (using the North American Symptomatic Carotid Endarterectomy Trial method),8) ulceration, calcified plaque presence, and contralateral carotid stenosis. Procedural variables included guiding catheter size (8 or 9 Fr) and a type III aortic arch configuration.

CAS procedure

The indications were symptomatic stenosis ≥50% or asymptomatic stenosis ≥70%.9) Dual antiplatelet therapy was administered for at least 2 weeks before the procedure. CAS was performed with dual protection (distal filter plus flow reversal with aspiration) using an 8 or 9 Fr balloon-tipped guiding catheter placed in the common carotid artery by the coaxial method.10)

Statistical analysis

Associations between non-ipsilateral DWI lesions and clinical, imaging, and procedural factors were first assessed using the Mann-Whitney U test for nonparametric comparisons or the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for continuous variables and χ2 or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables. Subsequently, univariate analyses were conducted to identify the factors associated with the volume of aortic arch calcification. Spearman's rank correlation coefficient was used to evaluate the relationship between aortic arch calcification volume and age, in addition to the degree of carotid stenosis.

Variables that were significant in the univariate analysis, along with those associated with the calcification burden, were entered into multivariate logistic regression. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to determine the optimal calcification volume cutoff, and the area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, and specificity were calculated. Patients were stratified into the high- and low-calcification groups on the basis of this cutoff, and subgroup analyses were conducted to evaluate the effect of arch type and catheter diameter.

Calcification volume was also categorized into quartiles for testing the linear trend in risk using logistic regression. Statistical analyses were performed using JMP version 16 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA). p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The median age of the 99 patients included in this analysis was 75.0 years [range, 53-92; interquartile range, 70.0-79.9], and 15 (15.2%) were women. Symptomatic carotid stenosis was present in 37 cases (37.4%). Non-ipsilateral high-intensity DWI lesions occurred in 14 patients (14.1%). In none of the patients with non-ipsilateral DWI high lesions did permanent neurological deficits develop, and no deterioration in the modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score at discharge was observed.

Patients with non-ipsilateral high-intensity DWI lesions had significantly greater aortic arch calcification volumes (median 2.2 vs. 1.0 mL, p = 0.005) (Figure 2), and were older (median, 79.5 vs. 74.0 years, p = 0.013) than those without lesions. No significant association was evident between contralateral lesions and other clinical, procedural, or carotid imaging factors. Although aortic arch calcification volume was significantly associated with non-ipsilateral DWI high lesions, the mere presence of calcified plaque showed no such association.

Discussion

In this study, quantitative aortic arch calcification measured using preoperative CTA was significantly associated with the occurrence of non-ipsilateral DWI high lesions after transfemoral CAS. This association persisted after adjustment for major clinical and carotid imaging factors, suggesting that the calcification burden may play an important role in the risk of embolism during CAS. Nevertheless, the results should be interpreted with caution given the number of outcome events was limited, and potential model overfitting cannot be excluded.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate aortic calcification using volumetric analysis and to identify a clinically applicable cut-off value for predicting remote embolic lesions after CAS.

Previous studies have suggested that aortic arch plaque morphology, ulceration, or calcification thickness may contribute to cerebral embolization during CAS.11) Kastrup et al.6) and Gröschel et al.7) reported that arch calcification visible on plain radiography or CTA was associated with increased perioperative embolic events, particularly in older patients. However, these assessments were qualitative or semiquantitative in nature. More recently, Ito et al.5) indicated that contralateral DWI lesions after CAS were associated with aortic arch plaque, although the calcification burden was not quantitatively assessed. Our findings build on those of these studies using CTA-based volumetric measurements, which provide an objective, reproducible parameter that better reflects total embolic risk than qualitative grading. A larger volume of aortic arch calcification may reflect a more extensive and rigid atherosclerotic burden, which can increase the risk of plaque disruption and microembolization during catheter manipulation. Heavily calcified plaques may also reduce vascular compliance, leading to greater mechanical stress when devices are advanced through the arch, thereby facilitating embolic release into the cerebral circulation.11)

Importantly, we found that a calcification volume of ≥1.3 mL was associated with non-ipsilateral DWI lesions with moderate accuracy (AUC, 0.73). This threshold may provide practical guidance for preprocedural risk stratification. In patients with extensive aortic calcification, alternative strategies―such as transcarotid artery revascularization (TCAR), brachial or radial access, or minimizing arch manipulation―may be considered to reduce embolic risk. Conversely, patients with low calcification volumes may safely undergo transfemoral CAS with standard protection devices. Although the discriminative performance of calcification volume was moderate (AUC, 0.73), and dichotomization did not yield significant group differences, volumetric assessment may remain more informative when analyzed as a continuous variable and may serve as a useful parameter for risk stratification rather than a definitive predictive marker.

Quantification was performed using the validated SYNAPSE VINCENT software, which applies predefined Hounsfield thresholds to segment and calculate calcification volume with high intra- and interobserver reproducibility. This semi-automated approach is practical for routine use and may be readily applied to preoperative CTA data.12)

Age was also an independent predictor of non-ipsilateral DWI lesions, consistent with prior studies.13) Although age and calcification volume were moderately correlated, both retained significance in the multivariate analysis, suggesting that calcification contributes to the risk of embolism, independent of age-related vascular changes. The mechanisms linking calcification and embolization may involve plaque fragmentation, catheter scraping, or loss of aortic compliance; however, these pathways require further investigation.

This study showed that quantitative aortic arch calcification measured on preoperative CTA is independently associated with the occurrence of non-ipsilateral DWI high lesions after transfemoral CAS. A calcification volume of ≥1.3 mL was identified as a potential threshold for risk stratification of remote embolic events. These findings suggest that the aortic arch calcification burden is associated with non-ipsilateral ischemic lesions after transfemoral CAS and may serve as a useful parameter for preprocedural risk stratification.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design and single-center setting may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, the sample size and number of ischemic events were relatively small, which may have yielded insufficient power to detect weaker associations, as suggested by the non-significant trend across calcification quartiles. Because only 14 patients experienced non-ipsilateral DWI high lesions, the multivariate model may be subject to overfitting, and the results should be interpreted cautiously. We did not evaluate the anatomical distribution of aortic arch calcification, which may also influence embolic risk. The potential role of optimized medical therapy, including antiplatelet or lipid-lowering treatment, in reducing embolic risk in patients with extensive aortic calcification was not evaluated in this study and should be addressed in future research. Third, procedural details such as the number of catheter manipulations or temporal improvements in technique were not systematically recorded.14) A prospective multicenter study with standardized imaging and procedural protocols is warranted to validate our findings.

Despite these limitations, our study highlights a practical and reproducible imaging biomarker that can be readily incorporated into routine CTA evaluations before CAS. Recognizing the vulnerability of transfemoral CAS to aortic arch-related embolic events may improve risk stratification and support more appropriate treatment selection from among CAS, carotid endarterectomy, and emerging approaches such as TCAR.
