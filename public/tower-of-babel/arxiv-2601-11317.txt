Constructing Orthogonal Rational Function Vectors with an application in rational approximation
Robbe Vermeiren

Abstract
. We present two algorithms for constructing orthonormal bases of rational function vectors with respect to a discrete inner product, and discuss how to use them for a rational approximation problem. Building on the pencil-based formulation of the inverse generalized eigenvalue problem by Van Buggenhout et al. (2022), we extend it to rational vectors of arbitrary length k k , where the recurrence relations are represented by a pair of k k -Hessenberg matrices, i.e., matrices with possibly k k nonzero subdiagonals. An updating algorithm based on similarity transformations using rotations and a Krylov-type algorithm related to the rational Arnoldi method are derived. The performance is demonstrated on the rational approximation of z \sqrt{z} on [ 0 , 1 ] [0,1] , where the optimal lightning + polynomial convergence rate of Herremans, Huybrechs, and Trefethen (2023) is successfully recovered. This illustrates the robustness of the proposed methods for handling exponentially clustered poles near singularities.

Problem formulation

In this section, we discuss the necessary machinery needed to reformulate the problem of constructing a set of orthonormal rational function vectors in terms of an inverse eigenvalue problem. These vectors are orthonormal with respect to a certain given discrete inner product. For the sake of exposition, this paper deals with the case of
2{-}

Definition 2.3

Let

4.
Rational vector Krylov subspaces

This section shows how to solve Problem
2.4
with a Krylov-type algorithm by exploiting the connection with rational vector Krylov spaces. This can be seen as a vector generalization of the classical rational Arnoldi algorithm, see, e.g.,
[
14
]
. Recall that for a matrix
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
and vector
v\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, the standard Krylov space is defined as
\mathcal{K}_{m}(A,\bm{v})=\operatorname{span}\{\bm{v},A\bm{v},\ldots,A^{m-1}\bm{v}\}
. A rational Krylov subspace can be seen as a standard Krylov space multiplied by some rational function evaluated in the matrix
A
.

Definition 4.1
.

(Rational Krylov Subspace)
Consider a matrix
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
, a vector
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, poles
\bm{p}_{m}=\begin{bmatrix}p_{i}\end{bmatrix}_{i=1}^{m-1}
disjoint from
A
’s spectrum, the rational Krylov subspace of order
m
is defined as

Q_{m}(A,\bm{v},\bm{p}_{m})=\frac{1}{\prod_{i=1,p_{i}\neq\infty}^{m-1}(A-p_{i}I)}\mathcal{K}_{m}(A,\bm{v}).

A rational vector Krylov subspace can be seen as the sum of two rational Krylov subspaces in the following sense.

Definition 4.2
.

(Rational vector Krylov Subspace)
Consider a matrix
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
, two vectors
\bm{v}_{1},\bm{v}_{2}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
, poles
\bm{p}_{m}=\begin{bmatrix}p_{i}\end{bmatrix}_{i=1}^{m}
disjoint from
A
’s spectrum and indexing vector
\bm{\pi}_{m}\in\{1,2\}^{m}
. Let vectors
\bm{p}_{k,1}
and
\bm{p}_{k,2}
contain all
p_{i}
’s,
i=1,\ldots,k
for which
\pi_{k}=1
and
\pi_{k}=2
, respectively. The rational vector Krylov subspace is defined as

\mathcal{\bm{Q}}_{m}(A,[\bm{v}_{1},\bm{v}_{2}],\bm{p}_{m})=\mathcal{Q}_{d_{m,1}}(A,\bm{v}_{1},\bm{p}_{m,1})+\mathcal{Q}_{d_{m,2}}(A,\bm{v}_{2},\bm{p}_{m,2}),

where
d_{m,1}
and
d_{m,2}
are the number of elements of the vectors
\bm{p}_{m,1}
and
\bm{p}_{m,2}
, respectively.

To simplify notation, the dependence on the indexing vector
\bm{\pi}_{m}
is omitted. Note that these rational vector Krylov subspaces closely resemble block Krylov spaces
[
17
]
. Consider a matrix
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
and block vector
\bm{B}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{b}_{1}\;\ldots\;\bm{b}_{s}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times s}
of size
s
, then the
classical
block Krylov space is defined as

\mathcal{K}_{m}^{\text{Cl}}(A,\bm{B})=\operatorname{blockspan}\{\bm{B},A\bm{B},\ldots,A^{m-1}\bm{B}\}=\left\{\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}A^{i}\bm{B}C_{i}:C_{i}\in\mathbb{C}^{s\times s}\right\},

and thus every vector
v\in\mathcal{K}_{m}^{\text{Cl}}(A,\bm{B})
belongs to
\mathcal{K}_{m}(A,\bm{b}_{1})+\ldots+\mathcal{K}_{m}(A,\bm{b}_{s})
. Similarly,
\mathcal{\bm{Q}}_{m}(A,\begin{bmatrix}\bm{v}_{1},\bm{v}_{2}\end{bmatrix},\bm{p}_{m})
is defined as a sum of two rational Krylov spaces; however, the individual spaces may have different dimensions, depending on
\bm{p}_{m}
and
\bm{\pi}_{m}
.
In order to have a relation with the rational function vectors in Definition
2.1
, we further assume that
p_{1}=p_{2}=\infty
,
\pi_{1}=1
and
\pi_{2}=2
implying that

\displaystyle\mathcal{\bm{Q}}_{1}(A,[\bm{v}_{1},\bm{v}_{2}],\bm{p}_{m})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{Q}_{1}(A,\bm{v}_{1},\bm{p}_{m,1})=\operatorname{span}\{\bm{v}_{1}\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{\bm{Q}}_{2}(A,[\bm{v}_{1},\bm{v}_{2}],\bm{p}_{m})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{Q}_{1}(A,\bm{v}_{1},\bm{p}_{m,1})+\mathcal{Q}_{2}(A,\bm{v}_{2},\bm{p}_{m,2})=\operatorname{span}\{\bm{v}_{1},\bm{v}_{2}\}.

This relation is made explicit by noting that every vector
\bm{v}_{k}\in\mathcal{\bm{Q}}_{m}(A,\begin{bmatrix}\bm{v}_{1},\bm{v}_{2}\end{bmatrix},\bm{p}_{m})
can be written as

(9)

v_{k}=\bm{\phi}^{T}(A)\begin{bmatrix}\bm{v}_{1}\\
\bm{v}_{2}\end{bmatrix},\;\;\bm{\phi}(z)\in\mathcal{R}_{m},

where
\mathcal{R}_{m}
is the space of rational function vectors defined by poles
\bm{p}_{m}
and indexing vector
\bm{\pi}_{m}
.
Let
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denote the standard Euclidean inner product on
\mathcal{\bm{Q}}_{m}(A,[\bm{v}_{1},\bm{v}_{2}],\bm{p}_{m})
, then there is an isometry between the space of rational function vectors
\mathcal{R}_{n}
and a rational vector Krylov subspace. Recall that the rows of the weight matrix
W\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times 2}
are denoted by
\bm{w}^{T}_{i}
(cf. Section
2
). In the following proposition, we denote the columns of this weight matrix with
\bm{\omega}_{i}
.

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{R}_{n}}
be the inner product on
\mathcal{R}_{n}
, defined by nodes
\{z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
and weight matrix

(see Definition

). There is an isometry between

and
\mathcal{\bm{Q}}_{m}(Z,[\bm{\omega}_{1},\bm{\omega}_{2}],\bm{p}_{m})
where

.

Proof.

Let
\bm{v}_{1},\bm{v}_{2}\in\mathcal{\bm{Q}}_{m}(Z,[\bm{\omega}_{1},\bm{\omega}_{2}],\bm{p}_{m})
, then there exist
\bm{\phi}(z),\bm{\psi}(z)\in\mathcal{R}_{n}
such that
\bm{v}_{1}=\bm{\phi}^{T}(Z)\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\omega}_{1}\\
\bm{\omega}_{2}\end{bmatrix}
and
\bm{v}_{2}=\bm{\psi}^{T}(Z)\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\omega}_{1}\\
\bm{\omega}_{2}\end{bmatrix}

\langle\bm{v}_{1},\bm{v}_{2}\rangle=\begin{bmatrix}\overline{\bm{\omega}_{1}^{T}}&\overline{\bm{\omega}_{2}^{T}}\end{bmatrix}\overline{\bm{\psi}}(Z)\bm{\phi}^{T}(Z)\begin{bmatrix}\bm{\omega}_{1}\\
\bm{\omega}_{2}\end{bmatrix}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\overline{\bm{w}_{i}\bm{\psi}(z_{i})}\bm{w}_{i}\bm{\phi}(z_{i})=\langle\bm{\phi}(z),\bm{\psi}(z)\rangle_{\mathcal{R}_{n}}.

∎

4.1.
Rational vector Arnoldi algorithm

Proposition
4.3
indicates that we can solve problem

, as defined for
\mathcal{R}_{n}
, by finding an orthonormal basis for the isometric space
\mathcal{\bm{Q}}_{m}(Z,[\bm{\omega}_{1},\bm{\omega}_{2}],\bm{p}_{m})
. In this section, we explain how an Arnoldi-like algorithm constructs such an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{\bm{Q}}_{m}(A,[\bm{v}_{1},\bm{v}_{2}],\bm{p}_{m})
with
A\in\mathbb{C}^{n\times n}
,
\bm{v}_{1},\bm{v}_{2}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}
and
\bm{p}_{m}=\begin{bmatrix}p_{i}=\frac{\nu_{i}}{\mu_{i}}\end{bmatrix}_{i=1}^{m}
.
An orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{\bm{Q}}_{k+1}(A,[\bm{v}_{1},\bm{v}_{2}],\bm{p}_{m})
can inductively be constructed given an orthonormal basis
\{\bm{q}_{1},\ldots,\bm{q}_{k}\}
of
\mathcal{\bm{Q}}_{k}(A,[\bm{v}_{1},\bm{v}_{2}],\bm{p}_{m})
. Consider the vector

(10)

\widehat{\bm{q}}_{k+1}=\left(\mu_{k+1}A-\nu_{k+1}I\right)^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(\rho_{i,k}A-\eta_{i,k}I\right)\bm{q}_{i}\coloneqq\left(\mu_{k+1}A-\nu_{k+1}I\right)^{-1}\bm{r}_{k}.

The
continuation parameters

should be carefully chosen such that the vector
\widehat{\bm{q}}_{k+1}
expands the preceding Krylov space, i.e.,
\widehat{\bm{q}}_{k+1}\in\mathcal{\bm{Q}}_{k+1}(A,[\bm{v}_{1},\bm{v}_{2}],\bm{p}_{m})\setminus\mathcal{\bm{Q}}_{m}(A,[\bm{v}_{1},\bm{v}_{2}],\bm{p}_{m})
.
The new vector
\bm{q}_{k+1}
is then obtained by orthonormalizing

(11)

h_{k+1,k}\bm{q}_{k+1}=\widehat{\bm{q}}_{k+1}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}h_{i,k}\bm{q}_{i},

against the already known orthonormal vectors

.
Combining equations (

\displaystyle\begin{split}A\begin{bmatrix}\bm{q}_{1},\ldots,\bm{q}_{l+1}\end{bmatrix}K^{\ell}&=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{q}_{1},\ldots,\bm{q}_{l+1}\end{bmatrix}H^{\ell},\\

&&\mu_{\ell+1}h_{\ell+1,\ell}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{C}^{(\ell+1)\times(\ell-1)},\\

\nu_{3}h_{21}&\ddots&\vdots\\

and

are zero by construction. Let

denote the matrices obtained by removing the last two rows of

and

A\begin{bmatrix}\bm{q}_{1},\ldots,\bm{q}_{n}\end{bmatrix}K_{n}=\begin{bmatrix}\bm{q}_{1},\ldots,\bm{q}_{n}\end{bmatrix}H_{n},

where
(H_{n},K_{n})
is a 2-Hessenberg pencil.

We have not yet specified how to choose the continuation parameters
\rho_{i,k}
and
\eta_{i,k}
. These parameters depend on the pole
p_{k+1}
being finite or infinite, we discuss both cases.

Finite pole

Let
p_{k+1}\in\mathbb{C}
which is added in the first component function, meaning that
\pi_{k+1}=1
. We set
\rho_{i,k}=\eta_{i,k}=0
for all

, except for
\eta_{1,k}=1
, so that
\bm{r}_{k}=\bm{q}_{1}=c\bm{v}_{1}\in\operatorname{span}\{\bm{v}_{1}\}
, with

. The isometry in equation (

\widehat{\bm{q}}_{k+1}=c(A-p_{k+1}I)^{-1}\bm{v}_{1}\longleftrightarrow\bm{\phi}(z)=c\begin{bmatrix}\frac{1}{z-p_{k+1}}\\
0\end{bmatrix}.

The vector
\bm{q}_{1}
is a scaling of
\bm{v}_{1}
, and hence, by the connection with the rational function vectors, the pole

is added only in the first component.

The case
\pi_{k}=2
is treated analogously, with the only difference that
\bm{r}_{k}
must now be a scaling of
\bm{v}_{2}
in order to add the pole only in the second component. Therefore, we choose coefficients
\eta_{1,k}
and
\eta_{2,k}
such that

\bm{r}_{k}=\eta_{1,k}\bm{q}_{1}+\eta_{2,k}\bm{q}_{2}=c\bm{v}_{2}\in\operatorname{span}\{\bm{v}_{2}\},

and set all remaining
\rho_{i,k}
and
\eta_{i,k}
to zero. Again, we get the desired correspondence relation

\widehat{\bm{q}}_{k+1}=c(A-p_{k+1}I)^{-1}\bm{v}_{2}\longleftrightarrow\bm{\phi}(z)=c\begin{bmatrix}0\\
\frac{1}{z-p_{k+1}}\end{bmatrix}.

Infinite pole

Let
p_{k+1}=\nu_{k+1}/\mu_{k+1}=\infty
,
\pi_{k+1}=1
and furthermore assume that it is the
first
time we add a pole at infinity (without counting
p_{1}=\infty
). We choose
\nu_{k+1}=-1
,
\mu_{k+1}=0
and add the appropriate vector to our basis by setting
\bm{r}_{k}=\rho_{1,k}A\bm{q}_{1}=c\rho_{1,k}A\bm{v}_{1}
. Note that this gives the following correspondence relation

\widehat{\bm{q}}_{k+1}=c\rho_{1,k}A\bm{v}_{1}\longleftrightarrow\bm{\phi}(z)=c\rho_{1,k}\begin{bmatrix}z\\
0\end{bmatrix}.

Again, the case
\pi_{k}=2
is completely analogous except that we now take
\bm{r}_{k}=\rho_{1,k}A\bm{q}_{1}+\rho_{2,k}A\bm{q}_{2}
such that
\bm{r}_{k}\in\operatorname{span}_{\mathbb{C}}\{\bm{v}_{2}\}
.

Finally, we consider the case where the pole at infinity is added once more, i.e. not for the first time in a certain component. Assume
\pi_{k}=1
, the other case is treated similarly. Suppose
\ell
is the previous index where we added a pole at infinity in the first component, i.e.
p_{\ell}=\infty
,
\pi_{\ell}=1
and there exists no index
\ell<i\leq k
such that
p_{i}=\infty
and
\pi_{i}=1
. Consider the corresponding rational function vectors of
\bm{q}_{\ell-1}
and
\bm{q}_{\ell}

\begin{array}[]{ccc}\bm{q}_{\ell-1}&&\bm{q}_{\ell}\\
\big\updownarrow&,&\big\updownarrow\\
\bm{\phi}_{\ell-1}(z)=\begin{bmatrix}\phi_{(\ell-1),1}\\
\phi_{(\ell-1),2}\end{bmatrix}&&\bm{\phi}_{\ell}(z)=\begin{bmatrix}\phi_{\ell,1}\\
\phi_{\ell,2}\end{bmatrix}.\end{array}

We cannot just take

because this would mean that the polynomial degrees of both component functions

and
\phi_{\ell,2}
is increased. Since
\pi_{k+1}=1
, we cannot increase
\deg_{p}(\phi_{\ell,2})
. For this reason, we take a linear combination of
\bm{q}_{\ell-1}
and
\bm{q}_{\ell}
to lower the polynomial degree of the second component before multiplying with
A
. Let us denote
\deg_{p}(\phi_{\ell,1})=\delta_{1}
and
\deg_{p}(\phi_{\ell,2})=\delta_{2}
. Also, the pole
p_{\ell}
at infinity was added in the first component, and thus,
\deg_{p}(\phi_{(\ell-1),1})<\delta_{1}
and
\deg_{p}(\phi_{(\ell-1),2})\leq\delta_{2}
. Hence, taking the continuation parameters
\rho_{\ell-1,k},\rho_{\ell,k}
such that the polynomial degree of the second component is reduced, i.e.

\rho_{\ell-1,k}\bm{q}_{l-1}+\rho_{k,\ell}\bm{q}_{\ell}\longleftrightarrow\widehat{\bm{\phi}}(z)=\begin{bmatrix}\widehat{\phi}_{1}\\
\widehat{\phi}_{2}\end{bmatrix},\;\;\deg_{p}(\widehat{\phi}_{1})=\delta_{1}\;\text{and}\;\deg_{p}(\widehat{\phi}_{2})<\delta_{2},

yields that
\widehat{\bm{q}}_{k+1}=A(\rho_{\ell-1,k}\bm{q}_{l-1}+\rho_{k,\ell}\bm{q}_{\ell})
correctly expands the basis by adding a pole at infinity only in the first component.

Theoretically, this procedure should give an orthonormal set
\{\bm{q}_{1},\dots,\bm{q}_{k+1}\}
.
In practice, finite precision arithmetic causes a loss of orthogonality due to accumulated round-off errors. To correct this, we employ
full
re-orthogonalization in which the current vector is orthogonalized against
all
the previous vectors a second time (cf. equation (
11
)).
To reduce computational cost, one could use
partial
re-orthogonalization (performing the correction only when necessary) or
selective
re-orthogonalization (orthogonalizing against only a subset of the previous vectors).

Numerical Experiments

To compare our algorithms, we replicate the two numerical experiments presented by Van Buggenhout et al.
[
24
]
. The first experiment uses equidistant nodes on the complex unit circle and equidistant poles on a circle of radius
3/2
. We assign weights
\bm{w}_{j}
uniformly distributed within the complex square
\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{3}{2}\right)\times\left(\frac{1}{2}i,\frac{3}{2}i\right)
and use a uniformly random indexing vector
\bm{\pi}_{n}\in\{1,2\}^{n}
.

The following four error metrics are used to compare our algorithms:

•

orthonormality of
Q
,

\operatorname{err}_{Q}=\|Q^{H}Q-I_{n}\|_{2},

•

orthonormality of
\bm{\phi}_{j}
’s,

\operatorname{err}_{\phi}=\|M-I_{n}\|_{2},\;\text{with}\;M_{i,j}=\langle\phi_{i},\phi_{j}\rangle_{\mathcal{R}_{n}},

•

relative accuracy of the poles,

\operatorname{err}_{p}=\max_{1\leq i\leq n-2}\Bigg|\frac{h_{i+2,i}}{k_{i+2,i}}-p_{i+2}\Bigg|/|p_{i+2}|

•

and the relative accuracy of the recurrence relation,

\operatorname{err}_{r}=\frac{\|ZQK-QH\|_{2}}{\max\{\|ZQK\|_{2},\|QH\|_{2}\}}.

Application: rational approximation of
\sqrt{z}

In this final section, we show the robustness of the updating algorithm by applying it to the rational approximation of
\sqrt{z}
on the interval
[0,1]
, where the poles are exponentially clustered near the singularity at the origin
5

MATLAB
implementation can be found at

.
. Herremans, Huybrechs and Trefethen
[
15
]
extended the “lightning method” for analytic functions with branch point singularities, by adding a polynomial term or partial fractions with poles clustered towards infinity. For the case of
f(z)=\sqrt{z}
, they show that the optimal minimax convergence rate
\mathcal{O}(\exp(-\pi\sqrt{2{N}}))
, where
N=N_{1}+N_{2}
, is attained by a
lightning + polynomial
approximation of the form

(12)

r(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{1}}\frac{a_{j}}{z-p_{j}}+\sum_{j=0}^{N_{2}}b_{j}z^{j},

with tapered exponentially clustered poles,

(13)

p_{j}=-C\exp(-\sigma(\sqrt{N_{1}}-\sqrt{j})),\quad j=1,2,\ldots,N_{1}.

Similarly as done by Herremans et al.
[
15
]
, we use
C=2
,
\sigma=2\sqrt{2}\pi
. Furthermore, we take the polynomial degree
N_{2}=\text{ceil}(2\sqrt{N_{1}})
.
The lightning method uses least-squares fitting to find the coefficient vector
\bm{c}
containing the
a_{j}
’s and
b_{j}
’s,

\min_{\bm{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{N+1}}\|A\bm{c}-\bm{f}\|_{2},\;A_{i,j}=\psi_{j}(t_{i}),\;\bm{f}_{i}=f(t_{i}),\

with sampling points
\{t_{i}\}_{i=1}^{M}
and the approximation set
\{\psi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N}
containing the partial fractions
\{\frac{p_{j}}{z-p_{j}}\}_{j=1}^{N_{1}}
and polynomial basis
\{z^{j}\}_{j=0}^{N_{2}}
. We will assume that the number of sampling points
M
is at least equal to
N
.
The sampling points are chosen as an exponentially graded
grid between

10^{-10}
and
1
, i.e., in Matlab notation:

logspace(-10,0,M-1)
, with the additional value
0
.
Although the obtained system is severely ill-conditioned, one still finds a good approximation using oversampling and regularization
[
15
,
1
]
.

The remainder of the section focuses on three aspects:

•

The reformulation of the approximation problem in the framework of orthogonal rational vectors functions (of length 2) belonging to
\mathcal{R}_{N}\subset\mathcal{R}_{M}
.

•

The choice of parameters for constructing the vector space
\mathcal{R}_{M}
and a corresponding inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{R}_{M}}
.

•

An experiment showing the minimax convergence rate
\mathcal{O}(\exp(-\pi\sqrt{2{N}}))
attained by the approximants.

The approximant in our framework is of the form

r(z)=\frac{r_{1}(z)}{r_{2}(z)}=\frac{n_{1}(z)/d_{1}(z)}{n_{2}(z)/d_{2}(z)},

where
n_{1},n_{2},d_{1},d_{2}
are polynomials of an appropriate degree and such that
d_{1}(z)
and
d_{2}(z)
are determined in advance, i.e., we fix the poles of
r_{1}(z)
and
r_{2}(z)
. The goal is to have
r_{1}(t_{i})/r_{2}(t_{i})\approx f(t_{i})
, which we achieve by solving the least-squares problem,

(14)

\min_{r(z)}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\left(r_{1}(t_{i})f_{i,2}-r_{2}(t_{i})f_{i,1}\right)^{2},

with
f(t_{i})=f_{i,1}/f_{i,2}
. This can be seen as a linearization of the standard least-squares problem with the rational function
r(z)
. The approximant
r(z)=r_{1}(z)/r_{2}(z)
is obtained from a vector

\bm{r}(z)=\begin{bmatrix}r_{1}(z)\\
r_{2}(z)\end{bmatrix}\in\mathcal{R}_{N},

where
\mathcal{R}_{N}\subset\mathcal{R}_{M}
is the space of rational function vectors of dimension
N
. The pole vector
\bm{p}_{N}
defining this space
\mathcal{R}_{N}
contains poles of the first component function
r_{1}(z)
and poles of the second component function
r_{2}(z)
. The extension
\mathcal{R}_{M}
is constructed by adding
M-N
additional poles. As before, the indexing vector
\bm{\pi}_{M}
determines if the pole is located in
r_{1}(z)
or
r_{2}(z)
. Finally, we define an inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{R}_{M}}
given by the following nodes and weights,

z_{i}=t_{i},\;\quad\bm{w}_{i}=\begin{bmatrix}f_{i,2},\;-f_{i,1}\end{bmatrix}^{T}.

Assume we have an orthonormal basis
\{\bm{\phi}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{M}
of
\mathcal{R}_{M}
obtained by either the updating or Krylov-type algorithm and write
\bm{r}(z)
as a linear combination of these basis functions
\bm{r}(z)=\sum_{i=1}^{M}c_{i}\bm{\phi}_{i}(z)
where
c_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
. This allows us to rewrite equation (
14
) as

\displaystyle\min\langle\bm{r}(z),\bm{r}(z)\rangle_{\mathcal{R}_{M}}

\displaystyle=\min\Big\langle\sum_{i=1}^{M}c_{i}\bm{\phi}_{i}(z),\sum_{i=1}^{M}c_{i}\bm{\phi}_{i}(z)\Big\rangle_{{\mathcal{R}_{M}}}

\displaystyle=\min\sum_{i=1}^{M}c_{i}^{2}.

Since we have the constraint
\bm{r}(z)\in\mathcal{R}_{N}
, we have
c_{N+1}=\cdots=c_{M}=0
. We could impose extra conditions on
\bm{r}(z)
to have a unique solution for this minimization problem. For example, requiring
\|\bm{r}(z)\|_{\mathcal{R}_{n}}=1
and that
\bm{r}(z)\in\mathcal{R}_{N}\setminus\mathcal{R}_{N-1}
, then we obtain the solution
c_{1}=\ldots=c_{N-1}=0
and
c_{N}=1
. However, we propose a different strategy by choosing
\bm{r}(z)=\bm{\phi}_{i}(z)
, i.e. set
c_{j}=1
if
j=i
and
c_{j}=0
otherwise. We select the vector
\bm{\phi}_{i}(z)
for which
\|f-r\|_{\infty}
is closest to the optimal convergence rate. Evaluating
r(z)
, needed to compute this maximal error, is done by solving the linear system (
8
). The typical convergence behaviour, as seen by the blue/red line in Figure
3(a)
, is a rapid initial decrease, followed by stagnation occurring at the end because of round-off errors. We exploit this by taking
\bm{r}(z)=\bm{\phi}_{i}
where
i
is selected from this stagnation region
\mathbb{S}\subset[1,N]
, and for which
\|f-r\|_{\infty}
is closest to the optimal convergence rate
\mathcal{O}(\exp(-\pi\sqrt{2N}))
.

To approximate
f(z)=\sqrt{z}
, one could take a
ligtning + polynomial
approximant
r_{1}(z)
and set
r_{2}(z)=1
. This agrees with the approximant (
12
) used by Herremans et al.
[
15
]
, but does not make full use of the fact that we have rational
vectors
since the second component function
r_{2}(z)
is just a constant. Therefore, in the context of approximating
f(z)=\sqrt{z}
, we impose
r(0)=0
which is satisfied by taking
r_{2}=1/z
, for example. Therefore, the parameters are

r_{2}(z)=1/z,\;f_{i,1}=\sqrt{t_{i}}\;,\;f_{i,2}=1,

and
r_{1}(z)
being of the form (
12
). The appropriate vector space
\mathcal{R}_{M}
and inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathcal{R}_{M}}
for these parameters are defined by taking
\begin{aligned} \bm{p}_{M}&=\begin{bmatrix}\infty,\infty,0,p_{1},\ldots,p_{N_{1}},\infty,\ldots,\infty\end{bmatrix}^{T}\in\overline{\mathbb{C}}^{3+N_{1}+N_{2}+N_{3}},\\
\bm{\pi}_{M}&=\begin{bmatrix}1,2,2,1,\ldots,1,2,\ldots,2\end{bmatrix}^{T}.\end{aligned}

More precisely, the poles consist of two initial poles at infinity in the first and second component, a pole at zero in the second component,
N_{1}
finite tapered poles
p_{j}
(

N_{2}

To circumvent this, the
N_{2}+N_{3}

10^{16}\exp\left(\frac{2\pi k\mathrm{i}}{N_{2}+N_{3}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}.

We remark that we use a total amount of
M=3+N_{1}+N_{2}+N_{3}
poles in
\mathcal{R}_{M}
, while the number of poles used for the approximant
r(z)
is
N=N_{1}+N_{2}+3
. The additional
N_{3}
poles at infinity are included for oversampling; in our experiments, we set
N_{3}=20N
.

As indicated before, a solution is selected from a certain stagnation region
\mathbb{S}

\mathbb{S}=[N-N_{2},N]

\bm{r}(z)=\bm{\phi}_{i}
where
i\in\mathbb{S}
and the error is closest to the optimal convergence rate. More concretely, this approximant is of the form

(15)

r(z)=z\left(\sum_{j=1}^{N_{1}}\frac{a_{j}}{z-p_{j}}+\sum_{j=0}^{k}b_{j}z^{j}\right),

where
k<N_{2}
. Observe that, in Figure

r(0)

(16)

\widehat{r}(z)=\sum_{j=1}^{N_{1}}\frac{a_{j}}{z-p_{j}}+\sum_{j=0}^{k}b_{j}z^{j},

where
k<N_{2}
. This is a
lightning + polynomial
approximation and still follows the optimal minimax convergence rate. However, the value
r(0)
initially decreases at a rate of
\mathcal{O}(\exp(-\pi\sqrt{2N}))
, but for larger
N
, it also drops to the size of the machine precision.

7.
Conclusion and future work

In this paper we have shown that computing an orthonormal basis of rational
function vectors having
k
components with respect to a discrete inner product is equivalent
to an inverse eigenvalue problem concerning a pencil of
k
-Hessenberg matrices.
Two algorithms to solve this problem were presented: an updating algorithm
and a rational Krylov-type algorithm.
The numerical experiments indicate that the updating algorithm leads to more
accurate results compared to the rational Krylov-type method.
To show the robustness of the updating algorithm, we considered the problem
of approximating
\sqrt{z}
on
[0,1]
by a rational function having
tapered exponentially clustered poles.
For future work, it would be interesting to investigate how a downdating procedure—i.e., removing a node and its corresponding weight—might be designed. As noted at the end of Section
3
, one could develop an algorithm that manipulates the factorization of
K_{k}
using techniques similar to those in
[
18
]
. Such an approach may improve both numerical stability and computational efficiency.
