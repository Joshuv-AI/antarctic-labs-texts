Dear Editor,

We thank the correspondent for their careful reading of our manuscript and for raising the important issue regarding the reporting of measurement error. We fully agree that transparency in reliability analysis is essential when interpreting longitudinal radiographic data. We address the concerns below.

1. Omission of SEM and MDC95 in the Results

The correspondent correctly notes that our Methods section stated that SEM and MDC95 were calculated, yet neither value appeared in the Results. This was an oversight on our part, and we appreciate the opportunity to clarify this important methodological aspect.

We have now performed a formal reliability analysis based on a randomly selected subset of 30 patients from the original cohort. All radiographic parameters were measured independently by two spine surgeons. Intra-observer reliability was assessed by having one observer re-measure the same 30 radiographs after a minimum 2-week interval, blinded to initial results. Inter-observer reliability was evaluated using the initial measurements from both observers on the same subset. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) were calculated using a two-way mixed-effects model (absolute agreement) for intra-observer reliability and a two-way random-effects model (absolute agreement) for inter-observer reliability. Because longitudinal follow-up in our cohort was performed by the same observer, we used the intra-observer ICCs to derive the SEM and MDC95, which best reflect the error relevant to detecting within-subject changes over time (Table 1.).

2. Interpretation of Observed Changes Relative to MDC95 Thresholds

The correspondent correctly points out that the observed mean changes over 12 years are smaller than the MDC95 thresholds (e.g., SVA: +2.21 mm vs. MDC 9.60 mm; T1S: +1.55° vs. MDC 9.08°; ROM: −8.96° vs. MDC 12.63°). This is an important observation, and we have now addressed it explicitly in the revised Discussion.

However, we respectfully contend that this does not invalidate our group-level conclusion of a genuine physiological drift toward sagittal imbalance, for the following reasons:

2.1. MDC applies to individuals, not to group means

The MDC95 is used to assess whether a change in an individual exceeds measurement error, with its threshold derived from between-subject variability. In contrast, the present study examines changes in group means over time, and the paired t-test relies on the standard error of the mean (SEMean), calculated as the standard deviation of change scores divided by √n. With a sample size of 198, the SEMean is smaller than the individual-level MDC95. Thus, although the 12-year mean increase in SVA of 2.21 mm is smaller than the individual MDC95, the change is statistically significant at the group level (P = 0.004) and is consistent in direction with other sagittal parameters. This finding suggests a group-level trend toward sagittal imbalance, but it should not be used to determine change in an individual patient.

2.2. Convergent biomechanical pattern

ROM significantly decreased, T1S and C7S significantly increased, SVA significantly increased, and Cobb angle failed to compensate. All parameters shifted in the theoretically predicted direction of sagittal imbalance. The probability that all these parameters would drift in the same direction purely due to random measurement error is extremely low. 1

2.3. Comparison with previous reliability studies

Our ICC values (0.828–0.941) and the derived SEM/MDC95 are consistent with previous reliability studies on cervical radiographic measurements. 2 The slightly larger SEM/MDC in our study may reflect the inclusion of a broader range of parameters (e.g., SVA, TIA), measurements performed by spine surgeons with varying levels of experience, and the inherent variability of real-world clinical imaging. 3

3. Additional Clarification

We agree that these changes may not be large at the individual level, but the purpose of this study was to establish the physiological “baseline drift” occurring naturally over a decade, providing a background reference for evaluating long-term surgical outcomes. While there is a certain selection bias in the original non-surgical patient population chosen. Even small changes, if they are statistically significant at the group level and exhibit a convergent pattern, are scientifically meaningful.

5. Conclusion

We are grateful to the correspondent for providing us the opportunity to clarify this important methodological issue. Based on the reliability analysis of 30 repeated measurements, our measurement protocol demonstrated good to excellent reproducibility (intra-observer ICC 0.828–0.929; inter-observer ICC 0.852–0.941). Although individual-level changes were smaller than the MDC95, the group-level convergent drift was statistically significant, supporting the conclusion of a progressive sagittal drift with aging.
