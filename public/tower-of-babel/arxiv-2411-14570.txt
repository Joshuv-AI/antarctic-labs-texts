Gradient-based optimization for variational empirical Bayes multiple regression
Saikat Banerjee, Peter Carbonetto, Matthew Stephens

Abstract
Variational empirical Bayes (VEB) methods provide a practically attractive approach to fitting large, sparse, multiple regression models. These methods usually use coordinate ascent to optimize the variational objective function, an approach known as coordinate ascent variational inference (CAVI). Here we propose alternative optimization approaches based on gradient-based (quasi-Newton) methods, which we call gradient-based variational inference (GradVI). GradVI exploits a recent result from Kim et al . [arXiv:2208.10910] which writes the VEB regression objective function as a penalized regression. Unfortunately the penalty function is not available in closed form, and we present and compare two approaches to dealing with this problem. In simple situations where CAVI performs well, we show that GradVI produces similar predictive performance, and GradVI converges in fewer iterations when the predictors are highly correlated. Furthermore, unlike CAVI, the key computations in GradVI are simple matrix-vector products, and so GradVI is much faster than CAVI in settings where the design matrix admits fast matrix-vector products ( e.g ., as we show here, trendfiltering applications) and lends itself to parallelized implementations in ways that CAVI does not. GradVI is also very flexible, and could exploit automatic differentiation to easily implement different prior families. Our methods are implemented in an open-source Python software, GradVI available from https://github.com/stephenslab/gradvi .

2.3
Variational Empirical Bayes (VEB)

Given the prior
g
and residual variance
\sigma^{2}
, Bayesian inference for the regression parameters
\mathbf{b}
is based on the posterior distribution

p_{\textrm{post}}\kern-0.86108pt\left(\,\mathbf{b}\mid\mathbf{y},\mathbf{X},g,\sigma^{2}\,\right)\propto p\kern-0.86108pt\left(\,\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{X},\mathbf{b},\sigma^{2}\,\right)p\kern-0.86108pt\left(\,\mathbf{b}\mid g,\sigma^{2}\,\right).

(3)

Unfortunately this posterior distribution is intractable for most priors of interest, and so must be approximated. Given some suitably-specified family
\mathcal{Q}
of approximating distributions, variational methods seek to find the

that minimizes the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence from

to the true posterior
[
12

37

\displaystyle\widehat{q}

\displaystyle\triangleq\argmin_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(q\parallel p_{\textrm{post}}\right)

(4)

where
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(q\parallel p\right)
denotes the KL divergence
[
38
]

from a distribution
q
to a distribution
p
.
Since
D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(q\parallel p_{\textrm{post}}\right)
is itself computationally intractable, it is standard to recast the optimization problem (
4

[
39

37
,
40

\widehat{q}=\argmax_{q\in\mathcal{Q}}F(q,g,\sigma^{2})\,,

(5)

where

\displaystyle F(q,g,\sigma^{2})

\displaystyle=\log p\kern-0.86108pt\left(\,\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{X},g,\sigma^{2}\,\right)-D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(q\parallel p_{\textrm{post}}\right)

(6)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q}\kern-0.43057pt\left[\log p\kern-0.86108pt\left(\,\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{X},\mathbf{b},g,\sigma^{2}\,\right)\right]-D_{\mathrm{KL}}\left(q\parallel g\right).

(7)

The function

is often called the “Evidence Lower Bound” (ELBO) because it is a lower-bound for the “evidence”,

\log p\left(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{X},g,\sigma^{2}\right)

The variational Empirical Bayes (VEB) approach builds on the above by also estimating

by optimizing

\widehat{q},\widehat{g},\widehat{\sigma}^{2}\triangleq\argmax_{q\in\mathcal{Q},g\in\mathcal{G},\sigma^{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}F(q,g,\sigma^{2})\,.

(8)

Kim
et al

[

used this VEB approach

They use the so-called “mean field" approximation

\mathcal{Q}=\left\{q:q\left(\mathbf{b}\right)=\prod_{j=1}^{P}q_{j}(b_{j})\right\},

3
Quasi-Newton Method for Variational Inference

Our goal is to solve the VEB optimization problem in (
14
) using gradient-based methods.
To implement quasi-Newton optimization, one needs to compute the objective function in (
15
) and its
derivatives with respect to the optimization parameters, namely
\boldsymbol{\theta}
,
g
and
\sigma^{2}
.
However, computing the penalty
\rho(\theta_{j},g,v_{j}^{2})
using the relation (
16
),
requires computing the inverse of the posterior mean operator
S
, which we denote
T
:

T(\theta_{j},g,v_{j}^{2})\triangleq{S}_{g,v_{j}}^{-1}(\theta_{j}).

(19)

Indeed, combining (
19
) and
(
16
), the penalty function can be written in terms of
T
:

\displaystyle\rho\left(\theta_{j},g,v_{j}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\rho\left({S}_{g,v_{j}}(T(\theta_{j},g,v_{j}^{2})),g,v_{j}^{2}\right)

(20)

\displaystyle=-\ell_{\NM}(T(\theta_{j},g,v_{j}^{2}),g,v_{j})-\frac{1}{2v_{j}^{2}}\left(T(\theta_{j},g,v_{j}^{2})-\theta_{j}\right)^{2}\,.

(21)

While
{S}_{g,v_{j}}
is available analytically for many prior distributions of interest, its inverse is not generally available (an exception being the normal prior family).
In this work we investigate two different strategies to circumvent this difficulty:
(a) use numerical inversion techniques, and
(b) use a change of variable to make things analytically tractable.

3.1
Numerical Inversion of Posterior Mean

In this approach, we minimize the objective function in (
15
)
using numerical approximation to invert the posterior mean operator
S
and to obtain
T
, see (
19
).
We use the form of the penalty function defined in (
21
).
The partial derivatives of
\rho
are given by:

\displaystyle\partial_{1}\rho\left(\theta_{j},g,v_{j}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{v_{j}^{2}}\left(T\left(\theta_{j},g,v_{j}^{2}\right)-\theta_{j}\right)\,,

(22)

\displaystyle\partial_{2}\rho\left(\theta_{j},g,v_{j}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=-\partial_{2}\ell_{\NM}\left(T\left(\theta_{j},g,v_{j}^{2}\right),g,v_{j}^{2}\right)\,,

(23)

\displaystyle\partial_{3}\rho\left(\theta_{j},g,v_{j}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=-\partial_{3}\ell_{\NM}\left(T\left(\theta_{j},g,v_{j}^{2}\right),g,v_{j}^{2}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left(\ell_{\NM}^{\prime}(T(\theta_{j},g,v_{j}^{2}),g,v_{j}^{2})\right)^{2}.

(24)

Here, (
22
) follows directly from Eq. (
17

To derive (
23
), we note that,

\displaystyle\partial_{2}\rho\left(\theta_{j},g,v_{j}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\quad=\Bigg[-\partial_{1}\ell_{\NM}(T(\theta_{j},g,v_{j}^{2}),g,v_{j}^{2})\,\partial_{2}T(\theta_{j},g,v_{j}^{2})-\partial_{2}\ell_{\NM}(T(\theta_{j},g,v_{j}^{2}),g,v_{j}^{2})-\frac{\left(T(\theta_{j},g,v_{j}^{2})-\theta_{j}\right)}{v_{j}^{2}}\,\partial_{2}T(\theta_{j},g,v_{j}^{2})\Bigg]

\displaystyle\quad=\Bigg[-\cancel{\ell_{\NM}^{\prime}(T(\theta_{j},g,v_{j}^{2}),g,v_{j}^{2})\,\partial_{2}T(\theta_{j},g,v_{j}^{2})}-\partial_{2}\ell_{\NM}(T(\theta_{j},g),g,v_{j}^{2})+\cancel{\ell_{\NM}^{\prime}(T(\theta_{j},g,v_{j}^{2}),g,v_{j}^{2})\,\partial_{2}T(\theta_{j},g,v_{j}^{2})}\Bigg].

In the last step, we used (
13
) to substitute

(T(\theta_{j},g,v_{j}^{2})-\theta_{j})/v_{j}^{2}=\ell_{\NM}^{\prime}(T(\theta_{j},g,v_{j}^{2}),g,v_{j}^{2})\,.

Finally, (
24
) can be derived using the same strategy as used for (
23
).
Therefore, it becomes possible to calculate the penalty function and its derivatives
with respect to the optimization parameters if we have a numerical way to compute
T(\theta_{j},g,v_{j}^{2})
.
The other terms in the objective function (
15
) are straightforward
and we can solve the optimzation problem in (
14
)
using quasi-Newton updates, until a minimum is found.

Several numerical methods exist to compute the inverse of a function,
{S}_{g,v_{j}}^{-1}\left(\theta_{j}\right)
.
The general idea is to find the root (vector)
\mathbf{z}
of the vector-valued equation

{S}_{g,\mathbf{v}}\left(\mathbf{z}\right)-\boldsymbol{\theta}=\mathbf{0}
, for any given
\boldsymbol{\theta}
,
g
and
\mathbf{v}
.
Newton iteration methods, although computationally fast, are not guaranteed to converge.
Bisection methods are guaranteed to converge but computationally expensive.
In our algorithm, we used the trisection method
[
42
]
, which reduces the number of iterations compared to the bisection method.
Instead of finding
{S}_{g,v_{j}}^{-1}\left(\theta_{j}\right)
for each
j

we further improved the efficiency by implementing the trisection algorithm in a way such that

{S}_{g,v_{j}}^{-1}\left(\theta_{j}\right)
is updated for all
j
simultaneously during each update.
For a constant
v_{j}
(
v_{j}=v
for all
j

the function
{S}_{g,v}\left(\cdot\right)
is symmetric and monotonic –
allowing us to leverage the fast technique of switching variables
followed by spline interpolation (FSSI)
[
43
]
.

3.2
Compound Penalty

In this approach, we reparameterize the optimization problem in terms of variables

(so
\theta_{j}={S}_{g,v_{j}}(z_{j})
, which we write in vector form as
\boldsymbol{\theta}={S}_{g,\mathbf{v}}(\mathbf{z})
). The objective function, in terms of
\mathbf{z}
, becomes

\displaystyle\widetilde{h}(\mathbf{z},g,\sigma^{2})

\displaystyle\triangleq h({S}_{g,\mathbf{v}}(\mathbf{z}),g,\sigma^{2})

(25)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\sigma^{2}}{\left\lVert\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{X}{S}_{g,\mathbf{v}}(\mathbf{z})\right\rVert}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\rho({S}_{g,v_{j}}(z_{j}),g,v_{j}^{2})-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\log\left(d_{j}^{2}\right)+\frac{n-p}{2}\log\left(2\pi\sigma^{2}\right),

and we solve the optimization problem

\widehat{\mathbf{z}},\widehat{g},\widehat{\sigma}^{2}=\argmin_{\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{p},\,g\in\mathcal{G},\,\sigma^{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}\widetilde{h}(\mathbf{z},g,\sigma^{2})\,.

(26)

The reparametrization yields an analytically tractable objective function for many priors, because the “compound penalty function" (
16
) is analytically tractible for many prior families. Once
\widehat{\mathbf{z}}

is obtained we can obtain the solution to the original problem (
14
) by

\widehat{\mathbf{b}}={S}_{\widehat{g},\widehat{\mathbf{v}}}(\widehat{\mathbf{z}}).

(27)

The reparametrization to solve for
\mathbf{z}
instead of
\boldsymbol{\theta}

helps to us avoid the numerical inversion during each gradient descent iteration,
but the initialization of
\mathbf{z}
is less straightforward than
\boldsymbol{\theta}
from the previous approach, as discussed below.

3.3
Choice of prior

The methods discussed above is generally applicable for any choice of the prior distribution
g
.
However, the tractability of the objective functions (
15
,
25
)
and their gradients depend on the tractability of

\ell_{\NM}
and its derivative.
Our software GradVI is designed to obtain the prior family as an input module from the user.
For any prior family, one has to define the number of parameters that needs to be estimated,
a real bound for each of those parameters and a function for the gradient descent step for those parameters.
This allows flexibility to implement suitable priors, as compared to coordinate ascent methods.
As examples, we have implemented the following two priors:

•

Adaptive shrinkage (Ash) prior
uses a scale mixture of normals
[
28
]
.
Specifically

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}\left(\sigma_{1}^{2},\ldots,\sigma_{K}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\triangleq\left\{g(\cdot)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}\mathcal{N}\left(\cdot\mid 0,\sigma_{k}^{2}\right):w_{k}\in S\right\}\,,

(28)

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle\triangleq\left\{w_{k}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{K}:\sum_{k=1}^{K}w_{k}=1\right\}\,.

(29)

Here,
0\leq\sigma_{1}^{2}\leq\ldots\leq\sigma_{K}^{2}<\infty
is a
pre-specified grid of component variances, and
w_{1},\ldots,w_{K}
are
unknown mixture proportions, which are estimated from the data.
Typically, the first variance
\sigma_{1}^{2}
is set exactly to zero
to allow for a sparse regression model. In our numerical experiments we followed
[
29
]
in using
K=20
and
\sigma_{k}^{2}=(2^{{(k-1)}/K}-1)^{2}
.

•

Point-normal prior
, also known as “spike-and-slab” prior,
which is used in
[
12
]
for example.
It is defined as,

g\left(\cdot\right)=\left(1-w\right)\delta_{0}+w\mathcal{N}\left(\cdot\mid 0,\sigma_{1}^{2}\right)\,,

(30)

where
\delta_{0}
is the Dirac delta function with a point mass at zero.
Here,
w
and
\sigma_{1}
are unknown parameters, which are estimated from the data.

3.4
Initialization

Since the VEB optimization problem is non-convex, the solution obtained may depend on the initialization.
Both approaches (numerical inversion and compound approach) can be initialized by specifying initial values
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{init}},g_{\textrm{init}},\sigma^{2}_{\textrm{init}}
. These can be used to compute initial values
\mathbf{v}^{2}_{\textrm{init}}=\sigma^{2}_{\textrm{init}}\mathbf{d}
following the definition of
v_{j}
in (
15
).
For the compound approach one can then initialize
\mathbf{z}
by computing
\mathbf{z}_{\textrm{init}}={S}_{g_{\textrm{init}},\mathbf{v}_{\textrm{init}}}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{init}})
using numerical inversion.

Initial values
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{init}}
can be specified either by simply setting
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{init}}=0
or by using the estimated regression coefficients from another approach such as lasso. (As shown below in our numerical experiments, the null initialization is good enough
in many use cases, particularly for sparse problems.)
In results presented here we used
\sigma^{2}_{\textrm{init}}=1
and initialized mixture priors
g_{\textrm{init}}
by setting all components to have equal mixture proportions. For example, in the ash prior above we initialize
w_{k}=1/K
for all
k
.

In cases where
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{\textrm{init}}
is carefully chosen (
e.g
., from lasso) then it may be helpful to perform some initial iterations (of the numerical inversion approach) that update
g
with
\boldsymbol{\theta},\sigma^{2}
held fixed at their initial values. These are relatively low-dimensional updates that can be applied
to quickly improve the initial estimates of
g
.
This strategy is an option in our software, and was used in the numerical experiments below.

3.5
Software implementation

Our software GradVI implements the two strategies described above for solving the optimization problem. We used the L-BFGS-B algorithm
[
44
,
45
,
46
]
,
a popular quasi-Newton method for minimizing the two objective functions
defined in (
25
) and (
15
) respectively.
For large
p
problems the most expensive operation for the function and the derivative evaluations are matrix-vector multiplications of the form
\mathbf{X}\mathbf{r}
and
\mathbf{X}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{r}
for an arbitrary vector
\mathbf{r}
. Consequently, if
\mathbf{X}
has special structure that can be exploited to speed up these products then our algorithms can easily exploit this.
Our software is open-source and released under the MIT license.

We consider two sets of experiments: the first involves generic high-dimensional (

4.2
Bayesian Trend Filtering

Trend filtering is a method for finding an estimate
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
of
f(\mathbf{x})
in the
following non-parametric regression model:

\displaystyle y_{i}

\displaystyle=f\left(x_{i}\right)+\epsilon_{i},\quad i=1,\ldots,n,

(33)

\displaystyle\epsilon_{i}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\,\cdot\mid 0,\sigma^{2}\right)

where
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
are evenly spaced inputs over the interval
\left[0,1\right]
,
and
f:\left[0,1\right]\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is the underlying function to be estimated.

Specifically,
\ell_{1}
trend filtering,
which was introduced by Kim
et al
.
[
4
]
, is shown by
[
5
]

to be equivalent to the following Lasso problem:

\widehat{\mathbf{b}}=\argmin_{\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{1}{2}{\left\lVert\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{H}^{(k+1)}\mathbf{b}\right\rVert}_{2}^{2}+\lambda\sum_{j=k+2}^{n}\left|b_{j}\right|\,,

(34)

where the matrix
\mathbf{H}^{(k+1)}
depends on the order
k
of the trend filtering.
For
k=0
,

\mathbf{H}^{(1)}=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0&\ldots&0\\
1&1&0&\ldots&0\\
1&1&1&\ldots&0\\
\vdots&&&\ddots&\vdots\\
1&1&1&\ldots&1\end{bmatrix}.

(35)

In this special case trend filtering corresponds to assuming that
f
is piecewise constant, and the non-zero elements of
\mathbf{b}
determine the locations at which the function
f
changes values (“changepoints"). For general
k
the form of
\mathbf{H}^{(k)}
is given in
[
5
]
.
The matrix
\mathbf{H}^{(1)}
has special structure that allows
\mathbf{H}^{(1)}\mathbf{v}
to be computed efficiently
2
2

2

eg in R or numpy
\mathbf{H}^{(1)}v
is
cumsum
(
\mathbf{v}
)
, in
O(n)
operations. More generally, for moderate
k

\mathbf{H}^{(k)}
also allows fast matrix-vector multiplication (
\mathbf{H}^{(k)}\mathbf{v}
involves
O(kn+2(k-1)^{2})
operations in our implementation).

To summarize the above:
k
th order (
\ell_{1}
) trend-filtering is equivalent to fitting the multiple regression model (
1
) with an
\ell_{1}
penalty on
\mathbf{b}
and a predictor matrix
\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{H}^{(k+1)}
that admits fast matrix vector products.

This EB version of trend filtering has features that make our gradient-based GradVI approach far preferable to the coordinate-based CAVI algorithm used in

]
. First, and most important,
the matrix-vector products
\mathbf{H}\mathbf{b}
and

can be solved very efficiently, with linear complexity in the number of data points

, and without explicitly forming
\mathbf{H}

whereas GradVI has linear complexity in

are very highly colinear, a situation that makes coordinate-based optimization particularly challenging (see Figure

The locations of the changepoints, which were chosen uniformly at random.

The noise parameter

5
Discussion

In this study, we have presented a novel gradient-based optimization method
for variational inference (GradVI) in multiple linear regression.
GradVI complements the conventional coordinate ascent variational inference (CAVI), and can show superior performance in some settings, including when the variables are highly correlated,
or when the design matrix yields fast matrix-vector multiplications,

e.g
., trend filtering.

The main challenge in implementing our gradient-based approach is that the penalty function is not analytically tractible. We developed two variants of GradVI, Compound and Direct, which address this problem in different ways.
The optimal choice between these variants depends on the specific problem at hand.
Generally, we recommend using the compound penalty,
which is the default setting in our software,
due to the numerical challenges associated with the functional inverse required in the direct method.
However, in cases where the matrix of explanatory variables is well-conditioned and uniform,
the direct method may be more effective
(e.g. we have seen this in higher order,
k\geq 1
, trendfiltering problems,
which we have not extensively covered in this manuscript).
For situations where users are constrained to null initialization,
such as when cross-validation is computationally prohibitive,
GradVI may be preferable to standard CAVI.

The recent trend towards utilizing gradient-based optimization techniques
in variational inference aims to achieve efficient and scalable posterior inference
in advanced Bayesian computation.
While low-dimensional illustrations often motivate the choice of approximating family and divergence,
these intuitions do not necessarily extend to high-dimensional settings.
For example, stochastic variational inference
[
47
]

assumes that the prior distribution belongs to the exponential family,
which is suboptimal for high-dimensional scenarios
that require efficient sparsity-inducing priors (
e.g
., slab-and-spike prior, adaptive shrinkage prior).
Methods like black-box variational inference
[
48
]

and automatic differentiation variational inference
[
49
]

sometimes fail to converge due to the high variance of nested gradient estimates.
Moreover, optimizing the KL divergence in these methods necessitates updating all variational parameters,
which becomes unmanageable as dimensionality increases.

GradVI addresses this issue by optimizing the mean of the approximate variational posterior,
thereby reducing the number of parameters to be optimized and enhancing computational scalability.
For example, GradVI enables trend filtering for datasets with
n=10^{6}

on a single core with 100 GB of memory, a task where both genlasso cross-validation
and CAVI methods exhaust available memory.
Our software leverages the L-BFGS-B method for numerical optimization,
though Newton’s method could further accelerate computations
if the Hessian for the penalty function is implemented.
While we have performed some initial experiments that confirm the potential speed benefits of Newton’s method,
it is not yet integrated into our software.

Another significant challenge in variational inference methods
for sparse multiple linear regression is their sensitivity to initialization,
particularly with correlated variables.
Therefore, in most cases, variational inference methods
are initialized from Lasso results.
In large datasets, where it becomes computationally challenging to run Lasso due to the cross-validation,
GradVI offers a notable advantage over CAVI in terms of accuracy,
as it is less sensitive to initialization than CAVI.

A significant advantage of GradVI is its simplicity and flexibility in handling different prior distributions.
The CAVI updates for different prior distributions must be derived and implemented separately,
which involves substantial effort in terms of derivation, programming, debugging, and scaling.
This complexity often prevents full utilization of the benefits associated
with exploring different prior distributions for any regression problem.
In contrast, implementing any prior distribution with GradVI requires solving
a relatively simpler Normal Means problem, making it a more practical choice for practitioners.

GradVI is a robust and scalable alternative for high-dimensional linear regression
and Bayesian trend filtering.
The promise of GradVI extends beyond the scope of this study.
Future work will focus on integrating Newton’s method for further speed improvements
and exploring its application in other complex Bayesian models.
Additionally, we plan to incorporate automatic differentiation tools
to further simplify the implementation of different priors.
We are excited about the potential of GradVI to make advanced Bayesian methods
more accessible and efficient for a wide range of applications.
