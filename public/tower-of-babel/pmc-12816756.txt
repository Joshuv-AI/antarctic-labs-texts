Scenario-adaptive hierarchical optimisation framework for design in hybrid energy storage systems
Authors: Jiacheng Guo, Hao Wu, Tao Ma, Rongxin Yin, Yuekuan Zhou, Ji Li, Jinyue Yan, Jinqing Peng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
As renewable energy penetration increases, mismatches between generation and demand lead to underutilization of clean energy, particularly in industrial parks with overlapping of building and process loads. To enhance system flexibility and renewable utilization, hybrid energy storage systems integrating electrical, thermal, and cooling storage technologies offer a promising solution. However, a scalable and generalizable design framework for such systems remains lacking. Here, we propose a general and scenario-adaptive design framework for hybrid energy storage systems. The framework encompasses five core stages: demand analysis, energy storage selection, energy system modeling, optimization design, and performance evaluation. We develop a hierarchical optimization method to jointly optimize equipment configuration and operation scheduling through iterative feedback between the two layers, achieving better scalability and robustness than existing collaborative approaches. The proposed framework is systematically evaluated across industrial parks spanning different climate zones and energy demand levels. Results show that the proposed framework significantly enhances energy cost savings (43.7%) and reduces carbon emissions (69.9%). This work provides a practical and transferable pathway for deploying hybrid energy storage systems in carbon-intensive sectors, thereby facilitating the low-carbon transition of industrial sectors.

Introduction

Achieving deep decarbonization of the global energy system requires not only increasing the penetration of renewable energy sources1, but also addressing the structural mismatches between intermittent renewable generation2 and the diverse and inflexible profiles of end-use energy demand3. Industrial parks, as a crucial part of both the building and industrial sectors, experience a dual accumulation of carbon emissions from both building operations and industrial production, which makes them crucial to the global energy transition and carbon neutrality efforts. Their coexisting and often overlapping energy loads, spanning electricity, heating, and cooling, are characterized by high intensity, limited flexibility, and temporal rigidity4. These features make industrial parks not only major energy consumers, but also representative testbeds for the broader challenges facing energy system transformation5.

In response to the pronounced supply–demand imbalances of industrial parks, recent studies have explored a variety of strategies, including supply-side regulation, intelligent control, demand-side management, and the energy storage deployment6,7. Among these options, deployment of energy storage has emerged as a pivotal technology, offering strong capabilities for peak shaving and power fluctuation mitigation8. Its integration is increasingly recognized as a critical enabler for the development of resilient and low-carbon energy systems for industrial parks. However, given the diverse, rigid, and time-varying nature of energy demand in industrial parks, no single storage technology can fully satisfy operational requirements under complex conditions (Fig. 1a). Hybrid energy storage systems (HESSs) integrate electrical, thermal, and cooling storage to exploit the complementary strengths of different storage technologies9. Building on this trend, recent research on HESSs for industrial parks has advanced steadily, with a primary focus on design frameworks, system modeling, and optimization methods12,13. The design frameworks supporting the planning and design of HESSs are mainly based on planning software or case-specific design procedures. Existing energy system planning tools, such as HOMER14 and RETScreen15, provide some support for HESS planning but focus mainly on electrical storage, while overlooking the integration of electrical, thermal, and cooling storage. This limits their suitability for industrial parks with integrated thermal and cooling storages12. Some studies have proposed design procedures for HESS tailored to specific cases, which cover steps such as system construction, optimization, and performance evaluation16,17. However, these approaches generally lack conceptual clarity and structural scalability, limiting their applicability for cross-scenario deployment in diverse types of industrial parks. In modeling studies of HESSs, some research works have considered combinations of electrical storage technologies such as lithium-ion batteries (Li-ion battery) and supercapacitor energy storages (SCESs)8,18. However, in such studies, thermal and cooling storages are still rarely integrated, which makes it difficult to capture the complementary strengths of electricity, thermal, and cooling coordination in a unified way19,20. Another research priority focuses on coordinated modeling of electrical, thermal, and cooling storage, yet power-type storages such as SCESs are often insufficiently integrated, limiting the system’s ability to respond quickly and adapt flexibly16,21,22. More recently, several studies have sought to incorporate all these storage technologies into unified modeling frameworks, but these are often based on specific system configurations or scenarios, restricting their generality and scalability21,23. Recent advances in system optimization methods have moved from coordinated to hierarchical optimization4,24,25. While coordinated optimization aims for joint optimization of configuration and operation to achieve theoretical global optima26, in industrial parks where energy systems are highly coupled across multiple energy forms and structurally complex, this approach often faces challenges such as complicated model formulation, high computational costs, and difficulty in achieving genuine coordination between configuration and operation (Supplementary Table 1)6. By contrast, hierarchical optimization decouples the configuration and operation stages, and defines distinct objectives, decision variables, and constraints for each27. This approach offers scalable and easy-to-implement solutions, which improves computational efficiency and better aligns with the optimization needs of HESSs.

Despite progress in system design, modeling, and optimization methods, current research on HESSs for industrial parks still exhibits several gaps. (1) In terms of design frameworks, some studies have explored tools and frameworks for HESS planning and design, such as software platforms or case-specific designs, but most lack a generalizable framework that can be applied across different climate zones and energy consumption levels. (2) In the context of storage modeling, most existing studies focus either on hybrid electric storage systems or on energy-type hybrid storage systems, yet few offer a unified model that integrates power-type and energy-type electric storage with multiple thermal storage technologies. (3) In terms of optimization methods, conventional coordinated optimization approaches are often tightly coupled and computationally intensive. While hierarchical optimization offers better scalability, most existing methods are designed for specific applications, which restricts their application to diverse operational contexts. These limitations not only hinder the large-scale application of HESSs for industrial parks but also constrain their role in facilitating low-carbon energy transitions. To address these challenges, this study proposes a general design framework for HESS with a universal structure, adaptable to different scenarios, and capable of cross-regional deployment.

Within this framework, the study focuses on HESS for industrial parks and develops an energy system model that integrates distributed photovoltaics (PV), the utility grid, multiple types of storage devices (such as Li-ion batteries, SCESs, chilled water storages (CWSs), and high-temperature heating storage tank (HT-HST)), electricity–heat/cooling conversion units (such as electric boiler systems (EBSs), heat pump (HP), and chillers), as well as building and industrial loads (Fig. 1c). The system covers electricity, thermal, and cooling energy forms and accounts for bidirectional interaction with the utility grid. Based on this architecture, a scenario-adaptive design framework is proposed to select appropriate storage technologies according to the characteristics of energy sources and demand of industrial parks, and to determine the optimal capacity configurations using source and demand data from a typical meteorological year (TMY). The study does not address life-cycle assessment or real-time operational optimization; instead, it focuses on optimization design across different climatic and load profiles, with the aim of providing methodological support and quantitative evidence for the rational design and cross-regional deployment of HESSs.

Based on the above system architecture and research objectives, the main contributions of this study are as follows. First, a generalizable and adaptive design framework of HESSs for industrial parks, comprising five components, viz., demand characterization, storage technology selection, multi-domain system modeling, optimization design, and performance evaluation, is proposed to guide system planning. This framework supports the integration of diverse storage technologies, cross-energy coupling, and scalable deployment across heterogeneous scenarios. Second, a unified modeling approach is developed to cover power-type and energy-type electric storage, as well as various thermal and cooling storage units, which supports accurate representation of heterogeneous storage behaviors in complex industrial park energy systems. Finally, a hierarchical optimization method is proposed to decouple capacity planning from operational scheduling, which in turn reduces model complexity, enhances computational efficiency, and addresses the scalability and coupling challenges inherent in conventional coordination optimization methods.

Unlike previous design frameworks, such as general-purpose planning tools (e.g., HOMER14 and RETScreen15) that mainly address electrical storage while neglecting thermal coupling, and case-specific HESS design procedures that lack conceptual generality and scalability, the proposed framework overcomes these limitations. It is technology-inclusive and scenario-flexible, which enables broad applicability across diverse industrial park conditions.

Existing collaborative methods mainly fall into two categories. One combines heuristic algorithms (e.g., non-dominated sorting genetic algorithm II (NSGA-II)) with rule-based scheduling, which is fast but often leads to over-allocation of costly devices due to static control logic. The other uses mathematical programming (e.g., Gurobi34 with YALMIP35) for joint optimization, offering better accuracy but suffering from high dimensionality, long runtimes, and limited scalability. In addition, rule-based scheduling lacks adaptive adjustment to real-time supply–demand fluctuations, which reduces the effectiveness of some storage units and further amplifies the risk of over-investing in high-cost technologies. These limitations reduce operational adaptability and economic efficiency. In contrast, hierarchical optimization separates sizing from scheduling. The configuration layer first generates candidate configurations, and the scheduling layer then simulates full-cycle operations under representative annual conditions. Key performance indicators, including utilization rates, operating frequencies, and economic contributions, are fed back to the sizing layer for iterative updates. If a device shows low utilization during scheduling, its capacity is reduced in the subsequent iterations, and as a result, redundant configurations are gradually eliminated. This mechanism avoids excessive investment in costly equipment and enables the final system design to achieve a balance between economic efficiency and carbon reduction benefits.

Figure 4d–g compares deployment capacities between the two methods across four cases, and the results highlight the advantages of hierarchical optimization. When compared to collaborative optimization, hierarchical optimization reduces the redundant allocation of high-cost equipment, such as Li-ion batteries, while increasing the utilization ratio of low-cost equipment, such as HT-HST. For example, Li-ion battery deployment is reduced by 15.1–44.2 MWh, while HT-HST capacity increases by 145.8–250.6%. The deployment of SCES is generally increased under hierarchical optimization, particularly in regions with significant load fluctuations, where the system can fully utilize its rapid response capability. In contrast, CWS deployment decreased in the cases with lower cooling load demand (e.g., Cases 1 and 3), with reductions of 17.2 MWh (61.3%) and 38.4 MWh (84.0%), respectively.

Figure 4h–k compares the system benefits of the two methods. Hierarchical optimization consistently outperforms collaborative optimization in PESR, energy cost savings rate (ECSR), and carbon emission reduction rate (CERR). For example, the PESR in Case 2 increases from 54.9 to 63.2% by using collaborative optimization, which represents a growth of 8.3%. In Guangzhou (Case 2), the ECSR increases from 16.9 to 43.7%, a growth of over 25%. Similarly, in Beijing (Case 1), the ECSR rises from 16.0 to 39.7%, reflecting an increase of 23.7%. In Case 2 and Case 4, the CERR increases from 55.6 to 50.5% under collaborative optimization to 66.7% and 61.9% with the hierarchical optimization, which corresponds to growth rates of 11.1% and 11.4%, respectively. This highlights the superior performance of hierarchical optimization in enhancing the low-carbon benefits of HESSs. These results confirm that hierarchical optimization achieves greater cost savings, higher energy efficiency, and stronger carbon reduction benefits.

Energy storage configuration of HESSs in diverse scenarios

This section explores how climate characteristics and energy consumption levels influence the energy storage configuration of HESSs in different industrial parks, as summarized in Fig. 3.

Figure 3a–d shows the capacity distributions of SCES, Li-ion battery, HT-HST, and CWS. The capacity of SCESs is higher in the parks with abundant solar resources. For example, the median SCES capacity for the high-energy industrial park (Case 1) in Beijing is 1401 kWh, surpassing Harbin (1247 kWh) and Shanghai (1256 kWh). The distribution of Li-ion battery capacities is relatively uniform across cases, with medians ranging from 103.2 to 106.4 MWh, which suggests that Li-ion batteries reliably meet short-term (2–5 h) demands. Deployed capacity of HT-HSTs is strongly influenced by high-temperature heating demand and solar resources. High-energy-consumption industrial parks exhibit notable annual high-temperature heating load demands, with the median capacity reaching up to 147.0 MWh in Beijing, which is the highest among all cases.

A well-designed HESS enhances renewable integration, reduces operating costs, and maximizes emissions mitigation. We further analyze the impacts of climate and energy demand on optimal configurations and benefits, which is conducted based on the technique for order preference by similarity to an ideal solution (TOPSIS) method decisions36,37 (Fig. 3). Figure 3h–m illustrates the deployment capacities of the HESS across different scenarios. HT-HSTs play a critical role in high-energy-consumption industrial parks. For example, both Guangzhou (Case 2, 212.6 MWh) and Shanghai (Case 4, 211.7 MWh) deploy over 210 MWh. For industrial parks in Beijing, the deployment of HT-HSTs reaches 187.7 MWh in high-energy-consumption industrial parks (Case 1), while medium-energy-consumption (Case 5) and low-energy-consumption industrial parks (Case 6) do not deploy HT-HSTs. LT-HSTs are primarily used to meet medium- and low-temperature heating demands, such as heating and domestic hot water. In severe cold climate regions like Harbin (Case 3), the deployment of LT-HSTs is the highest at 74.3 MWh, followed by Beijing (Case 1) at 39.5 MWh. In warmer climates such as Guangzhou (Case 2) and Shanghai (Case 4), deployment is close to zero, reflecting the limited medium- and low-temperature heating load demand in these regions. Li-ion battery deployment varies across climate zones and energy consumption levels. Harbin (Case 3) has the highest deployment at 104.2 MWh, followed by Beijing (Case 1) at 81.0 MWh and Guangzhou (Case 2) at 64.8 MWh.

The energy storage deployment capacities and system benefit distributions of the HESS vary across cases, which reflects the climate and demand-specific characteristics. For instance, Guangzhou (Case 2), with high cooling loads, shows substantially larger capacities of CWS. By contrast, Beijing (Case 1) and Harbin (Case 3), which have strong thermal demands, rely more on HT-HST. In high-energy-consumption industrial parks (Case 1), Li-ion batteries and SCESs account for larger shares of the total capacity, as they are critical for balancing frequent and large-scale power fluctuations. In low-energy-consumption parks (Case 6), configurations are more dispersed, which demonstrates the framework’s flexibility under lighter load structures. These specific patterns confirm that regional variations and load intensities impact the composition of energy storage configurations.

System-level benefits of HESS under climate and demand variations

The system-level benefits of HESSs under varying climate and demand conditions are analyzed across different industrial parks, as shown in Fig. 3.

Figure 3e–g analyzes the distribution of annual system performance indicators across six scenarios (Cases 1–6). Guangzhou (Case 2) achieved a median ECSR of 41.8%, higher than those in Beijing (Case 1, 37.2%) and Harbin (Case 3, 35.7%). High-energy-consumption industrial parks (Case 1) demonstrated the best cost savings rate (median: 37.2%), while the low-energy-consumption parks (Case 6) showed limited savings (22.6%). The median and maximum primary energy savings rate (PESR) in Guangzhou (Case 2) are 62.9% and 64.7%, respectively, which surpass those of Beijing (Case 1, 60.0%), Harbin (Case 3, 59.9%), and Shanghai (Case 4, 57.1%). The distribution trend of CERRs aligns with that of PESRs.

Figure 3n–p presents the annual performance values for the six cases. The benefits of HESSs are influenced by both climate zones and energy consumption levels. In terms of climate zones, Guangzhou (Case 2) achieves the best performance, with an ECSR of 43.7%, PESR of 63.2%, and CERR of 66.7%. Low-energy parks (Case 6) exhibit limited cost savings (ECSR: 25.2%) but comparable emissions reductions (CERR: 66.9%), demonstrating that carbon benefits can remain high despite lower energy intensities.

System-level coordination is the primary driver of HESS performance. When compared with conventional supply systems, which operate heating, cooling, and electricity separately, the HESS integrates multi-energy scheduling and enables synergistic operation. For example, in the high-energy-consumption industrial park in Beijing (Case 1), the system achieved median reductions of 37.2% in energy costs and 62.1% in carbon emissions, respectively. This confirms that HESSs function as integrated solutions rather than simple aggregations of storage units. Moreover, integrated HESS deployment improves ECSR, PESR, and CERR. The underlying reason lies in the complementarity and coordination of power-type and energy-type storage technologies. SCESs, Li-ion batteries, HT-HST, LT-HST, and CWS take on distinct roles under different conditions, ranging from short-term power regulation to daily peak shaving and cooling or heating supply, thereby enabling a joint scheduling mechanism that spans multiple energy forms. Overall, HESSs consistently deliver ECSRs of 25.2–43.7% and CERRs of 61.9–66.9%. These findings demonstrate that the systematic design of HESSs can provide significant economic and environmental benefits under varying climate and load conditions, highlighting their broad applicability and effectiveness.

The scheduling results for the four typical days reveal a clear functional division and coordination among storage technologies. The SCES, as a power-type storage, ensures short-term balancing and system stability, while the Li-ion battery and HT-HST, as energy-type storages, provide intra-day peak shaving and reduce both costs and emissions. This consistent performance across climates underscores the generalizability of the proposed hierarchical optimization strategy. The equivalent circuit of SCESs is mainly composed of a low equivalent series resistance and a capacitance (C). Its time constant (τ = RC) is very small, which gives it a millisecond-level response speed and a wide bandwidth38. SCESs also support ultra-high charge/discharge rates (tens of C-rate) and maintain a long cycle life (105–106) with negligible degradation cost, which makes them ideal for second-to-minute-scale fluctuation control and frequent cycling. By contrast, Li-ion batteries are constrained by electrochemical diffusion and interfacial kinetics, which lead to higher internal resistance. They are highly sensitive to high C-rate operation and steep ramping, and frequent cycling accelerates their degradation. As a result, they are more suitable for hour-level energy shifting rather than rapid power balancing. Thermal and cooling energy storages, with large capacities and slower dynamics (minute-to-hour response), are optimal for long-duration energy balancing and peak management but unsuitable for fast electrical disturbances.

Discussion

HESSs can effectively alleviate supply–demand mismatches and reduce operational and maintenance costs as well as carbon emissions, and as a result, they are playing an increasingly important role in industrial parks. However, the existing design frameworks and optimization methods often fall short in meeting the design and optimization requirements of HESSs, thereby limiting their effectiveness, scalability, and broader applicability in industrial parks. These limitations highlight the urgent need for a general, scenario-adaptive design framework tailored to the specific characteristics of HESSs. This study proposed a unified framework integrating demand analysis, energy storage selection, system modeling, optimization design, and performance evaluation. The feasibility and effectiveness of the proposed framework were systematically evaluated across industrial parks with diverse climate zones and energy consumption levels. This study bridges the gap between design theory and practical application, facilitating the large-scale deployment of HESSs in complex scenarios. The proposed general design framework is applicable to complex industrial park scenarios across diverse climatic conditions and energy consumption levels, supporting efficient renewable energy utilization and deep decarbonization. The insights from this work also offer references for broader applications involving renewable integration and sustainable energy transitions. Although this study proposed a general and scenario-adaptive design framework for HESSs of industrial parks, it also has several limitations: (1) the system modeling adopts idealized assumptions regarding the coupling among different storage technologies, and it fails to fully account for uncertainties and dynamic interactions in practical operations; (2) the research focuses on the design of HESSs, and it does not consider the day-ahead, intra-day, and real-time multi-stage optimization scheduling, which may affect the assessment of short-term operational performance; (3) all evaluations are based on simulation results, and it lacks experimental validation or field testing in real industrial park scenarios, which limits the assessment of practical applicability. Therefore, future work will focus on: (1) the investigation of the coupling characteristics among different energy storage technologies, which will include full consideration of variations in response time, power and energy density, and technology maturity, to more accurately represent their behavior under real-world operating conditions; (2) extending the current design framework to encompass the operation phase, with a focus on intelligent scheduling strategies, demand-side response, and operational and maintenance cost control, thereby enabling life-cycle performance evaluation for HESSs; (3) establishing a small-scale experimental platform and a semi-experimental simulation platform to improve its practical applicability and engineering robustness.

Configuration of HESSs for industrial parks

HESSs for industrial parks integrate various types of equipment, such as energy storage and renewable energy systems, to efficiently meet the demands of electricity, heating, and cooling (Fig. 1c). The system consists of various modules, including PV generation, HESS, and renewable energy utilization. Distributed PV generation serves as the primary source of clean energy, which reduces reliance on conventional energy sources. The utility grid acts as a supplementary energy source, ensuring a reliable energy supply during peak loads or extreme weather conditions. The electricity generated by PVs can be converted into high-temperature thermal, medium/low-temperature thermal, and cooling energy by an EBS, HP, and chiller, respectively.

For the energy storage segment, Li-ion battery serves as the primary equipment for electrical energy storage, addressing the mismatch between energy supply and user demand in industrial parks. SCES, with their rapid response capabilities, handle short-term power balancing and fluctuation regulation. HT-HST and LT-HST are employed to meet the thermal requirements of industrial parks, particularly for heat-intensive industrial processes. CWS is utilized to address cooling demands, enhancing the overall energy efficiency of the system. The reference system is defined as the existing energy infrastructure of industrial parks, which includes the gas boiler, the EBS, the chiller, and the utility grid, without any energy storage or renewable generation units (see Supplementary Note 2).

The mathematical modeling of equipment serves as the theoretical foundation for the optimization and analysis of HESSs. (1)–(59), to maintain the clarity and coherence of the main text.

General design framework of HESS

The reasonable design of HESSs requires a general design framework as a foundation. This section proposed a general design framework (Fig. 2), providing theoretical support for the optimized design of HESSs, which encompasses steps such as demand analysis, energy storage selection, system modeling, optimization design, and performance evaluation. The framework establishes a solid theoretical foundation for the optimization design of HESSs.

Demand analysis

The primary application scenarios of HESSs, including renewable energy integration, utility grid peak shaving, and backup power supply, are analyzed. The types of loads (e.g., electricity, thermal, cooling) and their characteristic parameters (e.g., duration, peak demand) that the HESS must accommodate are identified. The energy supply methods of industrial parks (e.g., PV power, utility grid) are clarified, and the inherent volatility and randomness of renewable energy are fully considered.

Selection of energy storage technologies

The principles, characteristics, advantages, and limits of various energy storage methods are analyzed. Suitable energy storage technologies are selected after the application scenarios, and the energy demands of industrial parks are evaluated. The synergistic benefits among different energy storage methods are evaluated to enhance system performance. Comprehensive considerations, such as economic costs, safety, cycle efficiency, and regulatory compliance, are considered to determine the most suitable energy storage methods.

System modeling

Mathematical models that accurately reflect the operational characteristics of each energy storage method are established, and these models incorporate constraints such as cycle efficiency and power/capacity restrictions. A HESS model is developed, considering the synergistic benefits of electricity, heating, cooling, and gas, as well as the coupling of different energy storage methods, based on these models. Control strategies for the HESS are formulated to meet requirements such as active energy storage and utility grid peak shaving. The operating characteristics and overall benefits of HESSs are simulated and analyzed.

Optimization design

The integration method between the HESS and the park’s energy supply system is determined. An optimization design model is developed based on the demand analysis of the industrial park, which incorporates decision variables, objective functions, operational constraints, and control strategies. To address the multi-dimensional complexity of system design and ensure computational tractability, two optimization methods are considered, viz., hierarchical optimization and collaborative optimization, each applicable to different design requirements. In the hierarchical optimization method, capacity planning and operational scheduling are decoupled. The planning layer focuses on sizing HESS components, while the operational layer optimizes dispatch under fixed capacities. These two layers are solved separately, typically using heuristic algorithms for planning and mathematical programming for operation, allowing flexible system configuration and dispatch strategies. In contrast, the collaborative optimization method integrates planning and dispatch into a unified model, enabling co-optimization of design and operation.

Performance evaluation

A comprehensive evaluation framework is established for the HESS. This framework assesses the system’s performance in terms of reliability, carbon reduction, and flexible potential, and provides a holistic evaluation of its capabilities.

Hierarchical optimization method

This study introduced a hierarchical optimization method to address the complex energy management requirements of HESSs (Fig. 4b and Supplementary Fig. 2). The method consists of two parts, viz., the upper-layer capacity optimization and the lower-layer daily operational scheduling, while the detailed optimization procedure is described in Supplementary Note 3. The capacity configuration, which is determined at the configuration layer, serves as input to the scheduling optimization of the operational layer, which in turn refines the configuration through feedback from daily operation. By integrating capacity planning and operational scheduling, the method provides a systematic solution to achieve economic efficiency, carbon reduction, and operational flexibility, and maintains generality and adaptability across diverse industrial park scenarios.

Nevertheless, hierarchical optimization still faces challenges in computational complexity. The sizing layer has a broad search space but a relatively short time horizon, while the scheduling layer must address year-round operation with 105120 intervals at a 5-min resolution. This scheduling process involves device coupling as well as mixed-integer and nonlinear constraints, which significantly increase the computational burden and make it the most resource-intensive part of the framework. The iterative feedback between the two layers further increases the overall complexity. To address this, two simplifications were introduced: (i) replacing year-round scheduling with rolling daily optimization, which reduces each run to 288 intervals, and (ii) linearizing device models where possible to reduce variables and constraints.

Looking forward, computational efficiency can be further improved. One direction is the use of clustering methods such as k-means to condense TMY load and weather data into a limited number of representative days, which in turn reduces computational requirements while preserving system dynamics. Another is the development of design software based on this framework, which would allow users to input energy consumption and weather data for a given industrial park and quickly obtain the optimized system configurations.

To implement this approach, a detailed optimization model is formulated with clearly defined decision variables, objective functions, and operational constraints, along with the solution methods and the optimization procedure.

Decision variables at the configuration layer

At the configuration layer, the decision variables are the deployment capacities of energy storage equipment, such as SCES, Li-ion batteries, HT-HSTs, LT-HSTs, and CWSs. These variables are optimized to determine the appropriate scale of each energy storage technology. Detailed descriptions are provided in Supplementary Note 3: Decision Variables.

Decision variables at the operational layer

At the operational layer, the real-time output power of each energy storage equipment is treated as the decision variable. This includes the charging and discharging power of energy storage equipment, such as SCES, Li-ion batteries, HT-HSTs, and CWSs. Detailed descriptions are provided in Supplementary Note 3: Decision variables.

In addition to the optimization objectives, the hierarchical optimization incorporates constraints at both the configuration and operation layers to ensure feasibility and stability. Configuration layer constraints (long-term, static constraints): The configuration layer addresses the capacity design of the HESS, with constraints ensuring technology selection, investment feasibility, and long-term stability. The rated capacity of each storage unit, SCES, Li-ion battery, HT-HST, LT-HST, and CWS, is kept within a feasible range to balance supply reliability and cost-efficiency (Supplementary Eq. (80)). Charging and discharging power is matched to device-specific characteristics, such as allowable charge–discharge rates for electrical storage and heat transfer limits for thermal storage, ensuring operational safety (Supplementary Eq. (81)). Lifetime and cycling limits are imposed to prevent excessive use and maintain economic performance over the service life (Supplementary Eq. (82)). An annual energy balance constraint ensures the system meets electricity, heating, and cooling demands with a reserve margin, enabling reliable multi-energy supply under load fluctuations and renewable generation uncertainties (Supplementary Eqs. (83)–(86)). Detailed mathematical formulations are provided in Supplementary Note 3: Constraints.

Operation-layer constraints (short-term, dynamic)

The operation layer focuses on real-time dispatch of the HESS at 5-min interval. The SOC of each storage unit is maintained within a specified range to avoid overcharging or over-discharging and to preserve scheduling flexibility (Supplementary Eqs. (10), (16), (30), (38), and (49)). Charging and discharging power and ramp rates must comply with device-specific technical characteristics (Supplementary Eqs. (2), (3), (8), (13), (14), (14), (24), (25), (28), (32), (33), (36), (43), (44), (47), and (87)–(91)). Efficiency and self-loss constraints account for device efficiencies and self-losses, preventing cumulative deviations in long-term operation (Supplementary Eqs. (1), (5), (12), (23), (31), (39), (42), (50), and (53)–(59)). For conversion devices coupling different energy forms, the input and output power must satisfy specified ratios and meet the corresponding heating or cooling demands (Supplementary Eqs. (56)–(59)). At the system level, the total supply of electricity, heating, and cooling in each scheduling interval is balanced with the corresponding demand (Supplementary Eqs. (83)–(86)). The implementation of the hierarchical optimization method consists of four main steps, viz., optimization initialization and parameter settings, capacity optimization at the configuration layer, daily scheduling optimization at the operation layer, and iteration and decision-making (Supplementary Fig. 2). A detailed description of the optimization workflow is provided as follows.

Parameter settings and optimization initialization

NSGA-II parameters (e.g., population size and generations) are initialized, along with feasible capacity ranges for each storage technology. Inputs include TMY meteorological data, load profiles of electricity, heating, and cooling, market parameters (electricity prices, carbon emission factors), and technical specifications of devices.

Capacity optimization (configuration layer)

The decision variables are the installed capacities of various energy storage technologies. The NSGA-II begins by generating a set of candidate capacity configurations, which are then passed to the scheduling layer. The scheduling layer conducts daily optimization under TMY operating conditions with a 5-min time step, considering electricity, heating, and cooling demands. The results are aggregated to generate the annual operational data for each configuration. Based on the operational results returned from the scheduling layer, the configuration layer calculates key performance indicators such as ECSR and CERR, which incorporate system initial investment costs, market electricity prices, and carbon emission factors. These indicators are used as fitness values in the NSGA-II algorithm to evaluate and update the candidate capacity configurations.

Daily scheduling optimization (operation layer)

A mixed-integer linear programming (MILP) model is formulated in YALMIP and solved with Gurobi. Scheduling is performed for each day of a TMY, and the system states are updated at 5-min intervals. Net load (PV generation minus the park’s electrical demand) is decomposed using variational mode decomposition (VMD)41 into high- and low-frequency components. High-frequency fluctuations are charged or discharged by the SCES, while low-frequency variations are balanced by Li-ion batteries and thermal storage units. Any residual shortfall is met by the utility grid, while any surplus is fed into it. The decision variables in the MILP model are the charging and discharging power of each energy storage device at each step, subject to constraints including energy balance, device power limits, and SOC bounds. The optimization aims to minimize daily energy cost and reduce carbon emissions. The resulting schedules are used to update the initial SOC for the following day and to provide the configuration layer with accurate operational data.

Iteration and decision-making

After evaluating all candidate capacity configurations, the outer-layer NSGA-II performs non-dominated sorting and crowding distance calculation based on their fitness values (ECSR and CERR). This process is repeated until the maximum number of iterations is reached or the convergence criteria are met. The resulting Pareto-optimal set typically contains multiple solutions that reflect different trade-offs between the objectives. To select a single preferred configuration, the TOPSIS method36 is applied. By computing the relative distance of each solution to the ideal and negative-ideal solutions, TOPSIS incorporates the assigned weights of economic and carbon-reduction objectives to identify the configuration that best matches decision-making preferences.

Collaborative optimization method

This study adopts the collaborative optimization method33 (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 3) as a reference, and the detailed optimization process is provided in Supplementary Note 4. The optimization process includes the following steps:

Parameter initialization and population generation: the control parameters of NSGA-II are first defined, including population size, maximum number of iterations, and crossover and mutation probabilities. Based on the decision variables, the capacity configuration of each energy storage technology, an initial population of feasible configurations is randomly generated within the allowable ranges, where each individual represents a candidate scheme. The fitness function is then evaluated for all individuals in the initial population, and this evaluation provides the baseline for subsequent evolutionary iterations.

Fitness function evaluation: fitness is determined by simulating each configuration under a rule-based operational strategy (see Supplementary Note 4: Rule-based operation strategies). Daily net power is decomposed via VMD, and the response of each storage technology is assessed according to the predefined allocation rules. At each 5-min interval, the power output and SOC of every storage unit are updated. This process runs over 288-time steps per day and is repeated for 365 days to cover a full representative year. The final outputs of this step are the ECSR and CERR values associated with each individual.

Population update: the tournament selection method is used to identify high-performing individuals from the current population. Offspring are generated via simulated binary crossover and polynomial mutation, and they are then combined with the parents. Non-dominated sorting and crowding distance calculation preserve diversity and retain elite solutions. The next generation is then formed for the next iteration.

Convergence and solution selection: iterations continue until the maximum number is reached or convergence occurs, and this process produces a Pareto-optimal front for ECSR and CERR. The TOPSIS method ranks the solutions based on weighted preferences and identifies the configuration and strategy with the best performance.
