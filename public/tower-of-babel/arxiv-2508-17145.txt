Alternative statistical inference for the first normalized incomplete moment
Jiannan Lu, Peng Ding and Anqi Zhao

Abstract
This paper re-examines the first normalized incomplete moment, a well-established measure of inequality with wide applications in economic and social sciences. Despite the popularity of the measure itself, existing statistical inference appears to lag behind the needs of modern-age analytics. To fill this gap, we propose an alternative solution that is intuitive, computationally efficient, mathematically equivalent to the existing solutions for “standard” cases, and easily adaptable to “non-standard” ones. The theoretical and practical advantages of the proposed methodology are demonstrated via both simulated and real-life examples. In particular, we discover that a common practice in industry can lead to highly non-trivial challenges for trustworthy statistical inference, or misleading decision making altogether.



Introduction

1.1
Background

The past decade witnessed a surge in attention on the equality aspect of artificial intelligence, from recommendation system
(
Speicher et al., 2018
)
to machine learning model performance benchmarking
(
Lazovich et al., 2022
)
, to A/B testing
(
Niculescu et al., 2021
)
. For example,
Polonioli et al., 2023
stressed the need to account for all individuals in ML model evaluations, while
Lum et al., 2022
pointed out the lack of rigorous measures (and the uncertainty quantifications thereof) for AI systems.

From the economic and social science literature on income inequality
(
Champernowne and Cowell, 1998
)
, one common measure is the share of income by the
p
th percentile
(
Butler and McDonald, 1989
)
, which finds wide applications in economics and beyond, is free of arbitrary utility functions or “tuning parameters” (e.g.,
\epsilon
in Atkinson’s Index), and is among the most intuitive and actionable in the context of data mining. As an example,
Xie and Aurisset, 2016
investigated the correlation between streaming hours and user retention at Netflix, and concluded that “users with low hours are more likely to be on the fence of cancellation, and more sensitive to product changes.” Consequently, a measure of inequality can be share of streaming hours from the bottom 10% active users.

Mathematically, let
X\in(0,\infty)
be a positive-valued random variable with continuous probability density function
f(x)
and cumulative distribution function

F(x).

In addition, we let

\mu

and

\sigma^{2}

denote the mean and variance of
X.
Worth noting that
f,F,\mu
and
\sigma^{2}
are
unknown
but may be estimated from the observed data. Given known constant
p\in(0,1),
the share of
X
at percentile
q=F^{-1}(p)
is

m(q)=\frac{\mathbb{E}\left(X1_{\{X\leq q\}}\right)}{\mu}=\frac{\int_{0}^{q}sf(s)ds}{\int_{0}^{\infty}sf(s)ds}.

(1)

Remark 1
.

(
1
) is the Lorenz curve co-ordinate at
q,
or as
Butler and McDonald, 1989
referred to, the “first normalized incomplete moment.” It is closely related to other measures of inequality, e.g., the Pietra Index
(
Eliazar and Sokolov, 2010
)

P=F(\mu)-m(\mu).

and the Gini Index
(
Lerman and Yitzhaki, 1984
)

G=\int_{0}^{\infty}f(s)\left\{\frac{sF(s)}{\mu}-m(s)\right\}ds.\qed

Example 1
.

We consider two distributions that are widely leveraged in data mining. First, for the log-normal distribution, let
\Phi(x)
denote the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal random variable. By definition, the cumulative distribution function of log-normal
(\mu,\sigma)
is

F_{\mathrm{LN}(\mu,\sigma)}(x)=\Phi\left(\frac{\mathrm{ln}x-\mu}{\sigma}\right),

resulting

q=\mathrm{exp}\left\{\mu+\sigma\Phi^{-1}(p)\right\},\quad\mu=\mathrm{exp}\left(\mu+\sigma^{2}/2\right).

For log-normal, (
1
) can be solved in closed-form
(
Butler and McDonald, 1989
)
, namely,

m(q)=F_{\mathrm{LN}(\mu+\sigma^{2},\sigma)}(q),\quad p\in(0,1).

Second, consider the exponential distribution. Let

f_{\lambda}(x)=\lambda\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda x}

denote its probability density function, and

F_{\mathrm{exp}(\lambda)}=1-\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda x},

denote its cumulative distribution function, which implies

q=-\mathrm{ln}(1-p)/\lambda,\quad\mu=\lambda^{-1},

and

m(q)=1-\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda q}(1+\lambda q),\quad q\in(0,\infty).

It is worth noting that the scaling factor
\lambda
is not relevant here. ∎

1.2
Literature review and contribution

Although (
1
) is well-established, there are few discussions around its statistical inference. For example,
Butler and McDonald, 1989
among many others focused on pre-specified values of
q,
but in reality we need to estimate
q
from the observed data – given independently and identically samples

X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}

from unknown distribution
F,
how to construct statistically valid point estimate and confidence interval for
m(q)?

From the literature, analytical
(
Beach and Davidson, 1983
)
and bootstrap-based solutions
(
Dupuis and Victoria-Feser, 2006
)
had been proposed. Unfortunately, however, they lag behind the needs of modern-age analytics (e.g., on-line A/B experiments). On the one hand, closed-form solutions tend to be unintuitive and less applicable to “non-standard” scenarios in industry. For example, when the sample size
n
is sufficiently large, should we treat

\hat{q}

as fixed and the true value for
q?
On the other hand, re-sampling is computationally expensive or even infeasible for big data
(
Kleiner et al., 2014
)
.

In light of the opportunities, we make a three-fold contribution to the community. First, we increase awareness to the potential of these measures of inequality in modern-age analytics. Second, we create an
alternative
solution that is intuitive, closed-form (therefore computationally efficient), mathematically equivalent to
Beach and Davidson, 1983
in “standard” settings, and generalizable to others in practice. Third, we reveal a somewhat surprising insight – the often default and seemingly harmless exercise of assuming

q\approx\hat{q}

leads to highly non-trivial challenges for, or worse, outright misleading, statistical inference.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section
2
employs
m
-estimation
(
Newey and McFadden, 1994
;
Stefanski and Boos, 2002
)
to derive point estimate and confidence interval for
m,
highlighting why closed-form solutions are preferred, both theoretically and practically. Section
3
illustrates the proposed methodology using simulated and real-life examples. Section
4
concludes and presents future directions.

Methodology

2.1
Theory and practice

For any fixed
p\in(0,1),
we estimate
q=F^{-1}(p)
by its sample analogue

\hat{q}=X_{\left(\lfloor np\rfloor\right),}

resulting the following plug-in estimator for (
1
):

\hat{m}(\hat{q})=\frac{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}1_{\{X_{i}\leq\hat{q}\}}}{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}}.

(2)

Its asymptotic consistency is guaranteed by

\hat{q}\overset{p}{\rightarrow}q,

and by applying the central limit theorem to both the denominator and the numerator of (
2
), respectively.

To quantify the uncertainty of (
2
), although it is possible to leverage bootstrap, closed-form solutions are much preferred. Unfortunately, however, deriving closed-form variance estimate is challenging, because the sample percentile
\hat{q}
(as a non-linear function of
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
) renders

X_{1}1_{\{X_{1}\leq\hat{q}\}},\ldots,X_{n}1_{\{X_{n}\leq\hat{q}\}}

“non-trivially” correlated.

To overcome this challenge, we re-write (
1
) as

\mathbb{E}\left\{X\mathrm{1}_{\{X\leq q\}}-m(q)X\right\}=0.

(3)

Solving the sample analogue of (
3
) yields (
2
). To infer uncertainty, we let

Y=X\mathrm{1}_{\{X\leq q\}}-m(q)X,\quad Z=\mathrm{1}_{\{X\leq q\}}-p,

and augment the estimating equation (
3
) as

\bm{g}(X,m,q)=\left(Y,Z\right)^{T},

following the strategy of
Newey, 1984
. It is trivial that
\mathbb{E}\bm{g}=\bm{0}.
We now present the main result of the paper.

Proposition 1
.

Let

\bm{B}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left\{(Y-qZ)^{2}\right\}}{\mu^{2}}&-\frac{\mathbb{E}\left(YZ\right)+qf(q)\mathbb{E}\left(Z^{2}\right)}{\mu f(q)}\\
-\frac{\mathbb{E}\left(YZ\right)+qf(q)\mathbb{E}\left(Z^{2}\right)}{\mu f(q)}&\frac{p(1-p)}{f^{2}(q)}\end{bmatrix}.

The joint asymptotic distribution of
\hat{m}(\hat{q})
and
\hat{q}
is

\sqrt{n}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{m}(\hat{q})-m(q)\\
\hat{q}-q\end{pmatrix}\overset{d}{\rightarrow}\bm{N}\left\{\bm{0},\bm{B}\right\}.

(4)

∎

Proof.

The theory of
m-
estimation ensures that

\sqrt{n}\begin{pmatrix}\hat{m}(\hat{q})-m(q)\\
\hat{q}-q\end{pmatrix}\overset{d}{\rightarrow}\bm{N}\left(\bm{0},\bm{B}\bm{M}\bm{B}^{T}\right),

(5)

where

\bm{B}=\mathbb{E}\left\{\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\partial\bm{g}}{\partial m},&\frac{\partial\bm{g}}{\partial q}\end{pmatrix}^{-1}\right\}=\begin{bmatrix}-\mu&qf(q)\\
0&f(q)\end{bmatrix}^{-1}=-\frac{1}{\mu f(q)}\begin{bmatrix}f(q)&-qf(q)\\
0&-\mu\end{bmatrix},

(6)

and

\bm{M}=\mathbb{E}(\bm{g}\bm{g}^{T})=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{E}\left(Y^{2}\right)&\mathbb{E}\left(YZ\right)\\
\mathbb{E}\left(YZ\right)&\mathbb{E}\left(Z^{2}\right)\end{bmatrix}.

(7)

Combining (
5
)–(
7
),

\displaystyle\bm{B}\bm{M}\bm{B}^{T}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\left(f(q)\mu\right)^{2}}\begin{bmatrix}f(q)&-qf(q)\\
0&-\mu\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbb{E}\left(Y^{2}\right)&\mathbb{E}\left(YZ\right)\\
\mathbb{E}\left(YZ\right)&\mathbb{E}\left(Z^{2}\right)\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}f(q)&0\\
-qf(q)&-\mu\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{f(q)\mathbb{E}\left(Y^{2}\right)-qf(q)\mathbb{E}\left(YZ\right)}{\left(f(q)\mu\right)^{2}}&\frac{f(q)\mathbb{E}\left(YZ\right)-qf(q)\mathbb{E}\left(Z^{2}\right)}{\left(f(q)\mu\right)^{2}}\\
-\frac{\mu\mathbb{E}\left(YZ\right)}{\left(f(q)\mu\right)^{2}}&-\frac{\mu\mathbb{E}\left(Z^{2}\right)}{\left(f(q)\mu\right)^{2}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}f(q)&0\\
-qf(q)&-\mu\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left(Y^{2}\right)-2q\mathbb{E}\left(YZ\right)+q^{2}\mathbb{E}\left(Z^{2}\right)}{\mu^{2}}&-\frac{\mu\mathbb{E}\left(YZ\right)+qf(q)\mu\mathbb{E}\left(Z^{2}\right)}{f(q)\mu^{2}}\\
-\frac{\mu\mathbb{E}\left(YZ\right)+qf(q)\mu\mathbb{E}\left(Z^{2}\right)}{f(q)\mu^{2}}&\frac{\left(\mu\right)^{2}\mathbb{E}\left(Z^{2}\right)}{f^{2}(q)\mu^{2}}\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\mathbb{E}\left\{(Y-qZ)^{2}\right\}}{\mu^{2}}&-\frac{\mathbb{E}\left(YZ\right)+qf(q)\mathbb{E}\left(Z^{2}\right)}{\mu f(q)}\\
-\frac{\mathbb{E}\left(YZ\right)+qf(q)\mathbb{E}\left(Z^{2}\right)}{\mu f(q)}&\frac{\mathbb{E}\left(Z^{2}\right)}{f^{2}(q)}\end{bmatrix}.

To complete the proof, note that

\mathbb{E}\left(Z^{2}\right)=p(1-p)

because

\mathrm{1}_{\{X\leq q\}}\sim\mathrm{Bern}(p).\qed

∎

Now that the theoretical foundation is established, we can present the variance estimator for (
2
). For

i=1,\ldots,n,

let

\hat{Y}_{i}=X_{i}\mathrm{1}_{\{X_{i}\leq\hat{q}\}}-\hat{m}\left(\hat{q}\right)X_{i},\quad\hat{Z}_{i}=\mathrm{1}_{\{X_{i}\leq\hat{q}\}}-p.

(
4
) implies the following plug-in variance estimator:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbb{V}}_{\mathrm{a}}\left\{\hat{m}(\hat{q})\right\}=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(\hat{Y}_{i}-\hat{q}\hat{Z}_{i})^{2}}{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\right)^{2}}.

(8)

Remark 2
.

We highlight the following key benefits of the proposed alternative defined in (
4
)–(
8
):

1.

Validity
: As a sanity check, (
4
) suggests the textbook result
(
Van der Vaart, 2000
)
in regard to the asymptotic variance of sample percentiles, namely

\hat{\mathbb{V}}_{\mathrm{a}}\left(\hat{q}\right)=\frac{p(1-p)}{nf^{2}(q)}.

We will examine in greater details the validity of (
4
) in Section
3.1
;

2.

Simplicity
: In Appendix
A
, we prove that under the “standard” setting of

\hat{q}=X_{\left(\lfloor np\rfloor\right)},

(
4
) is mathematically equivalent to the asymptotic variance by
Beach and Davidson, 1983
. However, (
4
) is much more intuitive and simpler to compute;

3.

Efficiency
: (
8
) is more computationally efficient than bootstrap. Furthermore, after a single-pass to compute

\hat{q},

it can be directly transformed into distributed algorithms like MapReduce
(
Dean and Ghemawat, 2008
)
.

2.2
Treating
\hat{q}
as fixed

The main challenge in (
4
) is to properly account for the uncertainty of
\hat{q}.
In practice where the sample size
n
is large, we often consider

\hat{q}

as fixed and the true value for
q.
In order words, we assume

q\approx\hat{q}

to be known, in which case we similarly estimate (
1
) by its finite-population analogue

\hat{m}(q)=\frac{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}1_{\{X_{i}\leq q\}}}{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}},

(9)

whose asymptotic distribution of
\hat{m}_{q}
is

\sqrt{n}\left\{\hat{m}(q)-m(q)\right\}\overset{d}{\rightarrow}N\left(0,\frac{\mathbb{E}Y^{2}}{\mu^{2}}\right).

(10)

Proof.

With
q
known, (
5
) is reduced to

\sqrt{n}\left\{\hat{m}(q)-m(q)\right\}\overset{d}{\rightarrow}N\left[0,\left\{\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{dY}{dm}\right)\right\}^{-2}\mathbb{E}\left(Y^{2}\right)\right]=N\left(0,\frac{\mathbb{E}Y^{2}}{\mu^{2}}\right).

Alternatively, we can re-write (
9
) via first-order Taylor expansion
(
Deng et al., 2018
)
:

\displaystyle\hat{m}(q)-m(q)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}1_{\{X_{i}\leq q\}}-\mathbb{E}\left(X1_{\{X\leq q\}}\right)}{\mu}

\displaystyle-\frac{\mathbb{E}\left(X1_{\{X\leq q\}}\right)}{\left(\mu\right)^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}-\mu\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n\mu}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\{X_{i}1_{\{X_{i}\leq q\}}-\frac{\mathbb{E}\left(X1_{\{X\leq q\}}\right)}{\mu}X_{i}\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n\mu}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}.

(
10
) follows immediately, because

Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}

are i.i.d. with zero means.
∎

2.3
A surprising insight

To guide the trade-off between statistical rigor and computation efficiency, we quantify the difference between (
4
), which appropriately treats
\hat{q}
as a random variable, and (
10
), which incorrectly treats
\hat{q}
fixed. As it turns out, the latter can lead to misleading inference and decision-making.

Corollary 1
.

The difference between the variances of
\hat{m}(q)
and that of
\hat{m}(\hat{q})
is

\mathbb{V}_{\mathrm{a}}\left\{\hat{m}(q)\right\}-\mathbb{V}_{\mathrm{a}}\left\{\hat{m}(\hat{q})\right\}=\frac{q\left[2m(q)\{1-m(q)\}\mu-qp(1-p)\right]}{n\mu^{2}}.

(11)

Proof.

Recall

Y=X\mathrm{1}_{\{X\leq q\}}-m(q)X

and

Z=\mathrm{1}_{\{X\leq q\}}-p.

By definition,

\mathbb{E}Y=\mathbb{E}Z=0\quad\mathbb{E}\left(Z^{2}\right)=p(1-p).

By (
4
) and (
10
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(YZ)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(X\mathrm{1}^{2}_{\{X\leq q\}}-m(q)X\mathrm{1}_{\{X\leq q\}}\right)

\displaystyle=m(q)\{1-m(q)\}\mu.

(12)

Therefore,

\displaystyle n\mu^{2}\left[\mathbb{V}_{\mathrm{a}}\left\{\hat{m}(q)\right\}-\mathbb{V}_{\mathrm{a}}\left\{\hat{m}(\hat{q})\right\}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(Y^{2}\right)-\mathbb{E}\left\{(Y-qZ)^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle=2q\mathbb{E}\left(YZ\right)-q^{2}\mathbb{E}\left(Z^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=2qm(q)\{1-m(q)\}\mu-q^{2}p(1-p).

(13)

∎

Remark 3
.

Intuitively, (
11
) should be negative, because (
10
) “leaves out” the uncertainty around
\hat{q}.
Surprisingly, the opposite is true for a wide range of distributions. Indeed, ignoring the uncertain around
\hat{q}
often diminishes the negative correlation between
X
and
1_{\{X\leq\hat{q}\}},
inflating the overall variance estimate.

We illustrate this counter-intuitive phenomenon with two common distributions. First, for
X\sim\mathrm{Unif}(0,L),
by (
1
)

q=pL,\quad\mu=L/2,\quad m(q)=q^{2}/L^{2}=p^{2}.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{\mathrm{a}}\left\{\hat{m}(q)\right\}-\mathbb{V}_{\mathrm{a}}\left\{\hat{m}(\hat{q})\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{4p^{4}(1-p)}{n}\geq 0.

Second, for Exp
(\lambda),

q=\mathrm{ln}(1-p)/\lambda,

or equivalently

p=1-\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda q}.

Furthermore, from Example
1

\mu=\lambda^{-1},\quad m(q)=1-\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda q}(1+\lambda q).

To prove that (
11
) is non-negative, we show that

2m(1-m)\mu\geq qp(1-p),

or equivalently,

h(t)=2\left(\mathrm{e}^{t}-1-t\right)(1+t)-t\left(\mathrm{e}^{t}-1\right)\geq 0

(14)

for all
t=\lambda q\in(0,\infty).
To prove (
14
), note that

h^{\prime}(t)=t\mathrm{e}^{t}+3\mathrm{e}^{t}-4t-3

and

h^{\prime\prime}(t)=t\mathrm{e}^{t}+4\mathrm{e}^{t}-4.

Is it trivial that
h^{\prime\prime}(t)\geq 0
for all
t\geq 0.
Consequently,
h^{\prime}(t)\geq h^{\prime}(0)=0
for all
t\geq 0,
which implies that
h(t)\geq h(0)=0
for all
t\geq 0.

∎

Concluding remarks

This paper proposed an alternative variance estimator for the first normalized incomplete moment. Our proposed methodology was statistically valid, computationally cheap, and easily adaptable. In particular, we discover that a common practice under the “big data” assumption can lead to highly non-trivial challenges for trustworthy statistical inference, or misleading decision making.

Our work suggests multiple future directions. First, we can potentially generalize our results to the joint inference of
\left\{\hat{m}(\hat{q})\right\}_{q\in(0,\infty)},
which converges to a gaussian process. Second, we can extend our methodology to other inequality measures (e.g., Gini Index), which are also closely related to incomplete moments. Third, it might be interesting to investigate under which conditions (
11
) is non-negative.

Appendix A
Mathematical equivalence under “standard” settings

As pointed out by
Brzezinski, 2013
, when

\hat{q}=X_{\left(\lfloor np\rfloor\right)},

the asymptotic variance of

\hat{m}(\hat{q})

proposed by
Beach and Davidson, 1983
is

\mathbb{V}_{\mathrm{bd}}\left\{\hat{m}(\hat{q})\right\}=\frac{p}{N\mu^{2}}\left[\sigma^{2}\frac{p\gamma^{2}}{\mu^{2}}+\epsilon^{2}\left(1-2\frac{p\gamma}{\mu}\right)+(1-p)(q-\gamma)^{2}-2\frac{p\gamma}{\mu}(q-\gamma)(\mu-\gamma)\right],

(15)

where

\gamma=\mathbb{E}(X\mid X\leq q)=\frac{m(q)\mu}{p},

(16)

and

\epsilon^{2}=\mathbb{V}(X\mid X\leq q)=\frac{\mathbb{E}\left(X^{2}\mathrm{1}_{\{X\leq q\}}\right)}{p}-\gamma^{2}

(17)

are the conditional mean and variance of
X
given
X\leq q,
respectively. Related asymptotic analyses appear in
Bickel, 1965
and
Stigler, 1973
.

Lemma 1
.

The following holds:

\mu^{2}m^{2}-(2m-1)p\gamma^{2}+p(1-p)q^{2}-2qm(1-m)\mu=p(1-p)(\gamma-q)^{2}-2pm(q-\gamma)(\mu-\gamma).

(18)

Proof.

To simplify notation, we denote
m(q)
by
m.
Suggested by (
16
),

\frac{p\gamma}{\mu}=m,\quad p\gamma=m\mu.

Consequently,

\displaystyle-2qm(1-m)\mu

\displaystyle=-2qm\mu+2mqm\mu

\displaystyle=-2qp\gamma+2mqp\gamma

\displaystyle=-2pq\gamma+2p^{2}q\gamma-2pmq\mu+2pmq\gamma

\displaystyle=-2p(1-p)q\gamma-2pm(q\mu-q\gamma).

(19)

First, adding
\mu^{2}m^{2}
to the left hand side of (
A
), and

2pm\gamma\mu-p^{2}\gamma^{2}(=\mu^{2}m^{2})

to its right hand side,

\displaystyle\mu^{2}m^{2}-2qm(1-m)\mu

\displaystyle=2pm\gamma\mu-p^{2}\gamma^{2}-2p(1-p)q\gamma-2pm(q\mu-q\gamma)

\displaystyle=-p^{2}\gamma^{2}-2p(1-p)q\gamma-2pm(q\mu-\gamma\mu-q\gamma).

(20)

Next, subtracting

(2m-1)p\gamma^{2}

to both sides of (
A
),

\displaystyle\mu^{2}m^{2}-(2m-1)p\gamma^{2}-2qm(1-m)\mu

\displaystyle=-p^{2}\gamma^{2}-2p(1-p)q\gamma-2pm(q\mu-\gamma\mu-q\gamma)-(2m-1)p\gamma^{2}

\displaystyle=p(1-p)\gamma^{2}-2p(1-p)q\gamma-2pm(q\mu-\gamma\mu-q\gamma+\gamma^{2})

\displaystyle=p(1-p)(\gamma^{2}-2q\gamma)-2pm(q-\gamma)(\mu-\gamma).

(21)

Finally, to complete the proof of (
18
), simply add

p(1-p)q^{2}

to both sides of (
A
).
∎

Proposition 2
.

Beach and Davidson, 1983
’s formula (
15
) is mathematically equivalent to (
4
).

Proof.

On the one hand, by (
17
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}Y^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(m^{2}X^{2}-2mX^{2}\mathrm{1}_{\{X\leq q\}}+X^{2}\mathrm{1}^{2}_{\{X\leq q\}}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left\{m^{2}X^{2}-(2m-1)X^{2}\mathrm{1}_{\{X\leq q\}}\right\}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}m^{2}+\mu^{2}m^{2}-(2m-1)\mathbb{E}\left(X^{2}\mathrm{1}_{\{X\leq q\}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}m^{2}+\mu^{2}m^{2}-(2m-1)p(\epsilon^{2}+\gamma^{2}).

(22)

Using (
A
) and (
2.3
), we re-write (
4
) as

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{\mathrm{a}}\left\{\hat{m}(\hat{q})\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N\mu^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left(Y^{2}-2qYZ+q^{2}Z^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N\mu^{2}}\left\{\sigma^{2}m^{2}+\mu^{2}m^{2}-(2m-1)p(\epsilon^{2}+\gamma^{2})+q^{2}p(1-p)-2qm(1-m)\mu\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N\mu^{2}}\left\{\sigma^{2}m^{2}+(1-2m)p\epsilon^{2}+\mu^{2}m^{2}-(2m-1)p\gamma^{2}+q^{2}p(1-p)-2qm(1-m)\mu\right\}.

(23)

On the other hand, we re-write (
15
) as

\mathbb{V}_{\mathrm{bd}}\left\{\hat{m}(\hat{q})\right\}=\frac{1}{N\mu^{2}}\left[\sigma^{2}m^{2}+(1-2m)p\epsilon^{2}+p(1-p)(q-\gamma)^{2}-2pm(q-\gamma)(\mu-\gamma)\right].

(24)

By Lemma
1
, (
24
) = (
A
), and the proof is complete.
