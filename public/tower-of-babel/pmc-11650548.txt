Cephalic ganglia transcriptomics of the American cockroach Periplaneta americana (Blattodea: Blattidae)
Authors: Ilana Levy, Ryan Arvidson
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe American cockroach Periplaneta americana (L.) (Blattodea, Blattidae) has been a model organism for biochemical and physiological study for almost a century, however, its use does not benefit from the genetic tools found in key model species such as Drosophila melanogaster. To facilitate the use of the cockroach as a model system in neuroscience and to serve as a foundation for functional and translational experimentation, a transcriptome of the cephalic ganglia was assembled and annotated, and differential expression profiles between these ganglia were assessed. The transcriptome assembly yielded >400 k transcripts, with >40 k putative coding sequences. Gene ontology and protein domain searches indicate the cerebral and gnathal ganglia (GNG) have distinct genetic expression profiles. The developmental Toll signaling pathway appears to be active in the adult central nervous system (CNS), which may suggest a separate role for this pathway besides innate immune activation or embryonic development. The catabolic glycolytic and citric acid cycle enzymes are well represented in both ganglia, but key enzymes are more highly expressed in the GNG. Both ganglia express gluconeogenic and trehaloneogenic enzymes, suggesting a larger role of the CNS in regulating hemolymph sugar homeostasis than previously appreciated. The annotation and quantification of the cephalic ganglia transcriptome reveal both canonical and novel pathways in signaling and metabolism in an adult insect and lay a foundation for future functional and genetic analysis.

Introduction

The cockroach (Periplaneta americana) has been used as a model organism in neurobiology and pharmacology research for almost a century (Crampton 1925, Minami and Dallenbach 1946, Stankiewicz et al. 2012, Namiki and Kanzaki 2016). However, we are limited in our current capabilities because many of the genetic tools in Drosophila and mouse models have not been adapted to the cockroach until recently. Embryo injection, often used for Drosophila transgenesis, is confounded in cockroaches as the cockroach eggs are protected inside a heavily scleritized ootheca. In a 2022 study, a nascent technique was developed to overcome this limitation in which Cas9 ribonucleoproteins were injected into the haemocoal of adult cockroach females. Although results suggested positive introduction of heritable mutation in the oocytes, the technique still needs to be developed further considering the diverse set of reproductive stages in insects (Shirai et al. 2022). Further, P. americana has a long juvenile development period, on the order of one year with a variable number of instars (Griffiths and Tauber 1942). Therefore, it is not yet feasible to perform genetic crosses in a reasonable time frame as is common in mice, Drosophila, and now Aedes (Robinson et al. 2004, Kokoza and Raikhel 2011, van der Weyden et al. 2011, Hales et al. 2015).

Despite these difficulties, advances in transcriptomics and curation of gene databases have opened new possibilities for discovery and experimentation in diverse organisms. Our interest lies in probing how the brain integrates sensory data into decision-making and how the brain maintains homeostasis in response to stress, e.g., nutrient deprivation, toxicological and oxidative insults, and aging. To this end, an understanding of the type, quantity, and location of gene expression in the brain is invaluable.

The arthropod cephalic ganglia, composed of six neuromeres, has been historically described as the subesophageal and supraesophageal ganglia. Different developmental patterns in arthropods yield two varying anatomical placements of these groups of neuromeres with respect to the esophagus (Harzsch 2004). Therefore, new naming conventions were employed to synonymize the description of the two groups. The term “subesophageal ganglion,” referring to the mandibular, maxillary, and labial sensory “subcenters,” has been replaced with “gnathal ganglia” (GNG). The term “supraesophageal ganglion,” referring to the compilation of the protocerebrum, deutocerebrum, and tritocerebrum, has been replaced with “cerebral ganglia” (CRG). CRG then represents well-known “supercategories” of neuropils that include the mushroom bodies, central complex, and antennal lobes, among others (Ito et al. 2014).

Locomotion, posture, and learning and memory have been noted functions of the central complex and mushroom bodies in arthropods (Guo and Ritzmann 2013, Arican et al. 2019). The behaviors associated with the central complex include goal-oriented locomotion, visual orientation and place memory, sleep and arousal, courtship and mating, and reward-seeking (Strausfeld and Hirth 2013, Martin et al. 2015, Tuthill and Wilson 2016). In Drosophila and cockroaches, modifications or deviations in the central complex and neuropeptides lead to offsets of motor actions resembling Parkinson-like behaviors due to the local abundance of dopaminergic neurons (Bayersdorfer et al. 2010, Strausfeld and Hirth 2013, Hamanaka et al. 2016). In Drosophila, RNA interference targeting tachykinin and short neuropeptide F, expressed in the central complex, resulted in increases of avoidant behaviors and motor activity levels (Kahsai et al. 2010). Furthermore, the distinction between the GNG and CRG in terms of their respective roles in locomotion functions, such as leg coordination, has been noted. Disconnection of GNG descending interneurons from thoracic neural networks in P. americana resulted in uncoordinated leg movements when walking on water. By contrast, P. americana from which the CRG had been removed exhibited normal coordination on land and water (Gal and Libersat 2006). While some functional overlap exists between these ganglia, there appears to be a discernible anatomical and functional compartmentalization of locomotor behaviors.

The goal of this study is to describe the transcriptome of the CRG and GNG to further functional genetics in P. americana, identify targets for in vivo studies in plasticity and injury response, and facilitate generalization of pathways and processes identified in model organisms, such as Drosophila. Gene identification and annotation allow for the selection of subsets of genes of interest for functional studies, including quantitative PCR, in situ hybridization, and RNA interference. A high-quality assembly can facilitate high-throughput spatial and temporal RNA-seq experiments to monitor the development of the brain or the effects of drugs or environmental toxins on the brain. These may be particularly salient considering the significantly longer life span of P. americana with respect to Drosophila. In the data herein, we describe the annotation, quantification, and differential gene expression between the cerebral and GNG, and emphasize the annotation and expression of signaling systems, e.g., G-protein coupled receptors (GPCRs), receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs), Toll receptors, and their respective ligands, which give insight into the molecular underpinnings of the signaling infrastructure of the cockroach central nervous system (CNS). GPCRs are primary targets of neurotransmission and G-protein coupled receptor (GPCR) ligands control a vast array of cellular functions and organismal behaviors (Caers et al. 2012). Receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK) signaling is involved in cell survival, growth, differentiation, and stress responses (Sopko and Perrimon 2013). We also reveal the presence of gluconeogenic/trehaloneogenic pathways in each of the ganglia. Lastly, we highlight genes important in synaptic plasticity and those implicated in neurodegenerative disorders. The assembly and annotation of the CRG transcriptome improve our understanding of the neurobiology of the cockroach and lay a foundation for future functional analyses.

Materials and Methods

Transcriptome Assembly

Sequencing data was generated previously and deposited under Bioproject accession number PRJNA356979, Biosample SAMN06130820, runs SRR5097510-SRR5097513 from nonstung animals (Kaiser et al. 2019). In brief: CRG and GNG from four staged 10-days-old adult male P. americana, were extirpated, combined into two technical replicates, and RNA was extracted using the Trizol method according to manufacturer’s instructions (Invitrogen). RNA quantity and integrity was assessed via Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer and sequencing libraries prepared using a NEBNext Ultra Directional RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (New England Biolabs). Libraries were sequenced as 150 base-pair paired-end reads on an Illumina NextSeq 550. Library preparation and sequencing were performed at the University of California, Riverside, Institute of Integrative Genome Biology. All libraries were concatenated into left and right pairs and transcriptomes were assembled de novo using the following programs: Trinity ver. 2.11 (Haas et al. 2013), Soapdenovo 1.03 using k-mers of 35 and 75 (Xie et al. 2014), Abyss ver. 2.0.1 (Robertson et al. 2010), Spades in both RNA and Single-cell modes ver. 3.14.1 (Nurk et al. 2013), and Velvet/Oases vers.1.1.10 and 0.2.09 respectively (Schulz et al. 2012), all with default parameters for paired-end assembly. The output of the individual programs were concatenated, then processed with the EvidentialGene pipeline (Gilbert 2019). Coding sequences were extracted with the Trinity plugin TransDecoder (Haas et al. 2013), and the resulting output was used for downstream functional annotation. Each assembly was assessed by BUSCO ver. 4.1.2 with the dataset insecta_odb10 (Simao et al. 2015) and TransRate (Smith-Unna et al. 2016).

Annotation

Transcripts were quantified using Kallisto ver. 0.46.2 (Bray et al. 2016), and differential expression was calculated using DESeq2 plugin for Trinity (Love et al. 2014). Heatmaps were generated from TMM-normalized values from Kallisto outputs (Robinson and Oshlack 2010). Functional annotation, Gene Ontology (GO) term assignment, and protein signature identification were performed using translated open reading frames (ORFs) using the following programs: Blast2GO (Gotz et al. 2008), InterProScan on OmicsBox (BioBam) software version 1.4 (Jones et al. 2014), and Standalone BLAST (as blastp—protein query against a protein database) version 2.2.31 (Camacho et al. 2009), all against the UniProt Swiss-Prot database (Boeckmann et al. 2003). Blast2GO combines BLAST searches and GO for functional annotation, InterProScan predicts protein identity by domain search, and Standalone BLAST compares query sequences against a chosen database to make identifications based on sequence similarly. Swiss-Prot database was chosen as a primary database as it is a high-quality, nonredundant, manually annotated, and standardized to other UniProt databases (Boutet et al. 2016). GPCRs were identified by combining results from InterProScan, TMHMM (transmembrane prediction using hidden Markov models) (Krogh et al. 2001) output for 6 or 7 transmembrane domain-containing transcripts, and manually, by searching the BLAST output for entries from the FlyBase Gene Group List (FlyBase ID FBgg0000172) (Larkin et al. 2021). A similar approach was used to identify neuropeptides (FlyBase ID FBgg0000179) and RTKs (FlyBase ID FBgg0000220), but without TMHMM input for RTKs as there are many single-pass transmembrane proteins that are not RTKs. All other specific genes were manually searched in the BLAST output using UniProt naming conventions, using only those whose e-value was less than 10−5 and confirmed by reciprocal BLAST (Boeckmann et al. 2003). Phylogenetic analyses were performed on Galaxy (usegalaxy.org) (Afgan et al. 2018) using MAFFT for alignments, ver. 7.525, using amino acid sequences, BLOSUM62 matrix, and the FFT-NS-i method (Katoh and Standley 2013). IQ-Tree, ver. 2.3.5 (Minh et al. 2020), was used to generate Maximum-Likelihood phylogenies, with ultrafast bootstrap 1,000 replicates (Hoang et al. 2018), using ModelFinder (Kalyaanamoorthy et al. 2017) for model selection. QMaker (Q.pfam), Probability Matrix from Blocks (PMB), FreeRate models (R), empirical amino acid frequencies (F), and General “Variable Time” matrix (VT) were selected by ModelFinder (Kalyaanamoorthy et al. 2017) and combined as follows: Q.pfam+R6 as the best fit model for GPCRs, WAG+F+R4, as the best fit model for RTKs, and VT+F+R2 as the best fit model for G-alpha subunits.

Results

Transcriptome Assembly and Evaluation

The approach of combining multiple individual assemblies from several programs and filtering out redundant sequences via the EvidentialGene pipeline yielded a single assembly with 460k contigs. The final assembly was compared to the component assemblies for completeness and redundancy via BUSCO, and while completeness only improved slightly (93.1% of BUSCOs present in the final), there was a substantial reduction in redundancy assessed as “duplicated BUSCOs.” The open reading frames extracted with TransDecoder yielded a transcriptome with 42k transcripts (which was used in all functional downstream analysis) and only saw a slight reduction in BUSCOs to 92.3% present. Metrics for the individual assemblies and EvidentialGene and TransDecoder output are given in Supplementary Table S1. The final set of annotated transcripts has been deposited at DDBJ/ENA/GenBank under the accession GKQS00000000. The version described in this paper is the first version, GKQS01000000. The top BLAST hits with coding sequences and read counts given in Supplemental Contig Data.tsv.

Differential Expression Between Ganglia

Each ganglion assessed has distinct expression profiles. We define differentially expressed transcripts as having a P-adjusted (Padj) < 10−4, and > 4× fold change between ganglia. The stringent Padj threshold and fold change requirement (log2 change of 2) reflect the control of the false discovery rate (FDR) and help in the identification of biologically meaningful differences between ganglia. Of the 42k TransDecoder-generated transcripts, the CRG expresses 881 significantly higher than the GNG, while the GNG expresses 583 more highly than the CRG (Fig. 1C). Gene products most significantly over-expressed in the CRG include neuroparsin-A and insulin-related peptides ILP1 and ILP4, and cysteine sulfinic acid decarboxylase, which plays a key role in the generation of inhibitory neurotransmitters β-alanine and taurine (Liu et al. 2012). Also, among the most CRG over-expressed genes are adipokinetic hormone (AKH1), which acts similarly to glucagon in mammals, and AKH/corazonin-related peptide (ACP), whose function is less understood but is important in energy utilization in locusts during flight (Hou et al. 2021). The presence of these peptides supports the well established role of neurosecretory cells in the CRG in the detection and maintenance of energy homeostasis (Lorenz and Gade 2009). The GNG differentially expresses both subunits of the rickets ligand, Bursicon, which is a heterodimer of Bursicon-alpha and Partner of Bursicon (Luo et al. 2005). Nicotinic acetylcholine receptor subunits are also over-expressed in the GNG, presumably as part of a complete channel specific to the ganglia. The transcription factor drumstick, which is important in embryonic development (Iwaki et al. 2001) and thought to act downstream of Notch, Wingless, and Hedgehog signaling (Benitez et al. 2009), appears only in the GNG with no reads detected in the CRG (Fig. 1B). A comparison of read counts between ganglia for all quantifiable transcripts is given in Fig. 1C.

Expression of GPCRs and RTKs

A combinatorial approach to GPCR discovery yielded 87 GPCRs with homologs in the SwissProt database and FlyBase (Fig. 3). Representatives of all the major classes of GPCRs are identified. Class A (rhodopsin-like) GPCRs are the most numerous and diverse in most organisms, which is reflected in the transcriptome. This includes monoamine neurotransmitter receptors, muscarinic acetylcholine receptors, neuropeptide receptors, and the nucleoside adenosine receptor (Fig. 3A and B). There are six isoforms of CCHamide receptor-1 and one isoform of CCHamide receptor-2, which are implicated in increasing starvation-induced olfactory responses (Farhan et al. 2013, Ren et al. 2015), three leucine-rich repeat-containing (Lgr1) and three muscarinic acetylcholine receptors (mAChR). The class B (secretin-like) receptors are dominated by adhesion GPCRs and Methuselah receptor subtypes which play a role in nutrient-sensing coupling to insulin release (Farhan et al. 2013, Ren et al. 2015). Homologs to the Methuselah ligand, stunted (sun), were not identified in the transcriptome. Additional class B GPCRs include latrophilins, a starry night (stan) homolog flamingo (fli), and the neuropeptide pigment dispersing factor (PDF) receptor. The class C (mGluR-like) GPCRs include six isoforms of the metabotropic glutamate receptor (mGluR) and three isoforms of the GABAB receptor, which are activated by the excitatory neurotransmitter glutamate and the inhibitory neurotransmitter GABA respectively. The class C receptor smog and an abundant orphan receptor CG31760-like are also identified, however, the smog ligand fog was not and the CG31760 receptor ligand is unknown. The class F (frizzled-type) GPCRs family is composed of frizzled (Fz) and smoothend (Smo). Three isoforms of Fz and one isoform of Smo were identified. Frizzled is activated by Wnt, whereas Smo is indirectly activated by hedgehog pathway. Both are implicated in neuroprotection and synaptic plasticity in the adult Drosophila brain (He et al. 2018, Rallis et al. 2020). The cyclic-AMP-modulating G-proteins Gαs (1 isoform), Gαi (2 isoforms), Gαo (1 isoform), as well as the phospholipase C—calcium mobilizing Gαq (5 isoforms when including Gα14 and Gα15) are present, representing canonical coupling schemes (Fig. 3C). An isoform of Gαf (also referred to as Gα73B) was identified in the transcriptome as well. In addition to being involved in embryogenesis, Gαf was also discovered to be a transcriptional target of the Jak homolog, hopscotch (which is expressed in both ganglia), and activates Rho in hemocytes (Quan et al. 1993, Bausek and Zeidler 2014). Several of the GPCRs significantly differentially expressed with a Padj < 10−4, including two of the four identified serotonin (5-hydroxytryptamine) receptors, dopamine ecdysteroid receptor (DopEcR), myosuppressin receptor (MyoR), diuretic hormone receptor 2 (DHR2), two Methuselah receptor isoforms and three metabotropic glutamate receptor (mGluR) isoforms. The majority of the differentially expressed receptors or more highly expressed in the CRG, except the diuretic hormone receptor 2 and the Methuselah receptor-like 3 which are expressed more highly in the GNG. Some receptors are exclusively found in the CRG, e.g., D1 and D2 dopamine receptors, and the muscarinic acetylcholine receptor M4 subtype, and the mushroom body-specific octopamine receptor (OAMB), though not statistically differentially expressed.

Sixteen RTKs were identified in the transcriptome, including developmental and metabolic regulators, axon guidance mediators, among others (Fig. 4A). None of the RTKs are differentially expressed at the Padj < 10−4, however torso is exclusively expressed in the CRG with no read counts in the GNG (Padj = 0.054 for differential expression). Phylogenetic analysis shows strong conservation, as the BLAST-identified RTKs cluster with Drosophila and human orthologs for all receptor clades (Fig. 4B). Most of the RTKs have one isoform, except the insulin receptor, of which there are two distinct isoforms, and three PDGF/VEGF (PVFR) receptor isoforms which appear to be splice variants, consistent with Drosophila PVFR. The invertebrate-specific Venus kinase is expressed in the ganglia and in most species, it is activated by the amino acid L-arginine. Venus kinase from the desert locust, Schistocerca gregaria, was used for comparison as Venus kinase is apparently absent in D. melanogaster, but present in other Diptera, including other Drosophila (Vanderstraete et al. 2013, Lenaerts et al. 2017).

Neuropeptides, Growth Factors, and Neurotransmitter Synthesis Enzymes

For each GPCR and RTK identified, we searched for cognate ligands and for homologs to entries in the FlyBase neuropeptide gene group (FBgg0000179) (Fig. 5A and C). As many GPCRs are activated by small molecule monoamines, we also included key enzymes in pathways for their synthesis. Unlike their receptors, peptide ligands AKH and several insulin-related peptides are significantly more expressed in the CRG, consistent with a subset of CRG neurons being involved in nutrient sensing and communication on an endocrine level (Fig. 5A). In addition to Burs. and PBurs., crustacean cardioactive peptide (CCAP), insulin-related peptide 5 (SIRP5) are more highly expressed in the GNG. Five insulin-like peptides were identified and named according to the convention of Jan Veenstra (Veenstra 2023).

Growth factors and other RTK ligands are presented separately due to their relatively lower read counts and were further confirmed phylogenetically (Fig. 5C and D). Both the CRG and GNG express bride of sevenless (boss)-the ligand of the RTK sevenless, jelly belly (jeb)-the activating ligand of anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) receptor, and ephrin. Epidermal growth factor isoforms (EGF1 and EGF2, spitz in Drosophila), two fibroblast growth factors, FGF and branchless (Bnl) homolog, one insulin-like growth factor, and six putative PVFR ligands (PVF-A though PVF-F) are expressed in both ganglia. PVF-B clusters well with Drosophila PFV3, whereas all other PVF isoforms cluster well with Drosophila PVF1, with PVF-A the most similar by sequence. No identified cockroach PVFs cluster with Drosophila PVF2. The torso ligand prothoracicotropic hormone (PTTH) is differentially expressed more highly in the CRG with very low read counts in the GNG, consistent with the CRG exclusive expression of torso. Wnt proteins are also included, however they were assembled as fragments and have very low read counts. Wnt subtypes are ligands of the Class F GPCR frizzled (Nichols et al. 2013), and specifically Wnt5 activates frizzled 2 with the RTK ROR1 as a co-receptor in Drosophila neurons (Ripp et al. 2018 Jan 1).

As many ligands for GPCRs are amino acid-derived small molecules, we searched for key enzymes in the biosynthetic pathways for the synthesis of the major monoamines (Fig. 5B). The pathway from tryptophan to serotonin requires hydroxylation by tryptophan hydroxylase (TPH), followed by decarboxylation by L-amino acid decarboxylase. Similarly, synthesis of dopamine begins with hydroxylation of tyrosine to L-DOPA by tyrosine hydroxylase (TH), then decarboxylation to dopamine. Five isoforms of L-amino acid decarboxylase were found, though it is not clear by homology search if they are promiscuous, or specific to an amino acid. A homolog to the Drosophila hydroxylase, henna, is also present, which appears to have the capacity to hydroxylate tryptophan to 5-hydroxytryptophan and phenylalanine to tyrosine (Alcañiz and Silva 1997). Tyramine is synthesized from tyrosine by decarboxylation by an L-amino acid decarboxylase. Tyramine then can be converted to octopamine by tyramine beta hydroxylase (TβH). The synthesis of acetylcholine requires choline acetyltransferase, which was found albeit lowly expressed, and the synthesis of GABA from glutamate requires glutamate decarboxylase, which was found in abundance. While both acetylcholine and GABA are well-known activators of ion channels, they are also activators of the GPCRs mAChR and GABAB respectively.

Developmental Toll Signaling in an Adult Brain

The Toll receptor ligand Spätzle is activated by protease cascades induced either by developmental cues in dorsal-ventral patterning, or by fragments of invading pathogens. This activation leads to expression of antimicrobial peptides (Meng et al. 1999, Weber et al. 2003, Sun et al. 2004, Arnot et al. 2010). Interestingly, the developmental pathway appears to persist in the adult brain (Fig. 6A). The protease cascade, beginning with gastrulation defective (Gd), leads to the processing of the zymogens of the serine proteases, Easter and Snake. Snake then processes the serine protease Easter to its active form, which finally converts Spätzle to its active form as a ligand for Toll (LeMosy et al. 2001). Active Toll induces gene expression changes through an Nf-κB homologous pathway leading to the phosphorylation and activation of the transcription factor dorsal (Fig. 6B) (Belvin and Anderson 1996). In Drosophila embryogenesis it was found that the heparan sulfate 2-O-sulfotransferase, pipe, and its disulfide isomerase-related chaperone windbeutel, are required to spatially restrict Toll signaling for productive dorsal-ventral patterning (Ma et al. 2003, Stein et al. 2013). A candidate homolog for the serine protease nudel, considered to be an upstream activator of gastrulation defective, was not found in the transcriptome (Cho et al. 2010). The presence of each of these components in the cockroach CRG, may indicate that the functional processing of the Toll ligand without immune response persists in the adult brain.

Gluconeogenesis and Trehaloneogenesis in the CRG and GNG

The gluconeogenic pathway, consisting of glycolytic enzymes and four “bypass” enzymes, is expressed in both cephalic ganglia. Additionally, expression levels of the four gluconeogenic specific enzymes are comparable to that of the glycolytic enzymes, indicating active gluconeogenesis (Fig. 7A). To begin gluconeogenesis, pyruvate is supplied, presumably from glial cells, as the precursors alanine or lactate. In Drosophila, glia of the blood-brain barrier are known to be glycolytically active and secrete alanine and lactate to be taken up by neurons for oxidative catabolism in the citric acid cycle (Volkenhoff et al. 2015). The glycolytic enzymes after the investment phase (the two sugar kinases and isomerase) are more highly expressed in the GNG (Fig. 7A). Several citric acid cycle enzyme isoforms are also statistically differentially expressed higher in the GNG than in the CRG, but none conversely (Supplementary Fig. S4), though read count differences are less than the four-fold cutoff. The bifunctional enzyme trehalose-6-phosphate synthase/phosphatase converts glucose-6-phosphate and UDP-glucose to trehalose-6-phosphate, then to trehalose, bypassing glucose-6-phosphatase to complete the trehaloneogenic pathway (Fig. 7B). Trehalose then may exit the cell via the facilitated trehalose transporter (TRET1; 9 isoforms present in transcriptome) or be converted to glucose via trehalase, depending on the energy demand of the tissue.

Glycolytic and gluconeogenic/trehaloneogenic pathway expression in the CRG and GNG. A) Expression of bolded glycolytic enzymes is significantly differentially expressed after the “investment phase” of glycolysis, with higher expression in the GNG. B) Pyruvate can be synthesized from alanine by alanine aminotransferase or from lactate by lactate dehydrogenase. Enzymes in the gluconeogenic and trehaloneogenic pathways are not differentially expressed. Tps1 is a bi-functional enzyme with both trehalose-6-phosphate synthase and trehalose phosphatase activities, which generates trehalose from glucose-6-phosphate and UDP-glucose. UDP-glucose is synthesized by UTP-glucose-1-phosphate uridylyltransferase. The combination of Tps1 and trehalase (Treh) can bypass G6Pase, the terminal reaction of gluconeogenesis, to generate glucose from glucose-6-phosphate.

Identification of Genes Involved in Synaptic Plasticity and Human Neurodegenerative Diseases

Several genes involved in neuroplasticity, neurite outgrowth, synaptic adhesion, and development have been identified. Two isoforms of Notch and several Notch ligands (serrate, delta, jagged, and DNER) are present and while crucial for development, continue to have critical roles in the adult brain including neuronal stem cell renewal, memory, and sleep (Ables et al. 2011, Salazar et al. 2020). Two isoforms of the roundabout receptor (ROBO) and eight isoforms of its ligand slit1 are present and are involved in axon guidance and connectivity (Oliva et al. 2016). Four isoforms of neurexin and two isoforms of its ligand LRRTM (LRRTM1 and LRRTM3) are present and are involved in synapse formation (Gomez et al. 2021). Another neurexin ligand, neuroligin, was identified in the assembly, but its read counts were very low and so it is omitted here. A relatively larger number of DSCAM isoforms (5 DCAM1 and 20 DSCAM2) were discovered. DSCAM is required for preventing fasciculation and neuron self-avoidance, and in promoting tiling where neurons of the same type avoid overlap (Soba et al. 2007) (Fig. 8).

The cockroach CRG as a model system for synaptic plasticity and neurodegenerative disorders. Multiple isoforms (represented by numbers in parentheses) of Notch, Roundabout (ROBO), and Neurexin, as well as their activating ligands (Serrate, Delta, Jagged, and DNER for Notch; SLIT for Roundabout; and LRRTM for Neurexin) are expressed in the cockroach CNS. Cell adhesion and cell-cell interaction proteins are represented, including DSCAM, critical in neuron self-avoidance, and NCAM, involved in homophilic cell-cell interactions. Manipulation of these may allow for the use of the cockroach as an experimental system for synaptic plasticity. Additionally, the gene products implicated in or known to cause neurodegenerative disorders in humans are present, including Alzheimer’s disease (APPL and associated secreteases and binding protein), Parkinson’s disease (enzymes involved in mitochondrial maintenance), spinocerebellar ataxia, and Friedreich ataxia (various ataxins and frataxin, respectively). Phenotypes of mutants of these genes have been successfully studied in Drosophila, and thus experimentation may be adapted to the cockroach CNS. Figure generated using Biorender.com.

We also identify genes involved in human neurodegenerative disorders. These include amyloid precursor protein-like (APPL) and its associated secretases; parkin, PINK, DJ-1 and HTRA2 known to be involved in Parkinson’s disease; ataxins and frataxin known to be involved in spinocerebellar ataxia and Friedreich ataxia respectively; and huntingtin, important in Huntington’s disease. APPL is important for endosome regulation in the adult Drosophila brain, and mutations in its human homolog, APP, are a well-known cause of familial Alzheimer’s disease (Kessissoglou et al. 2020). Parkin, PINK, DJ-1, and HTRA2 are all critical enzymes in mitochondrial maintenance and are well established as targets for Parkinsonism in Drosophila (Whitworth 2011). Ataxins, frataxin, and huntingtin have diverse functions, but all have been implicated in neurodegenerative disorders, commonly through polyglutamine expansions, and have been successfully modeled in Drosophila (Fig. 8). Given these disease phenotypes have been successfully applied to Drosophila, P. americana may be a tractable system for research into mechanisms of neurodegenerative diseases.

Discussion

Transcriptome Assembly Benchmarks

Leveraging the strengths of individual de novo transcriptome assembly programs by combining the individual assemblies, then filtering by redundancy generates a final assembly that is more complete than any single program alone (Bairoch and Apweiler 1996, Gilbert 2019). Spades in single-cell mode was the most efficient in terms of completeness (89.3% of BUSCOs assembled) with minimal redundancy (11 duplicates), while Abyss with multiple k-mer assemblies merged was the most complete (94.8% of BUSCOs assembled), with over 106 transcripts and 858 duplicated BUSCOs, was highly redundant with many false positives. Velvet/Oases was very similar to Abyss (Supplementary Table S1) in terms of completeness, redundancy, and total number of transcripts. The Trinity plug-in TransDecoder was highly efficient at retaining the BUSCOs and by extension, presumably the majority of expressed proteinaceous gene products into a compact and mostly complete transcriptome.

Differential Expression Between Ganglia

The cerebral and GNG express the majority of transcripts statistically nondifferentially, but nevertheless have distinct gene expression profiles reflecting their separate functions. For example, the CRG’s executive functions serve to integrate sensory data, regulate circadian fluctuations, respond to internal energy cues, etc., then integrate these into permissive or inhibitory descending signals ultimately culminating in behavior. These signals must pass through the GNG, which controls posture and walking coordination and is also implicated in the initiation of spontaneous walking (Emanuel et al. 2020). The repertoire of ganglia-specific genes reflects these diverse functions. Some examples include a nicotinic acetylcholine receptor and metabotropic glutamate receptor 8 specific to the GNG, while the CRG controls nutrient sensing and energy homeostasis through AKH and insulin-related peptide (Nelson et al. 2021, Ratnaparkhi and Sudhakaran 2022). Burs. and PBurs., the monomers for the heterodimer Bursicon, are differentially more highly expressed in the GNG. The Bursicon receptor, rickets, was not detected; however, three isoforms of a close homolog of rickets, the leucine-rich repeat-containing G-protein coupled receptor 1 (Lgr1) were found. Bursicon is known to be critical in cuticle hardening (Dewey et al. 2004), but its role in the mature adult, particularly secreted from the GNG is not well established. The D1- and D2- like dopamine receptors are expressed in less abundance than the other monoamine GPCRs, and only in the CRG. The lower number may be reflective of the relatively small number of dopaminergic neurons (approximately 250), which are concentrated in the antennal lobes, mushroom bodies, and central complex (Hamanaka et al. 2016). Dopaminergic signaling has been established as critical for feeding and grooming behavior in the cockroach, particularly in the central complex (Marg et al. 2004, Allen et al. 2011, Kaiser and Libersat 2015). In contrast, the dopamine/ecdysteroid receptor (DopEcR) is expressed in both ganglia, though statistically more highly in the CRG. The rate-limiting enzyme in dopamine synthesis is TH, which is expressed in the GNG (Fig. 5B) and may indicate a functional dopamine circuit in the GNG without expression of D1-like or D2-like dopamine receptors.

Most Abundant Functional Domains in the Transcriptome

InterProScan identified C2H2 zinc finger (IPR013087) as the most abundant domain by far, with 1,904 assignments to transcripts (Supplementary Fig. S2). These structural domains are common in transcription factors in eukaryotes, and this finding is consistent with a study demonstrating a member of this family is required for limb regeneration in P. americana where they found C2H2 domain-containing zinc finger transcription factors were the most abundant (Ren et al. 2024). The second-most identified InterPro domain in the transcriptome is the reverse transcriptase domain (IPR000477) with 553 transcripts containing this domain, indicating a significant number of active transposons. In the Drosophila brain, active transposons generate genetic heterogeneity and may influence the properties of certain neurons in the mushroom bodies (Perrat et al. 2013). The presence of active transposable elements may indicate increased expression but is not necessarily proportional to mobilization (Treiber and Waddell 2017). A study in Drosophila neurons found that putative transposon insertions can be a result from library preparation or other artifacts and not bona fide somatic events at near equivalent rates (Treiber and Waddell 2017). Additionally, it was shown that certain active transposons are unique to subsets of cells depending on whether those cells also express the required machinery (Treiber and Waddell 2017). Nevertheless, the abundant presence of these domains in the transcriptome is compelling, suggesting roles in cryptic alternative splicing, neuroplasticity and heterogeneity, and adaptability (Reilly et al. 2013, Treiber and Waddell 2020). This domain has been found in many eukaryotic model organisms, but significantly more hits were found in P. americana, even compared to other cockroaches (Supplementary Fig. S5).

GPCRs, RTKs, and Their Ligands

GPCRs represent the most diverse family of receptors and control a vast array of physiological and behavior phenomena. All four major classes of GPCRs are represented: class A (rhodopsin-like), class B (secretin-like), class C (mGluR-like) and class F (frizzled-like). Many receptors of small molecule neurotransmitters were identified, including the monoamine serotonin, dopamine tyramine and octopamine receptors, as well as acetylcholine receptors, glutamate (mGluRs), and GABAB receptors. While many GPCR ligands, such as neuropeptides, are genetically encoded and can be identified through homology searches, we searched for key enzymes in the synthesis of the small molecules as a proxy to localize their synthesis. For example, TPH, TH, and glutamate decarboxylase are commonly used to identify serotonergic, dopaminergic, and GABAergic neurons, respectively (Kolodziejczyk and Nässel 2011). The pattern of synthesis for the monoamines generally follows the receptors, except for dopamine, where the D1- and D2- like dopamine receptors are localized only to the CRG, but TH is expressed in both the GRG and GNG, though more highly in CRG (Padj = 0.04). Two serotonin receptors (5HT1B and 5HT7 homologs) are differentially expressed more highly in the CRG, consistent with TPH (Padj = 0.006). Tyramine synthesis is differentially expressed more highly in the GRG, and the tyramine receptor is also expressed more highly in the GNG (Padj = 0.007), however, the other tyramine responding receptor, octopamine-tyramine receptor (Oct-TyrR), is slightly more highly expressed in the CRG, though not significantly so (Padj = 0.2). The Drosophila protein, henna, can also hydroxylate tryptophan to 5-hydroxytryptophan and phenylalanine to tyrosine, which can contribute to the generation of serotonin or to tyrosine, which can further be modified to dopamine, but its expression appears restricted to dopamine neurons in Drosophila (Huser et al. 2012).

Many neuropeptide receptors with broad neuromodulatory functions are also present, including tachykinin, pyrokinin, leucokinin, and corazonin, among others. Seven isoforms of CCHamide receptor 1 and one isoform of CCHamide receptor 2 were found, which are implicated in feeding behavior and response to starvation in Drosophila (Farhan et al. 2013, Ren et al. 2015). The majority of the class B receptors found in the transcriptome are adhesion GPCRs which also include the latrophilin Cirl and flamingo (also known as starry night in Drosophila), and Methuselah receptors. The adhesion family GPCRs (aGPCR) contain N-terminal GAIN domain which can undergo autoprotolysis spontaneously upon trafficking to the membrane, and after cleavage, the N-terminal fragment remains important in ligand binding and signal transduction regulation. These receptors play a diverse role in neuronal function, including synapse formation, mechanosensation, nociception, and development, besides their role in cell-cell adhesion and extracellular matrix interactions (Scholz et al. 2015, Lala and Hall 2022, Vieira Contreras et al. 2024). The related Methuselah receptors, so named because their mutants have extended lifespans, are important in response to stressors. They appear to be promiscuous in response to activating ligands, which include stunted, sex peptide, and SPAM (Ja et al. 2009). Small molecule antagonism of Methuselah demonstrated that Methuselah signaling is upstream of the target of rapamycin (TOR), and that subsequent decrease in TOR activity was responsible for the extended lifespan via the concomitant decrease in the ERK pathway (Wang et al. 2015). This is not dissimilar from the nutrient deprivation-induced lifespan extension associated with a decrease in insulin receptor activation of TOR (Partridge et al. 2011).

Perhaps the most well-known class C GPCRs are the metabotropic glutamate receptor (mGluR) and the GABAB receptor, which respond to the excitatory neurotransmitter glutamate and the inhibitory neurotransmitter GABA, respectively. Each are well represented in the transcriptome, with six mGluR isoforms and three GABAB isoforms. There are two additional Class C receptors that cluster well together, a smog homolog, and a homolog to the orphan receptor CG31760, which we are calling CG31760-like. The smog receptor, whose activating ligand is Fog, works synergistically with the fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR) receptor heartless to regulate embryonic CNS development in Drosophila (Shweta et al. 2021a, 2021b). The Class F GPCRs frizzled (three isoforms) and smoothened (one isoform) were found in the transcriptome, which are involved in Wnt and hedgehog signaling, respectively, and both are critical in cell proliferation and development. Wnt (wingless in Drosophila) is the activating ligand for frizzled receptors. Interestingly, Wnt5 and Frizzled 2 mediate axonal extension and stabilization in the adult Drosophila brain (Srahna et al. 2006).

RTKs are mediators of signals for survival, proliferation, and metabolic regulation. Sixteen RTKs were found in the transcriptome. ALK and its ligand, jelly belly (jeb), were identified and expressed in both ganglia. The ALK is part of the insulin receptor superfamily and its mutation in humans in implicated in many cancers (Hallberg and Palmer 2013). In Drosophila, ALK is required for development of internal structures during embryogenesis, photoreceptor axon guidance and neuroprotection from nutrient deprivation during pupation and is implicated in maintaining circadian rhythms in adult flies (Kumar Sukumar et al. 2024). Two Discoidin domain-containing receptors were identified (DDR1 and DDR2), whose major ligand is collagen in mammalian homologs. The DDRs are poorly characterized in insects, but conservation suggests that collagen or other extracellular matrix components may be activating ligands (Sopko and Perrimon 2013). Additionally, the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) and two putative ligands were identified. EGFR controls a variety of critical functions, including cell survival and proliferation. EGFR signaling in adults regulates sleep (Foltenyi et al. 2007) and its dysregulation leads to age-related neurodegeneration (Botella et al. 2003). A FGFR and two putative ligands, FGF and a homolog to branchless (Bnl) in Drosophila were also identified. While EGFR and FRGR receptors and cognate ligands are well conserved between humans and insects, the homolog to the human vascular endothelial growth factor receptor (VEGFR) and the platelet derived growth factor (PDGFR) is PDGF- and VEGF-receptor related (PVR). We found three PVR receptors and five Pvfs (Pfv-A though Pfv-H to discriminate from Drosophila Pdf1-3), which are putative ligands of the PVRs. The cockroach Pvf-A is most similar to the Drosophila Pvf1, which was identified to control cell migration in oogenesis and neuroblast survival (Duchek et al. 2001, Read 2018). Pvf-B clusters distinctly with Drosophila Pvf3, the remaining Pvfs (Pvf-C-Pvf-F) cluster with Pvf1; there are no identified homologs for Drosophila Pvf2 herein. Ephrin and its receptor are predominantly expressed in the nervous system in developing Drosophila larva and are important for axon targeting in optic ganglia and the mushroom bodies; both are expressed in the transcriptome (Sopko and Perrimon 2013).

Two insulin receptors and five insulin-related peptides were identified. Insulin is released in response to increasing nutrient status and is repressed under fasting and starvation. In Drosophila, insulin-producing cells (IPCs) in the pars intercerebralis secrete insulin-like peptides in response to not only nutrient status but also inputs from serotonergic and octopaminergic signaling and are suppressed during locomotion (Luo et al. 2005, Liessem et al. 2023). Nine insulin-like peptides have been identified in P. americana, coined short IGF-related peptides (SIRPs), and five located to the pars intercerebralis (Veenstra 2023). While the vast majority of read counts for the SIRPs are expectedly in the CRG, there are nonzero levels of transcript in the GNG. Interestingly, SIRP-5 is differentially expressed more highly in the GNG. The insulin receptor isoforms are expressed in both ganglia, with INSR-1 relatively high with respect to other RTKs. The “inactive” ROR1 receptor is so named because its kinase domain, often referred to as a pseudokinase domain, has low or nondetectable activity (Petrova et al. 2014); however, it appears to continue to be important in neurite outgrowth and synapse formation (Green et al. 2014). A homolog of the Drosophila RTK sevenless (Sev) and its ligand bride of sevenless (Boss) were identified. Sevenless is among the most highly expressed RTKs in both ganglia, and its ligand, boss, is differentially expressed more highly in the CRG. Boss has a seven transmembrane domain structure, similar to GPCRs, and mediates paracrine activity of sevenless in retinal development in Drosophila, specifically the R7 photoreceptor cells (Tomlinson et al. 2019). To our knowledge, not much is known about the role of sevenless in the adult insect brain, which is compelling given the relatively high expression of both sevenless and boss. The Venus kinase is an interesting receptor with a GABAB-like Venus flytrap (VFT) extracellular domain and an insulin receptor-like intercellular kinase domain, and whose ligand appears to be L-arginine. Venus kinase has been implicated in reproduction in Anopheles (Vanderstraete et al. 2013) and Aedes mosquitoes (Vogel et al. 2015), but is remarkably absent in D. melanogaster. Venus kinase has also been found in the desert locust Schistocerca gregaria, Tribolium, and Apis, and even sea urchins and the human parasite Schistosoma mansoni (Ahier et al. 2009, Lenaerts et al. 2017). Similar to sevenless, to our knowledge, there is no known role for Venus kinase in an adult insect nervous system, and so it is an exciting target for functional analysis.

Developmental Pathways Persisting into the Mature Adult Stage

Innate immune system activation within the CNS is critical to protect against infection and injury, but excessive or chronic signaling may be damaging. The term “inflammaging” has been coined to describe persistently increased levels of cytokines that activate NF-κB signaling pathways (Johnson et al. 2013). Toll and its ligand Spätzle were originally discovered in Drosophila (Anderson et al. 1985, Morisato and Anderson 1994, Lemaitre et al. 1996), but subsequently multiple medically relevant Toll-like receptors were identified in mammals, many of which are part of pathogen sensing and inducing inflammatory phenomena and have chemically diverse ligands (Kawasaki and Kawai 2014). Innate immunity-triggering receptors, peptidoglycan recognition proteins (PGRPs; 16 isoforms) involved in the IMD pathway (Dziarski and Gupta 2006), Toll (1 isoform) and Toll-like (7 total isoforms, including Toll-like 2, 6, 7, and Tollo) receptors are present. Toll and its ligand Spätzle are crucial in embryogenesis and are activated by a protease cascade that is different from the cascade triggered by the presence of pathogens and involves the proteases Snake, Easter, and Gd (Lye and Chtarbanova 2018). This specific cascade is well represented in the transcriptome and may indicate other aspects of Toll signaling outside of immunity and development. Spätzle, a cytokine and neurotrophin (Lewis et al. 2013), may be involved in maintaining homeostasis in the CNS in adult P. americana, as Spätzle activation of Toll supports neuron survival and axon guidance and synaptic plasticity in larval Drosophila (Zhu et al. 2008). The serine protease, nudel, is required for activation of Gd during embryogenesis, but a candidate homolog of nudel was not found in the transcriptome (Cho et al. 2010). However, it has not been shown that Nudel directly reacts with Gd, and in fact, under certain conditions in vitro, Gd may auto-activate, and even mature Snake and Easter may activate the zymogen Gd to a mature protease (Stein et al. 2013). Thus, the absence of a functional Nudel does not preclude the activation of Gd, particularly if not spatially restricted. Interestingly, the processing of Easter by Snake appears dependent on Pipe, but not the processing of Snake or Gd, and the functional expression of Pipe requires Windbeutel, indicating a more indirect or circuitous pathway than a direct linear protease cascade (Cho et al. 2010). Future work will address questions regarding Toll signaling being involved in neurogenesis or synaptic plasticity in adult cockroaches. Recent findings also demonstrate the roles of juvenile hormone (JH) and ecdysis triggering hormone (ETH) in adult Drosophila in reproduction and stress response (Meiselman et al. 2018). Juvenile hormone acid O-methyltransferase (JHAMT; 4 isoforms) and methyl farnesoate epoxidase (6 isoforms) are expressed in both ganglia, suggesting the presence of a redundant and critical pathway for juvenile hormone. The RTK torso and its ligand PTTH are expressed specifically in the CRG, while torso-like is specific in the GNG. Both torso and torso-like are known to be important in embryonic development (Rewitz et al. 2009, Johnson et al. 2013). Additionally, torso-like has been shown to act synergistically with torso upon activation by the torso ligand trunk, however, the role of torso-like in the activation of torso under PTTH activation remains unclear (T. K. Johnson et al. 2015).

Gluconeogenesis and Trehaloneogenesis Pathways are Expressed in Both Ganglia

The glycolytic pathway converts glucose to pyruvate, which then enters the citric acid cycle to generate sufficient ATP to power the ganglia (Volkenhoff et al. 2015). In Drosophila, the glia that compose the blood-brain barrier take up glucose (as trehalose) from the hemolymph and convert it through glycolysis to pyruvate. Pyruvate, through either transamination or reduction, is converted to alanine or lactate, which are then secreted by the glial cell (Volkenhoff et al. 2015). The neurons then take up alanine and lactate and convert these back to pyruvate to enter the citric acid cycle (Fig. 7 and Supplementary Fig. S4) (Rabah et al. 2023). Neuronal gluconeogenesis has been reported in a small number of CRG neurons in Drosophila, acting as a sensor for blood sugar, leading to peptidergic endocrine signaling to stimulate the fat body (analogous to mammalian liver) and Malpighian tubules (analogous to mammalian kidneys) to maintain nominal energy homeostasis (Miyamoto and Amrein 2019). The read counts for the gluconeogenic and trehaloneogenic enzymes in P. americana are comparable in number to those in the glycolytic pathway. While it is impossible to know how many neurons or glia are expressing these enzymes from these data, it appears more widespread than was reported in Drosophila, and are expressed in both ganglia, not just the CRG. Future work will aim to assess if these pathways are serving a function as a blood sugar sensor for the brain alone, or for the whole organism (similar to the mammalian pancreas), or if they are expressed highly and broadly enough to provide sufficient energy for the CRG per se. In contrast, the CRG and GNG express several isoforms of the Methuselah receptor (Mth), which is activated by stunted, a moonlighting peptide identical to the ε subunit of the F1 ATP synthase. Stunted is secreted by the fat body in response to increased hemolymph nutrients after feeding and induces Mth-expressing neurons to release insulin-related peptides (Delanoue et al. 2016). Thus, nutrient-level endocrine signaling between the brain and body and vice versa is well established in the transcriptome.

Expanding the Role of the Cockroach as a Model System in Synaptic Plasticity and Human Neurodegenerative Disorders

Drosophila melanogaster has been used successfully as a model system to probe the biochemical basis of learning and memory as well as the molecular basis of several neurodegenerative disorders (Chan and Bonini 2000). Drosophila expresses homologs for approximately 75% of neurodegenerative diseases (Pandey and Nichols 2011). P. americana expresses these same relevant homologs and thus may be used as another tool to understand the complex interactions of neurons with each other and their environment on a biochemical level, as well as exploring the conditions when these processes break down. Isoforms of the amyloid precursor-like protein (APPL), ADAM10 (an α-secretase), β-secretase, γ-secretase, and presenilin are expressed in this cockroach. Mutations in these genes are known to cause Alzheimer’s disease in humans, and their associated disease phenotypes have been successfully modeled in Drosophila (Sofola et al. 2010, Fernandez-Funez et al. 2015, Jeon et al. 2020). Further, γ-secretase is required for frizzled receptor 2 (Fz2) cleavage to promote postsynaptic maturation (Restrepo et al. 2022). Parkinson’s disease can be caused by mutations in mitochondrial quality-control enzymes PINK (PTEN-induced kinase) and Parkin (an E3 ubiquitin ligase) (Whitworth 2011, Song et al. 2013, Tufi et al. 2014). Additionally, mutations in the serine protease HTRA2 (M’Angale and Staveley 2017) and protein deglycase DJ-1 (Hao et al. 2010) can lead to mitochondrial dysfunction and susceptibility to Parkinson’s disease phenotype in Drosophila (Whitworth 2011). Further, dopamine signaling perturbation leads to ataxia in Drosophila, which has parallels with Parkinson’s Disease (Draper et al. 2007). Several ataxins and frataxin were identified in the transcriptome, whose mutants cause spinocerebellar ataxia and Friedreich’s ataxia in humans. Ataxin-2 is also implicated in Parkinson’s disease (Laffita-Mesa et al. 2021). Mutations in ataxins associated with disease are polyglutamine expansions, except in ataxin-10 and frataxin, where disease is associated with a pentanucleotide expansion and an intronic expansion, respectively (Sullivan et al. 2019, Doni et al. 2023). Similarly, Huntington’s disease is caused by a polyglutamine expansion in the Huntingtin protein. Spinocerebellar ataxias associated with specific ataxins, Friedreich’s ataxia, and Huntington’s disease have all been modeled in Drosophila (Al-Ramahi et al. 2007, Krench and Littleton 2013, Calap-Quintana et al. 2018, S. L. Johnson et al. 2019, Sujkowski et al. 2024). Since these neurodegenerative diseases have been successfully modeled in Drosophila and given these homologs are found within the cockroach CNS, the cockroach may become useful as a model system to probe the molecular basis of these diseases (Fig. 8). P. americana may be particularly beneficial over Drosophila in some cases, considering the cockroach’s significantly longer lifespan, which will allow for a longer time course to observe neurodegeneration and for interventions. The current powerful genetic tools in Drosophila are not available (e.g., GAL4-UAS system), and in many cases cannot be developed in cockroaches, e.g., PhiC31 integrase for site-specific integration or MiMIC Minos-Mediated Integration Cassette sites, for instance. While the CRISPR-CAS9 system has been demonstrated in cockroaches, the long developmental time and requirement for back-crossing can render it impractical (Shirai et al. 2022). However, the cockroach is susceptible to RNA interference by injection of dsRNA into the head capsule or hemolymph (Garbutt et al. 2013, French et al. 2015). Thus, with the cDNA sequences given herein, any of the disease-relevant genes may be silenced, cloned, or targeted pharmacologically for in vitro and in vivo studies.

Study Limitations and Future Directions

There are several caveats that should be considered when interpreting these data. First, these data represent only one snapshot in time, reflecting the timing of the dissection, and are limited to only adult males, so sexual dimorphism in gene expression cannot be determined. Second, we cannot discriminate between gene expression of cellular subtypes or locations in individual ganglia, i.e., within glia or neurons, within the central complex, or antennal lobes, etc. Lastly, to narrow the scope of the study, we have omitted several critical gene families in these data, such as ion channels and kinase cascades (among many others), which will be the subject of future characterization. Future work aims to address these questions through single-cell transcriptomics approaches and functional analyses in primary cell culture derived from the CNS. This assembly can serve to open many experimental avenues in using P. americana as a model system in modern neuroscience research, including neuronal circuitry elucidation, development of a brain-injury response and repair model, and a novel experimental platform of studies in several human neurodegenerative disorders.

The assembly, annotation, and quantification of the cephalic ganglia transcriptome give insight into differential expression between the CRG and GNG, as well as identifies the presence of diverse signaling pathways. This includes the developmental Toll pathway, from the protease zymogens to the transcription factor Dorsal. The gluconeogenic and trehaloneogenic metabolic pathways are present in both ganglia and comparable in expression to the glycolytic enzymes. This indicates the pathways may be more active in the CNS than previously appreciated. As a database, these data lay the foundation for functional genetics studies using high-throughput sequencing technologies while also providing potential for targeted hypothesis-driven in vivo studies. Homologs of gene products important in human neurodegenerative diseases were identified in the P. americana CNS, such as Parkin, APP-like, and Huntingtin, which may allow for the development of a new model system for mechanistic analyses into these diseases. Further, the significantly longer lifespan of the cockroach allows for studies in neurodevelopment and plasticity, as well as the assessment of the efficacy of therapeutics or interventions in disease models over a much longer time frame than is available in Drosophila. The differential expression profiles herein yield a greater insight into the diversity of signaling infrastructure and metabolism within and between the cephalic ganglia and a clearer appreciation of how the distinct functions of these ganglia vary on a molecular level.
