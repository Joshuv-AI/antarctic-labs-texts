Sociodemographic and Clinical Profile of Adult Males With Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy
Authors: Manju Jayasimha Pula Jayaram, Hollie A. Clark, Natalie Street, Yining Yang, Shannon Kilburn, Shiny Thomas, Christina Westfield, Katherine D. Mathews, Emma Ciafaloni, Aida Soim, Muscular Dystrophy Surveillance, Tracking, and Research Network (MD STAR
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction/Aims:Due to improvements in clinical care, individuals with Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) are living into adulthood, but little has been published about adults with DMD. We describe key characteristics of adults with DMD using US population-based surveillance data.Methods:Muscular Dystrophy Surveillance Tracking and Research network (MD STARnet) data were used to describe sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of living and deceased adult males with DMD, born since January 1, 1982, who were ≥ 18 years old at last healthcare visit, using healthcare visit data through 2016. Frequencies and percentages were used to describe the distribution of characteristics, and ages at key milestones were described using medians and interquartile ranges.Results:As of December 31, 2016, of 173 adult males, 61.8% were living. At their last visit, median age was 22.5 years and 35.3% had completed some college. Over 75% were on noninvasive ventilation, over 70% had cardiomyopathy, and 83.8% were prescribed cardiac medications. Corticosteroids were used by 67.1%, including 8.7% as of last clinic visit/death. Public insurance was more common among deceased (66.7%) than living (52.3%). Cough assist was used by 82.2% of living versus 57.6% of deceased individuals.Discussion:Except for cough assist and insurance type, sociodemographic and clinical characteristics were similar between the living and deceased. Low continuation of corticosteroids into adulthood needs interpretation in the context of the study's timeframe. These results can assist clinicians in anticipating the care needs of adults with DMD.

Introduction

In the last few decades, improvements in medical care such as noninvasive ventilation (NIV) [1-3], better cardiac management, corticosteroids, and scoliosis surgery [2, 4, 5] have improved the life expectancy in Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) up to the age of 40 years [6, 7]. Given the increasing number of individuals with DMD who are living into adulthood [1, 3, 6-11], it is important to better understand these individuals' health status. The transition from adolescence to adulthood marks a critical juncture in the lives of those with DMD, as they confront a unique combination of social and clinical challenges, including social isolation, lack of access to employment, and limited transition-related informational resources for adolescents and parents [12]. While the natural history and clinical progression of DMD in childhood are extensively documented, literature on the trajectories and challenges encountered by individuals with DMD as they transition into adulthood and during their adult life is relatively limited [12-14]. This knowledge gap not only impedes our understanding of the needs of this emerging population and long-term health implications of individuals with DMD but also hinders the development of tailored interventions and support systems to address their evolving needs. Most studies reporting on the sociodemographic and health outcomes for adult males with DMD are clinic-based or based outside of the US [8, 15-21]. The Muscular Dystrophy Surveillance Tracking and Research Network (MD STARnet) provides a unique opportunity to describe the growing population of adults with DMD using a US population-based surveillance system. Our aim was to describe sociodemographic and clinical characteristics by vital status in adult males with DMD.

Methods

Study Population

MD STARnet is a Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC)-funded population-based surveillance system for muscular dystrophies (MDs) that captures data from specialty clinics, hospital discharge systems, and birth and death certificates of individuals diagnosed with MDs and living in select US surveillance sites [22]. The detailed surveillance protocol and case classification process have been previously described [23]. Retrospective data from three sites that followed individuals with DMD or Becker muscular dystrophy (BMD) between 1982 and 2016 were used for this analysis. Eligible individuals were born since January 1, 1982, diagnosed before 21 years of age, and resided in the surveillance sites of Colorado (CO), Iowa (IA), and 12 counties of western New York (WNY). These sites obtained institutional review board approval (WNY) or exemption (CO, IA), and had public health authority to conduct MD surveillance.

Study Cohort

To derive the study cohort (N = 173), we applied inclusion and exclusion criteria based on case status, phenotypic classification, and age (Figure 1). MD STARnet applies a set of criteria to define eligible cases of DMD and BMD [24]. We included those with a definite or probable case status. A definite case had dystrophinopathy-related symptoms including elevated creatine kinase (CK) and diagnosis by DNA analysis or muscle biopsy in an individual or their x-linked relative [24]. A probable case had dystrophinopathy-related symptoms and elevated CK with family history of DMD X-linked inheritance [24]. Phenotypic classification was based on age at motor symptom/sign onset, ambulation status, steroid usage, and western blot/genetic phenotype; we limited further inclusions to those with phenotypic classifications of high, medium, and low DMD indicating certainty level for DMD [25]. Those excluded had either a BMD or unclassifiable phenotype. Finally, we excluded individuals < 18 years old at last clinic visit.

Study Variables

MD STARnet collects vital status information from medical records and death certificates. MD STARnet data through 2011 were linked to state death certificates and the National Death Index (NDI). Follow-up data from 2012 to 2016 were linked to state death certificates only as NDI linkage had not previously identified many additional deaths. We categorized last known vital status as living and deceased. Last clinic visit included visits to a medical provider, including primary care provider, neuromuscular, and other specialty care providers.

MD STARnet collects information on race/ethnicity of the individual and parents from medical records and birth certificates and on education, employment, and health insurance from medical records. To maintain confidentiality, for race/ethnicity, education, and age variables, we collapsed categories with cell sizes < 5 into larger categories. Race/ethnicity was combined into the following categories: non-Hispanic White, Hispanic or Latino, Other, and Unknown. Other included non-Hispanic Black or African American, American Indian or Alaska Native, Asian, Native Hawaiian or Pacific Islander, and multiple race.

We used the last available data points for health insurance, education, and employment status. For health insurance, we used the most recent information and classified data as private, public (Medicaid, Medicare, Child Health Plan, Veteran's Affairs/military), private and public, none, and unknown. Education refers to the highest level of education known; data on education type (mainstream, special education, college, and other) and grade level were combined into the categories of incomplete high school, completed high school/general educational development (GED), post high school training/transition program, in college/completed college or graduate degree, and unknown. For employment, we categorized the most recent data found on employment status as employed (defined as working part time, working but full time/part time status unknown, or volunteering), unemployed (defined as able and looking for work), or disabled/unable to work. The disabled/unable to work categorization was based on available medical record data under employment, and we did not collect additional information to ascertain why the person was not able to work. No one in our cohort was working full time.

MD STARnet collects information on diagnoses (cardiomyopathy, fractures, neurobehavioral issues) and interventions (respiratory support, surgeries, gastrostomy, cardiac and steroid medications) from medical records. Cardiomyopathy diagnoses were based on findings of shortening fraction less than 28% and/or ejection fraction less than 55%. For fractures, we included the first documented fracture of the spine, wrist, arm, leg, foot, ankle, clavicle, or other area. For neurobehavioral issues, MD STARnet collects medical record data from care providers' diagnosis notes and maps these into predefined categories of anxiety disorders, depression, and behavioral problems. If interventions such as counseling, referral, neurobehavioral or neurocognitive exam, pharmacological treatment, or psychotherapy were recommended or completed for the documented neurobehavioral issue, we considered these true diagnoses and included the first identified issues. Behavioral problems included aggression, anger management, conduct problems, and externalizing behaviors [26]. For respiratory interventions, surgeries, and gastrostomy, we included first dates at which they were mentioned in the medical record. NIV included bilevel positive airway pressure (BiPAP), continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP), and noninvasive mechanical ventilation (face mask, sip). Gastrostomy included percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG) tube and gastrostomy (G) tubes. Cardiac medications included angiotensin converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitors, angiotensin receptor antagonists, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, and beta blockers. For corticosteroids, we assessed whether individuals had ever used corticosteroids (prednisone, prednisolone, deflazacort, or an unspecified corticosteroid), the age at first use, and use as of the last clinic visit.

Statistical Analyses

We performed a descriptive analysis, calculating frequencies and percentages for sociodemographic and clinical characteristics. For the age at which a clinical milestone was first reported, we calculated measures of central tendency (median and interquartile range [IQR]). For calculating medians, we used birth dates and the respective dates of death, diagnoses and interventions. A p value < 0.05 was used to determine statistically significant comparisons of the frequencies and median ages observed between the living and deceased groups. Analyses were conducted using SAS v9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC) and were replicated using R v4.4.0.

Results

Sociodemographic Profile

Sociodemographic characteristics of the deceased, living, and entire study cohort are described in Table 1. (Data described here for median, IQR, and range are not shown in the table). At the last clinic visit, the study cohort (N = 173) had a median age of 22.5 years (IQR = 20.0, 25.4 range = 18.0, 34.4). Over a third (38.2%) were deceased, with a median age at death of 22.6 years (IQR = 20.1, 24.1 range = 18.6, 31.4). The median age at last clinic visit among those who were still living was 23.1 years (IQR = 20.2, 26.1 range = 18.0, 34.4). Over 90% of deceased individuals were born during 1982–1993 as compared to about 60% of living individuals born during the same birth years. Differences were observed between the living and deceased for type of health insurance coverage, highest known level of education accomplished, and last known employment status.

Clinical Profile

Table 2 describes clinical diagnoses and interventions. In our cohort, 57.8% received NIV only, 19.7% received NIV followed by invasive ventilation, 19.1% did not receive ventilation and the remainder had either received invasive ventilation followed by NIV or received invasive ventilation only (data not shown). Percentage of individuals prescribed respiratory interventions and ages at initiation were similar between living and deceased, except for cough assist, which was higher in those living than in those deceased.

Median age and percentage of those reaching cardiovascular, musculoskeletal, gastrointestinal, and neurobehavioral clinical milestones were similar between deceased and living individuals. Over two-thirds had used corticosteroids, and 12.9% (8.7% of cohort) of those individuals were using corticosteroids as of last clinic visit/death.

Discussion

Overall, the percentage and median age at reaching most clinical milestones was similar between living and deceased individuals. However, we found a higher percentage of deceased individuals had only public insurance while living individuals had a higher percentage of both private and public. Additionally, we found a higher percentage of living individuals had initiated cough assist devices than deceased individuals. The median age for reaching all clinical milestones and interventions was younger than 20 years, except for invasive ventilation and gastrostomy.

Mortality

When compared with two previous MD STARnet studies, median age at death in our study was about a year older than Pandya et al. [14] and a year younger than Paramsothy et al. [27]. Our results differ from other US and international studies of adults with DMD, which reported lower [2] or higher percentages of deceased individuals [3, 28] or older mean and median ages at death [2, 3, 28]. Differences in attributes of the study populations (e.g., age of study cohort, single center-based study) could account for differences in median age at death [2, 3, 14, 28].

Health Insurance

The higher percentages of public insurance among the deceased, and public and private insurance among the living, warrant further examination of sociodemographic or healthcare use factors. One study found a higher comorbidity burden and higher resource use among the Medicaid cohort than the commercial cohort in the US DMD population [29]. However, investigators were unable to distinguish individuals that had only public health insurance from those who had public and private insurance.

Education and Employment

Our finding of a higher percentage of at least some college among the living may be explained as ages at death for 50% of those who died coincided with undergraduate college education age range. In comparison with Japanese and Danish studies, our study had lower [21] or similar high school completion [18] but higher attendance/completion of college/graduate school [21].

Employment levels in our cohort were higher when compared to US and Danish studies [18, 30] and lower when compared to the Japanese and Dutch studies [17, 21]. However, in all the studies, employment levels were generally low. Employment status was often not found in medical records.

Respiratory Interventions

Use of cough assist devices in our study was within the range found by other studies [14, 20, 27]. Median age at initiation of cough assist device was over a year earlier than a previous MD STARnet study that had an older cohort of individuals with DMD [14]. Our finding of higher usage of cough assist device among the living is consistent with a previous MD STARnet study that reported a 45% lower hazard of death for cough assist users [27]. Compared to cross-sectional studies of adult males with DMD performed in the United States, Italy, Japan, the Netherlands, and Denmark, we found a higher proportion of NIV and a mid-range proportion of invasive ventilation [14, 17, 18, 21, 31]. Studies reporting on the median age at initiation for both noninvasive and invasive ventilation are scarce. Our cohort had an earlier median age at starting NIV, when compared to the US and French studies (18 to 18.3 years) [3, 14]. Our study had a higher proportion of assisted ventilation compared to that reported by Koeks et al. study for those aged ≥ 20 years [18].

Cardiomyopathy and Cardiac Management

Our findings for cardiomyopathy were similar to or higher than those of the other US and international studies [14, 16, 31, 32]. Median age at cardiomyopathy for our cohort was 1.5 years older than the previous MD STARnet study [14]. Cardiac medication usage in our cohort was within the range found by previous studies of adults with DMD [2, 14, 17, 20, 27, 28, 31, 33]. Our finding of earlier median age at starting cardiac medications in living vs. deceased did not reach significance. Studies have shown that earlier initiation of cardiac medications delays the onset of cardiomyopathy and improves survival; prophylactic initiation of cardiac medications is recommended to delay the onset of cardiomyopathy [34-37].

Scoliosis and Gastrostomy

Scoliosis surgery has been reported to improve survival [27]. Our scoliosis surgery percentage was similar to [19] or higher than other US and international studies [14, 16, 27, 32]. In United States and other international studies that reported on percentages of gastrostomy among adolescent and adult males with DMD, our results were within the range of other studies [14, 17, 32, 33, 38].

Corticosteroids

We reported a higher proportion of having ever used corticosteroids than other studies [2, 14, 32]. Despite findings from studies that have shown benefits from continuing corticosteroid treatment after the loss of ambulation [39, 40], a low proportion of our study cohort still used corticosteroids into adulthood. This finding is also lower than what was reported in previous studies [14, 16]. Our findings for corticosteroids (continuing usage and never used) can be understood in the context of our study, which spanned several decades during which corticosteroid acceptance increased significantly, and given that most findings of the beneficial effects of continued corticosteroid use were published after our data collection years.

Neurobehavioral Issues

Our findings on the percentage of adults with neurobehavioral issues are similar to a previous MD STARnet study of males of all ages with DMD [26]. Although not reaching significance, the 3-year earlier and 1-year earlier median ages at diagnosis of depression and behavioral problems in the deceased compared to the living point towards the possible role of factors associated with earlier death (e.g., disease progression, additional comorbidities). The percentage with depression was higher and anxiety lower than in two international studies [20, 41].

Limitations

A limitation of the study is that results may not be generalizable to DMD populations outside of the geographic areas covered in this study. In addition, the primary data source for MD STARnet is medical records. Challenges associated with medical record abstraction include difficulty in accessing older or non-electronic records. Documentation errors can be made by healthcare staff, and some variables (e.g., education, employment status) are not consistently collected in healthcare settings or documented in medical records. Education and employment data were incomplete and need to be cautiously interpreted. Incomplete data poses challenges in interpreting our findings. This study did not report all potential clinical manifestations and interventions in individuals with DMD but focused on the most common that aligned with the 2010 DMD Care Considerations [37]. Data on certain aspects of clinical care, particularly those that may be more relevant as life expectancy increases, were either not collected or were not frequent enough to report.

Conclusion

Key findings from our study include higher usage of cough assist among living adults with DMD and higher percentage of only public insurance among deceased individuals. The finding of lower continuing corticosteroid use should be interpreted in the context of data collection years, which preceded new evidence for benefits of continuing corticosteroids after loss of ambulation. As of 2016, our multi-site study results provide a reference for understanding the characteristics of adult males with DMD, especially those aged 18 to < 28 years (age range of nearly 90% of our cohort). Care for DMD continues to evolve; care guidelines are widely adopted, and new medications are being introduced. Understanding the impact of these changes will require ongoing surveillance and reporting, which are expected to aid in counseling and in planning to meet the needs of this growing population. MD STARnet is well-positioned to track the longitudinal changes in this growing population.
