An integrated assessment of climate change on landscape adaptive capacity, vulnerability, and divergence in Avicennia species
Authors: Masoud Sheidai, Laleh Malekmohammadi, Fahimeh Koohdar

ABSTRACT
AbstractClimate change poses a major threat to biodiversity and is accelerating the risk of extinction for rare and ecologically important plant species. To mitigate these impacts, it is essential to understand the adaptive capacity, genetic structure, sensitivity, and connectivity of target species, which form the basis for effective conservation planning. Mangroves represent critical coastal ecosystems that provide invaluable ecological services such as shoreline stabilization, nutrient cycling, carbon sequestration, and biodiversity maintenance. Within this group, the genus Avicennia particularly Avicennia marina is among the most widely distributed mangroves, extending across tropical and subtropical coasts from the Indo-Pacific to the Atlantic. Despite their ecological significance, Avicennia populations are increasingly threatened by climate change, sea-level rise, habitat degradation,and anthropogenic pressures. These drivers are expected to alter genetic structure,reduce adaptive capacity, and undermine long-term resilience, potentially leading to local extinctions or range shifts.In this study, we conducted an integrated assessment of Avicennia species, with a focus on geographically distinct populations of A. marina in Iran. Using a comprehensive framework that combined genetic, functional, ecological, and climatic variables, we evaluated landscape adaptive capacity, vulnerability, and divergence across populations. Our analyses identified genetic corridors, patterns of local adaptation, and the relationships between genetic diversity and connectivity networks. Furthermore, we detected highly divergent and at-risk populations that warrant urgent conservation attention. Introduction

Climate change is among the most pressing global challenges, threatening biodiversity and accelerating the extinction risk of species with restricted distributions1,2. Conservation programs therefore require a thorough understanding of species’adaptive capacity, genetic structure,sensitivity, and connectivity in order to design effective strategies for at-risk taxa. Modeling and quantifying these responses under changing climates provides a predictive framework for Conservation planning3.

Mangroves are critical coastal ecosystems that deliver essential ecological services such as shoreline stabilization, nutrient cycling, carbon sequestration, and biodiversity support. Within this group, the genus Avicennia , particularly Avicennia marina, is widely distributed across tropical and subtropical coasts from the Indo-Pacific to the Atlantic. Despite their ecological importance, Avicennia populations are increasingly threatened by climate change, sea-level rise, habitat degradation, and anthropogenic pressures. These stressors are expected to disrupt genetic structure, reduce adaptive capacity, and compromise long-term resilience, potentially causing local extinctions or range shifts.

Recent advances in landscape genetics and integrative biogeography have enabled researchers to combine genetic, morphological, climatic and soil datasets to evaluate species’ capacity to persist under global change. In this context, two complementary approaches the landscape adaptive index (LAI) and the Computational Taxonomic Index (CTI) provide powerful quantitative tools for assessing adaptive potential and evolutionary divergence. LAI integrates genetic variation, environmental gradients, and landscape connectivity to estimate a population’s adaptive capacity4. CTI provides a multidimensional numerical framework for quantifying taxonomic divergence, thereby identifying potential ecotypes, subspecies, or cryptic lineages. When applied together, LAI and CTI link ecological resilience with taxonomic distinctiveness, offering a refined approach to conservation prioritization.

Adaptive capacity refers to the potential of species or populations to tolerate or adapt to climate change through phenotypic plasticity5–7. This capacity depends on both intrinsic traits and extrinsic pressures such as habitat loss and fragmentation. Characterizing reaction norms the shape of phenotypic responses to environmental variation further informs how genotypes or populations respond to climate variability8,9. While some phenotypic responses are adaptive and enhance fitness, others are non-adaptive consequences of resource limitations10.

Although A. marina is considered one of the most widespread and tolerant mangrove species, its genetic and ecological variability across its range remains underexplored. Moreover, no study has applied a combined LAI-CTI framework to mangrove taxa, despite its potential to identify geographic ecotypes and refine conservation units.

The present study addresses this gap by:

Quantifying the landscape adaptive capacity and vulnerability of Avicennia species globally and of nine geographically distinct A. marina populations in Iran, using integrated genetic, morphological, climatic, soil, and connectivity data.

This work represents the first global-to-local scale evaluation of Avicennia using a fully integrated framework that combines nuclear ITS sequences, population-level molecular markers (SCOT), climate and soil data, morphological traits, least-cost connectivity, and genetic diversity metrics. By jointly applying LAI and CTI, we address both ecological resilience and evolutionary distinctiveness.

Genetic connectivity among populations is a key factor influencing the resilience and adaptive potential of species in heterogeneous landscapes. To quantify and visualize genetic connectivity, we employed covariance-informed population (CIP) networks, which describe gene flow among populations based on genetic covariance structures. CIP networks use node-based metrics, including closeness centrality, betweenness centrality, mean inverse edge weight (MIW), and clustering coefficient, to capture the structural importance and connectivity of each population within the network11. By integrating these network metrics, CIP analysis provides a powerful framework for identifying populations that act as hubs, bridges, or strongly connected clusters in the landscape, informing conservation prioritization and management strategies.

Our findings are expected to advance theoretical approaches in computational taxonomy and landscape genetics while providing practical guidance for mangrove conservation under climate change. Identifying adaptive hotspots,vulnerable populations, and divergent ecotypes will inform habitat restoration, assisted gene flow, and protected area design, ensuring the maintenance of both functional and evolutionary potential in mangrove ecosystems.

Hypothesis/prediction

We hypothesize that Avicennia populations will exhibit significant spatial heterogeneity in both adaptive capacity and vulnerability, reflecting differences in genetic diversity, environmental.

pressures, and connectivity. Specifically, we expect that populations located in environmentally variable or fragmented habitats will show reduced adaptive capacity and higher vulnerability, whereas populations with greater genetic diversity and connectivity will act as adaptive hotspots. Furthermore, the CTI framework is predicted to identify distinct population clusters corresponding to potential ecotypes or cryptic lineages, thereby refining taxonomic boundaries within Avicennia.

To explicitly test these predictions, our analytical framework integrates multiple methodological layers, including the landscape adaptive index (LAI) to quantify adaptive capacity, multivariate reaction norm analyses to characterize sensitivity, least-cost and circuit-based models to assess connectivity, and the computational taxonomic index (CTI) to detect evolutionary divergence. By combining genetic markers (ITS, SCOT), morphological traits, climatic and soil gradients, and landscape structure, these methods allow us to directly evaluate whether spatial patterns of genetic diversity, environmental stress, and functional differentiation correspond to the hypothesized variation in adaptive capacity, vulnerability, and ecotype formation across Avicennia populations. Additionally, the integrated results will helpidentify the speies or populations that should be prioritized for conservation under future climate scenarios.

Least-cost distance, habitat fragmentation, and genetic corridor modeling

To assess functional connectivity and landscape-level movement potential among Avicennia.

populations, we implemented a series of spatial analyses using least-cost path modeling, habitat fragmentation metrics, and genetic corridor mapping. All analyses were conducted in R version 4.3.2, utilizing packages including raster, sf, gdistance, geosphere, and sp.

Least-cost distance analysis finds the path with the lowest accumulated cost between two locations, where cost is assigned to different land cover types or other factors. Least-cost distance is a geospatial analysis technique that identifies the most cost-effective path between two points on a surface, considering factors like distance and resistance25,26.

Habitat fragmentation index (HFI)

The Habitat Fragmentation Index (HFI) quantifies the degree to which a once-continuous habitat has been subdivided into smaller, more isolated patches. It is a critical metric for assessing the ecological consequences of fragmentation, including reduced gene flow, increased edge effects, and diminished ecosystem resilience.

HFIs are commonly applied across a range of ecosystems, including forests, grasslands, and urban landscapes, to evaluate spatial patterns that may threaten biodiversity and ecological connectivity. The impact of fragmentation is often scale-dependent27.

To quantify habitat fragmentation for each population, we calculated a local fragmentation index based on spatial isolation. Specifically, we used the distm() function from the geosphere R package (using the Haversine formula) to compute a pairwise geographic distance matrix among all populations (in kilometers). For each population, we identified the three nearest neighboring populations (excluding zero distances) and calculated the mean Euclidean distance to these neighbors. This means distance serves as a proxy for fragmentation: higher values indicate greater spatial isolation and, thus, increased fragmentation, which may impede gene flow and ecological connectivity. The results were exported as a CSV file for further analysis.

Genetic corridor modeling

The genetic corridors enable movement of individuals or gene flow between populations, which helps to prevent negative effects of inbreeding and reduced genetic diversity, often caused by genetic drift, that can occur in isolated populations. Corridors are thought to facilitate movement between connected patches of habitat, thus increasing gene flow, promoting the reestablishment of locally extinct populations, and increasing species diversity within otherwise isolated areas.

The least-cost modelling is one commonly employed approach in which dispersal costs are assigned to distinct habitat types, and the least-costly dispersal paths among habitat patches are calculated using a geographical information system (GIS).

To identify potential genetic corridors and areas of high connectivity across the landscape, we implemented an accumulated cost surface modeling using the accCost () function from ththe gdistance package. Spatial points representing population centers were used as sources, and a resistance raster (as described above) was used to simulate movement cost across the landscape.

A transition layer was generated and corrected using geoCorrection(), and the accumulated cost surface was calculated for all population points, representing cumulative resistance-weighted distance from each source location. The resulting genetic corridor map highlights landscape regions of low resistance where gene flow is most likely to occur under current conditions. The raster output was visualized and saved in GeoTIFF format for use in GIS-based conservation planning.

CIP genetic and resistance networks analyses

Quantifying the variation in genetic connectivity of Avicennia populations was made by using node-based network metrics (CIP network).

CIP networks represent the genetic covariance structure and describe gene flow among the nodes that make up the network.

In the CIP network, four node-based network metrics are estimated. These are 1- closeness centrality, 2- betweenness centrality, 3- mean inverse edge weight (MIW), and 4- clustering coefficient.

These network metrics describe how a node (population) is connected within a network and therefore quantify the genetic or functional connectivity of the nodes within a CIP network28.

Closeness centrality is calculated as the reciprocal of the sum of the shortest path lengths between a given node and all other nodes in a network. This means that a node with high closeness centrality is located close to many other nodes in the network, allowing for efficient gene flow and exchange11.

Betweenness centrality is a measure of a node's importance in a network, specifically how often it lies on the shortest paths between other nodes. A node with high betweenness centrality acts as a crucial bridge, controlling the geneflow within the network11.

The mean inverse edge weight (MIW) is the average of the inverse of edge weights in a graph or network. It's a way to quantify the connectivity of a node by considering the weights of its adjacent edges, giving more weight to connections with smaller edge weights (stronger connections).

The MIW is calculated by summing the inverse edge weights of all edges connected to a particular node and then dividing by the number of those edges. This provides a single value representing the average strength of connection for that node11.

The clustering coefficient in a network measures the degree to which nodes tend to cluster together. It indicates how likely two neighbors of a node are to also be neighbors of each other, and hence reveals the local neighborhoods that are interconnected within the network29.

To evaluate population connectivity and compare spatial structures inferred from genetic and resistance distances, we constructed two undirected, weighted Covariance-Informed Population (CIP) networks: one based on pairwise genetic distance (Nei’s genetic distance) and the other.

based on landscape resistance distance. All analyses were conducted in R version 4.3. Genetic distance matrix and diversity indices were calculated before network construction.

Computational taxonomic index (CTI) as a measure of population divergence

Species delimitation in plants remains one of the most persistent challenges in biological sciences, due largely to the limitations of single-line evidence approaches and the complex evolutionary dynamics of plant species in general. To address these challenges, we present the computational taxonomic index (CTI), a novel, integrative, and data-driven framework designed to objectively quantify divergence among populations, ecotypes, and species. CTI synthesizes multilayered datasets encompassing genetic markers, functional traits, ecological niche differentiation, geographical distribution, and indicators of gene flow, adaptation, and connectivity. The empirical research across diverse plant lineages demonstrates that no single dataset is sufficient for robust taxonomic inference. Instead, CTI combines these complementary dimensions into a unified index that can capture both incipient and well-established divergence.

For CTI estimation we used the following data sources:

Genetic Data: DNA sequences of the ITS region (Internal transcribed spacer DNA) were obtained from the NCBI center for 30 samples representing Avicennia species and their geographical populations (Table 1).

Morphological and other traits data:

Several quantitative and qualitative morphological and the other traits were used for the studied taxa (Supplementary Table 1).

Geographical variables:

Latitude and longitude coordinates were used for geographic distance calculations of the taxa studied.

Ecological niche data:

For each sampling locality, environmental data were extracted from WorldClim v2.1 at a resolution of 30-arcsecond (~ 1 km) spatial resolution. A total of 19 bioclimatic variables were initially considered in the analysis. Additionally, we extracted data from Bio_Oracel to have the sampling areas water properties (Supplementary Tables 2, 3).

Data preprocessing and distance matrix calculation

Before integration, each dataset was independently processed to derive distance matrices between all sampled individuals/populations. For this, the R statistical environment 4.3 was used, leveraging specialized packages.

Genetic distance

For the Avicennia species ITS nucleotides we estimated the Kimura 2-parameter distance, while for A. marina populations with SCoT molecular markers we used jaccard distance17. These matrices quantified the genetic divergence between all pairs of individuals.

Morphological distance

A Gower dissimilarity matrix was computed for the combined morphological and dispersal traits using the daisy function from the cluster package. Gower distance is robust to mixed data types (continuous, ordinal, binary) and handles missing values.

Geographical distance

Pairwise great-circle distances (Haversine formula) between sampling localities were calculated in kilometers using the distm() function from the geosphere package.

Ecological niche distance

To reduce dimensionality and account for correlations among bioclimatic and water variables, a Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed on the standardized environmental data using prcomp function. The first two principal components (PCs) explaining collectively > 80% of the total variance were retained for subsequent analysis.

Ecological niche distance between individuals in this reduced environmental space was then calculated using the regularized Mahalanobis distance. This method accounts for the covariance structure of the environmental variables, providing a more robust measure of niche separation. The regularization term of 1 × 10 − 6 was added to the covariance matrix to ensure numerical stability.

Computational taxonomic index (CTI) construction

The computational taxonomic index (CTI) quantifies the multi-dimensional divergence of each sampled individual/population relative to a designated target reference individual/population.

Target reference selection

An initial target reference (e.g., the first sampled individual from a geographically central, well-studied population or the known species) was selected for preliminary CTI estimation. To ensure the robustness and biological representativeness of the final CTI, the most central individual within the largest inferred taxonomic cluster from preliminary CTI calculations was subsequently identified. This "median specimen" was chosen by computing the average pairwise distance of each individual to all other individuals in a multidimensional space derived from the combined scaled distance metrics, selecting the individual with the lowest average distance. All subsequent CTI calculations utilized this empirically determined central individual as the new target reference. In case of the Avicennia species, one of the populations from A. Alba _Bangladesh was used as the target reference (Population 1).

This normalization ensures that all metrics contribute proportionally to the final CTI regardless of their initial units or scale.

Weighted Integration of Standardized Distances (CTI Calculation).

The Computational Taxonomic Index (CTI) for each non-target individual or population was calculated as a weighted arithmetic mean of its standardized distances from the target reference across all evaluated dimensions:

CTIi = (wgen . Dgen,i ) + (wmorph . Dmorph,i) + (wgeo . Dgeo,i) + (wniche . Dniche,i).

Where: CTI = Computational Taxonomic Index for individual i; Dgen,i = standardized genetic distance from the target reference; Dmorph,i = standardized morphological distance; Dgeo,i = standardized geographical distance; Dniche,i = standardized ecological niche distance; wgen, wmorph, wgeo, wniche = weights assigned to each metric, empirically determined based on data reliability or analytical design.

This weighted integration allows multiple biological dimensions—genetic, morphological, spatial, and ecological—to be combined into a unified numerical index that represents the taxonomic proximity or divergence of each individual/population relative to the target reference.

Based on extensive preliminary analyses and our experience with plant taxonomy in similar systems, the following weights were assigned to reflect their relative contribution to species delineation in our study system:

Genetic (wgen ): 0.40, Morphological (wmorph ): 0.30, Geographical (wgeo ): 0.30, Ecological Niche (wniche ): 0.30.

CTI interpretation and taxonomic delimitation

A CTI score ranges from 0 to 1, where 0 indicates perfect similarity to the target reference across all measured dimensions (i.e., the target itself, or an identical individual), and 1 indicates maximal divergence. Intermediate values reflect varying degrees of multi-dimensional differentiation.

Threshold-based delimitation

To identify divergent populations, we considered the higher CTI value of > 0.70 (It shows at least 70% total difference in all characteristics studied), as a divergence criteria.

Discussion

The present study demonstrates considerable variation in landscape adaptive capacity (LAI) across the genus Avicennia, with some species exhibiting higher levels of vulnerability. Our CIP network analyses revealed differential connectivity among populations, highlighting populations that serve as key genetic hubs or bridges. Populations with high closeness centrality and MIW are well-connected and may facilitate gene flow across the landscape, whereas those with high betweenness centrality act as crucial connectors, controlling genetic exchange. Clustering coefficients further identified local groups of strongly interconnected populations, indicating potential sub-structuring within the network. These findings complement our genetic and phenotypic analyses, emphasizing that conservation strategies should prioritize maintaining connectivity for key populations to enhance overall landscape-level adaptive potential. Incorporating CIP network metrics thus provides an integrative perspective on both the spatial and functional dimensions of population connectivity29.

While previous studies have highlighted substantial morphological and genetic diversity within the genus e.g.17, our results reveal extensive variation not only in species’ genetic connectivity networks but also in their resistance networks, which are influenced by connectivity barriers among Avicennia species. This is further corroborated by the genetic corridors mapped for the studied taxa.

Divergence analyses using the Computational Taxonomic Index (CTI) indicate that Avicennia species and populations form distinct categories based on CTI values and divergence magnitude. Some species exhibit deep divergence from others (e.g., species 6–8, Fig. 4), which may result from extensive local adaptation, strong environmental selection, divergent genetic structure, or limited gene flow due to geographic barriers.

Environmental changes are known to drive rapid adaptation in geographically isolated populations, even at microgeographic scales, by acting on standing genetic variation, particularly those sequences with adaptive potential. However, frequent admixture among Avicennia species can render ancient, differentially selected alleles polymorphic across populations, facilitating rapid evolution of one ecotype from a small subset of individuals from another population. Cases of rapid parallel ecological divergence, as observed in Avicennia, may therefore result from evolution occurring at two temporal scales: past divergence followed by repeated selection on the same alleles after admixture17,30.

Marine species must adapt to dynamic environmental and climatic conditions, relying on sufficient genetic variation and other adaptive traits31. This study investigates multiple genetic and functional aspects of Avicennia species, assessing adaptive capacity and vulnerability to environmental and climate changes, following an integrated approach recommended by marine ecologists31.

Comparable research on Brazilian mangroves, including six populations of Avicennia germinans and ten populations of A. schaueriana, showed that geographic and environmental variation strongly influences genetic diversity. Populations diverged geographically also exhibited spatial genetic structure due to limited gene flow between northern and southern populations, resulting in local adaptations along the latitudinal gradient of the Brazilian coast32.

Significant variation in leaf morphology has been reported within Avicennia species and even within single species across different geographic conditions33. Such phenotypic plasticity facilitates coping with environmental changes and contributes to landscape adaptive capacity. For instance, studies on Avicennia officinalis in the Sundarbans, Bangladesh, demonstrated that propagules exhibit adaptive plasticity in length, width, and weight along salinity gradients, with propagules from higher salinity zones initiating growth more rapidly and vigorously under high salinity conditions34.

Geographical population divergence of Avicennia marina

Our study revealed variation in LAI and vulnerability across geographic populations of A. marina in Iran, highlighting populations that should be prioritized for conservation. Predicted responses to climate change indicate shifts in divergence magnitude and vulnerability.

Similarly, studies on A. marina in Australia35 using isozyme molecular markers and morphological traits found that while tree characteristics were inadequate as genetic markers, leaf morphology provided useful insights. Isozyme/allozyme analyses showed that genetic distance correlated with geographic distance, although local metal concentrations may exert selective pressures. Each estuary exhibited high levels of heterozygote deficiency, potentially threatening future viability. Microsatellite analyses of 14 widely scattered populations across the Indo-Pacific supported a discrete subpopulation model, where in subpopulations are separated by barriers to gene flow, but random mating occurs within each subpopulation.

Genetic-environment association analyses of A. marina in the Arabian Peninsula revealed high adaptive divergence and multi-locus local adaptation driven by temperature extremes and.

hypersalinity, supporting rapid diversification influenced by both historical and ecological factors. Triest et al.36 studied A. marina populations in a transboundary conservation area between Kenya and Tanzania, showing that while allele and gene diversity levels were not significantly different overall, geographic distance shaped admixed gene pools. Migration analyses supported a stepping-stone model with south-to-north migration and bidirectional gene flow between adjacent bays, with large rivers and creek networks influencing connectivity.

Conclusion

This study provides an integrated assessment of landscape adaptive capacity and vulnerability for Avicennia species and geographic populations of A. marina in Iran. By combining genetic corridors, local adaptation patterns, connectivity networks, and functional traits, we identified populations with higher divergence and vulnerability. These findings offer valuable guidance for prioritizing conservation efforts within the genus Avicennia, improving our understanding of genetic, environmental, and functional factors shaping species resilience under changing climatic conditions.
