Computing the Bernstein Polynomial and the Krull-type Dimension of finitely generated 𝑫 \boldsymbol{D} -modules
Harry Prieto

Abstract
. We establish the existence of the Bernstein polynomial in one indeterminate t t , and provide a method for its explicit computation. The Bernstein polynomial is associated with finitely generated modules over the Weyl algebra, known as D D -modules, and is notoriously difficult to compute directly. Our approach is constructive, offering a systematic method to compute the Bernstein polynomial and its associated invariants explicitly. We begin by introducing the Weyl algebra as a ring of operators and stating some of its main properties, followed by considering the class of numerical polynomials. We then develop a generalization of the theory of Gröbner bases specifically for D D -modules and use it to compute the Bernstein polynomial and its invariants. As an application of the properties of the Bernstein polynomial, we develop the concept of the Krull-type dimension for D D -modules, which sheds light on the structure of these modules.



1.
Introduction

The Bernstein polynomial is a numerical polynomial, analogous to the Hilbert polynomial in commutative algebra, that accounts for the dimension of a finitely generated module over the Weyl algebra. The principal aim of this paper is to provide a constructive approach to the existence of the Bernstein polynomial, developing a methodology to compute it and its invariants, as will be seen in the results of Section
4
. The existence of the Bernstein polynomial is usually established nonconstructively and, as noted in
[
6
, p. 95]
, its explicit computation is notoriously complicated. The approach we take here relies on a generalization of the classical theory of Gröbner bases and on results about numerical polynomials
[
12
]
to obtain a relatively straightforward methodology for the computation of the Bernstein polynomial. Similar approaches have been successfully implemented in other contexts, for example, by Dönch and Levin
[
7
]
for bivariate dimension polynomials, and by Levin
[
13
]
for multivariable dimension polynomials with respect to several orderings, multivariate Berstein-type polynomials
[
15
]
, and bivariate difference dimension polynomials
[
16
]
.

In Section
2
, we begin by introducing the Weyl algebra as a ring of operators and stating some of its basic properties. Section
3
introduces the class of numerical polynomials
f(t)
, which become integer-valued for sufficiently large values of the indeterminate
t
. We also state two important facts about numerical polynomials that will assist in computing the Bernstein polynomial. Section
4
is devoted to developing a generalization of the classical theory of Gröbner bases for the case of finitely generated modules over the Weyl algebra
A_{n}
. There, we will first introduce a way of ordering the elements of the Weyl algebra, followed by a concept of “divisibility.” These notions are then extended to the case of a finitely generated free module over
A_{n}
. Next we define a concept of reduction and present an analogue of the division algorithm for the Weyl algebra. We then define the concept of a Gröbner basis for
D
-modules, state some of its properties and provide a criterion to find one (Theorem
6
, Buchbereger’s algorithm). Section
5
contains the main results of the paper, namely, establishing the existence of and computing the Bernstein polynomial of a finitely generated
D
-module (Theorem
9
and Theorem
10
) using both the theory of Gröbner bases and the results about numerical polynomials from previous sections. We also use these ideas to exhibit the invariants of the Bernstein polynomial (Theorem
14
), namely, its degree and multiplicity. This section features fully worked examples of explicitly computing the Bernstein polynomial of
D
-modules, thus illustrating the main result. In Section
6
, we use the Bernstein polynomial and some of its properties to define the concept of the Krull-type dimension for
D
-modules. These concepts present a further application of the ideas of Section
5
. The paper concludes with a result about the Krull-type dimension of a finitely generated
D
-module (Theorem
15
).

2.
Preliminaries

Throughout this paper, every ring homomorphism is unitary (maps identity onto identity), and every subring of a ring contains the identity of the ring. By a module over a ring
R
we mean a unitary left
R
-module. In what follows, we consider a Weyl algebra as an algebra of differential operators over a polynomial ring. More precisely, let
K
be a field and
K[X]
the ring of polynomials
K[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]
in
n
commuting indeterminates with coefficients in
K
(
n\geq 1)
.
We denote by
End_{K}(K[X])
the
K
-algebra of linear operators (mappings)
K[X]\rightarrow K[X]
. This is a noncommutative ring with identity, whose operations are addition and composition of operators.

Let
f\in K[X]
be a polynomial. Multiplication by
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
gives
K
-linear operators on
K[X]
denoted by
\hat{x}_{1},\ldots,\hat{x}_{n}
, respectively, acting on
f
by the formula
\hat{x}_{i}(f)=x_{i}\cdot f
, for
i=1,\ldots,n
. For simplicity of notation, we will write
x_{i}
for both the operator
\hat{x}_{i}
and the variable
x_{i}
. On
K[X]
we also have pairwise commuting
K
-linear derivations
\partial_{1},\ldots,\partial_{n}
that are elements of
End_{K}(K[X])
defined by
\partial_{i}(f)=\partial f/\partial x_{i}
, for
i=1,\ldots,n
.
With this notation, we make the following definition.

Definition 1
.

The
\boldsymbol{n}
th Weyl algebra
, denoted by
A_{n}(K)
, is the
K
-subalgebra of
End_{K}(K[X])
generated by the operators

x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}

and

\partial_{1},\ldots,\partial_{n}
.

We will adopt the convention that
A_{0}(K)=K
. Furthermore, we will often write
A_{n}
instead of
A_{n}(K)
if the field over which the algebra is defined is clear from the context, or no emphasis is needed.
Although commutativity holds within each operator (
x_{i}x_{j}=x_{j}x_{i}
and
\partial_{i}\partial_{j}=\partial_{j}\partial_{i}
for any
i,i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, and
x_{i}\partial_{j}=\partial_{j}x_{i}
whenever
i\neq j
), the Weyl algebra is not commutative. Indeed, for any
f\in K[X]
, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{i}x_{i}(f)=x_{i}\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_{i}}+f,

obtained by applying the product rule for differentiation —also called the Leibniz rule. Thus,

\partial_{i}\cdot x_{i}=x_{i}\cdot\partial_{i}+1
, where
1
is the identity operator.

The elements of
A_{n}
are linear combinations, with coefficients in
K
, of the products of the generators
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}

and

\partial_{1},\ldots,\partial_{n}
.
To describe these elements, we use a multi-index notation.
Multi-indices

\alpha
and
\beta
of size
n
are elements of
\mathbb{N}^{n}
, that is,
n
-tuples of nonnegative integers
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n})
and
\beta=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})
.
In this notation, we set
x^{\alpha}=x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}

and

\partial^{\beta}=\partial_{1}^{\beta_{1}}\cdots\partial_{n}^{\beta_{n}}
.
We also define the
degrees
of the power products
x^{\alpha}
and
\partial^{\beta}
as the numbers

|\alpha|=\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{n}
and

|\beta|=\beta_{1}+\cdots+\beta_{n}
, respectively.
A power product of the form
\theta=x^{\alpha}\partial^{\beta}\in A_{n}
, with
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, is called a
monomial
in
A_{n}
.
The monomials
x^{\alpha}
and
\partial^{\beta}
will be denoted by
\theta_{x}
and
\theta_{\partial}
, respectively.
The
degree
of such a monomial
\theta
is defined as
|\alpha|+|\beta|
, and it is denoted by
deg(\theta)
.
Furthermore, we set the factorial of a multi-index
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
as
\alpha!=\alpha_{1}!\cdots\alpha_{n}!
.
In what follows,
\Theta
will denote the set of all monomials
x^{\alpha}\partial^{\beta}
of
A_{n}
.
Moreover, for any
r\in\mathbb{N}
,
\Theta(r)
will denote the set
\{\theta\in\Theta:deg(\theta)\leq r\}
.

The following proposition says that when considered as a vector space over
K
, the Weyl algebra has a particular basis.

Proposition 1

(
[
5
, Proposition 1.2]
)
.

Each element in
A_{n}
can be written in a unique way as a finite sum

\sum k_{\alpha,\beta}x^{\alpha}\partial^{\beta}
, where the coefficients
k_{\alpha,\beta}\in K
.
In other words, the set of monomials

\mathcal{B}=\{x^{\alpha}\partial^{\beta}:\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{n}\}

is a basis of
A_{n}
as a
K
-vector space.

The Weyl algebra can be considered as a filtered ring by means of the following filtration on
A_{n}
known as the
Bernstein filtration
, which will be defined using the degree of the elements of
A_{n}
.
For
r\geq 0
, let
B_{r}
denote the
K
-vector space of all elements of
A_{n}
generated by
\{x^{\alpha}\partial^{\beta}:|\alpha|+|\beta|\leq r\}
. These are finite-dimensional
K
-vector subspaces of
A_{n}
:

B_{0}=K
;
B_{1}=K+Kx_{1}+\cdots+Kx_{n}+K\partial_{1}+\cdots+K\partial_{n}
, and so on.

3.
Numerical Polynomials in one indeterminate

Definition 2
.

A polynomial
f(t)
in one indeterminate
t
with rational coefficients is called
numerical
if
f(t)\in\mathbb{Z}
for all sufficiently large
t\in\mathbb{N}
, that is, if there exists
r\in\mathbb{N}
such that
f(s)\in\mathbb{Z}
for any
s\in\mathbb{N}
with
r\leq s
.

Clearly, every polynomial with integer coefficients is numerical. For a general example of a numerical polynomial with non-integer coefficients, we can consider the polynomial in one indeterminate
t

\displaystyle\binom{t}{n}=\frac{t(t-1)\cdots(t-n+1)}{n!}\quad(n\geq 1);

\displaystyle\binom{t}{0}=1.

The following proposition, proved in
[
12
, Proposition 2.1.3]
, gives a canonical representation of a numerical polynomial.

Proposition 2
.

Let
f(t)\in\mathbb{Q}[t]
be a polynomial of degree
m
in one indeterminate
t
. Then
f(t)
is numerical if and only if it can be represented in the form

(3.1)

\displaystyle f(t)=\sum^{m}_{i=0}a_{i}\binom{t+i}{i},

where the coefficients
a_{0},\ldots,a_{m}
are integers uniquely determined by
f(t)
.

For the remaining of this section we will deal with subsets of
\mathbb{N}^{m}
, where
m
is a positive integer. If
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{m}
,
V_{A}
will denote the set of all
m
-tuples
v=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{m})\in\mathbb{N}^{m}
that are no greater than or equal to any point of
A
with respect to the product order on
\mathbb{N}^{m}
. The
product order
on
\mathbb{N}^{m}
is a partial order
\leq_{P}
such that
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{m})\leq_{P}(c^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,c^{\prime}_{m})
if and only if
c_{i}\leq c^{\prime}_{i}
, for all
i=1,\ldots,m
. Clearly, an element
v=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{m})\in\mathbb{N}^{m}
belongs to
V_{A}
if and only if for any element
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})\in A
there exist
i\in\mathbb{N}
, with
1\leq i\leq m
, such that
a_{i}>v_{i}
.
Also, we let
\mathbb{N}_{p}=\{1,\ldots,p\}

(p\in\mathbb{Z},p\geq 1)
be the set of the first
p
integers.
Furthermore, for any
r\in\mathbb{N}
, we will denote by
A(r)
the subset of
A
consisting of all
m
-tuples
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{m})
such that
a_{1}+\cdots+a_{m}\leq r
.

The next two propositions, proved respectively in
[
11
, Chapter 0, Lemma 16]
and
[
12
, Proposition 2.2.11]
, introduce certain numerical polynomias associated with subsets of
\mathbb{N}^{m}
, and give explicit formulas for their computation.

Proposition 3
.

Let
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{m}
. There exists a numerical polynomial
\omega_{A}(t)
with the following properties:

(i)

\omega_{A}(s)=Card\;V_{A}(s)
for all sufficiently large
s\in\mathbb{N}
(as usual,
Card~V
denotes the number of elements of a finite set
V
).

(ii)

deg(\omega_{A})\leq m
; the equality occurs if and only if the set
A
is empty, in which case we have

\displaystyle\omega_{A}(t)=\binom{t+m}{m}.

(iii)

\omega_{A}(t)=0
if and only if
(0,\ldots,0)\in A
.

Definition 3
.

The polynomial
\omega_{A}(t)
, whose existence is established in the preceding proposition, is called the
Kolchin dimension polynomial
of the set
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{m}
.

Remark.
In
[
11
, Chapter 0, Lemma 16]
it is shown that any infinite subset of
\mathbb{N}^{m}
contains a sequence that is strictly increasing with respect to the product order. It follows that if
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{m}
, then the set
A^{*}
of all minimal points of
A
with respect to the product order is finite. It is easy to see that
\omega_{A}(t)=\omega_{A^{*}}(t)
. The following proposition, proved in
[
12
, Chapter II]
, provides a way to compute the Kolchin polynomial of any finite subset of
\mathbb{N}^{m}
, and therefore, of any subset of
\mathbb{N}^{m}
.

Proposition 4
.

Let
A=\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{p}\}
be a finite subset of
\mathbb{N}^{m}
, where
m
is a fixed positive integer, and let
a_{i}=(a_{i1},\ldots,a_{im})
for
i=1,\ldots,p
. Furthermore, for any
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}
,
0\leq\ell\leq p
, let
\Lambda(\ell,p)
denote the set of all
\ell
-element subets of
N_{p}
, and for any
\lambda\in\Lambda(\ell,p)
let
\bar{a}_{\lambda j}=max\{a_{ij}:i\in\lambda\}
for
1\leq j\leq m
(in other words, if
\lambda=\{i_{1},\ldots,i_{l}\}
, then
\bar{a}_{\lambda j}
denotes the greatest
jth
coordinate of the elements
a_{i_{1}},\ldots,a_{i_{l}}
). Also, let
b_{\lambda}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\bar{a}_{\lambda j}
. Then

\displaystyle\omega_{A}(t)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{p}(-1)^{\ell}\sum_{\lambda\in\Lambda(\ell,p)}\binom{t+m-b_{\lambda}}{m}.

4.
Gröbner Bases for
D
-modules

In this section, we adapt the classical technique of Gröbner bases to
D
-modules or modules over the Weyl alegbra
A_{n}
. The theory we develop here will be used in the next section to prove the existence of the Bernstein polynomial and compute its invariants. Analogous methodologies have been implemented by A. Levin in
[
13
]
,
[
15
]
,
[
16
]
, and by Dönch and Levin in
[
7
]
for the computation of a dimension polynomial in two variables, showing that such a polynomial carries more invariants than the Bernstein dimension polynomial. The approach we take in what follows draws on some of the ideas presented in those works. Complete presentations of the classical theory can be found in
[
1
]
and
[
3
]
(which includes applications to the computation of Hilbert polynomials of graded and filtered modules over polynomial rings). Generalizations of the notion of Gröbner bases to rings of differential operators are carried out in
[
9
]
and
[
12
, Chapter 4]
.

We will begin by defining the concept of an order for the monomials of the Weyl Algebra, followed by a definition of divisibility. Using these ideas, we will then define the concept of reduction, which in turn will lead to an analog of the division algorithm for
D
-modules over
A_{n}
. Then we define the concept of a Gröbner basis in this context, giving equivalent conditions for it. Finally, we discuss how to compute Gröbner bases, developing the concept of
S
-polynomials and a version of Buchberger’s algorithm for that purpose. Once this is accomplished, we will use this method to compute the Bernstein polynomial and its invariants in the next section.

Let
\Theta
denote the set of all monomials in the Weyl algebra
A_{n}
. We can define a natural order on the monomials of
\Theta
with respect to degree as follows.

Definition 4
.

An
order
on the set of all monomials
\Theta
of
A_{n}
is a total order
<
on
\Theta
, such that

\partial_{n}<\cdots<\partial_{1}<x_{n}<\cdots<x_{1}
,
and if

\theta=x^{\alpha}\partial^{\beta}=x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}\partial_{1}^{\beta_{1}}\cdots\partial_{n}^{\beta_{n}}

and

\theta^{\prime}=x^{\gamma}\partial^{\delta}=x_{1}^{\gamma_{1}}\cdots x_{n}^{\gamma_{n}}\partial_{1}^{\delta_{1}}\cdots\partial_{n}^{\delta_{n}}

are two elements of
\Theta
, then

\displaystyle\theta<\theta^{\prime}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad(deg(\theta),\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n},\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})<_{lex}(deg(\theta^{\prime}),\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{n},\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{n}),

where
<_{lex}
denotes the lexicographic order on
\mathbb{N}^{2n+1}
.

Note that we are using the symbol “
<
” in two different ways, both for the order relation on the multi-indices in
\mathbb{N}^{n}
and for the order introduced on the monomials of
\Theta
. The context will make clear which meaning is intended.

Also note that since the set
\mathbb{N}^{2n+1}
is well-ordered with respect to the lexicographic order, our ordering of
\Theta
turns it into a well-ordered set.
Additionally, if
\theta<\theta^{\prime}
, we also write
\theta^{\prime}>\theta
. In what follows, the monomial order on
\Theta
introduced above is fixed.

Definition 5
.

Let
D=\sum k_{\alpha,\beta}x^{\alpha}\partial^{\beta}
be a nonzero element of
A_{n}
and let
<
be a monomial order on
\Theta
.

(i)

The
leading monomial
of
D
, denoted by LM
(D)
, is the greatest monomial
x^{\alpha}\partial^{\beta}
that appears in
D
with a nonzero coefficient.

(ii)

The coefficient of the leading monomial of
D
is called the
leading coefficient
of
D
and is denoted by LC
(D)
.

(iii)

The
leading term
of
D
is defined as LT
(D)=
LC
(D)\cdot
LM
(D)
.

Next, we define a notion of divisibility for the elements of
A_{n}
as follows.
Let

\theta=x^{\alpha}\partial^{\beta}=x_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\ldots x_{n}^{\alpha_{n}}\partial_{1}^{\beta_{1}}\ldots\partial_{n}^{\beta_{n}}
,
and

\theta^{\prime}=x^{\gamma}\partial^{\delta}=x_{1}^{\gamma_{1}}\ldots x_{n}^{\gamma_{n}}\partial_{1}^{\delta_{1}}\ldots\partial_{n}^{\delta_{n}}

be two elements of
\Theta
.
We say that
\theta

divides

\theta^{\prime}
if
x^{\alpha}
divides
x^{\gamma}
and
\partial^{\beta}
divides
\partial^{\delta}
, that is,

\alpha_{i}\leq\gamma_{i}
and
\beta_{i}\leq\delta_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
. In this case we also say that
\theta^{\prime}
is a
multiple
of
\theta
and write
\theta|\theta^{\prime}
.

According to this definition, if
\theta|\theta^{\prime}
, then there exist elements
\theta_{0},\theta_{1},\ldots\theta_{k}\in\Theta
such that

\theta^{\prime}=\theta_{0}\theta-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\theta_{i}
,
where the

deg(\theta_{0})+deg(\theta)=deg(\theta^{\prime})
,
and

deg(\theta_{i})<deg(\theta^{\prime})

for
i=1,\ldots,k
. The monomial
\theta_{0}
is denoted by

\frac{\theta^{\prime}}{\theta}
.

To illustrate, if
n=1
, then
\theta=x\partial^{2}
divides
\theta^{\prime}=x^{2}\partial^{3}
, and we can write
\theta^{\prime}=\theta_{0}\theta-\theta_{1}
, where
\theta_{0}=x\partial
, and
\theta_{1}=x\partial^{2}
.

By the
least common multiple
of two elements
\theta^{\prime},\theta^{\prime\prime}\in\Theta
we mean the element
lcm
(\theta^{\prime},\theta^{\prime\prime})=
lcm
(\theta^{\prime}_{x},\theta^{\prime\prime}_{x})\cdot
lcm
(\theta^{\prime}_{\partial},\theta^{\prime\prime}_{\partial})
.
Thus, if

\theta=
lcm
(\theta^{\prime},\theta^{\prime\prime})
,
then both

\theta^{\prime}|\theta
,
\theta^{\prime\prime}|\theta
,
and whenever
\theta^{\prime}|\tau
and
\theta^{\prime\prime}|\tau
, for some
\tau\in\Theta
,
we have
\theta|\tau
.

Let
E
be a free
A_{n}(K)
-module with a finite set of free generators
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}\}
. Then
E
can be regarded as a
K
-vector space with basis

\Theta e=\{\theta e_{i}:\theta\in\Theta,1\leq i\leq m\}

whose elements are called
monomials
.

Since the set of all monomials
\Theta e
is a basis of the
K
-vector space

, every nonzero element

has a unique representation of the form

(4.1)

\displaystyle f=a_{1}\theta_{1}e_{i_{1}}+\cdots+a_{m}\theta_{m}e_{i_{m}},

where
\theta_{j}\in\Theta
,
0\neq a_{j}\in K

(1\leq j\leq m)
and the monomials

\theta_{1}e_{i_{1}},\ldots,\theta_{m}e_{i_{m}}

are all distinct. We say that a monomial
u

appears in f
(or that
f contains

) if
u
is one of the monomials
\theta_{k}e_{i_{k}}
in the representation (
4.1
), that is, the coefficient of

is not zero.
The greatest monomial that appears in

with a nonzero coefficient is called the
leading monomial
of

and is denoted by

. The coefficient of the leading monomial of
f
is called the
leading coefficient
of

and is denoted by
LC(f)
. The
leading term
of

.

We say that a monomial

is a
multiple
of a monomial
v=\theta e_{j}\quad(\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta,1\leq i,j\leq m)
if

and
\theta|\theta^{\prime}
. In this case we also say that

divides

, write

, and define

\displaystyle\frac{u}{v}\coloneqq\frac{\theta^{\prime}}{\theta}.

If
c_{1}u
and
c_{2}v
are terms, their ratio is defined as
\frac{c_{1}u}{c_{2}v}=(c_{1}c_{2}^{-1})\frac{\theta^{\prime}}{\theta}
. Then we say that the term

divides

.

The
least common multiple
of two monomials
w_{1}=\theta_{1}e_{i}
and
w_{2}=\theta_{2}e_{j}
is defined as

\displaystyle\text{lcm}(w_{1},w_{2})\coloneqq\begin{cases}\text{lcm}(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})e_{i},&\text{if }i=j,\\
0,&\text{if }i\neq j.\end{cases}

We will consider a monomial order on the set
\Theta e
defined as follows.
For monomials
\theta e_{i}=x^{\alpha}\partial^{\beta}e_{i}
and

\theta^{\prime}e_{j}=x^{\delta}\partial^{\gamma}e_{j}
of
\Theta e
, we say that

\displaystyle\theta e_{i}<\theta^{\prime}e_{j}\Longleftrightarrow\begin{cases}&\theta<\theta^{\prime}\\
&\text{or}\\
&\theta=\theta^{\prime}\;\;\text{and}\;\;i<j.\end{cases}

Example 1
.

Consider a free
A_{2}
-module
E
with two free generators
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
.
Let
f=-2x^{3}_{1}\partial_{1}e_{1}-\partial^{3}_{2}+5x_{2}e_{1}+3x_{1}\partial^{2}_{1}e_{2}+4x_{2}e_{2}+\partial^{2}_{2}e_{2}\in E
.
Arranging the monomials of

in decreasing order, we get

\displaystyle f=-2x^{3}_{1}\partial_{1}e_{1}+3x_{1}\partial^{2}_{1}e_{2}-\partial^{3}_{2}e_{1}+\partial^{2}_{2}e_{2}+4x_{2}e_{2}+5x_{2}e_{1},

and so
LM(f)=x^{3}_{1}\partial_{1}e_{1}
,
LC(f)=-2
, and
LT(f)=-2x^{3}_{1}\partial_{1}e_{1}
.

Definition 6
.

Let
f,g\in E
, with
g\neq 0
. We say that
f
is
reduced
with respect to
g
if
f
does not contain any multiple of
LM(g)
.

Definition 7
.

Given
f,g,h\in E
, with
g\neq 0
, we say that the element
f
reduces to

modulo
g
in one step, written

\displaystyle f\xrightarrow{g}h,

if and only if

such that LM
(g)|w
, and

\displaystyle h=f-\frac{aw}{LT(g)}g.

Example 2
.

Let

Lemma 1
.

Let

(s\geq 1)

for all

(1\leq i\leq s)

First we write

\displaystyle=c_{1}f_{1}+\cdots+c_{s}f_{s}.

\displaystyle=c_{1}a_{1}\left(\frac{1}{a_{1}}f_{1}\right)+\cdots+c_{s}a_{s}\left(\frac{1}{a_{s}}f_{s}\right)

With the preceding definition and lemma we are now ready to state and prove the theoretical foundation of the algorithm for computing Gröbner bases.

Theorem 6

(Buchberger’s Criterion)
.

Let
G=\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{s}\}
be a set of nonzero elements of an
A_{n}
-submodule of
E
with respect to an order
<
. Then
G
is a Gröbner basis for the submodule
N=\langle g_{1},\ldots,g_{s}\rangle
of
E
if and only if for all
i\neq j
,

\displaystyle S(g_{i},g_{j})\xrightarrow{G}0.

Proof.

First suppose that
G=\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{s}\}
is a Gröbner basis for
N=\langle g_{1},\ldots,g_{s}\rangle
. Let
f\in E
. Then, by Theorem
4
, there exists
r\in E
, reduced with respect to
G
, such that
f\xrightarrow{G}r
.
Thus
f-r\in N
and so
f\in N
if and only if
r\in N
. Clearly, if
r=0
(that is,
f\xrightarrow{G}0
),
then
f\in N
. Conversely, if
f\in N
and
r\neq 0
then
r\in N
, and there exists
i\in\{1,\ldots,s\}
such that LM
(g_{i})
divides LM
(r)
. This contradicts the fact that
r
is reduced with respect to
G
. Thus,
r=0
and
f\xrightarrow{G}0
.

To prove the implication in the other direction,
suppose that
S(g_{i},g_{j})\xrightarrow{G}0
for all
i\neq j
. We use the fact that
G
is a Gröbner basis for
N
if an only if for all
f\in N
, there exist

h_{1},\ldots,h_{s}\in A_{n}
such that

f=h_{1}g_{1}+\cdots+h_{s}g_{s}

and LM
(f)=\max_{1\leq i\leq t}\{\text{LM}(h_{i}g_{i})\}
. So let
f\in N
. From among all the representations of
f
as a linear combination of the
g_{i}
, we can choose to write
f=\sum_{i=1}^{s}h_{i}g_{i}
, where

\displaystyle\delta=\max_{1\leq i\leq s}\{LM(h_{i})LM(g_{i})\}

is minimal, by the well-ordering property of our term order. If
\delta=LM(f)
, then
LM(f)
is divisible by
LM(g_{i})
for some
i
, and the result follows.

It remains to consider the case when
LM(f)<\delta
. First we isolate the terms whose degree equals
\delta
. To that end, let
\mathcal{I}=\{i:LM(h_{i})LM(g_{i})=\delta\}
. For
i\in\mathcal{I}
, we write
h_{i}=c_{i}\delta+
lower terms. Set
g=\sum_{i\in S}c_{i}\delta_{i}g_{i}
. Then,
LM(\delta_{i}g_{i})=\delta
, for all
i\in\mathcal{I}
, but
LM(g)<\delta
. By Lemma
1
, we can write
g
as a linear combination with coefficients
d_{ij}\in K
, that is,

\displaystyle g=\sum_{i,j\in\mathcal{I},i\neq j}d_{ij}S(\delta_{i}g_{i},\delta_{j}g_{j}).

Since
\delta=lcm(LM(\delta_{i},g_{i}),LM(\delta_{j},g_{j}))
, then

\displaystyle S(\delta_{i}g_{i},\delta_{j}g_{j})

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta}{LT(\delta_{i}g_{i})}\delta_{i}g_{i}-\frac{\delta}{LT(\delta_{j}g_{j})}\delta_{j}g_{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{\delta}{LT(g_{i})}g_{i}-\frac{\delta}{LT(g_{j})}g_{j}=\frac{\delta}{\delta_{ij}}S(g_{i},g_{j}),

where
\delta_{ij}=lcm(LM(g_{i}),LM(g_{j}))
. By hypothesis,
S(g_{i},g_{j})\xrightarrow{G}0
, and thus
S(\delta_{i}g_{i},\delta_{j}g_{j})\xrightarrow{G}0
. We can now write the
S
-polynomial as

\displaystyle S(\delta_{i}g_{i},\delta_{j}g_{j})=\sum_{\ell=1}^{t}h_{ij\ell}g_{\ell},

with the property that (by Theorem
4
)

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq\ell\leq s}\{LM(h_{ij\ell}LM(g_{\ell})\}

\displaystyle=LM(S(\delta_{i}g_{i},\delta_{j}g_{j}))

\displaystyle<\max\{LM(\delta_{i}g_{i}),LM(\delta_{j}g_{j})\}=\delta.

Substituting these expressions into
g
above allows us to write
f
as a polynomial combination of the
g_{i}
, namely,
f=\sum_{i=1}^{s}h^{\prime}_{i}g_{i}
, where
\max_{1\leq i\leq s}\{LM(h^{\prime}_{i})LM(g_{i})\}<\delta
. This contradicts the minimality of
\delta
and completes the proof.
∎

The last theorem allows us to construct a Gröbner basis of an

-submodule

(with respect to the monomial order

Theorem 7
.

Let

(f_{i}\neq 0,\;1\leq i\leq s)
, be a subset of a free

-submodule

Proof.

First we show that the algorithm terminates. Suppose, for contradiction, that the algorithm does not terminate. Then, we can construct a set

and obtain a strictly increasing infinite sequence

\displaystyle G_{1}\subsetneq G_{2}\subsetneq G_{3}\subsetneq\cdots.

Each

is obtained from

by adding some

to

, where

. This gives

\displaystyle LT(G_{1})\subsetneq LT(G_{2})\subsetneq LT(G_{3})\subsetneq\cdots.

-submodules, contradicting the fact that

is a left and right Noetherian ring
[

We have

, and hence

. Thus,

is a generating set for the submodule

. Also, if

are elements of

, then

-submodule

∎

5.
Berstein polynomials of
D
-modules and their invariants

This section provides a constructive proof of the existence of the Bernstein polynomial, which accounts for the dimension of a finitely generated
D
-module. This polynomial was introduced by I. Bernstein in
[
4
]
as an analog of the classical Hilbert polynomial (see
[
8
, Section 1.9]
) for a finitely generated filtered module over a Weyl algebra. Among the many applications of this concept, it allowed Bernstein to prove Gelfand’s conjecture about the meromorphic extensions of the function the complex gamma function. The proof of the existence of the Bernstein polynomial we present here will apply the theory of Gröbner bases we developed in the previous section, along with the properties of numerical polynomials stated in Section
2
.

The first result we present is the main step in computing the Bernstein polynomial.

Theorem 9
.

Let
M
be a finitely generated
A_{n}
-module with system of generators

\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}\}
,
and
E
a free
A_{n}
-module with basis
e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}
.
Let
\pi:E\rightarrow M
be the natural
A_{n}
-epimorphism of
E
onto
M
, defined by
\pi(e_{i})=f_{i}
for all
i=1,\ldots,m
. Furthermore, let
N=Ker(\pi)
, and let
G=\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{s}\}
be a Gröbner basis of
N
.
Finally, we treat
M
as a filtered
A_{n}
-module with the filtration

M_{r}=\sum^{m}_{i=1}B_{r}f_{i}
, where
\{B_{r}\}_{r\in\mathbb{N}}
is the Bernstein filtration with
B_{r}=\{D\in A_{n}:deg(D)\leq r\}
, and let

U_{r}
denote the set
\{w\in\Theta e:deg(w)\leq r
, and
w
is not a multiple of any
LM(g_{j})\;(1\leq j\leq s)\}
.
Then
\pi(U_{r})
is a basis of the
K
-vector space
M_{r}
.

Proof.

First we show that every element
\theta f_{i}
(
1\leq i\leq m,\;\theta\in\Theta
) not belonging to
\pi(U_{r})
can be written as a finite linear combination of elements of
\pi(U_{r})
with coefficients in
K
, so that the set
\pi(U_{r})
generates the
K
-vector space
M_{r}
.
Since
\theta f_{i}\notin\pi(U_{r})
, then
\theta e_{i}\notin U_{r}
, and thus
\theta e_{i}
is a multiple of some
LM(g_{j})
(
1\leq j\leq s
). Consider the element
g_{j}=a_{j}LM(g_{j})+\cdots
(
0\neq a_{j}\in K
), where the dots stand for the sum of the other terms of
g_{j}
whose monomials are less than
LM(g_{j})
.
Since
g_{j}\in N
, we have

\pi(g_{j})=a_{j}\pi(LM(g_{j}))+\cdots=0
.
Since
\theta e_{i}
is a multiple of
LM(g_{j})
, that is,
\frac{\theta e_{i}}{LM(g_{j})}=\theta^{\prime}
, for some
\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta
, we have

\pi(\theta^{\prime}g_{j})=a_{j}\pi(\theta^{\prime}LM(g_{j}))+\cdots=a_{j}\pi(\theta e_{i})+\cdots=a_{j}\theta f_{i}+\cdots=0
. Thus,
\theta f_{i}
is a finite
K
-linear combination of some elements
\tilde{\theta}f_{k}
(
1\leq k\leq m
) such that
\tilde{\theta}\in\Theta(r)
and
\tilde{\theta}e_{k}<\theta e_{i}
. We can now apply induction on the well-ordered set
\Theta e
to obtain that every element
\theta f_{i}
(
\theta\in\Theta(r)
,
1\leq i\leq m
) can be written as a finite linear combination of elements
\pi(U_{r})
with coefficients in the field
K
.

Next, we show that the set
\pi(U_{r})
is linearly independent over
K
.
Suppose that
a_{1}\pi(u_{a})+\cdots+a_{q}\pi(u_{q})=0
for some
u_{1},\ldots,u_{q}\in U_{r}
and
a_{1},\ldots,a_{q}\in K
. Then
h=a_{1}u_{1}+\cdots+a_{q}u_{q}
is an element of
N
. If an element
u=\theta e_{j}
appears in

, that is,
u=u_{i}
for some
i=1,\ldots,q
, then
u
is not a multiple of any
LM(g_{i})
(for
1\leq i\leq s
). Thus,

is reduced with respect to
G
. By the first part of the proof of Theorem
6
, we have that

, and it follows that
a_{1}=\cdots=a_{q}=0
. Therefore, the set
\pi(U_{r})
is linearly independent over
K
, and this concludes the proof that
\pi(U_{r})
is a basis of
M_{r}
.
∎

The following theorem establishes the existence of the Bernstein polynomial and provides a formula for its computation. The constructive proof of existence below is the main result of this paper.

Theorem 10
.

Let
M
be a finitely generated
A_{n}
-module with a system of generators
\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}\}
, and let
\{M_{r}\}_{r\in\mathbb{N}}
be the corresponding filtration of
M
with respect to the Bernstein filtration
\{B_{r}\}_{r\in\mathbb{N}}

(M_{r}=\sum^{p}_{i=1}B_{r}f_{i})
.
Furthermore,
Let
E
be a free
A_{n}
-module with basis
e_{1},\ldots,e_{m}
, let
N
be the kernel of the natural epimorphism
\pi:E\rightarrow M

(e_{i}\mapsto f_{i})
, and let
G=\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{s}\}
be a Gröbner basis of
N
.
For any
i=1,\ldots,m
, let
V_{i}=\{(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n},\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{n})\in\mathbb{N}^{2n}:x^{\alpha_{1}}\cdots x^{\alpha_{n}}\partial^{\beta_{1}}\cdots\partial^{\beta_{n}}e_{i}
is not a multiple of any
LM(g_{j})

(1\leq j\leq s)\}
.
Then there exists a numerical polynomial
\chi(t)
in one indeterminate
t
such that:

(i)

\chi(r)=dim_{K}(M_{r})
for all sufficiently large
r\in\mathbb{Z}
, that is, there exists
r_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that the equality holds for all
r\geq r_{0}
.

(ii)

deg(\chi(t))\leq 2n
, and the polynomial
\chi(t)
can be represented as

(5.1)

\displaystyle\chi(t)=\sum^{2n}_{i=0}a_{i}\binom{t+i}{i},

where
a_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
, and
i=1,\ldots,2n
.

(iii)

(5.2)

\displaystyle\chi(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\omega_{i}(t)

where
\omega_{i}(t)=\omega_{V_{i}}(t)
, and
\omega_{V_{i}}(t)
is the Kolchin polynomial of the set
V_{i}\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{2n}

(1\leq i\leq n)
.

Proof.

With the notation of Theorem
9
,
the set
\pi(U_{r})
is a basis of the
K
-vector space
M_{r}
, for any
r\in\mathbb{N}
. The second part of the proof of that theorem shows that the restriction of the map
\pi
to
U_{r}
is bijective because
Ker(\pi|_{U_{r}})=0
. From this it follows that
dim_{k}M_{r}=Card~\pi(U_{r})=Card~U_{r}
.

Furthermore, for any
r\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle Card~U_{r}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}Card~V_{i}(r).

By Proposition
3
, for every
i=1,\ldots,m
, there exists a numerical polynomial
\omega_{i}(t)

in one indeterminate
t
, such that

\omega_{i}(r)=Card~V_{i}(r)
, for all sufficiently large
r\in\mathbb{N}
. Hence,
Card~U_{r}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\omega_{i}(r)
, for all sufficiently large
r
.
Proposition
3
also shows that
deg(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\omega_{i}(t))\leq 2n
, because each one of the terms in the sum has degree at most
2n
.
Therefore, the polynomial
\chi(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\omega_{i}(t)
satisfies all the conditions of the theorem.
∎

Definition 12
.

The polynomial
\chi(t)
, whose existence is established in Theorem
10
, is called the
Bernstein polynomial
of the
A_{n}
-module associated with the system
\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}\}
of generators of
M
(or with the filtration
\{M_{r}\}_{r\in\mathbb{N}}
).

Remark.
Formula (
5.2
) reduces the computation of the Bernstein polynomial of an
A_{n}
-module to the computation of the Kolchin dimension polynomial of subsets of
\mathbb{N}^{m}
.

Example 11
.

With the notation of Theorem
10
, let

, and let an

-module

be generated by a single element

The following result establishes the invariants of the Bernstein polynomial.

Theorem 14
.

Let
M
be a finitely generated
A_{n}
-module and let

\displaystyle\chi(t)=\sum^{2n}_{i=0}a_{i}\binom{t+i}{i}

be the Bernstein polynomial associated with some finite system of generators
\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}\}
of
M
(we write
\chi(t)
in the form (
3.1
) with integer coefficients
a_{i},~0\leq i\leq 2n
).
Then the numbers
a_{2n}
,
d=deg(\chi(t))
and
a_{d}
do not depend on the finite system of generators of the
A_{n}
-module
M
with which this polynomial is associated.

Proof.

Let
\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{p}\}
be another finite system of generators of the
A_{n}
-module
M
, and let
\{M_{r}\}_{r\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{M^{\prime}_{r}\}_{r\in\mathbb{N}}
be the filtrations (with respect to the Bernstein filtration) associated with the systems of generators
\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}\}
and
\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{p}\}
, respectively. That is, for
r\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\displaystyle M_{r}=\sum^{m}_{i=1}B_{r}f_{i},\quad\quad\text{and}\quad\quad M^{\prime}_{r}=\sum^{p}_{i=1}B_{r}g_{i},

where
\{B_{r}\}_{r\in\mathbb{N}}
is the Bernstein filtration with
B_{r}=\{D\in A_{n}:deg(D)\leq r\}
.
Furthermore, let

\displaystyle\chi^{*}(t)=\sum^{2n}_{i=1}b_{i}\binom{t+i}{i}

be the Bernstein polynomial associated with the system
\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{p}\}
.

Since
\bigcup_{r\in\mathbb{N}}M_{r}=\bigcup_{r\in\mathbb{N}}M^{\prime}_{r}=M
and all
M_{r}
and
M^{\prime}_{r}
are finitely generated
K
-linear vector spaces, there exists an element
r_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that

M_{r}\subseteq M^{\prime}_{r+r_{0}}
and

M^{\prime}_{r}\subseteq M_{r+r_{0}}

for all
r\in\mathbb{N}
.
It follows that

\chi_{M}(r)\leq\chi^{*}(r+r_{0})
and
\chi^{*}(r)\leq\chi(r+r_{0})
for all sufficiently large
r\in\mathbb{N}
.
Therefore,
deg(\chi)=deg(\chi^{*})=d
and

\displaystyle a_{d}

\displaystyle=d!\lim_{r\to\infty}\frac{\chi(r)}{r^{d}}

\displaystyle\leq d!\lim_{r\to\infty}\frac{\chi^{*}(r+r_{0})}{r^{d}}

\displaystyle=d!\lim_{r\to\infty}\frac{\chi^{*}(r)}{r^{d}}=b_{d}.

Similarly, we have
b_{d}\leq a_{d}
, and therefore
a_{d}=b_{d}
. Note that if
d<2n
, then
a_{2n}=0
. This completes the proof that
d
and
a_{d}
are independent of the choice of generators of
M
.
∎

Remark.
C. Dönch and A. Levin
[
7
, Theorem 5.9]
considered bifiltrations of finitely generated
D
-modules and bivariate numerical polynomials associated with such filtrations. It turned out that these bivariate polynomials carry some invariants of the corresponding
D
-modules that are not carried by univariate Bernstein polynomials.

The degree of the polynomial
\chi(t)
is called the
Bernstein dimension of
M
and is denoted by
d(M)
. Since
\chi(t)
is a numerical polynomial, the number
d!a_{d}
(where
d=deg(\chi(t))
and
a_{d}
is the coefficient of
\binom{t+d}{d}
in (
5.1
)) is a positive integer called the
multiplicity of
M
and is denoted by
m(M)
.

Theorem
14
, in the next section, shows that both the dimension and multiplicity are invariants of the polynomial
\chi(t)
, that is, they do not depend on the choice of the system of generators
\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}\}
of
M
with which the polynomial
\chi(t)
is associated.

6.
Generalized Krull Dimension of
D
-modules

As an application of the properties of the Bernstein polynomial, we now turn to a generalization of the concept of the Krull dimension of a commutative ring to the case of modules over the Weyl algebra
A_{n}
. Generalizations for the case of differential and difference modules similar to the one presented here are developed with corresponding results in
[
10
]
and
[
14
, Section 3.6]
, respectively. We first state some definitions.

Let
A_{n}
be a Weyl algebra,
M
a finitely generated
A_{n}
-module, and
U
a family of
A_{n}
-submodules of
M
.
Furthermore, let
\mathscr{B}_{U}
denote the set of all pairs
(N,N^{\prime})\in U\times U
such that
N\supseteq N^{\prime}
, and let
\bar{\mathbb{Z}}
be the augmented set of integers, that is, the set obtained by adjoining a new symbol,
\infty
, to the set
\mathbb{Z}
(
\bar{\mathbb{Z}}
is considered as a totally ordered set whose order
>
is the extension of the natural order of
\mathbb{Z}
such that
a<\infty
for any
a\in\mathbb{Z}
). With this notation and conventions, we have the following proposition.

Proposition 5
.

There exists a unique mapping

\mu_{U}:\mathscr{B}_{U}\rightarrow\bar{\mathbb{Z}}
,
with the following properties:

(i)

\mu_{U}(N,N^{\prime})\geq-1
for every pair
(N,N^{\prime})\in\mathscr{B}_{U}
;

(ii)

if
d\in\mathbb{N}
, then
\mu_{U}(N,N^{\prime})\geq d
if and only if
N\neq N^{\prime}
and there exists and infinite chain

N\supseteq N_{0}\supseteq N_{1}\supseteq\cdots\supseteq N^{\prime}

such that
\mu_{U}(N_{i-1},N_{i})\geq d-1
, for all
i=1,2\ldots
.

Proof.

We define the desired mapping
\mu_{U}
as follows.
If
N\in U
, we set
\mu_{U}(N,N)=-1
. If
(N,N^{\prime})\in\mathscr{B}_{U}
,
N\neq N^{\prime}
, and condition (ii) holds for all
d\in\mathbb{N}
, we set
\mu_{U}(N,N^{\prime})=\infty
. Finally, if a pair
(N,N^{\prime})\in\mathscr{B}_{U}
satisfies condition (ii) for some
d\in\mathbb{N}
, we define
\mu_{U}(N,N^{\prime})
as the greatest of such integers
d
. It is easy to see that the mapping
\mu
is well-defined, satisfies both conditions (i) and (ii), and is uniquely determined by these conditions.
∎

With the notation above we state the following definitions.

Definition 13
.

The least upper bound of the set

\{\mu_{U}(N,N^{\prime}):(N,N^{\prime})\in\mathscr{B}_{U}\}

is called the
type
of the
A_{n}(K)
-module
M
over the family of its
A_{n}
-submodules
U
, and is denoted by
type_{U}(M)
.

Definition 14
.

Let
A_{n}
be a Weyl algebra,
M
a finitely generated
A_{n}
-module, and
U
a family of
A_{n}
-submodules of
M
.
Then the least upper bound of the lengths of all chains

N_{0}\supsetneq N_{1}\supsetneq\cdots\supsetneq N_{p}
,
where
N_{i}\in U
and
\mu_{U}(N_{i-1},N_{i})=type_{U}M\;(i=1,\ldots,p
and the number
p
is considered as the length of the chain
)
, is called the
dimension
of the
A_{n}
-module
M
over the family
U
.

The dimension of an
A_{n}
-module
M
over a family of its
A_{n}
-submodules
U
is denoted by
dim_{U}M
.

Let
M
be a
A_{n}
-module and let
U
be a family of
A_{n}
-submodules of
M
. It is easy to see that if
type_{U}M<\infty
, then
dim_{U}M\geq 1
. At the same time, if
type_{U}M=\infty
, then
dim_{U}M
may equal zero.

In what follows we use the following notation, where
m(M)
denotes the multiplicity of
M
, defined in Section 4:

\displaystyle\delta(M)=\begin{cases}0\quad&\text{if}\quad d<2n,\\
m(M)\quad&\text{if}\quad d=2n.\end{cases}

The following proposition, proved in
[
6
, Chapter 7, § 5]
, shows that a good filtration
\Gamma
of an
A_{n}
-module
M
induces good filtrations on
A_{n}
-submodules and factor modules of
M
. These filtrations are called the filtrations
induced
by
\Gamma
.

Proposition 6
.

Let
M
be an
A_{n}
-module with the filtration
\Gamma=\{M_{r}\}_{r\in\mathbb{N}}
with respect to the Bernstein filtration
\{B_{r}\}_{r\in\mathbb{N}}
, and let
N
be a
A_{n}
-submodule of
M
. Then
\{N\cap M_{r}\}_{r\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{M_{r}/(M_{r}\cap N)\}_{r\in\mathbb{N}}
are good filtrations for
N
and
M/N
, respectively.

We will use the following lemma and proposition to arrive at the central result of this section, Theorem
15
, about the generalized Krull dimension and type of
D
-modules. First, we recall a definition from homological algebra
[
2
]
.

Let
R
be a ring. A sequence of
R
-modules and
R
-homomorphisms

\displaystyle\cdots\longrightarrow M_{i-1}\xrightarrow{\;\phi_{i}\;}M_{i}\xrightarrow{\phi_{i+1}}M_{i+1}\longrightarrow\cdots

is said to be
exact
at
M_{i}
if
Im(\phi_{i})=Ker(\phi_{i+1})
. The sequence is
exact
if it is exact at each
M_{i}
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
K
be a field, and let
0\rightarrow N\xrightarrow{i}M\xrightarrow{j}P\rightarrow 0
be a an exact sequence of finitely generated
A_{n}
-modules. Then
\delta(N)+\delta(P)=\delta(M)
.

Proof.

Let
\{M_{r}\}_{r\in\mathbb{N}}
be a good filtration of the
A_{n}
-modules
M
and set
N_{r}=i^{-1}(i(N)\cap M_{r})
and
P_{r}=j(M_{r})
for any
r\in\mathbb{N}
. Clearly, the filtration
\{P_{r}\}_{r\in\mathbb{N}}
of
P
is good, and by Proposition
6
the filtration
\{N_{r}\}_{r\in\mathbb{N}}
of
N
is also good. Let
\chi_{N}(t)
,
\chi_{M}(t)
and
\chi_{P}(t)
be the Bernstein dimension polynomials of
N
,
M
and
P
, respectively, associated with these filtrations. Since for all sufficiently large
r\in\mathbb{N}
the sequence

\displaystyle 0\rightarrow N_{r}\rightarrow M_{r}\rightarrow j(M_{r})\rightarrow 0

is exact, it follows that
dim_{K}N_{r}+dim_{K}\;j(M_{r})=dim_{K}M_{r}
, and so
\chi_{N}(t)+\chi_{P}(t)=\chi_{M}(t)
. Therefore, by the last statement in Proposition
2
, we have

\displaystyle\delta(M)

\displaystyle=\Delta^{2n}(\chi_{M}(t))=\Delta^{2n}\big(\chi_{N}(t)+\chi_{P}(t)\big)

\displaystyle=\Delta^{2n}(\chi_{N}(t))+\Delta^{2n}(\chi_{P}(t))=\delta(N)+\delta(P),

thus establishing the result.
∎

Proposition 7
.

Let
M
be a finitely generated
A_{n}
-module. Then the number
\delta(M)
is equal to the maximal number of elements of the module
M
that are linearly independent over
A_{n}
.

Proof.

First, we will show that
\delta(M)=0
if and only if every element of
M
is linearly dependent over
A_{n}
. Suppose that
\delta(M)=0
and, for the sake of contradiction, that there exists
z\in M
that is linearly independent over
A_{n}
. Then the map
\phi:A_{n}\rightarrow M
, defined by
\phi(D)=Dz
for every
D\in A_{n}
, is a monomorphism of
A_{n}
-modules. Applying Lemma
2
to the exact sequence of finitely generated
A_{n}
-modules
0\rightarrow A_{n}\xrightarrow{\phi}M\rightarrow M/\phi(A_{n})\rightarrow 0
, we have

\delta(A_{n})=\delta(M)-\delta(M/\phi(A_{n}))\leq\delta(M)=0
. But
\delta(A_{n})=m(A_{n})=1
, because
d(A_{n})=2n
. This contradiction proves that any element of
M
is linearly dependent over
A_{n}
.

Conversely, suppose that every element of
M
is linearly dependent over
A_{n}
. Let
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{k}
be generators of the
A_{n}
-modules
M
(so that
M=\sum^{k}_{i=0}A_{n}\xi_{i})
. Let
N_{i}=A_{n}\xi_{i}~(1\leq i\leq k)
, and
L_{i}
denote the kernel of the mapping
\psi_{i}:A_{n}\rightarrow N_{i}~(1\leq i\leq k)
such that
\psi_{i}(u)=u\xi_{i}
for any
u\in A_{n}
. By assumption,
\xi_{i}
is linearly dependent over
A_{n}
, and therefore,
L_{i}\neq 0~(1\leq i\leq k)
. We have
\delta(L_{i})\neq 0
(because
\delta(L_{i})=0
implies that all elements of
A_{n}
are linearly dependent, giving
\xi_{i}u\neq 0~(0\neq u\in L_{i})
, and
A_{n}
would have zero divisors, which is not the case). Applying Lemma
2
to the exact sequence of finitely generated
A_{n}
-modules
0\rightarrow L_{i}\rightarrow A_{n}\xrightarrow{\psi_{i}}N_{i}\rightarrow 0
, we obtain that
\delta(L_{i})+\delta(N_{i})=\delta(A_{n})=1
. Since each nonzero element of
L_{i}
is linearly independent over
A_{n}
, the above reasoning shows that
\delta(L_{i})\neq 0
, and hence
\delta(L_{i})\geq 1~(1\leq i\leq k)
. Therefore,
0\leq\delta(N_{i})=\delta(A_{n})-\delta(L_{i})=1-\delta(L_{i})\leq 0
, and so
\delta(N_{i})=0~(1\leq i\leq k)
. Since
M=\sum^{k}_{i=1}N_{i}
, we have
0\leq\delta(M)=\delta\left(\sum^{k}_{i=1}N_{i}\right)\leq\sum^{k}_{i=1}\delta(N_{i})=0
. To prove the last inequality, it is sufficient to show that
\delta(N_{1}+N_{2})\leq\delta(N_{1})+\delta(N_{2})
for all finitely generated
A_{n}
-modules
N_{1}
and
N_{2}
. Let
M^{\prime}=N_{1}+N_{2}
. Applying Lemma
2
to the canonical exact sequence
0\rightarrow N_{1}\rightarrow M^{\prime}\rightarrow M^{\prime}/N_{1}\rightarrow 0
we obtain that
\delta(M^{\prime})=\delta(N_{1})+\delta(M^{\prime}/N_{1})=\delta(N_{1})+\delta((N_{1}+N_{2})/N_{1})+\delta(N_{1})+\delta(N_{2}/(N_{1}\cap N_{2}))\leq\delta(N_{1})+\delta(N_{2})
. Thus, it has been shown that every element of
M
is linearly independent over
A_{n}
if and only if
\delta(M)=0
.

Now, to complete the proof of the proposition, let
p
be the maximal number of elements of the
A_{n}
-module
M
that are linearly independent over
A_{n}
. Let
\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{p}\}
be any system of elements of
M
that are linearly independent over
A_{n}
, and let
F=\sum^{p}_{i=1}A_{n}z_{i}
. Then
\{\sum^{p}_{i=1}A_{n_{r}}z_{i}\}_{r\in\mathbb{N}}
is a good filtration of
F
. By Proposition
3
(ii), the Bernstein polynomial associated with this filtration has the form
\chi(t)=p\binom{t+2n}{2n}
, and so
\delta(F)=\Delta^{2n}\chi(t)=p
. Furthermore, the maximality of the system
\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{p}\}
, linearly independent over
A_{n}
, implies that every element of the finitely generated module
M/F
is lineraly dependent over
A_{n}
, and hence
\delta(M/F)=0
. Applying Lemma
2
to the exact sequence of finitely generated
A_{n}
-modules

\displaystyle 0\rightarrow F\rightarrow M\rightarrow M/F\rightarrow 0,

we obtain that
\delta(M)=\delta(F)+\delta(M/F)=\delta(F)=p
.
∎

Theorem 15
.

Let
M
be a finitely generated
A_{n}
-module, and let
U
be the family of all
A_{n}
-submodules of
M
. Then we have the following:

(i)

If
\delta(M)>0
, then
type_{U}M=2n

and

dim_{U}M=\delta(M)
;

(ii)

If
\delta(M)=0
, then
type_{U}M<2n
.

Proof.

Let
\{M_{r}\}_{r\in\mathbb{N}}
be a finite system of generators of the
A_{n}
-module
M
, and let

M_{r}=\sum^{m}_{i=1}B_{r}f_{i}
be the corresponding filtration of
M
with respect to the Bernstein filtration
\{B_{r}\}_{r\in\mathbb{N}}
, where
B_{r}=\{D\in A_{n}:deg(D)\leq r\}
. Let

N,L\in U
, with
N\supseteq L
.
By Proposition
6
,
\{N\cap M_{r}\}_{r\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\{L\cap M_{r}\}_{r\in\mathbb{N}}
are good filtrations of the
A_{n}
-submodules
N
and
L
, respectively.
Thus, we can consider the corresponding Bernstein polynomials

\chi_{N}(t)
and
\chi_{L}(t)
associated with these filtrations. These polynomials describe the dimension of the vector space
\{N\cap M_{r}\}_{r\in\mathbb{N}}
over the field
K
, and the dimension of the vector space
\{L\cap M_{r}\}_{r\in\mathbb{N}}
over the field
K
, respectively. The inclusion
N\supseteq L
, implies

N\cap M_{r}\supseteq L\cap M_{r}
for any
r\in\mathbb{N}
, and
thus

\chi_{L}(t)\leq\chi_{N}(t)
(where
\leq
denotes the natural order on the set of numerical polynomials in one indeterminate
t
:
f(t)\leq g(t)
if and only if
f(r)\leq g(r)
for all sufficiently large
r\in\mathbb{N}
).
Furthermore,
N=L
if and only if
\chi_{N}(t)=\chi_{L}(t)
(indeed, if
\chi_{N}(t)=\chi_{L}(t)
but
N\subsetneq L
, then there exists
r_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
(N\cap M_{r})\subsetneq(L\cap M_{r})
for all
r>r_{0}
, in which case
\chi_{N}(t)<\chi_{L}(t)
, contradicting our assumption).

Next, we will prove that if
(N,L)\in\mathscr{B}_{U}=\{(P,Q)\in U\times U:P\supseteq Q\}
and

\mu_{U}(N,L)\geq d
(
d\in\mathbb{Z},d\geq-1
), then
deg(\chi_{N}(t)-\chi_{L}(t))\geq d
.
We proceed by induction on
d
. Since
deg(\chi_{N}(t)-\chi_{L}(t))\geq-1
for any pair
(P,Q)\in\mathscr{B}_{U}
, and
deg(\chi_{N}(t)-\chi_{L}(t))\geq 0
if
L\supsetneq N
(as we noted above, in this case
\chi_{L}(t)<\chi_{N}(t)
), then the statement is true for
d=-1
and
d=0
.

Now, let
d>0
, and for any
i\in\mathbb{N}
, with
i<d
, suppose the inequality
\mu_{U}(N,L)\geq i

((N,L)\in\mathscr{B}_{U}
) implies that
deg(\chi_{N}(t)-\chi_{L}(t))\geq i
. Assume further that
(N,L)\in\mathscr{B}_{U}
and
\mu_{U}(N,L)\geq d
holds. Then there exists an infinite strictly descending chain

\displaystyle N=N_{0}\supsetneq N_{1}\supsetneq\cdots\supsetneq L

of
A_{n}
-submodules of
M
such that
\mu_{U}(N_{i-1},N_{i})\geq d-1
for
i=1,2,\ldots
. If
deg(\chi_{N_{i-1}}(t)-\chi_{N_{i}}(t))\geq d
for some
i\geq 1
, then
deg(\chi_{N}(t)-\chi_{L}(t))\geq d
and the statement would be proven.

For the sake of contradiction, suppose that
deg(\chi_{N_{i-1}}(t)-\chi_{N_{i}}(t))=d-1
for all
i=1,2,\ldots
. Then every polynomial
\chi_{N_{i-1}}(t)-\chi_{N_{i}}(t)
(
i\in\mathbb{N},i\geq 1
) can be written in the form

\displaystyle\chi_{N_{i-1}}(t)-\chi_{N_{i}}(t)=\sum^{d-1}_{k=0}c_{i,k}\binom{t+k}{k},

where
c_{i,0},\ldots,c_{i,d-1}\in\mathbb{Z}
and
c_{i,d-1}>0

(see Proposition
2
).
In this case,

\chi_{N}(t)-\chi_{N_{i}}(t)=\sum^{d-1}_{k=0}c^{\prime}_{ik}\binom{t+k}{k}
,
where
c^{\prime}_{i,0},\ldots,c^{\prime}_{i,d-1}\in\mathbb{Z}
and

c^{\prime}_{1,d-1}=\sum^{i}_{\nu=1}c_{\nu,d-1}
.
Therefore,
c^{\prime}_{1,d-1}<c^{\prime}_{2,d-1}<\cdots
. If

deg(\chi_{N}(t)-\chi_{L}(t))=d-1
, then
\chi_{N}(t)-\chi_{L}(t)=\sum^{d-1}_{k=0}a_{k}\binom{t+k}{k}
. Since

\chi_{N_{i-1}}(r)-\chi_{N_{i}}(r)<\chi_{N}(r)-\chi_{L}(r)
for all sufficiently large
r
, then
c^{\prime}_{1,d-1}<c^{\prime}_{2,d-1}<\cdots<a_{d-1}
, a contradiction because
a_{d-1}
is a fixed number and thus it is impossible to have a strictly increasing chain of natural numbers less than
a_{d-1}
. Therefore,

deg(\chi_{N}(t)-\chi_{L}(t))\geq d
for any pair
(N,L)\in\mathscr{B}_{U}
with
\mu_{U}(N,L)\geq d
.

This last statement, together with the fact that
deg(\chi_{N}(t)-\chi_{L}(t))\leq 2n
for any
(N,L)\in\mathscr{B}_{U}
, implies that
\mu_{U}(N,L)\leq 2n
for all pairs
(N,L)\in\mathscr{B}_{U}
. It follows that

(6.1)

\displaystyle type_{U}M\leq 2n.

Next we will prove that
type_{U}M\geq 2n
. If
\delta(M)=p>0
, then Proposition
7
shows that
M
contains
p

elements
z_{1},\ldots,z_{p}
that are linearly independent over
A_{n}
. If
U^{\prime}
is the family of all
A_{n}
-submodules of the
A_{n}
-module
A_{n}z_{1}
, then
type_{U^{\prime}}A_{n}z_{1}\leq type_{U}M
. Thus, to complete the proof of the first statement of the theorem, it is sufficient to consider an
A_{n}
-module
M=A_{n}z_{1}
, where
z_{1}
is linearly independent over
A_{n}
, and show that
type_{U^{\prime}}A_{n}z_{1}\geq 2n
.
Let
U^{\prime\prime}
denote the family of all
A_{n}
-submodules of
A_{n}z_{1}
that can be represented as finite sums of
A_{n}
-modules of the form
A_{n}x^{\alpha}\partial^{\beta}z_{1}
, where
\alpha
and
\beta

are multi-indices from
\mathbb{N}^{n}
. We are going to use induction on
n
to show that
\mu_{U^{\prime\prime}}(A_{n}z_{1},0)\geq 2n
. If
n=0
, the inequality is obvious. So let
n>0
. Since the element
z_{1}
is linearly independent over
A_{n}
, we have the following strictly descending chain

\displaystyle A_{n}z_{1}\supsetneq A_{n}x_{n}z_{1}\supsetneq A_{n}x^{2}_{n}z_{1}\supsetneq\cdots\supsetneq 0

of elements of
U^{\prime\prime}
. Let us show that
\mu_{U^{\prime\prime}}(A_{n}x^{i-1}_{n}z_{1},A_{n}x^{i}_{n}z_{1})\geq 2n-1
for all
i=1,2,\ldots
. Let
L=A_{n}x^{i-1}_{n}z_{1}/A_{n}x^{i}_{n}z_{1}
and let
y
be the image element
x^{i-1}_{n}z_{1}
under the natural epimorphism
A_{n}x^{i-1}_{n}z_{1}\rightarrow L
.
Then
x_{n}y=0
and
x_{n}\theta^{\prime}y=\theta^{\prime}x_{n}y=0
for any element
\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta^{\prime}
, where

\displaystyle\Theta^{\prime}=\{\prod^{n}_{i=1}\prod^{n}_{j=1}x^{\alpha_{i}}_{i}\partial^{\beta_{j}}_{j}:\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{n},\partial_{1},\ldots,\partial_{n}\in\mathbb{N}\}.

Let
A^{\prime}_{n}=A_{n-1}\partial_{n}
, where
A_{n-1}
is the Weyl algebra generated by
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n-1}
and
\partial_{1},\ldots,\partial_{n-1}
.
Then
L=A_{n}^{\prime}y
, and the element
y
is linearly independent over
A_{n}^{\prime}
.

Furthermore,
\mu_{U^{\prime\prime}}(A_{n}x^{i-1}_{n}z_{1},A_{n}x^{i}_{n}z_{1})=\mu_{U^{\prime\prime}_{1}}(L,0)
where
U^{\prime\prime}_{1}
is the family of all
A_{n}^{\prime}
-submodules of
L
that can be represented as finite sums of the
A_{n}^{\prime}
-modules of the form
A_{n}^{\prime}\theta^{\prime}y~(\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta)
.
The chain
L\supseteq A_{n-1}\partial_{n}y\supseteq A_{n-1}\partial^{2}_{n}y\supseteq\cdots\supseteq\{0\}

shows that if
U^{*}
is the set of all
A_{n-1}
-submodules of
C_{i}=A_{n}\partial^{i}_{n}y/A_{n-1}\partial^{i+1}_{n}y~(i\in\mathbb{N})
, then

\mu_{U}(A_{n}z_{1},0)\geq\mu_{U^{*}}(C_{i},0)+2\geq 2(n-1)
.
By the induction hypothesis,

\mu_{U^{\prime\prime}_{1}}(L,0)\geq 2n-1
, and so
\mu_{U^{\prime\prime}}(A_{n}x^{i-1}_{n}z_{1},A_{n}x^{i}_{n}z_{1})\geq 2n-1~(i=1,2,\ldots)
. Thus
\mu_{U^{\prime\prime}}(A_{n}z_{1},0)\geq 2n
.

Hence,

\displaystyle type_{U}M\geq type_{U^{\prime}}A_{n}z_{1}\geq type_{U^{\prime\prime}}A_{n}z_{1}\geq 2n.

Combining these inequalities with (
6.1
), we obtain

\displaystyle type_{U}M=2n.

The proof of this last equality also shows that
type_{U^{\prime}_{i}}A_{n}z_{i}=2n
for every
i=1,2,\ldots
, where
U^{\prime}_{i}
denotes the family o all
A_{n}
-submodules of the
A_{n}
-module
A_{n}z_{i}
. Therefore,

\mu_{U}\left(\sum^{k}_{i=1}A_{n}z_{i},\sum^{k-1}_{i=1}A_{n}z_{i}\right)=2n=type_{U}M
for every
k=1,\ldots,q
, and the chain

\displaystyle M=\sum^{q}_{i=1}A_{n}z_{i}\supsetneq\sum^{q-1}_{i=1}A_{n}z_{i}\supsetneq\cdots\supsetneq A_{n}z_{1}\supsetneq 0

leads to the inequality

(6.2)

\displaystyle dim_{U}M\geq\delta(M).

Let
N_{0}\supsetneq N_{1}\supsetneq\cdots\supseteq N_{p}
be a chain of
A_{n}
-submodules of
M
such that
\mu_{U}(N_{i-1},N_{i})=type_{U}M=2n
, for
i=1,\ldots,p
. The arguments used at the beginning of the proof show that the degree of each polynomial
\chi_{N_{i-1}}(t)-\chi_{N_{i}}(t)~(1\leq i\leq p)
is equal to
2n
, and so every such polynomial can be written as

\displaystyle\chi_{N_{i-1}}(t)-\chi_{N_{i}}(t)=\sum^{2n}_{k=0}a_{ik}\binom{t+k}{k},

where
a_{ik}\in\mathbb{Z}~(0\leq k\leq 2n)
. It follows that

\displaystyle\chi_{N_{0}}(t)-\chi_{N_{p}}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum^{p}_{i=1}\big(\chi_{N_{i-1}}(t)-\chi_{N_{i}}(t)\big)

\displaystyle=\sum^{2n}_{k=0}a^{\prime}_{k}\binom{t+k}{k},

where
a^{\prime}_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}~(0\leq k\leq 2n)
.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\chi_{N_{0}}(t)-\chi_{N_{p}}(t)\leq\chi_{N_{0}}(t)\leq\chi_{M}(t)=\sum^{2n}_{k=0}a_{k}\binom{t+k}{k},

where
a_{2n}=\delta(M)
(we write
f(t)\leq g(t)
for two numerical polynomials
f(t)
and
g(t)
if
f(r)\leq g(r)
for all sufficiently large
r\in\mathbb{N}
). It follows that
a_{2n}\geq a^{\prime}_{2n}\geq p
, and hence
\delta(M)\geq dim_{U}M
. Combining the last inequality with (
6.2
) we obtain the desired inequality
dim_{U}M=\delta(M)
.

Finally, we prove Part (ii) of the theorem. If
\delta(M)=0
, then for any pair
(N,L)\in\mathscr{B}_{U}
, we have the equality
\delta(N)=\delta(L)=0
, and hence
deg(\chi_{N}(t))<2n,deg(\chi_{L}(t))<2n
, and
deg\big(\chi_{N}(t)-\chi_{L}(t)\big)<2n
. As it has been shown above, the last inequality implies the inequality
\mu_{U}(N,L)<2n
. Therefore, if
\delta(M)=0
, then
type_{U}M<2n
.
∎
