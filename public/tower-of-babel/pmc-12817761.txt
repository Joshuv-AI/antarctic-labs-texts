Oral health–related quality of life and its determinants among the Saudi adult population: a cross-sectional analytical study
Authors: Azhar Iqbal, Farooq Ahmad Chaudhary, Saud Hamdan Almaeen, Muhsen Alnasser, Nadeem Baig, Yasir Dilshad Siddiqui, Osama Khattak, Mohammed Mustafa, Mohmed Isaqali Karobari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOral Health-Related Quality of Life (OHRQoL) reflects the subjective impact of oral health on individuals’ social, psychological, and functional well-being. While oral health challenges in Saudi Arabia persist despite advancements in healthcare, there is a scarcity of research examining OHRQoL across various demographic groups. This study aimed to evaluate the OHRQoL of adults in Saudi Arabia using the Oral Health Impact Profile (OHIP-14) and examine its association with sociodemographic factors and self-perceived oral health behaviors.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 1054 participants from all regions of Saudi Arabia, recruited through stratified sampling. Data were collected using a structured, validated Arabic version of the OHIP-14 questionnaire and analyzed using descriptive statistics, chi-square tests, t-tests, ANOVA, and multiple logistic regression.ResultsThe mean OHIP-14 score was 12.6 (SD: 11.7), with 89.5% of participants reporting at least one problem affecting OHRQoL. Higher OHIP-14 scores, indicating poorer OHRQoL, were significantly associated with being male, aged 40–49 years, residing in the southern region, being married, working full-time, having a higher monthly income, smoking, and reporting poor general and oral health. Behavioral factors, such as irregular dental checkups, infrequent tooth brushing, and bad breath, also contributed significantly to worse OHRQoL. Regression analysis identified age, place of residence, dental checkups, and bad breath as significant predictors of problems and frequent problems on the OHIP-14 scale.ConclusionThis study highlights significant variations in OHRQoL among the Saudi population, driven by sociodemographic factors, oral health behaviors, and self-perceived oral health. The findings emphasize the importance of tailored public health interventions focusing on oral hygiene awareness, regular dental checkups, and access to care, particularly in underserved regions.Keywords: OHRQoL, OHIP-14, Sociodemographic factors, Oral health behaviors, Public health interventions

Introduction

Health, as defined by the World Health Organization (WHO), encompasses a state of complete physical, mental, and social well-being, beyond merely the absence of disease or infirmity [1]. Oral health is an integral component of general health and significantly impacts Health-Related Quality of Life (HRQoL) []. Oral health is an integral component of general health and significantly impacts Health-Related Quality of Life (HRQoL) [2]. Oral Health-Related Quality of Life (OHRQoL) reflects an individual’s subjective perception of how oral health conditions affect their social, psychological, and functional well-being [1]. This approach helps identify how oral health influences an individual’s quality of life across multiple dimensions.

In recent decades, researchers have created various psychometric tools to evaluate OHRQoL [3, 4]. Tools like the Oral Health Impact Profile (OHIP-14) are widely used to assess OHRQoL, and this instrument has been adapted and validated in different languages including Arabic Language [5–8]. This questionnaire captures the impact of oral health on daily life, providing insights into the social and psychological dimensions often overlooked in traditional clinical outcomes. While research on OHRQoL is growing, much of the focus has been on older adults or individuals with specific dental issues [9, 10].

In Saudi Arabia, despite significant advancements in healthcare infrastructure, oral health challenges such as dental caries, periodontal diseases, and tooth loss persist [11]. Cultural, dietary, and socioeconomic influences—such as high sugar consumption and the traditional use of miswak for oral hygiene—play an important role in shaping oral health outcomes [12]. Moreover, rapidly changing lifestyles and cultural practices, coupled with socioeconomic disparities, add further complexity to oral health outcomes and their perception, emphasizing the necessity of localized research. However, most studies assessing Oral Health-Related Quality of Life (OHRQoL) in Saudi Arabia have predominantly focused on older adults or individuals with pre-existing dental conditions [9, 11]. This study aims to evaluate the OHRQoL of adults in Saudi Arabia using the OHIP-14 tool, examining its relationship with sociodemographic and self-perceived behavioral factors. By addressing the psychosocial dimensions of oral health, this research seeks to bridge the gap between clinical outcomes and patient-reported outcomes, contributing to improved oral health and quality of life across the Kingdom.

Materials and methods

This cross-sectional analytical study was conducted among the adult Saudi population. The study participants were recruited from various regions across Saudi Arabia, namely the Northern, Southern, Eastern, Western, and Central regions, to ensure a representative sample of the adult population.

The recruitment process was carried out in community-based environments, dental healthcare facilities, and via public announcements to achieve a diverse sample with varying sociodemographic and oral health backgrounds. This strategy was implemented to minimize any potential regional bias.

Saudi nationals aged 18 and above who were willing to provide informed consent were eligible for the study, while those with systemic conditions or cognitive impairments that could affect their ability to complete the questionnaire, as well as those who chose not to participate, were excluded.

A stratified sampling technique was applied to guarantee proportional representation based on gender, age, and regional distribution. The sample size was calculated with the single proportion option to estimate the proportion of individuals experiencing oral health issues and related frequent problems. Parameters included a small effect size of 0.05, a significance level of 0.05, and a study power of 85%. The initial sample size was estimated at 900 participants. To account for a 15% allowance for missing data, the adjusted sample size was calculated as 900 ÷ (1 − 0.15) = 1058 participants. A small effect size was chosen to ensure sufficient statistical power and enhance the reliability of the study’s conclusions.

Ethical approval was obtained from the Local Committee of Bioethics (LCBE) at Jouf University (Approval No: 1–09-45). The study adhered to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Before data collection, participants were informed about the purpose of the study, and written informed consent was obtained. Participation was voluntary, and confidentiality and anonymity were assured by excluding personally identifiable information during data processing and analysis. Being principal investigator, my contact details were given at the end of informed consent form so that participants can contact me to clarify any questions and to ensure completeness of the responses.

The validated Arabic version of the OHIP-14 tool, as adapted by Habashneh et al. [5], was used in this study. The tool demonstrated satisfactory internal consistency with a Cronbach’s alpha value of > 0.70. For logistical convenience, the adapted questionnaire was converted into a Google Form. A pilot study was conducted on 25 patients at the dental clinics of the College of Dentistry, Jouf University, yielding a Cronbach’s alpha value of 0.86, confirming the tool’s reliability. Data collection was conducted from June 2024 to December 2024. The structured questionnaire consisted of three sections: First section gathered information about Sociodemographic factors (age, gender, marital status, educational level, employment status, current health status, and regional origin). Second section assessed Oral Health-Related Quality of Life (OHRQoL) using the Arabic version of the OHIP-14. The instrument comprises 14 items grouped into seven domains: functional limitation, physical pain, psychological discomfort, physical disability, psychological disability, social disability, and handicap. Items were scored on a 5-point Likert scale (0 = never to 4 = very often), with higher scores indicating poorer OHRQoL. Last section assessed the self-perception of oral health and behavior with seven closed-ended questions about participants’ perceptions of their dental and gingival health, frequency of tooth brushing, smoking habits, dental checkups, bad breath, and the number of teeth present.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data, reporting means, standard deviations (SD), frequencies, and percentages. Pearson’s chi-square test was applied to examine associations between categorical variables and problems reported on the OHIP-14. Independent t-tests and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were conducted to assess mean differences in OHIP-14 scores across study variables. Multiple logistic regression analysis was employed to identify significant predictors of experiencing problems and frequent problems on the OHIP-14 scale. Parametric test results were compared with non-parametric test results, which yielded consistent conclusions. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 29, with a p-value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 1054 participants responded to the survey, yielding a response rate of 99.6%. The sample comprised 47.9% males and 52.1% females. Most participants belonged to the 30–39 age group (39.8%) and the northern region (46.4%). Additionally, the majority were married (72.8%), had a high school education (41.5%), worked full-time (42.1%), reported a monthly income of SAR 1001–2000 (38.3%), and were non-smokers (46.0%) (Table 1).

Attending dental checkups significantly reduced the likelihood of experiencing problems, with participants being 67% less likely to report problems compared to those who did not attend (AOR = 0.33, p < 0.001). Participants reporting bad breath were 18.8 times more likely to experience problems compared to those without bad breath (AOR = 18.77, p < 0.001). Those unsure about their breath status were 4.2 times more likely to experience problems than those without bad breath (AOR = 4.23, p = 0.007).

For frequent problems, significant predictors included sex, age, place of residence, dental checkups, and bad breath. Males were 2.2 times more likely to report frequent problems compared to females (AOR = 2.19, p < 0.001). Participants aged 40–49 years were 3.9 times more likely to experience frequent problems compared to those aged 21–29 (AOR = 3.91, p < 0.001), and those aged 50–59 years were 93% more likely to report frequent problems (AOR = 1.93, p = 0.015).

Participants from the southern (AOR = 5.16, p < 0.001), eastern (AOR = 3.66, p < 0.001), and central (AOR = 2.59, p = 0.002) regions were significantly more likely to report frequent problems compared to those from the northern region. Conversely, participants from the western region were 49% less likely to report frequent problems (AOR = 0.51, p = 0.016).

Finally, participants unsure about their breath status were 64% less likely to report frequent problems compared to those without bad breath (AOR = 0.36, p < 0.001).

Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate the Oral Health-Related Quality of Life (OHRQoL) among the Saudi adult population using the OHIP-14 instrument and examine its association with sociodemographic factors and self-perceived oral health behaviors. The findings highlight significant variations in OHRQoL across demographic, behavioral, and health-related variables, offering insights into the psychosocial dimensions of oral health in Saudi Arabia.

The mean OHIP-14 score in this study (15.6 ± 11.7) suggests a moderate impact of oral health issues on participants’ quality of life. This is consistent with similar studies conducted in Saudi Arabia, such as those by [13] who reported comparable scores among middle-aged adults. In addition, the regional differences observed in our study, where participants from the southern region reported significantly higher OHIP-14 scores (27.8), highlight the influence of geographic and cultural factors on participants perceived oral health–related quality of life. This finding cannot be directly compared with previous research, as, to our knowledge, this is the first study to examine regional differences in OHIP scores within Saudi Arabia.

In contrast, the mean OHIP-14 scores in this study are significantly higher compared to studies conducted in developed countries. For instance, studies on Norwegian adults [14] reported a mean OHIP-14 score of 4.1 (SD = 6.2), while in Sweden, the mean score for the general population was 6.4 (SD = 7.1) [15], and among adults in Hong Kong, the mean score was 6.3 (SD = 5.8) [16]. The higher OHIP-14 scores observed in this study could be attributed to several plausible reasons, such as differences in healthcare systems, levels of oral health awareness, and access to preventive dental services. In developed countries like Norway, Sweden, and Hong Kong, robust healthcare infrastructure, comprehensive public dental programs, and widespread oral health education initiatives may contribute to better oral health outcomes and lower OHIP-14 scores. Conversely, in Saudi Arabia, varying levels of access to care, regional disparities, and cultural perceptions about oral health might contribute to the higher impact of oral health issues on quality of life.

Variation is seen in gender as well, with males reporting higher OHIP-14 scores (17.2) than females (14.1), suggesting greater perceived impacts on oral health-related quality of life among men. This finding contrasts with studies conducted in other contexts, where women reported worse oral health-related quality of life (OHRQoL). For instance, a study in Brazil found a significantly greater prevalence of negative oral health impacts among women compared to men [17]. Similarly, in Indonesia, women perceived a larger number of negative impacts and reported worse OHRQoL [18]. The higher OHIP-14 scores reported by males in this study may be attributed to differences in oral health behaviors, as men generally exhibit lower rates of regular toothbrushing and dental checkups compared to women. This aligns with previous research highlighting the role of oral hygiene practices in influencing OHRQoL [19, 20]. Additionally, cultural and societal factors unique to Saudi Arabia may also contribute to these findings, as men’s healthcare-seeking behaviors and attitudes toward oral health might differ significantly from those observed in other countries.

Although higher income is often assumed to be protective of health [12], our results revealed an unexpected pattern. Participants with monthly incomes between 4001 and 5000 SAR reported the highest mean OHIP-14 scores (30.6), indicating poorer oral health-related quality of life, coupled with the highest prevalence of problems (97.4%) and frequent problems (95.3%). Interestingly, both the lowest-income group (0–1000 SAR) and the highest-income group ( > 5000 SAR) reported comparatively lower OHIP-14 scores. This finding may suggest that individuals in the upper-middle income range face unique pressures, such as lifestyle habits, dietary choices, or increased health expectations—that negatively influence their perceived oral health.

Age was another significant predictor, with participants aged 40–49 years and 50–59 years reporting worse OHRQoL than younger age groups. This finding aligns with previous research in Saudi Arabia and globally, where middle-aged adults often experience the cumulative effects of oral health problems, such as periodontal disease and tooth loss, impacting their quality of life [13, 21].

Oral health behaviors, dental checkups and brushing frequency, significantly influenced OHRQoL [19]. Participants who did not attend dental checkups were more likely to report poor OHRQoL, consistent with findings from studies in New Zealand and the United Kingdom [22, 23]. Additionally, the strong association and worse OHIP-14 scores emphasize the psychosocial impact of halitosis, consistent with research by Briceag et al., which highlighted its negative association with self-esteem and social interactions [24].

This study is not without limitations. The study’s cross-sectional nature prohibits causal inferences about the relationships between sociodemographic factors, oral health behaviors, and OHRQoL. Although, a stratified sampling approach was used, certain subpopulations, such as individuals from lower-income brackets, may have been underrepresented. The findings may not fully represent the entire Saudi population due to regional variations in oral health access and cultural practices. Other potential determinants of OHRQoL, such as psychological stress or dental anxiety, were not assessed in this study.

Conclusion

This study highlights significant variations in OHRQoL among the Saudi population, influenced by sociodemographic factors, oral health behaviors, and self-perceived oral health status. The findings underscore the need for tailored public health interventions to improve oral health awareness and access to care, particularly in underserved regions such as the southern and central parts of Saudi Arabia. Regular dental checkups and effective oral hygiene practices should be promoted to enhance OHRQoL. Future research should explore longitudinal associations and integrate psychosocial determinants of oral health to provide a comprehensive understanding of OHRQoL in Saudi Arabia.
