Evaluating GNSS infrastructure readiness for IGS contribution: the case of King Sejong Station, Antarctica
Authors: Seung-Jun Lee, Hong-Sik Yun, Hyung-Mi Yoon, Sang-Hoon Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study evaluates the readiness of the GNSS infrastructure at King Sejong Station (KSEJ), Antarctica, for integration into the International GNSS Service (IGS) network. Dual-epoch GNSS observations from 2009 and 2023 were reprocessed using the GAMIT/GLOBK 10.7 software suite in the ITRF2014 reference frame to assess long-term positional stability. The results show average annual horizontal velocities of 22 mm/yr toward the north-northeast, consistent with regional crustal motion driven by post-glacial rebound and tectonic processes. Signal quality analysis indicates a data availability of 99.37% and a cycle slip rate of 0.10%, both within IGS thresholds, although elevated multipath values were detected due to nearby infrastructure and limited azimuthal visibility, underscoring the need for site-specific mitigation. To ensure vertical reference consistency, geodetic leveling was conducted in 2023 to connect the tide gauge benchmark with nearby GNSS control points. A new temporary benchmark was installed following the loss of the original 2009 marker, enabling reliable vertical datum transfer and integration of tide gauge data with GNSS-derived heights. Overall, KSEJ meets most IGS requirements but requires infrastructural upgrades to address multipath interference, metadata automation, and data transmission reliability. These findings highlight the station’s potential as a high-latitude geodetic reference site, contributing to global reference frame densification, sea level monitoring, and geodynamic studies in Antarctica.Keywords: King Sejong Station (KSEJ), GNSS infrastructure, IGS network integration, Long-term positional stability, Crustal displacement, Multipath interference, Tide gauge dataSubject terms: Climate sciences, Environmental sciences, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Global Navigation Satellite System (GNSS) technology has become an essential tool for geodetic applications, enabling high-precision positioning and long-term monitoring of crustal deformation worldwide. In polar regions such as Antarctica, where traditional geodetic methods face logistical and environmental challenges, GNSS offers a reliable solution for tracking geodynamic processes including glacial isostatic adjustment, tectonic uplift, and regional strain accumulation. As a result, the deployment of continuously operating GNSS stations (CORS) has gained strategic importance for scientific and operational purposes in the polar sciences.

GNSS applications in Antarctic geodynamics are extensive and growing. Long-term GNSS networks have provided crucial observations of bedrock motion, contributing to the understanding of plate tectonics, post-seismic deformation, and glacial isostatic adjustment1,2. In Victoria Land, GNSS data have revealed the activation of major tectonic faults and distinguished between vertical and lateral crustal movements2.

Recent advances in GNSS instrumentation and data processing have further improved positioning reliability in challenging environments. The integration of multi-constellation, multi-frequency GNSS receivers with enhanced error modeling has enabled robust performance even in remote polar regions3,5. Rigorous metadata reassessment and consistent reprocessing strategies have yielded coordinate time series with minimal bias, extending spatiotemporal coverage for geodynamic applications1. Field validation campaigns have confirmed horizontal precision at the 1–2.5 mm level and vertical precision near 2.5 mm, supporting high-accuracy geodynamic monitoring4. Collectively, these technological and methodological advancements have transformed GNSS-based space geodesy, pushing accuracy toward the millimeter scale and enabling more robust interpretation of geophysical processes in Antarctica3,5.

These developments underscore GNSS’s transformative role in polar geodynamics, offering consistent, high-resolution data for monitoring Earth’s most remote and dynamic regions.

King Sejong Station (KSEJ), operated by the Republic of Korea, is one of the few GNSS stations located in the maritime Antarctic region. Established in 1988 and equipped with a permanent GNSS receiver since 2009, KSEJ has been used for various geodetic and geophysical studies. However, the station is not yet registered in the International GNSS Service (IGS) network, which limits its integration into global reference frames and its contribution to international datasets. Recent improvements in instrumentation, data quality, and environmental monitoring at the station have raised the question of whether KSEJ now satisfies the technical and operational requirements for IGS inclusion.

Understanding crustal deformation around King Sejong Station is essential not only for assessing its suitability for IGS registration, but also for evaluating regional tectonic stability and potential seismic hazards. While previous studies have identified measurable horizontal displacements across parts of the Antarctic Peninsula, long-term continuous GNSS data in this region remain scarce. By utilizing GNSS observations from 2009 and a follow-up survey in 2023, this study quantifies crustal motion over a 13-year period, providing valuable baseline information for future geodynamic investigations in maritime Antarctica.

This study has two primary objectives: (1) to assess the feasibility of registering the KSEJ GNSS station with the IGS by performing a detailed quality control (Q/C) analysis and comparing its operational characteristics against IGS site requirements, and (2) to estimate the velocity field and horizontal strain parameters of geodetic control points in the vicinity of King Sejong Station using long-term GNSS data. The outcomes of this work aim to contribute to the international geodetic community by providing a validated pathway for IGS registration of polar stations and by offering high-resolution geodetic insights into crustal deformation dynamics in the South Shetland Islands region.

Methods

Study area and GNSS station overview

King Sejong Station (KSEJ), located on King George Island (62.22°S, 58.78°W) in the South Shetland Islands, serves as the primary Antarctic research base operated by the Republic of Korea. The region is characterized by a cold maritime climate, permafrost terrain, and frequent cloud cover, with numerous geophysical and environmental factors influencing geodetic observations. Situated on partially exposed metamorphic bedrock near the coast, KSEJ provides a strategic location for continuous GNSS monitoring in the maritime Antarctic zone.

The permanent GNSS station at KSEJ was initially installed in 2009 as part of a national geodetic infrastructure expansion for polar scientific research. The station was equipped with a geodetic-grade GNSS receiver and choke ring antenna, mounted on a concrete pillar embedded in the exposed bedrock. However, due to harsh environmental conditions and progressive structural degradation, periodic maintenance and reinstallation have been required. In particular, observations conducted in 2023 revealed widening rock fissures at the base of the monument, necessitating a reassessment of the station’s stability and suitability for long-term monitoring and IGS registration.

The KSEJ GNSS station is surrounded by several geodetic control points forming a local triangulated network. A new benchmark was also established due to the loss of the original TBM, ensuring continuity in the vertical reference system.

The location of the KSEJ station presents both advantages and limitations for high-precision GNSS observations. Its relatively low latitude for an Antarctic site and accessibility during the austral summer facilitate year-round maintenance and data acquisition. However, nearby physical structures partially obstruct satellite signals in specific azimuthal directions, introducing variability in observation geometry.

Horizontal distances and vertical elevations of surrounding features were recorded, and the adjusted height of each obstruction was calculated by subtracting the effective antenna phase center height from the measured elevation. The antenna reference point (ARP) height was directly measured using a steel ruler, and the calibrated phase center offset provided in the IGS antenna model was applied to derive the effective phase center height (2.05 m; see Fig. 3).

Survey campaigns and observation configuration

To evaluate long-term crustal displacement and assess the suitability of the KSEJ GNSS station for potential IGS registration, two high-precision GNSS survey campaigns were conducted. The first campaign took place in December 2009, followed by a second in February 2023. Both campaigns involved static GNSS observations of a local geodetic network surrounding King Sejong Station. To ensure the comparability of results between the two epochs, consistent configurations and observation procedures were maintained throughout.

The local GNSS network consisted of six geodetic control points: the permanent KSEJ station, three triangulation points (KSJ1, KSJ2, KSJ3), a unified control point (UCP1), and a tide gauge benchmark (TBM). All points were located within 500 m of each other, enabling short-baseline processing with high positional accuracy.

During the 2023 campaign, the original TBM was found to be missing. As a result, a new tide gauge reference point was established and simultaneously observed with the remaining control stations to determine its coordinates with sufficient precision.

The 2023 survey campaign was conducted over a 48-h period from February 13 to 14, using dual-frequency geodetic-grade GNSS receivers (Leica GS16) and LEIGS16 antennas with antenna heights precisely measured and recorded (see Table 2).

The selected stations—PALM (Palmer Station), MAW1 (Mawson Station), SYOG (Syowa Station), CAS1, COTE, SCTB, and ARHT—provided continuous 24-h RINEX observation files retrieved from IGS data centers. The inclusion of these global stations stabilized the regional network solution in the ITRF2014 reference frame and introduced essential constraints for baseline processing and error mitigation.

To ensure methodological consistency across epochs, both the 2009 and 2023 datasets were processed using the GAMIT/GLOBK v10.7 software suite. The coordinated design of the observation campaigns—characterized by extended occupation periods, the use of geodetic-grade instrumentation, and rigorous referencing to global IGS sites—provides a robust basis for reliable velocity estimation and rigorous quality control in Antarctic geodetic studies.

Study area and GNSS station overview

GNSS observations in polar regions are particularly susceptible to ionospheric disturbances due to the prevalence of polar patches, auroral activity, and increased variability in total electron content (TEC). These effects can introduce significant phase delays and degrade positioning accuracy, especially for single-frequency receivers and long baseline solutions. To mitigate such errors, this study applied advanced ionospheric correction models and accounted for site-specific environmental conditions during data processing11,12.

For the 2023 dataset, the Center for Orbit Determination in Europe (CODE) CKMG23i global ionospheric model was adopted. This model, derived from global GNSS observations including high-latitude stations, provides ionospheric delay estimates at 2-h intervals with a spatial resolution of 5° × 2.5°. The CKMG series is known for outperforming conventional models—such as Klobuchar and NeQuick2—under high-latitude conditions, with reported RMS improvements of up to 25% in previous studies. In contrast, the Klobuchar model has shown limited correction capability in the Antarctic region, particularly during ionospheric storms and periods of strong geomagnetic activity13–15.

Additional environmental constraints at King Sejong Station were also considered. The GNSS antenna at KSEJ is surrounded by multiple station buildings and infrastructure, causing partial signal obstructions and elevated multipath effects. Field measurements of azimuthal visibility indicated that the station’s effective elevation mask varies from 5° to 9°, which may exceed the IGS minimum standard of 5° in certain directions. This non-uniform visibility affects satellite geometry and contributes to signal degradation, especially at low elevation angles.

To quantify these effects, multipath error levels were monitored using MP1 and MP2 metrics extracted during the quality control analysis ("Tide Gauge–GNSS benchmark connection via geodetic leveling"). The results showed average MP1 and MP2 values of 0.46 m and 0.38 m, respectively, slightly exceeding the IGS-recommended thresholds of 0.30 m. These elevated values were attributed to signal reflections from nearby metal surfaces, seasonal snow accumulation, and localized terrain variability.

Multipath remains a persistent limitation for high-precision GNSS. Hardware-based strategies such as choke ring and ground plane antennas effectively suppress ground-reflected signals16,17. Dual-polarization antennas and array beamforming further enhance mitigation by separating direct and reflected signals18,19. In addition, shielding or absorbing structures installed around reflective surfaces can attenuate unwanted reflections and improve positioning quality16,20. Complementary approaches, including spatiotemporal modeling and machine learning, provide further reductions in multipath errors21. An integrated strategy combining antenna design, environmental treatment, and signal processing is therefore most effective.

In sum, a combination of high-resolution ionospheric modeling and environmental diagnostics was essential to ensuring the precision and reliability of GNSS positioning at King Sejong Station. These corrections play a critical role in both IGS registration compliance and the derivation of accurate deformation parameters.

GNSS data processing strategy

GNSS data collected during the 2023 campaign were processed using the GAMIT/GLOBK 10.7 software suite developed at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT)7–9. This software was selected for its ability to handle regional geodetic networks with high-precision estimation of station coordinates, velocities, and covariance information. The processing strategy followed established IGS data analysis protocols, with specific adaptations for polar environmental conditions.GNSS observations acquired during the 2023 campaign were processed using the GAMIT/GLOBK 10.7 software package7–9, 2018a, 2018b), which enables precise estimation of station coordinates, velocities, and covariance parameters for regional geodetic networks. The workflow followed IGS analysis standards, with adjustments to accommodate the logistical and environmental context of the study site.

In the GAMIT module, daily coordinates were derived from double-differenced carrier-phase observations incorporating IGS final orbit and clock products. An elevation cut-off angle of 10° was applied to reduce low-elevation multipath. Atmospheric delay modeling was performed according to standard IGS procedures; however, no specialized tropospheric or ionospheric analysis was undertaken, as these effects fall outside the scope of the present investigation.

Daily solutions were combined in GLOBK using a loosely constrained network adjustment and a Kalman filter to estimate station velocities and their formal uncertainties. The network was aligned to the ITRF2014 reference frame using globally distributed IGS reference stations (PALM, MAW1, SYOG, CAS1, ARHT, COTE, SCTB). Outlier sessions were removed based on residual statistics and chi-square testing, with only solutions exhibiting normalized RMS values below 1.0 retained.

Tide gauge–GNSS benchmark connection via geodetic leveling

To ensure vertical reference consistency between the tide gauge benchmark (TGBM) and nearby GNSS control points, geodetic leveling (precise spirit leveling) was performed during the 2023 field campaign using a Trimble Dini10 digital level and Invar staff. This configuration enabled millimeter-level precision in determining height differences.

The leveling survey established connections among the primary geodetic control points at King Sejong Station (KSEJ, UCP, KSJ1, KSJ2) and a newly installed temporary benchmark (TBM) located near the tide gauge (Fig. 4).The original TBM, which had been established in 2009, was lost due to site modifications and environmental conditions. Consequently, a new TBM was installed on a stable rock platform in 2023, and its elevation was determined through the leveling survey. Control and IGS registration criteria evaluation

To assess the suitability of the KSEJ GNSS station for potential registration within the International GNSS Service (IGS) network, a comprehensive quality control (Q/C) protocol was established. This protocol was designed to verify the station’s adherence to key performance metrics as outlined in the IGS Site Guidelines. Specifically, the evaluation targeted parameters such as observation data availability, cycle slip rate, multipath signal distortion, and RINEX file integrity. In addition to signal-level indicators, station-level compliance factors—including data transmission practices, metadata standardization, antenna environment, and time synchronization methods—were also reviewed.

GNSS observations from October 17 to November 26, 2022, were processed using the GAMIT/GLOBK v10.7 software suite. Daily 30-s RINEX files were analyzed over this 41-day interval to evaluate performance metrics, including multipath errors (MP1 and MP2), cycle slips, and residual statistics. Quality diagnostics such as chi-square testing and RMS filtering were applied to identify low-quality epochs and verify compliance with IGS benchmarks.

To complement this short-term assessment, Appendix A (Table A1 and Figure A1) presents long-term QC records covering six years (2010–2015). These include annual statistics on utilization rate, multipath errors, and cycle slip rates, offering a broader temporal perspective on station performance. Due to technical issues, observation data are not available after 2015, limiting the temporal extent of this long-term evaluation.

In parallel, a formal system audit was conducted in 2023 to examine compliance with broader IGS registration requirements. The audit included 15 evaluation categories, ranging from instrument metadata completeness and antenna site characteristics to dual-center data transfer capability and timekeeping infrastructure. Each category was rated as “Fully Compliant,” “Acceptable,” or “Needs Improvement,” based on IGS documentation and best practices. Special attention was given to the station’s ability to generate and transmit standardized RINEX datasets, the implementation of real-time data delivery pathways, and the presence or absence of external atomic time references (e.g., cesium or hydrogen maser clocks).

The results of the quality control and registration audit are summarized and discussed in detail in "Results".

Processing was performed using the GAMIT/GLOBK software suite, with 30-s sampling RINEX files from the KSEJ CORS receiver.

Over the 41-day observation period, the station achieved an average data availability of 99.37%, surpassing the IGS-required minimum of 99.0%. The cycle slip rate remained low, averaging 0.10%, which also satisfies the IGS guideline of fewer than one slip per 1,000 observations. These results indicate high temporal continuity and stable signal reception.

Multipath effects, however, were more prominent. The average MP1 and MP2 metrics were found to be 0.46 m and 0.38 m, respectively—both exceeding the IGS-recommended threshold of 0.30 m. These elevated values are attributed to reflective interference from surrounding infrastructure, snow accumulation, and partial obstructions in the antenna’s azimuthal field of view. Azimuthal visibility analysis confirmed elevation masking in the 5°–9° range in certain directions, marginally exceeding the IGS minimum cut-off of 5°.

Despite these environmental limitations, the overall signal quality metrics suggest that the KSEJ station demonstrates acceptable performance in terms of data integrity and observation reliability. With mitigation strategies—such as antenna repositioning, shielding, and the use of choke ring antennas—the station’s compliance with IGS standards can be further improved, reinforcing its potential as a high-latitude geodetic reference node.

Inter-epoch displacement and velocity computation

To quantify long-term crustal displacement at King Sejong Station (KSEJ), coordinate comparisons were conducted between GNSS datasets from 2009 and 2023. For consistency, the 2009 observations (DOY 349–358) were reprocessed using the GAMIT/GLOBK 10.7 software suite, the ITRF2014 reference frame, and IGS final orbit and clock products, under the same conditions as the 2023 campaign. The results indicate a net horizontal displacement of approximately 0.29 m northward and − 0.01 m eastward over a 13.17-year period. This corresponds to an average annual horizontal velocity of 22.0 mm/yr toward the north-northeast (NNE) and − 0.76 mm/yr toward the east, yielding a total horizontal velocity magnitude of 22.0 mm/yr. These values are consistent with regional crustal motion patterns previously reported for the northern Antarctic Peninsula, primarily associated with post-glacial rebound and plate-boundary-related tectonic processes22. Gauge operations and geodetic leveling assessment at King Sejong Station

Tide gauge systems are essential for recording sea level variations at fixed coastal benchmarks, enabling the determination of tidal ranges and the temporal characteristics of high and low tides. At King Sejong Station, long-term tide gauge records, in conjunction with meteorological data, provide critical insights into the complex interactions among oceanic, glacial, and atmospheric systems in the Antarctic region. These datasets contribute to the characterization of relative sea level changes and serve as a fundamental reference for vertical datum realization in geodetic applications.

However, the extreme environmental conditions of Antarctica—including prolonged sea ice formation, seasonal ice drift, and the potential for structural damage due to ice loading—necessitate the development of robust operational strategies to ensure continuous and reliable tide gauge performance. In this context, the integration of precise leveling surveys with tide gauge benchmarks plays a key role in maintaining vertical reference consistency and enhancing the accuracy of coastal elevation monitoring.

Assessment of KSEJ readiness for IGS network integration

The KSEJ GNSS station was evaluated for compliance with the core categories of the IGS registration requirements—site installation, antenna/receiver specifications, RINEX data standards, meteorological observations, tide gauge linkage, and receiver time synchronization—through an on-site evaluation conducted in 2023. Analysis of data collected between 17 October and 26 November 2022 showed a data availability of 99.37% and a cycle slip rate of 0.10%, both exceeding the IGS minimum standards. However, multipath noise (MP1 = 0.46 m, MP2 = 0.38 m) exceeded the recommended threshold, attributable to reflections from nearby metallic structures, partial sky obstructions, and snow accumulation. For IGS registration, the assignment of a DOMES number is required; the station is equipped with a TRIMBLE NETR8 receiver (firmware version 3.80) and a TRM59800.00 DOME antenna, consistent with IGS site log specifications.

Discussion

GNSS signal quality and crustal displacement at KSEJ

This study provides a detailed assessment of the geodetic and infrastructural readiness of the KSEJ GNSS station for integration into the International GNSS Service (IGS) network, with a focus on signal quality, long-term positional stability, and compliance with international standards. The findings offer new insights into both the local crustal dynamics at King Sejong Station and the operational challenges associated with maintaining high-precision GNSS infrastructure in extreme polar environments.

The multi-epoch GNSS analysis revealed a consistent pattern of horizontal displacement across geodetic control points, with an average annual movement of 30.06mm. This displacement is in line with previously observed crustal responses in the northern Antarctic Peninsula, attributed primarily to post-glacial rebound and plate-boundary-related tectonic activity. The relatively low variability in displacement among control points suggests that the KSEJ local geodetic network is internally consistent and well-suited for long-term deformation monitoring. However, the vertical component showed greater variability, which may reflect differential frost heave, monument instability, or unmodeled environmental influences. These findings underscore the need for sustained high-precision GNSS observations and rigorous monumentation standards, particularly in permafrost-prone regions.

Despite harsh environmental conditions, the KSEJ station demonstrated strong performance in key GNSS signal quality metrics. The data availability exceeded 99.37%, and the cycle slip rate remained below 0.10%, both well within IGS registration thresholds. Nevertheless, elevated multipath levels (MP1 and MP2) exceeding IGS-recommended limits were observed, primarily due to nearby infrastructure and variable azimuthal visibility. While these factors do not disqualify the site, they highlight the importance of local environmental diagnostics and mitigation—such as antenna relocation or shielding—for improving observational fidelity.

Tide gauge operations and vertical datum Consistency

In the context of Antarctic geodetic infrastructure, tide gauge operations face unique challenges that can significantly affect the reliability and consistency of vertical reference systems. At King Sejong Station (KSEJ), while tide gauge systems provide invaluable long-term sea-level and oceanographic data, their operational stability is compromised by extreme environmental conditions such as seasonal sea ice formation, ice drift, and potential damage to sensing infrastructure from ice loading and dynamic coastal processes. These factors pose a significant risk to the continuity of tide gauge observations, particularly during the polar winter months when access and maintenance are limited.

The comparative analysis in Table 6 reveals a systematic vertical offset between historical tide-gauge-based benchmarks and the newly derived GNSS-based orthometric elevations, ranging from –0.071 m to –0.135 m. This offset is primarily attributed to differences in the Mean Sea Level (MSL) reference surfaces adopted in each epoch. The original benchmarks established in 2009 were tied to an MSL defined from approximately one month of tidal observations, whereas the GNSS-derived heights in this study reference a more robust MSL surface based on a continuous one-year record spanning 2020–2021. The ∼90 mm discrepancy underscores the sensitivity of vertical datums to the temporal representativeness of tide-gauge observations and highlights the necessity of long-term monitoring to ensure consistent and reliable vertical reference frames in polar regions.

This systematic offset further emphasizes the necessity for continuous, year-round tide gauge operations in polar environments. To achieve this, technological upgrades—such as reinforced stilling wells, sensor housing insulation, and real-time environmental diagnostics—should be considered to protect instrumentation from freeze-induced damage and ensure uninterrupted data collection. Additionally, coupling tide gauge data with high-precision GNSS vertical control through repeated leveling campaigns will enhance the accuracy of local geoid models and support the long-term harmonization of vertical datums.

IGS compliance, strategic role, and future directions

Ensuring compliance with International GNSS Service (IGS) standards is a prerequisite for the global integration of high-latitude GNSS stations such as KSEJ. Beyond meeting baseline technical thresholds, the station also holds strategic value for scientific applications, including sea-level monitoring, satellite altimetry validation, and refinement of the global geodetic reference frame in Antarctica. Strengthening tide gauge infrastructure, enhancing GNSS data quality, and refining regional geoid models are therefore critical steps toward establishing KSEJ as a reliable node within the IGS network.

The compliance assessment revealed several infrastructural and operational gaps that currently limit the station’s readiness. These include the absence of a dual-path data transmission system, limited automation of metadata management, and the lack of an external atomic time reference. While these shortcomings are not uncommon among high-latitude stations, they must be addressed if KSEJ is to transition from a regional reference node to a globally integrated IGS site. Encouragingly, most of these gaps are logistical rather than systemic and can be resolved through targeted upgrades.

In addition to meeting technical requirements, KSEJ holds unique potential within the broader IGS framework. Its high-latitude location provides distinct advantages for global geodetic products, particularly in the areas of space weather monitoring, polar motion studies, and sea-level research. By reinforcing its infrastructure and operational protocols, KSEJ could emerge as a key contributor to the global geodetic observing system and significantly expand South Korea’s scientific presence in Antarctic geodesy.

Building on these findings, several research directions are proposed. First, the integration of GNSS, tide gauge, and meteorological observations over extended time scales would enable joint inversion of sea-level trends and geoid refinement in polar regions. Second, implementation of external atomic timing standards and dual-path data streaming to global IGS data centers will be essential for achieving full compliance. Third, mitigation of multipath and frost heave effects—through improved monumentation, antenna shielding, or relocation—will further enhance data reliability. Finally, incorporating KSEJ time series into Antarctic crustal deformation models and satellite altimetry correction frameworks would position the station as a scientific hub for high-latitude geodynamics and Earth system monitoring.

According to recent studies, continuous GNSS stations in Antarctica are indispensable for quantifying post-glacial rebound (GIA), modeling crustal deformation, and refining global reference frames1,30,31. Multi-year GNSS time series enable direct measurement of bedrock uplift and subsidence, capturing both long-term isostatic trends and short-term elastic responses to ice mass variability. The horizontal and vertical velocities derived from such networks support high-precision geodynamic modeling, particularly in tectonically active or ice-load-sensitive regions such as the Antarctic Peninsula.

In the context of sea level science, co-located GNSS and tide gauge stations allow for accurate separation of vertical land motion from true sea level signals. This integration is especially critical in glaciated regions where both uplift and subsidence affect long-term sea level trends. Recent advancements, including GNSS reflectometry (GNSS-R) techniques, further enhance the spatial resolution and temporal coverage of sea surface monitoring32,33.

Importantly, standardized GNSS data processing and long-term time series reanalysis—such as the GIANT-REGAIN initiative—are expanding the utility of Antarctic networks for continental-scale geophysical modeling1. These data not only support the densification of the International Terrestrial Reference Frame (ITRF) but also provide essential constraints for satellite gravimetry and altimetry corrections.

Given these scientific imperatives, high-latitude stations such as KSEJ hold unique strategic value. Enhancing KSEJ’s observational capability—through improved monument stability, dual-stream data transmission, and tide gauge integration—will not only elevate South Korea’s geodetic contribution in Antarctica but also reinforce global geodynamic infrastructure essential for monitoring Earth system change.

Conclusions

This study assessed the geodetic performance of the continuous GNSS station KSEJ and associated benchmarks at King Sejong Station, Antarctica. Reprocessed dual-epoch data (2009–2023) indicated a horizontal velocity of ~ 22 mm/yr toward the north-northeast, consistent with regional crustal motion driven by post-glacial rebound and tectonic activity. Signal quality metrics confirmed compliance with IGS standards, with data availability of 99.3% and a cycle slip rate of 0.1%, although elevated multipath levels remain a challenge. Technical upgrades, including monument reinforcement, redundant data transmission, and external timing standards, are needed to meet full registration requirements. Nevertheless, KSEJ already demonstrates the stability and data quality required for integration into the IGS network. Its inclusion would enhance high-latitude reference frame densification, improve sea level monitoring, and provide critical constraints on Antarctic geodynamics within the global geodetic observing system.
