IgM nephropathy is a controversial entity: a distinct nephropathy or part of the podocytopathy spectrum? A systematic review of the literature
Authors: Seyda Gul Ozcan, Yagmur Ersoy, Ogulcan Karalı, Ahmet Murt, Mevlut Tamer Dincer, Sinan Trabulus, Nurhan Seyahi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractImmunoglobulin M nephropathy (IgMN) has traditionally been defined by dominant mesangial IgM staining, but its significance remains debated. IgM deposition may be non-specific, diagnostic thresholds are inconsistently applied, and clinical patterns largely overlap with minimal change disease (MCD) and focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS), questioning whether IgMN constitutes a distinct entity or part of the podocytopathy spectrum. We conducted a PubMed literature review using the terms “IgM nephropathy,” “IgMN,” and “Immunoglobulin M nephropathy,”. A total of 49 individual cases have been identified in scientific literature, but only 22 cases were confirmed adopting a widely accepted definition of IgM Nephropathy: the presence of a dominant, diffuse, global mesangial IgM deposits (≥ 2 + compared with other immunofluorescence stainings) in nonsclerotic areas, supported by the presence of electron-dense deposits in the mesangial and paramesangial areas at electron microscopy. These cases underscore the heterogeneous presentation of IgMN, from steroid-resistant nephrotic syndrome to stable non-nephrotic proteinuria. Its relationship to MCD and the occasional progression to FSGS highlight the dynamic nature of IgM nephropathy as a clinico-pathological entity. A major interpretive gap is inconsistent EM incorporation; fewer than half of reported cases met strict ultrastructural criteria. Current evidence favors positioning IgMN along the podocytopathy spectrum rather than as an independent entity. Standardized diagnostic criteria integrating routine ultrastructural validation, individualized management based on clinical phenotype, and molecular biomarker identification are warranted. Prospective multicenter studies are needed to clarify the pathogenic significance of mesangial IgM and guide evidence-based therapy.Keywords: IgM nephropathy, Literature review, Rare glomerular disease, Mesangial IgM deposition

Introduction

Immunoglobulin M nephropathy (IgMN) is a rare and long-debated glomerular disease, traditionally defined by dominant or co-dominant mesangial IgM deposition on immunofluorescence microscopy [1]. First described in the 1970s, IgMN has been discussed in the nephrology literature for more than five decades; however, its clinical significance, diagnostic boundaries, and even its status as a distinct disease entity remain uncertain [2].

Clinically, IgMN exhibits a heterogeneous presentation. Patients may present with nephrotic syndrome, hypertension, microscopic hematuria, or asymptomatic proteinuria, and the disease course can be variable, ranging from steroid-responsive cases to those progressing to chronic kidney disease (CKD) [3]. Histologically, light microscopy (LM) findings are nonspecific and heterogeneous, ranging from minimal changes to mesangial proliferation, with some cases evolving toward focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS) on repeat biopsy [3].

Despite decades of study, several fundamental controversies surrounding IgMN remain unresolved. First, its nosological status is debated. Some experts consider it within the spectrum of podocytopathies encompassing minimal change disease (MCD) and FSGS [1, 3]. Second, diagnostic criteria have been inconsistently applied across studies. Most contemporary series define IgMN by diffuse, global mesangial IgM deposition on immunofluorescence, typically at least 1 + to 2 + in intensity as the sole or dominant immunoglobulin, with many authors recommending electron microscopy (EM) to demonstrate mesangial or paramesangial electron-dense deposits, although thresholds and the mandatory use of EM are not uniform across studies [4–6]. Third, the pathogenic significance of mesangial IgM deposition itself is unclear. Recent evidence suggests that circulating anti-nephrin antibodies, including both IgG and IgM isotypes, may underlie primary podocytopathies such as MCD and FSGS, raising the possibility that similar mechanisms contribute to some forms of IgMN [7]. However, whether mesangial IgM deposition reflects a primary immunological mechanism or represents nonspecific trapping in injured glomeruli remains uncertain. These definitional inconsistencies have important consequences for both diagnosis and management.

In this narrative review, we summarize the available literature on IgMN, with particular emphasis on how IgMN has been defined over time, how strictly contemporary criteria have been applied, and how these issues shape our understanding of its clinical presentation, prognosis, and therapeutic response. We aim to clarify the current state of knowledge and highlight priorities for future research on this still poorly defined condition.

Methods

We performed a literature review via the PubMed interface (pubmed.gov) using the keywords “Immunoglobulin M nephropathy,” “IgMN,” and “IgM nephropathy,” combined with the logical operator “OR.” We ran the query in December 2025. A total of 68 articles were initially identified. We excluded reviews, editorials, studies published in non-English languages (n = 3), articles unrelated to the topic (n = 12), and those for which full texts were inaccessible (n = 16). After applying the exclusion criteria and manually reviewing the remaining content, we included 37 relevant publications comprising case reports, case series, and cohort studies encompassing a total of 49 individual cases.

Each case was evaluated against current diagnostic criteria for IgM nephropathy. Cases not fulfilling these criteria, due to segmental IgM distribution, co-dominant or dominant IgA/IgG deposition, or absence of mesangial electron-dense deposits on EM—were marked with a double asterisk (**) in Table 1 and excluded from demographic and outcome analyses. Cases with immunofluorescence findings consistent with IgM nephropathy but lacking EM confirmation were marked with a single asterisk (*) and classified as probable IgMN; these were also excluded from the analysis. After exclusion of cases not meeting confirmed diagnostic criteria, 22 cases remained for analysis. Summary of 22 cases is presented in Table 2. Percentages were calculated based on cases with available data for each variable.

Results

Demographics and geographic distribution

Among the 22 confirmed cases, we observed a slight female predominance (59.1% female, 40.9% male), and a broad age range (3–67 years, mean 36.8 years), confirming that IgMN affects both children and adults. Pediatric cases accounted for 31.8% of the cohort. The wide geographical representation reinforces its global relevance and suggests findings are not limited by regional factors. While original descriptions emerged from Western academic centers in the 1970s and 1980s, contemporary publications increasingly originate from South Asia, the Middle East, and other developing regions.

Histopathological findings

By definition, all 22 confirmed cases demonstrated diffuse mesangial IgM deposition on immunofluorescence with electron-dense deposits confirmed on EM. IgM staining intensity ranged from 2 + to 3 + in the majority of cases. Complement co-deposition was reported in 50.0% of cases. Of the four confirmed cases with clearly described light microscopic findings, two demonstrated mesangial proliferation, while the remaining two showed either FSGS-like changes or crescentic glomerulonephritis.

Clinical presentation

Nephrotic syndrome was the most common presentation, occurring in 54.5% of confirmed cases. Proteinuria without nephrotic-range was present in an additional 31.8%. Hematuria was reported in 27.3%. Hypertension at presentation was documented in two patients. Associated systemic conditions or potential triggers were identified in 36.4%, including Kimura’s disease (n = 1), neurofibromatosis type 1 (n = 1), coexisting Fabry disease (n = 1), dermatomyositis following COVID-19 vaccination (n = 1), immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy (n = 1), D-penicillamine exposure (n = 1), chronic NSAID use (n = 1), and seasonal allergic rhinitis (n = 1).

Treatment approaches

Treatment data were available for 20 of 22 confirmed cases. Corticosteroids were the most commonly used initial therapy (75.0%). Second-line immunosuppressive agents included calcineurin inhibitors (cyclosporine or tacrolimus; 30.0%), rituximab (20.0%), cyclophosphamide (20.0%), and mycophenolate mofetil (15.0%). Supportive care alone, including RAAS blockade, was employed in 15.0% of patients, primarily those with non-nephrotic proteinuria or drug-induced cases where removal of the offending agent was sufficient.

Outcomes

Outcome data were available for 19 of 22 confirmed cases. The majority (78.9%) maintained stable or improved renal function at last follow-up. Complete or partial remission of proteinuria was achieved in 68.4%. Steroid-sensitive disease was associated with favorable short-term outcomes. However, a challenging disease course was not uncommon: steroid dependence or frequent relapse was observed in 5 of 19 patients (26.3%), all of whom ultimately achieved at least partial remission with prolonged corticosteroid courses or second-line agents (cyclophosphamide, calcineurin inhibitors, or rituximab). In contrast, true steroid resistance or multidrug resistance was observed in 2 patients (10.5%), one of whom progressed to ESRD and one who showed no meaningful response despite sequential immunosuppression and LDL apheresis. This distinction is clinically relevant, as steroid-dependent/relapsing patients generally retain a favorable long-term prognosis with appropriate intensification of therapy, whereas truly resistant cases carry a substantially worse outlook. These outcomes should be interpreted with caution given the retrospective nature of case reports, publication bias, and variable follow-up periods.

Discussion

The nosological controversy: distinct entity or part of the podocytopathy spectrum?

The central question surrounding IgMN is whether it constitutes an independent disease entity or represents a variant of MCD or a transitional stage toward FSGS. Several observations from our review and the broader literature support IgMN as a distinct condition. The main immunopathologic feature, dominant IgM deposits in the mesangium, resembles the distinctive staining patterns that define other well-recognized kidney diseases [2, 4, 45–47]. Moreover, the documentation of disease recurrence in renal allografts has been interpreted as evidence for a circulating pathogenic factor [20, 23]. However, post-transplant recurrence does not necessarily support nosological independence; indeed, such recurrence is entirely compatible with podocytopathies such as primary FSGS, which is known to recur rapidly after transplantation due to circulating permeability factors [48].

Arguments against independent disease status emphasize the ubiquity of IgM in injured glomeruli and question whether IgM deposition represents cause or consequence of podocyte injury. Connor et al. reported that, among 10 patients who underwent repeat biopsy, focal segmental glomerulosclerosis was identified in 8 (80%), while mesangial IgM deposition persisted in all cases and only one biopsy still fulfilled the current ultrastructural definition of IgM nephropathy [5]. Similarly, Zeis et al. followed 64 children with IgM nephropathy for up to 14 years and found that 13 (20%) developed FSGS on repeat biopsy, with IgM-positive patients showing significantly higher rates of progression compared to IgM-negative controls [49]. These observations support the podocytopathy spectrum concept, which positions IgMN alongside MCD and FSGS as manifestations of podocyte injury differing primarily in severity and chronicity.

Diagnostic criteria and their limitations

A major limitation in interpreting published IgMN cases is the inconsistent application of diagnostic criteria. The commonly accepted definition requires dominant, diffuse, global mesangial IgM deposits (≥ 2 + intensity compared with other immunofluorescence stainings) in nonsclerotic areas, supported by the presence of electron-dense deposits in mesangial or paramesangial regions on EM [4, 50, 51]. However, many reported cases have used looser thresholds [52]. Connor et al. found that 64.9% of their cohort showed only 1 + IgM staining, and notably observed no prognostic significance to staining intensity [5]. In our review, application of strict diagnostic criteria reduced the cohort from 49 identified cases to 22 confirmed cases, underscoring that a substantial proportion of cases reported as “IgMN” in the literature may not represent the same underlying disease process. Histopathological spectrum: a critical appraisal

Light microscopy findings in IgMN are heterogeneous. Chae et al. demonstrated that the accompanying LM pattern—rather than IgM deposition itself—may be the primary determinant of outcomes, with FSGS-like histology portending significantly worse prognosis [53].

Several case reports have suggested unusual histologic variants; however, these require critical appraisal. Kazi and Mubarak reported crescentic glomerulonephritis with mesangial IgM, representing an aggressive form with rapid progression [30]. However, this case lacks EM confirmation, and the immunofluorescence showed bright IgM staining along peripheral capillary loops in addition to the mesangium. Capillary wall staining combined with crescentic changes could indicate a different immune-complex process, rather than true IgMN. Definitive diagnosis in such cases requires EM confirmation of deposit location.

Similarly, Wu et al. described coexisting Fabry disease with mesangial IgM [38]. While IF showed mesangial IgM (2+), the EM documented characteristic myelin bodies but did not demonstrate convincing mesangial immune deposits. Without such confirmation, it remains unclear whether this represents genuine IgMN or non-specific IgM staining in the context of Fabry-related injury. The authors proposed a “two-hit” hypothesis wherein metabolic podocyte injury may prime immune activation, potentially favoring an IgM-associated response with complement activation in predisposed patients. The dynamic histopathology observed in serial biopsies—with some cases evolving from MCD-like to FSGS patterns—supports the view that IgMN may represent a continuum rather than a static diagnosis.

Clinical implications and prognostic considerations

Clinical presentations in our confirmed cohort varied widely, ranging from nephrotic syndrome to isolated hematuria, underscoring the importance of considering IgMN in both nephrotic and non-nephrotic contexts. The favorable short-term outcomes observed in our case series should be interpreted cautiously given publication bias and variable follow-up. Larger cohort studies provide a more sobering long-term perspective. Myllymäki et al. reported that, over 15 years of follow-up, 36% of patients developed renal insufficiency and 23% progressed to end-stage renal disease [47]. More recently, data from the Korean Renal Pathology Consortium further support the notion that IgMN is not a benign lesion. In that multicenter cohort of 63 IgMN patients followed for a mean of 79.5 months, renal events (defined as > 50% eGFR decline, eGFR < 15 mL/min/1.73 m², or ESRD) occurred in 46.0% of IgMN patients, a rate similar to FSGS (40.8%, p = 0.52) but significantly higher than MCD (18.7%, p < 0.001) or IgAN (26.4%, p = 0.001). Clinically, IgMN patients resembled FSGS patients with respect to higher blood pressure, lower eGFR at presentation, and greater prevalence of global and segmental sclerosis on biopsy. Histopathological features independently associated with adverse renal outcomes included global sclerosis, segmental sclerosis, and lower baseline eGFR, findings that align IgMN more closely with FSGS than with MCD in terms of both clinical phenotype and prognostic trajectory [54].

Although typically considered idiopathic, several cases in our review were associated with identifiable triggers or systemic conditions. Drug-induced IgMN has been reported with D-penicillamine, NSAIDs, and immune checkpoint inhibitors [13, 40, 43]. Al-Ahmad et al. described recurrent seasonal nephrotic syndrome occurring exclusively during grass pollen season, supporting a potential allergic mechanism in some cases [21]. Associations with systemic inflammatory conditions include Kimura’s disease, ulcerative colitis, familial Mediterranean fever, and dermatomyositis following COVID-19 vaccination [16, 17, 22, 24, 44]. Rarer associations include neurofibromatosis type 1 [33, 38]. Whether these represent true pathogenic links or coincidental findings remains uncertain.

Treatment approaches

The lack of standardized guidelines has resulted in considerable heterogeneity in treatment approaches. Corticosteroids remain the most commonly used initial therapy, though evidence for their specific efficacy in IgMN is limited [8, 14, 20, 55]. Rituximab has shown promise in relapsing cases, with documented disappearance of IgM deposits on follow-up biopsy reported after treatment [25]. In post-transplant recurrence, early aggressive intervention with plasma exchange, IVIG, and rituximab has been successful [23].

Notably, supportive care alone was sufficient in a subset of patients, particularly those with non-nephrotic proteinuria or drug-induced cases [43]. This suggests that aggressive immunosuppression may not be universally necessary, and treatment should be individualized based on clinical severity, histological features, and response to initial therapy.

Future directions

The potential contribution of genetic factors remains largely unexplored. Although familial clustering has been suggested in the literature, the evidence is limited and complicated by inconsistent diagnostic criteria. Notably, several reported familial cases used diagnostic definitions that do not meet current strict criteria for IgMN, making conclusions about heritability uncertain [14, 18]. Given that podocytopathies including FSGS are frequently associated with genetic mutations affecting podocyte structure and function, genetic testing may still be warranted to identify subsets of IgMN patients with underlying hereditary predisposition.

Additionally, advances in glomerular transcriptomics and proteomics could help characterize the molecular signatures distinguishing primary IgMN from secondary IgM deposition, potentially enabling more precise diagnosis and targeted therapy. Most critically, the field requires consensus on standardized diagnostic criteria, including mandatory EM confirmation and uniform IF thresholds, to enable meaningful comparisons across studies. Prospective multicenter registries applying such criteria would help establish the true natural history and optimal management of this condition.

The absence of standardized diagnostic criteria, inconsistent use of EM, and variable follow-up protocols limit the comparability of studies. Our review is constrained by the retrospective nature of published reports and their heterogeneity in diagnosis and treatment. Future priorities include establishing uniform diagnostic thresholds, conducting prospective multicenter studies, identifying biomarkers for disease monitoring, and designing controlled trials to guide therapy.

In conclusion, the central question, whether IgMN represents a distinct disease entity or a manifestation within the podocytopathy spectrum, cannot be definitively resolved based on current evidence. Our interpretation, based on the evidence reviewed, is that current data favor positioning IgMN along the podocytopathy spectrum rather than as a fully independent entity analogous to ANCA-associated vasculitis or lupus nephritis. The clinical phenotype and prognosis appear determined primarily by the severity of podocyte injury and accompanying histological changes (particularly FSGS-like features) rather than by IgM deposition per se. However, important uncertainties remain: the pathogenic significance of mesangial IgM deposits is unresolved; the role of anti-nephrin IgM antibodies requires further investigation; and the possibility that a subset of “true” IgMN exists within the broader category of cases currently labeled as such cannot be excluded. Until these questions are resolved through prospective studies with standardized diagnostic criteria, molecular characterization, and long-term follow-up, we recommend that clinicians: (1) apply strict diagnostic criteria including mandatory EM; (2) recognize IgMN’s clinical and prognostic similarities to FSGS; (3) individualize treatment based on clinical severity, histological features, and steroid responsiveness; and (4) consider repeat biopsy in cases of relapse or progression to assess for evolution to FSGS. Collaborative multicenter efforts with uniform diagnostic standards are essential to clarify whether IgMN warrants recognition as a distinct entity or should be definitively incorporated into the podocytopathy classification.
