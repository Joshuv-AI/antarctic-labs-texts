Efficient sampling approaches based on generalized Golub-Kahan methods for large-scale hierarchical Bayesian inverse problems
Elle Buser, Julianne Chung

Abstract
Uncertainty quantification for large-scale inverse problems remains a challenging task. For linear inverse problems with additive Gaussian noise and Gaussian priors, the posterior is Gaussian but sampling can be challenging, especially for problems with a very large number of unknown parameters (e.g., dynamic inverse problems) and for problems where computation of the square root and inverse of the prior covariance matrix are not feasible. Moreover, for hierarchical problems where several hyperparameters that define the prior and the noise model must be estimated from the data, the posterior distribution may no longer be Gaussian, even if the forward operator is linear. Performing large-scale uncertainty quantification for these hierarchical settings requires new computational techniques. In this work, we consider a hierarchical Bayesian framework where both the noise and prior variance are modeled as hyperparameters. Our approach uses Metropolis-Hastings independence sampling within Gibbs where the proposal distribution is based on generalized Golub-Kahan methods. We consider two proposal samplers, one that uses a low-rank approximation to the conditional covariance matrix and another that uses a preconditioned Lanczos method. Numerical examples from seismic imaging, dynamic photoacoustic tomography, and atmospheric inverse modeling demonstrate the effectiveness of the described approaches.

Introduction

Inverse problems arise in many scientific applications, where the main goal is to use collected measurements or observations to estimate some underlying unknown parameters of physical models. We focus on inverse problems in imaging, where the unknown parameters represent detailed spatial or spatiotemporal reconstructions of physical properties such as images of attenuation coefficients in X-ray tomography or spatiotemporal maps of greenhouse gas emission fluxes in atmospheric inverse modeling
Chung and Gazzola (2024)
. In dynamic inverse problems, the unknown parameters can change over time, easily resulting in millions of unknown parameters. For example, in dynamic atmospheric inverse modeling, the goal is to estimate thousands of emission fluxes at 3-hourly time intervals over multiple months or years. Obtaining such estimates is computationally challenging, and recent works in the field of computational inverse problems have addressed various theoretical and computational advancements (e.g., developing improved reconstruction algorithms that enable faster reconstructions at higher resolutions with higher accuracy), e.g.,
Cho et al. (2022)
;
Pasha et al. (2023)
;
Chung et al. (2018)
.
Many of these approaches rely on sophisticated tools from optimization and numerical linear algebra for obtaining reconstructions. However, to provide quantification of uncertainty about the solutions of inverse problems, we follow a Bayesian interpretation of inverse problems.

In a Bayesian formulation, the parameters of interest and the observed data are modeled as random variables, and any prior knowledge or lack thereof (e.g., uncertainty in the parameters) is encoded in the prior distribution and any noise or measurement error is encoded in the likelihood function (along with the forward process). Contrary to deterministic approaches, where a single solution is provided, the Bayesian approach provides a distribution of plausible solutions in the form of samples from
the
posterior probability distribution. Given the observation data, Bayes’ law allows uncertainty quantification (UQ) via incorporation of prior knowledge about the unknown parameters (in the form of the prior) and the likelihood. Good references on Bayesian or statistical approaches to inverse problems and computational UQ include
Calvetti and Somersalo (2023)
;
Bardsley (2018)
;
Calvetti and Somersalo (2007)
.

However, there are various challenges that have hindered the extension of many of these approaches to the large-scale problems of interest. For hierarchical Bayesian approaches where the prior and/or likelihood distributions depend on additional (hyper-)parameters, hyperpriors must be incorporated. This usually results in complicated posterior distributions that do not have a closed form, thereby requiring expensive approximation techniques
Bui-Thanh et al. (2013)
;
Bui-Thanh and Ghattas (2014)
;
Flath et al. (2011)
. Moreover, even when it is possible to derive a closed-form for the posterior distribution, drawing samples from the posterior distribution can
be
computationally expensive.

Overview of contributions

For sampling the posterior distribution in hierarchical Bayesian
inverse
problems, we focus on Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods, particularly Gibbs sampling and its variants. For large-scale inverse problems, the main computational bottleneck of these MCMC routines is the repeated sampling from high-dimensional Gaussian random variables, which requires a symmetric factorization of a large, and often dense, covariance matrix that is changing at each iteration. We seek to reduce the computational burden of repeated sampling by using Metropolis-Hastings within Gibbs, with proposal samplers based on generalized Golub-Kahan methods. Similar to the approach described in
Brown et al. (2018)
, one approach we consider is to use a proposal distribution based on a low-rank approximation of the prior-preconditioned Hessian. We exploit generalized Golub-Kahan approximations for independence sampling, where the added benefits are that more general prior covariance matrices can be included (since we only require matrix-vector
(mat-vec)
multiplications with the prior covariance matrix) and we can reuse Krylov matrices across MCMC iterations (since they are independent of the hyperparameters).
For these independence samplers, we derive explicit formulas for the acceptance rates and demonstrate the computational benefits of their use for hierarchical Bayesian inverse problems in a wide range of applications
and
for problems with thousands of unknown parameters.

Related work

For hierarchical Bayesian inverse problems, efficient optimization techniques have been considered for
maximum a posteriori (MAP)
estimation, where many previous works use an iterative alternating scheme or Krylov-based iterative methods
Calvetti et al. (2015)
;
Calvetti et al. (2019)
;
Lindbloom et al. (2025a)
;
Lindbloom et al. (2025b)
. A unified theoretical framework and reconstruction error bounds for MAP estimates are provided in a recent survey paper
Sanz-Alonso and Waniorek (2025)
.

For UQ, sampling techniques have been considered for hierarchical Bayesian inverse problems, but they can be challenging, especially for problems with many unknown parameters.

The most common approach is to use centered algorithms, such as block Gibbs and
Metropolis-Hastings
-within-Gibbs algorithms, but a potential concern is poor mixing in the presence of strong correlations
Agapiou et al. (2014)
;
Ascolani et al. (2024)
. Thus, there have been various approaches that exploit prior normalization for accelerating MCMC for problems with heavy-tailed priors
Glaubitz and Marzouk (2025)
;
Calvetti and Somersalo (2024)
;
Calvetti and Somersalo (2025)
;
Agrawal et al. (2022)
.
Although these works highlight the recent and increased interest in sampling for hierarchical Bayesian inverse problems, many of these approaches are demonstrated for small problems with moderately sized unknowns (e.g., on the order of hundreds) or consider special cases, e.g., where the size of the data space is significantly smaller than the number of unknown parameters
Calvetti and Somersalo (2025)
.

Other existing works
focus on sparsity-promoting priors that assume conditionally Gaussian priors with variances that are mutually independent and distributed according to a generalized gamma hyperprior
Calvetti et al. (2020)
.

Our proposed approach is more closely related to previous approaches that have considered low-rank approximations obtained via randomization within Gibbs sampling
Brown et al. (2018)
;
Saibaba et al. (2021)
. However, since such methods can only provide good approximations for severely low-rank matrices, they are not suitable for large-scale
linear
inverse problems of interest where
the forward model matrix
has a slowly decaying spectrum. There is also related work on marginal then conditional samplers that work for image deblurring applications
Fox and Norton (2016)
.
Our approach also builds on previous work on sampling from high-dimensional Gaussians that was considered in
Saibaba et al. (2020)
. Since these methods were considered for fixed hyperparameters, a simple extension to hierarchical problems would be expensive, since each MCMC iteration would require a large linear solve. By exploiting the shift invariance property of the generalized Krylov subspaces, we provide a computationally efficient approach for hierarchical sampling where the subspace vectors can be reused across MCMC iterations. This is a feature that is not possible in other settings (e.g., in the sparsity-promoting setting where the hyperparameters define the diagonals of the covariance matrix). Also, by allowing priors defined using covariance kernel functions, we allow hierarchical sampling for more general smoothness priors.
We mention that although most approaches for sampling from a high-dimensional posterior are based on MCMC techniques
Ghanem et al. (2017)
;
Dashti and Stuart (2015)
, there are related works that exploit machine learning techniques, e.g., using deep posterior sampling
Adler and Öktem (2019)
, or hybrid MCMC algorithms that use emulators and autoencoders for Bayesian UQ
Lan et al. (2022)
. However, these approaches require supervised training data that may not be readily available.

Hierarchical Bayesian inverse problem

Consider a linear inverse problem of the form

{\bf b}={\bf A}{\bf x}+{\bf e}

(1)

where observation data
{\bf b}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
are corrupted by measurement error
{\bf e}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
{\bf A}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
represents the forward parameter-to-observable map, and
{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
contains the unknown solution. In a deterministic inverse problem setting, the goal of the inverse problem is to compute a reconstruction of
{\bf x}
(e.g., a point estimate), given
{\bf A}
and
{\bf b}
. In a Bayesian setting, the goal is to fully characterize the posterior probability distribution (thereby quantifying uncertainty about the solutions), given assumptions or prior knowledge about the unknowns.

Given
\lambda,\delta>0
and
{\bm{\mu}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we assume that
{\bf e}
and
{\bf x}
are independent Gaussian random variables such that

{\bf e}\mid\lambda\sim\mathcal{N}\left({\bf 0},\lambda^{-1}{\bf R}\right)\quad\mbox{and}\quad{\bf x}\mid\delta\sim\mathcal{N}\left({\bm{\mu}},\delta^{-1}{\bf Q}\right)

(2)

where
{\bf Q}
and
{\bf R}
are symmetric positive definite covariance matrices. With this model,
{\bf b}\mid{\bf x},\lambda\sim\mathcal{N}({\bf A}{\bf x},\lambda^{-1}{\bf R})
so that the likelihood is given by

\pi({\bf b}\mid{\bf x},\lambda)\propto\lambda^{m/2}\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda}{2}({\bf b}-{\bf A}{\bf x})^{\top}{\bf R}^{-1}({\bf b}-{\bf A}{\bf x})\right)

(3)

and the prior is given by

\pi({\bf x}\mid\delta)\propto\delta^{n/2}\exp\left(-\frac{\delta}{2}({\bf x}-{\bm{\mu}})^{\top}{\bf Q}^{-1}({\bf x}-{\bm{\mu}})\right).

(4)

For fixed
\lambda
and
\delta
, we get a conditional that is also Gaussian. That is,
{\bf x}\mid{\bf b},\lambda,\delta\sim\mathcal{N}({\bf x}_{\rm cond},{\bm{\Gamma}}_{\rm cond})
, where

{\bm{\Gamma}}_{\rm cond}=(\lambda{\bf A}^{\top}{\bf R}^{-1}{\bf A}+\delta{\bf Q}^{-1})^{-1}\mbox{ and }{\bf x}_{\rm cond}={\bm{\Gamma}}_{\rm cond}(\lambda{\bf A}^{\top}{\bf R}^{-1}{\bf b}+\delta{\bf Q}^{-1}{\bm{\mu}}).

(5)

More specifically,

\pi({\bf x}\mid{\bf b},\lambda,\delta)\propto\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda}{2}({\bf b}-{\bf A}{\bf x})^{\top}{\bf R}^{-1}({\bf b}-{\bf A}{\bf x})-\frac{\delta}{2}({\bf x}-{\bm{\mu}})^{\top}{\bf Q}^{-1}({\bf x}-{\bm{\mu}})\right).

(6)

The conditional posterior mode is the minimizer of the negative log-likelihood and corresponds to
the solution of
a general-form Tikhonov problem,

\widehat{\bf x}=\argmax_{\bf x}\pi({\bf x}\mid{\bf b},\lambda,\delta)=\argmin_{\bf x}\frac{\lambda}{2}\|{\bf b}-{\bf A}{\bf x}\|_{{\bf R}^{-1}}^{2}+\frac{\delta}{2}\|{\bf x}-{\bm{\mu}}\|_{{\bf Q}^{-1}}^{2},

(7)

where
\left\|{\bf x}\right\|_{{\bf M}}=\sqrt{{\bf x}^{\top}{\bf M}{\bf x}}
for
symmetric positive definite matrix

{\bf M}
.

We assume that hyperparameters
\lambda
and
\delta
are unknown, and we assume that they are random variables distributed according to some hyperprior. For example, a common assumption is to use gamma hyperpriors defined by

\pi(\lambda)\propto\lambda^{\alpha_{\lambda}-1}\exp(-\beta_{\lambda}\lambda)\quad\mbox{and}\quad\pi(\delta)\propto\delta^{\alpha_{\delta}-1}\exp(-\beta_{\delta}\delta),

(8)

where
\alpha_{\lambda},\alpha_{\delta}
and
\beta_{\lambda},\beta_{\delta}
are given parameters defining the shape and rate of the distributions.
Using Bayes’ theorem with assumptions (
3
), (
4
), and (
8
), the (non-Gaussian) joint posterior probability density
function
is given by,

\displaystyle\pi({\bf x},\lambda,\delta\mid{\bf b})

\displaystyle\propto\lambda^{m/2}\delta^{n/2}\pi({\bf b}\mid{\bf x},\lambda)\pi({\bf x}\mid\delta)\pi(\delta)\pi(\lambda)

\displaystyle\propto\frac{\lambda^{m/2+\alpha_{\lambda}-1}\delta^{n/2+\alpha_{\delta}-1}}{\left((2\pi)^{n+m}|{\bf Q}||{\bf R}|\right)^{1/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\|{\bf A}{\bf x}-{\bf b}\right\|_{{\bf R}^{-1}}^{2}-\frac{\delta}{2}\left\|{\bf x}-{\bm{\mu}}\right\|_{{\bf Q}^{-1}}^{2}-\beta_{\lambda}\lambda-\beta_{\delta}\delta\right)

(9)

where
\pi({\bf b}|{\bf x},\lambda)
and
\pi({\bf x}\mid\delta)
are the likelihood and prior density functions respectively and
|\cdot|
denotes the determinant.

In a Bayesian framework,
\pi({\bf x},\lambda,\delta\mid{\bf b})
is the solution to the inverse problem. However, since the full joint posterior (
9
) is non-Gaussian, exploring the posterior is more challenging, especially for large-scale problems. There are various approaches
that can be
used to describe the posterior distribution. One idea is to compute point estimates such as the
MAP
estimate, corresponding to the maximum of the posterior density function.
Obtaining this point estimate requires sophisticated nonlinear optimization techniques, e.g., computing the MAP requires solving

\min_{{\bf x},\lambda,\delta}\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\|{\bf A}{\bf x}-{\bf b}\right\|_{{\bf R}^{-1}}^{2}+\frac{\delta}{2}\left\|{\bf x}-{\bm{\mu}}\right\|_{{\bf Q}^{-1}}^{2}+\beta_{\lambda}\lambda+\beta_{\delta}\delta-(m/2+\alpha_{\lambda}-1)\log\lambda-(n/2+\alpha_{\delta}-1)\log\delta.

(10)

For UQ,
a common approach is to approximate (
9
) by a Gaussian distribution (e.g., by linearizing around the MAP estimate), but such approximations can be poor (e.g., if far from the MAP estimate) and
may
yield unsatisfactory uncertainty estimates
Gelman et al. (2013)
;
Ghosh et al. (2006)
;
Agrawal et al. (2022)
.

An alternative approach is
to use Monte Carlo methods for sampling from the
joint
posterior (
9
), and
the samples can be used to
obtain summary statistics (e.g., estimation of the posterior mean and variances).

For example, for nonlinear inverse problems, stochastic Newton MCMC approaches could be used, where Gaussian proposals are constructed from local gradient information and local (or MAP-based) Hessian information
Martin et al. (2012)
;
Petra et al. (2014)
. Such methods could be extended to sample from (
9
), where the Hessian matrix of the negative log posterior consists
of the inverse conditional covariance matrix, augmented with two rows and two columns (coming from the 2 hyperparameters), but these approaches would not exploit the directions in which the distribution is Gaussian (e.g., due to linearity of the problem in
{\bf x}
).

A common type of MCMC algorithm is the standard block Gibbs approach
Geman and Geman (1984)
;
Bardsley (2018)
. The main idea is to alternate sampling from the conditional distributions,

\displaystyle\lambda\mid{\bf b},{\bf x},\delta

\displaystyle\sim\Gamma\big(m/2+\alpha_{\lambda},\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\bf A}{\bf x}-{\bf b}\right\|_{{\bf R}^{-1}}^{2}+\beta_{\lambda}\big),

(11)

\displaystyle\delta\mid{\bf b},{\bf x},\lambda

\displaystyle\sim\Gamma\big(n/2+\alpha_{\delta},\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\bf x}-{\bm{\mu}}\right\|_{{\bf Q}^{-1}}^{2}+\beta_{\delta}\big),

(12)

\displaystyle{\bf x}\mid{\bf b},\lambda,\delta

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}\left({\bf x}_{\rm cond},{\bm{\Gamma}}_{\rm cond}\right),

(13)

where
{\bm{\Gamma}}_{\rm cond}
and
{\bf x}_{\rm cond}
are

{\bf x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

\min(1,{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\rho})
where, for fixed
\lambda

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\rho}({\bf x}^{t-1},{\bf x}^{\star})=\frac{h({\bf x}^{\star})q({\bf x}^{t-1}\mid{\bf x}^{\star})}{h({\bf x}^{t-1})q({\bf x}^{\star}\mid{\bf x}^{t-1})}

where

is the density of the proposal distribution. The algorithm generates a Markov chain
\{{\bf x}^{t}\}
that converges to the target distribution
Robert and Casella (2004)

A Metropolis-Hastings independence sampler generates proposal states from a density that is independent of the current state of the chain, i.e., the proposal density has the form

\frac{h({\bf x}^{\star})g({\bf x}^{t-1})}{h({\bf x}^{t-1})g({\bf x}^{\star})}=\frac{w({\bf x}^{\star})}{w({\bf x}^{t-1})}

(14)

where

3
Generalized Golub-Kahan based proposals for independence sampling

The target conditional Gaussian density function is given by

h({\bf x}):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{n}|{\bm{\Gamma}}_{\rm cond}|}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}({\bf x}-{\bf x}_{\rm cond})^{\top}{\bm{\Gamma}}_{\rm cond}^{-1}({\bf x}-{\bf x}_{\rm cond})\right),

(15)

and samples from the distribution
\mathcal{N}({\bf x}_{\rm cond},{\bm{\Gamma}}_{\rm cond})
can be generated as
{\bf x}={\bf x}_{\rm cond}+{\bf G}{\bm{\epsilon}}
where
{\bm{\Gamma}}_{\rm cond}={\bf G}{\bf G}^{\top}
and
{\bm{\epsilon}}\sim\mathcal{N}({\bf 0},{\bf I})
. However, it is computationally expensive to compute
{\bf x}_{\rm cond}
and the product
{\bf G}{\bm{\epsilon}}
. Works such as
Brown et al. (2018)
;
Bui-Thanh et al. (2013)
exploit the low-rank structure of the forward operator
{\bf A}
and rely on factorizations of the prior covariance
{\bf Q}^{-1}={\bf L}{\bf L}^{\top}
to get efficient representations for
{\bm{\Gamma}}_{\rm cond}
that can exploit the fast decay of singular values. These approaches may not be computationally feasible. For example, in atmospheric emissions tomography, the forward model matrices are nowhere near low-rank and, moreover, prior covariance matrices are defined using complicated spherical covariance kernels that allow seasonal changes in variability
Cho et al. (2022)
.
The approach we describe herein can handle these scenarios.

Specifically, we use a Krylov subspace method based on the generalized Golub-Kahan (genGK) bidiagonalization process to approximate
{\bf x}_{\rm cond}
. Then we consider two proposal distributions, one which uses the resulting genGK matrices to form an approximation of
{\bm{\Gamma}}_{\rm cond}
and another which uses preconditioned Krylov sampling. Similar approximations were considered in
Saibaba et al. (2020)
;
Chung et al. (2018)
, but the main difference in this work is that we will use the genGK approximations to define a proposal distribution
g({\bf x})
that approximates the target distribution (
15
). To the best of our knowledge, the use of genGK low-rank approximations within MCMC sampling approaches has not been explored. We begin in
Section

3.1
with a brief overview of the genGK approach, followed by details of the genGK approximation to the target distribution in
Section

3.2
. We provide details regarding the Metropolis-Hastings within Gibbs algorithm with the genGK approximation used for the proposal distribution. For many problems, the genGK approximate distribution provides a computationally efficient approach for generating proposal samples. However, for problems where the genGK proposal distribution may require very large ranks to obtain a sufficient approximation, we propose in
Section

3.3
an alternative proposal sampler that is based on efficient preconditioned Krylov methods and consider its use in Metropolis-Hastings within Gibbs sampling.

3.1
Generalized Golub-Kahan methods

The genGK bidiagonalization process is an iterative Krylov subspace projection method that was developed in
Arioli (2013)
and can be used to efficiently compute general-form Tikhonov solutions (
7
)
Chung and Saibaba (2017)
. The genGK approach is well suited for problems where
mat-vecs
with
{\bf A},

{\bf A}^{\top}
, and
{\bf Q}
can be done efficiently, but
{\bf Q}^{-1}
or any factorization of
{\bf Q}
is expensive. We assume that the inverse and square root of
{\bf R}
are computationally feasible, e.g, an identity or diagonal matrix. For the prior covariance matrix
{\bf Q}
, we focus on problems where explicit computation of the square root and inverse of the covariance matrix
{\bf Q}
are not computationally feasible. This scenario often arises for large-scale problems where
{\bf Q}
is highly structured or is constructed from covariance kernels, possibly on unstructured grids. We focus on the Matérn class of covariance kernels, where
a mat-vec
multiplication with
{\bf Q}
can be performed easily, e.g., in
\mathcal{O}(n\log n)
time by exploiting fast Fourier transforms if the solution is represented on a uniform equi-spaced grid. For such covariance matrices, a symmetric factorization of
{\bf Q}^{-1}={\bf L}^{\top}{\bf L}
is not available, and thus it is not possible to reformulate the regularization term as
\|{\bf L}({\bf x}-{\bm{\mu}})\|_{2}^{2}
, i.e., transforming to standard-form. Instead, we develop methods that work directly with
{\bf Q}
.

With the change of variables
{\bf y}={\bf Q}^{-1}({\bf x}-{\bm{\mu}})
, we get the
equivalent problem,

\min_{{\bf y}}\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\|{\bf A}{\bf Q}{\bf y}-({\bf b}-{\bf A}{\bm{\mu}})\right\|_{{\bf R}^{-1}}^{2}+\frac{\delta}{2}\left\|{\bf y}\right\|_{{\bf Q}}^{2},

(16)

which is projected onto subspaces of increasing dimension in an iterative Krylov projection process. That is, given matrices
{\bf A},{\bf R},
and
{\bf Q}
and vectors
{\bf b}
and
{\bm{\mu}}
,
let
\gamma_{1}{\bf u}_{1}={\bf b}-{\bf A}{\bm{\mu}}
and
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\zeta}_{1}{\bf v}_{1}={\bf A}^{\top}{\bf R}^{-1}{\bf u}_{1},

where
\gamma_{1}=\|{\bf b}-{\bf A}{\bm{\mu}}\|_{2}
and
\zeta_{1}=\|{\bf A}^{\top}{\bf R}^{-1}{\bf u}_{1}\|_{2}
.
At the
k
th iteration of the genGK process, we generate vectors
{\bf u}_{k+1}
and
{\bf v}_{k+1}
such that

\gamma_{k+1}{\bf u}_{k+1}={\bf A}{\bf Q}{\bf v}_{k}-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\zeta}_{k}{\bf u}_{k},\qquad{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\zeta}_{k+1}{\bf v}_{k+1}={\bf A}^{\top}{\bf R}^{-1}{\bf u}_{k+1}-\gamma_{k+1}{\bf v}_{k},

where after
k
iterations, we have matrices

{\bf U}_{k+1}=[{\bf u}_{1},\ldots,{\bf u}_{k+1}],{\bf V}_{k+1}=[{\bf v}_{1},\ldots,{\bf v}_{k+1}],\,\,\mbox{and}\,\,{\bf B}_{k}=\begin{bmatrix}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\zeta}_{1}&&\\
\gamma_{2}&\ddots&\\
&\ddots&{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\zeta}_{k}\\
&&\gamma_{k+1}\end{bmatrix}

that satisfy the following relationships, in exact arithmetic,

{\bf A}{\bf Q}{\bf V}_{k}={\bf U}_{k+1}{\bf B}_{k}\quad\mbox{and}\quad{\bf A}^{\top}{\bf R}^{-1}{\bf U}_{k+1}={\bf V}_{k}{\bf B}_{k}^{\top}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\zeta}_{k+1}{\bf v}_{k+1}{\bf e}_{k+1}^{\top},

(17)

with

{\bf U}_{k+1}^{\top}{\bf R}^{-1}{\bf U}_{k+1}={\bf I}_{k+1}\quad\text{and}\quad{\bf V}_{k}^{\top}{\bf Q}{\bf V}_{k}={\bf I}_{k}.

(18)

The vector
{\bf e}_{i}
is the
i
th column of the identity matrix of the appropriate size.

The genGK process constructs a basis for the Krylov subspaces,

\mathcal{R}({\bf V}_{k})=\mathcal{K}_{k}({\bf A}^{\top}{\bf R}^{-1}{\bf A}{\bf Q},{\bf A}^{\top}{\bf R}^{-1}{\bf b})

and

\mathcal{R}({\bf U}_{k})=\mathcal{K}_{k}({\bf A}{\bf Q}{\bf A}^{\top}{\bf R}^{-1},{\bf b}),

where
\mathcal{K}_{k}({\bf M},{\bf g})=\text{Span}\left\{{\bf g},{\bf M}{\bf g},\ldots,{\bf M}^{k-1}{\bf g}\right\}
and
\mathcal{R}
denotes the column space. At the
k
th iteration, we have an approximate solution for (
7
), given by

{\bf x}_{k}={\bm{\mu}}+{\bf Q}{\bf V}_{k}{\bf z}_{k}
, where
{\bf z}_{k}
solves the projected problem. That is,

\min_{{\bf y}_{k}\in\mathcal{R}({\bf V}_{k})}\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\|{\bf A}{\bf Q}{\bf y}_{k}-({\bf b}-{\bf A}{\bm{\mu}})\right\|_{{\bf R}^{-1}}^{2}+\frac{\delta}{2}\left\|{\bf y}_{k}\right\|_{{\bf Q}}^{2}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\min_{{\bf z}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}}\frac{\lambda}{2}\left\|{\bf B}_{k}{\bf z}_{k}-\gamma_{1}{\bf e}_{1}\right\|_{2}^{2}+\frac{\delta}{2}\left\|{\bf z}_{k}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(19)

Note that by using the genGK approach, we avoid
{\bf Q}^{-1}
and rely on projections of the original problem to obtain a reconstruction in
k
iterations (
k\ll n
).
Moreover, by using the genGK relations, we can define oblique projectors,

\mathcal{P}_{{\bf V}_{k}}={\bf V}_{k}{\bf V}_{k}^{\top}{\bf Q}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad\mathcal{P}_{{\bf U}_{k+1}}={\bf U}_{k+1}{\bf U}_{k+1}^{\top}{\bf R}^{-1},

and we can build a low-rank approximation for
{\bf A}
as

{\bf A}\approx{\bf A}\mathcal{P}_{{\bf V}_{k}}^{\top}={\bf U}_{k+1}{\bf B}_{k}{\bf V}_{k}^{\top}\equiv\widehat{\bf A}.

Such approximations are related to the generalized singular value decomposition and were used in the context of hyperparameter estimation in
Hall-Hooper et al. (2024)
. As described in
Chung and Saibaba (2017)
, the generalized singular values are related to the singular values of
{\bf R}^{-1/2}{\bf A}{\bf Q}^{1/2}
and tend to exhibit faster decay compared to the singular values of
{\bf A}
. Next we will use the genGK approximation to build a proposal distribution that can be used within a Metropolis-Hastings within Gibbs approach for sampling from (
9
). A key feature to note is that the genGK bidiagonalization process does not depend on
\lambda
and
\delta
. That is, all of the resulting matrices
{\bf V}_{k},{\bf B}_{k},{\bf U}_{k+1}
are independent of
\lambda
and
\delta
and can be reused if these parameters change. We will exploit this property in
Section

3.2
for efficient sampling from the genGK proposal distribution and again in
Section

3.3
for efficient conditional mean estimation.

3.2
genGK approximation to the target distribution

Now consider the target conditional distribution (
15
) with conditional mean
{\bf x}_{\rm cond}
and covariance matrix
{\bm{\Gamma}}_{\rm cond}
.
Recall that after
k
iterations of the genGK process, we have
{\bf x}_{k}\approx{\bf x}_{\rm cond}
and matrices
{\bf U}_{k+1},\ {\bf V}_{k},
and
{\bf B}_{k}
that satisfy the relations in (
17
) and (
18
). Following a similar approach as in
Saibaba et al. (2020)
, we aim to use these matrices to approximate
{\bm{\Gamma}}^{1/2}
. Notice that one can factorize the covariance matrix as
{\bm{\Gamma}}_{\rm cond}={\bm{\Gamma}}_{\rm cond}^{1/2}{\bm{\Gamma}}_{\rm cond}^{1/2}
, where

{\bm{\Gamma}}_{\rm cond}^{1/2}=\delta^{-1/2}{\bf Q}^{1/2}\left({\bf I}+\frac{\lambda}{\delta}{\bf Q}^{1/2}{\bf A}^{\top}{\bf R}^{-1}{\bf A}{\bf Q}^{1/2}\right)^{-1/2}.

(20)

Although we write out such a factorization for exposition purposes, we will not compute
{\bf Q}^{1/2}
explicitly. We will use a Lanczos algorithm to access it in a matrix-free fashion, following the procedure outlined in the appendix
Section

6
, and it will be part of a preprocessing step (that is independent of MCMC sampling).

Prior to sampling, we compute the low-rank representation,
{\bf Q}^{1/2}{\bf V}_{k}{\bf B}_{k}^{\top}{\bf B}_{k}{\bf V}_{k}^{\top}{\bf Q}^{1/2}={\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\bf P}_{k}}{\bm{\Theta}}_{k}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\bf P}_{k}}^{\top}
.
Then using the genGK approximation
{\bf A}^{\top}{\bf R}^{-1}{\bf A}\approx\widehat{\bf A}^{\top}{\bf R}^{-1}\widehat{\bf A}={\bf V}_{k}{\bf B}_{k}^{\top}{\bf B}_{k}{\bf V}_{k}^{\top}
, we can define the matrix approximation,

\displaystyle\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}_{\rm cond}

\displaystyle=(\lambda{\bf V}_{k}{\bf B}_{k}^{\top}{\bf B}_{k}{\bf V}_{k}^{\top}+\delta{\bf Q}^{-1})^{-1}

\displaystyle={\bf Q}^{1/2}(\lambda\underbrace{{\bf Q}^{1/2}{\bf V}_{k}{\bf B}_{k}^{\top}{\bf B}_{k}{\bf V}_{k}^{\top}{\bf Q}^{1/2}}_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\bf P}_{k}}{\bm{\Theta}}_{k}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\bf P}_{k}}^{\top}}+\delta{\bf I})^{-1}{\bf Q}^{1/2}.

Thus, the square root of the conditional covariance matrix can be approximated as,

\displaystyle{\bm{\Gamma}}_{\rm cond}^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\delta^{-1/2}{\bf Q}^{1/2}\left(\frac{\lambda}{\delta}{\bf Q}^{1/2}\underbrace{{\bf A}^{\top}{\bf R}^{-1}{\bf A}}_{\approx{\bf V}_{k}{\bf B}_{k}^{\top}{\bf B}_{k}{\bf V}_{k}^{\top}}{\bf Q}^{1/2}+{\bf I}\right)^{-1/2}

\displaystyle\approx\delta^{-1/2}{\bf Q}^{1/2}\left(\frac{\lambda}{\delta}\underbrace{{\bf Q}^{1/2}{\bf V}_{k}{\bf B}_{k}^{\top}{\bf B}_{k}{\bf V}_{k}^{\top}{\bf Q}^{1/2}}_{\approx{\bf P}_{k}{\bm{\Theta}}_{k}{\bf P}_{k}^{\top}}+{\bf I}\right)^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=\delta^{-1/2}{\bf Q}^{1/2}({\bf I}-{\bf P}_{k}{\bf D}_{k}{\bf P}_{k}^{\top})\equiv\widehat{{\bm{\Gamma}}}_{\rm cond}^{1/2}

where
{\bf D}_{k}\equiv{\bf I}_{k}-({\bf I}_{k}+\frac{\lambda}{\delta}\mathbf{\Theta}_{k})^{-1/2}
. Finally, we can define the proposal distribution
\mathcal{N}({\bf x}_{k},\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}_{\rm cond})
, i.e.,

g_{1}({\bf x}):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{n}|\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}_{\rm cond}|}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}({\bf x}-{\bf x}_{k})^{\top}\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}_{\rm cond}^{-1}({\bf x}-{\bf x}_{k})\right),

(21)

and draw a sample,

\displaystyle{\bf x}^{\star}

\displaystyle={\bf x}_{k}+\widehat{{\bm{\Gamma}}}_{\rm cond}^{1/2}{\bm{\xi}}

(22)

\displaystyle={\bm{\mu}}+{\bf Q}{\bf V}_{k}{\bf z}_{k}+\delta^{-1/2}{\bf Q}^{1/2}\left({\bf I}-{\bf P}_{k}{\bf D}_{k}{\bf P}_{k}^{\top}\right){\bm{\xi}}

(23)

for
{\bm{\xi}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bf 0,{\bf I})
.
Theoretical results regarding the accuracy of the approximate distribution to the true conditional distribution can be found in
Saibaba et al. (2020)
.

Next we incorporate the genGK approximation above as a proposal distribution within a Metropolis-Hastings within Gibbs approach to sample from (
9
). We need to investigate the acceptance ratio. Let
{\bf x}
denote the current state of the chain and let
{\bf x}^{\star}
denote the proposed state. Then from Proposition 1 of
Brown et al. (2018)
, we have that the acceptance ratio can be computed as
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\rho}_{1}=\frac{w({\bf x}^{\star})}{w({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\bf x}^{t-1}})}
where

w({\bf x})=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}{\bf x}^{\top}\left({\bm{\Gamma}}_{\rm cond}^{-1}-\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}_{\rm cond}^{-1}\right){\bf x}\right).

Note that at each iteration, we have the weight from the previous iteration
w({\bf x}^{t-1})
but we must compute the weight
w({\bf x}^{\star})
. An efficient implementation of this can be obtained by observing that

\displaystyle\log w({\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\bf x}})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\bf x}}^{\top}\left({\bm{\Gamma}}_{\rm cond}^{-1}-\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}_{\rm cond}^{-1}\right){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\bf x}}

(24)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\bf x}}^{\top}\left(\lambda{\bf A}^{\top}{\bf R}^{-1}{\bf A}+\delta{\bf Q}^{-1}-{\bf Q}^{-1/2}(\lambda{\bf P}_{k}{\bm{\Theta}}_{k}{\bf P}_{k}^{\top}+\delta{\bf I}){\bf Q}^{-1/2}\right){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\bf x}}

(25)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\lambda}{2}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\bf x}}^{\top}\left({\bf A}^{\top}{\bf R}^{-1}{\bf A}-{\bf Q}^{-1/2}{\bf P}_{k}{\bm{\Theta}}_{k}{\bf P}_{k}^{\top}{\bf Q}^{-1/2}\right){\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{\bf x}}.

(26)

Similarly, the quality of the low-rank approximation to the target distribution will be evident from the acceptance ratio. The
Metropolis-Hastings
within Gibbs algorithm with the genGK approximation used for proposal sampling is summarized in
Algorithm

2
. Notice that mat-vecs with
{\bf A}
and
{\bf A}^{\top}
are required in the genGK process (i.e.,
k
multiplications with
{\bf A}
and
{\bf A}^{\top}
) and again in the computation of the acceptance ratio. Also, for each new sample of
\lambda^{t}
and
\delta^{t}
,
{\bf B}_{k}
and
{\bf V}_{k}
are reused for efficient computation of
{\bf x}_{k}
, and
{\bf P}_{k}
and
{\bm{\Theta}}_{k}
are reused for the efficient computation of the proposal sample.
From Proposition 3 in
Brown et al. (2018)
, the subchain from
Algorithm

has stationary distribution
h({\bf x})=\pi({\bf x}\mid{\bf b},\lambda,\delta)
and is uniformly ergodic
Robert and Casella (2004)

3.3
Proposal sampling using a preconditioned Lanczos method

Next we consider an alternative proposal distribution, where we generate an approximate sample from
\mathcal{N}({\bf x}_{k},{\bm{\Gamma}}_{\rm cond})
by using a preconditioned Lanczos method. This approach follows that of Method 2 in
Saibaba et al. (2020)
. First rewrite the covariance matrix as

{\bm{\Gamma}}_{\rm cond}=\left(\delta{\bf Q}^{-1}+\lambda{\bf A}^{\top}{\bf R}^{-1}{\bf A}\right)^{-1}=\lambda{\bf Q}{\bf F}^{-1}{\bf Q}

where

{\bf F}=\frac{\delta}{\lambda}{\bf Q}+{\bf Q}{\bf A}^{\top}{\bf R}^{-1}{\bf A}{\bf Q}.

Next define

{\bf S}_{F}=\lambda^{-1/2}{\bf Q}{\bf F}^{-1/2}

which is a square root matrix of
{\bm{\Gamma}}_{\rm cond}
, that is
{\bm{\Gamma}}_{\rm cond}={\bf S}_{F}{\bf S}_{F}^{\top}
. Let
{\bf G}
be a preconditioner satisfying
{\bf G}{\bf G}^{\top}\approx{\bf F}^{-1}
which allows us to compute a square root of
{\bf F}
. Now we have the exact factorization

{\bm{\Gamma}}_{\rm cond}={\bf S}_{F}{\bf S}_{F}^{\top}\qquad{\bf S}_{F}=\lambda^{-1/2}{\bf Q}{\bf G}^{\top}\left({\bf G}{\bf F}{\bf G}^{\top}\right)^{-1/2}.

Then we draw the sample as

{\bf x}^{\star}={\bf x}_{k}+{\bf S}_{F}{\bm{\xi}}

(27)

for
{\bm{\xi}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bf 0,{\bf I})
. It should be noted that while the factorization
{\bm{\Gamma}}_{\rm cond}={\bf S}_{F}{\bf S}_{F}^{\top}
is exact, the mean
{\bf x}_{k}
is the genGK solution computed after
k
iterations. Thus, the sample drawn is not from the exact target conditional density function
h({\bf x})
but a proposal distribution
\mathcal{N}({\bf x}_{k},{\bm{\Gamma}}_{\rm cond})
with a probability density given by

g_{2}({\bf x}):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{n}|{\bm{\Gamma}}_{\rm cond}|}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}({\bf x}-{\bf x}_{k})^{\top}{\bm{\Gamma}}_{\rm cond}^{-1}({\bf x}-{\bf x}_{k})\right).

As shown in the appendix
Section

8
, the acceptance ratio for
g_{2}({\bf x})
can be evaluated efficiently as

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\rho}_{2}({\bf x}^{t-1},{\bf x}^{\star})

\displaystyle=\frac{h({\bf x}^{\star})g_{2}({\bf x}^{t-1})}{h({\bf x}^{t-1})g_{2}({\bf x}^{\star})}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\left({\bf x}^{\star}-{\bf x}^{t-1}\right)^{\top}\left(\lambda\left(\gamma_{1}{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\zeta}_{1}{\bf v}_{1}-{\bf V}_{k+1}\begin{bmatrix}{\bf B}_{k}^{\top}\\
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\zeta}_{k+1}{\bf e}_{k+1}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}{\bf B}_{k}{\bf z}_{k}\right)-\delta{\bf V}_{k}{\bf z}_{k}\right)\right).

Lastly, to draw the proposal sample (
27
), the matrix
{\bf S}_{F}
is never actually formed. Instead applications of the matrix
{\bf G}^{\top}({\bf G}{\bf F}{\bf G}^{\top})^{-1/2}
on a vector
{\bm{\xi}}
are performed using a preconditioned Lanczos approach. Note that a mat-vec with
{\bf F}
requires one mat-vec with
{\bf A}
and
{\bf A}^{\top}
and two mat-vecs with
{\bf Q}
. For computing the acceptance ratio, we can avoid further mat-vecs with
{\bf A}
and
{\bf A}^{\top}
by exploiting the
genGK
relationship.
This approach can be computationally expensive, compared to the approach described in

Section

3.2
, especially if the forward operator is expensive; however, it serves as a good alternative in cases where the goal is to get a few, very good samples from the proposal density.

4
Numerical results

In this section, we provide multiple examples to demonstrate the effectiveness and applicability of the proposed sampling approaches. We begin in
Section

4.1
with a seismic tomography example, where the problem is small enough so that comparisons to existing methods are feasible. Then in
Section

4.2
we consider an atmospheric tomography example where comparisons to existing methods are not feasible, but the genGK approximation provides good proposal samples. Finally, in
Section

4.3
we consider a dynamic photoacoustic tomography problem, where the goal is to reconstruct a spatiotemporal image. This is a highly underdetermined problem, where the number of unknowns is very large, so the prior is critical.

In all of the examples, three chains, each with
T
samples, are run in parallel.
We use a wavelet based estimation of the noise covariance parameter
\lambda_{est}

Donoho (1995)
and an estimate of
\delta_{est}

using the genHyBR method
Chung and Saibaba (2017)
with the weighted
generalized cross-validation
method on the projected problem. Each chain is initialized using a random number within an interval centered at
\lambda_{est}
and
\delta_{est}
.
Following
Bardsley (2018)
, the parameters defining the shape and rate of the gamma hyperpriors in
Equation

8
are set to be
\alpha_{\lambda}=\alpha_{\delta}=1
and
\beta_{\lambda}=\beta_{\delta}=10^{-4}
.

To ensure that there is no bias in the results, we remove samples from the initial stage of the MCMC chain. During this stage, called burn-in, the samples are moving from the starting position to a region that has a higher probability of being the target distribution
Bardsley (2018)
. For all examples, the first
50\%
of samples from the initial burn-in stage are removed before any analysis is done on the mean and variance of
\{{\bf x}^{t}\}
or the hyperparameter chains.

Next, to examine whether the MCMC chain is in equilibrium, we perform the Geweke test on the individual
\lambda
and
\delta
chains. Here, we compare the mean of the first
10\%
of samples, denoted
\mu_{10}
, to the mean of the last
50\%
, denoted
\mu_{50}
, and either accept or reject the null hypothesis,
H_{0}:\mu_{10}=\mu_{50}
. For
p
values close to 1, there is strong evidence that the chain is in equilibrium.

As a convergence diagnostic,

we use the Gelman-Rubin statistic, denoted as
\widehat{R}
, which compares the variance between multiple chains run in parallel.
A desired value for

\widehat{R}
is close to 1
Gelman and Rubin (1992)
. The cutoff is commonly set at 1.01, but in practice it ranges from 1.003 to 1.1
Vats and Knudson (2021)
.

Finally, we test the independence of the individual
\lambda
and
\delta
chains through autocorrelation functions (ACF), as described in
Bardsley (2018)
. If the ACF decays to 0 fast enough, this means that there is no correlation between the samples in the individual chains.
To estimate the number of independent samples,
we
compute
the effective sample size (ESS)
,
T_{\rm ESS}=\frac{T}{\tau_{\rm int}},
where
\tau_{\rm int}
is the integrated autocorrelation time approximated using Sokal’s adaptive truncated periodogram-estimator
Sokal (1997)
.

5
Conclusions

This paper provides an approach to perform
UQ
for large-scale hierarchical Bayesian inverse problems, where Metropolis-Hastings independence sampling within Gibbs is used to overcome the limitations of
MCMC
methods. We consider two proposal distributions, both of which are based on
genGK
methods. First,
we define a proposal distribution using a low-rank genGK approximation to the conditional covariance matrix, where we can reuse the genGK matrices to efficiently draw proposal samples.
Second, for matrices where a low-rank approximation is not sufficient, we describe a preconditioned Lanczos-based method to efficiently perform computations with the square-root of the conditional covariance to draw proposal samples. In addition to comparisons with existing approaches for small problems, we demonstrate the performance of these methods on a variety of large-scale inverse problems, including atmospheric models and dynamic tomography problems.
Although it is known that Gibbs and Metropolis-Hastings within Gibbs sampling have potential drawbacks, few alternatives exist for efficient sampling for hierarchical large-scale inverse problems. The described approaches provide a computationally convenient approach for sampling, where
genGK
approximations can be used for proposal sampling. Future work includes further investigations, e.g., for scenarios with strong correlations and for other prior models, as well as incorporating further convergence diagnostics such as the multivariate version of the ESS.

Low-Rank representation of square-root covariance

First note that operations with the square-root matrix
{\bf Q}^{1/2}
can be performed efficiently, using a Lanczos algorithm that only requires mat-vecs with
{\bf Q}
, see
Saibaba et al. (2020)
and references therein. Next recall after
k
iterations of the genGK bidiagonalization process, we have

{\bf Q}^{1/2}{\bf A}^{\top}{\bf R}^{-1}{\bf A}{\bf Q}^{1/2}\approx{\bf Q}^{1/2}{\bf V}_{k}{\bf B}_{k}^{\top}{\bf B}_{k}{\bf V}_{k}^{\top}{\bf Q}^{1/2}.

Let
{\bf Q}^{(1)}{\bf R}^{(1)}={\bf B}_{k}
and
{\bf Q}^{(2)}{\bf R}^{(2)}={\bf Q}^{1/2}{\bf V}_{k}({\bf R}^{(1)})^{\top}
be QR decompositions, then

\displaystyle{\bf Q}^{1/2}{\bf V}_{k}{\bf B}_{k}^{\top}{\bf B}_{k}{\bf V}_{k}^{\top}{\bf Q}^{1/2}

\displaystyle={\bf Q}^{(2)}{\bf R}^{(2)}({\bf R}^{(2)})^{\top}({\bf Q}^{(2)})^{\top}.

Taking the eigenvalue decomposition
{\bf R}^{(2)}({\bf R}^{(2)})^{\top}={\bf W}_{k}\mathbf{\Theta}_{k}{\bf W}_{k}^{\top}
and assigning
{\bf P}_{k}={\bf Q}^{(2)}{\bf W}_{k}
, we get the low-rank representation,

{\bf Q}^{1/2}{\bf A}^{\top}{\bf R}^{-1}{\bf A}{\bf Q}^{1/2}\approx{\bf P}_{k}\mathbf{\Theta}_{k}{\bf P}_{k}\quad\text{where}\quad\mathbf{\Theta}_{k}={\rm diag\!}\left(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{k}\right).

(29)

To compute the inverse square-root
\left(\delta{\bf I}+\lambda{\bf Q}^{1/2}{\bf A}^{\top}{\bf R}^{-1}{\bf A}{\bf Q}^{1/2}\right)^{-1/2}
, following
Bui-Thanh et al. (2013)
, we use the Woodbury identity to get

\displaystyle\left(\delta{\bf I}+\lambda{\bf Q}^{1/2}{\bf A}^{\top}{\bf R}^{-1}{\bf A}{\bf Q}^{1/2}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\delta^{-1}\left({\bf I}+{\bf P}_{k}(\lambda/\delta)\mathbf{\Theta}_{k}{\bf P}_{k}^{\top}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\delta^{-1}\left({\bf I}-{\bf P}_{k}\left({\bf I}_{k}+(\lambda/\delta)^{-1}\mathbf{\Theta}_{k}^{-1}\right)^{-1}{\bf P}_{k}^{\top}\right).

Then we can compute a square-root matrix as

\displaystyle\delta^{-1/2}\left({\bf I}+{\bf P}_{k}(\lambda/\delta)\mathbf{\Theta}_{k}{\bf P}_{k}^{\top}\right)^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=\delta^{-1/2}\left({\bf I}_{n}-{\bf P}_{k}{\bf D}_{k}{\bf P}_{k}^{\top}\right)

(30)

where
{\bf D}_{k}\equiv{\bf I}_{k}-\left((\lambda/\delta)\mathbf{\Theta}_{k}+{\bf I}_{k}\right)^{-1/2}
.

Prior norm for genGK proposal

\left\|{\bf x}^{t-1}\right\|_{{\bf Q}^{-1}}^{2}
, we use an equivalent expression that avoids computations with
{\bf Q}^{-1}
. Let
{\bf x}_{k}={\bf Q}{\bf V}_{k}{\bf z}_{k}
be the genGK approximation used as the mean of the distribution from which
{\bf x}^{t-1}
was drawn and
{\bf D}_{k}
be the matrix used to form the corresponding
\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}_{\rm cond}^{1/2}

Then, the norm can be written as

\displaystyle\left\|{\bf x}^{t-1}\right\|_{{\bf Q}^{-1}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left({\bf Q}{\bf V}_{k}{\bf z}_{k}+\frac{{\bf Q}^{1/2}}{\sqrt{\delta}}\left({\bf I}-{\bf P}_{k}{\bf D}_{k}{\bf P}_{k}^{\top}\right){\bm{\xi}}\right)^{\top}{\bf Q}^{-1}\left({\bf Q}{\bf V}_{k}{\bf z}_{k}+\frac{{\bf Q}^{1/2}}{\sqrt{\delta}}\left({\bf I}-{\bf P}_{k}{\bf D}_{k}{\bf P}_{k}^{\top}\right){\bm{\xi}}\right)

\displaystyle=\left({\bf Q}^{1/2}{\bf V}_{k}{\bf z}_{k}+\delta^{-1/2}\left({\bf I}-{\bf P}_{k}{\bf D}_{k}{\bf P}_{k}^{\top}\right){\bm{\xi}}\right)^{\top}\left({\bf Q}^{1/2}{\bf V}_{k}{\bf z}_{k}+\delta^{-1/2}\left({\bf I}-{\bf P}_{k}{\bf D}_{k}{\bf P}_{k}^{\top}\right){\bm{\xi}}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(({\bf V}_{k}{\bf z}_{k})^{\top}{\bf Q}^{1/2}+\delta^{-1/2}{\bm{\xi}}^{\top}\left({\bf I}-{\bf P}_{k}{\bf D}_{k}{\bf P}_{k}^{\top}\right)\right)\left({\bf Q}^{1/2}{\bf V}_{k}{\bf z}_{k}+\delta^{-1/2}\left({\bf I}-{\bf P}_{k}{\bf D}_{k}{\bf P}_{k}^{\top}\right){\bm{\xi}}\right)

so finally we have

\left\|{\bf x}^{t-1}\right\|_{{\bf Q}^{-1}}^{2}=\left({\bf V}_{k}{\bf z}_{k}\right)^{\top}({\bf x}_{k}+2\widehat{\bm{\Gamma}}_{\rm cond}^{1/2}{\bm{\xi}})+{\bm{\xi}}^{\top}\delta^{-1}\left({\bf I}-{\bf P}_{k}\left(({\bf D}_{k})^{2}-2{\bf D}_{k}\right){\bf P}_{k}^{\top}\right){\bm{\xi}}.

(31)

SVD and rSVD approximations of the conditional covariance matrix

Assume the factorization
{\bf Q}={\bf L}^{\top}{\bf L}
is accessible. Then, for fixed
\lambda
and
\delta
,

{\bm{\Gamma}}_{\rm cond}=\left(\delta{\bf L}^{-1}{\bf L}^{-\top}+\lambda{\bf A}^{\top}{\bf R}^{-1}{\bf A}\right)^{-1}={\bf L}^{\top}\left(\delta{\bf I}+\lambda{\bf L}{\bf A}^{\top}{\bf R}^{-1}{\bf A}{\bf L}^{\top}\right)^{-1}{\bf L}.

To form a low-rank approximation of
{\bf H}={\bf L}{\bf A}^{\top}{\bf R}^{-1}{\bf A}{\bf L}^{\top}
, we consider two approaches.

One approach is to use the truncated SVD (tSVD) where a rank
k
approximation is formed by computing
{\bf R}^{-1/2}{\bf A}{\bf L}^{\top}\approx\widehat{\bf U}_{k}{\bm{\Sigma}}_{k}\widehat{\bf V}_{k}
where
\widehat{\bf U}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times k}
and
\widehat{\bf V}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
are orthonormal matrices containing the first
k
left and right singular vectors of
{\bf R}^{-1/2}{\bf A}{\bf L}^{\top}
respectively and
{\bm{\Sigma}}_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
is a diagonal matrix with the first
k
largest singular values. Then
{\bf H}\approx\widehat{\bf V}_{k}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{2}_{k}\widehat{\bf V}_{k}^{\top}
.

Another approach is to use the randomized SVD (rSVD) algorithm which begins by using a random matrix
{\bm{\Omega}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(k+p)}
whose entries are realizations of i.i.d. standard Gaussian random variables to approximate the column space of
{\bf H}
, i.e.,
{\bf Y}={\bf H}{\bm{\Omega}}
. Here
k
is the target rank and
p
is an oversampling parameter usually taken to be a small integer (
p=5
or
p=10
). Compute a thin-QR factorization,
{\bf Y}=\widehat{\bf Q}\widehat{\bf R}
. Now an approximation of
{\bf H}
is given by

{\bf H}\approx\widehat{\bf Q}\widehat{\bf Q}^{\top}{\bf H}\widehat{\bf Q}\widehat{\bf Q}^{\top}=\widehat{\bf Q}\tilde{{\bf U}}\tilde{{\bm{\Sigma}}}\tilde{{\bf U}}^{\top}\widehat{\bf Q}^{\top}=\tilde{{\bf V}}\tilde{{\bm{\Sigma}}}\tilde{{\bf V}}^{\top}

where
\widehat{\bf Q}^{\top}{\bf H}\widehat{\bf Q}=\tilde{{\bf U}}\tilde{{\bm{\Sigma}}}\tilde{{\bf U}}^{\top}
is an eigendecomposition and
\tilde{{\bf V}}=\widehat{\bf Q}\tilde{{\bf U}}
.

For both methods, the approximation of
{\bf H}
can be used to form a low-rank representation of
{\bm{\Gamma}}_{\rm cond}
following a similar approach to the one in
Section

3.2
. Using the rSVD approximation as a proposal sampler was considered in
