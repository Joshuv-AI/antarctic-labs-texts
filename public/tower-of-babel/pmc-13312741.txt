Impact of a real-time automatic quality control system for magnetically controlled capsule gastroscopy: a multicenter randomized controlled trial
Authors: Xiaoya Wang, Dawei Shen, Yusha Zhao, Jiaqi Xing, Jing Liu, Ruchen Zhou, Chongmei Yang, Chengxia Liu, Xingbin Ma, Jian Ge, Hao Zhang, Wenjin Yuan, Hang Zhang, Yu Ma, Ping Hu, Xiuli Zuo, Yanqing Li, Zhen Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundQuality control can reduce variations in operators’ performance conducting magnetically controlled capsule gastroscopy (MCCG). However, an optimal method for quality control in routine MCCG procedures is still lacking. This study aimed to develop an automatic quality control system (AQCS) and assess its effectiveness in a clinical trial.MethodsWe developed the AQCS using convolutional neural network (CNN) models to monitor inspection completeness, evaluate gastric cleanliness, and identify suspicious lesions. Then, patients were prospectively randomized to undergo routine MCCG with or without AQCS assistance. The primary outcome was the blind spot rate in the AQCS and control groups.ResultsThe CNN model demonstrated specificity of 98.27–99.30% and sensitivity of 76.33–96.35% in gastric site identification. Between August 27, 2021, and July 28, 2022, a total of 200 patients were randomized, with 98 and 96 patients analyzed in the AQCS and control groups, respectively. Compared to the control group, the AQCS group achieved lower blind spot rates (median: 0.00% vs. 16.67%, P < 0.01), higher lesion detection rates (75.51% vs. 60.42%, P = 0.02), and comparable gastric examination time (28.63 min vs. 27.58 min, P = 0.48). Additionally, AQCS showed high consistency with expert evaluations in cleanliness assessment (Kappa = 0.95, P < 0.01). No serious adverse events occurred in either group.ConclusionsAQCS significantly reduced the blind spot rate during MCCG procedures. It could be a powerful assistant tool to mitigate operator skill variability and enhance the overall quality of routine MCCG examinations.Trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov Identifier NCT04954677.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-026-04901-0.

Background

Gastric pathologies, particularly gastric cancer and peptic ulcer disease, represent the most prevalent gastrointestinal disorders globally. Gastric cancer is the fifth most common cause of cancer-related death worldwide [1]. Magnetically controlled capsule gastroscopy (MCCG) has emerged as a valuable examination for gastric diseases with an accuracy comparable to conventional gastroscopy [2–4]. Additionally, MCCG is noninvasive, requires no sedation, and eliminates the risk of cross-infection, making it a highly attractive option for both patients and clinicians.

The diagnostic performance of MCCG relies on complete and clear visualization of the entire gastric mucosa. However, blind areas may occur during MCCG examinations, particularly in the gastric fundus and cardia, which may lead to the omission of early gastric cancers or premalignant lesions [5–7]. In contrast to conventional gastroscopy, MCCG is inherently limited by its inability to actively perform gastric mucosal cleansing via water irrigation. Insufficient gastric preparation reduces the cleanliness of the gastric mucosa, compromises the integrity of the visual field, and potentially leads to misdiagnosis or omission [8]. Therefore, MCCG exhibits significant operator dependence, and robust quality control measures are essential to ensure diagnostic reliability. Although several guidelines and consensuses recommend a standardized approach for optimizing MCCG, including complete gastric mapping and thorough mucosal cleansing [9, 10], covering everything for each case in real clinical scenarios was challenging, especially in high-volume endoscopy centers. These limitations underscore the need for a feasible and effective method for quality control in routine MCCG procedures.

Recent advancements in artificial intelligence (AI) have shown great potential in improving bowel preparation and minimizing blind spots during endoscopy [11–13]. AI systems can automatically recognize anatomical landmarks, identify incomplete areas, evaluate mucosal cleanliness, and provide immediate feedback to operators, thereby reducing operator dependency and variability. Research has explored the application of AI for MCCG in detecting gastric lesions [14], whereas few studies focus on real-time quality control specifically for MCCG. Existing studies often suffer from limited sample sizes, single-center designs, and retrospective data without prospective validation, which significantly constrains their clinical relevance and general applicability [15]. Addressing these gaps, mainly through randomized controlled trials (RCTs), is essential to unlocking AI’s full potential in MCCG.

In this study, we aimed to develop a real-time automatic quality control system (AQCS) for MCCG to monitor inspection completeness, evaluate gastric preparation, and detect lesions. To assess the clinical applicability of AQCS, we conducted a multicenter RCT, providing a more rigorous and reliable assessment of its performance.

Methods

We initially developed two convolutional neural network (CNN) models: Model I for identifying gastric sites and Model E for evaluating gastric cleanliness. By integrating these models with the existing lesion detection model from Ankon Technologie, we constructed a real-time AQCS for MCCG. We then evaluated its clinical applicability through a multicenter RCT.

AQCS establishment

Image preparation and distribution

Between January 1, 2019, and January 1, 2020, 35,980 MCCG images were retrospectively collected from 380 patients across three endoscopy centers: Qilu Hospital of Shandong University, Shandong Meizhao Health Technology Co., Ltd., and Juxian Meinian Health Examination Management Co., Ltd. Images were obtained using the NaviCam MCCG system (Ankon Technologies (Wuhan) Co., Ltd). Two physicians, each with over 300 cases of experience reading MCCG images, independently labeled all images, noting the gastric sites and gastric cleanliness [10]. Disagreements were resolved by a third physician with over 500 cases of experience in reading MCCG images.

We utilized 32,375 images for the Model I and 3,605 for the Model E. The number of images in training and test sets was eight-to-two ratio. The site classification followed the clinical guideline for MCCG(2021 edition) [10], resulting in the identification of 12 gastric sites: cardia, fundus, greater curvature, lesser curvature, anterior and posterior walls of the gastric body, angulus, anterior and posterior walls of the gastric angulus, antrum, lesser curvature of the gastric antrum, and pylorus. To address the issue of insufficient stomach folds stretching, we introduced a category labeled “unstretched mucosa.” In addition, we used a four-point grading scale to evaluate gastric cleanliness. The scale was defined as follows: 4 (excellent), no obvious mucus, foam, contents, or turbidity in the stomach; 3 (good), a small amount not interfering with complete observation; 2 (fair), a considerable amount interfering with complete observation; 1 (poor), a large amount precluding observation. Images used for cleanliness scoring were equally distributed among the four levels [10] (Additional file 1: Fig. S1).

Development of AQCS based on convolutional neural networks

Model I and Model E used the ResNet50 CNN as the backbone [16, 17]. We desensitized the related data and personal information of cases in each sub-center. Meanwhile, several data augmentation strategies were employed to enhance the diversity of the training set, including random rotations, distortion, and mirror flipping. Model training employed early termination when test set loss plateaued to minimize overfitting. The existing lesion detection model was a three-class classification system based on the ResNet50 CNN architecture, achieving recognition accuracies of 98.69% for normal images, 97.32% for inflammation/bleeding/ulcer, and 88.01% for polyp/elevation (Additional file 1: Fig. S2).

The distinction between anterior and posterior walls of the gastric body and angulus was established based on the CNN model and the following rules [18]: anterior wall—supine position, capsule facing upward; posterior wall—supine position, capsule facing downward. The accelerometer inside the capsule determined the capsule’s orientation (Additional file 1: Fig. S3).

We developed a real-time AQCS by integrating the CNN models into the MCCG operating system. This system enables real-time highlighting of inspection completeness, mucosal cleanliness, and suspicious lesion areas. The interface between the AQCS and the MCCG control system is fully unified with the same monitoring screen (Fig. 1).

A multicenter RCT

Trial design

We conducted a multicenter, single-blind RCT at three endoscopy centers between August 27, 2021, and July 28, 2022, to evaluate the performance of MCCG with or without the assistance of AQCS. The participating centers included Qilu Hospital of Shandong University, Binzhou Medical University Hospital, and Shandong Provincial Hospital Affiliated to Shandong First Medical University. Central ethics approval was obtained from the institutional review board of Qilu Hospital of Shandong University (KYLL-202011-087), and all institutional review boards of the participating hospitals approved this study. The clinical trial was registered on July 8, 2021 (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier, NCT04954677). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Participants

Consecutive patients aged 18 to 75 years scheduled for MCCG examinations were enrolled. Patients with any of the following conditions were excluded: [1] allergic to the gastric preparation ingredients; [2] history of gastrointestinal bleeding or ulcers within the last 24 months; [3] esophageal and gastric tumor diseases that have been scheduled for surgical treatment; [4] suspected or known gastrointestinal obstruction, stenosis, fistula, or diverticula; symptoms of gastrointestinal obstruction; [5] history of gastrointestinal or abdominal surgery altering gastrointestinal anatomy; [6] inoperative conditions or refusal to undergo abdominal surgery if required (i.e., if the capsule is retained and cannot be removed by endoscopy); [7] cardiac pacemaker implantation except magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-conditional pacemaker; [8] implanted electromedical devices; [9] planned MRI examination before excretion of the capsule endoscopy; [10] pregnant women; [11] dysphagia or delayed gastric emptying; [12] refusal to participate.

Randomization and masking

All eligible patients were randomly assigned (1:1) to either the AQCS or the control group via block randomization with a block size of four, stratified by center. The randomization lists, generated by a computer, were put into sealed opaque envelopes and then opened at the commencement of the procedure by the staff assistant to ensure masking of allocation. MCCG operators were not blinded to randomization status, while patients, physicians of MCCG image reading, MCCG experts involved in the subsequent video review, and statistical analysts were all blinded.

Procedures

The enrolled patients were randomly assigned to undergo an MCCG examination with or without the assistance of AQCS. In the AQCS group, except for the original videos, there were four additional information presented to MCCG operators: [1] the completion of 12 sites; [2] the cleanliness score for 12 sites; [3] lesion detection; [4] gastric examination time (the time for examination of gastric sites under external magnetic field guidance). Each center had one participating operator with at least 100 procedures of performing MCCG and one participating physician with at least 300 experiences in reading MCCG images and providing diagnoses and reports. Before the initiation of this study, all operators underwent standardized training and performed five cases of MCCG with AQCS assistance to ensure familiarity with the procedure. Video 1 shows the AQCS operating in real-time for MCCG quality control. The gastric examination time is calculated from capsule image timestamps and magnetic manipulation logs and displayed on screen in real time. For the control group, the AI-assisted interface was turned off during routine operator examination, but the workstation still recorded the examination time.

After the MCCG examination, all patients were followed up for up to two weeks to confirm capsule excretion and any adverse events. Patients were asked to observe their stool regularly to confirm the excretion of the capsule. If they did not excrete the capsule after two weeks, radiography or a capsule locator was used to locate it.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the blind spot rate (number of uncompleted sites in each patient /12) in the AQCS and control groups. Secondary outcomes included: [1] consistency of cleanliness evaluation between AQCS and MCCG experts; [2] gastric examination time; [3] lesion detection rate; [4] incidence of adverse events. The MCCG expert with over 500 cases of experience in reading MCCG images reviewed all videos (with AI prompts blinded) post-examination and assessed the completion and cleanliness of 12 sites in each video. Based on the MCCG reports, identifying any lesion other than chronic gastritis in a patient was considered a positive result, regardless of the number of lesions detected. Furthermore, all adverse events were recorded, including those related to gastric preparation, the examination process, capsule retention and the others.

Sample size calculation

The trial was powered to demonstrate a difference in blind spot rate during MCCG with or without the assistance of the real-time AQCS. According to the previous pilot study and related literature reports [11], we set the blind spot rate as 10% in the AQCS group and 22% in control, and the standard deviation was 0.15. With a power of 90%, a two-sided significance level of 0.05, and a superiority margin of 0.05, 80 patients per group were required. Assuming 20% loss to follow-up, the sample size was 100 per group.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics and secondary outcomes were compared between AQCS and control groups using χ² tests for categorical variables and the two-sample t-tests for continuous variables. The primary outcome was assessed using the Mann-Whitney U test, with the requirement that the upper 95% confidence interval (CI) bound for the blind spot rate difference should be < − 5% to confirm AQCS superiority [11]. The bootstrapping method was used to estimate the 95% CI for median differences. Cohen’s kappa was used to evaluate cleanliness scoring consistency between AQCS and experts. The Clopper-Pearson method was used to calculate 95% CIs for proportions, and 95% limits for the relative risks were determined using Javastat (https://statpages.info/ctab2x2.html). The statistical significance threshold was set at a two-sided P < 0.05. All analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics version 25.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA).

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination plans of this study.

Results

The performance of the quality control system model

The specificity, sensitivity, and precision of Model I for each anatomical site ranged from 98.27% to 99.30%, 76.33% to 96.35%, and 75.29% to 94.25%, respectively. Among all gastric sites, the highest sensitivity of 96.35% was observed for the greater curvature of the gastric body. Conversely, the lowest sensitivity of 76.33% was noted for the anterior and posterior walls of the gastric body. The highest precision of 94.25% was recorded for the antrum, and the identification specificity for all sites was above 98% (Table 1, Additional file 1: Fig. S4).

Site identification model efficacy

For gastric cleanliness evaluation, Model E achieved specificity, sensitivity, and precision ranging from 92.85% to 98.03%, 72.64% to 97.56%, and 91.46% to 95.37%, respectively. Notably, the highest sensitivity of 97.56% was observed for cleanliness score four, while the lowest was for cleanliness score two at 72.64%. Overall, the recognition specificity and precision for all cleanliness scores were above 90% (Additional file 1: Table S1, Additional file 1: Fig. S5).

Study population and baseline characteristics in RCT

Between August 27, 2021, and July 28, 2022, 200 patients were randomly assigned to the AQCS or the control group. After excluding three patients who failed to swallow capsule, two patients with poor gastric preparation, and one with capsule retention in esophagus, the final analysis included 98 patients in the AQCS group and 96 patients in the control group. Primary outcome

The blind spot rate was significantly lower in the AQCS group compared with the control (0.00% vs. 16.67%, P < 0.01), and the median difference was − 16.67% (95% CI, − 16.67 to − 8.33). The higher 95% CI bound was less than − 5% of the difference for blind spot rate between the two groups (Table 3). The percentage of patients overlooked in five gastric sites was significantly lower in the AQCS group compared to the control group. These differences were noted in the cardia (0.00% vs. 15.63%, P < 0.01), the fundus (6.12% vs. 25.00%, P < 0.01), the anterior (1.02% vs. 14.58%, P < 0.01) and posterior (13.27% vs. 41.67%, P < 0.01) walls of the gastric angulus, and the lesser curvature of the antrum (2.04% vs. 29.17%, P < 0.01). In the AQCS group, no patients were overlooked in the cardia, the lesser curvature of the gastric body, or the anterior walls of the gastric body (Table 4).

Secondary outcomes

The Cohen’s kappa coefficient between AQCS and experts’ evaluations on cleanliness was 0.95 (95% CI, 0.94 to 0.97, P < 0.01), indicating strong consistency. Although the mean overall cleanliness scores showed no significant difference between the two groups (2.88 vs. 2.78, P = 0.06), the AQCS group had higher mean cleanliness scores in specific sites (Table 5). These sites included the anterior walls of the gastric angulus (2.94 vs. 2.75, P = 0.02), the posterior walls of the gastric angulus (2.88 vs. 2.69, P = 0.03), the lesser curvature of the antrum (3.21 vs. 2.99, P = 0.01), and the pylorus (3.71 vs. 3.38, P < 0.01). The two groups had no significant difference in the gastric examination time (28.63 min vs. 27.58 min, P = 0.48).

The lesion detection rate was higher in the AQCS group than in the control group (75.51% vs. 60.42%, P = 0.02). When stratified by lesion type, no significant differences were observed between the two groups for any specific category, including erosion/erythema, verrucous elevation, polyp, circumscribed mucosal abnormalities, ulcer, bleeding, or xanthoma. In addition, we evaluated the AI’s diagnostic accuracy for the 98 patients in the AQCS group, using expert readings as the gold standard. The overall sensitivity of AQCS for detecting gastric lesions was 95.86% (95% CI, 93.99% to 97.72%), and the sensitivities for detecting gastric inflammation/bleeding/ulcer and polyp/elevation were 97.44% (95% CI, 95.81% to 99.07%) and 93.53% (95% CI, 89.60% to 97.46%), respectively. No serious adverse events were reported in either group, but one adverse event occurred in the control group: capsule retention in the esophagus due to unexpected esophageal stenosis, which was retrieved endoscopically.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first multicenter RCT to evaluate the performance of AI in quality control for MCCG procedures. In this study, we established and validated an AQCS to monitor inspection completeness, evaluate gastric cleanliness, and detect suspicious lesions during MCCG. Both Model I and Model E exhibited robust specificity. The prospective RCT demonstrated that AQCS significantly reduced the blind spot rates, particularly in the gastric fundus and cardia, areas often overlooked. Additionally, AQCS showed a high consistency with expert evaluations of gastric cleanliness. Moreover, AQCS enhanced the detection of gastric lesions by combining quality control with lesion identification. This study underscores the utility of AQCS in real-world practice and provides a practical solution to the inherent challenges associated with MCCG.

MCCG has emerged as a noninvasive alternative to conventional gastroscopy, offering comparable diagnostic accuracy for gastric diseases, yet its effectiveness is highly operator-dependent. Based on our previous successful development and validation of automatic quality control systems for gastroscopy and colonoscopy [19, 20], we have now extended our efforts to MCCG. Although previous diagnostic studies have implemented AI-based anatomical recognition in MCCG [15], it has not been further validated to demonstrate its clinical value in reducing blind spots during MCCG examinations. Our model refines anatomical mapping by dividing the stomach into 12 distinct sites, with focused segmentation of regions prone to early cancer and intestinal metaplasia, including the gastric body, angulus, and antrum [21, 22]. Studies have also identified the gastric fundus and cardia areas frequently ignored during MCCG [7, 23]. In our research, operators can receive warnings from the AQCS interface to supplement inspections, significantly reducing the percentage of patients overlooked in these areas. Besides, mucosal folds can obscure lesions, increasing the difficulty of adequate examination. When the AQCS detects many images of “unstretched mucosa,” operators will guide the patient to change position or drink water to ensure a complete examination. Gastric cancer occurs in every part of the gastric cavity. Observing the whole stomach is a seminal prerequisite for MCCG examination. In China, the majority of small (< 2 cm) gastric cancers are concentrated on the lesser curvature, mainly in the fundus and antrum [22]. However, we found that 25.00% and 29.17% of patients were overlooked in the control group in the fundus and the lesser curvature of the antrum, respectively. In the AQCS group, patients with overlooked fundus and lesser curvature of the antrum decreased to 6.12% and 2.04%, respectively. This indicates that AQCS may increase the detection of early gastric cancers or precancerous lesions, thereby improving patients’ health outcomes.

Sufficient gastric cleansing is one of the most critical factors affecting the interpretation and reliability of endoscopic findings. As MCCG relies on retrospective video image interpretation, it depends more on gastric preparation quality than conventional endoscopy. In clinical practice, gastric mucus, bubbles, and other residual debris obscure mucosal visualization and impair gastric cleanliness [24, 25]. Indeed, effective gastric preparation for MCCG has demonstrated a significant improvement in the gastric cleanliness and visualization [26]. Studies have shown the value of AI in assessing bowel preparation during colonoscopy and small bowel capsule endoscopy, but it has not yet been used to evaluate gastric cleanliness during MCCG [20, 27]. In clinical practice, each patient underwent a standard gastric preparation protocol, and gastric cleanliness was assessed using the four-point scale [10]. We found that the AQCS’s evaluation of gastric cleanliness was highly consistent with expert assessments (kappa = 0.95, P < 0.01). The AQCS alerts operators to focus on areas with poor cleanliness and facilitates real-time interventions, such as changing the patient’s position or repeating water intake. Consequently, the AQCS group demonstrated higher mean cleanliness scores in four sites, including the anterior and posterior walls of the gastric angulus, the lesser curvature of the antrum, and the pylorus. This enhancement in cleanliness scores may promoted comprehensive observation of anatomical sites and improved lesion detection. Furthermore, continuous feedback from the AQCS might refine operator performance, and higher cleanliness scores could bolster the credibility of an endoscopy center’s MCCG reports.

Examination time is also an important indicator of MCCG performance. The over-pursuit of inspection completeness and mucosal cleanliness may increase examination time, reducing patient compliance and affecting subsequent bowel inspection. Yet in our study, AQCS reduced the blind spot rate without significantly increasing gastric examination time (28.63 min vs. 27.58 min, P = 0.48). Possible reasons may be that, with real-time prompting, AQCS can help the operator locate the site, reducing the repeated inspections for the completed sites. Moreover, conventional gastroscopy screening requires a minimum of seven minutes [21], whereas few guidelines specify the time for MCCG screening examinations. AQCS accurately records the examination time, providing a valuable reference for quality control in the endoscopy center.

Studies indicated that MCCG achieved an acceptable overall sensitivity of 87% in detecting gastric lesions. The detection values ranged between 82% for gastric ulcers and polyps to 95% for gastric erosions [3]. In this study, all types of gastritis were defined as negative results because almost all types of gastritis are diffuse changes, and diagnosing these diseases was not a challenge for MCCG [2]. AQCS’s capability of lesion detection allows operators to focus on them immediately, and scrutinize these areas from distant, close-up, and multiangles. Consequently, the resulting lesion images are more comprehensive, facilitating a more accurate description of the lesion and supporting subsequent diagnostic and therapeutic decisions. Finally, the AQCS group demonstrated a higher lesion detection rate (75.51% vs. 60.42%, P = 0.02), which might be attributed to the system’s ability to reduce blind spots, improve gastric cleanliness, and capture more images of suspicious lesions.

Our study has several limitations. First, Hawthorne bias still might exist due to the inability to blind the operators to the patient assignment. Specifically, operators in the AQCS group may be more attentive during the procedure. This heightened attentiveness could potentially lead to an overestimation of the system’s effectiveness in reducing blind spots and increasing lesion detection. Second, our model’s fewer gastric sites than the gastroscopy models may still result in potential blind spots. However, the wide-angle imaging of 105° in MCCG can make it challenging to precisely categorize the gastric sites, leading to confusion and overlap in the test set images. Third, our primary outcome does not focus on early cancer detection rates. Future studies with larger sample sizes from high-risk gastric cancer populations, incorporating pathological follow-up, are needed to fully assess the effectiveness of AQCS in enhancing early cancer detection. Currently, MCCG is primarily used for detecting malignant and premalignant gastric lesions, which are more prevalent in Asia. As biopsy function is integrated, costs are progressively reduced, and efficacy for screening conditions such as Barrett’s esophagus is demonstrated, broader adoption of MCCG within Western healthcare systems is anticipated in the long term [28, 29].

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study developed an AQCS that integrates real-time procedural quality control with automated lesion detection to standardize MCCG examinations. The prospective randomized trial demonstrated that AQCS can effectively reduce blind spots, minimize critical area omission, provide objective cleanliness scoring, and improve gastric lesion detection. These results position AQCS as a valuable auxiliary tool to mitigate operator-dependent variability and enhance the overall quality of routine MCCG examinations.
