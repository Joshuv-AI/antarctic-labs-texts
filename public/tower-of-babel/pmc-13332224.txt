The impact of electric heating cape on local thermal sensation and thermal comfort of officers
Authors: Yuxi Wang, Meng Wei, Shangjie Lin, Wei Mao, Xi Meng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study focuses on evaluating the thermoregulatory performance of a graphene-based electric heating cape and determining its optimal temperature for use in cool indoor environments during winter. Through controlled experiments with 30 participants in a climate chamber maintained at 15.5 °C, five heating conditions (no heating, 35 °C, 40 °C, 45 °C, and 50 °C) were systematically tested. Skin temperature and heart rate were continuously monitored, and subjective thermal sensation and comfort votes were collected using 7-point scales. Results demonstrated that the electric heating cape significantly improved thermal comfort, with 40 °C identified as the optimal temperature setpoint: overall thermal sensation shifted from slightly cool to slightly warm, and overall thermal comfort state attained ‘slightly comfortable’ level on the adaptive comfort scale. Repeated-measures ANOVA revealed that heating temperature had a highly significant effect on both overall thermal sensation and overall thermal comfort. Post-hoc tests identified 40 °C as the optimal temperature setpoint under these environmental conditions (15.5 °C). At this temperature, the overall thermal sensation vote improved significantly from − 1.23 (no heating) to -0.13 (p < 0.01), approaching a neutral sensation, while overall thermal comfort increased significantly from − 1.08 to 0.35, reaching a “slightly comfortable” level. The most significant improvements were observed in the abdomen and lumbar regions. While skin temperature showed a positive correlation with thermal perception, heart rate remained stable (± 5 bpm), indicating a low physiological burden. Marked individual differences in temperature preference underscore the importance of personalized thermal regulation. This study provides empirical evidence to guide the application and control of localized heating devices in cool indoor office settings during winter.

Introduction

Energy and environment are two major challenges faced by mankind1,2. The effectiveness of personalized heating solutions and the determination of optimal operating temperatures have become increasingly important in office environments. While traditional centralized heating systems create uniform thermal conditions, they often fail to address individual comfort requirements. In modern open-plan offices with high occupant density and prolonged occupancy periods, standardized thermal comfort indices prove inadequate. Under identical thermostat settings, rates of local thermal dissatisfaction can vary by up to 40% between workstations, and documented thermal discomfort has been shown to reduce work performance by 6–8%. This highlights the critical need for targeted thermal interventions that can accommodate personal comfort preferences.

Conventional heating systems operate on a whole-space principle, uniformly conditioning entire building volumes. This approach demonstrates several limitations: it consumes substantial energy, responds slowly to changing conditions, cannot adapt to individual or zonal variations, and frequently creates uneven thermal environments. Particularly in open-plan offices, uniform supply temperatures often generate conflicting thermal sensations, resulting in simultaneous hot and cold complaints from different occupants. These challenges underscore the importance of developing more responsive and personalized heating solutions that can deliver precise thermal comfort where needed.

Personal Comfort Systems (PCSs) have emerged as a promising solution to address these challenges3. Unlike conventional HVAC systems, PCSs provide targeted heating or cooling directly to occupants, enabling personalized thermal regulation without conditioning unoccupied spaces4,5. Recent systematic reviews indicate that PCSs can improve thermal satisfaction while allowing ambient temperature setpoints to be extended by 2–6 °C, yielding substantial energy savings6,7.

The theoretical basis for PCS effectiveness lies in the selective heating of thermally sensitive body regions. The torso including the chest, abdomen, and back is one of the body regions with the largest heat production and blood perfusion under sedentary office activity8,9. Therefore, it presents significant potential for localized thermal intervention10. Studies have demonstrated that local heating of torso regions can significantly improve overall thermal comfort in cool environments, with different torso sub-regions exhibiting varying warming effectiveness11. This suggests that the torso’s thermal response is not uniform and warrants detailed investigation of sub-regional heating effects.

A wide variety of PCS devices have been developed, ranging from heated seats and foot warmers to desk-mounted radiant panels12–14. Among these, heated seats and foot warmers have received considerable research attention, with studies quantifying their effects on thermal comfort and energy savings15,16. However, systematic investigations of optimal operating parameters for different PCS form factors remain limited. As noted in recent reviews, there is insufficient documentation regarding how specific device characteristics influence user experience and comfort outcomes17.

Against this background, localized heating devices that provide controllable warmth to specific body areas have become an effective solution for personalized thermal management, attracting widespread attention from both academia and engineering fields1,2. The theoretical basis lies in the fact that selective heating of thermally sensitive areas of the human body can improve overall thermal comfort without raising the temperature of the entire space, thereby achieving significant energy savings3,4. A series of studies have validated the effectiveness of this approach. For example, Vesely et al.5 pointed out through a systematic review that personal comfort systems have significant energy-saving potential in non-uniform thermal environments; Zhang et al.6 experimentally demonstrated that using heated seats could improve subjects’ overall thermal comfort by 0.7 scale units and allow the ambient temperature setpoint to be reduced by 2 °C. Liu et al.7 developed an intelligent local heating system based on real-time thermal comfort feedback, which can increase upper body skin temperature by 1.6–1.8 °C, with the most significant warming observed in the waist and abdominal areas. These core areas, being close to major organs and blood vessels, play a key role in influencing overall thermal perception when heated8,9.

The electric heating cape represents a particularly underexplored PCS category. While seated heating devices have been extensively studied, the cape form—which warms the upper torso including shoulders, back, and abdomen—offers unique advantages: it directly targets core body regions with high thermosensitivity, allows continuous heating while users move freely, and aligns with natural human behaviors of draping garments for warmth18. Moreover, wearable heating systems offer distinct advantages for office scenarios: they maintain direct contact with the body for efficient conductive heat transfer and do not occupy desk space19.

The development of local heating technology is closely related to advances in electrothermal materials. In recent years, important breakthroughs have been made in the research of flexible and efficient heating elements. Wang et al.10 improved the electro-thermal conversion efficiency of graphene electrothermal films by 12% by optimizing their microstructure. This research is in line with explorations of new nanomaterials such as carbon nanotubes11 and silver nanowires12, which, with their rapid thermal response, excellent flexibility, and low power consumption, have become ideal choices for wearable personal comfort systems. Recent advances in e-textiles and flexible heating materials have enabled the development of lightweight, thin, and conformable heating garments that can be worn unobtrusively under regular clothing20,21. In terms of control systems, early devices mostly used simple user-set modes13, while current research focus has shifted to closed-loop control strategies based on physiological signals, such as automatically adjusting heating intensity through parameters like skin temperature14,15 or heart rate variability16.

The application value of local heating has gone beyond basic comfort needs, with studies showing its positive impact on cognitive performance. Research by Amin et al.17 and Chen et al.18 indicates that using personalized heating devices in cold office environments can mitigate the negative impact of cold stress on work efficiency, particularly in tasks requiring high concentration. Li et al.19 found that using foot warmers could reduce typing errors by 6–8% among officers. Additionally, the psychological mechanisms of thermal comfort are increasingly receiving attention. Studies by Zhang and Arens20 and Melikov21 emphasize that giving individuals autonomous control over their micro-environment can significantly improve their acceptance of environmental conditions, even when the actual thermal environment is not entirely within the theoretical neutral range.

Although significant progress has been made in research, critical gaps remain unresolved. Existing literature has predominantly focused on establishing the overall effectiveness of PCSs22 or comparing different device types23, rather than systematically evaluating optimal operating parameters for specific PCS form factors under standardized office conditions24. Particularly noteworthy is that the cape, as one of the oldest warming methods in human history, remains highly valuable due to its unique physical characteristic: efficient warmth can be achieved by merely covering the core shoulder and back areas. Modern electric heating capes inherit this design wisdom and demonstrate significant advantages through technological innovation. Unlike fixed-seat heating devices, the cape form allows users to move freely in office environments while maintaining continuous heating to core body areas. This flexibility makes it particularly suitable for modern dynamic office requirements. However, comprehensive studies that integrate subjective evaluations with objective physiological parameters to systematically assess the thermal comfort of electric heating capes remain scarce25,26. More importantly, the quantitative relationship between specific heating setpoints and local thermal comfort has not been clearly established, which severely restricts the optimization and intelligent control of new-generation localized heating devices27.

To bridge these knowledge gaps, this study employs a combined approach of controlled climate-chamber experiments and subjective questionnaires to systematically evaluate a temperature-regulated electric heating cape. The specific innovations of this work are threefold: first, it provides a detailed assessment of local thermal sensation and comfort across multiple body segments (cheek, arm, abdomen, lumbar, and posterior thigh) under varying heating temperatures; second, it quantitatively examines the effects of heating intensity on comfort through multiple controlled scenarios; and third, it integrates objective physiological indicators with subjective evaluations to establish a comprehensive assessment framework. The findings are expected to offer a scientific basis for optimizing the design and intelligent control of localized heating devices, particularly the flexible electric heating cape, to better align with human thermoregulatory needs and modern work patterns.

Methodology

The systematic research framework established in this study is illustrated in Fig. 1, which delineates the experimental and analytical process into three interconnected phases. The first phase, designated as data collection and experimental procedure, involved the synchronized measurement of environmental parameters, objective physiological signals, and subjective perceptions. Meteorological variables included air temperature, relative humidity, air velocity, and globe temperature, while physiological monitoring focused on skin temperature and heart rate. Simultaneously, subjective responses regarding thermal sensation and comfort were gathered through standardized questionnaires to provide a holistic view of the participants’ thermal state.

Fig. 1Technical flowchart of the research methodology.

Technical flowchart of the research methodology.

The second phase addressed data analysis and case configuration, wherein five controlled experimental scenarios were systematically evaluated, ranging from a no-heating baseline to a 50 °C setpoint. During this stage, the investigation focused on two primary physiological indicators recorded across five specific body segments, including the cheek, arm, abdomen, lumbar, and thighs, to capture the localized thermal impact of the graphene-based heating cape. In the final phase, the experimental results were integrated through a comprehensive evaluation of thermal sensation and comfort trends. This stage utilized correlation analysis between mean skin temperature and subjective votes to establish the quantitative relationship between physiological stimulus and psychological response, thereby identifying the optimal thermal regulation performance of the system.

Compared to conventional centralized heating or traditional localized devices such as electric blankets and heated seats, the graphene-based electric heating cape examined in this study offers superior flexibility, rapid thermal response, and high electro-thermal conversion efficiency. By delivering targeted conductive heating to core body regions, this wearable solution enables precise, personalized thermal regulation while maintaining lower power consumption than radiant alternatives. This human-centered approach provides a robust scientific basis for developing intelligent, adaptive control algorithms for personalized thermal environments in modern office settings.

Experimental platform and procedure

This study investigates the effects of different heating setpoints on the local thermal sensation and comfort of indoor officers. As shown in Fig. 1(a), the experiments were carried out in December 2024 at the Binhai Center Laboratory of Qingdao University of Technology, Shandong, China. The average ambient temperature was 15.5 ± 0.9 °C, air velocity was less than 0.08 m/s, and relative humidity was 34.7%±3%. These parameters ensured environmental stability and minimized radiative effects on thermal sensation. The electric heating cape utilized graphene as the heating material, known for its excellent electrical and thermal conductivity, enabling rapid and uniform heat conversion.

The experiment was conducted from 6 December 2024 to 28 December 2024 and was split into two daily sessions: morning (08:00–12:30) and afternoon (13:30–18:00). Thirty participants were recruited and randomly assigned to different test orders; five subjects entered the climate chamber simultaneously for each run. This study employed a within-subject repeated-measures design, in which each participant experienced all five experimental conditions: no heating, 35 °C, 40 °C, 45 °C, and 50 °C. To control for potential order effects, a Latin square design was implemented to counterbalance the sequence of exposure to the five heating conditions. Participants were randomly assigned to one of five sequences, ensuring that each condition appeared equally often in each position of the experimental order. Experimental instruments

To ensure the accuracy and reliability of physiological and environmental measurements during the experiment, all instruments were calibrated before the experiment. Skin temperature was measured using Pyrobutton sensors (Model: DS1922L, Maxim Integrated, USA) with an accuracy of ± 0.5 °C within the range of 15–45 °C. Heart rate was monitored using a Polar H10 chest strap (Polar Electro, Finland) with a sampling frequency of 1 Hz. Environmental parameters were measured using a calibrated thermal microclimate station (Model: HD32.3, Delta Ohm, Italy) with the following accuracies: air temperature ± 0.1 °C, relative humidity ± 2%, air velocity ± 0.05 m/s. Power consumption of the heating cape was recorded using a digital power meter (Model: PM9812, accuracy: ±0.1 W).

Evaluation indicators

To study the effects of different heating temperatures on human thermal sensation and comfort, a personalized local heating model was designed. The temperature-regulated electric heating cape provided localized heating to the upper body. During the experiments, participants’ skin temperature was measured using Pyrobutton sensors at 30-second intervals. Heart rate was monitored to assess physiological responses to the thermal environment. Figure 3 shows the five measurement points: left cheek, right upper arm, left lumber, left abdomen, and posterior thigh. Note that for areas directly heated, measurement points were located outside the heated zones to evaluate indirect effects. Both subjective perceptions and objective physiological indicators were recorded under heating cases.

Anthropometric point and questionnaire information.

The questionnaire used in this study included overall and local thermal evaluations. Overall thermal evaluation covered three aspects: thermal sensation, thermal comfort. Given that localized heating might induce stronger local thermal sensations, a 7-point scale from − 3 to + 3 was used for thermal sensation. Additionally, since enhanced local heating might alleviate overall thermal discomfort and induce local thermal pleasure, the thermal comfort scale was expanded from the traditional − 3 (very uncomfortable) to + 3 (very comfortable), with added positive levels to accommodate potential pleasure. Detailed explanations of each scale were provided in the questionnaire appendix to ensure consistent understanding among all participants.

Participants

The study involved 30 healthy Chinese graduate and undergraduate students (15 males and 15 females), aged 18–25 years. Based on body mass index (BMI), 59% of participants were within the normal range (18.5 < BMI < 23.9), and 41% were overweight (BMI > 24). All participants provided written informed consent and were confirmed to have normal body temperature before the experiments. To ensure accuracy, participants were instructed to avoid caffeine, alcohol, and strenuous physical activity prior to the experiments. Female participants were scheduled outside their menstrual periods to minimize physiological influences. During the experiments, all participants wore standardized clothing with a total thermal insulation of 0.60 Clo. Low-metabolic activities such as reading or using a laptop were permitted, with an estimated metabolic rate of 1.1 Met. The experimental procedures were in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and were approved by the Ethics Committee of Qingdao University of Technology (Approval Number: QUT-HEC-2025012), and informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Results and analysis

This section presents a comprehensive analysis of the experimental results, encompassing both objective physiological data and subjective perceptual evaluations. The findings are structured to first examine the variations in key physiological parameters - skin temperature and heart rate - across the different experimental cases. Subsequently, a detailed presentation and analysis of the subjective questionnaire data, including overall and local thermal sensation votes (TSV) as well as thermal comfort votes, are provided. Correlation analyses between physiological indicators and subjective perceptions are also conducted to elucidate the underlying relationships. This multi-faceted approach offers a holistic understanding of the electric heating cape’s performance and its impact on officers’ thermal experience.

Skin temperature

The trends in mean skin temperature under different experimental cases are shown in Fig. 4. Mean skin temperature over time under different heating conditions. Data points represent the group mean skin temperature at each 5 min interval during the 40 min localized heating phase. Error bars indicate standard deviations (SD). Under constant metabolic rates and heating modes, participants’ mean skin temperature increased with rising heating temperatures. During the first 40 min of localized heating, skin temperature rose rapidly in the face, arm, lumber, and abdomen, while the thigh showed a slower increase.

The heated groups exhibited higher mean skin temperatures than the non-heated group. Across the five cases, the maximum increases in mean skin temperature compared to no heating were 3.1 °C (cheek), 1.7 °C (arm), 1.6 °C (abdomen), 1.5 °C (lumber), and 1.2 °C (thigh). As the heating temperature increased, the rise in mean skin temperature became more pronounced, indicating enhanced effectiveness of the electric heating cape at higher temperatures.

For directly heated regions (arm, lumber, abdomen), local skin temperature increases were more substantial. Without heating, the local mean skin temperature of the arm was 32.2 °C; with heating, it rose to 33.9 °C, an increase of 1.7 °C. The lumber showed a 1.4 °C increase, and the abdominal skin temperature rose from 32.1 °C to 33.7 °C, an increase of 1.6 °C. Notably, non-directly heated areas such as the cheek also exhibited a marked rise in skin temperature with increasing heating setpoints. This phenomenon suggests that localized torso heating may enhance systemic circulation, facilitating heat transfer via blood flow to distal body parts, thereby contributing to an improved overall thermal sensation.

Heart rate

Heart rates under different experimental cases are shown in Fig. 5. Across cases, heart rates ranged between 69 and 82 bpm, 72–84 bpm, 70–87 bpm, 71–90 bpm, and 74–84 bpm. Analysis revealed that heating temperature and the electric heating cape had no significant effect on heart rate. In case 1, heart rate fluctuated slightly around 85–89 bpm, primarily influenced by basal heart rate and minor environmental factors. case 2 showed mild fluctuations with a slight downward trend, reaching a minimum near 77 bpm. case 3 exhibited mild fluctuations with a slight upward trend, peaking near 87 bpm. case 4 had larger fluctuations with a slight downward trend, dipping to 83 bpm. case 5 also showed larger fluctuations with a slight upward trend, reaching 84 bpm. Overall, the electric heating cape had minimal impact on average heart rate across cases, with variations within ± 3–5 bpm. This stability indicates that the thermal load imposed by the heating cape, even at 50 °C, was insufficient to trigger significant cardiovascular adjustments mediated by the autonomic nervous system, suggesting a low physiological stress response.

Overall thermal sensation

Overall Thermal Sensation Vote serves as a primary psychophysical metric for quantifying the subjective thermal experience of subjects across systematically varied heating conditions. By capturing real-time shifts in perceived warmth along the ASHRAE 7-point scale, TSV provides granular insight into how localized radiant heating modulates whole-body thermal perception — a prerequisite for evidence-based optimization of indoor thermal environments.

As shown in Fig. 6, the experimental protocol comprised three temporally distinct phases: a 10-minute baseline period without heating, a 30-minute active heating phase from 10 to 40 min at fixed setpoint temperatures of 35 °C, 40 °C, 45 °C, and 50 °C, and a concluding 10-minute autonomous adjustment phase during which subjects exercised volitional control over heating intensity. To examine the effect of heating condition on TSV, a one-way repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted with heating condition as the within-subject factor. Where the assumption of sphericity was violated, Greenhouse-Geisser corrections were applied. Significant main effects were followed up with pairwise post-hoc comparisons using Bonferroni correction to control for familywise error rate across multiple comparisons.

Under unheated conditions, subjects reported a mean TSV of − 1.23, corresponding to slightly cool on the ASHRAE scale with a standard deviation of 0.78, establishing a clear thermal deficit at baseline. The elevated dispersion reflects substantial inter-individual variability in cold sensitivity under ambient conditions.

Introducing heating at 35 °C elevated the mean TSV to − 0.40 with a standard deviation of 0.65. Post-hoc comparison confirmed that this improvement was statistically significant relative to the no-heating baseline at p < 0.001, indicating that even moderate localized heating produced a reliably detectable shift in perceived thermal sensation. The effect magnitude, however, remained modest — subjects’ TSV stayed on the cool side of neutral, and the reduction in standard deviation from 0.78 to 0.65 suggests only partial inter-subject convergence.

At 40 °C, the mean TSV reached − 0.13 with a standard deviation of 0.54, approaching thermal neutrality. The improvement relative to the no-heating baseline remained highly significant at p < 0.001. Critically, the pairwise comparison between 35 °C and 40 °C did not reach statistical significance at p > 0.05, despite a 0.27-unit numerical increase in mean TSV. This non-significant result suggests that within the low-warmth range of the perceptual scale, a 5 °C increment in setpoint temperature is insufficient to produce a statistically reliable change in subjective thermal sensation — consistent with the compressed psychophysical sensitivity often observed near the neutral zone of thermal perception. The standard deviation at 40 °C was the lowest across all conditions at 0.54, reflecting the highest degree of inter-subject consensus.

Under the 45 °C condition, the mean TSV rose to + 0.23 with a standard deviation of 0.48, crossing the neutral threshold into the positive warmth domain. While the comparison with the no-heating baseline remained highly significant at p < 0.001, the pairwise test between 40 °C and 45 °C again failed to reach significance at p > 0.05. The null result across two consecutive 5 °C increments points to a perceptual plateau in this mid-range heating zone: despite a cumulative 10 °C increase in setpoint, the corresponding TSV shift of approximately 0.36 units fell below the threshold of statistical detectability, likely reflecting the insensitivity of verbal rating scales to incremental thermal stimuli near neutrality. The continued compression of standard deviation to 0.48 further reinforces the interpretation that subjects converged on a shared near-neutral percept regardless of which of these two setpoints was applied.

The highest setpoint of 50 °C produced a mean TSV of + 0.35 with a standard deviation of 0.61. The comparison with the no-heating baseline remained highly significant at p < 0.001. The pairwise contrast between 45 °C and 50 °C likewise did not attain statistical significance at p > 0.05, extending the plateau pattern observed across the 35–50 °C range. Across three consecutive pairwise comparisons — 35 °C vs. 40 °C, 40 °C vs. 45 °C, and 45 °C vs. 50 °C — none reached significance, suggesting that the entire 35–50 °C heating range, while perceptually distinguishable from no heating, does not produce statistically separable increments in TSV between adjacent setpoints. The slight rebound in standard deviation to 0.61, compared to 0.48 at 45 °C, signals the re-emergence of inter-individual divergence at the upper end of the heating range, consistent with the onset of over-heating discomfort in a subset of subjects — corroborated by behavioral evidence of volitional temperature reduction at the 50-minute mark in Fig. 4.

The repeated-measures framework revealed a clear and consistent statistical structure: all heated conditions differed significantly from the no-heating baseline at p < 0.001, confirming the unambiguous thermophysiological efficacy of localized radiant heating across the full 35–50 °C range. However, no adjacent pairwise comparison between heating setpoints reached statistical significance, indicating that 5 °C increments within this range lie below the perceptual resolution of the TSV instrument. This pattern of significant between-extremes differences combined with non-significant adjacent-step differences is characteristic of a monotonic but psychophysically compressed dose-response relationship — one in which cumulative heating produces meaningful comfort improvement, but fine-grained setpoint differences are not reliably registered by subjects on a discrete verbal rating scale. These findings underscore the importance of distinguishing statistical significance from practical significance in thermal comfort research: a 15 °C total range in setpoint temperature translated into a TSV shift of only approximately 1.58 units, highlighting the limited dynamic range of verbal thermal sensation ratings under localized heating paradigms.

Local thermal sensation

Local Thermal Sensation Vote serves as a critical metric for deciphering the granular impact of the thermal environment on specific anatomical regions. Unlike whole-body thermal sensation, which aggregates subjective warmth perception into a single scalar index, local TSV resolves the spatial heterogeneity of thermal experience across distinct body segments — information that is indispensable for optimizing the design and operational control of localized heating systems. To examine regional differences in thermal response, a one-way repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted separately for each body segment, with Bonferroni-corrected post-hoc comparisons applied where significant main effects were detected.

As illustrated in Fig. 7, the graphene-based heating cape produced variable improvements across all monitored body segments as setpoint temperature increased, with the magnitude of improvement differing substantially by anatomical region and proximity to the heating source.

The abdomen and lumbar regions exhibited the most pronounced shifts in local thermal sensation. The abdomen’s mean rating improved from − 1.20 under no-heating conditions to + 0.78 at the highest setpoint, a total shift of 1.98 scale units, while the lumbar region rose comparably from − 1.15 to + 0.82. Post-hoc comparisons confirmed statistically significant improvements in both regions beginning at 35 °C relative to baseline, with further significant gains at higher setpoints. These findings reflect the high thermoreceptor density, proximity to visceral organs, and direct contact with the heating element that characterize these core regions, collectively accounting for their superior thermal responsiveness.

The upper limbs and facial cheeks demonstrated notable but comparatively attenuated improvements. The arm improved from − 1.02 to + 0.56 across the heating range, reaching statistical significance relative to baseline from 40 °C onward, while the cheek improved more gradually from − 0.89 to + 0.24, with significant changes emerging only at higher setpoints. The warming of the cheek — which receives no direct conductive heat — is attributable to systemic vasodilatory responses triggered by torso heating, which enhance peripheral blood flow at sites remote from the heating element.

In contrast, the thighs showed the most limited improvement, with mean TSV rising from − 1.25 to only − 0.45 — a shift of 0.80 units that left thigh sensation on the cool side of neutral even at 50 °C. Thigh TSV did not reach statistical significance relative to baseline until the highest setpoint conditions, and remained significantly smaller in magnitude than the abdomen and lumbar regions across all pairwise comparisons, reflecting the physiological lag in heat transfer to the lower extremities and their greater anatomical distance from the heating source.

These findings reveal a clear hierarchy of regional thermal response: core regions benefit most substantially, upper-body segments show intermediate gains, and distal lower-body segments remain the least responsive. This spatial gradient provides empirical support for a torso-focused heating strategy as the most efficient approach for improving whole-body thermal comfort in cool indoor environments, while identifying the lower extremities as a residual thermal deficit zone that may warrant supplementary heating in more demanding cold exposure scenarios.

Overall thermal comfort

Overall Thermal Comfort Vote serves as the core indicator for evaluating the impact of the thermal environment on whole-body subjective comfort. Unlike thermal sensation, which captures the directional perception of warmth or coolness, thermal comfort reflects the affective dimension of the thermal experience — the degree to which subjects find their current thermal state acceptable or desirable. Examining how this indicator responds to systematically varied heating conditions thus provides a complementary and functionally distinct lens through which to assess the efficacy of localized radiant heating, and offers a scientific basis for the development of personalized thermal regulation technologies.

To examine the effect of heating condition on Overall Thermal Comfort Vote, a one-way repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted with heating condition as the within-subject factor. Where the assumption of sphericity was violated, Greenhouse-Geisser corrections were applied. Significant main effects were followed up with pairwise post-hoc comparisons using Bonferroni correction to control for familywise error rate across multiple comparisons.

As illustrated in Fig. 8, overall thermal comfort exhibited a non-monotonic trajectory across heating conditions — rising from discomfort at baseline to peak comfort at 40 °C, then declining progressively at higher setpoints. This inverted-U pattern distinguishes the comfort response from the monotonically increasing TSV profile, and reflects the existence of an upper comfort boundary beyond which additional heating becomes counterproductive.

Under unheated conditions, subjects reported a mean thermal comfort value of − 1.08, corresponding to slightly uncomfortable with a standard deviation of 0.68, establishing a clear comfort deficit at baseline. The relatively elevated dispersion reflects inter-individual variability in cold-related discomfort sensitivity. As the unheated phase progressed, the proportion of subjects reporting discomfort increased monotonically, underscoring the urgency of thermal remediation in low-temperature environments.

Introducing heating at 35 °C produced a marked improvement, with the mean thermal comfort value rising to − 0.05 with a standard deviation of 0.57, approaching the neutral point of the comfort scale. Post-hoc comparison confirmed that this improvement was statistically significant relative to the no-heating baseline at p = 0.01, indicating that moderate localized heating was sufficient to reliably shift subjects’ affective thermal state from discomfort toward neutrality. The reduction in standard deviation from 0.68 to 0.57 suggests a degree of inter-subject convergence around the comfort threshold, though individual responses remained moderately variable.

At 40 °C, the mean thermal comfort value reached its peak across all conditions at + 0.35 with a standard deviation of 0.49, entering the slightly comfortable range. This represented the most favorable comfort outcome observed throughout the experiment, and the lowest within-condition dispersion, indicating the strongest inter-subject consensus. The improvement relative to the no-heating baseline was highly significant at p < 0.001. Notably, during the autonomous adjustment phase at the 50-minute mark, a subset of subjects elected to slightly increase the heating temperature, resulting in a marginal subsequent decline in reported comfort — a behavioral signal that even at the comfort peak, individual thermal preferences were not fully homogeneous.

Under the 45 °C condition, the mean thermal comfort value declined to − 0.23 with a standard deviation of 0.55, falling back below the neutral threshold into the slightly uncomfortable range. Post-hoc comparison confirmed that this decrease relative to the 40 °C peak was statistically significant at p = 0.008, providing strong evidence that 45 °C exceeds the upper comfort boundary for the sample as a whole. The widening standard deviation relative to 40 °C signals the re-emergence of inter-individual divergence, consistent with a subset of subjects experiencing thermal discomfort while others remained near-neutral — a pattern indicative of heterogeneous upper comfort thresholds across individuals.

At the highest setpoint of 50 °C, the mean thermal comfort value declined further to − 0.42 with a standard deviation of 0.60. The pairwise comparison with the 40 °C peak remained statistically significant at p = 0.002, confirming that the comfort degradation was not incidental but reflected a systematic over-heating effect. The continued increase in standard deviation to 0.60 reinforces the interpretation that thermal discomfort at high setpoints was unevenly distributed across subjects, with individual differences in heat tolerance playing an increasingly prominent role as setpoint temperatures rose above the comfort optimum.

Taken together, the statistical evidence reveals a well-defined comfort optimum centered at 40 °C, flanked by a comfort deficit at lower temperatures and a statistically confirmed comfort degradation at higher ones. The significant contrasts between 40 °C and both 45 °C and 50 °C establish that the decline in comfort above the optimum was not attributable to sampling variability but reflected a genuine psychophysiological response to thermal overload. In contrast to the TSV results — where no adjacent pairwise comparison between heating setpoints reached significance — the thermal comfort data exhibit sharper statistical boundaries, particularly on the descending limb of the comfort curve. This divergence between the two metrics highlights that sensation and comfort, while correlated, are governed by distinct psychophysical mechanisms: subjects may register incremental increases in warmth without translating those perceptions into improved — or indeed worsened — comfort until a threshold is crossed. These findings provide a quantitative foundation for the calibration of intelligent temperature control systems, and underscore the practical value of setpoint optimization over broad-range heating in personalized thermal environment design.

Discussion

Figure 10 presents the results of the correlation analysis between mean skin temperature and overall thermal sensation under different heating cases. By comparing the scenarios under different heating temperatures with Case 1, the effect of heating on thermal sensation can be more clearly evaluated. Under all heating cases, mean skin temperature and overall thermal sensation showed a positive correlation, indicating that an increase in skin temperature enhances thermal sensation. However, the strength and sensitivity of the correlation varied significantly under different heating cases.

As a key physiological input for human thermal perception, skin temperature demonstrated a positive correlation with overall thermal sensation across all heating conditions, which aligns with fundamental principles of thermophysiology. However, the strength of this correlation, as indicated by R² values, exhibited a general decreasing trend with rising heating setpoints, dropping from 0.83 at 35 °C to 0.59 at 50 °C. This overall trend suggests that the relationship between skin temperature and thermal sensation is not static but is modulated by the heating intensity itself. This observation can be better understood through two physiological mechanisms: first, when local skin temperature enters the warm range (> 34–35 °C), the firing rate of thermoreceptors tends to saturate or adapt, leading to reduced sensitivity to further temperature increases; second, at higher heating intensities, subjective thermal sensation may be increasingly influenced by other factors such as thermal uniformity, the onset of mild sweating, or even psychological expectations, which are not captured by mean skin temperature alone. Therefore, while skin temperature remains a reliable indicator of thermal state at lower heating levels, its predictive power for thermal sensation diminishes at higher intensities, highlighting the multi-faceted nature of thermal comfort under localized heating. Physiological effects: All PCS forms examined increased local skin temperature at the heated sites, with foot warmers showing the largest local increases19. A distinctive finding of this study is that the heating cape produced measurable skin temperature increases in non-heated areas, suggesting systemic heat distribution via enhanced circulation. This whole-body effect may contribute to the superior comfort improvement observed. Heart rate remained stable across all conditions (± 5 bpm), consistent with findings from heated seat studies4, indicating that localized heating imposes minimal cardiovascular load regardless of the heating form.

Energy-saving potential: The electric heating cape consumed 21.2 Wh over 40 min at 40 °C, equivalent to approximately 32 W average power. This is substantially lower than typical radiant panels (100–200 W)6 and comparable to or lower than heated seats (40–60 W)4 and foot warmers (20–50 W)19. When combined with the ability to lower ambient temperature setpoints, the cape offers significant energy-saving potential. Assuming an 8-hour workday and a 3 °C setpoint reduction, the annual energy savings per workstation could reach 200–300 kWh, depending on building characteristics and climate28–30.

In summary, the electric heating cape demonstrates competitive or superior performance across all three evaluated dimensions thermal comfort, physiological effects, and energy efficiency while offering unique advantages in portability and torso-focused heating. These findings support its consideration as a viable and potentially preferable PCS option for office environments, particularly in scenarios requiring mobility and systemic comfort improvement.

Conclusion

This study systematically investigated the effects of different heating temperatures from a graphene-based electric heating cape on local thermal sensation and thermal comfort of officers through controlled experiments and subjective evaluations. The experimental results demonstrate that the electric heating cape significantly improved subjects’ thermal state, with 40 °C identified as the optimal heating temperature. At this set-point, overall thermal sensation improved from − 1.23 under no-heating condition to -0.13, while overall thermal comfort increased from − 1.08 to 0.35. The most substantial improvements were observed in the core body regions, with abdominal and lumbar areas showing the most pronounced enhancement in both thermal sensation and comfort, confirming the effectiveness of torso-focused heating strategy.

The research revealed notable individual differences in thermal preference. At both 40 °C and 45 °C heating conditions, approximately 30% of participants voluntarily adjusted the cape temperature during the autonomous regulation phase, with adjustment ranges varying between 2 and 5 °C. This inter-individual variation underscores the necessity of personalized thermal regulation rather than adopting a one-size-fits-all approach. Correlation analysis indicated that skin temperature served as a reliable physiological indicator for thermal perception at lower heating intensities, with the strongest correlation observed at 35 °C. However, this correlation weakened at higher temperatures, suggesting that factors beyond skin temperature, such as thermal uniformity and individual sensitivity, become increasingly important in determining comfort at elevated heating levels.

The trade-off analysis between thermal comfort and energy consumption revealed that 40 °C represents the optimal balance point under the tested conditions (15.5 °C ambient temperature). At this setpoint, overall thermal comfort reached its maximum (TCV = 0.35) while avoiding the discomfort and energy inefficiency associated with higher temperatures. For users prioritizing energy savings, 35 °C offers a reasonable compromise, reducing energy consumption by 24% compared to 40 °C while maintaining a “near-neutral” comfort level (TCV = -0.05). Temperatures above 40 °C are contraindicated for both comfort and efficiency reasons, as they produce negative marginal returns—decreased comfort accompanied by increased energy use.

Despite these insights, several limitations should be acknowledged. The study primarily examined steady-state heating under a single ambient condition, using a homogenous sample of young adults. The dynamic thermal response of the cape and its interaction with varying environmental parameters remain uncharacterized. Additionally, the potential safety risks associated with prolonged use of high-temperature settings were highlighted and warrant further investigation.

Comparative analysis with other PCS forms revealed that the electric heating cape offers competitive or superior performance across multiple dimensions. It achieved the largest thermal comfort improvement among the compared devices, which may be attributed to its targeted heating of thermosensitive core body regions. The cape’s conductive heating mechanism proved more energy-efficient than radiant alternatives, consuming only 32 W at the optimal 40 °C setpoint—substantially lower than typical radiant panels and comparable to other conductive devices. The observed systemic physiological effects, including skin temperature increases in non-heated areas, suggest that torso-focused heating may produce whole-body comfort benefits beyond localized warming.

Based on this multi-dimensional comparison, the electric heating cape is positioned as a uniquely versatile PCS option: it combines the mobility advantage of wearable devices with the efficiency of conductive heating and the comfort benefits of torso targeting. These characteristics make it particularly suitable for dynamic office environments where occupants require continuous thermal intervention while moving between tasks. The cape may serve as either a standalone solution or a complement to other PCS forms.

Future research should address these limitations by investigating the dynamic thermal performance of electric heating capes, including their response characteristics and thermal inertia under transient conditions. Further studies should encompass diverse demographic groups, particularly older adults who may have different thermal requirements and sensitivity. Research exploring the integration of localized heating with varying ambient conditions such as humidity and air velocity would provide more comprehensive insights for practical applications. The development of intelligent control systems incorporating machine learning algorithms to automatically adjust heating parameters based on real-time physiological feedback and personal preference history represents a promising direction for achieving truly adaptive personalized thermal comfort. Such advancements could significantly enhance both energy efficiency and occupant satisfaction in office environments while maintaining the flexibility and wearability advantages inherent to electric heating cape solutions.
