Protocol for the preparation, heat treatment, and structural and magnetic characterization of Cu-doped τ-MnAl ribbons produced by melt spinning
Authors: Alvaro José Echeverri Restrepo, Lina Vanessa Girón, Jose Luis Valenzuela, Fernando Maccari, Daniel Salazar, Juan Sebastián Trujillo Hernández
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryHere, we describe a protocol for preparation (arc melting and melt spinning) and heat treatment of two compositional series: Mn55-xAl45Cux and Mn55Al45-xCux (x = 0.0, 2.0, and 2.5). We provide details of their structural and microstructural characterization by X-ray diffraction with Rietveld analysis and scanning electron microscopy with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM/EDX), along with magnetic characterization using a vibrating-sample magnetometer (VSM). In these series, Cu partially substitutes for Mn and Al, respectively, yielding the hard-magnetic tetragonal τ-phase (L10 structure).For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Echeverri et al.1

Before you begin

The hard magnetic τ-MnAl L10 phase is metastable and requires rapid solidification to retain the high-temperature ε-MnAl precursor, followed by short-time annealing at moderate temperature to promote ε → τ transformation.2,3,4,5 At higher annealing temperatures, or under insufficiently controlled cooling, τ tends to decompose into the non-magnetic β and γ2 phases, reducing the magnetic response. Small Cu additions can modify grain-boundary chemistry and microstructure, thereby enhancing coercivity through domain-wall pinning.6,7,8,9,10,11,12

This protocol enables the reproducible fabrication of melt-spun ribbons and their subsequent conversion to τ-MnAl–Cu, matching the methodology used in the study.

The two alloy designs included here were selected to compare the effect of the Cu substitution site under identical processing conditions, following the comparative analysis developed in the associated Heliyon article. When Cu partially replaces Mn (Mn55−xAl45Cux), the as-spun ribbons retain a large fraction of the ε precursor and, after annealing near 450°C/20 min, the τ phase is retained with a lower fraction of secondary phases, giving the best remanence and (BH)max within the present processing window. When Cu partially replaces Al (Mn55Al45−xCux), the alloys show higher coercivity but also a stronger tendency toward β/γ2 formation and a lower remanence, particularly after annealing above 450°C.

Phase identification in this protocol should be interpreted in the context of the Mn–Al phase diagram. ε-MnAl is the hexagonal high-temperature precursor retained by rapid quenching; τ-MnAl is the metastable tetragonal L10 ferromagnetic target phase; and β and γ2 are equilibrium, non-magnetic decomposition products favored during over-annealing. Accordingly, phase assignment is based primarily on Rietveld-refined XRD patterns, including comparison with the reference τ-MnAl (CIF 98-006-5881) and β-Mn (CIF 98-008-9582) patterns used in the workflow, while SEM-BSE/EDX is used as complementary evidence to distinguish bright τ-rich regions, intermediate-contrast β-rich regions, and polishing artifacts. Dark SEM features should not be assigned to γ2 without XRD support because pull-out/cavities can produce similar contrast.

Innovation

This protocol provides a reproducible end-to-end workflow for producing Cu-doped τ-MnAl ribbons by melt spinning and verifying their structural and magnetic response after short annealing treatments. Its main methodological advances are: (i) standardized small-batch preparation through pellet compaction, arc melting, and melt spinning; (ii) a single characterization workflow that links XRD/Rietveld, SEM-BSE/EDX, and VSM to troubleshooting decisions; and (iii) the inclusion of two Cu-substitution routes (Cu→Mn and Cu→Al) within the same protocol, enabling direct comparison under identical processing conditions. The broader interpretation of the structural and magnetic differences between these two routes is presented in expected outcomes, consistent with the STAR Protocols format.

Expected outcomes

Following this protocol, a researcher should expect to produce chemically homogeneous Mn–Al–Cu ingots from 5 g batches (with minor mass losses during handling and melting), and rapidly solidified melt-spun ribbons suitable for phase transformation studies. The melt-spinning step provides the rapid cooling required to stabilize the high-temperature ε-phase precursor, resulting in ribbons that are typically continuous and metallic, with reproducible geometry (width/thickness depending on nozzle–wheel alignment and ejection stability). Across multiple compositions, reproducibility is best achieved when pellet pressing, arc-melting remelts, melt-spinning wheel speed, and Ar pressure are kept constant, allowing composition and annealing temperature to be the primary controlled variables.

For structural outcomes, XRD patterns collected from as-spun ribbons18,19,20 are expected to show signatures consistent with an ε-rich microstructure with a smaller fraction of τ, whereas short anneals (20 min) in the 450–550°C window should promote ε → τ transformation while also enabling τ decomposition into secondary phases at higher temperatures. This is the same structure–property trend discussed in the Results and discussion section of the associated Heliyon article and is summarized here in protocol form. In the Mn55−xAl45Cux series, the as-spun ribbons can retain a substantial ε fraction (for example, 68.8 wt% for AS-Mn2.0 and 93.4 wt% for AS-Mn2.5), indicating that Cu at the Mn site helps stabilize the high-temperature precursor before annealing. After annealing, the most favorable τ-rich condition within the present window is obtained near 450°C/20 min; as temperature increases, the τ fraction decreases and β increases. By contrast, in the Mn55Al45−xCux series, the Cu→Al route makes the alloy more prone to decomposition, so higher-temperature anneals promote β and γ2 more readily. The primary data produced in this section typically include (i) raw diffractograms for each composition/condition, (ii) refined phase fractions and lattice parameters (a, c, unit cell volume), and (iii) refinement quality indicators (e.g., residuals) that provide validation of phase identification. If a different X-ray tube is used (based on laboratory availability), peak positions and relative intensities should remain internally consistent within that measurement configuration, and the refinement model should be adjusted accordingly to maintain reliable phase quantification.

Microstructurally, SEM in backscattered electron mode should yield contrast that enables practical mapping of phase regions and polishing-related artifacts. In agreement with the interpretation developed in the associated Heliyon article, brighter regions are expected to correspond mainly to τ-rich areas, intermediate contrast regions to β-rich areas, and dark features often to surface-preparation defects (e.g., cavities) rather than intrinsic porosity or γ2 assigned solely from contrast. EDX maps or point analyses should confirm that Mn/Al/Cu ratios are close to nominal at.% targets within typical experimental variability, supporting that the alloying route and melt-spinning did not introduce large compositional segregation at the scale probed. The main outputs here should include representative SEM-BSE micrographs (plan-view and/or cross-section, depending on your mounting), annotated phase-contrast interpretation, and a compact EDX summary table reporting average composition from multiple regions per sample to validate nominal stoichiometry.

Magnetically, VSM hysteresis loops measured at 23°C are expected to display hard-magnetic behavior after successful τ formation, with measurable coercivity and remanence that vary systematically with annealing temperature and substitution site (Cu→Mn vs Cu→Al). This expected behavior follows the Results and discussion section of the associated Heliyon article. For the Cu→Mn series, annealing at 450°C/20 min yields the best balance of τ retention, limited β formation, and microstructural refinement; representative values reported for 450Mn2.0 are Hcj ≈ 134.1 kA/m, Mr ≈ 41.0 A·m2/kg, and (BH)max ≈ 13.7 kJ/m3, while 450Mn2.5 reaches higher coercivity (≈145.2 kA/m) but lower Mr and energy product. For the Cu→Al series, coercivity can increase, but the stronger β/γ2 formation causes a larger drop in remanence and overall magnetic performance. The principal magnetic data products should include (i) full hysteresis loops acquired over the available VSM field range (the sweep range may vary depending on instrument capability), (ii) extracted metrics (Hcj, Mr, and magnetization at the maximum field used), and (iii) a clear record of sample mass and mounting geometry to ensure comparability across compositions. These datasets provide a direct, protocol-level validation that τ-phase formation translated into measurable hard-magnetic performance consistent with the expected phase evolution under the processing window described in the associated study.

We encourage including original, representative figures illustrating (1) a workflow schematic (arc melting → melt spinning → annealing → characterization), (2) representative XRD patterns for one composition across as-spun and annealed states highlighting phase evolution, (3) representative SEM-BSE images with annotated contrasts for τ/β and artifacts, and (4) representative VSM hysteresis loops comparing at least two annealing temperatures for one composition. These figures should be prepared from your own experimental output to document protocol performance and provide visual benchmarks for troubleshooting.

Limitations

This protocol is sensitive to the intrinsic metastability of the τ-MnAl phase. Small deviations in annealing temperature, dwell time (nominally 20 min), or the heating/cooling profile can shift the balance between ε → τ transformation and τ decomposition into secondary phases (β and, depending on the substitution route, γ2). As a result, the protocol can become unreliable if temperature control is poor (e.g., large furnace gradients, inaccurate thermocouple placement, or inconsistent sample positioning), because nominally identical heat treatments may yield different phase fractions and, consequently, different magnetic responses.

Mechanical and environmental factors during melt spinning can also limit reproducibility. The effective quench rate depends on wheel surface condition (oxidation, roughness), wheel–nozzle alignment, melt superheat, ejection stability, and the argon pressure range used in the chamber. Variations in any of these parameters can change ribbon thickness and cooling rate, leading to differences in the initial ε/τ content of as-spun ribbons and altering the subsequent annealing outcome. Additionally, exposure to air and humidity can promote surface oxidation of ribbons and powders, which may affect melting behavior, interfacial chemistry, and the apparent magnetization (especially for thin ribbons with high surface-to-volume ratio).

The microstructural characterization step has limitations that can affect interpretation. Metallographic preparation can introduce polishing cavities, pull-out, or smeared surfaces that appear as dark features in SEM-BSE images and may be misinterpreted as porosity or a secondary phase if not carefully controlled. Furthermore, SEM/EDX provides composition at a local scale and may not capture subtle nanoscale segregation or grain-boundary enrichment that can influence coercivity; therefore, absence of obvious segregation in EDX does not fully rule out microchemistry effects relevant to magnetic hardening.

Finally, magnetic measurements are constrained by instrument range and sample geometry. If the available VSM cannot reach sufficiently high fields, some samples may not approach technical saturation, which can bias comparisons of “magnetization at maximum field” between laboratories. Demagnetization effects and mounting orientation are also important for ribbon samples; small changes in alignment or sample shape can change the measured loop squareness and coercivity. For inter-lab comparability, researchers should report the field range used, sample mass, and mounting configuration, and interpret differences with caution when instrument limits or geometry differ.
