Dold-Gauss Congruences, Norm Descent, and Rational Rigidity
Hartosh Singh Bal

Abstract
We develop a Witt–Hadamard calculus for Euler products that unifies the classical Gauss congruences with their modern refinement, the Dold congruences. Within this framework we prove norm descent : Dold congruences are functorial under finite extensions and preserved by prime–ideal norms N K / ℚ N_{K/\mathbb{Q}} , yielding integer ghosts from algebraic ones. We extend the theory from ℤ \mathbb{Z} to Dedekind domains, and show that integrality is stable under both Hadamard and Witt products. Two rigidity theorems lie at the core: a cyclotomic residues theorem , asserting that if the logarithmic derivative has only cyclotomic poles then integrality forces rationality; and a stronger Dold + rigidity theorem , showing that any algebraic series satisfying refined Dold congruences is necessarily rational. These results sharpen the Gauss–Dold picture: ordinary congruences enforce integrality, while the strengthened form collapses algebraic cases to rational ones. Applications include prime–ideal ladders in number fields and exact product laws for dynamical zeta functions, illustrated for subshifts of finite type and circle doubling.

Introduction

The study of congruences for integer sequences goes back to Gauss. His classical congruences say that certain natural sequences, such as powers
a^{n}
, satisfy divisibility laws that can be expressed in Möbius form:

\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)\,a^{n/d}\equiv 0\pmod{n}.

This Möbius-inversion perspective has become the standard way to phrase what are often called
Gauss congruences
.

In the 1960s, Dold introduced a broader framework in algebraic topology and dynamics. Instead of looking only at powers
a^{n}
, he considered sequences
(a_{n})
of fixed-point counts and the corresponding orbit counts
(c_{n})
, related by Möbius inversion:

a_{n}=\sum_{d\mid n}d\,c_{d},\qquad c_{n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{d\mid n}\mu\!\left(\tfrac{n}{d}\right)a_{d}.

The requirement that every
c_{n}
be an integer is exactly what we now call the
Dold congruences
. They characterize which integer sequences can arise as fixed-point data of a dynamical system. Later work—for instance Du, Huang, and Li on generalized Fermat sequences
[
6
]
, or Neumärker on realizability
[
11
]
—showed how these congruences connect to symbolic dynamics and to the broader problem of classifying integer sequences via Möbius inversion.

A recent step, due to Gładki and Pulikowski
[
8
]
, was to extend the classical Gauss congruences from the integers to arbitrary number fields. In this setting, the divisors of
n
are replaced by ideal divisors of
\mathfrak{n}\subseteq\mathcal{O}_{K}
, and the role of
d
in the exponent is played by its absolute norm
N(\mathfrak{d})
. The resulting
ideal Möbius function
and
norm exponents
give a clean version of Gauss congruences valid in every Dedekind domain.

The ghost map
s_{n}=\sum_{d\mid n}d\,f(d)
and its compatibility with Frobenius and Verschiebung operators were studied already in the
necklace ring
of Metropolis–Rota
[
14
]
, which they showed to be isomorphic to the classical Witt vector ring. Their treatment is algebraic and combinatorial, based on necklace polynomials. In contrast, our approach emphasizes congruence conditions (Dold and Dold
+
), norm functoriality over number fields, and rigidity phenomena. The results are therefore complementary: we extend the ghost formalism from
\mathbb{Z}
to Dedekind domains, prove new divisibility and descent theorems, and apply them to algebraicity and rationality questions.

This work builds directly on the author’s earlier joint work with Bhatnagar, where a series–divisor calculus, akin to a calculus of the ghost map, was developed in
[
2
]
over
\mathbb{Z}
. That earlier calculus is extended here to a wider setting, with number fields, ideals, and norms entering the picture.

We obtain three main kinds of results. First, the Dold congruences themselves admit a natural
norm–ideal formulation
(Theorem
8.2
), so that orbit/fixed-point sequences in number fields are controlled by congruences along Frobenius ladders defined by prime ideals and their norms. Second, we prove new
rigidity theorems
. A
cyclotomic residues theorem
shows that if the logarithmic derivative has only cyclotomic poles, then integrality forces rationality. A stronger
Dold
+
theorem
(Theorem
16.8
) shows that any algebraic series satisfying refined Dold congruences must already be rational. Already under plain Dold we obtain a strong restriction: an algebraic integral series which is not rational must have at least one rational zero away from the roots of unity. Equivalently, the presence of a rational non–root-of-unity pole certifies that an integral
A
is algebraic but not rational. Together these sharpen the Gauss–Dold picture: congruences ensure integrality, while their strengthened forms collapse algebraic cases to rational ones. Related work on dynamical zeta functions, periodic point counts, and Pólya–Carlson phenomena can be found in the papers of Bell, Miles, and Ward
[
4
]
, which develop a complementary rational/natural boundary dichotomy.

Finally, we establish a
norm–descent principle
(Theorem
10.2
). If
s(n)
is a Dold ghost over the ring of integers
\mathcal{O}_{K}
of a number field
K
, then its field norm
N_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(s(n))
is again a Dold sequence over
\mathbb{Z}
. Equivalently, the descended Euler exponents are obtained by Dirichlet convolutions of the conjugates of the original exponents. This functoriality preserves
S
–integrality, interacts harmoniously with rigidity, and produces genuinely new integer Dold sequences. In the quadratic case, for example, conjugate cancellation eliminates prime-level contributions and leaves support only at prime squares. In higher degree, support can be pushed to prime cubes or higher powers.

In Section 11, we identify reciprocal polynomials as explicit Euler factors whose ghost sequences are given by power sums of their roots. This observation, while elementary, appears not to have been noted in the Dold or Witt literature, and it provides a direct certificate for the realizability of power–sum sequences within our framework.

Organization.

We begin in Sections 2–5 by recalling the sigma–Hadamard framework and showing how Dold congruences and their
S
-integral variants arise in this setting.
Sections 6–10 extend the theory to number fields, introducing Frobenius ladders, prime–ideal formulations, and the Norm–Descent Principle.
Sections 11–14 develop classification and closure results: a sieve for rational, polynomial, and algebraic images, effective certification algorithms, and stability under Hadamard/Witt products.
Sections 15–17 apply these tools in dynamics, including Artin–Mazur zeta functions, cyclotomic residues, and the Dold
+
rigidity theorem.
The appendices collect background on Ramanujan sums and function fields.

Setup and notation

For a commutative ring
R
with
1
, write

\Lambda(R):=1+q\,R[[q]],\qquad\sigma:\Lambda(R)\to qR[[q]],\qquad\sigma(A):=-\,q\,\frac{A^{\prime}(q)}{A(q)}=\sum_{n\geq 1}s_{n}q^{n}.

We call
S(q):=\sigma(A)
the
sigma–divisor
of
A
; in the Witt vector literature the components
s_{n}
are known as
ghost coordinates
. On
qR[[q]]
we use ordinary addition together with the
Hadamard product
(termwise multiplication of coefficients, in contrast to the usual Cauchy product):

\Big(\sum_{n\geq 1}s_{n}q^{n}\Big)\odot\Big(\sum_{n\geq 1}t_{n}q^{n}\Big)=\sum_{n\geq 1}(s_{n}t_{n})q^{n}.

Every
A\in\Lambda(R)
has
Euler–product coordinates

A(q)=\prod_{d\geq 1}(1-q^{d})^{f(d)}\qquad\text{with Euler exponents }f(d)\in R,

(1)

and then the sigma–divisor coefficients are given by

s_{n}=\sum_{d\mid n}d\,f(d)\qquad(n\geq 1).

(2)

Convention.

Unless otherwise indicated, all operations on power series
(differentiation, division, exponentiation, logarithm, etc.)
are understood formally in
R[[q]]
.
In particular,
A\in 1+qR[[q]]
is always invertible,
so expressions such as
A^{\prime}/A
are purely formal and
no analytic convergence is assumed.

Notation.

Throughout the paper we reserve the symbol
\sigma
for the

ghost map

\sigma(A)\;=\;-\,\frac{qA^{\prime}}{A}\,.

To avoid confusion, whenever we refer to a field embedding

K\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}
we write
\tau
(or occasionally

\tau_{i}
if several embeddings are involved).
For example, in the Norm–Descent principle we use

\sigma\!\big(N_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(A)\big)=\boxplus_{\;\tau:K\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}}\;\tau\!\big(\sigma(A)\big).

We also work with infinite cyclotomic products

\prod_{m}\Phi_{m}(q)^{-E_{m}}
, but we adopt the convention
that the first factor is written
(1-q)^{-E_{1}}
rather than

\Phi_{1}(q)^{-E_{1}}=(q-1)^{-E_{1}}
. This choice ensures
normalization
A(0)=1
and expansions in
1+q\mathbb{Z}[[q]]
.
A fuller discussion of this sign convention is given in
Appendix
Appendix B. Infinite Cyclotomic Products
.

Sigma–Hadamard isomorphism over
\mathbb{Q}

Theorem 3.1

(Sigma–Hadamard over
\mathbb{Q}
)
.

Let
R
be a
\mathbb{Q}
-algebra. Equip
qR[[q]]
with
(+,\odot)
.
There is a unique ring structure
(\Lambda(R),\boxplus,\boxtimes)
with

A\boxplus B:=AB\quad\text{\emph{(ordinary product)}},

such that

\sigma:\ (\Lambda(R),\boxplus,\boxtimes)\ \xrightarrow{\ \cong\ }\ (qR[[q]],+,\odot)

is a ring isomorphism, where
\sigma(A)=-qA^{\prime}(q)/A(q)
is the
sigma–divisor map

(known in the Witt and necklace ring literature as the
ghost map
).
The inverse map is given by

\sigma^{-1}\!\Big(\sum_{n\geq 1}s_{n}q^{n}\Big)=\exp\!\Big(-\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{s_{n}}{n}\,q^{n}\Big).

In the Euler coordinates (
1
)–(
2
), the transported multiplication
\boxtimes
is characterized by

(id*(f\boxtimes g))(n)\;=\;(id*f)(n)\,(id*g)(n),

equivalently,

(f\boxtimes g)(n)\;=\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)\,(id*f)\!\left(\frac{n}{d}\right)\,(id*g)\!\left(\frac{n}{d}\right).

Proof.

Over a
\mathbb{Q}
-algebra,
\log
and
\exp
give inverse bijections between
qR[[q]]
and
\Lambda(R)
.
Linearity of
-q\frac{d}{dq}
yields
\sigma(AB)=\sigma(A)+\sigma(B)
, so
\sigma
is a homomorphism between
(\Lambda(R),\boxplus)
and
(qR[[q]],+)
.
Define
\boxtimes
by transport of structure:
\sigma(A\boxtimes B)=\sigma(A)\odot\sigma(B)
.
Writing
A(q)=\prod_{d\geq 1}(1-q^{d})^{f(d)}
and
B(q)=\prod_{d\geq 1}(1-q^{d})^{g(d)}
, one has

\sigma_{A}(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}d\,f(d),\qquad\sigma_{B}(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}d\,g(d).

Thus
\sigma_{A\boxtimes B}(n)=\sigma_{A}(n)\sigma_{B}(n)
. Since also
\sigma_{A\boxtimes B}(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}d\,(f\boxtimes g)(d)
, weighted Möbius inversion gives the displayed formula for
f\boxtimes g
. The choice
\sigma=-q(\log A)^{\prime}
(with the minus sign) ensures that Euler and cyclotomic factors map to the positive divisor/Ramanujan-sum transforms used in (
1
)–(
2
).
∎

Lemma 3.2

(Big Witt product on Euler coefficients)
.

Let
R
be a commutative ring, and for
f:\mathbb{N}\to R
define

\mathcal{E}(f)(q)=\prod_{m\geq 1}(1-q^{m})^{-f(m)}.

If
h=f\boxtimes g
, then for every
n\geq 1
one has the explicit formula

h(n)\;=\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}a,b\geq 1\\
\mathrm{lcm}(a,b)=n\end{subarray}}\!\!(a,b)\,f(a)\,g(b),

where
(a,b)=\gcd(a,b)
.

Proof.

Define ghost components
G_{n}(f)=\sum_{d\mid n}d\,f(d)
. Then

\sigma_{A}(n)=G_{n}(f),\quad\sigma_{B}(n)=G_{n}(g),\quad\sigma_{A\boxtimes B}(n)=G_{n}(h).

The isomorphism ensures
G_{n}(h)=G_{n}(f)\,G_{n}(g)
. Both sides are multiplicative in
n
, so it suffices to compute at
n=p^{k}
.

For
k\geq 0
, put

F_{k}=\sum_{i=0}^{k}p^{i}f(p^{i}),\qquad G_{k}=\sum_{j=0}^{k}p^{j}g(p^{j}),\qquad A_{k}=\sum_{t=0}^{k}p^{t}h(p^{t}).

Then
A_{k}=F_{k}G_{k}
for all
k
. To extract
h(p^{k})
we take the difference:

h(p^{k})=\frac{A_{k}-A_{k-1}}{p^{k}}=\frac{F_{k}G_{k}-F_{k-1}G_{k-1}}{p^{k}}.

Now write
F_{k}=F_{k-1}+p^{k}f(p^{k})
and
G_{k}=G_{k-1}+p^{k}g(p^{k})
, so

F_{k}G_{k}-F_{k-1}G_{k-1}=p^{k}f(p^{k})G_{k-1}+p^{k}g(p^{k})F_{k-1}+p^{2k}f(p^{k})g(p^{k}).

Dividing by
p^{k}
gives

h(p^{k})=f(p^{k})\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}p^{j}g(p^{j})+g(p^{k})\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}p^{i}f(p^{i})+p^{k}f(p^{k})g(p^{k}).

Equivalently,

h(p^{k})=\sum_{\max(i,j)=k}p^{\min(i,j)}f(p^{i})g(p^{j}),

which is the desired gcd–lcm formula at prime powers. Multiplicativity then reassembles the general case for arbitrary
n
.
∎

4
Image over
\mathbb{Z}
: Dold congruences

For a prime
p
, integers
a\geq 1
and
(m,p)=1
, the
tower (Euler) congruence at
p
for a sequence
(s_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is

s_{p^{a}m}\equiv s_{p^{a-1}m}\pmod{p^{a}}.

(3)

These are the classical Dold congruences over
\mathbb{Z}
, equivalent to the condition that the orbit counts obtained from Möbius inversion are integers.

Theorem 4.1

(Image over
\mathbb{Z}
)
.

A series
S(q)=\sum_{n\geq 1}s_{n}q^{n}\in q\mathbb{Z}[[q]]
equals
\sigma(A)
for some
A\in\Lambda(\mathbb{Z})
if and only if
(s_{n})
satisfies (
3
) for every prime
p
. Equivalently,
S
lies in the image if and only if there exists a unique sequence of Euler exponents
f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{Z}
such that

s_{n}=\sum_{d\mid n}d\,f(d)\qquad(n\geq 1).

(4)

Proof.

(
\Rightarrow
) Suppose
A\in\Lambda(\mathbb{Z})
. Construct
f
recursively: assume
f(1),\dots,f(n-1)
are already defined, and set

B_{n}(q):=A(q)\prod_{d<n}(1-q^{d})^{-f(d)}\in 1+q^{n}\mathbb{Z}[[q]].

Writing
B_{n}(q)=1+b_{n}q^{n}+O(q^{n+1})
, define
f(n):=b_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then
(1-q^{n})^{f(n)}B_{n}(q)\in 1+q^{n+1}\mathbb{Z}[[q]]
. This recursive construction yields a unique Euler product factorization (
1
) with
f(d)\in\mathbb{Z}
. Taking
\sigma
gives (
4
). Finally, if
n=p^{a}m
with
(m,p)=1
then

s_{p^{a}m}-s_{p^{a-1}m}=\sum_{d\mid m}\big(p^{a}d\big)\,f(p^{a}d)\in p^{a}\mathbb{Z},

so (
3
) holds.

(
\Leftarrow
) Assume (
3
) for all
p
. Define

f(n):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{d\mid n}\mu(d)\,s_{n/d}\qquad\text{a priori in $\mathbb{Q}$}.

For
n=p^{a}m
with
(m,p)=1
,

n\,f(n)=\sum_{e\mid m}\sum_{i=0}^{a}\mu(p^{i})\mu(e)\,s_{p^{a-i}(m/e)}=\sum_{e\mid m}\mu(e)\big(s_{p^{a}(m/e)}-s_{p^{a-1}(m/e)}\big)\in p^{a}\mathbb{Z}.

Thus
f(n)\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
for all primes
p
, hence
f(n)\in\mathbb{Z}
. The Euler product (
1
) with these exponents lies in
\Lambda(\mathbb{Z})
, and (
2
) gives
\sigma(A)=S
.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

This description of the image over
\mathbb{Z}
is the classical form of the Dold congruences.
It appears in different guises in the literature, for example in the work of Du, Huang, and Li on generalized Fermat sequences
[
6
]
and in Neumärker’s realization criterion for fixed point sequences
[
11
]
.

Remark.

Closure of the Dold subring under the Hadamard product
\odot
is proved later
(see Lemma
14.6
(tower formulation, hence Dold over
\\
mathbb{Z}
)). In particular, the image

\sigma(\Lambda(\mathbb{Z}))\subset q\mathbb{Z}[[q]]
is indeed a subring
under both addition and
\odot
.

Terminology note.
In this paper we use the following conventions. The term
Dold
(or Gauss/Dold) refers to the Möbius formulation of congruences, while the
tower (Euler) formulation
expresses the equivalent prime-power conditions. Over
\mathbb{Z}
the two are equivalent, as the above results show, but for
S
–integral variants we state results in the tower form. Our refinements (Dold
+
, Dold
++
) strengthen the divisibility along prime-power towers.

5
S
-integral lifting over
\mathbb{Z}

Let
S
be a finite set of primes and
\mathbb{Z}[S^{-1}]
the localization.

Theorem 5.1

(
S
-integral lifting)
.

For
S(q)=\sum s_{n}q^{n}\in q\mathbb{Z}[[q]]
the following are equivalent:

1.

(s_{n})
satisfies the Dold congruences outside
S
: for every prime
\ell\notin S
, all
a\geq 1
, and
(m,\ell)=1
one has

s_{\ell^{a}m}\equiv s_{\ell^{a-1}m}\pmod{\ell^{a}}.

2.

The Euler product
\sigma^{-1}(S)=\exp\!\big(-\sum_{n\geq 1}\tfrac{s_{n}}{n}q^{n}\big)
belongs to
\Lambda\big(\mathbb{Z}[S^{-1}]\big)
.

Proof.

The argument of Theorem
4.1
localizes prime by prime.
Condition (i) implies via Möbius inversion that
f(n)\in\mathbb{Z}_{\ell}
for each
\ell\notin S
, hence
f(n)\in\mathbb{Z}[S^{-1}]
, so the Euler product (
1
) has exponents in
\mathbb{Z}[S^{-1}]
.
Conversely, if
A\in\Lambda(\mathbb{Z}[S^{-1}])
, then for every
\ell\notin S
the exponents lie in
\mathbb{Z}_{\ell}
, which forces the
\ell
-adic Dold congruences.
∎

Remark 5.2

(One bad prime)
.

Taking
S=\{p\}
, the failure of Dold congruences only at
p
is equivalent to
A\in\Lambda(\mathbb{Z}[1/p])
.
In other words, the Euler exponents may acquire denominators supported at
p
, so the coefficients of
A
lie in
\mathbb{Z}[1/p]
and nowhere smaller.

Remark 5.3
.

This
S
-integral lifting principle is the natural localization of the classical Dold congruences.
It is directly analogous to the way Gauss congruences localize prime by prime, cf. the number field generalization of Gładki–Pulikowski
[
8
]
.

Remark 5.4
.

The
S
–integral lifting theorem, though elementary, is new in this explicit
form. It isolates the precise set of primes allowed in denominators of the
ghost sequence
(s_{n})
, thereby linking integrality properties of
\sigma(A)

to the algebraic or transcendental nature of the underlying series
A(q)
.

6
Frobenius ladders and progression-zero congruences

Lemma 6.1

(
p
-adic lifting of the freshman’s dream)
.

Let
p
be prime and
A\in\Lambda(\mathbb{Z}_{p})
. For all
a\geq 1
,

A(q)^{p^{a}}\ \equiv\ A\!\big(q^{p^{a}}\big)\pmod{p^{a}}.

Proof.

Modulo
p
one has
A(q)^{p}\equiv A(q^{p})
in
\mathbb{F}_{p}[[q]]
.
Set
G_{a}(q):=A(q)^{p^{a}}/A(q^{p^{a}})\in 1+p\mathbb{Z}_{p}[[q]]
. Then

G_{a+1}=\frac{A^{p^{a+1}}}{A(q^{p^{a+1}})}=\Big(\frac{A^{p^{a}}}{A(q^{p^{a}})}\Big)^{p}\cdot\frac{A(q^{p^{a}})^{p}}{A(q^{p^{a+1}})}\equiv\big(G_{a}\big)^{p}\pmod{p}.

If
G_{a}\equiv 1\pmod{p^{a}}
, write
G_{a}=1+p^{a}H
; then

(1+p^{a}H)^{p}=1+p^{a+1}H+\binom{p}{2}p^{2a}H^{2}+\cdots\equiv 1\pmod{p^{a+1}}.

Thus raising to the
p
th power increases the modulus by one, and induction gives the claim.
∎

Theorem 6.2

(Prime-power progression zeros)
.

If
A\in\Lambda(\mathbb{Z})
, then for every prime
p
and
a\geq 1
,

[q^{n}]\,A(q)^{p^{a}}\equiv 0\pmod{p^{a}}\qquad\text{whenever }p^{a}\nmid n.

Equivalently, coefficients supported outside the
p^{a}
-progression vanish modulo
p^{a}
.

Proof.

Work in
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
and apply Lemma
6.1
:

A(q)^{p^{a}}\equiv A(q^{p^{a}})\pmod{p^{a}}.

Since
A(q^{p^{a}})
only involves terms
q^{p^{a}k}
, no monomials
q^{n}
with
p\nmid n
appear, so the coefficients off the progression vanish modulo
p^{a}
.
∎

Remark 6.3
.

These progression-zero congruences are the
Frobenius ladders
:
they describe the divisibility pattern of coefficients along prime-power progressions.
They are the integer analogue of the norm–tower (Euler) congruences in number fields, where Frobenius powers are measured by absolute norms of prime ideals.

A valuation lemma from the master recurrence

Lemma 7.1

(Master recurrence)
.

If
A(q)=\sum_{n\geq 0}a(n)q^{n}\in\Lambda(R)
and
\sigma(A)=\sum_{n\geq 1}s_{n}q^{n}
, then for all
n\geq 1
,

n\,a(n)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}\,a(n-i)\quad\text{in }R.

Proof.

From
\log A(q)=-\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{s_{n}}{n}q^{n}
, differentiation gives

\frac{A^{\prime}(q)}{A(q)}=\sum_{n\geq 1}s_{n}q^{\,n-1},

hence
A^{\prime}(q)=A(q)\sum_{n\geq 1}s_{n}q^{\,n-1}
.
Comparing coefficients of
q^{n-1}
yields the stated recurrence, which we refer to as the
master recurrence
between coefficients
a(n)
and sigma–divisor coefficients
s_{n}
.
∎

Theorem 7.2

(Off-progression
p
-adic divisibility)
.

Let
R=\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. Suppose all off-progression sigma–divisor coefficients satisfy

s_{i}\in p^{r}\mathbb{Z}_{p}\qquad\text{for every $i$ with $p\nmid i$}.

Then all off-progression coefficients of
A
satisfy

a(n)\in p^{r}\mathbb{Z}_{p}\qquad\text{for every $n$ with $p\nmid n$}.

Proof.

Induct on
n
. If
p\nmid n
, then by Lemma
7.1
the left-hand side has
p
-adic unit
n
.
On the right, terms with
p\nmid i
contribute
p^{r}\mathbb{Z}_{p}
by hypothesis, and terms with
p\mid i
involve
a(n-i)
where
p\nmid(n-i)
, so by the induction hypothesis
a(n-i)\in p^{r}\mathbb{Z}_{p}
.
Hence the sum lies in
p^{r}\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, forcing
a(n)\in p^{r}\mathbb{Z}_{p}
.
∎

Remark 7.3
.

This lemma shows that
p
-adic divisibility of the sigma–divisor coefficients away from the
p
-progression transfers directly to divisibility of the underlying coefficients
a(n)
.
It is a key technical input for integrality and rigidity results, echoing similar recurrence arguments in realizability criteria (cf. Neumärker
[
11
]
).

Number fields: norm–tower (Euler) image and S–integrality

Let
K/\mathbb{Q}
be a number field with ring of integers
\mathcal{O}=\mathcal{O}_{K}
.
For a nonzero prime ideal
\mathfrak{p}\subset\mathcal{O}
write
q_{\mathfrak{p}}:=N\mathfrak{p}
.
Congruences “
\bmod\ \mathfrak{p}^{a}
” are taken coefficientwise in
\mathcal{O}
.
Here we switch notation and write
c(d)\in\mathcal{O}_{K}
for the algebraic Euler exponents,
reserving
f(d)
for the integral case. After norm descent we denote the descended
integer exponents by
c_{\mathbb{Z}}(d)
.

Definition 8.1

(Norm–tower (Euler) at
\mathfrak{p}
)
.

A sequence
(s_{n})\subset\mathcal{O}
satisfies
norm–tower (Euler)

Remark 8.3

(Ideal Möbius vs. norm–tower formulations)

In

Remark 8.5

(Scope of coefficient rings)
.

Throughout we have assumed the coefficient ring is a Dedekind domain

\mathbb{Z}
or the ring of integers
\mathcal{O}_{K}
of a number field).
This ensures that every nonzero prime ideal corresponds to a discrete

Remark 8.6

(Tower versus ideal Gauss in number fields)
.

Over
\mathbb{Z}

Over number fields, however, the situation diverges. The ideal Gauss

\mathfrak{p}
–adic denominators that appear in Möbius inversion. The
norm–tower (Euler) congruences at each prime ideal are always
necessary

(local shadows of the ideal Gauss congruences, produced by Frobenius ladders),
but in general they are not sufficient to guarantee integrality of the Euler

p
–adic arguments
(rigidity,

and

Theorem 9.1

10
The Norm–Descent Principle

Idea.
Euler products over a number field
K
have ghosts
s_{K}(n)\in\mathcal{O}_{K}
.
Taking norms termwise produces an
integer
ghost
u(n)=N_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(s_{K}(n))

that still satisfies the Dold divisibility. A single Möbius inversion then yields
integer exponents downstairs. Equivalently, on prime powers the descended exponents
are Dirichlet convolutions of the Galois–conjugate exponents upstairs. In quadratic
fields this already forces “square support” in many natural cases.

Remark.

We use the
field norm

N_{K/\mathbb{Q}}
, applied coefficientwise to
elements of
\Lambda(\mathcal{O}_{K})
. No Galois hypothesis is required: the norm
is taken as a product over all embeddings
\tau:K\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}
.
A key identity is

\sigma\!\big(N_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(A)\big)\;=\;\boxplus_{\;\tau:K\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}}\;\tau\!\big(\sigma(A)\big),

that is, the ghost of the norm is the
\boxplus
–sum (Dirichlet convolution on
coefficients) of the conjugate ghosts.

Proposition 10.1

(Norm–ideal divisibility)
.

For every
n\geq 1
, one has
N_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(s_{K}(n))\equiv 0\pmod{n}
.

Proof.

Write
n=p^{a}m
with
(m,p)=1
for a rational prime
p
. For each embedding

\tau:K\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}
put
s_{\tau}(n):=\tau(s_{K}(n))
.
Partition the divisor sum

s_{K}(p^{a}m)=\sum_{d\mid p^{a}m}d\,c(d)

according to the
p
-adic valuation of
d
, giving

s_{\tau}(p^{a}m)=s_{\tau}(p^{a-1}m)+p^{a}\,t_{\tau},\qquad t_{\tau}:=\sum_{d\mid m}d\,\tau\!\big(c(p^{a}d)\big)\in\mathcal{O}_{K}.

Now

u(p^{a}m)=N_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(s_{K}(p^{a}m))=\prod_{\tau}\big(s_{\tau}(p^{a-1}m)+p^{a}t_{\tau}\big).

Subtracting
u(p^{a-1}m)=\prod_{\tau}s_{\tau}(p^{a-1}m)
and expanding gives

u(p^{a}m)-u(p^{a-1}m)=\sum_{\emptyset\neq J}p^{a|J|}\,U_{J},

where
J
ranges over nonempty subsets of embeddings and each
U_{J}
is a symmetric
polynomial in the algebraic integers
\{s_{\tau}(p^{a-1}m),t_{\tau}\}_{\tau}
.

Each
s_{\tau}(p^{a-1}m)
and
t_{\tau}
is the image of an algebraic integer
under an embedding
\tau
, hence itself an algebraic integer. Because
U_{J}

arises as a polynomial in these quantities that is
symmetric
under
permutations of the embeddings, the fundamental theorem of symmetric polynomials
implies that
U_{J}
can be written as an integer–coefficient polynomial in the
elementary symmetric polynomials of the conjugates. Those elementary symmetric
polynomials are the coefficients of the minimal polynomial of the underlying
algebraic integer, and thus lie in
\mathbb{Z}
. Consequently every
U_{J}\in\mathbb{Z}
,
so the entire difference
u(p^{a}m)-u(p^{a-1}m)
is an integer combination of
powers of
p
.

Every term is therefore divisible by
p^{a}
, so

u(p^{a}m)\equiv u(p^{a-1}m)\pmod{p^{a}}.

Induction on
a
yields
u(n)\equiv 0\pmod{n}
for all
n
.

∎

Theorem 10.2

(Norm–descent for Dold ghosts)
.

Let
K/\mathbb{Q}
be a number field with Dold ghost
s_{K}(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}d\,c(d)\in\mathcal{O}_{K}
.
Set
u(n):=N_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(s_{K}(n))\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then:

1.

u(n)
is a Dold ghost over
\mathbb{Z}
, i.e.
u(n)\equiv 0\pmod{n}
for all
n
.

2.

The descended exponents

c_{\mathbb{Z}}(n)\;:=\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{d\mid n}\mu\!\Big(\frac{n}{d}\Big)\,u(d)\ \in\ \mathbb{Z}

satisfy
u(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}d\,c_{\mathbb{Z}}(d)
.

3.

Writing
\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{r}
for the embeddings
K\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}
and

a_{j}(n):=(id*(\tau_{j}\circ c))(n)=\sum_{d\mid n}d\,\tau_{j}\!\big(c(d)\big),

one has the pointwise identity of ghosts

\boxed{\quad(id*c_{\mathbb{Z}})(n)\;=\;\prod_{j=1}^{r}a_{j}(n)\quad}

equivalently,

c_{\mathbb{Z}}(n)\;=\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{d\mid n}\mu\!\Big(\frac{n}{d}\Big)\,\prod_{j=1}^{r}a_{j}(d).

Proof.

(1) is Proposition
10.1
. For (2), the Dold equivalence and Möbius
inversion over
\mathbb{Z}
show that
c_{\mathbb{Z}}
is integral and recovers

as its ghost.
For (3), work multiplicatively at
p^{k}
and expand

u(p^{k})=\prod_{j=1}^{r}\sum_{i=0}^{k}p^{i}\,\tau_{j}\!\big(c(p^{i})\big)=\prod_{j=1}^{r}a_{j}(p^{k}).

Since

, weighted Möbius inversion yields the displayed identities.
∎

Remark 10.4

(On the depth of norm–descent)
.

The

lies in Proposition

terms can cancel entirely, pushing support to squares or cubes as in the examples below.
In this sense norm–descent is closely analogous to the way Galois symmetry forces
traces and norms of algebraic integers to be integral, even though this is not visible

.

Remark 10.6
.

The descended Euler products are of course ordinary integer Euler products — one

and verifying the Dold congruences. What norm descent adds is

or

. In this sense norm descent explains and systematizes certain integer Dold sequences that would otherwise only be found by working backwards.

S

11
Algebraicity and rationality sieve via the logarithmic derivative

Remark.

The criteria in this section and the next are classical facts from the
function–field theory of power series: rational functions are detected by
their partial fraction residues, and algebraic functions by the form of their
logarithmic derivatives.
We restate these results entirely in the ghost/Dold language and in a
prime–ideal localized form so that they connect directly with the
congruence and integrality results of Sections 4–10.
In this way the algebraic/transcendental boundary becomes visible
coefficientwise through the same sigma–Hadamard formalism.

Having described the integrality side of the theory through Dold and norm–tower (Euler) congruences,
we now turn to algebraicity and rationality.
Here the decisive tool is the logarithmic derivative: it detects whether a series is rational, algebraic, or transcendental by the form of
S(q)/q
.

Theorem 11.1

(Logarithmic-derivative criterion)
.

Let
K
be a characteristic-
0

1.

A(q)
is
rational
over
K

A(0)=1
if and only if

\frac{S(q)}{q}=-\frac{F^{\prime}(q)}{F(q)}

for some
F\in K(q)
with
F(0)=1
, and equivalently
R(q):=-S(q)/q
has

only simple poles
,
integer residues
, and no polynomial part, with the
multiset of pole–residue data
\{(\alpha_{i},k_{i})\}

Galois–stable over
K
.
In that case

A(q)=\prod_{i=1}^{t}(1-\alpha_{i}q)^{-k_{i}}\in K(q),\qquad s_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{t}k_{i}\alpha_{i}^{\,n},\quad k_{i}\in\mathbb{Z},\ \alpha_{i}\in\overline{K}^{\times},

so the ghost sequence

is an exponential–polynomial sequence
(a linear recurrence with constant coefficients).

2.

A(q)

if and only if
\tfrac{S(q)}{q}=-F^{\prime}(q)/F(q)
for some algebraic

Proof.

If

Conversely, if

.
For (1), if

over

The exponential–polynomial formula for

∎

Corollary 11.2

(Denominators vs. algebraicity)

If

Remark 11.3

Proposition 11.4

(Reciprocal polynomial
\Rightarrow
ghost components and Euler exponents)
.

Let
P(X)=X^{n}+a_{n-1}X^{n-1}+\cdots+a_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}[X]
be monic with roots

in an algebraic closure, and set its reciprocal

R(x)\;:=\;x^{n}P(1/x)\;=\;\prod_{i=1}^{n}(1-r_{i}x)\;\in\;1+x\mathbb{Z}[x]\subset 1+x\mathbb{Z}[[x]].

Then the
\sigma
–ghost of

is the power–sum generating series

\sigma(R)(x)\;=\;-\,x\frac{R^{\prime}(x)}{R(x)}\;=\;\sum_{m\geq 1}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}^{\,m}\Big)x^{m}\;=\;\sum_{m\geq 1}s(m)\,x^{m},

so
s(m)=\sum_{i}r_{i}^{\,m}
for all
m\geq 1
. Writing the (unique) Euler product

R(x)\;=\;\prod_{n\geq 1}(1-x^{n})^{c(n)},

the exponents are given by the Möbius transform

\boxed{\;c(n)\;=\;\frac{1}{n}\sum_{d\mid n}\mu\!\left(\frac{n}{d}\right)s(d)\;}\qquad(n\geq 1).

In particular,
c(n)\in\mathbb{Z}
for all

(hence
R\in 1+x\mathbb{Z}[[x]]
indeed), and if

for a Dedekind domain

, then
s(m)\in\mathcal{O}_{K}
for all

Proof.

The factorization
R(x)=\prod_{i}(1-r_{i}x)
gives

-\,x\frac{R^{\prime}}{R}(x)=x\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{r_{i}}{1-r_{i}x}=\sum_{m\geq 1}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{n}r_{i}^{\,m}\Big)x^{m},

which is the claimed ghost expansion. For the Euler exponents, combine

-\,x\frac{R^{\prime}}{R}(x)=\sum_{n\geq 1}\Big(\sum_{d\mid n}d\,c(d)\Big)x^{n}

with the previous identity and apply Möbius inversion to obtain the boxed formula. Integrality of the
c(n)
follows because
s(d)
is an integer polynomial in the coefficients

via Newton identities.
∎

Corollary 11.5

(Relative (and exact) realizability of power–sum sequences)
.

With

for all

[

is

Proof.

By Proposition

,
\operatorname{orb}(h)_{n}=c(n)\in\mathbb{Z}
for all

[

∎

Remark 11.6
.

The link between reciprocal polynomials, ghost components, and Euler

While Newton sums and Möbius inversion are classical, the explicit

as the Euler factor whose ghost

Remark 11.7
.

The description in Proposition

also indicates
that the ghost–recurrence formalism extends naturally beyond power series.
Whenever

is monic, the reciprocal

defines a finite Euler product whose ghost sequence consists of the power

Thus the Dold–ghost language lifts canonically to polynomial rings, where

Remark 11.8
.

Because the

are power sums of algebraic integers,
their

-adic valuations are controlled by the valuations of the individual roots

.
This ties directly into the

-integrality and Frobenius ladder lemmas developed earlier.
Analytically, if

, then

as

, so the dominant root dictates the exponential growth of the ghost components.
Thus reciprocal polynomials provide a clean bridge between algebraic integrality
(Newton identities),

-adic valuations, and analytic asymptotics.

Example 11.9
.

Take

with roots

.

Then

,

12
Rational and algebraic images: exact integration criteria

The map
\sigma(A)=-qA^{\prime}(q)/A
can be viewed as a logarithmic derivative
sieve. A natural question is: what kind of series
S(q)
arise if
A
is
rational, exponential of a polynomial, or algebraic? Classical finite
integration theory already gives the answer. We record the classification
here in our setting, and refer to standard sources for proofs
(e.g.
[
18
,
13
]
).

Remark.

In the rational residues criterion, the condition “no polynomial part” means
that
R(q)=-S(q)/q
has only simple poles with integer residues and vanishing
polynomial part at
q=0
. Equivalently,
S(0)=0
. The decomposition

A(q)=\prod_{i}(1-\alpha_{i}q)^{-k_{i}}
is unique up to reordering.

Rational case.

If
A(q)\in K(q)
with
A(0)=1
is rational, then
R(q)=-A^{\prime}(q)/A
has only
simple poles, with integer residues equal to the multiplicities of the
zeros and poles of
A
. Conversely, if
R(q)\in K(q)
has this property,
then integrating reconstructs
A(q)
as a product

\prod(1-\alpha q)^{-k}
. Thus
S(q)=-qA^{\prime}/A
is rational if and only if

R(q)
has only simple poles with integer residues. In this case the ghost
sequence
(s_{n})
is a linear recurrence sequence.

Lemma 12.1

(Rational residues criterion)
.

Let
S(q)\in qK(q)
with
R(q)=-S(q)/q\in K(q)
. Then there exists a rational

A(q)\in K(q)
with
A(0)=1
and
\sigma(A)=S
if and only if
R(q)
has only
simple poles with integer residues and no polynomial part. In that case

A(q)=\prod_{i}(1-\alpha_{i}q)^{-k_{i}},\qquad s_{n}=\sum_{i}k_{i}\alpha_{i}^{\,n}.

Corollary 12.2

(Eventual periodicity modulo
M
)
.

Let
A(q)\in K(q)
with
A(0)=1
be rational, and let
(s_{n})
be its ghost
sequence. For any nonzero ideal
\mathfrak{M}
of the ring of integers of a
number field containing all
\alpha_{i},k_{i}
, the sequence
(s_{n}\bmod\mathfrak{M})
is
eventually periodic. If all
\alpha_{i}
are roots of unity, then
(s_{n})
is
strictly periodic.

Remark.

In Corollary
12.2
it is understood that

\mathfrak{M}
is coprime to the denominators of the
\alpha_{i}
.
In this case each
\alpha_{i}
has finite order in

(\mathcal{O}_{K}/\mathfrak{M})^{\times}
, so the sequences

(\alpha_{i}^{n})
are periodic modulo
\mathfrak{M}
, and hence any
finite linear combination is eventually periodic.

Polynomial case.

If
S(q)
is a polynomial, then

A(q)=\exp\!\Big(-\sum_{n=1}^{N}\tfrac{s_{n}}{n}\,q^{n}\Big)

is the exponential of a polynomial with no constant term. Unless

S\equiv 0
, this exponential is transcendental over
K(q)
. Thus
polynomial ghosts correspond exactly to exponential–polynomial functions,
which are almost never algebraic.

Proposition 12.3

(Polynomial ghosts
\Rightarrow
transcendence)
.

If
S(q)\in qK[q]
and
S\not\equiv 0
, then

A(q)=\exp\!\Big(-\sum_{n=1}^{N}\tfrac{s_{n}}{n}q^{n}\Big)

is transcendental over
K(q)
. If
S\equiv 0
, then
A\equiv 1
is rational.

Algebraic case.

If
A
is algebraic over
K(q)
, then its logarithmic derivative

A^{\prime}/A
is the logarithmic differential of an algebraic function. Conversely,
if
-S/q=F^{\prime}/F
for some algebraic
F
with
F(0)=1
, then integrating
gives
A=F
. Thus algebraic images correspond exactly to logarithmic
differentials of algebraic functions.

Theorem 12.4

(Algebraic image)
.

Let
S(q)\in qK[[q]]
. Then
A(q)=\exp(-\sum_{n\geq 1}\tfrac{s_{n}}{n}q^{n})
is
algebraic over
K(q)
if and only if
-S(q)/q
is the logarithmic derivative
of an algebraic function
F(q)
with
F(0)=1
. In that case
A=F
.

Summary.

•

Rational images
\Leftrightarrow
partial fractions with integer residues.

•

Polynomial images
\Leftrightarrow
exponentials of polynomials
(typically transcendental).

•

Algebraic images
\Leftrightarrow
logarithmic differentials of
algebraic functions.

This recovers, in the sigma–Hadamard framework, the same trichotomy that
finite integration theory establishes for rational and algebraic
integrability. It also sets the stage for our rigidity results: while the
rational and algebraic cases are well understood, the Dold
+
theorem
shows that in the presence of congruences, the algebraic case collapses
back to the rational one.

13
Residues and linear recurrences

Section
12
classified rational, polynomial,
and algebraic images of the
\sigma
–map in the classical
integration-theoretic sense. We now record a complementary description:
every ghost with finite residue data is a finite exponential sum, hence a linear recurrence
sequence. This “residues picture” will be useful later in analyzing
congruences.

Lemma 13.1

(Generalized residues criterion)
.

Fix a finite set of pairs
(\alpha_{i},k_{i})_{i=1}^{t}
with

\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
and
k_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
. Define

R(q)=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\frac{k_{i}}{1-\alpha_{i}q},\qquad S(q)=-\,qR(q)=\sum_{i=1}^{t}\frac{k_{i}\alpha_{i}q}{1-\alpha_{i}q}.

Then there exists an analytic germ
A(q)
at
q=0
with
A(0)=1
and

\sigma(A)=S
, given explicitly by

A(q)=\prod_{i=1}^{t}(1-\alpha_{i}q)^{-k_{i}}.

Conversely, every such finite product satisfies

\sigma(A)=\sum_{i}\tfrac{k_{i}\alpha_{i}q}{1-\alpha_{i}q}
.
The ghost coefficients are

s_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{t}k_{i}\,\alpha_{i}^{n}\qquad(n\geq 1).

Remark 13.2
.

If all
k_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
, then
A\in K(q)
is rational.
If all
k_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}
, then
A
is algebraic over

K(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{t})(q)
.
For arbitrary complex
k_{i}
,
A
is typically transcendental, though

\sigma(A)
remains rational.
Thus Lemma
12.1
is the special case of
Lemma
13.1
with integer residues.

Proposition 13.3

(Additivity)
.

If
A=\prod_{j=1}^{r}A_{j}
with each
A_{j}(0)=1
and ghosts

S_{j}=\sigma(A_{j})=\sum_{n\geq 1}s_{j,n}q^{n}
, then

\sigma(A)=\sum_{j=1}^{r}\sigma(A_{j}),\qquad s_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{r}s_{j,n}\ \ (n\geq 1).

Corollary 13.4

(Ghosts are linear recurrence sequences)
.

Let
s_{n}=\sum_{i=1}^{t}k_{i}\alpha_{i}^{n}
with
k_{i},\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
.
Then
(s_{n})_{n\geq 0}
satisfies a linear recurrence with constant
coefficients: if

P(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{t}(x-\alpha_{i})=x^{t}-c_{1}x^{t-1}-\cdots-c_{t},

then

s_{n+t}=c_{1}s_{n+t-1}+\cdots+c_{t}s_{n}\qquad(n\geq 0).

In particular:

•

If the
\alpha_{i}
are algebraic, then
c_{j}\in\mathbb{Q}(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{t})
.

•

If the
\alpha_{i}
are algebraic integers and the multiset

\{\alpha_{i}\}
is Galois-stable, then
c_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Remark 13.5

(Irreducible factors)
.

By Proposition
13.3
, if

A=\prod_{j}A_{j}
is decomposed into factors, then the ghost of
A
is the
sum of the ghosts of the factors. Thus one can analyze
S=\sigma(A)

componentwise: each pole
\alpha
contributes a ghost component

s_{n}^{(\alpha)}=k\alpha^{n}
, and the full ghost is the sum.

Transition.

Sections
12
and
13
give two
complementary views of the image of
\sigma
: the classical trichotomy
(rational, polynomial, algebraic) and the residues picture (exponential sums
with integer, rational, or arbitrary residues). In particular, the

algebraic case
corresponds to rational residues
k_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}
,
hence to ghosts that are linear recurrence sequences with rational
coefficients. It is precisely this intermediate situation that will be
ruled out by congruence conditions: Dold
+
forces the residues to be
integers, collapsing the algebraic case back into the rational one.

13.1
Integral refinements and effective tests

The trichotomy admits natural refinements in the
S
–integral setting.
Let
K
be a number field with ring of integers
\mathcal{O}_{K}
, and

S
a finite set of prime ideals.

Corollary 13.6

(
S
–integral variants)
.

1.

(
Rational case
) In Theorem
12.1
, if the
data can be chosen with
k_{i}\in\mathcal{O}_{K}[S^{-1}]
and the

K
–Galois orbit of
\{\alpha_{i}\}
integral outside
S
, then

A(q)\in K(q)\cap\Lambda(\mathcal{O}_{K}[S^{-1}])
and the ghost sequence
satisfies norm–tower (Euler) congruences outside

.

2.

(
Algebraic case
) In Theorem
12.4
, if

F\in\Lambda(\mathcal{O}_{K}[S^{-1}])
, then

S=\sigma(F)
satisfies norm–tower (Euler) congruences at all
\mathfrak{p}\notin S
.

Decision procedure.

Given
S(q)=\sum_{n\geq 1}s_{n}q^{n}\in qK[[q]]
, one can decide its type as follows:

•

If only finitely many
s_{n}
are nonzero, then
S
is polynomial and

is exponential of a polynomial
(Prop.
12.3
); transcendental unless
S\equiv 0
.

•

If

and its partial fractions. If all

is rational
(Lemma

); otherwise not.

•

If

is logarithmic on
the minimal curve of

(Theorem

Examples.

1.

Polynomial:

gives

, transcendental over

.

2.

Rational:

gives

with integer residues
(3,1)
, so

is rational and
(s_{n})
a linear recurrence, periodic mod any modulus.

3.

Algebraic:

defined by

is algebraic but not rational;

is algebraic, and Theorem

shows

These refinements and examples illustrate each branch of the classification,
and show how

–integrality and congruence tests give effective

a practical check for integrality of the residues.

14
Hadamard closure via
\sigma
and consequences for the Witt product

Remark.

Hadamard closure for Witt vectors is well known, but the present
formulation simplifies its description considerably.
At the level of Euler coefficients the product rule is immediate,
whereas in the classical literature (cf. Roitman) one typically works
directly with the coefficients of
A(q)
, leading to complicated
explicit formulas.
Thus the ghost–Euler language makes closure both more transparent
and easier to apply.

Recall from Theorem
3.1
that over a
\mathbb{Q}
-algebra
R
we have a ring isomorphism

\sigma:\ (\Lambda(R),\boxplus,\boxtimes)\ \xrightarrow{\ \cong\ }\ (qR[[q]],+,\odot),

where
\boxplus
is ordinary multiplication of series on
\Lambda(R)
,
\boxtimes
is the transported big-Witt product, and
\odot
is the Hadamard (termwise) product on
qR[[q]]
(so we never use the Cauchy product on the image side).
Thus for
A,B\in\Lambda(R)
with
S=\sigma(A)
and
T=\sigma(B)
we have

\sigma(A\boxtimes B)=S\odot T\qquad\text{and}\qquad\log(A\boxtimes B)=-\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{s_{n}t_{n}}{n}\,q^{n}.

We now record closure properties of
\odot
for polynomial/rational/algebraic classes and translate them to
\boxtimes
.

Polynomials

Proposition 14.1

(Polynomials)
.

If
S,T\in qK[q]
then
S\odot T\in qK[q]
with
\deg(S\odot T)\leq\min(\deg S,\deg T)
. Consequently,

A\boxtimes B=\exp\!\Big(-\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{s_{n}t_{n}}{n}\,q^{n}\Big)

is the exponential of a polynomial (hence transcendental over
K(q)
unless
S\odot T\equiv 0
). If either input polynomial is zero, then
S\odot T\equiv 0
and
A\boxtimes B\equiv 1
is rational.

Proof.

If
S=\sum_{n=1}^{N}s_{n}q^{n}
and
T=\sum_{n=1}^{M}t_{n}q^{n}
, then
S\odot T=\sum_{n=1}^{\min\{N,M\}}s_{n}t_{n}q^{n}
. The description of
A\boxtimes B
follows from
\sigma^{-1}(U)=\exp(-\sum u_{n}q^{n}/n)
for
U=\sum u_{n}q^{n}
(Theorem
3.1
) applied to
U=S\odot T
.
∎

Rationals

For
m\geq 0
and
\alpha\in K
, set the rational kernel

\mathcal{K}_{\alpha,m}(q):=\frac{\alpha q}{(1-\alpha q)^{m+1}}=\sum_{n\geq 1}\binom{n+m-1}{m}\alpha^{n}q^{n}.

Note that
\{\binom{n+m-1}{m}\}_{m=0}^{M}
spans the
K
-vector space of polynomials in
n
of degree
\leq M
. Thus any rational ghost with poles of order
\leq M
is a
K
-linear combination of
\mathcal{K}_{\alpha,m}
with
m\leq M
.

Theorem 14.2

(Rational
\odot
rational is rational)
.

If
S,T\in qK(q)
, then
S\odot T\in qK(q)
. More explicitly, if

S(q)=\sum_{i}\sum_{m=0}^{M_{i}}c_{i,m}\,\mathcal{K}_{\alpha_{i},m}(q),\qquad T(q)=\sum_{j}\sum_{\ell=0}^{L_{j}}d_{j,\ell}\,\mathcal{K}_{\beta_{j},\ell}(q),

then

(S\odot T)(q)=\sum_{i,j}\sum_{m,\ell}c_{i,m}d_{j,\ell}\,\mathcal{K}_{\alpha_{i}\beta_{j},\,m+\ell}(q)\ \in\ qK(q).

Consequently, if
S=\sigma(A)
and
T=\sigma(B)
are rational, then
\sigma(A\boxtimes B)
is rational, and

A\boxtimes B=\prod_{i,j}(1-\alpha_{i}\beta_{j}q)^{-K_{ij}},\qquad K_{ij}=\sum_{m,\ell}c_{i,m}d_{j,\ell}\,\binom{m+\ell}{m}.

In particular, when
S=\sum_{i}k_{i}\,\frac{\alpha_{i}q}{1-\alpha_{i}q}
and
T=\sum_{j}\ell_{j}\,\frac{\beta_{j}q}{1-\beta_{j}q}
(i.e. all
M_{i}=L_{j}=0
), we have

and collecting exponents in the Euler product. The exponents

Corollary 14.10

(Radius of convergence and coefficient asymptotics)
.

Assume an embedding
K\hookrightarrow\mathbb{C}
. With
S,T
as above, write

U(q):=(S\odot T)(q)=\sum_{n\geq 1}u_{n}q^{n}.

Then the radius of convergence
\rho(U)
satisfies

\rho(U)^{-1}=\max_{i,j}|\alpha_{i}\beta_{j}|,

and if
\Gamma:=\{(i,j):|\alpha_{i}\beta_{j}|=\rho(U)^{-1}\}
is the set of dominant pairs, then

u_{n}=\sum_{(i,j)\in\Gamma}\ \sum_{m,\ell}c_{i,m}d_{j,\ell}\,\binom{m+\ell}{m}\binom{n+m+\ell-1}{m+\ell}\,(\alpha_{i}\beta_{j})^{n}\;+\;O\!\big(n^{M-1}R^{n}\big),

where

and any

.
In particular, for each fixed dominant pair and indices
(m,\ell)
,

\binom{n+m+\ell-1}{m+\ell}=\frac{n^{m+\ell}}{(m+\ell)!}+O(n^{m+\ell-1}),

so the leading constant for that contribution is
\dfrac{c_{i,m}d_{j,\ell}}{m!\,\ell!}
.

Proof.

Kernel coefficients are
\binom{n+m-1}{m}\alpha^{n}

\binom{n+m-1}{m}\binom{n+\ell-1}{\ell}=\binom{n+m+\ell-1}{m+\ell}\binom{m+\ell}{m}

gives the explicit coefficient formula and hence the dominant exponential rate
\max_{i,j}|\alpha_{i}\beta_{j}|

\binom{n+r}{r}=\frac{n^{r}}{r!}+O(n^{r-1})
and collecting subdominant pairs into the

term.
∎

Proof.

The Witt–Hadamard calculus developed above is not only a structural
dictionary but also a tool for operations. In applications, one frequently
needs to multiply zeta functions or orbit-counting series. On the
\sigma
-side
this corresponds to the Hadamard product of ghosts, while on the Witt side
it becomes the
\boxtimes
-product of Euler products. Understanding which
classes of series are closed under Hadamard is therefore central: it tells
us when rational or algebraic input data remain in the same class after
combination, when transcendence is forced, and how arithmetic properties
such as Dold or norm–tower (Euler) congruences behave under these operations.
The results of this section record these closure laws and translate them
into precise statements about Witt products, paving the way for dynamical
applications in later sections.

15
Dynamics via sigma: Artin–Mazur zeta, products, and congruences

Remark.

The Artin–Mazur zeta function of a dynamical system

\zeta_{f}(q)=\exp(\sum_{n\geq 1}|\mathrm{Fix}(f^{n})|q^{n}/n)

is a standard construction.
It is well known (see Byszewski–Cornelissen–Miles–Ward
[
5
]
)
that the coefficients
|\mathrm{Fix}(f^{n})|
form a Dold sequence,
so the Dold congruences characterize realizable fixed–point data.
What is new here is that we place this observation inside the
sigma–Hadamard framework: the ghost map, Witt operations,
and refined congruences (
S
–integrality, Dold
+
, rigidity)
apply directly to dynamical zeta functions, allowing structural
results for Euler products to be transported to the dynamical setting.

This section interprets
\sigma
and
\boxtimes
in the dynamical context: the Artin–Mazur zeta function
[
1
]
, Dold congruences, products of systems, and Frobenius ladders for periodic points.

Let

be a self-map for which each

has finitely many fixed points of

\mathrm{Fix}_{n}(f):=\#\mathrm{Fix}(f^{n})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0},\qquad\zeta_{f}(q):=\exp\!\Big(\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{\mathrm{Fix}_{n}(f)}{n}\,q^{n}\Big)\in 1+q\mathbb{Z}[[q]].

(For compact dynamical systems this is the Artin–Mazur zeta; for symbolic systems or finite-state maps it is the usual dynamical zeta.)

Proposition 15.1

(Sigma of a dynamical zeta)
.

For
A(q):=\zeta_{f}(q)
one has

\sigma(A)=-\,q\,\frac{A^{\prime}(q)}{A(q)}=-\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathrm{Fix}_{n}(f)\,q^{n}.

Proof.

By definition
\log A=\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{\mathrm{Fix}_{n}(f)}{n}q^{n}
. Differentiate and multiply by
-q
.
∎

Proposition 15.2

(Orbit decomposition and Dold congruences)
.

Let

be the number of

\mathrm{Fix}_{n}(f)=\sum_{d\mid n}d\,P_{d}(f)\qquad(n\geq 1).

Consequently,

4.1

\zeta_{f}(q)=\prod_{d\geq 1}(1-q^{d})^{-P_{d}(f)}\in\Lambda(\mathbb{Z}).

(\mathrm{Fix}_{n}(f))
always lies in
\sigma(\Lambda(\mathbb{Z}))

Proof.

Each orbit of exact length

contributes

precisely when

4.1
.
∎

(Products of systems = Witt products)

For maps

\boxed{\ \zeta_{f\times g}=\zeta_{f}\ \boxtimes\ \zeta_{g}\ }.

The fixed points of

are pairs of fixed points of

and

15.1
and the ring isomorphism Theorem

.
∎

The following are equivalent:

This is Theorem

Remark 15.9

(Algebraic vs transcendental zetas)
.

By Theorem
12.4
,
\zeta_{f}
is algebraic iff
-\,\sigma(\zeta_{f})/q
is a logarithmic derivative of an algebraic function on its ambient algebraic curve. This separates algebraic/rational cases (e.g. SFTs, toral automorphisms) from transcendental ones (e.g. many substitution systems), and is stable under products with rational zetas by Theorem
14.4
. Thus the
\sigma
-framework provides a structural criterion distinguishing systems with rational/algebraic zetas from those with genuinely transcendental zetas.

Worked examples

Example A (Fibonacci SFT,
2\times 2
).

Let
A=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\[2.0pt]
1&0\end{pmatrix}
with eigenvalues

\phi=\tfrac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}
and
\psi=\tfrac{1-\sqrt{5}}{2}
. For the subshift of finite
type with adjacency matrix
A
,

\mathrm{Fix}_{n}=\mathrm{tr}(A^{n})=\phi^{\,n}+\psi^{\,n}\quad(n\geq 1),\qquad\zeta(q)=\frac{1}{\det(I-qA)}=\frac{1}{1-q-q^{2}}=\frac{1}{(1-\phi q)(1-\psi q)}.

By Proposition
15.1
,
\sigma(\zeta)=-\sum_{n\geq 1}\mathrm{Fix}_{n}\,q^{n}
.
Set
R:=\zeta^{\prime}/\zeta=-\sigma(\zeta)/q
. A direct computation gives

R(q)=\frac{1+2q}{1-q-q^{2}}=\frac{-1}{q-\phi^{-1}}+\frac{-1}{q-\psi^{-1}},

so the poles are simple at
q=\phi^{-1},\psi^{-1}
with residues
-1
each. In the
language of Theorem
12.1
this means the eigen–residues are

k_{\phi}=k_{\psi}=1
at eigenvalues
\alpha=\phi,\psi
, and indeed

\zeta(q)=\prod_{\alpha\in\{\phi,\psi\}}(1-\alpha q)^{-k_{\alpha}},\qquad\sigma(\zeta)(q)=-\sum_{\alpha\in\{\phi,\psi\}}k_{\alpha}\,\frac{\alpha q}{1-\alpha q}=-\sum_{n\geq 1}(\phi^{\,n}+\psi^{\,n})q^{n}.

Thus
\mathrm{Fix}_{n}=\phi^{n}+\psi^{n}
matches the linear recurrence structure predicted by Theorem
15.4
.

Progression zeros.
For every
a\geq 1
,

\zeta(q)^{2^{a}}\equiv\zeta(q^{2^{a}})\pmod{2^{a}}
by Theorem
15.6
; hence

[q^{n}]\zeta(q)^{2^{a}}\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2^{a})
whenever
2^{a}\nmid n
.

Example B (Circle endomorphism
x\mapsto 2x\pmod{1}
).

Here
\mathrm{Fix}_{n}=2^{n}-1
, so

\zeta(q)=\exp\Big(\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{2^{n}-1}{n}q^{n}\Big)=\frac{1-q}{1-2q}=(1-2q)^{-1}(1-q)^{+1}.

Thus
R(q)=\zeta^{\prime}(q)/\zeta(q)=\dfrac{2}{1-2q}-\dfrac{1}{1-q}
, and in residue form

R(q)=\frac{-1}{q-\tfrac{1}{2}}+\frac{+1}{q-1}.

Equivalently, in the eigen–residue parametrization of
Theorem
15.4
we have
(\alpha,k)=(2,+1)
and
(\alpha,k)=(1,-1)
,
and indeed

\zeta(q)=(1-2q)^{-1}(1-q)^{+1}.

Moreover, by Proposition
15.1
,

\sigma(\zeta)(q)=-\sum_{n\geq 1}(2^{n}-1)\,q^{n}=-\frac{2q}{1-2q}+\frac{q}{1-q},

which is the Hadamard sum of two rational kernels (Example of Theorem
14.2
).
The negative residue at
\alpha=1
corresponds exactly to the corrective factor

in
\zeta

Progression zeros.
For each

,

\zeta(q)^{2^{a}}\equiv\zeta(q^{2^{a}})\pmod{2^{a}}

[q^{n}]\zeta(q)^{2^{a}}\equiv 0\ (\mathrm{mod}\ 2^{a})
whenever

These examples illustrate: (i) the residues test (Theorem

);
(ii) pole locations at reciprocals of eigenvalues; and (iii) prime-power progression

Remark 15.10

(What these examples add)
.

For systems such as the Fibonacci shift or the doubling map it is already
well known that the dynamical zeta function is rational, with poles determined
by eigenvalues and entropy given by the spectral radius.
What the sigma–Dold framework contributes is an additional arithmetic layer.
The fixed–point counts automatically satisfy Dold congruences, imposing exact
divisibility conditions along prime–power progressions.
Residue expansions give integral weights to poles and zeros, explaining
corrective factors such as the
(1-q)
term in the doubling map.
And under products of systems, the Witt product description shows that not
only entropy but also the entire congruence structure is preserved.
Thus even in these basic cases, the sigma–Hadamard formalism enriches the
dynamical picture by adding
p
–adic and arithmetic rigidity.

Remark 16.2

(Scope)
.

The theorem applies whenever the pole set of
R(q)=-S(q)/q
is cyclotomic. In particular, it yields a quick rationality test for many dynamical zetas whose residues concentrate at cyclotomic points.

Recall from Section
12
that images of

\sigma(A)=-qA^{\prime}/A
fall into a trichotomy: rational, polynomial, or
algebraic, depending on whether
R(q)=-A^{\prime}(q)/A
has integer residues,
represents a polynomial differential, or is the logarithmic derivative of
an algebraic function. Section
13
rephrased this in
the finite residues picture: every ghost sequence is a linear recurrence

s_{n}=\sum_{i}k_{i}\alpha_{i}^{n}
, with
k_{i}\in\mathbb{C}
, and the cases are
distinguished by the arithmetic of the residues:

k_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}\;\;\Rightarrow\;\;A\text{ rational},\qquad k_{i}\in\mathbb{Q}\;\;\Rightarrow\;\;A\text{ algebraic},\qquad k_{i}\in\mathbb{C}\;\;\Rightarrow\;\;A\text{ transcendental}.

Thus the
algebraic case
corresponds precisely to having rational but
nonintegral residues, i.e. ghosts that are linear recurrence sequences
with rational coefficients. In this section we prove the rigidity theorem:
under the Dold
+
congruences, this intermediate algebraic case cannot
occur. More concretely, if
A
is algebraic and its ghost satisfies
Dold
+
, then all residues are forced to be integers and
A
is in fact
rational.

Roadmap.

We start from a series
A(q)\in 1+q\mathbb{Z}[[q]]
and its “ghost”

S(q)=-\,\frac{qA^{\prime}(q)}{A(q)}=\sum_{n\geq 1}s_{n}q^{n}.

The Dold
+
congruences say that along every
p
-power tower
the differences
s_{p^{a}m}-s_{p^{a-1}m}
are divisible by
p^{a+1}
(for
(m,p)=1
).

Step 1:
from these congruences we deduce a simple divisibility
on the Euler exponents
f(n)
in the Euler product

A(q)=\prod_{d}(1-q^{d})^{f(d)}
.

Step 2:
assuming
A
is algebraic, we show there can be

no pole of
R(q)=A^{\prime}/A
at a non-cyclotomic point
q=\beta^{-1}

(i.e. with
\beta
not a root of unity). This is the only subtle step; we give
an elementary case-split argument using reductions mod a good prime and, when

\beta\in\mathbb{Q}
, a
p
-adic lifting-the-exponent estimate.

Step 3:
with only cyclotomic poles left, earlier cyclotomic-residue
facts kick in:
R
has only simple poles at roots of unity with
integer residues and no polynomial part. Hence
R
is rational and integrates
to a finite Euler product; thus
A
is rational.

Setup and notation

Write the Euler product with (a priori rational) exponents

A(q)=\prod_{d\geq 1}(1-q^{d})^{f(d)},\qquad f(d)\in\mathbb{Q},

so that the ghost coefficients satisfy

s_{n}=\sum_{d\mid n}d\,f(d)\quad(n\geq 1).

We say
S
satisfies
Dold
+
if for every prime
p
, every
a\geq 1
,
and every
m\geq 1
with
(m,p)=1
,

s_{p^{a}m}\ \equiv\ s_{p^{a-1}m}\pmod{p^{\,a+1}}.

If
A
is algebraic over
\mathbb{Q}(q)
then
R(q):=A^{\prime}(q)/A(q)
is algebraic
and has only finitely many poles on
\mathbb{P}^{1}
.

Lemma 16.3

(Euler-exponent divisibility forced by Dold
+
)
.

Fix a prime
p
. If Dold
+
holds at
p
, then for all
a\geq 1
and all

m\geq 1
with

,

Proof.

For
(m,p)=1
,

s_{p^{a}m}-s_{p^{a-1}m}=\sum_{d\mid m}(p^{a}d)\,f(p^{a}d).

By Dold

; divide by

\sum_{d\mid m}d\,f(p^{a}d)\ \equiv\ 0\pmod{p}.

Define

Then

Lemma 16.4

(Non-cyclotomic poles contradict Dold
+
)
.

Assume
A\in 1+q\mathbb{Z}[[q]]
is algebraic over
\mathbb{Q}(q)

and
S=-\,qA^{\prime}/A
satisfies Dold
+
. Then
R(q)=A^{\prime}/A
has no pole
at any
q=\beta^{-1}
with
\beta
not a root of unity.

Proof.

Suppose to the contrary that
q=\beta^{-1}
is a pole of
R
,
with
\beta
not a root of unity.
Local theory gives a finite principal part

R(q)=\sum_{j=1}^{t}\frac{c_{j}}{(1-\beta q)^{j}}+H(q),

with
c_{t}\neq 0
,

algebraic and holomorphic nearby.
Multiplying by
-q
and extracting coefficients yields

s_{n}=P(n)\,\beta^{n}+b_{n}\qquad(n\geq 1),

where
P(n)
is a nonzero polynomial (deg
t-1
) with coefficients in

K=\mathbb{Q}(\beta,c_{1},\dots,c_{t})
, and
(b_{n})
are coefficients of
-qH(q)
.

We inspect the differences

\Delta_{a}=s_{p^{a}}-s_{p^{a-1}}.

Case A:
\beta\notin\mathbb{Q}
.

By excluding finitely many primes we may pick a rational prime
p

and a prime
\mathfrak{p}\mid p
of
K
such that

•

\beta
reduces to
\overline{\beta}\in\mathbb{F}_{p^{f}}\setminus\mathbb{F}_{p}
with

,

•

coefficients of

are
\mathfrak{p}
-integral, and

•

\overline{P}(0)\neq 0
.

Such primes exist in abundance (by Čebotarev’s theorem); see, e.g.,
[
10

Reducing mod
\mathfrak{p}
gives

\overline{\Delta}_{a}=\overline{P}(0)\,(\overline{\beta}^{p^{a}}-\overline{\beta}^{p^{a-1}})+(\overline{b}_{p^{a}}-\overline{b}_{p^{a-1}}).

Since
\overline{\beta}^{p^{a}}\neq\overline{\beta}^{p^{a-1}}
for all

(Frobenius

Hence

for infinitely many

contradicting Dold

Non-cancellation at a Chebotarëv prime.

Let the (finitely many) non-cyclotomic poles be
\beta_{1}=\beta,\beta_{2},\dots,\beta_{t}
with residues

be the compositum of the fields

\mathfrak{p}
of

that are unramified in

such that

\mathrm{Frob}_{\mathfrak{p}}\ \text{acts nontrivially on }\mathbb{Q}(\beta)\quad\text{(so }f(\mathfrak{p}|\mathbb{Q}(\beta))\geq 2\text{),}

for

(i.e.
\mathfrak{p}
splits completely in those fields).
Avoiding the finite set of primes dividing the denominators of the

r_{j}
are
\mathfrak{p}
-units.

Reducing modulo
\mathfrak{p}
, we have for

For

the Frobenius is the identity on

\overline{\beta_{j}}^{\,p}=\overline{\beta_{j}}
and

\overline{\beta_{j}}^{\,p^{a}}-\overline{\beta_{j}}^{\,p^{a-1}}=0

For

\overline{\Delta_{a}}=\overline{r_{\beta}}\,\big(\overline{\beta}^{\,p^{a}}-\overline{\beta}^{\,p^{a-1}}\big)\neq 0\quad\text{in }\mathcal{O}_{L}/\mathfrak{p}.

Thus
\Delta_{a}\not\equiv 0\pmod{\mathfrak{p}}
(already for

congruence which requires

for all

. Again, Dold itself suffices.
This completes Case A.

Case B:
\beta\in\mathbb{Q}^{\times}

Write
\beta=u/v
in lowest terms.
Choose an odd prime
p\nmid uv(u-v)
with coefficients
p

(by Lemma

Then

\Delta_{a}
vanishes mod

To control valuations, write

\beta^{p^{a}}-\beta^{p^{a-1}}=\beta^{p^{a-1}}\bigl(\beta^{(p-1)p^{a-1}}-1\bigr).

By the lifting-the-exponent lemma (LTE) for odd

Since

After excluding finitely many

p

Thus the tail difference

Since the principal term of

Thus

, while Dold

,
a contradiction.

∎

Theorem 16.8

(Dold
+
rigidity: algebraic
\Rightarrow
rational)
.

Let
A(q)\in 1+q\mathbb{Z}[[q]]
be algebraic over
\mathbb{Q}(q)
,
and suppose
S=-\,qA^{\prime}(q)/A(q)
satisfies Dold
+
.
Then
A(q)
is a rational function. More precisely,

R(q)=\frac{A^{\prime}(q)}{A(q)}\in\mathbb{Q}(q)

has only simple poles at roots of unity, with integer residues and no polynomial part;
consequently

A(q)=\prod_{i=1}^{t}(1-\alpha_{i}q)^{-k_{i}}\in\mathbb{Q}(q),\qquad\alpha_{i}\ \text{roots of unity},\ k_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}.

Proof.

By algebraicity,
R=A^{\prime}/A
is algebraic and regular at
q=0
.
By Lemma
16.4
, all its poles lie at roots of unity.
There are finitely many such poles.
At a primitive
m
th root
\zeta_{m}
, the formal identity

R(q)=-\sum_{d\geq 1}\frac{d\,f(d)\,q^{d-1}}{1-q^{d}}

shows the residue is
-\sum_{k\geq 1}f(mk)
. This is an integer because Dold
+
implies the plain Dold congruences,
and Theorem
4.1
ensures that all Euler exponents
f(d)
lie in

\mathbb{Z}
. (Over number fields this appeal is replaced by the ideal–Dold
condition of Theorem
8.2

Subtracting these finitely many principal parts
and the (possibly empty) polynomial part at
\infty
produces an algebraic
function holomorphic on
\mathbb{P}^{1}
, hence constant; and the value at
q=0

is
0
, so it is identically zero. Thus

is exactly that finite partial
fraction expansion. Integrating reconstructs the claimed Euler product form.
∎

Lemma 16.10

(Cyclotomic reconstruction under Dold

(d) Prime–wise cyclotomic criterion.

Let

A(q)=\prod_{i=1}^{r}(1-q^{M_{i}})^{-k_{i}},\qquad\gcd(M_{i},M_{j})=1\ (i\neq j).

Then
S=-qA^{\prime}/A
satisfies Dold
+

iff
for every prime
p
and every
i

with
p\mid M_{i}
one has
p\mid k_{i}
.
Equivalently, for a finite cyclotomic Euler product

A(q)=\prod_{m\in\mathcal{S}}\Phi_{m}(q)^{-E_{m}}\quad(E_{m}\in\mathbb{Z},\ \mathcal{S}\ \text{finite}),

Dold
+
holds
iff
for each prime
p
one has
p\mid E_{m}
for every
m\in\mathcal{S}

with
p\mid m
. In particular, the verification is completely prime–by–prime,
and is nothing but the ideal–Dold condition expressed in terms of cyclotomic
Euler factors.

Concluding remarks and further directions

Witt–vector perspective.

Via the standard isomorphism between the big Witt ring and

\Lambda(R)=1+qR[[q]]
, our three main results translate directly into Witt
language:

•

Norm–descent (Theorem
10.2
) shows that the
componentwise field norm on ghost components produces an integral Witt vector
over
\mathbb{Z}
, with coordinates given by Dirichlet convolution of the
conjugate exponents.

•

S
–integrality is characterized by norm–tower (Euler) congruences
on the ghost sequence (Corollary
14.7
).

•

The Dold
+
rigidity theorem (Theorem
16.8
) asserts that
algebraic big Witt vectors over
\mathbb{Z}
are necessarily rational.

These statements appear to be new in the Witt setting, and complement the
classical necklace/Witt framework of Metropolis–Rota
[
14
]
.

Further work.

Several natural directions remain. One is a systematic study of the

Dold congruence ring
under addition and Hadamard product, where the
ideal–Dold condition of Section
16
suggests a
natural prime–by–prime structure. Another is a more detailed analysis of
norm–descent and Frobenius ladders as operations on general Witt rings.
Finally, it would be of interest to investigate whether the rigidity
phenomena established here can be pushed beyond algebraicity, leading to
transcendence or arithmetic results for more general classes of Euler
products. We hope to return to these questions in future work.

Appendix A. Ramanujan sums

For
m\geq 1
define the Ramanujan sum
[
12
]

c_{m}(n)\;=\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k\leq m\\
(k,m)=1\end{subarray}}e^{2\pi ikn/m}.

Standard facts:

•

Multiplicativity in
m
: if
(m_{1},m_{2})=1
, then
c_{m_{1}m_{2}}(n)=c_{m_{1}}(n)c_{m_{2}}(n)
.

•

Closed form
:

c_{m}(n)=\sum_{d\mid(m,n)}\mu\!\Big(\frac{m}{d}\Big)\,d=\mu\!\Big(\frac{m}{(m,n)}\Big)\,\frac{\varphi(m)}{\varphi\!\big(m/(m,n)\big)}.

•

Orthogonality/averages
: for each fixed
m
,

\frac{1}{\varphi(m)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k\leq m\\
(k,m)=1\end{subarray}}e^{2\pi ikn/m}=\frac{c_{m}(n)}{\varphi(m)}.

Ramanujan expansion and inversion (finite,
m
–periodic case).

If
f:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C}
is
m
–periodic, then there exist unique coefficients

a_{d}
for
d\mid m
such that

f(n)=\sum_{d\mid m}a_{d}\,c_{d}(n)\qquad(n\in\mathbb{Z}),

with the inversion formula

a_{d}=\frac{1}{m\,\varphi(d)}\sum_{n=1}^{m}f(n)\,c_{d}(n)\qquad(d\mid m).

In particular, if each
a_{d}\in\mathbb{Z}
, then all
f(n)\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Application to residues in Theorem
16.1
.

If
S(q)
has only simple poles at
q=\zeta^{-1}
(with
\zeta
a root of unity),
no polynomial part, and
S=\sigma(A)
with integer ghosts
s_{n}
, then grouping poles
by primitive order
d
gives

S(q)=-\sum_{d\in D}k_{d}\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\zeta\ \mathrm{primitive}\\
\zeta^{d}=1\end{subarray}}\frac{\zeta\,q}{1-\zeta q},

and extracting coefficients yields

s_{n}=-\sum_{d\in D}k_{d}\,c_{d}(n),\qquad k_{d}\in\mathbb{Z}.

The integrality follows from the Ramanujan expansion above.

Appendix B. Infinite Cyclotomic Products

Cyclotomic Ghosts

Let
A(q)=\prod_{n\geq 1}(1-q^{n})^{c(n)}\in 1+q\mathbb{Z}[[q]]
and write its cyclotomic factorization

A(q)=\prod_{m\geq 1}\Phi_{m}(q)^{e(m)}\quad\text{in }\mathbb{Q}[[q]].

Then the ghost coefficients
s(n)
of
S(q)=-\,qA^{\prime}(q)/A(q)=\sum_{n\geq 1}s(n)q^{n}
satisfy

s(n)\;=\;\sum_{m\geq 1}e(m)\,c_{m}(n),

where
c_{m}(n)
are Ramanujan sums. Equivalently,

S(q)\;=\;\sum_{m\geq 1}e(m)\,\Big(-\,q\,\frac{\Phi_{m}^{\prime}(q)}{\Phi_{m}(q)}\Big)\quad\text{and}\quad-\,q\,\frac{\Phi_{m}^{\prime}(q)}{\Phi_{m}(q)}=\sum_{n\geq 1}c_{m}(n)\,q^{n}\ \ (|q|<1).

Proof.

Since
\Phi_{m}(q)=\prod_{(k,m)=1}(1-\zeta_{m}^{k}q)
, we have

\log\Phi_{m}(q)=\sum_{(k,m)=1}\log(1-\zeta_{m}^{k}q)=-\sum_{r\geq 1}\frac{q^{r}}{r}\sum_{(k,m)=1}\zeta_{m}^{kr}=-\sum_{r\geq 1}\frac{c_{m}(r)}{r}\,q^{r},

so

-\,q\,\frac{\Phi_{m}^{\prime}(q)}{\Phi_{m}(q)}=-\,q\,\frac{d}{dq}\log\Phi_{m}(q)=\sum_{r\geq 1}c_{m}(r)\,q^{r}.

Now
\log A(q)=\sum_{m\geq 1}e(m)\log\Phi_{m}(q)
, hence

S(q)=-\,q\frac{A^{\prime}(q)}{A(q)}=-\,q\frac{d}{dq}\log A(q)=\sum_{m\geq 1}e(m)\Big(-\,q\,\frac{\Phi_{m}^{\prime}}{\Phi_{m}}\Big)=\sum_{r\geq 1}\Big(\sum_{m\geq 1}e(m)\,c_{m}(r)\Big)q^{r},

which gives
s(r)=\sum_{m\geq 1}e(m)\,c_{m}(r)
.
∎

Let
A(q)=\prod_{n\geq 1}(1-q^{n})^{c(n)}\in 1+q\mathbb{Z}[[q]]
. Writing

1-q^{n}=\prod_{m\mid n}\Phi_{m}(q),\qquad A(q)=\prod_{m\geq 1}\Phi_{m}(q)^{e(m)}\ \ \text{in }\mathbb{Q}[[q]],

the exponents are related by

e(m)=\sum_{k\geq 1}c(km),\qquad c(n)=\sum_{m\mid n}\mu\!\Big(\frac{n}{m}\Big)\,e(m).

Moreover, for any primitive
m
-th root of unity
\zeta_{m}
one has

\operatorname{ord}_{q=\zeta_{m}}A=e(m)
.

While considering these infinite cyclotomic products, a clarification is necessary:

Cyclotomic Product Convention

The first cyclotomic polynomial is
\Phi_{1}(z)=z-1
, but in our expansions
we prefer the factor
1-z
, since

(1-q)^{-1}=\sum_{n\geq 0}q^{n}\in 1+q\mathbb{Z}[[q]]
while

(q-1)^{-1}=-\sum_{n\geq 0}q^{n}
has constant
-1
.
Up to an overall sign, these are equivalent, so in Euler products we
always write
(1-q)^{-k}
in place of
\Phi_{1}(q)^{-k}
to keep the
normalization
a_{0}=1
.

Remark .13

(The
d=1
block and the sign)
.

Recall
\Phi_{1}(z)=z-1
, but we work with
1-z
so that expansions lie in
1+q\mathbb{Z}[[q]]
.
For the cyclotomic residue expansion of the ghost,

S(q)\;=\;-\sum_{d\in D}k_{d}\!\!\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\zeta\ \mathrm{primitive}\\
\zeta^{d}=1\end{subarray}}\frac{\zeta\,q}{1-\zeta q},

the
d=1
contribution is simply

-\;k_{1}\,\frac{q}{1-q}\;=\;-k_{1}\sum_{n\geq 1}q^{n},\qquad\text{since }c_{1}(n)=\sum_{\zeta^{1}=1}\zeta^{\,n}=1.

Thus, in the Ramanujan sum formula
s_{n}=\sum_{d}E_{d}\,c_{d}(n)
one has

s_{n}=E_{1}+\sum_{d>1}E_{d}\,c_{d}(n)
; matching coefficients with the
partial fractions shows
E_{1}=-k_{1}
(and in general
E_{d}=-k_{d}
).

At the
A
-level, the
d=1
block is written as
(1-q)^{-k_{1}}
rather than

\Phi_{1}(q)^{-k_{1}}=(q-1)^{-k_{1}}
:

(1-q)^{-k_{1}}=(-1)^{-k_{1}}\,\Phi_{1}(q)^{-k_{1}}.

The global constant
(-1)^{-k_{1}}
is harmless and absorbed by the
normalization
A(0)=1
, so the product

A(q)=\prod_{d\in D}\prod_{\zeta^{d}=1\ \mathrm{prim}}(1-\zeta q)^{-k_{d}}

is the correct (normalized) preimage with
a_{0}=1
.
