Iconicity Emerges From Language Experience: Evidence From Japanese Ideophones and Their English Equivalents
Authors: Hinano Iida, Kimi Akita
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIconicity is a relationship of resemblance between the form and meaning of a sign. Compelling evidence from diverse areas of the cognitive sciences suggests that iconicity plays a pivotal role in the processing, memory, learning, and evolution of both spoken and signed language, indicating that iconicity is a general property of language. However, the language‐specific aspect of iconicity, illustrated by the fact that the meanings of ideophones in an unfamiliar language are hard to guess (e.g., shigeshige ‘staring at something’ in Japanese), remains to be fully investigated. In the present study, native speakers of Japanese and English rated the iconicity and familiarity of Japanese ideophones (e.g., gatagata ‘rattling’, butsubutsu ‘murmuring’) and their English equivalents (e.g., rattle, murmur). Two main findings emerged: (1) individuals generally perceived their native language as more iconic than their non‐native language, replicating the previous findings in signed language, and (2) the familiarity of words in their native language boosted their perceived iconicity. These findings shed a light on the language‐specific, subjective, and acquired nature of iconicity.Keywords: Iconicity, Ideophones, Language‐specificity, Familiarity, Japanese, English

Introduction

Iconicity as a general property of language

The relationship between form and meaning in language is not invariably arbitrary. The current study investigates iconicity—traditionally defined as the resemblance between form and meaning (Peirce, 1932)—as a major subtype of nonarbitrariness in language. It focuses on the acquired aspect of the sense of iconicity, which may emerge from our exposure to a particular language.

Iconic form–meaning mappings are pervasive in various aspects of signed and spoken language (Dingemanse et al., 2015; Perniss, Thompson, & Vigliocco, 2010). In the phenomenon called “sound symbolism,” certain phonemes can be iconically associated with certain meanings. For example, the associations of /i/ and /a/ with smallness and largeness, respectively, are arguably partly attributed to the oral cavity size involved in the articulation of these vowels (Sapir, 1929). English phonesthemes, submorphemic elements that are conventionally associated with particular meanings (Bergen, 2004), are also often iconic. The phonestheme cr‐ of crack, creak, and crunch expresses a noisy impact with its abrupt amplitude change (Rhodes, 1995). Ideophones, including onomatopoeia (e.g., bowwow, cock‐a‐doodle‐doo), which abound in many of the world's languages, are examples of word‐level iconicity. They depict sensory information, such as sound, motion, texture, and emotion, in an imitative fashion (e.g., kirakira ‘twinkling’ in Japanese). Recent extensive cross‐linguistic comparisons of the world's languages have also found iconic form–meaning correspondences in basic vocabulary items (Blasi, Wichmann, Hammarström, Stadler, & Christiansen, 2016; Erben Johansson, Anikin, Carling, & Holmer, 2020; Joo, 2020). For example, texture words for roughness in Indo‐European languages often contain trilled /r/ (Winter, Sóskuthy, Perlman, & Dingemanse, 2022). English shape nouns, such as round object names (e.g., softball, globe, tomato), tend to contain rounded vowels (e.g., /oʊ/) and bilabial consonants (e.g., /m/, /b/), contrasting with spiky object names (e.g., spike, fork, porcupine) containing obstruents (e.g., /k/) and unrounded front vowels (e.g., /ɪ/) (Sidhu, Westbury, Hollis, & Pexman, 2021). Furthermore, Haiman (1980) demonstrates that syntactic structure may be iconically motivated, as illustrated by the contrast between kill and cause to die, in which the linear distance between morphemes (i.e., cause and die) corresponds to conceptual distance (i.e., indirectness of causation). This pervasive presence of iconicity indicates that it is “a general property of language” (Perniss et al., 2010).

Compelling evidence from diverse areas of the cognitive sciences suggests that iconicity plays a pivotal role in the processing (Sidhu, Khachatoorian, & Vigliocco, 2022), learning (Imai et al., 2015; Imai, Kita, Nagumo, & Okada, 2008; Kantartzis, Imai, & Kita, 2011), memory (Sidhu et al., 2023; Sonier, Poirier, Guitard, & Saint‐Aubin, 2020), and evolution of language (Imai & Kita, 2014; Akita & Imai, 2022). For example, Imai et al. (2008) showed that 3‐year‐olds were able to generalize novel verbs to the same manner of motion performed by a different actor when the sounds of the verbs were iconically associated with the manner (e.g., nosunosu‐suru ‘to walk heavily’ where /n/ expresses sluggishness and /s/ expresses friction), but they failed to generalize verbs that were not iconically associated with the manner of motion (e.g., nekeru ‘to walk heavily’).

Language‐specificity of iconicity

Research on iconicity is characterized by the diverse approaches to its definition and operationalization (Motamedi, Little, Nielsen, & Sulik, 2019). Notably, iconicity has often been assumed to be universal and accessible to non‐native users of the language. For example, Bosworth and Emmorey (2010) operationalized iconicity by asking hearing nonsigners to rate iconicity. Some studies that collect native speakers’ subjective iconicity ratings of numerous words in spoken and signed language also assume the universal nature of iconicity, as is evident in the following definitions of iconicity they used in their experimental instructions:

“You would be able to guess this sign's meaning even if you did not know BSL.” (Vinson, Cormier, Denmark, Schembri, & Vigliocco, 2008)

“You might be able to guess these words’ meanings even if you did not know English.” (Perry, Perlman, & Lupyan, 2015; Thompson, Akita, & Do, 2020; Winter, Perlman, Perry, & Lupyan, 2017)

“How accurately (on a 0–100 scale) a ‘space alien’ could guess the meaning of each word based only on its sound.” (Perry et al., 2015)

“Even people who do not speak any English can get a sense of the meaning of these words.” (McLean, Dunn, & Dingemanse, 2023)

However, other studies argue that iconicity is inherently subjective and dependent on interpretative processes and not always accessible to non‐native users of the language (Dingemanse, 2019; Occhino, Anible, Wilkinson, & Morford, 2017; Wilcox, 2004; Winter, Woodin, & Perlman, 2025). A good example of this is found in ideophones in spoken language. Some ideophones exhibit formal similarities across languages. For instance, onomatopoeic (i.e., sound‐imitating) ideophones representing a cat's cry in many languages share common phonological features, such as nasal consonants and long low vowels or diphthongs (e.g., English meow, French miaou, Russian myau, Chinese miao, Japanese nyaa, Korean yaong). However, many languages have considerable numbers of ideophones that do not seem to be apparently iconic (Dingemanse, 2012). This argument is empirically supported by Iwasaki, Vinson, and Vigliocco (2007), who conducted a study in which native speakers of Japanese and English were asked to rate Japanese ideophones for laughing (e.g., geragera ‘laughing loudly’, kusukusu ‘giggling’) and walking (e.g., petapeta ‘slapping’, yochiyochi ‘toddling’) on 14 semantic differential scales, such as “Graceful–Vulgar” for laughing and “Big strides–Small strides” for walking. English‐speaking participants failed to accurately guess the semantic contrast between voiced and voiceless obstruents, especially in walking ideophones (e.g., burabura ‘wandering’ vs. furafura ‘walking unsteadily’). This implies that non‐Japanese speakers lacking prior exposure to the language, in which voicing plays a crucial role in sound symbolism (Akita, 2021; Hamano, 1998; Saji, Akita, Kantartzis, Kita, & Imai, 2019), may not have the same sound‐symbolic intuition about voicing as native speakers (see also Kumagai, Uno, & Shinohara, 2021 for the Japanese‐unique association between voicing and hardness).

Dingemanse et al. (2016) conducted a guessing experiment with 203 ideophones from five languages, involving 82 Dutch listeners who had no prior exposure to these languages, in a binary‐choice task. The study showed that cross‐linguistic guessing accuracies for ideophones were generally very low, often barely above chance level, indicating that iconicity in ideophones can be largely language‐specific. Additionally, the study found that participants were better at guessing the meanings of foreign words whose prosodic information was retained. This suggests that a lifetime of language experience with performed ideophones, where factors such as segments, prosody, and gesture play a role, is important for the perceived iconicity of these expressions.

The language‐ and culture‐specific nature of iconicity in ideophones is further supported by the fact that the acquisition of ideophones is a huge obstacle for learners of Japanese as a second language (Hamano, 1998; Iwasaki et al., 2007). Jin (1989) asked 27 Chinese students majoring in the Japanese language to translate ideophones between Japanese and Chinese. The results revealed that the students often failed to translate ideophones in one language that do not have similar‐sounding counterparts in the other (e.g., kokekokkoo [Japanese] and wowo [Chinese] ‘cock‐a‐doodle‐doo’). Moreover, Jin (1989) discusses the culture‐specific aspect of ideophones and the challenges of learning foreign ideophones, stating as follows: “Chinese students who imagine houses made of concrete and bricks wouldn't be familiar with amado (a Japanese‐style wooden shutter) and shikii (a Japanese‐style wooden doorsill). Therefore, they have no way of knowing what gatapishi ‘a rattling sound of opening a Japanese‐style shutter’ refers to or what kind of state it represents” (p. 85).

All these cases indicate that (ideophonic) sound symbolism is largely language‐ and culture‐specific. However, as described above, iconicity has frequently been assumed to be accessible to non‐native speakers of the language (Bosworth & Emmorey, 2010; Perry et al., 2015; among others). This tendency persisted until very recently in the operationalization of iconicity in spoken language.

Iconicity in sign language may also be language‐specific. Occhino et al. (2017) asked American Sign Language (ASL) signers and German Sign Language (DGS) signers to rate the iconicity of both ASL and DGS, 43 signs each. They found that ASL signers gave higher iconicity ratings to ASL signs, and DGS signers, to DGS signs. Similarly, Börstell (2024) asked signers and nonsigners to rate the iconicity of individual letters of ASL and Swedish Sign Language manual alphabets, revealing the same patterns as Occhino et al. (2017). These results clearly indicate that iconicity can be language‐specific, inherently subjective, and contingent on an individual's interpretation. In this view, the subjectively established connection between form and meaning imparts a sense of naturalness to the word in the mind of the language user. The current study follows this renewed understanding of iconicity and demonstrates its importance in spoken language.

The acquired aspect of iconicity

A range of developmental studies has supported the view that iconic form–meaning associations may emerge from experiential factors (e.g., Namy, 2008; Majid & Pyers, 2017). This view may contrast with Imai and Kita's (2014) “sound symbolism bootstrapping hypothesis,” which claims that there is a robust innate foundation of sensitivity to sound symbolism in the brain that helps infants to learn words (see also Spector & Maurer, 2009). In contrast with this hypothesis, Sidhu et al. (2023) demonstrate that infants under 1‐year‐old exhibit limited sensitivity to shape sound symbolism, known as the maluma/takete (or bouba/kiki) effect, in which sonorant consonants (e.g., /m/, /l/) and rounded vowels (e.g., /o/) are associated with roundness, whereas voiceless stops (e.g., /t/, /k/) and high/front vowels (e.g., /i/) are associated with spikiness (Köhler, 1929; Ramachandran & Hubbard, 2001). They also reveal that 12‐month‐olds who babbled more showed greater sensitivity to sound symbolism, suggesting that sensitivity to iconic form–meaning mappings can emerge through language experience, including articulatory experience. Moreover, Fernández‐Prieto, Spence, Pons, and Navarra (2017) report that 6‐month‐old, but not younger, infants can associate high/low pitch with small/large size, arguing that experience is needed to fully develop this crossmodal correspondence. Fort et al. (2018) also highlight the importance of learning for the development of iconic form–meaning associations, drawing on their meta‐analysis of the emergence of sound‐symbolic perception in early language acquisition. Another meta‐analysis (Styles & Gawne, 2017) shows that people fail to detect the maluma/takete effect when the pseudowords used in the task do not conform to the sound structure of their languages, suggesting that language experience constrains sound‐symbolic intuition.

It is also worth mentioning that iconicity or naturalness can emerge through second language acquisition. Kinoshita (1989), who was a second language learner of Japanese, anecdotally states that “the phrase kankan deri‐no taiyoo ‘blazing sun’ has become more natural as I've been in Japan longer” and “the intensity of the rain expressed by botsubotsu ‘dripping heavily’, shitoshito ‘drizzling’, and zaazaa ‘raining heavily’ seems to have become real through my experiences of being caught in numerous rain showers or enduring typhoons” (p. 144).

However, the perceived iconicity emerging from second language learning may not override the perceived iconicity acquired from the native language. Yim (1989) conducted a survey of 312 first‐generation immigrants between South Korea and the United States, investigating changes in the perceived naturalness of ideophones in Korean (meongmeong ‘bowwow’, ggangchung ggangchung ‘boingboing’) and English (bowwow, boing boing) due to their migration. He asked the following question to the immigrants: “The sound of a dog barking is meongmeong in Korean and bowwow in English. How do these sound to you?” The results suggested that the perceived iconicity of ideophones in English varied significantly depending on one's language proficiency and place of residence during language formative years (from 5 to 15 in the study), rather than one's duration of residence. The findings suggest that native language plays a more important role than second language exposure in that more than 80% of people with long‐term experience (e.g., more than 21 years) perceived ideophones in their native language as more iconic.

These previous findings collectively indicate that the perception of iconicity is shaped by individuals’ experiences with the world, culture, and language and is subjectively mediated by their interpretation of form and meaning (Occhino et al., 2017). Consequently, people tend to perceive words in their native language as most natural and iconic (Akita, 2021; Occhino et al., 2017), aligning with Jorden's (1989) remark that “Americans may wonder why Japanese people hear a dog's bark as wanwan even though it certainly barks bowwow” (p. 118). While these personal impressions have been directly tested in sign language (Occhino et al., 2017), they have not yet been explored in spoken language. Therefore, the present study aims to replicate Occhino et al.’s (2017) findings in two phylogenetically unrelated spoken languages: Japanese and English. The focus is on imitative words—Japanese ideophones and their English equivalents—as both imitative words in spoken language and a majority of signs in signed language are generally considered iconic and often compared with each other (Ferrara & Hodge, 2018), despite the ongoing debate on the existence of ideophones in signed language (Dingemanse, 2019). We argue that lexical iconicity in general is partly acquired through language experience, demonstrating its inherently subjective nature.

Methods

The present study replicates the learned nature of lexical iconicity with spoken language data. It also explores the impact of experience with specific lexical items, as measured by familiarity, on the perceived iconicity of words. To operationalize iconicity, we utilize people's subjective ratings of words, which were collected by asking them to rate “the extent to which the words sound like what they mean” (Perry et al., 2015; Thompson et al., 2020; Vinson et al., 2008; Winter & Perlman, 2021; Winter, Lupyan, Perry, Dingemanse, & Perlman, 2024). This section outlines the methods of the current experiment.

Participants

Japanese speakers

A total of 125 native speakers of Japanese were recruited via CrowdWorks, a Japanese equivalent of Amazon Mechanical Turk. They self‐reportedly had no abnormalities in their five senses and declared nonfluency in non‐native languages. They were paid 450 JPY for their participation.

English speakers

A total of 105 native speakers of American English were recruited via Amazon Mechanical Turk. They affirmed the absence of abnormalities in their five senses and their nonfluency in non‐native languages. They were paid 3 USD for their participation.

Stimuli

A total of 392 Japanese ideophones and 44 English words were used. The English words were the translation equivalents for a subset of the Japanese ideophones. All words were presented auditorily, with their definitions visually provided in the participant's language, as illustrated below:

wanwan: inu‐no nakigoe ‘a dog's cry’

inu‐no nakigoe ‘a dog's cry’

bowwow:‘the sound of a dog barking’

‘the sound of a dog barking’

The audio recordings featured a female native speaker of each language. They were embedded in the experimental website that allowed participants to play each target word as many times as they wanted.

Japanese stimuli

For the construction of the stimulus lists, the 300 most frequent ideophones in the Balanced Corpus of Contemporary Written Japanese (BCCWJ) as sorted by NINJAL‐LWP (National Institute for Japanese Language and Linguistics and Lago Institute of Language, 2012) were extracted. Subsequently, 20 ideophones (e.g., kunekune ‘twisting’, nobinobi ‘freely’) lacking definitions in the Dictionary of Iconic Expressions in Japanese (Kakehi, Tamori, & Schourup, 1996) were excluded for the specific purposes of this study.

We then identified whether the ideophones are polysemous (e.g., bon'yari ‘dimly; absent‐mindedly’) or not (e.g., shigeshige ‘staring at something’) by consulting Kakehi et al. (1996). When an ideophone has more than two meanings (e.g., torotoro ‘soft or glutinous; moving slowly; flames burning slowly; dozing off’), we only included two of them (e.g., ‘soft or glutinous’ and ‘moving slowly’). Consequently, the stimulus set used in this study comprised a total of 392 Japanese ideophone meanings. Definitions were sourced from Kakehi et al. (1996), simplified, and translated into English for English‐speaking participants. The stimulus set was partitioned into 11 lists, each containing 37 test items, 9 with English equivalents and 28 without.

We systematically coded the ideophones based on a range of factors that previous studies have identified as related to iconicity. (1) Sensory modality (Lynott et al., 2020): words of different modalities show different degrees of iconicity (Winter et al., 2017), and this is also true for Japanese (Iida & Akita, 2023). We judged the sensory type of each ideophone based on Kakehi et al.’s (1996) definition: haptics (e.g., nurunuru ‘slimy’), audition (e.g., garigari ‘creaking’), olfaction (e.g., punpun ‘smelling strongly’), gustation (e.g., aQsari ‘tasting light’), vision (e.g., yurayura ‘swinging’), interoception (i.e., inner body sensations) (e.g., guQtari ‘extremely tired’).1 (2) Phonological features: Akita (2020) shows that, in Japanese ideophones, the obstruency of the initial consonant is associated with high iconicity, and intervocalic /r/ and vowel assonance are particularly common in visual ideophones (e.g., buruQ ‘shivering once’, goron ‘rolling over’). (3) Morphological features: the third most productive ideophone template CVCCVri (e.g., kongari ‘nicely brown’) is assumed to be low in iconicity because it is shared with nonideophonic words, such as yaQpari ‘also’ and ‐baQkari ‘only’ (Akita, 2020). Also, monomoraic‐based ideophones (e.g., pon ‘popping’) have been argued to be more iconic than bimoraic‐based ideophones (e.g., poron ‘a light object dropping’) (Hamano, 1998). (4) Frequency: Each ideophone was coded for frequency of occurrence using NINJAL‐LWP for BCCWJ.

English stimuli

After the selection of Japanese ideophones, we looked for equivalent English words (i.e., words which are semantically similar to the Japanese ideophones) by consulting multiple dictionaries—Collins English Dictionary (2020), Kakehi et al. (1996), Mito and Kakehi (1981), and Konishi (2013).2 Seventy‐eight of the obtained English expressions (e.g., whack for pokan ‘a dull, resonant sound’, murmur for sarasara ‘murmuring’) have imitative origins according to the Oxford English Dictionary Online (2022) and the Online Etymology Dictionary (Harper, 2001–2016). In contrast, we could not find English equivalents for a majority of the Japanese ideophones (e.g., kongari ‘nicely brown’, iraira ‘irritated’, yoroyoro ‘staggering’) due to the limited inventory of imitative words in English. By our count of the Oxford English Dictionary Online (2022), English has 1551 words with putatively “imitative,” “onomatopoeic,” or “echoic” origins, whereas the largest dictionary of Japanese ideophones (Ono, 2007) contains 4500 entries (for the list of English imitative words, see this study's OSF page: https://osf.io/bdvq6/). We randomly selected 44 from the 78 English imitative words for the current study due to budget limitations. All selected terms received ratings above the average in Winter et al. (2024). Subsequently, we divided the stimulus set into two lists of 22 test items (pairs of 22 Japanese ideophones and their corresponding English words). The frequency of each English word in the SUBTLEX corpus was taken from Winter et al.’s (2024) dataset.

Instructions

Participants engaged in four rating tasks. They played the audios and listened to them as many times as they wanted. The instructions for familiarity, iconicity, and similarity ratings were basically modeled after Occhino et al. (2017), but we gave minimum examples of high‐ and low‐iconicity words because laypeople do not normally know the concept of iconicity (Winter et al., 2024). In Japanese, we used shakishaki ‘crunching’ as a highly iconic word (Thompson et al., 2020) and biyo ‘bowwow’ (an obsolete/dialectal ideophone) as a less iconic word. In English, swish was used as a highly iconic word, and bumble was used as a less iconic word based on Winter et al.’s (2024) iconicity ratings. Both words have imitative origins according to the Oxford English Dictionary.

In Task 1 (familiarity), participants listened to words from their native language, accompanied by their visually presented definitions, and were asked how familiar they were with the words (how often they used, heard, or saw the words). In Task 2 (native language iconicity), participants listened to words from their native language accompanied by their visually presented definitions and were asked how much the words sounded like their meaning. In Task 3 (non‐native language iconicity), participants listened to non‐native language words accompanied by their visually presented definitions and were asked how much the words sounded like their meaning. In Task 4 (cross‐linguistic sound similarity), participants listened to pairs of their native and non‐native language words and were asked how similar the words sounded. All test items and tasks were presented in a random order.

For all tasks, we used a 7‐point scale from 1 “not at all” to 7 “very much.” We did not use a negative scale, following Occhino et al. (2017), Vinson et al. (2008), and Winter et al. (2024). The actual instructions for all tasks are available on this study's OSF page.

Procedure

We created online survey questionnaires using the Qualtrics survey platform. Participants were informed of the nature of the experiment in their native language. Those who consented to participate proceeded to the four rating tasks. In all tasks, the participants listened to audio‐recorded words and read their definitions in their native language and then rated the words on a scale from 1 to 7 by moving a cursor on a horizontal scrollbar, as in Fig. 1. The Japanese data were collected between October 10 and 31, 2023, and the English data between November 1 and 6, 2023.

An example of the trial presented to Japanese participants.

We stopped data collection when each set of Japanese stimuli was answered by 10−12 participants, in line with the methodology for collecting iconicity norms recommended by Motamedi et al. (2019). The English stimulus sets received responses from a larger participant pool, ranging from 50 to 55 individuals, as recommended by Webb and Tangney (2022), to minimize the impact of unreliable responses. To the best of our knowledge, such a concern has not been raised for CrowdWorks (Majima, Nishiyama, Nishihara, & Hata, 2017). Each participant answered only one set.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses reported in this paper were conducted using R version 4.2.3 (R Core Team, 2023), and all plots were generated from averaged data using the ggplot2 package version 3.4.2. For the current analysis, we employed cumulative link mixed models, a statistical approach designed for the analysis of ordinal response variables—those with a natural order but potentially unequal intervals between categories. These models were constructed using the ordinal package (Christensen, 2019) to assess the ordinal response variable (iconicity), while incorporating stimulus language or familiarity as a random slope for two random effects: participant and item. All data and R scripts are available at: https://osf.io/bdvq6/.

The correlation in the Japanese data remained reliable even when other possible factors—sensory modality, morphological features (template and root), phonological features (initial obstruency, intervocalic /r/, and vowel assonance), and frequency—were to the model (b = 0.73, SE = 0.02, z = 34.58, p < .001). The full model indicated no problem for (multi)collinearity as it did not have variance inflation factor values greater than 5.0; the highest value was 2.71 for familiarity. As for meaning, haptic words were rated more iconic than other words, including auditory words (b = 0.50, SE = 0.12, z = 4.03, p < .001), replicating Iida and Akita's (2023) findings about both ideophonic and prosaic words.3 Regarding morphological features, reduplicated forms such as fuwafuwa ‘fluffy’ were rated more iconic than others (b = 0.26, SE = 0.11, z = 2.31, p = .02). There was no reliable difference in iconicity ratings between bimoraic‐based ideophones, such as tsurutsuru ‘slurping’, and monomoraic‐based ideophones, such as tonton ‘knock’ (b = 0.08, SE = 0.14, z = 0.54, p = .59). None of the phonological features had a reliable effect on perceived iconicity (initial obstruent: b = 0.21, SE = 0.11, z = 1.85, p = .06; intervocalic /r/: b = –0.03, SE = 0.11, z = –0.24, p = .81; vowel assonance: b = –0.17, SE = 0.10, z = –1.85, p = .06). We did not find a reliable correlation between frequency and iconicity in Japanese (b = –0.04, SE = 0.05, z = –0.91, p = .37), nor in English (b = 0.10, SE = 0.07, z = 1.39, p = .16). For the detailed results of the regression analyses, see the OSF page.

For a fairer comparison, the effects of language and familiarity were reexamined with the subset of the data in which all Japanese words have English equivalents. We fitted the same model as in the above analyses to the paired data. The model replicated the reliable interaction between the participant's language and stimulus language (b = 1.85, SE = 0.10, z = 17.01, p < .001). This result reinforces the idea that linguistic forms in an individual's native language are perceived as more iconic than their equivalents in another language.

We also examined the relationship between similarity between native and non‐native words and non‐native language iconicity using cumulative link mixed models to predict non‐native language iconicity from similarity, with word and similarity relative to participant as random effects. Both Japanese speaker and English speaker models revealed a reliable correlation between similarity and non‐native language iconicity. In the Japanese speakers model, for each one‐unit increase in similarity, non‐native language iconicity (English) increases by 0.41 (b = 0.41, SE = 0.05, z = 7.65, p < .001). Similarly, in the English speakers model, for each one‐unit increase in similarity, non‐native language iconicity (Japanese) increases by 0.28 (b = 0.36, SE = 0.05, z = 5.45, p < .001). The more similar non‐native words are to native words, the more familiar they should be perceived. Therefore, these findings suggest that the perception of iconicity in a non‐native language can also be influenced by the speaker's native language experience.

Discussion

Iconicity may emerge from language experience

The results of the current experiment offer new evidence for the pivotal influence of language experience on the perception of iconicity. First, we compared iconicity ratings between native and non‐native speakers of two spoken languages. Japanese‐speaking participants gave relatively higher iconicity ratings to Japanese words than to English words, while English‐speaking participants gave higher iconicity ratings to English words than to Japanese words. These results replicate Occhino et al.’s (2017) findings about ASL and DGS. We have further demonstrated that the familiarity of specific lexical items has a notable influence on the perception of iconicity in one's native language. The more familiar people get with a word through their long‐time exposure to their native language, the more iconic they perceive the word. As an anonymous reviewer noted, the obtained interaction between participant language and stimulus language in Fig. 2 could be attributed to the low iconicity of an exotic language, rather than the high iconicity of one's native language. This might be correct. However, our finding that iconicity also varies as a function of familiarity in one's native language may be more consistent with the latter. Additionally, the correlation between similarity (between native words and non‐native words) and iconicity indicates that familiarity may also influence iconicity in a non‐native language.

The findings of the current study suggest that language experience plays a role in iconicity through at least two key aspects: familiarity and systematicity. Occhino et al. (2020, 2017) argue that it is the integration into a language's morphological patterns that enables language‐specific perceptions of iconicity. For example, the ASL sign for DANCE, produced with an inverted V‐handshape, may not initially appear iconic to nonsigners. However, as a signer learns more signs that use the inverted V‐handshape, they may begin to recognize that DANCE is part of a broader lexical system, thereby increasing its perceived iconicity. The current finding that familiarity has a strong impact on the perception of iconicity suggests that, in addition to systematicity across words, experience with a specific pair of form and meaning also crucially enhances perceived iconicity.

These findings robustly indicate the presence of an acquired dimension in the perception of iconicity. This subjective aspect of iconicity cannot be equated with its universality, as this dimension emerges through language development and one's exposure to their native language. Certain instances of iconicity, such as some onomatopoeia, can transcend linguistic boundaries, receiving high iconicity ratings from both native and non‐native speakers. For example, in the current experiment, bowwow received high iconicity ratings not only from English speakers (M = 5.20, SD = 1.24) but also from Japanese speakers (M = 5.87, SD = 1.56). However, individuals generally tended to perceive their own language more iconic than their non‐native language. Japanese speakers rated wanwan ‘bowwow’ more iconic (M = 6.73, SD = 0.47) than bowwow, and English speakers rated bowwow more iconic than wanwan ‘bowwow’ (M = 4.78, SD = 1.71). This subjective nature of iconicity partly explains why even prosaic words are perceived iconic to some extent (Perlman, Little, Thompson, & Thompson, 2018; Thompson et al., 2020; Winter et al., 2017; Winter et al., 2024). Language users may perceive a form‐meaning resemblance even in seemingly noniconic words through their repeated encounters with those words.4 The current findings also align with the observation that Japanese speakers rate native lexemes (e.g., toki ‘time’, karada ‘body’), which constitute the core of the Japanese lexicon, more iconic than peripheral loanwords from Chinese (e.g., jiko ‘accident’, toshi ‘city’) or European languages (e.g., deeta ‘data’, hoteru ‘hotel’) (Thompson et al., 2020). This suggests that the perception of iconicity in prosaic words is also influenced by long‐term exposure to native words (Akita & Imai, 2022).

Some issues remain as to the relationship between familiarity and other variables. In particular, Winter et al. (2024) report that the frequency of words, which one may expect to contribute to the sense of familiarity, in actuality negatively correlates with iconicity. This result appears to be attributed to the fact that their dataset contained many frequent low iconicity words, including function words. In fact, this negative correlation was not obtained in the current dataset, which only consists of highly iconic words. Moreover, frequency and familiarity are conceptually different variables. Familiarity is more subjective and contingent on one's language experience, whereas frequency represents the distributions of words in a corpus, which does not necessarily correspond to one's intuition about the words (Connine, Mullennix, Shernoff, & Yelen, 1990).

Furthermore, our results might look incompatible with Sehr Sevcikova and Emmorey's (2019) finding that native signers rated signs less iconic overall compared to nonsigners. However, the participants in that study only rated signs. It might be that, as in the current study, signers rate spoken words less iconic than signs and nonsigners rate spoken words more iconic than signs. The former possibility may be hard to test due to signers’ limited hearing ability, and the latter possibility might be rejected because the visual modality affords more iconicity compared to the vocal modality (Vigliocco, Perniss, & Vinson, 2014).

Implications for the definition of iconicity

The current findings lead to the characterization of iconicity as a subjective interpretation of a sign. This raises a question about the following definition of iconicity proposed most recently by Winter et al. (2025).

A signal in any modality or medium exhibits iconicity when its production and/or interpretation involves a sense of resemblance between at least some aspect of its form and at least some aspect of its meaning (Winter et al., 2025, p. 3).

Specifically, the last part of the definition “at least some aspect of form and at least some aspect of its meaning” emphasizes the selective nature of iconicity (Hassemer & Winter, 2018). In other words, “the resemblance between form and intended meaning is never perfect” (Winter, 2019). For example, we mimic the height of a referent by a gesture at the expense of conveying information about its shape. Selectivity also explains the cross‐linguistic difference of onomatopoeia, such as English cock‐a‐doodle‐doo and German kikeriki (Winter, 2019, p. 22). The difference arises from language‐specific selections regarding what aspect of a rooster's cry to iconically represent as well as the cross‐linguistic difference in sound inventories.

However, if the subjective construal of form and meaning is the core mechanism for iconicity, there is a potential for the entire linguistic form of a word to be reinterpreted in a way that the word feels fitting to its meaning through long‐time frequent exposure to a particular language. In fact, the highly specific lexical meaning of ideophones in Japanese cannot be obtained from the sound symbolism of each constituent phoneme (Akita, 2009), and native speakers feel ideophones are iconic regardless of most of the formal lexical properties (see the full model analysis in Section 3.3). Thus, in general, “it may be hard to put a finger on the precise structural correspondences between form and meaning” (Dingemanse, 2019, p. 19). For example, the lexical meaning of the ideophone pichapicha ‘splashing liquid repetitively’ cannot be fully derived from the sound‐symbolic meanings of their component segments identified by Hamano (1998) (i.e., /p/ = tautness of the surface of a light, small object; /i/ = tenseness of an object or movement in the first phase; /t/ = hitting of a surface; /Cy/ (palatalization) = childishness, uncontrolled energy; /a/ = largeness of an object or movement in the second phase) or morphological form (i.e., total reduplication = repetitiveness). Moreover, while a gesture encodes information about height at the expense of information about shape, conventionalized ideophones can evoke richer, multifaceted sensory imagery in a holistic manner (Akita, 2022; Dingemanse, 2011; Nuckolls, 2019). For example, karikari ‘crunching’ can simultaneously represent the auditory (a crunching sound), visual (a hard‐looking surface), and haptic aspects (a hard texture) of a scene, which verbs and adjectives may not express together (e.g., kajiru ‘to bite’ [primarily visual], katai ‘be hard’ [primarily haptic]) (Akita, 2022). Thus, it is possible that native language users perceive the iconicity of ideophones holistically. The question of whether iconicity is always selective or can be perceived holistically requires further empirical examinations. At the very least, it is apparent that the iconicity ratings of real words would not provide relevant insights into the selectivity of iconicity, as they ask people to rate entire forms.

Conclusion

In this study, we have demonstrated that the sense of iconicity can emerge from one's long‐time exposure to their native spoken language. We first compared English and Japanese speakers’ iconicity ratings for their native and non‐native languages. We then observed a striking correlation between iconicity and familiarity and argued that the effect of language experience on perceived iconicity is also found within the participant's native language. The current findings show that iconicity is largely language‐specific, inherently subjective, and contingent on the individual's interpretation, thereby establishing a connection between form and meaning that imparts a sense of naturalness to the word in the minds of language users.

Some previous iconicity rating studies assume that iconicity is accessible to non‐native users of the language (Section 1.2). The current finding that iconicity can be acquired questions this assumption and prompts us to revisit what iconicity is and, in turn, what iconicity ratings really measure (Winter & Perlman, 2021). One possible approach to this issue would be to compare iconicity ratings with other variables, such as guessability (McLean et al., 2023).

Given the acquired nature of iconicity, further investigations need to be conducted to identify which aspects of iconicity in ideophones are language‐specific. One way to pursue this issue will be to examine language‐specific subtypes of ideophones, such as reduplicated versus nonreduplicated ideophones in Japanese (e.g., kirakira ‘glittering’ vs. kirari ‘glistening’). While native Japanese speakers may perceive all ideophones iconic (Dingemanse, 2019; Kita, 1997), the iconicity of reduplicated forms might be relatively more accessible to users of other languages, considering the prevalence of reduplication in ideophones across languages (Dingemanse, 2015).5 Likewise, certain transparent mappings between articulatory movement and semantic domains, such as the wind–fricative mapping (e.g., /hu.hu/ ‘wind blowing’ in Chaoyang), might be stable across languages (Thompson & Do, 2019).

The current study also encourages us to further explore whether native speakers really perceive language‐specific ideophones as iconic as more cross‐linguistically shared ideophones and distinctly more iconic than prosaic words. Rating studies (Thompson et al., 2020; Winter et al., 2024) show that ideophones are overall more iconic than prosaic words. (Note, however, that these studies do not show that all ideophones are iconic to the same degree.) This observation leads us to ask “what makes us think of ideophones as iconic in the first place” (Dingemanse, 2019, p. 13). Dingemanse et al. (2016) suggest that ideophones are strongly iconic to native speakers of the language because they “have a lifetime of experience with performed ideophones in which various factors—segments, prosody, gesture—help to build a representation of the words that emphasizes their iconic potential” (p. 128). This special performative nature of ideophones may explain why native speakers find language‐specific ideophones as iconic as more universal ideophones.

Moreover, the relationship between familiarity and iconicity could be further explored by investigating whether the positive correlation we obtained can also be found in prosaic words. Another open question is whether the same correlation can be extended to one's non‐native languages. It appears to be possible that, as people learn another language, they also “learn” iconicity, aligning with Kinoshita's (1989) anecdote. Such an investigation will facilitate a nuanced understanding of how iconicity and familiarity intersect and contribute to pinpointing the precise circumstances and conditions that significantly heighten the perception of iconicity.

Another crucial inquiry focuses on the developmental trajectory of iconicity, particularly its manifestations that are deeply rooted in specific languages and cultures. This leads us to question at which stage in language acquisition children learn the language‐specific iconicity of ideophonic and prosaic words. Furthermore, exploring language‐ and culture‐specific iconicity in cognitive and linguistic development can illuminate the role of iconicity in language, such as memory, processing, and learning. By addressing these questions, we can gain a better understanding of the nature of iconicity and why it is so ubiquitous in human language.
