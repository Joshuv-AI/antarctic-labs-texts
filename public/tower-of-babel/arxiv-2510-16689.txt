Geometric Control Theory Over Networks: Minimal Node Cardinality Disturbance Decoupling Problems
Luca Claude Gino Lebon, Claudio Altafini

Abstract
In this paper we show how to formulate and solve disturbance decoupling problems over networks while choosing a minimal number of input and output nodes. Feedback laws that isolate and eliminate the impact of disturbance nodes on specific target nodes to be protected are provided using state, output, and dynamical feedback. For that, we leverage the fact that when reformulated in terms of sets of nodes rather than subspaces, the controlled and conditional invariance properties admit a simple graphical interpretation. For state and dynamical feedback, the minimal input and output cardinality solutions can be computed exactly in polynomial time, via min-cut/max-flow algorithms.



I
Introduction

The Disturbance Decoupling Problem (DDP) is a basic problem in control theory, and consists in rendering a set of variables (which we refer to as targets) insensitive to disturbances entering into the model of the system.
It is known since the late Sixties that for linear systems the DDP can be solved in terms of invariant subspaces
[
2
,
28
]
, and in fact it has been one of the main motivating problems behind the development of geometric control theory
[
3
,
27
,
22
]
.
It relies on notions such as controlled and conditioned invariance, and on the feedback synthesis that follows from them.
These concepts are normally formulated for subspaces and, while well-understood algebraically and geometrically, they remain computationally challenging because of the numerical ill-conditioning associated with operations on subspaces, such as invariance, intersection, sum etc.

In this paper we show that when applied to (linear) networked control systems in which disturbances, targets, measurements and controls all act on individual nodes (rather than on linear combination of nodes), many of the concepts of geometric control simplify drastically.
For instance, subspaces become sets of nodes, and invariance of a subspace becomes lack of outgoing edges from a set of nodes. The controlled invariance property of a set of nodes corresponds to having edges that stay in the set or, when they exit it, land on the input nodes.
Similarly, conditioned invariance corresponds to a set of nodes whose edges can leave the set only if they start on the outputs.

These interpretations of controlled/conditioned invariance, which were first presented in our knowledge in
[
7
]
, are here developed in full and exploited.
Their simplicity and graphical meaning, in fact, pave the way for a systematic application of all tools from geometric control theory to networked systems.
In particular, for the DDP, we show that its simplest version, DDP by State Feedback (DDPSF), corresponds to checking a set containment condition involving the maximal controlled invariant set of nodes. Also more advanced forms of DDP, like DDP by Output Feedback (DDPOF) and DDP by Dynamical Feedback (DDPDF) can be reformulated along similar lines.
All three correspond graphically to being able to block (or to compensate exactly for) all paths from disturbances to targets on the graph of the system through a feedback design.

The simplicity of the setting allows also to solve a more complex and novel problem, namely determine what is the most parsimonious selection of input (and output) nodes that can solve a DDP. We formulate this problem as a minimal cardinality problem with respect to the number of inputs in the DDPSF, and with respect to the number of inputs and outputs in the DDPOF and DDPDF.
All can be mapped to graphical conditions, which can be solved optimally for DDPSF and DDPDF in polynomial time using variants of min-cut/max-flow.

In all three cases, we also provide feedback laws that achieve the decoupling.
For the DDPSF, the feedback consists in exactly canceling the arcs immediately upstream of the input nodes.
For the DDPOF, feasibility corresponds to output and input laying on the inner and outer boundary of a set of nodes which must be simultaneously controlled and conditioned invariant, and the output feedback corresponds to canceling all edges that cross the boundary.
For the DDPDF, an observer-based feedback is instead used, in which the observer corresponds to the nodes lying between the maximal controlled invariant set and the minimal conditioned invariant set.

Literature review.
The classical literature on DDP is summarized in well-known geometric control books such as
[
3
,
27
,
22
]
. When it comes to networked systems, we notice that there exists another graphical approach to the DDP, based on the notion of structured systems
[
5
,
8
,
9
]
, i.e., on seeking conditions valid for almost all values of the system parameters.
In this context, it is worth mentioning e.g.
[
6
,
24
]
, which provide necessary and sufficient conditions for the DDP from state feedback in terms of the structure at infinity of the structured system, and
[
25
]
, where these conditions are extended to the DDP with measurement feedback.
While the structural approach is related to ours, a one-to-one mapping is impossible because this approach uses different conventions in its problem formulation (e.g. nodes that carry inputs are separated from “state” nodes, and from output nodes), and the input, output and disturbance matrices are not elementary columns/rows as in our approach, meaning that an explicit association between invariant sets of nodes and invariant subspaces is absent.
In any case, none of the classical papers deals with the minimal cardinality control and output assignment problems which are at the core of our work.

Apart from the aforementioned structured systems and
[
7
]
, some other sporadic efforts have been made to translate the invariance conditions into network terms. For instance, the paper
[
17
]
establishes sufficient conditions for disturbance decoupling in diffusive-type multi-agent systems by means of graph partitions.

A broad literature, starting with
[
26
]
, exists also on the joint problem of DDP and stability, or more generally on pole placement in DDP.
In this perspective, recent studies have formulated the DDP as a constraint within an optimization framework
[
20
]
.
A joint minimal input cardinality DDP and pole placement problem is investigated for instance in
[
4
]
for general linear systems, using very different tools such as matroids and submodular optimization.
On a network, the extra requirements associated to pole allocation may hamper the graphical interpretation and analysis. We therefore decided to disregard them, since, as it is well known, stability can always be recovered by adding sufficiently many (negative) self-loops, until diagonal dominance is achieved in the state update matrix
[
14
]
. Self-loops do not interfere with our DDP formulation.

Finally, while in this paper we are not focusing on any specific application, it is worth mentioning that there exists a wide range of networked systems in which DDP is of relevance. Examples from recent literature include

[
18
]
, where leader-follower formation control is framed as a DDP, or
[
23
]
, where DDP is formulated in a dynamic game context.
Another context in which our tools could be applied is fault detection and isolation
[
15
]
, as well as the related problem of attack detection and identification; see e.g.,
[
19
]
.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. After a recap of geometric control theory in Section
II
, the DDP problem over networks is solved in Section
III
, while in Section
IV
we formulate and solve the minimal-node DDP. The synthesis of feedback laws is in Section
V
.

A preliminary version of this paper, which will appear as conference proceedings
[
16
]
, deals only with the DDPSF case, and lacks the proofs of the main results. The material on DDPOF and DDPDF is presented here for the first time.

Notation

We consider a digraph
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\,\mathcal{E},A)
, where

is a set of

, with the convention that

, i.e.,

is the adjacency matrix associated to
\mathcal{G}
:

.

Denote

the class of adjacency matrices of

having “support” in
\mathcal{E}
:
\mathcal{A}=\{A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}\;\text{ s.t.}\;A_{ji}\neq 0\;\text{iff}\;(v_{i},\,v_{j})\in\mathcal{E}\;\text{and}\;A_{ji}=0\;\text{iff}\;(v_{i},\,v_{j})\notin\mathcal{E}\}

A special matrix in
\mathcal{A}
is the indicator matrix

, i.e., the unweighted version of
A

\mathbb{A}_{ij}=\mathbb{I}(A_{ij}\neq 0)

\mathbb{I}(\cdot)
denoting the indicator function, that is, 1 if the condition is true and 0 otherwise.

Given a digraph
\mathcal{G}
, a subset
\mathcal{Z}\subset\mathcal{V}
is called a terminal subset of nodes if there is no outgoing edge from
\mathcal{Z}
, i.e.,
\forall\,(v_{i},\,v_{j})\in\mathcal{E}
if

then also

.
A directed path
\mathcal{P}
from node

to node

is a sequence of distinct nodes
v_{i},v_{i+1},\dots,v_{j-1},v_{j}
s.t.
(v_{k},v_{k+1})\in\mathcal{E}

\forall\,k\in\{i,i+1,\dots,j-1\}
:
\mathcal{P}=\{v_{i},\dots,v_{j}\}
(or
\mathcal{P}^{v_{i},v_{j}}
when we need to specify the extremities of the path).
The number of edges in the path is called the length of the path. Given a set of nodes
\mathcal{Z}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
, with a slight abuse of notation, we can use
\mathcal{Z}
to express subspaces of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
:
\mathcal{Z}\sim\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;\text{s.t.}\;x_{i}\neq 0\;\forall\;i\in\mathcal{Z}\;\text{and}\;x_{i}=0\;\forall\;i\notin\mathcal{Z}\right\}
.
In the following, we identify
\mathcal{Z}
and the associated vector subspace, and sometimes write
x\in\mathcal{Z}
.

Remark 1
.

Since a vector subspace contains the
0
element, the identification “sets of nodes”
\sim
“vector subspaces” holds almost always, except for a zero measure set. This is the same principle behind the structural linear systems approach
[
5
]
. The only case in which this fact has to be made explicit is when we have equalities involving some matrix
A\in\mathcal{A}
, such as
Ax=y
, where
x\in\mathcal{X}
,
y\in\mathcal{Y}
with
\mathcal{X},\,\mathcal{Y}
sets of nodes / vector subspaces.
In this case, to avoid complications, we always replace
A
with the indicator matrix
\mathbb{A}
, and consider the expression
\mathbb{A}\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{Y}
.
This is not needed when we have inequalities such as
A\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathcal{Y}
.

For any
\mathcal{Z}\subset\mathcal{V}
, the notation
\mathcal{Z}^{\perp}
is used for both the complement set
\mathcal{V}\smallsetminus\mathcal{Z}
and the subspace orthogonal to (the vector space)
\mathcal{Z}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Remark 2
.

For each subspace
\mathcal{Z}
,
\exists
a full column rank matrix
Z
s.t.
{\rm Im}Z=\mathcal{Z}
and
\ker Z=\{0\}
.
Such matrix is called a basis matrix of
\mathcal{Z}
. When
\mathcal{Z}
is a set of nodes, then
Z
is a collection of elementary column vectors (i.e., one column for each node
v_{i}\in\mathcal{Z}
, having 1 in the
v_{i}
-th slot and 0 elsewhere).
As a consequence, the sum of vector subspaces and their union can be identified:
\mathcal{Z}_{1}+\mathcal{Z}_{2}\sim\mathcal{Z}_{1}\cup\mathcal{Z}_{2}
. Moreover,
Z^{\top}
is full-row rank and
\ker Z^{\top}=({\rm Im}Z)^{\perp}
. If
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathcal{V}
denotes another set of nodes, then
\mathcal{S}\cap\ker Z^{\top}
corresponds to the component of
\mathcal{S}
orthogonal to
{\rm Im}Z
. In this setting, set subtraction and orthogonal complement coincide because all subspaces involved are spanned by disjoint subsets of orthonormal basis vectors. We therefore write
\mathcal{S}\cap\ker Z^{\top}\sim\mathcal{S}\smallsetminus{\rm Im}Z
.

On
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\,\mathcal{E},A)
, for any subset of nodes
\mathcal{W}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
we can define the in- and out-boundary as follows.

Definition 1
.

The in-boundary of
\mathcal{W}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
in the digraph
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\,\mathcal{E},A)
is the set of nodes in
\mathcal{W}
that have at least one outgoing edge leading to a node in
\mathcal{W}^{\perp}
, i.e.,
\partial_{-}(\mathcal{W},A):=\{v_{i}\in\mathcal{W}\;\text{s.t.}\;\exists\,(v_{i},v_{j})\in\mathcal{E},\,v_{j}\in\mathcal{W}^{\perp}\}
. Similarly, the out-boundary of
\mathcal{W}
is the set of nodes in
\mathcal{W}^{\perp}
that have at least one incoming edge from
\mathcal{W}
, i.e.,
\partial_{+}(\mathcal{W},A):=\{v_{j}\in\mathcal{W}^{\perp}\;\text{s.t.}\;\exists\,(v_{i},v_{j})\in\mathcal{E},\,v_{i}\in\mathcal{W}\}
.

From the previous definition, it is obvious that
\partial_{\pm}(\mathcal{W},A)=\partial_{\mp}(\mathcal{W}^{\perp},A^{\top})
.
Notice that in general
\partial_{+}(\mathcal{W},A)\subseteq A\partial_{-}(\mathcal{W},A)
and the inclusion can be strict.

\begin{split}\dot{x}&=Ax+Bu,\\

(1)

Maximal controlled invariance, and minimal conditioned invariance

Proposition 4

(

\mathbf{\mathcal{Z}^{\ast}}
)
.

Given a set

(

)

II-E

Duality

It is well known that the notions of controlled and conditioned invariance are dual notions.
Here we repeat these arguments for sets of nodes, rather than subspaces.

Lemma 1

(Property 3.1.2 of
[
3
]
)
.

Given a linear map identified by the matrix
A:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and any two subsets

III
Disturbance decoupling problems over networks: formulation and solution

For a given network system (
1
), we have two additional sets of nodes
\mathcal{D},\mathcal{T}\subset\mathcal{V}
, of cardinality
|\mathcal{D}|
and
|\mathcal{T}|
, corresponding, respectively, to nodes affected by disturbances and target nodes. Each disturbance
w_{i}
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,|\mathcal{D}|\}
, is assumed to act independently on a node of
\mathcal{D}
.
Both
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{T}
can be identified with elementary matrices
D\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times|\mathcal{D}|}
and
T\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{T}|\times n}
, i.e., matrices composed of elementary columns, respectively rows, representing subselections of nodes in
\mathcal{V}
.
We can then associate to
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{T}
vector subspaces, respectively
{\rm Im}D=\mathcal{D}
and
\ker T=\mathcal{V}\smallsetminus\mathcal{T}
.

Denoting
w=[w_{1}\ldots w_{|\mathcal{D}|}]^{\top}
, the complete system is therefore the following generalization of (
1
):

\begin{split}\dot{x}&=Ax+Bu+Dw,\\
y&=Cx,\\
z&=Tx.\end{split}

(2)

For this network system, the disturbance decoupling problem studied in the literature can be formulated as follows.

Problem 1

(
DDP
)
.

Given the system (
2
), find a control law
u(t)
that renders the target nodes in
\mathcal{T}
unaffected by the disturbances in
\mathcal{D}
.

The feasibility of Problem
1
for (
2
) can be verified in a purely topological way, independent of the weight values of the edges in
A
.

The construction of the control law
u(t)
requires instead to specify a feedback law, which in turn requires to know the numerical entries of
A
. The feedback can be constructed differently depending on the context. Common choices in the literature are:

•

DDPSF
- Disturbance Decoupling Problem by State Feedback : if
u(t)=-Fx(t)
;

•

DDPOF
- Disturbance Decoupling Problem by Output Feedback: if
u(t)=-Gy(t)
;

•

DDPDF
- Disturbance Decoupling Problem by Dynamical Feedback: if
u(t)=-M\hat{x}(t)-Gy(t)
, where
\hat{x}(t)
denotes the state estimate.

Obviously solvability of the DDPOF (or of the DDPDF) implies solvability of the DDPSF, but not necessarily vice versa.

In Propositions
4
and
5
we assume the following initializations.

Assumption 1
.

The algorithm for
\mathcal{Z}^{\ast}
, presented in Proposition
4
, is initialized with
\mathcal{Z}_{o}=\ker T=\mathcal{V}\smallsetminus\mathcal{T}
.

Under Assumption
1
,
\mathcal{Z}^{\ast}
is the maximal controlled invariant subset contained in
\mathcal{V}\smallsetminus\mathcal{T}
, that is, the largest set of nodes contained in
\mathcal{V}\smallsetminus\mathcal{T}
which can be rendered invariant by an appropriate choice of state feedback. In other words,
\mathcal{Z}^{\ast}
is rendered
(A-BF)
-invariant for a suitable choice of a friend
F\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
.

Assumption 2
.

The algorithm for
\mathcal{S}^{\ast}
, presented in Proposition
5
, is initialized with
\mathcal{S}_{o}={\rm Im}D=\mathcal{D}
.

Under Assumption
2
,
\mathcal{S}^{\ast}
is the minimal conditioned invariant superset containing
\mathcal{D}
, i.e., the smallest set of nodes containing
\mathcal{D}
which can be rendered invariant by a suitable “output injection”. We also say that
\mathcal{S}^{\ast}
is
(A-HC)
-invariant for a proper choice of a friend
H\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
.

In order to avoid degenerate situations in the problem, we also make assumptions on the sets
\mathcal{D}
,
\mathcal{T}
,
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{C}
.

Assumption 3
.

\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{T}=\emptyset
,
\mathcal{D}\cap\mathcal{B}=\emptyset
and
\mathcal{T}\cap\mathcal{C}=\emptyset
.

In matrix form, the conditions of Assumption

can be expressed as

,

and

VII
Conclusion

One way of protecting strategic nodes in real-world networks—such as power grids, traffic systems, and IT infrastructures—is to formulate the problem as a node-based DDP, and to design suitable feedback laws achieving the decoupling.
The node-based formulation allows to elucidate the connection between input/output allocation and controlled/conditioned invariance, and to formulate the minimal input/output node allocation as a graphical optimization problem, for which polynomial-complexity solutions are in many cases available.

Ongoing work focuses on testing and validating the proposed algorithms on real-world datasets.
