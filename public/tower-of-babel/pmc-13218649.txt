Choroid plexus cyst burden is associated with glymphatic dysfunction and cognitive impairment in hepatic encephalopathy
Authors: Li Chen, Zhiqiang Zhang, Jian Fang, Lirong Cai, Zhiyu Zeng, Yuzhen Liu, Jiaolong Zheng, Liyuan Fu, Chengkun Hong, Yaling Chen, Xiaoyang Wang, Dongliang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Choroid plexus cysts (CPCs) are often considered incidental imaging findings in adults, but their phenotype and clinical relevance in HE are unclear. Our study aims to evaluate CPCs and choroid plexus volume (CPV) in HE and examine their associations with glymphatic clearance estimated by diffusion tensor image analysis along the perivascular space (DTI-ALPS) index, brain structure, and cognition.Methods:In this cross-sectional study, 3T MRI was performed in 3 groups: HE, cirrhosis without hepatic encephalopathy (NHE), and healthy controls (HCs). We assessed CPC burden, CPV, the DTI-ALPS index, structural brain measures and cognitive performance. Group differences were tested with ANCOVA.Results:CPCs were detected in 100% of participants with HE versus 60% in NHE and 26.7% in HCs. Normalized CPC mean diameter and CPV were higher in HE than in NHE and HCs (all p values <0.05). The DTI-ALPS index decreased stepwise from HCs to NHE to HE. Cirrhosis was associated with widespread subcortical atrophy and cortical thinning, more extensive in HE. CPC burden and CPV correlated inversely with cognitive and structural metrics, whereas the DTI-ALPS index correlated positively. Mediation analyses suggested that CP phenotypes may be associated with cognitive impairment through glymphatic dysfunction accompanied by structural brain alterations.Conclusions:HE is characterized by increased CPC burden and enlarged CPV, accompanied by a lower DTI-ALPS index, structural brain alterations, and cognitive impairment. CPC burden and CPV are potential auxiliary choroid plexus-related MRI phenotypes of HE.

INTRODUCTION

HE is a common and severe complication of cirrhosis that causes cognitive, psychiatric, and motor abnormalities, potentially progressing to coma or death.1 Its pathophysiology is multifactorial, involving hyperammonemia, systemic inflammation and neuroinflammation, blood-brain and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) barrier alterations, glymphatic system disturbances, abnormal energy metabolism, and disrupted neurotransmission.2–4 The choroid plexus (CP) is a central hub in this multifactorial network, consisting of a single layer of cuboidal epithelial cells. It is the principal source of CSF and a key component of the blood-CSF barrier, contributing to immune surveillance and metabolic waste clearance.5 Experimental HE models show CP swelling with ventricular enlargement and aquaporin-1 mislocalization during hyperammonemia, implicating CP structural and hydrodynamic abnormalities in HE.6 However, evidence on CP morphology in HE remains limited.

Choroid plexus cysts (CPCs) are cystic changes in the CP, first reported in mid-gestational ultrasonography, with detection rates of ~1%–2%.7 Although a minority are associated with fetal chromosomal abnormalities, CPCs are generally benign and have no substantial impact on neurodevelopment.8,9 In adults, CPCs are typically incidental and asymptomatic.10 Recent studies, however, report a higher prevalence and number of CPCs in neurodegenerative disorders, particularly Alzheimer disease, suggesting links to aging, neurodegeneration, or altered central metabolic states.11 This observation offers new insight into the potential imaging significance of CPCs under specific pathological conditions. Currently, the presentation of CPCs in the HE population remains unreported.

In the pathophysiology of HE, the glymphatic system provides a framework for metabolite clearance in the brain. CSF flows along the perivascular space into the brain parenchyma, exchanges substances with interstitial fluid, and is then drained via the venous perivascular space into the deep cervical lymph nodes, thereby facilitating solute clearance.12 Rodent HE models indicate impaired inflow and clearance along this pathway, resulting in the accumulation of metabolic byproducts and cognitive decline.3 Given that the CP serves as both a source of and an exchange node for CSF, structural and microenvironmental abnormalities, such as cystic changes represented by CPCs and enlargement of choroid plexus volume (CPV), may biologically influence clearance flux and efficiency as an upstream perturbation of this pathway. To noninvasively assess this function in vivo, our study uses diffusion tensor imaging analysis along the perivascular space (DTI-ALPS) to quantify diffusion anisotropy perpendicular to the dominant fiber directions at the level of the lateral ventricles, as an imaging marker of brain clearance function.13 On this basis, we evaluate the relationships between CP-associated structural phenotypes and glymphatic clearance.

Neuroimaging in HE further reveals widespread structural involvement, including microstructural white-matter disruption and edema associated with cognitive deficits,14 gray-matter volume loss,15 T1 hyperintensity in deep gray matter often attributed to manganese deposition,16 and focal cortical thinning that correlates with impairment.17 Collectively, these findings delineate a neuroimaging profile of widespread involvement in HE.

Although these studies individually support the significance of CP abnormalities, glymphatic clearance dysfunction, and structural involvement in HE, a systematic analysis integrating CP, glymphatic system, brain structure, and cognitive performance in the same cohort is still lacking. Based on this evidence and research gap, the present study aims to construct a multilevel association map focusing on “CP—glymphatic clearance—brain structure—cognition” to further elucidate the central nervous system damage mechanism in HE and provide a basis for identifying new imaging biomarkers.

METHODS

Study design and participants

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and the Declaration of Istanbul. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Biomedical Ethics Committee of the 900th Hospital of the Joint Logistics Support Force (approval No. 2025-087) and was registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2500106101). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

From December 2024 to September 2025, we consecutively screened adult patients aged 18 to 75 years with liver cirrhosis from both the outpatient clinic and inpatient ward of the Department of Hepatobiliary Internal Medicine, the 900th Hospital of PLA Joint Logistic Support Force. Cirrhosis was diagnosed according to the Evidence-based Clinical Practice Guidelines for Cirrhosis.18 Two hepatologists independently performed eligibility assessment and West Haven grading using prespecified criteria. Disagreements were resolved by consensus. Healthy control (HC) candidates without systemic disease were recruited from the Health Examination Center of the same hospital and were frequency-matched to the cirrhosis cohort by age, sex, and education level. Initially, 76 patients with cirrhosis and 34 HC candidates were screened. Exclusion criteria were (1) alcohol-associated cirrhosis or heavy alcohol intake within the past 6 months, defined as >60 g/d for men or >40 g/d for women,19 (2) current psychoactive substance use disorder within the past 6 months, defined as a documented diagnosis in the medical record or ongoing treatment for psychoactive substance use (excluding nicotine), (3) age below 18 years or above 75 years, (4) contraindications to MRI, (5) concomitant hepatic malignancy or rapidly progressive liver failure, and (6) West Haven grade 3–4 at enrollment.1

Participants with cirrhosis were assigned to the HE or cirrhosis without HE (NHE) group at enrollment on the MRI day after clinical assessment and psychometric testing. Participants were classified as HE if they met either of the following criteria: (1) a documented episode of overt HE, defined as West Haven grade ≥2, within 1 month before MRI, verified by hepatology records including West Haven grading and HE-directed treatment; or (2) HE at enrollment. At enrollment, HE was categorized as covert or overt. Covert HE included minimal HE and West Haven grade 1 HE; minimal HE was defined as West Haven grade 0 with a Psychometric Hepatic Encephalopathy Score (PHES)≤−4. Overt HE at enrollment was defined as West Haven grade 2. The NHE group comprised participants with no documented history of overt HE based on medical record review and structured interviews with patients and caregivers, and with no evidence of covert HE at enrollment, defined as West Haven grade 0 and PHES>−4. Medication use before and at enrollment was recorded for all participants, including HE-directed therapy. Because minimal HE may fluctuate over time, the PHES assessed on the MRI day was used to determine minimal HE status at enrollment.

Neuropsychological tests

All participants completed the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), and the PHES. All assessments were performed on the day of MRI in a quiet, well-lit room by Li Chen and Zhiqiang Zhang, with quality control provided by Dongliang Li. Using age-adjusted and education-adjusted norms established for Chinese populations,20 a PHES score≤-4 indicated minimal HE.

Laboratory tests

To characterize disease status in patients with cirrhosis, we assessed markers of hepatocellular injury, synthetic and reserve capacity, detoxification and excretory function, and related complications. Peripheral venous blood was collected within 3 days before cranial MRI. Assays included alanine aminotransferase, AST, albumin, cholinesterase, prothrombin time, international normalized ratio, total bilirubin, creatinine, estimated glomerular filtration rate, serum sodium, serum ammonia, erythrocytes, leukocytes, platelets, and hemoglobin. Disease severity and prognosis were evaluated with the Child-Pugh score and the MELD score.

MRI acquisition and processing

All imaging was performed on a 3T United Imaging uMR 770 scanner equipped with a 32-channel head coil. Detailed acquisition sequences, parameters, and processing pipelines are provided in the Supplemental Material, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C329. Two radiologists independently counted bilateral CPCs and measured the longest and shortest diameters of the largest cyst, using their mean value. Discrepancies were adjudicated by a senior radiologist. CPV was segmented automatically on 3D T1-weighted imaging with a deep-learning algorithm.21 Liyuan Fu performed quality control and manual edits when needed, and Xiaoyang Wang independently confirmed the results. Diffusion-weighted imaging preprocessing followed standard steps in MRtrix3 and FSL 6.0.7 with registration to the JHU-ICBM-FA template. The ALPS index was calculated according to the published method by contrasting diffusivity components perpendicular to principal fiber orientations.13 Spherical ROIs 2.5 mm in diameter were placed bilaterally near the medullary veins at the level of the lateral ventricles on color-coded fractional anisotropy maps in FSLeyes; the exact ROI placement is illustrated in Supplemental Figure S1, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C329. Left, right, and bilateral means were extracted for analysis. Subcortical white matter and deep gray matter were segmented with FreeSurfer 8.0.22 Cortical thickness was estimated via the surface-based morphometry pipeline in CAT12 (SPM12, MATLAB R2022b).23

Statistical analysis

All the analyses were conducted by authors Li Chen and Zhiqiang Zhang via SPSS (version 25.0; IBM) or R (Version 4.4.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Assumption checks for normality and homoscedasticity preceded all analyses. Where these assumptions held, data were summarized as means ± SDs and between-group differences were tested with an independent-samples t test (2 groups) or 1-way ANOVA (3 groups); otherwise, results were presented as medians (IQRs) and group differences were assessed using the Mann-Whitney U test (2 groups) or the Kruskal-Wallis test (3 groups), with Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc analyses. Categorical variables were expressed as counts or percentages and compared using the χ² test. For inferential modeling, age, sex, education, and total intracranial volume were prespecified covariates. Group differences in CPV, ALPS index, and subcortical volumes were examined using ANCOVA with group as a fixed effect. Assumptions of slope and variance homogeneity were verified. Multiple comparisons were controlled with Bonferroni and Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate procedures. Vertex-wise whole-brain cortical thickness analyses used general linear models with the same covariates. Pairwise contrasts were evaluated with cluster-level family-wise error correction. Clusters were defined at an uncorrected vertex-level threshold of p<0.001, and significance was determined at the cluster level with P_family-wise error<0.05 with a minimum cluster size of k≥20. Partial Spearman rank correlations were computed for all numeric variables while controlling for the prespecified covariates, and p values were adjusted with the Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate. Mediation analyses were performed with SPSS PROCESS v3.2, incorporating the same covariates. All tests were 2-sided, with statistical significance defined as p<0.05 or a 95% CI that excluded 0.

RESULTS

Clinical and demographic characteristics

Data from 76 participants with cirrhosis and 34 HC candidates were screened. After exclusions, 90 participants were included, with 30 in each of the HE, NHE, and HC groups (Figure 1). As summarized in Table 1, there were no group differences in age, sex, and education. Cirrhosis etiology was comparable between the HE and NHE groups. No participant had an alcohol use history or exposure to sedating and psychotropic medications. Lactulose use at MRI was observed only in the HE group, reflecting secondary prophylaxis in participants with recent overt HE. Compared with NHE and HC, the HE group had lower MMSE, MoCA, and PHES scores (all p<0.001). Regarding clinical indices, the HE group showed higher serum ammonia and Child-Pugh scores but lower hemoglobin than NHE (p<0.001, p=0.012, p=0.033, respectively). No other clinical measures differed between groups (Supplemental Table S1, http://links.lww.com/HC9/C329).

CP imaging characteristics

Representative images of CPCs are shown in Figure 2. CPCs were present in 100% (30 of 30) of HE, 60% (18 of 30) of NHE, and 26.7% (8 of 30) of HC. Median counts were 11.5 (95% CI: 8.25, 15.75), 1 (95% CI: 0, 2.75), and 0 (95% CI: 0, 0.75), respectively (Table 1 and Figure 3A). The normalized mean cyst diameter was greater in HE (median, 3.68; 95% CI: 3.08, 4.24) than in NHE (median, 2.54; 95% CI: 2.21, 3.20; p=0.002) and HC (median, 2.93; 95% CI: 2.67, 3.08; p=0.023) (Table 1, Figure 3B). Thus, HE showed the highest prevalence and burden of CPCs. Normalized CPV was also higher in HE (median, 1.77; 95% CI: 1.51, 1.94) compared with NHE (median, 1.49; 95% CI: 1.25, 1.60; p=0.002) and HC (median, 1.36; 95% CI: 1.30, 1.58; p=0.002) (Figure 3C). DTI-ALPS analysis

Controlling for sex, age, education, and total intracranial volume, ANCOVA indicated that the mean and the left ALPS index were lower in HE than in both HC and NHE (all Bonferroni-corrected p<0.05; Figure 3D–E). Compared with HC, the NHE group also exhibited a reduced mean ALPS index (Bonferroni-corrected p=0.029; Figure 3D) and left ALPS index (Bonferroni-corrected p=0.046; Figure 3E). Overall, the ALPS index declined stepwise across groups, highest in HC, intermediate in NHE, and lowest in HE.

Subcortical structure volumes

Across multiple subcortical structures, the main effect of group was significant. False discovery rate-corrected post hoc comparisons showed that (Figure 4A), relative to HC, both HE and NHE had reduced volumes of the nucleus accumbens area, caudate, putamen, and thalamus, along with increased total CSF volume and enlarged lateral ventricles. Notably, HE displayed smaller volumes of the amygdala, caudate, putamen, and hippocampus than NHE. Taken together, HE was characterized by widespread atrophy of deep gray matter with ventricular enlargement and increased total CSF volume, whereas NHE generally fell between HE and HC, consistent with a severity-related gradient.

Cortical thickness

Compared with HC, HE exhibited widespread cortical thinning, predominantly involving the left superior frontal, insula, precentral, and precuneus, as well as the right caudal middle frontal, superior temporal, superior frontal, and superior parietal (Figure 4B). In NHE versus HC, thinning was mainly confined to the left insula and right pars triangularis (Figure 4C). No clusters survived family-wise error correction in the direct comparison between HE and NHE at the cluster level. Overall, both patient groups showed focal cortical thinning, with a broader extent in HE.

Correlation analyses

Across all participants, partial Spearman rank correlations (Figure 5A) showed that CPC burden was positively correlated with CPV and negatively with the ALPS index, MMSE, MoCA, PHES scores, most subcortical volumes, and cortical thickness. Conversely, the ALPS index exhibited positive relationships with cognitive scores, subcortical volumes, and cortical thickness. Higher CPV was aligned with lower cognitive scores, smaller volumes across multiple subcortical regions, and reduced cortical thickness, and increased with lateral ventricular volume. All associations remained significant after Benjamini-Hochberg correction (q<0.05). In analyses limited to HE and NHE, serum ammonia correlated positively with CPC number (r=0.768, p<0.001; Figure 5B), normalized CPC mean diameter (r=0.531, p=0.003; Figure 5C), and CPV (r=0.413, p=0.013; Figure 5D). The Child-Pugh score correlated negatively with left precuneus cortical thickness (r=−0.373, p=0.035; Figure 5E) and with right superior parietal cortical thickness (r=−0.481, p=0.002; Figure 5F). Overall, higher CPC burden and larger CPV were linked to worse structural profiles and poorer cognitive performance, whereas a lower ALPS index co-occurred with widespread structural abnormalities and cognitive impairment.

Serial mediation effects

A serial mediation analysis was performed using PROCESS v3.2 (Model 6) with 5000 bootstrap samples. Within the “CPV—mean ALPS index—right caudal middle frontal gyrus—cognition” model, CPV showed significant direct effects on PHES (β=−0.355, p<0.001, Figure 6A) and MoCA (β=−0.390, p<0.001, Figure 6B). Indirect effects of CPV operating sequentially through the mean ALPS index and cortical thickness of the right caudal middle frontal gyrus were also significant, as the bootstrap 95% CI for these serial indirect pathways did not include 0, consistent with partial mediation. These findings suggest that altered CP structure is associated with cognitive decline, with significant serial indirect associations involving impaired glymphatic clearance and downstream structural changes, in addition to significant direct associations.

DISCUSSION

Using 3T MRI, we found that participants with HE had higher CPC detection rates, greater CPC burden, and larger CPV than those in the NHE and HC groups. These differences co-occurred with lower values on the DTI-ALPS index, widespread subcortical atrophy, and cortical thinning. Correlation and mediation analyses suggested that the association between CP structural alterations and cognitive impairment involved both direct associations and serial indirect associations related to reduced glymphatic clearance and downstream structural injury. Collectively, these findings suggest that CPCs/CPV are not incidental findings in HE and may serve as an auxiliary imaging marker.

In this cohort, CPCs were present in all participants for HE, and both the number and the normalized diameter exceeded those in NHE and HC. This pattern argues against CPCs being incidental but is more compatible with an imaging response to sustained pathological burden. Three nonexclusive mechanisms may explain the observation. First, glymphatic impairment may contribute. Prior work indicates reduced glymphatic clearance in HE,24 and in our data, the ALPS index declined with disease severity, suggesting diminished clearance. Under hyperammonemia and inflammation, altered aquaporin localization and blood-CSF barrier dysfunction can disturb CSF hydrodynamics and metabolic clearance, promoting local CSF retention and cyst formation. Second, a cellular defensive response may be involved. Vacuolation has been proposed as a CP defense against toxic load,25 potentially facilitating cyst-like changes. Third, histological remodeling with hydrodynamic redistribution is plausible. CPV was markedly increased in HE, and evidence from Alzheimer disease indicates that CP enlargement can reflect remodeling under chronic metabolic and inflammatory stress with impaired clearance.26 Persistent stress on barrier and transport functions may thicken the extracellular matrix and interstitium, remodel the basement membrane and microvasculature, and alter permeability and pressure gradients, creating a microenvironment conducive to cyst formation and expansion.

Notably, the link between glymphatic dysfunction and cognitive impairment does not appear to be unique to HE, but has shown a degree of consistency across different disease contexts. Recent multiomics studies further suggest that glymphatic dysfunction may function as an intermediate phenotype linking systemic metabolic disturbance, brain microenvironmental imbalance, and cognitive decline. For example, in white matter hyperintensities, integrated analyses of the gut microbiome, metabolome, and multimodal MRI identified a plausible microbiota-metabolite-glymphatic function-cognition framework, in which the ALPS index was incorporated into the mediation pathway.27 Similarly, in schizophrenia, gut dysbiosis, reduced ALPS indices, and cognitive impairment have also been observed to be interrelated.28 Taken together, these findings support the view that glymphatic dysfunction may represent a shared intermediate process associated with cognitive impairment across multiple brain disorders, providing a broader context for the concurrent CP alterations, impaired glymphatic clearance, and cognitive decline observed in HE.

Beyond CP phenotypes, we observed widespread subcortical atrophy and cortical thinning in cirrhosis, with broader involvement in HE, consistent with prior HE neuroimaging literature. At the whole-brain systems level, using topographic co-localization based on the network parcellation of Thomas Yeo et al,29 we found that cortical thinning in HE primarily mapped to core or adjacent nodes of the frontoparietal control, dorsal attention, salience, and default mode networks. Subcortical volume loss involved nuclei within the cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical loop, the hippocampus-nucleus reuniens-medial prefrontal pathway with coupling to the control and default networks, and the basolateral amygdala-mediodorsal thalamus-prefrontal pathway, with projections to the insula and anterior cingulate. Inefficient cortico-striato-thalamo-cortical loop gating undermines task-set maintenance and top-down attention,30 manifesting as executive dysfunction, reduced vigilance, and psychomotor slowing. This loop also serves as an integrative hub for executive control and affective regulation.31 Its disruption may lead to cognitive decline and psychiatric manifestations such as anxiety, agitation, and apathy. Disturbance of hippocampal-prefrontal coupling and of interactions between hippocampal, control, and default networks would impair episodic memory and internally oriented processing.32,33 Imbalance within amygdala-thalamus-prefrontal circuitry and altered modulation of the insula and anterior cingulate may destabilize salience-driven switching between control and default networks, yielding task-switching difficulty and affective lability.34 Notably, these circuits converge on the thalamus, a key relay and gain-control hub, underscoring its central role in cortical functional organization.35 Against the backdrop of widespread gray-matter loss, enlarged lateral ventricles and increased total CSF volume indicate global atrophy with altered fluid dynamics, consistent with reports that greater atrophy correlates with cognitive decline in cirrhosis.36

This study has several limitations. First, this was a single-center, cross-sectional study with a relatively small sample and heterogeneity across the HE spectrum. These features limit causal inference and prevent fine-grained characterization of stage-specific dynamics between disease course and CPC burden. Future studies should expand enrollment across multiple centers and include stratified analyses by disease stage. In addition, differences in HE-directed therapy may have influenced neuropsychological performance and, therefore, cannot be excluded as a potential source of residual confounding. Second, the ALPS index was used as a readout of glymphatic clearance, which may be influenced by local white matter microstructural features and thereby introduce bias.37 Third, the mechanisms underlying the high prevalence of CPCs in HE remain inferential. Independent evidence from animal models and histopathology, including assessments of CP barrier and secretory function, aquaporin localization, and CSF hydrodynamics, is needed to validate these hypotheses.

CONCLUSIONS

In summary, our findings underscore the CP as a plausible contributor at the CSF–brain interface in HE and highlight CPC burden and CPV as candidate MRI phenotypes. In conjunction with the ALPS index, our results are consistent with a framework in which CP phenotypes may relate to neurocognitive impairment through reduced glymphatic clearance function and downstream structural brain vulnerability. Future multicenter, longitudinal studies integrating MRI with histopathological and other mechanistic evidence are warranted to clarify the biological basis of CPC formation and to determine the clinical utility of CPCs/CPV in cirrhosis with HE.
