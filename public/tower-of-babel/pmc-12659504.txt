Alistipes putredinis CCUG 45780T exacerbates DSS-induced colitis in mice via modulation of gut microbiota and succinate metabolism
Authors: Min Niu, Qiu-yue He, Ying-xuan Chen, Shu-min Liu, Yu-qing Pan, Xin-rui Zhu, Ying-lei Miao, Yan Du
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlistipes is a relatively new genus in the phylum Bacteroidetes, and its role in UC remains unclear. Among its members, Alistipes putredinis is particularly prominent. In this study, we investigated the effects of Alistipes putredinis CCUG 45780T on colitis using both in vitro and in vivo experiments, aiming to provide a theoretical basis for understanding the contribution of gut microbiota to UC pathogenesis.ResultsWhole-genome sequencing of Alistipes putredinis CCUG 45780ᵀ revealed 13 virulence genes and 11 resistance genes. Targeted metabolomic analysis identified 9 metabolites significantly increased in the bacterial culture supernatant, among which only succinate exhibited proinflammatory effects. Oral administration of Alistipes putredinis CCUG 45780ᵀ did not cause overt pathogenicity in healthy mice but significantly exacerbated DSS-induced colitis, as indicated by weight loss, elevated disease activity index (DAI) scores, colon shortening, and aggravated mucosal damage. This was accompanied by markedly increased expression of TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and NLRP3. Moreover, treatment altered the gut microbial community, increasing Bacteroidetes abundance while reducing Actinobacteria and Lactobacillus, elevated colonic succinate levels, and upregulated SUCNR1 expression. Collectively, these findings suggest that Alistipes putredinis CCUG 45780ᵀ aggravates colitis through microbiota modulation and succinate-mediated inflammation.ConclusionAlistipes putredinis CCUG 45780ᵀ exacerbates DSS-induced colitis in mice, likely through gut microbiota dysregulation and succinate-driven inflammatory pathways.Keywords: Alistipes putredinis, Ulcerative colitis, Gut microbiota, Metabolites, Succinate

Background

Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic, recurrent inflammatory disease of the colon that substantially reduces patient quality of life and may lead to severe complications such as toxic megacolon and colorectal cancer. In 2023, the global prevalence of UC was estimated at ~ 5 million cases, with incidence rates rising sharply in low- and middle-income countries [1, 2]. The onset of UC is thought to involve a sustained immune response to gut microbiota in genetically susceptible individuals. Sequencing-based studies of the human gut microbiome have revealed an increased abundance of Bacteroidetes in UC patients [3–5], though their roles vary across species [6–8]. Our previous work demonstrated that non-enterotoxigenic Bacteroides fragilis exerted protective effects against experimental colitis in mice [9].

Alistipes, a recently identified genus within the phylum Bacteroidetes, was first isolated from pediatric appendicitis samples in 2003 [10]. Taxonomically, it belongs to the phylum Bacteroidetes, class Bacteroidia, order Bacteroidales, and family Rikenellaceae. According to the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) taxonomy database, 13 Alistipes species had been documented by April 2020 [11]. Alistipes is typically present in the healthy human gut, with lower infection rates compared to other Bacteroidetes members, yet it has been implicated in several microbiota-associated diseases [11]. The relationship between Alistipes and UC remains poorly defined. Most studies suggest that Alistipes abundance is negatively correlated with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) [12–14]. For example, Lin et al. reported that oral administration of Alistipes shahii alleviated colitis by reducing proinflammatory cytokine release, protecting the intestinal epithelial barrier, and modulating gut microbial and metabolic profiles [15]. In contrast, Fu et al. showed that Alistipes-associated TNF-α and IL-6 might activate LRG1/TGF-β1 signaling, promoting colorectal cancer (CRC) development, thereby highlighting a potential proinflammatory role of Alistipes in carcinogenesis [16].

Among Alistipes genus, Alistipes putredinis is of particular interest, yet its relationship with UC has not been reported. In this study, we investigated the effects and underlying mechanisms of Alistipes putredinis CCUG 45780ᵀ in UC using both in vitro bacterial assays and an in vivo DSS-induced colitis mouse model, providing new insights into the role of the gut microbiota in UC pathogenesis.

Methods

Bacterial cultivation and identification

The standard strain Alistipes putredinis CCUG 45780ᵀ was obtained from the Industrial Microbial Strain Engineering Technology Research Center of Henan Province (Zhengzhou, Henan, China). The strain was revived on Columbia blood agar plates and cultured under anaerobic conditions (5% H₂, 10% CO₂, and 85% N₂) at 37 °C for 3–5 days. Single colonies were observed by Gram staining and examined under a 100× optical microscope. Strain identification was performed using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS), and oxygen tolerance was assessed through aerobic growth experiments.

Whole-genome sequencing of Alistipes putredinis CCUG 45780ᵀ

Total DNA was extracted from the strain and subjected to whole-genome sequencing using the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Gene prediction and annotation were conducted with Prokka (v1.10) and the NCBI non-redundant (nr) database. Functional annotation was performed using the Gene Ontology (GO), Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG), and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) databases. Virulence and antibiotic resistance genes were predicted using the Virulence Factor Database (VFDB) and the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD). Genomic maps were generated to visualize genome structure, virulence, and resistance gene distributions. Targeted metabolomics detection of the culture supernatant of Alistipes putredinis CCUG 45780T.

Targeted metabolomics of Alistipes putredinis CCUG 45780ᵀ culture supernatant

The strain was cultured in improved minced meat broth medium (ATCC medium 1490; TOPBIO, Shandong, China). After 5 days of incubation, the culture medium was collected and centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 15 min to remove bacterial cells. The resulting supernatant was analyzed for key metabolites involved in energy metabolism using liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS). Uninoculated medium served as the control. Each group included three biological replicates. Metabolite extraction was followed by separation using a Shimadzu Nexera X2 LC-30AD high-performance liquid chromatography system(Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). Detection was performed on a QTRAP 5500 mass spectrometer (AB SCIEX)(Framingham, MA, USA) in both positive and negative ion modes. Chromatographic peak areas and retention times were processed with MultiQuant software.

Animal experiments

Experimental animals

Specific pathogen–free (SPF)–grade male C57BL/6J mice (6–8 weeks old) were purchased from Beijing SPF Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). All animal experiments were conducted in the Experimental Animal Department of Kunming Medical University, with ethical approval from the Animal Ethics Committee (Approval No. kmmu20241275).

Toxicity testing of Alistipes putredinis CCUG 45780ᵀ

After a 7-day acclimatization period, mice were randomly divided into two groups (n = 10 per group): a control group and an Alistipes putredinis (AP) group. The AP group received 200 µL of Alistipes putredinis CCUG 45780ᵀ bacterial suspension (1 × 10⁹ CFU/day) via oral gavage, while the control group received 200 µL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) daily. Food intake, fecal characteristics, and body weight were monitored daily. The experimental design is illustrated in Fig. 4A.

On day 14, mice were anesthetized, and blood samples were collected via orbital venipuncture. Mice were then euthanized, and the colon, liver, spleen, and kidney were collected within 20 min. Blood samples were analyzed for white blood cell and neutrophil counts using a Mindray small animal hematology analyzer(Shenzhen, Guangdong, China). Colon, liver, spleen, and kidney tissues were subjected to histopathological examination, and inflammatory factor expression in the colon was assessed.

Detection of the effects of Alistipes putredinis CCUG 45780ᵀ on mice with colitis

After a 7-day acclimatization period, mice were randomly assigned to three groups (n = 10 per group): DSS, DSS + AP, and control. Mice in the DSS + AP group received 200 µL of Alistipes putredinis CCUG 45780ᵀ suspension (1 × 10⁹ CFU/day) by oral gavage for 21 days, while mice in the DSS and control groups received 200 µL of PBS daily for the same duration.

On day 15, drinking water for the DSS and DSS + AP groups was replaced with 3.0% (w/v) dextran sulfate sodium (DSS) solution (YEASEN, Shanghai, China). From day 15 onward, food intake, fecal consistency, activity/mental state, and body weight were monitored daily. Fecal samples were collected, and fecal occult blood was tested using the ortho-methylbenzidine method. The experimental design is shown in Fig. 5A.

qPCR detection of the colon

Approximately 100 mg of colon tissue was homogenized, and total RNA was extracted using the Total RNA Extraction Kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China). RNA concentration and purity were measured spectrophotometrically. One microgram of RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the abm 5× All-In-One RT MasterMix Kit (Richmond, BC, Canada). Gene expression levels of TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, NLRP3, and SUCNR1 were quantified using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Tiangen, Beijing, China) on a Roche LightCycler® 96 real-time PCR system (Basel, Basel-Stadt, Switzerland). Primer sequences are listed in Table 3. The PCR cycling conditions were: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 120 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 15 s, with a final melting curve analysis. β-actin served as the internal reference gene, and data were analyzed using the 2^-ΔΔCt method [17].

Fecal microbiota 16 S rRNA sequencing

Total DNA was extracted from fecal samples, and sequencing libraries were prepared via PCR amplification, purification, and recovery. Paired-end high-throughput sequencing was performed using the NovaSeq platform.

Bioinformatic analyses were conducted as follows: α-diversity was evaluated to assess richness and evenness based on ASVs/OTUs, and sequencing depth was confirmed by rarefaction curves. At the ASV/OTU level, distance matrices were constructed, and unsupervised clustering and ordination methods, combined with statistical tests, were applied to evaluate β-diversity between groups. Taxonomic composition analyses were performed at multiple taxonomic levels using supervised and unsupervised approaches, coupled with statistical modeling, to identify differences in microbial abundance and potential biomarker taxa.

Quantitative detection of the succinate concentration in the colon

Absolute quantification of succinate in colonic contents samples was performed using LC–MS/MS, following the same protocol described above for metabolite detection.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 24 software. Data are expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Differences between two groups were analyzed using Student’s t-test, whereas one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by least significant difference (LSD) post hoc testing was applied for multiple group comparisons. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Strain characteristics of Alistipes putredinis CCUG 45780T

Alistipes putredinis CCUG 45780T was cultured on Colombian blood agar plates for 4 days, forming gray, moist, nonhemolytic small colonies (Fig. 1A). Microscopic examination of the strain smear revealed Gram-negative short rod-shaped bacteria (Fig. 1B). The bacterium showed weak growth and gas production in improved minced meat culture medium. Oxygen tolerance tests indicated a gradual decrease in viable bacteria with increasing exposure time, with a small fraction surviving after 120 min (Fig. 1C). MALDI-TOF MS analysis confirmed the strain as Alistipes putredinis, with an identification score above 2.0 (Fig. 1D).

Whole-genome sequencing of alistipes putredinis CCUG 45780T

Genome sequencing revealed a total genome length of 1,373,322,789 bp with a GC content of 53.63%. The chromosome is circular, 2,599,205 bp in length, and contains 2,479 genes, including 2,423 coding sequences, 6 rRNA genes, 49 tRNA genes, 1 ncRNA gene, and 4 repetitive sequences(Fig. 2A).

Whole-genome sequencing of Alistipes putredinis CCUG 45780T. A Genomic map of bacterial strains; (B) Classification of homologous species in the NR database; (C) COG annotation distribution map; (D) GO annotation distribution map; (E) KEGG pathway classification map

Functional annotation against NR, COG, GO, and KEGG databases indicated that most genes could be annotated. The analysis of the NR database annotation for the sequenced genome revealed that the vast majority of the top BLAST hits were assigned to species within the genus Alistipes (Fig. 2B). The most abundant species annotation was Alistipes sp. (1,193 hits), followed closely by Alistipes putredinis (461 hits) and its specific strain Alistipes putredinis DSM 17,216 (302 hits). In summary, the NR annotation profile strongly supports the classification of the sequenced isolate as Alistipes putredinis.

The analysis of the COG database annotation provides a comprehensive overview of the strain’s functional potential and metabolic capabilities. The majority of the predicted protein-coding genes were assigned to COG categories. Among these, the most predominant category was general function prediction only, followed by translation, ribosomal structure, biogenesis and cell wall/membrane/envelop biogenesis (Fig. 2C). The COG functional profile depicts Alistipes putredinis CCUG 45780T as a metabolically versatile bacterium, equipped with the genetic tools for nutrient acquisition, environmental adaptation, and persistence in the gastrointestinal tract.

The GO analysis categorizes gene functions into three independent domains: Biological Process, Cellular Component, and Molecular Function (Fig. 2D). In the Biological Process domain, genes involved in cellular processes and metabolic processes constituted the most abundant category. Within the Cellular Component domain, the vast majority of genes were predicted to be associated with the protein containning complex and organelles. Regarding Molecular Function, genes encoding proteins with catalytic activity formed the predominant group. In summary, the GO functional profile depicts Alistipes putredinis CCUG 45780T as a metabolically active bacterium equipped with sophisticated sensory and regulatory systems.

The KEGG database annotation analysis revealed that a predominant portion of the annotated genes were associated with Metabolism, underscoring the organism’s strong metabolic capabilities essential for survival and competition in the gut ecosystem. Within the Metabolism module, the most prominently represented pathways included: carbohydrate metabolism, amino acid metabolism, energy metabolism and cofactors and vitamins metabolism(Fig. 2E). Beyond metabolism, a significant number of genes were involved in: Genetic Information Processing, Environmental Information Processing and Cellular Processes. The KEGG pathway annotation portrays Alistipes putredinis CCUG 45780T as a metabolically versatile bacterium, genetically adept at nutrient acquisition, energy production, and environmental sensing.

Virulence gene prediction using the VFDB database identified 13 Set A core virulence genes (e.g., katA, htpB, clpP) associated with adhesion, anti-phagocytosis, and magnesium ion uptake, and 28 Set B genes related to surface structures and environmental adaptability. Drug resistance gene prediction identified 11 genes conferring resistance to macrolides, tetracyclines, and aminoglycosides, mostly linked to efflux pumps, with a few associated with target modifications or alternative protection mechanisms.

Targeted metabolomics detection of the culture supernatant of Alistipes putredinis CCUG 45780T

LC‒MS/MS analysis of the culture supernatant revealed 36 metabolites related to energy metabolism. Univariate statistical analysis highlighted 13 differentially abundant metabolites, 9 of which were significantly upregulated, including phosphoenolpyruvate, pyruvate, and D-glucose-6-phosphate, indicating key metabolites in energy metabolism. Among these, succinate may possess potential proinflammatory properties (Fig. 3A-B).

Targeted metabolomics detection of the culture supernatant of Alistipes putredinis CCUG 45780T. A Metabolite heatmap of bacterial strains; B Bar chart of differentially abundant metabolites

Alistipes putredinis CCUG 45780T has no pathogenicity in healthy mice

During gavage with the bacterial suspension, both the PBS control and AP groups of mice maintained normal diet, activity, mental state, and fecal consistency, with steadily increasing body weights. On day 14, there was no statistically significant difference in weight gain between the groups (P ≥ 0.05; Fig. 4B). Similarly, peripheral white blood cell and neutrophil counts showed no significant differences (P ≥ 0.05; Fig. 4C). H&E staining of the colon, spleen, liver, and kidneys revealed no inflammatory cell infiltration or notable pathological changes (Fig. 4D). qPCR analysis of colonic inflammatory factors indicated no significant differences in TNF-α and IL-6 expression between groups (P ≥ 0.05; Fig. 4E). These results indicate that Alistipes putredinis CCUG 45780T does not exhibit pathogenicity in healthy mice and behaves as a symbiotic anaerobic gut bacterium.

Alistipes putredinis CCUG 45780T exacerbates DSS-induced colitis in mice

Following administration of 3.0% (w/v) DSS solution, mice in the DSS and DSS + AP groups began to show reduced activity, weight loss, diarrhea, and bloody stools from day 4, confirming successful induction of acute colitis. By day 7, body weights of the DSS + AP group were significantly lower than those of the DSS and control groups (P < 0.05; Fig. 5B). Both DSS-treated groups exhibited increased disease activity index (DAI) scores from day 4 onward, with the DSS + AP group showing significantly higher scores than the DSS and control groups on day 7 (P < 0.05; Fig. 5C). Colon length was significantly reduced in both DSS-treated groups compared to the control (P < 0.05; Fig. 5D-E). Spleen size and spleen index were significantly elevated in the DSS + AP group relative to the DSS and control groups (P < 0.05; Fig. 5F-G).

H&E staining revealed extensive mucosal damage, crypt loss, and inflammatory cell infiltration in both DSS-treated groups, with more severe tissue damage and higher pathological scores in the DSS + AP group (P < 0.05; Fig. 6A and C). AB-PAS staining showed reduced mucin content in both DSS-treated groups, with a more pronounced reduction in the DSS + AP group (Fig. 6B). qPCR analysis of colonic inflammatory factors demonstrated significantly higher expression of TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, and NLRP-3 in the DSS + AP group compared to the DSS and control groups (P < 0.05; Fig. 6D-G). These findings indicate that Alistipes putredinis CCUG 45780T exacerbates DSS-induced colitis in mice.

Effects of Alistipes putredinis CCUG 45780T on the gut microbiota in DSS-induced colitis mice

Species composition analysis

At the phylum level, the dominant taxa in the control group were Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, Proteobacteria, and Actinobacteria. In the DSS and DSS + AP groups, the phylum composition shifted: the DSS + AP group was dominated by Firmicutes, Verrucomicrobia, Bacteroidetes, and Proteobacteria. Compared with the DSS group, gavage with Alistipes putredinis CCUG45780T significantly increased Bacteroidetes abundance and decreased Actinobacteria abundance (Fig. 7A).

At the genus level, dominant genera in the control group were Allobaculum, Lactobacillus, and Desulfovibrio. The DSS + AP group showed a significant decrease in Lactobacillus and a significant increase in Alistipes compared with the DSS group (Fig. 7B).

Analysis of α diversity and β diversity

α Diversity was assessed using multiple indices: richness (Chao1 and observed species), diversity (Shannon and Simpson), evolutionary diversity (Faith’s PD), evenness (Pielou’s), and coverage (Good’s). No significant differences were observed among the control, DSS, and DSS + AP groups for Chao1, Shannon, observed species, or Faith’s PD (P ≥ 0.05). However, the Simpson index, Pielou’s evenness, and Good’s coverage were significantly lower in the DSS and DSS + AP groups compared with the control (P < 0.05). No significant differences were observed between the DSS and DSS + AP groups across all indices (P ≥ 0.05), indicating that gavage with Alistipes putredinis CCUG 45780T did not significantly alter α diversity (Fig. 7C).

β Diversity was evaluated using principal coordinate analysis (PCoA). Samples from the DSS and DSS + AP groups partially overlapped, with the first principal component contributing 14.4% and the second component contributing 6.2% of the observed variation (Fig. 7D). These results suggest that Alistipes putredinis CCUG 45780T had limited impact on the overall community structure of the gut microbiota in DSS-induced colitis mice.

Analysis of species differences

Using the ASV/OTU abundance table, a Venn diagram was created to characterize the unique ASV/OTU species in each group. 2944, 2944, and 3622 microbial amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were detected in the control group, DSS group, and DSS + AP group, respectively. Among them, 2677 ASVs were unique to the DSS + AP group (Fig. 7E).

To display the distribution trend of species abundance in each group, a heatmap was drawn using the abundance data of the top 50 genera with average abundance for species composition analysis (Fig. 7F). Compared with those in the control group and DSS group, the bacterial genera with increased abundance in the DSS + AP group were Olsenella, Enterococcus, Bifidobacterium, Parabacterides, Enterobacter, Odorobacter, Escherichia, Alistipes, Sutterella, and Jeotgalicoccus. The genera with decreased abundance included Christensenella, Blautia, Adlercreutzia, Ruminococcus, Dehalobacterium, and Aerococcus.

LEfSe analysis was used to screen for differentially expressed species between groups, and a total of 9 differentially expressed species were identified in the DSS + AP group. Among them, the differentially expressed species at the genus level were Alistipes, Bacteroides, and Parabacteroides (Fig. 7G).

Succinate is the primary organic acid produced by Alistipes in vitro and has been reported to exert pro-inflammatory effects in vivo and in vitro. Metabolomic analysis of the Alistipes putredinis CCUG 45780T culture supernatant confirmed high succinate production during energy metabolism. Therefore, succinate levels in the colonic contents of DSS and DSS + AP mice were quantified via LC‒MS/MS. Succinate concentration in the DSS + AP group was significantly higher than in the DSS group (P < 0.05; Fig. 8A).

Discussion

Alistipes is a recently identified genus within the phylum Bacteroidetes, first isolated and characterized in 2003. This genus predominantly colonizes the gut microbiota of healthy humans [10]. To date, few studies have explored the association of Alistipes with human diseases. In 2020, Fernando et al. reported a case of human abdominal infection caused by Alistipes onderdonkii. [18]. Subsequently, Minguela et al. described peritonitis in a peritoneal dialysis patient caused by Alistipes finegoldii in 2021 [19], and in 2023, Fernando reported bacteremia secondary to Alistipes finegoldii-induced peritonitis [20]. Emerging evidence also suggests a potential role for Alistipes in CRC. Fu et al. reported that Alistipes abundance was positively correlated with elevated inflammatory cytokines, indicating proinflammatory properties during CRC progression [16], while Hasan et al. identified Alistipes as a tissue-specific bacterial biomarker for CRC [21]. Alistipes putredinis is one of the earliest described species within the genus Alistipes [11] and is consistently among the most abundant members in human gut microbiome surveys [22]. Despite its prominence, its potential roles in human health and disease remain largely unexplored. To address this knowledge gap, we have chosen Alistipes putredinis as the focus of this study.

Conversely, several studies have highlighted potential beneficial effects of Alistipes on human health. Kwan et al. demonstrated that oral administration of Alistipes onderdonkii reduced TNF production in T cells and antigen-presenting cells, thereby improving allogeneic transplant survival [23]. Similarly, Lee et al. reported that Alistipes onderdonkii inhibited pancreatic cancer cell proliferation in vitro [24]. Xu et al. found that oral administration of Alistipes indistinctus enhanced intestinal barrier function, reduced hepatic inflammation, increased Lactobacilli abundance, and prevented the progression of nonalcoholic fatty liver disease [25].

Most studies suggest that Alistipes is negatively correlated with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) [14]; however, its potential protective role remains insufficiently explored. Lin et al. showed that oral administration of Alistipes shahii modulated inflammatory cytokine release, reduced intestinal epithelial barrier damage, and altered gut microbiota composition and fecal metabolites, alleviating colitis symptoms in mice [15]. Similarly, Zhong et al. reported that Alistipes onderdonkii and its metabolite 5-hydroxyindole-3-acetic acid effectively alleviated DSS-induced colitis in mice [26]. Dziarski et al. found that Alistipes finegoldii mitigated DSS-induced colitis in both wild-type and antibiotic-depleted mice, suggesting a protective role against colitis [27].

In contrast, our study demonstrated that oral administration of Alistipes putredinis CCUG 45,780T did not cause significant pathogenicity in healthy mice, but significantly increased susceptibility to DSS-induced colitis, exacerbating disease severity. This finding differs from previous reports and may reflect species-specific differences within the genus Alistipes, with distinct bacterial characteristics determining their roles in ulcerative colitis. Through whole genome sequencing of Alistipes putredinis CCUG 45780T, we identified 13 virulence factor genes, such as adhesion related genes (such as katA, htpB, and clpP) and genes involved in anti phagocytic effects (such as cpsA, algI). While these genes are often annotated in pathogens, their relevance to UC is significant. Functional annotation against NR, COG, GO, and KEGG databases indicated that Alistipes putredinis CCUG 45780T as a metabolically versatile bacterium, equipped with the genetic tools for nutrient acquisition, environmental adaptation, and persistence in the gastrointestinal tract.

Alistipes putredinis CCUG 45780T is non-pathogenic to normal mice, but exacerbates the development of colitis in mice with DSS-induced colitis. We explain that healthy mice have a complete intestinal barrier, a balanced microbial community, and normal immune function. In this case, although Alistipes putredinis possesses virulence genes, it cannot overcome these defenses and may colonize or be cleared at low levels without causing significant pathology. The “two-hit” model can be used to explain that DSS-induced colitis creates a permissive environment (severely disrupts the homeostatic balance) and Alistipes putredinis exploits the permissive environment(enhanced colonization).

To investigate the effect of oral administration of Alistipes putredinis CCUG 45780T on gut microbiota in colitis mice, we performed 16 S rRNA sequencing on the colonic contents. Following gavage, the abundance of Alistipes significantly increased, indicating successful colonization of the strain in the intestinal tract. α-Diversity analysis revealed no significant differences in the Chao1 or Shannon indices between the strain-gavaged group and the disease group, suggesting that Alistipes putredinis CCUG 45780T colonization did not markedly affect overall gut microbiota diversity. However, significant changes in species composition were observed. At the phylum level, Bacteroidetes abundance increased while Actinobacteria abundance decreased. At the genus level, Lactobacillus abundance decreased. Differential species analysis also indicated that gavage with the bacterial strain increased the abundance of certain opportunistic pathogens, such as Enterococcus, Enterobacter, and Escherichia. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that colonization by Alistipes putredinis CCUG 45780T disrupts gut microbiota homeostasis and alters the dominant bacterial populations. It is unclear how Alistipes putredinis interacts with gut microbiota. To further investigate and solve this problem, we plan to conduct the following research: use germ-free or antibiotic-treated mice colonized with a simplified microbial community to better dissect these bacterial interactions without the confounding factor of a complex background microbiota.

To explore whether Alistipes putredinis CCUG 45780T exacerbates experimental colitis through its metabolites, we performed targeted metabolomic analysis of its in vitro culture supernatant. Among the nine major energy metabolism-related metabolites identified, only succinate exhibited potential pro-inflammatory effects. Succinate, a metabolic intermediate of the tricarboxylic acid cycle in host cells, is primarily derived from the intestinal microbiota [28]. Elevated luminal succinate levels are associated with gut microbiota dysbiosis and can accumulate in areas of inflammation and metabolic stress [29, 30]. Fremder et al. reported that fecal and serum succinate concentrations in IBD patients were significantly elevated, corresponding to changes in succinate-metabolizing gut bacteria [31]. Similarly, metabolomic analysis of the intestinal mucosa of IBD patients revealed higher succinate levels in inflamed tissues compared to healthy controls [32]. Dalal et al. reported that succinate-producing bacteria and succinate levels are increased in the intestines of UC patients, with succinate exacerbating colitis via activation of TH9 cells [33]. Diandra et al. found that plasma succinate levels in patients with active Crohn’s disease were significantly higher than those in inactive disease or healthy controls, and were correlated with the inflammatory marker high-sensitivity C-reactive protein [34].

Succinate exerts its biological effects primarily through succinate receptor 1 (SUCNR-1) [35, 36]. SUCNR1, a G protein-coupled receptor, regulates cellular energy metabolism [37, 38] and can promote immune cell activation and inflammation upon activation [39, 40]. Firstly, succinate may bind to the SUCNR-1 of macrophages and upregulate the expression of IL-1 β by stabilizing HIF-1 α [41]. Secondly, succinate may upregulate the expression of ASC, caspase-1, and IL-1 β/IL-18 by enhancing the activation of NLRP3 inflammasome [42]. Again, succinate may bind to the SUCNR-1 of antigen-presenting cells, enhance their antigen presentation ability, promote the differentiation of immature T cells into pro-inflammatory Th17 cells, and may also inhibit the function of regulatory T cells (Treg) [43].

In this study, oral administration of Alistipes putredinis CCUG 45780T increased succinate levels in the intestines of colitis mice and upregulated colonic SUCNR-1 gene expression. These results reveal that Alistipes putredinis–derived succinate exacerbates colitis is supported by emerging evidence on the role of succinate as a pro-inflammatory metabokine. However, further research is required to elucidate the underlying mechanism. For example, studies using SUCNR1 knockout mice or SUNCR1 antagonists to determine the direct causal effect of this pathway and exclude contributions from other bacterial components.

Conclusion

Alistipes putredinis CCUG 45780T does not exhibit significant pathogenicity in healthy mice but significantly exacerbates DSS-induced colitis. This effect may be mediated, at least in part, through the bacterial metabolite succinate.
