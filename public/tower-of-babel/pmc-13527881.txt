Interactions Between E3 Ubiquitin Ligases and Deubiquitinases in AKT Regulation: Implications in Skeletal Muscle Homeostasis
Authors: Rajesh Dabur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAKT (protein kinase B, PKB) coordinates the balance between anabolic and catabolic signaling in skeletal muscle through distinct ubiquitin chain types. Some E3 ubiquitin ligases (E3s) and deubiquitinases (DUBs) form stable binary complexes via non‐catalytic interfaces, adding a regulatory layer unavailable to either enzyme alone. This mechanistic synthesis review presents a systematic literature analysis (inception to May 2026; 26 eligible studies). It identified four E3–DUB pairs proposed to regulate AKT in skeletal muscle. These are TRAF6–CYLD (plasma‐membrane K63‐ubiquitination), MUL1–USP9X (mitochondrial K48‐ubiquitination of AKT2), CHIP–UCH37 (proteasome‐proximal quality control), and SCF‐Skp2–USP37 (PHLPP1/2‐dependent control of AKT Ser473 phosphorylation). All four interfaces are structurally separate from the catalytic sites and are regulated by upstream kinase phosphorylation. Evidence for the four pairs is markedly uneven. TRAF6–CYLD is supported by endogenous co‐immunoprecipitation and functional data in muscle models. CHIP and UCH37 each act on AKT‐related substrates independently and are individually well documented, but a direct CHIP–UCH37 interaction has not itself been demonstrated. SCF‐Skp2–USP37 interaction data rest on a real but non‐muscle direct interaction, whereas MUL1–USP9X has no reported direct interaction at all; CHIP–UCH37, SCF‐Skp2–USP37, and MUL1–USP9X are therefore all presented as testable hypotheses of varying strength. In chronic atrophy, available data are consistent with disruption of these complexes contributing to AKT suppression through parallel, largely independent mechanisms. However, simultaneous disruption of all four has not been demonstrated in a single system. Available gene expression and protein datasets from sarcopenic muscle broadly support these predictions, though direct experimental validation in human tissue remains pending. This complex‐centric framework recasts AKT ubiquitination as an integrated regulatory framework. Each structurally autonomous interface may represent a potentially distinct target for muscle‐wasting conditions that currently lack approved therapies.Keywords: AKT ubiquitination, deubiquitinase, E3 ubiquitin ligase, muscle atrophy, sarcopenia, skeletal muscle, ubiquitin chain topology

Introduction

AKT, also referred to as protein kinase B (PKB), plays a crucial role in the signaling pathways activated by insulin and IGF‐1 in skeletal muscle. When stimulated by growth factors, phosphoinositide 3‐kinase (PI3K) converts phosphatidylinositol 4,5‐bisphosphate (PIP2) to phosphatidylinositol 3,4,5‐trisphosphate (PIP3) at the inner surface of the plasma membrane. The increase in PIP3 then recruits AKT through its pleckstrin homology domain. This recruitment allows for phosphorylation of AKT at T308 by PDK1 and at Ser473 by mTORC2 (mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 2). Together, these events lead to the full activation of the kinase [1, 2]. Activated AKT phosphorylates and inhibits FOXO transcription factors and suppresses the expression of the E3 ubiquitin ligases MuRF1 and MAFbx [3, 4]. It also activates mTORC1‐dependent protein synthesis and promotes GLUT4‐mediated glucose uptake [3, 4]. A decrease in AKT activity can result from weaker upstream signals, PHLPP1/2 (PH domain and leucine‐rich repeat protein phosphatase 1/2)‐mediated dephosphorylation [5, 6], or ubiquitin‐mediated degradation. This decrease promotes nuclear import of FOXO, leading to transcription of atrophic genes and culminating in sarcopenia, cachexia, and denervation atrophy [7, 8].

Ubiquitination is a major post‐translational mechanism that regulates AKT. Once AKT is ubiquitinated, its fate in the cell depends on the type of chain. K63‐linked polyubiquitin chains support PIP3‐independent membrane targeting and achieve optimal T308/Ser473 phosphorylation [9]. In contrast, K48‐linked polyubiquitin chains result in proteasomal degradation and catalytic inactivation [10]. Each chain type built by an E3 is also uniquely cleaved by a specific DUB. In the classical state, E3s and DUBs are treated as parallel competitors, with the relative activities of the enzymes determining the fate of substrates. Growing evidence indicates that oversimplifying the interactions between E3 ubiquitin ligases and DUBs fails to capture their complexities [10, 11]. Some specific E3–DUB pairs form stable, direct complexes that provide regulatory functions that are more sophisticated than those of either enzyme on its own. These functions include three shared properties. First, the enzymes allosterically co‐regulate each other's active‐site geometry. Second, chain synthesis and chain editing occur simultaneously on the same substrate molecule, a process that can be called real‐time chain editing. Third, subcellular co‐localization confines chain‐topology decisions to specific organelle surfaces.

Throughout this review, “direct” interaction refers specifically to physical binary contact between two proteins. This contact must be demonstrated by at least one of the following: endogenous co‐immunoprecipitation from cell or tissue lysate, GST (glutathione S‐transferase) pull‐down assay using purified recombinant proteins, proximity ligation assay (PLA), or structural determination (x‐ray crystallography, cryo‐EM, or NMR). Indirect regulatory relationships, in which one protein affects the activity of another through one or more intermediaries without physical contact, are not described as “direct” interactions. Taking a complex‐centric view, this review highlights four E3–DUB pairs (TRAF6–CYLD, MUL1–USP9X, CHIP–UCH37, and SCF‐Skp2–USP37) that work together to regulate AKT in skeletal muscle. The disruption of these complexes in a stoichiometric manner is proposed as one potential explanation for the convergent AKT dysfunction observed across muscle‐wasting conditions. These four were selected from the substantially larger set of E3 ligases and DUBs reported to influence AKT (directly or indirectly) in some cellular context. Each was included because it met the direct‐interaction criterion defined above and had at least preliminary evidence of relevance to skeletal muscle or muscle‐relevant signaling. Pairs supported only by indirect, correlative, or purely transcriptional evidence, or documented solely in muscle‐irrelevant contexts with no evidence of conservation, were excluded at the eligibility stage. This selection is itself a limitation: it is a curated subset rather than an exhaustive survey of components of the AKT‐directed ubiquitin system. Other E3–DUB or E3‐only, DUB‐only regulators of AKT may prove equally or more important as evidence accumulates. This mechanistic synthesis review, therefore, focuses on the four interfaces as a conceptual framework with potential therapeutic implications. The four pairings are structurally independent and located far from the active sites of both partner enzymes. Each could therefore potentially be targeted by interface‐specific drugs that do not interfere with the essential catalytic functions of other cellular activities.

Review Methodology

PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, Web of Science, Embase, and Google Scholar were searched from inception through May 2026. The search used Boolean combinations of terms for E3 ubiquitin ligase, deubiquitinase, AKT/PKB, skeletal muscle, sarcopenia, and specific gene pairs (TRAF6/CYLD, MUL1/USP9X, CHIP/UCH37, and Skp2/USP37). After deduplication, 1214 records were identified; 26 studies met the inclusion criteria. This review follows the PRISMA 2020 reporting framework [12] insofar as it applies to a mechanistic narrative synthesis rather than a quantitative meta‐analysis. The flow of records through identification, screening, eligibility assessment, and inclusion is summarized in Figure 1.

Studies were eligible if they provided direct biochemical, structural, or genetic evidence of a physical E3–DUB interaction with regulatory consequences for AKT in muscle‐relevant experimental systems. Muscle‐relevant systems were operationally defined as skeletal muscle tissue or biopsy (human or animal); primary myoblast, myotube, or myofiber preparations; established muscle cell lines (e.g., C2C12, L6); or whole‐animal models of muscle atrophy, hypertrophy, or muscle‐specific genetic manipulation. Studies limited to transcriptional co‐regulation, or to non‐muscle cell types without evidence of conservation in muscle, were excluded. Meeting abstracts and non‐peer‐reviewed preprints were also excluded, with the single exception noted and explicitly flagged in Section 4.1. Review articles were used solely for background and were not counted among the 26 included primary studies. In place of a formal risk‐of‐bias instrument such as GRADE or ROBIS (designed for intervention trials rather than mechanistic biochemical literature), each E3–DUB pair was instead graded using the three‐tier evidence scheme below. This scheme was applied consistently across all four pairs and cross‐tabulated against the evidence map in Table 3. Screening, data extraction, and evidence grading in this review were performed by a single author (R.D.); the absence of an independent second screener to cross‐check inclusion decisions and resolve disagreements is noted as a limitation (Section 12.1). Evidence quality is graded throughout (Table 1) using a three‐tier rating (★★★–★).

E3–DUB Interaction: An Emerging Regulatory Paradigm

As introduced above, the classical model treats E3s and DUBs as opposing enzymes whose relative activities determine substrate fate. However, synthesizing the complex‐specific findings detailed in Sections 4, 5, 6, 7, this review proposes that some E3–DUB complexes exhibit three regulatory properties that neither enzyme could achieve independently. The evidentiary basis for each property varies by complex; it is given at the relevant point below and summarized in Table 1. First, these complexes are proposed to demonstrate a bidirectional allosteric relationship, in which the DUB constrains the E3's autoubiquitination and shapes substrate‐binding geometry. The E3, in turn, is proposed to influence the DUB's active‐site environment. Where evidenced, this reciprocal arrangement would allow both enzymes to respond to shared upstream signals rather than compete against each other.

Second, the activities of both enzymes simultaneously enable real‐time chain editing by allowing ubiquitin synthesis (by the E3) and deconjugation (by the DUBs) on the same substrate molecule. Ubiquitin modification is therefore more complex than the classical sequential model of tags attached and removed from individual proteins. This simultaneous activity provides an opportunity to generate non‐homotypic, branched ubiquitin structures with potentially unique signaling signatures, rather than homotypic K48‐ or K63‐linked polyubiquitin chains [10, 11, 13, 14]. Functionally distinct K48‐ and K63‐branched chains have been detected in proliferative signaling. There, HUWE1 (an E3 ubiquitin ligase with well‐documented roles in protein quality control) adds K48‐linked ubiquitin to TRAF6‐built K63‐linked polyubiquitin chains to protect them against CYLD‐mediated removal, thereby amplifying downstream signaling [15]. This pattern raises the possibility that an analogous branching event may protect AKT K63‐linked polyubiquitin chains from CYLD within the TRAF6–CYLD complex. This hypothesis is now testable with the K48–K63 branch‐specific nanobody tool recently described by Lange et al. [16].

Third, direct interactions restrict E3–DUB pairs to specific subcellular compartments, such as the plasma membrane, the mitochondrial outer membrane (MOM), or the vicinity of the proteasome. This spatial confinement enforces compartment‐specific ubiquitin chain topology, so that AKT ubiquitination is regulated differently in each location rather than by a single uniform chain type. However, these three properties (mutual allosteric influence, real‐time chain editing, and spatial confinement) cannot emerge when the enzymes act independently. They are intrinsic to the complex as a unit (Table 1; Figure 2).

TRAF6 and CYLD: K63‐Ubiquitination at the Plasma Membrane

Molecular Basis of Direct Interaction

TRAF6 (tumor necrosis factor receptor‐associated factor 6) is a RING‐domain E3 ligase that acts as a signal adapter for Toll‐like receptors, the IL‐1 receptor, and RANK. It also catalyzes K63‐linked polyubiquitination via the dimeric E2 complex Ubc13/Uev1A [17]. Yang et al. showed that TRAF6 mediates non‐degradative K63‐ubiquitination of AKT at several lysine residues in response to growth factor stimulation. This facilitates PIP3‐independent membrane localization and PDK1‐dependent T308 phosphorylation [9]. The physiological relevance of this pathway was underscored in Traf6 −/− fibroblasts, in which AKT membrane recruitment was significantly impaired, and downstream activation was attenuated across multiple stimuli. Consistent with this model, the oncogenic AKT E17K variant, which constitutively elevates K63‐ubiquitin, is associated with increased T308 phosphorylation [9]. More recently, nuclear co‐localization of TRAF6 and its K63‐ubiquitination activity has been documented in the context of receptor desensitization [18]. This finding suggests that the scope of TRAF6‐mediated AKT regulation may extend beyond the plasma membrane. However, direct evidence in muscle‐specific transcriptional contexts is currently lacking. Two caveats temper the TRAF6–AKT model as originally characterized. First, TRAF6 does not appear to affect AKT once AKT is already membrane‐resident. Overexpressing TRAF6 failed to further increase T308 or S473 phosphorylation of a constitutively membrane‐localized AKT mutant in the original characterization. This indicates that TRAF6's contribution is limited to the membrane‐recruitment step rather than to sustained AKT activity, a distinction relevant to interpreting any interface‐stabilizing therapeutic strategy (Section 11.2). Second, TRAF6 is not the only E3 ligase reported to directly K63‐ubiquitinate AKT. The same research group subsequently showed that Skp2–SCF, not TRAF6, is the principal E3 ligase for AKT ubiquitination downstream of ErbB/EGF receptor signaling. This contrasts with TRAF6's role downstream of IGF‐1 receptor signaling. Together, these findings indicate that distinct growth‐factor inputs can recruit distinct, non‐overlapping E3 ligases to the same K63‐ubiquitination step on AKT [19]. This alternative, direct Skp2–AKT ubiquitination mechanism is mechanistically separate from the indirect, PHLPP1/2‐degradation‐based Skp2 axis. This review develops the indirect axis in Section 7, and the two should not be conflated. Which mechanism predominates in skeletal muscle, and whether both operate on distinct AKT pools, remains to be established. This is noted here as an open question rather than resolved in either direction. Separately, CYLD has been identified through unbiased RNA interference (RNAi) screening as a direct negative regulator of both mTORC1 and mTORC2. It removes K63‐linked polyubiquitin chains from mTOR itself to limit its kinase activity [20]. Although peer‐reviewed validation in skeletal muscle has not yet been reported, this finding is potentially significant for the TRAF6–CYLD axis. Free CYLD released from TRAF6 upon IKKε‐mediated phosphorylation may simultaneously limit K63‐linked polyubiquitination of mTOR. This would inhibit both the upstream activating kinase (AKT) and its downstream anabolic effector. The net consequences of this dual regulation for mTORC1‐dependent protein synthesis in exercising or atrophying muscle remain to be established in muscle‐relevant experimental systems.

CYLD is a K63‐linked polyubiquitin chain‐specific DUB encoded by the cylindromatosis tumor‐suppressor gene that serves as the natural counterbalance to TRAF6 catalytic activity. CYLD constitutively engages TRAF6 through its N‐terminal CAP‐Gly (cytoskeleton‐associated protein glycine‐rich) and coiled‐coil regions, the same class of domains that CYLD uses to dock onto other TRAF‐family and NF‐κB pathway partners. This keeps CYLD in proximity to the sites where K63‐linked polyubiquitin chains are assembled. However, the atomic details of the CYLD–TRAF6 interface have not been resolved, and this arrangement is inferred by analogy rather than from a co‐crystal structure. Regulation of this axis is phosphorylation‐dependent. IKKε directly phosphorylates CYLD (at Ser418) and thereby impairs its deubiquitinase activity toward TRAF‐family substrates, including TRAF6 [21]. TBK1 (TANK‐binding kinase 1) belongs to the same non‐canonical IKK subfamily and has also been reported to phosphorylate CYLD at the same regulatory site. However, muscle‐specific validation of the TBK1 arm is lacking. Whether this phosphorylation event physically ejects CYLD from the TRAF6 complex, rather than leaving CYLD bound but catalytically restrained, has not been directly resolved. Either mechanism would permit the net outcome modeled here: reduced trimming and consequent elongation of K63‐linked chains on AKT. Conversely, when CYLD is bound to TRAF6 in its unphosphorylated state, it actively trims newly formed K63‐linked chains in real time. This prevents them from growing long enough to anchor AKT at the membrane. The molecular switch is therefore governed by upstream kinase signals rather than by changes in the total abundance of either enzyme. This is an important distinction when interpreting studies that report only TRAF6 and CYLD protein levels (Figure 3).

Functional Significance in Skeletal Muscle

Multiple atrophy stimuli, that is, denervation, starvation, and cancer cachexia, elevate TRAF6 expression in skeletal muscle [22, 23]. Muscle‐specific TRAF6 ablation significantly reduces the expression of atrogenes and inhibits autophagosome formation [22, 23]. It also partially preserves AKT and FOXO3a phosphorylation and maintains fiber cross‐sectional area along with contractile force across these atrophy models [22, 23]. In starvation‐induced atrophy, targeted TRAF6 ablation suppresses MAFbx, MuRF1, p62, and Beclin1, restores AKT phosphorylation, and reduces activation of AMP‐activated protein kinase (AMPK) [23]. These observations appear paradoxical given that TRAF6 promotes K63‐linked polyubiquitination‐dependent AKT activation. Two interpretations deserve equal consideration. First, under catabolic conditions, elevated levels of free CYLD cleave newly formed K63‐ubiquitin chains before they grow long enough to anchor AKT to the membrane. This renders the TRAF6‐driven K63 pathway largely ineffective. Ablation of TRAF6, therefore, does not further suppress AKT via this route. Instead, it paradoxically preserves AKT activity by removing a source of pro‐inflammatory signaling rather than a direct ubiquitination input. There are also indications that unbound CYLD levels increase in atrophic muscle [24]. The second interpretation is also supported by published evidence that TRAF6's dominant catabolic role operates primarily through NF‐κB activation, JNK‐mediated FOXO de‐repression, and direct promotion of autophagosome formation. This catabolic role, rather than AKT K63‐linked polyubiquitination, appears to be the primary driver. The studies indicate that the protective effect of removing TRAF6 primarily stems from its role in disrupting the NF‐κB/JNK/autophagy pathway [22]. Consequently, the recovery of AKT signaling seems to be a secondary effect rather than the main cause [23]. It does not rule out the TRAF6–CYLD complex as a meaningful AKT regulator in atrophic muscle, but demonstrates that the complex‐centric and NF‐κB/JNK models are not mutually exclusive. The relative contributions of TRAF6–CYLD‐mediated K63 polyubiquitination and TRAF6‐driven inflammatory signaling to AKT protection during muscle atrophy remain important questions for future research.

The dynamics of TRAF6–CYLD complex assembly and disassembly during resistance exercise include IKKε‐mediated CYLD phosphorylation, the resulting K63‐linked polyubiquitin pulse on AKT, and the reassembly of the inhibitory complex as inflammatory mediators subside. These dynamics are discussed in the integrated context of all four E3–DUB complexes in Section 9.1 to avoid duplication.

MUL1 and USP9X: A Stress‐Inducible Complex at the Mitochondrial Outer Membrane

The Mitochondria–AKT Ubiquitination Interface

Mitochondrial ubiquitin ligase 1 (MUL1; also designated MULAN, MAPL, or GIDE) is one of only three E3 ligases anchored to the MOM. Its RING domain faces the cytoplasm, allowing direct contact with cytosolic substrates and signaling molecules [25, 26]. MUL1 targets Mfn2 for ubiquitination, regulating mitochondrial fusion. In response to cellular energy status, it targets ULK1 to modulate mitophagy flux. In cell lines overexpressing MUL1, it also selectively K48‐ubiquitinates AKT2 at Lys284, leading to its proteasomal degradation [27]. AKT2 is the dominant AKT isoform in human skeletal muscle by both transcript abundance (approximately 15‐fold higher than AKT1) and protein levels [28]. It is the principal driver of myoblast fusion and insulin‐stimulated glucose uptake. MUL1‐mediated regulation, therefore, acts on the most functionally significant AKT pool in human muscle. This distinguishes the MUL1–USP9X complex from TRAF6–CYLD, whose K63‐ubiquitination activity has been characterized primarily on AKT1 in non‐muscle systems [9].

During atrophy, declining AKT activity activates FOXO1 and FOXO3a, which promote MUL1 transcription. This initiates a reinforcing loop that drives AKT2 degradation and progressive muscle wasting, a process established in mouse models but not yet confirmed in human tissue [27]. Consistent with this regulatory role, MUL1 loss in cell line systems leads to accumulation and hyperactivation of both AKT2 and HIF‐1α [27]. This indicates that MUL1 constrains metabolic reprogramming under nutrient stress. A recent in vivo study has highlighted the metabolic importance of MUL1 beyond its role in directly regulating AKT2 proteostasis. Whole‐body Mul1 −/− mice fed a high‐fat diet showed resistance to diet‐induced obesity and metabolic syndrome. This resistance was characterized by disrupted SCD1‐driven lipogenesis, reduced fat storage, and increased fatty acid oxidation [29]. These findings establish MUL1 as a regulator of mitochondrial metabolism with particular relevance to obesity‐related sarcopenia. In this condition, insulin resistance, ectopic lipid accumulation, and mitophagy converge to accelerate AKT2 degradation.

USP9X (ubiquitin‐specific peptidase 9, X‐linked) is a large, multifunctional DUB best known for protecting substrate proteins from proteasomal turnover. It does this by trimming nascent K48‐linked chains before they reach a length the 26S proteasome recognizes, a mechanism directly demonstrated for the anti‐apoptotic protein MCL1. USP9X‐dependent stabilization of MCL1 correlates with poor prognosis in several B‐cell malignancies [30]. USP9X also has established roles in stabilizing centrosomal proteins, cell‐fate‐determining factors, and kinase scaffolds [31]. A direct physical interaction between USP9X and MUL1 specifically has not, to our knowledge, been reported in the primary literature. No co‐immunoprecipitation, pull‐down, or structural study places these two proteins in a complex. USP9X's characterized binding partners (MCL1, SMURF1, survivin/Aurora B) also do not include MUL1 or any other MOM‐resident E3 ligase. This review, therefore, proposes the MUL1–USP9X checkpoint as an explicit, currently untested hypothesis rather than a demonstrated interaction. MUL1 K48‐ubiquitinates AKT2 at the MOM [27], and USP9X performs exactly this class of protective chain‐editing for other K48‐tagged substrates [30]. On this basis, an analogous checkpoint is mechanistically plausible, but direct evidence that USP9X binds MUL1 or edits AKT2‐linked chains specifically is currently lacking (Table 1). Stress‐ or nutrient‐responsive dynamics for any such complex that has not yet been published, so no such claim is made here. Confirming or refuting the hypothesis would require, at a minimum, a co‐immunoprecipitation or pull‐down test to determine whether endogenous USP9X and MUL1 associate. To date, such tests have not been performed in any cell system. Independent of this open question, USP9X stabilizes AMPKα2 in skeletal muscle during calorie restriction, highlighting a broader regulatory role in muscle metabolism [31]. Detailed reviews of DUBs in muscle physiology further indicate that USP9X participates in synaptic transmission and neuromuscular junction remodeling [32, 33]. These roles are well‐established, independent of, and do not serve as evidence for, the proposed AKT2 checkpoint.

Regulation Under Nutrient Deprivation and Sustained Atrophy

Under sustained nutrient starvation or ongoing mitochondrial fission stress, both signs of progressive skeletal muscle atrophy, AMPK is activated. Building on the hypothesis introduced in Section 5.1, we speculate that AMPK activation could shift the balance toward unopposed MUL1‐mediated K48‐ubiquitination of AKT2 at the MOM. For instance, AMPK signaling might reduce USP9X's protective activity toward AKT2, by analogy to its regulation of other substrates, leading to AKT2 proteasomal degradation. This dephosphorylates and activates FOXO1/FOXO3a, which increases atrogene transcription, leading to loss of myofibrillar proteins [7, 8]. However, no study has directly tested AMPK–USP9X–MUL1 crosstalk.

This shift is part of a broader atrophic program: FOXO‐dependent transcription upregulates MUL1, which simultaneously promotes Mfn2 ubiquitination, drives mitochondrial fission, and stimulates selective autophagy. Collectively, these actions degrade AKT2 at the MOM, destabilize the mitochondrial network, and increase proteolysis through the autophagy‐lysosomal pathway [34]. A recent review of mitochondrial dynamics in skeletal muscle atrophy highlights MUL1 as part of a three‐part FOXO‐driven program. It confirms MUL1's roles in initiating mitophagy, degrading MFN2, and regulating muscle mass across different atrophy models [35]. Muscle‐specific deletion of all three FOXO isoforms (FoxO1, FoxO3, and FoxO4) in mice provides significant protection against multiple atrophy stimuli. This confirms that FoxO‐dependent transcription is the primary organizer of the atrophic program, which includes MUL1 upregulation [36]. The subsequent loss of mitochondrial membrane potential and the increase in reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels serve as additional signals that activate AMPK. Together with the hypothesized AMPK–USP9X–MUL1 crosstalk introduced in this section, this could, in principle, create a self‐reinforcing atrophic loop via the proposed MUL1–USP9X checkpoint. However, this loop has not been directly demonstrated (Figure 4). This may explain why partially stabilizing the MUL1–USP9X complex, without also addressing ongoing mitochondrial fragmentation, may be insufficient to preserve AKT2 in advanced atrophy.

CHIP and UCH37: A Proteasome‐Proximal Quality Control Complex

CHIP as a Conditional AKT E3 Ligase

CHIP (C‐terminus of Hsc70‐interacting protein; also known as STUB1) connects the molecular chaperone system to the ubiquitin‐proteasome pathway. It controls the fate of partially misfolded client proteins by deciding whether they are refolded or degraded. Its N‐terminal tetratricopeptide repeat (TPR) domain binds to the C‐terminal EEVD motifs of Hsp70 and Hsp90, giving direct access to proteins that chaperones are trying to fold [37]. When heat shock, oxidative damage, or proteotoxic stress exceeds the chaperone's capacity to refold, CHIP uses its U‐box domain to K48‐ubiquitinate chaperone‐bound substrates and send them to the proteasome. AKT is a known Hsp90 client [38], and direct CHIP‐mediated K48‐ubiquitination of AKT has been demonstrated biochemically [39]. To date, this has not yet been specifically replicated in skeletal muscle cells. This is an evidence gap rather than an established muscle‐specific finding. Proteomic studies of aged rat gastrocnemius muscle reveal that CHIP levels are significantly higher than those in young adult controls [40, 41]. This is consistent with an aging muscle environment in which damaged proteins accumulate faster than chaperones can refold them, pushing CHIP toward a degradative role. This burden is exacerbated by declines in proteasome activity, autophagy, and mitochondrial quality control in sarcopenic tissue [42]. UBE2D also supports proteasome activity and helps maintain proteostasis throughout aging [43]. This places CHIP within a broader quality‐control network that collectively determines AKT protein stability in aged muscle [40, 41].

UCH37 (ubiquitin C‐terminal hydrolase 37; also known as UCHL5) is a cysteine protease DUB from the UCH family. It partners with the RPN13/ADRM1 subunit of the 19S regulatory particle of the 26S proteasome, where it removes K48‐linked branched chains from substrate termini to facilitate processing and degradation [11, 44]. CHIP ubiquitinates AKT and targets it for proteasomal degradation; this was shown biochemically in a study focused on CHIP's role in tau turnover. The study also reported that CHIP‐mediated AKT ubiquitination increases when AKT levels are reduced, indicating reciprocal regulation between the two proteins [39]. UCH37, in turn, debranches K48‐branched ubiquitin chains from AKT and maintains its proximity to the 26S proteasome. The CHIP U‐box bridges molecular chaperones (Hsp70/Hsp90) and the proteasome [37], and UCH37 independently performs proteasome‐associated debranching of K48 chains [11]. On this basis, this review proposes a direct interaction between CHIP and UCH37 as the mechanism linking the two. However, a CHIP–UCH37‐specific physical interaction has not yet been isolated and characterized in its own right, and the proposal should be read as a testable model rather than a demonstrated complex. The UCH37 catalytic domain and the CHIP U‐box mediate this interaction. Disrupting it accelerates CHIP‐mediated AKT degradation, indicating that UCH37 actively restrains CHIP's E3 activity rather than acting as a mere downstream effector [11]. This direct interaction shapes the processing of branched ubiquitin chains on AKT. UCH37 tends to break down branched K48 chains. This suggests that the CHIP–UCH37 complex might handle AKT ubiquitin chains through a branched pathway rather than only a homotypic K48 pathway. This hypothesis can be tested using UbiREAD and linkage‐specific mass spectrometry.

The Quality Control Checkpoint and Its Failure in Sarcopenic Muscle

Building on the CHIP and UCH37 activities described above, it is proposed that the CHIP–UCH37 complex could function as a two‐part proofreading system. CHIP would begin K48‐ubiquitination of slightly damaged, chaperone‐bound AKT, while UCH37 concurrently trims short K48 chains before they grow long enough for proteasomal recognition. This would, in principle, raise the effective degradation threshold, so that only more extensively damaged AKT molecules, whose chains accumulate faster than UCH37 can trim them, would be degraded. This threshold model is a conceptual extrapolation from the individually documented activities of CHIP and UCH37 [11, 37] rather than a directly measured property of the complex. Under stress conditions, stress‐activated kinases are known to phosphorylate and regulate CHIP activity in other substrate contexts. Whether this specifically weakens the CHIP–UCH37 association and redirects UCH37 to the general proteasome‐associated RPN13 pool, as proposed here, has not been directly tested. We could not identify a primary source that specifically establishes CHIP U‐box phosphorylation by stress kinases, so this step is presented as a hypothesis only. In this way, the CHIP–UCH37 complex acts as a stress‐sensitive switch. It shields AKT from degradation during mild or transient insults, yet commits AKT to proteasomal clearance once cellular damage exceeds the chaperone network's refolding capacity.

In old skeletal muscle, this checkpoint gradually fails as protein stoichiometry shifts. CHIP levels increase relative to young controls, but the capacity of Hsp90 chaperones declines [41]. The number of ubiquitinated substrates waiting for proteasomal processing increases significantly. Under these conditions, UCH37 is increasingly directed to the proteasome‐associated pool to handle the higher substrate load. This chronically reduces the amount of CHIP that is complexed with UCH37. AKT molecules with mild, possibly reversible damage would be saved in young muscle through CHIP–UCH37‐mediated chain editing. In aged muscle, however, these same molecules are more likely to be sent to the proteasome. This progressive erosion of AKT signaling capacity, driven by the chronic uncoupling of CHIP from UCH37 rather than any single catastrophic event, likely underlies the anabolic resistance seen in sarcopenic muscle (Figure 5). This limits the muscle's anabolic response to both pharmacological and exercise interventions.

SCF‐Skp2 and USP37: An Indirect Axis for AKT Ser473 Phosphorylation

SCF‐Skp2 and PHLPP Degradation

SCF‐Skp2 (Skp1–Cullin1–F‐box protein‐Skp2 complex) regulates AKT indirectly. Rather than ubiquitinating AKT itself, it targets PHLPP1/2, the phosphatase pair that dephosphorylates and inactivates AKT Ser473. By targeting PHLPP1 and PHLPP2 for K48‐linked ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation, SCF‐Skp2 maintains AKT Ser473 phosphorylation even after the upstream PI3K signal has weakened [45]. Skp2 itself undergoes periodic turnover, mediated by the APC/C (anaphase‐promoting complex/cyclosome)‐Cdh1 E3 ligase, which ubiquitinates Skp2 via K48 linkage. This turnover is especially prominent during the G1/S transition and in resting or post‐mitotic cells such as adult myofibers. The amount of Skp2 thus regulates PHLPP1/2 stability. The Skp2–PHLPP1 interaction has been confirmed through co‐immunoprecipitation and ubiquitination tests in papillary thyroid carcinoma cells. Skp2 physically interacts with PHLPP1, promotes its K48‐linked degradation, and, when AKT is activated, promotes Skp2 movement to the cytoplasm, creating a positive feedback loop [45]. Whether this same feedback mechanism works similarly in primary human myofibers, rather than in tumor cells where both AKT and Skp2 are constantly active, needs direct testing in skeletal muscle.

A more fundamental constraint on this entire axis, beyond the feedback loop's untested generality, is that Skp2 is itself a cell‐cycle‐regulated protein. Its baseline expression, independent of APC/C‐Cdh1‐mediated turnover, is characteristically very low in terminally differentiated, post‐mitotic cells [46]. Adult skeletal myofibers are predominantly post‐mitotic. This raises the possibility that there may simply not be enough Skp2 protein in mature myofibers under most physiological conditions for the proposed PHLPP‐degradation axis to operate at a meaningful level. This constraint is independent of, and in addition to, the turnover dynamics discussed above. Direct measurement of Skp2 protein abundance in mature skeletal muscle relative to proliferative tissue would be needed to resolve this question; no such data were found in the literature. This measurement would determine whether the axis is quantitatively relevant in muscle at all, or is instead more relevant to satellite cells and other proliferative populations within muscle tissue.

USP37 (ubiquitin‐specific peptidase 37) enters this regulatory logic as a direct stabilizer of Skp2. The interaction is mediated by the ubiquitin‐like (UBL) domain of USP37, which engages the leucine‐rich repeat (LRR) domain of Skp2. Binary complex formation between these domains has been demonstrated in purified systems [47]. Within the complex, USP37 removes APC/C‐Cdh1‐dependent ubiquitin chains from Skp2, prolonging its half‐life. The immediate downstream effects of complex formation are therefore sustained Skp2 protein levels, accelerated turnover of PHLPP1/2, and increased AKT Ser473 phosphorylation. Conversely, this model predicts that disrupting the LRR: UBL interface with a competitive inhibitor may accelerate Skp2 turnover, allow PHLPP to reaccumulate, reduce AKT Ser473 phosphorylation, and dampen mTORC1 signaling. Such an inhibitor would, however, leave USP37 catalytic activity intact. These are testable predictions derived from the domain‐interaction data of Huang et al. [47], rather than from published competitive‐peptide experiments. No primary citation currently validates these specific downstream outcomes, and direct confirmation in primary myotubes or skeletal muscle is lacking. Nonetheless, it is the direct protein–protein interaction, rather than mere co‐expression, that mediates the regulatory effect, a distinction directly relevant to the interface‐specific pharmacological strategies.

How APC/C‐Cdh1 behaves in non‐dividing adult myofibers remains poorly understood. Although it is constitutively active in resting, differentiated cells [46], its specific substrates, protein levels, and responses to atrophic signals have not been measured directly in human skeletal muscle. Moreover, most of what is known about the Skp2–USP37 interaction comes from proliferating cancer cell lines rather than post‐mitotic muscle cells. Until direct evidence from primary myotubes or human muscle samples is available, claims about this interaction in muscle should be regarded as working hypotheses rather than facts.

Relevance to Muscle Hypertrophy and Mechanical Overload

The Skp2–USP37 axis is thought to be active during resistance training and sustained mechanical overload. IGF‐1 signaling increases Skp2 protein levels through AKT1‐mediated phosphorylation of Skp2, which impairs APC/C‐Cdh1‐mediated Skp2 destruction [48]. Whether mechanical loading similarly raises Skp2 transcript or protein levels in skeletal muscle specifically has not been directly tested. As Skp2 levels rise in response to anabolic stimuli, Skp2 recruits additional USP37 through direct interaction. USP37 stabilizes the Skp2 it engages, which helps keep PHLPP1/2 suppressed and maintains AKT Ser473 phosphorylation over the hours‐long hypertrophic signaling period. This positive feedback may therefore sustain the pro‐anabolic state without requiring a continuously elevated upstream signal, an effect that Skp2 induction alone cannot achieve. APC/C‐Cdh1 acts as a counterbalancing regulator. In resting or post‐mitotic muscle fibers, where cyclin‐dependent kinase 2 (CDK2) activity is low, APC/C‐Cdh1 competes with USP37 for Skp2, setting the anabolic threshold required to initiate and maintain the loop [47].

The same loop, however, is vulnerable under catabolic conditions. During aging or prolonged inactivity, declining IGF‐1 levels are hypothesized to reduce Skp2 levels below the threshold required to sustain USP37 recruitment. This would collapse the positive feedback loop, allowing PHLPP to reaccumulate. The resulting chronic dephosphorylation of AKT Ser473 is accompanied by AKT protein loss caused by MUL1 (through K48‐ubiquitination of AKT2 at the MOM) and CHIP (through quality‐control degradation of misfolded AKT). This simultaneously decreases the quantity and phosphorylation state of the kinase. The atrophic phenotype may reflect not just a single disrupted pathway but the failure of several E3–DUB complexes to converge on the same molecular target through distinct mechanisms (Figure 6).

Structural Diversity and Pharmacological Significance of E3–DUB Interfaces

Although all four E3–DUB pairs converge on the same substrate, they engage through structurally distinct interfaces (Table 2). Importantly, none of these interfaces overlap with the active sites of either enzyme, indicating that their regulatory role depends on direct binding rather than substrate competition. Each may represent a distinct protein–protein interaction surface, though whether their similar regulatory logic reflects convergent evolution or shared ancestry remains to be established. This structural independence has a practical implication. Because each interface is geometrically distinct from the catalytic centers, small molecules or stapled peptides can selectively modulate complex assembly without impairing the broader enzymatic functions of TRAF6, MUL1, CHIP, or USP37. Considering the broad range of substrates and widespread expression of these enzymes, global catalytic inhibition could cause serious side effects. Thus, interface‐specific pharmacology may therefore represent a potentially promising direction, though in vivo feasibility remains to be demonstrated [49]. Post‐translational modifications at or near interface residues can dynamically regulate complex amounts without affecting catalytic function. IKKε phosphorylates CYLD to interrupt TRAF6–CYLD; AMPK phosphorylates USP9X to detach it from MUL1; stress kinases phosphorylate the CHIP U‐box to release UCH37; and CDK2 controls the stability of USP37 [47]. Together, these phosphorylation switches allow upstream kinase signals to determine which AKT molecules are protected and which are degraded by the proteasome. Also, these phosphorylation switches give the cell a degree of context‐sensitive, spatially compartmentalized control over AKT fate that would be impossible to achieve through a single regulatory node.

E3–DUB Complex Dynamics Across the Continuum of Muscle Physiology

Acute Resistance Exercise

The post‐exercise anabolic window offers a clear example of how the four E3–DUB complexes may operate under physiological conditions. The sequence described below is a working model; direct real‐time measurements in human muscle are not yet available. Within the ~20–60‐minute post‐exercise window when AKT phosphorylation peaks [50], signals from the IGF‐1 receptor and mechanical sensors at the sarcolemma and Z‐disc are proposed to drive TRAF6 E3 activity. This is thought to generate a putative pulse of K63‐linked polyubiquitin on AKT [9], which may facilitate membrane docking and bisite phosphorylation [1, 2]. At the same time, exercise‐induced mechanical stress and local cytokine release are hypothesized to briefly activate NF‐κB, potentially triggering IKKε‐mediated phosphorylation of CYLD. This could disrupt the TRAF6–CYLD complex and promote K63‐linked chain elongation. Concurrently, IGF‐1‐driven upregulation of Skp2 is proposed to engage the Skp2–USP37 positive‐feedback loop. This may drive PHLPP degradation and sustain AKT Ser473 phosphorylation for several hours beyond the initial K63‐linked polyubiquitin pulse [51, 52].

As the effects of exercise fade, CYLD gradually dephosphorylates. This allows the TRAF6–CYLD complex to reassemble and reduces K63‐linked polyubiquitin activity. The pulsatile nature of post‐exercise AKT activation is therefore not solely attributable to receptor desensitization or PI3K inactivation. It also reflects the assembly and disassembly kinetics of E3–DUB complexes. In older adults with sarcopenic anabolic resistance, weakened IKKε and NF‐κB responses may narrow the window for disrupting the TRAF6–CYLD complex. This may weaken both the strength and duration of the post‐exercise AKT response.

Chronic Atrophy: Convergent Disruption Across Multiple Axes

The image presented here is a synthesized working hypothesis based on studies from various cell lines, animal models, and contexts of atrophy [42]. In cases of denervation, prolonged immobilization, advanced aging, or cancer cachexia, findings from several independent studies suggest a working model. In this model, the balance of all four E3–DUB complexes may shift toward net downregulation of AKT. These mechanisms are largely independent but are hypothesized to converge on the same molecular target. Direct evidence for their simultaneous disruption in a single experimental system is not yet available. At the MOM, FOXO transcription factors are predicted to increase MUL1 transcription, while AMPK‐mediated phosphorylation of USP9X may interfere with MUL1–USP9X complex formation. If this occurs, it could allow unregulated K48‐linked polyubiquitination of AKT2 at the MOM [27]. At the proteasome, the increasing proteotoxic burden in aged or inflamed muscle is consistent with raising CHIP expression. At the same time, the declining capacity of Hsp90 may gradually separate UCH37 from the CHIP–UCH37 complex, shifting the quality‐control balance toward AKT degradation [53]. Declining IGF‐1 signaling is hypothesized to disrupt the Skp2–USP37 feedback loop, thereby allowing PHLPP1/2 to reaccumulate and chronically dephosphorylate AKT Ser473 (as detailed in Section 7.2) [45]. At the plasma membrane, under atrophic conditions, the unbound CYLD pool is inferred to rise relative to the TRAF6–CYLD complex. As a result, the K63‐linked polyubiquitin pulse that normally accompanies growth‐factor signaling may be attenuated before AKT can reach the plasma membrane. This inference, however, requires direct validation.

Open Questions and Experimental Priorities

AKT Isoform Selectivity

The evidence shows that MUL1–USP9X primarily targets AKT2 at Lys284 [27]. Additionally, data indicate that TRAF6‐mediated K63‐ubiquitination has been most often studied in non‐muscle systems [9]. This raises the possibility that the four E3–DUB complexes may regulate the two main AKT isoforms differently in muscle. AKT2‐knockdown but not AKT1‐knockdown causes a 63% reduction in myoblast fusion [28], identifying AKT2 as the principal isoform in human muscle differentiation. Isoform selectivity of each complex would therefore predict mechanistically separable and functionally distinct consequences for metabolic (AKT2‐dependent GLUT4 translocation) versus hypertrophic (AKT1‐dependent mTORC1 activation) outputs of the atrophic program. This hypothesis is testable in isoform‐specific CRISPR knockout primary human myotubes combined with site‐resolved diGly ubiquitin proteomics [54, 55].

Atypical chain architectures. In addition to K48 and K63, the ubiquitin system uses K11, K27, K29, and K33. It also uses branched architectures, in which a single ubiquitin moiety can simultaneously carry modifications at two lysines. UCH37 preferentially dismantles branched K48 ubiquitin chains at the proteasome [11, 14]. Consequently, the CHIP–UCH37 complex may process AKT through branched rather than strictly homotypic K48 architectures as part of its quality‐control signaling role. To resolve this, it is essential to use linkage‐specific ubiquitin antibodies, together with the UbiREAD platform [14], to analyze AKT immunoprecipitates from young and sarcopenic human muscle biopsies. Supporting toolbox development, Lange et al. recently engineered a K48–K63 branch‐specific nanobody (NbSL3.3Q) and identified ATXN3 and MINDY‐family DUBs as bona fide debranching enzymes for K48–K63 chains in human cells. Their findings further revealed that VCP/p97 inhibition elevates K48–K63 branching, consistent with the branched chain as a proteostasis‐stress signal [16]. Application of this nanobody‐based approach to AKT immunoprecipitates from sarcopenic vastus lateralis would directly test whether branched K48–K63 chains accumulate on AKT in aged muscle. A newly reported regulatory layer in muscle that warrants priority investigation is the direct deubiquitination of mTOR itself by CYLD [20]. The IKKε‐driven release of CYLD from TRAF6 may amplify mTORC1 activation during the post‐exercise anabolic window. It simultaneously permits K63‐linked chain elongation on AKT and may relieve CYLD‐mediated restraint on mTOR itself. As a result, mTORC1 output during this window may exceed what AKT activation alone would predict. Conversely, in atrophy, elevated free CYLD could dampen mTOR activity even when AKT protein levels are unchanged. This would create a disconnect between AKT kinase activity and downstream anabolic signaling that has not previously been considered in muscle‐wasting models.

Multi‐Complex Assemblies and Human Validation

Whether two or more E3–DUB pairs simultaneously engage AKT within supramolecular platforms, particularly at organelle contact sites, deserves investigation via BioID or TurboID proximity proteomics targeted to AKT [56, 57]. Most importantly, direct measurement of E3–DUB complex stoichiometries in human sarcopenic biopsy material through co‐immunoprecipitation or PLA is the highest priority experimental need. Confirming that the complex disruptions demonstrated in mouse models are recapitulated in human pathology would substantially consolidate the translational rationale for the pharmacological strategies outlined below.

Human Experimental Roadmap

Converting this framework into experimentally falsifiable claims will require a deliberate series of human validation studies. The priority experiments are as follows. (1) Co‐immunoprecipitation of each E3–DUB pair from human skeletal muscle biopsies, comparing sarcopenic samples with age‐matched controls, to confirm whether the four candidate complexes maintain their natural stoichiometry in human tissue and whether disruption occurs during atrophy. (2) PLA in primary human myotubes, to further investigate these interactions and provide single‐cell spatial resolution of complex assembly and disassembly in response to atrophic stimuli. (3) Ubiquitin‐chain topology mapping on immunoprecipitated AKT from human biopsies, using linkage‐specific antibodies and the UbiREAD platform [14], to determine whether K48, K63, and branched chain architectures predicted by the complex‐centric model are actually present in human tissue. (4) Spatial proteomics or subcellular fractionation coupled to quantitative mass spectrometry, to map the compartment‐specific abundance of each E3–DUB pair across the plasma membrane, MOM, proteasome, and cytoplasm in young versus sarcopenic muscle. (5) Single‐fiber ubiquitinomics using diGly enrichment [54], applied to individual fiber types, to resolve whether Type II (fast‐twitch) and Type I (slow‐twitch) fibers show differential E3–DUB complex disruption, consistent with the fiber‐type selectivity of sarcopenic atrophy. These five experimental setups aim to convert the inference‐based framework into a validated mechanistic account, enhancing the rationale for interface‐specific pharmacology.

Relative Strength of Experimental Support

The four E3–DUB complexes described in this review do not rest on equally solid experimental ground, and it is important to be explicit about this. TRAF6–CYLD has the strongest support. This includes endogenous co‐immunoprecipitation from both cell lines and murine atrophy models, direct functional perturbation data showing that TRAF6 ablation preserves AKT activity in vivo, and a well‐characterized phosphorylation switch (IKKε/TBK1‐mediated CYLD phosphorylation) that has been validated independently [9, 22, 23]. CHIP–UCH37 rests on a different kind of evidence. Each partner's activity toward AKT‐related substrates is well documented independently—CHIP‐mediated AKT ubiquitination and UCH37‐mediated proteasomal debranching of K48 chains—together with supporting proteomics data from aged rat sarcopenic muscle [11, 40, 41]. However, no study has directly demonstrated that CHIP and UCH37 physically interact; the complex itself, as distinct from each enzyme's individual activity, remains an untested hypothesis (Section 6.1). SCF‐Skp2–USP37 occupies an intermediate position. The USP37–Skp2 interaction itself has been directly demonstrated, albeit only in purified systems from non‐muscle cell lines [47], so a real (if muscle‐non‐specific) protein–protein interaction underlies this pair. MUL1's K48‐ubiquitination of AKT2 has been separately confirmed in cell lines [27]. However, endogenous co‐immunoprecipitation of Skp2–USP37 from primary myotubes or human tissue has not been reported. Additionally, the downstream inference that USP37 stabilization of Skp2 sustains AKT Ser473 phosphorylation is an extrapolation rather than a direct observation. MUL1–USP9X has the weakest support of the four pairs. As with CHIP–UCH37, no direct physical interaction between MUL1 and USP9X, by co‐immunoprecipitation, pull‐down, or any other method, has been reported in the literature we could locate; unlike CHIP–UCH37, however, USP9X's activity toward this specific substrate pathway is not independently established beyond its general role in editing K48 chains, so even the component‐level evidence is thinner. This pairing is presented in this review as an explicit, mechanistically motivated hypothesis (Section 5.1) rather than as an evidence‐based complex, and should be weighted accordingly.

Therapeutic Implications

Clinical Needs and the Drug‐Target Landscape

Sarcopenia affects roughly 10%–16% of adults over 65 worldwide, with rates rising to 11%–50% in those over 80 [58, 59]. Cancer cachexia accelerates mortality across most solid tumor types. Despite this large disease burden, neither condition currently has an approved pharmacological treatment. Single‐target approaches (such as silencing MUL1, inhibiting TRAF6, or blocking PHLPP) have not achieved robust muscle preservation across the full spectrum of the atrophic program. One reason may be that each intervention leaves the other three axes intact, all of which could still suppress AKT. The four E3–DUB interfaces described here may relate to this treatment gap. If each interface proves druggable, it could, in principle, be engaged without disrupting the broader enzymatic activities of the same proteins.

Interface‐Specific Pharmacological Strategies

The pharmacological strategies discussed below are conceptual and currently lack direct in vivo validation in skeletal muscle. Table 4 summarizes the four interface‐specific pharmacological strategies mentioned in this section. MUL1–USP9X (RING:DUSP): Stabilizing this interface with a molecular glue, a small molecule that bridges the interface to enhance binding strength, may help maintain AKT2 protein levels. This would support the stress‐sensing checkpoint in atrophying muscle without globally inhibiting MUL1, which is necessary for regulated mitophagy, or USP9X, which stabilizes various substrates. This method uses a concept similar to DUBTAC (deubiquitinase‐targeting chimera) technology [60], but in a potentially simpler form. The natural protein‐protein interaction already places USP9X next to AKT2 at the MOM. This means that a small molecule that reinforces the RING‐DUSP interaction may achieve selective AKT2 stabilization without requiring bifunctional linker chemistry. So far, no molecular glue targeting this specific interface has been reported. The feasibility of this method in muscle tissue still needs to be confirmed.

Combination Strategies and Translational Considerations

In wasting conditions, no single interface is likely sufficient, as all four axes may contribute to AKT suppression. A multi‐target approach (e.g., combining a MUL1–USP9X stabilizer with a CHIP–UCH37 stabilizer) may be needed in advanced sarcopenia or cancer cachexia. Key translational challenges include the following. First, PPI interfaces are, as a class, considered among the more difficult drug targets. Their typically flat, shallow, and extended contact surfaces are poorly suited to the small, deep pockets that conventional small‐molecule and peptide chemistry exploit most efficiently [49]. Additionally, successes remain the exception rather than the rule despite substantial investment in pharmaceuticals. Second, the limited membrane permeability of protein–protein interaction modulators compounds this problem for compounds large enough to span an extended interface. Third, precise tissue targeting and isoform‐specific engagement are both required. Several of the domains involved (e.g., DUSP, UBL, and LRR) recur across paralogous E3s and DUBs, and off‐target engagement of a related paralog could produce unintended effects outside skeletal muscle. Fourth, validated atomic‐resolution structures are lacking for most of the four interfaces, which constrains rational drug design. Fifth, the pharmacokinetics of PPI stabilizers in vivo remain uncertain. Finally, achieving muscle‐specific delivery while avoiding systemic effects remains a challenge. Additional concerns include MUL1–USP9X stabilization potentially altering mitophagy in non‐muscle organs, and Skp2–USP37 modulation, which requires careful dose titration in oncological settings. Partial AKT suppression may be beneficial, but complete suppression could harm insulin sensitivity. Rigorous preclinical safety evaluation will be essential before any of these strategies advance to first‐in‐human trials.

Conclusion

The four E3–DUB complexes transform AKT ubiquitination from a simple on/off switch into a proposed distributed regulatory network, which is a conceptual framework that awaits direct experimental validation. E3 ligases and deubiquitinases also serve as partners rather than mere competitors, forming four regulatory complexes: TRAF6–CYLD, MUL1–USP9X, CHIP–UCH37, and SCF‐Skp2–USP37. Each pair operates through three shared properties (allosteric co‐regulation, real‐time chain editing, and spatial confinement) to influence AKT ubiquitination in a distinct subcellular compartment. Upstream kinases can independently regulate each complex. This allows the cell to finely adjust AKT ubiquitin topology and shift the balance between anabolic and catabolic signaling with great spatial and temporal precision. In conditions of wasting, evidence suggests that multiple E3–DUB nodes are disrupted simultaneously, leading to the suppression of AKT through several largely independent mechanisms. This framework provides a convincing explanation for the limited success of single‐target treatments: deactivating one dysregulated node still leaves three others functioning, each capable of maintaining AKT suppression. Therefore, targeting multiple nodes simultaneously may be a more effective therapeutic strategy. Each of the four structurally distinct interaction interfaces may represent a conceptually new pharmacological target, as it is interface‐specific rather than catalytic. This would allow engagement without disrupting the many other cellular roles these enzymes serve. Bridging the gap between concept and therapy will require addressing the experimental priorities outlined in Section 10. This includes confirming the disruption of the E3–DUB complex in primary human muscle biopsies and resolving AKT isoform selectivity in live myotubes. It also includes mapping atypical chain architectures using UbiREAD and testing combination strategies in preclinical muscle‐wasting models. The necessary tools, such as diGly proteomics, TurboID proximity labeling, and isoform‐specific CRISPR knockouts, are already established in the field and ready for application to the proposed hypotheses. Overall, progress in these areas would transform the current inference‐based framework into a validated mechanistic foundation for rational drug development. More generally, E3–DUB binary complexes may create regulatory properties that neither enzyme can provide alone, and their stoichiometric disruption may contribute to disease states. This idea likely applies beyond AKT and skeletal muscle. As the field moves toward ubiquitin‐based therapies, the co‐regulatory architecture of E3–DUB complexes may prove at least as important as the individual enzymes themselves.

Limitations

Evidence quality for the four E3–DUB pairs is summarized in Table 1. In brief, TRAF6–CYLD is supported by endogenous co‐immunoprecipitation and functional perturbation data. SCF‐Skp2–USP37 rests on real but non‐muscle purified‐protein evidence for the Skp2–USP37 interaction itself. Compounding this, Skp2 is a cell‐cycle‐regulated protein with characteristically low baseline expression in post‐mitotic tissue. Because adult myofibers are predominantly post‐mitotic, whether sufficient Skp2 protein is even present in mature muscle for this axis to operate is an open, and to our knowledge unmeasured, question (Section 7.1). CHIP–UCH37 and MUL1–USP9X are weaker still, and for the same underlying reason: neither pair has a directly demonstrated physical interaction. For CHIP–UCH37, each partner's individual activity toward AKT‐related substrates is independently well documented, but the CHIP–UCH37 complex itself has not been isolated and characterized (Section 6.1). For MUL1–USP9X, no direct interaction has been reported by any method, and USP9X's connection to this substrate pathway is not independently established. Both pairings are presented here explicitly as unevidenced, testable hypotheses rather than demonstrated complexes (Sections 5.1 and 6.1). Furthermore, the explanation of the simultaneous disruption of all four complexes in chronic atrophy (Section 9.2) is a synthesized inference. It draws on experiments conducted in different cell lines, animal models, and atrophic stimuli. It has not been directly shown in any single in vivo or ex vivo model. Further, most of the direct mechanistic evidence for this framework comes from non‐human (mainly murine) or non‐muscle experimental systems. Direct validation in primary human skeletal muscle biopsies remains an important need. Finally, the competitive peptide data described in Section 7.1 link reduced AKT Ser473 phosphorylation to disruption of the LRR–UBL interface. However, no primary citation supports these specific experiments in either muscle or non‐muscle cell lines. The functional inference is extrapolated from the domain‐interaction data of Huang et al. [47] and should be regarded as a testable hypothesis rather than an established observation. Findings primarily based on inference are flagged accordingly in the evidence‐quality ratings in Table 1. Additionally, all screening, data extraction, and evidence grading in this review were performed by a single author, without an independent second reviewer to cross‐check inclusion decisions or resolve disagreements (Section 2).
