BouquetFL: Emulating diverse participant hardware in Federated Learning
Arno Geimer

Abstract
In Federated Learning (FL), multiple parties collaboratively train a shared Machine Learning model to encapsulate all private knowledge without exchange of information. While it has seen application in several industrial projects, most FL research considers simulations on a central machine, without considering potential hardware heterogeneity between the involved parties. In this paper, we present BouquetFL, a framework designed to address this methodological gap by simulating heterogeneous client hardware on a single physical machine. By programmatically emulating diverse hardware configurations through resource restriction, BouquetFL enables controlled FL experimentation under realistic hardware diversity. Our tool provides an accessible way to study system heterogeneity in FL without requiring multiple physical devices, thereby bringing experimental practice closer to practical deployment conditions. The target audience are FL researchers studying highly heterogeneous federations. We include a wide range of profiles derived from commonly available consumer and small-lab devices, as well as a custom hardware sampler built on real-world hardware popularity, allowing users to configure the federation according to their preference.

Introduction

Federated Learning
7

Much of this work focuses on the cross-device setting, where many participants with limited data and resource-constrained devices jointly train a global model. Real-world deployments are inherently heterogeneous: clients range from mobile phones to laptops and mixed consumer-grade GPUs. Nevertheless, most FL experiments assume homogeneous hardware, typically running all clients on a single high-end machine. This masks the performance variability that arises when devices differ in CPU speed, memory capacity, or GPU throughput.

2
Functionalities

BouquetFL is a framework designed to counter the previously observed lack of heterogeneity in experimental FL research. Currently, there are two possible solutions to this problem. Researchers could deploy their experiments on real heterogeneous hardware, i.e. hundreds of different computers, each representing a single user in the federation. This would require acquiring and setting up potentially tens to hundreds of machines, a task which is out of scope for most research labs. Trying a cloud environment instead is not a viable solution either: Besides the fact that many consumer-level hardware is not available with cloud providers, setting up a cloud-hosted federation on that many different hardware nodes is infeasible. The other solution is the local emulation of different computers, all on a single machine. This method is much cheaper and easier to deploy, and most research labs and universities already have the right tools for this task.

BouquetFL implements this second approach within Flower, the most popular FL framework, emulating heterogeneous hardware (different "flowers", together making up a bouquet) through temporary hardware restriction on a single machine. The framework emulates a broad spectrum of hardware configurations spanning multiple device families and vendors, enabling realistic heterogeneity within a single-machine setup. BouquetFL operates independently of the underlying model architecture, training procedure, or aggregation strategy, making it compatible with any Flower-based FL pipeline. Documentation and source code are available on
https://github.com/arnogeimer/bouquetfl
.
Deployment requires a Linux runtime environment with CUDA drivers and sudo privileges, a fairly common assumption in research environments.
As we will show, BouquetFL is easy to deploy, versatile, and provides useful approximations of relative performance.

2.1
Integration With Flower

BouquetFL integrates seamlessly into the Flower federated framework. Prior to launching a federation, users define participant profile types by specifying the target CPU, GPU, and RAM characteristics for each class of client. Alternatively, the user can use a built-in hardware generator to automatically sample representative hardware configurations. The underlying Flower system architecture stays exactly the same, as detailed in Figure
1
. The central ServerApp process launches ClientApps which call a pre-defined
.fit
method for local training. When the method is called, BouquetFL spawns a new, hardware-restricted environment based on the ClientApp’s hardware specifications. This virtual environment is partially isolated from the rest of the Flower architecture, ensuring strict preservation of hardware limitations, and no outside interference on performance. The ClientApp’s training is executed within the virtual environment, and model parameter updates are communicated accordingly to the ClientApp. Upon training termination, the Flower ClientApp continues as normal and the virtual environment is deactivated.

2.2
Representative hardware sampler

To ease configuration of the client population, especially in large federations, we provide a hardware sampler that automatically generates realistic client-hardware lists. The sampler draws from the Steam Hardware Survey
15
, which collects CPU, GPU, and RAM information from millions of users on the Steam gaming platform. We then matched survey entries against our own database of hardware specifications to build a configuration generator that returns representative distributions based on real-world device popularity. The sampler is constrained to currently available consumer hardware, preventing the selection of unrealistically high-end configurations. Alternatively, client profiles can be configured manually.

3
Limitations

Most hardware controls used by BouquetFL are applied globally rather than per process, preventing concurrent execution of clients with different hardware profiles. Consequently, clients must be executed sequentially to ensure isolation of hardware configurations. BouquetFL requires system-level resource control, restricting usage to Linux systems with NVIDIA GPUs and administrative privileges.

By design, restricting hardware resources to match specific device profiles should result in training behaviour that closely mirrors that of the target hardware. However, BouquetFL can only approximate real devices: certain architectural features, such as cache hierarchy or PCIe bandwidth, cannot be directly constrained. As a result, discrepancies may arise for highly memory-bound workloads or scenarios dominated by data transfer overhead.

Despite these limitations, BouquetFL offers a cost-effective and accessible alternative to deploying large heterogeneous testbeds.

4
Experimental validation of BouquetFL

An important question arises: Can BouquetFL preserve the relative performance ordering and inter-generational scaling trends of consumer hardware through enforcing constraints? To answer this question, we emulate training performance of various consumer GPUs using BouquetFL. Since direct benchmarks of machine learning training performance across consumer GPUs are not common, we contextualise our results using widely available video game benchmarks (PassMark software single videocard + UserBenchmark effective 3D speed), a practical proxy for relative GPU performance. While gaming workloads differ from machine learning training, given that both are compute- and memory-intensive, these benchmarks provide a reasonable approximation of inter-generational performance differences.

4.1
Experimental setup

All experiments are conducted on a single host system running Ubuntu 24.04, equipped with an AMD Ryzen 1800X CPU (8 cores / 16 threads, 3.6–4.0 GHz), 32GB of DDR4 RAM, and an NVIDIA RTX 4070 Super GPU (7168 CUDA cores, 1980–2475 MHz, 12GB GDDR6X). To ensure comparability, all simulated clients share the same host CPU and memory configuration. We emulate commonly used NVIDIA consumer GPUs spanning four hardware generations.

4.2
Results

Rather than matching absolute training times, we evaluate whether
BouquetFL
reproduces the relative performance hierarchy observed across GPU generations. Figure
2
reports the training times of a ResNet-18 model by heterogeneous clients in a federation. Our results suggest that BouquetFL effectively captures relative hardware performance differences and provides a meaningful approximation of training behaviour across heterogeneous consumer GPUs. Sampled GPUs are the GTX 1060 - 1080, GTX 1650 - 1660 Ti, RTX 2060 - 2080 and RTX 3050 - 3080. Resulting in a Spearman correlation coefficient
\rho=0.92
and a Kendall ranking correlation of
\tau=0.80
, our results suggest that BouquetFL credibly replicates the behaviour of modern GPUs. We emphasize that training time is only a minor aspect of the full capabilities of BouquetFL: Indeed, out-of-memory errors, differing performances due to RAM sizes, or dissimilar training speeds due to different data loading capacities through CPU discrepancies are all aspects that BouquetFL can model. BouquetFL’s out-of-memory error handling has been tested and confirmed through high batch size training on low-memory hardware devices. As have data loading speed differences by emulating CPUs with different core counts. The accompanying demonstration video illustrates hardware profile switching, runtime differences, memory failures, and dataloader bottlenecks in real time.

5
Conclusion

Motivated by the observation that device heterogeneity is rarely reflected in experimental federated learning studies, we introduce BouquetFL, a framework for simulating heterogeneous client hardware within the standard Flower workflow. BouquetFL enables controlled emulation of CPU, GPU, and memory constraints on a single machine, allowing researchers to study hardware-aware FL behaviour without deploying large multi-device infrastructures. Our experiments show that BouquetFL can approximate the relative training performance of a range of consumer-grade CPUs and GPUs. By enforcing hardware-level constraints, BouquetFL offers an accessible and cost-effective alternative to physical testbeds, opening the way for more detailed future research in an understudied aspect of Federated Learning. Beyond heterogeneous federation emulation, a possible application is the determination of client hardware requirements before training. Future development includes incorporating network latency simulation and support for limited parallel client execution.
