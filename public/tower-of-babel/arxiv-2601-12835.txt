Temporal Fair Division of Indivisible Goods with Scheduling
CHOI Kui Wang, Minming LI

Abstract
We study temporal fair division, where agents receive goods over multiple rounds and cumulative fairness is required. We investigate Temporal Envy-Freeness Up to One Good (TEF1) and Up to Any Good (TEFX), its approximation α \alpha -TEFX, and Temporal Maximin Share (TMMS). Motivated by known impossibilities in standard settings, we consider the model in various restricted settings and extend it by introducing scheduling. Our main contributions draw the boundary between possibility and impossibility. First, regarding temporal fair division without scheduling, we prove that while constant-factor α \alpha -TEFX is impossible in general, a 1 / 2 1/2 -approximation is achievable for generalized binary valuations and identical days with two agents. Second, regarding temporal fair division with scheduling, we demonstrate that a scheduling buffer of size at least n / 2 n/2 enables TEF1 for identical days. However, we establish that TEFX and TMMS remain largely impossible even with scheduling or restricted domains. These results highlight the inherent difficulty of strict temporal fairness and quantify the trade-offs required to achieve approximation guarantees.

Introduction

Fair division is a fundamental problem in multi-agent systems and economics, traditionally focusing on the static allocation of resources, such as splitting a cake or distributing a set of goods at a single point in time
[
26
,
10
,
17
,
11
,

. However, many real-world allocation problems are dynamic. From cloud computing resources and food bank donations to organ exchange networks, goods often arrive over time and must be allocated online
[

,

,
8

24
,
7

. Although this approach captures the dynamic feature for real-world problems, it ignores the fairness in intermediate allocations. Therefore, the goal of temporal fair division is to maintain fairness cumulatively over the long run, rather than just instantaneously in each round.

The transition from dynamic to temporal settings introduces significant theoretical challenges. Unlike dynamic fair division, where intermediate allocations do not have to achieve fairness, temporal mechanisms must take this into consideration. Recent work has painted a bleak picture for this domain: temporal fair allocations are often impossible to guarantee without compromising efficiency or social welfare
[
19
,
14
,
13
]
. For instance, strong fairness concepts like Envy-Freeness up to any good (EFX) are frequently unattainable in temporal settings because an agent’s perception of fairness fluctuates wildly as new goods arrive.

These impossibility results from the temporal fair division model raise a natural question: Are these failures inherent to the problem, or are they artifacts of the model’s rigidity? Standard temporal fair division assumes that goods must be consumed or allocated immediately upon arrival. However, in many applications, such as job scheduling on a server or assigning shifts, a natural flexibility exists: A system administrator can delay a job slightly; a manager can reshuffle next week’s roster; a student affairs office can assign part-time jobs to students within a flexible interval
[
29
,
25
,
21
]
.

Therefore, our main research question is to
examine the boundaries of temporal fairness across various settings, with or without scheduling, in temporal fair division
.

Related Works

Our work is related to online fair division
[
1
,
7
,
2
,
31
]
, where some (possibly zero) items arrive in consecutive rounds. We study a stronger version of it, known as temporal fair division, in which fairness must hold at every round
[
19
,
14
,
13
]
.
[
19
]
showed that maintaining EF1 for goods is impossible in uninformed setting without reallocating past items, and proposed a reallocation-based algorithm for two agents. However, they proved that and provided a polynomial-time algorithm, where at each round, the allocation is EF1 in the informed setting with two agents.
[
14
]
formalized temporal fairness for goods and chores, proving that TEFX allocations may not exist and providing TEF1 algorithms for two agents with chores or restricted settings: two item types, generalized binary valuations, and unimodal preferences. Finally,
[
13

2
Preliminaries

First,
\forall k\in\mathbb{N}
, denote
[k]:=\{1,\ldots,k\}
.
We study temporal fair division under an informed online fair division setting, denoted by the tuple
\mathcal{I}=\left(N,T,\left\{O_{t}\right\}_{t\in[T]},v=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}),r\right)
.

We have a set of
N=[n]
agents, and a set of items
\left\{O_{t}\right\}
that arrive in round
t\in[T]
, which are to be allocated to the agents.
Denote
O^{t}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{t}O_{i}
as the cumulative set of goods up to round
t
, and
O=O^{T}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{T}O_{i}
as the set of all goods.
For each agent, there is an additive valuation function
v_{i}:2^{O}\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
over the items, where for
S\subseteq O
,
v_{i}(S)=\sum_{g\in S}v_{i}(\{g\})
. We slightly abuse the notation and assume that
v_{i}(g)=v_{i}(\{g\})
. In addition, this work focuses on goods allocation, i.e.
\forall g\in O,\forall i\in N,v_{i}(g)\geq 0
.

An allocation
A=(A_{1},\dots,A_{n})
is defined as an ordered partial partition of
O
, where
A_{i}
is the goods allocated to agent
i
, such that
\forall i,j\in[N]
where
i\neq j
,
A_{i}\cap A_{j}=\emptyset
and
\bigcup_{i\in\mathcal{N}}A_{i}=O
.
In addition, under the online fair division setting, denote
A^{t}=(A^{t}_{1},\dots,A^{t}_{n})
as the allocation from round
1
to after round
t
, that is
A^{t}_{i}=A^{t-1}_{i}\cup(A_{i}\cap O^{t})
.

We adopt standard fairness definitions from
[
3
]
. Envy-Freeness (EF)
[
30
]
, which requires agents to value their own bundle most, is not always achievable with indivisible goods. Thus, we study two relaxations: EF1
[
26
,
10
]
allows envy if it is eliminated upon removing some item from the envied bundle, while the stronger EFX
[
11
,
17
]
requires envy to be eliminated upon removing any item. Given that EFX existence is open for
n>3
, we also consider
\alpha
-EFX
[
28
]
, which approximates EFX by requiring the ratio of an agent’s value to the envied bundle (minus any item) to be at least
\alpha
. Additionally, we consider Maximin Share (MMS)
[
10
]
, which guarantees agents the value they could secure if they partitioned the goods themselves and received the worst bundle.In the temporal setting, we prefix these notions with “T” (i.e., TEF1, TEFX,
\alpha
-TEFX, TMMS) to indicate that fairness must hold cumulatively at every round
[
14
]
.

Next, we introduce various settings that we will study.

Definition 1

(Identical Days)
.

A temporal fair division instance is under the identical days setting iff
\forall t,t^{\prime}\in[T]
, there is a bijection
f_{t,t^{\prime}}:O_{t}\Rightarrow O_{t^{\prime}}
s.t.
\forall i\in[n],\forall g\in O_{t},v_{i}(g)=v_{i}(f_{t,t^{\prime}}(g))
(studied by
[
20
]
).

Definition 2

(Generalized Binary Valuation)
.

A temporal fair division instance is under the generalized binary valuation setting iff
\forall t\in[T],\forall g\in O_{t},\forall i\in[n],v_{i}(g)\in\{0,b\}
where
b\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
(studied by
[
18
]
).

Definition 3

(Bi-valued Valuation)
.

A temporal fair division instance is under the bi-valued valuation setting iff
\forall t\in[T],\forall g\in O_{t},\forall i\in[n],v_{i}(g)\in\{b_{1},b_{2}\}
where
b_{1},b_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
(studied by
[
4
]
).

Definition 4

(Identical Valuation)
.

A temporal fair division instance is under the identical valuation setting iff
\forall t\in[T],\forall g\in O_{t},\forall i,j\in[n],v_{i}(g)=v_{j}(g)
(studied by
[
28
]
).

Definition 5

(House Allocation)
.

A temporal fair division instance is under the house allocation setting iff
\forall t\in[T],|O_{t}|=n
, and
\forall i\in N,|A^{t}_{i}\backslash A^{t-1}_{i}|=1
(studied by
[
15
]
).

Commonly Used Fair Division Algorithms

For completeness and to avoid ambiguity in our proofs, we will present the Envy-Cycle Elimination algorithm and the Round-Robin algorithm, along with their respective properties. We outline the high-level idea of each algorithm and provide the pseudocode in the appendix.

3.1
Preliminaries

A fair division instance is denoted by the tuple
\mathcal{I}=\left(N,M,v=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})\right)
, where
M
is the set of items with
m=|M|
to be allocated to the set of agents
N
with
n=|N|
and valuation profile
v
.

3.2
Envy-Cycle Elimination

Algorithm
4
, the Envy-Cycle Elimination algorithm, will be referred to as ECE throughout the paper. Intuitively, ECE repeatedly selects the agent that is unenvied and allocates a good to them. it guarantees the existence of at least one unenvied agent by swapping bundles if an envy cycle occurs.

Theorem 1
.

ECE returns an EF1 allocation and runs in polynomial time
[
26
]
.

Algorithm
5
will be referred to as MAX-ECE throughout the paper. MAX-ECE is a variant of ECE, where Theorem
1
still holds for MAX-ECE. MAX-ECE shares the same high-level idea as ECE, but instead of allocating any unallocated good, we allocate the maximum valued good to the unenvied agent. This modification ensures some nice properties regarding EFX and
\alpha
-EFX.

Theorem 2
.

MAX-ECE returns an EFX allocation for two agents if both agents have the same valuation profile
[
28
]
.

Theorem 3
.

MAX-ECE returns a
\frac{1}{2}
-EFX allocation
[
12
]
.

3.3
Round-Robin

Algorithm
6
, the Round-Robin algorithm, will be referred to as RR throughout the paper. Intuitively, RR allocates goods in a round-robin manner according to some arbitrarily fixed given ordering, and each agent is allocated their most valuable good among the available ones.

Theorem 4
.

RR returns an EF1 allocation and runs in polynomial time
[
6
]
.

Observation 1
.

If agent
i
has just been allocated a good by RR,
\forall j\leq i,|A_{i}|=|A_{j}|
and
\forall j>i,|A_{i}|=|A_{j}|+1
.

4
Temporal Fair Division without scheduling

4.1
TEF1

The existence of TEF1 for goods under a general setting has been addressed by
[
19
]
; thus, we focus on TEF1 under various other settings. Some restricted settings for goods have been studied by
[
14
]
. In particular, they proposed polynomial-time algorithms for two types of goods under the generalized binary valuations setting, assuming agents have unimodal preferences over the goods. Additionally, they studied the case when multiple goods arrive in each round, and showed that when there are
T=2
rounds, by running two Round-Robin algorithms, with one of the algorithms having its allocation sequence reversed, we achieve TEF1.

4.1.1
Identical Days

In the identical days setting with the house allocation setting, we can compute a TEF1 allocation in polynomial time when there are three rounds. We apply the result from
[
14
]
, as mentioned above, to compute a TEF1 allocation in two rounds. We show that applying this result in rounds
2
and
3
in our setting yields a TEF1 allocation.

Theorem 5
.

A TEF1 allocation can be computed in polynomial time under the house allocation setting when
T=3
.

The existence of a TEF1 allocation under the identical days setting for more than two rounds remains open.

4.2
TEFX

The existence of TEFX when there are at least two agents is determined to be negative by
[
14
]
. They used a counterexample to show that even when there are two agents with identical valuations, with bi-valued goods, and
T=2
, a TEFX allocation may not exist. We then consider TEFX under various settings.

4.2.1
Identical Days

Theorem 6
.

A TEFX allocation is not guaranteed under the identical days setting when
T>2
, even if there are only two agents with identical valuations.

The next setting we study is the generalized binary valuation. This class of valuation functions generalizes both identical and binary valuations. We demonstrate that a TEFX allocation exists when there are two agents in this setting. Additionally, when there are two agents, EFX implies MMS. Therefore, we also find a TMMS allocation when there are two agents.

4.2.2
Generalized Binary Valuation

Theorem 7
.

A TEFX and TMMS allocation can be computed in polynomial time when there are two agents under the generalized binary valuation setting.

Next, we derive the following theorem to complement the positive results for two agents.

Theorem 8
.

A TEFX allocation may not exist when there are three agents under the generalized binary valuation setting.

It was shown that TEFX allocation may not exist when all agents have identical valuations
[
14
]
. We combine the identical valuation setting with the generalized binary valuation setting, and establish a strong positive result by modifying the Round-Robin algorithm.

Theorem 9
.

A TEFX allocation can be computed in polynomial time when all agents’ valuations are both generalized binary and identical.

4.3
\alpha
-TEFX

In this section, we consider the approximation variant of TEFX,
\alpha
-TEFX. We can provide positive results in a general setting and achieve stronger results under various settings. We note that the main challenge for obtaining an
\alpha
-TEFX allocation is when there exist some goods that are zero-valued for some agents. Unlike online
\alpha
-EFX, which were studied previously
[
27
]
, in
\alpha
-TEFX, we focus on avoiding cases such as the following: after a round, an agent
i
that has no goods allocated to them, envies another agent, that has one good positively valued for
i
, and another good that is zero-valued for
i
. This is acceptable for standard online
\alpha
-EFX, but results in an invalid approximate TEFX allocation.

4.3.1
General Settings

We first demonstrate that the approximation ratio must depend on the values of the goods.

Theorem 10
.

A positive approximation for
\alpha
-TEFX may not exist if the approximation is independent of the values of the goods, even when there are only two agents with identical valuations.

As a result, we observe that the approximation ratio should be expressed in terms of the minimum- and maximum-valued goods. Therefore, we propose a loose bound for approximating TEFX when all goods are valued positively by all agents. Intuitively, we compute a
1/2
-EFX allocation in each round and add it to the allocation.

Theorem 11
.

A
\frac{\frac{1}{2}\cdot min_{g\in O}v_{i}(g)}{min_{g\in O}v_{i}(g)+\frac{1}{2}\cdot max_{g\in O}v_{i}(g)}
-TEFX allocation can be computed in polynomial time, if all agents value all goods positively.

Under the identical days setting, we propose a stronger approximation guarantee for TEFX with two agents using ECE based on Theorem
3
.

4.3.2
Identical Days

Theorem 12
.

A
\frac{1}{2}
-TEFX allocation can be computed in polynomial time when there are two agents under the identical days setting.

We also propose a stronger approximation guarantee for TEFX under the generalized binary valuation setting.

4.3.4
Identical Valuation

Theorem 14
.

A
\frac{min_{g\in O,v(g)>0}v(g)}{max_{g\in O}v(g)+min_{g\in O,v(g)>0}v(g)}

Lastly, we consider the bi-valued goods setting. In this setting, we define two positive constants, and each agent values each good at one of them. Notice that we exclude zero-valued goods; otherwise, it degenerates into the generalized binary valuation setting. We propose that the approximation ratio is the ratio between the two constants. Surprisingly, this ratio is tight.

For bi-valued goods valued

\frac{a}{b}
-TEFX allocation without scheduling, and it is tight.

4.4
TMMS

In this section, we consider Temporal Maximin Share Fairness (TMMS). Note that MMS does not exist when there are more than two agents
[

,
23

. We are the first to consider MMS under the temporal fair division model.

Unfortunately, under most settings, a TMMS allocation may not exist. In Theorem

Here, we note that under the generalized binary valuation setting, EF1 implies MMS

. Therefore, since
[

proved the possibility for TEF1 under the generalized binary valuation setting, TMMS also exists under this setting.

4.4.1
Identical Days

We consider the identical days setting. Here, we show that a TMMS allocation may not exist.

Theorem 16
.

A TMMS allocation for goods under the identical days setting may not exist, even when there are two agents with identical valuations, and
T>1
.

5
Temporal Fair Division with Scheduling

Scheduling is a process that allows a good that arrives in round
t
to be scheduled in round
t^{\prime}
such that
t^{\prime}\geq t
and
t^{\prime}-t+1\leq r
. We define this process with a function
delay(g,t,r^{\prime})
, where
r^{\prime}\leq r
, which denotes
O_{t}\leftarrow O_{t}\backslash\{g\}
and
O_{t+r^{\prime}-1}\leftarrow O_{t+r^{\prime}-1}\cup\{g\}

5.2
TEFX

Since a TEFX allocation may not exist without scheduling, the next question is whether TEFX exists when scheduling is allowed. We answer this question by providing a negative answer, provided the instance does not degenerate into the standard EFX model, where there exists only one round.

Theorem 18
.

A TEFX allocation may not exist, even with scheduling, when there are only two agents with identical valuations, for
r\leq T-1
and
T>1
.

Therefore, we consider TEFX under various settings. We first study the identical days setting. In Theorem
6
, we show that TEFX does not exist under the identical days setting when
T>2
. Surprisingly, if we schedule the goods when there are two agents, a TEFX allocation can be achieved under the identical days setting. As mentioned previously, when there are two agents, EFX implies MMS. Therefore, we also find a TMMS allocation when there are two agents.

Theorem 19
.

A TEFX and TMMS allocation can be computed in polynomial time when there are two agents under the identical days setting with scheduling, and
r\geq\lfloor\frac{T}{2}\rfloor
.

5.3
TMMS

We consider TMMS with scheduling, and show that even when there are two agents with identical valuations, after scheduling, TMMS may not exist, provided the instance does not degenerate into a standard MMS setting.

Theorem 20
.

A TMMS allocation for goods may not exist, even with scheduling, when there are only two agents with identical valuation, for
r\leq T-1
, and
T>1
.

In the identical days setting with scheduling, Theorem
19
shows that when there are two agents, a TMMS allocation exists.

6
Discussion

In this work, we explored the boundaries of fairness in temporal resource allocation, analyzing both approximation ratios and the power of scheduling. We show that while standard temporal fairness (such as TEFX and TMMS) remains largely unachievable even under restricted domains, meaningful guarantees are achievable if requirements are relaxed. Specifically, we established that scheduling is a powerful but limited tool. By introducing a buffer of size
r\geq n/2
, we can guarantee TEF1 in the identical days setting, providing a practical mechanism for system designers who can tolerate some amount of latency. However, we also showed that scheduling is not a panacea: the impossibility of TEFX and TMMS persists across most settings, suggesting that these metrics may be too demanding for temporal fair division. Regarding approximations without scheduling, our results for
\alpha
-TEFX reveal interesting properties. While constant-factor approximations are impossible in the general case, we proved that, under domain restrictions or in other settings such as generalized binary valuations, they admit stronger guarantees.

Several open questions remain regarding the extension of the temporal fair division framework. One key question is to tighten the scheduling bounds. Our result establishes
r\geq T/2
as a buffer for TEF1, which may not be a tight constraint. It remains an open question whether a smaller scheduling buffer can be obtained. Another key question is to study temporal fair division with varying strategic behavior for agents. Our model assumes agents truthfully report valuations. Investigating the compatibility of temporal fairness with strategy-proofness is crucial for ensuring that agents cannot manipulate the schedule by misreporting, which is essential for deploying these mechanisms in competitive environments, such as cloud computing markets.
