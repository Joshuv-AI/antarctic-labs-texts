Rare common femoral vein adventitial cystic disease presenting with lower-extremity swelling
Authors: Harsimran Panesar, Akmal Shahzad, Combiz Rezayat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdventitial cystic disease (ACD) is a rare, nonatherosclerotic disorder caused by mucin-filled cysts within the vessel wall that produce luminal compression. Venous ACD is uncommon and may mimic deep vein thrombosis. A 24-year-old woman presented with right leg pain and swelling from a 2.8-cm common femoral vein cyst. The patient underwent cyst excision and bovine patch venoplasty but developed postoperative thrombosis from venoplasty-site stenosis and hematoma compression. Thrombectomy and angioplasty failed, requiring venous stenting. Pathology confirmed ACD. The patient recovered on enoxaparin and clopidogrel. This case emphasizes confirming durable venous outflow after cyst excision.Keywords: Venous adventitial cystic disease, Common femoral vein, Deep vein thrombosis, Lower-extremity swelling, Venous obstruction, Venous stent

Adventitial cystic disease (ACD) is a rare, nonatherosclerotic vascular disorder characterized by mucin-filled cysts within the adventitia that compress the media and produce luminal stenosis.1 The exact prevalence of ACD remains unknown; however, most documented cases involve the popliteal artery.2 Far fewer cases of venous ACD have been reported, with the common femoral vein (CFV) being the most frequently affected vessel.2 Venous ACD may cause progressive leg edema and mimic deep vein thrombosis (DVT) creating a diagnostic challenge.3 ACD typically affects younger, otherwise healthy men, although cases can occur across a broad age range.2,4 The youngest documented patient was 5 years old.5

The pathogenesis of ACD remains uncertain. The prevailing “joint hypothesis” suggests the presence of an aberrant connection between a joint space and the juxta-articular portion of a vessel allowing synovial fluid to leak into the adventitia by tracking along a capsular branch.6 This is supported by the observation that the most commonly affected vessels are located near the knee and hip joints. Furthermore, these aberrant connections have been confirmed radiologically and intraoperatively.6,7

Management includes percutaneous aspiration, open cyst excision, and vein resection with graft interposition.3 Percutaneous treatment has higher recurrence because of incomplete cyst removal, whereas open excision generally provides more durable results.3,8 We present a case of CFV ACD in a young woman. The patient provided written informed consent for publication of the clinical details and images.

Case presentation

A 24-year-old woman with no medical history presented with 1 month of right leg pain and swelling. The leg was edematous and erythematous with intact distal pulses and sensorimotor function. Venous duplex was negative for DVT but showed a 3 cm cystic structure compressing the CFV. This was redemonstrated on computed tomography (CT) angiography and magnetic resonance imaging as a 2.8 cm ovoid lesion along the right CFV; however, its relationship to the lumen was indeterminate, raising concern for an intraluminal filling defect or an adventitial cyst (Fig 1, A-C).

Preoperative imaging of the right common femoral vein adventitial cyst. A, Axial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) demonstrating a cystic lesion adjacent to and compressing the right common femoral vein. B, Coronal computed tomography (CT) image showing the lesion along the course of the vein. C, Coronal MRI further characterizing the cystic lesion and its relationship to the adjacent venous structures.

The patient was discharged home on apixaban 2.5 mg and returned 4 days later for resection of suspected ACD. A transverse incision was made over the right groin. After longitudinal incision of the fascia lata, a firm cystic mass was noted with significant inflammation surrounding the femoral vein. The external iliac vein, femoral vein, and proximal venous branches were carefully dissected, allowing the cyst to be mobilized (Fig 2, A-C). Once the posterior aspect of the cyst was dissected, the cyst wall was resected leaving a small portion of the posterior cyst wall adherent to the CFV. As complete removal of the adherent portion would have required segmental venous resection and reconstruction, decompression with cyst wall excision was initially considered sufficient. The specimen was sent for pathology. A venogram performed through the distal femoral vein demonstrated approximately 60% stenosis of the CFV. We then performed venotomy, and the intimal surface appeared normal. The decision was made to perform venoplasty with a bovine patch following which the vein appeared widely patent. The patient tolerated the procedure well and was started on a postoperative heparin drip.

Intraoperative resection of a right common femoral vein adventitial cyst. A, Exposure of the cystic mass on the femoral vein (asterisk) with surrounding inflammation. B, Mobilization of the cyst (asterisk) after dissection of the external iliac vein, femoral vein, and proximal venous branches; the arrow indicates the femoral artery. C, Operative field following cyst wall resection, with venous patch.

On postoperative day 1, the patient was transitioned to rivaroxaban 10 mg for DVT prophylaxis because of a rash attributed to apixaban. On postoperative day 2, the patient developed significant right leg swelling. Venous duplex demonstrated extensive deep venous thrombosis despite anticoagulation. The patient was taken for venography, which showed patent distal popliteal and femoral veins with slow contrast transit. There was tapered narrowing of the CFV at the previous venoplasty site with associated thrombus. Intravascular ultrasound demonstrated some thrombus at the CFV. A near-occlusive lesion was identified at the level of the previous patch venoplasty (Fig 3), whereas the external iliac, femoral, and popliteal veins remained free of thrombus. A thrombectomy catheter was introduced, and multiple passes were performed across the area of concern. A small amount of fresh thrombus was retrieved; however, repeat venography showed no significant improvement. The lesion was then angioplastied with a 10 × 80 mm balloon for 3 minutes at burst pressure. Subsequent venography again demonstrated no significant change concerning external compression on the vein.

Postoperative venography following patch venoplasty. A near-occlusive lesion is present at the level of the previous common femoral vein patch venoplasty, with associated impaired venous outflow. The external iliac, femoral, and popliteal veins remain patent and free of thrombus.

Given the patient’s young age, the initial decision was made to avoid stent placement. The patient was started on therapeutic enoxaparin, and CT venography was obtained to evaluate for external compression, which demonstrated a small posterior hematoma with significant compression of the vein. Therefore, the decision was made to proceed with hematoma evacuation. The previous incision was opened, and after evacuation of the hematoma, a repeat venography demonstrated persistent approximately 90% stenosis of the CFV over a 6 cm segment. The lesion was predilated, and a 14 × 75 mm FORTEGRA venous stent (W. L. Gore & Associates, Inc.) was deployed across the stenosis, followed by angioplasty (Fig 4, A and B). A No. 7 Jackson-Pratt drain was placed. After irrigation and meticulous hemostasis, the groin incision was closed. The patient was discharged home on therapeutic enoxaparin and clopidogrel for 1 month. Pathology returned demonstrating a fibrotic adventitial cyst within the wall of the vein containing mucinous material, which was consistent with ACD. The patient continued to do well at 1- and 3-month follow-up visits, with no further complications related to the procedure.

Venographic evaluation and treatment of persistent common femoral vein stenosis. A, Repeat venography demonstrating approximately 90% stenosis of the common femoral vein extending over a 6 cm segment. B, Completion venography following venous stent placement and angioplasty, demonstrating improved luminal caliber and venous outflow.

Discussion

Venous ACD should be considered in young patients with unilateral leg swelling, few thrombotic risk factors, and a negative initial DVT study. Imaging may resemble thrombus or an extrinsic mass, so correlation across duplex, CT, and magnetic resonance imaging is often necessary. Anticoagulation alone does not correct the mechanical obstruction, which may lead to stasis and secondary thrombosis.

Operative strategy should reflect the relationship between the cyst and vein. Cyst excision preserves the native vessel when complete removal is feasible, whereas segmental resection with interposition grafting should be considered when the cyst is inseparable from the venous wall or excision would leave a substantial defect.9,10 In this case, residual posterior cyst wall was left because complete excision required segmental resection. Although patch venoplasty restored patency initially, segmental resection and reconstruction might have provided more definitive treatment.

The patient’s postoperative thrombosis was likely multifactorial. A posterior hematoma substantially compressed the reconstructed vein, and anticoagulation may have contributed to hematoma formation. Autologous great saphenous vein would also have been a reasonable patch option in this young patient; however, the early failure appeared related to external compression rather than choice of patch material.

Stenting was initially avoided because of the patient’s age. After thrombectomy, angioplasty, and hematoma evacuation failed to relieve approximately 90% stenosis over 6 cm, stenting was used as salvage. Repeat open reconstruction was considered, but postoperative inflammation and residual hematoma raised concern for continued compression of an interposition graft. This case emphasizes complete treatment of the involved segment and confirmation of durable venous outflow.

Conclusions

In conclusion, venous ACD should be considered in young patients with unilateral lower-extremity swelling and imaging demonstrating a cystic lesion associated with venous stenosis. Complete removal of the diseased adventitial tissue is important to restore durable venous outflow. When the cyst is inseparable from the venous wall or complete excision would compromise the vessel, segmental venous resection with reconstruction should be considered. Persistent postoperative obstruction warrants prompt evaluation for residual stenosis, thrombosis, or external compression, with endovascular intervention reserved for selected cases where surgical decompression or reconstruction is inadequate.
