Intra-articular antibiotic infusions versus intravenous antibiotic administration for infected knee arthroplasty: a study protocol for a randomized multicenter clinical trial
Authors: Baochao Ji, Yicheng Li, Xiaoyan Yan, Qi Sun, Guoqiang Zhang, Wei Chai, Peng Xu, Jianbing Ma, Yihe Hu, Jie Xie, Zongke Zhou, Huiwu Li, Ping Keung Chan, Weijun Wang, Xinzhan Mao, Wei Huang, Asihaerjiang Maimaitiyiming, Xiaogang Zhang, Guoqing Li, Boyong Xu, Li Cao

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeriprosthetic joint infection (PJI) poses a formidable challenge following total joint arthroplasty (TJA). Whether using Debridement, Antibiotics, and Implant Retention (DAIR) for acute cases or one/two-stage revision for chronic scenarios, postoperative antibiotics administered intravenously is standard. However, this delivery method inevitably risks hepatic and renal function impairment, lacking a sufficiently minimal biofilm eradication concentration. Intra-articular infusion as a novel antibiotic delivery method can circumvent systemic circulation, reduce the risk of hepatic and renal function impairment, and provide a sufficiently high antibiotic concentration at the prosthetic infection site. Yet, the efficacy of these methods has never been evaluated in a randomized controlled trial. The study aims to investigate infection control rates within 2 years post intra-articular antibiotic infusion versus intravenous antibiotic administration in a randomized design.MethodsThis pragmatic, multicenter, two-armed, parallel group, open, randomized non-inferiority trial (non-inferiority margin—15%) involves 11 high-volume tertiary referral centers (1:1 allocation). One hundred forty-two eligible PJI patients following total knee arthroplasty, undergoing one-stage revision, were randomly assigned to intra-articular or intravenous antibiotics. Blinding is not possible due to the nature of the intervention. However, data collectors and statistical analysts remain blind to the groups. All patients will be followed up for 2 years. The primary outcome is the infection control rate, and the secondary outcomes include functional outcome, concentrations of antibiotics in peripheral blood and joint fluid, along with liver and kidney injury, the reoperation rate, and antibiotic usage for both patient groups.DiscussionThis is the first randomized controlled trial to compare the efficacy of intra-articular antibiotic infusions and intravenous antibiotic administration. If intra-articular antibiotic infusion demonstrates non-inferiority in infection control rate compared to traditional intravenous antibiotic administration, with lower rates of hepatic and renal impairment, fewer complications, and reduced antibiotic usage, then this mode of drug administration should be further advocated in the treatment of periprosthetic knee infections. Introduction

Background and rationale {6a}

Periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) is one of the most severe complications following primary total joint arthroplasty (TJA) due to its challenging diagnosis, diverse treatment strategies, variable prognosis, and persistently high morbidity and mortality. Five-year mortality rates after hip and knee PJI can reach 21.1% and 21.6%, respectively [1, 2]. PJI imposes substantial physical and psychological burdens on patients and contributes significantly to healthcare costs. Recent US data estimate annual expenditures for hip and knee PJI could reach $1.85 billion by 2030 [3, 4].

For PJI, whether using Debridement, Antibiotics, and Implant Retention (DAIR) for acute cases or one- or two-stage revision for chronic infections, postoperative intravenous antibiotics remain standard. Reported success rates are 50–70% for acute infections [5–7] and 75–94% for chronic infections [8–10]. However, intravenous administration has limitations. Prolonged use of potent antibiotics such as vancomycin and meropenem can cause hepatic and renal toxicity [11], disrupt microbial balance, and increase secondary infection risk and mortality [12]. Liver and kidney metabolism shortens antibiotic half-life, requiring frequent infusions and raising costs. Biofilm-associated pathogens need antibiotic levels 100–1000 times the MIC, which are difficult to achieve intravenously, potentially reducing efficacy and promoting antibiotic resistance [13, 14].

Intra-articular antibiotic infusion is increasingly recognized as an alternative delivery route for PJI treatment. By directly injecting antibiotics into the infected joint via puncture or catheter, this method bypasses systemic circulation, potentially reducing hepatic and renal toxicity and preserving systemic microbial balance. It also allows prolonged maintenance of minimal biofilm eradication concentrations (MBEC), reducing administration frequency and potentially improving infection control while lowering resistance risk [15]. At our center, postoperative intra-articular infusions have been used for over 10 years in more than 300 cases, achieving infection control rates of 84.2–94.3% [16–18]. Other studies report similar outcomes: Whiteside et al. observed a 94.4% infection control rate [19], and Chaiyakit et al. reported 86.6% success in infected knees treated with DAIR plus intra-articular infusion over a mean 93.3-month follow-up [20].

We find it reasonable to hypothesize that the infection control rate, functional outcome, and quality of life of patients are non-inferior when compared intra-articular antibiotic infusions with intravenous antibiotic administration after one-stage revision. A randomized comparison of intra-articular antibiotic infusion, a promising yet debated strategy, with standard intravenous therapy following identical surgical procedures is essential for an objective evaluation of its clinical value {6b}.

Objectives {7}

The aim of this study is primarily to investigate the infection control rate within the 2 years after intra-articular antibiotic infusion versus intravenous antibiotic administration in a randomized study design. Secondary, to report the functional outcome, concentrations of antibiotics in peripheral blood and joint fluid, along with liver and kidney injury, the reoperation rate, and antibiotic usage for both patient groups.

Trial design {8}

This study is a pragmatic, multicenter two-armed, parallel group, open, randomized, non-inferiority trial with 1:1 allocation. This trial will comply with the CONSORT 2010 Statement [21] and the SPIRIT guidelines [22]. The study obtained initial ethical approval from the ethics committee of our institution on 19th June 2023 (No. 230310-05). Then, the protocol was modified and gained final approval on 23rd March 2024 (No. 230310-01−2403B-X2).

Methods

Participants and study setting {9}

One hundred and forty-two participants will be included consecutively in the study. Patients will be eligible for the study if they have a chronic infected knee arthroplasty and are deemed suitable for one-stage surgery by their treating surgeon. All participant centers followed consistent criteria for indications and contraindications of one-stage revision (Table 1). The infection was confirmed based on the Musculoskeletal Infection Society guidelines [23], which currently represent the most widely adopted and feasible diagnostic standard globally. Chronic PJI was defined as the duration of sudden symptoms of infection occurring more than 4 weeks. Patients will provide their written informed consent to participation before entering the study.

Flow {26a, 26b}

Patients with a clinically infected knee arthroplasty requiring one-stage revision will be screened for eligibility by an arthroplasty surgeon and subsequently offered inclusion in the study. The clinician is required to provide eligible patients with a detailed overview of the trial procedures, randomization details, collection and use of participant data and biological specimens, as well as associated benefits and risks of this study. After the patient fully comprehends the details of this trial, the informed consent form can be signed only if the patient agrees to participate.

Surgical technique of one-stage revision

The one-stage revision is divided into 3 steps following a direct sample taken through the capsule. Step one is aggressive debridement involving the removal of all necrotic and fibrous tissue, bone sequestra, and inflammatory synovium. Next, the components and cement debris are removed thoroughly. The surgical area is then exhaustively irrigated with 3–6 L of saline, 200 ml 3% hydrogen peroxide, and 0.5% aqueous betadine, then soaked in 400 to 500 ml 0.5% aqueous betadine (all soft tissues steeped) for 15 min. Step two: the surgical area is resterilized and redraped, while the surgical team rescrubs, dons new gowns, and replaces the entire set of instruments. Step three: after further pulsatile lavage, a new prosthesis is then implanted. Before suturing, sprinkle antibiotic powder in the surgical area based on preoperative pathogen culture results.

Interventions

After the one-stage revision surgery, all patients received intravenous antibiotic administration within 48 h. They were then randomly assigned to either the intra-articular antibiotic infusion group or the intravenous administration group in a 1:1 ratio after 48 h. The antibiotic selection principle was based on preoperative pathogen culture results in the absence of intraoperative culture results. Once the intraoperative culture results were confirmed, antibiotic selection was further adjusted accordingly, as outlined in Fig. 1. In case of Gram-positive cocci identified in the pathogen results, vancomycin (1 g:250 ml saline) was chosen, administered every 12 h. In case of Gram-negative bacillus identified, meropenem (1 g:100 ml saline) was chosen, administered every 8 h. In the case of negative culture results, a combination of vancomycin and meropenem was administered (1 g vancomycin:250 ml saline + 1 g meropenem:100 ml saline).

Antibiotic selection

Intra-articular antibiotic infusions {11a}

These patients received once-daily local intra-articular antibiotic infusions starting 48 h postoperatively, with a treatment duration of 10 days. In case of Gram-positive cocci identified in the pathogen results, vancomycin (0.5 g:10 ml saline) was chosen. If the bacteria were Gram-negative rods, meropenem (0.5 g:10 ml saline) was selected. In the case of negative culture results, a combination of vancomycin and meropenem was administered (0.5 g vancomycin:7 ml saline + 0.5 g meropenem:7 ml saline).

Intravenous antibiotic administration {6b}

A randomized comparison between intra-articular antibiotic infusion—a potentially advantageous but controversial approach—and the conventional standard intravenous antibiotic therapy after the same surgical intervention is necessary to objectively evaluate its true efficacy. According to the 2013 Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) guidelines [24], the patients in the intravenous antibiotic administration group continued intravenous antibiotic infusion starting 48 h postoperatively, with a treatment duration of 10 days. Based on local bacterial profiles, in the case of Gram-positive cocci identified in the pathogen results, vancomycin (1 g: 250 ml) was chosen, administered every 12 h. Starting from the fifth dose of intravenous vancomycin, serum trough concentrations of vancomycin are monitored every other day, maintaining the blood trough concentrations of vancomycin between 15 and 20 mg/L. Subsequently, antibiotic therapy may be modified according to culture and sensitivity results to optimize treatment efficacy and minimize the risk of antimicrobial resistance. If the bacteria were Gram-negative rods, meropenem (1 g:100 ml saline) was selected, administered every 8 h. In the case of negative culture results, a combination of vancomycin and meropenem was administered. Antimicrobial stewardship evaluation will be conducted throughout the trial to monitor and guide appropriate antibiotic use.

Postoperative vancomycin dosage adjustment {11b}

Postoperative vancomycin dosage adjustment is initiated in the event of renal function impairment. The use of renal protective medications is administered based on the severity of renal impairment, and detailed records are maintained in the medical records and eCRF. If, despite the corresponding renal protective measures, the patient’s renal function deteriorates further, with creatinine exceedingly twice the baseline value or a urine output less than 0.5 ml/kg within 12 h, a proportional reduction in vancomycin dosage is necessary based on the patient’s creatinine clearance and serum trough concentrations of vancomycin.

Oral antibiotics

Oral antibiotics were initiated on the 13th day after the one-stage revision surgery. Levofloxacin (500 mg once daily) and rifampin (450 mg once daily) are selected preferentially. In cases where susceptibility testing indicated resistance to the aforementioned antibiotics for Gram-positive bacteria, linezolid (600 mg twice daily) was administered. For Gram-negative bacteria, faropenem (200 mg/3 times daily) was prescribed. The oral antibiotic regimen was continued for a duration of 8 weeks.

Clinical follow-up

The patients were routinely followed up at 1, 3, 6, 12, and 24 months postoperatively. During follow-up, the serum erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and C-reactive protein (CRP) levels for infection, Hospital for Special Surgery knee score (HSS) for function, Short Form 12-Item (SF-12) for overall health status, and Visual Analog Scale (VAS) for pain were assessed. The occurrence of re-infection will be determined by the presenting clinical signs and consistent with the preoperative diagnosis of infection (Table 2).

Strategies to improve adherence to interventions {11c}

Participants are briefed daily on their condition and treatment plan, including medication details and timings. Post-treatment, they are queried about any discomfort. Daily activities such as psychological counseling, rehabilitation guidance, and dietary advice ensure participants understand the standardized, regulated, and personalized nature of each treatment step. They are also apprised of the research project’s benefits, such as infection control, functional improvement, restoration of daily habits, and associated compensation measures.

Each group assigns dedicated physicians to treat participants, who consult them in specialized treatment rooms to minimize inter-participant communication. Participants communicate directly with their physicians at least 3 times daily to address any discomfort or concerns during treatment.

Operation record forms/medication box retrieval. Participants register unused pills and bottles at each follow-up visit, with unused pills counted and recorded in the appropriate eCRF.

Relevant concomitant care permitted or prohibited during the trial {11d}

All intervention measures in this study are conducted during the patient’s hospitalization; hence, there are no specific accompanying care measures to prohibit.

Safety

All participating centers in this trial are well-established tertiary hospitals, with responsible physicians having extensive experience in PJI treatment and supported by comprehensive disease management teams. The operating physicians will undergo specialized training to ensure uniform standards and procedural consistency in completing intra-articular antibiotics infusions. Patient follow-up adheres to a standardized protocol. All adverse events (AEs) are meticulously recorded and receive appropriate management, with severe adverse events promptly reported to our institutional review board. All AEs will be classified by severity and their potential relationship to the study intervention. Predefined stopping rules will be applied: if intra-articular infusion is suspected to cause significant joint damage (e.g., persistent joint effusion or severe skin inflammatory response), the intervention will be immediately discontinued, and the patient will receive standard care.

In addition, monitoring for the emergence of antibiotic resistance and superinfections will be integrated into the safety assessment. Culture and sensitivity testing will be performed if clinical signs of persistent or recurrent infection are observed. Cases of multidrug-resistant organisms or superinfections will be promptly reported to the Data Monitoring Committee (DMC), which has the authority to recommend protocol modifications or early termination of the study if safety concerns arise.

Intravenous infusion is a standard clinical practice. Based on our records of over 300 cases, intra-articular antibiotic infusion has not been associated with any significant risks. In addition to the inherent surgical risks, patients enrolled in this trial do not face additional treatment-related risks.

Provisions for post-trial care {30}

The primary responsible unit for this project, the First Affiliated Hospital of Xinjiang Medical University, procures clinical trial liability insurance to conduct clinical trials. This insurance covers any harm to patients’ health related to the research procedures during the trial. If patients experience any discomfort or illness, they are asked to inform their doctor immediately. Patients will receive prompt treatment, and compensation will be provided by the insurance company in accordance with relevant Chinese laws and regulations.

Outcomes {12}

Primary outcome

Infection control rate is defined as the proportion of patients with no recurrence of infection among the total number of participants upon completion of the study follow-up. The definition of no recurrence of infection will adhere to the MSIS standard. Until the last follow-up, ESR and CRP should be within normal limits, and the affected limb should exhibit no infection-related symptoms (resting pain, swelling, increased skin temperature, wound drainage, sinus tract formation). For participants with autoimmune diseases, slight elevation of ESR and CRP (not exceeding 2 times the normal values) is acceptable, given it remains stable for two consecutive weeks, and the participants are devoid of any infection-related symptoms.

Hospital for Special Surgery Knee Score (HSS): A total of 100 points, comprising local conditions and overall functionality after knee joint surgery. It includes pain (30 points), joint function (22 points), range of motion (18 points), muscle strength (10 points), flexion deformity (10 points), and joint stability (10 points).

Sample size {14}

The purpose of this study is to evaluate the non-inferiority of infection control rates between intravenous and local antibiotic injection 2 years after primary revision surgery. Therefore, the sample size formula for non-inferiority of qualitative data is employed, and the calculations were performed using PASS 2021 (NCSS, LLC, Kaysville, UT, USA).

With an alpha of 0.025 (one-sided), a power of 0.8, a literature search indicating a 2-year postoperative infection control rate (PC) of 90% for the intravenous injection group, and an anticipated infection control rate for the local injection group non-inferior to the intravenous injection group, a non-inferiority margin (Δ) of −15% is chosen. The non-inferiority margin (Δ) based on clinical considerations: previous studies and expert consensus suggest that a reduction in infection control rate of up to 15% would be clinically acceptable if accompanied by potential benefits such as reduced systemic toxicity, fewer adverse events, and improved patient compliance. This trade-off threshold has been used in similar orthopedic infection trials and is considered reasonable in the context of balancing efficacy and safety. Substituting these values into the formula yields a sample size of n = 64. Considering a 10% dropout rate, the final sample size for both groups is at least 71 patients and 142 patients in total. The sensitivity analysis (15% and 20% dropout) was conducted as a stress test and indicates that, should attrition be higher than anticipated, approximately 75–80 participants per group would be required to preserve power. We will implement retention measures and monitor attrition throughout recruitment to keep dropout at or below 10%; if needed, we will consider modest over-recruitment to maintain target power. To account for the planned interim analyses and the possibility of early trial termination, the sample size and statistical power calculations incorporate an alpha-spending function using the O’Brien-Fleming approach. This adjustment preserves the overall type I error rate across multiple looks at the data while maintaining adequate statistical power.

Recruitment strategies {15}

Recruitment directly from the clinical care process involves two scenarios: (1) when the participant’s physician acts as the investigator of the clinical trial, confirming the participant’s eligibility and recommending participation; (2) when the physician is not the investigator, they still evaluate the participant’s eligibility, inquire about their willingness to participate, and then coordinate with the investigator for enrollment.

Open recruitment of participants includes (1) establishing informational boards and inquiry desks in waiting areas, lounges, or hospital entrances of outpatient and inpatient departments to provide consultation services to potential participants and (2) conducting professional training for healthcare personnel across various hospitals and utilizing peer and subordinate medical centers to support recruitment efforts.

Randomization and data collection

Sequence generation {16a}

After patients have provided informed consent and agreed to be randomized, preoperative outcome measures will be collected. The randomization system is managed by a non-blinded statistician, responsible for randomization. Randomization tables are generated using R software, specifically employing the sample () function to create a reproducible random allocation sequence. These tables are then uploaded to the central randomization system.

Concealment mechanism {16b}

After the generation of random numbers and allocation information by the central randomization system, researchers implement corresponding interventions according to the sequence of participants enrolled in the trial. The random sequence and allocation information remain fully concealed from all study personnel, except the independent statistician who generated the sequence, until the primary endpoint analysis. The researchers implementing interventions are not permitted to change the allocated interventions. Personnel responsible for participant recruitment and medical history collection are prohibited from receiving allocation information. Throughout the trial, research assistants record all data in the electronic case report form (eCRF), which is subsequently handed over to researchers not involved in the experiment for computer data entry. The random allocation sequence is solely preserved by the independent statistician, and it cannot be accessed or opened under normal circumstances, except in the case of severe adverse events requiring unblinding.

Implementation {16c}

Upon successful subject screening, researchers employ the IWRS (Interactive Web Response System) to perform the randomization process and obtain randomization numbers. Central randomization and grouping will be conducted 48 h postoperatively. The randomization outcomes are promptly communicated to each participating center for subsequent treatment procedures. The independent statistician overseeing the randomization ensures that allocation information is disclosed to the centers only after participant enrollment and assignment, preventing any risk of unblinding.

Blinding

Due to the nature of the interventions, it is not possible to blind patients or staff to allocation. Patients will be informed of the allocated group when they complete the treatment within the first 48 h post-surgery {17a}. However, data collectors and statistical analysts remain blind to the groups. It is mandated that the allocation status of participants should not be disclosed during data assessment, and assessors will undergo training on the evaluation procedures {17b}. Independent assessors will not be involved in randomization or intervention and will not have access to information regarding participant allocation or specific treatment measures. Importantly, the outcome assessors responsible for determining infection control status at follow-up are also blinded to treatment allocation, ensuring that endpoint evaluation is conducted without knowledge of group assignment.

Statistical analysis

Initially, we aim to ensure the rationality and clarity of the statistical methods by delineating and comparing the baseline characteristics of subjects within each group. The objective is to evaluate the comparability of baseline data across groups. Following this, assuming balanced and comparable baseline characteristics, we will assess differences in outcome indicators. Paired statistics, either parametric or nonparametric, will be employed based on the data’s nature to analyze changes between pre- and postoperative statuses. Continuous variables will be reported using means with standard deviation or median with inter-quartile range, while categorical or binary variables will be presented as frequencies and proportions, as appropriate.

Analyses will adhere to both the “intention-to-treat” (ITT) and per-protocol (PP) principles. ITT analysis will report outcomes for all participants, detailing patient adverse events and complications, while PP analysis will focus on infection control rates in patients who completed the intervention. For the comparison of general conditions between the two groups, intergroup comparisons of quantitative indicators are conducted using paired t-tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests based on the distribution of the data. For categorical indicators, the chi-square test or exact probability method (if the chi-square test is not applicable) is employed, and for ordinal data, Wilcoxon rank-sum tests or CMH tests are utilized {20a}. All statistical tests, unless otherwise specified, are two-sided, and a P value less than 0.05 (two-sided) is considered statistically significant. To address missing data, the primary approach will be multiple imputation under the assumption of missing at random (MAR). Sensitivity analyses will also be conducted using Last Observation Carried Forward (LOCF) to test robustness. In addition to the listed statistical methods, detailed and supplementary exploratory analyses may be necessary and should be confirmed in the statistical analysis plan (SAP) {20b}.

The primary endpoint is the infection control rate at 2 years postoperatively. Evaluation is conducted based on the full analysis set (FAS) and per-protocol set (PPS). With a significance level of α = 0.025 (one-sided), the infection control rates at 2 years postoperatively for both groups, the difference in infection control rates, and their 95% confidence intervals are separately calculated. If the lower limit of the 95% CI is higher than −∆, it is considered that the non-inferiority hypothesis for the experimental group compared to the control group is established {20a}.

The HSS, SF-12, VAS score, antibiotic concentrations in peripheral blood, antibiotic concentrations in joint fluid, and antibiotic usage (mg) are statistically described and compared between groups using quantitative measures. Surgical rates are statistically described and compared between groups based on categorical measures {20a}. For secondary outcomes involving multiple functional scores, adjustments for multiple comparisons will be performed using Bonferroni correction. False discovery rate (FDR) control will be applied as a sensitivity approach.

The time interval for infection recurrence within 2 years is analyzed using the Logrank test. The Cox proportional hazards model is employed to calculate the hazard ratio (HR) and its 95% confidence interval (CI). Kaplan-Meier curves for infection recurrence are plotted according to treatment groups {20b}. Predefined subgroup analyses will be conducted based on clinically relevant factors, including infection type, age groups, and baseline comorbidities. These analyses are prespecified to minimize the risk of post hoc bias. The complete case analysis is used to handle missing data {20c}.

Interim analyses {21b}

The study includes a predefined termination criterion to ensure patient safety and trial integrity. Termination may be considered if one of the allocated groups demonstrates a substantially higher treatment failure rate (re-infections necessitating re-revision). Specifically, this is defined as a relative risk ratio (RR) of ≥2.0 and an absolute difference of at least 10% between groups. This evaluation will occur at the earliest after the inclusion of 40 participants, with a minimum of 10 treatment failures required for consideration. This dual criterion ensures that early stopping decisions are based on both clinically meaningful differences and a sufficient number of events. Interim analyses will be performed following the enrollment of 50 participants and subsequently on an annual basis until the full sample size is achieved. To preserve the overall type I error rate across multiple evaluations, these analyses will employ an alpha-spending function based on the O’Brien-Fleming approach. Reports of any treatment failures triggering interim evaluation will be submitted to the Ethics Committee and the Clinical Trial Management Office as serious adverse events.

Monitoring

The coordinating center and the Trial Steering Committee (TSC) are composed of department heads from each participating center and personnel from the clinical trial office. Their main responsibilities include (1) finalizing protocol agreements and ensuring adherence to the study protocol across all sites; (2) recruiting participants and maintaining communication with principal investigators at each site; (3) reviewing study progress, organizing research progress discussion meetings every 3 months, and, when necessary, approving protocol amendments and/or investigator manuals to facilitate the smooth conduct of the research; (4) overseeing the day-to-day operations of the trial, including coordinating study activities, monitoring site performance, ensuring compliance with regulatory requirements, and providing organizational support; and (5) monitoring data quality and participant safety, liaising with other oversight committees as needed.

The day-to-day trial operations are managed by a dedicated operational team within the coordinating center, which meets at least monthly to review ongoing activities and resolve operational issues. The TSC meets quarterly to provide strategic oversight, review trial progress, and ensure the study is conducted according to high-quality RCT standards.

Composition of the data monitoring committee, its role and reporting structure {21a}

The DMC, independent of sponsors and competing interest parties, is primarily comprised a third-party clinical trial management organization responsible for (1) establishing the EDC system, (2) maintaining the trial IT system and data entry, and (3) data verification. The DMC will convene at each planned interim analysis—after 50 participants have been enrolled and annually thereafter—to review accumulating safety and efficacy data.

Adverse event reporting and harms {22}

General adverse events only require documentation in medical records and case reports, without additional reporting. In case of a serious adverse event (SAE), the researcher must promptly administer appropriate treatment and notify the clinical trial institution, ethics committee, and sponsor within 24 h. Each SAE will be assessed for severity and its relationship to the study treatment. The sponsor will evaluate and report all SAEs to regulatory authorities, researchers, trial institutions, and ethics committees as mandated. SAE reports should be thorough, documenting all known event details.

Frequency and plans for auditing trial conduct {23}

Pre-trial audits involve preparing research documents, submitting ethical materials, signing the trial protocol, and training personnel.

During the trial, audits include routine visits, document submissions to the ethics committee, adverse event reporting, participant compliance monitoring, and ensuring accurate documentation and protocol compliance. The Project Management Group will meet on a monthly basis to review overall trial progress and operational conduct. The Trial Steering Group will convene regularly throughout the study period to provide oversight of trial implementation and quality. In addition, the DMC will meet at predefined intervals to independently review trial conduct, evaluate safety data, and assess interim analyses. The DMC will make recommendations regarding trial continuation, modification, or termination based on their findings.

Post-trial audits involve eCRF collection, addressing statistical queries, verifying trial document integrity, ethics committee notification, center closure, and report preparation. Audits occur at least every 4 weeks as required by the trial. The process will be independent from investigators.

Discussion

Prosthetic knee infections, due to their unique anatomical features, allow for the direct application of antibiotics into the local joint. This approach completely avoids the adverse effects that systemic circulation of antibiotics may have on vital organs and the overall systemic microbiota of patients. In this trial, we aim to compare the new method of intra-articular antibiotic infusions with the current standard of intravenous antibiotic administration, using a randomized controlled design. If intra-articular antibiotic infusion demonstrates non-inferiority in infection control rate compared to traditional intravenous antibiotic administration, with lower rates of hepatic and renal impairment, fewer complications, and reduced antibiotic usage, then this mode of drug administration should be further advocated in the treatment of periprosthetic knee infections. Alternative outcomes will initiate informed discussions regarding treatment strategies for periprosthetic knee infections.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first randomized controlled trial to compare intra-articular antibiotic infusions and intravenous antibiotic administration. Previous studies on intra-articular antibiotic infusions have been predominantly single-center retrospective analyses with small sample sizes and diverse surgical approaches.

In patients with periprosthetic knee infection, who have endured significant physiological and psychological distress, the paramount objective is to maximize the infection control rate. Achieving a higher infection control rate remains the primary goal of all treatments. The prerequisite for the widespread adoption of any new treatment modality is that it should not exhibit inferior efficacy compared to existing treatment approaches. Therefore, the infection control rate has been selected as the primary outcome measure for this study. Subsequent evaluations of secondary outcomes, such as postoperative complications, joint function, and reoperation rates, are considered clinically meaningful in situations where treatment efficacies are comparable. The injection method, dosing frequency, and dosage employed in the intra-articular antibiotic infusion group of this trial adhere to a protocol previously successful in over 300 cases of PJI that we have implemented [16–18]. Theoretically, this protocol, characterized by a quarter of the antibiotic dosage compared to traditional intravenous administration, may significantly reduce postoperative healthcare costs for patients. The protocol has some limitations. This trial focuses exclusively on chronic PJI treated with one-stage revision, limiting generalizability to acute or two-stage cases. Although the multicenter design enhances external validity, variability in surgical techniques may introduce bias. Additionally, while the 2016 MSIS criteria were applied in this study, the 2018 criteria provide a more precise diagnostic framework, which is acknowledged as a study limitation. Lastly, all patients with Gram-positive infections in this study received vancomycin, which may increase the risk of resistance.

With the rapid increase in the number of primary total knee arthroplasties, the patient population at risk for periprosthetic joint infections is inevitably expanding. Currently, global population growth, coupled with the ongoing pandemic sweeping across the world, has placed a substantial burden on the global economy. Many countries are grappling with the overwhelming challenges of societal healthcare. A drug delivery approach that can offer a reliable infection control rate while reducing complications and associated costs of medications and treatments might contribute significantly to alleviating the burden on national healthcare systems. The data obtained from our trial may prompt arthroplasty surgeons to reevaluate the traditional route of antibiotic administration and confidently explore the postoperative use of intra-articular antibiotic infusions for treating periprosthetic knee infections.
