Climate classification, sociodemographic development, and the burden of inflammatory bowel disease in tropical countries
Authors: Sijie Gong, Jianwei Yu, Feiyang Zhong, Pingyu Zhong, Qinfen Lan, Xuejing Zhong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGeographic definitions of the tropics may obscure substantial climatic heterogeneity. Climate classification provides a more ecologically meaningful framework for evaluating inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) in tropical settings. However, few studies have examined tropical IBD burden using an integrated framework that combines climate classification, sociodemographic development, decomposition analysis, frontier analysis, and long-term forecasting.MethodsWe analyzed 1990–2023 estimates of IBD incidence, prevalence, mortality, years lived with disability (YLDs), years of life lost (YLLs), and disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2023. Climatic heterogeneity was characterized using the Koppen–Geiger climate classification. Temporal trends were evaluated with estimated annual percentage change (EAPC), and associations with tropical climate proportion and the sociodemographic index (SDI) were examined using Spearman rank correlation. Das Gupta decomposition quantified the contributions of population growth, population aging, and epidemiological change to changes in absolute burden. Frontier analysis and autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) models were used to assess potential for improvement and forecast age-standardized burden through 2050.ResultsIn 2023, IBD burden varied substantially across the 96 tropical countries and territories matched to GBD 2023. A higher tropical climate proportion was negatively correlated with the age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR; rho = − 0.279), age-standardized prevalence rate (ASPR; rho = − 0.318), and age-standardized YLD rate (rho = − 0.313). SDI was positively correlated with ASIR (rho = 0.248) and ASPR (rho = 0.270), but negatively correlated with the age-standardized death rate (ASDR; rho = − 0.310), age-standardized YLL rate (rho = − 0.372), and age-standardized DALY rate (rho = − 0.223). Population growth and aging accounted for most increases in absolute burden. For both sexes combined, population growth explained 58.7% of the increase in prevalent cases, 81.2% of the increase in DALYs, and 97.6% of the increase in YLLs. ARIMA forecasts suggested that ASDR would decline from 0.41 to 0.29 per 100,000 between 2024 and 2050. The age-standardized DALY rate was projected to decline from 15.33 to 13.28 per 100,000, and the age-standardized YLL rate from 10.14 to 8.24 per 100,000. Incidence, prevalence, and YLD rates were projected to remain broadly stable.ConclusionsIBD burden in tropical regions varies across climatic and sociodemographic contexts. Climate-related environmental factors may contribute to geographic differences, but this ecological analysis does not support causal inference.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s41182-026-01050-1.

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), primarily comprising Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC), is a lifelong immune-mediated disorder of the gastrointestinal tract. Historically, IBD was viewed as a predominantly Western disease concentrated in high-income regions, including North America, Western Europe, and Australia. Over the past three decades, however, its epidemiology has changed markedly. Evidence from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study and population-based investigations indicates that the number of people living with IBD, and the need for long-term care, have increased across Asia, Latin America, Africa, and the Caribbean [1–3]. This expansion reflects population growth as well as dietary industrialization, urbanization, population aging, changing sanitation patterns, and improved diagnostic capacity. Systematic reviews and projection studies suggest that IBD prevalence in some high-income countries may ultimately approach or exceed 1% of the population [4–6].

Incidence and prevalence alone do not capture the clinical burden of IBD. CD often involves segmental, transmural inflammation and may lead to strictures, fistulas, perianal disease, or intestinal resection. UC is usually limited to the colonic mucosa but can cause recurrent hematochezia, anemia, acute severe colitis, and increased colorectal cancer risk. Current evidence emphasizes interactions among genetic susceptibility, host–microbiome crosstalk, epithelial barrier dysfunction, and dysregulated immune responses [7–9]. Diet, antibiotic exposure, infections, air pollution, sunlight, temperature, and social behavior may modify these processes. The gut microbiota and its metabolic networks, including short-chain fatty acid production, bile acid metabolism, mucus-layer integrity, and regulatory immune-cell function, are central to this pathophysiological framework [10–12]. Research on IBD in tropical regions therefore needs to consider climate-related environmental factors, infectious disease spectra, and health care capacity, rather than uncritically applying risk frameworks derived from temperate high-income settings.

The global evolution of IBD is often described in four epidemiological stages: increasing incidence during early industrialization, accumulation of prevalence, a high-prevalence plateau, and a growing long-term management burden as populations age and survival improves [13–15]. This framework helps explain the lag between high-income countries and emerging economies, but it does not fully explain heterogeneity within the tropics. Studies from Asia, South Asia, and Africa indicate that IBD is increasing, although growth rates, diagnostic quality, disease severity, and treatment access vary markedly across countries [16–18]. In some resource-limited settings, medication supply is unstable, endoscopic and pathological capacity is limited, and specialist availability is insufficient. Patients with chronic diarrhea, hematochezia, or weight loss may initially be treated as having intestinal tuberculosis, amebiasis, or other infectious enteropathies, thereby delaying standardized IBD diagnosis [19, 20]. Tropical IBD burden may therefore reflect both true epidemiological change and changes in diagnostic visibility.

A further challenge is that the geographic tropics are not synonymous with the climatic tropics. The land area between the Tropic of Cancer and the Tropic of Capricorn includes humid rainforests, monsoon regions, savannas, arid deserts, plateaus, and mountain systems. These environments differ in precipitation, humidity, temperature variability, vegetation, biodiversity, air pollution, sunlight exposure, and outdoor activity patterns. The Koppen–Geiger climate classification is based on temperature and precipitation thresholds and provides a reproducible approach for characterizing this heterogeneity [21, 22]. Although previous GBD-based studies have described global and regional IBD burden, few have integrated long-term forecasting, frontier analysis, Das Gupta decomposition, SDI benchmarking, and climate classification within a single tropical-region framework [1, 23]. To address this gap, we combined geographic tropical status with Koppen–Geiger climate classification and SDI to examine temporal trends, burden drivers, potential for improvement, and projected trajectories of IBD in tropical countries and territories. We hypothesized that latitude alone would not sufficiently capture environmentally relevant heterogeneity within the tropics and that climate-related ecological differences and socioeconomic development would be associated with variation in IBD burden.

Methods

Data sources

IBD estimates for 1990–2023 were obtained from GBD 2023. IBD was defined according to the GBD cause hierarchy and primarily included Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis, corresponding mainly to ICD-10 codes K50–K52. We extracted estimates of incidence, prevalence, mortality, years lived with disability (YLDs), years of life lost (YLLs), and DALYs, together with age-standardized rates and 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs), from the Global Health Data Exchange results tool (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-results/). The forecast package was used for time-series modeling, tseries for stationarity testing, ggplot2 for visualization, dplyr for data management, and sf or terra for spatial processing. SDI, a composite measure of income per capita, educational attainment, and fertility, was used to characterize social and demographic development. GBD point estimates and 95% UIs were used to describe uncertainty in burden estimates.

Koppen–Geiger climate classification

We initially defined the tropical region geographically as land areas located between the Tropic of Cancer and the Tropic of Capricorn, approximately 23 degrees 26 min north to 23 degrees 26 min south. Countries and territories intersecting this latitude band were identified from global administrative boundary data. We then used the Koppen–Geiger climate classification to characterize climatic heterogeneity within the tropical belt. This system was selected because it is globally standardized, ecologically interpretable, and based on long-term monthly temperature and precipitation thresholds, making it appropriate for reproducible cross-country ecological comparisons.

We overlaid gridded Koppen–Geiger climate data with the geographic tropical belt and national administrative boundaries. For each country or territory, we calculated the proportion of land within the tropical belt classified as group A tropical climate. In the Koppen–Geiger system, group A climates include Af, Am, Aw, and As and are defined by monthly mean temperatures of at least 18 degrees C throughout the year. These climates are further classified by precipitation seasonality as tropical rainforest, tropical monsoon, and tropical savanna subtypes. The availability of updated high-resolution global Koppen–Geiger products for 1991–2020, together with associated confidence estimates, improves reproducibility compared with broad geographic labels or ad hoc regional categories.

EAPC analysis

To evaluate long-term trends in age-standardized IBD burden from 1990 to 2023, we calculated the EAPC for the 96 tropical countries and territories matched to GBD data. EAPCs were estimated separately for ASIR, ASPR, ASDR, and age-standardized DALY, YLL, and YLD rates. We fitted a linear regression model in which the natural logarithm of each age-standardized rate was regressed on calendar year: ln(ASR) = alpha + beta x year + epsilon. In this model, ASR denotes the age-standardized rate, alpha is the intercept, beta is the regression coefficient, and epsilon is the error term. The sign of beta indicates whether the age-standardized rate increased or decreased over time. Frontier analysis and decomposition analysis

To evaluate the relationship between IBD burden and sociodemographic development, we conducted frontier analysis using SDI and age-standardized incidence, prevalence, mortality, DALY, YLL, and YLD rates. Frontier analysis was interpreted as a benchmarking method rather than a causal model. At each SDI level, the frontier represented the estimated lower bound of age-standardized burden observed across countries and territories. The frontier gap was defined as the vertical distance between the observed burden and this lower bound. A larger frontier gap indicated greater potential for improvement relative to peers with similar development levels, but it should not be interpreted as a direct measure of health care quality alone. We used LOESS and local polynomial regression with smoothing spans of 0.3, 0.4, and 0.5 to generate smooth frontier curves for the nonlinear association between SDI and age-standardized rates. Robustness was assessed with 1000 bootstrap samples.

We also applied Das Gupta decomposition to partition changes in IBD burden between 1990 and 2023 into contributions from population growth, population aging, and epidemiological change. This approach separates demographic effects from changes in age-specific rates, allowing population expansion, age-structure shifts, and epidemiological change to be interpreted separately. Component contributions were also expressed as percentages of net change. Negative percentages suggest that a component offset the net increase, whereas percentages exceeding 100% can occur when positive contributions are partly counterbalanced by negative epidemiological change.

ARIMA forecasting

To forecast IBD burden in tropical regions for 2024–2050, we fitted ARIMA models to annual age-standardized burden estimates for both sexes combined from 1990 to 2023. ASIR, ASPR, ASDR, and age-standardized DALY, YLL, and YLD rates were modeled separately. Because the data were annual and nonseasonal, we considered nonseasonal ARIMA specifications. All series were log-transformed before model fitting to reduce scale differences and ensure positive predicted values. Stationarity was evaluated using first-difference plots, autocorrelation and partial autocorrelation functions, and unit-root tests when applicable. Candidate models were selected using auto.arima() in the R forecast package, and final models were chosen according to AICc and residual diagnostics. Residual adequacy was assessed using residual plots, residual autocorrelation, and the Ljung-Box test. Forecast accuracy was evaluated using RMSE, MAE, and MAPE in hold-out or rolling-origin validation. Forecasts for 2024–2050 were reported with 95% prediction intervals, which represent model-based forecast uncertainty rather than uncertainty in the underlying GBD estimates.

Statistical analysis

The unit of analysis was the tropical country or territory matched to GBD 2023. We conducted descriptive, trend, climate-stratified, decomposition, frontier, and forecasting analyses of IBD burden from 1990 to 2023. Numbers of incident cases, prevalent cases, deaths, YLDs, YLLs, and DALYs, as well as age-standardized rates, were reported with 95% UIs. All rates were expressed per 100,000 population. EAPC was used to assess long-term changes in ASIR, ASPR, ASDR, and age-standardized DALY, YLD, and YLL rates. Countries and territories were stratified by the proportion of Koppen–Geiger group A tropical climate area or by dominant climate type. Das Gupta decomposition partitioned changes in absolute burden into contributions from population growth, population aging, and epidemiological change. Frontier analysis assessed potential for improvement at different SDI levels, and ARIMA models forecast age-standardized burden for 2024–2050. All analyses and visualizations were performed using R version 4.5.3. A two-sided P value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Spatial distribution and long-term trends in disease burden

In 2023, age-standardized IBD burden varied markedly across the 96 tropical countries and territories matched to GBD data. Higher nonfatal burden was concentrated mainly in high-SDI tropical settings, whereas higher mortality-related burden clustered primarily in parts of sub-Saharan Africa, the Caribbean, and tropical Latin America. This pattern suggests a distinction between diagnostic or prevalence accumulation and premature mortality burden. ASIR, ASPR, and age-standardized YLD rate ranged from 0.22 (95% UI: 0.18–0.27) to 18.97 (95% UI: 16.43–22.04) per 100,000, 1.82 (95% UI: 1.54–2.16) to 216.7 (95% UI: 187.30–247.26) per 100,000, and 0.29 (95% UI: 0.19–0.44) to 31.8 (95% UI: 21.08–44.78) per 100,000, respectively. In contrast, ASDR, age-standardized DALY rate, and age-standardized YLL rate ranged from 0.05 (95% UI: 0.03–0.08) to 1.31 (95% UI: 0.76–2.10) per 100,000, 2.62 (95% UI: 1.98–3.32) to 58.3 (95% UI: 33.18–95.23) per 100,000, and 1.10 (95% UI: 0.57–1.93) to 55.9 (95% UI: 31.09–93.52) per 100,000, respectively. Overall, nonfatal and mortality-related IBD burdens did not fully overlap geographically, indicating that tropical countries and territories face distinct forms of disease pressure.

EAPC maps for 1990–2023 indicated that long-term changes in age-standardized IBD rates differed by indicator and by country or territory. EAPC ranges for incidence, prevalence, and YLD rates were − 0.44%(95% CI − 0.64–0.24%) to 2.59%(95% CI 2.07–3.13%), − 0.33%(95% CI − 0.54–0.12%) to 2.20%(95% CI 1.61–2.79%), and − 0.40%(95% CI − 0.61–0.20%) to 2.09%(95% CI 1.51–2.68%), respectively, with most countries showing stable or increasing trends. By contrast, EAPCs for mortality, DALYs, and YLLs ranged from − 3.76%(95% CI − 4.06–3.45%) to 3.54%(95% CI 2.62–4.47%), − 2.78%(95% CI − 3.14–2.42%) to 1.29%(95% CI 1.02–1.56%), and − 3.73%(95% CI − 4.08–3.39%) to 3.0%, respectively. Mortality and premature mortality-related burden therefore decreased in many countries and territories, although increases persisted in some settings. Taken together, tropical IBD showed a divergent pattern: nonfatal burden persisted or increased, whereas mortality-related burden improved in many areas.

Koppen–Geiger climate classification and tropical climatic heterogeneity

Overlaying national boundaries with the geographic tropical belt identified 98 candidate countries and territories intersecting the tropics. Of these, 70 were entirely within the geographic tropics, whereas 28 were only partly within the tropical belt. After incorporating 1991–2020 Koppen–Geiger climate data, we observed marked climatic heterogeneity within the geographic tropics. Among the 98 candidate countries and territories, the median proportion of group A tropical climate area was 77.50%, and the mean was 59.61%. Thus, geographic tropical location did not always correspond to tropical climate status.

Countries and territories were categorized by the proportion of group A tropical climate area. In total, 27 were entirely tropical climate, 20 were core tropical climate, 13 were predominantly tropical climate, 22 had partial tropical climate areas, and 16 were geographically located wholly or partly within the tropics but had no group A tropical climate area. By dominant climate group, group A tropical climate was most common (62 countries and territories), followed by group B arid climate (28), group C temperate climate (7), and group E polar climate (1).

At the subtype level, Aw tropical savanna climate was the most common dominant subtype (36 countries and territories), followed by BWh hot desert climate (19), Af tropical rainforest climate (16), and Am tropical monsoon climate (10). Cwa and BSh subtypes were each present in 6 countries and territories, BSk in 2, and BWk, Cfa, and ET in 1 each. Spatially, group A tropical climates were concentrated in the Amazon Basin, Congo Basin, West African coast, Caribbean, Southeast Asia, and parts of Oceania. Group B arid climates were distributed mainly across the Sahara-Sahel belt, the Horn of Africa, the Arabian Peninsula, and inland Australia. These findings suggest that latitude alone does not adequately represent climatic and ecological variation in tropical IBD analyses, whereas Koppen–Geiger classification provides a useful additional stratification tool.

Age and sex distribution

In 2023, IBD burden across the 96 tropical countries and territories differed substantially by age and sex. Absolute numbers of incident cases, prevalent cases, and YLDs were concentrated in middle-aged and older adults, whereas age-specific rates were highest in older age groups. Incident cases peaked at ages 40–44 years in both sexes, with 13,357 cases in males and 12,597 in females. Age-specific incidence rates peaked at ages 75–79 years, reaching 8.50 per 100,000 in males and 8.01 per 100,000 in females. Prevalent cases peaked at ages 50–54 years in both sexes, with 124,045 cases in males and 127,633 in females. Age-specific prevalence rates also peaked at ages 75–79 years, reaching 99.17 per 100,000 in males and 97.14 per 100,000 in females. YLDs showed a similar age distribution, with counts peaking at ages 50–54 years (18,626 in males and 19,893 in females) and rates peaking at ages 75–79 years, at approximately 13.70 per 100,000 in both sexes.

Deaths and premature mortality-related burden were more strongly concentrated in older populations. Deaths peaked at ages 75–79 years in males (1600 deaths) and at ages 80–84 years in females (1700 deaths). Age-specific death rates increased sharply with age and were highest among those aged 95 years or older, reaching 18.84 per 100,000 in males and 26.06 per 100,000 in females. YLL counts peaked at ages 60–64 years in males (31,567) and 70–74 years in females (26,555). YLL rates were highest among those aged 95 years or older, reaching 151.04 per 100,000 in males and 209.02 per 100,000 in females. DALY counts peaked at ages 60–64 years in males (47,351) and 40–44 years in females (44,258). DALY rates also peaked among those aged 95 years or older, reaching 162.12 per 100,000 in males and 221.12 per 100,000 in females. Overall, nonfatal IBD burden in tropical regions was concentrated mainly in middle age and early older adulthood, whereas deaths, YLLs, and DALYs shifted toward the oldest age groups.

Correlation between tropical climate proportion and IBD burden

Among the 96 tropical countries and territories matched to GBD data, the proportion of Koppen–Geiger group A tropical climate area in 2023 showed weak to moderate inverse correlations with several age-standardized IBD indicators. Tropical climate proportion was inversely correlated with ASIR (rho = − 0.279, P = 0.006) and ASPR (rho = − 0.318, P = 0.002). Age-standardized YLD rate also decreased as tropical climate proportion increased (rho = − 0.313, P = 0.002). These results suggest that countries and territories with a higher proportion of tropical climate area generally had lower incidence, prevalence, and nonfatal disability burden.

No significant association was identified between tropical climate proportion and mortality or premature mortality-related burden. ASDR was nearly uncorrelated with tropical climate proportion (rho = 0.005, P = 0.958), and age-standardized YLL rate also showed no significant association (rho = 0.057, P = 0.584). Age-standardized DALY rate was similarly not associated with tropical climate proportion (rho = − 0.060, P = 0.560). Overall, tropical climate proportion was inversely associated with incidence, prevalence, and YLD burden, but not with mortality, YLLs, or DALYs. Tropical climate status may therefore capture variation in nonfatal IBD burden more clearly than variation in mortality-related burden.

Association between SDI and IBD burden

Among the 96 tropical countries and territories matched to both GBD and SDI data, SDI showed divergent associations with age-standardized IBD burden in 2023. SDI was weakly positively associated with incidence, prevalence, and nonfatal disability burden: ASIR (rho = 0.248, P = 0.015), ASPR (rho = 0.270, P = 0.008), and age-standardized YLD rate (rho = 0.249, P = 0.015). Scatterplots showed higher incidence, prevalence, and YLD burden in high-SDI countries, particularly Australia and the United States.

By contrast, SDI was inversely correlated with mortality and premature mortality-related burden. SDI was negatively associated with ASDR (rho = − 0.310, P = 0.002), and the inverse association was stronger for age-standardized YLL rate (rho = − 0.372, P < 0.001). SDI was also weakly negatively associated with age-standardized DALY rate (rho = − 0.223, P = 0.029). Some low- and middle-SDI countries and territories had higher mortality, YLL, and DALY rates, whereas high-SDI settings generally had lower mortality-related burden, although high-burden outliers remained. Overall, IBD burden in tropical regions showed a differentiated relationship with sociodemographic development. Higher SDI was associated with greater incidence, prevalence, and YLD burden, but with lower mortality, YLL, and overall DALY burden.

Decomposition analysis of changes in absolute burden

Between 1990 and 2023, absolute IBD burden increased overall across the 96 tropical countries and territories (Fig. 7 and Supplementary Table S1). For both sexes combined, incident cases increased by 133,273, prevalent cases by 1,241,626, deaths by 13,040, DALYs by 435,473 person-years, YLLs by 248,230 person-years, and YLDs by 187,243 person-years. Sex-stratified analyses showed increases in all burden indicators in both males and females, with net increases generally slightly higher among females. In females, incident cases, prevalent cases, deaths, DALYs, YLLs, and YLDs increased by 67,023, 637,961, 6,804, 224,536 person-years, 126,566 person-years, and 97,970 person-years, respectively; the corresponding increases in males were 66,250, 603,664, 6236, 210,937 person-years, 121,664 person-years, and 89,272 person-years. This pattern suggests that rising service demand reflects population growth, sex-specific demographic structure, longer survival, and the accumulation of long-term prevalent cases.Fig. 7Decomposition analysis of changes in absolute IBD burden across 96 tropical countries and territories, 1990–2023

Decomposition analysis of changes in absolute IBD burden across 96 tropical countries and territories, 1990–2023

Decomposition analysis showed that population growth was a major contributor to increasing absolute burden. It contributed 76,208 additional incident cases, 728,499 prevalent cases, 353,710 DALYs, 242,384 YLLs, and 111,326 YLDs. Population aging also contributed positively to most indicators, particularly prevalent cases (499,232 cases), DALYs (221,165 person-years), and YLLs (147,718 person-years). As percentages of net change, population growth accounted for 58.7% of the increase in prevalent cases, 81.2% of the increase in DALYs, and 97.6% of the increase in YLLs. Epidemiological change offset 32.0% of the DALY increase and 57.2% of the YLL increase. Sex-stratified decomposition results showed broadly similar patterns across males and females, with detailed percentage contributions provided in Supplementary Table S1. Overall, the rise in absolute IBD burden in tropical regions was associated mainly with population growth and aging, while improvements in age-specific rates partly offset mortality and premature mortality-related burden. Thus, even when age-standardized mortality improves, case volume, follow-up demand, and chronic disability burden may continue to expand.

SDI frontier analysis

Frontier analysis showed a nonlinear relationship between SDI and age-standardized IBD burden across tropical countries and territories from 1990 to 2023 (Fig. 8). Most observations for incidence, prevalence, and YLD rates were concentrated at relatively low burden levels, but prominent high-burden outliers remained in the higher SDI range, particularly Australia and the United States. In 2023, Australia and the United States had large frontier gaps for incidence, prevalence, and YLD rates. In Australia, ASIR, ASPR, and age-standardized YLD rate were 18.97, 216.74, and 31.80 per 100,000, with corresponding frontier gaps of 18.80, 215.10, and 31.53 per 100,000. Mortality-related burden showed a different frontier pattern. In 2023, large frontier gaps in DALY, death, and YLL rates were observed mainly in selected low- and middle-SDI countries, although some high-SDI countries also had large gaps. Guinea-Bissau had the largest frontier gaps for DALY and YLL rates, with age-standardized DALY and YLL rates of 58.29 and 55.89 per 100,000 and corresponding frontier gaps of 53.06 and 52.85 per 100,000, respectively. For mortality, Guinea-Bissau, the United States, Guyana, and Sierra Leone were among the countries with large frontier gaps, with ASDRs of 1.31, 1.16, 1.10, and 1.08 per 100,000, respectively. For DALYs, the United States, Australia, Sierra Leone, Guyana, and The Gambia also showed large gaps, indicating high overall burden relative to SDI level. The frontier gap should therefore not be interpreted as a simple ranking of economic development. It likely reflects a combination of diagnostic timeliness, access to long-term treatment, complication control, mortality-registration quality, and continuity of care.

Temporal changes from 1990 to 2023 differed by burden dimension. In 2023, ASIR, ASPR, and age-standardized YLD rate were higher than in 1990 in 86, 85, and 83 of the 96 countries and territories matched to GBD data, respectively. By contrast, ASDR, age-standardized YLL rate, and age-standardized DALY rate declined in 65, 74, and 69 countries and territories, respectively. Overall, frontier analysis indicated that potential for improvement in tropical IBD differs by burden dimension. Large gaps in incidence, prevalence, and YLD burden were concentrated mainly in selected middle- and high-SDI or high-SDI countries, reflecting increased diagnosis, longer survival, and long-term disability-management needs. Large gaps in mortality, YLL, and DALY burden occurred in both low-SDI and high-SDI countries, suggesting that reducing premature mortality and severe outcomes requires interventions tailored to local health system gaps.

Discussion

Principal findings

This study first shows that IBD burden in tropical regions is spatially heterogeneous. Broad labels such as low latitude or developing-country status do not adequately explain this heterogeneity. High-SDI tropical countries, and countries with advanced urbanization or mature health care systems, increasingly show a pattern similar to that observed in Western settings: higher incidence and prevalence, but lower mortality and premature mortality burden. Conversely, some low-SDI countries show low reported incidence alongside relatively high mortality or DALY burden. This polarization suggests that IBD in the tropics is not simply rare; rather, its visibility is shaped by diagnostic capacity, registration systems, infectious disease background, and treatment access. Crude rates or single-year estimates alone may therefore underestimate true disease pressure in resource-limited settings.

Beyond spatial heterogeneity, demographic forces and health care transition jointly determine total burden. Many tropical countries continue to experience rapid population growth, and some are also entering a period of population aging. Consequently, absolute case numbers, long-term follow-up needs, and demand for health services may increase even when age-standardized rates remain stable. Our decomposition analysis showed that population growth and aging were the main contributors to the rising absolute burden, whereas epidemiological change partly offset deaths, DALYs, and YLLs. This pattern is consistent with possible improvements in treatment access, earlier diagnosis, or mortality control. Without separating these drivers, policy makers may incorrectly attribute all increases in burden to worsening risk factors or assume that stable age-standardized rates justify delaying service expansion. For a chronic condition such as IBD, the accumulation of prevalent cases is itself a major source of health system pressure.

Climate stratification provides a more informative framework for heterogeneity within the tropics than latitude alone. Latitude captures solar angle and broad geographic position, but it does not adequately represent precipitation, humidity, vegetation, microbial exposure, air quality, elevation, or extreme-temperature events. These factors are unlikely to affect IBD through a single linear pathway. Instead, they may be linked indirectly to host immune homeostasis through the gut microbiome, vitamin D status, diet, outdoor activity, infection exposure, and hygiene-related behavior. The inclusion of Koppen–Geiger climate classification was therefore intended to convert environmental exposure from a vague background descriptor into a stratifiable, comparable, and interpretable public health variable.

These multidimensional patterns have implications for clinical practice and health policy planning. ARIMA forecasts suggested that age-standardized mortality, YLL, and DALY rates may continue to decline, whereas incidence, prevalence, and YLD rates are unlikely to decrease to the same extent. IBD management in tropical regions may therefore shift from a predominant focus on preventing death and premature mortality toward long-term control of relapse, disability, and medical costs. In high-incidence, low-mortality settings, planning may emphasize primary prevention, relapse monitoring, long-term complication control, and cost management. In low-reporting, high-mortality settings, priorities may include primary-care recognition, differential diagnosis, endoscopic and pathological capacity, and access to essential medications. These contexts require different resource packages: the first resembles a chronic disease-management challenge, whereas the second reflects a need to strengthen diagnostic and treatment infrastructure.

Spatial differences in burden should not be interpreted as a simple ranking of national health care performance. IBD burden reflects exposure level, population structure, diagnostic access, treatment capacity, and mortality-registration quality. Higher prevalence may indicate higher underlying risk, stronger diagnostic capacity, or longer survival, whereas lower reported incidence may conceal a large undiagnosed population. Interpretation should therefore avoid performance judgments based solely on numerical values. More relevant questions are whether a region has adequate endoscopy and pathology services, whether long-term treatment is affordable, and whether patients with chronic diarrhea can be referred promptly from infectious disease pathways to IBD diagnostic pathways. Incorporating these questions gives burden research greater public health relevance.

Epidemiological trends and socioeconomic drivers

The increase in IBD incidence in tropical regions likely reflects the delayed emergence of epidemiological transition at low latitudes. Reviews and umbrella reviews of environmental risk factors have shown that diet, smoking, antibiotics, infection exposure, urbanization, and changes in sanitation can jointly influence IBD risk [24–26]. In rapidly developing Asian countries and other emerging economies, modern lifestyle transitions coexist with traditional infectious exposures, creating a dual risk spectrum. Tropical regions may therefore not simply repeat the historical trajectory of Western countries. Instead, dietary Westernization, food industrialization, increased antibiotic use, and improved diagnostic capacity may occur over a compressed time scale.

Dietary transition is among the most modifiable risk dimensions. Ultra-processed foods, sugar-sweetened beverages, animal fat, and low-fiber diets may increase IBD risk by altering gut microbial composition, reducing beneficial metabolites, increasing intestinal barrier permeability, and promoting low-grade inflammation [27–29]. In many tropical cities, traditional diets based on grains, legumes, vegetables, fruits, and fermented foods are being replaced by fast food, processed meats, refined carbohydrates, and sugary beverages. This transition often begins during childhood and adolescence, making it relevant to early-onset IBD. Public health strategies may therefore extend beyond dietary management after diagnosis to include schools, communities, and food-policy settings.

Antibiotic exposure and early-life factors represent another plausible pathway. Broad-spectrum antibiotics in childhood, cesarean delivery, lower breastfeeding rates, and overly hygienic environments may disrupt early gut microbiota development, impair immune tolerance, and increase susceptibility to immune-mediated intestinal inflammation in genetically predisposed individuals [30–32]. In tropical regions, this issue is especially complex. Inappropriate antibiotic use may damage the microbiome, but the high burden of infectious disease also creates legitimate clinical need for antimicrobial treatment. Locally adapted antimicrobial stewardship may reduce unnecessary antibiotic use for viral diarrhea and nonspecific abdominal pain while preserving timely treatment for true bacterial infection.

Age and sex patterns also require careful interpretation. The incidence peak among younger adults may reflect exposure of the maturing immune system to modern diets, urban stress, antibiotics, and pollution, whereas the older-age peak may be related to immunosenescence, chronic low-grade inflammation, comorbid medication use, and reduced gut microbial diversity [33]. Sex differences include biological and social components. Estrogen, mucosal barrier regulation, and immune–response differences may influence disease onset and course in females, whereas higher smoking prevalence, occupational exposures, delayed care-seeking, and poorer adherence may contribute to worse mortality or complication outcomes in males [34, 35]. In tropical countries, health care access, household decision-making power, and occupational structure may further amplify sex differences. Sex-stratified reporting is therefore a useful entry point for identifying potentially modifiable inequalities.

The relationship between SDI and IBD burden is not simply positive; it is more consistent with a nonlinear epidemiological transition. In low-SDI settings, incomplete registration and diagnostic delay may suppress apparent incidence while increasing severe complications and mortality risk. In middle- and high-SDI settings, urbanization, dietary change, and improved diagnostic capacity may jointly drive rapid increases in incidence. In high-SDI settings, standardized treatment and long-term management reduce mortality, but accumulated prevalence and medical costs continue to rise. This nonlinear relationship helps explain the apparent paradox of increasing incidence but declining mortality in some settings. It does not necessarily suggest that the disease is becoming milder; rather, it may reflect earlier diagnosis, more effective treatment, and longer survival. SDI-stratified findings should therefore be translated into differentiated policies: high-SDI tropical settings may focus on relapse control and cost containment, whereas low-SDI tropical settings should prioritize diagnosis and essential treatment.

Frontier analysis is useful because it identifies the gap between observed burden and the burden attainable at a given level of development. If a country has higher mortality or DALY rates than countries with similar SDI, this pattern suggests room for health system improvement. Such gaps may arise from delayed diagnosis, insufficient endoscopy and pathology, unstable drug supply, excessive treatment costs, weak specialist referral, or poor differentiation from infectious enteropathies. For middle- and high-SDI countries with large incidence, prevalence, or YLD frontier gaps, policy planning may prioritize relapse reduction, disability prevention, and cost control. For low- and middle-SDI countries with large mortality, YLL, or DALY gaps, planning may emphasize early recognition, differentiation from infectious bowel diseases, continuous essential-medication supply, and severe-case referral systems. Advanced therapies can produce population-level benefit only when these foundational systems are stable.

Diagnostic visibility should be considered separately when interpreting SDI-stratified results. Confirmation of IBD generally requires clinical symptoms, endoscopic findings, histopathology, and exclusion of infection, but these components are often incomplete in resource-limited settings. Without colonoscopy, patients may remain classified as having chronic diarrhea or nonspecific enteritis. Without pathology, the distinction between CD, intestinal tuberculosis, ischemic colitis, and post-infectious enteritis may remain uncertain. Without longitudinal follow-up, recurrence, steroid dependence, and surgical outcomes may not be registered. Lower incidence in low-SDI regions should therefore be interpreted cautiously: it may represent truly lower risk, but it may also reflect underdiagnosis.

Climatic heterogeneity: moving beyond latitude

One of the main contributions of this study is the explicit recognition that the geographic tropics are not synonymous with the climatic tropics. Koppen–Geiger classification defines climate types according to temperature and precipitation patterns, allowing ecological differences within low-latitude regions to be described explicitly rather than merging all tropical countries into a single broad category [21, 22]. Within the same tropical belt, rainforest, monsoon, savanna, arid, and plateau regions may differ in microbial exposure, food sources, outdoor activity, water safety, air pollution, and infectious disease spectra. The following discussion of biodiversity, microbiome, altitude, pollution, and climate change should therefore be interpreted as a set of literature-informed biological hypotheses rather than findings directly generated by the present ecological analysis.

In tropical rainforest and monsoon climates, high humidity, high precipitation, vegetation cover, and environmental biodiversity may provide richer natural microbial exposures. According to the biodiversity hypothesis, early-life exposure to diverse microorganisms may help promote immune tolerance and regulatory capacity, potentially reducing the risk of some immune-mediated diseases [36]. This does not imply that IBD is absent in rainforest or monsoon regions. Rather, it suggests that their risk structure may differ from that of highly urbanized, low-biodiversity, indoor-oriented environments. As these regions undergo rapid urbanization, deforestation, dietary industrialization, and increasing antibiotic use, microbiome-related protective hypotheses should be tested directly in cohort and exposure studies.

In tropical savanna and arid climates, intense sunlight does not necessarily imply low risk. Extreme heat can reduce outdoor activity and increase sedentary indoor time. Dust, air pollution, and urban heat-island effects may increase respiratory and systemic inflammatory burden, while challenges in water safety and food preservation may alter enteric infection exposure. Vitamin D deficiency has been consistently associated with IBD risk and disease activity, and temperature variation may also be related to global IBD incidence patterns [37, 38]. Studies of air pollution further suggest that particulate and gaseous pollutants may modify IBD risk through oxidative stress, barrier injury, and immune activation [39]. In this ecological study, these pathways should be interpreted as hypotheses alongside diagnostic access, socioeconomic development, and health care capacity.

Plateau and mountainous tropical regions may involve another set of plausible mechanisms. Higher altitude can be associated with hypoxic exposure, larger temperature variation, ultraviolet and cloud-cover differences, and changes in agricultural structure, food sources, and health care accessibility. Hypoxia-inducible factor pathways participate in intestinal epithelial barrier maintenance, mucosal repair, and inflammatory regulation, suggesting a possible link between high-altitude environments and IBD susceptibility or disease course [40]. At the same time, difficult transportation and concentration of specialist resources in urban centers may prolong the interval from symptom onset to diagnosis. Future studies should therefore evaluate climate classification together with health service geography, rather than treating either as a direct causal explanation.

Air pollution and climate change may also inform long-term monitoring frameworks for tropical IBD. Previous studies have suggested that pollution exposure may be associated with IBD onset or exacerbation, although causal pathways require further clarification [41]. In tropical regions, extreme heat, flooding, drought, and wildfires may affect water safety, food supply, infectious disease spectra, and migration, thereby indirectly influencing immune-mediated intestinal diseases. Future research should treat climate as a dynamic exposure system rather than a static background variable by incorporating temperature anomalies, precipitation extremes, air quality, and land-use change. This approach may help explain why countries classified as tropical can nevertheless show different rates of growth, severity, and health care pressure for IBD.

Seasonality also warrants further study. Although many tropical regions have high temperatures throughout the year, rainfall and humidity often vary markedly between dry and wet seasons. These seasonal changes may influence drinking water sources, food preservation, enteric infection exposure, air pollution concentrations, and outdoor activity. Such exposures may not immediately alter annual incidence, but they may affect symptom relapse, health care visits, hospitalization risk, and fecal inflammatory markers. Linking monthly or quarterly climate data with outpatient visits, hospitalizations, medication use, and endoscopic activity would help move research from differences between climate zones to exposure fluctuations within the same region. This approach could make climate-adaptive interventions more precise, including stronger pollution and hydration management during dry seasons and enhanced enteric infection differentiation and drinking water safety monitoring during wet seasons.

Public health implications

From a system-design perspective, our findings support the development of regional IBD registries and monitoring networks in tropical regions, particularly where diagnostic visibility remains limited. The most urgent need in low-SDI countries may not be immediate access to complex molecular phenotyping, but establishment of basic case definitions, diagnostic pathways, endoscopy records, pathological confirmation, treatment outcome tracking, and cause of death registration. Without stable registration systems, low incidence may simply reflect invisibility, and high mortality cannot be reliably attributed to diagnostic delay, medication inaccessibility, or infectious complications. Registries should include secondary hospitals and primary referral points so that they do not represent only patients treated in major urban specialist centers.

Alongside basic surveillance, differentiating IBD from tropical infectious enteropathies should be a central component of primary care training. In regions with high prevalence of intestinal tuberculosis, amebiasis, parasitic infections, and bacterial enteritis, patients with chronic diarrhea, hematochezia, abdominal pain, weight loss, and anemia are often treated repeatedly with empirical antimicrobials. This practice may delay IBD diagnosis and can also lead to inappropriate immunosuppression if infection has not been excluded. Diagnostic pathways in tropical regions should therefore incorporate infection screening, endoscopic assessment, pathological confirmation, and medication safety monitoring, rather than mechanically applying protocols developed in countries with low infectious burden.

Prevention and management strategies may also be tailored to climatic subregions. Arid and high-temperature regions may prioritize drinking-water safety, air-pollution exposure, vitamin D status, dietary fiber intake, and guidance on outdoor activity. Rainforest and monsoon regions may strengthen dual-pathway assessment for infection and inflammation among patients with chronic intestinal symptoms. Plateau and mountainous regions may address transportation, telemedicine, and specialist referral while also examining biological exposure hypotheses. Such climate-adaptive strategies are more specific than general calls to strengthen IBD prevention and control and may be more readily translated into local public health programs.

Medication and diagnostic decentralization may be implemented in a sequence consistent with local resource constraints. In low-SDI settings, near-term priorities may include basic colonoscopy, pathology services, fecal calprotectin or other inflammatory screening tools, standardized corticosteroid use, first-line immunomodulators, nutritional support, and referral mechanisms for severe disease [42–44]. On this foundation, access to biologics and newer small-molecule therapies can be expanded gradually, and stratified treatment pathways can be developed with reference to international UC and CD guidelines [45, 46]. For resource-limited settings, the key issue is not having every available drug, but ensuring that the right patients receive affordable, sustainable, and monitorable treatment at the right time.

From a long-term population-health perspective, early-life microbiome protection may be considered within a primary prevention framework for IBD, although the present ecological analysis could not evaluate these interventions directly. Breastfeeding, appropriate antibiotic use, improved school diets, reduced intake of ultra-processed foods, maintenance of outdoor activity, and contact with natural environments may all represent long-term prevention directions in rapidly urbanizing tropical regions. Psychological stress and gut-brain interactions also influence symptom burden, quality of life, and relapse risk and may be incorporated into broader public health strategies [47]. Even if their specific effect on IBD requires further validation, these measures may also improve obesity, metabolic disease, enteric infection, and mental health outcomes.

Climate change could be considered within a One Health framework for tropical IBD as a policy implication rather than a direct conclusion from the present data. Tropical regions are particularly vulnerable to extreme climate events, food-system disruption, flooding, and water contamination, all of which may affect intestinal disease through infection exposure, dietary change, migration, and interruption of health services. Future IBD monitoring could be integrated with meteorological data, air quality, water safety, food prices, and land-use change to develop cross-sector early-warning systems. Such systems may help health services identify areas at risk of rising burden before increases in hospitalization, surgery, or mortality become apparent.

Finally, public health strategies require evaluable indicators. Registries may be assessed by case completeness, median diagnostic delay, and the proportion of cases with pathological confirmation. Primary-care training may be evaluated by referral rates among patients with chronic diarrhea and adherence to differential diagnostic pathways for intestinal tuberculosis and IBD. Medication decentralization may be assessed by steroid-dependence rates, immunomodulator availability, treatment interruption, and disease-related catastrophic expenditure. Climate-adaptive interventions may monitor vitamin D deficiency, dietary fiber intake, air pollution exposure, seasonal hospitalization peaks, and clinic volume after extreme climate events. These indicators could translate the epidemiological findings of this study into actionable and accountable policy tools while generating real-world evidence for future research.

Strengths and limitations

The main strength of this study is the breadth and systematic structure of the data. GBD 2023 provides comparable estimates across countries, years, age groups, and sexes, enabling systematic comparison in tropical low-SDI countries where primary registration data are often sparse. Building on this foundation, we introduced a multidimensional analytical framework that combined geographic tropical status, Koppen–Geiger climate classification, and SDI stratification. This approach avoided treating tropical regions as a homogeneous entity and improved the identification of high-risk climate-development subregions. The study also went beyond describing current status and trends by using decomposition analysis to quantify the relative contributions of population growth, aging, and epidemiological change and by applying ARIMA models to generate future trend scenarios. This methodological combination is well suited to policy-relevant questions about why burden changes, how it may evolve, and where resources may be directed.

Several limitations should also be considered. First, GBD estimates depend on national registries, literature sources, hospital and outpatient data, and statistical models. In some tropical low-SDI countries, limited primary data may lead to underestimation of incidence or wide uncertainty intervals, whereas improved diagnostic visibility in higher-SDI settings may increase reported incidence and prevalence. Second, stable subtype-specific burden estimates for Crohn’s disease and ulcerative colitis were not available across all countries in GBD, preventing consistent comparisons of these subtypes across climate zones and SDI strata. Third, the ecological design and absence of patient-level data did not permit individual-level causal inference and prevented assessment of treatment access, disease phenotype, medication exposure, diet, microbiome composition, and residential environmental exposure. Fourth, underdiagnosis and overdiagnosis may both occur in settings where chronic infectious enteropathies, intestinal tuberculosis, and limited pathology capacity complicate case ascertainment.

Climate classification also has scale-related limitations. The present study used country- or territory-level climate categorization, which may obscure within-country microclimatic differences, urban–rural variation, elevation gradients, land-use change, and individual-level exposure heterogeneity. For example, in a country spanning rainforest, savanna, and plateau environments, a national average climate label may not reflect the residential environment of patients. Future studies integrating gridded population distribution, patient geolocation, and high-resolution climate data would be better positioned to identify small-scale risks. In addition, ARIMA models extrapolate from historical trends and cannot fully incorporate nonlinear shocks caused by future medications, insurance policies, conflict, migration, extreme climate events, or major public health emergencies. Forecasts should therefore be interpreted as trend scenarios rather than deterministic predictions.

With respect to causal inference, ecological analyses cannot establish whether climate-related environmental factors cause differences in IBD burden. Associations between national climate types and IBD burden may be confounded by diet, income, diagnostic capacity, genetic structure, health insurance, migration, and cultural behavior. Accordingly, the mechanistic interpretations presented here should be regarded as biologically plausible, literature-informed hypotheses rather than causal conclusions. Future work should integrate GBD burden studies with cohort studies, case–control studies, microbiome research, environmental exposure monitoring, and health services research to test the proposed climate–microbiome–SDI framework.

The supporting literature is also geographically uneven. High-quality evidence on environmental risk factors, genetic susceptibility, and treatment strategies for IBD is derived mainly from Europe, North America, and parts of East Asia, whereas evidence remains limited for tropical Africa, Pacific island countries, the Caribbean, and parts of Latin America. Consequently, some mechanistic interpretations in this study necessarily extrapolate evidence from high-income or nontropical contexts to tropical settings. To reduce this risk, we emphasized directionality and testability rather than treating external evidence as direct local causal proof. Establishing tropical multicenter IBD cohorts with synchronized collection of climate, diet, antibiotic exposure, infection, microbiome, and treatment access data would substantially improve evidence quality in this field.

Conclusion

In summary, tropical IBD burden varies across climatic and sociodemographic contexts. Climate-related environmental factors may contribute to geographic differences, but this ecological analysis does not permit causal inference. In high-incidence, low-mortality settings, policy planning may focus on relapse monitoring, long-term complication control, and cost management. In low-diagnosis, high-mortality settings, priorities may include primary-care recognition, endoscopic and pathological capacity, essential medications, and referral systems. Future research that integrates high-resolution climate data, patient-level clinical information, and longitudinal cohorts will help move the field from descriptive burden assessment toward mechanistic validation and precision intervention.
