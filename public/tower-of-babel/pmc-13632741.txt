Intramolecular charge redistribution of phosphate anions enabling ultrahigh-voltage lithium metal battery of 600 Wh kg−1
Authors: Shenghang Zhang, Fanteng Meng, Lixuan Yang, Hongquan Liu, Xiaofan Du, Xiangchun Zhuang, Bin Xie, Shitao Wang, Lixin Qiao, Jun Ma, Shanmu Dong, Chung-Kai Chang, Yu-Chun Chuang, Chang-Yang Kuo, Chien-Te Chen, Zhiwei Hu, Zili Cui, Gaojie Xu, Guanglei Cui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The advancement of ultrahigh-voltage lithium metal batteries (LMBs) is hindered by the lack of efficient guidelines for electrolyte material development, with current approaches still largely relying on inefficient trial-and-error methods. Herein, we report a high-performance additive design and screening strategy. A library of phosphorus-centered candidates (containing 15,200 molecules) is constructed by simultaneously introducing electron-withdrawing and electron-donating groups to induce intramolecular charge redistribution and tune the electrochemical properties of the molecules. Through unsupervised machine learning–assisted screening, lithium diperfluoropinacolatophosphate (LiDPFP) is identified as the optimal candidate. LiDPFP features localized electron-deficient and electron-rich regions, which tune interfacial reactivity and enhance lithium ion (Li+) coordination to promote contact ion pairs. This unique structure leads to the formation of a phosphorus-oxygen polymer-rich cathode electrolyte interphase and a Li2O (lithium oxide)–rich solid electrolyte interphase, effectively suppressing cathode degradation and lithium dendrite growth. Consequently, the as-assembled NCM811||Li cells exhibit stable cycling at 4.8 volts over a wide temperature range from −40° to 80°C. A practical 4.7-volt pouch cell with the energy density of 628 Wh kg−1 (watt-hours per kilogram) exhibits an 80% capacity retention rate after 112 cycles. This work combines machine learning with molecular design, exhibiting a paradigm for practical ultrahigh-energy-density LMBs.

INTRODUCTION

Tackling urgent environmental challenges necessitates substantial innovations in battery technology to accelerate the transition to a low-carbon society (1, 2). Lithium metal batteries (LMBs), coupling layered high-nickel transition metal (TM) oxide cathodes (e.g., LiNixCoyMnzO2, NCM, x ≥ 0.8) with high-capacity lithium metal anodes (3860 mA·hour g−1), represent a promising next-generation energy storage system, capable of achieving gravimetric energy densities exceeding 500 watt-hours (Wh) kg−1 (3, 4). Increasing the charge cutoff voltage of NCM cathodes from the standard 4.3 V to ultrahigh voltages (>4.6 V versus Li+/Li) can further boost the energy density of LMBs by 15 to 35% (5–7). However, unlocking this potential bottleneck is hampered by severe interfacial instability of the cathode (8, 9). Under ultrahigh-voltage conditions, high-nickel cathodes suffer from TM dissolution (10), microcracking (11, 12), and oxygen loss (13, 14). Furthermore, the aggressive cross-talk of cathode oxidation–derived intermediates significantly exacerbates the degradation of the highly reactive Li anode (15–17). These challenges are magnified in practical pouch cells operating under stringent conditions of high cell capacity (18, 19), low negative-to-positive (N/P < 2) ratios (20, 21), and lean electrolyte (E/C < 2 g A·hour−1) (22). Thus, achieving long-cycle-life high-energy-density LMBs at ultrahigh voltages becomes critically challenging (23).

Electrolyte engineering has proved to be very successful in the cycle life improvement of ultrahigh-voltage LMBs (24, 25). Strategies such as localized high-concentration electrolytes (24, 26), novel solvents (5, 27), and polymer electrolytes (28) have been demonstrated to be promising. However, their widespread implementation is often hampered by huge hurdles regarding prohibitive cost, high viscosity, or poor processability when scaled to practical conditions (29). In contrast, functional sacrificial additives (6) offer a more efficient and cost-effective alternative. Among them, lithium difluorophosphate (LiDFP) stands out as an excellent ultrahigh-voltage additive (6). Even at trace concentrations, LiDFP effectively mitigates cathode interfacial degradation by deriving protective species rich in LiF and LixPOy at the cathode electrolyte interphase (CEI) layer. Despite these advantages, LiDFP is inherently limited by its low solubility and an inability to simultaneously provide adequate protection for the lithium metal anode. The ideal additive requires both a high highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and a low lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) to ensure preferential reactivity at both electrode surfaces (30, 31), whereas strong lithium ion coordination ability (32) is essential for promoting the formation of contact ion pairs (CIPs), coupled with the appropriate dipole moment (32, 33) and polarizability (34) features to drive interfacial enrichment. Customized phosphorus-based salt additives exhibit immense potential for application in ultrahigh-voltage LMBs. However, identifying compounds with precise electronic configurations from the vast chemical space of organophosphorus molecules using traditional trial-and-error approaches is akin to finding a needle in a haystack (35, 36).

Herein, to overcome this obstacle, we report a systematic design and screening strategy for high-performance phosphorus-based salt additives. The specific combination of electron-withdrawing groups (EWGs; e.g., -F, -CF3, and -CN) and electron-donating groups (EDGs; e.g., -O− and -CH3) within the molecular architecture can induce targeted intramolecular charge redistribution, thereby tailoring the electrochemical properties of the molecules. Guided by this principle, we select 14,779 phosphorus-centered molecules from open-source chemical repositories (e.g., PubChem) and supplement them with 421 proprietary structures designed in-house, ultimately constructing a comprehensive library comprising 15,200 phosphorus-centered candidates. Next, an unsupervised machine learning (ML) approach (K-means clustering) is used to accelerate the screening process, effectively narrowing the scope of potentially viable candidates. To quantitatively assess the shortlisted candidates, we further developed a comprehensive feature-scoring system that assigns tailored weights to key structural descriptors (37). Ultimately, this workflow successfully identifies lithium diperfluoropinacolatophosphate (LiDPFP) as the optimal candidate. As predicted by the screening criteria, LiDPFP exhibits a narrowed HOMO-LUMO gap, a unique charge distribution, and excellent coordination ability with Li+. Experimental validation confirms that LiDPFP functions as a powerful dual-purpose additive. On the cathode side, it undergoes preferential electropolymerization to form a robust, P-O polymer–rich CEI that suppresses TM dissolution, oxygen release, and solvent oxidation decomposition. On the anode side, it guides the formation of a Li2O-enriched inorganic solid electrolyte interphase (SEI), effectively inhibiting dendrite growth and mitigating cathode cross-talk. Excellent CEI and SEI layers can prevent direct contact between the electrolyte and the active electrode materials, thereby conferring superior ultrahigh-voltage tolerance to the as-assembled LMBs. Consequently, the as-assembled NCM811||Li cells exhibit stable cycling at 4.8 V over a wide temperature range from −40° to 80°C. The homemade industrial-class 4.7-V NCM811||Li pouch cells with an energy density of 628 Wh kg−1 retain 80% of its capacity after 112 cycles. This study demonstrates that integrating ML-guided screening with rational molecular design provides a powerful paradigm for developing ultrahigh-energy-density LMBs.

RESULTS

ML-guided molecular design and screening workflow

To develop a phosphorus-centered additive capable of simultaneously stabilizing both the cathode and anode interfaces under ultrahigh-voltage conditions, we establish a hierarchical design and screening workflow (Fig. 1A). The protocol proceeds through three sequential stages: rational molecular construction, unsupervised ML-assisted screening, and comprehensive scoring. To endow a single molecule with appropriate dual interfacial reactivity, EWGs (e.g., -F, -CF3, and -CN) and EDGs (e.g., -O− and -CH3) are introduced into the same phosphorus-centered framework to induce intramolecular charge redistribution. This induces spatially separated electron-rich and electron-deficient regions, enabling simultaneous modulation of reactivity and coordination ability. By changing the type and position of functional groups, a chemically diverse library of 15,200 lithium salt additives (14,779 from the public database and 421 from the self-built database) is constructed [Fig. 1A (1)]. First, the candidates are selected through a rigorous prescreening based on functional group composition, such as functional density (≥0.30), atom number (≤55), and molecular weight (MW; 60 to 1000 Da), which narrowed the scope to a final library of 343 molecules (figs. S1 to S6). Next, unsupervised ML of K-means clustering [Fig. 1A (2)] is applied to classify these molecules by electronic properties, thereby further refining the target space. In the final stage [Fig. 1A (3)], the remaining candidates are subjected to a composite scoring scheme, enabling quantitative ranking and identification of the optimal structure.

The rational design of ideal high-voltage additives requires balancing complex parameter requirements. Specifically, a high HOMO energy level and a low LUMO energy level (30, 31) are necessary to facilitate controlled interfacial decomposition at both anode and cathode surfaces, whereas strong lithium ion coordination ability (32) is essential for promoting the formation of CIPs. Furthermore, high polarizability (34) and a large dipole moment (33) are desirable to drive additive enrichment at the electrode surfaces. However, introducing an excessive number of physicochemical parameters into unsupervised ML models frequently generates high-dimensional noise that obscures meaningful information. To address this issue, we performed density functional theory (DFT) calculations on 150 randomly chosen phosphorus-based salts (figs. S7 to S9). The Spearman rank correlation coefficient (ρ) (36) is used to identify monotonic relationships within the dataset. This preliminary analysis showed that both the HOMO energy levels (ρ = 0.77108; fig. S7) and the Li+ binding energies (ρ = 0.80095; fig. S8) correlate monotonically with the electrostatic potential minimum (ESPmin). Meanwhile, the LUMO energy levels (ρ = 0.6847; fig. S9) show a monotonic correlation with the electrostatic potential maximum (ESPmax). Consequently, the feature space of K-means clustering is selected to include only ESPmin, ESPmax, and polarizability. K-means clustering subsequently categorizes the library into distinct families, using the silhouette score to establish an optimal cluster number of K = 5 (Fig. 1B, fig. S10, and table S1). Cluster 2 is selected as the target for its high polarizability and wide electrostatic range (Fig. 1C). Then, to select the optimal structure from Cluster 2, a weighted scoring function is applied to balance theoretical properties with synthetic feasibility (Fig. 1D). By integrating electronic descriptors and synthesis difficulty, this metric identifies molecule 334 (Top 1, LiDPFP) as the top candidates due to its combined excellent film-forming ability and low synthesis difficulty.

Following the ML-assisted screening workflow, an optimal lithium salt additive is identified (Top 1, LiDPFP). To validate the screening criteria, representative lithium salts with diverse electronic characteristics are selected for comparison, including lithium hexafluorophosphate (LiPF6), LiDFP (LiPO2F2), lithium trifluorotris(trifluoromethyl)phosphate [LiTFTTP, LiPF3(CF3)3], lithium perfluoropinacolate phosphate (LiPFP), and LiDPFP (Fig. 2A). Commercial LiPF6 serves as a reference due to its broad stability window. DFT calculations reveal that PF6− exhibits a uniform ESP, low HOMO, high LUMO, low dipole moment, and low polarizability (Fig. 2, A and B, and figs. S11 and S12). These features ensure bulk stability but limit its interfacial film formation ability. To overcome this, EWGs (-CF3) and EDGs (-O−) are introduced into the PF6− framework to induce charge redistribution. In DFP−, -O− groups create electron-rich regions, raising the HOMO energy level (Fig. 2B). In contrast, -CF3 groups in TFTTP− generate electron-deficient regions, lowering the LUMO energy level (Fig. 2B). However, single-function modifications cannot simultaneously balance protection at both electrodes. Consequently, cointroducing -CF3 and -O− groups in DPFP− and PFP− significantly narrows HOMO-LUMO gaps, indicating enhanced film-forming capability on both electrodes. Beyond orbital modulation, lone pair–containing -O− groups enable DFP− and PFP− to adopt bidentate coordination with Li+, increasing binding energies (Fig. 2C and fig. S11, TFTTP−: −1.03 eV; PF6−: −0.99 eV; PFP−: −1.21 eV; DFP−: −1.17 eV). Notably, combined -F and O─P═O groups in DPFP− allow a tridentate geometry, enhancing binding energy to −1.41 eV (Fig. 2C and fig. S11). Furthermore, the introduction of multiple EWGs and increased molecular volume results in progressively larger dipole moments and polarizabilities, favoring field-driven anion migration toward the electrode interface and preferential interfacial decomposition (fig. S12). These results demonstrate LiDPFP as the most promising dual-function film-forming additive for ultrahigh-voltage LMBs among the candidates evaluated above.

The target lithium salt, LiDPFP, is synthesized via a multistep route (fig. S13). The molecular structure of LiDPFP is confirmed by high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS), which exhibits a prominent peak at mass/charge ratio (m/z) = 710.914 corresponding to the DPFP− anion (fig. S14). Furthermore, 19F nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) (fig. S15A) and 31P NMR (fig. S15B) spectra verify the high purity of the as-synthesized LiDPFP. Next, the baseline electrolyte and the LiDPFP-containing modified electrolyte are prepared for comparison. The baseline electrolyte (LiPF6-E) is 1.0 M LiPF6 in 2,2,2-trifluoroethyl acetate (TFA)/fluoroethylene carbonate (FEC) (3:1, v/v). The modified electrolyte (LiDPFP-E) is LiPF6-E with 1 wt % LiDPFP. LiDPFP addition has a negligible effect on ionic conductivity (7.93 versus 7.34 mS cm−1; fig. S16) and viscosity (4.528 versus 4.486 mPa·s; fig. S17) but significantly increased the Li+ transference number from 0.31 to 0.44, reflecting strong Li+ coordination (fig. S16) (23). Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) indicates a pronounced oxidation peak at about 3.55 V for LiDPFP-E, confirming preferential oxidation for CEI formation (Fig. 2D). Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations (fig. S18) and NMR elucidate the solvation structure. RDF curves (Fig. 2E) reveal a new peak at 1.89 Å in LiDPFP-E, corresponding to Li+-DPFP− coordination. Meanwhile, the coordination of Li+-DPFP− reduces the PF6− proportion in the first solvation shell (figs. S19 and S20, 25.0% versus 21.3%). The simulated solvation structures are well confirmed by NMR (fig. S21) and Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (3, 38) (fig. S22) experiments. Cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves (Fig. 2F) exhibit an additional reduction peak at 1.6 V and a decreased solvent reduction peak at 1.4 V, confirming that the DPFP− anion participates in SEI formation and effectively suppresses solvent decomposition. Furthermore, NMR tests (fig. S23) confirmed that LiDPFP can effectively scavenge reactive hydrogen species, thereby preventing the formation of corrosive HF.

Electrochemical performance evaluation

To assess the practical applicability of ultrahigh-voltage LMBs, the cycling performance of NCM811||Li cells is tested with a high areal capacity cathode (3.8 mA·hour cm−2) and a thin Li metal anode (20 μm). The NCM811||Li cells using LiDPFP-E exhibits stable cycling at a high voltage of 4.7 V (Fig. 3, A and B). Specifically, with an operating current of 0.2-C charge/0.3-C discharge, the as-prepared electrolyte enables NCM811||Li cells to achieve 80% capacity retention after 222 cycles and 77% retention after 300 cycles. In contrast, cells with the baseline electrolyte (LiPF6-E; Fig. 3A and fig. S24) retained 80% capacity only after 71 cycles. To elucidate the origin of enhanced cycle life, in situ electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) is conducted on NCM811||Li cells during the first charge (Fig. 3C). The mid- to high-frequency semicircle represents CEI resistance (RCEI), which increases from 3.8 to 4.3 V in both cells, indicating CEI formation. Beyond 4.3 V, clear differences appear: RCEI in the LiDPFP-E cell remains stable at about 3.9 Ω, confirming CEI robustness, whereas it rises from 4.4 to 5.6 Ω in the LiPF6-E cell, evidencing continuous electrolyte decomposition. The low-frequency semicircle reflects charge-transfer resistance (Rct), which initially decreases and then increases during charging. For LiDPFP-E, Rct changes slightly (6.5 to 5.2 Ω), whereas in LiPF6-E, it triples (11.5 to 31.3 Ω), revealing severe cathode degradation under ultrahigh-voltage conditions. In situ differential electrochemical mass spectrometry (DEMS) reveals substantial oxygen evolution in NCM811||Li LMBs with the baseline electrolyte above 4.5 V (Fig. 3D and fig. S25), indicating severe cathode structural degradation. In contrast, LiDPFP-E effectively suppresses oxygen release, confirming its role in forming a robust CEI layer. When the cutoff voltage increases to 4.8 V (Fig. 3E and fig. S26), NCM811||Li LMBs with LiDPFP-E deliver a high specific capacity of 238 mA·hour g−1 and maintain 80% capacity retention after 100 cycles, compared to only 35% for LMBs with the baseline electrolyte. Figure 3F and figs. S27 and S28 demonstrate that ultrahigh-voltage NCM811||Li cells with LiDPFP assistance maintain stable electrochemical performance under extreme temperatures, including 80° and −40°C. These cells also deliver excellent rate capability (fig. S29). Furthermore, when the solvent is switched to traditional fluorine-free carbonates, LiDPFP remains effective (fig. S30), demonstrating its broad practical applicability.

Characterization of the cathode crystal structure and CEI layer at ultrahigh voltage

This section delves into the protection mechanism of LiDPFP-E on the NCM811 cathode. Cross-sectional scanning electron microscopy (SEM) shows that NCM811 secondary particles cycled using the control group LiPF6-E electrolyte exhibit severe internal cracking (Fig. 4A) different from the pristine state (fig. S31). High-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) further revealed internal structural factors contributing to cracking. The NCM811 cathode cycled using the LiPF6-E electrolyte (Fig. 4, B and C) emerges with an around 6-nm disordered rock-salt phase on the surface. The cathode edge HAADF images highlight differing contrasts in the Li layer, suggesting the migration of TMs. The line profiles along the horizontal direction of the surface to bulk regions further confirm this observation (Fig. 4D). On the contrary, SEM shows that NCM811 secondary particles cycled using the LiDPFP-E electrolyte maintain intact structures (Fig. 4E). Meanwhile, HAADF-STEM confirms that the use of the LiDPFP-E electrolyte ensures high stability and reversibility of the layered crystal structure in cycled NCM811 (Fig. 4, F to H). Next, synchrotron-based high-energy x-ray diffraction (HEXRD; Fig. 4, I and J, and tables S2 and S3). Rietveld refinement proves that the NCM811 cathodes cycled with the baseline electrolyte contain a large number of Li/Ni mixing defects and oxygen vacancies. In situ XRD analysis (Fig. 4K) reveals that the (003) peak shift of the NCM811 cathode cycled with the LiDPFP-E electrolyte (Δθ = 1.29°) is smaller than that of the control group (Δθ = 1.39°), indicating reduced lattice distortion and improved structural stability of the cathode. Figure S32 shows the Ni L3-edge synchrotron radiation soft x-ray absorption spectroscopy (sXAS) spectra measured by total electron yield (TEY) mode (with a probing depth of ∼4 nm) on the different NCM811 cathodes. The sXAS is a powerful tool to determine the valence states, local bonding environments, and spin states of the studied system (39, 40). As shown in fig. S32A, the pristine NCM811 cathode consists of 23% Ni2+ and 77% Ni3+. Upon charging to 4.7 V, the NCM811 cathode in the LiPF6-E electrolyte (fig. S32C) exhibits a higher fraction of residual Ni3+ (24% Ni3+ and 76% Ni4+) compared to that in the LiDPFP-E electrolyte (fig. S32B, 10% Ni3+ and 90% Ni4+), indicating incomplete capacity utilization due to severe cathode electrolyte interfacial reactions. After 50 cycles (fig. S32, D and E), the NCM811 cathode in the LiPF6-E electrolyte shows a higher proportion of inactive Ni2+ in the discharged state (59% Ni2+ and 41% Ni3+) compared to that in the LiDPFP-E electrolyte (40% Ni2+ and 60% Ni3+), evidencing more severe structural degradation of the cathode. Overall, the degradation of NCM811 at ultrahigh voltages arises primarily from TM migration and oxygen release. The LiDPFP-derived CEI layer effectively mitigates these processes, thereby preserving the cathode’s structural and electrochemical integrity under extreme conditions.

Next, the LiDPFP-derived CEI and its impact on the NCM811 crystal structure under ultrahigh-voltage conditions are systematically characterized. Liquid chromatography–quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometry (LC-QTOF-MS) (30) combined with electrochemical analysis is used to elucidate the oxidative decomposition behavior of LiDPFP-E. Specifically, the pristine LiDPFP-E is polarized at 4.7 V for 10 hours in a carbon felt||Li model cell to induce electrolyte oxidation. The oxidized carbon felt is then immersed in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) for 2 hours to extract soluble species, which are subsequently analyzed together with the oxidized electrolyte. Comparative total ion chromatograms (Fig. 5, A and B) reveal several new peaks in the oxidized sample, indicating the generation of oxidative decomposition products. m/z analysis identifies the formation of oligomers such as C10H10F7O10P2− (m/z = 484.9637), C6H14O6P− (m/z = 213.0528), and C4H6FO7P2− (m/z = 246.9573), derived from the oxidative cleavage of DPFP− anions. These oligomers, containing cyclic phosphate esters and unsaturated bonds, are prone to polymerization under ultrahigh-voltage conditions, forming robust P-O polymer–enriched CEI layers on the NCM811 surface that contribute to interfacial stabilization (41–43). The abovementioned P-O–containing species are identified in x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF-SIMS) (Fig. 5C and fig. S34A). Furthermore, the CEI layer formed in the experimental group exhibits enriched LiF content (Fig. 5D and fig. S33) and reduced solvent decomposition (fig. S34B). The schematic illustration of the CEI layer enriched with P-O polymer and LiF is provided in Fig. 5E. The high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM; fig. S35) images of the NCM811 cathode identify that the LiDPFP-E electrolyte forms a thinner CEI layer (10 nm) compared to that of LiPF6-E (20 nm). Furthermore, EIS tests (fig. S36) across different cycles further confirm that the LiDPFP additive promotes the formation of thin, passivating, and low-impedance interphase layers. DFT simulations using the climbing image nudged elastic band (cNEB) method are performed to evaluate the effect of the P-O polymer protective layer on TM migration in deeply delithiated cathodes (Fig. 5, F and G). A fully delithiated Li-free Ni0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 model is used to represent the NCM811 cathode under ultrahigh-voltage conditions, whereas adsorption of the C4H6FO7P2− species (selected P-O oligomer) on its surface is used to simulate the modified system. As shown in Fig. 5F, the presence of the P-O polymer layer significantly increases the TM migration energy barrier, indicating its effectiveness in suppressing TM diffusion. The projected density of states (PDOS) calculation (Fig. 5G) shows that, compared to the control group, the cathode with the P-O polymer protective layer exhibits a lower O 2p band center (−2.435 eV versus −2.137 eV) and a larger energy gap (∆E) between the 2p and 3d band centers (0.459 eV versus 0.241 eV). These results indicate that the P-O polymer species derived from the LiDPFP additive suppresses the TM migration and oxygen release, thereby enhancing both bulk structural and interfacial stability of the NCM811 cathode at ultrahigh voltage.

Postmortem characterization of the cycled Li anode

This section examines the protective mechanism of the LiDPFP additive on the Li anode. SEM analysis of Li plating on Cu at an areal capacity of 4 mA·hour cm−2 (Fig. 6, A and C) shows that LiDPFP-E enables uniform, dendrite-free Li deposition with reduced surface area for parasitic reactions. In contrast, LiPF6-E produces a porous, dendritic morphology with voids and a loose structure. Cryogenic TEM (Fig. 6, B and D) further reveals that the SEI formed in LiDPFP-E (Fig. 6B and fig. S37) contains abundant, uniformly distributed Li2O nanocrystals and minor LiF, whereas the SEI from LiPF6-E (Fig. 6D and fig. S38) primarily consists of sparse LiF nanocrystals and amorphous organic components. Notably, the LiDPFP-modified SEI layer exhibits a more uniform nanocrystal size and a more homogeneous nanocrystal distribution, which is crucial for suppressing dendritic Li growth and enhancing the reversibility of the Li metal anode. XPS analysis is performed to further investigate the SEI layer composition. The XPS Li 1s spectra confirms that LiDPFP-E forms a Li2O-enriched SEI layer (Fig. 6E and fig. S39), whereas LiPF6-E exhibits a smaller Li2O signal, consistent with cryo-TEM observations. A reduced C─C/C─H peak in the C 1s spectra and corresponding data in fig. S40 suggest suppressed solvent decompositions in the LiDPFP-E system. TOF-SIMS analysis further reveals weaker F− (fig. S41B) and C2H− (fig. S41C) signals but a stronger O− signal (fig. S41A) in the SEI from LiDPFP-E, corroborating its higher Li2O content and enhanced solvent compatibility. To verify the origin of Li2O, a set of controlled experiments was conducted. The LiDPFP powder is contacted with the Li metal and left to react for 72 hours. The surface residue was then collected and analyzed using 7Li solid-state nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy (SSNMR; Fig. 6F and fig. S42). As shown in Fig. 6G, a distinct Li2O signal is observed in the reaction products, confirming that LiDPFP undergoes a chemical reaction with the Li metal to in situ generate Li2O, thereby contributing to the formation of a robust SEI layer. Atomic force microscopy (AFM) is used to assess the surface morphology and mechanical properties of the SEI layer. As shown in Fig. 6 (H and I), Li plated in the baseline electrolyte exhibited a rough surface with an average roughness of 170 nm, compared to 92 nm for LiDPFP-E. The SEI formed with LiDPFP-E also showed a higher Young’s modulus (600 MPa versus 302 MPa), attributed to its higher inorganic content. In addition, the Li+ transport activation energy through the SEI (figs. S43 and S44) is lower for LiDPFP-E (31.54 kJ mol−1) than for LiPF6-E (45.49 kJ mol−1), indicating improved charge-transfer efficiency. As a result, Li||Cu (Fig. 6J) and Li||Li (fig. S45) half-cells with LiDPFP-E exhibited enhanced Li plating/stripping stability.

Pouch cell performance at ultrahigh voltage

In subsequent, the practical applicability of the LiDPFP-E electrolyte in ultrahigh-voltage LMBs is validated using pouch cells. A key objective in LMB development is achieving stable long-term cycling at high energy density. Increasing the charge cutoff voltage of the high-nickel NCM cathode from normal 4.3 V to ultrahigh voltage exceeding 4.6 V (versus Li+/Li) can increase the energy density of LMBs by about 15 to 35%. However, such gains often come at the expense of cycle life due to intensified parasitic reactions at ultrahigh voltage. To date, no reports have demonstrated stable cycling for 100 cycles in 4.7-V industrial-class pouch cells with an energy density above 600 Wh kg−1.

In this work, the ultrahigh-voltage stability of LMBs with LiDPFP-E has been demonstrated. To enhance the gravimetric energy density of LMBs, a series of optimizations are subsequently performed on the baseline NCM811||Li pouch cells (4.3 V, E/C = 2 g A·hour−1; Fig. 7, A to C). First, the Cu current collector on the anode is removed as the Li metal can function as both an electrode and a current collector, increasing the energy density by ∼5%. Second, reducing the electrolyte injection volume from 2 g A·hour−1 to below 1 g A·hour−1 yielded an additional ∼15% improvement. Third, increasing the cutoff voltage from 4.3 to 4.7 V can further enhance the energy density by ∼20%. By integrating these three strategies into the baseline NCM811||Li pouch cells, the gravimetric energy density can be enhanced by ∼40%. Following this design, ultrahigh-voltage LMB pouch cells achieved gravimetric energy densities of 526 and 628 Wh kg−1 at 2.52 and 6.19 A·hour, respectively. Notably, the pouch cell (526 Wh kg−1) retained 80% capacity after 106 cycles (figs. S46 and S47 and table S4). During long-term cycling, the cell thickness irreversibly increased from 3.01 to 4.31 mm without noticeable gas generation (fig. S48). The cell (628 Wh kg−1) retained 80% capacity after 112 cycles (Fig. 7, D and E, and table S5). The voltage polarization exhibits only a slight increase during cycling (Fig. 7E). As shown in Fig. 7F, considering cycle life, energy density, and areal capacity, the as-prepared LiDPFP-E electrolyte ranks among the advanced electrolytes for LMBs. This exceptional performance is attributed to the simultaneous stabilization of both electrodes, enabled by robust electrode-electrolyte interfaces under ultrahigh-voltage operating conditions.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we successfully integrated molecular design with unsupervised ML to overcome the interfacial instability of ultrahigh-voltage LMBs. By constructing a candidate library based on intramolecular charge redistribution and applying descriptor-based clustering, we efficiently identified LiDPFP as optimal dual-functional additives. LiDPFP exhibits a narrowed electrochemical window and enhanced Li+ coordination, which translates into distinct interfacial chemistries: a P-O polymer–enriched CEI that suppresses cathode degradation (TM migration, oxygen release, and electrolyte oxidation decomposition) and a Li2O-rich SEI that stabilizes the Li metal anode (dendrite-free). Excellent CEI and SEI layers can prevent direct contact between the electrolyte and the active electrode materials, thereby conferring superior ultrahigh-voltage tolerance to the as-assembled LMBs. Encouragingly, the homemade NCM811||Li cells exhibit stable cycling at 4.8 V over a wide temperature range from −40 to 80°C. The homemade industrial-class 4.7-V NCM811||Li pouch cells with an energy density of 628 Wh kg−1 achieve a cycle life exceeding 100 cycles for the first time (80% capacity retention rate after 112 cycles), which is among the best high-energy-density LMB performances. These results demonstrate that bridging rational molecular design with data-driven screening offers a powerful paradigm for accelerating the development of next-generation high-energy LMBs.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials

TFA, ethylene carbonate (EC), ethyl methyl carbonate (EMC), FEC, 1,1,2,2-tetrafluoroethyl 2,2,3,3-tetrafluoropropylether (HFE), lithium difluorophosphate (LiPF2O2, LiDFP), and lithium hexafluorophosphate (LiPF6) were procured from Duoduo Chem Co. Ltd. All solvents were treated with 4-Å molecular sieves to reduce water content to below 30 parts per million (ppm). Lithium metal foils were obtained from China Energy Lithium Co. Ltd. LiNi0.8Co0.1Mn0.1O2 (NCM811) cathodes (17.1 mg cm−2, 97.8 wt % NCM811) were self-fabricated. Lithium foil anodes were prepared as 16-mm-diameter disks, and cathodes were shaped into 12-mm-diameter disks, followed by vacuum drying at 80°C for 12 hours before use. The Celgard 2325 (PP/PE/PP) separator was supplied by Guangdong Canrd New Energy Technology Co. Ltd. and Duoduo Chem Co. Ltd.

Synthesis of LiDPFP

One gram of perfluoropinacol [hexafluoro-2,3-bis(trifluoromethyl)-2,3-butanediol; TCI] was dissolved in 2 ml of 1,2-dimethoxyethane (DME; TCI) in a round-bottom flask. Subsequently, 0.0476 g of lithium hydride (LiH; Aladdin) was added, and the mixture was stirred at 60°C for 12 hours. Then, 0.2274 g of LiPF6 was introduced, and the reaction proceeded at 120°C for an additional 12 hours under nitrogen atmosphere. The resulting product was precipitated by adding toluene (TCI), redissolved in DME, and reprecipitated with toluene to afford the target compound. 31P NMR (162 MHz, DMSO-d6): −39.91 (q, J = 753.3 Hz). 19F NMR (376 MHz): −68.39 (t, J = 48.9 Hz), −69.27 (t, J = 48.9 Hz).

Electrolyte preparation

The baseline electrolyte (LiPF6-E) was formulated with 1.0 M LiPF6 in a 3:1 mixture of TFA and FEC. The LiDPFP-assisted electrolyte (LiDPFP-E) was prepared by incorporating 1 wt % LiDPFP into LiPF6-E. The commercial electrolyte consisting of 1 M LiPF6 in EC/EMC (3:7 by volume) was also used. All electrolytes were prepared in an argon-filled glove box, maintaining oxygen and water levels below 0.01 ppm.

Cell assembling and testing

2032 coin-type cells were assembled in an argon-filled glove box with H2O and O2 levels maintained below 0.01 ppm. The cells included Li||Cu, Li||Li, and NCM811 (17.1 mg cm−2)||Li (20 μm), using Celgard (2325, PP/PE/PP) separators and 50 μl of electrolytes. Electrochemical tests were performed using a LAND system (Wuhan LAND Electronics Co. Ltd.). The NCM811||Li pouch cells [526 Wh kg−1 (2.52 A·hour) and 628 Wh kg−1 (6.19 A·hour)] were self-prepared.

For cycling tests, NCM811 (17.1 mg cm−2)||Li (20 μm) coin cells were charged/discharged at 0.2 C/0.3 C within voltage ranges of 2.7 to 4.7 V and 2.7 to 4.8 V at room temperature (RT) and 80°C and at 0.05 C/0.05 C within 2.8 to 4.3 V at −40°C. In addition, rate capability tests (with a cutoff voltage of 4.7 V) were performed on these cells at continuous rates of 0.2, 0.3, 0.5, and 1 C. The NCM811||Li pouch cell (526 Wh kg−1) was precycled for one cycle at 0.1 C (charge/discharge) from 2.8 to 4.7 V, followed by cycling at 0.1 C/0.3 C. The NCM811||Li pouch cell (628 Wh kg−1) was precycled for one cycle at 0.2 C (charge/discharge) from 2.8 to 4.7 V, followed by cycling at 0.1 C/0.2 C.

The cycling performance of symmetric Li||Li cells was tested at a current density of 0.5 mA cm−2 with a capacity density of 1.0 mA·hour cm−2. In the modified Aurbach’s protocol for Li||Cu coin cells, Li first plating of 5 mA·hour cm−2 on Cu was conducted, followed by stripping to 1 V at 0.5 mA cm−2. Then, another Li plating of 5 mA·hour cm−2 was performed, followed by stripping to 1 mA·hour cm−2 and replating to 1 mA·hour cm−2 at 0.5 mA cm−2 for 10 cycles. Last, the plated Li was stripped to 1 V at 0.5 mA cm−2.

In situ XRD was performed using a Rigaku Ultima IV (scan rate: 4° min−1, Cu Kα radiation, λ = 1.5406 Å) to investigate the correlation between the electrochemical performance and the crystal structure of the NCM811 cathode during cycling.

MD simulations

MD simulations were conducted to investigate the solvation structures of LiPF6-E and LiDPFP-E electrolytes. Optimized electrolyte molecules were packed into periodic boxes to construct bulk systems. The systems were equilibrated through NPT (isothermal-isobaric) MD simulations for 5 ns at 303 K and atmospheric pressure, followed by NVT (isothermal) MD simulations for 5 ns with a 1-fs time step. All simulations were performed using the COMPASS force field (45). Temperature control was achieved using a Nosé-Hoover-Langevin (NHL) thermostat, and pressure was regulated by a Berendsen barostat (46, 47). Electrostatic and van der Waals interactions were treated using the Ewald scheme (48, 49) and an atom-based cutoff radius of 15.5 Å, respectively. The stabilized densities from the MD simulations are ∼1.43 g cm−3 (LiPF6-E) and 1.45 g cm−3 (LiDPFP-E), respectively.

Gaussian calculation

ESP, HOMO energy, LUMO energy, polarizability, dipole moment, and Li+ binding energy were calculated using the Gaussian 16 software with the B3LYP functional (50). Geometry optimization was performed with the 6-311G(d) basis set to determine the optimal molecular geometry (51). Single-point energy calculations used the larger Def2TZVP basis set. Solvation effects were accounted for using the SMD (52) implicit solvation model. To improve accuracy, the DFT-D3 dispersion correction with BJ damping (53) was applied to account for weak interactions.

TM migration energy calculation

All calculations were performed using spin-polarized DFT as implemented in the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) (54). The projector augmented wave (PAW) method was used to describe ionic potentials, and the exchange-correlation energy was treated with the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) (55). A plane-wave energy cutoff of 500 eV was applied during geometry optimization. Brillouin zone sampling was conducted using a 2 × 2 × 1 Monkhorst-Pack k-point mesh (56). Structural optimizations were carried out with energy and force convergence criteria of 10−5 eV and 0.02 eV/Å, respectively. A 15-Å vacuum layer was added to eliminate periodic image interactions. TM migration pathways were investigated using the cNEB method with five intermediate images, and the minimum energy paths were optimized until the maximum force on each image was below 0.05 eV/Å.

Unsupervised ML clustering

Lithium salt library

To develop novel phosphorus-centered lithium salt additives for ultrahigh-voltage LMBs, we assembled EWGs (e.g., -CF3 and -CN) and EDGs (e.g., -CH3) within phosphorus-centered anions to induce intramolecular charge redistribution and thereby tune the electrochemical properties of the molecules. Guided by this principle, we selected 14,779 phosphorus-centered molecules from open-source chemical repositories (PubChem) and supplemented them with 421 proprietary structures designed by our team, ultimately constructing an initial molecular library of 15,200 different structures.

Massive screening

Given the stringent requirements of ultrahigh-voltage LMBs for phosphorus-centered lithium salt additives, a rigorous multistage filtration protocol was implemented using the RDKit cheminformatics toolkit on candidate molecules.

1) Anion extraction: All molecules were processed to remove lithium ions, other cations, and neutral molecules, retaining only the anionic fragments.

2) Elemental constraints: The allowed elemental composition was strictly limited to H, C, N, O, F, and P to avoid heavy metals or redox-unstable elements.

3) Labile proton filter: A blacklist based on SMARTS pattern matching was applied to exclude molecules containing labile protons [e.g., hydroxyls (-OH), carboxyl (-COOH), thiols (-SH), amines (-NH2), and phosphorus hydrogens (P-H)].

4) Phosphorus coordination number filter: Retain only molecules containing phosphorus atom with a coordination number greater than 3.

5) Negative charge localization: All atoms bearing a negative formal charge must be either phosphorus (P) or oxygen (O) directly bonded to P.

6) Physicochemical property constraints: The MW window was set to 60 Da ≤ MW ≤ 1000 Da. The number of atoms must be ≤55 to reduce the steric hindrance of the anion. Total formal charge must be −1.

7) Functional density threshold: To ensure high electrochemical efficacy per unit mass, we imposed a functional density constraint requiring the ratio of heteroatoms to heavy atoms to exceed 0.30 (Nhetero/Nheavy ≥ 0.30).

8) Active motif requirement: To obtain a more favorable SEI composition, the quantities of certain functional groups are strictly controlled (e.g., -F ≥ 1 or -CN ≥ 1 or -NO2 ≥ 1).

Following this rigorous sanitization, 343 high-quality precursor candidates were retained for feature engineering.

K-means clustering

The K-means clustering, as part of unsupervised ML techniques, is a well-known and widely used clustering method. Clustering in data science allows the finding in a dataset of the greatest similarity within the same cluster and the greatest dissimilarity between different clusters (57). In this work, a K-means unsupervised ML algorithm was implemented in Python via scikit-learn (version 1.8.0) with the sklearn.cluster module and the KMeans submodule for the clustering and selecting of lithium salt. To determine the cluster number (k), we rationally set the range of k between 5 and 15 and selected the value with the highest silhouette coefficient as the most optimal hyperparameter. For each data point xi within cluster C, the centroid of the cluster, uc, isuc=1D∑i=1Dxiwhere D denotes the cardinality of cluster C. Cluster centroids are indicated by red dots, whereas other data points were indicated by dots of other colors.

Scoring

The comprehensive score for each candidate is the weighted sum of normalized values derived from the following three dimensions:

1) Interfacial film-forming capability (weight: 4): Assesses the propensity of the anion redox decomposition and the tendency of the anion coordinate with Li+ to form CIPs. Descriptors: ESPmax and ESPmin. As established in our design principles, large dipole moments promote field-driven migration of the anion toward the electrode interfaces, whereas high polarizability enables dynamic electron density redistribution to buffer electrostatic repulsion under strong electric fields. Descriptors: molecular polarizability and dipole moment.

2) Functional group score (weight: 4): A semiempirical metric valuing functional groups based on their contribution to forming robust inorganic SEI species (e.g., LiF, Li2O, and Li3N) versus unstable organic components. First, an excessive hydrogen content in additives can compromise the stability of the resulting interfacial protective layer. In addition, fluorine-containing functional groups promote the formation of the desired LiF component within the SEI. Accordingly, the following assignments apply: F (+1) and H (−1). The -NO2 group can contribute to the generation of either Li3N or Li2O species and is therefore assigned a score of 0.5. For oxygen-based moieties capable of forming Li2O (e.g., C─O─C and ═O), a C─O─C group receives 0.5 points, whereas a ═O group is awarded 2 points because it more readily forms Li2O due to its enhanced coordination capability with Li+. The -CN group has been shown to coordinate with TM ions and suppress their dissolution from the cathode material, so it is also assigned a score of 1. Other functional groups are assigned 0 points as their influence is uncertain for the battery system.

3) Synthetic accessibility (weight: 2): Represents the estimated ease of synthesis. Definition: Synthesis Ability = 5.0 − SCScore(molecule). The SCScore is an ML-based metric for synthetic complexity (Coley et al.) (58) The value 5.0 corresponds to the SCScore of palytoxin, serving as the benchmark for maximum synthetic difficulty.
