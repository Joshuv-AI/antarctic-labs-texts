Outlier eigenvalues and eigenvectors of generalized Wigner matrices with finite-rank perturbations
Bishakh Bhattacharya, Arijit Chakrabarty, Rajat Subhra Hazra

Abstract
. A generalized Wigner matrix perturbed by a finite-rank deterministic matrix is considered. The fluctuations of the largest eigenvalues, which emerge outside the bulk of the spectrum, and the corresponding eigenvectors, are studied. Under certain assumptions on the perturbation and the matrix structure, we derive the first-order behavior of these eigenvalues and show that they are well separated from the bulk. The fluctuations of these eigenvalues are shown to follow a multivariate Gaussian distribution, and the asymptotic behavior of the associated eigenvectors is also studied. We prove central limit theorems that describe the asymptotic alignment of these eigenvectors with the perturbation’s eigenvectors, as well as their Gaussian fluctuations around the origin for non-aligned components. Furthermore, we discuss the convergence of the eigenvector process in a Sobolev space framework.

1.
Introduction

Random matrix theory (RMT) has played a pivotal role in understanding the behavior of large complex systems, with applications ranging from quantum physics to data science. One of the fundamental models in this area is the Wigner matrix, which refers to large symmetric matrices with independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.) zero-mean and unit-variance entries. The eigenvalue distribution of such matrices is well understood, with the celebrated Wigner semicircle law describing the bulk behavior of eigenvalues as the matrix size grows. There have been many extensions of the above result to the setting where entries are inhomogeneous, see
Anderson and Zeitouni, 2006
;
Shlyakhtenko, 1996
;
Zhu, 2020
, for example. In the inhomogeneous setting, the bulk measure satisfies the quadratic vector equations, studied elaborately in
Ajanki et al., 2017
;
Ajanki et al., 2019
. In
Chakrabarty et al., 2021
;
Zhu, 2020
, it is shown that in an inhomogeneous Erdős-Rényi setting, the bulk behaviour of the adjacency matrix is described by a deterministic compactly supported probability measure, which satisfies such an equation. In the classical Wigner setting, the behaviour of the top eigenvalues at the edge of the spectrum is much more complete. Under suitable moment assumptions, the largest eigenvalue converges to
2
(the right edge of the semicircle) and its fluctuation on the
N^{-2/3}
scale is governed by the Tracy–Widom law. Analogous edge universality results have been proved for a wide class of Wigner and Wigner-type ensembles, but a fully general theory for inhomogeneous variance profiles is still out of reach. Recently, for a random matrix with a (bounded)variance profile,
Ducatez et al., 2024
showed, amongst other things, that the largest eigenvalue, in the first order, converges to the right endpoint of the spectrum.

However, practical systems are often subject to structured perturbations. In particular, finite-rank perturbations of Wigner matrices have garnered significant attention, especially in connection with the Baik–Ben Arous–Péché (BBP) transition
Baik et al., 2005
;
Péché, 2006
;
Féral and Péché, 2007
. The BBP phenomenon describes a phase transition where, under a rank-one perturbation, the largest eigenvalue separates from the bulk and exhibits new behavior. This result has been extended to various settings, including spiked covariance models in principal component analysis (PCA), where the largest eigenvalue corresponds to the signal and the bulk reflects noise. Some notable references are
Benaych-Georges and Nadakuditi, 2011
;
Capitaine et al., 2009
;
Baik and Silverstein, 2006
;
Bassler et al., 2009
;
Benaych-Georges et al., 2011
.

In the rank-one case,
Féral and Péché, 2007
showed that if the perturbation strength
\theta
is below a critical value, then the largest eigenvalue sticks to the edge and has Tracy–Widom fluctuations, whereas above the said threshold, it separates from the bulk and fluctuates on an
O(1)
scale with a non-universal limit law. Building on this,
Capitaine et al., 2009
, and later
Pizzo et al., 2013
analysed general finite-rank perturbations
M_{N}=W_{N}/\sqrt{N}+A_{N}
, where
A_{N}
is a deterministic Hermitian matrix with finitely many non-zero eigenvalues
\alpha_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\alpha_{k}
. They proved that for each fixed
i
,

\mu_{i}\xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}}\begin{cases}2,&|\alpha_{i}|\leq 1,\\
\alpha_{i}+\alpha_{i}^{-1},&|\alpha_{i}|>1,\end{cases}

and established the following transition in the fluctuation behaviour: below the threshold one recovers the Tracy–Widom statistics at the edge, while above the threshold the outliers have asymptotically Gaussian fluctuations, with a variance that depends on the entry distribution of
W_{N}
. Related results in a more general framework, including a detailed description of the associated eigenvectors, were obtained by
Benaych-Georges and Nadakuditi, 2011
via free-probability techniques.

The finite rank perturbations of the symmetric matrix also play a crucial role in the study of random graphs; see, for example, (
Füredi and Komlós, 1981
). The properties of the outlier eigenvalues and the corresponding eigenvectors for the Erdős-Rényi was first studied by
Erdős et al., 2013
where it was shown that in a homogeneous Erdős-Rényi setting, subject to a rank one perturbation, the centred largest eigenvalue under suitable scaling exhibits Gaussian fluctuation. The study was later extended to other eigenvalues and their properties. For example, it was shown in
Erdős et al., 2012
that the second largest eigenvalue under suitable centring and scaling exhibits Tracy-Widom Fluctuation. Studies involving random perturbations can be found in
Ji and Lee, 2020
. The above results can be extended to the fully connected regime in the Erdős-Rényi random graphs and this was shown recently in
Tikhomirov and Youssef, 2021
. There has also been various extensions to the inhomogeneous Erdős-Rényi random graph and matrices with variance profile (
Chakrabarty et al., 2020
;
Boursier and Guionnet, 2024
;
Ajanki et al., 2017
).

In this paper, we consider a generalized Wigner matrix
W_{N}
with a bounded variance profile, perturbed by a deterministic finite-rank matrix of the form

A_{N}=W_{N}+\theta_{N}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}e_{i}e_{i}^{\prime},

where
\theta_{N}
is a scalar that may grow with
N
, the
\alpha_{i}>0
are fixed distinct signal strengths, and
e_{1},\dots,e_{k}
are deterministic unit vectors arising from discretizations of orthonormal functions on
[0,1]
. Our primary interest is in the
outlier
eigenvalues of
A_{N}
that separate from the
O(\sqrt{N})
bulk due to the perturbation, and in the finer properties of the associated eigenvectors. Under mild assumptions on the variance profile and on the growth rate of
\theta_{N}
, we prove central limit theorems for the outlier eigenvalues, establish Gaussian fluctuation results for the alignment of the leading eigenvectors with the planted directions
e_{i}
. Finally, we consider a functional eigenvector process by viewing the eigenvector as a function on
[0,1]
. In a Sobolev space framework, we prove a functional CLT, i.e., weak convergence of this eigenvector process to a Gaussian limit (which can be interpreted as a form of Gaussian random field on
[0,1]
) on the said space.

Main contributions:

This work provides a detailed analysis of eigenvector behavior in a class of dense random matrices with non-homogeneous variance profile, extending the scope of classical random matrix theory beyond the Wigner regime. Motivated by models in statistical physics and network theory, our setting captures inhomogeneity through a structured variance profile
f(x,y)
, enabling new phenomena that are absent in translation-invariant ensembles.

The principal contributions of this paper are the following.

•

Eigenvalue fluctuations (Theorem
2.1
).
We show that the top
k
outlier eigenvalues of a perturbed generalized Wigner matrix, where
k
is the rank of the perturbation, fluctuate on an
O(1)
scale and jointly converge to a multivariate Gaussian distribution.

•

Eigenvector alignment and delocalization (Theorems
2.3
and
2.4
).
Under mild regularity of the variance profile and the planted perturbation, the leading eigenvectors align strongly with the deterministic signal directions while all other components remain negligible, and each eigenvector satisfies a sharp sup‐norm delocalization bound.

•

Functional CLT for the eigenvector process (Theorem
2.5
).
Viewing the top eigenvectors as functions on
[0,1]
, we establish a Sobolev‐space central limit theorem for their fluctuation field, proving convergence to a centered Gaussian process with explicitly characterized covariance.

•

New technical estimates.
In the results for the eigenvalues, the Gaussian limit arises from quadratic forms of the type
e_{i}We_{i}^{\prime}
. In the eigenvector case, especially when it comes to understanding fluctuations, more nonlinearity enters the picture, and terms of the form
e_{i}W^{2}e_{i}^{\prime}
become the key building block. To obtain these results, we develop novel high‐moment and concentration bounds for quadratic and higher‐order forms of the random matrix, as well as precise resolvent expansions and delocalization controls that may be of independent interest. In fact, we give an application of the martingale central limit theorem for a quadratic form of the Wigner matrix (Theorem
7.1
).

In Section

, we formalize the model setup, assumptions, and state the main results. Section
3
contains an outline of the proof of the eigenvalue fluctuation results, including preliminary estimates and the eigenvalue CLT. Since the ideas of the eigenvalue fluctuations are similar to
Chakrabarty et al., 2020
, the details of the proof of eigenvalue fluctuations are postponed to Appendix
A
. Section
4
gives some technical estimates and series representations for the eigenvectors. Section
5
is devoted to the eigenvector alignment results: we prove the asymptotic alignment and CLTs for eigenvector entries, the eigenvector delocalization. The functional CLT for the eigenvector process in the Sobolev space setting is proved in Section
6
. The martingale CLT for a quadratic form of
W_{N}^{2}
is proved in Section
7
. Important technical lemmas and calculations are deferred to Appendix
B
.

2.
The Setup and the Main Results

In this section, we describe the model of a perturbed generalized Wigner matrix and present the main results on the behavior of its top eigenvalues and eigenvectors. The focus is on the fluctuations of these
quantities around their deterministic limits.

Setup and Assumptions:

For each
N\geq 1
, let
W_{N}
be a symmetric Wigner-type random matrix of size
N\times N
, defined by

(2.1)

W_{N}(j,\ell)\;=\;X_{\,j\wedge\ell,\,j\vee\ell}\,,

where
\{X_{j,\ell}:1\leq j\leq\ell\leq N\}
is a collection of independent (not necessarily
identically distributed) random variables with mean 0 and a variance profile given by

\displaystyle f\left(\frac{j}{N},\frac{\ell}{N}\right)\,,

for some fixed bounded Riemann-integrable function
f:[0,1]^{2}\to[0,\infty)
. We impose a mild moment condition on the entries of
W_{N}
: there exists a constant
B>0
such
that for all
m\geq 1
,

(2.2)

\mathbb{E}[\,|X_{j,\ell}|^{m}\,]\;\leq\;m^{\,Bm}\,.

This condition (sometimes called a sub-exponential tail bound) is used to ensure
concentration of measure for various random quadratic forms of
W_{N}
.
Next, we define a deterministic finite-rank perturbation to
W_{N}
. Let
k\geq 1
be fixed (not depending
on
N
). We consider

(2.3)

A_{N}\;=\;W_{N}\;+\;\theta_{N}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}\,e_{i}\,e_{i}^{\prime}\,,

where
\theta_{N}>0
is a scalar that may grow with
N
, and
\alpha_{1}>\alpha_{2}>\cdots>\alpha_{k}>0
are fixed distinct constants (the signal strengths). The vectors
e_{1},\dots,e_{k}
are deterministic unit vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
(thus
e_{i}
is
N\times 1
and
e_{i}^{\prime}
is its
transpose). These vectors represent the directions of the finite-rank perturbation. We assume that
e_{i}
is
the discretization of some function
h_{i}
on
[0,1]
. In particular, for each
1\leq i\leq k
,

e_{i}^{\prime}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\left(h_{i}(1/N),h_{i}(2/N),\ldots,h_{i}(1)\right).

Equivalently, the
\ell
-th component of
e_{i}
is
e_{i}(\ell)=h_{i}(\ell/N)/\sqrt{N}
. We refer to
h_{i}(x)
(for

x\in[0,1]
) as the underlying signal function corresponding to the perturbation vector
e_{i}
.
We will work under the following assumptions on the growth of
\theta_{N}
and the regularity of the
functions
h_{i}
. In many of our results, only the first two assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
are needed; the third will
be required for some finer results (notably the functional CLT)

Assumption 2.1

(
Growth of
\theta_{N}
).

There exists
\xi>4(B+1)\vee 8
such that

\sqrt{N}(\log N)^{\xi}\preceq\theta_{N}\preceq N

for all large
N
.

Here
a_{N}\preceq b_{N}
means
a_{N}=O(b_{N})
as
N\to\infty
. Intuitively,
\theta_{N}
grows at least on the order of
\sqrt{N}\,(\log N)^{\xi}
(so that the perturbation is
sufficiently strong to create outliers), but at most on the order of
N
.

Assumption 2.2

(
Orthogonality of
h_{i}
).

The collection
{h_{1},\ldots,h_{k}}
is an orthonormal set of bounded functions in
L^{2}[0,1]
.

Equivalently, one can say the vectors
e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}

defined above are asymptotically orthonormal in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
(indeed one can check
e_{i}^{\prime}e_{j}=\langle h_{i},h_{j}\rangle_{L^{2}[0,1]}+o(1)
as
N\to\infty
, which equals
\delta_{ij}
by
orthonormality).

Assumption 2.3

(
Regularity of
h_{i}
).

Each
h_{i}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
is Lipschitz continuous: there exists
L_{i}<\infty
such that

|h_{i}(x)-h_{i}(y)|\leq L_{i}|x-y|,\quad\forall x,y\in[0,1].

This assumption will only be needed for some results where we require strong regularity, for example in analyzing the eigenvector as a function on
[0,1]
.

Notation:

We use the notation
\lambda_{1}(M)\geq\lambda_{2}(M)\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{N}(M)
for the ordered eigenvalues of an
N\times N
symmetric matrix
M
. In particular,
\lambda_{1}(A_{N})
will denote the largest eigenvalue of
A_{N}
,
\lambda_{2}(A_{N})
the second largest, and so on. Similarly, for each
1\leq i\leq k
we denote by
v_{i}
a unit eigenvector of
A_{N}
corresponding to
\lambda_{i}(A_{N})
(we will often call
v_{i}
the
i
th eigenvector, understanding that it is determined up to a sign
\pm 1
which we will fix arbitrarily or via a convenient convention). Throughout, we focus on the outlier eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}(A_{N}),\ldots,\lambda_{k}(A_{N})
and their eigenvectors
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}
and these are the
k
eigenvalues that will be shown to escape the bulk of
W_{N}
’s spectrum due to the perturbation. We will use the following standard probabilistic notations to describe convergence as
N\to\infty
:

•

A sequence of events
E_{N}
is said to occur with high probability (w.h.p.) if

\mathbb{P}(E_{N}^{c})=O\left(e^{-(\log N)^{\eta}}\right),\quad\text{for some }\eta>1.

In words, the probability that
E_{N}
fails to occur decays super-polynomially fast as
N
grows.

•

For random variables (or random sequences)
Y_{N}
and
Z_{N}
, we write

Y_{N}=O_{hp}(Z_{N})

to mean that there exists a constant
C>0
such that
|Y_{N}|\leq C|Z_{N}|
w.h.p. (for all large
N
).

•

Similarly,

Y_{N}=o_{hp}(Z_{N})

means that for every
\delta>0
,
|Y_{N}|\leq\delta|Z_{N}|
w.h.p. as
N\to\infty
. Intuitively,
O_{hp}
and
o_{hp}
are high-probability versions of the usual
O(\cdot)
and
o(\cdot)
notations.

•

Similarly,
Y_{N}=O_{p}(Z_{N})
means

\lim_{x\to\infty}\sup_{N\geq 1}\mathbb{P}(|Y_{N}|>x|Z_{N}|)=0,

and
Y_{N}=o_{p}(Z_{N})
means

\lim_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{P}(|Y_{N}|>\delta|Z_{N}|)=0,\quad\text{for all }\delta>0.

Note that if
Z_{N}\neq 0
a.s., then
Y_{N}=O_{p}(Z_{N})
is equivalent to the stochastic tightness of
(Z_{N}^{-1}Y_{N}:N\geq 1)
, while
Y_{N}=o_{p}(Z_{N})
implies
Z_{N}^{-1}Y_{N}\xrightarrow{P}0
. Further,
O_{\mathrm{hp}}
and
o_{\mathrm{hp}}
are stronger than their
O_{p}
and
o_{p}
counterparts.

Throughout the paper, we omit the dependence on
N
in notation when there is no risk of confusion. For example, we often write
W
and
A
instead of
W_{N}
and
A_{N}
. All asymptotic notation (
O(\cdot),o(\cdot)
, etc.) is with respect to
N\to\infty
unless specified otherwise.

2.1.
Main Results.

We first summarize the first-order (law of large numbers) behavior of the outlier eigenvalues. Since the bulk of the eigenvalues of
A_{N}
lie around the scale
\|W_{N}\|=O(\sqrt{N})
, the perturbation causes
k
outliers at the larger scale of order
\theta_{N}
(we drop the subscript
N
from
\theta
subsequently). Indeed, under Assumptions
2.1
-
2.2
we will show that the outliers separate from the bulk for large
N
. More precisely, for each fixed
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
, the
i
-th largest eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}(A)
is approximately
\theta\alpha_{i}
. The deviation of
\lambda_{i}(A)
from
\theta\alpha_{i}
vanishes with high probability as
N\to\infty
. In particular, one obtains

\lambda_{i}(A)=\theta\alpha_{i}\left(1+o_{hp}(1)\right),

for each
1\leq i\leq k
. This shows that
\lambda_{i}(A)
is eventually bounded away from 0 w.h.p., so dividing by
\lambda_{i}(A)
in asymptotic formulas will be justified.

We now turn to the fluctuations of the outliers. Our first main theorem is a Central Limit Theorem (CLT) for the outlier eigenvalues, which describes their asymptotic distribution after proper centering. Let
\mathbb{E}[\lambda_{i}(A)]
denote the expected value of
\lambda_{i}(A)
(with respect to the randomness of
W
). We will show that
\mathbb{E}[\lambda_{i}(A)]
is close to
\theta\alpha_{i}
as
N
grows (more precisely, an expansion is given in Theorem
2.2
below). The CLT is stated in two parts: first an expansion of
\lambda_{i}(A)
around its mean, and then a description of the joint Gaussian law in the limit.

Theorem 2.1
.

(
Central Limit Theorem for Eigenvalues
) Under Assumptions (
2.1
) and (
2.2
), for all
i
such that
1\leq i\leq k
, we have the expansion

\lambda_{i}(A)=\mathbb{E}\left(\lambda_{i}(A)\right)+\frac{\theta\alpha_{i}}{\lambda_{i}(A)}e_{i}^{\prime}We_{i}+o_{p}(1)\,.

As a consequence, the vector of fluctuations of the top
k
eigenvalues converges in distribution to a multivariate Gaussian:

\bigg(\lambda_{i}(A)-\mathbb{E}(\lambda_{i}(A)):1\leq i\leq k\bigg)\Rightarrow(G_{i}:1\leq i\leq k)\,,

where
(G_{1},\ldots,G_{k})
is a centered Gaussian
\mathbb{R}^{k}
-valued random vector with covariance

\mathrm{Cov}(G_{i},G_{j})=2\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}h_{i}(x)h_{i}(y)h_{j}(x)h_{j}(y)f(x,y)\,dx\,dy\,,

for all
1\leq i,j\leq k
. In particular, for each fixed
1\leq i\leq k
,
\lambda_{i}(A)-\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[\lambda_{i}(A)]
is asymptotically
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{i}^{2})
with

\sigma_{i}^{2}=2\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}h_{i}(x)^{2}f(x,y)h_{i}(y)^{2}dxdy.

Remark 2.1
.

In the expansion above,
\theta\alpha_{i}\lambda_{i}(A)^{-1}e_{i}^{\prime}We_{i}
is the leading random term in
\lambda_{i}(A)-\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\lambda_{i}(A)
. Since
\lambda_{i}(A)\approx\theta\alpha_{i}
for large
N
, this leading term is roughly
e_{i}^{\prime}We_{i}
(up to a factor of order 1). Thus heuristically the fluctuation of
\lambda_{i}(A)
is governed by the random quadratic form
e_{i}^{\prime}We_{i}
. These forms for different
i
are correlated if the
h_{i}
have overlapping support or
f(x,y)
couples them, which results in the non-zero covariance
{\rm Cov}(G_{i},G_{j})
when
i\neq j
.

The next result provides an asymptotic description of the centering
\mathbb{E}[\lambda_{i}(A)]
of the outlier eigenvalues. In other words, it refines the first-order approximation
\mathbb{E}[\lambda_{i}(A)]\approx\theta\alpha_{i}
by giving the leading correction term. Define a
k\times k
deterministic matrix
B=B_{N}
by

(2.4)

B(j,l)=\sqrt{\alpha_{j}\alpha_{\ell}}\theta e_{j}^{\prime}e_{\ell}+\frac{\sqrt{\alpha_{j}\alpha_{\ell}}\mathbb{E}(e_{j}^{\prime}W^{2}e_{\ell})}{\theta\alpha_{i}^{2}}\,\text{ for }1\leq j,\ell\leq k.

Intuitively,
B
is like the “finite-rank part” of
\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}(A)
plus an
O(1/\theta_{N})
correction coming from the
W^{2}
term.

For the next result a bit stronger assumption on
\theta_{N}
than
2.1
is needed.

Theorem 2.2
.

(
Asymptotic Behavior of Eigenvalue Mean
) Suppose the Assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
hold. Additionally, assume

(2.5)

N^{2/3}\ll\theta_{N}.

Then we have

\mathbb{E}(\lambda_{i}(A))=\lambda_{i}(B)\left(1+O\left(\frac{N^{2}}{\theta^{3}}\right)\right)\,,

where
B
is as defined in (
2.4
) and
\lambda_{i}(B)
is the
i
-th eigenvalue of
B
.

Since the proofs of Theorem
2.1
and
2.2
are modifications of the proof from
Chakrabarty et al., 2020
we provide the details in the Appendix of this article.

We now summarize the main findings regarding the eigenvectors corresponding to the outlier eigenvalues. Fix
1\leq i\leq k
, and let
v_{i}
be the (unit-norm) eigenvector of
A
associated with
\lambda_{i}(A)
. We are interested in the
N
-dimensional vector
v_{i}=(v_{i}(1),\ldots,v_{i}(N))^{\prime}
and, in particular, its projections onto the perturbation directions
e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}
. Since
{e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}}
form the signal directions, an intuitive expectation is that
v_{i}
should be mostly aligned with
e_{i}
and approximately orthogonal to
e_{j}
for
j\neq i
. The following results confirm this picture and quantify the fluctuations.

First, it turns out that
v_{i}
is indeed asymptotically aligned with
e_{i}
and asymptotically orthogonal to
e_{j}
(
j\neq i
). In fact, one can show that

e_{i}^{\prime}v_{i}=1+o_{hp}\left(\frac{\sqrt{N}}{\theta}\right),\quad e_{j}^{\prime}v_{i}=o_{hp}\left(\frac{\sqrt{N}}{\theta}\right)\quad(j\neq i)

which means that
e_{i}
captures the vast majority of the weight of
v_{i}
, up to a small fraction on the order of
(\sqrt{N}/\theta)
. Note that
\sqrt{N}/\theta\to 0
by Assumption
2.1
, so indeed
e_{i}^{\prime}v_{i}\to 1
and
e_{j}^{\prime}v_{i}\to 0
in probability.

Our next theorem describes the Gaussian fluctuations of the alignment of
v_{i}
with
e_{i}
. In other words, we consider the inner product
e_{i}^{\prime}v_{i}
and show that it concentrates near
1
and that its fluctuations around that value are Gaussian in the
N\to\infty
limit. Moreover, for the inner products with non-matching directions (
e_{j}^{\prime}v_{i}
with
j\neq i
), we establish that they fluctuate around 0 and also follow an approximate Gaussian law. This can be viewed as a CLT for the coordinates of the eigenvectors in the basis of perturbation vectors
{e_{i}}
.

Theorem 2.3

(
Gaussian fluctuation of eigenvector alignment).

Under Assumptions
2.1
-
2.3
,

(a)

for each
i\in{1,\ldots,k}
, we have

\frac{\left(\theta\alpha_{i}\right)^{2}}{\sqrt{N}}\left(e_{i}^{\prime}v_{i}-\mathbb{E}\left[e_{i}^{\prime}v_{i}\right]\right)\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\sigma^{2}\right)

as
N\to\infty
, where

\sigma_{i}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}h_{i}(x)^{2}f(x,y)f(y,z)h_{i}(z)^{2}dxdydz

In particular,
e_{i}^{\prime}v_{i}
itself converges to 1 in probability, since
\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[e_{i}^{\prime}v_{i}]\to 1
and the fluctuations vanish as
N\to\infty
.

(b)

For any
j\neq i
,

\theta\left(e_{j}^{\prime}v_{i}-\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[e_{j}^{\prime}v_{i}]\right)\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\tau_{ij}^{2}\right),

for some
\tau_{ij}^{2}
given by the following expression

\displaystyle\tau_{ij}^{2}=\frac{1}{(\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j})^{2}\alpha_{j}}\int_{[0,1]^{2}}\left[h_{j}(x)h_{i}(y)+h_{i}(x)h_{j}(y)\right]^{2}f(x,y)\,dx\,dy.

In particular,
e_{j}^{\prime}v_{i}=o_{p}(1)
for
j\neq i
, showing asymptotic orthogonality of
v_{i}
and
e_{j}
.

In the above result the main fluctuation in
e_{i}^{\prime}v_{i}
comes from certain quadratic forms of
W
(specifically
e_{i}^{\prime}W^{2}e_{i}
); we prove a martingale CLT for those quadratic forms to deduce the above result. For
e_{j}^{\prime}v_{i}
with
j\neq i
, the leading contribution comes from a linear form
e_{j}^{\prime}We_{i}
, which is a sum of approximately
N^{2}
independent terms and hence by the Lindeberg CLT is asymptotically normal of order
N
. The
\theta
scaling above normalizes it to
O(1)
.

We now state an eigenvector delocalization result. Delocalization means that an eigenvector has no significant mass on any single coordinate. In our context,
v_{i}
is largely supported on the direction
e_{i}
, but we want to ensure it is not too concentrated even within
e_{i}
. The following theorem shows that all coordinates of
v_{i}
are
O_{hp}(1/\sqrt{N})
, up to some logarithmic corrections. In particular, the
\ell^{\infty}
norm of
v_{i}
goes to zero as
N\to\infty
. This is similar in spirit to the delocalization of eigenvectors in an unperturbed Wigner matrix (where typically
\|v_{i}\|_{\infty}=O(N^{-1/2})
), but here we must account for a slower decay due to the perturbation (hence the logarithmic factor appears).

Theorem 2.4

(
Eigenvector delocalization).

Under Assumptions
2.1
-
2.3
, for each
1\leq i\leq k
, as
N\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\|v_{i}-e_{i}\|_{\infty}=O_{hp}\left(\frac{(\log N)^{\xi/4}}{\theta}\right)=o_{hp}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\right).

Thus every coordinate
v_{i}(\ell)
of the eigenvector satisfies
|v_{i}(\ell)|=O_{hp}(N^{-1/2})
, and
\max_{1\leq\ell\leq N}|v_{i}(\ell)|=o_{hp}(N^{-1/2})
. This shows that
v_{i}
is delocalized across of order
N
coordinates.

We now turn to a more functional perspective by considering each eigenvector
v_{i}
as a
function on
[0,1]
. For
1\leq i\leq k
, define the piecewise-constant function

V_{N}^{(i)}:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}
by

(2.6)

V_{N}^{(i)}(t)=\begin{cases}\sqrt{N}\,v_{i}(\ell),&\text{if }\frac{\ell-1}{N}<t\leq\frac{\ell}{N},\quad\ell=1,\dots,N,\\[4.30554pt]
0,&t=0.\end{cases}

This is a convenient way to interpret
v_{i}
as a random element of
L^{2}[0,1]
. Since
v_{i}
is a unit vector,
V_{N}^{(i)}
is an
L^{2}
-normalized function (indeed,
\int_{0}^{1}V_{N}^{(i)}(t)^{2}dt=\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}v_{i}(\ell)^{2}=1
). For large
N
, we expect
V_{N}^{(i)}(t)
to concentrate near the function
h_{i}(t)
.

For
1\leq i\leq k
and
g\in C_{0}^{\infty}(0,1)
, we set

(2.7)

\langle\mathcal{V}_{N}^{(i)},g\rangle:=\theta_{N}\int_{0}^{1}\big(V_{N}^{(i)}(t)-\mathbb{E}[V_{N}^{(i)}(t)]\big)g(t)\,dt.

The scaling
\theta_{N}
is chosen so that
\langle\mathcal{V}_{N}^{(i)},g\rangle
converges to a non-degenerate limit; it comes from the fact that
V_{N}^{(i)}-\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[V_{N}^{(i)}
is of order
1/\theta_{N}
in the
h_{i}
-direction (from Theorem
2.3
). We will consider
\mathcal{V}_{N}^{(i)}
as an element of the dual of the Sobolev space
H^{1}_{0}[0,1]
(the space of
C_{0}^{\infty}
functions with norm given by the Dirichlet energy, see below), which is a subspace of distributions on
[0,1]
. We call
\mathcal{V}_{N}^{(i)}
the eigenvector process (fluctuation field) associated to the top eigenvector.

To describe the scaling limit of
\mathcal{V}_{N}^{(i)}
, it is useful to recall some facts about Sobolev spaces on
[0,1]
. Let
H^{1}_{0}[0,1]
be the Sobolev space of (equivalence classes of) functions on
[0,1]
that vanish at the boundary and have square-integrable first derivative. This is a Hilbert space with inner product

\langle g_{1},g_{2}\rangle_{H^{1}}=\int_{0}^{1}g_{1}^{\prime}(x)g_{2}^{\prime}(x),dx.

Let
\{\phi_{\ell}(x)\}_{\ell\geq 1}
be an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}[0,1]
consisting of eigenfunctions of the Dirichlet Laplacian
-d^{2}/dx^{2}
on
[0,1]
. We have
\phi_{\ell}(0)=\phi_{\ell}(1)=0
, and the corresponding eigenvalues
0<\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}<\cdots
tend to infinity as
\ell\to\infty
. It is well known that
\{\phi_{\ell}\}_{\ell\geq 1}
is also an orthogonal basis for
H^{1}_{0}[0,1]
, with
\|\phi_{\ell}\|_{H^{1}}^{2}=\lambda_{\ell}
. Moreover,
\{\lambda_{\ell}^{-1/2}\phi_{\ell}\}
forms an orthonormal basis of
H^{1}_{0}[0,1]
. The dual space
H^{-1}[0,1]
is defined to be the space of continuous linear functionals on
H^{1}_{0}[0,1]
or, equivalently, the completion of
L^{2}[0,1]
under the norm
\|h\|_{H^{-1}}=\sup_{|g|_{H^{1}}\leq 1}\langle h,\,g\rangle
. It can be characterized in terms of the basis
\{\phi_{\ell}\}_{\ell\geq 1}
: any
h\in H^{-1}
can be expanded as
h=\sum_{\ell\geq 1}a_{\ell}\phi_{\ell}
in the sense of distributions, and

\|h\|_{H^{-1}}^{2}=\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\frac{a_{\ell}^{2}}{\lambda_{\ell}}.

More generally, for any
c>0
, one can define
H^{c}_{0}[0,1]
as the closure of
C_{0}^{\infty}(0,1)
under the norm
\|g\|_{H^{c}}^{2}:=\sum_{\ell\geq 1}a_{\ell}^{2}\lambda_{\ell}^{c}
(where
g=\sum a_{\ell}\phi_{\ell}
); its dual
H^{-c}
has norm
\|h\|_{H^{-c}}^{2}=\sum a_{\ell}^{2}\lambda_{\ell}^{-c}
. Intuitively, larger
c
means a smaller dual space (because
\lambda_{\ell}^{-c}
decays faster), so convergence in
H^{-c}
is a stronger mode of convergence for larger
c
.

Using this framework, we can now state the convergence of the eigenvector process
\mathcal{V}_{N}^{(i)}
. Roughly,
\mathcal{V}_{N}^{(i)}
converges to a limit
\mathcal{V}^{(i)}
in
H^{-d}
for any
d>1/2
. The limit
\mathcal{V}^{(i)}
is a random element of
H^{-d}
which is Gaussian (a centered Gaussian field). In fact,
\mathcal{V}^{(i)}
can be viewed as a variant of Gaussian white noise on
(0,1)
with Dirichlet boundary conditions (zero average), as we explain after the theorem.

Theorem 2.5
.

(
Functional CLT for eigenvector process
) Fix
i\in\{1,\dots,k\}
and let
\mathcal{V}_{N}^{(i)}
be the eigenvector fluctuation field defined in (
2.7
). Under Assumptions
2.1
,
2.2
and
2.3
, the sequence
{\mathcal{V}_{N}^{(i)}}
is tight in
H^{-d}[0,1]
for every
d>\frac{1}{2}
. Moreover,
\mathcal{V}_{N}^{(i)}
converges weakly to a random element
\mathcal{V}^{(i)}
in
H^{-d}[0,1]
(for any
d>1/2
). The limit
\mathcal{V}^{(i)}
is a mean-zero Gaussian random distribution on
(0,1)
characterized by its covariance: for any test functions
g_{1},g_{2}\in C_{0}^{\infty}(0,1)
,

(2.8)

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle\mathcal{V}^{(i)},g_{1}\right\rangle\left\langle\mathcal{V}^{(i)},g_{2}\right\rangle\right]=C_{k}^{(i)}(g_{1},g_{2}),

where
C_{k}^{(i)}(g_{1},g_{2})
is an explicit function given in terms
\{h_{j},f,\alpha_{j}\}_{1\leq j\leq k}
in (
6.18
).

We shall provide an expression for the general case but the case
i=1
and
k=1
seem to be more tractable and for
k\geq 1
, the expression is much more complicated.
Theorem
2.5
states that the fluctuations of the eigenvector
v_{1}
(viewed as a function on
[0,1]
) converge to a Gaussian random distribution whose law is that of a mean-zero Gaussian process with covariance given by an explicit function. This process can be viewed as transformation of the white noise. As a sanity check, one can verify that in the simplest setting (say
k=1
and a flat variance profile
f(x,y)\equiv 1
), the previous results imply that
V_{N}^{(1)}(t)\approx h_{1}(t)
and
\mathcal{V}_{N}^{(1)}
converges to a white noise with unit intensity on
(0,1)
, projected to the subspace of zero-mean distributions. Indeed, in that case
h_{1}(t)
is constant since
e_{1}
is proportional to the all-1’s vector,
e_{1}^{\prime}v_{1}
fluctuates like Gaussian random variable around its mean (Theorem
2.3
) with

\int h_{1}^{2}(x)f(x,y)f(y,z)h_{1}^{2}(z)dxdydz=1.

Putting these together, one recovers that
\langle\mathcal{V}_{N}^{(1)},g\rangle
converges to
\mathcal{N}(0,C_{1}(g)^{2})
, where
C_{1}(g)
is given as below:

C_{1}(g)^{2}=\int_{0}^{1}g^{2}(x)\,dx-\left(\int_{0}^{1}g(x)\,dx\right)^{2}.

Remark 2.2

(Identification of the Limit in the Homogeneous Case)
.

In the special case where
f\equiv 1
and
h\equiv 1
, the limiting fluctuation field
\mathcal{V}^{(1)}(t)
admits a concrete representation as projected white noise:

\mathcal{V}^{(1)}(t)=\xi(t)-\int_{0}^{1}\xi(x)\,dx,

where
\xi(t)
denotes standard white noise on
[0,1]
. This is the projection of white noise onto the orthogonal complement of the constant function
\mathbf{1}\in L^{2}[0,1]
. In other words,
\mathcal{V}(t)
is the unique generalized process satisfying

\langle\mathcal{V}^{(1)},g\rangle=\langle\xi,g\rangle-\left(\int_{0}^{1}g(x)\,dx\right)\left(\int_{0}^{1}\xi(x)\,dx\right),\quad\text{for all }g\in L^{2}[0,1].

The covariance kernel of
\mathcal{V}^{(1)}
is given by

\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{V}^{(1)}(t)\mathcal{V}^{(1)}(s)]=\delta(t-s)-1,

which reflects the fact that the total mass
\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{V}^{(1)}(t)\,dt\equiv 0
almost surely. In particular, for any test function
g\in L^{2}[0,1]
, the variance of the linear functional
\langle\mathcal{V}^{(1)},g\rangle
is

\mathrm{Var}(\langle\mathcal{V}^{(1)},g\rangle)=\int_{0}^{1}g^{2}(t)\,dt-\left(\int_{0}^{1}g(t)\,dt\right)^{2}.

This shows that the eigenvector fluctuation field converges to white noise with the constant mode removed.

The proof of Theorem
2.5
leverages known results about the convergence of random fields to Gaussian Free Field-like distributions. See for example
Camia et al., 2015
and
Sheffield, 2007
for related techniques.

3.
Eigenvalue Fluctuations – Proofs of Main Results

In this section, we present the proofs of the results concerning the outlier eigenvalues of
A_{N}
. We will repeatedly use the assumptions and notation introduced earlier. First, we establish some useful estimates about the base random matrix
W_{N}
. These estimates will be used to control error terms and justify approximations in the proofs of the theorems. The proofs of these lemmas are technical but not too complicated; we defer them to the Appendix. Throughout this section, we assume that Assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
hold.

Lemma 3.1
.

(
Norm bound for
W
) Let
W
be the Wigner-type matrix defined in (
2.1
) satisfying (
2.2
) and with variance profile
f(x,y)
. Then

\|W\|=O_{hp}(\sqrt{N})\,.

This is a standard result since
\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[W_{N}(j,\ell)^{2}]\leq C
uniformly in
j,\ell
, the semicircle law suggests
\|W_{N}\|\leq 2\sqrt{NC}
, and more refined arguments give the above concentration. A proof is provided in the Appendix for completeness.

Lemma
3.1
implies that the bulk of the eigenvalues of
A_{N}=W_{N}+(\text{finite-rank perturbations})
lie in the interval
[-O(\sqrt{N}),O(\sqrt{N})]
with high probability. In particular, any eigenvalues that are of order
\theta\gg\sqrt{N}
must separate from the bulk and these will be the outliers we study.

The next two lemmas give bounds on certain quadratic forms involving
W
. They will be useful for controlling the higher-order terms in expansions. Let

L:=\lfloor\log N\rfloor.

Lemma 3.2
.

(
Bound on moment of quadratic forms
) For any two
N\times 1
deterministic vectors
u
and
v
whose entries are bounded by
N^{-1/2}
in absolute value, there exists a constant
C_{1}>0
such that for all integers
n
with
2\leq n\leq L
,

|\mathbb{E}(u^{\prime}W^{n}v)|\leq(C_{1}\sqrt{N})^{n}.

Lemma 3.3
.

(
Concentration of quadratic forms
) For
u,v
as above, there exists
\eta>1
such that

(3.1)

\displaystyle\max_{1\leq n\leq L}\mathbb{P}\left(\left|u^{\prime}W^{n}v-\mathbb{E}(u^{\prime}W^{n}v)\right|>N^{\frac{n-1}{2}}(\log N)^{\frac{n\xi}{4}}\right)=O(e^{-(\log N)^{\eta}})

This is an adaptation of a result from
Erdős et al., 2013
to our setup with a variance profile.

Combining Lemmas
3.2
and
3.3
, we see that for any fixed power
1\leq n\leq\log N
,
u^{\prime}W^{n}v=\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}(u^{\prime}W^{n}v)+O_{hp}(N^{(n-1)/2}(\log N)^{n\xi/4})
. In particular, taking
n=1
, we have
u^{\prime}Wv=O_{hp}((\log N)^{\xi/4}))
(since
\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}(u^{\prime}Wv)=0
for mean-zero entries in
W
). For
n=2
,
u^{\prime}W^{2}v=\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}(u^{\prime}W^{2}v)+O_{hp}(\sqrt{N}(\log N)^{\xi/2})=O_{hp}(N)
since
\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}(u^{\prime}W^{2}v)=O(N)
by Lemma
3.2
. These will be frequently used estimates.

We now prove the first-order behavior of the outliers and this is formalized in the next theorem.

Theorem 3.1
.

First-Order Behavior of Eigenvalues.
Under Assumptions (
2.1
) and (
2.2
), we have for every
1\leq i\leq k
,

\lambda_{i}(A)=\theta\alpha_{i}\left(1+o_{\mathrm{hp}}(1)\right)\,.

Proof.

Observe that
\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[A_{N}]=\theta uu^{\prime}
where
u:=[\sqrt{\alpha_{1}}e_{1},\ldots,\sqrt{\alpha_{k}}e_{k}]^{\prime}
is an
N\times k
matrix whose columns are the perturbation vectors scaled by
\sqrt{\alpha_{i}}
. The non-zero eigenvalues of
uu^{\prime}
coincide with those of the
k\times k
matrix
u^{\prime}u
, and by Assumption
2.2
we have

u^{\prime}u\to\mathrm{Diag}\left(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{k}\right)

as
N\to\infty
. Thus the eigenvalues of
\frac{1}{\theta}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[A_{N}]=uu^{\prime}
converge to
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}
(and the rest
N-k
eigenvalues are 0). In particular, for large
N
,
\lambda_{i}(\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[A_{N}])=\theta\alpha_{i}+o(\theta)
. Now, by the Weyl inequality, the difference between
\lambda_{i}(A_{N})
and
\lambda_{i}(\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[A_{N}])
can be bounded by the operator norm of the noise matrix:

\displaystyle\left|\lambda_{i}(A_{N})-\lambda_{i}\left(\mathbb{E}(A_{N})\right)\right|\leq\|W_{N}\|=O_{hp}\left(\sqrt{N}\right)\,,

by Lemma
3.1
. Therefore, for each fixed
\varepsilon>0
, with high probability we have

\frac{\lambda_{i}\left(A_{N}\right)}{\theta\alpha_{i}}\in\left(1-\frac{C\sqrt{N}}{\theta\alpha_{i}},1+\frac{C\sqrt{N}}{\theta\alpha_{i}}\right),

for some constant
C>0
. As
N\to\infty
, the quantity
\frac{\sqrt{N}}{\theta}\to 0
by Assumption
2.1
, so the right-hand side interval shrinks to 1. Thus
\lambda_{i}(A_{N})/\theta\alpha_{i}\to 1
in probability, completing the proof.
∎

Having established the location of the outliers to first order, we proceed to analyze their fluctuations. For notational convenience, in the remainder of this section we fix an index
i\in{1,\ldots,k}
and focus on the corresponding eigenvalue
\lambda_{i}(A)
.

Define
\mu:=\lambda_{i}(A)
, and for brevity denote
\mu_{0}:=\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[\lambda_{i}(A)]
which is a deterministic number that is close to
\theta\alpha_{i}
by the previous theorem. We will eventually show that
\mu-\mu_{0}=O_{p}(1)
. Let us also introduce a useful
k\times k
matrix
K=K_{N}
that depends on
W
and
\mu
:

(3.2)

K(j,\ell)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\theta\sqrt{\alpha_{j}\alpha_{\ell}}e_{j}^{\prime}\Big(I-\frac{1}{\mu}W\Big)^{-1}e_{\ell},&\text{if $\|W\|<\mu$},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Note that
\|W\|<\mu
holds with high probability for large
N
.
K
is an effective matrix capturing the interaction between different signal directions
e_{j}
and
e_{\ell}
through the resolvent
(I-\frac{1}{\mu}W)^{-1}
. The diagonal entries
K(j,j)
will turn out to be close to
\theta\alpha_{j}
, reflecting that the
j
-th outlier eigenvalue is near
\theta\alpha_{j}
.

Before proceeding, let us note an immediate consequence of Theorem
3.1
and Lemma
3.1
.

Remark 3.1
.

From Theorem
3.1
we have
\mu=\lambda_{i}(A)\sim\theta\alpha_{i}
for large
N
, with high probability. Meanwhile, Lemma
3.1
says
\|W\|=O_{hp}(\sqrt{N})=o_{hp}(\theta)
by Assumption
2.1
. Combining these, we get

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{W}{\mu}\right\|=O_{hp}\left(\frac{\sqrt{N}}{\theta}\right)=o_{hp}(1)

In particular, for large
N
, the operator
I-\frac{W}{\mu}
is invertible with high probability, which justifies the expansion of the resolvent in a Neumann series.

For the proof of Theorem
2.1
, the strategy is to express
\mu=\lambda_{i}(A)
in terms of the matrix
K
defined above, and then to carefully analyze the fluctuations of
K
. Recall that
\lambda_{i}(A)
is the
i
th largest eigenvalue of
A
.

Lemma 3.4
.

As
N\to\infty
, the matrix
K
satisfies

K(j,l)=\theta\alpha_{j}\left(\one(j=l)+o_{hp}(1)\right),\quad 1\leq j,l\leq k,

and with high probability,

\mu=\lambda_{i}(K).

Proof.

Fix
1\leq j,l\leq k
. On the high-probability event
\left\|\frac{W}{\mu}\right\|<1
, Remark
3.1
implies

\left\|\sum_{n\geq 1}\frac{W^{n}}{\mu^{n}}\right\|=O_{hp}\left(\frac{\|W\|}{\mu}\right)=o_{hp}(1),

so that

\left(I-\frac{W}{\mu}\right)^{-1}=I+o_{hp}(1).

Assumption (
2.2
) gives
\lim_{N\to\infty}e_{j}^{\prime}e_{l}=\one(j=l)
. Therefore,

\frac{K(j,l)}{\theta}=\sqrt{\alpha_{j}\alpha_{l}}\,e_{j}^{\prime}\left(I-\frac{W}{\mu}\right)^{-1}e_{l}=\sqrt{\alpha_{j}\alpha_{l}}\left(\one_{\{j=l\}}+o_{hp}(1)\right),

which simplifies to

V(j,l)=\theta\alpha_{j}\left(\one(j=l)+o_{hp}(1)\right).

For the second claim, the equality
\mu=\lambda_{i}(V)
holds with high probability by an argument identical to that of
(
Chakrabarty et al., 2020
, Lemma 5.2)
using the Gershgorin Circle theorem.
∎

3.1.
Proof of Theorem
2.1
: a sketch

Let us denote
Y_{n}
matrix as follows:

(3.3)

Y_{n}(j,\ell):=\sqrt{\alpha_{j}\alpha_{\ell}}\theta e_{j}^{\prime}W^{n}e_{\ell}.

We briefly sketch the main steps, deferring technical details to the Appendix
A
.

Step 1: Resolvent expansion.

By Theorem
3.1
, the outlier eigenvalue
\mu=\lambda_{i}(A)
is of order
\theta
, while a norm bound on the noise matrix
W
gives
\|W\|=O(\sqrt{N})
. Thus
\big\|\frac{W}{\mu}\big\|=o_{hp}(1)
, and we can invert
I-\frac{W}{\mu}
as a Neumann series. This yields an infinite series representation

(3.4)

K=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mu^{-n}Y_{n}

for an appropriately defined
k\times k
matrix
K
(with entries involving
W^{n}
) such that
\mu=\lambda_{i}(K)
.

Step 2: Truncation (Lemma
A.1
).

We truncate the above series at
n=L=\lfloor\log N\rfloor
. Lemma
A.1
shows that the tail contribution from
n>L
is negligible, giving

\mu=\lambda_{i}\left(\sum_{n=0}^{L}\mu^{-n}Y_{n}\right)+o_{hp}(1)

Step 3: Replacing higher-order terms (Lemma
A.2
).

Next we replace each random matrix
Y_{n}
in the truncated sum for
2\leq n\leq L
by its expectation. A concentration bound (Lemma
3.3
) ensures

\sum_{n=2}^{L}\mu^{-n}\left(Y_{n}-\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[Y_{n}]\right)=o_{hp}(1)

which yields

\mu=\lambda_{i}\left(Y_{0}+\frac{Y_{1}}{\mu}+\sum_{n=2}^{L}\mu^{-n}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[Y_{n}\right]\right)+o_{hp}(1).

Step 4: Deterministic approximation (Lemma
A.3
).

We now introduce a deterministic approximation for
\mu
. Consider the equation

x=\lambda_{i}\left(Y_{0}+\sum_{n=2}^{L}x^{-n}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[Y_{n}\right]\right),

which (by Lemma
A.3
) has a solution
x=\tilde{\mu}
for large
N
. Moreover,
\tilde{\mu}/\theta\to\alpha_{i}
as
N\to\infty
. Intuitively,
\tilde{\mu}
is the eigenvalue obtained by replacing all random quantities in the truncated sum with their deterministic limits or expectations.

Step 5: Bounding the difference (Lemma
A.4
).

We then show that
\mu
is close to
\tilde{\mu}
. In particular, Lemma
A.4
gives

\mu-\tilde{\mu}=O_{hp}\left(\frac{\left\|Y_{1}\right\|}{\theta}+1\right)

implying that
|\mu-\tilde{\mu}|=O_{hp}((\log N)^{\xi/4})
.

Step 6: Extracting the main fluctuation (Lemma
A.5
).

We next isolate the leading random term. Lemma
A.5
shows that

\mu=\bar{\mu}+\frac{1}{\mu}Y_{1}(i,i)+o_{hp}\left(\frac{\left\|Y_{1}\right\|}{\theta}+1\right),

for some deterministic
\bar{\mu}
in the vicinity of
\tilde{\mu}
(in fact,
\bar{\mu}=\lambda_{i}(X)
for a certain
k\times k
matrix
X
built from
\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[Y_{n}]
, and one can check
\bar{\mu}/\theta\to\alpha_{i}
). Here
Y_{1}(i,i)=\alpha_{i}\theta e_{i}^{\prime}We_{i}
is the key random component driving the fluctuation of
\mu
.

Step 7: Final expression.

Finally, since
\bar{\mu}
differs from
\mu_{0}:=\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[\mu]
by an
o(1)
term (one can show
\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[\mu]=\bar{\mu}+o(1)
as
N\to\infty
), we may replace
\bar{\mu}
with
\mu_{0}
in the previous equation without affecting the
o_{p}(1)
error. This yields

(3.5)

\mu-\mu_{0}=\frac{1}{\mu}Y_{1}(i,i)+o_{p}(1)

Also what we have an useful bound which will be frequently used throughout the article.

(3.6)

\mu-\mu_{0}=O_{hp}\left(\frac{\|Y_{1}\|}{\theta}+1\right).

Recalling that
\mu=\lambda_{i}(A)
,
Y_{1}(i,i)=\alpha_{i}\theta e_{i}^{\prime}We_{i}
, and
\mu_{0}=\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[\lambda_{i}(A)]
, we obtain

\lambda_{i}(A)=\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[\lambda_{i}(A)\right]+\frac{\theta\alpha_{i}}{\lambda_{i}(A)}e_{i}^{\prime}We_{i}+o_{p}(1),

as claimed in Theorem
2.1
.
For the joint CLT, we rely on the asymptotic normality of the family
(e_{i}^{\prime}We_{i})_{i=1}^{k}
. First, by a direct covariance computation one finds that for any
1\leq i,j\leq k
,

\mathrm{Cov}\left(e_{i}^{\prime}We_{i},e_{j}^{\prime}We_{j}\right)=\frac{2}{N^{2}}\sum_{1\leq a,b\leq N}h_{i}\left(\frac{a}{N}\right)h_{i}\left(\frac{b}{N}\right)h_{j}\left(\frac{a}{N}\right)h_{j}\left(\frac{b}{N}\right)f\left(\frac{a}{N},\frac{b}{N}\right)

which converges, as
N\to\infty
, to

2\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}h_{i}(x)h_{i}(y)h_{j}(x)h_{j}(y)f(x,y)\,dx\,dy

Moreover, since each
e_{i}^{\prime}We_{i}
is a (normalized) sum of many independent random variables (essentially a sum of
W
’s entries on the
i
th row/column), the multivariate Lindeberg–Lévy CLT applies. Hence as
N\to\infty
,

\left(e_{1}^{\prime}We_{1},e_{2}^{\prime}We_{2},\ldots,e_{k}^{\prime}We_{k}\right)\Rightarrow\left(G_{1},G_{2},\ldots,G_{k}\right),

where
(G_{1},\ldots,G_{k})
is a mean-zero Gaussian vector with the above covariance structure. Finally, by Theorem
3.1
we know
\lambda_{i}(A)
is w.h.p. nonzero (and of order
\theta
), and Theorem
2.1
provides the linear approximation linking
\lambda_{i}(A)-\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[\lambda_{i}(A)]
to
e_{i}^{\prime}We_{i}
. By Slutsky’s theorem, we can therefore deduce that

\left(\lambda_{1}(A)-\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[\lambda_{1}(A)\right],\ldots,\lambda_{k}(A)-\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[\lambda_{k}(A)\right]\right)\Rightarrow\left(G_{1},\ldots,G_{k}\right),

as required for Theorem
2.1
.

4.
Technical Estimates and Series representations for the Eigenvector

In this section, we provide some technical estimates which are crucial for the eigenvector results stated in Section
2.1
, namely
Theorem
2.3
(Gaussian fluctuation of the aligned component),
and Theorem
2.4
(eigenvector
delocalization). Throughout, we assume that Assumptions
2.1
–
2.3
hold. We
begin with two auxiliary lemmas on the eigenvector alignment and on a bound for powers of
W
.

Proof Outline of Theorem
2.3
.
We begin by expressing the deviation
e_{i}^{\prime}v_{i}-\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[e_{i}^{\prime}v_{i}]
in terms of the random matrix
W
. Using the eigenvector perturbation expansion (as in the proof of Theorem
3.1
), one shows that the leading random term is proportional to the quadratic form
e_{i}^{\prime}W^{2}e_{i}-\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[e_{i}^{\prime}W^{2}e_{i}]
. All other terms are of smaller order under Assumption (
2.1
). Since
e_{i}^{\prime}W^{2}e_{i}
is a sum of weakly dependent terms, a martingale central limit theorem (or concentration lemmas) imply that
(e_{i}^{\prime}W^{2}e_{i}-\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[e_{i}^{\prime}W^{2}e_{i}])/\sqrt{N}
converges to a Gaussian law. This yields the asymptotic normality of
(\theta\alpha_{i})^{2}(e_{i}^{\prime}v_{i}-\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[e_{i}^{\prime}v_{i}])/\sqrt{N}
. For
j\neq i
, one similarly expresses
e_{j}^{\prime}v_{i}-\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[e_{j}^{\prime}v_{i}]
in terms of the linear form
e_{j}^{\prime}We_{i}
plus negligible error; then the classical Lindeberg CLT gives the Gaussian limit for
\theta(e_{j}^{\prime}v_{i}-\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[e_{j}^{\prime}v_{i}])
. The detailed argument is given below.

4.1.
Preliminary lemmas for the proof of Theorem

We begin with a few essential estimates from the eigenvalue analysis.

Similar to Remark

Lemma 4.1

With Assumptions

-

Following the arguments in the proof of Theorem

Here we have used the fact that

Next, by Lemma
3.3
and Lemma
3.2
along with Assumption
2.3
, we can bound each term in (

(4.4)

\displaystyle e_{j}^{\prime}\left(I-\frac{W}{\mu}\right)^{-1}e_{l}

(4.5)

\displaystyle=e_{j}^{\prime}e_{l}+O_{hp}\left(\frac{(\log N)^{\xi/4}}{\theta}\right)+O_{hp}\left(\frac{N}{\theta^{2}}\right)+O\left(\frac{N^{3/2}}{\theta^{3}}\right)+o_{hp}\left[\frac{N|\mu-\mu_{0}|}{\theta^{3}}+\frac{\sqrt{N}}{\theta^{2}}\right]

Hence

e_{j}^{\prime}\Bigl(I-\frac{W}{\mu}\Bigr)^{-1}e_{l}=\one(j=l)+o_{hp}\Bigl(\frac{\sqrt{N}}{\theta}\Bigr),

as claimed. The case

∎

In what follows we define

(4.7)

S_{m}^{ij}=\sum_{n=m}^{L}\frac{\mathbb{E}(e_{i}^{\prime}W^{n}e_{j})}{\mu_{0}^{n}}\text{ and }\tilde{S}_{m}^{ij}=\sum_{n=m}^{L}\frac{\mathbb{E}(e_{i}^{\prime}W^{n}e_{j})(n+1)}{\mu_{0}^{n}}

Lemma 4.2
.

Under assumptions
2.1
-
2.3
, as

we have for
j,l\neq i

\displaystyle\Bigl[e_{j}^{\prime}\Bigl(I-\frac{W}{\mu}\Bigr)^{-1}e_{i}\Bigr]\Bigl[e_{l}^{\prime}\Bigl(I-\frac{W}{\mu}\Bigr)^{-1}e_{i}\Bigr]

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}(e_{j}^{\prime}W^{2}e_{i})\,\mathbb{E}(e_{l}^{\prime}W^{2}e_{i})}{\mu_{0}^{4}}+\frac{\mathbb{E}(e_{j}^{\prime}W^{2}e_{i})}{\mu_{0}^{2}}\,S_{3}^{li}

\displaystyle+\frac{\mathbb{E}(e_{l}^{\prime}W^{2}e_{i})}{\mu_{0}^{2}}\,S_{3}^{ji}+S_{3}^{ji}\,S_{3}^{li}+o_{hp}\left(\frac{\sqrt{N}}{\theta^{2}}\right)

Using (

\displaystyle\left[e_{j}^{\prime}\left(I-\frac{W}{\mu}\right)^{-1}e_{i}\right]\left[e_{l}^{\prime}\left(I-\frac{W}{\mu}\right)^{-1}e_{i}\right]

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{e_{j}^{\prime}W^{2}e_{i}\cdot e_{l}^{\prime}W^{2}e_{i}}{\mu^{4}}+\frac{e_{j}^{\prime}W^{2}e_{i}}{\mu^{2}}S_{3}^{li}+\frac{e_{l}^{\prime}W^{2}e_{i}}{\mu^{2}}S_{3}^{ji}+S_{3}^{ji}S_{3}^{li}

(4.8)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad+o_{hp}\left(\frac{N|\mu-\mu_{0}|}{\theta^{3}}+\frac{\sqrt{N}}{\theta^{2}}\right)+o_{hp}\left[\frac{N|\mu-\mu_{0}|}{\theta^{3}}+\frac{\sqrt{N}}{\theta^{2}}\right]^{2}.

Using Lemma
3.3
and Remark
4.1
, we further estimate the first term on the right-hand side of (

(4.9)

\displaystyle\frac{e_{j}^{\prime}W^{2}e_{i}\cdot e_{l}^{\prime}W^{2}e_{i}}{\mu^{4}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbb{E}(e_{j}^{\prime}W^{2}e_{i})\mathbb{E}(e_{l}^{\prime}W^{2}e_{i})}{\mu_{0}^{4}}+O_{hp}\left(\frac{N\sqrt{N}(\log N)^{\xi/2}}{\theta^{4}}\right)+O_{hp}\left(\frac{N^{2}|\mu-\mu_{0}|}{\theta^{5}}\right)

\displaystyle\frac{e_{j}^{\prime}W^{2}e_{i}}{\mu^{2}}S_{3}^{li}

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{\mathbb{E}(e_{j}^{\prime}W^{2}e_{i})\mathbb{E}(e_{l}^{\prime}W^{2}e_{i})}{\mu_{0}^{4}}+\frac{\mathbb{E}(e_{j}^{\prime}W^{2}e_{i})}{\mu_{0}^{2}}S_{3}^{li}+\frac{\mathbb{E}(e_{l}^{\prime}W^{2}e_{i})}{\mu_{0}^{2}}S_{3}^{ji}+S_{3}^{ji}S_{3}^{li}+o_{hp}\left(\frac{\sqrt{N}}{\theta^{2}}\right).

∎

Lemma 4.3

Under Assumptions
2.1
-
2.3
, as

\displaystyle D_{lpp}

\displaystyle=\frac{3\,\mathbb{E}(e_{j}^{\prime}W^{2}e_{i})\mathbb{E}(e_{l}^{\prime}W^{2}e_{i})}{\mu_{0}^{4}}+\frac{\mathbb{E}(e_{l}^{\prime}W^{2}e_{i})}{\mu_{0}^{2}}\tilde{S}_{3}^{pp}+\frac{3\,\mathbb{E}(e_{p}^{\prime}W^{2}e_{p})}{\mu_{0}^{2}}S_{3}^{li}

\displaystyle\quad+e_{l}^{\prime}e_{i}e_{p}^{\prime}e_{p}+e_{p}^{\prime}e_{p}S_{2}^{li}+S_{3}^{li}\tilde{S}_{3}^{pp}.

\displaystyle\left[e_{l}^{\prime}\left(I-\frac{W}{\mu}\right)^{-1}e_{i}\right]\left[e_{p}^{\prime}\left(I-\frac{W}{\mu}\right)^{-2}e_{p}\right]

(4.14)

\displaystyle=\frac{e_{l}^{\prime}We_{i}}{\mu}(e_{p}^{\prime}e_{p})+\frac{e_{l}^{\prime}W^{2}e_{i}}{\mu^{2}}(e_{p}^{\prime}e_{p})+(e_{l}^{\prime}e_{i})(e_{p}^{\prime}e_{p})+\frac{3(e_{l}^{\prime}W^{2}e_{i})(e_{p}^{\prime}W^{2}e_{p})}{\mu^{4}}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{e_{l}^{\prime}W^{2}e_{i}}{\mu^{2}}\tilde{S}_{3}^{pp}+(e_{p}^{\prime}e_{p})S_{3}^{li}+\frac{3(e_{p}^{\prime}W^{2}e_{p})}{\mu^{2}}S_{3}^{li}+S_{3}^{li}\tilde{S}_{3}^{pp}

(4.15)

\displaystyle\quad+o_{hp}\left(\frac{N|\mu-\mu_{0}|}{\theta^{3}}+\frac{\sqrt{N}}{\theta^{2}}\right)+o_{hp}\left(\frac{N|\mu-\mu_{0}|}{\theta^{3}}+\frac{\sqrt{N}}{\theta^{2}}\right)^{2}.

\displaystyle\left[e_{l}^{\prime}\left(I-\frac{W}{\mu}\right)^{-1}e_{i}\right]\left[e_{p}^{\prime}\left(I-\frac{W}{\mu}\right)^{-2}e_{p}\right]

\displaystyle\left[e_{j}^{\prime}\left(I-\frac{W}{\mu}\right)^{-1}e_{i}\right]\left[e_{l}^{\prime}\left(I-\frac{W}{\mu}\right)^{-1}e_{i}\right]\left[e_{p}^{\prime}\left(I-\frac{W}{\mu}\right)^{-2}e_{p}\right]

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
1\leq i\leq k
, and let

denote the normalized eigenvector corresponding to the eigenvalue

Thus, for

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|\bar{R}|\leq o\left(\frac{\sqrt{N}(\log N)^{3\xi/4}}{\theta^{2}}\right)+\mathbb{E}^{1/2}[\bar{R}^{2}]\cdot O\left(e^{-(\log N)^{\eta}}\right).

Since
\rho
and
e_{j}^{\prime}v_{i}
are uniformly bounded and

\mathbb{E}[\widetilde{\Sigma}^{2}]={O}\left(\frac{1}{\theta^{2}}\right),\quad\mathbb{E}[\rho\widetilde{\Sigma}]={O}\left(\frac{1}{\theta^{2}}\right),

we deduce:

\mathbb{E}[\bar{R}^{2}]={O}\left(\frac{1}{\theta^{2}}\right)\Rightarrow\mathbb{E}|\bar{R}|=o\left(\frac{1}{\theta}\right).

Thus, the mean is approximated as:

(5.11)

\mathbb{E}[e_{j}^{\prime}v_{i}]=\rho+o\left(\frac{1}{\theta}\right).

Finally, from (
5.10
) and (
5.11
), we conclude

(5.12)

e_{j}^{\prime}v_{i}-\mathbb{E}[e_{j}^{\prime}v_{i}]=\frac{e_{j}^{\prime}We_{i}}{\mu_{0}Y_{jj}\sqrt{\alpha_{j}}}+o_{hp}\left(\frac{1}{\theta}\right).

We will now analyze the leading contribution, namely the first-order term:

e_{j}^{\prime}We_{i}.

By definition of
e_{j}
and
e_{i}
in terms of the function
h_{j}
and
h_{i}
, we have

\displaystyle e_{j}^{\prime}We_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq a\neq b\leq N}W(a,b)\left[\frac{h_{j}\left(\frac{a}{N}\right)h_{i}\left(\frac{b}{N}\right)}{N}+\frac{h_{i}\left(\frac{a}{N}\right)h_{j}\left(\frac{b}{N}\right)}{N}\right]

(5.13)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{1\leq a\leq N}\frac{W(a,a)h_{j}\left(\frac{a}{N}\right)h_{i}\left(\frac{a}{N}\right)}{N}.

The diagonal contribution vanishes in probability by the weak law of large numbers:

\sum_{1\leq a\leq N}\frac{W(a,a)h_{j}\left(\frac{a}{N}\right)h_{i}\left(\frac{a}{N}\right)}{N}\xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}0.

Now consider the variance of the off-diagonal sum in (
5.13
). Define

\displaystyle S_{N}^{2}:=\mathrm{Var}(e_{j}^{\prime}We_{i})

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq a\neq b\leq N}\mathrm{Var}(W(a,b))\left[\frac{h_{j}\left(\frac{a}{N}\right)h_{i}\left(\frac{b}{N}\right)}{N}+\frac{h_{i}\left(\frac{a}{N}\right)h_{j}\left(\frac{b}{N}\right)}{N}\right]^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq a\neq b\leq N}\left[\frac{h_{j}^{2}\left(\frac{a}{N}\right)h_{i}^{2}\left(\frac{b}{N}\right)}{N^{2}}+\frac{h_{i}^{2}\left(\frac{a}{N}\right)h_{j}^{2}\left(\frac{b}{N}\right)}{N^{2}}\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.+\frac{2h_{j}\left(\frac{a}{N}\right)h_{i}\left(\frac{b}{N}\right)h_{i}\left(\frac{a}{N}\right)h_{j}\left(\frac{b}{N}\right)}{N^{2}}\right]f\left(\frac{a}{N},\frac{b}{N}\right).

As
N\to\infty
, we may pass to the limit using Riemann sums and the boundedness of
f
and
h_{i},h_{j}
, to conclude:

(5.14)

\displaystyle\lim_{N\to\infty}S_{N}^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,1]^{2}}\left[h_{j}(x)h_{i}(y)+h_{i}(x)h_{j}(y)\right]^{2}f(x,y)\,dxdy.

The sum in (
5.13
) consists of a finite sum of independent mean-zero variables with variances given above. Since the fourth moments of
W(a,b)
are uniformly bounded (by assumption A2), Lyapunov’s condition is satisfied. Therefore, by Lyapunov’s central limit theorem, we conclude:

e_{j}^{\prime}We_{i}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{ij}^{2}),

where
\sigma_{ij}^{2}
is given by the right-hand side of (
5.14
).

Observe that

Y_{jj}\to\frac{\alpha_{i}}{\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j}},\qquad\text{and}\qquad\frac{\mu_{0}}{\theta}\to\alpha_{i}.

Hence multiplying both sides by
\theta
gives:

\theta\left(e_{j}^{\prime}v_{i}-\mathbb{E}[e_{j}^{\prime}v_{i}]\right)\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal{N}\left(0,\,\tau_{ij}^{2}\right),

where

\displaystyle\tau_{ij}^{2}=\frac{1}{(\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{j})^{2}\alpha_{j}}\int_{[0,1]^{2}}\left[h_{j}(x)h_{i}(y)+h_{i}(x)h_{j}(y)\right]^{2}f(x,y)\,dx\,dy.

∎

\displaystyle\hskip 18.49988pt+\int_{[0,1]^{2}}g_{p}(x)h_{i}(x)h_{i}(y)h_{l}(y)f(x,y)\,dxdy\Bigg]

\displaystyle-2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq l\leq k\\

\displaystyle\hskip 18.49988pt+\int_{[0,1]^{2}}g_{q}(x)h_{i}(x)h_{i}(y)h_{l}(y)f(x,y)\,dxdy\Bigg]

\displaystyle-2\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq l\leq k\\

\displaystyle+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq l_{1},l_{2}\leq k\\
l_{1},l_{2}\neq i\end{subarray}}\frac{\alpha_{i}^{2}\sqrt{\alpha_{l_{1}}}\sqrt{\alpha_{l_{2}}}}{(\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{l_{1}})(\alpha_{i}-\alpha_{l_{2}})}\left(\int_{0}^{1}g_{p}(x)h_{l_{1}}(x)\,dx\right)\left(\int_{0}^{1}g_{q}(x)h_{l_{2}}(x)\,dx\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 18.49988pt\times\Bigg[\int_{[0,1]^{2}}h_{l_{1}}(x)h_{l_{2}}(x)h_{i}^{2}(y)f(x,y)\,dxdy+\int_{[0,1]^{2}}h_{l_{1}}(x)h_{i}(x)h_{i}(y)h_{l_{2}}(y)f(x,y)\,dxdy\Bigg].

Fix any
\varepsilon>0

d>\frac{1}{2}
. The finite-dimensional convergence above ensures that for any
l\in\mathbb{N}
and test functions
g_{1},g_{2},\ldots,g_{l}\in\mathcal{H}^{d}_{0}

\mathcal{V}_{N}

(1)

in

(2)

Step 4: Tightness.

Tightness depends on the following lemma.

Lemma 6.1

(Uniform bound for Fourier modes)
.

There exists a constant
C>0
such that

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\sup_{j\geq 1}\mathbb{E}\left(\langle\mathcal{V}^{(i)}_{N},\phi_{j}\rangle^{2}\right)<C.

Proof of Lemma
6.1
.

We use the representation from Step 1 with
k=1
and
\alpha_{1}=1
. Fix
g=\phi_{j}\in C^{\infty}[0,1]
for some
j\geq 1
, and define
\underline{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
as in (
6.1
). Then, from Step 1 we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\langle\mathcal{V}^{(i)}_{N},\phi_{j}\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\theta\sqrt{N}(\underline{g}^{\prime}v_{i}-\mathbb{E}[\underline{g}^{\prime}v_{i}])\right)^{2}=N\theta^{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}\left(\underline{g}^{\prime}v_{i}-\mathbb{E}[\underline{g}^{\prime}v_{i}]\right)^{2}

(6.19)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\mathbb{E}(e^{\prime}v_{i})\frac{\theta}{\mu^{2}}\underline{g}^{\prime}We-\mathbb{E}(e^{\prime}v_{i})\frac{\theta^{2}}{\mu^{3}}\underline{g}^{\prime}e\cdot e^{\prime}We+o_{hp}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}\theta}\right)\right)^{2}N\theta^{2}.

Using the uniform bound
\sup_{j\geq 1}\sup_{x\in[0,1]}|\phi_{j}(x)|\leq\sqrt{2}
, we note that the error terms denoted by
\mathbf{R}
and
\mathbf{R}^{2}
in Step 1 are independent of
j
, uniformly over all
j\geq 1
.

Now applying the variance expressions from (
6.9
)–(
6.11
), and the error control from (
6.15
) and
\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[\mathbf{R}^{2}]
, we find that for all
j\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\langle\mathcal{V}^{(i)}_{N},\phi_{j}\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i_{1}=1}^{N}h\left(\frac{i_{1}}{N}\right)\right]^{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}^{2}(e^{\prime}v)\cdot\frac{\theta^{6}}{\bar{\mu}^{6}}\cdot\mathbb{E}(e^{\prime}We)^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\theta^{4}}{\bar{\mu}^{4}}\mathbb{E}^{2}(e^{\prime}v)\cdot\frac{2}{N^{2}}\left[\sum_{i_{1},i_{2}=1}^{N}h^{2}\left(\frac{i_{2}}{N}\right)f\left(\frac{i_{1}}{N},\frac{i_{2}}{N}\right)+\sum_{i_{1},i_{2}=1}^{N}h\left(\frac{i_{1}}{N}\right)h\left(\frac{i_{2}}{N}\right)f\left(\frac{i_{1}}{N},\frac{i_{2}}{N}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\quad+2\left[\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}h\left(\frac{i}{N}\right)\right]\cdot\mathbb{E}^{2}(e^{\prime}v)\cdot\frac{\theta^{5}}{\bar{\mu}^{5}}\cdot\frac{1}{N^{2}}\left[\sum_{i_{1},i_{2}=1}^{N}h\left(\frac{i_{1}}{N}\right)h^{2}\left(\frac{i_{2}}{N}\right)f\left(\frac{i_{1}}{N},\frac{i_{2}}{N}\right)\right.

(6.20)

\displaystyle\hskip 40.00006pt\left.+\sum_{i_{1},i_{2}=1}^{N}h^{2}\left(\frac{i_{1}}{N}\right)h\left(\frac{i_{2}}{N}\right)f\left(\frac{i_{1}}{N},\frac{i_{2}}{N}\right)\right]+o\left(\frac{1}{N\theta^{2}}\right).

Taking the limit as
N\to\infty
, we conclude:

\displaystyle\limsup_{N\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\langle\mathcal{V}^{(i)}_{N},\phi_{j}\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 4\left(\int_{0}^{1}h(x)\,dx\right)^{2}\cdot\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}h^{2}(x)h^{2}(y)f(x,y)\,dxdy

\displaystyle\quad+2\left[\int_{0}^{1}h^{2}(y)f(x,y)\,dxdy+\int_{0}^{1}h(x)h(y)f(x,y)\,dxdy\right]

(6.21)

\displaystyle\quad+4\left(\int_{0}^{1}h(x)\,dx\right)\cdot\int_{0}^{1}h(x)h^{2}(y)f(x,y)\,dxdy.

All terms are bounded uniformly in
j
, and hence we conclude:

\sup_{N\geq 1}\sup_{j\geq 1}\mathbb{E}\langle\mathcal{V}^{(i)}_{N},\phi_{j}\rangle^{2}<\infty.

This completes the proof.
∎

Using Lemma
6.1
, the Monotone Convergence Theorem, and the definition of the
\mathcal{H}^{-d}
-norm via the orthonormal basis
\{\phi_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}
, we obtain:

(6.22)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\mathcal{V}_{N}^{(i)}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{-d}}^{2}=\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{\mathbb{E}\langle\mathcal{V}_{N}^{(i)},\phi_{j}\rangle^{2}}{j^{2d}}\leq C\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{1}{j^{2d}}<\infty.

To conclude tightness, we construct a compact subset of
\mathcal{H}^{-d}
with high probability content. The following compact embedding is standard (see
Cipriani et al., 2019
)

Fact 6.1

(Rellich’s Theorem)
.

For
c_{1}<c_{2}
, the embedding
\mathcal{H}^{-c_{1}}\hookrightarrow\mathcal{H}^{-c_{2}}
is compact. In particular, for any
R>0
, the closed ball
\overline{B_{\mathcal{H}^{-c_{1}}}(0,R)}
is compact in
\mathcal{H}^{-c_{2}}
.

Applying this with
d>d^{\prime}>\frac{1}{2}
and using Markov’s inequality, we get:

(6.23)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{V}_{N}^{(i)}\notin\overline{B_{\mathcal{H}^{-d^{\prime}}}(0,R)}\right)\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}\|\mathcal{V}_{N}^{(i)}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{-d^{\prime}}}^{2}}{R^{2}}.

Choosing
R=\sqrt{\frac{C\sum_{j\geq 1}j^{-2d^{\prime}}}{\varepsilon}}
ensures that the above probability is at most
\varepsilon
, thereby establishing tightness of
\mathcal{V}_{N}
in
\mathcal{H}^{-d}
for any
d>\frac{1}{2}
.

Step 6: Uniqueness of the limit

Let
\mathcal{E}_{g}:\mathcal{H}^{-1}\to\mathbb{R}
denote the evaluation functional defined by
\mathcal{E}_{g}(h):=\langle h,g\rangle
for
g\in\mathcal{H}_{0}^{1}
. For
m\in\mathbb{N}
and test functions
f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}\in\mathcal{H}_{0}^{1}
, define the vector-valued map:

\mathcal{E}_{f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}}(h):=\left(\langle h,f_{1}\rangle,\ldots,\langle h,f_{m}\rangle\right).

Since
\mathcal{H}_{0}^{1}
is a separable Hilbert space, let
\{g_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
be a countable dense subset (we may assume
g_{n}\in C_{0}^{\infty}(0,1)
without loss of generality). The metric topology on
\mathcal{H}^{-1}
is generated by sets of the form:

B_{\mathcal{H}^{-1}}(h_{0},\varepsilon)=\bigcup_{k\geq 1}\bigcap_{n\geq 1}\mathcal{E}_{g_{n}}^{-1}\left(-\infty,\langle h_{0},g_{n}\rangle+\varepsilon-\frac{1}{k}\right].

This implies that the Borel
\sigma
-algebra is generated by evaluations against smooth test functions:

(6.24)

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{H}^{-1}}=\sigma\left(\mathcal{E}_{f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}}:f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}\in C_{0}^{\infty}(0,1),\ m\geq 1\right).

Now, suppose
P
is any weak limit of the sequence of laws
P_{N}
of
\mathcal{V}_{N}
in
\mathcal{H}^{-d}
. Then, there exists a subsequence
N_{m}\to\infty
such that

P_{N_{m}}\Rightarrow P\quad\text{in }\mathcal{H}^{-d}.

By the Continuous Mapping Theorem, for any finite collection
f_{1},\ldots,f_{l}\in C_{0}^{\infty}(0,1)
,

P_{N_{m}}\circ\mathcal{E}_{f_{1},\ldots,f_{l}}^{-1}\Rightarrow P\circ\mathcal{E}_{f_{1},\ldots,f_{l}}^{-1}.

From the previous steps we have shown that the left-hand side converges to a multivariate Gaussian:

P_{N_{m}}\circ\mathcal{E}_{f_{1},\ldots,f_{l}}^{-1}\Rightarrow\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma),

where
\Sigma
is the covariance structure given explicitly in (
6.18
). Therefore, we conclude:

(6.25)

\displaystyle P\circ\mathcal{E}_{f_{1},\ldots,f_{l}}^{-1}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma).

The same argument holds for any other limit point
Q
, so:

(6.26)

\displaystyle Q\circ\mathcal{E}_{f_{1},\ldots,f_{l}}^{-1}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma).

Finally, since the Borel
\sigma
-algebra is generated by
\{\mathcal{E}_{g}:g\in C_{0}^{\infty}(0,1)\}
(see (
6.24
)) and all finite-dimensional marginals agree, the
\pi
-
\lambda
Theorem implies that
P=Q
. Hence, the limiting distribution is unique, and the sequence
\mathcal{V}_{N}
converges in law to a mean-zero Gaussian element of
\mathcal{H}^{-d}
.

This completes the proof.

∎

.

is given, using (

), (

7.
Martingale CLT

The next result establishes a central limit theorem for the quadratic form
e_{i}^{\prime}W^{2}e_{i}
, which plays a crucial role in the proof of eigenvector alignment. Beyond its immediate application, this theorem is of independent interest: it provides a clean example of the martingale CLT applied to structured quadratic forms of random matrices with inhomogeneous variance profiles. Moreover, the argument generalizes naturally to higher matrix powers
W^{m}
, and we expect this technique to have further uses in analyzing functionals of inhomogeneous random graphs and related spectral statistics.

Theorem 7.1
.

Martingale Central Limit Theorem.
For all
i
such that
1\leq i\leq k
, we have

\frac{e_{i}^{\prime}W^{2}e_{i}-\mathbb{E}(e_{i}^{\prime}W^{2}e_{i})}{\sqrt{N}}\Rightarrow N\bigg(0,\sigma_{2}^{2}\bigg)\,,

where

\sigma_{2}^{2}=2\int_{x,y\in[0,1]^{3}}h_{i}^{2}(x)f(x,y)f(y,z)h_{i}^{2}(z)\,dx\,dy\,dz.

Proof of Theorem
7.1
.

We fix an index
i
with
1\leq i\leq k
, and for notational simplicity denote
e=e_{i}
and
h=h_{i}
. Our goal is to study the fluctuations of the quadratic form

T_{N}\;=\;e^{\prime}W^{2}e\;=\;e_{i}^{\prime}W^{2}e_{i}.

We begin by decomposing
T_{N}
into sums that reveal a martingale structure. Expanding
e^{\prime}W^{2}e
in terms of the matrix entries yields

\displaystyle T_{N}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{N}\sum_{r=1}^{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p<r\\
q\neq p,r\end{subarray}}W_{pq}W_{qr}\,h\Bigl(\frac{p}{N}\Bigr)\,h\Bigl(\frac{r}{N}\Bigr)

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{N}\sum_{p=1}^{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r=1\\
r\neq p\end{subarray}}^{N}W_{pp}W_{pr}\,h\Bigl(\frac{p}{N}\Bigr)\,h\Bigl(\frac{r}{N}\Bigr)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{p,q=1}^{N}W_{pq}^{2}\,h^{2}\Bigl(\frac{p}{N}\Bigr).

Here we have used symmetry of the summation to combine the terms
W_{pq}W_{qr}
for
p<r
with those for
r<p
, hence the factor
2
. We denote these three sums by
M_{N}
,
A_{N}
, and
B_{N}
respectively, so that

(7.1)

T_{N}=\frac{2}{N}M_{N}+\frac{2}{N}A_{N}+\frac{1}{N}B_{N}.

Our strategy is to show that
M_{N}
is the dominant term contributing to Gaussian fluctuations, while the terms
A_{N}
and
B_{N}
are of smaller order. Thus we focus on analyzing
M_{N}
first.

Martingale Decomposition of
M_{N}

Recall that

M_{N}=\sum_{r=1}^{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p<r\\
q\neq p,r\end{subarray}}W_{pq}W_{qr}\,h\Bigl(\frac{p}{N}\Bigr)\,h\Bigl(\frac{r}{N}\Bigr).

To expose its martingale structure, we introduce partial sums. For each
r=0,1,\dots,N
, define

M_{N,r}=\sum_{u=1}^{r}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p<u\\
q\neq p,u\end{subarray}}W_{pq}W_{qu}\,h\Bigl(\frac{p}{N}\Bigr)\,h\Bigl(\frac{u}{N}\Bigr),

with the convention
M_{N,0}=0
. Notice that
M_{N,r}
accumulates the contributions up to the index
u=r
. In particular,
M_{N,N}=M_{N}
. Writing
M_{N}
as a telescoping sum,

M_{N}=M_{N,N}=\sum_{r=1}^{N}(M_{N,r}-M_{N,r-1}).

We will show that the sequence
(M_{N,r})_{r=0}^{N}
is a martingale with respect to an increasing filtration.

Let
\mathcal{F}_{r}=\sigma(W_{pq}:1\leq p\leq q\leq r)
be the sigma-field generated by the matrix entries in the first
r
rows and columns. In particular,
\mathcal{F}_{r-1}
contains all randomness up to index
r-1
.
Observe that
M_{N,r}-M_{N,r-1}
can be written as

M_{N,r}-M_{N,r-1}=\sum_{p<r}\sum_{q\leq r-1,q\neq p}W_{pq}W_{qr}h(p/N)h(r/N).

Here, for each term,
W_{pq}
(with
p,q<r
) is
\mathcal{F}_{r-1}
-measurable, whereas
W_{qr}
(which involves the new index
r
) is independent of
\mathcal{F}_{r-1}
and has mean zero. Therefore
\mathbb{E}\left[M_{N,r}-M_{N,r-1}\mid\mathcal{F}_{r-1}\right]=0
, so
\left(M_{N,r},\mathcal{F}_{r}\right)_{r=0}^{N}
is a martingale.

Conditional Variance Computation.

We next compute the conditional second moment of the martingale increments. For each
r=1,\dots,N
, consider

\mathbb{E}\bigl[(M_{N,r}-M_{N,r-1})^{2}\,\big|\;\mathcal{F}_{r-1}\bigr].

Expanding the square and noting that the
W_{pq}
are independent with mean zero, we obtain

(7.2)

\mathbb{E}[(M_{N,r}-M_{N,r-1})^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{r-1}]=\mathrm{Var}\Bigl(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p<r\\
q\neq p,r\end{subarray}}W_{pq}W_{qr}h\Bigl(\frac{p}{N}\Bigr)h\Bigl(\frac{r}{N}\Bigr)\Bigm|\mathcal{F}_{r-1}\Bigr).

This variance can be split into two parts depending on whether the index
q
is at most
r-1
or strictly greater than
r-1
. Decomposing, we get

\displaystyle\eqref{eq:condvar}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Var}\Bigl(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p<r,\,q\neq p\\
q\leq r-1\end{subarray}}W_{pq}W_{qr}h\bigl(\tfrac{p}{N}\bigr)h\bigl(\tfrac{r}{N}\bigr)\Bigm|\mathcal{F}_{r-1}\Bigr)

\displaystyle+\mathrm{Var}\Bigl(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p<r,\,q\neq p\\
q>r-1\end{subarray}}W_{pq}W_{qr}h\bigl(\tfrac{p}{N}\bigr)h\bigl(\tfrac{r}{N}\bigr)\Bigm|\mathcal{F}_{r-1}\Bigr)

\displaystyle+2\,\mathrm{Cov}\Bigl(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p<r,\,q\neq p\\
q\leq r-1\end{subarray}}W_{pq}W_{qr}h\bigl(\tfrac{p}{N}\bigr)h\bigl(\tfrac{r}{N}\bigr),\,\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p<r,\,q\neq p\\
q>r-1\end{subarray}}W_{pq}W_{qr}h\bigl(\tfrac{p}{N}\bigr)h\bigl(\tfrac{r}{N}\bigr)\Bigm|\mathcal{F}_{r-1}\Bigr).

Splitting the sum into the cases
q<r
and
q>r
(where for
q<r
,
W_{pq}
is
\mathcal{F}_{r-1}
–measurable and
W_{qr}
is independent, and for
q>r
, both
W_{pq}
and
W_{qr}
are independent of
\mathcal{F}_{r-1}
) and computing variances, one obtains:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(M_{N,r}-M_{N,r-1}\right)^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{r-1}\right]=h^{2}

\displaystyle\left(\frac{r}{N}\right)\left[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q=1\\
r-1\end{subarray}}^{r-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p=1\\
p\neq q\end{subarray}}^{r-1}W_{pq}^{2}h^{2}\left(\frac{p}{N}\right)f\left(\frac{q}{N},\frac{r}{N}\right)\right.

\displaystyle+\sum_{q=1}^{r-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p,p^{\prime}=1\\
p,p^{\prime}\neq q\end{subarray}}^{r-1}W_{pq}W_{p^{\prime}q}h\left(\frac{p}{N}\right)h\left(\frac{p^{\prime}}{N}\right)f\left(\frac{q}{N},\frac{r}{N}\right)

\displaystyle\left.+\sum_{p=1}^{r-1}\sum_{q=r+1}^{N}h^{2}\left(\frac{p}{N}\right)f\left(\frac{p}{N},\frac{q}{N}\right)f\left(\frac{q}{N},\frac{r}{N}\right)\right].

For convenience, define

V_{N}\;=\;N^{-3}\sum_{r=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\bigl[(M_{N,r}-M_{N,r-1})^{2}\mid\mathcal{F}_{r-1}\bigr].

We will show that
V_{N}
converges in probability to a constant, which will be the limiting variance of the martingale.

Asymptotic Variance of the Martingale.

We first compute the expected value of
V_{N}
. Taking expectation and noting that the
W_{pq}
in
V_{N}
appear in squared terms, one obtains

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[V_{N}]

\displaystyle=N^{-3}\sum_{r=1}^{N}\Bigl[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p<r\\
q<r,\,q\neq p\end{subarray}}h^{2}\Bigl(\tfrac{p}{N}\Bigr)h^{2}\Bigl(\tfrac{r}{N}\Bigr)\,f\Bigl(\tfrac{p}{N},\tfrac{q}{N}\Bigr)\,f\Bigl(\tfrac{q}{N},\tfrac{r}{N}\Bigr)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p<p^{\prime}<r\\
q<r,\,q\neq p,p^{\prime}\end{subarray}}h\Bigl(\tfrac{p}{N}\Bigr)h\Bigl(\tfrac{p^{\prime}}{N}\Bigr)h^{2}\Bigl(\tfrac{r}{N}\Bigr)\,f\Bigl(\tfrac{p}{N},\tfrac{q}{N}\Bigr)\,f\Bigl(\tfrac{q}{N},\tfrac{r}{N}\Bigr)

\displaystyle+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p<r\\
q>r\end{subarray}}h^{2}\Bigl(\tfrac{p}{N}\Bigr)h^{2}\Bigl(\tfrac{r}{N}\Bigr)\,f\Bigl(\tfrac{p}{N},\tfrac{q}{N}\Bigr)\,f\Bigl(\tfrac{q}{N},\tfrac{r}{N}\Bigr)\Bigr].

As
N\to\infty
, Riemann sum approximations show that this sum converges to an integral

\mathbb{E}[V_{N}]\;\longrightarrow\;\frac{1}{2}\int_{[0,1]^{3}}h^{2}(x)\,f(x,y)\,f(y,z)\,h^{2}(z)\,dx\,dy\,dz.

The factor
1/2
arises from accounting for the two symmetric sums involving
p<r<q
and
p<q<r
.

We now verify that
{\rm Var}(V_{N})
tends to zero. First, note that for
p_{1}<q_{1}
and
p_{2}<p_{3}
with
q_{2}\neq p_{2},p_{3}
, the covariance

{\rm Cov}\bigl(W_{p_{1}q_{1}}^{2},\;W_{p_{2}q_{2}}W_{p_{3}q_{2}}\bigr)=0,

since distinct entries of
W
are independent. Hence, by expanding
{\rm Var}(V_{N})
one obtains

\displaystyle\frac{N^{6}}{4}{\rm Var}(V_{N})

\displaystyle={\rm Var}\Bigl(\sum_{r=1}^{N}\sum_{p<q<r}W_{pq}^{2}\,h^{2}\bigl(\tfrac{p}{N}\bigr)\,h^{2}\bigl(\tfrac{r}{N}\bigr)\,f\bigl(\tfrac{q}{N},\tfrac{r}{N}\bigr)\Bigr)

\displaystyle+{\rm Var}\Bigl(\sum_{r=1}^{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p<p^{\prime}<r\\
q<r,\,q\neq p,p^{\prime}\end{subarray}}W_{pq}W_{p^{\prime}q}\,h\bigl(\tfrac{p}{N}\bigr)h\bigl(\tfrac{p^{\prime}}{N}\bigr)\,h^{2}\bigl(\tfrac{r}{N}\bigr)\,f\bigl(\tfrac{q}{N},\tfrac{r}{N}\bigr)\Bigr).

We bound each term separately. For the first term, re-indexing gives

{\rm Var}\Bigl(\sum_{r=1}^{N}\sum_{p<q<r}W_{pq}^{2}\,h^{2}\bigl(\tfrac{p}{N}\bigr)\,h^{2}\bigl(\tfrac{r}{N}\bigr)\,f\bigl(\tfrac{q}{N},\tfrac{r}{N}\bigr)\Bigr)={\rm Var}\Bigl(\sum_{1\leq p<q<N}h^{2}\bigl(\tfrac{p}{N}\bigr)\,W_{pq}^{2}\Bigl(\sum_{r=q+1}^{N}h^{2}\bigl(\tfrac{r}{N}\bigr)f\bigl(\tfrac{q}{N},\tfrac{r}{N}\bigr)\Bigr)\Bigr).

Using
\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[W_{pq}^{4}]\leq 4^{4B}
and boundedness of
f,h
, we obtain

\displaystyle{\rm Var}\Bigl(\sum_{r=1}^{N}\sum_{p<q<r}W_{pq}^{2}\,h^{2}\bigl(\tfrac{p}{N}\bigr)\,h^{2}\bigl(\tfrac{r}{N}\bigr)\,f\bigl(\tfrac{q}{N},\tfrac{r}{N}\bigr)\Bigr)

\displaystyle\quad\leq 4^{4B}\|f\|_{\infty}^{4}\|h\|_{\infty}^{8}\sum_{1\leq p<q<N}(N-q)^{2}=4^{4B}\|f\|_{\infty}^{4}\|h\|_{\infty}^{8}\sum_{q=1}^{N-1}(q-1)(N-q)^{2}

(7.3)

\displaystyle\quad=O(N^{4}).

For the second term, one checks that non-zero contributions arise only when indices match suitably. Note that, for
p_{1},p_{2},p_{3},p_{4},q,q^{\prime}
such that
p_{1}<p_{2}
,
p_{3}<p_{4}
,
q\neq p_{1},p_{2}
and
q^{\prime}\neq p_{3},p_{4}
, we can have a non-zero contribution from
{\rm Cov}(W_{p_{1}q}W_{p_{2}q},W_{p_{3}q^{\prime}}W_{p_{4}q^{\prime}})
when

p_{1}=p_{3},p_{2}=p_{4},q=q^{\prime}\,\quad\text{or}\,\quad p_{1}=p_{4},p_{3}=p_{2},q=q^{\prime}\,.

Arguing similarly yields

\displaystyle{\rm Var}\Bigl(\sum_{r=1}^{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p<p^{\prime}<r\\
q<r,\,q\neq p,p^{\prime}\end{subarray}}W_{pq}W_{p^{\prime}q}\,h\bigl(\tfrac{p}{N}\bigr)h\bigl(\tfrac{p^{\prime}}{N}\bigr)\,h^{2}\bigl(\tfrac{r}{N}\bigr)\,f\bigl(\tfrac{q}{N},\tfrac{r}{N}\bigr)\Bigr)

\displaystyle\quad\leq 2\,\|f\|_{\infty}^{4}\|h\|_{\infty}^{8}\sum_{p<p^{\prime}<N,\;q<N}(N-\max\{p^{\prime},q\})^{2}

\displaystyle\quad=2\,\|f\|_{\infty}^{4}\|h\|_{\infty}^{8}N^{2}\sum_{p<p^{\prime}<N,\;q<N}\Bigl(1-\frac{p^{\prime}\vee q}{N}\Bigr)^{2}

(7.4)

\displaystyle\quad\sim N^{5}\int_{0}^{1}\!\!\int_{0}^{1}\!\!\int_{0}^{1}(1-\max\{y,z\})^{2}\one(x<y)\,dx\,dy\,dz=O(N^{5}).

Combining (
7.3
) and (
7.4
), we conclude that

\lim_{N\to\infty}{\rm Var}(V_{N})=0.

Hence
V_{N}
converges in probability to its mean limit:

V_{N}\;\xrightarrow{P}\;\frac{1}{2}\int_{[0,1]^{3}}h^{2}(x)\,f(x,y)\,f(y,z)\,h^{2}(z)\,dx\,dy\,dz.

This identifies the limiting conditional variance of the martingale increments.

Verification of the Lindeberg Condition

To apply the martingale central limit theorem, we must verify a Lindeberg-type condition. Set

Y_{N,r}\;=\;N^{-3/2}\,(M_{N,r}-M_{N,r-1}),\quad r=1,\dots,N,

and extend
Y_{N,r}=0
for
r>N
. Then

\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}Y_{N,r}=\frac{M_{N}}{N^{3/2}}.

We already have from the martingale property that
\mathbb{E}[Y_{N,r}|\mathcal{F}_{r-1}]=0
and

\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}[Y_{N,r}^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{r-1}]

\displaystyle=N^{-3}\sum_{r=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}[(M_{N,r}-M_{N,r-1})^{2}|\mathcal{F}_{r-1}]\xrightarrow{P}\frac{1}{2}\int_{[0,1]^{3}}h^{2}(x)\,f(x,y)\,f(y,z)\,h^{2}(z)\,dx\,dy\,dz.

Thus the triangular array
(Y_{N,r})
satisfies the martingale variance condition. It remains to check the Lindeberg condition:

\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\bigl[Y_{N,r}^{2}\,\mathbf{1}(|Y_{N,r}|>\varepsilon)\bigr]\;\to\;0\quad\text{for each }\varepsilon>0.

Observe that by Minkowski’s inequality we have

\mathbb{E}^{1/4}[(M_{N,r}-M_{N,r-1})^{4}]\;\leq\;\mathbb{E}^{1/4}[Z_{N1}^{4}]\;+\;\mathbb{E}^{1/4}[Z_{N2}^{4}],

where we split the sum in
M_{N,r}-M_{N,r-1}
into the two parts according to
q\leq r-1
or
q>r-1
:

Z_{N1}\;=\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p<r\\
q\leq r-1,\,q\neq p\end{subarray}}W_{pq}W_{qr}\,h\Bigl(\tfrac{p}{N}\Bigr)h\Bigl(\tfrac{r}{N}\Bigr),\quad Z_{N2}\;=\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p<r\\
q>r-1\end{subarray}}W_{pq}W_{qr}\,h\Bigl(\tfrac{p}{N}\Bigr)h\Bigl(\tfrac{r}{N}\Bigr).

One shows by combinatorial estimates and the bounded moments of
W_{pq}
that

\mathbb{E}[Z_{N1}^{4}]=O(N^{4}),\qquad\mathbb{E}[Z_{N2}^{4}]=O(N^{4}).

It follows that
\mathbb{E}[(M_{N,r}-M_{N,r-1})^{4}]=O(N^{4})
uniformly in
r
. Consequently, for any fixed
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\sum_{r=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}\bigl[Y_{N,r}^{2}\,\mathbf{1}(|Y_{N,r}|>\varepsilon)\bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon^{-2}\sum_{r=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}[Y_{N,r}^{4}]=\frac{\varepsilon^{-2}}{N^{6}}\sum_{r=1}^{N}\mathbb{E}[(M_{N,r}-M_{N,r-1})^{4}]

\displaystyle=\frac{\varepsilon^{-2}}{N^{6}}\times O(N^{5})\;\longrightarrow\;0\quad\text{as }N\to\infty.

Thus the Lindeberg condition holds, and we can apply the martingale central limit theorem, see for example
(
Billingsley, 2017
, Theorem 35.12)
. We conclude that

\sum_{r=1}^{N}Y_{N,r}\;\;=\;\;\frac{M_{N}}{N^{3/2}}\;\xrightarrow{d}\;N\Bigl(0,\sigma^{\prime 2}\Bigr),

where

\sigma^{\prime 2}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{[0,1]^{3}}h^{2}(x)\,f(x,y)\,f(y,z)\,h^{2}(z)\,dx\,dy\,dz.

This establishes that the scaled martingale term
M_{N}
converges in distribution to a mean-zero normal with variance
\sigma^{\prime 2}
.

Negligibility of Remaining Terms.

It remains to show that the other terms
A_{N}
and
B_{N}
in the decomposition (
7.1
) are of smaller order than
M_{N}
and do not affect the limit. Recall

A_{N}=\sum_{p=1}^{N}\sum_{r\neq p}W_{pp}W_{pr}\,h\Bigl(\tfrac{p}{N}\Bigr)h\Bigl(\tfrac{r}{N}\Bigr),\qquad B_{N}=\sum_{p,q=1}^{N}W_{pq}^{2}\,h^{2}\Bigl(\tfrac{p}{N}\Bigr).

One can directly compute (or bound) their variances. Using the boundedness of moments and summing over the index ranges yields

\mathrm{Var}(A_{N})=O(N^{2}),\qquad\mathrm{Var}(B_{N})=O(N^{2}).

Since we are normalizing by
N^{3/2}
for the central limit theorem, these terms are negligible:
\mathrm{Var}(A_{N}/N^{3/2})=O(N^{-1})\to 0
and similarly for
B_{N}
. Hence

\frac{2}{N}A_{N}+\frac{1}{N}B_{N}=o_{P}(N^{1/2}),

in the sense that it vanishes after scaling by
\sqrt{N}
.

Putting everything together, from the decomposition we have

\frac{T_{N}-\mathbb{E}[T_{N}]}{\sqrt{N}}=\frac{2M_{N}}{N^{3/2}}+\frac{2(A_{N}-\mathbb{E}[A_{N}])}{N^{3/2}}+\frac{B_{N}-\mathbb{E}[B_{N}]}{N^{3/2}}.

We have shown that
2M_{N}/N^{3/2}
converges in distribution to
N(0,4\sigma^{\prime 2})
, while the remaining terms vanish in probability. Therefore, by Slutsky’s theorem,

\frac{T_{N}-\mathbb{E}[T_{N}]}{\sqrt{N}}\;\xrightarrow{d}\;N\Bigl(0,4\sigma^{\prime 2}\Bigr).

Recalling the definition of
\sigma^{\prime 2}
, we see that
4\sigma^{\prime 2}
equals

\sigma_{2}^{2}=2\int_{[0,1]^{3}}h^{2}(x)\,f(x,y)\,f(y,z)\,h^{2}(z)\,dx\,dy\,dz,

which matches the variance stated in Theorem
7.1
. This completes the proof of the Martingale Central Limit Theorem for the quadratic form
e_{i}^{\prime}W^{2}e_{i}
.

∎

Appendix A
Eigenvalue CLT: Proof of
2.1

The proof of Theorem
2.1
follows along the lines of
Chakrabarty et al., 2020
so we provide the details in the appendix. Some of the estimates are crucial in the results about the eigenvectors so we need to reprove some of the crucial lemmas from
Chakrabarty et al., 2020
.
Let us denote

Z_{j,l,n}:=e_{j}^{\prime}W^{n}e_{l}\,,1\leq j,l\leq k\,,n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}\,,

Also, let
Y_{n}
be the
k\times k
matrix with entries

(A.1)

Y_{n}(j,l)=\sqrt{\alpha_{j}\alpha_{l}}\theta Z_{j,l,n},\qquad 1\leq j,l\leq k,.

Remark A.1
.

An immediate observation from the definition of
Y_{n}
is that

\|Y_{n}\|=O_{hp}(\theta N^{\frac{n}{2}})\,.

We now proceed towards the proof of Theorem
2.1
through several steps, organized as Lemmas
A.1
–
A.5
. The key idea is to break the infinite sum in (
3.4
) at
L
, where
L=\lfloor\log N\rfloor
.

Lemma A.1
.

\mu=\lambda_{i}\left(\sum_{n=0}^{L}\mu^{-n}Y_{n}\right)+o_{hp}(1)\,.

Proof.

Using the definitions of
K
and
Y_{n}
from (
3.2
) and (
A.1
), respectively, it is immediate that

K=\one(\|W\|<\mu)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mu^{-n}Y_{n}\,.

Lemma
3.4
gives that with high probability,

\mu=\lambda_{i}(V)=\lambda_{i}(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mu^{-n}Y_{n}).

It then suffices to show

(A.2)

\Big\|\sum_{n=L+1}^{\infty}\mu^{-n}Y_{n}\Big\|=o_{hp}(1),.

Consequently, Remark
A.1
implies that there exist constants
C,C^{\prime}>0
such that

\displaystyle\Big\|\sum_{n=L+1}^{\infty}\mu^{-n}Y_{n}\Big\|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=L+1}^{\infty}|\mu|^{-n}\,\|Y_{n}\|

\displaystyle=O_{hp}\Big(\theta\sum_{n=L+1}^{\infty}\Big(\frac{C\sqrt{N}}{\theta}\Big)^{n}\Big)

\displaystyle=O_{hp}\Big(\frac{(C^{\prime})^{L+1}\sqrt{N}}{(\log N)^{L\xi}}\Big)

\displaystyle=o_{hp}(1),

where
\xi>0
is as in Assumption
2.1
. This verifies (
A.2
) and completes the proof.
∎

In the next step, we approximate
Y_{n}
by
\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}(Y_{n})
for
2\leq n\leq L
.

Lemma A.2
.

\mu=\lambda_{i}\left(Y_{0}+\frac{Y_{1}}{\mu}+\sum_{n=2}^{L}\mu^{-n}\mathbb{E}(Y_{n})\right)+o_{hp}(1)\,.

Proof.

In view of Lemma
A.1
, it suffices to show that

(A.3)

\sum_{n=2}^{L}\mu^{-n}\,\|Y_{n}-\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}(Y_{n})\|=o_{hp}(1).

To this end, define the following events (depending on
N
):

E=\bigcap_{2\leq n\leq L,1\leq j,l\leq k}\left[\left|Z_{j,l,n}-\mathbb{E}(Z_{j,l,n})\right|\leq N^{(n-1)/2}(\log N)^{n\xi/4}\right],

E^{\prime}=\left[\frac{\mu}{\theta}\geq\frac{\alpha_{i}}{2}\right],

E^{\prime\prime}=\left[\sum_{n=2}^{L}\mu^{-n}\|Y_{n}-\mathbb{E}(Y_{n})\|\leq C_{2}\frac{\sqrt{N}}{\theta}(\log N)^{\xi/2}\right],

where
C_{2}>0
is a constant. We will show that for all large
N
,

E\cap E^{\prime}\subset E^{\prime\prime}\,.

Assume that the event
E\cap E^{\prime}
occurs. Then using the definitions above, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n=2}^{L}\mu^{-n}\|Y_{n}-\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}(Y_{n})\|

\displaystyle\leq\theta\alpha_{1}\sum_{n=2}^{L}\mu^{-n}\sum_{1\leq j,l\leq k}\big|Z_{j,l,n}-\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}(Z_{j,l,n})\big|

(
spectral norm
\leq
sum of entries)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha_{1}}{\alpha_{i}}k^{2}\sum_{n=2}^{L}2^{n}(\theta\alpha_{i})^{-n+1}N^{(n-1)/2}(\log N)^{n\xi/4}

(
by the definition of
E
)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha_{1}}{2\alpha_{i}}k^{2}\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\Big(\frac{2\sqrt{N}}{\theta\alpha_{i}}\Big)^{n-1}(\log N)^{n\xi/4}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\alpha_{1}}{2\alpha_{i}}k^{2}\left[1-\frac{2\sqrt{N}}{\theta\alpha_{i}}(\log N)^{\xi/4}\right]^{-1}\frac{2\sqrt{N}}{\theta\alpha_{i}}(\log N)^{\xi/2}.

In view of Assumption
2.1
, the above inclusion
E\cap E^{\prime}\subset E^{\prime\prime}
holds for all large
N
(by choosing
C_{2}
appropriately).

Moreover, Lemma
3.3
(a concentration bound for sums of independent entries of
W
) yields that for all
1\leq j,l\leq k
and
2\leq n\leq L
, uniformly in
N
,

P\left(\left|Z_{j,l,n}-\mathbb{E}(Z_{j,l,n})\right|>N^{(n-1)/2}(\log N)^{n\xi/4}\right)=O\left(e^{-(\log N)^{\eta_{1}}}\right)

for some
\eta_{1}>0
. Using the complement of the event inclusion and Theorem
3.1
, we thus obtain an
\eta_{2}>1
such that

\displaystyle P\Big(\sum_{n=2}^{L}\mu^{-n}\|Y_{n}-\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}(Y_{n})\|>C_{2}\frac{\sqrt{N}}{\theta}(\log N)^{\xi/2}\Big)

\displaystyle\ =

\displaystyle\sum_{n=2}^{L}P\Big(\big|Z_{j,l,n}-\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}(Z_{j,l,n})\big|>N^{(n-1)/2}(\log N)^{n\xi/4}\Big)+P\Big(\frac{\mu}{\theta}<\frac{\alpha_{i}}{2}\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle O\Big(\log Ne^{-(\log N)^{\eta_{1}}}\Big)+O\Big(e^{-(\log N)^{\eta_{2}}}\Big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle o\Big(e^{-(\log N)^{(1+\eta_{1})/2}}\Big)+o\Big(e^{-(\log N)^{(1+\eta_{2})/2}}\Big).

Hence (
A.3
) is established. This completes the proof.
∎

In the next step, we remove the randomness entirely from

\sum_{n=2}^{L}\mu^{-n}\mathbb{E}(Y_{n})\,.

by replacing the factor
\mu
suitably with a deterministic value.

Lemma A.3
.

For
N
large, the deterministic equation

(A.4)

x=\lambda_{i}\Big(\sum_{n=0}^{L}x^{-n}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}(Y_{n})\Big),\qquad x>0,,

has a solution
\tilde{\mu}
, and moreover

(A.5)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\tilde{\mu}}{\theta}=\alpha_{i}.

Proof.

Consider the function (for each fixed
N
)
h:(0,\infty)\to{\mathbb{R}}
by

h(x)=\lambda_{i}\left(\sum_{n=0}^{L}x^{-n}\mathbb{E}(Y_{n})\right)\,.

We first show that for every fixed
x>0
,

(A.6)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{h(x\theta)}{\theta}=\alpha_{i}.

To see this, note that
\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}(Y_{1})=0
(since
\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[W]=0
under Assumption
2.1
). Thus

\displaystyle h\left(x\theta\right)=\lambda_{i}\left(\mathbb{E}(Y_{0})+\sum_{n=2}^{L}(x\theta)^{-n}\mathbb{E}(Y_{n})\right)\,.

By definition of
Y_{0}
,

Y_{0}(j,l)=\theta\sqrt{\alpha_{j}\alpha_{l}}\,e_{j}^{\prime}e_{l}\,,1\leq j,l\leq k\,.

Then Assumption
2.2
implies

(A.7)

\lim_{N\to\infty}\theta^{-1}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}(Y_{0})=\mathrm{Diag}(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{k}).

Moreover, by a moment bound (Lemma
3.2
), there exists a constant
D>0
(not depending on
N
) such that for all
2\leq n\leq L
,

(A.8)

\|\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}(Y_{n})\|\leq\theta\big(D\sqrt{N}\big)^{n}\,.

Using the above bound, we obtain

\displaystyle\|\sum_{n=2}^{L}(x\theta)^{-n}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}(Y_{n})\|

\displaystyle\leq\theta\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\Big(\frac{D\sqrt{N}}{x\theta}\Big)^{n}

\displaystyle=o(1)\qquad\text{as }N\to\infty,

uniformly for
x>0
. The last line, along with (
A.7
), implies

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{\theta}\left(\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left(Y_{0}\right)+\sum_{n=2}^{L}(x\theta)^{-n}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left(Y_{n}\right)\right)=\operatorname{Diag}\left(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}\right),

from which (
A.6
) follows. With (
A.6
) at our disposal, fix any
0<\delta<\alpha_{i}
. Then

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\theta\left(\alpha_{i}+\delta\right)-h\left(\theta\left(\alpha_{i}+\delta\right)\right)}{\theta}=\delta,

and hence for all sufficiently large
N
,

h\left(\theta\left(\alpha_{i}+\delta\right)\right)<\theta\left(\alpha_{i}+\delta\right).

Similarly, one finds for large
N
that
h(\theta(\alpha_{i}-\delta))>\theta(\alpha_{i}-\delta)
. This means that for all sufficiently large
N
, the equation
h(x)=x
(i.e. (
A.4
)) has a solution
x=\tilde{\mu}
in the interval
[\theta(\alpha_{i}-\delta)\,,\theta(\alpha_{i}+\delta)]
. In particular,
\tilde{\mu}
exists and satisfies

\theta\left(\alpha_{i}-\delta\right)\leq\tilde{\mu}\leq\theta\left(\alpha_{i}+\delta\right)

for large
N
. Since
\delta>0
was arbitrary, this yields (
A.5
) (uniqueness of the solution
\tilde{\mu}
is not needed and not asserted).
∎

In the next step, we estimate the error incurred when approximating
\mu
by the deterministic
\tilde{\mu}
obtained in Lemma
A.3
.

Lemma A.4
.

\mu-\tilde{\mu}=O_{hp}\left(\frac{\|Y_{1}\|}{\theta}+1\right)\,.

Proof.

Lemmas
A.2
and
A.3
jointly imply

\displaystyle|\mu-\tilde{\mu}|

\displaystyle=\Big|\lambda_{i}\Big(Y_{0}+\mu^{-1}Y_{1}+\sum_{n=2}^{L}\mu^{-n}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}(Y_{n})\Big)-\lambda_{i}\Big(\sum_{n=0}^{L}\tilde{\mu}^{-n}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}(Y_{n})\Big)\Big|+o_{hp}(1)

\displaystyle\leq\|\mu^{-1}Y_{1}\|+|\mu-\tilde{\mu}|\sum_{n=2}^{L}\mu^{-n}\tilde{\mu}^{-n}\|\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}(Y_{n})\|\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\mu^{j}\tilde{\mu}^{n-1-j}+o_{hp}(1)

\displaystyle=|\mu-\tilde{\mu}|\sum_{n=2}^{L}\mu^{-n}\tilde{\mu}^{-n}\|\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}(Y_{n})\|\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\mu^{j}\tilde{\mu}^{n-1-j}+O_{hp}\Big(\frac{\|Y_{1}\|}{\theta}+1\Big)\,.

Clearly, to obtain the desired result, it suffices to show that the first term on the RHS above is
o_{hp}(1)
. The above display simplifies to

(A.9)

|\mu-\tilde{\mu}|\Bigg[1-\sum_{n=2}^{L}\mu^{-n}\tilde{\mu}^{-n}\|\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}(Y_{n})\|\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\mu^{j}\tilde{\mu}^{n-1-j}\Bigg]=O_{hp}\Big(\frac{\|Y_{1}\|}{\theta}+1\Big).

As in the proof of Lemma
A.3
, Lemma
3.2
ensures that for all
2\leq n\leq L
,

\mathbb{\|}E(Y_{n})\|\leq\theta\left(D\sqrt{N}\right)^{n}\,,

for some
D>0
. Also, by (
A.5
) (from Lemma
A.3
), for all sufficiently large
N
,

\frac{\alpha_{i}}{2}<\frac{\tilde{\mu}}{\theta}<\frac{3\alpha_{i}}{2}.

By Theorem
3.1
, we similarly have w.h.p.

\frac{\alpha_{i}}{2}<\frac{\mu}{\theta}<\frac{3\alpha_{i}}{2}.

Thus for large
N
, with high probability,

\displaystyle\Big|\mu^{-n}\tilde{\mu}^{-n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\mu^{j}\tilde{\mu}^{n-1-j}\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\theta^{-2n}\Big(\frac{2}{\alpha_{i}}\Big)^{2n}\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\theta^{n-1}\Big(\frac{3\alpha_{i}}{2}\Big)^{j}\Big(\frac{3\alpha_{i}}{2}\Big)^{n-1-j}

\displaystyle\leq n\Big(\frac{6}{\theta\alpha_{i}}\Big)^{n+1}.

Using this and the estimate on
\|\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}(Y_{n})\|
provided by Lemma
3.2
, we get with high probability

\displaystyle\sum_{n=2}^{L}\mu^{-n}\tilde{\mu}^{-n}\|\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}(Y_{n})\|\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\mu^{j}\tilde{\mu}^{n-1-j}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=2}^{L}n\Big(\frac{6}{\theta\alpha_{i}}\Big)^{n+1}\theta\big(D\sqrt{N}\big)^{n}

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{6}{\alpha_{i}}\Big)^{2}\frac{D\sqrt{N}}{\theta}\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}n\Big(\frac{6D\sqrt{N}}{\theta\alpha_{i}}\Big)^{n-1}

\displaystyle=o(1),

as
N\to\infty
. This in conjunction with (
A.9
) completes the proof.
∎

The next step is the most crucial one and will propel us to the penultimate step in proving Theorem
2.1
.

Lemma A.5
.

\exists
a deterministic constant
\bar{\mu}=\bar{\mu}_{N},
such that,

\mu=\bar{\mu}+\mu^{-1}Y_{1}(i,i)+o_{hp}\left(\frac{\|Y_{1}\|}{\theta}+1\right)\,.

Proof.

With
\tilde{\mu}
as in Lemma
A.3
, consider the deterministic
k\times k
matrix

(A.10)

X:=\sum_{n=0}^{L}\tilde{\mu}^{-n}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}(Y_{n}).

Using the argument and final estimate from the proof of Lemma
A.4
, we have

\displaystyle\Big\|X-\sum_{n=0}^{L}\mu^{-n}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}(Y_{n})\Big\|

\displaystyle\leq|\mu-\tilde{\mu}|\sum_{n=2}^{L}\mu^{-n}\tilde{\mu}^{-n}\|\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}(Y_{n})\|\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\mu^{j}\tilde{\mu}^{,n-1-j}

\displaystyle=o_{hp}\big(|\mu-\tilde{\mu}|\big)

\displaystyle=o_{hp}\Big(\frac{\|Y_{1}\|}{\theta}+1\Big).

Then an application of Lemma
A.2
along with Weyl’s inequality gives

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=\lambda_{i}\Big(Y_{0}+\frac{Y_{1}}{\mu}+\sum_{n=2}^{L}\mu^{-n}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}(Y_{n})\Big)+o_{hp}(1)

(A.11)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{i}\Big(\frac{Y_{1}}{\mu}+X\Big)+o_{hp}\Big(\frac{\|Y_{1}\|}{\theta}+1\Big).

Define

H_{1}=X-X(i,i)I\,,

and

H_{2}=X+\mu^{-1}Y_{1}-\left(X(i,i)+\mu^{-1}Y_{1}(i,i)\right)I\,,

and

\bar{\mu}=\lambda_{i}(X)\,.

By Lemma
A.3
(see (
A.5
)),
\bar{\mu}
coincides with
\tilde{\mu}
and hence

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\bar{\mu}}{\theta}=\alpha_{i}\,.

Also, we have

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}\big(\mu^{-1}Y_{1}+X\big)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{i}(H_{2})+X(i,i)+\mu^{-1}Y_{1}(i,i)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{i}(H_{2})-\lambda_{i}(H_{1})+\bar{\mu}+\mu^{-1}Y_{1}(i,i).

Then (
A.11
) implies

\mu=\bar{\mu}+\frac{Y_{1}(i,i)}{\mu}+\lambda_{i}(H_{2})-\lambda_{i}(H_{1})+o_{hp}\left(\frac{\|Y_{1}\|}{\theta}+1\right)

The proof will follow if we can show that

(A.12)

\lambda_{i}(H_{1})-\lambda_{i}(H_{2})=o_{hp}\Big(\frac{\|Y_{1}\|}{\theta}\Big).

If
k=1
, then necessarily
i=1
and
H_{1}=H_{2}=0
, so (
A.12
) holds trivially. For the remainder of the proof, assume
k\geq 2
(so that there are “other” spike indices
j\neq i
to consider).

To this end, let

U_{1}=\theta^{-1}H_{1}\,\,\,\,and\,\,\,U_{2}=\theta^{-1}H_{2}

Clearly, using the equivalence of norms in finite dimensions, there exists some constant
\tilde{D}>0
such that

\|H_{1}-H_{2}\|=\|\mu^{-1}(Y_{1}-Y_{1}(i,i)I)\|\leq\tilde{D}\left\|\frac{Y_{1}}{\mu}\right\|

Then, from Theorem
3.1
and Remark
A.1
we get

(A.13)

|U_{1}-U_{2}|=O_{hp}\Big(\frac{\|Y_{1}\|}{\theta^{2}}\Big)=o_{hp}(1),.

Combining this with a bound on
\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}(Y_{n})
(Lemma
3.2
), we find that for
m=1,2
and all
1\leq j,l\leq k
,

(A.14)

U_{m}(j,l)=(\alpha_{j}-\alpha_{i})\one{j=l}+o_{hp}(1)\qquad(N\to\infty).

In particular, as
N\to\infty
, both
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
converge (with high probability) to the diagonal matrix
\mathrm{Diag}(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{i},\ldots,\alpha_{k}-\alpha_{i})
. Consequently,

(A.15)

\lambda_{i}(U_{m})=o_{hp}(1),,\qquad m=1,2.

Let
\tilde{U}_{m}
(for
m=1,2
) be the
(k-1)\times(k-1)
matrix obtained by deleting the
i
th row and column of
U_{m}
, and let
\tilde{u}_{m}
be the
(k-1)\times 1
vector obtained from the
i
th column of
U_{m}
by deleting its
i
th entry. It is worth noting that

\displaystyle\|\tilde{u}_{m}\|=o_{hp}(1)\,,m=1,2

and

(A.16)

|\tilde{u}_{1}-\tilde{u}_{2}|=O_{hp}\Big(\frac{\|Y_{1}\|}{\theta^{2}}\Big),

both of which follow from (
A.14
). Also, (
A.14
) and (
A.15
) together imply that the matrix
\tilde{U}_{m}-\lambda_{i}(U_{m})I_{k-1}
converges (w.h.p.) to

\mathrm{Diag}(\alpha_{1}-\alpha_{i},\ldots,\alpha_{i-1}-\alpha_{i},\alpha_{i+1}-\alpha_{i},\alpha_{k}-\alpha_{i})\,.

Define
(k-1)
-dimensional vectors

\tilde{v}_{m}=-\left[\tilde{U}_{m}-\lambda_{i}(U_{m})I_{k-1}\right]^{-1}\tilde{u}_{m}\,,m=1,2\,,

and extend each
\tilde{v}_{m}
to a
k
-vector
v_{m}
by inserting a
1
in the
i
th position:

v_{m}=\left[\tilde{v}_{m}(1),\ldots,\tilde{v}_{m}(i-1),\,1,\,\tilde{v}_{m}(i),\ldots,\tilde{v}_{m}(k-1)\right]^{\prime}\,,m=1,2\,.

Now, proceeding exactly along the lines of the proof of Lemma 5.8 in
Chakrabarty et al., 2020
, we get

\ \|v_{1}-v_{2}\|=O_{hp}\left(\frac{\|Y_{1}\|}{\theta^{2}}\right)\,\text{ and }\,\,\|\tilde{v}_{m}\|=o_{hp}(1)\,,m=1,2\,.

(Note also that
U_{m}(i,i)=0
and by construction
v_{m}(i)=1
for
m=1,2
.)

Therefore, we finally have

\displaystyle\big|\lambda_{i}(U_{1})-\lambda_{i}(U_{2})\big|

\displaystyle=\Big|\sum_{j\neq i}U_{1}(i,j)v_{1}(j)-\sum_{j\neq i}U_{2}(i,j)v_{2}(j)\Big|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j\neq i}|U_{1}(i,j)||v_{1}(j)-v_{2}(j)|+\sum_{j\neq i}|U_{1}(i,j)-U_{2}(i,j)||v_{2}(j)|

\displaystyle=O_{hp}\Big(\|\tilde{u}_{1}\|\|v_{1}-v_{2}\|+\|U_{1}-U_{2}\|\|\tilde{v}_{2}\|\Big)

\displaystyle=o_{hp}\Big(\frac{\|Y_{1}\|}{\theta^{2}}\Big).

Thus (
A.12
) is established, and the proof is complete.
∎

Now we are in a position to complete the proof of Theorem
2.1
.

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

The proof will be complete if we can show that

(A.17)

\mu-\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}(\mu)=\mu^{-1}Y_{1}(i,i)+o_{p}(1),.

Hence, replacing
\bar{\mu}
by
\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}(\mu)
in Lemma
A.5
(which is justified since
\bar{\mu}-\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}(\mu)=o(1)
as we will see) will serve our purpose.

Recalling that
Y_{1}(i,i)=\alpha_{i}\theta e_{i}^{\prime}We_{i}
, Lemma
A.5
and Remark
A.1
together imply

\displaystyle\mu-\bar{\mu}

\displaystyle=\mu^{-1}Y_{1}(i,i)+o_{hp}\Big(\frac{\|Y_{1}\|}{\theta}+1\Big)

(A.18)

\displaystyle=O_{hp}\Big(\frac{\|Y_{1}\|}{\theta}+1\Big).

From the proof of Lemma
A.5
we also know that

\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\bar{\mu}}{\theta}=\alpha_{i}.

This ensures

\displaystyle\Big|\frac{1}{\bar{\mu}}Y_{1}(i,i)-\frac{1}{\mu}Y_{1}(i,i)\Big|

\displaystyle=O_{hp}\Big(|\mu-\bar{\mu}|\frac{\|Y_{1}\|}{\theta^{2}}\Big)

\displaystyle=o_{hp}\big(|\mu-\bar{\mu}|\big)

(A.19)

\displaystyle=o_{hp}\Big(\frac{\|Y_{1}\|}{\theta}+1\Big)

(A.20)

\displaystyle=o_{p}(1),

where we used Remark
A.1
in the second step and (
A.18
) in the third.

Using Lemma
A.5
and (
A.19
), we get

(A.21)

\mu=\bar{\mu}+\frac{1}{\bar{\mu}}Y_{1}(i,i)+o_{hp}\Big(\frac{\|Y_{1}\|}{\theta}+1\Big).

Now define

R=\mu-\bar{\mu}-\frac{Y_{1}(i,i)}{\bar{\mu}}\,.

Clearly,

\mathbb{E}(R)=\mathbb{E}(\mu)-\bar{\mu}\,,

and (
A.21
) implies that for any
\delta>0
there exists
\eta>1
and
N_{0}
, such that for all
N>N_{0}

\displaystyle\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}|R|

\displaystyle\leq\delta\Big(1+\frac{\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\|Y_{1}\|}{\theta}\Big)+\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}^{1/2}\Big(\mu-\bar{\mu}-\frac{1}{\bar{\mu}}Y_{1}(i,i)\Big)^{2}O\big(e^{-(\log N)^{\eta}}\big).

For the first term on the RHS, recalling the definition of
Y_{1}
, we note that

(A.22)

\|Y_{1}\|\leq\alpha_{1}\theta\sum_{j,l=1}^{N}|e_{j}^{\prime}We_{l}|,

and hence

\mathbb{E}(\|Y_{1}\|)=O(\theta\sum_{j,l=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}|e_{j}^{\prime}We_{l}|)\leq O(\theta\sum_{j,l=1}^{k}{\rm Var}(e_{j}^{\prime}We_{l}))=O(\theta)\\

Thus

\mathbb{E}|R|\leq o(1)+\mathbb{E}^{1/2}\left(\mu-\bar{\mu}-\frac{1}{\bar{\mu}}Y_{1}(i,i)\right)^{2}O\left(e^{-(\log N)^{\eta}}\right)\,.

Let
H:=\max_{1\leq j\leq k}\sup_{x\in[0,1]}|h_{j}(x)|
. (Clearly
H<\infty
by Assumption A1.) Since
|\mu|\leq\|A\|
and
\theta=O(N)
, we have

|\mu|^{2}\leq\left(\sum_{j,l=1}^{N}|W(j,l)|+\alpha_{1}KH^{2}N^{2}\right)^{2}\,.

Then it follows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{j,l=1}^{N}|W(j,l)|\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{j,l,j^{\prime},l^{\prime}}|W(j,l)||W(j^{\prime},l^{\prime})|\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j,l,j^{\prime},l^{\prime}}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[W(j,l)^{2}]}\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[W(j^{\prime},l^{\prime})^{2}]}=O(N^{4})\,

and similarly
\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\left[\sum_{j,l=1}^{N}|W(j,l)|\right]=O(N^{2})
. Therefore,

(A.23)

\displaystyle\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}(\mu^{2})=O(N^{4}).

Along with (
A.5
), this gives

(A.24)

\displaystyle\frac{\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}[Y_{1}(i,i)^{2}]}{\bar{\mu}^{2}}=O(1).

Using (
A.5
) and Assumption
2.1
again, we have for large
N
:

(A.25)

\displaystyle\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}(\mu\bar{\mu})\leq\bar{\mu}\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}^{1/2}(\mu^{2})=O(N^{3}),

and similarly

(A.26)

\displaystyle\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\Big(\mu\frac{Y_{1}(i,i)}{\bar{\mu}}\Big)=O(N^{2})\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}\big(\mu Y_{1}(i,i)\big)=O(N^{3}).

Therefore, (
A.23
)–(
A.26
) together imply

\mathbb{E}^{1/2}\left(\mu-\bar{\mu}-\frac{1}{\bar{\mu}}Y_{1}(i,i)\right)^{2}=O(N^{2})=o\left(e^{(\log N)^{\eta}}\right)

Hence we get

\mathbb{E}|R|=o(1)\,,

and therefore

(A.27)

\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}(\mu)=\bar{\mu}+o(1).

Then (
A.19
) and (
A.20
) imply that

\displaystyle\mu

\displaystyle=\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}(\mu)+\frac{1}{\bar{\mu}}Y_{1}(i,i)+o_{p}\Big(\frac{\|Y_{1}\|}{\theta}+1\Big)

\displaystyle=\mbox{${\mathbb{E}}$}(\mu)+\frac{1}{\mu}Y_{1}(i,i)+o_{p}(1).

This proves (
A.17
) and completes the proof of Theorem
2.1
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.2
.

Henceforth we will use Assumption
2.3
, i.e., the functions
h_{1},\ldots,h_{k}
are Lipschitz. Along with this Assumption
2.5
is also required. We aim to analyze the asymptotic behavior of the expectation of eigenvalue
\mu
, given by

\mu=\lambda_{i}\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mu^{-n}Y_{n}\right),

as described in Theorem
2.1
.

By Lemma
A.2
, we have:

\mu=\lambda_{i}\left(\sum_{n=0}^{3}\mu^{-n}\mathbb{E}(Y_{n})\right)+O_{hp}\left(\mu^{-1}\|Y_{1}\|+\sum_{n=4}^{L}\mu^{-n}\|\mathbb{E}(Y_{n})\|\right)+o_{p}(1).

Using Theorems
2.1
and
3.1
, we get

(A.28)

\displaystyle\mu=\lambda_{i}\left(\sum_{n=0}^{3}\mu^{-n}\mathbb{E}(Y_{n})\right)+O_{p}\left(1+\sum_{n=4}^{L}\mu^{-n}\|\mathbb{E}(Y_{n})\|\right).

Also, from (
A.8
) we deduce along lines of the proof of Lemma
A.1
,

\sum_{n=4}^{L}\mu^{-n}\|\mathbb{E}(Y_{n})\|=O_{p}\left(\frac{N^{2}}{\theta^{3}}\right)=o_{p}(1),

by Assumption
2.5
.
Hence,

\mu=\lambda_{i}\left(\sum_{n=0}^{3}\mu^{-n}\mathbb{E}(Y_{n})\right)+O_{p}\left(1+\frac{N^{2}}{\theta^{3}}\right).

Using the fact that

\mathbb{E}(e_{j}^{\prime}W^{3}e_{l})\sim N\quad\forall j,l

we get,

(A.29)

\mu=\lambda_{i}\left(\sum_{n=0}^{2}\mu^{-n}\mathbb{E}(Y_{n})\right)+O_{p}\left(1+\frac{N^{2}}{\theta^{3}}\right).

To simplify further, we again use the bound on
\mathbb{E}\|Y_{2}\|
from
A.2
to get:

\mu=\lambda_{i}(Y_{0})+O_{p}\left(1+\frac{N}{\theta}+\frac{N^{2}}{\theta^{3}}\right).

We now analyze
\lambda_{i}(Y_{0})
. Using Fact 5.1 from Lemma 5.2 in
Chakrabarty et al., 2020
, we know that

|\lambda_{i}(Y_{0})-Y_{0}(i,i)|\leq\sum_{j\neq i}|Y_{0}(i,j)|.

Since the functions
h_{1},\ldots,h_{k}
are Lipschitz, we have:

e_{i}^{\prime}e_{j}=\one(i=j)+O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right).

Using the definition of
Y_{0}
from (
A.1
), we obtain:

Y_{0}(i,j)=\begin{cases}\theta\alpha_{i}+O\left(\frac{\theta}{N}\right)&\text{if }i=j,\\
O\left(\frac{\theta}{N}\right)&\text{if }i\neq j.\end{cases}

Therefore,

(A.30)

\lambda_{i}(Y_{0})=\theta\alpha_{i}+O\left(\frac{\theta}{N}\right).

Substituting this into the previous equation yields:

\mu=\theta\alpha_{i}+O_{p}\left(\frac{\theta}{N}+1+\frac{N}{\theta}+\frac{N^{2}}{\theta^{3}}\right)=\theta\alpha_{i}+O_{p}\left(\frac{N}{\theta}\right).

We now refine the expansion by reintroducing the
\mathbb{E}(Y_{2})
term. Using the triangle inequality, we estimate

\left\|\mu^{-2}\mathbb{E}(Y_{2})-(\theta\alpha_{i})^{-2}\mathbb{E}(Y_{2})\right\|=O_{p}\left(\frac{N}{\theta^{4}}\|\mathbb{E}(Y_{2})\|\right)=O_{p}\left(\frac{N^{2}}{\theta^{3}}\right),

where we used that
\mu^{-2}-(\theta\alpha_{i})^{-2}=O_{p}(N/\theta^{4})
, and
\|\mathbb{E}(Y_{2})\|=O(N\theta)
, where the later bound follows from Lemma
3.2
.

Substituting this into (
A.29
), we finally get:

(A.31)

\displaystyle\mu=\lambda_{i}\left(Y_{0}+\frac{\mathbb{E}(Y_{2})}{(\theta\alpha_{i})^{2}}\right)+O_{p}\left(1+\frac{N^{2}}{\theta^{3}}\right).

Thus we have derived the required approximation for
\mu
. It remains to connect this to the mean of the eigenvalue. From Theorem
2.1
, we have:

\mu-\mathbb{E}(\mu)=O_{p}(1).

It follows from (
A.31
)

\lambda_{i}(B)-\mu=O_{p}\left(1+\frac{N^{2}}{\theta^{3}}\right),

where
B:=Y_{0}+\frac{\mathbb{E}(Y_{2})}{(\theta\alpha_{i})^{2}}
is the deterministic approximation matrix.

Combining the two bounds, we conclude:

\lambda_{i}(B)-\mathbb{E}(\mu)=O\left(1+\frac{N^{2}}{\theta^{3}}\right).

Now, appeal to equations (
A.27
), (
A.30
) and (
A.31
) completes the proof.
∎

Appendix B
Technical lemmas

Proof of Lemma
3.1
.

Let
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{n}
denote the largest and smallest eigenvalues of
W_{N}
, respectively. By our assumptions, the entries of
W_{N}
satisfy the moment conditions required by
(
Anderson et al., 2010
, Theorem 2.1.22)
. Using the notation from
Anderson et al., 2010
, define the normalized moment of the empirical spectral distribution as

\langle L_{N},x^{k}\rangle:=\frac{1}{N^{k/2+1}}\mathbb{E}(\mathrm{tr}(W_{N}^{k})).

Then, by the derivation in
(
Anderson et al., 2010
, Theorem 2.1.22)
, for some constant
\tilde{B}=\tilde{B}(B)
, we have:

\langle L_{N},x^{2n}\rangle\leq(2\sqrt{\|f\|_{\infty}})^{2n}\sum_{j=0}^{n}\left(\frac{n^{\tilde{B}}}{N}\right)^{j}.

Now define
n=n(N):=\lfloor(\log N)^{3}\rfloor
. Observe that as
N\to\infty
,

\frac{n(N)}{\log N}\to\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{n(N)}{N}\to 0.

Fix any constant
\tilde{C}>2\sqrt{\|f\|_{\infty}}
. By Markov’s inequality, we get:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\lambda_{1}>\tilde{C})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\|W_{N}\|>\tilde{C}\right)\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}(\mathrm{tr}(W_{N}^{2n}))}{\tilde{C}^{2n}}=N\cdot\frac{\langle L_{N},x^{2n}\rangle}{\tilde{C}^{2n}}\cdot N^{n},

\displaystyle\leq N\cdot\left(\frac{2\sqrt{\|f\|_{\infty}}}{\tilde{C}}\right)^{2n}\sum_{j=0}^{n}\left(\frac{n^{\tilde{B}}}{N}\right)^{j}.

Since
n=o(N)
, the sum is
O(1)
, and hence:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\lambda_{1}>\tilde{C})

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}N\left(\frac{2\sqrt{\|f\|_{\infty}}}{\tilde{C}}\right)^{2n}

\displaystyle=C_{1}\exp\left(\log N+2n\log\left(\frac{2\sqrt{\|f\|_{\infty}}}{\tilde{C}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=C_{1}\exp\left(-a(\log N)^{3}\right)

for some constant
a>0
, provided
\tilde{C}>2\sqrt{\|f\|_{\infty}}
is fixed.

Therefore,

(B.1)

\mathbb{P}\left(\lambda_{1}>\tilde{C}\right)\leq C_{1}\exp\left(-a(\log N)^{3}\right).

A symmetric argument applied to
-W_{N}
yields a similar bound for the smallest eigenvalue:

(B.2)

\mathbb{P}\left(\lambda_{n}<-\bar{C}\right)\leq C_{2}\exp\left(-b(\log N)^{3}\right),

for some constants
\bar{C}>2\sqrt{\|f\|_{\infty}}
and
b>0
.

Now let
C:=\max(\tilde{C},\bar{C})
and
c:=\min(a,b)
. Combining (
B.1
) and (
B.2
), we get:

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|\frac{W_{N}}{\sqrt{N}}\right\|>C\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\lambda_{1}>C\sqrt{N}\,\,\text{or}\,\,\lambda_{n}<-C\sqrt{N}\right)=O\left(\exp(-c(\log N)^{3})\right),

which completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Lemma
3.2
.

As in the proof of the previous lemma, we consider the high-probability event

E_{N}:=\left\{\|W_{N}\|\leq C\sqrt{N}\right\}

for some constant
C>0
. By Lemma
3.1
, we have

\mathbb{P}(E_{N}^{c})\leq e^{-a(\log N)^{3}}

for some constant
a>0
.

On the event
E_{N}
, we trivially have

|\mathbb{E}(e_{1}^{\prime}W^{n}e_{2}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{E_{N}})|\leq(C\sqrt{N})^{n}.

It remains to show that the contribution from the complement event
E_{N}^{c}
is negligible. For this, we estimate the second moment
\mathbb{E}(e_{1}^{\prime}W^{n}e_{2})^{2}
.

Write out the full expansion:

(e_{1}^{\prime}W^{n}e_{2})^{2}=\sum_{i_{1},\ldots,i_{n+1}}\sum_{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n+1}}\left[e_{1}(i_{1})\left(\prod_{l=1}^{n}W(i_{l},i_{l+1})\right)e_{2}(i_{n+1})\right]\left[e_{1}(j_{1})\left(\prod_{l=1}^{n}W(j_{l},j_{l+1})\right)e_{2}(j_{n+1})\right].

Let
S
denote a typical summand in the above expression. Using Hölder’s inequality and the moment assumptions on
W
, we get:

\mathbb{E}(|S|^{2n})\leq\frac{H^{8n}}{N^{4n}}(2n)^{4Bn^{2}},

where
H
and
B
are constants depending on the uniform moment bound on the entries of
W
.

Hence, for
2\leq n\leq L
and
N
sufficiently large, we can find a constant
C_{1}>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(e_{1}^{\prime}W^{n}e_{2})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq H^{4}\left(N(2n)^{B}\right)^{2n}\leq H^{4}\cdot(N^{B+1})^{2n}=N^{nC_{1}}.

Now using Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, we estimate:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(e_{1}^{\prime}W^{n}e_{2}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{E_{N}^{c}})

\displaystyle\leq\left[\mathbb{E}(e_{1}^{\prime}W^{n}e_{2})^{2}\right]^{1/2}\cdot\left[\mathbb{P}(E_{N}^{c})\right]^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(N^{nC_{1}}\right)^{1/2}\cdot e^{-\frac{a}{2}(\log N)^{3}}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(nC_{1}\log N/2-\frac{a}{2}(\log N)^{3}\right).

Since
n\leq L=\lfloor\log N\rfloor
, we have:

\mathbb{E}(e_{1}^{\prime}W^{n}e_{2}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{E_{N}^{c}})\leq\exp\left(-(\log N)^{3}\left[\frac{a}{2}-\frac{C_{1}}{2\log N}\right]\right),

which vanishes as
N\to\infty
. Thus, for
N
sufficiently large,

(B.3)

\mathbb{E}(e_{1}^{\prime}W^{n}e_{2}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{E_{N}^{c}})\leq\exp\left(-(\log N)^{3}\left[\frac{a}{2}-o(1)\right]\right)\to 0.

Combining the bounds on
\mathbb{E}(e_{1}^{\prime}W^{n}e_{2}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{E_{N}})
and
\mathbb{E}(e_{1}^{\prime}W^{n}e_{2}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{E_{N}^{c}})
, we conclude that:

\mathbb{E}(e_{1}^{\prime}W^{n}e_{2})\leq(C\sqrt{N})^{n}+o(1).

This completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Lemma
4.5
.

We prove the claims separately for the cases
j\neq i
and
j=i
.

Case 1: Off-diagonal alignment,
j\neq i
.

From the master equation (
4.20
), we have:

e_{j}^{\prime}v=\frac{\theta}{\mu}\sum_{l=1}^{k}\alpha_{l}\cdot e_{j}^{\prime}\left(I-\frac{W}{\mu}\right)^{-1}e_{l}.

Isolating the
l=j
term, this becomes:

e_{j}^{\prime}v=\frac{\theta\alpha_{j}}{\mu}\cdot e_{j}^{\prime}\left(I-\frac{W}{\mu}\right)^{-1}e_{j}+\frac{\theta}{\mu}\sum_{l\neq j}\alpha_{l}\cdot e_{j}^{\prime}\left(I-\frac{W}{\mu}\right)^{-1}e_{l}.

Multiplying both sides by the bracketed expression, we rearrange:

e_{j}^{\prime}v\left(1-\frac{\theta\alpha_{j}}{\mu}\cdot e_{j}^{\prime}\left(I-\frac{W}{\mu}\right)^{-1}e_{j}\right)=\frac{\theta}{\mu}\sum_{l\neq j}\alpha_{l}(e_{l}^{\prime}v)\cdot e_{j}^{\prime}\left(I-\frac{W}{\mu}\right)^{-1}e_{l}.

Now, using

(a)

The approximation
\mu/\theta\sim\alpha_{i}
from (
A.32
),

(b)

The expansion of
e_{j}^{\prime}\left(I-\frac{W}{\mu}\right)^{-1}e_{j}=1/\alpha_{j}+o_{hp}(\sqrt{N}/\theta)
from Lemma
4.1
,

(c)

The fact that
e_{j}^{\prime}v
is bounded,

we obtain:

(B.4)

e_{j}^{\prime}v\left(1-\frac{\alpha_{j}}{\alpha_{i}}+o_{hp}\left(\frac{\sqrt{N}}{\theta}\right)\right)=o_{hp}\left(\frac{\sqrt{N}}{\theta}\right).

Thus, for
j\neq i
, we conclude:

e_{j}^{\prime}v=o_{hp}\left(\frac{\sqrt{N}}{\theta}\right).

Case 2: Diagonal alignment,
j=i
.

From the second master equation (
4.23
), we have:

\sum_{l,m=1}^{k}\alpha_{l}\alpha_{m}(e_{l}^{\prime}v)(e_{m}^{\prime}v)\cdot e_{l}^{\prime}\left(I-\frac{W}{\mu}\right)^{-2}e_{m}=\theta^{-2}\mu^{2}.

Isolating the
(l,m)=(i,i)
term and grouping the rest:

\alpha_{i}^{2}(e_{i}^{\prime}v)^{2}\cdot e_{i}^{\prime}\left(I-\frac{W}{\mu}\right)^{-2}e_{i}=\theta^{-2}\mu^{2}-\sum_{(l,m)\neq(i,i)}\alpha_{l}\alpha_{m}(e_{l}^{\prime}v)(e_{m}^{\prime}v)\cdot e_{l}^{\prime}\left(I-\frac{W}{\mu}\right)^{-2}e_{m}.

Using the bounds:

e_{j}^{\prime}v=o_{hp}(\sqrt{N}/\theta)
for
j\neq i
,
\mu/\theta\sim\alpha_{i}
, and Lemma
4.1
again for the matrix term,

we get:

\alpha_{i}^{2}(e_{i}^{\prime}v)^{2}\cdot e_{i}^{\prime}\left(I-\frac{W}{\mu}\right)^{-2}e_{i}=\alpha_{i}^{2}\left(1+o_{hp}\left(\frac{\sqrt{N}}{\theta}\right)\right)+o_{hp}\left(\frac{N}{\theta^{2}}\right).

Using the bound
e_{i}^{\prime}\left(I-\frac{W}{\mu}\right)^{-2}e_{i}=1+o_{hp}(1)
from Lemma
4.1
, we conclude:

(e_{i}^{\prime}v)^{2}=1+o_{hp}\left(\frac{\sqrt{N}}{\theta}\right),

and hence

e_{i}^{\prime}v=1+o_{hp}\left(\frac{\sqrt{N}}{\theta}\right),

by the Taylor expansion
(1+x)^{1/2}=1+O(x)
for small
x
.

This completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Lemma
3.3
.

We divide the proof into two parts:
n\geq 2
and
n=1
.

Step 1: Case
2\leq n\leq L

We begin by recalling the moment bound assumption from Section 1. For
k\geq 2
,

\mathbb{E}\left(\left|\frac{W_{ij}}{\sqrt{N}}\right|^{k}\right)\leq\frac{k^{Bk}}{N^{k/2}}\leq\frac{k^{Bk}}{N}.

With a slight abuse of notation, we continue writing
W_{ij}
for the normalized entries
\frac{W_{ij}}{\sqrt{N}}
.

Let us consider the centered linear statistic:

(B.5)

\displaystyle e_{1}^{\prime}W^{n}e_{2}-\mathbb{E}(e_{1}^{\prime}W^{n}e_{2})=\sum_{\mathbf{i}\in\{1,\ldots,N\}^{n+1}}e_{1}(i_{1})e_{2}(i_{n+1})\left(\prod_{\ell=1}^{n}W(i_{\ell},i_{\ell+1})-\mathbb{E}\prod_{\ell=1}^{n}W(i_{\ell},i_{\ell+1})\right).

To exploit independence, we symmetrize
W
as
W=W^{\prime}+W^{\prime\prime}
, where:

W^{\prime}(i,j):=W(i,j)\cdot\mathbf{1}_{i\leq j},\quad W^{\prime\prime}(i,j):=W(i,j)\cdot\mathbf{1}_{i>j}.

This allows us to expand the product into
2^{n}
terms. Fix one such term:

\displaystyle P_{n}:=\sum_{\mathbf{i}}e_{1}(i_{1})e_{2}(i_{n+1})\left(\prod_{\ell=1}^{n}W^{\prime}(i_{\ell},i_{\ell+1})-\mathbb{E}\prod_{\ell=1}^{n}W^{\prime}(i_{\ell},i_{\ell+1})\right).

Let
H:=\max_{1\leq j\leq k}\sup_{x\in[0,1]}|h_{j}(x)|
, and assume without loss of generality that
H,B>1
. Following the argument in Lemma 6.5 of
Erdős et al., 2013
, and restoring the scaling
W_{ij}=\frac{W_{ij}}{\sqrt{N}}
, we obtain:

(B.6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(|P_{n}|^{p})\leq\frac{(2H^{2}np)^{np}(np)^{Bnp}N^{np/2}}{N^{p/2}}.

Since there are at most
2^{n}
such terms, all with the same bound, we apply Markov’s inequality:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|e_{1}^{\prime}W^{n}e_{2}-\mathbb{E}(e_{1}^{\prime}W^{n}e_{2})\right|>N^{(n-1)/2}(\log N)^{n\xi/4}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(4H^{2}(np)^{B+1})^{np}N^{np/2}}{N^{p/2}N^{(n-1)p/2}(\log N)^{pn\xi/4}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\Sigma np)^{(B+1)np}}{(\log N)^{pn\xi/4}},

where
\Sigma:=4H^{2}
. Choose
\eta\in\left(1,\frac{\xi}{4(B+1)}\right)
and define

p:=\frac{(\log N)^{\eta}}{\Sigma n}.

Then
p>1
for large
N
, and we obtain:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left|e_{1}^{\prime}W^{n}e_{2}-\mathbb{E}(e_{1}^{\prime}W^{n}e_{2})\right|>N^{(n-1)/2}(\log N)^{n\xi/4}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{(\log N)^{\eta}}{\Sigma}\left(\frac{\xi}{4}-\eta(B+1)\right)\log\log N\right),

which decays faster than any inverse polynomial as
N\to\infty
, uniformly for
2\leq n\leq L
.

Step 2: Case
n=1

We use the decomposition:

(B.7)

\displaystyle e_{1}^{\prime}We_{2}=\sum_{i\leq j}e_{1}(i)W(i,j)e_{2}(j)+\sum_{i>j}e_{1}(i)W(i,j)e_{2}(j)=:A+B.

By the proof of Lemma 3.8 in
Erdős et al., 2013
, we get (as in (
B.6
)) for
p\in 2\mathbb{N}
,

\mathbb{E}(|A|^{p})\leq p^{(B+1)p}\left[A_{1}+\left(\frac{\sum_{i\leq j}|e_{1}(i)e_{2}(j)|^{2}}{N}\right)^{1/2}\right]^{p}N^{p/2},

where
A_{1}:=\max_{i,j}|e_{1}(i)e_{2}(j)|=O\left(\frac{1}{N}\right)
. Thus, the quantity inside brackets is
O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\right)
.

Let
C_{1}>1
, then:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|A|>4(\log N)^{\xi/4})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(|A|>C_{1}\sqrt{N}(\log N)^{\xi/4}\cdot\left[A_{1}+\left(\frac{\sum_{i\leq j}|e_{1}(i)e_{2}(j)|^{2}}{N}\right)^{1/2}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{p^{(B+1)p}}{(\log N)^{p\xi/4}}.

Now take
\eta\in\left(1,\frac{\xi}{4(B+1)}\right)
and
p:=(\log N)^{\eta}
, which yields:

\mathbb{P}(|A|>4(\log N)^{\xi/4})\leq\exp\left(-(\log N)^{\eta}(\tfrac{\xi}{4}-\eta(B+1))\log\log N\right).

The same argument holds for
B
, and so by (
B.7
), we conclude:

e_{1}^{\prime}We_{2}=O_{hp}((\log N)^{\xi/4}).

Putting both parts together, we conclude that for all
1\leq n\leq L
,

e_{1}^{\prime}W^{n}e_{2}-\mathbb{E}(e_{1}^{\prime}W^{n}e_{2})=O_{hp}\left(N^{(n-1)/2}(\log N)^{n\xi/4}\right).

This completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Lemma
5.1
.

The proof is similar to Lemma 7.10 in
Erdős et al., 2013
, with minor modifications tailored to our setup.

Fix
j\in\{1,2,\ldots,k\}
and
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,N\}
. Then by definition,

(W^{n}e_{j})(i)=\sum_{l_{1},l_{2},\ldots,l_{n}}W(i,l_{1})W(l_{1},l_{2})\cdots W(l_{n-1},l_{n})\cdot\frac{h_{j}\left(\frac{l_{n}}{N}\right)}{\sqrt{N}}.

Let
H:=\max_{1\leq j\leq k}\sup_{x\in[0,1]}|h_{j}(x)|
, and note that
H<\infty
since
h_{j}\in C[0,1]
. Proceeding as in Lemma 7.10 of
Erdős et al., 2013
, we obtain the moment bound:

\mathbb{E}\left(|(W^{n}e_{j})(i)|^{p}\right)\leq(Hnp)^{np}\cdot N^{\frac{(n-1)p}{2}}.

Now apply Markov’s inequality. For any
\varepsilon>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|(W^{n}e_{j})(i)|>N^{\frac{n-1}{2}}(\log N)^{\frac{n\xi}{4}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}\left(|(W^{n}e_{j})(i)|^{p}\right)}{N^{\frac{(n-1)p}{2}}(\log N)^{\frac{n\xi p}{4}}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(Hnp)^{np}}{(\log N)^{\frac{n\xi p}{4}}}.

Choose
\eta\in\left(1,\frac{\xi}{4}\right)
, and let

p=\frac{(\log N)^{\eta}}{Hn}.

This choice ensures
p>1
for large
N
, and plugging it in gives:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|(W^{n}e_{j})(i)|>N^{\frac{n-1}{2}}(\log N)^{\frac{n\xi}{4}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-(\log N)^{\eta}\left(\frac{\xi}{4}-\eta\right)\log\log N\right).

This bound decays faster than any inverse polynomial, and it holds uniformly for all
2\leq n\leq L
.

For the case
n=1
, an identical argument as in the proof of Lemma
3.3
applies, yielding the same high-probability estimate.

This completes the proof.
∎
