Single cell RNA-seq analysis associates Osm signaling in macrophages with osteogenic differentiation during heterotopic ossification
Authors: Jian Wang, Jiangwei Fan, Xiaohui Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background Heterotopic ossification (HO) is an abnormal tissue repair program that occurs after musculoskeletal injury and involves the formation of ectopic bone within soft tissues, and the cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying its development are currently unclear. Methods We investigated the relationship between local inflammatory microenvironment and osteogenic differentiation of mesenchymal progenitor cells (MPC) in trauma-induced HO mice by single-cell transcriptomics.ResultsWe revealed diverse phenotypes of MPCs and macrophages in HO, and showed that macrophage-derived Osm was associated with aberrant chondrogenic progenitor cell differentiation. Furthermore, Spi1, as a novel regulator of early macrophage activation and regulation of Osm expression in HO, which activates M1 macrophages after initial injury thereby contributing to HO formation.ConclusionsOur findings provide novel insights into understanding the functional impact of macrophages on MPC fate, and we also believe that this may offer new therapeutic opportunities to reduce HO risk by targeting macrophage-driven aberrant progenitor cell differentiation.

Background

Heterotopic ossification (HO) is a pathological bone formation in non-skeletal tissues (e.g., muscle, tendon, ligament or other soft tissues) that commonly observed following trauma, burns, nerve injuries, etc [1]. HO occurs mostly around large joints, like the susceptible hip and elbow joints, causing pain and immobility, and in severe cases could lead to limb dysfunction and paralysis with important consequences on quality of life [2]. As explicit knowledge about the specific mechanisms related to HO is still limited, resulting in considerable challenges to clinical management, and these do not include any possible recurrences and complications, treatment outcomes are often unsatisfactory [3–5]. Pharmacological prophylaxis is a cornerstone strategy for reducing the risk of HO following total hip arthroplasty (THA), with non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) being the most extensively used agents for this purpose [6, 7]. Nonetheless, the optimal choice of NSAID for prophylaxis remains debated and should be tailored to clinical practice and patient-specific factors [8]. It has been demonstrated that potent treatment strategies are founded on understanding of the molecular mechanisms and aim to inhibit the pathological process at various stages of HO [9]. In essence, a deeper insight into the HO pathological process is instrumental in formulating effective therapeutic strategies.

HO is largely thought to be well-orchestrated by inflammation associated with tissue injuries [10], and the inflammatory response leads to the recruitment and inappropriate differentiation of mesenchymal progenitor cell (MPC) populations into osteoblastic stem cells and the subsequent activation of an osteogenic or osteochondrogenic program, culminating in the resultant progressive replacement of soft tissues with bone [11–13]. At different phases of inflammation, the activation of various immune cells is also crucial for the formation and progression of HO, especially macrophages involved in dynamic polarization. According to the activation state, macrophages can be categorized into two subtypes, M1 and M2. M1 macrophages primarily participate in pro-inflammatory responses, while M2 macrophages mainly contribute to anti-inflammatory responses. However, it is more important to acknowledge that they play an active role in triggering the inflammatory microenvironment of HO and influencing osteogenic factors and pathways [14–17]. Macrophage-mediated TGF-β signaling is well thought to be critical for wound healing in post-traumatic HO [18], and in addition macrophage-derived NF-κB signaling is also considered to play a key role in supporting HO development [19]. However, the precise sensory and immune signals associated with this disease are incompletely understood to date, and an in-depth understanding of the pathologic process of HO could help in the development of effective therapeutic strategies.

The advent of single-cell transcriptome sequencing (scRNA-seq) has profoundly advanced our understanding of cellular heterogeneity and pathological mechanisms in HO [20]. Several key studies have established comprehensive cellular atlases of HO tissues and delineated the dynamic transcriptional landscape during disease progression [21–23]. These seminal investigations, derived from well-established traumatic models, provide invaluable data resources for the community. While prior works has broadly characterized inflammatory responses in HO, the specific roles of distinct macrophage subsets and their signaling crosstalk with osteogenic precursors remain incompletely resolved. Notably, the existing scRNA-seq datasets offer unique opportunities to explore the underlying cellular and molecular mechanisms of HO. In this study, we performed an integrated re-analysis of published scRNA-seq datasets (GSE255500 and GSE126060) from a mouse model of traumatic HO. Both datasets capture dynamic immune response across critical time points during HO formation and contain abundant macrophage and MPC populations. These resources are well-suited for in-depth exploration of distinct macrophage subpopulations associated with musculoskeletal injury and repair process, as well as their aberrant cell fates mediating HO progression. Our analysis reveals that Osm-expressing M1 macrophages play an important role in driving aberrant MPC differentiation leading to HO through endochondral ossification. These findings provide novel insights into understanding the pathological impact of macrophages on HO formation and may serve as potential direction for future HO treatment.

Methods

Data retrieval

The single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) datasets GSE255500 [22] and GSE126060 [21] used in present study were retrieved and downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database, and all samples were extremity tissues isolated from injury site of previously validated burn/tenotomy (B/T) mouse model of trauma-induced HO, mainly the Achilles tendon area and a small portion of muscle. For GSE255500, we included samples from the uninjured tendon (day 0) as well as days 7 and 21 post-injury. Similarly, all samples from GSE126060 matching the aforesaid groupings were incorporated. Finally, the two original scRNA-seq datasets were integrated to obtain a sufficiently large cell population for subsequent single-cell re-analysis.

Data processing and quality control

The unique molecular identifier (UMI) count matrix of scRNA-seq data was converted into a Seurat object by the R package Seurat (v4.3.0) [24]. Cells with UMI numbers < 1000 or with detected genes < 200 or with over 25% mitochondrial-derived UMI counts were considered low-quality cells and were removed. Genes detected in less than 5 cells were removed for downstream analyses. After quality control, the UMI count matrix was log normalized and then identified the top 2000 highly variable genes. Subsequently, data were scaled using the ScaleData function and batch effects were corrected with the Harmony (v1.2.3) package [25]. Next, we selected the top 30 principal components for dimensionality reduction using Seurat’s ElbowPlot function. The main cell clusters were identified using the FindClusters function with resolution set to 0.4, and then visualized with UMAP plots. To determine the cell type of each cluster, we used the FindMarkers function to detect the marker genes of each cluster, and then annotated the cell types using ScType [26].

Differential expression genes (DEGs) analysis

DEGs were determined with the FindMarkers/FindAllMarkers function from the Seurat package (one-tailed Wilcoxon rank sum test, p-values adjusted for multiple testing using the Bonferroni correction). For computing DEGs, all genes were probed that the expression difference on a natural log scale was at least 0.5 and the difference of percent of detected cells was at least 0.15 and adjusted p-value was less than 0.05.

Transcription factor regulatory network analysis

The modules of transcription factors (TFs) were identified by the pySCENIC (v0.11.2) [27] workflow using default parameters. Activated TFs were identified in the AUC matrix, and differentially activated TFs were selected using R package limma [28] based on the fold change (log2FC ≥ 0.5 or ≤ -0.5) and false discovery rate (FDR ≤ 0.05). To identify cluster-specific regulons, we used the Regulon Specificity Score (RSS) [29] for evaluation. Networks of the modules with TFs and their target genes were visualized by Cytoscape (v3.9.1) [30].

Pseudotime trajectory analysis

Monocle2 (v2.26.0) [31] was used for pseudotime analysis, trajectory construction, and calculation of pseudotime-dependent gene expression. DDRTree (v0.1.5) method is used for dimension reduction. Cell trajectory was visualized according to cell subtypes and cell states. The Basic Differential Analysis algorithm is used to identify differently expressed gene according to pseudotime function. BEAM (Branched Expression Analysis Modeling) was used to identify genes that are regulated in a branching-dependent manner.

Cell–cell communication analysis

Cell–cell interactions based on the expression of known ligand–receptor pairs in different cell types were inferred using CellChat (v2.1.1) [32]. To identify potential cell–cell communication networks perturbed or induced in mice tendons, we followed the official workflow and used the precompiled protein–protein-interactions as a priori network information. Then, the communication probability was computed using the computeCommunProb function, and finally, the function netAnalysis_signallingRole was applied on the netP data slot to determine senders and receivers in the network.

Functional enrichment analysis

The clusterProfiler (v.4.12.6) package [33] was used to perform Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis to determine functional categories. Hyper geometric test and Benjamini-Hochberg FDR controlling procedure were used to define the enrichment of each term.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software. P-value < 0.05 was used as the significance criterion.

Results

ScRNA-seq identifies different cell compositions and changes within HO microenvironment

To understand the composition of the different cell types leading to HO formation and their functional roles, we re-analyzed the scRNA-seq data (GSE255500 and GSE126060) from traumatic mouse models to capture a comprehensive view of the HO microenvironment across key time points. After integrating and preprocessing the two datasets, the final combined dataset contained a total of 28,080 cells that were aligned by unsupervised clustering. To investigate the cellular heterogeneity in the HO niche, we first distinguished distinct cell populations based on the expression of previously described representative marker genes [22]. We defined two dissimilar MPC clusters, MPC1 primarily expressed the progenitor marker Pdgfra, a typical mesenchymal stem cell (MSC) population capable of multilineage differentiation, and dissimilarly, MPC2 additionally expressed the tenogenic marker Tnn, which may govern the origin of tenocytes. Meanwhile, lymphatic endothelial cells (LEC), blood endothelial cells (BEC), satellite cells, Schwann cells, smooth muscle cells (SMC), skeletal muscle cells (SKM), erythrocytes, proliferative cells, and immune cell populations such as T and B lymphocytes, as well as macrophages, were also covered in our data. Among macrophages, cluster specifically expressing the pro-inflammatory cytokine Il-1β (a key marker of M1 polarization) was designated as M1-like macrophages, while cluster expressing the canonical M2 macrophage marker Mrc1 was assigned as M2-like macrophages (Fig. 1A-B). From the perspective of cell proportion, the predominant cell type was satellite cells (26.51%), followed by MPCs (25.59%) and macrophages (19.12%), both of which are also believe to be important in HO formation and initial immune response. It was clearly observed that in the early post-injury period (day 7), the content of all macrophages began to rise rapidly, while MPCs showed a striking contrast, with a massive decrease in MPC1 and a noticeable increase in MPC2, suggesting that HO formation may have been initiated unobtrusively in the early stages of inflammation (Fig. 1C). We then examined the expression of top-ranked marker genes within each cell cluster. The results revealed pronounced specificity among distinct cell types, while similar populations varied widely despite sharing certain core signatures (Fig. 1D). This unique transcriptional landscape not only confirms the robustness of our clustering but, more importantly, may indicate fundamental functional differences among these cell populations, for example, MPC1 was outstanding in driving cell adhesion and migration, whereas MPC2 was probably superior in regulating angiogenesis (Fig. 1E), and responses by these cells after injury might contribute to aberrant tissue regeneration. This finding suggests that imperfect development of MPCs was the most causative factor in inducing HO formation, and led us to next examine whether any flaws occurred in the developmental trajectory of MPCs. For macrophages, in addition to their immunomodulatory functions, their activation or polarization imbalance is even more emphatically emphasized to play a key role in osteogenic differentiation. Taken together, macrophage-associated changes may be important in the local microenvironment, leading to aberrant tissue regeneration in MPC, as seen in HO formation.

Trajectory analysis from scRNA-seq indicated HO formation is skewed between MPC cells

To understand the hypothetical developmental relationships that might exist within the MPC clusters, we extracted all MPCs for pseudotime analysis, identified two branch points and five cell states (Fig. 2A), and mapped developmental trajectories along pseudotemporal time (Fig. 2B). Cells (state 1) at the beginning of the trajectory are predominantly MPC1 with MSC characteristics, while MPC2 with the potential to differentiate into tenocytes are primarily distributed at the late or end stages along the pseudotemporal trajectory (Fig. 2C), similar to what was predicted. However, as state 1 cells (referred to as S1 herein) transitioned towards differentiated states S2 and S2 further progressed towards S3, two additional distinct cell fates arose during this period, highlighting the aberrant developmental program present in MPCs at the site of injury during trauma-induced HO. Next, we tracked expression changes in pseudotime-dependent genes that could potentially lead the MPC developmental trajectory astray, with the high probability that they were central drivers of osteogenesis. Specifically, changes after the first branch resulted in two cellular states (S3 and S4), with S3 demonstrating an overly aggressive immune-inflammatory response and S4 expressed markers of tendon development and endochondral ossification, while the pre-branch showed enrichment of pathways associated with signal transduction, extracellular matrix (ECM), and cell differentiation. After the second branch there was a new subset (S5) enriched for ECM-related genes and pathways related to skeletal system development and an intermediate state characterized by regulation of cell population proliferation, migration, angiogenesis, osteoblast differentiation, and multicellular organism development (Fig. 2D). Notably, since the aberrant trajectory deviations all involved the extracellular matrix, a bio-environment essential for tissue repair response as well as bone formation and regeneration, it is likely that these MPC1-directed states are dynamic and interconvertible, suggesting that they might undergo remodeling in HO, thereby influencing cell fate decisions and regulating HO episodes. In short, the skewing of the MPC developmental trajectory in HO contributes greatly to osteogenic differentiation potential and drives osteochondrogenic capacity.

Macrophage-derived Osm signaling contribute to triggering the osteogenic potential of MPCs.

Considering that HO develops from stimulating MPCs within an inflammatory context, we focused on crosstalk among various types of cells in the microenvironment. Analyzing cell-cell crosstalk provided clear insights into the key signaling pathways by which macrophage promote the osteogenic differentiation of MPCs. As expected, the cell interaction information and strength were markedly more active on day7 and day21 after injury compared to day0 (Fig. 3A). Notably, macrophages, as senders, displayed a sharp contrast in the strength of interactions with the receivers MPC1 and MPC2, possibly related to different cell fate decisions (Fig. 3B). Among the several inferred ligand-receptor pairs with significant differential expression, we identified a number of ligand genes expressed by macrophages that are associated with cartilage and bone formation, such as collagens, osteoblastin, transforming growth factor β, and others, indicating HO-dependent macrophage-MPC crosstalk (Fig. 3C). Some ligands showed reciprocally segregated expression in macrophages, in particular oncostatin M (Osm), expressed specifically in pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages, which mediated signaling pathway that might strongly influence MPC1 differentiation, thereby inducing HO formation (Fig. 3D). Correspondingly, its receptors Osmr and Il6st in MPC1 also significantly upregulated after injury (Fig. 3E), suggesting that M1 macrophages might interplay with MPC1 via Osm signaling, thereby promoting the osteogenic potential of latter in response to proinflammatory stimuli.

Spi1 is a potential regulator of macrophage activation and Osm expression.

Given the pivotal role of M1 macrophages in inflammation and induction of Osm secretion, we further explored the potential mechanisms underlying this process. We next focused our analysis on the activity of regulons in M1 macrophages based on gene regulatory networks consisting of TFs and their putative target genes, and determined the preferential activity of regulons corresponding to Nfe2, Batf, and Cebpe (Fig. 4A). To identify transcriptional regulators potentially governing M1 macrophage activation in HO pathology, we analyzed TFs with differentially transcriptional activities (Fig. 4B), and observed substantial overlap between the two post-injury time points, suggesting that 14 shared differentially expressed TFs (DETFs) were significantly activated during disease progression (Fig. 4C). Among these DETFs, we found that Spi1 was favorably involved in the regulation of Osm expression, with a much higher relative regulatory importance score than other candidates (Fig. 4D), and the Spi1 target genes showed significant enrichment during cell activation and immune inflammatory response (Fig. 4E), implying the importance of Spi1 in regulating M1 macrophage activation. Additionally Spi1 was obviously highly expressed in M1 macrophages, and its expression level and regulatory activity were significantly up-regulated in two post-injury time points (Fig. 4F). This result strongly suggests that Spi1 is produced by activated M1 macrophages in the context of inflammatory injury and promotes Osm expression to induce MPC osteogenic differentiation in mice following HO development.

Discussion

HO is a pathological disease of inflammation-mediated bone formation [34], and while macrophages have been well characterized in inflammatory response and tissue repair, the phenotypic and functional heterogeneity of this cell population in musculoskeletal trauma remains poorly understood. This study used scRNA-seq data to elucidate a crucial role of Osm-expressing M1 macrophages in HO formation after musculoskeletal injury through activation of MPCs differentiation into bone tissue. Our work also reveals that Spi1, a putative TF for Osm, not only stimulates activation of M1 macrophages after injury, but also regulates Osm expression of these cells, leading to ectopic bone generation.

Tendon injuries first typically trigger an inflammatory response that leads to a cascade of cellular and molecular events aimed at tissue repair [35]. Likewise, in our results, a massive recruitment of macrophages, both M1 and M2 macrophages, can be clearly observed after injury. Simultaneously, the contrasting alterations in the different subsets of MPCs are also likely to reflect the multilineage differentiation potential of precursor cells. Specifically, the marked decrease in Pdgfra-expressing MPC1 was largely attributed to the fact that it might promote ectopic cartilage formation in HO [36], whereas conversely, the dramatic expansion of MPC2 after injury favored differentiation into tenocytes because of it specifically expressed Tnn and in this way restored stability to the tendon status. From the prospect of MPC differentiation trajectories, the abnormal chondrogenic cell fate of MPCs has long been predetermined, indicating that the inflammatory phase is a critical time window for promoting osteogenesis. Moreover, our pseudotemporal analysis provides additional evidence that signals from the microenvironment control their differentiation or migration. These insights open avenues for therapeutic strategies aimed at blocking stromal immune signals or manipulating MPCs differentiation, which may alter cell fate determination.

A growing body of evidence indicates that immune cells induce ectopic bone formation by modulating the microenvironment [15, 37]. Macrophages, mast cells, neutrophils, and lymphocytes have all been implicated in HO activation [18, 38–40]. These cells are recruited to the injury site following trauma, where they initiate tissue repair by interacting with tissue-resident stem cells within the inflammatory microenvironment. Typically, immune cells primarily regulate immune responses and influence HO development through the secretion of cytokines [41]. Moreover, the involvement of various immune checkpoint proteins and immunomodulatory factors adds further complexity to this pathological process [42, 43]. Undeniably, immune cells make substantial contributions to HO formation.

To better characterize the contribution of inflammatory cells during aberrant MPC differentiation, we targeted the phenotype and function of macrophages, which are required for different inflammatory stages in HO. It is generally accepted that macrophages are key mediators linking inflammation to the HO pathogenesis, exerting their influence across different stages through dynamic polarization states and secretion of various cytokines [16]. During the initial phase of HO, M1 macrophages extensively infiltrate the affected tissue, where they release pro-inflammatory cytokines and interact with MSCs to facilitate their transition from a quiescent to a proliferative state [44, 45]. In the subsequent later stage of inflammation, M2 macrophages become dominant and support the chondrogenic differentiation capacity of MSCs by establishing an immunosuppressive microenvironment and secreting numerous osteogenic factors [46, 47]. We found that Osm-producing macrophages may serve as a critical link between inflammation and aberrant MPC differentiation, it is expressed by recruited M1 macrophages and absent in M2 macrophages, and could significantly promote crosstalk with MPCs after injury. Previous reports have shown that Osm is a macrophage-secreted inflammatory cytokine driving the progression of numerous inflammatory diseases and is also required for the induction of osteogenesis [48–50]. Therefore, it is likely that Osm signaling contributes to osteogenic differentiation of progenitor cells in HO, and mice with Osm receptor (Osmr)-deficiency further confirm that Osm involved in this process of ectopic bone generation [51]. Overall, our results suggest that the Osm/Osmr signaling pathway between M1 macrophages and MPCs is an important promoter of HO development.

Osm has been reported to regulate macrophage M1/M2 polarization through the transcriptional co-activator YAP and to induce the expression of osteogenic-related factors [52]. Nevertheless, the effect and mechanism of Osm in HO remains largely unclear. Our analysis results provide a possibility to understand the gene regulatory network associated with Osm expression in HO, and one promising transcriptional regulator that emerged was Spi1, an early macrophage-specific pioneer transcription factor critical for macrophage migration and development in the innate immune response [53]. Spi1 has been found to mediate pro-inflammatory effects in several diseases, accompanied by macrophage polarization toward the M1 phenotype [54, 55]. Moreover, Spi1 has previously been shown to play important roles in osteoblast differentiation and pathological bone formation [56]. Collectively, these evidences suggests that Spi1 might contribute to the phenotypic modulation of macrophages toward an M1-like state during HO and promote the activation of the osteogenic program.

In conclusion, our findings reveal the critical role of macrophage subpopulations interacting with MPCs at different moments of traumatically induced HO progression in mice, present that Osm-expressing M1 macrophages govern the differentiation fate of aberrant stem cells. Meanwhile, Spi1 was identified as a novel regulator of early macrophage activation and Osm expression regulation in HO. Future work will require further comprehensive multimodal characterization in conjunction with complex human lesions, and in vitro investigations to enhance important mechanistic insights from the current findings and provide novel therapeutic targets for HO or other musculoskeletal injuries.
