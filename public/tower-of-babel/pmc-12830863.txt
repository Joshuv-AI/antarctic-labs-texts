Integrated geographical and ecological analysis reveals environmental drivers of Gardenia jasminoides distribution and chemical variation
Authors: Mingxu Zhang, Cong Zhou, Suhua Huang, Hui Wang, Tingting Shi, Meng Li, Zhixian Jing, Xiaobo Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGardenia jasminoides, a widely distributed resource rich in Crocin, has generated substantial market demand due to its potential value as a saffron substitute. This necessitates the exploration of efficient and sustainable cultivation strategies to obtain target compounds for specific purposes. To enhance cultivation efficiency and secure supply chains, we integrated MaxEnt modeling, spatial interpolation, and geodetector analysis. This framework aimed to predict suitable habitats for G. jasminoides across China, map spatial variation in bioactive compounds including Crocin, Gardenia Yellow, and Geniposide, and identify environmental drivers influencing their distribution. MaxEnt achieved high predictive accuracy (AUC = 0.960), identifying Jiangxi, Zhejiang, and Guangdong as key high-suitability regions. Precipitation of the driest month and human population density emerged as dominant factors shaping species distribution. Spatial gradients revealed that Crocin and Gardenia Yellow decrease from southwest to northeast, whereas Geniposide exhibits latitudinal differentiation characterized by higher concentrations in northern regions. Geodetector analysis highlighted vegetation type as the primary driver of compound variation, with q values of 0.618 for Crocin, 0.606 for Gardenia Yellow, and 0.639 for Geniposide. These results indicate that the accumulation of target compounds is strictly modulated by ecological niches, where specific vegetation types drive metabolic differentiation through microclimate regulation and interspecific competition. Based on these findings, we advocate for an industry-oriented divergent cultivation strategy. Southwestern China should be prioritized for Crocin-rich germplasm to support the natural pigment industry, whereas northern regions are designated as premium zones for pharmaceutical-grade Geniposide sourcing. Furthermore, recognizing vegetation type as a critical driver facilitates the implementation of targeted habitat management techniques. These findings provide a direct guide for designating priority cultivation zones and optimizing harvest timing to maximize the yield of target compounds for specific industrial uses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-32876-0.Keywords: Gardenia jasminoides, Suitable distribution, Spatial differentiation, Quality variation, Environmental driversSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences, Plant sciences

Introduction

Gardenia jasminoides, a member of the Rubiaceae family, is widely distributed and possesses extensive applications. Regardless of its utilization as a medicinal ingredient or food source, the consumption of plant materials remains substantial. The dried mature fruits of G. jasminoides are recognized as a traditional medicinal herb1. Modern research has demonstrated that G. jasminoides, particularly its bioactive component Geniposide, exerts hepatoprotective and choleretic effects. Notably, it does not induce secondary drug-induced liver injury, thereby conferring distinct advantages as a hepatoprotective agent2. In 2002, G. jasminoides was officially listed in China as a dual-purpose substance for both food and medicinal use, establishing its role as a common food ingredient3. Botanical surveys in China identify five species and one variety of Gardenia, primarily distributed south of the Yangtze River. Crocin represents a class of apo-carotenoid compounds recognized for diverse pharmacological activities, notably anti-tumor, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant effects. Beyond medicinal applications, this compound serves as a versatile additive in food production, fragrance formulation, and dye manufacturing. In nature, Crocin primarily accumulates in two plant sources, namely the stigmas of Crocus sativus of the Iridaceae family and the fruits of G. jasminoides. Although C. sativus stigmas contain the highest Crocin content among known plant sources, the low yield and labor-intensive harvesting process result in persistently high market prices. In contrast, G. jasminoides exhibits distinct advantages, including wide geographical distribution, large-scale cultivation potential, and high fruit productivity. With a Crocin content second only to C. sativus, it represents a promising alternative industrial source4,5. Despite these comparative advantages, the industrial advancement of G. jasminoides is currently constrained by a critical lack of ecological understanding. Existing research has predominantly focused on pharmacological applications and extraction technologies while neglecting the environmental drivers that regulate the spatial quality of the raw material. Specifically, the mechanisms by which distinct geographical variables differentially shape the accumulation of Geniposide for medicinal use versus Crocin for pigment production remain unquantified. This scientific void disconnects market demand from cultivation planning, as the absence of data-driven habitat suitability assessments hinders the implementation of precision agriculture. Consequently, the industry faces challenges in establishing stable supply chains that can specifically target high-yield regions for distinct bioactive compounds.

In recent years, a growing consensus indicates that understanding the geographical distribution patterns of plant species is crucial for the sustainable management of plant resources6. Various models for assessing species habitats and predicting spatial distributions have been widely applied in ecological research. Among these, niche models are extensively utilized due to their dual advantages of requiring relatively limited input data while delivering high-precision predictive outcomes7,8. As the most prevalent niche model, MaxEnt integrates two core elements: maximum entropy, which represents ideal ecological conditions, and the ecological niche, which reflects actual environmental constraints. By utilizing known species distribution locations and multi-dimensional environmental data, the model simulates and predicts the potential distribution ranges of target species9. MaxEnt employs environmental variable data from both species occurrence points and background sample points to estimate the probability of occurrence under specific environmental conditions10. Notably, the model can yield reliable results even with a limited number of sampling points. This capability is particularly critical for this study on G. jasminoides, as it effectively addresses the practical challenge of sparse and uneven field sampling data across China to ensure accurate habitat suitability predictions even in under-surveyed southern mountainous regions. This framework establishes a foundation for the targeted protection of wild G. jasminoides and science-based artificial cultivation while providing a visual guide for optimizing industrial zones. This approach helps prevent ecological damage from over-development and resource depletion while enhancing the industrial chain. Ultimately, the study bridges wild resource conservation and cultivation development, promoting sustainable G. jasminoides utilization and maximizing integrated benefits across ecological protection, local agricultural economic growth, and the stable supply of high-quality raw materials for the medicinal and food additive sectors.

Result

Gardenia jasminoides habitat suitability distribution

The AUC value reached 0.960 (Figure 1), indicating that the predictive performance of the model is excellent and highly reliable. Based on the distribution probabilities predicted by MaxEnt, habitat suitability was categorized into four spatial classes using the Natural Breaks algorithm: unsuitable, low suitability, medium suitability, and high suitability. Areas of high and medium suitability are primarily concentrated in Hunan, Jiangxi, Zhejiang, and Guangdong provinces. Low suitability areas are distributed in southeastern China, surrounding the high and medium suitability zones. Notably, a relatively suitable distribution area for G. jasminoides that is geographically separate from southeastern China has formed at the junction of Chongqing Municipality and Sichuan Province. Furthermore, the southeastern coastal region exhibits a continuous belt of medium-to-high suitability habitats (Figure 2).

Environmental drivers of the spatial distribution of Gardenia jasminoides

Jackknife analysis quantified the relative contribution of environmental factors to the MaxEnt model predictions of G. jasminoides habitat suitability (Table 1), while response curves for these variables are illustrated in Figure 3. Precipitation of Driest Month and Human Population Density exhibited substantial contributions to the model predictions. This indicates that Precipitation of Driest Month plays a crucial role in the growth of G. jasminoides and likely acts as a limiting factor for its distribution, whereas Human Population Density exerts a significant promotional effect on spatial distribution. Precipitation of Driest Month reaches optimal conditions for G. jasminoides distribution at approximately 48 mm. Beyond this threshold, the distribution probability of the species decreases with increasing precipitation. Regarding Human Population Density, its influence on G. jasminoides distribution rises significantly at lower values and peaks around 20,000. Subsequently, the probability declines gradually but remains at a level sustaining high habitat suitability for the species. This pattern indicates a strong spatial overlap between Human Population Density and G. jasminoides distribution.

Trend surface analysis and spatial interpolation results

Trend surface analysis and spatial interpolation were performed on Gardenia Yellow, Crocin, and Geniposide to investigate the potential spatial variation trends of distinct bioactive compounds in G. jasminoides (Figure 4 A–C). The spatial patterns of chemical constituents revealed by interpolation should be interpreted as preliminary trends, as they are based on a limited set of 23 sampling points. Gardenia Yellow generally exhibits a spatial pattern characterized by higher concentrations in the west and lower concentrations in the east, which presents a more gradual gradient, alongside lower concentrations in the north and higher concentrations in the south, exhibiting a more pronounced gradient. Spatially, this translates to a gradual decrease in Gardenia Yellow content from the southwest to the northeast, as visualized in the map. Crocin shares a similar distribution trend to Gardenia Yellow, with only minor deviations observed in northern Fujian Province and southern Zhejiang Province. In contrast, Geniposide displays an opposing distribution pattern characterized by a gentle east-west gradient and a distinct trend of higher concentrations in the north relative to the south.

Ecological drivers of the spatial differentiation of chemical components

In the factor detection results of the Geodetector analysis, the q-statistic quantified the explanatory power of ecological factors regarding the spatial distribution of chemical components in G. jasminoides, where the q-statistic represents the proportion of the component’s spatial variance explained by a given factor (Figure 4D). Among all analyzed factors, Vegetation Type exerted the strongest influence on the spatial differentiation of the three key chemical components, including Crocin, Gardenia Yellow, and Geniposide. For Geniposide specifically, Soil Type and Precipitation of Driest Month also contributed notably to spatial variability, although their explanatory power was lower than that of Vegetation Type.

Discussion

Variable selection rationale and key drivers of Gardenia jasminoides habitat suitability

This study demonstrated that Precipitation of Driest Month and Annual Precipitation are the most influential factors shaping the habitat suitability of G. jasminoides. This finding aligns with prior ecological research and underscores the practical relevance of our analysis. For instance, Miao et al.11 identified average precipitation in April and November, alongside average August temperature, as critical drivers of G. jasminoides growth. They further noted that the suitable regions for the species are relatively concentrated, spanning from southern Shaanxi, southern Henan, and central Anhui in the north to northern Hainan in the south, central Sichuan in the west, and extending eastward to coastal Zhejiang and northeastern Taiwan. Notably, Annual Precipitation exhibited a low percent contribution of 0.7% but the highest permutation importance of 46.9%. This discrepancy can be attributed to the collinearity among precipitation variables and the algorithmic mechanics of MaxEnt. While Precipitation of Driest Month likely captured the primary contribution during the iterative model building process due to shared information, the high permutation importance of Annual Precipitation reveals its irreplaceable role in the final model. This suggests that Annual Precipitation acts as a fundamental limiting factor, defining the broad climatic boundaries necessary for the species’ survival, whereas other variables refine local suitability within these boundaries.

To comprehensively unpack the drivers of G. jasminoides spatial distribution, we first selected 19 widely recognized bioclimatic variables from the WorldClim database, consistent with standard protocols in ecological niche modeling, to capture the combined influence of biological and climatic processes. Furthermore, we integrated human activity-related data, specifically Human Population Density, a factor long overlooked in prior habitat suitability assessments for this species. This inclusion aligns with a well-established scientific consensus that human activities exert a profound impact on plant distribution patterns12, driving global species redistribution while creating opportunities for populations to establish outside native ranges13. Consequently, incorporating data on human activity enhances the predictive rationality of G. jasminoides distribution models.

The dual effects of precipitation constraints and human population density on Gardenia jasminoides

Our results delineate a clear mechanistic hierarchy driving the distribution of G. jasminoides, where natural hydrothermal conditions define the fundamental boundaries while Human Population Density actively shapes the actual distribution range. Specifically, Precipitation of Driest Month emerges as the decisive natural constraint, outweighing Annual Precipitation. This finding refines the understanding of water dependence, suggesting that the survival of the species is governed not merely by total annual water availability but critically by the cumulative moisture stress during the dry season. This aligns with prior phenological observations by Cao et al.14, who demonstrated that key phenotypic traits including fruit fresh weight exhibit significant positive correlations with Annual Precipitation, further emphasizing the physiological sensitivity of G. jasminoides to water deficits.

In contrast to these natural climatic constraints, the prominent contribution of Human Population Density elucidates a key dynamic regarding the spatial distribution of G. jasminoides. We acknowledge that the occurrence records utilized in this model likely represent a composite of both wild and cultivated populations, which may affect the results of this study. Consequently, the high predictive power of Human Population Density suggests that modeled suitability reflects areas supported by cultivation management rather than a strictly natural wild distribution. We speculate that a strictly wild distribution would likely exhibit a fragmented pattern confined to natural refugia in mountainous terrains and demonstrate stronger dependence on natural precipitation regimes due to the absence of anthropogenic irrigation buffering. Moderate Human Population Density acts as a facilitative driver for G. jasminoides by indicating the presence of agricultural infrastructure and labor availability. This pattern correlates with standardized G. jasminoides cultivation practices in southern China15, where moderate human intervention has facilitated the formation and expansion of suitable habitats. This facilitation is evident in the identification of the independent high-suitability region around Chongqing and the continuous belt along the southeastern coast.

However, this influence of Human Population Density exhibits a threshold effect. While moderate density associated with agricultural management promotes distribution, excessive density typical of urbanization exerts negative pressure. Furthermore, while high-suitability areas constitute the core of industrial production, marginal habitats including northern Hainan and the Chongqing-Sichuan border serve as critical reservoirs for genetic diversity. Under specific environmental fluctuations or through targeted management, these marginal regions possess the potential to transition into stable habitats, necessitating strengthened conservation strategies to ensure the long-term resilience of the species.

Spatial differentiation of chemical quality and implications for precision resource utilization

To maximize economic and ecological benefits of G. jasminoides resource utilization, production areas should be strategically selected based on distinct utilization objectives. Using geospatial analysis approaches, this study predicted the geographical distribution patterns of the key chemical components of the species. Crocin and Gardenia Yellow exhibit a distribution gradient characterized by higher concentrations in the south and lower concentrations in the north. In contrast, Geniposide, the medicinal indicator specified in the Chinese Pharmacopoeia, shows the opposite pattern, with higher content in northern regions and lower content in southern regions. These predictions align with findings from prior research. For instance, Hu et al.16 confirmed that Geniposide content is higher in northern Hubei Province, whereas Crocin-I and Gardenia Yellow pigment contents are elevated in southern Hunan Province. Notably, most existing studies on G. jasminoides quality across different production areas focus solely on comprehensive quality evaluation using various statistical methods17. In contrast, the results of this study highlight that distinct evaluation frameworks should be tailored to the development of G. jasminoides resources for different end uses, addressing a critical gap in current quality assessment practices. By mapping the spatial trends of bioactive compounds, we address a pressing industry challenge regarding the optimization of raw material sourcing for divergent applications. For instance, the production of Crocin and Gardenia Yellow should prioritize southern regions, including eastern Guangxi Province, where their concentrations reach peak levels. This strategy not only ensures high yields but also reduces extraction costs. Conversely, manufacturing processes dependent on Geniposide, including traditional medicines and healthcare supplements, benefit from raw materials sourced from northern regions, such as northwestern Hubei Province, to enhance compliance with pharmacopeial standards for medicinal quality.

It is important to acknowledge that the limited number of field-collected samples may have impacted the study results. To mitigate the influence of insufficient sample size on MaxEnt predictions, we augmented the dataset with G. jasminoides distribution records from the Global Biodiversity Information Facility18. Regarding the chemical analysis, the twenty-three sampling sites were strategically distributed to maximize geographical coverage across the primary production regions. We further mitigated the sample size constraint by employing spatial interpolation and trend surface analysis. These geostatistical methods leverage inherent spatial autocorrelation to estimate continuous chemical surfaces from discrete sampling points, effectively extracting underlying spatial patterns driven by macro-environmental factors. However, the prediction of geographical patterns for different chemical components may still be constrained in transitional zones characterized by complex topography. As indicated by the spatial interpolation anomalies located at the Guizhou–Guangxi border (Figure 2 and Figure 4C), denser sampling is required to refine predictions in these complex areas. Future studies should integrate multi-year harvest data to account for temporal variability. Furthermore, although G. jasminoides exhibits a widespread global distribution, the geographical scope of this study was specifically concentrated on China to ensure the methodological coupling of habitat suitability modeling with spatial chemical analysis. Extending the ecological predictions to a global scale without synchronized field sampling for chemical quantification would decouple environmental drivers from metabolic outcomes. Given the logistical constraints associated with acquiring standardized samples worldwide, focusing on the primary industrial production region ensures that the integrated analysis relies on verifiable data and provides high-precision guidance for the core supply chain.

Vegetation type may have an impact as a comprehensive proxy variable for local-scale microclimates

Geodetector analysis indicates that Vegetation Type exerts a significant influence on the spatial differentiation of Gardenia Yellow, Crocin, and Geniposide in G. jasminoides. Regarding Geniposide, Soil Type and Precipitation of Driest Month also play contributory roles. Most current research suggests that Soil Type impacts the formation of chemical components19,20. However, the relationship between Vegetation Type and plant chemical profiles remains under-discussed. We speculate that, unlike single edaphic or climatic factors, Vegetation Type functions as a comprehensive proxy variable for multiple ecological factors, driving the synthesis and accumulation of secondary metabolites. First, Vegetation Type is closely associated with variations in the local microclimate. Studies indicate that variations in vegetation structures modulate regional microclimates by influencing temperature, relative humidity, wind speed, evapotranspiration, and solar radiation21–24. Reciprocally, variables including topography and temperature shape regional Vegetation Type25. Research demonstrates that the biosynthesis of Gardenia Yellow and Crocin, both carotenoid derivatives, is closely linked to photosynthesis. Consequently, Vegetation Type likely serves as a comprehensive proxy for these light-dependent variables26. Specifically, open vegetation types expose G. jasminoides to higher solar radiation, whereas closed canopies maintain stable humidity and temperature despite reducing photosynthetic potential. Alternatively, specific microclimates may induce environmental stress in G. jasminoides, thereby significantly promoting the accumulation of secondary metabolites27. Second, a close correlation and mutual driving effect exist between Vegetation Type and soil microbiome interactions28–30. Previous research confirms that the formation of plant secondary metabolites is significantly modulated by soil microbial communities31,32. Therefore, we hypothesize that specific Vegetation Type and Soil Type may indirectly affect the biosynthesis of bioactive components in G. jasminoides by stimulating specific metabolic pathways mediated by distinct soil microbial communities. Finally, Vegetation Type may reflect different stages of community succession, thereby influencing interspecific competition33,34. This biotic stress leads to the differential allocation of secondary metabolites, resulting in spatial differentiation of chemical composition. Evidence suggests that moderate environmental stress or interspecific competition can induce the upregulation of synthesis pathways for defensive secondary metabolites35. For instance, Geniposide, an iridoid glycoside, is generally recognized as a critical chemical defense agent against herbivores and pathogens36. Based on this multi-dimensional mechanistic exploration, future research should integrate genomics and proteomics to verify, at the molecular level, the mechanisms by which variables including Vegetation Type and Soil Type drive the secondary metabolism of G. jasminoides. This approach will help accurately elucidate the molecular mechanisms by which the ecological environment shapes the accumulation of secondary metabolites.

Conclusions

In summary, this study integrated ecological modeling with spatial chemical analysis to delineate the precise habitat suitability and quality formation mechanisms of G. jasminoides. Our findings identify Jiangxi, Zhejiang, and Guangdong as the core production regions, where the distribution is predominantly constrained by Precipitation of Driest Month and Human Population Density. Beyond habitat suitability, we revealed that key chemical components exhibit distinct spatial trends driven by ecological factors. Crocin and Gardenia Yellow exhibit a southwest-to-northeast decreasing gradient, whereas Geniposide displays a clear latitudinal preference for northern regions. Crucially, this research establishes Vegetation Type as the primary environmental driver, implying that this factor likely acts as a comprehensive proxy for complex ecological conditions. We infer that the spatial differentiation of chemical components is potentially shaped by the distinct microclimates and soil microbial communities associated with different vegetation structures. Consequently, we propose a precision zoning strategy to maximize industrial value. Southwestern regions should be prioritized for the cultivation of pigment-rich germplasm to serve the natural dye and food sectors, whereas northern high-latitude areas are designated as premium zones for pharmaceutical sourcing to ensure high Geniposide content. This approach advances the transition from indiscriminate cultivation to precision production zoning, providing a solid scientific foundation to refine resource distribution and ensure the long-term availability of this medicinal crop.

Materials and method

Collection of spatial distribution data and samples of Gardenia jasminoides

The spatial distribution data for G. jasminoides were primarily derived from the Global Biodiversity Information Facility (GBIF) and field collections (10.15468/dl.nehc7t). Initially, 2,287 records spanning from 1911 to 2024 were retrieved. To align with current climatic conditions, we retained only records from the past two decades. Subsequently, ENMtools was employed to mitigate model overfitting by removing duplicates and rarefying points within a 1 km distance3738. This process yielded 150 valid records from GBIF. Regarding field data, we established 23 sampling sites through fieldwork conducted in autumn 2020 and 2022, comprising 15 initial sites and 8 supplementary sites. The spatial distribution of these sites is illustrated in Figure 5. Although the chemical composition analysis was restricted to twenty-three sampling sites due to the resource-intensive nature of field collection, these points were strategically distributed to encompass the primary production regions of G. jasminoides, thereby ensuring high representativeness. Furthermore, to analyze spatial heterogeneity, we employed spatial interpolation and trend surface analysis to mitigate potential biases associated with discrete sampling points.

Collection and selection of environmental data

To explore the potential impact of bioclimatic variables on the spatial distribution of G. jasminoides, this study selected the widely recognized WorldClim database (https://www.worldclim.org/). WorldClim provides high-resolution global weather and climate data extensively used for mapping and spatial modeling. Version 2.1 of the database offers 19 bioclimatic variables derived from monthly temperature and precipitation values, representing biologically meaningful indicators39. Additionally, we utilized the corresponding Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (SRTM) elevation data.

We also acquired Vegetation Type data and the 2020 Chinese population spatial distribution grid dataset. Vegetation Type data were obtained from the 1:1,000,000 Vegetation Atlas of the Resource and Environmental Science Data Platform (https://www.resdc.cn/). A close relationship between plant distribution and human activities has long been confirmed12. Human activities drive the redistribution of global species and provide opportunities for populations to establish beyond their native ranges13. Therefore, incorporating Human Population Density into model construction enhances the predictive rationality of the models. Vegetation Type data reflect the structure, function, and interactions of ecosystems. The population spatial distribution grid dataset incorporates various factors, including administrative population data, land use types, night-time lighting, and settlement density. This dataset employs a multi-factor weight allocation method to project population data, originally based on administrative units, onto a spatial grid network40.

Soil Type data were sourced from the High-Resolution World Soil Database version 2.0 (HWSD2), a global database providing detailed soil properties and classification information crucial for understanding the relationships between soil, environment, ecology, and agriculture41. Through data resampling in ArcGIS, we ensured that the spatial resolution of all environmental data was standardized to 30 arc-seconds (~1 km2). MaxEnt model parameter settings

MaxEnt 3.4.1 software was utilized, employing a 10-fold cross-validation approach to generate response curves for environmental variables. The importance of environmental variables was assessed using the Jackknife procedure. The MaxEnt prediction results were generated in the Logistic output format, yielding a predicted distribution probability ranging from 0 to 1. This format enhances result interpretability and was saved as an ASC file type. The ASC result files were subsequently converted into TIFF format using ArcGIS for map rendering. Finally, habitat suitability was categorized into four spatial classes using the Natural Breaks algorithm: unsuitable, low suitability, medium suitability, and high suitability. The Natural Breaks algorithm (Jenks optimization) is a standard clustering method in cartography that minimizes within-group variance and maximizes between-group variance. Therefore, this method allows for a more reasonable delineation of the hierarchical structure of the potential spatial distribution of G. jasminoides43.

The model accuracy was tested using the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve (ROC). The ROC curve is based on a threshold-independent evaluation of model accuracy, utilizing every predicted value as a potential judgment threshold from which corresponding sensitivity and specificity are calculated. The Area Under the Curve (AUC) is the metric derived from the ROC curve, quantifying the accuracy of the model’s predictive performance within a range of [0, 1]. A higher AUC value signifies stronger model discrimination ability and predictive power. The evaluation criteria are generally defined as follows: an AUC of 0.80 to 0.90 indicates fair prediction results; 0.90 to 0.95 indicates better prediction results; and 0.95 to 1.00 indicates excellent prediction results44.

Determination of chemical components in Gardenia jasminoides

To ensure the reliability of the results, chemical component analysis was conducted on 15 samples collected in 2020 and supplemented with 8 additional samples collected in 2022 (totaling 23 sites). The same instruments and methods were utilized for both batches of measurements45. The analysis was performed using UPLC-MS/MS with 70% ethanol as the extraction solvent. Chromatographic separation was carried out on an ACQUITY UPLC™ BEH C18 column (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm). The mobile phase system consisted of acetonitrile containing 0.1% formic acid and 0.1% aqueous formic acid under gradient elution conditions. The flow rate was set at 0.6 mL/min, the column temperature was maintained at 40 °C, and the injection volume was 1.0 μL.

Trend surface analysis and spatial interpolation analysis of chemical components in Gardenia jasminoides

Trend surface analysis (TSA) is a geostatistical method for studying spatial development trends and gradual change patterns at a regional scale. It decomposes each attribute observation value into a trend component and a residual value to reflect systematic variation while isolating effects caused by local and accidental variations46,47). The essence of TSA lies in data fitting, utilizing planar or geographical coordinates as independent variables against the attribute value as the dependent variable. It assumes that local anomalies and random errors possess zero expectation and covariance. The least squares method is applied to obtain the estimate that minimizes the sum of squared residuals, reflecting the overall regional trend. The implementation primarily utilizes polynomial fitting, which fits observed values or polynomial regression results to calculate the linear model of the mathematical surface, thereby generating a continuous spatial representation4849,. Geodetector analysis of the ecological drivers of chemical component spatial differentiation

The Geodetector is a statistical method designed to detect spatial heterogeneity and reveal the underlying driving forces51. The q-statistic measures spatial heterogeneity, identifies explanatory factors, and analyzes interactions between variables52. A larger q-statistic indicates a stronger influence of the independent variable on the spatial differentiation of the dependent variable,conversely, the influence is weaker. In this study, analyses were performed using the gdm function within the GD package in R, which automatically selects the optimal stratification parameters for the analysis53. Utilizing the Geographical Detector, we quantified the extent to which ecological factors explain the spatial differentiation of chemical components in G. jasminoides.
