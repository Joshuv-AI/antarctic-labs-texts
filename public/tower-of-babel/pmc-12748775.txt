Experimental study on track irregularity of straddle-type monorail
Authors: Zengchuang Zhao, Pingbo Wu, Lihui Ren
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAs straddle-type monorail systems evolve toward higher operational speeds, steeper gradients, and more diverse structural configurations, the design of guideway beams has become increasingly complex. Surface irregularities on the guideway beams have emerged as major sources of excitation in the vehicle–guideway interaction system, making it imperative to investigate the characteristics of track irregularities specific to straddle-type monorail lines. Although track irregularity profiles can be acquired through field measurements, the large volume of data collected poses challenges for numerical modeling. In current dynamic simulations, irregularities on the running, guidance, and stabilization surfaces are often approximated using standard spectra such as ISO 8608 Class A road profiles or empirical railway spectra, due to the scarcity of high-fidelity measured data. To improve the operational performance of monorail vehicles and to refine the boundary conditions used in dynamic modeling, a field test was conducted on a revenue-service straddle-type monorail line. Dedicated onboard measurement equipment was installed to collect real-world track irregularity data. A full-length spectral analysis of the test line was carried out and compared against U.S. Class 3, 4, 5 and 6 railway spectra and ISO 8608 Class A and B reference levels. The vertical irregularity spectra, along with the lateral irregularities on the guidance and stabilization surfaces, were quantitatively assessed and fitted using numerical methods. Sections with both relatively smooth and rough track profiles were selected for detailed power spectral density (PSD) analysis and spectral fitting. Based on amplitude distributions, the measured spectra were categorized into two quality levels: Class A (excellent) and Class B (poor). Corresponding spectral fitting coefficients were proposed for each category. This study characterizes the spectral features of straddle-type monorail track irregularities and provides critical reference data for the coupled dynamic design and optimization of vehicle–guideway systems.Subject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The straddle-type monorail system offers distinct advantages such as low noise levels, strong climbing capability, and excellent adaptability to tight-radius curves, making it particularly suitable for regions with significant topographic variations. It has been widely and successfully implemented in cities like Chongqing, China, known for its mountainous terrain1,2. Track irregularities serve as the primary excitation source for vehicle vibrations, representing a critical factor in track-related influences on operational safety, ride comfort, and stability. These irregularities alter wheel-rail contact relationships and significantly impact the dynamic characteristics of the wheel-rail system. When subjected to track irregularity excitation, vehicle vibrations are transmitted through wheel-rail contact points to the track beam structure, creating dynamic interactions within the vehicle-track beam system. Consequently, track irregularities are recognized as one of the main excitation sources for vibrations in vehicle-bridge systems. Unlike conventional rail transit systems, straddle-type monorails differ not only in their operation and guidance mechanisms as well as wheel-rail contact relationships but also in the surface irregularities of their track beams compared to traditional steel-wheel-steel-rail systems. While research on track irregularities in conventional rail transit systems is relatively mature, the running tracks of monorail systems differ substantially from traditional rail tracks. Existing literature contains limited studies on surface irregularities of monorail track beams.

Building on road surface spectral models, Lee3,4 developed and experimentally validated (using a 36m steel bridge) simulation methods for monorail track irregularities, establishing foundational work widely cited in subsequent studies5–8.

Wang9 proposed a method for measuring vertical irregularities in monorail track beams using the tilt correction method, completing field measurements for three projects and performing spectral estimation and fitting of monorail vertical irregularities. However, the tilt correction method suffers from low measurement efficiency, significant influence from subjective human factors, and cannot be applied to measure irregularities in track beam web plates. Chen10 established vibration equations for two substructures using Lagrange equations and modal synthesis methods respectively to solve random dynamic responses. The results demonstrated that deck irregularities significantly affect vibration responses in both the vehicle body and track beam. Cui11, in a study on straddle-type monorail ride comfort, employed numerical simulation to derive the U.S. Class 6 track irregularity spectrum, which was then converted into track beam surface roughness and applied as excitation in straddle-type monorail simulations. Zhang et al.12,13, in research on PC track beam surface roughness, recommended using the Japanese spectrum for simulating irregularities on guide wheel and stabilizing wheel track beams, while suggesting that running wheel track surface irregularities should fall between the U.S. Class 6 track spectrum and the “very smooth road” grade in highway spectra. Xiao14 developed a straddle-type monorail track beam alignment detection system based on the chord measurement method, and created a monorail alignment analysis algorithm by combining track irregularities with road roughness measurements, ultimately obtaining characteristic parameters of the track beam. Zheng15, in vehicle dynamics simulation and optimization for straddle-type monorails, used the Class A road spectrum from national standards as the random road input in simulations after comparing with actual measured acceleration curves. Feng et al.16 designed a detection system for straddle-type monorail track beam surface irregularities using the fixed reference method as the measurement principle, and proposed two post-processing methods for irregularity data. Liu et al6. derived vertical control equations for the coupled motion of straddle-type monorail beams and vehicles based on force compatibility conditions, calculating vertical dynamic responses of both track beams and vehicles under different train speeds and track irregularity conditions. Gao et al.17 established a vehicle-bridge coupling model using SIMPACK and ANSYS software, investigating the effects of train speed, pier height, track irregularities, and vehicle load on ride comfort.

Currently, the monorail track girders are primarily made of prestressed concrete (PC), with a small number being steel-concrete composite girders and steel girders. However, it must be noted that the wheel-rail contact relationship in the straddle-type monorail system (running wheel-running surface, guiding wheel-guiding surface) is mechanistically closer to the interaction between road tires and pavement, which is significantly different from the spatial coupling relationship of traditional steel wheel-steel rail systems in railways. Despite this fundamental difference, this study primarily draws on the concept and evaluation system of railway track irregularity in its analytical framework, mainly for the following two reasons: First (research continuity and comparability), systematic research on track irregularity in straddle-type monorail systems is still insufficient both domestically and internationally, lacking unified analytical standards. In contrast, the railway system has matured in this field, and adopting its methods helps establish a preliminary research framework and enables effective comparisons with internationally recognized spectra (such as the American track spectrum and ISO road spectrum). Second (similarity in excitation mechanisms), the geometric irregularity of the track girder surface, as the primary excitation source causing vibrations in the vehicle system, shares common physical characteristics with the railway context in terms of how geometric deviations affect vehicle dynamic responses. Therefore, methods such as power spectral density (PSD) analysis and amplitude statistics are applicable and effective. In light of this, this study does not aim to directly apply railway standards but rather hopes to use this transitional framework to address the current lack of large-scale measured data on the surface irregularity of track girders in operational straddle-type monorail lines, both domestically and internationally. Additionally, design specifications for straddle-type monorail systems still lack standards for track girder surface irregularity, and the power spectral density function of track girders remains to be studied and determined. Thus, experimental research on track irregularity is necessary.

Track irregularity type

Track irregularity refers to the deviation of the track’s geometric profile, dimensions, and spatial position from its nominal or designed state. In straddle-type monorail systems, the primary types of track irregularities can be classified into five categories: vertical irregularity, horizontal irregularity, beam width irregularity, beam alignment irregularity, and beam inclination irregularity. The manifestations of these five types of irregularities in monorail systems are as follows:

In straddle-type monorail systems, vertical irregularity refers to the unevenness in elevation along the longitudinal direction ((X)-axis) at the positions of the two running wheels. This type of irregularity primarily affects the vertical dynamic response of the vehicle. Horizontal irregularity is defined as the differential vertical displacement between the two running wheel positions within the same transverse section of the guideway beam, and it mainly influences the lateral dynamic behavior of the vehicle. These five irregularities can be derived from the measured vertical irregularities of the running surfaces (, ) and the lateral irregularities of the guiding (, ) and stabilizing surfaces (, ). In vehicle–track coupled dynamic simulations, the measured irregularity profiles are directly applied to the wheel–rail contact points to represent the effects of beam irregularities on vehicle dynamics. The track beams, including PC beams, steel–concrete composite beams, and a small number of steel beams, may develop beam width and beam cant irregularities due to manufacturing, installation, and operational factors such as deformation and settlement. Beam width irregularity alters horizontal wheel loads and affects curving guidance and anti-roll performance, while beam cant irregularity leads to uneven tire wear, lateral vibration, and reduced stability. In data analysis, both the guiding and stabilizing surface widths and their longitudinal irregularities are represented by beam width indices but within different wavelength ranges. The widths are obtained from direct measurements, whereas the irregularities are extracted through spatial filtering in the range of 1.5–42 m. According to TB/T 3355, beam width variations outside this range are excluded from the irregularity evaluation.

Track irregularity testing protocol

Testing protocol

By installing onboard track inspection equipment on the underframe of the electric train, geometric dynamic inspection was conducted on the track beams of a monorail line. The track beams along the entire line are primarily PC continuous beams, with steel-concrete composite beams used at individual large-span locations. Vehicle load: AW0 condition; Test speed: approximately 15km/h; Total line length: about 30km.

The detection of track irregularities for straddle-type monorail vehicles includes the running surface, stabilizing surface, and guiding surface irregularities. Therefore, sensors need to be installed at corresponding positions, and the spatial vector integration method is used for measurement and analysis. Testing was performed on the main line by installing an onboard track geometry dynamic inspection system (referred to as the track inspection system) on the electric train. The track inspection system mainly consists of a high-speed 2D digital laser measurement unit, an inertial measurement unit, and a data acquisition unit. The digital laser sensor is used to measure the track profiles of the running surface, guiding surface, and stabilizing surface; the inertial navigation system is used to measure the vehicle body attitude angles; and the data acquisition unit is used to measure the vehicle travel distance. The track irregularity status is calculated through multi-data fusion and the spatial vector integration method. It is worth noting that straddle-type monorail transit has distinctive characteristics: it adopts a railway-based operational mode while its running gear follows an automobile tire model. At present, however, no relevant standards or industry guidelines exist that describe or specify the measurement, analysis, and evaluation of track irregularities. Therefore, in this study we refer to the railway track spectrum standard TB3355 and the highway road spectrum standard ISO8608, and conduct a comparative analysis between the railway and highway spectra. Test principle

The onboard track inspection system primarily measures and calculates track geometric irregularities through multi-data fusion and spatial vector integration methods. Among these, the high-speed 2D digital laser displacement sensor measures profile variations of the running surface, guiding surface and stabilizing surface in real-time. Due to its high sampling frequency (500-600 cross-sections/second) and fast response time (exposure time less than 10 microseconds), the high-speed 2D laser sensor avoids the influence of vehicle vibration at the installation position on detection results. To reduce the influence of the vehicle’s inherent vibrations on the measurement system, the operating speed was lowered to 15 km/h, thereby minimizing the effect of car-body oscillations on the recorded data. The high-speed 2D sensors in the measurement system simultaneously measure the lateral and vertical movements between the car body and the track, compensating for relative displacements induced by vehicle motion, including those caused by body vibrations and swaying. In combination with the inertial navigation system, this setup enables accurate measurement of the spatial track irregularities. Moreover, due to the discontinuities in the cross-sectional profiles of certain local sections of the straddle-type track, it is necessary to first preprocess the line model data acquired by the track inspection system by removing outliers, particularly displacement jumps caused by beam joint gaps. In addition, the inspection system is equipped with a high-precision inertial navigation system, which records the vehicle’s attitude angles rather than relying directly on vibration acceleration data. Notably, even under relatively large acceleration conditions, fluctuations in the vehicle’s attitude angles remain minor. Finally, the displacement data obtained through laser scanning are integrated with the vehicle attitude data to derive the track geometry irregularities. The main testing methods for each parameter are described as follows.

Principle of vertical profile irregularity detection

As shown in Fig. 3, the definition of vertical profile irregularity is the vertical undulation along the longitudinal direction of the track running surface. It is mainly caused by elevation deviations from track construction, bridge deflection deformations, and residual deformations in trackbed and subgrade. The calculation method adopts the spatial vector integration principle, which computes the irregularity by utilizing the relationship between the pitch angle measured by the inertial navigation system and the vertical displacement between the vehicle and track measured by high-speed 2D lasers.

Impact of flat spot on axle dynamic stress.

Principle of alignment irregularity detection

The alignment irregularity is defined as the lateral undulation of the gauge point along the inner side of the rail in the longitudinal direction of the track. The calculation method also adopts the spatial vector integration principle, which computes the irregularity by utilizing the relationship between the azimuth angle measured by the inertial navigation system and the lateral displacement between the vehicle and track measured by high-speed 2D lasers. In the irregularity analysis, the minimum wavelength considered was 1.5 m. In addition, the large fluctuations in the raw data caused by the beam joints were smoothed in the data processing stage.

Comparison with U.S. spectra

The power spectral densities of U.S. Class 3, 4, 5, and 6 spectra were analyzed and compared with the vertical profile irregularity spectrum of the running surface, as shown in Fig. 22. The characteristics of the full-length vertical profile spectrum are as follows: the energy at wavelengths around 10m and 30m exceeds that of Class 3 spectrum; the energy at wavelengths around 20m is relatively close to that of Class 4 and 5 spectra. This phenomenon can be attributed to the particular characteristics of straddle-type monorail systems, in which the entire alignment is constructed on bridge structures. Consequently, within certain bridge spans, the power spectral density of track irregularity wavelengths appears more pronounced than that of the American spectrum. However, this deviation remains within an acceptable range and does not compromise the validity of the subsequent analysis.

Track spectrum classification and fitting

To facilitate operation and maintenance management of monorail track beam spectra, normal track sections without exceeding irregularity limits and abnormal sections with severe exceedances were selected for separate analysis. As shown in Figs. 28 and 29,the standard track spectra were classified into two categories based on amplitude distribution: A (Good) and B (Poor), with spectrum fitting performed. Monorail track spectra

Based on the fitted curves, the straddle-type monorail track spectral function was obtained. The track spectrum fitting was performed using the PSD Voigt2 model, which is primarily applied to data exhibiting Voigt-line characteristics. The Voigt function is a convolution of Gaussian and Lorentzian functions and is commonly used to describe spectral line broadening phenomena (e.g., in spectroscopy or XRD diffraction peaks). The PSD (Pseudo-Voigt) function is an approximation of the Voigt function, combining Gaussian and Lorentzian components with a weighting factor to achieve higher computational efficiency. From the full-line test data, sections with smaller irregularity amplitudes and relatively stable vehicle operation were selected to extract Type-A track spectra, while sections with larger irregularity amplitudes and poorer vehicle performance were used to extract Type-B track spectra.

Conclusion

Based on the track irregularity testing and comparative analysis of running surfaces, guiding surfaces, and stabilizing surfaces of the full-line monorail track beams, the main conclusions are formed as follows:

(1) The analysis of full-line track irregularities, evaluated according to Class I line standards for speeds below 80km/h in TB3355, shows that local sections exceed the ± 12 mm limit for vertical profile irregularities and the ± 10 mm limit for alignment irregularities, while overall measurements remain within standard requirements;

(2) The power spectral density analysis of full-line running surfaces, guiding surfaces and stabilizing surfaces reveals that the vertical profile irregularity spectrum of running surfaces shows relatively concentrated energy at certain wavelengths, greater than that of guiding and stabilizing surfaces; the track irregularity spectrum of steel-concrete composite beams shows relatively concentrated energy at certain wavelengths, greater than that of concrete beams;

(3) For vertical profile irregularity spectrum: the energy at wavelengths around 10 m and 30 m exceeds that of U.S. Class 3 spectrum, while the energy at wavelengths around 20 m is relatively close to that of U.S. Class 4 and 5 spectra; for alignment irregularity spectrum: the energy at wavelengths around 30–40 m exceeds that of U.S. Class 3 spectrum, while the energy at other wavelengths is comparable to that of U.S. Class 4 spectrum. Both vertical profile and alignment spectra are generally comparable to ISO 8608 Class A spectrum overall, without reaching ISO 8608 Class B spectrum level, though some wavelengths around 10 m and 30 m approach ISO 8608 Class B spectrum;

(4) Numerical fitting calculations were performed for the vertical profile spectrum and alignment spectra of guiding and stabilizing surfaces; the power spectral density was analyzed and numerically fitted for track sections with both smaller and larger irregularities; the track spectra were classified into Category A (Good) and Category B (Poor) based on amplitude distribution, and the classified fitting coefficients for track spectra were provided.
