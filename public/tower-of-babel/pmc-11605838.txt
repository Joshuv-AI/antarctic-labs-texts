Interferon-α as a biomarker to predict renal outcomes in lupus nephritis
Authors: Laura Patricia Whittall Garcia, Dafna D Gladman, Murray Urowitz, Dennisse Bonilla, Raphael Schneider, Zahi Touma, Joan Wither
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractABSTRACTObjectiveTo determine if serum interferon (IFN)-α levels at the time of a lupus nephritis (LN) flare are associated with renal outcomes.MethodsPatients with an LN flare who had a preflare estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) ≥60 mL/min were included in the study. The following outcomes were ascertained: (1) Time to first and second LN flares during follow-up, (2) Time to a sustained decline in eGFR by 30% and 50%, and progression to end-stage renal disease (ESRD, <15 mL/min), and (3) Time to an adverse renal event (≥2 renal flares and/or at least a 30% sustained decline in eGFR during follow-up). Serum IFN-α was measured by Simoa.Results92 patients with active LN were included in the study. Elevated serum baseline levels of IFN-α predicted poor renal outcomes. Patients with higher baseline IFN-α had a greater risk of having two or more subsequent LN flares (HR: 1.31 (1.08–1.59), p=0.006), sustained 30% decline in eGFR (HR: 1.27 (1.14–1.40), p<0.001), 50% decline in eGFR (HR: 1.27 (1.12–1.33), p<0.001) and progressing to ESRD (HR: 1.29 (1.14–1.47), p<0.001). Receiver operating characteristic analysis identified an IFN-α cut-off, 0.6 pg/ml, for predicting an adverse renal event.ConclusionsElevated serum IFN-α levels measured at the time of an LN flare are associated with poor renal outcomes, including the development of ≥2 LN flares, and a clinically meaningful decline in kidney function.Keywords: Lupus Nephritis; Interferon Type I; Lupus Erythematosus, Systemic

Introduction

Lupus nephritis (LN) affects up to 50% of patients with SLE1 2 and is an important predictor of morbidity and mortality.2,7 Despite progress in the management of LN, around 30% of patients will fail to respond to conventional treatment, and 30%–40% will experience a subsequent renal flare in the following 3–4 years.8 Both non-response to treatment and recurrent renal flares have the potential to cause renal damage and kidney functional decline. Currently, up to 20% of patients with LN will progress to end-stage renal disease (ESRD), requiring dialysis or renal transplant within 10–15 years after onset.2 9 One of the primary objectives in managing LN is maintaining kidney function. Therefore, it is crucial to identify patients at risk of poor renal outcomes during the early stages of the disease to optimise their treatment and surveillance. Unfortunately, baseline clinical, biological or pathological parameters have not been proven to reliably predict renal outcomes.8

Studies of the peripheral blood transcriptional profile in SLE have revealed that the majority of patients have increased expression of interferon-induced genes (IFIG), known as the interferon (IFN) gene signature, which is observed in over 70% of adults with SLE.10 11 It is now recognised that IFN, especially IFN-α, a type I IFN (IFN-I), plays a central role in the pathogenesis of SLE.12 13 In recent years, accumulating evidence supports the concept that activation of the IFN-I pathway is also associated with SLE disease severity14 15 and may predict a more severe disease course.16 17 However, the limited accessibility and considerable complexity of transcriptome-microarray technology means that IFN scores cannot be readily employed in clinical practice. In the past, it was not possible to measure serum levels of IFN-α, as the levels in most patients with SLE were below the threshold for detection of conventional ELISAs. With the development of digital ELISA technology (Single MOlecule Array (SIMOA)), it’s now possible to measure cytokines in the fg/ml range, allowing measurement of previously undetectable cytokines, including IFN-α. In the current study, we have used this technology to investigate whether the serum IFN-α level at the time of an LN flare is associated with renal outcomes.

Methods

Patients

Patients were recruited between April 2006 and May 2016 from the University of Toronto Lupus Cohort, where patients undergo regular follow-up every 2–6 months. Standardised protocols were performed during each assessment to gather clinical and laboratory data, and kidney biopsies were performed when clinically indicated. All patients fulfilled the European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology/American College of Rheumatology 2019 classification criteria.18 All patients who met inclusion criteria were included in the study, which were as follows: (1) LN flare defined as a urinary protein excretion of ≥500 mg/day in a 24-hour urine collection requiring modification/escalation in immunosuppressive treatment by the physician in charge; (2) Preflare (3 months before flare) estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) ≥60 mL/min; and (3) Serum stored within 3 months from the beginning of the LN flare. We excluded patients who had ESRD (eGFR <15 mL/min/1.73 m2, dialysis and/or kidney transplant) at baseline. The baseline was the time of the serum sample collection (within 3 months from the beginning of the LN flare). The serum samples came from two biobanks, the University of Toronto Lupus biobank and the LuNNET biobank.19 20

eGFR was calculated using the non-race-corrected Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation.21

Outcomes

To determine the association between IFN-α levels and renal outcomes, the following outcomes were used: (1) Time to first and second LN flares (defined by an increase in proteinuria to at least 1000 mg/d after attaining proteinuria of <500 mg/d or doubling of proteinuria if the reached proteinuria was ≥500 mg/d), that prompted an escalation or modification of glucocorticoids and/or immunosuppressive therapy by the treating physician, (2) Time to 30% and 50% sustained decline in eGFR (documented in two consecutive visits), and progression to ESRD from the baseline, and (3) Time to an adverse renal event, a composite outcome that was defined by either the time to the second renal flare and/or the time to at least a 30% sustained decline in eGFR.

IFN-α quantification

Archived samples (thawed once, subaliquoted and stored at −80°C) were used to measure IFN-α levels. Serum IFN-α levels were determined using the IFN-α Simoa Assay Kit (Quanterix, Massachusetts, USA), which has a linear detection range of 0–60 pg/mL.

Statistical analysis

We compared descriptive statistics of patients who met an adverse renal outcome, defined as two or more renal flares and/or at least a 30% sustained decline in eGFR, with those who did not. Baseline categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages, and continuous variables are presented as medians and IQRs.

Multivariable Cox proportional regression (HRs and 95% CIs) analysis was performed to determine the association between IFN-α levels and renal outcomes, including time to first and second LN flares, time to 30% and 50% sustained decline in eGFR, time to ESRD, and time to an adverse renal event. For the outcome of time to first and second LN flares, ESRD was considered as a competing risk, and Fine and Grey’s subdistribution hazard model was used. Confounding variables were selected a priori based on literature review.22,24 The type of immunosuppressive medication used for induction, sex and glucocorticoid dose used at baseline were not included in the multivariable analysis based on their high p values (p>0.3) in the likelihood ratio (LR) test, suggesting no impact on our outcomes.

The bootstrap validation method with 100 iterations was used to assess the models’ overfitting. Collinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor.25 The Cox proportional hazards assumption was checked using the ZPH function26 and log-log plots.

We performed receiver operating characteristics (ROC) analysis to determine the optimal predictive values for serum IFN-α and other predictors for adverse renal events. We chose the best cut-off value with the higher Youden Index (equivalent to the point on the ROC closest to the top-left corner). Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive values (PPV), negative predictive values (NPV), positive likelihood ratio test (+LR), and negative likelihood ratio test (–LR) for the IFN-α cut-off and other predictors for an adverse renal event were estimated with 95% CIs. Sensitivity, specificity, PPV and NPV above 80% were considered good and above 90% excellent. Likelihood ratios above 10 for +LR and below 0.1 for –LR were considered to provide strong evidence for developing an adverse renal event.27 To determine if similar findings were present in the patients with biopsy-proven LN at baseline (n=71), we conducted the same analysis on this subcohort as we did for the entire group.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software V.4.1.2 and all values of p were two-sided, with values <0.05 considered statistically significant. GraphPad Prism V.9 was used to generate graphs.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or plans for dissemination of our research.

Results

Cohort characteristics

A total of 92 patients with active LN were included in the study (table 1). The median (IQR) follow-up was 132.0 (96.0, 162.5) months and the time between the start of the renal flare and the serum sample collection was 1 (0, 2) month. The median (IQR) age of the patients was 29 (22.9, 42.0) years and the disease duration was 6.1 (0.2, 10.0) years. Seventy-six (82.6%) were women, 37 (40.2%) were Caucasian, 21 (22.8%) were Black and 22 (23.9%) were Asian. The median preflare eGFR was 113 (99.0, 127.0) mL/min, and the baseline eGFR was 94.0 (67.0, 119.4). Seventy-one (77.2%) patients had a kidney biopsy at the time of the LN flare, with 51 (71.8%) having a proliferative or mixed class and 20 (28.2%) having a non-proliferative class (16 patients with class V and 4 patients with classes I and II). The Median Activity Index was 6 (1, 12) and the Chronicity Index was 2 (1, 4). The median (IQR) of the baseline prednisone dose was 40 mg (30, 50) and 79 (85.9%) received antimalarial therapy. The most common immunosuppressive medication used was mycophenolate (63%), followed by azathioprine (19.5%) and cyclophosphamide (9.8%). Sixty-three (68.5%) patients were treated with an ACE inhibitor or angiotensin receptor blocker.

Renal outcomes in the cohort

Patients who developed an adverse renal outcome were younger and had a longer disease duration. The percentage of patients who had a kidney biopsy at the time of the LN flare was lower in the group of patients who had an adverse renal outcome (66 vs 90%, p=0.005). However, the activity and chronicity indexes were similar between the two groups stratified based on the development of an adverse renal outcome, as was the treatment.

Thirty-nine (42.4%) patients had at least one flare, and 23 (25.0%) had two or more flares during their follow-up. The median (IQR) time to the first renal flare was 60.5 (40, 104.5) months, and the time to the second flare was 91 (71, 122) months. Forty (43.5%) patients had at least a 30% sustained decline in eGFR, and 31 (33.7%) had a 50% sustained decline in eGFR. The median times for these outcomes were 68 (32, 118) months and 70 (33, 121) months for 30% and 50% decline in eGFR, respectively. Twenty-three (25.0%) patients progressed to ESRD, with a median time to ESRD of 74 (34, 123) months. All patients who developed ESRD had a proliferative class on renal biopsy and 52% of the patients had a previous LN flare with a disease duration of 9.5 (6.9, 15.7) years. Fifty (54.3%) patients developed an adverse renal event (≥2 renal flares and/or at least a 30% sustained decline in eGFR) during their follow-up, with a median (IQR) time to event of 78 (52.0, 121.3) months.

Elevated serum IFN-α levels were associated with poor renal outcomes

Serum IFN-α levels were significantly higher in patients who experienced an adverse renal event, with a median (IQR) of 1.27 (0.47, 3.11), compared with those who did not, who had a median (IQR) of 0.30 (0.02, 0.90) (p=0.001) (figure 1A).

Multivariable models adjusted for variables associated with poor renal outcomes, including age, race, the presence of a prior LN flare and baseline serum creatinine levels22,24 showed that patients with higher baseline levels of IFN-α had a greater risk of having two or more subsequent renal flares, with every 1 unit increase (1 pg/mL) in IFN-α increasing the risk of having ≥2 flares by 30% (HR: 1.31 (1.08–1.59), p=0.006). Also, higher levels of baseline IFN-α increased the risk of a sustained 30% and 50% decline in eGFR (HR: 1.27 (1.14–1.40), p<0.001; HR: 1.27 (1.12–1.33), p<0.001, respectively) and of progressing to ESRD (HR: 1.29 (1.14–1.47), p<0.001). A higher baseline level of IFN-α also increased the risk of developing an adverse renal event (two or more flares or at least a 30% sustained decline in eGFR) (HR: 1.29 (1.17–1.44), p<0.001) (table 2).

To determine if similar findings were present in the patients with biopsy-proven LN at baseline (n=71), we analysed this subcohort. Online supplemental table 1 shows the demographic and clinical characteristics of this subcohort. The percentage of patients with a prior LN flare was higher in the group of patients who developed an adverse renal outcome, but the difference was not significant (39% vs 21%, p=0.09). Furthermore, the activity and chronicity indexes in the kidney biopsies in patients with and without the development of renal events were similar. In this subcohort of patients, the serum levels of IFN-α were also higher in patients who developed an adverse renal event (median (IQR) of 1.35 (0.6, 4.72) vs 0.27 (0.02, 0.74), p=0.0002, in patients who did versus didn’t develop an adverse renal event, respectively, online supplemental figure 1A). Likewise, IFN-α continued to be associated with renal outcomes (adverse renal event HR: 1.26 (1.13–1.41), p<0.001), as seen in online supplemental table 2.

Other variables associated with poor renal outcomes

We next examined whether other variables, besides IFN-α, were associated with poor renal outcomes. From the traditional biomarkers, higher baseline titres of anti-double stranded DNA (dsDNA) antibodies (abs) and serum creatinine were associated with a greater risk of experiencing a decline in kidney function (30% sustained decline in eGFR HR: 1.73 (1.23–2.42), p=0.01, and 1.19 (1.04–1.37), p=0.01, for anti-dsDNA abs and serum creatinine, respectively), but neither of them was associated with a higher risk of developing renal flares. Other associated variables with poor renal outcomes were age (adverse renal event HR: 0.64 (0.47–0.86), p=0.003) and a prior LN flare (adverse renal event HR: 2.00 (1.14–3.80), p=0.01) (table 2).

The analysis performed in the subcohort of patients with biopsy-proven LN at baseline (n=71) had similar findings, except for the history of a prior LN flare, which was only associated with the development of the first LN flare (online supplemental table 2).

Establishing cut-offs

IFN-α had the highest area under the curve (AUC) for discriminating between patients who developed an adverse renal event and those who did not (0.72), followed by anti-dsDNA abs (0.69), as seen in figure 1. Based on the Youden Index, the best cut-offs were ≥0.6 pg/mL, ≥65 IU/L and ≤29 years for IFN-α, anti-dsDNA abs and age, respectively. Given the low AUC for serum creatinine (0.59), no cut-off value was established for this variable, and it was dropped from further analysis.

The ROC curves in the subcohort of patients with biopsy-proven LN at baseline (n=71) showed similar AUC results (IFN-α: 0.75 (0.63, 0.87), p=0.0003) (online supplemental figure 1B).

In the group of patients who experienced an adverse renal event, 37 (74%) had an IFN-α level ≥0.6 pg/mL, compared with 13 (31%) of the patients who didn’t develop an adverse renal event (p<0.001). Similar results were found in the subcohort of patients with biopsy-proven LN at baseline (IFN-α ≥0.6 pg/mL: 79% vs 29%, p<0.001, in the group of patients who did and did not develop an adverse renal event, respectively).

Accurately identifying patients at risk for an adverse renal event

Using the defined cut-offs, comparisons of the operating characteristics of the four variables associated with an adverse renal event showed that IFN-α was the best discriminator compared with anti-dsDNA antibody (ab) titres, age and a prior LN flare. IFN-α had the highest PPVs compared with the other variables, although the results were low to fair in the whole cohort and the subcohort of patients with a kidney biopsy at the time of the flare (74% (60, 85) in the whole cohort) and had low +LRs (2.39 (1.48, 3.87) in the whole cohort). On the other hand, the NPVs were fair to good, especially in the subcohort of biopsied patients (79% (62, 91)), and had the lowest −LRs compared with the other variables (−LR: 0.30 (0.15, 0.59)).

Since none of the variables had excellent operating characteristics, we analysed whether different combinations could more accurately identify patients at risk for an adverse renal event. The presence of a baseline IFN-α of ≥0.6 and a history of a prior LN flare had an excellent specificity (95% (84, 99)) and PPV (91% (71, 99)), with a high +LR of 8.40 (2.08, 33.88). This combination had even better results in the subcohort of patients with a kidney biopsy at the time of the LN flare, with a +LR above 10 (+LR of 11.52 (1.56, 85.26)). The combination of anti-dsDNA abs ≥65 and a prior LN flare had similar results with high specificity and PPV. However, the +LR was lower compared with the combination of IFN-α of ≥0.6 and a history of a prior LN, especially in the subcohort of patients with a kidney biopsy at the time of the LN flare (+LR of 3.45 (0.75, 15.97)). All combinations had low NPVs and high −LRs (table 3).

Discussion

In the study reported here, we assessed the capacity of serum IFN-α levels measured during an LN flare to predict poor renal outcomes using a cohort with long-term follow-up and meticulously documented clinical and laboratory parameters. Our results indicate that IFN-α measured at the time of an LN flare is a predictor for poor renal outcomes. After adjusting for other confounding variables,22,24 patients who had higher levels of IFN-α at the time of an LN flare had a higher risk of developing two or more LN flares and a clinically meaningful decline in kidney function over an 11-year period.

Type I IFNs play a pivotal role in both the pathogenesis of SLE and the resulting organ damage.12 13 Although multiple studies have demonstrated an association between IFN and the presence of renal involvement as well as SLE disease activity,14 15 17 only a few studies have addressed the impact of high levels of IFN on disease course and outcomes. Consistent with the results reported in this study, a previous investigation performed by our group showed that a higher baseline IFIG expression level in the peripheral blood predicted a more severe disease course over the subsequent 5 years, characterised by a higher number of flares, an increased adjusted mean SLE Disease Activity Index-2000, and a requirement for more aggressive therapy.16 Similar findings were reported by Northcott et al,28 who observed that patients with elevated IFN signatures exhibited greater activity across multiple organ systems and spent less time in a lupus low disease activity state. Furthermore, Mathian el at25 demonstrated a similar association between elevated serum IFN-α levels and an increased risk of relapse in patients with clinically quiescent lupus. Recently, Der E et al29 showed that the IFIG expression in renal tubular cells from patients with proliferative LN was higher in those who failed to achieve a complete renal response, suggesting that IFNs may play a role in defining renal outcomes. They further reported that the IFIG signature in peripheral blood mononuclear cells was not associated with response to therapy, suggesting a disconnect between the IFIG signature in peripheral blood and the IFIG signature in the renal tubular cells. In our study IFN-α levels in the peripheral blood were associated with renal outcomes. The contrasting results between our study and the Der et al29 study could be explained by the number of patients analysed (92 vs 8, in our cohort and Der et al, respectively).

IFN-α exhibited superior predictive ability for renal outcomes compared with proteinuria and complement levels. Although anti-dsDNA abs and serum creatinine were also associated with the decline in function, they had no association with subsequent renal flares. Other variables associated with renal outcomes in this cohort were age and a history of a prior LN flare. These results support those from prior studies that have proposed anti-dsDNA abs, serum creatinine, age and a history of a prior LN flare as potential predictors of poor renal outcomes.22,2430 31

The operating characteristics for IFN-α were superior to the other variables. Overall, the presence of an IFN-α level ≥0.6 and a prior LN flare identified patients at the highest risk for an adverse renal event, with excellent specificities, PPVs and +LRs, suggesting that these patients may benefit from more aggressive immunosuppressive therapy and close surveillance. The presence of anti-dsDNA abs ≥65 and a prior LN flare also had a high specificity and PPV but a lower +LR. Even though an IFN-α of <0.6 had the best NPV and −LR, the NPV was only fair to good, and the −LR was 0.3, meaning a small decrease (around 25%) in the probability of developing an adverse renal event.27 This finding suggests that in addition to IFN-α, other biomarkers are needed to detect all patients at risk for poor renal outcomes accurately. In view of the findings by Der et al,29 who found a disconnect between the association of the IFIG signature in renal tissue and the blood, it may be that high IFIG levels in the kidney tissue are more predictive of poor renal outcomes than those in the blood.

Even though an IFN-α level of ≥0.6 and a prior LN flare, and an anti-dsDNA ab level of ≥65 and a prior LN flare had similar specificities, PPVs, and +LRs for the development of an adverse event, multivariable analysis showed that IFN-α outperformed anti-dsDNA abs for the development of two or more LN flares. Unfortunately, our sample size was insufficient to directly contrast the operating characteristics of IFN-α and anti-dsDNA abs for each individual outcome (ie, two or more LN flares and a meaningful decline in kidney function).

Since IFN-I facilitates anti-dsDNA ab production32 33 as well as the generation of short-lived plasmablasts33 34 and ab-secreting cells,35 IFN-I and anti-dsDNA abs are linked to the same pathogenesis pathway, potentially explaining the similar results obtained for both variables.

Data from murine lupus models suggest that IFN-α can also contribute more directly to renal damage since the administration of type I IFN amplifies ab-triggered nephritis, whereas ablating IFN activity ameliorates the disease.36 Mechanistically, it has been shown that a dysregulated IFN-I response can lead to tissue injury and inflammation through direct damage to renal resident cells as well as indirect damage through recruitment and activation of inflammatory cells. IFN-α can induce renal endothelial cell dysfunction and apoptosis, leading to increased permeability and ultimately causing a breakdown in the glomerular filtration barrier function.37 It can also prevent podocyte repair by causing cell-cycle arrest and promoting podocyte autophagy and damage via reactive oxygen species production and mammalian target of rapamycin 1 (mTORC1) inhibition.38 39 IFN-I stimulation also enhances renal mesangial cell proliferation and collagen synthesis by increasing the expression of transforming growth factor40 and CXC chemokine ligand 10.41 In addition, IFN-α can cause apoptosis of tubular epithelial cells.42 43 Furthermore, IFN-I can increase the recruitment of inflammatory monocytes and neutrophils to the kidney36 43 44 and can orchestrate a wide range of pleomorphic effects on the immune system, promoting activation and differentiation of myeloid cells and self-reactive T cells and B cells, all of which are known players in LN pathogenesis.3544,46

Our study has several limitations. Even though the IFN signature has been shown to be stable within patients despite treatment,16 17 it would be important to determine if the same is true for IFN-α. IFN-α may be more sensitive to change and, therefore, fluctuate longitudinally, potentially modulating longer-term outcomes. We cannot address this important question as we only measured IFN-α at baseline. Furthermore, we only measured IFN-α. Other type I IFNs, as well as type II and type III IFNs, have been shown to play a role in LN pathogenesis47 48 and may add to the predictive ability of IFN-α. We also did not include social determinants of health in our multivariable analysis, which have been shown to impact outcomes in LN,49 limiting the interpretation of our results. Additionally, our sample size was limited, preventing us from calculating the operating characteristics for each renal outcome independently. Finally, our cohort included, by chance, a high proportion of patients who developed adverse renal outcomes (25% of the patients developed ESRD). Thus, the results could be biased and need to be validated in an independent cohort.

The current study has multiple strengths. Individuals included in the study came from a multiethnic cohort with diverse clinical presentations (77.2% had a kidney biopsy and 57% were experiencing their first LN flare), allowing us to determine the utility of IFN-α in different clinical settings. Patients were followed prospectively and monitored with regular visits (every 3–6 months), ensuring a thorough assessment of renal outcomes. Furthermore, patients were followed long-term, which allowed us to determine late renal events associated with LN, such as renal flares and decline in kidney function, and investigate the association of IFN- α and other variables with renal outcomes. Furthermore, the findings are based on real-world treatments at the time of these observations.

In conclusion, the results from this study suggest that an elevated baseline level of IFN-α is associated with poor renal outcomes, including the development of two or more renal flares and a significant decline in kidney function, and could serve as a biomarker for upfront identification of patients at high risk of poor renal outcomes.
