Strategic cancer therapy planning: optimizing treatment and quality of life with Markov decision processes
Authors: Seema Singh, Chandrahas Sahu, Pushpendra Singh, Alka Mishra, Santosh Kumar Mishra, Pawan Kumar Patnaik
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn managing the progression of diseases, particularly cancer, Markov decision processes (MDP) and dynamic therapy regimes are gaining prominence. Despite this, cancer treatments often negatively impact patients’ quality of life, leading many to abandon effective, accessible, and affordable therapies.Materials and methodsThis paper introduces a novel MDP-based mathematical framework for optimizing multi-therapy treatment schedules in malignancy therapy. Through practical illustrations, we demonstrate the utility and applicability of the proposed framework. Our approach integrates both patient utility and the physician’s net benefit function, accounting for treatment options and survival probabilities across diverse clinical profiles. The system state in our MDP model is defined by tumor progression and normal tissue side effects, while the response field encompasses treatment outcomes categorized into recurrence, tumor regression, and healthy tissue safety. At each decision stage, the physician assesses the patient’s condition and selects the optimal treatment strategy to maximize the final reward, determined by the patient’s health at the end state.Results/ConclusionsThis framework offers a holistic approach to improving overall treatment outcomes while recognizing the importance of preserving patients’ quality of life.

Introduction

Cancer remains one of the most lethal diseases, and its threat is amplified by the rapidly aging population, making it a critical issue now and in the future [1, 2]. However, ongoing advancements in technology and medical science are helping to lower cancer-related mortality rates and mitigate the adverse effects of treatments. This study aims to contribute to these efforts by optimizing tumor growth reduction while minimizing side effects, ultimately enhancing the survival chances and quality of life for future cancer patients [3].

For the treatment of the disease there are numerous therapies are available but as per the oncologist, for the complete removal of the disease, only one therapy is not sufficient; therefore, in the course of controlling their cancer, most patients receive therapy from two or more means of therapy, frequently in sequential order [4, 5]. Building multiple therapy judgements suited for every particular patient is exceedingly difficult, and it is not viable to make optimal selections depending merely on specific physicians’ expertise and practical knowledge [6].

Immunotherapy, hormone treatment, surgery, chemotherapy, radiation, and targeted therapy are some of the procedures utilized to treat cancer [7]. Due to tumor heterogeneity and medicine opposition, single-therapy malignancy therapy frequently results in inadequate medical outcomes, whereas multiple therapies utilized in combination therapy can increase anti-malignancy movement by minimizing medications for each therapy, reducing adverse effects [8, 9]. In the course of treating cancer, most of the patients receive treatment from two or even more therapies, which are generally administered in sequential order [10].

Decisions for the use of particular sequences of therapies are currently made primarily based on individual therapists’ abilities, with the optimality of the ensuing treatment regimens unknown [11]. Given the wide range of therapies accessible through medical technology, as well as the various probable consequences connected with each course of therapy, relying on perception or a systematic selection of a suitable multi-therapy treatment plan can be expensive and time consuming [12].

In this work, we use a Markov decision process (MDP) strategy to present a unique mathematical framework for optimizing multi-therapy treatment plans scheduling for malignancy therapy, and we illustrate the practicality and usefulness of the developed framework with illustrations. A MDP with a definite projection period is a computational problem for enhancing a set of events in a nonlinear model, where the system state is known at the start of each judgement stage and the objective is to obtain maximum benefit at the completion of the projection period. By considering the task as a sequential decision-making problem and formulating it as a MDP, Bao et al. [13] propose a DRL-based technique for intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT) bean angle optimization.

MDPs have been applied to various fields, such as finance [14, 15], gaming [16] and, more recently, medicine [17, 18]. They have been employed to determine optimal therapies for epidemic management [19], drug infusion [20], kidney transplantation [21], spherocytosis [22], ischemic heart disease [23], breast cancer [24], as well as to optimize dosing in sequential radiation therapy phases [25] and make decisions regarding organ transplantation acceptance [26].

By taking into account the variances in risk assessments of people, Ayvaci et al. [27] created a stochastic modeling approach employing MDPs to optimize risk-sensitive diagnosis choices following a mammography test. Tunç et al. [28] used an enormous-scale MDP framework that included multiple finite stages to optimize breast cancer diagnosis choices in order to minimize excessive diagnosis expenses throughout a patient’s lifespan. Imani et al. [24] developed an MDP framework to maximize expenses and quality of life for breast tumor therapies that included preventative treatments such as chemotherapy, radiation therapy, and combinations of these treatments. The development of rules for an extensive array of cancer detection issues, including prostate [29, 30], colorectal [31], liver [32], cervical [33], and lung cancer[34], has been done using MDPs.

The system state in our MDP model is defined as growth of the malignant cells and normal tissue side effects. The response field is made up of distinct therapeutic approaches that are divided into three categories: recurrence, tumor regression, and safety to healthy tissue. The physician observes the patient’s condition during every judgment stage and selects the best treatment method to optimize the estimated final reward that is determined by the patient’s end state.

MDP model formulation for treatment planning

For T treatment cycles and phases, the MDP model is created as shown in Fig.1. The key steps and decision epochs are depicted schematically in Figure 2. At the start of stage t, and for the time interval between t and t + 1, the decision t is made. The system is either transited from one stage to the next or remains in the present state at each stage. In step t, the decision-maker chooses an action based on the system’s condition, incurring a cost mt(c,a). The treatment period is completed at the end of stage T. Because the MDP model is designed for a single treatment period, each of which is made up of numerous treatment sessions, the phases of the system are made up of treatment cycles.

The most significant aims in radiation therapy malignancy volume control optimization problems are to regulate the patients’ lifespan, toxicities, and radiotherapy costs [36, 37]. Because the measures taken in the MDP model are quite comparable to the radiation therapy combinations suggested by the optimization model, they incur the same satisfactory cost of care regardless of which phase of the MDP framework they have chosen, and there are no substantial cost variations between these therapeutic interventions. The rationale for this is that the suggested combinations are less expensive than many of the model’s possible solutions as well as several practical tests.

The reaction should be integrated into the model in some way in order to take into consideration the patient’s progression with the therapy regime utilized during the treatment phase. For this reason, patients’ reactions are regarded as the system’s states, while therapeutic approaches are the system’s actions because the action is chosen based on the observed condition. This implies that the therapy regimens are chosen depending on the patients’ responses. The system’s states, on the other hand, are defined as distinct toxicity levels because the patient’s response manifests as toxicities.

Another important element of the MDP model is the transition probability function. This concept determines the likelihood that toxicity may shift from one stage to another during the medication interval when an appropriate therapeutic regimen is used. These probabilities could be computed based on the levels of toxicity seen in patients during each cycle. They are taken into account as inputs in the form of a matrix known as the “transition probability matrix”.

The cost parameter mt(c,a) is used to examine the effectiveness of a decision maker’s performance at a given instant. The ratio of “toxic effects generated in patients” to “patient’s life expectancy” is used to calculate the cost of utilizing a drug regimen for a patient with toxicity level s.

Because the costs of various combinations are so similar, lowering the cost of care is no longer the objective of the problem. As a result, the subject of successive strategic planning is the selection of therapeutic interventions based on the patient’s present toxicity level in order to maximize the patient’s lifespan while also managing his or her toxicity level.

Therapies considered

Here, the treatments have been divided into three categories: if the patient is in the final stage, give the highest doses of radiation; if the patient is in the second stage, give lower doses of radiation or chemotherapy depending on the rate of tumor growth and the level of toxicity; and if the patient is in the first stage, use immunotherapy instead, which can also be given when the level of toxicity is highest.

We categorize therapeutic strategies into three groups while keeping in mind that some are more advantageous but can only be used occasionally. Whole-brain irradiation, if the metastases phase occurs, can only be done once in a patient’s lifetime due to the accompanying normal-tissue toxicity, but gamma knife and, on the selected region of the brain, external beam radiation therapy (EBRT) can be administered numerous times as long as the adverse effects on the normal cells are bearable [38].

A2 and A3 can each represent a collection of therapies in general, but to ease the nomenclature, we will start with the example when each class has only one aspect. Furthermore, we limit the use of class 1 therapy to a single time throughout treatment, although this is not a necessity of our model. It should also be highlighted that our model has no restrictions on the order in which therapies are delivered, giving complete flexibility in deciding the correct therapy for a specific clinical situation.

State transition probabilities

When a malignancy in state ct is treated with therapeutic approach at∈A in period t, the malignancy status in period t + 1 is considered to be ct+1 with probability St(ct+1|ct,at).

This expression is consistent with our initial theory that malignancy development and side effects on normal cells are solely dependent on the therapy class but not on one another.

Costs functions

An additional parameter to the MDP framework is the cost matrix. It is calculated as the ratio of toxicity to survival time for a patient receiving the dose combination a at toxicity level s. The solution values of the optimization model are used to determine these parameters. In the course of their therapy session, i.e., at t = t+1, the patient is in state c is quantified by the real-valued terminal cost function mt+1(c).

The patient may also get a transitional cost, mt(ct,at,ct+1), after each course of therapy that is connected to the action taken at time t, like the cost of utilizing therapy at phase c or the projected result at the subsequent phase, mt+1. Although our approach can be used with any cost function, patient benefit linked to improved conditions has to be at least as effective as patient benefit associated with bad ones. Since states are arranged in such a way that smaller states indicate better patient conditions, the valuable, impairment-improved, and nutritious equivalent lives are some of the most often used utility measures in treatment.

Data pre-processing

The dataset used in this study is derived from tumor registries included in the SEER program, which represents approximately 14% to 25% of the U.S. population. The prostate cancer dataset includes over 1.6 million records collected between 1973 and 2016 from various U.S. regions. These comprehensive records are critical for analyzing the long-term burden and progression of prostate cancer.

Each entry in the SEER database includes standardized variables such as year and month of diagnosis, tumor grade, clinical stage, and treatment modality. Using the unique patient ID, we construct a tensor-based data structure, where each layer stores all records associated with a specific patient. This allows for temporal tracking of disease progression.

MDP modelling of prostate cancer

The proposed MDP framework is designed to support optimized decision-making in prostate cancer treatment by modeling disease progression and therapy outcomes based on patient-specific factors such as age, health status, and prior treatments. This finite-horizon MDP includes key components: decision epochs, state space, action space, decision rules, state transitions, and reward functions. Decision epochs span patient ages from 25 to 70, reflecting the typical clinical intervention window for prostate cancer. The state space is defined by a tuple s = (t,c,i), where t represents the patient’s age, c denotes the clinical stage of cancer (e.g., localized, regional, or metastatic), and i captures the treatment history—comprising interventions such as prostatectomy, radiation therapy, hormone therapy, and chemotherapy. These states are modeled as a stochastic process X = {Xt:t∈[25,70]}, where the next state depends solely on the current state and action, thereby satisfying the Markov property.

The action space A consists of available treatment options at each decision epoch, including active surveillance, surgery, radiation therapy, hormone therapy, chemotherapy, or their combinations. The probability of selecting a particular action a∈A given a state s at time t is represented by the decision rule Q = (st,a). This rule governs clinical choices based on the patient’s current condition and treatment trajectory. Transitions between states are governed by the probability Pt = (s,s’), which denotes the likelihood of moving from state s at age t to state s’ at age t+1, conditioned on the action taken. These components work together to determine optimal, personalized treatment strategies by maximizing expected rewards, such as quality-adjusted life years (QALYs), across the patient’s clinical timeline.

Discussion

This study presents a novel approach for sequential treatment decision-making in clinical trials, employing a finite-horizon MDP model to address challenges in planning cancer therapy trials. Our approach successfully identifies the optimal sequence of radiotherapy regimens for a defined treatment period by minimizing a cost function. By predicting optimal treatment pathways, the model offers significant potential for personalized cancer therapy.

Through numerical simulations, we demonstrated the practical applicability of the model, using simplified patient conditions and cost systems that reflect therapeutic relevance. These simulations highlight the model’s potential to enhance treatment outcomes, allowing healthcare providers to visualize predicted treatment paths for patients. The dynamic use of state transition probabilities, which evolve based on patient responses, empowers clinicians to make more informed and adaptive decisions.

As treatment planning becomes more complex with the introduction of additional patient-specific factors, such as age, side effects, and malignancy type, the model can be further customized using unique objective functions to accommodate individual patient preferences. However, this increases computational demands as the model incorporates more diverse and authentic clinical variables.

The selection of a specific therapy at each decision epoch within the MDP framework is governed by the patient’s current clinical state, particularly tumor progression and treatment-induced toxicity levels. Immunotherapy is generally prioritized when the patient is in the early stages of disease progression or when toxicity levels are critically high. This preference stems from the fact that immunotherapy is associated with fewer and less severe adverse effects compared to more aggressive modalities such as chemotherapy or high-dose radiation therapy. As a result, it is considered a safer option for patients with compromised health status due to existing toxicity burdens.

Conversely, in scenarios where the disease has advanced significantly, and tumor growth is aggressive, the model favors more intensive treatment options. In such cases, the potential benefits of aggressive tumor suppression outweigh the risks associated with increased toxicity. This strategic selection mechanism ensures that treatment decisions are both patient-specific and adaptive, aiming to balance therapeutic efficacy with safety across the entire treatment horizon.

Despite its strengths, the model presents certain limitations that warrant consideration. Notably, the computational complexity increases with the incorporation of additional clinical variables and treatment options, which may affect scalability and real-time decision-making capabilities. Furthermore, the current formulation assumes estimated transition probabilities, which may introduce uncertainty and may not fully capture inter-patient variability in disease progression or treatment response.

Conclusions

The proposed finite-horizon MDP model establishes a dynamic and adaptive framework for optimizing radiotherapy dosage schedules based on real-time patient response data. By recommending the most suitable dosage combinations at each treatment cycle, the model demonstrates its capacity to identify effective therapeutic strategies early in the treatment planning process, potentially before clinical validation. This highlights the model’s applicability in enhancing personalized cancer therapy through informed, data-driven decisions that adapt to the evolving clinical state of the patient. Looking ahead, there is significant potential to extend the model’s utility. Future adaptations could incorporate emerging therapies such as CAR-T cell therapy, personalized cancer vaccines, or immunotherapy strategies, enabling the framework to remain relevant in the context of rapidly evolving oncological treatments. Additionally, integrating patient-specific genomic, molecular, or proteomic data into the MDP structure could substantially improve the precision and personalization of treatment recommendations.
