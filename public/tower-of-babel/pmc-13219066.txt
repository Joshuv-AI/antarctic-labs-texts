Photosensitive and extracellular matrix-mimicking hydrogels promote the tendon-bone healing in rotator cuff injury
Authors: Yuxuan Liu, Huanv Mao, Ziyang Tian, Xiaoting Wang, Chunyan Bao, Bingchen An, Chunxi Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe healing of the tendon-bone interface remains a significant challenge due to the lack of suitable bioactive materials for promoting interface regeneration. To address this, we developed a photosensitive hydrogel system designed to mimic the extracellular matrix. This system is based on light-induced imine crosslinking between type I collagen (Col I) and o-nitrobenzyl alcohol (NB)-modified hyaluronic acid (HANB). Experimental results demonstrate that the hydrogel (Col I-HANB) exhibits excellent controllability, low swelling rate, biodegradability, and tissue-adhesive properties. Using a fluorescent dye (8-hydroxypyrene-1,3,6-trisulfonic acid trisodium salt, HPTS) as a model molecule, we confirmed that the hydrogel enables localized and sustained release, high-lighting its potential as a drug-delivery platform for tissue engineering. To further enhance tendon-bone interface repair, the hydrogel was functionalized with two key components: (I) The E7 peptide, known for its stem cell-homing properties, was covalently immobilized into the hydrogel network via conjugation to bovine serum albumin (BSA) and followed by imine-ligation between UV-triggered aldehyde generation from HANB and BSA’s amino groups; and (II) Kartogenin (KGN)-loaded nanoparticles (KGN-NPs) were incorporated to potentially promote chondrogenic differentiation, with release assays confirming sustained KGN delivery over 21 days. The composite hydrogel demonstrated excellent biocompatibility in cytotoxicity and live/dead assays. Importantly, in a rabbit rotator cuff tear model, it effectively promoted fibrocartilage regeneration and significantly improved tendon-bone healing, creating a favorable microenvironment for repair. Introduction

The tendon-bone interface, also known as the enthesis, is a transitional tissue between tendon and bone [1]. The tendon-bone interface consists of four clearly defined yet continuous tissue regions tendon, non-mineralized fibrocartilage, mineralized fibrocartilage, and bone [2, 3]. The tendon is composed of fibroblasts situated between aligned collagen fibers, with a matrix rich in type I collagen (Col I), along with a small amount of type III collagen and proteoglycans. The non-mineralized fibrocartilage region contains fibrocartilage cells, and its matrix includes type I, II, and III collagen, with fibers oriented perpendicular to the calcified interface region. The mineralized fibrocartilage region is composed of fibrocartilage cells, with a matrix that contains type I, II, and X collagen. Bone is constituted of osteoblasts, osteoclasts, and osteocytes, with a matrix rich in Col I. The multi-tissue structure of the tendon-bone interface facilitates load transfer between tendon and bone, minimizing stress concentrations, and supports the heterogeneous cellular interactions essential for interface function and homeostasis [2–4]. The complex structure of the enthesis enables the functional interplay between muscle and bone, yet it poses significant challenges for tissue repair and regeneration. From a clinical perspective, traditional conservative treatments or surgical repairs are insufficient in effectively reconstructing the complex transitional structure between tendon and bone, thereby hindering tendon healing and regeneration. Consequently, the repair and regeneration of the tendon/ligament-bone interface have become a focal topic within the field of orthopedic research [5–8].

Research on tendon-bone healing predominantly centers on biomaterials, growth factors, and mesenchymal stem cells [9–11]. Bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) serve as the primary progenitor cells in tendon-bone healing. Moreover, the exosomes derived from BMSCs and adipose-derived stem cells can enhance the polarization of M2 macrophages, thereby facilitating the formation of fibrocartilage and promoting tendon-bone healing [10, 11]. However, considering the safety concerns and high costs associated with the clinical application of growth factors and exogenous stem cells in tendon-bone healing, increasing research efforts are directed towards the development of tissue-engineered scaffold materials to recruit autologous stem cells to enhance tendon-bone healing. Among the various materials employed for this purpose, hydrogels have attracted significant attention due to their potential as ideal matrices or scaffold materials for tissue repair [12, 13]. These hydrogels not only offer structural support to cells, thereby facilitating cell adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation, but also function as delivery vehicles for bioactive agents, enabling the controlled release of such factors to enhance the regeneration of tendon-bone interfaces [14, 15].

In recent years, the rapid advancement of nanotechnology has enabled hydrogels to be integrated with nanoparticles (NPs), such as liposomes, polymeric micelles, mesoporous silica, gold nanorods, and nano-hydroxyapatite, thereby generating more complex and functionally enhanced hydrogel materials capable of achieving diverse targeted tissue regeneration effects [16]. These nanoparticles originate from a variety of sources and can be custom-designed in terms of size, morphology, and composition to meet specific application requirements. The incorporation of different NPs into hydrogel systems can elicit distinct biological responses across various tissue types. Although nanoparticle toxicity remains a frequently raised concern, surface modification using highly biocompatible polymers-such as hyaluronic acid (HA), chitosan, or polyethylene glycol-or biomimetic membranes-including cell membranes, exosomal membranes, and vesicular membranes-can effectively reduce potential cytotoxicity. By integrating NPs into hydrogel matrices, the functional properties of these materials are significantly improved, offering extensive therapeutic potential for a wide range of diseases, particularly in the repair of tendon-bone interface injuries. Synthetic polymer micelles can enhance the solubility of poorly soluble drugs and improve the stability of formulations, making them widely utilized in nanodrug delivery systems. Incorporating nanomicelles into hydrogel scaffolds can alter the drug release rate and enhance the mechanical properties of the scaffolds [17].

Nanoliposomes are closed spherical carriers formed by the self-assembly of monolayer or multilayer phospholipid bilayers dissolved in water. Liposomes possess hydrophilic heads and hydrophobic tails, endowing them with amphiphilic properties. Due to their excellent biocompatibility, straightforward preparation processes, and amphiphilicity, nanoliposomes are widely employed in the delivery of drugs, peptides, and mRNA. Compared to micrometer-sized liposomes, nanoliposomes exhibit a higher specific surface area and possess the potential to enhance the solubility of molecular compounds [18, 19].

In addition to organic polymer materials, inorganic materials have emerged as candidate materials for the preparation of nanocarriers due to their biocompatibility and low cost. For instance, hydroxyapatite, a primary inorganic component of human bones and teeth, exhibits excellent bone conduction and superior bone bonding behavior, along with outstanding biocompatibility, making it a promising candidate for bone tissue repair. Compared to traditional hydroxyapatite, nano-hydroxyapatite possesses a larger specific surface area and higher bioactivity, which facilitates cell adhesion and growth [20]. Fe3O4 nanoparticles represent another common inorganic metal material that has also been utilized in the field of regenerative medicine [21].

Extracellular matrix (ECM) refers to the components outside of cells within tissues, providing a microenvironment essential for cellular survival and a three-dimensional scaffold for growth [22]. The constituents, biochemical properties, and biophysical characteristics of the ECM exist in a dynamic form, regulated by the cells and their neighboring counterparts [23, 24]. The objective of tissue engineering is to mimic the natural, dynamic in vivo environment while minimizing unnecessary host responses to regenerate defective tissues. To achieve this goal, a primary focus is on the construction of scaffold materials that can fulfill the supportive functions of tissue regeneration and adequately simulate the complex and dynamic nature of the ECM.

Hydrogel biomaterials are frequently employed to simulate the ECM due to their exceptional biocompatibility, appropriate mechanical strength, biodegradability, and ease of chemical modification, thereby rendering them ideal biomaterials for meeting the demands of tissue engineering. Among these, natural polymers such as sodium alginate, gelatin, collagen, and HA are extensively utilized as hydrogel materials owing to their advantages of good biocompatibility, biodegradability, and ease of modification [25–32]. Collagen serves as a principal structural protein within the ECM, providing tensile stiffness and strength to tissues. HA, a glycosaminoglycan, is distributed throughout the ECM of various tissue types and plays a significant role in numerous biological processes and tissue functions [33, 34]. Hydrogels composed of collagen and HA have been utilized as supportive scaffolds or adjustable delivery systems for tissue repair. However, the inherent weakness of natural hydrogel materials often results in mechanical properties that do not meet the requirements for tissue repair, necessitating the introduction of additional covalent crosslinking to enhance gel strength. Light offers advantages such as non-physical contact and spatiotemporal control. Consequently, employing photochemical reactions to provide additional covalent crosslinking for hydrogels represents an effective strategy for strengthening natural hydrogel materials. Moreover, by simply adjusting light intensity, exposure duration, and illumination area, one can achieve spatiotemporal control over photocrosslinked hydrogels. The process of preparing hydrogels via photocrosslinking is straightforward and exhibits good controllability, enabling the materials to seamlessly fill defect sites and form in situ under illumination. Therefore, such hydrogels are highly attractive for applications in tissue engineering [35]. Additionally, the network structure formed by covalent bonds, hydrogen bonds, and physical entanglements within the hydrogels can provide a physical barrier for drug release. However, standalone hydrogels often lead to burst release of encapsulated drugs due to a lack of interaction with the drugs. The research on liposomes as drug carriers is well-established; incorporating liposomes into hydrogels to create composite carriers is expected to effectively mitigate drug burst release [36].

In our previous research, we proposed a novel light-induced imine-crosslinked hydrogel, which utilizes the photogenerated aldehyde groups from o-nitrobenzyl alcohol (NB) modified HA (HANB) to react with amino-bearing biopolymers, resulting in a hydrogel with excellent biocompatibility and tissue adhesion properties. This study aims to construct a light-crosslinked hydrogel material based on HANB and Col I through this “photo-coupling reaction.” Furthermore, it seeks to combine this hydrogel with drug-loaded nanocarriers to achieve a controlled drug release function. The E7 peptide is a small-molecule polypeptide composed of 7 amino acids (EPLQLKM), which has specific affinity for BMSCs and can promote the adhesion, proliferation, and migration of BMSCs both in vitro and in vivo [37]. Immobilizing the E7 peptide in the hydrogel network can endow the hydrogel with the ability to recruit mesenchymal stem cells. Kartogenin (KGN) is a synthetic heterocyclic small molecule that effectively induces the differentiation of mesenchymal stem cells into chondrocytes [38]. Additionally, KGN exhibits good stability and low immunogenicity. Consequently, in recent years, KGN has frequently been employed to explore its applications in various regenerative fields, including cartilage repair, tendon-bone healing, and wound healing. However, due to its low water solubility and hydrophobic characteristics, KGN cannot be effectively incorporated into highly hydrophilic hydrogel systems [39]. Furthermore, the controlled release of biological signals is crucial for cellular differentiation [40]. Therefore, it is essential to encapsulate KGN within other carriers to achieve effective and sustained release of KGN within the tissue microenvironment. NPs loaded with the cartilage repair-promoting drug KGN will be incorporated into the hydrogel, and E7 peptide, which has the ability to recruit stem cells, will be grafted onto bovine serum albumin (BSA) [41, 42]. The photogenerated aldehyde groups of NB will react with the amino groups of the protein to immobilize the E7 peptide within the hydrogel, thereby endowing the light-crosslinked hydrogel with new biological functions for the application in promoting rotator cuff injury repair.

Synthesis and characterization of HANB molecule

Synthesis of HANB molecule

The molecule of NB with amino functional groups was synthesized following the synthetic route illustrated in Fig. 1A.

The synthesis steps of HANB are illustrated in Fig. 1B: Add sodium hyaluronate (2 g, 890 kDa) to 100 mL of 2-(N-morpholino) ethanesulfonic acid buffer (MES buffer, 10 mM, pH = 5.5) and stir mechanically until completely dissolved. Dissolve the NB small molecule(0.26 g, 0.8 mmol) in a minimal amount of MES (5 mL) and then add it dropwise to above sodium hyaluronate solution. Subsequently, introduce 4-(4,6-dimethoxy-2-triazinyl)-4-methylmorpholinium chloride (DMTMM, 4.5 g, 15 mmol) in three aliquots, with a 30-min interval between each addition. The obtained mixed solution was stirred overnight at room temperature. After the reaction was complete, NaCl (3 g, 51 mmol) was added while maintaining stirring. Once the NaCl was completely dissolved, the reaction mixture was transferred to a dialysis bag with a molecular weight cutoff of 7000 Da, and dialyzed against deionized water for three days to remove unreacted small molecules. Finally, the dialyzed solution was freeze-dried to yield a yellow fibrous product, referred to as HANB (1.6 g). The product was characterized by 1H Nuclear Magnetic Resonance (NMR) (Advance 400 MHz NMR spectrometer, Bruker), and the grafting ratio of NB on HA was calculated based on the integral area ratio.

Photolysis test of HANB molecule

Prepare a 2 mg/mL solution of HANB in Dulbecco’s Phosphate Buffered Saline (DPBS) and introduce it into a quartz cuvette. Illuminate with a 365 nm LED light source (10 mW/cm²). Monitor the photolysis process at specific time intervals using ultraviolet-visible (UV-Vis) absorption spectroscopy.

Synthesis and characterization of Col I-HANB hydrogel

Synthesis of Col I-HANB hydrogel

Weigh 20 mg of HANB into a centrifuge tube, add 1 mL of 1× DPBS (pH = 7.4), and thoroughly vortex the mixture before placing it on a shaking incubator at 37 °C until fully dissolved, resulting in a HANB solution with a solid content of 2% (w/v). Pre-cool a clean centrifuge tube on ice, then transfer 1 mL of Col I (5 mg/mL) dissolved in 6 mM acetic acid into the tube, followed by the addition of 65 μL of deionized water. Subsequently, introduce 60 μL of 0.1 M NaOH and mix immediately. Next, add 125 μL of 10× DPBS (1 M, pH = 6.8) and mix immediately to obtain a neutral Col I solution with a solid content of 0.4% (w/v). Mix the aforementioned HANB and Col I solutions in a 1:1 (v/v) ratio to yield the Col I-HANB hydrogel precursor solution. Given that Col I can rapidly gel at room temperature and neutral conditions, it is imperative to maintain low temperatures throughout the procedure. Transfer 200 μL of the hydrogel precursor solution into a polytetrafluoroethylene mold, allow it to form a physical gel at room temperature, and then irradiate in situ with a 365 nm LED light source for 300 s (10 mW/cm²) to produce a further covalently cross-linked hydrogel.

Analysis of rheological properties of hydrogels

The rheological properties of hydrogels were measured by HAAKE MARS Ⅲ rotational rheometer. The pre-configured hydrogel precursor solution was transferred to the rheometer parallel plate (P20Til, d = 20 mm), and the modulus change rule of the hydrogel was tested under the change of temperature and light external conditions, respectively. Among them, the temperature was varied from 4 to 37 °C, and the light source used was a 365 nm UV LED light curing machine (10 mW/cm2).

Characterization of compression properties of hydrogels

A high-precision GT-TCS-2000 double-column tensile testing machine with a range of 100 N was used to test the mechanical properties of hydrogels. Hydrogel samples (10 mm in diameter and 3 mm in height) were made in a polytetrafluoroethylene mould with light exposure times of 3 min, 5 min and 8 min, respectively, and the specimens were subjected to compression and cyclic compression tests at a speed of 1 mm/min and an ultimate deformation point of 2.4 mm. After the tests were completed, the compression modulus of the hydrogels was calculated based on the stress-strain curves in the elastic deformation region (EComp). At least three parallel samples were set up for each group.

Evaluation of the tissue adhesive strength of the Col I-HANB hydrogel

Fresh porcine muscle tissue from leg (approx. 3 × 6 cm, thickness 5 mm) was chosen as the substrate to visualize the tissue adhesion ability of the hydrogel, which was rinsed with DPBS to remove surface impurities and blotted dry with filter paper. 200 µL of Col I-HANB hydrogel precursor solution was mixed with a small amount of crystal violet dye(0.1 mg) and dropped on the pork tissue, and the thickness of the gel was about 2 mm. 365 nm LED light (10 mW/cm2) was then applied to the hydrogel precursor solution in situ on the pork for 5 min to cause gelation. Afterwards, an external force was applied to the hydrogel-adhered pork tissues, and the hydrogel adhesion state was observed when the pork was repeatedly twisted, and photographs were taken to record the results. Finally, the hydrogel-adhered pork tissues were immersed in DPBS buffer solution (pH = 7.4), and the hydrogel adhesion on the surface of pork tissues was observed again after 2 d. Photographs were taken to document the adhesion.

Quantitative measurement of hydrogel tissue adhesion

Fresh porcine enteric coats were selected as the substrate, which were cut to a fixed size (2.5 cm × 2.5 cm) and glued onto a glass sheet (6.5 cm × 2.5 cm). 150 µL of hydrogel precursor solution was taken on the surface of the enteric-coated tissue and spread evenly, and then the glass slice was closed so that the upper and lower pieces of enteric coats were positioned opposite to each other. Following a period of storage at room temperature, the hydrogel precursor solution was irradiated with a 365 nm LED light source (10 mW/cm2) for 5 min, so that the hydrogel precursor solution formed a gel in situ between the two pieces of enteric coats. Subsequently, the ends of the glass sheets were fixed to a tensile machine fixture and set to stretch at a speed of 1 mm/min until the two sheets were disconnected. In order to demonstrate that the hydrogels formed covalent connections with the tissue surface by imine bonds, we tested the adhesion between the amino-partially blocked enteric coats and the photogenerated aldehyde-based hydrogels using the quantitative measurements described above. A 3% formaldehyde solution was prepared and the enteric coats were immersed in it so that the amino groups on their surfaces were partially blocked. After 2 h of immersion, the enteric coats were taken out, cut into the appropriate sizes, and the above test was repeated, and the results were compared with the adhesion between enteric coats untreated with formaldehyde and the hydrogels.

Synthesis of liposome-composite hydrogels

Preparation of liposomes

A chloroform solution containing 400 μL of 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DOPC, 25 mg/mL) and 100 μL of 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine (DOPE, 25 mg/mL) was added to a glass centrifuge tube. Subsequently, the organic solvent was slowly evaporated by a gentle stream of argon gas, resulting in the formation of a uniform phospholipid film on the inner wall of the glass tube. The tube was then placed in a vacuum drying oven for 6 h to completely remove any residual chloroform. Following this, 1 mL of a DPBS buffer solution (10 mM, pH = 7.4) containing sucrose (0.2 M) and 8-hydroxypyrene-1,3,6-trisulfonic acid trisodium salt (HPTS, 2 mM, as a model drug) was added, and the mixture was hydrated at 37 °C for 12 h. The resulting liposome suspension was subjected to ultrafiltration centrifugation, and after centrifugation, fresh DPBS buffer was added, repeating this process five times to eliminate any unencapsulated HPTS small molecule dye. Finally, the liposomes encapsulating HPTS were collected and resuspended in DPBS buffer to a final volume of 1 mL. The prepared liposome suspension was stored at 4 °C for future use. The fluorescence intensity of the ultrafiltrate was measured at an emission wavelength of 510 nm, and the encapsulation efficiency (EE) and drug loading (DL) of the liposomes were calculated based on the standard curve of HPTS in DPBS. Preparation of liposome-composite hydrogels

A total of 40 mg of HANB was dissolved in 1 mL of DPBS (10 mM, pH 7.4). Subsequently, 1 mL of the aforementioned liposome suspension was added and thoroughly mixed. Then, 2 mL of neutral Col-I solution, prepared according to the procedure described in section 2.2.4 with a solid content of 0.4% (w/v), was incorporated to obtain the precursor hydrogel solution. The precursor solution was transferred into a circular mold and allowed to undergo physical gelation at room temperature. In situ photopolymerization was then performed under irradiation with a 365 nm LED light source for 300 s (10 mW/cm²), resulting in the formation of the hydrogel, designated as HG1.

Scanning electron microscopy testing of liposome-composite hydrogel

The microstructure of the hydrogel was observed using a scanning electron microscope (SEM) (S-3400N, HITACHI). The hydrogel samples, prepared under 365 nm light exposure, were placed in a –20 °C freezer for freezing. Once frozen, the samples were sectioned and subsequently dried in a freeze dryer for over 48 h. After freeze-drying, the samples were adhered to a copper stage with the observation surface facing upwards using conductive adhesive, and the surface was coated with gold prior to SEM observation.

Laser confocal microscopy imaging of liposome-composite hydrogel

The precursor solution of the hydrogel was placed in a confocal dish at room temperature until it formed a physical gel, followed by in situ illumination with a 365 nm LED light source for 5 min (10 mW/cm²) to obtain the liposome-composite hydrogel. The morphology and distribution of liposomes within the hydrogel were observed using laser confocal microscopy.

Mechanical force-responsive drug release testing

Cylindrical hydrogel samples (diameter 10 mm, height 6 mm) were produced using molds. Subsequently, the hydrogel samples were placed on the compression platform of a dual-column tensile testing machine and subjected to varying degrees of compression treatment: cycling compression for 20 times at a 20% strain rate, cycling compression for 40 times at a 20% strain rate, and cycling compression for 20 times at a 40% strain rate. The compression speed was set at 10 mm/min. Control samples underwent no compression treatment. Each group consisted of at least three parallel samples.

The calculation of cumulative release of encapsulated HPTS: Uncompressed and compressed hydrogel samples (n = 3 per group) were placed in a 24-well plate, each well containing 2 mL DPBS (pH 7.4). Plates were maintained at 37 °C in a shaking incubator (100 rpm). At each interval, 100 μL of release medium was aspirated and transferred to a 96-well plate. Fluorescence intensity was measured (λex = 405 nm, λem = 510 nm) using a microplate reader. Immediately after sampling, 100 μL fresh DPBS was added to maintain constant volume (2 mL). The release quantity was determined by the standard concentration-intensity curve. To ascertain the mechanical force response and release behavior resulting from the covalent fixation of liposomes to the hydrogel, Liposomes devoid of amino groups were prepared using the amino-free phospholipid DOPC as the raw material, following the procedures outlined in Section 2.4.1. These liposomes were physically blended into the hydrogel, which was designated as HG2. The aforementioned compression treatment and HPTS release testing were conducted on HG2.

Synthesis and characterization of nanocarrier composite photosensitive Col I-HANB hydrogel

Synthesis of BSA-E7

Dissolve BSA(50 mg, 0.75 μmol) in 4 mL of DPBS. Dissolve sulfo-succinimidyl-4-(N-maleimidomethyl)cyclohexane-1-carboxylate (Sulfo-SMCC, 5 mg, 11 mmol) in 1 mL of DPBS at 37 °C, and then gradually add this solution to the BSA solution while stirring at room temperature for 1 h after the addition is complete. Subsequently, perform desalting of the reaction solution using a Sephadex G-50 gel filtration column with DPBS as buffer at room temperature to remove unreacted Sulfo-SMCC. Add E7 peptide (1 mg/mL) to the desalted solution and incubate at room temperature overnight. The reaction mixture is then transferred to a dialysis bag with a molecular weight cutoff of 3500 and dialyzed against deionized water for three days. After freeze-drying, the BSA-E7 is obtained. The labeling quantity of the E7 peptide on BSA-E7 is calculated using MALDI-TOF MS (Shimadzu, MALDI-8030 EasyCare).

Synthesis and characterization of PLGA nanoparticles loaded with KGN

KGN-NPs were prepared by emulsion solvent evaporation. 45 mg of PLGA was dissolved in 2.5 mL of dichloromethane, and then 5 mg of KGN dissolved in 0.5 mL of acetone was added to form an organic phase. The resulting organic phase was added to 6 mL of 1% (w/v) PVA aqueous solution and emulsified by ultrasonication under an ice bath for 4 min to form an oil-in-water (O/W) emulsion. The emulsion was then added dropwise to 50 mL of 0.1% (w/v) PVA aqueous solution at a homogeneous rate and mechanically stirred at 200 rpm overnight to evaporate the organic solvent. The KGN-NPs were then collected by centrifugation (10,000 rpm, 15 min). To remove excess PVA and free drug from the surface of the NPs, the NPs were resuspended in deionised water and centrifuged again to collect the NPs, which was repeated three times. Then, the drug-loaded NPs were obtained after 2 days of freeze-drying. The morphology and particle size of KGN-NPs were characterized with SEM, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) (JEM1400/2100), and dynamic light scattering (DLS) (Zetasizer Nano ZSP, Malvern). Preparation of nanocarrier composite photosensitive Col I-HANB hydrogel

Col I-HANB hydrogel solution containing BSA-E7 and KGN-NPs was prepared by adding an additional 0.7 mg of BSA-E7 (10 μM) and 4 mg of KGN-NPs into per ml of Col I-HANB hydrogel precursor solution. The hydrogel precursor solution (200 μL) was pipetted into a cylindrical mould, and the hydrogel was formed into a physical gel at room temperature and then illuminated with a 365 nm LED light source for 300 s (10 mW/cm2) in situ. The product is designated as KGN/E7-HG.

Release testing of hydrophobic drug KGN in hydrogel

To quantify drug release, hydrogel samples (n = 3) were immersed in 10 mL DPBS (pH 7.4, 37 °C) under gentle shaking (50 rpm). At predetermined time intervals (0-21 days), 1 mL aliquots were withdrawn and replaced with fresh DPBS. Drug concentration was determined by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC, UV detection at 254 nm) against a standard curve. Cumulative release was calculated accounting for sample removal. HPLC analysis (HPLC system, Agilent) was performed on a BetaBasic C18 column with the mobile phase of acetonitrile: water = 40:60 (v/v), 0.1% formic acid solution was added to the aqueous phase, and the flow rate was set at 1 mL/min. The flow rate was set at 1 mL/min, the injection volume was 20 μL each time, and the detection wavelength was 278 nm. More than three parallel samples were set up in this experiment.

Scanning electron microscopy analysis of nanocarrier composite photosensitive Col I-HANB hydrogel samples

SEM was used to observe the microscopic morphology of the hydrogels. The prepared hydrogel samples were frozen in a refrigerator at –20 °C, and then transferred to a lyophilizer for freeze-drying for more than 48 h. The samples were carefully sliced, and then the samples were separated from the samples. The freeze-dried samples were carefully sliced and pasted on a copper stage with the observation side facing upwards using conductive adhesive, and their surfaces were sprayed with gold, and then the surface morphology of the samples was observed by SEM.

First, transfer the sterilized hydrogel precursor solution into a confocal dish, and irradiate it with 365 - nm ultraviolet light for 5 min to prepare hydrogel samples. Then, obtain a uniformly dispersed cell suspension with a cell density of 1 × 10⁵ cells/mL. Add 100 μL of the cell suspension to each hydrogel, so that there are approximately 10,000 cells on each hydrogel, and make the cell suspension stay on the hydrogel surface as much as possible. Next, place the confocal dishes in an incubator and incubate for 1 h. After the cells adhere to the hydrogel surface, add 1 mL of culture medium to each confocal dish, and culture them in an incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 1 day and 3 days, respectively. After the cultivation period, the cells were subjected to viability staining using a live/dead cell dual staining kit (Calcein-AM/PI). Remove the culture medium from the confocal culture dish and add a prepared DPBS solution containing Calcein-AM (2 μL/mL) and PI (3 μL/mL). Return the dish to the incubator and incubate for 20 min. Imaging was performed using a laser confocal microscope (Leica TCS SP8). The objective lens utilized during the imaging process was 10×, with Calcein-AM being excited at 488 nm and PI being excited at 561 nm.

In vivo bone-tendon healing of nanocarrier composite photosensitive Col I-HANB hydrogel in rabbit rotator cuff injury model

Construction of rabbit rotator cuff injury model

All animal experiments were approved by the SJTU Biotechnology Experimental Co. Ltd animal experimental ethics committee, Shanghai, China (JDLL-R-20250111(2)). The 15 mature New Zealand white rabbits (male, 2500–3000 g) were randomly divided into three groups: (1) an experimental group treated with KGN/E7-HG hydrogel; (2) an experimental group treated with HG hydrogel (pure Col I-HANB hydrogel); and (3) a control group without any implants. Following general anesthesia, the rabbits were positioned in a prone orientation. A longitudinal skin incision approximately 4 cm in length was made at the shoulder joint using a surgical scalpel. Subsequently, blunt dissection was performed to separate the subcutaneous tissue, fascia, and deltoid muscle, thereby exposing the insertion point of the supraspinatus tendon at the greater tubercle. The supraspinatus tendon was sharply transected at the greater tubercle insertion point, and a 0.5 cm × 0.5 cm segment of tendon tissue was excised to create a full-thickness rotator cuff injury model. The hydrogel precursor solution was then injected into the space between the supraspinatus tendon and the greater tubercle of the humerus, followed by exposure to 365 nm ultraviolet light (10 mW/cm²) for 5 min to facilitate in situ gelation at the site of the defect. The control group received no treatment following the transection of the supraspinatus tendon. Finally, the wound was sutured and closed in layers. Postoperatively, the rabbits were housed in cages allowing free movement, with no immobilization of the affected limb.

Histological analysis

Four weeks later, the rabbits were euthanized, and the Achilles tendons, along with the greater tuberosity of the humerus, were excised for histological analysis. The tissue samples were immersed in 10% formalin solution and fixed at room temperature for 48 h, after which they were removed and decalcified using a 10% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) solution. After 3 weeks of decalcification process, paraffin-embedded tissue sections were prepared, and 5 µm thick slices of the tissue samples were obtained using a microtome. The resulting tissue sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and safranin O-green, followed by microscopic examination of the tissue morphology and cellular composition at the tendon-bone interface, with photographic documentation for subsequent histological analysis.

Statistical analysis

The data in this study were expressed as means ± standard deviation SD and evaluated using a one-way analysis of variance followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison post hoc test. A P value of 0.05 was defined statistically significant. GraphPad Prism 10.1.2 (GraphPad Software) was used for statistical calculations.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of Col I-HANB hydrogel

Characterization of compounds

We synthesized a small molecule of o-nitrobenzyl alcohol (NB) with amino functional groups and completed its labeling on the HA polymer chain through an amide bond, forming a HA derivative with the function of photo-generated aldehyde groups. The NMR spectrum of HA labeled with NB small molecules (HANB) is shown in Fig. 2. The NMR peaks with chemical shifts at 7.67 ppm and 7.17 ppm are assigned to the hydrogens on the benzene ring of NB, while the peak with a chemical shift at 1.86 ppm is assigned to the hydrogens of the methyl group on HA. The grafting rate of NB on the HA macromolecule was calculated to be 3.6% from the ratio of peak areas.

Analysis of the photodegradation behavior of HANB

We conducted a photolysis test on the HANB macromolecules using UV - vis spectroscopy. As shown in Fig. 3A, with the increase of light irradiation time, the absorption peak at approximately 280 nm increased rapidly, and an isosbestic point appeared at 295 nm. These changes are consistent with the photolysis properties of NB small molecules [43]. This indicates that NB small molecules can undergo rapid photolysis under 365 nm light irradiation, and neither being modified onto the polymer HA nor being introduced into the hydrogel affects its photosensitivity.

Rheological analysis of physicochemical co-crosslinked hydrogels

We conducted a rheological analysis of the gelation process of the Col I-HANB hydrogel precursor solution using a HAAKE MARS Ⅲ rotational rheometer under temperature elevation and photoinitiation. Initially, 200 µL of the hydrogel precursor solution, prepared in a low-temperature environment, was transferred to the testing parallel plates, followed by an immediate temperature ramp test. The conditions were set to increase the temperature from 4 to 37 °C over a duration of 300 s. As illustrated in Fig. 3B, during the heating process, the storage modulus (G’) and loss modulus (G”) curves began to intersect; this intersection point signifies the gel point, at which the material initiates gelation and exhibits characteristics of a solid gel. This indicates that the system forms a physical gel under elevated temperature conditions. After the formation of the physical gel, the temperature was maintained at 37 °C, and at 600 s, an in situ illumination was performed using a 365 nm ultraviolet light source. At this point, the G’ of the hydrogel exhibited an exponential increase, stabilizing after 300 s of illumination, ultimately reaching a modulus of approximately 220 Pa, indicating complete gelation of the system. Although Col I alone exhibits relatively low strength upon forming a physical gel, the coupling of aldehyde groups generated from HANB under UV light with the amino groups on its chains significantly enhances the mechanical properties of the hydrogel.

Characterization of the compressive properties of hydrogels

Compression and cyclic compression tests were conducted on Col I-HANB hydrogels subjected to varying illumination durations (3 min, 5 min, 8 min) using the GT-TCS-2000 dual-column tensile testing machine. Cylindrical hydrogel samples with a diameter of 10 mm and a height of 3 mm were prepared and placed on the compression platform for testing. As illustrated in Fig. 3C, D, with the increase in illumination time from 3 to 8 min, the stress at 65% strain of the hydrogel gradually increased from 55.6 kPa to 159.7 kPa, indicating that the mechanical strength of the hydrogel is modulated by light exposure conditions. Prolonged illumination enhances covalent crosslinking within the hydrogel matrix, thereby improving its mechanical properties.

Subsequently, a cyclic compression test was conducted on the Col I-HANB hydrogel sample with a light exposure time of 5 min. As shown in Fig. 3E, after ten cycles of compression at a strain rate of 40%, the strength of the hydrogel exhibited no significant decline. However, during the recovery process, the hydrogel was unable to revert immediately to its original state; at a strain of approximately 20%, the stress value dropped to zero, and the hysteresis loop was notably large. This phenomenon is primarily attributed to the presence of collagen-induced non-covalent crosslinking within the Col I-HANB hydrogel. Under external force, collagen can dissipate energy through structural reorganization at multiple hierarchical levels, thereby endowing the hydrogel with enhanced toughness.

Evaluation of the tissue adhesive strength of the Col I-HANB hydrogel

The tissue adhesion ability of hydrogels is an important parameter for assessing their tissue engineering applicability, and hydrogel materials with good adhesion ability are of great interest in tissue repair [44]. According to the photocrosslinking mechanism, under UV illumination, the o-nitrobenzyl alcohol group of HANB will be converted to o-nitrobenzaldehyde group, which can be coupled with the reactive amino groups on the surface of biological tissues; therefore, Col I-HANB hydrogel can form covalent connections with the amino groups on the surface of tissues through illumination to chemically immobilise the hydrogel on the surface of tissues, and to achieve a strong interaction between the hydrogel and biological tissues. In order to assess the tissue adhesion ability of Col I-HANB hydrogels, we verified it by visual observation. As shown in Fig. 4A, we prepared Col I-HANB hydrogels by in situ photoexposure on the surface of fresh pork tissues. In order to facilitate observation, a small amount of crystal violet dye was mixed into the hydrogel precursor solution to increase the contrast between the material and the background tissue, so that the hydrogel samples in the picture appear purple. After the Col I-HANB hydrogel precursor solution was gelatinised in-situ on the surface of the pork tissue, we repeatedly twisted the pork tissue to cause it to deform, and it can be observed that at this time, the hydrogel had no tendency to be peeled off the tissue, and still adhered firmly to the tissue. It can be observed that the hydrogel does not tend to peel off from the tissue, but still adheres firmly to the surface of the pork tissue. Subsequently, we immersed the deformed pork tissues in DPBS and observed that the Col I-HANB hydrogel was still firmly adhered to the surface of the tissues after 48 h. The results indicate that the photoluminescent hydrogel is still adhering to the surface of the pork tissues. The above results indicate that the photoluminescent crosslinked hydrogel has good tissue adhesion ability and cannot be separated from the tissue under certain external force and deformation.

Further, we quantitatively measured the tissue adhesion of Col I-HANB hydrogels with fresh porcine enteric coats with good light transmittance were selected as the substrate, so that the hydrogel precursor solution was in situ formed into a gel between two pieces of enteric coats (Fig. 4B–E). As shown in Fig. 4E, the tissue adhesion of the Col I-HANB hydrogel was around 13.5 kPa, whereas after the amino groups on the surface of the porcine enteric coats were partially blocked, the tissue adhesion of the hydrogel was measured to have a significant decrease of around 3 kPa. Tissue adhesion experiments confirmed that Col I-HANB hydrogels have good adhesion ability through covalent attachment to the tissue surface, and can be integrated with tissues, which is an important prerequisite for their subsequent tissue engineering applications.

Analysis of the swelling properties of hydrogels

Typically, hydrogels undergo water absorption swelling in aqueous environments, and the swelling characteristics of the material are crucial for determining their applicability in various fields. In the domain of tissue repair, an excessively high swelling ratio is evidently detrimental. Consequently, we established hydrogel groups subjected to different illumination durations (3 min, 5 min, and 8 min) to evaluate their swelling properties. The swelling ratios were calculated based on the mass ratios of the hydrogels before and after swelling. As illustrated in Fig. 4F, the Col I-HANB hydrogels subjected to 5 min of illumination exhibited negligible differences in appearance and dimensions before and after swelling. Furthermore, after 24 h of swelling, all hydrogel systems reached a near equilibrium state, with swelling ratios approximately around 115%, indicating a relatively modest increase. After 14 days of swelling, the swelling ratios of the hydrogels stabilized around 120%, with minimal variation. Additionally, varying the illumination time from 3 to 8 min did not significantly influence the swelling ratios of the hydrogel systems. Overall, the Col I-HANB hydrogel system demonstrates a low swelling ratio, which adequately meets the requirements for applications in tissue engineering.

Testing of in vitro degradation behavior of hydrogel

Biomaterials intended for tissue regeneration should possess enzymatic degradability. HA and Col I, as constituents of the natural ECM, exhibit excellent biodegradability. In this study, the enzymatic degradation behavior of Col I-HANB hydrogels was investigated under conditions where both collagenase and HAse were present at 37 °C. As is shown in Fig. 4G, in degradation media containing 10 U/mL of collagenase I and 20 U/mL of HAse, the hydrogel’s crosslinked network was mostly disrupted after 24 h. It was completely disrupted after 48 h. Control samples of the hydrogel were immersed in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) without enzymes and maintained their three-dimensional network structure with negligible mass loss after 14 days. The accelerated degradation rate of the hydrogel in the presence of enzymes suggests a potential strategy for utilizing such materials as drug delivery carriers in tissue repair applications, enabling controlled release of therapeutic agents.

Characterization of the structure and morphology of liposome-composite hydrogels

We employed scanning electron microscopy to observe the microstructure of the hydrogel and the encapsulated liposomes. As illustrated in Fig. 5A, both Col I-HANB hydrogel encapsulated and not encapsulated with liposomes exhibited a porous three-dimensional network structure after freeze-drying, with the presence of collagen fibers discernible on the surface. Upon magnification, micron-sized particles were observed within the hydrogel containing encapsulated liposomes, indicating successful incorporation of the liposomes into the hydrogel, with the structural integrity maintained and no damage to the liposomes due to photo-crosslinking. In comparison, the incorporation of liposomes resulted in a denser porous structure of the hydrogel.

Since the freeze-drying operation is involved in the sample preparation process for scanning electron microscopy, the water in the sample forms ice crystals during freeze-drying, which will change the morphology of the hydrogel and fail to present the hydrogel structure in its real hydrated state. Therefore, based on the luminescent properties of liposomes encapsulating the fluorescent dye HPTS, we used a laser scanning confocal microscope to more intuitively observe the liposomes encapsulated within the hydrogel. As shown in Fig. 5B, the liposomes stably exist inside the hydrogel.

Analysis of the release behavior of HPTS from liposome-composite hydrogels

To enhance the transmission of mechanical stress from the hydrogel matrix to the liposomes, we introduced amino group-containing phospholipids, specifically DOPE, onto the liposomal surface. Covalent linkage of the liposomes to the hydrogel network was achieved through photo-initiated conjugation between aldehyde groups and amino groups. To evaluate the drug-loading capacity and mechanosensitive release behavior of the liposome-hydrogel system, we conducted drug release experiments using HPTS dye as a model therapeutic agent, designated as sample HG1.

To illustrate the significant role of liposomes in sustained drug release, we utilized a hydrogel sample devoid of liposomes, physically encapsulating HPTS, as a control sample (HG). Additionally, we prepared liposomes encapsulating HPTS, which are devoid of amino groups, using phospholipid (DOPC). Following the incorporation into the hydrogel, this served as a comparative sample (HG2) to investigate the contribution of covalently linked liposomes to the mechanical response of the hydrogel network. To facilitate normalization, prior to the drug release experiments, the encapsulation efficiency and drug loading of the liposomes were initially determined using an enzyme-linked assay. The pure DOPC liposomes exhibited an encapsulation efficiency of 9.2% and a drug loading of 0.96 wt%. In contrast, liposomes prepared from a mixture of DOPC and DOPE demonstrated an encapsulation efficiency of 13.2% and a drug loading of 1.38 wt%.

As illustrated in Fig. 5C, within the HG hydrogel, HPTS exhibits a rapid initial release, with approximately 90% of the dye released within the first 24 h. In the HG2 hydrogel, approximately 40% of the dye is released over a 48-h period. Conversely, in the HG1 hydrogel, only about 30% of the total dye content is released within 48 h. This indicates that the standalone hydrogel system exhibits significant burst release behavior. The composite system, combining liposomes with hydrogels, substantially mitigates this burst release phenomenon, thereby enhancing the controlled release profile. Such a delivery system demonstrates considerable potential for tissue repair applications that require precise drug delivery.

To investigate the drug release behavior of hydrogels under compression, a dual-column tensile testing machine was employed to subject the samples to repeated compressions at strain levels of 20% and 40%. Following the completion of the compression cycles, the samples were immersed in a buffer solution, and aliquots were taken at predetermined time intervals to measure the release of HPTS, resulting in the generation of the release kinetics curve for HPTS over a period of 120 h. As illustrated in Fig. 5D, there is a marked increase in the release of HPTS from HG1 hydrogel with the escalation of compression levels. Interestingly, the compression treatment exhibited negligible effects on the release of HPTS from HG2 hydrogel, indicating that the pure DOPC liposomes are not chemically bonded to the hydrogel network (Fig. 5E). Consequently, the liposomes are able to evade the stress applied to the hydrogel, rendering mechanical forces ineffective in influencing their drug release. In contrast, when mechanical force is applied to HG1 hydrogel, the force is transmitted to the liposomes, leading to the rupture of liposomes that are covalently anchored within the hydrogel or increasing the permeability of their membranes, thereby accelerating drug release and ultimately achieving a mechanical force-sensitive drug delivery system.

Synthesis and characterization of BSA-E7

To increase the immobilization amount of the E7 peptide, we modified the E7 peptide onto the BSA (BSA—E7), and then achieved the immobilization of the E7 peptide within the hydrogel by utilizing the coupling reaction between the photo—generated aldehyde groups of HANB and the amino groups on BSA. As shown in Fig. 6A, we used the Sulfo—SMCC bifunctional coupling agent to connect the E7 peptide containing a sulfhydryl group with BSA containing an amino group. As shown in Fig. 6B, the MALDI—TOF data showed that the molecular weight of unmodified BSA was 66433, and that of BSA modified with the E7 peptide was 77483. Through calculation, it was found that approximately 10 E7 peptide molecules were modified on the surface of each BSA protein on average.

Characterization of nanocarrier composite photosensitive Col I-HANB hydrogel

We employed PLGA NPs as carriers for the KGN drug and prepared KGN-loaded PLGA nanospheres using the emulsion solvent evaporation method. As shown in Fig. 6C, TEM revealed that the KGN-NPs prepared by the aforementioned method exhibited a spherical morphology with a relatively uniform size distribution, averaging around 290 nm. As depicted in Fig. 6D, DLS indicated that the average diameter of the NPs was 330 nm (PDI = 0.238). The DLS measurement reflects the average hydrodynamic diameter of the NPs in solution, which is typically larger than the size obtained from TEM. Additionally, the loading capacity of KGN-NPs was determined to be 6.5 wt% using UV-Vis spectrophotometry, with an encapsulation efficiency of 65%.

Sustained release of KGN in nanocarrier composite photosensitive Col I-HANB hydrogels

KGN-NPs were incorporated into the hydrogel precursor solution, and the Col I - HANB hydrogel material encapsulating KGN-NPs was formed under 365—nm light irradiation. The release behavior of KGN in the nanocomposite hydrogel was studied by high—performance liquid chromatography. Due to the anticipated future application of nanocomposite hydrogels in the human body, the test was conducted at 37 °C. A higher temperature could potentially accelerate drug diffusion and alter release kinetics. As shown in Fig. 6E-F, the nanoparticle—composite hydrogel material can continuously release KGN within the 21—day experimental period, achieving the expected effect and creating conditions for the long—term in vivo drug release of the material.

Characterization of the morphology of nanocarrier composite photosensitive Col I-HANB hydrogel structures

As shown in Fig. 6G, both the Col I—HANB hydrogels with and without KGN—NPs loading presented a porous three—dimensional network structure after freeze—drying, which was conducive to the release of the KGN drug encapsulated inside and promoted the repair of rotator cuff injuries. After magnification, it could be observed that the NPs aggregated in the hydrogel structure. Since the amount of NPs added was small, it had little effect on the morphology of the hydrogel.

Evaluation of nanocarrier composite photosensitive Col I-HANB hydrogel biocompatibility

Cell compatibility is a crucial prerequisite for the application of materials in the field of tissue engineering. To evaluate the impact of the addition of BSA-E7 and KGN-NPs on the cell compatibility of hydrogel materials, three different compositions of hydrogels were established, designated as HG (pure Col I-HANB hydrogel), E7-HG (photosensitive hydrogel incorporating E7 peptide), and KGN/E7-HG (hydrogel incorporating E7 peptide and encapsulated KGN-NPs). HUVEC were employed as the cell model, and a co-culture approach was utilized. HUVEC are primary human cells and widely used for cytotoxicity and biocompatibility testing in the field of biomaterials. The cell viability on the hydrogel surface was assessed using a CCK-8 assay after co-culturing for 1 and 3 days, thereby evaluating the cell compatibility of the hydrogels. As illustrated in Fig. 6H, after co-culturing for both 1 and 3 days, the cell viability on the surfaces of the three types of hydrogels exceeded 90%. After 3 days of co-culture, the cell viability on the surfaces of HG and E7-HG hydrogels was 96% and 98%, respectively, while the cell viability on the surface of KGN/E7-HG hydrogel reached 108%. The differences in cell viability between these groups were statistically significant (p < 0.05). These results indicate that the Col I-HANB series hydrogels exhibit minimal cytotoxicity, and the incorporation of KGN-NPs and BSA-E7 does not adversely affect the cell compatibility of the hydrogels; rather, it appears to promote cell proliferation to some extent.

Cell adhesion on nanocarrier composite photosensitive Col I-HANB hydrogel surfaces

HUVEC cell suspension was seeded onto the surface of the Col I-HANB series hydrogels and co-cultured with the addition of culture medium for 1 day and 3 days. Subsequently, live-dead staining was performed, and the viability and adhesion status of the cells were observed using laser confocal microscopy. As illustrated in Fig. 7, the cells maintained a high survival rate across all three groups of hydrogels, with almost no red dead cells observed. Additionally, the cells not only adhered to the surface of the hydrogels but also exhibited spreading and demonstrated favorable growth conditions, further confirming the superior cell compatibility of Col I-HANB hydrogels.

In vivo animal experiments

The morphology and structure of the rabbit supraspinatus muscle are analogous to those of humans. From a histological perspective, the rabbit rotator cuff tendon contains a transition zone similar to that found in humans, facilitating the study of tendon-to-bone healing. Consequently, we established a rabbit rotator cuff injury model, as illustrated in Fig. 8A–D, to investigate the repair capabilities of KGN/E7 peptide-complexed phot cross-linked hydrogel for rotator cuff injuries.

The rabbits were evenly divided into three groups for the rotator cuff injury repair experiment: (1) the experimental group treated with KGN/E7-HG hydrogel; (2) the experimental group treated with HG hydrogel; and (3) the control group without any implants (Control). In the experiment, the hydrogel precursor solution was filled between the supraspinatus tendon and the greater tubercle of the humerus, photo-crosslinked to form a gel, and fixed at the site, followed by surgical suturing. Conclusion

In this study, we constructed a photochemical imine-crosslinked hydrogel system using HANB and Col I. Furthermore, the formation of imine bonds between aldehyde groups generated by NB illumination and amino groups on the surface of liposomes facilitated the covalent immobilization of liposomes within the hydrogel. This system exhibits a low swelling ratio, biodegradability, and excellent tissue adhesion capabilities. The liposome-composite hydrogel system provides two physical barriers for the encapsulated dye (drug) molecules, effectively reducing the burst release of the encapsulated drugs.

Based on the demand for tendon-bone injury repair, E7 peptide and the small molecule drug KGN were incorporated into hydrogels to endow them with biological functions that facilitate tendon-bone injury repair. PLGA nanoparticles were employed as carriers for KGN, which was physically blended into the hydrogel, enabling sustained release of KGN within the hydrogel system over a 21-day experimental period. In vitro cytotoxicity and live/dead staining assays demonstrated that the hydrogel material possesses excellent cytocompatibility and cell adhesion capabilities. Finally, the hydrogel was applied to a rabbit rotator cuff injury model, confirming that the composite hydrogel material exhibits superior biocompatibility and the ability to promote fibrocartilage regeneration. These results indicate that our hydrogel material can provide a favorable microenvironment for tendon-bone healing at the site of rotator cuff injuries, suggesting a promising application in the treatment of such injuries.
