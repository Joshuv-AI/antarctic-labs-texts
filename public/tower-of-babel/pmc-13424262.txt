CYP2C19 Genotyping to Guide Clopidogrel Prescribing for Neurovascular Indications: A Survey of Institutional Approaches
Authors: Rachael M Stone, Glenda Hoffecker, April Schultz, Brittney H Davis, Salma A Bargal, Amber L Beitelshees, Nihal El Rouby, Madeline Norris, Kevin J Rodriguez, Nita Limdi, Larisa H Cavallari, Craig R Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) with aspirin and clopidogrel is commonly used for secondary prevention following acute ischemic stroke (AIS) and neurointerventional procedures. However, the efficacy of clopidogrel is reduced in carriers of CYP2C19 no‐function alleles. While CYP2C19‐guided DAPT is well‐established for some cardiovascular indications, its use in neurovascular settings has remained limited until recently. This study characterized current implementation strategies for CYP2C19‐guided DAPT for neurovascular indications across institutions in the Pharmacogenomics Global Research Network Implementation Working Group. A REDCap survey was distributed to 54 institutions targeting those currently utilizing or planning to implement CYP2C19 testing to guide clopidogrel use for neurovascular indications. The survey collected data on implementation status, workflows, testing logistics, clinical decision support (CDS), and guideline concordance. Fifteen institutions met inclusion criteria; 80% were in the United States and 87% were academic centers. CYP2C19 testing was used for neurovascular indications at 12 sites, most commonly for AIS, intracranial aneurysm repair, and intracranial stenting. Implementation was often built on infrastructure previously established for cardiovascular indications. Most sites used multigene panels, offered testing in both inpatient and outpatient settings, and returned results within 7 days. Eighty percent of sites provided CDS to support application of results. All sites recommended an alternative P2Y12 inhibitor (e.g., ticagrelor) for poor metabolizers, and most did for intermediate metabolizers. CYP2C19‐guided antiplatelet therapy for neurovascular indications is expanding, with many implementations leveraging preexisting infrastructure from cardiovascular settings. Broader adoption and outcome‐focused research are needed to optimize the clinical utility of genotype‐guided DAPT in neurovascular care.

Study Highlights

WHAT IS THE CURRENT KNOWLEDGE ON THE TOPIC?

Dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) with aspirin + clopidogrel is commonly used for secondary prevention after acute ischemic stroke and neurointerventional procedures. Clopidogrel requires bioactivation by CYP2C19, and CYP2C19 no‐function variant alleles are associated with decreased inhibition of platelet reactivity and higher ischemic event risk in clopidogrel‐treated patients. Growing evidence supports a benefit of CYP2C19 genotype‐guided DAPT for neurovascular indications, and a recent update to the American Heart Association‐American Stroke Association acute ischemic stroke guidelines recommends an alternative P2Y12 medication for patients who are known to be CYP2C19 no‐function allele carriers. Despite this, the prevalence of CYP2C19 genotyping in neurovascular care is unknown and implementation strategies for this population are not well‐characterized.

WHAT QUESTION DID THIS STUDY ADDRESS?

What is the current state of CYP2C19‐clopidogrel implementation for neurovascular indications, and what strategies are institutions utilizing to implement CYP2C19 testing in this population?

WHAT DOES THIS STUDY ADD TO OUR KNOWLEDGE?

Across international sites, CYP2C19 testing for neurovascular care is emerging, often leveraging prior infrastructure for testing in the cardiovascular population. Most sites test inpatients, return results in the electronic health record with clinical decision support, and recommend alternative P2Y12 inhibitors for CYP2C19 no‐function allele carriers. Implementation is largely clinician‐initiated and heterogeneous in testing timing (preemptive vs. reactive).

HOW MIGHT THIS CHANGE CLINICAL PHARMACOLOGY OR TRANSLATIONAL SCIENCE?

These real‐world implementations identify strategies such as infrastructure build, clinical decision support, and clinician education to accelerate uptake of CYP2C19 genotype‐guided antiplatelet therapy in neurovascular care.

Dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) with aspirin and a P2Y12 inhibitor is commonly prescribed for secondary prevention after acute ischemic stroke (AIS) and transient ischemic attack (TIA), and following neurointerventional procedures such as intracranial aneurysm repair and intracranial stentings. 1 , 2 Clopidogrel is widely used in DAPT due to its lower cost and favorable side effect profile compared with alternative P2Y12 inhibitors like ticagrelor. 2 Clopidogrel is a prodrug that requires bioactivation via cytochrome P450 2C19 (CYP2C19). Common no‐function, also referred to as loss‐of‐function (LOF), variant alleles in CYP2C19 impair clopidogrel activation. CYP2C19 intermediate metabolizers (IMs) carrying one no‐function allele and poor metabolizers (PMs) carrying two no‐function alleles are at significantly increased risk of high platelet reactivity (HPR) and diminished clinical effectiveness when treated with clopidogrel. 3 , 4

Clopidogrel is also frequently used following percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), and use of CYP2C19 genotyping to guide P2Y12 inhibitor prescribing has been most common in this setting. 5 , 6 Significant evidence from randomized controlled trials (RCTs), meta‐analyses, and observational implementation studies demonstrates that, compared with patients without a no‐function allele, clopidogrel‐treated patients carrying a CYP2C19 no‐function allele are at increased risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) following a PCI procedure, which is mitigated by use of an alternate P2Y12 inhibitor such as prasugrel or ticagrelor. 3 , 6 , 7 , 8 A 2024 American Heart Association (AHA) scientific statement 9 and an international consensus statement 10 both support CYP2C19 genotyping to guide antiplatelet therapy among patients with acute coronary syndrome (ACS) and/or PCI.

Emerging evidence in the neurovascular setting, particularly among patients with AIS or TIA, has similarly demonstrated HPR and higher ischemic event risk among CYP2C19 no‐function allele carriers treated with clopidogrel. 11 , 12 A meta‐analysis showed that clopidogrel‐treated stroke patients carrying a CYP2C19 no‐function allele had a 2.2‐fold increased risk of recurrent stroke compared with noncarriers. 13 The CHANCE‐2 RCT demonstrated that ticagrelor reduced the relative risk of recurrent stroke by 23%, compared with clopidogrel, among CYP2C19 IMs or PMs. 14 Although data in clopidogrel‐treated patients undergoing neurointerventional procedures remain limited, 11 recent studies suggest that CYP2C19 no‐function allele variants are associated with HPR, mirroring data in stroke/TIA patients. 15 Based on this accumulating evidence, the Clinical Pharmacogenetics Implementation Consortium (CPIC) recommends an alternative P2Y12 inhibitor in both CYP2C19 IMs and PMs for neurovascular indications. 3 Similarly, the Dutch Pharmacogenetics Working Group (DPWG) recommends avoiding clopidogrel and choosing an alternative agent for CYP2C19 PMs with a stroke or TIA indication, and to either choose an alternative agent or double the clopidogrel dose (150 mg/day) in IMs. 16 Citing the CHANCE‐2 trial, a recent update to the AHA‐American Stroke Association (ASA) AIS management guidelines recommended DAPT with ticagrelor + aspirin over clopidogrel + aspirin for patients who are CYP2C19 no‐function allele carriers. 17

Despite growing evidence supporting the clinical benefit of CYP2C19 testing in neurovascular settings, its adoption in practice has remained limited. 18 This is likely influenced by the relative recency of RCT data and guideline updates addressing its utility in neurovascular as compared with cardiovascular indications. To characterize the current state of CYP2C19‐clopidogrel implementation for neurovascular indications, we leveraged the Pharmacogenomics Global Research Network (PGRN) Implementation Working Group 19 to investigate the infrastructure and procedures of CYP2C19 testing within neurovascular contexts across sites that have implemented or plan to implement CYP2C19 testing.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

A REDCap survey was distributed to 114 members of the PGRN Implementation Working Group (representing 54 institutions) from 1/3/2025 to 2/17/2025 to capture implementation experiences from institutions that utilize CYP2C19 genotyping to guide clopidogrel prescribing for neurovascular indications. The survey was approved by the University of Virginia Institutional Review Board. Only one survey response was permitted per site. Descriptive statistics were used to analyze survey responses.

Participation was requested from PGRN member institutions that had either (1) implemented or were planning to implement within 6 months CYP2C19‐clopidogrel testing for one or more neurovascular indications, or (2) observed CYP2C19‐clopidogrel testing in the neurovascular space using previous implementation infrastructure for other (e.g., cardiovascular) indications. For the purposes of this survey, neurovascular indications for clopidogrel included AIS or TIA, intracranial aneurysm repair, intracranial stents, and any other indication for which clopidogrel may be considered for prevention or treatment of a primarily neurologic indication. Implementation was defined as pharmacogenetic testing that is done on‐site or through an outside laboratory with results returned for clinical use (e.g., entered into the electronic health record [EHR]) and having an infrastructure in place to support test ordering, test interpretation, clinical application, and/or returning results to patients.

The survey gathered information about site characteristics, including indications for which CYP2C19 results were used to guide prescribing, neurovascular specialties that utilized CYP2C19 testing, CYP2C19 testing workflows, result interpretation, clinical recommendations, as well as whether and how platelet function tests (PFT) were used to inform antiplatelet therapy changes.

RESULTS

Survey responses were received from 19 of 54 institutions (35%). Four institutions were excluded from the analysis because they had not yet observed CYP2C19 testing for neurovascular indications and did not have plans to intentionally implement within 6 months (Figure S1 ). Results from the 15 sites included in the analysis are summarized in Table 1 . Most respondents were academic institutions (87%) and located in the United States (80%); other countries included Canada, Slovenia, and Zimbabwe (Table S1 ). Of the 15 sites, 12 (80%) reported that CYP2C19 testing is currently used in neurovascular patients. Five of these 12 sites made concerted efforts to implement CYP2C19 testing for neurovascular indications, while the other seven sites followed more passive implementation efforts. The remaining three sites had not started testing for neurovascular indications but were in the planning stages. Eleven of the 15 sites (73%) previously implemented CYP2C19 testing to guide clopidogrel prescribing for non‐neurovascular indications, including ACS and/or PCI.

At the 12 sites currently utilizing CYP2C19 testing for neurovascular indications, the most common neurovascular specialties using testing were vascular neurology/stroke, interventional neuroradiology, and neurosurgery, while AIS/TIA, intracranial aneurysm repair, and intracranial stenting were the most common neurological indications for testing. The five sites that intentionally implemented CYP2C19 testing for neurovascular indications did so in 2024 or early 2025. Clinicians were responsible for initiating CYP2C19 testing orders at 13 of the 15 sites (87%). No institution reported use of an interruptive alert to remind clinicians to order CYP2C19 genotyping for neurovascular indications. Only two respondents had genotyping integrated into an order set. Twelve of the 15 sites (80%) performed testing for inpatients, six of which were also testing outpatients; two additional sites reported that the setting differed by indication/specialty or provider, and one reported exclusively testing outpatients.

Workflows for CYP2C19 testing are described in Figure 1 a and Figure S2 . A similar proportion of sites reported testing preemptively before clopidogrel initiation (47%, n = 7) and reactively after clopidogrel initiation (47%, n = 7), with multiple sites reporting a combination of both (Figure S2A ). Most sites reported use of a multigene testing panel (60%, n = 9) (Figure S2B ) and a turnaround time of 7 days or less (73%, n = 11) (Figure S2C ). Three sites (20%) reported a same‐day turnaround time using a point of care or rapid test (Figure S2C ).

Regarding laboratory testing, six institutions (40%) exclusively used an in‐house clinical laboratory or commercial point of care/rapid testing modality, six sites (40%) sent out tests to an outside laboratory, and three (20%) reported both options. Nine institutions (60%) used blood as the sample type, three (20%) used buccal samples, and three (20%) reported use of both blood and buccal samples. All but one institution tested for all three of the Association of Molecular Pathology (AMP) CYP2C19 “must‐test” alleles (*2, *3, *17), 20 and multiple institutions tested additional alleles (Table S3 ).

All 13 sites in the US and Canada reported following CPIC guidelines for CYP2C19‐guided antiplatelet therapy, while both sites outside North America followed DPWG recommendations. For IMs, 14 of 15 sites (93%) recommended use of an alternative P2Y12 inhibitor, with one of the 14 also recommending a higher dose of clopidogrel. For PMs, all sites recommended use of an alternative P2Y12 inhibitor.

Among the 15 sites, 13 (87%) reported that CYP2C19 results are recorded as discrete data in the EHR and eight (53%) entered a pharmacogenetics consult note (Figure 1 b ). Thirteen sites (87%) reported either active or passive CDS for genotype‐guided clopidogrel prescribing. Of these 13 sites, two used only passive CDS, three reported only interruptive CDS, and eight incorporated both active and passive CDS. Responsibility for CYP2C19‐guided clopidogrel therapy recommendations most commonly fell to neurovascular physicians (80%), precision medicine or pharmacogenetics specialists (47%), pharmacists (40%), and/or advanced neurovascular practice providers (33%) (Figure 1 c ). Clinician education was offered at 12 (80%) sites, with in‐services or workshops (67%; n = 8) and one‐on‐one communication or education (58%; n = 7) being most common. Similar implementation strategies were observed across sites that had vs. had not previously implemented CYP2C19 testing for non‐neurovascular indications (Table S2 ).

Nearly all sites (93%; n = 14) returned results to patients (Figure 1 d ). The patient portal was the most frequently reported method of returning patient results (64%; n = 9), followed by a paper copy or letter (36%; n = 5) or verbally relaying results during a clinic visit (36%; n = 5).

Seven of 15 sites (47%) reported that PFT was also used to inform clopidogrel prescribing for neurovascular indications. Two institutions reported PFT use for intracranial stenting, either alone (n = 1) or in conjunction with CYP2C19 genotyping (n = 1). One institution indicated PFT use for AIS/TIA, and four reported using PFT for intracranial aneurysm repair. Only one of seven sites had a formal algorithm to guide the application of PFT results for clopidogrel prescribing.

DISCUSSION

We previously reported that CYP2C19 testing to guide selection of P2Y12 inhibitor therapy was the most common example of pharmacogenetic testing across members of the PGRN Implementation Working Group. 19 Implementation strategies for CYP2C19 testing following PCI have been described. 5 Herein, we collectively share strategies across 15 PGRN institutions who reported implementation of CYP2C19‐guided antiplatelet therapy for neurovascular indications, which represent approximately half of the PGRN institutions previously reporting CYP2C19 testing for any indication. 19

Most survey respondents were from academic institutions within the US and had previously established infrastructure to support CYP2C19‐guided antiplatelet therapy following ACS or PCI, with testing for neurovascular indications arising organically without an intentional effort to implement in this space. CYP2C19 testing was most often ordered following AIS or TIA, with some sites also testing for intracranial stenting, intracranial aneurysm repair, or other neurovascular indications. Sites commonly integrated CYP2C19 results into the EHR and used CDS alerts to guide alternative therapy for IMs and PMs.

Genotype‐guided recommendations provided by sites were consistent with those in CPIC or DPWG guidelines. 3 , 16 For neurovascular indications, CPIC provides a “moderate” recommendation to consider an alternative P2Y12 inhibitor for both CYP2C19 IMs and PMs, reflecting the somewhat limited prospective outcomes evidence in this area and less certain benefit–risk balance. 3 Notably, prasugrel is contraindicated in patients with a history of stroke or TIA and should not be used as alternative therapy in these patients. 21 While the DPWG provides an option for doubling the clopidogrel dose (150 mg/day) in IMs, this recommendation has not been updated since 2018, is not included in the 2022 CPIC guideline update, and studies have demonstrated insufficient inhibition of platelet aggregation with this approach. 22 , 23 Fourteen of 15 institutions responding to our survey recommended use of an alternative P2Y12 inhibitor for IMs, with one site also recommending higher dose clopidogrel. All sites recommended an alternative P2Y12 inhibitor for PMs. Overall, as demonstrated by our survey, implementation of a CYP2C19‐guided P2Y12 inhibitor selection strategy, in alignment with CPIC and DPWG guidelines, is emerging in neurovascular clinical practice at several institutions. However, our study did not delineate what specific alternative treatments (e.g., ticagrelor, others) were prescribed for each indication at each institution, which is a limitation and necessitates further study. Moreover, our study was limited to institutions with PGRN members, and the results may not be generalizable to all institutions and settings.

Previous studies assessing strategies for CYP2C19 testing following PCI did not specifically ask about PFT. 5 Strategies for guided DAPT for cardiovascular indications generally include either CYP2C19 genotyping or PFT, rather than the two in combination. 10 Our survey of neurovascular settings found that PFT was used in addition to CYP2C19 testing at nearly half (n = 7) of sites. Only one out of these seven sites reported having a formal algorithm to guide clinical decision making based on PFT. Future studies are needed to assess how PFT is used in conjunction with CYP2C19 genotyping among various neurovascular indications and the impact on outcomes.

In summary, we describe strategies used across 15 international sites for implementing CYP2C19 genotype‐guided P2Y12 inhibitor therapy for neurovascular indications. Although there are clinical trial data and clinical practice guidelines supporting such an approach after AIS/TIA, 14 , 17 there remains a gap in outcomes evidence in real‐world practice and for other neurovascular indications. 10 Future real‐world outcome studies of genotype‐guided P2Y12 inhibitor therapy in the neurovascular setting are needed. Multisite collaborations, such as through the PGRN Implementation Working Group, may provide the opportunity to fill this evidence gap and evaluate the impact of implementing CYP2C19‐guided antiplatelet therapy in patients with neurovascular disease.
