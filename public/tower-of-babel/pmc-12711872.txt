Antagonistic activity against innate immunity determines virulence in mammalian bornaviruses
Authors: Akiko Makino, Chiaki Tanaka, Kan Fujino, Keizo Tomonaga
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractVariegated squirrel bornavirus 1 (VSBV-1) is a lethal emerging disease that has been discovered in privately owned and zoo squirrels, but its pathogenicity as a zoonotic pathogen remains uncertain. To better understand VSBV-1 virulence, we investigated its virological properties and developed a recombinant virus with Borna disease virus 1 (BoDV-1) carrying the VSBV-1 X/P sequence. Our study revealed that the recombinant BoDV-1 had a 100% lethality rate and was significantly more pathogenic in adult rats than the wild-type BoDV-1. These findings not only enhance our understanding of VSBV-1 virulence, but also provide crucial insights for the development of vaccines and therapeutics against emerging bornaviruses.Subject terms: Microbiology, Virology

Introduction

Variegated squirrel bornavirus 1 (VSBV-1) is an emerging zoonotic pathogen that has been found in the brains of three squirrel breeders who developed neurological symptoms and died of progressive meningoencephalitis or encephalitis between 2011 and 20131. Subsequently, a retrospective study demonstrated VSBV-1 infection in the brain specimen of a zoo animal caretaker suffering from fatal myeloencephalitis2. In addition, other studies identified VSBV-1 infection in squirrel keepers who developed and died of encephalitis3,4. To date, VSBV-1 infection has been confirmed in squirrels and humans, and squirrels in the subfamilies Sciurinae and Callosciurinae are considered possible carriers of the virus to humans5. While VSBV-1-infected squirrels seem to be asymptomatic with only mild inflammation-related alterations observed in the brains6, no asymptomatic cases have been identified in humans7.

VSBV-1 belongs to the species Orthobornavirus sciuri in the genus Orthobornavirus and is closely related to the classic mammalian bornavirus, Borna disease virus 1 (BoDV-1), which causes severe meningoencephalitis in horses and sheep, known as Borna disease1,8. Recent studies have shown that BoDV-1 possesses shrews as a natural host, and causes lethal encephalitis in humans4,9–12, indicating that these two mammalian bornaviruses are deleterious pathogens that should be monitored for transmission to humans. Although both mammalian bornaviruses cause severe disease in humans, the cumulative number of lethal human cases reported to date is higher for BoDV-1 than for VSBV-1; nevertheless, VSBV-1 has shown a high case fatality among reported infections7.

The underlying molecular mechanisms contributing to the high virulence of VSBV-1 in humans remain elusive. Pathological examinations of VSBV-1-infected individuals have demonstrated CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell infiltration in antigen-positive brain lesion areas13, paralleling the T-cell-mediated immunopathology observed in BoDV-1 infections14–17. On the other hand, experimental infection of VSBV-1 isolated from an infected squirrel into the brains of neonatal rats revealed that VSBV-1 has low pathogenicity, with some rats evading infection18. Moreover, a recent study showed that rhesus macaques intracerebrally inoculated with VSBV-1 developed severe neurological signs with high viral titers and meningeal inflammatory cell infiltrates into the brain19. Collectively, these findings indicated that VSBV-1 evokes host immune responses leading to severe neuropathology.

Neuropathogenesis in BoDV-1 involves several proteins, with the nucleoprotein (N) being the most abundantly produced and a primary target for both humoral and CD8+ T-cell-mediated immune responses in infected hosts20,21. The phosphoprotein (P), a viral RNA-dependent RNA polymerase cofactor, also emerges as a significant virulence factor. We previously demonstrated that transgenic mice expressing BoDV-1 P in the brain develop symptoms similar to BoDV-1 infection22,23. It has been shown that phosphorylation of P contributes to neuronal dysfunction and neurobehavioral abnormalities24,25. Notably, BoDV-1 P suppresses innate immunity, using its phosphorylation site to act as a decoy for TBK-126,27. In addition, the X protein of BoDV-1, overlapping with P ORF, is not vital for viral particle formation but modulates viral polymerase activity and apoptosis, facilitating BoDV-1 persistent infection28–31. Despite these insights in BoDV-1 infection, the roles of VSBV-1 proteins in the pathogenesis have remained unclear.

In this study, we evaluated the functions of VSBV-1 genes to understand the highly pathogenic potential of VSBV-1. Notably, it was shown that VSBV-1 P did not inhibit innate immunity and contrasted with the subcellular localization of BoDV-1 P. By generating a recombinant BoDV-1 (rBoDV-1) carrying the X/P gene of VSBV-1, we found a potential link between VSBV-1 virulence and its minimal antagonism of the innate immune response in rats. Our results suggest that the differences in inhibitory activity against the innate immune response may drive the difference in the virulence among mammalian bornaviruses.

Results

Characterization of VSBV-1 genes

Similar to BoDV-1, VSBV-1 has at least six ORFs1,8. We artificially synthesized cDNAs based on the genomic sequence of a VSBV-1 strain (LN713681.1) detected in humans in 20141 and inserted them into a pCAGGS plasmid to generate expression plasmids for six genes, namely, the N, X, P, matrix (M), glycoprotein (G), and large (L) genes, and evaluated their functions (Fig. 1a). To evaluate VSBV-1 G-mediated entry, we transiently expressed the G gene of VSBV-1 in 293T cells infected with rBoDV-1 ΔG-GFP, which lacks the G gene, to generate pseudotyped viruses as previously reported (Fig. 1a)32,33. Forty-eight hours after transfection, cells were subjected to a freeze–thaw cycle, and the cell-free pseudotyped virus was clarified by centrifugation to remove debris. We infected cells with pseudotyped viruses and incubated for 3 days, after which GFP-positive cells were counted under fluorescence microscopy. A pseudotyped virus carrying VSBV-1 G showed the same infectivity to cell lines as BoDV-1 G; both viruses did not infect HeLa, CHO-K1, or NIH3T3 cells (Fig. 1b), indicating that the G-mediated cell tropism of VSBV-1 is similar to that of BoDV-1. Hierarchical clustering of pairwise % identity across full-length G proteins separated BoDV-1 and VSBV-1 into tight within-species blocks while also revealing substantial identity between species (Supplementary Fig. 1). These patterns indicate that G is highly conserved within each species and largely conserved between species, consistent with strong functional constraints on the glycoprotein.

To evaluate the compatibility of the N and P genes between VSBV-1 and BoDV-1, which comprise the viral ribonucleoproteins (vRNPs), we next used the BoDV-1 minigenome system to replace each BoDV-1 protein with the corresponding VSBV-1 protein. As shown in Fig. 1c, VSBV-1 N exhibited significantly reduced activity of the BoDV-1 minigenome, while the P substitute increased the polymerase activity of BoDV-1 L. Because N and P were detected using antibodies with different affinities and epitope accessibility, their band intensities are not directly comparable; we therefore interpret differences in minigenome activity as functional rather than reflective of relative protein abundance. Consistent with the finding that BoDV-1 X suppresses viral polymerase activity29–31, VSBV-1 X induced decreased polymerase activity to the same extent as BoDV-1 X in the BoDV-1 minigenome assay (Fig. 1d). These results indicate that N, X, and P in VSBV-1 and BoDV-1 are compatible, and that VSBV-1 P increases the polymerase activity of a BoDV-1 minigenome.

To ensure successful infection, many viruses have mechanisms to suppress the innate immune response in infected cells. The P of BoDV-1 is known to play a pivotal role in suppressing innate immunity through an interaction with TBK-126,27. To evaluate whether VSBV-1 genes have an inhibitory effect against innate immunity pathways, we measured the promoter activity of IFN-β; a reporter plasmid with repeated IRF binding sites (p-55C1B-Luc)34 and each viral gene was cotransfected into 293T cells, and 24 h later, polyinosinic-polycytidylic acid (poly I:C) treatment was administered. As shown in Fig. 1e, BoDV-1 P suppressed the induction of innate immunity, while none of the tested VSBV-1 genes showed significant differences compared to the negative control. Indeed, by aligning the orthobornavirus P sequences, we confirmed that the amino acid residues important for the immunosuppressive activity of BoDV-1 P are not present in VSBV-1 P (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Subcellular localization of VSBV-P

Since VSBV-1 P appears to have different properties from BoDV-1 P, we next examined the subcellular localization of P, which has been shown to affect intranuclear viral replication and de novo virion assembly. Previous reports have shown that BoDV-1 P shuttles between the nucleus and cytoplasm in virus-infected cells, whereas it remains restricted to the nucleus when expressed alone35,36. In addition, we have shown that BoDV-1 P can alter its subcellular localization by interacting with X to control the intracellular dynamics of vRNPs37. Using plasmids expressing C-terminal FLAG-tagged BoDV-1 or VSBV-1 P, we observed that BoDV-1 P localized primarily to the nucleus upon transfection in oligodendroglioma (OL) cells, as previously reported. Interestingly, however, VSBV-1 P was predominantly detected in the cytoplasm (Fig. 2a). Furthermore, coexpression of BoDV-1 X altered the subcellular distribution of BoDV-1 P. However, VSBV-1 X did not alter that of VSBV-1 P (Fig. 2a).

VSBV-1 P localizes to the cytoplasm due to the amino acid residues at positions 32, 33, and 36 in the N-terminal NLS.

a Cellular localization of wild-type VSBV-1 P and BoDV-1 P and mutants with changes to the NLS and NES. Scale bar: 10 μm. b Schematic of the NLSs and NES in P.

Previous reports have identified two nuclear localization signals (NLSs) at the termini and a nuclear export signal (NES) in the middle of BoDV-1 P36,38,39(Fig. 2b). We next quantified motif conservation by aligning P sequences from all publicly available complete genomes of BoDV-1 (n = 105) and VSBV-1 (n = 24; unique protein IDs). In BoDV-1, the N-terminal NLS was identical in 92.38% of sequences, the central NES in 90.48%, and the C-terminal NLS in 99.05%. In VSBV-1, all three motifs were invariant (100% identity across all sequences). The accessions and metadata for all sequences analyzed are provided in Supplementary Data 3. Therefore, to understand the differential subcellular distribution of BoDV-1 and VSBV-1 P, we generated P mutants by substituting each signal sequence in one virus with that of the other and then examined their subcellular localization in a plasmid-based FLAG immunofluorescence assay (no infection). We found that mutants with substitutions in the N-terminal NLS showed distinct subcellular localization patterns from wild-type P, namely mixed nuclear and cytoplasmic signals in both directions (VSBV-1 P carrying the BoDV-1 N-terminal NLS and BoDV-1 P carrying the VSBV-1 N-terminal NLS), whereas the other substitution mutants showed localization patterns similar to the wild-type protein (Fig. 2a). Alignment of the amino acid sequences of P in VSBV-1 and BoDV-1 revealed that three amino acid residues in the N-terminal NLS were different (Fig. 2b). Taken together, our results show that VSBV-1 P has a different subcellular localization pattern than BoDV-1 P, and its responsible amino acid residues are K, R, and Q at positions 32, 33, and 36.

Generation of rBoDV-1 carrying the X/P gene of VSBV-1

To investigate the significance of the distinct properties of VSBV-1 P in viral replication, we generated rBoDV-1 with VSBV-1 P instead of BoDV-1 P on the genome. Since the P gene is expressed as the same transcript as the X gene, and the P ORF partially overlaps with the X gene, we designed a cDNA encoding an antigenomic RNA of BoDV-1 with an X/P gene sequence of VSBV-1 (Fig. 3a) and rescued the recombinant virus using the reverse genetics system40. The rescue of the recombinant virus, named rBoDV-1 XPV (XPV), was confirmed by RT-PCR analysis, sequencing, and Western blotting (Fig. 3b, c). As shown in Fig. 3b–d, we found that no difference in viral titer was detected between the cells infected with wild-type rBoDV-1 (Wt) and XPV. Furthermore, although the growth kinetics of XPV was slightly reduced by cell-free virus inoculation into Vero cells at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.1, both recombinant viruses replicated comparatively and established persistent infection (Fig. 3e). These results suggest that the X/P gene of VSBV-1 is exchangeable with that of BoDV-1 during viral replication in cultured cells.

The X/P gene of VSBV-1 induces lethal infection in adult rats

To investigate the role of the VSBV-1 X/P gene in viral pathogenicity in vivo, we next inoculated 105 infectious units (IU) of recombinant GFP virus intracranially into neonatal rats or intranasally into 4-week-old adult rats (LEW/SsN strain), and monitored body weight, survival rate, and brain viral titer (Fig. 4a). In neonatal rats, survival rates did not differ between Wt- and XPV-infected animals, although weight gain was lower in XPV-infected rats (Fig. 4b, c). On the other hand, the average brain viral titer was 12 times higher in XPV-infected rats compared to Wt-infected rats at 69 days post infection (Fig. 4d). In contrast, all XPV-infected adult rats died within 63 days of virus inoculation, whereas all Wt-infected rats survived (Fig. 4c). Adult rats inoculated with XPV gained weight until 42 days post-infection, but by 49 days these rats began to show nosebleeds, neurological symptoms such as stridor and ataxia, and weight loss (Fig. 4b). Brain viral titers showed significantly high titers in XPV-infected adult rats at 63 days post infection (Fig. 4d). These results suggest that while the propagation of Wt was controlled in neonatal and adult rats, that of XPV was not suppressed and consequently exhibited lethal virulence in adult rats.

Histopathological assessment of the infected brain

To observe brain lesions in infected rats, we performed the histopathological analysis of infected brains. Hematoxylin–eosin sections were scored in a semi-quantitative fashion across major neuroanatomical regions—cerebral cortex, hippocampus, thalamus, cerebellum, and brainstem—using an ordinal scale (0 = not detected, 1 = minimal, 2 = mild, 3 = moderate, 4 = severe). Each group contained n = 3 animals. In order to visualize the distribution of ordinal scores, we summarized six pre-specified endpoints using dot plots. The endpoints included cortex: microgliosis and perivascular cuffing; hippocampus: microgliosis and granule-cell necrosis; thalamus and cerebellum: neuronal cell-body necrosis. Each dot in the plots represents one animal, and the bars denote the median with IQR (Fig. 5a). Group differences were assessed by Kruskal–Wallis across the three groups followed by Dunn’s multiple-comparisons with Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction across all pre-specified endpoints and pairwise contrasts; where indicated, Cliff’s delta is reported as a non-parametric effect size (see Supplementary Data 5 for exact statistics). Relative to mock-infected rats, XPV-infected rats consistently exhibited higher inflammatory and degenerative readouts, whereas Wt-infected rats showed only minimal–mild changes. The individual per-animal scores underlying Fig. 5a are listed in Supplementary Data 6.

In the cerebral cortex, microgliosis and perivascular cuffing were the most discriminative readouts. Group means reached 2.0 (microgliosis) and 2.67 (perivascular cuffing) in XPV, versus 0.67 and 1.67 in Wt, and 0 in mock. Representative fields at identical magnification illustrate multi-layered perivascular cuffs in XPV (Fig. 5b), contrasting with mild perivascular changes in Wt. Neuronal cell-body necrosis in cortex remained low overall (means 0.33 in both XPV and Wt; 0 in mock). In the hippocampus, XPV showed microgliosis (1.0 vs 0.33 in Wt; 0 mock) and granule-cell necrosis (0.67 vs 0 in Wt/mock), while pyramidal-cell necrosis stayed low in both infected groups (0.67 in XPV and Wt). In the thalamus, neuronal cell-body necrosis was higher in XPV (1.0) than in Wt (0.33) and mock(0), with clear foci of eosinophilic, shrunken neurons showing nuclear pyknosis/karyorrhexis in XPV representative images (Fig. 5c). In the cerebellar nuclei, XPV displayed low-grade neuronal cell-body necrosis, whereas Wt and mock showed no detectable necrosis at the same magnification (Fig. 5d). By contrast, the brainstem showed minimal necrosis across groups (means 0 in XPV, 0.33 in Wt, 0 in mock). Together, these results indicate that XPV-infected rats exhibited stronger cortical and hippocampal inflammation (microgliosis and perivascular infiltration) and detectable neuronal injury in thalamus and cerebellar nuclei, while Wt causes only minimal–mild changes under our experimental conditions.

rBoDV-XPV induces an exaggerated immune response

Finally, to assess the innate immunity response triggered by rBoDV-1 XPV in vivo, we examined the relative mRNA levels of CXCL10, IL-6, IL-1β, MCP-1, CCL4, and TNF-α, which are cytokines known to be upregulated in BoDV-1-infected rats41. As shown in Fig. 6, enhanced expression of CXCL10 was observed in the brains of both Wt- and XPV-infected adult rats, whereas except for CCL4 and TNF-α, the upregulation of cytokine expression was observed only in XPV-infected rats. The data indicating that the expression of IL-6 and IL-1β was substantially increased in the brains of XPV-infected, but not Wt-infected, rats (Fig. 6) suggests that rBoDV-1 XPV is less capable than Wt in suppressing innate immunity and aggravates a lethal immune response in the brains of infected rats.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the functions of the VSBV-1 genes to elucidate the virulence of the emerging mammalian bornavirus. We found that VSBV-1 has a similar cell tropism to BoDV-1 and that the vRNP components of these two mammalian bornaviruses have the same essential functions for viral replication (Fig. 1). We also evaluated the compatibility of vRNP components between BoDV-1 and VSBV-1 using minigenome system and found that BoDV-1 polymerase activity was detected regardless of whether N or P was replaced with the corresponding gene from VSBV-1. In addition, it was shown that VSBV-1 X efficiently suppresses the activity of BoDV-1 polymerase. Since rBoDV-1 with the VSBV-1 X/P gene was successfully rescued and replicated in rat brains (Figs. 3 and 4), it is conceivable that the replication unit of VSBV-1 is genetically compatible with that of BoDV-1. On the other hand, interestingly, VSBV-1 P significantly enhanced BoDV-1 polymerase activity in the minigenome system. Since BoDV-1 P plays an essential role in viral replication, it is possible that VSBV-1 P may be responsible for the difference in virological characteristics between these two mammalian bornaviruses.

Bornaviruses undergo transcription and replication in the nucleus, and newly synthesized vRNPs in the nucleus are transported to the cytoplasm, where they assemble into viral particles just below the plasma membrane to produce progeny viruses35,36,38,42–45. It has been shown that BoDV-1 P localizes to the nucleus when expressed alone and is exported into the cytoplasm when co-expressed with X35,37. This may be the viral strategy to regulate the replication in the nucleus and promote virion assembly. In this study, we showed that VSBV-1 P predominantly localizes to the cytoplasm even when expressed alone (Fig. 2b), suggesting that the nuclear export activity of VSBV-1 P exceeds its nuclear localization activity, and that VSBV-1 has a distinct mechanism to control its replication independently of X expression. In addition, this property of VSBV-1 P suggests that VSBV-1 vRNPs have a greater tendency to translocate outside the nucleus than to remain inside the nucleus, indicating that VSBV-1 may have the propensity to produce more progeny viral particles than BoDV-1. Beyond the NLS/NES motifs, BoDV-1 and VSBV-1 P differ at multiple positions; therefore, the mixed nuclear/cytoplasmic distribution observed after motif swapping likely reflects contributions from residues outside these signals, and we refrain from attributing the localization phenotype solely to the three N-terminal NLS residues. P was visualized by anti-FLAG immunofluorescence of the FLAG epitope fused to P; the signal therefore reports FLAG-tagged P rather than endogenous P. As we did not benchmark alternative tags or untagged constructs, potential tag-dependent effects on subcellular distribution cannot be excluded, and our interpretation is accordingly tempered. To better understand the role of VSBV-1 P in virion assembly, it is also necessary to examine the cooperative function of P with other viral proteins.

Many studies demonstrated that the phosphoproteins of mononegaviruses play important roles in suppressing the innate immune response in infected cells46–51. Previous reports have indicated that BoDV-1 also has an immune suppressive mechanism through the viral proteins including N, P, and X27,52,53. Among these proteins, BoDV-1 P may play a pivotal role in innate immune suppression in the infection. Unterstab et al. showed that BoDV-1 P binds to Traf family member-associated NF-κB activator (TANK)-binding kinase 1 (TBK-1) to inhibit IFN-β expression in cells27. It has also been demonstrated that BoDV-1 P itself is phosphorylated by TBK-1 and functions as a viral decoy that inhibits the activation of TBK-1 target proteins. We demonstrated that no VSBV-1 proteins including the P inhibit IRF pathway-inducing activity. In this study, although we could not determine whether the lack of the inhibitory effect of P is dependent on its differential subcellular localization or interaction ability with TBK-1, this strongly suggested that the virulence of VSBV-1 is due to an enhanced immune response caused by the inability of the VSBV-1 genes to counteract the innate immune response. The tissue readouts and cytokine profiles converge on an immunopathology-driven mechanism. The cortical/hippocampal microgliosis and perivascular infiltration observed in XPV (Fig. 5a–c) coincide with brain upregulation of CXCL10, IL-6, and IL-1β, whereas Wt lacked IL-1β activation. Together with our finding that VSBV-1 P did not inhibit IRF-pathway–inducing activity, these data suggest that weakert innate immune antagonism, rather than fundamental differences in cell tropism, underlies the stronger inflammatory pathology in XPV-infected brains.

To understand whether the different properties of VSBV-1 P are responsible for the virulence of the virus, we generated rBoDV-1 with an X/P gene exchange with VSBV-1. We showed that rBoDV-1 XPV replicated slightly slower than the Wt virus in cultured cells, but was highly virulent in infected rats, with significantly higher titers compared to Wt virus infection in both neonatal and adult rat brains (Fig. 4). Studies of experimental infection with BoDV-1 and VSBV-1 have shown that neonatal immune tolerance contributes to low pathogenicity and controlled viral replication in infected rats14,18,54. These observations suggest that differences in immune responses due to accelerated viral proliferation and lack of innate immunity antagonistic activity are responsible for the virulence of rBoDV-1 XPV in rats. It is also possible that the increase in viral production due to the cytoplasmic-prone distribution of P leads to a strong immune response in infected animals.

In vivo, the histopathology clearly separated XPV from Wt. Semi-quantitative scoring across five neuroanatomical regions showed that XPV elicited robust cortical and hippocampal microgliosis with prominent perivascular cuffs, together with detectable neuronal cell-body necrosis in thalamus and, to a lesser extent, cerebellum, whereas Wt produced only minimal–mild changes under our conditions (Fig. 5a–f; Supplementary Data 4). These lesion patterns are consistent with the overall increase in disease severity observed in XPV-infected rats. These histopathological data also contextualize prior reports that adult rats infected with BoDV-1 can develop disease16,20,54. In our study, Wt showed low-grade or absent lesions, a difference that may reflect strain background, observation window, and presence of reporter gene. Indeed, comparison of our Wt construct with the GenBank reference He/80/FR (AJ311522.1) revealed six amino acid substitutions in the G and L genes (Supplementary Table S7). While these differences may have influenced the relatively mild pathology observed in Wt, they cannot account for the marked virulence of XPV, which differs from Wt only by the VSBV-1 X/P genes. By contrast, XPV reproducibly exhibited widespread inflammation and region-specific neuronal injury, aligning with its higher brain titers and the lack of innate immune antagonism. Overall, the pathology, cytokines, and protein functions together argue that XPV’s virulence seems to be primarily driven by enhanced host sensing and downstream inflammatory responses.

A strong immune response to VSBV-1 infection in the brain has been observed in both naturally and experimentally infected hosts. In previous pathologic analyses of humans infected with VSBV-1, inflammatory cells infiltrated sites positive for VSBV-1 RNA, antigens were associated with CD4+ and CD8 + T cells, and high microglial activity was observed13. Activated microglia release cytokines and chemokines46,47, which may have contributed to the exacerbation of symptoms. In our study, we observed elevated levels of cytokines and chemokines, particularly CXCL10, IL-6, and IL-1β, in rBoDV-1 XPV-infected adult rats (Fig. 6). Most interestingly, IL-1β activation did not occur in the brain of rBoDV-1 Wt-infected rats. IL-1β can activate Toll-like receptors, and the activation mechanism of cytokine stimulation via IL-1β promotes the overproduction of cytokines and exacerbates inflammatory pathologies. IL-1β also activates inflammatory responses by inducting the downstream targets, such as IL-6 and CXCL10, an immunostimulatory chemokine recruiting various immune cells to the site of infection. All these responses correlate with severe immunopathology in viral infections41,55–57. Consistent with our result in rats, a recent study also showed that VSBV-1-infected rhesus monkeys with severe clinical symptoms had a high viral titer in brain tissues and showed perivascular mononuclear cuffs dominated by lymphocytes and meningeal inflammatory cell infiltration in the brain19. Although the levels of cytokine expression associated with the induction of innate immune responses in these infected hosts were not clear, it is highly likely that the difference in innate immune responses between BoDV-1- and VSBV-1-infected brains is responsible for the high virulence of VSBV-1.

Our quantification emphasized infectivity-linked and tissue-level endpoints (infectious titers, histopathology). We did not perform absolute-copy-number RT-qPCR of viral genomes, which would quantify total viral RNA including non-infectious species; future work will integrate RNA-based measures with infectivity and pathology to refine burden estimates across regions and time points. In addition, subcellular localization analyses were restricted to P because the protein is directly implicated in innate immune antagonism. A systematic localization survey of all VSBV-1 proteins represents an important next step but lies beyond the scope of the present study.

In this study, we showed the VSBV-1 gene functions related to cell tropism, polymerase activity, and innate immune antagonism; many of which had been unknown. We also demonstrated that the X/P gene of VSBV-1 may be involved in the active proliferation and virulence of VSBV-1 and may be also important for the induction of cytokine expression in vivo. This study will contribute to not only improving the understanding of the virulence of this newly emerging bornavirus but also the research and development of therapeutics and vaccines against VSBV-1 and BoDV-1 in the future.

Methods

Biosafety statement

The experiments conducted with recombinant bornaviruses were performed solely for scientific research and within the Bio-Safety Level 3 (BSL-3) containment facilities at LiMe KU. The experimental protocols were carefully crafted to adhere to the rigorous biosafety regulations. It is important to note that these experiments were not intended for dual-use purposes, nor did they involve gain-of-function research aimed at increasing the pathogenicity or transmissibility of the viruses. The Minister of MEXT granted approval for these experiments (Approval No. 31−146), thus ensuring compliance with all relevant national biosafety standards.

Cells

Vero cells expressing a puromycin resistance gene, HEK293T cells, and wild-type Vero cells were cultured in low-glucose Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (Nacalai, Kyoto, Japan) containing 10% fetal calf serum (FCS) (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (PS) (Nacalai). OL and BHK cells were cultured in high-glucose DMEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific) containing 5% FCS (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 1% PS (Nacalai). HeLa, MDCK, and NIH3T3 cells were cultured in high-glucose DMEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% FCS (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 1% PS (Nacalai). THP-1 cells were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% FCS (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 1% PS (Nacalai). CHO-K1 cells were cultured in Ham’s F12 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% FCS (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 1% PS (Nacalai).

Plasmids

The N, X, P, M, G, and L gene sequences of VSBV-1 (accession number LN713681.1) reported in 2015 were artificially synthesized (FASMAC, Kanagawa, Japan) and inserted into pCAGGS. For the P expression plasmid used for an indirect immunofluorescence assay (IFA), a FLAG tag was added to the C-terminus, and the sequence was inserted into pcDNA3. The NLS and NES mutants of P were generated by PCR-based mutagenesis. The primer sequences used in this study will be disclosed upon request.

Viruses and sequence references

All experiments involving wild-type BoDV-1 used the He/80/FR strain (GenBank AJ311522.1). The VSBV-1 sequences used for cloning were derived from isolate “patient 3 brain” (GenBank LN713681.1). Where pseudotyped particles or recombinant BoDV-1 constructs were generated, the VSBV-1 inserts were taken from LN713681.1, and BoDV-1 sequences were from He/80/FR (AJ311522.1).

Antibodies

Primary antibodies used in this study were as follows: mouse monoclonal anti-FLAG M2 (Sigma-Aldrich, F1804; applications: IFA [1:5000], WB [1:10000]); mouse monoclonal anti-α-tubulin, clone B-5-1–2 (Sigma-Aldrich, T5168; WB [1:2000]) used as a loading control; rabbit polyclonal anti-X (in-house; applications: WB [1:100], and rabbit polyclonal anti-P (in-house; applications: WB [1:1000], IHC [1:1000]). Secondary antibodies were: goat anti-mouse IgG (H + L), Alexa Fluor 488, highly cross-adsorbed (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A-11029; IFA [1:1000]); Peroxidase AffiniPure donkey anti-mouse IgG (H + L) (Jackson ImmunoResearch, 715-035-150; WB [1:1000]); and Peroxidase AffiniPure donkey anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (Jackson ImmunoResearch, 711-035-152; WB [1:1000]).

Pseudotyped virus assay

The pseudotyped virus was generated as previously described32,33. Briefly, 293 T cells infected with rBoDVΔG-GFP were transiently transfected with BoDV-G or VSBV-G using TransIT-293 (TaKaRa, Shiga, Japan). After 48 h, the cells were frozen and then thawed, and the cell-free virus was recovered by centrifugation to remove cell debris. After allowing the collected virus to adsorb onto test cells for 1 h, the inoculum was washed off, and the cells were incubated for 3 days. The number of GFP-positive cells was counted 3 days after infection using an ECIPSE TE2000-U fluorescence microscope (Nikon). The generation of pseudotyped viruses was approved by the safety committee of recombinant DNA experiments of Kyoto University. All experiments using the pseudotyped viruses were performed in a biosafety level 3 (BSL-3) containment laboratory at Kyoto University, which has been approved for use by the Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries, Japan.

Minigenome assay

A BoDV-1 minigenome assay was performed as previously described28,48. 293T cells were transfected with N, P, and L expression plasmids and a minigenome with Gaussia luciferase as the reporter using TransIT-293 (TaKaRa). After 48 h, the supernatant was collected, and luciferase activity was measured using a BioLux Gaussia Luciferase Assay Kit (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) on a Lumat LB 9507 luminometer (BERTHOLD TECHNOLOGIES, Bad Wildbad, Germany). Tetrazolium salt (WST-1) (TaKaRa) was added to the cells to count viable cells to normalize luciferase activity.

IFN-β reporter assay

293T cells were transfected with a plasmid expressing N, X, P, M, G, and L of VSBV-1 and an IFN-β promoter plasmid (p-55 C1B-Luc)33 using Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After 24 h, the double-stranded RNA analog poly I:C (Sigma, Saint Louis, USA) was introduced using Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After another 24 h, the cells were washed, and firefly luciferase activity was measured using a luciferase assay system (Promega, Madison, USA) on a Lumat LB 9507 luminometer (BERTHOLD TECHNOLOGIES). WST-1 (TaKaRa) was added to the cells to count viable cells for normalizing luciferase activity. Each condition was assayed in duplicate (technical replicates) in three independent experiments (n = 3).

IFA

OLs were transfected with an expression plasmid encoding P or its mutants with a FLAG tag on the C-terminus using Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 48 h later, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde-phosphate buffer (PFA) (Wako). After membrane permeabilization with 0.1% Triton-X, the cells were subjected to blocking with phosphate-buffered saline containing 2% FCS and incubated with an anti-FLAG M2 monoclonal antibody (Merck) for 1 h at room temperature. After the primary antibody was washed off, an anti-mouse IgG antibody conjugated with Alexa 488 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added for staining. The secondary antibodies and DAPI were washed off, and then, the cells were observed using a C1 confocal laser microscope (Nikon).

Reverse genetics

We rescued rBoDV-1 using a modified method described in a previous report33. 293T cells were transfected with plasmids encoding N and L of BoDV-1 and P of VSBV-1 and the antigenome of rBoDV-GFP or rBoDV-XPV-GFP (Fig. 3a) using TransIT-293, and 4 days later, puromycin-resistant Vero cells were cocultured in the presence of puromycin. After ~35 days, when the percentage of GFP-positive cells was close to 100%, RNA, protein, and cell-free virus were collected from the cells using TRIzol reagent, sample buffer (Wako, Osaka, Japan), and sonication39, respectively. The generation of recombinant viruses was approved by the safety committee of recombinant DNA experiments of Kyoto University and the Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science and Technology in Japan. All experiments using rBoDV-XPV were performed in a BSL-3 containment laboratory at Kyoto University, which has been approved for use by the Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries, Japan.

RT‒PCR

RNA collected from Vero cells infected with a recombinant virus was used as a template for reverse transcription with SuperScript III (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with random hexamers, and PCR was performed using KOD-one (TOYOBO, Osaka, Japan) with X- and P-specific primer sets for BoDV-1 or VSBV-1. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Data 4. PCR products were run on a 1.5% agarose gel and imaged with a UVP BioDoc-It instrument (Analytik Jena, Jena, Germany).

Western blotting

Proteins from cells infected with a recombinant virus were denatured in sample buffer at 95 °C for 10 min and run on Mini-Protean TGX™ precast gels (BioRad, Richmond, USA). The gels were transferred to a Trans-Blot Turbo PVDF Transfer Pack (Bio-Rad). The membranes were blocked with Blocking One (Nacalai) and subjected to primary anti-P or anti-X polyclonal antibodies or an anti-alpha tubulin monoclonal antibody (Merck). The primary antibodies were washed off, and the membrane was treated with a secondary anti-rabbit or mouse IgG HRP-conjugated antibody. Chemiluminescence reactions were induced with Clarity Western ECL Substrate (BioRad). The bands were detected and imaged with a LAS 3000 (Fujifilm, Tokyo, Japan).

Titration

Cell-free virus collected from rBoDV-GFP- or rBoDV-XPV-GFP-infected Vero cells or virus recovered from homogenates of infected rat brains was titrated as follows: Vero cells seeded in 48-well plates were inoculated with a serial 10-fold dilution of virus and incubated for 3 days. The number of GFP-positive cells was counted 3 days later under an ECIPSE TE2000-U fluorescence microscope (Nikon), and the titer was calculated.

Growth kinetics

Cell-free viruses were inoculated into Vero cells at an MOI of 0.1 or 0.001, and the cells were passaged every 3 or 4 days, at which time the GFP-positive rate of PFA-fixed cells was measured using a Tali Image cytometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Animal experiments

Neonatal and 4-week-old female rats (LEW/SsN) were inoculated intracranially and intranasally, respectively, with 105 IU of virus (Fig. 4a). Every week, the infected rats were weighed, and the surviving rats were euthanized at 10 or 9 weeks. For all procedures, anesthesia was induced and maintained by inhalation of isoflurane at 2–3% in oxygen using a dedicated rodent anesthesia system (NARCOBIT-E, Natsume Seisakusho, Tokyo, Japan). Euthanasia at study endpoints was performed by overdose of isoflurane (≥5% in oxygen) delivered via the same system. The brains were removed from the dead rats, and homogenates were prepared using BioMasher II (Nippi, Tokyo, Japan). The homogenates were subjected to titration and RNA extraction using TRIzol (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The animal experiments were approved by the Kyoto University Experimental Animal Committee and conducted in a biosafety level 3 A containment laboratory, which has been approved by the Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry, and Fisheries.

Histopathology

At the experimental endpoints described in Fig. 4, brains were collected from mock-infected, rBoDV-1 XPV, and rBoDV-1 Wt rats (n = 3 per group). Whole brains were immersion-fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin (FUJIFILM Wako), processed routinely, dehydrated, and embedded in paraffin. Paraffin blocks were sectioned in the coronal plane, mounted on glass slides, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Five neuroanatomical regions were evaluated: cerebral cortex, hippocampus, thalamus, cerebellum, and brainstem. Semi-quantitative scoring was pre-specified for the following histopathological endpoints: neuronal cell-body necrosis, microgliosis, perivascular cuffing, gitter-cell infiltration (lipid-laden macrophages), and segmented-nucleus (neutrophil-like) cell infiltration. Each endpoint was scored on a 5-point ordinal scale (0 = not detected; 1 = minimal; 2 = mild; 3 = moderate; 4 = severe). All tissue processing, slide preparation, and pathological assessments were performed in a blinded manner at Kyoto Institute of Nutrition & Pathology, Inc. (Kyoto, Japan), with assessors masked to group allocation (mock, Wt, XPV).

Histopathology statistics

For each endpoint, group differences were first assessed by a Kruskal–Wallis test (three groups). When a global effect was detected, two-sided pairwise Mann–Whitney tests (XPV vs Wt, XPV vs mock, Wt vs mock) were performed and the false discovery rate across all pairwise tests (18 comparisons: 6 endpoints × 3 contrasts) was controlled by the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure (q < 0.05). Cliff’s delta is reported as a non-parametric effect size. Individual per-animal scores underlying panel A are listed in Supplementary Data 6; pairwise P values and FDR-adjusted q values are provided in Supplementary Data 5.

RT-qPCR

RNA extracted from infected rat brain homogenates was subjected to reverse transcription using random hexamers and SuperScript III (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and the resulting cDNA was used as a template for RT‒qPCR using specific primers for CXCL10, IL-6, IL-1β, MCP-1, CCL4, TNF-α, and GAPDH. Primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Data 3. RT‒qPCR was performed with THUNDERBIRD SYBR (TOYOBO) using Rotor gene Q (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). The relative expression levels of each mRNA transcript were evaluated by the ΔΔCt method.

Pairwise identity and clustered heatmap of G

Full-length G proteins were aligned globally in a reference-guided framework (each sequence aligned to a BoDV-1 reference G; aligned, non-gap positions used for all pairwise comparisons). Pairwise percent identity was computed as the fraction of identical residues over positions where neither sequence contained a gap. The resulting matrix (Supplementary Data 1) was converted to a distance matrix (% identity) and subjected to average-linkage hierarchical clustering (UPGMA). The heatmap displays the clustered matrix; the corresponding row/column order is given in Supplementary Data 2.

Sequence collection and motif-conservation analysis

Publicly available complete genome records for BoDV-1 and VSBV-1 were retrieved from GenBank in September 2025. From each record, the phosphoprotein (P) CDS translation was identified using the annotated gene (“P”) and/or protein fields and collected once per unique protein_id (BoDV-1, n = 105; VSBV-1, n = 24). Multiple-sequence alignments were generated (MAFFT, --auto) to verify residue indexing; however, motif calls were computed on ungapped sequences using 1-based positions. Motifs were defined a priori from the literature (Fig. 2b) as follows: BoDV-1 N-terminal NLS (aa 29–36, PRPRKIPR), BoDV-1 NES (aa 145–165, MKTMMETMKLMMEKVDLLYAS), BoDV-1 C-terminal NLS (aa 181–193, PPRIYPQLPSAPT); and the corresponding VSBV-1 strings (PRPKRIPQ, MKTMMETMRLMMEKIDLLYAS, QPRLYPTLPSAPT), noting that the third VSBV-1 motif begins at aa 182 (i.e., 182–194). For each sequence, we recorded exact-match identity to the expected string at the specified positions and summarized the fraction of sequences identical to each motif. Additionally, BoDV-1 sequences were scanned both at the homologous positions and across the full P length to confirm the absence of the VSBV-1 motif strings. All accessions and motif calls are listed in Supplementary Data 3.
