Forecasting the rheological properties of alkali-activated concrete utilizing gene expression programming
Authors: Hisham Alabduljabbar, Irfan Ullah, Muhammad Faisal Javed, Mohammed Jameel, Waseem Ullah Khan, Furqan Ahmad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Accurate prediction of fresh-state properties is essential for understanding the rheological behaviour of alkali-activated concrete (AAC), as these parameters directly influence mix uniformity and overall material performance. Gene expression programming (GEP) effectively captures complex relationships between mixture components and material properties, offering superior flexibility, interpretability, and adaptability compared to traditional regression and black-box models. It generates explicit mathematical equations suitable for engineering design while automatically selecting relevant features. Accordingly, this study employed gene expression programming (GEP) to develop a robust predictive model for the slump (SL), dynamic yield stress (DYS), static yield stress (SYS), and plastic viscosity (PV) of AAC, eliminating the need for time-consuming and resource-intensive physical experiments. The GEP model demonstrated excellent performance, achieving a coefficient of determination (R2) of 0.979 and a root mean square error (RMSE) of 1.183 for SL, and an R2 of 0.960 with an RMSE of 3.293 for SYS. Similarly, strong predictive accuracy was observed for DYS (R2 = 0.962, RMSE = 0.161) and PV (R2 = 0.962, RMSE = 5.266). The study also derived explicit equations using GEP for practical use. To enhance accessibility, a user-friendly graphical interface was developed, enabling instant predictions from input parameters. The GUI simplifies mix design, minimizes testing requirements, and speeds up decision-making, thereby promoting the adoption of AAC in construction and ensuring improved control over fresh-state properties.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-28892-9.

Introduction

Concrete is a composite material composed of cementitious paste that binds coarse and fine aggregates together, gradually hardening to form a solid mass1. Owing to its high strength, durability, versatility, and cost-effectiveness, it has become the most widely used construction material worldwide2,3. It is estimated that each person consumes approximately two cubic meters of concrete annually4. Ordinary Portland cement (OPC), the key binding ingredient, constitutes around 10–12% of the total concrete volume5. However, its production is highly energy-intensive, consuming nearly 4 gigajoules of energy and emitting about 0.85 tons of CO₂ per ton of cement produced6. With global OPC production exceeding 3 billion tons annually5, reducing its environmental footprint has become a major research priority.

To address this challenge, alkali-activated (AA) cementitious materials have emerged as sustainable alternatives to OPC7. Alkali activation involves the chemical reaction between aluminosilicate precursors and alkaline activators, forming a hardened binder. Common precursors include industrial by-products such as fly ash (FA), metakaolin (MK), ground granulated blast furnace slag (GGBFS), silica fume (SF), red mud (RM), and rice husk ash (RHA)8. These materials not only reduce CO₂ emissions but also utilize industrial waste, promoting circular economy practices. AA binders (AABs), often referred to as geopolymers, offer high strength, acid and fire resistance, and superior durability, while reducing the carbon footprint by up to 50–75% compared with OPC9–11. Furthermore, AABs can be locally sourced and tailored to match or exceed OPC performance, making them a viable eco-efficient alternative.

Despite their environmental advantages, the engineering application of AA concrete (AAC) requires a comprehensive understanding of its mechanical and rheological properties. Previous studies revealed that the mechanical performance of AAC depends strongly on precursor type, activator concentration, and curing temperature12–14. For instance, AA slag concrete typically shows high early-age strength and low slump loss, while FA-based systems often require heat curing to achieve sufficient strength. The elastic modulus, shrinkage, and workability of AACs vary with activator type and mix composition. Recent studies have further advanced understanding of microstructural changes, mechanical behaviour, and erosion processes15–17, while innovative use of industrial by-products like lithium slag has demonstrated improved durability and crack resistance in cement-based materials18. However, the absence of standardized mix design procedures and design codes continues to limit large-scale application19–21.

Experimental evaluation of AAC properties is time- and resource-intensive, motivating the need for predictive modeling approaches. Conventional empirical models struggle to represent the complex nonlinear interactions among mixture components and performance. Machine Learning (ML) techniques have shown great promise in this domain, providing accurate, data-driven predictions without requiring explicit physical formulations22. A random forest (RF) model was employed to forecast compressive strength (CS) and slump flow of AACs based on FA. These properties are crucial for compliance purposes and are predicted regarding the physicochemical characteristics, curing methods and mixing techniques of the concretes23. Five distinct RF models were developed, capable of accurately predicting both hardened and fresh characteristics, including slump, CS, static/dynamic yield stress, and flow resistance, for AAC21. ML model, informed by chemistry principles, was created to predict the CS of AAMs. This model relies on information regarding the blend ratios and chemical makeups of both the activators and precursors used in the AAMs24. A Gaussian process (GP) model was developed to forecast the CS of AAC using a well-structured dataset from existing literature, based on binder composition and concrete mix design25. Different ML models, including RF, Ridge regression, gradient boosting Gradient boosting (XGBoost), and LightGBM, were assessed to determine their efficacy in accurately estimating the CS of a one-part AAM binder26. A mathematical model utilizing the LightGBM was developed to forecast the workability of AAMs27. Four distinct ML techniques were devised to establish a reliable predictive model for the CS of ACCs derived from slag and FA28. XGBoost and RF methods were employed to forecast the CS of geopolymers derived from building waste in AA conditions29.

However, most existing studies emphasize mechanical performance, while predictive modeling of rheological parameters remains limited. These fresh-state properties are critical because they directly govern the rheological behavior of AAC mixtures, which in turn influence the quality and uniformity of the hardened material. Accurate prediction of slump (SL), dynamic yield stress (DYS), static yield stress (SYS), and plastic viscosity (PV) enables better control of mix design before casting, ensuring desired performance in both fresh and hardened states, and serving as an essential step before evaluating mechanical properties such as compressive, tensile, and flexural strength. This study employs gene expression programming (GEP) to develop a robust predictive model for forecasting the SL, DYS, SYS, and PV of AAC, thereby minimizing the need for time- and resource-intensive experimental testing. GEP provides an optimal balance of flexibility, interpretability, and adaptability among data-driven modelling techniques. Unlike traditional regression models that depend on predefined functional forms, GEP automatically develops relationships between variables, enabling it to capture the complex and nonlinear behavior of material properties30,31. Its framework allows customization of functions, constants, and fitness criteria, producing closed-form explicit equations that are practical for engineering design and even manual computation, which offers a distinct advantage over black-box models. Additionally, GEP has demonstrated superior performance in predicting various properties of concrete32–35. Furthermore, GEP performs automatic feature selection during its evolutionary process, effectively eliminating redundant or weakly correlated inputs without requiring extensive preprocessing. Overall, GEP integrates evolutionary flexibility, transparency, and practicality, making it superior to conventional regression approaches and a powerful alternative to complex ML models36. To enhance usability, a graphical user interface (GUI) was developed, enabling instant predictions from input parameters.

Overview of ML method utilized

Gene expression programming

GEP is a widely recognized evolutionary algorithm created to address user-specified problems through evolving computer programs. They evolve through natural operations such as mutation and crossover. GEP has been validated as an effective technique for developing obvious and accurate computer programs37. GEP utilizes linear chromosomes characterized by their structure consisting of genes that are arranged into a beginning and an end. These chromosomes are modified via methods such as transposition, mutation, gene transposition, root transposition, one- and two-point and gene recombination. They represent expression trees, which are the focus of selection. The separation of the expression tree and genome into different entities with specific functions enables the algorithm to perform with exceptional efficiency, significantly surpassing current adaptive methods38. GEP shares the same type of tree representation as genetic programming. This similarity allows GEP to both retrace the measures implemented by genetic programming and the exploration of novel horizons by crossing the phenotype threshold39. The reproduction approach, combined with the chromosome’s structural arrangement and its conversion into an expression tree, enables unrestricted genetic alterations, consistently resulting in reliable expression trees. Research methodology

Dataset overview

The database employed for analysis consisted of 145 experimental datapoints gathered from various sources in existing literature21. The predictor variables are curing time (TA), SiO2/Na2O (S/N), sodium hydroxide (NaOH), blast furnace slag ratio (BFSR), water (W), aggregate (Agg), and precursor content (PC). The target variables are SL, DYS, SYS, and PV of AAC. Figure 3 depicts the predictors and response variables. Missing values were addressed using the K-nearest neighbor (KNN) imputation method to ensure data completeness and reliability. Subsequently, all variables were normalized using the min–max scaling technique to eliminate scale-related bias and maintain uniform feature weighting40. Outliers were identified and removed using box plot analysis (Fig. 4) to enhance data integrity and model robustness. Model development

A minimum of three data points per input variable is recommended to avoid overfitting, with five or more preferred for robust models. The dataset used in this study has a ratio of 20.71 (145 data points to 7 inputs), which far exceeds the recommended minimum, indicating it is well-suited to minimize the risk of overfitting45. For model development, 70% of the dataset was used for training and 30% for testing. The success of ML models relies heavily on proper hyperparameter tuning to minimize errors and enhance accuracy. To achieve this, a grid search combined with tenfold cross-validation was used to identify the best hyperparameters for the models. This systematic approach ensured robust model performance through careful selection and optimization of parameter ranges. Figure 6 outlines the model development process, and Table 2 summarizes the final hyperparameters. Additionally, various statistical metrics and uncertainty measures were employed to assess model performance. A graphical user interface (GUI) was then developed based on the trained model and validated using the external dataset. This alignment demonstrates a strong agreement between the model and the dataset, suggesting a good fit49,50. The DYS parameter exhibits a regression slope of 0.949 and 0.959 in the training and testing phases, respectively. Lastly, the PV parameter shows an RS of 0.938 for the learning stage and 0.956 for the testing stage. To ensure model generalizability, an additional dataset from independent studies34,51–53 were employed for external validation. External validation using an independent dataset confirmed the robustness of the GEP models, with R2 values between 0.944 and 0.955, WI from 0.931 to 0.947, and KGE ranging 0.940 to 0.952. RMSE, MAE, and U95 values were consistent with training and testing results, demonstrating strong generalizability and reliable prediction of AAC rheological properties.

K-fold cross validation

This study employed a tenfold cross-validation approach, which involved splitting the dataset into ten equal parts54. During each iteration, 90% of the data was used for training the model, while the remaining 10% was reserved for testing (Fig. 10). Model performance was evaluated by averaging the results across all folds, helping to minimize overfitting, reduce sampling bias, and provide a robust assessment of model accuracy (Fig. 11). The mean R2 values for SL, SYS, DYS, and PV were 0.96, 0.95, 0.94, and 0.93, respectively, with standard deviations indicating stable and consistent performance. Similarly, the mean RMSE values of 0.91 for SL, 1.13 for SYS, 1.02 for DYS, and 1.62 for PV were accompanied by standard deviations within an acceptable range, reflecting minimal variability. Limitations and research directions for future

Other advanced programming techniques, such as the M5P tree, can be used to make further predictions. Optimization algorithms may be used to optimize the hyperparameters of ML models. Additional predictor variables, including the physical and chemical characteristics of ingredients, corrosion, temperature, and resistance to acid and chloride attacks, can also influence the properties of AAC and should be considered in future studies.

Conclusion

Accurate prediction of fresh-state properties is essential for understanding the rheological behaviour of AAC, as these parameters directly influence mix uniformity and overall material performance. Accordingly, this study employed GEP to predict the SL, SYS, DYS, and PV of AAC. The dataset used for analysis consisted of 145 experimental datapoints gathered from various sources in existing literature.
