Variability-Aware Detection and Repair of Compilation Errors Using Foundation Models in Configurable Systems
Rohit Gheyi 3 , Lucas Albuquerque 3 ,Márcio Ribeiro 2 ,Eduardo Almeida 6 ,Danyllo Albuquerque 4 , Mirko Perkusich 4

Abstract
Modern software systems often rely on conditional compilation to support optional features and multiple deployment scenarios. In configurable systems, compilation errors may arise only under specific combinations of features, remaining hidden during development and testing. Such variability-induced errors are difficult to detect in practice, as traditional compilers analyze only a single configuration at a time, while existing variability-aware tools typically require complex setup and incur high analysis costs. In this article, we present an empirical study on the use of foundation models to detect and fix compilation errors caused by feature variability in configurable C systems. We evaluate two models, namely GPT-OSS-20B and Gemini 3 Pro , and compare them with TypeChef , a state-of-the-art variability-aware parser. Our evaluation considers two complementary settings: 5,000 small configurable systems designed to systematically exercise variability-induced compilation behavior, comprising both systems with and without compilation errors, and 14 real-world GitHub commits, as well as an additional set of mutation testing scenarios (42). Our results show that foundation models can effectively identify variability-induced compilation errors. On small configurable systems, GPT-OSS-20B achieved a precision of 0.97, recall of 0.90, and accuracy of 0.94, substantially increasing detection coverage compared to TypeChef , and exhibiting performance comparable to Gemini 3 Pro . For compilation error repair, GPT-OSS-20B produced compilable fixes in over 70% of the cases. In the analysis of real commits, ChatGPT-5.2 detected all injected faults except for two cases and identified a potential real compilation bug in a Linux commit with more than 1,000 modified lines. Overall, our findings indicate that current state-of-the-art foundation models provide a practical and low-effort complement to traditional variability-aware analyses, offering accurate detection and human-readable fixes, while still facing challenges related to execution time, cost, and the limited context window when analyzing large configurable systems.

II
Motivating Example

Configurable systems are software systems that support multiple variants derived from a single shared code base. These variants are obtained by enabling or disabling configuration options, allowing developers to tailor software to different platforms, hardware constraints, and functional requirements. In the context of C systems, such variability is typically implemented through preprocessor directives such as
#ifdef
, which control the inclusion or exclusion of code fragments at compile time. Due to their simplicity and flexibility, these mechanisms are widely adopted in practice.

Throughout this paper, we adopt the terminology of Software Product Lines (SPL) to describe configurable C systems. Specifically, we treat preprocessor macros as features, each valid combination of macro assignments as a product (or configuration), and the overall configurable system as a software product line. This conceptual mapping is well established in the SPL literature and provides a standard abstraction for reasoning about variability in systems that rely on conditional compilation
[
21
]
.

However, this flexibility comes at a cost. Each configuration option introduces conditional code, and combinations of options may interact in subtle and non-obvious ways. As the number of configuration options grows, the number of possible variants increases exponentially, making it increasingly difficult for developers to fully understand, test, and maintain the system. In particular, errors may arise only under specific combinations of options, remaining hidden during standard development and testing
[
4
,
22
]
. Prior work has shown that the way variability is managed has a direct impact on code quality, maintainability, and system reliability
[
2
,
4
,
23
]
.

To illustrate this problem concretely, we present a small but representative example that shows how interactions among a few configuration options can lead to a compilation error affecting only a single variant of the system.

II-B

Traditional Compilers

Traditional compilers, such as
clang
, compile and analyze only one software configuration at a time. As a result, they can detect compilation errors only for the specific combination of macros that is currently enabled. If a problematic configuration is not explicitly built and tested, the corresponding error remains undetected.
In systems with many configuration options, such as the Linux kernel, which contains hundreds of configuration macros
[
26
]
, the number of possible variants quickly becomes too large to test exhaustively. Consequently, conditional compilation errors like the one shown in the example may persist for long periods of time and affect only rare or specialized variants of the system.

II-C

Variability-Aware Tools

To address the inherent limitations of traditional compilers in the presence of conditional compilation, variability-aware tools such as
TypeChef

[
10
]
and
SuperC

[
11
]
have been proposed. These tools analyze multiple configurations simultaneously by explicitly modeling conditional compilation constructs and their dependencies.
While such tools can detect errors that occur only under specific combinations of configuration options, they rely on sophisticated static analyses, require careful environment setup, and may struggle to scale to large real-world systems. Moreover, these analyses must be continuously updated to keep pace with the evolution of programming languages and compiler standards, which imposes additional maintenance effort on tool developers. In addition, variability-aware tools often fail to detect certain classes of compilation errors and typically provide limited explanations, making their results difficult for developers to interpret and act on.

II-D

Foundation Models

Recently, foundation models have emerged as a promising alternative for a wide range of software engineering tasks
[
15
,
16
]
. These models are trained on large corpora of code and text and exhibit strong capabilities in understanding code structure, reasoning about program behavior, and generating explanations and fixes. Unlike traditional analysis tools, they do not rely on explicitly defined rules for each configuration scenario.

This property makes foundation models particularly attractive for configurable systems, where the number of possible configurations is large and difficult to manage. Despite their rapid adoption, however, their applicability to compilation-related problems remains largely unexplored. A recent survey on LLM-enabled compilation explicitly identifies the lack of empirical studies evaluating whether such models can detect and repair compilation errors caused by configuration variability
[
17
]
.

RQ

To what extent can
GPT-OSS-20B
correctly detect the presence of compilation errors across configurations of configurable systems?

RQ
2

To what extent can
GPT-OSS-20B
correctly fix compilation errors in configurable systems, producing code that compiles successfully across affected configurations?

To answer these RQs, we compare the model outputs against the ground truth established by the language compiler for each configuration. For error detection, compiler diagnostics indicate whether a given configuration exhibits a compilation error. For error correction, the model produces a modified version of the configurable system, which must still define multiple configurations and from which all configurations are derived and compiled using the C compiler (
clang-1600.0.26.6
). A fix is considered correct if and only if all derived configurations compile successfully without any errors.

We rely on standard classification metrics to characterize different aspects of model effectiveness. Precision quantifies how often detected errors or generated fixes are correct, whereas recall captures the model’s ability to identify all compilation errors across configurations. Accuracy provides an overall measure of correctness, while the F
1
-score balances precision and recall.

Dataset

In this section, we give more details about our dataset.

III-B
1
Creation

Several prior studies have cataloged variability bugs in configurable systems
[

]
. However, these datasets are relatively small and limited in diversity. To mitigate this limitation, we started from 30 real bugs previously reported in the literature
[

]
and asked
ChatGPT-5.2
to generate 5,000 similar configurable systems, including both systems without compilation errors and systems that exhibit compilation errors in at least one configuration. The generation process was explicitly constrained to produce a balanced set of instances and was conducted in January 2026.

III-B
2
Baseline

The generated instances were organized into a CSV file containing 5,000 configurable systems. Each configuration derived from these systems was then compiled with
clang-1600.0.26.6
on macOS Tahoe 26.1 using the default parameters. This validation step revealed that 2,665 configurable systems contained at least one compilation error, while the remaining instances compiled successfully. Notably, 3.3% of the systems that were initially generated by
ChatGPT-5.2
as compiling correctly actually exhibited at least one compilation error in at least one configuration. This result highlights the importance of systematically validating synthetically generated data using automated tooling to establish a trustworthy experimental baseline.

III-B
3
Characteristics

The dataset comprises 5,000 configurable systems with a compact code size, ranging from 4 to 25 lines of code (LOC), with a median of 16 LOC and a mean of 14.7 LOC. Each system contains between 1 and 5 feature macros (median: 3) and may exhibit between 0 and 16 compilation errors across its configurations (median: 1, mean: 3.3), reflecting the combinatorial effects of conditional compilation.

Regarding error classification, the most frequent category consists of redeclaration and redefinition faults, including multiple redefinitions of the same symbol and redefinitions with incompatible types. Such errors typically arise when alternative declarations are conditionally introduced under different feature combinations. A second dominant category comprises type resolution and type consistency errors, such as
unknown type name
and
conflicting types
, which often result from missing type definitions, inconsistent prototypes across features, or conditional
typedef
and
struct
declarations that are not simultaneously visible.

Another frequent category involves missing declarations and name resolution failures, captured by diagnostics such as
use of undeclared identifier
. These errors commonly occur when a declaration is guarded by one macro while its corresponding use is guarded by another, leading to configuration-specific inconsistencies. In addition, the dataset includes classic syntax and grammar errors (e.g.,
expected expression
and
expected ’;’ after top-level declarator
), as well as C99-specific diagnostics where implicit typing is rejected (e.g.,
type specifier missing, defaults to ’int’
).

Overall, these error categories are representative of common C compiler diagnostics, particularly those related to name and type visibility, and are especially prevalent in configurable systems that rely on conditional compilation. While the dataset does not aim to exhaustively cover all compilation and build failures in arbitrary C projects (e.g., link-time errors or fine-grained type-conversion diagnostics), it provides broad coverage of variability-induced compilation errors.

The code fragments make use of preprocessor directives (e.g.,
#if
,
#ifdef
,
#ifndef
, and
#endif
) to conditionally include or exclude code based on feature selections, combined with fundamental C constructs such as
struct
definitions, global variable declarations, and occasional function declarations.

TASK

1) Consider the software product line induced by the feature macros used in the file. A “product” is any assignment of those macros to enabled/disabled (on/off).

First, when the C language standard was not explicitly specified, the models tended to generate code that implicitly assumed different C versions, leading to inconsistencies in compilation behavior. To mitigate this issue, we explicitly instructed the models to adhere to a specific ISO C99 standard in all experiments.

Moreover, when the expected form of the fix was not clearly constrained, the models frequently proposed solutions by adding or removing configuration macros with

For this reason, we explicitly constrained the prompts to require fixes that modify the code itself without introducing or removing macros, ensuring that the resulting configurable system remains meaningful and that all configurations are considered during evaluation.

Models

We selected

from OpenAI as a representative open-weight foundation model to enable fully reproducible experiments and fine-grained control over the execution environment.

III-E

Evaluation Procedure

After running
GPT-OSS-20B
, we analyze the results by comparing the model’s output against the baseline established using
clang-1600.0.26.6
. For the detection task, we assess whether the model correctly identifies the presence of compilation errors. A response is considered correct if the model reports at least one non-compiling configuration and the baseline confirms the existence of at least one compilation error in the corresponding configurable system.

For the fixing task, we consider only cases where the baseline indicates the presence of compilation errors and the model proposes a fix. In these cases, we evaluate the resulting configurable system by compiling all derived configurations using
clang-1600.0.26.6
on macOS Tahoe 26.1 using the default parameters. A fix is considered correct if and only if all configurations compile successfully without errors. To mitigate potential threats to validity, we complement this automated analysis with a manual inspection of a random sample of the results.

A closer inspection reveals that

GPT-OSS-20B

The most prevalent category consists of preprocessor macro redefinition errors, where conditional compilation leads to multiple definitions of the same macro with non-identical replacement lists. A third category comprises missing or inconsistent identifiers, in which feature-dependent code references symbols that are undefined, misspelled, or inconsistently named between their definitions and uses. Finally, some failures arise from malformed declarations introduced by preprocessing, including invalid identifiers produced by macro expansion.

The compilation errors observed in incorrect fixes to

V
Discussion

In this section, we discuss the results of our work.

V-A

Accuracy and Stability

Foundation models are inherently nondeterministic, meaning that repeated executions on the same input may yield different outputs. As a result, evaluating such models requires assessing their accuracy and understanding the stability and consistency of their predictions across multiple runs. This distinction is particularly important in the context of compilation analysis, where developers must trust both the correctness and the reliability of reported results.

Accordingly, this section evaluates the accuracy and stability of
GPT-OSS-20B
by analyzing its behavior across multiple executions and quantifying both correctness and consistency using complementary metrics.

V-A
2
Sampling Strategy

To limit computational cost while preserving statistical representativeness, we evaluated accuracy and stability on a stratified random sample drawn from the full dataset of 5,000 instances. The required sample size was computed using the standard proportion-based formula with finite population correction, assuming a 95% confidence level (
z=1.96
) and a conservative proportion
p=0.5
, which maximizes variance:

n_{0}=\frac{z^{2}\,p(1-p)}{e^{2}},\qquad n=\frac{n_{0}}{1+\frac{n_{0}-1}{N}}.

With a margin of error
e=0.05
, this yields a sample size of approximately
n=357
instances.

We then applied stratified sampling based on the binary compilation outcome in the original dataset (Compile: 2,335/5000 = 0.467; Does not Compile: 2,665/5000 = 0.533), selecting 167 compilable and 190 non-compilable instances via random sampling without replacement. This procedure preserves the original class distribution while enabling statistically grounded estimates at the chosen confidence level and error tolerance.

V-C

Execution Time

Analyzing 5,000 configurable systems is computationally demanding. The complete analysis required a total execution time of 55.5h for
GPT-OSS-20B
, covering both compilation error detection and fixing. Although the evaluated systems are relatively small, the average execution time per instance was 39.9s for
GPT-OSS-20B
and 31.3s for
Gemini 3 Pro
, respectively. Executing the evaluation with
Gemini 3 Pro
incurred an approximate cost of $4 USD in API usage, highlighting the monetary overhead associated with proprietary models.
These results indicate that the analysis remains time-consuming and potentially costly, underscoring the importance of considering execution time when applying foundation-model-based approaches at scale.

V-D

TypeChef

We evaluate
TypeChef
, one of the best-known variability-aware parsers, on the dataset described in Section
III-B
to assess its effectiveness in detecting compilation errors from the perspective of developers working with configurable systems. Since
TypeChef
focuses exclusively on error detection and does not provide mechanisms to automatically fix compilation errors, we restrict our evaluation to the detection task.
The experiments were conducted using
TypeChef
version 0.3.7 (JAR distribution available from its GitHub repository)
[
37
]
, executed with the OpenJDK Runtime Environment Temurin-21.0.7+6 on macOS Tahoe 26.1. All analyses were performed using the default configuration parameters of
TypeChef
on the 5,000 configurable systems.

In terms of detection performance,
TypeChef
achieved a precision of 0.54, a recall of 1.00, an accuracy of 0.59, and an F1-score of 0.70. The tool correctly identified 2,335 true positives and 638 true negatives, while producing 2,027 false positives and no false negatives. These results indicate that
TypeChef
is conservative, successfully identifying all configurable systems that contain compilation errors, but at the cost of a number of false positives. This behavior reflects a design trade-off that prioritizes completeness over precision, leading developers to inspect a large number of reported errors that do not manifest in practice.

GPT-OSS-20B
outperforms
TypeChef
in terms of overall detection performance on our dataset (see Section
IV-A
). In addition, the foundation models provide more informative, human-readable explanations of detected compilation errors and support interactive dialogue, enabling developers to further clarify and explore the reported issues. Beyond error detection, the foundation models also demonstrate strong effectiveness at fixing compilation errors, a capability not supported by
TypeChef
. Finally, foundation models are easier to use than
TypeChef
, as they do not require the complex setup and configuration process needed by variability-aware compilation tools.

Both approaches yielded correct results in 2,744 cases (54.9%). In addition,
TypeChef
was exclusively correct in 229 instances (4.58%), whereas
GPT-OSS-20B
alone correctly identified compilation errors in 1,962 instances (39.2%). Notably, there were 65 instances (1.3%) in which neither approach produced a correct result. For example, both

Beyond differences in aggregate performance metrics, we assessed whether the observed improvements of
GPT-OSS-20B
over

[
38
]
test confirms that the performance difference is highly significant (p < 10
-300
), indicating that it is not attributable to random variation. Moreover, the effect size analysis shows a substantial practical impact: the odds ratio of 8.57 (95% CI [7.47, 9.82]) indicates that, in cases where the two approaches disagree,
GPT-OSS-20B
is more than eight times more likely than

These results indicate that, although the two techniques frequently agree, each one can identify compilation errors missed by the other, and neither is sufficient on its own to cover all cases. Overall, the findings highlight a complementary relationship between

V-E

Real Projects

In this section, we extend our evaluation to larger and more realistic configurable systems by analyzing changes in real-world projects. Specifically, we propose and apply a change-based analysis that assesses whether foundation models can detect compilation errors introduced during software evolution, as reflected in Git commit diffs from widely used C projects.

V-E
1
Limitations

A straightforward approach would be to analyze the complete source code of such systems. However, foundation models currently face limitations in context window size
[
39
]
, which prevent them from processing entire code bases with hundreds or thousands of configuration options. This scalability challenge is not unique to foundation models; it also affects state-of-the-art tools such as traditional compilers and
TypeChef
and
SuperC
.
For example, compiling a single Linux kernel configuration with
clang-1600.0.26.6
can take several minutes. Given that Linux exposes thousands of configuration options, exhaustively compiling all possible products becomes prohibitively expensive, rendering such an approach impractical in practice
[
21
]
.

Despite several proposals in the literature, this challenge remains only partially addressed. The problem is inherently complex, as the number of possible configurations grows exponentially, making exhaustive analysis impractical in realistic systems. In this context, Braz et al.
[
40
,
41
]
proposed a file-level analysis that focuses on modified files and the macros affected by a given change. Although limited to analyzing a single file at a time, their approach was able to detect variability-related bugs in real-world systems. However, it relies on static analyses that must be continuously updated to keep pace with the evolution of programming languages and toolchains, and it may still produce false positives.

V-E
2
Diff-Based Prompting Strategy

Following a similar motivation
[
40
,
41
]
, we adopt a Git-diff-based analysis strategy. Instead of analyzing entire configurable systems, we focus on the code changes introduced in individual commits. We leverage Meta Prompting
[
29
]
to derive a prompt analogous to the one presented in Section
III-C
, replacing the full configurable-system code with the corresponding
diff
.

You are an expert C engineer specialized in configurable systems (C code with #if/#ifdef feature flags).

LANGUAGE STANDARD

Assume the code must be valid according to the ISO C99 standard.
Use C99 rules for syntax, declarations, and semantics when reasoning about compilation errors.

INPUT

You will receive a single unified diff of a commit. The diff may contain one or more C source files (and possibly other files). Treat the diff as plain text.

<diff>
diff
</diff>

TASK

1. Identify which C source files are modified by the diff (e.g., *.c, *.h). Ignore non-C files for compilation analysis.

2. For EACH modified C file, analyze the post-commit code paths that are visible in the diff:

* Use added lines (+) and context lines as the primary source of information.

* Removed lines (-) indicate code that no longer exists and should not be considered part of the post-commit version.

* If the diff does not provide enough context to fully reconstruct a file, analyze only what can be confidently inferred from the visible hunks.

3. For each analyzed C file, consider the software product line induced by the feature macros used in that file. A “product” is any assignment of those macros to enabled/disabled (on/off).

4. Determine whether there exists at least one product configuration for which the post-commit code does NOT compile under a standard C99 compiler (e.g., gcc or clang with -std=c99).

* Focus only on compilation errors that can be justified from the code and preprocessor conditions visible in the diff (e.g., missing struct/type definitions, inconsistent declarations, undeclared identifiers, incompatible conditional blocks, etc.).

5. If at least one product fails to compile:

* Identify the failing products as boolean assignments over macros (e.g., ENABLE_PAM=1,ENABLE_AUTH_MD5=0).

* List only products that fail. If there are many, list the minimal set of distinct failing products you can confidently justify.

6. Instead of providing full corrected source code, describe how to fix the compilation problem(s):

* Explain what must be changed in the code to ensure that ALL products compile under C99.

* You MUST NOT rely on external build flags, compiler options, or macro redefinitions (-D/-U).

* You MUST NOT introduce #define or #undef directives.

* You MUST NOT remove variability or delete features unless absolutely necessary.

* Prefer localized fixes, such as aligning declarations and uses under the same conditions, adding missing forward declarations, guarding uses with appropriate #if conditions, or restructuring conditional code.

* Preserve the intended behavior as much as possible.

OUTPUT FORMAT (MANDATORY — JSON ONLY)

Return a single valid JSON object with exactly the following keys:

"result": "<semicolon-separated list of failing products, or empty string if none>",

"explanation": "<short explanation of the compilation issue(s), referencing macros and files when relevant; or a brief statement that no failing products exist>",

"fix": "<clear, concrete description of how to fix the compilation issue(s) so that all products compile under C99; or empty string if no fix is needed or cannot be confidently proposed>"

CONSTRAINTS

* If no failing products exist, set "result" to an empty string ("").

* If you cannot confidently propose a fix, set "fix" to an empty string ("").

* Do NOT include markdown, comments outside the JSON, or any additional keys.

* The output must be strictly valid JSON and directly machine-readable.

V-E
3
Evaluation

We evaluate this change-based analysis approach by considering two commits from each of seven popular real-world C projects, each comprising thousands of lines of code and making extensive use of conditional compilation through
#ifdef
directives. The selected commits range from 13 to 1,197 lines of code in Git diff size, with a median of 53 lines, and span a diverse set of application domains, including a text editor (Vim), a plotting and visualization tool (Gnuplot), a language interpreter (Python), a collection of common UNIX utilities (BusyBox), a security and cryptography library (OpenSSL), an operating system kernel (Linux), and a standard C library (Glibc). All selected commits include at least one C preprocessing directive (e.g.,
#ifdef
). By analyzing commits from these heterogeneous systems, we aim to capture a wide range of variability patterns, coding practices, and configuration styles, thereby increasing the diversity of the evaluation and reducing the likelihood of project-specific bias.

V-E

Results

We performed an automated change-based analysis using
GPT-OSS-20B
following the same experimental setup described in Section
III

ChatGPT-5.2
, employing one temporary chat per query to mitigate threats to validity arising from conversational context retention, which could otherwise influence subsequent responses and bias the analysis. This manual analysis did not identify compilation errors in most of the analyzed commits, with one notable exception. All model outputs were independently reviewed by two authors of this article, who reached full agreement on the classification of the results.

In a Linux commit involving 1,197 added and removed lines,
ChatGPT-5.2
identified a potential compilation-related issue associated with conditional compilation. When
CONFIG_MODULES
is enabled, the modified code in
lib/alloc_tag.c
references the functions
reserve_module_tags
and
release_module_tags
via the

CONFIG_MODULES=1
, this change may result in unresolved symbol errors during the build or linking phase. In this case,
GPT-OSS-20B
produced an empty result and did not flag the issue, which may be attributable to context window limitations given the size and complexity of the commit diff.

For the remaining commits,
ChatGPT-5.2
did not report compilation errors. In all cases, the model provided concise justifications for the aspects of the changes analyzed. In some instances, it explicitly reported potential warnings, noting that the absence of the full code base constrained its ability to verify issues such as undeclared identifiers or name-resolution problems that require additional context.
In contrast,
GPT-OSS-20B
reported a compilation error in a Gnuplot commit involving 95 modified lines, indicating a missing closing brace. Based solely on the diff, this suggestion appeared plausible, as the changes were non-trivial and difficult to reason about locally. However, a subsequent manual inspection of the entire source file, comprising 2,122 lines of code, confirmed that no compilation errors were present. This example illustrates an important limitation of change-based analyses: when only partial context is available, local reasoning may lead to false positives, even when the reported issue appears reasonable in isolation.

V-E
5
Mutation-Based Evaluation

To further strengthen our evaluation, we apply mutation testing
[
19
,
20
]
, a technique that deliberately introduces faults into code in order to assess the effectiveness of analysis approaches. Rather than relying on test suites to expose these faults, we leverage our change-based analysis to evaluate whether
ChatGPT-5.2
and
GPT-OSS-20B
can detect the resulting compilation errors directly from commit diffs.

For each real-world commit included in the evaluation (Section
V-E4
), we generate three additional mutated commits, each containing a single injected fault applied directly to the Git diff. This strategy increases both the number and diversity of compilation errors while preserving the original context and structure of the change.

The mutations span a broad range of syntactic and semantic defects commonly observed in practice and in prior studies on variability-related faults
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
22
,
8
]
. They include incorrect or substituted keywords (e.g., replacing
if
with
for
, or using
elif
instead of
else if
), malformed control-flow constructs (such as ill-formed
if
statements or
#ifdef
directives, as well as missing or extra parentheses and braces), and invalid preprocessing directives. We also inject declaration-related faults, including invalid variable, parameter, pointer, array, and structure declarations, duplicate structure fields under nested conditional compilation, undeclared variables, and invalid numeric or string literals (e.g., unterminated strings). Finally, we introduce expression-level errors such as missing or extra commas and semicolons, missing arithmetic or assignment operators, invalid operators (e.g.,
++=
), incorrect structure member access (using
*
instead of
.
), and removed assignment operators.

In all but two mutated cases,
ChatGPT-5.2
successfully detected and explained the compilation errors and correctly identified the macros involved. Despite having access only to the commit diff and not the full code base, the model consistently provided high-level yet accurate explanations of the root causes and plausible fixes. One particular example involves a Linux commit with 1,197 modified lines, in which a mutation was introduced within a nested
#ifdef
. In this case,
ChatGPT-5.2
correctly detected that a
struct
declaration contained two fields with the same name and clearly explained why this would cause a compilation failure. However, the two cases missed by
ChatGPT-5.2
involved a missing semicolon in a BusyBox commit with 116 modified lines and an incorrect preprocessing directive in an OpenSSL commit with 62 modified lines.

Overall,
GPT-OSS-20B
achieved a lower accuracy (0.64) than
ChatGPT-5.2
(0.95) in the mutation-based evaluation. In several cases,
GPT-OSS-20B
failed to detect simple syntactic faults, such as missing semicolons. There were also instances in which the model produced no output, possibly due to the size of the modified code regions in the commit diff. Nevertheless,
GPT-OSS-20B
exhibited strengths in specific scenarios: for example, when a mutation introduced an invalid preprocessing directive (e.g.,
#els
),
GPT-OSS-20B
successfully identified the issue and proposed a correct fix. Whenever either model detected a compilation error, the corresponding explanations and suggested corrections were generally clear and easy to follow.

These results highlight a performance gap between larger proprietary models such as
ChatGPT-5.2
and smaller open-weight models such as
GPT-OSS-20B
. At the same time, they suggest a practical deployment strategy in which smaller, lower-cost models can be used as a first-line analysis, with larger models invoked selectively when confidence is low or when results disagree. While the mutations evaluated in this study do not cover the full spectrum of faults encountered in practice, they provide an evidence that foundation models can reason effectively about variability-induced compilation errors in realistic change scenarios. As future work, we plan to investigate more complex mutations involving larger changes, deeper feature interactions, and cross-file dependencies.

V-F

Implications

From a developer’s perspective, our results suggest that foundation models can support the detection and fixing of compilation errors arising from complex interactions among configuration options. By providing actionable feedback and human-readable explanations, these models can reduce the effort required to reason about rarely exercised configurations and help identify errors that would otherwise remain hidden. In practice, developers may adopt a cost-effective workflow in which open and less expensive models are used for routine analyses, resorting to proprietary models only in cases where additional confidence or more detailed explanations are required. Such a layered usage strategy helps balance cost, performance, and trust in real-world development settings.

The results also have implications for tool developers. The findings indicate opportunities to complement or augment existing variability-aware analysis tools with foundation models. Unlike traditional tools that rely on sophisticated static analysis and often require complex setup procedures, foundation models offer a more accessible, interactive interface. Their ability to explain detected issues and suggest fixes makes them particularly attractive for integration into development tools. At the same time, challenges remain when scaling these approaches to large code bases with hundreds of configuration options, especially given current limitations on context window size. Despite these challenges, our analysis based on Git commit diffs shows promising results, indicating that foundation models can still provide valuable assistance when reasoning about localized code changes.

For researchers, this study highlights the potential of foundation models as a complementary direction for analyzing configurable systems. While full-system analysis of large configurable code bases remains challenging, our results indicate that commit-level analyses can already be effective. Notably, the models identified a real compilation error introduced in an actual commit, demonstrating their practical relevance. These findings open avenues for future research on hybrid approaches that combine compiler-based validation, variability-aware analyses, and foundation models, as well as investigations into how advances in model architectures and context handling may further improve scalability.

It is also important to clarify the scope of the evidence provided by this study. Our results are based on small configurable systems and on analyses derived from Git commit diffs extracted from real-world projects. Although this scope does not yet encompass full-scale configurable systems with hundreds of configuration options, it demonstrates that foundation models can already provide meaningful support in realistic development scenarios, particularly when reasoning about localized code changes.

Another implication concerns the evolution and maintenance of tools. Foundation-model-based approaches require less setup effort and naturally evolve alongside programming languages, as their knowledge is updated through retraining or fine-tuning. In contrast, traditional variability-aware tools often depend on language-specific parsers and analyses that must be explicitly revised to accommodate new language features or standard updates. This difference suggests that foundation models may offer a more sustainable and adaptable solution for long-term tool support.

V-G

Threats to Validity

Several threats to the validity of our study must be considered
[
45
]
.
First, there is a potential selection bias in the analyzed code samples. If the evaluated instances do not sufficiently reflect the diversity and complexity of compilation errors encountered in practice, the results may overestimate the models’ effectiveness. To mitigate this risk, we constructed a synthetic dataset inspired by compilation error patterns reported in prior empirical studies on real-world configurable systems, and systematically generated additional examples designed to cover well-known classes of variability-induced errors. Furthermore, all generated instances were validated using a standard C compiler, ensuring that the experimental baseline reflects actual compilation behavior.

A second threat is data leakage during LLM evaluation. Because foundation models may have been trained on public code repositories, there is a risk that some of the evaluated instances were already known to the models. To reduce this threat, we deliberately generated the synthetic dataset using
ChatGPT-5.2
and conducted the main evaluation using a different proprietary model,
Gemini 3 Pro
, thereby minimizing potential biases associated with model self-evaluation. In addition, we introduced variations and synthesized new configurable systems and mutations following evaluation practices recommended in recent work on LLM assessment
[
45
]
. Moreover, for proprietary models, we avoided reusing conversational context across queries.

Third, our evaluation relies on compiler diagnostics as the ground truth for determining compilation correctness. While modern compilers such as
clang-1600.0.26.6
are widely trusted, compiler bugs or implementation-specific behaviors cannot be entirely excluded
[
46
]
. To increase confidence in the results, we manually inspected compiler outputs for a subset of cases and confirmed their correctness.

Finally, the scale of the analyzed configurable systems represents a limitation. The 5,000 systems contain up to 25 lines of code, whereas real-world configurable systems often span thousands of lines and involve complex feature interactions across multiple files. Smaller systems may simplify error detection and repair, potentially inflating observed performance. We partially address this threat through a complementary change-based evaluation on real-world Git commit diffs and mutation testing, which expose the models to larger and more realistic code changes. Nevertheless, future work is required to assess scalability to full-scale configurable systems with deeper variability and cross-file dependencies.

VII
Conclusions

In this article, we investigated the ability of foundation models to detect and fix compilation errors in configurable C systems. We introduced a synthesized dataset of 5,000 configurable systems and evaluated both open-weight and proprietary models in a variability-aware setting that requires correctness across product configurations. Our results show that
GPT-OSS-20B
is effective at identifying compilation errors, achieving strong precision, recall, and accuracy, and often outperforming a state-of-the-art variability-aware tool. Moreover,
GPT-OSS-20B
was able to generate correct fixes in over 70% of the cases, even when errors were induced by subtle interactions between conditional compilation and C language rules. We also demonstrated that change-based analysis using Git diffs can successfully identify real compilation errors in large, real-world projects, suggesting that
ChatGPT-5.2
can provide practical value despite current context window limitations.

Our findings indicate that foundation models represent a promising and complementary approach to traditional variability-aware analyses, offering low setup cost, adaptability to language evolution, and human-readable explanations. At the same time, several challenges remain before such approaches can be broadly adopted in practice. In particular, current limitations in context window size restrict the analysis of large configurable systems with hundreds or thousands of features, often requiring a focus on small systems or change-based scenarios. In addition, execution time and monetary cost remain non-trivial, especially when analyzing large datasets or performing exhaustive evaluations across configurations.

As future work, we plan to extend our evaluation to a broader set of real-world configurable systems, including larger codebases with richer language constructs, more complex control-flow patterns, and deeper nesting of conditional compilation. We also intend to investigate the impact of fine-tuning foundation models on domain-specific datasets for configurable systems, aiming to improve accuracy, consistency, and robustness in reasoning about variability-induced compilation errors. In addition, we aim to expand the scope of analysis beyond compilation failures to other classes of variability-related bugs, such as vulnerabilities

Finally, we plan to explore hybrid approaches that combine foundation models with compiler-based and variability-aware analyses, as well as their integration into AI-assisted development environments, to provide continuous and configuration-aware feedback throughout software evolution.
