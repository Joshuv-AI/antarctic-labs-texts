Resectional Roux-en-Y Gastric Bypass Versus Standard Roux-en-Y Gastric Bypass: Impact of Concomitant Gastric Remnant Resection on Postoperative Complications and Weight Loss Outcomes in a High-Volume Center
Authors: João Pedro Araújo Teixeira, Inês Faria Teixeira, Joana Lamego, Bárbara Peleteiro, Frederica Gonçalves, César Alvarez, Fernando Resende, André Costa Pinho, John Preto, Paula Freitas, Silvestre Carneiro, Hugo Santos Sousa, Eduardo Lima da Costa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeConcomitant gastric remnant resection (GRR) during Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB)—hereafter resectional RYGB (rRYGB)—may be required in selected cases, including premalignant conditions, persistent Helicobacter pylori infection, or revisional surgery, but is associated with increased morbidity. We evaluated the impact of rRYGB on surgical outcomes in a high-volume center.Materials and MethodsA retrospective analysis included patients undergoing primary and revisional RYGB, divided by whether a concomitant GRR was performed (rRYGB) or not (standard RYGB). Intra- and postoperative complications, reintervention, mortality and weight loss were assessed.ResultsOf 2,103 patients, 89 (4.2%) underwent rRYGB. The overall complication rate was higher with rRYGB, without reaching significance (14.6% vs. 8.9%; P=0.067), but was significant in primary RYGB (16.9% vs. 8.7%; P=0.023). rRYGB was associated with higher rates of leak/fistula (2.2% vs. 0.2%; P=0.001), food intolerance (2.2% vs. 0.3%; P=0.003), intra-abdominal collections (2.2% vs. 0.2%; P<0.001), and reintervention (7.9% vs. 3.1%; P=0.013). After adjustment, rRYGB remained independently associated with complications (odds ratio [OR], 2.30, 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.16–4.54) and reintervention (OR, 3.23, 95% CI, 1.31–7.96) in primary RYGB. Ninety-day mortality was zero. Weight loss tended to be greater with rRYGB (2-year total weight loss [TWL], 38.9% vs. 36.0%; excess weight loss, 99.7% vs. 92.0%), reaching significance for TWL in some adjusted analyses.ConclusionrRYGB is associated with increased postoperative morbidity, particularly fistula, food intolerance, and intra-abdominal collections. The trend toward greater weight loss was inconsistent across measures and time points and should be regarded as exploratory. Careful patient selection and individualized decision-making are essential.

INTRODUCTION

Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB) remains one of the most widely performed bariatric procedures for the treatment of severe obesity, owing to its durable effects on weight loss and metabolic improvement. A fundamental anatomical feature of RYGB is the creation of a small gastric pouch and an excluded gastric remnant, which typically comprises the fundus, antrum, and most of the gastric body. While excluded from alimentary continuity, the gastric remnant remains physiologically active, producing acid and pepsinogen, and is susceptible to a variety of complications [12]. These range from acute events, such as bleeding, necrosis, and perforation, to late complications including gastrogastric fistula, refractory peptic ulcer disease, dilatation, and, rarely, malignancy. The diagnostic and therapeutic management of remnant-related complications is challenging, given its inaccessibility to conventional upper endoscopy [34567].

Gastric remnant resection (GRR) has emerged as a surgical option in this context, performed either concomitantly with RYGB—an approach hereafter referred to as resectional RYGB (rRYGB)—or as an isolated secondary remnant gastrectomy. Indications for GRR at the time of primary RYGB include premalignant conditions, persistent Helicobacter pylori infection, incidental neoplastic findings, and preventive strategies in regions with a high prevalence of gastric cancer [345]. Revisional or secondary GRR is usually reserved for intractable complications such as bile reflux, refractory marginal ulcers, gastro-gastric fistulas, massive or recurrent bleeding, and remnant malignancy [89]. Despite its therapeutic rationale, GRR is technically demanding and has been associated with increased perioperative morbidity, including hemorrhage, fistula formation, bowel obstruction, ischemia, food intolerance, and duodenal stump leak, as well as long-term nutritional deficiencies due to the loss of acid and intrinsic factor–secreting cells [1289]. Furthermore, GRR may significantly influence the decision-making process in revisional bariatric surgery, either by constraining the available surgical options for the management of recurrent weight gain or by limiting therapeutic alternatives for the treatment of procedure-specific complications, such as postbariatric hypoglycemia.

The impact of GRR on postoperative outcomes remains a subject of ongoing debate. Some reports suggest that weight loss outcomes are comparable to those achieved with standard RYGB or sleeve gastrectomy, while others emphasize its role in resolving complications and facilitating renewed weight reduction in patients with insufficient prior results. However, robust data addressing the balance between benefits and risks of GRR in contemporary bariatric practice are scarce.

Although remnant-related complications and the rationale for GRR have been described, the available evidence is largely limited to case reports, small single-center series, and a recent systematic review [10], with few studies directly comparing perioperative outcomes between RYGB with and without concomitant remnant resection in a contemporary, high-volume setting. In this study, we present the experience of a high-volume bariatric surgery center with rRYGB, evaluating its impact on postoperative complications and weight loss outcomes. By analyzing a large patient cohort, we aim to provide further insight into the indications, safety profile, and clinical consequences of this challenging surgical strategy.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

We performed a retrospective observational study conducted at a high-volume center. All consecutive adult patients who underwent either primary or revisional RYGB between 2019–2023 were eligible for inclusion. Patients were divided into 2 groups according to whether a concomitant GRR was performed (rRYGB) or not (standard RYGB). This study was approved by the Comissão de Ética e do Centro de Epidemiologia Hospitalar of São João Local Health Unit (ULS), Porto, Portugal (approval number CE46/2021), and due to its retrospective design, the requirement for informed consent was waived. All procedures performed in this study involving human participants were in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. Patient confidentiality was strictly maintained, and no identifiable personal data were disclosed. This study did not involve animal subjects.

1. Study population

Demographic and clinical data, surgical details, and follow-up information were retrieved from a prospectively maintained institutional database. Recorded baseline variables included sex, age, body mass index (BMI), obesity-related comorbidities (type 2 diabetes and antidiabetic therapy, arterial hypertension, dyslipidemia and obstructive sleep apnea with non-invasive ventilation use), foregut pathology (gastroesophageal reflux disease and hiatal hernia), H. pylori status and eradication outcome, left hepatic lobe diameter, smoking status, super-obesity (BMI >50 kg/m2) and, for revisional cases, the type of primary procedure. Patients were categorized as having undergone primary RYGB or revisional RYGB, and further stratified according to the performance of concomitant GRR (rRYGB vs. standard RYGB).

2. Outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was the comparison of surgical complications between patients undergoing rRYGB and those undergoing standard RYGB. Complications were evaluated both intraoperatively (including bleeding, iatrogenic injury, or technical mishaps) and postoperatively, within 90 days after surgery. Postoperative complications were further classified by type: hemorrhage, anastomotic leak or dehiscence, intra-abdominal abscess or collection, food intolerance, complicated hernia, thromboembolic events, and surgical site infection. Complications were graded according to the Clavien–Dindo classification. The overall complication burden was additionally summarized using the Comprehensive Complication Index (CCI). Reintervention rate, defined as the need for any unplanned surgical, endoscopic, or radiologic procedure related to the index surgery, and 90-day mortality were also assessed. The secondary outcome was weight loss, measured by total weight loss (TWL, %) and excess weight loss (EWL, %), calculated at 1 and 2 years of follow-up. Remission of obesity-related comorbidities (type 2 diabetes, arterial hypertension and dyslipidemia) was assessed at 1 and 2 years according to standardized definitions [11] and calculated only among patients presenting the respective comorbidity at baseline; obstructive sleep apnea was not analyzed owing to insufficient numbers.

3. Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were expressed as absolute numbers and percentages and compared using the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation or median with range, depending on distribution, and compared using Student’s t-test or Mann-Whitney U test. A P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. To account for baseline differences, the association between rRYGB and the composite outcomes of total postoperative complications and reintervention was assessed using multivariable logistic regression, adjusting for sex, age, BMI, diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, obstructive sleep apnea and type of surgery; left hepatic lobe diameter was not included because missing values would have halved the analytic sample. In a sensitivity analysis, the models for total postoperative complications and reintervention were additionally adjusted for gastroesophageal reflux disease and hiatal hernia. Weight loss was analyzed by multivariable linear regression and comorbidity remission by multivariable logistic regression, restricted to patients with each condition at baseline. Individual complications were compared descriptively only, owing to their low frequency. As all consecutive eligible patients were included, the sample size was fixed by the available cohort; minimum detectable differences (α=0.05, 80% power) were therefore computed for the principal outcomes in order to characterize the statistical precision of the estimates. All analyses were performed with Stata® (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA).

RESULTS

A total of 2,103 patients underwent RYGB during the study period, of whom 89 (4.2%) underwent rRYGB—65 (73.0%) in the primary and 24 (27.0%) in the revisional setting. In the primary setting, failed H. pylori eradication—a recognized indication for remnant resection—was documented in 8 patients (12.3%). The remaining primary indications (premalignant lesions, incidental neoplastic findings and prophylaxis) and the revisional indications (bile reflux, refractory marginal ulcer, gastro-gastric fistula, recurrent or massive bleeding, and remnant malignancy) were recorded in narrative form in the operative and clinical notes and could not be retrieved as a structured, per-case variable from the institutional database; a complete per-indication breakdown stratified by primary vs. revisional setting is therefore not available, and this is acknowledged as a limitation. 1. Baseline characteristics

Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Patients undergoing rRYGB were more frequently female (96.6% vs. 85.9%; P=0.004) and had a slightly lower mean BMI (40.5 vs. 41.8 kg/m2; P=0.013). They also showed a markedly higher prevalence of foregut pathology, including gastroesophageal reflux disease (51.1% vs. 28.6%; P<0.001) and hiatal hernia (38.6% vs. 20.7%; P<0.001). Among patients with prior H. pylori infection, failed eradication was more frequent in the rRYGB group (30.8% vs. 0.8%; P<0.001). The prevalence of type 2 diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, obstructive sleep apnea, smoking and super-obesity did not differ significantly between groups.

2. Postoperative complications

The overall postoperative complication rate was numerically higher in the rRYGB group compared with standard RYGB (14.6% vs. 8.9%), although the difference did not reach statistical significance (P=0.067). In patients undergoing primary RYGB, the complication rate remained higher in those with remnant resection (16.9% vs. 8.7%) and was statistically significant (P=0.023).

When individual complications were analyzed, rRYGB was associated with significantly higher rates of anastomotic/staple line fistula (2.2% vs. 0.2%; P=0.001), food intolerance (2.2% vs. 0.3%; P=0.003), and intra-abdominal collection or abscess (2.2% vs. 0.2%; P<0.001). In total, 15 complications were recorded in the rRYGB group. Most events (13 cases) occurred in the postoperative period and were managed conservatively or with minimally invasive approaches. Two patients developed anastomotic or staple-line fistula—one requiring surgical management with defect closure and reinforcement of the staple line; the other one presented with a pancreatic fistula associated with a duodenal stump abscess, which was successfully treated with antibiotics and conservative measures. Hemorrhagic complications included upper gastrointestinal bleeding and melena, both managed non-operatively, as well as an abdominal wall hematoma treated conservatively. Hernia-related complications were also observed, including umbilical and incisional hernias, all addressed electively after the initial postoperative period. Additional complications included a gastrojejunal anastomotic stricture treated endoscopically and one case of dumping syndrome. No complication required emergent reoperation with major morbidity, and all cases were managed successfully according to their clinical severity. Two intraoperative complications occurred—one consisted of an extraluminal bleeding resulting in a retroperitoneal hematoma, which was successfully managed conservatively with local compression; the other one was an inadvertent jejunal injury during adhesiolysis and was immediately recognized and repaired with an absorbable suture reinforcement. No significant differences were observed for hemorrhage, marginal ulcer, internal hernia, thromboembolic events, surgical site infection, or complicated incisional hernia.

The reintervention rate was also higher in patients undergoing rRYGB (7.9% vs. 3.1%; P=0.013), with an even greater difference in the subgroup of primary bypass procedures (9.2% vs. 3.0%; P=0.005). Indications for reintervention included duodenal stump fistula, staple line leak, abdominal wall drainage due to necrotizing fasciitis, and abdominal hematoma requiring drainage. No deaths were recorded in the rRYGB group, and 90-day mortality was similar between groups. According to the Clavien–Dindo classification, most complications were grade I–II in both groups.

The CCI reflected this higher morbidity: although the median was 0 in both groups, the distribution was shifted towards higher values in the rRYGB group (0 [0–8.7] vs. 0 [0–0]; P=0.002). After multivariable adjustment for baseline differences, rRYGB remained independently associated with total postoperative complications (adjusted odds ratio [OR], 1.85, 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.00–3.44 overall; 2.30, 95% CI, 1.16–4.54 in primary RYGB) and with reintervention (adjusted OR, 2.53, 95% CI, 1.10–5.84 overall; 3.23, 95% CI, 1.31–7.96 in primary RYGB) (Table 4). These associations were robust in a sensitivity analysis additionally adjusting for gastroesophageal reflux disease and hiatal hernia: the excess of complications remained significant in primary RYGB (OR, 2.34, 95% CI, 1.17–4.67), although attenuated and no longer significant in the overall cohort (OR, 1.74, 95% CI, 0.91–3.32), while the association with reintervention persisted overall (OR, 2.55, 95% CI, 1.09–5.94) and in primary RYGB (OR, 3.15, 95% CI, 1.26–7.87) (Table 4).

3. Primary RYGB: weight loss and comorbidity remission

To isolate the bariatric outcomes, the analysis was restricted to the primary setting, comparing standard RYGB with rRYGB. Weight loss was consistently greater with resection, and after multivariable adjustment this difference reached statistical significance for 2-year TWL in the overall cohort (adjusted mean difference +2.86%, 95% CI, 0.36–5.37) and for 2-year EWL in the primary setting (+7.40%, 95% CI, 0.10–14.70), while remaining non-significant in the corresponding analyses. Among patients with each comorbidity at baseline, remission of diabetes, hypertension and dyslipidemia did not differ significantly between groups in adjusted analyses (all 95% CIs including 1; Table 4).

4. Weight loss outcomes

At 1 year (n=1,522) and 2 years (n=965) of follow-up, both TWL and EWL were comparable between groups. Nonetheless, a consistent, though not statistically significant, trend was observed in favor of rRYGB, particularly in primary RYGB patients. At 2 years, mean TWL was 38.9% in the rRYGB group vs. 36.0% in the non-resection group (P=0.052), and mean EWL was 99.7% vs. 92.0% (P=0.057), respectively. The minimum detectable difference for these comparisons (e.g., 4.2% for 2-year TWL and 11.4% for 2-year EWL in the primary setting) exceeded the observed differences, indicating that the unadjusted comparisons were underpowered; these weight loss findings should therefore be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

DISCUSSION

This study, conducted within a high-volume bariatric surgery center, aimed to assess the impact of concomitant GRR—rRYGB—on postoperative complications and weight loss outcomes. rRYGB is generally reserved for specific indications, such as the presence of pre-neoplastic lesions, a family history of gastric cancer, persistent H. pylori infection, conditions requiring endoscopic surveillance, or in revisional settings to treat bile reflux, refractory marginal ulcer, or gastro-gastric fistula [1012]. An important observation from our cohort is that patients selected for rRYGB differed systematically from those undergoing standard RYGB, with a markedly higher prevalence of gastroesophageal reflux disease and hiatal hernia and, among previously infected patients, of failed H. pylori eradication. These differences are consistent with confounding by indication, remnant resection being preferentially undertaken in patients with foregut pathology or unresolved infection—features reflecting a more complex operative field. Reassuringly, the association between rRYGB and postoperative morbidity persisted after multivariable adjustment and remained significant in the primary RYGB subgroup even when the models were additionally adjusted for gastroesophageal reflux disease and hiatal hernia (adjusted OR, 2.34 for complications and 3.15 for reintervention). In the overall cohort, the association with complications was attenuated once foregut pathology was accounted for—consistent with part of the excess reflecting the more complex anatomy that motivates resection—whereas the association with reintervention remained robust, indicating that the excess morbidity is not merely explained by baseline imbalance.

GRR is a technically complex procedure known to be associated with increased morbidity [7891013]. Our results confirm increased risk, demonstrating that the overall postoperative complication rate trended higher in the resection group compared to standard RYGB (14.6% vs. 8.9%; P=0.067). Crucially, this difference achieved statistical significance within the subgroup of patients undergoing primary RYGB with concomitant GRR (16.9% vs. 8.7%; P=0.023). In contrast, no statistically significant differences were observed in the revisional subgroup, which may be explained by the smaller sample size and limited statistical power for this comparison. The increased complexity associated with resecting the gastric remnant, which involves extensive dissection and creation of a closed duodenal stump, translates into a higher risk for specific surgical morbidities [7814]. We found that rRYGB was associated with significantly higher rates of: anastomotic or staple line leak/fistula (2.2% vs. 0.2%; P=0.001)—this rate was particularly high in primary bypass patients with resection (3.1% vs. 0.2%; P<0.001); intra-abdominal collections/abscesses (2.2% vs. 0.2%; P<0.001); food intolerance (2.2% vs. 0.3%; P=0.003). These findings are discussed in relation to potential underlying mechanisms and supported by the literature.

The higher rates of fistula/leak are consistent with the known technical challenges and risks of GRR, including potential duodenal stump leak or issues at the remnant staple line closure [12]. Remnant gastrectomy does not necessarily increase the overall length of staple lines compared with standard RYGB. In fact, standard RYGB includes staple lines from both the gastric pouch and the excluded remnant, whereas in RYGB with remnant resection the remnant staple lines are removed with the specimen, leaving primarily the pouch staple line and the duodenal stump closure. Therefore, the increased risk of leak cannot be attributed simply to a greater staple line burden. Rather, the added complexity of remnant resection lies in the duodenal dissection, which is anatomically demanding due to its close relationship with the pancreas, biliary structures, and major vascular elements—particularly in obese patients, where exposure is limited. This technical challenge may contribute to a higher risk of complications, including fistula formation. In our cohort we found 2 cases of leak. In one case, there was a pancreatic fistula which was successfully managed conservatively (an abdominal drain had been left intraoperatively and guided the fistula). In another case, a leak from the gastric pouch staple line required surgical revision, including closure of the defect using a barbed, slowly absorbable suture with a lesser omental patch, as well as reinforcement of the staple line from the angle of His with a continuous slowly absorbable suture. Intraoperatively, incarceration of a jejunal loop at the remnant resection trocar site was identified, which may have contributed to upstream obstruction and subsequent fistula formation. The systematic review supports that fistula formation and intra-abdominal abscesses are recognized complications of RYGB [8].

Food intolerance occurred in 2 of our patients and were related to dumping syndrome and stricture of the gastro-jejunal anastomosis. The elevation in food intolerance rates (2.2% vs. 0.3%), which is an outcome also reported in the literature, may reflect the more extensive tissue manipulation required during GRR compared to standard partitioning [681013]. Also, removal of the remnant alters gastric physiology. The removal of the remnant eliminates its buffering and regulatory role in gastric physiology, which may accentuate the rapid delivery of nutrients to the small intestine already characteristic of RYGB. This accelerated transit has been associated with symptoms such as early satiety, nausea, and dumping-related intolerance. Additionally, disruption of normal gastric and duodenal coordination may further impair digestive adaptation. Prior studies have shown that alterations in gastric reservoir function and motility after bariatric procedures contribute significantly to postprandial intolerance and gastrointestinal symptoms, particularly when gastric capacity is further reduced or anatomical continuity is modified [113]. The single case of gastrojejunal anastomotic stricture observed in our cohort was successfully managed with consecutive endoscopic dilations. From a technical perspective, we routinely calibrate the gastrojejunal anastomosis using a 36 Fr orogastric tube, regardless of whether remnant resection is performed. Moreover, this complication is also encountered in standard RYGB without GRR, and therefore we were unable to identify a specific mechanism directly attributable to remnant resection.

We found 2 cases of intra-abdominal collections/infections. Both were successfully managed conservatively with endovenous antibiotics and no need of percutaneous or surgical drainage. We believe that the additional dissection required to mobilize and resect the remnant increases operative time and tissue trauma raises the likelihood of contamination and abscess formation. Furthermore, staple line leaks and duodenal stump leaks can lead to peritonitis and intra-abdominal abscesses (which was the case in one of our patients), especially when the remnant is resected in a hostile or inflamed field.

Despite the increased morbidity, the safety of the procedure appears robust when performed in a high-volume setting. Ninety-day mortality was zero in the rRYGB group and did not differ significantly from the non-resection group (0.0% vs. 0.1%; P=0.833). Furthermore, most complications in both groups were classified as Clavien–Dindo grade I–II (Table 2). This favorable mortality outcome, despite increased technical complications, may reflect the expertise and timely management available at a high-volume surgical center.

Reintervention rates provide a direct measure of surgical complexity and necessity for subsequent operative management. Our data shows that the need for reintervention was significantly higher in patients undergoing rRYGB (7.9% vs. 3.1%; P=0.013). This disparity was even more pronounced in the primary bypass subgroup (9.2% vs. 3.0%; P=0.005). This finding aligns with literature noting the increased need for surgical reintervention following GRR [89]. The specific complications identified in our study, particularly staple line leaks and intra-abdominal collections, often necessitate operative or interventional drainage, contributing directly to the elevated reintervention rate.

The literature on rRYGB suggests that it yields weight loss outcomes comparable to standard RYGB or Sleeve Gastrectomy (SG) [1015]. Our findings support the idea that rRYGB does not compromise metabolic outcomes. At 1 and 2 years of follow-up, both TWL and EWL were comparable between groups (Table 3). Interestingly, our sample showed a consistent, though not statistically significant, trend favoring resection in primary RYGB patients. At 2 years, mean TWL was 38.9% in the rRYGB group versus 36.0% in the non-resection group (P=0.052), and mean EWL was 99.7% vs. 92.0% (P=0.057). After multivariable adjustment, the difference reached statistical significance in some analyses (notably 2-year TWL), but the findings were not consistent across weight-loss measures or time points. They should therefore be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than as evidence of a weight-loss benefit of remnant resection, and warrant prospective evaluation. The mechanism of weight loss after RYGB and rRYGB is governed by the size of the gastric pouch, length of the limbs, hormones’ alteration (with increased incretins) and, of course, patient adherence.

Remnant resection may contribute to weight loss primarily by eliminating pathological pathways such as gastrogastric fistula, which compromise restriction, and by preventing the remnant from functioning as an accessory reservoir. Beyond these mechanical considerations, emerging evidence [16] suggests that the excluded stomach remains metabolically active after RYGB. Proteomic studies indicate that the remnant continues to secrete bioactive factors capable of influencing incretin signaling and energy homeostasis, and experimental data have shown that refeeding the remnant can partially reverse the metabolic benefits of RYGB. These findings support the hypothesis that the remnant may act as a source of counter-regulatory signals to weight loss. In this context, its resection could theoretically enhance metabolic outcomes by eliminating this potential “counter-signal.” Nevertheless, the clinical relevance of this mechanism remains uncertain, and its contribution is likely secondary to the well-established intestinal and neurohormonal pathways underlying weight loss after RYGB.

Furthermore, the potential for enhanced weight loss after rRYGB is rooted in other neuroendocrine changes. In standard RYGB, weight loss is mainly due to hormonal changes, although also enhanced by the restrictive mechanisms of the small gastric pouch and limb lengths. The physical removal of the gastric remnant (which contains the majority of the stomach’s oxyntic mucosa) eliminates a significant population of ghrelin-secreting cells. Ghrelin, a potent orexigenic hormone, plays a central role in hunger signaling and energy homeostasis; its permanent reduction through resection may lead to more pronounced and durable satiety compared to the simple exclusion of the remnant [12]. These endocrine effects, combined with the elimination of any potential for future remnant enlargement (i.e., gastrogastric fistula), likely contributes to the consistent trend of improved TWL and EWL observed in our primary RYGB cohort.

When restricted to patients with each comorbidity at baseline, remission of diabetes, hypertension and dyslipidemia was comparable between resectional and standard RYGB after adjustment, indicating that concomitant remnant resection does not compromise the metabolic efficacy of the procedure.

rRYGB achieves its primary clinical aims: removing potential sources of future pathology, particularly cancer risk associated with pre-neoplastic lesions like intestinal metaplasia, and definitively treating complex revisional problems like refractory gastro-gastric fistula, marginal ulcer, or bleeding. The systematic review confirms that the primary reason for performing GRR is often the risk of remnant stomach neoplasia [10]. Our findings confirm that, while achieving comparable (and possibly trending higher) weight loss outcomes, surgeons must be aware that concomitant remnant resection, particularly at the time of primary RYGB, carries a significantly higher incidence of specific complications—namely anastomotic fistula, intra-abdominal collections, and reintervention.

These findings highlight the importance of careful patient selection and individualized surgical decision-making. The absence of mortality in the rRYGB group emphasizes that this procedure, when indicated, can be performed safely by experienced teams, though with an expectation of increased perioperative complexity and risk of leakage requiring intervention, thus requiring more careful monitoring.

The oncological rationale for remnant resection depends heavily on the background risk of gastric cancer, which varies markedly across populations. Eastern Asia carries the highest global burden of the disease, accounting in 2021 for approximately 60.8% of incident cases and 55.2% of deaths worldwide, with age-standardized incidence rates in the region roughly twice those reported for Europe. Age-standardized incidence and mortality have declined substantially over the past 3 decades—most steeply in South Korea and Japan, largely through organized endoscopic screening programs and H. pylori eradication—yet absolute case numbers remain high, and countries such as Mongolia continue to bear a disproportionate burden. High seroprevalence of H. pylori, a predominance of cagA-positive strains, high-sodium dietary patterns and tobacco use all contribute to this excess risk [1718].

These epidemiological considerations bear directly on bypass anatomy, because the excluded remnant is not accessible to conventional upper endoscopy; the population-level benefit of the surveillance programs that have driven mortality reduction in East Asia therefore cannot be realized in patients after standard RYGB, and remnant tumors tend to be detected at an advanced stage [3456]. In high-incidence settings, the threshold for concomitant remnant resection—or, alternatively, for selecting a procedure that preserves endoscopic access to the whole stomach—may reasonably be lower than in low-incidence Western populations. Our data suggest that such a decision should be made explicitly, weighing the anticipated oncological gain against the higher rates of leak, intra-abdominal collection and reintervention documented here, and that it is best taken at a high-volume center.

This study has both strengths and limitations. To our knowledge, it represents one of the largest single-center cohorts to date comparing resectional and standard RYGB, and one of the few to characterize the complication profile, reintervention burden and weight loss trajectory stratified by primary vs. revisional setting. Several limitations should nonetheless be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design and the marked difference in group sizes introduce a risk of confounding by indication; although this was addressed through multivariable adjustment, including a sensitivity analysis accounting for foregut pathology (gastroesophageal reflux disease and hiatal hernia), residual confounding cannot be excluded. Second, the number of resection cases was limited—particularly in the revisional subgroup (n=24)—and individual complications were infrequent; the corresponding estimates are therefore imprecise, and subgroup and rare-event comparisons, as well as the unadjusted weight loss analyses, were underpowered and should be interpreted as exploratory rather than as evidence of absence of an effect. The statistically significant differences observed for overall complications, staple-line leak, intra-abdominal collections and reintervention remain interpretable. Third, the specific per-case indication for remnant resection could not be retrieved as a structured variable, so a complete per-indication breakdown by primary versus revisional setting is unavailable; failed H. pylori eradication was assumed to represent a valid indication where present, but was not confirmed as the operative indication by chart review. Fourth, several potentially relevant variables were unavailable, including systemic immunosuppression, anticoagulant therapy, prior abdominal surgery other than cholecystectomy, the interval between primary and revisional surgery, and pre-operative anti-obesity medication. Finally, longer-term nutritional and oncological follow-up beyond the 2-year window presented here will be required, particularly for the remnant-related outcomes that motivate resection.

CONCLUSION

rRYGB—RYGB with concomitant GRR—is associated with a higher incidence of postoperative complications, including anastomotic fistula, food intolerance, intra-abdominal collections, and the need for reintervention. A trend toward greater weight loss was also observed, reaching significance in some adjusted analyses; because this finding was not consistent across weight-loss measures or time points, it remains exploratory. These results underscore the importance of careful patient selection and individualized surgical decision-making when considering remnant resection. Although increased morbidity was observed, the absence of mortality in this cohort likely reflects the safety of the procedure when performed in a high-volume center with substantial expertise. Further prospective studies are warranted to better define the risk–benefit profile of this approach.
