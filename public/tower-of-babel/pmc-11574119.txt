Psychometric appraisal of the Kurdish version of the Revised Hearing Handicap Inventory-Screening
Authors: Sherko Saeed F Zmnako
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Revised Hearing Handicap Inventory-Screening (RHHI-S) is a robust 10-item Patient-Reported Outcome Measure (PROM) that can quantify impacts of hearing loss (HL). Employing a cross-sectional study, the psychometric properties of a cross-culturally adapted Kurdish version of RHHI-S (RHHI-S-CK) were investigated. According to the degrees of HL, 168 enrollees (64.3% male; mean-age 63.1 ± 12.6 years) were divided into three subgroups; that is, mild (n = 56; 67.9% male; mean-age 60 ± 12.5 years), moderate (n = 64; 60.9% male; mean-age 64.6 ± 12.5), & moderately-severe (n = 48; 64.6% male; mean-age 63.1 ± 12.6). The internal cohesion among the items was confirmed by Cronbach’s alpha (0.94). Confirmatory factor analysis recognised structural validity when a strong unidimensional factor was extracted. Convergent validity was verified (P-value < 0.001) via Spearman’s correlation between the scale and a subjective correlator (visual analogue scale). A large effect size (0.88) from Kruskal-Wallis & Dunn-Bonferroni post-hoc tests established the discrimination between different degrees of HL. Lastly, intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) proved the external reliability of the adapted version of the tool (Test-retest ICC = 0.95). RHHI-S-CK is a robust PROM that can measure the impacts of HL in adults across all age ranges. Introduction

Hearing loss (HL) emerges as a considerable global concern for public health, and its implications are higher than before1. Recent analyses showed links between HL and reductions of cognitive ability2,3. Hearing loss is a frequent occupational hazard in developing and developed countries4. Accordingly, early diagnosis is crucial; however, because of the nature of the disease and the absence of obvious signs, the disorder is underdiagnosed. Conventional pure-tone audiometry is the standard criteria to diagnose HL. Nevertheless, the test cannot measure various consequences and impacts that arise because of the disorder, namely functional, social, and emotional impacts5,6. One of the credible approaches to measuring these impacts is the validated questionnaire, self-administered Patient-Reported Outcome Measure (PROM), which has captivated global attention in diverse clinical and academic domains7.

The hearing handicap inventory for the elderly (HHIE) is one of the popular validated tools developed in 1982 by Ventry and Weinstein to examine the social and emotional influences of HL on older adults. It is composed of 25 items with two subscales, demonstrating the emotional (13 items) and social (12 items) outcomes of the hearing impairment8. Subsequently, in 1990, the inventory was changed to the Hearing Handicap Inventory for Adults (HHIA) to quantify the same constructs in younger patients, those below the age of 65 years6,9. The psychometric properties of both HHIE and HHIA have been meticulously assessed5,10–12; they have been cross-culturally validated in many other languages13–16.

It is anticipated that a longer questionnaire with a higher number of items is associated with better reliability and validity; however, a questionnaire with many items may affect the interest of the respondents and eventually the accuracy of the data; consequently, the literature advocates an effortless and quick process of rating by the respondents17,18. Recently, the psychometric properties of both instruments (HHIE and HHIA) have been reevaluated by Cassarly et al. Ultimately, they developed an updated robust inventory, that is, the Revised Hearing Handicap Inventory—Screening (RHHI-S). The updated tool contains only one factor with ten items and can be utilised to measure handicaps resulting from hearing impairment in all adult groups19.

To the best of the author’s knowledge, RHHI-S was not culturally validated in Kurdish; appropriately, the present study translated RHHI-S into the Central Kurdish dialect (RHHI-S-CK) and confirmed the maintenance of its reliability and validity.

Content, and face validity

The linguistic aspect of the translated tool was subjected to appraisal through a pilot test conducted on 14 educated patients belonging to the target language. Moreover, the focus group assessed and rated each translated item and confirmed its content and face validity. Eventually, the final version of the RHHI-S-CK (Supplementary file 1) was produced and reported to the scientific and ethical committees of the college.

Setting, sample size, randomisation, and recruitment

Three tertiary audio-vestibular clinics in Sulaymaniyah Governorate were utilised to recruit participants. Based on the ratio between participants and the number of variables in the tool; namely, ten subjects for each item23, the study presumed that 168 patients would be a sufficient sample to validate the translated version of the instrument. Appropriately, from November 2023 to June 2024, employing systematic random sampling, previously diagnosed as well as new patients were invited and enrolled.

Subgroups

To acquire a convenient and exemplary sample, cases with unilateral, severe, and profound HLs were excluded. Consequently, the subgroups were classified based on the degrees of HL; that is, mild (Subgroup 1), moderate (Subgroup 2), and moderately-severe (Subgroup 3) HLs were the components of the sample.

Grading of the items

Although RHHI-S-CK is a self-grading instrument, in other words, the grading does not need assistance from any interviewer; however, most of the participants were from an older age group with a lesser interest in reading and grading many items; additionally, in this locality, some participants were illiterate; consequently, the author transformed the self-grading to interviewer-grading, for that reason, two well-trained female interviewers were recruited.

Correlator

Besides the rating of the 10-item scale, each patient was requested to rate the perceived global impacts resulting from the HL on a visual analogue scale (PGI-VAS) from zero to 100, referring to no to maximum impacts, respectively.

Design

A single baseline outcome measure (cross-sectional design) was sufficient to appraise most aspects of the validity of the translated tool; however, to examine the external consistency, two repeated measures were mandatory; therefore, for the reliability group (73 patients), the author transformed the design to a short-longitudinal.

Measurement errors

Most of the participants were old; therefore, to provide a suitable setting, interviews were conducted in a properly calm room. To avoid recall bias in the reliability subgroup, the sequence of the items on the second occasion was changed.

Statistics of psychometric appraisal

Univariate & multivariate distributions

Cases with missing values were excluded. All variables were free of floor and ceiling effects. Data screening of both the scale and the comparator revealed univariate normal distributions; nonetheless, the Mardia test showed multivariate asymmetry between the ten items (kurtosis statistics > 3)24. Moreover, the maximum value of Mahalanobis distance was greater than the corresponding critical Chi-Square value25. Consequently, both parametric and non-parametric tests were computed to examine the psychometric properties. Furthermore, the Shapiro–Wilk test and Median-based Levene’s tests, respectively, verified the non-normality and non-homogeneity of the variances of the total score and the scores of the subgroups as well.

Structural validity

Statistical analysts argued about suitable methods for factor extraction in asymmetric data, some strongly opted for a parametric approach, specifically in small sample31; on the other hand, others empowered diagonally weighted least squares (DWLS)24. Hence, regardless of the priori hypotheses that the tool is unidimensional19, the internal relationship and dimensionality of the developed Kurdish version were examined via exploratory factor analysis (EFA) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) using extraction methods maximum likelihood (ML) and Polychoric correlations (DWLS), respectively. Thereafter, the derived values related to the fitting of the model—that is comparative fit index (CFI) and root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) were compared with cutoff values of ≥ 0.95 and < 0.08, respectively32.

Convergent validity

To demonstrate the convergent validity, the total value of the perceived global impacts through a visual analogue scale (PGI-VAS-T) and the total value of the 10-item (R-HHI-S-CK-T) were correlated via Pearson’s and Spearman’s correlations33.

Discriminating validity (external discrimination)

The tool is supposed to measure the impacts of HL; therefore, the author hypothesised that the tool could discriminate between degrees of HL. Accordingly, to fulfil this speculation and to confirm discriminating validity, the author utilised Kruskal-Wallis and Dunn-Bonferroni post-hoc tests to compare the sum of ranks within subgroups and between subgroups, respectively.

External reliability

The analysis has assessed the consistency between two short-interval (3–5 days) repeated outcome measures through the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Values above 0.75 and 0.9 suggest good and excellent external reliability, respectively34,35.

Structural validity

Both the EFA via ML and CFA via DWLS have extracted one factor (Fig. 2). The eigenvalues in two analyses were 6.36 and 7.43, while the AVE were 0.46 and 0.57, respectively. The factor in ML has adequately loaded all items (from 0.63 to 0.86), whereas in DWLS the loading was more productive (from 0.71 to 0.95) (Table 2).

The calculated composite reliabilities (rhoC) (Supplementary file 2) were 0.92 and 0.94 for ML and DWLS, respectively. These finding, as well as the model fit indices, that is, CFI = 0.998 and RMSEA = 0.063 (Supplementary file 3) have confirmed the priori hypothesis that the scale is sturdy and one-dimensional19.

Convergent validity

Two tests (Pearson and Spearman) have investigated the correlations between two scales: the total of R-HHI-S-CK (R-HHI-S-CK-T) and the PGI-VAS-T. Both tests have significantly confirmed (P < 0.001) the positive correlations (0.85) between the two scales (Table 2); hence, establishing convergent validity.

Discriminating validity

The mean ranks of the RHHI-S-CK of the Subgroup-1, 2, &3 were 28.5, 105.4, and 122, respectively; as a result, the Kruskal-Wallis test verified a statistically significant difference within subgroups’ scores, X2(2) = 115.18, P < 0.001. Additionally, the Dunn-Bonferroni post-hoc test made pairwise subgroup comparisons, which also demonstrated significant differences (P-values < 0.001) between the scores of patients with mild HL (Subgroup 1) and scores of patients with moderate and moderately-severe HLs (subgroups 2 & 3). Nevertheless, there were no significant differences (P < 0.072) between the scores of subgroups 2 and 3, that is, patients with moderate and patients with moderately-severe HLs (Table 3; Fig. 3).

Discussion

The present study produced a Kurdish version of a robust unidimensional PROM in the field of HL, that is, RHHI-S-CK. The methods endorse that the process of adaptation strictly followed international standards. A specialised focus group with previous experience has supervised the process and conducted a pilot test to verify the content and face validity of the Kurdish version.

The exclusion and inclusion criteria made the sample as representative as possible; that is, the study has excluded suspected cases with possible confounding variables that might distort the genuine association between hearing loss and its impacts, for example, cases with associated bothersome tinnitus, discharging ears, and all cases with conductive and mixed hearing losses.

About general demographic outcomes, Table 1 showed homogenous samples across the subgroups; yet a departure from normality and heterogeneity in the total score and the scores of the subgroups were witnessed. That is why a careful statistical road map was planned.

While the author searched in Google Scholar and PubMed, except for the original version that was developed by Cassarly et al., we could not find previous comprehensive psychometric assessments for the present 10-item tool. Besides, they developed and appraised the tool via a non-classical approach; namely, the Mokken scale analysis37 which is substantially different from the classical appraisal of the ongoing study.

Polychoric correlation is recommended to investigate structural and discriminant validity specifically when the data are asymmetrically distributed38. Consequently, besides parametric exploratory factor analysis (ML) which extracted one factor (Table 2), the author has also utilised DWLS to reveal the internal attributes of the developed Kurdish tool. The confirmatory factor analysis through Polychoric correlation has also demonstrated and extracted a single robust factor with the efficient ability to load all 10 items. This hidden variable excellently loaded three items: 3, 4, and 5 with value of 0.92, 0.90, and 0.95, respectively. Additionally, the loadings of the last four items: 7, 8, 9, and 10 were outstanding: 0.85, 0,89, 0.88, and 0.88, respectively; for the remaining three items: 1, 2, and 6 the loadings were good: 0.71, 0.73, and 0.77, respectively. Similarly, the internal consistency reliability of the tool was also examined through relevant parameters; that is, α, AIID, CI-TC, AIC, rhoC, and AVE and all these parameters have obtained satisfactory levels (Table 2). Consequently, results from aforementioned classical approaches have replicated the robust unidimensional characteristic of the original version19; appropriately, verified the internal coherency, and the structural validity of the Kurdish version.

Pearson’s and Spearman’s correlations between the two subjective tools (RHHI-S-CK-T & PGI-VAS-T) that can measure the same construct (impacts of HL) have significantly (P-values < 0.001) approved the positive correlations (Table 2); subsequently, they confirmed the convergent validity of the Kurdish tool.

The ability of the Kurdish version in screening and discrimination between participants with normal hearing and patients with hearing loss were also performed through recruitment of a homogeneous additional sample with normal hearing. However, during the process of rating, we have noticed that most of the normal hearing respondent cannot interrelate their responses with all the ten questions of the tool which start with “Does your hearing problem cause you to …?”; while they do not have any hearing problem. Accordingly, to avoid floor effects, measurement errors, and to assemble an alternative interrelated sample, the author included cases with mild hearing loss for pairwise comparison. Appropriately, Table 3 demonstrated the ability of the developed version in discriminating various degrees of hearing losses which manifested the significant competency of the tool in distinguishing patients with mild HL from patients with moderate (P-value < 0.001, Effect Size (ES) = 0 > 0.65) and moderately-severe (P-value < 0.001, ES = 0 > 0.88) hearing losses; as a result, this propensity of discrimination between different levels of hearing loss certainly verify the ability of the tool to discriminate between patients with hearing loss and people with normal hearing. Although the tools’ capability to differentiate between patients with moderate and moderately-severe HL was not significant, there was mean difference between the scores of the two subgroups, 27.78 and 31.04, respectively. Similarly, the agreement ability of the original RHHI-S has also been inspected by Dillard et al., who reported a justifiable agreement between the score of RHHI-S and audiometric findings39.

Lastly, the external reliability of the Kurdish version was also appraised and affirmed through the ICC (Table 4). The scores of two short-interval occasions were correlated and proved consistency between the two measures. There were absolute agreements between the two scores for intra-interviewer-1, intra-interviewer-2, inter-interviewer, and test-retest correlations.

Strengths and limitations

During the process of grading and from our previous experiences, we observed that questionnaires with a smaller number of items are much easier, more realistic, and have fewer measurement errors compared to those with higher numbers, which was linked an occasional lack of interest from the participants40. Nonetheless, summoning and enrolment of previously recorded cases archived in our medical database required substantial repeated efforts from the interviewers to schedule the meetings. Finally, requesting the participants from districts in the reliability group to stay and attend on two occasions was challenging; accordingly, we were obliged to reduce the time interval from at least one week to 3–5 days.

Conclusion

The study has produced RHHI-S-CK, and its reliability and validity have been thoroughly investigated and confirmed. The ten items in the instrument are closely related and influenced by one construct; that is, the impacts of HL. RHHI-S-CK is a feasible PROM that can measure the impacts of HL in adults across all age ranges. The instrument augmented the clinical and academic practice of the Kurdish medical community.
