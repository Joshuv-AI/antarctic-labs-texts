Assessing the quality of care in low- and middle-income countries: a rapid review of the landscape of assessment tools
Authors: Nukunté David Lionel Togbenon, Sarah Cooper, Armelle Akouavi Vigan, Sorel Edgard Lokossou, Rémi Danhoundo, Nihad Jessica Eyitayo Agoligan Tometin, Mahugnon Christian Urlyss Agossou, Rosette Sègbévi Liebe Dossou, Jean-Paul Dossou, Bart Criel, Kéfilath Bello
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDespite its broad conceptual scope, the Donabedian model (input–process–outcome) remains one of the most widely used frameworks for evaluating quality of care globally. Numerous tools have been developed to operationalise this model, yet no synthesis has focused specifically on those applied in low-resource settings or examined their validity and applicability in real-world settings.ObjectiveThis study sought to review the landscape of tools used to assess each component of quality of care—according to Donabedian’s model—in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), and to evaluate their reported validity and applicability in routine health system settings.MethodA rapid review was conducted from June to November 2023. The following databases were searched using English and/or French keywords with no time limitation: Google Scholar and PubMed.ResultsA total of 1451 articles were retrieved from PubMed (n=1164) and Google Scholar (n=287), with an additional 74 articles identified through Google. After title, abstract and full-text screening, 85 studies met the inclusion criteria. The review identified a wide range of tools used to assess quality of care in LMICs, characterised by considerable variation in the aspects of quality of care covered, the types of tools and the assessment techniques applied. These tools were categorised as institutional—such as Services Availability and Readiness Assessment, Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) and Patient Satisfaction with Nursing Care Quality Questionnaire—or investigator-developed, often tailored to particular contexts. While most tools focused on input and process components, outcome measures—especially objective health outcomes—were under-represented. Gaps were also noted in the reporting of tool validation and cost-effectiveness, with few tools integrated into routine health system practices.ConclusionsThis review highlights the need for context-appropriate, validated and cost-effective tools for assessing quality of care in LMICs. Tools should be user-friendly and embedded within routine monitoring systems to better support continuous quality improvement.

Introduction

Over the past two decades, global health efforts have increasingly emphasised not only access to healthcare services but also the quality of those services.1 While early health system strengthening policies in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) often prioritised expanding access and ensuring the availability of essential inputs (such as health infrastructure, pharmaceutical products and a qualified workforce), more recent initiatives have actively sought to improve the processes of care delivery and patient outcomes through clinical audits, guideline adherence monitoring and performance-based financing mechanisms.2–5 These reforms reflect a growing recognition that access without quality is insufficient to achieve meaningful health improvements.

The concept of quality of care is multifaceted and has long influenced how healthcare systems are structured and evaluated.6–9 A foundational framework is the Donabedian model, which defines quality of care as ‘care that is expected to maximise an overall measure of patient well-being, after taking into account the balance of expected gains and losses within the care process and structure’. Introduced in 1966, Donabedian’s model continues to serve as a widely accepted framework, organising quality assessment around three interrelated components: structure (input), process and outcome.10 11 The input component includes system-level resources (eg, infrastructure, technologies, qualified workforce), the process component relates to the technical and interpersonal aspects of care delivery, and the outcome component captures both clinical results and patient-reported experiences. Donabedian posits that strong structural foundations are necessary to support effective care processes, which in turn influence outcomes.

In LMICs, there has been growing political and technical engagement in efforts to improve quality of care across both public and private sectors. These efforts are increasingly integrated into broader health system reforms guided by the WHO health systems framework, which identifies six essential building blocks: service delivery, health workforce, health information systems, access to essential medicines, financing and leadership/governance.12 In this framework, improvements in these foundational elements are expected to lead to desirable system outputs such as improved access, quality, equity, efficiency and responsiveness, ultimately contributing to better health outcomes. This aligns conceptually with the Donabedian model, in which the structural building blocks of the system influence the processes of care, which in turn shape outcomes. Together, these frameworks emphasise that system inputs must be leveraged to ensure high-quality, equitable and patient-centred care.

As part of this shift, many LMICs have prioritised the continuous assessment of quality of care using appropriate tools. For such assessments to enable comparison across settings and inform local quality improvement, it is important that tools be both validated and applicable to real-world practice (operational). Here, we define applicability broadly to include cost-effectiveness and relevance to varying levels of technical capacity and the overall health systems. For instance, in countries such as Benin, policymakers have expressed strong interest in using low-cost and locally adaptable (operational) quality assessment tools.

To our knowledge, no prior review has systematically mapped the range of tools used to assess each component of quality of care—as defined by Donabedian—in LMICs, nor explored their practical applicability in real-world settings. To fill this gap, this rapid review aimed to: (1) identify tools used to assess quality of care in LMICs and categorise them according to Donabedian’s framework (input, process and outcome); (2) assess whether the included studies reported information on the validity of these tools and (3) determine whether any of the included studies reported evidence regarding their applicability in low-resource contexts.

Methodology

This study was a rapid review conducted from June to November 2023. According to Hamel et al,13 ‘A rapid review is a form of knowledge synthesis that accelerates the process of conducting a traditional systematic review through streamlining or omitting specific methods to produce evidence for stakeholders in a resource-efficient manner’.

To guide our review, we followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement, which allowed for transparent reporting of our search strategy and our findings; and the guidelines proposed by Tricco et al for conducting rapid reviews to strengthen health policy and systems.14 15

Eligibility criteria

Our main research questions were the following: (1) What tools have been used to assess the quality of care in LMICs and how can these tools be categorised according to Donabedian’s model (input, process, outcome)? (2) What information was reported regarding the validity of these tools used in the included studies? (3) Do included studies provide evidence on the cost-effectiveness of these tools? To be included, papers had to: (1) be an empirical peer-reviewed study, survey or activity reports (grey literature) published and available online, (2) explicitly be related to a quality of care assessment carried out in a LMIC, (3) be dated to any period and (4) be written in either English or French. Exclusions were made for conceptual papers, theoretical papers, conference proceedings, theses and dissertations, as well as papers whose full text was not available.

Information sources

To identify papers, we systematically searched empirical studies in the following databases: Google Scholar and PubMed. The broad scope of these databases allowed us to assess various studies in our literature review.

Study selection process

Search queries were established using keywords and/or Medical Subject Headings terms and Boolean operators to identify studies matching our topic. Online supplemental appendices 1 and 2 present the search queries looking at investigating studies in PubMed and Google Scholar respectively (on 10 July 2023). The keywords included in our query were found using a two-step method commonly used in reviews.16–18 The first step was to use the following search structure: “Tools or Methods related terms” AND “Quality of care related terms”. This search was conducted in each database. 50 random studies per database were screened (abstract and title) to deduce any additional search terms that may have been missed. The second step is to use the following search structure: “Developing country related terms” AND “Quality of care related terms”. This was again searched in each database, with a maximum of fifty random studies reviewed.18

After applying the search strategies mentioned in online supplemental appendices 1 and 2, which aimed at investigating studies in PubMed and Google Scholar, respectively, the references were exported into the bibliography manager Zotero where duplicates were automatically detected and deleted manually.19

To extend the scope of our study to more literature from French-speaking countries and health projects ongoing specifically on quality of care improvement in these countries, we used the ‘berry-picking searching’ technique.20 As mentioned in online supplemental appendix 3, this technique consisted of entering in Google the French term ‘Évaluation de la qualité des soins’ associated with ‘the name of each French-speaking country’ listed as LMICs according to the last update of the World Bank Group.21 The results displayed were scanned in their entirety, and papers were selected according to the relevance of their title.

The papers identified were then transferred into Rayyan,22 which automatically deleted the duplicates remaining that had bypassed the first process.

To ensure concordance before proceeding with record screening, an inter-coder reliability score was calculated using the percentage agreement (we agreed in advance that if it reached >75%, we would move on).14 18 Specifically, in a pilot test, two researchers (NDLT and SC) independently reviewed the title and abstract of a random sample of 50 records based on the four selection criteria and specified which criteria were not met if the study was excluded.18 Their work was then compared. Two rounds of pilot testing were required. The first round with both researchers screening 26 of the 50 records in the same way (ie, a score of 52%). The second round after improving inclusion criteria, with both researchers screening 43 of the 50 records in the same way (ie, a score of 86%).

The title and abstract of the records were then screened by two independent reviewers (NDLT and SC; AAV and KB; MCUA and RSLD). The conflicts that occurred between reviewers were resolved by a third reviewer (J-PD, RD and BC). After title and abstract screening, we proceeded to the full text screening where two independent reviewers reviewed each paper (NDLT and SC; AAV and KB; RD and NJEAT). The conflicts that occurred between reviewers were also resolved the same way as for the previous step (J-PD, RD and BC).

Data extraction process

First, after a thorough reading, two members of the research team (NDLT and SC; AAV and KB; MCUA and RSLD; RD and NJEAT) listed each of the full-texts in detail in a summary spreadsheet, recording various characteristics related to the papers and the reported results. These sheets were manually compared and found to be highly similar. The rare dissimilarities that occurred were resolved through discussion within the research team. Synthesis methods

To synthesise data, we conducted a descriptive synthesis of data coupled with a narrative synthesis of evidence.23 Our analysis and synthesis were guided by the Donabedian framework, which organises quality assessment into input (structure), process and outcome components. The input component includes all characteristics of the care environment, such as infrastructure, technical facilities, qualified workforce, pharmaceutical products, health information systems, financial resources, training systems and management systems. The process component pertains to the technical quality of the care provided to patients and their overall experience across the care continuum. The outcome component focuses on the effects of healthcare on individuals, including patient satisfaction and health outcomes.10 11 A mapping of the LMICs covered by the included papers was made with R software. The world shapefile was retrieved from the spData package (V.0.3.0, https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=spData) and the map was created using the tmap package (V.3.2.2, https://CRAN.R-project.org/package=tmap). We could not indicate on the map the number of papers included per country, as there were studies involving many countries together.

Results

Selection of source evidence

Our search strategies (online supplemental appendices 1 and 2) reported 287 for Google Scholar and 1164 for PubMed for a total of 1451 papers. The purging process in Zotero yielded 11 duplicates. After eliminating the duplicates, we ended up with 1440 papers. The berry-picking search strategy (online supplemental appendix 3) performed in Google recorded 74 papers. The 1514 papers identified records were then transferred into Rayyan, which automatically deleted 12 duplicates that had bypassed the first process. Hence, 1502 records were identified for the title and abstract screening phase in Rayyan.

The title and abstract of the 1502 records were then screened. Based on the selection criteria, 1280 studies were deemed not relevant for our review. The full-text screening was then performed on a total of 222 papers. Throughout the full-text screening section, we excluded 137 which did not conform to the inclusion criteria. In the end, a total of 85 studies were included in this review. Figure 1 shows our PRISMA flow diagram.

Of the 85 papers included in this study, 78 were peer-reviewed empirical research while seven were survey or activity reports (grey literature) published on government sites24–26 and international organisation sites.27–30 The papers included in this study covered the period from 1994 to 2023. 80 studies were carried out in the geographical area of a single developing country while five studies involved several developing countries.31–35 The studies included in this review covered 43 countries classified as low- and middle-income by the World Bank group in 202321 (figure 2). Studies were conducted in the field of maternal and neonatal health in 38.82% of cases (33 out of 85). Studies that were not specific to any field of medicine and concerned the overall healthcare facilities accounted for 20% of cases (17 out of 85). Studies focused on infectious diseases, paediatric health and cardiovascular diseases were found in 8, 7 and 6 papers out of 85, respectively (figure 3).

Quality of care aspects in the tools

Of the 85 studies included in this review, most assessed multiple aspects of quality of care concomitantly. The technical quality of care was the most frequently assessed (n=48), followed by infrastructure availability (n=35) and patient experience (n=24). Fewer studies focused on the availability of a qualified workforce (n=14), pharmaceutical products (n=17), hospital management systems (n=17) and patient satisfaction (n=18). Very few addressed health information systems (n=7), continuous training systems for healthcare staff (n=3) or patient health outcomes (n=4). None of the studies assessed financial resource management or conducted any form of cost analysis (table 1).

Types of tools assessing quality of care in LMICs

Overall, the assessment tools predominantly relied on checklists, observation grids, structured questionnaires and interview guides. Most tools were investigator-developed, although some drew on institutional initiatives, including Services Availability and Readiness Assessment (SARA) survey guide, the MEASURE DHS healthcare facility inventory questionnaire, and Patient Satisfaction with Nursing Care Quality Questionnaire (PSNCQQ). Among both investigator-developed and institutional-developed tools, the majority did not target a single aspect of the quality of care. In contrast, the PSNCQQ was the only tool explicitly designed to assess patient satisfaction alone. Examination of the tools’ content revealed that several investigator-developed instruments drew inspiration from established institutional tools (table 1).

Assessment techniques of the quality of care in LMICs

The studies included in this review employed a variety of data collection techniques to assess the quality of care. These include: the review of patient medical records, administrative charts, and facility documentation; direct observation at points of care, facility’s pharmacies and archives; face-to-face interviews with patients, healthcare providers and administrative staff; written questionnaires administered to patients and healthcare providers; and focus groups (qualitative study) among stakeholders. In a few studies (three papers), more robust techniques were applied to assess the technical quality of the care provided, including clinical vignettes, direct clinician observation, and the use of standardised patients. For outcomes assessment, the majority of tools focused on patient satisfaction, based on subjective judgments obtained via interviews or questionnaires (table 1).

Discussion

Key findings

This rapid review on the ‘tools used to assess each component of quality of care—according to Donabedian’s model—in LMICs, and their reported validity and applicability in routine health system settings’, presented a very complex landscape.

What tools have been used to assess the quality of care in LMICs, and how can these tools be categorised according to Donabedian’s model (input, process, outcome)?

A wide range of tools has been employed to assess the quality of care in LMICs, reflecting varying degrees of complexity, heterogeneity and alignment with Donabedian’s model of quality (structure/input, process and outcome). These tools can be broadly categorised into institutional tools—such as SARA, DHS tools and the PSNCQQ—and investigator-developed tools, which are often tailored to specific research contexts. Under the input component, institutional tools like SARA and DHS provide scalable, standardised assessments of infrastructure, equipment, workforce and essential medicines. Investigator-developed tools were also widely used, often drawing inspiration from the institutional instruments. In the process component, most studies employed investigator-developed tools to assess clinical practices, provider–patient interactions and adherence to protocols. These tools are context-specific and flexible but vary considerably in their methodological rigour. Regarding the outcome component, patient satisfaction was the most frequently assessed aspect, commonly measured through adapted investigator-developed tools or institutional instruments such as the PSNCQQ. While these tools are often validated and offer valuable insights into users’ perceptions, they primarily capture subjective experiences. Objective health outcomes were rarely assessed, likely due to contextual constraints such as limited resources, fragmented data systems and insufficient capacity for longitudinal follow-up.

A key finding of our review is the marked heterogeneity of the tools employed across studies. Several factors might explain this diversity. Indeed, the health systems of LMICs are themselves heterogeneous, varying widely in governance structures, financing mechanisms, service-delivery models and existing data infrastructures.98 These systemic differences are likely to shape distinct measurement needs that encourage the development of context-specific tools. For instance, while institutional tools like SARA and DHS offer standardised and comparable metrics, they are often considered insufficiently granular to capture the nuances of daily clinical practice or the specificities of local disease burdens. This limitation might drive researchers to design investigator-developed tools that more closely align with national or subnational priorities—whether maternal health, infectious diseases or non-communicable conditions—thereby contributing to variability in tool scope and content. Moreover, heterogeneity might also reflect the historically fragmented architecture of donor-driven and vertical programmes in LMICs. For example, over time, global health initiatives in fields such as HIV, malaria, tuberculosis and Reproductive, Maternal, Newborn, and Child Health have each promoted their own measurement frameworks, resulting in multiple parallel tools that continue to coexist rather than converge. This layering effect could produce a landscape in which health facilities and researchers could choose among—or combine—multiple, non-harmonised approaches, further reinforcing diversity in measurement practices. On the other hand, operational and methodological constraints—such as limited staffing, insufficient training in data collection, weak routine information systems and unreliable record-keeping—shape what is practically measurable in real-life settings. These constraints often necessitate simplifying, adapting or selectively applying tools to fit local capacity, resulting in additional variation in the depth and rigour of assessments. Finally, the absence of a global consensus on minimum quality-of-care standards across all levels of the health system means that LMIC stakeholders retain substantial discretion in determining which indicators to prioritise. As a result, the tools in use reflect both the flexibility afforded by this lack of standardisation and the need to respond pragmatically to resource and capacity constraints. These contextual, institutional and methodological factors might explain why quality of care assessment in LMICs relies on a heterogeneous array of tools that differ in scope, depth, content focus and alignment with Donabedian’s framework.

What information was reported regarding the validity of the tools used in the included studies?

Across the included studies, comprehensive information on validity of the quality assessment tools was sparse and inconsistently reported. Most tools—particularly investigator-developed—underwent only face or content validity checks, often based on expert opinion or adaptation from prior tools, without formal documentation of the validation process. For tools measuring subjective constructs, such as patient or provider experience and patient satisfaction, psychometric testing (eg, factor analysis, internal consistency measures like Cronbach’s alpha) was rarely conducted, and inter-rater reliability assessments were seldom reported. A few exceptions included standardised tools such as the PSNCQQ, which had documented construct validity and reliability coefficients, but these were applied in limited settings and without cross-cultural revalidation. Institutional tools, while methodologically standardised, also lacked detailed reporting of validation procedures in the reviewed papers. Their reliability is typically assumed based on institutional origin, yet empirical evidence of context-specific adaptation or testing was largely absent.

What information was reported regarding the applicability of the tools used in the included studies? (Did included studies provide evidence on the cost-effectiveness of the tools? Did included studies’ distribution and content demonstrate the relevance of the tools to varying levels of technical capacity and the overall health systems?)

The applicability of tools used to assess quality of care in LMICs was rarely examined in a structured manner across the included studies. Cost-effectiveness was not reported in any study, and very few tools demonstrated clear alignment with national systems’ operational and financial constraints. Notably, most assessments were one-off, research-driven initiatives, with only four led by national health authorities, underscoring the reality that these tools were largely designed for academic purposes rather than for sustained, system-integrated quality monitoring.

While the implementation of institutional tools offers broad validity and scalability, it seems often resource-intensive and typically relies on external funding. Conversely, investigator-developed tools, though often contextually tailored, frequently lacked standardisation and were rarely embedded into routine practice. They also tend to oversimplify quality constructs or overlook systemic factors that influence care.

Actually, the absence of cost-effectiveness reporting found in our review does not necessarily imply that such evidence is nonexistent in the broader quality-of-care literature. Rather, it highlights a gap in LMIC research, where economic evaluation of quality-assessment tools is rarely undertaken. This gap likely stems from several intersecting factors.98 First, many quality assessments in LMICs are conducted within donor-driven or vertical programmes that emphasise data collection for programmatic monitoring but seldom require or sponsor economic evaluations of the measurement tools themselves. Second, Ministries of Health often operate under resource constraints, prompting prioritisation of service delivery and workforce needs over cost analyses of assessment tools. Third, methodological challenges—such as fragmented information systems, limited routine data and the difficulty of attributing costs across multiple partners—make economic evaluation technically demanding. These contextual limitations reduce the likelihood that implementers or researchers will systematically measure the cost, cost-efficiency or return on investment of quality of care assessment tools.

Insights from high-income countries (HICs) illustrate that even in resource-rich settings, the cost-effectiveness of quality of care assessment is not consistently examined. Economic evaluations in HICs tend to focus on system-level quality improvement strategies—such as hospital accreditation, clinical audits, pay-for-performance mechanisms or electronic quality-monitoring platforms—rather than on the cost-effectiveness of individual assessment tools.99–101 Although these studies demonstrate that robust quality of care assessment mechanisms can be costly,100 their findings are not readily transferable to LMIC contexts due to substantial differences in workforce capacity, financing, digital infrastructure and service-delivery models. Thus, even if our search strategy had explicitly incorporated economic evaluation terms (eg, cost analysis, implementation cost, economic evaluation of quality tools), the yield of cost-related evidence for LMICs would likely still have been minimal. The absence of LMIC-focused economic evaluations therefore appears to reflect a structural void in the global literature rather than a limitation of our review strategy.

Finally, the limited reporting on the operational applicability of the tools—such as resource requirements, training needs, integration with routine systems or sustainability—further illustrates the lack of systematic attention to feasibility in real-world health system environments. These findings indicate that the economic, logistical and institutional conditions under which quality of care tools are implemented remain an underexplored dimension of the field. Addressing this gap will be essential for informing future investments in quality of care assessment, particularly in settings where resources are scarce and measurement capacity must be carefully aligned with health-system priorities.

Comparison with existing reviews on quality assessment tools

Our findings deepen and extend the contributions of previous reviews by Fracolli et al,102 Brizuela et al103 and Quach et al,104 which focused respectively on primary care, maternal and newborn health and child health facility assessments. These earlier reviews documented the diversity of available quality-of-care assessment tools—including SPA, SARA, WHO Hospital Care Tools, Primary Care Assessment Tool (PCAT)—and provided detailed descriptions of their content and methodological characteristics. However, their analytical scope remained confined to specific clinical sectors. As a result, they did not fully capture how such tools are deployed across the broader, more heterogeneous health-system landscape in LMICs. By examining a wider spectrum of clinical areas and levels of care, our study was able to identify cross-cutting patterns of tool selection and use that remain invisible in more narrowly focused analyses.

A central contribution of our review is the demonstration of a substantial gap between validated tools described in previous literature and the tools actually used in empirical studies conducted in LMICs. Several standardised tools highlighted in earlier reviews—such as the WHO Primary Care Evaluation Tool, the ADHD Questionnaire for Primary Care Providers, the General Practice Assessment Questionnaire, PACOTAPS and the PCAT—were entirely absent from the studies included in our sample. Although these tools have been validated for assessing various dimensions of quality of care, their operational applicability in LMIC contexts remains insufficiently examined. Their absence in practice likely reflects constraints related to time, training requirements, data infrastructure and financial resources, which collectively reduce their feasibility for routine use in resource-limited health systems. Furthermore, many of these tools primarily emphasise input and process components of care, mirroring the partial coverage observed in several of the tools identified in our review.

In relation to previous literature, our study both confirms and extends several recurrent observations. Consistent with Brizuela et al,103 we found a predominance of input and process components coverage over outcome component. Similar to Quach et al,104 our review highlighted that even the most comprehensive tools—such as WHO Hospital Care Assessment tools—remain insufficient to capture all relevant components of care quality and are infrequently employed in LMIC settings. Echoing the conclusions of Fracolli et al,102 our findings show that validated tools for quality of care assessment remain largely absent in empirical studies in LMICs, despite their potential usefulness. Taken together, these comparisons underscore persistent gaps in the harmonisation, comprehensiveness and practical uptake of quality of care assessment tools across diverse clinical domains and health-system levels.

Future prospects

Quality of care assessment in LMICs is marked by a proliferation of heterogeneous tools, each capturing fragments of the broader quality construct. Input component is better covered than process and outcome. Methodological standardisation remains a key challenge. Future efforts should prioritise comprehensive, context-sensitive and scalable tools that integrate structure, process and outcomes in a coherent evaluative framework. The weak tools’ validation landscape revealed by this review undermines the comparability, replicability and interpretive robustness of findings across studies. Without rigorous validity or reliability metrics, conclusions drawn from many tools remain methodologically fragile, particularly in diverse LMIC contexts where cultural and systemic differences may affect measurement properties. Future tool development and deployment should embed systematic psychometric testing and contextual revalidation to enhance the credibility of quality assessment efforts. Evidence from this review suggests that quality assessment tools seem not to be simpler for usage by stakeholders in LMICs. Indeed, we found that the quality of care was most frequently assessed in tertiary hospitals. Actually, these hospitals are most often university hospitals, where clinicians are also researchers at the same time in these regions. Moreover, we noted that the idea of assessing the quality of care was the initiative of the health authorities in only four studies. In none of the studies was it the initiative of non-academic clinicians. In all other papers, it was initiated by a team of researchers (academic clinicians). This underlines the need to develop tools that would be simpler and easier to use for both clinical practitioners and health programme managers at all levels, to empower them in the process of improving the quality of care in LMICs. Future studies should also aim to develop well-contextualised tools to assess financial resource allocation in low-resource settings. However, despite the potential theoretical relevance of investigator-developed to varying health system levels, the tools’ practical suitability for routine use in diverse LMIC settings remains questionable. None of the studies reported on the cost-effectiveness of the quality-of-care assessment for LMICs. But we did note that several of the studies benefited from external funding. This points to the assumption that the quality-of-care assessment would not be affordable for resource-limited countries in the absence of external financial support. To enhance real-world applicability, future tools must be codeveloped with local health authorities, incorporate cost and capacity considerations and be designed for integration into existing health information systems to support continuous quality improvement rather than isolated assessments.

Study limitations

In this study, we included papers from formal scientific sources (empirical peer-reviewed papers), but also papers from non-scientific sources (grey literature) available on either government sites or international organisation sites. Nonetheless, these non-scientific papers have made a significant contribution to our study and do not constitute a blatant bias to the reliability of our findings. This review did not include an independent cost-effectiveness analysis of the identified tools, nor did it undertake an extended secondary search specifically targeting cost-effectiveness (as relevant terms were not included in the search strategy). Therefore, even if this would likely have been minimal, the observed absence of tools addressing cost-effectiveness or the financial aspect of health services may reflect limitations in the search strategy rather than the actual nonexistence of such tools in the broader literature. We found that the assessment of quality of care was most often initiated by academic clinicians. This may be partly due to the limited public availability of government-led assessments, which could have introduced a selection bias in this review.

Conclusions

This review highlights the urgent need for validated, context-sensitive and cost-effective tools to assess quality of care in LMICs. Existing tools are often fragmented, poorly validated, and misaligned with health system capacities. Future tools must be codeveloped with local stakeholders, designed for integration into routine systems, and adapted to the realities of resource-limited settings. Prioritising simplicity, methodological rigour and sustainability will be essential to make quality assessment a meaningful driver of health system improvement.
