MCoGCN-motif high-order feature-guided embedding learning framework for social link prediction
Authors: Nan Xiang, Wenjing Yang, Xindi Rao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractTraditional social link prediction models primarily concentrate on the adjacency features of the network, overlooking the rich high-order structural information within. Therefore, the study of effective extraction and encoding of these high-order features, and their integration into prediction models, holds significant theoretical and practical value. To address this challenge, we propose a novel embedding learning framework guided by motif high-order features for social link prediction tasks. Firstly, we utilize the motif adjacency matrix to capture complex patterns in social networks. Through a propagation process, node embeddings can carry the structural information of the network. Subsequently, we design a simplified attention mechanism, allowing embeddings carrying motif high-order features to guide the representation of embeddings based on adjacency features. We then employ a feed-forward neural network to optimize node embeddings. Specifically, this framework addresses the issue of weakly correlated nodes in the network, which struggle to learn effective embeddings due to a lack of direct information. By guiding with high-order motif features, the framework enhances the similarity and predictive power of these node embeddings. Finally, we conducted a detailed evaluation of the predictive performance of our model on four social networks. The experimental results indicate that our model exhibits high accuracy and advantages in predicting social links.Keywords: Link prediction, Social network, Graph neural network, Motif, Weak linkSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

With social networks evolving at a rapid pace, social link prediction has become an important research topic. The goal is to predict potential new links in social networks, which is of great significance for understanding the structure and dynamic changes of social networks. Approaches grounded on conventional topological characteristics, like Common Neighbors (CN), Jaccard index, and Adamic/Adar index (AA), are very classic baselines in link prediction1, providing inspiration and foundations for subsequent research. These established attributes are not just potent but also computationally economical due to their reliance on triangular graph substructures2. However, due to their reliance on manual feature engineering, limited ability to handle high-dimensional data, and inability to fully consider global and local features of the graph, later methods such as Non-negative Matrix Factorization (NMF)3 and graph embeddings4 emerged. NMF can be effectively used for link prediction, especially when dealing with network sparsity and combining node attribute information. Graph embedding techniques have the ability to transform each node’s information into low-dimensional vectors to represent graph-structured data, but there are challenges in attribute selection, scalability, and embedding dimension selection. Therefore, in recent years, researchers’ interest has shifted to Graph Neural Networks (GNNs)5,6, which have demonstrated greater effectiveness in explicitly encoding structural information and node features7,8, resulting in notable achievements and successes9.

However, not all GNN models outperform simple heuristic algorithms in link prediction tasks. Most traditional GNNs have limitations in their expressive power when dealing with link prediction tasks, and they might lack the capability to effectively grasp complex structural features in the graph, such as triangle counts. The network contains numerous connected subgraphs, so these complex structural features can be obtained by studying motifs. Network motifs of high-order connectivity are considered as the basic building blocks of networks that exhibit complex structural patterns10. With the in-depth study of complex networks, motif analysis has been employed across various networks, for example, social networks and internet topologies, to understand their structures and characteristics.

Additionally, nodes and edges in a network can form structures at multiple scales, such as local node neighborhoods, community structures, and global network features, with complex correlations between different structural scales11. In previous research, many methods for social link prediction have been proposed, but most of these methods consider only a single scale of the network, ignoring its multi-scale characteristics.

In the network, not only do influential nodes exist12, typically characterized by having numerous connections or close relationships with other nodes, but there are also nodes that exhibit weak correlations, sharing few or no common neighbors. When two nodes show weak correlations, especially when they are located in different communities, the link prediction model tends to separate the embedding vectors trained for these weakly linked vertices in space due to a lack of sufficient direct information and context to guide the learning process. These vectors each represent their community characteristics, resulting in low similarity. Therefore, learning effective embeddings for these weakly linked vertices becomes a challenge.

Therefore, we put forward a new approach named MCoGCN, which learns embedding representations under the guidance of Motif high-order features to achieve the Collaboration of features at different scales, thereby improving the performance of link prediction. High-level data reduces specific details that may contain noise, so high-level features help correct prediction biases that may arise from detailed data. To achieve this goal, we define a simplified version of the attention mechanism that adjusts the vectors obtained from low-order features from the perspective of high-order features. In addition, we use the motif adjacency matrix as a tool for propagating high-order information and judge the intimacy of connections between nodes through the values in the matrix. In the motif, any two vertices have strong connectivity. Although the direct neighbors of two nodes may not be the same, the strong connectivity of the motif structure can help us discover and make up for the weak relevance between nodes, as shown in Fig. 1. Furthermore, in order to obtain higher quality node embeddings without significantly increasing the model complexity, we use feed-forward neural networks for noise reduction.

Related work

Work related to motifs

For motif research, with the rapid development of computing power and algorithms, the ability to enumerate motifs that encompass more than three nodes has been significantly enhanced13. Furthermore, research2 has also demonstrated that high-order motif characteristics substantially improve the accuracy of link prediction algorithms. Early motif research focused on exploring important patterns and substructures in biological networks14, especially protein–protein interaction networks, and demonstrated that specific motif patterns are related to the robustness of perturbations, showing their modeling capabilities in biological networks. Chen et al.15 proposed MGNN, which involves generating node representations, minimizing motif redundancy, integrating representations from multiple motifs, and leveraging an injection function to enhance the discriminative capability of graph neural networks. Wang et al.16 introduced a motif-based algorithm for learning node embeddings specifically tailored for link prediction tasks. This algorithm harnesses the concept of motifs, incorporating two levels of proximity within an autoencoder framework, to capture high-order structural information and mitigate the challenges associated with predicting weak connections. To measure the similarity between two nodes within their immediate surroundings, Li et al.17 integrates existing similarity definitions and motifs, proposing a similarity index based on motifs. To address the issue that existing link prediction methods for directed networks mostly consider only one motif and ignore the impact of multiple motifs, Liu et al.18 proposed a single motif naive Bayes model and studied a two-motif naive Bayes model along with a machine learning framework that utilizes multi-motif features to enhance link prediction performance. The literature19 introduced a specific algorithm for creating motif adjacency matrices and introduces a graph attention network based on motifs for service recommendation. This network captures high-order information of various motifs and makes recommendation predictions using a collaborative filtering model. DeepGL20 leverages motif learning to induce relational functions, serving as a general inductive graph representation learning framework. A paper21 introduces a generalized motif-based framework for network clustering. The clusters formed based on this framework can be mathematically guaranteed to be optimal, and it is capable of scaling to networks with billions of edges. The literature22 describes a versatile structure called High-Order Network Embedding (HONE) based on network motifs for learning from graph data. The HONE framework is remarkably adaptable and expressive, comprising numerous exchangeable elements. A research of graph node classification23 introduces a self-attention model at the motif level that learns the weights of various motifs via differentiation. Luo et al.24 presented a graph neural network, termed as MotifGNN, which is motif-based and is utilized for recommending matches in online dating.

Work related to GNNs

GNNs generate representations of nodes or entire graphs by integrating information from the surrounding neighborhood of a node, enabling them to more effectively capture the graph structures and node features. Velickovic et al.25 introduced Graph Attention Networks (GATs), which leverage self-attention layers to allocate varying weights to distinct neighbors, making them more accurately capture the relationships between nodes. He et al.26 proposed LightGCN, which only contains the fundamental element of GCNs for collaborative filtering - neighborhood aggregation. This simplifies the design of GCNs, making them more concise and suitable for recommendation tasks. Bojchevski et al.27 presented PPRGo, which uses an effective approximation of information diffusion in GNNs, thereby significantly speeding up computation while improving predictive performance. xGCN28 is an extreme graph convolutional network used in large-scale networks. It abandons the traditional paradigm of GNN and proposes a new iterative paradigm, namely [Propagation, Refnement, SGD, Refresh], which achieves SOTA results and speed improvements in link prediction tasks on large-scale social graphs.

However, none of the above studies unifiedly modeled the multi-scale information of networks. Regarding multi-scale collaboration research, the literature11 mentions that networks consist of individuals and their connections to other individuals, thus macro-level dynamics originate from micro-level dynamics. Conversely, the behavior of individuals is also influenced by their social relationships, including political orientations, musical tastes, and even the way people choose new friends29,30. Therefore, there are complex interconnections between various levels. For in-depth studies of networks, it is inevitable to consider their multi-scale characteristics.

Model framework

The model comprises four key operations: Embedding Propagation, Embedding Fusion, Optimization Learning, and Embedding Refresh. These four stages are executed sequentially in a chained fashion, iterating repeatedly until convergence is achieved. The ultimate objective is to learn high-quality node embeddings that are sensitive to the graph structure, thereby enhancing the model’s resistance to noise and anomalies. The implementation process is illustrated in Fig. 3. We randomly initialize the embedding table Z, and then perform graph convolution operations to obtain smoothed embeddings and propagate node information along the graph structure, as shown in Fig. 3-(i). Next, in the Node Embedding Fusion module in Fig. 3-(ii), we effectively fuse the obtained low-order and high-order embedding tables through a simplified attention network. This hierarchical interaction mechanism can enhance the consistency and richness of node representations. To filter out the noise brought by the propagation process, we use the optimization neural network shown in Fig. 3-(iii) to further transform and refine the fused embeddings, thereby improving the quality of the embeddings. All of the model’s parameters are updated through the conventional gradient descent method. Finally, we obtain embeddings X that contain multi-scale structural features and are of higher quality, and replace Z with X. Each round of iteration brings the embedding table Z closer to the real graph structure. This method not only improves the accuracy of the embeddings but also enhances the model’s overall understanding of graph data, making the node embeddings more comprehensive and detailed. Embedding fusion

After the propagation process, the structural information of the network is enhanced. Now, we fuse the obtained low-order embedding table and high-order embedding table through an attention mechanism, as shown in Fig. 5. Firstly, we merge the two embedding tables into one embedding table, which can be the addition of two embedding tables or concatenation along the second dimension. Then, through a fully connected layer with a ReLU activation function, we perform a non-linear transformation on the merged embedding table to obtain the attention score for each element. Next, we transform the attention scores into weights through another fully connected layer and a Softmax function. Finally, we carry out an element-wise multiplication between the weights and the low-order embedding table to derive the ultimate weighted embedding table. This procedure realizes dynamic weighting of the embedding table, allowing high-order features to guide and direct the representation of low-order features. In addition, this mechanism is simpler than traditional attention mechanisms, making the model easier to understand and implement.

Optimization learning

Through the multi-scale fusion module, the representation of each node carries information of different structural scales. However, although the propagation process can encode graph structural information into node embeddings, it is an unsupervised operation and inevitably brings a large amount of noise. Therefore, an optimization learning module is necessitated to glean pertinent details from the embeddings and to filter out the noise. It is well known that Feed-Forward Network (FFN) holds a vital position in retaining knowledge and carrying out basic transformations of information in the Transformer architecture, providing the ability to deeply transform and learn features of local information. Therefore, we have chosen FFN as our optimized learning network, paired with a Scaling Neural Network (SNN) to adjust the amplitude of the output vector from the FFN to an appropriate level.

Loss function

We adopt a pairwise ranking loss, BPR, to uniformly adjust the parameters in the fusion module and optimization learning module, which can make the model generalize better during the training process. The BPR loss function is a loss function used to learn user personalized preferences in recommendation systems, assuming that users prefer positive items over all other unobserved items. Evaluation strategy

We use Recall and Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain (NDCG)26,31 as main evaluation metrics. Recall@K assesses the model’s effectiveness in identifying positive target nodes within the top K recommendations. It is defined as:

For example, suppose there are 10 correct answers, and the model found 5 correct answers in the top K recommendations, then Recall@K is 5/10 = 0.5. We compute the mean of individual Recall@K values across all users to get an estimate of the model’s average performance across all users. NDCG measures the degree to which the positive target nodes are positioned prominently relative to the ideal ranking order. In an ideal scenario, all positive target nodes would be positioned at the top of the recommendation list, resulting in an NDCG value of 1. Other evaluation metrics also include AUC and MRR.

Baseline

We compare MCoGCN with various models, including the motif-based approach MGNN15, and graph embedding techniques: node2vec32, SimpleX33, UltraGCN34, RandNE35 which does not use graph convolution layers and purely utilizes propagation between nodes to learn representations, as well as other competitive baselines GraphSage36, GAT25, GIN37, xGCN28, SGC38, SIGN39, SSGC40, GBP41.

Motif extraction

When dealing with datasets of different scales, we flexibly choose the motif extraction strategy according to the characteristics of the dataset and training requirements. For the Twitch-DE, Facebook, and Github datasets, we extract 7 motifs from the network structure for training to capture the rich interaction patterns and community structures in these networks. These 7 motifs represent the most common and important relational patterns in the network and have broad applicability across different types of networks. By utilizing these motifs, we can map out the complex interactions between nodes more deeply, rather than being limited to simple pairwise connections. For example, the triangular motif M1 can reflect the mutual acquaintance relationship among three people, which is very common in social networks.

Considering the large scale and computational cost of the Pokec dataset, in order to balance computational resources and model performance, we decide to extract only one representative motif for training on this dataset. Although this strategy may sacrifice the capture of some complex structural information to some extent, we believe that through carefully designed models and training processes, we can still achieve good link prediction results. Weak link

Here, we specifically define a weak link as a link between two nodes with fewer than three common neighbors. To validate the model’s ability to recognize and distinguish weak links, we identify all positive weak links (actual existing links) in the graph and sample 100 negative weak links (links that do not actually exist). We use the embeddings obtained through training to calculate the inner product scores of these two types of links, serving as a quantitative indicator of link strength. In the experiment, we calculated the average inner product scores of positive and negative weak links for different models on the Facebook and Github datasets, respectively. As shown in Fig. 10, MCoGCN shows the highest average score on positive instances and the lowest average score on negative instances compared to other models. This significant difference not only validates the effectiveness of our model in capturing subtle structural features of the network, but also strongly demonstrates the superior performance of MCoGCN in recognizing and distinguishing weak links in the network.

Visualization of node embeddings

After the model training was completed, we employed the t-SNE tool42 to project the high-dimensional embeddings onto a two-dimensional space and examined whether the node embeddings exhibited a discernible community distribution pattern. Prior to this, we used spectral clustering techniques to divide the social graph into eight independent clusters and labeled each node with the ID of its cluster. To enhance the visualization effect, we assigned a unique color to each cluster. Figure 11 shows the visualization results of four models on the Facebook dataset. Among them, the SimpleX model’s embedding points are relatively dispersed, demonstrating no clear clustering trend. This indicates that the SimpleX model’s ability to capture node similarity and social network structure is relatively weak, resulting in less distinct clustering effects in its embeddings. In contrast, the GAT and xGCN models exhibit more prominent performance in terms of clustering. They are capable of dividing the embedding points in the graph into multiple distinct clusters, with points in the same cluster (represented by the same color) gathering more closely together. However, even within the clustering results of GAT and xGCN, we observed some overlap of embedding points from different clusters (represented by different colors), indicating that these models still face certain limitations in distinguishing different clusters.Notably, by introducing the learning of high-order topological features, our MCoGCN model demonstrated significant advantages in clustering. As shown in Fig. 11d, the embedding points of MCoGCN formed more refined and distinct clusters. This confirms that high-order topological features can provide the model with richer information, aiding the model in more accurately capturing and representing complex relationships between nodes, thereby achieving more refined and accurate clustering. This further underscores the significance of high-order topological features in the analysis of social networks.

Conclusions

In this paper, we propose a novel Graph Neural Network (GNN) model called MCoGCN, which addresses the issue of information loss caused by traditional GNN models that mostly consider only single-scale features of the network. This model enhances the accuracy and stability of link prediction tasks based on graph embedding methods, and it exhibits excellent encoding capabilities for weakly linked nodes. MCoGCN learns rich graph structure information through a chain-like execution of four processes: propagation, fusion, optimization, and refresh. We capture high-order topological features of the network through motif structures and fuse low-order and high-order embeddings through a simple, flexible attention mechanism. Our experiments on four social network datasets showcase MCoGCN’s effectiveness in link prediction, offering new insights for various research applications utilizing graph embedding techniques, such as recommendation systems, social network analysis, community division, etc. Future work will focus on mining more effective low-order structural features and more efficient high-order structures, and expanding the model to dynamic link prediction tasks and heterogeneous graphs to better satisfy the demands of social development.
