Approximation and orthogonality on fully symmetric domains Thanks: The author was partially supported by Simons Foundation Grant #849676
Yuan Xu

Abstract
. We study orthogonal polynomials on a fully symmetric planar domain Ω \Omega that is generated by a certain triangle in the first quadrant. For a family of weight functions on Ω \Omega , we show that orthogonal polynomials that are even in the second variable on Ω \Omega can be identified with orthogonal polynomials on the unit disk composed with a quadratic map, and the same phenomenon can be extended to the domain generated by the rotation of Ω \Omega in higher dimensions. The connection allows an immediate deduction of results for approximation and Fourier orthogonal expansions on these fully symmetric domains. It applies, for example, to analysis on a double cone or a double hyperboloid.

1.
Introduction

Most of the study of orthogonal polynomials (OPs) in several variables is carried out on structured domains, and
one of the most well-studied cases is the unit ball (see, for example,
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
5
,
13
]
).
OPs on the unit ball share, together with a handful of other families on regular domains, two characteristic
properties of classical OPs: they are eigenfunctions of a second-order differential operator, and their
reproducing kernel satisfies a closed-form formula, both of which serve as essential tools for studying
approximation and Fourier orthogonal expansions (cf.
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
,
11
,
12
]
and their references).
They are also building blocks for OPs on domains of revolution that have been studied recently

[
10
,
15
,
16
,
18
,
19
]
and reference therein. In particular, orthogonal structure on a double conic
domain
{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1}
is studied in
[
15
]
,
where

{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1}=\left\{({\mathbf{x}},t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d+1}:\|{\mathbf{x}}\|\leq|t|,\,\,{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},\,t\in[-1,1]\right\}.

It follows that OPs for a weight function on the double cone are divided into two subspaces, depending
on whether they are even or odd in the
t
-variable, of different characteristics. In particular, for the weight
function

{\mathbf{W}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}({\mathbf{x}},t)=|t|\left(t^{2}-\|{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}\right)^{{\beta}-\frac{1}{2}}\left(1-t^{2}\right)^{\gamma},\quad({\mathbf{x}},t)\in{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1},

OPs that are even in the
t
-variable enjoy two characteristic properties of classical OPs. The same phenomenon
also holds for double hyperbolic domains, and it motivates the recent study in
[
19
]
, where the domain of
revolution is formulated as from rotating a two-dimensional region
\Omega
, which can be further reduced, when

\Omega
is fully symmetric, to the domain
\Lambda
so that
\{(x^{2},y^{2}):(x,y)\in\Lambda\}
is in the first
quadrant of
\Omega
. In the case of the double cone,
\Lambda
is the right-angled triangle
\{(s,t):0\leq s\leq t\leq 1\}
.
In this setting, OPs on the domain of revolution can be deduced from those of two variables on
\Lambda
and
spherical harmonics. In particular, by considering different types of triangles, OPs that are even
in
t
-variable are shown to share the two characteristic properties of classical OPs for a family of domains
of revolution in
[
19
]
.

The purpose of the present paper is to reveal a hidden relation between several families of OPs in
[
19
]
,
generated by those on triangles, to OPs on the unit ball. The relation shows, for example, OPs even in the

t
-variable for
{\mathbf{W}}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}
on
{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1}
are essentially semi-classical, classical if
{\beta}=0
, OPs on the
unit ball
{\mathbb{B}}^{d+1}
composed with a quadratic transformation. With this relation, it is now easy to establish
the two characteristic properties for OPs on a family of domains of revolution. This is somewhat unexpected
since the ball is homogeneous with a smooth boundary, but the conic domains are not. Still, it may not be as
surprising in retrospect, once the relation is understood. The correspondence has the effect of reducing the
analysis on the double cone and its generations to the familiar ground of the unit ball. For example,
approximation and localized polynomial frames on the double cone and hyperboloid were studied in
[
18
]
,
as a special case of the general framework developed in
[
16
]
based on highly localized kernels. While
establishing the localized kernels took substantial efforts in
[
18
]
, it can now be deduced from the
existing result on the unit ball effortlessly.

Restriction to OPs even in the
t
-variable may seem to be incomplete for approximation or Fourier orthogonal
expansions, but it is sufficient for functions that are even in the
t
-variable. Moreover, the fully symmetric
domain has a symmetry in the
t
-variable, so that we can consider only the upper half of the domain, call
it
{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}
. For the double cone
{\mathbb{X}}^{d+1}
, for example, the upper half domain
{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}
is the
single cone. Given a function
f
defined on
{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}
, we can extend it to the full domain by defining

f({\mathbf{x}},-t)=f({\mathbf{x}},t)
evenly in the
t
-variable. Consequently, OPs even in the
t
-variable consist of a
complete basis for
L^{2}
space on
{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}
, which leads to an alternative Fourier orthogonal expansion
and a complete set for polynomial approximation that works for the spaces equipped with evenly symmetric
weight functions. This orthogonal structure is fundamentally different from the
L^{2}
space on
{\mathbb{V}}^{d+1}

equipped with the Jacobi weight
(t^{2}-\|{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2})^{{\beta}-\frac{1}{2}}(1-t)^{\gamma}
, studied in
[
14
]
, which also shares
the two characteristic properties and has OPs of full dimension.

The main ingredient for the hidden relation lies in the OPs of two variables on the fully symmetric domains
arising from the triangles, which extends the relation between semi-classical OPs on the disk and the
classical OPs on the triangle. While the latter relation has been considered, see, for example,

[
5
,
19
]
, the space of OPs that are even in one of the variables has
hardly been singled out and studied as its own entity in the literature. As a result, we shall treat the case
of two variables first and separately in this work.

The paper is organized as follows. In the next section, we review the basics of OPs of two
variables, including the orthogonal structure in the fully symmetric setting. The particular family of OPs,
generated by triangles, and the approximation theory of such polynomials are studied in the third section.
These are extended in the fourth section to domains of revolution.

2.
Preliminary: orthogonal polynomials of two variables

In this preliminary section, we discuss orthogonal polynomials (OPs) of two variables and the Fourier orthogonal
expansions. The first subsection reviews the basics and classical OPs on the triangle and the disk. The second subsection
discusses OPs for a fully symmetric weight function.

2.1.
OPs of two variables

Let
\Omega
be a domain in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
with a positive area. Let
{\mathsf{W}}
be a weight function on
\Omega
.
We consider OPs under the inner product

(2.1)

{\langle}f,g{\rangle}_{\mathsf{W}}={\mathsf{b}}_{\mathsf{W}}\int_{\Omega}f({\mathbf{u}})g({\mathbf{u}}){\mathsf{W}}({\mathbf{u}})\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{u}},\qquad{\mathbf{u}}=(u_{1},u_{2}),

where
{\mathsf{b}}_{\mathsf{W}}
is the normalization constant of
{\mathsf{W}}
such that
{\langle}1,1{\rangle}_{\mathsf{W}}=1
. If the weight function

{\mathsf{W}}
is regular, that is,
\int_{\Omega}\|{\mathbf{u}}\|^{n}{\mathsf{W}}({\mathbf{u}})\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{u}}<\infty
for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, then OPs exist for
all
n
. Let
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}={\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathsf{W}},\Omega)
denote the space of OPs of degree
n
for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
. Then
the space
\Pi_{n}^{2}
of polynomials of degree
n
in two variables satisfies

\Pi_{n}^{2}=\bigoplus_{k=0}^{n}{\mathcal{V}}_{k}({\mathsf{W}},\Omega)
and

\dim{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathsf{W}},\Omega)=n+1\quad\hbox{and}\quad\dim\Pi_{n}^{2}=\binom{n+2}{2},\quad n=0,1,2,\ldots.

Let
\{{\mathsf{P}}_{j,n}:0\leq j\leq n\}
be an orthonormal basis for
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathsf{W}},\Omega)
. Then the Fourier orthogonal
expansion of
f
is defined by

f=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{n}\hat{f}_{j,n}{\mathsf{P}}_{j,n},\quad\hbox{where}\quad\hat{f}_{j,n}={\langle}f,{\mathsf{P}}_{j,n}{\rangle}_{{\mathsf{W}}}.

The reproducing kernel
{\mathsf{P}}_{n}({\mathsf{W}};\cdot,\cdot)
of the space
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathsf{W}},\Omega)
is defined by

(2.2)

{\mathsf{P}}_{n}({\mathsf{W}};{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}})=\sum_{j=0}^{n}{\mathsf{P}}_{j,n}({\mathbf{u}}){\mathsf{P}}_{j,n}({\mathbf{v}}),

which is the kernel of the projection operator
\operatorname{proj}_{n}({\mathsf{W}}):L^{2}({\mathsf{W}},\Omega)\mapsto{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathsf{W}},\Omega)
,

\operatorname{proj}_{n}({\mathsf{W}};f,{\mathbf{u}})={\mathsf{b}}_{\mathsf{W}}\int_{\Omega}f({\mathbf{v}}){\mathsf{P}}_{n}({\mathsf{W}};{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}}){\mathsf{W}}({\mathbf{v}})\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{v}}.

For regular
{\mathsf{W}}
, the Fourier orthogonal series satisifes

L^{2}({\mathsf{W}},\Omega)=\bigoplus_{n=0}^{\infty}{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathsf{W}},\Omega):\quad f=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\operatorname{proj}_{n}({\mathsf{W}};f).

We are interested in the cases when an orthogonal basis can be written explicitly in terms of classical OPs of
one variable. Among various classes of such families (cf.
[
5
,
7
]
), we single out two examples, one on the
triangle and the other on the disk, which will play essential roles in our study in this paper.

2.1.1.
Classical OPs on triangle

On the triangle

\triangle=\{(u,v):u\geq 0,v\geq 0,u+v\leq 1\},

the classical Jacobi weight function
{\mathsf{W}}_{{\alpha}}^{\triangle}
is defined by, for
{\alpha}=({\alpha}_{1},{\alpha}_{2},{\alpha}_{3})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
,

(2.3)

{\mathsf{W}}^{{\alpha}}_{\triangle}(u,v)=u^{{\alpha}_{1}}v^{{\alpha}_{2}}(1-u-v)^{{\alpha}_{3}},\quad{\alpha}_{i}>-1.

The normalization constant
{\mathsf{b}}^{\triangle}_{{\alpha}}
of this weight function is given by

(2.4)

{\mathsf{b}}^{\triangle}_{{\alpha}}=\left[\int_{\triangle}{\mathsf{W}}^{{\alpha}}_{\triangle}(u,v)\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}v\right]^{-1}=\frac{\Gamma(|{\alpha}|+3)}{\Gamma({\alpha}_{1}+1)\Gamma({\alpha}_{2}+1)\Gamma({\alpha}_{3}+1)},

where
|{\alpha}|:={\alpha}_{1}+{\alpha}_{2}+{\alpha}_{3}
. OPs for this weight function can be given explicitly via the Jacobi polynomials

P_{n}^{{\alpha},b)}
of one variable,
which are orthogonal with respect to the weight function

w_{a,b}(t)=(1-t)^{a}(1+t)^{b},\qquad a,b>-1

on the interval
[-1,1]
. In particular an explicit orthogonal basis of
{\mathcal{V}}_{m}({\mathsf{W}}_{{\alpha}}^{\triangle},\triangle)
consists of
polynomials
\{{\mathsf{T}}_{j,m}^{{\alpha}}:0\leq j\leq m\}
, where
[
5
, Section 2.4]

(2.5)

{\mathsf{T}}_{j,m}^{{\alpha}}(u,v)=P_{m-j}^{(2j+{\alpha}_{1}+{\alpha}_{3}+1,{\alpha}_{2})}(2v-1)(1-v)^{j}P_{j}^{({\alpha}_{3},{\alpha}_{1})}\left(\frac{2u}{1-v}-1\right).

These are classical orthogonal polynomials in two variables and possess many interesting properties. We mention
two of them. The first one is a closed-form formula for the reproducing kernel
{\mathsf{P}}_{n}({\mathsf{W}}^{{\alpha}}_{\triangle};\cdot,\cdot)
,

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}}_{n}\left({\mathsf{W}}^{{\alpha}}_{\triangle};{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}}\right)=\,

\displaystyle c_{\alpha}\int_{[-1,1]^{3}}Z_{2n}^{|{\alpha}|+2}\left(\sqrt{u_{1}}\sqrt{v_{1}}\,t_{1}+\sqrt{u_{2}}\sqrt{v_{2}}\,t_{2}+\sqrt{1-|{\mathbf{u}}|}\sqrt{1-|{\mathbf{v}}}\,t_{3}\right)

(2.6)

\displaystyle\times(1+t_{1})(1-t_{1}^{2})^{{\alpha}_{1}-\frac{1}{2}}(1+t_{2})(1-t_{2}^{2})^{{\alpha}_{2}-\frac{1}{2}}(1-t_{3}^{2})^{{\alpha}_{3}-\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{t}},

where
Z_{n}^{\lambda}
is defined in terms of the Gegenbauer polynomial
C_{n}^{\lambda}
,

(2.7)

Z_{n}^{\lambda}=\frac{n+{\lambda}}{{\lambda}}C_{n}^{\lambda}(t),

and
c_{\alpha}=c_{{\alpha}_{1}}c_{{\alpha}_{2}}c_{{\alpha}_{3}}
with
c_{\alpha}=\int_{-1}^{1}(1-t^{2})^{{\alpha}-\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}t
. The identity (
2.1.1
) holds
for
{\alpha}_{i}\geq-\frac{1}{2}
with the understanding that, if one of more
{\alpha}_{i}=-\frac{1}{2}
, then it holds under the limit

(2.8)

\lim_{a\to-\frac{1}{2}+}c_{\alpha}\int_{-1}^{1}f(t)(1-t^{2})^{{\alpha}-\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}t=\frac{f(1)+f(-1)}{2}.

The second one states that these OPs are eigenfunctions of a second-order differential operator defined by

(2.9)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}}^{{\alpha}}_{\triangle}:=\,

\displaystyle u(1-u)\partial_{uu}-2uv\partial_{uv}+v(1-v)\partial_{vv}

\displaystyle+\,

\displaystyle({\alpha}_{1}+1-(|{\alpha}|+3)u)\partial_{u}+({\alpha}_{2}+1-(|{\alpha}|+3)v)\partial_{v}.

More precisely,

(2.10)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}}^{{\alpha}}_{\triangle}Y=-n(n+|{\alpha}|+2)Y,\qquad Y\in{\mathcal{V}}_{m}\Big({\mathsf{W}}^{{\alpha}}_{\triangle},\triangle\Big).

2.1.2.
Semi-classical OPs on the unit disk

On the unit disk
{\mathbb{B}}^{2}=\{(u,v):u^{2}+v^{2}\leq 1\}
, we consider the weight function

(2.11)

{\mathsf{W}}^{{\kappa}}_{\mathbb{B}}(u,v)=|u|^{2{\kappa}_{1}}|v|^{2{\kappa}_{2}}\left(1-u^{2}-v^{2}\right)^{{\kappa}_{3}},\quad{\kappa}_{1},{\kappa}_{2}>-\tfrac{1}{2},\,\,{\kappa}_{3}>-1.

In the case of
{\kappa}_{1}={\kappa}_{2}=0
, they are the classical OPs, which have been extensively studied. OPs for

{\mathsf{W}}^{\kappa}_{\mathbb{B}}
can be given in terms of the generalized Gegenbauer polynomials
C_{n}^{({\lambda},\mu)}
that are
orthogonal with respect to the weight function

g_{{\lambda},\mu}(t)=|t|^{2\mu}(1-t^{2})^{{\lambda}-\frac{1}{2}},\qquad\mu,{\lambda}>-\tfrac{1}{2}

on
[-1,1]
, which reduce to the classical Gegenbauer polynomials
C_{n}^{\lambda}
when
\mu=0
.
An explicit orthogonal basis of
{\mathcal{V}}_{m}({\mathsf{W}}^{{\kappa}}_{\mathbb{B}},{\mathbb{B}}^{2})
consists of
polynomials
\{{\mathsf{G}}_{j,n}^{{\kappa}}:0\leq j\leq n\}
, where
[
5
]

(2.12)

{\mathsf{G}}_{j,n}^{\kappa}(u,v)=C_{n-j}^{(j+{\kappa}_{1}+{\kappa}_{3}+1,{\kappa}_{2})}(v)(1-v^{2})^{\frac{j}{2}}C_{j}^{({\kappa}_{3}+\frac{1}{2},{\kappa}_{1})}\bigg(\frac{u}{\sqrt{1-v^{2}}}\bigg).

These polynomials share analogs of the two properties we cited for OPs on the triangle. The first is
a closed-form formula for the reproducing kernel
{\mathsf{P}}_{n}({\mathsf{W}}^{\kappa}_{\mathbb{B}})
of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathsf{W}}^{\kappa}_{\mathbb{B}},{\mathbb{B}}^{2})

given by
[
5
, Theorem 8.1.16]

(2.13)

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}}_{n}({\mathsf{W}}^{\kappa}_{\mathbb{B}};{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}})=

\displaystyle c_{\kappa}\int_{[-1,1]^{3}}Z_{n}^{|{\kappa}|+1}\left(u_{1}v_{1}t_{1}+u_{2}v_{2}t_{2}+\sqrt{1-|{\mathbf{u}}|^{2}}\sqrt{1-|{\mathbf{v}}|^{2}}\,t_{3}\right)

\displaystyle\times(1+t_{1})(1+t_{2})(1-t_{1}^{2})^{{\kappa}_{1}-1}(1-t_{2}^{2})^{{\kappa}_{2}-1}(1-t_{3}^{2})^{{\kappa}_{3}-\frac{1}{2}}\mathrm{d}t,

which holds for
{\kappa}_{1},{\kappa}_{2}\geq 0
and
{\kappa}_{3}>-\frac{1}{2}
and under the limit (
2.8
) when needed.

The second is a second-order differential-difference operator,
{\mathcal{D}}_{\kappa}
, defined by

(2.14)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}}^{\kappa}_{\mathbb{B}}=(1-u^{2})\partial_{uu}

\displaystyle\,-2uv\partial_{uv}+(1-v^{2})\partial_{vv}-(2|{\kappa}|+3)(u\partial_{u}+v\partial_{v})

\displaystyle+{\kappa}_{1}\left(\frac{2}{u}\partial_{u}-\frac{1-\sigma_{1}}{u^{2}}\right)+{\kappa}_{2}\left(\frac{2}{v}\partial_{v}-\frac{1-\sigma_{2}}{v^{2}}\right),

where
|{\kappa}|={\kappa}_{1}+{\kappa}_{2}+{\kappa}_{3}
, and
\sigma_{i}
denotes the reflection operator defined by

\sigma_{1}f(u,v)=f(-u,v)\quad\hbox{and}\quad\sigma_{2}f(u,v)=f(u,-v),

that has OPs for
{\mathsf{W}}_{\kappa}^{\mathbb{B}}
as eigenfunctions; more precisely,

(2.15)

{\mathcal{D}}^{\kappa}_{\mathbb{B}}Y=-n(n+2|{\kappa}|+2)Y,\qquad Y\in{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathsf{W}}^{\kappa}_{\mathbb{B}},{\mathbb{B}}^{2}).

This is stated in
[
5
, Theorem 8.1.3]
in terms of the Dunkl Laplacian
\Delta_{h}
with
d=2
, which is defined in

[
5
, (8.1.6)]
. In particular, if
{\kappa}_{1}={\kappa}_{2}=0
, then
{\mathcal{D}}^{\kappa}_{\mathbb{B}}
reduces to the second-order differential operator
for classical OPs on the disk.

It is worth mentioning that, according to the classification in
[
8
]
, there exists a second-order linear differential
operator with polynomial coefficients having OPs in two variables as eigenfunctions, up to an affine transform,
only in five cases, three are cross products of Laguerre and Hermite polynomials, and the other two are the classical
OPs on the triangle and the unit disk given above. The classification does not cover the semi-classical OPs on the disk,
for which the operator contains a difference part.

2.2.
Fully symmetric OPs in two variables

We are interested in OPs when the domain and the weight function are fully symmetric.

Definition 2.1
.

The domain
\Omega
is called fully symmetric if
(u,v)\in\Omega
implies
(\pm u,\pm v)\in\Omega
, and
the weight function
{\mathsf{W}}
defined on such a domain is called fully symmetric if
{\mathsf{W}}(\pm u,\pm v)={\mathsf{W}}(u,v)
for
(u,v)\in\Omega
.

2.2.1.
Structure of fully symmetric OPs

A fully symmetric weight
{\mathsf{W}}
is determined by its values on the positive quadrant

\Omega_{+,+}=\{(u,v)\in\Omega:u\geq 0,v\geq 0\}
and it is even in both variables. Hence, it can be written as

(2.16)

{\mathsf{W}}(u,v)={\mathsf{w}}\left(u^{2},v^{2}\right),\quad(u,v)\in\Omega,

where the weight function
{\mathsf{w}}
is defined on the domain

(2.17)

\sqrt{\Omega}=\left\{(s,t):s=\sqrt{u},\,t=\sqrt{v},\,\,(u,v)\in\Omega_{+,+}\right\}.

OPs for a fully symmetric weight function can be derived by four families of OPs on
\sqrt{\Omega}
that
are orthogonal with respect to
{\mathsf{w}}_{\pm\frac{1}{2},\pm\frac{1}{2}}
defined by

(2.18)

{\mathsf{w}}_{\pm\frac{1}{2}}(s,t)=s^{\pm\frac{1}{2}}t^{\pm\frac{1}{2}}{\mathsf{w}}(s,t),\qquad(s,t)\in\sqrt{\Omega}.

Moreover, OPs in the space
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathsf{W}},\Omega)
inherit symmetry that can be described by

{\mathcal{V}}_{2m}({\mathsf{W}},\Omega)={\mathcal{V}}_{2m}^{{\mathsf{E}},{\mathsf{E}}}({\mathsf{W}},\Omega)\cup{\mathcal{V}}_{2m}^{{\mathsf{O}},{\mathsf{O}}}({\mathsf{W}},\Omega)\,\,\hbox{and}\,\,{\mathcal{V}}_{2m+1}({\mathsf{W}},\Omega)={\mathcal{V}}_{2m+1}^{{\mathsf{E}},{\mathsf{O}}}({\mathsf{W}},\Omega)\cup{\mathcal{V}}_{2m+1}^{{\mathsf{O}},{\mathsf{E}}}({\mathsf{W}},\Omega),

where
{\mathcal{V}}_{2m}^{{\mathsf{E}},{\mathsf{E}}}({\mathsf{W}},\Omega)
consists of OPs even in both variables in
{\mathcal{V}}_{2m}({\mathsf{W}},\Omega)
,
and
{\mathcal{V}}_{2m+1}^{{\mathsf{E}},{\mathsf{O}}}({\mathsf{W}},\Omega)
consists of OPs even in the first variable and odd for the second
variable in
{\mathcal{V}}_{2m+1}({\mathsf{W}},\Omega)
, for example. The other cases are defined similarly.

Let
{\mathsf{b}}({\mathsf{w}})
denote the normalization of
{\mathsf{w}}
. Then it is easy to see that
{\mathsf{b}}({\mathsf{w}}_{-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2}})={\mathsf{b}}_{\mathsf{W}}
for
{\mathsf{W}}

in (
2.16
).

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
\Omega
and
{\mathsf{W}}
be fully symmetric. Let
\{{\mathsf{P}}_{j,m}\big({\mathsf{w}}_{\pm\frac{1}{2},\pm\frac{1}{2}}\big):0\leq j\leq m\}

be an orthonormal basis under the inner product (
2.1
) defined via

{\mathsf{w}}_{\pm\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2}}
on
\sqrt{\Omega}
, Then

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}}_{j,2m}^{{\mathsf{E}},{\mathsf{E}}}({\mathsf{W}};u,v)

\displaystyle={\mathsf{P}}_{j,m}\left({\mathsf{w}}_{-\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2}};u^{2},v^{2}\right),\quad 0\leq j\leq m,

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}}_{j,2m}^{{\mathsf{O}},{\mathsf{O}}}({\mathsf{W}};u,v)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{{\mathsf{b}}\big({\mathsf{w}}_{\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}}\big)}}{\sqrt{{\mathsf{b}}_{\mathsf{W}}}}uv{\mathsf{P}}_{j,m-1}\left({\mathsf{w}}_{\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}};u^{2},v^{2}\right),\quad 0\leq j\leq m-1

consist of an orthonormal basis for
{\mathcal{V}}_{2m}^{{\mathsf{E}},{\mathsf{E}}}({\mathsf{W}},\Omega)
and
{\mathcal{V}}_{2m}^{{\mathsf{O}},{\mathsf{O}}}({\mathsf{W}},\Omega)
respectively,
and

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}}_{j,2m+1}^{{\mathsf{E}},{\mathsf{O}}}({\mathsf{W}};u,v)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{{\mathsf{b}}\big({\mathsf{w}}_{-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}}\big)}}{\sqrt{{\mathsf{b}}_{\mathsf{W}}}}v{\mathsf{P}}_{j,m}\left({\mathsf{w}}_{-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}};u^{2},v^{2}\right),\quad 0\leq j\leq m,

\displaystyle{\mathsf{P}}_{j,2m+1}^{{\mathsf{O}},{\mathsf{E}}}({\mathsf{W}};u,v)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{{\mathsf{b}}\big({\mathsf{w}}_{\frac{1}{2},-\frac{1}{2}}\big)}}{\sqrt{{\mathsf{b}}_{\mathsf{W}}}}u{\mathsf{P}}_{j,m}\left({\mathsf{w}}_{-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}};u^{2},v^{2}\right),\quad 0\leq j\leq m.

consist of an orthonormal basis for
{\mathcal{V}}_{2m+1}^{{\mathsf{E}},{\mathsf{O}}}({\mathsf{W}},\Omega)
and
{\mathcal{V}}_{2m+1}^{{\mathsf{O}},{\mathsf{E}}}({\mathsf{W}},\Omega)

respectively.

Proof.

The orthogonality of these polynomials follows from parity and simple change of variables; see
[
19
, Theorem 4.2]
.
To see the polynomials are orthonormal, consider
{\mathsf{P}}_{2m}^{{\mathsf{E}},{\mathsf{O}}}({\mathsf{W}};u,v)
as an example. Let

\Omega_{+,+}=\{(u,v)\in\Omega:u\geq 0,v\geq 0\}
. By symmetry and changing variables
s=u^{2}
and
t=v^{2}
,
we obtain

\displaystyle\Big\langle{\mathsf{P}}_{2m+1}^{{\mathsf{E}},{\mathsf{O}}}({\mathsf{W}}),{\mathsf{P}}_{2m+1}^{{\mathsf{E}},{\mathsf{O}}}({\mathsf{W}})\Big\rangle_{{\mathsf{W}}}

\displaystyle=4{\mathsf{b}}\big({\mathsf{w}}_{-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}}\big)\int_{\Omega_{+,+}}v^{2}\Big|{\mathsf{P}}_{j,m}\Big({\mathsf{w}}_{-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}};u^{2},v^{2}\Big)\Big|^{2}{\mathsf{w}}\big(u^{2},v^{2}\big)\mathrm{d}u\mathrm{d}v

\displaystyle={\mathsf{b}}\big({\mathsf{w}}_{-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}}\big)\int_{\sqrt{\Omega}}s^{-\frac{1}{2}}t^{\frac{1}{2}}\Big|{\mathsf{P}}_{j,m}\Big({\mathsf{w}}_{-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}};s,t\Big)\Big|^{2}{\mathsf{w}}(s,t)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\Big\langle{\mathsf{P}}_{j,m}\Big({\mathsf{w}}_{-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}}\Big),{\mathsf{P}}_{j,m}\Big({\mathsf{w}}_{-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}})\Big\rangle_{{\mathsf{w}}_{-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}}}=1.

A similar proof works for the norms of
{\mathsf{P}}_{2m+1}^{{\mathsf{O}},{\mathsf{E}}}({\mathsf{W}})
and
{\mathsf{P}}_{2m+1}^{{\mathsf{O}},{\mathsf{O}}}({\mathsf{W}})
.
∎

We are particularly interested in the subspace
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{\circ,{\mathsf{E}}}
that consists of OPs even in the second variable in

{\mathcal{V}}_{n}
, and the subspace
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{{\mathsf{E}},\circ}
that consists of OPs even in the first variable in
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}
. By definition,

{\mathcal{V}}_{2m}^{\circ,{\mathsf{E}}}({\mathsf{W}},\Omega)={\mathcal{V}}_{2m}^{{\mathsf{E}},{\mathsf{E}}}({\mathsf{W}},\Omega)\quad\hbox{and}\quad{\mathcal{V}}_{2m+1}^{\circ,{\mathsf{E}}}({\mathsf{W}},\Omega)={\mathcal{V}}_{2m+1}^{{\mathsf{O}},{\mathsf{E}}}({\mathsf{W}},\Omega),

and

{\mathcal{V}}_{2m}^{{\mathsf{E}},\circ}({\mathsf{W}},\Omega)={\mathcal{V}}_{2m}^{{\mathsf{E}},{\mathsf{E}}}({\mathsf{W}},\Omega)\quad\hbox{and}\quad{\mathcal{V}}_{2m+1}^{{\mathsf{E}},\circ}({\mathsf{W}},\Omega)={\mathcal{V}}_{2m+1}^{{\mathsf{E}},{\mathsf{O}}}({\mathsf{W}},\Omega),

In particular, it follows that

\dim{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{\circ,{\mathsf{E}}}=\dim{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{{\mathsf{E}},\circ}=\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor+1.

Because of symmetry, we shall consider mainly the space
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{\circ,{\mathsf{E}}}({\mathsf{W}})
in the following.

Although the space
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{\circ,{\mathsf{E}}}
consists of only half of the OPs in
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}
, it is nevertheless sufficient
for the Fourier orthogonal expansions and approximation on the domain

(2.19)

\Lambda:=\Omega^{\circ,{\mathsf{E}}}:=\{(u,v)\in\Omega:v\geq 0\},

which is the upper half of the fully symmetric domain
\Omega
, equipped with the weight function
{\mathsf{W}}
.
Let
{\mathsf{P}}_{n}^{\circ,{\mathsf{E}}}({\mathsf{W}};\cdot,\cdot)
be the reproducing kernels of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{\circ,{\mathsf{E}}}
. By definition,

{\mathsf{P}}_{n}^{\circ,{\mathsf{E}}}({\mathsf{W}};{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}})=\sum_{j=0}^{\left\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\right\rfloor+1}{\mathsf{Q}}_{j,n}({\mathbf{u}}){\mathsf{Q}}_{j,n}({\mathbf{v}}),

where

consists of an orthonormal basis of

. Moreover, if
f
is even in its
second variable, then its orthogonal projection operator

\operatorname{proj}_{n}^{\circ,{\mathsf{E}}}:L^{2}({\mathsf{W}},\Lambda)\mapsto{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{\circ,{\mathsf{E}}}

satisfies the relation

\operatorname{proj}_{n}^{\circ,{\mathsf{E}}}({\mathsf{W}};f,{\mathbf{u}})={\mathsf{b}}_{\mathsf{W}}\int_{\Lambda}f({\mathbf{v}}){\mathsf{P}}_{n}^{\circ,{\mathsf{E}}}({\mathsf{W}};{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}}){\mathsf{W}}({\mathbf{v}})\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{v}}.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
\Omega
and
{\mathsf{W}}
be fully symmetric. If
f\in L^{2}({\mathsf{W}},\Lambda)
, then

(2.20)

f=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\operatorname{proj}_{n}^{\circ,{\mathsf{E}}}({\mathsf{W}};f).

Proof.

The kernel
{\mathsf{P}}_{n}^{\circ,{\mathsf{E}}}({\mathsf{W}})
can be written in terms of the reproducing kernel
{\mathsf{P}}_{n}({\mathsf{W}})
of
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathsf{W}})
as

(2.21)

{\mathsf{P}}_{n}^{\circ,{\mathsf{E}}}({\mathsf{W}};{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}})=\frac{1}{2}\left[{\mathsf{P}}_{n}\big({\mathsf{W}};(u_{1},u_{2}),(v_{1},v_{2})\big)+{\mathsf{P}}_{n}\big({\mathsf{W}};(u_{1},u_{2}),(v_{1},-v_{2})\big)\right],

which follows since the integral kernel in the right-hand side reproduces all polynomials in
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}({\mathsf{W}},\Omega)
that
are even in the second variable. For
f
defined on
\Lambda
, we can extend it to the fully symmetric domain

\Omega
by defining
f(u,-v)=f(u,v)
for
(u,v)\in\Lambda
. Evidently,
f\in L^{2}(\Lambda,{\mathsf{W}})
is equivalent to

f\in L^{2}({\mathsf{W}},\Omega)
. For the extended

on
\Omega
, the identity (
2.21
) leads to the relation

\displaystyle\operatorname{proj}_{n}^{\circ,{\mathsf{E}}}({\mathsf{W}};f,{\mathbf{u}})\,

\displaystyle=\frac{{\mathsf{b}}_{\mathsf{W}}}{2}\int_{\Lambda}f({\mathbf{v}})\frac{1}{2}\left[{\mathsf{P}}_{n}\big({\mathsf{W}};{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}}\big)+{\mathsf{P}}_{n}\big({\mathsf{W}};{\mathbf{u}},(v_{1},-v_{2})\big)\right]{\mathsf{W}}({\mathbf{v}})\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{v}}

\displaystyle={\mathsf{b}}_{\mathsf{W}}\int_{\Omega}f({\mathbf{v}}){\mathsf{P}}_{n}\big({\mathsf{W}};{\mathbf{u}},{\mathbf{v}}\big){\mathsf{W}}({\mathbf{v}})\mathrm{d}{\mathbf{v}}=\operatorname{proj}_{n}({\mathsf{W}};f,{\mathbf{u}}).

Hence, the stated identity is simply the restriction of the Fourier orthogonal expansion to
f
that is even
in the second variable.
∎

Remark 2.1
.

The space

contains a basis of OPs, so that it has the Fourier orthogonal expansion according to
the decomposition

The orthogonal expansion stated in the theorem, however, is different. It is the restriction of the Fourier orthogonal

can be constructed, whereas such a basis is not available for

Proof.

\displaystyle\partial_{1}f(u,{\mathfrak{t}}(u,v))\,

3.2.
Approximation on the curved domain

Based on the discussion in the previous section and Theorem

by polynomials as examples.

Since
{\kappa}_{1}=0

\Lambda_{{\mathfrak{a}},{\mathfrak{b}},{\mathfrak{c}}}
, we replace
{\kappa}
by
({\beta},{\gamma})
for
{\beta}>-\frac{1}{2}

{\gamma}>-1
in
this subsection. Let
\Pi_{n}^{\circ,{\mathsf{E}}}
be the space of polynomials of degree at most

The Fourier orthogonal series (
2.20

For

E_{n}(f)_{L^{p}\big({\mathsf{W}}_{{\mathfrak{a}},{\mathfrak{b}},{\mathfrak{c}}}^{{\beta},{\gamma}},\Lambda_{{\mathfrak{a}},{\mathfrak{b}},{\mathfrak{c}}}\big)}=\inf_{g\in\Pi_{n}^{\circ,{\mathsf{E}}}}\|f-g\|_{L^{p}\big({\mathsf{W}}_{{\mathfrak{a}},{\mathfrak{b}},{\mathfrak{c}}}^{{\beta},{\gamma}},\Lambda_{{\mathfrak{a}},{\mathfrak{b}},{\mathfrak{c}}}\big)},

r
-th continuous

({\mathfrak{D}}_{{\mathfrak{a}},{\mathfrak{b}},{\mathfrak{c}}}^{{\beta},{\gamma}})^{\frac{r}{2}}\operatorname{proj}_{n}^{\circ,{\mathsf{E}}}({\mathsf{W}}_{{\mathfrak{a}},{\mathfrak{b}},{\mathfrak{c}}}^{{\beta},{\gamma}};f)=-n(n+2{\beta}+2{\gamma}+2)^{\frac{1}{2}}\operatorname{proj}_{n}^{\circ,{\mathsf{E}}}({\mathsf{W}}_{{\mathfrak{a}},{\mathfrak{b}},{\mathfrak{c}}}^{{\beta},{\gamma}};f)

for all

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
{\beta}\geq 0
,
{\gamma}\geq-\frac{1}{2}
, and

,
and

and

(ii)

K_{r}(f;n^{-1})_{p,{\mathsf{W}}_{{\mathfrak{a}},{\mathfrak{b}},{\mathfrak{c}}}^{{\beta},{\gamma}}}\leq c\,n^{-r}\sum_{k=0}^{n}(k+1)^{r-1}E_{k}(f)_{L^{p}\big({\mathsf{W}}_{{\mathfrak{a}},{\mathfrak{b}},{\mathfrak{c}}}^{{\beta},{\gamma}},\Lambda_{{\mathfrak{a}},{\mathfrak{b}},{\mathfrak{c}}}\big)}.

Proof.

This follows from the corresponding result on the unit ball established in
[

, for which the
stated characterization holds for

K_{r}(f;\rho)_{p,{\mathsf{W}}_{\mathbb{B}}^{\kappa}}=\inf_{g\in C^{r}({\mathbb{B}})}\left\{\|f-g\|_{L^{p}({\mathsf{W}}_{\mathbb{B}}^{\kappa},{\mathbb{B}}^{2})}+\rho^{r}\left\|({\mathcal{D}}_{\mathbb{B}}^{\kappa})^{\frac{r}{2}}g\right\|_{L^{p}({\mathsf{W}}_{\mathbb{B}}^{\kappa},{\mathbb{B}}^{2})}\right\}

follows from the corresponding result for functions that are even on the unit disk. This applies to many other

[

]

[

We end this section by specializing our setup to the special case of
{\mathfrak{a}}=0
,
{\mathfrak{b}}=1
, and

.
In this case, we denote

The weight funciton

By the result established in the previous subsection, OPs that consist of an orthogonal basis for

are

4.
Approximation and OPs on domains of revolution

We consider OPs on domains defined via a rotation around an axis. The simplest example of such a
domain is the unit ball in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. OPs on the unit ball have been studied extensively; we review
relevant results that we shall need in the first subsection. Our goal is to consider OPs on a class of
domains arising from the rotation of a fully symmetric domain, studied in
[
19
]
, which we recall
in the second subsection. We show that these families of OPs can be related to OPs on the unit
ball, as an extension of the results in the previous section, in the third section.

4.1.
OPs on the unit ball

The classical weight function
W_{\mu}
on the unit ball
{\mathbb{B}}^{d}
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is defined by

W_{\gamma}({\mathbf{x}})=(1-\|{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2})^{{\gamma}},\quad{\gamma}>-1.

An orthogonal basis for
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}(W_{\gamma},{\mathbb{B}}^{d})
can be given in terms of the Jacobi polynomials and
spherical harmonics. The latter are the restrictions of homogeneous harmonic polynomials on the unit
sphere
{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
, and they are OPs on
{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
. Let
{\mathcal{H}}_{n}^{d}
be the space of spherical harmonics of degree

n

d

(4.1)

\dim{\mathcal{H}}_{n}^{d}=\binom{n+d-1}{n}-\binom{n+d-3}{n-2}.

Spherical harmonics of different degrees are orthogonal in
L^{2}({\mathbb{S}^{d-1}})
. More precisely, let
\{Y_{\ell}^{n}\}

be an orthonormal basis of
{\mathcal{H}}_{n}^{d}
. Then,

\frac{1}{{\omega}_{d}}\int_{{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}Y_{\ell}^{n}(\xi)Y_{\ell^{\prime}}^{n^{\prime}}(\xi)\mathrm{d}\sigma(\xi)=\delta_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}\delta_{n,n^{\prime}},

where
\mathrm{d}\sigma
denotes the surface measure
\mathrm{d}\sigma
on
{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
and
{\omega}_{d}
denotes the surface area of
{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}
.
An orthogonal basis of
{\mathcal{H}}_{n}^{d}
can be given in terms of Jacobi polynomials in spherical coordinates.

Let
\{Y_{\ell}^{n-2m}:1\leq\ell\leq\dim{\mathcal{H}}_{n-2m}\}
be an orthonormal basis of
{\mathcal{H}}_{n-2m}^{d}
for
0\leq m\leq n/2
.
Define
[
5
, (5.2.4)]

(4.2)

{\mathbf{P}}_{\ell,m}^{n}({\mathbf{W}}_{\gamma};{\mathbf{x}})=P_{m}^{({\gamma},n-2m+\frac{d-2}{2})}\left(2\|{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}-1\right)Y_{\ell}^{n-2m}({\mathbf{x}}).

Then
\{{\mathbf{P}}_{\ell,m}^{n}({\mathbf{W}}_{\gamma}):0\leq m\leq n/2,1\leq\ell\leq\dim{\mathcal{H}}_{n-2m}\}
is an orthogonal basis of

{\mathcal{V}}_{n}^{d}(W_{\gamma},{\mathbb{B}}^{d})
. OPs in
{\mathcal{V}}_{n}(W_{\mu},{\mathbb{B}}^{d})
are eigenfunctions of a second-order differential operator
defined by

{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathbb{B}}^{\gamma}:=\Delta-{\langle}x,\nabla{\rangle}^{2}-(2{\gamma}+d){\langle}x,\nabla{\rangle};

For our purpose, we need to consider another class of OPs on the unit ball with respect to the
weight function

(4.3)

{\mathbf{W}}_{\mathbb{B}}^{{\beta},{\gamma}}({\mathbf{y}})=|y_{d+1}|^{2{\beta}}(1-\|{\mathbf{y}}\|^{2})^{\gamma},\qquad{\mathbf{y}}\in{\mathbb{B}}^{d+1},

which we state on the ball

instead of

in comparison with the notation in the next subsection.
This is a special case of the weight function

invariant under the group

[

Instead of going through the tedious reduction from the existing basis (cf.

Proposition 4.1

\{{\mathbf{Q}}_{\ell,j,k}^{n}({\mathbf{W}}_{\mathbb{B}}^{{\beta},{\gamma}}):1\leq\ell\leq\dim{\mathcal{H}}_{n-2j}^{d},0\leq m\leq k/2,0\leq k\leq n\}

\displaystyle{\langle}{\mathbf{Q}}_{k}^{n},{\mathbf{Q}}_{k^{\prime},n^{\prime}}^{{\beta},{\gamma}}{\rangle}_{{\beta},{\gamma}}\,

\displaystyle=\delta_{k,k^{\prime}}\int_{-1}^{1}p_{n-k}(t)p_{n^{\prime}-k}(t)|t|^{2{\beta}}(1-t^{2})^{{\gamma}+\frac{d}{2}}\mathrm{d}t,

so that the orthogonality holds with

being a generalized Gegenbauer polynomial given by

\displaystyle\partial_{d+1}{\langle}{\mathbf{u}},\nabla_{d}{\rangle}f\big({\mathbf{u}},{\mathfrak{t}}(\|{\mathbf{u}}\|,v)\big)\,

\displaystyle\quad-({\mathfrak{a}}-{\mathfrak{c}})({\mathfrak{b}}-{\mathfrak{a}}){\mathfrak{t}}(\|{\mathbf{u}}\|,v)\|{\mathbf{u}}\|^{2}\left(\frac{1}{v^{2}}\partial_{v}^{2}+\frac{1}{v^{3}}\partial_{v}\right)\bigg]F({\mathbf{u}},v),

\displaystyle{\langle}{\mathbf{u}},\nabla_{d}{\rangle}^{2}f\big({\mathbf{u}},{\mathfrak{t}}(\|{\mathbf{u}}\|,v)\big)\,

\displaystyle=\bigg[{\langle}{\mathbf{u}},\nabla_{\mathbf{u}}{\rangle}^{2}-({\mathfrak{a}}-{\mathfrak{c}})\|{\mathbf{u}}\|^{2}\Big(\frac{1}{v}\partial_{v}{\langle}{\mathbf{u}},\nabla_{\mathbf{u}}{\rangle}+\frac{\|{\mathbf{u}}\|^{2}}{v^{2}}\partial_{v}^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\quad-({\mathfrak{a}}-{\mathfrak{c}})\|{\mathbf{u}}\|^{2}\left[({\mathfrak{a}}-{\mathfrak{c}})\|{\mathbf{u}}\|^{2}+2v^{2}\right]\frac{1}{v^{3}}\partial_{v}\bigg]F({\mathbf{u}},v).

These computations are straightforward but tedious. Using them and other likewise identities on the right-hand side of (
4.14
), we obtain, after a careful simplification, the stated result.
∎

f
on

.

Theorem 4.8
.

Let
{\beta}\geq 0
and
{\gamma}>-1

(4.17)

f=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\operatorname{proj}_{n}^{\circ,{\mathsf{E}}}\left({\mathbf{W}}_{{\mathfrak{a}},{\mathfrak{b}},{\mathfrak{c}}}^{{\beta},{\gamma}},f\right).

Let
\Pi_{n}^{\circ,{\mathsf{E}}}
denote the space of polynomials at most
n

variables that are even in

For
f\in L^{p}({\mathbf{W}}_{{\mathfrak{a}},{\mathfrak{b}},{\mathfrak{c}}}^{{\beta},{\gamma}},\Lambda_{{\mathfrak{a}},{\mathfrak{b}},{\mathfrak{c}}}^{d+1})

where the space becomes

if

{\mathbf{K}}_{r}(f;\rho)_{p,{\mathbf{W}}_{{\mathfrak{a}},{\mathfrak{b}},{\mathfrak{c}}}^{{\beta},{\gamma}}}=\inf_{g}\left\{\|f-g\|_{L^{p}\big({\mathbf{W}}_{{\mathfrak{a}},{\mathfrak{b}},{\mathfrak{c}}}^{{\beta},{\gamma}},\Lambda_{{\mathfrak{a}},{\mathfrak{b}},{\mathfrak{c}}}^{d+1}\big)}+\rho^{r}\left\|\big({\mathfrak{D}}_{{\mathfrak{a}},{\mathfrak{b}},{\mathfrak{c}}}^{{\beta},{\gamma}}\big)^{\frac{r}{2}}g\right\|_{L^{p}\big({\mathbf{W}}_{{\mathfrak{a}},{\mathfrak{b}},{\mathfrak{c}}}^{{\beta},{\gamma}},\Lambda_{{\mathfrak{a}},{\mathfrak{b}},{\mathfrak{c}}}^{d+1}\big)}\right\},

Theorem 4.9
.

Let
{\beta}\geq 0
,
{\gamma}>-1
, and
f\in L^{p}(\Lambda_{{\mathfrak{a}},{\mathfrak{b}},{\mathfrak{c}}}^{d+1},{\mathbf{W}}_{{\mathfrak{a}},{\mathfrak{b}},{\mathfrak{c}}}^{{\beta},{\gamma}})

and
f\in C(\Lambda_{{\mathfrak{a}},{\mathfrak{b}},{\mathfrak{c}}})

and

(i)

E_{n}(f)_{L^{p}\big({\mathbf{W}}_{{\mathfrak{a}},{\mathfrak{b}},{\mathfrak{c}}}^{{\beta},{\gamma}},\Lambda_{{\mathfrak{a}},{\mathfrak{b}},{\mathfrak{c}}}^{d+1}\big)}\leq c\,K_{r}(f;n^{-1})_{p,{\mathbf{W}}_{{\mathfrak{a}},{\mathfrak{b}},{\mathfrak{c}}}^{{\beta},{\gamma}}};

(ii)

K_{r}(f;n^{-1})_{p,{\mathbf{W}}_{{\mathfrak{a}},{\mathfrak{b}},{\mathfrak{c}}}^{{\beta},{\gamma}}}\leq c\,n^{-r}\sum_{k=0}^{n}(k+1)^{r-1}E_{k}(f)_{L^{p}({\mathbf{W}}_{{\mathfrak{a}},{\mathfrak{b}},{\mathfrak{c}}}^{{\beta},{\gamma}},\Lambda_{{\mathfrak{a}},{\mathfrak{b}},{\mathfrak{c}}}^{d+1})}.

The definitions of the two quantities in the theorem are verbatim extensions of the one in Section 3.2, and

, this theorem is established

[

]

for

Let
\widehat{a}\in C^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}_{+})
be a non-negative function on the real line such that

\widehat{a}(t)=1\,\,\hbox{if $0\leq t\leq 1$},\quad\hbox{and}\quad\widehat{a}(t)=0\,\,\hbox{if $t\geq 2$}.

For
X,Y\in\Lambda_{{\mathfrak{a}},{\mathfrak{b}},{\mathfrak{c}}}^{d+1}
and
{\gamma}>-1
, define the kernel

L_{n}\left({\mathbf{W}}_{{\mathfrak{a}},{\mathfrak{b}},{\mathfrak{c}}}^{0,{\gamma}};X,Y\right)=\sum_{k=0}^{2n}\widehat{a}\left(\frac{k}{n}\right){\mathbf{P}}_{k}\left({\mathbf{W}}_{{\mathfrak{a}},{\mathfrak{b}},{\mathfrak{c}}}^{0,{\gamma}};X,Y\right).

By (
4.15
) and the corresponding result on the unit ball
[
11
]
, this kernel is highly localized in the sense
that it decays almost exponentially if
X
and
Y
are away from the diagonal. The precise statement requires the

\Lambda_{{\mathfrak{a}},{\mathfrak{b}},{\mathfrak{c}}}^{d+1}
defined by

\mathrm{d}_{\Lambda_{{\mathfrak{a}},{\mathfrak{b}},{\mathfrak{c}}}}(X,Y)=\arccos\left({\langle}X,Y{\rangle}+\sqrt{1-\|{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}-{\mathfrak{t}}(\|{\mathbf{x}}\|,t)^{2}}\sqrt{1-\|{\mathbf{y}}\|^{2}-{\mathfrak{t}}(\|{\mathbf{y}}\|,s)^{2}}\right)

) and the distance

of

. The
inequalities that quantify the localization follow from the corresponding inequalities on the unit ball by the change

[
18
]
, which can now be avoided by appealing to the

The
K
-functional defined by the spectral operator in Theorem

has an equivalent modulus of

We shall not go in this direction since it involves another set of definitions and notations, and the result is essentially

To keep the notation simple, we consider the case
{\mathfrak{a}}=0
and
{\mathfrak{b}}=1
and denote

\Lambda_{\mathfrak{c}}^{d+1}=\Lambda_{0,1,{\mathfrak{c}}}^{d+1}\quad\hbox{and}\quad{\mathbf{W}}_{{\mathfrak{c}}}^{\gamma}={\mathbf{W}}_{0,1,{\mathfrak{c}}}^{0,{\gamma}}.

With
{\beta}=0
, the weight function
{\mathbf{W}}_{{\mathfrak{c}}}^{{\gamma}}({\mathbf{x}},t)
is rotationally invariant in
{\mathbf{x}}
. We introduce
the angular derivatives
D_{i,j}
defined by, for
{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}

D_{i,j}=x_{i}\partial_{j}-x_{j}\partial_{i},\qquad 1\leq i,j\leq d,

which is the angular derivative in the polar coordinates of the
(x_{i},x_{j})
plane. Let

\phi_{c}({\mathbf{x}},t)=\sqrt{1-(1-{\mathfrak{c}})t^{2}-\|{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}}

for
({\mathbf{x}},t)\in\Lambda_{\mathfrak{c}}^{d+1}

{\mathfrak{b}}=1

{\mathfrak{D}}_{i}=\partial_{x_{i}}+{\mathfrak{c}}\frac{x_{i}}{t}\partial_{t},\quad 1\leq i\leq d,\quad\hbox{and}\quad{\mathfrak{D}}_{d+1}=\frac{\sqrt{t^{2}-{\mathfrak{c}}\|{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}}}{t}\partial_{t}.

It follows readily

{\mathfrak{D}}_{i,j}:=x_{i}{\mathfrak{D}}_{j}-x_{j}{\mathfrak{D}}_{i}=x_{i}\partial_{x_{j}}-x_{j}\partial_{x_{i}}=D_{i,j},\qquad 1\leq i,j\leq d,

and, furthermore,

{\mathfrak{D}}_{i,d+1}:=x_{i}{\mathfrak{D}}_{d+1}-{\mathfrak{t}}(\|{\mathbf{x}}\|,t){\mathfrak{D}}_{i}=-\frac{\sqrt{t^{2}-{\mathfrak{c}}\|{\mathbf{x}}\|^{2}}}{t}\left(t\partial_{x_{i}}-(1-c)x_{i}\partial_{t}\right),\quad 1\leq i\leq d.

With these notations, we then define our second
K
-functional by

\displaystyle\widehat{K}_{r}(f;\rho)_{p,{\mathbf{W}}_{{\mathfrak{c}}}^{{\gamma}}}=\inf_{g\in C^{r}(\Lambda_{\mathfrak{c}}^{d+1})}\Big\{\|f-g\|_{L^{p}({\mathbf{W}}_{{\mathfrak{c}}}^{\gamma},\Lambda_{{\mathfrak{c}}}^{d+1})}

\displaystyle+\max_{1\leq i\leq d+1}\left\|\phi_{\mathfrak{c}}^{r}{\mathfrak{D}}_{i}^{r}g\right\|_{L^{p}({\mathbf{W}}_{{\mathfrak{c}}}^{\gamma},\Lambda_{{\mathfrak{c}}}^{d+1})}\Big\}.

K

Theorem 4.10
.

Let
{\gamma}>-1
, and
f\in L^{p}(\Lambda_{{\mathfrak{c}}}^{d+1},{\mathbf{W}}_{{\mathfrak{c}}}^{\gamma})
if
1\leq p<\infty
,
and
f\in C(\Lambda_{{\mathfrak{a}},{\mathfrak{b}},{\mathfrak{c}}})

. Then, for

and

, there hold

(i)

direct theorem:

(ii)

inverse theorem:

\widehat{K}_{r}(f;n^{-1})_{p,{\mathbf{W}}_{{\mathfrak{c}}}^{{\gamma}}}\leq c\,n^{-r}\sum_{k=0}^{n}(k+1)^{r-1}E_{k}(f)_{L^{p}({\mathbf{W}}_{{\mathfrak{c}}}^{{\gamma}},\Lambda_{{\mathfrak{c}}}^{d+1})}.

Proof.

If we replace
{\mathfrak{D}}_{i}
by
\partial_{i}
,
\phi_{\mathfrak{c}}
by
\phi({\mathbf{y}})=\sqrt{1-\|{\mathbf{y}}|^{2}}
, and the norm by
the norm of

with
{\mathbf{W}}({\mathbf{y}})=(1-\|{\mathbf{y}}\|^{2})^{\gamma}
, then the

-functions
become
\widehat{K}_{r}(f;\rho)_{p,{\mathbf{W}}_{\mathbb{B}}^{\gamma}}
for functions defined on the unit ball
{\mathbb{B}}^{d+1}
. Moreover,
by (
4.10

{\mathfrak{D}}_{i}g=\left(\partial_{i}g\circ\psi^{-1}\right)\circ\psi

in fact how

is defined. Consequently, we can conclude that

\widehat{K}_{r}(f,\rho)_{p,{\mathbf{W}}_{{\mathfrak{c}}}^{\gamma}}=\widehat{K}_{r}(f\circ\psi^{-1},\rho)_{p,{\mathbf{W}}_{\mathbb{B}}^{\gamma}},

so that both the direct and the inverse estimates follow from the corresponding results on the unit ball

[
2

3.5

∎

In particular, if
