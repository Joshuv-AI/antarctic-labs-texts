A seismic reservoir permeability prediction approach based on gaussian process machine learning
Authors: Jinyong Gui, Jianhu Gao, Shengjun Li, Bingyang Liu, Xin Guo, Hui Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPredicting permeability using seismic attributes is challenging and fraught with great uncertainty. The inverse relationship, which acts as a bridge between permeability and seismic attributes, needs to be established accurately. Machine learning, especially Gaussian Process (GP), provides a new potential. However, its application to permeability prediction is influenced by the quantity and quality of input features. This study proposes an integrated approach for permeability prediction based on GP. The approach automatically generates 222 extended features from three elastic attributes for GP training. In addition, the synthetic minority oversampling technique is used to overcome the problem of training with imbalanced samples. With the help of feature importance measures based on Shapley values, the features that are important for permeability prediction can be preferentially selected. Validation on a dolomite reservoir in Western China illustrates the role of our approach in enhancing the performance of GP in permeability prediction.Keywords: Permeability, Gaussian process, Extended features, Feature selection, Imbalance dataSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

Permeability is a crucial parameter for characterizing hydrocarbon reservoirs and fluid mobility, playing a significant role in quantitative analyses such as commercial valuation, reserve estimation, well location optimization, and development plan design. Currently, the petroleum industry primarily relies on well logs data and core analysis for permeability prediction. Babadagli et al., conducted a comprehensive review of permeability-prediction methods focusing on the application of well-log data to support reservoir evaluation and engineering practices1. Shi et al. proposed a gated network stacking model driven by both data and physical models to achieve accurate permeability prediction in tight sandstone reservoirs, integrating dual-driven advantages for enhanced reliability2. Hao et al. developed a permeability prediction model for fractal rough fractures, which accounts for the coupled effects of shear and normal stress and incorporates fracture aperture heterogeneity and surface roughness for robust performance3. Hussain et al. realized advanced permeability prediction by extracting two-dimensional geological features from well logs data and applying CNN regression, emphasizing high accuracy and efficiency in feature learning and prediction4. However, these measurements have a limited spatial resolution where it only estimates the well location and certain depth. On the other hand, seismic data, with its superior spatial resolution, offers a means to estimate spatial permeability. Permeability prediction using seismic data remains challenging due to the weak direct petrophysical correlation and strong reservoir heterogeneity. Because permeability, compared with porosity and saturation, is highly sensitive to various factors such as pore scale and shape, as well as pressure and temperature, it is difficult to establish an accurate transformation relationship based on explicit rock physics model equations in most cases5,6. In practice, permeability estimation based seismic information are predominantly data-driven, relying on empirical relationships between permeability and seismic attributes7,8.

Machine learning (ML) has a powerful ability to uncover the complex statistical relationship between reservoir properties and seismic attributes by learning a favorable predictor9–11. However, most of ML algorithms only estimate the target deterministically, which gives a unique result. If the process is repeated with a different initial value, it will produce a different output. It shows that the model does not incorporate uncertainty in its prediction of the non-uniqueness of the parameter itself12. Recently, the uncertainty quantification using the ML model has been actively researched in the geoscience community. Several uncertainty-aware ML studies have successfully addressed the highly nonlinear relationship between seismic data and reservoir properties by leveraging combinations of seismic attributes. Several studies have focused on quantifying uncertainty in reservoir property prediction. Feng et al. employed a Random Forest approach combined with quantile regression trees to quantify uncertainty in the imputation of missing well log data, utilizing principal component analysis to address multicollinearity among input logging attributes13. Zou et al. adopted an ensemble of Random Forest models to assess uncertainty in porosity estimation using a combination of seismic attributes14. To address uncertainty directly from seismic data, Pradhan and Mukerji introduced a Bayesian evidential analysis framework that employs a deep neural network to estimate reservoir properties from offset seismic waveforms, complete with confidence intervals15. Hossain et al. utilized a Bayesian Neural Network (BNN), where the Bayesian learning of hidden layer parameters allows uncertainty to be characterized by the distribution of network nodes16. Li et al. combined data distribution domain transformation with BNNs to simultaneously predict permeability well log curve and estimate both aleatoric and epistemic uncertainties, addressing label distribution mismatches17. Feng et al. adopted a physics-informed generative adversarial network approach to assess uncertainty in rock physical inversion for reservoir parameter estimation using a combination of rock physics equations and ensemble predictions generated by varying latent vectors18. Nevertheless, these methods often come with significant computational costs; the need for multiple training runs in ensemble models and distribution sampling in Bayesian networks can substantially increase both training and testing times. Additionally, quantile regression requires retraining the model for each target quantile, while physics-informed frameworks involve extra complexity in balancing physics-based constraints with data-driven learning.

Gaussian Process (GP), as a nonparametric Bayesian approach, has unique advantages in dealing with such problems19. It does not require predefining the specific functional form of the input-output relationship, but assigns a Gaussian process prior to the underlying mapping function, and probabilistically infers the covariance structure of the function from training data. More importantly, GP regression can generate a complete predictive distribution for the predicted value, and quantify the prediction uncertainty through the variance of the distribution, which makes up for the deficiency of traditional methods in uncertainty analysis. GP has been successfully applied to the estimation of well logs including porosity, TOC, and permeability, demonstrating significant advantages and high accuracy. Yu et al. employed GP to derive Total Organic Carbon from well logs data20. Huang et al. proposed a GP-based methodology to estimate intact rock strength21. Furthermore, Asante-Okyere et al. and Mahdaviara et al. extended the use of GP to petrophysical property prediction, successfully estimating porosity and permeability22,23. Rahma et al. employed GP to estimate porosity directly from seismic attributes, capturing nonlinear relationships while quantifying uncertainty through predictive standard deviations24. Nevertheless, the usage of GP is still sparsely documented in reservoir parameter prediction based seismic data, particularly for permeability prediction which exhibits weaker correlations with seismic information, remains an area requiring further investigation. Existing GP-based permeability prediction research is primarily confined to well locations, with its success relying on well logs characteristics that have a direct relevant relationship with permeability (e.g., using porosity and irreducible water saturation well logs as input features). However, at the seismic scale, 3D data volumes for these microscopic well logs characteristics cannot be obtained, making it difficult to generalize the GP model established at well-sites to a 3D space.

In our investigation into the application of GP to seismic reservoir permeability prediction, three key challenges should be addressed: (1) Arbitrariness in feature construction. As with other ML approaches, the impact of feature selection and feature quantity on prediction accuracy is not yet fully clarified, especially for continuous parameters like permeability. The relationship between seismic attributes and permeability is highly complex and nonlinear. Conventional approaches rely heavily on manually extracting a large number of attributes based on empirical knowledge, which is not only inefficient but also prone to introducing noise. (2) Information redundancy and the curse of dimensionality. When massive 3D seismic feature volumes are fed indiscriminately into models, the result is often wasteful computation. (3) Extreme sample imbalance. In most study areas, high-permeability reservoirs are typically developed as thin interbeds within thick low-permeability sequences. This leads to a severe scarcity of “high-permeability” samples in the training dataset, significantly undermining the model’s ability to predict these reservoir properties of interest.

To address these challenges, this paper proposes an end-to-end workflow for seismic reservoir permeability prediction based on Gaussian process regression. Unlike conventional approaches that rely on manually selected elastic attributes, our workflow introduces a novel integration of three key strategies: automatic feature generation to enrich the input space, robust feature selection to manage dimensionality, and synthetic minority over-sampling to improve prediction for scarce high-permeability layers. Rather than treating these as isolated techniques, our core innovation lies in their seamless integration into a cohesive workflow that systematically transforms raw seismic data into reliable permeability predictions. The workflow-level innovation is threefold: (1) data-driven feature expansion replaces heuristic manual attribute extraction; (2) multicollinearity-robust feature selection ensures stable dimensionality reduction; and (3) targeted minority-class augmentation enhances sensitivity to economically valuable reservoirs. The proposed workflow has been successfully validated in a dolomite reservoir, demonstrating superior performance in seismic reservoir permeability prediction.

Methods

Extended features generation

In general, a richer feature space may enhance representational capacity, provided that redundancy and overfitting are controlled. Alvarez et al. proposed a method where eleven common elastic attributes from pre-stack seismic inversion were mathematically transformed into a large number of extended attributes as input features for linear regression, demonstrating robust performance for predicting conventional reservoir parameters25. Nevertheless, this approach remains vulnerable to subjective bias in attribute selection. The number of elastic attributes is much more than eleven and the target-sensitive elastic attributes might be missed. In addition, the extraction of target-sensitive elastic attributes is a time-consuming and expert-knowledge-requiring. Each elastic attribute must be individually derived through pre-stack seismic inversion or various transformation formulas. This process is not only less automated but also risks error accumulation and amplification, especially for dolomite reservoirs whose elastic attributes are anisotropic and difficult to invert accurately from seismic data.

GP regressor

As a nonparametric Bayesian approach, GP regression avoids explicit assumptions about the functional form of input-output relationships. Instead, it probabilistically infers covariance structures directly from training data to generate predictive distributions19. A GP is a collection of random variables where any finite subset follows a joint Gaussian distribution. It is defined by a mean function m(x) and a covariance function

In reality, permeability distributions are invariably non-Gaussian. They typically exhibit a strong positive skew, characterized by a predominance of low-permeability areas interspersed with limited, high-permeability zones. GP assigns a prior distribution to the function f itself, specifically a Gaussian process. The key point is that a GP assumes a Gaussian distribution for the function mapping, rather than assuming that the observed label values are Gaussian. This flexible assumption about the underlying function allows the GP to capture complex nonlinear relationships.

Features selection

According to the proposed strategy, 222 features are automatically generated using the , and ρ volumes obtained by conventional pre-stack seismic inversion as the input features. However, a large number of features may bring too much redundant information and calculation consumption. Some features are more accurate predictors, and other features may include little information for prediction.

SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) is a powerful tool for feature selection and model interpretation, rooted in cooperative game theory28. It quantifies how each feature contributes to the predictions of a machine learning model by assigning each feature a “Shapley value”. Specifically, Shapley values compute the contribution of each feature by treating it as a “player” in a cooperative game, ensuring a fair distribution of the prediction’s “credit” among all features. where S is a subset of all features N excluding i, f(S) denotes the model’s prediction using only the features in subset S and represents the marginal contribution of feature i. Shapley values ensure fairness and consistency in distributing the contributions of features. The weight term ensures that all possible combinations of features are fairly weighted.

Unlike other feature importance measures such as Gini importance and Permutation importance, which may be biased towards features with more unique values or higher variance and have multicollinearity problems, SHAP ensures a fair distribution of the prediction’s “credit” among all features by considering all possible combinations28. This comprehensive approach guarantees a fair and consistent distribution of the prediction’s “credit”, enabling it to accurately discern individual feature effects even when correlations are present.

Sample balancing

In most cases, favorable reservoirs are predominantly developed as thin layers interbedded within thick, non-reservoir sequences. This setting often results in imbalanced training data: a scarcity of high-permeability samples from the thin reservoirs and an abundance of low-permeability samples from the non-reservoir rocks. Such imbalanced data often worsen the performance of ML algorithms. Over the last few years, the influence of imbalanced data in ML has received increasing attention, as such data imbalance often compromises model performance by inducing a predictive bias toward majority-class samples29. To further improve the generalization ability of the GP regressor, we apply an improved Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) to augment the scarce samples representing high-permeability reservoirs. The SMOTE algorithm analyzes a small number of samples, synthesizes new samples manually, and adds the new samples to the dataset30. However, the standard SMOTE process generates synthetic samples across the entire minority category, it often introduces overlapping instances and produces uninformative samples that can degrade ML accuracy. BSMOTE (Borderline-SMOTE) is an improved oversampling technique derived from the original SMOTE framework31. In contrast, BSMOTE strategically synthesizes new samples exclusively from minority samples located near the class boundary, thereby optimizing the sample category distribution. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the BSMOTE begins by categorizing minority samples into three types: Safe, Danger, and Noise. A “Safe” sample (e.g., point A) is surrounded predominantly by other minority samples. A “Danger” sample (e.g., point B) is located in a region where majority samples prevail, indicating its proximity to the class boundary. A “Noise” sample (e.g., point C) is entirely surrounded by majority samples and is considered an outlier. The algorithm subsequently applies oversampling only to the “Danger” category, which most critically influences the decision boundary.

Case study

The proposed approach was validated using a deep dolomite gas reservoir in western China as a case study. In this area, thick sequences of limestone strata are developed, within which dolomite reservoirs formed through dolomitization and hydrothermal alteration are present in the background of extensive limestone deposits. While initial wells yielded high gas flows, indicating substantial potential, further drilling revealed strong production variability laterally, with reservoir permeability being the key determinant. Therefore, accurately predicting permeability and reliably quantifying its uncertainty are essential for guiding optimal well placement in this area. The permeability of four wells W1, W2, W3 and W4 in the study area, together with the corresponding the borehole-side curves extracted from the pre-stack seismic inversion volumes of , and ρ, are selected as the basic labeled dataset for the test application in this study. Specifically, permeability from well logs is determined by interpreting conventional well logs in combination with core analysis data, where standard petrophysical models are employed to transform well-log responses into quantitative permeability values1. Since the seismic inversion results of the borehole-side traces are selected as samples, the corresponding permeability log data also need to be converted into the time domain (using Backus average33, and the sampling rate is kept consistent with the seismic data to reduce the resolution difference with the seismic data.

The primary advantage of GP is that it can provide the variance of the prediction results as a measure of the uncertainty associated with the predictions. As shown in Fig. 9a, it displays the predicted permeability curves and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI) when using all 222 features, only the top 15 features selected by SHAP, and 11 conventional elastic attributes as input features. We find that the predicted curves of all 222 features and the top 15 features are almost completely coincident, which is better than the prediction result of the conventional 11 elastic attributes as input. This indicates that the extended features can well replace the conventional manually provided features and that, with the help of appropriate feature selection methods, the number of input features can be effectively reduced. However, even when all 222 features are utilized, the predictions in the high-permeability interval still exhibit significant deviations (as indicated by the red arrow), accompanied by relatively wide confidence interval (CI). This suggests that the model exhibits high uncertainty and insufficient predictive reliability in this interval. As illustrated in Fig. 9b, high-permeability samples are notably scarce in Well 5, comprising merely 12% of the total samples. The results in Fig. 10 show that utilizing the top 15 features after BSMOTE balancing significantly improves the prediction accuracy for the high-permeability interval, while the corresponding CI also narrower. This phenomenon demonstrates that sample imbalance significantly impacts GP predictive performance. A direct comparison of the histograms in Fig. 10a and b reveals that, after BSMOTE processing, the high-value interval samples has seen a pronounced increase while faithfully preserving the distributional shape of the high-value interval.

We statistically evaluated the reliability of the model’s uncertainty quantification by calculating the 95% CI coverage probability and interval width distribution of the samples in Fig. 10, with the results presented in Fig. 11. The actual coverage probability of the 95% CI is 96.1%, which is slightly higher than the theoretical target of 95%, demonstrating that the constructed confidence intervals can reliably encompass the true values and that the model’s uncertainty quantification possesses sound statistical validity without significant under- or over-coverage issues. Meanwhile, the predictive uncertainty distribution is highly concentrated within a narrow range, with an average width of 0.381 and a median width of 0.360 for the 95% CI. This indicates that the model maintains a low level of predictive uncertainty while ensuring high coverage probability, achieving a favorable balance between accuracy and reliability. Collectively, these results confirm that the model’s uncertainty quantification meets the statistical requirements of the 95% CI, and the compact interval width attests to its robust predictive capability and reliable uncertainty characterization, which can effectively support practical engineering decision-making in reservoir permeability prediction.

Discussion

This study drew upon the feature transformation approach of Alvarez et al., applying a series of mathematical transforms (lnx, ex,1/x, x2, x− 2) to the EEI data25. These transforms adopt classical mathematical forms from the fields of seismic attributes and fluid factors, with the initial intention of amplifying implicit patterns within the data through nonlinear mapping35,36. It is important to note that the physical meaning of these individual transformations remains unclear at this stage, which represents a methodological limitation of this study. However, it also leaves room for future research to infer physical laws retrospectively from a data-driven perspective. From a geological perspective, raw elastic attributes (e.g., P-wave impedance, S-wave impedance) contain mixed information about lithology, porosity, fluid, and pore structure. Mathematical transformations help decouple these effects by nonlinearly projecting the data into a higher-dimensional space. Crucially, it is the collective combination of multiple transformed features, rather than any single feature in isolation, that captures the geological characteristics of pore structure and fluid mobility. Building on this, we employed the SHAP algorithm for feature selection, effectively isolating core variables with statistical significance from a large set of features.

For feature selection, we utilized the SHAP method, which grounded in solid game-theoretic principles, provides consistent and stable feature attribution. This robustness allowed us to reliably identify the most influential features and attain satisfactory prediction accuracy with a relatively small feature set. However, as inherent to the data-driven methods employed in this study, the feature importance ranking is derived from statistical correlations, information gain, or contributions to prediction errors. It reflects the mathematical “strength of association” with the target variable (permeability) but does not directly equate to “causality” in a geological genesis context. Clarifying whether the features with high importance scores specifically highlight particular physical or geological characteristics of permeability is a common challenge for purely statistical learning black-box models in oil and gas exploration. Therefore, the high-importance features identified by SHAP should be understood as an effective set of proxy indicators that collectively characterize reservoir quality evolution within the specific geological context of the study area, rather than isolated physical controlling factors. To move beyond purely statistical association and toward greater geological interpretability, we plan to incorporate GP native interpretability techniques in future work. Specifically, we will: (1) replace the isotropic Matérn kernel with an Automatic Relevance Determination (ARD) kernel, in which each feature is assigned an independent length scale parameter that directly quantifies its relevance within the Gaussian process’s own probabilistic framework; (2) map the learned ARD length scales back onto the χ angle transform grid to identify which angle ranges and nonlinear transformations are most informative for permeability prediction; and (3) decompose the GP predictive variance by feature groups to evaluate each feature’s contribution to uncertainty reduction. Unlike post hoc methods such as SHAP and Permutation Importance, these GP native techniques derive interpretability directly from the kernel hyperparameters and covariance structure, thereby complementing SHAP’s role in efficient feature pruning.

The study case we presented involves a relatively small dataset, consisting of only 2,092 samples. As a non-parametric Bayesian approach, the GP model’s requirement for training data is highly problem-dependent, and there is currently no unified standard at the intersection of geological modeling and machine learning. Theoretically, the optimal sample size is governed by multiple interacting factors, including: (1) the dimensionality and redundancy of input features; (2) the inherent variability and heterogeneity of reservoir type and geological parameters; (3) the signal-to-noise ratio of the data; and (4) the spatial correlation structure of the target variable. In the specific geological context of our study area (thick sequences of limestone strata are developed, within which dolomite reservoirs formed through dolomitization and hydrothermal alteration are present in the background of extensive limestone deposits.), we experimented with different training set sizes and observed that prediction accuracy tends to plateau after a certain threshold. However, given the complexity of the aforementioned controlling factors, it is difficult to prescribe a single optimal number that can be generalized to other areas or datasets. We believe a more rigorous approach is to determine the optimal training data size dynamically through multiple random sampling tests based on the specific data characteristics of each study area, rather than relying on a predetermined universal threshold.

It should be noted that the focus of this study is not on the GP model itself but on its better application to seismic reservoir permeability prediction. Thus, the Matérn kernel is directly adopted in the study case without investigating the influence of kernel function selection on prediction results. However, this does not mean that kernel function selection is unimportant. The selection of kernel function is closely related to the characteristics of permeability data. For the permeability distribution with different smoothness and scale characteristics, the appropriate kernel function needs to be selected to fit the data characteristics. The influence of kernel functions and the methods for their selection have been studied in detail by Mahdaviara et al.23. By adopting the same kernel function optimization method, we ultimately selected the Matérn kernel as well. The Matérn kernel is a flexible kernel function with a smoothness parameter, which compensates for the “oversmoothing” drawback of the Squared Exponential kernel, and this may be why it is more suitable for modeling permeability data that exhibits non-infinitely smooth spatial variations in actual geological formations. While the Matérn kernel was adopted in this study for its robustness, we recognize that a composite kernel (e.g., Matérn + Linear kernel) may offer improved capability in characterizing the multi-scale heterogeneity of dolomite reservoirs. Future work could explore such composite kernel structures to better separate and model large-scale trends and small-scale permeability variations. In a composite kernel, the linear component might primarily capture large-scale (kilometer-scale) background permeability trends controlled by burial compaction and regional diagenesis, potentially representing the smooth, systematic permeability-depth gradient of the host limestone. In contrast, the Matérn component (once-differentiable) might capture small-to-moderate scale (tens to hundreds of meters) localized heterogeneity arising from dolomitization fronts, hydrothermal alteration zones, and fracture-enhanced permeability corridors that deviate from the background trend. This tentative decomposition appears broadly consistent with the underlying geological process, i.e., regional burial establishes the baseline while localized fluid-rock interactions superimpose deviations.

Conclusions

This study proposes a GP machine learning approach integrating automated Extended feature generation strategy, SHAP-based feature selection, SMOTE-based sample balancing to resolve key challenges in seismic-driven permeability prediction. Validation results from the dolomite reservoir demonstrate that the GP machine learning can effectively predict permeability while quantifying prediction uncertainty. However, particular attention must be paid to the significant impact of both feature quantity and quality on GP performance. In our approach, the proposed automated extended feature generation strategy can eliminate the need for manual feature extraction while comprehensively encompassing common elastic properties, and the SHAP-based feature selection can enable precise identification of the most significant features with minimal quantity. Moreover, for the study area where favorable reservoirs are developed within a large background lithologic formation, the issue of imbalanced training samples for GP modeling must be addressed and sample balancing methods such as BSMOTE can effectively enhance the performance of GP. What is particularly valuable is that GP machine learning can also provide the variance of the prediction results, which serves as an indicator of uncertainty evaluation, enabling interpreters to assess the reliability of the results in a more quantitative manner. We believe that the proposed approach can also be applied to predict other reservoir properties, such as total carbon content, brittleness, and stress, providing a reproducible solution for the quantitative characterization of complex reservoirs with weak rock physics relationships.
