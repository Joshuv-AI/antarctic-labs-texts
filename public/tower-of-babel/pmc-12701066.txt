Effect of posture on photoplethysmography signals from the posterior tibial artery in adults with and without type 2 diabetes
Authors: Apakrita Tayade, Saurav Kumar, Amber Shrivastava, Ravi Bhallamudi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDiabetic foot complications remain a major cause of disability in diabetes and represent a severe consequence of poor glycaemic control, primarily driven by peripheral arterial obstruction, neuropathic damage, and compromised tissue perfusion. Photo-plethysmography (PPG) signals offer a non-invasive means of assessing vascular health. The objective is to provide insights that aid in tailoring interventions for subjects with diabetes. The study examines posture-related changes on PPG parameters at the posterior tibial artery in healthy subjects (Group A) and subjects with diabetes (Group B). Physiological parameters analysed included pulse amplitude, mean Peak-to-Peak-Interval (PPI), SDPP, Low-Frequency to High-Frequency ratio (LF/HF), b/a ratio, and (b-c-d-e)/a ratio. Postural effects were evaluated in 30 subjects per group using two-way ANOVA and Mann–Whitney U tests. Morphological analysis of PPG waveforms in Group B revealed a gradual systolic rise, prolonged diastolic decay, and a less prominent dicrotic notch, indicating accelerated vascular aging. Significant main effects of posture were found for pulse amplitude, mean PPI, SDPP, LF/HF, b/a, and (b–c–d–e)/a ratio. Furthermore, posture × group interaction effects reached significance for mean PPI, SDPP, LF/HF, and b/a ratio. The study reveals posture-related variations in PPG signal quality and autonomic function, with supine posture yielding the most stable waveforms. These findings may offer preliminary insights into posture-sensitive PPG measures that could support future research and aid in the clinical assessment of diabetic foot complications.Keywords: Diabetic foot complications, Photoplethysmography, Posture, Posterior tibial artery autonomic dysfunctionSubject terms: Physiology, Diseases, Engineering

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus (DM) continues to affect millions globally, posing serious challenges to both individual health and public healthcare systems. According to the International Diabetes Federation (IDF), every seven seconds, someone dies due to diabetes or its complications1. Approximately 589 million adults (20–79 years) are currently living with diabetes worldwide and this number is projected to increase to 643 million by 2030 and 853 million by 20501. Diabetes is associated with both microvascular and macrovascular complications, among which peripheral arterial disease (PAD) and diabetic peripheral neuropathy (DN) are particularly important contributors to diabetic foot complications. These complications lead to impaired circulation, loss of protective sensation, foot ulcers, and, in severe cases, lower limb amputations. Globally, diabetic foot complications affect millions of individuals, with a lifetime risk estimated at 15–34% among people with diabetes2,3. These diabetic foot complications impose a tremendous global burden of disease through increased disability, reduced quality of life, and rising healthcare costs. This indicates an urgent need for improved strategies focused on early detection, prevention, and timely management of diabetic foot complications. Encouragingly, studies estimated that up to 85% of diabetes-related amputations can be prevented through appropriate treatment if detected early4.

Photo-plethysmography (PPG) is one of the methods utilized to quantify blood flow in the arteries. The PPG is based on light attenuation as skin or tissue is exposed to the same. The attenuation varies depending on the blood volume entering the tissue under observation. The PPG signal comprises pulsatile (AC) and superimposed (DC) components. The AC component represents the synchronized changes in blood volume caused by heartbeats, and the DC part represents breathing and activity in the sympathetic nervous system. The derivatives of PPG waveforms are often used to evaluate the waveform changes better. Acceleration plethysmography (APG) (second derivative of PPG) indicates blood acceleration and consists of five waves: a, b, c, d, and e. The healthcare professionals can analyze the shape of this waveform to identify physiological trends in subjects and assess the risk of developing PAD (Table 1)5. This non-invasive method is promising for early cardiovascular health detection and intervention.

Many researchers have reported the influence of measurement sites and breathing patterns on the PPG waveform characteristics. One study examined the PPG waveform from different measurement sites to evaluate the variations in PPG waveform across various regions of the body6. Another study used PPG and electrocardiography (ECG) with a multiple linear regression model for estimating systolic blood pressure (SBP). According to the study, the estimation performance of the model using PPG signals from the finger and forehead did not differ significantly. Nevertheless, posture change (lying, sitting, and standing) significantly impacted SBP estimation accuracy, highlighting the need for separate models for different postures7. Another study examined the effect of the depth gap between red and reference wavelengths on remote SpO2 calibration in the visible spectrum. The posture transition from sitting to supine or vice versa causes a modification in the ratio of amplitudes of red over green (RoG) wavelengths by 23%. The results emphasize the need for further investigation of the technical challenges of remote SpO2 estimation8.

A few studies focused on the effects of posture change on baroreflex sensitivity (BRS) and cardio-pulmonary interactions. BRS is a parameter that quantifies the relationship between arterial blood pressure variations and heart rate changes. Montano et al. conducted a multivariate time series analysis to evaluate the strength of baroreflex and direct and indirect cardio-pulmonary coupling during graded head-up tilt9. Faes et al. investigated changes in the interactions among cardiac, respiratory, and vascular systems during head-up tilt and paced breathing, highlighting the role of low-frequency (LF) and high-frequency (HF) power calculations in classifying postural change effects10. Singh et al. introduced a new BRS index utilizing an improved Hilbert–Huang transform (HHT) with a weighted coherence (CW) criterion to assess baroreflex function in d-standing postures, reporting an increased rate of change in LF/HF power ratios and enhanced BRS indices compared to standard methods11. Another researcher showed that RR interval variability decreases during standing posture, indicating reduced respiratory sinus arrhythmia and parasympathetic withdrawal12. Mary et al. emphasized the interaction between cardiac and respiratory signals during postural changes using a directed coherence approach in the frequency domain. The above study found that the dominant frequency shifts towards lower frequencies during standing, aligning with power spectral density results13. These studies collectively demonstrate the impact of posture changes on cardiac, respiratory, and vascular systems, emphasizing the complex nature of autonomic regulation in the human body and the need for advanced methods to understand these underlying physiological mechanisms.

Recent studies have also explored cardiovascular variability in type 2 diabetes, particularly during postural transitions. Moura-Tonello et al. demonstrated that men with type 2 diabetes, even in the absence of clinical signs of cardiovascular autonomic neuropathy, showed increased sympathetic modulation14. However, nonlinear HRV indices reflecting signal complexity were preserved, and autonomic adjustments to active standing remained comparable to controls. A subsequent study using heart period and systolic arterial pressure variability in patients without clinically evident neuropathies found reduced vagal modulation and impaired BRS at rest and during standing, while sympathetic responses appeared to remain intact15. Another study in coronary artery disease patients reported that diabetes exerted minimal additional impact on BRS once vascular stiffness had been established. This suggests that structural vascular alterations may mask the manifestations of diabetes-related autonomic dysfunction16. Recently, Galdino et al. introduced heart rate fragmentation analysis and reported greater fragmentation in subjects with diabetes, which was associated with reduced vagal activity and altered HRV indices. These findings highlight heart rate fragmentation as a promising marker for detecting subtle autonomic dysfunction in males with type 2 diabetes17. Overall, these studies indicate that postural changes provide a valuable framework for detecting subtle autonomic dysfunction in type 2 diabetes.

A few researchers have reported variations in PPG signals with different hand elevations. One of the studies looked at changes in baseline posture and pulsatile light absorbance in the fingertip of subjects who raised their hand above the horizontal plane in a supine posture18. The results demonstrated that arm elevation decreased red light absorbance and increased the range of PPG amplitudes. According to the findings, fingertip vessels pulsate more strongly when raised, possibly due to venous collapse. Another research examined how venous and arterial affect PPG signals during exercise. The hand-raising alters venous return to the finger, which leads to the variation of AC and DC components of the PPG signals. The study suggested that hand position should be considered when analysing arterial stiffness because venous resistance affects PPG morphology more than arterial resistance19.

Zheng et al. investigated the effect of passive leg raising on the blood volume change for lower limbs, using a tissue optics model and a dual probe PPG system20. The study reported that the AC/DC ratio decreases when the leg is raised in the supine position. Additionally, the DC value increases in both the supine and reclining positions. The results suggest a local and systemic physiological phenomenon in lower limb blood volume change caused by postural changes. Another study examined the regulation of blood pulse volume utilizing photo-plethysmography (PPG) signal detection from the toe while the lower limb is passively raised to various height positions (supine and reclined at 45°)21. The study revealed that the normalized AC signal changes significantly, whereas the normalized DC signal changes minimally for both postures. With the leg raised, the normalized AC/DC ratio significantly decreased for supine posture, while the normalized DC value increased significantly for both supine and reclining positions.

Previous research on posture-induced changes in PPG waveforms has mainly focused on healthy subjects and upper-limb measurement sites. Several studies have investigated cardiovascular variability in type 2 diabetes during postural changes using ECG and blood pressure analysis, but few have employed PPG to assess peripheral vascular dynamics under different postures. Evidence on posture-related PPG variations in the lower limbs remains scarce, particularly in diabetes where vascular complications are common. Given the altered blood flow dynamics and increased risk of diabetic foot complications, it is critical to examine arterial function in the lower limbs. The present study addresses this gap by employing PPG to quantitatively analyse posture-induced changes in waveforms recorded at the posterior tibial artery, a clinically relevant site for evaluating diabetic foot complications. By applying PPG in a direct comparison of age-matched healthy subjects and individuals with diabetes, this study provides novel insights into how posture modulates peripheral vascular responses in the lower limbs, extending beyond the scope of previous cardiovascular variability studies based on ECG or blood pressure.

The study used a PPG device to collect data from the posterior tibial artery of age-matched controls (AMC) and people with diabetes. The ankle-brachial index (ABI) was then measured using the ankle-to-brachial systolic blood pressure ratio. ABI is a quick and effective way to identify peripheral artery disease (PAD) and is a precise method for assessment of vascular risk in otherwise asymptomatic patients23.

Signal acquisition and pre-processing

Signals from the right posterior tibial artery (PTA) were acquired using a multi-wavelength optical PPG sensor. The sensor was secured at the ankle with an adjustable strap to ensure stable optical contact and minimize motion-related signal disturbances. In order to assess the impact of posture on artery parameters, PPG measurements were obtained in three distinct postures for each subject. PPG measurements were obtained sequentially in three postures: supine, sitting at 90° on a chair, and standing (Fig. 1). The subjects were instructed to maintain each posture for 3 min to allow physiological stabilization. The PPG measurements were evaluated to determine any variation in arterial parameters associated with the various postures. Detailed specifications of the sensor are presented in Table 4.

A robust preprocessing pipeline was implemented to ensure reliable feature extraction while preserving physiologically relevant waveform morphology. The pipeline included baseline correction, denoising, peak detection, segmentation, and averaging (Fig. 2). PPG signals were sampled at 400 Hz to restrict the bandwidth of the signals and remove any unwanted noise. The initial and last 10 s of each stage were excluded to reduce any non-stationarities in the signal. For baseline wander removal, a discrete wavelet transform (db6) decomposition was applied, which effectively eliminated low-frequency drift while preserving the signal’s pulsatile components. The baseline-corrected signal was then filtered using a third-order band-pass Butterworth filter with cut-off frequencies of 0.5 and 5 Hz24. These cut-off frequencies were chosen to reduce extraneous noise and amplify the pulsating component of the PPG signal. The second derivative (APG) of the corrected signal was used to assess the waveform alterations (Fig. 3). In order to assess the morphological characteristics of the pulse wave, various fiducial points were estimated from PPG signals, such as systolic peak, dicrotic notch, and diastolic peak. To additionally evaluate robustness, white Gaussian noise was added to the PPG signals at different signal-to-noise ratios (30–5 dB), and the signals were reprocessed using the same pipeline for comparison.

Feature extraction

The PPG signal is divided into two phases: the rising edge of the pulse, known as the Anacrotic phase (representing systole), and the falling edge of the pulse, known as the Catacrotic phase (representing the diastole). Additionally, a dicrotic notch is typically visible at the catacrotic phase25. The time from the onset of the pulse to the systolic peak is called systolic rise time, while diastolic decay refers to the time duration of the diastolic phase26. An algorithm was implemented in MATLAB (R2023b, MathWorks Inc., Natick, MA, USA) to extract features from PPG signals in the time, frequency, and morphology domains, with all feature definitions, computational steps, and equations detailed in the following subsections.

Time-domain feature extraction

Time-domain features are quantitative indices derived directly from the morphology of the arterial pulse waveform24,27. These features reflect the temporal and amplitude characteristics of the PPG signal and provide information about vascular function. As illustrated in Fig. 3, pulse amplitude (PA) is defined as the difference between the maximum (systolic) value and the minimum (end-of-diastolic) value within a single cardiac cycle6. The peak amplitude reflects the pulsatile change in blood volume produced by arterial inflow at the measurement site28. Acceleration plethysmogram

The second derivative of the PPG is known as the acceleration plethysmogram (APG). The APG waveform consists of five waves: the a-wave (early systolic positive wave), b-wave (early systolic negative wave), c-wave (late systolic re-increasing wave), d-wave (late systolic re-decreasing wave), and e-wave (early diastolic positive wave). Each wave corresponds to specific phases of vascular activity and is used to derive clinically relevant amplitude ratios and aging indices. Statistical analysis

The study is structured into two parts. The first part (Study 1) quantified posture-related differences in PPG signals using a two-way ANOVA. The second part (Study 2) focuses on analysing the PPG characteristics of both groups, specifically in a supine posture. A comprehensive analysis was conducted on thirty subjects in each group to examine the effect of posture change on the PPG features. The features extracted from PPG signals across different postures were compared using a two-way ANOVA, with posture (supine, sitting, standing) and group (A, B) specified as fixed factors. This analysis allowed the evaluation of both main effects and posture × group interaction effects. Additionally, the Mann-Whitney U-test was employed to analyse the differences in extracted PPG features between the two groups in the supine posture33.

Discussion

Morphological and frequency analysis of the waveform

The influence of posture on acquiring PPG signals is an important consideration in this study. As subjects transition from supine posture to sitting and standing postures, notable changes in the PPG waveform characteristics become apparent. The recorded PPG waveform displays evident and well-defined fiducial points for the supine posture, indicating distinct pulsatile components. Additionally, the presence of harmonics in the frequency spectrum suggests regular and clearly defined pulsations, providing a stable baseline for signal analysis26. The stability in the supine posture is attributed to minimal hydrostatic pressure variation along the body, resulting in consistent blood flow and reduced gravitational impact on the cardiovascular system. Upon transitioning to a sitting posture, the PPG waveform maintains distinct characteristics observed in the supine position, although with a slight variation in the identifiable fiducial points of the arterial pressure (APG) wave. However, PPG and its derivatives exhibit challenges in identifying fiducial points for standing posture. The Fourier transforms further reveal a lack of harmonics, indicating irregular pulsations and high-frequency components in the standing posture (Fig. 4). Based on these signal characteristics, the supine posture yields PPG waveforms with the most clearly defined features, lower signal-to-noise ratio, and reproducible waveforms, making it preferable for signal acquisition compared to sitting and standing postures. Statistical analysis further supports supine posture, allowing for distinct and easily identifiable fiducial points, ensuring robust and reliable data measurement. To further confirm the stability of these findings, analysis with added white Gaussian noise (30–5 dB SNR) showed that fiducial detection and APG morphology were preserved at practical noise levels (≥ 20 dB) and remained stable even under moderate noise (10–12 dB). This reinforces that the posture-related differences arise from physiological effects rather than noise sensitivity.

The morphological analysis of PPG waveforms between group A and group B highlights significant observations regarding the vascular implications of diabetes (Figs. 5 and 6). The PPG of group A consistently displayed more distinct systolic peaks and more prominent dicrotic notches, indicative of better arterial compliance and vascular function compared to group B. In contrast, group B exhibited rounded systolic peaks and slower diastolic decays34. The impaired vascular function associated with diabetes may contribute to the rounder systolic peaks and wider pulse widths observed in the PPG waveform. These occlusions reduce the arterial flow, leading to a more gradual systolic rise and a prolonged diastolic decay of the PPG waveform.

In addition, the study demonstrates that both groups had age-related variations in the shape of waveforms, including missing or less prominent dicrotic notches, which indicate the natural aging of blood vessels. However, subjects with diabetes in Group B exhibited more significant variations in PPG waveforms and diabetes-accelerated vascular aging. The increased rise and decay times observed in Group B explain the impaired venous return caused by autonomic dysfunction. This could lead to the accumulation of blood in the peripheral arteries, impacting the overall shape of the PPG waveform. The above variation aligns with research highlighting diabetes as a catalyst for arterial stiffening and altered wave reflections, quantified by metrics like ∆T and SI35.

The study revealed that as age increases, the ∆T decreases and SI increases, which indicates a rise in arterial stiffness. Group B consistently showed lower ∆T values and higher SI values across different age groups, suggesting diminished arterial elasticity and increased stiffness compared to Group A36. Despite the effects of aging, higher ΔT and lower SI values were observed in Group B subjects in their 50 s compared to those in their 40 s with a higher HbA1c index. The contradiction implies that subjects with diabetes may experience accelerated vascular aging processes as a result of elevated HbA1c levels, highlighting the severity of vascular complications associated with inadequately managed diabetes. Hence, early diagnosis of changed waveform characteristics could support timely management and prevention of diabetic foot complications.

From Group A, the subjects in the 60 s displayed tertiary peaks in PPG waveforms despite having abnormal ABI values36. This finding suggests some unaltered vascular dynamics with age in healthy subjects. Conversely, in Group B, subjects in the 60 s with abnormal ABI values showed no tertiary peaks and had diminished dicrotic notches. The result indicates a more severe level of vascular damage due to diabetes.

Statistical analysis of the waveform

The statistical analysis in this study evaluated posture-related changes in PPG-derived parameters at the posterior tibial artery using a two-way ANOVA, incorporating both posture and group effects. The results of study 1 demonstrated that posture significantly influenced all parameters, with significant interaction effects for mean PPI, SDPP, LF/HF, and b/a ratio. These interactions indicate that individuals with diabetes display altered patterns of autonomic and vascular modulation during postural transitions compared with healthy controls, reflecting a potential complication in the autonomic nervous system (ANS) function. ANS is a component of the peripheral nervous system that regulates involuntary physiologic processes, including heart rate, blood pressure, respiration, and digestion37. The present findings are consistent with previous evidence that diabetes impairs autonomic control and vascular responsiveness, thereby limiting the ability to adjust to hemodynamic shifts induced by postural changes38.

For mean PPI, significant main effects of posture and group were observed, along with a significant interaction (p = 0.046), indicating that postural changes influenced mean PPI differently between groups. Comparable trends have been documented in controlled physiological studies, where heart period and pulse interval indices exhibited significant modulation with postural transitions, reflecting underlying autonomic adjustments16. Study 2 further revealed a significant difference between groups in the supine position, with higher mean PPI values in Group B compared to Group (A) Within Group A, mean PPI values were higher among older participants, consistent with age-related reductions in arterial compliance and blood flow39. In Group B, particularly among subjects with abnormal ABI values, mean PPI was even higher, reflecting more severe vascular impairment due to the combined influence of diabetes and compromised peripheral circulation. These changes arise from stiffer and narrower arteries, which affect the timing and regularity of the pulse wave and thereby increase the time between peaks. For SDPP, posture exerted a significant main effect (p = 0.014), while the interaction between posture and group was also significant (p = 0.030). These findings indicate that postural transitions influenced short-term variability of pulse intervals in both groups, but the magnitude and direction of change differed between Group A and Group (B) This suggests that while variability in pulse timing is physiologically posture-dependent, diabetes modifies the extent of this modulation, pointing to altered autonomic and vascular adaptability.

For the LF/HF and b/a ratios, study 1 demonstrated significant main effects of posture and significant posture × group interactions, indicating dynamic autonomic adjustments during postural transitions. Study 2 further reveals significant variations in these ratios during the supine posture. The variations highlight a distinct sympathovagal balance, reflecting the autonomic state resulting from the interaction between sympathetic and parasympathetic influences. The LF/HF and b/a ratios indicate the dynamic regulation of the autonomic nervous system, where variations in posture lead to modifications in the balance between sympathetic and parasympathetic activity and changes in vascular responsiveness40. However, these adjustments are affected by autonomic dysfunction associated with diabetes. Hence, LF/HF demonstrated a statistically significant positive association, differing from Farz et al.‘s findings on HRV in diabetic neuropathy subjects41. Additionally, the results show that the b/a and (b-c-d-e)/a ratios increase for Group B subjects in the 60 s compared to their AMC counterparts. These higher values suggest that diabetes and vascular aging further affect the constriction and relaxation of blood vessels.

Although the findings are encouraging, certain limitations should be considered when interpreting the results. The relatively small sample size (n = 30 per group) may influence the external validity of the findings; however, it was sufficient for statistical inference in line with the Central Limit Theorem and provides a meaningful foundation for future studies with larger cohorts42.The study population was drawn from a single geographic region (India), which reduced heterogeneity and minimized potential confounding from cultural and environmental variability. From a methodological perspective, this cross-sectional study provided a robust framework for evaluating group differences and for identifying clinically relevant associations within the study population43.

Conclusion

The study highlights significant variations in PPG signal quality and autonomic function across different postures, with supine posture providing the distinct and most stable waveforms. Subjects in Group B (people with diabetes) display significantly different PPG characteristics compared to those in Group A (healthy). Gradual systolic rise and prolonged diastolic decay are shown in the waveforms of Group B. These variations are attributed to arterial occlusions and reduced vascular compliance. Age-related changes are evident in both groups, but diabetes accelerates vascular aging, as indicated by lower ∆T values and higher stiffness index (SI). Statistical analysis reveals significant main effects of posture on pulse amplitude, mean PPI, SDPP, LF/HF, b/a, and (b–c–d–e)/a ratios with additional posture × group interactions for mean PPI, SDPP, LF/HF, and b/a ratios. The study recommends supine posture due to its physiological relevance. Noteworthy observations have emerged regarding critical parameters. Specifically, the mean PPI, LF/HF, b/a, and the (b-c-d-e)/a ratios consistently exhibited significantly higher values for Group B in comparison to Group A. These variations suggest that these parameters are responsive to postural changes and may have potential relevance for assessing cardiovascular and autonomic responses. Nonetheless, it is important to recognize the study’s limitations, particularly the modest sample size, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings and highlights the need for validation in larger, multi-center, and multi-ethnic cohorts. Future studies could further build upon this work by incorporating posture randomization and direct monitoring of respiratory activity to refine the assessment of posture-related PPG variations. Despite these considerations, the current findings contribute to understanding how posture and physiological factors influence PPG parameters, offering valuable insights for clinical applications and future research in diabetic foot complication.
