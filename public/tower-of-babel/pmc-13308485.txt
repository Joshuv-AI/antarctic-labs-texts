ATP7A and LIAS promote lung adenocarcinoma progression through regulation of cuproptosis
Authors: Wen-Zhou Liu, Fei-Hai Liang, Jian Feng, Yuan Chen, Cheng Cao, You-Rong Chen, Jin-Feng Ge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCuproptosis has been implicated in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), but whether it contributes to the biological functions of ATPase copper transporting alpha (ATP7A) and lipoic acid synthetase (LIAS) remains unclear. This study aimed to investigate the roles of ATP7A and LIAS in LUAD progression and their association with cuproptosis-related alterations.MethodsATP7A and LIAS expression was assessed in LUAD-related human tissues (n = 20 per group) and LUAD cell lines (A549 and PC9) by RT-qPCR, western blotting, and immunohistochemistry. Gain- and loss-of-function experiments were used to evaluate cell proliferation, migration, and epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) (CCK-8, wound-healing, and Transwell assays, with molecular analyses). Cuproptosis-related changes were assessed using FDX1 expression, pyruvate and α-ketoglutarate levels, mitochondrial ultrastructure, and lipoylation-related readouts. Nude mouse subcutaneous xenografts were used for in vivo validation, with an additional independent cohort for Ki-67 immunohistochemistry and TUNEL staining.ResultsATP7A and LIAS were significantly upregulated in LUAD tissues and cell lines compared with controls (all p < 0.05) and were associated with poorer differentiation, lymph node metastasis, and more advanced TNM stage (p < 0.05). Downregulation of ATP7A or LIAS significantly inhibited LUAD cell proliferation and migration and suppressed EMT-related changes (p < 0.05), whereas LIAS overexpression partially reversed the inhibitory effects of ATP7A silencing (p < 0.05). Co-immunoprecipitation supported an interaction between ATP7A and LIAS, and LIAS knockdown reduced ATP7A expression (p < 0.01). ATP7A or LIAS depletion was associated with increased FDX1 expression, elevated pyruvate and α-ketoglutarate levels, mitochondrial structural abnormalities, and altered lipoylation-related readouts (p < 0.05), several of which were partially attenuated by LIAS overexpression (p < 0.05). Reduced cell viability after ATP7A or LIAS depletion was not prominently mediated by caspase-dependent apoptosis or ferroptosis (p > 0.05). In vivo, ATP7A or LIAS knockdown suppressed xenograft tumor growth (p < 0.01), with reduced Ki-67 staining and increased TUNEL positivity in the independent validation cohort (both p < 0.01).ConclusionsATP7A and LIAS promote LUAD progression and are associated with cuproptosis-related alterations, supporting a role for ATP7A/LIAS-regulated cuproptosis-related pathways in LUAD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-026-04302-6.Keywords: Lung adenocarcinoma, Cuproptosis, Epithelial–mesenchymal transition, Lipoylation, Xenograft model

Introduction

According to 2022 global cancer statistics, lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related incidence and mortality, accounting for approximately 2.5 million new diagnoses and 1.8 million deaths in 2022 [1]. Management of lung cancer includes surgical resection, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy, alone or in combination, depending on disease stage and patient characteristics [2–4]. However, most patients with lung cancer are diagnosed at an advanced stage [5], and in many countries, the 5-year survival rate for lung cancer is below 20% due to factors such as delayed diagnosis and frequent late-stage detection [6]. These alarming epidemiological trends and clinical challenges underscore the urgent need for innovative precision therapeutic strategies for lung cancer. Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), the predominant histological subtype of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), constitutes roughly 40% of pulmonary malignancies [7]. Research on LUAD facilitates a deeper understanding of lung cancer pathogenesis and aids in the identification of potential therapeutic biomarkers.

Cuproptosis is a recently described copper-dependent form of regulated cell death [8, 9]. Specifically, cuproptosis is primarily driven by the intracellular buildup of copper ions, which directly interact with the lipoylated components of the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle. This binding induces protein aggregation and functional impairment, thereby disrupting the TCA cycle, provoking proteotoxic stress, and ultimately leading to cell death [8]. This mechanism has been implicated in various diseases, including NSCLC [10–12], providing novel insights into potential therapeutic targets.

The ATPase copper transporting alpha (ATP7A) gene encodes the copper-transporting ATPase alpha chain, which mediates copper transport across cellular membranes and contributes to copper homeostasis [13]. ATP7A is crucial for maintaining normal copper metabolism; however, it can also mediate the efflux of copper ions, thereby attenuating the effects of cuproptosis and contributing to tumor cell invasion and chemoresistance [13]. For instance, studies have shown that NSCLC cells resistant to cisplatin exhibit high levels of ATP7A, and NSCLC patients with ATP7A-positive tumors have poorer chemotherapy responses and shorter overall survival [14]. Despite these findings, most investigations into ATP7A in LUAD have been bioinformatics-based, and conflicting evidence exists regarding differential ATP7A expression between LUAD and normal tissues [15–17], warranting further experimental validation. More importantly, it remains unclear whether the biological functions of ATP7A in LUAD are directly related to cuproptosis. Therefore, exploring the role and potential molecular mechanisms of ATP7A in LUAD from the perspective of cuproptosis is of significant importance.

Mitochondria are key targets of cuproptosis. Lipoic acid synthetase (LIAS) is essential for mitochondrial protein lipoylation and thereby influences the activity of lipoylated TCA-cycle enzymes, mitochondrial function, and energy metabolism [9, 18]. Previous studies have demonstrated the clinical and molecular significance of LIAS in various cancers. For example, LIAS has been reported to be differentially expressed between cancerous and control tissues in renal clear cell carcinoma, rectal adenocarcinoma, and breast cancer, with high LIAS expression being associated with favorable prognosis in these tumors [19, 20]. However, in LUAD, high LIAS expression correlates with poor prognosis [20]. These disparate findings suggest that LIAS may exert different roles in various tumors, and its molecular mechanisms in LUAD remain poorly understood. Moreover, similar to ATP7A, it remains unknown whether the effects of LIAS in LUAD are mediated, at least in part, through cuproptosis. Thus, further investigation is needed to fill this gap.

In this study, we aimed to elucidate the roles of ATP7A and LIAS in LUAD progression and to uncover their underlying molecular mechanisms. Initially, the expression of ATP7A and LIAS was evaluated in LUAD tissues and cell lines. Subsequently, in vitro and in vivo experiments were performed to assess the impact of ATP7A and LIAS on LUAD cell proliferation and migration. Finally, we further investigated whether the effects of ATP7A and LIAS on LUAD progression were associated with cuproptosis-related mechanisms, thereby providing mechanistic insights into LUAD progression.

Materials and methods

LUAD-related tissues

The study was approved by the Ethical Review Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University (2024-KY [0840]). In this study, five types of tissue samples (n = 20 per group) were collected from the Second Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University, including lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) tumor tissues, paired adjacent non-cancerous (ANC) lung tissues, non-lung cancer (LNC) lung tissues, lymph nodes from LUAD patients (LUAD-ND), and lymph nodes from non-lung cancer patients (LNC-ND). The ANC lung tissues were paired with the LUAD tumor tissues from the same 20 patients.

Consecutive patients who underwent surgery between January 1, 2024, and August 12, 2024, were screened. The inclusion criteria were: (i) availability of surgical specimens with intact tissue architecture suitable for downstream analyses; and (ii) pathologically confirmed diagnosis. The exclusion criteria were: (i) receipt of any antitumor therapy prior to surgery (e.g., chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy, or immunotherapy); and (ii) age < 18 years. Clinicopathological data—including age, sex, tumor size, histologic grade, lymph node metastasis status, and TNM stage—were extracted from medical records. Tumor staging was assigned according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer TNM classification (8th edition) and verified against the pathology report.

Cell culture

Three cell lines utilized in this study were A549 (purchased from the Kunming Cell Bank, Chinese Academy of Sciences), PC9 (purchased from Wuhan PunoSai Life Technology Co., Ltd.), and BEAS-2B (purchased from the Typical Culture Preservation Committee Cell Bank, Chinese Academy of Sciences). All cell lines were cultured in media containing 10% serum and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (P1400, Solarbio) at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. All cell lines were routinely tested negative for mycoplasma contamination, and cell line authentication by STR profiling was provided by the vendors.

Gene modulation

For in vitro experiments, ATP7A knockdown was achieved by transient transfection with ATP7A-specific siRNA (Indite Biotechnology Co., Ltd.), with a scrambled siRNA serving as the negative control (si-NC). Knockdown efficiency was validated by RT-qPCR and western blotting. For xenograft experiments, stable knockdown of ATP7A or LIAS was established by lentiviral transduction of shRNA constructs in the pLVX-shRNA2-PURO vector (Sangon Biotech; L6927 sh-ATP7A and L18034 sh-LIAS), with a non-targeting shRNA as the control (sh-NC). A549 cells were infected at an MOI of 5 in the presence of polybrene (1:200 dilution); the medium was replaced after 6–8 h, and cells were selected with puromycin (2 µg/mL) at 72 h post-infection and maintained in puromycin (1 µg/mL). Stable knockdown was confirmed by RT-qPCR and western blotting. LIAS overexpression was achieved by transient transfection with a pcDNA3.1–3×Flag-C plasmid encoding LIAS (OE-LIAS), and assays were performed 24–48 h post-transfection.

In vitro functional assays

Cell viability assay

Cell viability was assessed using Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Biosharp). At 24 h after transfection/transduction, cells were seeded into 96-well plates (3 × 103 cells/well). At the indicated time points (0, 24, 48, and 72 h after seeding), 10 µL CCK-8 reagent was added to each well and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a FilterMax F3 microplate reader (Molecular Devices). For the additional independent experiments, cells were treated with the pan-caspase inhibitor Z-VAD-FMK and the ferroptosis inhibitor ferrostatin-1, and absorbance was measured 24 h post-transfection. Each condition was analyzed in multiple technical replicates per experiment, and experiments were independently repeated at least three times.

Transwell assay

Cell migration capacity was evaluated through Transwell assays employing 6-well polycarbonate membrane inserts (Corning). Transfected cells (1 × 105 cells/insert) suspended in serum-free DMEM were plated in upper chambers, with lower chambers containing 10% FBS-supplemented DMEM as chemoattractant. Following 24-hour incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO2, membranes were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (30 min), stained with 0.1% crystal violet (20 min), and imaged under phase-contrast microscopy (Nikon Ts2-FL). Quantitative analysis was performed by enumerating transmigrated cells in three randomly selected visual fields per chamber using ImageJ (version 1.52a) with particle analysis module.

Wound-healing assay

A wound-healing (scratch) assay was performed to evaluate cell migration. Cells were seeded into 6-well plates and cultured to a near-confluent monolayer. A linear scratch was generated using a sterile 10-µL pipette tip, and detached cells were removed by washing twice with PBS. Cells were then cultured in serum-reduced medium to minimize proliferation-related effects on wound closure. Images were acquired at 0 h and at the indicated time points at the same marked fields. Wound closure was quantified using ImageJ (version 1.52a), and the migration rate (%) was calculated as: (scratch width at 0 h − scratch width at the indicated time point)/scratch width at 0 h × 100%.

Molecular assays

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

TriQuick Reagent (Solarbio) was used to extract total RNA. A 20 µL reverse transcription reaction was prepared using 4 µL MonScript™ 5× RTIII All-in-One Mix, 1 µg total RNA, 1 µL MonScript™ dsDNase, and nuclease-free water to a final volume of 20 µL. RT-qPCR was performed in triplicate with Hieff® qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Low Rox Plus) on an AriaMx Real-Time PCR System (Agilent). Cycling conditions: 95 °C (60 s); 40 cycles of 95 °C (5 s), 60 °C (30 s), 72 °C (30 s). Relative mRNA levels were normalized to ACTB and calculated via the 2–ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are shown in Supplementary Table 1.

Western blotting

Cells were lysed in RIPA buffer supplemented with PMSF and protease inhibitors (Solarbio) on ice. Lysates were clarified by centrifugation (12,000 × g, 15 min, 4 °C), and protein concentration was determined using a BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equal amounts of protein (20 µg per lane) were separated on 5–15% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore). Membranes were blocked with 8% non-fat milk (Oxoid) in TBST for 1 h at room temperature (~ 25 °C) and incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: anti-ATP7A (Abcam; cat. no. ab308524; 1:1,000), anti-LIAS (Abcam; cat. no. ab246917; 1:2,000), anti-E-cadherin (Proteintech; cat. no. 20874-1-AP; 1:30,000), anti-N-cadherin (Affinity; cat. no. AF5239; 1:3,000), anti-vimentin (Abcam; cat. no. ab92547; 1:40,000), anti-PCNA (Proteintech; cat. no. 60097-1-Ig; 1:5,000), anti-Cyclin D1 (Cell Signaling Technology; cat. no. 2978; 1:1,000), anti-cleaved caspase-3 (Cell Signaling Technology; cat. no. 9664; 1:1,000), anti-cleaved PARP (Cell Signaling Technology; cat. no. 9541; 1:1,000), anti-lipoic acid (Santa Cruz Biotechnology; cat. no. sc-101354; 1:1,000), anti-GAPDH (Proteintech; cat. no. 60004-1-Ig; 1:40,000), anti-β-actin (Abclonal; cat. no. AC026; 1:10,000), and anti-FDX1 (Proteintech; cat. no. 12592-1-AP; 1:5,000). After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Abcam; cat. nos. ab6789 and ab6721; 1:8,000) for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were developed using ECL Prime (Biosharp; cat. no. BL523A) and imaged with a chemiluminescence detection system. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ (version 1.52a) and normalized to the corresponding loading control (GAPDH or β-actin), as indicated.

For assessment of protein lipoylation, anti-lipoic acid immunoblotting was used to detect major lipoylation-related bands. Putative lipoylated DLAT (lip-DLAT) and lipoylated DLST (lip-DLST) bands were assigned according to their expected molecular weights, and band intensities were quantified by densitometry using ImageJ (version 1.52a).

Immunofluorescence staining for lipoic acid–modified proteins

Cells were seeded onto sterile glass coverslips in 24-well plates and allowed to adhere. After treatment/transfection as indicated, cells were fixed with pre-chilled 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min at room temperature, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 5 min, and blocked with 5% BSA for 90 min. Coverslips were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-lipoic acid antibody (Santa Cruz Biotechnology; cat. no. sc-101354; 1:100), followed by goat anti-mouse IgG H&L (Alexa Fluor 647) secondary antibody (Abcam; cat. no. ab150115; 1:500) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (20 µg/mL) for 5 min. Coverslips were mounted and imaged using a fluorescence microscope under identical acquisition settings across groups.

Metabolite level detection and mitochondrial damage assessment

The intracellular levels of pyruvate and α-ketoglutarate were measured to assess cuproptosis-related metabolic alterations associated with ATP7A and LIAS modulation. Pyruvate concentrations were determined using a Pyruvate Content Assay Kit (BC2205, Solarbio) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. α-Ketoglutarate levels were quantified using an α-Ketoglutarate Assay Kit (ab83431, Abcam) following the manufacturer’s instructions, with absorbance measured at the specified wavelength.

Mitochondrial ultrastructural alterations (e.g., cristae collapse, endoplasmic reticulum disruption, and plasma membrane rupture) were analyzed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM; HT7800, Hitachi). LUAD cell pellets were fixed in 4 °C electron microscopy fixative (G1102, Servicebio) for 2–4 h, post-fixed with 1% osmium tetroxide (18456, Ted Pella Inc), and dehydrated through an ethanol gradient. Samples were embedded in EPON 812 resin (90529-77-4, SPI), sectioned into 60–80 nm ultrathin slices using a UC7 ultramicrotome (Leica), and double-stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. TEM images were acquired to document ultrastructural features associated with cuproptosis-related mitochondrial alterations.

Animal studies

Male SPF-grade BALB/c nude mice (4 weeks old, 15–25 g) were purchased from Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. and housed under controlled conditions (20–25 °C; 40–70% relative humidity; 12-h light/dark cycle; 15–20 lx) with ad libitum access to food and water. For xenograft experiments, mice were randomly assigned to four groups (n = 5 per group): control, sh-NC, sh-ATP7A, and sh-LIAS. A549 cells with stable shRNA-mediated knockdown were used as indicated. A549 cells were harvested in the logarithmic growth phase, dissociated into a single-cell suspension, and resuspended in PBS at 2 × 107 cells/mL. Each mouse was subcutaneously inoculated with 100 µL of cell suspension (2 × 106 cells). Body weight and gross images were recorded weekly until tumors became palpable (~ 7 days post-inoculation; ~2 mm in diameter). Tumor size was then measured every 4 days using calipers, and tumor volume was calculated as 0.52 × L × W2, where L is the longest diameter and W is the shortest diameter.

For Ki-67 immunohistochemistry (IHC) and TUNEL assays, an additional independent xenograft cohort was established using the same cell lines, grouping, inoculation procedure, and endpoint criteria as the primary experiment. Tumors from this validation cohort were processed for paraffin embedding and subsequent staining.

Histology and staining of tissue sections

Immunohistochemistry

Tumor tissues were fixed in 4% PFA, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned (3 μm). After deparaffinization and antigen retrieval (EDTA antigen retrieval solution [C1034, Solarbio], pH = 9.0), endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched with 3% H2O2. Sections were blocked with 5% BSA and incubated overnight with anti-ATP7A (1:800), anti-LIAS (1:200), or anti-Ki-67 (Abclonal Technology; A20018; 1:500). Secondary antibody Goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L (Abcam; ab6721; 1:1000) and DAB substrate (ZLI-9018; ZSGB Biotechnology Co., Ltd) were applied for signal development. Slides were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, and imaged using a BX-53 microscope (Olympus). Human LUAD tissues and xenograft tumors were processed using the same IHC workflow unless otherwise specified. For IHC images, protein expression was semi-quantified using the H-score. The H-score (range, 0–300) was calculated within predefined tumor regions of interest using the weighted formula: H-score = (1 × % weakly stained tumor cells) + (2 × % moderately stained tumor cells) + (3 × % strongly stained tumor cells) [21].

TUNEL

Tumor tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned at 3 μm. Sections were deparaffinized (Servicebio; cat. no. G1128), rehydrated through graded ethanol (Sinopharm; cat. no. 100092683), and subjected to TUNEL staining using a commercial kit (Servicebio; cat. no. G1501). After proteinase K treatment (Servicebio; cat. no. G1205) at 37 °C for 22 min, sections were incubated with the TUNEL reaction mixture (TdT enzyme: dUTP: buffer = 1:5:50) at 37 °C for 1 h. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Servicebio; cat. no. G1012) for 10 min, mounted with an antifade medium (Servicebio; cat. no. G1401), and imaged using a fluorescence microscope.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 25.0) and GraphPad Prism (version 8). Data are presented as mean ± SD. Comparisons between two groups were conducted using an independent-samples t-test. For multiple-group comparisons, a one-way analysis of variance was employed. When homogeneity of variance was violated, the Games–Howell test was used; otherwise, the LSD test was applied. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Expression of ATP7A and LIAS in LUAD tissues and cells

Figure 1 presents the assessment of ATP7A and LIAS expression in LUAD tissues compared with ANC, LNC, and LUAD-ND and LNC-ND tissues. RT-qPCR analyses revealed that ATP7A and LIAS mRNA levels were significantly elevated in LUAD tissues compared to the control groups (p < 0.01; Fig. 1A). Immunohistochemical analysis of tissue specimens further showed that the protein expression levels of ATP7A and LIAS were significantly higher in LUAD tissues than in ANC and LNC (both p < 0.01; Figs. 1B and C). In addition, ATP7A and LIAS showed predominantly cytoplasmic staining with minimal nuclear staining (Fig. 1C).

ATP7A and LIAS expression were not significantly associated with sex, age, or maximum tumor diameter (Supplementary Figs. 1 A–C). However, both proteins were significantly associated with aggressive clinicopathological features, with higher expression in poorly differentiated tumors and in tumors with lymph node metastasis (p < 0.05; Supplementary Figs. 1D and E). Their expression also increased with TNM stage, with significantly higher levels in stage III than in stage I (p < 0.01; Supplementary Fig. 1 F), suggesting that ATP7A and LIAS upregulation is associated with more aggressive clinicopathological features in LUAD.

Consistent with IHC results, Western blot analysis demonstrated a marked increase in ATP7A and LIAS protein expression in LUAD (p < 0.01; Figs. 1D and E). Subsequently, the expression of ATP7A and LIAS was evaluated in the LUAD lines A549 and PC9 relative to the normal lung epithelial cell line BEAS-2B. As illustrated in Figs. 1G and H, both mRNA and protein levels of ATP7A and LIAS were markedly upregulated in A549 and PC9 cells (p < 0.05). Representative Western blot images confirmed these observations (Fig. 1F). These findings indicate that ATP7A and LIAS are significantly overexpressed in both LUAD tissues and cell lines.

Validation of ATP7A and LIAS modulation in LUAD cells

Figure 2 illustrates the outcomes of manipulating ATP7A and LIAS expression. In A549 cells, siRNA-mediated knockdown of ATP7A led to a pronounced decrease in ATP7A mRNA levels (p < 0.01; Fig. 2A), which was mirrored by a corresponding reduction in ATP7A protein expression using the Western blot analysis (p < 0.01; Figs. 2B and C). Meanwhile, shRNA-mediated silencing of LIAS significantly downregulated LIAS mRNA and protein levels in A549 cells (p < 0.01; Figs. 2D–F). Overexpression of LIAS (OE-LIAS) in A549 cells markedly elevated LIAS mRNA and protein levels (p < 0.01; Figs. 2G–I). Consistent with the findings in A549 cells, the transfection of si-ATP7A, sh-LIAS, and OE-LIAS in PC9 cells was successfully validated (p < 0.01; Supplementary Figs. 2A–I).

Interaction between ATP7A and LIAS in LUAD cells

Given that ATP7A and LIAS exhibited similar expression patterns in LUAD tissues and cells, this study further investigated whether an interaction exists between these two genes in LUAD. Notably, LIAS knockdown in A549 cells resulted in a significant reduction in ATP7A mRNA and protein levels (p < 0.01; Fig. 2J and K), and this observation was corroborated in PC9 cells (p < 0.01; Supplementary Figs. 2 J and K), indicating a potential regulatory interplay between these two genes.

Figure 3 demonstrates the coordinated regulation and direct interaction between ATP7A and LIAS in LUAD cells. In A549 cells, siRNA-mediated knockdown of ATP7A significantly reduced ATP7A mRNA and protein expression levels (p < 0.01, Figs. 3A, B, and I). Overexpression of LIAS (OE-LIAS) partially reversed the decline in ATP7A expression at both mRNA and protein levels induced by siRNA-mediated ATP7A knockdown (p < 0.01, Figs. 3A, B, and I). Similar results were observed in PC9 cells (p < 0.01, Figs. 3C, D, and J). LIAS overexpression remained effective under si-ATP7A co-treatment, as evidenced by sustained LIAS upregulation (p < 0.05, Figs. 3A–J).

Moreover, co-immunoprecipitation assays supported a physical interaction between ATP7A and LIAS in both A549 and PC9 cells (Fig. 3K). Collectively, these results underscore the functional interplay between ATP7A and LIAS in LUAD cells and suggest that LIAS overexpression may modulate the impact of ATP7A knockdown, thereby contributing to LUAD pathogenesis.

Effects of ATP7A and LIAS on LUAD cell proliferation

To systematically evaluate the effects of ATP7A and LIAS knockdown or overexpression on LUAD cell proliferation, CCK-8 assays were performed. Transfection with si-ATP7A resulted in a significant decrease in OD values at 72 h compared to both the untreated and negative control groups (si-NC) (p < 0.05, Fig. 4A), indicating that ATP7A knockdown suppresses cell proliferation. Similarly, LIAS knockdown (sh-LIAS) significantly reduced cell proliferation relative to its negative control (sh-NC) (p < 0.05, Fig. 4B). Notably, when si-ATP7A was combined with LIAS overexpression (OE-LIAS), a partial recovery of the proliferation curve was observed (p < 0.05, Fig. 4C), suggesting that upregulation of LIAS can partially offset the proliferation inhibition induced by ATP7A knockdown.

Furthermore, similar inhibitory effects of si-ATP7A and sh-LIAS on LUAD cell proliferation were validated in PC9 cells using the CCK-8 experiment (Supplementary Figs. 3 A–D).

Effects of ATP7A and LIAS on proliferation- and apoptosis-related proteins

To further clarify whether ATP7A or LIAS regulates proliferation- and apoptosis-associated proteins in LUAD cells, we performed western blot analyses. si-ATP7A significantly reduced the protein levels of the proliferation markers PCNA and Cyclin D1 compared with the corresponding negative control (si-NC) in both A549 and PC9 cells (p < 0.05; Supplementary Figs. 4 A and B). Similarly, sh-LIAS resulted in a significant decrease in PCNA and Cyclin D1 relative to sh-NC in both cell lines (p < 0.05; Supplementary Figs. 4 A and B). In contrast, the apoptosis markers cleaved caspase-3 and cleaved PARP showed no significant changes following either si-ATP7A or sh-LIAS treatment compared with their respective negative controls (p > 0.05; Supplementary Figs. 4 A and B). Collectively, these findings provide molecular support for the growth-inhibitory phenotype observed in the CCK-8 assays and indicate that depletion of ATP7A or LIAS suppresses LUAD cell proliferation without overt activation of caspase-dependent apoptosis under the current experimental conditions.

Effects of ATP7A and LIAS on LUAD cell migration

In the assessment of cell migration, the wound-healing assay demonstrated that, compared with the corresponding negative controls, si-ATP7A or sh-LIAS treatment significantly impaired cell migration, as evidenced by reduced migration rates at both 24 h and 48 h (Supplementary Fig. 5). Notably, under si-ATP7A conditions, LIAS overexpression partially restored migratory capacity (p < 0.05). These findings were consistent in both A549 (Supplementary Figs. 5 A and C) and PC9 (Supplementary Figs. 5B and D) cells. Similarly, Transwell assays demonstrated that treatment with either si-ATP7A or sh-LIAS markedly reduced the number of migrating cells (Fig. 4D), with quantitative analyses further corroborating these findings (p < 0.05, Figs. 4E and F). Moreover, co-transfection with si-ATP7A and OE-LIAS led to a partial restoration of migration activity compared to si-ATP7A treatment alone (p < 0.05, Figs. 4G and H), indicating that LIAS overexpression can partially reverse the inhibitory effect of ATP7A knockdown on cell migration. Similar results were validated in PC9 cells (Supplementary Figs. 3D–H).

Impact of ATP7A and LIAS expression on epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT)

In this study, the impact of ATP7A and LIAS expression on EMT was investigated by examining changes in the EMT-related markers E-cadherin, N-cadherin, and Vimentin. As shown in Fig. 5, ATP7A and LIAS modulate the expression of these key EMT markers in A549 cells. Knockdown of ATP7A (si-ATP7A) or LIAS (sh-LIAS), alone or in combination, led to marked alterations in the mRNA levels of E-cadherin, N-cadherin, and Vimentin. Notably, E-cadherin expression was significantly upregulated, whereas N-cadherin and Vimentin were downregulated, indicating a partial reversion of the mesenchymal phenotype (p < 0.05, Figs. 5A and C). Western blot analysis confirmed these observations at the protein level (p < 0.05, Figs. 5B, D, E, and F). These results were also validated in PC9 cells (p < 0.05, Supplementary Fig. 6).

In A549 cells, si-ATP7A led to a significant upregulation of E-cadherin mRNA levels and a concomitant reduction in N-cadherin and Vimentin mRNA levels, whereas OE-LIAS reversed these effects (p < 0.05, Fig. 6A). Impact of ATP7A and LIAS depletion on apoptosis- and ferroptosis-dependence of viability loss

To delineate the mode of cell death underlying the observed reduction in viability, we performed inhibitor-based assays. Specifically, to determine whether the decreased cell viability induced by ATP7A or LIAS depletion is associated with caspase-dependent apoptosis or ferroptosis, LUAD cells were treated with the pan-caspase inhibitor Z-VAD-FMK or the ferroptosis inhibitor ferrostatin-1 following ATP7A or LIAS depletion. In both A549 and PC9 cells, Z-VAD-FMK did not significantly restore the reduced cell viability caused by si-ATP7A or sh-LIAS compared with the corresponding knockdown groups without inhibitor treatment (p > 0.05; Supplementary Figs. 7 A and C). Consistently, ferrostatin-1 also failed to reverse the viability reduction induced by si-ATP7A or sh-LIAS in either cell line (p > 0.05; Supplementary Figs. 7B and D). These findings suggest that, under the current experimental conditions, the loss of cell viability following ATP7A or LIAS depletion is not prominently mediated by caspase-dependent apoptosis or ferroptosis.

ATP7A and LIAS are associated with cuproptosis-related alterations in LUAD cells

To investigate whether ATP7A and LIAS are associated with cuproptosis-related alterations in LUAD cells, we examined cuproptosis-related phenotypes following modulation of ATP7A and LIAS expression. To further evaluate cuproptosis-related mitochondrial ultrastructural changes, mitochondrial morphology was assessed by transmission electron microscopy. Electron microscopy images revealed that si-ATP7A and sh-LIAS disrupted the mitochondrial structure, rendering it disorganized and fragmented, whereas OE-LIAS ameliorated these alterations (Supplementary Figs. 8 A–F).

FDX1 is a key regulator associated with cuproptosis, and elevated levels of pyruvate and α-ketoglutarate represent key biological characteristics of this process. Accordingly, this study examined how ATP7A and LIAS affect FDX1 expression, as well as pyruvate and α-ketoglutarate levels, to elucidate their roles in cuproptosis. Based on experiments in A549 cells, under si-ATP7A or sh-LIAS treatment, FDX1 mRNA expression was significantly elevated (p < 0.01; Figs. 7 A and B), suggesting that ATP7A and LIAS are associated with FDX1 regulation. Moreover, overexpression of LIAS reversed the si-ATP7A–induced upregulation of FDX1 (p < 0.01; Fig. 7C). These transcriptional changes were further confirmed at the protein level (p < 0.01; Figs. 7D–F). Knockdown of either ATP7A or LIAS resulted in significant increases in the concentrations of pyruvate (p < 0.01; Figs. 7G and H) and α-ketoglutarate (p < 0.01; Figs. 7J and K), and overexpression of LIAS reversed these effects (p < 0.01; Figs. 7I and L).

Tumor growth curves (Fig. 9A) and representative images of excised tumors (Fig. 9B) showed that mice receiving sh-ATP7A or sh-LIAS exhibited significantly reduced tumor volumes and weights compared with the control and sh-NC groups (p < 0.01; Figs. 9C and D).

Hematoxylin and eosin and IHC analyses were further employed to validate the effects of ATP7A and LIAS on FDX1 expression. Discussion

LUAD ranks among the most threatening malignancies in terms of both incidence and mortality [1, 7]. This study, leveraging diverse sample types (tissues and cells), multiple omics levels (mRNA and protein), and a range of experimental techniques (CCK8 and Transwell assays), demonstrated that ATP7A and LIAS synergistically promote the proliferation, invasion, and migration of LUAD cells. In addition, both in vitro cell models and in vivo nude mouse models were employed to elucidate the regulatory mechanisms of ATP7A and LIAS on cuproptosis, thereby deepening the understanding of the molecular processes driving LUAD progression. These findings offer valuable insights for mechanism-based research on LUAD and the identification of potential therapeutic targets.

ATP7A and LIAS are known to be aberrantly expressed in various cancers. Previous reports have indicated that ATP7A is overexpressed in cholangiocarcinoma, colon adenocarcinoma, kidney chromophobe, liver hepatocellular carcinoma, and stomach adenocarcinoma [15, 16]. In contrast, ATP7A expression is downregulated in certain malignancies, such as bladder urothelial carcinoma and lung squamous cell carcinoma [15, 16]. Similarly, abnormal expression of LIAS has been observed in several tumors, including cholangiocarcinoma, stomach adenocarcinoma, and stomach adenocarcinoma [15, 17]. Bioinformatics studies by Zhou et al. [15] revealed that the mRNA levels of both ATP7A and LIAS are upregulated in LUAD tissues, while studies by Liu et al. [17] and Li et al. [16] did not supported this findings. The experimental data presented herein unequivocally demonstrate that both mRNA and protein expression levels of ATP7A and LIAS are elevated in LUAD tissues and cell lines. Co-immunoprecipitation assays demonstrated a direct physical interaction between ATP7A and LIAS. Furthermore, the higher expression of ATP7A and LIAS in lymph node tissues from patients with LUAD than in control lymph node tissues, together with the association of ATP7A and LIAS upregulation with more aggressive clinicopathological features in LUAD, suggests that aberrant expression of these genes may be associated with LUAD metastasis. Collectively, these results imply that ATP7A and LIAS may be critical determinants in LUAD pathogenesis and progression, with a synergistic interaction between them.

ATP7A and LIAS appear to facilitate LUAD development. Prior studies have implicated ATP7A in the oncogenesis of hepatocellular carcinoma, ovarian cancer, esophageal cancer, and breast cancer [22–25]. In NSCLC, elevated ATP7A expression is associated with poor chemotherapeutic responses, reduced overall survival [14, 26], and cisplatin resistance [27, 28]. In contrast, LIAS may play divergent roles in different tumor types; while high LIAS expression correlates with a favorable prognosis in renal clear cell carcinoma, rectal adenocarcinoma, and breast cancer [19, 20], in LUAD, elevated LIAS expression is linked to poor patient outcomes [20]. In the present study, downregulation of ATP7A or LIAS inhibited LUAD cell proliferation and migration, whereas LIAS overexpression partially rescued these effects. Consistent with these phenotypic changes, ATP7A or LIAS depletion reduced the expression of the proliferation-related proteins PCNA and Cyclin D1, supporting the growth-inhibitory phenotype observed in the CCK-8 assays. In contrast, cleaved caspase-3 and cleaved PARP were not significantly altered, suggesting that the reduced viability/proliferative capacity was not accompanied by overt caspase-dependent apoptosis in vitro under the current experimental conditions. Together with the xenograft data, these findings support a pro-tumorigenic role for ATP7A and LIAS in LUAD.

The potential association among ATP7A, LIAS, and EMT may enhance the migration and invasion of LUAD cells. EMT causes epithelial cells to lose cell–cell adhesion and polarity, thereby promoting malignant behaviors, such as increased migration, invasion, and resistance to apoptosis [29]. Key markers of EMT include E-cadherin, N-cadherin, and Vimentin; typically, EMT activation results in decreased E-cadherin expression, while N-cadherin and Vimentin are upregulated, thereby facilitating cell migration and invasion [30, 31]. In our study, downregulation of ATP7A or LIAS significantly affected the expression of these markers, and overexpression of LIAS partially restored the effects of ATP7A on E-cadherin, N-cadherin, and Vimentin. These results suggest that high expression of ATP7A and LIAS may synergistically regulate EMT and thereby promote LUAD progression.

ATP7A and LIAS also play critical roles in regulating cuproptosis in LUAD cells. ATP7A, a copper-transporting ATPase, mediates the efflux of copper ions, thereby reducing intracellular copper levels and negatively modulating the initiation of cuproptosis [18, 32]. In contrast, LIAS maintains mitochondrial function and energy metabolism by catalyzing lipoylation modifications essential for the activity of TCA cycle enzymes [9]. When intracellular copper levels become abnormally elevated, impaired LIAS function may lead to mitochondrial dysfunction and increased oxidative stress, triggering cuproptosis [9, 18]. Although previous studies have established the clinical significance of ATP7A and LIAS in NSCLC [27, 28], research on their association with cuproptosis in LUAD remains limited. In our study, silencing ATP7A or LIAS resulted in pronounced mitochondrial damage, including cristae collapse and endoplasmic reticulum disruption, which are hallmark features of cuproptosis [8, 33]. Moreover, FDX1—a key mitochondrial electron transfer reductase that facilitates the reduction of Cu2+ to Cu+ and enhances the binding efficiency between copper and lipoylated proteins—was upregulated upon induction of cuproptosis [9, 34]. The resulting decrease in key TCA cycle enzyme activity led to the accumulation of pyruvate and α-ketoglutarate, metabolic changes that are considered hallmark indicators of cuproptosis [8]. Our findings indicate that ATP7A and LIAS are associated with cuproptosis-related alterations in LUAD cells. In addition to changes in FDX1 expression and cuproptosis-related metabolites, depletion of ATP7A or LIAS also altered lipoylation-related readouts, and these effects were partially reversed by LIAS overexpression. Under the current experimental conditions, the reduction in cell viability following ATP7A or LIAS depletion was not prominently mediated by caspase-dependent apoptosis or ferroptosis. Taken together with the in vivo xenograft data, these findings suggest that ATP7A and LIAS promote LUAD development, at least in part, through modulation of cuproptosis-related pathways.

Despite providing significant insights into the roles of ATP7A and LIAS in LUAD, this study has several limitations. First, the clinical value of ATP7A and LIAS in LUAD requires further validation using additional clinical parameters, such as prognostic data, from multicenter studies. Second, a deeper investigation into the upstream signaling networks regulating ATP7A and LIAS is necessary for a comprehensive understanding of their roles in LUAD. Finally, further in vitro and in vivo experiments are warranted to explore the therapeutic potential of targeting ATP7A and LIAS in LUAD treatment.

In summary, the results of this study indicate that ATP7A and LIAS synergistically regulate cuproptosis, thereby promoting LUAD progression. These findings suggest that ATP7A and LIAS may serve as promising therapeutic targets for LUAD, with the potential to improve patient outcomes by inducing cuproptosis.
