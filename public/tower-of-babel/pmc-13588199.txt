Systems biology framework for the rational design of operational conditions for in vitro/in vivo translation of tissue models
Authors: Jose L. Cadavid, Nikolaos Meimetis, Tyler Matsuzaki, Erin N. Tevonian, Linda G. Griffith, Douglas A. Lauffenburger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Preclinical models are used extensively to study diseases and therapies. In vitro monoculture or microphysiological system (MPS) platforms incorporating multiple different human cell types can emulate diseased tissues, but determining experimental conditions (e.g., media supplements) that provide the most effective translatability to humans (in vivo) is a major challenge. Using metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) as a case study, we developed a machine learning framework [called LIV2TRANS (Latent In Vitro to In Vivo Translation)] that first maps MPS onto in vivo data, then elucidates translation insights, and lastly nominates experimental conditions that increase translatability. Our findings highlight TGFβ (transforming growth factor–β) as a crucial cue for MPS translatability and indicate that adding interferon-mediated JAK (Janus kinase)-STAT (signal transducer and activator of transcription) signaling perturbations could increase the predictive performance of MPS for MASLD. Last, an optimization algorithm highlights key signaling pathways to maximize germane human-relevant information captured by this MPS. This work establishes a mathematically principled approach for identifying experimental conditions that most beneficially capture in vivo–relevant molecular processes, generalizable to a wide range of diseases where suitable molecular data exist.

INTRODUCTION

Ethical, practical, and economic constraints on human subject research for the study of mechanisms of diseases and their treatments necessitate the use of preclinical models of disease. While animal models are a gold standard in preclinical research, they have only an ∼10% rate, broadly, of successful translation to the clinic (1) and often fail to predict drug toxicities resulting from inherent interspecies differences (2). An emerging alternative approach is that of in vitro complex cell culture models engineered with human cells, also known as microphysiological systems (MPSs), as they can avoid interspecies differences. MPS platforms, one pillar of the emerging field of “NAMs” [novel alternative methods or novel approach methodologies (3, 4)], can be designed to comprise diverse cell types, extracellular matrix components, and diffusible molecular stimuli relevant to a particular disease. Expectedly, NAM approaches are being increasingly adopted by industry (5, 6) and regulatory agencies (7). However, MPSs exist along a wide spectrum of biological and operational complexity associated with a range of experimental throughput and cost. Therefore, defining biological parameters (cell types, extracellular matrix components, and molecular stimuli), or “cues,” for the operation of a given MPS to enhance its translational potential is crucial for its adoption as a useful preclinical model. We posit that combining MPS NAMs with another NAM pillar, computational systems biology, will enable tremendous new power in using NAMs to understand human disease and devise new therapies.

Among disease areas for which MPS platforms are being used extensively is metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) (8–10) and hepatotoxicity (11, 12). Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, a form of MASLD, is one of the most common liver diseases in the world, occurring in almost 25% of the population worldwide (13). MASLD’s clinical outcome ranges from simple steatosis to metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis (MASH; previously called NASH) and can lead to liver cirrhosis (14). Fat tissue–derived free fatty acids, among other products, can lead to steatosis and lobular inflammation. Accumulation of inflammation-associated cells, cytokines, and other signals can lead to chronic necroinflammation, marking an MAFL-to-MASH transition. Chronic hepatocyte cell death and mild to advanced fibrosis, together with increased levels of tumor necrosis factors, transforming growth factor–β (TGFβ), and interleukin-18, result in increased hepatocellular carcinoma risk (14). The complexity of the disease and the plethora of molecular signals in the tissue microenvironment have led to the development of a wide array of in vitro models of disease that mimic specific phenotypes of interest, such as fibrosis and steatosis, each providing different trade-offs between throughput, complexity, and translatability (15).

Without a complete understanding of the molecular and cellular processes driving disease pathophysiology, the definition of “cues” required in vitro to achieve good MPS translation is often on the basis of putative factors based on prior knowledge of disease biology (6). A direct functional and molecular (e.g., transcriptomic) comparison between in vitro and in vivo systems (16, 17) is commonly used to rank MPS models for specific contexts of use (18), and a computational comparison at the pathway level can be used to assess whether a perturbation-induced response of an in vitro model is similar to the response observed in vivo (19). However, these approaches assume the need for a direct one-to-one mapping of the most obvious phenotypic characteristics (e.g., lipid droplets and collagen) between MPS and clinical samples, whereas multiple pathways contribute to the development of a complex compendium of phenotypic traits in vivo and in vitro. In cases with mechanistic insights arising from vast troves of historical data of outcomes in human clinical populations, even simple molecular cues provided to monocultures in a two-dimensional MPS can result in clinically relevant responses to perturbations and treatments (20, 21), if they provide the vital combination of effects at the pathway level to recapitulate known human in vivo responses. Our overarching goal is to determine the most informative set of cues for increasing the predictive power of MPS models for a desired application without necessarily increasing the MPS complexity, i.e., we strive to identify the minimal degree of complexity required for the intended use case. To this end, a framework capable of mathematically relating multiple pathways, integratively to well-considered phenotypic metrics across a landscape of modulatory cues, should be valuable.

A data-driven systems biology framework, in combination with machine learning (ML) computational modeling, can provide the tools to enable the systematic understanding and design of in vitro models (22). Systems biology as a field enables comprehension of the operating principles of complex biological systems by observing their responses under multiple perturbation conditions (23, 24) and developing tools that allow insightful, metric-oriented comparison between different biological systems (e.g., in vitro and in vivo models). In addition, ML can fill this gap by acting as a global function estimation framework that maps observations between in vitro and in vivo models, i.e., when there is no causative mechanistic link driving an obvious closed-form mathematical formulation on the basis of a priori–identified specific molecular signatures, ML incorporates all the data to estimate a single mathematical function (learned from data) that translates perturbation responses in one system to expected responses in the other. Existing ML-based work has focused on either direct translation of molecular profiles between different biological systems (25–27), to make predictions of how humans will respond to a drug or other intervention on the basis of responses in preclinical models (animal, simple cell culture, or MPS), or identifying in the process molecular signatures of the reference experimental system that appear most germane for understanding phenotypes in the human patient (or vice versa) (28–31). These approaches enable some degree of translation of observations made in the experimental models back to humans in the clinic. However, when outcomes in humans fail to replicate outcomes in the experimental system, these approaches are not inherently designed to identify conditions needed to enhance an existing experimental system so that it better recapitulates the observed human phenotypic characteristics while also having some level of fidelity to the underlying molecular processes governing these characteristics.

To mitigate these gaps in prediction, using MASLD/MASH as a case study, we developed an ML approach for the rational design of cues to be added as enhancements to tune an in vitro system toward more faithful recapitulation of a particular human disease state and, ultimately, therapeutic response. We call this approach LIV2TRANS (Latent In Vitro to In Vivo Translation). At a conceptual level, LIV2TRANS operates according to the following logic (Fig. 1): (i) analyze patient data to define which molecular patterns (here, gene expression patterns) are strongly implicated in clinically defined disease phenotypes (here, phenotypes are based on well-accepted histopathology scores); (ii) determine how much of that clinically relevant molecular information is either already represented or still missing in the current MPS dataset, where the MPS was operated to include various cues (e.g., inflammation signals like TGFβ) intended to induce disease phenotypes, and tease out the operating conditions (or condition) that most strongly align with and represent the disease-predictive molecular directions captured in patients (in our case study, conditions including TGFβ emerged as the dominant contributors to the translatable signal); and (iii) last, on the basis of a comparative analysis of how patient-derived and disease-predictive gene expression signatures are retained or lost when looking into the MPS gene expression space, nominate additional cues that would move the MPS toward gene expression directions that are relevant for the disease phenotype in the clinical data but are missing in the MPS dataset.

Data-driven modeling pipeline to identify translatable and missing information from MPSs and human data.

Our approach, LIV2TRANS, finds translatable information between in vitro and in vivo systems by operating in a latent space built from molecular features measured in both systems.

In this example study, to illustrate the LIV2TRANS computational NAM, we interrogated the patient-pathology axis in the clinical bulk RNA dataset from Govaere et al. (32). Using pathway activity inference revealed that the supervised disease-associated gene expression variation (as previously defined) driven by Janus kinase (JAK)-signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT) signaling is missing in the MPS (10). From this analysis, together with publicly available libraries (33) linking stimuli to transcriptional patterns, we predicted that interferon (IFN)–mediated JAK-STAT stimulation would generate missing, disease-relevant molecular variance in the liver MPS and enhance its predictive power, a prediction we subsequently validated experimentally. This increased predictive power using the augmented molecular space of the MPS is replicated using two independent clinical datasets (34, 35) as external test sets for the validation of the approach. Repeating the same in vitro–to–clinical translation analysis with data from additional in vitro (8, 9) liver models, paired with the aforementioned clinical datasets (34, 35), shows that JAK-STAT signaling perturbation is a feature that is generally missing across existing in vitro systems that purport to model MASLD/MASH.

Last, recognizing that not only are the clinical scores imperfect in capturing all biological phenomena associated with disease but that patients may have other attributes (notably, sex and age) not considered in our supervised analysis, we conduct a second unsupervised analysis on gene expression variance in the entire dataset, which we call unsupervised total variance, to discern groups of genes that describe the total variance in the data. We expect that this will include the disease-associated variance, as it should be one of the dominant sources of variation in the clinical dataset. We then interrogate the genes revealed by the unsupervised total variance analysis to discern additional molecular pathways that may be informative in disease; pathways revealed in this way (e.g., sex) may or may not be suspected already as important in delineating subgroups of patients with similar disease scores. We observed that, while this unsupervised total variance analysis also revealed JAK-STAT signaling to be a missing feature in the MPS model, androgen signaling emerged as well, suggesting that sex differences (not considered in the supervised analysis) may be a critical feature to capture in the MPS.

This integrated approach of translation between human patients and MPS data (and vice versa) is arguably particularly powerful because longitudinal and intervention experiments can be conducted in an MPS that would be logistically or ethically very difficult to conduct in real human patients. In the case of MASLD/MASH, sufficient data on disease state and interventions in the clinical setting already exist and continue to emerge steadily because of the increasing prevalence worldwide. This enables LIV2TRANS to facilitate better in vitro/in vivo translation across a landscape of molecular signatures associated with disease states, as well as additional patient population features (such as sex), affording a real-world way to build and evolve computational and experimental NAMs quickly and efficiently.

RESULTS

Model translation pipeline to identify translatable and missing information in MPSs

To be informative, first and foremost, a preclinical model should capture the disease-associated variation of the clinical data, i.e., the patient-to-patient molecular (here, gene expression) differences that track with clinically accepted differences in disease severity (MASLD and fibrosis scores), and can be used by regression models for making predictions. Ideally, an MPS would be designed in a way that allows one-to-one mapping between in vitro and in vivo conditions. In reality, this is impractical, not only due to the barriers in capturing donor-to-donor variability in genetic backgrounds, comorbidities, and age but also with reasonable limits on experimental throughput. It is also challenging because, even regarding the disease phenotype, only certain phenotypic features are described with metrics in the clinical data; some of them may be superfluous, and other critical phenotypic metrics that are difficult or impossible to measure may be missing. Which metrics are truly essential remains an ongoing process of discovery as robust in vivo and in vitro datasets evolve in the literature. In this study, we use two clinically accepted phenotypic scores (available in the public datasets) derived via histopathology of liver biopsies: the MASLD score (MAS) and fibrosis stage. Both scores are numerical ordinal values that track the disease progression with fibrosis stage ranging from 0 (no fibrosis) to 4 (advanced fibrosis to the extent of cirrhosis). MASLD ranges from 0 to 8 and is defined as the sum of three different histopathology scores: steatosis (defining lipid accumulation), lobular inflammation, and hepatocyte ballooning. The aforementioned one-to-one mapping requirement is unnecessarily restrictive, as prohibitively complex in vitro models would be required to capture the precise spectrum of all features. Moreover, there should be no expectation that a single in vitro model will correlate well with human data across all potential scenarios. In reality, the required functional mapping between in vivo and in vitro systems is only partial and needs to capture only the features that are salient for the particular biological question requiring translation. We accordingly use a data-driven ML modeling framework that maps information from in vitro models to in vivo models without explicitly requiring one-to-one mapping by exploiting the covariance among common predictive molecular features measured in both contexts, similarly to that recently demonstrated for improved translation of preclinical animal model data (36).

Our aim here is to illustrate how the resulting LIV2TRANS approach is deployed for the rational design of cues to identify translatable and missing information in in vitro systems using an MPS model of MASLD/MASH as a case study, linking in vitro and in vivo systems through a set of molecular features that are predictive of human clinical phenotypes and that are measured in both systems. We use transcriptomic data as such data are the most prevalent and accessible for the situation of MASLD/MASH, but the approach is amenable to any type of molecular measurement, provided that sufficient data are available in both systems. In LIV2TRANS, instead of assuming that particular molecular features should match between systems or domains, we posit that a more important requirement for translation is that the MPS model should capture disease-associated biological variance (here, in gene expression) that is predictive of clinically relevant phenotypes in response to disease-related perturbations. Practically, this means that if variance in clinical gene expression is predictive of the clinical phenotype, then its predictive power would be lost when discarding biological variance that occurs in clinical data but is not captured in the MPS.

As a first step, we use clinical transcriptomic and phenotypic datasets across MASLD/MASH disease states to construct an initial supervised ML model that predicts human clinical phenotypes from molecular data to determine its information content (Fig. 1, step 1). For the case study of MASLD/MASH, we used partial least squares regression (PLSR) to build a model because PLSR accommodates more than one predicted phenotype, and it can be implemented to calculate latent variables (LVs) that contain disease-associated variance in the data. This multiple-phenotype approach is especially important for a progressive disease situation like MASLD/MASH, as clinical metrics define several phenotypes that emerge or take on increased severity at different disease stages. For other applications of LIV2TRANS where clinical phenotypes are categorical variables (e.g., healthy versus disease) instead of quantitative or ordinal, linear models such as partial least squares discriminant analysis would be more appropriate instead of PLSR. We then build a representation of the gene expression space spanned by the MPS model using principal components analysis (PCA) and project human gene expression data onto this MPS principal component (PC) space (Fig. 1, step 2). This projection truncates clinically derived data to the gene expression space spanned by the MPS and therefore eliminates biological variance not captured in vitro. Projected clinically derived data are then back-projected to the clinical subject gene expression space and used to predict clinical phenotypes with the model trained in step 1. The loss in predictive power of the model when using clinical data that have been “passed” through the MPS PC space is a quantitative metric of the extent to which the MPS captures supervised disease-associated molecular variance. In what follows, we refer to the projection/back-projection step as “truncating” the clinically derived data for simplicity.

In general, the MPS PC space may only contain a limited number of directions in the gene expression space (defined by the PCs) that are relevant for preserving the portion of clinical gene expression variance that is predictive of MASLD and fibrosis scores in a clinical cohort (while other directions can be removed with little or no loss in predictive performance). We refer to the combinations of these relevant directions as translatable components (TCs). Operationally, TCs are constructed as linear combinations of MPS PCs chosen so that, when clinical gene expression is projected and then back-projected through only these linearly combined directions, the clinical data–derived PLSR model yields essentially the same MASLD and fibrosis predictions as when clinical data are filtered through the full MPS PC space (Fig. 1, step 3, and Materials and Methods). Intuitively, TCs are the few “information-carrying” directions in the MPS (if any at all) that align with the phenotype-predictive axes learned from clinical data; all remaining PC directions can be truncated without affecting the phenotype prediction for the clinical response. TCs are not defined a priori by known cues, but we identify them purely from this prediction-preservation criterion and only afterward interpret them using pathway and perturbation signatures.

Once we have identified the TCs that capture the portion of disease-associated patient variance (related to the MASLD score and fibrosis stage) already aligned within the MPS PC space, we next ask what disease-associated variance remains outside that space in the clinical dataset. To do this, we conceptually truncate the clinical data through the MPS PC space and compare the resulting PLSR predictions to those obtained using the full clinical dataset. Any observed loss in predictive performance reflects disease-associated variance that cannot be expressed by any linear combination of MPS PCs and suggests that experimental conditions need enhancements to fully capture the human clinical condition. We then identify a set of extra LVs (LV Extra), defined as a set of directions in the gene expression space in the clinical dataset that are orthogonal to the MPS PCs, which define the gene expression variance that is covered by all the MPS experimental conditions present in the dataset, yet the LV Extra restore the PLSR predictive performance when included alongside the TCs (Fig. 1, step 3). In other words, LV Extra parameterize gene expression directions, predictive of disease phenotype, that are present in human patients but absent from the current MPS. Interrogating the TCs can enable the identification and prioritization of cues already present in the MPS that are key for capturing the human clinical phenotype, while the LV Extra can inform the selection of new cues that generate additional biological variance in the MPS to potentially capture more of the disease-associated molecular variability (of the clinical data) in future MPS experiments (Fig. 1, step 4). Summarizing these mathematical steps conceptually, instead of aiming to replicate human phenotypes perfectly in a single MPS, our approach leverages the controllability of MPS models to propose cues that can facilitate better in vitro/in vivo translation by pooling the variance, hence information, contained in a set of MPS models with diverse cues.

Human gene expression is predictive of MASLD/MASH histological scores

The first step in our computational pipeline was to build a mathematical model that links the measured patient molecular features to the relevant clinical phenotype. Using MASLD/MASH as a case study, we reanalyzed a large clinical dataset obtained from human biopsies collected across different stages of the disease and characterized with RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) (32). Given that both the MASLD score and fibrosis stage are clinically relevant for the diagnosis and prognosis of MASLD/MASH (37, 38), we sought to model both phenotypes simultaneously from gene expression data. While it is possible to build independent models for each phenotype (35), in reality, both scores correlate as the disease progresses (Fig. 2B), and modeling them independently ignores the biology common to both. Therefore, we trained a PLSR model to predict both phenotypes jointly, thereby learning gene expression weights associated with disease progression, as this is defined by the MASLD score and fibrosis stage (although more granular phenotypic metrics could further benefit disease modeling). PLSR reduces the dimensionality of the gene expression data, projects the human clinical samples onto a set of LVs required to predict a set of phenotypes, and therefore provides an estimate of the gene expression subspace relevant to model MASLD/MASH. The projected human scores are then regressed against each phenotype of interest, linking gene expression to phenotype. After tuning the optimal number of LVs (see Materials and Methods and fig. S1), we trained PLSR models using a 10-fold cross-test split with all the data. The resulting PLSR model remains predictive in 10-fold cross-validation and performs significantly better than models built with shuffled data (Fig. 2C) and generalizes well to independent clinical datasets (fig. S2).

Having verified the robustness of the PLSR model, we retrained the model with the complete dataset and obtained predictions that correlate well with the annotated scores (Fig. 2D) within training data (Spearman’s rank correlation values of 0.85 and 0.94, respectively). The variance in patient gene expression captured by the eight supervised LVs in the model corresponds to only 42% of total clinical gene expression variance (around 15% in the first two LVs; Fig. 2E), suggesting that a substantial portion of the variability in clinical gene expression data is not predictive of the MASLD/MASH stage, at least not in terms of MASLD score and fibrosis stage. The remaining variance in the clinical data reflects other biology (e.g., sex) and/or technical noise that, while present in the cohort, does not contribute to the prediction of the disease scores as they are currently defined clinically. Overall, these results confirm that gene expression contains information about disease progression and indicate that the PLSR model captures the gene expression variance relevant to characterizing the progression of patients’ MASLD/MASH, i.e., what we have already defined as the supervised disease-associated gene expression variance in the clinical data. As a note, other nonlinear models with potentially better fit to the data could be used instead of PLSR. However, as a linear model, PLSR requires fewer samples than nonlinear models and is inherently more interpretable, which allows us to more easily examine the biological significance of the latent axes of gene expression variation.

Biological clinical variance predictive of MASLD/MASH scores is not fully captured in a microphysiological model of disease

Having built a model to predict human MASLD/MASH scores from gene expression, we next evaluated the extent to which a well-characterized liver MPS captures disease-associated clinical variance (Fig. 2A). Operationally, we define supervised disease-associated gene expression variance as the component of the total patient gene expression variance that contributes to predicting the MASLD score and fibrosis stage. When we assess how much of this variance is captured by the MPS, we are asking what fraction of these phenotype-linked directions in human gene expression can be reproduced by varying cues in the MPS. We chose this particular MPS for our case study because it was operated with a diverse set of cues intended to recapitulate key phenotypic features of MASH, as determined by standard in vitro assays for lipid accumulation and other features (10, 39). Furthermore, the authors collected a large compendium of gene expression data obtained from multiple experimental conditions that included combinations of exogenous TGFβ, cholesterol, lipopolysaccharide, fructose, free fatty acids, and different ratios of hepatocytes and nonparenchymal cells (10). Using such MPS data was important because our approach relies on calculating common covariance structures between MPS and human subjects, which benefits from using large and comprehensive datasets.

As a first step, we sought to quantify the transcriptional similarity between the MPS samples and human samples. We scored the different MPS samples using a published gene expression signature that correlates well with the MASLD score and fibrosis stage (fig. S3, A and B) (35). The particular combination of experimental cues added to the MPS had a significant impact on the calculated enrichment scores, with samples containing TGFβ displaying an overall higher enrichment score for both phenotypes. Expectedly, this result is consistent with known disease biology because TGFβ is a strong fibrogenic factor known to be involved in the progression of MASLD/MASH (40, 41) and other fibrotic diseases (42, 43), which is also why it was included in the original MPS study. However, further comparisons between each of the MPS and human samples revealed that most MPS samples correlated more strongly with human samples with intermediate fibrosis stages (1 to 3) and MASLD scores (4 to 6) (fig. S3, C and D). This analysis highlighted that, while the MPS model captures some disease-relevant biology, it does not map directly to the full range of human disease as assessed by direct gene expression similarity.

Hence, we used our data-driven approach (Fig. 1) to map the MPS dataset to the clinical dataset in a more unbiased way that does not require one-to-one sample mapping. We performed PCA on the MPS dataset after averaging experimental replicates to preserve only gene expression variation resulting from changes in experimental treatments (Fig. 3A). Given that PCs are the eigenvectors of the covariance matrix of the gene expression matrix, they span a gene expression subspace where the MPS has a nonzero variance. We projected the clinical gene expression onto the MPS PCA space and then back-projected the data to the original clinical data space so that it could be projected onto the LVs of the PLSR model (Fig. 2E) to assess changes in the predictive performance of the model. The projection/back-projection step, or truncation, effectively removed patient gene expression variance not captured in the MPS PC space, as evidenced by a smaller spread of the human data when visualized in the LVs of the PLSR model (Fig. 3B). Moreover, truncation of the clinical data through the MPS gene expression space resulted in a notable drop in the predictive capability of the PLSR model, as shown by the lower correlation coefficients between the measured and predicted phenotypic scores (Fig. 3C). Note that data truncation is mathematically equivalent to finding the best representation of clinical data as linear combinations of MPS samples (Supplementary Notes). While retraining the regression coefficients in the PLSR model improved the apparent fit between measured and predicted scores, it did not increase the correlation coefficients to the values obtained with the nontruncated clinical dataset when assessed in individual data partitions (fig. S4). This indicates that the truncation step removed important biological information necessary for resolving differences between human clinical phenotypes. Last, we validated that the drop in predictive performance due to the truncation is not an artifact of PLSR modeling by comparing the performance before and after truncation with other nonlinear ML and deep learning algorithms (fig. S5). Together, these results suggest that the MPS dataset used in this case study does not capture all of the important disease-associated variance of patient data, even if it recapitulates certain known features of MASH (10).

Forward model translation highlights TGFβ responses as the main translatable factor in the liver MPS

While our approach reveals what predictive information is lost when truncating human clinical data through the MPS PC space, the resulting PLSR model still performs significantly better than a shuffled PLSR control (fig. S4), indicating that the MPS dataset still contains useful predictive information. This truncation step still retains substantially higher information than what would be expected when truncating through a random gene expression space of the same dimensions as that of the MPS dataset used here (Supplementary Notes). We thus sought to find key directions in the MPS gene expression space that are responsible for preserving the translatable information. We defined TCs as a set of orthogonal LVs in the MPS gene expression space that can be used instead of the complete set of PCs in the data truncation step while achieving the same model predictions. TCs were calculated successively as linear combinations of MPS PCs to guarantee that they contain nonzero MPS variance and that they are orthogonal to each other (Materials and Methods).

For the current case study, a set of two TCs was sufficient to achieve identical predictions to those obtained when using the complete set of MPS PCs (Fig. 3D), with the first TC capturing most of the translatable information for both phenotypes. The two TCs accounted for around 13% of the total variance in the MPS dataset and did not correspond exactly to any of the MPS PCs (fig. S6), highlighting that the most translatable directions in the MPS dataset do not necessarily correspond to the directions of highest variance in the MPS data. Projecting the MPS samples onto these two TCs resulted in two distinct clusters, which corresponded to conditions that included TGFβ or not, regardless of the other experimental stimuli (Fig. 3E). Consistent with this observation, performing inference of pathway activity analysis on the gene loadings in the two TCs confirmed a strong TGFβ response signature in both TCs (Fig. 3F). Together, these results suggest that, for our chosen MPS, the addition of TGFβ to the culture medium was the added cue that resulted in most of the translatable information by inducing transcriptional changes at least partially associated with disease stage.

Reverse model translation reveals additional LVs required to further tune MPS translation

After analyzing the source of translatable information in the MPS dataset, we next asked whether we could find unexplored directions in the MPS gene expression space that would improve information translation when truncating human clinical data. We defined LV Extra as a set of LVs orthogonal to the MPS PC space and to each other that represent directions where the MPS should have nonzero variance but originally did not. Because PLSR models are linear, we were able to determine LV Extra analytically (Materials and Methods). For our case study, two additional LVs were sufficient to retrieve the missing predictive information. Projecting the human clinical samples onto the two calculated LVs arranges the samples qualitatively on the basis of their phenotypic scores, where most of the trend with the MASLD score was associated with LV Extra 1, while the fibrosis stage also varied along LV Extra 2 (Fig. 4A). These observations are consistent with our optimization approach, where we first optimized for the MASLD score with LV Extra 1 and then used LV Extra 2 to further tune for the fibrosis stage. Given that the fibrosis stage and MASLD are correlated (Fig. 2B), LV Extra 1 was expected to also capture information relevant to estimating the fibrosis stage.

Quantitatively, an augmented set of LVs that included the full MPS PCs and the two LV Extra (optimized MPS) performed significantly better on cross-validation than both a shuffled PLSR model and the PLSR model with truncation on just the MPS PC space, with no significant differences compared to the PLSR model with the full in vivo human clinical dataset (Fig. 4B). Because the LV Extra are calculated sequentially, they gradually increased the performance of the PLSR model in training and in testing until the performance of the full in vivo human clinical dataset was achieved (Fig. 4C). The additional LVs also retrieved predictive information in PLSR models trained on independent human clinical datasets (Fig. 4D), suggesting that they are generalizable and represent disease biology not captured in the MPS dataset. The additional LVs found using PLSR are enough to retrieve the dropped performance of other ML models as well (fig. S5). Last, we validated that, for even slightly lower data partitions (i.e., the size of the dataset for training), we get similar LV Extra and pathways loaded on them (figs. S7 and S8). Overall, this novel approach allowed us to identify potential directions in the gene expression space where inducing variance in the MPS by applying new experimental cues can further tune the translatability of the MPS model.

Analysis of additional LVs suggests a role for IFN response as an additional cue in liver MPS

While LV Extra are orthogonal to each other, they are calculated sequentially and are therefore not independent. Any set of two orthogonal vectors spanned by the two LV Extra will be equivalent in terms of retrieving the missing information in the clinical dataset. Therefore, we posed the question of whether a single extra LV corresponding to a linear combination of the two LV Extra calculated could capture most of the information that the two calculated LVs capture. We assessed the performance of the PLSR model when using a single additional LV defined as a weighted sum of the two calculated LV Extra (Fig. 5A). As expected, using a mixed LV closer to LV Extra 2 decreased the correlation between the measured and calculated MASLD scores, given that LV Extra 1 was defined to capture variance relevant to predicting the MASLD score. An intermediate mixed LV resulted in a higher correlation between predicted and measured fibrosis stages than that achieved with either LV Extra 1 or LV Extra 2 alone, which confirms that choosing a single extra LV leads to a trade-off between predicting either phenotype.

The calculated LV Extra revealed directions in the gene expression space where increasing MPS variance can help preserve translatable information, but they do not directly indicate how to achieve this additional variance in new MPS samples. To aid with interpretation, we performed pathway activity inference (44) on the gene loadings of the two LV Extra and on intermediate LVs (Fig. 5B and fig. S9). This analysis revealed that the region in which a single mixed LV results in good model performance for both clinical phenotypes (fibrosis and MASLD scores) corresponds to directions in the gene expression space dominated by the activity of the JAK-STAT and p53 pathways, while the contribution of the NF-κB (nuclear factor κB) pathway is diminished. This result suggests that it may not be necessary to increase MPS variance exactly along the calculated LVs as long as the appropriate pathways, hence gene expression profiles, are perturbed.

Therefore, we focused on interpreting LV Extra 1, as it is the direction that provides most of the information retrieval for both the MASLD score and fibrosis stage. Pathway inference analysis on this extra LV confirmed a significant association between its gene loadings and perturbation signatures along the p53 and JAK-STAT pathways with opposing signs (Fig. 5C), indicating that any proposed experimental perturbation should perturb the JAK-STAT and p53 pathways in opposing directions. These results were corroborated by performing gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) on the gene loadings, which highlighted a significant enrichment of genes associated with IFN response and cell cycle regulation, along with other inflammatory and metabolic pathways (Fig. 5D). To assess whether LV Extra 1 was associated with known perturbagens, we compared its gene loadings with published transcriptional signatures curated from multiple non–cancer cell types (Fig. 5E) (33). Several perturbagens were identified with this approach, including IFNs highlighted as agents capable of inducing gene expression signatures consistent with LV Extra 1. Consistent with these findings, JAK-STAT signaling emerged as a relevant translational axis when repeating the analysis across additional in vitro-clinical dataset pairs (fig. S10).

Given that our reference MPS model only included hepatocytes, stellate cells, and macrophages, we first verified whether the lack of MPS variance along LV Extra is due to the lack of a specific cell type present in human tissue. We used a human MASLD/MASH single-nucleus RNA-seq dataset (45) and pseudobulked gene expression by a patient for each annotated cell type (Materials and Methods). We then projected the resulting data onto the two calculated LV Extra to obtain projection scores and to calculate their correlation with the phenotypic scores for each patient (fig. S11). As anticipated, significant correlations between the projected scores and the MASLD score were mostly limited to LV Extra 1, which was obtained by optimizing for the MASLD score, while most of the significant correlations with LV Extra 2 scores were only apparent for the fibrosis stage. Hepatocytes had the most significant correlations between both phenotypes and LV Extra, which is expected because they comprise the bulk of the liver. Endothelial cells, which are not present in the MPS model, displayed a significant correlation between the fibrosis stage and their LV Extra 2 score, which suggests that these cells might account for some of the variance along LV Extra 2 in clinical data that is not recapitulated in vitro.

Next, given that LV Extra 1 is associated with JAK-STAT activity and correlates negatively with the MASLD score and fibrosis stage (Fig. 5C), we hypothesized that cues that activate the JAK-STAT pathway—specifically interferon-α (IFN-α) (Fig. 5E)—could antagonize TGFβ1-induced MASLD phenotypes. To functionally validate this antagonistic relationship in vitro, we created a hepatic triculture spheroid model of MASLD using a published alginate well method, culturing the spheroids in a previously established, high-fat, high-sugar, and high-insulin, base medium (8, 46). These spheroids were then treated with TGFβ1 in the presence or absence of IFN-α (Fig. 6A). We assessed steatosis via staining for lipid droplets (BODIPY; see Materials and Methods) and fibrosis with immunofluorescence for α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) in hepatic stellate cells (fig. S12, A and B). Consistent with the previous literature, TGFβ1 increased α-SMA expression while reducing lipid accumulation (47). Notably, cotreatment with IFN-α further reduced lipid levels and restored α-SMA to near-baseline (Fig. 6, B and C), which demonstrates a potent antagonism of TGFβ1-driven phenotypes. However, the increase in vimentin-positive area induced by TGFβ1 persisted during IFN-α cotreatment, indicating that the antagonism is selective and does not reverse all aspects of stellate cell activation (fig. S12C).

To gain deeper insight into the transcriptional effects of IFN-α and TGFβ addition, we performed bulk RNA-seq in parallel spheroid cultures. PCA clustered the data on the basis of experimental conditions (Fig. 6D), with principal component 1 (PC1) aligning with the addition of TGFβ and principal component 2 (PC2) aligning with addition of IFN-α. Consistent with these observations, pathway inference analysis on the PC loadings confirmed high TGFβ and JAK-STAT activities associated with PC1 and PC2, respectively (Fig. 6E). We then projected our new data onto the previously calculated TC1 and LV Extra 1 components, which preserved the clustering by experimental condition (Fig. 6F). However, this projection accounted for only a small fraction of the variance with LV Extra 1, suggesting that the IFN-α stimulus activation of the otherwise-missing JAK-STAT pathway remained mild in relation to the entirety of all the LV Extra 1 pathways, even while it did counter the TGFβ fibrotic effect on the phenotype noticeably, as shown in Fig. 6C. These experimental results illustrate that JAK-STAT signaling activation can contribute to counteracting pathological features generated by TGFβ1 in an in vitro model of MASLD and highlight JAK-STAT as a target pathway for further study in in vivo translation of in vitro models of MASLD/MASH.

An unbiased translation approach identifies conditions to better capture human diversity in liver MPS

Beyond capturing disease-associated biological variance in the clinical data, it might be desirable for in vitro models to also capture a broader range of patient biological variance to model different covariates of the clinical data (such as sex, genetic background, etc.) and, thus, the potential adverse effects of new drugs outside the desired target pathway or signaling node. Here, we define unsupervised total variance as the total statistical variance of gene expression across all patients in the human cohort. This defines how widely the expression of each gene spreads across individuals as a result of donor-to-donor differences, disease heterogeneity, sex, and other biological or technical factors. Given this definition, we can quantify how much of the unsupervised total variance (in terms of gene expression here) is covered by the MPS by computing the fraction of that variance that lies within the subspace spanned by the MPS PCs.

We developed an unsupervised optimization approach (Fig. 7A), using stochastic gradient descent (48), to maximize the total captured clinical variance by the MPS. Whereas LIV2TRANS focuses on the disease-associated portion of patient variance used to predict MASLD/MASH and fibrosis scores, this optimization step is phenotype-agnostic and instead enlarges the total region of the patient gene expression space that can be covered by MPS perturbations. Briefly (see Materials and Methods for more details), a differential gene expression vector (ΔX), which would be caused by a hypothetical experimental perturbation, is initialized randomly using a normal distribution with a mean and standard deviation equal to those of the MPS gene expression. This ΔX is then added to the centroid gene expression vector of the lean control samples in the MPS to yield a new gene expression vector (X∗lean) that represents a new sample to be added to the data to maximize the total clinical variance that can be captured by the MPS PC space in an unsupervised way. Iteration proceeds, updating the differential gene expression vector (ΔX) until convergence, repeating the process with other random initializations, and ultimately taking the average across all identified ΔX as the final determined perturbation. Adding just this one new sample results in an increase in the unsupervised total variance captured by the MPS PCs from ∼32 to ∼43%. In other words, although the cohort still contains gene expression variance that lies outside the MPS space, the optimized perturbation broadens the region of the patient gene expression landscape that can be emulated by varying cues in the MPS. To interpret the identified gene expression perturbations, we inferred the activity of pathways that can generate the calculated perturbation (Fig. 7B). We observed that perturbing JAK-STAT signaling can also increase the total captured patient variance, even though it is not the most prominent perturbation in this case. IFN-based responses are again found enriched when performing GSEA using Hallmark gene sets (Fig. 7C), while transcription factors (TFs) such as STAT2 and IRF9 (IFN regulatory factor 9), downstream of JAK-STAT signaling, are highly ranked on the basis of their inferred activity (fig. S13). HIF1A (hypoxia-inducible factor 1-α) activity was also highly ranked (fig. S13), consistent with a significant enrichment of a response to hypoxia (Fig. 6C). These suggest that inducing hypoxia in the in vitro model may prime the system to capture more of the patient variance.

The dominant effects to increase in vitro variance seem to be derived by perturbing, primarily, androgen signaling and, secondly, epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) signaling (Fig. 7B). Androgens, as well as estrogens, whose responses were enriched in the identified perturbation (Fig. 7C), have been reported to be associated with both liver metabolism and MASLD, as well as sex-associated differences in MASLD, because of the difference in the activity and levels of androgens and estrogens between males and females (49–52). Differences between male and female animals have also been observed regarding hepatic regeneration, depending on androgen and estrogen signaling (49), with the latter having a reported bidirectional relationship with EGFR (53). The MPS in our case study likely contains only male samples (figs. S14 and S15), while the LV Extra, which were calculated to better recapitulate the MASLD score and fibrosis stage, do not explain the sex of the human patients (Fig. 7D). Thus, we hypothesize that the identified perturbation, which maximizes the total patient variance, can potentially be equivalent to stimuli that can prime the in vitro system to be sensitive to sex-related differences. Alternatively, and perhaps more simply, additional MPS samples could be built using female-derived cells maintained in a female-specific culture medium.

DISCUSSION

The primary objective of MPSs is to facilitate improved generation of preclinical information relevant for patient contexts, reducing the dependency on animal models. In this study, we introduce a methodology that applies broadly across various in vitro models to identify translatable information within MPSs. By leveraging transcriptomic data from both clinical cohorts and MPSs, we used a case study focused on MASLD. Our approach effectively retrieves the sources of key translatable information from a given MPS model and allows for prioritization of relevant experimental cues. Moreover, our method retrieves phenotypically relevant patient variation by identifying LV Extra pertinent to each phenotype of interest that allow for improved predictive capabilities of the MPS models and suggests cues to achieve MPS variance along the identified LVs. We denote as disease-associated the part of the gene expression variance that is predictive of the two histological scores (MASLD score and fibrosis stage), which we used to track disease progression in patients. Our approach does not directly analyze the projection of patient samples onto the MPS space as done in previous work (28, 29), and it goes beyond the idea of finding a gene expression subspace with good alignment between in vitro and clinical data (54, 55) because we also calculate important LVs that are important for predicting patient phenotypes regardless of whether they contain zero MPS variance. In this regard, our work represents a novel approach to systematically determining cues or perturbations that can be added to MPS models to improve their translatability without aiming to mimic clinical samples on a one-to-one basis.

For our particular case study, our results suggest that TGFβ serves as a crucial cue that preserves substantial translatable information in the selected MPS. While the role of TGFβ in MASLD/MASH and fibrosis has long been recognized, our results highlight the potential of our method to prioritize experimental stimuli in other MPS models where key cues might not be as obvious. In contrast, the lack of additional information from other cues in the MPS may be attributed to the timing of sample collection. It is plausible that the MPS has adapted to initial perturbations by the time samples were collected for sequencing, therefore diminishing the net impact of specific cues. This observation aligns with the multihit theory of disease progression (56), where diverse initial insults lead to a cascade of cellular events, fundamentally altering cell behavior and intercellular communication that ultimately dictate the phenotype of the tissue. However, noting that MASDL/MASH are progressive conditions that develop over multiple years, it is also possible that MPS samples were collected earlier than the time that is truly necessary for some of the used perturbations to fully result in a disease-relevant transcriptomic change in the MPS. Extending LIV2TRANS to include temporal dynamics when appropriate data exist will therefore be a crucial direction for future work.

The identification of LV Extra suggests that perturbations along the JAK-STAT signaling pathway—potentially mediated by IFN—can further enhance the predictive accuracy of the MPS for human clinical phenotypes by capturing molecular processes relevant to disease progression. The LV Extra are calculated sequentially, and in our case, we started by optimizing for the MASLD score, followed by tuning for the fibrosis stage. However, our overall conclusions are still valid if we choose to optimize first for the fibrosis stage and then tune for MASLD, given that, in this case, the resulting LV Extra are simply a rotation of the original LV Extra and therefore do not provide additional information. Notably, IFN-based perturbations of the JAK-STAT signaling pathway are identified by applying the approach also with other pairs of clinical-in vitro datasets, highlighting that this perturbation is potentially missing by many current in vitro models of the disease (fig. S10). IFNs have been shown to be involved in various inflammatory pathologies of the liver (57). IFN-γ is known to promote the inflammatory response of macrophages, which leads to the recruitment of additional immune cells, promoting inflammation and contributing to hepatocyte injury and fibrosis (41). Meanwhile, type I IFNs are associated with the increased production of pro-inflammatory cytokines in the liver. However, IFN-γ and IFN-α have also been observed to have an antifibrotic effect in various contexts (58–61), by, e.g., directly antagonizing SMAD3 activation (62). Thus, the involvement of IFNs in disease development and progression makes them not only interesting potential therapeutic targets but also potentially useful stimuli to be added to the medium conditions of the in vitro model to better mimic the tissue microenvironment of MASLD.

Encouragingly, our experiments show that IFN-α cotreatment in hepatic triculture spheroids modulated the phenotypic response to TGFβ1, further reducing lipid accumulation and reducing α-SMA to the baseline, thereby functionally validating the role of the model-derived IFN/JAK-STAT direction. Transcriptomic analysis revealed a strong effect of adding TGFβ alone or in combination with IFN-α in our culture system, and pathway activity inference of PC1 and PC2 confirmed the predominant activation of the TGFβ and JAK-STAT pathways, respectively. PC1 was associated with higher activity of multiple pathways, while PC2 had a dominant JAK-STAT signature, suggesting that TGFβ leads to a cascade of biological processes that, over time, modulate multiple pathways, while addition of IFN-α overwhelmingly and more uniquely activates the JAK-STAT pathway. Yet, our experimental data exhibited only mild variance along the LV Extra identified with LIV2TRANS. In particular, the IFN-α–driven PC2 of our data was not associated with opposing JAK-STAT/p53 signaling as required by LV Extra 1, which indicates that other missing cues might need to be combined with IFN-α to recapitulate the molecular processes associated with disease progression. That is, while IFN-α was the top candidate expected to induce changes consistent with LV Extra 1, the cue ranking is relative within a library of perturbations, and there is no guarantee that the induced transcriptional response will match the desired outcome perfectly. Using interpretable ML models of cell signaling (63, 64) can potentially overcome this issue by enabling direct estimation of the required cues that can produce the desired transcriptional outcomes.

In the context of recapitulating the overall patient variance in vitro, meaning gene expression variance induced in total by the combining effects of disease progression, donor-to-donor variability, sex differences, etc., our numerical, unsupervised maximization algorithm of total clinical variance revealed once again a potential role for JAK-STAT signaling. These results are consistent with the calculated LV Extra and are expected given that this particular clinical dataset was collected with the purpose of studying MASLD/MASH, and we therefore expect that a substantial proportion of patient variance in the data is directly associated with disease biology (as defined by the MASLD score and fibrosis stage). However, our results highlighted, for example, a lack of potentially sex-related transcriptional responses in the MPS model, which was made with mostly male-derived cells. Given that sex differences in MASLD have been described (49–52), our results strengthen the need to include sex as an important variable in MPS studies (65).

To interpret LVs and identify translatable and missing cues in the MPS, we relied on prior knowledge sources such as the PROGENy for pathway activity inference (66), the DoRothEA regulon (67) for TF activities, Hallmark gene sets for gene-set enrichment, and ChemPert for mapping drug perturbations to inferred TF signatures (33). These compendia provide high-coverage, mechanistically annotated signatures, but most give insights into signaling biology, and a few of them were originally constructed for studying cancer or immortalized cell lines. Therefore, even though they are appropriate in general for proliferative, inflammatory, apoptotic, and other processes present both in cancer and MASLD/MASH, they are not perfectly matched to primary hepatocytes or liver MPS contexts. This situation likely biases interpretation toward well-studied stress and proliferation pathways and may underrepresent liver-specific metabolic, stromal, or immune programs. Moreover, biophysical cues such as the extracellular matrix composition and stiffness are absent from these resources, which precludes us from inferring their importance for MPS translation, even though the extracellular matrix is known to change throughout MASLD/MASH progression (68). We view this as both a limitation and opportunity, given that the same LIV2TRANS framework can be rerun with alternative signature libraries to refine pathway attribution, as liver- and disease-specific perturbational resources and regulatory networks will become more available. An immediate extension of the same dataset and model could be used to infer upper and lower bounds of the activities of different metabolic tasks of hepatocytes, to suggest a general direction for metabolic cues, or to pinpoint key metabolic tasks to measure reliably and directly in the future.

While LIV2TRANS can generate hypotheses about priming preclinical models with specific stimuli (e.g., medium supplements) to tune MPS translatability, it lacks the ability to fully define experimental conditions such as time, dose, the material of the platform used, etc. Our approaches operate at a molecular level (and from a specific omics view), providing insights into the necessary mechanism of action of a perturbation or proposed medium condition, but they do not provide insights about the temporal dynamics of the system or the appropriate stimulus strength, at least not explicitly. For example, we propose to prime an MPS that uses primary hepatocytes with IFN-α to potentially better capture cases of early fibrosis stages and MASLD/MASH, while priming hepatocytes with TGFβ when simultaneously inhibiting IFN responses can potentially better capture cases of advanced fibrosis and MAS. However, LIV2TRANS does not directly indicate how much IFN-α and TGFβ we should use, whether we should add them at the beginning of culturing or after a few days, how long to sustain this stimulus, or if we should repeat the perturbation and, if yes, how many times is it appropriate to do so. The temporal dynamics of TGFβ signaling itself not only have implications on the mechanism of action of diseases, but also different insights can be gained from each time point (61, 69). Transient and temporal dynamics can lead cells to different states, thus mimicking a different disease phenotype than that in human patients. Overall, in the current state of the LIV2TRANS pipeline, deciding the timing and dose of the different cues predicted to be useful for the MPS requires experimentation. This limitation likely contributed to the differences we observed between LV Extra 1 and the IFN-α–induced response in our spheroid model and warrants further work.

An important feature of LIV2TRANS is that it decomposes the molecular variance to correctly identify translatable and missing cues in vitro by relying on the selected phenotypic metrics chosen in the clinical dataset. Our focus was on the MASLD score and fibrosis stage because they are frequently reported, are available for large patient groups, and have a strong correlation with disease progression and prognosis in MASLD/MASH (38, 70, 71). However, these metrics are still relatively coarse summaries of liver pathology. They are only semiquantitative readouts, where expert pathologists integrate various histological factors (e.g., steatosis, hepatocellular ballooning, and lobular inflammation) into clinically validated summary measures (71, 72). For other indications, more granular or mechanistically targeted phenotypes (for example, quantitative imaging readouts, decomposed histological features, or circulating biomarkers) may provide a richer and more faithful description of the disease state. The clinical management of MASLD/MASH is increasingly using noninvasive tests on the basis of serum and imaging biomarkers, such as FIB-4 (fibrosis-4) and ELF (enhanced liver fibrosis), which capture elements of steatosis and fibrosis without the need for repeated liver biopsies (73). In the future, end points, such as the serum markers, could be incorporated as additional phenotypes or as external validation targets for LIV2TRANS. With the availability of more detailed clinical annotations, LIV2TRANS could be used to model several phenotypes at once. In practice, however, the number of independent phenotypic axes we can reliably model is limited by the size of the cohort and how well each phenotype is represented across patients. Moreover, surrogate phenotypic measures in the MPS are required for in vitro validation. This makes it crucial for wet-lab, clinical, and computational teams to collaborate in defining phenotypes that are both practical to measure in the clinic and genuinely representative of the disease aspects that the MPS is designed to capture.

Another limitation of our approach is that it requires molecular features measured across a wide range of experimental conditions to produce a good assessment of how much disease-associated biological variance a particular MPS can capture. This is because our method relies on leveraging covariance structures in molecular data. Because the regression on the clinical data is multivariate, the same ML approach extends naturally to more than two phenotypes, provided that sufficiently many patient samples are available to constrain the additional model parameters that arise. Nevertheless, collecting molecular data from at least two conditions (e.g., control versus disease) is enough in principle to calculate a single LV that can then be used to calculate additional LVs required to capture more of the patient-relevant biological variance. Our approach can be integrated as part of an iterative cycle of perturbing MPS models and assessing translatable information to refine in vitro models progressively. Notably, we relied solely on gene expression data, mainly due to data availability and their high predictive power for the case study of MASLD. However, this limits us to understanding the disease from the perspective of transcriptomics, and thus, we can mostly provide insights into the gene expression, gene sets, and signaling levels. It is possible that other types of molecular features (i.e., proteomic and metabolomic) could provide complementary information that would lead to different assessments of MPS quality, e.g., the metabolic fidelity of the MPS to actual humans in vivo, which currently could only be inferred in theory using genome-scale metabolic modeling. Moreover, the predictive ML model used can be extended to a multiomic approach that simultaneously accounts for different levels of molecular profile and their interactions, inspired by LIVE (Latent Interacting Variable Effects) modeling (74).

Overall, despite its current limitations, we anticipate that our approach, LIV2TRANS, will be useful in addressing the question of how complex an MPS model needs to be to model a disease of interest accurately. It provides a principled way for selecting cue conditions that align with relevant phenotypic directions in patients, thereby advancing our understanding of disease mechanisms and their therapeutic solutions and accelerating the process of in vitro model design. Our method builds on the premise that, instead of attempting to design complex in vitro models that allow for simple one-to-one mapping to patient samples, an in vivo response can be built as a function of responses in diverse in vitro models that are, on the whole, informative because they capture important biological variance (75).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Datasets

Publicly available human MASLD liver and MPS transcriptomic datasets were used in this study. The main clinical dataset used for the development of our pipeline [Govaere et al. (32)] can be obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) with the number GSE135251. Two additional datasets used for model validation [Hoang et al. (35) and Pantano et al. (34)] can be obtained from GEO with accession numbers GSE13090 and GSE162694, respectively. Sample metadata were directly obtained from GEO. However, to reduce data variability resulting from different preprocessing bioinformatic pipelines, clinical datasets were downloaded as gene counts from ARCHS4 (76), which guarantees uniform preprocessing from raw sequencing files. The liver MPS dataset (10) was not available in ARCHS4 and was obtained as gene counts along with sample metadata from GEO with accession GSE168285. Single-nucleus RNA-seq data from human livers across MASLD stages of disease (45) were downloaded as an annotated Seurat file with accession number GSE202379.

Model translation pipeline

Data preprocessing

For a given pair of human/MPS datasets, only common features (i.e., genes) found in both datasets were included. Gene counts were then normalized for each sample in each dataset as counts per million (CPM) and normalized as log2(1 + CPM).

Partial least squares modeling of human MASLD/MASH

PLSR was used to build a model to jointly predict MASLD scores and fibrosis stage in the human dataset as a function of normalized gene expression data. Eight LVs were determined to be optimal on the basis of cross-validation. The PLSR model can be represented asYh=XhWhBh(1)where Yh is a centered matrix of human phenotypes (samples by phenotypes), Xh is a matrix of normalized and centered human gene expression data (samples by genes), Wh is a matrix of weights (genes by LVs), and Bh is a matrix of regression coefficients (LVs by phenotypes). The phenotypic scores (Yh) consist of the MASLD score and fibrosis stage, which are ordinal histopathology scores tracking disease progression and range from 0 to 8 and 0 to 4, respectively. The obtained PLSR model was initially tuned using 10-fold cross-validation [using 80% of the data from Govaere et al. (32)] and evaluated in a smaller test set (20% of the data) for potential overfitting in the validation set because of the selection of a specific number of LVs. Afterward, the number of LVs was set to 8, and for the rest of the comparisons, the obtained model was trained and evaluated using a new 10-fold cross-test split of all the data from Govaere et al. (32). One-fold (10%) data were hidden during training and used to evaluate performance in unseen data, while 90% of the data were used for training. The 80%-20% train-test split was constructed by randomly trying to split 10,000 times and keeping the split that minimized the difference in the distribution of MAS scores and fibrosis stages between the splits. The 90%-10% train-validation CV splits were constructed in the same way, but also simultaneously, the splits were kept only if all possible MAS scores present in the validation sets were also present in the train sets (fibrosis stages were not possible to also satisfy that constraint). In addition, for the validation of the performance of the identified additional LVs (as described later in Materials and Methods) in the external clinical datasets [Hoang et al. (35) and Pantano et al. (34)], repeated random 10-fold cross-validation was performed (repeated 10 times) so that performance is less affected by the random splitting into imbalanced distributions of scores.

Translation of human data through MPS data

PCA was applied to the normalized and centered MPS gene expression data (Xm) after averaging the replicates for each experimental condition on the basis of sample metadata. The averaging of replicates was performed so that the PCA model only captured variance in the data associated with the experimental conditions and not with technical variability resulting from experimental or sequencing variability. The rotation matrix of the PCA model (Wm) contains the PCs of the data, which are a set of orthonormal vectors that span the subspace in the gene expression space that captures the full variance of the MPS dataset.

Human gene expression was then projected and back-projected onto the MPS PCA space as XhWmWmT, where the superscript T denotes the transpose of the matrix. The first projection onto the MPS space effectively truncates components of the human gene expression data that are not captured by the MPS dataset, while the back-projection places the data again in the human gene expression space, which is necessary so that this truncated human data can be used with the human PLSR model. These truncated, or filtered, data are then evaluated with the trained human PLSR model to obtain a prediction of human phenotypes asYh,pred=(XhWmWmT)WhBh(2)

When more than one phenotype is present, each phenotype has an αTC associated with its particular Bh. Therefore, to aid with interpretation and to guarantee that the order of TCs is meaningful, we performed a line search to get an intermediate vector wTC between the two vectors WmATC that minimized an overall normalized prediction error (Eq. 7).E=∑[(XhwTCwTCT)WhBh,NAS−Yh,NAS]2∑Yh,NAS2+∑[(XhwTCwTCT)WhBh,Fib−Yh,Fib]2∑Yh,Fib2(7)

The intermediate vector with the lowest prediction error was defined as the first TC wTC,1. The second TC wTC,2 was then calculated as a vector orthogonal to wTC,1 such that both TCs span the same space as WTC=WmATC. This approach can be extended to cases with more than two phenotypes by performing successive rounds of optimization of a prediction error, although direct line searches will become more inefficient in this case.

Calculation of additional LVs

Additional, or extra, LVs were defined as latent vectors orthogonal to the MPS space that contain translatable human information. These variables were calculated analytically by noting that the PLSR model is a linear model withYh=XhWhBh=XhφPLS(8)where φPLS=WhBh is a matrix of size genes by the number of phenotypes. Therefore, the phenotypes predicted when including the projection-back-projection step areYh,pred=XhWmWmTφPLS(9)

The vectors wextra are defined to be unit-norm and orthogonal to each other and to Wm such that they provide information not contained in the MPS, which implies that the matrix M=Wm∗TWm∗ equals an identity matrix and that any linear combination λ of the columns of Wm∗ results in Wm∗Wm∗Tλ=λ. Given that each phenotype in the model has a vector Bh associated with a distinct φPLS, any set of vectors that span the space spanned by the wextra calculated for each phenotype is a valid set of LV Extra, although in general, these wextra will not be orthogonal to each other. To solve this issue, another possibility is to calculate wextra for one phenotype and define this as LV Extra 1, then augment Wm with this vector, and calculate wextra again with the φPLS of a second phenotype using the augmented Wm, which guarantees that the new wextra is orthogonal to Wm and to LV Extra 1, and so forth, until all phenotypes have been examined. This latter procedure was used in the MASLD/MASH case study, given that both the MASLD score and fibrosis score are correlated, and therefore, LV Extra 1, calculated from the MASLD score, also retrieves information relevant to fibrosis, while LV Extra 2 retrieves the remaining information.

Experimental methods

Cell culture

Primary human hepatocytes were sourced from a 53-year-old Hispanic male (LifeNet Health LifeSciences). Primary human stellate cells were sourced from a 48-year-old Caucasian male (LifeNet Health LifeScience), and primary human Kupffer cells were purchased from Lonza Biosciences. Before spheroid formation, hepatic stellate cells were thawed and resuspended in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Cells were then counted and seeded at a density of 12,000 cells/cm2 into a collagen I–coated plate. A complete medium change was performed after 4 hours, and half medium changes were performed every day until ready for use.

On the day of spheroid formation, cryopreserved hepatocytes were thawed in Cryopreserved Hepatocyte Recovery Media (Thermo Fisher Scientific), centrifuged at 100g for 8 min, and resuspended in 1 ml of seeding media previously established for multicellular spheroids. Cryopreserved Kupffer cells were thawed in ice-cold seeding media, centrifuged at 500g for 5 min at 4°C, and resuspended in 500 μl of seeding media. Hepatic stellate cells were lifted from culture. Following resuspension, viable cells were counted using a hemocytometer with trypan blue.

Multicellular hepatic spheroid formation

Spheroids were formed using a published alginate microwell system (8). Before seeding, alginate microwells were primed with 300 μl of seeding media for at least 1 hour at 37°C. The priming media were then aspirated and replaced with 75 μl of fresh media. Then, a cell suspension containing 120,000 hepatocytes, 12,000 Kupffer cells, and 12,000 hepatic stellate cells in 75 μl of seeding media was added dropwise to each well with a wide-bore pipette tip. To facilitate cell aggregation, the plate was centrifuged at 50g for 2 min, followed by addition of an additional 150 μl of media to each well. After 24 hours, a complete medium change was performed, marking day 1 of the experiment.

Spheroid culture

Spheroids were grown in a previously established medium to induce a type 2 diabetic phenotype, containing elevated levels of insulin, free fatty acids, and glucose, referred to as “base media” in this work (46). Complete medium changes (300 μl per well) were performed every 48 hours following day 1. Collected media were spun down at 1000g for 5 min to clear cell debris. The resulting supernatant was then frozen and stored at −80°C. When applicable, cytokine treatments were added to the media starting on day 3 of the experiment for a total of 8 days of exposure. Spheroids were harvested from alginate wells by pipetting with a wide-bore pipette tip and then transferred to a 96-well plate for imaging. Albumin concentrations were measured in collected media via enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (Bethyl Laboratories, E80-129) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. End-point absorbances were measured at 450 and 540 nm on a SpectraMax i3x plate reader before data analysis.

Lipid and nuclear staining

After harvesting, spheroids were stained with NucBlue Live ReadyProbes Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, R37605) and 1 μM BODIPY 493/503 for 15 min at 37°C. Following five washes in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), spheroids were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min at room temperature. After another five PBS washes, spheroids were imaged on a Zeiss LSM 880 confocal microscope with a 10× magnification objective. Lipid quantification was performed before immunostaining to prevent signal degradation from permeabilization detergents.

Immunofluorescence imaging

Following initial imaging, spheroids were blocked and permeabilized overnight at 4°C in PBS containing 2% donkey serum and 0.2% Triton X-100 (staining buffer). Spheroids were then incubated overnight at 4°C with mouse anti–α-SMA (Abcam, ab7817) at a dilution of 1:200 and with a 568-conjugated rabbit anti-vimentin antibody (1:1000 in staining buffer; Abcam, ab202504). After four washes with PBS, the spheroids were then incubated with an Alexa Fluor–conjugated secondary antibody (1:500 in staining buffer). After final four washes in PBS, spheroids were reimaged on the Zeiss LSM 880 with 10× and 20× magnification objectives.

Image analysis

Following image acquisition, maximum-intensity z-projections were generated from the image stacks using ImageJ (Fiji). A primary region of interest (ROI) defining the total spheroid area was created by applying an automated intensity threshold to the bright-field channel. For lipid quantification, the mean fluorescence intensity of the BODIPY signal was measured within each spheroid ROI. To quantify fibrosis, the mean fluorescence intensity of the α-SMA channel was measured in each spheroid along with the vimentin-positive area. For quality control, segmentation masks were overlaid on the original images, allowing for manual review and removal of any erroneously identified ROIs. The entire analysis pipeline was automated using a custom-written ImageJ macro to ensure consistency and reproducibility.

RNA extraction and sequencing

After harvesting, spheroids were suspended in 300 μl of DNA/RNA shield (Zymo Research, R1100-50) and stored at −80°C. RNA was extracted using the Quick-RNA Microprep Kit (Zymo Research, R1050) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA with low 260/230 absorbance ratios (<1.5) was purified using the RNA Clean & Concentrator-5 kit (Zymo Research, R1013). Purified RNA was sent to Plasmidsaurus for bulk RNA-seq using the Illumina sequencing technology. The resulting count matrices were processed as done for other datasets, as detailed in the “Data preprocessing” section.

Statistical analysis of imaging results

Statistical analysis was performed using R. Data are presented as box plots showing the median, interquartile range, and data distributions, with individual spheroids overlaid as a strip plot. The two-sample Wilcoxon post hoc test was used for all pairwise comparisons. Statistical significance was defined as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, and ****P < 0.0001.

Unsupervised maximization of total clinical variance

To identify a perturbation to maximize the patient gene expression variance that is captured by the MPS model, a differential gene expression vector that is added to a sample of interest is tuned using stochastic gradient descent (48). In particular, the mean of the lean control samples in the MPS is selected as a starting point, and a vector (ΔX), with dimension the same as the number of genes, is randomly initialized from a standard normal distribution. The Adam optimizer (77) in the PyTorch framework (version 1.12) (78) is picked to perform optimization using a learning rate of 0.1 and 1000 iterations for the optimization process. During the optimization process, the goal is to maximize the variance that is captured by the MPS PC space through the minimization of the objective function: Loss=−σz2σh2+λ∗∣∣ΔX∣∣1, where σz2 is the variance of the projected human samples, and σh2 is the unsupervised total variance of patient gene expression. The second term applies L1 regularization on the learnable differential gene expression vector (ΔX). The process is repeated 30 times to account for the random initialization, and the average of all the learned vectors is taken as the identified differential gene expression perturbation that can maximize the captured patient variance in an unsupervised way.

Pathway and signature analysis of latent vectors

The activity of pathways and TFs that can drive the observed gene expression or contributions was inferred from transcriptomics data using the VIPER algorithm (79) from the decoupleR resource (44). The VIPER algorithm calculates the enrichment of known source entities (such as TFs or pathways), which act as proxies of TF and pathway activity. The activity of a TF is calculated on the basis of the expression of downstream genes known to be regulated by this specific TF using a known transcription regulatory network, while for the pathways, the effect after perturbation on known downstream genes is used as a regulon. In particular, for the TFs, the Dorothea regulon (67) was used with interactions of confidence levels A and B. For the pathways, the network describing the effect of 14 pathways to downstream genes, which is contained in the PROGENy resource (66), was used.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA)

GSEA was performed using the FGSEA library (80, 81) from the Bioconductor resource (80). Thus, the genes’ contributions to the identified vector basis, differential gene expression vectors, and the gene-level profile of each perturbation were transformed into a gene set–level profile of normalized enrichment scores. Statistical significance is calculated internally in FGSEA by calculating how likely it is to observe an equally high or higher enrichment score randomly on the basis of a null distribution of enrichment scores, constructed by randomly permutating the data 10,000 times. FGSEA is also performing P value adjustment using the Benjamini-Hochberg correction.

ChemPert-based perturbation inference

To identify perturbations that can drive the observed gene contribution profiles of the PC space, the TCs, the extra basis, and the differential gene expression vector that maximizes the captured patient variance, the VIPER algorithm (79) from the decoupleR resource (44) was used, coupled with a drug-to-TF regulatory network, contained in the ChemPert database (33). The TFs’ activity is first inferred as already described and is used as an observed downstream signal that is regulated by the perturbations. The absolute value of the inferred perturbation activity is used as a proxy for the prioritization of conditions for subsequent experiments.
