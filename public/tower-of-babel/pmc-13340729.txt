Introduction

Lower gastrointestinal bleeding (LGIB) in older adults is most commonly attributed to diverticulosis[1]; however, a singular diagnostic focus may obscure coexisting gastrointestinal conditions with potential clinical relevance. In medically complex patients, multimorbidity and antiplatelet therapy contribute to increased bleeding risk[2]. Retrograde single-balloon enteroscopy (SBE) enables direct ileal evaluation, allowing detection of lesions not accessible to conventional modalities[3]. This case illustrates a context-driven extension of the diagnostic evaluation in the presence of a focal ileocecal abnormality. Presentation of case

A 78-year-old man presented to the emergency department with the abrupt onset of painless hematochezia. He denied abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, fever, or constitutional symptoms and reported no family history of gastrointestinal disease.

His medical history was notable for supraventricular tachycardia treated with bisoprolol, coronary artery disease requiring percutaneous coronary intervention with stent placement at age 70, and arterial hypertension managed with bisoprolol and losartan. At age 72, he underwent radical prostatectomy for localized prostate cancer without an indication for adjuvant therapy. A motor vehicle accident at age 68 had resulted in the traumatic amputation of the right upper limb at the shoulder.

Four years earlier, he had undergone an elective left hemicolectomy with preservation of the sigmoid colon following the incidental discovery of multiple asymptomatic adenomatous polyps during a screening colonoscopy, several of which exhibited high-grade dysplasia, though without invasive carcinoma. He reported long-standing constipation alternating with occasional loose stools, but no prior hematochezia, melena, or systemic gastrointestinal symptoms.

On arrival, the patient was pale and hypotensive, with a blood pressure of 85/55 mmHg, a heart rate of 115 bpm, and a shock index of 1.35, consistent with grade III hypovolemic shock. He was afebrile and normoxic on room air. Examination showed localized tenderness in the left iliac fossa without peritoneal signs. Digital rectal examination confirmed active, non-melenic bleeding.

Laboratory evaluation revealed a hemoglobin level of 7.5 g/dL (hematocrit 23%), an INR of 1.7, a creatinine level of 1.1 mg/dL, and a lactate level of 3.6 mmol/L. Arterial blood gas analysis demonstrated metabolic acidosis. Liver function tests, electrolytes, and platelet count were normal. The Oakland Score was 28, indicating a high-risk LGIB episode.

Resuscitation was initiated with intravenous crystalloids and transfusion of two units of packed red cells, and clopidogrel was withheld. After 48 hours of stabilization, upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was unremarkable. Colonoscopy, performed following preparation with 4 L of polyethylene glycol, demonstrated extensive sigmoid diverticulosis and a sigmoid diverticulum containing an adherent clot, representing high-risk stigmata of recent hemorrhage. Endoscopic hemostasis was performed with circumferential submucosal injection of diluted epinephrine (1:10 000 in normal saline) and placement of a single hemoclip.

Further inspection of the cecum revealed an ulcerated ileocecal valve. Retrograde SBE was performed, allowing direct visualization of the terminal ileum, where multiple serpiginous ulcers were identified and biopsied. Approximately 70 cm beyond the ileocecal valve, a solitary Meckel’s diverticulum was incidentally found (Fig. 1). Periprocedural prophylaxis consisted of a single intravenous dose of ceftriaxone (1 g). Post-procedural management included esomeprazole and the continuation of his usual medications. Clopidogrel was reintroduced 24 hours after endoscopic hemostasis (72 hours after admission). The patient recovered uneventfully and was discharged in stable condition the following day.

At the 10-day follow-up, he remained asymptomatic. Histopathology from the ileal biopsies showed chronic active ileitis with ulceration, granulation tissue, and non-caseating epithelioid granulomas, consistent with Crohn’s disease. Outpatient management was initiated, and at 3 weeks, he continued to do well.

At the 6-month follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic, with no recurrent bleeding or Crohn-related symptoms. Given the absence of obstructive or penetrating complications and the patient’s age and comorbidity profile, immediate disease-modifying therapy was deferred. Management consisted of clinical surveillance.

Discussion

LGIB remains a major cause of hospital admission in older adults. In this patient, the bleeding source was a solitary sigmoid diverticulum. This case illustrates how a confirmed diverticular bleeding source may coexist with unrelated but clinically relevant gastrointestinal pathology.

The patient presented with grade III hypovolemic shock (shock index 1.35, lactate 3.6 mmol/L), consistent with systemic hypoperfusion. Current European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy guidelines recommend early computed tomography angiography (CTA) in patients with hemodynamic instability (e.g., shock index ≥1, altered mentation, elevated lactate) to localize bleeding and expedite embolization when necessary[5]. In this case, however, rapid hemodynamic stabilization with fluid resuscitation and transfusion obviated the immediate need for CTA, permitting a strategy of deferred imaging and timely colonoscopy. This approach is concordant with American College of Gastroenterology (ACG) guideline recommendations, which emphasize stabilization before invasive diagnostics when hemorrhage has slowed and high-quality endoscopic resources are available[6].

Colonoscopy performed within 48 hours demonstrated a sigmoid diverticulum with high-risk stigmata of recent hemorrhage (adherent clot), treated effectively with epinephrine injection and placement of a single hemoclip – an endoscopic therapy supported in the literature[7,8]. Yet the procedure also disclosed a deeply ulcerated ileocecal valve, an unexpected finding that shifted the diagnostic trajectory.

To further characterize this finding, retrograde device-assisted enteroscopy was performed. Given the distal ileocecal location of the abnormality, a retrograde approach was selected, and SBE was used as a targeted modality for terminal ileal evaluation. Although double-balloon enteroscopy (DBE) may achieve greater insertion depth, comparative data in retrograde enteroscopy demonstrate similar technical success, diagnostic yield, therapeutic yield, and safety between SBE and DBE. Notably, prior studies have shown that SBE is frequently employed in the evaluation of distal ileal lesions, supporting its use as a targeted modality in this anatomical context. Balloon-assisted enteroscopy is operator dependent, with outcomes influenced by local expertise. In this case, SBE reflected anatomical accessibility, operator experience, and evidence-supported equivalence in diagnostic performance rather than a compromise in efficacy[9,10].

SBE permitted direct visualization of the terminal ileum, where serpiginous, aphthous-border ulcers typical of Crohn’s disease were identified and biopsied. Histopathology confirmed Crohn’s disease. Elderly-onset IBD represents a recognized clinical subset that may present atypically, often mimicking ischemic or nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug-induced colitis, with colonic involvement being more common than in younger-onset disease[11,12]. The ileal localization in this case was notable and supported the use of enteroscopy as a selective adjunct when ileal pathology is suspected but not accessible to standard colonoscopy[13]. In this asymptomatic patient, Crohn’s disease represents a relevant diagnostic finding that, while not altering acute management, establishes a baseline for longitudinal surveillance and supports a conservative approach to therapeutic escalation. Importantly, in this case, this finding did not influence acute management or short-term outcomes.

SBE also uncovered an incidental Meckel’s diverticulum, a rare finding that often remains asymptomatic in older adults[14]. While asymptomatic, its detection reopens long-standing debates over prophylactic resection[14,15]. In the absence of high-risk features – ectopic mucosa, inflammation, ulceration, or fibrous bands – conservative management was favored.

The coexistence of multiple gastrointestinal pathologies is notable. Although no causal association between Meckel’s diverticulum and Crohn’s disease has been established, diagnostic overlap and implications have been described[16]. Similarly, diverticulosis has been linked to increased adenomatous polyp incidence[17], while Crohn’s disease confers an elevated risk of adenocarcinoma, with dysplasia often arising in polypoid lesions that obscure recognition[18]. In this patient, these findings likely reflected cumulative age-related gastrointestinal vulnerability.

Antiplatelet management posed a particular challenge. Hypovolemic shock increased the risk of ischemic complications in the context of antiplatelet interruption. Current guidelines address antiplatelet interruption primarily in the context of aspirin or dual therapy but provide little explicit direction for patients on isolated P2Y12 inhibition undergoing urgent gastrointestinal interventions[5,6,19]. In this case, clopidogrel was withheld during initial resuscitation and reintroduced 24 hours after endoscopic hemostasis (72 hours after admission), with a favorable outcome. This timing parallels case-based approaches in which early reinitiation of antiplatelet therapy has been accomplished without ischemic sequelae[20]. Although gastrointestinal bleeding following percutaneous coronary intervention is relatively uncommon, it portends higher mortality and cardiovascular morbidity, especially in elderly patients and those receiving combined antithrombotic regimens[21].

Interpretation of this report is constrained by its single-patient design and limited follow-up, precluding generalizability or standardization; findings are context specific and operator dependent, and no inference can be drawn regarding the impact of selective enteroscopy or incidental diagnoses on clinical outcomes. Future studies should aim to better define the role of selective enteroscopy within diagnostic algorithms for LGIB, particularly in patients with distal ileal abnormalities, and to establish standardized criteria guiding the choice between available enteroscopic techniques.

Conclusion

Definitive hemostasis was achieved endoscopically. A focal ileocecal abnormality prompted targeted evaluation, identifying clinically silent ileal pathology that did not influence the acute course but informed subsequent surveillance. The clinical implications of these incidental findings remain uncertain. Reintroduction of P2Y12 inhibition after hypovolemic shock was accomplished without complication, supporting individualized multidisciplinary management.
