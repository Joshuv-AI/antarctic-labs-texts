Beyond Valve Deployment
Authors: Parasuram Krishnamoorthy, Brian Kliewer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) has transformed the management of patients with severe aortic stenosis, and it is now an established therapy across the full spectrum of surgical risk, including younger and lower risk cohorts.1 As TAVR expands into these populations, an increasing number of patients are expected to outlive their transcatheter heart valves (THVs), bringing long-term durability into sharper focus. Consequently, there is heightened interest in optimizing procedural techniques to enhance THV performance. Valve underexpansion has been associated with higher rates of paravalvular regurgitation, hypoattenuating leaflet thickening (HALT), and elevated transvalvular gradients—all of which are linked to adverse clinical outcomes.2,3

Despite this, there remains a paucity of data evaluating the role of routine postdilation to improve valve expansion and its downstream hemodynamic consequences. In this context, recent studies have investigated routine postdilation using the double-tap technique in balloon-expandable THVs. This approach uses the deployment balloon to perform postdilation at nominal or supranominal volumes, and it has been associated with improved fluoroscopic and computed tomography–derived valve expansion, along with low rates of paravalvular regurgitation, without an increase in adverse events.4,5 However, the invasive hemodynamic effects of this strategy have remained largely unexplored.

In this issue of JACC: Case Reports, Nishimura et al6 present a series of 6 cases evaluating the hemodynamic impact of the double-tap technique in patients undergoing implantation of the SAPIEN 3 Ultra RESILIA valve (Edwards Lifesciences). Using invasive pressure monitoring with the SavvyWire (OpSens Medical), the authors obtained real-time transvalvular gradients after valve deployment and after postdilation. Their findings demonstrate an immediate reduction in the mean transvalvular gradient of 6.5 mm Hg, providing objective functional evidence beyond fluoroscopic assessment alone. This was accompanied by an increase in the midportion THV diameter of 0.8 mm and a reduction in aortic regurgitation severity after postdilation. Importantly, no patients experienced major adverse events, including permanent pacemaker implantation, stroke, or cardiovascular death. Collectively, these observations suggest that postdilation using the double-tap technique confers an immediate hemodynamic benefit.

The use of the SavvyWire for beat-to-beat hemodynamic assessment requires stable positioning within the left ventricular apex, which may pose practical challenges. Notably, in this series, wire repositioning was not required in any case, supporting the feasibility of incorporating real-time invasive hemodynamic monitoring into routine practice without significantly disrupting procedural workflow. Although limited by its small sample size and lack of a comparator group, this report provides important mechanistic insight. It introduces a practical intraprocedural tool that may help identify patients most likely to benefit from postdilation through real-time physiologic assessment, thereby moving beyond reliance on echocardiographic endpoints alone.

From a broader perspective, the work by Nishimura et al6 advances the field by shifting the paradigm from purely anatomical and imaging-based optimization toward a more integrated physiologic approach. While echocardiography remains essential, it may not fully capture the functional consequences of valve expansion. Valve frame expansion on real-time fluoroscopy and invasive hemodynamic assessment offers a complementary dimension, enabling operators to tailor procedural decisions based on immediate physiologic feedback. This concept aligns with the evolving goal of precision-based structural interventions, where therapy is dynamically adapted to patient-specific anatomy and physiology.

Looking Ahead

Future studies are needed to validate these findings in larger, controlled cohorts and to determine whether intraprocedural hemodynamic optimization translates into improved long-term clinical outcomes, including valve durability and freedom from reintervention. Standardization of thresholds for gradient reduction that warrant postdilation, as well as integration with echocardiographic gradients, will be critical. Additionally, the balance between achieving optimal valve expansion and avoiding potential complications, such as annular injury, increased risk of stroke, or conduction disturbances, must be carefully defined. As TAVR continues to expand into younger populations, strategies that enhance valve performance and longevity will become increasingly important.
