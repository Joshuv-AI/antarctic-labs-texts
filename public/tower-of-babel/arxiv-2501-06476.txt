The direct linearization scheme with the Lamé function: The KP equation and reductions
Xing Li, Ying-ying Sun, Da-jun Zhang

Abstract
The paper starts from establishing an elliptic direct linearization (DL) scheme for the Kadomtsev-Petviashvili equation. The scheme consists of an integral equation (involving the Lamé function) and a formula for elliptic soliton solutions, which can be confirmed by checking Lax pair. Based on analysis of real-valuedness of the Weierstrass functions, we are able to construct a Marchenko equation for elliptic solitons. A mechanism to obtain nonsingular real solutions from this elliptic DL scheme is formulated. By utilizing elliptic N N th roots of unity and reductions, the elliptic DL schemes, Marchenko equations and nonsingular real solutions are studied for the Korteweg-de Vries equation and Boussinesq equation. Illustrations of the obtained solutions show solitons and their interactions on a periodic background. Key Words: direct linearization approach, Lamé function, Marchenko equation, elliptic soliton solution, Kadomtsev-Petviashvili equation. MSC: 33E05, 35C08, 35Q51, 37K40

Introduction

The method of direct linearization (DL) was established by Fokas and Ablowitz in 1981
[
12
]
.
It is based on singular linear integral equations over arbitrary contours in the complex space of the spectral parameter.
For the Korteweg-de Vries (KdV) equation

u_{t}+6uu_{x}+u_{xxx}=0,

(1.1)

the linear integral equation in the DL approach is
[
12
]

\varphi(k;x,t)+\mathrm{i}e^{\mathrm{i}(kx+k^{3}t)}\oint_{L}\frac{\varphi(l;x,t)}{l+k}\mathrm{d}\lambda(l)=e^{\mathrm{i}(kx+k^{3}t)},

(1.2)

where
\mathrm{i}^{2}=-1
,
\mathrm{d}\lambda(l)
and
L
are appropriate measure and contour.
The plane wave factor (PWF)
e^{\mathrm{i}(kx+k^{3}t)}
is notable
as an element to indicate dispersion relation of the equation under investigation.
For the function
\varphi(k;x,t)
defined above, it turns out that

\psi(k;x,t)=\varphi(k;x,t)e^{-\frac{\mathrm{i}}{2}(kx+k^{3}t)}
and

u=-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\oint_{L}\varphi(l;x,t)\mathrm{d}\lambda(l)

satisfy the Lax pair of the KdV equation (
1.1
) and
consequently
u
is a solution of the KdV equation.
Not only solutions, but the Gel’fand-Levitan-Marchenko (GLM) equation in the inverse scattering transform
can be derived from the integral equation
[
12
]
.
In the framework of
[
12
]
, solutions of the investigated nonlinear equation are verified
through checking its Lax pair.
Such a scheme was extended to the Kadomtsev-Petviashvili (KP) equation,
the Benjamin-Ono equation, etc, to find their solutions, e.g.

[
1
,
2
,
13
,

,

Soon after Fokas and Ablowitz’s pioneer work
[
12
]
, the DL idea was developed by
the Dutch group in
[
42
,
43
,
48
]
into a flexible machinery involving infinite matrix structures,
in which nonlinear equations can be constructed together with their solutions,
without needing to check Lax pairs.
It allows the treatment of discrete as well as continuous equations on one and the same
footing, within one formulism.
Such a DL scheme has shown effectiveness in particular in the investigation of integrable lattice equations,
e.g.
[

,
40

A quite recent progress of the DL approach is the establishment of its elliptic scheme
involving infinite matrix structure and ‘discrete’ Lamé-type PWFs
[
44
]
.
The Lamé function is a solution of the Lamé equation
(known as the Schrödinger equation with an elliptic potential)

y^{\prime\prime}+(A+B\wp(x))y=0

when taking
A=-\wp(k)
and
B=-2
.
The solution reads
y=\frac{\sigma(x+k)}{\sigma(x)\sigma(k)}e^{-\zeta(k)x}
,
where
\sigma,\zeta
(and
\wp
) are the Weierstrass functions.
For the KdV equation, there are exact solutions involving the Lamé function,
which
emerged in
[
30
]
and
[
66
]
1970s.
In 2010, Nijhoff and Atkinson
[
38
]
employed a discrete analogue of the Lamé function
and constructed solutions for the Adler-Bobenko-Suris
[
3
]
(except Q4 equation) lattice equations.
The obtained solutions are presented in terms of the elliptic Cauchy matrix and
called ‘elliptic solitons’ to distinguish from the algebro-geometric (finite-gap) solutions (cf.
[
10
]
).
Later, their treatment was extended to the lattice KP equations
[
69
]
.
The elliptic DL scheme established in
[
44
]
is based on the infinite matrix structures.
The concept of ‘elliptic
N
th root of unity’ was introduced to define discrete dispersion relations
as well as make reductions from the lattice KP system to the lower dimensional equations,
namely, the discrete KdV and Boussinesq equations.
As for the elliptic solitons for the continuous equations, a recent progress is due to
[
32
]
,
which established a framework of bilinear approach that involves the Lamé function, including formulae of
\tau
functions, vertex operators and bilinear identities for the KP hierarchy
and their reductions. Periodic degenerations (i.e. trigonometric/hyperbolic and rational forms)
of the elliptic matter were also discussed in
[
32
]
.
Later, the study of
[
32
]
was extended to discrete case in
[
33
]
.
More recently, the consideration of such type of elliptic soliton solutions from
Sato’s approach was developed by Kakei
[
27

where the Darboux transformation based on pseudo-differential operators and the Lamé function
was established getting elliptic
N

and the KdV hierarchy.
The elliptic solitons are also related to a recent work by Nakayashiki
[
36
]
.

2
Elliptic DL scheme of the KP equation

In this section, we will focus on the KP equation:

u_{t}+u_{xxx}+6uu_{x}+3\partial^{-1}_{x}u_{yy}=0,

(2.1)

where
\partial^{-1}_{x}\partial_{x}=\partial_{x}\partial^{-1}_{x}=1
,
\partial_{x}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}
.
We will present its elliptic DL scheme and the Marchenko equation
when the equation has a periodic background solution

u_{0}(x)=-2\wp(x).

(2.2)

Real-valued solutions and their dynamics will also be presented.

2.1
Elliptic DL scheme

It is well known that the KP equation (
2.1
) admits a Lax pair

\displaystyle P\,\psi(x,y,t)=0,~~~P\doteq\partial_{y}-\partial_{x}^{2}-u(x,y,t),

(2.3a)

\displaystyle M\,\psi(x,y,t)=0,~~~M\doteq\partial_{t}+4\partial_{x}^{3}+6u(x,y,t)\partial_{x}+3u_{x}(x,y,t)+3\partial_{x}^{-1}u_{y}(x,y,t),

(2.3b)

and the KP equation arises from the compatibility
[P,M]=PM-MP=0
.
When
u(x,y,t)=u_{0}(x)
, the above Lax pair admits a special solution

\psi_{0}(x,y,t;k)=\Psi_{x}(k)\rho_{k}(y,t),

(2.4)

where
k
is a parameter,
\rho_{0}(k)
is a function of
k
but independent of
(x,y,t)
,

\rho_{k}(y,t)=\rho_{0}(k)\exp\Bigl(\wp(k)y-2\wp^{\prime}(k)t\Bigr),

(2.5)

and
\Psi_{x}(k)
is known as the Lamé function

\Psi_{x}(k)=\frac{\sigma(x+k)}{\sigma(x)\sigma(k)}e^{-\zeta(k)x}.

(2.6)

\psi_{0}(x,y,t;k)
can be viewed as a Lamé-type PWF for the
KP equation
[
32
]
.
Note that among the Weierstrass functions,

\wp(z)
is even and doubly periodic, i.e.,

\wp(z+2mw+2nw^{\prime})=\wp(z),~~m,n\in\mathbb{Z}

where
w
and
w^{\prime}
are the fundamental half-periods,

\zeta(z)
and
\sigma(z)
are odd and quasi-periodic,
in the sense that

\displaystyle\zeta(z+2mw+2nw^{\prime})=\zeta(z)+2m\zeta(w)+2n\zeta(w^{\prime}),

(2.7a)

\displaystyle\sigma(z+2mw+2nw^{\prime})=(-1)^{m+n+mn}\sigma(z)e^{(z+mw+nw^{\prime})(2m\zeta(w)+2n\zeta(w^{\prime}))}.

(2.7b)

When
k\neq w,w^{\prime}
,
\Psi_{x}(k)
and
\Psi_{x}(-k)
are linearly independent.

\Psi_{x}(k)
is doubly periodic with respect to
k
:

\Psi_{x}(k+2mw+2nw^{\prime})=\Psi_{x}(k).

(2.8)

In the following we describe the elliptic DL scheme of the Fokas-Ablowitz type
for the KP equation.

Theorem 2.1
.

Consider the integral equation of
\psi(x,y,t;k)
:

\psi(x,y,t;k)+\rho_{k}(y,t)\iint_{D}\psi(x,y,t;l)\gamma_{l^{\prime}}(y,t)\Psi_{x}{(k,l^{\prime})}\mathrm{d}\lambda(l,l^{\prime})=\Psi_{x}(k)\rho_{k}(y,t),

(2.9)

where
D
and
\mathrm{d}\lambda(l,l^{\prime})
are general integration domain and measure
in the space of the spectral variables
l
and
l^{\prime}
,

\rho_{k}(x,y,t)
and
\Psi_{x}(k)
are defined as in (
2.5
) and (
2.6
),

\Psi_{x}{(k,l^{\prime})}
is a generalized Lamé function defined as

\Psi_{x}{(k,l^{\prime})}=\frac{\sigma(x+k+l^{\prime})}{\sigma(x)\sigma(k+l^{\prime})}e^{-\big(\zeta(k)+\zeta(l^{\prime})\big)x}

(2.10)

and
\gamma_{l^{\prime}}(y,t)
is defined as

\gamma_{l^{\prime}}(y,t)=\gamma_{0}(l^{\prime})\exp\Big(-\wp(l^{\prime})y-2\wp^{\prime}(l^{\prime})t\Big)

(2.11)

with
\gamma_{0}(l^{\prime})
being a function of
l^{\prime}
.
It is assumed that the differential
\partial_{\mu}
commutes with
\iint_{D}
for
\mu=x,y,t

and the homogeneous integral equation has only zero solution.
Under these two assumptions,
solution of the KP equation (
2.1
) is provided through

u(x,y,t)=-2\wp(x)-2\partial_{x}\iint_{D}\psi(x,y,t;l)\gamma_{l^{\prime}}(y,t)\Psi_{x}(l^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\lambda(l,l^{\prime}).

(2.12)

Proof.

Thanks to the generalization of direct linearization in
[
49
]
, we apply it to the elliptic case.
We aim to prove that
\psi(x,y,t;k)
defined by (
2.9
)
together with
u(x,y,t)
defined by (
2.12
) satisfy the KP Lax pair (
2.3
),
i.e.
P\psi=0
and

, thereby confirming the theorem.
To achieve that, we consider the following general integral equation

\psi(x,y,t;k)+\iint_{D}\psi(x,y,t;l)h(x,y,t;k,l^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\lambda(l,l^{\prime})=\psi_{0}(x,y,t;k),

(2.13)

where
\psi_{0}(x,y,t;k)
is the wave function given by (
2.4
)
and

is a function to be determined.
We omit the independent variables and spectral parameters in case where it does not lead to confusion.
Next, we look for

such that (
2.3a
) holds for
\psi(x,y,t;k)
defined by (

).
Applying the operator

\displaystyle P\psi(x,y,t;k)+\iint_{D}(P\psi(x,y,t;l))h(x,y,t,k,l^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\lambda(l,l^{\prime})=0

(2.14)

if we require

(u+2\wp(x))\psi_{0}=\iint_{D}\Big(2\psi_{x}h_{x}-\psi\big(h_{y}-h_{xx}\big)\Big)\mathrm{d}\lambda(l,l^{\prime}).

(2.15)

Assume that there exist

and

such that the derivatives of

admit the following variable separation forms:

\displaystyle 2h_{x}(x,y,t;k,l^{\prime})=f(x,y,t;l^{\prime})\psi_{0}(x,y,t;k),

\displaystyle h_{y}(x,y,t;k,l^{\prime})-h_{xx}(x,y,t;k,l^{\prime})=-g(x,y,t;l^{\prime})\psi_{0}(x,y,t;k),

The compatibility

Noticing that (

2.3a
) with

,
we take (cf.(

f(x,y,t;l^{\prime})=-2\gamma_{l^{\prime}}(y,t)\Psi_{x}(l^{\prime})

(2.20)

where
\gamma_{l^{\prime}}(y,t)
is defined as (

).
Thus,
h(x,y,t;k,l^{\prime})
is well defined via (
2.16a
) and (
2.19
) with
\psi_{0}(x,y,t;k)

and the above

It is not difficult to get

by integrating (

) and (

).
Thus, we have

h(x,y,t;k,l^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2(\wp(k)-\wp(l^{\prime}))}\left(f(x,y,t;l^{\prime})\psi_{0,x}(x,y,t;k)-f_{x}(x,y,t;l^{\prime})\psi_{0}(x,y,t;k)\right),

which can be written as

(2.22)

where the following formulas have been used:

\displaystyle\wp(z)-\wp(u)=-\frac{\sigma(z+u)\sigma(z-u)}{\sigma^{2}(z)\sigma^{2}(u)},

(2.23a)

\displaystyle\zeta(u)+\zeta(v)+\zeta(z)-\zeta(u+v+z)=\frac{\sigma(u+v)\sigma(u+z)\sigma(z+v)}{\sigma(u)\sigma(v)\sigma(z)\sigma(z+u+v)}.

(2.23b)

Substituting (
2.22
) into (
2.13
) and substituting (

into (
2.17
), we can recover the integral equation (

) and the solution (

), respectively.
Meanwhile, with (
2.22
), the homogeneous integral equation (
2.14
)
has only zero solution
P\psi(x,y,t;k)=0
.

Now, what remains is to show
M\psi(x,y,t;k)=0
in light of the integral equation (
2.9
)
and the solution (
2.12
).
First, note that the expression (
2.21
) of
h(x,y,t;k,l^{\prime})
gives

h_{t}(x,y,t;k,l^{\prime})=2\psi_{0,x}(x,y,t;k)f_{x}(x,y,t;l^{\prime})-2\Big(\wp(k)+\wp(l^{\prime})+\wp(x)\Big)\psi_{0}(x,y,t;k)f(x,y,t;l^{\prime}).

(2.24)

Applying the operator

\begin{split}&M\psi+\iint_{D}(M\psi)h\mathrm{d}\lambda(l,l^{\prime})+\iint_{D}\psi h_{t}\mathrm{d}\lambda(l,l^{\prime})\\
&+12\iint_{D}(\psi_{x}h_{xx}+\psi_{xx}h_{x})\mathrm{d}\lambda(l,l^{\prime})+\iint_{D}(4\psi h_{xxx}+6u\psi h_{x})\mathrm{d}\lambda(l,l^{\prime})\\
=~&3\Big((u+2\wp(x))_{x}+\partial^{-1}u_{y}\Big)\psi_{0}+6(u+2\wp(x))\psi_{0,x}.\end{split}

By inserting
h_{t}(x,y,t;k,l^{\prime})
(
2.24
) and

(
2.12
),
it reduces to a homogeneous integral equation

\displaystyle M\psi(x,y,t;k)+\rho_{k}(y,t)\iint_{D}(M\psi(x,y,t;l))\gamma_{l^{\prime}}(y,t)\Psi_{x}(k,l^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\lambda(l,l^{\prime})=0,

which implies that

Thus, we complete the proof.

Connection with the Marchenko equation

Note that

\psi(x,y,t;k)+\rho_{k}(y,t)\iint_{D}\psi(x,y,t;l)\gamma_{l^{\prime}}(y,t)\widetilde{\Psi}_{x}{(k,l^{\prime})}\mathrm{d}\lambda(l,l^{\prime})=\widetilde{\Psi}_{x}(k)\rho_{k}(y,t),

the KP equation (
2.1
) allows a solution

u(x,y,t)=-2\wp(x+w^{\prime})-2\partial_{x}\iint_{D}\psi(x,y,t;l)\gamma_{l^{\prime}}(y,t)\widetilde{\Psi}_{x}(l^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\lambda(l,l^{\prime}).

(2.31)

The integral equation (
2.30
) is ready for deriving the Marchenko equation.
We multiply it by
\widetilde{\Psi}_{s}(k^{\prime})\gamma_{k^{\prime}}(y,t)

and integrate each term on the domain
D
in the space of variables
k
and
k^{\prime}
.
For the first term on the left-hand side and the term on the right-hand-side, we denote them as

\displaystyle K(x,s,y,t)

\displaystyle=-\iint_{D}\psi(x,y,t;l)\widetilde{\Psi}_{s}(l^{\prime})\gamma_{l^{\prime}}(y,t)\mathrm{d}\lambda(l,l^{\prime}),

(2.32a)

\displaystyle F(x,s,y,t)

\displaystyle=\iint_{D}\widetilde{\Psi}_{x}(k)\rho_{k}(y,t)\widetilde{\Psi}_{s}(k^{\prime})\gamma_{k^{\prime}}(y,t)\mathrm{d}\lambda(k,k^{\prime}).

(2.32b)

The remaining term reads

\iint_{D}\mathrm{d}\lambda(k,k^{\prime})\iint_{D}\mathrm{d}\lambda(l,l^{\prime})\widetilde{\Psi}_{s}(k^{\prime})\gamma_{k^{\prime}}(y,t)\psi(x,y,t;l)\rho_{k}(y,t)\widetilde{\Psi}_{x}(k,l^{\prime})\gamma_{l^{\prime}}(y,t).

(2.33)

Note that by making use of the formula (
2.23b
), we have a relation

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\widetilde{\Psi}_{x}(k,l^{\prime})=-\widetilde{\Psi}_{x}(k)\widetilde{\Psi}_{x}(l^{\prime}).

(2.34)

In what follows, we restrict ourselves to the case where
x,g_{2},g_{3}\in\mathbb{R}
,
w>0
,

\mathrm{Im}w^{\prime}>0
and
\Delta>0
.
Particularly, we take
k,l^{\prime}\in(0,w)
.
It then follows from item (2) and (3) of Proposition
2.2
that

v(x;k,l^{\prime})
is bounded on the real axis, and
p(k)+p(l^{\prime})<0
,
and consequently,
\widetilde{\Psi}_{x}(k,l^{\prime})
exponentially decreases to zero
as
x\to+\infty
.
Thus, from (
2.34
) we have

\int_{x}^{+\infty}\widetilde{\Psi}_{\xi}(k)\widetilde{\Psi}_{\xi}(l^{\prime})\mathrm{d}\xi=\widetilde{\Psi}_{x}(k,l^{\prime}),

(2.35)

which leads (
2.33
) to a form

-\int_{x}^{+\infty}K(x,\xi,y,t)F(\xi,s,y,t)\mathrm{d}\xi.

(2.36)

Finally, we can conclude the following.

Theorem 2.3
.

There exist
K(x,s,y,t)
and
F(x,s,y,t)
satisfying the Marchenko equation

K(x,s,y,t)+F(x,s,y,t)+\int_{x}^{+\infty}K(x,\xi,y,t)F(\xi,s,y,t)\mathrm{d}\xi=0,

(2.37)

and the solution of the KP equation (
2.1
) is given by

u(x,y,t)=-2\wp(x+w^{\prime})+2\partial_{x}K(x,x,y,t).

(2.38)

2.3
Elliptic soliton solutions

In the following, we construct elliptic soliton solutions of the KP equation from the integral equation (
2.9
)
in Theorem

We impose the following condition on the measure (cf.
[
18
,
44

\mathrm{d}\lambda(l,l^{\prime})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N^{\prime}}B_{j,i}\delta(l-k_{i})\delta(l^{\prime}-k^{\prime}_{j})\mathrm{d}l~\mathrm{d}l^{\prime},

where
B_{i,j}\in\mathbb{C}
,
k_{i}
and
k^{\prime}_{j}
belong to the fundamental periodic parallelogram
formed by
2w
and
2w^{\prime}
, and we assume
w>0,\mathrm{Im}w^{\prime}>0

Then the integral equation (
2.9
) reduces to a linear algebraic system

\displaystyle\psi(x,y,t;k_{i})+\rho_{k_{i}}(y,t)\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N^{\prime}}B_{j,i}\psi(x,y,t;k_{i})\gamma_{k^{\prime}_{j}}(y,t)\Psi_{x}(k_{i},k^{\prime}_{j})=\rho_{k_{i}}(y,t)\Psi_{x}(k_{i})

(2.39)

and the solution (

\displaystyle u(x,y,t)=-2\wp(x)-2\partial_{x}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N^{\prime}}B_{j,i}\psi(x,y,t;k_{i})\gamma_{k^{\prime}_{j}}(y,t)\Psi_{x}(k^{\prime}_{j}).

(2.40)

Introduce

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{(0)}=(\psi(x,y,t;k_{1}),~\psi(x,y,t;k_{2}),~\cdots,~\psi(x,y,t;k_{N}))^{T},

(2.41a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}=(\rho_{k_{1}}(y,t)\Psi_{x}(k_{1}),~\rho_{k_{1}}(y,t)\Psi_{x}(k_{2}),~\cdots,~\rho_{k_{N}}(y,t)\Psi_{x}(k_{N}))^{T},

(2.41b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{s}=(\gamma_{k^{\prime}_{1}}(y,t)\Psi_{x}(k^{\prime}_{1}),~\gamma_{k^{\prime}_{2}}(y,t)\Psi_{x}(k^{\prime}_{2}),~\cdots,~\gamma_{k^{\prime}_{N^{\prime}}}(y,t)\Psi_{x}(k^{\prime}_{N^{\prime}}))^{T}.

(2.41c)

Then, (
2.39
) and (
2.40
) can be formulated in matrix form

\displaystyle(\mathbf{I+MB})\mathbf{u}^{(0)}=\mathbf{r}

(2.42)

and

u(x,y,t)=-2\wp(x)-2\partial_{x}(\mathbf{s}^{T}\mathbf{B}\mathbf{u}^{(0)}),

(2.43)

where
\mathbf{I}
is a
N\times N
unit matrix,

\mathbf{B}=(B_{j,i})_{N^{\prime}\times N}
is an arbitrary constant matrix,
and
\mathbf{M}
is a (dressed) elliptic Cauchy matrix
with each element being doubly periodic with respect to
k_{i}
and
k^{\prime}_{j}
, defined as

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}=(M_{i,j})_{N\times N^{\prime}},\quad M_{i,j}=\rho_{k_{i}}(y,t)\Psi_{x}(k_{i},k^{\prime}_{j})\gamma_{k^{\prime}_{j}}(y,t).

(2.44)

Solving for
\mathbf{u}^{(0)}
from (
2.42

), we arrive at

u(x,y,t)=-2\wp(x)-2\partial_{x}(\mathbf{s}^{T}\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{I+MB})^{-1}\mathbf{r}),

(2.45)

which presents an elliptic multi-soliton solution for the KP equation (
2.1
).

We can construct the
\tau
-function by defining

\tau=|\mathbf{I+MB}|.

(2.46)

Using a trace formula (see
[
67
]
)

\mathbf{s}^{T}\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{I+MB})^{-1}\mathbf{r}=\mathrm{Tr}\left(\mathbf{r}\mathbf{s}^{T}\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{I+MB})^{-1}\right)=\mathrm{Tr}\left(-(\mathbf{I+MB})_{x}(\mathbf{I+MB})^{-1}\right)=-\frac{\tau_{x}}{\tau}

where relation
\mathbf{M}_{x}=-\mathbf{r}\mathbf{s}^{T}
has been used,
solution (

u(x,y,t)=2\partial_{x}^{2}\ln\big(\sigma(x)\tau\big).

(2.47)

In the following, for convenience, we consider a special case where we take
N=N^{\prime}
and
\mathbf{B}=\mathbf{I}
.
The
\tau
function can be expanded into Hirota’s form using the expansion

|\mathbf{I+M}|=1+\sum_{i=1}^{N}|M_{i,i}|+\sum_{i<j}\left|\begin{array}[]{cccc}M_{i,i}&M_{i,j}\\
M_{j,i}&M_{j,j}\end{array}\right|+\cdots+|\mathbf{M}|,

(2.48)

and each term can be expanded by the Frobenius formula
[
15
]

\begin{split}\Big|\rho_{k_{i}}\Psi_{x}(k_{i},l_{j})\gamma_{l_{j}}\Big|_{1\leq i,j\leq N}\!\!\!=\!\!\left(\!\prod_{i}^{N}\rho_{k_{i}}\gamma_{l_{i}}e^{-(\zeta(k_{i})+\zeta(l_{i}))x}\!\right)\!\!\frac{\sigma(x+\sum_{i=1}^{N}(k_{i}+l_{i}))}{\sigma(x)\prod_{i=1}^{N}\sigma(k_{i}+l_{i})}\!\!\prod_{i<j}\frac{\sigma(k_{i}-k_{j})\sigma(l_{i}-l_{j})}{\sigma(k_{i}+l_{j})\sigma(l_{i}+k_{j})}.\end{split}

Consequently, we obtain an explicit form for
\tau

[
27

32
]

\tau=\sum_{J\subset S}\left(\prod_{i\in J}c_{i}\right)\left(\mathop{\rm{\prod}}_{i<j\in J}A_{i,j}\right)\frac{\sigma(x+\sum_{i\in J}(k_{i}+k^{\prime}_{i}))}{\sigma(x)\prod_{i\in J}\sigma(k_{i}+k^{\prime}_{i})}\mathrm{exp}\left(\sum_{i\in J}\theta_{[e]}(x,y,t;k_{i},k^{\prime}_{i})\right),

where

J

\displaystyle\theta_{[e]}(x,y,t;k_{i},k^{\prime}_{i})=-(\zeta(k_{i})+\zeta(k^{\prime}_{i}))x+(\wp(k_{i})-\wp(k^{\prime}_{i}))y-2(\wp^{\prime}(k_{i})+\wp^{\prime}(k^{\prime}_{i}))t,

(2.50a)

\displaystyle A_{i,j}=\frac{\sigma(k_{i}-k_{j})\sigma(k^{\prime}_{i}-k^{\prime}_{j})}{\sigma(k_{i}-k^{\prime}_{j})\sigma(k^{\prime}_{i}-k_{j})}.

(2.50b)

If we start from Theorem
2.2

3
Reduction to the KdV and Boussinesq

3.1
Elliptic
N
th roots of unity

It is well known that soliton solutions of the KP equation can be reduced to those of the KdV equation,
Boussinesq equation and higher order equations in the Gel’fand-Dickey hierarchy.
This can be done by imposing certain constraints on the eigenvalues (i.e.
k_{i}
and
l_{i}
)
of the KP solution so that the dispersion relations of the lower dimensional equations are obtained.
In soliton case, such reductions are achieved through the constraint
l=\omega k

where
\omega
is
N
th root of unity.
In elliptic soliton case, the elliptic
N
th roots of unity were introduced in
[
44
]
,
which is used to reduce the elliptic DL schemes of the lattice KP system to the DL scheme of the lattice KdV system
and lattice Boussinesq system
[
44
]
,
as well as to reduce
\tau
-functions and vertex operators in the continuous case
[
32
]
.
Elliptic
N
th roots of unity are defined as the following.

Definition 3.1
.

[
44
]
There exist distinct
\{\omega_{j}(\delta)\}_{j=0}^{N-1}
,
up to the periodicity of the periodic lattice, such that the following equation holds,

\prod^{N-1}_{j=0}\frac{\sigma(\kappa+\omega_{j}(\delta))}{\sigma(\kappa)\sigma(\omega_{j}(\delta))}=\frac{1}{(N-1)!}(\wp^{(N-2)}(-\kappa)-\wp^{(N-2)}(\delta)),

(3.1a)

where
\omega_{0}(\delta)=\delta
and all
\{\omega_{j}(\delta)\}
are independent of
\kappa
.

\{\omega_{j}(\delta)\}_{j=0}^{N-1}
are called elliptic
N
th roots of the unity.
These roots also satisfy (modulo the period lattice)

\sum^{N-1}_{j=0}\omega_{j}(\delta)=0,\quad\sum^{N-1}_{j=0}\zeta^{(l)}(\omega_{j}(\delta))=0,~l=0,1,\cdots,N-2.

(3.1b)

3.2
Reduction to the KdV equation

3.2.1
DL scheme

The integral equation (
2.9
) of the KP equation can be reduced to the case of the KdV equation
by taking a specific measure

\mathrm{d}\lambda(l,l^{\prime})=\delta(l^{\prime}+\omega_{1}(l))\mathrm{d}\lambda(l)\mathrm{d}l^{\prime},

(3.2)

in which we use the elliptic square roots
\omega_{0}(l)=l
, and
\omega_{1}(l)=-l
.
The trick to get the integral equation for the KdV equation is the following.
In the first step, using (
3.2
) we reduce the integral equation (
2.9
) from
a double-integral form to a single-integral form

\psi(x,y,t;k)+\rho_{k}(y,t)\int_{L}\psi(x,y,t;l)\gamma_{l}(y,t)\Psi_{x}{(k,l)}\mathrm{d}\lambda(l)=\Psi_{x}{(k)}\rho_{k}(y,t),

(3.3)

where
L
,
\mathrm{d}\lambda(l)
are suitable contour and measure.
Next, we introduce
\phi(x,y,t;k)
such that

\psi(x,y,t;k)=\phi(x,y,t;k)\rho_{k}(y,t),

(3.4)

by which it follows from (
3.3
) that

\phi(x,y,t;k)+\int_{L}\phi(x,y,t;l)\rho_{l}(y,t)\gamma_{l}(y,t)\Psi_{x}{(k,l)}\mathrm{d}\lambda(l)=\Psi_{x}{(k)}.

(3.5)

It turns out that in the kernel

\rho_{l}(y,t)\gamma_{l}(y,t)\Psi_{x}{(k,l)}=\rho_{0}(l)\gamma_{0}(l)\Psi_{x}{(k,l)}e^{-4\wp^{\prime}(l)t}

is independent of
y
,
which means there exists a solution
\phi(x,y,t;k)
that is independent of
y
as well.
We denote the above
\phi(x,y,t;k)
to be
\phi(x,t;k)
without making any confusion.
Then we introduce

\varrho_{k}(t)=\varrho_{0}(k)e^{-2\wp^{\prime}(k)t},

(3.6)

and
\varphi(x,t;k)=\phi(x,t;k)\varrho_{k}(t)

and take
\gamma_{0}(k)=\varrho_{0}(k)
,
we arrive at an integral equation from (
3.5
)

\varphi(x,t;k)+\varrho_{k}(t)\int_{L}\varphi(x,t;l)\varrho_{l}(t)\Psi_{x}{(k,l)}\mathrm{d}\lambda(l)=\Psi_{x}{(k)}\varrho_{k}(t).

(3.7)

Repeating the same procedure, the solution can be reduced as

u(x,t)=-2\wp(x)-2\partial_{x}\int_{L}\varphi(x,t;l)\varrho_{l}(t)\Psi_{x}(l)\mathrm{d}\lambda(l).

(3.8)

These two settings consist of the elliptic DL scheme for the KdV equation (
1.1
).

Theorem 3.1
.

If the integral equation (
3.7
) satisfies:
(a) the contour
L
and measure
\mathrm{d}\lambda(l)
permit the interchange of
\int_{L}

and the differentials w.r.t.
x,t
,
(b) the homogeneous integral equation has only the zero solution,
then the function
u(x,t)
defined by (
3.8
) solves the KdV equation (
1.1
).

The proof is similar to the KP case. One can prove that
\varphi(x,t;k)
and
u(x,t)
defined by
(
3.7
) and (
3.8
) agree with the Lax pair of the KdV equation:

\displaystyle P\varphi(x,t;k)=\Big(\partial_{x}^{2}+u(x,t)-\wp(k)\Big)\varphi(x,t;k)=0,

(3.9a)

\displaystyle M\varphi(x,t;k)=\Big(-\partial_{t}+u_{x}(x,t)-(4\wp(k)+2u(x,t))\partial_{x}\Big)\varphi(x,t;k)=0.

(3.9b)

We skip presenting the proof.

3.2.2
Marchenko equation

Similar to the treatment in section
2.2
,
here we again restrict our discussion to the case where

,
w>0
,

\mathrm{Im}w^{\prime}>0
and
\Delta>0

These settings allow us to have an alternative elliptic DL scheme which can yield real non-singular

Theorem 3.2
.

Assume
\varrho_{k}(t)
,
\widetilde{\Psi}_{x}(k)
and
\widetilde{\Psi}_{x}(k,l)
are defined as
in (
3.6
), (
2.25a
) and (

For
\varphi(x,t;k)
defined by the integral equation

\varphi(x,t;k)+\varrho_{k}(t)\int_{L}\varphi(x,t;l)\varrho_{l}(t)\widetilde{\Psi}_{x}{(k,l)}\mathrm{d}\lambda(l)=\widetilde{\Psi}_{x}(k)\varrho_{k}(t),

(3.10)

the KdV equation (
1.1
) has a solution

u(x,t)=-2\wp(x+w^{\prime})-2\partial_{x}\int_{L}\varphi(x,t;l)\varrho_{l}(t)\widetilde{\Psi}_{x}(l)\mathrm{d}\lambda(l).

(3.11)

It is worth noting that the relation (
2.34
), i.e.

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}x}\widetilde{\Psi}_{x}(k,l)=-\widetilde{\Psi}_{x}(k)\widetilde{\Psi}_{x}(l)

(3.12)

still holds for
\widetilde{\Psi}_{x}(k)
and
\widetilde{\Psi}_{x}(k,l)
defined in (
2.25

The key clue is to ensure that the right-hand side of the aforementioned equation
converges to zero rapidly enough as

Multiplying both sides of the integral equation (

)
by the function
\widetilde{\Psi}_{s}(k)\varrho_{k}(t)
and integrating along

in the spectral parameter

the establishment of the following relation

(3.13)

allows the separation of parameters
k
and
l

Consequently we can have a Marchenko equation

K(x,s,t)+F(x,s,t)+\int_{x}^{\infty}K(x,\xi,t)F(\xi,s,t)\mathrm{d}\xi=0,

(3.14)

and the corresponding solution for the KdV equation:

u(x,t)=-2\wp(x+w^{\prime})+2\partial_{x}K(x,x,t),

(3.15)

where

\displaystyle K(x,s,t)

\displaystyle=-\int_{L}\varphi(x,t;l)\widetilde{\Psi}_{s}(l)\varrho_{l}(t)\mathrm{d}\lambda(l),

(3.16a)

\displaystyle F(x,s,t)

\displaystyle=\int_{L}\widetilde{\Psi}_{x}(k)\widetilde{\Psi}_{s}(k)\varrho_{k}^{2}(t)\mathrm{d}\lambda(k).

(3.16b)

In
[
30
]

Consequently,

\displaystyle K(x,s,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{N}\varphi(x,t;k_{m})\widetilde{\Psi}_{s}(k_{m})\varrho_{k_{m}}(t),

(3.17a)

\displaystyle F(x,s,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=1}^{N}\widetilde{\Psi}_{x}(k_{m})\widetilde{\Psi}_{s}(k_{m})\varrho_{k_{m}}^{2}(t),

(3.17b)

which yield elliptic
N
-soliton solutions from the Marchenko equation (

Similar case also happened when deriving the Marchenko equation from the DL integral equation

[

which actually excludes the continuous spectrum from

3.2.3
Real-valued elliptic solitons

We assume that

-w-w^{\prime},w-w^{\prime},w+w^{\prime},-w+w^{\prime}

\{k_{j}\}

L

and the measure
\mathrm{d}\lambda(l)

\mathrm{d}\lambda(l)=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\frac{\mathrm{d}l}{l-k_{j}}.

(3.18)

Then the integral equation (
3.7

(3.19a)

where

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}=\left(M_{i,j}\right)_{N\times N},~~~M_{i,j}=\varrho_{k_{i}}(t)\Psi_{x}(k_{i},k_{j})\varrho_{k_{j}}(t),

(3.19b)

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{(0)}=(\varphi(x,t;k_{1}),\cdots,\varphi(x,t;k_{N}))^{T},

(3.19c)

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}=(\varrho_{k_{1}}(t)\Psi_{x}(k_{1}),\cdots,\varrho_{k_{N}}(t)\Psi_{x}(k_{N}))^{T}.

(3.19d)

Consequently, (

where the
\tau

\tau=|\mathbf{I+M}|=\sum_{J\subset S}\left(\prod_{i\in J}c_{i}\right)\Biggl(\prod_{i,j\in J\atop i<j}A_{i,j}\Biggr)\frac{\sigma(x+2\sum_{i\in J}k_{i})}{\sigma(x)\prod_{i\in J}\sigma(2k_{i})}\exp\left(\sum_{i\in J}\theta_{[e]}(x,t;k_{i})\right),

(3.21)

where
c_{i}=\varrho_{0}^{2}(k_{i})\in\mathbb{C}
,
\sum_{J\subset S}

means the summation runs over all subsets
J
of
S=\{1,2,\cdots,N\}
, and

\displaystyle\theta_{[e]}(x,t;k_{i})=-2\zeta(k_{i})x-4\wp^{\prime}(k_{i})t,\qquad A_{i,j}=\left(\frac{\sigma(k_{i}-k_{j})}{\sigma(k_{i}+k_{j})}\right)^{2}.

(3.22)

Similar to the KP case, starting from the integral equation (
3.10
) rather than (
3.7
),
we will have non-singular soliton solutions

u=2\partial_{x}^{2}\ln\big(\sigma(x+w^{\prime})\tau\big),

(3.23)

where

\tau=\tau_{N}=\sum_{J\subset S}\left(\prod_{i\in J}c_{i}\right)\Biggl(\prod_{i,j\in J\atop i<j}A_{i,j}\Biggr)\frac{\sigma(x+2\sum_{i\in J}k_{i}+w^{\prime})}{\sigma(x+w^{\prime})\prod_{i\in J}\sigma(2k_{i})}\exp\left(\sum_{i\in J}\theta_{[e]}(x,t;k_{i})\right),

(3.24)

and
\theta_{[e]}(x,t;k_{i})
and
A_{i,j}

but

c_{i}=\widetilde{c}_{i}\exp\left(-2\zeta(w^{\prime})k_{i}\right),~~~\widetilde{c}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}.

(3.25)

The first two

\tau_{1}=1+c_{1}\frac{\sigma(x+w^{\prime}+2k_{1})}{\sigma(x+w^{\prime})\sigma(2k_{1})}e^{\theta_{[e]}(x,t;k_{1})},

(3.26)

and

\displaystyle\tau_{2}

\displaystyle=1+c_{1}\frac{\sigma(x+w^{\prime}+2k_{1})}{\sigma(x+w^{\prime})\sigma(2k_{1})}e^{\theta_{[e]}(x,t;k_{1})}+c_{2}\frac{\sigma(x+w^{\prime}+2k_{2})}{\sigma(x+w^{\prime})\sigma(2k_{2})}e^{\theta_{[e]}(x,t;k_{2})}

\displaystyle+c_{1}c_{2}A_{1,2}\frac{\sigma(x+w^{\prime}+2k_{1}+2k_{2})}{\sigma(x+w^{\prime})\sigma(2k_{1})\sigma(2k_{2})}e^{\theta_{[e]}(x,t;k_{1})+\theta_{[e]}(x,t;k_{2})}.

3.3
Reduction to the Boussinesq equation

3.3.1
DL scheme

In this section, we reduce the DL scheme and solutions of the KP equation to those of the Boussinesq equation,

u_{xxxx}+6u^{2}_{x}+6uu_{xx}+3u_{yy}=0,

(3.28)

for which the elliptic cube roots
\omega_{i}(\delta)

(i=0,1,2)
as defined
in Definition
3.1
are needed, i.e.
\wp^{\prime}(\delta)=\wp^{\prime}(\omega_{i}(\delta))
,
where, especially, we set
\omega_{0}(\delta)=\delta
.
The reduction can be achieved by introducing a special measure
in the linear integral equation of the KP equation (
2.9
) (cf.
[
44
]
):

\mathrm{d}\lambda(l,l^{\prime})=\sum_{j=1}^{2}\delta(l^{\prime}+\omega_{j}(l))\mathrm{d}\lambda_{j}(l)\mathrm{d}l^{\prime}.

(3.29)

The reduction procedure follows a similar approach to that of Theorem
3.1
.
Thus we omit it and only present the main results.

Theorem 3.3
.

We assume that if
\psi(x,y;k)
solves

\psi(x,y;k)+\mu_{k}(y)\sum_{j=1}^{2}\int_{L_{j}}\psi(x,y;l)\gamma_{-\omega_{j}(l)}(y)\Psi_{x}{(k,-\omega_{j}(l))}\mathrm{d}\lambda_{j}(l)=\mu_{k}(y)\Psi_{x}{(k)},

(3.30)

where
L_{j}
,
\mathrm{d}\lambda_{j}(l)
are certain contour and measure,

\displaystyle\mu_{k}(y)=\mu_{0}(k)e^{\wp(k)y},\qquad\gamma_{-\omega_{j}(l)}(y)=\gamma_{0}(\omega_{j}(l))e^{-\wp(\omega_{j}(l))y},

(3.31)

and
\omega_{i}(\delta)
are the elliptic cube roots for
j=1,2
.
We also assume that differentials with respect to
x,y
can interchange with
\int_{L_{j}}
,
and the homogeneous integral equation of (
3.30
) has only zero solution.
Then the Boussinesq equation (
3.28
) allows a solution

u(x,y)=-2\wp(x)-2\partial_{x}\sum_{j=1}^{2}\int_{L_{j}}\psi(x,y;l)\gamma_{-\omega_{j}(l)}(y)\Psi_{x}(-\omega_{j}(l))\mathrm{d}\lambda_{j}(l).

(3.32)

This theorem can also be proved by verifying
\psi(x,y;k)
and
u(x,y)

satisfy the Lax pair of the Boussinesq equation:

\displaystyle P\psi(x,y;k)=\Big(\partial_{y}-\partial_{x}^{2}-u(x,y)\Big)\psi(x,y;k)=0,

(3.33a)

\displaystyle M\psi(x,y;k)=\Big(\partial_{x}^{3}+\frac{3}{2}u(x,y)\partial_{x}+\frac{3}{4}u_{x}(x,y)+\frac{3}{4}\partial_{x}^{-1}u_{y}(x,y)-\frac{1}{2}\wp^{\prime}(k)\Big)\psi(x,y;k)=0.

(3.33b)

Note that here (
3.33b
) serves as a third-order spectral problem for the Boussinesq equation.

3.3.2
Marchenko equation

For the Boussinesq equation, to obtain real-valued elliptic soliton solutions,
we should require all three elliptic cube roots to be real, which means that
\wp^{\prime}(z)=\wp^{\prime}(k)

should have three distinct real roots within the real period for a given
k\in\mathbb{R}
.
However, according to Proposition
B.1
, in the case where
\Delta>0
,
there are no distinct real roots for
\wp^{\prime}(z)=\wp^{\prime}(k)
in
(0,2w]
.
We exclude the case
\Delta<0
where distinct real roots exist,
because prefer the formulation of
w
being real and
w^{\prime}
purely imaginary
so that we can shift the singularity to the complex plane.
Thus, we need to modify some settings to reduce the Marchenko equation to the Boussinesq case.

In the following, we consider an extended Boussinesq equation
which is motivated by some lattice Boussinesq equations with parameters
[
23
,
72
]

(similar to the continuous case in
[
25
]
),
where the PWFs are defined using the roots of (see
[
24
,
72
]
)

G(z,k)=z^{3}-k^{3}+b_{0}(z^{2}-k^{2})=0,

which allows distinct real roots for suitable parameter
b_{0}

and leads to non-singular real soliton solutions for the lattice Boussinesq equation
[
24
]
.
In the elliptic soliton case, we start from the following KP equation with a real parameter
b_{0}

u_{T}+b_{0}u_{Y}+u_{XXX}+6uu_{X}+3\partial_{X}^{-1}u_{YY}=0,

(3.34)

which is connected with the KP equation (
2.1
) via coordinate transformations

x=X,\quad y=Y-b_{0}T,\quad t=T.

(3.35)

The Lax pair (
2.3
), integral equation (
2.9
) (as well as (
2.30
))
and solution (
2.12
) (as well as (
2.31
)) can be accordingly and easily rewritten out in terms of
(X,Y,T)
.

With selected parameters
b_{0}
and
l
in
\mathbb{R}
, the equation

\displaystyle 2\wp^{\prime}(z)+b_{0}\wp(z)=2\wp^{\prime}(l)+b_{0}\wp(l)

(3.36)

allows three distinct real roots
z=\omega_{j}(l)
,
j=0,1,2
in
(0,2w]

when
g_{2},g_{3}\in\mathbb{R}
and
\Delta>0
(see Appendix
B.2
).
In the following we always set
\omega_{j}(l)=l
.
Choosing the measure

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\lambda(l,l^{\prime})=\sum_{j=1}^{2}\delta(l^{\prime}+\omega_{j}(l))\mathrm{d}\lambda_{j}(l)\mathrm{d}l^{\prime},

(3.37)

we can reduce the
(X,Y,T)
-counterpart of (
2.30
) into an integral equation

\displaystyle\psi(X,Y;k)+\mu_{k}(Y)\sum_{j=1}^{2}\int_{L_{j}}\psi(X,Y;l)\gamma_{-\omega_{j}(l)}(Y)\widetilde{\Psi}_{X}{(k,-\omega_{j}(l))}\mathrm{d}\lambda_{j}(l)=\mu_{k}(Y)\widetilde{\Psi}_{X}{(k)},

(3.38a)

where

\displaystyle\mu_{k}(Y)=\mu_{0}(k)e^{\wp(k)Y},\qquad\gamma_{-\omega_{j}(l)}(Y)=\gamma_{0}(\omega_{j}(l))e^{-\wp(\omega_{j}(l))Y}.

(3.38b)

And from (
2.31
) we have

\displaystyle u(X,Y)=-2\wp(X+w^{\prime})-2\partial_{X}\sum_{j=1}^{2}\int_{L_{j}}\psi(X,Y;l)\gamma_{-\omega_{j}(l)}(Y)\widetilde{\Psi}_{X}(-\omega_{j}(l))\mathrm{d}\lambda_{j}(l).

(3.39)

Let us conclude the following.

Theorem 3.4
.

Assume
X,Y,k,b_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
,
\Delta>0

and
\omega_{j}(l),j=1,2,
are the distinct real roots of (
3.36
).
For
\psi(X,Y;l)
defined by (
3.38
),
the function
u(X,Y)
defined by (
3.39
) provides a non-singular real elliptic soliton solution
for the extended Boussinesq equation

(b_{0}u_{Y}+u_{XXX}+6uu_{X})_{X}+3u_{YY}=0.

(3.40)

The term
u_{XY}
can be transformed into
u_{XX}
via
\partial_{Y}\to\partial_{Y}-\frac{1}{6}b_{0}\partial_{X}
, which gives rise to the standard Boussinesq equation with a mass term

-\frac{1}{12}b_{0}^{2}u_{XX}+u_{XXXX}+6u_{X}^{2}+6uu_{XX}+3u_{YY}=0.

(3.41)

The Theorem
3.4
can also be proven with the aid of the Lax pair

\displaystyle P\psi(X,Y;k)=

\displaystyle\Big(\partial_{Y}-\partial_{X}^{2}-u(X,Y)\Big)\psi(X,Y;k)=0,

(3.42a)

\displaystyle M\psi(X,Y;k)=

\displaystyle\Big(\partial_{X}^{3}+\frac{3}{2}u(X,Y)\partial_{X}+\frac{3}{4}u_{X}(X,Y)+\frac{3}{4}\partial_{X}^{-1}u_{Y}(X,Y)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\wp^{\prime}(k)-\frac{1}{4}b_{0}\wp(k)+\frac{1}{4}b_{0}\partial_{Y}\Big)\psi(X,Y;k)=0.

(3.42b)

To have a Marchenko equation for the extended Boussinesq equation (
3.40
), we introduce

\displaystyle K_{i}(X,s,Y)

\displaystyle=-\int_{L_{i}}\psi(X,Y;l)\widetilde{\Psi}_{s}(-\omega_{i}(l))\gamma_{-\omega_{i}(l)}(Y)\mathrm{d}\lambda_{i}(l),

(3.43a)

\displaystyle F_{i}(X,s,Y)

\displaystyle=\int_{L_{i}}\widetilde{\Psi}_{X}(k)\mu_{k}(Y)\widetilde{\Psi}_{s}(-\omega_{i}(k))\gamma_{-\omega_{i}(k)}(Y)\mathrm{d}\lambda_{i}(k).

(3.43b)

Multiplying both sides of the integral equation (
3.38
) by

\widetilde{\Psi}_{s}(-\omega_{i}(k))\gamma_{-\omega_{i}(k)}(Y)
and integrating along the path
L_{i}
,
similar to the KdV case (see Sec.
3.2.2
),
with the help of the valid integral

\int_{x}^{+\infty}\widetilde{\Psi}_{\xi}(k)\widetilde{\Psi}_{\xi}(-\omega_{i}(l))\mathrm{d}\xi=\widetilde{\Psi}_{x}(k,-\omega_{i}(l)),

(3.44)

we can get

K_{i}(X,s,Y)+F_{i}(X,s,Y)+\int_{x}^{\infty}F_{i}(\xi,s,Y)\left(K_{1}(X,\xi,Y)+K_{2}(X,\xi,Y)\right)\mathrm{d}\xi=0

(3.45)

for
i=1,2
.
Next, setting

K(X,s,Y)=\sum_{i=1}^{2}K_{i}(X,s,Y),~~~F(X,s,Y)=\sum_{i=1}^{2}F_{i}(X,s,Y),

we arrive at a Marchenko equation, i.e.

K(X,s,Y)+F(X,s,Y)+\int_{x}^{\infty}K(X,\xi,Y)F(\xi,s,Y)\mathrm{d}\xi=0,

(3.46)

for the extended Boussinesq equation (
3.40
),
and its solution can be given by

u(X,Y)=-2\wp(X+w^{\prime})+2\partial_{X}K(X,X,Y).

(3.47)

In some special cases, one can get real solutions from the above Marchenko equation.
However, we prefer to derive solutions directly from the integral equation (
3.38
).
We now proceed to the next step for real elliptic soliton solutions.

3.3.3
Real-valued elliptic solitons

To derive elliptic soliton solutions of the extended Boussinesq equation (
3.40

in (
3.38a

\mathrm{d}\lambda_{j}(l)=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\sum_{\beta=1}^{N}\frac{\mathrm{d}l}{l-k_{\beta}},~~j=1,2,

and

u(X,Y)=-2\wp(X+w^{\prime})-2(\mathbf{s}^{T}(\mathbf{I+M})^{-1}\mathbf{r})_{X}=2\partial_{X}^{2}\ln\big(\sigma(X+w^{\prime})\tau\big),

for

4
Conclusions

In this paper, we have established the elliptic DL schemes and Marchenko equations for the KP,
KdV and Boussinesq equations.
Non-singular real elliptic soliton solutions for these equations were obtained.
The DL scheme of the KP equation consists of an integral equation (e.g.(
2.9
))
and a formula of solution (e.g.(
2.12
)).
This is confirmed by checking the Lax pair.
Since the Marchenko equation involves integration on the real axis,
we have discussed real-valuedness of the Weierstrass functions in Appendix
A
.
Based on these discussions, we can choose parameters
k
and
l^{\prime}

such that the integration (
2.35
) on
(x,\infty)
makes sense.
Then we were able to construct a Marchenko equation for elliptic soliton solutions (see (
2.37
)).
To have non-singular real solutions, in addition to utilizing real-valuedness of the Weierstrass functions,
we also employed a trick,
x\to x+w^{\prime}
, to shift zeros of
\sigma(z)
from the real axis to the half complex plane.
As a result, we obtained
\tau
function for non-singular real elliptic solitons of the KP equation.
The illustration shows line solitons on a periodic background.
We also obtained resonant Y-shape elliptic line solitons.

The concept of elliptic
N
th roots of unity can be used to reduce the elliptic DL scheme and
\tau
function
of the KP equation to those of the KdV and Boussinesq equations.
However, in this paper, we have only obtained formal Marchenko equations for the KdV and Boussinesq equations.
This is because, for the KdV equation,
we must consider simultaneously the convergence of the integral (
3.13
)
and the significance of the contour integrals in (
3.16
).
Same problems are encountered for the Boussinesq equation.
Note that if we are only concerned with elliptic solitons,
which correspond to discrete eigenvalues, the Marchenko equations can always be obtained,
and they are in the same form as for the usual solitons.

With respect to constructing elliptic soliton solutions, the DL approach allows more flexibility,
compared with using the Marchenko equation (cf.
[
30
]
for the KdV).
One may also try other measures to get solutions of other types,
e.g. multiple-pole type elliptic solitons, which have not been investigated before.
In addition, the DL scheme is closely related to the Cauchy matrix structure of soliton solutions.
For usual soliton solutions, such structures are formulated by a Sylvester equation
together with dispersion relations (corresponding to the equations under investigation,
see e.g.
[
71
,
67
]
).
For elliptic soliton solutions, it seems there is no Sylvester type equations available (see also
[
38
]
).
Note that the Cauchy matrix approach has been well developed for the usual soliton case,
e.g.
[
39
,
71
,
67
,
56
,
31
]
.
In particular, it allows explicit multiple pole solutions by solving the Sylvester equation.
How to get multiple-pole elliptic solitons in terms of elliptic Cauchy matrix is also an interesting problem
for consideration.

Real-valuedness of the Weierstrass functions

For this part we mainly refer to

Appendix C
On elliptic solitons

The authors are grateful to the referee for the comments on elliptic solutions and related references,
which inspire us to complete this brief review on elliptic solitons.

The investigation of “elliptic solitons” dates back to the research of Krichever
[
28
]

on elliptic solutions of the KP equation and the KdV equation.
He proposed a criterion for such solutions that
the solutions should be elliptic in the spatial variable
x
.
For the KP equation, Krichever proved its elliptic solutions
should take the form (
[
28
]
, Theorem 4)

u=\mathrm{const.}+\sum_{j=1}^{n}\wp(x-x_{j}(y,t)),

(C.1)

where
\{x_{j}(y,t)\}
obey some dynamical system of finite dimension.
He also showed that (
C.1
) can be expressed in terms of
\theta
-function (
[
28
]
, Corollary 3).
The reduction to the KdV equation recovers the 2-gap elliptic solution in
[
10
]

and the results of the paper
[
4
]

that for the first time links the elliptic solutions of integrable equations
with the theory of finite-dimensional dynamic systems.

The term “elliptic soliton” is introduced by Verdier in
[
64
]
,
where he described solitons using the language of algebraic geometry.
In his definition, “algebraic solitons” are governed by some linear algebraic subgroup
G
,
while when
G
is an elliptic curve, the solutions are called “elliptic solitons”.
Verdier pointed out in
[
64
]
that the elliptic solitons of the KP equation
must be of the form

u=\mathrm{const.}+2\sum_{j=1}^{n}m_{j}\wp(x-x_{j}(y,t)),

(C.2)

where
m_{j}
are triangular numbers,
and some new elliptic source potentials (without time evolution) for the KdV equation were listed out in

[
64
]
.
Verdier’s research
was elaborated later with his collaborator Treibich in

[
58
,
62
,
63
,
59
]

by using the tool of “tangential cover”.
In particular, in
[
63
]
they presented the following elliptic source potential for the KdV equation:

u=\sum_{j=0}^{3}a_{j}(a_{j}+1)\wp(x-x_{j}),~~~~~\sum_{j=0}^{3}a_{j}(a_{j}+1)=2n,

(C.3)

where
n
is the degree of the tangential cover.
This is more general than the Lamé-Ince potential
[
26
]

which reads
u=g(g+1)\wp(x),~g\in\mathbb{N}
.
The function (
C.3
) is known as the Treibich-Verdier potential
and now the Darboux-Treibich-Verdier (DTV) potential.
It is Matveev and Smirnov
[
35
]
who pointed out that
Darboux found the same potential in 1882
[
9
]

but given in terms of Jacobi elliptic functions.
They also showed in
[
35
]
that the Darboux equation is a canonical form the de Sparre equation

[
8
]
.
There are other mathematicians and physicists joined the
research of the DTV potential, e.g.

[
6
,
20
,
50
,
51
,
53
,
57
,
65
]
.
Later, finite-gap elliptical potentials of a more general form (e.g.
[
54
]
, Eq.(0.10))
of the Schrödinger spectral problem

\psi_{xx}(x,\lambda)+u(x)(x,\lambda)=\lambda\psi(x,\lambda)

were found
[
54
,
61
]
.
It is also remarkable that Gesztesy and Weikard
[
21
]

investigated the Schrödinger spectral problem
by utilizing a theorem of Picard which
concerned with the existence of solutions
that are elliptic of the
n
th-order ordinary differential equations
with elliptic coefficients.
They proved that all elliptic KdV algebro-geometry potentials
are equivalent to the Picard potentials
[
21
]
.
As examples they gave several elliptic potentials corresponding to genus
g=4,5

(
[
21
]
, Eqs.(5.40) and (5.43); also
[
20
]
, Eqs.(3.86)-(3.89)).
It is also worth mentioning that Krichever et.al. investigated the case of matrix KP equation
[
29
]

and the analogue research of
[
58
]
for matrix case was developed by Treibich in
[
60
]
.

In addition, Belokolos and Enol’skii considered reduction from the general finite-gap solution to the DTV potential

[
6
]
. Later, they
[
7
]
described a technique on how elliptic finite-gap potentials are
derived from the Matveev-Its formula for the finite-gap potential.
Besides, Enol’skii and Kostov
[
11
]
discussed the conditions on the moduli of the
corresponding algebraic curve under which the general finite-gap solution reduces to elliptic solitons.
They indicated a completely algorithmic construction of the elliptic soliton moduli.
There are also studies on the elliptic potentials related to the Ablowitz-Kaup-Newell-Segur spectral problem,
for example,
[
52
]
from Smirnov and
[
22
]
by Gesztesy and Weikard.

The solutions we derived in this paper can be considered as special excitations of elliptic potentials
without increasing genus.
In illustration they are solitons living on an elliptic background.
Strictly speaking, these solutions are not elliptic in
x
any longer.
We follow
[
38
]
and still employ the name “elliptic solitons” in this paper.
