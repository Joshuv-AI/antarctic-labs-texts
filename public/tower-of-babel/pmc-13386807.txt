Epidemiology and severity of mountain rescue missions in Poland: a retrospective study of 9,477 patients rescued by the Tatra Mountain Rescue Service
Authors: Łukasz Migiel, Sylweriusz Kosiński, Marcin Waligóra, Jan Krzysztof, Maciej Mikiewicz, Andrzej Górka, Michał Jaros, Wojciech Kącki, Matthieu Pasquier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMountain activities have become increasingly popular in recent years, leading to a growing number of mountain rescue missions. Despite this trend, detailed epidemiological analyses of mountain accidents in the Polish Tatra Mountains are lacking. The aim of this study was to describe the epidemiology of mountain rescue missions in the Polish Tatra Mountains, with particular emphasis on the severity of the case mix of injuries and illnesses and their implications for rescue response.MethodsA retrospective analysis of prospectively collected data from the Tatra Mountain Rescue Service (TOPR) covering the period of 2015–2024 was conducted. Demographic, epidemiological, and operational data were extracted from the electronic rescue registry. The severity of the patient’s condition was classified using the National Advisory Committee for Aeronautics (NACA) score. The population at risk was estimated on the basis of the number of ticket entries to Tatra National Park during the study period. Seasonal differences in rescue incidence and severity were analysed, and absolute risk increase (ARI) and number needed to harm (NNH) were calculated.ResultsA total of 9,477 patients were included in the final analysis. The median age of the rescued individuals was 36 years (IQR 25–48); 48% (n = 4,539) were women, and 10% (n = 998) were minors. The median NACA score was 2 (IQR, 0–3), while 38% of the patients were classified as having a NACA score ≥ 3. Deaths occurred in 2% of the rescue missions. The median duration of rescue missions was 1.75 h (1.25–2.75). For every 100,000 visitors to the Tatra National Park, approximately 24 rescue missions were performed, including one involving severe injury or illness (NACA ≥ 4). Severe cases were significantly more frequent during the winter season (6.3% vs. 4.7%; p = 0.001). Helicopters were used in approximately one-third of the missions, and their use was correlated with higher NACA scores.ConclusionMountain rescue missions in the Polish Tatra Mountains were associated with seasonal patterns of mountain activities and the number of visitors to Tatra National Park. Systematic assessment of a patient’s severity using the NACA score may support decision-making and resource allocation in mountain rescue. Epidemiological analyses based on comprehensive rescue databases may contribute to improved planning, training, and organization of medical search and rescue systems in mountain environments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13049-026-01631-3.

Background

Mountain activities are becoming increasingly popular worldwide, and the number of mountain rescue missions has risen accordingly. Several epidemiological studies have investigated mountain accidents in different regions and have identified common mechanisms of injury, environmental hazards, and patterns of rescue activation. The importance of understanding both the epidemiology and clinical severity of events occurring in mountain environments has been emphasized [1–7].

Reliable epidemiological data are essential for the planning and organization of mountain rescue systems. Knowledge of injury patterns, environmental conditions, and severity of the injury or illness may support more effective resource allocation, improve operational planning, and optimize the deployment of rescue personnel and helicopters [2, 4, 7–11]. One commonly used tool for assessing patient condition in a prehospital setting is the National Advisory Committee for Aeronautics (NACA) score. This tool provides a rapid and standardized method for classifying the severity of injuries or illnesses, can facilitate communication between rescue personnel, support triage decisions, and enable retrospective analyses of rescue missions, allowing services to assess their effectiveness and identify areas for improvement [8, 9, 12–19].

Despite the growing number of visitors to the Tatra Mountains, comprehensive epidemiological analyses of rescue missions in this region remain limited. In particular, little is known about the relationship between injury or illness severity and the operational characteristics of rescue missions. The aim of this study is to describe the epidemiology of mountain rescue missions in the Polish Tatra Mountains, with a particular emphasis on the severity of the case mix of injuries and illnesses. An additional objective was to investigate the relationships between injury or illness severity and rescue response characteristics.

Methods

Study design

This study was based on a retrospective analysis of prospectively collected data from the Tatra Mountain Rescue Service (TOPR) over a ten-year period, from 2015 to 2024. The project was reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Board of Jagiellonian University Medical College (No. 118.0043.1.184.2024). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The reporting is in accordance with the applicable STROBE guidelines.

Study setting

Tatra National Park (TPN) is characterized by a high mountain landscape with alpine elements, such as rocks, sharp ridges, glacial valleys, and lakes. The highest peak is Rysy (2,499 m above sea level). Visitors have access to 275 km of marked trails, most of which are open year-round. Artificial aids (steel steps, chains, and ladders) have been installed in exposed or technically challenging areas. Downhill skiers can use grooming trails in the Kasprowy Wierch area (1,987 m above sea level). A network of trails and areas for off-piste skiing and ski mountaineering is also available. Six caves are open to visitors, and 25 caves are open to climbers. The altitude zones in the Tatra Mountains translate into distinct environmental variations corresponding to changes in altitude. These zones include the lower montane forest (up to ~ 1250 m a.s.l.), the subalpine zone (up to ~ 1550 m a.s.l.), the alpine zone (up to ~ 1850 m a.s.l.), and the subnival zone above 1850 m a.s.l. When documenting incidents, two ranges are included: up to 1500 m above sea level and above 1500 m above sea level.

The Tatra Mountain Rescue Service is the only entity authorized to provide mountain rescue services in an area of ​​582 km2, including the TPN and the surrounding mountain ranges (Fig. 1). TOPR provides 24-hour on-call service to bring immediate assistance to victims upon receiving a report of an accident in the area it serves. Rescue missions and the provision of qualified first aid may be undertaken only by appropriately qualified mountain rescuers: approximately 240 volunteers and 40 to 50 professional rescuers. Medical services are provided by paramedics and trained mountain doctors. TOPR cooperates with the national emergency medical system (PRM) and is entitled to request assistance from other entities during rescue missions. TOPR operates a rescue helicopter and a fleet of off-road vehicles, including those adapted for transporting victims.

Data source

Since 2015, rescue mission reports have been automatically entered into the Rescue Operations Register (eTOPR). A rescue mission is defined as a rescue response triggered by a callout. Definitions of the different categories of rescue response have been established in Poland for reporting, workload assessment, risk assessment, and benchmarking (Supplement). Individual reports contain the following data for one rescued person within a mission: demographic data (personal data, citizenship, age, gender); time of the notification of the incident; time of occurrence; exact location of the incident; altitude above sea level (above and below 1500 m above sea level); type of incident (hiking, skiing, ski touring, cycling, mountaineering, caving, canyoning, hang gliding/paragliding, forestry work, suicide attempt, searching for the missing, cooperation with the PRM); meteorological conditions (clear weather, sudden change in weather, snowfall, fog, rain, strong wind); type of incident (injury, illness); circumstances of the incident (fall on the same level, fall from height, being buried by an avalanche, being hit by a falling rock, being struck by lightning, the impact of low ambient temperature, the impact of high ambient temperature, loss of itinerary, lack of skills, lack of appropriate equipment); type of injury (contusion, joint sprain, bone fracture, frostbite, hypothermia, suffocation); location of the injury (head and neck, chest, abdomen, pelvis, spine, upper limb, lower limb); type of transport used (helicopter, snowmobile, quad bike, off-road vehicle, cable car, rescue toboggan); severity of injury or illness on the NACA score; medical treatment (cardiopulmonary resuscitation, haemorrhage control, wound dressing, immobilization, protection against hypothermia, body cooling, oxygen therapy, psychological support, initial medical triage, evacuation from the scene). The reports are supplemented by descriptive medical reports, which are created by medical personnel (paramedics or physicians). All the data were extracted, anonymized, and coded by members of the research team.

Injury or illness severity was classified via the NACA score. The NACA score is an 8-point scale (0–7) ranging from no injury or disease (NACA score = 0) to lethal conditions (NACA score = 7). A score of ≥ 3 is defined as requiring medical evaluation, and scores of 4–6 correspond to serious injury or disease. A score of 6 corresponds to a victim whose vital signs were present but who required resuscitation measures before reaching the hospital. A score of 7 corresponds to a victim who was found dead (no vital signs) at the scene [13, 20, 21]. The NACA score was determined on the basis of the most severe clinical condition occurring during the rescue mission. Missing data were not imputed.

The primary search included all reports on rescue response triggered by a callout between January 1, 2015, and December 30, 2024. The exclusion criteria included rescue missions conducted in the Slovak Tatra Mountains, missions without an assigned NACA grading, and missions in which CPR was not performed on deceased victims.

The total population at risk of incidence was determined on the basis of the number of entry tickets that each person entering the Tatra National Park (TPN) area must purchase. The minority who are exempt from this requirement are permanent residents of the surrounding municipalities and employees of the few companies operating in the area. Therefore, the number of tickets sold provides a close approximation of the total number of visitors and was therefore used as the denominator for risk calculations.

Data analysis

Continuous variables are presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), and categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages. Differences between groups of continuous variables were evaluated via the Mann–Whitney U test, whereas comparisons across multiple NACA categories were performed via the Kruskal–Wallis test. Categorical variables were compared via the χ² test. Correlations between continuous variables were assessed via Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

To assess seasonal differences in the rescue workload per visitor, we compared the proportion of rescue interventions per admission ticket between winter and the rest of the year via a two-proportion z test. For this analysis, “winter” was defined a priori as the period from December through April, corresponding to the main snow and avalanche season in the Tatra Mountains. The absolute risk increase (ARI) and the number needed to harm (NNH) were used to quantify the difference in risk between the winter (w) and non-winter (nw) periods. Results

Epidemiology

Among the 8,908 rescue missions, 9,695 individual victims were recorded. After the exclusion of 45 individuals who were declared dead without a resuscitation attempt and individuals who were rescued outside of the administrative borders of Poland (n = 174), 9,477 individuals were included in the main analysis (Fig. 1). The median age was 36 years (IQR 25–48); 47% were women, and 10% (n = 998) of those rescued were minors. Polish citizens were most frequently assisted (8,718 individuals, 90%). Mechanism and location of accidents

The locations of the callouts are presented in Fig. 3. For 9,470 interventions, the type of sporting activity associated with the incident was recorded, 83% of which occurred during hiking. Mountaineering and climbing, biking, caving, and suicide attempts accounted for 4%, 3%, 0.6%, and 0.5% of the activities, respectively. During the winter season, 25% of the incidents were skiing accidents (Fig. 4).

The most common causes of accidents were injuries caused by falling on the same level and falling from a height, accounting for 41% and 7%, respectively. Injuries most frequently involved the lower limbs (85%) and the head (18%) and least frequently involved the abdominal and pelvic areas (3 and 2%, respectively).

Illnesses accounted for 19% of the rescue missions. Resuscitation efforts were initiated for 70 individuals (0.7%). A detailed analysis of cases of sudden nontraumatic cardiac arrest has been presented elsewhere [22].

A lack of skills, lack of adequate equipment, or loss of planned route was found in over 40% of rescued individuals. Rescue missions were often hampered by weather conditions, with rainfall and fog being the most common (15% and 13%, respectively).

The median duration of rescue missions was 1.75 h (1.25–2.75) and differed significantly between the categories of rescue response (p < 0.0001): 2.0 h (1.25–5.0) for Category 1, 1.5 h (1.25–2.25) for Category 2, and 1.0 h (0.5–1.5) for Category 3. A total of 38% of individuals were evacuated by helicopter, with the highest number (40%) occurring in May and June.

More than half of the rescue missions were carried out at altitudes above 1,500 m above sea level. These missions were characterized by longer rescue times (2.0 [1.25–4.0] vs. 1.5 [1.25–2.0] hours, p < 0.0001) and required more rescuers (4 [0–5] vs. 3 [2–4], p < 0.0001) than those below 1,500 m.

Severity of injury/ilness

The median NACA score was 2 (IQR, 0–3; n = 5809). 39% of individuals (n = 3669) were classified as having a NACA score ≥ 3, and death (i.e., NACA score = 7) was the outcome of 2% of the missions (Table 1; Figs. 2 and 5). Among the helicopter missions, 48% were conducted with a NACA score of 3 or above (Fig. 6).

The proportion of severe injuries (NACA ≥ 4) was significantly greater in the winter than in the other months (6.3% vs. 4.7%; p = 0.001). The observed absolute risk increase of 1.65% corresponded to an NNH of 61, meaning that approximately 61 additional winter interventions would result in one extra case with an NACA ≥ 4. When adjusted for the number of visitors, the risk of sustaining a severe injury remained nearly twofold greater in the winter than in the rest of the year (0.002% vs. 0.001%), with an absolute risk increase of 0.001%, corresponding to an NNH of approximately 97,000 winter visitors per additional severe-case rescue.

Individuals rescued above 1,500 m had lower NACA scores (1 [0–3] vs. 2 [1–3], p < 0.0001). They were also younger (35 [26–45] vs. 38 [24–54] years, p < 0.0001) and more often female (52.9% vs. 47.1%, p < 0.0001). The proportion of accidents occurring above 1,500 m was significantly greater in the winter than in the May–November period (59.7% vs. 53.7%; p ≤ 0.001). This corresponds to an absolute risk increase of 5.97% and an NNH of approximately 17, meaning that during winter conditions, approximately 17 additional rescue missions are associated with one extra incident occurring above 1,500 m.

The duration of missions differed significantly across NACA categories (p < 0.0001). It was longest in NACA 0 (2.25 [1.25–4.5] hours), shortest in NACA 1 and 2 (1.5 [1–2] hours for both), and remained within approximately 1.5–2 h for NACA 3–6 (1.5 [1.25–2.25] for NACA 3, 1.5 [1–2] for NACA 4, 2.0 [1.25–2.88] for NACA 5, and 1.75 [1.5–2.5] hours for NACA 6). The median number of rescuers dispatched per incident was 3 (IQR 2–5). The overall number of rescuers correlated with the victim’s NACA score (R = 0.35 for the entire cohort, p < 0.001; R = 0.30 for patients with a NACA ≥ 3). For rescues involving victims with an NACA of 0–2, the median number of rescuers was lower than that for those with an NACA ≥ 3 (2 [1–4] vs. 4 [3–5], p < 0.0001). Among victims with NACA ≥ 3, the number of rescuers differed significantly across NACA categories (K–W p < 0.0001), with 4 [2–5] for NACA 3, 5 [4–7] for NACA 4, 5 [5–7] for NACA 5, and 5 [5–7] for NACA 7.

On the basis of the 9,477 individuals recorded during the study period, the overall incidence of rescue missions was 23.9 per 100,000 visitors. The annual incidence of rescue missions ranged from 19.7 to 28.2 per 100,000 visitors between 2015 and 2024, with the highest rate observed in 2024 and the lowest in 2016. The proportion of rescue missions per visitor was significantly greater in the winter than in all the other months combined (0.032% vs. 0.022%, p < 0.0001). The ARI for winter versus the rest of the year was 0.0107%, corresponding to an NNH of approximately 9,358 visitors (95% CI: 8,305–10,730), indicating that under winter conditions, approximately 9,358 additional visitors are required for one extra mountain rescue intervention compared with other months.

Discussion

Our study aimed to describe the epidemiology of mountain rescue missions in the Polish Tatra Mountains and to examine the relationship between injury or illness severity and operational rescue response. We observed that the incidence of rescue missions varied across seasons and was closely related to visitor volume. Hiking accounted for the majority of incidents, whereas severe cases occurred more frequently during winter activities. Higher NACA scores were associated with increased deployment of the helicopter and larger rescue teams in rescue missions. These findings suggest that grading the severity of the clinical condition may be valuable for planning, organizing, and seasonal preparedness in mountain rescue systems. The major strength of our study is the use of visitor statistics as a population denominator, allowing the rescue incidence to be estimated per visitor exposure rather than per population size.

The profile of victims in terms of age, sex, and activity, as well as the bimodal distribution of rescue interventions resulting from seasonal peaks in mountain activity, is similar to that reported in other mountain studies [2, 5, 7, 10, 11, 17, 23–26]. Few previous studies have used the NACA score to grade the severity of injury and illness, with most studies focusing strictly on helicopter rescue [3, 16–19, 27]. In a study on incidents in British Columbia (Canada), the median NACA score was 2, and fewer than half of the medical calls were classified as having a NACA score of ≥ 3, which is consistent with the results of our study [17]. In our study, however, the NACA score was determined prospectively, providing a basis for real-time decision-making. However, the subjectivity of assessment must be considered with possible variability between rescuers who may undercode or upcode in the operational documentation.

Interestingly, a considerable proportion of complex rescue missions involved individuals with no or only minor injuries or illnesses. In several mountain epidemiological studies, a trend toward the activation of rescuers in the case of mountaineers with overall low severity has been observed [2, 7, 10, 19, 24, 28]. These are emergencies in which mountaineers are unable to complete their expedition alone owing to exhaustion, sudden weather changes, loss of itinerary, technical problems such as rope jams during the descent, or equipment problems such as a lost or damaged piece of equipment. Being stranded may be a reason for up to 42% of ground rescue missions and 30% of helicopter missions in Alpine countries [10, 19]. Such rescue missions may be technically difficult and pose a real threat to rescuers. These missions can be viewed as preventative in nature, as early evacuation may reduce the risk of deterioration due to environmental exposure or secondary accidents during self-rescue attempts. While this strategy ensures the safety of stranded victims, the increasing number of NACA score 0–2 cases raises important questions regarding resource allocation, cost-effectiveness, and environmental impact. This strategy is easier to implement in a smaller operational area (such as in the Tatra Mountains), but it is risky if the number of available helicopters is limited. When planning preventive missions, every effort should be made to maintain access to remote areas and maintain capacity for time-critical rescue missions.

Other mountain rescue studies have shown that such preventive missions often take place at relatively high altitudes (i.e., > 2,500 m above sea level) [19]. Surprisingly, we observed a trend toward lower NACA scores above 1,500 m a.s.l. This may have been due to the tendency for unprepared hikers to take excessive risks entering higher terrain where technical climbing equipment may be needed. A similar trend was observed in a study conducted in Catalonia [7].

The fatality rate (NACA score of 7) of search and rescue missions ranges from 4.5% to 31% worldwide [2, 3, 10, 11, 17, 24, 25]. In our study, victim deaths occurred in 2% of missions. The relatively low mortality rate observed in our cohort may reflect the efficient organization but also case mix, geography, or registry inclusion criteria.

In larger operational areas, the duration of interventions is longer, averaging 5–12 h [2, 17, 24], with a similar helicopter deployment rate of approximately 30% [3, 17, 24, 25]. Similarly, the number of rescuers involved in interventions in larger areas of operation is naturally greater and can reach 5 or even 12 per mission [2, 24]. In our study, the median number of rescuers per intervention was 3, which was closely correlated with the severity of the victims’ condition. However, missions involving more than 4 rescuers for individuals without injury or illness are noteworthy.

Helicopter deployment was more frequent in patients with higher NACA scores, suggesting that clinical severity was associated with transport decisions. In an observational study of consecutive missions from nine HEMS bases from four countries (Slovenia, Austria, Switzerland and Spain), the average NACA score of victims was 3.3 [16]. As many as 81% of the evacuations were performed for victims with a NACA score of 3 or more, and 32% had NACA scores of 4 or more. In 10% of the cases, the helicopter was dispatched to victims with a NACA score of 0–2. Indications for helicopter use in mountain rescue include an NACA score of 3 or higher; the need for ALS procedures; injuries, illnesses, and symptoms that may deteriorate rapidly or during ground transport; and any other injuries or illnesses for which helicopter transport has a clear advantage over ground transport in the opinion of on-scene medical personnel [15]. In our study, the helicopter deployment rate for casualties with mild illness and injury (NACA 0–2) was similar to that for missions with a NACA ≥ 3. This does not mean, however, that the helicopter might be overused; in many cases, the missions were preventative, as recommended [15]. In a Swiss study on the indications and outcomes of patients rescued by helicopters from mountain huts, two-thirds of missions involved NACA 1–2 [18]. Notably, the helicopter deployment rate for fatal accidents (NACA 7) in the Tatra Mountains was half of the rate reported in other studies [16].

The epidemiology of mountain accidents may differ from the usual patterns seen in illnesses and injuries. This is because operational factors such as terrain, accessibility, and evacuation logistics can play key roles. In this context, prehospital severity grading may serve not only as a clinical descriptor but also as a proxy for operational complexity and resource utilization. Our observations support the idea that combining epidemiological monitoring with severity grading may assist rescue organizations in planning staffing levels, helicopter readiness, seasonal deployment strategies, training programs, and preventive strategies in mountain environments.

Limitations

Systematic linkage to hospital reports or mortality registries was not available; therefore, in-hospital outcomes, long-term mortality, and the predictive validity of the NACA score could not be assessed. The dataset included only individuals for whom the TOPR rescue intervention was activated. Individuals who self-evacuated were not captured in the registry. Although NACA scores were assigned prospectively during routine operational practice, the interrater reliability between rescuers could not be evaluated. Some variability in severity classification is therefore possible. The Polish Tatra Mountains represent a moderate-altitude alpine environment with specific tourism patterns; therefore, the study findings may not be generalizable to other mountain rescue systems.

Conclusions

In this ten-year analysis of mountain rescue missions in the Polish Tatra Mountains, the rescue incidence clearly varied seasonally and was closely related to visitor volume. Severe cases occurred more frequently during winter activities, and higher NACA scores were associated with increased helicopter use and greater rescue team response. These findings suggest that integrating epidemiological monitoring with clinical severity assessment, such as the NACA score, may support dispatch planning, rescue allocation, and preparedness in mountain rescue systems. Information campaigns and training for hikers and mountaineers, who are often inadequately trained and equipped, are highly recommended.
