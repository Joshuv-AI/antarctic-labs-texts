Integral equation methods for scattering by general compact obstacles: wavenumber-explicit estimates

C_{k,R}(\Omega):=\|\chi_{R}R(k;\Omega)\chi_{R}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)\to L^{2}(\Omega)}.

(2.9)

Our main bound on
A_{k,k}^{-1}
is a bound in terms of these resolvent norms,
C_{k}(\Omega_{-})
and
C_{k,R}(\Omega)
. We use here and subsequently the notation

\Sigma(\Omega_{-}):=\{k>0:k^{2}\in\sigma(-\Delta_{D}(\Omega_{-})\}.

(2.10)

Theorem 2.3
.

Given any
k_{0}>0
and
R>R_{\Gamma}
there exists
C>0
such that

\|A_{k,k}^{-1}\|_{k}\leq Ck^{2}\left(C_{k,R}(\Omega)+C_{k}(\Omega_{-})\right),\hskip 20.00003ptk\in[k_{0},\infty)\setminus\Sigma(\Omega_{-}),

in particular

\|A_{k,k}^{-1}\|_{k}\leq Ck^{2}C_{k,R}(\Omega),\hskip 20.00003ptk\in[k_{0},\infty),

if
\Gamma=O
so that
\Omega_{-}=\emptyset
.

The above result is useful because much is known about the growth of
C_{k,R}(\Omega)
with

and how that depends on the geometry of
O
, in particular whether or not
O
is trapping and, if
O
is trapping, the strength of that trapping. For an overview see, e.g.,
[

, §1.1, Table 6.1]
,
[

, §1.1]
, or
[

, §1.2]
; as usual (see
[

]
for more detail) we say that

is nontrapping if, for some

, all billiard trajectories starting in

escape from

after some uniform time, and say that

is trapping otherwise. In particular, as shown in
[

(and see
[

for the case

and

such that

C_{k,R}(\Omega)\leq C_{R}k^{-1},\hskip 20.00003ptk\geq k_{0},

(2.11)

if the compact set
O
is star-shaped in the following sense. (The result in
[

builds on classical results of Morawetz for the case where

Definition 2.4

(Star-shaped)
.

We say that a set

if there exists

such that the line segment

is contained in

.

We note that the bound (

) is optimal, in the sense that a simple quasi-mode construction (see, e.g., the discussion before Lemma 3.10 in

) shows that, for every compact obstacle

,
R>R_{\Gamma}
, and
k_{0}>0
, there exists

such that

C_{k,R}(\Omega)\geq C_{R}^{\prime}k^{-1},\hskip 20.00003ptk\geq k_{0}.

(2.12)

Combining (

) with Theorem
2.3
and Proposition

, we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 2.5
.

If
\Gamma=O
and

such that

\|A_{k,k}^{-1}\|_{k}\leq Ck,\hskip 10.00002pt\mathrm{cond}_{k}(A_{k,k})\leq Ck^{2},\hskip 20.00003ptk\in[k_{0},\infty).

A variety of other bounds on

Combining Theorem

) and Proposition

Corollary 2.6
.

If

in

arbitrarily fast increase, as

.

Proposition 2.7
.

Suppose that

Then there exists a compact obstacle

. The first estimate is that, if

and

\displaystyle\|R(k;\Omega_{-})\|_{L^{2}(\Omega_{-})\to L^{2}(\Omega_{-})}

\displaystyle=C_{k}(\Omega_{-})=\left(\mathrm{dist}(k^{2},\sigma(-\Delta_{D}(\Omega_{-}))\right)^{-1}

Corollary 2.8
.

For every

,

and

\|A_{k,k}^{-1}\|_{k}\leq Ck^{2n+2+\delta},\hskip 10.00002pt\mathrm{cond}_{k}(A_{k,k})\leq Ck^{2n+3+\delta},\hskip 20.00003ptk\in[k_{0},\infty)\setminus E.

2.2.
Significance and relationship to previous work

The integral equations that appear in the studies cited in §
1
, and our own IE (
1.7
), take the form

B_{k}\phi=g,

(2.18)

where
B_{k}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}^{*}
is a continuous mapping from a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
to its dual space
\mathcal{H}^{*}
. In each case
\mathcal{H}
is a space of functions defined on
\Gamma
, a compact set satisfying (
1.6
), with
\Gamma:=\partial\Omega
in the usual case that the IE is a BIE. In (
2.18
),
g\in\mathcal{H}^{*}
is some known data and
\phi
is the solution to be determined.

One motivation for estimating
\|B_{k}\|
and
\|B_{k}^{-1}\|
– the focus of this paper, and of many of the papers cited in §
1
– is that this leads to estimates for the condition number
\mathrm{cond}(B_{k}):=\|B_{k}\|\|B_{k}^{-1}\|
, and this, by standard estimates for linear operators (e.g.,
[
41
, §2.7.2]
) leads to estimates for the perturbation in
\phi
induced by perturbations in
g
or the operator
B_{k}
, this relevant, for example, to the study of uncertainty quantification (e.g.,
[
52
,
62
]
). For example, in the simplest case that
g
is replaced by perturbed data
g^{\delta}
in (
2.18
), it is elementary that the corresponding perturbed solution
\phi^{\delta}
satisfies

\frac{\|\phi-\phi^{\delta}\|_{\mathcal{H}}}{\|\phi\|_{\mathcal{H}}}\leq\mathrm{cond}(B_{k})\frac{\|g-g^{\delta}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{*}}}{\|g\|_{\mathcal{H}^{*}}}.

To appreciate the relevance of estimates for
\mathrm{cond}(B_{k})
to the numerical analysis of (
2.18
), assume
k>0
is such that
B_{k}
is invertible and consider solution of (
2.18
) by a least squares method in which one seeks an approximation
\phi_{N}\in\mathcal{H}_{N}
from some
N
-dimensional subspace of
\mathcal{H}
, and chooses
\phi_{N}
so as to minimise
\|B_{k}\phi_{N}-g\|_{\mathcal{H}^{*}}
. Equivalently (e.g.,
[
56
, §3.2.1]
), one chooses
\phi_{N}\in\mathcal{H}_{N}
so as to solve the variational problem

b_{k}(\phi_{N},\psi_{N})=(g,B_{k}\psi_{N})_{\mathcal{H}^{*}},\hskip 20.00003pt\forall\psi_{N}\in\mathcal{H}_{N},

(2.19)

where
(\cdot,\cdot)_{\mathcal{H}^{*}}
is the inner product on
\mathcal{H}^{*}
and
b_{k}(\cdot,\cdot)
is the least squares sesquilinear form given by

b_{k}(\chi,\psi):=(B_{k}\chi,B_{k}\psi)_{\mathcal{H}^{*}},\hskip 20.00003pt\chi,\psi\in\mathcal{H}.

Trivially,
b_{k}(\cdot,\cdot)
is symmetric (i.e.,
b_{k}(\chi,\psi)=\overline{b_{k}(\psi,\chi)}
, for
\chi,\psi\in\mathcal{H}
), and is continuous and coercive with continuity and coercivity constants
\|B_{k}\|^{2}
and
\|B_{k}^{-1}\|^{-2}
, i.e.

|b_{k}(\chi,\psi)|\leq\|B_{k}\|^{2}\|\chi\|_{\mathcal{H}}\|\psi\|_{\mathcal{H}},\hskip 10.00002pt|b_{k}(\psi,\psi)|\geq\|B_{k}^{-1}\|^{-2}\|\psi\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\hskip 20.00003pt\forall\chi,\psi\in\mathcal{H}.

Thus, by a standard refinement of Céa’s lemma in the symmetric case (e.g.,
[
10
, (2.8.5), Rem. 2]
),

\|\phi-\phi_{N}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq\mathrm{cond}(B_{k})e_{N}(\phi),\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{where}\hskip 10.00002pte_{N}(\phi):=\min_{\psi_{N}\in\mathcal{H}_{N}}\|\phi-\psi_{N}\|_{\mathcal{H}}

is the error in best approximation of
\phi
from the subspace
\mathcal{H}_{N}
.

To ensure that the error remains small as
k
increases it is sufficient to increase
N
sufficiently rapidly (and usually one also chooses
\mathcal{H}_{N}
to depend on
k
) to ensure that
e_{N}(\phi)
decreases at least as fast as
(\mathrm{cond}(B_{k}))^{-1}
. Thus, understanding how
\mathrm{cond}(B_{k})
grows with
k
is key to the design of a discretisation space
\mathcal{H}_{N}
that will ensure an accurate solution.

In the 2D case (
n=2
) it is known, for certain geometries of
O
and choices of
\mathcal{H}
(e.g.,
[
49
,
22
,
48
]
), how to choose
\mathcal{H}_{N}
dependent on
k
so that, for every
k_{0}>0
and
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
there exists
c,C,m>0
such that

e_{N}(\phi)\leq Ck^{m}\mathrm{e}^{-cN^{1/2}},\hskip 20.00003ptk\geq k_{0},\hskip 10.00002ptN\geq N_{0}.

If such an estimate holds, and as long as
\mathrm{cond}(B_{k})
increases at most at a polynomial rate with
k
(as implied for
\mathrm{cond}_{k}(A_{k,k})
, outside some set
E
of small measure, by Proposition
2.1
and Corollary
2.8
), it is enough for
N
to increase in proportion to
\log^{2}k
, to ensure, for any given
\epsilon>0
, that
\|\phi-\phi_{N}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq\epsilon
for
k\geq k_{0}
. See Löhndorf and Melenk
[
63
]
and the discussions in
[
33
,
59
]
for other, related motivations for establishing a growth of
\mathrm{cond}(B_{k})
with
k
that is at most polynomial.

Note that understanding conditioning, and its dependence on
k
and the geometry of
O
, is also important for understanding the convergence of the iterative solvers that are needed to solve the large dense linear systems that typically arise in the numerical solutions of BIE formulations (that all take the form (
2.18
)) for obstacle scattering problems (see, e.g.,
[
43
,
3
,
64
]
,
[
32
, Thm. 4.11]
). (The relevant solvers are the conjugate gradient method and MINRES in the case (as for discretisations of (
2.19
)) that the matrix is symmetric and positive definite, or GMRES for general complex matrices; see
[
43
]
for specifications of these algorithms.)

The above provides motivation for the results that we have described in §
2.1
. Regarding the novelty of our results compared to previous studies of wavenumber dependence of norms and condition numbers of integral operators, and the relationship of this paper to previous work, we make the following remarks.

Remark 2.9

(
1st vs. 2nd kind IEs)
.

This paper is concerned with the
k
-dependence of conditioning of operators arising in first kind IEs. The vast majority of the papers cited in §
1
deal with second kind BIE formulations of either the Dirichlet
[
58
,
57
,
1
,
20
,
4
,
36
,
27
,
18
,
77
,
8
,
19
,
66
,
75
,
47
,
78
,
5
,
40
,
33
,
59
]
or the Neumann
[
9
,
39
]
scattering problem. In each of these papers the obstacle
O
is Lipschitz or smoother, and in each case the integral operator acts on the relatively simple Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}=L^{2}(\Gamma)
, where
\Gamma=\partial\Omega
.

Importantly, there are theoretical and computational reasons for preferring first kind IEs, as noted, for example, in
[
12
]
, namely that the associated operators are compact perturbations of coercive operators, as we exhibit, even for general compact obstacles, in Proposition
4.4
below.
Thus, by a standard generalisation of Céa’s lemma (e.g.,
[
31
, Thm. 2.3]
), all Galerkin methods based on asymptotically dense sequences of subspaces are convergent. By contrast, the operators in the standard second kind IEs are not compact perturbations of coercive operators for general Lipschitz domains, or even for all polyhedra in 3D
[
31
]
, so that Galerkin methods, in particular based on standard boundary element approximation spaces, need not be convergent
[
31
, Thm. 1.4]
.

Remark 2.10

(
Previous work on 1st kind IEs)
.

The only previous
k
-explicit bounds on condition numbers for first kind IEs are those of Chandler-Wilde and Hewett
[
21
]
, building on work of Ha Duong
[
44
,
45
]
.
The paper
[

]
considers first kind BIEs in fractional order Sobolev spaces on
\Gamma=O
for both the Dirichlet and Neumann problems (for the Dirichlet case the BIO is the operator
S_{k}
of (
2.22
)), but for the special case that
O
is a flat screen, meaning that
O
is contained in some hyperplane in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In that case the BIOs are convolution operators so that Fourier analysis methods can be used to estimate norms. (A similar, but only partly rigorous, Fourier analysis is carried out for a modified version of the single-layer BIO
S_{k}
in the case that

[

]
.)

Remark 2.11

(
Generality of the geometry in previous results)
.

In contrast to all previous studies of
k
-dependence, the obstacle
O
in this paper can be any compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For example,
O
might be a fractal, with fractal dimension
d
taking any value in
[0,n]
, see §

for more detail (though the case

is uninteresting as the scattered field is zero; see
[

, Rem. 3.5]
). All previous studies of
k
-dependence assume that
\Omega
is Lipschitz or smoother, with the IE a BIE posed on

which has integer dimension

, or assume
[

,

,

]
that the integral equation is posed on

, where

is the closure of some relatively open subset of a hyperplane in

(so that, again,

has dimension

).

Remark 2.12

(
Failure of invertibility for all

)
.

This is the first paper to derive wavenumber-explicit estimates for the inverse of an integral operator

in a case where

is not invertible for all

. This introduces a new element; clearly
\|B_{k}^{-1}\|
and

must blow up at values of

Remark 2.13

(
Results similar to §
2.1
for 2nd kind BIEs)
.

The BIO in the standard 2nd kind BIE on

for the sound-soft scattering problem when

[

). Essentially the same IE applies when

Our bounds on the norms of

and

(denoted

and the norm of its inverse by

\|S_{k}^{-1}\|\leq Ck^{2}\|A_{k,k}^{-1}\|_{k},\hskip 20.00003ptk\in[k_{0},\infty)\setminus\Sigma(\Omega_{-}).

Clearly, estimates for
\|S_{k}^{-1}\|
that are explicit in their dependence on

.

In the case that

and

and

with the results of §

Corollary 2.16
.

Suppose that

is a Lipschitz screen and

. Then, for every

there exists

such that

\|S_{k}\|\leq c\|A_{k,k}\|_{k}\leq Ck\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{and}\hskip 10.00002pt\|S_{k}^{-1}\|\leq Ck^{2}\|A_{k,k}^{-1}\|_{k},\hskip 20.00003ptk\geq k_{0}.

Further,
S_{k}
is invertible for all

and, for every

,

, and
k_{0}>0
, there exists

\|S_{k}^{-1}\|\leq C^{\prime}k^{2n+4+\delta},\hskip 20.00003ptk\in[k_{0},\infty)\setminus E.

(2.23)

In the case that

and thus

, a

(2.24)

We also, as a corollary of Propositions

We make the following additional remarks regarding the novelty of the above results and their relationship to previous work.

Remark 2.18

(
Bounds on
\|S_{k}\|
)
.

Our bound on
S_{k}
in Corollary
2.15
improves, in higher dimensions and for non-smooth boundaries, on the bounds that have been obtained by Graham et al
[
42
]
and by Galkowski and Spence
[
40
]
. The bound of
[
42
, Thm. 1.6 (i)]
, which applies, as does our result, when
\Gamma
is the boundary of a Lipschitz domain, is that, for some constant
C>0
dependent on
k_{0}
,
\|S_{k}\|\leq Ck^{(n-1)/2}
for
k\geq k_{0}
. This is sharper than our bound for dimension
n=2
, and less sharp for
n>3
. The bounds of
[
40
, Thm. 1.5]
are that, for
k\geq k_{0}
,
\|S_{k}\|\leq Ck^{1/2}\log(1+k)
if
\Gamma
is piecewise smooth,
\|S_{k}\|\leq Ck^{1/3}\log(1+k)
if
\Gamma
is piecewise curved
(as defined in
[
40
]
). These bounds are shown in
[
40
]
to be sharp, to within the
\log
factors, for their respective geometry classes, but the proofs rely on the (piecewise) smoothness of the geometry.

The bound in Corollary
2.16
that
\|S_{k}\|\leq Ck
, for
k\geq k_{0}
, can be deduced from the corresponding bound in Corollary
2.15
. Similarly, bounds on
S_{k}:\widetilde{H}^{-1/2}(U)\to H^{1/2}(U)
can be deduced from the results in
[
42
,
40
]
; for example, for
k\geq k_{0}
,
\|S_{k}\|\leq Ck^{(n-1)/2}
and, if
\Gamma
is piecewise smooth,
\|S_{k}\|\leq Ck^{1/2}\log(1+k)
. In the special case that
U
(and so
\Gamma=O
) is contained in a hyperplane, it is shown in
[
21
]
that
\|S_{k}\|\leq Ck^{1/2}
, and that this estimate is sharp.

Remark 2.19

(
Bounds on
\|S_{k}^{-1}\|
)
.

Our bounds on
\|S_{k}^{-1}\|
in Corollary
2.16
are the first
k
-explicit bounds for a screen that is not flat. The only previous
k
-explicit bound for
\|S_{k}^{-1}\|
for a screen is that of
[
21
]
for the case that the screen
O
is contained in a hyperplane. The Fourier analysis in that paper
[
21
, Thm. 1.7]
– which relies on the flatness of the screen – implies, together with (
3.3
), that, for every
k_{0}>0
there exists
C>0
such that
\|S_{k}^{-1}\|\leq Ck
,
k\geq k_{0}
.

Our bounds on
\|S_{k}^{-1}\|
in Corollary
2.15
are the first
k
-explicit bounds for the case that
\Gamma
is the boundary of a Lipschitz domain. Sharper bounds for this case (sharper by a factor

) are provided in our companion paper
[

]
through additional arguments, including

[

, §3.3]
,
[

, §4.2]
). We emphasise that these methods and the associated sharper bounds do not apply in other cases, in particular do not apply when
\Gamma
is a Lipschitz screen.

3.
Function spaces

We give brief details in this section of the function space notations that we will need to state and prove our results.

3.1.
Sobolev spaces on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and on domains
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

Given a domain (i.e., a non-empty open set)
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)\subset C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
denote the set of smooth complex-valued functions which are compactly supported in
\Omega
. As usual (e.g.,
[
65
]
), let
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\supset C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
denote the Schwartz space of rapidly decreasing,
C^{\infty}
functions,
\mathcal{S}^{*}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
its dual space, the space of tempered distributions. (Throughout, to suit a Hilbert space setting, our distributions and other functionals will be anti-linear rather than linear, so that our dual spaces are spaces of anti-linear continuous functionals.)

For
u\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
let
\hat{u}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
denote the Fourier transform of
u
, choosing the normalising constant in our definition so that the mapping
u\mapsto\hat{u}
is unitary on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
; precisely,

\hat{u}(\xi):=(2\pi)^{-n/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\xi\cdot x}u(x)\,dx,\hskip 20.00003pt\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.

We extend the domain of the Fourier transform to the tempered distributions
\mathcal{S}^{*}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
in the usual way. For
s\in\mathbb{R}
, let
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
denote the standard Sobolev space of those
u\in\mathcal{S}^{*}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
whose distributional Fourier transform
\hat{u}
is locally integrable and satisfies

\|u\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}:=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\hat{u}(\xi)|^{2}(1+|\xi|^{2})^{s}\,\mathrm{d}\xi\right)^{1/2}<\infty.

(3.1)

As usual, we identify
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with
H^{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, so that
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, for
s\geq 0
, and identify
H^{-s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
with the dual space
(H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))^{*}
.

We will often equip
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, as is common in the study of
k
-dependence of operators and their norms (see, e.g., the discussion around equation (26) in
[
21
]
), with a
k
-dependent norm given by

\|u\|_{H_{k}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}:=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\hat{u}(\xi)|^{2}(k^{2}+|\xi|^{2})^{s}\,\mathrm{d}\xi\right)^{1/2},

(3.2)

and
H_{k}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
will denote the set
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
equipped with the norm (
3.2
). (
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
H^{s}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
are both Hilbert spaces equipped with the inner products implied by their respective norms.)
Clearly,
\|\cdot\|_{H_{k}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=\|\cdot\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
for
k=1
, and, for every
k>0
, the norms
\|\cdot\|_{H_{k}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
and
\|\cdot\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
are equivalent, with

\min(1,k^{-s})\|u\|_{H_{k}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\|u\|_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\max(1,k^{-s})\|u\|_{H_{k}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},\hskip 20.00003ptu\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

(3.3)

For
s\in\mathbb{R}
and
k>0
,
H_{k}^{-s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a natural realisation of the dual space of
H_{k}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
through the duality pairing

\langle u,v\rangle:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\hat{u}(\xi)\overline{\hat{v}(\xi)}\,d\xi,\hskip 20.00003ptu\in H_{k}^{-s}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),\hskip 10.00002ptv\in H_{k}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

(3.4)

that extends the inner product on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and the duality pairing on
\mathcal{S}^{*}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

We will use at one point that, for every
k>0
, an application of
[
23
, Cor. 3.2]
(as corrected in
[
25
]
), gives that
\{H_{k}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):s\in\mathbb{R}\}
is an
exact interpolation scale
in the sense of
[
23
, Rem. 3.8]
(as corrected in
[
25
]
). This means that, for
s,t\in\mathbb{R}
and
0<\theta<1
,
(H_{k}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),H_{k}^{t}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))_{[\theta]}=H_{k}^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, with equality of norms, where
r=(1-\theta)s+\theta t
and
(H_{k}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),H_{k}^{t}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))_{[\theta]}
denotes the result of complex interpolation between the indicated spaces (our notation is that of
[
7
]
); equivalently,
(H_{k}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),H_{k}^{t}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))_{[\theta]}
denotes the space
(H_{k}^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),H_{k}^{t}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))_{\theta,2}
that is the result of real interpolation with either the
K
- or the
J
-method, with an appropriate normalisation in each case (see
[
23
,
25
]
for details).

Given a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we say that
u\in H^{1}(\Omega)
if
u\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and its weak derivative
\nabla u\in(L^{2}(\Omega))^{n}
. Let
\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
denote the usual
L^{2}(\Omega)
norm. Then
H^{1}(\Omega)
is a Hilbert space when equipped with the norm
\|\cdot\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}
, defined by

\|u\|^{2}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}=\||\nabla u|\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\hskip 10.00002ptu\in H^{1}(\Omega).

Let

H_{0}^{1}(\Omega):=\text{clos}_{H^{1}(\Omega)}(C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)),

the closure of
C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
in
H^{1}(\Omega)
.

The
k
-dependent Sobolev space
H_{k}^{1}(\Omega)
,
k>0
, is defined as the set
H^{1}(\Omega)
equipped with the Hilbert space norm
\|\cdot\|_{H_{k}^{1}(\Omega)}
, defined by

\|u\|^{2}_{H_{k}^{1}(\Omega)}=\||\nabla u|\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+k^{2}\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\hskip 10.00002ptu\in H_{k}^{1}(\Omega).

Note that, in the case
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{n}
, these definitions of
H^{1}(\Omega)
and
H^{1}_{k}(\Omega)
agree, with equality of norms, with our definitions above for
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
H^{1}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
Generalising
H_{k}^{1}(\Omega)
, we will need, for
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
k>0
, at one point the space
H_{k}^{m}(\Omega)
, defined, for the case
\Omega\neq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, as the space of those
u\in L^{2}(\Omega)
whose (weak) partial derivatives of all orders
\leq m
are square integrable on
\Omega
, a Hilbert space equipped with the norm
\|\cdot\|_{H_{k}^{m}(\Omega)}
defined by

\|u\|^{2}_{H_{k}^{m}(\Omega)}:=k^{m}\sum_{0\leq|\alpha|\leq m}\|(k^{-1}\partial)^{\alpha}u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)},\hskip 20.00003ptu\in H_{k}^{m}(\Omega).

This definition coincides, for
m=1
, with our definition of
H_{k}^{1}(\Omega)
, and is consistent (to within equivalence of norms, uniformly for
k>0
) to the definition above of
H^{m}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

We will use certain spaces of compactly supported and locally integrable functions. Let
L^{2}_{\mathrm{comp}}(\Omega)
denote the space of those functions in
L^{2}(\Omega)
that have bounded support, a topological vector space (indeed an LF-space, e.g.
[
80
]
) in which a sequence
(u_{n})
is convergent if it is convergent in
L^{2}(\Omega)
and, for some bounded
V\subset\Omega
,
\mathrm{supp}(u_{n})\subset V
for each
n
. Let
L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)
denote the set of functions on
\Omega
that satisfy
u|_{V}\in L^{2}(V)
for every bounded, measurable
V\subset\Omega
, a Fréchet space with the obvious topology (e.g.,
[
84
, p. 52]
) for which a sequence
(u_{n})\subset L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)
is convergent if
(u_{n}|_{V})
is convergent in
L^{2}(V)
for every bounded
V\subset\Omega
. Let

\displaystyle H^{1,\text{loc}}(\Omega)

\displaystyle:=\{u\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega):\nabla u\in(L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega))^{n}\}

\displaystyle=\{u\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):\chi|_{\Omega}\,u\in H^{1}(\Omega),\forall\chi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\},

and let

H_{0}^{1,\text{loc}}(\Omega):=\{u\in H^{1,\text{loc}}(\Omega):\chi|_{\Omega}\,u\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega),\forall\chi\in C^{\infty}_{0}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\}.

3.2.
Subspaces of
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

We need also certain subspaces of
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. For
s\in\mathbb{R}
and every domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let

\widetilde{H}^{s}(\Omega):=\text{clos}_{H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}(C^{\infty}_{0}(\Omega)).

Importantly, if
u\in\widetilde{H}^{1}(\Omega)
, then
u=0
a.e. on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega
and
u|_{\Omega}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
. Conversely, if
u\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
and the definition of
u
is extended to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
by setting
u=0
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega
, then the extended
u\in\widetilde{H}^{1}(\Omega)
.

For any closed set
F\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we set

H^{s}_{F}:=\{u\in H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):\text{supp}(u)\subset F\},

noting that
H^{s}_{F}
is trivial, meaning that
H^{s}_{F}=\{0\}
, if
\mathrm{int}(F)
, the interior of
F
, is empty and
s
is large enough (see
[
50
]
); in particular,
H^{-1}_{F}=\{0\}
if and only if
F
has zero
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
capacity
[
50
, Thm. 3.15]
. (Note that
F
has positive
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
capacity if
\dim_{H}(F)>n-2
, but not if
\dim_{H}(F)<n-2

[
50
, Thm. 2.12]
, where
\dim_{H}(F)
denotes the Hausdorff dimension of
F
, as defined, e.g., in
[
38
]
.) The subspace
\widetilde{H}^{-s}(\Omega)^{\perp}\subset H^{-s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, where
\Omega:=\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus F
and
\perp
denotes orthogonal complement, is an isometric realisation of the dual space
(H^{s}_{F})^{*}
via the duality pairing (
3.4
) restricted to
\widetilde{H}^{-s}(\Omega)^{\perp}\times H^{s}_{F}

[
24
, Thm. 3.15]
.

More generally, arguing as in
[
24
, Thm. 3.15]
, and where
\perp_{k}
denotes orthogonal complement when
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is equipped with the norm
H^{s}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
\widetilde{H}^{-s}(\Omega)^{\perp_{k}}\subset H^{-s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is an isometric realisation of the dual space
(H^{s}_{F})^{*}
via the duality pairing (
3.4
) restricted to
\widetilde{H}^{-s}(\Omega)^{\perp_{k}}\times H^{s}_{F}
, when these spaces are equipped with the norms
H^{-s}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
H^{s}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, respectively. Precisely, for
k>0
, the mapping
\mathcal{I}:\widetilde{H}^{-s}(\Omega)^{\perp_{k}}\to(H^{s}_{F})^{*}
, defined by

\mathcal{I}u(v):=\langle u,v\rangle,\hskip 10.00002ptv\in H^{s}_{F},

is an isometric isomorphism when
\widetilde{H}^{-s}(\Omega)^{\perp_{k}}
and
H^{s}_{F}
are equipped with the norms
H^{-s}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
H^{s}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, enabling us to identify
(H^{s}_{F})^{*}=(\widetilde{H}^{-s}(\Omega))^{\perp_{k}}
, with equality of norms.

Extending this further, let
P:H^{-s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to H^{-s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be any projection operator with closed range
R(P)\subset H^{-s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and kernel
\widetilde{H}^{-s}(\Omega)
, so that the mapping
\widetilde{P}:\widetilde{H}^{-s}(\Omega)^{\perp_{k}}\to R(P)
, defined by
\widetilde{P}u:=Pu
,
u\in\widetilde{H}^{-s}(\Omega)^{\perp_{k}}
, is bijective and so an isomorphism by the Banach theorem (e.g.,
[
73
, Corollaries 2.12]
). Then we can identify
R(P)
with the dual space
(H_{F}^{s})^{*}
, through the isomorphism

R(P)\to(H_{F}^{s})^{*},\;u\mapsto\tilde{u},\mbox{ where }\tilde{u}(v):=(\mathcal{I}\widetilde{P}^{-1}u)(v)=\langle\widetilde{P}^{-1}u,v\rangle=\langle P^{+}u,v\rangle,

(3.5)

for
u\in R(P)
,
v\in H_{F}^{s}
, where
P^{+}:H^{-s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to H^{-s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is the Moore-Penrose inverse of
P
(e.g.,
[
46
, §2.1.2]
).

Note that (
3.5
) is an isometric isomorphism in the special case that
P
is orthogonal projection onto
R(P)=\widetilde{H}^{-s}(\Omega)^{\perp_{k}}
, in which case
\widetilde{P}
is the identity operator,
P^{+}=P
, and the isomorphism coincides with
\mathcal{I}
. Conversely, if the isomorphism (
3.5
) is isometric then, since
\mathcal{I}
is isometric, also
\widetilde{P}
is isometric, and so (e.g.,
[
73
, Theorem 12.13]
) unitary, so that, if
u\in\widetilde{H}^{-s}(\Omega)^{\perp_{k}}
, then, where
(\cdot,\cdot)_{k}
denotes the inner product on
H^{-s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
compatible with the norm
\|\cdot\|_{H_{k}^{-s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
,

\|u-Pu\|^{2}_{H_{k}^{-s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}=(u-Pu,u-Pu)_{k}=(P(u-Pu),P(u-Pu))_{k}=0,

since
P
is a projection. Thus
\widetilde{P}
is the identity operator,
R(P)=\widetilde{H}^{-s}(\Omega)^{\perp_{k}}
, and
P
is orthogonal projection onto
\widetilde{H}^{-s}(\Omega)^{\perp_{k}}
.

3.3.
Boundary Sobolev spaces

We will need in §
7
also Sobolev spaces defined on the boundaries of bounded Lipschitz domains. Suppose that
\Omega^{*}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a bounded Lipschitz domain with boundary
\Gamma=\partial\Omega^{*}
.
For
|s|\leq 1
we can define boundary Sobolev spaces
H^{s}(\Gamma)
on
\Gamma
in one of several standard equivalent ways (which give the same space, with equivalent norms). Concretely, we define
H^{s}(\Gamma)
, for
|s|\leq 1
, as in
[
19
, §A.3]
(or see
[
65
, pp. 98–99]
), so that
H^{s}(\Gamma)
is a Hilbert space for
|s|\leq 1
,
H^{s}(\Gamma)\subset L^{2}(\Gamma)
,
0\leq s\leq 1
, indeed
H^{0}(\Gamma)=L^{2}(\Gamma)
with equivalence of norms,
H^{s}(\Gamma)
is continuously and densely embedded in
H^{t}(\Gamma)
, for
-1\leq t<s\leq 1
. and
H^{-s}(\Gamma)=(H^{s}(\Gamma))^{*}
,
|s|\leq 1
, with duality pairing
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\Gamma}
that extends the inner product
(\cdot,\cdot)_{L^{2}(\Gamma)}
.

We will need also subspaces of
H^{s}(\Gamma)
, analogous to the subspaces of
H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
introduced in §
3.2
. Suppose that
\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2}\subset\Gamma
are disjoint, non-empty, relatively open subsets of
\Gamma
, with
\Pi
as their common boundary in
\Gamma
, which are such that
(\Gamma_{1},\Pi,\Gamma_{2})
is a Lipschitz dissection of
\Gamma
in the sense of
[
65
, p. 99]
. (Roughly speaking, this means that
\Gamma_{1}
is a subset of
\Gamma
with a Lipschitz boundary
\Pi
, and we will refer to
\Gamma_{1}
as a
Lipschitz subset
of
\Gamma
, or as a
Lipschitz screen
.) Let
C_{0}^{\infty}(\Gamma):=\{u|_{\Gamma}:u\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\}
,
C_{0}^{\infty}(\Gamma_{1}):=\{\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Gamma):\mathrm{supp}(\phi)\subset\Gamma_{1}\}
and, for
|s|\leq 1
,

\widetilde{H}^{s}(\Gamma_{1}):=\text{clos}_{H^{s}(\Gamma)}(C^{\infty}_{0}(\Gamma_{1})).

For
|s|\leq 1
, let
H^{s}(\Gamma_{1}):=\{\phi|_{\Gamma_{1}}:\phi\in H^{s}(\Gamma)\}
, which we equip with the quotient norm
\|\cdot\|_{H^{s}(\Gamma_{1})}
, defined by

\|\psi\|_{H^{s}(\Gamma_{1})}:=\inf_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\phi\in H^{s}(\Gamma)}}{{\phi|_{\Gamma_{1}}=\psi}}}\|\phi\|_{H^{s}(\Gamma)},\hskip 20.00003pt\psi\in H^{s}(\Gamma_{1}).

H^{s}(\Gamma_{1})
is a natural realisation of
(\widetilde{H}^{-s}(\Gamma_{1}))^{*}
, with duality pairing extending the
L^{2}(\Gamma_{1})
inner product (see
[

, p. 99, Theorem 3.30]
). Our notation

is consistent with our notation in §
3.1
for
H^{1}(\Omega)
by
[

, p. 99, Theorem 3.30]
.

4.
The IE formulation of Caetano et al, and its extension to higher dimensions

In this section we recall the IE formulation of
[
15
, §3(a)]
and its justification. As we do this we make non-trivial extensions of the results of
[
15
]
. In particular, we provide additional argument that clarifies that the results apply in all space dimensions
n\geq 2
(the focus in
[
15
]
is
n=2,3
). We show that there is large choice available in the selection of the projection operator
P
in the definition (
4.12
) of the operator
A_{k}
below (this flexibility is crucial to our
k
-explicit arguments; see §
4.1
). We also provide justification that, even when uniqueness fails, the IE has a solution, and that any such solution provides a solution to the scattering problem.

As we recalled in §
1
, given some compact obstacle
O\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
and some incident field
u^{i}\in H^{1,\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, the approach of Caetano et al
[
15
]
to the exterior sound-soft scattering problem is to choose a compact
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfying (
1.6
) and look for a solution in the form
u|_{\Omega}
, where
u=\mathcal{A}_{k}\phi
, for some
\phi\in H^{-1}_{\Gamma}
. Here
\mathcal{A}_{k}
is the acoustic Newtonian potential, defined by (
1.4
) for
\phi\in L^{2}_{\mathrm{comp}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, where
\Phi_{k}(x,y)
is the outgoing fundamental solution of the Helmholtz equation in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, given by
(e.g.,
[
65
, Eqn. (9.14)]
)

\Phi_{k}(x,y):=\frac{\mathrm{i}}{4}\left(\frac{k}{2\pi|x-y|}\right)^{(n-2)/2}H_{(n-2)/2}^{(1)}(k|x-y|),\hskip 10.00002ptx,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\;\;x\neq y,

(4.1)

where
H_{m}^{(1)}
denotes the Hankel function of the first kind of order
m
. This reduces (see
[
71
, Eqn. 10.16.2]
) to the familiar (
1.5
) in the physically important case
n=3
.

It is standard (e.g.,
[
65
, Lem. 3.24]
) that
L^{2}_{\mathrm{comp}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is dense in
H^{s}_{\mathrm{comp}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for every
s<0
and (e.g.,
[
74
, Thm. 3.1.2]
,
[
65
, Thm. 6.1]
) that, for every
s\in\mathbb{R}
,
\mathcal{A}_{k}
extends to a continuous map

\mathcal{A}_{k}:H^{s}_{\mathrm{comp}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to H^{s+2,\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

(4.2)

Further, for every
\phi\in H^{-1}_{\Gamma}
, since
\Gamma\subset O
,
\mathcal{A}_{k}\phi
satisfies (
1.2
) (e.g.,
[
65
, Eqns. (6.2), (9.14)]
); indeed, for every
s\in\mathbb{R}
,

(\Delta+k^{2})\mathcal{A}_{k}\phi=\mathcal{A}_{k}(\Delta+k^{2})\phi=-\phi,\hskip 20.00003pt\phi\in H^{s}_{\mathrm{comp}}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

(4.3)

Moreover,
\mathcal{A}_{k}\phi
satisfies the radiation condition (
1.3
) (e.g.,
[
65
, Lem. 7.14, Thm. 9.6]
). In the proof of Proposition
4.4
we will need the following lemma.

Lemma 4.1
.

\mathcal{A}_{k}:\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, the mapping is continuous, and (
4.3
) holds for
\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.

Proof.

It follows from (
4.1
) and the definition and asymptotic properties of the Hankel function
[
71
, (10.2.2)-(10.2.5)]
that
\Phi(x,y)=G(x-y)
, with
G\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})+L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, so that (
1.4
) can be written as
\mathcal{A}_{k}\phi=G*\phi=\phi*G
, where
*
is convolution (e.g.,
[
65
, p. 58]
). By Young’s inequality (e.g.,
[
65
, Thm. 3.2 and its proof]
), the mapping
\phi\mapsto G*\phi
is continuous from
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to C(\mathbb{R})
, providing the unique extension of the definition (
1.4
) from
\phi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
to
\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Further, by standard Leibniz-rule arguments (cf.
[
65
, Thm. 3.3]
),
\mathcal{A}_{k}\phi\in C^{m}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, for every
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, with
\partial^{\alpha}(\mathcal{A}_{k}\phi)=(\partial^{\alpha}\phi)*G
, for every multi-index
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m})\in\{1,\ldots,n\}^{m}
, so that, again by Young’s inequality,
\mathcal{A}_{k}:\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is continuous. The continuity of this mapping, and that (
4.3
) holds for the dense subspace
C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
of
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, implies that (
4.3
) holds also for
\phi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
.
∎

It follows from the observations in the paragraph above Lemma
4.1
that, if
u:=\mathcal{A}_{k}\phi
with
\phi\in H^{-1}_{\Gamma}
, then
u\in H^{1,\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, so that
u^{t}:=u+u^{i}\in H^{1,\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and that
u|_{\Omega}\in H^{1,\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)
satisfies the exterior sound-soft scattering problem if and only if
u^{t}|_{\Omega}\in H^{1,\mathrm{loc}}_{0}(\Omega)
. Further, where
\Gamma^{c}
is defined by (
2.1
),
u^{t}|_{\Omega}\in H^{1,\mathrm{loc}}_{0}(\Omega)
if
u^{t}|_{\Gamma^{c}}\in H^{1,\mathrm{loc}}_{0}(\Gamma^{c})
, since
\Omega\subset\Gamma^{c}
and
\partial\Omega=\partial O\subset\Gamma
, so that
\partial\Omega\subset\partial\Gamma^{c}
. Let

C^{\infty}_{0,\Gamma}:=\{\chi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):\chi=1\mbox{ in a neighbourhood of }\Gamma\},

and note that

\displaystyle u^{t}|_{\Gamma^{c}}\in H^{1,\mathrm{loc}}_{0}(\Gamma^{c})

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow(\chi u^{t})|_{\Gamma^{c}}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Gamma^{c}),\;\forall\chi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow(\chi u^{t})|_{\Gamma^{c}}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Gamma^{c}),\;\mbox{ for some }\chi\in C^{\infty}_{0,\Gamma},

since
(\chi u^{t})|_{\Gamma^{c}}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Gamma^{c})
, for all
\chi\in C^{\infty}_{0,\Gamma}
, if and only if
(\chi u^{t})|_{\Gamma^{c}}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Gamma^{c})
, for one
\chi\in C^{\infty}_{0,\Gamma}
.
Thus, fixing some
\chi\in C^{\infty}_{0,\Gamma}
, it holds that
u^{t}|_{\Omega}\in H^{1,\mathrm{loc}}_{0}(\Omega)
if
(\chi u^{t})|_{\Gamma^{c}}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Gamma^{c})
, which holds if
\chi u^{t}\in\widetilde{H}^{1}(\Gamma^{c})
.
Let
P:H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be some continuous projection operator with kernel
\widetilde{H}^{1}(\Gamma^{c})
and range
R(P)
that is a closed subspace of
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
that we may identify with
(H_{\Gamma}^{-1})^{*}
through the isomorphism (
3.5
). Then
\chi u^{t}\in\widetilde{H}^{1}(\Gamma^{c})
if and only if
P(\chi u^{t})=0
.

We have shown all but the last sentence of the following result (cf.
[
15
, Thm. 3.4, Rem. 3.8]
). Note that the definition (
4.5
) of
A_{k}
in this result is independent of the choice of
\chi
, since
(\chi_{1}-\chi_{2})\Psi\in\widetilde{H}^{1}(\Gamma^{c})
, so that
P((\chi_{1}-\chi_{2})\Psi)=0
, for all
\Psi\in H^{1,\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\chi_{1},\chi_{2}\in C^{\infty}_{0,\Gamma}
. Thus the following proposition and all subsequent results hold for every choice of
\chi\in C^{\infty}_{0,\Gamma}
. We will comment on the choice of
P
in §
4.1
below. In
[
15
]

P
is chosen to be orthogonal projection onto
R(P)=\widetilde{H}^{1}(\Gamma^{c})^{\perp}
, with the orthogonality that of
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
equipped with its standard norm (
3.1
).

Proposition 4.2
.

Suppose that the compact set
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfies (
1.6
) and that
\phi\in H^{-1}_{\Gamma}
. Then
u|_{\Omega}
, where
u:=\mathcal{A}_{k}\phi
, satisfies the exterior sound-soft scattering problem if

A_{k}\phi=g:=-P(\chi u^{i}),

(4.4)

where
A_{k}:H^{-1}_{\Gamma}\to R(P)\subset H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is defined by

A_{k}\psi:=P(\chi\mathcal{A}_{k}\psi),\hskip 20.00003pt\psi\in H^{-1}_{\Gamma}.

(4.5)

Conversely, if
u\in H^{1,\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)
satisfies the exterior sound-soft scattering problem and the definition of
u
is extended to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
by setting
u:=-u^{i}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega
, then
u\in H^{1,\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
u=\mathcal{A}_{k}\phi
, where
\phi:=-(\Delta+k^{2})u\in H^{-1}_{\partial O}\subset H^{-1}_{\Gamma}
satisfies (
4.4
).

Proof.

It remains to show the last sentence of the proposition. Extend the definition of
u
to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
in the way indicated, and set
u^{t}:=u+u^{i}
. Then
(\chi u^{t})|_{\Omega}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)
and
u^{t}=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\Omega
, so that
\chi u^{t}\in\widetilde{H}^{1}(\Omega)
, so that
u\in H^{1,\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Defining
\phi:=-(\Delta+k^{2})u\in H^{-1,\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, we have that
\phi=0
in
\Omega
by (
1.2
) and
\phi=-(\Delta+k^{2})u^{i}=0
by (
1.1
) in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
. Thus
\mathrm{supp}(\phi)\subset\partial O\subset\Gamma
. Further, defining
v:=\mathcal{A}_{k}\phi-u\in H^{1,\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, it follows from (
4.3
) that
\Delta v+k^{2}v=0
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and also (see the arguments above Lemma
4.1
)
v
satisfies the radiation condition (
1.3
), so by the uniqueness of solution of the exterior scattering problem in the simplest case
O=\emptyset
, we have that
v=0
so that
u=\mathcal{A}_{k}\phi
. Thus
A_{k}\phi=P(\chi u)=P(\chi(u^{t}-u^{i}))=-P(\chi u^{i})
, since
\chi u^{t}\in\widetilde{H}^{1}(\Omega)\subset\widetilde{H}^{1}(\Gamma^{c})
.
∎

Since the exterior sound-soft scattering problem has a unique solution for all
k>0
, the above proposition has the following corollary (cf.
[
15
, Prop. 3.6]
).

Corollary 4.3
.

Suppose that the compact set
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfies (
1.6
). Then (
4.4
) has a solution
\phi\in H^{-1}_{\partial O}\subset H^{-1}_{\Gamma}
, for all
k>0
.

Invertibility of
A_{k}:H^{-1}_{\Gamma}\to R(P)
is studied in
[
15
]
via a variational formulation of (
4.4
). Define, in terms of the duality pairing (
3.4
), the sesquilinear form
a_{k}(\cdot,\cdot)
on
H^{-1}_{\Gamma}\times H^{-1}_{\Gamma}
associated to
A_{k}
by

a_{k}(\varphi,\psi):=\langle A_{k}\varphi,\psi\rangle,\hskip 20.00003pt\varphi,\psi\in H^{-1}_{\Gamma},

(4.6)

for
k>0
. Since
\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle=0
if
\varphi\in\widetilde{H}^{1}(\Gamma^{c})
and
\psi\in H^{-1}_{\Gamma}
, and
R(Q)=\widetilde{H}^{1}(\Gamma^{c})
, where
Q:=I-P
, we see that

a_{k}(\varphi,\psi)=\langle P(\chi\mathcal{A}_{k}\varphi),\psi\rangle=\langle\chi\mathcal{A}_{k}\varphi,\psi\rangle,\hskip 20.00003pt\varphi,\psi\in H^{-1}_{\Gamma},

(4.7)

so that the choice of
P
does not affect the definition of
a_{k}(\cdot,\cdot)
.
Further, since
A_{k}\phi\in R(P)
and
R(P)
is a realisation of
(H^{-1}_{\Gamma})^{*}
through the isomorphism (
3.5
),
\phi\in H^{-1}_{\Gamma}
satisfies (
4.4
) if and only if it satisfies the variational equation

a_{k}(\phi,\psi)=\langle g,\psi\rangle,\hskip 20.00003pt\forall\psi\in H_{\Gamma}^{-1},

(4.8)

which can be written equivalently as

\langle\chi\mathcal{A}_{k}\varphi,\psi\rangle=-\langle\chi u^{i},\psi\rangle,\hskip 20.00003pt\forall\psi\in H_{\Gamma}^{-1}.

(4.9)

Since
P
does not appear in (
4.9
) the solution set of equation (
4.4
), which is equivalent to (
4.9
), is independent of the choice of
P
.

The following result, showing that
A_{k}
is a compact perturbation of a continuous, coercive operator, is
[
15
, Lem. 3.3]
. (For detail of our terminology, of compactness and coercivity of operators and associated sesquilinear forms, see, e.g.,

[
26
, §2.2]
; in particular we say that an operator
A_{k}:H_{\Gamma}^{-1}\to R(P)
is coercive if the associated sesquilinear form, defined by (
4.6
), is coercive.) Because the result in
[
15
]
, and the references cited there to justify parts of the proof, are claimed only for
n=2,3
, we provide an independent proof of this proposition (for all
n\geq 2
) modelled in part on
[
65
, Thm. 6.1]
.

Proposition 4.4
.

Suppose that the compact set
\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfies (
1.6
).
Then, for each
k>0
, the sesquilinear form
a_{k}(\cdot,\cdot)
is continuous and compactly perturbed coercive on
H^{-1}_{\Gamma}\times H^{-1}_{\Gamma}
, indeed, for some constants
C_{k},\alpha_{k}>0
, and some compact sesquilinear form
\tilde{a}_{k}(\cdot,\cdot)
,

\displaystyle|a_{k}(\varphi,\psi)|

\displaystyle\leq C_{k}\|\varphi\|_{H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\,\|\psi\|_{H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}\,(a_{k}(\psi,\psi)-\tilde{a}_{k}(\psi,\psi))

\displaystyle\geq\alpha_{k}\|\psi\|^{2}_{H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},\hskip 20.00003pt\forall\varphi,\psi\in H^{-1}_{\Gamma}.

Proof.

The first estimate follows immediately from (
4.2
), or from our proof below of the second estimate. To see the second estimate, we first make a splitting of
\mathcal{A}_{k}
into a part
\mathcal{A}_{k}^{0}:H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
that is continuous and coercive, and a remainder that is a smoothing operator.
Let
\mathcal{F}
denote the Fourier transform operator
u\mapsto\hat{u}
on the space
\mathcal{S}^{*}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
of tempered distributions; recall that
\mathcal{F}
is an isomorphism on both
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
\mathcal{S}^{*}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
(e.g.,
[
65
]
). If
\psi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, then

\mathcal{F}((\Delta+k^{2})\psi)(\xi)=(k^{2}-|\xi|^{2})\hat{\psi}(\xi)
, for
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Choose
\sigma\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
0\leq\sigma(\xi)\leq 1
, for
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and such that
\sigma(\xi)=1
,
|\xi|\leq\sqrt{2}k
,
\sigma(\xi)\geq 1/2
,
|\xi|\leq 2k
,
\sigma(\xi)\leq 1/2
,
|\xi|\geq 2k
. Let
\mathcal{A}_{k}^{0}
be a regularised version of
-(\Delta+k^{2})^{-1}
, defined by

\mathcal{A}_{k}^{0}\psi=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\alpha_{k}^{0}\mathcal{F}\psi),\hskip 20.00003pt\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

where
\alpha_{k}^{0}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is given by

\alpha_{k}^{0}(\xi):=\sigma(\xi)+(1-\sigma(\xi))(|\xi|^{2}-k^{2})^{-1},\hskip 20.00003pt\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

so that, for some
\xi_{0}>2k
,
\alpha_{k}^{0}(\xi)=(|\xi|^{2}-k^{2})^{-1}
, for
|\xi|>\xi_{0}
.
Then, for some constants
C,\alpha>0
,

C(1+|\xi|^{2})^{-1}\geq|a_{k}^{0}(\xi)|\geq\mathrm{Re}\,a_{k}^{0}(\xi)\geq\alpha(1+|\xi|^{2})^{-1},\hskip 20.00003pt\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

so that, for
s\in\mathbb{R}
,
\mathcal{A}^{0}_{k}:H^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to H^{s+2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is continuous. Further, the sesquilinear form on
H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, defined by
a^{0}_{k}(\varphi,\psi):=\langle\mathcal{A}^{0}_{k}\varphi,\psi\rangle
,
\varphi,\psi\in H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, is coercive since, for
\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, which is dense in
H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and where
(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes the inner product on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,

\mathrm{Re}\,a^{0}_{k}(\psi,\psi)=\mathrm{Re}\,(\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\alpha_{k}^{0}\mathcal{F}\psi),\psi)=\mathrm{Re}\,(\alpha_{k}^{0}\mathcal{F}\psi,\mathcal{F}\psi)\geq\alpha\|\psi\|_{H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2}.

Define
A_{k}^{0}:H^{-1}_{\Gamma}\to R(P)
by
A_{k}^{0}\psi:=P(\chi\mathcal{A}_{k}^{0}\psi)=P\mathcal{A}_{k}^{0}\psi
, for
\psi\in H^{-1}_{\Gamma}
, and note that
a_{k}^{0}(\cdot,\cdot)
, restricted to
H^{-1}_{\Gamma}\times H^{-1}_{\Gamma}
, is coercive, and is the sesquilinear form associated to
A_{k}^{0}
, so that (by definition)
A_{k}^{0}
is coercive.

To complete the proof of the lemma it is enough to show that
A_{k}-A_{k}^{0}
is compact. Note that multiplication by
\alpha_{k}^{0}
is a continuous operator on
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, so that
\mathcal{A}_{k}^{0}:\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and is continuous. Thus, for
\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
\mathcal{A}_{k}^{0}\psi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, so that
\mathcal{F}((\Delta+k^{2})\mathcal{A}^{0}_{k}\psi)(\xi)=(k^{2}-|\xi|^{2})\alpha_{k}^{0}(\xi)\hat{\psi}(\xi)
, for
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
and, where
\tau\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is defined by
\tau(\xi):=\sigma(\xi)(1+k^{2}-|\xi|^{2})
,
\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

(\Delta+k^{2})\mathcal{A}_{k}^{0}\psi=-\psi+K\psi,\hskip 10.00002pt\mbox{where}\hskip 10.00002ptK\varphi:=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(\tau\mathcal{F}\varphi),\hskip 20.00003pt\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

Now, for
s\in\mathbb{R}
,
\mathcal{F}:H^{s}_{\mathrm{comp}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and is continuous; in particular, this holds for
s=-1
so that
K:H^{-1}_{\mathrm{comp}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and is continuous; in particular,
K\psi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
for
\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Thus,
multiplying both sides of the above equation by
\mathcal{A}_{k}
and using Lemma
4.1
, we see that

\mathcal{A}_{k}\psi=\mathcal{A}_{k}^{0}\psi+\mathcal{A}_{k}K\psi,\hskip 20.00003pt\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

(4.10)

Now
\mathcal{A}_{k}
,
\mathcal{A}_{k}^{0}
, and
\mathcal{A}_{k}K
are continuous as mappings
H^{-1}_{\mathrm{comp}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to H^{1,\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, indeed
\mathcal{A}_{k}K
is continuous as a mapping
H^{-1}_{\mathrm{comp}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, and so as a mapping
H^{-1}_{\mathrm{comp}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to H^{s,\mathrm{loc}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, for all
s\in\mathbb{R}
. This implies, since
C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is dense in
H^{-1}_{\mathrm{comp}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, that (
4.10
) holds in fact for all
\psi\in H^{-1}_{\mathrm{comp}}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, in particular, for all
\psi\in H^{-1}_{\Gamma}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Thus
A_{k}=A_{k}^{0}+P\chi\mathcal{A}_{k}K
, and
\chi\mathcal{A}_{k}K:H^{-1}_{\Gamma}\to H^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is continuous with range in
H^{2}_{\mathrm{supp}(\chi)}
, so that, by the compactness of the embedding
H^{2}_{\mathrm{supp}(\chi)}\subset H^{1}_{\mathrm{supp}(\chi)}

[
65
, Thm. 3.27(i)]
,
\chi\mathcal{A}_{k}K:H^{-1}_{\Gamma}\to H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is compact, so that

it follows that

\displaystyle\|\phi\|_{H_{k}^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle=\|2\nabla u\cdot\nabla\psi+u\Delta\psi-\lambda^{2}\psi u-\Delta\tilde{u}\|_{H^{-1}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\leq 2k^{-1}C_{\psi}\||\nabla u|\|_{L^{2}(B_{R})}+(k^{-1}+2k)C_{\psi}\|u\|_{L^{2}(B_{R})}+\||\nabla\tilde{u}|\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\leq(1+2k^{-1})C_{\psi}\||\nabla u|\|_{L^{2}(B_{R})}+(k^{-1}+1+2k)C_{\psi}\|u\|_{L^{2}(B_{R})},

from which the claimed bound follows.

In the following lemma and subsequently we define
A_{k,\lambda}
, for

and

, by (

Lemma 5.2
.

Suppose that
k_{0}>0
. Then there exists
C_{2}>0
such that, for all
k\geq k_{0}
,
g\in\widetilde{H}^{1}(\Gamma^{c})^{\perp_{k}}
, and

\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
with
\lambda_{i}:=\mathrm{Im}\,\lambda>0
and
\lambda^{2}=k^{2}+\mathrm{i}\beta(k)
, with
0<\beta(k)\leq k^{2}
, it holds that

\|w\|_{H^{1}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq\frac{C_{2}k^{2}}{\beta(k)}\|g\|_{H^{1}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},

where
w:=\mathcal{A}_{\lambda}\mu
and
\mu:=A_{k,\lambda}^{-1}g\in H^{-1}_{\Gamma}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
k\geq k_{0}
and
\lambda
,
g
,
\mu
, and
w
are as in the statement of the lemma. Let us define
b_{\lambda}:H_{k}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times H_{k}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
by

b_{\lambda}(u,v):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\lambda^{2}u\bar{v}-\nabla u\cdot\nabla\bar{v}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x,\hskip 20.00003ptu,v\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

This is the sesquilinear form that appears in the weak form of the Helmholtz equation on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
:
if
u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and
f\in H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, then
\Delta u+\lambda^{2}u=-f
in a distributional sense if and only if

b_{\lambda}(u,v)=-\langle f,v\rangle,\hskip 20.00003pt\forall v\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}).

In particular, since
(\Delta+\lambda^{2})w=-\mu
and
\langle\mu,v\rangle=0
for all
v\in\widetilde{H}^{1}(\Gamma^{c})
, we have that

\displaystyle|b_{\lambda}(w,w)|

\displaystyle=|\langle\mu,\mathcal{A}_{\lambda}\mu\rangle|=|\langle\mu,A_{k,\lambda}\mu\rangle|=|\langle\mu,g\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq\|\mu\|_{H_{k}^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\|g\|_{H^{1}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq C_{1}\|w\|_{H_{k}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\|g\|_{H^{1}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},

by Lemma
5.1
. Further, for
u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,

\bar{\lambda}b_{\lambda}(u,u)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(\lambda|\lambda|^{2}|u|^{2}-\bar{\lambda}|\nabla u|^{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x

so that

|\bar{\lambda}b_{\lambda}(u,u)|\geq\mathrm{Im}(\bar{\lambda}b_{\lambda}(u,u))=\lambda_{i}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\left(|\lambda|^{2}|u|^{2}+|\nabla u|^{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x\geq\lambda_{i}\|u\|_{H^{1}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}^{2},

i.e.,
b_{\lambda}(\cdot,\cdot)
is coercive, and

\lambda_{i}\|w\|_{H_{k}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leq C_{1}|\lambda|\|g\|_{H^{1}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.

Further,
\beta(k)\leq k^{2}
so that
|\lambda|\leq\sqrt{2}\,k
and

\lambda_{i}=\frac{\beta(k)}{\sqrt{2}\sqrt{k^{2}+\sqrt{k^{4}+\beta^{2}(k)}}}\geq\frac{\beta(k)}{\sqrt{2}\sqrt{1+\sqrt{2}}\,k},

from which the claimed result follows.
∎

6.
The case that
\Gamma
is a
d
-set

In this section we focus on the case that
\Gamma
is a
d
-set, in the sense of (
6.2
) below, in which case
\Gamma
has Hausdorff dimension
d
. Significant examples of
d
-sets are: i)
\Gamma
is the closure of a bounded Lipschitz domain (
d=n
); ii)
\Gamma
is the boundary of a bounded Lipschitz domain (
d=n-1
); iii)
\Gamma
is the attractor of an iterated function system of contracting similarities satisfying the standard open set condition (see, e.g.,
[
38
, §9.2]
or
[
15
, §2.1]
), for example the Sierpinski triangle, the Cantor set or Cantor dust, etc., in which case
\Gamma
is a
d
-set with
d=\dim_{\mathrm{H}}(\Gamma)
, the Hausdorff dimension of
\Gamma
(see
[
81
, Theorem 4.7]
).
We will restrict attention to the case
n-2<d\leq n
; if
\Gamma
is a
d
-set with
0\leq d\leq n-2
then
H_{\Gamma}^{-1}=\{0\}

[
15
, Rem. 3.5]
, so that (
1.7
) has only the trivial solution and the scattered field
u=\mathcal{A}_{k}\phi
is zero.

The significance of the
d
-set case is as follows. Following
[
15
]
, in a slight abuse of notation, we have described (
1.7
) as an integral equation because the operator
A_{k}
, given by (
4.5
), is a composition of an integral operator
\mathcal{A}_{k}
, given by (
1.4
), with two simpler operators (a multiplication and a projection operator). In the case that
\Gamma
is a
d
-set, for some
n-2<d\leq n
, we can go further and write (
1.7
) equivalently as an equation

\mathbb{A}_{k}\boldsymbol{\phi}=\gamma g,

(6.1)

where
\mathbb{A}_{k}
is itself an integral operator, a generalisation of the standard single-layer BIO
S_{k}
(cf. (
2.22
) and (
6.5
)). Further, we can relate the norms of
\mathbb{A}_{k}
and
A_{k}
and their inverses, which we do, as the main results of this section, in (
6.8
) and (
6.10
) below. As we point out, this leads to
k
-explicit bounds on the norms of
\mathbb{A}_{k}
and its inverse, by application of the results of §
2.1
. This leads in turn, see §
7
, to bounds on the classical single-layer BIO and its inverse, by applying (
6.8
) in the case
d=n-1
.

As in
[
54
, §1.1]
and
[
81
, §3]
, given
0\leq d\leq n
we say that a closed set
F\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a
d
-set
(or an
Ahlfors-David
d
-regular set
) if there exist constants
0<c_{1}\leq c_{2}
such that

\displaystyle c_{1}r^{d}\leq\mathcal{H}^{d}(F\cap B(x,r))\leq c_{2}r^{d},\hskip 20.00003ptx\in{F},\hskip 10.00002pt0<r\leq 1,

(6.2)

where
B(x,r)\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
denotes the closed ball of radius
r
centred on
x
and
\mathcal{H}^{d}
denotes Hausdorff measure. (For convenience we adopt the normalisation of
[
37
, Def. 2.1]
, so that

\mathcal{H}^{d}
coincides with Lebesgue measure for
d=n

[
37
, Thm. 2.5]
.)
Condition (
6.2
) implies that
F
is uniformly locally
d
-dimensional in the sense that
\dim_{\mathrm{H}}(F\cap B(x,r))=d
for every
x\in{F}
and
r>0
.

Let
\gamma:\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to C(\Gamma)
denote the trace (or restriction) operator, defined by
\gamma u:=u|_{\Gamma}
,
u\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. For
d>n-2
this trace operator has a unique extension to a bounded linear operator
\gamma:H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to L^{2}(\Gamma,\mathcal{H}^{d})
(see
[
53
, Theorem 1]
), where
L^{2}(\Gamma,\mathcal{H}^{d})
denotes the Hilbert space of complex-valued functions defined on
\Gamma
that are square-integrable with respect to
\mathcal{H}^{d}
. Identifying
L^{2}(\Gamma,\mathcal{H}^{d})
with its own dual in the standard way, the adjoint of this extension is
\gamma^{*}:L^{2}(\Gamma,\mathcal{H}^{d})\to H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, given by

\langle\gamma^{*}\boldsymbol{\psi},u\rangle=(\boldsymbol{\psi},\gamma u)_{L^{2}(\Gamma,\mathcal{H}^{d})},\hskip 20.00003pt\boldsymbol{\psi}\in L^{2}(\Gamma,\mathcal{H}^{d}),\hskip 10.00002ptu\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

(6.3)

where
(\cdot,\cdot)_{L^{2}(\Gamma,\mathcal{H}^{d})}
denotes the inner product on
L^{2}(\Gamma,\mathcal{H}^{d})
. Following
[

Proposition 6.1
.

Suppose that
\Gamma
is a
d
-set with

k_{0}>0

\|\mathbb{A}_{k}\|_{L^{2}}\leq Ck^{-1+\varepsilon+n-d},\hskip 20.00003ptk\geq k_{0}.

Proof.

Clearly it is enough to show the above bound in the case that

.
Arguing as we did to obtain (
6.11
), we have that

\|\mathbb{A}_{k}\|_{L^{2}}\leq\|\iota^{*}\|_{k}\|\mathbb{A}_{k}\|_{k}\|\iota\|_{k},

where
\|\iota^{*}\|_{k}
and
\|\iota\|_{k}
denote the norms of the embeddings
\iota^{*}:L^{2}(\Gamma,\mathcal{H}^{d})\to\mathbb{H}_{k}^{*}(\Gamma)
and
\iota:\mathbb{H}_{k}(\Gamma)\to L^{2}(\Gamma,\mathcal{H}^{d})
, respectively, and note that
\|\iota\|_{k}=\|\iota^{*}\|_{k}
. Note further that
\gamma^{*}:\mathbb{H}_{k}^{*}(\Gamma)\to H^{-1}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is an isometry and

, for
\boldsymbol{\psi}\in L^{2}(\Gamma,\mathcal{H}^{d})
, with
\gamma^{*}\boldsymbol{\psi}\in H^{-1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
given by (
6.3
). Thus, and since
\gamma^{*}:L^{2}(\Gamma,\mathcal{H}^{d})\to H^{-(\varepsilon+n-d)/2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

\varepsilon>0
, and recalling (
3.3
), given

and
0<\varepsilon<2+d-n
there exists
C^{\prime},C^{\prime\prime}>0
such that, for all
\boldsymbol{\psi}\in L^{2}(\Gamma,\mathcal{H}^{d})
and
k\geq k_{0}
, where
s:=(\varepsilon+n-d)/2\in(0,1)
,

\displaystyle\|\iota^{*}\boldsymbol{\psi}\|_{\mathbb{H}_{k}^{*}(\Gamma)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\gamma^{*}\boldsymbol{\psi}\|_{H^{-1}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle k^{-1+s}\|\gamma^{*}\boldsymbol{\psi}\|_{H_{k}^{-s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C^{\prime}k^{-1+s}\|\gamma^{*}\boldsymbol{\psi}\|_{H^{-s}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

\displaystyle C^{\prime\prime}k^{-1+s}\|\boldsymbol{\psi}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma,\mathcal{H}^{d})}.

Thus

2.1
