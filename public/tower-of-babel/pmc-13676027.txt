Circulating Natural Killer Cells in Patients With Type 2 Diabetes and Peripheral Neuropathy Are Altered in Their Frequency and Exhibit Impaired Functions and Angiogenic Activities
Authors: Maria Teresa Palano, Federica Riccio, Luisa Sambado, Elena Tagliabue, Milena Bertolotti, Marzia Campanile, Martina Mutoli, Fiorenza Lotti, Andrea Rampin, Cristian Rubuano, Giorgia Maspero, Antonio Antico, Francesca Pitzalis, Matteo Fassan, Piero Maria Stefani, Paola Ostano, Giovanna Chiorino, Nicolò Baranzini, Laura Pulze, Annalisa Grimaldi, Antonino Bruno, Maria Sambataro, Gaia Spinetti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPatients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and critical limb‐threatening ischemia (CLTI), a severe vascular complication associated with amputation and high mortality, exhibit chronic inflammation and endothelial dysfunction. We previously reported an increased frequency of natural killer (NK) cells in advanced CLTI; however, whether NK cell alterations are already present in patients with peripheral neuropathy, which often precedes diabetic foot lesions with or without distal macroangiopathy, remains unclear. We conducted an observational pilot study to assess circulating NK cell frequency by flow cytometry and inflammatory plasma cytokines profiling by ELISA in 177 subjects stratified into five groups: healthy controls (HC, n = 25); T2DM without neurovascular complications (D, n = 26); non‐macrovascular neuropathic T2DM without foot lesions (N, n = 40); non‐macrovascular neuropathic T2DM with lower‐limb ulcers/lesions/osteomyelitis (N1, n = 42); and macrovascular revascularized neuropathic T2DM with ulcers/lesions/osteomyelitis (NV, n = 44). NK cell subset frequencies were evaluated in an independent cohort of patients with non‐macrovascular neuropathic T2DM (n = 22) and healthy controls (n = 23). NKs were further characterized for degranulation capacity, secretome composition, and pro‐angiogenic activity in vitro and in vivo (Hirudo verbana). NK cell frequency was significantly increased in patients with non‐macrovascular neuropathic T2DM compared with controls and was accompanied by elevated plasma levels of soluble mediators associated with NK cell activation and function, including TNF‐α, IL‐12p70, MCP‐1, and IL‐4. NKs from these patients exhibited impaired degranulation and reduced angiogenic capacity in vitro and in vivo. Their secretome is characterized by decreased release of pro‐angiogenic factors and increased secretion of mediators involved in monocyte/macrophage recruitment, resulting in enhanced macrophage recruitment in vivo. If confirmed in larger cohorts, higher frequency and functional impairment of circulating NKs may act as novel therapeutic targets and prognostic biomarkers in peripheral vascular complications of T2DM.Keywords: critical limb‐threatening ischemia, immune dysfunction, innate lymphoid cells, vascular regeneration

Background

Diabetes is a leading metabolic disorder affecting 11.11% of adults aged 20–79 years in 2024, with this percentage constantly rising and projected to increase further to nearly 13% by 2050 [1]. Several cardiovascular complications are associated with the high glucose concentration in the peripheral blood (PB) of people with diabetes, as supported by robust epidemiological, mechanistic, and clinical trial evidence from the global population [2]. In T2DM, the coexistence of neuropathy and distal peripheral artery disease (PAD) dramatically increases the risk of foot ulceration, limb loss, and death [3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8]. In particular, neuropathy, which affects approximately 50% of patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) [3, 4], leads to loss of protective sensation, foot deformity, and autonomic dysfunction, which predispose to ulceration. PAD, especially when severe (critical limb‐threatening ischemia, CLTI), impairs wound healing and increases the risk of infection and amputation [9, 10, 11, 12].

A better knowledge of the cellular and molecular mediators contributing to impaired neuro/vascular axis in patients with diabetes may help in the understanding of the disease pathophysiology and identify future prognostic biomarkers of PAD and CLTI, a still unmet clinical need [13].

In diabetes, chronic inflammation is a key host‐dependent hallmark that contributes to vascular complications [14] and is characterized by elevated circulating levels of pro‐inflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor‐α (TNF‐α), interleukin (IL)‐1β, IL‐2, and IL‐6, thereby exacerbating diabetic complications [4, 15].

Owing to their remarkable functional plasticity, innate immune cells (monocytes/macrophages, neutrophils, and natural killer [NK] cells) and adaptive immune cells (T cells) can release cytokines and pro‐angiogenic factors [16, 17, 18, 19, 20], switching from canonical cytotoxic functions to pro‐reparative and pro‐angiogenic activities. This functional transition has been reported in several pathological conditions, including cancer, cardiovascular diseases, and neurodegenerative disorders.

NK cells represent a subset of type‐1 innate lymphoid cells (ILCs) [21], involved in the recognition and elimination of virus‐infected and tumor cells [22]. They are characterized by two major phenotypic subsets, based on the expression of CD56/NCAM (Neural Cell Adhesion Molecule) and CD16 (Fcγ‐RIII). The predominant NK cell subset, CD56dimCD16+ NKs (90%–95% of circulating NKs), displays cytolytic capabilities through the release of perforin, granzymes, and through a mechanism called Antibody‐Dependent Cellular Cytotoxicity (ADCC). The minor subset, CD56brightCD16− (5%–10%), primarily secretes lytic cytokines such as IFN‐γ [22].

A third subset, CD56superbrightCD16− NKs, has been identified within the decidua, and it is known as decidual NK (dNK) cells (50% of total lymphocytes) [23]. dNK cells exhibit a tolerogenic phenotype and pro‐angiogenic functions, producing vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), placental growth factor (PlGF), and CXCL8/IL‐8 [23], and are necessary to guide the generation of the spiral artery in the developing fetus. In diverse pathophysiological contexts, particularly in solid cancers, the presence of pro‐angiogenic NK cells characterized by a decidual‐like phenotype and defined as CD56brightCD16−CD9+CD49a+ has been observed [24, 25]. No data are yet available on NK cell phenotypes and functionality in the context of neurovascular complications in patients with T2DM.

With this aim, we conducted a pilot observational study profiling, by multicolor flow cytometry, the circulating immune landscape of subjects, with or without diabetes and neurovascular complications.

Methods

Description of Patients Enrolled in the Study

The study was approved by the Local Ethics Committee of Treviso, Italy, and authorized by the Local Health Department (protocol n. 741 del 12.04.2024). All subjects signed written informed consent, and all procedures were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Patients with T2DM were consecutively recruited from the outpatient Metabolic Unit (Complex Diabetes Management) of the Ca′ Foncello Hospital, Treviso, Italy. An exploratory cohort was composed of T2DM without neurovascular foot complications non‐neuropathic (diabetic neuropathy index (DNI < 2) [26] and neuropathy disability score (NDS < 6) [27] negative) (n = 26; D); non‐macrovascular T2DM peripheral neuropathic (DNI and NDS positive) without foot lesions (n = 40; N); neuropathic T2DM with lower‐limb ulcers/lesions/osteomyelitis diagnosed by clinical criteria [28] at least 6 weeks ahead of study enrollment (n = 42; N1), and neuropathic T2DM with ulcers/lesions/osteomyelitis treated with distal peripheral endo and/or chirurgical revascularization (basal transcutaneous dorsal hallux skin partial pressure oximetry (TcPO2 ≤ 30 mmHg) before treatment) (n = 44; NV). Twenty‐five healthy subjects (healthy controls, HC) were used as controls (Table 1).

For blood collection, patients in the N1 and NV groups were enrolled only if their lesions had been present for at least 6 weeks, and blood samples were collected after a minimum of 2 weeks of standardized wound treatment. All patients were managed according to current international guidelines for diabetic foot lesions [29]. Lesion recurrence and an obesity cut‐off value (BMI > 30 kg/m2) were not exclusion criteria. Active smoking, dialysis, pregnancy, heavy myocardial insufficiency (NYHA IV class), recent (within 6 months) myocardial infarction, or cerebral ischemia were exclusion criteria. Subjects older than 75 years of age and diabetes diagnosis within 5 years were excluded. T2DM patients controlled their blood sugar levels with diet (1600 kcal/day: 55% carbohydrate, 20% protein, and 25% fat with < 10% saturated fat), exercise, anti‐diabetic drugs, and/or insulin. According to self‐reporting diaries, leisure exercise, energy, and nutrient intake were not different between the groups.

Patients with neuroischemic lesions and distal macroangiopathy were hospitalized for endovascular or hybrid revascularization. Minor amputations, debridement, and foot lesion management with targeted antibiotics were performed during hospitalization or as day surgeries.

T2DM complications are described in Table 1. Diabetic autonomic neuropathy (DAN) was assessed by the so‐called Ewing battery of autonomic cardiac tests: heart rate variations during deep breathing (DB), lying to standing (LS), standardized Valsalva maneuver (VM), and analysis of blood pressure variations on standing (PH) [30]. Clinical nephropathy was assessed by spot microalbuminuria, 24‐h macroalbuminuria, or glomerular filtration rate using the Modification of Diet in Renal Disease equation (90 mL/min/BSA). Diabetic retinopathy was defined using the Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study criteria [31]. Dyslipidemia and arterial hypertension treatment with statins, ACE inhibitors, beta‐blockers, and diuretics were equally distributed in all diabetic groups.

Plasma Cytokine Profiling

Plasma from all diabetic patients and healthy subjects was collected from whole blood (7 mL) in EDTA spotted tubes. Whole blood was centrifuged at 1.500 RPM for 15 min at room temperature. The first collected plasma was further centrifuged at 14.000 RPM for 15 min at 4°C, to deplete platelets. Platelet‐depleted plasma was stored at −80°C until use. Plasma samples were used to profile their cytokine content by ELISA kits (RayBiotech, Norcross, GA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Optic density was read at 450 nm in a microplate ELx800 Absorbance reader (Biotek, Winooski, VT, USA) for the detection of TNF‐α, IL‐10, MCP‐1, IL‐12, IL‐15, IFN‐γ, IL‐4, IL‐6, and TGF‐β.

Cell Culture and Maintenance

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated by density gradient stratification using the Ficoll‐Histopaque (Sigma) reagent.

The human erythroid K562 cells (ATCC) were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Euroclone) supplemented with 10% FBS (Euroclone), 2 mM L‐glutamine (Euroclone), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Euroclone) at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere, and used for the NK cell degranulation assay as target cells.

Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs, Lonza) were cultured in endothelial cell basal medium (EBM, Lonza) supplemented with endothelial cell growth medium (EGM SingleQuots, Lonza), 2% FBS (Euroclone), 2 mM L‐glutamine (Euroclone), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Euroclone), at 37°C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. HUVECs between the third and fifth passages were used for functional experiments.

Immunophenotyping of PBMCs by Flow Cytometry

The frequency of T, B, and NK cells was measured on PB collected in EDTA tubes from patients of the exploratory cohort by flow cytometry (FC), as previously described [32]. Briefly, before staining, PBMCs were treated with fetal calf serum for 10 min and washed with PBS and 0.5% BSA and then stained with BD Multitest 6‐color TBNK reagent (Becton, Dickinson and Company, New Jersey, USA) for 15 min in the dark. A total of 100.000 events were acquired with FACS Canto II and analyzed with FACS Diva Software (Becton, Dickinson and Company, New Jersey, USA). The kit allows for the identification of total T cells (CD45+CD3+), CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, and, within the CD45+CD3− gate, cytolytic NKs (CD56+CD16+) and B cells (CD19+) (Figure 1A).

NK Cell Degranulation Assay

The degranulation capability of NKs, co‐cultured with K562 target cells, was assessed by FC, measuring CD107a. PBMCs were activated overnight with 100 U/mL of human recombinant IL‐2 (Miltenyi Biotec), in RPMI 1640 medium (Euroclone), at 37°C, 5% CO2. For degranulation detection, 2 × 105 PBMCs were co‐cultured with 2 × 105 K562 (ratio effectors/targets 1:1), in the presence of anti‐CD107a. PBMCs were used as controls for basal degranulation. Cells were stimulated for 6 h with GolgiStop plus GolgiPlug (both from BD Biosciences). CD107a levels were detected on CD3−CD56+ total NK‐gated cells (gating strategy in Figure 4B), using a BD FACS Fortessa ×20 flow cytometer. Data were analyzed using the FlowJo v10 software, Three Star. Antibodies used for FC are listed in Table S1.

NK Cell Isolation and Generation of Conditioned Media (CM)

Total circulating NK cells were isolated from PBMCs using a BD FACS Aria II cell sorter, after staining with anti‐CD3 and anti‐CD56 antibodies. Samples with at least 98% cell purity were used to generate conditioned media (CM). The gating strategy for NK cell isolation and its efficiency are shown in Figure S1A,C, together with brightfield images of NK cells as PBMCs (pre‐sort) and NK cells (post‐sort) in Figure S1B,D.

FACS‐sorted NKs were cultured (1 × 105 cells in 150 μL) in serum‐free RPMI 1640 medium (Euroclone), 2 mM L‐glutamine (Euroclone), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin (Euroclone), at 37°C, 5% CO2. After 24 h, the medium was collected at 3000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was separated from the cell pellet and harvested as cell‐conditioned medium (CM). CM and NK cell pellets were stored at −80°C till use.

Secretome Analysis of NK‐Derived Conditioned Media (CM)

Soluble factors in CM of FACS‐sorted circulating NKs were characterized using RayBio C‐Series Human Cytokine Antibody Array C1000 series, C6‐C7 (RayBiotech Inc., Norcross, GA) as in [24]. Chemiluminescent signals were captured using the Alliance Q9 (Uvitec) and quantified using ImageJ software and the DotBlot Analyzer plug‐in.

After averaging signals from replicated spots, the blank was subtracted to retrieve net signals for each protein. Negative values were replaced with “NA”, and positive net signals were log2 transformed and normalized by setting the median equal to 8.5 within each sample.

Endothelial Cell Tube Formation Assay

HUVECs were serum‐starved overnight and seeded at a density of 1.6 × 104 cells per well in 96‐well plates pre‐coated with 50 μL of Matrigel (10 mg/mL; Corning). Cells were cultured in a total volume of 150 μL conditioned media derived from FACS‐sorted circulating NK cells. Experimental media were prepared by pooling CM from three healthy control donors (7 μg total, as quantified by Bradford assay) or from three patients with neurological disease (7 μg total, as quantified by Bradford assay). Endothelial Basal Medium (EBM) without fetal bovine serum (FBS) or growth factors was used as the negative control, whereas EBM supplemented with 10% FBS and VEGF served as the positive control. HUVECs were incubated for 16 h at 37°C, after which microphotographs were acquired at 5× magnification using a LEICA DMi1 inverted microscope equipped with LAS X software. Tube formation was quantified using ImageJ software with the Angiogenesis Analyzer plug‐in. Endothelial network formation was expressed as a set of parameters assessed across the indicated experimental conditions and including the number of segments, the number of branches, the total segment length, and total branch length.

Endothelial Cell Spheroid Collagen Invasion Assay

HUVECs were cultured as hanging‐drop spheroids by seeding 1.000 cells per drop in Vascular Cell Basal Medium supplemented with growth factors and 2% FBS (ATCC Primary Cell Solutions, Cat. No. PCS‐100‐030). Spheroids were incubated for 24 h at 37°C under normoxic conditions. 12 spheroids per condition were collected, resuspended in 1.5 mg/mL rat tail collagen Type I (Corning, Cat. No. 354249) and embedded in a 96‐well plate. The collagen matrix was allowed to polymerize at 37°C before being overlaid with Vascular Basal Medium. Medium with neither growth factors nor FBS was used as a negative control, while medium supplemented with 10% FBS and growth factors was used as a positive control. Experimental conditions consisted of medium supplemented with 7 μg, as determined by Bradford assay, and conditioned medium obtained either from a pooled sample of three control subjects or from a pooled sample of three neuro patients. Endothelial cell invasion into the surrounding collagen matrix was assessed after 24 h. Images were acquired at 5× magnification using a LEICA DMi1 inverted microscope and LAS X software. A total of 12 spheroids were analyzed for the patient pool, and 10 spheroids for the control pool. The invasion area was quantified using ImageJ software and normalized to the spheroid area measured at time 0. Spheroids treated with conditioned medium derived from neuro patients exhibited a significantly reduced invasive capacity compared with those treated with conditioned medium from control subjects.

RNA Isolation and RT‐qPCR

Total RNA from FACS‐sorted circulating NKs was isolated using the miRNeasy Kit (Qiagen), following the manufacturer's instructions. RNA concentration was evaluated by NanoDrop One (Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 50 ng of RNA was retro‐transcribed using Applied Biosystems TaqMan Reverse Transcription Reagents (Life Technologies). Real‐time PCR was performed using the SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Life Technologies), according to the manufacturer's instructions on a QuantStudio 6 Flex Real‐Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Primer sequences are reported in Table S2. After normalization on 18S, the 2−ΔCt method was used.

In Vivo Experiments With the Leech Hirudo verbana

The medicinal leeches (Hirudo verbana, Annelida), derived from Italian Leech Farm (ILFARM SRL, Italy), were kept in lightly salted water (NaCl 1.5 g/L) at 20°C in aerated tanks. For the experiments, leeches were injected at the level of the 20th metamere using a micro‐insulin syringe, with 10 μL of CM from FACS‐sorted circulating NKs, containing 20 μg of total protein derived from CMs (pool of 3 different donors for each experimental group) from healthy controls (CM NK–HC) or diabetic subjects with neuropathy (CM NK–N). Pools were generated as follows, based on their belonging to the HC and N group and gender balanced: Pool1 (HC: C005, C0012, C045 all males, age 62–66), Pool2 (HC: C014, C027, C058, all females, age 40–42), Pool3 (N: N216, N230, N277, all males, age 68–71), Pool4 (N: N211, N285, N215, all females, age 65). Protein concentration was determined using the Bradford assay. For each pool, a total of five animals were used. The injected body wall region was dissected using a scalpel after 6 h. Treated animals were anesthetized by immersion in a 10% ethanol solution until they appeared completely asleep before being injected and dissected.

Optical and Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

Injected areas of leech tissue were fixed in 4% glutaraldehyde diluted in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer (pH 7.4) for 2 h. After several washings in cacodylate buffer, samples were post‐fixed for 1 h with 1% osmium tetroxide in cacodylate buffer. After washing, samples were dehydrated and embedded in an Epon‐Araldite 812 mixture of epoxy resin (Sigma‐Aldrich). Sections were obtained with a Reichert Ultracut S ultratome (Leica).

Sections for light microscopy (700 nm thickness) were stained with crystal violet and basic fuchsin and observed under the light microscope Nikon Eclipse equipped with DS‐5 M‐L1 digital camera system (Nikon). Ultrathin sections (80 nm thickness) for TEM analysis were placed on copper grids, stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, and observed with a Jeol 1010 EX electron microscope (Jeol) equipped with MORADA digital camera system (Olympus). Microscopy analysis was performed at the Centro di Ricerca e Trasferimento Tecnologico (CRIETT), University of Insubria, Varese, Italy (instrument code MIC01).

CD31 Immunofluorescence Assays and Acid Phosphatase (ACP) Staining

Leech tissues were embedded in OCT (TebuBio), frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C. Cryosections (7 μm thickness) were obtained with a cryostat (Leica CM1850) and collected on gelatinized slides. For the immunofluorescent assays, slides were rehydrated for 10 min in PBS and incubated for 30 min in blocking solution containing 2% Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) and 0.1% Tween in PBS. Primary and secondary antibodies were diluted in blocking solution. Samples were incubated at RT for 1 h with rabbit α‐mouse‐CD31 antibody, diluted 1:200 (Novocastra). After washing in PBS, samples were incubated at RT for 45 min with goat anti‐rabbit Cy3‐conjugated secondary antibody (Jackson Immuno Research Laboratories), diluted 1:200. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (0.1 mg/mL 4,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole, 1:5000 in PBS) for 3 min, and slides were mounted with Cityfluor (Citifluor Ltd.). Negative control experiments were performed by omitting primary antibodies. For acid phosphatase staining, after rehydration, cryosections were incubated at RT for 5 min in acetic acid–sodium acetate buffer 0.1 M and subsequently at 37°C for 90 min in the reaction mixture (sodium acetate–acetic acid buffer 0.1 M, 0.01% naphthol phosphate, 2% N, N‐dimethylformamide, 0.06% Fast Red, and MnCl2 0.5 nM). After washing in PBS, slides were mounted with PBS/glycerol 2:1. Photos were acquired using the fluorescence microscope (Nikon Digital Sight DS‐SM) equipped with a DS‐5 M‐L1 digital camera system (Nikon).

Blood vessels (CD31+) and macrophages (ACP+) were counted in five different slides (ten random fields of 45.000 μm2) for each experimental group, using ImageJ software (https://imagej.net/ij/index.html).

Statistical Analysis

Data were presented as frequency and percentage or mean and standard deviation. Categorical data were compared between groups by the Chi‐Square test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate. Continuous data were pairwise compared between groups by the non‐parametric two‐sample Wilcoxon test. For multiple comparisons, the significance threshold was p < 0.01 (Bonferroni adjustment for 5 groups). Logistic regression models were implemented to evaluate the association between variables and neuropathy, when compared with nondiabetic controls. Multivariable models adjusted by significant variables at univariate analysis (i.e., age, sex, BMI, and smoking habits) were also implemented. Analyses were performed with SAS Software v.9.4. For all univariate and multivariable analyses, p‐values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. For pairwise comparisons, p‐values < 0.01 were considered statistically significant due to Bonferroni correction for 5 groups.

For data obtained from the in vivo leech model, a non‐parametric two‐sample Wilcoxon test was used. Statistical analyses were performed with GraphPad Prism8, with the threshold value of p < 0.05.

For the secretome analysis of NK‐derived CM, a two‐sided Wilcoxon test was applied to retrieve proteins with differential expression between the two classes of interest using the matrixTests R package. The first 12 proteins based on the ascending Wilcoxon test p‐value were further selected to build a protein expression matrix that was uploaded to the TMeV software (https://sourceforge.net/projects/mev‐tm4/). Hierarchical clustering was carried out to cluster samples and proteins according to the expression profiles, using Spearman's rank correlation as a similarity metric and average as the linkage method. Before clustering, log intensities were median‐centered across samples and divided by the standard deviation. Using STRING online software (version 12.0), the most modulated proteins between HC and N were used to evaluate interconnections based on experimental data, databases, and co‐expression analysis of biological processes related to the modulated factors. The graphs showing mean ± SEM were generated using GraphPad Prism 8 software.

Results

Circulating NK Cell Frequency Is Elevated in Patients With T2DM and Peripheral Neuropathy Without Foot Lesions Compared With Healthy Controls

To identify new circulating cellular biomarkers of early stages of peripheral vascular disease in diabetic patients, we profiled peripheral blood immune cell populations (gating strategy in Figure 1A) of a cohort of 177 subjects enrolled in the outpatient Metabolic Unit of Ca′ Foncello Hospital. The cohort was composed of five study groups of subjects with T2DM at different stages of neurovascular complications, as detailed in the methods section. Briefly, Group 1 was composed of subjects with T2DM without neuropathy (n = 26; D); Group 2 of patients with T2DM and neuropathy without foot lesions (n = 40; N); group 3 of non‐macrovascular peripheral neuropathic T2DM (n = 42; N1); group 4 of macrovascular neuropathic T2DM patients (N = 44; NV), and group 5 of healthy control subjects (n = 25; HC). Clinical features are detailed in Table 1.

Total circulating mononuclear cells monitored by flow cytometry included total CD3+ T lymphocytes (Figure 1B), CD4+ (Figure 1C), and CD8+ (Figure 1D) T lymphocytes, total NKs (Figure 1E), and B lymphocytes (Figure 1F). Compared to healthy controls, we found a statistically significant increase in the frequency of CD56+CD16+ NKs in the group of patients with diabetes and peripheral neuropathy without foot lesions (N) (p = 0.004) (Figure 1E) and a significantly decreased frequency of CD19+ cells in the neuropathic group of patients without ischemia and with foot lesions (N1) (p = 0.009) (Figure 1F). We did not observe differences in the frequency of other immune cells. Applying multivariable logistic regression models, the increased frequency of NK was significantly associated with peripheral neuropathy, after adjusting for significant variables at univariate analysis (age, sex, smoking habit, and BMI). Also adjusting for both age and sex led to a significant result that confirmed the association between NK frequency and peripheral neuropathy (OR = 1.12, p = 0.014, AUC = 0.773).

To explore systemic inflammatory profiles associated with peripheral neuropathy in diabetes, we analyzed a panel of plasma cytokines. Representative heatmaps provided an overview of cytokine modulation across patient groups (Figure 2A,B), suggesting a gradual shift toward a pro‐inflammatory profile with increasing neuropathy severity. Plasma concentrations of TNF‐α (Figure 2C), MCP‐1 (Figure 2D), IL‐12p70 (Figure 2E), and IL‐4 (Figure 2F) further highlighted the involvement of inflammatory and immune‐regulatory soluble factors, relevant to disease progression. Diabetic subjects exhibited significantly higher plasma levels of all four cytokines detected, compared with healthy controls, with cytokine concentrations progressively increasing according to the severity of peripheral neuropathy, and with TNF‐α as the most abundantly soluble factor produced.

NK Cell Subset Distribution and Paracrine Activity Are Altered in Patients With Neuropathic T2DM

Based on the results obtained, next we detailed NK phenotype and function validated in a new cohort of patients with T2DM and neuropathy without foot lesions (n = 22) and healthy controls (n = 23) (N vs. HC) (Table 2). Circulating NKs were characterized for the frequency of NK‐specific subsets, based on the expression of CD56 and CD16, the NKG2D activation marker, and CD9 (teraspanin) associated with decidual‐like polarization (gating strategy in Figure 3A).

Although we did not observe any modulation of total NK frequency between groups (Figure 3B), we observed a modulation of NK subsets with a statistically significant decrease of CD56dimCD16− NKs (Figure 3F) and an increase of NKG2D+ NKs (Figure 3G) in neuropathic T2DM subjects compared to controls.

We next assessed the functional activity of circulating NK cells from patients with T2DM and peripheral neuropathy by performing a degranulation assay against K562 target cells. Patients with peripheral neuropathy (N) exhibited a significantly lower percentage of CD107a+ degranulating NK cells than healthy controls (HC) (Figure 4A,B). This impairment in degranulation capability was associated with reduced expression levels of IFN‐γ (Figure 4C), and increased expression of genes of immune cell exhaustion, namely CD96/Tactile (Figure 4D) and TIM3 (Figure 4E) in NK cells from patients with T2DM and peripheral neuropathy.

To further investigate the paracrine activity of circulating NK cells, conditioned medium (CM) collected from FACS‐sorted NK cells isolated from patients with neuropathic T2DM (CM NK–N) or healthy controls (CM NK–HC) was applied to endothelial cells in vitro. Compared with CM from healthy control NK cells, CM NK–N significantly impaired the angiogenic capacity of HUVECs, as demonstrated by reduced capillary‐like tube formation (Figure 4F–L and Figure S2A–E) and diminished collagen invasion in the spheroid sprouting assay (Figure 4M,N and Figure S3A,B). Collectively, these findings indicate that circulating NK cells from patients with neuropathic T2DM acquire a dysfunctional secretory phenotype that impairs endothelial regenerative and angiogenic responses.

The Secretome of Circulating NK Cells From Patients With Neuropathic T2DM Exhibits Impaired Pro‐Angiogenic Activity In Vivo

To confirm the results obtained in vitro by the angiogenesis assays, we assessed the effects of CM derived from FACS‐sorted circulating NKs in the leech (H. verbana, Annelida), an invertebrate model that allows studying angiogenesis in vivo (Figure 5 and Figure S4A,B). Injection of CM obtained from FACS‐sorted NK cells from healthy controls (CM NK–HC) into the leech body wall resulted in the presence of large subepithelial blood vessels (Figure 5A). Resident macrophages and granulocytes, identified by their ruffled surface and numerous electron‐dense granules, respectively (see TEM inset, CM NK–HC in Figure 5A), were only sparsely detected within the extracellular matrix (ECM) surrounding the muscle cells. Conversely, injection of CM obtained from FACS‐sorted NK cells of patients with neuropathic T2DM (CM NK–N) elicited a marked inflammatory response, characterized by abundant macrophage and granulocyte infiltration beneath the epithelium and throughout the extracellular matrix (ECM) (see TEM inset, CM NK–N in Figure 5A). In parallel, the number of large intramuscular vessels was markedly reduced, whereas only the physiological small circular vessels remained detectable in the subepithelial region (Figure 5A). Quantitative analysis confirmed a significant reduction in the number of large vessels in CM NK–N‐treated leeches compared with CM NK–HC‐treated animals (Figure 5B).

Immunofluorescence assays (Figure 5C) confirmed that injection of CM from control circulating NK‐HC was associated with a higher CD31 expression (Figure 5D), compared to that observed in leeches treated with CM obtained from circulating NK cells FACS‐sorted from neuropathic T2DM patients (CM NK–N).

Ultrastructural analysis at TEM showed that leeches injected with CM from control circulating NK‐HC (Figure 5E) showed a continuous monolayer of endothelial cells (ECs), lined by a compact basal lamina (BM) and formed tight junctions, without overlap at the edges. On the contrary, in leeches treated with CM of circulating NKs from neuropathic T2DM patients, NK CM showed projections of some ECs within the vessel lumen, with a loose basal membrane. These finger‐like projections generated openings or small intercellular gaps in the vessel wall (Figure 5F).

Regarding macrophage recruitment, as detected by acid phosphatase reaction (ACP) (Figure 5G,H and Figure S4C), we selectively stained the lysosomal enzyme of leech macrophages [33], observing an increased number of these immune cells in animals treated with CM from circulating NKs from neuropathic T2DM patients, compared to those from controls (CM NK‐HC) (Figure 5G). The ACP+ cell counting confirmed a statistically significant increase in macrophage recruitment in leeches injected with CM from circulating NK–N (Figure 5H).

These in vivo findings support the hypothesis that T2DM‐associated NK cells exert paracrine effects on vascular remodeling and macrophage recruitment.

Circulating NK Cells From Patients With Neuropathic T2DM Display a Pro‐Inflammatory Secretome

Results obtained in vivo on the capability of CM from circulating NKs from neuropathic T2DM patients to affect blood vessels while supporting the recruitment of macrophages prompted us to assess whether circulating NKs from subjects with diabetes and neuropathy and healthy subjects have different secretomes. We performed the cytokine profile of circulating NK‐derived CM from neuropathic T2DM patients and controls, using a commercially available antibody membrane protein‐array (Figure 6A and Figure S5). The principal component analysis (PCA) plot of the cytokine array data showed a distinct secretome pattern between the CM derived from the FACS‐sorted NKs of the T2DM subject with neuropathy group compared to controls (Figure 6B). Twelve soluble factors were differentially released (two‐sided Wilcoxon test p‐value < 0.2) (Figure 6C), 6 up‐regulated in the CM of circulating NK FACS‐sorted from T2DM subject with neuropathy group (fold‐change > 1.2, with MIG/CXCL9 reaching the highest fold‐change of 15.6) (Figure 6C) and 6 up‐regulated in controls (fold‐change < −1.5, with Interleukin 8 (IL‐8)/CXCL‐8 reaching the lowest fold‐change of −5.1) (Figure 6C). Pathway analysis for the most up‐ and down‐regulated factors in the T2DM neuropathic group, performed by STRING software, showed the interconnection among the chemokines mainly involved in angiogenesis modulation and immune cell recruitment. Factors up‐regulated in NK‐derived CM from neuropathic T2DM patients are mainly linked to immune cell recruitment and inflammation maintenance (Figure 6D), while factors down‐modulated, such as IL‐8/CXCL‐8 and platelet‐derived growth factor B (PDGF‐B), which are angiogenesis mediators, are related to vascularization and angiogenesis and are linked to artery‐related pathologic conditions (Figure 6E). Overall, these findings indicate that circulating NK cells from neuropathic T2DM patients exhibit a markedly altered secretome, enriched in inflammatory chemokines and depleted of pro‐angiogenic mediators. This molecular signature is consistent with the enhanced macrophage recruitment and impaired vascular remodeling induced by NK‐derived conditioned media.

Discussion

Neuropathy and distal peripheral vascular disease are distinct complications of T2DM that may coexist and contribute to more than 50% of diabetic foot ulcer complications, synergistically increasing the risk of ulceration and amputation [34]. As we and others have previously demonstrated, neuroinflammation is a key driver of diabetic neuropathy, with multiple immune cell populations and cytokines contributing to disease pathogenesis [35, 36].

In this pilot observational study, we tested the hypothesis that an alteration in circulating NK frequency and function is associated with peripheral neuropathy in T2DM and may contribute to the vascular complications of advanced diabetic peripheral disease. Our results demonstrated that circulating NK cell frequency is increased in patients with T2DM and neuropathy, even in the absence of macrovascular complications. Moreover, we found that circulating NK cells display altered degranulation capacity and impaired interactions with endothelial cells through soluble mediators, suggesting that NK cell dysfunction may contribute to the progression of diabetic peripheral vascular complications. These findings highlight circulating NK cells and their secretome as potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets for identifying and managing patients at higher risk of adverse outcomes.

NK cells are innate immune effector cells with remarkable functional plasticity, capable of exerting cytotoxic or pro‐angiogenic activities depending on the local and systemic microenvironment in which they reside. Accordingly, NK cells are emerging as key regulators in several pathological conditions [37, 38, 39, 40, 41]. However, to our knowledge, no previous study has investigated NK cell alterations in patients with T2DM before the onset of advanced disease stages.

Diabetic neuropathy is a common complication of T2DM that contributes to the development of chronic peripheral complications through progressive damage to peripheral nerve fibers. This leads to impaired sensory and autonomic signaling, resulting in reduced perception of noxious stimuli and increased susceptibility to foot injuries. However, the contribution of neuropathy to the progression and distribution of critical limb‐threatening ischemia and infection remains poorly understood. Another relevant host‐dependent hallmark of this condition is immune dysregulation, characterized by an altered and dysfunctional inflammatory response. In diabetes, neuropathy may promote systemic low‐grade inflammation, which contributes to vascular damage, atherosclerosis, and arterial calcification, ultimately worsening peripheral perfusion and ischemic conditions. In this context, impaired angiogenesis represents an additional host‐dependent hallmark of diabetes mellitus, further limiting tissue repair and vascular recovery [42, 43]. Diabetes‐associated systemic chronic inflammation induces immune cell polarization to adapt to the pathophysiological environment [44]. Monocyte/macrophage and T cell polarization in diabetes has been the subject of several studies [45, 46]; however, less is known about NK cells. While pro‐angiogenic NKs have been characterized in patients with solid cancers [24, 25], NK cell contribution to aberrant angiogenesis in cardiovascular disorders still represents a poorly explored topic [40, 47]. Here, we hypothesized that NK cell dysfunction may represent the detrimental link between diabetic neuropathy, impaired innate immunity—which is known to increase susceptibility to microbial infections—and vascular disease with prevalent longitudinal Mönckeberg medial calcification. In this way, NK cell biology could provide a unifying framework that redefines the classical triad underlying the pathogenesis of the diabetic foot [35].

Our results demonstrate a marked increase in the frequency of total circulating NKs in patients with T2DM and moderate neuropathy, without overt vascular disease, compared to control subjects. In addition, we showed an anti‐angiogenic activity of circulating NKs in T2DM patients with moderate neuropathy, suggesting a role of these cells in the predisposition to vasculopathy.

Moreover, in a selected cohort of patients, we characterized circulating NK cell subsets and observed a significant reduction of CD56dimCD16− NK cell subsets, increased percentage of NKG2D+ NKs (representing potentially activated NKs), together with a trend toward an increase in decidual‐like CD9+ (tetraspanin) NKs. These data suggest the prevalence of mature (NKG2D+) but potentially non‐fully activated cytokine‐producing NKs (CD56dimCD16−), which could display reduced cytotoxic capabilities with limited ability to kill target cells. The contribution of peripheral neuropathy to the generation of this peculiar NK cell subpopulation still needs further investigation.

By characterizing the cytokine contents of plasma from neuropathic T2DM patients, compared to HC, we found an enrichment of circulating pro‐inflammatory cytokines, namely TNF‐α, as the highest grade released soluble factor, and IL‐12, involved in activation of NKs [48, 49]. We also observed increased circulating levels of IL‐4 in the plasma of diabetic and neuropathic patients. Despite being a typical Th2‐type cytokine, IL‐4 has been reported to cooperate with IL‐12 and other cytokines, involved in NK cell activation [50, 51, 52]. Also, IL‐12, which we found increased in the plasma of T2DM and neuropathy patients, is one of the major activators of NKs [53, 54].

Based on the observed plasma cytokine profile, we hypothesized that this altered cytokine milieu could influence NK cell functionality, as assessed by degranulation capacity. Indeed, chronic exposure to activating cytokines may drive sustained NK cell stimulation, eventually resulting in functional exhaustion rather than effective immune activation [55, 56]. The chronic inflammatory milieu observed in our patients may therefore favor a transition from an effector NK‐cell phenotype toward a dysfunctional or exhausted state, characterized by reduced degranulation capacity and increased expression of inhibitory receptors. Consistent with this hypothesis, circulating NK cells from patients with T2DM and neuropathy exhibited a significant reduction in CD107a expression, together with a trend in decreased expression of IFN‐γ (the major anti‐tumor cytokine/effector produced by NKs) and increased expression of markers of immune cell exhaustion, namely CD96/Tactile and TIM3. Therefore, the pro‐inflammatory plasma cytokine profile observed in patients with T2DM and neuropathy may promote a compensatory increase in the expression of immune checkpoint molecules on circulating NK cells. In line with this possibility, circulating NK cells showed elevated expression of CD96 and TIM‐3, inhibitory receptors that suppress NK‐cell cytotoxicity and cytokine production, especially during persistent antigenic or cytokine stimulation [57, 58]. Their increased expression in our patients might reflect an intrinsic regulatory response to prolonged activation signals, functioning to dampen NK‐cell effector activity.

This result appears crucial since the presence of hypo functional NKs in diabetic patients would impact their efficient response against infections and probably tissue repair, thus exacerbating diabetes‐related complications in patients and foot infective complications. Thus, infection may represent both a consequence and a mediator of immune dysregulation in diabetic neuropathy. Future studies considering longitudinal monitoring of infectious burden or microbial signatures could clarify whether infection‐driven immune remodeling underlies the observed NK cell alterations.

Based on the literature, although mostly in the oncological settings, pro‐angiogenic functions of NK cells are mediated via paracrine interactions. Based on this evidence, we used CM of FACS‐sorted circulating NK cells to test in vitro and in vivo CM functionality. We tested the activities of NK CM in supporting the generation of capillary‐like structures and invasive properties on HUVEC, as a readout of pro‐angiogenic functionality. We found that CM of circulating NKs from patients with T2DM and neuropathy was less effective in supporting angiogenesis, compared to CM from NK cells isolated from HC. We therefore tested NK CM using the leech H. verbana, an emerging in vivo model for preclinical studies to investigate angiogenesis, due to its simple anatomical structure and the presence of unique botryoidal tissue, located between the gut and body wall [33, 59, 60, 61]. This tissue, composed of round, granular botryoidal cells and small, flattened, endothelial‐like cells, plays a central role in angiogenesis, which is similar to that observed in vertebrates [33, 59, 60, 61]. The newly formed vessel, originating from the botryoidal tissue and extending from the gut area through the muscle fibers, becomes easily detectable in the stimulated body wall of leeches. Indeed, as already described in the literature, the muscular layer is almost avascular in physiological condition, while few small subepithelial vessels are present [62]. Despite its simple body plan organization, H. verbana displays a level of cellular and molecular complexity in several key biological processes, including wound healing and inflammation, that are similar to those found in vertebrates [60]. The growing availability of genome (Accession PRJNA551036) and transcriptome (Accession PRJNA257563, PRJNA435743, PRJNA257563) data from H. verbana, along with available recombinant proteins, has enabled the study of gene networks involved in biological processes (i.e., innate immunity and angiogenesis), supporting the use of this invertebrate as a reliable model organism [60]. We found that neuropathic‐derived NK CM limits the generation of new large intramuscular blood vessels, in vivo, compared to those from healthy controls, and these vessels were characterized by ultrastructural instability, as detected by TEM.

Furthermore, we detected a massive ACP+macrophages infiltration in the leeches injected with neuropathic‐derived circulating NK CM, suggesting an exacerbated pro‐inflammatory response.

The characterization of the secretome of circulating NK CM from neuropathic and healthy subjects allowed discrimination between patients and control subjects, as observed by distribution. Moreover, neuropathic‐derived circulating NK CM are enriched in factors involved in immune cell recruitment and inflammation, corroborating the observed increased number of macrophages in leeches. Conversely, the downregulation of specific factors in neuropathic‐derived circulating NKs' CM suggests that, despite the observed increasing trend in circulating CD9+ NKs, they are not able to support vascular‐related processes, at least in vitro. Notably, leptin, IL‐8/CXCL‐8, and PDGF‐β‐key mediators of blood vessel development and neovascularization‐ are significantly downregulated in the CM of circulating NKs from the neuropathic subjects. This is consistent with our in vivo angiogenic studies in leeches, which showed impaired angiogenesis and the formation of structurally abnormal, non‐functional blood vessels.

The limited differences observed in vitro in tube network formation likely reflect that the primary impairment associated with neuropathic diabetic circulating NKs' secretome involves vessel maturation and the development of complex vascular structures, processes better recapitulated in vivo. This is consistent with the modulation of factors regulating vessel maturation. Moreover, the effect on HUVECs may be driven by higher levels of inflammation‐related factors in circulating NKs from patients with T2DM and neuropathy, compared to controls.

Dysfunction of NK cells in patients with T2DM and neuropathy appears to contribute to vascular impairment and promote local inflammation by recruiting other immune cells, like macrophages (as we observed in the leech), which in turn may amplify the inflammatory milieu and support the development of disease‐associated damage.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study characterizes the phenotype and functions of circulating NKs in subjects with diabetic neuropathy and provides evidence of their alteration in degranulation capabilities, together with their detrimental effect on the endothelium, via the released soluble factors, in vivo. Our study suggests that detecting the frequency of NK cells in the peripheral blood of T2DM patients could therefore inform the risk of vasculopathy by using a minimally invasive procedure based on a blood sample.

Although this study is limited by its relatively small sample size, and the findings warrant validation in a larger, age‐ and sex‐matched cohort, an important strength is the use of flow cytometry, a widely available laboratory technique that can be performed on an easily accessible clinical sample, namely peripheral blood. This feature supports the potential for rapid translation of our findings into clinical practice.
