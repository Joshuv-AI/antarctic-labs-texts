Three Tone Networks and a Tessellation
Jeffrey R. Boland 1 and Lane P. Hughston 2

Abstract
We show that the Eulerian tonnetz, which associates three minor chords to each major chord and three major chords to each minor chord, can be represented by a bipartite graph with twelve white vertices signifying major chords and twelve black vertices signifying minor chords. This so-called Levi graph uniquely determines the combinatorial geometry of a certain remarkable configuration of twelve points and twelve lines in the real projective plane with the property that three points lie on each line and three lines pass through each point. Interesting features of the tonnetz, such as the existence of Cohn’s four hexatonic cycles, crucial for the understanding of nineteenth-century voice leading and extended harmony, can be read off rather directly as properties of the configuration. We show that analogous tone networks can be constructed for pentatonic music and twelve-tone music. Keywords: Music and mathematics, tonnetz, self-dual configurations, Levi graphs, real projective space, Desargues configuration, pentatonic scale, hexachords, twelve-tone music, Cremona-Richmond configuration.



I
Introduction

We consider three examples of so-called tone networks or
tonnetze
. The first example that we look at is the well-known Eulerian tonnetz which consists of a certain network of relations between the conventional major and minor chords of Western music, introduced in its basic form by Leonhard Euler (1739) and later developed variously in works Gottfried Weber (1824), Ernst Naumann (1858), Arthur von Oettingen (1866), and Hugo Riemann (1902). Investigation of the tonnetz from a modern standpoint in the setting of equal temperament was initiated in the works of Lewin (1982, 1987), Hyer (1989), Cohn (1996, 1997), and others, attracting a great deal of attention. As a second example of a tone network we introduce a version of the tonnetz that arises rather naturally in connection with pentatonic music and shows a surprising richness. Our third example is associated with the use of unordered hexachords as a basis for twelve-tone music.

Our goal is to show an interesting connection between these tone networks and the theory of
configurations
. We look both at abstract ‘combinatorial’ configurations as well as the so-called projective or geometric configurations realized by incidence patterns in an
n
-dimensional projective space formed over a field. We typically have in mind configurations in the two-dimensional projective space formed over the real numbers – the real projective plane
\mathbb{RP}^{2}
– but other fields and other dimensions also enter the discussion.

We presume some familiarity on the part of the reader with the Eulerian tonnetz, and indeed when we refer to ‘the tonnetz’ without further qualification, we always mean the tonnetz of Euler and Riemann – but to establish some terminology and notation we develop the relevant ideas
ab initio
.

We fix the dodecagonic chromatic scale with equal temperament and give the notes their conventional names
C,C_{\sharp}/D_{\flat},D,D_{\sharp}/E_{\flat}
, and so on, making no distinction between enharmonic pairs such as
C_{\sharp}
and
D_{\flat}
. We may favour one notation over the other, depending on the context: for example, we write the
C
-minor chord as
\{C,E_{\flat},G\}
, not as the enharmonically equivalent
B_{\sharp}
-minor chord. If we choose our unit of time such that
C=1
, then
C_{\sharp}=D_{\flat}=2^{1/12}
,
D=2^{1/6}
,
D_{\sharp}=E_{\flat}=2^{1/4}
,
E=2^{1/3}
, and so on. For example, if we assume that
C
is tuned to
256
Hz, then to ensure that
C=1
we choose the unit of time to be
1/256
sec and the frequencies are as given. Note the use of subscripts for sharps and flats.

For simplicity we often write
C
for
C_{4}
(as above) and
C^{\prime}
for
C_{5}
(an octave above) and so on. We drop the explicit specification of the octave to which a note belongs if it is obvious or if it is unimportant. For example, if we introduce a major triad
\{C,E,G\}
and then, in the same context, refer to the relative minor triad
\{A,C,E\}
, then it will be safe to assume that the
C
and the
E
are the same in each chord (typically
C_{4}
and
E_{4}
) and that the
A
is
A_{3}
(the
A
below
C_{4}
). One removes some clutter from the notation if occasionally one is a bit vague about details that become evident in context; this is not to suggest that one is being imprecise – rather, merely that one need not always state the obvious.

We use the term ‘duad’ to signify an unordered pair of distinct notes, for example
(C,E)
. The duad can be represented by either of the ordered pairs
\{C,E\}
and
\{E,C\}
. We use the notation
(C,E,G)
to represent an unordered triad of the notes
C
,
E
and
G
, each of which might be located within any octave. In such a context,
C
denotes an equivalence class of notes modulo multiplicative factors of two. We write
\{C,E,G\}
, with curly brackets to denote an ordered triad
C
,
E
,
G
, in order of increasing pitch, with common sense specification of the octaves within which the various notes appear. Thus, we write
CM=(C,E,G)
for the ‘abstract’
C
-major chord (i.e., the chord of equivalence classes), perhaps with a ‘representative’ triple in mind but without firm commitment to the precise triad. Then, we write
CM=\{C,E,G\}
for an ordered triad, usually with a specific choice of octave in mind for the three notes, dictated by context or common sense. For the first inversion we write
CM^{(1)}=\{E,G,C\}
and for the second inversion
CM^{(2)}=\{G,C,E\}
.
In a situation where the inversions are important, we write
CM^{(0)}=\{C,E,G\}
for the chord in its root position. If we prefer to say exactly which notes are intended, then we can write, for example,
\{C_{4},E_{4},G_{4}\}
for the chord in root position and
CM^{(1)}=\{E_{3},G_{3},C_{4}\}
for the first inversion.
Even if we regard the precise position of the notes as being understood, it should be clear that there are three further permutations in the case of a triad, namely
\{E,C,G\}
,
\{G,E,C\}
and
\{C,G,E\}
, making six different forms for the triad altogether. The three ‘odd’ permutations are not given separate names in music theory textbooks, though clearly from a mathematical perspective they are as important as the three ‘even’ permutations of the chord: and indeed, the ‘colour’ of a chord is sensitive to the permutation of its elements.

The term ‘first inversion’ is often applied to both of the chords with
E
in the bass position, viz.
\{E,G,C\}
and

\{E,C,G\}
; and the term ‘second inversion’ is applied to both
\{G,C,E\}
and
\{G,E,C\}
. Our discussion perhaps overly simplifies the situation, since in practice the inversions will typically occur in the context of four-voice part writing, where the triad, in one of its six possible arrangements, will have one of the voices doubled.
This doubling adds richness to the chord and positions it so that there are more possibilities for transitions.

An example of the first inversion can be found at the opening of the Andante of Beethoven’s Piano Sonata No. 15, Opus 28, ‘Pastoral’; similar use of it is made repeatedly by Wagner in the ‘Wanderer’ motiv appearing in the Prelude to Act 3 of
Siegfried
.

More generally, in the case of four-note chords one has twenty-four permutations of the way in which the four notes can be distributed over the four voices. For example, the
Cm^{\,6}
chord, which takes the form

\{C,E_{\flat},G,A\}
in root position, admits, among its twelve odd permutations, the form
\{A,E_{\flat},G,C\}
. The significance of this particular permutation is more apparent if we transpose the root chord down by a major third to give us an
A_{\flat}m^{\,6}
chord

\{A_{\flat},C_{\flat},E_{\flat},F\}
in root position and its much-discussed odd permutation
\{F,C_{\flat},E_{\flat},A_{\flat}\}
which, in Wagner’s notation

\{F,B,E_{\flat},A_{\flat}\}
we recognize as the ‘Tristan’ chord in the form in which it appears in the opening bars of the eponymous opera. The same chord, at the same pitch levels, appears prominently, played by the left hand, as an arpeggio, fortissimo, in a passage of Chopin’s
G
-minor Ballade, Opus 23, just before the
più animato
, in the enharmonically equivalent representation
\{E_{\sharp},B,D_{\sharp},G_{\sharp}\}
, a permutation of the
G_{\sharp}m^{\,6}
chord. We have digressed on to the consideration of minor sixths to stress the importance of the group of permutations of
n
objects in the formation of chords.

With these notational developments we proceed with our analysis of the tonnetze we study with the theory of configurations. In Section II we show how the Eulerian tonnetz can be characterized by a Levi graph. The result is stated in Proposition 1 and our Levi graph is drawn in Figure 1. We show how the four hexacycles of Cohn can be understood in the setting of the Levi graph and we comment on the role of some of the other cycles that arise naturally in this graph. As an application of our analysis of these cycles we look at the problem of the resolution of Tristan chords in works by Chopin, Wagner and Tchaikovsky.

In Section III we show how the tonnetz can be regarded as a combinatorial configuration. This is achieved by treating the transformations between major and minor chords as incidence relations in a combinatorial geometry, illustrated in Figure 3.

Then in Section IV we show that the tonnetz can be realized as a self-dual configuration of type
\{12_{3}\}
in the real projective plane. In particular, the tonnetz configuration turns out to be a
\{12_{3}\}
of Daublebsky von Sterneck type D222. The result is summarized in Proposition 2 and the configuration is drawn in Figure 4.

In Section V we look at pentatonic music and show in Figures 5, 6, and 7 how a tonnetz for such music can be modelled by use of the Desargues configuration. The associated Levi graph is shown in Figure 8, from which the cycles of the pentatonic system can be deduced.

In Section VI we consider twelve-tone music and show how Sylvester’s theory of duads and synthemes can be used to construct a tonnetz for music based on the specification of an unordered hexachord. The resulting Levi graph is shown in Figure 9 and the associated configuration of type
\{15_{3}\}
in the real projective plane is drawn in Figure 10.

In Section VII we conclude with a return to the analysis of tonal music in the common practice in connection with which we introduce type of tonnetz that admits a Levi graph, shown in Figure 11, which splits into two disjoint components, but for which there is no associated configuration. Instead, we obtain a representation for this tonnetz as a double Archimedean tessellation of the plane with dodecagons, hexagons and squares, for which one component is shown in Figure 12, and we stress the potential of this so-called Archimedean tonnetz as a basis for composition and rhythm.

II
The Tonnetz as a Levi Graph

The tonnetz has been studied extensively in the literature of music and mathematics. It would be out of place to attempt a bibliography, but we mention Hyer (1989) Cohn (1996, 1997, 1998, 2012), Tymoczko (2011, 2012), Catanzaro (2011), Yust (2017) and Rietsch (2024) as examples of work that has been undertaken on the tonnetz in recent decades. When we refer to ‘the tonnetz’ with no other qualification then we mean, of course, the Eulerian tonnetz, which can be constructed as follows. We observe that a major chord can be changed into a minor chord in three different ways by fixing two of the tones in an ordered triad and altering the third. For example, in the case
CM^{(0)}=\{C,E,G\}
, we can lower the
C
by a semitone to obtain
Em^{(2)}=\{B,E,G\}
, the second inversion of the
E
-minor chord. If we lower the
E
by a semitone we obtain
Cm^{(0)}=\{C,E_{\flat},G\}
, the
C
-minor chord in root position. If we raise the
G
by a tone we obtain
Am^{(1)}=\{C,E,A\}
, the first inversion of
A
-minor.
Similarly, a minor chord can be changed into a major chord in three different ways by fixing two of the tones and altering the third. For example, in the case
Cm^{(0)}=\{C,E_{\flat},G\}
, we can lower the
C
by a tone to obtain
E_{\flat}M^{(2)}=\{B_{\flat},E_{\flat},G\}
or we can raise the
E_{\flat}
by a semitone to obtain
CM^{(0)}=\{C,E,G\}
or we can raise the
G
by a semitone to obtain
A_{\flat}M^{(1)}=\{C,E_{\flat},A_{\flat}\}
. These are the only major chords that can be obtained from
Cm
by fixing two of the tones and altering the third.
Then if we project the resulting map onto the system of unordered chords (so no distinction is made between a chord and its inversions) it follows that associated to each unordered major chord there are three unordered minor chords, each sharing two of the pitches of the major chord, and associated to each unordered minor chord there are three unordered major chords, each sharing two of the pitches of the minor chord.
Thus, we obtain a three-to-three map from the set of unordered major chords to the set of unordered minor chords. This map is the tonnetz.

It is easy to see that if we transpose each of the chords up by
k
semitones for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the map remains unchanged. That is, the tonnetz map commutes with transpositions. The tonnetz can be represented by a bipartite graph of the form shown in Figure 1. Each major chord is represented by an open (white) vertex and each minor chord is represented by a closed (black) vertex. Each major chord is connected to the vertices associated with the three minor chords under the tonnetz map and each minor chord is connected to the vertices associated with the three major chords. For example,
CM
is connected to
Em
,
Cm
and
Am
, whereas
Cm
is connected to
E_{\flat}M
,
CM
and
A_{\flat}M
.

Various well-known features of the tonnetz can be read off from this graph and less well-known aspects of the tonnetz also become apparent.
Let us recall some terminology from graph theory (Harary 1969, Tutte 2008). We mainly consider graphs that are finite, simple and undirected. Such a graph is a pair
G=(V(G),E(G))
where
V(G)
is a finite set of elements called
vertices
, and
E(G)
is a subset of the set of unordered pairs of vertices, so

\displaystyle E(G)\subset\{(a,b)\,|\,a,b\in V(G),\,a\neq b\}.

(1)

The elements of
E(G)
are the
edges
of
G
. By this definition at most one such edge joins any two distinct vertices and no vertex is joined to itself. If the choice of
G
is understood, we can simply refer to
V
and
E
. The number of edges joining a vertex is the
degree
of that vertex. The maximum of the degrees of the vertices is the degree of the graph. If all of the vertices have the same degree then the graph is
regular
. If
x\in E
and
x=(a,b)
, where
a
and
b
are in
V
, we say that
a
and
b
are
joined
by
x
and that
x
is
incident
with
a
and
b
. If the edges
x
and
y
are incident with a common vertex, we say that
x
and
y
are adjacent.

We refer to an ordered sequence of
k
distinct consecutively adjacent edges as a
k
-path. A graph is
connected
if every two vertices are joined by some
k
-path. A closed sequence of
k
distinct consecutively adjacent edges that begins at some vertex and eventually comes back to that vertex is called a
k
-cycle.

The minimum value of
k
that a graph admits for some
k
-cycle is the
girth
of the graph. The length of a path is defined to be the number of edges that it contains. If
V
has
N
elements we refer to
N
as the ‘order’ of the graph. If the vertices of a regular graph are of degree
d
, then
dN=2M
, where
M
is the number of edges.

By a
Levi graph
(Levi 1929, 1942, Coxeter 1950) of type
\{n_{r},m_{k}\}
we mean a bipartite graph for which
V=V_{0}\cup V_{1}
with
V_{0}\cap V_{1}=\varnothing
with the property that
V_{0}
consists of
n
‘white’ vertices and
V_{1}
consists of
m
‘black’ vertices in such a way that (i) each edge joins a white vertex and a black vertex, and (ii)
r
edges meet each white vertex and
k
edges meet each black vertex. In the case of a Levi graph we have

\displaystyle E\subset\{(a,b)\,|\,a\in V_{0},\,b\in V_{1}\}.

(2)

With this terminology at hand we gather what we have shown so far with a statement of the following:

Proposition 1
.

The tonnetz can be represented by a Levi graph of type
\{12_{3},12_{3}\}
. The twelve major chords are represented by white vertices and the twelve minor chords are represented by black vertices. The thirty-six edges of the graph are arranged such that each white vertex is connected to three black vertices and each black vertex is connected to three white vertices.

It should be apparent by inspection of Figure 1 that the girth of the tonnetz is six. The associated minimal hexacycles can be classified by the number of major/minor crossings; that is, by parallel links such as
[CM,Cm]
. Figure 1 shows that there are exactly four hexacycles, each admitting three such parallel crossings.

These are as follows:

\displaystyle[CM,Cm,A_{\flat}M,G_{\sharp}m,EM,Em,CM],\hskip 14.22636pt[FM,Fm,D_{\flat}M,C_{\sharp}m,AM,Am,FM],\hskip 14.22636pt

\displaystyle[B_{\flat}M,B_{\flat}m,G_{\flat}M,F_{\sharp}m,DM,Dm,B_{\flat}M],\hskip 14.22636pt[E_{\flat}M,E_{\flat}m,BM,Bm,GM,Gm,E_{\flat}M].

(3)

Here, when we write out a sequence of chords forming a cycle, we find it convenient to repeat the initial chord at the end of the sequence to show how it closes up. It is a matter of convention which chord we take to be the ‘initial’ element of such a cycle. One recognizes these cycles as the four so-called
hexatonic sequences
arising in neo-Riemannian theories of post-tonal music (Cohn 1998, 2012).

Each such sequence contains three distinct parallel transformations represented by moving across the graph of the tonnetz. We call these sequences
thrice-parallel
, abbreviated
3p
. One of the advantages of the use of a Levi graph for the tonnetz configuration is that it allows one to see very clearly the symmetries of the four
3p
-hexatonic cycles and how they exhaust the tonnetz.

It is reasonable to ask if there are any other hexatonic cycles in the Levi graph of the tonnetz apart from the four cycles just considered and interestingly the answer is affirmative. In fact, there are twelve further hexatonic cycles, one beginning (say) at each major chord, of which a typical example is

\displaystyle[CM,Am,FM,Fm,A_{\flat}M,Cm,CM].

(4)

Note that this cycle includes only
two
parallel transformations – viz., from
CM
to
Cm
and from
FM
to
Fm
. The twelve
2p
-hexatonic cycles that we have just introduced are therefore distinct from the four
3p
-hexatonic cycles that we looked at earlier.

The general pattern of a
2p
-hexatonic cycle can be understood as follows. Beginning at the chord
CM
the cycle moves two steps along the graph to
FM
; then it crosses the graph with a parallel transformation to
Fm
; then it moves counter-clockwise to
Cm
, and finally back to
CM
with another parallel transformation.

The remaining eleven
2p
-hexatonic cycles can be obtained by moving the initial point around in a circle of fourths – that is, from
CM
to
FM
to
B_{\flat}M
and so on. In this way we obtain twelve overlapping
2p
-hexatonic cycles.

We have observed that a
2p
-hexatonic cycle contains precisely two major/minor pairs, such as
CM/Cm
and
FM/Fm
in the case of (
4
). In such a pair, the two chords differ from one another in the root position by a semitone in the second note of the chord.

It is interesting to note that the remaining two chords in a
2p
-hexatonic cycle differ from one another by a semitone in the first and third notes of the chord. Thus, in the case of (
4
) we see that
Am
and
A_{\flat}m
differ by semitones in the first and third notes.
One can check that each triad, major and minor, belongs to three different
2p
hexacycles.

Thus, for example, the
CM
chord belongs to the
2p
hexacycles beginning at
CM
,
EM
and
GM
; whereas the
Cm
chord belongs to the
2p
hexacycles beginning at
CM
,
E_{\flat}M
and
GM
. A similar pattern is applicable to other major and minor chords.

That the twelve
2p
hexacycles overlap in this way adds a depth of complexity to the scheme which may at first glance seem formidable but on reflection adds interest in a way that contrasts with the segregational sanitation of the four disjoint
3p
-hexatonic cycles, hence allowing for architectural modalities.

As an example, we mention the possibility of surgical insertion of one cycle within another. Suppose we consider the cycle (
4
) alongside the modulated sequence lying a fourth above it,

\displaystyle[FM,Dm,B_{\flat}M,B_{\flat}m,D_{\flat}M,Fm],

(5)

here truncated at
Fm
. Since the short sequence
[FM,Fm]
appears as a subsequence of the cycle (
4
), we can ‘snip’ the loop (
5
) at
FM
, splicing in the string (
5
)
at the juncture
[FM,Fm]
in (
4
), in this way creating a longer cycle

\displaystyle[CM,Am,FM,Dm,B_{\flat}M,B_{\flat}m,D_{\flat}M,Fm,A_{\flat}M,Cm,CM].

(6)

The result is not displeasing and is immediately suggestive of a new manner of composition. For example, the procedure can be iterated, allowing one to splice in the sequence

\displaystyle[B_{\flat}M,Gm,E_{\flat}M,E_{\flat}m,G_{\flat}M,B_{\flat}m,B_{\flat}M]

(7)

by a similar snip-and-insert technique, leading to the still longer cycle

\displaystyle[CM,Am,FM,Dm,B_{\flat}M,Gm,E_{\flat}M,E_{\flat}m,G_{\flat}M,B_{\flat}m,D_{\flat}M,Fm,A_{\flat}M,Cm,CM].

(8)

We observe, finally, that beginning at the chord
CM
, the progression moving clockwise around the graph involves a sequence of ‘leading tone’ elevations. More precisely, from the initial chord
CM^{(0)}=\{C,E,G\}
we move to
Am^{(2)}=\{C,E,A\}
by raising the
G
to an
A
. Then we convert
Am^{(2)}
to
Am^{(0)}
by an action of the permutation group and perform another leading tone elevation, taking us from
\{A,C,E\}
to
\{A,C,F\}
by raising the note
E
to an
F
. Then, converting the
FM^{(2)}
to
FM^{(0)}
by use of the permutation group and performing another leading tone elevation by raising the
C
to a
D
, we get to
Dm^{(2)}
.

In this way, we march from node to node through all twenty-four major and minor chords, giving us a Hamiltonian cycle, i.e., a path that includes every vertex of the graph without repetitions. As Tymoczko (2012) remarks, a Hamiltonian path touches on all the vertices in a graph without passing through any of them twice, and is in that sense a generalization of the twelve-tone row.

as well as the example of the
2p
-decatonic ‘floppy bow-tie’ cycle

[CM,Am,FM,Dm,B_{\flat}M,B_{\flat}m,D_{\flat}M,Fm,A_{\flat}M,Cm,CM]
.

Numerous examples of the use of melodic and harmonic structures based on hexatonic cycles by composers of the eighteenth, nineteenth and early twentieth centuries have been noted (Cohn 2012) in well-known works by composers such as Mozart, Beethoven, Schubert, Robert Schumann, Chopin, Wagner, Liszt, Bruckner, Mahler and Richard Strauss. Such use is not immediately obvious to the classically trained ear since the progressions associated with hexatonic cycles do not form part of the training in basic harmony and counterpoint taught in everyday music theory. Nor would the composers listed above have been consciously aware of their own use of the structural determinants implicit in the various cycles of the tonnetz. It should be emphasized, in particular, that there is no reason
a priori
why such use would be confined to the
3p
hexacycles alone, important as these are. If the unconscious mind will tap the resources of the mathematical structures embedded in the geometry of the tonnezt then it will seek to avail itself of the full richness of these resources.

The
3p
hexacycles can be studied in the analysis of music alongside our more numerous
2p
hexacyles, the
4p
octacyles, the
2p
decacyles, and other cycles. As an example, let us return to the problem of understanding the resolution of the Tristan chord. Wagner offers a well known ‘tentative’ resolution of his permuted
G_{\sharp}m^{\,6}
into an
EM^{\,7}
. Chopin, on the other hand, resolves the chord into a
B_{\flat}M^{\,7}
. Chopin’s resolution, in particular, is definitive: it brings that lengthy first section of the piece to a decisive close, and paves the way forward at the
più animato
for a change of key and the introduction of new material and a new atmosphere. Wagner’s resolution is inconclusive, in line with the ever shifting sensibilities of the protagonists.

A glance at Figure 1 is revealing. Chopin’s resolution takes the initial
G_{\sharp}m
chord along the path of a
2p
decatonic cycle to its polar opposite
B_{\flat}M
. Thus, from
G_{\sharp}m
we follow the ‘floppy bow-tie’ trajectory
to
BM
, then to
E_{\flat}m
, then across to
E_{\flat}M
, then up to
Gm
, and finally up to
B_{\flat}M
at the opposite side of the decagon.
Somehow, this arrival at the polar opposite gives this resolution its firmness.

The situation with Wagner’s ambivalent resolution is much less conclusive:
he merely moves the chord one step counter-clockwise along the unique 3
p
-hexatonic cycle to which both chords belong from
G_{\sharp}m
to
EM
. The simplicity of this transformation is also suggestive of why its character is so unsettled: nothing has really changed. One is merely moving sideways, from indecisiveness to indecisiveness.

It is interesting to take this analysis a step further. Another example of the resolution of a Tristan-like chord can be found in the Finale ‘Adagio Lamentoso’ of Tchaikovsky’s Symphony No. 6 in B-Minor, Opus 74, ‘Pathetique’. Tchaikovsky had been studying Wagner’s works intensively and evidently wished to make use of the harmonic material in
Tristan und Isolde
, without making a direct quotation. His modified version of the chord, heard forte in the strings at the outset of the Lamentoso is memorable, carrying the full force of Wagner’s permutation. Recall that Wagner’s chord is
[F,B,E_{\flat},A_{\flat}]
, whereas Tchaikovsky, in keeping with the overall key of his symphony uses a
Bm^{6}
chord, in the permutation
[D,G_{\sharp},B,F_{\sharp}]
. Tchaikovsky’s chord resolves into an
F_{\sharp}M^{\,7}
. If we transpose this down three semitones to facilitate comparison with Wagner and Chopin the result is a modified Tristan chord

[B,F,A_{\flat},E_{\flat}]
which resolves into an
E_{\flat}M^{\,7}
. Thus, he swaps around the two lower voices and the two upper voices of the Tristan chord and the resolution is more like that of Chopin than that of Wagner, only leading to
E_{\flat}M
rather than
B_{\flat}M
.

Hence, as in the case of Chopin, there is a sense of definition in Tchaikovsky’s resolution; but there is no joy in it, this resolution has more the character of a grim facing up to reality taken with reluctant finality. Of course, we are speaking of the sense of emotion that Tchaikovsky manages to create in the opening measures of the last movement. But how is this achieved? It is by means of a
2p
hexacycle. Beginning at
G_{\sharp}m
in its guise as a permuted
G_{\sharp}m^{\,6}
(for convenience, we stick with the transposition into Wagner’s pitch classes) there is a ‘straight bow-tie’
2p
hexacycle that takes one across to
A_{\flat}M
then up to
Cm
then finally to
E_{\flat}M
.
As in the case of Chopin’s resolution, following through the hexcycle to the polar opposite position contributes to the sense of completion. But in the case of a
2p
hexacycle, less distance is traversed across the tonnetz than in Chopin’s example. Tchaikovsky’s resolution into
E_{\flat}M
has a more perfunctory character, that of a plagal cadence, and hence its desolation. It is interesting then to note, by way of contrast, that in the
G
-minor Ballade it is the passage
leading up
to the Tristan chord that is in the key of
E_{\flat}M
(compare this with the tonnetz analysis of Chopin’s Opus 23 undertaken in Cohn 2012 at pp. 96-98).

The octatonic cycles that we mentioned earlier are of some interest in their own right on account of their relationship to so-called octatonic scales. An octatonic scale is defined to be a scale with eight notes in it, as opposed to the usual seven notes of the conventional major and minor scales, with the property that adjacent notes within the scale differ either by a semitone or a tone, alternating the gap at each step.

One construction of such scales is as follows. We recall that there are three diminished seventh chords, each composed of four notes. If we form the union of two of these sets of four notes, then this can be done in three different ways, leading to three distinct sets of eight notes. Once these are ordered by frequency, we obtain three distinct octatonic scales. The three diminished seventh chords are
X=[C,E_{\flat},G_{\flat},A]
,
Y=[D_{\flat},E,G,B_{\flat}]
, and
Z=[D,F,A_{\flat},B]
. These sets are obviously disjoint. Then by taking the three distinct unions of pairs we obtain the three scales

\displaystyle\hskip 8.5359ptO_{12}=X\cup Y=C,D_{\flat},E_{\flat},E,G_{\flat},G,A,B_{\flat},

(9)

\displaystyle O_{23}=Y\cup Z=D_{\flat},D,E,F,G,A_{\flat},B_{\flat},B

(10)

\displaystyle O_{31}=Z\cup X=D,E_{\flat},F,G_{\flat},A_{\flat},A,B,C.

(11)

Any octatonic scale, starting at any chosen note, belongs to one of these three types, possibly after a cyclic permutation of its elements. Octatonic scales have been used to good effect by a number of well-known composers, among them Rimsky-Korsakov, Scriabin, Ravel, Stravinsky and Messiaen – and the use of these scales can be found in the works of earlier musicians as well, at least in passing (Messiaen 1956, Dallin 1964). The relation with the tonnetz is as follows.

Four major chords and four minor chords can be constructed from the constituents of an octatonic scale. For example, from
O_{12}
one constructs
CM
,
Am
,
AM
,
F_{\sharp}m
,
G_{\flat}M
,
E_{\flat}m
,
E_{\flat}M
, and

. But these are the chords of the octatonic cycle starting at
CM
in the tonnetz (see the lower left quadrant of Figure 2). One can easily check that the other two octatonic cycles of the tonnetz can be constructed similarly from the other two octatonic scales. It is interesting to note that the octatonic cycles are intimately tied to the three diminished seventh chords. In the same spirit, it can be remarked that the four
3p
-hexatonic sequences are intimately tied to the four augmented triads. By taking the six possible unions of the four augmented triads we obtain six distinct hexachords. From four of these, one can construct the four
3p
-hexatonic sequences. The remaining two anomalous hexachords are of interest as well – they contain the two standard disjoint whole-tone scales, each of which contains six notes. These are

\displaystyle W_{1}=C,D,E,G_{\flat},A_{\flat},B_{\flat},\hskip 5.69046ptW_{2}=D{\flat},E{\flat},F,G,A,B.

(12)

These beautiful arrays of six equally spaced whole notes have been exploited by Bartok, Berg, Borodin, Debussy, Janacek, Liszt, Puccini, Rimsky-Korsakov, and many other composers.

III
The Tonnetz as a Combinatorial Configuration

If we accept the widely-held view that Euler’s tonnetz is not a mere musico-mathematical curiosity but is in some sense fundamental to the structure of music (specifically, to that of the Western common practice), then it makes sense to look at the mathematics of the tonnetz from every angle – combinatoric, group-theoretic, number-theoretic, set-theoretic, and geometric – for any insights that might be gained.

One key concept, lying on a nexus of the various branches of mathematics mentioned above, is the idea of a
configuration
. By a configuration, in the broadest sense, by which we mean combinatorial, we mean a disjoint pair of finite sets
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{L}
, whose elements we call
points
and
lines
respectively, satisfying certain conditions. We assume that
\mathbb{P}
and
\mathbb{L}
are equipped with an incidence relation

\displaystyle R:\mathbb{P}\times\mathbb{L}\to\{0,1\}

(13)

such that for any
p\in\mathbb{P}
and any
L\in\mathbb{L}
either
R(p,L)=1
, in which case we say that
p
and
L
are
incident
, or
R(p,L)=0
, in which case we say that they are not incident. To aid intuition, we often use geometrical terms such as ‘
p
lies on
L
’ or ‘the lines
L
and
M
intersect at the point
p
’ to express incidence relations, though the use of such language is purely for convenience; and likewise the terms ‘line’ and ‘point’ have no
a priori
geometrical meaning. We assume further that two distinct lines intersect at most once and that two distinct points lie on at most one line in common; that is to say, if
L,M\in\mathbb{L}
, then
\exists
at most one
p\in\mathbb{P}
such that
R(p,L)=1
and
R(p,M)=1
; and if
p,q\in\mathbb{P}
, then
\exists
at most one
L\in\mathbb{L}
such that
R(p,L)=1
and
R(q,L)=1
.

We refer to a triple
\{\mathbb{P},\mathbb{L},R\}
satisfying these conditions as an
incidence structure
.
By a configuration of type
\{m_{r},n_{k}\}
for
m,n,r,k\in\mathbb{N}
we mean an incidence structure with
m
points and
n
lines such that
k
points lie on each line and
r
lines go through each point. We use the term
type
here since in some cases several different configurations of the same type may exist. Examples will follow shortly.

For some purposes it turns out to be useful to introduce the concept of a
flag
, by which we mean a pair
\{p,L\}
where
p\in\mathbb{P}
and
L\in\mathbb{L}
with the property that
R(p,L)=1
. One then easily sees that for any
\{m_{r},n_{k}\}
we have

\displaystyle mr=nk,

(14)

since each side of this equation gives a way of counting the total number of distinct flags in the configuration. The fundamental inequality

\displaystyle m\geq r(k-1)+1,

(15)

then follows, since the total number of points must be no less than the total number of points lying on any given set of flags sharing a point in common. It should be clear that if
k=2
then the resulting configuration satisfies the definition of a regular graph, since each point of the configuration can be regarded as a vertex and each line can be regarded as an edge. Each such edge then joins two vertices and each vertex is met by
r
edges.

If the number of lines equals the number of points in a configuration then by (
14
) the number of points on each line must equal the number of lines through each point. In that situation we say that the configuration is ‘symmetric’ (or ‘balanced’, in the terminology of Grünbaum 2009). Since symmetric configurations are of interest and are frequently studied, the abbreviation
\{m_{k}\}
is used to denote a configuration of type
\{m_{k},m_{k}\}
.

In particular, the situation for configurations of the form
\{m_{3}\}
has attracted much attention over the years and the combinatorics of such configurations have been investigated at length. The inequality (
15
) shows that no
\{m_{3}\}
configurations exist for
m\leq 6
.

It can be interesting to take stock of some of the configurations that exist for higher values of
m
. There is a unique configuration of type
\{7_{3}\}
, which can be modelled by the combinatoric geometry of the seven points and seven lines of the projective plane based on the finite field
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
. This is the so-called Fano plane. There is a unique combinatorial configuration of type
\{8_{3}\}
, which can be modelled by a system of eight points and eight lines in the complex projective plane
\mathbb{CP}^{2}
. No real coordinatization of
\{8_{3}\}
exists. The situation is more complicated for higher values of
m
. There are three distinct
\{9_{3}\}
combinatorial configurations, all of which can be modelled as systems of points and lines in the real projective plane
\mathbb{RP}^{2}
. One of these is the so-called Pappus configuration, the existence of which can even be regarded as a axiom (Hodge & Pedoe 1947).

In fact, all three of the
\{9_{3}\}
configurations can be modelled projectively over
\mathbb{Q}
, the rational numbers. There are ten distinct
\{10_{3}\}
combinatorial configurations, nine of which can be modelled as geometric configurations in
\mathbb{RP}^{2}
and indeed are rationally realizable. Among these is the Desargues configuration, about which we have more to say later. But one of the
\{10_{3}\}
combinatorial configurations admits no projective representation at all in the usual sense of projective geometries based on a (commutative) field.

This is the point at which it becomes evident that the realizability of a combinatorial configuration as a projective configuration is a nontrivial property that may or may not hold. The 31 distinct
\{11_{3}\}
configurations were identified by Daublebsky von Sterneck (1894), all of which can be realized over the real numbers, that is to say, as geometric configurations in the real projective plane. The situation with
\{12_{3}\}
is not completely straightforward.
Daublebsky von Sterneck (1895) claimed the existence of 228 distinct configurations of this type and this figure was accepted by Sturmfels & White (1990).

But later it was shown (Gropp 1993, 1997) that one crucial case was missing from Daublebsky von Sterneck’s list: hence, in fact, the number of
\{12_{3}\}
combinatorial configurations is 229. Sturmfels & White (1990) were investigating the conjecture of Grünbaum that every
\{m_{3}\}
that can be realized over the reals can be realized over the rationals. They established by use of diophantine computations that all the

\{11_{3}\}
and
\{12_{3}\}
cases considered by Daublebsky von Sterneck were realizable over
\mathbb{Q}
and later it was recognized that the remaining configuration is also realizable over
the rationals.

Now, the significance of Levi graphs is their one to one correspondence with combinatorial configurations. More precisely, as Grünbaum 2009 states, a simple bipartite graph
G
is the Levi graph
L(C)
of an
\{m_{r},n_{k}\}
configuration
C
if and only if (a) all the white vertices are
r
-valent and all the black vertices are
k
-valent; (b)
G
has girth at least six, that is, all cycles in
G
have length at least six. It is therefore logical to ask if the Eulerian tonnetz admits a representation as a combinatorial configuration. The answer is affirmative.

Thus we learn that the geometers already had the mathematical structure of the tonnetz in their hands at the time of Hugo Riemann, even if they did not perceive its significance. IV
The Tonnetz as a Projective Configuration

We have seen that the tonnetz is a combinatorial configuration of type
\{12_{3}\}
. It follows then from the work of Daublebsky von Sterneck (1896), Sturmfels & White (1990) and others (see, for example, Alazemi & Betten 2014) that the tonnetz admits a realization as a geometric configuration in the real projective plane.
To see this in more detail, let us recall a few details of the relevant background in projective geometry.
We begin by defining the real projective space of dimension
n
.

This space is obtained by taking
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\backslash\{0\}
with coordinates
X^{\alpha},\alpha=1,.\,.\,.\,,n
, and forming the quotient under the equivalence relation
X^{\alpha}\sim\lambda X^{\alpha},\lambda\neq 0
. Thus each point of
\mathbb{RP}^{n}
corresponds to a line through the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. By a
projective line

L
in
\mathbb{RP}^{n}
we mean the linear space spanned by a pair of lines through the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, modulo the equivalence relation. By a
projective plane
we mean the linear space spanned by three lines through the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, modulo the equivalence relation.

A similar definition can be given for higher dimensional linear subspaces of
\mathbb{RP}^{n}
. When the term ‘projective’ is understood from context (as it often is) then it can be dropped, so we refer to a ‘line’ in
\mathbb{RP}^{n}
when we mean a projective line.

If a point
p
belongs to a line
L
, then we say that
p
lies on
L
and that
L
passes through
p
. Equivalently, we say that
p
and
L
are incident.
The terminology is rather loose in this respect, though never imprecise. The goal rather is to choose the language in such a way as to conjure the most vivid picture of the mathematical objects under discussion.

If
p
and
q
are distinct points and if both lie on
L
then we say that
L
joins
p
and
q
or that
L
is the join of
p
and
q
. If two distinct lines
L
and
M
have a point
p
in common, we say
L
intersects (or meets)
M
at the point
p
. If three distinct points lie on the same line, we say that they are collinear. If three lines intersect at a common point, we say they are concurrent.

In much of what follows we shall be concerned with the real projective plane
\mathbb{RP}^{2}
. By a
triangle
, we mean a set of three points
a,b,c
and three lines
A,B,C
in
\mathbb{RP}^{2}
such that
A\cap B=c
,
B\cap C=a
and
A\cap C=b
. We call
A,B,C
the edges of the triangle and
a,b,c
the vertices. Similarly, by a quadrangle in
\mathbb{RP}^{2}
we mean four points
p_{1},p_{2},p_{3},p_{4}
and four lines
L_{12},L_{23},L_{34},L_{41}
such that
p_{1}p_{2}=L_{12}
,
p_{2}p_{3}=L_{23}
,
p_{3}p_{4}=L_{34}
, and
p_{4}p_{1}=L_{41}
. Here
p_{1}p_{2}=L_{12}
means that the line
L_{12}
is the join of
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
; then
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
lie on
L_{12}
.

By a configuration
\{m_{r},n_{k}\}
in the real projective plane, we mean a collection of
m
points and
n
lines such that
r
of the
n
lines are incident with each point and
k
of the
m
points lie on each line. For example, the triangle is a configuration of type

\{3_{2},3_{2}\}
comprising three points and three lines, with two of the points on each line and two of the lines through each point. The quadrangle
\{4_{2},4_{2}\}
, the pentangle
\{5_{2},5_{2}\}
, and the hexagon
\{6_{2},6_{2}\}
can be defined similarly.

We also have the notion of a ‘complete’ quadrangle, which is a configuration that consists of four points in general position and six lines, where the six lines are the six ways of joining the four points. The complete quadrangle is evidently a configuration of type
\{4_{3},6_{2}\}
, since two points lie on each line and three lines pass through each point. By ‘general position’
\mathbb{RP}^{2}
in we mean that no three points lie on a line. Similarly, the complete pentangle, which consists of five points and the ten lines joining them in pairs, is a configuration of type
\{5_{4},10_{2}\}
, concerning which we have more to say in the next section.

Alternatively, we can consider a set of four lines in general position, by which we mean that no more than two of the lines pass through any point. Then the lines will admit a set of six intersections, and we refer to the resulting configuration
\{6_{2},4_{3}\}
is called a ‘complete quadrilateral’. Three points lie on each line and two lines pass through each of the six points. It is easy to see that the complete quadrangle and the complete quadrilateral are ‘dual’ to each other, in the sense that the roles of the lines and the points are interchanged. The triangle, on the other hand, is ‘self-dual’ in the sense that if we interchange the roles of lines and points then we get another triangle. Clearly, in the case of a self-dual configuration the number of points must be equal to the number of lines and the number of lines through each point must be the same as the number of points on each line.

.

Here we have a good illustration of the principle (Coxeter 1950, p 419) that, ‘interesting configurations are represented by interesting graphs’.

V
Pentatonic Tone Network

Musical keys and modulations do appear to arouse certain respondent moods or emotions. But is this, primarily, a matter of historical convention, of schooled expectation? What makes a minor third ‘sad’? Is G-minor, in the Western scale, intrinsically
triste
(and just what could such a statement signify?), or does its desolation stem from the use Mozart made of it in his great Quintet K 516? What of the reflexes of sensibility motivated by keys, pitch, chordal blocks in non-Western tonic systems? Is a pentatonic structure any less universal than ours?
—George Steiner (1997),
Errata

According to Piston (1985), ‘The pentatonic scale, whose intervallic pattern corresponds to that of the black keys on the piano, is very ancient, having been used in the music of Oriental cultures perhaps longer even than the diatonic scale in the West. It is also the scale used in many European folksongs, particularly those of the British Isles.’ Examples of the use of pentatonic scales can also be found in the works of many of the well-known modern Western composers, including Bartok, Chopin, Debussy, Mahler, Ravel, and Stravinsky, to name but a few. Could there be an analogue of the tonnetz based on the pentatonic scale? Although some bare rudiments of the system of major and minor chords can be glimpsed in the pentatonic system, nothing like the rich dodecaphonic system of Western music exists, so it is not easy to imagine what form the pentatonic tonnetz might take.

The theory of configurations suggests a way forward. If we have a system of music based on five tones, what kind of combinatorial geometry can be constructed? A hint comes from the work of Cayley (1846) who observed that five points in general position in
\mathbb{RP}^{3}
determine ten lines and ten planes that meet a generic plane in a Desargues configuration of ten points and ten lines such that three points lie on each line and three lines pass through each point (Coxeter 1950). Thus we obtain a
\{10_{3}\}
which we can take as our pentatonic tone network.

The fact that the Desargues configuration exists in the rational projective plane and that the relevant Diophantine problem has a solution hints at the deep connection this configuration admits with the theory of numbers.

The five tones of the pentatonic scale yield ten two-note ‘major’ clusters and ten three-note ‘minor’ clusters. VI
Remarks on the Twelve-Tone System

The twelve-tone system promoted by Arnold Schönberg, Anton Webern, Alban Berg and their followers in the Second Viennese School provided an approach to composition that was both disciplined and flexible and in the right hands capable capable of embodying a full range of musical expression. Yet, apart from the general attributes of intelligence and musicality held in common with other such works of art, the character of the music thus produced is so different from that of the preceding generations that, even if one allows for the extremes of extended tonality, we are tempted to speculate that fundamentally different structural principles are in operation. Could it be that another tone network is involved?

The role of unordered hexachords in twelve-tone music has long been recognized. For example, Lewin (1968) approaches the structure of Schönberg’s Violin Fantasy with a study based on the unordered hexachord
[F,G,A,B_{\flat},B,C_{\sharp}]
and its complement
[C,D,D_{\sharp},E,F_{\sharp},G_{\sharp}]
. It is not unreasonable then to take the view that at the very outset of any approach to the theory of twelve-tone music the combinatorics of such unordered sets should be investigated in the spirit we have pursued the Eulerian and pentatonic tonnetze.

We fix a hexachord
H_{0}
– that is, an unordered set of six distinct tones from the chromatic scale with equal temperament, with the identification of octave equivalents – and label its elements with a set of numbers
[1,2,3,4,5,6]
. These numbers are not, of course, the pitch classes on the six tones; their role is purely combinatoric. For example, in the first hexachord mentioned above, we set
1=F
,
2=G
,
3=A
,
4=B_{\flat}
,
5=B
,
6=C_{\sharp}
. At first glance, such an assignment might seem a bit structureless; but the permutation group for six objects is rather special, and the highly nontrivial structure of this group is directly related to the particular type of tone network that we wish to propose for twelve-tone music.

The method that we consider is based on Sylvester’s theory of
duads
and
synthemes
(Sylvester 1844, 1861). By a duad, we mean an unordered pair of distinct tones taken from the given hexachord.
A little thought shows there are fifteen duads. Thus, a duad
D
is an unordered set of the form

\displaystyle D=\{(i,j):i,j\in H_{0},\,i\neq j\}.

(16)

By a syntheme we mean an unordered triple of duads taken in such a way that each element of
H_{0}
appears exactly once as one of the elements of the three duads. Hence, a syntheme is a set of the form

\displaystyle S=\{(i,j),(k,l),(m,n):i,j,k,l,m,n\in H_{0},\,i\neq j\neq k\neq l\neq m\neq n\}.

(17)

In fact, there are fifteen synthemes altogether. Now, each syntheme contains three duads. But it is also the case that each duad is contained in three distinct synthemes. In particular, the duad
(i,j)
is contained in three distinct synthemes, namely
\{(i,j),(k,l),(m,n)\}
,
\{(i,j),(k,m),(n,l)\}
and
\{(i,j),(k,n),(l,m)\}
, where
i\neq j\neq.\,.\,.\neq n
. For example, the syntheme
\{(1,2),(3,4),(5,6)\}
contains the duads
(1,2
),
(3,4)
,
(5,6)
; and the duad
(1,2)
is contained in the synthemes
\{(1,2),(3,4),(5,6)\}
,
\{(1,2),(3,5),(4,6)\}
,
\{(1,2),(3,6),(4,5)\}
.

Thus one sees that we have constructed a combinatoric configuration. More precisely, we have two sets, viz., the set of duads and the set of synthemes. Each has fifteen elements and they are in three-to-three correspondence. We say that the duad
D
is incident with the syntheme
S
if it is among the three duads contained in
S
. One can check that two synthemes have at most one duad in common. See Tables I and II. This gives us an incidence structure and we can construct the corresponding Levi graph.

In fact, there is one more layer of structure, which comes as a surprise, and is of great importance, and this is the following. Suppose we consider a collection of five synthemes with the property that each duad appears exactly once. This makes sense since there are fifteen duads, and a collection of five synthemes must include fifteen duads. Such a collection of five ‘non-overlapping’ synthemes is called a pentad. It turns out that there are exactly six such pentads that can be constructed.

VII
Summary and Conclusion

We have shown that the Eulerian tonnetz can be represented by a Levi graph with twelve white vertices and twelve black vertices. Each white vertex of this bipartite graph abuts three black vertices; each black vertex abuts three white vertices. The white vertices represent major chords; the black vertices represent minor chords. We have established the existence of a self-dual geometric configuration known as a
\{12_{3}\}
of type D
222
, with the Levi graph, comprising twelve points and twelve lines in two-dimensional real projective space. This geometric configuration in projective space matches the combinatorial configuration of the Eulerian tonnetz.

One can ask if other geometric configurations give rise to interesting tonal configurations. We have shown that the system of ten lines and ten points in
\mathbb{RP}^{2}
occurring in the Desargues theorem arises naturally in the consideration of pentatonic music. In particular, if a scale is based on five tones, there is a three-to-three map between the set of unordered two-tone chords (duads) and the set of unordered three-tone chords (triads). This system of interlocking chord relations can be modelled geometrically in
\mathbb{RP}^{2}
by a
\{10_{3}\}
known as the Desargues configuration.

In the context of the twelve-tone system of Schönberg and the Second Viennese School, it is natural to consider so-called ‘hexachords’ – unordered collections of six tones – as a basis for the analysis and composition of music. Beginning with a hexachord, one can construct a system comprising fifteen duads (unordered pairs of tones) and fifteen synthemes (unordered triples of non-overlapping duads). These can be represented geometrically by a
\{15_{3}\}
configuration comprising fifteen lines and fifteen points in
\mathbb{RP}^{2}
. The associated Levi graph is again suggestive of a host of musical structures.

In much of modern literature of the tonnetz, great emphasis has been placed on the role of this structure in understanding
so-called voice leading in the analysis of music. Although its significance in this respect is undeniable, one wonders if this has been at the expense bypassing the consideration of other structure which is clearly there in the mathematics and hence ought to play a similar role in the associated soundscape. We have given the examples of the
2p
-hexatonic cycles, the
2p
-octatonic cycles, and the
3p
-decatonic cycles, whose role alongside of the four well known
3p
hexacycles, may be of the same level of significance as that of that austere quartet. To emphasize the point, we give another example of a tone network that can be constructed by purely combinatorial arguments and yet admits a geometric representation that is not unpleasing.

Again, we consider the sets of major and minor chords and we construct another map between these sets, this time of a somewhat different character. It seems natural that given a major chord one might ask for all the minor chords that can be constructed from it by holding one note fixed and altering the other two. The spirit of this construction is rather similar to that of the original tonnetz and hence does not require elaborate justification. Thus (as a brief exercise in musical combinatorics will verify), given
CM
we can construct three minor chords differing from it at two of the tones – namely,
Fm
,
Gm
, and
C_{\sharp}m
. This is clearly an interesting result: a pair of chords that are harmonically and even diatonically rather close to the original, and one quite remote.

Similarly, if we are given
Cm
, then we can construct three major chords differing from it at two of the tones – namely,
FM
,
GM
, and
Bm
. Once more, one sees that two of the chords that result under this map are diatonically related to the original, and one is remote.
Interestingly, under the new tonnetz the major and minor chords split into two distinct classes. More precisely, the associated bipartite graph is not connected, but rather is given by the union of two distinct connected components. Thus we obtain two sets of twelve chords, each containing six major chords and six minor chords, shown in Figures 11. Since the presence of any one chord in one of these Archimedean tonnetze determines the rest, we can without loss of generality call the first one the
C
-major tonnetz and the other one the
C
-minor diatonic tonnetz.

As with the other tonnetze that we have considered one can read off the resulting cycles rather directly from the Levi graph.There are hexacycles, octacylces, and dodecacycles – but there are also quadracycles in this case.
The significance of the quadracycles is that these graphs are not the graphs of configurations, for we recall that in order for a bipartite graph to be the Levi graph of a configuration it must be of girth at least six, which has indeed been the case in the previous examples that we have considered.

But as always in mathematics, when one is working with an interesting category of structures and one makes some alteration in the assumptions, if one property is lost, then another may well be gained. In present case, if we lay out identical copies of the dodecagons in a lattice and connect adjacent vertices across the lattice (rather than across the dodecagon), what emerges is a tessellation of the plane, composed of decagons, hexagons, and squares. Each dodecagon is surrounded by six hexagons and six squares in a remarkable alternating pattern known in the mathematical literature as an example of an Archimedean tessellation (Grünbaum & Shephard 1977, Conway, Burgiel & Goodman-Strauss 2008).

The musical possibilities that this tessellation generates are abundant because of the rhythmic possibilities implicit in the movements along the multitude of cycles that can be realized as one explores the space of this tonnetz.
