Structured covariance estimation via tensor-train decomposition
Artsiom Patarusau, Nikita Puchkin, Maxim Rakhuba, Fedor Noskov

Abstract
We consider a problem of covariance estimation from a sample of i.i.d. high-dimensional random vectors. To avoid the curse of dimensionality, we impose an additional assumption on the structure of the covariance matrix Σ \Sigma . To be more precise, we study the case when Σ \Sigma can be approximated by a sum of double Kronecker products of smaller matrices in a tensor train (TT) format. Our setup naturally extends widely known Kronecker sum and CANDECOMP/PARAFAC models but admits richer interaction across modes. We suggest an iterative polynomial time algorithm based on TT-SVD and higher-order orthogonal iteration (HOOI) adapted to Tucker‑2 hybrid structure. We derive non-asymptotic dimension-free bounds on the accuracy of covariance estimation taking into account hidden Kronecker product and tensor train structures. The efficiency of our approach is illustrated with numerical experiments.

Introduction

Given
\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{X}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
i.i.d. centered random vectors, we are interested in estimation of their covariance matrix
\Sigma=\mathbb{E}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
. Despite its long history, this classical problem still gets considerable attention of statistical and machine learning communities. The reason is that in modern data mining tasks researchers often have to deal with high-dimensional observations. In such scenarios they cannot rely on classical estimates, for instance, sample covariance

\widehat{\Sigma}=\frac{1}{n}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{X}_{i}\mathbf{X}_{i}^{\top},

suffering from the curse of dimensionality. To overcome this issue, statisticians impose additional assumptions on
\Sigma
in order to exploit the data structure and reduce the total number of unknown parameters. Some recent methodological and theoretical advances in covariance estimation are related with Kronecker product models, which are particularly useful for analysis of multiway or tensor-valued data
(
Werner et al. 2008
;
Allen and Tibshirani 2010
;
Greenewald et al. 2013
;
Sun et al. 2018
;
Guggenberger et al. 2023
)
. For example, motivated by multiple input multiple output (MIMO) wireless communications channels,
Werner et al. 2008
assumed that
\Sigma
can be represented as a Kronecker product of two smaller matrices
\Phi\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
and
\Psi\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times q}
, such that
pq=d
:

\Sigma=\Phi\otimes\Psi=\begin{pmatrix}\varphi_{11}\Psi&\dots&\varphi_{1p}\Psi\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\varphi_{p1}\Psi&\dots&\varphi_{pp}\Psi\end{pmatrix}.

(1)

It is known that (see, for instance, the proof of Theorem 1 in
(
Van Loan and Pitsianis 1993
)
)
\Sigma
of form (
1
) can be reshaped into a rank-one matrix using an isometric rearrangement (or permutation) operator
\mathcal{P}:\mathbb{R}^{pq\times pq}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{p^{2}\times q^{2}}
(see
(
Puchkin and Rakhuba 2024
, Definition 2.1)
). Based on this fact,
Werner et al. 2008
suggested to estimate
\mathcal{P}(\Sigma)
applying singular value decomposition to
\mathcal{P}(\widehat{\Sigma})
and showed that this estimate is asymptotically efficient in the Gaussian case. They called this approach covariance matching. This idea was further developed by
(
Tsiligkaridis and Hero 2013
;
Masak et al. 2022
;
Puchkin and Rakhuba 2024
)
, who considered the sum of Kronecker products model

\Sigma=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}\Phi_{k}\otimes\Psi_{k},

(2)

where
\Phi_{1},\Psi_{1},\dots,\Phi_{K},\Psi_{K}
are symmetric positive semidefinite matrices, such that
\Phi_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
,
\Psi_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times q}
for all
j\in\{1,\dots,K\}
and
pq=d
.
They studied properties of the permuted regularized least squares (PRLS) estimates. In
(
Tsiligkaridis and Hero 2013
;
Puchkin and Rakhuba 2024
)
, the authors regularized the loss function using the nuclear norm

\widehat{\Sigma}^{\circ}=\mathcal{P}^{-1}(\widetilde{R}),\quad\text{where}\quad\widetilde{R}\in\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}\limits_{R\in\mathbb{R}^{p^{2}\times q^{2}}}\left\{\left\|R-\mathcal{P}(\widehat{\Sigma})\right\|_{\rm F}^{2}+\lambda\|R\|_{*}\right\},

(3)

while
Masak et al. 2022
considered a rank-penalized estimate

\widecheck{\Sigma}=\mathcal{P}^{-1}(\widecheck{R}),\quad\widecheck{R}\in\operatornamewithlimits{argmin}\limits_{R\in\mathbb{R}^{p^{2}\times q^{2}}}\left\|R-\mathcal{P}(\widehat{\Sigma})\right\|_{\rm F}^{2}+\lambda\,\operatorname{rank}(R).

(4)

Following the covariance matching approach of
Werner et al. 2008
, both (
3
) and (
4
) reduce the problem of covariance estimation to recovering of a low-rank matrix
\mathcal{P}(\widehat{\Sigma})
from noisy observations. We would like to note that the estimates
\widehat{\Sigma}^{\circ}
and
\widecheck{\Sigma}
admit explicit expressions based on the singular value decomposition of
\mathcal{P}(\widehat{\Sigma})
. For this reason, they can be computed in polynomial time.

In the present paper, we consider a covariance model combining Kronecker product and tensor train (TT) structure. To be more precise, we consider
\Sigma
of the form

\Sigma=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{J}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}U_{j}\otimes V_{jk}\otimes W_{k},

(5)

where
U_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
,
V_{jk}\in\mathbb{R}^{q\times q}
, and
W_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
for any
j\in\{1,\dots,J\}
and
k\in\{1,\dots,K\}
. The numbers
p
,
q
, and
r
are assumed to be such that
pqr=d
. Let us note that (
5
) naturally extends (
2
) to the case of three-way data and coincides with it when
J=1
and
U_{1}=1
. The rationale for selecting our model is that the TT decomposition
(
Oseledets 2011
)
is recognized for its computational efficiency compared to the canonical polyadic (CP) decomposition, while providing a robust framework for representing higher-order tensors. Notice that the CANDECOMP/PARAFAC model

\Sigma=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}\Phi_{k}\otimes\Psi_{k}\otimes\Omega_{k},

(6)

which has recently got considerable attention in the literature (see, for example,
(
Pouryazdian et al. 2016
;
Greenewald et al. 2019
;
Yu et al. 2025
)
and the references therein), is a particular case of (
5
).
Following the covariance matching approach, we can reshape a matrix
\Sigma
of the form (
5
) into a third-order tensor with low canonical rank. Indeed, given a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{pqr\times pqr}
, let us define a rearrangement operator
\mathcal{R}:\mathbb{R}^{pqr\times pqr}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{p^{2}\times q^{2}\times r^{2}}
componentwise: for any
1\leqslant a\leqslant p^{2}
,
1\leqslant b\leqslant q^{2}
, and
1\leqslant c\leqslant r^{2}

\mathcal{R}(\Sigma)_{a,b,c}=\Sigma_{(\lceil a/p\rceil-1)\cdot qr+(\lceil b/q\rceil-1)\cdot r+\lceil c/r\rceil,((a-1)\%p)\cdot qr+((b-1)\%q)\cdot r+(c-1)\%r+1},

(7)

where
y\%x\in\{0,\ldots,x-1\}
stands for the residual of
y
modulo
x
.
Then it is easy to check that

\mathcal{R}(\Sigma)=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{J}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}{\rm\bf vec}(U_{j})\otimes{\rm\bf vec}(W_{jk})\otimes{\rm\bf vec}(V_{k}),

(8)

where, for any matrix
A
,
{\rm\bf vec}(A)
is a vector obtained by stacking the columns of
A
together.
Unfortunately, a formal extension of the approach suggested by
Tsiligkaridis and Hero 2013
to the CANDECOMP/PARAFAC model will not result in a practical algorithm. The main obstacle is that approximation of the nuclear norm of a tensor is an NP-hard problem
Hillar and Lim 2013
. The statistical-computational gap was discussed in several papers including
(
Barak and Moitra 2016
;
Zhang and Xia 2018
;
Han et al. 2022a
;
Luo and Zhang 2022
;
Luo and Zhang 2024
)
. For this reason, when developing an algorithm for estimation of the covariance matrix (
5
), we must take into account both its computational and sample complexities. In the present paper, we extend the approach of
Zhang and Xia 2018
and suggest an iterative procedure similar to the higher-order orthogonal iteration (HOOI) with the notable distinction of utilizing the Tucker-2 representation of the tensor. Our algorithm successfully adapts to the structure (
5
) but requires less time, than Tucker decomposition and HOOI.

While statisticians (see, for example,
(
Tsiligkaridis and Hero 2013
;
Puchkin and Rakhuba 2024
)
) established rates of convergence of the PRLS estimate (
3
), the CANDECOMP/PARAFAC model (
6
) and the more general tensor train model (
5
) remain underexplored. In Section
2
(see (
9
) below), we discuss that the tensor train model (
5
) can be represented in a way, which is very similar to the low Tucker rank tensor model (see, for instance,
(
Han et al. 2022a
, Definition 2.1)
). The only difference is that (
9
) includes two factors with orthogonal columns while in Tucker decomposition one has three such factors. For this reason, some bounds on the estimation accuracy of
\Sigma
of the form (
5
) with respect to the Frobenius norm follow from the results on tensor estimation
Zhang and Xia 2018
;
Han et al. 2022b
;
Kumar et al. 2025
, scalar-on-tensor regression
Khavari and Rabusseau 2021
;
Wang et al. 2025
, and tensor-on-tensor regression
Raskutti et al. 2019
;
Luo and Zhang 2024
with constraints on Tucker ranks. However, these bounds are dimension dependent, while many recent results in covariance estimation establish dimension-free bounds (see, for instance,
Koltchinskii and Lounici 2017
;
Bunea and Xiao 2015
;
Abdalla and Zhivotovskiy 2022
;
Zhivotovskiy 2024
;
Puchkin and Rakhuba 2024
;
Puchkin et al. 2025
). To our knowledge, the existing dimension-free results on tensor estimation only cover the case of simple rank-one tensors
(
Vershynin 2020
;
Zhivotovskiy 2024
;
Al-Ghattas et al. 2025
;
Chen and Sanz-Alonso 2025
)
.
In the present paper, we derive high-probability dimension-free bounds on the accuracy of estimation of third-order tensors with low TT-ranks and of the covariance matrices, which can be well approximated by (
5
).

Contribution.

Our main contribution is a comprehensive non-asymptotic analysis of this estimation procedure. We first derive a general deterministic perturbation bound for our TT-SVD-like algorithm, which may be of independent interest. We then leverage this result to establish a high-probability error bound for our covariance estimator. The final bound clearly decomposes the error into a bias term, related to how well the true
\Sigma
can be approximated by our model, and a variance term. This variance term scales gracefully with the sample size
n
, the TT-ranks
(J,K)
, and data-dependent effective dimensions that capture the intrinsic complexity of the covariance structure. To our knowledge, this is the first work to provide a computationally efficient and theoretically guaranteed method for covariance estimation with this flexible TT-based structure.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we present our algorithm and main theoretical guarantees. We provide some practical analysis in Section
3
and conclude with a discussion in Section
4
. All proofs are deferred to the Appendix.

Given a matrix
M\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}}

\displaystyle{\rm\bf vec}(M)_{(a-1)\cdot d_{2}+b}=M_{a,b},\quad a\leqslant d_{1},b\leqslant d_{2}.

For a tensor

\displaystyle(M\times_{i}\mathcal{T})_{a_{1}a_{2}\ldots a_{i}a_{i+1}\ldots a_{k}}=\sum_{a_{i}^{\prime}=1}^{d_{i}}M_{a_{i}a_{i}^{\prime}}\mathcal{T}_{a_{1}a_{2}\ldots a_{i-1}a_{i}^{\prime}a_{i+1}\ldots a_{k}},

and

takes values in
\{1,\ldots,d^{\prime}\}
.

It will be convenient to assume that random vectors
\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{n}
lie in a tensor product space
\mathbb{R}^{p}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{q}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{q}
, so
\Sigma=\mathbb{E}\mathbf{X}\mathbf{X}^{\top}
belongs to the space of SDP Hermitian operators
\mathcal{H}_{+}(\mathbb{R}^{p}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{q}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{r})
from
\mathbb{R}^{p}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{q}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{q}
to itself. Then, we will define partial traces of
\Sigma
as follows. Given linear spaces
L_{1},L_{2}
and linear operators
X:L_{1}\to L_{1},Y:L_{2}\to L_{2}
, we define the partial trace
{\rm Tr}_{L_{i}}
,
i=1,2
, w.r.t.
L_{i}
as follows:

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}_{L_{1}}(X\otimes Y)={\rm Tr}(X)\cdot Y,\quad{\rm Tr}_{L_{2}}(X\otimes Y)=X\cdot{\rm Tr}(Y).

We extend
{\rm Tr}_{L_{i}}(\cdot)
to all operators from
L_{1}\otimes L_{2}\to L_{1}\otimes L_{2}
by linearity. In our case, for operators from
\mathcal{H}_{+}(\mathbb{R}^{p}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{q}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{r})
, we define
{\rm Tr}_{1}(\cdot)
as a partial trace w.r.t.
\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
{\rm Tr}_{2}(\cdot)
as a partial trace w.r.t.
\mathbb{R}^{q}
and
{\rm Tr}_{3}(\cdot)
as a partial trace w.r.t.
\mathbb{R}^{r}
.
Partial traces will play in important role in our theoretical analysis. We define

\displaystyle\mathtt{r}_{1}(\Sigma)

\displaystyle=\max\limits\left\{\frac{\|{\rm Tr}_{1}(\Sigma)\|}{\|\Sigma\|},\frac{\|{\rm Tr}_{1,2}(\Sigma)\|}{\|{\rm Tr}_{2}(\Sigma)\|}\right\},

\displaystyle\mathtt{r}_{2}(\Sigma)

\displaystyle=\max\limits\left\{\frac{\|{\rm Tr}_{2}(\Sigma)\|}{\|\Sigma\|},\frac{\|{\rm Tr}_{2,3}(\Sigma)\|}{\|{\rm Tr}_{3}(\Sigma)\|}\right\},

\displaystyle\mathtt{r}_{3}(\Sigma)

\displaystyle=\max\limits\left\{\frac{\|{\rm Tr}_{3}(\Sigma)\|}{\|\Sigma\|},\frac{\|{\rm Tr}_{1,3}(\Sigma)\|}{\|{\rm Tr}_{1}(\Sigma)\|},\frac{\|{\rm Tr}_{1,2,3}(\Sigma)\|}{\|{\rm Tr}_{1,2}(\Sigma)\|}\right\},

where
{\rm Tr}_{i_{1}i_{2}\ldots i_{k}}
stands for the composition of the traces
{\rm Tr}_{i_{1}},{\rm Tr}_{i_{2}},\ldots,{\rm Tr}_{i_{k}}
.
Quantities
\mathtt{r}_{1}(\Sigma),\mathtt{r}_{2}(\Sigma),\mathtt{r}_{3}(\Sigma)
play the role of effective dimensions. From
(

, display (23))
, we know that
\mathtt{r}_{1}(\Sigma)\leqslant p,\mathtt{r}_{2}(\Sigma)\leqslant q,\mathtt{r}_{3}(\Sigma)\leqslant r
. We define them as maxima over ratios of some partial traces to ensure that for any non-empty set
S\subset\{1,2,3\}
we have

\displaystyle\frac{\|{\rm Tr}_{S}(\Sigma)\|}{\|\Sigma\|}\leqslant\prod_{s\in S}\mathtt{r}_{s}(\Sigma).

For a tensor
\mathcal{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{p^{2}\times q^{2}\times r^{2}}
, we introduce the unfolding operator with respect to the first mode as

\mathtt{m}_{1}(\mathcal{T})_{x,y}=\mathcal{T}_{x,\lceil y/r^{2}\rceil,(y-1)\%r^{2}+1}.

Similarly, the unfolding operators with respect to the second and the third modes are define as follows:

\mathtt{m}_{2}(\mathcal{T})_{x,y}=\mathcal{T}_{(y-1)\%p^{2}+1,x,\lceil y/p^{2}\rceil},\quad\mathtt{m}_{3}(\mathcal{T})_{x,y}=\mathcal{T}_{\lceil y/q^{2}\rceil,(y-1)\%q^{2}+1,x}.

We denote the output of SVD algorithm with hard thresholding via rank
J
as
SVD_{J}
. We denote matrices with orthonormal columns of size
\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}
by
\mathbb{O}_{d,r}
. In what follows,
[m]
stands for the set of integers from
1
to
m
.

Main results

Let us return to the estimation of the covariance matrix
\Sigma
of the form (
5
). As discussed in the introduction, we can reshape
\Sigma
into a third-order tensor
\mathcal{R}(\Sigma)
using the rearrangement operator (
7

\mathcal{R}(\Sigma)=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{J}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}{\rm\bf vec}(U_{j})\otimes{\rm\bf vec}(W_{jk})\otimes{\rm\bf vec}(V_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{p^{2}\times q^{2}\times r^{2}},

where vectors
{\rm\bf vec}(U_{j})
are assumed to be linearly independent, as well as vectors
{\rm\bf vec}(V_{k})

Stacking together vectors
{\rm\bf vec}(U_{j})

j=1,\dots,J
into a matrix
U\in\mathbb{R}^{p^{2}\times J}
, vectors
{\rm\bf vec}(V_{k})

and matrices

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\Sigma)=U\times_{1}V\times_{3}\mathcal{W}.

(9)

Note that this decomposition is not unique. In particular, multiplying

from the right and
\mathcal{W}
by

from the first mode does not change the right-hand side of (

and

and

,

We extend the model (

\Sigma

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}=\mathcal{T}^{*}+\mathcal{E}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}\times d_{3}},

Since

Assumption 2.1

4
Conclusion

In the present paper, we suggest a novel computationally efficient algorithm for estimation of high-dimensional covariance matrix. We provide a comprehensive theoretical analysis of this algorithm, establishing sufficient conditions for its application and rigorous guarantees that take into account both bias and variance of the proposed estimator. Our analysis is non-asymptotic and relies on the intrinsic dimensions of the covariance matrix associated to our algorithm, without involving the ambient dimension. We illustrate our theory with numerical experiments.

In this section, we provide the sketch of the proof of Theorem

Zhang and Xia 2018
and
Puchkin and Rakhuba 2024
. First, we consider the problem of estimating a tensor
\mathcal{T}^{*}=U^{*}\times_{1}V^{*}\times_{3}\mathcal{W}^{*}
from a noisy observations
\mathcal{Y}=\mathcal{T}^{*}+\mathcal{E}
, without any assumptions on the error term
\mathcal{E}
. Let
\widehat{\mathcal{T}}
be the estimator obtained by Algorithm
1
on the input
\mathcal{Y}
. The noise
\mathcal{E}
influence the estimation of
\widehat{\mathcal{T}}
in several ways. First, one need to impose some assumptions depending on the norms of
\mathtt{m}_{1}(\mathcal{E})
and
\mathtt{m}_{3}(\widehat{U}_{0}\times_{1}\mathcal{E})
on the singular numbers of matricizations
\mathtt{m}_{1}(\mathcal{T}^{*}),\mathtt{m}_{3}(\mathcal{T}^{*})
to be able to recover left singular subspaces of these matricizations up to a

\operatorname{Im}\widehat{U}_{t},\operatorname{Im}\widehat{V}_{t}

\mathcal{E}
at step

\|\widehat{\mathcal{T}}-\mathcal{T}^{*}\|_{\rm F}
into terms depending on the singular subspaces estimation and the error of estimating
\mathcal{W}^{*}
. Combining all types of errors, we obtain the following theorem. Its proof if postponed to Section
D
.

Step 1. Reduction to the PAC-bayes inequality.
The analysis will be based the following lemma, which is known as the PAC-Bayes inequality (see, e.g.,

Lemma C.5
.

Let

\mu

1-\delta

\rho\ll\mu
.

Let us rewrite
\|\mathtt{m}_{1}(\widehat{\mathcal{E}})\|
as the supremum of a certain empirical process. We have

\displaystyle\|\mathtt{m}_{1}(\widehat{\mathcal{E}})\|=

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{S}^{d_{1}-1},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{S}^{d_{2}d_{3}-1}}\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathtt{m}_{1}(\widehat{\mathcal{E}})\mathbf{y}=\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{S}^{d_{1}-1},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{S}^{d_{2}d_{3}-1}}\langle\mathtt{m}_{1}(\widehat{\mathcal{E}}),\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}^{\top}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{S}^{d_{1}-1},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{S}^{d_{2}d_{3}-1}}\langle\widehat{\Sigma}-\Sigma,\mathcal{R}_{1}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}^{\top})\rangle

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}}\log\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}}\exp f_{\mathbf{X}}(\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\eta})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}}\log\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}}\exp\left\{\lambda\left(\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathcal{R}_{1}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}^{\top})\mathbf{X}-\mathbb{E}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathcal{R}_{1}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}^{\top})\mathbf{X}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\leqslant\lambda^{2}\omega^{2}\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}}\|\Sigma^{1/2}\mathcal{R}_{1}^{-1}(\boldsymbol{\xi}\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\top})\Sigma^{1/2}\|_{\rm F}^{2}.

(21)

So, to control the above and keep the left-hand side of (
20
) bounded, we do the following. Define independent random vectors
G_{1}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{1}^{2}I_{d_{1}}),G_{2}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{2}^{2}I_{d_{2}d_{3}})
, and consider a function

\displaystyle g(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{y}^{\prime})=\|\Sigma^{1/2}\mathcal{R}_{1}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}(\mathbf{y}^{\prime})^{\top})\Sigma^{1/2}\|_{\rm F}.

(22)

By the triangle inequality, we have

\displaystyle g(\mathbf{x}+G_{1},\mathbf{y}+G_{2})\leqslant g(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})+g(\mathbf{x},G_{2})+g(G_{1},\mathbf{y})+g(G_{1},G_{2}),

so

\displaystyle g^{2}(\mathbf{x}+G_{1},\mathbf{y}+G_{2})\leqslant 4g^{2}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})+4g^{2}(\mathbf{x},G_{2})+4g^{2}(G_{1},\mathbf{y})+4g^{2}(G_{1},G_{2}).

Then, the distribution
\rho_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}
of the random vector
(\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\eta})
is equal to the distribution of
(\mathbf{x}+G_{1},\mathbf{y}+G_{2})
subject to the condition

\displaystyle(G_{1},G_{2})\in\Upsilon=\left\{g^{2}(a,b)\leqslant 4\mathbb{E}g^{2}(a,b)\mid(a,b)\in(\{\mathbf{x},G_{1}\}\times\{\mathbf{y},G_{2}\})\setminus\{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\}\right\}.

Note that by the union bound and the Markov inequality, we have

\displaystyle\Pr\left((G_{1},G_{2})\not\in\Upsilon\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{(a,b)\in(\{\mathbf{x},G_{1}\}\times\{\mathbf{y},G_{2}\})\setminus\{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\}}\Pr\left(g^{2}(a,b)>4\mathbb{E}g^{2}(a,b)\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{(a,b)\in(\{\mathbf{x},G_{1}\}\times\{\mathbf{y},G_{2}\})\setminus\{(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\}}\frac{1}{4}=\frac{3}{4}.

(23)

Let us check, that
\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}}\boldsymbol{\xi}\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\top}=\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}^{\top}
. Since the Gaussian distribution is centrally symmetric and the function
g
does not change its value when multiplying any of its argument by
-1
, we have

\displaystyle(\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\eta})\overset{d}{=}(\mathbf{x}+\varepsilon_{1}(\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x}),\mathbf{y}+\varepsilon_{2}(\boldsymbol{\eta}-\mathbf{y})),

(24)

where
\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2}
are i.i.d. Rademacher ramdom variables independent of
(\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\eta})
. Then, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\boldsymbol{\xi}\boldsymbol{\eta}^{\top}=\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}^{\top}+\mathbb{E}\varepsilon_{1}\mathbb{E}(\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x})\mathbf{y}^{\top}+\mathbb{E}\varepsilon_{2}\mathbb{E}\mathbf{x}(\boldsymbol{\eta}-\mathbf{y})^{\top}+\mathbb{E}\varepsilon_{1}\mathbb{E}\varepsilon_{2}\mathbb{E}(\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x})(\boldsymbol{\eta}-\mathbf{y})^{\top}=\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}^{\top}.

Hence, to satisfy the assumption (
20
) and use (
21
), it is enough to bound expectations
\mathbb{E}g^{2}(a,b)
for
(a,b)\in\{\mathbf{x},G_{1}\}\times\{\mathbf{y},G_{2}\}
.

Step 3. Bounding expectations
\mathbb{E}g^{2}(\cdot,\cdot)
.
Let us start with
g^{2}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
. From the definition (
22
), we have

\displaystyle g^{2}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\|\Sigma^{1/2}\mathcal{R}_{1}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}^{\top})\Sigma^{1/2}\|_{\rm F}^{2}

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}(\Sigma^{1/2}\mathcal{R}_{1}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}^{\top})\Sigma\mathcal{R}_{1}^{-\top}(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}^{\top})\Sigma^{1/2})

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}(\Sigma\mathcal{R}_{1}^{-1}(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}^{\top})\Sigma\mathcal{R}^{-\top}_{1}(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}^{\top}))

(25)

Since
{\rm Tr}(AB)\leqslant\|A\|_{\rm F}\|B\|_{\rm F}
for any matrices
A,B
, we have

\displaystyle g^{2}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\leqslant\|\Sigma\mathcal{R}^{-1}_{1}(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}^{\top})\|_{\rm F}\|\Sigma\mathcal{R}^{-\top}_{1}(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}^{\top})\|\leqslant\|\Sigma\|^{2}\|\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}^{\top}\|^{2}_{\rm F}=\|\Sigma\|,

where we used the fact that
\mathcal{R}^{-1}_{1}(\cdot)
does not change the Frobenius norm and that
\|\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}^{\top}\|_{\rm F}=\|\mathbf{x}\|\|\mathbf{y}\|=1
.

It will be convenient for future purposes to rewrite (
25
) in a slightly different form. We introduce the following tensors, that are reshapings of the matrix
\Sigma
and vectors
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},G_{1},G_{2}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{p_{1}q_{1}r_{1}p_{2}q_{2}r_{2}}=\Sigma_{(p_{1}-1)qr+(q_{1}-1)r+r_{1},(p_{2}-1)qr+(q_{2}-1)r+r_{2}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{(1)}_{p_{2}p_{3}}=(G_{1})_{(p_{2}-1)\cdot p+p_{3}},\quad\mathcal{G}^{(2)}_{q_{2}q_{3}r_{2}r_{3}}=(G_{2})_{(q_{2}-1)qr^{2}+(q_{3}-1)r^{2}+(r_{2}-1)r+r_{3}},

\displaystyle\mathtt{x}_{p_{2}p_{3}}=\mathbf{x}_{(p_{2}-1)p+p_{3}},\quad\mathtt{y}_{q_{2}q_{3}r_{2}r_{3}}=\mathbf{y}_{(q_{2}-1)qr^{2}+(q_{3}-1)r^{2}+(r_{2}-1)r+r_{3}}.

Following the Einstein notation, we obtain

\displaystyle g^{2}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle={\rm Tr}(\Sigma\mathcal{R}^{-1}_{1}(G_{1}\mathbf{y}^{\top})\Sigma\mathcal{R}_{1}^{-\top}(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}^{\top}))

\displaystyle=\Sigma_{(p_{1}-1)qr+(r_{1}-1)r+r_{1},(p_{2}-1)qr+(q_{2}-1)r+r_{2}}

\displaystyle\quad\times(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y})^{\top}_{(p_{2}-1)p+p_{3},(q_{2}-1)qr^{2}+(q_{3}-1)r^{2}+(r_{2}-1)r+r_{3}}

\displaystyle\quad\times\Sigma_{(p_{3}-1)qr+(q_{3}-1)r+r_{3},(p_{4}-1)qr+(q_{4}-1)r+r_{4}}

\displaystyle\quad\times(\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y})^{\top}_{(p_{1}-1)p+p_{4},(q_{1}-1)qr^{2}+(q_{4}-1)r^{2}+(r_{1}-1)r+r_{4}}.

\displaystyle=\mathcal{S}_{p_{1}q_{1}r_{1}p_{2}q_{2}r_{2}}\mathtt{x}_{p_{2}p_{3}}\mathtt{y}_{q_{2}q_{3}r_{2}r_{3}}\mathcal{S}_{p_{3}q_{3}r_{3}p_{4}q_{4}r_{4}}\mathtt{x}_{p_{1}p_{4}}\mathtt{y}_{q_{1}q_{4}r_{1}r_{4}}

(26)

Note that the above holds for any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{2}d_{3}}
.

Then, we bound
\mathbb{E}g^{2}(G_{1},\mathbf{y})
. Following (
26
), we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}g^{2}(G_{1},\mathbf{y})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathcal{S}_{p_{1}q_{1}r_{1}p_{2}q_{2}r_{2}}\mathcal{G}^{(1)}_{p_{2}p_{3}}\mathtt{y}_{q_{2}q_{3}r_{2}r_{3}}\mathcal{S}_{p_{3}q_{3}r_{3}p_{4}q_{4}r_{4}}\mathcal{G}^{(1)}_{p_{1}p_{4}}\mathtt{y}_{q_{1}q_{4}r_{1}r_{4}}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{1}^{2}\delta_{p_{2}p_{1}}\delta_{p_{3}p_{4}}\mathcal{S}_{p_{1}q_{1}r_{1}p_{2}q_{2}r_{2}}\mathtt{y}_{q_{2}q_{3}r_{2}r_{3}}\mathcal{S}_{p_{3}q_{3}r_{3}p_{4}q_{4}r_{4}}\mathtt{y}_{q_{1}q_{4}r_{1}r_{4}}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{1}^{2}\mathcal{S}_{p_{1}q_{1}r_{1}p_{1}q_{2}r_{2}}\mathtt{y}_{q_{2}q_{3}r_{2}r_{3}}\mathcal{S}_{p_{3}q_{3}r_{3}p_{3}q_{4}r_{4}}\mathtt{y}_{q_{1}q_{4}r_{1}r_{4}}

where
\delta
is the Kronecker delta symbol.
The above can be rewritten as the following trace:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}g^{2}(G_{1},\mathbf{y})=\sigma_{1}^{2}\cdot{\rm Tr}({\rm Tr}_{1}(\Sigma)Y{\rm Tr}_{1}(\Sigma)Y^{\top}),

where entries of the matrix
Y
are defined by
Y_{(q_{2}-1)r+r_{2},(q_{3}-1)r+r_{3}}=\mathtt{y}_{q_{2}q_{3}r_{2}r_{3}}
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}g^{2}(G_{1},\mathbf{y})\leqslant\sigma_{1}^{2}\|{\rm Tr}_{1}(\Sigma)Y\|_{\rm F}\cdot\|{\rm Tr}_{1}(\Sigma)Y^{\top}\|_{\rm F}\leqslant\sigma_{1}^{2}\|{\rm Tr}_{1}(\Sigma)\|^{2}\cdot\|Y\|_{\rm F}^{2}=\sigma_{1}^{2}\|{\rm Tr}_{1}(\Sigma)\|.

Next, we bound
\mathbb{E}g^{2}(\mathbf{x},G_{2})
. Using (
26
), we derive

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}g^{2}(\mathbf{x},G_{2})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathcal{S}_{p_{1}q_{1}r_{1}p_{2}q_{2}r_{2}}\mathtt{x}_{p_{2}p_{3}}\mathcal{G}^{(2)}_{q_{2}q_{3}r_{2}r_{3}}\mathcal{S}_{p_{3}q_{3}r_{3}p_{4}q_{4}r_{4}}\mathtt{x}_{p_{1}p_{4}}\mathcal{G}^{(2)}_{q_{1}q_{4}r_{1}r_{4}}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{2}^{2}\delta_{q_{2}q_{1}}\delta_{q_{3}q_{4}}\delta_{r_{2}r_{1}}\delta_{r_{3}r_{4}}\mathcal{S}_{p_{1}q_{1}r_{1}p_{2}q_{2}r_{2}}\mathtt{x}_{p_{2}p_{3}}\mathcal{S}_{p_{3}q_{3}r_{3}p_{4}q_{4}r_{4}}\mathtt{x}_{p_{1}p_{4}}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{2}^{2}\cdot{\rm Tr}({\rm Tr}_{2,3}(\Sigma)X{\rm Tr}_{2,3}(\Sigma)X^{\top}),

where entries of the matrix
X
are defined by
X_{p_{2},p_{3}}=\mathtt{x}_{p_{2}p_{3}}
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}g^{2}(\mathbf{x},G_{2})\leqslant\sigma_{2}^{2}\|{\rm Tr}_{2,3}(\Sigma)X\|_{\rm F}\cdot\|{\rm Tr}_{2,3}(\Sigma)X^{\top}\|_{\rm F}\leqslant\sigma_{2}^{2}\|{\rm Tr}_{2,3}(\Sigma)\|\cdot\|X\|_{\rm F}^{2}=\sigma_{2}^{2}\cdot\|{\rm Tr}_{2,3}(\Sigma)\|^{2}.

Finally, we bound
\mathbb{E}g^{2}(G_{1},G_{2})
. Using (
26
), we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}g^{2}(G_{1},G_{2})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\mathcal{S}_{p_{1}q_{1}r_{1}p_{2}q_{2}r_{2}}\mathcal{G}^{(1)}_{p_{2}p_{3}}\mathcal{G}^{(2)}_{q_{2}q_{3}r_{2}r_{3}}\mathcal{S}_{p_{3}q_{3}r_{3}p_{4}q_{4}r_{4}}\mathcal{G}^{(1)}_{p_{1}p_{4}}\mathcal{G}^{(2)}_{q_{1}q_{4}r_{1}r_{4}}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{1}^{2}\sigma_{2}^{2}\delta_{p_{1}p_{2}}\delta_{p_{3}p_{4}}\delta_{q_{1}q_{2}}\delta_{q_{3}q_{4}}\delta_{r_{1}r_{2}}\delta_{r_{3}r_{4}}\mathcal{S}_{p_{1}q_{1}r_{1}p_{2}q_{2}r_{2}}\mathcal{S}_{p_{3}q_{3}r_{3}p_{4}q_{4}r_{4}}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{1}^{2}\sigma_{2}^{2}\cdot{\rm Tr}^{2}(\Sigma).

Hence, we have
\rho_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}
-almost surely:

\displaystyle g(\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\eta})

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\sqrt{\|\Sigma\|^{2}+\sigma_{1}^{2}\|{\rm Tr}_{1}(\Sigma)\|^{2}+\sigma_{2}^{2}\|{\rm Tr}_{2,3}(\Sigma)\|^{2}+\sigma_{1}^{2}\sigma_{2}^{2}{\rm Tr}^{2}(\Sigma)}.

Set
\sigma_{1}^{2}=\mathtt{r}_{1}^{-2}(\Sigma)
and
\sigma_{2}^{2}=\mathtt{r}_{2}^{-2}(\Sigma)\mathtt{r}_{3}^{-2}(\Sigma)
. By the definition of
\mathtt{r}_{i}(\Sigma)
, for this choice of
\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2}
, the function
g(\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\eta})
is bounded by
4\|\Sigma\|
almost surely.
Thus, using (
20
) and (
21
), we deduce that for any
\lambda
satisfying

\displaystyle\lambda\leqslant(4\omega\|\Sigma\|)^{-1},

we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}}\log\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{X}}\exp f_{\mathbf{X}}(\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\eta})

\displaystyle\leqslant\lambda^{2}\omega^{2}\cdot\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}}g^{2}(\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\eta})\leqslant 16\lambda^{2}\omega^{2}\|\Sigma\|^{2}.

(27)

Due to (
C.1
), it remains to bound the Kullback-Leibler divergence
\mathcal{KL}(\rho_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}},\mu)
.

Step 4. Bounding the Kullback-Leibler divergence.
The density of
\rho_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}
is given by

\displaystyle\rho_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{(2\pi)^{-(d_{1}+d_{2}d_{3})/2}\sigma_{1}^{-d_{1}}\sigma_{2}^{-d_{2}d_{3}}}{\Pr((G_{1},G_{2}\in\Upsilon)}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{1}^{2}}\|x-\mathbf{x}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{2}^{2}}\|y-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad\times\mathbbm{1}\{(x-\mathbf{x},y-\mathbf{y})\in\Upsilon\}.

The density of the prior
\mu
is given by

\displaystyle\mu(x,y)

\displaystyle=\frac{(2\pi)^{-(d_{1}+d_{2}d_{3})/2}}{\sigma_{1}^{d_{1}}\sigma_{2}^{d_{2}d_{3}}}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{1}^{2}}\|x\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{2}^{2}}\|y\|^{2}\right\}.

Then, the KL-divergence can be computed as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{KL}(\rho_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}},\mu)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}d_{3}}}\rho_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}(x,y)\log\frac{\rho_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}(x,y)}{\mu(x,y)}dxdy

\displaystyle=\log\frac{1}{\Pr((G_{1},G_{2})\in\Upsilon)}

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d_{1}\times d_{2}d_{3}}}\rho_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}(x,y)\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{1}^{2}}(\|x-\mathbf{x}\|^{2}-\|x\|^{2})-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{2}^{2}}(\|y-\mathbf{y}\|^{2}-\|y\|^{2})\right\}dxdy.

Due to (
23
), the first term is bounded by
\log 4
. Note that the second term is equal to:

\displaystyle-\frac{\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2}}{2\sigma_{1}^{2}}+\frac{2}{2\sigma_{1}^{2}}\langle\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}}\boldsymbol{\xi},\mathbf{x}\rangle-\frac{\|\mathbf{y}\|^{2}}{2\sigma_{2}^{2}}+\frac{2}{2\sigma_{2}^{2}}\langle\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}}\boldsymbol{\eta},\mathbf{y}\rangle.

Using (
24
), we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}}\boldsymbol{\xi}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{x}+\mathbb{E}\varepsilon_{1}\mathbb{E}(\boldsymbol{\xi}-\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}}\boldsymbol{\eta}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{y}+\mathbb{E}\varepsilon_{2}\mathbb{E}(\boldsymbol{\eta}-\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{y},

so we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{KL}(\rho_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}},\mu)

\displaystyle\leqslant\log 4+\frac{\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}}{2\sigma_{1}^{2}}+\frac{\|\mathbf{y}\|_{2}^{2}}{2\sigma_{2}^{2}}=\log 4+\mathtt{r}_{1}^{2}(\Sigma)/2+\mathtt{r}_{2}^{2}(\Sigma)\mathtt{r}_{3}^{2}(\Sigma)/2.

Step 5. Final bound.
Substituting the above bound and bound (
27
) into (
46
) and using

\displaystyle\|\mathtt{m}_{1}(\widehat{\mathcal{E}})\|=\frac{1}{\lambda}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{S}^{d_{1}-1}\\
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{S}^{d_{2}d_{3}-1}\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}),

we get

\displaystyle\|\mathtt{m}_{1}(\widehat{\mathcal{E}})\|

\displaystyle\leqslant 16\lambda\omega^{2}\|\Sigma\|^{2}+\frac{\mathtt{r}_{1}^{2}(\Sigma)/2+\mathtt{r}_{2}^{2}(\Sigma)\mathtt{r}_{3}^{2}(\Sigma)/2+\log(4/\delta)}{\lambda n}

for any positive
\lambda\leqslant(4\omega\|\Sigma\|)^{-1}
with probability at least
1-\delta
. Since
n\geqslant\mathtt{r}_{1}^{2}(\Sigma)+\mathtt{r}_{2}^{2}(\Sigma)\mathtt{r}_{3}^{2}(\Sigma)+\log(4/\delta)
, we choose

\displaystyle\lambda=(4\omega\|\Sigma\|)^{-1}\sqrt{\frac{\mathtt{r}_{1}^{2}(\Sigma)/2+\mathtt{r}_{2}^{2}(\Sigma)\mathtt{r}_{3}^{2}(\Sigma)/2+\log(4/\delta)}{n}},

and get

\displaystyle\|\mathtt{m}_{1}(\widehat{\mathcal{E}})\|

\displaystyle\leqslant 8\omega\|\Sigma\|\sqrt{\frac{\mathtt{r}_{1}^{2}(\Sigma)/2+\mathtt{r}_{2}^{2}(\Sigma)\mathtt{r}_{3}^{2}(\Sigma)/2+\log(4/\delta)}{n}}

\displaystyle\leqslant 32\omega\|\Sigma\|\sqrt{\frac{\mathtt{r}_{1}^{2}(\Sigma)+\mathtt{r}_{2}^{2}(\Sigma)\mathtt{r}_{3}^{2}(\Sigma)+\log(1/\delta)}{n}}.\qed

\displaystyle\sup_{U\in\mathbb{O}_{d_{1},J},V\in\mathbb{O}_{d_{2},K}}\|U^{\top}\times_{1}V^{\top}\times_{3}\widehat{\mathcal{E}}\|_{\rm F}=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}U\in\mathbb{O}_{d_{1},J},V\in\mathbb{O}_{d_{2},K}\\
W\in\mathbb{R}^{J\times d_{2}\times K},\|W\|_{\rm F}\leqslant 1\end{subarray}}\langle U^{\top}\times_{1}V^{\top}\times_{3}\widehat{\mathcal{E}},W\rangle.

Then, we apply Theorem
C.6
with
\mathbb{S}_{1}=\mathbb{O}_{d_{1},J},\mathbb{S}_{2}=\{W\in\mathbb{R}^{J\times d_{2}\times K}:\|W\|_{\rm F}\leqslant 1\},\mathbb{S}_{3}=\mathbb{O}_{d_{3},K}

Step 4. Bounding the Kullback-Leibler divergence.
Define
I=\{i\in[3]\mid l_{i}\mathtt{r}_{i}(\Sigma)>\log|\mathbb{S}_{i}|\}

i\in I
, we have
\mathcal{D}_{i}=\operatorname{Uniform}(\mathbb{S}_{i})
and the density of
\rho_{A_{1},A_{2},A_{3}}
is given by

\displaystyle\rho_{A_{1},A_{2},A_{3}}(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})

\displaystyle=\prod_{i\in I}\delta_{0}(a_{i}-A_{i})\times\prod_{i\in[3]\setminus I}\frac{\sigma_{i}^{-l_{i}d_{i}}}{(2\pi)^{l_{i}d_{i}/2}}\exp\left\{-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}}\|a_{i}-A_{i}\|_{\rm F}^{2}\right\}

\displaystyle\quad\times\frac{\mathbbm{1}\left\{(a_{1}-A_{1},a_{2}-A_{2},a_{3}-A_{3})\in\Upsilon\right\}}{\Pr((G_{1},G_{2},G_{3})\in\Upsilon)}.

\Upsilon

\rho_{A_{1},A_{2},A_{3}}
can be decomposed into product of the truncated Gaussian
\rho_{-I}
and delta measures
\bigotimes_{i\in I}\delta_{A_{i}}
. Hence, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{KL}(\rho_{A_{1},A_{2},A_{3}},\mu)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{KL}(\rho_{-I}\otimes\bigotimes_{i\in I}\delta_{A_{i}},\mathcal{D}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{D}_{2}\otimes\mathcal{D}_{3})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{KL}(\rho_{-I},\bigotimes_{i\in[3]\setminus I}\mathcal{D}_{i})+\sum_{i\in I}\mathcal{KL}(\delta_{A_{i}},\operatorname{Uniform}(\mathbb{S}_{i}))

\displaystyle=\mathcal{KL}(\rho_{-I},\bigotimes_{i\in[3]\setminus I}\mathcal{D}_{i})+\sum_{i\in I}\log|\mathbb{S}_{i}|.

i\in[3]\setminus I

\mathcal{D}_{i}
is the centered Gaussian with the covariance matrix
\sigma_{i}^{2}I_{d_{i}l_{i}}
up to the reshaping, so the density of
\bigotimes_{i\in[3]\setminus I}\mathcal{D}_{i}

\displaystyle\mu_{-I}((a_{i})_{i\in[3]\setminus I})=\prod_{i\in[3]\setminus I}\frac{\sigma_{i}^{-d_{i}l_{i}}}{(2\pi)^{d_{i}l_{i}/2}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}}\|a_{i}\|^{2}_{\rm F}\right).

\displaystyle\mathcal{KL}(\rho_{-I},\otimes_{i\in[3]\setminus I}\mathcal{D}_{i})

\displaystyle\qquad\times\log\left[\frac{\prod_{i\in[3]\setminus I}\exp\left(\|a_{i}\|_{\rm F}^{2}/2\sigma_{i}^{2}-\|a_{i}-A_{i}\|_{\rm F}^{2}/2\sigma_{i}^{2}\right)}{\Pr((G_{1},G_{2},G_{3})\in\Upsilon)}\right]\prod_{i\in[3]\setminus I}{\rm d}a_{i}

\displaystyle=\log\frac{1}{\Pr((G_{1},G_{2},G_{3})\in\Upsilon)}-\sum_{i\in[3]\setminus I}\frac{1}{2\sigma_{i}^{2}}\|A_{i}\|_{\rm F}^{2}+\sum_{i\in[3]\setminus I}\frac{1}{\sigma_{i}^{2}}\langle\mathbb{E}\boldsymbol{\xi}^{i},A_{i}\rangle,

(P,Q,R)\sim\rho_{A_{1},A_{2},A_{3}}

53

\displaystyle\mathcal{KL}(\rho_{-I},\otimes_{i\in[3]\setminus I}\mathcal{D}_{i})

\displaystyle\leqslant\log 8+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in[3]\setminus I}l_{i}\mathtt{r}_{i}^{2}(\Sigma),

for

\displaystyle\mathcal{KL}(\rho_{A_{1},A_{2},A_{3}},\mu)

\displaystyle\leqslant\log 8+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\in[3]\setminus I}l_{i}\mathtt{r}_{i}^{2}(\Sigma)+\sum_{i\in I}\log|\mathbb{S}_{i}|

\displaystyle\leqslant\log 8+\sum_{i=1}^{3}\min\limits\{\mathtt{r}_{i}^{2}(\Sigma)\cdot l_{i},\log|\mathbb{S}_{i}|\}.

(67)

),(

\displaystyle\leqslant 2^{12}\lambda\omega^{2}\|\Sigma\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{\log 8+\sum_{i=1}^{3}\min\limits\{\mathtt{r}_{i}(\Sigma)\cdot l_{i},\log|\mathbb{S}_{i}|\}+\log\frac{1}{\delta}}{\lambda n}

with probability at least

It implies

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}A_{1}\in\mathbb{S}_{1},\\
