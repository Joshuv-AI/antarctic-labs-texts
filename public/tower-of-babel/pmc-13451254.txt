B10 Adoptive Transfer Ameliorates Inflammation and Bone Loss in Experimental Periodontitis Through Promoting Macrophage-Mediated Pro-Resolving Functions
Authors: Guoqin Cao, Elaheh Dalir Abdolahinia, Takumi Memida, Shengyuan Huang, Sunniva Ruiz, Satoru Shindo, Toshihisa Kawai, Jiang Lin, Xiaozhe Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractImmune cell-cell interaction plays a critical role in the regulation of inflammatory responses. IL-10-expressing regulatory B cells (B10) alleviate the periodontal inflammation and bone loss by secreting IL-10, which can stimulate pro-resolving macrophage differentiation. However, little is known about the dynamic B10-macrophage interaction and the specific B10-induced pro-resolving functions in vivo in periodontitis. This study aimed to determine the dynamic local infiltration of B10 and macrophages, and the B10-induced macrophage differentiation and functions in experimental periodontitis. B10 were adoptively transferred via tail vein injection in a mouse model of experimental periodontitis. In some experiments, macrophages were depleted by intraperitoneal injection of clodronate liposomes. Dynamic gingival infiltration of B10 and macrophages were evaluated by in vivo imaging system (IVIS). The level of bone loss was evaluated by Micro-CT and TRAP staining. Cytokine productions, macrophage efferocytosis and autophagy were assessed by immunohistochemical / immunofluorescence analysis. Production of specialized pro-resolving mediators (SPMs) was detected by LC–MS/MS. B10 adoptive transfer inhibited alveolar bone resorption and osteoclast differentiation. B10 adoptive transfer promoted the number of pro-resolving macrophages compared with the control groups. A significant increase in IL-10, Arg-1, and YM-1, ATG5⁺, LC3B⁺, CD68⁺LC3B⁺, CD68+ATG5+ and CD68⁺Ly6G⁺ cells were detected in gingival tissues following B10 transfer, accompanied by a marked decrease in IL-17 A, TNF-α, and IL-1β expression. However, the B10-mediated suppression of alveolar bone resorption, osteoclast differentiation, TNF-α and IL-1β expression, as well as the upregulation of IL-10, ATG5, and LC3B, was attenuated upon macrophage depletion, whereas IL-17 A expression remained suppressed regardless of the macrophage presence. Notably, B10 adoptive transfer promoted RvD2 release in a macrophage-dependent manner. This study suggested that B10 adoptive transfer inhibited inflammation and bone loss in experimental periodontitis through the induction of macrophage-mediated pro-resolving functions, including RvD2 production, enhanced neutrophil clearance, and induction of macrophage autophagy.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10753-026-02536-0.Keywords: Regulatory B Cells, Macrophage, Periodontitis, Inflammation Resolution, SPMs

Introduction

Periodontitis is a chronic inflammatory disease caused by the disruption of homeostasis between plaque biofilm and the host immune response. It leads to the destruction of periodontal supporting tissues, including alveolar bone, and is the primary cause of tooth loss in adults [1]. The breakdown of immune homeostasis results in a significant increase in pro-inflammatory factors such as TNF-α, IL-1β, MMPs, and RANKL in periodontal tissues. These inflammatory mediators promote osteoclast activation, leading to irreversible alveolar bone resorption [2]. The excessive immune response-driven inflammation and bone destruction remain the most challenges in periodontitis treatment. The proper control of inflammation is crucial for the treatment of periodontitis, which involves the downregulation of inflammatory mediators, reduction of the infiltration of inflammatory cells, and timely clearance of necrotic tissue and apoptotic cells [3–5].

Interactions among immune cells, such as B10, Tregs, and macrophages, play an important role in regulating the progression of periodontitis. Regulatory B cells (Bregs), a population of suppressor B cells, maintain immune homeostasis through the secretion of IL-10, IL-35, TGF-β, PD-L1, and other mediators [6–8]. The subset that inhibits inflammation primarily via IL-10 is termed B10, which has been observed to increase in gingival tissue and peripheral blood in patient with periodontitis [9, 10]. It has been demonstrated that either adoptive transfer of Pg-LPS/CpG induced-B10 or the induction of B10 by IL-21/anti-Tim1/CD40L can ameliorate inflammatory cytokine production and alveolar bone resorption in periodontitis [11, 12]. This effect is associated with an decreased Th17 proliferation [13] and a reduced frequency of RANKL⁺ neutrophils [14]. Our recent study further showed that the regulatory function of B10 in periodontitis is dependent on the presence of macrophages [15]. Consistently, our study reported that B10 adoptive transfer promoted M2 macrophage polarization through the PD-L1/PD-1 pathway, thereby reducing alveolar bone resorption [8].

Macrophages play crucial roles in both innate and adaptive immune responses. Due to their high plasticity, macrophages polarize into distinct functional phenotypes that exhibit strikingly divergent-and sometimes opposing-immune functions. Pro-inflammatory macrophages exacerbate inflammatory responses by secreting pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β and iNOS [16]. In contrast, pro-resolving macrophages produce mediators such as IL-10 and TGF-β to modulate inflammation and facilitate tissue repair by clearing apoptotic cells and tissue debris, as well as by upregulating Arg-1 expression [17, 18]. Neutrophils, the first line of defense, phagocytose pathogens but subsequently undergo apoptosis or necrosis. Impaired clearance of these cells in periodontitis elevates pro-inflammatory mediators (TNF-α, IL-1β, MMPs), perpetuating inflammation [19, 20 ]. The clearance of apoptotic neutrophils is primarily carried out by pro-resolving macrophages [21, 22]. Furthermore, efferocytosis, together with cytokines such as IL-4, IL-13, IL-10, and TGF-β, promotes macrophage polarization toward a pro-resolving phenotype [5]. This phenotypic shift enables them to produce specialized pro-resolving mediators (SPMs), such as Resolvins and Maersins [23, 24]. SPMs are bioactive lipid mediators synthesized from polyunsaturated fatty acids during the resolution phase of inflammation and act as stop signals for the inflammatory response and help to coordinate the resolution process [25–27]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that upregulating SPMs in periodontal tissues can modulate the Th17/Treg balance and inhibit alveolar bone resorption [28, 29]. Additionally, SPMs (e.g. Resolvins) further promote macrophage polarization toward a pro-resolving phenotype and enhance their ability to phagocytose neutrophils [30–32]. This positive feedback actively drives the resolution of inflammation.

Autophagy is a crucial pathway for prolonging cell lifespan and maintaining physiological functions. Promoting autophagy helps attenuate periodontal inflammation and reduce alveolar bone resorption [33]. Enhancement of autophagy in macrophages by SPMs increases their phagocytic activity [34]. Increasing evidence indicated that IL-10 play a critical role in regulating cellular autophagy. In particular, IL-10 enhances mitochondrial autophagy in macrophages, thereby attenuating macrophage inflammation [35]. Our previous study demonstrated that B10 promote a pro-resolving macrophage phenotype in vitro—enhancing the expression of specialized pro-resolving mediators (SPMs) and improving phagocytic activity via IL-10, PD-1, and direct cell-cell contact [36, 37]. Collectively, these findings indicate that the crosstalk between B10 and macrophages plays a critical role in the control of periodontitis progression. However, the dynamic interaction between B10 and macrophages, as well as the impact of B10-induced pro-resolving macrophage functions in vivo, remain unclear. This study aimed to investigate the role of B10-macrophage interaction and macrophage-mediated inflammation resolution in B10-induced amelioration of experimental periodontitis.

Materials and Methods

Animals

Six-to-eight-week-old C57BL/6J, CX3CR1GFP (strain #005582), and CX3CR1GFPCCR2RFP (strain #032127) mice (equal numbers of males and females, weight 18–24 g) were obtained from Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA) and housed under specific pathogen-free conditions with a 12-hour light–dark cycle at 25 °C. Mice were acclimated for 3 days before the experiments. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Nova Southeastern University (Approval number: 2022.02.XH1).

P. gingivalis culture

P. gingivalis (strain ATCC 33277) was initially grown on blood agar (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) under anaerobic conditions for 5 to 7 days. A single colony was then selected and cultured for an additional 48 h in brain heart infusion medium supplemented with 0.1% yeast extract, 0.5‰ hemin, and 0.001‰ menadione (all from Sigma-Aldrich, Saint Louis, MO, USA) under anaerobic conditions at 37 °C. The anaerobic environment was maintained using an AnaeroPack (MGC, Tokyo, Japan). Bacterial cell density was measured by the absorbance at 600 nm (OD600).

Pre-immunization of Donor Mouse with P. gingivalis

Pre-immunization of donor mice with P. gingivalis was performed as previously described [38], which suggested that antigen specificity dictate the local infiltration of P. gingivalis pre-immunized B10 cells into periodontal tissue. In detail, formalin-fixed P. gingivalis suspensions were prepared by fixing the bacteria in 4% paraformaldehyde and resuspending them in PBS at a concentration of 1 × 10⁸ CFU/200 µL PBS. The fixed P. gingivalis was then administered to C57BL/6J mice via intraperitoneal (IP). After 7 days, the mice received a booster IP injection containing 1 × 10⁷ CFU of fixed P. gingivalis in 200 µL PBS. Four days after the booster injection, the mice were sacrificed, and the spleens were harvested for subsequent cell culture and B cell isolation.

Cell Culture and B10 Isolation

Spleen cells were obtained from C57BL/6J mice, and red blood cells were removed using ACK lysing buffer (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA). The remaining splenocytes were then cultured in IMDM supplemented with 5% FBS, 1% penicillin–streptomycin, and 5 × 10− 5M 2-mercaptoethanol (2-ME) for 48 h (all from Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA). During culture, B10 expansion was achieved with 10 µg/mL P. gingivalis -LPS (InvivoGen, San Diego, CA, USA) and 5 nM CpG (InvivoGen, San Diego, CA, USA). At the end of stimulation, B10-enriched B cells were isolated using a Pan B Cells Isolation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, Cambridge, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, non-B cells were labeled for 10 min with a cocktail of biotin-conjugated antibodies against CD4, CD11c, CD49b, CD90, Gr-1, and Ter119, followed by incubation with anti-biotin magnetic microbeads for 15 min. After incubation, the cell suspension was then passed through a magnetic separation column. The proportion of B10 cells in the Pg-LPS/CpG-stimulated group was significantly increased compared to the control group (Control vs. P. gingivalis -LPS/CpG: 1.22 ± 0.46 vs. 15.73 ± 2.59, P < 0.01)(Sup Fig. 1). The purity of the enriched B10 cells exceeded 95%, and the cells were used for subsequent experiments. In the B cells or B10 adoptive transfer experiment, 1 × 10⁶ cells in 100µL of PBS were adoptively transferred into recipient mice via tail vein injection.

Establishment of Ligature-Induced Periodontitis Model

Before ligation, all mice were given drinking water containing 850 µg/mL sulfamethoxazole and 170 µg/mL trimethoprim for 3 days to suppress oral bacterial growth. Afterward, the mice were provided with sterilized drinking water for an additional 4 days prior to the establishment of the periodontitis model. For ligation, 6 − 0 silk sutures were immersed in P. gingivalis (1 × 10⁹ CFU, dissolved in 2% carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC)) and placed around the maxillary second molar, tying the suture on the palatal side. This was followed by oral infection with P. gingivalis (1 × 10⁹ CFU) for 3 days. The experimental groups were as follows: healthy control group (Con), ligation with P. gingivalis infection (L + Pg), ligation with P. gingivalis infection and B cell transfer (L + Pg + B), ligation with P. gingivalis infection and B10 transfer (L + Pg + B10), ligation with P. gingivalis infection and macrophage depletion (MD + L + Pg), and ligation with P. gingivalis infection, macrophage depletion, and B10 transfer (MD + L + Pg + B10). The gingival tissues were obtained at 3 days, 7 days, and 10 days after B cells/B10 adoptive transfer (n = 4/group/time point). The maxillary tissue blocks were obtained for histology and Micro CT analysis at 10 days after B cells/B10 adoptive transfer (n = 5/group).

Macrophage Depletion

To investigate the role of macrophages in B10-mediated inflammation resolution during periodontitis, macrophages were depleted as previously described by Viniegra A et al. [39]. Specifically, 5 µg/g Clo-Lipo (Encapsula NanoSciences, Brentwood, TN, USA) was administered via IP injection into C57BL/6J mice 3 days before ligation, followed by subsequent injections every two days thereafter until tissue collection at 3 days, 7 days, and 10 days after B cell/B10 adoptive transfer.

IVISense™ 680 Fluorescent Labeling of B/B10 Cells

B cells or B10 cells were labeled with IVISense™ 680 (Revvity, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, IVISense™ 680 was dissolved in warm PBS to a final concentration of 1.2 mg/mL. The B/B10 suspension (1 × 10⁷ cells) was then mixed with IVISense™ 680 at a 1:1(v/v) ratio and incubated at room temperature for 15 min. After incubation, the cells were washed twice with complete medium, followed by one wash with PBS. The cells were then resuspended in PBS at a concentration of 1 × 10⁷ cells/mL. Finally, 1 × 10⁶ labeled cells were adoptively transferred through tail vein injection into recipient mice 3 days after ligation.

Immune Cell Trafficking by In Vivo Imaging System (IVIS)

CX3CR1GFP and CX3CR1GFPCCR2RFP mice were divided into the following groups (4 mice/group): healthy control group (Con), ligation with P. gingivalis infection (L + Pg), ligation with P. gingivalis infection and B cell transfer (L + Pg + B), and ligation with P. gingivalis infection and B10 transfer (L + Pg + B10). A 6 − 0 silk ligature was placed around right side of the maxillary second molar, while the other side was left without ligation as a negative control. All mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (Dechra, Fort Worth, TX, USA), and the Xtreme II (Bruker, Billerica, MA, USA) IVIS system was used to detect dynamic changes in B cells, B10, total macrophage infiltration, and pro-resolving macrophage infiltration at 3, 7, and 10 days after B/B10 cell transfer.

The imaging parameters were set as follows: IVISense™ 680 fluorescence: excitation: 670 nm, emission: 700 nm, exposure time: 0.1 s. GFP fluorescence: excitation: 480 nm, emission: 630 nm, exposure time: 1.2 s. RFP fluorescence: excitation: 630 nm, emission: 700 nm, exposure time: 1.2 s. A 30 × 50 pixel region of interest (ROI) on the palatal side of the maxillary second molar of each image was selected for fluorescence intensity analysis. For each mouse, the mean fluorescence intensity of two images was calculated and used as a single data point for statistical analysis. Fluorescence images were analyzed using ImageJ software. The analysis procedure was as follows: images were converted to 8-bit format, and the Shanbhag algorithm was applied to automatically adjust the threshold and obtain total fluorescence intensity for both IVISense™ 680 and GFP fluorescence, as well as the total fluorescence intensity of the GFP/RFP dual-positive region in the merged GFP/RFP composite image.

Micro-CT

Maxillary tissues were analyzed using Micro-CT to obtain high-resolution three-dimensional images. All scans were performed using a micro-CT scanner (SkyScan 1276, Bruker micro CT, Belgium) under the following conditions: X-ray source voltage of 80 kV, current of 114 µA, and an isotropic voxel size of 9 μm. A 0.5 mm aluminum filter was used to reduce beam hardening artifacts. Reconstructed data were analyzed using CTAn software (Version 1.23) to quantify BV/TV and the distance between the cementoenamel junction and alveolar bone crest (CEJ–ABC). Regions of interest (ROIs) were defined using a rectangular selection based on three anatomical landmarks: the apex of the mesial root of the first molar, the contact point between the first and second molars, and the apex of the mesial root of the third molar. This rectangular area was saved as a new ROI, and a total of 65 layers were selected to define its thickness. Subsequently, the alveolar bone boundary within the ROI was manually delineated using a polygonal contour to calculate BV/TV. Three-dimensional visualizations were generated using CTvox (version 3.3) or DataViewer (version 1.7) software.

Tartrate-Resistant Acid Phosphatase (TRAP) Staining

Four micrometer-thick paraffin sections of mouse maxillae were baked at 58 °C for 2 h, followed by deparaffinization and rehydration through xylene and a graded series of ethanol (100%, 100%, 95%, 85%, and 75%) to distilled water. The sections were then incubated in 0.2 M Tris buffer (pH 9.0) at 37 °C for 1 h, followed by a 5-minute wash in distilled water (DH₂O). The sections were incubated with 0.2 M acetate buffer (containing 0.2 M sodium acetate and 50 mM tartaric acid) for 20 min at room temperature, followed by incubation in trap stain buffer (0.5 mg/mL Naphthol AS-MX phosphate and 1.1 mg/mL Fast Red ASTR salt dissolved in 0.2 M acetate buffer) for 50 min at 37 °C. After incubation, the sections were washed with DH₂O for 2 min. The sections were then counterstained with hematoxylin, air-dried at room temperature for 30 min, and mounted with an aqueous mounting medium. Images of the mesial, central, and distal regions of the second molar were captured under 200× magnification using a Zeiss AXIOCAM 208 color camera (Zeiss, Jena, Germany). Multinucleated TRAP-positive cells on the surface of the alveolar bone were identified and counted as osteoclasts.

Immunohistochemistry Staining

Four micrometer-thick paraffin sections of mouse maxillae were baked at 58 °C overnight, followed by deparaffinization and rehydration. After rehydration, the tissue sections were incubated with 3% hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) at room temperature for 15 min to block endogenous peroxidase activity. Antigen retrieval was then performed in Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 9.0) by heating the sections in a 95 °C water bath for 10 min, followed by cooling at room temperature for 10 min. Sections were washed with PBS (5 min × 3 times) and then blocked with 5% goat serum at room temperature for 30 min. After removing the blocking solution, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. The following day, the slides were brought to room temperature and equilibrated for 30 min, followed by PBS washes (5 min × 3 times). The sections were then incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG or goat anti-rat IgG secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. After washing with PBS (5 min × 3 times), color development was carried out using DAB substrate. The nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin, differentiated in acidic ethanol, and blued in a saturated lithium carbonate solution. Finally, the sections were dehydrated, cleared, and mounted with neutral balsam mounting medium. The following primary antibodies were used: Rabbit anti-mouse IL-1β (1:200, Cat# ab205924), TNF-α (1:200, Cat# ab34674), IL-17 A (1:200, Cat#ab79056) (all from Abcam, UK), Rabbit anti-mouse CD68 (1:200, Cat# 97778 S, Cell Signaling Technology, USA), Rat anti-mouse IL-10 (1:100, Cat# ab189392, Abcam, UK). The following secondary antibodies were used: HRP-conjugated goat anti-rat IgG (1:500, Cat# 405405, Biolegend, San Diego, CA, USA), HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:5000, Cat# AB205718, Abcam, UK). Images were captured using a Zeiss AXIOCAM 208 color camera (Zeiss, Jena, Germany) at 200× and 400× magnifications. Tissue sections were obtained from each mouse with a 5-section interval. Two sections per mouse were selected for analysis. For each section, three regions of interest were identified: the mesial, furcation, and distal areas of the second molar. In each region, three 600 × 600 pixel squared areas were randomly selected for cell counting. The average number of positive cells across the two selected sections was calculated and used as a single data point for statistical analysis.

Immunofluorescence Staining

Sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to antigen retrieval, followed by blocking with 5% goat serum. Sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, washed with PBS, and incubated with fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies at room temperature for 1 h. Nuclei were stained with DAPI (1:1000) for 10 min, followed by PBS washes. Sections were mounted with an anti-fade mounting medium. The following primary antibodies were used: Rabbit anti-mouse Ly6G, Rabbit anti-mouse ATG5, Rabbit anti-mouse LC3B, Rabbit anti-mouse CD206 (1:600, Cat# ab64693) (all from Abcam, UK), Alexa Fluor® 488 Conjugate rabbit anti-mouse CD68 (1:150, Cat# 51644s, Cell Signaling Technology, USA). The following secondary antibody was used: Alexa Fluor 647–conjugate goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:500, Cat# ab150083, Abcam, UK). Fluorescent images were captured using an Evos M5000 microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) at 400× magnification. Tissue sections were obtained from each mouse with a 5-section interval. Two sections per mouse were selected for analysis. For each section, four random images were acquired from the mesial, central, and distal gingival epithelial regions for cell counting. The average number of positive cells across the two selected sections was calculated and used as a single data point for statistical analysis.

Liquid Chromatography - Tandem Mass Spectrometry Analysis

Gingival tissues from the buccal and palatal sides of the bilateral maxillary second molar regions were collected on days 3, 7, and 10 after B/B10 cell transfer. The tissues were weighed and then snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen before being stored at -80 °C for subsequent LC-MS/MS analysis. For sample preparation, tissues were homogenized in pre-cooled PBS and centrifuged, and the supernatant was collected. For each sample, 1 ng of internal standards—dissolved in 150 µL methanol—was added for recovery and quantification. The internal standards included 15(S)-HETE-d8, 14(15)-EpETrE-d11, Resolvin D2-d5, Leukotriene B4-d4, and Prostaglandin E1-d4. Polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA) metabolites were extracted using C18 solid-phase extraction columns and analyzed with a QTRAP 7500 mass spectrometer. A total of 64 specialized pro-resolving mediators (SPMs) were quantified, and metabolite concentrations were normalized to tissue weight. Statistical analysis was performed using MetaboAnalyst 6.0 with the following settings: Missing data handling: variables with > 50% missing values were removed; remaining missing values were imputed using 1/5 of the minimum detected value. Normalization: samples were automatically normalized before statistical analysis.

Statistical Analysis

For mass spectrometry, differences among groups were analyzed by ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test for multiple comparisons. For normally distributed data, comparisons between two groups were performed using the Student’s t-test, while comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey’s or Dunnett’s post-hoc test. For non-normally distributed data, comparisons were made using the Mann–Whitney U test. For all analysis, data are presented as the mean ± SD. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 23.0 and GraphPad Prism 8.0.2. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of the data, and Levene’s test was applied to evaluate the homogeneity of variance. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Adoptive Transfer of B10 Inhibited Periodontal Inflammation and Alveolar Bone Loss

To explore the role of B10 in alveolar bone loss, micro-CT imaging was employed to assess alveolar bone resorption in mice. The results demonstrated that the BV/TV was reduced after ligation plus P.g. infection (L + Pg) when compared to the control group (Con). However, a significantly increased BV/TV was observed in the group receiving ligation plus P.g. infection plus B10 cell transfer (L + Pg+B10) when compared to both the L + Pg group and the group receiving ligation plus P.g. infection plus B cells transfer (L + Pg+B). No significant difference in BV/TV was observed between the L + Pg group and L + Pg+B group (Fig. 1A, B). The distance of CEJ-ABC in the L + Pg group was significantly increased compared to the Con group. However, no significant difference was observed between the L + Pg and L + Pg+B group. Notably, this distance was markedly reduced in the L + Pg+B10 group, compared to both the L + Pg group and the L + Pg+B group (Fig. 1A, C). A similar result was noted in osteoclast differentiation detected by TRAP staining, which mirrored the pattern seen in the CEJ-ABC measurements (Fig. 1D, E). It was observed that the expressions of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-17 A was significantly elevated in L + Pg group compared to the Con group. No significant difference was found between the L + Pg group and the L + Pg+B group. However, the expression of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-17 A was markedly reduced in the L + Pg+B10 group, compared to the L + Pg group and L + Pg+B group (Fig. 1H-M). Notably, the expression of IL-10 was significantly increased in the L + Pg+B10 group compared to the L + Pg and L + Pg+B groups. However, there was no significant difference between L + Pg and L + Pg+B groups (Fig. 1F, G).

Dynamic Gingival Infiltration of B10/B Cells and Macrophages, and the Detection of SPMs

IVIS was used to detect the presence of fluorescently labelled B10/B cells and macrophages in periodontal tissue. In the control group, neither B10/B cells nor macrophages were detected by IVIS. However, by day 3, the presence of both B cells and B10 reached a peak in the periodontitis tissue of the B10/B cells transfer groups (Fig. 2B, C). Concurrently, the number of macrophages also increased significantly three days after B10/B cells transfer (Fig. 2D, E). In contrast, from day 7, there was no significant difference in macrophage numbers among the L + Pg group, the L + Pg+B group, and the L + Pg+B10 group (Fig. 2D, E). To further explore the regulatory effect of B10 in periodontal inflammation resolution, LC-MS was employed to quantify SPMs production in gingival tissues. The results showed that, among the 64 SPMs targets analyzed, the levels of RvD4 and AT-RvD4 were significantly elevated in the L + Pg+B10 group compared to both the L + Pg group and the L + Pg+B groups; However, there was no significant difference in SPM production between the L + Pg group and the L + Pg+B group. Compared to the control group, RvD4 production was significantly elevated in the L + Pg group, while AT-RvD4 level remained unchanged (Fig. 2F-J).

Adoptive Transfer of B10 Promotes Pro-Resolving Macrophage Polarization in Periodontitis

To further investigate the dynamic change of pro-resolving macrophages, IVIS was used to detect the pro-resolving macrophage (CX3CR1+CCR2+) cells. The results showed that no fluorescence was detected in the Con group, the fluorescence intensity of CX3CR1+CCR2+ cells was significantly increased in the L + Pg+B10 group at both day 7 and day 10 following B10 transfer, compared to the L + Pg group and the L + Pg+B group. However, there was no significant difference between the L + Pg group and the L + Pg+B group (Fig. 3A, B). For further verification, pro-resolving macrophages and their markers were evaluated by IHC at the termination of the experiment. Research has shown that the expression of CD206, Arg-1, and YM-1 is significantly upregulated when macrophages (CD68⁺) switch to M2 phenotype [40]. Therefore, the phenotypic identification of M2 macrophages was conducted using CD68+CD206+, and the expression levels of M2-related markers, YM-1 and Arg-1, were assessed. The results showed that the number of CD68+CD206+ pro-resolving macrophages, the expression of YM-1 and Arg-1 were significantly higher in the L + Pg group compared to the Con group. No significant difference was observed between the L + Pg group and the L + Pg+B group. In contrast, compared to the L + Pg group and the L + Pg+B group, the number of CD68+CD206+ pro-resolving macrophage, and the expression of YM-1 and Arg-1 were significantly increased in the L + Pg+B10 group (Fig. 3C-H).

Adoptive Transfer of B10 Promotes Macrophage Efferocytosis and Autophagy

Efficient clearance of apoptotic neutrophils represents a critical step in the resolution of periodontal inflammation, a process primarily mediated by macrophages [41]. To assess neutrophil clearance, immunofluorescence staining was performed to detect CD68⁺Ly6G⁺ cells. The number of Ly6G⁺ cells was markedly increased in the L + Pg group compared with the Con group, with no significant difference between the L + Pg and L + Pg+B groups. In contrast, the L + Pg+B10 group exhibited a significant reduction in Ly6G⁺ cells compared with the L + Pg and L + Pg+B groups. Furthermore, the number of CD68⁺Ly6G⁺ cells was significantly increased in the L + Pg group compared with the control group, with no difference between the L + Pg and L + Pg+B groups. However, the L + Pg+B10 group displayed the highest proportion of CD68⁺Ly6G⁺ cells among all experimental groups (Fig. 4A-C). Additionally, the expression of MER proto-oncogene tyrosine kinase (MerTK), a typical marker for efferocytosis, was increased in CD68⁺ cells in L + Pg, L + Pg+B, and L + Pg+B10 groups compared to control, and was the highest in the L + Pg+B10 group among all experimental groups (Fig. 4D E). Upregulation of autophagy is beneficial for the resolution of periodontal inflammation and serves as a key mechanism promoting pro-resolving macrophage polarization [42, 43]. To elucidate the regulatory role of B10 in autophagy during periodontitis, the expression of autophagy-related markers was examined. The results demonstrated that LC3B and Atg5 levels were significantly elevated in the L + Pg group compared with the control group. No significant difference was observed between the L + Pg and L + Pg+B groups. However, compared with both the L + Pg and L + Pg+B groups, the expression of LC3B and Atg5 was markedly increased in the B10 adoptive transfer group (Fig. 4F-I). The co-expression of autophagy markers with macrophages was further analyzed. The numbers of CD68⁺LC3B⁺ and CD68+ATG5+ macrophages were dramatically increased in the L + Pg+B10 group compared with the L + Pg and L + Pg+B groups, whereas no significant difference was detected between the L + Pg and L + Pg+B groups (Fig. 4J-M).

Adoptive Transfer of B10 Alleviates Periodontal Inflammation Through Macrophages

To investigate whether macrophages are directly involved in the regulatory function of B10 in periodontal inflammation resolution, macrophages were depleted by intraperitoneal injection of Clodronate Liposomes (Clo-Lipo) prior to ligation. For alveolar bone resorption, the BV/TV ratio was significantly reduced in the L + Pg group compared with the Con group. The BV/TV was higher in the L + Pg+B10 group, compared to L + Pg and MD + L+Pg+B10 group (Ligation plus P. gingivalis infection plus macrophage depletion plus B10 adoptive transfer). There was no significant difference between MD + L+Pg group (Ligation combine P. gingivalis infection with macrophage depletion) and MD + L+Pg+B10 group (Fig. 5A, B). Additionally, the CEJ–ABC distance was significantly increased in the L + Pg group compared with the Con group, indicating severe bone loss. However, the CEJ–ABC distance was markedly reduced in the L + Pg+B10 group compared with the L + Pg group, suggesting a protective effect of B10 cells. In contrast, the MD + L+Pg+B10 group showed a higher CEJ–ABC distance than the L + Pg+B10 group. Compared with the L + Pg group, the MD + L+Pg group exhibited a decreased CEJ–ABC distance; however, there was no significant difference between the MD + L+Pg and MD + L+Pg+B10 groups. A similar trend was observed for osteoclast activation along the alveolar bone surface detected by TRAP staining, consistent with the results of CEJ-ABC distance (Fig. 5D, E). The expression levels of IL-17 A, IL-1β, and TNF-α were significantly increased in the L + Pg group compared with the Con group, but notably decreased in the L + Pg+B10 group relative to the L + Pg group. Discussion

The resolution of periodontitis is regulated by multiple precise mechanisms. Among these, the upregulation of anti-inflammatory mediators, the reduction of pro-inflammatory mediators, and the timely clearance of apoptotic cells and necrotic tissues are critical for resolving inflammation [3–5]. Immunoregulatory cells—such as B10 and pro-resolving macrophages—play important roles in promoting inflammation resolution [15, 39]. This study is the first to demonstrate that B10 cells enhance pro-resolving effects in periodontitis through macrophage-mediated resolvin release and autophagy. Although this study has a small sample size, which may impact the generalizability of the findings, it provides valuable preliminary insights in B10-macrophage interaction in immune regulatory network.

In this study, IVIS was used to observe the dynamic change between B/B10 cells and macrophages in periodontal tissues. The results showed that the expression of B/B10 cells in periodontal tissue peaked on the third day after transfer; meanwhile, the expression of macrophages also significantly increased at the same time point (Fig. 2). This suggests that adoptive transfer of B cells or B10 may promote the early recruitment of macrophages into periodontal inflammatory tissues. Further study revealed that B10 transfer, but not B cells transfer, promoted the polarization of pro-resolving macrophages (CX3CR1+CCR2+) in periodontal inflammatory tissues on the 7th day after transfer (Fig. 3). This finding is consistent with our previous research, which demonstrated that transferred B10 promote the polarization of pro-resolving macrophages (M2) in periodontal tissues [15]. This effect may be due to the increased IL-10 levels in periodontal tissues following B10 transfer, as IL-10 has been shown to promote M2 macrophage differentiation [44]. This has also been confirmed in our recent studies, which showed that B10 promoted pro-resolving macrophage polarization and enhanced their phagocytic function via IL-10, PD-1, and direct cell-cell contact [36, 37]. These collected evidence suggest that B10 cells contribute to the amelioration of periodontitis through regulation of macrophage pro-resolving functions. Although CX3CR1 and CCR2 double positivity is commonly used to identify M2 macrophages, and CX3CR1 is also an important marker of macrophages, the expression of CX3CR1 and CCR2 is not exclusive to macrophages, as both B cells and T cells can also express these molecules. Therefore, the in vivo imaging approach used in this study cannot completely exclude the potential involvement of B cells or T cells. To address this, at the endpoint of the experiment, we employed CD68 and CD206 to verify pro-resloving macrophage (M2) polarization, as these markers are widely accepted for identifying M2 macrophages, which are associated with the pro-resolving phenotype.

The elevation of anti-inflammatory cytokines and the reduction of pro-inflammatory cytokines are key indicators of periodontal inflammation resolution [45]. The results in this study showed that the expression of IL-10 in periodontal tissues was significantly increased, while the expressions of IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-17 A were markedly decreased after B10 transfer, suggesting that B10 adoptive transfer promote the resolution of periodontal inflammation by enhancing anti-inflammatory cytokine and suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokines (Fig. 1). Furthermore, a negative correlation was observed between pro-resolving macrophage (M2) and pro-inflammatory mediator levels after B10 transfer (Figs. 1 and 3), indicating that the B10-mediated resolution of periodontitis is associated with the presence of pro-resolving macrophages.

To further investigate whether B10 adoptive transfer promotes the periodontal inflammation resolution through macrophages, macrophages were depleted via intraperitoneal injection of clodronate liposomes [39]. The results showed that in macrophage-depleted mice, B10 failed to suppress the expression of TNF-α and IL-1β or prevent alveolar bone resorption, and no longer upregulated IL-10 expression (Fig. 5). However, in our previous study, by 5 days post-transfer, the expression of IL-1β and alveolar bone resorption were still reduced in macrophage depletion with B10 transfer group, compared to the macrophage depletion group [15]. This difference may be due to the different time point of detection. In the current study, by 10 days post-transfer, the expression of IL-1β and the activation of osteoclasts were significantly reduced by B10 in the presence of macrophages. However, there was no significant difference between the groups with or without B10 transfer after macrophage depletion. This loss of the effect of B10 transfer, along with the observed decline in B10 numbers over time, suggests that the immunoregulatory function of B10 diminishes as their population decreases. These finding further indicated that the sustained regulatory effect on periodontal inflammation, osteoclast differentiation and alveolar bone loss at later stages (10 days) depends on macrophage function. This notion was supported by the fact that transplantation of M2 macrophages was shown to alleviate periodontal inflammation and alveolar bone resorption [46]. A limitation of this study is that we cannot exclude the potential influence of clodronate liposomes on cells other than monocytes/macrophages, such as osteoclast [47], which need further investigation. In addition, the depletion of macrophages resulted in unexpected changes in the inflammatory responses, such as increased fibrosis and vascularization [48]. The results of this study also demonstrate that B10 consistently suppressed IL-17 A regardless of macrophage presence. It has been reported that Th17, the main source of IL-17 A, expression in periodontitis tissue was significantly decreased in B10 adoptive transfer group [13]. Combined with our findings, this suggests that the suppression of Th17 by B10 cells in periodontitis does not require macrophage mediation.

SPMs are endogenous lipid molecules actively secreted by the host during inflammatory responses to facilitate the resolution of inflammation. It has been reported that upregulating the SPMs in periodontally inflamed tissues ameliorated alveolar bone resorption and pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion [28]. Our results showed that B10 adoptive transfer promoted RvD4 production and the recruitment of CX3CR1+ monocytes/macrophages in periodontal tissues on day 3, and induced pro-resolving macrophage expression by day 7 (Figs. 2 and 3). Studies have reported that RvD4 promotes the early infiltration of pro-resolving monocytes in thrombi and enhances the anti-inflammatory and phagocytic functions of M2 macrophages [49]. Integrating these findings with published research, the results suggest that B10 adoptive transfer may promote M2 macrophage polarization in periodontal inflammatory tissues by enhancing RvD4 production. It should be noted that due to the experimental design, we were unable to determine what cells secret RvD4 after B10 transfer, which will help further understand the immune cellular network interaction and the subsequent B10-induced SPMs cascade.

Numerous studies have indicated that a positive feedback loop between M2 macrophages and the production of SPMs, such as Resolvin D series and Maresin, have been demonstrated to promote M2 macrophage polarization and enhance their phagocytosis of apoptotic cells [23, 24, 30–32, 50, 51]. Meanwhile, pro-resolving macrophages are recognized as important sources of SPMs, such as RvD2, RvD5, and MaR1. Our results showed that M2 macrophages were significantly increased on day 7 post B10 transfer. By day 10 after B10 transfer, RvD2 production in periodontal tissues was significantly increased in a macrophage-dependent manner (Fig. 6). RvD2 has been reported to promote IL-10 expression in hypothalamic inflammatory tissues, thereby modulating disease progression [52]. IL-10 expression in periodontal tissues has been demonstrated in a macrophage-dependent manner after B10 transfer [15]. Collectively, these findings suggest that B10 adoptive transfer-induced macrophage-mediated RvD2 production may represent one potential mechanism of sustained IL-10 upregulation in periodontal tissues, and the subsequent promotion of the inflammation resolution. It is noted that we can’t rule out that the RvD2 is secreted by cells other than macrophages, which warrants further investigation.

Clinical evidence shows that neutrophils are responsible for a substantial portion of inflammatory tissue destruction, and their numbers show a positive correlation with the severity of periodontitis [53]. Consistent with this, our previous study demonstrated a robust negative correlation between the B10-mediated reduction in RANKL+ neutrophils and the severity of alveolar bone resorption [14], suggesting that diminished neutrophil infiltration signifies periodontal inflammation resolution. In this study, the B10 transfer group exhibited a significant decrease in neutrophil numbers alongside a marked increase in their phagocytosis by macrophages (Fig. 4). This finding aligns with the established role of macrophage-mediated efferocytosis in clearing damaged and dying neutrophils [5, 53]. The exacerbation of inflammatory progression due to impaired clearance of apoptotic neutrophils is well-documented [53]. Notably, we found that B10 transfer enhanced the release of Resolvin D2 (RvD2), which is known to promote macrophage phagocytosis of neutrophils [54]. This also suggested that B10 adoptive transfer-promoted inflammation resolution is via macrophage-mediated RvD2 production. The engulfment of apoptotic neutrophils, in return, promotes macrophage differentiation toward a pro-resolving phenotype [55], creating a positive feedback that further drives inflammation resolution in periodontitis.

Autophagy is a crucial cellular process for eliminating harmful intracellular substances and apoptotic organelles, thereby maintaining cellular function. It has been reported that enhanced autophagy facilitate the amelioration of periodontitis [33]. We have shown that B10 transfer increased the expressions of autophagy markers ATG5 and LC3B in periodontitis tissue, but such an effect was attenuated by macrophage depletion (Figs. 4 and 6). Meanwhile, macrophage autophagy was dramatically increased after B10 transfer. These results suggested that B10 inhibits the progression of periodontal inflammation at least partly by promoting macrophage autophagy. Autophagy also plays an important role in regulating the polarization of pro-resolving macrophages [56]. Our study showed that B10-promoted macrophage autophagy is positively correlated with the number of pro-resolving macrophages (Figs. 3 and 4). Since SPMs are known to induce macrophage autophagy [57], and our study demonstrated that B10 adoptive transfer increased the expression of RvD4 and RvD2 in periodontal tissues (Figs. 2 and 6), it suggests that adoptive transfer of B10 may enhance macrophage autophagy through SPMs induction, thereby further driving pro-resolving macrophage functions.

Conclusion

This study suggests that adoptive transfer of B10 contributes to the amelioration of periodontal inflammation and alveolar bone resorption, at least partly through promoting macrophage-mediated pro-resolving functions, including the production of RvD2, enhanced neutrophil clearance, and induction of macrophage autophagy. Continued studies will include further investigations involving more complete phases of inflammation and molecular level pathways on B10-macrophage interactions and resolution controls.
