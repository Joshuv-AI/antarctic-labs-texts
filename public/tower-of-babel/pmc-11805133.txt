The Effect of Low-Level Laser Therapy on Accelerating Tooth Movement in Orthodontic Patients: A Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Mohammad K. Alam, Mohammad Y. Hajeer, Dalia A. Alanazi, Shahad A. Alanazi, Haya O. Alruwaili
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:An adjuvant treatment, known as low-level laser therapy (LLLT), has been suggested to quicken orthodontic tooth movement and perhaps shorten the course of treatment.Materials and Methods:For 60 orthodontic patients aged between 15 and 25, a randomized controlled experiment was carried out (30 males and 30 females). Two groups, Group A (LLLT group, n = 30) and Group B (control group, n = 30), were randomly allocated to the participants. For the first 8 weeks of therapy, Group A got LLLT three times a week using a diode laser with a wavelength of 808 nm and a power output of 100 mW. The laser was delivered for 30 s on each side of the target tooth. Beyond routine orthodontic care, Group B did not get any extra care. Using computerized models, the rate of tooth movement was monitored every 2 weeks for a duration of 12 weeks. The degree of tooth movement was the main result; patient-reported discomfort and gingival health were the secondary outcomes.Results:The group that had LLLT (Group A) demonstrated a notably elevated frequency of tooth movement in contrast to the control group (Group B). Group A’s average monthly tooth movement was 1.2 mm, whereas Group B’s average monthly movement was 0.8 mm (P < 0.01). Furthermore, throughout the early phases of therapy, patients in the LLLT group reported less discomfort, and there were no significant variations in gingival health between the two groups.Conclusion:With its ability to speed orthodontic tooth movement, LLLT may shorten the course of therapy. Additionally, it seems to lessen pain while starting orthodontic therapy.

INTRODUCTION

In order to attain ideal dental and facial aesthetics and correct malocclusions, orthodontic therapy usually requires longer treatment periods, sometimes lasting between 18 and 24 months.[1] Extended treatment times might cause pain to the patient, lower motivation, and lead to a higher chance of problems such as periodontal disease, root resorption, and enamel demineralization.[2]

A potentially useful method to improve the biological mechanisms behind tooth movement is low-level laser treatment (LLLT).[3] Low-intensity laser therapy, or LLLT, applies low-intensity laser light to tissues in an attempt to speed bone remodeling, boost cellular activity, and move teeth more quickly via orthodontics.[4] According to a number of studies, LLLT may considerably speed up tooth movement, lessen orthodontic treatment-related discomfort, and enhance patient results.[56]

Despite the possible advantages, the therapeutic effectiveness of LLLT in quickening tooth movement is still up for question. Although some research has shown promising results, other investigations have shown no discernible differences between LLLT and traditional orthodontic therapy.[7]

MATERIALS AND METHODS

For the research, a total of 60 patients—30 men and 30 women—aged between 15 and 25, who needed orthodontic treatment with permanent appliances, were enlisted. Those in good health without any systemic disorders, those who did not smoke, and those whose orthodontic treatment plan included the evacuation of their first premolars were among the inclusion criteria. Patients on drugs that influence bone metabolism, those with a history of periodontal disease, and those who had previously had orthodontic treatment were all excluded.

Group Allocation and Randomization

Using a computer-generated randomization list, participants were randomized at random to one of the two groups: Group A (LLLT group, n = 30) or Group B (control group, n = 30).

An impartial researcher who was not engaged in the participants’ care or evaluation carried out the randomization.

Intervention

In addition to receiving regular orthodontic treatment, participants in Group A also got LLLT. A diode laser with a wavelength of 808 nm and a power output of 100 mW was used to administer the LLLT. For the first 8 weeks of therapy, the laser was delivered three times a week for 30 s to the buccal and lingual surfaces of the canine and second premolar teeth that were close to the extraction site.

Group B participants had conventional orthodontic therapy without the use of LLLT. Throughout the course of the trial, all patients in both groups had treatment with fixed orthodontic equipment (0.022-inch slot MBT brackets) and were routinely monitored.

Calculating the movement of teeth

The rate of tooth movement served as the main outcome measure. For a duration of 12 weeks, digital models of the dental arches were collected at baseline (T0) and 2-week intervals (T1, T2, T3, etc.).

Measures of secondary outcomes

Gingival health and pain levels, as indicated by the patient, were secondary outcomes. During the first 8 weeks, pain levels were measured at each follow-up visit using a Visual Analog Scale (VAS), which ranged from 0 (no pain) to 10 (worst possible pain). At the start of the research and its conclusion, gingival health was evaluated using the Gingival Index (GI).

Statistical Analysis IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA’s SPSS software, version 23, was used to analyze the data.

RESULTS

Rate of tooth movement

The rate of tooth movement was significantly higher in the LLLT group (Group A) compared to the control group (Group B) over the 12-week study period. The mean monthly tooth movement in Group A was 1.2 ± 0.2 mm, whereas in Group B, it was 0.8 ± 0.3 mm (P < 0.01) [Tables 1-3].

Pain levels

Participants in the LLLT group reported significantly lower pain levels compared to the control group during the initial 8 weeks of treatment. The average VAS pain score in Group A was 3.5 ± 1.1, while in Group B, it was 5.6 ± 1.4 (P < 0.01).

Gingival health

No significant difference in gingival health was observed between the two groups at the end of the study. The mean GI score was 0.9 ± 0.2 in Group A and 1.0 ± 0.3 in Group B (P = 0.35).

DISCUSSION

This randomized controlled trial’s results show that low-level laser treatment (LLLT) dramatically quickens orthodontic tooth movement; in the LLLT group, mean monthly increases were 1.2 mm, whereas in the control group, they were only 0.8 mm (P < 0.01). These findings are consistent with earlier research showing improved tooth mobility with LLLT, indicating that laser therapy may efficiently activate cellular mechanisms involved in bone remodeling, thereby expediting the orthodontic treatment procedure.[12]

The biological mechanism by which LLLT improves tooth movement is hypothesized to include enhanced bone remodeling at the orthodontic force application site by stimulating osteoblast and osteoclast activity.[3] Animal research, like that of Kawasaki and Shimizu,[4] who found that rats receiving orthodontic pressures in addition to LLLT had a faster bone turnover, lends credence to this theory. The results of the present research complement these observations and provide further evidence for the possibility of LLLT as an adjuvant therapy in clinical orthodontics.

This research discovered that LLLT was connected with reduced pain levels throughout the early phases of orthodontic therapy, in addition to hastening tooth movement. Consistent with previous research demonstrating the analgesic benefits of LLLT, participants in the LLLT group reported substantially reduced VAS pain ratings (P < 0.01) when compared to those in the control group. The anti-inflammatory properties of laser treatment, which have been shown to lessen the production of inflammatory mediators and encourage quicker healing of the periodontal tissues, may be responsible for pain reduction.[7]

CONCLUSION

This research concludes that, without sacrificing gingival health, LLLT may dramatically speed up tooth movement and lessen discomfort in orthodontic patients. These results validate the usefulness of LLLT as an adjuvant therapy in orthodontics, with the potential to reduce treatment durations and improve patient comfort.
