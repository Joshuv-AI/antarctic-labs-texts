Modulation of host functions by bacterial serine/threonine protein kinase effector proteins
Authors: Jinli Ge, Jiazhang Qiu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractProtein phosphorylation, one of the most ubiquitous and evolutionarily conserved posttranslational modifications, serves as a master regulator of cellular signaling networks. In host–pathogen interactions, bacterial subversion of phosphorylation-dependent signaling pathways has emerged as a pivotal mechanism of microbial pathogenesis. Notably, recent advances in eukaryotic-like serine/threonine protein kinase effector proteins have profoundly advanced our understanding of molecular pathogenesis through their ability to hijack host cell signaling. In this review, we will summarize the sophisticated strategies by which bacterial serine/threonine protein kinase effectors manipulate host phosphorylation networks to enhance virulence and promote infection.Keywords: posttranslational modifications, phosphorylation, serine/threonine protein kinase (STPK), bacterial pathogens, host substrate

Eukaryotic hosts have evolved complex pathogen recognition mechanisms to initiate immediate innate immune signaling cascades upon detecting microbial invasion (1). To overcome these immune responses, bacterial pathogens employ numerous protein effectors that are translocated directly into the host cytosol via specialized secretion systems, such as the type III secretion system (T3SS) and type IV secretion system (2). These effectors target key host proteins to reprogram signal transduction pathways, thereby promoting bacterial infection (3, 4, 5).

Protein phosphorylation represents the most ubiquitous and evolutionarily conserved posttranslational modifications (PTMs), occurring across all domains of life from prokaryotes to eukaryotes (6). Protein kinases transfer phosphate groups (PO43−) group from energy molecules ATP or GTP to specific amino acid residues on substrates (7). They can be divided into five major classes based on their target residues, including serine/threonine protein kinases (STPKs), tyrosine protein kinases, bispecific protein kinases, histidine-specific kinases, and aspartate protein kinase acid/glutamate-specific protein kinases (8). The human genome encodes over 500 protein kinases, most of which are STPK (9). Importantly, eukaryotic kinases exhibit amino acid sequence homology within their kinase domains, particularly with highly conserved functional motifs participating in positioning the phosphate donor ATP molecule and the protein substrate for catalysis (10, 11). Structurally, eukaryotic kinase domains exhibit a conserved bilobal structure comprising 12 subdomains (Hanks domains), forming a catalytic core where the N-terminal β-strand–rich lobe and C-terminal α-helical lobe create a deep cleft housing the active site (Fig. 1) (10, 11). STPK phosphorylate diverse substrates and regulate critical processes such as growth, proliferation, metabolism, and programmed cell death (12, 13, 14). Abnormal kinase signaling is closely linked to various diseases (15). To date, more than 200,000 serine and threonine phosphorylation sites on human proteins have been identified, yet fewer than 4% have been linked to a specific kinase (9). The substrates of most STPK remain unknown, greatly limiting our understanding of phosphorylation-dependent signaling networks.

Eukaryotic kinases catalytic domain. The twelve conserved subdomains (numbered with Roman numerals) fold into a characteristic two-lobed catalytic core. In this structure, the smaller N-terminal lobe is responsible for ATP binding, while the larger C-terminal lobe engages the protein substrate and catalyzes phosphate transfer.

Given the central role of protein phosphorylation in eukaryotic signaling, it is not surprising that many bacterial pathogens have evolved effector protein with STPK activities to manipulate cell signaling pathways, thereby promoting bacterial infection. Consequently, research on the biochemical characterization of bacterial STPK as well as identification of their phosphorylation targets would greatly advance our understanding on the mechanisms of host–pathogen interaction. In this review, we will explore the roles of bacterial STPK effector proteins in modulating host cellular processes, including immune evasion, signal transduction, and cytoskeletal rearrangement. By summarizing recent advances in this field, the review aims to provide a comprehensive understanding of how bacterial STPKs contribute to pathogenesis and to highlight their potential as targets for novel therapeutic strategies.

Salmonella SteC

Salmonella enterica is a leading cause of foodborne gastroenteritis in humans and animals. The pathogenesis of Salmonella is largely dependent on T3SS-1 and T3SS-2 encoded by SPI-1 and SPI-2, respectively (16). These specialized protein translocation apparatuses deliver effector proteins directly into the host cytosol, where they function to modulate various host cellular functions to facilitate bacterial invasion as well as subsequent intracellular survival and proliferation. T3SS-1 effectors orchestrate actin cytoskeletal remodeling, thereby producing membrane ruffling that drives bacterial invasion (17). Following internalization, Salmonella survives in a plasma membrane–derived vacuole called Salmonella-containing vacuoles (SCVs). Subsequently, the acidic environment and low nutrient levels of the SCV lumen activate T3SS-2 expression (18, 19). Effectors translocated by T3SS-2 manipulate cellular functions (e.g., vesicle trafficking) and work synergistically to generate a favorable environment that supports intracellular bacterial growth.

SteC is a critical T3SS-2 effector that functions as an STPK. Upon translocation into host cells, SteC triggers extensive reorganization of the actin cytoskeleton surrounding the SCVs through its kinase activity, culminating in the assembly of a dense F-actin meshwork (20). This specialized actin structure reinforces the SCV membrane, thereby preserving vacuolar integrity and promoting intracellular bacterial proliferation (20). The assembly process occurs through a coordinated multistep mechanism. Initially, SteC phosphorylates heat shock protein 27 (HSP27) (21). HSP27 functions as an actin monomer-sequestering protein. Stimulus-dependent phosphorylation of Ser-15, Ser-78, and Ser-82 in HSP27 resulted in its dissociation with actin, thereby providing free G-actin monomers for nucleation and promoting F-actin polymerization (22, 23). Importantly, SteC phosphorylates at least six residues on HSP27, including two physiologically relevant sites (Ser-15 and Ser-82) (21). Consequently, HSP27 phosphorylated by SteC might elevate the local concentration of free G-actin, thus increasing polymerization and stabilizing F-actin structures surrounding the SCVs. Indeed, overexpressing SteC in HSP27 knockdown cells led to significantly lower levels of F-actin bundles (21). Subsequently, SteC orchestrates actin cytoskeletal remodeling through phosphorylation of formin-like proteins (FMNLs) (24). FMNLs play a pivotal role in initiating actin polymerization and mediating F-actin bundling, processes typically regulated by the small GTPase Cdc42 (25). Intriguingly, SteC directly interacts with the N-terminal domain of FMNL1 in a kinase-independent manner (24). Phosphoproteomic analysis identified multiple SteC-dependent phosphorylation sites on FMNL proteins. Of particular significance, a phosphomimetic mutation at one of these sites substantially increased FMNL1's binding affinity for Cdc42 (24). These findings suggest that SteC-mediated phosphorylation may potentiate FMNL activation by Cdc42, thereby stimulating localized actin assembly and bundling around the bacterial vacuoles (24, 25). Finally, SteC activates the MEK/ERK signaling cascade through direct phosphorylation of MEK1 at Ser-200 (26). Phosphorylation of Ser-200 is predicted to disrupt an autoinhibitory helix, promoting autophosphorylation at the canonical MEK activation sites (i.e., Ser-218 and Ser-222) (26). Consequently, this modification stimulates the MEK/ERK signaling cascade, ultimately activating myosin light chain which induces actomyosin contractility and promotes F-actin bundling (26). Beyond its role in maintaining the intracellular replicative niche, SteC critically enables systemic dissemination. Salmonella breaches the gut-vascular barrier via SPI-2–dependent mechanisms that involve hijacking CX3CR1 macrophages (27, 28). SteC directly phosphorylates Ser-19 and Thr-20 residues on the myosin light chain Myl12a, triggering actomyosin contraction and cytoskeletal reorganization independently of the canonical MEK/ERK–MLCK pathway (28). Notably, SteC exhibits broad nucleotide substrate specificity (utilizing ATP/GTP/CTP/UTP), enabling sustained kinase activity under host ATP-depleted conditions to drive macrophage motility (Fig. 2) (28). Consequently, whereas WT Salmonella hijacks macrophages to transit into the bloodstream, ΔsteC fail to induce actin rearrangement, resulting in their retention near intestinal vasculature, reduced gut-vascular barrier penetration efficiency, and diminished hepatic/splenic colonization (28). Furthermore, although ribosomal preservation factor Serbp was recently identified as a host substrate of SteC via thermal proteome profiling and in vitro kinase assays (29), its potential roles in Salmonella replication or F-actin restructuring during infection remain to be elucidated.

Yersinia YpkA/YopO

Among the 26 Yersinia species, only Yersinia pestis, Yersinia pseudotuberculosis, and Yersinia enterocolitica are pathogenic to humans. These pathogenic Yersinia species share a conserved T3SS encoded on a 70 kb virulence plasmid that plays a crucial role in immune evasion (30). The T3SS delivers Yersinia outer proteins (Yops) into host cells to inhibit phagocytosis, suppress inflammatory cytokine production, and modulate cell death signaling pathways (30). One of these Yersinia outer proteins, Yersinia protein kinase A (YpkA/YopO), is a critical virulence factor that modulates host actin cytoskeletal rearrangements and contributes to the antiphagocytic response (31). YpkA/YopO is a multidomain protein that contains an N-terminal STPK domain, a C-terminal guanine dissociation inhibitor (GDI) domain, and a carboxyl-terminal actin binding domain (32). The GDI domain interacts with host small GTPases RhoA and Rac1, regulators of cytoskeletal organization, locking them in their inactive GDP-bound forms to prevent cytoskeletal rearrangement (32). Once secreted into the host cytosol, YpkA/YopO binds globular actin, which activates its kinase activity (33). Actin is sandwiched between the kinase domain and GDI domain, stabilizing the kinase in an active conformation. The STPK activity of YpkA/YopO is essential for virulence, as deletion mutants show significantly attenuated lethality in murine infection models (33).

An earlier study by Navarro et al. demonstrated that YpkA/YopO phosphorylates the heterotrimeric G proteins Gαq (34). Gαq couples with G protein–coupled receptors to orchestrate actin cytoskeletal remodeling and regulate diverse cellular processes, including cell growth and proliferation, platelet aggregation, and actin cytoskeletal reorganization (35). This regulatory protein exhibits intrinsic GTPase activity and undergoes conformational cycling between inactive GDP-bound and active GTP-bound states through nucleotide exchange at its guanine-nucleotide binding domain. YpkA/YopO specifically phosphorylates Gαq at Ser-47, a critical residue located in the highly conserved diphosphate binding loop of the GTPase (34). This phosphorylation event impairs the GTP-binding capacity of Gαq, preventing its activation and consequently disrupting actin filament organization in host cells (34). Notably, YpkA/YopO does not solely target G protein signaling, it also exploits actin as a molecular scaffold to recruit and phosphorylate a broader repertoire of cytoskeletal regulators, including VASP, EVL, mDia1, INF2, gelsolin, cofilin, WASP, and WIP (36). Among these, VASP is a critical regulator of actin assembly, cell adhesion and motility. The kinase activity of YpkA/YopO has been found to be essential for VASP phosphorylation on Ser-157 (37). The YpkA/YopO-mediated phosphorylation of VASP significantly inhibits actin polymerization and promotes actin cytoskeleton disruption, thereby contributing to phagocytosis inhibition (37). Furthermore, YpkA/YopO also targets gelsolin (38). Gelsolin normally remodels actin organization through its Ca2+-dependent actin filament severing and capping activities (39). This protein contains six gelsolin homology domains (G1–G6) that adopt a compact globular conformation in the absence of calcium. Calcium binding induces a conformational change that exposes the actin-binding sites, enabling its actin binding and severing function (40, 41). Singaravelu et al. demonstrated that YpkA/YopO phosphorylates gelsolin within the interdomain linker region connecting G3 and G4 domains, which partially mimics the calcium-dependent activation of gelsolin (38). Modification of gelsolin by YpkA/YopO resulted in decreased filamentous actin and perturbed actin dynamics in phagocytic cells, suggesting that YpkA/YopO can bypass native calcium-signaling pathways to activate gelsolin, thereby manipulating the host actin cytoskeleton and promoting resistance to phagocytosis (38). Collectively, these studies provide mechanistic insights into how YpkA/YopO's kinase activity contributes to Yersinia pathogenesis.

Shigella OspG

Shigella flexneri is a Gram-negative facultative intracellular pathogen that is responsible for a spectrum of symptoms ranging from watery diarrhea to severe dysentery (42). The pathogenesis of S. flexneri is primarily mediated by a 200 kb plasmid coding for T3SS and its associated effector proteins (43). Among the effector proteins, OspG shares significant structural homology with the catalytic domain of STPK. Despite the absence of subdomains VIII–XI, OspG displays robust kinase functionality, as evidenced by its in vitro autophosphorylation activity (44). Importantly, OspG forms stable complexes with host ubiquitin-conjugating E2 enzymes (e.g., UbcH5c/UbcH5b) and ubiquitin without phosphorylating them (44, 45). Instead, such interaction appears to be critical for the activation of the kinase activity of OspG (45, 46, 47).

OspG has been demonstrated to suppress the host innate immune response by inhibiting NF-κB signaling, a phenotype dependent on its kinase activity and ubiquitin-binding capability (44). However, the cellular substrates of OspG mediating this immune evasion remained unidentified. In a recent study, Xian et al. employed an unbiased large-scale phosphoproteomic approach and identified (cullin-associated NEDD8-dissociated protein 1 [CAND1]), a key regulator of cullin-RING ubiquitin ligase assembly, as a major phosphorylation target of OspG (48). Intriguingly, OspG phosphorylates CAND1 on multiple serine and tyrosine residues, suggesting that OspG functions as a noncanonical dual-specificity (serine/tyrosine) kinase. Phosphorylation of CAND1 disrupts its interaction with cullins, which is predicted to impair the activity of numerous cullin-RING ubiquitin ligase E3 ligases in host cells (48). Consistent with this, ubiquitome profiling revealed widespread alterations in host protein ubiquitination upon infection with OspG-expressing S. flexneri compared to the ΔospG mutant (48). Notably, Gene Ontology analysis highlighted significant enrichment of proteins involved in diverse biological processes, including the NF-κB pathway (e.g., TRAF2, IKBKG), providing mechanistic insight into OspG-mediated NF-κB suppression (48). Strikingly, several septin family members (SEPT2, SEPT7, SEPT9, SEPT10, and SEPT11) exhibited elevated ubiquitination levels in cells challenged with S. flexneri strain expressing OspG (48). Septins are cytoskeletal components that polymerize into filamentous structures (49). Previous studies reported that septins assemble into cage-like structures around cytosolic bacteria, including S. flexneri, thereby restricting bacterial dissemination and promoting autophagic clearance (50, 51, 52). Xian et al. (48) further demonstrated that OspG-induced septin ubiquitination disrupts the formation of these antimicrobial cages, facilitating bacterial evasion. Together, these findings reveal a sophisticated pathogen strategy in which S. flexneri exploits OspG to hijack host ubiquitin signaling, thereby neutralizing both NF-κB–mediated immunity and septin-based cytoskeletal defenses.

Escherichia NleH1/2

Enteropathogenic Escherichia coli (EPEC) and enterohemorrhagic E. coli, two diarrheagenic strains of E. coli, contribute significantly to the health burden of food-borne diseases. The pathogenicity of both EPEC and Enterohemorrhagic E. coli requires the functional T3SS that translocates effector proteins into host enterocyte during infection (53). Among these effectors, NleH1 and NleH2 are OspG homologs that possess a minimal kinase fold lacking subdomains VIII–XI (54). Earlier studies have demonstrated that NleH effectors participate in suppressing host innate immune responses. Gao et al. (55) revealed that both NleH1 and NleH2 interact with the N-terminal region of ribosomal protein S3 (RPS3) in a kinase activity–independent manner. RPS3 plays a crucial role in guiding the recruitment of the NF-κB p65 subunit to specific promoters in response to various stimuli (56). Nonetheless, only NleH1 decreases nuclear accumulation of RPS3, thereby blocking NF-κB signaling. The nuclear localization of RPS3 is promoted by IκB kinase (IKK) β phosphorylation on Ser-209 which increases its association with importin-α (57). Interestingly, the binding between NleH1 and RPS3 specifically inhibits Ser-209 phosphorylation by IKKβ (55). However, neither RPS3 nor IKKβ was found to be direct phosphorylation substrates of NleH1. Subsequent high-throughput screening identified v-Crk sarcoma virus CT10 oncogene-like protein (CRKL) as a bona fide host phosphorylation target of NleH1 (58). CRKL was shown to interact with both IKKβ and NleH1, suggesting a potential bridging function (58). Based on these findings, a mechanistic model was proposed wherein CRKL mediates the recruitment of NleH1 to the IKKβ complex through protein–protein interactions, thereby enabling NleH1 to inhibit the RPS3/NF-κB signaling pathway.

Additionally, during EPEC infection, NleH effectors suppress apoptosis by inhibiting elevation of cytosolic Ca2+ concentrations, nuclear condensation, caspase-3 activation, and membrane blebbing. This antiapoptotic activity of NleH is independent of its kinase function but may be mediated through its specific interaction with Bax inhibitor-1 (Fig. 3) (59).

In contrast to abovementioned findings, a recent study by Pollock et al. (60) failed to observe NF-κB inhibition or suppression of caspase-3/caspase-8 cleavage by NleH effectors. Instead, their phosphoproteomic analysis identified the microvillar protein Eps8 as a bona fide phosphorylation substrate of both NleH1 and NleH2. Both effectors bind to the Src homology 3 domain of Eps8 via their noncanonical “proline-rich” motifs (PxxDY) (60). Eps8 is a multifunctional actin regulatory protein that exhibits both capping and bundling activities, playing an essential role in the proper morphogenesis of intestinal microvilli (61). During EPEC infection, phosphorylation of Eps8 at Ser-775 disrupts its actin-bundling function and triggers its dissociation from attaching and effacing lesions (Fig. 3) (60).

Mycobacterium tuberculosis PknG

M. tuberculosis, the etiological agent of tuberculosis, remains one of the most lethal infectious pathogens worldwide (62). This sophisticated pathogen establishes intracellular niches within host macrophages through a complex interplay of secreted virulence determinants and targeted manipulation of host cellular pathways (63, 64). Among these, PknG is a STPK that has emerged as a key regulator of M. tuberculosis pathogenesis, operating at the critical interface of host–pathogen interactions to ensure bacterial survival (65). Upon secretion into the host cytoplasm, PknG disrupts host vesicular trafficking, blocks phagosome-lysosome fusion, suppresses immune pathways (including NF-κB and xenophagy), and modulates redox balance. These actions promote the establishment of a protected niche within macrophages (66, 67, 68). Interestingly, a recent study demonstrates that PknG also suppresses proinflammatory TNFα expression and secretion (69). During early M. tuberculosis infection, host-generated reactive nitrogen intermediates trigger S-nitrosylation of PknG at Cys-109, which is critical for its secretion into macrophages (69). Phosphoproteomic analysis revealed that secreted S-nitrosylated PknG phosphorylates macrophage silencer of death domains at Thr-405. Phosphorylated silencer of death domain irreversibly interacts with TNFR1 death domain, thereby blocking activation of caspase-8 and concomitant extrinsic apoptosis (69).

Legionella STPKs

Legionella pneumophila, a Gram-negative facultative intracellular pathogen, is the etiological agent of Legionnaires' disease, a severe and often fatal form of pneumonia (70). The defective in organelle trafficking/intracellular multiplication (Dot/Icm) T4SS delivers an extensive repertoire of over 330 effector proteins into the host cytosol (71). These effectors act in concert to hijack multiple host cellular pathways, enabling the biogenesis and maturation of the Legionella-containing vacuole (LCV) (71). This specialized membrane-bound compartment evades lysosomal fusion and provides a protected niche that supports intracellular bacterial survival and replication (72). Many of the Dot/Icm substrates have acquired structural domains that are evolutionarily conserved in eukaryotic proteins, particularly those mediating protein interactions or enzymatic activities (e.g., kinases, E3 ubiquitin ligases, phospholipases) (71, 72). Among these effectors, LegK1-LegK4, LegK7, and Lem28 were predicted to function as protein kinases based on significant sequence homology to STPKs (73, 74, 75). Importantly, their potential kinase activities were validated via detection of self-phosphorylation in vitro biochemical reactions (76). Over the past decade, significant progress has been made in identifying host substrates and elucidating the biological functions of these effector kinases, providing crucial mechanistic insights into their roles in Legionella pathogenesis.

LegK1 functions as an STPK that directly activates NF-κB signaling through its kinase activity and independent of upstream NF-κB components including TRAFs, NIK, MEKK3, and TAK1 (77). Interestingly, both in vitro and in vivo studies showed that LegK1 could induce phosphorylation of IκB, an inhibitor the NF-κB, on Ser-32 and Ser-36 in an IKK-independent manner (77). In addition, LegK1 also phosphorylates other IκB family members such as p100, in the noncanonical NF-κB pathway, promoting its processing into p52 form (77). Therefore, it is suggested that LegK1 functions as a mimicry of host IKK. These findings establish LegK1 as a bacterial effector capable of directly modulating both canonical and noncanonical NF-κB signaling, likely contributing to the regulation of inflammatory responses during L. pneumophila infection (Fig. 4).

The Legionella effector kinases exhibit distinct host-targeting activities. LegK1 phosphorylates IκB, resulting in NF-κB pathway activation. LegK2 phosphorylates the ARP2/3 complex to inhibit actin polymerization. LegK3 phosphorylates caspase-3, caspase-7, and caspase-9, thereby blocking apoptosis and pyroptosis. LegK4 impairs global protein synthesis by phosphorylating Hsp70. LegK7 hijacks the Hippo signaling pathway via MOB1 phosphorylation. HSP, heat shock protein.

LegK2 is a Dot/Icm effector essential for L. pneumophila virulence, as evidenced by the significant intracellular growth defect of ΔlegK2 mutants in Acanthamoeba castellanii (73). The legK2 mutant exhibits impaired recruitment of endoplasmic reticulum–derived vesicles to the LCV, resulting in delayed bacterial replication. These defects strictly depend on its kinase activity (73). Michard et al. demonstrated that LegK2 targets the actin nucleating ARP2/3 complex, specifically phosphorylating its ARPC1B and ARP3 subunits (78). This phosphorylation induces global actin cytoskeleton reorganization in host cells. During infection, LegK2 localizes to the LCV membrane where it suppresses actin polymerization on the vacuolar surface. By inhibiting actin polymerization, LegK2 prevents late endosome/lysosome fusion with the phagosome, thereby facilitating the conversion of the LCV into a replication-permissive niche (78). The LegK2–ARP2/3 interaction represents a novel virulence mechanism whereby bacterial-mediated local actin remodeling subverts host vesicular trafficking to evade immune defenses (Fig. 4).

Our recent study identified the Legionella effector LegK3 as a bacterial STPK that suppresses host cell apoptosis in a kinase-dependent manner (79). Mechanistic investigations revealed that LegK3 directly phosphorylates key apoptotic caspases (caspase-3, caspase-7, and caspase-9). Interestingly, phosphorylation of caspases by LegK3 occurs at residues located in the prodomain or interdomain linkers (Ser-29 in caspase-3, Ser-199 in caspase-7, and Thr-102 in caspase-9, respectively) (79). These phosphorylation events functionally impair caspase activation by initiator caspases or upstream regulators, while preserving their intrinsic proteolytic activity (79). These findings highlight a novel mechanism used by which L. pneumophila preserves infected cell viability to facilitate intracellular replication (Fig. 4).

LegK4 represents a novel dimeric STPK whose activity is regulated through dimerization rather than conventional activation loop phosphorylation (80). Using a chemical genetic approach, Moss et al. (81) revealed Hsp70 chaperones (Hsc70, Hsp72, and BiP) as direct substrates of LegK4. The kinase specifically phosphorylates a conserved threonine residue in the substrate-binding domain (Thr-495 in Hsc70/Hsp72 and Thr-518 in BiP), leading to impaired ATPase activity and protein refolding capacity (81). LegK4-mediated phosphorylation of cytosolic Hsp70 suppresses global host translation and enhances its recruitment to actively translating polysomes (81). This finding establishes a novel role for Hsp70 in regulating protein synthesis, revealing how a single PTM can directly interface with the host translational apparatus (Fig. 4).

Unlike the abovementioned L. pneumophila effector kinases, LegK7 shows limited primary sequence homology to STPKs but exhibits strong structural similarity based on profile HMM predictions (75). Using an innovative phosphorylation target identification platform that combines thiophosphate labeling with human protein microarrays, Lee and Machner demonstrated MOB1, a central scaffold protein in the Hippo signaling pathway, as a key phosphorylation substrate of LegK7 (75). Strikingly, LegK7 phosphorylates MOB1 at Thr-12 and Thr-35, the same residues modified by the host kinases MST1/2 (the mammalian Hippo orthologs), suggesting that LegK7 functionally mimics host Hippo kinase activity (75). LegK7-mediated phosphorylation activates MOB1, leading to the degradation of downstream transcriptional regulators YAP1/TAZ (75). This cascade ultimately reprograms host gene expression to create a favorable environment suitable for bacterial replication. Similar to LegK4, LegK7 does not require phosphorylation of the activation loop. Instead, its kinase activity is stimulated upon interaction with MOB1, revealing MOB1's dual role as both a substrate and an allosteric activator of LegK7 (82, 83). Structural analysis of the LegK7–MOB1A complex revealed that the N-terminal extension of MOB1A and the kinase domain of LegK7 constitute the interaction interface. Importantly, in addition to regulating LegK7–MOB1A complex formation, the N-terminal extension serves as a docking platform to recruit downstream substrates including the transcriptional coregulator YAP1 (82). Collectively, these findings establish a complicated molecular mimicry mechanism employed by L. pneumophila to manipulate the host Hippo signaling pathway (Fig. 4).

Conclusion and outlook

To counteract host immune defenses and facilitate infection, many bacterial pathogens have evolved effector proteins that are delivered into host cells. These effectors frequently possess enzymatic activities that mediate distinct PTMs (e.g. protein phosphorylation), enabling rapid manipulation of host signaling pathways during infection (71). Recent advances in studying effector proteins with STPK activity have significantly enhanced our understanding of their pathogenic roles. Notably, although some bacterial effectors lack primary sequence homology with eukaryotic kinases, they exhibit remarkably similar secondary structures. Bioinformatics approaches like HHpred, which predicts protein structures and detects distantly related sequences, have become invaluable tools for identifying potential STPKs. Comprehensive genomic analyses of hypothetical proteins across bacterial species may reveal additional STPK repertoires. For instance, examination of Legionella effector proteins identified five additional putative kinases (Lpg1104, Lpg1316, Lpg1317, Lpg1930, and Lpg2050) containing canonical kinase domains, though their enzymatic activities require experimental validation (Table 1).

Identifying host substrates represents a critical step in understanding effector kinases' roles in microbial pathogenesis. However, this process remains particularly challenging due to the transient nature of kinase–substrate interactions and the complex phosphorylation networks resulting from endogenous host kinase activity. Although genetic, biochemical, proteomic, and high-throughput screening approaches have successfully identified host substrates for several bacterial effector kinases, and many targets remain elusive. For instance, while the Legionella effector kinase Lem28 requires inositol hexakisphosphate for activation, its cellular substrates and specific role in Legionella pathogenesis have not been fully elucidated (74). Moreover, since a single STPK may phosphorylate multiple host substrates, comprehensive identification of these substrates through phosphoproteomic approaches would significantly broad our understanding of the host pathways they disrupt.

Collectively, we have summarized how effector proteins from diverse pathogens subvert phosphorylation-mediated host signaling pathways to compromise fundamental host cellular processes, such as immune responses and maintenance of cell shape and integrity. These examples underscore the critical importance of protein phosphorylation in the host and highlight the remarkable diversity of substrates targeted by effector kinases during infection. Ultimately, advances in the discovery and characterization of effector kinases and their roles in disrupting host defenses to promote bacterial survival and replication reveal potential novel antimicrobial targets and therapeutic strategies.
