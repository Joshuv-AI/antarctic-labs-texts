Normal covering numbers for S n S_{n} and A n A_{n} and additive combinatorics Thanks: SE is supported by the Royal Society. CM was supported by an MSRC summer internship.
Sean Eberhard, Connor Mellon

Abstract
. The normal covering number γ ⁡ ( G ) \gamma(G) of a noncyclic group G G is the minimum number of proper subgroups whose conjugates cover the group. We give various estimates for γ ⁡ ( S n ) \gamma(S_{n}) and γ ⁡ ( A n ) \gamma(A_{n}) depending on the arithmetic structure of n n . In particular we determine the limsups over γ ⁡ ( S n ) / n \gamma(S_{n})/n and γ ⁡ ( A n ) / n \gamma(A_{n})/n over the sequences of even and odd integers, as well as the liminf of γ ⁡ ( S n ) / n \gamma(S_{n})/n over even integers. In general we explain how the values of γ ⁡ ( S n ) / n \gamma(S_{n})/n and γ ⁡ ( A n ) / n \gamma(A_{n})/n are related to problems in additive combinatorics. These results answer most of the questions posed by Bubboloni, Praeger, and Spiga as Problem 20.17 of the Kourovka Notebook.



1.
Introduction

A
normal covering
of a group
G
is a family
\mathcal{C}
of proper subgroups
H<G
whose conjugates together cover the group:
G=\bigcup_{g\in G}\bigcup_{H\in\mathcal{C}}H^{g}
.
Clearly a group has a normal covering if and only if the group is noncyclic.
The
normal covering number

\gamma(G)
of a noncyclic group
G
is the minimal cardinality of a normal covering of
G
.
In this paper we only consider normal coverings and so, to be brief, we will often refer to a normal covering
\mathcal{C}
simply as a
covering
or a
covering family
.
Note that if
G
if finite (or even finitely generated) there is no loss in generality in assuming
\mathcal{C}
contains only maximal subgroups.

There are plenty of groups for which
\gamma(G)=1
.
For example,
\operatorname{GL}_{n}(\mathbf{C})
(
n\geq 2
) is covered by the conjugates of the Borel subgroup consisting of upper triangular matrices.
However, a well-known theorem of Jordan asserts that no finite group
G
is the union of conjugates of a proper subgroup.
Thus
\gamma(G)>1
for every finite (noncyclic) group
G
.
There is a wide variety of finite groups with
\gamma(G)=2
(see Garonzi–Lucchini
[
10
]
).
The finite simple groups with
\gamma(G)=2
were recently classified by Bubboloni, Spiga, and Weigel
[
6
]
.

Normal covering numbers arise naturally in the study of intersective polynomials. A polynomial
f\in\mathbf{Z}[x]
is
intersective
if it has no root in
\mathbf{Q}
but it has a root modulo
m
for every positive integer
m
.
Examples include
f(x)=(x^{2}-13)(x^{2}-17)(x^{2}-221)
and
f(x)=(x^{3}-19)(x^{2}+x+1)
.
Let
n=\deg(f)
and let
G
be the Galois group of
f
, which we may consider to be a subgroup of
S_{n}
. Suppose
f
factorizes as
f_{1}\cdots f_{m}
. Let
K
be a splitting field of
f
and let
\theta_{i}\in K
be a root of
f_{i}
for each
i=1,\dots,m
. Then it follows from the existence of the Frobenius element and the Chebotarev density theorem that the subgroups
H_{i}=\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbf{Q}(\theta_{i}))
for
1\leq i\leq m
form a normal covering of
G
.
Thus
m\geq\gamma(G)
.
In particular, the theorem of Jordan quoted above implies that
f
must be reducible.
The two examples given above have Galois groups
C_{2}\times C_{2}
and
S_{3}
, which have
\gamma(G)=3
and
\gamma(G)=2
, respectively.
See
[
1
]
for more on this.

Since a generic Galois extension of
\mathbf{Q}
has Galois group
S_{n}
or
A_{n}
, there is particular reason to be interested in the values of
\gamma(S_{n})
and
\gamma(A_{n})
.
For exact values for small
n
, see
[
2
]
*Table 1.
These numbers were first investigated in earnest by Bubboloni and Praeger in
[
5
]
, who proved bounds of the form
a\phi(n)\leq\gamma(G)\leq bn
for constants
a,b>0
, where
\phi
is Euler’s totient function.
A linear (but weak) lower bound was subsequently established by Bubboloni, Praeger, and Spiga
[
3
]
.
An exact formula for
\gamma(S_{n})
was conjectured in
[
2
]
(and
[
15
]
*18.23),
and then refuted in
[
4
]
, using a construction of Maróti.
The problem of determining the limsups and liminfs of
\gamma(S_{n})/n
and
\gamma(A_{n})/n
over even and odd integers, respectively (
8
questions in all) is now listed as Problem 20.17 of the Kourovka Notebook
[
15
]
.

We add a new chapter to this story by showing how normal covering numbers for
S_{n}
and
A_{n}
are related to some elementary puzzles in additive combinatorics. Using this translation, we obtain some new exact formulas for some special cases. For example, we have
\gamma(A_{p})=\left\lfloor{(p+1)/3}\right\rfloor
for all sufficiently large primes
p
not of the form
(q^{d}-1)/(q-1)
.
Moreover, we find
5
of the
8
limits in question (
3
of which are new),
and obtain much tighter bounds on the remaining
3
than were previously known.

Theorem 1.1
.

We have the following asymptotic estimates for the normal covering numbers
\gamma(S_{n})
and
\gamma(A_{n})
:

\displaystyle\limsup_{n~\textup{even}}\gamma(S_{n})/n=1/4,

\displaystyle\limsup_{n~\textup{even}}\gamma(A_{n})/n=1/4,

\displaystyle\limsup_{n~\textup{odd}}\gamma(S_{n})/n=1/2,

\displaystyle\limsup_{n~\textup{odd}}\gamma(A_{n})/n=1/3,

\displaystyle\liminf_{n~\textup{even}}\gamma(S_{n})/n=1/6,

\displaystyle\liminf_{n~\textup{even}}\gamma(A_{n})/n\in[1/18,1/6],

\displaystyle\liminf_{n~\textup{odd}}\gamma(S_{n})/n\in[1/12,1/6],

\displaystyle\liminf_{n~\textup{odd}}\gamma(A_{n})/n\in[1/6,4/15].

Moreover, the three outstanding questions are essentially reduced to two questions in additive combinatorics related to sum-free and cube-free sets with coprimality conditions.
Let us now describe these problems. First, call a subset
X\subset\mathbf{Z}/{n}\mathbf{Z}

coprime-sum-free
if there does not exist
x,y,x+y\in X
with
\gcd(x,y,n)=1
.

Problem 1.2
.

Determine the size of the largest symmetric coprime-sum-free subset of
\mathbf{Z}/{n}\mathbf{Z}
for all
n\geq 1
.

Absent the coprimality condition, the solution to this problem is a well-known consequence of Kneser’s generalization of the Cauchy–Davenport Theorem: see for example Diananda–Yap
[
7
]
. The answer is as follows. If
n
has a prime factor
p\equiv 2\pmod{3}
then the answer is exactly
(1+1/p)n/3
, where
p
is the smallest such factor.
Otherwise, if
n
is divisible by
3
then the answer is
n/3
.
Finally, if every prime factor of
n
is congruent to
1\pmod{3}
then the answer is
(1-1/n)/3
.

The coprimality condition makes the problem more complicated.
If
n
is divisible by
6
, the example
X=\{x\in\mathbf{Z}/{n}\mathbf{Z}:2\mid x~\text{or}~3\mid x\}
shows that
X
can have size
(2/3)n
, and moreover we can show (see
Proposition

5.4
) that no coprime-sum-free set can have size greater than
(2/3)n
. This fact turns out to give the value
\liminf_{n~\textup{even}}\gamma(S_{n})/n=1/6
.
But what if
n
is odd? Then the constant ought to drop to
7/15
, and even smaller if
n
is not divisible by
3
, etc.
A complete answer to
Problem

1.2
would clarify how
\gamma(S_{n})/n
(
n
even) and
\gamma(A_{n})/n
(
n
odd) depend on the presence of small factors.

Next, for integers
x,y,z
define

\operatorname{cube}(x,y,z)=\{x,y,z,x+y,x+z,y+z,x+y+z\}.

Call a subset
X\subset\{1,\dots,n-1\}

coprime-cube-free
if there does not exist
x,y,z
with
\gcd(x,y,z,n)=1
and
\operatorname{cube}(x,y,z)\subset X
.

Problem 1.3
.

Determine the size of the largest symmetric coprime-cube-free subset of
\{1,\dots,n-1\}
for all
n\geq 1
.

Even without the coprimality condition, avoidance problems for higher-order configurations like cubes can be difficult. For example, recently Meng
[
16
]
considered a version of the above problem without the coprimality condition.

If
n
is divisible by
3
, the example
X=\{x:3\nmid x\}
shows that
X
can have size
(2/3)n
.
We show (see
Proposition

5.7
) that
|X|\leq(5/6)n+O(1)
.
A solution to this problem should lead to the determination of the limits
\liminf_{n~\textup{even}}\gamma(A_{n})/n
and
\liminf_{n~\textup{odd}}\gamma(S_{n})/n
.
Again, it is reasonable to expect that the solution should be sensitive to the presence of small prime factors, for example the prime factor
3
.
Consequently it is reasonable to expect for example that

\liminf_{\gcd(n,6)=1}\gamma(S_{n})/n>\liminf_{n~\text{odd}}\gamma(S_{n})/n.

Throughout the paper, the cases (
S_{n}
,
n
even) and (
A_{n}
,
n
odd) will often be considered together, as will the complementary cases (
S_{n}
,
n
odd) and (
A_{n}
,
n
even).
The reason for this is easy to explain.
First, since we may use
A_{n}
in a covering family of
S_{n}
, covering
S_{n}
is almost the same as covering all odd permutations, while obviously covering
A_{n}
is the same as covering all even permutations.
It turns out that the most difficult permutations to cover are the ones with only
3
or
4
cycles.
Now recall that if
\pi\in S_{n}
has
k
cycles then the sign of
\pi
is
(-1)^{n-k}
.
Therefore
k=3
corresponds to the cases (
S_{n}
,
n
even) and (
A_{n}
,
n
odd)
while
k=4
corresponds to the cases (
S_{n}
,
n
odd) and (
A_{n}
,
n
even).
The case
k=4
is more difficult.

2.
Upper bounds

In this section we summarize the known upper bounds and add two new observations (
Propositions

2.3
and
2.5
).
Let us emphasize that the results with citations are not new, nor are the proofs, but it is valuable to recall them here to give context to the arguments for the lower bounds that we will give in the next sections.

Proposition 2.1

(
[
5
]
*Theorem A)
.

For
G=S_{n}
or
A_{n}
with
n\geq 4
,

\gamma(G)\leq\begin{cases}\left\lfloor{n/2}\right\rfloor&\text{if}~G=S_{n},~n~\text{odd},\\
\left\lfloor{n/3}\right\rfloor+1&\text{if}~G=A_{n},~n~\text{odd},\\
\left\lfloor{n/4}\right\rfloor+1&\text{if $n$ is even}.\end{cases}

For the sake of comparison with our lower bound arguments, we indicate the covering families and sketch the proof.

Sketch.

Case 1:
G=S_{n}
with
n
odd.

Let
p
be the smallest prime factor of
n
. If
p<n
then

\{S_{k}\times S_{n-k}:1\leq k\leq(n-1)/2,~p\nmid k\}\cup\{S_{p}\wr S_{n/p}\}

is a covering family of cardinality at most
(n-1)/2
. If
p=n
then consider instead

\{S_{k}\times S_{n-k}:1<k\leq(n-1)/2\}\cup\{\operatorname{AGL}_{1}(p)\}.

The subgroup
\operatorname{AGL}_{1}(p)
covers the cycle types
(n)
and
(n-1,1)
, and the subgroups
S_{k}\times S_{n-k}
with
1<k\leq(n-1)/2
cover all others.

Case 2:
G=A_{n}
with
n
odd.

Since
n
is odd, every element of
A_{n}
has an odd number of cycles, and is therefore either an
n
-cycle or has a cycle of length at most
n/3
. A covering family is therefore given by

\{(S_{k}\times S_{n-k})\cap A_{n}:k\leq n/3\}\cup\{M\cap A_{n}\},

where
M
is any maximal subgroup of
S_{n}
other than
A_{n}
containing an
n
-cycle.
Indeed,
|M:M\cap A_{n}|=2
, so
M\cap A_{n}
is normalized by at least one odd permutation and therefore
M\cap A_{n}
intersects both
A_{n}
-conjugacy classes of
n
-cycles.
If
n
is composite one may take
M=S_{p}\wr S_{n/p}
where
p
is the smallest prime factor of
n
, and if
n
is prime one may take
M=\operatorname{AGL}_{1}(p)
.

Case 3:
G=S_{n}
or
A_{n}
with
n
even.

Every permutation without odd cycles is contained in a conjugate of
S_{2}\wr S_{n/2}
, and so are the permutations of type
(n/2,n/2)
, and every other permutation is contained in
S_{k}\times S_{n-k}
for some odd
k<n/2
. Therefore

\{(S_{k}\times S_{n-k})\cap G:1\leq k<n/2,~k~\text{odd}\}\cup\{(S_{2}\wr S_{n/2})\cap G\}

is a covering family of size
\left\lfloor{n/4}\right\rfloor+1
.
∎

Remark 2.2
.

Equality holds above for
S_{p}
(

prime),
A_{2^{k}}
(
k\neq 3
), and
A_{2p}
(

see
[

We will show later that equality also holds for
S_{2^{k}}

(

for almost all primes

The following bound improves the bound in
Proposition

2.1
by
1
in one special case.
The covering family may seem mysterious for now.
Its origin will be clarified later when the connection to additive combinatorics is fleshed out.

Proposition 2.3
.

Suppose
p\geq 5
is prime and
p\equiv 1\pmod{3}
. Then

\gamma(A_{p})\leq(p-1)/3.

Proof.

Write
p=3m+1
, so
m=(p-1)/3
.
Let
K
be the set of integers
k\in\{1,\dots,p-1\}
such that
(m+1)k\in\{1,\dots,m-1\}\pmod{p}
.
Since
p
is prime,
|K|=m-1
.
Let
I
be the middle interval
\{m,\dots,2m+1\}
mod
p
and note

\mathbf{Z}/{p}\mathbf{Z}=\{0\}\sqcup(m+1)K\sqcup I\sqcup-(m+1)K.

We claim that

\mathcal{C}=\{(S_{k}\times S_{p-k})\cap A_{p}:k\in K\}\cup\{\operatorname{AGL}_{1}(p)\cap A_{p}\}

is a covering family of cardinality
|\mathcal{C}|=|K|+1=m=(p-1)/3
.
Let
\pi\in A_{p}
and suppose
\pi
has cycle type
(x_{1},\dots,x_{\ell})
. Note that
\ell
is odd.

Case
\ell=1
:
If
\ell=1
then
\pi
is a
p
-cycle, so
\pi
is covered by
\operatorname{AGL}_{1}(p)\cap A_{p}
.

Case
\ell=3
:
If
x_{i}\in K
or
p-x_{i}\in K
for some
i
then
\pi
is covered by
S_{x_{i}}\times S_{p-x_{i}}
, so assume
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\notin K\cup(p-K)
. For
y_{i}=(m+1)x_{i}\bmod p
for
i=1,2,3
. Then
y_{1},y_{2},y_{3}\in I
and
y_{1}+y_{2}+y_{3}=(m+1)p=0
. This implies that
y_{1},y_{2},y_{3}\in\{\pm m,\pm(m+1)\}
.
Noting that
(m+1)(p-1)/2=3(m+1)m/2\equiv m\pmod{p}
, it follows that
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\in\{\pm 1,\pm(p-1)/2\}\pmod{p}
,
so
\pi
must have type
(1,(p-1)/2,(p-1)/2)
.
Thus
\pi
is covered by
\operatorname{AGL}_{1}(p)\cap A_{p}
.

Case
\ell\geq 5
:
We are obviously done if
x_{i}=1
for all
i
, so assume without loss of generality that
x_{1}>1
. Let
x=x_{1}
,
y=x_{2}+x_{3}
,
z=x_{4}+\cdots+x_{\ell}
. Arguing as in the previous case, we are done unless
x,y,z\notin K\cup(p-K)
, and if
x,y,z\in K\cup(p-K)
then since
x+y+z=p
it follows that
(x,y,z)
is
(1,(p-1)/2,(p-1)/2)
in some order, but this is impossible because
x,y,z>1
.
∎

If
n
has small prime factors then the values of
\gamma(S_{n})
and
\gamma(A_{n})
are much smaller than the upper bound in
Proposition

2.1
indicated above.

Proposition 2.4

(
[
2
]
*Propostion 3.1)
.

Assume
G=S_{n}
and
n
is even or
G=A_{n}
and
n
is odd.
Suppose
n
is not a prime power and let
p_{1}<p_{2}
be the smallest prime factors of
n
.
Then

\gamma(G)\leq\frac{n}{2}{\left(1-\frac{1}{p_{1}}\right)}{\left(1-\frac{1}{p_{2}}\right)}+2.

Proof.

A covering family is given by
\mathcal{C}_{1}\cup\mathcal{C}_{2}
, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{1}=\{(S_{k}\times S_{n-k})\cap G:1\leq k<n/2,\ \gcd(k,p_{1}p_{2})=1\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{2}=\{(S_{p_{1}}\wr S_{n/p_{1}})\cap G,(S_{p_{2}}\wr S_{n/p_{2}})\cap G\}.

Let
\pi\in G
. For
i=1,2
, if all the cycles of
\pi
have length divisible by
p_{i}
then
\pi
is contained in a conjugate of
(S_{p_{i}}\wr S_{n/p_{i}})\cap G\in\mathcal{C}_{2}
. Otherwise
\pi
must have an invariant set of cardinality coprime with
p_{1}p_{2}
, given by either a cycle of
\pi
or a sum of two cycles, so
\pi
is contained in a conjugate of some
(S_{k}\times S_{n-k})\cap G\in\mathcal{C}_{1}
.
∎

The following new observation is partly inspired by Maróti’s construction described in
[
2
]
*Section 2.
Recall that
\phi(n)
denotes Euler’s totient function and
\omega(n)
denotes the number of prime factors of
n
.

Proposition 2.5
.

Assume
G=S_{n}
and
n
is odd or
G=A_{n}
and
n
is even.
Suppose
n
is divisible by
3
. Then

\gamma(G)\leq\begin{cases}n/6+\phi(n)/2+\omega(n)&\text{if}~G=A_{n},~n~\text{even},\\
n/6+\phi(n)/2+\omega(n)+1&\text{if}~G=S_{n},~n~\text{odd}.\\
\end{cases}

Proof.

A covering family is given by
\mathcal{C}_{1}\cup\mathcal{C}_{2}\cup\mathcal{C}_{3}
where

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{1}=\{(S_{k}\times S_{n-k})\cap G:1\leq k\leq n/2,\ 3\mid k\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{2}=\{(S_{k}\times S_{n-k})\cap G:1\leq k\leq n/2,\ \gcd(n,k)=1\}.

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{3}=\{(S_{p}\wr S_{n/p})\cap G:p\mid n\},

together with
\mathcal{C}_{4}=\{A_{n}\}
if
G=S_{n}
.
Note
|\mathcal{C}_{1}|\leq n/6
,
|\mathcal{C}_{2}|=\phi(n)/2
,
|\mathcal{C}_{3}|=\omega(n)
,
|\mathcal{C}_{4}|\leq 1
.
To see why this works, let
\pi\in G
, and if
G=S_{n}
assume
\pi\notin A_{n}
.
Then
\pi
has an even number of cycles.
First suppose
\pi
has only two cycles, say of lengths
k
and
n-k
where
k\leq n/2
. If
\gcd(k,n)>1
then
\pi
is contained in a conjugate of
(S_{p}\wr S_{n/p})\cap G\in\mathcal{C}_{3}
where
p
is any prime dividing
\gcd(k,n)
.
Otherwise
\pi
is contained in a conjugate of
(S_{k}\times S_{n-k})\cap G\in\mathcal{C}_{2}
.
Now suppose
\pi
has at least four cycles. Let
a,b,c
be the lengths of three of the cycles. Then some number
k\in\{a,b,c,a+b,a+c,b+c,a+b+c\}
is divisible by
3
, and
\pi
is contained in a conjugate of
(S_{k}\times S_{n-k})\cap G\in\mathcal{C}_{1}
.
∎

3.
Cycle types covered by transitive subgroups

To reduce to additive combinatorics we need to argue that transitive subgroups cannot help much.
For this we need to know when elements with a small number of cycles are covered by a transitive subgroup.
This is the content of the next two lemmas.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\pi\in S_{n}
have cycle type
(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
, where
k>1
.

(1)

If
\gcd(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})>1
, then
\pi
is contained in an imprimitive transitive subgroup conjugate to
S_{p}\wr S_{n/p}
where
p
is any prime divisor of
\gcd(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})
.

(2)

If
\gcd(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=1
then
\pi
is contained in an imprimitive transitive subgroup if and only if there is a partition
\Pi
of
\{1,\dots,k\}
, neither discrete nor trivial, such that if
b=\gcd(\sum_{i\in C}x_{i}:C\in\Pi)
then for each cell
C\in\Pi
and
i\in C
we have
(\sum_{j\in C}x_{j})/b\mid x_{i}
.
In this case
\pi
is contained in a conjugate of
S_{b}\wr S_{n/b}
.

In particular, if
2\leq k\leq 4
and
\gcd(x_{1},\dots,x_{k})=1
then
\pi
is contained in an imprimitive transitive subgroup if and only if either
k=3
and for some permutation of
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}
we have

(x_{1}+x_{2})/\gcd(x_{3},n)\mid x_{1},x_{2},

or
k=4
and for some permutation of
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}
we have either

(1)

(x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3})/\gcd(x_{4},n)\mid x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}
,

(2)

(x_{1}+x_{2})/\gcd(x_{3},x_{4},n)\mid x_{1},x_{2}
, or

(3)

(x_{1}+x_{2})/\gcd(x_{1}+x_{2},n)\mid x_{1},x_{2}
and
(x_{3}+x_{4})/\gcd(x_{1}+x_{2},n)\mid x_{3},x_{4}
.

Proof.

(Cf.
[
2
]
*Section 4.)
It is well-known and easy to check that
\pi
is contained in an imprimitive transitive subgroup if and only if there is a nontrivial proper divisor
b\mid n
, a partition
(d_{1},\dots,d_{m})
of
n/b
, and a partition of
\Omega
into
\pi
-invariant sets
A_{1},\dots,A_{m}
(
m\leq k
) such that
|A_{i}|=d_{i}b
and every cycle of
\pi\mid A_{i}
is divisible by
d_{i}
. (See, e.g.,
[
3
]
*Section 4 or
[

3.2
to make this precise.

By
Proposition

5.4
, it would be enough to remove a small symmetric set
T
from
X
so that
X\smallsetminus T
is free of triples of the form
x,x+h,2x+h
where
h\in\{1,2,4\}
and
\gcd(x,h,n)=1
.
Suppose
x,x+h,2x+h\in X
and
\gcd(x,h,n)=1
.
By including the intervals
[-2,2]
and
[n/2-2,n/2+2]
in
T
and using symmetry of
X
and
T
we may assume
2<x,x+h<n/2-2
, so
2x+h<n
.
Now
Lemmas

3.1
and
3.2
apply to the triple
(x,x+h,n-2x-h)
, which must be covered by some transitive subgroup in
\mathcal{C}
. Note that
\gcd(x,x+h,n-2x-h)=\gcd(x,h,n)=1
.
Since
1\in T
, the cases
Lemma

3.2
(3, 4) are already eliminated.
We may eliminate
Lemma

3.2
(5) by including in
T
all
O(\log n)^{2}
points of the form
\pm(q^{d_{1}}-1)/(q-1)
with
n=(q^{d}-1)/(q-1)
.
Now consider
Lemma

3.1
(2). There are three conditions to consider:

(1)

(2x+h)/\gcd(2x+h,n)\mid x,x+h
,

(2)

(n-x-h)/\gcd(n-x-h,n)\mid x,n-2x-h
,

(3)

(n-x)/\gcd(n-x,n)\mid x+h,n-2x-h
.

We consider each case in turn for fixed
h\in\{1,2,4\}
.

(1) We have
(2x+h)/\gcd(2x+h,n)\mid h
, so
2x+h=de
where
d\mid n
and
e\mid h
.
The number of solutions is therefore bounded by
2\tau(n)\tau(h)
.

(2) In this case
(n-x-h)/\gcd(n-x-h,n)\mid n-h
, so
n-x-h=de
where
d\mid n
and
e\mid n-h
. The number of solutions is therefore bounded by
\tau(n)\tau(n-h)
.

(3) Similarly,
(n-x)/\gcd(n-x,n)\mid n+h
, so
n-x=de
for some
d\mid n
and
e\mid n+h
.
The number of solutions is therefore bounded by
\tau(n)\tau(n+h)
.

Applying the divisor bound (
5.1
) and summing over
h=1,2,4
, it follows that the total number of triples is only
n^{o(1)}
.
Pick a point
x
from each such triple and include
\pm x
in
T
.
Then we have obtained the required set
T
, and
|T|\leq n^{o(1)}
, so
Proposition

5.4
implies
|X\smallsetminus T|\leq(2/3)n
,
so
|X|\leq(2/3)n+n^{o(1)}
.
∎

Finally we give a lower bound in the case
G=S_{n}
with
n
odd or
G=A_{n}
with
n
even that is approximately within a factor of
2
of the main term in the upper bound
Proposition

2.5
.

Again we start by isolating the essential additive-combinatorial problem.
For elements
x,y,z
in an abelian group define

\operatorname{cube}(x,y,z)=\{x,y,z,x+y,x+z,y+z,x+y+z\}.

Call
X\subset\{1,\dots,n-1\}

coprime-cube-free
if there does not exist
x,y,z
with
\gcd(x,y,z,n)=1
and
\operatorname{cube}(x,y,z)\subset X
.
The following proposition gives a bound for the size of any symmetric coprime-cube-free subset of
\{1,\dots,n-1\}
.

Proposition 5.7
.

If
X\subset\{1,\dots,n-1\}
is symmetric and coprime-cube-free then

|X|\leq\tfrac{5}{6}n+O(1).

In fact the same bound holds provided only that
X
is free of degenerate cubes of the form

\operatorname{cube}(x,x,x+h)=\{x,x+h,2x,2x+h,3x+h\}\qquad(h=\pm 1).

Proof.

Let
f
denote the indicator function of
X
. Then for all
x
and
h=\pm 1
we have identically

(5.3)

f(x)+f(x+h)+f(2x)+f(2x+h)+f(3x+h)\leq 4.

Consider the sum over all
x\in[0,n/3]
and
h=\pm 1
.
With
O(1)
error (the edge effect of values of
x
near the ends of the interval),
the linear forms
x,x+h
run each twice over the interval
[0,n/3]
,
the linear form
2x
runs twice over the even integers in the interval
[0,2n/3]
,
the linear from
2x+h
runs twice over the odd integers in the interval
[0,2n/3]
, and lastly
the linear form
3x+h
runs once over the ingers in the interval
[0,n]
not divisible by
3
.
Therefore we obtain

4\sum_{[0,n/3]}f+2\sum_{[0,2n/3]}f+\sum_{[0,n]\smallsetminus 3\mathbf{Z}}f\leq 8n/3+O(1),

where the error term takes care of the edge effects. By symmetry of
f
we have

4\sum_{[0,n/3]}f=2\sum_{[0,n/3]\cup[2n/3,n]}f=2\sum_{[0,n]}f-2\sum_{[n/3,2n/3]}f+O(1)

and

2\sum_{[0,2n/3]}=\sum_{[0,n]}f+\sum_{[n/3,2n/3]}f+O(1),

so it follows that

4\sum_{[0,n]}f-\sum_{[n/3,2n/3]}f-\sum_{[0,n]\cap 3\mathbf{Z}}f\leq 8n/3+O(1).

Since the sums of
f
over
[n/3,2n/3]
and
[0,n]\cap 3\mathbf{Z}
are at most
n/3+1
, it follows that

4|X|=4\sum_{[0,n]}f\leq 10n/3+O(1),

so
|X|\leq 5n/6+O(1)
, as claimed.
∎

Remark 5.8
.

The constant
5/6
is almost certainly not sharp.
We believe that
2/3
is the optimal constant, which is attained by the example

X=\{x\in\{1,\dots,n-1\}:3\nmid x\}

whenever
n
is divisible by
3
(cf. the definition of
\mathcal{C}_{1}
in
Proposition

2.5
),
and a stronger bound probably holds if
3\nmid n
.

There is a conjecture attributed to the first author and Pohoata that the largest (ordinary, not necessarily symmetric) cube-free subset of
\{1,\dots,n-1\}
has density at most
2/3+o(1)
, which is attained by both the above example and
[n/3,n]
(see Meng
[
16
]
).
We can prove this in the ordinary symmetric case.

The following proposition is not as natural, but it turns out to be needed in what follows.

Proposition 5.9
.

Assume
n
is even. If
X\subset\{1,\dots,n-1\}
is symmetric and free of cubes of the form
\operatorname{cube}(x,x,x+h)
with
h=\pm 1
and
x
even then

|X|\leq\tfrac{8}{9}n+O(1).

Proof.

The proof is similar. Summing (
5.3
) over all even
x\in[0,n/3]
and
h=\pm 1
gives

2\sum_{[0,n/3]}f+2\sum_{[0,2n/3]\cap 4\mathbf{Z}}f+\sum_{[0,2n/3]\cap(2\mathbf{Z}+1)}f+\sum_{[0,n]\cap(6\mathbf{Z}\pm 1)}f\leq 4n/3+O(1).

Adding the three trivial inequalities

\displaystyle 2\sum_{[0,2n/3]\cap(4\mathbf{Z}+2)}f\leq n/3,

\displaystyle\sum_{[0,2n/3]\cap(2\mathbf{Z}+1)}f\leq n/3,

\displaystyle\sum_{[0,n]\smallsetminus(6\mathbf{Z}\pm 1)}f\leq 2n/3,

we obtain

2\sum_{[0,n/3]}f+2\sum_{[0,2n/3]}f+\sum_{[0,n]}f\leq 8n/3+O(1).

By symmetry of
f
, the left-hand side is equal to
3\sum_{[0,n]}f=3|X|
.
The claimed bound follows.
∎

Proposition 5.10
.

Let
G=S_{n}
with
n
odd or
G=A_{n}
with
n
even. Then

\gamma(G)\geq\begin{cases}n/12-n^{o(1)}&\text{if}~G=S_{n},~n~\text{odd},\\
n/18-n^{o(1)}&\text{if}~G=A_{n},~n~\text{even}.\end{cases}

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a normal covering consisting of maximal subgroups of
G
and as usual let
K\subset\{1,\dots,n-1\}
be the set of integers
k
such that
\mathcal{C}
contains a conjugate of
(S_{k}\times S_{n-k})\cap G
. Let
X=\{1,\dots,n-1\}\smallsetminus K
.
We claim there is a small set
T\subset\{1,\dots,n-1\}
so that
X\smallsetminus(T\cup(n-T))
is free of cubes of the form
\operatorname{cube}(x,x,x+h)
with
h=\pm 1
and
\gcd(n,x+1,2)=1
.

Suppose
\operatorname{cube}(x,x,x+h)\subset X
. Then the partition
(x,x,x+h,n-3x-h)
is a coprime
4
-partition of
n
not covered by
\{(S_{k}\times S_{n-k})\cap G:k\in K\}
.
Since permutations with
4
cycles are contained in
A_{n}
when
n
is even and
S_{n}\smallsetminus A_{n}
when
n
is odd,
(x,x,x+h,n-3x-h)
must be covered by a transitive subgroup in
\mathcal{C}
smaller than
A_{n}
.

First consider imprimitive subgroups. Applying
Lemma

3.1
, one of the following cases holds for some permutation
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}
of the partition
x,x,x+h,n-3x-h
.

(1)

(
\Pi
has type
(3,1)
) We have
b=\gcd(x_{4},n)>1
and

(x_{1}+x_{2}+x_{3})/b\mid x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}.

(1)

(
x_{4}=n-3x-h
)
(3x-h)/\gcd(3x-h,n)\mid x,x+h
. Since
\gcd(x,x+h)=1
, in this case we must have
3x-h\mid n
. The number of possibilities is at most
\tau(n)
.

(2)

(
x_{4}=x+h
)
(n-x-h)/\gcd(x+h,n)\mid x,n-3x-h
. In this case
n-x-h=bc
for some
b\mid n
and some
c\mid n-h
. The number of possibilities is at most
\tau(n)\tau(n-h)
.

(3)

(
x_{4}=x
)
(n-x)/\gcd(x,n)\mid x+h,n-3x-h
. In this case
n-x=bc
for some
b\mid n
and some
c\mid n+2h
. The number of possibilities is at most
\tau(n)\tau(n+2h)
.

(2)

(
\Pi
has type
(2,1,1)
) We have
b=\gcd(x_{3},x_{4},n)>1
and

(x_{1}+x_{2})/b\mid x_{1},x_{2}.

Note that the hypothesis
\gcd(n,x+1,2)=1
eliminates the important case
x_{1}=x_{2}=x
. In fact
\gcd(x_{3},x_{4},n)>1
is only possible if
x_{3}=x_{4}=x
. In this case we must have
(n-2x)/\gcd(x,n)\mid x+h,n-3x-h
, which implies that
n-2x=bc
for some
b\mid n
and some
c\mid n+2h
, so the number of possibilities is at most
\tau(n)\tau(n+2h)
.

(3)

(
\Pi
has type
(2,2)
) We have
b=\gcd(x_{1}+x_{2},n)=\gcd(x_{3}+x_{4},n)>1
and

(x_{1}+x_{2})/b\mid x_{1},x_{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad(x_{3}+x_{4})/b\mid x_{3},x_{4}.

There are essentially just two cases, depending on whether the two copies of
x
appear in the same part.

(1)

(
x_{1}=x_{2}=x
)
2x/\gcd(2x,n)\mid x
and
(n-2x)/\gcd(2x,n)\mid x+h,n-3x-h
. In this case (
n
is even and)
n-2x=bc
for some
b\mid n
and some
c\mid n+2h
. The number of possibilities is at most
\tau(n)\tau(n+2h)
.

(2)

(
x_{1}=x,x_{2}=x+h
)
(2x+h)/\gcd(2x+h,n)\mid x,x+h
and
(n-2x-h)/\gcd(2x+h,n)\mid x,n-3x-h
. In this case
2x+h\mid n
, so the number of possibilities is at most
\tau(n)
.

All together the number of cases is at most

\sum_{h=\pm 1}(2\tau(n)+\tau(n)\tau(n-h)+3\tau(n)\tau(n+2h))\leq n^{o(1)}

by the divisor bound (
5.1
).
For each such cube
\operatorname{cube}(x,x,x+h)
, include
x
in
T
.

Next consider primitive subgroups.
By including
1
in
T
we eliminate the cases
Lemma

3.2
(6–8),
but the other two cases require further argument.

Consider
Lemma

3.2
(9). If
(x,x,x+h,n-3x-h)
has the form
\tau=(k_{1}k_{2},k_{1}(m-k_{2}),(m-k_{1})k_{2},(m-k_{1})(m-k_{2}))
we may assume (possibly swapping
k_{1}
and
k_{2}
, or
k_{1}
and
m-k_{1}
, or
k_{2}
and
m-k_{2}
)
that
k_{1}k_{2}=x
.
If
k_{1}(m-k_{2})=x
then we must have
k_{2}=m/2
, in contradiction with
\gcd(k_{2},m-k_{2})=1
, and similarly if
(m-k_{1})k_{2}=x
, so we must have
(m-k_{1})(m-k_{2})=x
and say
k_{1}(m-k_{2})=x+h
.
Since
\gcd(x,x+h)=1
this implies that
k_{1}=1
and
k_{2}=x=m-x-h
, but then
(m-k_{1})(m-k_{2})=(m-1)(x+h)>x
.
Therefore this case does not arise.
(Alternatively, by the solution to Erdős’s multiplication table problem, the set of all products
k_{1}k_{2}
with
k_{1},k_{2}\leq m
has cardinality
O(m^{2}/(\log m)^{c})=O(n/(\log n)^{c})
for some
c>0
, so we may just include all such elements in
T
at the cost of a somewhat poorer error bound.)

Finally consider
Lemma

3.2
(10).
Suppose the quadruple
(x,x,x+h,n-3x-h)
is equal to

\tau={\left(\frac{q^{d_{1}}-1}{q-1},\frac{q^{d_{2}}-1}{q-1},\frac{(q^{d_{1}}-1)(q^{d_{2}}-1)}{2(q-1)},\frac{(q^{d_{1}}-1)(q^{d_{2}}-1)}{2(q-1)}\right)}

up to permutation of the entries.
As in the proof of
Proposition

5.6
, we can eliminate this case by including in
T
all
O(\log n)^{2}
elements of the form
(q^{d_{1}}-1)/(q-1)
with
d_{1}\leq d
and
n=(q^{d}-1)/(q-1)
.
(Alternatively, one may check directly that this case does not arise for
n\geq 6
.)

Thus we have a set
T\subset\{1,\dots,n-1\}
of size
|T|\leq n^{o(1)}
such that
Y=X\smallsetminus(T\cup(n-T))
is free of cubes of the
\operatorname{cube}(x,x,x+h)
with
h=\pm 1
and
\gcd(n,x+1,2)=1
.
By
Propositions

5.7
and
5.9
, we have

|Y|\leq\begin{cases}(5/6)n+O(1)&\text{if $n$ is odd},\\
(8/9)n+O(1)&\text{if $n$ is even},\end{cases}

and obviously
|X|\leq|Y|+2|T|\leq|Y|+n^{o(1)}
.
Thus
|K|=n-1-|X|\geq n-|Y|-n^{o(1)}
, and
\mathcal{C}
contains at least
|K|/2
different intransitive subgroups.
This completes the proof.
∎

6.
Limits

Theorem 6.1

(
Theorem

1.1
restated)
.

We have the following asymptotic estimates for
\gamma(S_{n})
and
\gamma(A_{n})
:

\displaystyle\limsup_{n~\textup{even}}\gamma(S_{n})/n=1/4,

\displaystyle\limsup_{n~\textup{even}}\gamma(A_{n})/n=1/4,

\displaystyle\limsup_{n~\textup{odd}}\gamma(S_{n})/n=1/2,

\displaystyle\limsup_{n~\textup{odd}}\gamma(A_{n})/n=1/3,

\displaystyle\liminf_{n~\textup{even}}\gamma(S_{n})/n=1/6,

\displaystyle\liminf_{n~\textup{even}}\gamma(A_{n})/n\in[1/18,1/6],

\displaystyle\liminf_{n~\textup{odd}}\gamma(S_{n})/n\in[1/12,1/6],

\displaystyle\liminf_{n~\textup{odd}}\gamma(A_{n})/n\in[1/6,4/15].

Proof.

The eight cases naturally split into 4 groups of 2 according to the formula
\operatorname{sgn}(\pi)=(-1)^{n-k}
, where
k
is the number of cycles of
\pi
.
Recall that to bound a limsup above we need to consider the whole sequence, while to bound a limsup below we can pass to any subsequence we like, and exactly the reverse for liminfs.

Cases
\limsup_{n~\textup{even}}\gamma(A_{n})/n
and
\limsup_{n~\textup{odd}}\gamma(S_{n})/n
:

See
Propositions

2.1
and
2.2
. These results are already in
[
5
]
.
The limsups are attained along the sequences
2p
and
p
respectively.

Cases
\limsup_{n~\textup{even}}\gamma(S_{n})/n
and
\limsup_{n~\textup{odd}}\gamma(A_{n})/n
:

See
Proposition

2.1
for the upper bounds and
Propositions

4.1
and
4.3
for the lower bounds.
The limsups are attained again along the sequences
2p
and
p
respectively.
According to
Proposition

5.3
, in the latter case the limit is also attained along any sequence in which the smallest prime factor of
n
tends to infinity.

Cases
\liminf_{n~\textup{even}}\gamma(S_{n})/n
and
\liminf_{n~\textup{odd}}\gamma(A_{n})/n
:

See
Proposition

2.4
for the upper bounds and
Proposition

5.6
for the lower bounds.
In the former case, the liminf is attained along the sequence
n=6m
.

Cases
\liminf_{n~\textup{even}}\gamma(A_{n})/n
and
\liminf_{n~\textup{odd}}\gamma(S_{n})/n
:

The lower bounds are immediate from
Proposition

5.10
.
To establish the upper bounds we may restrict to a subsequence in
\phi(n)/n\to 0
.
For example if
p_{1},\dots,p_{k}
are the first
k
odd primes then we may take
n=2p_{1}\cdots p_{k}
or
n=p_{1}\cdots p_{k}
.
For these sequences,
Proposition

2.5
implies
\liminf\gamma(G)/n\leq 1/6
.
∎
