Delayed sciatic nerve palsy due to a subepineural hematoma following a gluteal gunshot injury: A case report
Authors: Fouad Assaf, Antoine Saber, Rita Saad, Georges Sakhat, Elie Makhoul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGunshot wounds (GSWs) to the lower extremities are common in civilian trauma and may lead to a spectrum of neurovascular complications. While acute sciatic nerve injury is frequently reported, delayed neuropathies—particularly those caused by intraneural or subepineural hematomas—are rare and often underrecognized. We present the case of a 25-year-old male who sustained a gluteal gunshot injury with no initial neurological deficit. The bullet was surgically removed, and the patient was discharged on neuropathic pain medication. Ten days later, he developed progressive paresthesia and new-onset motor weakness involving ankle and toe extension. Magnetic resonance imaging performed 20 days after injury revealed a 12 × 6 mm hematoma compressing the sciatic nerve along the ballistic tract. Surgical exploration confirmed a subepineural hematoma, which was evacuated through epineurotomy. The patient experienced gradual neurological improvement and achieved full motor and sensory recovery at two-year follow-up. Subepineural hematoma causing delayed sciatic nerve palsy represents an uncommon sequela of penetrating trauma. Early recognition of delayed neurological deterioration, prompt imaging, and timely surgical decompression are critical to prevent irreversible nerve damage. Increased awareness of this rare entity may facilitate earlier diagnosis and improve patient outcomes.Keywords: Intraneural hematoma, Sciatic nerve, Gunshot wound, Delayed neuropathy, Peripheral nerve compression, Subepineural hematoma, Surgical decompression

Introduction

Gunshot wounds (GSWs) to the lower extremities are a frequent cause of civilian trauma and are associated with complex soft-tissue, vascular, and neurological injury. In the United States, firearm-related trauma accounts for more than 45,000 deaths annually, with a significantly larger number of nonfatal injuries [1]. Ballistic trauma may result in direct neural damage, traction injury, or indirect compression secondary to hematoma formation [3].

Sciatic nerve injury is one of the most concerning complications of lower extremity GSWs and occurs in approximately 14% of cases [2]. Although most neurological deficits present immediately following injury, delayed neuropathies can occur days to weeks later and are often overlooked. One potential mechanism is hematoma formation along the ballistic tract, which can subsequently expand or organize and exert pressure on adjacent nerves [2], [3], [4].

Intraneural hematomas—particularly subepineural hematomas—are rare, with fewer than 20 cases reported across all peripheral nerves and only two involving the sciatic nerve. Their nonspecific presentation and rarity contribute to diagnostic delay [5]. Early recognition is essential, as prolonged compression can lead to irreversible nerve damage [6].

We report a rare case of delayed sciatic nerve palsy caused by a subepineural hematoma following a gluteal GSW, emphasizing the importance of clinical vigilance and timely intervention.

Case presentation

A 25-year-old male presented to the emergency department after sustaining a gunshot wound to the left gluteal region (Fig. 1). He was hemodynamically stable with minimal blood loss. Initial examination revealed no motor or sensory deficits in the left lower limb. Radiographs of the pelvis and femur showed no fractures (Fig. 2, Fig. 3).

The bullet was surgically removed, and the wound was closed. Postoperatively, the patient reported mild paresthesia in the left lower limb but retained full motor strength. He was started on pregabalin, NSAIDs, and acetaminophen.

Three days after surgery, he developed worsening paresthesia along the lateral aspect of the left leg. Under the suspicion of inflammatory neuritis, prednisone was initiated. Ten days after injury, the patient experienced burning pain in the lateral foot and new-onset motor weakness in ankle and toe extension (Medical Research Council grade 3/5). The wound showed no signs of infection or local complications.

Twenty days post-injury, magnetic resonance imaging of the pelvis and thigh showed a hyperintense trajectory compatible with the bullet path and a 12 × 6 mm hematoma compressing the sciatic nerve, with associated edema and hemorrhagic infiltration (Fig. 4, Fig. 5, Fig. 6).

The patient was readmitted for surgical exploration. Intraoperatively, a subepineural hematoma was identified, producing focal compression of the sciatic nerve. An epineurotomy was performed, and the hematoma was evacuated (Fig. 7, Fig. 8). Biopsy confirmed the diagnosis. Postoperative recovery was uneventful, with progressive motor improvement over several weeks. At two-year follow-up, the patient demonstrated full motor recovery and complete resolution of sensory deficits.

Discussion

Intraneural hematomas represent a rare subset of peripheral nerve pathology in which hemorrhage occurs within the confines of the nerve sheath [7]. Depending on the anatomical plane of bleeding—subparaneurial, subepineural, intrafascicular, or multicompartmental—these hematomas exert variable degrees of mechanical compression, ischemia, and fascicular distortion. Subepineural hematomas, such as in this case, are thought to arise predominantly from traumatic mechanisms, including penetrating injuries, rapid traction, or direct neural contusion [6], [8], [9], [10], [11].

Despite the sciatic nerve being one of the most frequently injured nerves in lower extremity ballistic trauma, the majority of injuries result from direct laceration or neuropraxia. Intraneural hematoma formation—particularly delayed—is exceedingly uncommon, with fewer than 20 cases described across all peripheral nerves and only two involving the sciatic nerve. Both previously documented sciatic intraneural hematomas occurred in patients on anticoagulants, suggesting a predisposition to spontaneous or minimally traumatic hemorrhage [12], [13]. Our case is unique as it represents a traumatic subepineural hematoma secondary to a gunshot injury, with delayed neurological deterioration despite initially normal findings.

The delayed onset of symptoms in this patient highlights the dynamic nature of hematoma evolution. Early bleeding may be clinically silent, but progressive organization and clot retraction can increase intraneural pressure over days to weeks. Moreover, the sciatic nerve's deep location in the gluteal region may obscure early signs of swelling or external compartmental pressure [12], [13].

MRI remains the diagnostic modality of choice for intraneural hematomas. Typical findings include fusiform nerve enlargement, heterogeneous T1/T2 signal intensities, and surrounding edema [12], [13]. Gradient-echo or susceptibility-weighted sequences are particularly sensitive for identifying hemosiderin deposition [12], [13]. In this case, MRI performed 20 days post-injury demonstrated clear extrinsic compression on the sciatic nerve, enabling timely surgical planning.

Management strategies for intraneural hematomas vary in the literature, with some authors advocating conservative treatment in stable or minimally symptomatic patients. However, prolonged compression has well-established deleterious effects on nerve physiology, including conduction block, demyelination, and eventual Wallerian degeneration [7], [14]. For this reason, most authors support early decompression in cases of progressive neuropathy, particularly when motor deficits emerge. Epineurotomy for subepineural hematomas allows direct evacuation while minimizing damage to fascicles [7].

The patient in this case underwent surgical decompression approximately one month after the onset of symptoms yet still demonstrated full neurological recovery at two-year follow-up. This favorable outcome underscores the potential for meaningful recovery even when intervention is not immediate. Nevertheless, earlier detection could plausibly reduce the degree of axonal injury and accelerate functional restoration [11].

Overall, this case reinforces the need for heightened vigilance in patients presenting with delayed neurological changes following ballistic trauma. Clinicians should maintain a low threshold for advanced imaging when new symptoms emerge, and timely surgical intervention should be considered to prevent irreversible neural damage.

Conclusion

Subepineural hematoma of the sciatic nerve is a rare and often underrecognized cause of delayed neuropathy following ballistic trauma. This case emphasizes the need for careful monitoring of patients with GSWs, as neurological deficits may manifest days to weeks after the initial injury. Early imaging and timely surgical decompression can prevent irreversible nerve damage and lead to excellent functional outcomes. Further reporting of similar cases is essential to establish clearer diagnostic and management guidelines.
