Categorizing immunotherapy resistance mechanisms in non-small cell lung cancer: how can we perform better?
Authors: Daphne van der Geest, Alex van Vliet, Daniel S Peeper, John B A G Haanen, Egbert F Smit, Willemijn S M E Theelen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe introduction of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) has revolutionized the treatment of many solid tumors including advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) without targetable driver alterations. Unfortunately, the majority of patients with NSCLC do not respond to ICI treatment or will eventually experience disease progression. A wide variety of resistance mechanisms is hypothesized as responsible for tumor immune escape. Also, at what stage they interfere in the anticancer immune response, and the possible complex interactions or overlap between these resistance mechanisms complicate the matter further. Although progress has been made, our current understanding of the resistance mechanisms underlying the failure of ICI treatment is still limited. This is reflected in the lack of positive phase III clinical trials for second-line treatment after failure of ICI in advanced NSCLC. This might be the result of inadequate patient selection. Here, we summarize the current available data on resistance mechanisms in NSCLC and propose a classification system to categorize patients based on resistance mechanisms present. By doing so, we could guide preclinical research and improve selection of patients for clinical trials, thereby ultimately improving outcomes for patients with advanced NSCLC who have progressed on ICI.Keywords: Immunotherapy, Lung Cancer, Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor

Introduction

Over a decade ago, the revolution of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) in solid cancers kicked off with the Food and Drug Administration approval of the cytotoxic T-lymphocyte associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) inhibitor ipilimumab for the treatment of unresectable melanoma.1 In advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), registration of ICI targeting programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1)/programmed death 1 (PD-1) as a second-line treatment followed 5 years later.2 3 In subsequent years, ICI treatment has been integrated into first line and is now standard of care (SoC) for metastatic NSCLC without targetable driver alterations either as monotherapy4 5 or combined with platinum-doublet chemotherapy,6 7 depending on tumor PD-L1 expression level. PD-1 inhibition monotherapy improved median overall survival (mOS) from 11.6 to 26.3 months in patients with high PD-L1-expressing tumors (≥50%),8 while the addition of ICI to chemotherapy across all PD-L1 expression levels improved mOS from 10.6 months to 22.0 months compared with chemotherapy alone.9 Although ICIs provide a clear benefit for many patients with NSCLC, the majority still either does not respond, that is, exhibit primary resistance, or experiences disease recurrence, that is, acquired resistance.

Considering that most patients will eventually progress to currently approved ICI regimens, there is a need for new treatment strategies to help tackle this issue. Unfortunately, new immunotherapy agents or combinations tested in advanced NSCLC have not been able to outperform the current SoC second-line docetaxel, which has a dissatisfying objective response rate of~10%.10,13 These negative phase III trials in the ICI-resistant population may very well be explained by insufficient understanding of specific underlying ICI resistance mechanisms, with inadequate patient selection and poor response rates as a consequence. NSCLC is a heterogeneous disease,14 and the cancer-immune system interaction is extremely complex. Resistance to ICI is heterogeneous, with a wide variety of mechanisms that can be exploited by the tumor. Furthermore, a tumor may employ more than one resistance mechanism, adding to the complexity. Improving our understanding of the landscape of NSCLC resistance mechanisms to ICI is urgently needed in order to improve outcomes for patients with advanced NSCLC.

Here, we summarize the current available data on ICI resistance mechanisms in NSCLC and provide an overview towards a clinically useful categorization of ICI resistance mechanisms (figure 1) including potential biomarkers (table 1). As a guidance, we based these categories on the three main immune phenotypes in solid tumors as presented by Chen and Mellman: desert, excluded, and infiltrated.15 The immune-desert phenotype refers to a state with poor T-cell infiltration of the tumor, surrounding margins or stroma. The immune-excluded phenotype describes a tumor where immune cells can be found only in the tumor margin, unable to penetrate the tumor. The immune-infiltrated phenotype describes a subtype with abundant CD8+T cell infiltration. The latter is associated with high PD-L1 expression and better responses to ICI.16 17 Each of these phenotypes is associated with specific biological mechanisms relevant in the anticancer immune response and in ICI resistance (figure 1). The goal of this review is to categorize resistance mechanisms in a clinically useful way to guide preclinical research and to refine patient selection for clinical trials in the ICI-resistant setting, ultimately leading to better care for patients with NSCLC.

Resistance mechanisms, diagnostics and therapeutic strategies

Immune-desert phenotype

Foreignness: neoantigens and HLA

Under healthy conditions, the immune system is self-tolerant and only cells expressing antigens that are recognized by the immune system as foreign will be attacked. Tumor foreignness refers to the extent to which the tumor is regarded as non-self by the immune system. In cancer, somatic mutations can accumulate as a consequence of different mechanisms, for example, defective DNA repair or enzymatic modification of DNA. In NSCLC, a large proportion of these mutations is generated by tobacco exposure, placing smoking-related NSCLC among the highest mutated cancer types.18

Non-synonymous somatic mutations may produce mutated peptides known as neoantigens, which can be presented by human leukocyte antigen (HLA) molecules on tumor cells. This neoantigen-HLA complex can be recognized by the T-cell receptor (TCR) on CD8+ and CD4+ T cells, resulting in T-cell activation.19

Lack of neoantigens in a tumor with a low tumor mutational burden (TMB) will result in the absence of these neoantigens presented on the tumor surface. This can result in resistance to immune-mediated killing through the inability of the immune system to recognize them as non-self. TMB is commonly used as a surrogate marker for tumor foreignness. It is defined as the number of somatic mutations per megabase of DNA and, consequently, is correlated with the number of potential neoantigens a tumor can present. TMB is widely studied in NSCLC and overall seems to be an adequate predictor of response to ICI.20 However, when applying TMB as a marker for foreignness, one incorrectly assumes that all mutations that contribute to a high TMB result in neoantigens presented by HLA and contribute to a sustained immune response.21 22 Each of an individual’s six HLA class I alleles-encoded proteins can present a specific range of epitopes and therefore, some neoantigens cannot be presented if they fall outside this range. Correcting TMB for the number of HLA-compatible neoantigens has shown an improved association with ICI response.23

In line with this essential role of HLA, alterations in HLA expression at various steps in the antigen presentation machinery pathway can result in immune escape. Tumor-intrinsic stimulator of interferon genes (STING) signaling is important for HLA class I expression and defects in STING signaling have been shown to result in immune evasion and resistance to immune-mediated killing in melanoma.24 This is likely to be relevant in NSCLC as well, where Kirstin rat sarcoma virus (KRAS) is often mutated, accompanied by liver kinase B1 (LKB1) loss, which leads to STING silencing.25 Immune resistance through HLA expression alteration can also result from loss of beta-2 microglobulin (B2M), found in NSCLC with acquired resistance to ICI.26 B2M is an essential component in HLA class I complex stability and, therefore, (acquired) homozygous mutations in B2M result in loss of HLA class I expression on the cell surface.27 Aside from the loss of B2M, change in antigen presentation capability can also result from loss of heterozygosity (LOH) of any or all HLA alleles: LOH will result in a decreased variety of the HLA repertoire and thus can result in a decreased selection of neoantigens that can be presented. This is an important mechanism in tumor evolution and progression and it might also be an important immune escape mechanism28 and a potential biomarker of response to ICI.29

When immune resistance is the result of loss of HLA expression, natural killer (NK) cells could step in and control tumor growth through their ability to eliminate HLA-negative cells. This function of NK cells can be exploited with therapeutic strategies including NK cell engagers or CAR NK cells.30 Directly inducing HLA expression was shown after radiotherapy, a STING pathway activator, and could be used therapeutically in this context.31 Furthermore, recent work has shown a positive role of γδ T cells in the response to ICI in B2M-deficient (HLA class I-negative) cancers and this could also lead to new therapeutic strategies in resistant tumors in the future.32

Priming and activation: dendritic cells

Priming and activation of T cells take place in tumor-draining lymph nodes (TDLN) where dendritic cells (DCs) interact with CD8+T cells. Before DCs travel to TDLN and interact with T cells, tumor-intrinsic factors in the tumor microenvironment (TME) already influence DC functioning. Decreased DC efficacy, for example, by tumor-intrinsic activity of the WNT/β-catenin pathway, reduces T-cell infiltration through suppressed DCs in various cancer types including NSCLC.33 34 Radical radiotherapy or surgical resection of TDLN can have a detrimental effect on ICI efficacy, as this inevitably leads to decreased DC function and T-cell activation.35 36 This theory is supported by the overwhelmingly positive results in clinical trials studying neoadjuvant treatment in resectable NSCLC, which also underlines this theory,37 although a direct comparison of results between neoadjuvant and adjuvant regimens is currently not available.

Other factors in the TDLN that can compromise the priming and activation process are the presence of regulatory T cells (Tregs) by inhibiting DC maturation38 as high interferon gamma (IFN-γ) levels within TDLNs drive Treg expansion. Establishing the level of T-cell priming can be challenging, although DCs in blood have shown potential as a predictive biomarker in patients with NSCLC treated with ICI.39 40 WNT/β-catenin pathway activity might be another option to check for resistance mechanisms related to priming by DCs, but its value as a biomarker is still unclear. Therapeutic strategies to overcome resistance in this category may be found in DC-vaccination, which has been studied in preclinical NSCLC models41 and is currently being investigated in combination with PD-1 inhibition in advanced NSCLC (NCT03546361).

Trafficking: chemokines

Once CD8+T cells specific for tumor antigens are correctly primed, they need to reach the tumor site. This T-cell trafficking is performed by a variety of chemokines, for example, C-C motif ligand 5 (CCL5), C-X-C motif ligand 9 (CXCL9), and CXCL10. Indeed, CXCL9 expression is positively correlated with ICI response in different cancers.42 IFN-γ enhances the expression of CXCL9 and CXCL10,43 and hence, reduced IFN-γ signaling can decrease the amount of chemokines in the circulation, correlating with reduced ICI sensitivity of NSCLC tumor cells.44 STING pathway signaling positively influences the expression of CCL5 and CXCL1045 and silencing of STING signaling can cause resistance, for example, in KRAS-mutant NSCLC with LKB1 loss.25 46 Whether LBK1 loss is directly responsible for resistance to ICI is debatable, since tumors with a high TMB show response to ICI regardless of the presence of LKB1 alteration.47 This might imply that the effect of different resistance mechanisms is subject to a form of hierarchy where LKB1 loss only contributes to resistance when there are also insufficient neoantigens present.

Overall, we can conclude that an immune desert tumor can be the result of a variety of resistance mechanisms: lack of neoantigens, defects in antigen presentation or dysfunction of DCs leave the tumor unresponsive to ICI. When regarded as a sequence of events, this resistance occurs early in the anticancer immune response,48 subsequently leading to a lack of trafficking and infiltration of the tumor, resulting in an immune desert tumor.

Tackling the underlying resistance mechanism, especially hampered IFN-γ signaling, might be challenging due to the many different roles IFN-γ plays in the anticancer immune response. Substitution of the previously mentioned chemokines or treatment with a STING agonist seems more straightforward, but has not yet shown clinical efficacy due to both technical (eg, intratumoral application) and biological challenges (eg, short half-life, risk of overstimulation of T cells)49 and need to be studied further.

Immune-excluded phenotype

Infiltration: chemokines, VEGF and CAFs

Once cytotoxic CD8+T cells have reached the tumor, infiltration is required for tumor cell killing, as illustrated by the association of adequate T-cell infiltration with clinical benefit of anti-PD-L1 treatment.16 50 Chemokines involved in T-cell trafficking are also involved in, and often required for, T-cell infiltration, emphasizing the importance of the previously discussed resistance mechanisms in IFN-γ signaling and the STING pathway. Another example is loss of phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN), which results in an immune-excluded tumor through reduced expression of CXCL10 and increased expression of the angiogenesis-inducing vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) reducing T-cell infiltration.51 52 VEGF can contribute to immune evasion of a tumor in three ways. First, tumor-secreted VEGF often produces malfunctioning blood vessels, with impaired tissue perfusion and increased vascular permeability, leading to diminished T-cell infiltration in the tumor.53 Second, VEGF causes defective DC maturation,54 and third, VEGF blocks the differentiation of early T-cell progenitors.55 Blocking VEGF combined with PD-(L)1 inhibition has shown negative results in second-line treatment in patients with previously treated NSCLC,11 possibly due to the lack of patient selection based on the emergence of (new) resistance mechanisms like checkpoint expression or analysis of the immune infiltrate of the tumor. Fortunately, promising results are seen in studies with bispecific antibodies targeting PD-1 and VEGF,56 and serum VEGF might have potential as a biomarker for patient selection in the future.57

VEGF blocking is an example of “stromal reprogramming”: an effort to suppress tumor development not by directly targeting the tumor, but rather its surrounding bed of stromal cells. Stroma includes cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), pericytes, and mesenchymal stromal cells.58 CAFs make up the largest portion of stromal cells in many solid tumors, with reported percentages of up to 70% in NSCLC.59 Their presence is associated with an immune-excluded phenotype and resistance to PD-L1 and CTLA-4 inhibition.60 This resistance is the result of a combination of the physical barrier these CAFs form to prevent infiltration of T cells, and their release of immunosuppressive chemokines (eg, transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), VEGF) regulating the function of different immune cells.61

Several trials have attempted to counteract the negative effects of CAF-derived TGF-β, but a phase III trial in treatment-naïve advanced NSCLC failed to show increased progression-free survival (PFS) or OS when comparing patients treated with bintrafusp alfa, a bifunctional fusion protein composed of a TGF-β trap and a monoclonal antibody blocking PD-L1, with patients treated with PD-1 inhibition.62 In this trial, no efforts were made to preselect patients based on presence of CAFs or TGF-β expression. We speculate that the largely disappointing outcome of this study, and equally studies with VEGF inhibition, may in part be attributed to lack of patient selection based on presence of biological mechanisms targeted by the study drug, which could explain the discrepancies between preclinical and even phase I/II results compared with phase III clinical trial outcomes.

In summary, reduced infiltration of CD8+T cells leads to ICI resistance within the immune-excluded phenotype. Reduced IFN-γ signaling or PTEN loss decreases chemokine production and suppresses T-cell infiltration. Also, increased VEGF expression and the presence of abundant CAFs result in the inability of T cells to properly infiltrate the tumor. However, VEGF also has the ability to dampen T-cell priming through DC malfunctioning, highlighting VEGF as a good example of the overlap between the different resistance categories discussed here. This emphasizes the highly complex interplay in various steps of the anticancer immune response.

Immune-infiltrated phenotype

Loss of neoantigens

Loss of neoantigens presented by tumors can result in resistance to immune-mediated killing. It is indicative of a tumor’s adaptation to immune pressure called immune editing.63 In a small patient study, Anagnostou et al observed an increase in antigen-specific T cells in peripheral blood at the time of response, followed by their marked decline during disease progression, accompanied by loss of predicted neoantigens.64 Neoantigen loss through clonal selection provides an evolutionary advantage to the tumor and is a natural consequence of tumor-immune interaction: as immune-recognizable tumor cells are killed, the resistant population survives through the capability to evade immune detection. The concept of clonal selection as a driver of resistance is supported by the observation that tumors with low intratumor heterogeneity (ITH) of neoantigens, that is, high clonal mutation load, more often show a durable response to ICI than tumors with high ITH of neoantigens.65

Patients with tumors resistant to ICI due to loss of neoantigens might be good candidates for cell therapies or vaccination through selection of persistent neoantigens, treatment with STING-agonists or a combination of radiotherapy and ICI through induction of immunologic cell death and HLA expression.31 66

T-cell exhaustion

In an immune-infiltrated tumor, continuous exposure of T cells to tumor antigens can lead to T-cell dysfunction and exhaustion.67 T-cell exhaustion is characterized by expression of co-inhibitory markers (eg, PD-1, lymphocyte activation gene-3 (LAG-3), CD39, thymocyte selection-associated high mobility group box protein (TOX)) and diminished effector cytokine production.68 Upon continued exposure to IFN-γ, cancer cells show IFN-associated epigenetic memory resulting in enhanced expression of interferon-stimulated genes hindering immune function. This could be restored through disruption of IFN-signaling in tumor cells.69 Another way to tackle T-cell exhaustion might be found in blocking additional checkpoints expressed by exhausted T cells.

Immunosuppression and tumor growth through T-cell exhaustion can be promoted by the presence of interleukin (IL)-17-producing immune cells in the TME like CD4+T cells and T helper 17 cells. In melanoma, blocking IL-17 inhibits this exhaustion and could be a promising therapeutic target.70 IL-17 is also frequently found in NSCLC samples and may therefore be a relevant resistance mechanism in NSCLC as well, but this needs further exploration.

The presence of T-cell exhaustion before treatment or early on treatment with ICI is a sign of a pre-existing antitumor immune response and is predictive for clinical benefit in NSCLC.71 However, when T-cell exhaustion is found post-treatment, it correlates with a less favorable outcome.72 This paradox might be explained by the function of ICI in the setting of an already present anticancer immune response where T-cell exhaustion can be undone by initiation of ICI versus ongoing antigen stimulation in the setting of persistent tumor. Therapeutic strategies to overcome or even prevent T-cell exhaustion might be found in adoptive T-cell therapies with genetically engineered T cells.73

Counteraction: checkpoints and suppressive TME

An immune-infiltrated tumor is the result of an anticancer immune response, but there are different ways a tumor can counteract this response. A well-known route is through checkpoint upregulation either by tumor or immune cells.

The inhibitory checkpoint CTLA-4 is expressed by T cells and is activated through its ligands CD80 and CD86, which can be expressed by other immune cells as well as tumor cells. The expression of CD80, predominantly by macrophages, correlates with worse prognosis in NSCLC,74 but solid data on CTLA-4/CD80/CD86 expression as a biomarker of immunotherapy resistance is lacking. Nevertheless, combining CTLA-4 inhibition with PD-(L)1 inhibition is studied extensively in NSCLC but did not result in better duration of response or improved 5-year OS compared with PD-(L)1 inhibition alone.75 The benefit of combining CTLA-4 inhibition with PD-(L)1 inhibition was shown in a subgroup of the POSEIDON trial, where patients with NSCLC with serine-threonine kinase 11 (STK11) and/or Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (KEAP1) mutation experienced clinical benefit from this combination compared with PD-L1 inhibition (both combined with chemotherapy),76 emphasizing the importance of correct patient selection in these trials.

Another checkpoint, LAG-3, is frequently expressed by tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) in advanced NSCLC after TCR stimulation. Datar et al found that LAG-3 expression was associated with shorter PFS.77 Increased expression of the LAG-3 ligand fibrinogen-like protein 1 (FGL1) is linked to loss of STK11, the third most frequently mutated gene in NSCLC.78 Current available evidence shows limited clinical efficacy of ieralimumab, an antibody blocking the interaction of LAG-3 with its ligands HLA class II and FGL1, with stable disease as best response seen in patients with NSCLC in this study.79 In melanoma, LAG-3 expression has shown correlation with response to anti-LAG-3 treatment80 and might have potential as a biomarker.

The inhibitory checkpoint T-cell immunoglobulin and mucin-domain containing-3 (TIM-3) is expressed by CD8+T cells, but more often by macrophages and NK cells. Its ligands, galectin-9 and carcinoembryonic antigen-related cell adhesion molecule-1 (CEACAM1), can be expressed by tumor cells. TIM-3 expression is found in the immune infiltrate of approximately 25% of NSCLC samples,77 and TIM-3 expression is regarded as an acquired resistance mechanism to anti-PD-1 therapy, with upregulation of TIM-3 on PD-1 antibody-bound T cells at the moment of progression.81 Furthermore, soluble TIM-3 shows potential as a biomarker for response to immunotherapy in NSCLC.82 There is scarce clinical data about TIM-3 blockade therapy in a heterogeneous population, where it was well tolerated, but showed limited clinical activity.83

Another checkpoint expressed on T cells is T-cell immunoreceptor with Ig and ITIM domain (TIGIT) and its ligand is CD155 with NSCLC expressing CD155 showing a lower response to anti-PD-1 treatment compared with CD155 negative tumors.84 Furthermore, TIGIT is highly expressed by Tregs and exhausted CD8+T cells, promoting an immunosuppressive TME.85 Disappointingly, a large clinical trial investigating an anti-TIGIT agent combined with anti-PD-L1 in NSCLC failed to meet its primary endpoint of PFS improvement.86 Despite this negative result, TIGIT is still thought to be a relevant checkpoint, especially in acquired resistance, where the role of Tregs and exhausted CD8+T cells is of special interest and selecting tumors resistant to ICI through one of these mechanisms might improve efficacy of TIGIT blocking antibodies.

When moving forward, we should learn from previous clinical trials showing no benefit with inhibiting these checkpoints. These studies are performed in an unselected patient population, which may undermine the therapeutic potential of tackling these checkpoints and this is strengthened by the observation that the expression of a certain checkpoint or ligand corresponds with response or stable disease. This is shown in small cohorts for TIM-3,87 CD155/TIGIT,84 and LAG-3.79

Effector function: IFN-γ, TNF-α and granzyme B

The CD8+T cell effector function is the final step in the tumor cell recognition and elimination and therefore, the last group of resistance mechanisms in immune-infiltrated tumors discussed here. To carry out this essential cytotoxic role, CD8+T cells must recognize and bind to tumor cells.48 Cytotoxicity is propagated by so-called immune effectors like IFN-γ, granzyme B and tumor necrosis factor (TNF), and different mechanisms can interfere with their function.

Of the cytokines involved in T-cell-mediated killing of tumor cells, IFN-γ is one of the best studied and involved in practically every resistance mechanism discussed earlier in this review. IFN-γ is a T-cell effector molecule that stimulates both immune cells and tumor cells directly through interferon gamma receptors 1 and 2 (IFNGR1/2) but with paradoxical functions in the tumor immune response, making its role highly context-dependent.

IFN-γ production is regulated by a variety of signaling molecules and other stimulatory factors such as ILs, (tumor) antigens, and IFN-γ itself as part of a positive feedback loop.88 IFN-γ production and killing by TILs can also be enhanced by activation of the STING pathway,24 while inactivation of STING may result in decreased sensitivity to immune-mediated killing with resistance to immunotherapy as a result.25 45

The positive antitumor effects of IFN-γ can be found in its stimulatory role in HLA class I expression, DC activity, direct stimulation of CD8+cytotoxic T cells and the induction of apoptosis in tumor cells. However, IFN-γ can also have negative effects in the context of cancer, for example, by inducing checkpoint molecule expression.

Whether IFN-γ functions as a protumorigenic or antitumorigenic agent seems to be at least partially dose-dependent: high concentrations of IFN-γ cause apoptosis in NSCLC cell lines through Janus-kinase/signal transducers and activators of transcription-1 (JAK-STAT1) activation, whereas low IFN-γ induces tumor stemness and increased metastasis.89 As a secreted factor, IFN-γ has long-range effects that can induce resistance to T cells before they have entered the tumor,90 potentially leading to immune exclusion. Indeed, preclinical and clinical studies have shown that prolonged IFN-γ signaling contributes to ICI resistance.69 91 92

There might also be context-dependency with regard to the role of IFN-γ when looking at in vitro versus in vivo but also primary versus acquired resistance. In the tumor, defective IFN-γ signaling results in reduced expression of IFNGR1, which correlates with resistance to CTLA-4 inhibition in a melanoma mouse model.93 This was recently argued by Song and Chow, who showed that tumor intrinsic mutations in IFN-γ signaling genes when present prior to treatment do sensitize a tumor to ICI treatment.94 Whether this mechanism also persists in acquired resistance is debatable since (acquired) loss-of-function alterations in the JAK-STAT pathway decrease the IFN-γ sensitivity in NSCLC resistant to ICI.95

Next to IFN-γ-mediated killing by TIL, T cell-derived TNF-α is able to provide antitumor immunity. Sensitivity of tumors to TNF-induced cell death is enhanced by inactivation of TNF receptor-associated factor 2 (TRAF2),96 and loss of TRAF2 is associated with improved response to immunotherapy, making it an attractive biomarker candidate and therapeutic target.42 A tumor’s TNF-α mutation status also induces immunogenicity through increased chemokine production, CXCL9 and CXCL10, among other mechanisms resulting in enhanced T-cell trafficking to the tumor.97

Lysis of tumor cells by granzyme B is the primary killing method of CD8+T cells, and low circulating granzyme B at the start of ICI treatment is predictive of shorter PFS and OS in stage IV NSCLC.98 SERPINB9 is a protease that inhibits granzyme B and, when overexpressed, causes resistance to T-cell killing in both NSCLC and melanoma.99 A small-molecule SERPINB9 inhibitor showed to slow tumor progression in lung cancer cell lines as well as melanoma.100

Concluding, different elements can counteract the anticancer immune response in immune-infiltrated tumors. Upregulation of various checkpoints or their ligands can be found in NSCLC resistant to ICI. Therapeutically, tackling these mechanisms seems straightforward and thus many new checkpoint inhibitor trials for NSCLC have been conducted or are in progress. However, they have yet to result in clinical benefit for a significant number of patients. We believe that this may again be an example of the consequences of a lack of patient selection for trials and, moving forward, we suggest to better study the expression of target molecules before including patients in these trials instead of disregarding a promising treatment combination due to lack of efficacy in all-comer ICI resistant patients.

And last but not least, the key player in this group of resistance mechanism is IFN-γ, and IFN sensitivity can be diminished with JAK-STAT or STING inactivity. Furthermore, continuous antigen stimulation and IFN signaling can result in resistance through T-cell exhaustion. An immune-infiltrated tumor can still be resistant to immune-mediated killing if one of these resistance mechanisms is present. Direct targeting of STING or combining ICI with chemotherapy or radiotherapy to reverse T-cell exhaustion might provide therapeutic solutions in this category of resistance. Both of these therapeutic strategies have been described above in different groups of resistance mechanisms. This highlights the complex overlap between groups, but also the opportunities to tackle different resistance mechanisms with one therapeutic strategy.

Concluding remarks and future perspectives

We have summarized the current evidence on resistance mechanisms in the cancer-immune interaction with a focus on advanced NSCLC. Essential elements in the anticancer immune response are the expression of neoantigens by HLA and the IFN-γ response. Without proper neoantigen formation and presentation by HLA, there will not be a robust anticancer immune response. On the other hand, even with adequate neoantigen presentation and successful sequential steps in priming, trafficking, and infiltration of T cells, a tumor insensitive to IFN-γ will still be resistant to immune-mediated killing. Furthermore, IFN-γ activity is involved in almost all elements of the anticancer immune response. Recent work of Song and Chow has challenged our view on this essential role of IFN-γ at least in the context of primary resistance,94 highlighting the complexity and also our currently still limited understanding of the matter.

This complexity of immune resistance is not only the result of the non-exhaustive wide variety of resistance mechanisms. It is further increased by how these resistance mechanisms interact with each other, interweaving the elements presented here as separate entities, and the occasionally contradictory roles that certain elements can play. Currently, in clinical trials testing therapeutic strategies to overcome ICI resistance, a one-size-fits-all approach is used, which has proven to be ineffective so far. We hypothesize that this is the result of the heterogeneity of resistance mechanisms underlining the complexity of the anticancer immune response. Therefore, it is necessary to translate the resistance mechanisms described in this review into a clinically applicable model that will help us to better define the resistance mechanisms present and make a better-informed decision on therapeutic strategies (figure 1).

With the current available studies mainly focusing on advanced disease, the scope of this review is also advanced NSCLC. It is unclear to which extent the here summarized data on resistance mechanisms can be extrapolated to earlier stages of the disease. Another limitation of this review is the limited availability of data on resistance mechanism emerging on different treatment regimens, for example, ICI with or without chemotherapy.

Currently, the presented approach is still conceptual, especially from a daily clinical practice perspective, but in order to move the field forward we must change our approach to tackle ICI resistance. At first, this approach may assist guidance in biomarker-selected, immunotherapy-centered clinical trials. When shown feasible in this setting a more targeted approach based on clinical consequence may guide the transition into daily clinical practice. Therefore, the proposed groups form a workable starting point when trying to create some order in the complexity of immune resistance mechanisms. Within the proposed categorization, we first divide tumors based on immune cell infiltration because of its crucial role in the anticancer immune response.20 When a tumor is immune desert, one should start with investigating the presence and presentation of neoantigens by HLA. When immune cells are only present at the tumor margins, one should search for stromal elements hindering immune cell infiltration. And when a tumor is immune infiltrated, one can assume that there once was an immune response and more adaptive mechanisms (eg, loss of neoantigens or checkpoint upregulation) are present.

With a focused and more or less hierarchical analysis of the tumor it may be possible to identify one or more resistance mechanisms that, based on the underlying mechanism, in the future can also guide treatment decisions. These treatment strategies need to be studied further, but knowledge of the overlap in resistance mechanisms in different groups and associations between these elements will definitely help the search for successful therapeutic strategies. The use of a resistance mechanism-based group definition may lead research and clinical practice in the right direction and be the next step in finally improving outcomes for patient with NSCLC who have shown resistance to PD-(L)1 checkpoint inhibition.
