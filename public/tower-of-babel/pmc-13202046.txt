Enhancing parental health knowledge through digital knowledge mobilisation tools: a randomised controlled trial and qualitative study
Authors: Sarah A Elliott, Samantha Cyrkot, Shannon D Scott, Lisa Hartling
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo assess the effectiveness of blogshots (ie, image-based digital knowledge mobilisation tools) to increase knowledge and manage expectations with respect to six Choosing Wisely Canada (CWC) recommendations about common childhood conditions.DesignMultimethod study including a non-pharmacological randomised controlled trial and qualitative interviews.SettingOnline questionnaires and semistructured interviews across Canada.ParticipantsParents or legal guardians of children under 5 years.InterventionParticipants were randomly allocated to receive one of two sets of blogshots whereby a blogshot on a new CWC topic was delivered weekly over 3 weeks (Group A: pharyngitis (diagnosis), common cold, asthma; Group B: pharyngitis (treatment), bronchiolitis, otitis media). Study investigators were blinded to participant allocation. Baseline and follow-up (week 5, month 3, month 6) questionnaires were administered. Qualitative interviews contextualised intervention data.Main outcome measuresThe primary outcome was change in knowledge. Secondary outcomes were change in expectations, intentions, actions over time and usability of the blogshots.Results167 participants (n=133 (80%) mothers) were included in the final analysis (group A, n=85; group B, n=82). Baseline knowledge scores did not differ between groups. At follow-up 1, those who received the blogshot for a given topic scored significantly higher (p<0.001, d=0.30) on knowledge questionnaires, compared with those that did not. No differences were observed in knowledge scores between groups at follow-up 2 and 3. Participants expressed intentions to use the blogshots (eg, share with friends, physicians), as well as actions (eg, wait/watch approach vs seek healthcare) to manage their child’s condition. Qualitative data (n=13 interviews) showed that blogshots are aesthetically pleasing and contain easy to read and understandable information.ConclusionsBlogshots can be an effective tool to disseminate child health information to parents. Due to the online dissemination, this approach could be scaled to a broader international level and applied to different health topics that are relevant across the world.Trial registration numberNCT06005337.

Introduction

Connecting parents and caregivers to research-based evidence has the power to improve health decision-making and reduce health system costs.1 The heaviest users of unscheduled healthcare are children, making up a large proportion of emergency department (ED) attendances that may be treatable elsewhere (eg, primary care or home).2 Subsequently, parents should be the target of efforts to promote empowerment, that is, the ‘process through which parents are able to increase the control they have over decisions and actions affecting their child’s health’.3

One mechanism for empowering parents to manage common acute childhood conditions appropriately could be through knowledge mobilisation (KM) of the Choosing Wisely recommendations.4 The international Choosing Wisely campaign aims to help physicians, patients and families engage in conversations around unnecessary tests, treatments and procedures, which often do more harm than good and result in significant waste for the healthcare system.4

In Canada, conditions such as ear infections, the common cold, sore throats, bronchiolitis and asthma are extremely common among children.5 These are conditions for which antibiotic treatment is usually unnecessary; however, antibiotics are frequently prescribed.6 Parents often seek care for these conditions from primary care providers or through the ED, representing a large burden on healthcare resources. While there are a number of paediatric specific Choosing Wisely Canada (CWC) recommendations, there is currently no outward facing CWC campaign targeting parents in the community.

Research has demonstrated that parent-informed and parent-mediated KM strategies can increase knowledge and influence decision-making and expectations regarding healthcare seeking and interventions.79 In turn, expectations can impact the care delivered by healthcare professionals (eg, prescriptions).10 11 These conceptual pathways align with the Theory of Planned Behaviour (TPB),12 which suggests that changes in parental knowledge and expectations can shape key determinants of behaviour, namely attitudes, perceived social norms and perceived behavioural control, thereby strengthening intentions and increasing the likelihood of engaging in evidence-aligned care decisions.13

Our goal was to evaluate the effectiveness of blogshots (image-based digital KM tools) for improving parents’ knowledge and expectations. Grounded in the TPB, this work examined how brief, parent-focused KM tools can support meaningful knowledge uptake and real-world behaviour change.

Methods

Study design

Following an a priori protocol, we conducted a multimethod online study including a non-pharmacological randomised controlled trial (RCT) and qualitative interviews (figure 1), (Clinicaltrials.gov NCT06005337). Results are reported following established guidelines.1416 The intervention and outcomes were not modified during the course of the evaluation.

Study design.

Patient and public involvement

Our Paediatric Parent Advisory group (P-PAG)17 was involved in planning (eg, refining the study design, codeveloping the recruitment strategy), developing and pilot testing the intervention and study processes (eg, six blogshots tailored to parents, recruitment materials, questionnaires, interview guide).

Population

Participants were eligible for study enrollment if they self-identified as a parent or legal guardian living in Canada with at least one child under 5 years of age, able to complete study tasks in English and had access to email and the internet via an electronic device. Participants were recruited using convenience sampling through traditional (eg, e-newsletters) and non-traditional methods (eg, social media).

Intervention

Six blogshots (ie, image-based digital KM tools)9 18 corresponding to six different CWC4 paediatric recommendations were developed (online supplemental appendix 1: Blogshots design and development process). The blogshots served as the primary intervention in this study and were delivered electronically over a 4-week period. The six chosen CWC recommendations aligned with common childhood conditions (sore throat, common cold, ear infections, asthma, bronchiolitis), which parents often seek care for either from their primary care provider or through the ED, representing a large burden on healthcare resources.31922 Parents were randomised into two parallel groups.

Group A received three CWC topic-specific blogshots corresponding to the following recommendations:

Topic 1: Do not routinely do a throat swab when children present with a sore throat if they have a cough, rhinitis or hoarseness;

Topic 1: Do not routinely do a throat swab when children present with a sore throat if they have a cough, rhinitis or hoarseness;

Topic 2: Do not recommend the use of cough and cold remedies in children under 6 years of age;

Topic 2: Do not recommend the use of cough and cold remedies in children under 6 years of age;

Topic 3: Do not use antibiotics for acute asthma exacerbations without clear signs of bacterial infection.

Topic 3: Do not use antibiotics for acute asthma exacerbations without clear signs of bacterial infection.

Group B received three CWC topic specific blogshots corresponding to the following recommendations:

Topic 4: Do not routinely use antibiotics in children with uncomplicated sore throats;

Topic 4: Do not routinely use antibiotics in children with uncomplicated sore throats;

Topic 5: Do not prescribe antibiotics in adults with bronchitis/asthma and children with bronchiolitis;

Topic 5: Do not prescribe antibiotics in adults with bronchitis/asthma and children with bronchiolitis;

Topic 6: Do not use antibiotics in adults and children with uncomplicated acute otitis media.

Topic 6: Do not use antibiotics in adults and children with uncomplicated acute otitis media.

Randomised controlled trial details

Data collection instruments

The research team developed four online questionnaires (baseline and 3 follow-up questionnaires) to measure the primary (knowledge) and secondary outcomes (intentions, expectations, actions, usability) before and after the intervention.23 Each questionnaire was pilot tested within our research team’s network and with our P-PAG, and took approximately 15–20 min to complete. Response options were a combination of multiple-choice, select all that apply, 5-point Likert scale and open text (online supplemental appendix 2: Questionnaire examples).

Procedures

An overview of the study procedures is provided below and in figure 1.

Step 1: Interested participants visited the online study platform (accessed via a study link found on the recruitment materials), completed five eligibility questions, reviewed the study information letter and provided informed consent to self-enrol in the study. The platform was managed by a third-party independent company (Nooro, Ontario, Canada) and was used to deliver all study questionnaires. Once enrolled, participants were randomised using a computer-generated block randomisation sequence (developed by a statistician) into group A or group B. Study investigators were blinded to participant allocation.

Step 2: At baseline (week 0) and directly following step 1, participants completed a 49-item online questionnaire. The questionnaire included 15 demographic items (eg, gender, age, ethnicity), 4 questions about participants’ experiences with common acute childhood conditions, and 18 knowledge questions (3 per recommendation topic) assessing understanding of the management of the 6 conditions. Participants also answered six questions about their expectations of healthcare providers (one per recommendation topic) and 6 questions about their own intentions to manage each condition (one per recommendation topic) (online supplemental appendix 2). Regardless of group assignment, all participants completed the same knowledge, expectation and intention questions for all six topics to allow each participant to act as their own control.

Step 3: After completing the baseline questionnaire, participants received one blogshot via email (delivered via Nooro) per week for 3 weeks and in week 4, they received a summary email containing the same three blogshots. Groups A and B received different sets of blogshots but followed the same email sequence and timing.

Step 4: At week 5 (1 week postintervention), participants in groups A and B received an email inviting them to complete a 37-item online questionnaire. The week 5 follow-up questionnaire 1 assessed knowledge retention (18 questions, 3 per recommendation topic), expectations (6 questions, 1 per recommendation topic), intentions (6 questions, 1 per recommendation topic) and usability of the blogshots (7 questions).23 The knowledge retention, expectation and intention questions were identical to those asked at baseline. Participants were also invited to participate in an optional one-on-one interview.

Step 5: At month 3 (8 weeks postintervention) and month 6 (20 weeks postintervention), participants received an email inviting them to complete a 22-item online questionnaire that assessed knowledge retention (18 questions, 3 per recommendation topic) and any actions they had taken to manage any of the six conditions during the study period (4 questions). The knowledge retention questions were identical to those asked at baseline and follow-up 1.

Sample size

We aimed to recruit 180 participants (90 per group) which allowed for an anticipated 25% dropout rate based on a minimum required sample of 128 participants (64 per group, assuming a 10% difference in knowledge scores between groups, a SD of 2.0, and with 80% power). These calculations were based on previously calculated sample sizes assessing knowledge in parents after a KM intervention.7 24

Outcomes and analysis

Our primary outcome was the change in participants’ knowledge scores, assessed using 9 questions (three per recommendation topic) related to the blogshots they received during the intervention period. Each question was scored from 0 to 2 points, with partial credit awarded when applicable (eg, for select-all-that-apply items), yielding a maximum possible score of 18. Secondary outcomes were: (1) change in expectations, (2) intentions, (3) actions over time and (4) usability of the blogshots. Likert-scale responses that assessed expectations (topics 1–6) and intentions (topics 1–3, 5) were converted into numerical values (1=strongly agree, 5=strongly disagree), and all responses that leaned towards 5=strongly disagree were preferred. Responses related to intentions (topics 4 and 6, assessed via multiple choice) as well as actions and usability were descriptively summarised.

Analysis followed an intention-to-treat approach, including all participants as originally randomised. Missing data were handled using regression-based multiple imputation.25 Paired t-tests assessed within-group changes in knowledge from baseline to follow-up, as well as overall change over time. Independent t-tests compared group differences in expectation and intention scores at baseline and follow-up 1, as well as changes in these scores. Statistical uncertainty was expressed using 95% one-sided CIs. A p<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen’s d.26

Qualitative component

Semistructured interviews

Participants were invited through purposeful sampling (based on parenting roles, education, ethnicity) to participate in optional one-on-one semistructured interviews using Zoom video conferencing software. Participants who expressed interest were contacted by the research team and interviewed. Interviews were used to gain an in-depth understanding of parents’ perceptions of the blogshots, intention to use the blogshots, and actions to manage their child’s condition. The semistructured interview guide was field tested and adapted using in-house research staff and parent volunteers (online supplemental appendix 3: Interview guide). Interviews were conducted by SC (woman) who is a research coordinator with a Master of Science and has previous qualitative research experience. Consenting participants had no prior relationship with the coordinator and were informed of the objectives at the beginning of the interview. No other individuals were present during interviews besides the participant and the research coordinator. Interviews were audio-recorded, deidentified and transcribed verbatim using a third-party transcription service (SimplyTranscription, Ontario, Canada). Field notes were made throughout the interviews. No participants withdrew from the interviews, no repeat interviews were conducted, and transcripts were not returned to the participants for corrections or to provide feedback on the findings.

Interview sample size

Data collection and analysis occurred iteratively until data redundancy was achieved. Sample size was informed by data saturation, which was assessed regularly through considering data comprehensiveness, variation, richness and redundancy.27

Interview outcomes and analysis

Thematic analysis was used to synthesise and identify common parent behaviours, processes and preferences.28 Data management and analysis were facilitated using NVivo V.14 Software (V.14.23.1, 2023 QSR International).

Interviews were coded by SC and categorised to facilitate development of themes. An inductive approach guided analysis.29 Coding for all interviews was conducted using a ‘line by line’ approach. Codes stayed close to the data by using participants’ own words as much as possible. Focused coding, grouping similar codes together, was then used to identify patterns in the data. The coding system was refined throughout the iterative data collection and analysis stages employing a secondary coder (SAE) to code 10% of the transcripts and compare to maintain intra-rater reliability.30 Discrepancies between the coders were discussed and resolved via consensus. The focused codes were further refined via collaboration between the authors into themes and sub-themes. All codes and transcripts were then re-examined to ensure consistency and accuracy of the interpretation. Other strategies for maintaining rigour included detailed study logs and audit trail to ensure transparency.30

Participant compensation and dissemination of study findings

Participants received a $C10 electronic gift card for completing each of the three follow-up questionnaires (up to $C30 in total). Those who participated in interviews received an additional $C25 electronic gift card. Once published, the research team will share the results with participants and P-PAG members by email, and will also disseminate the findings on social media for broader public reach.

Results

Participant characteristics

The final analysis included 167 participants (n=133 (80%) mothers; n=85 group A; n=82 group B) recruited between July and October 2023; follow-up data were collected until April 2024 (figure 2). Groups did not differ with respect to demographic variables (table 1). Sensitivity analyses, including multiple imputation for missing data and analysis of a per-protocol population, yielded similar results, indicating robustness of the findings. No adverse study events or unintended effects were reported.

Knowledge scores

No significant differences in average knowledge scores were noted between groups at baseline (mean (SD): 11.5 (4.1) vs 11.2 (3.8)), follow-up 2 (mean (SD):12.3 (3.4) vs 12.0 (3.5)) and follow-up 3 (mean (SD): 12.4 (3.3) vs 12.2 (3.2)). At follow-up 1, however, participants who received a recommendation-specific blogshot scored significantly higher than those who did not (mean (SD): 13.0 (3.4) vs 12.1 (3.3), p<0.001, d=0.30) (table 2).

A significantly greater change in expectations from baseline to follow-up 1 was found for topic 5 (bronchiolitis), where participants who received the blogshot were more likely to disagree with the expectation that a physician should prescribe antibiotics, compared with those who did not (mean (SD): 0.76 (0.91) vs 0.44 (1.13); p<0.05; d=0.31). No significant changes in expectations over time were observed for the remaining topics (1–4 and 6) (table 3).

Intentions

No significant differences in intentions to treat a condition (eg, taking a child for a throat swab, requesting antibiotics) were observed at baseline or follow-up 1 for topics 1, 3 and 5 between participants who received a topic-specific blogshot and those who did not. However, for topic 2 (cough and cold), a significant and favourable difference emerged at follow-up 1: participants who received the topic-specific blogshot were more likely to disagree with giving over-the-counter cough and cold medications to a child under five (mean (SD): 4.31 (0.87) vs 3.76 (1.21); p<0.001; d=0.52), despite no baseline difference (table 3). No significant changes in intentions over time were observed between groups for any topic.

For intentions related to antibiotic use for a sore throat (topic 4, assessed via multiple choice), most participants who received the topic-specific blogshot (77%, n=53/69) indicated they would manage their child’s symptoms at home for 2 days using remedies such as warm fluids, rest and pain medication, similar to those who did not receive the topic-specific blogshot (74%, n=56/76). For antibiotic use for an ear infection (topic 6, assessed via multiple choice), over half of participants who received the topic-specific blogshot (54%, n=37/69) reported they would fill a prescription only if their child’s symptoms worsened or failed to improve within 1–2 days, while 44% (n=30) said they would fill it immediately. Among those who did not receive the topic-specific blogshot, 45% (n=34/76) would wait, and 51% (n=39) would fill the prescription right away.

Action

Most participants (71%, n=91/128) reported their child experienced one of the acute conditions during the study period, most commonly the common cold. About 30% (n=38) sought care for a condition covered by a CWC blogshot they received. Of those, half (n=19) found the blogshots helpful in communicating with healthcare providers. Nearly half (n=18) received antibiotics; 24% (n=9) discussed side effects, and 29% (n=11) discussed a delayed or ‘wait and watch’ prescription in line with the recommendations in the blogshots (online supplemental appendix 4: Actions).

Usability

Over 90% of participants (out of n=145) agreed or strongly agreed that the blogshots improved their knowledge (n=139) and increased their confidence (n=134) to make decisions about their child’s health. Over 85% of these participants indicated they would use the blogshots to support future decisions (n=129), felt the content met their information needs (n=132), and found the blogshots easy to understand (n=142).

Qualitative interviews

Data were categorised into four sections representative of key elements that emerged from 13 interviews (30–60 min each). Overall, participants found the blogshots aesthetically pleasing with relevant and useful information. Participants reported learning new information from the blogshots and found them easy to read, understand and recall. They also reported an intention to use the blogshots and share them with others. Participants took various actions (eg, a ‘wait and watch’ approach) if their child became sick during the study (online supplemental appendix 5: Interview themes and sub-themes).

Discussion

This is one of a few RCTs examining the use of blogshots to communicate with healthcare consumers (ie, parents).31 We have previously found that blogshots compared with plain language summaries and Wikipedia pages showed significant (favourable) differences in terms of parent preferences, knowledge and usability.8 Another recent study compared comprehension of information between plain language summaries of health information and blogshots, and found higher comprehension scores among those who received blogshots.11

This study, which focused on CWC recommendations demonstrates the usefulness of blogshots for improving knowledge over the short-term, but also suggests that their effectiveness wanes over time. At the first follow-up, those who received blogshots scored significantly higher, indicating a meaningful increase in knowledge. However, the effect was not sustained over time. Previous studies have found similar trends with many strategies like patient education materials or self-management tools showing initial effectiveness, but few have demonstrated sustained impact.32 Studies assessing knowledge retention often highlight that long-term retention requires repetition, reinforcement or integration into practice and frequent use. One key area to study is the development of strategies to sustain the knowledge acquired, observed at follow-up 1. Periodic reinforcement of content, gamification or integrating blogshots into broader, continuous learning systems are some possible strategies that may help counteract the decline in knowledge scores observed at later follow-ups.33 Understanding the mechanisms behind this decline is equally important. Longitudinal studies can help identify the factors contributing to knowledge decline, such as cognitive overload, lack of engagement, or insufficient reinforcement.34 35 Nevertheless, our qualitative interviews revealed that some parents felt minimal pressure to recall information, as the blogshots were accessible and could be easily revisited when needed. Moreover, since our focus was on acute self-limiting conditions, parents may not have been motivated to retain information that was not directly relevant to their child at the time. Our findings related to parents’ actions and intentions also align with previously published work that notes the use of KM tools can help parents become more informed and confident health decision-makers.13638 Studies have shown that parents who used these tools were more likely to ask questions, engage in shared decision-making, and feel empowered during healthcare visits.1 This was emphasised by our qualitative interviews where parents expressed that the blogshots put their mind at ease and that they felt more confident and empowered to manage a condition in the future.

Despite being delivered through brief, single-email exposures, the blogshot intervention produced measurable benefits, indicating that even low-dose contact can influence parent outcomes. Future work should assess alternative delivery channels, as email may not be the most effective way for all parents to encounter this information. Identifying where parents naturally access health information could strengthen reach and engagement. While blogshots show promise for improving parental knowledge, larger-scale impact will likely require embedding them within broader, sustained public-facing strategies, supported by their low cost, scalability and potential to enhance parent–clinician communication.

Improving parents’ knowledge and awareness of CWC recommendations is essential for aligning expectations with evidence-based care and reducing requests for unnecessary tests and interventions, changes that may ultimately help lower healthcare costs and wait times. The TPB provides a useful framework for understanding how KM strategies, such as blogshots, may influence parental decision-making. According to the TPB, behaviour is shaped by three interconnected cognitive determinants: attitudes toward the behaviour, subjective norms, and perceived behavioural control.13 In child health, KM tools like blogshots can positively influence each of these determinants. For example, by outlining the benefits of evidence-aligned management and the harms of unnecessary tests or treatments, blogshots can shift parental attitudes while also reinforcing supportive subjective norms by showing that evidence-based approaches are widely endorsed by clinicians and health organisations. At the same time, their concise, actionable guidance can strengthen perceived behavioural control, increasing parents’ confidence in managing common childhood conditions at home. Together, these influences are expected to enhance behavioural intentions, which in turn could increase the likelihood that parents will engage in appropriate care-seeking and treatment decisions.

Future research could also draw on advances in motivational science while applying more intentional, theory-driven KM development processes.39 Ahmadi et al’s self-determination-based classification of motivational behaviours,40 for example, may help identify strategies that strengthen parents’ intrinsic motivation to engage with blogshots and other CWC resources. Complementing this, the three-phase storytelling KT framework outlined by Brooks et al offers a structured approach to developing such interventions: specifying clear goals, mechanisms of action, and behaviour change techniques; iteratively modelling and refining content with end-users; and formally evaluating impact.39 Integrating these approaches could help clarify which motivational strategies best support sustained engagement and improved understanding of the six CWC recommendations.

This study represents the first consumer-facing CWC initiative developed and tested specifically for parents showing potential effectiveness of blogshots for increasing parent knowledge. Participants valued the concise, visually appealing format of the blogshots, particularly the use of bolded key words, bullet points, and clear icons and graphics. Their accessibility, being free to view, easily shared online, and compatible with social media and messaging platforms further supports their potential for broad reach. Parents also emphasised the usefulness of embedded links and QR codes to access additional information when needed. The blogshot format could be adapted for other Choosing Wisely recommendations and scaled for international dissemination. However, cultural and linguistic tailoring will be essential to ensure relevance and acceptance across diverse populations. This may involve collaborating with communities to refine wording, translate content, and adjust visual design elements to reflect cultural norms and preferences.41

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study was the use of mixed methods. The RCT provided quantitative evidence on specific outcomes, while the qualitative interviews offered deeper insight into participants’ experiences and perspectives. Although we were not able to recruit comparable numbers of mothers and fathers (which limited our ability to fully explore potential differences in parenting roles), the sample did show diversity across several demographic elements. For example, household income was distributed across multiple strata, a substantial proportion of participants were not born in Canada, and 39% identified as a racial or ethnic minority. Targeted recruitment strategies are essential to ensure representation across gender and other demographic groups, particularly those who may experience differing levels of access to healthcare services. Strategies may include building strong relationships with, and approaching clinical, healthcare and community (eg, partnerships with faith-based organisations, community centres, cultural events and businesses such as barbershops) settings, snowball sampling, newspaper and social media advertising, and mass mailing of letters.42 For fathers specifically, targeted ads and in-person face-to-face recruitment may be an effective approach.43 All participants resided in Canada with access to a comparable healthcare system which may limit generalisability to other geographic regions. Lastly, although we collected and reported demographic information on gender, we did not collect information on sex which is a study limitation.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated the ability of blogshots to increase parent knowledge and revealed parents’ positive perspectives and utility of the tools. Connecting parents to research evidence through tools like blogshots has the potential to empower them, improve shared decision-making, and align healthcare decisions with best practices, ultimately reducing costs and enhancing care quality. Due to the nature of online dissemination, this approach could be scaled and applied to different topics to support international child health initiatives.
