A Denjoy-Wolff theorem for bounded symmetric domains
Cho-Ho Chu

Abstract
Let D D be a bounded symmetric domain of finite rank, realised as the open unit ball of a complex Banach space, which can be infinite dimensional. Given a fixed-point free compact holomorphic map f : D ⟶ D f:D\longrightarrow D , with iterates f n = f ∘ ⋯ ∘ f ⏟ n -times , f^{n}=\underbrace{f\circ\cdots\circ f}_{\mbox{$n$-times}}, such that the limit points of one orbit { a , f ⁡ ( a ) , f 2 ​ ( a ) , … } \{a,f(a),f^{2}(a),\ldots\} in an f f -invariant horoball of unit hororadius lie in the extended Shilov boundary of D D , we show that there is a holomorphic boundary component Γ \Gamma of D D , with closure Γ ¯ \overline{\Gamma} , such that ℓ ⁡ ( D ) ⊂ Γ ¯ \ell(D)\subset\overline{\Gamma} for all subsequential limits ℓ = lim k → ∞ f n k \ell=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}f^{n_{k}} of ( f n ) (f^{n}) . This generalises the Denjoy-Wolff theorem for a fixed-point free holomorphic self-map f f on the disc 𝔻 = { z ∈ ℂ : | z | < 1 } \mathbb{D}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|<1\} .

1
Introduction

The celebrated Denjoy-Wolff theorem
[
12
,
29
]
for the complex open unit disc

\mathbb{D}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|<1\}

asserts that the iterates

f^{n}=\underbrace{f\circ\cdots\circ f}_{\mbox{$n$-times}}

of a fixed-point free
holomorphic map
f:\mathbb{D}\longrightarrow\mathbb{D}
always converge locally uniformly
to a constant function
f_{0}(\cdot)=\xi
with
|\xi|=1
.

The unit disc
\mathbb{D}
is
the unique one-dimensional bounded symmetric domain of rank one (up to biholomorphism).
The Denjoy-Wolff theorem has been extended to higher dimensional
rank-
1
symmetric domains, namely, the complex Euclidean balls, by Hervé
[
17
]
, but fails for higher rank bounded symmetric domains,
even for the rank-
2
bidisc
\mathbb{D}^{2}=\mathbb{D}\times\mathbb{D}

[
18
]
.

There have been various generalisations of the Denjoy-Wolff theorem to higher and infinite dimensional bounded
domains (e.g.
[
2
,
7
,
8
,
26
]
).
Notably, a definitive generalisation to the bidisc
\mathbb{D}^{2}
has been achieved by Hervé
[
18
]
,
which can be formulated by stating that the images
\ell(\mathbb{D}^{2})
of all subsequential limits
\ell=\lim_{k}f^{n_{k}}
,
in the topology of locally uniform convergence,
of the iterates
(f^{n})
of a fixed-point free holomorphic self-map
f
on
\mathbb{D}^{2}
are contained in the
closure
\overline{\Gamma}
of one single holomorphic boundary component
\Gamma
of
\mathbb{D}^{2}
.

The holomorphic boundary components of
\mathbb{D}^{2}
are sets of the form

\{(\xi,\eta)\},\quad\{\xi\}\times{\mathbb{D}},\quad{\mathbb{D}}\times\{\eta\}\qquad(|\xi|=|\eta|=1)

which are faces of
\mathbb{D}^{2}
.
Hence three distinct possibilities for the iterates
(f^{n})
on
\mathbb{D}^{2}
can occur, as shown in
[
18
]
.
The holomorphic boundary components of
\mathbb{D}
are the singletons
\{\xi\}
with
|\xi|=1
. In view of this,
Hervé’s result generalises completely the Denjoy-Wolff theorem to the bidisc.

Despite many forms of generlisations of the Denjoy-Wolff theorem
to various higher dimensional domains delivered by many authors, the question
of extending Hervé’s version to all (finite dimensional) bounded symmetric domains has remained illusive
since Hervé’s publication
[
18
]
in 1954.
Hervé’s approach is specific to the bidisc
\mathbb{D}^{2}
and in the case of polydiscs
\mathbb{D}^{d}
(
d>2)
,
a version of the Denjoy-Wolff theorem
has been obtained in
[
2
]
, where it is shown that the images of all subsequential limits
\lim_{k}f^{n_{k}}
for a fixed-point free
holomorphic self-map
f
are contained in a closed set larger than a union
T
of holomorphic boundary components
of the polydisc. A similar result has also been shown in
[
10
]
for a finite Cartesian product of open unit balls of Hilbert spaces.
Nevertheless, it was remarked in
[
2
]
that
“it is impossible to determine a priori”
that
the image of a subsequential limit is contained in a particular
component of
T
, and for this,
“it is necessary to know something more about the map
f
”
.

To address the last remark, we first observe that the behaviour of the subsequential limits
\lim_{k}f^{n_{k}}
of
a fixed-point free compact holomorphic self-map
f
on a domain

D
are inevitably affected by the geometry of the topological boundary
\partial D
of
D

since
\partial D

attracts
all the orbit limit points, that is to say, the limit points of each and every
orbit

\mathcal{O}(a)=\{a,f(a),f^{2}(a),\ldots\}\qquad(a\in{D})

lie in
\partial{D}
(cf. Lemma
5.3
).

In the case of Euclidean (unit) balls
\mathbb{B}_{d}\subset\mathbb{C}^{d}

(d\in\mathbb{N})
,
a special geometric feature is that these domains are
strictly convex
,
that is,
\partial\mathbb{B}_{d}

coincides
with its (Bergman) Shilov boundary
\Sigma(\mathbb{B}_{d})
,
consisting of extreme points of the closure
\overline{\mathbb{B}}_{d}
,
and consequently all orbit limit points accumulate in
\Sigma(\mathbb{B}_{d})
.
This fact is crucial for the Denjoy-Wolff theorem for
\mathbb{B}_{d}
(cf. proof of Theorem
5.7
).
The Euclidean balls (and the open unit balls of Hilbert spaces)
are the only bounded symmetric domains which are strictly convex and therefore, for other domains
D

with Shilov boundary
\Sigma(D)
, the orbit limit points may
just lie in
\partial{D}\backslash\Sigma(D)
. In other words, there is no guarantee that

\Sigma(D)
would contain any orbit limit point.
In view of this, one would seek
a larger set in
\partial D
to attract orbit limit points, playing the role of
\Sigma(\mathbb{B}_{d})
in Euclidean balls.

Indeed, there is a natural candidate sandwiched between
\Sigma(D)
and
\partial D
, namely, the
real analytic manifold
T(D)
consisting of the limits
of geodesic rays in
D
emanating from a base point. These limits are called
tripotents
, which will be explained
in more detail in the next section. In the case of rank-
1
domains
D
, including the open unit balls of Hilbert spaces, we actually have

\Sigma(D)=T(D)=\partial D

but for other domains
the inclusions
\Sigma(D)\subset T(D)\subset\partial D
are proper. On the other hand, if
D
is a polydisc,

T(D)
coincides with its submanifold
T_{1}(D)
of
structural
tripotents (also defined in the next section). If
D
is a Lie ball,
then we have
\Sigma(D)=T_{1}(D)\neq T(D)
. In view of these examples,
one is led to consider the submanifold
\Sigma^{*}(D)=\Sigma(D)\cup T_{1}(D)
of
T(D)
so that

\Sigma^{*}(D)=T(D)
for both polydiscs and rank-
1
domains
D
. We call
\Sigma^{*}(D)
the

extended Shilov boundary
of
D
.
Although
\Sigma^{*}(D)
is a larger manifold than
\Sigma(D)
,
it is still inconclusive that
\Sigma^{*}(D)
would contain any orbit limit point.
This appears to be the crux of the matter and explains the obstacle to extending fully the Denjoy-Wolff theorem
to other domains. If we remove this obstacle (by letting
\Sigma^{*}(D)
attract enough orbit limit points),
then a full generalisation of the Denjoy-Wolff theorem
emerges.

In this paper, we prove in Theorem
5.5
the following extension of
the Denjoy-Wolff theorem
to all finite-rank bounded symmetric domains,
which include all finite dimensional ones, but can also be infinite dimensional.

Main Theorem
.

Let
D
be a bounded symmetric domain of finite rank, realised as the open unit ball of a complex Banach space, and
f:D\longrightarrow D
a fixed-point free compact holomorphic map. If the extended Shilov boundary
\Sigma^{*}(D)

contains the limit points of
one orbit
\mathcal{O}(a)
in an
f
-invariant horoball of unit hororadius, then
there is a holomorphic boundary component
\Gamma
in the boundary of
D
such that

\ell(D)\subset\overline{\Gamma}
for all subsequential limits
\ell=\lim_{k}f^{n_{k}}
of
(f^{n})
,
in the topology of locally uniform convergence.

If
D
is the bidisc or the open unit ball of a Hilbert space, the condition on
\Sigma^{*}(D)
is superfluous.

In the case of the open unit ball
D
of a Hilbert space (including
\mathbb{D}
), the assumption on
\Sigma^{*}({D})
is vacuous since

\Sigma^{*}({D})
is the boundary of
D
as noted earlier. In the exceptional case of the bidisc
\mathbb{D}^{2}
,
we have already noted that Hervé has shown the above result without the condition on
\Sigma^{*}(\mathbb{D}^{2})=T(\mathbb{D}^{2})
.
However, Hervé’s proof is protracted and only applicable to
\mathbb{D}^{2}
. For completeness, we provide
in the Appendix a simplification of Hervé’s proof, which exposes the special role of
\mathbb{D}^{2}
.

The compactness assumption on
f
in the theorem is only necessary if
D
is contained in
an infinite dimensional Banach space
V

since the result is false without this condition even in the case of a rank-
1
infinite dimensional
domain, namely, the open unit ball of an infinite dimensional complex Hilbert space
[
9
]
.
We say that
f:D\longrightarrow D\subset V
is
compact
if the closure
\overline{f(D)}
is compact
in
V
.
If
V
is finite dimensional, then a continuous map
f:D\longrightarrow D
is always compact.

For
non-compact
holomorphic self-maps
f
on an infinite dimensional rank-
1
domain
D
, of which the
open unit balls of infinite dimensional Hilbert spaces are the only example,
we show in Theorem
5.7
the two alternatives for all subsequential limits
\ell=\lim_{k}f^{n_{k}}
to conclude the paper.

By a
horoball
we mean a (nonempty) convex subdomain in
D
of the form

\{x\in D:F(x)<s\}\qquad(s>0)

for some continuous function
F:D\longrightarrow(0,\infty)
, where the defining function
F
is the exponential of a
horofunction

on
D
(à la Gromov
[
15
]
) and
s
is called the
hororadius
. It is called
f
-invariant
if

f(\{x\in D:F(x)<s\})\subset\{x\in D:F(x)<s\}.

The
f
-invariance of all horoballs of hororadius
s>0
is equivalent to
F\circ f\leq F

in which case,
F
is called
f
-invariant.
It has been shown
in
[
11
, Theorem 4.4]
that
D
contains an abundance of
f
-invariant horoballs
and is actually entirely covered by them, given any
fixed-point free compact holomorphic self-map
f
on
D
. Relevant details of horoballs
and horofunctions will be given in Section
4
.

The main ingredients of our novel approach, as hinted above, are the horoballs, defined in
[
11
]
and (
4.4
),
and their defining functions, which are related to Gromov’s notion of horofunctions in
[
3
,
15
]
,
together with the Jordan algebraic structures of symmetric domains, in particular,
the Bergman operator introduced in (
2.3
), which plays a prominent role
and facilitates several computations involving the horoballs and horofunctions.

Given a fixed-point free compact holomorphic map

f:D\longrightarrow D
, a key in proving Theorem
5.5
is the existence of

f
-invariant horoballs in
D
, in other words, the existence of an
f
-invariant function
F
,
which is exposed in Lemma
5.4
. The function
F

is defined by a sequence in
D
converging to the
boundary. A crucial element in our arguments involves the computation of
F
with such a sequence.
Theorem
4.11
provides another key
which reveals that the intersection of all closed horoballs defined by
F

is exactly the closure of a holomorphic boundary component of
D
. To accomplish all this, we rely on
a detailed analysis of the
Jordan algebraic structures of a symmetric domain to provide an explicit and tractable description of the horoballs and the
associated horofunctions, in terms of the Bergman operators.

We begin by introducing Jordan structures in the next section and prove several relevant results
for later applications. We discuss holomorphic boundary components in the following section.
After this, we derive a number of important properties of horoballs and their defining functions, which
are used to prove the Denjoy-Wolff theorem in the final section.
Various novel results we prove for Jordan structures and horofunctions on bounded symmetric domains
may be of independent interest.

In what follows, all Banach spaces are over the field of complex numbers, unless stated otherwise,
and the complex vector space
H(D,W)
of holomorphic maps
from a
domain
, that is, an open connected set,
D
in a Banach space
to some Banach space
W
is equipped with the topology of
locally uniform
convergence
so that a sequence
(f_{n})
in
H(D,W)
converges to some
f\in H(D,W)
in this topology
if and only if it converges to
f

uniformly on any finite union of closed balls strictly contained in
D
(cf.
[
6
, 1.1]
).

The topological boundary of a bounded domain
D
in a Banach space will always be denoted by

\partial D
.

2
Jordan structures of bounded symmetric domains

A bounded domain
D
in a complex Banach space is called
symmetric
if at each point
p\in D
,
there is a (necessarily unique) symmetry
s_{p}:D\longrightarrow D
, which is a biholomorphic map
such that
s_{p}\circ s_{p}
is the identity map and
p
is the only fixed-point of
s_{p}
in a neighbourhood of
p
.
Bounded symmetric domains are examples of symmetric Banach manifolds, which have been
discussed in detail in
[
28
]
(see also
[
6
]
).
In finite dimensions, they are exactly the class of Hermitian symmetric spaces
of non-compact type via Harish-Chandra’s realisation
[
16
]
, and have been classified by É. Cartan using Lie theory

[
4
]
.

The key to applying Jordan theory in our setting is the seminal result of Kaup
[
20
]
, and its finite dimensional
precursor by Loos
[
24
]
, asserting that
bounded symmetric domains are exactly (via biholomorphisms) the open unit balls of the complex Banach spaces
equipped with a particular Jordan structure, called
JB*-triples
. This offers a Jordan algebraic description as well as
a unified treatment of bounded symmetric domains in both finite and infinite dimensions. Nevertheless, this Jordan
structure is closely related to the Lie structure associated to a bounded symmetric domain
D
. Indeed, the JB*-triple
structure is derived from the Lie triple system
\mathfrak{p}
in the Cartan decomposition

\mathfrak{g}=\mathfrak{k}\oplus\mathfrak{p}

(2.1)

of the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{g}
of complete holomorphic vector fields on
D
, induced by the symmetry
s_{p}
at a point

p\in D
.

A
JB*-triple
is a complex Banach space
V
equipped with
a continuous triple product

\{\cdot,\cdot,\cdot\}:V\times V\times V\longrightarrow V,

called a
Jordan triple product
, which is symmetric and linear in the outer variables, but conjugate linear in the
middle variable, and satisfies the
triple identity

\{a,b,\{x,y,z\}\}=\{\{a,b,x\},y,z\}-\{x,\{b,a,y\},z\}+\{x,y,\{a,b,z\}\}\quad(a,b,x,y,z\in V)

such that the bounded linear map

a{\,\vbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.4pt\hbox{\vrule width=0.4pt,height=4.0pt\kern 4.0pt\vrule width=0.4pt}\hrule height=0.4pt}}\,}\,a:x\in V\mapsto\{a,a,x\}\in V\qquad(a,x\in V)

satisfies

(i)

a{\,\vbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.4pt\hbox{\vrule width=0.4pt,height=4.0pt\kern 4.0pt\vrule width=0.4pt}\hrule height=0.4pt}}\,}\,a
is hermitian, that is,

\|\exp it(a{\,\vbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.4pt\hbox{\vrule width=0.4pt,height=4.0pt\kern 4.0pt\vrule width=0.4pt}\hrule height=0.4pt}}\,}\,a)\|=1
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}
;

(ii)

a{\,\vbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.4pt\hbox{\vrule width=0.4pt,height=4.0pt\kern 4.0pt\vrule width=0.4pt}\hrule height=0.4pt}}\,}\,a
has a non-negative spectrum;

(iii)

\|a{\,\vbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.4pt\hbox{\vrule width=0.4pt,height=4.0pt\kern 4.0pt\vrule width=0.4pt}\hrule height=0.4pt}}\,}\,a\|=\|a\|^{2}.

From the triple identity, one can derive the useful identity

\{\{x,y,x\},z,\{x,y,x\}\}=\{x,y,\{x,z,x\},y,x\}\qquad(x,y,z\in V)

(2.2)

in a JB*-triple
V

[
5
, Lemma 1.2.4]
.

Remark
.

We note from Kaup’s result that a complex Banach space is a JB*-triple if and only if its open unit ball
is a symmetric domain (cf.
[
5
, Theorem 2.5.27]
) and further, a bounded symmetric domain
D
is biholomorphic
to the open unit ball of a
unique
JB*-triple
V
, in the sense that if
D
is biholomorphic to the open
unit ball of another JB*-triple
W
, then
V
and
W
have identical JB*-triple structure via an isomorphism
(cf.
[
6
, p. 176]
). Hence in the sequel, we can, and will,
always identify a bounded symmetric domain with the open unit ball of a JB*-triple
unambiguously, and define its
dimension
,
\dim D
, to be that of the underlying JB*-triple.

Examples of JB*-triples include Hilbert spaces and C*-algebras. The Jordan
triple product in a Hilbert space
V
with inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is given by

\{a,b,c\}=\frac{1}{2}(\langle a,b\rangle c+\langle c,b\rangle a)\qquad(a,b,c\in V).

In particular,
(\mathbb{C},|\cdot|)
is a JB*-triple with the complex modulus
|\cdot|

and the standard inner product. As usual, the complex conjugate of a complex number
\alpha
is denoted
throughout by
\overline{\alpha}
.

In a C*-algebra
\mathcal{A}
, the Jordan triple product is

\{a,b,c\}=\frac{1}{2}(ab^{*}c+cb^{*}a)\qquad(a,b,c\in\mathcal{A}).

More generally, the Banach space
L(H,K)
of bounded linear operators between Hilbert spaces
H
and
K

is a JB*-triple in
the triple product
\{a,b,c\}=\frac{1}{2}(ab^{*}c+cb^{*}a)
, where
b^{*}
denotes the adjoint operator of
b
.
The symbol
L(K,K)
is shortened to
L(K)
.

Definition
.

The open unit ball of a complex Hilbert space will be called
a
Hilbert ball
.

Example 2.1
.

Let
\{V_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\Lambda}
be a family of JB*-triples. We define their
\ell_{\infty}
-sum

V
to be the direct sum

V=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\Lambda}{}_{{}_{\infty}}V_{\alpha}=\{(v_{\alpha})\in\mbox{\Large$\boldsymbol{\times}$}_{\alpha}V_{\alpha}:\sup_{\alpha}\|v_{\alpha}\|<\infty\},

equipped with the
\ell_{\infty}
-norm

\|(v_{\alpha})\|_{\infty}:=\sup_{\alpha}\|v_{\alpha}\|.

Then
V
is a JB*-triple in the coordinatewise triple product

\{(a_{\alpha}),(b_{\alpha}),(c_{\alpha})\}=(\{a_{\alpha},b_{\alpha},c_{\alpha}\}).

If
\Lambda
is finite, the open unit ball
D
of
V
is the Cartesian product

\mbox{\Large$\boldsymbol{\times}$}_{\alpha}D_{\alpha}
of the open unit balls
D_{\alpha}
of
V_{\alpha}
.
In particular, the polydisc
\mathbb{D}^{d}
is the open unit ball of the JB*-triple

\mathbb{C}\oplus_{\infty}\cdots\oplus_{\infty}\mathbb{C}
of
d
-fold
\ell_{\infty}
-sum of
\mathbb{C}
.

Let
V
be a JB*-triple with open unit ball
D
.
Given
a,b\in V
, we define the
box operator

a{\,\vbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.4pt\hbox{\vrule width=0.4pt,height=4.0pt\kern 4.0pt\vrule width=0.4pt}\hrule height=0.4pt}}\,}\,b:V\longrightarrow V
by

a{\,\vbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.4pt\hbox{\vrule width=0.4pt,height=4.0pt\kern 4.0pt\vrule width=0.4pt}\hrule height=0.4pt}}\,}\,b(x)=\{a,b,x\}\qquad(x\in V)

where
\|a{\,\vbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.4pt\hbox{\vrule width=0.4pt,height=4.0pt\kern 4.0pt\vrule width=0.4pt}\hrule height=0.4pt}}\,}\,b\|\leq\|a\|\|b\|
. We note that
\|(a{\,\vbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.4pt\hbox{\vrule width=0.4pt,height=4.0pt\kern 4.0pt\vrule width=0.4pt}\hrule height=0.4pt}}\,}\,a)(a)\|=\|a\|^{3}
.

The
Bergman operator

B(b,c):V\longrightarrow V
, where
b,c\in V
, plays a crucial role
in our investigation. It is a linear map defined by

B(b,c)(x)=x-2(b{\,\vbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.4pt\hbox{\vrule width=0.4pt,height=4.0pt\kern 4.0pt\vrule width=0.4pt}\hrule height=0.4pt}}\,}\,c)(x)+\{b,\{c,x,c\},b\}\qquad(x\in V).

(2.3)

We note that

\|B(b,c)(x)\|\leq\|x\|+2\|b\|\|c\|\|x\|+\|b\|^{2}\|c\|^{2}\|x\|=(1+\|b\|^{2}\|c\|^{2})\|x\|.

(2.4)

For each
a\in D
, the square root
B(a,a)^{1/2}
exists and there is an induced
biholomorphic map
g_{a}:D\longrightarrow D
,
called a
transvection
(or
Möbius transformation
),
given by

g_{a}(x)=a+B(a,a)^{1/2}(I+x{\,\vbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.4pt\hbox{\vrule width=0.4pt,height=4.0pt\kern 4.0pt\vrule width=0.4pt}\hrule height=0.4pt}}\,}\,a)^{-1}(x)\qquad(x\in D)

(2.5)

where
I
denotes the identity operator on
V
and the inverse

(I+x{\,\vbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.4pt\hbox{\vrule width=0.4pt,height=4.0pt\kern 4.0pt\vrule width=0.4pt}\hrule height=0.4pt}}\,}\,a)^{-1}:V\longrightarrow V
exists as
\|x{\,\vbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.4pt\hbox{\vrule width=0.4pt,height=4.0pt\kern 4.0pt\vrule width=0.4pt}\hrule height=0.4pt}}\,}\,a\|\leq\|x\|\|a\|<1
.
In particular,
g_{0}
is the identity map on
D
and is the only transvection
having a fixed point in
D
. We note that

g_{a}(0)=a
and
g_{a}^{-1}=g_{-a}

[
6
, Lemma 3.2.25]
.
In the Cartan decomposition associated to
D
in (

),
\mathfrak{g}
is the Lie
algebra of the automorphism group of
D
and

\exp(\mathfrak{p})
is the set of transvections.

On the unit disc
\mathbb{D}
, we have

B(a,a)z=(|1-|a|^{2})^{2}z\quad{\rm and}\quad B(a,a)^{1/2}z=(|1-|a|^{2})z\quad(z\in\mathbb{C}).

(2.6)

The Kobayashi distance
\kappa
on
D
cam be computed using transvections:

\kappa(z,w)=\tanh^{-1}\|g_{-z}(w)\|=\kappa(w,z)\qquad(z,w\in D).

(2.7)

The distance
\kappa
is contracted by holomorphic maps and

\|w\|=\tanh\kappa(0,w)
for

[

, Theorem 3.5.9]
.

The quadratic operators
Q_{a}:V\longrightarrow V
, where
a\in V
, also play an important role in
JB*-triples. These operators are defined by

Q_{a}(x)=\{a,x,a\}\qquad(x\in V).

We note that
B(a,b)
is invertible for
\|a\|\|b\|<1
(cf.

The proof of the following two identities

where
\psi_{\beta_{j}}:\mathbb{D}\longrightarrow\mathbb{D}
, for

,
are transvections induced
\beta_{j}\in\mathbb{D}
, with

\psi_{\beta_{j}}(z)=\frac{\beta_{j}+z}{1+\overline{\beta}_{j}z}\qquad(z\in\mathbb{D}).

Proof.

First, we have

\displaystyle g_{{}_{\beta_{1}e_{1}}}(\alpha_{1}e_{1})=\beta_{1}e_{1}+B(\beta_{1}e_{1},\beta_{1}e_{1})^{1/2}(I+\alpha_{1}e_{1}{\,\vbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.4pt\hbox{\vrule width=0.4pt,height=4.0pt\kern 4.0pt\vrule width=0.4pt}\hrule height=0.4pt}}\,}\,\beta_{1}e_{1})^{-1}(\alpha_{1}e_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta_{1}e_{1}+B(\beta_{1}e_{1},\beta_{1}e_{1})^{1/2}(I+\alpha_{1}\overline{\beta}_{1}e_{1}{\,\vbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.4pt\hbox{\vrule width=0.4pt,height=4.0pt\kern 4.0pt\vrule width=0.4pt}\hrule height=0.4pt}}\,}\,e_{1})^{-1}(\alpha_{1}e_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta_{1}e_{1}+B(\beta_{1}e_{1},\beta_{1}e_{1})^{1/2}(I-\alpha_{1}\overline{\beta}_{1}e_{1}{\,\vbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.4pt\hbox{\vrule width=0.4pt,height=4.0pt\kern 4.0pt\vrule width=0.4pt}\hrule height=0.4pt}}\,}\,e_{1}+\alpha_{1}^{2}\overline{\beta}_{1}^{2}(e_{1}{\,\vbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.4pt\hbox{\vrule width=0.4pt,height=4.0pt\kern 4.0pt\vrule width=0.4pt}\hrule height=0.4pt}}\,}\,e_{1})^{2}-\cdots)(\alpha_{1}e_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta_{1}e_{1}+B(\beta_{1}e_{1},\beta_{1}e_{1})^{1/2}(1-\alpha_{1}\overline{\beta}_{1}+\alpha_{1}^{2}\overline{\beta}_{1}^{2}-\cdots)(\alpha_{1}e_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta_{1}e_{1}+B(\beta_{1}e_{1},\beta_{1}e_{1})^{1/2}(1+\alpha_{1}\overline{\beta}_{1})^{-1}(\alpha_{1}e_{1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\beta_{1}e_{1}+\frac{\alpha_{1}(1-|\beta_{1}|^{2})}{1+\alpha_{1}\overline{\beta}_{1}}e_{1}\qquad({\rm by}~(\ref{eq:Bergman Sq Rt}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\beta_{1}+\alpha_{1}}{1+\alpha_{1}\overline{\beta}_{1}}e_{1}=\psi_{\beta_{1}}(\alpha_{1})e_{1}.

Now a repeated application of Lemma
2.7
gives

\displaystyle g_{a}(\alpha_{1}e_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{p}e_{p})=g_{{}_{\beta_{1}e_{1}}}\circ\cdots\circ g_{{}_{\beta_{p}e_{p}}}(\alpha_{1}e_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{p}e_{p})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g_{{}_{\beta_{1}e_{1}}}\circ\cdots\circ g_{{}_{\beta_{p-1}e_{p-1}}}(\alpha_{1}e_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{p-1}e_{p-1}+g_{{}_{\beta_{p}e_{p}}}(\alpha_{p}e_{p}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g_{{}_{\beta_{1}e_{1}}}\circ\cdots\circ g_{{}_{\beta_{p-1}e_{p-1}}}(\alpha_{1}e_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{p-1}e_{p-1}+\psi_{\beta_{p}}(\alpha_{p})e_{p})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g_{{}_{\beta_{1}e_{1}}}\circ\cdots\circ g_{{}_{\beta_{p-2}e_{p-2}}}(\alpha_{1}e_{1}+\cdots+g_{{}_{\beta_{p-1}e_{p-1}}}(\alpha_{p-1}e_{p-1})+\psi_{\beta_{p}}(\alpha_{p})e_{p})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g_{{}_{\beta_{1}e_{1}}}\circ\cdots\circ g_{{}_{\beta_{p-2}e_{p-2}}}(\alpha_{1}e_{1}+\cdots+\psi_{\beta_{p-1}}(\alpha_{p-1})e_{p-1}+\psi_{\beta_{p}}(\alpha_{p})e_{p})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\psi_{\beta_{1}}(\alpha_{1})e_{1}+\cdots+\psi_{\beta_{p}}(\alpha_{p})e_{p}.

Let
D
be a bounded symmetric domain realised as the open unit ball of a JB*-triple
V
.
A vector subspace
E
of
V
is called a
subtriple
if
a,b,c\in E
implies

\{a,b,c\}\in E
. The
rank
of

,
and of

, is defined to be the extended real number

\sup\{\dim V(a):a\in V\}\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}

where
V(a)
denotes the smallest closed subtriple of

containing
a
.
For instance, the polydisc
\mathbb{D}^{d}
is of rank
d
.
In finite dimensions,
the rank is the (common) dimension of a maximal abelian subspace of
\mathfrak{p}
in the Cartan decomposition
(
2.1
) associated with

(cf.
[
6
, p. 185]
).

We say that

, or

, is of

if it is of rank
r\in\mathbb{N}
, in which case its rank is the cardinality of a maximal family of
mutually orthogonal minimal tripotents
[
6
, p. 187]
. Finite-rank JB*-triples are reflexive Banach spaces

[
6
, Theorem 3.3.5]
and hence their open unit ball

is relatively weakly compact, that is,
the norm closure
\overline{D}
is compact in the weak topology

.

The

of a tripotent

is defined to be the rank
of the Peirce 2-space
V_{2}(e)
. If
e
is a finite sum
e=e_{1}+\cdots+e_{p}
of pairwise orthogonal minimal tripotents,
then
e_{1},\ldots,e_{p}\in V_{2}(e)
and the rank of

is

.
If

is of finite rank

and

is a maximal tripotent in

.

É. Cartan’s classification of finite dimensional bounded symmetric domains in
[
4
]
can be extended to

[

, Theorem 3.3.5;(3.31)]
.
The irreducible ones are (biholomorphic to) the open unit balls of the following six types of JB*-triples,
called

\displaystyle{\rm(I)}

\displaystyle{\displaystyle L}(\mathbb{C}^{d},K)\quad(d=1,2,\ldots),~{\rm rank}\,=d\leq\dim K,\quad

\displaystyle\text{\rm(II)}

\displaystyle\{z\in{L}(\mathbb{C}^{d}):z^{t}=-z\}\quad(d=5,6,\ldots),~{\rm rank}\,=\left[\frac{d}{2}\right]

\displaystyle\text{\rm(III)}

\displaystyle\{z\in{L}(\mathbb{C}^{d}):z^{t}=z\}\quad(d=2,3,\ldots),~{\rm rank}\,=d

\displaystyle\text{\rm(IV)}

\displaystyle\text{\rm spin
factor},~{\rm rank}\,=2

\displaystyle\text{\rm(V)}

\displaystyle M_{1,2}(\mathcal{O}),~{\rm rank}\,=2

\displaystyle\text{\rm(VI)}

\displaystyle H_{3}(\mathcal{O}),~{\rm rank}\,=3

where
{L}(\mathbb{C}^{d},K)
is the JB*-triple of bounded linear
operators from
\mathbb{C}^{d}
to a Hilbert space

and

z^{t}
denotes
the transpose of
z
in the JB*-triple
{L}(\mathbb{C}^{d})

of
d\times d
complex matrices. The JB*-triple
M_{1,2}(\mathcal{O})
consists of
1\times 2
matrices over the Cayley algebra
\mathcal{O}
,
and
H_{3}(\mathcal{O})
consists of
3\times 3
hermitian matrices over
\mathcal{O}
.

A

is a JB*-triple
(V,\{\cdot,\cdot,\cdot\})
, with
\dim V>2

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle

and a conjugate linear isometry

(i)

,
\langle x^{*},y^{*}\rangle=\langle y,x\rangle
,

(ii)

\{x,y,z\}=\frac{1}{2}(\langle x,y\rangle z+\langle z,y\rangle x-\langle x,z^{*}\rangle y^{*})\quad(x,y,z\in V)
.

The open unit ball of a spin factor

is known as a
Lie ball
.
The nonzero tripotents in
V
are either minimal or maximal, and the minimal tripotents are exactly
the extreme points
e
of the closed unit ball of the Hilbert space
(V,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
satisfying

\langle e,e^{*}\rangle=0
, in which case
e^{*}
is also a minimal tripotent and we have

P_{2}(e)=\langle\cdot,e\rangle e,\quad P_{0}(e)=\langle\cdot,e^{*}\rangle e^{*}.

(2.28)

We refer to
[
6
, 3.3]
for more details.

Evidently, the rank of a finite dimensional domain
is finite although an infinite dimensional bounded symmetric domain can be of finite rank,
as shown in the examples of (I) and (IV) above.

For a finite-rank bounded symmetric domain

realised as the open unit ball of a JB*-triple, it has been shown
in
[

, Theorem 3.4.6]
that its Shilov boundary

is exactly the manifold

of maximal tripotents
in

Example 2.9
.

Given a Lie ball

in a spin factor

, we have

.
Indeed,

always contains minimal tripotents and they are not maximal tripotents.
Given a maximal tripotent

Hence

The spectral values

Given a sequence

Definition 2.13

3
Boundary structures

To generalise the Denjoy-Wolff theorem, we will show that all limit points of every
orbit
\mathcal{O}(a)
for a fixed-point free compact holomorphic self-map
f
on a
finite-rank bounded symmetric domain
D
are
contained in one single
holomorphic boundary component
in
\partial D
,
provided that the extended Shilov boundary of
D
contains sufficient orbit limit points.
We first describe these
components and their properties in this section.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
U
be a convex domain, with closure
\overline{U}
,
in a complex Banach space
V
.
A subset
\Gamma\subset\overline{U}
is called a

holomorphic
component

if the following conditions
are satisfied:

(i)

\Gamma\neq\emptyset
;

(ii)

for each holomorphic map
\gamma:\mathbb{D}\longrightarrow\overline{U}
,
either
\gamma(\mathbb{D})\subset\Gamma
or
\gamma(\mathbb{D})\subset\overline{U}\backslash\Gamma
;

(iii)

\Gamma
is minimal
with respect to (i) and (ii).

Two holomorphic components are either equal or
disjoint. The domain
U
is the unique open holomorphic component of
\overline{U}
, all others
are contained in the boundary
\partial U

[
21
]
. The components in
\partial U
form a
partition of
\partial U
and are called the
holomorphic boundary components
of
U
.

For each
a\in\overline{U}
, we denote by
\Gamma_{a}
the holomorphic
component containing
a
.
Let
D
be a bounded symmetric domain realised as the open unit ball
of a JB*-triple
V
.
Given a tripotent
e\in\partial D
, it has been shown in
[
21
]
that the holomorphic
boundary
component

\Gamma_{e}
containing
e

is the convex set

\Gamma_{e}=e+(V_{0}(e)\cap D)\subset\partial D

(3.1)

where
V_{0}(e)
is the Peirce
0
-space
of
e
. In particular,
\Gamma_{e}=\{e\}
if
e
is a maximal tripotent.
Further, if
D
is of finite rank, then all holomorphic boundary components
in
\partial D
are of this form.

It has been shown in
[
6
, Remark 3.4.14]

that the norm closure
\overline{\Gamma}_{e}

is a face of
\overline{D}
, that is,
x,y\in\overline{\Gamma}_{e}
whenever
\lambda x+(1-\lambda)y\in\overline{\Gamma}_{e}
with

0<\lambda<1
and
x,y\in\overline{D}
.

Example 3.2
.

Let
D
be the open unit ball of a Hilbert space

.
The tripotents of

are exactly the extreme points of

\overline{D}
. The Peirce
0

vanishes for each tripotent

and hence
the boundary components in
\partial D
are the singletons

with

Let

of

for

is the open unit ball of the JB*-triple

A tripotent

is of the form

,
where

\|\xi_{j}\|=\left\{\begin{matrix}1&(j\in J)\\
0&(j\notin J)\end{matrix}\right.

for some non-empty set

.
We have

V_{0}(\boldsymbol{e})=\{(v_{1},\ldots,v_{p}):(\xi_{j}{\,\vbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.4pt\hbox{\vrule width=0.4pt,height=4.0pt\kern 4.0pt\vrule width=0.4pt}\hrule height=0.4pt}}\,}\,\xi_{j})(v_{j})=0\,\forall j\}=\{(v_{1},\ldots,v_{p}):v_{j}=0~{\rm for}~j\in J\}.

Hence the boundary component

\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{e}}=\boldsymbol{e}+(V_{0}(\boldsymbol{e})\cap D)
is of the form

\Gamma_{\boldsymbol{e}}=\Gamma_{1}\times\cdots\times\Gamma_{p}\quad{\rm and}\quad\Gamma_{j}=\left\{\begin{matrix}\{\xi_{j}\}&(j\in J)\\
D_{j}&(j\notin J)\end{matrix}\right.

and

.

Example

Let

the nonzero tripotents in

are either minimal or maximal. Hence a boundary component

\Gamma_{c}
in
\partial D
is of the form
c+\mathbb{D}c^{*}
if
c
is a minimal tripotent
since
V_{0}(c)=\mathbb{C}c^{*}
, or of the form
\{c\}
if
c
is a maximal tripotent.

The following useful result has been shown in
[
6
, Lemma 3.4.11]
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
D
be a domain in a Banach space and
h:D\longrightarrow\overline{U}
a
holomorphic map, where
U
is a convex domain in some Banach space. Then the image
h(D)
is entirely contained in
one holomorphic component
\Gamma_{h(a)}\subset\overline{U}
with
a\in D
.

4
Horoballs

Horoballs, introduced in
[
11
]
, are a crucial tool in our approach to holomorphic dynamics on bounded symmetric domains.
They are convex domains. Given a sequence
(z_{k})
in
a bounded symmetric domain
D
norm converging to a boundary point
\xi\in\partial D
, the
horoball

H(\xi,s)
for

s>0
is defined in
[
11
, Theorem 4.4]
as

H(\xi,s)=\{x\in D:F(x)<s\}

(4.1)

where
F:D\longrightarrow[0,\infty)
is a continuous function given by

F(x)=\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1-\|z_{k}\|^{2}}{1-\|g_{-x}(z_{k})\|^{2}}=\limsup_{k\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1-\|z_{k}\|^{2}}{1-\|g_{-z_{k}}(x)\|^{2}}\qquad(x\in D)

in which
g_{-x}:D\longrightarrow D
is the transvection induced by
x
.
Convexity of
H(\xi,s)
has been shown in
[
11
, Theorem 4.4]
.

Remark
.

Actually, the horoball in (
4.1
) is denoted by
H(\xi,\frac{1}{s})
in
[
11
]
.
We will make suitable adjustments when making use of the relevant results in
[
11
]
.

Evidently, the definition of
H(\xi,s)
depends on the sequence
(z_{k})
. Nevertheless, by choosing a subsequence, we may
assume in later applications that
(z_{k})
satisfies an additional condition (
4.2
) below. Let

z_{k}=\alpha_{k1}e_{k1}+\cdots+\alpha_{kr}e_{kr}\qquad(\|z_{k}\|=\alpha_{k1}\geq\alpha_{k2}\geq\cdots\alpha_{kr}\geq 0)

be a spectral decomposition. Since
\overline{D}
is weakly compact and

0<1-\alpha_{k1}^{2}\leq 1-\alpha_{ki}^{2}\qquad(i=1,\ldots,r),

we may assume, by choosing a subsequence of
(z_{k})
, that the limits

\alpha_{i}=\lim_{k}\alpha_{ki},~\sigma_{i}=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1-\alpha_{k1}^{2}}{1-\alpha_{ki}^{2}}~~{\rm exist,~and~}(e_{ki})~{\rm weakly~converges~to~}e_{i}

(4.2)

for
i=1,\ldots,r
. We note that
\alpha_{1}=\lim_{k}\alpha_{k1}=\lim_{k}\|z_{k}\|=1=\sigma_{1}
.

Given a sequence
(z_{k})
converging to
\xi\in\partial D
and satisfying condition (
4.2
),
we will see in Theorem
4.3
that the limit

F_{\xi}(x)=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1-\|z_{k}\|^{2}}{1-\|g_{-x}(z_{k})\|^{2}}\qquad(x\in D)

(4.3)

exists. In view of this,
we will henceforth define, in place of (
4.1
),

H(\xi,s)=\{x\in D:F_{\xi}(x)<s\}

(4.4)

and call it a
horoball defined by the sequence
(z_{k})
,
with defining function
F_{\xi}
given in (
4.3
). In this definition, it is understood
that
(z_{k})
satisfies (
4.2
).
We note that

H(\xi,s)\subset H(\xi,r)\quad{\rm for}\quad 0<s<r.

We will show in Lemma
4.2
that
F_{\xi}(x)>0
for all
x\in D
.
In fact,

F_{\xi}
is related to the notion of a
horofunction
introduced by Gromov
[
3
,
15
]
for metric spaces
which are length spaces.
More precisely,
\frac{1}{2}\log F_{\xi}
is a horofunction on
D
.
We explain this connection below.

Let
(M,d)
be a metric space and fix a base point
b\in M
. For each
z\in M
,
we define a function
h_{z}:M\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
by

h_{z}(x)=d(x,z)-d(b,z)\qquad(x\in M).

Then
h_{z}
is a Lipschitz
1
-function, that is,

|h_{z}(x)-h_{z}(y)|\leq d(x,y)\qquad(x,y\in M).

A
horofunction
on
M
is a function
h:M\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
of the form

h(x)=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}h_{z_{k}}(x)\qquad(x\in M)

(4.5)

where
(z_{k})
is a sequence in
M
satisfying
\lim_{k}d(b,z_{k})=\infty
.
We note that
h
is also a Lipschitz
1
-function.

It may be useful to point out the fact (although not needed later)
that if
(M,d)
is locally compact, then one can always extract a pointwise convergent subsequence

(h_{z_{m}})
from a sequence
(h_{z_{k}})
with
\lim_{k}d(b,z_{k})=\infty
.
This can be seen as follows.

Let
Lip_{b}(M)
be the real Banach space of real-valued Lipschitz functions
f
on
M
satisfying
f(b)=0
, equipped with the norm

\|f\|_{lip}=\sup\left\{\frac{|f(x)-f(y)|}{d(x,y)}:x\neq y\right\}.

It is well-known that
Lip_{b}(M)
has a predual
\mathcal{F}(M)
. On the closed unit ball of
Lip_{b}(M)
, denoted by
Lip_{b}^{1}(M)
, the weak* topology,
the topology
\tau_{p}
of pointwise convergence as well as the topology
\tau_{0}
of uniform convergence on compact sets
in
M
all coincide,
and
Lip_{b}^{1}(M)
is compact in these topologies (cf.
[
19
, p. 642]
), which contains the horofunctions on
M
.

Let
(M,d)
be locally compact and equip the vector space
C(M)
of real continuous functions on
M

with the topology
\tau_{0}
of uniform convergence on compact sets. Then
Lip_{b}^{1}(M)

is a compact subset of
C(M)
. The dual
C(M)^{*}
of
C(M)
identifies with
the space of regular Borel measures
\mu
on
M
with compact support, in the duality

(f,\mu)\in C(M)\times C(M)^{*}\mapsto\int_{M}fd\mu.

It follows that
Lip_{b}^{1}(M)
is weakly compact in
C(M)

since a net
(f_{\alpha})

\tau_{0}
-converges to
f
in
Lip_{b}^{1}(M)

also converges in the weak topology in the above duality. Hence each sequence

(f_{k})
in
Lip_{b}^{1}(M)
admits a weakly convergent subsequence
(f_{m})
, which
is also pointwise convergent. In particular, the sequence
(h_{z_{k}})
above admits a
pointwise convergent subsequence
(h_{z_{m}})
.

Let
D
be a bounded symmetric domain realised as the open unit ball of a JB*-triple
V
. Then
D
is a metric space
with the Kobayashi distance
\kappa
as the metric.
We can define horofunctions on
(D,\kappa)
with the origin
0\in D

as a base point. In this setting, we show that
\frac{1}{2}\log F_{\xi}
is a horofunction on
(D,\kappa)
.

Let
(z_{k})
be a sequence in
D
, norm converging to a boundary point
\xi\in\partial D

and satisfying (
4.2
). Then

\lim_{k}\|z_{k}\|=1
and hence

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\kappa(0,z_{k})=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\tanh^{-1}\|z_{k}\|=\infty.

By definition, we have

\displaystyle h_{z_{k}}(x)=\kappa(x,{z_{k}})-\kappa(0,{z_{k}})=\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{1+\|g_{-x}({z_{k}})\|}{1-\|g_{-x}({z_{k}})\|}-\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{1+\|{z_{k}}\|}{1-\|{z_{k}}\|}

(4.6)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\log\left[\left(\frac{1+\|g_{-x}({z_{k}})\|}{1+\|{z_{k}}\|}\right)\left(\frac{1-\|{z_{k}}\|}{1-\|g_{-x}({z_{k}})\|}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\log\left[\left(\frac{1-\|{z_{k}}\|^{2}}{1-\|g_{-x}({z_{k}})\|^{2}}\right)\left(\frac{1+\|g_{-x}({z_{k}})\|}{1+\|{z_{k}}\|}\right)^{2}\right]\qquad(x\in D)

where
\lim_{k}\left(\frac{1+\|g_{-x}({z_{k}})\|}{1+\|{z_{k}}\|}\right)^{2}=1
by
Lemma
2.2
. Hence

\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}h_{z_{k}}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\log\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1-\|{z_{k}}\|^{2}}{1-\|g_{-x}({z_{k}})\|^{2}}=\frac{1}{2}\log F_{\xi}(x)\qquad(x\in D)

(4.7)

by (
4.3
) and
\frac{1}{2}\log F_{\xi}

is a horofunction on
D
. In particular, we have

|\log F_{\xi}(x)-\log F_{\xi}(y)|\leq 2\kappa(x,y)\qquad(x,y\in D)

(4.8)

as horofunctions are
1
-Lipschitz.

From now on and for later reference,
we will rename the horofunction
h
in (
4.5
) a
Gromov horofunction
, but instead
and more appropriately,
call
F_{\xi}
in (
4.3
) a
horofunction
, which defines the horoballs
H(\xi,s)
for
s>0
.

Example 4.1
.

We note that not all Gromov horofunctions on
(D,\kappa)
are of the form
\frac{1}{2}\log F_{\xi}
.
Let
D
be the open unit ball of a Hilbert space
V
with inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
,
and let
(z_{k})
be a sequence in
D
such that
\kappa(z_{k},0)\rightarrow\infty
as
k\rightarrow\infty
.
Then we have
\lim_{k}\|z_{k}\|=1
, but
(z_{k})
need not be norm convergent to a boundary point in
\partial D

if
\dim V=\infty
.

For instance, let
(e_{k})
be an orthonormal sequence in the Hilbert space
\ell_{2}
of square summable complex sequences and

(\alpha_{k})
a sequence in
(0,1)
converging to
1
, then the sequence
(\alpha_{k}e_{k})
in
D
converges weakly to
0
,
by Bessel’s inequality, whereas
\lim_{k}\|\alpha_{k}e_{k}\|=1
.

Nevertheless, weak compactness
of
\overline{D}
implies that
(z_{k})
admits a subsequence
(z_{j})
weakly convergent to
a\in\overline{D}
, but
\|a\|<1
can occur
as shown above.

We have, by (
2.10
),

\displaystyle\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}h_{z_{j}}(x)=\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{2}\log\left[\frac{|1-\langle x,z_{j}\rangle|^{2}}{1-\|x\|^{2}}\left(\frac{1+\|g_{-x}(z_{j})\|}{1+\|z_{j}\|}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{|1-\langle x,a\rangle|^{2}}{1-\|x\|^{2}}\qquad(x\in D).

If, for instance,
(z_{k})
is the sequence
(\alpha_{k}e_{k})\in\ell_{2}
given above, then
a=0
and hence

\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}h_{z_{j}}(x)=\frac{1}{2}\log\left(\frac{1}{1-\|x\|^{2}}\right)

which is a Gromov horofunction.

If, however,
(z_{k})
is norm convergent, then
\|a\|=1
and the horofunction
F_{a}
is given by

F_{a}(x)=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1-\|z_{k}\|^{2}}{1-\|g_{-x}(z_{k})\|^{2}}=\frac{|1-\langle x,a\rangle|^{2}}{1-\|x\|^{2}}\qquad(z\in D)

from (
2.10
).

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
D
be a bounded symmetric domain realised as the open unit ball of a JB*-triple
V
.
Then the horofunction
F_{\xi}(x)
in (
4.3
) satisfies

F_{\xi}(0)=1\quad{\rm and}\quad\frac{1-\|x\|}{1+\|x\|}\leq F_{\xi}(x)\leq\frac{1+\|x\|}{1-\|x\|}\qquad(x\in D).

(4.9)

Proof.

We have

F_{\xi}(0)=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1-\|z_{k}\|^{2}}{1-\|g_{0}(z_{k})\|^{2}}=\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1-\|z_{k}\|^{2}}{1-\|z_{k}\|^{2}}=1.

For the inequalities,
it suffices to prove, for each
z\in D
, we have

\frac{1-\|x\|}{1+\|x\|}\leq\frac{1-\|z\|^{2}}{1-\|g_{-x}(z)\|^{2}}\leq\frac{1+\|x\|}{1-\|x\|}\qquad(x\in D).

The last inequality has already been shown in
[
11
, Lemma 4.1]
.
We deduce the first inequality using (
2.4
) and (
2.9
) as follows.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-\|z\|^{2}}=\frac{1}{1-\|g_{x}(g_{-x}(z))\|^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|B(g_{-x}(z),g_{-x}(z))^{-1/2}B(g_{-x}(z),-x)B(-x,-x)^{-1/2}\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-\|g_{-x}(z)\|^{2}}(1+\|g_{-x}(z)\|\|x\|)^{2}(1-\|x\|^{2})^{-1}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1-\|g_{-x}(z)\|^{2}}(1+\|x\|)^{2}(1-\|x\|^{2})^{-1}\leq\frac{1+\|x\|}{(1-\|g_{-x}(z)\|^{2})(1-\|x\|)}.

∎

Evidently, (
4.9
) implies
F_{\xi}(x)>0
for all
x\in D
. It also implies

\bigcap_{s>0}H(\xi,s)=\emptyset
for the horoballs
H(\xi,s)
defined by
F_{\xi}
in (
4.4
),
since any
x\in\bigcap_{s>0}H(\xi,s)
would lead to the contradiction that
\|x\|<1
as well as

(1-\|x\|)(1+\|x\|)^{-1}\leq F_{\xi}(x)<s
for all
s>0
. On the other hand, we have
D=\bigcup_{s>0}H(\xi,s)

by (
4.3
).

We now establish an explicit formula for
F_{\xi}
in (
4.3
) for finite-rank domains
D
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
D
be a bounded symmetric domain of finite rank
r
, realised as the open unit
ball of a JB*-triple
V
. Then the horofunction
F_{\xi}
in (
4.3
), defined by a sequence
(z_{k})
in
D
converging to a boundary point
\xi\in\partial D
and satisfying (
4.2
), can be expressed as

F_{\xi}(x)=\left\|\sum_{1\leq i\leq j\leq p}\rho_{i}\rho_{j}B(x,x)^{-1/2}B(x,\xi)P_{ij}\right\|\qquad(x\in D)

(4.10)

for some
p\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
and
\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{p}\in[0,1]
,
where
\rho_{1}=1

and
\xi
has a spectral decomposition

\xi=\alpha_{1}e_{1}+\alpha_{2}e_{2}+\cdots\alpha_{p}e_{p}\qquad(1=\alpha_{1}\geq\alpha_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\alpha_{p}>0)

with joint Peirce projections
P_{ij}:V\longrightarrow V
induced
by the minimal tripotents
e_{1},\ldots,e_{p}
.

Proof.

Let
F_{\xi}
be defined by
(z_{k})
satisfying (
4.2
), with
a spectral decomposition

z_{k}=\alpha_{k1}e_{k1}+\cdots+\alpha_{kr}e_{kr}\qquad(\|z_{k}\|=\alpha_{k1}\geq\cdots\geq\alpha_{kr}\geq 0).

By Lemma
2.12
,
there exists
p\in\{1,\ldots,r\}
such that

(i)

\alpha_{i}>0
and
(e_{ki})_{k}
norm converges to
e_{i}
for each
i=1,\ldots,p
,

(ii)

\alpha_{i}=0
for
i>p
,

(iii)

e_{1},\ldots,e_{p}
are mutually orthogonal minimal tripotents

and

\xi=\lim_{k}z_{k}=\alpha_{1}e_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{p}e_{p}\qquad(\alpha_{1}=1).

By (
2.9
), we have

\frac{1-\|z_{k}\|^{2}}{1-\|g_{-x}(z_{k})\|^{2}}=(1-\|z_{k}\|^{2})\|B(x,x)^{-1/2}B(x,z_{k})B(z_{k},z_{k})^{-1/2}\|

where

B(z_{k},z_{k})^{-1/2}=\sum_{0\leq i\leq j\leq r}(1-\alpha_{ki}^{2})^{-1/2}(1-\alpha_{kj}^{2})^{-1/2}P_{ij}^{k}\qquad(\alpha_{{}_{k0}}=0)

and
P_{ij}^{k}
is the joint Peirce projection
P_{ij}(e_{k1},\ldots,e_{kr})
.

For
1\leq i\leq j\leq p
, we have
P_{ij}(e_{k1},\ldots,e_{kp})=P_{ij}^{k}
by (
2.22
).
By
[
11
, Remark 5.9]
, we have the following norm
convergence

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}P^{k}_{ij}=P_{ij}(e_{1},\ldots,e_{p}).

Since
(z_{k})
satisfies (
4.2
), the following limit

\rho_{i}=\lim_{k}\sqrt{\frac{1-\alpha_{k1}^{2}}{1-\alpha_{ki}^{2}}}=\sqrt{\sigma}_{i}\in[0,1]

(4.11)

exists for
i=0,1,\ldots,r
, where
\rho_{1}=1
and
\rho_{0}=\rho_{i}=0
for
\alpha_{i}<1
.
In particular,
\|P_{ij}^{k}\|\leq 1
implies

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\left\|\sqrt{\frac{1-\alpha_{k1}^{2}}{1-\alpha_{ki}^{2}}}\sqrt{\frac{1-\alpha_{k1}^{2}}{1-\alpha_{kj}^{2}}}P_{ij}^{k}\right\|\leq\rho_{i}\rho_{j}=0

for
p<i\leq j\leq r
, and for
i=0
.
It follows that

\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}(1-\|z_{k}\|^{2})B(z_{k},z_{k})^{-1/2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{0\leq i\leq j\leq r}\sqrt{\frac{1-\alpha_{k1}^{2}}{1-\alpha_{ki}^{2}}}\sqrt{\frac{1-\alpha_{k1}^{2}}{1-\alpha_{kj}^{2}}}\,P_{ij}^{k}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq i\leq j\leq p}\rho_{i}\rho_{j}P_{ij}(e_{1},\ldots,e_{p}).

Therefore we have

\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1-\|z_{k}\|^{2}}{1-\|g_{-x}(z_{k})\|^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}(1-\|z_{k}\|^{2})\|B(x,x)^{-1/2}B(x,z_{k})B(z_{k},z_{k})^{-1/2}\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{1\leq i\leq j\leq p}\rho_{i}\rho_{j}B(x,x)^{-1/2}B(x,\xi)P_{ij}(e_{1},\ldots,e_{p})\right\|\quad(x\in D).

and hence

F_{\xi}(x)=\left\|\sum_{1\leq i\leq j\leq p}\rho_{i}\rho_{j}B(x,x)^{-1/2}B(x,\xi)P_{ij}\right\|\quad(P_{ij}=P_{ij}(e_{1},\ldots,e_{p})).

∎

Remark 4.4

Corollary 4.5
.

The function

Example 4.6
.

For later application, we need a more explicit description of the horoballs

.
By

with defining horofunction

Theorem 4.11
.

Let

be a bounded symmetric domain of rank

, realised as the open
unit ball of a JB*-triple
V
. Let
(z_{k})
be a sequence in
D

which defines the horoballs

Then the intersection

. More explicitly,

\bigcap_{s>0}\overline{H}(\xi,s)=c+(V_{0}(c)\cap\overline{D})=\overline{\Gamma}_{c}

where

is the horocentre of

is the Peirce

Proof.

Let
\xi=\alpha_{1}e_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{p}e_{p}
, with
\alpha_{p}>0

, as in Theorem

. By (

\overline{H}(\xi,s)=\sum_{j=1}^{q}\frac{\sigma_{j}}{\sigma_{j}+s}e_{j}+B\left(\sum_{j=1}^{q}\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_{j}}{\sigma_{j}+s}}e_{j},\,\sum_{j=1}^{q}\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_{j}}{\sigma_{j}+s}}e_{j}\right)^{1/2}(\overline{D})

Let

we have

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\,B\hskip-2.168pt\left(\sum_{j=1}^{q}\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_{j}}{\sigma_{j}+1/k}}e_{j},\,\sum_{j=1}^{q}\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_{j}}{\sigma_{j}+1/k}}e_{j}\right)^{1/2}=B(c,c)^{1/2}=P_{0}(c).

.

Conversely, let

. Then there is a point

such that

For

, let

y_{n}=\sum_{j=1}^{q}\frac{\sigma_{j}}{\sigma_{j}+1/n}e_{j}+B\hskip-2.168pt\left(\sum_{j=1}^{q}\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_{j}}{\sigma_{j}+1/n}}e_{j},\,\sum_{j=1}^{q}\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_{j}}{\sigma_{j}+1/n}}e_{j}\right)^{1/2}(z)\in\overline{H}(\xi,1/n).

∎

Remark 4.12

Given a sequence

In the case of Hilbert balls, we have a simpler result.

Theorem 4.13
.

Let
D
be a Hilbert ball. Then each horoball

in Theorem

\overline{H}(\xi,s)\cap\partial D=\{\xi\}.

Proof.

By a remark before Theorem

, we have
\xi\in\overline{H}(\xi,s)

By Example

, we have

F_{\xi}(x)=\frac{|1-\langle x,\xi\rangle|^{2}}{1-\|x\|^{2}}\qquad(x\in D)

5
Denjoy-Wolff Theorem

In this final section, we prove a Denjoy-Wolff theorem for a fixed-point free compact holomorphic map
f:D\longrightarrow D

on a bounded symmetric domain
D
of finite rank, including the case of all fixed-point free
holomorphic self-maps on finite dimensional bounded symmetric domains. As before, we identify
D
with the open unit ball
of a JB*-triple.

In the language of dynamics,
which studies the limit set

\omega(a)
of each point
a\in D
,
consisting of limit points of the orbit
(f^{n}(a))
:

\omega(a)=\bigcap_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\overline{\bigcup_{k\geq n}\{f^{k}(a)\}},

the Denjoy-Wolff theorem
reveals a striking phenomenon that
all
limit sets
\bigcup_{a\in D}\omega(a)

accumulate in one single face
\overline{\Gamma}

of
\overline{D}
.

Indeed, given a limit point
\xi=\lim_{k}f^{n_{k}}(a)
in
\omega(a)
, we can find a locally uniformly convergent
subsequence
(f^{m_{k}})
of
(f^{n_{k}})
by Lemma
5.1
below. Hence
\xi=\ell(a)
, where

\ell
is the subsequential limit
\ell=\lim_{k}f^{m_{k}}
and the Denjoy-Wolff theorem says
\ell(D)\subset\overline{\Gamma}
.

We begin with some lemmas.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
f:D\longrightarrow D
be a compact holomorphic map. Then every subsequence
of the iterates
(f^{n})
contains a locally uniformly convergent subsequence.

Proof.

This has been proved in
[
9
, Lemma 1, Remark 1]
.
∎

In contrast, we note that a sequence of holomorphic self-maps on an infinite dimensional domain, even on a
separable Hilbert ball, need not
have any locally uniformly convergent subsequence
[
22
, Example 3.1]
.

Definition 5.2
.

A locally uniform subsequential limit
\ell=\lim_{k}f^{n_{k}}
of the iterates
(f^{n})

is called a
limit function
of
(f^{n})
.

If a compact holomorphic map
f:D\longrightarrow D
on a bounded symmetric domain
D
has no fixed-point,
then the image
\ell(D)
of each limit function
\ell=\lim_{k}f^{n_{k}}

must lie entirely in the boundary
\partial D
. This is a consequence of
the following result proved in
[
23
]
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
D
be the open unit ball of a complex Banach space and
f:D\longrightarrow D

a compact holomorphic map. Then
f
is fixed-point free if and only if

\sup_{k}\|f^{n_{k}}(a)\|=1
for each subsequence
(f^{n_{k}})
of the iterates
(f^{n})
and each
a\in D
.

Given a fixed-point free compact holomorphic self-map
f
on a bounded symmetric domain
D
, we will make use of
the existence of a family of
f
-invariant horoballs
H(\xi,s)
in
D
, constructed in
[
11
, Theorem 4.4]

and stated below. This fact is a generalisation of a boundary version of the Schwarz lemma for
\mathbb{D}
, proved by
Wolff
[
30
]
.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
f
be a fixed-point free compact holomorphic self-map on a bounded symmetric domain

D
of any rank in
\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
.
Then there is a sequence
(z_{k})
in
D
converging
to a boundary point
\zeta\in\partial D
such that the horoballs

H(\zeta,s)
defined by
(z_{k})
are
f
-invariant, that is,
f(H(\zeta,s))\subset H(\zeta,s)
,
for all
s>0
.

Further, if
D
is a Hilbert ball, the compactness assumption on
f
can be removed.

Proof.

For completeness,
we show the construction of
(z_{k})
and refer to
[
11
, Theorem 4.4]

for other details.

Choose an increasing sequence
(\beta_{k})
in
(0,1)
with
limit
1
. Then
\beta_{k}f
maps
D
strictly inside itself and
the fixed-point theorem of Earle and Hamilton
[
13
]
implies the existence of some
z_{k}\in D

such that
\beta_{k}f(z_{k})=z_{k}
. Note that
z_{k}\neq 0
. Since
\overline{f(D)}
is compact, we may assume, by choosing
a subsequence if necessary, that
(z_{k})
converges to a point
\zeta\in\overline{D}
. Since
f
has no fixed point in
D
, the point

\zeta
must lie in the boundary
\partial D
. Choosing a subsequence, we may assume that

(z_{k})
satisfies (
4.2
).

The
f
-invariance of the horoballs
H(\zeta,s)
defined by
(z_{k})

is a consequence of
f=\lim_{k}\beta_{k}f
and the Schwarz-Pick lemma for
D

[
6
, Lemma 3.5.18]
:

\|g_{-z_{k}}(\beta_{k}f(x))\|=\|g_{-\beta_{k}f(z_{k})}(\beta_{k}f(x))\leq\|g_{-z_{k}}(x)\|\qquad(x\in H(\zeta,s)).

For a Hilbert ball
D
, the compactness assumption of
\overline{f(D)}
can be dropped.
Indeed, by weak compactness of
\overline{D}
and choosing a subsequence in this case, we may assume
that
(z_{k})
converges
weakly
to some
\xi\in\overline{D}
. We show
\|\xi\|=1
.
Otherwise
\xi\in D

and the Schwarz-Pick lemma implies
\|g_{-f(\xi)}(f(z_{k}))\|\leq\|g_{-\xi}(z_{k})\|
.

Using (
2.10
) and substituting
f(z_{k})=\beta_{k}^{-1}z_{k}
, one deduces

\frac{|1-\langle\beta_{k}^{-1}z_{k},f(\xi)\rangle|^{2}}{1-\|f(\xi)\|^{2}}\leq\frac{1-\beta_{k}^{-2}\|z_{k}\|^{2}}{1-\|z_{k}\|^{2}}\frac{|1-\langle z_{k},\xi\rangle|^{2}}{1-\|\xi\|^{2}}.

Since
\frac{1-\beta_{k}^{-2}\|z_{k}\|^{2}}{1-\|z_{k}\|^{2}}\leq 1
, letting
k\rightarrow\infty
gives

\frac{1}{1-\|g_{-f(\xi)}(\xi)\|^{2}}\leq 1

and hence
\|g_{-f(\xi)}(\xi)\|=0
. This implies

f(\xi)=\xi
, contradicting the non-existence of a fixed-point in
D
. It follows that

(z_{k})
actually
norm
converges to

\ell(a)

is a maximal tripotent.

If
\dim D<\infty
, the map
f
is compact and hence
\ell(D)\subset\partial D
, by Lemma
5.3
.
∎

Remark
.

The preceding proof simplifies considerably the one given by Chu and Mellon in
[
9
, Theorem]

for
compact
holomorphic maps.

Appendix

In the exceptional case of the bidisc
D=\mathbb{D}^{2}
, Theorem
5.5
,
without the condition on
\Sigma^{*}(\mathbb{D}^{2})
,
has been proved by Hervé

[
18
]
with protracted arguments specific for the bidisc
\mathbb{D}^{2}
, via
the Cayley transform of
\mathbb{D}
onto the right-half complex plane and Harnack’s theorem
for harmonic functions.
We simplify Hervé’s proof (still dependent on
\mathbb{D}^{2}
) below, using horofunctions and invariant horoballs.

Let

\pi_{1}:(x,y)\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}^{2}\mapsto x\in\overline{\mathbb{D}},\quad\pi_{2}:(x,y)\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}^{2}\mapsto y\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}

be the coordinate maps.

Given a fixed-point free
holomorphic self-map
f
on
\mathbb{D}^{2}
, Hervé first observed in
[
18
,
7^{0}
]
that, by a simple reduction,
one need only consider the following three mutually exclusive cases (in our notation):

(a)

There is a boundary point
\zeta=(e^{i\alpha},0)
such that for each

(x,y)\in\mathbb{D}^{2}
, the image
f(x,y)
is contained in a horoball

H(\zeta,s_{x})=\mathbb{H}(e^{i\alpha},s_{x})\times\mathbb{D}\qquad(s_{x}>0)

with
x\in\partial\mathbb{H}(e^{i\alpha},s_{x})
,
and there is holomorphic function
\eta:\mathbb{D}\longrightarrow\mathbb{D}

such that
\pi_{2}f(x,y)=y
if and only if
y=\eta(x)
.

(b)

There is a boundary point
\zeta=(e^{i\alpha},e^{i\beta})
such that for each

(x,y)\in\mathbb{D}^{2}
, the image
f(x,y)
is contained in

\mathbb{H}(e^{i\alpha},s_{x})\times\mathbb{H}(e^{i\beta},s_{y})\qquad(s_{x},s_{y}>0)

with
(x,y)\in\partial\mathbb{H}(e^{i\alpha},s_{x})\times\partial\mathbb{H}(e^{i\beta},s_{y})
.

(c)

There are two holomorphic functions
\nu,\eta:\mathbb{D}\longrightarrow\mathbb{D}
such that

\pi_{1}f(x,y)=x\Leftrightarrow x=\nu(y),\quad{\rm and}\quad\pi_{2}f(x,y)=y\Leftrightarrow y=\eta(x).

In case (b), one can follow the arguments in
[
18
,
12^{0}
]
to establish that,
given
(x,y)\in\mathbb{D}^{2}
, either

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\pi_{1}f^{n}(x,y)=e^{i\alpha}\quad{\rm or}\quad\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\pi_{2}f^{n}(x,y)=e^{i\beta}.

It follows that, in the former case,
for each limit function
\ell=\lim_{m}f^{m}
and
a\in\mathbb{D}^{2}
,
we have

\ell(a)=e_{1}+\alpha e_{2}\quad{\rm with}\quad 0\leq\alpha\leq 1,\quad e_{1}=(e^{i\alpha},0),e_{2}=(0,e^{i\theta})

and
\ell(\mathbb{D}^{2})\subset\overline{\Gamma}_{(e^{i\alpha},0)}
.

Likewise, in the latter case, we have

\ell(\mathbb{D}^{2})\subset\overline{\Gamma}_{(0,e^{i\beta})}

for all limit functions
\ell
.

We deal with cases (a) and (c) in the following proof.

Theorem
.

Let
f:\mathbb{D}^{2}\longrightarrow\mathbb{D}^{2}
be a fixed-point free holomorphic map.
Then there is a holomorphic boundary component
\Gamma
in the boundary
\partial\mathbb{D}^{2}
such that

\ell(\mathbb{D}^{2})\subset\overline{\Gamma}
for all limit functions
\ell=\lim_{k}f^{n_{k}}
of the iterates

(f^{n})
.

Proof.

It suffices to consider cases (a) and (c) above. For this, one need only consider
the
f
-invariant horoballs in
\mathbb{D}^{2}

of the form

H(c,s)=\mathbb{H}(e^{i\alpha},s)\times\mathbb{D}

(6.1)

or of the form

H(c,s)=\mathbb{H}(e^{i\alpha},s)\times\mathbb{H}(e^{i\beta},s/\sigma)\qquad(0<\sigma\leq 1)

(6.2)

which are defined by a sequence
(z_{k})
converging to
c
via Lemma
5.4
, where we assume

c=(e^{i\alpha},0)
in (
6.1
) without loss of generality.

Indeed, if
\lim_{k}z_{k}=(0,e^{i\beta})

so that
H(c,s)=\mathbb{D}\times\mathbb{H}(e^{i\beta},s)
,
we can make use of the transformation

T:(x,y)\in\mathbb{D}^{2}\mapsto(y,x)\in\mathbb{D}^{2}
and consider the transformed sequence
(Tz_{k})

which converges to
(e^{i\beta},0)
, with the holomorphic map
\widetilde{f}=TfT
and the iterates
\widetilde{f}^{n}=Tf^{n}T
.

Let
0<s<1
and
a\in H(c,s)
in (
6.1
) or (
6.2
).
In cases (a) and (c), the Lemma in
[
18
, p. 11]
reveals a dichotomy in that there exist
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}>0
, depending on
a
,
such that for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
the following inequalities cannot hold simultaneously:

\frac{1-|\pi_{2}f^{n}(a)|}{1-|\pi_{1}f^{n}(a)|}<\alpha_{1}\quad{\rm and}\quad\frac{1-|\pi_{2}f^{n+1}(a)|}{1-|\pi_{2}f^{n}(a)|}<1+\alpha_{2}.

(6.3)

Making use of this, we first show that either

\lim_{n}|\pi_{1}f^{n}(a)|=1

(6.4)

or there is a convergent subsequence
(f^{m})
of the iterates
(f^{n})
such that

\lim_{m}|\pi_{1}f^{m}(a)|=\lim_{m}|\pi_{2}f^{m}(a)|=1.

(6.5)

Observe that
\|f^{n}(a)\|=\max(|\pi_{1}f^{n}(a)|,|\pi_{2}f^{n}(a)|)
and
\lim_{n}\|f^{n}(a)\|=1
by Lemma
5.3
.
If from some
n
onwards,
|\pi_{1}f^{n}(a)|>|\pi_{2}f^{n}(a)|
, then we have

\lim_{n}|\pi_{1}f^{n}(a)|=\lim_{n}\|f^{n}(a)\|=1

and we are done. Otherwise, there is a subsequence
(f^{m})
of
(f^{n})
such that
|\pi_{1}f^{m}(a)|\leq|\pi_{2}f^{m}(a)|

and we may assume that
(f^{m})
is convergent by choosing a subsequence if necessary. Hence

\lim_{m}|\pi_{2}f^{m}(a)|=\lim_{m}\|f^{m}(a)\|=1.

Let
h=\lim_{m}f^{m}
.
If we also have
\lim_{m}|\pi_{1}f^{m}(a)|=1
, then we are done. Otherwise,

\pi_{1}h(a)=\lim_{m}\pi_{1}f^{m}(a)\in\mathbb{D}

(6.6)

and since
\|h(a)\|=1
, we would have a spectral decomposition

h(a)=u_{1}+\gamma u_{2}\in\Gamma_{u_{1}}\quad\mbox{where $u_{1}=(0,e^{i\theta_{1}})$,
$\theta_{1}\in\mathbb{R}$ and $0\leq\gamma<1$}.

It follows that
h(\mathbb{D}^{2})\subset\Gamma_{u_{1}}
. In particular,
h(f(a))\in\Gamma_{u_{1}}
implies

\lim_{m}|\pi_{1}f^{m+1}(a)|=|\pi_{1}h(f(a))|<1
. Hence
\|h(f(a))\|=1
implies
\lim_{m}|\pi_{2}f^{m+1}(a)|=|\pi_{2}(h(f(a))|=1

and from some
m
onwards, we have
|\pi_{1}f^{m+1}(a)|<|\pi_{2}f^{m+1}(a)|
as well as

|\pi_{1}f^{m}(a)|<|\pi_{2}f^{m}(a)|
.

Since
\lim_{n}\|f^{n}(a)\|=1
, the sequence
(\|f^{n}(a)\|)
is increasing from some
n
onwards. Therefore, from some

m
onwards, we have

|\pi_{2}f^{m+1}(a)|=\|f^{m+1}(a)\|\geq\|f^{m}(a)\|=|\pi_{2}f^{m}(a)|.

Now Hervé’s dichotomy gives the contradiction that, from some
m
onwards,

\frac{1-|\pi_{2}f^{m}(a)|}{1-|\pi_{1}f^{m}(a)|}<\alpha_{1}\quad{\rm and}\quad 1\geq\frac{1-|\pi_{2}f^{m+1}(a)|}{1-|\pi_{2}f^{m}(a)|}\geq 1+\alpha_{2}>1

where
\lim_{m}\frac{1-|\pi_{2}f^{m}(a)|}{1-|\pi_{1}f^{m}(a)|}=0
.
Hence (
6.6
) cannot occur and the claim is proved.

Now let
\ell=\lim_{k}f^{n_{k}}
be a limit function with a spectral decomposition

\ell(a)=\lim_{k}f^{n_{k}}(a)=e^{\prime}_{1}+\alpha_{2}e^{\prime}_{2}\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}^{2}\qquad(0\leq\alpha_{2}\leq 1).

Then
f^{n_{k}}(a)\in H(c,s)
implies

\left\|\sum_{1\leq i^{\prime}\leq j^{\prime}\leq d_{c}}\rho_{i^{\prime}}\rho_{j^{\prime}}P_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}B(c,f^{n_{k}}a))B(f^{n_{k}}(a),f^{n_{k}}(a))^{-1/2}\right\|=F_{c}(f^{n_{k}}(a))<s.

where
d_{c}=1
for
H(c,s)
in (
6.1
), and
d_{c}=2
in (
6.2
).

Write
c_{1}=(e^{i\alpha},0)
and
c_{2}=(0,e^{i\beta})
. In the special case of the bidisc
\mathbb{D}^{2}
, we must have
either
c_{1}{\,\vbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.4pt\hbox{\vrule width=0.4pt,height=4.0pt\kern 4.0pt\vrule width=0.4pt}\hrule height=0.4pt}}\,}\,e_{1}^{\prime}\neq 0
or
c_{2}{\,\vbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.4pt\hbox{\vrule width=0.4pt,height=4.0pt\kern 4.0pt\vrule width=0.4pt}\hrule height=0.4pt}}\,}\,e_{1}^{\prime}\neq 0
, which implies either
c_{1}=e^{i\theta_{1}}e_{1}^{\prime}
or

c_{2}=e^{i\theta_{2}}e_{1}^{\prime}
for some
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
, by Lemma
2.4
.

As in the the derivation of (
5.10
) and (
5.12
) in Remark
5.6
, we have, for
c_{j}=e^{i\theta_{j}}e^{\prime}_{1}

(j=1, 2),

\displaystyle\|P_{2}(c_{j})B(c,\,\ell(a))(e^{\prime}_{1})\|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\,\|P_{2}(c_{j})B(c,\,f^{n_{k}}(a))(e^{\prime}_{n_{k},1})\|

(6.7)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\rho_{j}^{-2}(1-\alpha_{n_{k},1}^{2})s=0

where
f^{n_{k}}(a)=\alpha_{n_{k},1}e^{\prime}_{n_{k},1}+\alpha_{n_{k},2}e^{\prime}_{n_{k},2}\rightarrow e^{\prime}_{1}+\alpha_{2}e^{\prime}_{2}
as

.
This gives

({\rm Re}\,e^{i\theta_{j}}-|e^{i\theta_{j}}|^{2})c_{j}=\{c_{j},\{c_{j},(e^{\prime}_{1})^{1},(e^{\prime}_{1})^{1}\},c_{j}\}=0

where

because
\mathbb{D}^{2}
is abelian. Hence
e^{i\theta_{j}}=1
and

.

It follows that, for the case
\lim_{n}|\pi_{1}f^{n}(a)|=1
in (
6.4
), we have
c_{1}{\,\vbox{\vbox{\hrule height=0.4pt\hbox{\vrule width=0.4pt,height=4.0pt\kern 4.0pt\vrule width=0.4pt}\hrule height=0.4pt}}\,}\,e^{\prime}_{1}\neq 0
which
gives
c_{1}=e^{\prime}_{1}
and

\ell(\mathbb{D}^{2})\subset\overline{\Gamma}_{(e^{i\alpha},0)}

for each limit function
\ell=\lim_{k}f^{n_{k}}
.

Finally, consider the case (
6.5
) in which
\lim_{m}|\pi_{1}f^{m}(a)|=\lim_{m}|\pi_{2}f^{m}(a)|=1
. We can write

\displaystyle h(a)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(e^{i\theta_{1}},e^{i\theta_{2}})=e_{1}+e_{2}\quad{\rm with}\quad e_{1}=(e^{i\theta_{1}},0),~e_{2}=(0,e^{i\theta_{2}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{m}f^{m}(a)=\lim_{m}(\alpha_{m1}e_{m1}+\alpha_{m2}e_{m2}).

Since
e_{j}=\lim_{m}e_{mj}
for
j=1,2
and
\mathbb{D}^{2}
is abelian, we have
e_{mj}=e^{i\theta_{mj}}e_{j}

with
\theta_{mj}\in\mathbb{R}
from some
m
onwards, by Lemma
2.4
,
and
\lim_{m}\alpha_{mj}e^{\theta_{mj}}=1
.

By Corollary
4.5
, the horofunction
F_{c}
for the horoballs in (
6.1
) can be computed
via a sequence
(\beta_{k1}c_{1})
,
where
c_{1}=(e^{i\alpha},0)
and
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\beta_{k1}=1
, whereas the horofucntion
F_{c}

for (
6.2
) can be computed by a sequence
(\beta_{k1}c_{1}+\beta_{k2}c_{2})
with

c_{1}=(e^{i\alpha},0)
,
c_{2}=(0,e^{i\beta})
and
\lim_{k}\beta_{kj}=1
for

We show both horofunctions satisfy

\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}F_{c}(f^{m}(a))=0.

(6.8)

To show this, we first observe that, by
making use of (
6.7
) as before, we have
e_{1}=c_{1}=(e^{i\alpha},0)
in (
6.1
)
whereas in case (
6.2
), we have
e_{1}=c_{1}
and
e_{2}=c_{2}=(0,e^{i\beta})

Hence the horofunction in case (
6.1
) is given by

\displaystyle F_{c}(f^{m}(a))=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1-\beta_{k1}^{2}}{1-\|g_{-\beta_{k1}c_{1}}(\alpha_{m_{1}}e^{i\theta_{m1}}e_{1}+\alpha_{m2}e^{i\theta_{m2}}e_{2})\|^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1-\beta_{k1}^{2}}{1-\|\psi_{-\beta_{k1}}(\alpha_{m_{1}}e^{i\theta_{m1}})c_{1}+\alpha_{m2}e^{i\theta_{m2}}c_{2}\|^{2}}\quad({\rm Lemma}~\ref{k=0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1-\beta_{k1}^{2}}{1-|\psi_{-\beta_{k1}}(\alpha_{m_{1}}e^{i\theta_{m1}})|^{2}}=\frac{|1-\alpha_{m_{1}}e^{i\theta_{m1}}|^{2}}{1-|\alpha_{m_{1}}e^{i\theta_{m1}}|^{2}}\quad(\rm{by}~\ref{hh})

where
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}|\psi_{-\beta_{k1}}(\alpha_{m_{1}}e^{i\theta_{m1}})|=1>|\alpha_{m2}e^{i\theta_{m2}}|

In case (

\displaystyle F_{c}(f^{m}(a))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1-\beta_{k1}^{2}}{1-\|g_{-(\beta_{k1}c_{1}+\beta_{k2}c_{2})}(\alpha_{m1}e^{i\theta_{m1}}e_{1}+\alpha_{m2}e^{i\theta_{m2}}e_{2})\|^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1-\beta_{k1}^{2}}{1-\|\psi_{-\beta_{k1}}(\alpha_{m1}e^{i\theta_{m1}})c_{1}+\psi_{-\beta_{k2}}(\alpha_{m2}e^{i\theta_{m2}})c_{2}\|^{2}}

\displaystyle\max(\frac{|1-\alpha_{m1}e^{i\theta_{m1}}|^{2}}{1-|\alpha_{m1}e^{i\theta_{m1}}|^{2}},\frac{|1-\alpha_{m2}e^{i\theta_{m2}}|^{2}}{1-|\alpha_{m2}e^{i\theta_{m2}}|^{2}}).

It follows that
\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}F_{c}(f^{m}(a))=0
in both cases since for

\frac{|1-z|^{2}}{1-|z|^{2}}=\frac{1-2r\cos\theta+r^{2}}{1-r^{2}}=\frac{1}{{\rm Re}\,\left(\frac{1+z}{1-z}\right)}\quad{\rm and}\quad\lim_{z\rightarrow 1}\frac{|1-z|^{2}}{1-|z|^{2}}=0.

We conclude by showing that, in each case of (

) and (

\ell(\mathbb{D}^{2})\subset\overline{\Gamma}_{c}

for every limit function

.
As

\ell(a)\in\bigcap_{s>0}\overline{H}(c,s)=\overline{\Gamma}_{c}

and so
\ell(\mathbb{D}^{2})\subset\overline{\Gamma}_{c}
.
∎
