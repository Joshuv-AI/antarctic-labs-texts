Identification of poultry commensal bacteria with the ability to inhibit the growth of Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium in co-culture
Authors: Zhixuan Feng, Valérie Dagenais, Valérie Leclerc, Clara Pigozzo, Jennifer Ronholm
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe widespread increase of antibiotic resistant bacteria (ARB) in agriculture has led to an interest in improving the competitive exclusion ability of the microbiota in livestock through the application of probiotics. In this study, we examined a collection of gram-negative bacteria isolated from healthy chickens for their ability to inhibit the growth of Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium.MethodsTwenty-five bacteria isolated from healthy chickens were mixed at a 1:1 or a 10:1 ratio with S. enterica ser. Typhimurium and grown overnight in co-culture on a solid media that contained bile salt. The S. enterica in each co-culture was quantified using on Salmonella Shigella Agar (SSA) to determine which isolates can inhibit the growth of S. enterica in vitro. Isolates that showed inhibitory action against S. enterica ser. Typhimurium were further analyzed by whole genome sequencing (WGS) to identify potential mechanisms. The WGS analysis, included using a variety of tools to identify potential mechanisms of S. enterica ser. Typhimurium antagonism.ResultThree bacterial isolates: Alcaligenes faecalis Ae-14, Citrobacter braakii J-D0-20, and Escherichia ruysiae J-D0-44 significantly reduced the growth of S. enterica ser. Typhimurium in co-culture. Growth inhibition was most pronounced during the exponential growth phase. Sequence analysis revealed that the potential mechanisms of S. enterica ser. Typhimurium inhibition by C. braakii J-D0-20 and E.ruysiae J-D0-44 could be through T6SS-mediated competition, bacteriocin (carocin D) production, having a similar quorum sensing system, or by competition for similar nutrients. The potential antagonistic mechanisms for A. faecalis Ae-14 were unclear.ConclusionSome bacteria, found naturally in the fecal material of healthy chickens, are capable inhibiting the growth of S. enterica ser. Typhimurium in co-culture and should be further investigated as potential probiotic prophylactics to provide protection against S. enterica ser. Typhimurium infections.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04482-7.

Background

Antibiotics are widely used in livestock production systems to increase animal productivity [1], as well as to treat and prevent bacterial infections [2–4]. It is estimated that 73% of the antibiotics produced globally are used in food production [5, 6]. The volume of antibiotics used in agriculture is increasing annually as the demand for animal-based protein increases internationally [6]. All antibiotic use results in an opportunity for antimicrobial resistance (AMR) to emerge and proliferate, and AMR is becoming an increasingly catastrophic problem. In 2019 it was estimated that AMR was a contributing factor to 4.95 million human deaths [7], and forecasts show that by 2050 this could increase to 8.22 million deaths annually [8]. However, the complete cessation of antibiotic use in high-intensity poultry production could significantly increase the prevalence of bacterial pathogens in food – posing serious biosafety concerns for both humans and poultry while potentially reducing food security and agricultural profits [9]. The reduction in antibiotic use in poultry farming has been linked to increases in the prevalence of Salmonella and Campylobacter in poultry products, and this poses a direct threat to food safety [10]. In this context, novel tools are required to reduce the burden of avian and zoonotic pathogens in the poultry industry without continued reliance on antibiotics.

Salmonella enterica is an important pathogen for the poultry industry, and poultry products are a common source of S. enterica infections in humans. In 2013, 44% of human salmonellosis cases were associated with eggs and egg products in the EU [11]. In addition, some serovars such as S. enterica serovar Pullorum and S. enterica serovar Gallinarum result in serious acute systemic disease in chicks [12], and mature chickens, respectively [13]. Vaccinations are available to prevent infections by some [14], but not all serovars [15]. Other options, such as probiotics, have been explored and have successfully protected chickens from S. enterica [16].

Currently, commercialized bacterial chicken probiotics are almost all gram-positive bacteria. The most frequently used probiotics in poultry feed are Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, Streptococcus, Pediococcus, Enterococcus, and Weissella [17]. In addition, most poultry probiotics are marked to improve digestion, feed efficiency, gut health, and immune function – while very few offer to improve the competitive exclusion of bacterial pathogens [18]. Work examining the potential of gram-negative probiotics, as well as the ability of gram-negative probiotics to antagonize gram-negative pathogens in vivo is limited. This is likely because a high proportion of gram-negative bacteria in the gastric microbiome is often considered a sign of infection or dysbiosis. In addition, the potential transfer or AMR genes (ARGs) from gram-negative pathogens to the native microbiome is a concern [19, 20]. However, the gram-negative probiotic Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 has been shown to provide a number of health benefits in humans [21], and gram-negative bacteria are an important and stable group of bacteria in the chicken gastric microbiota. Novel probiotics composed in part or entirely of gram-negative bacteria may be beneficial to poultry health.

The aim of this study was to investigate the potential of gram-negative bacteria, isolated from healthy chickens, to inhibit the growth of pathogenic S. enterica ser. Typhimurium in co-culture. We screened a library of 25 gram-negative isolates taken from a culture collection developed in our lab [22] and identified three isolates: Alcaligenes faecalis Ae14, Citrobacter braakii J-D0-20, and Escherichia ruysiae J-D0-44 which were able to significantly suppress the growth of S. enterica ser. Typhimurium in co-culture. Additional characterization suggested that A. faecalis Ae14 shows the highest safety profile and offers the best competitive exclusion ability.

Methods

High-throughput bacterial competition assay

We selected diverse gram-negative bacteria from a culture collection previously established by our group [22, 23]. The culture collection was established by collecting fecal samples from healthy chickens from across Canada and using a broad array of culture media to create a highly diverse culture collection [22, 23]. An inter-bacterial co-culture competition protocol was adapted from Lin et al. [24]. Briefly, both the commensal isolate and the pathogen (S. enterica ser. Typhimurium 14028s) were streaked for isolation from − 80 °C stock cultures on Tryptic Soy Agar (TSA) (BD Company, USA) and grown for 24 h at 37 °C. Isolated colonies of both the commensal and the S. enterica were resuspended in 1mL phosphate buffered saline (PBS) (Ward’s Science, USA) until the OD600 was 0.3. The two bacterial suspensions were mixed at a 1:1 or a 10:1 ratio of commensal to pathogen and mixed gently by pipetting. A 10 µl drop of the mixture was spotted onto a competition agar plate, which contained TSA with 0.05% bile salt (MilliporeSigma, USA). The competition plate was incubated at 37 °C for 16 to 18 h. Each competition colony was recovered by stamping the colony with a UV-sterilized PCR plate and resuspending the mixed bacterial culture in 150 µl of PBS. This suspension was serially diluted to a final dilution of 10− 8. A 5 µl aliquot of each dilution was spotted onto Salmonella Shigella Agar (SSA) (Becton Dickinson, Mexico) and grown at 37 °C overnight. Monocultures were used as a control. A detailed workflow is presented in Figure S1. After incubation S. entercia ser. Typhimurium colonies were enumerated. Each competition reaction was carried out in technical and biological triplicate. Statistical analysis and visualization were conducted on R Studio (v 4.2.2) [25], with packages ggplot2 (v 3.4.4) [26], ggpubr (v 0.6.0) [27], and rstaix (v 0.7.2) [28]. A two-sample t-test was used to determine if a commensal isolate could significantly inhibit the growth of S. entercia ser. Typhimurium compared to S. enterica growth in monoculture. P-values were adjusted using Bonferroni correction method, and the adjusted P-values that were lower than 0.05 were considered significant.

Competition growth curves

Based on the draft co-culture assays, Alcaligenes faecalis Ae-14, Citrobacter braakii J-D0-20, and Escherichia ruysiae J-D0-44 were selected for further characterization since they each consistently inhibited the growth of S. enterica ser. Typhimurium. A growth curve co-culture assay was used to determine if isolates showed inhibitory activity at different times in the growth cycle. Each isolate was recovered from the − 80 °C stock culture by streaking for isolation on TSA plates and incubating at 37 °C for 24 h. A single colony was used to inoculate 5 mL Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB) (BD Company, USA), and this was incubated overnight at 37 °C with shaking at 10,000 rpm. After overnight growth, cells were recovered by centrifuging 1 mL of culture at 10,000 x g for 1 min. Cells were washed once in PBS and then resuspended in PBS to an OD600 of 0.3 for S. enterica ser. Typhimurium, and an OD600 of either 0.3 or 3.0 for each of the commensal isolates. S. enterica ser. Typhimurium was mixed with both dilutions of commensal and mixed gently. A 10 µl drop of the bacterial co-culture mixture was spotted onto the competition plate (TSA with 0.05% bile salt). A S. enterica ser. Typhimurium monoculture was used as a control. Biological and technical triplicates were performed at each time point. The competition plate was incubated at 37 °C, and colonies were enumerated every three hours (0 h, 3 h, 6 h, 9 h, 12 h, and 15 h) by resuspending the colonies in PBS and serially diluting to 10− 7. Each dilution was spotted onto SSA and incubated at 37 °C overnight. The colonies were enumerated, and statistical analysis was conducted using a t-test and a Berroni adjustment.

DNA extraction, library preparation, and whole genome sequencing

To determine the phylogenetic identity and potential antagonistic mechanisms of each commensal isolate, each isolate was whole genome sequenced. Each commensal isolate was recovered from the − 80 °C stock culture by streaking for isolation on TSA agar and incubating the plates overnight at 37 °C. Around 5 to10 colonies from the plate were streaked and resuspended into 300 µl Maxwell® RSC blood DNA extraction kit lysis buffer (Promega, USA) and 30 µl Proteinase K (Promega, USA). The suspension was incubated at 56 °C for 20 min and vortexed twice during the incubation period. The suspension was then transferred into the DNA extraction cartridge and placed in the Maxwell® RSC instrument (Promega, USA). The DNA was extracted using the pre-programmed “Blood DNA extraction” program. The DNA quality and quantity were assessed using the Nanodrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher, USA) and the Quant-iT dsDNA High-Sensitivity Assay Kit (Invitrogen, USA).

A sequencing library was prepared for each isolate according to the manufacturer’s instructions using the Illumina DNA prep (M) Tagmentation (Illumina, USA) kit and Nextera DNA CD indexes (Illumina, USA). Paired-end sequencing (2 × 300) was preformed using a MiSeq benchtop sequencer (M04085) and a MiSeq Reagent Kit v3, 600 Cycles (Illumina, USA).

Genome assembly and quality assessment

Raw reads were uploaded to the Digital Research Alliance of Canada Beluga Server and analyzed with the bash script for assembly. This script was published in the GitHub [26]. The script removed adapters and low-quality reads (average score of 20 at least in any 5-base window) with Trimmomatic [29]. After sequences were assembled with the SPAdes assembler [30], the contigs smaller than 1 kb were removed using the BBmap tool ‘reformat’ [31]. The quality of contigs was checked with Quast [32], and the coverage of each genome was assessed with BBmap [31]. The assembled contigs genome completeness and inter-species contamination were evaluated with CheckM [33].

Genome annotation, taxonomic classification, virulence and ARG identification

A phylogenetic classification of each isolate based on whole genome sequencing and the BV-BRC comprehensive genome report. BV-BRC used high-quality genomes from the NCBI and PATRIC databases for the phylogenetic analysis [34]. Mash/MinHash was used to identify and select the closet reference genomes from the bacterial strains from the database [35]. Then, the relevant PATRIC global protein families (PGFams) [36] were extracted and aligned with MUSCLE [37], and mapped to the nucleotides sequences. Finally, RAxML [38] was used to analyze the data matrix of amino acid and nucleotides, and fast bootstrapping to generate support values [39].

Assembled contigs were submitted to the Bacterial and Viral Bioinformatics Resource Center (BV-BRC) server and annotated using a comprehensive genome analysis tool and the default setting [40]. This tool generated the assembly quality report, annotated the genome with RAST-tk, and extracted specialty genes [41]. The BV-BRC analysis used National Database of Antibiotic Resistant Organisms (NDARO) and Pathosystems Resource Integration Center (PATRIC) databases to identify ARGs [42]. The presence of ARGs in each genome was also analyzed using the The Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD) along with the Resistance Gene Identifier (RGI) tool [43]. The presence of putative virulence factors in each genome of candidate probiotics isolates was also analyzed using BV-BRC using the Pathosystems Resource Integration Center Virulence Factor (PATRIC VF), Virulence factor database (VFDB), and Victors database [42, 44]. The presence of virulence genes as well as the probability of each isolate being a human pathogen was additionally assessed using PathogenFinder 1.1 [45].

Identification of potential mechanisms of S. enterica ser. Typhimurium antagonism

To identify potential mechanisms of S. enterica ser. Typhimurium antagonism the output of the RSAT-tk annotation was submitted to the BlastKOALA platform [46]. This platform uses suffix arrays to perform KEGG Orthology (KO) assignments to classify genes and preform metabolic pathway reconstruction. The KO number and modules for S. enterica ser. Typhimurium 14,028 S genome (Entry: T01714, GenBank: CP001363.1) were compared to the sequences of each commensal using KEGG website. Based on the KEGG result, the genes related to quorum sensing, carbohydrate, fatty acid, and amino acid utilization were also examined.

The presence of known and specific potential mechanisms of inter-bacterial antagonism such as Type VI Secretion Systems (T6SSs) and bacteriocins were also examined. Based on the annotation, any contig that carried T6SS components were extracted and uploaded to the SecReT6 platform [47, 48]. The T6SS prediction tool was used to identify and predict T6SS proteins in each of the isolates and the T6SS-dependent killing risk estimation tool was used to predict if S. enterica ser. Typhimurium or the commensal would kill each other based on only the T6SS and the combination of effectors, immunity proteins, and effector targets in each genome [47]. Secondary metabolites were analyzed with AntiSMASH [49] and BAGEL4 [50] to search for the production of known antimicrobials or bacteriocins.

Results

Several chicken commensals inhibit S. enterica in co-culture

Several bacterial isolates from healthy chickens were evaluated for the ability to inhibit the growth of S. enterica ser. Typhimurium in co-culture. At a 1:1 ratio of commensal to pathogen, several different E. coli strains including 4-AB0-21, 5-AB7-55, and J-D0-08; an A. faecalis strain Ae-14; a C. braakii strain J-D0-20; and a Morganella morganii strain S1-B1-05, each showed significant inhibitory effects on the growth of S. enterica ser. Typhimurium after 16–18 h of co-culture (Fig. 1). At a 1:10 ratio of pathogen to commensal, several E. coli isolates, including 4-AB0-10, 4-AB0-12, 4-AB0-21, 4-AB0-5, 4-AB7-55, 4-AB7-56, and J-D0-10; an Escherichia ruysiae strain J-D0-44; a Pantoea agglomerans strain J-D0-35, and a Paenalcaligens hominis strain S2-B3-18, each showed significant inhibitory effects on the growth of S. enterica in co-culture.

A single E. coli strain J-D0-50 was shown to significantly promote the growth of S. enterica in co-culture.

S. enterica antagonists had different patterns inhibition over time

To determine if the ability of some commensals to antagonize S. enterica ser. Typhimurium varied based on the growth phase of the co-culture, growth curves of co-cultures were constructed (Figure S2). A. faecalis Ae-14 showed significant inhibition of S. enterica ser. Typhimurium after only 6 h of co-culture at a 1:10 ratio, and after 9 h at a 1:1 ratio (Fig. 2). C. braakii J-D0-20 only had significant inhibition of S. enterica ser. Typhimurium at a 1:10 ratio after 15 h of growth, and significant inhibition of S. enterica ser. Typhimurium growth was not observed at a 1:1 ratio for this strain. Finally, E. ruysiae J-D0-44 showed significant and consistent inhibition of S. enterica ser. Typhimurium after 9 h of co-culture at a 1:10 ratio of S. enterica ser. Typhimurium to E. ruysiae J-D0-44, while inhibition was also observed at a 1:1 ratio it was inconsistent over time. Commensals were unable to antagonize S. enterica ser. Typhimurium during the lag phase but were able to antagonize S. enterica ser. Typhimurium growth during the log and stationary phases.

In silico identification of antibiotic resistance and virulence factor genes

A. faecalis Ae-14, C. braakii J-D0-20, and E. ruysiae J-D0-44 were each whole genome sequenced to gain insight into pathogenicity and antibiotic resistance – which would both be important factors for selecting an appropriate candidate for probiotic development. Each genome was sequenced to >50X coverage, 100% completeness, and < 1% contamination (Table S1). The species level identity of each isolate was examined using PGfam and closely related genomes in the BV-BRC server [40]. Phylogenetic trees were generated for each isolate (Figure S3). The presence of ARGs was assessed using the BV-BRC and CARD-RGI algorithms with appropriate databases. Each genome contained ARGs, but the number and identity of ARGs identified depended heavily on which algorithm and database was used (Table S2). Based on CARD-RGI A. faecalis Ae-14 contained the fewest ARGs where only 2 ARGs were identified (Table S2). C. braakii J-D0-20 contained 24 predicted ARGs, and E. ruysiae J-D0-44 contained 44 ARGs (Table S3).

The presence of virulence genes in each isolate was assessed using BV-BRC and each of three databases: Pathosystems Resource Integration Center Virulence Factor (PATRIC VF), Virulence factor database (VFDB), and Victors database. A. faecalis Ae-14 carried between 1 and 4 potential virulence genes, and C. braakii J-D0-20 and E. ruysiae J-D0-44 contained between 48 and 193 putative virulence genes based on comparison to the same databases (Table S2). PathogenFinder identified 9 potential virulence genes in the A. faecalis Ae-14 genome and did not predict that this isolate was a human pathogen (Table S4). PathogenFinder identified 77 putative virulence genes in the C. braakii J-D0-20 genome and 124 in the E. ruysiae J-D0-44 genome. The probability of C. braakii J-D0-20 and E. ruysiae J-D0-44 being human pathogens was 0.86 and 0.91, respectively (Table S4).

In silico investigation of potential mechanisms of competitive exclusion

Each whole genome sequence was analyzed to identify potential mechanisms of S. enterica ser. Typhimurium antagonism. S. enterica ser. Typhimurium contains a type i3 T6SS with the Tae, Tde, and Tse toxins (Table 1). A. faecalis Ae14 has a type i3 T6SS, but a toxin was unable to be identified in this genome. C. braakii J-D0-20 had a type i2 with a Tse toxin; and E. ruysiae J-D0-44 had two complete T6SSs in different contigs, representing both types i2 and i3, with Tle and Tse toxins (Table 1). Based on whole genome analysis using the T6SS-dependent killing risk estimation tool, there was a high risk that S. enterica ser. Typhimurium could kill each of the commensals since none of the commensals had a Tae immunity protein, but each contained the susceptibility factor. Neither A. faecalis Ae14 nor C. braakii J-D0-20 were predicted to be capable of killing S. enterica ser. Typhimurium via a T6SS. However, E. ruysiae J-D0-44 was predicted to be able to kill S. enterica ser. Typhimurium since it can express the Tle effector – for which S. enterica ser. Typhimurium has the susceptibility factor but no immunity protein.

Following genome annotation, KEGG was used to assign putative molecular functions using the KO database with BlastKoala [51] (Table S5). The KO numbers were assigned to each genome and a pathway map was generated using KEGG mapper. The KEGG analysis was first used to determine if quorum sensing was potentially responsible for antagonism (Table S6). S. enterica ser. Typhimurium carries autoinducer 2 (AI-2), while C. braakii J-D0-20 also carries AI-2 cassette encoded by lsrABCD (Table 1) [52, 53]. This makes it possible that C. braakii J-D0-20 may be able to suppress S. enterica ser. Typhimurium growth via a high-concentration AI-2 signal factor.

Bacteria can also antagonize each other by producing important secondary metabolites like compatible solutes, siderophores, bacteriocins, or antibiotic peptides. To assess the ability of probiotic candidates to produce these metabolites, we searched for biosynthetic gene clusters using AntiSmash4 [49] and BAGEL4 [50]. AntiSMASH predicted that A. faecalis Ae-14 produces ectoine, C. braakii J-D0-20 and E. ruysiae J-D0-44 produce enterobactin and Bottromycin, and that C. braakii J-D0-20 produces Carocin D (Table 1).

We compared the nutrient utilization of each bacteria using BlastKoala [51] to determine if outcompeting S. enterica ser. Typhimurium might be a simple matter of more efficient competition for the same carbon and amino acid sources. C. braakii J-D0-20 and E. ruysiae J-D0-44 had significant overlap with S. enterica ser. Typhimurium in terms of carbon source utilization. S. enterica ser. Typhimurium is hypothetically able to metabolize glucose, fructose, xylose and mannose and C. braakii J-D0-20 and E. ruysiae can both also use these carbohydrates (Table S7). S. enterica ser. Typhimurium can also degrade histidine for energy (Table S8), as can A. faecalis Ae-14 and C. braakii J-D0-20.

During the KEGG module analysis, it was also found that each commensal bacteria carried the full module of biosynthesis for gamma-aminobutyric acid, γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) while S. enterica ser. Typhimurium does not (Table S9). Some bacteria can secrete GABA to improve acid tolerance ability to enhance bacterial survival ability under stress. However, the detailed mechanism of how GABA improve bacteria’s competitive ability is unknown.

Discussion

In this study, it was demonstrated that some gram-negative commensal bacteria isolated from a healthy chicken’s feces could reduce the growth of S. enterica ser. Typhimurium in vitro, while other bacteria could promote its growth. Each commensal bacteria that inhibited the growth of S. enterica ser. Typhimurium showed different patterns of growth inhibition terms of the optimal ratio of pathogen to commensal, the growth phase for optimal antagonism, and the possible mechanisms of inhibition. These findings are encouraging first steps that indicate creating a poultry probiotic to reduce S. enterica ser. Typhimurium colonization might be possible.

The poultry probiotics currently available on the market are all gram-positive organisms and most are lactic acid bacteria. Few, have a modest ability to suppress the proliferation of certain bacterial pathogens. For example, FloraMax-B11, which contains a mixture of Lactobacillus acidophilus and Enterococcus faecium reduce poultry intestinal colonization by S. enterica ser. Enteritidis, but only if administered in day-old chicks [54]. Another probiotic prepared by Prepro Microbiome Labs, which includes Bacillus subtilis and Bacillus licheniformis, was also shown to have a modest decrease in S. enterica ser. Enteritidis after a challenge [55]. However, in both instances, the probiotics lower colonization rates, and mitigate damage done to the intestinal tract – neither effectively block colonization. In this study, we are attempting to find an optimal S. enterica competitor and reasoned that gram-negative bacteria might have more success as they account for most of the inter-individual differences in terms of microbiome composition and functionality [56].

A. faecalis is a facultative aerobe commonly found in soil, water, mammalian intestinal tracts, avian intestinal tracts and feces [57, 58]. Our work indicated that A. faecalis may be an excellent poultry probiotic candidate as it showed significant and consistent growth inhibition of S. enterica ser. Typhimurium after only 6 h of co-culture and was not predicted to have pathogenic potential. A. faecalis Ae-14 may antagonize S. enterica via the production of ectonine or through effective competition for amino acid utilization. Ectoine is a small organic compound that promotes bacterial survival in high-salt or extreme environments and may provide a competitive advantage in this environment [59].

A. faecalis can be an opportunistic pathogen in humans [57]. However, several studies have demonstrated success A. faecalis probiotics as biocontrol agents in fish, plants, and mammals. A. faecalis is a heterotrophic nitrifying bacterium and has the potential to improve water quality in aquaculture operations by removing ammonia nitrogen and phosphorus [60]. Beyond water quality benefits, A. faecalis Y311 (originally isolated from fish tank sediment) was also shown to reduce the relative abundance of potential pathogens in fish mucosal tissues [60]. A. faecalis A12C (originally isolated from a fish gill) has shown antagonistic effects against the aquaculture pathogens Vibrio anguillarum, Vibrio harveyi, and Vibrio alginolyticus during in vitro growth [61]. This strain was also effective in reducing the clinical signs and infection progression of E. coli peritonitis in rats; although, based on the experimental design, this effect was likely through anti-inflammatory/ immunomodulatory effects rather than inter-bacterial antagonism [62]. Other studies have demonstrated that various strains of A. faecalis could produce anti-microbial substances against Aspergillus spp [63], Mycobacterium chubuense [64], Enterobacter spp., and Serratia spp [65].

In the current study, C. braakii J-D0-20 was able to effectively limit the growth of S. enterica ser. Typhimurium growth when it outnumbers the pathogen in the inoculum by a 1:10 ratio and the effects of C. braakii J-D0-20 only become apparent at late log phase. Given that both C. braakii J-D0-20 and S. enterica ser. Typhimurium use the same carbon sources, it is likely that competitive exclusion is taking place in this co-culture; although, the expression of Carocin D, a bacteriocin, activity could also play a role [66]. In humans, Citrobacter spp. has been associated with several types of nosocomial infections, including urinary tract infections, bacteriemia, and neonatal sepsis, in patients with severe underlying conditions [67, 68]. The C. braakii species is specifically linked to bacteriemia [69]. C. braakii is not currently known to cause infections in chickens. While very little work has been conducted to evaluate the potential of C. braakii as a probiotic, one strain T7, was evaluated for pathogenicity in a fish model and did not cause clinical symptoms [70].

E. ruysiae J-D0-44 was able to might be able to reduce S. enterica ser. Typhimurium growth in mid-log phase, although the significance of inhibition decreased over time. E. ruysiae J-D0-44 has a similar carbohydrate utilization pattern to S. enterica ser. Typhimurium and may be able to limit its growth through direct nutrient competition. In addition, E. ruysiae J-D0-44 also contains a Tle T6SS effector protein for which S. enterica ser. Typhimurium has the susceptibility factor but no immunity protein – T6SS mediated antagonism is also a possibility.

E. ruysiae is a relatively recently described species that was proposed to encompass the cryptic clades III and IV of the Escherichia genus [71]. It is found commonly in healthy chickens [72], but is also reported – albeit rarely, in healthy humans [71, 73]. There is currently no evidence in the literature to suggest that this species has pathogenic potential, and it has not previously been evaluated for probiotic potential – although a close relative, E. coli, has strains ranging from a deadly pathogen to a probiotic [21, 74].

Both C. braakii J-D0-20 and E. ruysiae J-D0-44 were predicted by PathogenFinder to be human pathogens [45]. While will undergo safety testing before being developed as probiotics, it’s important to note that results from PathogenFinder are predictions. PathogenFinder is reported to have very low accuracy predicting non-pathogenic Enterobacteriaceae including achieving only a 68% success rate in predicting non-pathogenic E. coli; in addition, PathogenFinder is known to identify all opportunistic strains as pathogenic strains [45].

There is increasing evidence that probiotics may be a viable alternative to conventional antibiotic use in poultry production. In this study, we have demonstrated that in a controlled, reductionist environment bacterial commensals isolated from healthy chickens are able to strongly inhibit the growth of S. enterica. Thus, we may be able to use this finding to build novel probiotics that prevent Salmonella in chickens and provide a suitable alternative for antibiotics to prevent Salmonella colonization of egg laying hens. Future work will focus on evaluating the safety profile of these isolates – for humans and chickens, determining if a multi-strain probiotic may offer more effectiveness for disease prevention, and determining if these isolates are effective at limiting the growth of any other bacterial pathogens.
