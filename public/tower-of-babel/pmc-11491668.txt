Hepatocellular adenoma update: diagnosis, molecular classification, and clinical course
Authors: Sarah Poetter-Lang, Ahmed Ba-Ssalamah, Nina Bastati, Sami A Ba-Ssalamah, Jacqueline C Hodge, Giuseppe Brancatelli, Valérie Paradis, Valérie Vilgrain
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractHepatocellular adenomas (HCA) are acquired focal liver lesions, that occur mainly in young-to-middle-aged women who are on long-term estrogen-containing contraceptives or young men after prolonged use of anabolic steroids. Furthermore, distinct underlying diseases, such as obesity, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease, glycogen storage disease, etc. are considered risk factors. The 2017 Bordeaux classification, in particular Nault et al, divided HCAs into eight subtypes according to their pheno- and genotypic characteristics. This includes HCAs with hepatocyte-nuclear-factor (HNF1-alpha mutation), HCAs with β-catenin mutation, and HCAs without either of these genetic mutations, which are further subdivided into HCAs with and without inflammatory cells. HCAs should no longer be classified as purely benign without histologic workup since three of the eight subtypes are considered high-risk lesions, requiring adequate management: malignant transformation of the pure (ßex3-HCA) and mixed inflammatory/β-catenin exon 3 (ßex3-IHCA) adenomas, as well as potential bleeding of the sonic hedgehog HCA and pure (ßex7/8-HCA) and mixed inflammatory/β-catenin exon 7/8 (ßex7/8-IHCA). Elective surgery is recommended for any HCA in a male, or for any HCA exceeding 5 cm. Although MRI can classify up to 80% of adenomas, if findings are equivocal, biopsy remains the reference standard for adenoma subtype.Keywords: liver, hepatocellular adenoma, MRI, contrast agents, molecular imaging

Introduction

Hepatocellular adenoma (HCA) is a rare liver lesion predominantly found in women of reproductive age.1,2 Oral contraceptive pills (OCP) for more than 5 years are one of the major risk factors for HCAs,3,4 increasing the annual incidence of HCA in OCP users from 1 to more than 30 cases per million among nonusers. The female-to-male ratio has changed from circa 11:1-4:1 as anabolic steroid use becomes more prevalent.5 Other risk factors for HCAs include glycogen storage diseases,6 obesity, metabolic syndrome, alcohol intake, and perhaps those with an endogenous imbalance of sex hormones, such as Klinefelter, MODY type 3 diabetes, and polycystic ovarian syndromes.7 More recently it has been recognized that clomiphene and barbiturates are also risk factors for adenomas.5

Although the vast majority of HCAs have a benign course, they are referred to as borderline tumours because certain subtypes are prone to haemorrhage, while male gender, ß-catenin mutation exon 3 and larger size, that is, >5 cm, increase the risk of developing HCC.8

Additionally, when HCAs in OCP users exceed 5 cm, they are also prone to rupture—usually during menstruation or pregnancy9 which may end in death if hypotension is serious enough.5 Though very rare in our experience, in a series of almost 1200 patients, rupture and intraperitoneal bleeding occurred in 17.5%.10 This overview article shall update the histopathologic, genetic, and imaging features of hepatocellular adenomas (HCA), concluding with patient management and outcomes.

Histology and immunohistochemistry

HCAs consist of hepatocytes without bile ducts or portal veins and are supplied only arterially.2 A pseudo-capsule may be present, morphologically similar to HCC. As histology is often insufficient, especially to differentiate HCA subtypes, as well as borderline HCAs from HCC,11 immunohistochemical (IHC) and molecular analysis may be necessary. Though these newer techniques are expensive and require larger tissue samples, they can provide pivotal information about the presence of specific proteins and genetic mutations, respectively.

Although they did not measure C-reactive protein (CRP) levels across the board, they found an elevated CRP in many patients with I-HCA.

In-depth research soon showed that many of the same aforementioned proteins are present in healthy hepatocytes and focal nodular hyperplasia (FNH), the common benign don’t touch liver lesion that can mimic HCAs, but also HCC, too. For example, ß-catenin was found in 90% of hepatoblastomas, as well as fibrolamellar HCC, and even in NASH where it regulates gluconeogenesis at baseline and during starvation, and in hepatic fibrosis where it activates hepatic stellate cells to lay down extracellular matrix. Further investigation discovered that during normal hepatic development, ß-catenin is activated by local proteins while in the setting of mutations, ß-catenin is activated autonomously and uncontrollably. Therefore, the pathologist’ task has become more complicated. Simply noting the presence of a particular protein is not enough. He must also assess its pattern of distribution, for example, diffuse or patchy; cytoplasm or nucleus, etc, in the context of the liver background2 to make an accurate diagnosis.

In 2017, Nault et al4 made a great leap forward by analyzing the molecular and IHC data from more than 500 HCAs and correlating it with the risk of bleeding, malignant transformation, and tumour growth. This led them to recommend resection of any adenoma exceeding 5 cm.

Furthermore, they identified four new entities, expanding the classification to 8 subtypes. The new subtypes include two distinct types of ß-catenin HCAs, that is, the more common ßex3-HCA which has much stronger ß-catenin activation than the ßex7,8-HCA and carries a high risk of HCC transformation; the mixed ß-catenin and I-HCA, that is, either ßex3-IHCA or ßex7,8-IHCA, depending upon its ß-catenin component; and lastly, the sonic hedgehog adenoma (sh-HCA) which is recognized on IHC by argininosuccinate synthase 1 and is associated with intra-tumoral bleeding.4,13 Including the 6% of unclassified HCAs, the new entities account for one-third of adenomas. As a rule of thumb then, one-third of adenomas are pure I-HCA, one-third are pure H-HCA, and one-third are other (Table 1).

Modalities for HCA imaging

In symptomatic patients complaining of vague RUQ pain,5 sonography (US) is often the initial study14 as these patients are of reproductive age.

Conventional US’s sensitivity in detecting HCA ranges from 50% to 70%, often depending upon the size of the lesion and the presence of steatosis hepatis. In 2010, van den Esschert et al14 performed a meta-analysis specifically aimed at the ability of imaging modalities to differentiate FNH from HCA. They concluded that conventional ultrasound (US) was not useful in this setting due to non-specific features. Serial US, however, can be used to document lesion stability if the adenoma(s) are well-visualized on sonography.

Despite overlapping features of HCAs with both benign and malignant liver lesions, US has moderate specificity for differentiating HCA from lesions like FNH or HCC. Doppler US may show variable vascular patterns in HCAs, but again, these findings are not specific enough to reliably differentiate HCA from other liver lesions.15

On the contrary, contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) is superior to US since FNH and adenomas have different arterial-phase enhancement patterns.16 However, subsequent phases can’t distinguish the HCA subtypes since hypoechogenicity doesn’t correlate with the subtypes.17

Based on a meta-analysis of 18 studies, the 2020 guidelines reported an overall sensitivity of 88% for FNH on CEUS. But, due to the different subtypes of HCAs, they cautioned that characterization and differentiation of HCAs from FNH and HCC could be difficult on CEUS.18

There is no role for diagnostic CT in this population. Adenomas are usually CT incidentalomas, appearing very hypodense if fat and/or necrosis is present or hyperdense if haemorrhage and/or calcification are present.19 Otherwise, they are isodense to liver and can’t be classified. Dystrophic calcifications, usually eccentric, in areas of old hemorrhage, occur in ca.10% of adenomas.20 In a young woman, FNH, considered an asymptomatic hyperplastic response to vascular insult, would be the leading differential diagnosis since it is far more common than adenomas. Accounting for 8% of liver lesions, FNH may be multiple, and may show interim growth or regression, independent of OCP usage.21

CT’s detection rate for HCA differs from 86% in nonenhanced, 100% in the hepatic arterial phase, 82% in the portal venous phase (PVP), and 88% in the delayed phase.22 Based upon lesion size, contrast enhancement patterns, and the presence of hemorrhage or fat within the adenoma may allow a distinction from other hepatic masses, especially with the use of multiphase contrast-enhanced CT, but with limited differentiation of HCA from lesions such as FNH, HCC, or metastases.

Regarding MRI, a sensitivity and specificity approaching 88% and 100%, respectively, was reported for HCAs with 70% sensitivity and 98% specificity for FNH.23 Although extracellular gadolinium-based contrast agent (ECCA)-enhanced MRI is usually the initial cross-sectional imaging study done, it can’t always confidently distinguish FNH from adenoma subtypes.19,24

When an adenoma is the primary working diagnosis, then multiparametric MRI with hepatobiliary contrast agent (HBCA) should be the initial study for the hepatobiliary phase’s (HBP) ability to help subtype the adenomas.25–29

Relying on the HBP, relative enhancement of the lesion, and lesion-to-liver enhancement ratio on gadoxetic acid-enhanced MRI, Grieser et al30 found significantly lower mean signal intensities for HCAs compared with FNHs, (P < .001). Furthermore, visually strong uptake in FNHs compared to hypointensity of the HCAs on the HBP yielded 92% accuracy. This parameter outperformed all other morphological and dynamic imaging criteria.31

MRI is the best imaging tool we have because of its high spatial resolution, and ability to determine chemical content (ie, fat, iron, inflammation, and fibrosis). By showing us the imaging phenotype, that is, its morphology and signal intensity, we can subtype up to 80% of HCAs34 and also assess liver background.35 Adding HBCA, we can almost always distinguish firstly HCA from FNH and secondly ßex3-HCA (either mixed or pure forms) from the remaining HCA. That makes HBCA an imaging biomarker.36

Lastly, although, there is no diagnostic role for PET-CT in adenomas, incidental HCAs have been described in young-to-middle-aged oncology patients on hybrid imaging, that is, 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography-computed tomography (18F-FDG PET-CT), which is typically used to differentiate benign from malignant lesions on the basis of cellular glucose utilization. Interestingly, the H-HCAs, with only a negligible risk of malignant degeneration, is overwhelmingly the most frequent subtype to accumulate 18F-FDG.37 These relatively high median maximum standardized uptake values (SUVmax) of 7.8 have been attributed to overexpression of glucose transporter type 1 rather than true hypermetabolic activity.25 More recently, three I-HCAs were found to express FDG avidity, surprisingly with far lower SUVmax, that is, 2.8 vs 5.3 for four H-HCAs.38 The presence of fat content on the CT images (Figure 1), as well as patient gender, age, and clinical history, should alert the reader to the possibility of an adenoma39 rather than misdiagnosis of a metastasis. MRI is indicated and might be followed by a biopsy if inconclusive (Figure 1).

Recent molecular classification of HCAs

HNF1α inactivated-HCA

H-HCA, histopathologically defined by its hepatocyte nuclear factor 1α inactivated, is associated with a TCF1 gene mutation that manifests with excess lipid accumulation in the affected hepatocytes. Furthermore, the lack of 1-FABP in this lesion explains its pathognomonic feature, that is, diffuse intra-tumoral steatosis. The H-HCA has very low malignant potential because it has no cytologic atypia nor nuclear abnormalities.16 More recently, maturity-onset diabetes of the young (MODY) type 3 which develops from a defect in a single allele, has also been implicated in the development of H-HCAs.40

On US and CT, the typical H-HCA is uniformly hyperechoic and the liver lacks obvious steatosis (Figure 1).41 On CEUS and CECT, the H-HCA will strongly enhance during the arterial phase. However, their appearance is more variable than that of other HCA such as I-HCA.42 On CEUS, adenomas will have numerous small peripheral vessels (angiogenesis pattern) converse to central and radiating artery in FNH. A common finding is the “multidot” appearance on the hepatic arterial phase followed by diffuse enhancement (Figure 3).43

Inflammatory HCA

The most common subtype of HCAs is the inflammatory one which includes the pure IHCA (Figures 4) and mixed ß-catenin IHCAs (either ßex3-IHCA or ßex7,8-IHCA). These three subtypes comprise approximately 40%-50% of HCAs. Its association with obesity, hepatic steatosis, and metabolic syndrome, as well as elevated tumour markers such as γ-glutamyl transferase, and elevated inflammatory parameters (eg, serum amyloid-associated protein and CRP) are well known. Because rising obesity affects both genders, IHCAs are increasingly diagnosed in men too. Furthermore, their abundant vascularity causes up to 30% of them to haemorrhage. Although the malignant transformation was initially thought to be rare in I-HCAs, HCC may develop in the ßex3-IHCA and less commonly in pure I-HCA, which was previously called a “telangiectatic FNH”, by both radiologists and pathologists, because its imaging and staining appearance resembled FNH2,40,43,44 (Figures 5 and 6) The mixed ßex3-IHCA can be challenging, especially if the ß-catenin component has a scar and is HBP-iso-or hyperintense, similar to FNH. Only IHC analysis can distinguish it from FNH.40,45 Moreover, recent research has shown that its molecular signature is interleukin-6 and the JAK-STAT signalling system, both of which are major risk factors for malignant transformation.40 The other mixed I-HCA subtype, ßex7,8-IHCA, like other HCAs has negligible malignant potential but may bleed.

ß-catenin HCAs

Pure ß-HCAs account for up to 10% of all HCAs. A mutation of the CTNNB1 gene causes either insertions or deletions in β-catenin exons 3, 6, 7, or 8. This activation prolongs the half-life of ß-catenin, which activates the Wnt-pathway responsible for HCC transformation.40 However, exons 7 and 8 are weak activators of the Wnt-pathway, which explains why these HCAs have a very low risk of malignant transformation, contrary to ßex3-HCAs. Specific risk factors include glycogen storage diseases, testosterone use, and familial polyposis syndrome.49,50 This subtype occurs in both women and men. At the histologic level, ß-HCA shows elevated nuclear-cytoplasmic ratios, nuclear atypia, and/or acinar formations, quite similar to the hepatocytes in well-differentiated HCC.49

On the contrary, exon 7/8 mutations carry a negligible risk of HCC transformation and, on histology, are associated with microscopic bleeding. ßex7,8-HCAs are also the subtype most often associated with glycogen storage diseases. Intralesional haemorrhage seen on CT or MRI may suggest the diagnosis51 (Figure 2).

Mixed βex3-IHCAs and the majority of the ß-HCAs, that is, exon 3 are iso- to hyperintense on HBP with a specificity of more than 90% of the cases52 (Figure 10). Depending on the clinical context, the differential diagnosis can include FNH, if there’s also strong arterial-phase enhancement and well-differentiated HCC. The remaining are HBP-hypointense, that is, ß-catenin adenoma exon 7/8. Like most other HCAs, ß-HCAs shows arterial-phase enhancement which can persist into the PVP and TP (Figure 10). Using manganese-based HBCA, ß-HCAs show uptake and with SPIO demonstrate SI loss when Kupffer cells are present (Figure 10).

Sonic hedgehog HCA

Recently argininosuccinate synthase 1, a major periportal protein, has been proposed as the signature for the rare (ie, 4%) sh-HCA and is thought to be responsible for its tendency to bleed. Furthermore, the presence of prostaglandin D synthase on IHC staining supports the diagnosis. Obesity and hepatic steatosis are associated with the sh-HCA.40 On MRI, nearly half of sh-HCAs had fluid-filled cavities, a unique feature among HCAs. Additional features included either haemorrhage or necrosis (71%), hepatic steatosis (66%), and obesity (>90%)53,54 (Figure 2). The sh-HCA enhances during the arterial phase, has a variable appearance during the PVP and is hypointense during the HBP (Figure 2).

Unclassified HCA

The remaining 10% of HCAs do not have any specific genetic alteration or histopathologic abnormality, so they are assigned to the unclassified HCA group.50

Algorithmic approach

HCAs have a wide range of overlapping imaging features that makes it impossible to accurately predict subtype from imaging features alone 100% of the time. Therefore, it is advisable to use an algorithmic approach, that is, to narrow the differential based on the patient history and demographics first.13 Young to middle-aged women who could be on OCP, male bodybuilders who could be taking anabolic steroid, or patients suffering from obesity, glycogen storage disease are prime candidates for HCAs.

Secondly, assess the appearance of lesions on the HBP images 20 min after Gd-EOB-DTPA administration to classify as iso-/hyperintense or hypointense lesions.26 From experience, we know two things: that 90% of HCAs are HBP-hypointense,55 and that only pure ßex3-HCA and mixed ßex3-IHCAs are iso- or hyperintense on the HBP due to OATP1B3 or in some cases due to background liver steatosis.54 Review of conventional MR images, including subtraction images, can help if non-contrast T1-weighted imaging shows hyperintensity or if there is hepatic steatosis.25,56 But I-HCAs may occasionally appear HBP-hyperintense too, if ßex3 component is present. If there is still doubt, biopsy remains the reference standard. Lastly, since ßex3-HCA carries the highest risk of malignant transformation, any HBP-iso-/hyperintense lesion should raise concern.

An HBP-iso-/hyperintense lesion could also be a well-differentiated HCC as a link has been shown between ßex3-HCA exon 3 and HCC. Furthermore, WD-HCC and HCC in noncirrhotic livers, have a bimodal age distribution with peaks during the seventh decade and less likely during the second decade of life.57 In particular, fibrolamellar carcinoma (FLC) should be considered in a young patient, too, since it may have a central scar. However, it usually shows HBP hypointensity,58 and thus can also mimic ßex7,8-HCA (Figure 11). Therefore, biopsy may be warranted.

Furthermore, because the majority of lesions have low signal intensity on the HBP, this list of differential diagnoses is much longer. Even if we limit it to the noncirrhotic liver, the possibilities include any liver lesion that does not contain hepatocytes, for example, metastases, lymphoma, etc., in addition to the vast majority of HCAs. An unclassified HCA can be a challenging diagnosis in young women with underlying malignancy with a predilection for liver metastases. In this setting, it may be necessary to use manganese-based HBCA which is taken up by transferrin receptor 2 on hepatocytes or SPIO contrast agent which is taken up by Kupffer cells of the reticuloendothelial system or perform biopsy.59,60

Clinical course and management of HCA

Vernuccio et al61 found that 78% of HCAs were stable or had regressed at median 5-year follow-up. Similar results were reported by Demory et al62 recently, who found that after OCP discontinuation, radiological evolution of HCAs was strongly associated with changes in weight, that is, growth with weight gain and regression with weight loss. Malignant transformation occurs in up to 4% and 40% of steatotic and ß-catenin exon 3 HCAs, respectively.25 The silver lining is that if HCA degenerates into malignancy, it becomes a non-proliferative, less aggressive HCC, usually moderate- or well-differentiated which carries a good prognosis, in comparison to proliferative HCCs that may be a sequelae of chronic hepatitis B.50 In a retrospective study of 72 patients with glycogen storage disease, the 10-year tumour-free survival exceeded 80% versus a 5-year survival of 8% in a cohort where 75% had viral-induced (ie, hepatitis B and/or C) HCC.63

In men, HCA resection is recommended regardless of size.36 In women, discontinuation of OCPs and/or weight loss is the first step in adenoma management. Six-month follow-up US or MRI shows whether HCA, liver steatosis and/or inflammation, that is, NASH, have regressed. Resection is recommended for lesions that exceed 5 cm or that continue to progress.64 Thereafter, annual or biennial MRI surveillance is suggested, depending upon risk factors and the lesion’s natural history.50 Cross-sectional imaging surveillance is preferred to sonography because lesion progression is based on RECIST criteria.50 However, provided that these HCAs are detectable in the US, sonography surveillance is a cost-effective option.

Large HCAs may require a staged surgical approach, that is, pre-operative transarterial embolization (either for actively bleeding HCAs or adenomas at very high-risk of intraoperative bleeding) followed by elective surgery. Transarterial embolization is also performed for unresectable lesions. Thermal ablation (either radiofrequency or microwave) is used for lesions under 5 cm. Because such small lesions are usually monitored, unless they grow, thermal ablation is not commonly performed in HCA, unless there is patient preference for intervention rather than surveillance.25

In the setting of multiple HCAs, circa 70% of the lesions will be of the same subtype. Further, the size of the largest lesion should determine management. If the biopsy is warranted, the largest lesion should be sampled since it is the most likely to contain ßex3-HCA.4

If the disease is confined to one lobe, lobectomy is an option, whereas if disease is more widespread, they may choose to excise the largest lesion. Unless there is underlying liver disease, for example, glycogen storage disease, multiple HCAs are not an indication for liver transplantation36 (Figure 12).

Conclusion

Thanks to molecular imaging, two-thirds of adenomas, that is, either H-HCA or I-HCA, can usually be diagnosed by MRI. Most important are CSI and the 20-min HBP using HBCAs. In ambiguous cases, the gender, risk factors, and conventional imaging findings should be taken into account. Biopsy, resection or close follow-up is recommended, according to the clinical context. In all males and in anyone with proven ßex3-HCA, resection is the standard of care due to the risk of malignant degeneration. In women, the goal is to avoid hemorrhage and/or rupture.65 Whereas surgical guidelines recommend resection of only non-steatotic adenoma >5 cm, EASL guidelines recommend excision of all adenomas >5 cm or any adenoma which is increasing in size.66
