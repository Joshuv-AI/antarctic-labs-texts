Phishing Awareness via Game-Based Learning
Argianto Rahartomo, Ahmed Tareq Ali Ghaleb, Mohammad Ghafari

Abstract
The increased use of digital devices and applications has led to a rise in phishing attacks. We develop a serious game to raise awareness about phishing attacks and help people avoid these threats in a risk-free learning environment. This game targets three types of phishing—clone phishing, SMS phishing, and spear phishing—and uses a Large Language Model to generate dialogues and questions dynamically. It also incorporates state randomization and time-limited challenges to enhance the gameplay. We evaluated two groups of participants and found that those who played the game showed, on average, a 24% increase in awareness and a 30% boost in confidence.



I
Introduction

Phishing is a threat in which attackers pretend to be trusted organizations in emails or messages to steal personal information, such as usernames, passwords, and credit card details.
Typically, victims receive emails or messages that appear legitimate but point them to fake websites.
This stolen information can enable unauthorized access to financial accounts and potentially lead to identity theft.
Hence, it is important to learn how to recognize and avoid phishing.

Three common types of phishing attacks are clone phishing, SMS phishing, and spear phishing
[
1
]
.
Clone phishing involves creating duplicates of legitimate websites to trick users into thinking they are on the original site.
Victims, often deceived by the similar appearance of the site, are tricked into entering sensitive information such as usernames, passwords, or PINs, typically through cloned login pages.
SMS phishing, also known as “smishing”, involves attackers using SMS messages to perform phishing scams.
These messages often appear to come from reputable sources like banks or government agencies and usually urge recipients to act quickly or respond to an urgent alert.
Spear phishing targets employees within corporate or industrial entities through email campaigns.
In these attacks, deceivers use specific details to gain recipients’ trust and leverage this trust to steal sensitive information or gain unauthorized system access.

Research has shown that games can significantly improve learning outcomes.
For example,Rahartomo
et al.

[
2
]
applied a game-based teaching approach to successfully educate dyslexic students about cryptography.
Jayakrishnan
et al.

[
3
]
developed a serious game aimed at training organizational employees to identify and avoid email phishing.
In another effort, Yasin
et al.

[
4
]
created a serious game to raise awareness of clone phishing, but their approach is limited to predefined URLs, restricting its versatility.

To address the gap in tackling the human element of phishing defense
[
5
]
, we designed a 3D game to improve phishing awareness in an engaging and risk-free environment. The game features a variety of missions in which players encounter scenarios that involve clone phishing, SMS phishing, and spear phishing, to develop their ability to recognize and respond to phishing threats.
The game leverages state randomization and time-based challenges to create a dynamic and stimulating experience. Additionally, it integrates a Large Language Model to dynamically generate dialogues and questions, providing educational insights about phishing threats and further enriching the gameplay experience.

To assess the effectiveness of our game in improving players’ awareness of phishing, we randomly assigned 28 participants to two groups: experimental and control.
Each group consisted of 14 individuals from diverse backgrounds and age ranges.
All participants completed a survey to evaluate their familiarity with phishing activities (pre-survey), revealing a general lack of awareness about phishing attempts.
Participants in the experimental group played the game and then completed a post-survey.
We then evaluated both groups using a standardized Google phishing quiz.

The results indicate that the experimental group achieved a 24% higher average awareness score compared to participants who did not play the game.
The game players also showed an increase of 30% in confidence in detecting phishing attempts.

Game Design Components

We present our serious game components and design.
The game aims to test players’ decision-making skills.
It includes the game’s dynamics, design, and narratives.
The game features basic components such as web, question, and dialogue generators, as well as scenarios that demand quick strategic thinking due to time-limited challenges.
We describe how these elements work together to train players in identifying and addressing phishing threats.

II-C

Game Narratives

This game is designed to improve players’ abilities to recognize and avoid phishing attempts by presenting them with various scenarios that challenge their quick-thinking skills.
Players take on the role of a cybersecurity consultant who helps victims by analyzing how phishing attacks occurred and dealing with related challenges.
At the beginning of the game, players select a difficulty level that reflects the severity of real phishing incidents.
They then navigate a city using an interactive map, completing tasks to identify different types of phishing, such as clone, SMS, or spear phishing.
The map shows the real-time locations of both the player and the targets.
Gameplay includes interactive sessions with NPCs where players gather information and solve phishing-related puzzles within specific time limits.
Advancing in the game depends on completing tasks, with each correct answer earning players points and badges.

III
Experiment Design

We outline the design of our experimental study, which aims
to evaluate the effectiveness of a serious game in improving
awareness of phishing.
We collect demographic data to ensure a diverse and representative sample.
We then proceeded with a detailed data collection phase, recording participants’ interactions with the game and gathering their feedback.
Subsequently, we assessed both groups using a standardized phishing quiz and recorded their performance scores.
The quiz comprises all three types of phishing that we cover in this game.

III-A

Demography

We conducted a survey with human participants to evaluate the game, recruiting them through postings on online forums such as Reddit and Medium, as well as through our connections.
A total of 28 participants were recruited and evenly divided into two groups: an experimental group and a control group, each consisting of 14 participants.
The survey included questions about participants’ age, gender, and education level. Among the participants, 4 were female, and 24 were male, with ages ranging from 13 to 55 years (average age = 26.57, standard deviation = 8.53).
The participants represented diverse fields, including IT, business administration, mechatronics, and English literature, with some still attending high school. The highest level of education achieved among them was a master’s degree.

IV
The Result

In this section, we discuss the findings of our study, which evaluated the impact of our serious game on participants’ awareness of phishing, confidence, and ability to identify phishing attempts.
The results show differences in behavior, confidence levels, and quiz scores between the experimental and control groups.
We provide detailed analyses in three main areas: overall phishing awareness, changes in participants’ confidence, and their performance on a standardized phishing quiz.
The experimental group completed a pre-survey, played the game, filled out a post-survey, and then took the phishing quiz.
On the other hand, the control group only participated in the pre-survey and the phishing quiz.

IV-C

Phishing Quiz Result

We organized participants into two groups: experimental and control.
We measured each participant’s success as a percentage and sorted these percentages into 10 intervals.
We present this distribution for both groups using a bar chart, as shown in Figure
5

The control group scored an average success rate of 62.14% (standard deviation = 15.78%), while the experimental group scored higher at 85.71% (standard deviation = 11.87%), outperforming the control group by 24%.
The data indicate that over 90% of the experimental group can correctly answer at least 75% of the quiz questions, compared to only 28.57% in the control group achieving this benchmark.

We conduct a t-test to evaluate the significance of our findings. The
null hypothesis
states that “serious games do not increase awareness”, while the alternative hypothesis suggests that they do. With a t-value of 4.46772 and a p-value of 0.00014 (which is less than 0.05), we reject the null hypothesis and conclude that our game improves phishing awareness.

V
Threats to Validity

The selection of participants for this study may show a bias, as volunteers often have a pre-existing interest or expertise in cybersecurity.
This could result in the findings reflecting higher-than-average knowledge or enthusiasm for phishing, which may not accurately represent the general population.
To address this, we recruit participants through various channels such as social media platforms like Reddit and Medium, universities, and community centers.

Participants may also feel social pressure to provide favorable answers, possibly overstating their vigilance or downplaying their susceptibility to phishing.
This could lead to artificially positive results.
We ensure honest responses by guaranteeing the anonymity and confidentiality of all answers.

It is necessary to ensure the reliability of the tools used to measure awareness and behavioral change.
Inconsistent quizzes or surveys might produce unreliable results, making it difficult to assess the effectiveness of the serious game in increasing phishing awareness.
Additionally, we analyze the first response from each participant to maintain data integrity.

VI
Conclusion

We built a serious game to increase players’ awareness to uncover phishing attacks in a risk-free learning environment.
We found that the game helped players to identify and mitigate malicious schemes, promoting stronger security practices.
According to the post-survey, 85% of players gained a deeper understanding of phishing, and The game motivated 75% of players to adopt better security practices, such as verifying email authenticity and avoiding suspicious links in their daily lives.
We also found that those who played the game showed, on average, a 24% increase in awareness and a 30% boost in confidence.

Future improvements could focus on refining game features, adding more scenarios, and collecting more data such as click streams.
