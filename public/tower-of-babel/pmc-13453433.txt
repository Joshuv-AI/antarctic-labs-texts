Reproductive Biology and Life‐History Plasticity of the Yellowfin Snapper Lutjanus xanthopinnis in the Southern South China Sea
Authors: Takaomi Arai, Iy Vonne Tan, Norhayati Ahmad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTStudying fish reproductive biology provides critical insights into population dynamics, supporting effective fisheries management and wildlife conservation. Although the yellowfin snapper Lutjanus xanthopinnis was recently identified as a new species, its reproductive traits across the southern South China Sea remain poorly understood. This study elucidated the reproductive biology of L. xanthopinnis off Borneo and Brunei Darussalam, including spawning seasonality, gonadal development, sex ratio and fecundity. A total of 385 specimens were examined monthly over a one‐year period. The overall sex ratio was 1.16:1, indicating a stable regional equilibrium (Chi‐square test, p > 0.05). Based on monthly fluctuations in the gonadosomatic index (GSI) and histological analysis, both sexes exhibited a prolonged, nearly year‐round spawning pattern; females showed a distinct peak from October to August, while males remained mature year‐round. The estimated total length at 50% maturity (L 50) was nearly identical between the sexes (18.3 cm for females; 18.8 cm for males), which is generally smaller than in related Lutjanidae species. Total fecundity ranged from 8000 to 65,000 eggs and showed no significant correlation with maternal body size. Contrasting these findings with recent data from adjacent regional populations reveals remarkable intraspecific plasticity in the life‐history strategies of L. xanthopinnis. Unlike neighbouring populations that exhibit monsoon‐restricted spawning, pronounced intersexual size differences and male‐biased sex ratios, the Brunei population adopts a balanced, continuous reproductive regime. These striking intraspecific variations demonstrate that localised environmental conditions and oceanographic regimes drive significant ecological and evolutionary adaptations in the life cycle of coral reef fishes, even within closely situated geographic regions.

Introduction

Worldwide, key tropical reef fish stocks are under continuous overexploitation (Jennings and Polunin 1996; Edgar et al. 2014). While effective management relies on understanding economic, political and social interactions, ecological data remain essential for guiding policies (McClanahan and Castilla 2007; Edgar et al. 2014). Small‐scale artisanal fisheries pose particular assessment and management challenges, yet they can greatly impact reef ecosystems by depleting target species and affecting ecological processes (Roberts 1995; Hawkins and Roberts 2004). For instance, overfishing of piscivorous fish can alter benthic community structures via trophic cascades (Estes et al. 2011). In tropical Southeast Asia, small‐scale fisheries are a crucial economic resource for local communities and residents, especially in ensuring food security (Pauly 2006; Béné 2006; Arai 2014).

Snappers, part of the Lutjanidae family, are vital to fishery resources in tropical and subtropical waters, including the Caribbean and Indo‐Pacific regions (Allen 1985). Many studies have explored their spawning, settlement, feeding behaviours, age and growth (e.g., Newman et al. 2000; Denit and Sponaugle 2004; Kritzer 2004; Heyman et al. 2005; Amezcua et al. 2006; Freitas et al. 2014; Arai et al. 2015). Their spawning periods have been documented across various research sites (e.g., Grimes 1987; Kaunda‐Arara and Ntiba 1997; Shimose and Tachihara 2005; Russell and McDougall 2008; Freitas et al. 2014; Palla and Sotto 2021). In reef fish populations, large, long‐lived species like those in Lutjanidae—characterised by slow growth and late maturity—are often targeted by fisheries (Morris et al. 2000; Y. Sadovy 2001). Additionally, some Lutjanidae species form spawning aggregations at specific times and locations, which are critical for sustainable fishery management (Colin et al. 2003; Sadovy de Mitcheson et al. 2008). Snappers are valuable ecological indicators because of their crucial roles and susceptibility to overfishing (Russ and Alcala 1996).

Lutjanus is the most diverse genus within the lutjanidae family, comprising 114 recognised species (Froese and Pauly 2025). These fish are primarily found in inshore reef areas, sandy bays and estuaries. A recently described species is the yellowfin snapper, Lutjanus xanthopinnis Iwatsuki, Tanaka & Allen, 2015 (urn:lsid:zoobank.org:act:DE9BB7ED‐04B0‐49D0‐9D67‐1DB0EF9769FA), which was previously called Lutjanus madras (Iwatsuki et al. 2015). It is common throughout the Indo‐Pacific, where it is fished commercially and consumed. Recent population genetics studies indicate that L. xanthopinnis exists as a single, widespread population across the Indo‐Pacific (Arai et al. 2023). Despite its economic importance, there remains limited information on its biology, ecology and stock levels compared to other snapper species.

Reproductive biology, such as spawning period, sex ratio, maturation and potential fecundity, is crucial for understanding variations in specific fish populations. It plays a key role in analysing fish population dynamics, especially for exploited species (Alonso‐Fernandez et al. 2011). From an evolutionary perspective, assessing these traits is fundamental to testing life‐history theory, as variation in reproductive effort reflects adaptive responses to local environmental pressures and energetic trade‐offs. Key reproductive characteristics like spawning frequency help inform management strategies to safeguard critical life stages. Moreover, information on spawning frequency is essential for fisheries assessments, such as estimating spawning stock biomass per recruit, annual reproductive output, or spawning potential ratios (Sadovy de Mitcheson et al. 2008). This information also aids in assessing overexploitation and overfishing levels.

Information on the reproductive biology of L. xanthopinnis remains scarce across its distribution range. To date, the only comprehensive data come from the east coast of Peninsular Malaysia, where Rahman et al. (2024) reported an extended spawning season from January to August, a male‐biased sex ratio and batch fecundity ranging from 16,405 to 94,357 eggs. In their study, the length at 50% maturity (L 50) was estimated at 14.53 cm TL for females and 20.56 cm TL for males. However, comprehensive reproductive parameters for this species have not yet been investigated in the southern South China Sea, particularly off the coast of Brunei Darussalam. Investigating this population offers a unique empirical case study for assessing intra‐specific life‐history plasticity, given that localised oceanographic and monsoonal regimes can strongly drive divergent reproductive adaptations in coral reef fishes.

The aim of this work was to investigate the reproductive biology (e.g., spawning season, sex ratio, size at maturity and fecundity) of the yellowfin snapper Lutjanus xanthopinnis off Borneo, Brunei Darussalam, in the southern South China Sea. By comparing our results with adjacent regional datasets, this study explores intra‐specific variability and adaptive strategies of this newly described tropical fish, thereby enhancing our understanding of its evolutionary ecology and providing critical, region‐specific parameters for effective marine resource management and conservation.

Materials and Methods

The location map was downloaded from the OpenStreetMap (open access) at https://www.openstreetmap.org. and edited in Adobe Illustrator.

The Institutional Ethics Committee at Universiti Brunei Darussalam (UBD) approved the animal study protocol (UBD/OVACRI/CRGWG(003)/161101). All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with UBD's ethics guidelines.

Study Site

The yellowfin snapper Lutjanus xanthopinnis (Figure 1) was collected from waters off Brunei Darussalam in the southern South China Sea (Figure 2). For fisheries management, the marine capture fishing area in Brunei Darussalam is divided into four operational zones based on the distance from the coastline: Zone 1 (0–3 nautical miles; nm), which is reserved for small‐scale traditional fishing; Zone 2 (3–20 nm); Zone 3 (20–45 nm); and Zone 4 (45–200 nm), which extends to the border of the Exclusive Economic Zone (EEZ) (Haji and Rahim 2015). The specimens were captured exclusively within Zone 1. This nearshore coastal zone is characterised by shallow waters (typically 10–20 m) and a soft‐bottom substrate of sandy‐muddy sediments and intertidal mudflats, heavily influenced by high river runoff and suspended terrestrial particulates from major river systems (Chua et al. 1987; Cinco et al. 2015). Natural nearshore coral reefs are relatively limited by high turbidity and sedimentation in the immediate coastal fringe, but scattered submerged patch reefs and artificial reef structures occur within approximately 4 to 25 km of the coast at depths exceeding 5 m (Chua et al. 1987; Allen 2009).

Freshly collected specimen of the yellowfin snapper, Lutjanus xanthopinnis, from the coastal waters of Brunei Darussalam.

The collection site of the yellowfin snapper Lutjanus xanthopinnis. The map illustrates the waters of Brunei Darussalam near Borneo Island in the southern South China Sea.

This region has a tropical monsoon climate with distinct wet (northeast monsoon, December–March) and dry (southwest monsoon, April–September) seasons. Heavy rainfall causes fluctuating salinity and high turbidity. Sea surface temperatures remain consistently warm at 28°C–30°C (Allen 2009). Zone 1 is strictly managed for artisanal, small‐scale fishing using gear such as handlines and traps, with commercial trawling prohibited, making it a critical coastal nursery (Salleh 1997; Cinco et al. 2015).

Study Design

All fish specimens were purchased monthly at the Jerudong Market, a local fish market situated near the fishing sites. The specimens used in this study were caught by local fishermen operating within Zone 1 and promptly landed at the market.

Fish

A total of 385 Lutjanus xanthopinnis specimens were collected monthly from September 2017 to August 2018. The monthly counts were as follows: September 2017 (31), October (36), November (31), December (35), January 2018 (31), February (31), March (32), April (32), May (34), June (30), July (32) and August (30). These figures demonstrate a consistent and objective sampling effort, with at least 30 specimens collected per month throughout the study period. After collection, the specimens were taken to the laboratory for identification, following the methods described in Allen (2009) and Iwatsuki et al. (2015).

The total length (TL) and body weight (BW) of each fish were measured with an accuracy of 0.01 cm and 0.01 g, respectively. The fish's abdomens were dissected to remove and weigh the gonads of both females and males using an analytical balance precise to 0.001 g. The sex of each fish was determined by macro observation of the gonads and confirmed by histological analysis.

For subsequent analyses, the ovary of each female specimen was longitudinally sectioned into two halves. To ensure optimal tissue fixation for histological assessment of the maturation stage, one half was immediately preserved in an individual vial containing 10% neutral buffered formalin (Hunter et al. 1985; Pradeep 2017). The remaining half of the ovary was utilised for fecundity analysis.

The gonadosomatic index (GSI), which shows the proportion of gonad weight to total body weight, was determined. It represents the percentage of gonad weight (GW) in relation to the fish's body weight (BW). For the females sampled, GSI was computed using the formula: GSI = (GW/BW) × 100.

Histology

All specimens underwent histological analysis following standard procedures outlined by Slaoui and Fiette (2011), Arai and Abdul Kadir et al. (2017) and Arai et al. (2025). Resin‐embedded samples were processed routinely, while paraffin‐embedded sections, sliced at 4–5 μm thickness, were stained with haematoxylin‐eosin for microscopic evaluation. Each slide was examined using a light microscope (Nikon Eclipse L200N; Nikon Metrology Inc., USA), and photographs were taken to document the observations accurately.

Gonadal development stages in L. xanthopinnis were classified using the histological criteria described by Brown‐Peterson et al. (2011). In females, ovarian development was classified into six observed stages: immature (Stage 0), early maturing (Stage I), mid‐maturing (Stage II), late maturing (Stage III), fully maturing (Stage IV) and spent (Stage V) (Figure 3). The regenerating stage (Stage VI) was not observed during the study period and has therefore been excluded from the description and figures. Maturation stages were identified at the cellular level by examining oogonia, primary growth oocytes, vitellogenic oocytes and post‐ovulatory follicles.

The development of testicles in males was divided into four observed stages: early germinal epithelium development (Stage II), mid germinal epithelium development (Stage III), late germinal epithelium development (Stage IV) and spent stage (Stage V) (Figure 4). Immature (Stage 0) and preparatory (Stage I) stages were excluded because they were not detected. Male staging was confirmed by the presence of spermatogonia, spermatocytes, spermatids and spermatozoa.

Fecundity

The female mature gonads were assessed for fecundity using the hydrated oocyte method (Lowerre‐Barbieri and Barbieri 1993). Three subsamples were taken from the anterior, middle and posterior regions after weighing each ovary (Abdul Kadir et al. 2017; Arai et al. 2025). Each subsample was weighed to the nearest 0.001 g and preserved in 4% neutral buffered formalin. Then, each subsample slide was examined using a macroscopic staging system, and the total oocyte number was recorded (Abdul Kadir et al. 2017). The oocyte count in each subsample (E1, E2, E3) was estimated with the formula: E1, E2, or E3 = (oocytes in subsample × gonad weight)/subsample weight. The fish's fecundity (F) was calculated as follows: F = (E1 + E2 + E3)/3.

Length at 50% Maturity

The TL (cm) at 50% sexual maturity for both females and males was estimated using the logistic equation, following the methods of Karna and Panda (2011), Mili et al. (2011) and Almamari et al. (2017). Proportional frequencies were modelled with a two‐parameter logistic curve as shown below. P=1/1+exp–aL–L50 where P is the proportion of mature individuals at TL, a is the constant parameter affecting the maturity curve's slope, and L 50 is the total length at which half of the individuals are mature. The L 50 value was estimated by solving for the coefficient a in the equation using the Excel add‐in ‘Solver’ (Karna and Panda 2011). The goodness of fit of the logistic model was evaluated using the coefficient of determination (r 2), which assessed the correlation between the observed proportions of mature individuals and the proportions predicted by the model.

Statistics

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 22. Deviation of the sex ratio from 1:1 was tested using a chi‐squared test. Monthly changes in gonad maturation stages and differences in mean oocyte diameter at maturation Stage IV were analysed using a one‐way ANOVA, followed by Tukey's post hoc tests to assess significance at α = 0.05. Pearson's correlation was used to assess relationships between GSI and maturation stages, and between fecundity and body size.

Results

Sex Ratio

Lutjanus xanthopinnis was observed year‐round in the southern South China Sea, with 205 females and 180 males recorded, leading to a sex ratio of 1.16:1. No significant deviation from the expected 1:1 ratio was found (Chi‐square test, χ 2 = 1.6234, df = 1, p = 0.2026).

GSI and Histology in Females

The female gonadosomatic index (GSI) ranged from 0.25 to 5.59, exhibiting significant monthly variations (Figure 5a, Table 1). GSI values in March and April were significantly higher than those in February, May and August through December (one‐way ANOVA, F(11, 193) = 10.9626, p < 0.001). Conversely, no significant differences were observed among January, June and July (p > 0.05).

Ovarian development was classified into six stages based on cellular characteristics (Figure 3). The immature stage (Stage 0) was characterised by the presence of oogonia (Og), whereas the early maturing stage (Stage I) was marked by the appearance of cortical alveoli (CA). The accumulation of yolk granules (YG) and fused yolk (FY) defined progression from the mid‐ to the fully maturing stages (Stages II–IV). The spent stage (Stage V) was characterised by the presence of post‐ovulatory follicles (POFs).

Histological analysis revealed that fully maturing individuals (Stage IV) were present in October, November and from January to August (Figure 6a). Based on these monthly fluctuations in GSI and histological stages, females exhibited a prolonged and distinct peak spawning season extending from October to August.

Mean GSI values increased with advancing maturation stages, showing a significant positive correlation (Pearson's correlation, r = 0.5742, df = 203, p < 0.001; Table 1). The highest GSI was recorded at Stage IV (fully maturing), which was significantly higher than at all other stages (one‐way ANOVA, F(5, 199) = 30.8943, p < 0.001), except for Stage III. The lowest GSI occurred at Stage 0 (immature), which did not differ significantly from Stages I, II and V (p > 0.05).

The mean ± SD of total length (TL) across Stages 0–V ranged from 16.18 ± 2.65 cm to 19.17 ± 1.39 cm, while body weight (BW) ranged from 58.04 ± 25.83 g to 88.47 ± 15.34 g (see Table 1 for details per stage). Statistically significant differences in both TL and BW were observed between the maturation stages (one‐way ANOVA for TL: F(5, 199) = 13.4120, p < 0.001; for BW: F(5, 199) = 10.6709, p < 0.001).

Oocyte Development and Fecundity

Oocyte diameter significantly increased alongside gonadal maturation (Pearson's correlation, r = 0.8346, df = 203, p < 0.001; Table 1). The largest oocyte diameters were observed at Stage IV. Immature oocytes (Stage 0) were significantly smaller than those at Stages III, IV and V (one‐way ANOVA, F(5, 199) = 288.1331, p < 0.001), but showed no significant size differences compared to Stages I, II and V (p > 0.05).

A total of 39 fully mature (Stage IV) females with intact, unovulated ovaries were examined for fecundity analysis. The average fecundity of L. xanthopinnis was estimated at 2.8 × 104 ± 1.3 × 104 eggs, ranging from 8 × 103 to 6.5 × 104. Pearson correlation analysis revealed no statistically significant relationship between total fecundity and maternal body size (for total length (TL): r = 0.2309, p = 0.1573; for body weight (BW): r = 0.2370, p = 0.1463).

GSI and Histology in Males

The male GSI ranged from 0.12 to 6.13 (Figure 5b, Table 1). Monthly GSI values peaked significantly in March compared to May, June and August through December (one‐way ANOVA, F(11, 168) = 9.8732, p < 0.001). No significant differences were detected between March and January, February, April or July (p > 0.05).

The testicles were divided into four developmental stages (Figure 4). Early‐stage development (Stage II) was characterised by the presence of spermatogonia (Sg), which progressed to the appearance of spermatozoa (Sz) in Stage III. In Stage IV, the lobules were densely packed with spermatozoa (Sz), whereas the spent stage (Stage V) was identified by residual spermatozoa (RSz).

Histologically, the late germinal epithelium stage (Stage IV) was observed year‐round except in January (Figure 6b). Based on these monthly fluctuations in GSI and histological stages, males exhibited continuous reproductive activity throughout the year, with no distinct chronological peak.

Mean GSI values did not differ significantly among male maturation stages (one‐way ANOVA, F(3, 87) = 1.0967, p = 0.3550; Table 1). No statistically significant correlation was observed between testis maturation stages and GSI in males (Pearson's correlation, r = −0.0980, df = 89, p = 0.3556).

Based on monthly fluctuations in GSI and histological stages, males exhibited continuous reproductive activity throughout the year, with no distinct chronological peak.

For males, the mean ± SD of TL across stages II to V ranged from 17.81 ± 2.29 cm to 18.62 ± 1.65 cm and BW ranged from 76.26 ± 22.66 g to 82.63 ± 19.76 g (Table 1). No significant variations in body size were detected among the maturation stages (one‐way ANOVA for TL: F(3, 87) = 1.0594, p = 0.3707; for BW: F(3, 87) = 1.1091, p = 0.3499).

Length at 50% Maturity

The estimated total length at 50% maturity (L 50) for females was 18.3 cm (Figure 7a). The non‐linear logistic regression model for females showed an exceptionally high goodness‐of‐fit (r 2 = 0.99, p < 0.001), reflecting a clear, size‐dependent progression in maturity. In contrast, the standard logistic regression model for males yielded a calculated mathematical L 50 of 18.8 cm with an apparent high fit (r 2 = 0.98, p < 0.001; Figure 7b); however, this estimation carries a distinct biological caveat. An audit of the complete male dataset (n = 180) revealed that immature or early‐developing individuals (Stages 0–II) were virtually absent in our samples, with only 3 individuals in total. Notably, 98.3% of the captured males (177 out of 180 individuals) were already fully mature (Stages III–V), and even the smallest individuals sampled (down to 11.2 cm TL) exhibited fully developed Stage V testes. Consequently, due to the complete lack of an immature baseline in smaller size classes to construct a gradual “0%–100%” transition curve, the male maturity model represents a near‐instantaneous maturity regime across all captured sizes rather than a size‐dependent progression, which mathematically precluded the calculation of asymptotic 95% confidence intervals.

Discussion

This study is the first to examine the reproductive biology of the yellowfin snapper, Lutjanus xanthopinnis, off Borneo and Brunei Darussalam (4°–5° N) in the southern South China Sea. Both females and males showed nearly year‐round spawning. Females' peak spawning occurred nearly year‐round from October to August, whereas males did not show a clear peak. This year‐round reproductive pattern contrasts with a recent study of the same species on the east coast of Peninsular Malaysia (1°–7° N), where spawning activity was more restricted and ceased during the peak monsoon season from September to December (Rahman et al. 2024). Our observed continuous pattern resembles that of other Lutjanidae species at similar low latitudes, such as the bigeye snapper L. lutjanus (8°–12° N; Pradeep 2016), the brownstripe red snapper L. vitta in New Caledonia (20°–22° S; Loubens 1980) and the Philippines (10°–15° N; Palla and Sotto 2021), which spawn throughout the year in the Indo‐Pacific (Table 2). This year‐round reproductive pattern in Brunei Darussalam waters can be robustly explained by the localised oceanographic stability characteristic of the southern South China Sea basin. According to Zu et al. (2020), the mature summer circulation in the South China Sea features a competitive bifurcation near the Vietnamese coast (around 12° N), driving a powerful eastward offshore jet and an associated anticyclonic recirculation in the southern basin. By contrast, adjacent regions such as the east coast of Peninsular Malaysia (1°–7° N) are strongly influenced by shifting monsoonal wind‐stress curls and intense jet oscillations, whereas the nearshore coastal fringe of Brunei Darussalam (4°–5° N) remains buffered within a relatively stable, warm tropical marine environment. This localised oceanographic setting minimises severe seasonal stress, thereby permitting the continuous, non‐monsoon‐restricted reproductive regime observed in the Brunei population of L. xanthopinnis.

Multiple studies indicate that fish in warm‐temperate and tropical waters often spawn year‐round (e.g., Loubens 1980; Y. J. Sadovy 1996; Freitas et al. 2011; Arai and Abdul Kadir 2017; Palla and Sotto 2021). Thus, L. xanthopinnis was expected to reproduce year‐round in the southern South China Sea, though spawning may be skipped under adverse conditions (Rick and Tomkiewicz 2011). Conversely, more distinct seasonal spawning patterns are observed in Lutjanidae at higher latitudes, such as October to February in Queensland's mangrove jack snapper, L. argentimaculatus (10°–20° S; Russell and McDougall 2008), June to October in L. decussatus in Okinawa (24°–26° N; Nanami et al. 2010) and November to April or April to June in Kenya's blackspot snapper, L. flaviflamma (Kaunda‐Arara and Ntiba 1997) and in Okinawa (26°–28° N; Shimose and Tachihara 2005) (Table 2). Such variations in reproductive timing, seasons and peaks among Lutjanidae and even within the same species across different geographic locations, likely reflect differences in localised spawning strategies and environmental conditions associated with latitudinal gradients (Rahman et al. 2024). Key influences include water temperature and photoperiod, which show greater seasonal fluctuations at higher latitudes and affect spawning timing and duration (Grimes 1987; Head et al. 2014; Rahman et al. 2024). Further research on the spatial and temporal variability of L. xanthopinnis's spawning will deepen understanding of its reproductive biology.

Coral reef fishes display diverse reproductive patterns across multiple time scales (Munro et al. 1973; Thresher 1984; Robertson 1991; Y. J. Sadovy 1996; Claydon et al. 2014; Abesamis et al. 2015). In this study, L. xanthopinnis was found to spawn nearly year‐round, suggesting that conditions may support pelagic larval survival outside the monsoon seasons (Abesamis et al. 2015). However, a seasonal reproductive pattern persisted in Lutjanidae and other coral reef fishes, as reproductive cycles in demersal and pelagic fishes, and invertebrates, are often affected by the monsoon (Johannes 1978; Nzioka 1979; Pauly and Navaluna 1983; McClanahan 1988; McIlwain et al. 2006; Abesamis and Russ 2010; Abesamis et al. 2015; Anand and Reddy 2017). For instance, Arai et al. (2025) reported that female Java Rabbitfish, Siganus javus , spawn from June to August in Brunei Darussalam, aligning with the southwest monsoon season, which features minimal rainfall and weaker winds from April to September (Allen 2009).

The observed year‐round spawning in L. xanthopinnis, despite seasonal trends in other species, may reflect its ecological characteristics. Unlike shallow‐water species such as S. javus , which are more susceptible to monsoon‐driven fluctuations in salinity and turbidity, L. xanthopinnis may inhabit more stable, deeper waters that buffer it against such seasonal stressors. These results highlight significant interspecific differences in spawning biology among coral reef fishes within the same habitat. While our market‐based sampling may have reflected catches from more sheltered fishing grounds during peak monsoon months, the consistent monthly collection of 30–36 mature individuals suggests that reproductive activity at the population level remains robust throughout the year.

This study spanned over a year, during which the immature stage (Stage 0) and early maturing stage (Stage I) in males were not observed. Coral reef fishes, such as snappers, generally inhabit seagrass beds and mangroves during their immature phases, migrating to coral reefs as they approach the pre‐adult stage (Ogden and Thomas 1984). The absence of these early maturation stages in male L. xanthopinnis likely reflects different habitat preferences and migration patterns during early life. Additionally, habitat use may differ between females and males, as all maturation stages in females were consistently found in the same habitat (fishing ground) throughout the year. This potential sexual segregation in habitat use has been observed in various marine fishes and is often attributed to differing energetic requirements or reproductive strategies between the sexes (Wearmouth and Sims 2008). Sexual segregation in coral reef fishes is often driven by differing reproductive costs between the sexes. While females tend to occupy stable, food‐rich habitats to maximise egg production, males may use different areas to reduce intraspecific competition or manage predation risk (Warner 1984). Such spatial partitioning has significant implications for fisheries management, as seen in certain serranids, where non‐spawning distributions differ markedly from spawning aggregations (Beets and Friedlander 1999). By occupying different niches, the species can reduce competition for food and space within the same fishing ground, thereby optimising the overall fitness of the population.

The sex ratio between males and females showed no significant difference. A ratio approaching 1:1 suggests a balanced fish population with similar numbers of males and females. This result aligns with findings in other Lutjanidae species, such as the blackspot snapper L. fulviflamma in Tanzania (Kamukuru and Mgaya 2004) and the lane snapper L. synagris in Brazil (Freitas et al. 2014). Interestingly, this balanced sex ratio contrasts with the population on the east coast of Peninsular Malaysia, where L. xanthopinnis exhibited a significantly male‐biased sex ratio of 1:0.75 (Rahman et al. 2024). Such regional discrepancies within the same species may stem from variations in localised fishing pressures, sex‐specific mortality rates, or sampling habitats. An equal sex ratio may facilitate both genders in finding mates before spawning, potentially boosting reproductive success. Conversely, an uneven sex ratio during the reproductive season, such as that reported by Rahman et al. (2024), can alter population dynamics and lead to increased competition for mates (Lima 2018).

At the maturation stage (Stage IV), female L. xanthopinnis show larger TL and BW than males. This pattern is consistent with other Lutjanidae species, such as L. fulviflamma us in Kenya (Kaunda‐Arara and Ntiba 1997), L. analis in Florida, USA (Burton 2002) and L. argentimaculatus in Australia (Russell and McDougall 2008). This size dimorphism may reflect increased energy investment in reproduction, as oocyte development advances markedly with maturation. Remarkably, this intersexual size relationship is completely reversed in the Peninsular Malaysia population, where L. xanthopinnis males mature at a substantially larger size (20.56 cm TL) than females (14.53 cm TL) (Rahman et al. 2024). Such contrasting intraspecific variation in size at maturity across regions highlights the high plasticity of this species' reproductive traits under different environmental regimes. These differences in size at maturity are closely linked to localised reproductive strategies aimed at boosting reproductive success (Barson et al. 2015). Life‐history theory indicates that delayed maturation can be disadvantageous because fish require more time to grow larger (Roff 1992; Stamps and Krishnan 1997). To enhance reproductive success, females in the Brunei population appear to feed longer and mature later, reaching larger sizes, since larger females usually achieve higher reproductive success than males (Barson et al. 2015). Additionally, larger females produce larger, higher‐quality eggs (Diego et al. 2018). Lutjanus xanthopinnis off Brunei Darussalam adopts this size‐maximisation strategy to optimise fecundity, whereas the Malaysian population may be under different ecological or fishing pressures that favour larger males (Rahman et al. 2024).

The highly significant differences in both TL and BW across maturation stages (p < 0.001) demonstrate a clear, size‐dependent ontogenetic progression in the reproductive development of L. xanthopinnis. This somatic‐growth‐linked maturation pattern aligns with standard teleost reproductive strategies, ensuring that individuals attain sufficient energy reserves and physical capacity before undertaking energy‐intensive spawning (Grimes 1987; Y. J. Sadovy 1996).

The TL at 50% maturity (L 50 values) estimated in this study was generally smaller than those reported in earlier research on Lutjanidae: L. fulviflammaus in Tanzania (20.6–21.7 cm in females and 19.6–22.1 cm in males) (Kamukuru and Mgaya 2004); L. argentimacuiatus in Australia (53.1 cm in females and 47.1 cm in males) (Russell and McDougall 2008); Lutjanus synagris in Brazil (23.0 cm in females and 24.2 cm in males) (Freitas et al. 2014); the blue‐lined snapper L. coeruleolineatus in Oman (29.7 cm in females and 28.1 cm in males) (Almamari et al. 2017); and L. vitta (Palla and Sotto 2021) in the Philippines. However, the size was similar to that of L. synagris in Brazil (18.1 cm in females and 18.7 cm in males) (Freitas et al. 2014). More importantly, compared with the same species in a different geographic region, our estimated L 50 values (18.3 cm for females and 18.8 cm for males) differed markedly from those of the population on the east coast of Peninsular Malaysia, where females matured at a smaller size (14.53 cm TL) and males at a larger size (20.56 cm TL) (Rahman et al. 2024). Furthermore, while Rahman et al. (2024) demonstrated a clear intersexual difference in maturity size, our results showed that the size at biological maturity was nearly identical between the sexes. These findings suggest that size at maturity is largely species‐specific, and L. xanthopinnis can exhibit high intraspecific plasticity depending on the locality. Environmental factors such as water temperature, population size and food availability can influence sexual maturation and growth rates, as previously documented for Lutjanus synagris in Brazil (Freitas et al. 2011, 2014) and now observed in L. xanthopinnis within the South China Sea (Rahman et al. 2024). Regional differences in population dynamics and localised habitat productivity likely drive these variations in size at maturity between the Brunei and Peninsular Malaysia populations.

Lutjanus xanthopinnis exhibits fecundity ranging from 8000 to 65,000. Similar, overlapping ranges are reported for L. vitta from India (3717–56,090) (Pradeep 2017) and the Philippines (11,596–251,798) (Palla and Sotto 2021). Higher fecundities are reported for L. argentimacuiatus (250,000–4,000,000) (Russell and McDougall 2008) and L. synagris (104,743–568,400) (Freitas et al. 2014). Compared with the same species in Peninsular Malaysia, our estimated fecundity range is similar to that reported by Rahman et al. (2024), who observed a range of 16,405–94,357 eggs. However, a major biological discrepancy concerns the relationship between fecundity and maternal body size. While Rahman et al. (2024) demonstrated that fecundity correlated positively and significantly with fish size (TL and BW), our results showed no significant correlation between these parameters. Within Lutjanidae, fecundity generally correlates positively with fish size (Kaunda‐Arara and Ntiba 1997; Freitas et al. 2014; Pradeep 2017), as observed in larger L. argentimaculatus (Russell and McDougall 2008) and L. synagris (Freitas et al. 2014). The absence of a size‐fecundity correlation in the Brunei population is unusual within this family. This absence suggests that factors other than body size—such as individual energetic condition, fluctuating food availability, or fractional spawning, in which females release eggs in multiple batches—may play a more dominant role in determining batch fecundity in the southern South China Sea ecosystem.

This study elucidated the reproductive biology—including spawning seasonality, gonadal development, sex ratio and fecundity—of the yellowfin snapper Lutjanus xanthopinnis in the southern South China Sea off Brunei Darussalam. By integrating our results with recent regional data from Peninsular Malaysia (Rahman et al. 2024), this work highlights remarkable intra‐specific plasticity in the reproductive strategies of L. xanthopinnis. While the Malaysian population exhibits monsoon‐restricted spawning and distinct intersexual size differences, the Brunei population adopts a prolonged, nearly year‐round spawning pattern and a more balanced biological maturity between the sexes.

Reproductive biology plays a vital role in shaping broader life‐history traits, such as developmental rates, predation risks and migratory behaviours. Consequently, our findings demonstrate that local environmental regimes and oceanographic conditions can drive significant ecological and evolutionary variation in the life cycle of coral reef fishes, even within closely situated geographic regions. Understanding this intra‐specific variability is key to predicting how tropical fish populations adapt to changing marine environments, and it provides indispensable baseline data for designing effective, region‐specific conservation policies and sustainable fisheries management for this commercially important species.
