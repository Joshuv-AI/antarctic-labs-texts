Patterns and changes in lifestyle habits of patients with rheumatoid arthritis during the first three years post-diagnosis
Authors: Marie-Louise Karlsson, Simon Steiger, Helga Westerlind, Lars Klareskog, Lars Alfredsson, Susanne Pettersson, Daniela Di Giuseppe, Katerina Chatzidionysiou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to, in patients with rheumatoid arthritis (RA), describe patterns and changes in healthy lifestyle habits (HLHs: alcohol use, smoking and physical activity) from diagnosis of RA to three years post-diagnosis, and to identify factors associated with having multiple HLHs.MethodsPatients’ data were collected from the Swedish Epidemiological Investigation of RA (EIRA). Physical activity (PA) was categorized to be on a healthy level if >120 min/week at moderate intensity. Smoking was categorized as smoking/non-smoking. Alcohol consumption of <120 g/week was deemed healthy. Participants were grouped by number of HLHs: none, one, two or three. Factors associated with having three HLHs at year three were analysed using logistic factor regression.ResultsAmong 1097 participants, 56% had three HLHs at baseline, increasing slightly to 58% at year three. Non-smoking increased from 77% to 83%, and healthy alcohol use from 85% to 97%. However, the proportion of participants who achieved a healthy level PA decreased from 84% to 71%. Having three HLHs at year three was associated with younger age [OR = 2.45 (95% CI = 1.50–4.00) for <35 vs 55–65 years] and living with another adult age [OR = 1.68 (95% CI = 1.25–2.26)].ConclusionMore than half of the participants had three HLHs at diagnosis and maintained them after three years. Smoking and alcohol habits improved, but the proportion of participants who achieved a healthy level PA decreased. Older patients may need more support in adopting and maintaining HLHs.

Introduction

Rheumatoid arthritis (RA) is a chronic autoimmune disease that can lead to significant morbidity and increased mortality. If not adequately treated, RA is associated with a doubled risk of cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) [1, 2]. Active RA inflammation and traditional risk factors for CVD—physical inactivity, obesity and smoking—contribute to this increased risk [3]. Adhering to healthy lifestyle habits (HLHs)—alcohol use, smoking and physical activity—is crucial [4].

HLHs include no-smoking habits [5], consuming 10 standard alcoholic drinks or less per week [6], engaging in at least 150 min/week of moderate intensity physical activity (PA) or 75 min/week of high-intensity PA [7] and a diet rich in fish, vegetables and fruit, with minimal sugar [6]. Having multiple unhealthy HLH can raise the risk of CVDs in patients with established RA [8]. Apart from the risk for comorbidities, lifestyle habits can impact RA symptoms, treatment responses and prognosis. In early post-diagnosis years, smokers have higher disease activity [9] and increased disease burden (e.g. articular and extra-articular manifestations) [9, 10], and smoking cessation is strongly recommended [4]. PA has positive effects on pain and activity limitation [11]. High alcohol intake may increase infection risk [12] and, combined with pharmacotherapy, increase hepatotoxicity risk [13]. Healthcare professionals should support their patients’ early adoption of HLHs [4, 14].

This study investigated lifestyle habit patterns among patients with RA living in the south and middle of Sweden during their initial post-diagnosis years, including (i) the proportion of patients reporting HLH regarding alcohol consumption, smoking habits and PA at disease onset and at year three; (ii) the proportion showing positive lifestyle changes and/or maintaining HLHs from disease onset to year three; and (iii) predictors of changes and/or maintaining HLHs, including social factors (e.g. social support, employment and education).

Methods

Study population

This study used data from the ongoing Epidemiological Investigation of Rheumatoid Arthritis (EIRA) project [15]. Inclusion criteria were a clinical RA diagnosis and Swedish literacy. Only participants recruited between year 2007 and year 2016 were included in the current study due to changes in the survey questions pertaining to lifestyle habits, and those who answered both the questionnaire at baseline and the year three follow-up.

Exposure (lifestyle habits)

Physical activity (PA)

At baseline (i.e. time of RA diagnosis), participants reported their PA levels during the previous year by selecting one of four predefined groups: (i) ‘sedentary leisure’; (ii) ‘moderate physical activity’; (iii) ‘moderate exercise’; or (iv) ‘vigorous activity’. In year three, the participants provided details about their weekly engagement in light-intensity activities (e.g. slow walking), moderate-intensity activities (e.g. light walking or cycling, causing slight breathlessness and sweating) and high-intensity activities (e.g. jogging or cycling causing breathlessness and sweating), including daily duration in minutes and hours. The year three responses were converted into the baseline groups for comparison, as presented in detail in Supplementary Table S1. Study participants in groups two to four were considered to meet the criteria of healthy level of PA in this study. Based on the design of the PA questions at baseline, a threshold of 120 min/week of moderate-intensity PA was defined. Participants also reported their PA levels five years before inclusion (Supplementary Table S2).

Smoking

Both at baseline and year three, the participants answered questions about their smoking habits and were classified as current, former or never smokers. Never and former smokers were considered as having healthy smoking habit (Supplementary Table S2).

Alcohol

The participants reported their alcohol consumption, including frequency (from ‘never’ to ‘several times per day’) type (e.g. beer, wine, spirits), and centilitres on each occasion. Alcohol intake (in centilitres) was converted into grams per week, with participants categorized as consuming over or under 120 g/week, where ≤120 g/week was considered healthy alcohol consumption. The definition of a standard drink (12 g of alcohol) followed national clinical guidelines and is routinely used for alcohol screening, risk classification and public health surveillance in Sweden [6].

Number of (HLH)

The participants were categorized as having zero, one, two or three HLHs at baseline and year three, respectively. Subgroups were created for participants who had three HLHs at baseline but fewer at year three and for those who did not have three HLHs at baseline but had increased their HLHs by year three. Subgroups were also created for participants who reported smoking cessation and for those who achieving a healthy level of PA to year three.

Covariates

Demographics and disease characteristics were collected at baseline. Age was divided into five categories (i.e. <35, 36–45, 46–55, 56–65 and >65 years). Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg)/height2 (m2) and defined as overweight/obese vs not overweight (BMI >24.9 vs ≤ 24.9). Patients also reported comorbidities, CVDs and type one or two diabetes. Work status was categorized as (i) employed, self-employed or student; (ii) sick leave/unemployed, disability pension or involuntarily unemployed. Participants were also categorized based on university education and cohabitation status (i.e. living with another adult). Baseline sera were screened for IgG anti-CCP2 using CCPlus® ELISA (Euro-Diagnostica AB, Malmö, Sweden), and positivity was defined as a level ≥25 arbitrary units/ml following the manufacturer’s instructions. Rheumatoid factor (RF) status was determined locally and reported by the clinician. Seropositivity was defined as being positive for anti-CCP2 and/or RF. We also included information about PA five years before diagnoses of RA.

Statistical analyses

Descriptive statistics were used to describe the different subgroups. Means and standard deviations (SD) were used for continuous data, medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) were used when the data were not normally distributed, and categorical data were summarized using frequencies and percentages.

Multivariable logistic regression models were used to analyse the covariates associated with (i) having three HLHs at year three; (ii) smoking cessation by year three; and (iii) maintaining meeting the criteria of healthy level of PA at year three. Model A was adjusted for age, sex and each of the following covariates separately (seropositivity, BMI, university degree, living without another adult, work status, CVDs, diabetes and the proportion of participants who achieve a healthy level PA five years before inclusion) while Model B was adjusted for all the above variables and covariates.

For the regression analysis of smoking cessation, age dichotomized (≤56 vs > 56 years) due to few numbers in the analysis and in order to decrease the statistical uncertainty. Baseline smoking, alcohol consumption and PA were considered mediators for having three HLHs at year three and were explored in a stratified analysis.

Statistical analyses were conducted using SAS software (v. 9; StataCorp, Cary, NC, USA).

No imputation was performed for missing data. The number of missing data was small; the numbers of missing data is described in Tables 1 and 2.

Sensitivity analysis

To compare the habits among the participants who developed RA until 2012 and after. As general awareness of the importance of HLHs for optimizing health outcomes in patients with RA increased during the data collection period (2007–2016), and as the Swedish National Board of Health and Welfare issued recommendations in 2011 encouraging healthcare professionals across the healthcare system to provide lifestyle-related counselling, we conducted a stratified analysis by year of RA diagnosis (before and after 2012).

Ethics

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants and approved by the Regional Ethical Review Board in Stockholm (registration numbers 1996–174 2007/889–31/ and 2015/1844–31/2).

Results

A total of 1097 participants responded to the baseline and year three questionnaire. Table 1 summarizes baseline characteristics.

Changes in the number of HLHs

Over 50% of the participants had three HLHs at baseline and year three (Table 2). A similar proportion (19%) of participants decreased or increased their HLHs by one category, while less than 1% decreased and 3% participants increased their HLHs by more than one category.

Fig. 1A illustrates the changes in number of HLHs from baseline to year three. Although the proportion of participants with three, two or one HLH remained stable, there was considerable movement between categories. All participants with no HLHs and the majority of those with one HLH increased their HLHs from baseline to year three. Most participants with two or three HLHs maintained or improved their HLHs by year three. However, 27% of those with three and 12% of those with two HLHs at baseline reported a decrease in their level of HLH. PA was the main lifestyle habit driving the changes in HLHs (Fig. 1C).

Combination of two HLHs

Over time, the proportion with both healthy smoking and healthy alcohol habits increased from 30% to 65%, whereas combinations including achieving a healthy level of physical activity declined, notably healthy smoking with physical activity (28% to 5%) and physical activity with healthy alcohol habits (42% to 30%) (Supplementary Table S4).

Alcohol consumption

At baseline, the participants consumed an average of 69 g alcohol/week (SD = 88; median = 43; IQR = 70), which dropped to 32 g/week (SD = 35; median = 24; IQR = 48) by year three. The proportion who consumed <120 g alcohol per week increased from 85% to 97% in year three (see Fig. 1B, Table 2). Sixty-one participants did not respond to the baseline question about alcohol consumption and were excluded from alcohol-related analyses and baseline HLH classification. However, they were included in the regression analyses (having three HLH at year three) if they reported alcohol habits at year three. Those who did not answer the question about alcohol habits at baseline appeared to be women (82%) and more were RF-positive (77%) and ACPA-positive (75%), but they showed the same proportion of healthy smoking habits at baseline (75%) and met the criteria of healthy level of PA (83%) (Supplementary Table S3).

Physical activity

The percentage of participants who reported achieving a healthy level of PA decreased from 84% at baseline to 71% by year three (Table 2). However, some participants increased their level of PA while others decreased their level of PA between baseline and year three.

Out of 177 participants who were in the group with insufficient PA at baseline, 55% had improved their PA levels by year three. In the groups ‘moderate PA’ and ‘moderate vigorous PA’, 43% and 42% increased their PA; however, 32% and 39% of the participants in the groups ‘moderate PA’ and ‘moderate vigorous PA’ decreased their PA, respectively. Most of the participants in the ‘extensive vigorous PA’ group (64%) maintained their level of PA (Fig. 1C).

Smoking

The proportion of healthy smoking habits increased from 77% to 83% to year three (Table 2). Among the smokers at baseline, 33% stopped smoking, while a very small group of previous smokers relapsed 3% (Fig. 1D). Among those who continued to smoke (67%), the average number of cigarettes per day decreased slightly from 12 cigarettes per day (SD = 8) at baseline to 11 cigarettes per day (SD = 7) at year three.

Sensitivity analysis

Participants with inclusion pre vs post the year 2012 demonstrated no differences in alcohol consumption, PA or having three HLHs at baseline. In particular, the proportions of current smokers at baseline remained similar (23% pre-2012 vs 22% post-2012); however, smoking cessation at year three increased from 30% in the group with inclusion pre-2012 to 37% in the post-2012.

Regression analysis

The regression analysis examined potential associations between demographic characteristics and health-related behaviours at year three (Table 3). Younger age and living with another adult were associated with having three HLH at year three, with smoking cessation at year three and achieving a healthy level of PA year three. Achieving a healthy level of PA five years before inclusion was positively associated with having three HLHs as well as achieving a healthy level of PA at year three. Sick leave/unemployed at baseline was negatively associated with achieving a healthy level of PA at year three and having three HLHs by year three. Seropositivity was negatively associated with achieving a healthy level of PA at year three, while a university degree was positively associated with having three HLH at year three.

Regression analysis; having three HLH, healthy PA and smoking cessation, at year three.

For participants with less than three HLHs at baseline who had increased their HLHs by year three, living with another adult [odds ratio (OR) = 1.98; 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.25–3.14] and age 35–45 years were covariates associated with increasing HLHs by year three (OR = 2.27; 95% CI: 1.12–4.62). In addition, age 45–55 years (OR = 1.77; 95% CI: 1.03–3.05) and achieving a healthy level of PA five years before inclusion (OR = 1.97; 95% CI: 1.02–3.79), were also associated with having three HLH at year three. Conversely, those aged >56 years were more likely to lose at least one HLH (OR = 1.84; 95% CI: 1.14–2.97) by year three.

Discussion

This study provides new insights into HLH changes between RA diagnosis and three years into the disease. Over half of the participants maintained three HLHs from baseline to year three, but a substantial proportion of patients do not. All participants with no HLHs and most participants with only one HLH at baseline improved their HLHs by year three. While the groups with healthy smoking and healthy alcohol habits increased, the proportion of participants achieving a healthy level of PA decreased.

The European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) strongly recommends that patients with RA acquire and maintain HLHs [4]. To our knowledge, only two other studies have assessed HLHs in patients with inflammatory joint diseases [8, 16], but none have assessed patients with newly diagnosed RA and followed these during the first years after diagnosis. Our observation is comparable to that of Karstensen’s study [8], which measured alcohol habits, PA, BMI and smoking status in two cohorts of patients with established RA from Sweden and Denmark. In the Swedish cohort, 53% had three to four HLHs, while in the Danish cohort, 34% had three to four HLHs. However, there were notable differences between the studies. Karstensen et al. [8] included BMI as a lifestyle habit and used a higher cut-off for a healthy level of PA. PA demonstrated the greatest variability among the lifestyle habits in this study, as the proportion of participants with achieving a healthy level of PA decreased from 84% to 71%. Interestingly, the changes in PA went in both directions. Given that both pain and fatigue significantly affect the ability to be physically active in patients with RA, these factors may explain the observed increase of participants who do not exchange a healthy level of PA. The majority of those participants improved to a higher group, while >40% of participants in the moderate PA and moderate vigorous PA groups improved to higher groups. However, a substantial proportion of individuals also reported a decrease in their level of PA. It is therefore essential that healthcare professionals identify these patients and provide appropriate support to encourage the continuation of regular PA [17].

A total of 33% of patients ceased smoking during the first three years; however, some previous smokers at baseline relapsed and reported that they had resumed smoking. We have previously performed an interventional study on smoking cessation in RA, where 20% of the participants stopped smoking [18]. In a study based on The Nurses’ Health Study, it was reported that participants who received an RA diagnosis had a cessation rate of about 40% within the first four years, compare with 36% in non-RA cases [19]. In our present study, 17% of the participants were current smokers at year three compared with 6% in the general population in Sweden (year 2022) [20]. Moreover, we also observed that smoking cessation was more likely among the younger participants in our study. Considering the increased risk of CVD [21] and extra-articular manifestations [10] among smokers with RA, it is crucial to provide targeted support for smoking cessation.

Social factors are important to consider in relation to HLHs. In our study, living with another adult was associated with having three HLH at year three, quitting smoking and achieving a healthy level of PA at year three. Living with another adult may act as a proxy for social support, which is known to facilitate lifestyle changes [22, 23]. A potential explanation for the association between living with another adult and healthier lifestyle habits may be better financial circumstances and greater access to practical support. In addition, living with another adult is likely to be negatively associated with social loneliness, which is linked to depression. Depression, in turn, has been associated with lower levels of PA [24] and smoking [25]. Being on sick leave or unemployed was negatively associated with having three HLH at year three and being physically active at least at moderate intensity. Younger age was positively associated with having three HLH at year three, smoking cessation and achieving a healthy level of PA at year three. A review of PA in patients with RA reported mixed results for age [26]. Older people were suggested to have a higher nicotine dependence due to having smoked for a longer time [27]. Moreover, younger people may feel that they have more to gain when they quit smoking compared to people in an older age group. The muscle strength and aerobic capacity decline with older age, which also can be a possible explanation for younger age being associated with PA [28], which may act as barriers to PA.

Taken together, our findings highlight the importance of including social aspects when discussing and supporting patients in adopting HLH.

Limitations

Some limitations with this project should be acknowledged. First, as high socioeconomic status and higher education increase participation in research in general and EIRA in particular [29, 30], education [31] and higher income [32] are factors also associated to HLHs, which might lead to a higher percentage of patients with HLH. Public health reports have also found that people born outside Sweden were more likely to smoke and be less physically active, therefore in our study we may have underestimated numbers of participants who were smokers and participants with and not achieving a healthy level of PA [33]. Second, our study relied on self-reported data, which can introduce inaccuracies due to misunderstandings, memory errors or social desirability bias (that participants reported what they thought the researcher expected of them). Consequently, the prevalence of HLHs may be overestimated, as participants may have reported behaviours they perceived as socially desirable. The fact that the participants responded to the questionnaire at the time they were diagnosed with a chronic disease could also have influenced their responses, since patients might have reinterpreted their past behaviour differently. However, this misinterpretation can go towards both directions (over- and underestimating their lifestyle habits). For PA, a systematic review has shown that self-reported measures may both overestimate and underestimate true activity levels [34].

Additionally, different questions were used at baseline and year three to measure PA. This could weaken the longitudinal inference by introduction of potential measurement errors. We did our best to mitigate this by constructing groups that achieved conceptual comparability, i.e. category 1 represents individuals who do not meet the recommended level of healthy PA, category 2 represents those who meet the recommended level, and categories 3 and 4 represent progressively higher levels of structured exercise. It should be acknowledged that despite these groupings, differences between the PA levels at baseline and follow-up might still be present. However, despite this weakness, our primary aim was to capture overall changes in physical activity patterns over time. Accordingly, we focused on shifts between recreational physical activity and structured exercise, as well as on participants’ ability to achieve recommended levels of physical activity for healthy PA. Based on the instructions in the questionnaire and the underlying conceptual framework, we consider these distinctions to reflect meaningful and adequately comparable gradations in PA and the effect on the study to be limited.

In collaboration with experienced epidemiologist/biostatisticians and physiotherapists, the continuous data from year three were transformed to the baseline categories. Achieving a healthy level of PA was defined as ≥120 min/week in this study instead of the standard 150 min/week, which may have overestimated the prevalence of participants that achieve a healthy level of PA.

Because this study relies on relatively broad, threshold-based categories for lifestyle behaviours, participants may have made meaningful changes without necessarily moving from one category to another. Consequently, some within-category variation in PA, alcohol consumption or smoking behaviour may not be fully captured.

Another limitation in our study is the lack of information on disease activity. Seropositivity in our study was negatively associated with achieving a healthy level of PA. It is known that seropositivity is associated with higher disease activity [35], and in turn it may be that disease activity might have affected the level of PA in our study, as participants with high disease activity could have a reduced ability to be physically active [36]. Other potentially important factors missing from these analyses are pain, fatigue, functional limitations, medical treatment and shorter symptom duration until RA diagnosis [23].

In addition, pain may also reinforce smoking behaviour [37]. Self-efficacy is important for PA [38]; however, this was not assessed in our study.

While few participants in our study had diabetes (n = 56, 5%), it was important to include diabetes as a covariate, as participants with RA may have been educated about lifestyle habits following their diabetes diagnosis. We did not include diabetes in the regression analysis for smoking cessation due to the small number of cases (n = 82, 7%), resulting in limited statistical power and increased risk of false negatives.

This study is conducted in a context where inhabitants could receive healthcare to relatively low costs, and the absolute majority of patients can afford their drugs and new drugs are available in the healthcare system. The healthcare system and mainly the primary care is supporting lifestyle habits changes, and HLHs are also debated in the media. These results may therefore be generalizable to countries with similar healthcare systems.

Despite these limitations, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate changes in lifestyle habit patterns and changes after RA diagnosis in a real-world setting. Recall bias was considered low, as the participants were asked about their current lifestyle habits at the time point that they received the questionnaire. In addition, this study benefits from a large sample size and from having recruited participants from several rheumatology centres across Sweden, enhancing its representativeness.

Conclusion

With almost half of RA patients not exhibiting three HLHs at year three (42%), this study emphasizes the important unmet need for the non-pharmacological treatment of RA patients. Although smoking cessation and reduced alcohol consumption were observed in a substantial number of patients, the proportion of participants achieving a healthy level of PA decreased. Healthcare workers should regularly discuss lifestyle habits with RA patients, especially those who are older, living alone and having a lower educational degree. By identifying factors associated with inability to follow HLH, we can better tailor our advice and support to help patients with RA sustain healthy behaviours.
