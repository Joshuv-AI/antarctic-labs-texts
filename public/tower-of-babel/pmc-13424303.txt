Geometry-aware localization evaluation of grad-CAM for wafer map defect classification
Authors: Tushar Dudeja, Prachi Sharma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigated the localization accuracy of Grad-CAM as an Explainable AI (XAI) technique for industrial inspection, specifically evaluating it on the WM811K semiconductor wafer defect dataset — a large-scale, publicly available collection of 25,519 labeled wafer maps. Localization performance was measured using the Top-10% Intersection over Union (IoU) metric on a held-out test sample (N = 3828), yielding a baseline accuracy of 0.129. A consistent radial attribution bias was observed across both Grad-CAM and XGrad-CAM, with both methods producing structurally similar bias profiles, suggesting the bias reflects a property of the model’s learned representations rather than a gradient-weighting artifact. To address the radial attribution bias, the authors implemented a radial suppression method, which improved the average Top-10% IoU by 19.4% (from 0.129 to 0.155), verified through a two-tailed paired t-test (p ≪ 0.001). Class-level analysis revealed significant improvements for edge-ring defects while other defect types remained stable. Finally, an oracle-based refinement demonstrated that geometric representation characteristics account for a substantial portion of residual localization errors, establishing an upper bound of 0.278 IoU.

Introduction

Deep-learning-based visual inspection systems are being increasingly implemented into semiconductor manufacturing and into many other high-precision industrial domains1–5. Contemporary convolutional neural network architectures and transformers are capable of achieving very high levels of accuracy for wafer defect classification and associated inspection tasks6,7. While predictive accuracy is the primary requirement in manufacturing environments where process reliability, yield optimization and root cause analysis are key, it is also critical to be able to determine what spatial region(s) in an image are influencing model decision making, especially when those models will be utilized to assist in diagnostic or corrective action8.

Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) approaches attempt to provide improved interpretability of deep neural networks through post-hoc explanation of model behavior9. Among the most popular XAI techniques used with vision models is Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM), which generates coarse localization heatmaps illustrating image regions contributing to a particular class prediction10. Grad-CAM accomplishes this task by applying the gradient of the target class to all of the intermediate feature maps to generate class-discriminative attribution maps. These visual explanations are frequently employed within both academia and industry to qualitatively evaluate whether a model is attending to the correct area of a defect11.

While Grad-CAM and many other attribution-based methods are widely utilized within defect classification research12, evaluation of the spatial reliability of these methods is generally confined to qualitative visualization13. Many studies present example images demonstrating that the highlighted regions are consistent with the location of defects; however, there is a relative lack of quantitative evaluation of the degree of spatial alignment between the generated attribution maps and the corresponding ground truth defect masks14. As image domains become increasingly structured, such as in the case of semiconductor wafer maps, geometric constraints may severely limit the ability of attribution methods to accurately capture true defect-relevant information15. However, the extent and implications of such geometric bias in attribution maps has not yet been evaluated comprehensively across large-scale industrial test datasets.

Within this study, we perform a comprehensive test set evaluation of the localization fidelity of Grad-CAM on the WM811K semiconductor wafer defect dataset consisting of 3,828 test samples with annotated ground truth16. We quantify the localization performance utilizing a Top-10% Intersection-over-Union (IoU) metric between the attribution maps and ground truth defect masks, allowing us to perform a systematic assessment of the degree of spatial alignment. By performing a comprehensive analysis of our results, we consistently observe a radial attribution bias affecting the localization performance across multiple defect classes. To alleviate the effects of this radial attribution bias, we propose a simple post-hoc correction method, referred to as radial suppression, which suppresses the dominant radial component of attribution intensity specific to Grad-CAM maps on circularly-structured wafer images, partially reducing geometric bias while preserving the original output of the underlying classifier. Whether analogous radial bias patterns arise under alternative gradient-based or perturbation-based attribution methods remains an open question beyond the scope of this study.

The results presented in this study indicate that, within the WM811K wafer defect domain, a significant portion of the Grad-CAM attribution error is geometrically influenced and systematically degrades localization performance. The proposed radial correction constitutes a cost-efficient post-hoc strategy that improves Grad-CAM spatial alignment on this dataset without necessitating any modification to the underlying classifier architecture or its learned weights. Furthermore, the findings highlight the importance of conducting systematic and quantitative evaluations of attribution methods, particularly in highly structured industrial image domains17, where geometric constraints may influence the representations learned by neural networks. Consequently, systematic bias assessment and correction mechanisms may be beneficial for improving the trustworthiness of explainable AI systems deployed in high-stakes manufacturing environments, particularly where structured image geometry may induce learnable spatial priors in model representations.

Baseline grad-CAM localization performance (pilot subset)

Spatial fidelity was evaluated by calculating the Top-10% Intersection over Union (IoU) between attribution maps generated from the baseline Grad-CAM model and defect cluster masks, on a pilot subset (N = 200).

The spatial fidelity metrics were visualized through a histogram of Top-10% IoU scores in Fig. 2a. The mean IoU was 0.121 (with σ = 0.071) which suggests a lack of spatial correspondence between the activated regions of the attribution map and the actual defect locations. The histogram of IoU scores is positively skewed, which means that many of the data points fall into the region of low-to-moderate values (between 0.05 and 0.15) and there are a few data points that are significantly higher than the rest (> 0.40).

IoU metrics per defect type are reported in Fig. 2b. Spatially localized defects (Center and Edge-Loc) have slightly greater IoU values (mean = 0.167 and mean = 0.153, respectively). Defects with ring-structured patterns (Donut and Edge-Ring) have significantly lower IoU values (mean = 0.082 and mean = 0.083, respectively) suggesting that the Grad-CAM model has difficulty capturing extended geometric features.

A box plot of the IoU values is presented in Fig. 2c. The median IoU value is approximately 0.11 with an Inter Quartile Range (IQR) of approximately 0.07 to 0.16. Full-test baseline localization statistics (N = 3828)

To get a reliable statistic of the baseline Grad-CAM localization behavior, we expanded the Top-10% IoU evaluation from the pilot subset to the full WM811K test set (N=3828).

The mean IoU over the whole test set was 0.1294 with a std dev of 0.0731. The 95% CI for the mean was [0.1271, 0.1317] showing the localization performance estimate was stable as the result of the large number of samples used to calculate it.

A median IoU of 0.1145 was less than the mean, this shows there is positive skew in the data; the majority of samples have very poor spatial alignment. An IQR of [0.0760, 0.1655] shows a wide range in how well samples were localized; there are many samples that poorly localize and fewer that do so relatively well. A skewness value of 1.3689 indicates a long right tail of poor localization, the great majority of samples are far off from localizing their defects well.

As compared to the pilot subset (Sect. "Results"), the increase in the full-test mean IoU (0.121 to 0.1294) is small, but still shows that the localization performance at scale is consistently weak and not just a product of selecting a smaller subset.

Most importantly, these results demonstrate that high classification accuracy does not imply spatial attribution fidelity: the top-10% binarised attribution maps overlap with only approximately 13% of the ground-truth defect region on average, motivating the geometry-aware refinement strategy presented in the following section.

Geometry-aware refinement improves spatial fidelity

To determine whether radial attribution bias contributes systematically to localization error, we applied the proposed radial suppression refinement to all 3,828 samples in the WM811K test set. Localization performance was evaluated using the Top-10% Intersection-over-Union (IoU) metric.

Global improvement across the full test set

To establish if the radial attribution bias affects localization accuracy as a whole, radial suppression was applied to each of the 3,828 samples in the WM811K test data-set. Localization precision was measured by computing the top 10% Intersection over Union (IoU) statistic between the binarized attribution map (the top 10% activated activations) and the corresponding ground truth defect mask.

The mean IoU value for the Baseline Grad-CAM model was 0.1294 (with SD = 0.0731). After applying radial suppression, the mean IoU value rose to 0.1545 (with SD = 0.0686) with a resultant absolute increase of 0.0251 and a relative increase of 19.4% (as can be seen in Table 2).

Oracle upper bound localization analysis

The extent of recoverable geometric alignment under an ideal spatial guide was determined by using an oracle that uses a distance-based refinement process for the attribution maps based upon how close they are in space to the actual (ground truth) location of defects. Because it utilizes ground truth spatial information, the use of this oracle will provide us with the maximum possible degree of localization accuracy theoretically attainable when using geometry aware corrections.

Global upper bound performance

When evaluated across the full WM811K test set (N = 3828), the oracle refinement achieved a mean Top-10% IoU of 0.2775, compared to 0.1294 for baseline Grad-CAM, corresponding to an absolute increase of 0.1481.

Expressed relatively, the oracle mean IoU is approximately 114.4% higher than baseline. This increase should be interpreted as an estimate of the theoretically recoverable geometric component of attribution misalignment, rather than as a practically achievable model improvement.

Paired statistical testing confirmed that the increase was highly significant, with both the paired t-test and Wilcoxon signed-rank test yielding p ≈ 0. The corresponding effect size was large (Cohen’s d = 1.7024), indicating that a substantial portion of localization error is geometrically structured and may be recoverable under ideal spatial guidance (Table 3).

Comparison to geometry-aware radial suppression

To help illustrate the extent of improvement over the theoretical maximum produced by radial suppression when compared with the baseline Grad-CAM, the mean IoU values were provided in Table 4 (below) for the three described methods.

The mean IoU of 0.1294 was achieved by the baseline Grad-CAM. The mean IoU was increased to 0.1559 using radial suppression, while the mean IoU of 0.2775 was achieved through the use of oracle refinement. The fact that radial suppression improved the mean IoU as much as it did indicates that radial suppression recovered a percentage of the geometrically recoverable alignment, however, there is still a sizeable difference between the refined attribution maps and the theoretically maximum possible mean IoU.

Interpretation of recoverable geometric error

The oracle analysis reveals how the geometric aspect of attribution misalignment in wafer defect classification occurs.

The large difference between the baseline Grad-CAM (0.1294) and oracle upper bounds (0.2775) shows that a large part of the localization error is geometrically based as opposed to being simply random or stochastic.

Radial suppression only accounts for approximately 17.9% of the theoretically possible recovery of this alignment, which suggests that radial bias is an identifiable part of the geometric distortion; however, it does not represent all of the spatial misattribution that is occurring.

The size of the oracle improvement (Cohen’s d = 1.7024) also supports the idea that there are spatially-based systematic attribution errors. Specifically, the wafer maps have geometric structures (i.e. symmetry of the circle, morphology of the defects) and therefore attribution methods that do not include these structures will be unable to correctly align activation with defect areas even if they have high classification accuracy.

Therefore, the results demonstrate that geometry-aware refinement could lead to improvements in the spatial aspects of the classification. To achieve performance similar to the oracle upper bound would likely need additional spatial modeling strategies than the simple radial correction used here.

Qualitative analysis of geometry-aware refinement

To complement the quantitative evaluation presented in Sects. "Discussion" and "Methods", we conducted a qualitative analysis of representative samples to visually examine the spatial behavior of Grad-CAM before and after geometry-aware refinement.

Visual manifestation of radial attribution bias

The qualitative review of the baseline Grad-CAM feature map for defects shows a consistent trend of radial activation patterns for many types of defects. The activation tends to radiate from the center of the sample towards the edge, regardless of whether the ground truth defect was localized near the edge of the sample or elsewhere. The radial pattern was most pronounced for the structural type of defect known as Edge Ring (Edge-Ring), where the activations appear to be somewhat aligned with the shape of the circular wafer and not strictly bounded by the location of the defect itself. Therefore, the visual evidence supports the hypothesis that there exists systematic localization errors due to geometric bias; and supports the low mean Intersection over Union (IoU) reported in Sect. "Discussion".

Effect of radial suppression on spatial attribution

Figure 7 illustrates how qualitative comparison is made of baseline Grad-CAM and radial suppression (i.e. across three defect classes - Edge-Ring, Near-Full, Center). Each case includes the original wafer image, ground-truth defect mask, baseline attribution map, and radially-suppressed attribution map along with the associated Top-10% IoU values.

The qualitative difference for the Edge-Ring example is demonstrated by radial suppression reducing the extent of central attribution and increasing it at the edges of the defect. As expected from the quantitative changes presented for this class, the attribution map produced after applying radial suppression demonstrates a better spatial relationship to the ring-shaped ground-truth structure.

As illustrated in the Near-Full example, radial suppression has a relatively modest effect on the distribution of the activation within the defective area when the region of interest covers a substantial fraction of the surface area of the wafer. Compared to the baseline, the refined map shows an overall reduction in global intensity; however, as a result of the diffuse nature of the morphology of the Near-Full defects, the changes in the overlap resulting from the application of the radial suppression are small.

Finally, as illustrated in the Center example, the location of the major attribution is preserved during the application of radial suppression, while the degree of localization of the attribution in space is only marginally increased, which is consistent with the minor per-class change measured in Sect. "Methods".

Qualitative evidence of the geometric upper bound

To further evaluate the oracle analysis described in Sect. "Methods", we conduct a qualitative comparison of the baseline Grad-CAM, radial suppression (Model A) and the distance-based oracle refinement (Model B). The oracle refinement provides an upper bound for the amount of geometric alignment that can be recovered through re-weighting the attribution based on the proximity of the attribution to the actual ground truth defect locations.

The oracle refinement produced significantly higher amounts of spatially concentrated attribution within defect clusters than either the radial suppression or the baseline attribution across all of the representative samples evaluated. For example, in cases of edge ring and other clustered defect patterns, attribution mass that had previously been spread out over the entire wafer surface became highly localized around the true defect locations. This resulted in much sharper alignment between the highest activation areas and the true mask locations.

Important to note here, is that the oracle refinement did not provide any additional semantic information. Rather, the oracle refinement redistributed the attribution in the model based upon spatial relationships. Therefore, the degree of improvement from the oracle refinement is reflective of the degree to which the original localization errors were geometrically structured vs. randomly located.

A direct comparison between Model A and Model B illustrates that the radial suppression method addressed only one aspect of the spatial misalignment - specifically radial intensity bias. By contrast, the oracle refinement addressed a broader range of spatial distributions and geometries of the defect clusters, producing larger improvements in localization accuracy.

The collective qualitative assessments identified previously are congruent with the quantitative assessments depicted in Section 5; specifically the radial suppression results demonstrated an order of magnitude greater oracle upper bound than did the baseline or radially suppressed results collectively. The combination of numeric and visual assessments clearly demonstrates that there is a large amount of geometric localization error involved in the classification of wafer defects.

Geometric error decomposition

To put both the quantitative and qualitative results into perspective, Fig. 8 provides a breakdown of how the mean localizing accuracy of each of the models described above (the baseline model, the radial suppression model [A], and the oracle upper bound [B]) compare.

The baseline model using Grad-CAM achieved a mean Top-10% IoU of .1294. When radial suppression was applied to the baseline model, the resulting mean IoU increased to .1559 (an increase of .0265). As shown in Fig. 8, this represents a significant portion of the spatial displacement due to radial attribution bias.

As shown in Fig. 8, when the oracle refinement was applied, the mean IoU increased to 0.2775. The difference between Model A and the oracle (.1216) indicates that there is recoverable geometric structure that cannot be attributed solely to radial intensity bias. The remainder of the gap is indicative of the significant contribution of the spatial distribution and cluster geometry to the localizing error.

Importantly, the oracle refinement did not add any new semantic information to the network; it merely redistributed the existing attribution according to the ground truth spatial structure. Therefore, the size of the improvement is indicative of the large amount of geometrically structured vs. random localizing error.

Together, the decomposition of the results as shown in Fig. 8 indicate that radial suppression accounts for approximately 18% of the total geometric misalignment that could have been recovered through radial suppression. Furthermore, the results demonstrate that the majority of the geometric misalignment that could be recovered is due to spatial cluster structure beyond simple radial patterns. Preprocessing ablation confirms geometric origin of radial bias

To determine whether the observed radial attribution bias originates from preprocessing choices rather than the data geometry itself, we trained an ablation model under three simultaneous modifications: (i) native single-channel input (no three-channel replication), (ii) simple scaling by 1/2 with no ImageNet normalization, and (iii) random weight initialization (no ImageNet pretraining). All other hyperparameters, architecture choices, and the random seed were kept identical to the main model. This ablation directly addresses the possibility that the radial bias could be an artifact of the preprocessing pipeline or the transferred ImageNet prior rather than an intrinsic property of wafer map geometry.

The ablation model achieved classification performance sufficient to produce structured attribution maps over the full test set (N = 3828). The radial attribution bias profile estimated from the ablation model’s training-set Grad-CAM maps exhibits a characteristic mid-radial peak at normalised distance ≈ 0.4, structurally consistent with the profile observed in the main model despite the substantially different preprocessing conditions, as illustrated in Fig. 11.

The mean Top-10% baseline IoU for the ablation model was 0.0911, compared to 0.1294 for the main model, indicating that the preprocessing choices contribute positively to overall localization quality. However, the presence of the radial bias profile under single-channel, non-pretrained, non-normalized conditions is the critical finding: it demonstrates that the geometric structure of the radial bias cannot be attributed to the preprocessing pipeline.

Applying radial suppression to the ablation model using its own training-set radial profile yielded a mean IoU of 0.0717, a change of -0.0194 (-21.3%) relative to the ablation baseline. This contrasts with the +19.4% improvement observed in the main model (Table 5). This divergence in suppression effectiveness is consistent with the lower classification quality of the ablation model producing less spatially structured attribution maps, for which a profile-based correction is less well-calibrated. Importantly, this result does not contradict the geometric origin hypothesis: the radial profile exists in both models, but the suppression corrects it most effectively when the underlying attribution maps carry sufficient spatial structure to benefit from the correction.

These results demonstrate that the radial attribution bias observed in the primary model is not attributable to the preprocessing pipeline, three-channel replication, or ImageNet pretraining. Discussion

The purpose of this research was to determine if there were patterns of geometric shape or size (spatial alignment) within the defects identified by Grad-CAM when it was used as an attribute map in the classification of defects in wafers. Radial suppression significantly improved localization accuracy across the entire WM811K test dataset compared to the baseline method of using the original WM811K test dataset. Additionally, the use of oracle-based refinement resulted in a much greater portion of the total recoverable geometric information present in the original Grad-CAM image. Qualitatively, and with respect to radial-profile analysis, the results also indicated that the strength of the attributed location on the wafer was dependent upon the geometry of the wafer and not just random in space. The results therefore suggest that, for Grad-CAM applied to the WM811K wafer domain, the errors in attributing defect locations are not simply random but include a level of structured radial geometric bias. A simple radial correction partially reduces this bias; whether more general geometry-based methods could further improve localization remains an open question.

Summary of principal findings

The objective of this research is to assess the spatial reliability of Grad-CAM attribution maps for the classification of defects on semiconductor wafers. Although, the Swin Transformer performed at high predictive levels for the classification of defects, an examination of all test data found the spatial reliability of attributions as determined by the Top-10% Intersection-over-Union metric to be significantly lower than the predictive accuracy. Similar separations between predictive accuracy and explanation fidelity have been observed in attribution robustness studies13,19.

Radial suppression generated statistically significant increases in localizability for 3,828 test samples, indicating that some portion of the localization mismatch can be attributed to radial-intensity-bias. However, radial modulation was shown to explain only a small percentage of the total recoverable geometric localization error relative to the oracle upper bound.

Furthermore, evaluation of XGrad-CAM18 on the same test set revealed a structurally identical radial bias profile despite employing a distinct activation-normalised gradient weighting scheme, suggesting that, for this model, the bias reflects a property of the learned representations rather than an artifact of Grad-CAM’s specific gradient averaging mechanism.

Radial-profile analysis also showed that the baseline attribution strength varied systematically with the geometry of the wafer. Following improvement, these systematic modulations were reduced, thus providing a mechanistic basis that the localization error was not solely based upon random chance but, rather, was influenced by prior knowledge of geometric characteristics of the dataset that were learned during the training process. Dataset-induced geometric priors in learned representations have been discussed in structured visual domains20.

Attribution reliability in structured domains

Post-hoc interpretability techniques like Grad-CAM are used to provide attribution for image processing models, but reliability evaluations typically involve either qualitative visualization or perturbation-based quality assessments13,14,19. Both types of assessments measure sensitivity or robustness, however, neither can directly measure how well attribution is aligned in space with annotated ground truth structures.

This study contributes a quantitative, large-scale localization evaluation of Grad-CAM on a structured industrial domain, complementing existing attribution reliability assessments that typically rely on qualitative visualisation or perturbation-based sensitivity measures. By evaluating attribution-to-mask overlap over the entire test set, the present study demonstrates that, in Grad-CAM attribution maps generated by the primary classifier on the WM811K test set, a systematic radial geometric bias was identified that is difficult to reliably quantify through visual inspection alone. The identification of radial modulation also indicates that attribution maps are capable of representing learned geometric patterns that exist in the training data distribution, consistent with prior work on representation bias and structural priors in deep networks20,21.

Thus, this study illustrates an important difference: it is possible for an explanation method to accurately represent model behavior and yet inherit structural biases present in learned representations19,21. Therefore, the reliability of explanations should be assessed not only based on their stability but also based on their spatial correspondence with relevant morphological structure in a particular task.

Industrial and practical implications

Explainability in semiconductor inspection systems provides engineers with diagnostic information to help determine where defects have been localized and what corrective actions are required1,12. Explainability also provides attribution maps that indicate where a defect has been localized by the classifier used for detection.

If an engineer relies on a highly accurate classifier to identify a defect, but the classifier incorrectly attributes the defect to the wrong spatial region, this can introduce ambiguity into the engineer’s decision-making process.

The results of this study demonstrate that, within the WM811K wafer defect domain, a radial post-hoc correction can improve the spatial accuracy of Grad-CAM attribution maps without modifying the model weights or predictive outputs of the classifier. Similar post-hoc correction strategies have been proposed as practical deployment mechanisms in safety-critical AI systems22–25. Furthermore, the findings suggest that interpretability pipelines for circularly-structured image domains such as semiconductor wafer maps may benefit from incorporating geometric priors when generating explanations. If domain geometry is not considered, explanations may appear visually plausible while remaining spatially biased.

Conceptual implications for geometry-aware XAI

The separation of localization performance into the two geometric components (radial and residual) offers a systematic way to think about how attribution error can be decomposed.

In contrast to viewing misalignment as noise, the results indicate that there is a significant proportion of error that can be recovered when redistributing based on geometric awareness. This aligns with recent discussions emphasizing that attribution quality is influenced by both model architecture and dataset structure19,21.

Radial structure forms the most prominent spatial prior in the case of circular wafer maps. The present study evaluates this bias specifically under Grad-CAM and XGrad-CAM; whether analogous geometric biases manifest across perturbation-based methods such as RISE, or attention-based explainers such as attention rollout, remains an open empirical question. Similarly, whether the findings generalise beyond the WM811K wafer domain to other structured industrial or medical imaging settings is outside the scope of this study26.

While radial suppression will likely reduce one of the identifiable sources of bias, the large gap between Model A and the oracle upper bound for this task indicates that significantly more general methods for reducing bias through use of geometric awareness may be necessary.

Future research could investigate the incorporation of spatial priors during the training process, or the development of attribution mechanisms that take advantage of the geometric constraints of the specific dataset.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations must be taken into account when considering the above. The oracle refinement is based upon the ground-truth defect mask and thus it serves as an idealized upper-bound rather than a deployable solution. All primary experiments were conducted under a single fixed random seed (42); multi-seed variance analysis of both classification and localization performance, as well as evaluation of whether the observed radial attribution structure generalises across independent training runs, remain directions for future work.

Attribution method comparisons in this study are limited to gradient-based methods (Grad-CAM and XGrad-CAM). The convergence of radial bias profiles across these two methods suggests the bias reflects a property of the learned representations rather than a method-specific artifact; however, evaluation across perturbation-based methods, attention-based explainers, and alternative backbone architectures remains a direction for future work13,19.

Model architecture

Swin transformer backbone

To model wafer defect patterns, we employ a hierarchical Vision Transformer based on the Swin Transformer architecture7. Specifically, we use the Swin-Tiny configuration (patch size = 4, window size = 7, input resolution = 224) initialized with ImageNet-pretrained weights27. For theoretical completeness, we also acknowledge the original Vision Transformer formulation28.

After preprocessing, each wafer map is resized to a fixed spatial resolution of 224 × 224, replicated to three channels, and normalized using ImageNet statistics. The resulting input tensor is\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$x_i \in \mathbb {R}^{3 \times 224 \times 224}.$$\end{document}The Swin Transformer partitions the image into non-overlapping patches of size 4 × 4. Each patch is linearly embedded into a token representation, producing an initial token sequence:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_0 \in \mathbb {R}^{N_p \times d},$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_p$$\end{document} denotes the number of patches and d the embedding dimension.

Optimization strategy

Model parameters were optimized using the AdamW optimizer29 with a learning rate of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$3 \times 10^{-4}$$\end{document}, weight decay of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$1 \times 10^{-4}$$\end{document}, batch size of 32, and a total of 30 training epochs.

A two-phase learning rate schedule was employed. During the first \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$T_{\textrm{warmup}} = 5$$\end{document} epochs, the learning rate increased linearly:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\eta _t = \eta _0 \frac{t}{T_{\textrm{warmup}}}.$$\end{document}After warmup, cosine annealing30 was applied:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\eta _t = \frac{1}{2}\eta _0 \left( 1 + \cos \left( \frac{\pi t}{T} \right) \right) .$$\end{document}Mixed-precision training (Automatic Mixed Precision, AMP) was used to improve computational efficiency while preserving numerical stability. All experiments were conducted using a fixed random seed of 42 to ensure deterministic reproducibility.

Evaluation metrics

Classification performance was evaluated on the held-out test set using accuracy, macro-averaged F1-score, balanced accuracy, macro-averaged ROC-AUC, and Expected Calibration Error (ECE).

Calibration quality was quantified using the Expected Calibration Error31:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{ECE} = \sum _{b=1}^{B} \frac{|S_b|}{N} \left| \textrm{acc}(S_b) - \textrm{conf}(S_b) \right| ,$$\end{document}where predictions are grouped into B confidence bins \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$S_b$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{acc}(S_b)$$\end{document} denotes empirical accuracy within bin b, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{conf}(S_b)$$\end{document} denotes average predicted confidence.

Geometry-aware attribution refinement

To investigate whether localization errors exhibit structured geometric bias, we introduce two post-hoc refinement strategies: (i) radial suppression, which removes systematic radial intensity bias without using ground-truth information, and (ii) an oracle distance-based refinement, which provides a theoretical upper bound on recoverable geometric alignment.

Both refinements operate directly on the raw Grad-CAM attribution maps10 prior to Top-10% thresholding. To assess whether the observed radial attribution bias is specific to Grad-CAM, XGrad-CAM18 was evaluated on the same test set using an identical experimental protocol.

Radial suppression

Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_i(p)$$\end{document} denote the raw Grad-CAM attribution intensity at pixel location \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p \in \mathbb {R}^2$$\end{document} for sample i.

Let c denote the wafer center and define the normalized radial distance:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$r(p) = \frac{\Vert p - c\Vert _2}{R_{\max }},$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R_{\max }$$\end{document} is the maximum wafer radius.

To estimate radial bias, we compute the empirical radial intensity profile:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu (r) = \mathbb {E}_{p : r(p)=r} \left[ L_i(p) \right] .$$\end{document}Radial suppression is applied by dividing out the radial mean component:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_i^{\text {rad}}(p) = \frac{L_i(p)}{\mu (p) + \varepsilon },$$\end{document}where μ (p) is the spatially-resolved radial bias map estimated from the training set (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N = 17{,}863$$\end{document}), and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\varepsilon = 10^{-6}$$\end{document} ensures numerical stability. The refined map is clipped at the 99th percentile to suppress extreme amplification artefacts and renormalised to [0, 1]. Critically, μ (p) was estimated exclusively from training-set attribution maps and saved prior to any test-set evaluation, ensuring no leakage between the correction procedure and the held-out evaluation data.

The refined attribution map \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_i^{\text {rad}}$$\end{document} is subsequently evaluated using the Top-10% IoU protocol described earlier.

Oracle distance-based refinement

To estimate the maximum recoverable geometric alignment, we define an oracle refinement using ground-truth defect regions.

Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Omega _i$$\end{document} denote the ground-truth defect region:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Omega _i = \{ p \in \mathbb {R}^2 \mid M_i(p) = 1 \}.$$\end{document}We compute the Euclidean distance from each pixel to the nearest defect location:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$d(p, \Omega _i) = \min _{q \in \Omega _i} \Vert p - q\Vert _2.$$\end{document}A spatial weighting function is defined as\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$w_i(p) = \exp \bigl ( -\lambda \cdot \tilde{d}(p, \Omega _i) \bigr ),$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\tilde{d}(p, \Omega _i)$$\end{document} denotes the Euclidean distance to the nearest defect pixel, normalised to [0, 1], and λ = 5.0 controls the spatial decay rate. A sensitivity analysis across \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda \in \{0.5, 1.0, 2.0, 5.0, 10.0\}$$\end{document} confirmed that the oracle upper-bound conclusion holds consistently across all tested values.

The oracle-refined attribution map is then:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$L_i^{\text {oracle}}(p) = L_i(p) \cdot w_i(p).$$\end{document}Because this refinement uses ground-truth spatial information, it represents a theoretical upper bound rather than a deployable method.

Statistical evaluation protocol

To rigorously assess whether geometry-aware refinement improves localization performance, we performed paired statistical analysis on per-sample Intersection-over-Union (IoU) scores computed over the full test set.

Let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{IoU}_i^{\textrm{base}}$$\end{document} denote the baseline Grad-CAM IoU for sample i, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textrm{IoU}_i^{\textrm{ref}}$$\end{document} the corresponding refined IoU. The paired difference is defined as1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} d_i = \textrm{IoU}_i^{\textrm{ref}} - \textrm{IoU}_i^{\textrm{base}}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Statistical significance of the mean improvement was evaluated using a paired Student’s t-test, which tests whether the expected paired difference \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathbb {E}[d_i]$$\end{document} differs from zero. To ensure robustness against potential deviations from normality, we additionally conducted the Wilcoxon signed-rank test as a non-parametric alternative.

To quantify practical significance, we computed Cohen’s d for paired samples:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} d = \frac{\mu _d}{\sigma _d}, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu _d$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma _d$$\end{document} denote the mean and standard deviation of the paired differences, respectively. Effect sizes were interpreted according to conventional thresholds (0.2 small, 0.5 medium, 0.8 large).

In addition to hypothesis testing, we report mean ± standard deviation, 95% confidence intervals (Student-t), median, interquartile range (IQR), and distribution skewness.

All statistical analyses were conducted on the full held-out test set (N = 3828).

Implementation and reproducibility

The entire codebase was implemented in PyTorch, utilizing the timm library for model instantiation. The Swin-Tiny backbone7 was initialized with ImageNet-pretrained weights27 and subsequently fine-tuned on the WM811K dataset. Optimization was performed using the AdamW optimizer29 with a cosine learning-rate schedule30 and a linear warm-up during the initial training phase. Mixed precision training (Automatic Mixed Precision, AMP) was employed to improve computational efficiency while maintaining numerical stability.

Grad-CAM attribution maps were generated following the methodology described by Selvaraju et al.10, using forward and backward hooks applied to the final transformer block. The resulting continuous attribution maps were refined using geometry-aware operations prior to percentile-based thresholding for Top-10% IoU evaluation.

Localization performance was evaluated exclusively on the held-out test set; no test samples were used during training, hyperparameter tuning, or model selection.

All experiments were conducted in a CUDA-enabled GPU environment. To ensure deterministic reproducibility, a fixed random seed of 42 was used throughout. The seed governed dataset partitioning, weight initialization, data loading order, and attribution map generation.
