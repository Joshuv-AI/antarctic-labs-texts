Aging brain lesions of a 60-year-old Bornean orangutan (Pongo pygmaeus)
Authors: Yutaro NAKAYAMA, James K CHAMBERS, Kaori OTA, Ayumi YAMAGUCHI, Kosuke TODA, Saori SATO, Akane KATO, Yuka KAMAKURA, Kazuyuki UCHIDA
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A female Bornean orangutan (Pongo pygmaeus) raised in a zoo died at 60 years of age. The animal showed no clinical signs besides decreased gait speed. Pathological examination revealed small hemorrhagic foci in the cerebral cortex and liver fibrosis with fatty degeneration of the hepatocytes. To evaluate aging lesions, the brain was examined histologically and immunohistochemically. Severe amyloid β (Aβ) deposition was observed in the cerebral cortex and vascular walls. Parenchymal Aβ deposits in the cerebral cortex appeared as diffuse plaques. Loss of smooth muscle was observed in the vascular walls affected by Aβ deposition. In contrast, only a few phosphorylated tau aggregates were observed. This report describes severe Aβ deposition in the brain of the oldest orangutan ever reported, which did not exhibit significant tau pathologies.

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most prevalent age-related neurodegenerative disease in humans and causes dementia. The pathology of AD is characterized by parenchymal amyloid β (Aβ) deposits in the cerebral cortex, called senile plaques (SPs), and phosphorylated tau (p-tau) aggregates in neurons, called neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) [7, 33]. In addition, Aβ deposits in the vascular walls, known as cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA), are observed in approximately 80% of AD patient brains [1, 9]. These pathological changes have been reported in non-human primates, including great apes such as orangutans, chimpanzees, and gorillas. Aβ deposits and p-tau aggregates have been observed in the brains of aged chimpanzees and gorillas; however, most p-tau aggregates were non-fibrillar pretangles, an early form of NFT [8, 10, 12, 17, 20, 21]. In the brains of 28–46-year-old orangutans, Aβ deposits were observed without p-tau aggregates [11, 25]. In this study, we examined Aβ deposits and p-tau aggregates in the brain of a 60-year-old orangutan and compared them with those in humans and other mammalian species.

A female orangutan (studbook ID: 0726) born and raised in a zoo died at 60 years and three months of age. The animal showed decreased gait speed but no signs suggestive of dementia. Molecular subtypes of the C-terminal region of Aβ are classified according to the amino acid cleavage sites (Aβ40, Aβ42, and Aβ43) [23]. Among these subtypes, Aβ42 is the primary component of SPs in AD, whereas Aβ40 is predominant in CAA. This difference in deposition patterns is associated with the higher aggregation potential of Aβ42 than that of Aβ40 [13, 15, 22]. In several Caniformia species, such as dogs and sea lions, the degree of Aβ42 positivity is more severe than that of Aβ40 in SPs and CAA [26]. In contrast, the degree of Aβ40 positivity was comparable to or even more severe than that of Aβ42 in orangutans [11, 12]. In this orangutan, the severity of Aβ40 and Aβ42 positivity was comparable in both SPs and CAA. Therefore, Aβ40 in orangutans may have a different aggregation potential for forming SPs and CAA than in humans and Caniformia species. In addition, Aβ43 has recently attracted attention for its high aggregation potential and neurotoxicity [5, 6, 24] and acts as a core component in the formation of SPs in AD patients [16, 32]. Similarly, the role of Aβ43 in Aβ deposition in animals has received increasing attention. Aβ43-positive SPs and CAA equivalent to Aβ42 have been observed in dogs, cats, sea lions, gorillas, and rhesus monkeys, suggesting that Aβ43 deposition, like Aβ42 deposition, may play an important role in the formation of SPs and CAA in animals [14, 17, 26]. However, in this orangutan, Aβ43 deposition was limited to demarcated round SPs and CAA of capillary walls. This restricted distribution suggests that the contribution of Aβ43 to Aβ deposition may vary among species and may be associated with patterns of SPs and CAA deposition, particularly in the maturation and structural organization of these deposits.

SPs are known to spread progressively in the human brain with aging, and the Thal phase, based on SP distribution, is widely used for staging AD pathology [27, 29]. The distribution of SPs in the Thal phase is as follows: phase 1, neocortex; phase 2, entorhinal cortex, hippocampal area, amygdala, and cingulate gyrus; phase 3, striatum, thalamus, and cerebral medulla; phase 4, midbrain and medulla oblongata; and phase 5, pons and cerebellum. Phases 4 and 5 are considered symptomatic. In the present orangutan, the degrees of SPs were severe in the neocortex, entorhinal cortex, and hippocampal area, moderate in the thalamus, and mild in the striatum, midbrain, and cerebral medulla, corresponding to Thal phases 3–4. Moreover, mature plaques with a central core and neuritic plaques containing dystrophic neurites, both of which are associated with cognitive decline in humans [31, 35], were not observed in the present study. Only plaques without a central core, including diffuse amorphous and demarcated round SPs, on PAM-stained sections were detected. Demarcated round SPs in the orangutan were positive for Aβ43, which has also been documented in mature plaques in an aged Gorilla [17], suggesting maturation of SPs compared to diffuse plaques. In addition, coarse-grained plaques and primitive plaques containing vessel structures have been observed in humans and dogs, and these plaques have been suggested to indicate a relationship between SPs and CAA [2, 26]. In this orangutan, Aβ deposition was observed in areas adjacent to or surrounding CAA, and some SPs contained blood vessel-like structures. In addition, the distribution of CAA was similar to that of SPs. These findings suggest that the expansion of CAA throughout the brain may lead to the formation of additional SPs.

CAA is associated with increased vulnerability of the vascular wall in the brain [18]. The severity of CAA is graded according to the degree of destruction of the vascular smooth muscle [28, 30]: grade 1, focal Aβ deposition in the vascular wall without major destruction of the lamina media; grade 2, significant destruction of the lamina media; and grade 3, CAA associated with hemorrhage. In this orangutan, circumferential Aβ deposition in the vascular wall with loss of SMA in the tunica media was observed, corresponding to grade 2 lesions. Although no CAA-associated hemorrhage was observed, Aβ deposition in the vascular wall with loss of SMA can be a risk factor for brain hemorrhage in aged orangutans.

Despite severe Aβ deposition, p-tau aggregates were rare, and pretangles and NFTs were not formed. The degree of p-tau aggregates did not exceed that of Braak stage 1 describes in humans [3, 4], consistent with previous reports in orangutans [11, 25]. In humans, Aβ Thal phase 2–3 and p-tau can be observed in the hippocampus and entorhinal cortex of elderly individuals without dementia [31]. There is currently no established method for evaluating cognitive dysfunction in non-human primates. Behavioral changes, such as disorientation, reduced interactions with other individuals, altered sleep-wake cycle, reduced activity level, and gait abnormalities, have been reported in a super-aged rhesus macaque with SPs and p-tau aggregates in its brain [34]. In the present case of an orangutan, no behavioral changes other than a decrease in gait speed were observed. The association between decreased gait speed and Aβ and p-tau pathologies could not be determined, as it may be attributable to multiple etiologies, including age-related frailty. In chimpanzees and gorillas, which are closely related to orangutans, p-tau aggregates have been observed, but most are pretangles, and NFTs are rare [8, 10, 12, 17, 20, 21]. In contrast, in humans, NFTs are prominent in the later Thal phase [19]. Therefore, despite significant Aβ deposition in the brains of non-human great apes, they may have a lower potential to form NFTs than humans. Further analysis of p-tau aggregation in great apes are expected to elucidate the reasons for the rarity of p-tau aggregation. Phosphorylated α-synuclein and phosphorylated TDP43 were not detected in the present orangutan. Humans and orangutans share a high degree of amino acid sequence identity in α-synuclein and TDP43 (Supplementary Table 2), suggesting that the antibodies used in the present study cross-react with orangutan proteins.

In conclusion, this study described severe Aβ deposition in a super-aged orangutan without significant p-tau aggregation. In addition, the detailed distribution and morphology of SPs and CAA are described. Further studies are necessary to clarify the relationship between Aβ deposition and cognitive decline in orangutans.
