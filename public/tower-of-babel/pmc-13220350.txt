Stress first aid for the harm reduction workforce: pilot test results of an occupational stress intervention
Authors: Anmol Desai, Juliette Rau, Jake Samora, Katie A McCormick, Haresh Rochani, Patrick Chang, Richard J Westphal, Patricia J Watson, Justin K Benzer, Kasey R Claborn, Suzannah K Creech
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHarm reduction workers (HRWs), while fighting the overdose crisis, are exposed to lifetime occupational stress and trauma, which in turn are associated with the long-term work and social functioning of this key sector. There is a critical gap in research on the occupational health of HRWs. The aim of this study was to adapt and pilot Stress First Aid (SFA), a peer-to-peer occupational stress intervention, for HRWs.MethodsThe adapted SFA/HRW model was a 2-hour, in-person training followed by four, 30-minute, monthly learning collaboratives piloted in a non-profit recovery community organization. A convenience sample of workers and organizational leaders (N = 35) was recruited from the pilot site. Participants completed surveys assessing knowledge, confidence, beliefs, and use of SFA and preliminary well-being outcomes at baseline, 2-months, and 4-months after the training. Participants also completed a preliminary implementation outcomes survey, and randomly selected participants (n = 13) completed 30-minute virtual implementation interviews of the SFA model. Quantitative analyses included descriptive statistics, paired tests between timepoints, and Bayesian hierarchical models to estimate changes over time and assess intervention effects with uncertainty quantification. Qualitative data were analyzed using applied thematic analysis.ResultsResults indicated a positive effect on knowledge (β = 0.87, 95% CrI, 0.56–1.17), confidence (β = 0.84, 95% CrI, 0.52–1.16), and beliefs (β = 0.29, 95% CrI, 0.11–0.48) in SFA with increased use of SFA (β = 0.91, 95% CrI, 0.39–1.44) and observation of others using SFA (β = 0.69, 95% CrI, 0.17–1.21). There was a positive effect on overall work engagement (β = 0.27, 95% CrI, 0.02–0.51). Results also indicate > 80% acceptability, appropriateness, and feasibility of SFA/HRW, with participants qualitatively reporting SFA aligned with their work and that SFA would be a valuable supplement to their pre-existing coping strategies.ConclusionsThere was an increase in observed practices of SFA and stability in well-being, coping, secondary traumatic stress, burnout, and turnover intention. Additionally, participants reported a significant increase in work engagement. SFA is highly acceptable, appropriate, and feasible among HRWs. The next steps are to test the effects of SFA/HRW against a control group in a randomized controlled trial.Trial registrationThe study NCT07149155 is registered on https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT07149155.Keywords: Occupational health, Harm reduction workforce, Community outreach workers, Peer workers, Persons who use drugs, Overdose prevention, Population informed adaptation, Pilot test

Background

There is an opioid epidemic in the United States (US) in which opioid overdose deaths have substantially increased over the last two decades and remain high [1]. Harm reduction workers (HRWs) including peer support and community health workers, behavioral health workers, and medical staff, play a critical role on the frontlines of opioid epidemic response and in providing care for vulnerable persons who use drugs (PWUD) [2, 3]. HRWs are responsible for many tasks related to reducing overdoses, including but not limited to distributing harm reduction supplies, responding to overdoses in the field, providing linkage to services, and advocacy [2]. The harm reduction workforce is a diverse population, that may include licensed mental health and medical professionals, social workers, and peer workers with lived experience, working in a variety of organizational structures (i.e. grassroots, community-based organizations) [3, 4].

In this work, HRWs are exposed to high levels of occupational stress and trauma due to the complex challenges of working with vulnerable PWUD [5, 6]. While many in the harm reduction workforce find their work rewarding, they often face significant job-related stressors (e.g., client deaths, COVID burdens, political red tape) on top of possible pre-existing trauma, lack of social support, and lack of personal resources (i.e. low wages, understaffing, increased workloads) [7]. Given the demanding nature of their work and lack of resources, HRWs may experience job-level, organizational-level and environmental-level stressors including but not limited to an unpredictable outreach environment, frustrations with external organizations, lack of internal organizational support, and gaps in competency training for working with PWUD [8]. The organizational stressors from working in harm reduction such as taking on additional responsibilities, supply shortages, uncertainty of stable and sufficient funding, job insecurity, and inadequate organizational support contribute to burnout among the harm reduction workforce [9, 10].

There is a critical gap in research on the occupational health of HRWs, whose exposure to occupational stressors may be compounded by prior experiences of trauma and concomitant mental health conditions such as PTSD, anxiety, and depression [6, 11]. High rates of unaddressed occupational stress have been shown to have an adverse impact on client care and HRWs turnover [12], and may detrimentally affect occupational well-being, including compassion fatigue, burnout, and secondary trauma [4, 9, 13–15] that HRWs reported have become normalized in their field [16] HRWs need an occupational stress intervention that addresses multi-level challenges HRWs experience in order to enhance their capacity to engage with the complex demands of delivering safe, high-quality harm reduction care to PWUD, that represent a key role in addressing the overdose crisis [16, 17].

Stress first aid

Stress First Aid (SFA) is an evidence-informed, self-care and peer-to-peer, psychological support model developed to reduce the experience of occupational stress [18]. SFA provides a flexible framework, derived from Hobfoll’s five essential principles (safety, calm, connect, competence, hope) for early and mid-term trauma interventions that are theoretically linked to improved well-being and functioning [19, 20]. The 7 core elements of SFA are (1) Check, (2) Coordinate, (3) Cover (safety), (4) Calm, (5) Connect, (6) Competence, and Confidence (hope) [21]. Check and Coordinate are ongoing steps to continuously identify stress and respond effectively using the other core elements of SFA. Cover promotes a sense of safety while calming may be utilized to address heightened stress and anxiety. Connection is a strong protective factor against sever persistent distress, also known as a stress injury, and supporting recovery. A sense of self and collective efficacy promotes belief or competence in the ability to overcome stress, while confidence may promote a sense of hope and motivation in one’s work. These core elements indicate targeted areas for knowledge to changes in attitudes and behaviors towards identifying and effectively addressing occupational stress in real time.

Importantly, SFA is an organizational-level intervention that is designed to target change in the organizational culture relative to mental health and thereby strengthen employee well-being by promoting meaningful peer connection, self-care, and modeling leadership support of open communication around occupational stress. SFA was originally developed for military personnel and has since been adapted for service-oriented professions that are particularly susceptible to stress and trauma, such as healthcare workers and firefighters [19, 20, 22–24]. SFA also has been proven to be an effective model for supporting frontline healthcare and hospital staff, improving resilience and coping self-efficacy during the COVID-19 pandemic through increased peer support and connection [20, 25–27].

This translational research study is innovative in its focus on adapting the evidence-informed SFA model to the specific needs of HRWs with a population-informed approach. The aim of this pilot test was to implement and evaluate the SFA model adapted for HRWs (hereafter SFA/HRW) and its impact on their well-being, through (1) longitudinal quantitative data on SFA/HRW 1a) primary outcomes and 1b) secondary outcomes, and (2) post-training data and qualitative interviews on preliminary implementation outcomes. To understand the real-world impact of SFA/HRW, the RE-AIM (Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, and Maintenance) framework guided the evaluation of primary and implementation outcomes [28].

Methods

Adaptations to SFA from the harm reduction workers

Co-adaptation of the SFA/HRW model was guided by occupational stressors and resilience strategies identified by workers and organizational leaders in the harm reduction workforce during a series of focus groups [8]. Retaining core elements of the SFA model, adaptations were made by research staff informed by the population, prior to the pilot test, to integrate examples of frontline safety protocols, highlight existing resources to support competence and well-being, and to create space to share stressors encountered on the job and reflect on current strategies for managing them [8]. SFA/HRW focuses on increasing knowledge and equipping the harm reduction workforce with tools to address stress in real-time, applicable to harm reduction work, targeting attitude and behavior changes at an organizational level.

Design

We conducted a pilot test of the 2-hour, in-person SFA/HRW training followed by four 30-minute monthly learning collaboratives at a non-profit recovery community organization serving PWUD in a Southern U.S. state. Participants completed longitudinal surveys and qualitative interviews to assess SFA/HRW and preliminary well-being and implementation outcomes. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at the University of Texas at Austin.

Participants

The pilot site was identified through the research team’s existing Harm Reduction Community Advisory Board (CAB) members, a network established through the development of the Texans Connecting Overdose Prevention Efforts (TxCOPE) platform [17].

A convenience sampling method was used to recruit participants from the pilot site. All organization staff, including peer support workers and leaders, were invited to enroll in the study. Organizational leadership received an email and flyer containing a QR code linking to the REDCap consent form for enrollment into the study, which they were asked to share with their staff. Informed consent to participate in the study was obtained from all participants, after which they received a REDCap baseline survey. All participants were part- to full-time paid employees.

Procedure

The SFA/HRW training was held in October 2024 and facilitated by three organizational staff who participated in a 3-day SFA train-the-trainer session in August 2024. The 30-minute monthly learning collaboratives were held over Zoom, between October 2024 and January 2025. Monthly learning collaboratives were brief reviews of core SFA concepts facilitated by research staff trained on the SFA/HRW model followed by discussion about implementing SFA within their organization.

Study outcomes

The primary outcomes of the pilot were (1) participant familiarity with SFA measured by 1a) knowledge, confidence, beliefs in SFA, and 1b) use of SFA, and (2) preliminary implementation outcomes measured by acceptability, appropriateness, and feasibility of the SFA/HRW among the workforce [29].

Secondary outcomes focused on well-being to include 1) social support (emotional and informational support, 2) burnout, 3) job-related affective well-being, 4) secondary traumatic stress, 5) coping strategies, 6) general self-efficacy to handle stressful situations, 7) work engagement, and 8) turnover intention [30–38].

Measures

Participants were asked to complete surveys at baseline (pre-training), 2-months, and 4-months post-training to assess knowledge, confidence, beliefs, use of SFA, and preliminary well-being outcomes (Table 1). Participants were compensated $50 for each survey upon completion. A brief five-minute post-training survey was also assessed to measure the preliminary implementation outcomes [29].

The SFA developers designed the knowledge, confidence, and beliefs in SFA and use of SFA measures to assess the perceptions of SFA and the understanding of when and how to apply SFA when experiencing stress in substance misuse settings in real-time. To understand how well the adaptation of SFA was perceived by the harm reduction workforce, the Acceptability of Intervention Measure (AIM), Intervention Appropriateness Measure (IAM), and Feasibility of Intervention Measure (FIM) were chosen as psychometrically sound measures for assessing intervention implementation [29]. Though there is no empirical cut-off on this measure, mean scores of ≥ 4 were targeted.

Based on the literature, we selected secondary well-being outcomes that represented important domains of personal or occupational well-being that can be impacted by high levels of stress. To assess the preliminary efficacy of the adapted SFA program among the harm reduction workforce, psychometrically sound, validated measures were selected to assess these well-being outcomes (Table 1).

After the 4-month study period, a random sample of participants were also asked to complete implementation interviews, guided by the RE-AIM framework, to understand reactions to the SFA/HRW training and curriculum [28]. Participants were asked to describe their experiences with using SFA/HRW, including successes and challenges, and additional support that may help sustain using SFA/HRW at their organization, in order to assess if SFA/HRW adequately addressed supporting implementation and sustainability. Participants were compensated $25 for participating in an implementation interview. Implementation interviews were recorded using the secure Zoom platform. Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim using Zoom and Microsoft Word transcription and anonymized.

Statistical methods

The baseline demographic characteristics of the study participants were summarized using means (M) and standard deviations (SD) for continuous variables and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables. We tested the within-person change in study outcomes from (1) baseline to two months, and (2) baseline to four months.

Paired-sample t-tests were used for the Likert-scale variables and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used for the categorical variables, reporting test statistics and corresponding two-sided p-values to determine statistical significance, with a threshold of p < 0.05.

The parameters of interest were estimated longitudinally using Bayesian hierarchical models. This method accounts for within-subject correlations and provides a robust framework for estimating changes while incorporating prior distributions to guide parameter estimation. Each outcome variable was modeled using a Bayesian linear mixed-effects model with time as a fixed effect and a random intercept for each participant (record ID) to account for repeated measures. The model was specified as:

A Bayesian hierarchical approach was chosen instead of a traditional generalized linear model (GLM). Unlike GLMs, which rely on asymptotic assumptions, Bayesian inference offers a more comprehensive uncertainty quantification, making it particularly advantageous in pilot studies where data may be sparse or exhibit high variability.

Priors were specified to impose reasonable constraints on parameter estimates while allowing flexibility for data-driven inference. Specifically, a normal (0, 10) prior was placed on the regression coefficients (𝛽) to provide non-informative guidance, preventing extreme estimates while maintaining broad uncertainty. A Cauchy (0, 2) prior was used for the standard deviation of the random intercepts to allow moderate shrinkage while accommodating variability across participants.

Posterior distributions were estimated using Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling with four chains, 5,000 iterations per chain, and a warm-up period of 2,000 iterations. Convergence diagnostics were conducted using the Gelman-Rubin statistic (R̂), which confirmed model stability, with all R̂ values equal to 1 or very close to 1, indicating proper mixing and convergence of the MCMC chains.

Statistical analyses were conducted using R 4.1.1 and the brms package for Bayesian modeling. Summary statistics, including posterior means estimates of beta regression coefficients (β), standard errors (SE) and 95% credible intervals (CrI) were extracted for interpretation.

Qualitative analysis

Informed by the RE-AIM guided interview guides, a deductive approach was taken to define the codes and develop the codebook. Using a pragmatic approach, Applied Thematic Analysis was conducted by two experienced qualitative analysis coders to understand the why and how of implementing SFA/HRW in addition to the efficacy [40]. An iterative process was used to revise and finalize the codes. The two coders independently coded each transcript and then met to discuss and negotiate applied codes. This iterative process was applied to all thirteen transcripts. Another experienced researcher was a tie-break coder, as needed. Coding was initially done manually in Microsoft Word, and final coding was done in NVivo 15 to draw out major themes relevant to the acceptability, appropriateness, and feasibility of the SFA/HRW model, guided by the quantitative post-training survey.

Participants reported having average emotional support (M = 51.59, SD = 7.85) and informational support (M = 54.34, SD = 9.86), on a mean T-score scale of 0-100. At baseline, on a scale of 0-100, participants reported experiencing slightly above average client-related burnout (M = 67.93, SD = 16.49), and average work-related (M = 51.28, SD = 16.63) and personal-related burnout (M = 49.24, SD = 18.35).

Overall job-related affective well-being at baseline was an average mean of 3.56 (SD = 0.54), with below average negative well-being at a mean of 2.17 (SD = 0.57) on a 5-point Likert scale. Overall, secondary-traumatic stress was below average with a mean of 2.08 (SD = 0.62) on a 5-point Likert scale. On a 4-point Likert scale, problem-focused coping was reported above average (M = 2.92, 0.69). There was a below average general self-efficacy to handle stressful situations among participants at baseline (M = 36.67, SD = 8.09), on a mean T-score scale of 0-100. Participants reported above average work engagement (M = 5.07, SD = 1.09) with dedication to their work (M = 5.57, SD = 1.29) on a 0–6 score. Participants reported below average turnover intentions at baseline, on a 0–6 sum score scale, with a mean of 2.01 (SD = 1.28).

SFA primary outcomes – knowledge, confidence, beliefs, and use

There was a significant increase between baseline and 2-months and that difference was also significant at 4 months. The Bayesian model indicated that the SFA training and supporting monthly learning collaboratives had a statistically significant positive effect on SFA knowledge (β = 0.87, 95% CrI, 0.56–1.17), confidence in using SFA (β = 0.84, 95% CrI, 0.52–1.16), and endorsement of SFA beliefs (β = 0.29, 95% CrI, 0.11–0.48) (Table 4).

There was a statistically significant, large, and positive effect of the SFA/HRW intervention correlating with an increase in participants’ use of SFA practices (β = 0.91, 95% CrI, 0.39–1.44), supported by participants indicated using SFA across the four-month study period.

“So, my experience with using Stress First Aid over the past four months has been it’s been an educational tool for me… I think what I’ve noticed is just being better able to handle stress factors that come up.” (Participant #12, Worker).

There was a statistically significant, medium, positive effect of increased observation of others using SFA (β = 0.69, 95% CrI, 0.17–1.21) (Table 4). The use of SFA practices to help a coworker notably increased by more than 1.6 times from baseline (52%) to 92.31% at 2-months and 85% at 4-months.

Secondary outcomes

There was no significant change in any of the well-being outcomes (i.e., social support, burnout, Job-related Affective Well-being, and secondary traumatic stress, coping strategies, and self-efficacy).

Though there was no significant change in well-being outcomes, including coping strategies, participants discussed the relevance of SFA to managing their well-being, including using SFA as a tool that may add to their existing coping strategies.

“I think that I have natural supports that kind of have prepared me to deal with stressful situations, but I did see that the Stress First Aid kind of mirrored those…I felt it [was] of added value.” (Participant #2, Worker).

The non-significant changes for social support were in the expected direction. There were no changes in turnover intentions, or work engagement, with the exception of the absorption scale. Unexpectedly, there were non-significant increases in burnout, Job-related Affective Well-being, secondary traumatic. Stress, particularly in the two-month time period.

Preliminary implementation outcomes

The SFA/HRW model was on average considered highly acceptable (88%), appropriate (91%), and feasible (89%) across the sample population using the Acceptability of Intervention Measure (AIM), Intervention Appropriateness Measure (IAM), and Feasibility of Intervention Measure (FIM) [29].

Participants found SFA/HRW valuable and critical to how they handle stress in their daily work.

“I do think it’s valuable. I do think it’s important to acknowledge it. I do think it’s important to have that culture that addresses and understands that there’s stress at the workplace… I’m just grateful we got to be a part of [SFA training] and I think it’s a critical part of the work we do.” (Participant #6, Leader).

Participants believed SFA to be appropriate for the harm reduction workforce, aligning with the work they do.

“[SFA] goes hand in hand with what we do… that goes right along again with the peer support. And the fact that because we’re all peer support except for a few administrative and we’re in the recovery world and we’re all connected. It’s easy, yeah. Because wellness tools are in our lives. Yeah, in our daily lives, right? Yeah. And so, I connect this a lot with wellness tools and action planning.” (Participant #15, Worker).

Participants also described that SFA helped them identify experiences adversely impacting their well-being that they had not previously recognized.

“It wasn’t until I worked here that I realized I dealt with secondary trauma as a case manager. And I did crisis case management and recognizing all the secondary trauma experienced with that was quite a bit and it was in fact affecting me, but no one, no one dealt with it. No one talked about it. No one even identified it, and I think you know there’s just a critical part of this whole process is even just identifying. But then also being able to understand where you’re at in the process. You know, I know going through the [surveys] and stuff, I was able to say ‘No, I’m not as stressed as I sometimes think I [am].’ You know, there’s a lot of things that aren’t, you know, that was helpful for me too.” (Participant #6, Leader).

Participants not only found SFA to be feasible and sustainable, but also a tool they will maintain in the future.

“I do foresee myself using this quite a bit more in the near future- the future…” (Participant #26, Leader).

Additionally, participants commented on the SFA/HRW model and how the monthly learning collaboratives created for a psychologically safe, non-judgmental space to discuss occupational stress and feel accepted by their peers.

“Just to know that the stress is validated. And it’s not that we don’t have to be ashamed about it today and that your other colleagues also feel stressed at times and that you know, it’s accepted, and we are able to have like ways to get over it or get better. You know what I mean? Like coping mechanisms and just to be able to share it with another person, ‘Hey, like I’m really stressed out’ and to not feel like The Lone Ranger, you know?” (Participant #1, Worker).

Discussion

The harm reduction workforce is exposed to high levels of occupational stress and trauma due to the complex nature and context of their work with vulnerable people who use drugs (PWUD). Additionally, policy changes around funding for community health work are frequent, which has implications for the workforce including job security and extraneous occupational stress levels. However, little attention has been paid to the occupational well-being of harm reduction workers, and, to the authors’ knowledge, there are no evidence-informed interventions tailored to this workforce. The aim of this study was to pilot test and evaluate the preliminary acceptability and feasibility of Stress First Aid (SFA), a peer-to-peer occupational stress intervention, for the harm reduction workforce. Using mixed methods, we assessed knowledge, confidence, beliefs, use of SFA, and preliminary implementation and well-being outcomes among a sample of HRWs in the U.S.

Our findings indicated that the adapted SFA model significantly improved SFA-specific knowledge, confidence, and observed practices among participants. Although small, the credible intervals suggest a statistically significant positive effect of the SFA model effect on endorsement of SFA beliefs. We also found a significant increase in self-reported use of SFA and observation of others using SFA practices, though this slightly decreased in effect by the 4-month follow-up. These results, paired with qualitative findings, indicate the adapted SFA model is appropriate, acceptable, useful, and shows preliminary effectiveness for HRWs.

Though the results regarding burnout, social support, and job-related well-being are mixed, with some positive short-term improvements at 2-months, measures of well-being, coping, secondary traumatic stress, burnout, and turnover intentions remained stable. The non-significant change in secondary traumatic stress was a non-significant decrease in intrusive thoughts, avoidance of thoughts related to trauma, and arousal or irritability. Though job-related well-being saw an overall positive increase at 2-months that was not sustained at 4-months, it remained relatively stable to that at baseline. Similar stability and fluctuations have been observed in other SFA studies where burnout remained relatively unchanged and job-related well-being tends to increase short term but is not always sustained long-term [20, 27]. Future research testing SFA/HRW with a time-matched control group over a longer follow-up period is needed to better assess the impact of SFA on these well-being outcomes.

Though SFA/HRW had uncertain effects on coping strategies, participants qualitatively indicated that SFA/HRW was an added asset to their existing repertoire of coping strategies. Throughout the 4-month study period, we observed a positive trend in self-efficacy or confidence to manage difficult situations among participants. Bellehsen and colleagues [27] evaluated SFA with frontline healthcare workers during the COVID-19 pandemic and similarly found significant increases from baseline to 12-month follow-up for self-efficacy, as well as resource awareness and resilience.

We also observed low ratings on turnover intentions, which remained stable over time. In a volatile environment both in the field and externally (e.g., policy changes and funding changes), stability suggests no deterioration of well-being, which may be as important as improvements, and suggests resilience among this population. Additionally, there was perceived organizational care of the harm reduction workforce as participants felt a sense of validation from peers and leadership by connecting in non-judgmental spaces on experiencing similar stressors and leaders providing reminders of existing available mental health resources. These opportunities for open communication and peer support have been noted as indicators of culture change in the recognition of the importance of organizational-level support on the well-being of the harm reduction workforce [41].

SFA has the potential to drive organizational change and establish norms that prioritize managing occupational stress within the harm reduction workforce [20], who tend to prioritize the well-being of the populations they serve over their own well-being [42]. This re-prioritization of stable well-being in the harm reduction workforce may lead to improving quality care for the populations they serve.

Study limitations

These findings should be considered in light of several limitations. Notably, the pilot was implemented during a period marked by significant external events, including the 2024 presidential election and accompanying policy shifts related to healthcare services, personnel changes impacting the pilot site, and the winter holidays falling around the 2-month timepoint. These contextual factors may have contributed to a drop in well-being outcomes at 2-months, followed by a rebound at 4-months. It is important to note that well-being outcomes ultimately remained stable across the study.

Additionally, changes in well-being outcomes often require more time to process and sustain. While findings were mixed or inconclusive for certain measures, this does not rule out the possibility that SFA could influence these outcomes in the future. Additionally, we were not satisfied that the emotional and informational support measures adequately captured social support in a workplace setting. A measure of coworker and supervisor support may be a more sensitive way of capturing occupational social support. Additionally, moral injury was expressed by this population and should be measured in the future as part of measuring the well-being of the harm reduction workforce.

Without a control group, the generalizability of the significance of our findings is limited. The next steps of this study are to conduct a randomized controlled trial that will reach a larger geographic and demographic breadth of the harm reduction workforce and enable assessment of well-being outcomes across a longer period of time.

Conclusions

The harm reduction workforce is critical in fighting the opioid overdose crisis and in providing preventative care to PWUD. In this work, HRWs are exposed to high rates of occupational stress and trauma, which in turn are associated with the long-term negative impacts on their well-being. This study addresses the critical gap in research on the occupational health of the harm reduction workforce.

The adapted SFA model is a promising organizational intervention that may be used to inform responses to occupational stressors and impact trends in occupational well-being among the harm reduction workforce. Additionally, SFA/HRW has shown promise in building a sustainable workforce in a high-stress environment. The next steps are to conduct a randomized controlled trial to examine the effectiveness of SFA/HRW across a broader sample of the harm reduction workforce across multiple states and tribal areas and measure well-being across a longer period with a control group comparator.
