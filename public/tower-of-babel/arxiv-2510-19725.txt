CommonSense : Efficient Set Intersection (SetX) Protocol Based on Compressed Sensing
Jingfan Meng, Tianji Yang, Jun Xu

Abstract
Set reconciliation (SetR) is an important research problem that has been studied for over two decades. In this problem, two large sets 𝖠 \mathsf{A} and 𝖡 \mathsf{B} of objects (tokens, files, records, etc.) are stored respectively at two different network-connected hosts, which we name Alice and Bob respectively. Alice and Bob need to communicate with each other to learn the set union 𝖠 ∪ 𝖡 \mathsf{A}\cup\mathsf{B} (which then becomes their reconciled state), at low communication and computation costs. In this work, we study a different problem intricately related to SetR: Alice and Bob collaboratively compute 𝖠 ∩ 𝖡 \mathsf{A}\cap\mathsf{B} . We call this problem SetX (set intersection). Although SetX is just as important as SetR, it has never been properly studied in its own right. Rather, there is an unspoken perception by the research community that SetR and SetX are equally difficult (in costs), and hence “roughly equivalent.” Our first contribution is to show that SetX is fundamentally a much “cheaper” problem than SetR, debunking this long-standing perception. Our second contribution is to develop a novel SetX solution, the communication cost of which handily beats the information-theoretic lower bound of SetR. This protocol is based on the idea of compressed sensing (CS), which we describe here only for the special case of 𝖠 ⊆ 𝖡 \mathsf{A}\subseteq\mathsf{B} (We do have a more sophisticated protocol for the general case). Our protocol is for Alice to encode 𝖠 \mathsf{A} into a CS sketch 𝐌𝟏 𝖠 \mathbf{M}\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{A}} and send it to Bob, where 𝐌 \mathbf{M} is a CS matrix with l l rows and 𝟏 𝖠 \mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{A}} is the binary vector representation of 𝖠 \mathsf{A} . Our key innovation here is to make l l (the sketch size) just large enough (for the sketch) to summarize 𝖡 ∖ 𝖠 \mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A} (what Alice misses). In contrast, in existing protocols l l needs to be large enough to summarize 𝖠 \mathsf{A} (what Alice knows), which is typically much larger in cardinality. Our third contribution is to design a CS matrix 𝐌 \mathbf{M} that is both “friendly” to (the performance of) applications and “compliant” with CS theory.

Introduction

Set reconciliation (SetR) is a fundamental algorithmic problem that
arises in many networking, system, and database applications, and hence has
been studied for over two
decades
[

,
2
,
3
,

In this problem, two large sets
\mathsf{A}

\mathsf{B}

Alice and Bob need to communicate with each other to learn the set union

{\mathsf{A}\cup\mathsf{B}}

communication (between Alice and Bob) and computation (at both Alice and Bob)

In this work, we study a different research problem that is intricately related

In this problem, which we call SetX (

Alice and Bob need to collaboratively compute
{\mathsf{A}\cap\mathsf{B}}

{\mathsf{A}\cup\mathsf{B}}

{\mathsf{A}\setminus\mathsf{B}}

{\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}}
).

1.1
The First Contribution: SetX
Is Fundamentally Cheaper Than SetR

Perhaps due to the
above fact, there is an unspoken perception by the research community that the
two research problems SetR and SetX are equally difficult and
expensive (in communication and computation costs).
Indeed, this perception is in full display in a fairly recent SetR
solution called Graphene
[
7
]
.
In Graphene, Alice and Bob need to solve SetX in the special case of

\mathsf{A}\subseteq\mathsf{B}
.
The proposed solution, to be described in
§​​ 8.3
, employs a

full-fledged
SetR protocol as a subroutine, even though it only
uses the “SetX part” of this subroutine’s output.
Had the Graphene authors questioned this perception, they would have searched
for a much cheaper SetX-specific subroutine as we do in this paper.

For the reasons above, it is fair to say that SetX has never been
properly studied in its own right, although SetX has just as many applications
as SetR that we will elaborate in
§​​ 2
.
The
first contribution
of this paper is to show conclusively that this
long-standing perception is not correct, and that SetX is
fundamentally a much “cheaper” problem than SetR.
We do so by establishing a large gap between the information-theoretic lower
bounds on the communication costs of SetR and SetX (as we
will show shortly).
Moreover, we show that a practical SetX protocol (which will be our
next contribution) handily beats the information-theoretic lower bound of the
SetR established in
[
1
]

Before describing the lower bounds, we introduce some notations on set
cardinalities and state an assumption implicitly made in all existing studies
of
SetR
[
5
,
2
,
8
]

We denote Alice’s (set’s) cardinality by
|\mathsf{A}|

d\triangleq|{\mathsf{A}\triangle\mathsf{B}}|

is the symmetric difference between

\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{B}

The assumption is that
d\ll|\mathsf{A}|
and
d\ll|\mathsf{B}|

In other words,
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{B}
are much larger than their SDC.
This assumption holds in virtually all practical applications of SetR.
For example, in file synchronization, the difference between two files is
usually much smaller than the two files per se
[
9
]
.
We continue to use this assumption in studying SetX, because it also
holds in virtually all applications of SetX.

In fact, without this assumption, SetR would not be an interesting
problem (that is worth studying), for the following reason.
When this assumption does not hold (or when
|{\mathsf{A}\triangle\mathsf{B}}|
is on the same order
as
|\mathsf{A}|
and
|\mathsf{B}|

of sending
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{B}
respectively (in its entirety or its

summary, see
§​​ 8.1
) to each

SetR, so all advanced protocols (despite having high computation costs)
can only achieve meager savings.
The same statement and reason can also be said about SetX.

For the SetX problem, we further assume that the size of the universe

\mathsf{U}
is much larger than
|\mathsf{A}|
and
|\mathsf{B}|
.
This assumption holds for virtually all applications of SetX and
SetR for the following reason: The identifier of an object is usually
its hash value (say
u
bits long, where
|\mathsf{U}|=2^{u}

which needs to be long enough so that no two distinct objects in
{\mathsf{A}\cup\mathsf{B}}
can
have the same hash value (with a “surprisingly” high probability predicted by

[
10

For example, in the Ethereum dataset in

2^{256}
(as the identifier of each Ethereum account is a

Finally, we add a “benign” assumption for the ease of presentation:

|\mathsf{A}|\leq|\mathsf{B}|
without loss of generality (since otherwise, we can
swap the roles of “Alice” and “Bob”).

We now formalize these three assumptions into the following inequality.

d\ll|\mathsf{A}|\leq|\mathsf{B}|\ll 2^{u}.

(1)

As we will show in

bits by

1.2
Second Contribution:
CommonSense

Protocol for SetX

In
this section, we describe the second contribution of this work: a novel
solution to SetX.
We call our solution
CommonSense
because of the following two facts.
First, in SetX Alice and Bob learns their
common
elements (
{\mathsf{A}\cap\mathsf{B}}
).
Second, our solution is based on the idea of compressed
sensing

(CS)
[
13
]
.

To describe
CommonSense
, we first provide a crash course on CS.
For reasons that will be clear later, we need to recover a

d
-sparse
(containing
d
nonzero scalars)

signal
vector
\vec{\mathbf{x}}
, which lies in a high-dimensional space

\mathcal{S}
(say of dimension
n
), from an indirect
linear
measurement
(vector)
\vec{\mathbf{r}}\triangleq\mathbf{M}\vec{\mathbf{x}}
of it, wherein

\mathbf{M}
is an
l\times n

CS matrix
(which is
randomly generated and fixed hereafter).
This vector
\vec{\mathbf{r}}
is often also called a
linear sketch
, because in
many CS applications it can be viewed as sketching (succinctly summarizing) a
sparse (system state) vector
\vec{\mathbf{x}}
using a suitable linear transformation

\mathbf{M}
.
We introduce the term
sketch
here solely for the ease of presentation in
the sequel.
Although there is indeed a deep connection between CS and data
streaming/sketching
[
14
]
, this connection is mostly
orthogonal to
CommonSense
.

A key result in CS theory is that, when
\mathbf{M}
satisfies a nice property
(called
restricted isometry property
, or RIP, which we will elaborate in

Definition

4
), with high probability the dimension
l
of the
linear measurement
\vec{\mathbf{r}}
only needs to be slightly larger than
d
,
or more precisely
l=O(d\log(n/d))
, to
mathematically “pin down”
\vec{\mathbf{x}}
, in the sense
\vec{\mathbf{x}}
is the unique

sparse
solution to the system of linear equations
\vec{\mathbf{r}}=\mathbf{M}\vec{\mathbf{v}}
(here, the word “sparse” is essential, since by
l\ll n
, there are infinitely many dense solutions).
Furthermore, we can recover (compute) the correct sparse solution
\vec{\mathbf{x}}

using MP (matching pursuit, to be elaborated in
Procedure 1
), a family
of fast decoding algorithms.
This computation is called
sparse recovery
in the CS literature.

Here, we describe a strawman CS-based
CommonSense
protocol only for the
special (
unidirectional
) case of
\mathsf{A}\subseteq\mathsf{B}
while
postponing the presentation of the
CommonSense
protocol for the general
(
bidirectional
) case of
\mathsf{A}\not\subseteq\mathsf{B}
and
\mathsf{B}\not\subseteq\mathsf{A}
until
§​​ 5
.
Our protocol (
Figure

1(a)
) is for Alice to encode
\mathsf{A}
into a CS
sketch vector
\mathbf{M}\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{A}}
(an
l
-dimensional linear
measurement) in step
1
, where
\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{A}}
denotes an

|\mathsf{A}|
-sparse binary vector representation of
\mathsf{A}
(to be precisely
defined in
§​​ 3.1
), and so on for

\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}}
and
\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{B}}
that will appear shortly.
Once Bob receives
\mathbf{M}\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{A}}
, he computes
\mathbf{M}\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}}
as
\mathbf{M}\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{B}}
(which Bob knows) minus

\mathbf{M}\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{A}}
(step
2
).

We need to highlight a fact here: Since the signal that Bob tries to recover is

\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}}
,
l
only needs to be slightly larger than

|{\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}}|
, for him to losslessly recover
{\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}}
with high
probability empirically (so that he can compute
{\mathsf{A}\cap\mathsf{B}}=\mathsf{B}\setminus({\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}})
).
In other words, in our solution,
l
only needs to be large enough
(for the sketch) to summarize
{\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}}
(what Alice misses).
In contrast, in existing SetX protocols such as Graphene,

l
(or its equivalence) needs to be large enough to summarize

\mathsf{A}
(what Alice knows), which is typically much larger in cardinality as
we have assumed in
§​​ 1.1
.
Thanks to this difference (that significantly reduces
l
) and an
entropy compression scheme (to be described in
C
) that
encodes each of the
l
scalars in
\mathbf{M}\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{A}}
using as few
bits as possible, our
CommonSense
protocol is extremely
communication-efficient and can handily beat the information-theoretic lower
bound of the SetR problem, as will be shown
in
§​​ 7.2
.

1.3
Third Contribution: Friendly and Compliant CS Matrix

In our third contribution, we identify a CS matrix
\mathbf{M}
that
simultaneously has the following two desirable properties.
First,
\mathbf{M}
needs to be “friendly” to (the performance of) the
networking applications of CS in general, and our
CommonSense
protocol in
particular in the following sense: The computation cost of updating
the compressed-sensed signal
\mathbf{M}\vec{\mathbf{x}}
for a
1-sparse change
(to be defined shortly)
should be very low.
Second,
\mathbf{M}
needs to be “compliant” with CS theory in the sense
it needs to satisfy a certain form of the aforementioned RIP, so that
using
\mathbf{M}
allows us to “provably benefit” from the correctness and
(decoding) time complexity guarantees established in the CS theory.
However, to satisfy both properties turns out to be a tall order because the
“friendly” property generally demands that
\mathbf{M}
be a binary sparse
matrix whereas the “compliant” property demands that
\mathbf{M}
be a dense
matrix, as we will elaborate next.
We feel incredibly lucky to find an
\mathbf{M}
that manages to “walk the
tightrope” between these two properties.

We first explain why being “compliant” demands
\mathbf{M}
to be dense.
As mentioned earlier, for the sparse recovery to succeed (in computing the
correct
{\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}}
),
\mathbf{M}
needs to satisfy the RIP, which, by

Definition

4
, means that
\mathbf{M}
approximately preserves the
L_{2}

distance when operating on (multiplying to) any vector
\vec{\mathbf{v}}
.
All CS matrices that satisfy RIP are dense
[
15
]
, with the
Gaussian random matrix being a notable example.

However, to be “friendly” to networking applications, it is much more
desirable for
\mathbf{M}
to be sparse than to be dense, for the following two
reasons.
First, in some networking applications of CS, the signal
\vec{\mathbf{x}}
to be
measured often needs to be constantly updated in a 1-sparse manner, that is,

\vec{\mathbf{x}}_{new}\leftarrow\vec{\mathbf{x}}_{old}+\eta\vec{\mathbf{e}}_{i}
where
\eta
is a scalar
and
\vec{\mathbf{e}}_{i}
is the
i^{th}
standard basis vector (in which only the

i^{th}
scalar is one).
In this case,
\mathbf{M}\vec{\mathbf{x}}
needs to be suitably updated (from

\mathbf{M}\vec{\mathbf{x}}_{old}
to
\mathbf{M}\vec{\mathbf{x}}_{new}
), which is to add the

i^{th}
column of
\mathbf{M}
(scaled by
\eta
) to the vector

\mathbf{M}\vec{\mathbf{x}}_{old}
.
When
\mathbf{M}
is sparse, the cost (time complexity) of this update is only

O(\log(|\mathsf{B}|/d))
(by
Theorem

8
), whereas when
\mathbf{M}
is
dense, this cost is
O(l)=O(d\log(|\mathsf{B}|/d))
,
which can be orders of magnitude larger.
In network measurement applications where
\mathbf{M}\vec{\mathbf{x}}
is maintained as a
(linear)
sketch
such as in
[
16
]
, and this sketch needs to be
updated upon every packet arrival, the
O(l)
update cost (when

\mathbf{M}
is dense) can be too high for the packet stream on a high-speed
link (e.g., 100 Gbps).
Furthermore, even in applications where the encoding of
\vec{\mathbf{x}}
is done by
multiplying
\mathbf{M}
to it in one shot, using a sparse
\mathbf{M}
can
reduce this encoding cost by a factor of
d
, as we will elaborate
in
§​​ 3.3
.
Second, the computation cost of sparse recovery when
\mathbf{M}
is dense can
be at least
O(d/\log|\mathsf{B}|)
times larger than when
\mathbf{M}

is sparse, according to
B
.

In fact, our SetX solution is even pickier on
\mathbf{M}
: We want

\mathbf{M}
to be not only sparse, but also binary.
In this way, most scalars in
\mathbf{M}\vec{\mathbf{x}}
are small integers that take
few bits to store (and transmit), which achieves our goal of aggressively
compressing
\mathbf{M}\vec{\mathbf{x}}
.
However, this ideal
\mathbf{M}
appears to contradict a recent negative result
established experimentally in
[
16
]
, which shows that such

\mathbf{M}
is so “far away” from the RIP that correct sparse recovery is
infeasible using existing algorithms (such as OMP
[
17
]
).
Since the work
[
16
]
also needs the matrix to be sparse (to maintain
a sketch), it proposes a compromise: a sparse
\mathbf{M}
with each nonzero
entry having a Gaussian distribution.
It is shown in
[
16
]
that this
\mathbf{M}
is “slightly closer” to
the RIP in the sense that it empirically leads to successful (nearly correct)
decoding but still has no theoretical guarantees.
However, this design does not work for our purpose, since each linear
measurement in the message
\mathbf{M}\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{A}}
would be a real (floating point)
number, which takes several times more space to store (and transmit) than a
small integer that a binary sparse would afford us, and as a result cancels out
much of the hard-earned compression (ratio) that can be achieved by our
solution.

We solve this problem (
\mathbf{M}
being far away from the RIP) by discovering
and proving that the binary sparse
\mathbf{M}
used in
CommonSense

satisfies a different RIP condition called RIP-1, a concept introduced
in
[
18
]
that means approximate distance preservation in
L_{1}

(Manhattan) distance.
Thanks to this RIP-1, sparse recovery can be done efficiently using a variant
of MP designed for the
L_{1}
space: SSMP (sparse sequential matching
pursuit)
[
19
]
.
We also discover that, since
\vec{\mathbf{x}}
in our CS equation
\vec{\mathbf{r}}=\mathbf{M}\vec{\mathbf{x}}
must be a binary vector, the vanilla MP
algorithm
[
20
]
designed for the
L_{2}
space, which
runs faster than SSMP, can also correctly recover
\vec{\mathbf{x}}
empirically.
Furthermore, we are the first to implement the data structures powering SSMP
[
19
]
, to use them for speeding up the vanilla MP (with
nontrivial adaptions such as
9
), and to publish our source code and datasets at
[
21
]
.
To the best of our knowledge, these data structures do not have a publicly available software
implementation as are typical for theory works like SSMP.

2.1
Propagation of New Blocks (Blockchain)

Blockchains are distributed ledgers that often run on peer-to-peer computer
networks
[
7
]
.
In a typical blockchain system, new (unvalidated) transactions are often
relayed among peers upon generation, and they are temporarily stored in a

mempool
.
A special group of peers, called
miners
, validate transactions and add
them into blocks that are certified by cryptographic
hashing
[
22
]
.
A newly generated block needs to be propagated to the entire network and agreed
on by all peers under a
consensus mechanism
before the transactions on
it are finalized.
Hence, efficient propagation of new blocks is crucial to achieving a high
throughput (transactions per second).

The Graphene paper
[
7
]
has shown that communication-efficient
propagation of new blocks can be reduced to unidirectional SetX as
follows.
Suppose a peer named Alice just received a new block and wants to send it to
another peer named Bob.
Let the set of all transactions in that block be Alice’s set
\mathsf{A}
, and
the set of all unvalidated transactions in Bob’s mempool be his set
\mathsf{B}
.
In most cases (thanks to the aggressive relay of new transactions in most
blockchain implementations
[
7
]
),
\mathsf{B}
is a superset of

\mathsf{A}
.
As a result, Bob will find all transactions on the new block if he computes

{\mathsf{A}\cap\mathsf{B}}
(
=\mathsf{A}
) using a unidirectional SetX protocol.
According to
[
7
]
, this approach uses much
less communication cost than a full-fledged SetR protocol.

2.2
Packet Loss Detection (Network
Measurement)

Detecting packet losses in data center networks is crucial to diagnosing the
root causes of failures and mitigating their impacts.
LossRadar
[
23
]
is a well-known algorithm to this problem.
In LossRadar, packet losses are detected by computing the difference between
the packets that traverse an upstream meter (switch) and a downstream meter,
with each packet identified by its signature consisting of a 5-tuple flow ID
and a packet ID which is consecutive within its flow.
Each switch along the path, in the data plane, digests all traversing packets
into an invertible Bloom lookup table (IBLT)
[
5
]
.
Then, packet losses are identified in the control plane by comparing the
differences of the two digests (IBLTs).
A key performance metric of this problem is the size of the digest, since
high-speed data plane memory is a scarce commodity on programmable network
switches.
However, the IBLTs are not memory efficient, as we will show
in
§​​ 8.2
.

We note that the streaming version of
CommonSense
, to be elaborated
in
§​​ 4
, generates much leaner digests than IBLTs (to recover
the same number of lost packets), since the detection of packet losses between
two switches can be solved by a SetX protocol as follows.
Treating the downstream meter as Alice and the upstream meter as Bob, with the
packets traversing them denoted by
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{B}
respectively
(
\mathsf{A}\subseteq\mathsf{B}
), the packets lost in transmission are those in

\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}
, which Bob computes in a SetX protocol
as mentioned in
§​​ 1.2
.

Attentive readers who have read
§​​ 4
may realize that

CommonSense
’s decoder in the data streaming scenario requires a superset

\mathsf{B}^{\prime}\supseteq\mathsf{B}
of all packets along this path.
Fortunately,
\mathsf{B}^{\prime}
can be readily collected by only incurring a small
overhead on the first switch on the path for the following reasons.
The set of all 5-tuple flow IDs can be recorded using existing methods such as
FlowRadar
[
24
]
, and it is not hard to conservatively estimate
the range of packet IDs of each flow given that packet IDs are consecutive.

2.3
Straggler Identification (Data Stream
Processing)

Identifying the
stragglers
in a data stream is an important research
problem motivated by the need to track outstanding resource allocations with a
minimum working set size
[
25
]
.
To describe this problem, imagine that a library manages a catalog of millions
of books using a single computer that is
very fast but has limited
memory
.
Many people visit this library each day, who generate a data stream consisting
of millions of borrowing and returning records.
It is stated in the rules that all borrowed books must be returned by the end
of the day, and most (but regrettably not all) visitors abide by the rules.
The staffs want to know, at the end of a day, the set of books that are not
returned yet (i.e., the stragglers).
This is a nontrivial data streaming problem, since their
computer does not have enough memory either to load the entire catalog or to
track all outstanding books (borrowed but not returned) during the day.

The state-of-the-art solution is to keep an IBLT in
memory
[
25
]
.
Similarly as above, this IBLT can also be replaced by a much smaller digest from
the streaming version of
CommonSense
as follows.
Denote by
\mathsf{A}
the set of outstanding books.
In
§​​ 4
, we will show that
CommonSense
can track any
addition to
\mathsf{A}
(new borrows) or deletion from it (new returns)
efficiently using a small digest.
Given the catalog of books (the aforementioned
\mathsf{B}^{\prime}
), the final state of

\mathsf{A}
can be losslessly recovered from the digest.

2.5
Matching Changes for Delta Synchronization (Cloud File Storage)

File synchronization is an essential functionality of cloud storage services
such as Dropbox
[
28
]
.
To save communication bandwidth, most cloud storage services adopt a

delta synchronization
mechanism, in which only the modifications (i.e.,

delta
) are transmitted between the server and the clients.
A typical delta synchronization protocol, such as rsync
[
9
]
,
works as follows.
During preprocessing, all stored files are cut into multiple
chunks
so
that most user edits only involve one or two chunks
1
1

1

conventional fixed-size chunks can only handle replacement edits, recent

content-defined chunking

[
29

29

.
Then, in the

The problem of identifying deltas is actually equivalent to bidirectional

Without loss of generality, consider a client named Alice who is making edits
and a server named Bob who is synchronizing these edits.
We denote the set of Alice’s and Bob’s chunks by
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{B}
,
respectively.
In the matching stage, Alice’s goal is to compute
\mathsf{A}\setminus\mathsf{B}

her unique (recently modified) chunks to be sent to Bob, and Bob’s goal is to

\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}

In fact, many SetR protocols also take file synchronization as a key

The functionality of a SetX protocol, as we just mentioned, is to
allow Alice to “push” her edits (
\mathsf{A}\setminus\mathsf{B}
) to Bob.
In comparison, a SetR protocol also allows Bob to list these chunks
(that he misses from Alice), so that he can actively “fetch” such a chunk if
he needs it urgently.
However, this fetching functionality comes with a much larger communication
cost.

As mentioned in

(2)

The following

CommonSense
on Data Streams

In this section, we describe how

can be adapted to data

§​​ 2.2

and
§​​ 2.3
.
This adaptation addresses the following algorithmic aspects in which a typical

in
§​​ 1
.

1.

The sets
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{B}
are not directly given; instead,
their
elements (or deletions of their elements as
in
§​​ 2.3
)

The time complexity of operating on each element needs to be low.

2.

Instead of a small communication cost, the primary goal is low memory
usage (small sketch size)
when processing the data streams.

3.

The sketch is usually decoded offline (after
both data streams are processed), and in general, the decoder only
knows a predetermined
superset
\mathsf{B}^{\prime}
of
\mathsf{B}
(since recording
\mathsf{B}
also

To address

, the streaming version of

\vec{\mathbf{r}}
in memory, which is initialized to all zeros.
Upon the addition (or deletion) of an element
i
,
\vec{\mathbf{m}}_{i}
is added to
(or subtracted from)
\vec{\mathbf{r}}
(which addresses
item 1
).
In this way, we can still get
\mathbf{M}\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}}
by subtracting
Alice’s measurement from Bob’s and then reconstruct
\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}}

losslessly using the MP decoder (with
\mathsf{B}
replaced by
\mathsf{B}^{\prime}

as a result of
item 3
).
For this reason, we need to substitute
|\mathsf{B}|
with

|\mathsf{B}^{\prime}|
when stating all the time and space complexity results in

Theorem

2
.
That is, the space usage of storing the measurement is
O(l)=O(d\log(|\mathsf{B}^{\prime}|/d))
, the time complexity of updating each
element is
O(m)=O(\log(|\mathsf{B}^{\prime}|/d))
, and the time
complexity for decoding
{\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}}
is
O(|\mathsf{B}^{\prime}|(\log|\mathsf{B}^{\prime}|)\log(|\mathsf{B}^{\prime}|/d))
.

Bidirectional Solution

In this section, we extend
CommonSense
into a bidirectional SetX
protocol that continues to work when neither set is a subset of the other (i.e.,

\mathsf{A}\not\subseteq\mathsf{B}
and
\mathsf{B}\not\subseteq\mathsf{A}
).
In this case, Alice needs to recover all her
unique
elements

{\mathsf{A}\setminus\mathsf{B}}
to arrive at
{\mathsf{A}\cap\mathsf{B}}=\mathsf{A}\setminus({\mathsf{A}\setminus\mathsf{B}})
, and
similarly, Bob needs to recover
{\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}}
.
To this end, Alice and Bob perform
ping-pong decoding
3
3

3

Although
the term
ping-pong decoding
is borrowed from the Graphene
paper
[
7

(
§​​ 5.1
) as follows.
Alice first sends
\mathbf{M}\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{A}}
to Bob like in the unidirectional case
above.
Bob then computes
\mathbf{M}\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{B}}-\mathbf{M}\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{A}}=\mathbf{M}\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}}-\mathbf{M}\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{A}\setminus\mathsf{B}}
(note the RHS
is not just
\mathbf{M}\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}}

tries to reconstruct the
signal component
(
\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}}
) therein using
the MP decoder. Then, the residue (to be formally defined shortly) is sent back to Alice for further reconstruction.
In this way, the residue ping-pongs between Alice and Bob until it becomes
\vec{0}
.
A zero residue guarantees that both Alice and Bob arrive at the correct set intersection
(to be proved in
§​​ 5.1
) provided that all so-called

common hallucinations
are avoided (to be described in
§​​ 5.2

Should Bob’s reconstruction (from
\mathbf{M}\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}}-\mathbf{M}\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{A}\setminus\mathsf{B}}
) be completely correct (in precisely recovering
\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}}
),
the residue would be precisely (
-\mathbf{M}\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{A}\setminus\mathsf{B}}
).
Then, sending this residue back to Alice would let her recover
\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{A}\setminus\mathsf{B}}
precisely (if
\mathbf{M}
satisfies RIP-1),
since in this case Alice is faced with the same reconstruction problem as in a hypothetical “reversed" (with
\mathsf{B}\subseteq\mathsf{A}
and Bob
initiating the protocol) unidirectional case. In other words, in this (ideal) case, a single ping-pong exchange between Alice and Bob is enough.

In reality, however, the following more realistic scenario usually happens.
Since (
-\mathbf{M}\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{A}\setminus\mathsf{B}}
) is purely
noise
to Bob as will be explained shortly, Bob usually cannot
reconstruct the signal (
\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}}
) from the “mixture" (
\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}}-\mathbf{M}\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{A}\setminus\mathsf{B}}
) in a completely
correct manner in the following sense: Bob can usually reconstruct “most of" its signal (
\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}}
), while making some (false positive and negative) errors.
As a result, the residue sent back to Alice would be (
-\mathbf{M}\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{A}\setminus\mathsf{B}}
) plus (the “effect of") these errors.
Since Bob’s signal (
\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}}
) is purely noise to Alice, Bob’s (mostly correct) reconstruction significantly reduces this noise to just these errors, which in turn makes it much
easier for Alice to reconstruct most of her signal (
-\mathbf{M}\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{A}\setminus\mathsf{B}}
) in the next step.
As such, the actions of Alice and Bob in this ping-pong decoding process are mutually beneficial: Bob’s reconstruction (of his
signal) significantly reduces noise to Alice’s reconstruction, and vice versa.

While this mutually beneficial interaction explains how the ping-pong decoding
process makes (often good) progress, it alone cannot explain why the process can usually successfully finish (by reducing the final residue to
\vec{0}
) after only a few ping-pong rounds
empirically. The missing explanation is the
noise-resilience
property of MP
decoders: By design, an MP decoder can correct its
own
(decoding) errors (in the past)
once the
external
noise is removed or significantly reduced from the residue.
For example, as mentioned above, in the first round Bob would likely make some errors in reconstructing its signal (
\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}}
) in the presence of strong noise (
-\mathbf{M}\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{A}\setminus\mathsf{B}}
);
but the MP decoder allows Bob to correct, in the second round, most of his earlier errors, after Alice has significantly reduced this noise (which is Alice’s signal).

The communication cost and time complexity of
our bidirectional protocol can be summarized as follows.

Observation 10
.

CommonSense
empirically solves bidirectional SetX in
R\leq 10

rounds of communications, using
O(d\log(|\mathsf{B}|/d))
bits of total communication cost
(about twice the cost in the unidirectional case)
and
O(|\mathsf{B}|(\log|\mathsf{B}|)(\log(|\mathsf{B}|/d)+R))

complexity (see the paragraph after the proof of
Theorem 14

\mathbf{M}\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}}
is the noise.
Comparing these two scenarios (Alice being the initiator or Bob doing so), the
former case leads to a higher reconstruction quality (and a smaller communication
cost to reach lossless reconstruction eventually), because, under the assumption above,
its noise level
(
\|\mathbf{M}\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{A}\setminus\mathsf{B}}\|_{1}
) is statistically lower than the other
way around (
\|\mathbf{M}\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}}\|_{1}
).

5.2
Avoiding Common Hallucinations with Set Membership Filter
(SMF)

In this subsection, we describe the aforementioned
common hallucination

problem, in which an element (say
i
) becomes “hallucinatory” to both
Alice and Bob.
When this happens, both Alice and Bob will regard
i
as being unique to them,
even though by definition,
i
belongs to the set intersection

{\mathsf{A}\cap\mathsf{B}}
.
Hence, in order for Alice and Bob to compute the correct
{\mathsf{A}\cap\mathsf{B}}
, they
need to avoid all common hallucinations.
We describe one such solution using set membership filters (SMFs) in this
subsection.

Unfortunately, in the vanilla ping-pong decoding procedure shown
in
§​​ 5.1
,
hallucinations are contagious
.
We describe this fact using an example, in which Bob first develops
a hallucination on an element
i\in{\mathsf{A}\cap\mathsf{B}}
when trying to decode
\vec{\mathbf{r}}_{(1)}
(due to the noise
component
-\mathbf{M}\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{A}\setminus\mathsf{B}}
therein).
As a result, he mistakenly subtracts
\mathbf{M}\vec{\mathbf{e}}_{i}
(
\vec{\mathbf{e}}_{i}
is the
1-sparse binary vector whose only one is on the
i^{th}
coordinate) from the
residue
\vec{\mathbf{r}}_{(1)}
according to
9
, so
\vec{\mathbf{r}}_{(2)}

has a “signal” component
-\mathbf{M}\vec{\mathbf{e}}_{i}
in it (which is encompassed in the

-\mathbf{M}\mathbf{1}_{{\widehat{\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}}}}
term in
(4)
).
When
\vec{\mathbf{r}}_{(2)}
is passed on to Alice, Alice considers
\vec{\mathbf{e}}_{i}
to be a
part of her signal, because
i\in\mathsf{A}
(which means she can decode
i
),
and
-\mathbf{M}\vec{\mathbf{e}}_{i}
has the same minus sign as her actual signal

-\mathbf{M}\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{A}\setminus\mathsf{B}}
in
(4)
.
For this reason, Alice will likely include
i
in
{\widehat{\mathsf{A}\setminus\mathsf{B}}}
, or

catching this hallucination from Bob
, which results in a common
hallucination.

The aftermath of such a common hallucination is that
i
will be (mistakenly)
missing from the
{\mathsf{A}\cap\mathsf{B}}
computed by both Alice and Bob, for the following
reason.
When Alice catches the hallucination on
i
, she also subtracts

\mathbf{M}\vec{\mathbf{e}}_{i}
from
\vec{\mathbf{r}}_{(2)}
by
9
.
However, since her signal has an opposite sign as Bob’s, this substraction

cancels out
Bob’s previous (mistaken) subtraction of
\mathbf{M}\vec{\mathbf{e}}_{i}
, so
i
“disappear" from the residue for good.
As a result, both Alice and Bob will “happily” accept
i
as being unique to
them when the residue is reduced to zero.
Had Alice not developed the common hallucination on
i
, the signal
-\mathbf{M}\vec{\mathbf{e}}_{i}
would remain
in the residue, which would allow Bob to eventually
correct his hallucination
later in
ping-pong decoding, thanks to the aforementioned error resilience of MP decoders.

Before we state our fix to this problem, we highlight two aforementioned facts.
First, this problem does not affect the correct decoding of other “innocent”
elements unique to Bob (or Alice).
Second, although having some elements missing from (the computed)
{\mathsf{A}\cap\mathsf{B}}

is technically incorrect, it is innocuous in some applications.
For example, in file synchronization described in
§​​ 2.5
,
it is acceptable to have a few hallucinations in the delta (since they will
eventually be detected and removed when deltas are resolved).

The fix to common hallucination is to enforce the invariant that
{\widehat{\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}}}

and
{\widehat{\mathsf{A}\setminus\mathsf{B}}}
do not share any common element (or
({\widehat{\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}}})\cap({\widehat{\mathsf{A}\setminus\mathsf{B}}})=\emptyset
as mentioned
earlier), since such elements
are precisely common hallucinations.
To this end, Alice needs to know which elements have been added to

{\widehat{\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}}}
by Bob, and vice versa.
Here we only describe our solution for Alice, since that for Bob is
“symmetrically similar." In this solution, we let Bob encode
({\widehat{\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}}})

into a
set membership filter
(SMF, in
§​​ 8.1
) and
sends this filter to Alice along with his residue.
Correspondingly, the MP decoder (
9
) at Alice’s end will not update a signal
coordinate
i^{*}
(selected in the matching stage) if
i^{*}
tests positive in this filter.

So far, this solution is still incomplete, because all (implementations of) SMFs have
false positives
.
If an element in
{\mathsf{A}\setminus\mathsf{B}}
is a false positive to the SMF, then it will not
add
{\widehat{\mathsf{A}\setminus\mathsf{B}}}
(and hence will be erroneously missing from the
{\mathsf{A}\setminus\mathsf{B}}
computed by Alice).
This “false positive” problem can be solved by a “last inquiry” step as
follows.
After a few rounds of communications, when Alice becomes confident that Bob has corrected almost all his
hallucinations (which is expected now with the use of SMF to prevent Alice from
joining Bob in these hallucinations), she can proactively verify with Bob whether
each positive element (judged by the SMF) actually belongs to
{\mathsf{A}\setminus\mathsf{B}}
or is Bob’s
hallucination.
The communication cost of this “last inquiry” can be made very small, by
representing every positive element by a hash signature (that is large enough
to avoid “birthday attacks”
[
10
]
in which any two elements in
{\mathsf{A}\cup\mathsf{B}}
are
hashed to the same value).

In summary, whenever a signal coordinate
i^{*}
is selected by Alice’s MP
decoder but tests positive in the SMF, she has the following two options.

1.

Collision avoidance:
Initially, she ignores
this coordinate (to give Bob a chance to correct his hallucinations)
and selects the next coordinate that minimizes the
L_{2}
residue error.

2.

Collision resolution:
When confident,
she can first tentatively update these coordinates according to

9
and then verify these updates with Bob (through the “last inquiry").
All erroneous (hallucinatory) updates are then reverted.

Our invariant (
{\widehat{\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}}})\cap({\widehat{\mathsf{A}\setminus\mathsf{B}}})=\emptyset
always holds under
both options.
Therefore, as shown above, both Alice and Bob obtain the correct set
intersection once the residue is reduced to zero.

6
Information-Theoretic Lower Bound of SetX

In this section, we derive an information-theoretic lower bound on the
communication cost of the
\mathrm{SetX}_{\mathsf{U}}(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B})
problem
(computing the intersection between
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{B}
in universe

\mathsf{U}
).
It is well-known in information theory that the communication cost is lowered
bounded by the
reduction in entropy
.

In our derivation, we assume that both
|{\mathsf{A}\setminus\mathsf{B}}|
and
|{\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}}|
are known
beforehand (so we do not charge the communication cost of computing these
values).
From Alice’s perspective, the number of possible ways to partition
\mathsf{A}

into
{\mathsf{A}\cap\mathsf{B}}
and
{\mathsf{A}\setminus\mathsf{B}}
is
\binom{|\mathsf{A}|}{|{\mathsf{A}\setminus\mathsf{B}}|}
, which
corresponds to
\log_{2}\binom{|\mathsf{A}|}{|{\mathsf{A}\setminus\mathsf{B}}|}
bits of entropy under a
uniform prior distribution.
Similarly, Bob’s entropy is
\log_{2}\binom{|\mathsf{B}|}{|{\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}}|}
bits a
priori.
After running a SetX protocol, Alice and Bob know the exact set
intersection, so their entropies are both reduced to zero.
As a result, we have

\begin{split}\mathrm{Comm.\;Cost}[\mathrm{SetX}_{\mathsf{U}}(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B})]&\geq\log_{2}\binom{|\mathsf{A}|}{|{\mathsf{A}\setminus\mathsf{B}}|}+\log_{2}\binom{|\mathsf{B}|}{|{\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}}|}\\
&\approx|{\mathsf{A}\setminus\mathsf{B}}|\log_{2}(e|\mathsf{A}|/|{\mathsf{A}\setminus\mathsf{B}}|)+|{\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}}|\log_{2}(e|\mathsf{B}|/|{\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}}|)\geq d\log_{2}(e|\mathsf{A}|/d).\end{split}

(6)

In
(6)
, the approximation (
\approx
) is by the Stirling’s
formula, and the last inequality is by the facts
|\mathsf{A}|\leq|\mathsf{B}|
,
|{\mathsf{A}\setminus\mathsf{B}}|\leq d
, and
|{\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}}|\leq d
(
d
being the SDC).

Before moving on to evaluation, we remark that it is possible that the
information-theoretic lower bound in
(6)
cannot be achieved by
any practical protocol (say with a reasonable time complexity).
In fact, in theoretical computer science, the minimum communication cost
achievable by any (deterministic or randomized) protocol is called the

communication complexity

[
41
]
.
So far, we do now know whether the communication complexity of SetX (under a certain number of rounds of communications)
is equal to
(6)
or is strictly higher.
To the best of our knowledge, the most related result in the literature is on

set disjointness
(deciding whether
\mathsf{A}\cap\mathsf{B}
is
empty)
[
42
,
43
]
, and its randomized
communication complexity (when
|\mathsf{A}|=|\mathsf{B}|
) has been shown to be

\Omega(|\mathsf{B}|)

[
42
]
.
This value is much higher than the communication cost of
CommonSense
(a
randomized protocol), which is
O(d\log_{2}(|\mathsf{B}|/d))
(by

Theorem

2
) relying on a small
d
.
There is no contradiction between these two complexity values, however, because the former
result does not assume known
d
.

8.1
Approximate Set Membership Filters (SMF)

In the
set membership
problem, Alice wants to summarize
\mathsf{A}
into
a small filter, so that Bob, by querying this filter, knows whether an
arbitrary element
x
belongs to
\mathsf{A}
.
Different from SetX and SetR, a set membership filter (SMF)
cannot exploit shared elements between Alice and Bob.
It has been shown that the size of exact SMFs (that answer all queries
correctly) is lower bounded at

\log_{2}\binom{|\mathsf{U}|}{|\mathsf{A}|}

[
52
]
, so in large
universes, all practical SMFs are approximate.

Bloom filters (BF)
[
53
]
are arguably the
best-known approximate SMF.
The filter is an array of
l
bits (cells) that are initialized to
zeros.
To encode BF, for each element
a\in\mathsf{A}
,
a
is hashed to
m
cells
(by
m
independent hash functions) in the array, and these bits are set
to ones.
Finally, given an element
x
, the test (on whether
x
belongs to
\mathsf{A}
)
is positive if and only if all
m
cells that
x
is hashed to are ones.
It is well-known that BF tests have zero false negatives but occasional false
positives at a rate of
2^{-(\ln 2)\cdot|\mathsf{A}|/l}
.

Counting Bloom filters (CBFs)
[
37
]
extend BFs to support deletions of
elements from a BF.
In a CBF, each cell is a counter instead of a bit.
When an element
a\in\mathsf{A}
is added (or deleted) from the CBF, all counters
that
a
is hashed to are incremented (or decremented).
The resulting CBF can be tested similarly as BFs, by treating all nonzero
counters as ones.
However, deleting a false positive element from the CBF can lead to false
negative test results even for elements that are actually in
\mathsf{A}
.

Compressed BFs
[
54
]
are designed to reduce the communication cost
of BFs.
Its idea is to populate the BF with only
m=1
hash function and to
compress the array using an entropy coding scheme (for example
[
12
]
)
before sending it to Bob.
By
[
54
]
, compressed BFs are about 15% smaller than traditional
ones (under the same false positive rate), at a small computation cost of the
entropy coding.

8.2
Set Reconciliation (SetR) Protocols

We have formulated the SetR problem in
§​​ 1
.
Most existing SetR protocols are based on either invertible Bloom
lookup tables (IBLT)
[
5
]
or
error-correction codes (ECC).

Invertible Bloom Lookup Tables

In an IBLT, each element (from a set) is inserted into
m
cells indexed
by
m
independent hash functions.
Whereas each cell is a single bit in a BF, it has three fields and takes up
about
1.5u
bits (if the universe size is
2^{u}
) in an IBLT.
Therefore, IBLTs are much more powerful than BFs: The set difference

\mathsf{A}\triangle\mathsf{B}
of sets
\mathsf{A}
and
\mathsf{B}
can be recovered
from the “difference” of their IBLTs using a “peeling process” similar to
the belief propagation algorithm
[
55
]
widely used in low-complexity
decoding of erasure-correcting codes, such as Tornado
codes
[
56
]
.
For this decoding process to succeed with a high enough probability, IBLT-based
SetR protocols, such as Difference Digest
(D.Digest)
[
5
]
, have to use roughly

1.36d
cells.
This translates into a communication overhead of roughly

2.04ud
, or more than double the theoretical minimum for
SetR.

Set Reconciliation Protocols Based on Error Correction Codes

We use PinSketch
[
2
]
as an example to illustrate the
basic ideas of ECC-based
protocols
[
1
,
2
,
57
,
58
]
.
Recall from
§​​ 3.1
that
\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{A}}
and
\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{B}}

denote the binary vector representations of Alice’s set
\mathsf{A}
and Bob’s
set
\mathsf{B}
, respectively.
In PinSketch, Alice encodes
\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{A}}
into a BCH
[
11
]
codeword and
sends it to Bob.
Bob treats
\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{B}}
as the “corrupted message” and finds out the positions
of bit flips between
\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{A}}
and
\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{B}}
(which correspond to unique
elements in Alice or Bob) by decoding the BCH codeword.

ECC-based protocols use a smaller communication cost compared with IBLT-based
ones, but they have much higher computation complexity, mainly because of the
BCH decoder, whose best time complexity (so far) is

O(d^{2})

[
59
]
.
A recent protocol called PBS
[
6
]
(which stands for parity bitmap
sketch) combines both protocols.
PBS partitions the universe
\mathsf{U}
with a hash function, uses an ECC-based
approach to identify partitions that contain unique elements, and reconciles
the unique elements within each such partition using an IBLT-like approach.
As a result, PBS uses slightly more communication than ECC-based protocols, but
has a low time complexity comparable to IBLT-based protocols.

8.3
Existing SetX
Protocols

The following protocols were proposed for
solving “set reconciliation,” but in retrospect, they are in fact
SetX protocols, since they compute the set intersection instead of
the union.

Counting Bloom Filters

The SetX protocol proposed in
[
3
]
is
based on counting Bloom filters (CBFs).
In this protocol, Alice sends
CBF(\mathsf{A})
, a CBF that encodes
\mathsf{A}
,
to Bob, and Bob approximates
{\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}}
with all elements in
\mathsf{B}
that
pass the test of
CBF(\mathsf{B})-CBF(\mathsf{A})
(the difference between Bob’s
CBF that encodes
\mathsf{B}
and the one received from Alice).
This protocol is related to
CommonSense
, because as mentioned
in
§​​ 3.3
, when the CS matrix
\mathbf{M}{}
follows

Definition

6
, Alice’s sketch
\mathbf{M}\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{A}}
in
CommonSense

can be regarded as
CBF(\mathsf{A})
, and furthermore,
\mathbf{M}\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}}=\mathbf{M}\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{B}}-\mathbf{M}\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{A}}
, from which Bob
reconstructs
{\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}}
in
CommonSense
, can also be regarded as

CBF(\mathsf{B})-CBF(\mathsf{A})
.

Although both protocols use the same sketches (
\mathbf{M}\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{A}}
in
transmission and
\mathbf{M}\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}}
to compute
{\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}}
),
their recovery procedures and hence recovery qualities (of
{\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}}
) are
completely different.
In
§​​ 3.3
, we have shown using the RIP-1 argument that

\mathbf{M}\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}}
contains enough information for lossless
reconstruction of the exact
{\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}}
, and
CommonSense
achieves this
goal using the MP decoder in
§​​ 3.4
.
The protocol in
[
3
]
, however, treats

\mathbf{M}\mathbf{1}_{\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}}
simply as a CBF (and hence its decoding has
nothing to do with CS), which does not make full use of the information
therein.
For this reason, it can only compute an approximate result that contains both
false positives and false negatives
[
37
]
.
Moreover,
CommonSense
is technically much more general than the protocol
in
[
3
]
, because it works also on other sparse
RIP-1 CS matrices (such as the one in
[
60
]
, in which half of the

1
’s are flipped to
-1
’s) whereas the latter works only for the “CBF
matrix."

Graphene

Graphene
[
7
]
, the state-of-the-art protocol for unidirectional
SetX, combines IBLT with BF.

Alice sends both a BF and an IBLT of
\mathsf{A}
to Bob, with the latter encoded
in just enough number of cells for BF’s false positives.
Bob first computes
\hat{\mathsf{A}}
, the set of elements in
\mathsf{B}
that
pass the BF, deducts the elements of
\hat{\mathsf{A}}
from the received IBLT,
and finally decodes the BF’s false positives
\hat{\mathsf{A}}\setminus\mathsf{A}
from the resulting IBLT.
Therefore, the set difference
{\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}}
can be computed as the union of

\mathsf{B}\setminus\hat{\mathsf{A}}
and BF’s false positives.

Graphene uses a small communication cost on a wide range of SDCs (
d=|{\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}}|
) for the following reason.
On the one hand, the BF’s size is
O(|\mathsf{A}|)
, but it cannot compute the
exact set intersection because of its false positives.
On the other hand, the IBLT is exact, but its size is
O(du)
.
In most scenarios,
d
is not too small, so the BF is smaller than the
IBLT.
By combining both protocols, Graphene can compute the exact set intersection
without paying a large communication cost.
Note that when
d
is very small, the IBLT becomes smaller than the BF,
in which case Graphene drops the BF and degenerates to IBLT.
In comparison,
CommonSense
only uses

O(|\mathsf{B}|\log(|\mathsf{B}|/d))
bits of communication, which, as shown
in
Figure

2(a)
, is much smaller than that used by Graphene unless

d
is very large.
Finally, we comment that, as mentioned in
§​​ 1.1
, Graphene actually
solves a unidirectional SetX problem (of computing

\hat{\mathsf{A}}\setminus\mathsf{A}
with
\mathsf{A}\subseteq\hat{\mathsf{A}}
)
using IBLT as a subroutine, to which end
CommonSense
uses much less
communication cost than IBLT.

8.4
Comparison with CS-Sketch

CS-Sketch
[
51
]
is a recent sketching scheme for full traffic measurement.
The goal of that problem is, given a stream of packets in the data plane of a network device, count the number of packets in each flow, which is identified by a unique 5-tuple (source IP, destination IP, source port, destination port, and protocol).

In
[
51
]
, the set of all unique 5-tuples (flow labels) can be identified accurately using a Bloom filter.
The nontrivial problem is to record and recover the count associated with each flow label (signal).
In CS-Sketch, the recording scheme is the same as that of Count-Min sketch
[
61
]
, which is equivalent to updating a CS codeword when the CS matrix is a sparse binary one.
According to
[
51
]
, their CS matrix can also be viewed as the adjacency matrix of a binary expander graph, so it also has the RIP-1 for a similar reason as shown by
Theorem

8
.
The recovery (decoding) algorithm is based on OMP (orthogonal matching pursuit), and the authors propose to reduce the decoding time complexity by inverse Cholesky decomposition.

Although both
CommonSense
and CS-Sketch use a similar CS matrix and rely on the RIP-1 for accurate reconstruction, they are fundamentally different in the following two aspects.
First, the signal to recover (decode) is very different. In CS-Sketch, the support of the signal (indices of nonzero scalars) are already known, and the goal is to recover the flow sizes (values of these scalars).
In
CommonSense
, however, the scalars are zero-one, but the support corresponds to a very large unknown set (
{\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}}
for Bob).
Second, their use very different algorithms to account for the differences in signal.
CS-Sketch uses OMP to accurately recover scalar values, whereas
CommonSense
uses MP adapted to binary signals (
9
) to efficiently identify the support of the signal.

8.5
Private Set Intersection

Private set intersection (PSI) is a cryptographic problem that is loosely
related to SetX.
In PSI, two hosts, say Alice and Bob, want to collectively compute the
intersection
\mathsf{A}\cap\mathsf{B}
without revealing any information about
the
private
(unique) elements (
{\mathsf{A}\setminus\mathsf{B}}
for Alice, and
{\mathsf{B}\setminus\mathsf{A}}

for Bob) to the other host
[
62
]
.
As a result of this privacy requirement, the design of PSI protocol is a
completely different research problem than SetX.
According to a recent lecture note
[
63
]
, all existing PSI
protocol therein are based on public key cryptography and they use a much
larger communication cost than
CommonSense
.

9
Conclusion

In this paper, we show, from both a theoretical and a practical perspective,
that SetX (set intersection) is fundamentally much cheaper than
SetR (set reconciliation), debunking a longstanding unspoken perception
that they are equally difficult in costs.
To this end, we develop a novel SetX protocol called
CommonSense
,
the communication cost of which handily beats the information-theoretic lower
bound of SetR.
We also identify five example applications, in all of which
CommonSense

improves over existing SetR-based protocols.
In addition, we identify a CS matrix that is both friendly to applications and compliant to the
existing theories on CS.
Finally, we show, by extensive experiments on both synthetic and real life
datasets, that
CommonSense
has a smaller communication cost than the state of the
arts by a factor of up to 7.4 on unidirectional SetX and around an
order of magnitude on bidirectional SetX.
