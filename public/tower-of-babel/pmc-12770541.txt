Safety evaluation in the weaving segment between minor roads and U-turns on arterial roadways
Authors: Kimiya Amani, Amin Mirza Boroujerdian, Mahdi Aali
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccess points play a critical role in traffic safety along arterial roadways. Among the various elements of access management, the segment between minor road connections and U-turn openings presents unique safety challenges due to frequent weaving maneuvers. A widely applied design in such contexts is the Right Turn Followed by U-Turn (RTUT) configuration, an access management strategy that replaces direct left turns with a right turn followed by a downstream U-turn. In these situations, drivers entering from minor roads and intending to make U-turns often need to quickly shift lanes from slower outer lanes to faster inner ones. When the separation distance (SD) between the minor road and the U-turn is short, drivers have limited time and space to accelerate and merge safely, increasing the likelihood of conflicts with through traffic. This study aims to determine the optimal SD and other contributing factors affecting road user safety in RTUT segments. Unlike most previous research that has focused on isolated variables or relied primarily on simulation based analysis, this study integrates multiple geometric and operational factors into a unified regression framework using high resolution UAV trajectory data. By combining field based trajectories with the Time to Collision (TTC) conflict measure, a proactive surrogate safety indicator, the analysis provides a more detailed and reliable assessment of conflict risk compared with traditional crash based approaches. To achieve this, UAV technology was used to monitor 10 arterial segments with SD ranging from about 200 to 500 m (186–488 m). The collected trajectories were compiled into a comprehensive database for regression analysis. Results indicate that SD has the most significant impact on safety, with TTC increasing by up to approximately 1 s for every 100 m increase in SD under maximum entry speeds, highlighting the critical role of SD in mitigating conflicts. Other variables, such as vehicle entry speed, number of lanes, acceleration lane length, and hourly traffic volume also showed statistically significant effects. By integrating UAV based trajectory data with a multivariate regression framework, this study provides a novel and generalizable method for evaluating RTUT safety under real traffic conditions. The findings deliver practical insights for designing safer arterial facilities by optimizing separation distance and other critical geometric and operational factors.Keywords: Road safety, RTUT, Time to collision, Weaving segment, Safety performance functionsSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Enhancing safety on arterial roadways is essential, as these routes connect major urban activity centers and play a critical role in the overall performance of the transportation network. Due to the typically high traffic volumes on arterial roads, the probability of severe conflicts increases substantially, thereby elevating the risk of crashes1. Consequently, improving safety along arterial corridors requires a systematic evaluation and implementation of effective strategies aimed at reducing conflicts and enhancing overall traffic safety. One well established approach is access management, which reduces hazardous interactions by regulating and controlling access points along arterial roadways2. Recent research has emphasized that mixed traffic environments, particularly in merging zones, require safety assessments that extend beyond conventional geometric evaluations. For example, He, Xiang, and Wang (2025) examined driver behavior in such contexts and highlighted that unsafe interactions are often the result of limited decision making time and lane changing pressure3. Similar challenges are observed in weaving segments between minor roads and U-turn locations, where abrupt lane transitions frequently give rise to near crash situations4. Among access management strategies, the indirect left turn method has been identified as an effective alternative to the traditional direct left turn (DLT). In this configuration, vehicles entering from minor roads and intending to travel in the opposite direction complete their maneuver by using a downstream U-turn5. Previous studies have demonstrated that, compared with the direct left turn, the indirect left turn method not only reduces the number of conflict points but also decreases traffic delays and the incidence of angle related crashes. Furthermore, increasing the separation distance (SD) between minor road connections and U-turn locations provides drivers with additional time and space to complete their maneuvers, which can significantly improve safety outcomes6,7. The indirect left turn concept has been implemented through several design variations, each differing in terms of conflict mitigation and vehicle movement patterns. One such design is the Right Turn Followed by U-Turn (RTUT) method, introduced by Liu (Fig. 1-a). In this design, vehicles entering from minor roads and intending to travel in the opposite direction either make their turn at the next signalized intersection or complete it at a downstream U-turn location8. Another design, the Restricted Crossing U-Turn (RCUT) (Fig. 1-b), proposed by Ulak et al., follows a similar principle, with minor road vehicles completing their maneuver at a downstream U-turn. This configuration significantly reduces conflict points and helps prevent severe angle crashes, and can be managed with either traffic signals or stop control9. A third variation, the Shifting Movements (SM) method (Fig. 1-c), introduced by Al-Omari, also builds on the RTUT concept by altering vehicle movement patterns to reduce conflicts compared with conventional intersections. However, the SM design introduces subtle modifications that have been shown to perform more effectively under moderate to high traffic volumes on arterial roadways10.

Types of indirect left turn plans: (a) RTUT method (b) RCUT method and (c) SM method8–10.

Numerous studies have demonstrated that the RTUT method outperforms the DLT design in terms of traffic efficiency and travel time. Abdelfatah and Taha, using VISSIM and Synchro traffic simulation software, showed that although RTUT increases travel distance, it substantially decreases overall network delay11. Similarly, Zhou et al., through an evaluation of eight RTUT segments and a comparative analysis of delay and travel time models for both DLT and RTUT, concluded that RTUT provides superior performance in improving traffic flow12. Yang et al. also examined six roadway segments under various flow conditions and found that RTUT consistently offers lower delays and shorter travel times, particularly at higher traffic volumes13. Beyond operational efficiency, several studies highlight the safety advantages of RTUT. Lu et al. reported that while DLT segments experience an average of 6.7 conflicts per hour, RTUT reduces this figure to 2.4; similarly, the number of conflicts per thousand vehicles is 40.6 for DLT compared to 26.2 for RTUT14. Inman et al. found that RTUT reduces total crashes by 44% and fatal or injury crashes by 9%15. Sun et al., analyzing six RTUT segments, likewise emphasized its effectiveness in lowering crash frequency16. Consistently, Jagannathan reported reductions of 60% in overall crashes and 75% in injury related crashes when RTUT replaces DLT17.

Several studies have investigated critical variables influencing RTUT design. Stokes analyzed crash data from 9,000 segments across 11 major roads, focusing on the distance between minor roads and U-turns. His findings indicated that locating U turns within 50 m of minor roads nearly doubled accident frequency compared to distances of 50–100 m18. In contrast, Azizi et al., studying six RTUT implementations, reported a 13.22% increase in crashes following the conversion of a signalized intersection to an RTUT design, although some sections showed reductions. They emphasized that RTUT effectiveness depends on traffic volume (VT) and geometric design features, yet they did not provide specific quantitative guidelines19. Liu further demonstrated that VT and separation distance (SD) are strongly correlated with annual accident rates. Using VISSIM and SSAM simulations, he showed that reducing SD from 450 to 350 m had negligible safety effects, whereas reducing it to 200 m substantially increased conflicts8,20. In another study, Liu et al. classified road segments into three SD categories (< 107 m, 107–198 m, > 198 m) and found that a 10% increase in SD was associated with a 3.3% reduction in crashes21. Similarly, Sun et al. examined acceleration lane length (LA), deceleration lane length (LD), and SD using a driving simulator and Time to Collision (TTC) as a conflict measure. Their results indicated that longer LD increased TTC values, reflecting improved safety, and that when auxiliary lanes were present, the required SD could be reduced22. Collectively, these studies underscore the importance of accurately determining RTUT design parameters, such as SD, VT, LA, and LD to maximize safety and operational efficiency. Although considerable research has evaluated RTUT performance and related safety indicators (e.g., conflict rates, TTC), recent contributions have been relatively limited, suggesting the field is reaching maturity. Nevertheless, the absence of comprehensive design guidelines remains a critical gap. Current regulations typically provide only broad recommendations, particularly regarding SD, without addressing the full range of influencing variables.

Extensive research in road safety has established Surrogate Safety Measures (SSMs) as an efficient alternative to traditional crash based analyses, which are often limited by data availability, accuracy concerns, and psychological reporting biases23,24. Among SSMs, the Traffic Conflict Technique (TCT) is widely applied and primarily relies on two time based indicators: Time to Collision (TTC) and Post Encroachment Time (PET)25,26. TTC, originally defined by Hayward (1972) as the remaining time before two vehicles would collide if they maintain their current speed and trajectory, has become one of the most recognized surrogate measures27. Recent studies have reinforced its importance: Wu et al. (2020) demonstrated its effectiveness in identifying rear end conflicts28, while Javed et al. (2025) highlighted its relevance in U-turn and RTUT safety evaluations29. Compared to alternative indicators, TTC is the most consistently applied in weaving and merging segments and is widely regarded as the most reliable surrogate safety measure28,30,31. For these reasons, and to preserve analytical clarity, this study adopts TTC as the sole safety indicator rather than combining it with other measures such as PET or DRAC, which would complicate regression analysis without yielding substantial interpretive gains. This approach is also consistent with prior studies addressing similar topics22,32,33. The literature, however, presents a range of threshold values for TTC, reflecting differences in traffic and environmental conditions. For example, Sun et al. (2017) adopted a 6 s threshold, arguing that collisions are highly improbable beyond this value22. Jin et al. (2011) recommended 1.5–5 s for urban arterials34, while Hirst and Graham (2020) suggested 4 s as appropriate for moderate speed arterials with frequent flow variations35. Building on these traditional applications, recent advances in data collection and analysis such as UAV based trajectory tracking, computer vision, and machine learning have expanded the role of TTC in proactive safety studies. Today, TTC is integrated into both academic research and commercial traffic analysis platforms such as DataFromSky and PTV Vissim, enabling the detection of imminent conflicts even in the absence of crashes32,36,37. Moreover, traffic dynamics such as density and merging patterns significantly influence TTC based evaluations. Ding et al. (2024) demonstrated that incorporating these dynamics enhances conflict prediction and prevention strategies, particularly in environments with frequent lane changes such as RTUT segments38. In multi lane weaving areas, Xia et al. (2022) introduced a TTC based machine learning framework (TTC-ML) incorporating logistic regression and GBDT models to detect and predict vehicular conflicts from aerial and roadside video streams, further underscoring TTC’s effectiveness in capturing the severity and imminence of conflict events33. Similarly, Galiza and Regidor investigated U-turn weaving behavior at RTUT segments and found that TTC derived measures strongly correlate with unsafe weaving speed patterns, providing empirical support for TTC as a valid surrogate safety indicator in arterial U-turn environments39. Lu et al. (2022) also used UAV trajectory data to analyze merging conflicts between cars and trucks, demonstrating that TTC effectively differentiates conflict severity across vehicle types, thereby reinforcing its utility in UAV based safety studies32.

Based on these foundations, the present study adopts TTC as the primary surrogate safety measure. A regression based modeling framework, consistent with established traffic safety research paradigms, is employed to quantify the relationship between TTC and explanatory variables in RTUT sections. Advances in trajectory-based lane changing models have further enabled researchers to simulate and predict driver decision making in high conflict areas. For example Qu et al. (2025) applied a game theoretic framework to analyze strategy evolution in vehicle interactions, showing how competition for lane space can escalate safety risks. These findings align with the current study’s observations regarding the influence of entry speed and lane count on conflict severity in RTUT designs40. Although previous studies have investigated RTUT safety, they have generally relied on simulation or crash data, focused on a limited set of variables, or offered only qualitative recommendations. In particular, the combined influence of geometric, traffic, and operational factors has not been sufficiently addressed. For instance, the entry speed of vehicles merging from minor roads into arterial road (EVS) has received limited scholarly attention, despite its potential importance. Moreover, while TTC has been widely recognized as a reliable surrogate safety measure, its systematic application to weaving conflicts specifically between minor roads and U-turns remains underexplored. To address these gaps, the present study develops a regression based framework that integrates UAV derived trajectory data with TTC to evaluate conflict risk in RTUT segments. By simultaneously considering separation distance (SD), entry vehicle speed (EVS), number of lanes (NL), acceleration lane length (LA), and traffic volume (VT) as well as their interaction effects the study provides a more comprehensive and quantitative understanding of RTUT safety. Figure 2 synthesizes prior contributions and illustrates how earlier studies, although valuable in establishing foundational insights, often fail to capture contemporary traffic conditions, leverage advanced trajectory data, or integrate updated design standards. These gaps highlight the need for a systematic and data driven framework to guide RTUT design. While RTUT has been widely recognized as an effective strategy for improving traffic flow and reducing crashes, optimal design parameters have not yet been clearly defined. The present study therefore aims to identify the key safety related variables of RTUT and develop a robust regression model, based on UAV derived trajectory data and TTC, to provide practical guidance for safer roadway design and policy development.

Method

The present research evaluates the safety of minor roads connecting to U-turn areas along arterial roadways in Mashhad, Khorasan Razavi Province, Iran. Ten RTUT sections were selected, with separation distances (SD) between the minor road and U-turn ranging from about 200 to 500 m (exactly 186–488 m), based on the feasibility of video recording. Table 1 shows the geographical location of the studied (Standard UTM, Zone:40 and Hemisphere: N). These distances are consistent with geometric design standards and safety guidelines. For instance, AASHTO Green Book (2018) recommends comparable separation for arterial to collector access points to allow adequate weaving and decision making while avoiding excessive clustering of conflicts41. Very short separations may suppress speed differentials between vehicles, while excessively long distances can significantly reduce conflict frequency. Additionally, the study areas were chosen to accommodate UAV altitude constraints, ensuring continuous and high quality video coverage of all traffic maneuvers. To control for geometric and regulatory heterogeneity, all selected roadway segments shared similar longitudinal slopes, terrain features, and a uniform posted speed limit of 70 km/h. Only RTUT configurations with comparable traffic control schemes and access functions were included. Furthermore, all minor roads feeding into the main arterial were of the same hierarchical classification and functional role within the network, thereby minimizing behavioral variation due to roadway type. This high degree of geometric, regulatory, and operational uniformity provides a robust basis for integrating all observations into a single modeling framework. In this study, traffic data were collected using Unmanned Aerial Vehicle (UAV) technology, which enables rapid, detailed, and non intrusive acquisition of vehicle trajectories. UAVs have been widely applied in replicating and analyzing traffic scenarios, particularly in freeway work zones42. A DJI Mavic 2 Pro drone (Fig. 5), capable of recording 4 K video at 30 frames per second, was employed. The flight altitude at each site was determined by the required coverage area, and the UAV was maintained at a fixed position throughout the recording to ensure stable and consistent footage.

View of the drone used in the research.

Video recordings were conducted on weekdays between 10:00 AM and 12:00 PM, corresponding to periods of stable traffic flow and typical demand. This time window is consistent with international traffic data collection standards41 and previous UAV based studies43. The choice also satisfied technical requirements for aerial videography, including adequate lighting conditions, UAV battery capacity, and compatibility with the image processing specifications of DataFromSky. Similar to prior UAV based datasets, emphasis was placed on ensuring data quality and detail rather than maximizing the number of observation sites. For instance, the CitySim dataset included 12 diverse locations while prioritizing high resolution footage44, the Drone Traffic Metrics project in Hohhot, China, captured more than 100 h of video at only eight intersections45, the inD dataset comprised approximately 10 h of footage from four German intersections46, and Lu et al. (2022) focused on a limited number of freeway merging sites in Shanghai while achieving practically valuable conflict data32.

Following data collection, the footage was processed using DataFromSky image analysis software47. The software was calibrated through a ground control process, in which six to eight reference points with known coordinates were selected for each segment to align the video accurately with real world dimensions (Fig. 6). After calibration, DataFromSky extracted vehicle trajectories and identified potential traffic conflicts using the Time to Collision (TTC) metric. This approach is consistent with recent computer vision based traffic studies that employ deep learning for vehicle detection, tracking, and flow analysis48. DataFromSky applies advanced computer vision algorithms for object detection and tracking, enabling automatic identification of road users across video frames and generating trajectories with high spatial and temporal resolution. Surrogate safety measures (including TTC) were then computed by detecting potential collision points between conflicting vehicle paths. A conflict was registered when TTC values fell below the predefined threshold of 5 s, representing a near crash event. The TTC values extracted by DataFromSky formed the basis of the safety analysis in this study. Due to its proven capability in processing real world traffic scenarios and estimating microscopic safety indicators, DataFromSky is widely recognized as a reliable tool for proactive traffic safety assessment.

Video calibration in DataFromSky software.

In this study, only conflicts involving vehicles that entered the main road from minor roads with the intention of making a U-turn were analyzed. All other conflict types were excluded. After this filtering process, 1,136 relevant conflict events remained for analysis and modeling. For each conflict, the corresponding Time to Collision (TTC) value was extracted. Consistent with previous research, a TTC range of 0.5 to 5 s was adopted for the analysis.

It is important to emphasize that Time to Collision (TTC) is inherently a continuous measure extracted from calibrated video trajectories, and its value is independent of any critical threshold. Threshold values are only relevant when calculating secondary indicators such as Time Exposed TTC (TET) and Time Integrated TTC (TIT), which are threshold dependent. Thus, the choice of a specific threshold does not affect the TTC values themselves or the regression models built upon them.

To select the final modeling approach, we first examined how the collected data were associated with the dependent variable (TTC). For this purpose, various regression models, including linear and quadratic forms with different interaction terms among independent variables, were tested to identify their relationships with the dependent variable. In addition, random parameter models and GAM were applied as sensitivity analyses to explore potential nonlinearities or random effects across groups. The GAM results confirmed that the smooth terms for SD (edf = 0.98, p < 0.001) and EVS (edf = 1.31, p < 0.001) were essentially linear, indicating that their relationship with TTC can be sufficiently captured in a linear form. The model explained about 71.6% of the deviance (adj. R² = 0.715), and parametric coefficients for NL, LA, and VT categories were statistically significant. Similarly, the random parameter model showed negligible variance of random effects for all variables, and the LR test revealed no significant improvement compared with the final linear model (Prob > chi2 = 1.0). Therefore, considering the linear nature of the independent variables and key interactions with TTC, as well as the simplicity and interpretability of the model, a multivariate linear regression was selected as the final method for evaluating RTUT safety. Based on these examinations and model comparisons, a multiple linear regression model was ultimately selected to examine the effects of explanatory variables on the dependent variable. Multiple linear regression is widely used in traffic safety research due to its simplicity, efficiency, interpretability, and strong generalizability. These characteristics make it a powerful method for producing transparent and meaningful inferences, which explains its sustained application across various research domains49. Although advanced techniques such as machine learning and nonlinear models have gained prominence in recent years, multiple linear regression remains a robust and interpretable framework. It is particularly valuable in contexts where relationships among variables are reasonably assumed to be linear and the primary objective is to quantify the influence of individual predictors50,51. The coefficients of the regression model were estimated using the Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) method, which is the most widely adopted approach for linear regression analysis. One common approach for testing the normality of a distribution in Stata is the Shapiro–Wilk test, which is appropriate for sample sizes ranging from 4 to 2,000 observations. The null hypothesis assumes that the variable is normally distributed. A p-value below the significance threshold (typically 0.05) leads to rejection of the null hypothesis, suggesting that the variable deviates from normality. As reported in Table 4, the p-value for the TTC variable was greater than 0.05, indicating that, at the 95% confidence level, the distribution of TTC does not significantly depart from normality.

Results

As described earlier, aerial videos were captured using drones and calibrated to ensure accurate spatial measurements prior to trajectory extraction. Calibration involved selecting precise ground control points at each site through field surveying and GPS measurements. These georeferenced points were entered into the DataFromSky software to align video frames with real world coordinates, enabling reliable trajectory and motion analysis. Following calibration, DataFromSky generated both visual and numerical outputs, including vehicle trajectories and potential conflict events. Consistent with the methodological framework and based on TTC thresholds reported in prior studies, only conflicts involving vehicles entering the main road from side streets with the intention of making a U-turn, and with TTC values between 0.5 and 5 s were retained. This filtering process yielded a final dataset of 1,136 conflict events for modeling and analysis. In total, 2,660 conflicts were initially detected, of which 1,136 satisfied the inclusion criteria. Before developing the regression model, the Pearson correlation coefficients between the independent variables and the dependent variable were examined49. The analysis revealed statistically significant correlations, indicating meaningful relationships between TTC and the explanatory variables. Correlations among the independent variables themselves were also identified. Therefore, in constructing the final model, attention was given to selecting variables that minimized multicollinearity while preserving their explanatory power for TTC. To capture potential nonlinearities and improve predictive accuracy, alternative specifications were tested, including squared terms and interaction effects. All other variables were also statistically significant at this level. Traffic volume (VT) showed a negative correlation with TTC and had the weakest association with the dependent variable. It should be noted that a negative correlation indicates an inversely directional relationship, not the absence of a statistically significant relationship. The strength of the relationship is reflected by the absolute value of the Pearson coefficient, as reported in Table 7. All independent variables were retained for the final regression model due to their theoretical importance and statistical significance. After selecting the independent variables, a 5-fold cross validation procedure was applied to ensure robust and generalizable estimation of the regression parameters. In each fold, the model was trained on 80% of the data (approximately 910 conflict observations) and validated on the remaining 20% (approximately 226 observations). This process was repeated five times, allowing each subset to serve once as the validation set. The final model coefficients were obtained by averaging the estimates across all folds, thereby minimizing the influence of any particular data partition and enhancing the stability and reliability of the results. Following model construction, diagnostic tests were performed to assess its validity and adequacy. All analyses were conducted using STATA version 17. Homoscedasticity of residuals was evaluated using the Breusch–Pagan test52, which indicated no violation of the constant variance assumption (χ²=0.31, p = 0.58). Residual normality was also confirmed. Potential multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed via the Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) test. As shown in Table 8, all VIF values were below the commonly accepted threshold of 10, indicating no significant multicollinearity. Additionally, the scatter plot of residuals versus predicted values (Fig. 10) revealed no discernible patterns, further supporting the adequacy of the model. To assess the normality assumption of the residuals, Fig. 11 illustrates the histogram of their distribution, while Table 8 reports the results of the Shapiro–Wilk test, confirming that the residuals follow an approximately normal distribution.

Based on the diagnostic evaluations and the confirmation of standard linear regression assumptions, the final model was established. Table 10 presents the regression coefficients derived from the averaged estimates across the five cross validated folds. The overall statistical significance of the model was confirmed by the F-statistic (F = 577.15, p < 0.05), indicating that the set of independent variables collectively exerts a significant effect on TTC at the 95% confidence level. The explanatory power of the model, assessed using R² and adjusted R², was 79.34% and 79.20%, respectively. The close agreement between these values suggests an adequate sample size and indicates no evidence of overfitting. Approximately 80% of the variance in mean Time to Collision (TTC) is accounted for by the predictors included in the model. All individual regression coefficients were statistically significant at the 95% confidence level, as reported in Table 10. To ensure a robust and unbiased evaluation of the model’s predictive performance, a 5-fold cross validation procedure was implemented. The dataset, comprising 1,136 conflict observations, was randomly divided into five equal sized, non overlapping subsets. As the dataset lacks temporal or spatial ordering, partitioning into folds does not introduce systematic bias or violate independence assumptions. In each fold, 80% of the data (approximately 910 conflicts) were used for model training and calibration, while the remaining 20% (approximately 227 conflicts) were reserved for validation. This process was repeated five times, with each subset serving once as the validation set. The model was refitted in each iteration, and predictive accuracy was evaluated by comparing observed and predicted Time to Collision (TTC) values (measured in milliseconds) in the corresponding validation set. Figure 13 illustrates the results from the first fold. In this figure, the red line represents the regression model trained on 80% of the data, while the blue points correspond to the 20% of observations in the test set. The horizontal axis shows predicted TTC values, and the vertical axis displays observed TTC values. For ease of interpretation, TTC values originally measured in milliseconds were converted into seconds in this figure; accordingly, both axes are expressed in seconds. Perfect alignment between predicted and observed values would result in a regression line coinciding with Y = X, with a slope of one and an intercept of zero. As shown, the fitted model closely approximates this ideal relationship, with a Pearson correlation coefficient of approximately 0.8133, indicating excellent agreement between predicted and observed values for this fold. The final model was obtained by averaging the regression coefficients from all five folds. This approach reduces the influence of any single data partition and yields a more stable and generalizable set of parameter estimates.

Discussion

In this study, the factors affecting Time to Collision (TTC) were analyzed through the development of a multiple linear regression model, allowing examination of the combined effects of variables influencing minor road to U-turn areas. Given the inclusion of interaction terms involving separation distance (SD), entry vehicle speed (EVS), and the number of lanes (NL), both marginal effect and sensitivity analyses were conducted to better quantify the influence of each parameter and assess the relative importance of the independent variables in predicting TTC. Marginal effect analysis evaluates the impact of a unit change in one independent variable on the dependent variable while holding all other variables constant, thereby isolating the individual contribution of each predictor. Sensitivity analysis, in contrast, examines how variations in independent variables collectively influence the dependent variable, providing insights into the complex interrelationships among factors and identifying the key variables that most strongly affect TTC.

Figure 16 illustrates the marginal effect of separation distance (SD) on Time to Collision (TTC measured in milliseconds) at the 95% confidence level. The analysis indicates that the impact of SD depends on the entry vehicle speed (EVS) from the minor road, which ranges from 10 to 75 km/h. The effect is positive, suggesting that, for two sections with identical SD, the section with higher EVS tends to be safer. This occurs because smaller speed differences between entering vehicles and the main traffic stream reduce conflict likelihood, allowing smoother and more coordinated merging. It should be noted that a higher EVS alone does not necessarily imply greater safety. Rather, smaller speed differentials during off peak hours, within the observed range of 10 to 75 km/h, can lead to higher predicted Time to Collision (TTC) values. Sensitivity analysis further confirms that increasing the distance between the minor road and the U-turn location positively influences TTC, with the magnitude of this effect strongly dependent on EVS. At all speed levels, longer SD increases TTC and enhances safety, but the effect is more pronounced at higher speeds. These findings are consistent with previous studies, which report that greater separation distances provide more opportunities for lane changes and speed adjustments, thereby reducing conflict rates20,21. At higher entry speeds, extended SD allows vehicles sufficient time to match the main traffic stream’s speed, enhancing safety22. At lower speeds, while increasing SD still improves TTC, the safety benefits are more limited due to larger speed differentials with the main stream, aligning with the observations of Ruihua and Heng (2009)7. Overall, these results highlight the importance of considering both SD design and EVS management. The combination of greater separation distance and an entry speed close to that of the main traffic flow yields the highest TTC values and the greatest safety improvements, supporting prior findings that this strategy effectively reduces conflicts in RTUT configurations14.

Sensitivity analysis further confirms that increasing the number of main lanes enhances TTC, with the magnitude of this effect strongly influenced by EVS. At all speed levels, additional lanes lead to higher TTC values and improved safety, with more pronounced effects at higher speeds. These findings align with previous studies, which report that increasing NL provides more opportunities for lane changes and reduces traffic pressure during weaving maneuvers13,14. Our empirical findings standardized as shown in Eq. (2) and summarized in Table 11 are consistent with well-established behavioral and traffic flow mechanisms and can be interpreted through three complementary perspectives: gap acceptance and time pressure in lane changing, the role of speed differentials, and the influence of lane availability on bargaining for gaps.

First, separation distance (SD) primarily affects the time and distance budget available to entering drivers to accelerate and merge safely. Larger SD provides more longitudinal space for drivers to increase speed and execute lane changes without urgent, risky maneuvers; this reduces the need for abrupt lateral maneuvers and increases TTC. This mechanism has been reported in empirical studies of offset/separation and crash risk (larger separation reduces crashes) and in trajectory analyses of lane change duration and distance. Our positive and large standardized coefficient on SD therefore reflects the simple physical fact that more space more time to prepare and merge, which reduces immediate conflict likelihood21.

Second, the interaction effects involving entry speed (EVS) can be interpreted in terms of speed differential and gap acceptance. A higher EVS that is closer to the prevailing mainline speed reduces the speed differential between the entering vehicle and the mainline traffic; numerous studies have shown that smaller speed differentials are associated with larger TTC and lower collision risk during merging and lane changes. In our model the positive EVS×NL term (large standardized coefficient) indicates that when entering vehicles travel faster and there are more lanes, the effective opportunity to find an acceptable gap increases (drivers require smaller longitudinal acceleration or braking), which raises TTC. In short: higher EVS (under moderate traffic conditions, outside both free flow and peak hours, with vehicle speeds ranging from 10 to 75 km/h) as relative to mainline flow (i.e., reduced speed mismatch), especially on multi-lane sections, facilitates safer merges. This interpretation aligns with empirical analyses linking speed differential to TTC and with lane change decision models that emphasize bounded rational gap selection under speed heterogeneity56,57.

Third, the joint effect of EVS and NL can also be framed as a supply of gaps effect: additional lanes increase the number of potential lateral gaps and reduce the level of competition for any single gap. When entering vehicles are able to approach at speeds closer to the mainline and multiple lanes exist, drivers can use small adjustments (slightly higher EVS or lateral selection of an adjacent lane) to secure a safe passage hence the very large positive standardized coefficient for EVS×NL in our results. This mechanism is seen in game theoretic and empirical lane change literature where lane availability reduces the urgency of aggressive maneuvers and increases system resiliency to merging demand57.

Finally, the negative coefficient on VT and positive on LA further support the behavioral story: higher mainline volume reduces available gaps and compresses space, lowering TTC (higher conflict risk), whereas longer acceleration lane length (LA) functions similarly to increased SD by giving entering drivers extra distance to match speeds and look for gaps, thereby increasing TTC. These consistent signs reinforce the interpretation that the dominant safety mechanisms in RTUT weaving are driven by available time space to perform maneuvers and relative speed matching, both of which are strongly influenced by geometric parameters (SD, LA) and operational context (EVS, NL, VT)21.

Collectively, these behavioral and traffic flow concepts (gap acceptance, speed differential effects, supply of lateral gaps) offer a coherent theoretical explanation for why SD and the EVS×NL interaction emerge as the most influential predictors in our regression.

Conclusion

In the design of Right Turn U-Turn (RTUT) facilities which represent an essential access management strategy on arterial roadways, particular attention must be paid to conflicts between low speed vehicles entering from minor roads and high speed vehicles on the mainline. The primary objective is to minimize such conflicts and prevent accidents. This study investigated key factors affecting the safety of these areas. Data were collected from ten RTUT locations on arterial roads in Mashhad, and Time to Collision (TTC) was employed as the safety metric in a linear regression model incorporating geometric variables and weaving vehicle parameters. The main findings are summarized as follows:

Separation distance (SD): Among all variables analyzed, SD has the greatest impact on TTC. A 100 m increase in SD leads to an increase of approximately 1 s in TTC (228–783 milliseconds depending on entry vehicle speed, EVS). Longer SD allows vehicles more time and distance to adjust speed and execute lane changes safely. However, improving safety requires simultaneous optimization of other factors such as EVS and lane availability; increasing SD alone does not guarantee optimal safety.

Limitations

This study was conducted under clear weather conditions and during daytime hours, with recordings made before noon to ensure optimal image quality. UAV related constraints, including limited battery endurance and strict permitting processes, restricted both the duration of each flight and the number of feasible study sites within the Mashhad metropolitan area. Consequently, data were collected from 10 RTUT sites during a uniform mid morning period (10:00–12:00), which also ensured consistency across traffic conditions. A preliminary traffic assessment was conducted at several locations prior to UAV monitoring to evaluate the potential effects of peak traffic periods. The assessment revealed that during peak hours, mainline traffic volumes can increase to the point where flow may nearly stop. Under these conditions, vehicles entering from minor roads and intending to make U-turns have more time to change lanes and reach the U-turn, while mainline speeds are substantially reduced. Consequently, lane changing conflicts, which are the primary focus of this study, are significantly diminished, whereas congestion induced conflicts become more prevalent. Therefore, data collected during peak traffic hours would not provide representative insights for evaluating RTUT operations, and the selection of the mid-morning period (10:00–12:00) was intended to ensure both data quality and reliability.

Several methodological limitations should also be acknowledged. Aerial filming prevented the collection of driver specific information, so human factors were not incorporated. For safety assessment, only Time to Collision (TTC) was adopted as the surrogate measure, which may limit interpretive scope but enabled a clear and consistent regression framework. Reliance solely on TTC may introduce bias, as complementary measures such as Post Encroachment Time (PET) could provide a more comprehensive assessment. Despite high R² values and cross validation indicating strong model fit, potential overfitting or scaling artifacts may influence robustness and generalizability. Data collection during non peak periods further limits applicability to peak traffic conditions, and environmental factors and vehicle types were not included. Time and budget constraints precluded extending the study to other metropolitan areas.

Despite these limitations, the selected scope represents a practical balance between data quality, operational feasibility, and methodological rigor, consistent with previous UAV based safety studies.

Future work

For future research, Data collection could be extended to nighttime and adverse weather conditions to assess the effects of reduced visibility and various environmental factors on RTUT safety. Additionally, integrating driver behavioral data, such as reaction times and gap acceptance patterns, would allow for a more comprehensive evaluation of human factors in merging and U-turn maneuvers. Future studies could also validate the regression model and safety indicators against observed crash outcomes to strengthen predictive reliability. These measures would enhance the generalizability and practical applicability of the findings, providing valuable guidance for the design and improvement of urban arterial roads. Moreover, image processing software can provide additional safety metrics beyond TTC, such as PET, which could be incorporated into future modeling to examine the influence of other geometric or operational factors on road safety in these areas.
