Seasonal and environmental patterns of naturally occurring parasitoids with potential to attack the invasive pest Drosophila suzukii in the Andes
Authors: Déborah Fischbein, Mónica Daniela Germano, Andrés Santiago Martínez, Julian Mensch, Marcelo Eduardo Kun, Andrea Cardozo, Verónica Chillo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLandscape complexity shapes insect spatiotemporal population dynamics and trophic interactions. In this context, understanding seasonal and environmental patterns of potential resident natural enemies of novel polyphagous herbivores, such as the globally invasive fruit fly pest Drosophila suzukii, is fundamental for developing effective landscape-scale pest management strategies. Here, we investigated for the first time in the Andean Patagonian region of Argentina the spatiotemporal dynamics and parasitism of three resident parasitoids—Leptopilina heterotoma, Pachycrepoideus vindemiae, and Spalangia endius—with the potential to attack D. suzukii across heterogeneous landscape including fruit crops and surrounding natural habitats. All three parasitoid species were recorded across all environments, although their seasonal dynamics differed. L. heterotoma peaked in spring, P. vindemiae in summer, while S. endius showed a non-significant tendency toward higher activity in autumn. We further provide the first field-based evidence in the region that S. endius successfully attacks and completes development in D. suzukii, and that D. suzukii exploits wild fruit species as alternative hosts. These findings highlight the need for further research on the pupal parasitoid S. endius to assess its potential as a suitable candidate for the development of biological control strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-57845-z.Keywords: Spotted-wing drosophila, Natural enemies, Spatio-temporal dynamics, Wild fruit host plants, Cultivated fruit host plant, Biological controlSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Zoology

Introduction

Landscape complexity is a critical factor influencing abundance and diversity of insects1,2, besides it is a key driver of population dynamics and trophic insect interactions3. In agroecosystems, complexity can increase the abundance and diversity of natural enemies (parasitoids and predators) enhancing the system’s inherent capacity to regulate pests4. Nevertheless, such landscapes can also promote insect pest populations. On one hand, a more diverse habitat can provide natural enemies with essential resources such as shelter, overwintering sites and alternative food sources, prey or hosts, thereby facilitating their early recruitment and seasonal persistence1. On the other, it may also benefit polyphagous and highly mobile herbivore pests by enhancing their access to favorable habitats and resources5. This dual effect presents a significant challenge for sustainable insect pest management.

Drosophila suzukii Matsumura (Diptera: Drosophilidae), commonly known as the spotted wing drosophila, is a frugivorous fly native to southeastern Asia, which has successfully invaded and spread through Europe, North and South America, and Africa6,7. Combining factors render D. suzukii a severe damaging pest worldwide and a significant challenge regarding its management8,9. Females use a serrated ovipositor to lay eggs in ripe, soft- skinned healthy fruits still attached to the plant causing physical damage and exposing the fruit to pathogens10. Additionally, it is a highly polyphagous species capable of feeding and reproducing in a wide range of economically important crops (e.g., berries, cherries, strawberries, and other agronomic plants) as well as non-crop wild and ornamental plants11,12. Thus, habitat diversification, including both cultivated and non-cultivated fruit species growing in natural environments, in conjunction with fly high dispersal ability13, can facilitate the persistence and multiplication of D. suzukii populations within agricultural landscapes14–17.

Drosophila suzukii was first detected in Argentina in 2014 and nowadays occurs in most fruit-growing areas in this region12. Particularly, in Northwestern Argentinean Patagonia within the Andean Region of the Parallel 42° (CAP 42°, for its name in Spanish, Comarca Andina del Paralelo 42°), D. suzukii builds up large populations and adult individuals can be sampled year-round across cultivated areas and adjacent non-crop environments16. Non-crop environments near to commercial berry fields frequently consist of wild blackberries (Rubus spp.) and forests, both of which may serve as host as well as overwintering refuge for D. suzukii. Notably, the abundance of D. suzukii is often higher in adjacent natural habitats than in cultivated fields, especially in winter16,18. Thus, as in many regions of the world, managing D. suzukii in CAP 42° is particularly challenging due to the fly’s capacity to persist on non-crop hosts and subsequently reinvade crop fields from the surrounding natural habitats. Nevertheless, these surrounding habitats may also play a role by supporting a community of natural enemies, such as resident insect parasitoids of Drosophila, capable of attacking and developing in D. suzukii as a host17,19. Parasitoids may then disperse into crop fields and contribute to pest suppression. Despite some information on naturally occurring parasitoids with potential as natural enemies of D. suzukii in raspberry crops at CAP 42°20, knowledge is lacking regarding their seasonal patterns across different environments and direct interactions with the novel herbivore species in the field.

The present study focuses on Leptopilina heterotoma (Hymenoptera: Figitidae), Spalangia endius (Hymenoptera: Spalangiidae) and Pachycrepoideus vindemiae Rondani (Hymenoptera: Pteromalidae), three parasitoid wasps that naturally occur in the study area at the Andean region and which are known to parasitize D. suzukii globally, albeit with variable reproductive performance across geographic regions21. Leptopilina heterotoma is a generalist larval parasitoid typically associated with Drosophila hosts22. This species has demonstrated the ability to oviposit in and kill D. suzukii larvae23; however, the developmental success shows intraspecific variability across different regions due to host’s encapsulation response (see references for developmental failure24–27 or partial developmental success28,29. Spalangia endius and P. vindemiae are generalist pupal parasitoids. Both species are solitary, idiobiont ectoparasitoids that attack Dipteran hosts, including members of the Drosophilidae family30. Pachycrepoideus vindemiae has been shown to successfully develop on D. suzukii under laboratory conditions28,31,32; however, field-based evaluations of its effectiveness remain scarce and have yielded variable results30,33–35. Spalangia endius was recovered from D. suzukii puparia in northwestern Argentina36, and other Spalangia species have also been occasionally collected from D. suzukii-infested crops in different regions worldwide26,27,37.

Our working hypothesis is that the heterogeneous habitats that would benefit and support D. suzukii populations should also sustain Drosophila parasitoid species characterized by different temporal patterns and occurrence across habitats. In addition, rapidly multiplying invasive species such as D. suzukii would represent an abundant resource to be exploited by generalist parasitoids. We predicted that the temporal and spatial distribution of the parasitoids will align with that of D. suzukii populations and allow the detection of direct associations of host-parasitoid in the field. Therefore, considering the reported spatiotemporal dynamics of D. suzukii population across the heterogeneous landscape of the CAP 42°16 and the temporal pattern in fruit ripening, this study aimed to: (i) investigate the seasonal and environmental variation in the occurrence and abundance of the three aforementioned naturally occurring parasitoid wasps using insect traps; (ii) assess the trophic association between these parasitoid species and D. suzukii across different microhabitats (i.e., fruit on plants, fallen fruit and soil) within both cultivated and adjacent wild fruit habitats during the fruit-growing season at CAP 42°; and (iii) explore the natural infestation prevalence of D. suzukii in different species of field-grown fruit. Such knowledge is essential for the development of a biological control strategy based on resident parasitoids as component of landscape-scale pest management to reduce outbreak severity throughout the growing season.

Results

Several hymenopteran specimens belonging to 17 families were recorded in vinegar baited traps, with Aphelinidae, Pteromalidae, Spalangiidae and Figitidae being the most abundant families. Regarding the three parasitoid wasp species of interest in the present study, namely Leptopilina heterotoma, Spalangia endius and Pachycrepoideus vindemiae, 466 adult individuals were captured during the two sampling years. The most common species was P. vindemiae, with the highest overall number of individuals captured (n = 218), followed by S. endius (n = 140) and L. heterotoma (n = 108). These three species were recovered across the surveyed area. Concerning Drosophila suzukii, it was captured in considerably higher numbers (n = 166405) than parasitoids and it was consistently present over the entire sampling period, in all surveyed transects (Fig. 1; and see for more details Martinez et al. 202516).

Adult parasitoid captures followed a unimodal phenological pattern, with low or null captures during winter (July–August). Parasitoid occurrence largely overlapped with the fruiting season (November–May), with initial detections in late spring–early summer, matching with cherry fruiting in the CAP 42° region (Fig. 1). Abundance increased as fruiting progressed, peaking during crop berry ripening, and subsequently declined toward late autumn, with no further captures recorded by the end of the fruiting season.

Seasonal and environmental variation in occurrence and abundance of parasitoids

Significant interaction effects were found between species and season (χ2 = 76.48, df = 6, p < 0.0001) as well as species and environment (χ2 = 43.41, df = 10; p < 0.0001) on the number of parasitoids captured. Specifically, adult capture levels varied significantly among species depending on the season (Fig. 2). Post hoc pairwise comparisons revealed that during spring, L. heterotoma exhibited the highest capture level in comparison to S. endius and P. vindemiae; however, in autumn, it showed the lowest capture level (Fig. 2). During both spring and autumn, S. endius and P. vindemiae displayed similar capture levels. In summer, P. vindemiae showed the highest adult capture level compared to L. heterotoma and S. endius, whose levels did not differ significantly (Supplementary Table S4; Fig. 2). All three parasitoid species were captured in spring, summer, and autumn; however, S. endius was the only species captured during winter.

Leptopilina heterotoma

The complementary analysis for L. heterotoma revealed that there was no significant interaction effect between season and environment on the number of individuals captured (χ2 = 11.74, df = 15; p = 0.6). However, there was a tendency for higher capture levels in berries and wild blackberries, particularly during summer (Fig. 4a). Significant differences were detected between seasons (χ2 = 23.99, df = 3; p < 0.0001), with capture levels being significantly higher in spring and summer compared to autumn, regardless of the environments studied (z = 4.6, p < 0.0001 and z = 4.8 p < 0.0001 respectively; Fig. 4a). Conversely, capture levels were similar between spring and summer (Fig. 4a).

Pachycrepoideus vindemiae

The complementary analysis for P. vindemiae showed that interaction term between season and environment was not statistically significant (χ2 = 18.86; df = 15; p = 0.22). However, this interaction term had to be retained in the model because the likelihood ratio test indicated a significantly better fit compared to the reduced model without it (χ² = 26.29, df = 15, p = 0.035). There were significant differences between seasons (χ2 = 99.92; df = 3; p < 0.0001) and environments (χ2 = 13.56; df = 5; p = 0.018) on the number of individuals captured. Post hoc comparisons based on the full model finally revealed significant differences among specific combinations of season and environment levels (Supplementary Table S7; Supplementary Table S8). There was a seasonal pattern in tree curtains, cherry, berries and wild blackberries environments (Fig. 4b). In these environments, adult capture levels were significantly higher in summer compared to spring and autumn (Fig. 4b; Supplementary Table S7). Additionally, captures of P. vindemiae at tree curtain were significantly higher than those in berries and wild blackberry in autumn (Supplementary Table S8). No significant differences in capture levels were observed among environments within the other seasons (Fig. 4b).

Spalangia endius

Finally, the analysis for S. endius showed a significant interaction effect between season and environment on the number of individuals captured (χ2 = 28.38, df = 15; p = 0.019). Specifically, the captures were significantly higher in wild blackberries in summer compared to spring, autumn, and winter (Fig. 4c; Supplementary Table S9). Further, there were higher captures in berries in autumn compared to spring and winter, but there were no significant differences between summer and autumn. In contrast, there were no significant differences in capture levels among environments within each season, except that there was a marginal significant difference between cherry and wild blackberry in summer (Supplementary Table S10).

Host–parasitoid trophic association

Overall, 4259 emerged Drosophila suzukii adults were recovered from fresh fruits collected still attached to the plants (mean ± SE: 81.9 ± 14.5, n = 52) and 62 recovered from fallen fruits (mean ± SE: 7.75 ± 3.24, n = 8). Additionally, 414 D. suzukii and 20 adult parasitoids emerged from puparia recovered from soil samples collected beneath both cultivated and non-crop fruit plants (meanD. suzukii ± SE: 6.27 ± 1.20, n = 66; meanparasitoid ± SE: 0.303 ± 0.129, n = 66). All emerged parasitoids belonged to S. endius species. Contrary to expectations, no larval parasitoids emerged from D. suzukii puparia recovered from fresh fruit still attached to the plant. However, one parasitoid individual emerged from a D. suzukii puparium recovered from fresh cherries, which was subsequently identified as S. endius.

Parasitism by S. endius on D. suzukii puparia recovered from soil beneath host plants was 6.25% in cultivated raspberries, 6.59% in wild blackberries, 3.24% in wild elderberries, and 0.61% in wild cherries. A more detailed analysis of parasitoid emergence, accounting for fruit status and the number of recovered puparia, showed no significant interaction between fruit status and puparia abundance (χ² = 2.68, df = 3, p = 0.44). In contrast, both fruit status (χ² = 12.76, df = 3, p = 0.005) and the number of D. suzukii puparia recovered from soil (χ² = 11.57, df = 1, p < 0.001) had significant effects on parasitoid emergence. Pairwise comparisons indicated significantly higher parasitoid emergence in wild blackberry compared with wild cherry (β = 2.52, z = 2.95, p = 0.017), whereas no significant differences were detected between crop raspberry and any of the wild fruit species, nor among the remaining wild fruit species (all p > 0.10; Fig. 5a). Additionally, the number of parasitoid emergences increased significantly with the number of D. suzukii puparia recovered from soil (β (± SE) = 0.06 (± 0.01), z = 3.4, p < 0.001).

Natural infestation of fruits by Drosophila suzukii

Natural infestation of fresh fruits on plants by Drosophila suzukii differed significantly among fruit status levels (χ² = 51.91, df = 3, p < 0.001). Accounting for differences in fruit mass using an offset term, crop raspberry exhibited the highest infestation level in comparison to all wild fruit species (Fig. 5b). Pairwise comparisons indicated that infestation in crop raspberry was significantly higher than in wild cherry (β = 2.49, z = 4.79, p < 0.001), wild elderberry (β = 1.94, z = 4.72, p < 0.001), and wild blackberry (β = 1.55, z = 3.72, p = 0.001). In contrast, no significant differences were detected among the wild fruit species themselves (all Tukey-adjusted p > 0.48).

Further analysis on differences in the number of D. suzukii puparia recovered from soil beneath different host plant species revealed an effect of fruit status (χ² = 32.86, df = 3, p < 0.001; Fig. 5c). Pairwise comparisons indicated that significantly more puparia were recovered beneath wild blackberry and wild cherry than beneath crop raspberry (wild blackberry vs. crop raspberry: β = 2.53, z = 4.66, p < 0.001; wild cherry vs. crop raspberry: β = 2.21, z = 3.46, p = 0.003). In contrast, the number of puparia recovered beneath wild elderberry did not differ from that under crop raspberry (β = 0.1, z = 0.20, p = 0.997). Among wild host plant species, puparia abundance was significantly higher beneath wild blackberry and wild cherry compared with wild elderberry (wild blackberry vs. wild elderberry: β = 2.43, z = 3.91, p < 0.001; wild cherry vs. wild elderberry: β = 2.11, z = 2.819, p < 0.05), whereas no significant differences were detected between wild blackberry and wild cherry (p = 0.98).

Discussion

Insight into the spatiotemporal population dynamics of resident natural enemies of a novel herbivore, as well as their trophic interactions under field conditions, is essential for designing effective pest management strategies. Here, we provide the first characterization in the Andean Patagonia region of how environment and season influence the occurrence of three parasitoid wasp species—Leptopilina heterotoma, Pachycrepoideus vindemiae, and Spalangia endius—with the potential to exploit the invasive pest Drosophila suzukii as a host. Although all three parasitoids were recorded across all surveyed environments, they exhibited distinct seasonal patterns, with L. heterotoma peaking in spring, P. vindemiae in summer, and S. endius showing a non-significant tendency toward higher activity in autumn. Further, we provide the first field-based evidence in our region of a parasitoid, S. endius, successfully attacking and completing development in D. suzukii, suggesting an early stage of adaptation of the resident parasitoids community to this recently introduced invasive pest.

Parasitoids were recovered across all surveyed areas using vinegar-baited traps, indicating a broad distribution throughout the CAP 42° region. The phenology of the parasitoids revealed a seasonal co-occurrence with the peak abundance of D. suzukii adults during mid-summer. Parasitoid captures increased during periods of high availability of ripe fruits in berry fields and declined toward the end of the harvest season. Despite this temporal overlap, adult parasitoid capture levels were consistently much lower than those of D. suzukii. Nonetheless, these results should be interpreted with caution for two main reasons. First, the relative attractiveness of baited traps versus background odors may vary with fruit phenology and local microenvironmental conditions, potentially influencing capture and biasing estimates of parasitoid abundance and seasonal activity. Second, parasitoid species may differ in their preference for foraging hosts in foliage versus ground-level microhabitats32,38, which could further affect trap captures. Still, the suitability of vinegar-baited traps for sampling a broad range of parasitoid wasp species has been validated in previous research in the same study area20.

Consistent with other studies, we found that parasitoid wasps captures were influenced by seasonality27,39. However, capture levels differed among species across seasons, suggesting different temporal dynamics in the surveyed geographic region. Namely, adults of L. heterotoma exhibited greater activity in spring compared to S. endius and P. vindemiae, while P. vindemiae became more active during the summer. Finally, S. endius showed, though not significant, a tendency to being more captured in autumn. These seasonal patterns may partially reflect interspecific differences in overwintering strategies, including overwintering stage and microhabitat use. L. heterotoma can overwinter as an adult40, whereas P. vindemiae likely overwinters as a late-stage larva or pupa within the host puparium41, probably in sheltered sites such as leaf litter, decaying fruit, or soil. S. endius appears to follow a similar strategy, although specific studies on its overwintering biology remain limited.

Beyond seasonal variation, our results also indicate geographic differences in the occurrence of drosophilid parasitoids associated with D. suzukii between northern and southern (Andean Patagonia) Argentinean fruit-growing regions12,36,42. Particularly, the pupal parasitoid Trichopria anastrephae Lima (Hymenoptera: Diapriidae), native to the South American Neotropical region and commonly reported in northwestern Argentina, was not detected in Patagonia; while the cosmopolitan larval parasitoid of saprophytic drosophilids, Leptopilina boulardi (Hymenoptera: Figitidae), was recorded only rarely in this region, with L. heterotoma being the most frequently figitid species12,20. Variation in resident parasitoid composition has been also documented between regions in North America and Europe27,43. Such distribution patterns may be related to species-specific thermal tolerances that influence survival, development, and reproduction, as well as to the marked environmental heterogeneity and distinctive climatic conditions characterizing the regions41,43.

Our results of species-specific capture patterns provide further insights into which habitats may serve as seasonal host sources or refuges for adult parasitoids. For example, the seasonal increase in P. vindemiae captures in cherry, berries and wild blackberry suggests the presence of seasonal host sources there when fruits are available, while increase in trap captures in tree curtains, particularly during the summer months, indicate that this environment may serve as seasonal refuge for adult wasps. Likewise, the increased captures of S. endius in wild blackberries and in berry crops during summer and autumn, respectively, suggest that these environments may also act as important seasonal host sources for this parasitoid. In contrast to D. suzukii, which maintains high year-round abundance in non-crop habitats such as native forests and wild blackberry16, none of the sampled habitats appeared to function as a permanent refuge or continuous host source for adult parasitoids. The persistent presence of D. suzukii in non-crop surroundings has been consistently reported in several studies15,44–46 and is likely driven by the availability of out-of-season food and host resources, as well as favorable microclimatic conditions that promote adult survival and reproduction18. When here referring to an environment as a seasonal host source for parasitoids, the hosts involved are likely Drosophila species other than D. suzukii22,43. At present, these alternative hosts and others flies probably play a greater role in structuring resident parasitoid assemblages and shaping their population dynamics. Nonetheless, our results highlight the importance of environmental heterogeneity in sustaining ecosystem services and emphasize the value of understanding species-specific temporal and habitat use. Such knowledge can inform strategies aimed at optimizing the timing and spatial deployment of augmentative biological control releases.

Despite these patterns, all three parasitoid species were detected across all the environments sampled using baited traps, including areas with cultivated (berries) and non-cultivated host plants (wild blackberry), as well as natural habitats such as native forests. This widespread occurrence is likely related to the generalist nature of the parasitoids studied and suggests a capacity for dispersal across habitats over wide areas34. Further, this is consistent with findings from multiple studies reporting a positive response of natural enemies to landscape complexity4,17,47. Still, the relatively low overall number of parasitoid captures recorded in this study suggests that naturally occurring populations may currently be insufficient to suppress D. suzukii population growth and prevent economic losses. However, given the availability of resources for parasitoids across the landscape, it remains possible that some species’ populations increase and may eventually establish effective interactions with D. suzukii.

Spalangia endius was the only parasitoid species that successfully emerged from D. suzukii. Although overall parasitism level was low (< 10%), it is noteworthy that parasitized puparia were recovered from the soil beneath host plants under natural conditions. This finding indicates that S. endius can locate and successfully exploit buried D. suzukii puparia. Parasitoid emergence also numerically increased with increasing D. suzukii puparia in soil, suggesting a potential capacity to increase population size as pest density rises48. The detection of parasitism in wild host plants further suggests that these environments could sustain parasitoid populations where D. suzukii populations persist year-round16. Based on these findings, further studies under both laboratory (e.g., functional response and reproductive performance) and field conditions are required to evaluate the potential of this species as a biological control agent. In parallel, potential direct and indirect non-target interactions with the resident insect community should be carefully considered49,50.

Pachycrepoideus vindemiae — widely reported in association with D. suzukii and other Drosophila27,28,35,39— was also expected to emerge from D. suzukii puparia recovered from soil beneath host plants. However, parasitism by this species was not detected in the field, even at low levels as those reported by other authors36. This may reflect the relatively recent invasion history of D. suzukii in the region, which may not have allowed sufficient time for local parasitoid populations to adapt or shift host use. There is evidence that exotic herbivores can accumulate native natural enemies over time, mostly generalist species51. Given P. vindemiae broad host range30, it may currently exploit alternative hosts that are more readily available or with which it shares a longer co-evolutionary relationship in sympatry. Nevertheless, given the evidence from other regions, this species may eventually establish an association with D. suzukii. Alternatively, the lack of P. vindemiae parasitism observed in our study may be attributed to the physical properties of the soil, which could constrain the ability of this parasitoid species to penetrate the soil matrix and locate the D. suzukii puparia. It is known that the pupation behavior of D. suzukii larvae is influenced by soil type. In loam soils, pupation tends to occur in deeper layers, whereas in sandy soils, larvae pupate almost exclusively near the surface52,53. Lastly, P. vindemiae may prefer the foliage to the ground microhabitat for foraging hosts32,38.

Consistent with previous report by Fischbein et al. (2024), L. heterotoma adults were captured in vinegar-baited traps but not recovered from field-collected fresh fruits infested with D. suzukii. This suggests that this parasitoid is likely exploiting other Drosophila species as hosts. Some studies indicate geographic intraspecific variability in North American and European L. heterotoma population’s ability to overcome the immunological response of D. suzukii under laboratory conditions24–26,31. Accordingly, populations from northeastern Italy, France and the Netherlands seem to be partially able to develop on D. suzukii23,28,29. Thus, while L. heterotoma does not appear to be currently associated with D. suzukii in Patagonia, the possibility that local populations may eventually adapt to develop in this host cannot be excluded.

Numerous non-crop fruiting plant species are recognized as reservoirs for D. suzukii populations11,54, and the pest is known to move seasonally between crop stands and surrounding natural habitats55. Many landscape-scale surveys assessed temporal variation in D. suzukii abundance across natural habitats through trap captures15,16, while others examined direct association with non-crop host plants42,56–58. Such direct associations provide a more accurate estimate of the contribution of a particular host plant to pest population dynamics. In this context, our study, although limited in duration, provides the first evidence from the CAP 42° region confirming that several feral fruit species serve as host plants for D. suzukii. The results offer initial insight into the role of host resources such as cherry, elderberry, and wild blackberry—commonly found as feral plants in natural habitats, hedgerows and home gardens near fruit farms—in sustaining D. suzukii populations. Despite this, infestation levels were significantly higher in cultivated raspberries than in wild fruits during summer–early autumn, possibly reflecting the greater abundance and attractiveness of cultivated raspberries during this period, or their higher suitability for larval development compared with unmanaged wild fruits59. Nevertheless, differences in fly population density or other environmental factors may also contribute to this difference in infestation.

The number of puparia collected from soil beneath host plants was much lower than that recovered from infested fruits, particularly in raspberries, likely due to methodological limitations of soil sampling. Consequently, D. suzukii adult emergence was not compared between microhabitats (fruit vs. soil) within the same plant species. Interestingly, significantly fewer puparia were recovered from soil beneath cultivated raspberries than beneath wild host plants (blackberry and cherry), suggesting that frequent fruit harvesting—a common management practice in the region—may disrupt D. suzukii reproduction in cultivated areas. Nonetheless, the high infestation levels observed in raspberries may indicate a continuous influx of flies from nearby wild hosts, highlighting that despite the focalized pest managing efforts in farms, pest pressure is influenced by the surrounding habitat.

Overall, the findings of this study provide valuable baseline ecological information on the parasitoid species surveyed, particularly regarding their seasonal dynamics across different environments and the incipient parasitism of D. suzukii in the Argentinean Andean Patagonia. Although natural parasitism levels were low and exclusively attributable to S. endius, most parasitized puparia were recovered from soil beneath wild blackberry plants. In northwestern Patagonia, wild blackberry is a widespread perennial host plant that provides important late-season breeding sites and year-round refuges for D. suzukii16. Taken together, these findings highlight the need for further studies to evaluate the reproductive performance and parasitism efficiency of S. endius on D. suzukii under laboratory and field conditions, as well as to assess potential non-target effects. Such research will be essential to determine whether this species could represent a suitable candidate for augmentative biological control within area-wide integrated pest management programs21.

Methods

Study area

The study was conducted in the Andean Region of the Parallel 42° (northwestern Patagonia, Argentina, 42° S, 71.5° W, Supplementary Fig. S1). In this region, farming of berries and other fruits represents a primary source of income. The landscape is characterized by a highly heterogeneous matrix of diversified crop production systems and non-crop habitats60(forest, tree curtain, semi-urban areas, hedgerows and large patches of wild blackberry and sweetbriar rose). The climate is humid, cool-temperate, with marked seasonality.

Placement of traps

To determine seasonal and environmental variation in the occurrence and abundance of parasitoid wasps, 52 translucent plastic containers (500 ml) with lids were used as traps. These containers had 8 perforations (3 mm diameter) in the upper third of the container and were filled with 200 ml of apple cider vinegar (Casalta, Argentina). Fermentation volatiles in vinegar are attractive to D. suzukii and parasitoid wasps that can use drosophilid species as hosts19,20.

Baited traps were arranged in 4 transects extending from crop adjacent forest to the center of the productive valley at CAP 42° (Supplementary Fig. S1), including the peripheral forest, agricultural areas and semi-urban zones. The transects were laid out loosely to accommodate the terrain’s irregularities, the arrangement of farms, and general accessibility16. Consequently, the lengths, relative positions, and trap spacing varied among transects. Traps were hung in different environments which included: berries (mainly Rubus idaeus, n = 12), cherry (Prunus avium, n = 9), native forest (the edges of the productive valley are delimited by a hard edge composed of a mixed forest, with species such as Austrocedrus chilensis D. Don, Luma apiculata Burret, Nothofagus domeyii Oerst, n = 4), sweetbriar rose (Rosa rubiginosa L., n = 7), tree curtains (small patches of tall trees, mainly poplars [Populus spp.] and pine trees [Pinus spp], positioned strategically to minimize the effect of wind on crops, n = 7) and wild blackberry (Rubus ulmifolius Schott), an invasive berry found in high densities at CAP 42°, n = 13) (Supplementary Table S1). Unfortunately, not all environments could be represented in all transects (as cherry and sweetbriar rose).

Traps were hung 1.2 m above the ground, shaded most of the day by branches and inspected at 15-day intervals for 2 years (from June 30, 2021, to June 16, 2023). At each inspection, vinegar from traps were filtered through a fine mesh strainer to retain all captured insects, which were preserved in 96% ethanol for later classification and taxonomic identification in the laboratory under a binocular microscope. After filtering, traps were re-baited with new vinegar. In case of trap damage, the whole trap was replaced.

Host–parasitoid trophic association

Sampling area

To investigate direct trophic associations between D. suzukii and the three parasitoid wasp species of our interest a field survey was conducted in an area encompassing an organically managed, insecticide-free fruit farm and its surroundings, located in CAP 42° (transect 2 area, Supplementary Fig. S1). Raspberry (Rubus idaeus L.) was the main cultivated fruit and the focal crop for sampling. Additionally, semi-natural surrounding environments within 300 m of the farm were sampled, including D. suzukii host plants such as wild elderberry (Sambucus nigra L.), wild cherry (Prunus avium L.), and wild blackberry (Rubus ulmifolius Schott). Sample collections of fruit and soil were performed every 10–15 days throughout most of the D. suzukii reproductive period, from mid-summer to early autumn (February–April 2024), until fruit availability declined. In total, five sampling events were conducted (Supplementary Table S2).

Fruit sampling in crop and semi-natural surrounding habitats

Specifically, to explore larval parasitism of D. suzukii fresh fruit on plants were randomly collected from cultivated raspberries, as well as from non-crop elderberry, cherry, and blackberry plants in the sampling area mentioned above (transect 2 area, Supplementary Fig. S1). Fruit sampling was adjusted to fruit ripening patterns to ensure comprehensive coverage of the fruiting season. Particularly, wild blackberry is one of the most widespread non-crop fruits in the study area invaded by D. suzukii. It grows forming large patches adjacent to fruit crops, along woodland margins, and as hedgerows. Because D. suzukii could develop within fallen fruit54, fallen mature fruits were also planned to be collected as potential sources of parasitized larvae. However, fallen cultivated raspberries were scarce and difficult to obtain due to frequent harvesting; and only mature wild cherries were successfully collected for laboratory incubation. These samples were processed cautiously, as they could also contain puparia of other Drosophilidae species.

Each replicate of on plant or fallen fruit consisted of 10–15 individual fruits for raspberry, wild blackberry, and cherry (45 ± 5 g approximately per replicate); and in the case of elderberries, each replicate consisted of a cluster of many small berries randomly selected from the plant aimed to achieve the same weight per replicate as the other fruits (Supplementary Table S2). Replicates were placed in separate containers adopting the methodology suggested by Abram et al. 202261 and kept for 7 days at 22 ± 1 °C, 50–70% relative humidity (RH) and 16:8 (L: D) light cycle in laboratory incubators. Fruit state during incubation period was monitored regularly (every 2–3 days), either to remove insect adult emerged and puparia or to control the amount of liquid released by the fruits. Before the fruit was discarded the remaining D. suzukii puparia and adult flies were also carefully removed. Adult flies were counted, and puparia separated into plastic Petri dishes (9 cm diameter) with moistened filter paper to complete their development to adulthood. In a few cases, other Drosophila spp puparia were separated from D. suzukii. D. suzukii was differentiated from other flies by the unique morphology of the spiracles with small finger-like projections61.

D. suzukii puparia were kept for 6 weeks in the same previously mentioned incubator conditions to ensure adult emergence61. All specimens emerging inside Petri dishes and dead puparia, which could provide information about the presence of a recognizable fly or parasitoid, were preserved in 96% EtOH for counting and inspection under binocular microscope. The number of adult flies that emerged from each fresh fruit species collected still attached to plants was used to estimate the levels of fruits natural infestation by D. suzukii in the field.

Soil sampling in crop and semi-natural surrounding habitats

To assess pupal parasitism of D. suzukii soil samples were collected beneath fruit host plant species, since D. suzukii mature larvae usually leaves the fruit to pupate in the soil52, although pupation may also occur inside or beneath fallen fruits54. Sampling was also conducted in crop (i.e., raspberry) and non-crop host plants (elderberry, cherry, and blackberry) in the same study area mentioned above (transect 2 area, Supplementary Fig. S1) and adjusted according to the temporal patterns of fruit ripening (Supplementary Table S2). Sampling soil zones were intentionally selected to increase the probability of encountering puparia, based on visual indicators of previous fruit fallen, such as the presence of fruit remains or seeds.

Each soil replicate consisted of five shovelfuls (2–3 cm depth) collected with a 15 cm garden shovel, placed in plastic bags, and transported to the laboratory. In the lab, samples were sieved through a 20 cm diameter, 1 mm mesh sieve over a white container (35 × 25 cm). Fly puparia were removed with a moistened brush, and the container was then filled with 4 cm of water to collect the remaining floating puparia. Puparia were transferred to Petri dishes (9 cm diameter) with moistened filter paper, and those not morphologically identified as D. suzukii were separated.

The puparia were incubated for 6 weeks under controlled conditions (22 ± 1 °C, 50–70% RH and 16:8 LD) and moistened with water every three days to prevent desiccation. All emerged specimens and dead puparia were preserved in 96% EtOH for subsequent counting and identification under binocular microscope (Zeiss Stemi 80). Pupal parasitism levels were calculated as the total number of emerged adult parasitoids divided by the sum of the number of adult emerged flies, the number of emerged adult parasitoids and the number of dead puparia of fly, all recovered from those soil samples of crop and non-crop fruit plants (adapted from35,36,61.

Morphological identification of captured specimens

Drosophila suzukii and parasitoid specimens were identified by the same diagnostic characters used in Fischbein et al. 2024 and using the voucher collection made in that study, placed in the Insect Population Ecology Group (GEPI; IFAB INTA Bariloche).

Molecular identification of Leptopilina heterotoma

As Leptipilina heterotoma belongs to a species-rich genus containing more than 30 species morphologically similar22 and has recently been reported in the region20, we confirmed its identity through DNA barcoding. Total DNA was extracted from a specimen collected on berries using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen). A mitochondrial fragment of the COI gene was first amplified (~ 700 bp) using the universal primers LCO1490 and HCO219862, followed by a second PCR using the L. heterotoma-specific primer LhF-21263 and HCO2198, yielding a 420 bp nested amplicon. PCRs (20 µl) contained 7 µl GoTaq® Green Master Mix (Promega), 1 µl of each primer (10 µM), 7 µl ddH₂O, and 1 µl template DNA (genomic DNA or a 1:10 dilution of the first product).The product of the second reaction was cloned into a plasmid vector by Macrogen Inc. Ten clones were sequenced bidirectionally, all producing identical sequences. BLAST searches against the NCBI database confirmed the morphological identification of L. heterotoma (more details in Supplementary information, NOTE S1).

Data analysis

Seasonal and environmental variations in occurrence and abundance of parasitoids

To examine seasonal and environmental variation in the occurrence and abundance of resident parasitoid wasps, we employed a series of statistical models. Specifically, differences in occurrence and abundance between parasitoid species, were analyzed using a generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) with a Negative Binomial distribution and log link function using the glmmTMB() function64. The response variable was the number of insects captured per trap/sampling date. Fixed effects included Season (winter, spring, summer, autumn), Environment (berry farms, cherry orchards, native forests, sweetbriar rose, tree curtains, wild blackberry), and Species (Leptopilina heterotoma, Spalangia endius, Pachycrespoideus vindemiae), along with the interactions Species × Season and Species × Environment. Trap code (1–52) nested within Transect (1–4) was included as a random effect to account for spatial variation among transects and repeated measures over time, and Sampling Cycle (first and second sampling years) was included as an additional random factor to control for interannual variation.

To further explore species-specific patterns, we performed additional generalized linear mixed models (GLMMs) for each parasitoid species. The response variable was the number of individuals captured per trap and sampling date. The models included environment, season, and season × environment interaction as fixed terms. Particularly, for L. heterotoma, a GLMM with Negative Binomial errors was fitted using the glmer.nb() function (lme4 package) to account for overdispersion. The initial model included Trap nested within Transect as a random effect; however, due to convergence issues, the random effect’s structure was adjusted to include Transect and Sampling Cycle instead. For S. endius and P. vindemiae, GLMMs with Poisson errors were fitted using the glmer() function and, Trap nested within Transect and Sampling Cycle were included as random effects. Model comparisons were performed using likelihood ratio tests by maximum likelihood.

Host–parasitoid trophic association and fruits natural infestation by Drosophila suzukii in the field

Differences in the number of parasitoid wasps emerging from D. suzukii puparia recovered from soil were analyzed using GLMM with a negative binomial error distribution and a log link (glmmTMB function). The model included “status of fruit” (factor with four levels: crop raspberry, wild cherry, wild elderberry, wild blackberry), the covariate number of D. suzukii puparia, and their interaction as fixed effects, while sampling date was included as random effect. Differences in the number of D. suzukii puparia collected from soil were analyzed using a GLMM with a negative binomial distribution and a log link (glmmTMB function), including status of fruit as fixed effect and sampling dates as random effect. Finally, to assess fresh fruits natural infestation by D. suzukii, a GLMM with Negative Binomial error and a log link (glmer.nb function) was performed, using the number of adult flies emerging from fruit collected still attached to plants as response variable and including status of fruit as fixed effect, fruit weight as a log-offset and sampling date as random effect.

Model selection and performance were assessed using Akaike’s Information Criterion (AIC) to identify the best-fitting and most parsimonious model. Overdispersion and residuals were examined using the DHARMa package65 and, Tukey-adjusted pairwise comparisons of models estimated means were performed using the pair() function (emmeans package). Estimated marginal means were calculated using the emmeans() function66. All analyses were performed using R Statistical Software67, with data visualization performed using the ggplot2.
