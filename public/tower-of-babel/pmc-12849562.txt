nSOFA scores predict prolonged mechanical ventilation in neonatal respiratory distress syndrome: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Lijuan Wang, You Duan, Xiaohu Zhang, Liang Xie, Hanmin Liu, Yang Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeonatal respiratory distress syndrome (NRDS) is one of the most common respiratory diseases in the neonatal period and a major cause of neonatal mortality. Prolonged mechanical ventilation (MV) is associated with adverse outcomes in NRDS patients, highlighting the need for effective early risk stratification tools. The Neonatal Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (nSOFA) score has emerged as a potential predictor of adverse outcomes, but its association with prolonged MV remains unclear.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study utilized data from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care III (MIMIC-III) database, analyzing 642 NRDS infants admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) between 2001 and 2012. Patients were divided into two groups based on MV duration: prolonged MV (> 96 h) and non-prolonged MV (≤ 96 h). The highest nSOFA score within 24 h of admission was the primary exposure variable. Covariates included demographic data, clinical characteristics, and laboratory results. A multivariable logistic regression model was used to assess the association between nSOFA scores and prolonged MV. The predictive ability of nSOFA was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC).ResultsOf the 642 NRDS infants, 192 (29.9%) required prolonged MV. Each 1-point increase in the nSOFA score was associated with a 29% higher risk of prolonged MV (odds ratio [OR]: 1.29; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.16–1.44; p < 0.001). The nSOFA score demonstrated moderate predictive ability (AUC: 0.7245; 95% CI: 68.41%–76.49%), which was significantly better than its respiratory sub-score (AUC: 0.6936; p < 0.001) and comparable to the SOFA score (AUC: 0.7218; p = 0.89). Using an nSOFA cutoff of 3, the sensitivity and specificity for predicting prolonged MV were 64.06% and 70.22%, respectively.ConclusionThe nSOFA score is an independent risk factor for prolonged MV in NRDS infants, with moderate predictive ability. Its simplicity and effectiveness make it a valuable tool for early risk stratification in NICUs. Future multicenter studies are needed to validate these findings and explore the potential of dynamic nSOFA monitoring in improving predictive accuracy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-025-06395-w.Keywords: Neonatal respiratory distress syndrome, Mechanical ventilation, Neonatal sequential organ failure assessment, Neonate, Prediction

Introduction

Neonatal respiratory distress syndrome (NRDS) remains a significant cause of morbidity and mortality in neonates. Despite advancements in surfactant replacement therapy and respiratory support technologies, a subset of infants still require prolonged mechanical ventilation (MV) [1, 2]. This issue is associated with increased resource utilization, longer hospitalization, and a higher risk of complications such as ventilator-associated pneumonia and bronchopulmonary dysplasia, underscoring the urgent need for better early risk identification tools.

In recent years, organ function assessment tools have been increasingly applied to risk prediction in neonatal critical care. Among these, the neonatal Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (nSOFA), a tool designed to evaluate multi-organ functional status, has been validated for predicting the mortality risk in preterm infants with late-onset sepsis [3, 4]. Additionally, nSOFA demonstrates good to excellent discrimination for all-cause mortality in NICU patients with an AUC ranging from 0.88 to 0.95) [5]. Notably, Poggi et al. showed that the maximum nSOFA score within the first 24 h after the onset of late-onset sepsis could predict respiratory outcomes [6].

The nSOFA score quantifies early multi-organ dysfunction, including the intensity of respiratory support, vasopressor requirements, and thrombocytopenia, reflecting the evolving burden of systemic illness rather than being limited to sepsis alone. This scoring mechanism aligns with the pathophysiology of prolonged mechanical ventilation in NRDS patients, as the need for extended MV in NRDS is not solely driven by underlying pulmonary immaturity but also by subsequent management factors, hemodynamic instability, inflammation, and coagulation disorders. Therefore, the nSOFA score on the first day of life may effectively reflect the risk of persistent ventilator dependence and may serve as a valuable tool for early identification of high-risk neonates.

The advent of the big data provides new opportunities to address this gap. The Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care III (MIMIC-III) database, a large, publicly available clinical database, contains extensive information on neonatal intensive care. This resource enables an in-depth exploration of the relationship between nSOFA scores and the prognosis of NRDS patients. Compared to smaller, single-center studies, the MIMIC-III database offers a diverse and comprehensive dataset, which enhances the generalizability of findings and allows for robust statistical analyses.

This study aims to analyze the clinical data of NRDS patients in the MIMIC-III database to investigate the association between nSOFA scores and the need for prolonged MV. Specifically, we hypothesize that higher nSOFA scores within the first 24 h of life are associated with an increased risk of prolonged MV in NRDS patients. The ultimate goal is to provide a practical tool for the early identification of high-risk neonates, thereby improving clinical management and outcomes.

Methods

Database description

This study utilized data from the MIMIC-III (Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care III) database (version 1.4), a publicly accessible intensive care database developed by the MIT Laboratory for Computational Physiology (https://physionet.org/content/mimiciii/1.4/). The database contains detailed clinical data of patients admitted to the intensive care units (ICU) at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC) in the United States from 2001 to 2012. It includes information on 7,870 neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) patients, covering vital signs, laboratory test results, medication records, mechanical ventilation details, and other critical clinical data [7]. The MIMIC-III database has been widely used in critical care research, including prognosis analysis and predictive model development. All patient data were de-identified to protect privacy, and either ethical approval was not required. The first author of this study, Lijuan Wang, completed the “Collaborative Institutional Training Initiative” (CITI) courses and obtained the qualification to use the database for this study (Certificate ID: 13371357).

Study population

We extracted hospitalization information for infants diagnosed with Neonatal Respiratory Distress Syndrome (NRDS) using the ICD-9 code “769”. For patients with multiple admissions, only the first admission was retained, resulting in 1,306 NRDS patients. Patients were excluded based on the following criteria: 1). Missing blood gas analysis results within 24 h of admission; 2). Age > 24 h at admission; 3). Death after invasive ventilation for < 96 h. Ultimately, 642 infants were included in the study (Fig. 1).

Data collection

Clinical data were extracted using PostgresSQL and Navicate Premium software through structured query language (SQL). Variables collected included demographic information, vital signs, laboratory tests, diagnostic codes, medications, use of invasive ventilation for over 96 h, and survival data within the first 24 h of NICU admission. The highest nSOFA score within the first 24 h of admission was selected as the primary study variable. Clinical data required to calculate the nSOFA score were also collected, including intubation and mechanical ventilation status, the lowest PO2/FiO2 within 24 h of admission, the lowest platelet count, and the need for vasoactive drugs, inotropic drugs, or glucocorticoids. SpO2/FiO2 conversion used the arterial oxygen partial pressure (PaO2)/FiO2 ratio, where SpO2/FiO2 = 64 + 0.84 × (PaO2/FiO2) [8]. The remaining test values, except for PCO2 being the highest value within the first 24 h of admission and BE being the lowest value, were all based on the initial values within the first 24 h of admission. Heart rate was averaged. Comorbidities such as neonatal asphyxia, pneumonia, jaundice, and sepsis, were identified using ICD-9 codes.

Grouping and endpoint events

In this study, patients were divided into two groups.

Prolonged mechanical ventilation group, those using invasive ventilation for more than 96 h (n = 192);

Non-prolonged ventilation group, those not using invasive ventilation for more than 96 h (n = 476).

The primary endpoint was defined as a binary variable across the entire cohort: prolonged invasive mechanical ventilation (MV ≥ 96 h). For patients who never received IMV, the endpoint was coded as ‘not occurred’ (0); for those who received IMV for ≥ 96 h, the endpoint was coded as 1. This coding strategy enables analysis across all NRDS patients and ensures logical consistency.

Management of missing data and outliers

To minimize bias, variables with more than 20% missing values were excluded (C-reactive protein, albumin, creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, potassium, sodium, chloride, calcium, glucose). Variables with less than 5% missing values (initial weight, initial length, heart rate) were filled with the median of that variable. Variables with 5%−10% missing values (hemoglobin, white blood cells, red cell distribution width, platelets) were filled with bridging values to reduce missing values to less than 5%, then filled with the median. Variables with 10%−20% missing values (gestational age) were filled using the random forest method. Outliers were handled by visually inspecting scatter plots to remove significantly abnormal values (white blood cell count, 3 cases) and filling with the median. The handling of missing and outlier data was done using STATA software (version 16), and random forest was performed using R software (version 4.3.3).

Statistical analysis

The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Skewness and Kurtosis test. For normally distributed continuous variables, results were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD); for non-normally distributed continuous variables, results were expressed as median (interquartile range, IQR); categorical variables were expressed as count (%). Baseline characteristics were compared using the T-test or rank-sum test for continuous variables and Pearson’s chi-square test for categorical variables. Univariate logistic regression analysis was used to evaluate the relationship between the nSOFA score and the primary outcome, and restricted cubic spline regression was used to determine if there was a linear relationship. All children were divided into high nSOFA and low nSOFA groups by calculating the Youden index. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were used to assess the relationship between each 1-point increase in the score of high and low nSOFA groups and the primary outcome, as well as the comparison of the relationship between high and low nSOFA groups and the outcome. Univariate logistic regression was used for covariate screening, and variables with P < 0.1 in univariate regression analysis and those with potential clinical significance were included in the multivariate logistic regression model for analysis. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to evaluate the predictive ability of the nSOFA score and its subscale RS (Respiratory Score) for invasive ventilation lasting more than 96 h in neonates with NRDS. Sensitivity, specificity, and the area under the curve (AUC) were calculated for each indicator. CRIB/CRIB II primarily targets preterm infants born at ≤ 32 weeks (approximately 25% of infants in our cohort were > 32 weeks), and key SNAPPE II variables (e.g., multiple seizures occurring within a strict time window) cannot be reliably obtained from MIMIC III, making reproducible calculation infeasible. The primary comparisons focused on the predictive ability of nSOFA and its respiratory subscale.

Additionally, to explore the cross-population applicability of the multi-organ dysfunction scoring frameworks, we incorporated adult scoring systems into the analysis, including the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment Score (SOFA) and Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation III (APSIII). Although these scoring systems have limited direct clinical relevance for neonatal populations, their inclusion aims to provide preliminary insights into the potential universality of multi-organ dysfunction scoring frameworks across different populations.

Restricted cubic spline regression was performed using R software (version 4.3.3), and all other analyses were conducted using STATA software (version 16). A P-value < 0.05 in two-tailed tests was considered statistically significant. To evaluate the calibration of the final multivariable model (Model 3), we performed 1,000 bootstrap resampling iterations on the original cohort and plotted the bias-corrected calibration curve. The predicted probabilities for mechanical ventilation lasting > 96 h were grouped by deciles and compared with the corresponding observed proportions. Calibration was quantified using the bias-corrected calibration intercept and calibration slope obtained through the same bootstrap resampling process. The bias-corrected calibration intercept was − 0.013, and the calibration slope was 0.968, indicating a high degree of agreement between predicted probabilities and observed probabilities across the entire risk spectrum (Figure S1). No systematic overestimation or underestimation of risk was observed.

Results

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics

A total of 642 NRDS patients were included, with the primary endpoint ‘MV ≥ 96 h’ coded as a binary variable across the entire cohort (patients who did not receive IMV were defined as ‘not occurred’. The baseline characteristics of the group using invasive ventilation for more than 96 h and the group not using them for more than 96 h are listed in Table 1. Of the 642 children, 227 (43.15%) were female, and 365 (56.85%) were male. The median age of the children was 12.96 h (7.92, 18.00 h). Among the admitted children, 394 (61.37%) were Caucasian. Children using invasive ventilation for more than 96 h accounted for 30%. The overall median gestational age was 31–32 weeks (IQR: 29–30 to 35–36 weeks). We noted that the gestational age of children in the group using invasive ventilation for more than 96 h was mainly concentrated in the early ages, with 25–26 weeks being the most common (36.98%). In contrast, in the group not using invasive ventilation for more than 96 h, the most common gestational age was 27–28 weeks (21.33%). The difference in gestational age distribution between the two groups was statistically significant (χ2 = 251.8464, p < 0.001). The initial weight, length, PaO2/FiO2, SpO2/FiO2, hemoglobin, red cell distribution width (RDW), white blood cell count (WBC), platelet count, PH, and BE of children in the group using invasive ventilation for more than 96 h were significantly lower than those in the group not using them for more than 96 h, while heart rate (HR), PCO2, nSOFA, Respiratory score, SOFA, and APSIII were significantly higher than those in the group not using invasive ventilation for more than 96 h. Compared to the group not using invasive ventilation for more than 96 h, the group using them for more than 96 h had a higher proportion of congenital pneumonia, neonatal jaundice, and neonatal sepsis (p < 0.05).

By calculating the Youden index, all children were divided into a high nSOFA group (≥ 3 points) and a low nSOFA group (0–2 points). After grouping, it was found that the OR value for the low nSOFA group was 2.042 (95%CI: 1.511–2.759, p < 0.001), and the OR value for the high nSOFA group was 1.307 (95%CI: 1.184–1.444, p < 0.001), indicating that for each 1-point increase in the low group, the risk of using invasive ventilation for > 96 h increased more.

Subsequently, covariates selected by the stepwise backward method were included in the multivariate logistic regression analysis to adjust the analysis of the relationship between the nSOFA score after grouping NRDS patients and the use of invasive ventilation for > 96 h (Table 3). The results showed that the association between the nSOFA score and the use of invasive ventilation for > 96 h was statistically significant in all three models (all p < 0.05), and even after full adjustment, the nSOFA retained significant predictive value. The OR value of the low group decreased from 2.04 to 1.41 after adjustment, and the high group OR value decreased from 1.31 to 1.26, with the average variance inflation factor (VIF) of the model being 3.94, indicating that multicollinearity was within an acceptable range and did not significantly affect the regression analysis results. The OR value comparison between high and low groups decreased with adjustment (4.20 to 2.90), average VIF 3.46. However, after adjustment, the OR values of the low and high groups became closer (1.41 vs. 1.26).

We plotted the ROC curves for four indicators: nSOFA, Respiratory score, SOFA score, and APSIII, to predict the incidence of NRDS children using invasive ventilation for more than 96 h (Fig. 4; Table 5). The information in Fig. 4 is listed in Table 5. The AUC of nSOFA was [72.45% (95% CI: 68.41% − 76.49%)], significantly better than the Respiratory score [69.36% (95% CI: 65.39% − 73.34%)]. Meanwhile, the optimal cutoff value for nSOFA was determined to be 3, with a sensitivity of 64.06% and a specificity of 70.22%. The corresponding Youden index was 0.343.

Discussion

This study explored the association between the highest nSOFA score within 24 h of admission and prolonged use of invasive mechanical ventilation (MV > 96 h) in neonatal respiratory distress syndrome (NRDS) patients. Based on data from the MIMIC-III database, our findings confirm that the nSOFA score is an independent risk factor for prolonged mechanical ventilation. Specifically, each 1-point increase in the nSOFA score was associated with a 29% increase in the risk of MV > 96 h (OR: 1.29; 95% CI: 1.16–1.44; P < 0.001). The ROC analysis demonstrated moderate predictive ability for the nSOFA score (AUC: 72.45%, 95% CI: 68.41%–76.49%), which outperformed the respiratory subscore alone and was comparable to the more complex SOFA score (p = 0.89). These results highlight the potential of the nSOFA score as a practical tool for early identification of NRDS patients at high risk for prolonged mechanical ventilation.

Clinical implications

Neonatal respiratory distress syndrome (NRDS) is highly prevalent among preterm infants [9], with the incidence increasing as gestational age decreases [10]. NRDS remains one of the leading causes of neonatal mortality, especially in low-income countries [11]. It poses a serious health threat not only to preterm infants but also to full-term infants [12–14]. Mechanical ventilation is a key technique of NRDS management. However, prolonged mechanical ventilation significantly increase the risk of complications, including ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP), bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) [15], leading to prolonged hospitalization and poor long-term outcomes [16]. In neonatal intensive care units, providing adequate respiratory support while minimizing lung injury is one of the main challenges faced by clinicians [17].

The early identification of high-risk NRDS patients requiring prolonged MV is critical for optimizing respiratory support and minimizing ventilation-related complications. The early identification of high-risk NRDS patients requiring prolonged mechanical ventilation (MV) is crucial for optimizing respiratory support and minimizing ventilation-related complications. In this study, we focus on nSOFA—a dynamic multi-organ dysfunction score—because prolonged mechanical ventilation reflects an evolving systemic burden. Although SNAP II/SNAPPE II and CRIB/CRIB II have been validated for mortality assessment [18–20] and are associated with certain complications [19, 21–24], evidence regarding their ability to predict prolonged mechanical ventilation in neonatal respiratory distress syndrome (NRDS) is limited. Additionally, their applicability (CRIB II is restricted to ≤ 32 weeks of gestation) and data feasibility (missing SNAPPE II variables in MIMIC III) make direct comparisons unfeasible. This represents a limitation of the present study. Future prospective or multicenter studies with complete datasets should aim to construct SNAPPE/CRIB models in parallel and compare their predictive performance with nSOFA. Furthermore, strategies for their combined or sequential application should be explored.

Traditional assessment tools, such as the Silverman-Anderson score and Downes score, are subjective and lack standardization, while blood gas analysis, chest imaging, and respiratory mechanics monitoring are either invasive or unsuitable for routing use in all clinical settings [25]. For example, blood gas analysis objectively reflects oxygenation function, but it is invasive, and repeated blood sampling can cause repeated pain and blood loss. Chest imaging and respiratory mechanics monitoring are not suitable for all clinical scenarios. In contrast, the nSOFA score consists of objective indicators (e.g., mechanical ventilation status, platelet count, and vasoactive drug use) into a single, quantifiable metric, making it a simple, reproducible, and efficient tool for risk stratification in NICUs.

Regarding the threshold for “prolonged mechanical ventilation (PMV),” there is currently no universally accepted definition in clinical or research contexts. In adult studies, a commonly used criterion is “≥21 consecutive days with ≥ 6 hours of ventilation per day.” In pediatric and neonatal studies, the reported thresholds vary widely, ranging from 48 h to several months, with ≥ 7 days, ≥ 14 days, and ≥ 21 days being the most frequently cited [26]. Overall, high-quality evidence to establish a universal gold standard for PMV in children or neonates is lacking. In this study, we selected “≥96 hours (4 days)” as the threshold for prolonged ventilation, based on the following considerations: First, in terms of clinical relevance, the first 72–96 h of ventilation for NRDS patients typically encompass surfactant administration and optimization of initial ventilation strategies. Ventilation exceeding 4 days often indicates a more complex disease course, weaning difficulties, or increased risk of complications. Using ≥ 96 h as the threshold provides a clear distinction between “short-term acute-phase ventilation” and “prolonged ventilation dependency.” Second, from the perspective of data operability and reproducibility, the ≥ 96-hour threshold can be reliably constructed as a binary outcome in the MIMIC-III database using structured records. This approach avoids reliance on complex temporal reconstructions or subjective bridging rules, thereby reducing measurement error and facilitating reproducibility across studies, making it easier for readers to validate findings. Finally, the ≥ 96-hour threshold aligns with resource intensity stratification in several coding/grouping systems (e.g., in the context of DRG classifications), providing practical comparability. Furthermore, we have clearly defined the use of terminology in the manuscript, specifying that “prolonged mechanical ventilation” in this study refers to invasive ventilation lasting ≥ 96 h.

In practice, nSOFA may provide dynamic risk stratification to guide real-time management of NRDS patients. When nSOFA is elevated or shows a sustained increase, it prompts the initiation of a “high-risk ventilation pathway,” which includes protective lung strategies, HFOV and lung recruitment assessments, reinforcement of VAP prevention bundles, early intervention for infections and circulatory abnormalities, as well as enhanced neuroprotective measures. Conversely, when nSOFA is low or rapidly declining, it facilitates the transition to an extubation and weaning pathway, reducing unnecessary sedation and invasive monitoring. This “stratification-trigger-reassessment” closed-loop approach enables earlier identification of patients likely to require prolonged ventilation, optimizing resource allocation and potentially reducing complications such as BPD and VAP, as well as overall ventilation duration. Therefore, nSOFA serves as a valuable complement to existing NRDS assessment tools, aiding in strategy optimization and the prevention of further complications.

Comparison with existing literature

The SOFA was initially used in adult intensive care units (ICU) to assess the functional status of the respiratory, cardiovascular, liver, kidney, coagulation, and central nervous systems, quantify the severity of organ dysfunction, and predict patient mortality risk [27, 28]. However, due to significant physiological differences between neonates and adults, directly applying the SOFA score has limitations. Therefore, the nSOFA score was developed [29]. The nSOFA score is specifically designed for neonates, simplifying the assessment to include only three subscores: respiratory, cardiovascular, and hematologic systems, quantified by mechanical ventilation needs, use of vasoactive drugs, and platelet count [3]. Recent studies have shown that the nSOFA score demonstrates high accuracy in predicting mortality risk and adverse outcomes in neonatal sepsis [3, 4, 30, 31]. The application of the nSOFA score has also expanded to predicting mortality rates in preterm infants for conditions other than sepsis [32–34]. Shi et al. [1] showed that the nSOFA score is positively correlated with mortality risk in NICU neonatal NRDS cases. Poggi et al. [6] found that the maximum nSOFA score within the first 24 h after the onset of late-onset sepsis (LOS) could predict respiratory outcomes. Our study builds on these findings by providing evidence of the nSOFA score’s predictive value for prolonged mechanical ventilation in NRDS patients.

The respiratory subscore, which accounts for the largest proportion of the total nSOFA score, has been shown to correlate strongly with respiratory outcomes [3]. However, our findings indicate that the combined use of respiratory, cardiovascular, and hematologic subscores provides superior predictive performance compared to the respiratory subscore alone. This highlights the importance of a multi-system approach to risk assessment in critically ill neonates.

Limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations. First, it is a retrospective analysis based on a single database, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other populations or settings. Second, while the nSOFA score demonstrated moderate sensitivity (64.06%) and specificity (70.22%), its predictive accuracy is not sufficient for standalone use and should be combined with other assessment methods to improve precision. Third, direct comparisons between nSOFA and SNAP-II/SNAPPE-II or CRIB/CRIB-II were not feasible due to cohort heterogeneity (more than 25% of patients with gestational age greater than thirty-two weeks) and structural missing data for key variables in the MIMIC-III database. While this limits direct benchmarking, the mechanistic alignment of nSOFA and our sensitivity analyses support its applicability. Fourth, the study did not account for potential confounding factors, such as variations in clinical management practices or the use of surfactant therapy, which may have influenced the outcomes. Fifth, the data used in this study are relatively outdated, raising concerns about temporal drift and the need for recalibration. Neonatal respiratory support practices have evolved significantly in recent years, including the broader adoption of early non-invasive support, gentler mechanical ventilation strategies, and changes in surfactant therapy protocols. As a result, the discrimination and calibration of nSOFA observed in this study may not fully align with current practices, necessitating recalibration efforts, such as updates to the intercept and slope.

Given that patients who did not receive IMV inherently constitute structural zero values for the endpoint ‘MV ≥ 96 h,’ the outcome distribution exhibits zero inflation. This design and coding choice, based on the full NRDS framework, may dilute the event rate to some extent but enhances applicability to the real-world population spectrum. Future research should focus on validating the nSOFA score in prospective, multicenter studies and exploring its integration with other predictive models or biomarkers. Time recalibration based on current practices (e.g., updates to intercept and slope) is necessary to enhance its discrimination and calibration capabilities. Additionally, investigating the impact of interventions guided by nSOFA scores on clinical outcomes could provide valuable insights into its utility in clinical decision-making.

Conclusion

In this observational cohort study, the nSOFA score demonstrated discriminatory ability in predicting prolonged mechanical ventilation, supporting its role as a dynamic risk stratification tool for neonatal respiratory distress syndrome (NRDS). It can aid in the early identification of high-risk infants and the optimization of ventilation-related treatment strategies. However, its actual impact on management pathways and clinical outcomes requires confirmation through prospective, multicenter, and interventional studies, along with necessary recalibration to align with contemporary practices.
