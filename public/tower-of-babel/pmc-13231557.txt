Atractylenolide II alleviates LPS-induced acute lung injury in A549 cells via the TNIP2/NF-κB pathway
Authors: Lixia Yu, Huan Chen, Xue Chen, Yougang Ke, Feng Fang, Peng Ren, Yisheng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute lung injury (ALI) is a severe inflammatory disorder characterized by disruption of the alveolar-capillary barrier, uncontrolled inflammatory response, and excessive oxidative stress. Atractylenolide II (ATR II), a key sesquiterpenoid compound isolated from the Atractylodes macrocephala, has been shown to exert significant anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects in various disease models. However, its therapeutic potential in ALI and the underlying molecular mechanisms remain largely unexplored. The present study was designed to elucidate the underlying mechanisms by which ATR II mediates its effects in ALI.MethodsLipopolysaccharide (LPS)-stimulated human alveolar epithelial A549 cells were employed to establish an in vitro ALI model. EdU proliferation assay and flow cytometry were applied to evaluate cell proliferation and apoptotic rates, respectively. Inflammatory cytokines and oxidative stress levels were assessed by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) and specific commercial assay kits. Additionally, the protein expression of apoptosis-related molecules and TNIP2/NF-κB pathway-associated proteins were assessed by western blotting. The mRNA level of TNIP2 was measured by reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) .ResultsATR II significantly promoted cell proliferation and reduced apoptosis in LPS-induced A549 cells a dose-dependent manner. Furthermore, ATR II effectively inhibited the release of inflammatory factors and alleviated oxidative stress in LPS-stimulated A549 cells. Mechanistic investigations demonstrated that ATR II modulated the TNIP2/NF-κB signaling pathway: specifically, it upregulated TNIP2 protein expression, decreased phosphorylated p65 (p-p65) levels, and reduced the p-p65/p65 ratio. Knockdown of TNIP2 partially abrogated the protective effects of ATR II in LPS-exposed A549 cells.ConclusionOur findings revealed that ATR-II exerts protective effects against LPS-induced ALI in alveolar epithelial cells by regulating the TNIP2/NF-κB pathway. These results identified ATR-II as a potential therapeutic agent for the treatment of ALI.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13019-026-04037-5.Keywords: Atractylenolide II, Acute lung injury, TNIP2, NF-κB pathway, Oxidative stress

Introduction

ALI is a severe pulmonary disorder characterized by an acute inflammatory response and compromised alveolar-capillary membrane integrity, with a mortality rate of 35–40% [1]. The development of ALI induces disruption of the pulmonary microvascular membrane, the epithelial-endothelial barrier, and impairs macrophage-epithelial crosstalk, leading to a loss of control of the inflammatory cascade, increased oxidative stress, and aberrant activation of apoptosis [2, 3]. Currently, the clinical management of ALI relies on supportive therapy, including the bronchodilators to improve airway resistance, glucocorticoids to suppress the excessive inflammatory response, and mechanical ventilation to maintain oxygenation [4–6]. However, the available treatments only provide partial symptomatic relief and cannot fundamentally reverse the disease process. Therefore, exploring the core pathological mechanisms of ALI is essential for developing novel and effective therapeutic interventions.

The nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) signaling pathway acts as a master regulator in the inflammatory response, and its aberrant activation is widely recognized as a pivotal pathological event driving the initiation and development of ALI [7]. Seminal early studies have firmly established that excessive NF-κB activation promotes the transcription and release of downstream pro-inflammatory cytokines, aggravates lung tissue edema and barrier damage, and ultimately exacerbates the progression of ALI [7, 8]. For instance, Hydnocarpin D suppresses oxidative stress and inflammatory response via the MAPK/NF-κB and Keap1/Nrf2/HO-1 pathways, thereby alleviating LPS-induced ALI [9]. Similarly, Li et al. reported that the Xuanfei Baidu Formula lieves LPS-induced ALI via inhibiting NF-κB signaling pathway [10]. TNIP2, a protein-encoding gene, has been definitively identified as an endogenous negative regulator of NF-κB activation, and its dysregulation is implicated in the progression of multiple inflammatory disorder [11]. Tao et al. reported that TNIP2 overexpression effectively attenuated the production of interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and reduced cell apoptosis stimulated by hyperoxia in bronchopulmonary dysplasia [12]. Wang et al. proposed that long non-coding RNA NAV2-AS5 reduces chondrocyte inflammation through targeting the miR-8082/TNIP2 axis in the pathogenesis of osteoarthritis [13]. Given that TNIP2 exerts anti-inflammatory effects by negatively controlling NF-κB activation, and NF-κB overactivation is the core pathological mechanism of ALI, the biological function of TNIP2 in ALI is highly worthy of in-depth investigation. Nevertheless, the precise function of TNIP2 in ALI has not been fully elucidated. Accordingly, the specific mechanism by which TNIP2 mediates ALI progression by regulating NF-κB signaling remains largely unknown, which represents a key research gap in this field.

Atractylenolide II (ATR II) is a key bioactive constituent isolated from the rhizome of Atractylodes macrocephala, with diverse pharmacological properties [14]. A report by Dou et al. revealed that ATR II induces cycle arrest and apoptosis in breast cancer cells via ER pathway [15]. Moreover, ATR II has exhibited prominent anti-inflammatory effects in multiple inflammation-related diseases models. For example, the ethanol extract of Atractylodes rhizoma remarkably reduced the lung W/D ratio and the release of inflammatory cytokines in rat models of ALI [16]. However, the role and underlying mechanism of AT-II in ALI remain to be fully elucidated.

Therefore, this research was designed to explore whether ATR II alleviates ALI by regulating the TNIP2/NF-κB signaling axis, and to clarify its specific molecular mechanism, providing a theoretical basis for the clinical application of ATR II in ALI therapy.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and treatment

A549 cells (ATCC, CCL-185™) were maintained in DMEM (Gibco, MD, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, 40130ES76, YEASEN) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Cells were maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. 1 µg/mL LPS (SMB00610, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) was exposed to A549 cells for 24 h to establish the lung injury model in vitro. ATR II (HY-N0202, MedChemExpress) was then added to the cells at concentrations of 2, 4, and 8 µg/ml for 48 h.

Cell counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

A549 cells were implanted into 96-well plates and then treated with ATR II at concentrations of 2, 4, and 8 µg/ml for 48 h. Then, each well was added with 10 µl CCK-8 solution (C0038, Beyotime, Shanghai, China) and continuously incubated for additional 4 h. Then, the supernatant was discarded and 100 µl of DMSO was added to dissolve lysate without light. Finally, OD450 was measured by a microplate reader (BIOTEK, USA) following the protocol.

EdU proliferation assay

EdU-488 Cell Proliferation Assay Kit (Servicebio, G1601) was applied for evaluating cell proliferation. Briefly, cells were cultivated in 24-well plates and cultured under experimental conditions. Next, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Sinopharm, 80096618) for 20 min, and permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 (Sinopharm, 30188928) for 15 min, and subjected to EdU-488 detection for 30 min in the dark. Nuclei were then stained with 1× Hoechst33342 for 30 min. Finally, a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, IX51) was used to obtain the fluorescence images, and EdU-positive cells were quantified using ImageJ software.

Flow cytometry (FCM) assay

The Annexin V-FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit (BD Biosciences, Cat# 556547) was used to assess apoptosis in A549 cells according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Apoptotic cells were detected by a flow cytometer (Beckman CytoFLEX) and data were processed with Kaluza software per the manufacturer’s protocol.

ELISA assay

Following treatment, A549 cells were plated in 96-well plates. After centrifugation, the concentrations of IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α in supernatants were assessed via ELISA (ELK Biotechnology) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Absorbance was measured at the wavelength of 450 nm on a Multiskan Spectrum reader (Molecular, Cmax plus).

Measurement of ROS, SOD and CAT activity

The ROS levels were detected by Reactive Oxygen Species Assay Kit (E004-1, Nanjing Jiancheng), SOD and CAT activity were determined using Superoxide Dismutase Assay Kit (A001-3, Nanjing Jiancheng), and Catalase Assay Kit (A007-1-1, Nanjing Jiancheng) according to the manuals.

RT-qPCR analysis

TRIpure Total RNA Extraction Reagent (EP013, ELK Biotechnology) was used to extract the total RNA from A549 cells. Reverse transcription was performed with the EntiLink™ 1 st Strand cDNA Synthesis Kit (Eq. 003, ELK Biotechnology). RNA expression was determined using EnTurbo™ SYBR Green PCR SuperMix (Eq. 001, ELK Biotechnology) on a QuantStudio 6 Flex Real-Time PCR System (Life Technologies). The relative mRNA expression level of TNIP2 was analyzed via the 2−ΔΔCt method. Primers are presented in Table 1.

Western blot assay

Total protein from A549 cells was isolated using RIPA lysis buffer (AS1004, ASPEN), and a BCA assay kit (AS1086, ASPEN) was used to quantify the protein samples. Equal amounts of protein (20–50 µg) were resolved by 10% SDS-PAGE and then transferred onto PVDF membranes (IPVH00010, Millipore). After blocking with 5% non-fat milk, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: Cleaved Caspase-3 (AF7022, Affbiotech, 1:500), Caspase-3 (AF7022, Affbiotech, 1:500), TNIP2 (15459-1-AP, Proteintech, 1:1000), p-NF-κB p65 (#3033, CST, 1:500), NF-κB p65 (#8242, CST, 1:2000), and β-Actin (TDY051, TDY Biotech, 1:10,000). Subsequently, the membranes were treated with secondary antibodies for 2 h. Blots were visualized using an ECL reagent (AS1059, ASPEN).

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 8.0 was performed for statistical analyses and are presented as mean ± SD from three independent experiments. Statistical significance was determined by unpaired Student’s t-test (for two-group comparisons) or one-way ANOVA (for multiple groups) followed by Tukey’s test. The normality of data distribution was verified using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and the homogeneity of variances was confirmed via Levene’s test. *P < 0.05, and **P < 0.01 indicated as significant difference. All experiments were biologically repeated three times.

Results

ATR II attenuated LPS-induced cytotoxicity in A549 cells dose-dependently

Figure S1A presented the chemical molecular formula of ATR II. The CCK-8 assay was used to test the cytotoxic effect of ATR II on A549 cells, and the results indicated that different concentrations (2, 4, and 8 µg/ml) of ATR II had no significant effect on A549 cell viability (Figure S1B), suggesting that it had no toxic side effects on A549 cells. Then, to explore the effects of ATR II against LPS-stimulated cytotoxicity, we first examined cell proliferation using bright-field microscopy and an EdU assay. Bright-field imaging demonstrated that A549 cells in the control group maintained normal morphology, whereas LPS exposure induced marked apoptotic features and compromised cellular integrity (Fig. 1A). In contrast, ATR II treatment (2, 4, and 8 µg/ml) dose-dependently improved cell viability, as evidenced by enhanced confluence and reduced apoptosis. EdU staining further demonstrated that LPS significantly inhibited cell proliferation as opposed to the control group (Fig. 1B-C). Conversely, treatment with ATR II rescued proliferation in a dose-dependent manner, indicating that ATR II effectively counteracted LPS-treated cytotoxicity in A549 cells.

(A) Cellular morphology of A549 cells were observed by bright-field microscopy. (B) Cells viability was detected by EdU assay. (C) Quantification of EdU-positive cells. ***P < 0.001 vs. control; #, ### P < 0.05, 0.001 vs. LPS group

ATR II suppressed LPS-induced apoptosis in A549 cells dose-dependently

To further evaluate the anti-apoptotic effects of ATR II in ALI, flow cytometry was employed. Relative to the control group, LPS exposure remarkably promoted apoptosis. However, co-treatment with ATR II at concentrations of 2, 4, and 8 µg/ml dose-dependently attenuated LPS-induced apoptosis, with the 8 µg/ml ATR II group exhibiting the most pronounced protective effect (Fig. 2A and B). Consistently, western blot analysis demonstrated that LPS stimulation markedly upregulated Cleaved Caspase-3 expression and the Cleaved Caspase-3/Caspase-3 ratio (Fig. 2C and D). In contrast, ATR II treatment dose-dependently reversed these effects. These results further confirmed the role of ATR II in attenuating LPS-triggered apoptosis.

ATR II suppressed pro-inflammatory cytokine release and oxidative stress injury in LPS-stimulated A549 cells

Oxidative stress and inflammatory responses play pivotal roles in the development of ALI [17]. To illustrate the role of ATR II against LPS-induced oxidative damage, we examined the ROS, SOD, and CAT content in A549 cells. As shown in Fig. 3A and C, LPS stimulation significantly enhanced ROS levels while markedly reducing the activities of SOD and CAT compared with the control group. In contrast, ATR II reversed these effects dose-dependently. Additionally, LPS-stimulated A549 cells exhibited elevated TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 levels, whereas ATR II treatment inhibited the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines dose-dependently (Fig. 3D and F). Our results suggested that ATR II ameliorated alveolar epithelial cell injury by suppressing oxidative stress and inflammation in ALI.

ATR II attenuated LPS-stimulated ALI via TNIP2/NF-κB pathway

Increasing evidence have suggests that the TNIP2/NF-κB pathway exerts significant regulatory effects on inflammation and apoptosis [7]. Next, we assessed the effect of ATR II on the TNIP2, p-p65 and p65 expression in LPS-stimulated A549 cell. As shown in Fig. 4A and C, mRNA levels and protein expression of TNIP2 were reduced in LPS-stimulated A549 cells, while ATR II reversed these results. Besides, Western blot analysis suggested that p-p65 protein expression level and p-p65/p65 ratio was elevated in LPS-stimulated A549 cells. However, ATR II abolished the effects of LPS on the TNIP2/NF-κB signaling pathway dose-dependently (Fig. 4D and E). Our observations suggested that ATR II attenuated LPS-induced ALI through the TNIP2/NF-κB pathway.

Knockdown of TNIP2 reversed the influence of ATR II on proliferation and apoptosis of LPS-induced A549 cells

To study the role of TNIP2 in the ALI model, A549 cells were transfected with shRNA-NC or shRNA-TNIP2, followed by treatment with 1 µg/mL LPS and 8 µg/mL ATR II. We first determined the transfection efficiency of shRNA-TNIP2. Results from RT-qPCR and western blot assay indicated that TNIP2 mRNA levels and protein expression were reduced in shRNA-TNIP2 transfected cells, compared to the shRNA-NC group (Figure S2A-C). Our results confirm the successful transfection of shRNA-TNIP2 into A549 cells.

Further, as illustrated in Fig. 5, compared to the shRNA-NC group, TNIP2 knockdown remarkably inhibited cell proliferation (Fig. 5A and B), increased cell apoptosis (Fig. 5C and D), and promoted the cleaved-caspase3 protein expression level in A549 cells (Fig. 5E and F). These findings demonstrated that ATR II attenuated LPS-induced proliferation and apoptosis in A549 cells via mediating the TNIP2/NF-κB pathway.

Knockdown of TNIP2 reversed the roles of ATR II on inflammation and oxidative stress in LPS-induced A549 cells

In addition, we determined the regulatory roles of TNIP2 on inflammation and oxidative stress in ALI. Compared with the shRNA-NC group, TNIP2 knockdown enhanced ROS levels and inhibited the SOD and CAT activities in the LPS + ATR II treated A549 cells (Fig. 6A). Besides, the secretion of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 in A549 cells in shRNA-TNIP2 transfected cells obviously elevated, relative to the shRNA-NC group (Fig. 6B). Our observations suggested that ATR II alleviated the inflammatory response and oxidative stress in LPS-treated A549 cells through regulating the TNIP2/NF-κB pathway.

Discussion

ALI is a common respiratory disorder characterized by refractory hypoxemia, massive release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, and irreversible alveolar epithelial cell injury [18]. The five-year survival rate of ALI patients remains below 50%, indicating that existing clinical interventions are insufficient and novel therapeutic targets and agents are urgently warranted [19]. Atractylenolide II (ATR II), a bioactive sesquiterpenoid derived from Atractylodes macrocephala, has garnered extensive research interest owing to its anti-inflammatory, anti-tumor, immunoregulatory and organ-protective biological activities [20]. Our study is the first to verify that ATR II relieves LPS-triggered ALI in vitro via suppressing the TNIP2/NF-κB signaling axis. These findings confirm the protective effect of ATR II against ALI progression, and suggest that ATR II may be a promising candidate for the clinical treatment of ALI.

Lipopolysaccharide (LPS), a major constituent of Gram-negative bacterial cell walls, is widely used to establish in vitro ALI models for mechanistic research. For instance, Li et al. suggested that obacunone inhibits ferroptosis during LPS-induced ALI through activation of Nrf2-mediated antioxidant pathways [21]. Shan et al. also demonstrated that dynasore ameliorates LPS-triggered ALI through suppression of NLRP3 inflammasome-dependent pyroptotic pathways [22]. In the present study, A549 cells stimulated with 1 µg/mL LPS were employed as an in vitro ALI model, and the protective effects of ATR II at gradient concentrations (2, 4, and 8 µg/mL) were systematically evaluated.

Oxidative stress imbalance is recognized as a pivotal pathological driver in ALI progression. Excessive oxidative stress reflects an imbalance between oxidants and antioxidants in biological systems. MDA is a classic biomarker for evaluating oxidative damage, while antioxidant enzymes including SOD are responsible for scavenging ROS, and their activities are significantly suppressed under oxidative stress [23]. Zeng et al. confirmed that LPS stimulation leads to severe lung injury in mice, manifested as elevated MDA levels and decreased activities of GSH, SOD, and CAT [24]. Similarly, Tanreqing (TRQ) has also been verified to alleviate oxidative stress by enhancing SOD and GSH activities, while reducing MDA and ROS accumulation [25]. Consistently, our data showed that ATR II treatment markedly reduced ROS overproduction and restored the activities of antioxidant enzymes in LPS-stimulated A549 cells, which is consistent with the reported antioxidant properties of ATR II in other disease models [26].

Compelling evidence has demonstrated that ALI progression is closely associated with excessive inflammatory factor secretion, inflammatory cell infiltration and alveolar epithelial apoptosis [27]. TNF-α, IL-1β and (IL-6 are core pro-inflammatory cytokines in the ALI inflammatory cascade, and their abnormal overexpression induces inflammatory amplification and cell death via apoptosis and pyroptosis [28, 29]. We quantified the levels of IL-1β, IL-6 and TNF-α via ELISA to assess the inflammatory response in LPS-induced ALI. The results showed that LPS stimulation significantly inhibited cell viability, aggravated cell apoptosis and amplified inflammatory responses in A549 cells compared with the control group. Notably, ATR II intervention effectively reversed LPS-induced cytotoxicity, inflammatory injury and apoptosis in A549 cells. This dual regulatory effect on oxidative stress and inflammatory apoptosis distinguishes ATR II as a multi-target protective agent for ALI, which is rarely reported in previous studies on ATR II.

NF-κB activation serves as a crucial regulator in immune responses, cell proliferation, inflammation, differentiation and apoptosis [30]. Previous studies have confirmed that phosphorylation of NF-κB p65 subunit the transcription and secretion of downstream pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β, while pharmacological inhibition of the NF-κB pathway can effectively alleviate ALI [31]. Our results revealed that ATR II significantly downregulated the phosphorylation level of p65 in LPS-treated A549 cells, indicating that the anti-inflammatory effect of ATR II is partially mediated by inactivating the NF-κB signaling pathway.

TNIP2 is a well-characterized negative NF-κB signaling regulator and has been shown to limit inflammatory responses in various pathological conditions [32]. Luo et al. demonstrated that miR-15a-5p promotes inflammatory responses during sepsis through activating the NF-κB pathway through TNIP2 targeting [33]. Qian et al. demonstrated that TNIP2 relieves LPS-induced inflammatory injury in endometritis by suppressing NF-κB activation [34]. However, no prior studies have explored the interaction between ATR II and TNIP2, nor have they linked TNIP2/NF-κB signaling to the protective mechanism of ATR II against ALI. Our results clearly demonstrated that TNIP2 knockdown abolished the protective effects of ATR II against LPS-induced injury in A549 cells.

Notably, TNIP2 is a core component of the evolutionarily conserved TNIP2/ABIN–A20 (TNFAIP3) inhibitory module, which plays a pivotal role in ubiquitin-dependent NF-κB signaling regulation [35]. A20 (TNFAIP3), a ubiquitin-editing enzyme, is the central effector of this module: it suppresses NF-κB activation by deubiquitinating K63-ubiquitinated upstream adaptors (e.g., TRAF6, RIP1) and promoting K48-ubiquitination-mediated proteasomal degradation of these molecules. TNIP2/ABIN proteins act as adaptors to recruit A20 to the NF-κB signaling complex, thereby enhancing A20’s stability and catalytic activity. Given our finding that ATR II upregulates TNIP2 expression, we propose that ATR II may strengthen the functional activity of the TNIP2/ABIN–A20 module by increasing TNIP2 levels—either by promoting TNIP2 transcription or protein stability, or by facilitating the interaction between TNIP2 and A20 to amplify the inhibitory effect on NF-κB.

Our data demonstrate that ATR II reduces ROS levels and attenuates p65 phosphorylation/NF-κB activation, with TNIP2 upregulation implicated as a potential mediating factor. However, LPS/TLR4-driven inflammatory injury involves complex crosstalk between oxidative stress and NF-κB signaling, with multiple upstream sources of ROS contributing to inflammatory amplification. Major ROS-generating systems, including NADPH oxidases (e.g., NOX2) and xanthine oxidase (XO), are well-documented to play critical roles in LPS-associated oxidative stress beyond canonical TLR4/TRAF6 signaling [36, 37]. As such, our current dataset—limited to A549 cells—does not exclude the possibility that ATR II’s protective effects may also involve modulation of these ROS-centric pathways, either independently or in conjunction with TNIP2 regulation. We therefore interpret our findings as establishing an association between TNIP2 upregulation and ATR II’s anti-inflammatory/anti-oxidative effects, rather than definitively identifying TNIP2 as the primary causal driver. Future studies are warranted to resolve this causal ambiguity and expand the generalizability of our findings. First, validation in macrophage/innate immune cell models (e.g., RAW 264.7), where LPS-driven oxidative stress and NF-κB signaling are mechanistically robust [38], would help confirm whether the TNIP2-dependent pathway is conserved across cell types involved in inflammatory responses. Second, pathway-specific assessments—such as evaluating NRF2/HO-1-related responses [39] or directly targeting NOX2/XO activity—would enable discrimination between TNIP2-centric and ROS-centric mechanisms, clarifying their relative contributions to ATR II’s protective effects.

This study also has other limitations that need to be addressed in future research. First, all experiments in this study were performed solely in the A549 cell line in vitro, lacking in vivo animal model verification. Second, the specific upstream regulatory mode of ATR II on TNIP2 has not been illuminated. It is still unclear whether ATR II directly binds to TNIP2 protein to enhance its stability, or regulates TNIP2 transcription. Moreover, we did not directly detect key markers of the TNIP2/ABIN–A20 module, such as A20 protein expression, the ubiquitination levels (K63/K48) of NF-κB upstream molecules, and the interaction efficiency between TNIP2 and A20. Thus, the exact role of ATR II in regulating this module remains to be verified. In addition, this study only used a single A549 alveolar epithelial cell line, without verifying the results in primary alveolar epithelial cells or other lung-resident cells (such as macrophages, endothelial cells), which may limit the universality of the conclusions. Future studies should adopt a variety of cell models and in vivo models to confirm the reliability of the findings.

In summary, this study systematically elucidates the protective effect and molecular mechanism of ATR II against LPS-induced ALI in vitro, proving that ATR II exerts antioxidant, anti-inflammatory and anti-apoptotic effects by targeting the TNIP2/NF-κB signaling axis. These results provide a new theoretical basis for the development of natural small-molecule drugs for ALI treatment.
