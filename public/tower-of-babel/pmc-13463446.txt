Three-year sustained remission with mepolizumab in a rare eosinophilic disease: idiopathic chronic eosinophilic pneumonia
Authors: Hatice Serpil Akten, Sinem Inan, Zuleyha Galata, Su Ozgur, Yusuf Ozeke, Reyhan Gumusburun, Gulhan Demiroglu, Ozlem Goksel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundIdiopathic chronic eosinophilic pneumonia (ICEP) is a rare eosinophilic lung disease with a high relapse rate and substantial oral corticosteroid (OCS) burden. While mepolizumab, an anti-IL-5 monoclonal antibody, has shown promise in case reports and small series, evidence from long-term follow-up studies remains limited.MethodsWe conducted a single-centre, registry-based prospective observational study including a small case series of patients with ICEP and concomitant severe eosinophilic asthma who were treated with mepolizumab (100 mg every 4 weeks) for at least 36 months. Data were extracted from the Ege Severe Asthma Registry ESAR, a dynamic real-world database. ICEP relapses, asthma exacerbations, peripheral eosinophil counts, radiological findings, annual cumulative methylprednisolone dose and OCS use duration were evaluated longitudinally before and after mepolizumab initiation.ResultsDuring the two-year inclusion period, 150 new severe asthma patients were registered in the Ege Severe Asthma Registry ESAR; among them, six patients (n:6/150) were initiated on monthly 100 mg mepolizumab based on coexisting diagnoses of severe asthma and relapsing ICEP. All patients had a history of corticosteroid-responsive disease with frequent relapses. After completing a three-year follow-up under regular mepolizumab treatment, significant reductions were observed in oral corticosteroid (OCS) use duration (p = 0.027), annual cumulative methylprednisolone dose, which decreased from 3600 mg/year [2400–4680] to 1080 mg/year [0–1440] (p = 0.028), peripheral blood eosinophil count (p = 0.028), and the number of ICEP relapses, which declined from a median of 3.5 to 0.0 (p = 0.027).ConclusionTo our knowledge, this study represents one of the longest real-world follow-up reports of patients with the rare disease ICEP receiving Anti-IL-5 mepolizumab at the severe asthma treatment dose. Our results indicate that, over a three-year course, mepolizumab 100 mg monthly provided sustained relapse control and markedly reduced OCS dependency in ICEP, a disease well known for its frequent relapses.

Introduction

Idiopathic chronic eosinophilic pneumonia (ICEP) is a rare disorder of unknown aetiology, characterised by subacute or chronic respiratory and systemic symptoms (including cough, fatigue, fever, night sweats, joint pain, and unintended weight loss), alveolar and/or blood eosinophilia, and peripheral pulmonary infiltrates on chest imaging. Since there are no universally accepted diagnostic criteria, in clinical practice, the diagnosis is typically supported by the presence of respiratory symptoms lasting longer than two weeks, evidence of eosinophilia in the lungs or peripheral blood (such as ≥40% eosinophils in bronchoalveolar lavage or ≥1000/mm3 in circulating blood), characteristic pulmonary infiltrates, often with a peripheral predominance on imaging, and the exclusion of alternative causes of eosinophilic lung disease. Asthma may be diagnosed before, concurrently with, or after ICEP and often presents as severe asthma. ICEP generally responds well to oral corticosteroids (OCS), which may be administered either as long-term tapered therapy lasting three to six months or as short courses of four to six weeks. However, relapses are common, particularly during dose reduction. Additionally, the long-term adverse effects of OCS are well recognised [1,2].

Mepolizumab is a humanized monoclonal antibody (IgG1κ) targeting interleukin-5 (IL-5), a cytokine pivotal in eosinophil maturation, activation, proliferation, and recruitment. By binding to IL-5, mepolizumab inhibits these processes, thereby reducing blood and tissue eosinophil levels. This mechanism suggests its potential efficacy in diseases characterized by excessive eosinophil production and accumulation, such as ICEP. Currently, mepolizumab is approved for treating severe uncontrolled eosinophilic asthma at a dosage of 100 mg subcutaneously every four weeks. It received approval for asthma treatment in the United States and Europe in 2015, and in Turkey in 2019. Additionally, in the United States, mepolizumab is approved for eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA) at a dosage of 300 mg monthly and has demonstrated effectiveness as a corticosteroid-sparing agent in hypereosinophilic syndrome (HES) [3,4].

Despite its high potential, mepolizumab has not yet been approved for ICEP treatment. A recent comprehensive review by Murillo et al. analyzed existing literature on ICEP patients treated with anti-IL-5 therapies [5]. The review identified a limited number of studies, including a retrospective multicenter observational study by Brenard et al. in Belgium involving 10 patients with a nine-month follow-up [6], another retrospective case series by Tashiro et al in Japan with four patients [7], and 34 individual case reports all of which reported that mepolizumab was beneficial in reducing attack frequency, eosinophil counts, and OCS use in ICEP [8–15]. Murillo et al. concluded that while monoclonal antibodies targeting IL-5 are a safe option for controlling relapses in ICEP and may allow for reduction or discontinuation of oral corticosteroids, the optimal dosage and duration of mepolizumab therapy in ICEP remain to be determined.

As highlighted in the literature, there remains a need for large-scale trials with extended follow-up periods to assess the efficacy of anti–IL-5/5R therapies in ICEP. To address this gap, this study was specifically designed as a follow-up study using a dynamic asthma registry. Our objective was to document the impact of mepolizumab on reducing OCS dependency and preventing relapse in ICEP more reliably by reporting one of the largest reported single-centre case series from a large regional reference center for severe asthma, with a minimum of 36 months of treatment-based follow-up.

Materials and methods

Study design

This single-centre, registry-based prospective observational study included a small case series of patients with ICEP and concomitant severe eosinophilic asthma who were treated with mepolizumab, using data from the Severe Asthma Registry of Ege University Hospital, a tertiary referral university hospital in Izmir, Türkiye. The registry prospectively collects standardized demographic, clinical, laboratory, and treatment-related data at regular follow-up visits. Patients with severe eosinophilic asthma who had concomitant ICEP and were initiated on mepolizumab were consecutively enrolled between 2019 and 2021 after providing written informed consent. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ege University Ethics Committee (16-12/48; December 26, 2016).

For the purpose of this study, patients were followed longitudinally for a minimum of 36 months after initiation of mepolizumab. A within-patient analytical approach was used, in which each patient served as their own control, allowing comparison of clinical and laboratory parameters before and after treatment initiation. The study was completed in January 2024, following the last recorded visit of the final enrolled patient. The execution of this database began on 25 May 2016 and remains open-ended (Figure 1). Definition of severe asthma and indication for mepolizumab

Severe asthma was defined as asthma that remains uncontrolled despite optimized treatment with high dose inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) and long-acting beta agonist (LABA), or that requires high dose ICS-LABA to prevent it from becoming uncontrolled. Severe asthma was distinguished from asthma that is difficult to treat due to inadequate or inappropriate treatment, or persistent problems with adherence or comorbidities [16]. Patients with uncontrolled asthma despite high dose ICS-LABA or other controllers, a history of exacerbations in the previous year and higher peripheral blood eosinophil count ≥300 cells/µL (≥150 cells/µL in patients using long-term systemic steroids) are considered to have the possibility of refractory Type 2 inflammation according to current guidelines, and add-on biologic Type 2-targeted treatment with anti-IL-5 mepolizumab 100 mg by subcutaneous (SC) injection every 4 weeks was indicated for those patients in the study [16].

Diagnosis of ICEP and definition of outcomes

The diagnosis of ICEP was established based on a combination of clinical, radiological, and laboratory findings, including: subacute or chronic respiratory symptoms,peripheral blood eosinophilia (>1000 cells/µL) and/or pulmonary eosinophilia,characteristic radiological findings on high-resolution computed tomography (HRCT), andexclusion of alternative causes of eosinophilic lung disease such as infections, drug reactions, autoimmune diseases, and malignancy [1,2].

Radiological findings were systematically classified according to pattern (ground-glass opacities, consolidation, or mixed patterns) and distribution (peripheral or diffuse involvement) [1].

To address the overlap between asthma and ICEP, outcomes were categorized as predefined asthma-related or ICEP-specific.

Asthma-related outcomes included exacerbation frequency, lung function parameters, and corticosteroid use for asthma control [16].

ICEP-specific outcomes included radiological recurrence or progression of pulmonary infiltrates on HRCT, systemic symptoms consistent with ICEP such as fever, fatigue, night sweats, weight loss, or arthralgia, peripheral blood eosinophilia, and relapse events requiring initiation or escalation of systemic corticosteroid therapy.

According to previously published definitions, ICEP relapse was defined as recurrence or worsening of respiratory and/or systemic symptoms compatible with ICEP, accompanied by radiological recurrence, persistence, or progression of pulmonary infiltrates and peripheral blood eosinophilia, requiring initiation or escalation of systemic corticosteroid therapy. Remission was defined as the absence of clinical symptoms, no new or progressive radiological infiltrates, and stable or normalized eosinophil levels during follow-up [1,2]. Asthma exacerbation was defined separately as worsening of asthma symptoms requiring systemic corticosteroid treatment and/or hospitalization, without necessarily being accompanied by new or progressive pulmonary infiltrates.

Follow-up and data collection

Patients were followed at 3-month intervals within the registry. Assessments included clinical evaluation, lung function testing, peripheral blood eosinophil counts, and radiological imaging when clinically indicated.

The primary outcomes of interest were changes in ICEP relapse frequency, blood eosinophil levels, and corticosteroid use before and after initiation of mepolizumab. Secondary outcomes included asthma control and safety.

Annual cumulative OCS exposure was calculated as the total systemic methylprednisolone dose received during the 12 months before mepolizumab initiation and during the first 12 months after mepolizumab initiation. Only systemic corticosteroids were included in this calculation; inhaled, intranasal, or topical corticosteroids were not included. For annualized outcomes, the pre-mepolizumab observation period was defined as the 12 months immediately preceding mepolizumab initiation, and the post-mepolizumab observation period was defined as the first 12 months after mepolizumab initiation.

Asthma-related outcomes were assessed by evaluating the annualized exacerbation rate, need for systemic corticosteroids due to asthma exacerbations, and lung function parameters (FEV1 in mL and % predicted). Asthma exacerbations were defined as worsening of asthma symptoms requiring systemic corticosteroid treatment and/or hospitalization. For each patient, exacerbation frequency during the 12 months before mepolizumab initiation was compared with that observed during follow-up. Lung function measurements were obtained at baseline and at scheduled follow-up visits using standardized spirometry.

During the COVID-19 pandemic, follow-up visits were conducted either in person or via telemedicine, ensuring continuity of care and uninterrupted administration of mepolizumab (Figure 1).

Radiological findings were systematically classified based on pattern (ground-glass opacities, consolidation, or mixed) and distribution (peripheral or diffuse). Baseline HRCT refers to the most recent HRCT performed before mepolizumab initiation, obtained during clinical evaluation for relapsing or persistent ICEP activity. Radiological response was assessed by comparing this baseline HRCT with follow-up HRCT scans obtained when clinically indicated.

Statistics

The analyses of the study were performed using R programming language. Measurements of continuous variables are presented as mean ± SD, median [min-max] values, and categorical variables are presented as frequencies (%). The normality of the distribution of continuous measurements was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For dependent measurements (before-after), the paired t-test was used when the normality assumption was met, and the Wilcoxon test was used when it was not met. Statistical significance was evaluated at a p-value of <0.05.

Results

The median number of past ICEP relapses requiring OCS prescription up to treatment with mepolizumab was 3.5 [min:2 – max:6]. All of them experienced fatigue, fever, joint pain, and similar systemic complaints consistent with ICEP at the time of diagnosis and at the time of inclusion in this study.

ICEP diagnosis was supported by radiological and laboratory findings, with bronchoscopy performed in selected cases. In one case, ICEP was initially misdiagnosed as COVID-19 due to overlapping radiological findings. The median time from asthma diagnosis to mepolizumab initiation was 3.6 years [0.5–14.0], while the median time from ICEP diagnosis to mepolizumab initiation was 2.3 years [0.25–12.0].

Most patients had a history of frequent oral corticosteroid (OCS) use prior to mepolizumab, with a median duration of 8.5 months [3.0–36.0]. One patient was unable to tolerate long-term OCS due to comorbid conditions. Two patients had previously received omalizumab but were switched to mepolizumab due to insufficient disease control or relapse.

At baseline, median peripheral blood eosinophil levels were 2390 cells/µL [1030–14430], corresponding to 30.1% [15.0–60.2]. Lung function parameters were near normal, with a mean FEV1 of 79.5% predicted.

Asthma-related outcomes associated with mepolizumab treatment

Following initiation of mepolizumab, asthma control improved clinically in all patients. No patients required hospitalization for asthma exacerbations during follow-up. The median number of exacerbations decreased markedly from 4 [min: 2– max: 5] at baseline to 0 during follow-up. Lung function parameters remained stable, with a slight, non-significant improvement in FEV1 observed following treatment (FEV1 % predicted: 79.5% vs. 80.8%; FEV1 mL: 2590 vs. 2627 mL; p > 0.05). The need for systemic corticosteroids due to asthma exacerbations markedly decreased after treatment initiation.

ICEP-specific outcomes associated with mepolizumab treatment

Before mepolizumab initiation, all recorded ICEP relapses were characterized by recurrence or worsening of ICEP-compatible respiratory and/or systemic symptoms requiring initiation or escalation of systemic corticosteroids. These episodes were accompanied by peripheral blood eosinophilia in all patients and radiological recurrence or persistence of pulmonary infiltrates compatible with ICEP in all patients. Therefore, these events were considered ICEP relapses rather than isolated asthma exacerbations. In contrast, asthma exacerbations were recorded separately when worsening of asthma symptoms required systemic corticosteroids and/or emergency care without evidence of new or progressive ICEP-related infiltrates. The median follow-up after initiating mepolizumab was 40.0 months [min: 36.0 – max: 47.0] (Table 1). As shown in Figure 3, mepolizumab was associated with a significant reduction in the median number of relapses, from 3.5 [min: 2– max: 6] to 0.0 [min: 0 – max: 0] (p = 0.027), and a significant decrease in the eosinophil ratio, from a median of 30.1% [min: 15.0- max: 60.2] to 0.7% [min: 0.4- max: 4.3] (p = 0.028). Additionally, in all patients, OCS was gradually tapered and then discontinued within a median of 3 months [min: 0.0 – max:6 months] (p = 0.027). The median annual cumulative methylprednisolone dose decreased significantly from 3600 mg/year [2400–4680] in the 12 months before mepolizumab initiation to 1080 mg/year [0–1440] during the first 12 months after mepolizumab initiation (p = 0.028). No adverse events were observed in any of the patients. Discussion

In this single-centre, Ege Severe Asthma Registry ESAR-based prospective observational study we evaluated the long-term outcomes of Anti IL-5 mepolizumab treatment in patients with severe eosinophilic asthma accompanied by ICEP (n:6/150), with a median follow-up duration of 40 months. Given the rarity of ICEP, data on biologic therapies are largely limited to case reports and small retrospective series. In this context, our study contributes longitudinal real-world evidence by systematically assessing clinical, radiological, and laboratory parameters over time within the same patients. All ICEP patients, in addition to having uncontrolled severe asthma, had lung HRCT infiltration areas consistent with relapse and peripheral blood eosinophilia exceeding 1000/mcL at admission. Following initiation of mepolizumab 100 mg monthly, oral corticosteroids were gradually tapered and discontinued within six months in most patients, while sustained remission of ICEP was maintained throughout follow-up.

The absence of a control group and the coexistence of severe asthma represent important limitations of this study, as improvements in corticosteroid use and clinical outcomes may partly reflect better asthma control. A key methodological challenge was distinguishing the effects of mepolizumab on severe asthma versus ICEP, given the overlap in clinical presentation and treatment response. To address this, asthma-related and ICEP-specific outcomes were analyzed separately. While some improvements, particularly in corticosteroid use, may be attributable to improved asthma control, the complete absence of radiological relapse and sustained suppression of eosinophilia strongly support a direct therapeutic effect on ICEP. Nevertheless, complete disentanglement of treatment effects remains challenging and should be considered when interpreting the results.

There is currently no approved dose of mepolizumab for the treatment of ICEP, and it is being used off-label for this condition. Studies investigating the effective dose of mepolizumab in preventing ICEP recurrence are limited in the literature. In case reports, the asthma-approved dose of 100 mg every 4 weeks has been the preferred option for treating ICEP [8–15]. Unusually, one case report from the USA by Kisling et al. reported beneficial outcomes with the use of mepolizumab at the EGPA-indicated dose of 300 mg monthly in a patient with concomitant asthma and ICEP, with no relapse observed over two years [17]. In a retrospective study of 10 patients with ICEP, Brenard et al. found that 100 mg of mepolizumab was administered to six patients and 300 mg to four patients, with no difference in relapse rates between the two groups [6]. In recent years, case reports have also discussed new treatment modalities such as ‘Extended Interval Dosing’ to reduce the monthly dose, which involves extending the dosing interval from the standard 4 weeks to 6–8 weeks in clinically stable patients [18,19]. In our study, we prescribed mepolizumab at a fixed monthly dose of 100 mg/SC, which is the approved dose for severe asthma and lower than the doses typically used for EGPA and HES [20,21]. We were unable to modify the dose or dosing interval of mepolizumab, as we adhered to the existing indications approved for severe hypereosinophilic asthma. Although 100 mg every four weeks appears to be sufficient for ICEP, especially in patients with concomitant severe asthma, further clinical studies are needed to optimize this dosing regimen.

Our findings are consistent with previous reports, including those by Brenard et al. Tashiro et al. and the recent multicenter case series by Delcros et al. all of which demonstrated reductions in relapse rates and corticosteroid dependency in patients with relapsing ICEP treated with anti–IL-5/5R therapies [6,7,22]. In the study by Brenard et al. mepolizumab was administered at 100 mg every 4 weeks in six patients and 300 mg in four patients, with no apparent difference in relapse rates, and was associated with resolution of relapses, decreased OCS use, and improvement in pulmonary infiltrates [6]. Similarly, Tashiro et al. reported reduced corticosteroid requirements and sustained remission in patients receiving anti–IL-5 therapy, including mepolizumab, over an 18-month follow-up period [7]. In line with these findings, Delcros et al. reported in a multicenter cohort of 29 patients that anti–IL-5/5R therapies resulted in complete suppression of ICEP relapses, marked reductions in blood eosinophil counts, and substantial corticosteroid-sparing effects, along with significant radiological improvement during follow-up [22].

Compared with these studies, our analysis provides one of the longest follow-up durations reported to date (median 40 months) and incorporates a structured longitudinal within-patient design. This extended follow-up offers additional insight into the long-term disease course under sustained IL-5 inhibition. Notably, the absence of radiological relapse throughout follow-up in our case series further supports a potential disease-modifying effect of mepolizumab in ICEP.

While the sustained remission observed suggests that prolonged treatment may be necessary in some patients, the optimal duration of therapy remains uncertain. Our data do not allow definitive conclusions regarding the minimum effective duration, and the risk of relapse after treatment discontinuation remains unknown. Given the chronic and relapsing nature of ICEP, further studies with longer follow-up and larger cohorts are needed to better define long-term treatment strategies.

Although ICEP typically responds well and rapidly to OCS, there is a 50% risk of relapse following dose reduction, necessitating long-term steroid therapy and subjecting patients to corticosteroid-related adverse effects. In our study, all ICEP patients experienced relapses (100%), with a median of 3.5 relapses (range: 2–6) prior to the initiation of mepolizumab treatment. Following the introduction of mepolizumab, OCS was tapered and subsequently discontinued in all patients within a median duration of 3 months (range: 0–6 months). In addition, annual cumulative methylprednisolone exposure decreased significantly from 3600 mg/year [2400–4680] to 1080 mg/year [0–1440], supporting the corticosteroid-sparing effect of mepolizumab. The promising outcomes of the study indicate that mepolizumab may provide protection against the severe adverse events associated with prolonged OCS use in ICEP patients. Mepolizumab enabled rapid tapering and discontinuation of OCS in all patients, supporting its role as a steroid-sparing treatment. This may be particularly relevant for patients with comorbidities limiting long-term corticosteroid use.

ICEP is a rare disease with unknown prevalence; however, it is known to occur more frequently in patients with uncontrolled asthma. Even in a relatively large cohort of Ege Severe Asthma Registry ESAR with 150 severe asthma patients, who have been prospectively enrolled and followed with regular follow-ups since 2016, ICEP was present in only 6 cases, with a prevalence of 4%. This limited number of patients may be considered a limitation of the study; however, for a rare disease this number is comparable to only one multi-center study [6]. The strength of the study lies in its focused approach, specifically addressing adult patients diagnosed with ICEP and uncontrolled severe eosinophilic asthma who required management with mepolizumab. Additionally, a key strength of the study is the contribution of the Ege Severe Asthma Registry ESAR, which systematically and prospectively collects detailed clinical and laboratory data from patients. Ege Severe Asthma Registry ESAR is part of the Severe Heterogeneus Asthma Research collaboration by European Respiratory Society (ERS), and the evaluation of real-world mepolizumab use in severe asthma across Europe was published in 2023 with the contribution of this database [23].

The main limitation of this study is the small sample size, which reflects the rarity of ICEP rather than selection bias. Although limited, the case series is consistent with the expected prevalence of ICEP within severe asthma populations. Additional limitations include the lack of intermediate assessments and the inherent difficulty in distinguishing ICEP relapse from other causes of respiratory deterioration. Although annual cumulative methylprednisolone exposure was calculated from patient-level records, the small sample size limits the generalizability of this finding. Despite these limitations, the study benefits from a well-defined, prospectively maintained registry with extended follow-up.

Conclusion

Anti Il-5 Mepolizumab, with its favourable long-term safety profile and fewer side effects compared to systemic corticosteroids, represents a valuable therapeutic option for recurrent ICEP. Over the 3 years of its use, it significantly reduced relapse rates, eosinophil counts, and the need for oral corticosteroids. The 100 mg dose administered every 4 weeks, as used in severe eosinophilic asthma, appeared to be effective in this small case series. Further studies and case series are needed to better evaluate the efficacy of alternative treatment regimens, such as ‘Extended Interval Dosing’ at the 100 mg dose. Mepolizumab may represent a promising steroid-sparing therapeutic option in patients with relapsing ICEP, particularly in those with concomitant severe eosinophilic asthma. However, larger prospective studies are needed to confirm these findings.
