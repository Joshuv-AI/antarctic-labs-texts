A predictive solution of the EPR paradox
Henryk Gzyl

Abstract
In this work an incorrect argument in EPR’s paper is corrected. A predictive approach to further confirm the validity of quantum theory is also proposed. The essence of the detail that EPR missed is that in a state of given total momentum (in their example the total momentum is zero), since the total momentum operator 𝒑 ^ = 𝒑 ^ 1 + 𝒑 ^ 2 \hat{{\boldsymbol{p}}}=\hat{{\boldsymbol{p}}}_{1}+\hat{{\boldsymbol{p}}}_{2} does not commute with any of the position operators 𝒙 ^ 1 \hat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}_{1} and 𝒙 ^ 2 , \hat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}_{2}, then in an eigenstate of the total momentum operator, the standard deviation on the measurement of the position of any of the two particles has to be infinite. Below we compute the (quantum) conditional expectation of the momentum of any of the particles (say, the first) given the value of a measurement of the total momentum. Since the predictor of the momentum of the particle depends on the total momentum, and the total momentum does not commute with the position operator of any of the particles, the uncertainty principle is not violated, and no contradiction appears. We also prove, using the basic tenets of quantum measurement theory, that after measurement of the total momentum, the probability density (computed as the absolute square of the wave function), coincides with the (quantum) conditional density given the total momentum.

: EPR paradox, prediction, conditional probabilities as predictors,

That one can predict without disturbing the system, a priori implies that there is a real system upon which one can make measurements. To measure implies the physical existence of the quantity to be measured. Regarding the other assertion, measuring non-commuting observables imposes restrictions on their respective errors of prediction according to Heisenberg’s uncertainty relationship. But nowhere in the statement of the uncertainty principle does it say that they have to be measured consecutively. As far as measuring goes, one can have a large collection of systems in the same state, and in half of them measure one observable and in the other half measure the other. The measurement errors in the given initial state.will satisfy the Heisenberg uncertainty relationship. The same applies if we think of variances or standard deviations as prediction errors. The comment itemized as (2) in the second quotation, in which they claim that non-commuting observables can not have physical reality, very probably stems from their identification of measurements without error with physical reality.
To sum up, there are at least two different ways to think about expected (average) values and variances (or standard deviations). One is what our experimental colleagues do when reporting the result of an experiment, and the other is what a theoretician does when foretelling what the result of an experiment will be.
An experiment to measure a random quantity consists of a (hopefully) very large number of measurements performed on identical copies of the system. Each measurement results in a given value, and the experimenter usually reports the arithmetical average of these values and the average distance of the values observed to their mean (the standard deviation). The arithmetical average is the number that is closest to all observed values. The standard deviation is the average error of the measurements.
The other meaning of average and standard deviation is the result of an analytical or numerical procedure to tell what the result of a measurement of a random quantity will be, and by how much we may be off in our prediction. Here, when we talk about prediction, expected value and standard deviation, that is the meaning that we attach to those terms. 2
Prediction with prior observation

The core of the EPR argument goes as follows. They assume that the system consists of two, possibly interacting, “particles” that were in some initial joint entangled state, and that after some time the system evolves into a system of two non-interacting particles, of zero total momentum. In the state that they propose, the momentum of any particle can take a definite value (and thus the position can not be predicted),. As the total momentum is given, they assert, correctly, that if the moment of one particle is known, that of the other is also known without any need of measuring it. But, according to their interpretation of the uncertainty principle, since the momentum is not measured, the position can be measured with precision which violates the uncertainty relationship.
This argument is incorrect for several reasons. The simplest one: states of definite momentum are not very physical. Essentially because in the coordinate representation they are not in any Hilbert space, and therefore, they do not lead to probability densities. It is true that they are a computational convenience, and that many computations with them can be formalized invoking distribution theory, but in an argument that relies on Born’s probabilistic interpretation, the mathematical object that is used to describe the physical state, should belong to some Hilbert space. Apart from that, the state that they propose, is also an eigenstate of the total momentum, and as we have already mentioned, this implies uncertainty in the measurements of the positions of any of the two particles.
To explain how the same issue appears when the system is in a proper physical state, let us suppose that we have a system composed of two particles moving in a one-dimensional space, and that the system was prepared in some initial state, which in the momentum, representation is described by a wave function
\Psi(p_{1},p_{2})

In order to follow the lead in EPR we should either use a description of the initial state in which the total momentum is a coordinate. This can be realized in two related ways. Either by a choice of initial coordinates (the complete set of observables) in which the total momentum is one of the coordinates, or we can use the momentum of each particle as member of the complete set of observables and then:

(4.3)

Here we exhibit the predictor of the momentum of the first particle, given the total momentum
\hat{{\boldsymbol{p}}}
is measured, as a function of the total momentum. Again, this result emphasizes the difference between predicted and observed value of the total momentum. Observe that the predicted total momentum in the state
\rho
is
E_{\rho}[\hat{{\boldsymbol{p}}}]=\mu,
whereas a measurement of the total momentum can be any number.
Also, the prediction of
\hat{{\boldsymbol{p}}}-\hat{{\boldsymbol{p}}}_{1}
(the momentum of the second particle) is

E_{\rho}[\hat{{\boldsymbol{p}}}-\hat{{\boldsymbol{p}}}_{1}|\hat{{\boldsymbol{p}}}]=\hat{{\boldsymbol{p}}}-E_{\rho}[\hat{{\boldsymbol{p}}}_{1}|\hat{{\boldsymbol{p}}}]=\frac{1}{2}\big(\hat{{\boldsymbol{p}}}-(\mu_{2}-\mu_{1})\big).

Note that as
[\hat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}_{2},\hat{{\boldsymbol{p}}}]=[\hat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}_{2},\hat{{\boldsymbol{p}}}_{2}]=i\hbar.
then the measurement of
\hat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}_{2}
will indeed show an error bounded below according to Heisenberg’s uncertainty relationship.

5
Final remarks

To recap, if one uses the momentum
\hat{{\boldsymbol{p}}}_{1}
of the first particle and the total momentum
\hat{{\boldsymbol{p}}}
of the two particle system as basic dynamic variables, one has to keep in mind that the measured values of
\hat{{\boldsymbol{p}}}
have a distribution given by (
2.2
) and that predictions about
\hat{{\boldsymbol{p}}}
are subject o an error. Even though that observing that
\hat{{\boldsymbol{p}}}
has value
p
and then that
\hat{{\boldsymbol{p}}}_{1}
has value
p_{1}
implies that
\hat{{\boldsymbol{p}}}-\hat{{\boldsymbol{p}}}_{1}
has value
p-p_{1}
with probability
1,
this does not imply that the position of the second particle can be measured with arbitrary precision for the simple reason that
[\hat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}_{2},\hat{{\boldsymbol{p}}}]=i\hbar.

Another way of emphasizing this point is that the variable that is the canonical conjugate to the total momentum
\hat{{\boldsymbol{p}}}
is the position of the center of mass
\hat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}_{cm}.
If one measures
\hat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}_{1}
in order to determine
\hat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}_{2},
one must measure both
\hat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}_{1}
and
\hat{{\boldsymbol{x}}}_{cm},
and both measurements have variances bounded below according to Heisenberg’s uncertainty relationship.
To sum up: no violation of quantum mechanics occurs in the situation analyzed by EPR.
