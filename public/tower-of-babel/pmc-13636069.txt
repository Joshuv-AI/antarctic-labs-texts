Autocrine signaling may regulate exercise-induced interleukin-6 release from human skeletal muscle
Authors: Jonas Osted Hvidemose, Beckey Trinh, Alexandra Vassilieva, Anne-Sophie Worm Fenger, Peter Nissen Bjerring, Matthijs van Hall, Jens Otto Clemmesen, Sigurður þór Sigurðsson, Kirsten Møller, Gerrit van Hall, Mark A Febbraio, Bente K Pedersen, Peter Plomgaard, Helga Ellingsgaard
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundContracting skeletal muscle releases interleukin-6 (IL-6) which is primarily cleared by the liver. The tissue-specific regulation of IL-6 and soluble IL-6 receptor (sIL-6R) kinetics remains, however, poorly understood. To characterize tissue-specific IL-6 and sIL-6R exchange during exercise, we combined simultaneous arteriovenous measurements across the contracting leg, splanchnic region, and the brain with, or without, pharmacological IL-6 receptor (IL-6R) blockade.MethodsThirty young, well-trained men were randomized in a double-blind, placebo-controlled design to receive either tocilizumab (IL-6R blockade) or placebo before performing moderate-intensity cycling exercise. Net tissue flux of IL-6 and sIL-6R was determined from arteriovenous concentration differences across the femoral, hepatic, and internal jugular circulation.ResultsDuring exercise, leg IL-6 release increased with both placebo and IL-6R blockade but was significantly augmented by IL-6R blockade, suggesting a potential role for autocrine regulation of local IL-6 release. Under control conditions, the splanchnic bed was the predominant site of IL-6 clearance; however, IL-6R blockade shifted splanchnic IL-6 net balance from uptake to release. In addition, the exercising leg was identified as the source of circulating sIL-6R during exercise, whereas no consistent sIL-6R exchange occurred across the splanchnic bed or brain.ConclusionsThese findings suggest a potential role for IL-6 signaling in the autocrine regulation of IL-6 release from the contracting leg during exercise. Furthermore, exercise increases leg-derived sIL-6R, thereby contributing to increased circulating sIL-6R concentrations, while receptor-mediated splanchnic clearance appears to be a major determinant of systemic IL-6 availability during exercise in humans.Clinical trial registration numberClinicaltrials.gov (NCT06334653).Keywords: Interleukin-6, Soluble IL-6 receptor, Tocilizumab, Skeletal muscle, Arteriovenous balance, Autocrine signaling

Introduction

Interleukin-6 (IL-6) is a pleiotropic cytokine classically associated with early-phase inflammation [1], but it is also released from contracting skeletal muscle during exercise [2]. Indeed, skeletal muscle is considered the primary source of the transient increase in circulating IL-6 observed during exercise [3]. Despite decades of research on exercise-induced IL-6 responses, the tissue-specific regulation, release, and clearance of IL-6 during exercise in humans remains poorly understood.

Skeletal muscle IL-6 release increases dose-dependently with exercise intensity [[4], [5], [6]], duration [4,7], and low intramuscular glycogen availability [8,9]. These conditions are also associated with elevated lactate production, reactive oxygen species generation, and metabolic stress - all of which may regulate IL-6 secretion from contracting skeletal muscle [10,11]. Furthermore, exercise increases STAT3 phosphorylation, a downstream target of IL-6 receptor signaling, in contracting skeletal muscle [[12], [13], [14]], suggesting that IL-6 may engage a local autocrine signaling loop during exercise.

While skeletal muscle releases IL-6 during exercise, the liver is believed to be a major site of IL-6 clearance [15]. Hepatocytes express high levels of membrane-bound IL-6 receptor (IL-6R), enabling receptor-mediated uptake and clearance of circulating IL-6 [16]. Despite the relatively modest increase in circulating IL-6 during exercise compared with inflammatory conditions, these concentrations are sufficient to induce downstream hepatic responses, including C-reactive protein (CRP) production [10,17]. In addition, previous studies suggest that the brain releases small amounts of IL-6 during prolonged exercise [18].

IL-6 signaling occurs through both classical signaling via membrane-bound IL-6R, and trans-signaling via soluble IL-6 receptor (sIL-6R) [19]. Circulating sIL-6R concentrations increase during exercise [20,21], although the tissue origin of this response remains unknown. Because sIL-6R is generated by proteolytic cleavage of membrane-bound IL-6R [19], tissues with high IL-6R expression or high IL-6 turnover may contribute to the increase in circulating sIL-6R during exercise.

In the present study, we combined simultaneous arteriovenous measurements across contracting leg, the splanchnic bed, and the brain with pharmacological IL-6R blockade to characterize tissue-specific IL-6 kinetics during exercise in humans. Additionally, we assessed tissue-specific sIL-6R exchange to identify the sources of exercise-induced circulating sIL-6R.

Results

Study design

The study was randomized, placebo-controlled, and double-blinded. Participants were studied on one occasion, where they received either saline 0.9% as placebo (saline) or the IL-6 receptor antibody (IL-6R ab) tocilizumab, which blocks all IL-6-induced signaling [22] and thus IL-6 activity, and performed a continuous moderate-intensity exercise bout. Details regarding procedures and their timing are described in the methods section and in Figure 1.

Overview over procedures and progression of the experimental day. During the run-in phase, catheters were placed, stable isotope infusions initiated, and indirect calorimetry was performed. During the infusion phase, participants received either saline or the IL-6 receptor blocking antibody tocilizumab, followed by a resting period during which indirect calorimetry was performed. Participants then completed 60 min of cycling exercise at 50% of Wmax with indirect calorimetry. During the recovery phase, indirect calorimetry was performed. Blood samples were collected regularly from all four catheters across all study phases.

Discussion

The primary finding of the present study demonstrates that IL-6R blockade elevates leg IL-6 release during exercise. We speculate that this is due to an autocrine IL-6 signaling loop operating directly within contracting skeletal muscle. Furthermore, we provide the first evidence that the contracting leg releases sIL-6R during exercise - an insight that redefines our understanding of systemic sIL-6R regulation.

As expected, the hepatosplanchnic bed acted as a net site of IL-6 clearance under control conditions, whereas IL-6R blockade shifted the hepatosplanchnic net balance toward net IL-6 release. In contrast, no consistent uptake or release of sIL-6R was observed across the hepatosplanchnic bed.

Leg IL-6 release increased during exercise in both conditions but was substantially augmented during IL-6R blockade, consistent with our initial hypothesis. Exercise increases muscle STAT3 phosphorylation [14] and IL-6R blockade is expected to inhibit this canonical downstream signaling pathway of IL-6, thereby disrupting autocrine IL-6 signaling within contracting skeletal muscle. Loss of this negative feedback may augment IL-6 secretion during exercise. The estimated femoral plasma flow was significantly higher during IL-6 blockade; however, this does not undermine the observation, that the leg released higher amounts of IL-6 during the IL-6 blockade. On a speculative note, the increased femoral plasma flow observed with IL-6R blockade could be related to reduced STAT3 activation in endothelial cells, as STAT3 signaling has been implicated in arterial stiffening [23,24]. Reduced IL-6-mediated STAT3 activation could therefore influence vascular function and potentially contribute to the higher femoral plasma flow observed in the present study. However, as IL-6 is only one of several activators of the STAT3 pathway, IL-6R blockade would not be expected to abolish STAT3 signaling.

Most previous studies demonstrating an IL-6 release from the leg during exercise have used prolonged exercise protocols or higher exercise intensities [[4], [5], [6], [7]]. It is therefore notable that pronounced IL-6 kinetics across contracting skeletal muscle were observed during moderate-intensity cycling at 65% of VO2peak, suggesting that autocrine IL-6 signaling may be engaged even during physiologically relevant submaximal exercise. Furthermore, the modest femoral IL-6 uptake observed under control conditions due to an autocrine loop, likely underestimates true skeletal muscle IL-6 release, as autocrine IL-6 signaling retains a portion of locally produced IL-6 within the muscle. Together, these findings support contracting leg as the major contributor to exercise-induced circulating IL-6 and suggest that autocrine IL-6 signaling may participate in regulating its own secretion during exercise.

A second interesting finding was that contracting skeletal muscle or rather the leg appears to be the primary source of the exercise-induced increase in circulating sIL-6R. The mechanisms underlying this response remain unclear, although exercise generates several candidate stimuli for sIL-6R shedding within skeletal muscle, including elevated lactate concentrations and local hypoxia [25,26]. No correlation, however, was observed between lactate concentrations and either sIL-6R or IL-6 release (data not shown). As IL-6R mRNA expression in skeletal muscle has been reported to increase following exercise [27], it is possible that exercise also increases IL-6R shedding, thereby increasing local sIL-6R concentrations. However, the consequences for local IL-6 signaling are unclear. While shedding reduces membrane-bound IL-6R, IL-6 can bind to sIL-6R and subsequently interact with gp130 on muscle cells to induce trans-signaling. Thus, increased IL-6R shedding could potentially decrease, increase, or leave overall IL-6 signaling unchanged. Although IL-6 trans-signaling has been associated with pro-inflammatory and pathological effects in several disease settings, the physiological significance of exercise-induced sIL-6R release may be different. One possibility is that sIL-6R released from the exercising leg primarily modulates IL-6 signaling locally within the exercising tissue rather than promoting systemic trans-signaling. This remains speculative, however, and the present study cannot determine whether the observed sIL-6R release results in increased local or systemic trans-signaling.

The splanchnic bed was the primary site of IL-6 clearance under control conditions, consistent with previous studies and with the high expression of membrane-bound IL-6R on hepatocytes. Notably, arterial IL-6 concentrations increased immediately following IL-6R blockade, even before exercise, strongly supporting receptor-mediated clearance as a major determinant of circulating IL-6 kinetics in humans. Furthermore, IL-6R blockade shifted the splanchnic bed from net uptake to net release, suggesting that impaired hepatic IL-6 handling contributes substantially to systemic IL-6 accumulation when IL-6R is blocked. This shift may not solely reflect abolished receptor-mediated clearance but could also indicate altered IL-6 production within the splanchnic bed secondary to impaired IL-6 signaling.

Blocking receptor-mediated IL-6 uptake over the splanchnic bed revealed a release of IL-6, suggesting either that IL-6R blockade itself induces release or that a constitutive release is ordinarily masked by uptake. As hepatic venous blood represents drainage from the entire splanchnic bed, it is not possible to determine whether the observed IL-6 release during IL-6R blockade originates from the liver itself, visceral adipose tissue, or other splanchnic tissues. Adipocytes and infiltrating immune cells within adipose tissue are established sources of IL-6 [28], although this contribution was likely limited in the present study given the lean and physically active phenotype of the participants. Accordingly, the precise origin of the observed hepatosplanchnic IL-6 release cannot be determined from the current data.

Contrary to our initial hypothesis, no systematic uptake or release of sIL-6R was observed across the hepatosplanchnic region. IL-6 is known to undergo hepatic clearance [15]. In contrast, much less is known about the clearance of sIL-6R, and to our knowledge, there is no evidence supporting substantial hepatic clearance of sIL-6R. Consistent with this, our data do not suggest appreciable uptake of sIL-6R across the hepatosplanchnic bed. One explanation may be that sIL-6R kinetics reflect simultaneous bidirectional uptake and release across the region, which cannot be resolved using net flux measurements alone. A consistent net release of IL-6 across the cerebral region was observed in both conditions. Supporting previous observations that the brain contributes modestly to circulating IL-6 during exercise. There appeared to be a greater jugular IL-6 release during exercise in the saline condition, which was absent during IL-6R blockade, suggesting that intact IL-6 signaling may contribute to cerebral IL-6 release during exercise. However, the magnitude of cerebral IL-6 release was small relative to leg and the hepatosplanchnic region, indicating that the brain contributes minimally to systemic IL-6 accumulation under these conditions. In addition, no clear uptake or release of sIL-6R was observed across the cerebral circulation.

Systemically, arterial IL-6 concentration increased throughout exercise in both conditions, but to a greater extent with IL-6R blockade. However, when the exercise-induced increase was normalized to the pre-exercise baseline within each condition, the saline condition displayed a 54 % greater relative fold increase. This observation emphasizes that exercise itself is a potent stimulus for IL-6 release regardless of IL-6 bioactivity. Even though the relative systemic increases in exercise-induced IL-6 reached a similar magnitude, it must require a higher amount of IL-6 during IL-6 blockage to reach similar relative levels. Arterial sIL-6R concentrations also increased during exercise, potentially increasing the buffering capacity of IL-6 despite sIL-6R already existing in substantial molar excess relative to IL-6. The physiological implications of this exercise-induced increase in sIL-6R remain unclear.

Importantly, relative exercise intensity, assessed by % VO2peak, heart rate, and perceived exertion, was similar between conditions throughout exercise (Figure 2A–B, D-E), indicating that the observed differences in IL-6 and sIL-6R kinetics cannot be ascribed to differences in exercise intensity. Rather, the augmented IL-6 response appears to be a direct consequence of IL-6R blockade. Studies in mice, using both whole-body IL-6 knockout mice [29] and muscle-specific IL-6 knockout mice [30], have shown that the absence of IL-6 is associated with reduced exercise capacity. We did not observe an effect of IL-6R blockade on exercise performance in the present study. However, the study was designed to ensure comparable exercise stimulus between groups to assess IL-6 and sIL-6R kinetics, rather than to determine the role of IL-6 signaling in exercise performance. Whether this translates to humans, particularly during longer or more demanding exercise bouts, remains to be determined.

Conclusion

This study fundamentally advances our understanding of the tissue-specific IL-6 and sIL-6R kinetics during exercise in humans. We show that the contracting leg drives exercise-induced IL-6 release, which is markedly augmented by IL-6R blockade – suggesting an autocrine signaling loop within skeletal muscle.

In addition, the contracting leg also emerges as the major source of circulating sIL-6R. While the hepatosplanchnic bed drives IL-6 clearance under control conditions, IL-6R blockade reverses this into a net IL-6 release, highlighting the pivotal role of receptor-mediated clearance in regulating systemic IL-6 kinetics. Finally, the cerebral circulation exhibits a consistent but modest IL-6 release, indicating a limited contribution to systemic IL-6 accumulation during exercise. All together, these findings uncover a highly coordinated tissue-specific network that regulates IL-6 during exercise and metabolic stress in humans.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. First, arteriovenous balance measurements reflect net leg exchange and therefore cannot distinguish changes in release from changes in uptake of IL-6 or sIL-6R. Distinguishing these processes would require tracer-based kinetic measurements of IL-6 and sIL-6R.

Second, arteriovenous measurements across the femoral region do not allow us to determine the specific tissue of origin of IL-6 or sIL-6R. Thus, while our data demonstrate net release from the exercising leg, they cannot establish that IL-6 or sIL-6R are specifically derived from skeletal muscle.

Third, femoral blood flow was estimated rather than directly measured. We acknowledge the uncertainty associated with this approach; however, the difference in estimated blood flow between treatments cannot explain the observed difference in net IL-6 release.

Finally, measurements across the hepatosplanchnic bed were available from only 8 participants in the saline group and 10 participants in the IL-6R blockade group, compared with 15 participants per group for the other measurements. This smaller sample size increases the uncertainty of these estimates and should be considered when interpreting the hepatosplanchnic findings. Nevertheless, the observed effects were robust despite the reduced sample size.

Study design and participants

This randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study included 30 young, well-trained male participants. Participants were enrolled between March and June 2024, and all study procedures were conducted at the Centre for Physical Activity Research (CFAS) and the Neurointensive Care Unit at Rigshospitalet, Copenhagen, Denmark.

Eligibility criteria included age 18–40 years, normal body mass index (BMI) (18–25 kg/m2), and cardiorespiratory fitness >45 mL O2/min/kg. Participants were randomized in a 1:1 ratio to receive either tocilizumab (8 mg/kg bodyweight) (RoActemra, Roche Pharma), an IL-6 receptor blocking antibody, or placebo (0.9% saline). All participants provided written informed consent prior to participation. The study was approved by The Research Ethics Committee of the Capital Region of Denmark (H-23069670) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was registered on Clinicaltrials.gov (NCT06334653).

Screening and experimental day

All participants underwent a screening visit prior to the experimental day. Participants arrived after an overnight fast of at least 10 h and having refrained from strenuous exercise for 48 h. A medical doctor conducted a detailed medical history, physical examination, and electrocardiogram (ECG), blood pressure measurement, and blood sampling. Upon confirmation of eligibility, anthropometric measurements including height, body weight, waist circumference and hip circumference were obtained. Body composition was assessed by dual energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA), and cardiorespiratory fitness (VO2peak) was determined by indirect calorimetry using breath-by-breath gas exchange technology (Quark CPET, COSMED, Rome, Italy) during an incremental cycling test performed in a reclined position. The test consisted of a 5-min warm-up at 100 W followed by stepwise increases of 25 W every minute until volitional exhaustion.

At least one week later, participants returned for the experimental day following a 10-h overnight fast. An overview of the experimental day is shown in Figure 1. Participants reported at the laboratory at 07:00, changed into hospital clothing, and rested in the supine position. A peripheral venous catheter (Vasofix Certo, 20G x 25 mm, B. Braun, Melsungen, Germany) was inserted into the antecubital vein. Following collection of a baseline blood sample, the catheter was used for primed continuous infusion of stable isotope-labelled glucose, glycerol, palmitate, and selected amino acids, commencing at t = 0 min.

Venous catheters (Arrow CVC set, 16G x 12 cm, Teleflex, USA) were inserted retrogradely into the left common femoral vein and the right internal jugular vein under ultrasound guidance and local anesthesia. A hepatic venous catheter (MPA 1, 6 F, Cordis, USA) was inserted retrogradely via the right common femoral vein under fluoroscopic guidance, and correct positioning was confirmed by contrast-enhanced imaging. An arterial catheter (Arterial Cannula FloSwitch, 20G x 45 mm, BD, USA) was placed in the left radial artery under ultrasound guidance and local anesthesia for arterial blood sampling.

After a 2-h tracer equilibrium period, isotopic steady state was achieved and baseline measurements were performed, including blood sampling, hepatic blood flow measurements, indirect calorimetry, and breath sampling. Hepatic blood flow was assessed using indocyanine green (ICG) (Verdye, Diagnostic Green, Germany). Briefly, 75 mg ICG was dissolved in 500 mL isotonic saline containing 2% human albumin (CSL, Behring, Marburg, Germany) and infused at 2 mL/min through the antecubital venous catheter. Following a 40 min equilibrium period, four paired arterial and hepatic venous blood samples were collected at 5-min intervals. Hematocrit was measured and plasma isolated for subsequent ICG concentration analysis. Indirect calorimetry was performed for 30 min, and breath samples were collected following each measurement for the determination of isotopically labelled palmitate enrichment.

Subsequently, participants received either the IL-6 receptor blocking antibody tocilizumab or placebo (0.9% saline) via the antecubital venous catheter from t = 180–210 min, followed by a 1-hour resting period. A second measurement period, identical to baseline was then conducted to assess resting conditions after intervention.

Participants subsequently performed 1 h of cycling exercise from t = 270–330 min in the same reclined position used during screening. Exercise intensity was set at 50% of maximal power output achieved during the incremental test, corresponding to approximately 66% of VO2peak. Blood sampling, hepatic flow measurements, and indirect calorimetry were repeated during exercise. Perceived exertion was assessed every 15 min using both the Borg 6–20 and RPE 1–10 scales to capture different dimension of exertion, including overall fatigue and breathlessness.

Following exercise, participants rested for 1 h during which blood sampling, hepatic blood flow measurements, and indirect calorimetry were repeated. Heart rate and blood pressure were continuously monitored throughout the study day (Garmin HRM-Dual, Garmin, USA; CARESCAPE, GE HealthCare, USA).

In 14 participants, transient or persistent difficulties in drawing blood from the hepatic venous catheter occurred (8 placebo, 6 IL-6R ab). In 12 participants, the issues persisted throughout both exercise and recovery measurements (7 placebo, 5 IL-6R ab). In five participants, transient or persistent difficulties drawing blood from the femoral venous catheter occurred (2 placebo, 3 IL-6R ab), of whom three had persistent issues during both exercise and recovery measurements (1 placebo, 2 IL-6R ab). Blood samples from the radial artery and jugular venous catheter were successfully obtained in all 30 participants.

Sample analyses

Blood samples for isotope tracers, hormone, and cytokine analyses were collected into ice-cold EDTA tubes and immediately centrifuged (1.9×g, 10 min, 4 °C) to isolate plasma. Plasma samples were stored on dry ice throughout the experimental day and transferred to −80 °C for long-term storage at the end of the study day. Plasma IL-6 and sIL-6R concentrations were quantified using sensitive electrochemiluminescence assays (S- and R-PLEX, Meso Scale Diagnostics, USA) with intraassay CVs of 6.2% and 3.8% respectively. Samples for ICG were analyzed in 96 well plates and absorbance measured using a (Thermo Scientific Varioskan LUX Multimode Microplate Reader, Waltham, MA, USA) at 805 nm and 900 nm [31] with a standard curve prepared in human plasma in duplicates CV < 1%. Samples were analyzed within 3 months as ICG is not stable for longer at −80 °C.

Interference in immunoassays

A quality control experiment was performed in which increasing concentrations of tocilizumab were added to human plasma before sIL-6R quantification. A dose-dependent reduction in measured sIL-6R was observed, indicating assay interference between tocilizumab and the detection antibody used in the sIL-6R assay. Consequently, only sIL-6R measurements obtained from the placebo group were included in the subsequent analyses. No interference was observed between tocilizumab and the S-PLEX IL-6 assay.

Plasma flow and calculations

Hepatic plasma flow was calculated as previously described [32]. Femoral blood flow was estimated using a modified Fick's principle, as direct measurements of femoral blood flow were not feasible. At rest, femoral VO2 was assumed to account for 10% of whole-body oxygen consumption, whereas during exercise, 95% of the exercise-induced increase in oxygen uptake was attributed to the femoral region, in accordance with Poole et al. [33]. Cerebral blood flow could not be measured directly and as several studies find increases, decreases, or no change in cerebral blood flow during exercise [[34], [35], [36]], a fixed reference value of 0.8 L min−1 was used in the calculations [34], to avoid overestimating the cerebral contribution under conditions of potentially altered flow. Statistical analyses

Data were analyzed in RStudio (R version 4.4.2, RStudio version 2024.04.2), using the “lme4” and “emmeans” packages. Descriptive data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Baseline characteristics were compared between groups using unpaired t-test.

To account for the shared pre-randomization baseline, a constrained approach was applied in which the treatment variable was coded as 1 for the IL-6 receptor antibody condition after infusion and 0 otherwise. Linear mixed-effects models were fitted with Time, Treatment, and the Timex Treatment interactions included as categorical fixed effects and participant ID as a random effect, allowing for missing data under a missing-at-random assumption. Model assumptions of residual normality and homogeneity of variance were evaluated via residual plots. Omnibus F-tests for the fixed effects were obtained using Type III analysis of variance. Between-group differences at individual timepoints were evaluated using pairwise contrasts between the Saline and IL-6 receptor antibody conditions, stratified by timepoint, with p-values adjusted using Dunnett's correction.

To evaluate within-group changes over time relative to baseline, separate linear mixed-effects models were fitted for the Saline and IL-6R ab groups, respectively, each including Time as the sole categorical fixed effect and participant ID as a random effect. Post-hoc contrasts against baseline timepoint (t = 180 min) were performed using Dunnett's correction for multiple comparisons.

Statistical significance was accepted at p < 0.05. Figures display observed group means ± standard error of the mean (SEM). The number of participants (n) included in each analysis is indicated in the corresponding figure legends and tables.
