Endovascular repair of a complicated acute type B aortic dissection in a pediatric patient with visceral and lower-extremity malperfusion
Authors: Tara Zielke, Peter Hellwarth, Andres Fajardo, Joel S Corvera, Raghu L Motaganahalli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA previously healthy 14-year-old man presented with acute chest pain, abdominal pain, and right lower-extremity acute limb ischemia with sensorimotor deficit. Computed tomography angiography demonstrated a complicated type B aortic dissection with right common and internal iliac occlusion and visceral malperfusion. Emergent endovascular repair using thoracic stent grafts, a bare-metal dissection stent with septum rupture, and bilateral iliac stenting restored perfusion. The patient remained ambulatory with normal ankle-brachial indices at 12-month follow-up. This case illustrates an endovascular repair with adjunctive distal true lumen stabilization for a rare pediatric complicated acute type B aortic dissection.Keywords: Pediatric aortic dissection, Thoracic endovascular aortic repair, Type B aortic dissection, Acute limb ischemia, Dissection stenting

Acute aortic dissection in pediatric patients is rare and most commonly associated with trauma or heritable aortopathy; spontaneous dissection without an identifiable precipitating factor is exceedingly uncommon. Fewer than 1% of aortic dissections occur in patients younger than 21 years, with the majority in adolescent males.1,2 Delayed recognition is common, underscoring the importance of early cross-sectional imaging when aortic dissection is suspected.3

Thoracic endovascular aortic repair has become the preferred treatment modality for complicated Stanford type B aortic dissection in adults.4,5 However, in pediatric patients, experience with endovascular therapy remains limited, and concerns persist regarding device sizing, long-term durability, and the effects of continued somatic growth.6 Consequently, management must be individualized and performed in centers with multidisciplinary expertise. Informed consent for publication was obtained from the patient’s parent/guardian. This case report was granted exempt status by the Institutional Review Board.

Case presentation

A 14-year-old man with no past medical or surgical history, no family history of aortopathy, and no history of trauma presented with sudden-onset sharp midsternal chest pain while at rest. He weighed 61 kg and was 165 cm or 5ʹ5ʹʹ feet tall at initial presentation. Associated symptoms included abdominal pain and nausea. Approximately 30 minutes after initial symptom onset, the patient developed right lower-extremity numbness, weakness, and diffuse pain. Bedside examination demonstrated decreased sensation, motor weakness, and absent pedal signals in the right lower extremity, consistent with Rutherford IIB acute limb ischemia.

The patient was transferred emergently by air to a tertiary care center. On arrival, he was tachycardic with a systolic blood pressure in the 120-129 mmHg on a nicardipine infusion and had persistent abdominal pain and right lower-extremity sensorimotor deficit. Computed tomographic angiography (CTA) of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis with runoff demonstrated a type B aortic dissection with multiple fenestrations and complete right common and internal iliac artery occlusion. The celiac, superior mesenteric, inferior mesenteric, and left renal arteries were perfused via the false lumen; the right renal artery arose from the true lumen. Wedge-shaped perfusion defects in the spleen and left kidney were consistent with embolic sequelae (Fig 1). The aorta measured 2.35 cm at the midthoracic aorta, the left common femoral artery measured 7.47 mm, occluded right common iliac and internal iliac, and with reconstitution of the right common femoral artery with diminutive caliber. The radial artery was palpable. The patient had left vertebral dominance. A preoperative lumbar drain was not performed given the emergent need to restore right lower-extremity perfusion.

Preoperative computed tomographic angiography (CTA) chest/abdomen/pelvis demonstrates a complicated type B aortic dissection extending from distal to the left subclavian artery to the right external iliac artery with complete right common and internal iliac artery occlusion and perfusion defects in the spleen and left kidney consistent with infarction.

Informed consent was obtained from the patient’s mother. The acute nature of multilevel malperfusion, with Rutherford IIB limb ischemia and concern for visceral malperfusion, made expedient endovascular intervention the preferred approach over open surgery for this patient. A thorough risks and benefits discussion with the patient’s mother was performed and all questions answered.

Emergent endovascular repair was performed via bilateral common femoral artery access under systemic heparinization. ProGlide sutures were placed [Perclose ProGlide Suture-Mediated Closure System (Abbott Vascular)]. The left common femoral artery access had two ProGlide sutures for the large sheath, and a single ProGlide suture was placed for the right common femoral artery access. An 8F sheath in the left common femoral artery permitted advancement of an intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) catheter. True lumen identification was achieved only to the level of the diaphragm due to multiple fenestrations. To maintain wire access in the true lumen, right radial to left femoral through-wire access was established. The right arm was prepped, and the right radial arterial line upsized to a 5F long sheath. The radial arterial line was placed earlier using standard Seldinger technique.

A Glide Advantage wire (Terumo Interventional Systems) from the radial approach was captured in the descending thoracic aorta false lumen through the left femoral artery access using a snare and exchanged for a stiff exchange-length wire (Jag wire; Cook Medical) via a long support catheter (CXI; Cook Medical). In this case, true lumen identification via IVUS was achievable only to the level of the diaphragm because of the presence of multiple fenestrations and circumferential intimal delamination throughout the thoracic aorta. A through-and-through right radial to left femoral wire was therefore established to provide a stable rail for device delivery; this wire traversed the true lumen distally, crossed into the false lumen in the thoracic segment, and re-entered the true lumen proximally. The rationale for accepting this wire course was that: (1) the primary target (proximal true lumen seal) was achievable from the proximal entry point; (2) the Lunderquist wire (Cook Medical) provided sufficient column strength for endograft delivery; and (3) attempting to maintain an exclusively intraluminal true lumen wire course was not feasible given the complex fenestrated anatomy.

The left femoral access was then upsized to a 20F sheath (Gore DrySeal; W. L. Gore & Associates), and the right radial access to a 5F long sheath. Over a stiff exchange wire (Lunderquist; Cook Medical), a 21 mm × 10 cm thoracic endograft (Gore TAG; W. L. Gore & Associates) was deployed in zone 2. Twenty-one millimeter endograft was sized to the current true lumen diameter rather than oversized. Aortography confirmed an adequate landing zone, patency of the left common carotid artery, and delayed filling of the left subclavian artery. The left subclavian artery filled via antegrade flow. Two additional 21 mm × 10 cm conformable thoracic endografts (Gore Conformable TAG; W. L. Gore & Associates) were deployed with 3 to 4 cm of overlap and extended coverage to within 2 cm above the celiac artery. Three 10 cm grafts were used for several reasons: (1) modularity allows precise positioning and overlap adjustments given the dynamic anatomy; (2) no single longer device was available in the 21 mm diameter required; and (3) incremental deployment allowed reassessment of anatomy between grafts.

Circumferential dissection of the intima was identified in the distal 4 cm of the thoracic endograft, extending throughout the visceral and infrarenal abdominal aorta. To address this finding and ensure visceral perfusion, a 36 × 180-mm bare-metal dissection stent (Cook Zenith Dissection Endovascular System; Cook Medical) was deployed to extend coverage to within 1 cm of the aortic bifurcation, overlapping the thoracic endograft by 6 cm. IVUS demonstrated incomplete expansion of the Cook bare-metal stent, with marked compression in at least two discrete segments.

A 14 mm balloon was first advanced over the Lunderquist wire but produced no significant effect on stent expansion. A Coda balloon (Cook Medical; 40 mm) was then advanced over the same wire and used to gently dilate the bare-metal stent and disrupt the dissection septum, consistent with the Stent-Assisted Balloon-Induced Intimal Disruption and Relamination in Aortic Dissection (STABILISE) technique. The septum was intentionally ruptured under controlled balloon inflation, extending from 4 cm within the covered portion of the thoracic endograft to the aortic bifurcation, to obliterate the false lumen and promote aortic relamination. The dissection stent was now well expanded (Fig 2). Side branch vessels (such as celiac, superior mesenteric, and renal arteries) were not wire-protected before septal disruption. For the STABILISE technique, septotomy was performed under IVUS guidance with continuous confirmation of true lumen position. IVUS catheter showing excellent expansion of the Cook dissection stent and no evidence of a false lumen outside of it in the visceral and infrarenal abdominal aorta. Aortogram showed patent visceral and renal arteries.

Intraoperative fluoroscopic image demonstrating bare-metal dissection stent expansion following septum rupture with preservation of visceral and renal artery perfusion.

The right common and internal iliac arteries were occluded on presentation with Rutherford IIB acute limb ischemia. Kissing stents (VIABAHN VBX 8 × 59-mm; W. L. Gore & Associates) were used in the common iliac arteries to reconstruct the aortic bifurcation and ensure reliable, durable flow restoration in the setting of dissection. There was a distal flap extending into the right external iliac artery which was addressed with a self-expandable 8 × 100 bare-metal stent (Protege 8 × 100-mm; Medtronic).

All access sheaths were removed, and hemostasis was achieved with the percutaneous closure devices bilaterally. Owing to significant ischemia of the right lower extremity, a four-compartment right lower-extremity fasciotomy was performed which identified mild compartment swelling and minimal muscle contractility on electrocautery stimulation. There were no common femoral or radial artery access complications with good hemostasis and patency after sheath removal with the utilization of ProGlide sutures [Perclose ProGlide Suture-Mediated Closure System (Abbott Vascular)]. Completion IVUS and postoperative CTA confirmed true lumen remodeling and restoration of bilateral lower-extremity perfusion.

Postoperatively, the patient received one unit of packed red blood cells for a hemoglobin of 6.6 g/dL on postoperative day (POD) 4. Dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin, 81 mg; clopidogrel, 75 mg daily) was initiated on POD 5. Fasciotomy closure was performed on POD 12, and the patient was discharged home on POD 14 on dual antiplatelet therapy, atenolol, and losartan. Dual antiplatelet therapy (aspirin, 81 mg; clopidogrel, 75 mg) was initiated given the placement of multiple bare metal and covered stents at the risk of early thrombosis, particularly given the low flow states that can occur with dissection. Furthermore, we did not have any genetic testing, and there was concern for potential hypercoagulability. The care of this patient required a collaborative multidisciplinary team from vascular surgery, cardiothoracic surgery, plastic surgery, pediatric cardiology, genetics, hematology, critical care, child life, behavioral health, and physical and occupational therapy during this patient’s hospital and recovery. Genetic panel testing did not identify causative genetic aortopathy. However, regions of absence of heterozygosity were noted at loci encompassing collagen type III alpha 1 chain, myosin heavy chain 11 myosin heavy chain 11, and transforming growth factor beta 2. Genetic counseling was provided, and the family was enrolled in a no-cost CTA screening program.

At the 12-month follow-up, the patient was ambulatory without assistance, had normal bilateral ankle-brachial indices, and well-controlled blood pressure. CTA demonstrated persistent false lumen flow consistent with a type II endoleak from an intercostal adjacent to the distal thoracic endograft (Fig 3). If false lumen expansion is identified, consideration will be given to induce false lumen thrombosis with a modified stent graft and Amplatzer vascular plug (Abbott Vascular). This endovascular repair was performed as urgent, lifesaving treatment in the context of acute multilevel malperfusion, but that is not a definitive solution for a 14-year-old with decades of aortic disease ahead. Open surgical repair (eg, thoracoabdominal replacement) remains the more durable long-term option and has been discussed as a potential necessary future intervention.

Postoperative 12-month computed tomography angiography (CTA) chest/abdomen/pelvis imaging demonstrates patent aortoiliac stenting, no endoleak in the thoracic component, and a type II endoleak from an intercostal filling with increased size of the false lumen at the distal thoracic endograft.

Discussion

Fewer than 1% of aortic dissections occur in patients younger than 21 years, with trauma and heritable aortopathy as the most common precipitants. Spontaneous dissection is especially uncommon.1 Management must be individualized based on anatomic extent, malperfusion, suspected genetic aortopathy, and anticipated somatic growth.7 Thoracic endovascular aortic repair is the preferred approach for complicated type B dissection in adults. However, no prospective pediatric-specific guidelines exist, and existing guidance recommends individualized care with the recognition that endovascular repair may serve as a bridge to definitive surgical repair. Endografts in this case were deliberately sized to the current true lumen diameter rather than oversized, accepting the need for lifelong surveillance and potential reintervention as the aorta enlarges.8 The STABILISE technique of controlled balloon rupture of the dissection septum while preserving branch vessel perfusion was essential for achieving adequate dissection stent expansion throughout the visceral aorta. Persistent false lumen flow near the distal endograft represents a potential nidus for late-aneurysmal degeneration and reinforces the importance of ongoing surveillance imaging.

Conclusions

Emergent multicomponent endovascular repair of complicated acute type B aortic dissection with multilevel malperfusion was feasible in this adolescent and resulted in restoration of perfusion and functional recovery. This case highlights the importance of individualized device selection, careful procedural planning, and coordinated multidisciplinary care in pediatric patients with this rare but life-threatening condition. Lifelong surveillance imaging is essential.
