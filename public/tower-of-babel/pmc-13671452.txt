Process evaluation of a pharmacogenomic informed antidepressant prescribing intervention delivered in primary care: findings from the PRESIDE (PhaRmacogEnomicS in Depression) trial
Authors: Georgia Ramsay, Madeline Luke, Philip Ly, Rachel Brooks, Matthew Thoenig, Elise Dettmann, Jennifer Bibb, Floriana La Rocca, Patty Chondros, Cathrine Mihalopoulos, Mary Lou Chatterton, Melanie Galea, Adrian Laughlin, Jane Gunn, Timothy Chen, Thomas M Polasek, Victoria J Palmer, Chad Bousman, Sibel Saya, Jon Emery
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundMajor depressive disorder (MDD) impacts 300 million people globally, with most people being managed in primary care. Clinical response to antidepressant medications is highly variable. Pharmacogenomics (PGx) may improve prescribers’ ability to match an antidepressant medication with patient phenotype, increasing the likelihood of a response.ObjectivesThis process evaluation aims to understand factors that impacted PGx intervention implementation in the PRESIDE Trial, conducted in general practices in Victoria, Australia.MethodsAntidepressant prescribing data, evidence of intervention use and qualitative data from interviews with participants were included. Analysis and interpretation of the data were informed by the (UK) Medical Research Council’s Framework for Developing and Evaluating Complex Interventions.ResultsWhile clinicians and patients supported PGx informed antidepressant prescribing, documented uptake of the intervention was low, as were antidepressant medication initiations or changes in line with the PGx report. Participants reported a lack of clarity regarding follow-up processes post PGx report return. The impact of COVID-19 during trial implementation included delays to intervention delivery. The provision of mental health care in primary care settings was also impacted.ConclusionsFuture applications of PGx informed antidepressant prescribing would benefit from refined patient selection and ensuring PGx information is available when most useful.KEYWORDS: Pharmacogenomics, depression, primary care, antidepressants, Australia, process evaluation

Background

Major depressive disorder (MDD) impacts over 300 million individuals globally [1]. MDD is associated with significant disease burden in terms of lost productivity and costs to health systems. In Australia, the prevalence of MDD is significant, with 16% of the adult population reported to experience a depressive episode in their lifetime [2]. Additionally, the COVID-19 pandemic acted as a significant environmental trigger for mental health decline in Australia, with lockdown periods associated with a measurable worsening of mental health [3]. Most individuals experiencing mental health concerns access care in primary care settings, with diagnosis and treatment and care coordination being managed predominantly by their general practitioner (GP) [2].

Australian guidelines for the management of MDD include the use of antidepressant medication and psychological therapy for those experiencing moderate to severe depressive symptoms [4]. Treatment response to antidepressant medications is variable, with approximately half of patients who take an antidepressant medication not experiencing a clinically meaningful response. Given the role of genetic factors in antidepressant medication response [5], pharmacogenomic (PGx) approaches to match an individual’s genotype to a specific medication and dose have been proposed to improve treatment response [6,7]. Recent meta-analyses have shown the use of PGx to inform antidepressant medication is effective, though these trials almost exclusively have recruited participants from inpatient and/or outpatient specialist psychiatric settings [8–11]. Translation of PGx approaches into a primary care context, where the majority of antidepressant prescribing takes place, had not been rigorously evaluated prior to the PRESIDE trial. The PRESIDE trial is the first Australian or international trial conducted solely in primary care to test the effect of a PGx-informed prescribing report on depressive symptom improvement in patients with moderate to severe symptoms.

In this process evaluation, we explored factors that impacted the implementation of PGx-informed antidepressant prescribing from ‘ideal’ to ‘real-world’ conditions and the perspectives of GP and patient-participants on the use of PGx approaches to antidepressant prescribing in primary care. Together, the answers to these questions contextualize the findings of the PRESIDE trial [12] and provide recommendations for future studies and the implementation of PGx-informed antidepressant prescribing in primary care.

Methods

We used a mixed methods approach to inform this process evaluation. We specifically used both quantitative data on healthcare utilization, medication prescribing and usage alongside qualitative data from interviews with participating patients and GPs. The PRESIDE trial evaluated the effectiveness of a PGx-informed antidepressant prescribing report on self-reported depressive symptoms at 12 weeks among primary care patients aged 18–65 years with moderate to severe depressive symptoms. The published protocol for this randomized controlled trial (RCT) has been described previously and prospectively registered (ANZCTRN12621000181808) [13]. The study was approved by the University of Melbourne Human Research Ethics Committee (ID 20626) and all participants provided informed consent. In brief, participants allocated to the experimental intervention arm received antidepressant medication and dose recommendations based on the individual’s CYP2C19 and/or CYP2D6 genotype-predicted metaboliser phenotypes. Participants in the control arm received antidepressant medication recommendations based on the Australian Therapeutic Guidelines (TGA) [14]. The format of prescribing reports was identical for experimental and control arms to ensure participant blinding to arm. Importantly, the target population reflected a real-world context, as eligibility was not restricted based on prior antidepressant use (i.e., participants could be antidepressant-naïve, current users, or previous users). Potential participants who were taking antipsychotic medications, were pregnant, reported suicidal thoughts “nearly every day” on the PHQ-9, had a diagnosis of dementia, or active COVID-19 were excluded. The study hypothesized that participants in the intervention arm would have a greater change in PHQ-9 measured symptom severity from baseline to 12 weeks compared to the control arm. The primary and secondary outcomes of the PRESIDE trial, published elsewhere [12], found that the experimental intervention did not lead to a significant improvement in depressive symptoms. While participants in both the control and experimental arms reported clinically meaningful mean reductions in depressive symptoms at 12 weeks from baseline (primary outcome), those in the control arm experienced a small but statistically greater improvement (mean change: intervention −3.45, control −4.63; difference = 0.90, 95% CI 0.06–1.75; p = 0.036).

This complex intervention was developed using the Medical Research Council Framework for Developing and Evaluating of Complex interventions [15] (hereafter the MRC framework), as it involved multiple interacting components. At the beginning of their trial participation, GPs received education from the research team on PGx and its use to direct antidepressant prescribing. This was an effectiveness trial, in that GPs had the flexibility to use the PGx information alongside their usual clinical decision-making when managing depressive symptoms in their patients during the trial. This process evaluation was guided by the UK MRC framework to address key considerations, including the mechanisms of intervention impact, contextual factors impacting the trial, implementation factors impacting the fidelity of intervention delivery, and how these interacted to impact the outcome of the trial (see Figure 1) [15].

PRESIDE process evaluation components.

The contributing quantitative and qualitative data sources used within the evaluation included electronic medical record (EMR) audit at the general practice where participants were recruited, participants questionnaires, and healthcare utilization administrative datasets. Administrative data for health care consultation billing (Medicare Benefit Schedule [MBS]) and medication dispensing (Pharmaceutical Benefit Scheme [PBS]) where optional consent was provided by participants.

Health care utilization data

From each participant’s EMR, data on the number of mental health related GP consultations, recorded mental health diagnosis/es and associated management plan outcome of each instance (prescribed antidepressant medication type and dose, referral to psychology or psychiatry, etc.) were collected. MBS data on government-funded mental health related service access was collected.

PGx prescribing report use data

Direct measurement of prescribing report utilization was not feasible. However, as a proxy for use, documented discussions about the trial and the presence of the PRESIDE trial prescribing report in the EMR were captured. We also assessed antidepressant switching that occurred after the participant’s GP received the trial prescribing report. A switch did not include dose adjustments, cessation, or initiation.

Antidepressant medication use data

Antidepressant medication use was obtained from three sources: 1) At each follow-up questionnaire (at 4-, 8-, 12- and 26-weeks), trial participants were asked if they were currently taking an antidepressant medication. If they responded ‘yes,’ a list of antidepressant medications was provided from which they could select; 2) GP medical record audit provided antidepressant medication and dosage prescribed; 3) PBS data provided medication type and dose of each tablet (rather than the prescribed daily dose). This allowed for collection of prescription details documented by health services other than at the general practice from where the participant was recruited.

Self-reported medication use, GP prescription records, and dispensed prescription data were integrated to provide a comprehensive view of which medications were prescribed, dispensed, and taken by patient participants. When medication discrepancies occurred between data sources, a hierarchy based on data reliability was used: dispensing data was prioritized, followed by GP record data, and then self-reported questionnaire data. For antidepressant daily dose prescribed, GP record data was used, given daily dose is not recorded in the dispensing dataset. When a medication was present in the dispensing dataset but not in the GP record, the dose of each dispensed tablet was used as an estimate for the daily dose prescribed.

Qualitative interviews

Semi-structured interviews were conducted with a subset of participating PRESIDE trial patients and GPs. The interview guide was developed in line with the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) domains [16] to ensure broad perspectives of mental healthcare provision, PGx, and implementation of the intervention were covered. Interviews were conducted (researchers PL, GR) via Zoom or in-person between May 2022 and February 2024. All GP participants with at least one participating patient under their care were invited to a post-trial interview. Patient participants consented to be approached for an interview at trial enrollment. Purposive sampling of patient-participants aimed for broad coverage across demographics and antidepressant medication use at baseline. It was not feasible to include patient-GP dyads. Interviewees were provided with a $50 gift card as reimbursement for their time. Audio recordings of interviews were transcribed and imported into NVivo (Version 12; QSR International). A mixed inductive and deductive coding was used [17]. Transcript coding was conducted by GR and PL, with co-coding performed by SS for 10% of the interviews. Interviewers and investigators regularly met to resolve inconsistencies with transcript coding and support reflexivity. Codes were then mapped to each of the CFIR domains. Results

PRESIDE cohort and interviewee characteristics

Data for 546 randomized patient-participants were analyzed. EMR data were retrieved for all patient-participants. Billing and prescribing administrative data were available for 453 patient-participants. Thirty-four patient-participants were invited to interview, with 20 completed (3.6%; see Table 2). Interviews ranged from 26 to 64 minutes. Eleven GPs participated in an interview (see Table 2) from a total of 101 participating GPs that had ≥1 patient under their care enrolled in the trial (10.9%).

Additional diagnoses and antidepressant use

Among the total trial participants, the most commonly reported baseline diagnoses documented in the EMR were MDD (45.3%, n = 249) and generalized anxiety disorder (36.0%, n = 198), followed by other diagnoses (e.g., OCD, eating disorders, 8.9%, n = 49), posttraumatic stress disorder (4.5%, n = 25), attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (3.6%, n = 20), autism spectrum disorders (1.5%, n = 8), and bipolar disorder (0.9%, n = 5). Diagnoses did not differ by trial arm. Antidepressant medication use was a randomization stratum and therefore did not differ between trial arms at baseline. Of those not taking an antidepressant at baseline, 30% of experimental arm and 23% of control arm participants commenced an antidepressant medication within the follow-up period. Of those taking an antidepressant medication at baseline, 26% of experimental arm and 27% of control arm participants had a medication change documented. Across both trial arms, 19.4%, (n = 54) of participants taking an antidepressant medication at baseline (51%, n = 279) were already taking one of the four to six antidepressant medications included in their prescribing report. This remained similar at the 26-week timepoint (20.9%, n = 56).

Stakeholder perspectives on PGx-informed antidepressant prescribing

Main themes identified in the thematic analysis of participant interviews are outlined below, with illustrative quotes shown in Table 1. Further details can be found in the Supplementary Materials.

PGx is a useful tool, but only for people at specific points in their treatment process

The use of PGx to inform antidepressant prescribing was acceptable as a potentially useful tool by all participants. However, many participants reported that it would have been more valuable if offered at a different point in the treatment trajectory, particularly before commencing the first antidepressant medication or after a treatment failure. Qualitative findings indicated that the most prominent mechanism through which PGx-informed prescribing influenced care was agreement between participating patients and their GPs that an antidepressant medication initiation or change was warranted. When such agreement was absent, the value of PGx information was not considered useful. There was a low rate of medication changes in the overall PRESIDE cohort in both arms (experimental rate of change per 100 days = 0.129, control rate of change = 0.134). GPs expressed hesitancy in making medication change before sufficient time had elapsed to assess response to the current treatment, even when that medication was not recommended from a PGx perspective, or participants had persistent depressive symptoms despite treatment. This was particularly evident among participants who had recently initiated an antidepressant at the time of trial enrollment. Conversely, a minority of patients were already considering a medication change when they were offered PRESIDE participation, and for these individuals the prescribing report played an important role in the selection of their next antidepressant. For this group of patients, PGx information was highly relevant and directly influenced the medication subsequently prescribed by their GP. In this circumstance, PGx-informed prescribing was offered to the right patient, one already considering a medication change, and at the right time, when the prescribing decision could reasonably wait until PGx results in the report were available.

The importance of patient selection was echoed by GPs, who reported that they would preferentially use PGx in specific contexts rather than offer it to all patients presenting with depressive symptoms. For participants not taking an antidepressant at baseline, GPs may have also avoided initiating discussion about the prescribing report when they already knew their patient was reluctant to commence antidepressant therapy.

Lack of clarity on initiation of discussions about the PGx report (fidelity of the intervention)

A major barrier to intervention uptake was the incorrect assumption that participating patients and GPs would discuss the trial and associated prescribing report, highlighting a fidelity issue in the delivery of the trial intervention. Most participants (84.2%, n = 463) had at least one mental health related GP appointment within the 26 week follow up period. However, a documented discussion about the PRESIDE trial, including discussion about the trial prescribing report, was present in the EMR for 147 (26.7%) participants. While all participating patients were encouraged by researchers to return to their GP to discuss the results, 6 of the 20 interviewees reported talking about the report with their GP. Four assumed their GP would initiate a discussion if they considered the results important. There were several participants who had forgotten that these results would be available in their EMR or had competing priorities when attending GP appointments. Conversely, GPs reported that they thought their patients would return for an appointment to discuss their results if they were interested in commencing or changing an antidepressant medication. Some GPs (two of 11) suggested that patients already taking an antidepressant are often resistant to changing their medication even when the efficacy of the medication appears to be low, making them less inclined to initiate discussions about medication switching. There was also evidence that GPs were unsure of how to act on the prescribing reports.

The impact of COVID-19 (context)

A significant contextual factor that impacted the trial was the COVID-19 pandemic. In the original trial protocol, it was assumed that patients would return to their GP for review of the PGx results approximately 2 weeks after trial recruitment when the prescribing report would be available. The impact of COVID-19 during the trial, including the switch to telehealth consultations and pandemic-related supply chain delays, meant only 25% of the prescribing reports were delivered within the planned 2-week window.

All interviewed GPs reported that COVID-19 associated factors increased the prevalence of mental health concerns in their patient population due to lockdowns and employment changes, and created longer waiting times for an appointment with psychologists and psychiatrists. This fundamentally changed how patients were able to access mental health support and how support, including treatment, was provided by GPs.

Patient participants reported variable COVID-19-related mental health impacts, with some identifying lockdowns, employment disruptions, and study changes as major stressors. Most relevant to the delivery of the trial intervention, some patients experienced challenges in accessing mental health support from their usual or preferred GP or other care providers during this time.

Discussion

The primary outcome of the PRESIDE trial did not support the hypothesis that PGx-informed antidepressant prescribing would lead to a greater reduction in depressive symptoms compared to guideline-based care for people attending primary care with moderate to severe depressive symptoms [12]. This process evaluation suggests that PGx-informed prescribing requires carefully designed implementation strategies to ensure its appropriate widespread use. It may be most useful in specific contexts, notably when both the patient and clinician are open to initiating or switching antidepressant medication.

There was also uncertainty regarding whether patients or GPs were responsible for initiating discussions about the prescribing reports, which resulted in relatively few documented discussions about the trial itself or the prescribing report. Delays in returning the prescribing reports due to COVID-19 related supply chain issues impacting the testing laboratory were an important contextual factor that affected the delivery of the intervention as planned. These delays likely further reduced the likelihood that the prescribing recommendations were available at a clinically useful time. Furthermore, COVID-19 impacted both the volume and nature of mental health presentations to primary care and access to mental health services during the trial.

Comparison with the available literature

These findings from a primary care setting contrast with a rapidly expanding body of research supporting the efficacy of PGx-informed antidepressant prescribing, including meta-analyses of RCTs [8,9]. Previous trials were largely conducted within specialist psychiatry services, whereas the PRESIDE trial aimed to examine the effectiveness in a typical cohort of patients presenting to primary care with depressive symptoms. In addition, the PRESIDE trial was pragmatic in design where implementation occurred in a real-world primary care setting and antidepressant prescribing was left to the clinical discretion of the participating GPs.

The approach to patient-participant identification in PRESIDE involved sequentially screening for depressive symptoms all patients presenting to participating GPs on a given day. This contrasts markedly with the approaches used in previous RCTs where all potential participants have a confirmed MDD diagnosis, where the treating clinician endorsed participation, and/or where individuals with co-occurring psychiatric illness were excluded [8–11,18]. Benefits of the systematic approach used in the PRESIDE trial included that researchers could identify individuals reporting depressive symptoms that may not have been identified by their GP and significantly reduced the resource burden for participating GPs. This approach resulted in a patient-participant pool that was typical of GP patients with depressive symptoms but was more heterogeneous than those of previous studies. Notably, the PRESIDE trial cohort included a considerable number of participants who had a documented diagnosis of non-MDD mental health conditions, who may not have been open to medication initiation or change, or had complex presentations, including treatment resistant depression that required management by a psychiatrist rather than a GP. The existence of multiple conditions within the context of depression is however unsurprising and reported in many papers.

Previous qualitative research has reported that people for whom antidepressant medication was clinically indicated, they are hypothetically willing to have their primary care provider offer PGx testing and use this information to inform prescribing decisions [19]. In the PRESIDE trial, we did not collect data on participants’ willingness to commence or change antidepressant medication at baseline or during follow-up, which may have acted as a mediator of the potential PGx-informed prescribing effect. The inclusion of a substantial proportion of participants who were unlikely to initiate or change antidepressant therapy could have diluted the effect of the PGx intervention. Therefore, the outcomes of the PRESIDE trial should be interpreted within the context of how PGx was delivered and the characteristics of the patient-participants.

Prior work has also shown that GPs perceive potential utility of PGx testing to guide antidepressant prescribing in primary care [18,19], a perspective reflected in our GP interviews. However, GPs have also identified implementation barriers that may limit this utility, including challenges related to access to PGx results and the need for integration into the EMR [20]. It is therefore likely that similar implementation issues affected the use of PGx in our trial.

Intervention fidelity was impacted by a lack of clarity around which party would initiate discussions about the PGx report. Only 27% of the patient-participants had a documented discussion about the PRESIDE trial or the associated prescribing report present in their EMR. This likely underestimates the true number of conversations between participants and their GPs, as consultation notes often do not capture all topics discussed during an appointment, and documentation practices vary considerably among clinicians. Nevertheless, this fidelity issue suggests that assumptions made by the research team about how the intervention would integrate into existing GP workflows may not have been accurate.

There was also a low level of alignment in antidepressants being taken at baseline with the prescribing report recommendations, indicating that there was a strong potential for the report to influence prescribing decisions. However, the low rates of antidepressant change or initiation showing this influence did not occur as expected. A potential explanation is that GPs saw the prescribing reports as research reports, not equivalent to other patient results. Therefore, they did not integrate them into their regular follow up processes, such as recalling patients for non-urgent results appointments or discussing them when the patient next presented [20,21]. A previous trial employed a separate platform for access to PGx results, though it still showed a high uptake of PGx-informed prescribing by participants (majority of whom were primary care physicians) [22]. The limited translation observed in our trial highlights potential differences in education and support requirements for trials being conducted in primary care contexts, where GPs may have comparatively little experience with integrating experimental interventions into their usual care workflows and face considerable time pressure during consultations [20,22]. Multi-modal education strategies with the GPs involved, with further emphasis on the clinical utility of PGx for this purpose, may have increased GPs’ use of the PGx reports.

The breadth of health concerns managed by GPs is, by definition, wide. Patients returning to their GP following enrollment into the PRESIDE trial presented for a variety of reasons, of which mental health was only one and may not have been their main priority. This contrasts with most published studies on PGx-informed antidepressant prescribing where clinical encounters between trial participants and clinicians are specifically focused on the management of mental health conditions [8–11]. It is possible that some patients were unwilling or unable to pay for a follow-up appointment with their GP to specifically discuss their PGx results or found accessing an appointment with their preferred GP challenging, particularly given that this trial was conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic, where GP services were significantly stretched. The fidelity of the intervention may have benefited from the inclusion of a follow-up GP appointment, with no out-of-pocket cost to the patient participant, specifically to discuss the PGx results. Combining this with a more structured patient recall program, prompting patients to book this follow-up appointment, may further have supported timely engagement with PGx results.

Finally, recruitment and follow up for the PRESIDE trial took place between May 2021 and April 2024 in Victoria, Australia, with most participants being based in metropolitan Melbourne. Between March 2020 and November 2021, Melbournians spent 262 days under lockdown conditions and 613 days under restriction conditions due to the COVID-19 pandemic. While research has demonstrated modest overall impact of COVID-19 restrictions on the mental health of Melbournians compared to Australians in other jurisdictions, women were disproportionately impacted, particularly those aged 20–54 years [3]. The PRESIDE sample was 67% female with a mean age of 41 years [12]. Changes within the health sector, including the increased use of telehealth, discouragement of non-urgent appointments, and reduced access to psychology and psychiatry services, which meant that the trial was conducted during a period either directly impacted by these changes or while services were gradually returning to normal. Notably, the shift to telehealth resulted in fewer medication initiations by GPs in Australia [23], which may have contributed to the low rates of antidepressant initiation among participants who were not taking an antidepressant at baseline.

Strengths and limitations

This process evaluation provides a thorough, mixed-methods examination of the PRESIDE Trial exploring possible explanations of the trial results. We collected qualitative data from interviews with both patient- and GP-participants, which were triangulated with quantitative data from the full PRESIDE cohort. We also collected objective EMR and administrative health use data, as well as self-reported participant data on antidepressant use.

However, several limitations should be noted. The inclusion of additional self-reported participant outcomes would have strengthened the evaluation. Specifically, information on patient-participants’ willingness to initiate or switch antidepressant medication, as well as whether they discussed the prescribing report with their GP, could have provided further insight into the appropriateness of PGx-informed prescribing and the fidelity of intervention delivery, respectively. In retrospect, more structured recall program or clearly specified next steps for GPs and patients to follow after the return of the prescribing report could have improved intervention fidelity. Due to the incompleteness of medical record data and the format of PBS administrative data, we were able to report on medication initiation or changes but not dose modifications to the same medication.

Additionally, we did not collect information about non-mental health comorbidities through self-report or medical records. Given the well-established relationship between depression and common chronic conditions, including cancer, cardiovascular, metabolic, neurological and inflammatory disorders [24], this information may have helped provide a more complete understanding of the participant cohort and the trial outcomes.

Implications and future research

The results of the PRESIDE trial suggest that PGx-informed antidepressant prescribing may not be beneficial for all patients presenting to primary care with moderate to severe depressive symptoms. Future studies evaluating the effectiveness of PGx-informed antidepressant prescribing would benefit from enrolling participants most appropriate for this intervention, particularly those with a confirmed diagnosis of MDD who are willing to consider medication initiation or change, and those for whom concern about adverse effects is a major barrier to medication initiation. Additionally, providing support for GPs responsible for acting on PGx information, through pre-trial education and ‘just in time’ clinical resources, may improve confidence in interpreting and applying PGx results. A pilot study focused on end-user testing of the PGx report, streamlining clinical workflows, and developing a GP education module could strengthen future research aimed at implementing PGx-informed antidepressant prescribing in general practice. Future research should include more direct and extensive measures of intervention fidelity, including self-reported participant measures on engagement with PGx reports with GPs.

Conclusions

This process evaluation provides important context for interpreting the PRESIDE trial findings, particularly when considered alongside previous trials on PGx-informed antidepressant prescribing that were conducted in more selected populations and tightly controlled research environments. Overall, the PRESIDE trial showed that PGx-guided antidepressant prescribing did not improve depression severity compared with prescribing reports based on national guidelines. This process evaluation highlights the need for streamlined testing workflows and targeted rather than universal PGx testing, alongside GP education to overcome barriers to implementing PGx-informed antidepressant prescribing within general practice settings. Future research is needed to evaluate improved implementation strategies for PGx testing to understand its effectiveness and utility in the primary care setting.
