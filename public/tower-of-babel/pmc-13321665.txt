Reprogramming probiotic for uric acid modular degradation and hyperuricemia treatment by synthetic biology regulation
Authors: Jingyu Zhou, Xinyu Cheng, Liping Chen, Yongzhong Wang, Qiang Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hyperuricemia has emerged as the fourth most prevalent metabolic disorder, necessitating the development of safer and more effective therapeutic strategies. In this study, we constructed a recombinant probiotic strain expressing the PucL and PucM enzymes, which demonstrated a uric acid degradation rate of 65% in vitro. To enhance this activity, we performed modular optimization by employing three ribosome binding sites (RBSs) of different strengths—RBS 29, RBS 31, and RBS T7—to tune the expression levels of pucL and pucM, resulting in highly efficient uric acid degradation. Further improvement was achieved by overexpressing the uric acid transporter gene ygfU and the hydrogen peroxide–degrading catalase gene katG, leading to significant uric acid degradation. Furthermore, the engineered Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 strain was evaluated in a mouse model of hyperuricemia; treatment with the optimized probiotic reduced serum uric acid levels to 39.11 mg/L, representing a 15.98% decrease compared with the control group. Further analysis revealed that this engineered bacterium ameliorates hyperuricemia by modulating the Firmicutes-to-Bacteroidetes ratio, increasing microbial diversity, and promoting the growth of beneficial genera. Collectively, this study establishes an engineered probiotic cell factory for uric acid degradation and demonstrates a proof-of-concept for the microbial remediation of hyperuricemia.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12934-026-03022-w.

Introduction

Hyperuricemia (HUA) primarily results from an imbalance in purine metabolism, leading to excessive uric acid accumulation and posing a significant risk to physiological homeostasis [1–4]. Pathologically, HUA can be categorized into three types: overproduction of uric acid, reduced renal excretion, and impaired extrarenal excretion [3, 5]. Notably, extrarenal excretion disorders are closely linked to intestinal metabolism, as approximately one‑third of the body’s uric acid is eliminated via the intestines and metabolized by the gut microbiota [6, 7]. Consequently, native probiotics and prebiotics have been explored for their ability to modulate intestinal metabolism and reduce uric acid burden by influencing relevant metabolic pathways and host–microbe interactions [8–11]. Current clinical pharmacological options remain limited and are often associated with significant side effects. In contrast, probiotic‑based therapies offer advantages such as low cost, minimal toxicity, and high safety in mitigating HUA [12–14]. Given the prominent role of the intestine in regulating serum uric acid levels, probiotic intervention has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for HUA.

Conventionally, uric acid‑degrading probiotics have been isolated from fermented foods or natural environments and have been shown to alleviate HUA in vivo [15–18]. For instance, the fermented Chinese food “Jiangshui” was utilized to isolate the Limosilactobacillus fermentum JL-3 strain, which can utilize the uric acid as its sole carbon and nitrogen source and decreased the serum uric acid level by 31.3% 19. Similarly, Lacticaseibacillus paracasei JS-3 was also isolated from “Jiangshui”; it achieved a significant uric acid degradation rate of 49%, exhibiting the potential therapeutic value for uric acid degradation [20]. The related strain Lactobacillus fermentum F40-4 was also shown to decrease serum levels of uric acid by 40.84% 21. However, the screening of such native strains remains largely empirical and unpredictable [5, 22]. Advances in synthetic biology have enabled the rational engineering of recombinant probiotics for uric acid degradation through the expression of key enzymes such as urate oxidases [22–24]. For example, the probiotic yeast Saccharomyces boulardii was engineered to express the urate oxidase UOX and urate transporter UapA, achieving the uric acid-degrading activity by 365.32 ± 20.54 µmol/h/OD 25. Furthermore, the integration of urate oxidase and an oxygen-recycling system into E. coli Nissle 1917 significantly lowered serum uric acid [24]. These examples highlight the potential of rationally designed microbial cell factories for direct and efficient uric acid degradation [26–30].

In this work, we employed the human‑derived probiotic E. coli Nissle 1917 (EcN) as the chassis. EcN was selected specifically because it exhibits significantly stronger uric acid‑degrading activity than other probiotic candidates and offers a highly tractable genetic background, making it easier to construct recombinant strains compared to alternative platforms. First, a recombinant strain was constructed by introducing the uricase genes pucL and pucM for in vitro uric acid degradation. We then optimized the system by employing ribosome‑binding site (RBS) modules of different strengths to precisely tune the expression of pucL and pucM, which substantially enhanced degradation efficiency. Subsequently, the uric acid transporter gene ygfU and the E. coli catalase gene katG were incorporated to create a systematically enhanced engineered probiotic. Finally, the uric acid‑lowering efficacy of the optimized strain was validated in a mouse model of hyperuricemia, and alterations in gut microbiota structure and function were evaluated for their potential application (Fig. 1).

The primary objective of this study was to establish a high-performance microbial cell factory based on the E. coli Nissle 1917 chassis to overcome the inherent limitations and unpredictability of traditional probiotic screening for hyperuricemia therapy. Distinct from conventional overexpression approaches, this work introduces a modular synthetic biology strategy characterized by the precise tuning of metabolic flux through ribosome-binding site (RBS) optimization of the PucL and PucM uricase complex. By systematically integrating the uric acid transporter ygfU and the catalase katG, we engineered a robust degradation system capable of efficient substrate sequestration and the effective neutralization of hydrogen peroxide byproducts, ultimately achieving complete uric acid mineralization. This comprehensive approach was further validated in an HUA mouse model, where the optimized strain demonstrated significant efficacy in reducing serum uric acid, alleviating renal pathological damage, and restoring gut microbiota homeostasis. Consequently, this study provides a programmed framework for the development of next-generation probiotic therapeutics designed to remediate complex metabolic disorders.

Uric acid degradation in vitro

The uric acid degradation capability of the engineered strains was evaluated using two complementary methods [32]:

Liquid assay: Recombinant strains were cultured and harvested. The cell pellets were resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) spiked with a predetermined concentration of uric acid to achieve a final OD600 of 1.0. The resulting suspension was incubated at 37 °C under continuous shaking. At designated time points, samples were withdrawn, centrifuged, and the supernatants were collected. The residual uric acid concentration in each supernatant was determined using a commercial Uric Acid (UA) Assay Kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute). The degradation kinetics were then determined by plotting the decrease in uric acid concentration as a function of time. Solid-plate assay: Uric acid was supplemented into solid agar medium to prepare indicator plates. Freshly grown recombinant strains were spotted onto the plates and incubated. The formation of a clear halo around each colony, resulting from the degradation of insoluble uric acid in the medium, served as a qualitative indicator of degradation activity.

Uric acid degradation in vivo

(1) Preliminary experiment: To explore the feasibility of the modeling method, potassium oxonate (PO) was administered intragastrically to mice at a dose of 400 mg/kg (bw/d), and hypoxanthine was injected intraperitoneally into the mice at a dose of 400 mg/kg (bw/d); (2) Formal experiment: Modeling in the morning (intragastric and intraperitoneal injections), intervention in the evening (intragastric administration of recombinant bacteria), for a duration of 1 month; (3) Tissue collection and index detection: Collect mouse urine, feces, orbital blood, kidneys, liver, cecum, colon and colon feces, and detect uric acid indicators, antioxidant indicators, target protein expression indicators, as well as histopathological examination of the kidney. The initial CFU was controlled at 1 × 109 CFU/mL for intragastric administration. Hangzhou Ziyuan Experimental Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China) was used for purchasing forty-four specific pathogen-free male Kunming mice [33].

H&E and masson staining

Kidney tissues from dissected mice were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned (Service Bio Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China). Tissue sections (4 μm thickness) were subjected to standard hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and Masson’s trichrome staining according to established protocols. Stained sections were examined and imaged using an optical microscope to compare histological morphology among the control, model, and probiotic-treated groups [34].

Biochemical index detection of serum, liver and kidney

To prepare mouse tissue homogenates, approximately 100 mg of tissue was excised, weighed, and resuspended in 0.9% physiological saline to prepare a 10% (w/v) tissue homogenate. The tissue suspension was homogenized using an automatic grinding and disruption instrument. After a freeze-thaw cycle, the suspension was centrifuged to obtain the supernatant for subsequent analysis with commercial kits [33].

The levels of UA, H2O2, XOD, BUN, CRE and GSH in the control, model and experimental group were detected using commercial kits purchased from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute.

Fecal 16 S rDNA analysis treated with engineered strain. Bioinformatic analysis of the gut microbiota used the Majorbio Cloud platform (https://cloud.majorbio.com) [35].

Result

Construction and verification of uric acid-degrading probiotics

EcN is a well-characterized probiotic strain of human intestinal origin that has been approved for clinical use in managing inflammatory bowel disease and irritable bowel syndrome. It is recognized for its excellent safety profile and genetic tractability [36, 37]. With the advancement of synthetic biology, EcN has emerged as a promising chassis for engineering targeted drug delivery systems and enabling precise genetic modifications.

Building on previous studies that demonstrated the uricolytic activity of the enzymes PucL and PucM—which are capable of converting uric acid to allantoin both in vitro and in vivo [24] (Fig. 2A). We engineered a recombinant EcN strain for uric acid degradation. The genes encoding PucL and PucM were assembled into the expression vector pETM6R1, which carries an inducible Trc promoter (Fig. 2B). The resulting plasmid, pETM6R1-pucL-pucM, was subsequently transformed into EcN to generate the recombinant strain designated EcN 1–1 (Fig. 2C). The engineered strain EcN 1–1 showed robust growth in shake-flask culture, reaching an OD600 of 1.41 after 14 h (Fig. 2D). For uric acid degradation assays, expression of PucL and PucM was induced for 24 h at 30 °C. Cells were then harvested and resuspended to a density of 1 × 10⁹ CFU/mL in a solution containing 1 mM uric acid. Degradation kinetics revealed a rapid decrease in uric acid concentration, falling to 0.5 mM within 30 min and further to 0.35 mM after 120 min of co-culture (Fig. 2E). This corresponded to a degradation efficiency of 65% at the 120 min time point (Fig. 2F). Supplementary Fig. 1 exhibited the uric acid degradation process of EcN, which degraded the uric acid by about 50% at 12 h.

The above results demonstrate that our engineered E. coli Nissle 1917 efficiently degrades uric acid in vitro, whereas native probiotic strains typically require 24 h or longer to achieve comparable degradation [19, 20]. This exhibits the advantage of synthetic biology-assisted probiotic cell factory.

Modular optimization of key enzymes for uric acid degradation

The ribosome binding site (RBS) modulates the translation efficiency of genes in bacteria [31]. Translation initiation occurs at a specific mRNA sequence—the RBS—and the expression level of heterologous genes in E. coli is strongly influenced by the efficiency of this process [38]. Consequently, researchers have employed RBS sequences of varying strengths to fine-tune gene expression, thereby achieving optimal enzyme catalysis and improving the synthesis efficiency of industrial chemicals [38, 39].

In this study, we employed three RBS variants—strong (RBS 29), moderate (RBS T7), and weak (RBS 31)—to systematically modulate the translation initiation rates of pucL and pucM, aiming to improve uric acid degradation [31]. The strain EcN 1–1 was utilized as the parent strain to replace the PucL and PucM expression cassettes with three strengths RBS sequences for building 9 strains to confirm the optimal expression (Fig. 3A). Thus, we successfully constructed nine target recombinant strains.

As a result, it showed that the uric acid degradation was different with 60 min, due to the different RBS sequences for PucL and PucM enzyme expression, respectively. Thus, the final uric acid titer of strain EcN 2 − 1 to EcN 2–3 residual amounts from 0.40 to 0.46 mM, with a high level RBS 29 for PucL enzyme expression; the strain EcN 2–7 to EcN 2–9 remained the uric acid titer from 0.17 to 0.40 mM, with a moderate level RBS T7 for PucL enzyme expression. Under the control of low level RBS 31, the final uric acid titer of strain EcN 2–4 to EcN 2–6 can remain from 0.35 to 0.55 mM (Fig. 3B). Among these, the strain EcN 2–9 exhibited the optimized uric acid degradation with 0.196 mM accumulation, which carried moderate level RBS T7 for PucL and PucM enzyme expression, respectively. The uric acid degradation rate of the strain EcN 2–9 also possessed the optimal degradation efficiency, which showed the significant improvements compared to that of strain EcN 1–1 and EcN 2 − 1 to EcN 2–8. This indicated that the translation initiation rate of PucL and PucM need a suitable expression strength for uric acid degradation.

Furthermore, the cell growth of strains EcN 2 − 1 to EcN 2–9 exhibited differences during the 12 h culture. The cell growth of optimized uric acid degradation strain EcN 2–9 can reach to 1.296 (OD600), while the strain EcN 2–6 possessed the lower cell growth among these 9 strains (Fig. 3C). Due to the relatively low solubility of uric acid, uric acid salts often precipitate as solid particles in the body. Therefore, to assess solid uric acid degradation, the optimal T7-T7 strain was inoculated onto custom uric acid agar plates via serial dilution and spread plating, which can be used as a relative quantification. Clear zones of degradation appeared around the colonies, with the zone area exhibiting an approximate positive correlation with the dilution levels. This consistency confirms the validity of the experimental design and substantiates the strain’s efficacy in degrading solid uric acid. (Fig. 3D–E). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that RBS-based modular optimization is an effective strategy for enhancing uric acid degradation in engineered probiotic cell factories.

System strengthening of uric acid degradation

To enhance the uric acid degradation, we utilized the uric acid transporter and the degradation of the by-product H2O222, 24. YgfU (encoding a uric acid permease) is a uric acid transporter in the E. coli K12, and the KatG (encoding a catalase/hydroperoxidase) can remove the by-product H2O2 during the uric acid degradation (Fig. 4A).

Therefore, the optimized strain EcN 2–9 was used as the engineered chassis to co-express the YgfU and KatG proteins, resulting in the strain EcN 3 − 1. The SDS-PAGE was employed to verify the expression of the YgfU and KatG proteins in E. coli Nissle 1917 (Fig. 4B). The cell growth of strain EcN 3 − 1 was affected by the expression of these proteins (Fig. 4C). As YgfU is a proton gradient-dependent transporter in E. coli K12, its overexpression can generate the growth burden on cells and impact cellular metabolism, consistent with previous studies [24, 40]. Notably, the uric acid degradation rate reached 97.81% within 60 min (Fig. 4D). We also evaluated the ability of strain EcN 3 − 1 to degrade solid uric acid. Consistent with the previous analysis, Y-K exhibited a degradation zone of 1.668 cm2 at a 1:1 plating concentration, underperforming compared to T7-T7 (1.888 cm2), which can be setted as a relative quantification. This suggests that the inclusion of oxygen-enhancing genes might exert a greater metabolic burden on the strain in vitro, thereby limiting its overall degradation efficiency. (Fig. 4E–F). Our work furthermore illustrated that reduced possible damage of by-product H2O2 and improved uric acid transporter can efficiently degrade uric acid in vitro.

Testing of uric acid-degrading probiotics in mice

Based on the strong in vitro uric acid–degrading capacity of the engineered strains, we next evaluated their therapeutic potential in a HUA mouse model. Mice received daily intragastric administration of the recombinant strains EcN 1‑1, EcN 2‑9, or fully optimized EcN 3‑1, following a well‑established protocol combining potassium oxonate and hypoxanthine (PO/I) to induce HUA (Fig. 5A). Firstly, the data of serum uric acid levels, XOD enzyme activity, serum creatinine (CRE) and MDA in mice were measured to evaluate the effect changes of different treatment groups. The results showed that under the PO/I mixed gavage model condition, the indicators related to hyperuricemia showed an upward trend. The weight of experimental groups was decreased compared to that of control, which illustrated the effects of PO/I mixed gavage model condition (Fig. 5B). The optimized strain EcN 3 − 1 group could decrease the uric acid, serum creatinine (CRE), XOD enzyme activity, and MDA to 39.11 mg/L, 114.36 µM, 8.01 U/L, and 8.12 µM, and it showed a 15.98%, 27.29%, 36.44%, 29.35% decrease compared to that of MC group (Figs. 5C–D and 6A–C). Moreover, the strain EcN 1–1 and strain EcN 2–9 also exhibited the decrease trends compared to that of MC group and E. coli Nissle 1917. Furthermore, the relative serum BUN, H2O2 of optimized strain EcN 3 − 1 group were reduced to 7.02 mM, 56.39 mM, and showed a 26.03%, 34.84% compared to that of MC group. It also confirmed that the by-product H2O2 can be efficiently removed in mice (Fig. 6D–E). Therefore, these results illustrated that engineered the E. coli Nissle 1917 could degrade the uric acid in mice.

Consistent with the preceding results, hyperuricemia induction via intraperitoneal (I/P) or oral (PO) administration caused evident renal damage in mice of the model control (MC) group. Upon dissection, the kidneys of MC mice exhibited markedly abnormal morphology and texture, along with paler coloration compared to the control group. Treatment with the engineered probiotic strains EcN 1–1, EcN 2–9, and EcN 3 − 1 partially restored kidney morphology and condition, indicating that these strains alleviate hyperuricemia-induced renal injury through uric acid degradation (Supplementary Fig. 2 A). Histological examination using hematoxylin‑eosin (H&E) and Masson staining further confirmed substantial renal damage in MC mice, including tubular dilation, vacuolization, and focal necrosis. Administration of EcN 1–1, EcN 2–9, and particularly EcN 3 − 1 markedly attenuated these pathological changes and helped restore normal tissue architecture (Supplementary Fig. 2B). It should be noted, however, that despite clear improvement, the engineered probiotics did not completely reverse all histological damage under the current treatment regimen. This is likely attributable to the complexity of the in vivo environment and the multifactorial nature of hyperuricemic nephropathy. Further optimization—such as prolonged administration or combination therapy—may be required to achieve full tissue recovery.

Uric acid-degrading probiotics modulates the fecal microbiota

Our research mainly focused on the analysis of the diversity of 16 S intestinal flora in the feces of mice through intragastric administration of engineered bacteria and wild-type bacteria. The main discussion was about the impact of our engineered strains on the fecal flora of mice. The results demonstrate that the uric acid‑degrading engineering treatment is effective, as it alleviates the disruption of fecal flora induced by hyperuricemia and induces substantial alterations in the structure and composition of the fecal microbial community.

To further evaluate the ameliorative effect of the engineered strain EcN-P-P-YK on hyperuricemia, we performed 16 S rRNA gene sequencing on the mouse gut microbiota. Venn diagram analysis illustrated that modeling with potassium oxonate and hypoxanthine reduced the number of unique operational taxonomic units (OTUs) in the mouse gut, which was restored after EcN-P-P-YK intervention (Fig. 7A). Principal coordinate analysis revealed that the intervention altered the overall microbial structure, forming a distinct cluster (Fig. 7B). At the phylum level, the Firmicutes abundance increased slightly in the model control (MC) group compared with the normal control (NC) group, while Bacteroidetes showed an inverse trend (Fig. 7C). After EcN-P-P-YK intervention, Firmicutes abundance decreased markedly, and the proportion of Bacteroidetes increased accordingly. Alpha diversity indices, including Chao1 and Shannon, indicated that species richness and evenness were significantly elevated in the treatment group relative to the MC group, returning to levels comparable to the NC group (Fig. 7D-E).

At the genus level, the abundances of Desulfovibrio, Lactobacillus, and Limosilactobacillus were lower in the MC group than in the NC group (Fig. 7F). Correlation analysis revealed that these genera were negatively associated with serum uric acid levels, suggesting a potential role in modulating uric acid metabolism through mechanisms such as short‑chain fatty acid production and pathogen inhibition. In contrast, Thomasclavelia was positively correlated with serum uric acid levels and was enriched in the MC group (Fig. 7G). Collectively, these findings indicate that the engineered bacterium alleviates hyperuricemia by reshaping the gut microbial community structure.

Uric acid-degrading probiotics functional evaluation

In order to assess the colonization potential of the engineered strain EcN 3‑1 within the gastrointestinal environment, comparative analyses of auto‑aggregation and surface hydrophobicity were conducted between the engineered and wild‑type strains. The auto‑aggregation assay indicated that the engineered strain EcN 3‑1 displayed a significantly elevated self‑aggregation rate of 63.57%, markedly exceeding that of the wild‑type EcN (15.47%). This result suggests an enhanced capacity for intercellular interaction in the engineered strain, which may promote bacterial aggregation and support colonization in the intestinal tract. Concurrently, surface hydrophobicity measurements (Fig. 8A) revealed an average hydrophobicity of approximately 19.31% for EcN 3‑1, in contrast to about 10.64% for the wild‑type strain, confirming its comparatively higher hydrophobic character. Taken together, these findings imply that modifications in the surface properties of EcN 3‑1—potentially attributable to heterologous protein expression—strengthen its adhesive potential, aligning with the possibility of improved colonization ability.

Hemolytic activity is a critical safety criterion for probiotic strain evaluation. To confirm the safety profile of the engineered strain EcN 3‑1 for potential applications, its hemolytic potential was assessed following established standard protocols. As shown in Fig. 8B, neither the wild-type EcN nor the engineered EcN 3‑1 induced any discoloration or transparent zone around the bacterial colonies, a characteristic of γ-hemolysis (non‑hemolytic activity). In contrast, the positive control (Pseudomonas aeruginosa) exhibited a distinct, completely transparent hemolytic ring surrounding the colonies, confirming β-hemolysis. Collectively, these results demonstrate that the engineered strain EcN 3‑1 fully retains the non‑hemolytic phenotype of the parental EcN strain, exhibits no alteration in its hemolytic safety profile, and meets the essential safety prerequisites for probiotic use.

Discussion

This study aimed to address key bottlenecks in hyperuricemia treatment by developing an engineered probiotic. Guided by the principles of microbial therapeutics and synthetic biology, we constructed a genetically modified Escherichia coli Nissle 1917 (EcN) strain. The work focused on four objectives: establishing a probiotic cell factory for uric acid degradation, optimizing catalytic efficiency through modular ribosome binding site (RBS) engineering, systematically enhancing strain performance, and validating uric acid–lowering efficacy in vivo. We successfully created a high-performance engineered EcN strain capable of efficient uric acid degradation. These results provide important experimental evidence and a conceptual framework for developing safe and effective microbiome-based therapies against hyperuricemia.

Previous studies mainly focused on the screening of probiotics that can degrade uric acid, such as through the screening of uric acid-degrading strains from fermented foods [9, 19], or by screening some uric acid-degrading strains in fermented fruits to achieve the purpose of uric acid degradation both in vivo and in vitro [41]. Additionally, through the engineering modification of related strains, such as the modification and screening of genomic promoters in eukaryotic Saccharomyces boulardii to achieve uric acid degradation [25], or the expression of uric acid oxidase by probiotic E. coli Nissle 1917 to achieve effective uric acid degradation [24]. Based on the previous studies, this research optimized the expression level of uric acid oxidase through modularization of the RBS sequence, thereby achieving efficient degradation both in vivo and in vitro, and alleviating the effects such as kidney damage caused by hyperuricemia. Although the degradation effect in vivo of this study is not as obvious as that in vitro, due to the complexity of the in vivo environment, in subsequent studies, based on microbiomics, the relevant interacting microbial communities can be identified to achieve functional complementation and achieve efficient degradation in vivo [37, 42]. In summary, our results indicate that the engineered strain EcN-P-P-YK alleviates hyperuricemia by modulating the gut microbiota. This modulation involves restoring microbial richness, reducing the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio, and enriching beneficial genera that are inversely correlated with serum uric acid levels. Thus, the therapeutic effect is mediated, at least in part, by the restoration of a healthier gut microbial community.

In the future, as an increasing number of synthetic biology tools are designed to enhance uric acid degradation via microbial cell factories, the following directions may facilitate the development of probiotic cell factories [43, 44]: (i) engineering higher‑activity urate oxidases through artificial design leveraging machine learning and multi‑omics data [45, 46]; (ii) employing regulatory tools to dynamically adjust gene expression in response to stimuli such as light [47, 48], temperature [49, 50], metabolites [51], and so on; (iii) utilizing multiple strains that cooperate to achieve uric acid degradation in complex internal environments, guided by big data and microbiome analysis [52]; (iv) using light, temperature, and electronic regulation signals for directly regulating the physiological state of cells [3, 53–55].
