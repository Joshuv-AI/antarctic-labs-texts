Visualization-guided growth of CsPbBr3 microcrystals via water-induced phase transition
Authors: Yichi Zhong, Qian Wang, Jian Huang, Chuanyu Zhang, Kun Qian, Zhanpeng Wang, Hongyu Yang, Jingzhou Li, Hongxing Dong, Long Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This work presents a visual growth strategy based on water-induced phase transitions, which enables real-time tracking and structural regulation of Cs4PbBr6-to-CsPbBr3 microcrystal transformation under ambient conditions without the need for high-energy beams or vacuum. Driven by the difference in the dissolution rates of CsBr and PbBr2 in water, the slow progression of the reaction interface allows clear observation of crystal morphology evolution under a conventional fluorescence microscope. The microcrystals obtained through strategy possess highly regular structural morphology and superior optical properties. In the experiments, micron-scale-wire exhibiting size-dependent polarized emission and bulk-microcrystals supporting multimode lasing emission were successfully constructed. Further analysis revealed the significant influence of crystal size on excited-state dynamics, cavity mode selectivity, and emission characteristics, thereby establishing a direct link between structural visual regulation and functional photon output. This work delivers mechanistic insights and experimental evidence for controllable fabrication of low-threshold lasers and polarized light-emitting devices.

Introduction

Perovskite materials-particularly lead halide perovskites-have emerged as a focal point in optoelectronics research over the past decade, owing to their exceptional optoelectronic properties. With high absorption coefficients1, long carrier diffusion lengths2, high exciton binding energies3, and tunable bandgaps4, perovskites show great potential in applications such as photoluminescence (PL)3, solar cells5, photodetectors2, laser emission6, and integrated photonic devices7. Compared to traditional inorganic semiconductors (such as III-V materials)8, perovskites feature lower energy consumption and simpler fabrication processes, while enabling multidimensional performance optimization through compositional tuning and morphological engineering9,10. These advantages open new avenues for developing next-generation high-efficiency and low-cost optoelectronic devices. Among the various research directions, micron-scale perovskite crystals, due to their moderate size, high crystallinity, low defect density, and natural optical resonator function, have become an ideal foundation for realizing high-performance lasers, directional light-emitting devices, and single-photon sources11–13. Micron-sized crystals can support standing wave modes, enhance photon localization and exciton-photon coupling, lower lasing thresholds, and improve PL efficiency and stability14–16. Furthermore, size-controllable and shape-regular micron crystals serve as valuable experimental platforms for investigating mode selectivity, nonlinear emission behavior, and strong light-matter coupling in photonic systems17–19. Therefore, achieving high-quality, controllable synthesis of micron crystals and precisely understanding their growth dynamics is a key issue in advancing the practical application of perovskite materials.

Although various methods have been developed for synthesizing perovskite microncrystals20,21, such as the formation of CsPbBr3 through Cs4PbBr6 conversion22, traditional growth processes often lack real-time monitoring capabilities, making it difficult to systematically capture the dynamic processes of nucleation, growth, and morphology evolution, thereby limiting the understanding of underlying mechanisms. Recently, Prabhakaran et al. employed in-situ heating transmission electron microscopy (TEM) to observe in real-time the transformation process of CsPbBr3 nanosheets into nanoplates, revealing the local evolutionary mechanism of nanostructure coalescence during growth23. However, such techniques rely on high vacuum and high-energy electron beam environments, which can introduce irradiation damage and undesired side reactions. In addition, these methods suffer from limited spatial resolution and constrained time scales, and the experimental conditions deviate significantly from actual device operating environments. In contrast, a mild-environment visualization growth strategy enables real-time, continuous, and non-invasive monitoring of perovskite microcrystal growth under conditions close to actual applications.

In this work, we introduce a water-vapor-induced phase transition mechanism, in which differences in the solubility of CsBr and PbBr2 in water drive the spontaneous transformation of Cs4PbBr6 microncrystals into the CsPbBr3 structure24–26. During the reaction, water molecules preferentially dissolve CsBr, leading to local composition enrichment. Meanwhile, fluid movement allows Cs+ ions and [PbBr6]4- octahedra to migrate slowly, leading to growth process governed by extremely low reaction kinetics. Such controlled and gradual material migration, along with slow interface evolution, enables real-time, in situ observation of morphological reconstruction, dimensional changes, and luminescence evolution in individual microcrystals using a conventional fluorescence microscope. By adjusting the water vapor concentration, initial crystal morphology, and reaction kinetics, we achieve precise control over the growth rate, degree of deformation, and final microstructure, offering new possibilities for constructing highly controllable functional architectures. Based on this approach, the CsPbBr3 micron crystals synthesized via visualization growth exhibit not only excellent size controllability and regular crystal morphology but also high optical quality, with low defect density and high PL uniformity. Further investigations show that these microcrystals effectively support localized photonic modes, enable strong light-matter interactions, and achieve multimode lasing under high excitation power. The direct correlation among microcrystal size, cavity mode number, lasing threshold, and emission wavelength stability underscores the bridging role of visualized growth in connecting morphology control with functional performance. This provides solid experimental evidence and theoretical support for the design and application of perovskite-based miniature photonic devices.

Results and discussion

Optimized hot injection method was successfully used to prepare highly monodispersed Cs4PbBr6 nanocrystals (NCs)27, as seen as Fig. S1a, b. The resulting NCs, dispersed in toluene, formed a colorless and transparent solution, and their UV-vis absorption spectrum exhibited a strong peak around 314 nm (Fig. S2b). X-ray diffraction (XRD) confirmed that the sample matched well with the standard Cs4PbBr6 phase (PDF#73-2478), exhibiting good crystallinity (Fig. S2c). The surface of Cs4PbBr6 NCs was initially well-passivated by long-chain organic ligands, which endowed them with excellent ambient stability. The colloidal solution remained clear even after two weeks of storage, and high-resolution TEM images revealed uniform hexagonal NCs with well-defined shapes, confirming their structural stability and good dispersibility (Fig. S2d). Upon introducing a small amount of the polar solvent methyl acetate into the system, partial substitution or detachment of surface ligands occurred, significantly reducing the steric hindrance between NCs and promoting particle contact and oriented attachment. As the solvent gradually evaporated and aging, the NCs underwent a slow dissolution-recrystallization process, eventually growing and reorganizing into well-defined, micron-sized columnar crystals (Fig. S1c). As shown in Fig. 1a, the Cs4PbBr6 microncrystals appear as short rods or columns with sharp edges and regular morphology, indicative of highly ordered crystal growth. The crystal size is approximately in the micrometer range, with a uniform size distribution, indicating good control over the synthesis process. The inset presents a magnified view of a single microcrystal, further highlighting its dense interior and regular geometry. Under insufficient growth time, sub-micron structures can also be formed, as shown in Fig. S3. To assess the chemical composition and uniformity, energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping was performed on a single microcrystal (Fig. 1b-d). The Cs, Pb, and Br elements are uniformly distributed throughout the crystal, and no significant element segregation or phase separation was observed, indicating homogeneous composition and good chemical consistency. Further quantitative analysis (Fig. S4) showed that the atomic ratio of Cs, Pb, and Br are close to 4:1:6, consistent with the theoretical stoichiometry of Cs4PbBr628, suggesting no compositional change occurred during the transformation from NCs to microcrystals. This result confirms that the Cs4PbBr6 micron crystals are pure, regular, and highly crystalline in the initial synthetic state, providing a stable foundation for subsequent studies on structural evolution and phase transition behavior. Figure 1e shows the XRD pattern of the Cs4PbBr6 micron crystals. Compared to Cs4PbBr6 NCs, the diffraction peak intensities along the (012) and (024) planes are significantly enhanced in the microcrystals, indicating a higher degree of orientation and ordered arrangement along these directions. Additionally, due to their increased size, the microcrystals gradually settled to the bottom of the solution, exhibiting higher particle density. Upon gradual exposure to water, the structure of the micron-sized crystals underwent significant changes. When Cs4PbBr6 crystals came into contact with trace amounts of water, localized erosion occurred, forming hollow or fractured interiors (Fig. 1f), indicating that water molecules penetrated the surface and triggered initial restructuring. As more water was added, the crystals underwent pronounced disintegration, and numerous irregular submicron particles accumulated on their surfaces. Most of these submicron particles precipitated in situ from the remaining columnar crystal segments before complete decomposition (Fig. 1g, dashed area), suggesting that a portion of Cs4PbBr6 underwent a phase transition into a new crystalline structure. This water-induced phase transition is attributed to the continuous penetration of water molecules and the selective dissolution of CsBr, allowing isolated [PbBr6]4- octahedra to form corner-sharing linkages and assemble into a three-dimensional CsPbBr3 lattice (Fig. S6)27. After the phase transition, the submicron particles appeared yellow under optical microscopy due to partial absorption of blue and green light and strong reflection in the yellow region (Fig. S7), consistent with the color of bulk CsPbBr329. In fluorescence microscopy, the areas that came into contact with water emit bright green photoluminescence, with a central wavelength of 527.4 nm and a full width at half maximum of 16.6 nm (Fig. S8). This disintegration occurs in situ; thus, when water evaporates, the dissolved CsBr remains locally. The excess CsBr reacts with surface species on the submicron particles, regenerating Cs4PbBr6 that encapsulates the luminescent CsPbBr3, leading to a gradual fading of green fluorescence in the PL image (Fig. S9). Notably, the regenerated Cs4PbBr6 adheres only to the surface; under strong laser excitation, bright PL from the underlying CsPbBr3 remains observable. When a large amount of water is introduced, CsBr is not only extracted but also gains enhanced mobility-along with [PbBr6]4- octahedra-allowing these species to migrate and recombine at new sites, forming CsPbBr3 micro-wire with high aspect ratios or block-like microcrystals (Fig. 1h). To further confirm the Cs4PbBr6-to-CsPbBr3 phase transition, XRD measurements were performed after water vapor exposure. The emergence of new diffraction peaks characteristic of orthorhombic CsPbBr3 (PDF#18-0364) provides direct structural evidence of the water-induced lattice reconstruction (Fig. S10). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) further confirms the water-induced transformation from Cs4PbBr6 to CsPbBr3. As shown in Fig. S11, the Cs-related peaks markedly decrease after water exposure, while the relative intensities of Pb and Br signals increase, consistent with the selective dissolution of CsBr. The Pb 4 f spectra in both samples correspond to Pb2+, but the peaks in CsPbBr3 are narrower and more symmetric, indicating a more uniform Pb-Br coordination environment. Meanwhile, the Br 3 d binding energy slightly shifts to lower values in CsPbBr3, suggesting enhanced lattice stability due to the reconstruction of the Pb-Br framework. These results provide direct chemical evidence for the selective dissolution and structural reorganization mechanism underlying the water-induced phase transition.

We propose a water-induced phase transition strategy that enables the visualized transformation of Cs4PbBr6 microcrystals into CsPbBr3 microcrystals and microwires. Unlike vacuum or high-energy beam techniques, this method relies solely on the limited contact between atomized water droplets and the crystal surface, allowing the phase transition to be directly monitored in real time under a conventional fluorescence microscope. It is worth emphasizing that water here acts as a reactant. Its strong polarity facilitates the localized dissolution of CsBr and PbBr2 (with distinct solubilities; see Fig. S12d), thereby triggering the Cs4PbBr6-to-CsPbBr3 phase transition. In practice, irregular microdroplets tens of micrometers in size are generated on the sample surface using an atomizer (Fig. S12a-c). The limited amount of water ensures moderate surface fluidity, enabling controlled interfacial evolution, while preventing excessive solvent from causing rapid and irregular growth-thus maintaining the morphological regularity of the resulting structures. Remarkably, the transformation occurs within seconds: CsPbBr3 formation can be observed a few seconds after droplet contact, and complete microwire or microcrystal structures typically form within tens of seconds. By tuning the extent of water exposure, we can effectively control the final morphology of CsPbBr3 while maintaining good reproducibility of the process.

The growth process of micron-sized crystals was visualized using fluorescence microscopy (Fig. 2), along with a schematic illustration of the microscopic transformations occurring during the phase transition. This observation revealed the formation of both linear and block-like CsPbBr3 microcrystals. Although both morphologies originated from the same Cs4PbBr6 micron crystals, their final shapes and growth behaviors exhibited distinct characteristics, reflecting the highly tunable structural evolution. Under sufficiently moist conditions, the Cs4PbBr6 microncrystals underwent vigorous decomposition, releasing large quantities of mobile Cs+, Br+, and [PbBr6]4- octahedral species. Simultaneously, the presence of excess water and trace organic components rendered the system semi-fluid, allowing reactive species to migrate freely within the liquid. After stable nucleation, reactants continued to aggregate and eventually grew into CsPbBr3 micron structures (Fig. 2a). In Fig. 2b, the linear microcrystals rapidly extended in one direction, with sharply increasing PL intensity over a short timescale, indicating clear oriented growth behavior. In contrast, the block-like crystals observed in Fig. 2c displayed a balanced growth trend in multiple directions, with slower growth, clear crystal edges, and stable PL enhancement, reflecting the absence of significant directional control during the growth process, resulting in a nearly isotropic polyhedral expansion. This difference in growth may be due to factors such as concentration gradients, uneven solvent distribution, or surface structure induction within the local environment, causing Cs+ and [PbBr6]4- to accumulate along specific directions, with the crystals growing uniformly in the direction of higher material concentration. Additionally, if the crystal plane surface has a higher energy, the crystallization rate will be faster, and the crystal will preferentially grow along that direction, ultimately forming micron linear one-dimensional structures30,31. Accordingly, introducing trace amounts of oleylamine into the system promotes the formation of linear microcrystals32. When the components are evenly distributed in space, and the crystal morphology tends toward equiaxial, the growth rates of the crystal faces become similar, and the crystals expand outward with low orientation, forming a block-like structure with high symmetry. Notably, these two morphologies often coexist under identical conditions and can even emerge within regions only tens of micrometers apart (Fig. 1h). This suggests that crystal growth is not solely dictated by global reaction parameters but is strongly modulated by local microenvironments such as water distribution, ion gradients, and stress fields. The predominance of microrods over cuboids indicates that, during water-assisted decomposition, CsPbBr3 preferentially grows along specific crystallographic orientations. Such anisotropic growth originates from facet-dependent surface energies and growth rates: microrods extend along low-energy, fast-growing facets, whereas isotropic cuboids form when this preference is suppressed. Time-resolved statistics of micron-wire growth (Fig. 2d) show that the initial growth rate is rapid (~2.35 μm s–1), but stagnation occurs once the length exceeds ~20 μm, indicating a clear limitation in extension. This behavior arises from the interplay of thermodynamic equilibrium and kinetic constraints. First, as the crystal elongates, the growth front moves away from the reactant reservoir, increasing the diffusion distance for Cs+, Br+, and [PbBr6]4- species, which limits the supply of material required for sustained growth. Additionally, during long-range migration, ions may undergo re-coordination, surface adsorption, or form intermediate species, thereby further reducing the effective supply of precursors. Moreover, when the crystal reaches a certain size, the interface free energy difference decreases, and the thermodynamic driving force weakens, making it harder to continue extending and forming a stable structure33. Notably, in PL microscopy images, the front ends of microwire often appear blurry or even truncated. This is not an imaging artifact, but results from vertical (z-axis) growth beyond the microscope’s focal plane. This indirectly reflects the three-dimensional growth nature of the microwire, as opposed to strictly two-dimensional substrate-bound growth. Such vertical orientation imposes spatial constraints, which may also contribute to growth termination. Angle distribution analysis (Fig. 2d) reveals high fluidity of reactive species throughout the entire growth process. Fig. S13 (or Video 1) depicts the growth of microwire parallel to the substrate within a confined water droplet environment. The lengths of the microwire remain confined within the water droplet boundaries, indicating that reactants exert strict control over microcrystal growth. Fig. 2e presents a quantitative comparison of growth rates along the long and short axes of the block-shaped crystals. The data show that although the growth rate along the long axis is slightly higher than that of the short axis, the difference is minimal and the overall growth remains slow. Fig. 2e confirms the non-oriented growth of block-like microcrystals and further suggests that local environmental factors subtly influence growth rates, thereby affecting their final aspect ratios and morphological evolution.

As the water volume increases, the microenvironment becomes more fluid, resulting in significant changes in crystal size, morphology, and spatial confinement characteristics. These changes strongly influence the optical polarization behavior during absorption and emission. Figure 3a presents SEM images of two typical structures: the top shows submicron particles (~hundreds of nanometers) with disordered morphology, while the bottom shows high-aspect-ratio microwire exhibiting uniform directional growth. First, let’s analyze the emission optical behavior of the submicron particles. Figure 3b displays PL emission spectra collected along horizontal and vertical directions, showing no significant intensity difference between the two. Figure 3c shows the angular PL intensity distribution, which is nearly circular-indicating that submicron particles exhibit spatially isotropic emission without polarization preference. Although small in size, these particles do not reach the strong exciton confinement regime, and their excitonic wavefunctions remain sufficiently delocalized. The radiation dipoles are randomly oriented, resulting in macroscopically isotropic emission, similar to that observed in bulk materials34. In contrast, the micro-wire structures exhibit pronounced polarization-dependent behavior in both absorption and emission. Figure 3d shows that varying the polarization direction of the excitation laser induces some change in absorption response, though the overall polarization dependence remains weak. Notably, although the micro-wire features a one-dimensional confinement structure, its absorption involves transitions to higher-energy electronic states, which are relatively random in direction-resulting in reduced sensitivity to polarization orientation35. Therefore, even slender micro-wires exhibit weak polarization dependence in absorption. In contrast, during emission, the micro-wire exhibits pronounced one-dimensional anisotropy. Figure 3e shows that the emission intensity along the wire axis is significantly stronger than in the perpendicular direction, when detected along horizontal and vertical polarization angles. Furthermore, the polar plot in Fig. 3f reveals a typical two-lobed pattern, clearly indicating strong linearly polarized emission. To further investigate the PL properties of CsPbBr3 microcrystals under high excitation power, we performed a detailed analysis of the stimulated emission behavior in block-like microcrystals (Fig. 4). Unlike the one-dimensional micro-wire structures, block-like microcrystals possess larger dimensions in all directions and therefore do not exhibit the polarization response and anisotropic emission commonly associated with structural confinement. However, their more regular geometric shape facilitates internal light oscillation. Figure 4a displays a representative SEM image of a single block-like CsPbBr3 microcrystal, accompanied by its simulated electric field distribution within a rectangular geometry. The simulation reveals pronounced localized electric field enhancement within the crystal, indicating strong optical resonance capability-a prerequisite for stimulated emission and laser oscillation. Experimentally, gradual increases in excitation power led to pronounced changes in the emission spectrum of the block-like microcrystals (Fig. 4b). At low pump powers, only broadband spontaneous emission was observed. However, once the excitation exceeded a threshold, multiple sharp, narrowband peaks emerged abruptly with significantly enhanced intensity. This transition from spontaneous to narrowband stimulated emission confirms the establishment of stimulated emission within the microcrystal and the formation of an effective optical feedback loop for lasing. Unlike typical single-mode lasers44,45, Fig. 4b displays multiple discrete emission peaks, attributed to the large crystal size enabling simultaneous support of multiple standing-wave resonant modes6,46. To further quantify the lasing behavior, Fig. 4c presents the peak intensities of individual modes as functions of excitation power. Each mode exhibits marked nonlinear enhancement once its respective threshold is reached, consistent with typical lasing threshold behavior45. The power threshold (Pth) for the F3 peak is approximately 115 μJ cm−2. The inset presents a Lorentzian fitting of the emission spectrum at a representative pump power of 132 μJ cm–2, where a distinct stimulated emission peak emerges. According to the relation Q = λ/δλ, the cavity quality factor Q is calculated to be 2018. These lasing characteristics are comparable to those reported for perovskite micro- and nanolasers fabricated by other methods47,48. Figure 4d tracks the center wavelength shifts of individual modes as a function of pump power. A general blue shift is observed with increasing power, primarily due to carrier filling and refractive index reduction from local stress relaxation-further supporting the formation of laser emission49. Notably, Fig. S15 presents optical measurements on a smaller block-like microcrystal. As the crystal size decreases, a single narrowband laser mode emerges, exhibiting improved mode stability. This observation confirms the direct correlation between crystal size and mode number: larger crystals support multimode lasing, while smaller ones favor single-mode operation. Size variation modulates standing-wave mode formation by tuning cavity length and boundary conditions50. Conclusion

This work employs a water-induced phase transition strategy to achieve the visualization of the growth process of CsPbBr3 microcrystals and systematically reveals the evolution patterns of microcrystals in terms of morphology evolution, optical anisotropy, and stimulated emission behavior. In addition, this visualization-guided phase transition strategy may also be applicable to other halide perovskites with dissolution-reassembly characteristics, such as CsPbI3 and FA4PbX6, enabling in situ regulation and visualization of phase transitions. Through fluorescence microscopy, the complete process of Cs4PbBr6 microcrystals transforming into CsPbBr3 in a water environment was clearly observed, resulting in the formation of various microstructures with regular morphologies, including sub-micron particles, one-dimensional micro-wires, and block-like microcrystals. The one-dimensional micro-wires exhibited significant linear polarized PL emission, and the polarization degree increased as the crystal size decreased, verifying the controlled evolution of optical anisotropy under size regulation. Under high excitation power, the block-like CsPbBr3 microcrystals demonstrated typical stimulated emission behavior and were capable of supporting multi-mode laser emission in a single crystal. Through electric field simulation and experimental spectral analysis, it was confirmed that the microcrystals exhibit strong optical field confinement and resonance, providing ideal conditions for laser oscillation. Notably, the microcrystals obtained through the visualized growth process can directly realize strong light-matter interactions and trigger efficient laser emission. This not only highlights the close connection between material morphology and optical performance but also provides a practical basis and mechanistic understanding for the design of integrated micro-light sources, polarized light devices, and lasers based on perovskite microstructures. This work demonstrates the complete progression from controllable structural growth to functional laser output, highlighting the potential of perovskite microcrystals for next-generation photonic devices.

Experimental section

Materials and reagents

Cesium carbonate (Cs2CO3, 99.999%), oleic acid (OA, 90%) and 1-octadecene (1-ODE, 90%) were purchased from J&K Scientific. Oleylamine (OAm, 95%) and toluene (99.5%) were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Lead bromide (PbBr2, 99.999%) and methyl acetate (99%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. All reagents were utilized as received without additional purification.

Synthesis of Cs4PbBr6 NCs and micron-sized columnar crystal structures

0.2 mmol of PbBr2 was dissolved in a mixture of 10 mL 1-ODE, 0.4 mL OA, and 3 mL OAm at 120 °C under an air atmosphere, with stirring until fully dissolved. The temperature of the mixture was then lowered to 80 °C, and 1.5 mL of the prepared Cs precursor solution (0.4 g Cs2CO3 dissolved in 8 mL OA at 130 °C in air) was quickly added. After a brief waiting period, the solution turned milky white and was rapidly cooled to room temperature by using an ice-water bath.

The milky white crude solution was centrifuged (6000 rpm, 5 min) and the precipitate was dispersed in 16 mL of toluene to obtain crude Cs4PbBr6 NCs. The obtained sample required further purification. The crude Cs4PbBr6 NCs solution was centrifuged again (6000 rpm, 5 min) to separate the precipitate, and the supernatant was collected. To induce turbidity, 16 mL of methyl acetate was added to the solution, followed by centrifugation (9000 rpm, 5 min) to isolate the precipitate. Finally, the purified Cs4PbBr6 NCs were dispersed in 8 mL of toluene solution. The synthesized Cs4PbBr6 NCs solution was transferred to a 10 ml vial and stored in a refrigerator. After storage for two days, precipitate was observed at the bottom of the vial. The precipitate was homogeneously dispersed in the solution by shaking the vial. When the sample was dropped onto a substrate, self-assembled columnar microcrystals could be observed.

Characterization

X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of Cs4PbBr6 NCs and micron-sized columnar crystal structures were recorded using X-ray diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation over a 2θ range of 10° to 50°, operating at 40 kV and 40 mA by Bruker D8 Discover. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) were acquired by Zeiss Gemini500 microscope. Absorption spectra were measured by a HITACHI UV-Vis-NIR UH4150 absorption spectrophotometer. The spectra were measured using a spectrometer (Horiba, iHR320, grating: 600 lines/mm). PL images were acquired using a confocal fluorescence microscope (BX50, Olympus) equipped with a broadband white light source. A dichroic mirror filter set was applied to transmit excitation wavelengths below 450 nm. The objective lens used was 150× (NA = 0.9), with an exposure time of 200 ms and constant detector gain for all measurements. A custom-built confocal luminescence microscopy system was employed. The femtosecond laser (1030 nm, 290 fs, 100 kHz) can be tuned to a wavelength of 400 nm by using an optical parametric amplifier (OPA). The excitation laser was focused by a microscope objective (50X) to an 8 μm spot on the sample.
