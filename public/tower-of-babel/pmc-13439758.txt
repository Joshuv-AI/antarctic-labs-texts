ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlzheimer’s disease (AD) research produces extensive genomic and clinical data, yet general large language models (LLMs) often generate inaccurate or superficial outputs. We introduce AD-GPT, a domain-specific framework for reliable information retrieval and synthesis of AD-related knowledge.MethodsWe integrated curated genomic resources, including cis-eQTL and sQTL data across 13 brain regions from GTEx, genomic location information from NCBI, and gene function annotations from OMIM, together with approximately 150,000 AD-related publications from NCBI’s PubMed. AD-GPT adopts a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) workflow with task-specific database partitioning, a BERT-based query router, and fine-tuned Llama models, augmented with router and context verifiers to validate task assignment and evidence relevance, supporting three tasks: genetic information retrieval, association study reasoning, and general AD-related knowledge synthesis.ResultsAD-GPT consistently outperformed strong baseline LLMs in evidence-grounded evaluation metrics across all tasks, including factual consistency, citation validity, and instruction-level faithfulness. Task-specific retrieval and stacked routing improved evidence grounding and substantially reduced hallucination in complex AD-related queries.ConclusionAD-GPT harmonizes curated genomic databases with biomedical literature, offering a scalable and accurate informatics tool to advance AD research.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12911-026-03579-x.

Background

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder that profoundly impairs memory and cognition [1]. As the global burden of AD rises, large-scale studies have generated multi-modal data across neuroimaging, genomics, and clinical domains [2]. These datasets have revealed key biomarkers and mechanisms of AD, yet efficiently integrating the massive literature and databases remains challenging.

Information retrieval (IR) is an important tool that focuses on the identification and extraction of relevant information from vast datasets or document collections [3]. In the context of AD research, IR plays a pivotal role by enabling efficient access to critical data, thereby supporting a wide array of research applications. For instance, IR facilitates the retrieval of biomarker data, such as beta-amyloid plaque levels or tau protein concentrations, which provide valuable insights into disease mechanisms and enhance early diagnostic capabilities [4]. Additionally, IR is instrumental in accessing medical imaging data from repositories, allowing researchers to track and analyze patterns of brain atrophy and functional changes over time [5]. Furthermore, IR aids in identifying genetic studies [6], which could include those investigating APOE polymorphisms, which are strongly linked to AD risk. Therefore, IR is essential for navigating the growing body of AD research, ensuring that crucial data is accessible for advancing our understanding and treatment of AD.

Large Language Models (LLMs) are advanced tools in natural language processing (NLP) that have demonstrated remarkable capabilities across various domains. These models, such as the GPT (Generative Pre-trained Transformer) series and Llama, are designed to understand and generate human-like text, which makes them particularly effective for addressing challenges in IR [7]. In particular, LLM-based IR can streamline the process of retrieving complex biomedical databases, such as clinical records that focus on the relationships between various phenotypes and genotypes, making it an invaluable tool for AD researchers [8]. Although LLMs have demonstrated broad applicability in IR within the medical domain [9–12], they also exhibit several critical limitations. One of the most significant concerns is their propensity for hallucination, a phenomenon in which models generate responses that appear confident yet are factually incorrect or nonsensical [13]. This issue becomes particularly pronounced in tasks requiring domain-specific expertise, such as medical and legal inquiries, where LLMs frequently produce fabricated information presented with unwarranted certainty [14]. Beyond hallucination, LLMs often fall short in providing depth and comprehensiveness, particularly in specialized contexts. While models like ChatGPT can generate largely accurate responses, they are frequently criticized for their lack of nuanced understanding. For instance, in the field of epilepsy, LLM-generated content is often superficial, failing to capture the intricacies of the condition [15]. Similarly, in genetics, empirical evaluations suggest that ChatGPT’s performance is comparable to that of human respondents, offering no clear advantage in accuracy or insight [16]. These limitations largely stem from the models’ constrained exposure to medical-domain knowledge and their inherent difficulty in navigating the complexities of clinical reasoning.

To address these limitations, prior work has enhanced LLMs by pretraining or fine-tuning them on biomedical corpora. Early efforts such as Meditron [10], PubMedBERT [17], and BioGPT [18] demonstrated that domain-specific training improves biomedical language understanding. However, these models do not incorporate RAG, limiting their ability to provide verifiable citations for complex, knowledge-intensive queries. The recent work MedReason [19] has advanced medical reasoning capabilities in LLMs. Nevertheless, hallucination remains a significant challenge in citation-grounded settings, as generated answers may lack reliable or verifiable supporting references, constraining their usefulness for evidence-sensitive biomedical research.

Motivated by these observations, we introduce AD-GPT, an evidence-grounded retrieval and reasoning framework for AD. AD-GPT integrates curated AD-relevant genomic resources with literature retrieval and task-routed large language models to facilitate evidence-driven exploration and synthesis (see Fig. 1). The workflow begins with the curation of high-quality datasets from public resources, including integrated cis-eQTL and sQTL data across 13 brain regions from the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) Project [20], genomic location data from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) [21], and gene function annotations from the Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM) [22], alongside about 150,000 AD-related academic publications from NCBI’s PubMed. To ensure factual accuracy, AD-GPT adopts a RAG framework, with vector databases partitioned by task type. User queries are categorized into three task types: Task 1 focuses on genetic information retrieval using curated gene databases (e.g., genomic locations, gene functions); Task 2 synthesizes findings from association studies, emphasizing statistical evidence, biological mechanisms, and region-specific brain effects; and Task 3 addresses broader AD-related knowledge, including diagnostic tools and ethical considerations. This task-based structure enhances both relevance and retrieval efficiency. A BERT-based classifier routes each query to the appropriate fine-tuned Llama model, enabling precise, context-aware responses. For deployment, the entire system is containerized using Docker, with a FastAPI backend and an interactive HTML-based frontend, ensuring scalability, usability, and seamless integration into research and clinical workflows.Fig. 1Overview of the AD-GPT workflow. we construct our own AD-specific databases from multiple public resources and apply RAG to identify relevant data. Methods

Data collection

We constructed a comprehensive dataset centered around 142 experimentally validated AD-related genes [23]. These genes serve as the core framework for downstream analyses, with additional candidates expected to be incorporated in future updates.

Building on this foundation, we systematically curated and harmonized data from three authoritative sources: GTEx, OMIM, and NCBI. The GTEx project provides extensive data on expression and splicing quantitative trait loci (eQTLs and sQTLs) across 13 human brain regions, including the frontal cortex, amygdala, anterior cingulate cortex, caudate, cerebellum, nucleus accumbens, putamen, spinal cord, cortex, hypothalamus, hippocampus, and substantia nigra. These data, processed with FastQTL [24] following the GTExPortal pipeline [20], reveal region-specific variant effects, facilitating the identification of potential causal variants and regionally relevant therapeutic targets for AD [25]. OMIM contributes detailed gene-level functional annotations, including molecular mechanisms and inheritance patterns, while NCBI provides genomic coordinates (chromosome, start, end, strand) derived from multiple genome assemblies, including GRCh38.p14, T2T-CHM13v2.0, and GRCh37.p14. Together, these resources form a unified static genomic database, offering reliable structural and functional insights critical for AD research.

Recognizing the rapid evolution of AD research, we incorporated dynamic literature data retrieved from NCBI’s PubMed database. To ensure the reliability and scholarly quality of retrieved evidence, AD-GPT restricts literature retrieval to the MEDLINE subset of PubMed. MEDLINE indexes journals selected through a rigorous scientific and editorial review process conducted by the U.S. National Library of Medicine (NLM) and consists of peer-reviewed biomedical literature [26]. Using the NCBI E-utilities API, we retrieved MEDLINE-indexed records containing the keyword “Alzheimer’s disease,” resulting in approximately 150,000 publications published between January 2000 and January 2026. For each record, we extracted structured metadata, including PMID, article title, abstract, keywords, journal title, and publication type, which were subsequently parsed and organized for downstream retrieval and evidence synthesis.

In summary, our two-tiered data framework comprises a static database that integrates GTEx QTLs, OMIM gene functions, and NCBI genomic data, alongside a dynamic literature database sourced from PubMed. Corpora construction

To capture intrinsic relationships across multiple datasets, we constructed specialized corpora designed for multi-task, multi-level genomics fine-tuning. All datasets adhere to a standardized instruction-tuning format for Llama models, consisting of three components: a system prompt, an input query, and a response. The system prompt is consistently framed as: “You are a bioinformatics expert. Based on the following instruction, provide an accurate and professional response.”

Task 1 focuses on gene-centric knowledge representation. The dataset integrates: (i) gene symbols with chromosomal locations from NCBI, formatted into various text-based question–answer pairs, such as “On which chromosome is {gene symbol} located?”; (ii) eQTL and sQTL associations between AD-related genes and brain regions, curated from GTEx, where gene–region links are defined by significant variants (with a q-value threshold) that affect expression or splicing; and (iii) concise gene function descriptions from OMIM, enabling responses to queries like “What is the basic function of {gene symbol}?”. Through supervised fine-tuning, these components train AD-GPT to deliver structured, interpretable, and evidence-based outputs on genetic and functional annotations.

Tasks 2 and 3 expand the scope of AD-GPT to domain-level reasoning, enabling it to handle more complex and nuanced queries related to AD. In particular, Task 3 addresses broad AD-related topics that go beyond gene-specific retrieval (Task 1) and verdict generation on gene-AD associations (Task 2). It encompasses a broad spectrum of question types, spanning disease fundamentals and clinical presentation, risk and pathogenesis, epidemiology and population-level disparities, etc.

Model architecture

AD-GPT is implemented as a mixture-of-experts (MoE) [27] framework designed to support heterogeneous and safety-sensitive queries in AD research. The system explicitly separates task routing, evidence retrieval, and response generation, and introduces verification mechanisms at both the routing and retrieval stages to reduce misclassification, weak evidence grounding, and hallucinated outputs (See Fig. 2).Fig. 2AD-GPT system overview with task routing, evidence verification, and abstention to prevent misrouting and hallucination

AD-GPT system overview with task routing, evidence verification, and abstention to prevent misrouting and hallucination

MoE-based task routing. Incoming user queries are first processed by a lightweight BERT-based classifier that assigns each query to one of several predefined AD-specific tasks, including genetic information retrieval, association study reasoning, general AD knowledge synthesis, or a reject option. Each task is handled by an expert model within the MoE architecture. This design allows task-specialized reasoning while avoiding interference between fundamentally different query types.

Router verifier. To mitigate routing errors and unsupported requests, AD-GPT incorporates a router verifier that evaluates whether an incoming query can be reliably mapped to one of the supported tasks. When the initial task assignment confidence is insufficient, the system attempts to refine the query by enriching it with task-appropriate contextual cues and re-evaluates the routing decision. Only when no suitable task can be identified after refinement does the system refuse to answer. This mechanism reduces misrouting and prevents instruction-level hallucination arising from ill-posed or out-of-scope queries.

Evidence-grounded retrieval and context verifier. For queries that pass routing verification, AD-GPT performs retrieval-augmented generation using a domain-specific knowledge base curated from authoritative AD resources, including OMIM, NCBI Gene, GTEx QTL annotations, and approximately 150,000 AD-related publications from PubMed. Task-specific collections are used to ensure that retrieved evidence remains aligned with the intended query type.

A context verifier is applied after retrieval to evaluate the relevance and adequacy of the retrieved documents. When retrieved evidence fails to meet predefined relevance or quality thresholds, the system triggers a query refinement and re-retrieval step to improve evidence alignment. If no sufficient supporting evidence can be identified after refinement, the system explicitly abstains from answering rather than relying on parametric model knowledge. Evaluation methodology

The evaluation of AD-GPT was conducted using task-specific metrics designed to assess the response performance. Given the distinct nature of each task, we employed different evaluation approaches: automated accuracy assessment for Task 1, which has ground-truth answers, and hallucination assessment for Tasks 2 and 3, which involve complex reasoning and synthesis.

Task 1: Accuracy assessment

Task 1 focuses on gene information retrieval, including gene aliases, genomic coordinates, gene functions, and brain region-specific expression quantitative trait loci. These queries have objectively verifiable answers derived from our curated databases. For Task 1, we use “Accuracy” as the primary evaluation metric, defined as: \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{Accuracy} = \frac{\text{Number of Correct Responses}}{\text{Total Number of Queries}}$$\end{document}

We constructed test sets containing 216 queries for genomic location information and 1,014 queries for gene function and eQTL/sQTL associations. Each model generated responses to all test queries, and responses were automatically compared against ground-truth values using exact string matching for coordinates and gene symbols or semantic similarity with standard answers for gene function descriptions.

Tasks 2 and 3: Hallucination assessment

Tasks 2 and 3 involve complex text generation and multi-evidence reasoning, where no single ground-truth answer exists. As such, these tasks cannot be reliably evaluated using simple accuracy-based metrics. Instead, evaluation must focus on factuality, citation validity, instruction adherence, and evidence-grounded reasoning, which are core concerns in clinical and biomedical LLM assessment [28].

Expert annotation protocol

For the expert-assessed metrics (CSR, CVR, and Faithfulness), evaluation was conducted by three domain experts with backgrounds in AD research. For each query, each of the three experts independently reviewed all generated contexts of LLMs and provided a score in [0,1] for each of the three metrics according to the criteria defined above. The final score for each query was obtained by averaging across the three experts to mitigate potential individual bias. All experts were blinded to model identity throughout the evaluation process. Inter-expert agreement was assessed at the query level using the intraclass correlation coefficient, ICC(2,k) [31], where the “2” denotes a two-way random-effects ANOVA and “k” denotes that the reliability is estimated for the mean rating across k experts. We chose this setting because (i) the same set of experts evaluated every query, which corresponds to a two-way ANOVA; and (ii) the final metric score reported in our evaluation is the mean across the experts. The resulting ICC(2,k) values were 0.833 for Faithfulness, 0.935 for CSR, and 0.933 for CVR, indicating excellent inter-expert agreement [32]. See Supplementary Material 1 for detailed results.

Statistical analysis

For ablation and comparative evaluations, primary statistical analyses were performed at the metric level to quantify performance differences between AD-GPT and baseline models. In the ablation study (Section 3.1), comparisons were conducted using paired-sample statistical tests. Specifically, paired t-tests were applied to compare the difference between metric values (CSR, CVR, AR, and Faithfulness) across matched queries. Effect sizes were quantified using Cohen’s d. Statistical significance was assessed using two-sided tests with a significance threshold of p-value ≤ 0.05.

Graphical interface

Our graphical interface provides an intuitive way to interact with deployed models. All our models and FastAPI backend files are stored in a Docker container, making deployment as simple as starting the Docker instance. Once the container is running, we can easily use an HTML-based chat interface to communicate with the models. This setup ensures a smooth workflow, where the backend efficiently processes requests while the frontend delivers a seamless user experience. With this approach, we can quickly deploy, scale, and interact with our models in a structured and efficient manner.

Results

In this section, we present a comprehensive empirical evaluation of AD-GPT. We first conduct ablation studies to quantify the contributions of key system components, including the router verification module, task-specific fine-tuning, and retrieval-augmented generation, with a particular focus on evidence grounding and citation reliability. We then benchmark AD-GPT against a diverse set of state-of-the-art general-purpose and biomedical large language models across three tasks.

Ablation study

We first evaluated the router verification module using 100 manually designed queries, including 80 short or ambiguous phrases and 20 detailed queries. Without verification, the initial routing accuracy was 60%. After introducing the router verifier with automatic query refinement, the correct routing rate increased to 96%, demonstrating the effectiveness of verification-driven task assignment.

For Task 1, the base expert model was Llama3.2–3B without domain-specific fine-tuning. The base model achieved an accuracy of 32% on genetic information queries. After fine-tuning on AD-specific genomic data, AD-GPT substantially improved performance, achieving an accuracy of 90.84%, highlighting the importance of domain adaptation for genetic information retrieval.

Benchmark comparsion

These models vary in terms of scale and optimization strategies, offering a diverse benchmark to assess their biomedical knowledge retrieval and reasoning capabilities.

In Task 1, our base AD-GPT outperformed all competing models across all evaluated metrics (see Fig. 3a). Notably, AD-GPT achieved an accuracy of 90.84%, substantially exceeding the performance of Qwen2.5 (17.14%), Llama2-70B (59.59%), Llama3.1-70B (70.52%), and Claude3.5-Haiku (74.28%), while also demonstrating a clear advantage over ChatGPT-o1 (85.33%). Although ChatGPT-o1 performed relatively well on structured inputs, its accuracy declined when handling shorter or more ambiguous queries. Similarly, Llama3.2-70B exhibited significant difficulty with loosely structured inputs, leading to inconsistent performance. Discussion

Model and metric selection for performance comparison were conducted in advance. For Task 1, we limited the comparison to LLMs with at least some demonstrated understanding of genetic concepts. Smaller models, including Llama3.1-7B, Gemini1.5-Flash, and DeepSeek-8B, consistently struggled with these queries, often generating indecisive or inaccurate responses.

Based on our model evaluation, AD-GPT consistently delivered more decisive responses and generated more accurate, verifiable references. Our comparative analysis showed that while state-of-the-art LLMs, such as ChatGPT-5.2 and Claude4.5-Sonnet, exhibited impressive versatility across a range of tasks, they also displayed significant limitations in domain-specific expertise. Despite their large parameter counts, these models often relied heavily on web searches and external data sources to improve reasoning accuracy and reference reliability [41]. This dependence on online access introduced notable inconsistencies, particularly when addressing specialized biomedical knowledge. Although the responses generated by these models were often logically structured and contextually relevant, they frequently lacked decisiveness and included ambiguous or hedged statements [13]. More concerning, when confronted with knowledge gaps, these models often produced fabricated references, i.e., citations that were either non-existent or inaccurately attributed. Such behavior risks the dissemination of misleading information, raising serious concerns about their reliability in research and decision-making contexts (see Fig. 4). In contrast, AD-GPT delivered precise, well-reasoned responses grounded exclusively in a curated, domain-specific database. Unlike conventional LLMs, AD-GPT did not generate fictitious references, ensuring that all citations were verifiable and directly traceable to authoritative sources. This core advantage positions AD-GPT as a more dependable tool for specialized knowledge retrieval, particularly in high-stakes applications where accuracy, trustworthiness, and source integrity are critical.

We achieved easy deployment and data transparency with AD-GPT. Unlike large-scale LLMs such as the ChatGPT series, AD-GPT functions as a fully self-contained system with a compact architecture. This design enables secure, on-premise deployment, faster inference, and low latency, making it well-suited for real-time applications in both clinical and research environments. The knowledge base of AD-GPT was built from a rigorously curated dataset specifically tailored to AD. In contrast to models that rely on broad-spectrum training corpora or dynamic web searches, our system leverages RAG to ensure that all embedded knowledge is derived from validated, authoritative sources. This database-driven approach not only enhances the reliability of the model’s outputs but also supports explicit citation of references, a critical feature for clinical decision support and biomedical research. By providing transparent, evidence-based responses, AD-GPT aligns with the stringent standards of medical and scientific domains, offering a trustworthy tool for domain experts who require both accuracy and accountability.

Limitations and safe use of AD-GPT

While AD-GPT demonstrates the potential of retrieval-augmented LLM for evidence-grounded exploration of AD related knowledge, including curated genomic resources and biomedical literature, several important limitations should be acknowledged.

First, AD-GPT is not designed as a clinical decision-making or diagnostic tool. Its outputs are intended to support genetic information query or exploratory literature retrieval, rather than to provide patient-specific risk assessments or clinical recommendations. Accordingly, all conclusions generated by AD-GPT should be interpreted by domain experts and considered in conjunction with primary sources and established clinical guidelines.

Second, as a retrieval-augmented system, the quality and reliability of AD-GPT’s outputs are inherently dependent on the coverage, quality, and consistency of the retrieved evidence. Incomplete, outdated, or conflicting evidence may lead to partial, ambiguous, or inconclusive summaries [42]. Moreover, while our current evaluation framework focuses on hallucination-related criteria such as factuality, citation validity, and instruction-level faithfulness, it does not constitute a comprehensive assessment of broader considerations, including bias, fairness, or equity. These factors are particularly important in healthcare and biomedical research, where large language models may reflect or amplify biases present in underlying datasets [43]. For these reasons, AD-GPT should not be used as a standalone clinical decision support system. Instead, it is intended to serve as a research-oriented tool for structured evidence exploration and synthesis in AD research, with all outputs subject to expert interpretation and validation.

Third, evaluating the generated answers of LLMs in the biomedical domain remains challenging. Expert evaluation is generally considered more reliable for assessing factuality and evidence grounding, and we therefore adopted a multi-expert evaluation for our three core metrics (CSR, CVR, and Faithfulness). However, expert evaluation is inherently costly and difficult to scale to large-scale benchmarks. To complement the expert assessment with an automatic metric, our framework incorporates the AR metric. Although AR involves an LLM to generate auxiliary questions from the model’s output, the LLM serves solely as a question reformulator rather than as a judge, and the actual scoring is determined by a separate embedding model through deterministic cosine similarity computation. Compared with fully LLM-as-judge evaluation paradigms [44], this design substantially mitigates the circularity concern; however, systematic biases shared across language models may still influence the reformulation step and propagate into the final scores.

Fourth, regarding baseline coverage in benchmark comparison, several established biomedical LLMs, including Meditron [10], PubMedBERT [17], and Med-PaLM [43], were not included in our quantitative comparison because these models do not incorporate RAG and therefore cannot produce verifiable citations, making them incompatible with our citation-grounded evaluation framework. This exclusion does not imply that these models perform poorly in other evaluation settings. More broadly, a fair and comprehensive comparison of biomedical LLMs remains an unresolved challenge. Different models are designed for different objectives and evaluated under different protocols, and each evaluation strategy introduces its own biases. Developing a standardized, fair, and reproducible evaluation pipeline for biomedical LLMs remains an important gap for future research.

Future work

Future extensions of AD-GPT will focus on expanding both the modalities of evidence and the adaptivity of reasoning mechanisms, with an emphasis on AD specific research workflows.

Multi-modal Evidence Integration. While the current version of AD-GPT primarily integrates genomic resources (e.g., OMIM, GTEx QTLs, NCBI gene annotations) and biomedical literature, future work will incorporate additional modalities from established AD cohorts. In particular, we plan to integrate neuroimaging features from the ADNI [45] database, including MRI and PET derived measurements, alongside SNP features processed using standard PLINK pipelines [46]. This will enable AD-GPT to reason jointly over genetic, imaging, and clinical evidence, supporting more comprehensive hypothesis exploration in AD research.

Adaptive Reasoning via Reinforcement Learning. We also plan to explore Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) strategies to enhance the precision and adaptability of AD-GPT’s reasoning behavior [47]. Inspired by recent advances such as Guided Reinforcement with Preference Optimization (GRPO) based on comparative evaluations rather than fixed demonstrations [48], future versions of AD-GPT may leverage expert feedback and task-specific rewards to refine evidence selection, reasoning depth, and abstention decisions.

Toward Robust Multi-modal Reasoning. While recent advances have demonstrated the promise of reinforcement learning for multi-modal reasoning, important challenges remain, including sparse and delayed reward signals [49], reliance on predominantly text-centric supervision [50], and the substantial computational cost of stabilizing RL training in large multi-modal models [36]. These challenges motivate the development of robust multi-modal reasoning frameworks that more effectively integrate genetic, neuroimaging, and clinical evidence. Future efforts of AD-GPT will focus on well-designed RL paradigms, improved cross-modal supervision strategies, and task-specific reward formulations that explicitly encourage faithful and interpretable evidence integration in Alzheimer’s disease research.

Conclusion

In this study, we introduced AD-GPT, a domain-specific large language model designed to integrate curated genomic datasets and PubMed literature through an RAG workflow. By partitioning databases into task-oriented modules and utilizing a stacked model for query classification, AD-GPT demonstrates superior accuracy and reliability compared to general-purpose LLMs. Encapsulated within a scalable and user-friendly platform, AD-GPT offers both researchers and clinicians an efficient tool for genetic information retrieval and evidence synthesis in the context of AD. Future directions will focus on expanding the genomic scope, incorporating multi-modal data, and enhancing clinical interpretability, all aimed at further supporting precision medicine and translational research in AD.
