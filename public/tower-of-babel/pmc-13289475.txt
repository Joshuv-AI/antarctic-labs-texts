Genome-wide association study identifies genetic loci for soybean herbicide resistance in maize
Authors: Bao Chen, Pingfang Xiao, Xiaobing Jiang, Yulin Jiang, Yifei Xiao, Liang Tu, Pengfei Liu, Xiangyang Guo, Angui Wang, Yunfang Zhu, Zehui Chen, Xun Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMaize-soybean strip relay intercropping has become a key strategy for ensuring national grain and oil security and promoting sustainable agricultural development. However, weed control in this system remains challenging, and there is a severe shortage of maize germplasm resistant to herbicides commonly applied in soybean fields, which hinders the efficient implementation of this cropping model. In this work, a panel of 170 elite inbred lines and 735 landraces were evaluated for their responses to five soybean herbicides applied at the seedling stage. Chlorophyll content and injury rate were assessed after treatment.ResultsThe results revealed significant differences in herbicide-induced injury: chloransulam and lactofen caused relatively mild damage, while imazamox and clomazone resulted in the most severe injury. Population structure analysis classified the materials into nine inbred line subgroups and 12 landrace subgroups, indicating rich genetic diversity. Genome-wide association study identified 271 quantitative trait nucleotides (QTNs) associated with herbicide tolerance. A meta-analysis of QTNs further detected 10 consensus loci. Finally, a functional validation was performed for the chloransulam tolerance locus PZE-104,050,858, located at Chr4_S_87070067 on chromosome 4, where the G allele was significantly associated with tolerance.ConclusionThis study provides important genetic insights into maize tolerance to soybean herbicides and establishes a practical foundation for germplasm screening and marker-assisted breeding, thereby supporting the sustainable advancement of maize-soybean strip intercropping system.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-08780-8.

Introduction

Maize is a crucial grain crop in China, whereas soybeans function as an essential source of both edible oil and plant protein. However, the nation’s substantial dependence on imports to satisfy domestic consumption needs presents a notable threat to the food security [1, 2]. In China’s major grain-producing regions, maize and soybean cultivation compete for arable land. To address this dual challenge, the maize-soybean intercropping system has been demonstrated as an effective technical solution [3–5]. Optimizing row and plant spacing via a wide-narrow row alternate planting technique establishes a strip-based intercropping system, ultimately achieving the agronomic goal of harvesting both maize and soybean in a single season [6]. Furthermore, the maize-soybean intercropping system enhances soil stability, promotes nutrient uptake and root development, and ultimately improves crop yield [7–10]. Therefore, this system ensures the maize yield while generating an additional harvest of soybean and offers a practical pathway for the coordinated increase of grain and oil crop production [11–14].

However, effective weed control is critically important in the maize-soybean intercropping system. If weeds are not controlled promptly, severe infestations can occur [15], causing annual yield losses of 30–40% in maize and over 50% in soybeans in China [16, 17]. In current agricultural production, the absence of a compatible post-emergence herbicide regimen applicable to both maize and soybean has resulted in high weed control costs within the strip intercropping system, severely dampening farmers’ enthusiasm for its adoption. In maize-soybean intercropping systems, the two plant species exhibit interspecific differences in morphological architecture and physiological processes, leading to divergent capacities for enzymatic metabolism of herbicides, detoxification efficiency, and consequently, differential herbicide tolerance [18]. Currently, most herbicides developed for mixed cropping systems involving graminaceous and leguminous crops are soil-applied herbicides, primarily used pre-planting or pre-emergence [19]. In contrast, foliar-applied herbicides are typically designed for either monocot or dicot crops and are generally safe for only one of the two crop types [20]. Herbicides designed for soybean are generally unsafe for maize plants [21–24]. Therefore, screening and developing herbicide-tolerant maize germplasm, particularly with resistance to soybean herbicides, are of paramount importance.

Previous studies have successfully developed germplasm with multiple herbicide tolerance using various transgenic approaches. The novel W548M mutation in the OsALS gene confers high-level, broad-spectrum resistance to ALS-inhibiting herbicides in rice [25]. A CRISPR-associated protein 9 (CRISPR/Cas9)-mediated homology-directed repair (HDR) gene editing approach was employed to introduce the OsALS G628W mutation, which confers resistance to plant sulfonylureas in rice [26]. The adenine base editor WhieABE confers high dinitroaniline herbicide resistance in wheat by performing multiplexed A-to-G editing on tubulin alleles [27]. EMS mutagenesis-generated multiple mutant taals alleles confer high, dose-dependent resistance to imidazolinone herbicides in wheat [28]. Introducing the synergistic double mutation in AHAS genes via iterative EMS mutagenesis can generate rapeseed germplasm exhibiting synergistically enhanced resistance to sulfonylurea herbicides [29]. Introducing the synergistic combination of G2-aroA and gat genes can generate transgenic tobacco plants exhibiting significantly enhanced tolerance to glyphosate herbicide [30]. Introducing the foreign tfdA gene can generate transgenic maize lines exhibiting high resistance to 2,4-D [31]. Stable glyphosate-tolerant maize lines, designated as GATIPS-mZmEPSPS, were developed using CRISPR/Cas9-mediated site-specific gene editing. This was achieved through a process involving the knock-out of conserved regions and the knock-in of a homologous donor template to introduce triple amino acid substitutions into the native Zea mays 5-enolpyruvylshikimate-3-phosphate synthase (ZmEPSPS) gene [32]. Redesign of the bacterial EPSPS gene (AM79 aroA) produced a synthetic gene, mAM79, that granted transgenic maize high glyphosate tolerance and the capacity to survive fourfold the standard field application rate [33]. Furthermore, hybrid stacking of the two transgenic maize lines, ZDRF8 and nCX-1, successfully produced a stacked line (ZDRF8 × nCX-1) with a combined resistance against glyphosate, chlorsulfuron, 2-methyl-4-chlorophenoxyacetic acid (MCPA) herbicides, and the Asian corn borer [34]. Furthermore, mutation of the maize ALS gene can confer resistance to sulfonylurea herbicides [35, 36]. These studies provide fundamental genetic resources and transformation strategies for multi-target herbicide resistance breeding in maize. Nevertheless, the commercial application of transgenic herbicide-resistant maize remains restricted in China, highlighting an urgent need to evaluate and utilize non-transgenic herbicide-tolerant germplasm. Therefore, it is essential to screen or create maize germplasm with resistance/tolerance to soybean herbicides, identify the underlying genetic regions, and develop functional molecular markers. Such efforts will significantly accelerate the breeding of herbicide-tolerant maize varieties through marker-assisted selection, thereby effectively addressing the challenges of high cost and difficulties in weed control in maize–soybean strip intercropping systems.

Leveraging findings from the third national maize germplasm census, this study established a collection comprising a landrace population and an association panel that broadly captures the genetic diversity of maize germplasm. A total of 170 accessions from the association panel and 735 landrace accessions were genotyped using the MaizeSNP50 array and systematically phenotyped for their response to five soybean herbicides that are currently approved for dual application in both maize and soybean fields, including chloransulam, imazamox, flumioxazin, lactofen, clomazone [37]. We identified elite herbicide-resistant maize germplasm and characterized genomic regions underlying herbicide tolerance to facilitate molecular-assisted breeding, and validated the SNP significantly associated with herbicide resistance.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and herbicide treatment

This study utilized two independent maize populations: an association panel consisting of 170 elite inbred lines, and a landrace population comprising 735 accessions originating from multiple provinces across China (Sichuan: 252; Guizhou: 215; Yunnan: 125; Shaanxi: 62; Chongqing: 51; Hubei: 14; Gansu: 9; Guangxi: 5; Guangdong: 2). Both populations were genotyped using the MaizeSNP50 array, which contains approximately 100,000 single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) markers. Herbicide resistance-related traits were evaluated for all accessions under different herbicide treatments in a greenhouse at the Institute of Upland Food Crops, Guizhou Academy of Agricultural Sciences (Huaxi District, Guiyang, Guizhou Province).

Maize seeds were sown in seedling trays containing a 1:1 (v/v) mixture of vermiculite and nutrient-rich soil, with five replicates per genotype per herbicide treatment. Plants were grown under standard greenhouse conditions with a day/night temperature regime of 30 °C/26°C and natural illumination throughout the experiment. At the three-leaf stage, five soybean-specific herbicides were applied, with an untreated group included as control. The herbicides were applied at field-recommended concentrations as follows: chloransulam at 0.07 g/L, imazamox at 2.5 mL /L, flumioxazin at 2 mL/L, lactofen at 0.5 mL/ L, and clomazone at 5 mL/ L.

Phenotypic evaluation of herbicide resistance

Herbicide injury was assessed 14 days after treatment using a standardized injury rating scale (Table 1), and resistance levels were classified according to the established evaluation criteria (Table 2) [38]. The injury rate was calculated using the following formula: X(%)=[∑༈N×S༉/༈T×M༉]×100%, where X represents the injury rate, N denotes the number of injured plants at a given injury level, S is the corresponding injury level score, T is the total number of plants evaluated, and M stands for the maximum injury level score. Based on the resulting values, resistance levels were classified according to established evaluation criteria.

Genotyping

The genotypic data for the 170 inbred lines comprising the association panel were previously generated by our research group [39]. For the 735 landrace accessions, genomic DNA was extracted using the CTAB method [40]. Qualified DNA samples were normalized to a working concentration of 100 ng/µL and sent to Beijing Compson Corporation for genotyping. For the raw data, Single Nucleotide Polymorphism (SNP) markers were excluded if they had a missing rate > 20%, a minor allele frequency (MAF) < 5%, or no defined physical position in the B73 reference genome.

Population structure and kinship analysis

Pairwise kinship coefficients among accessions were calculated using R version 4.3.2. Population structure was analyzed separately for the 170-accession association panel and the 735 landrace accessions using ADMIXTURE 1.3.0 [41]. The number of ancestral populations (K) was set from 1 to 12, with the optimal K value was determined as the one yielding the lowest cross-validation error, indicating the most probable number of subpopulations [42]. Based on the Q matrix from ADMIXTURE analysis, accessions were assigned to specific subpopulations when their membership coefficient was ≥ 0.8; individuals with coefficients below this threshold were considered admixed individuals and were excluded from subsequent subpopulation-specific analyses. This approach strengthens the validation of population structure inference and ensures clearer genetic clustering.

Linkage disequilibrium (LD) analysis

Linkage disequilibrium (LD) analysis was performed using RTM-GWAS V1.1 software. The LD decay distance for each chromosome was determined by calculating the physical distance at which the average pairwise squared correlation coefficient (r²) dropped to half of its maximum value [43]. LD decay plots were generated using OriginPro 2021.

Genome-Wide Association Study (GWAS)

Genome-wide association analysis was performed by integrating phenotypic and genotypic data using the Fixed and Random Model Circulating Probability Unification (FarmCPU) method implemented in the GAPIT3.0 package (R version 4.4.2) [44]. Based on the Bonferroni correction, significance thresholds were set at 2.31 × 10⁻⁴ for the association panel and 7.87 × 10⁻⁵ for the landrace population to identify statistically significant marker-trait associations.

Genome-wide meta-analysis and molecular marker development

A genome-wide meta-analysis was performed using METAL to combine multiple GWAS results and identify key quantitative trait nucleotides (QTNs) controlling herbicide resistance. Following the method described by Sassou [45], a significance threshold of 5.87 × 10⁻⁵ was applied to detect statistically significant associations. Based on the GWAS mapping results, consensus loci were selected for further analysis. Sanger sequencing was employed to detect allelic variations at these target loci across different accessions. By integrating phenotypic evaluation data, the genetic effects of these loci were elucidated, leading to the validation of a SNP significantly associated with herbicide resistance.

Results

Statistical analysis of maize resistance to soybean herbicides

To identify maize germplasm resistant to soybean-specific herbicides, plants at the three-leaf stage were sprayed with clomazone, cloransulam, imazamox, flumioxazin, and lactofen. The chlorophyll content (SPAD values) and visual herbicide injury symptoms were assessed after application. The results indicated that all five herbicide treatments significantly reduced chlorophyll content across all maize germplasms compared to the control (35.09–35.53) in both the association panel and landrace population (Fig. 1A; Table 3). Among the herbicides tested, imazamox and clomazone caused the most severe chlorophyll loss, with landrace populations showing greater sensitivity (5.36–7.41) than the association panel (9.92–10.60). Notably, the landrace population also exhibited higher variability in response to clomazone (CV = 1.28), while both germplasm groups exhibited skewness values ranging from − 1.44 to 0.78, with the majority of treatments falling within the range of near-normal distribution (-0.70-0.78) (Fig. S1).

Moreover, maize plants showed symptoms such as stunted growth and leaf malformation after treatment with soybean herbicides (Fig. 1A). Responses varied widely among germplasms, revealing clear resistance differences and highlighting their genetic diversity in herbicide tolerance (Fig. 1A; Table 3). The herbicide injury rate varied substantially among maize germplasms, with imazamox and clomazone induced the highest injury rates (0.71–0.87), while chloransulam and lactofen induced the lowest phytotoxicity (0.54–0.58) (Table 3). The association panel exhibited exceptionally low variability in response to clomazone (CV = 0.08), whereas both populations demonstrated negatively skewed distributions across most herbicides (Fig. S2), indicating clustering toward higher injury scores. Notably, clomazone triggered extreme leptokurtosis (kurtosis = 9.29) in the association panel, suggesting acute susceptibility in this germplasm group.

Evaluation of maize germplasm resistant to soybean herbicides

Based on the injury rate, we systematically evaluated the resistance levels of the 170 inbred lines and found substantial variation in the number of resistant accessions identified for each herbicide (Fig. 1C). Chloransulam had the highest number of resistant materials, with 4 and 15 accessions classified as highly resistant (HR) and resistant (R), respectively, including QB2398 and QB191 (Table S1). In contrast, fewer accessions showed resistance to lactofen (11 accessions) and flumioxazin (4 accessions). Notably, resistance to imazamox was scarce, with only 3 and 6 accessions rated as R and moderately resistant (MR), respectively. No resistant accessions were identified for clomazone; only two (QB2229, QB2232) displayed moderate resistance, while the majority (142 accessions, 83.5%) were highly susceptible (HS).

Evaluation of the 735 landrace accessions also revealed considerable diversity in soybean herbicide resistance (Fig. 1B, Table S2). Chloransulam exhibited the largest number of resistant landraces, with 17 and 142 accessions classified as highly resistant (HR) and resistant (R), respectively, including traditional varieties such as “Bendi Bainuo Baogu” and “Bendi Bai Baogu”. In contrast, resistance was less frequent for lactofen (4 accessions) and flumioxazin (33 accessions). Resistance to imazamox was extremely scarce, with only 8 accessions (3 R and 5 MR) identified. Notably, no landrace accession was resistant to clomazone, and much of the population (566 accessions, 77.0%) was highly susceptible (HS). Together, these results revealed substantial variation in maize resistance to different soybean herbicides, with chloransulam showing the broadest spectrum of resistance among tested materials, while no resistant accessions were identified for clomazone across all germplasms, indicating its strong selective pressure.

Genetic structure and SNP-based genotyping of maize populations

To elucidate the genetic basis underlying the observed variation in herbicide resistance, we first analyzed the population structure of both germplasm sets. The results indicated distant genetic relatedness among the accessions in the association panel, with most pairwise kinship coefficients falling between 0 and 0.5 (Fig. 2A). The optimal population structure for the association panel was identified at K = 9 (Fig. 2B), which partitioned the panel into nine distinct subgroups. Based on a membership coefficient threshold of ≥ 0.8, a total of 78 accessions (45.9%) were assigned to these subgroups with high confidence, each represented by founder inbred lines: Huangzaosi and Chang7-2 (subgroup 1), QB446 (subgroup 2), HCL645 (subgroup 3), Mei22 (subgroup 4), QB662 (subgroup 5), QB2398 (subgroup 6), Xun9058 (subgroup 7), QR273 (subgroup 8), and PHW79 (subgroup 9). The remaining 92 accessions (54.1%) were classified as admixed individuals (Fig. 2C–F). Similarly, when the landrace population was partitioned into 12 subgroups (K = 12), only 51 accessions (6.9%) were assigned with high confidence, while the remaining 684 (93.1%) were classified as admixed (Fig. 2D-F). Among these, subgroups 2 (represented by Raohe Hongpao Pao Baogu (small)), 3 (Xiao Jinhuang), 5 (Bendi Hong Baogu), 6 (Da Baimaya), 8 (Jiuchang Bendi Nuo Baogu), 9 (Ci Baogu), 10 (Xiaojin Bai Yumi), 11 (Danfeng Yinlizi), and 12 (Bendi Bai Yumi) had representative accessions, whereas subgroups 1, 4, and 7 had no representatives with membership coefficient ≥ 0.8. The clear population subdivision and overall weak kinship within both panels support the suitability of these materials for genome-wide association studies (GWAS) of herbicide resistance traits.

To further examine whether the observed population structure corresponded to phenotypic differentiation in herbicide resistance, we analyzed the distribution of resistance traits across genetic subgroups. The evaluation of herbicide resistance across genetic subgroups revealed distinct phenotypic patterns (Table S3). In the association panel, Subgroup 9 displayed moderate resistance to chloransulam, lactofen, and flumioxazin. Subgroup 6 exhibited specific resistance to lactofen, whereas Subgroup 7 was highly susceptible to multiple herbicides. Within the landrace population, Subgroup 11 showed moderate resistance to chloransulam, in contrast to Subgroup 10, which was highly susceptible to both flumioxazin and imazamox. These results demonstrate that genetic subpopulation structures are closely associated with specific herbicide response patterns, providing a functional basis for subgroup classification and highlighting potential germplasm sources for resistance breeding.

For the genetic analysis of maize resistance to soybean herbicide, high-quality genetic maps were constructed based on whole-genome resequencing data (Fig. 2G, H). After filtering out SNPs with missing frequency > 20% and minor allele frequency (MAF) < 5%, a total of 43,252 high-quality SNPs were retained for the association panel and 127,035 high-quality SNPs for the landrace population. Marker distribution along chromosomes was non‑uniform, characterized by pronounced density variation at chromosomal ends (Fig. 2G), while certain genomic regions displayed low marker density (Fig. 2H). Overall, the high density and broad genome coverage of these SNP markers provide a robust foundation for the subsequent GWAS of herbicide resistance traits.

Mapping and meta-analysis identify key loci underlying soybean herbicide resistance in maize

To identify the genetic loci significantly associated with variations in herbicide resistance, we performed GWAS and identified 271 quantitative trait nucleotides (QTNs) associated with resistance to the five soybean herbicides (Table S4). These QTNs exhibited distinct distributions across phenotypes and chromosomes (Fig. S3-6). Subsequently, a genome-wide meta-analysis was performed, using a significance threshold of 5.87 × 10⁻⁵, further resolved 42 consensus QTNs, of which 23 were associated with injury rate and 19 with SPAD value (Fig. 3A, C, Table S5). The quantile-quantile (Q-Q) plots showed a strong agreement between observed and expected P-values across the main distribution, with marked deviations in the tails (Fig. 3B, D), confirming a well-controlled model and the presence of robust genotype-phenotype associations.

We next aimed to distill the most reliable and reproducible genetic signals by integrating results across multiple analytical conditions. Consensus analysis of the QTNs identified 10 stable loci that were distributed across chromosomes 2, 3, 4, 6, 7, and 8 (Table 4). Among them, five loci were detected for clomazone, including SYN4131 (on chromosome 2), S3_44589721 (chromosome 3), PZE-103,045,203 (chromosome 3), PZE-104,147,912 (chromosome 4), and S6_115079153 (chromosome 6). Two loci were detected for imazamox, namely S4_224214 (chromosome 4) and S8_64107365 (chromosome 8). Additionally, one locus, PZE-104,050,858, associated with chloransulam was mapped on chromosome 4. Two loci were detected for flumioxazin, located on chromosome 4 (S4_131271526) and chromosome 7 (S7_143644454), respectively.

Consensus QTNs associated with resistance to soybean herbicides

Among these consensus loci, PZE-104,050,858 exhibited the strongest genotype-phenotype association, explaining 15.20% of the variation in injury rate (P = 1.39 × 10⁻⁵) and 17.07% in SPAD value (P = 3.66 × 10⁻⁶) for chloransulam tolerance (Table 4). This locus was consistently detected in both the association panel and the landrace population. PZE-104,050,858 is located in a genomic region containing two annotated genes: Zm00001eb179770 (currently unannotated) and Zm00001eb179780, which encodes a probable leucine-rich repeat receptor-like serine/threonine-protein kinase (homolog of At3g47570). Another notable locus, S8_64107365, showed an extremely significant association with injury rate under imazamox treatment (P = 1.00 × 10⁻⁸) in the meta-analysis, though its phenotypic contribution was relatively moderate (R² = 0.0498). In contrast, most other loci displayed weaker statistical support (P > 0.05) and accounted for only minor proportions of phenotypic variance (R² < 0.03). Based on these consensus loci and the linkage disequilibrium (LD) decay distance (Fig. 3E, F), we pinpointed 59 candidate genes (Table S6). These results highlight that while multiple loci were consistently detected across analyses, only a subset possess both high statistical significance and meaningful explanatory power for herbicide resistance traits, with PZE-104,050,858 emerging as the most robust candidate for further functional characterization.

Functional validation of the key locus PZE‑104,050,858

Given the strongest genotype-phenotype association observed for locus PZE‑104,050,858, we selected it for in-depth functional characterization. We first analyzed the genotype of the PZE-104,050,858 locus and revealed three allelic variants among the 170 inbred lines: 43 AA, 88 CC, and 4 AC heterozygotes (Fig. 4A). Phenotypic analysis demonstrated that although the CC genotype exhibited a relatively higher frequency of chloransulam susceptible and highly susceptible (S/HS) phenotypes compared to other genotypes, a substantial overlap in phenotypic categories was observed across all three genotypes (AA, AC, CC) (Fig. 4B). Notably, each genotype group contains individuals spanning the full spectrum from highly susceptible (HS) to highly resistant (HR). This pattern suggests that while the locus may contribute to chloransulam resistance variation, the genotype at PZE-104,050,858 does not directly determine the resistance phenotype.

To validate the physical position of the target locus and verify its sequence variation, a validation panel consisting of randomly selected maize inbred lines and landraces was analyzed. A 401 bp fragment spanning the QTN region and its flanking sequences were amplified and sequenced (Table S7). Sequence alignment revealed seven additional SNPs in this region (Fig. 4C). Notably, the loci PZE-104,050,858 and Chr4_S87070067 exhibited significant associations with injury rate (Fig. 4D), suggesting their potential role in herbicide response. Since PZE-104,050,858 does not directly determine the herbicide resistance (Fig. 1B), we further examined allelic variation at SNP2 and found that accessions carrying the G allele (e.g., QB2398, Mo17) consistently displayed resistance to chloransulam, while those with the C allele (e.g., Chang 7‑2, Qi 319) were sensitive (Fig. 4E-G). These findings further substantiate the close association between SNP2 and chloransulam resistance, supporting its utility as a robust molecular marker for screening resistant germplasm.

Discussion

Phenotypic differences in maize seedling traits under different herbicide treatments

This work revealed substantial variation in the severity and symptomology of phytotoxicity caused by different types of soybean herbicides. Specifically, chloransulam and lactofen demonstrated superior crop safety in both inbred lines and landrace populations, as evidenced by higher post-application SPAD values and lower injury rates (Table 3). In contrast, imazamox and clomazone induced more severe phytotoxicity, characterized by significantly reduced SPAD values and substantially elevated injury rates. This divergence likely stems from differences in herbicide modes of action: acetolactate synthase (ALS) inhibitors like chloransulam primarily function by disrupting branched-chain amino acid synthesis, whereas protoporphyrinogen oxidase (PPO) inhibitors (e.g., flumioxazin) and 4-hydroxyphenylpyruvate dioxygenase (HPPD) inhibitors (e.g., clomazone) directly interfere with chlorophyll metabolic pathways [46–48]. The landrace population exhibited a higher average CV compared to the association panel, suggesting the potential presence of unique resistance alleles in these genetically diverse accessions [49, 50]. These findings indicate that chloransulam and lactofen represent preferable herbicide options for maize-soybean intercropping systems due to their enhanced safety profiles. Although the rapid efficacy of imazamox and clomazone offers advantages for weed control, their heightened phytotoxicity risks necessitate the deployment of herbicide-tolerant maize varieties for safe implementation.

Screening of elite maize germplasm with resistance to soybean herbicides

This study identified 22 maize inbred lines exhibiting resistance to soybean-specific herbicides (Table S1-2). Analysis of pedigree information and resistance statistics revealed that germplasm including Suwan-Lancaster materials (QB2787, QB1299, 16xh-90, 16xh-5, QB1310), Suwan-Iodent materials (17HG-824, 17HG-826, QB5721, QBH19), and Suwan materials (QB2218, QB2229, QB2232, QBH16_54) demonstrated strong resistance to soybean herbicides. This resistance may be attributed to the broad genetic foundation, superior stress tolerance, and rich genetic diversity characteristic of tropical maize germplasms [51–53]. These findings further illustrate the complementary advantages of integrating tropical and temperate germplasm in maize improvement, enabling the simultaneous achievement of high and stable yield, superior quality, and enhanced stress resistance in breeding programs [54]. Evaluation of hybrid combinations involving the identified inbred lines confirmed their practical breeding value, with several lines serving as parents for commercial hybrids, such as Jinyu 9608 (QB2896 × QB2208) and Jinyu 9632 (QB2896 × QB2232).

A total of 57 landrace accessions resistant to soybean-specific herbicides were identified, including Xiangdu Bai Yumi, Yangchang Bendi Xuehong Nian Baogu, Baiyun Bai Baogu, Niuchang Bendi Huangnuo Baogu, Hefeng Bai Yumi, and Maling Bendi Huang Baogu, among others. Comparative analysis of resistance levels among landraces from different regions suggested that the observed variation may be attributed to long-term regional selection and evolutionary adaptation. As valuable foundational germplasm resources, landraces exhibited remarkable environmental adaptability and demonstrate outstanding performance in resistance to biotic and abiotic stresses [55]. Their extensive genetic variability makes them particularly useful for broadening the genetic background of breeding materials, highlighting their significant potential for utilization in maize germplasm enhancement and genetic improvement.

Molecular marker validation

Genotyping specific loci with molecular markers can save labor, materials, and time in maize breeding programs, thus enhancing the efficiency of germplasm screening. Among the 10 consensus loci identified through meta-analysis integrating results from both the association panel and landrace population, PZE-104,050,858 exhibited the strongest and most consistent association with chloransulam tolerance, explaining 15.20% of the phenotypic variation in injury rate and 17.07% in SPAD value. Based on the consensus locus PZE-104,050,858, the SNP with significant association with chloransulam resistance was validated on maize chromosome 4. This marker targets the functional C/G allelic variation at locus Chr4_S_87070067, with a clear genomic localization and functional association. The observed association is indirect and most likely mediated by the linkage disequilibrium (LD) with a regulatory variant that influences the expression of a nearby gene. For example, SNP2 is situated approximately 5 kb upstream of Zm00001eb179780, which encodes a leucine-rich repeat receptor-like protein kinase (LRR-RLK), a gene family often implicated in stress perception and signaling. LD analysis revealed that SNP2 is in strong LD with a promoter polymorphism of Zm00001eb179780. Given that the genome-wide LD decay distance in both populations is approximately 150 kb, the approximately 5 kb interval between SNP2 and the gene falls well within the same LD block, supporting the conclusion that SNP2 reliably tags the functional variant. These findings suggest that SNP2 serves as a marker for a regulatory variant affecting Zm00001eb179780 expression. Thus, while PZE-104,050,858 and SNP2 do not directly confer herbicide resistance, they are linked to functional variants through LD, contributing to differential herbicide tolerance via indirect genetic mechanisms. Compared with traditional field phenotype-dependent evaluation methods, this marker demonstrates significant advantages such as rapidity, accuracy, and environmental independence. Validation across materials with different genetic backgrounds further confirmed its predictive accuracy, with consistent genotype-phenotype correspondence observed in all tested materials. Given this reliability, the marker can be integrated into marker-assisted selection systems to enable early and precise screening for chloransulam resistance, substantially improving breeding efficiency. It also facilitates high-throughput screening of germplasm resources, providing technical support for broadening the genetic basis of herbicide resistance.
