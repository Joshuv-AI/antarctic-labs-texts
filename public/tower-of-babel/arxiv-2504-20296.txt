SoK: Cryptocurrency Mixing and Anonymity – Architectures, Threat Models, Operational Aspects and Security
Juraj Mariani, Martin Perešíni, Ivan Homoliak

Abstract
Public blockchains record transaction histories that enable address clustering, taint analysis, and cross-service attribution, thereby motivating the development of mixers and privacy layers. Our work presents a structured scoping review of 22 representative systems, defining a common unlinkability objective and five adversary archetypes. We evaluate these systems against a taxonomy of attack surfaces, including chain analysis, timing inference, custodial compromise, coordination abuse, network metadata, and trusted execution compromise. While nominal anonymity-set size and cryptographic strength characterize privacy in theory, effective anonymity in practice depends on transaction denominations, cover traffic, relayer behavior, and compliance-interface design. Distinguishing nominal from effective anonymity, we derive four core lessons: (1) Privacy is strongest when integrated into everyday transactions, since standalone mixing creates an easily profiled user subset; (2) Trust points, including operators, peer quorums, and hardware enclaves, must be explicit so users know who can break privacy; (3) Network metadata, including gas funding and timing, must be treated formally as protocol data in privacy evaluations; and (4) Compliance should use auditable cryptographic predicates for selective disclosure rather than broad operator discretion. Ultimately, our systematization clarifies the strengths, failures, and future requirements of blockchain privacy architectures.



1
Introduction

Blockchain systems provide integrity and auditability by making transaction histories globally visible.
This transparency supports consensus and verification, but it also enables address clustering, taint analysis, behavioral profiling, and attribution across services
[
51
]
.
Users of public chains therefore receive pseudonymity rather than anonymity by default.
Its strength depends on the difficulty of linking ledger events to each other and to real identities.
Transaction privacy is consequently important for applications that must protect balances, counterparties, or spending patterns while retaining public verifiability.

Cryptocurrency mixing emerged as the first response to that gap.
In its narrowest sense, a mixer is an intermediary or protocol that obscures the relationship between a user’s input and output transactions.
The design space is broader in practice.
Some systems rely on custodial pooling and redistribution.
Others create ambiguity through joint transactions, encode withdrawal rights in private notes, or internalize privacy in the base protocol through ring signatures, shielded transactions, or confidential execution.
The literature is therefore fragmented.
Mixing can denote a service category or a more general unlinkability mechanism.

That fragmentation matters because privacy claims are often made for a cryptographic mechanism rather than for the complete deployed system.
The central premise of this work is that the gap between cryptographic and operational privacy drives many mixer failures.
A large advertised anonymity set need not yield a large effective set once timing, amount patterns, relayer behavior, network metadata, operator records, or hardware assumptions are considered.
Mixer forensics and smart contract analysis show that practical deanonymization often exploits this operational context rather than the core primitive
[
83
,
72
,
81
,
73
]
.
These observations motivate three research questions that structure the review.

Research Questions.

We specify the following research questions:

RQ1.
How can heterogeneous cryptocurrency mixing systems be organized and compared under a common transaction unlinkability objective and adversary model?

RQ2.
Which adversary archetypes remain effective under realistic conditions, and which operational factors most strongly determine the gap between nominal anonymity (the theoretical size of the user pool) and effective anonymity (the actual indistinguishability achieved in practice)?

RQ3.
Which design principles, evaluation practices, and open problems follow from the taxonomy, the adversary-driven comparison, and the matrix of attack surfaces?

Design Space.

We organize the design space around one guiding question: which actor can still reconstruct the transaction graph once the surrounding system is accounted for.
The taxonomy addresses RQ1.
The comparative review and the matrix of attack surfaces address RQ2.
The synthesis addresses RQ3.

This work studies cryptocurrency mixing as a systems problem rather than only as a cryptographic feature.
It explains why mixer families fail in practice, where their trust assumptions reside, and how implementation details reshape the privacy promised by a protocol.
Existing surveys focus on Bitcoin and Ethereum mixer catalogues
[
2
]
or address privacy-preserving payments more broadly
[
53
]
.
We instead treat mixing as a blockchain privacy problem that spans architectural families.

Contributions.

The main contributions are as follows.

\bullet

We define the scope across seven architectural families and position it against recent mixer and payment surveys.
We formalize transaction unlinkability from the adversary’s perspective and introduce five adversary archetypes, covering chain analysis, custodial compromise, active coordination, network observation, and hardware compromise.

\bullet

We review 22 representative systems and code each by trust anchor, source of cover traffic, observable metadata, and dominant failure mode.
Then we compared attack surfaces under an explicit rubric (
Table 4

\bullet

We derive design lessons for blockchain privacy systems, including metrics that reflect known attacks, accountable coordination, reduced metadata leakage, narrowly defined compliance mechanisms, and programmable private execution.

2
Scope, Methodology, and Positioning

This work is a structured scoping review rather than a narrow protocol survey.
Its scope includes systems designed to reduce the linkability between asset ingress and egress events on a public ledger.
It covers custodial services, coordinated Bitcoin protocols, account based contract mixers, privacy layers, and privacy native assets.
It also considers exchange mediated and cross chain obfuscation when a mechanism is used to interrupt tracing across ledgers, although this class does not resist the exchange operator.
Mix networks in the tradition of Chaum
[
12
]
are outside the scope because they protect messages rather than ledger asset events.
Network obfuscation, such as Dandelion++
[
24
]
, is considered only when combined with an on-chain mixing architecture.

2.1
Search Strategy and Corpus Construction

We constructed the corpus iteratively up to 10 July 2026.
Searches covered IEEE Xplore, the ACM Digital Library, SpringerLink, USENIX, FC, and WPES.
For deployed systems without an archival publication, we consulted authoritative technical artifacts, such as protocol specifications, technical reports, source repositories, operator reports, and forensic analyses.
We complemented database searches with backward and forward citation tracing from relevant surveys and empirical studies.

Searches combined terms for mixing and privacy mechanisms with concrete system names and forensic terms.
Representative terms included “cryptocurrency mixer,” “CoinJoin,” “Tornado Cash,” “shielded transactions,” “privacy layer,” “zkSNARK,” “unlinkability,” “clustering,” “deanonymization,” and “tracing,” with additional queries for Wasabi, RAILGUN, Obscuro, and Monero.
A source was included when it specified a mixing or unlinkability mechanism, described an implemented privacy system, analyzed the traceability of a deployed mixer, or reported reproducible evidence on a recurring operational or implementation failure.
We excluded sources that address regulatory policy without technical mechanism analysis, duplicate descriptions when a more authoritative source was available, generic graph analysis unrelated to a mixer architecture, and purely theoretical proposals without implementation, deployment, or empirical data.
The unit of analysis is a deployed system or protocol family rather than an individual publication.

2.2
Meaning of Mixing

We use mixing in a functional sense: a system qualifies when it intentionally increases uncertainty about the correspondence between a source and destination asset event.
Privacy-preserving cryptocurrencies and confidential execution platforms are legitimate comparison targets even when they are not mixers in the strict service sense, because they solve the same unlinkability problem at a different layer.
Centralized exchanges create ambiguity for external observers without offering privacy against the exchange itself.
They differ both in mechanism and in the entity that can collapse uncertainty.

2.3
Coding Protocol and Validity Threats

For each retained system we recorded the ledger and deployment model, privacy primitive, trust anchor, source of cover traffic, observable metadata, and dominant failure modes.
We used peer-reviewed empirical studies where available, followed by primary specifications and official documentation.
Each cell in the matrix of attack surfaces (
Table 4

The appendix records the evidence for non-trivial assignments.

Three validity threats apply.
First, documentation quality is uneven, especially for defunct or seized custodial services known mainly through forensic reconstruction.
Second, several recent systems have limited longitudinal evidence, so claims about them are primarily architectural.
Third, legal and operational conditions can change faster than protocol designs.
The technical comparisons remain meaningful even if particular sanctions or obligations evolve.

3
System Model and Adversary Model

To compare heterogeneous systems meaningfully, we treat mixing as a privacy objective over observable ledger events rather than as a specific protocol family.

3.1
Terminology

We adopt the terminology of Pfitzmann and Hansen
[
61
]
.
Two items of interest (IOIs), such as a deposit and a withdrawal, are unlinkable from an adversary’s perspective if the adversary cannot sufficiently distinguish whether they are related.
The anonymity set of an observed IOI is the set of alternatives consistent with that adversary’s observations and knowledge.
Transaction unlinkability is the special case in which the two IOIs are an ingress event and its true egress event.
Its candidate set is defined below.
We summarize uncertainty with a probability distribution over candidates rather than a raw count
[
69
,
21
]
.

3.2
Unlinkability Objective and Effective Anonymity

Let
d_{i}
denote a deposit, spend, or ingress event.
For an instantiated adversary
\mathcal{A}
and a fixed realized view
v
, let
W_{i}(v)
be the effective anonymity set.
It contains the candidate egress events consistent with the protocol rules and all evidence in
v
.
The view can include ledger data, timing, amounts, protocol messages, relayer interactions, operator records, or other side information allowed by the model.
A mixing system seeks to limit the adversary’s ability to link
d_{i}
to the correct egress event
w_{i}\in W_{i}(v)
.
The advantage is defined by

\mathrm{Adv}_{\mathrm{link}}(\mathcal{A};v)=\Pr[\mathcal{A}(d_{i},v)=w_{i}\mid V_{\mathcal{A}}=v]-\frac{1}{|W_{i}(v)|}.

(1)

The conditional probability is taken over protocol randomness, the hidden input and output relation consistent with
v
, and the adversary’s internal randomness.
The second term is a uniform cardinality baseline for the realized candidate set.
It is a reference point rather than an assertion that the posterior is uniform.

Equation (
1
) adapts the cryptographic convention of measuring success above a stated baseline
[
37
]
.
Equation (
2
) applies the established anonymity measurement principle that the posterior distribution, not only its support, determines the remaining uncertainty
[
69
,
21
,
16
]
.
To make the probability distribution explicit, let
p_{w}=\Pr[w=w_{i}\mid V_{\mathcal{A}}=v]
.
It is defined over
W_{i}(v)
, with
\sum_{w\in W_{i}(v)}p_{w}=1
and
p_{w}\geq 0
.
Anonymity research commonly evaluates the posterior rather than only its support size
[
69
,
21
,
16
]
.
We use the reciprocal of collision probability, equivalently the exponential of Rényi entropy of order two, as an effective candidate count:

k_{\mathrm{eff}}(v)=\frac{1}{\sum_{w\in W_{i}(v)}p_{w}^{2}}.

(2)

The value
k_{\mathrm{eff}}(v)
equals
|W_{i}(v)|
only when the posterior is uniform.
It is smaller when probability mass concentrates on fewer candidates.

The expressions adapt established tools and are not claimed as novel.
The contribution is their consistent application across mixer families.
Evaluating
\mathrm{Adv}_{\mathrm{link}}(\mathcal{A};v)
and
k_{\mathrm{eff}}(v)
under the same five archetypes and evidence classes yields comparable statements for custodial services, coordinated protocols, contract mixers, privacy layers, and privacy native ledgers.
This distinction matters when public interfaces advertise large pools but practical heuristics narrow the plausible candidates
[
72
,
81
]
.
The advertised pool size is not itself
W_{i}(v)
.
The candidate set remains after filtering by amount structure, timing rules, coordination metadata, cross chain conversion rates, visible operator records, and other deployment side channels.

3.3
Evaluation Criteria

A mixing design is robust only when it addresses more than unlinkability in isolation.
We evaluate five dimensions.

(1) Transaction unlinkability
concerns the input and output relation under the adversary model.

(2) Amount and destination privacy
concerns the values and recipients that remain visible.

(3) Liveness and disruption tolerance
concerns completion under churn, aborts, and low participation.

(4) Trust minimization
identifies which operator, coordinator, relayer, validator, exchange, or hardware vendor can reconstruct the linkage.

(5) Deployability
covers realistic fees, latency, liquidity, and regulatory constraints.

3.4
Adversarial Goals

Across mixer families, the adversary pursues one or more related goals.
Input and output linkage identifies which output belongs to which input.
Entity linkage clusters addresses, notes, or accounts into the same user.
Amount linkage narrows candidates through denominations, change, or conversions.
Timing and network inference exploits intervals between deposit and withdrawal, message timing, and IP metadata.
Anonymity set degradation and service denial reduce the cover set through Sybil participation
[
22
]
, targeted DoS attacks
[
30
]
, censorship, or aborts.
These goals bear on architectures differently, a mapping made precise by the matrix of attack surfaces (
Table 4
).

3.5

Depending on the scenario, it can also observe or control operator records, protocol participation, network metadata, or trusted hardware.

3.7
Assumptions and Limitations

Unless stated otherwise, we assume standard cryptographic primitives are not broken.
The adversary does not invert hashes, forge zero-knowledge proofs, or break signature schemes directly.
We do not, however, assume that surrounding systems are ideal.
Users may withdraw too quickly, reuse addresses, choose rare denominations, leak metadata to relayers, or rely on centralized operators.
Private execution platforms may inherit weaknesses from the hardware they trust.
These assumptions outside the cryptographic core are often decisive.

4.1
Architectural classes

We summarize each class here and defer the detailed system analysis to
Section 5
.

Custodial Mixers.

Custodial mixers accept user funds into operator-controlled addresses and later redistribute them from pooled reserves.
They need little coordination, but the operator, or anyone who later compromises it, can reconstruct the true mapping.
The pooling and redistribution also leave recognizable graph motifs such as batching and peeling chains
[
50
,
20
,
73
]
.

Coordinated Bitcoin Mixers.

CoinJoin, CoinShuffle, CoinParty, and Wasabi Wallet keep funds under user control until participants construct a joint transaction
[
45
,
66
,
87
,
88
]
.
Equal outputs create ambiguity for a passive observer.
The class removes custody but requires interactive liveness.
Its privacy therefore depends on Sybil resistance
[
22
]
, accountability, and sufficient compatible participants.

Contract Mixers.

Account-based platforms can hold privacy state in a contract.
Users deposit a fixed denomination, receive a note, and later prove a right to withdraw without revealing the deposit they own
[
42
,
68
,
60
]
.
Succinct arguments originate with Bitansky et al.
[
8
]
and were applied to anonymous currency in Zerocoin
[
48
]

The design removes custody and is auditable, but deposits, gas funding, contract interactions, and relayer patterns remain visible.
Effective anonymity thus depends on behavior as well as the cryptographic primitive
[
72
,
81
]
.

Privacy Layers.

Privacy layers embed private state in an application protocol above a contract platform.
RAILGUN maintains a shielded UTXO pool for arbitrary tokens and variable amounts
[
63
]
.
Aztec is a Layer 2 rollup in which private state transitions execute on the client and settle on chain with PLONK proofs
[
4
,
25
]

These designs support programmable private computation but inherit host chain constraints and require sufficient cover traffic. Additionally, zkSNARKs can protect complex DeFi instruments, such as Hopinzero’s on-chain options, by hiding commercially sensitive terms
[
38
]
.

Privacy Native Chains and Confidential Execution.

Some systems move unlinkability into the base protocol.
Monero uses ring signatures, stealth addresses, and confidential transactions.
Zcash uses shielded transactions backed by zkSNARKs.
Grin and Beam implement Mimblewimble variants
[
49
,
54
,
33
,
62
]
.
Oasis, Secret, Integritee, and Phala use confidential computation in trusted hardware
[

,
67
,
35
,
84
]
.
These systems show that privacy can be a base protocol property rather than a service overlay, a principle also applied to network-layer anonymization through natively integrated onion routing in consensus protocols
[
30
]
.
Mandatory, optional, and TEE-based privacy have different trust and deployment profiles.

Exchange Mediated and Cross Chain Obfuscation.

Movement through centralized exchanges or conversion venues can interrupt tracing by translating funds into a different asset graph or an internal off-chain ledger
[
86
,
65
,
3
]
.
Large user bases supply cover traffic, but the protection applies only to external observers because the operator retains the most complete view.
This is therefore a limited form of mixing. To avoid such reliance, decentralized zero-knowledge bridges like PPBridge

5.2

These protocols keep funds under user control until a joint transaction is assembled.
Privacy arises from equal outputs or cryptographic shuffling, but interactive rounds introduce liveness and Sybil surfaces.

6.2
Interpreting the Comparative Ratings

The ordinal ratings are not a scalar measure of overall privacy.
Each cell records a structural defense against one capability.
A resistant rating on one surface does not offset exposure on another, while a partial rating denotes dependence on an observable deployment condition.
The surfaces also compose: ledger and network observations can be combined, operator records can be correlated with public transactions, and round participants can use timing to select disruption targets.
The matrix separates these concerns.
The corpus table and appendix preserve the evidence behind its 17 rows.

6.3
Nominal Anonymity Is a Weak Metric

Across mixer families, nominal anonymity set size is an unreliable privacy proxy.
In CoinJoin systems, the set shrinks when outputs differ, rounds are small, or participants are adversarial.
In contract mixers, a large pool can conceal narrow timing windows or rare denominations.
Posterior uncertainty, captured by

(

Otherwise, candidate counts should be reported as heuristic bounds.
A formal model for distributed shuffling similarly shows that set size can overstate privacy when shuffling is partial and participants are corrupted

k_{\mathrm{eff}}

6.4
Trust, Usability, and Cross Chain Movement

Mixer architectures relocate trust to the operator, peers, contracts and relayers, protocol parameters, or hardware.
A contract without custody can still leak metadata, and a platform that uses a TEE can still fail under hardware compromise.
Designs should state their trust anchor and distinguish resistance to the operator from resistance to an outside observer.

Usability and liveness also affect privacy.
Interactive protocols need simultaneous participants and must tolerate churn and aborts.
Contract mixer users can weaken privacy by withdrawing quickly, reusing infrastructure, or behaving predictably.
Strong designs therefore pair cryptographic protection with accountable coordination, admission that limits Sybil participation, and guidance that avoids simple heuristics.
Movement across chains is not equivalent to privacy.
It changes the graph observed by an external analyst but does not remove the exchange operator’s view
[
86
]
.

6.5
Mixer Detection and Forensic Evolution

The adversarial landscape changes as forensics, graph analysis, and machine learning improve detection and partial deanonymization
[
83
,
70
,
41
,
82
,
58
]
.
Distinctive footprints, such as deposits in fixed denominations or custodial peeling chains, are easier to detect than private transactions that resemble ordinary activity.

6.6
Evaluation and Reporting Guidance

The comparison suggests four reporting obligations for future mixer evaluations, which separate a protocol claim from a deployment claim.
First, report the candidate population: state the observation period, the asset or denomination, the active participants, and the rules that exclude inactive or repeated activity, and describe the result as a candidate count when a posterior distribution cannot be justified.
Second, state the adversarial evidence: identify the adversary’s observations, auxiliary information, and permitted actions, and distinguish a demonstrated linkage method from a structural exposure inferred from the architecture.
Third, measure the operational context: participation volume, time between related events, relayer use, fee funding, wallet defaults, and network behavior often determine whether a mechanism has usable cover traffic.
Fourth, support reproducibility: describe the data source, extraction date, filtering rules, and heuristics used to construct links, and state which results depend on unavailable service records.

6.7
Design Lessons and Research Directions

Make privacy routine.

Privacy should be pooled rather than exceptional.
A privacy mechanism used only by a small subgroup creates a narrow and recognizable population.
Default private transfers, regular transaction patterns, and sufficient participation provide a more stable basis for anonymity than an exceptional mixing event.

Treat metadata as protocol data.

Gas funding, relayers, round admission, note lifetimes, and network propagation are observable parts of the system.
A design should specify which metadata it leaves visible and which practices it expects from users, and evaluations should report privacy after realistic heuristics rather than after an idealized execution of the cryptographic core.

State residual trust boundaries.

Every family retains an authority or assumption, whether an operator, a quorum of participants, a proof system, a relayer set, a wallet implementation, or an enclave.
A clear trust boundary distinguishes privacy against an external observer from privacy against the actor that operates the service.

Use accountable compliance mechanisms.

Compliance mechanisms should constrain narrowly defined and auditable predicates rather than grant broad operator discretion.
Selective disclosure can allow a user to demonstrate a required property without revealing a complete transaction history, provided that its governance rules, admissibility conditions, and failure modes are explicit.
Legal constraints continue to change.
The Fifth Circuit limited the sanctioning of immutable Tornado Cash contracts, and Treasury removed the designation in March 2025
[
77
,
79
]
.
Policy recognizes lawful privacy uses while enforcement against mixer operators continues
[
80
,
76
]
.
From 10 July 2027, Article 79 of the EU Anti-Money Laundering Regulation will restrict anonymizing accounts at regulated institutions and crypto asset service providers
[
23
]
.
Transaction controls can also be evaded by hidden protocol layers
[
13
]
.
RAILGUN’s Private Proofs of Innocence and zkMixer illustrate selective disclosure, but governance remains unresolved
[
64
,
18
]
.
Privacy layers such as Aztec
[
4
]
also support a future in which privacy is an execution property rather than a mixer overlay.
This requires efficient proof generation on client devices, private state management, and better integration with network anonymity
[
24
]
.
Hybrid designs that pair shielded proofs with trusted execution extend private computation but relocate rather than remove the hardware attack surface
[
15
]
.

7
Conclusion

A cryptographic mechanism alone cannot establish the privacy of a mixing system.
An advertised anonymity set is also insufficient.
Practical privacy emerges from the complete deployment, including participation volume, transaction timing, amount patterns, relayer use, network exposure, operator records, wallet behavior, and hardware assumptions.
Protection at one layer can remain ineffective when these surrounding conditions narrow the plausible transaction links.

The comparison leads to a broader design principle: privacy is strongest when it is routine, pooled across ordinary activity, and supported by explicit trust boundaries.
Future systems should report privacy against concrete observations and known heuristics, treat metadata as part of the protocol model, and reduce exceptional behavior that makes private transactions easy to recognize.
Compliance mechanisms should prove narrowly defined properties without granting an operator broad access to transaction histories.
These conclusions provide a common basis for comparing current architectures and for designing blockchain privacy systems whose operational guarantees more closely match their cryptographic claims.

The coding rule assigns a family by deployment locus.
Primitive and reconstruction authority then expose hybrid cases.
This rule keeps Obscuro in the custodial family without claiming that its operator sees the plaintext mapping under intact TEE assumptions.
TumbleBit is excluded because its primary object is a payment hub rather than a deployed ingress and egress pool.
Penumbra and Namada are contextual shielded designs that were outside the frozen system corpus.
