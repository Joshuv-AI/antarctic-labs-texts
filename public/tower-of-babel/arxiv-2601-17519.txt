The edge-isoperimetric number of graphs and their powers: approaches from spectral graph theory, optimization and finite geometry Thanks: Robin.Simoens@UGent.be Department of Mathematics: Analysis, Logic and Discrete Mathematics, Ghent University, Belgium Department of Mathematics, Universitat Politècnica de Catalunya, Spain
Aida Abiad, Nils van de Berg, Emanuel Juliano, Harper Reijnders, Thijs van Veluw, Jim Wittebol

Abstract
We obtain several sharp spectral bounds, approximations, and exact values for the isoperimetric number and related edge‑expansion parameters of graphs. Our results focus on graph powers and on families of graphs with rich algebraic or geometric structure, including distance‑regular graphs and graphs arising from finite geometries, among others. Our proofs use techniques from spectral graph theory, linear optimization, finite geometry, and probability, yielding new machinery for analysing edge-expansion phenomena in highly structured graphs. Keywords: Isoperimetric number, Graph, Eigenvalue bounds, Graph powers, Approximation, Linear optimization, Tight sets, Split graphs



1
Introduction

Edge expansion is a fundamental property in graphs that is captured by a variety of different but related parameters, for example the isoperimetric number, the Cheeger constant, and the sparsity number.
Consider a graph
G=(V,E)
and a subset of its vertices
S\subseteq V
. The
volume
of
S
,
\vol(S)
, is defined as the sum of the degrees of the vertices in
S
. Let
\partial_{G}(S)
denote the set of edges with exactly one endpoint in
S
. We define the following expansion parameters of
S

i_{G}(S)=\frac{|\partial_{G}(S)|}{|S|},\quad h_{G}(S)=\frac{|\partial_{G}(S)|}{\vol(S)},\quad\sigma_{G}(S)=\frac{|\partial_{G}(S)|}{|S||V\setminus S|}.

When the graph
G
is clear from the context, we use
\partial S
,
i(S)
,
h(S)
and
\sigma(S)
instead of
\partial_{G}(S)
,
i_{G}(S)
,
h_{G}(S)
and
\sigma_{G}(S)
. Let
n=|V|
be the order of the graph. The expansion parameters for a graph are defined as the minimum over all appropriate sets. In particular,

•

The
isoperimetric number
(or
edge-isoperimetric number
) of
G
is

i(G)=\min\left\{i_{G}(S)\colon\,S\subseteq V,\,1\leq|S|\leq\frac{1}{2}n\right\}.

•

The
Cheeger constant
of
G
is

h(G)=\min\left\{h_{G}(S)\colon\,S\subseteq V,\,1\leq\vol(S)\leq\vol(V)/2\right\}.

•

The
sparsity
of
G
is

\sigma(G)=\min\left\{\sigma_{G}(S)\colon\,S\subseteq V,\,\emptyset\neq S\neq V\right\}.

These parameters are highly related, especially in the case of regular graphs. In this paper, we focus mostly on the isoperimetric number. Note that there are also vertex expansion parameters such as the vertex-isoperimetric number (see for example
[
56
, Section 4.6]
), but we do not consider those.

The study of edge expansion parameters has deep connections to spectral graph theory, combinatorial optimization, and theoretical computer science. Since computing the isoperimetric number is NP-hard
[
58
, Theorem 2]
, a lot of literature focuses on bounding this parameter for specific graph classes or approximating it via spectral methods. In particular, isoperimetric-type inequalities relate
i(G)
to the second-largest eigenvalue of the adjacency matrix or the second-smallest eigenvalue of the Laplacian matrix, providing a bridge between combinatorial and spectral properties. These inequalities have been instrumental in the analysis of expander graphs, random walks, and mixing times of Markov chains. Two celebrated spectral bounds are

\frac{\mu_{2}}{2}\leq i(G)\leq\sqrt{\mu_{2}(2d_{1}-\mu_{2})},

(1)

where
\mu_{2}
is the second smallest Laplacian eigenvalue and
d_{1}
is the largest degree
[
12
,
69
]
. These two bounds have been a staple of spectral graph theory. In recent years other spectral bounds have followed, such as the following upper bound for the isoperimetric number of regular graphs (see for example
[
72
, Lemma 5]
):

i(G)\leq\frac{\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil}{n}\mu_{n},

(2)

where
\mu_{n}
is the largest Laplacian eigenvalue. Also, an alternative upper bound in terms of the graph degree sequence and the Laplacian eigenvalues is shown in
[
6
]
, and
an improvement of the lower bound (
1
) via a linear program (LP) that uses information of the Laplacian eigenvectors in
[
44
]
.

In this paper, we derive several sharp spectral bounds, approximations and exact values for the isoperimetric number and related edge-expansion parameters, focusing on graph powers and graphs with a rich algebraic and geometric nature (such as distance-regular graphs and graphs coming from finite geometries). Our proofs use techniques from spectral graph theory, linear optimization, finite geometry and probability.

We start in Section
3
by deriving sharp bounds on the isoperimetric number of graph powers, contributing to the existing literature in this direction
[
26
,
73
,
75
]
. In particular, we show that the lower bound (
1
) holds for the more general class of graph powers, even when the spectrum of the graph power and the base graph are not related, and we also derive an alternative upper bound. Both bounds are shown using the polynomial interlacing method. They depend on the spectrum of the base graph and on a polynomial which can be chosen freely; thus, one needs to make a good choice of the polynomial to obtain the strongest bound. We propose linear optimization methods to compute the new polynomial eigenvalue bounds, derive the best possible bounds for small values of the power, and present multiple graphs that exhibit tightness for the new bounds.

An alternative lower bound for the isoperimetric number comes from a relaxation of the LP for the sparsity parameter
[
66
]
. This LP has been studied for strongly regular graphs in
[
74
]
. In Section
4
, we derive a closed formula for the optimal value of this LP for distance-regular graphs. We show that the optimal value of the LP can be computed using only the intersection array, without having to construct the distance-regular graph. This contributes to the previous literature on expansion parameters of distance-regular graphs
[
60
,
72
,
74
]
.

Although computing the isoperimetric number of a general graph is NP‑complete, its exact value has been determined for several notable graph families
[
16
,
69
]
.
In Section
5
, we propose a new method for establishing exact isoperimetric values for graphs arising from finite geometry. Our approach is based on a simple but effective observation linking the isoperimetric number to the existence of tight sets in the underlying geometry. This connection yields a novel and easy method for exactly determining isoperimetric numbers in graphs originating from finite geometries, while previously only bounds have been studied, see e.g.
[
62
]
. We demonstrate its power and applicability by obtaining explicit formulas for the isoperimetric number of certain Grassmann graphs and various polar graphs.

Despite the existence of eigenvalue bounds for several NP-hard graph parameters, most of these seem not to be determined by the graph spectrum
[
1
,
23
,
51
,
52
,
67
]
. In Section
6
we demonstrate that the isoperimetric number is one of them. We do so by providing an infinite family of pairs of cospectral connected regular graphs with different isoperimetric numbers.

Motivated by similar research for other random graph models
[
28
,
9
,
22
]
, in Section
7
, we establish the isoperimetric number of the random split graph model and show that with high probability the spectral upper bound from
[
6
]
is tight for that model.

Preliminaries

For a graph
G
, we denote the number of its vertices by
n
, the degrees of the
n
vertices by
d_{1}\geq\dots\geq d_{n}
, the adjacency eigenvalues by
\lambda_{1}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{n}
, and the Laplacian eigenvalues by
0=\mu_{1}\leq\dots\leq\mu_{n}
.
If
G
is
k
-regular, that is, if
d_{1}=\dots=d_{n}=k
, then
\lambda_{i}+\mu_{i}=k
and in particular
\lambda_{1}=k
.

The
Cartesian product
of two graphs
G
and
H
is the graph
G\times H
with vertex set
V(G)\times V(H)
such that the vertices
(v,w)
and
(v^{\prime},w^{\prime})
are adjacent whenever
v
is adjacent to
v^{\prime}
in G and
w=w^{\prime}
, or
v=v^{\prime}
and
w
is adjacent to
w^{\prime}
in
H
.

Eigenvalue interlacing is a powerful method for obtaining spectral bounds in graph theory. We briefly recall interlacing of quotient matrices. For more details, see
[
32
, Sections 2.3 and 2.5]
.

Let
M
be a symmetric
n\times n
matrix, and let
\mathcal{P}=\{P_{1},\dots,P_{m}\}
be a partition of the set
\{1,\dots,n\}
into non-empty subsets. The
quotient matrix
of
M
with respect to the partition
\mathcal{P}
is defined as the
m\times m
matrix
B
with

B_{ij}=\frac{1}{|P_{i}|}\sum_{a\in P_{i}}\sum_{b\in P_{j}}M_{ab}.

The value
B_{ij}
can be interpreted as the average row sum of the submatrix of
M
on the rows indexed by
P_{i}
and the columns indexed by
P_{j}
. If
M
is the adjacency matrix of a graph, then
B_{ij}
counts the average number of neighbours in
P_{j}
among vertices in
P_{i}
.

The partition
\mathcal{P}
is called
equitable
or
regular
if for all
i
and
j
, the submatrix of
M
on
P_{i}
and
P_{j}
has the property that every row sums to
B_{ij}
. A subset
S
of the vertices of a regular graph is called
intriguing
if the partition
\{S,S^{c}\}
(where
S^{c}
is the complement of
S
in
\{1,\dots,n\}
) of the vertex set is non-trivial and equitable with respect to the adjacency matrix. In that case, every vertex of
S
has
B_{11}
neighbours in
S
and
B_{12}
neighbours outside of
S
, and every vertex outside of
S
has
B_{21}
neighbours in
S
and
B_{22}
neighbours outside of
S
.

If
\alpha_{1}\geq\dots\geq\alpha_{n}
and
\beta_{1}\geq\dots\geq\beta_{m}
are the eigenvalues of
M
and
B
, respectively, then the eigenvalues of
B

interlace
those of
A
, meaning that
\alpha_{n-m+i}\leq\beta_{i}\leq\alpha_{i}
for all
i\in\{1,\dots,m\}
. If the interlacing is
tight
(that is, there exists a
k\in\{0,\dots,m\}
such that
\alpha_{i}=\beta_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq k
and
\alpha_{n-m+i}=\beta_{i}
for
k+1\leq i\leq m
), then the partition is equitable
[
32
]
. In particular, if
S
is a subset of vertices of a regular graph and the eigenvalues of the quotient matrix
B
of
A
with respect to the partition
\{S,S^{c}\}
tightly interlace the eigenvalues of
A
, then
S
is an intriguing set.

Tight interlacing is used for establishing the equality case in the following theorem.

Theorem 2.1

(
[
50
, Theorem 3.5]
)
.

Let
G
be a
k
-regular graph on
n
vertices with Laplacian eigenvalues
0=\mu_{1}\leq\dots\leq\mu_{n}
. For any subset
S
of the vertices we have

\left(1-\frac{|S|}{n}\right)\mu_{2}\leq\frac{|\partial S|}{|S|}\leq\left(1-\frac{|S|}{n}\right)\mu_{n}.

If equality holds in either inequality, then
S
is an intriguing set.

For regular graphs, the lower bound of (
1
) now follows from applying the lower bound of Theorem
2.1
to a set
S
with
i(G)=i_{G}(S)
because
1-\frac{1}{n}|S|\geq\frac{1}{2}
. Similarly, (
2
) follows from applying the upper bound of Theorem
2.1
to any set
S
of size
\lfloor\frac{n}{2}\rfloor
. The upper bound of (
1
) does not follow as straightforwardly as the other two bounds, requiring also a spectral partitioning argument.

Specialising further from regular graphs, we consider distance-regular graphs. A connected graph
G
with diameter
D
is called
distance-regular
if there are constants
c_{i},a_{i},b_{i}
such that for all
i\in\{0,\dots,D\}
and all vertices
u
and
v
at distance
d(u,v)=i
, there are exactly
c_{i}
neighbours of
v
at distance
i-1
from
u
, there are exactly
a_{i}
neighbours of
v
at distance
i
from
u
, and exactly
b_{i}
neighbours of
v
at distance
i+1
from
u
.

In a distance-regular graph, the number of vertices at distance exactly
i
from a vertex
v
, is independent of the choice of
v
. The
intersection numbers

p_{ij}^{h}
denote the number of vertices
w
that are at distance
i
from
u
and
j
from
v
for a pair
u,v
with
d(u,v)=h
, and are independent of the choice of vertices.

3
Eigenvalue bounds for the isoperimetric number of graph powers

For a positive integer
t
, the
t
-th
graph power
of a graph
G=(V,E)
, denoted by
G^{t}
, is the graph with vertex set
V
where two distinct vertices are adjacent if there is a path in
G
of length at most
t
between them.
Graph powers help design efficient algorithms for specific combinatorial optimization problems, see for example

[
15
,
21
]
. In distributed computing, the
t
-th power of a graph
G
represents the possible flow of information during
k
rounds of communication in a distributed network of processors organized according to
G

[
65
]
.
The investigation of the behaviour of various parameters of powers of a fixed graph leads to many interesting problems, some of which are motivated by questions in information theory, communication complexity, Euclidian geometry and Ramsey theory, see
[
13
,
68
]
for an overview. There are multiple results on combinatorial parameters of graph powers, such as the chromatic number
[
10
,
53
,
59
]
, the independence number
[
5
,
2
]
, the Shannon capacity
[
4
,
11
]
, and the rainbow connection number
[
20
]
, among others. Less is known about spectral properties of the graph powers in terms of the spectral properties of the base graph
[
3
,
40
,
76
]
.

Motivated by the above, we investigate the isoperimetric number of graph powers, with a main focus on deriving sharp spectral bounds (Theorem
3.4
and Theorem
3.5
) using the eigenvalues of the base graph
G
instead of the eigenvalues of the power graph. Our bounds are based on the polynomial interlacing method developed in
[
2
]
and depend on a polynomial that can be chosen freely. For the case
t=2
we find the polynomial that gives the strongest possible bound (Theorems
3.7
and
3.9
), and we provide linear programming (LP) methods to find the best bound for general
t
(LP (
3
) and (
4
)).

Lower bounds on the expansion of
G^{t}
were also investigated in
[
61
]
; a well-known operation to improve graph expansion is the
t
-th power of
G
, which has a natural correspondence to simulating the random walk on
G
for
t
steps. However this was done in a different setting, in particular for
weighted graphs
: graphs (with self loops) together with a weight function
w:E(G)\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
on the edges. Accordingly, there are weighted variants of the definitions of the adjacency matrix, graph power, and isoperimetric number. The
weighted adjacency matrix

W
of a weighted graph has
W_{uv}=w(\{u,v\})
whenever
u
and
v
are adjacent, and 0 otherwise. The
weighted graph power

G^{t}
is the weighted graph with weighted adjacency matrix
W^{t}
. The
weighted isoperimetric number
can be expressed as

i_{W}(G)=\min\left\{\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{u\in S,v\notin S}w(u,v)\colon\,S\subseteq V,\,|S|\leq\frac{n}{2}\right\}.

The authors of
[
61
]
showed that for
1
-regular weighted graphs (meaning that the weights of the edges out of any vertex sum to 1) with all diagonal weights at least
\frac{1}{2}
it holds that

i_{W^{t}}(G^{t})\geq\frac{1}{20}(1-(1-i_{W}(G))^{\sqrt{t}}).

However, the weighted graph power is automatically spectrally related to the base weighted graph. In our unweighted setting, the spectra of a graph
G
and its graph power
G^{t}
are not related to each other in general (see for example
[
3
,
36
,
40
,
43
,
76
]
).

3.1
Bounds when the spectra of
G
and
G^{t}
are related

In the special case where the spectra of
G
and
G^{t}
are related by a polynomial (that is, if there exists a polynomial
p\in\mathbb{R}[x]
such that
A(G^{t})=p(A)
), we can use the existing bounds (
1
) and (
2
) to obtain bounds on the isoperimetric number of
G^{t}
in terms of the spectrum of
G
as follows. In particular, we can do this for distance-regular graphs, and, more generally, for
partially distance-polynomial graphs
(see
[
39
]
).

For a graph
G
and a polynomial
p\in\mathbb{R}[x]
, we define the following parameters.

\displaystyle\Lambda(p)

\displaystyle=\max_{2\leq i\leq n}p(\lambda_{i}),

\displaystyle\lambda(p)

\displaystyle=\min_{2\leq i\leq n}p(\lambda_{i}).

Lemma 3.1
.

Consider a regular graph
G
on
n
vertices such that there is a polynomial
p\in\mathbb{R}[x]
with
p(A)=A(G^{t})
. The following inequalities hold:

\displaystyle i(G^{t})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}(p(\lambda_{1})-\Lambda(p)),

\displaystyle i(G^{t})

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{p(\lambda_{1})^{2}-\Lambda(p)^{2}},

\displaystyle i(G^{t})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil}{n}(p(\lambda_{1})-\lambda(p)).

Proof.

Since
G
is regular, the all-ones vector is an eigenvector for the largest eigenvalue
\lambda_{1}
of
G
. Since
p(A)=A(G^{t})
, the all-ones vector is also an eigenvector of
G^{t}
, with eigenvalue
p(\lambda_{1})
. Now
G^{t}
is
p(\lambda_{1})
-regular, and
p(\lambda_{1})
is the largest adjacency eigenvalue of
G^{t}
. The other adjacency eigenvalues of
G^{t}
are
p(\lambda_{i})
for
i=2,\dots,n
. The result follows from Equations (
1
) and (
2
) applied to
G^{t}
.
∎

3.2
Bounds when the spectra of
G
and
G^{t}
are not related

In the more usual case where the spectra of
G
and
G^{t}
are not related, we need a different approach. In
[
2
]
, a method using interlacing of polynomials of the adjacency matrix of a graph was developed to generalize the inertia and Hoffman ratio bounds on the independence number of a graph to bounds on the independence number of a graph power. This polynomial interlacing method has also been used to obtain bounds of the chromatic number of a graph power
[
3
]
. We apply the method to derive results (Theorems
3.4
and
3.5
) that generalize the first and third bounds of Lemma
3.1
to all power graphs. Additionally, Theorems
3.4
and
3.5
can be seen as an extension of the lower bound of (
1
) and as an extension of (
2
) respectively. Since the upper bound of (
1
) (corresponding to the second bound of Lemma
3.1
) requires the spectral partitioning method, it is not directly suitable for generalization to general graph powers through the polynomial interlacing method.

To derive our main results in this section we need the following preliminary result.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
M
be a symmetric
n\times n
-matrix with eigenvalues
\nu_{1}\geq\dots\geq\nu_{n}
, such that
M
has constant row sum
\nu_{1}
. Let
S\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}
be a non-empty set of indices with
|S|\leq\frac{n}{2}
, and write
S^{c}=\{1,\dots,n\}\setminus S
. Write

i_{M}(S)=\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{u\in S}\sum_{v\in S^{c}}M_{uv}.

The following inequality holds:

\left(1-\frac{|S|}{n}\right)(\nu_{1}-\nu_{2})\leq i_{M}(S)\leq\left(1-\frac{|S|}{n}\right)\left(\nu_{1}-\nu_{n}\right).

Lemma
3.2
is a generalization of Theorem
2.1
, after applying it to the adjacency matrix of a regular graph, in which case
i_{A}(S)=i_{G}(S)
.

Proof.

Let
B
be the quotient matrix of
M
with respect to the partition
\{S,S^{c}\}
, then the eigenvalues of
M
interlace those of
B
. Since
M
has constant row sum
\nu_{1}
, also
B
has constant row sum
\nu_{1}
, and so
\nu_{1}
is an eigenvalue of
B
. Let
\beta
be the other eigenvalue of
B
.

Since the sum of eigenvalues is equal to the trace, we get

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle=\tr(B)-\nu_{1}=B_{1,1}+B_{2,2}-\nu_{1}=(\nu_{1}-B_{1,2})+(\nu_{1}-B_{2,1})-\nu_{1}

\displaystyle=\nu_{1}-(B_{2,1}+B_{1,2})=\nu_{1}-\frac{n}{n-|U|}B_{1,2},

where in the last step we use
B_{2,1}=\frac{|S|}{n-|S|}B_{1,2}
(which follows from the definition of the quotient matrix).

By interlacing,
\beta\leq\nu_{1}
, so that
\beta
is the second largest eigenvalue of
B
. Hence, again by interlacing, we have

\nu_{2}\geq\beta=\nu_{1}-\frac{n}{n-|S|}B_{1,2},

from which the first inequality follows, and
\nu_{n}\leq\beta
, from which the second inequality follows.
∎

Now we are ready to show the lower and upper bounds of the isoperimetric number of a graph power in terms of the spectrum of the base graph. Throughout the proofs, we use the following key observation.

Observation 3.3
.

If
p
is any polynomial, and
u
and
v
are vertices of
G
at distance more than the degree of
p
, then
p(A)_{uv}=0
. This is because
A^{i}_{uv}
counts the number of walks of length
i
between
u
and
v
; it is zero if
u
and
v
have distance more than
i
.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
G
be a regular graph on
n
vertices with adjacency eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{n}
. Let
p\in\mathbb{R}_{t}[x]
, and define
W(p)=\max_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(p(A))_{ij}
the maximum off-diagonal entry of
p(A)
. If
W(p)>0
and
p(\lambda_{1})\geq\Lambda(p)
, then

i(G^{t})\geq\frac{p(\lambda_{1})-\Lambda(p)}{2W(p)}.

Proof.

Since
p(\lambda_{1})\geq\Lambda(p)
, we know that
\nu_{1}=p(\lambda_{1})
and
\nu_{2}=\Lambda(p)
, and that the matrix
p(A)
has constant row sum
\nu_{1}=p(\lambda_{1})
because the all-ones vector is an eigenvector of
p(A)
with eigenvalue
p(\lambda_{1})
. We can now apply Lemma
3.2
to
p(A)
.

Let
S
be a subset of vertices of
G
such that
|S|\leq\frac{n}{2}
and

i(G^{t})=i_{G^{t}}(S)=\frac{|\partial_{G^{t}}(S)|}{|S|}.

The first inequality of Lemma
3.2
applied to
M=p(A)
and
S
, gives

i_{p(A)}(S)\geq\frac{p(\lambda_{1})-\Lambda(p)}{2}.

To complete the proof, note that
0\leq W(p)i(G^{t})
and that

i_{p(A)}(S)=\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{u\in S}\sum_{v\in S^{c}}(p(A))_{uv}\leq W(p)\cdot\frac{|\partial_{G^{t}}(S)|}{|S|}=W(p)i_{G^{t}}(S)=W(p)i(G^{t})

since
(p(A))_{uv}=0
if
d(u,v)>t
by Observation
3.3
, and
(p(A))_{uv}\leq W(p)
otherwise, which is precisely when
(u,v)
is part of the edge cut
(S,S^{c})
in
G^{t}
.
∎

Note that the first inequality of Lemma
3.1
, when
\deg(p)\leq t
, also follows from Theorem
3.4
, for any polynomial
p
with
p(A)=A(G^{t})
we have
W(p)=1
.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
G
be a non-empty regular graph on
n
vertices with adjacency eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{n}
. Let
p\in\mathbb{R}_{t}[x]
, and define

w(p)=\min\left\{(p(A))_{uv}\colon\,1\leq d(u,v)\leq t\right\}.

If
w(p)>0
, then

i(G^{t})\leq\frac{\lceil\frac{n}{2}\rceil}{n}\cdot\frac{p(\lambda_{1})-\lambda(p)}{w(p)}.

Proof.

Since the all-ones vector is an eigenvector of
p(A)
with eigenvalue
p(\lambda_{1})
, we have that
p(A)
has constant row sum
p(\lambda_{1})
. In order to apply Lemma
3.2
, we claim that this row sum
p(\lambda_{1})
is the largest eigenvalue of
p(A)
. Note that this statement does not change if we change the constant coefficient of
p
, so without loss of generality, we may assume that
p(A)
has only non-negative values on the diagonal. Also off-diagonal,
p(A)
is non-negative: if
u\neq v
and
d(u,v)\leq t
, then
p(A)_{uv}\geq w(p)>0
, and if
d(u,v)>t
, then
p(A)_{uv}=0
by Observation
3.3
. Let
\nu
be any eigenvalue of
p(A)
with eigenvector
x
, and let
j
be such that
|x_{j}|
is maximal. We get

|\nu x_{j}|=\left|\sum_{i}p(A)_{ij}x_{i}\right|\leq\sum_{i}p(A)_{ij}|x_{i}|\leq\sum_{i}p(A)_{ij}|x_{j}|=p(\lambda_{1})|x_{j}|,

so
\nu\leq|\nu|\leq p(\lambda_{1})
. We can now apply Lemma

to

.

Let

be any subset of vertices of

. The second inequality of Lemma
3.2
applied to

and

To complete the proof, note that

i_{p(A)}(S)=\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{u\in S}\sum_{v\in S^{c}}(p(A))_{uv}\geq w(p)\cdot\frac{|\partial_{G^{t}}(S)|}{|S|}=w(p)i_{G^{t}}(S)\geq w(p)i(G^{t}),

since all entries of

that do not correspond to an edge in

are zero (because the degree of

is at most

), and all entries of
p(A)
corresponding to an edge
(u,v)
in the edge cut
(S,S^{c})
of

are at least
w(p)
.
∎

The third inequality of Lemma
3.1
also follows from Theorem

, since for such a polynomial we have

.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
G
be a non-complete regular connected graph with adjacency eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{n}
. Let
\Lambda
be the maximum number of common neighbours of adjacent vertices of
G
, and let
M
be the maximum number of neighbours of non-adjacent vertices of
G
. The following choices of
p
are optimal for the bound of Theorem
3.4
when
t=2
.

i.

If
\Lambda-\lambda_{n}\leq M+\lambda_{2}
, then
p=x^{2}+(M-\Lambda)x
, giving

i(G^{2})\geq\frac{(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}+M-\Lambda)}{2M}.

ii.

If
M+\lambda_{2}\leq\Lambda-\lambda_{n}\leq\lambda_{1}
, then
p=x^{2}+(M-\Lambda)x
, giving

i(G^{2})\geq\frac{(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{n})(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{n}+M-\Lambda)}{2M}.

iii.

If
\max(\lambda_{1},M+\lambda_{2})\leq\Lambda-\lambda_{n}
, then
p=x^{2}-(\lambda_{2}+\lambda_{n})x
, giving

i(G^{2})\geq\frac{(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{n})}{2(\Lambda-\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{n})}.

Proof.

Let
p=ax^{2}+bx+c\in\mathbb{R}_{2}[x]
. Let
i(p)=\frac{p(\lambda_{1})-\Lambda(p)}{2W(p)}
denote the bound from Theorem
3.4
. We optimize
i(p)
for the parameters
a
,
b
, and
c
, provided that
p(\lambda_{1})\geq\Lambda(p)
and
W(p)>0
. First of all, note that scaling by a positive number and shifting does not change the value of
i(p)
, so that we may assume without loss of generality that
c=0
and
a\in\{-1,0,1\}
. We make a case distinction according to the value of
a
.

•

If
a=0
, then by
W(p)>0
and scaling, we may assume that
b=1
, and so
p=x
and
i(p)=(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{n})/n
.

•

If
a=-1
, then
p=-x^{2}+bx
. We show that this case gives bounds that are worse than the bound from the case
a=0
. To show this, we investigate the numerator and denominator of
i(p)
.

For the numerator of
i(p)
we note the following. For any
i,j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, we have that
p(\lambda_{i})\geq p(\lambda_{j})
if and only if
b\geq\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{j}
or
\lambda_{i}=\lambda_{j}
. Since
G
is connected,
\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}
by the Perron-Frobenius Theorem
[
32
]
. Thus, in order for
p(\lambda_{1})\geq\Lambda(p)
, by the above we need
b\geq\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}
, and consequently
\Lambda(p)=p(\lambda_{2})
, again by the above. Now
p(\lambda_{1})-\Lambda(p)=p(\lambda_{1})-p(\lambda_{2})=(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})(b-\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})
.

For the denominator of
i(p)
, we have the following. Write
\eta
for the minimum number of common neighbours of two adjacent vertices of
G
. In order to have

we need
b>\eta
because

only has non-negative entries. In this case
W(p)=b-\eta
.
So, for
p(\lambda_{1})\geq\Lambda(p)
and

, we need that

and

, in which case

i(p)=\frac{(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})(b-\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})}{2(b-\eta)}=\frac{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}}{2}\left(1+\frac{\eta-\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}}{b-\eta}\right).

Note that
\eta<\lambda_{1}-1<\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}
since

is not complete, so

, implying that the bounds coming from polynomials in this case are worse than the bound from the polynomial
p=x
as in the above case
a=0
.

•

If
a=1
, then
p=x^{2}+bx
. We again investigate the numerator and denominator of
i(p)
.

For the numerator of
i(p)
, again if

, then
p(\lambda_{i})\geq p(\lambda_{j})
if and only if

or
\lambda_{i}=\lambda_{j}
. In order to have

, we therefore need that

. Furthermore, by convexity of the parabola

, we have
\Lambda(p)\in\left\{p(\lambda_{2}),p(\lambda_{n})\right\}
, and if
b\geq-\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{n}
, then
\Lambda(p)=p(\lambda_{2})
. If instead
-\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{n}\leq b\leq-\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{n}
, then
\Lambda(p)=p(\lambda_{n})
. Summarizing, we have

p(\lambda_{1})-\Lambda(p)=\begin{cases}$negative$&$if $b<-\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{n},\\
(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{n})(b+\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{n})&$if $-\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{n}\leq b\leq-\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{n},\\
(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})(b+\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})&$if $-\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{n}\leq b.\end{cases}

For the denominator of
i(p)
, note that
W(p)=\max(\Lambda+b,M)
. Since
G
is non-complete and connected,

, so

is automatically satisfied. We now have

W(p)=\begin{cases}M&$if $b\leq M-\Lambda,\\
\Lambda+b&$if $M-\Lambda\leq b.\end{cases}

To optimize the value of
i(p)
, we look at the behaviour of
i(p)
on three (possibly empty) key intervals. Recall that
b\geq-\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{n}
.

1.

If
-\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{n}\leq b\leq M-\Lambda
, then the denominator of
i(p)
is constant and equal to
M
. The numerator is increasing, so

is increasing.

2.

If
\max(-\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{n},M-\Lambda)\leq b\leq-\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{n}
, then

i(p)=\frac{(\lambda-\lambda_{n})(b+\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{n})}{2(b+\Lambda)}=\frac{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{n}}{2}\left(1+\frac{\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{n}-\Lambda}{b+\Lambda}\right).

This is increasing if
\Lambda>\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{n}
, constant if
\Lambda=\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{n}
, and decreasing if
\Lambda<\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{n}
.

3.

If

, then

i(p)=\frac{(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})(b+\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})}{2(b+\Lambda)}=\frac{\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}}{2}\left(1+\frac{\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}-\Lambda}{b+\Lambda}\right).

.

To complete the proof, we now make the following case distinction.

–

If

, then by observations 1 and 3 we know that

is increasing for

, and decreasing for

, so that the optimal value for

is

, giving the bound

i(G^{2})\geq i(p)=\frac{(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}+M-\Lambda)}{2M},

which is greater than

because
\Lambda<\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}
.

–

If

and
\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{n}\leq\Lambda

is
-\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{n}
, giving the bound

i(G^{2})\geq i(p)=\frac{(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2})(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{n})}{2(\Lambda-\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{n})},

which is again greater than
i(x)
because
\Lambda<\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}
.

–

Lastly, if
M-\Lambda\leq-\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{n}
and

, then we must have
-\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{n}\leq M-\Lambda
, and so by observations 1, 2, and 3, the optimal value of

is
M-\Lambda
, giving the bound

i(G^{2})\geq i(p)=\frac{(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{n})(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{n}+M-\Lambda)}{2M}.

Again, this is greater than

, because

.

This concludes the proof.

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
G
be a non-empty graph with adjacency eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{n}
. If
\eta
is the minimum number of common neighbours of two adjacent vertices, then
\eta\leq\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{n}
.

Proof.

Suppose, by contradiction, that
\eta>\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{n}
.

First, assume that
G
is connected. Consider the polynomial
p(x)=x^{2}-(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{n})x
, and note that
p(\lambda_{1})=p(\lambda_{n})
. Note that
p(A)
is a non-negative matrix: if
u=v
, then
p(A)_{uv}=\deg(u)>0
, if
u\neq v
and
u\not\sim v
, then
p(A)_{uv}=(A^{2})_{uv}\geq 0
, and if
u\sim v
, then
p(A)_{uv}=(A^{2})_{uv}-(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{n})\geq\eta-\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{n}>0
. Furthermore, note that
p(A)^{m}
is a positive matrix if
m
is at least the diameter of
G
, so that
p(A)
is primitive. By the Perron-Frobenius Theorem
[
32
]
, the eigenvalue
p(\lambda_{1})
for the all-ones eigenvector of
p(A)
must be strictly larger in absolute value than any other eigenvalue of
p(A)
. In particular
p(\lambda_{1})>p(\lambda_{n})
, a contradiction.

Next, if
G
is disconnected, then let
C
be a connected component of
G
that has smallest eigenvalue
\lambda_{n}
. Such a connected component exists because the spectrum of
G
is equal to the disjoint union of the spectra of its connected components. Note that the largest eigenvalue
\lambda_{\max}(C)
of
C
is bounded above by
\lambda_{1}
. Applying the above argument to
C
, we get that there are two adjacent vertices in
C
that have at most
\lambda_{\max}(C)+\lambda_{n}\leq\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{n}
common neighbours. The result now follows for
G
.
∎

Theorem 3.9
.

Let
G
be a regular graph with adjacency eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}\geq\dots\geq\lambda_{n}

\eta
be the minimum number of common neighbours of adjacent vertices, and let

be the minimum number of common neighbours of two vertices at distance 2 in

be the eigenvalue of

Proof.

In order to prove this theorem we will use Proposition
3.8
. Let

for the parameters

,

, and

, provided that

. First of all, note that scaling by a positive number and shifting does not change the value of

, so that we may assume without loss of generality that

and

. However, if

, then let

and

for the parameter

For the denominator

w(p)=\begin{cases}\eta+b&$if $b\leq\xi-\eta,\\
\xi&$if $\xi-\eta\leq b.\end{cases}

In order for

For the numerator

of

We now show that

4
Approximating the isoperimetric number of distance-regular graphs

In this section we focus on distance-regular graphs and specifically on lower bounds for the sparsity of distance-regular graphs. The sparsity of a graph can be used as a
2
-approximation of the (normalized) isoperimetric number. In particular, one sees that

\frac{1}{2}\sigma(G)\leq\frac{i(G)}{n}\leq\sigma(G).

(5)

Upper bounds on the isoperimetric number of distance-regular graphs were studied in
[
60
,
72
]
. In
[
72
]
the authors conjecture that
i(G)\leq\mu_{2}
for all distance-regular graphs. This, combined with the lower bound
1
, would imply that
\mu_{2}
is a
2
-approximation for the isoperimetric number. They prove the bound for strongly regular graphs, that is, distance-regular graphs with diameter
D=2
. A more involved upper bound for distance-regular graphs, using limits of Green functions, is given in
[
60
]
.

An alternative lower bound for the isoperimetric number comes from a linear program relaxation for the sparsity
[
66
]
, see LP (
7
). For strongly regular graphs, a closed formula for the optimal value of LP (
7
) was found in
[
74
]
(note that this paper uses “isoperimetric number” to denote what we call the sparsity of
G
). The main contribution of this section is the extension of this approach to all distance-regular graphs, implying that the optimal value of the linear program can be computed using only the intersection array, without having to construct the distance-regular graph. Let
k_{j}
denote the number of vertices at distance
j
from any given vertex. The lower bound for the sparsity that we obtain is

\sigma(G)\geq\frac{k_{1}}{\sum_{j=1}^{D}jk_{j}},

(6)

see Corollary
4.3
.

As a byproduct (see Corollary
4.4
), we show that taking a single vertex gives a
2D
-approximation of the isoperimetric number of distance-regular graphs.

In
[
44
]
the authors use another LP relaxation to study the minimum cut size between any
S
and
S^{c}
with
|S|
is fixed. This is the so-called CR
⋆
bound. From this relaxation, one obtains a lower bound for the isoperimetric number that improves on Alon’s lower bound
1
. We will show that our bound is incomparable with these lower bounds.

Denote by
\text{LP}(G)
the objective value of the following linear program
[
66
]
:

\boxed{\begin{array}[]{rll}{\tt minimize}&\sum_{u<v,u\sim v}\,x_{uv}\\
{\tt subject\ to}&\sum_{u<v}x_{uv}\leq 1,\\
&x_{uv}+x_{vw}-x_{uw}\geq 0&\forall u,v,w:u<v<w,\\
&x_{uv}\geq 0&\forall u,v:u<v.\\
\end{array}}

(7)

Let
G
be a graph and let
S\subseteq V
such that
\sigma(S)=\sigma(G)
. Consider the assignment
x_{uv}=(|S||V\setminus S|)^{-1}
if
|\{u,v\}\cap\partial S|=1
and
0
otherwise. Note that such an assignment is feasible for LP (
7
) and has objective value
\sigma(S)
. Hence,
\text{LP}(G)\leq\sigma(G)
.

Let
W_{G}
denote
\sum_{u<v}d(u,v)
, the Wiener index of the graph.
If
G
is a distance-regular graph with diameter
D=2
, that is, a strongly regular graph, then the optimal solution for LP (
7
) is achieved by taking
x_{uv}=d(u,v)/W_{G}

[
74
]
.

We show that
x_{uv}=d(u,v)/W_{G}
achieves the optimal value for all distance-regular graphs. Note that this assignment is feasible and
gives (
6
). Following the strategy from
[
74
]
, we show that the dual of LP (
7
) admits a feasible solution with the same objective value.

Let
A
be the adjacency matrix of
G
. We write the dual of LP (
7
):

\boxed{\begin{array}[]{rll}{\tt maximize}&\psi\\
{\tt subject\ to}&\sum_{w\in V\setminus\{u,v\}}\,y_{uw}^{v}+y_{vw}^{u}-y_{uv}^{w}+\psi\leq A_{uv},&\forall u,v:u<v\\
&y_{uv}^{w}\geq 0&\forall u,v,w:u\neq v\neq w.\\
\end{array}}

(8)

Consider the following restriction on the variables of LP (
8
). Choose
y_{uw}^{v}
to be zero unless
w
is on a shortest path from
u
to
v
, in which case its value will only depend on
d(u,v)
. Distance-regularity of
G
implies that the constraint for each pair
u,v
depends only on
d(u,v)
. Hence this LP can be written using a condition and variable,

. Note that

does not appear in any of the constraints, because there can be no vertex on the path

\boxed{\begin{array}[]{rll}{\tt maximize}&\psi\\
{\tt subject\ to}&\psi=1-2\sum_{i=2}^{D}\,p_{i,i-1}^{1}y_{i},\\
&\psi=\sum_{i=1}^{h-1}\,p_{i,h-i}^{h}y_{h}-2\sum_{i=h+1}^{D}\;p_{i,i-h}^{h}y_{i},&\forall h\in\{2,\dots,D\},\\
&y_{h}\geq 0&\forall h\in\{2,\dots,D\}.\\
\end{array}}

(9)

We have separated the first constraint, which corresponds to

being adjacent. Since we are adding restrictions to a maximization problem, LP (

), which in turn gives a lower bound on the sparsity.

Before finding the solution to LP (

), we make one further observation about the intersection numbers of distance-regular graphs.

Lemma 4.1
.

For any distance-regular graph with diameter

and for all

, it holds that

\sum_{i=1}^{h-1}2hk_{i}p_{h,h-i}^{i}=hk_{h}\sum_{i=1}^{h-1}p_{i,h-i}^{h}.

Proof.

We first prove that
k_{i}p_{h-i,h}^{i}=k_{h-i}p_{i,h}^{h-i}
for all
1\leq i\leq h-1\leq D-1
by a double counting of the set

\{(u,v,w)\in V^{3}\colon\,d(u,v)=i,\,d(v,w)=h-i\text{ and }d(u,w)=h\}.

There are
n
possible values for
u
. By definition of
k_{i}
and
p_{h-i,h}^{i}
, there are
k_{i}
options for
v
given
u
and there are
p_{h-i,h}^{i}
options for
w
given
u
and
v
. For the other counting, we first fix

. There are
n
options for
w
,
k_{h-i}
options for
v
given

and
p_{i,h}^{h-i}
options for

given

and

. Hence
nk_{i}p_{h-i,h}^{i}=nk_{h-i}p_{i,h}^{h-i}
. From this it follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{h-1}2ik_{i}p_{h-i,h}^{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{h-1}ik_{i}p_{h-i,h}^{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{h-1}ik_{h-i}p_{i,h}^{h-i}

(10)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{h-1}ik_{i}p_{h-i,h}^{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{h-1}(h-i)k_{i}p_{h-i,h}^{i}

\displaystyle=h\sum_{i=1}^{h-1}k_{i}p_{h-i,h}^{i}.

By double counting the set

\{(u,v,w)\in V^{2}\colon\,d(u,v)=h\text{ and }\exists\,i\colon\,d(v,w)=i\text{ and }d(u,w)=h-i\}.

we deduce analogously that

nk_{h}\sum_{i=1}^{h-1}p_{i,h-i}^{h}=n\sum_{i=1}^{h-1}k_{i}p_{h,h-i}^{i}

It follows directly that

\displaystyle hk_{h}\sum_{i=1}^{h-1}p_{i,h-i}^{h}

\displaystyle=h\sum_{i=1}^{h-1}k_{i}p_{h,h-i}^{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{h-1}2ik_{i}p_{h,h-i}^{i}

where the second equality follows from Equation (

).
∎

Theorem 4.2
.

LP (

) has a solution with
\frac{k_{1}}{\sum_{j=1}^{D}jk_{j}}
.

Proof.

For

We show that, for each

cancel in the right hand side of the sum

. For any

the variable

only appears in the constraints

. In particular, the coefficient of

By Lemma

this is equal to zero. Hence

We show by induction on

with all

Corollary 4.3
.

The sparsity of a distance-regular graph

(11)

Corollary 4.4
.

Let

Let

On the other hand, consider any singleton

The

Note that Corollary

5
The exact isoperimetric number and a connection with finite geometry

The determination of the exact value of the isoperimetric number for a given graph is in general an NP-hard problem. The aim of this section is to find the exact value in some particular cases. First, we recall some known results, and derive the isoperimetric number for generalized Hamming graphs, connecting with previous results
[
69
,
73
]
. Next, we study the exact value for several new families of graphs. Using a simple but powerful observation derived from Corollary
5.5
, we show that the isoperimetric number attains the spectral lower bound
\frac{1}{2}\mu_{2}
from (
1
) if and only if there exists a
tight set
(of Type II) containing half the number of vertices of the graph. The link between the isoperimetric number and tight sets
opens a door to a connection between graph expansion and finite geometry, providing a way to derive the isoperimetric number of graphs coming from geometries.
As far as we know, previously only the exact
vertex
-isoperimetric number was studied in geometric graphs
[
57
,
71
]
, and on the (edge-)isoperimetric number of geometric graphs, only bounds have been studied
[
62
]
.
Tight sets in collinearity graphs of polar spaces have been widely studied in the literature, and the spectra of the related graphs are known, making these graphs natural candidates for examples of graphs where we can compute the isoperimetric number directly.

5.4
Tight sets

Let
G
be a regular graph on
n
vertices. Following
[
42
]
, we define:

•

If
S
is a set of vertices satisfying
\frac{|\partial S|}{|S|}=\left(1-\frac{|S|}{n}\right)\mu_{n}
, then
S
is called a
tight set of Type I
. These sets are exactly the ones meeting the upper bound of Theorem
2.1

•

If
S
is a set of vertices satisfying
\frac{|\partial S|}{|S|}=(1-\frac{|S|}{n})\mu_{2}
, then
S
is called a
tight set of Type II
. These sets are exactly the ones meeting the lower bound of Theorem
2.1
.

Following
[
18
,
19
,
70
]
, we simply write
tight set
without further specification, to refer to a tight set of Type II. However, be aware that the notions of a tight set (that is, a tight set of Type II) and tight set of Type I are complementary to each other, as the next result illustrates.

Lemma 5.4
.

A tight set of Type I is a tight set in the complementary graph.

Proof.

Since
\mu_{i}(G^{c})+\mu_{n+2-i}(G)=n
and
|\partial_{G^{c}}(S)|+|\partial_{G}(S)|=|S|\cdot\left(n-|S|\right)
, we have that

\frac{|\partial_{G}(S)|}{|S|}=\left(1-\frac{|S|}{n}\right)\mu_{2}(G)\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\frac{|\partial_{G^{c}}(S)|}{|S|}=\left(1-\frac{|S|}{n}\right)\mu_{n}(G^{c}).\qed

The notion of tight sets was first introduced in the context of generalized quadrangles in
[
70
]
, then extended to polar spaces of higher rank in
[
45
]
and later to regular graphs in
[
42
]
.
By definition of a tight set, we have the following.

Corollary 5.5
.

If
G
is a regular graph on
n
vertices, then
i(G)\geq\frac{1}{2}\mu_{2}
with equality if and only if there is a tight set of size
\frac{n}{2}
.

Proof.

This follows from Theorem
2.1
since
|S|\leq\frac{n}{2}\Leftrightarrow 1-\frac{|S|}{n}\geq\frac{1}{2}
.
∎

Note that, if
S
is a tight set. then every vertex in
S
has
i(G)=\frac{1}{2}\mu_{2}
neighbours outside
S
and
k-\frac{1}{2}\mu_{2}
neighbours within
S
, where

is the valency of
G
.

Corollary
5.5
is a powerful tool to determine the isoperimetric number in any graph class where tight sets are understood, as we illustrate in Sections

and
5.6
. As mentioned above, tight sets were originally introduced in a geometrical context, so it should not come as a surprise that it is exactly for those geometrical graph classes that we can say a lot about the existence of tight sets, and hence about graph expansion. Despite that, we are not aware of previous research studying the isoperimetric number of geometric graphs. The remainder of this section is dedicated to a number of cases in which the exact isoperimetric number can be determined.

Let

A

5.6
Polar graphs

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a classical polar space over
\mathbb{F}_{q}
. We refer to
[
33
, Chapter 2]
for the definitions and basic properties of polar spaces, including spectral data of their (strongly regular) collinearity graphs.

A maximal singular subspace with respect to inclusion is called a
generator
.
Every generator has the same vector space dimension
r
, which is called the
rank
of the polar space. In particular, any generator has
\frac{q^{r}-1}{q-1}
points. We further recall that if
(q,t)
is the order of
\mathcal{P}
, where
t\in\{1,q^{\frac{1}{2}},q,q^{\frac{3}{2}},q^{2}\}
, then
\mathcal{P}
has
(tq^{r-1}+1)\cdot\frac{q^{r}-1}{q-1}
points.
We consider the collinearity graph
\Gamma(\mathcal{P})
on the points of a polar space, where two points are adjacent if they are in a common generator.

To obtain tight sets of the desired size in the collinearity graphs, we use the concept of spreads.
A
partial spread
(of generators) is a set of generators that pairwise intersect trivially. A
spread
(of generators) is a partial spread that covers all points of
\mathcal{P}
.

Lemma 5.8
.

If there exists a partial spread
covering exactly half the number of points, then the isoperimetric number of the collinearity graph
\Gamma(\mathcal{P})
is equal to
\frac{1}{2}(tq^{r-1}+1)\frac{q^{r-1}-1}{q-1}
.

Proof.

A generator is a tight set, and the disjoint union of tight sets is again a tight set, see
[
45
, Theorem 8.1]
for a characterization of tight sets in polar spaces.
Hence, partial spreads are tight.

We conclude from
5.5
that
i(G)=\frac{1}{2}\mu_{2}
, which can be computed to be equal to
\frac{1}{2}(tq^{r-1}+1)\frac{q^{r-1}-1}{q-1}
using the spectral data in
[
33
, Theorem 2.2.12]
.
∎

If there is a (full) spread with an even number of generators (in which case
q
must be odd), the condition in Lemma
5.8
is met by taking half of a spread.
In fact, in the following examples, the polar space always has a spread.
We refer to
[
54
, Table 7.4]
for an overview of existence results on spreads in polar spaces.

•

The collinearity graph of the hyperbolic quadric

(

if

is odd, by taking half the number of lines of a regulus (line spread). Note that this graph is in fact isomorphic to the square lattice

, so this is a specific instance of the results on Hamming graphs (see Section
5.2
).

•

For the elliptic quadric
Q^{-}(5,q)
(

The hyperbolic quadric

The following examples serve to show that there are also cases where one can find a tight set of size

The complement of the collinearity graph of a polar space is again strongly regular and worth investigating. Tight sets in these graphs are tight sets of Type I in the collinearity graphs, see Lemma

Still, we can derive the isoperimetric number of some of these complements, because tight sets of Type I are equivalent to so-called

An

6
The isoperimetric number is not determined by the spectrum

There are many graph parameters that can be bounded by eigenvalues. One can wonder whether such properties or parameters are actually determined by the spectrum. Typically these are not. Instances of such properties include: distance-regularity
[
52
]
, having a given diameter
[
52
]
, admitting a perfect matching
[
23
]
, Hamiltonicity
[
67
]
, vertex or edge-connectivity
[
51
]
and having a given zero forcing number
[
1
]
.
In this section, we add the isoperimetric number to that list.
In fact, we show that there is an infinite family of pairs of regular connected cospectral graphs that have different isoperimetric numbers. We note that this has already been proven for the vertex-isoperimetric number in
[
48
]
.

The hypercube
Q_{n}\cong\mathrm{H}(n,2)
has isoperimetric number
i(Q_{n})=1

[
69
]
. Indeed, any subcube
Q_{n-1}
is a tight set of size
2^{n-1}
, see Corollary
5.5
. Consider the tesseract
Q_{4}
. It was shown in
[
55
]
that
Q_{4}
has exactly one cospectral mate, which we denote by
\widetilde{Q_{4}}
. It can be obtained by GM-switching
[
49
]
on the four neighbours of a fixed vertex (see
[
32
, Chapter 1.8.1]
for a figure).

Proposition 6.1
.

There are infinitely many pairs of regular connected graphs that have the same adjacency and Laplacian spectrum but different isoperimetric numbers.

Proof.

The graphs

Q_{4}\times H(n,4)\quad\text{ and }\quad\widetilde{Q_{4}}\times H(n,4),

where
\times
denotes the Cartesian graph product, form an infinite family of pairs of regular connected cospectral graphs because the spectrum of a product is determined by that of its factors. The graphs are regular, so they are also cospectral for the Laplacian spectrum. We have that
i(Q_{4})=1
, and it is known that
i(G\times K_{2n})=\min\{i(G),n\}
for all
n\geq 2
, see
[
69
, Theorem 5.1]
. In particular,
i(Q_{4}\times H(n,4))=1
and
i(\widetilde{Q_{4}}\times H(n,4))=\min\{i(\widetilde{Q_{4}}),2\}
. We prove that
i(\widetilde{Q_{4}})>1
, and hence
i(Q_{4}\times H(n,4))\neq i(\widetilde{Q_{4}}\times H(n,4))
. One can check by computer that
i(\widetilde{Q_{4}})=5/4
, but here we provide a purely mathematical proof.

By Corollary
5.5
, it suffices to prove that
\widetilde{Q_{4}}
does not have a tight set of size eight. Suppose, by contradiction, that
S
is a tight set of size eight in
\widetilde{Q_{4}}
. Every vertex in
S
has three neighbours in
S
and one neighbour in
S^{c}
(and vice versa).
Let
v
be the chosen vertex such that
\widetilde{Q_{4}}
is obtained by GM-switching
[
49
]
on
Q_{4}
with respect to the four neighbours of
v
. We may assume that
v\in S
, otherwise we can consider
S^{c}
instead of
S
. Now
v
has exactly one neighbour
w
with
w\notin S
. Note that, in the original graph
Q_{4}
,
w
is contained in a unique cube that does not contain
v
. Let
u_{1},u_{2}
and
u_{3}
be the three neighbours of
w
in that cube. They were originally adjacent to two neighbours of
v
(including
w
), so after switching they are adjacent to two neighbours of
v
in
S
. Hence they are also contained in
S
. Similarly, the vertices that used to be at distance two from
w
in the cube in
Q_{4}
are (still) adjacent to two of the vertices of
\{u_{1},u_{2},u_{3}\}
, so they are also in
S
. All the considered vertices are different, so we proved that
|S|\geq 10
, a contradiction.
∎

7
The isoperimetric number of random split graphs

In this section, we write
o(1)
for quantities that tend to
0
as
n\to\infty
and say that an event holds with high probability (w.h.p.) if its probability tends to
1
as
n\to\infty
.

Almost all
k
-regular graphs have isoperimetric number of order
k/2-\Theta(\sqrt{n})

[
28
,
9
]
. For irregular graphs, the situation seems to change. The simplest upper bound for the isoperimetric number is achieved by taking a vertex of minimum degree, and there is some evidence that this upper bound is typically tight for irregular graphs. In particular,
[
22
]
studied the isoperimetric number during a specific random graph process. In this process they start with the empty graph
E_{n}
, and at each step an edge is added uniformly at random from all the missing edges. The authors of
[
22
]
show that for almost all graphs in the process,
i(G)=\delta(G)
as long as
\delta(G)=o(\log n)
. In this section we study a different class of irregular graphs and show that the random split graph satisfies
i(G)=\delta(G)
with high probability.

A graph
G=(V,E)
is called
split
if the vertex set can be partitioned into two disjoint subsets
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
such that
G[V_{1}]\cong K_{k}
and
G[V_{2}]\cong E_{\ell}
for some
k
and
\ell

[
31
, Definition 3.2.3]
. Split graphs have attracted considerable attention within the spectral graph theory community in recent years, frequently appearing as extremal examples in eigenvalue maximization problems
[
35
]
, and playing a key role in the resolution of the Grone–Merris conjecture
[
17
]
. Based on the work of Grone and Merris, in
[
6
]
the authors showed a spectral upper bound for
i(G)
. As a byproduct of our results, we prove that this bound is tight w.h.p. for random split graphs.

We use the construction of
random split graphs
from
[
30
]
. Given two positive integers
k
and
\ell
, define
G_{k,\ell}
as the following random graph. The vertex set of
G_{k,\ell}
is a disjoint union
V_{1}\cup V_{2}
, where
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
have size
k
and
\ell
respectively. The induced subgraph on
V_{1}
is complete and the induced subgraph on
V_{2}
is edgeless. Every pair
\{v_{1},v_{2}\}
with
v_{1}\in V_{1}
and
v_{2}\in V_{2}
, is an edge with probability
1/2
. These
k\ell
choices are made independently. The main result of this section is the following:

Theorem 7.1
.

If
G_{k,k}
is a random split graph, then with high probability

i(G_{k,k})=\delta(G_{k,k}).

The proof idea is as follows. We consider two cases for the set
S
that minimizes
i(G_{k,k})
. If
|S|\leq|V|/4
, we show that every split graph
G
satisfies
\absolutevalue{\partial S}/\absolutevalue{S}\geq\delta(G)
as long as the minimum degree is bounded (Lemma
7.2
), a condition fulfilled by
G_{k,k}
w.h.p. (Lemma
7.3
). If
|S|\geq|V|/4
, we use Chernoff’s inequality (Lemma
7.4
) together with a union bound to show that w.h.p.
\absolutevalue{\partial S}/\absolutevalue{S}
is greater than
\delta(G_{k,k})
(Lemma
7.5
).

Lemma 7.2
.

Let
G=(V_{1}\cup V_{2},E)
be a split graph with
|V_{1}|=|V_{2}|=k
and with
\delta(G)\leq k/2
. For every
S\subseteq V_{1}\cup V_{2}
with
|S|\leq k/2
,

\frac{|\partial S|}{|S|}\geq\delta(G).

Proof.

Let
s:=|S|
and
a:=|S\cap V_{1}|
. Every vertex in
S\cap V_{1}
has at least
k-a
edges from
S
to
V_{1}\setminus S
and every vertex in
S\cap V_{2}
has at least
\delta(G)-a
edges from
S
to
V_{1}\setminus S
. So

\displaystyle\frac{|\partial S|}{|S|}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(k-a)a+(\delta(G)-a)(s-a)}{s}

\displaystyle=\delta(G)+\frac{a}{s}(k-s-\delta(G))

\displaystyle\geq\delta(G).\qed

Lemma 7.3
.

With high probability

\delta(G_{k,k})\leq\frac{k}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{k\log k}.

Proof.

The degree of each vertex
v_{i}\in V_{2}
is a binomial random variable
\text{deg}(v_{i})\sim\text{Bin}(k,1/2)
. It follows from
[
29
, Theorem 3.3]
that, w.h.p.,

\min_{i}\text{deg}(v_{i})=\frac{k}{2}-(1+o(1))\sqrt{\frac{k\log k}{2}}.

Since
\delta(G_{k,k})\leq\min_{i}\text{deg}(v_{i})
, the result follows.
∎

We proceed by using the typical Chernoff-type tail bound on the binomial distribution (see for example
[
14
, Appendix A]
).

Lemma 7.4

(
[
14
]
)
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
p\in[0,1]
, let
X\sim\text{Bin}(k,p)
and let
\mu=kp
. For every
t
with
0\leq t\leq\mu/2
,

\mathbb{P}\left[X<\mu-t\right]\leq\exp\left(-\frac{t^{2}}{2\mu}\right).

Lemma 7.5
.

With high probability, for every
S\subseteq V(G_{k,k})
with
k/2\leq|S|\leq k
,

\frac{|\partial S|}{|S|}\geq\frac{k}{2}-2\sqrt{k}.

Proof.

Fix a set
S
, with
S_{1}=S\cap V_{1}
,
S_{2}=S\cap V_{2}
,
\absolutevalue{S_1}=a,\absolutevalue{S_2}=b
. Define a random variable
X
for the number of non-deterministic edges in
\partial S
, that is,
X\sim\text{Bin}(m,\frac{1}{2})
, where
m:=a(k-b)+b(k-a)
. We have

\absolutevalue{\partial S}=a(k-a)+X.

If
E_{S}
denotes the event that
\absolutevalue{\partial S}<\absolutevalue{S}k/2-2|S|\sqrt{k}
, then

\mathbb{P}\left(E_{S}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(X<\frac{\absolutevalue{S}k}{2}-a(k-a)-2|S|\sqrt{k}\right).

Let
\mu:=\mathbb{E}\left[X\right]=m/2
. Since
a+b\leq k
,

\mu\geq\mu-a(k-a-b)=\frac{\absolutevalue{S}k}{2}-a(k-a).

Using this observation, we can apply Lemma
7.4
to bound
\mathbb{P}\left(E_{S}\right)
, which gives

\mathbb{P}\left(E_{S}\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(X<\mu-2|S|\sqrt{k}\right)\leq\exp\left(-\frac{4|S|^{2}k}{2\mu}\right)\leq\exp\left(-4|S|\right),

where the last inequality follows from
\mu\leq\absolutevalue{S}k/2
. The argument finishes by taking the union bound over all choices of
S
:

\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq V(G_{k,k}),\\
k/2\leq|S|\leq k\end{subarray}}E_{S}\right)\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}S\subseteq V(G_{k,k}),\\
k/2\leq|S|\leq k\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}\left(E_{S}\right)\leq\sum_{s=k/2}^{k}\binom{2k}{s}e^{-4s}\leq 2^{2k}e^{-2k}\to 0.\qed

We are now ready to show the main result of this section.

Proof of Theorem
7.1
.

Let
S\subseteq V(G_{k,k})
with
\absolutevalue{\partial S}/\absolutevalue{S}=i(G_{k,k})
. From Lemma
7.3
,
\delta(G_{k,k})\leq k/2
w.h.p., so if
|S|\leq k/2
, we can apply Lemma
7.2
and get that
\absolutevalue{\partial S}/\absolutevalue{S}=\delta(G_{k,k})
. Now, in the case
|S|\geq k/2
, we apply Lemma
7.5
and w.h.p., for any choice of
S
,

\frac{|\partial S|}{|S|}\geq\frac{k}{2}-2\sqrt{k}\geq\delta(G_{k,k}),

where the last inequality follows from Lemma
7.3
.
∎

In
[
6
]
, the authors provide an upper bound on
i(G)
based on the work of Grone and Merris on the majorization relation between the Laplacian eigenvalues and the degree sequence.

Proposition 7.6

(
[
6
, Proposition 4.6]
)
.

i(G)\leq\min_{\frac{n}{2}\leq m<n}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\mu_{n+1-i}-d_{i}).

(12)

The bound (
12
) beats the upper bound (
1
) for the complete split graph
S_{p,q}
obtained by adding all edges between
K_{p}
and
E_{q}

[
6
, Example 4.7]
. In this direction, Theorem
7.1
gives a family of split graphs for which equality occurs in (
12
).

Corollary 7.7
.

With high probability

i\left(G_{k,k}\right)=\min_{k\leq m<2k}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\mu_{n+1-i}-d_{i}).

Proof.

It follows from Theorem
7.1
that w.h.p. the minimum degree of
G_{k,k}
is equal to the isoperimetric number. Moreover, the following holds for every graph
G
on
n
vertices:

\displaystyle\min_{\frac{n}{2}\leq m<n}\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\mu_{n+1-i}-d_{i})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n-1}(\mu_{n+1-i}-d_{i})

\displaystyle=(2|E(G)|-\mu_{1})-(2|E(G)|-d_{n})

\displaystyle=\delta(G).\qed
