Introduction

The underlying mechanism of adenosine-sensitive atrial tachycardia (AT) originating from the atrioventricular annulus remains unclear. Adenosine-sensitive AT represents triggered activity or automaticity based on centrifugal patterns in activation maps and termination or suppression by adenosine triphosphate.1 Recently, some reports have suggested that the mechanism is reentry with a slow conduction zone (SCZ) composed of calcium channel–dependent tissues because adenosine-sensitive AT is sensitive to adenosine triphosphate and verapamil and can be terminated and initiated by atrial programmed pacing.2, 3, 4, 5 However, identifying the entire circuit of adenosine-sensitive AT remains challenging, although radiofrequency ablation targeting the entrance site has been reported to be safer and more effective than targeting the exit site, and entrainment pacing has been widely used as a strategy for determining the direction of and proximity to the circuit entrance.6 We present a case of adenosine-sensitive AT in which high-density mapping suggested the entire reentrant circuit, and an electrophysiological study was performed to evaluate the potential corresponding to the SCZ.

Case report

A 69-year-old man without overt structural heart disease developed palpitations and was referred to our institution for catheter ablation. Electrocardiography revealed a regular narrow QRS tachycardia with a long RP interval at 138 beats/min (Figure 1A).

A: 12-lead electrocardiogram during sinus rhythm (left) and tachycardia (right). B: Activation map during atrial tachycardia. EAS = earliest activation site; HBE = His bundle electrogram; TA = tricuspid annulus.

2 decapolar catheters were positioned in the coronary sinus and His bundle region, and a quadripolar catheter in the right atrium (RA) appendage and right ventricular apex. Bipolar electrograms recorded using a polygraph (CardioLab XT, GE HealthCare, Chicago, IL) filtered at 30–500 Hz with a sampling rate of 1000 Hz. A narrow QRS, long RP tachycardia with the earliest activation site (EAS) in the His bundle region was induced by an atrial extrastimulus without an atrio-His jump. Ventriculoatrial linking after differential atrial overdrive pacing was not observed,7 and after the cessation of ventricular entrainment pacing during tachycardia, the V-A-A-V sequence was observed. Although these findings were suggestive of AT, atypical atrioventricular nodal reentrant tachycardia using a slow pathway extending to the anterior tricuspid annulus (TA) could be considered.8,9 However, the AA interval was reproducibly prolonged compared with the HH interval after a 2-mg intravenous bolus of adenosine triphosphate (Supplemental Figure 1), leading us to diagnose this as adenosine-sensitive AT.

After diagnosis, high-density mapping of the AT was performed using the EnSite X EP System with Omnipolar Technology (Abbott, Abbott Park, IL) and Advisor HD Grid catheter (Abbott). The bipolar electrograms were filtered at 30–300 Hz and the unipolar electrograms at 0.5–50 Hz, with a sampling rate of 2000 Hz. The activation map exhibited a centrifugal pattern from the 12 o’clock position of the TA (Figure 1B), with activation propagating toward the lateral TA. Entrainment pacing was performed from the RA appendage, lateral TA, high posteroseptal RA, and coronary sinus ostium; however, only pacing from the lateral TA orthodromically captured the EAS (Supplemental Figure 2). Thus, the entrance of the SCZ was considered to be located between the lateral TA and the EAS. Radiofrequency ablation was performed 3 cm from the EAS in the direction of the SCZ entrance, following the strategy proposed by Yamabe et al.3 Using an irrigated-tip catheter (TactiFlex SE, Abbott) at 30 W, the AT terminated with cycle length prolongation (Figure 1).

After catheter ablation, during postprocedural review of the AT activation map, we observed dull components localized to the 9–12 o’clock region of the TA during the interval lacking atrial potentials in the tachycardia cycle (Figure 2A and 2B). These components could not be explained by atrial potentials, ventricular far-field potentials, His bundle potentials, or slow pathway potentials. The dull components were distributed continuously over a 4-cm segment extending from Site A to Site G and demonstrated a sequence opposite to that of the surrounding atrial activation.

Although ablation had already been completed, we performed additional mapping during RA lateral pacing to determine whether the dull components could also be observed during rhythms other than AT. During pacing, the atrial activation sequence proceeded from the lateral to anterior TA, with a conduction delay at the successful ablation site (Figure 2C). Notably, similar dull components were observed, localized only along the TA (Figure 2D and 2E). The amplitudes of the dull components during both AT and lateral TA pacing were markedly small, measuring 0.13 ± 0.07 and 0.06 ± 0.01 mV, respectively.

Figure 3A shows capture of the dull component during entrainment pacing from the lateral TA, and Figure 3B shows the corresponding catheter configuration. Entrainment pacing orthodromically captured the atrial potential; however, the dull component immediately after stimulation was captured by the last stimulus. At that time, the return cycle length of the dull component coincided with the tachycardia cycle length, and its morphology was identical to that during AT.

No recurrence was observed during a 20-month postoperative follow-up period.

Discussion

This case provides new insights into the delineation of the adenosine-sensitive AT reentrant circuit and electrophysiological evaluation of components presumed to represent the SCZ. The key observations are as follows: (1) AT mapping revealed dull components along the TA, which complemented the intervals of the AT cycle in which atrial excitation was absent. (2) During entrainment pacing from a direction that could entrain the EAS of the adenosine-sensitive AT, the dull components were also entrained.

Figure 3C shows a schematic representation of manifest entrainment. In the Advisor HD Grid, we simultaneously observed where the second-to-last stimuli captured the atrial potential orthodromically and where the last stimulus captured the dull component. These novel electrophysiological findings indicate that dull components are a part of the AT circuit. Figure 3D illustrates the conduction pattern observed during atrial pacing. An activation map during lateral TA pacing revealed that the conduction pattern of the dull components was reversed from Site G to Site D compared with that during AT, whereas it remained in the same sequence from Site A to Site C. These observations may suggest that the dull components are electrophysiologically isolated and that conduction propagates through both Site A and Site G.

These dull components resemble potentials of calcium channel–dependent tissues, suggesting the involvement of the atrioventricular ring.10, 11, 12 McGuire et al10 noted that the sleeve of atrioventricular nodal–type tissue, which is characterized by a low-frequency component and responds to adenosine triphosphate, is present around the TA. Recently, it has been indicated that atrioventricular ring–specialized tissues arise from the inferior extensions of the atrioventricular node, pass rightward and leftward to encircle the tricuspid and mitral valves, and reunite to form an extensive retroaortic node, which is separate from the compact node itself.11,12 Yamabe et al13 demonstrated a slow potential, characterized by a low amplitude with a slow rate of rise morphology, at the entrance of the SCZ in adenosine-sensitive AT arising from the atrioventricular annulus. This slow potential is observed during sinus rhythm at the entrance of the SCZ; however, the slow potential amplitude is attenuated and slow potential width is prolonged during AT. These histological and electrophysiological findings support our observations.

Whether these dull components represent actual electrophysiological phenomena remains unknown. However, their spatial and temporal continuity, together with their electrophysiological characteristics, suggests that they may reflect meaningful electrical phenomena (Figure 2). Although dull components were not clearly observed at Sites D and E during AT (Figure 2B), this likely reflects their coincidence with the ventricular electrogram, supported by similar components recorded at the same sites during lateral TA pacing (Figure 2D). The possibility of artifact was carefully evaluated. At Sites A and B, dull components were observed after ventricular electrograms during AT, and similar findings were noted during lateral TA pacing; thus, these components were considered more likely to represent artifacts. Similarly, the components observed before ventricular electrograms at Sites F and G were thought to represent artifacts (Figure 2B and 2D).

As this is a single case report, it is necessary to collect more cases of circuits located at other sites of the TA or mitral annulus to confirm the reproducibility of these findings. The limitations of this case also include the appearance of dull components during lateral TA pacing, which might have been changed by radiofrequency application and diagnostic uncertainty regarding the tachycardia.

Conclusion

This case provides novel insights into the mapping of the reentrant circuit of adenosine-sensitive AT and evaluation of the electrophysiological properties of potentials possibly related to the SCZ.
