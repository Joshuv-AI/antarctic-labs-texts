Microglial interferon signaling and Aβ plaque pathology are enhanced in female 5xFAD Alzheimer’s disease mice, independent of estrous cycle stage
Authors: L Calcines-Rodríguez, N Noyes-Martel, J L Becker, S K Nguyen, M A Osabutey, L A Trojanczyk, A K Majewska, M K O’Banion
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlzheimer’s disease (AD) presents with a sex bias in which women are at higher risk and exhibit more rapid cognitive decline and brain atrophy compared to men. Microglia play a significant role in the pathogenesis and progression of AD and have been shown to be sexually differentiated in health and disease. Whether and how microglia contribute to the sex differences in AD remains to be elucidated. Herein, we characterized the sex differences in amyloid-beta (Aβ) plaque pathology and microglia-plaque interaction using the 5xFAD mouse model and revealed microglial transcriptomic changes that occur in females and males. Despite women with symptomatic late-onset AD being in the post-menopausal stage, metabolic and pathological changes are seen prior to menopause. For this reason, and because Aβ pathology develops decades prior to clinical presentation, we focused on two hormonally distinct stages of the female rodent estrous cycle (proestrus and diestrus). Our results showed that Aβ plaque morphology is sexually distinct, with females having greater plaque volume and lower plaque compaction compared to males of the same age. Neuritic dystrophy was also increased in female 5xFAD mice, independent of estrous cycle stage. While microglia transcriptomes were not overtly different at the proestrus or diestrus stages, female 5xFAD microglia upregulated genes involved in glycolytic metabolism, antigen presentation, disease-associated microglia, and microglia neurodegenerative phenotype compared to males, some of which have been previously reported. In addition, we found a novel female-specific enhancement of IFN signaling in microglia, as evidenced by a striking proportion of differentially expressed type 1 interferon genes characteristic of interferon-responsive microglia (IRM). Finally, we validated our transcriptomic results at the protein level and observed that female 5xFAD mice had an enrichment in Aβ+ IRMs compared to males. Collectively, we show that there are sex-specific alterations in Aβ plaque morphology and that endogenous hormonal fluctuations across the estrous cycle do not overtly affect Aβ pathology or microglial transcriptomic profiles. Furthermore, our study identifies a novel sex-specific enhancement of interferon signaling in female microglia responding to Aβ, which may constitute a new therapeutic target for personalized medicine in AD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12974-025-03659-1.Keywords: Microglia, Sex, Estrous, 5xFAD, Amyloid, Alzheimer’s, Transcriptome, Plaques, Interferon, IRM

Introduction

Historically, researchers have neglected sex as a biological variable in study design, resulting in an incomplete understanding of disease pathogenesis and ultimately hampering clinical trials [1, 2]. Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a neurodegenerative disease that presents with a sex bias and is characterized by aberrant aggregation of the amyloid-β (Aβ) protein into senile plaques and the tau protein into neurofibrillary tangles [3–5]. This proteinopathy leads to synaptic and neuronal loss, microgliosis, astrogliosis, neuroinflammation, oxidative and metabolic stress, ultimately resulting in cognitive decline [6]. Women with AD exhibit greater rates of cognitive decline, brain atrophy, and increased hallmark pathology compared to age-matched men [3]; however, the intricate mechanisms behind these differences remain elusive, presenting challenges to the development of sex-specific therapeutic interventions.

Microglia, the resident immune cells of the central nervous system (CNS), play a variety of functions, including maintaining homeostasis through dynamic surveillance and phagocytosis of debris, with specific roles in neurogenesis, synaptic pruning, and plasticity [7–11]. In the context of AD, microglia are known to congregate around Aβ plaques [12, 13] and adopt unique transcriptional signatures, including disease-associated microglia (DAM) [14], activated response microglia (ARM) [15], and microglial neurodegenerative phenotype (MGnD) [16]. Moreover, microglia interact with plaques and are heavily involved in the phagocytosis, seeding, spreading, growth, and morphological remodeling of Aβ plaques [17–23]. Their critical roles in AD pathogenesis were confirmed when genome-wide association studies revealed that many genetic risk factors for late-onset AD (LOAD) are almost exclusively or highly expressed in microglia in the CNS [24]. Microglia can also modulate the course of disease pathology by releasing anti- and pro-inflammatory cytokines and other secreted molecules that may ameliorate or aggravate pathological conditions [25–27]. Interestingly, these highly plastic cells are not only transcriptionally diverse and regionally heterogeneous but also sexually differentiated in health and disease [28–36].

Sex influences numerous biological processes and leads to significant divergence as a function of age, particularly in the brain, where cells are sexually differentiated from developmental stages to adulthood and beyond [36–38]. Sex differences can arise from the sex chromosomes (XX in females and XY in males) and gonadal hormones, both of which establish sex-specific features early in development through epigenetic modifications and activate sex-specific phenotypes later in life [36, 37, 39]. It is postulated that microglia exhibit sex differences in their developmental trajectory and in adulthood, resulting in sex-specific variations in morphology, cell density, proliferation, migration, transcriptome, proteome, and overall function [31, 34–37]. In the context of injury and disease, microglia can also respond in a sexually distinct manner [31, 40–45]. For instance, in response to ischemic injury, female microglia minimize the damage more efficiently than males, and this ability seems to be sex-specific and cell-intrinsic [40]. Apart from a developmental role, changes in gonadal hormones during the menstrual cycle in adult women have been linked to changes in cortical thickness in imaging studies [46, 47]. In female rodents, fluctuations in spine density across the rodent estrous cycle are well documented [48], and emerging evidence reveals that ventral hippocampal neurons shift their epigenetic landscape and transcriptomic profile at two hormonally distinct stages of the estrous cycle [49]. Whether microglia may present with distinct transcriptional profiles at different stages of the estrous cycle in the context of disease is yet to be investigated. Notably, despite women with symptomatic LOAD being predominantly in the post-menopausal stage, Aβ pathology develops 2–3 decades before clinical presentation [50] and may overlap with regularly cycling pre-menopausal and irregularly cycling peri-menopausal stages, the latter showing metabolic alterations and increases in Aβ plaques in human imaging studies [51–53]. In addition, early-onset familial AD (EOAD) occurs in cycling women, yet little is known about the effects of hormonal fluctuations on AD pathology and neuroinflammation. Apart from the translational importance of studying cycling females in the context of AD, there is a limitation in the current published studies that use mouse models of AD and report sex differences between males and cycling females, but do not include the estrous cycle as a confounding variable. Currently, we do not fully appreciate the relative contributions of sex chromosomes and gonadal hormones to differences in microglia gene expression or function in health or disease. Understanding how biological sex influences microglia function may provide insights into the sex-specific prevalence of neurological and psychiatric diseases and allow for more personalized therapeutic interventions.

The present study seeks to investigate the sex and estrous cycle-dependent differences in Aβ-mediated pathology and microglial responses to Aβ. To this end, we characterized the differences in Aβ plaque morphology, neuritic dystrophy, and microglia-plaque interaction in 5.5-month-old 5xFAD mice. We then isolated cortical microglia from wild-type (WT) and 5xFAD mice of both sexes that develop extensive Aβ pathology and performed bulk mRNA sequencing (RNAseq). To further interrogate the effect of endogenous hormonal fluctuations on the microglial transcriptome, we selected females at proestrus and diestrus stages, characterized by high and low estrogen-to-progesterone ratios, respectively [54]. Our results indicate that plaque morphology, including volume and compaction, differs between female and male 5xFAD mice. Neuritic dystrophy was also different in female and male 5xFAD mice and was not influenced by estrous cycle stage. We found that despite similar microglia-plaque coverage, female microglia engulfed more Aβ in the entorhinal cortex compared to males. Interestingly, the level of Aβ engulfment was positively correlated with plaque volume in females but not in males. At the transcriptomic level, female and male 5xFAD microglia showed differences in pathways related to glycolytic metabolism, antigen presentation, as well as disease-associated phenotypes, with few significant differences noted in females at different stages of the estrous cycle. Strikingly, we found that a large number of genes upregulated in female 5xFAD microglia were type 1interferon (IFN-1) genes, characteristic of the interferon-responsive microglia (IRM) signature. Lastly, we observed an enrichment of Aβ+ IRMs in female 5xFAD mice compared to males.

Methods

Experimental animals

All animal procedures were reviewed and approved by the University Committee on Animal Resources of the University of Rochester Medical Center and performed according to the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee and guidelines from the National Institutes of Health (NIH). Animals were maintained on a 12:12 h light:dark cycle with access to food and water ad libitum. Male and female 5xFAD mice (B6.Cg-Tg(APPSwFlLon,PSEN1*M146L*L286V)6799Vas/Mmjax, MMRRC stock 34,840) were obtained from MMRC and maintained at the University of Rochester vivarium. These 5xFAD mice harbor Swedish, Florida, and London mutations in the amyloid precursor protein (APP) and the M146L and L286V mutations in presenilin 1 (PSEN1) [55]. They begin accumulating plaques starting at 2 months of age and have a rapid and progressive increase in Aβ plaque load [55]. Hemizygous 5xFAD female mice were bred with C57BL6/J male wild-type (WT) mice (JAX: 000664) to generate the experimental mice used in this study and to ensure that the parental origin of the transgene was similar across all progeny [56]. Non-transgenic WT female and male littermates were used as controls where applicable. Mice were sacrificed at 5.5–6 months of age, a time when they exhibit extensive Aβ plaque pathology, have a well-defined DAM/IRM microglial response, and females are of reproductive age, allowing us to assess sex-specific and estrous cycle-dependent differences. Two weeks prior to sacrifice, vaginal smears were assessed daily (10.00—11.00 a.m.) in female mice. This equates to approximately 3 estrous cycles as females cycle over a period of 4–5 days through proestrus (Pro), estrus (Est), metestrus (Met), and diestrus (Die) stages [54]. At the time of brain collection, a final vaginal cytology assessment segregated the females into proestrus or diestrus. These stages were chosen as they exhibit high and low estrogen-to-progesterone ratios and mimic the late follicular and luteal phases of the menstrual cycle in humans, respectively [54]. Dissection of the uterus and subsequent weighing were used to confirm correct staging, as proestrus females tend to have a bigger/heavier uterus compared to diestrus females. Only females whose cycle staging and uterus weight were congruent were used for further analyses, including the RNA sequencing experiment.

Vaginal cytology and uterus dissection

Estrous cycle staging was assessed based on vaginal smear and cytological examination (S.Fig. 1a). Starting two weeks prior to sacrifice, a pipette tip filled with 40 µL of 1X phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was used to lavage the area around the vaginal orifice, avoiding any penetration. Samples were smeared onto a glass slide and dried. Slides were submerged in methanol for 30 s for fixing, followed by a 1 min wash in eosin and 30 s in hematoxylin (Hema 3™ Manual Staining System and Stat Pack, Fisher Scientific, #23–123,869). Slides were then washed with water and dried before examination under the microscope. Daily staging was based on the preponderance of nucleated vs. cornified squamous epithelial cells and leukocyte presence (S.Fig. 1b). To meet the criteria for proestrus and diestrus, smears needed to show approximately 90% small nucleated epithelial cells or approximately 90% leukocytes, respectively. For estrus and metestrus stages, smears needed to show 95% cornified epithelial cells and 50/50% cornified epithelial cells/leukocytes, respectively. At the time of sacrifice, the uterus was dissected to exclude the ovaries and vaginal canal and subsequently weighed. Our selection of proestrus and diestrus is based on the well-established differences in the hormonal milieu at these stages, with the estrogen-to-progesterone ratios being high in proestrus and low in diestrus [54, 57]. We found that both WT and 5xFAD females cycled regularly and exhibited similar changes in uterine weight, with proestrus females having approximately a twofold increase compared to diestrus females (S.Fig. 1c, d), consistent with previous reports [58].

Brain collection

Mice were injected with a mixture of xylazine (10 mg/kg, i.p.) and ketamine (100 mg/kg, i.p.) and perfused intracardially with 0.15 M PBS containing 0.5% sodium nitrite and 2 IU heparin/mL. Following perfusion, the brain hemispheres were separated: one hemisphere was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA, pH 7.2) for immunohistochemistry (described below), and the other hemisphere was processed for flow cytometry (described below).

Immunofluorescence

Following perfusion, half-brains were fixed in 4% PFA overnight, then transferred to 30% sucrose for 24 h. Brains were then flash frozen in dry ice and cryosectioned into 30 µm sections on a freezing-stage sliding microtome. Free-floating sections were stored in a cryoprotectant at −20 °C until assayed. For fibrillar Aβ staining using AmyloGlo, the tissue was treated prior to antibody staining by washing with 1X PBS to remove cryoprotectant, incubating in 70% ethanol for 5 min, and rinsing in deionized water for 2 min at room temperature (RT). The tissue was then incubated in AmyloGlo (Biosensis, #TR-300-AG, 1:100 in 0.9% Saline) for 10 min at RT and rinsed with 0.9% saline for 5 min followed by a rinse in deionized water for 15 s at RT. Immunohistochemical assessment of proteins of interest involved the following steps: multiple washes with 1X PBS to remove cryoprotectant, incubation in 4% normal goat serum (NGS) in 0.4% PBST (Triton X-100 (Sigma-Aldrich, #X100) diluted in 1X PBS) for 1.5 h at RT to prevent non-specific binding and allow cell membrane permeabilization, respectively, followed by staining with primary antibodies with 4% NGS in 0.4% PBST for 72 h at 4 °C. Next, the tissue was washed multiple times with 1X PBS and incubated in secondary antibody in 4% NGS in 0.4% PBST for 1.5 h at 4 °C. For fibrillar Aβ staining using Thioflavin S (ThioS), the tissue was then washed with 1X PBS and incubated in 0.025% ThioS (Sigma-Aldrich, #T-1892) in water for 5 min at RT, followed by multiple washes in 50% ethanol at RT. The tissue was then mounted on 2 × subbed slides with Prolong Gold anti-fade mounting media (Invitrogen, #P36930) and cover slipped. Primary and secondary antibody dilutions included rabbit anti-Iba1 as a pan-microglial marker (Wako, #019–19741, 1:1000), biotinylated 6E10 (BioLengend, #803,008, 1:3000), rat anti-LAMP1 as a neuritic dystrophy marker (DSHB, Clone 1D4B, 1:2000), goat anti-rabbit Alexa 488 (Invitrogen, #A21245, 1:1500), Streptavidin Alexa 594 (Invitrogen, #S32356, 1:1500), and goat anti-rat Alexa 594 (Invitrogen, #A11007, 1:1000).

Image acquisition and analysis

For high-resolution imaging, individual cortical plaques from 3 coronal brain sections per brain (approximately 60–90 plaques/section) were imaged (n = 3 5xFAD brains per group), focusing on the entorhinal cortex. Imaging was performed with a Leica Stellaris 5 Laser Scanning Confocal Microscope using a 63× oil objective (N.A. 1.4, HC PL APO 63×/1.4 OIL CS2, Leica Microsystems) and a zoom factor of 3.1. Images of the entire plaque were taken with a Z-step size of 0.3 µm, a pixel resolution of 1024 × 1024, and an 8-bit color depth. All parameters (gain and intensity) stayed consistent across samples. Bitplane IMARIS v10.2.0 was used for image analysis. IMARIS “surface” feature was used to detect Iba1+ cells as well as ThioS+ and 6E10+ plaques with a surface grain size of 0.58 µm. Image pre-processing for ThioS included a background subtraction based on the relative intensities of the measured background (i.e., an area of the slide with no tissue) due to observable differences in intensity of the background, followed by a Gaussian filter. Surface filters of > 100 µm2 and > 130 µm2 area were applied to avoid rendering small plaque seeds within the field of view for ThioS and 6E10, respectively. Pre-processing for Iba1 included a background subtraction and a surface filter of > 800 voxels to avoid inclusion of small background speckles. The IMARIS batch processing feature was used across all samples, and images were visually inspected to confirm 3D rendition closely resembled the raw image. For Fig. 1c and S.Fig. 3f, sphericity was calculated by IMARIS using the following formula: ψ = (π1/3(6Vp)2/3)/Ap, where p is the particle (i.e., the plaque), Vp is the particle’s volume, and Ap is the particle’s area. For Fig. 2b, microglia plaque coverage was quantified by dilating the surface of the ThioS+ plaque border by 2 µm, excluding the main body of the ThioS+ plaque to ensure measurement of microglia coverage around the plaque border only. We then measured the overlapping voxels between the dilated ThioS border surface and the Iba1 surface. The resulting value was then normalized to the surface area of the ThioS+ plaque to assess the extent of microglia coverage 2 µm around the plaque border. For Fig. 2c and d, ThioS+ spots were quantified by excluding the main plaque core and including smaller ThioS+ spots with a volume filter of 0.5–50 µm3. Then, the overlap between these ThioS+ spots and Iba1+ cells was quantified and normalized to total Iba1 volume to obtain engulfed ThioS+ spots in microglia.

Methoxy-04 injection and flow cytometry/fluorescence- activated cell sorting (FACS)

Mice were intraperitoneally (i.p.) injected with 4 mg/kg of Methoxy-04 (Tocris Bioscience, #4920) 36–60 h prior to sacrifice and brain collection as previously described. The overlying cortex was microdissected from the half- brain, and the tissue was Dounce- homogenized in 3 mL of FACS buffer (0.5% BSA in 1X PBS). Brain homogenates were then filtered through a 70 µm MACS SmartStrainer (Miltenyi Biotec, #130–100–916) into a 15 mL conical tube containing 3 mL of FACS buffer, rinsed with 3 mL additional FACS buffer, and centrifuged at 600 × g for 6 min at 4 °C. The cell pellet was then resuspended in 9 mL of 40% Percoll (Cytiva, #17,089,101) diluted in 1X PBS and centrifuged at 2000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C with the brake off to avoid disrupting the gradient. Myelin and the supernatant were removed, and the cell pellet was resuspended and transferred to a new tube containing 8 mL of FACS buffer, followed by centrifugation at 600 × g for 6 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellet was transferred into a 1.5 mL Eppendorf tube, resuspended in 90 µL of FACS buffer containing Fc block (BD Pharmingen, #553,141, 1:90), and incubated for 15 min at 4 °C. Next, 10 µL of antibody master mix was added to the cells and incubated for 20 min shaking in the dark at 4 °C. Following incubation, cells were washed with FACS buffer and centrifuged at 400 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. Cells were then transferred into 5 mL Falcon tubes containing 7AAD Viability Staining Solution (eBioscience, #00–699-50, 1:80). In order to process the large number of animals required, this experiment extended over 2 days with careful consideration to antibody dilution and instrument calibration across days using the Rainbow Calibration Particles (Spherotech, Peak 6, #RCP-30-5A-6). Compensation (i.e., single-stain) and fluorescence-minus-one (FMO) controls were run in tandem with samples using compensation beads (Invitrogen, #01–2222-41) or cells, which informed the gating criteria. Samples were run on a FACSAria II (BD) cytometer. The following antibodies were used for Figs. 2f, g and 5d-f: CD11b-FITC (BioLegend, Clone M1/70, 1:400), CD45-APC/Cy7 (BioLegend, Clone 30-F11, 1:400), CD317-PE (Novus Biologicals, Clone 44E9R, 1:500). Gating strategy: Following debris, doublet, and dead cell (7AAD−) exclusion, microglia were identified as CD11b+/CD45low−intermediate (S.Fig. 2). Gating on the MeX04+ and MeX04− populations was based on the MeX04 FMO which included 99.41% of cells in the negative gate and 0.48% of cells in the positive gate (S.Fig. 2a, i). From the CD11b+CD45low−intermediate population, gating on the CD317+ and CD317− populations was based on the CD317-PE FMO, which included 0.08% of cells in the positive gate (S.Fig. 2b, i). This FMO was also confirmed with an intensity histogram showing lower intensity in the CD317-PE FMO compared to when CD317-PE was added (S.Fig. 2b, ii). From the CD317+ population, the CD317+MeX04+ gate was determined based on the MeX04 FMO, which included 0.43% of cells in the positive gate (S.Fig. 2a, ii). The following antibodies were used for S.Fig. 8b-d: CD11b-FITC (BioLegend, Clone M1/70, 1:400), CD45-APC/Cy7 (BioLegend, Clone 30-F11, 1:400), and CD317-PE (Novus Biologicals, Clone 44E9R, 1:100), AXL-APC (eBioscience, Clone MAXL8DS, 1:100). Stereotactic Injection

Mice (C57BL6/J) were anesthetized with isoflurane (4% induction and 2% maintenance) in a mixture of 30% oxygen and 70% nitrogen gas. The head was secured using ear bars and a head holder in a Kofp stereotactic apparatus. Mice received subcutaneous extended-release buprenorphine injection (0.07 mg/kg), and their eyes were treated with an ophthalmic ointment to prevent drying. Hair was removed, and the scalp was disinfected with 10% iodine and 70% ethanol. A midline incision was made and burr holes were drilled in each hemisphere to target the hippocampus (AP: 1.8 mm, ML: 1.8 mm, DV: 1.8 mm from bregma). A Hamilton syringe was mounted on a Micro-1 microsyringe pump (World Precision Instruments, Sarasota, FL) and the needle (33-gauge) was lowered over the course of 2 min to a depth of 1.9 mm for 1 min, after which it was lifted to the target position of 1.8 mm. A total of 1 µL of recombinant mouse IFN-β (8234-MB, R&D Systems) reconstituted in 0.1% w/v BSA in 1X PBS (Vehicle) was injected in one hemisphere at a concentration of 10 ng/µL and a rate of 200 nL/min. After 5 min of dwell time, the needle was lifted over the course of 2 min, and the injection was repeated, using a different needle, in the other hemisphere receiving vehicle. After injections, the burr holes were sealed with Ethicon bone wax and the incision was closed with tissue adhesive. Mice were placed on a heated area before being transferred to their home cage. The injections were performed over the course of 2 days, with both groups represented each day. Approximately 19–26 h post-injection, mice were sacrificed and their brains were collected for flow cytometry (as described above) (S.Fig. 8a).

Microglia isolation and bulk mRNA sequencing

Microglia were isolated from the microdissected cortex and processed for FACS as described above. The samples were incubated with 10 µL of antibody master mix containing CD11b-FITC (BioLegend, Clone M1/70, 1:400), CD45-APC/Cy7 (BioLegend, Clone 30-F11, 1:400), TMEM119-PE (Abcam, Clone 106–6, 1:500) and P2RY12-APC (BioLegend, Clone S16007D, 1:50) and subsequently stained with 7AAD Viability Staining Solution (eBioscience, #00–699-50, 1:80). Compensation and FMO controls were included, and samples were run on an Aurora CS (Cytek) spectral cytometer. Following debris, doublet, and dead cell (7AAD−) exclusion, microglia were identified as CD11b+CD45low−intermediate, and TMEM119 and P2RY12 were used to further confirm microglial identity. Cells were sorted into RLT Plus (Qiagen) + 10 µL/mL β-mercaptoethanol (BME) and stored at −80 °C until processing for bulk RNAseq.

Total RNA was isolated using the RNeasy Plus Micro Kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA). The concentration of RNA was determined using the NanoDrop One spectrophotometer (NanoDrop, Wilmington, DE), and RNA quality was assessed with the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA). Pre-amplification of 1 ng of total RNA was performed with the SMART-seq v4 Ultra-Low mRNA Input kit (Clontech, Mountain View, CA) per manufacturer’s instructions. The quantity and quality of the cDNA were determined using Qubit Fluorometer (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA) and the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA). A total of 150 pg of cDNA was used to generate Illumina-compatible sequencing libraries with the NexteraXT library preparation kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA) per manufacturer’s instructions. The libraries were then hybridized to the Illumina flow cell (10B-300) and sequenced using the NovaSeq X sequencer (Illumina, San Diego, CA) to obtain approximately 30 million paired-end reads of 150 nucleotides for each sample. Four biological replicates were sequenced for each group (sex, genotype, and estrous stage).

Bioinformatic analyses

Following sequencing, the resulting RNAseq raw reads were demultiplexed using bcl-convert v4.1.7, followed by quality filtering and adapter removal with FastP (v0.23.1). Cleaned reads (FASTQ files) were then mapped onto the GRCm39/gencode M31 reference using STAR (v2.7.9a), generating BAM output files. Gene-level read quantification was derived with subread (v2.0.1) package (featureCounts) with a GTF annotation file (GRCm39/gencode M31). Differential expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (v1.44.0) with a false discovery rate-adjusted p-value (FDR) threshold of 0.05 and LFC cutoff of ± 0.25 within R (v4.4.1, https://www.R-project.org/). A PCAplot was created within R using the ‘pcaExplorer’ (v2.30.0) to measure the variance of gene expression (top 500 genes) in each sample. Heatmaps were generated using the ‘pheatmap’ package (v1.0.12) given the Z-normalized rLog transformed expression values. Gene ontology analyses were performed with the EnrichR package (v3.2). Volcano plots and dot plots were created using ggplot2 (v3.5.1). Venn diagrams and the resulting list of overlapped or unique genes were derived from FunRich (v3.1.3) and plotted using BioRender. The following list of input genes were used in Venn diagrams: canonical glycolysis (Alliance Genome Consortium orthologs based on Hallmark Glycolysis, v2024.1.Mm), XY genes (GenBank, Mus musculus strain C57BL/6 J chromosome X and Y, GRCm39), antigen presentation (Antigen Processing and Presentation, GO:0019882, v2024.1.Mm), DAM genes [14], MGnD genes [16], IRM genes [59].

RT-qPCR

Flash-frozen cortices from 5.5-month-old 5xFAD mice were homogenized using a tissue homogenizer (Omni International) in RLT Plus Buffer with 10 μL/mL of BME. RNA was extracted using the RNeasy Plus Mini kit (Qiagen) as per the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA was acquired from 1 μg of RNA using TranscriptMe RNA kit (Blirt, Qiagen, RT31-020). Concentration of RNA and cDNA was measured using NanoDrop 2000 (ThermoFisher Scientific). Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) was run using a 10 µL reaction in a 96-well plate on QuantStudio Real Time PCR System (ThermoFisher Scientific). Two separate reactions were assembled using 100 ng of cDNA, TaqMan Fast Advanced Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, 2X), and the following TaqMan Gene Expression Assays: human APP (Hs00169098_m1, FAM-MGB, 20X), human PSEN1 (Hs00997789_m1, FAM-MGB, 20X), and murine GAPDH (Mm99999915_g1, VIC-MGB, 20X) as a reference control. A total of 6 biological replicates (cortices from individual 5xFAD mice) were used with 4 technical replicates per sample. Fold changes were quantified using the 2−ΔΔCt method, and the experimenter remained blind to the sex of the samples up to the moment of data analysis.

Results

Sex-specific alterations in plaque morphology and neuritic dystrophy in 5xFAD mice, independent of estrous cycle

Previous studies have reported sex differences in global Aβ plaque burden in the 5xFAD mouse model [60]. Here, we sought to confirm whether Aβ plaque morphology and neuritic dystrophy in the entorhinal cortex differed between the sexes, given that Aβ morphology, including Aβ plaque volume and compaction, is modulated by microglia and can impact neuronal health [12, 13, 18, 19]. To this end, we examined Aβ plaque morphology in 5xFAD female and male mice at 5.5 months of age, a time when the pathology is well manifested in this murine model (Fig. 1). We focused on the entorhinal cortex, given its functional role in memory and the dispersed distribution of Aβ plaque deposition, allowing high-resolution analysis of single plaques. We utilized immunohistochemistry combined with high-resolution imaging to generate 3D surface renderings of individual cortical plaques (Fig. 1a), using ThioS, which binds to fibrillar β-sheet conformations of Aβ and 6E10 (S. Fig. 3a), which recognizes all conformations. When assayed at high magnification (63×), we found that both ThioS+ (Fig. 1b) and 6E10+ (S.Fig. 3b) individual plaque volume was significantly greater in the entorhinal cortex of female compared to male 5xFAD mice. Females also had a greater 6E10+ plaque density compared to males (S.Fig. 3c). Importantly, when measuring the mRNA levels of the human APP (hAPP) and PSEN1 (hPSEN1) transgenes in the 5xFAD brain, we did not find any statistically significant sex differences (S.Fig. 3d, e), suggesting that the sex differences in Aβ load are not due to differences in transgene expression. Next, we sought to investigate plaque sphericity, which is often used as a proxy for plaque compaction [61, 62]. We observed that ThioS+ plaque sphericity was significantly lower in the entorhinal cortex of female 5xFAD mice compared to males (Fig. 1c), suggesting that female cortical fibrillar plaques are not only larger, but also less compact (Fig. 1b-d). Interestingly, this sex difference in plaque sphericity was not noted in 6E10+ plaques, suggesting that this morphological feature is specific to fibrillar forms of Aβ (i.e., ThioS+ plaques) (S.Fig. 3f). Given the reported association between lower plaque sphericity and increased neuronal damage [12, 63], we sought to assess whether neuritic dystrophy differed between female and male 5xFAD mice. To this end, we used LAMP1, a lysosomal marker of neuritic dystrophy, and AmyloGlo, another marker of fibrillar Aβ plaques (Fig. 1d). We quantified LAMP1+ neuritic dystrophy volumes normalized to the AmyloGlo+ plaque volumes to obtain LAMP1 burden per unit plaque (Fig. 1d, e). Indeed, female 5xFAD mice had increased LAMP1+ neuritic dystrophy compared to male 5xFAD mice in the entorhinal cortex (Fig. 1e), consistent with the findings that females have less spherical plaques (Fig. 1c). Notably, we did not find differences in LAMP1+ neuritic dystrophy or AmyloGlo+ plaque burden between females at two hormonally distinct stages (i.e., proestrus vs. diestrus) of the estrous cycle (Fig. 1e, S.Fig. 3g). Taken together, these results reveal sex-specific differences in plaque morphology and neuritic dystrophy in the 5xFAD model.

Microglia exhibit sex-specific plaque interaction in 5xFAD mice

Given the observed sex differences in plaque morphology (Fig. 1, S.Fig. 3) and prior work implicating microglia in plaque growth and compaction [12, 13], we sought to understand whether microglia remodel plaque morphology in a sex-specific manner in the 5xFAD mouse model. Owing to the close proximity of microglia to plaques, we first assessed the extent of microglial plaque coverage using Iba1, a pan-microglial marker, around the ThioS+ plaque border in 5.5-month-old female and male 5xFAD entorhinal cortex (Fig. 2a). To do this, we computed the voxel overlap between Iba1 and a 2 µm-dilated ThioS border that excluded the main ThioS+ plaque (Fig. 2a). We normalized the resulting value to the surface area of the ThioS+ plaque to obtain the extent of microglia-plaque coverage around the plaque border (Fig. 2a, b). Surprisingly, we did not find a significant sex difference in microglia coverage of ThioS+ plaques between female and male 5xFAD mice (Fig. 2b).

As brain-resident immune cells, microglia not only closely interact with plaques but also phagocytose Aβ species [64], a process that has been shown to be involved in the degradation as well as the extracellular deposition and growth of Aβ plaques [19, 20, 65]. To assess whether there was a sex difference in microglial phagocytosis of Aβ, we examined the presence of smaller ThioS+ spots (0.5–50 µm3 in size) not connected to the main plaque core that were inside Iba1+ cells, normalized to total Iba1 volume. We found that female microglia exhibited a significantly greater extent of ThioS+ Aβ engulfment in the entorhinal cortex compared to male counterparts (Fig. 2c). Interestingly, there was a positive correlation between ThioS+ Aβ engulfment in microglia and the main ThioS+ plaque volume in females but not in males (Fig. 2d).

To validate whether microglia phagocytose Aβ in a sex-specific manner, 6-month-old female and male 5xFAD mice were injected with Methoxy-X04 (MeX04, i.p.), a blood–brain barrier penetrant dye that labels Aβ fibrils [66, 67] (Fig. 2e) prior to sacrifice. We subsequently microdissected the entire overlying cortex and used flow cytometry to identify live microglia (7AAD−CD11b+CD45low−intermediate cells; S.Fig. 2a) that were positive or negative for MeX04 in WT and 5xFAD mice (Fig. 2f). The positive and negative gates were determined based on the FMO controls for MeX04 (S.Fig. 2a, i). When assessing the proportion of MeX04 + microglia, we found a significant difference between 5xFAD and WT microglia, as expected, but not between female and male 5xFAD microglia (Fig. 2g), suggesting no sex-specific differences in internalization of Aβ by microglia in the overlying cortex.

Estrous cycle stage does not affect the female microglial transcriptome in 5xFAD mice

Having found that plaque morphology, neuritic dystrophy, and microglia-plaque interaction was sex-specific, we wondered whether sex affected microglial molecular phenotypes in response to Aβ pathology (Fig. 3). Despite having found no differences in Aβ pathology in females at different stages of the estrous cycle, we also wanted to ensure that sex-specific microglial phenotypes were not masked by heterogeneity in female microglia caused by the hormonal fluctuations during the estrous cycle, given that microglia possess hormone receptors [68]. Following estrous cycle staging, bulk mRNA sequencing (RNAseq) was performed on isolated cortical microglia from 5.5-month-old WT and 5xFAD male, proestrus, and diestrus females (Fig. 3a). Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed a clear separation between groups with the variance explained primarily by genotype (5xFAD vs. WT; PC1: 68.92%) followed by sex (Female vs. Male; PC2: 12.39%) (Fig. 3b). Both the PCA (Fig. 3b) and the Euclidean sample-to-sample distance heatmap (S.Fig. 4a) revealed that all groups clustered within their respective genotype and sex, confirming there was little within-group but large between-group variability. Notably, proestrus and diestrus females clustered together within their genotype in the PCA space (Fig. 3b) and indiscriminately in the Euclidean heatmap within their genotype (S.Fig. 4a). Both results indicate that the transcriptomic profiles of proestrus and diestrus female microglia do not show a strong divergence in either genotype. Indeed, heatmaps of the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in a pairwise comparison revealed that proestrus and diestrus female microglia exhibited modest changes in gene expression in both the WT (S.Fig. 4b) and the 5xFAD (S.Fig. 4c) brain. In WT mice, we observed 7 upregulated and 7 downregulated genes in diestrus microglia compared to proestrus microglia (S.Fig. 4b). In 5xFAD mice, there were 7 upregulated and 5 downregulated genes in diestrus microglia compared to proestrus microglia (S.Fig. 4c). Interestingly, the expression of N4bp3, a gene involved in antiviral responses through the RIG-I-Like Receptor and Mitochondrial Antiviral Signaling protein (MAVS) known to induce type I interferon (IFN) signaling [69], was upregulated in proestrus female microglia regardless of genotype (S.Fig. 4d). Similarly, Nlrp3, a gene involved in the formation of the inflammasome and the production of cytokines such as interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and IL-18 [70], was increased in proestrus female microglia in both WT and 5xFAD mice (S.Fig. 4d, e). This suggests that proestrus female microglia may be more prone to inflammatory signaling compared to their diestrus female counterparts. However, due to the few DEGs identified, we conclude that the estrous cycle stage does not appear to overtly affect microglial gene expression in the healthy or Aβ-diseased brain. Intriguingly, when compared to male 5xFAD microglia, there are distinct gene sets and functional pathways that are more pronounced in either proestrus or in diestrus 5xFAD female microglia (S.Fig. 5), although these genes are not statistically significant in the direct pairwise comparison between proestrus and diestrus female 5xFAD microglia. However, given the relatively few differences between microglia in proestrus and diestrus females in WT or 5xFAD mice, we decided to group them into a single female group within their respective genotypes for the remaining analyses.

Female microglia show a more robust response to Aβ pathology, while male microglia retain a more homeostatic phenotype

Microglia are known to adopt distinct transcriptomic signatures in response to Aβ, namely DAM and MGnD, that are characterized by the downregulation of homeostatic microglia markers and upregulation of genes involved in lipid metabolism and phagocytosis [14, 16]. These signatures are especially prominent in plaque-associated microglia and phagocytic microglia observed in the context of neurodegenerative diseases, including AD [14, 16, 71]. To confirm that microglia were responding to Aβ in both sexes, we performed pairwise comparisons between 5xFAD and WT female (Fig. 3c) and male (Fig. 3d) microglia. As expected, volcano plots revealed that both female and male 5xFAD microglia responded to Aβ pathology and upregulated DAM genes such as Cst7, Lpl, Trem2, Tyrobp, Axl, Clec7a, Apoe, and Cstd, amongst others (Fig. 3c, d; Additional File 1). However, female 5xFAD microglia had nearly 4 times more total DEGs (2,944 DEGs in females: 5xFAD vs. WT; Fig. 3e) than male 5xFAD microglia (771 DEGs in males: 5xFAD vs. WT; Fig. 3f) when compared to their WT counterparts. When we compared genotype effects in females separately (S.Fig. 6) based on the estrous cycle stage (i.e., female proestrus 5xFAD microglia vs. female proestrus WT microglia (S.Fig. 6a, b) and female diestrus 5xFAD microglia vs. female diestrus WT microglia (S.Fig. 6c, d)), we still saw 2–3 times more total DEGs compared to males, suggesting that these results were not exclusively driven by the unequal sample size when females were pooled. Using Venn diagrams to examine the extent of overlap between the upregulated and downregulated DEGs and previously reported DAM genes [14], we found that when compared to their WT counterparts, female 5xFAD microglia had 65.2% differentially expressed DAM genes (326 out of 500 DAM genes; Fig. 3e), whereas males had 43% (215 out of 500 DAM genes; Fig. 3f). More specifically, female 5xFAD microglia upregulated 311 genes from the DAM phenotype, including Cst7, Lpl, Clec7a, Itgax, Spp1, Apoe, Axl, and Tyrobp, and downregulated 15 DAM genes, including canonical homeostatic makers such as P2ry12, Tmem119, and Cx3cr1, compared to female WT microglia (Fig. 3e; Additional File 1). On the other hand, male 5xFAD microglia upregulated 215 DAM genes, including those mentioned for females, and did not downregulate any DAM genes compared to male WT microglia (Fig. 3f; Additional File 1). This suggests that microglia from both sexes are responding to Aβ pathology; however, females exhibit a more robust response, particularly enriched in the DAM phenotype, whereas males retain a relatively more homeostatic signature.

Female and male 5xFAD microglia exhibit sex-specific gene expression profiles

Microglia are known to show sex-specific functional responses to insults and in disease contexts [34, 35, 38, 40]. To investigate sex differences in cortical microglia in the healthy and Aβ-diseased brain, we performed pairwise comparisons between female and male WT (Fig. 4a) and 5xFAD (Fig. 4b) microglia. Surprisingly, sex-specific gene regulation was much more apparent in 5xFAD than in WT cortical microglia. Well-known sex chromosome-linked genes [72, 73] were observed in both genotypes (Fig. 4c,d; X-linked: Xist, Tsix, Kdm6a, Eif2s3x, Kdm5c; Y-linked: Uty, Kdm5d, Eif2s3y, Ddx3y, and Gm4017), with a few additional X-linked genes observed only in 5xFAD microglia, including Cybb and Pgk1 (Fig. 4d), which are involved in oxidative metabolism and glycolysis, respectively. The remaining, and large majority, of DEGs between female and male WT and 5xFAD microglia were autosomal genes. For instance, in the pairwise comparison between female and male WT microglia, 69.7% of DEGs were autosomal genes (23 autosomal genes out of 33 total DEGs; Fig. 4c). In the pairwise comparison between female and male 5xFAD microglia, 93.2% of DEGs were autosomal genes (409 autosomal genes out of 439 total DEGs; Fig. 4d). This suggests that a chronic stimulus such as Aβ pathology induces a context-specific sex divergence that is not observed at baseline (i.e., in the non-diseased brain) and extends beyond sex chromosome-linked genes. Interestingly, a hierarchically-clustered heatmap of all the DEGs resulting from the female and male 5xFAD microglia comparison shows that female 5xFAD microglia are distinct from all other groups (Fig. 4e). For instance, it shows that male 5xFAD and WT microglia cluster indiscriminately, whereas female 5xFAD microglia cluster separately from female WT microglia and all other groups (Fig. 4e). This further supports the notion that male 5xFAD microglia retain a more homeostatic phenotype, as they cluster with WT male microglia, whereas female 5xFAD microglia respond in a discrete manner.

To query the functional roles of these DEGs, we performed Gene Ontology (GO) analysis with particular emphasis on the biological process. The top upregulated pathways in female 5xFAD microglia compared to their male counterparts included pathways involved in glycolytic metabolism (e.g., “canonical glycolysis” and “glucose catabolic process”) (Fig. 4f; Additional File 3). Interestingly, the genes that were upregulated in female 5xFAD microglia compared to male counterparts included Gapdh, Tpi, Pkm, Pgk1, Eno1, Aldoa, and Ldha, all of which are glycolytic enzymes (S.Fig. 7a, b). With the exception of Mpi, which was downregulated, other upregulated genes in female 5xFAD microglia linked to canonical glycolysis included anabolic/catabolic enzymes such as Got1, Pgam1, as well as Sdc3, Cdk1, Mif, Isg20, and Stmn1 (S. Figure 7a, b), the latter being involved in microtubule dynamics [74].

Another set of pathways enriched in female compared to male 5xFAD microglia was related to ribosomal biogenesis (“ribosome biogenesis” and “cytoplasmic translation”) (Fig. 4f, Additional File 3). Surprisingly, we did not find canonical DAM genes (Trem2, Ctsd; S.Fig. 7c) to be differentially expressed in female and male 5xFAD microglia, with the exception of Apoe, the gene that confers the greatest genetic risk for LOAD, which was significantly increased in female compared to male 5xFAD microglia (S.Fig. 7c). Instead, the majority of DAM genes found in this pairwise comparison were genes involved in ribosome biogenesis (S.Fig. 7 d, e). These included genes that encode for the ribosomal protein small subunit (Rpsa, Rps4x, Rps8, Rps11, Rps18, Rps20, Rps27a) and the ribosomal protein large subunit (Rplp0, Rpl4, Rpl5, Rpl6, Rpl7, Rpl10a, Rpl11, Rpl12, Rpl14, Rpl18a, Rpl22, Rpl23, Rpl27a) (S. Figure 7e; Additional File 2). Other genes within the DAM phenotype included glycolytic genes (Pgk1, Ldha, Tpi1, Mif, Aldoa, Got1, Pgam1) as well as antigen presentation genes (H2-D1, H2-K1, Fgl2) (S.Fig. 7 d, e; Additional File 2). MGnD genes, some of which overlap with DAM, were upregulated (Cxcl16, Cybb, and Csf1) and downregulated (P2ry12, Rhob, Nrip1, Cxxc5, Il21r, and Tgfbrap1) in female 5xFAD microglia compared to their male counterparts (S.Fig. 7f, g). Taken together, these data show that microglia exhibit sexually distinct transcriptomic profiles in the 5xFAD brain, with female microglia engaging ribosome biogenesis and glycolytic metabolism to a greater extent than male microglia.

Interferon-responsive microglia are enriched in female 5xFAD mice compared to males

Interferon-responsive microglia (IRM) have been identified near plaques in AD human and mouse model brains [15, 75–79]. We utilized a previously reported list of IRM genes [59] to investigate how many IRM genes were present in female 5xFAD microglia compared to male counterparts, which accounted for more than half of the IRM signature. We found that 11.6% of all DEGs between female and male 5xFAD microglia were IRM genes (51 IRM genes out of 439 DEGs; Fig. 5a). A heatmap of the expression levels of these IRM genes revealed stark sex differences with all 51 IRM genes being upregulated in female 5xFAD microglia compared to male counterparts (Fig. 5b). These genes are mainly involved in the type I IFN response and include IFN pathway initiation genes such as Irf7, Stat1, and Stat2, as well as canonical interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) such as Mx1, Ifitm3, Ifit3b, Ifit3, Ifi204, Ifi209, Isg15, Oas1g, Oasl2, Bst2, and Usp18, the latter being a negative regulator of IFN signaling [80] (Fig. 5b, Additional File 2). Apart from the canonical IFN-related genes, a few antigen presentation genes (H2-D1, H2-K1, H2-T23), which have also been identified to be ISGs [75, 81], were upregulated in female 5xFAD microglia. GO analysis revealed that female 5xFAD microglia were enriched in pathways such as “response to type I interferon”, “response to interferon-beta”, “response to interferon-alpha”, as well as “response to virus” (Fig. 4f; Additional File 3), a cellular process which involves IFN signaling and ISG expression [82]. Some of these IFN genes were upregulated in female but not male 5xFAD microglia compared to their WT counterparts (Isg15, Irf7, Oas1g; Fig. 5c), suggesting that male microglia may exhibit a blunted IFN response to Aβ plaques at 5.5 months of age. Taken together, these results suggest that IFN signaling is sexually distinct, with female 5xFAD microglia having greater IFN signaling compared to their male counterparts.

To validate our transcriptomic results at the protein level and determine the relationship between IRMs and Aβ phagocytosis, we examined the proportion of CD317+ microglia and CD317+MeX04+ microglia using flow cytometry (Fig. 5d-f). CD317 has been previously used as a marker of IRMs [72] and is encoded by the Bst2 gene, which we found to be significantly upregulated in female 5xFAD microglia compared to their male counterparts in the bulk RNAseq dataset (Fig. 5b). First, to confirm that both female and male microglia respond to IFN-I by increasing CD317 expression, we intracranially injected 6-month-old WT mice with recombinant IFN-β on one hemisphere and vehicle on the contralateral hemisphere (S.Fig. 8a). We then isolated microglia 19–26 h post-injection and examined the number of CD317+ microglia (IRMs) and AXL+ microglia (DAMs) as a non-IFN-inducible control (S.Fig. 8b-d). Indeed, we saw that IFN-β induced a significant increase in CD317+ microglia in both sexes (S.Fig. 8c), but had no effect on AXL+ microglia (S.Fig. 8 d), suggesting that CD317 is a suitable marker of IFN-I- responsive microglia. Moreover, the lack of a sex difference in CD317 induction suggests that female and male microglia do not differ in their baseline responsiveness to IFN-β; instead, this response might be context-specific. To address this question and assess the proportion of CD317 + microglia in the context of Aβ pathology, we isolated cortical microglia from female and male 5.5-month-old 5xFAD mice that received MeX04 injection prior to sacrifice. After gating debris-, singlet, live, and CD11b+CD45low−intermediate microglia, we gated on CD11b+CD317+ microglia (Fig. 5d) using the appropriate FMO (S.Fig. 2b, i). We found a significant increase in the proportion of total CD317+ microglia in 5xFAD compared to WT female mice with a similar trend in males, while no statistically significant difference was observed between female and male 5xFAD mice (Fig. 5e). However, when assessing the proportion of CD317+ microglia that were also MeX04+ (CD317+MeX04+; Fig. 5d; gating based on MeX04 + FMO; S.Fig. 2a, ii), we found that female 5xFAD mice had greater proportions of CD317+MeX04+ microglia compared to their male counterparts (Fig. 5f). Notably, in these measures, we used females at different stages of the estrous cycle (e.g., proestrus, estrus, metestrus, and diestrus) and saw no clear pattern of expression (Fig. 5e, f), although no formal statistical analyses were performed due to lack of power. Collectively, these results reveal an enrichment in Aβ-phagocytic IRMs in female 5xFAD mice compared to males.

Discussion

Understanding how sex contributes to disease pathogenesis, progression, and prognosis is pivotal to developing personalized disease-modifying therapies. In this study, we assessed sex differences in Aβ plaque morphology, neuritic dystrophy, and microglia-plaque interaction, as well as sex- and estrous cycle-dependent differences in microglial transcriptomic profiles in the 5xFAD model. We found that female 5xFAD brains had increased fibrillar Aβ plaque volume and decreased plaque sphericity, a measure of plaque compaction. This was accompanied by increased neuritic dystrophy in females which was independent of estrous cycle stage. We also found that, although there was no difference in microglia-plaque coverage, female microglia engulfed more Aβ in the entorhinal cortex. When assessing the microglial transcriptome in WT or 5xFAD mice, we found few differences between microglia at two stages of the estrous cycle. Likewise, female and male WT microglia did not substantially differ, except for sex chromosome-linked genes and a few autosomal genes. However, female and male 5xFAD microglia largely differed in autosomal genes involved in glycolysis, antigen presentation, DAM/MGnD, and IFN signaling. Finally, we found that female 5xFAD mice exhibited an enrichment in Aβ+ IRMs compared to their male counterparts. Taken together, this study reveals that sex affects plaque morphology, neuritic dystrophy, and microglial functionality in the context of Aβ pathology, independent of estrous cycle stage, and that interferon signaling is enhanced in female 5xFAD microglia. Our study extends previous work by assessing the effects of the estrous cycle on both Aβ plaque pathology and the microglial transcriptional landscape and by identifying a novel sex-specific enhancement of interferon signaling that may serve as a new therapeutic target.

Plaque morphology, neuritic dystrophy, and microglia-plaque interaction are sex-specific

Various studies have reported that microglia play a critical role in the initial seeding of Aβ plaques, contributing to their continuous growth [19, 23, 83] as evidenced by the lack of plaque deposition in the absence of microglia [23, 61, 84–87] or microglial receptors [19]. It is postulated that microglia phagocytose Aβ species, which subsequently aggregate in the acidic lysosome [17, 20, 83] and then seed these plaques via exocytosis or through apoptosis [83]. Our findings indicate that female microglia have enhanced Aβ engulfment, which could explain the larger plaques we observed in the female entorhinal cortex. Interestingly, we noted a positive correlation between Aβ engulfment and plaque size in female 5xFAD brains but not in males, suggesting a sex-specific relationship that warrants further investigation. Notably, we did not detect sex differences in Aβ phagocytosis (MeX04+ microglia) by flow cytometry. This discrepancy may reflect the broader cortical region sampled by flow cytometry compared with the entorhinal cortex examined by imaging, or methodological differences between the two techniques.

Microglia also remodel plaque ultrastructure over time [12, 13, 61]. Herein, we found a difference in fibrillar plaque volume and sphericity (3D analyses) between female and male 5xFAD mice. This is consistent with a previous report that noted a sex difference in plaque area and circularity (2D analyses) in EFAD (5xFAD-ApoE3) mice [62]. In line with our results, this prior study reported greater plaque area and lower plaque circularity in female compared to male brains [62]. Interestingly, we found that microglia coverage of the plaques was not different based on sex, in contrast to Stephen et al., 2019, who reported that males had greater microglia-plaque coverage compared to females [62]. These observations may have been influenced by the presence of the human ApoE3 variant in the EFAD model, which is not present in our study. Notably, we observed significantly higher Apoe expression in female 5xFAD microglia, which may have implications for the greater vulnerability of women with APOε4/ε4 [88, 89].

Female and male 5xFAD microglia are sexually distinct, independent of estrous cycle stage

The relative contributions of sex chromosomes and gonadal hormones to disease pathogenesis have yet to be disentangled, particularly regarding CNS immune responses. Microglia express hormone receptors such as estrogen receptor α and β, as well as G-coupled receptors (GPR30) [68], making them capable of responding to hormones [90–92]. However, to our knowledge, no other study has investigated whether microglia in gonadally intact mice may differ across the estrous cycle in the context of AD. Notably, women with symptomatic LOAD are typically in the post-menopausal stage, and sex differences in TSPO-PET signal, a marker of neuroinflammation, have been reported at this stage of the disease, with women exhibiting greater levels [93]. However, the timing of the emergence of these sex differences and the molecular mechanisms underlying increased neuroinflammation in women remain to be explored. Evidence points to the transition from pre-menopausal to peri-menopausal stages as a potential catalyst of sex divergence as seen by changes in glucose metabolism and Aβ plaque deposition in transitioning women [51–53]. Investigating how hormonal fluctuations affect plaque pathology and microglia phenotypes is also critical, given that 5% of all AD cases are EOAD, which presents as early as 25–40 years of age [94], with Aβ pathology beginning decades prior. Recently, attention has shifted to disease prevention, which will likely occur during the reproductive years (i.e., pre-menopausal stage) for women at high risk of AD [95]. Beyond its translational relevance, studying cycling females in AD mouse models is essential, given that prior reports of sex differences in these models do not account for the estrous cycle as a confounding factor.

To address this gap, we evaluated two hormonally distinct stages of the rodent estrous cycle, proestrus and diestrus, that mimic the late follicular and luteal phase of the menstrual cycle in humans and are characterized by high and low estrogen-to-progesterone ratios, respectively [54]. Except for a few genes, we did not find extensive differences in the microglial transcriptome in WT or 5xFAD mice, indicating that endogenous fluctuations in gonadal hormones do not overtly influence the female microglial transcriptome. This is consistent with a recent study that collected peripheral monocytes from women at different stages of their menstrual cycle and differentiated them into macrophages in vitro [57]. The investigators did not observe any differences in the enrichment of inflammatory-response genes linked to AD at these different stages, although they noted that the time spent in vitro could have impacted these results [57].

Previous studies investigating sex differences in microglia have mainly focused on the hippocampus; however, microglia are known to be regionally heterogeneous [28, 96]. Here, we examined transcriptional sex differences in the cortex, another disease-relevant brain region. We found that, similar to the hippocampus, female cortical microglia tended to shift their metabolism toward glycolysis in response to Aβ pathology [97, 98]. This metabolic reprogramming may enable microglia to perform high-energy-demanding functions such as phagocytosis [99]. Recent studies have found that this enhanced glycolytic metabolism seems to be more pronounced in aging WT microglia of both sexes, but particularly in older female microglia compared to older male microglia [72, 98], suggesting that sex divergence occurs as part of healthy aging and might contribute to sex differences in AD. Apart from metabolic reprogramming, microglia adopt specific transcriptional programs, namely DAM [14] and MGnD [16] which largely overlap. Our findings reveal that both DAM/MGnD genes are enriched in female compared to male 5xFAD microglia. Interestingly, however, we did not find canonical markers of these DAM/MGnD phenotypes, such as Trem2, Clec7a, Cst7, Lpl, Cd9, Spp1, and Axl, amongst others, to be sex-specific [14]. Notably, this set of genes belongs to stage 2 DAM (Trem2-dependent stage), whereas ApoE, Fth1, H2-D1, and Lyz2, which did show sex-specific expression, belong to stage 1 DAM (Trem2-independent stage) [14], an observation that could be due to the age (i.e., 5.5 months of age) assessed in this study. Overall, our results suggest that female microglia have greater levels of glycolytic and DAM genes in response to Aβ, consistent with previous reports.

Beyond these previously described metabolic and DAM/MGnD phenotypes, we identify a novel sex-specific enhancement of IFN-I signaling in female microglia in response to Aβ pathology. IFNs are cytokines commonly secreted by immune cells, including microglia, in response to viral infections [82]. Interestingly, IFNs seem to play a role in AD pathogenesis, but their exact function is currently understudied. Some reports have suggested that IFITM3, a prominent ISG, enhances γ-secretase activity, leading to more Aβ production [100]. Furthermore, a specific microglial transcriptomic signature characterized by IFN-I response genes was identified in AD post-mortem brains and AD mouse models [57, 75]. This IRM signature includes type-I ISGs (Ifitm3, Isg15, Mx1, Oasl2, Bst2) as well as IFN-related genes (Stat1, Stat2, Irf7), and microglia adopt this state when proximal to Aβ plaques [75–77]. Our results show that female 5xFAD microglia have increased levels of IFN-I genes compared to their male counterparts. Whether this increase in female brains is due to greater Aβ burden or a sex-specific microglial response remains to be dissected. Our data support the latter, as we observed no sex difference in Aβ phagocytosis (MeX04+ microglia) nor total IRMs (CD317+ microglia), but a clear sex difference in Aβ+ IRMs (CD317+MeX04+ microglia), suggesting a sex-specific IFN response to phagocytosed Aβ. Prior studies show that increased IFN expression may be associated with the internalization of Aβ plaques that contain potent stimulators of the IFN pathway (e.g., nucleic acids such as DNA and RNA) [75]. Alternatively, endogenous murine App (mApp) is expressed in microglia and has been shown to modulate microglial activation and secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines in an AD model [101]. We only observed subtle effects in mApp and murine Psen1 (mPsen1) expression, with increases in mApp (S.Fig. 9a) but not mPsen1 (S.Fig. 9b) in female 5xFAD microglia compared to WT microglia. This modest increase is unlikely to drive the observed IFN response. Collectively, our results reveal a sex-specific IFN response to Aβ that is greater in female brains.

Notwithstanding, this study has several limitations. For instance, we use the 5xFAD mouse model of amyloidosis, which has been reported to contain a putative estrogen receptor element (ERE) upstream of the transgene promoter, Thy1 [102]. However, the ERE sequence contains a base pair mutation that deviates from the consensus sequence, and thus it is unclear whether it is active or even functional [102]. Furthermore, the presence of the ERE sequence does not dictate whether it has a positive or negative impact on transcription, and oftentimes multiple EREs are required [103]. Studies that report a sex bias in transgene expression only observe a modest sex difference in the hAPP, but not hPSEN1 transgenes, both of which are under the same promoter, with some later studies not replicating the sex differences [102, 104, 105]. Our data indicate that there is no statistical difference in hAPP nor hPSEN1 transgene expression in the 5xFAD mouse model, suggesting that the differences described here are not driven by sex-dependent transgene expression in this model. Furthermore, our study is restricted to 5.5–6 months of age, a timepoint where Aβ pathology is widespread, microglia adopt DAM/IRM signatures, and females are of reproductive age. However, earlier or later timepoints may reveal divergent or convergent microglial responses, and further studies will be needed to assess when these sex differences emerge. Finally, we only perform statistical analyses on proestrus and diestrus microglia and not on estrus or metestrus stages, and therefore cannot exclude the possibility that the microglial transcriptome may differ at these other stages of the estrous cycle.

Overall, our study highlights the importance of including sex as a biological variable, especially in the context of disease. We present evidence of a novel female-specific enhancement of microglial IFN-I signaling, making microglia potential targets for precision-based therapeutic interventions. We also show that the estrous cycle does not overtly affect Aβ pathology or the microglial transcriptome in the healthy or Aβ-diseased brain. In addition to this newly described IFN signature, we also observe established sex-dependent differences in the microglial transcriptome, with female microglia displaying greater glycolytic metabolism and DAM/MGnD phenotypes, and male microglia retaining a more homeostatic signature. Overall, whether these microglial sex differences are cell-intrinsic or whether microglial phenotypes are driven by sexually distinct pathology remains to be unraveled.

Data Availability Statement

The RNAseq data generated and analyzed in this study has been deposited in the NCBI Gene Expression omnibus (GEO) under the accession number GSE306854. No original (non-standard) code was generated in this study. The remaining data from this study are available from the corresponding author upon request.
