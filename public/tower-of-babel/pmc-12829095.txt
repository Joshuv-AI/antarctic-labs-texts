Challenges in HIV care at a tertiary hospital repurposed for COVID-19: an observational study (2019–2022)
Authors: Karla Alejandra Romero-Mora, María Gómez-Palacio-Schjetnan, Vanessa Dávila-Conn, Carlos Alberto García-Delgado, Mariela Cervantes-Valenzuela, Claudia Alvarado-de la Barrera, Samuel Ehecatl Schulz-Medina, Santiago Ávila-Ríos
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe COVID-19 pandemic disrupted global healthcare systems, reducing access to care for people with chronic conditions, including HIV. In Mexico, the National Institute of Respiratory Diseases (INER) was fully repurposed as a COVID-19 referral center, interrupting routine HIV services. CIENI, an HIV clinic within INER, implemented mitigation strategies to preserve care for people living with HIV (PLWH). Concurrently, national guidelines introduced BIC/FTC/TAF as the new first-line antiretroviral regimen in 2019. We aimed to assess the impact of INER’s reconversion on clinical outcomes of PLWH, within the context of newly adopted treatment guidelines.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective, repeated cross-sectional study at an specialized HIV clinic located inside the largest referral center for complicated COVID-19 nationally. Three periods were defined: pre-pandemic (Jan 2019–Feb 2020), pandemic (Mar 2020–Dec 2021), and post-pandemic (Jan–Dec 2022). Outcomes included HIV-related clinical markers (viral load, CD4), follow-up indicators (adherence, antiretroviral [ART] delivery, treatment abandonment, hospitalization, referrals, death), chronic comorbidities (hypertension, obesity, dyslipidemia, diabetes, cancer), and common infections (syphilis, HBV, HCV, TB). Statistical comparisons between pre- and post-pandemic periods included bivariate analyses, linear regression model on hospitalization rates change, and logistic regression for weight gain after ART regimen change. All analyses were conducted in R.ResultsAmong 700 PLWH (85.7% men), 79.2% continued care at our center, 13.9% were referred elsewhere, 6.9% were lost to follow-up and 2% died. During repurposing, HIV testing, ART initiation, on-site consultations, and non-COVID-19 hospitalizations were suspended. The proportion with CD4 < 200 cells/µL decreased from 10.8% to 6.1% (p = 0.004), while undetectable viral load frequencies remained stable (90.4% vs. 90.7%, p = 0.421). Dyslipidemia increased from 65.3% to 77.8% (p < 0.001), and obesity from 11.3% to 20.2% (p < 0.001). No significant association was observed between BIC/FTC/TAF use and BMI change in the post-pandemic period. Frequencies of cancer, tuberculosis, HBV, and HCV declined, likely due to limited screening (all p < 0.014). Mitigation strategies included multi-month ART dispensation, hospitalization agreements, and telemedicine.ConclusionsLocally-implemented strategies and ART switch sustained HIV control and reduced mortality, but comorbidities were insufficiently addressed. Timely access to HIV care should be prioritized in future crises.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-12357-5.Keywords: HIV care continuum, AIDS, COVID-19 pandemic, Tertiary care centers, Bictegravir/emtricitabine/tenofovir alafenamide

Introduction

The coronavirus disease (COVID-19) pandemic imposed a substantial global burden on healthcare systems, affecting not only patients with COVID-19 but also those with other acute and chronic conditions [1]. Health services were widely disrupted, and personnel were frequently reassigned to COVID-19 care [2]. Many tertiary centers were converted into COVID-19 facilities due to their specialized infrastructure and expertise in infectious diseases, which enabled rapid repurposing to expand capacity but led to postponement of non-COVID-19 services. Remote consultations were adopted for chronic conditions, including HIV. During 2020–2021, global reports described reduced virological and immunological monitoring, limited appointment availability, and decreased HIV testing [3]. At that time, an estimated 39 million people were living with HIV, 76% of whom were receiving antiretroviral therapy requiring regular follow-up [4]. Consequently, disruptions in HIV care increased the risk of adverse outcomes such as opportunistic infections, drug resistance, comorbidities, and mortality [5].

The Center for Research in Infectious Diseases (CIENI), a specialized clinic for the care of PLWH in Mexico City, is housed within the National Institute of Respiratory Diseases (INER), Mexico’s largest respiratory specialty public hospital for people without health insurance. INER previously maintained a dedicated inpatient ward for the hospitalization of PLWH with complicated clinical presentations. During the COVID-19 pandemic, this space, along with the rest of the hospital, was fully repurposed to provide exclusive care for patients with severe COVID-19, in line with Ministry of Health mandates.

[6]. In this context, we describe the strategies implemented at the CIENI to mitigate the impact of the pandemic. Additionally, in 2019, a national public policy in Mexico introduced new treatment guidelines for PLWH, adopting bictegravir/emtricitabine/ tenofovir alafenamide (BIC/FTC/TAF) as the first-line regimen [7], which was implemented around mid-2019 at the CIENI. This study aimed to evaluate the impact of the reconversion of INER into the largest national COVID-19 referral center on the healthcare of PLWH, within the context of newly adopted treatment guidelines. Specifically, we assessed: (1) HIV-related clinical outcomes, including viral load and CD4 T cell counts; (2) discontinuation of clinical follow-up, including treatment abandonment, changes in hospitalization due to non-COVID-19 causes; referrals to other institutions, and death; (3) the diagnosis and laboratory-based follow-up of chronic comorbidities (hypertension, obesity, dyslipidemia, diabetes, and cancer) and other infectious diseases common in PLWH (syphilis, hepatitis B, hepatitis C, and tuberculosis). We further describe the strategies implemented at CIENI to mitigate the negative effects of this institutional reconversion on HIV care delivery.

Methods

Study population

This retrospective, repeated cross-sectional, observational study was conducted at CIENI-INER. The study was reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Committee and the Research Committee of INER (Approval No. C53-22). For this study, three distinct time periods were considered: the pre-pandemic (January 2019 to February 2020); the pandemic (March 2020 to December 2021); and the post-pandemic (January 2022 to December 2022). At the time of data collection, a total of 2,500 PLWH were receiving clinical care at the CIENI. Given constraints on available personnel (who could review only a limited number of clinical records per day) and the fact that most records were paper-based, requiring manual extraction of key variables, we opted to draw a representative sample of our center. Sampling is a well-established approach in such settings, offering logistical feasibility while preserving the ability to make valid inferences about the source population [8].

Participants selection and data collection

From a list of all “active” patients receiving care at CIENI during the pre-pandemic period, we selected 700 individuals based on the sample size calculation described above. To perform this selection, we generated random numbers in Excel and matched them to patient record IDs.

Each selected record was manually reviewed by a physician to identify documented diagnoses of chronic comorbidities and their approximate dates. For a subset of patients without explicit diagnoses recorded in the medical charts, some chronic conditions (e.g., diabetes, dyslipidemia) were inferred based on laboratory results, using current clinical definitions (see Outcomes section).

For infectious diseases, we extracted results from relevant diagnostic tests documented in the clinical records. All assessments were based on the most recent available records for each individual within each time period (pre-pandemic and post-pandemic). Laboratory data were obtained from CIENI and INER databases. Follow-up information for referred patients, including mortality, was retrieved from SALVAR, the national HIV treatment monitoring platform, which is audited to ensure reliability of ART and vital status data. A verification process was conducted in 2024 within the SALVAR platform to confirm the vital status of individuals lost to follow-up, determining whether they were alive or deceased. In cases of patients who migrated to another state or clinic, it was only possible to confirm whether they were alive, but no additional information could be obtained regarding ongoing treatment, regimen type, or clinical follow-up.

Study outcomes

To assess the potential negative impact of INER’s reconversion into a COVID-19 center on HIV care, we evaluated four domains: (1) HIV-related clinical outcomes: viral load and CD4 T cell counts to assess control and detect virological failure; (2) clinical follow-up: ART delivery, self-reported adherence, treatment abandonment, referrals, changes in hospitalization rates among PLWH, and death; (3) chronic non-communicable comorbidities: hypertension, obesity, dyslipidemia, diabetes, and cancer; and (4) other infections common in PLWH: syphilis, hepatitis B, hepatitis C, and tuberculosis.

Hypertension and cancer were identified based on documented diagnoses.

Dyslipidemia was defined per European Society of Cardiology/European Atherosclerosis Society guidelines. Type 2 diabetes was defined as glycated hemoglobin ≥ 6.5% and/or fasting glucose ≥ 126 mg/dL. Active hepatitis B and C infections were defined by detectable viral replication markers (HBV DNA or HBsAg and/or HBeAg for HBV; HCV RNA for HCV) following positive serologic screening. Active syphilis was defined as a positive treponemal test with a reactive non-treponemal test, indicating ongoing infection. For most infections, the presumed route of transmission was sexual, based on patient history and epidemiological data.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics for continuous variables were reported as median and interquartile range. Age was categorized as below or greater than/equal to the median; CD4+ T cell counts, and HIV viral load were stratified according to clinical criteria. Changes in quantitative laboratory parameters during follow-up were evaluated by comparing values from the pre-pandemic and post-pandemic periods. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. We performed exploratory and bivariate data analyses to compare the frequencies and distribution of systemic arterial hypertension (SAH), dyslipidemia, weight disorders, cancer, diabetes, tuberculosis, syphilis, hepatitis B virus (HBV), and hepatitis C virus (HCV) between the two periods. Continuous variables were compared using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for paired samples; categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Two-sided tests were used, and p values ≤ 0.05 were considered significant. In addition, we performed a logistic regression model to analyze the factors associated with transitioning from a healthy weight status in the pre-pandemic period to overweight or obesity in the post-pandemic period. Weight status was defined using post-pandemic measurements and categorized according to standard clinical cut-offs. A linear regression model using 2015–2019 data was built to identify predictors of annual admissions at CIENI and expected hospitalizations for 2020–2022, based on official data on newly reported HIV diagnoses at the national and Mexico City level [10], and estimates of new HIV infections in Mexico [11]. All analyses were performed using R and Rstudio version 3.4.3 (2017).

Results

Timeline of events and mitigation strategies against the COVID-19 pandemic

On February 20, 2020, the first case of SARS-CoV-2 infection in Mexico was diagnosed at the INER, and one month later, the hospital was converted into the main national referral center exclusively admitting patients with severe COVID-19 (Fig. 1). Consequently, the CIENI rapidly opened a mobile pharmacy for multi-month ART dispensation, established agreements with other hospitals for inpatient care of PLWH with emergencies or opportunistic infections, and coordinated with INER’s central laboratory to ensure continued blood sample collection and follow-up clinical testing. Subsequently, remote consultations in psychology, psychiatry, internal medicine, infectious diseases, neurology, and pneumology were initiated for the clinical follow-up of PLWH, which also included the evaluation of ART adherence. Between August 2020 and October 2021, a total of 4,576 teleconsultations were provided to CIENI patients. Rapid diagnostic testing for hepatitis C was also carried out (2019: 1,457 tests; 2020: 433 tests; 2021: 115 tests). Figure 1 illustrates the strategies implemented to maintain continuity of clinical care in the absence of in-person consultations. During the pandemic period, the CIENI interrupted HIV diagnostic testing, admissions of individuals newly diagnosed with HIV infection, ART initiation, and on-site consultations. PLWH newly diagnosed with HIV infection were referred to other healthcare centers according to their social security status (Table 1). Throughout the study period, CD4 T lymphocyte counts were performed every 6 or 12 months, as clinically indicated; HIV viral load was measured every 6 months; and serologic testing for HBV, HCV, and SARS-CoV-2 was conducted. Communication with PLWH was actively maintained through social media platforms.

Participant follow-up

A total of 700 PLWH were included, of whom 600 were men (85.7%). Throughout the study, 554 individuals (79.2%) continued receiving care at the CIENI; 97 individuals (13.9%) were referred to social security institutions based on employment status; and 48 individuals (6.9%) were lost to follow-up due to treatment abandonment or migration to another state within the country.

A total of 427 individuals consistently collected their ART, of whom 368 had undetectable viral loads (86.1%). An additional 125 individuals collected ART intermittently, with medication delivery records indicating potential treatment interruptions exceeding 30 days. Nevertheless, 114 of them (91.2%) had undetectable viral loads in the post-pandemic period. Intermittent ART collection does not necessarily indicate treatment interruption, as some individuals may have picked up medication later or had surplus from prior dispensations. Additionally, 132 individuals did not collect ART during the study period. Among them, 64 were lost to follow-up, 63 were referred to other institutions, and 4 were reported deceased (Table 1). Most PLWH were initially on EFV/FTC/TDF, but following the 2019 national policy recommending BIC/FTC/TAF as the preferred first-line regimen, 84% had switched by the time of review. The mortality rate remained stable at 2% across study periods, with 14 deaths recorded, all due to unknown causes.

Amid INER’s reconversion, only patients with severe COVID‑19 were admitted, suspending admissions of PLWH who required hospitalization for emergencies or opportunistic infections formerly treated in a dedicated unit. This contrasts with the number of hospitalizations for PLWH before the pandemic: 388 in 2017, 460 in 2018, and 416 in 2019. A linear regression model using 2015–2019 data showed positive associations between annual hospitalizations and national HIV diagnoses (coefficient = 0.197, p = 0.022) and estimated new infections (coefficient = 0.026, p = 0.028), and a negative association with HIV diagnoses in Mexico City (coefficient = − 0.157, p = 0.027). The model predicted 326 hospitalizations for 2020, 201 for 2021, and 149 for 2022. Observed hospitalizations were lower in 2020 (91) and 2021 (73), but exceeded expectations in 2022 (Fig. 2). Similarly, new patient enrollment at CIENI dropped sharply in 2020–2021, with partial recovery in 2022—suggesting those not yet in care were most affected.

HIV-related clinical outcomes

In the pre-pandemic period, 64 individuals (10.8%) had CD4 T cell counts < 200 cells/µL, which decreased to 36 individuals (6.1%) in the post-pandemic period (p = 0.004). The proportion of individuals with undetectable viral loads remained stable, with 535 (90.4%) in the pre-pandemic period and 537 (90.7%) in the post-pandemic period (p = 0.421; Table 2). HIV viral load data were unavailable for 102 individuals at the end of the study. Of those, 50 were referred to social security institutions, 40 were lost to follow-up, and 12 were reported deceased. The 6.9% loss-to-follow-up rate was considered too low to compromise the validity of our findings, suggesting that HIV control was maintained among PLWH who remained in care at CIENI during both periods. Notably, from 2019 to 2022, the annual rates of individuals with virological failure were 0.9%, 0.6%, 0.3%, and 0.5%, respectively, with no significant differences across years (p = 0.227).

Prevalence of comorbidities in the studied population

Comorbidity frequencies were compared between the pre- and post-pandemic periods. The prevalence of dyslipidemia increased significantly, from 65.3% to 77.8% (p < 0.001), while a slight increase was observed in the prevalence of systemic arterial hypertension (10.6% vs. 12.5%, p = 0.397).

During the pre-pandemic period, 99.2% of PLWH were tested for HCV, compared to 80.1% in the post-pandemic period. HBV testing rates also declined from 99.1% to 77.8%, as did syphilis testing, which dropped from 86.2% to 78.2%. However, the absolute number of syphilis diagnoses decreased only slightly between the pre- and post-pandemic periods (106 vs. 99) (Table 2). Similarly, the observed decline in the prevalence of comorbidities requiring comprehensive medical evaluations for diagnosis (e.g., cancer, tuberculosis) was largely driven by the suspension of on-site consultations during INER’s repurposing. This suggests that the important reduction in prevalence of certain comorbidities in the post-pandemic period was, at least in part, attributable to decreased screening.

Notably, the number of individuals with obesity (body mass index [BMI] ≥ 30) increased from 28 (11.3%) before the pandemic to 50 (20.2%) after it (p < 0.001), while median BMI rose from 24.7 to 26.0 (p < 0.001) (Fig. 3). Thus, the pre-pandemic median was within the normal weight range, but shifted to the overweight category post-pandemic. Of the total sample, ART regimen information was available of 696 individuals; among them, 84% (585/696) switched to a different regimen during the study period. Of those who switched, 86% (503/585) transitioned to BIC/FTC/TAF. However, switching to BIC/FTC/TAF was not associated with an increase in BMI (p = 0.131) or weight gain (p = 0.159) in the bivariate analysis. In the logistic regression model assessing factors associated with transitioning from a healthy BMI to overweight or obesity in the post-pandemic period, no significant associations were found between BMI change and sex, age, BIC/FTC/TAF use, or year of ART switch (all p > 0.05). Instead, weight gain was independently associated with uncontrolled dyslipidemia (aOR:2.848; 95% CI: 1.093–7.418; p = 0.032) and with de novo dyslipidemia (aOR: 3.986; 95% CI: 1.308–12.143; p = 0.015) (see Supplementary Figure).

The median glucose level in the pre-pandemic period was 92 mg/dL (25th percentile [p25]: 85, 75th percentile [p75]: 100), decreasing to 90 mg/dL in the post-pandemic period (p25: 84, p75: 96). Even though this decrease was significant (p = 0.004), median plasma glucose levels remained within the normal and accepted range, and were not clinically relevant. In contrast, median LDL cholesterol level in the pre-pandemic period was 108 mg/dL (p25: 86, p75: 130), and increased to 114 mg/dL (p25: 93, p75: 133) in the post-pandemic period. This rise was statistically significant (p < 0.001) and considered clinically relevant.

Discussion

We evaluated clinical outcomes among PLWH after the INER was converted into the national referral center for severe COVID-19. Despite service disruptions, ART provision, adherence, and virological control were maintained among patients who remained in care at CIENI. The stability in mortality and viral suppression rates suggests effective continuity of HIV management, contrasting with reports from other settings where disruptions occurred [12–15]. However, HIV testing, ART initiation, on-site consultations, and non-COVID-19 hospitalizations were suspended, limiting this analysis to patients already engaged in care, monitored through laboratory tests and telemedicine.

To mitigate the pandemic’s impact, CIENI implemented measures such as a mobile pharmacy for multi-month ART and vaccination, telemedicine services [16], agreements with other hospitals for emergency admissions, coordination with specialized clinics for HIV diagnosis, continued local monitoring of HIV and comorbidities, and active communication through social media. These strategies were progressively adapted to evolving needs. Similar initiatives in other HIV centers included self-testing, multi-month PrEP dispensing, integration of COVID-19 care, and expanded use of virtual platforms for education and supervision [17, 18].

The sharp decline in hospital admissions in 2020–2021, beyond model projections, likely reflects the widespread healthcare disruptions caused by the pandemic. PLWH not yet in care were especially impacted, aligning with global reports of reduced HIV testing and diagnosis services [19–22]. This is critical for newly diagnosed individuals, who often present with advanced disease; in Mexico, mortality among hospitalized PLWH doubled during this period [23].

Lower detection of cancer, TB, HBV, and HCV in the post-pandemic phase likely reflects reduced screening rather than true declines. Syphilis cases may also be underestimated based on regional trends in the Americas [24]. HBV and HCV are bloodborne pathogens, primarily sexually transmitted in this cohort, but perinatal and injection routes are relevant, with documented intrauterine HBV cases [41]. TB timely diagnosis and treatment remains a challenge [45].

Suspension of in-person consultations hampered diagnosis and monitoring of conditions where physical examination is essential, like hypertension and cancer. These disruptions likely led to underestimation of asymptomatic or undiagnosed conditions. Long COVID, for example, may affect PLWH in ways not captured in routine clinical records. Studies suggest persistent symptoms, immune dysregulation, and organ damage in the post-acute phase of COVID-19 [25, 26]. Some cases of fever of unknown origin (FUO) during the pandemic may have been driven by undiagnosed COVID-19 or overlapping opportunistic infections in PLWH [40].

Given the 2019 change in national guidelines recommending ART regimens based on integrase strand transfer inhibitor (INSTI) [27], we report the clinical outcomes associated with the switch in ART regimen. During the COVID-19 pandemic, a high proportion of participants experienced increases in weight or BMI. The weight change, however, was not associated with BIC/FTC/TAF regimen in this study, despite prior associations in the literature [28–30]. The extent to which weight gain was driven by the ART regimen remains unclear, given simultaneous unmeasured pandemic-related lifestyle modifications, that lead to a higher frequency of sedentary habits during the lockdown [31]. Factors like dietary changes and psychological stress due to prolonged isolation may have also influenced weight increase [32]. Additionally, variations may occur by gender [33] and ethnicity [34]. Overall, PLWH are at increased risk of cardiometabolic complications [35], and weight gain may exacerbate this risk [36–39].

The increase in dyslipidemia may be linked to deferred in-person care, which is essential for the prevention and management of comorbidities, and to TAF-associated effects [32, 33]. Weight gain in PLWH is also linked to dyslipidemia [34]. These findings underscore persistent comorbidity risks, despite high virological suppression.

Our study have several limitations. Its retrospective design precludes causal inference, and manual data collection from heterogeneous sources may have introduced misclassification. Unmeasured confounders could affect the observed association between weight and BIC/FTC/TAF use [37]. Patient loss to follow-up due to to the pandemic itself, repurposing of INER, or migration and transfer of PLWH, potentially introduces missing data bias. The absence of inter-institutional data sharing limited assessment of virological control of referred patients. The apparent reduction in comorbidities may reflect under-screening rather than a true decline. Our study population consisted of PLWH already engaged in care—individuals who are more likely to adhere to treatment and maintain regular follow-up—which can lead to selection bias if individuals who were less adherent or had poorer HIV control were more likely to be lost to follow-up and therefore excluded from our analysis. This could result in an overestimation of treatment success among PLWH in care, since our sample may disproportionately represent those with better outcomes. As this study was conducted in a tertiary care hospital, the generalizability of the findings to the broader national population may be limited.

Finally, our analysis was limited to conditions that were documented in clinical records or confirmed by lab results. As such, asymptomatic or subclinical conditions may have been missed. Given that the follow-up was laboratory-based, clinical complications that can influence prognosis were likely missed [42, 44]. Further research is needed to assess the burden of long COVID and other post-pandemic health effects in PLWH. The increasing use and improvement of prognostic markers, however, may help identify complications in settings with limited in-person follow-up [43].

In summary, the CIENI maintained continuity of care for PLWH already engaged in services, preserving virological control. Yet, the pandemic significantly disrupted testing, new diagnoses, consultations, and emergency admissions. Future responses should prioritize continuity of care for vulnerable populations disproportionately affected by service disruptions, such as PLWH, with emphasis on ensuring timely access to HIV services.

Supplementary Materials

Supplementary Material 1: Suppl F. Logistic regression model showing factors associated with transitioning from a healthy BMI to overweight or obesity. Odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) are presented for each variable. The model was adjusted for sex, age, ART regimen (BIC/FTC/TAF), dyslipidemia, glucose levels, and year of ART switch. Uncontrolled dyslipidemia and treated dyslipidemia were significantly associated with increased odds of developing overweight or obesity.
