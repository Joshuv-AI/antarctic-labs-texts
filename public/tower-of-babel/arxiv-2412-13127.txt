1.
Introduction

A graph
G=(V,E)
is called rigid in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, or
d
-rigid, if for a generic embedding
{\bf p}:V\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
(i.e., the
d|V|
coordinates of
{\bf p}
are algebraically independent over the rationals), every continuous motion of the vertices in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
that starts at
{\bf p}
, and preserves the lengths of all the edges of
G
, does not change the distance between any two vertices. Asimow and Roth
[
1
,
2
]
discovered that this is equivalent to
infinitesimal
generic rigidity. In particular, a graph with more than
d
vertices is
d
-rigid if the
|E|\times d|V|
rigidity matrix of the framework
(G,p)
is of rank
d|V|-\binom{d+1}{2}
(see more in Subsection
2.1
). It is easy to see that
1
-dimensional rigidity is equivalent to graph connectivity. In addition, Laman famously discovered a combinatorial characterization of planar rigidity
[
20
]
. In contrast, for all
d>2
the combinatorics of
d
-rigid graphs is far from being understood and the topic is studied extensively
[
5
,
9
,
15
]
.

The Erdős-Rényi
G(n,p)
random graph is one of the most well-studied models in random graph theory
[
4
,
7
]
. A graph in this model contains
n
vertices, and each edge appears independently with probability
p=p(n)
. The theory studies the properties that occur in
G(n,p)
asymptotically almost surely (a.a.s.), i.e., with probability tending to
1
as
n\to\infty
. A monotone graph property has a sharp threshold at
p_{c}=p_{c}(n)
if for every
\varepsilon>0
,
G(n,(1-\varepsilon)p_{c})
a.a.s. does not have the property, but
G(n,(1+\varepsilon)p_{c})
a.a.s. does have it.

Rigidity of random graphs had been primarily studied in Euclidean spaces of a fixed dimension
d
. The goal of this work is to study the generic rigidity of a
G(n,p)
random graph in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
where the dimension
d
diverges as
n\to\infty
. The question we investigate is:

What is the largest dimension
d=d(n,p)
for which
G(n,p)
is
d
-rigid?

Clearly, a graph cannot be
d
-rigid if
d
is greater than its minimum degree. In addition, it is known that for a fixed dimension
d
, the sharp threshold probability for
d
-rigidity of
G\sim G(n,p)
coincides with the threshold probability for the property that its minimum degree
\delta(G)
is at least
d
. The case
d=1
of this statement is precisely the seminal result of Erdős and Rényi regarding the connectivity threshold of random graphs
[
6
]
. The case
d=2
was solved by Jackson, Servatius and Servatius
[
11
]
. For larger fixed dimensions
d>2
, the problem was studied by Király and Theran
[
17
]
, Jordán and Tanigawa
[
13
]
, and was resolved by Lew, Nevo, the first author and Raz
[
21
]
. They showed that
G(n,p)
is a.a.s.
d
-rigid if
np-(\log n+(d-1)\log\log n)\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
, and that it is a.a.s. not
d
-rigid if this term tends to
-\infty
.

This tells us that if
np=\log n+O(\log\log n)
, then the bottleneck for rigidity of
G(n,p)
lies in its minimum degree. On the other hand, Krivelevich, Lew and Michaeli
[
18
]
conjecture that if
np=\omega(\log n)
, then the bottleneck for rigidity of
G(n,p)
lies in its
number of edges
. More precisely, it is clear that if
G\sim G(n,p)
is a.a.s
d
-rigid then the inequality
p\binom{n}{2}\geq dn-\binom{d+1}{2}
is satisfied, since the number of edges in
G
concentrates around its mean. This reformulates to the inequality
d\leq(1-o(1))n(1-\sqrt{1-p})
, and the conjecture is that this bound is sharp.

Conjecture 1

(Conjecture 1.11 in
[
18
]
)
.

For every
p=\omega(\log n/n)
, and every fixed
\varepsilon>0
, a random graph
G\sim G(n,p)
is a.a.s
d
-rigid if
d<(1-\varepsilon)n(1-\sqrt{1-p})
.

Among other things, their paper establishes, using strong
d
-rigid partitions, the weaker assertion that
G
is a.a.s
(c\cdot np/\log{np})
-rigid for some constant
c>0
.

Given these two trivial bottlenecks for rigidity, the first step in our research was to compute the regimes of
p
in which one bottleneck is more restrictive than the other. Namely, let
p_{c}:=C_{*}\log n/{n}
, where
C_{*}:={2}/{(1-\log 2)}\approx 6.518
. It turns out that for every
\varepsilon>0
, the inequality
\delta(G)<n(1-\sqrt{1-p})
holds a.a.s. if
p<(1-\varepsilon)p_{c}
, while the reverse inequality holds a.a.s. if
p>(1+\varepsilon)p_{c}
. Further details can be found in Subsection
2.3
and Figure
1
.

In particular, for every fixed
\varepsilon>0
, if
p>(1+\varepsilon)p_{c}
then
G
a.a.s. does not have enough edges to be
\delta(G)
-rigid. Our first main theorem asserts that this is the only bottleneck for
\delta(G)
-rigidity in
G(n,p)
.

Theorem 1
.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
, suppose
1-p=\omega(n^{-2})
, and let
G\sim G(n,p)
. Then,

\mathbb{P}(G\mbox{ is $\delta(G)$-rigid})\to\left\{\begin{matrix}1&p<(1-\varepsilon)\frac{C_{*}\log n}{n}\vskip 5.69046pt\\
0&p>(1+\varepsilon)\frac{C_{*}\log n}{n}\end{matrix}\right.,

as
n\to\infty
, where
C_{*}=2/(1-\log 2)
.

Theorem
1
proves, in particular, that (the complement of)
\delta(G)
-rigidity has a threshold in
G(n,p)
, even though it is not a monotone property. Indeed,
note that if
p=1-o(n^{-2})
then
G=K_{n}
, and thus
\delta(G)-rigid
, with probability bounded away from
0
.

For
p\geq p_{c}
, we are able to prove Conjecture
1
in the regime where
p=o(n^{-1/2})
. Note that
1-\sqrt{1-p}=(1/2+o(1))p
in this regime, since
p=o(1)
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
C_{*}=2/(1-\log 2)
, and suppose that
C_{*}\log n/n\leq p=o(n^{-1/2})
. If
G\sim G(n,p)
then, for every fixed
1/2>\varepsilon>0
and integers
d=d(n)
,

\mathbb{P}(G\mbox{ is $d$-rigid})\to\left\{\begin{matrix}1&d<(1/2-\varepsilon)np\vskip 5.69046pt\\
0&d>(1/2+\varepsilon)np\end{matrix}\right.,

as
n\to\infty
.

Figure 1
.
Rigidity of
G(n,p)
where
p=c\log n/n
. Here the average degree is asymptotic to
c\log n
, the edge-number bottleneck is asymptotic to
(c/2)\log n
, and the
Y
-axis is normalized by
\log n
. The shaded area illustrates the values of
(c,d)
for which
G(n,p)
is a.a.s.
d
-rigid. If
c<C_{*}
the minimum degree is the bottleneck for rigidity, and otherwise the edge number is the bottleneck.

The non-rigidity statements in both our theorems are immediate and follow directly from a dimension counting argument. The proofs of the rigidity statements are carried out using a similar strategy to
[
21
]
. First, we prove that the closure of
G
in the rigidity matroid is dense. Subsequently, we deduce that the closure contains a large clique. The final step uses the combinatorial expansion properties of the random graph. Namely, we ”bootstrap” the large clique in its closure to show it must be rigid. However, elements of this strategy that succeeded for fixed-dimensional rigidity break down when the dimension scales with the graph size, and two essential new ideas were needed.

First, for large
d
, we could not find an elementary way to derive the existence of a large clique in the closure from the assertion that it is dense. Fortunately, this can be derived using ideas from Villány’s beautiful proof of the Lovász-Yemini conjecture
[
24
]
. Primarily, it is our use of Villány’s argument that requires us assume that
p=o(n^{-1/2})
in Theorem
2
.

Second, as
\varepsilon
approaches
0
, i.e., as
p
approaches
C_{*}\log n/n
in Theorem
1
and as
d
approaches
np/2
in Theorem
2
, the lower bound our probabilistic method gives on the density of the closure deteriorates substantially. In consequence, the size of the clique in the closure we are able to guarantee is far too small to set off the final combinatorial “bootstrap” part of our argument. To overcome this challenge, we extend our probabilistic approach and show that the closure is, roughly speaking,
evenly distributed
across the vertices. This strategy allows us to establish the existence of a very large clique in the closure. To achieve this goal, we work with many projections of the generic rigidity matrix.

1.1.
Generic global rigidity and reconstruction

A
d
-dimensional framework
(G,{\bf p})
is called
globally

d
-rigid if every embedding
{\bf p}^{\prime}:V\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
that realizes the pairwise distances
\|{\bf p}(v)-{\bf p}(u)\|
for every edge
uv\in E
is obtained from
{\bf p}
by an isometry of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We say that a graph
G
is globally
d
-rigid if
(G,{\bf p})
is globally
d
-rigid for some generic embedding
{\bf p}
. For a comprehensive introduction to global rigidity, its distinctions from rigidity and infinitesimal rigidity, and an overview of key developments in this area over recent decades, we refer the reader to
[
23
,
14
]
and the references therein. For random graphs, it is shown in
[
21
]
that for a fixed
d
, a.a.s., a
G(n,p)
graph is globally
d
-rigid if its minimum degree is
d+1
. In addition, non-generic global
1
-rigidity in random graphs has been studied recently
[
3
,
8
,
22
]
in the context of reconstructing embeddings from random distance samples.

We recall two results from the theory of global rigidity that are needed here. First, the minimum degree of a globally
d
-rigid graph must be at least
d+1
because a vertex with a smaller degree can be reflected across an affine hyperplane spanned by its neighbors. This produces a distinct embedding of the vertices that preserves the distances between adjacent pairs (see
[
10
]
). Second, Jordán proved that every
(d+1)
-rigid graph is globally
d
-rigid
[
16
]
. Therefore, the following theorems follow directly from our main theorems.

Theorem 1.1
.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
and let
G\sim G(n,p)
. Then,

\mathbb{P}(G\mbox{ is globally $(\delta(G)-1)$-rigid})\to\left\{\begin{matrix}1&p<(1-\varepsilon)\frac{C_{*}\log n}{n}\vskip 5.69046pt\\
0&p>(1+\varepsilon)\frac{C_{*}\log n}{n}\end{matrix}\right.,

as
n\to\infty
.

Theorem 1.2
.

If
~C_{*}\log n/n\leq p=o(n^{-1/2})
and
G\sim G(n,p)
then, for every fixed
1/2>\varepsilon>0
and integers
d=d(n)
,

\mathbb{P}(G\mbox{ is globally $d$-rigid})\to\left\{\begin{matrix}1&d<(1/2-\varepsilon)np\vskip 5.69046pt\\
0&d>(1/2+\varepsilon)np\end{matrix}\right.,

as
n\to\infty
.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we present some necessary preliminaries in rigidity and random graphs. Afterwards, in Section
3
we prove the main theorems. We conclude with open problems in Section
4
.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Generic infinitesimal rigidity

A
d
-dimensional framework is a pair
(G,{\bf p})
consisting of a graph
G=(V,E)
and an embedding
{\bf p}:V\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Throughout this paper we assume that
|V|>d
and that
{\bf p}
is generic, i.e., the
d|V|
coordinates of
{\bf p}
are algebraically independent over the rationals. The
|E|\times d|V|
rigidity matrix
R=R(G,{\bf p})
of the framework is the total derivative of the function

\mathbb{R}^{d|V|}\to\mathbb{R}^{|E|}\,\,,\quad{\bf q}\mapsto\left(\frac{1}{2}\|{\bf q}(x)-{\bf q}(y)\|_{2}^{2}\right)_{xy\in E}

at point
{\bf q}={\bf p}
. Here we view a point
{\bf q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d|V|}
as a function from
V\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
. More directly, the row
\mathbf{r}_{xy}
in
R
indexed by
xy\in E
contains the vector
({\bf p}(x)-{\bf p}(y))^{T}
in the
d
coordinates corresponding to
x
, the vector
({\bf p}(y)-{\bf p}(x))^{T}
in the
d
coordinates corresponding to
y
, and
0
elsewhere. The tangent space at the identity of the isometry group of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
yields vectors in the right kernel of
R
. Moreover, under the assumptions that
|V|>d
and
{\bf p}
is generic, these vectors span a subspace of the kernel of dimension
\binom{d+1}{2}
whence
\dim\ker R\geq\binom{d+1}{2}
. A graph
G
is called
d
-rigid if this bound is attained, which in this case means that
\rank R=d|V|-\binom{d+1}{2}
. Asimow and Roth showed that if
{\bf p}
is generic, this notion is a well-defined graph property, namely it is independent of the choice of the generic embedding
{\bf p}

[
1
,
2
]
. If
|V|\leq d
then the rank of the rigidity matrix is at most
\binom{|V|}{2}
.

The
n
-vertex generic
d
-rigidity matroid is the linear matroid whose ground set consists of the edges of the complete graph
K_{n}
and a subset
E
is independent if the rows corresponding to
E
are linearly independent in the rigidity matrix of
K_{n}
with some generic embedding
{\bf p}
. The notion of
closure
from matroid theory is central in our work so we recall its definition. Recall that
\mathbf{r}_{e}
denotes the row corresponding to an edge
e
in the rigidity matrix.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
d\geq 1
an integer,
G
be an
n
-vertex graph, and
{\bf p}:[n]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
generic. The
d
-rigidity closure of
G
is the
n
-vertex graph whose edge set is

C_{d}(G)=\left\{f\in\binom{[n]}{2}~:~\mathbf{r}_{f}\in\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{R}}(\mathbf{r}_{e}~:~e\in G).\right\}

A graph is called closed in the
d
-rigidity matroid if
G=C_{d}(G)
.

Clearly,
G\subset C_{d}(G)
, and a graph is
d
-rigid if and only if
C_{d}(G)
is the complete graph. In addition,
C_{d}(G)\supseteq C_{d^{\prime}}(G)
if
d<d^{\prime}
since the
d
-rigidity matrix is a submatrix of the
d^{\prime}
-rigidity matrix.

2.1.1.
The structure of closed graphs

This subsection contains a number of structural claims on closed graphs that are needed in our argument. Most notably, a critical part of our argument builds upon the following key lemma from the recent proof of the Lovász-Yemini conjecture by Villányi
[
24
]
. While the lemma is not explicitly stated in the paper in the exact form we require, its underlying idea is fully present.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
d\geq 1
be an integer, and
G
a closed graph in the
d
-rigidity matroid with minimal degree at least
d(d+1).
Then, there is a vertex in
G
whose neighbors induce a clique.

Proof.

Suppose that
G
is a closed graph in the
d
-rigidity matroid, and let
v
be a vertex. Note that any two distinct maximal cliques
H_{1},H_{2}
in the subgraph induced by
v
’s neighbors intersect in at most
d-2
vertices. Indeed, otherwise
H_{1}\cup\{v\}
and
H_{2}\cup\{v\}
are two cliques in
G
that intersect in at least
d
neighbors, whence
H_{1}\cup H_{2}\cup\{v\}
is
d
-rigid. Since
G
is closed,
H_{1}\cup H_{2}\cup\{v\}
induces a clique in
G
, in contradiction to
H_{1},H_{2}
being maximal distinct cliques. Therefore, by Lemmas 3.1 and 3.2 in
[
24
]
either there is a vertex in
G
whose neighbors induce a clique, or the random subgraph
G_{\pi}
constructed by Villányi has, with positive probability, the contradictory property of being both independent in the
d
-rigidity matroid and having at least
d|V(G)|
edges.
∎

The following fact follows directly from the fact that
K_{d+2}
minus an edge is
d
-rigid.

Fact 2.3
.

Let
d\geq 1
be an integer, and
G
a closed graph in the
d
-rigidity matroid. Suppose that
A
induces a clique in
G
, and
v\notin A
is adjacent to at least
d
vertices from
A
. Then,
A\cup\{v\}
also induces a clique in
G
.

For the proof of the next claim, that was suggested to us by Orit Raz, we recall the notion of Henneberg steps. Given a graph
G
and a dimension
d
, a Henneberg
0
-step is the addition of a new vertex connected to
d
vertices of
G
, and a Henneberg
1
-step is the addition of a new vertex connected to
d+1
vertices
x_{1},....,x_{d+1}
of
G
, where
x_{1},x_{2}
are adjacent in
G
, followed by the removal of the edge
x_{1}x_{2}.
It is known that these steps preserve
d
-rigidity.

Claim 2.4
.

Let
d\geq 1
be an integer, and
G
a closed graph in the
d
-rigidity matroid. Suppose that
A
,
B
are disjoint vertex subsets that induce a clique in
G
, and that there exists a matching of
\binom{d+1}{2}
edges between
A
and
B
in
G
. Then,
A\cup B
induces a clique in
G
.

Proof.

It suffices to construct a
d
-rigid graph
H
on
d(d+1)
vertices, in which the edges between the first
\binom{d+1}{2}
and the last
\binom{d+1}{2}
vertices form a perfect matching. Indeed, the induced subgraph of
G
on
A\cup B
contains a copy of
H
and since
G
is closed and
H
is
d
-rigid the claim follows.

To construct
H
, we start with the complete graph on the vertices
x_{1},...,x_{d},y_{1}
. Then, we perform
d-1

0
-steps, where in the
i
-th step,
2\leq i\leq d
, we connect the new vertex
y_{i}
to
y_{1},...,y_{i-1},x_{i},...,x_{d}
. Note that besides the two cliques induced on
x_{1},...,x_{d}
and
y_{1},...,y_{d}
, this
d
-rigid graph contains the matching
x_{i}y_{i},~1\leq i\leq d
as well as
\binom{d+1}{2}-d
additional edges
e_{d+1},...,e_{\binom{d+1}{2}}
.

We would like to remove these additional edges and replace them by new vertices that form a matching. So, for each such additional edge
e_{k}
between
x_{i}y_{j},~i\neq j
, we (i) add a new vertex
x_{k}
, connect it to
y_{j},x_{1},...,x_{d}
and remove the edge
x_{i}y_{j}
, and afterwards (ii) add a new vertex
y_{k}
, connect it to
x_{k},y_{1},...,y_{d}
and remove the edge
x_{k}y_{j}
. Note that these two actions are
1
-steps hence they preserve
d
-rigidity. In addition, once all these steps are carried out, the only edges between
x_{1},....,x_{\binom{d+1}{2}}
and
y_{1},....,y_{\binom{d+1}{2}}
are those of the perfect matching
x_{k}y_{k}~,1\leq k\leq\binom{d+1}{2}
.
∎

2.1.2.
Projections of the rigidity matrix

Let
A\subseteq[n]
be a vertex subset and denote by
W_{A}
the orthogonal complement in
\mathbb{R}^{dn}
of
V_{A}:=\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\mathbf{r}_{e}~:~e\in\binom{A}{2}\right)
. In addition, denote the corresponding orthogonal projection by
P_{W_{A}}:\mathbb{R}^{dn}\to W_{A}
. Note that since
\dim V_{A}\geq d|A|-\binom{d+1}{2}
(equality holds if
|A|\geq d
) then,

\dim\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\left(P_{W_{A}}\mathbf{r}_{e}~:~e\in\binom{[n]}{2}\setminus\binom{A}{2}\right)\leq d(n-|A|).

(2.1)

We will consider the closure in the associated linear matroid,

C_{d,A}(G):=\left\{f\in\binom{[n]}{2}\setminus\binom{A}{2}~:~P_{W_{A}}\mathbf{r}_{f}\in\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{R}}(P_{W_{A}}\mathbf{r}_{e}~:~e\in G),\right\}.

Geometrically speaking,
C_{d,A}(G)
consists of the pairs whose distance is preserved in a rigid motion of
G
in which
A
is fixed, not including the trivial pairs
\binom{A}{2}
. The following claim is derived easily.

Claim 2.5
.

If
G
is an
n
-vertex graph and
A\subset[n]
induces a clique in
C_{d}(G)
then
C_{d,A}(G)\subset C_{d}(G)
.

Proof.

Let
f
be an edge that belongs to
C_{d,A}(G)
. There exists a linear combination

P_{W_{A}}\mathbf{r}_{f}=\sum_{e\in G}\alpha_{e}P_{W_{A}}\mathbf{r}_{e}.

Therefore, using
P_{W_{A}}=I-P_{V_{A}}
, we find that

\mathbf{r}_{f}=\sum_{e\in G}\alpha_{e}\mathbf{r}_{e}+P_{V_{A}}\left(\mathbf{r}_{f}-\sum_{e\in G}\alpha_{e}\mathbf{r}_{e}\right).

The claim follows by observing that (i)
P_{V_{A}}\mathbf{r}_{e^{\prime}}\in V_{A}
for every
e^{\prime}\in\binom{[n]}{2}
, and (ii)
V_{A}\subseteq\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{R}}\{\mathbf{r}_{e}:e\in G\}
since
A
induces a clique in
C_{d}(G)
.
∎

We note that this is a standard construction in matroid theory. Namely, the linear matroid that corresponds to
P_{W_{A}}\mathbf{r}_{e}~:~e\in\binom{[n]}{2}\setminus\binom{A}{2}
is the matroid obtained from the generic
d
-rigidity matroid by
contracting
all the edges in
\binom{A}{2}
.

2.3.
Minimum degree in random graphs

The main purpose of this subsection is to study the minimum degree of a
G(n,p)
random graph in the regime
p=c\log n/n
where
c>0
is a constant. This is a well-known topic in random graph theory
[
4
, Section 3]
and we include it here for completeness.

For a real number
c>1
, define the function

\varphi_{c}(t):=1-c+t-t\log(t/c).

(2.5)

and denote by
a=a(c)
the smallest non-negative root of
\varphi_{c}
. Note that
0<a<c
since
\varphi_{c}(t)
approaches
1-c<0
as
t\to 0
and
\varphi_{c}(c)=1
.

Roughly speaking, the number of vertices of degree
t\log n
in
G(n,c\log n/n)
is typically
n^{\varphi_{c}(t)+o(1)}.
The following claim is an important special case.

Claim 2.7
.

Suppose that
p=p_{n}
are real numbers such that the sequence
c_{n}:=(n-1)p/\log n
is bounded and satisfies
c_{n}\geq 1
for every
n
. If
G\sim G(n,p)
then, for every
\varepsilon>0
,

\mathbb{P}(|\delta(G)-a(c_{n})\log n|\leq\varepsilon\log n)\to 1

as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

The proof is based on straightforward applications of the first and second moment methods.
Let
\varepsilon>0
. Let
0<t<c=c_{n}
be a real number, and
X
the number of vertices of degree at most
t\log n
in
G
. The degree of a vertex in
G
is a binomial random variable
Y\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n-1,p)
, and therefore by Chernoff’s inequality (
2.2
),

\mathbb{P}_{\mathrm{Bin}(n-1,p)}(Y\leq t\log n)\leq\left(\frac{e^{-(1-t/c)}}{(t/c)^{t/c}}\right)^{c\log n}=n^{\varphi_{c}(t)-1}.

In conclusion, using linearity of expectation, we find that

\mathbb{E}[X]\leq n^{\varphi_{c}(t)}.

The lower bound follows directly since
t<a(c)-\varepsilon
implies that
\mathbb{E}[X]\to 0
as
n\to\infty
, and consequently
\delta(G)>t\log n
a.a.s.  as claimed.

On the other hand, if
k:=\lfloor t\log n\rfloor=(1+o(1))t\log n
then by Stirling’s approximation and the inequality
1-x\geq e^{-(x+x^{2})}
for sufficiently small
x
, we find that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Y=k)=

\displaystyle~\binom{n-1}{k}p^{k}(1-p)^{n-1-k}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle~\left((1+o(1))\frac{e(n-1)p}{k}\right)^{k}e^{-(p+p^{2})(n-1-k)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~n^{\varphi_{c}(t)-1+o(1)},

by a straightforward computation using
k=(1+o(1))t\log n
and
(n-1)p=c\log n
. Therefore, the expectation of the number
Z
of vertices of degree
k
is at least

\mathbb{E}[Z]\geq n^{\varphi_{c}(t)+o(1)}\to\infty,

assuming
t>a(c)+\varepsilon
.

In addition, if
D_{x},D_{y}
are the degrees of two vertices in
G
then a direct computation shows that

\mathbb{P}(D_{x}=k,D_{y}=k)=(1+o(1))\mathbb{P}(Y=k)^{2},

and consequently
\var(Z)=o(\mathbb{E}[Z]^{2})
. We obtain that a.a.s.
Z>0
by Chebyshev’s inequality, and therefore
\delta(G)\leq k\leq(a(c)+\varepsilon)\log n
a.a.s., as claimed.

∎

Using this claim, we can easily identify the critical probability at which the minimum-degree bottleneck for the rigidity of
G(n,p)
becomes less restrictive than the bottleneck induced by the number of edges. Specifically, the edge-number bottleneck asserts that the typical
d
-rigidity of
G(n,c\log n/n)
requires
d<(1+o(1))c\log n/2
. Let
C_{*}
denote the value of
c
such that
a=c/2
, i.e.,

\varphi_{C_{*}}(C_{*}/2)=1-C_{*}+(C_{*}/2)-C_{*}/2\log(1/2)=0,

Solving this yields
C_{*}=2/(1-\log 2)\approx 6.518
. Consequently, we find that
a<c/2
if and only if
c<C_{*}
(see Figure
1
).

2.3.1.
Matchings in random graphs

We also include here the following claim that is later used to bootstrap disjoint cliques into a larger clique in the closure of
G(n,p)

Claim 2.8
.

Fix
\delta>0
. If
p=\omega(n^{-1})
and
G\sim G(n,p)
, then, a.a.s., between every two disjoint vertex sets of sizes at least
\delta n
there is a matching of at least
\delta n/2
edges.

Proof.

A.a.s. there is at least one edge between every two disjoint vertex sets of sizes at least
\delta n/2
. Indeed, the expected number of disjoint vertex sets that violate this condition is at most

\left(2^{n}\right)^{2}\cdot(1-p)^{\delta^{2}n^{2}/4}\leq 4^{n}\cdot e^{-(\delta^{2}/4)\cdot pn^{2}}\to 0,

since
pn^{2}=\omega(n)
. Therefore, we can construct the matching by repeating the following greedy procedure
\delta n/2
times: select an arbitrary edge between the sets and remove its endpoints.
∎

3.
Proofs of the main theorems

3.1.
A well-spread dense closure

The first step in our proofs is to establish that for the values of
d
and
p
that we consider, the
d
-rigidity closure of
G(n,p)
is dense. Since the same argument is applied in our proof for the closures
C_{d,A}
of other linear matroids, we state it in a more general setting. Note that the proof is a direct extension of
[
21
, Lemma 3.1]
, and we include it here for the sake of completeness.

For a set
S
and a number
p\in(0,1)
, denote by
S_{p}
the random subset of
S
containing each element of
S
independently with probability
p
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
p,\varepsilon\in(0,1)
be real numbers and
S
a set of
m
vectors. If
\dim\mathrm{span}(S)\leq D
then

\mathbb{P}(|S\cap\mathrm{span}(S_{p})|<\varepsilon m)\leq\mathbb{P}(X\leq D),

where
X\sim\mbox{Bin}(m,(1-\varepsilon)p)
.

Proof.

We sample
S_{p}
in the following nonstandard
m
-step method. Let
r_{1},...,r_{m}
be independent and uniform in
[0,1]
. Initialize
B_{0}:=\emptyset
. At every step
i\in[m]
, we start by arbitrarily ordering the vectors in
S\setminus B_{i-1}
such that vectors in
S\setminus\mathrm{span}(B_{i-1})
appear first. Afterwards,

(1)

if
r_{i}>p
, set
B_{i}:=B_{i-1}
, and

(2)

otherwise, set
B_{i}:=B_{i-1}\cup\{v_{i}\}
, where
v_{i}
is the
\lceil(r_{i}/p)\cdot|S\setminus B_{i-1}|\rceil
-th vector in the current ordering of
S\setminus B_{i-1}
.

It is clear that the subset
B_{m}
has the same distribution as
S_{p}
. Indeed, its size is Binomial with parameters
m,p
, and in every step in which a new vector is added, it is chosen uniformly at random.

In addition, let
E
denote the event that
|S\cap\mathrm{span}(B_{m})|<\varepsilon m
, and
E_{i}
the event that
r_{i}<(1-\varepsilon)p
. We claim that
E
is contained in the event that at most
D
of the
E_{i}
’s occur. Indeed,
E
implies that

|S\cap\mathrm{span}(B_{i-1})|<\varepsilon m
whence if both
E
and
E_{i}
occur then

\lceil(r_{i}/p)\cdot|S\setminus B_{i-1}|\rceil\leq|S\setminus\mathrm{span}(B_{i-1})|.

Consequently,
v_{i}\notin\mathrm{span}(B_{i-1})
and
\dim\mathrm{span}(B_{i})>\dim\mathrm{span}(B_{i-1})
. Due to the fact that
\dim\mathrm{span}(S)\leq D
, this can occur at most
D
times. Therefore,

\mathbb{P}(E)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{m}\mathbf{1}_{E_{i}}\leq D\right)=\mathbb{P}_{X\sim\mbox{Bin}(m,(1-\varepsilon)p)}(X\leq D).

as claimed.
∎

For example, if
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{dn}
consists of the rows of
R:=R(K_{n},{\bf p})
, then the random variable
|S\cap\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{R}}(S_{p})|
, whose distribution is studied in the Lemma
3.1
, is equal to the size of
C_{d}(G(n,p))
. It turns out that the bound on
|C_{d}(G(n,p))|
given by this lemma is not sufficient for our purposes, and we need to simultaneously apply it on
C_{d,A}(G(n,p))
for all sets
A
that are not too large.

Lemma 3.2
.

Fix
1/2>\varepsilon>0
. Let
p=p(n)\geq\log n/n
,
d=d(n)
satisfying
d\leq(1/2-\varepsilon)np
and
G\sim G(n,p)
. Then, a.a.s.,
|C_{d,A}(G)|\geq\varepsilon n^{2}/15
for every
A\subset[n]
of size
|A|\leq 0.9n
.

Proof.

Let
A\subset[n]
of size
a
. By (
2.1
), we can apply Lemma
3.1
to

S=\left\{P_{W_{A}}\mathbf{r}_{f}~:~f\notin\binom{A}{2}\right\}
,
m=\binom{n}{2}-\binom{a}{2}
and
D=d(n-a)
. In such a case,
|S\cap\mathrm{span}_{\mathbb{R}}(S_{p})|=|C_{d,A}(G)|
and we obtain

\mathbb{P}(|C_{d,A}(G)|<\varepsilon m)\leq\mathbb{P}(X\leq D),

where
X\sim\mathrm{Bin}(m,(1-\varepsilon)p).

A straightforward computation yields that
D\leq(1-\varepsilon/2)\mathbb{E}X
for all sufficiently large
n
. Hence, by Chernoff’s inequality (
2.3
),

\mathbb{P}(X\leq D)\leq\exp(-(\varepsilon/2)^{2}m(1-\varepsilon)p/2).

In addition,
m>n^{2}/15
since
a\leq 0.9n
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(|C_{d,A}(G)|<\varepsilon n^{2}/15)

\displaystyle~\leq\mathbb{P}(|C_{d,A}(G)|<\varepsilon m)

\displaystyle~\leq\exp(-\varepsilon^{2}(1-\varepsilon)n\log n/120).

The proof is concluded by taking the union bound over the (less than
2^{n}
) sets
A
of size
a\leq 0.9n
.
∎

The next step in our argument is to use the fact that the graphs
C_{d,A}(G)
for every
|A|\leq 0.9n
are dense, in order to establish the existence of a large clique in
C_{d}(G)
.

We will need the following folklore fact from graph theory.

Fact 3.3
.

An
a
-vertex
b
-edge graph has an induced subgraph whose minimum degree is at least
b/a.

Lemma 3.4
.

Fix
1/2>\varepsilon>0
. Let
\log n/n\leq p=o(n^{-1/2})
,
d=d(n)
satisfying
d\leq(1/2-\varepsilon)np
and
G\sim G(n,p)
. Then, a.a.s.,
C_{d}(G)
contains a clique with at least
0.9n
vertices.

Proof.

Condition on the a.a.s. events from Lemma
3.2
and Claim
2.8
. Let
A
denote the largest clique in
C_{d}(G)
and suppose, in contradiction, that
|A|<0.9n
. By Lemma
3.2
, there are at least
\varepsilon n^{2}/15
edges in
C_{d,A}(G)
. In addition, since
A
induces a clique in
C_{d}(G)
, these edges also belong to
C_{d}(G)
by Claim
2.5
. We claim that less than
d|A^{c}|
of these edges connect a vertex from
A
to a vertex from
A^{c}
. Indeed, otherwise there exists a vertex
v\in A^{c}
with
d
neighbors from
A
in the graph
C_{d}(G)
whence, by Fact
2.3
,
A\cup\{v\}
also induces a clique in
C_{d}(G)
— contradicting the maximality of
A
.

Therefore,
A^{c}
induces at least
\varepsilon n^{2}/15-o(n^{3/2})
edges in
C_{d}(G)
. By Fact
3.3
, there is a subset of
A^{c}
that induces a subgraph
H
of
C_{d}(G)
with minimum degree

\delta(H)\geq\frac{\varepsilon n^{2}/15-o(n^{3/2})}{|A^{c}|}\geq(1+o(1))\varepsilon n/15.

The graph
H
is also a closed graph since it is an induced subgraph of
C_{d}(G)
. In addition, its minimum degree is larger than
d(d+1)=o(n)
. Therefore, by Lemma
2.2
, there exists a vertex
v\in H
whose neighbor set
B
induces a clique in
H
.

Consequently,
B
is a clique in
C_{d}(G)
that is disjoint from
A
. Moreover, by maximality,
|A|\geq|B|\geq\delta(H)\geq(1+o(1))\varepsilon n/15
. Therefore, since
d=o(n^{1/2})
, by Claim
2.8
there is a matching of size
\binom{d+1}{2}
between
A,B
in
G
. Hence, by Claim
2.4
, the union
A\cup B
also induces a clique in
C_{d}(G)
. This contradicts the maximality of
A
, and we deduce that
|A|\geq 0.9n
as claimed.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

If the probabilistic argument in Lemma
3.1
was applied only to show that
C_{d}(G)
a.a.s. has size
~\Omega(\varepsilon n^{2})
, our reasoning would merely guarantee that it a.a.s. contains a clique of size
O(\sqrt{\varepsilon}n)
. In contrast, Lemma
3.4
establishes the existence of a clique of size
0.9n
, which is independent of
\varepsilon
. This underscores the importance of applying Lemma
3.1
to
C_{d,A}(G)
for
every

A
satisfying
|A|\leq 0.9n
.

3.2.
Proofs of main theorems

In the previous subsection we have established the existence of a large clique in the
d
-rigidity closure of
G(n,p)
. To derive
d
-rigidity we need to show that
C_{d}(G)
is an
n
-vertex clique. We argue that for any large clique
A
in
C_{d}(G)
which does not contain all the vertices, there is a vertex
v\in B:=A^{c}
that is adjacent to at least
d
vertices in
A
— whence
A\cup\{v\}
is an even larger clique in the closure.

To prove this we show that either there is a vertex in
B
with (substantially) more than
d
neighbors in
A
, or — applicable only in the proof of Theorem
1
— there is an isolated vertex
v
in the subgraph of
G
induced by
B
, whence
v
has at least
\delta(G)
neighbors in
A
.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

First, if
o(1)>p>(1+\varepsilon)C_{*}\log n/n
then by Claim
2.7
and the discussion below it there exists
\delta=\delta(\varepsilon)>0
, where
\delta\to 1
as
\varepsilon\to\infty
, such that a.a.s.,
\delta(G)>(1+\delta)np/2
. Hence, since a.a.s.
|E(G)|=(1+o(1))pn^{2}/2
, we have that

|E(G)|<\delta(G)n-\binom{\delta(G)+1}{2},

(3.1)

and
G
does not have enough edges to be
\delta(G)
-rigid. We note that if
\Omega(1)\leq p\leq 1-\omega(n^{-2})
(
3.1
) follows as well by standard arguments.

For the main part of the proof, we start by noting that since
\delta(G)
-rigidity is not a monotone property we cannot assume that
p
is of the form
c\log n/n
for some constant
c>0
, which slightly complicates the technicalities of our argument.

Let
p=p_{n}<(1-\varepsilon)C_{*}\log n/n
. It is well-known that if
(n-1)p\leq\log n
then a.a.s.
\delta(G)
is either
0
or
1
and, in addition,
G
is connected (i.e.,
1
-rigid) if and only if
\delta(G)=1

[
4
]
.

Therefore we may assume that
c=c_{n}:=(n-1)p/\log n\in(1,(1-\varepsilon)C_{*})
. Recall that
a=a_{n}
is the smallest positive root of
\varphi_{c}(t)
defined in (
2.5
). We set the stage by selecting some parameters. Let
1>\beta>\varphi_{0.9}(1/2)\approx 0.894
, and we choose a constant
\delta>0
independent of
n
such that for every
n
,

(i)

a+\delta<c/2
, and

(ii)

\varphi_{c}(a+\delta)<(1-\beta)/4
.

Such a constant
\delta>0
exists since
a
is bounded away from
c/2
in the interval
c\in(1,(1-\varepsilon)C_{*})
.

Claim 3.6
.

The following event occurs a.a.s.: for every vertex subset
B
in
G
of size
1<b\leq 0.1n
, either

(i)

there is a vertex
v\in B
with at least
(a+\delta)\log n
neighbors in
G
outside
B
, or

(ii)

B
induces less than
b/2
edges in
G
.

Proof.

We bound the probability that a set
B
of size
b
violates both conditions. Note that the first condition deals with edges between
B
and its complement, while the second deals with edges within
B
, and therefore they are independent. In addition, for each of the
b
vertices in
B
, the events that each of them violate the first condition are independent. Therefore the probability of the event
E_{B}
that
B
violates both conditions is

\mathbb{P}_{Y\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n-b,p)}(Y<(a+\delta)\log n)^{b}\cdot\mathbb{P}_{Z\sim\mathrm{Bin}\left(\binom{b}{2},p\right)}(Z\geq b/2).

(3.2)

We give different bounds for this probability in two regimes of
b
.

First, if
b\leq n^{\beta}
then
n-b=n(1+o(1))
and by Chernoff’s inequality (
2.2
),

\mathbb{P}(Y<(a+\delta)\log n)\leq n^{\varphi_{c}(a+\delta)-1+o(1)}\leq n^{(1-\beta)/4-1+o(1)}

where the last inequality is by the second assumption on
\delta.
Additionally, we find by Chernoff’s inequality (
2.4
) that

\mathbb{P}(Z\geq b/2)\leq(ebp)^{b/2}<n^{-(1-\beta+o(1))b/2}.

Here the last inequality follows from
b\leq n^{\beta}
and
p=O(\log n/n).

Using these two inequalities and
\binom{n}{b}<n^{b}
we find that

\sum_{B:1\leq|B|\leq n^{\beta}}\mathbb{P}(E_{B})\leq\sum_{b=1}^{n^{\beta}}n^{-(1-\beta+o(1))b/4}\to 0,\quad\mbox{as $n\to\infty$.}

(3.3)

On the other hand, if
n^{\beta}\leq b\leq 0.1n
, we have that
n-b\geq 0.9n
. Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Y<(a+\delta)\log n)

\displaystyle~\leq\mathbb{P}_{Y^{\prime}\sim\mathrm{Bin}(0.9n,p)}(Y^{\prime}<(a+\delta)\log n)

\displaystyle~\leq n^{\varphi_{0.9c}(a+\delta)-1+o(1)}

\displaystyle~\leq n^{\varphi_{0.9}(1/2)-1+o(1)}.

Here the second inequality follows by Chernoff’s inequality and the last transition is derived from our first assumption on
\delta
, monotonicity of
\varphi
:

\varphi_{0.9c}(a+\delta)\leq\varphi_{0.9c}(c/2)\leq\varphi_{0.9}(1/2).

Therefore, using the last inequality and
\binom{n}{b}\leq(en/b)^{b}\leq n^{(1-\beta+o(1))b}
we find

\sum_{B:n^{\beta}\leq|B|\leq 0.1n}\mathbb{P}(E_{B})\leq\sum_{b=n^{\beta}}^{0.1n}n^{(\varphi_{0.9}(1/2)-\beta+o(1))b}\to 0,

(3.4)

as
n\to\infty
by our assumption on
\beta
.

The claim is derived by combining (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) that show that the expected number of sets violating both conditions vanishes.
∎

To conclude the proof, we condition on the following three events that occur a.a.s. and show that they deterministically imply
\delta(G)
-rigidity.

(1)

\delta(G)<(a+\delta/2)\log n
, which occurs a.a.s. by Claim
2.7
,

(2)

C_{d}(G)
contains a clique with
0.9n
vertices where
d=\lceil(1/2-\delta/(2c))np\rceil
, which occurs a.a.s. by Lemma
3.4
, and

(3)

the event in Claim
3.6
.

Our first assumption on
\delta
is that
a+\delta<c/2
. Therefore,

\delta(G)<(a+\delta/2)\log n<(c/2-\delta/2)\log n\leq d

Hence, by the monotonicity of the rigidity closure with respect to dimension, we derive that
C_{\delta(G)}(G)
also contains a clique with at least
0.9n
vertices.

Let
A
be the largest clique in this closure and suppose, in contradiction that
B=A^{c}
is non-empty. If
B
satisfies the first condition in Claim
3.6
, then there is a vertex
v\notin A
with more than
\delta(G)
neighbors from
A
. Hence, by Fact
2.3
,
A\cup\{v\}
is also a clique in
C_{\delta(G)}(G)
which contradicts the maximality of
A
. Otherwise,
B
satisfies the second condition in Claim
3.6
. In such a case, there is a vertex
v
in
B
that is isolated in the subgraph of
G
induced by
B
. Consequently, all its neighbors in

belong to
A
. But since
\delta(G)
is the minimum degree in

, this means that

has at least
\delta(G)
neighbors in

, and we apply Fact

again to contradict the maximality of

.

In conclusion,
C_{\delta(G)}(G)
is a clique and therefore

is
\delta(G)
-rigid, as claimed.
∎

The proof of Theorem
2
is very similar and slightly simpler.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

First, if
d>(1/2+\varepsilon)np
then a.a.s.
G
does not have enough edges to be
d
-rigid. Next, suppose
d<(1/2-\varepsilon)np
. By Lemma
3.4
we have that a.a.s.
C_{d}(G)
contains a clique of
0.9n
vertices. To prove the theorem we claim that a.a.s. for every vertex subset
B
in
G
of size
|B|=b\leq 0.1n
there is a vertex
v\in B
with
d
neighbors outside of
B
. The theorem follows from the claim using Fact
2.3
as in the proof of Theorem
1
, so we proceed directly to the proof of the claim.

Let
Y\sim\mathrm{Bin}(n-b,p)
. By combining Chernoff’s inequality with the bounds
\mathbb{E}[Y]\geq 0.9C_{*}\log n
and
d/\mathbb{E}[Y]<(1/2-\varepsilon)/0.9
, we find that there exists some constant
\beta>0
such that

\mathbb{P}(Y<d)\leq n^{-\beta}.

Therefore,

\sum_{b=n^{1-\beta/2}}^{0.1n}\binom{n}{b}(\mathbb{P}(Y<d))^{b}\leq\sum_{b=n^{1-\beta/2}}^{0.1n}n^{(-\beta/2+o(1))b}\to 0,

(3.5)

as
n\to\infty
. Here we use
\binom{n}{b}\leq(en/b)^{b}\leq n^{(\beta/2+o(1))b}
.

If
1\leq b<n^{1-\beta/2}
, then
\alpha:=d/\mathbb{E}[Y]<1/2-\varepsilon+o(1)
whence by Chernoff’s inequality,

\mathbb{P}(Y<d)\leq\left(\frac{e^{-(1-\alpha)}}{\alpha^{\alpha}}\right)^{np}\leq n^{\varphi_{C_{*}}(\alpha C_{*})-1},

where the last inequality uses
np\geq C_{*}\log n
. Note that
\alpha<1/2
implies that
\varphi_{C_{*}}(\alpha C_{*})<0
since
\varphi_{C_{*}}(C_{*}/2)=0
. Therefore, using
\binom{n}{b}<n^{b}
,

\sum_{b=1}^{n^{1-\beta/2}}\binom{n}{b}(\mathbb{P}(Y<d))^{b}\leq\sum_{b=1}^{n^{1-\beta/2}}n^{(\varphi_{C_{*}}(\alpha C_{*}))b}\to 0

(3.6)

as
n\to\infty
.

The claim is derived by combining (
3.5
) and (
3.6
) that show that the expected number of sets violating the condition in the claim vanishes.
∎

4.
Discussion and open problems

Several open problems and related developments suggest themselves:

(1)

The sharp form of Conjecture
1
remains open for
\Omega(n^{-1/2})\leq p<1
. Our proof for Theorem
2
uses the assumption
p=o(n^{-1/2})
in two critical ways: (i) using Villány’s Lemma
2.2
, which shows that a dense closed graph contains a large clique, and (ii) establishing the existence of a matching of
\binom{d+1}{2}
edges between any two disjoint vertex sets of linear size in
G
. Since the first version of this paper, Krivelevich, Lew and Michaeli
[
19
]
gave a partial answer, up to the multiplicative constant, in the sparse regime
p=o(1)
by proving that for
p\geq 2\log n/n
, the random graph
G(n,p)
is a.a.s.
\lfloor cnp\rfloor
-rigid for some absolute constant
c>0
.

(2)

The dense regime, where
p
is bounded away from
0
and
1
, presents significant challenges. In the first version of this paper, we asked whether
G(n,1/2)
is a.a.s.
(\varepsilon n)
-rigid for some
\varepsilon>0
. This question was answered affirmatively by Jordán, Liu and Villányi
[
12
]
, who proved that
G(n,1/2)
is a.a.s.
d
-rigid for
d\sim 7n/32
. Determining the correct constant remains an interesting open problem.

(3)

In the regime
p<p_{c}
, we are able to find the precise maximum dimension
d=\delta(G)
such that
G
is
d
-rigid. We conjecture, and this is verified by some numerical experiments, that for
p>p_{c}
, a.a.s.,
G
is
d
-rigid if and only if the event
|E(G)|\geq dn-\binom{d+1}{2}
occurs. However, our probabilistic approach (Lemma
3.1
) is meaningful only if the dimension
d
is bounded away from
np/2
, and is therefore too weak for this conjecture.

(4)

Finally, we note that our proof, in its current form, applies to all
1
-extendable abstract rigidity matroids. Do the theorems hold true for
all
abstract rigidity matroids? Note that the results from
[
21
]
hold for all abstract rigidity matroids.
