Artificial intelligence chatbots in response to patient's common inquiries about chordoma: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Shaohui He, Dongjie Jiang, Runyi Jiang, Mengchen Yin, Shangjiang Yu, Xin Jiang, Lei Zhou, Zhenhua Zhou, Haifeng Wei, Jianru Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionArtificial intelligence (AI) chatbots has been well studied in many common diseases. However, little was reported for chordoma, which is a rare disease with high rates of recurrence, disability, and mortality.Research questionThis study aims to assess the performance of oncologists and state-of-the-art AI chatbots in response to frequently-asked questions (FAQs) of chordoma from real world. The response performance was mainly evaluated by overall quality, empathy, and readability.Material and methodsSixty chordoma-related FAQs, collected from social media, were addressed by various chatbots and oncologists, and the best-performing chatbot-generated text was further edited and assessed again. Rated scores were ordered for quality and empathy in a blind way. The readability was measured objectively by calculating Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level (FKGL), Automated Readability Index (ARI), and Gunning-Fog Index (GFI).ResultsAI chatbots were universally superior to oncologists in response quality (3.86 ± 0.14 vs. 3.12 ± 0.25, p < 0.001) and empathy (3.28 ± 0.41 vs. 2.95 ± 0.48, p < 0.001). DeepSeek-R1 achieved highest rated score in response quality (4.20 ± 0.22), while Claude 3.5 Sonnet was considered as the best chatbots by comprehensive assessments. The chatbot drafted responses were easier to understand from patient's perspective (p < 0.001). Improved response quality (4.09 ± 0.12, p < 0.001), empathy (4.00 ± 0.39, p < 0.001), and readability (FKGL: 11.30 ± 2.42, p < 0.001) were obtained after editing the Claude-3.5-generated responses by oncologists.Discussion and conclusionsAI chatbots reached favorable quality and empathetic performance in response to chordoma-related FAQs, and generated equivalent readability compared to oncologists. With generative chatbot's assistance, oncologists may response more comprehensively and efficiently in addressing chordoma patient's common inquiries.Keywords: Chordoma, Artificial intelligence, Large language model, AI assistance, Physician-patient communication

Introduction

Chordoma is a rare malignant bone tumor which originates from the notochord remnants during embryonic development. It is associated with limited therapeutic modalities and high recurrence rate (Stacchiotti et al., 2017). Unlike other prevalent cancers, even most professional oncologists may be unfamiliar with chordoma unless the dedicated specialists in neuro-/spine surgery or radiotherapy manage it. Therefore, many patients cannot get the latest knowledge of chordoma promptly and fail to obtain standardized treatments at the initial visits. On the contrary, patient's conception and understanding is typically significant in treating chordoma properly.

Despite the progress of internet resource in providing the primary healthcare information (Swire-Thompson and Lazer, 2020), patients are often misled by the wrong and biased instructions (Koch-Weser et al., 2010). The emergence of artificial intelligence (AI), especially the generative chatbots based on the large language models (LLMs), has revolutionized the digital health field. The AI chatbots mimic clinicians to generate accurate and comprehensive information to the public in a very fast and user-friendly way (Ayers et al., 2023; Chen et al., 2024; Scaff et al., 2024). Moreover, AI chatbots are typically advantageous in managing rare diseases due to the limited clinical cases (Banerjee et al., 2023). To the best knowledge, the performance of AI chatbots in managing chordoma has not been thoroughly investigated. Therefore, we applied the state-of-the-art chatbots in responding the frequently-asked questions (FAQs) of chordoma from patient's perspective. The response quality, empathy, readability, and cognitive load were evaluated by comparing between chatbots and oncologists, and further effects were also assessed by editing the chatbot-generated text.

Methods

Inquiry setting

This cross-sectional study was approved by the institutional review board (IRB) of Changzheng Hospital, and reported according to the statement of the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (Skrivankova et al., 2021; von Elm et al., 2007). The chordoma inquiry were generated as follows: FAQs were preliminarily collected from chordoma patients in the real world, and further developed by incorporating items of Chordoma Foundation, the European Society for Medical Oncology, the National Comprehensive Cancer Network, and other well-known institutions (Mayo Clinic and Cleveland Clinic). Finally, the inquiry (Table S1), containing 60 FAQs, was modified by piloting in a certain group of chordoma representatives (N = 25, treated during January to June in 2024) (Table S2) and their family members. They all agreed that the final version of inquiry was tightly connected with their concerns. Written informed consents were waived by IRB due to observational design and no impact on the patient's outcomes.

Participants

Five LLMs were applied in response to the chordoma inquiry: ChatGPT-4o (OpenAI, updated on August 8th, 2024), ChatGPT-o1 preview (OpenAI, updated on November 20th, 2024), Claude-3.5 Sonnet (Anthropic, updated on October 22nd, 2024), Gemini-2.0 flash exp (Google, updated on December 11th, 2024), DeepSeek-R1 (DeepSeekAI, updated on January 20th, 2025). The 60 questions were loaded to each LLM one by one, and all chatbots’ responses were recorded on February 10th, 2025. Meanwhile, the junior (RYJ, SJY, and LZ, residents with 3-year experience) and senior (DJJ, MCY, ZHZ, specialists with over 10-year experience) oncologists specialized in neuro-oncology were invited to answer the inquiry independently. They were not allowed to use any generative AI chatbot to assist responses. In addition, the senior oncologist (DJJ) was further asked to edit the text generated by the best-performing chatbot to investigate the effect of AI chatbots in assisting clinicians in the clinical practice. All responses should be written in English, and were required not to exceed 150 words (this requirement was typed into the dialog box before AI generated any response).

Performance metrics

The primary performance metrics were quality, empathy, and readability. The overall quality was evaluated through “5C” components: Correctness, Coherence, Connection, Clarity, Completeness. In terms of empathy, the responses to FAQs with first-person perspective (No.15-16, 18-21, 23-28, 31, 34, 36-37, 40, 42-49, 52-60) were assessed based on the following three components: Emotional resonance, Perspective-taking, Empathetic expression. The Flesch-Kincaid Grade Level (FKGL), Automated Readability Index (ARI), and Gunning-Fog Index (GFI) were introduced to quantify the readability in an objective manner (Uzunçıbuk et al., 2024). Notably, for quality and empathy, a rated score ranging from 1 to 5 was applied for each category, representing terrible, bad, acceptable, good, and excellent, respectively (Table S3). The rated score was evaluated by three senior independent researchers (SHH, HFW, and JRX) who had at-least ten-year experience in managing chordoma. As previously reported (Ayers et al., 2023), the evaluators were provided with original questions and related responses in a blind way, which meant the evaluator did not know who (human or chatbot) proposed the answers. The cognitive load was further evaluated for comprehension via calculating mean dependency distance (MMD) and lexical diversity (LD) (Chen et al., 2024). Word count was also recorded for each LLM and oncologist. The definitions of each component above were described in details in Table S4.

Since the questions had no definitive standard answers, the rating procedures were mainly referring to the National Comprehensive Cancer Network clinical practice guidelines (Bone Cancer Version 1.2025 and Soft Tissue Sarcoma Version 4.2024, available at https://www.nccn.org/), global consensus (Combs et al., 2021; Stacchiotti et al., 2017), as well as information from the Chordoma Foundation (access at http://www. chordomafoundation.org). Besides, in order to assess the readability and cognitive load from patient's perspective, five independent chordoma patients were invited to evaluate the responses of oncologists or chatbots in a blind way. They were asked to make a rated score from 1 to 5 (very easy, easy, acceptable, hard, very hard) to clarify the degree of understandability.

Statistical analysis

The quantitative data was described as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and the categorical data was reported as count (percentage). The 95% confidence interval (CI) was also reported when appropriate. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA, two-tailed) was performed to test the differences among over three groups, and the Tukey's honest significant difference was further introduced to detect the variation between any two groups. Two-paired t-test was conducted for comparison between only two groups. The correlation between quality and empathy was further investigated by Pearson correlation coefficient. The significance cutoff was set as p < 0.05 in this study. Statistical analysis was performed in Python Software (Version 3.9.12) and figures were generated using GraphPad Prism Software (Version 8.0.2).

Results

For the response quality, each of the chatbots was superior to either junior or senior oncologists in both pooled performance (p < 0.001, Fig. 1A) and each component (Fig. S1A–E). Quantitatively, the mean rated score of chatbots was higher than oncologists by over 23.8% (3.86 ± 0.14 [95%CI: 3.82-3.90] vs. 3.12 ± 0.25 [95%CI: 3.06-3.18]) (p < 0.001) (Fig. 1B). Among five chatbots, the DeepSeek-R1 achieved highest performance score in both pooled response quality (4.20 ± 0.22 [95%CI: 4.15-4.26]) and each subcategory (Fig. S1A–E) (4.27 ± 0.41 [95%CI: 4.16-4.37] for Correctness, 4.11 ± 0.25 [95%CI: 4.04-4.17] for Coherence, 4.16 ± 0.29 [95%CI: 4.09-4.24] for Connection, 4.15 ± 0.40 [95%CI: 4.05-4.25] for Clarity, and 4.33 ± 0.40 [95%CI: 4.22-4.43] for Completeness). With regard to empathy, chatbots had a prevalent better performance in overall rated scores than the junior oncologist, while GPT-4o and DeepSeek-V1 were inferior to the superior oncologist (Fig. 2A). The mean rated scores of chatbots was higher than human raters by over 11.3% (3.28 ± 0.41 [95%CI: 3.14-3.43] vs. 2.95 ± 0.48 [95%CI: 2.78-3.12]) (p < 0.001) (Fig. 2B). Notably, the best-performing chatbot was Claude-3.5 Sonnet, with 3.68 ± 0.44 (95%CI: 3.52-3.83) for overall empathy, 3.21 ± 0.53 (95%CI: 3.02-3.39) for Emotional resonance, 3.45 ± 0.51 (95%CI: 3.28-3.63) for Perspective-taking, and 4.37 ± 0.48 (95%CI: 4.20-4.54) for Empathetic expression (Fig. S2A–C).

Measurements of response quality from oncologists and five representative AI chatbots. A: Rated scores from each response; B: comparison of mean rated scores between oncologists and chatbots.

Performance metrics of empathetic scoring from oncologists and five representative AI chatbots. A: Correctness; B: Coherence; C: Connection; D: Clarity; E: Completeness.

For readability, the objective calculations revealed similar level in reading difficulty between oncologists and chatbots (Fig. 3A–F), both reaching the college level. Specifically, the average FKGL scores of oncologists and chatbots' responses were 12.87 ± 1.91 (95%CI: 12.38-13.36) and 12.59 ± 1.83 (95%CI: 12.12-13.06) (p = 0.228), respectively. Likewise, the human raters and chatbots achieved close ARI (11.88 ± 2.31 [95%CI: 11.28-12.48] vs. 12.18 ± 1.86 [95%CI: 11.70-12.26], p = 0.291) and GFI (17.45 ± 2.35 [95%CI: 16.85-18.06] vs. 17.10 ± 2.31 [95%CI: 16.50-17.69], p = 0.213) scores, respectively. Intriguingly, from the patient's perspective, the responses generated by chatbots tended to be easier to understand comparing to those by oncologists (3.87 ± 0.18 [95%CI: 3.82-3.91] vs. 2.92 ± 0.26 [95%CI: 2.85-2.99], p < 0.001) (Fig. 3G–H). Among chatbots, the Claude-3.5 Sonnet (4.14 ± 0.43 [95%CI: 4.03-4.25]), Gemini-2.0-flash-exp (4.18 ± 0.32 [95%CI: 4.09-4.26]), and DeepSeek-R1 (4.13 ± 0.39 [95%CI: 4.03-4.23]) generated responses seemed to be equally easy to understand from the notions of chordoma patients.

Calculations of readability from oncologists and five representative AI chatbots. A-B: FKGL; C-D: ARI; E-F: GFI; G-H: rated scores of readability from patient's perspective.

For the cognitive load analysis, chatbots were superior to oncologists by calculating MDDS (3.12 ± 0.022 [95%CI: 3.06-3.18] vs. 3.39 ± 0.62 [95%CI: 3.23-3.55], p = 0.001) (Fig. 4A–B), but this superiority was not found in LDS (0.675 ± 0.039 [95%CI: 0.665-0.685] vs. 0.673 ± 0.048 [95%CI: 0.660-0.685], p = 0.768) (Fig. 4C–D). Additionally, the mean word count of chatbot's responses was far more than that of oncologist's responses (116.0 ± 8.5 [95%CI: 113.8-118.2] vs. 88.8 ± 22.0 [95%CI: 83.1-94.0], p < 0.001) (Fig. 5A–B), and Claude-3.5 Sonnet generated most words (129.9 ± 13.4 [95%CI: 126.4-133.3]) among chatbots. The word count appeared to be positively correlated with response quality, but the Pearson correlation analysis indicated no statistical significance (Fig. S3, p = 0.068).

Calculations of cognitive load scores from oncologists and five representative AI chatbots. A-B: MDDS; C-D: LDS.

Word counts of responses from oncologists and five representative AI chatbots.

Furthermore, considering the performance metrics comprehensively, Claude-3.5 Sonnet was regarded as the best-performing LLM in responding FAQs from chordoma patients. As shown in Fig. 6A, editing based on the best-performing chatbot-generated text (4.09 ± 0.12 [95%CI: 4.05-4.12]) enabled oncologist to improve the response quality compared with either human-only (3.35 ± 0.36 [95%CI: 3.26-3.44]) (p < 0.001) or chatbot (4.00 ± 0.19 [95%CI: 3.96-4.05]) (p < 0.001), respectively. This improvement seemed to be more significant in empathy (Fig. 6B), since the overall empathetic score (4.00 ± 0.39 [95%CI: 3.87-4.14]) increased over 26.8% (vs. oncologist-only, p < 0.001) and 8.8% (vs. AI-only, p < 0.001), respectively. For readability, compared to of single chatbot-generated text, the mean FKGL (11.30 ± 2.42 [95%CI: 10.67-11.92]), ARI (10.30 ± 2.63 [95%CI: 9.62-10.99]), and GFI (15.75 ± 2.77 [95%CI: 15.04-16.47]) decreased with statistical significance (all p < 0.001) after manual modification by oncologist (Fig. 6C), thus lowering the reading difficulty and increasing the understandability for chordoma patients. Notably, decreased cognitive load was found after editing based on AI generation by calculating MDDS but not LDS (Fig. S4A). Lastly, no statistical significance was found on the word count of AI-based modification (126.2 ± 45.7 [95%CI: 114.4-137.9]) towards human-only (111.9 ± 32.7 [95%CI: 103.5-120.3], p = 0.067) or chatbot-generated version (129.9 ± 13.4 [95%CI: 126.4-133.3], p = 0.784) (Fig. S4B).

Discussion

Chordoma, as a representative of rare malignant diseases, requires specialists with abundant experience to manage, and this phenomenon is usually restricted to a certain small group of oncologists globally. Our cross-sectional study has demonstrated the AI generative chatbots has distinct advantages in responding the common issues of chordoma patient's inquiry.

To our knowledge, it is easily accessible for patients with the well-studied common diseases (e.g. lower back pain (Scaff et al., 2024), digestive diseases (El-Sayed et al., 2025), or cardiovascular diseases (Meder et al., 2025)) to get prompt guidance and support either from local hospital or internet. However, rare diseases, such as chordoma, are more likely delayed in the diagnosis and treatment due to lack of universal cognition and requirement of specialized knowledge. Therefore, the AI chatbots may be more fit to these conditions (Nógrádi et al., 2024; Rider et al., 2025; Venus et al., 2025). In our study, the 60 FAQs were drafted and modified by collecting the concerns from twenty-five typical chordoma patients (illustrated in Table S2) in the real world, which could reflect typical patient-physician questions in the clinical practice.

The chatbots based on various state-of-art LLMs were prevalently superior to healthcare providers in “response quality” (measured by “5C” rules) towards chordoma-related FAQs. Briefly, the average rated score was up to over 4 of 5 for both Claude-3.5 Sonnet and DeepSeek-R1 (shown in Fig. 1A). Moreover, these two LLMs were totally free with open access. This meant they could be used as a promising and economical counselling tool for chordoma patients and their families. By using these instant messaging tools, at-least part of patients could get high-quality response immediately without expense or unnecessary visits to specialized institutions (Rasu et al., 2015). From our perspective, this approach was not only particularly suitable for chordoma, but also beneficial to all kinds of rare diseases that required to be managed by experienced specialists.

Compared with human raters, the chatbots obtained higher empathetic score (shown in Fig. 2) with similar readability (shown in Fig. 3A–F) and cognitive load (shown in Fig. 4A–D). Small WR and his colleagues reported the generative AI was able to communicate better with more empathy but less readability than healthcare provider (Small et al., 2024). However, the real-world chordoma patients in our study were simultaneously invited to evaluate the easiness of understandability, and the assessment indicated the AI generated responses were more readable from patient's point (shown in Fig. 3G–H). As a fact, the high-quality instant response with considerable empathy might help patients build trust and credibility (Abbasian et al., 2024), enhance their compliance and improve outcomes (Ayers et al., 2023; Inkster et al., 2018).

Notably, our study further reported the secondary editing of AI-written messages by oncologists not only improved the response quality, empathy and readability for patients (shown in Fig. 6), but also was beneficial to clinicians themselves. The assistance of chatbots help clinicians greatly alleviate the daily workload and respond with higher quality, which could result in more efficient medical resource resignment (Barak-Corren et al., 2024; Hao et al., 2025). Meanwhile, the junior clinicians might also achieve tutoring and progress in the process of reviewing AI-generated responses (Heidt, 2025; Yan et al., 2024). In addition, even though the correlation analysis did not reveal positive connection of word count with response quality (shown in Figure S3), we believed the AI-generated detailedness with more words might help patients understand comprehensively, and it also provided enough information for clinicians to revise in the secondary editing.

However, what requires vigilance is that AI hallucination is quite prevalent in daily life (Ji et al., 2023). Actually, generative AI tools have shown hallucination rates from 20% to 30% or more in generating drafts for clinical notes from patient encounters (Palm et al., 2025). Therefore, considering these potential errors, users, especially for patients largely relying on AI for critical information, must remain vigilant and verify critical responses against trusted sources. Although typical AI hallucinations were not captured in this work, it is definitely useful for the future study to shorten unnecessary attachments and omit some paragraphs in the questionnaire.

Limitations

Our study had some limitations. Firstly, the list of FAQs was generated by referring to the concerns raised by a small cohort containing twenty-five chordoma patients. It was somehow representative, but a multi-center cohort with larger sample was warranted to validate the real desire of chordoma patients. The chatbots did not incorporate comprehensive information (e.g. age, sex, medical history, blood tests, and radiological findings, etc), which failed to precisely provide customized counsels. Secondly, even though three independent experienced oncologists were asked to rate the performance of response quality and empathy, some assessments were inevitably subjective. Meanwhile, we did not collect the empathetic evaluation of chordoma patients, which might reflect the true feelings of the responses. Additionally, the generasibility of AI chatbots written in non-English also required further investigation. Last but not the least, the state-of-art AI chatbots had power of self-renewal by self-learning the latest information. This meant AI could also obtain incorrect information which might pose harmful impact on patient's outcomes during counselling. The potential risk might also put clinicians against medical ethics, and should be managed properly before broadly being applied in the clinical practice.

Conclusions

This cross-sectional study has demonstrated that AI generative chatbots have prevalent superiority than healthcare professionals in chordoma patient counselling. The AI assistance also enables clinicians to work more efficiently with improved response quality, empathy and readability. Rigorous research with large global cohort and multimodal data is warranted to standardize and facilitate the application of generative AI in the clinical settings.
