Comparative Effectiveness and Safety of Tofacitinib, Filgotinib, and Upadacitinib in Ulcerative Colitis: A Multicenter Real-World Cohort Study

ABSTRACT
IntroductionTofacitinib, filgotinib, and upadacitinib are Janus kinase inhibitors (JAKis) available for ulcerative colitis (UC) refractory or intolerant to at least one advanced therapy; their comparative effectiveness in clinical practice remains uncertain.MethodsWe conducted a multicenter retrospective study including adult UC patients initiating tofacitinib, filgotinib, or upadacitinib (September 2020–June 2025). Clinical remission (partial Mayo score ≤ 1), steroid-free clinical remission, biochemical remission, and endoscopic remission (Mayo endoscopic score = 0) were assessed at predefined time-points. Baseline differences were controlled using inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW), and time-to-event and longitudinal analyses were performed.ResultsAfter IPTW, the pseudo-population included 627 patients (tofacitinib = 179, filgotinib = 138, upadacitinib = 310). In the weighted cohort, clinical remission probabilities at weeks 8, 24, and 52 were 17.5%, 40.7%, and 61.3% with upadacitinib, 11.8%, 33.8%, and 54.6% with tofacitinib, and 6.7%, 29.0%, and 52.0% with filgotinib, respectively; p < 0.001 at weeks 8 and 24; p = 0.040 at week 52. Steroid-free clinical remission showed a similar gradient (14.2%, 39.2%, and 59.8% with upadacitinib; 9.2%, 31.2%, and 50.8% with tofacitinib; 4.4%, 27.6%, and 49.8% with filgotinib, respectively; all p < 0.001. Biochemical remission was also achieved more frequently with upadacitinib at weeks 8 and 16, although differences between treatments were no longer significant during maintenance. At week 52, endoscopic remission was 18.8% with tofacitinib, 36.6% with upadacitinib, and 19.5% with filgotinib (p = 0.039). Overall adverse events, particularly infections and herpes zoster, were more frequent with tofacitinib, whereas filgotinib had fewer non-serious events; serious adverse events and colectomy risk were uncommon and similar across groups.ConclusionsAll three JAKis were effective, but upadacitinib yielded the highest remission rates, while tofacitinib and filgotinib differed mainly in safety, supporting individualized drug positioning according to efficacy needs and patient-specific risks.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12325-026-03622-3.

Introduction

Ulcerative colitis (UC) is a chronic inflammatory disease affecting the colonic mucosa. No single specific trigger has been identified for its occurrence, which is the result of the interplay between genetic and environmental factors [1]. This disease presents a typically relapsing–remitting course, often causing complications, and an aggressive therapeutic approach is therefore required [2].

The revolution of the management of UC has started with the advent of biologics, in particular tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) inhibitors. Unfortunately, several patients either do not respond or lose their response over time, sometimes also developing intolerance to these drugs [3–5]. As a consequence, in recent years, novel therapeutic agents targeting alternative pathogenetic inflammatory pathways have been developed and approved for the treatment of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) [6].

In the landscape of IBD inflammation, the Janus kinase family of cytoplasmic tyrosine kinases (TYKs) plays a pivotal role in cytokine signaling and immune regulation [7]. Janus kinase (JAK) inhibitors, small-molecule drugs that target these molecules, have therefore been developed. At present, three JAK inhibitors—tofacitinib, filgotinib, and upadacitinib—have been approved for UC, each with distinct JAK selectivity [8], since all of them have been demonstrated to be significantly more effective than placebo in inducing and maintaining remission in patients with UC, including those with prior anti-TNF failure [9–11].

A key issue of the clinical practice is now how best to position the three JAK inhibitors. Tofacitinib, a preferential inhibitor of JAK1 and JAK3, was the first JAK inhibitor approved in Italy for the treatment of UC refractory to other therapies [12]. Filgotinib, a preferential JAK1 inhibitor, and upadacitinib, a predominantly JAK1 inhibitor with additional, less pronounced activity on JAK2 and JAK3, have been approved more recently in Italy for the treatment of UC in patients who have failed first-line anti-TNF-α therapy agents [13, 14]. In our experience, all three agents have proven effective in the treatment of UC [15–17].

Although observational studies using matched cohorts [18, 19] and meta-analyses [20, 21] have compared the efficacy and safety of JAK inhibitors, comparative real-world data with adequate sample sizes evaluating all three agents remain limited to Asian countries [22]. The current multicenter study aimed to analyze the real-world effectiveness of the three JAK inhibitors currently available in Italy for the treatment of UC, using an inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) approach.

Methods

This was a multicenter retrospective observational study including adult patients with UC who initiated tofacitinib, filgotinib, or upadacitinib between 1 September 2020 and 30 June 2025.

We included adults with a confirmed diagnosis of UC who were non-responders, intolerant, or had contraindications to anti-TNF-α therapy and were prescribed tofacitinib, filgotinib, or upadacitinib according to current guidelines. Patients were excluded in case of uncertain UC diagnosis or competing diagnoses (e.g., ischemic, infectious, radiation, microscopic or unclassified colitis, suspected Crohn’s disease), fulminant or acute severe UC, toxic megacolon, major colonic surgery, cancer within 6 months before baseline, or insufficient baseline data to classify key covariates.

From medical records, we extracted anonymized information on demographics (birth-assigned sex, age, smoking status), disease characteristics (age at UC diagnosis, disease duration, disease extent when available, history of appendicectomy), previous and concomitant therapies (conventional immunosuppressants, previous JAK inhibitors, anti-TNF agents, vedolizumab, ustekinumab, other advanced therapies; concomitant corticosteroids and thiopurines), and disease activity. Clinical activity was assessed by the partial Mayo score (PMS) at baseline and at weeks 8, 16, 24, 36, 48, and 52. Biochemical data included fecal calprotectin, C-reactive protein (CRP), erythrocyte sedimentation rate, serum lipids (including LDL-cholesterol), and liver enzymes, collected at baseline and during follow-up in accordance with clinical practice. Endoscopic findings at baseline and around week 52 were recorded when available.

For a subgroup of patients, daily patient-reported outcomes for diarrhea, abdominal pain, and bowel urgency were available from electronic diaries or structured forms and used for symptom-based time-to-event analyses. Safety data [occurrence, timing and type of adverse events (AEs) and serious adverse events (SAEs)] and dates of colectomy were collected throughout follow-up. Maintenance dose of tofacitinib, filgotinib, and upadacitinib and subsequent dose adjustments were also recorded.

Data were fully anonymized, with no possibility of re-identifying individual patients. The study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and the determination of the Italian Medicines Agency (20 March 2008). Given its retrospective, non-interventional, and anonymized design, formal ethics committee approval and informed consent were not required according to the AIFA (Agenzia Italiana del Farmaco) Guidelines. Reporting followed STROBE recommendations.

The primary objective was to compare the effectiveness of tofacitinib, filgotinib, and upadacitinib in inducing and maintaining clinical (PMS ≤ 1) and steroid-free clinical remission in an inverse probability of treatment weighted (IPTW) pseudo-population, in line with treat-to-target principles and STRIDE-II recommendations [23]. Steroid-free clinical remission was defined as clinical remission in the absence of any corticosteroid treatment, including systemic corticosteroids, budesonide MMX, and topical steroids.

Secondary effectiveness endpoints included the IPTW-adjusted probabilities of biochemical remission, defined as fecal calprotectin < 250 μg/g and CRP < 5 mg/L, and endoscopic remission, defined as a Mayo endoscopic score of 0, which were selected to reflect objective treatment targets recommended by STRIDE-II [23].

Safety outcomes included the proportion of patients with at least one AE or SAE, AE and SAE incidence rates per 100 patient-years, colectomy rates, and colectomy-free, AE-free, and SAE-free survival over 52 weeks. Maintenance-dose subanalyses assessed time to clinical remission by maintenance dose for each drug (i.e., tofacitinib 5 vs. 10 mg BID; filgotinib 100 vs. 200 mg; upadacitinib 15 vs. 30 vs. 45 mg). For tofacitinib, dose categories were defined based on exposure during the maintenance follow-up: the 10-mg BID stratum included patients receiving 10 mg BID at any time during maintenance (e.g., continuation of 10 mg BID or dose escalation/re-escalation from 5 mg BID). For upadacitinib, the 45-mg category was handled as an exposure-defined exploratory stratum, capturing patients who remained on 45 mg beyond induction or received re-induction and were on 45 mg during the maintenance follow-up. This subgroup likely represents patients with more refractory disease and was therefore not intended to support causal inference on dose-specific maintenance effectiveness.

Statistical analysis

The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to assess whether variables were normally distributed. Baseline characteristics were described in the unweighted cohort as mean ± standard deviation for continuous variables and as counts (percentages) for categorical variables. Between-group comparisons used one-way ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis tests for continuous variables and χ2 or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables.

To address confounding by indication, we used IPTW based on a multinomial propensity score model including the following baseline covariates according to standardized mean difference and clinical plausibility: age at inclusion, age at UC diagnosis, history of appendicectomy, previous exposure to JAK inhibitors, TNF inhibitors, vedolizumab, ustekinumab, concomitant corticosteroids, baseline partial Mayo score, endoscopic Mayo score, fecal calprotectin. Stabilized weights were applied to obtain an IPTW pseudo-population, and extreme weights were trimmed at the 1 st and 99th percentiles to reduce the influence of highly weighted observations. Covariate balance was assessed using standardized mean differences, aiming for values < 0.1. The IPTW-adjusted cohort was used for all primary and main secondary effectiveness analyses, adopting a complete-case approach for each outcome. As a sensitivity analysis, patients who discontinued treatment were considered non-remitters at all subsequent follow-up time points, and remission probabilities were recalculated accordingly.

Time-to-event outcomes were analyzed using Kaplan–Meier survival curves and Cox proportional hazards models with time-censoring (interval-censored Cox models for events occurring between visits and right-censored at the last available follow-up within 52 weeks for patients without events). Hazard ratios with 95% confidence intervals were estimated, and survival curves were compared using log-rank tests.

All analyses were performed using STATA/BE 18 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

In the unweighted cohort, 270/633 patients (42.7%) were female and 363/633 (57.3%) were male. Female patients accounted for 51.1% of the filgotinib group, 42.5% of the tofacitinib group, and 39.0% of the upadacitinib group, whereas male patients accounted for 48.9%, 57.5%, and 61.0%, respectively (Table 1). Mean age at study inclusion was 47.9 ± 15.4, 46.1 ± 14.1, and 43.1 ± 13.7 years in the filgotinib, tofacitinib, and upadacitinib groups, respectively (p = 0.002). Overall, significant between-group differences at baseline were observed for age at diagnosis (p < 0.001), history of appendicectomy (p = 0.036), previous exposure to JAK inhibitors (p = 0.011), TNF inhibitors (p = 0.017), vedolizumab (p = 0.001) and ustekinumab (p = 0.007), concomitant corticosteroid (p = 0.015) and thiopurine use (p = 0.019), partial Mayo score (p < 0.001), fecal calprotectin (p = 0.037), and LDL-cholesterol (p = 0.001).

After IPTW adjustment, the pseudo-population included 627 patients (filgotinib n = 138, tofacitinib n = 179, upadacitinib n = 310), and no statistically significant differences in baseline characteristics were observed between treatment groups (Table 2; Fig. 1). After IPTW, 267/627 patients (42.6%) were female and 360/627 (57.4%) were male; sex distribution was acceptably balanced across treatment groups (female: 50.9%, 42.5%, and 39.0%; male: 49.1%, 57.5%, and 61.0% in the filgotinib, tofacitinib, and upadacitinib groups, respectively; p = 0.124; SMD = 0.094) (Table 2). In the IPTW-adjusted cohort of 627 patients (Fig. 1) treated with upadacitinib (n = 310), tofacitinib (n = 179), or filgotinib (n = 138), the estimated probabilities of remission increased over time for all outcomes (Table 3). Time-to-Symptom Resolution Sub-analyses

Symptom-based time-to-event sub-analyses were conducted in patients with available daily patient-reported outcomes for diarrhea, abdominal pain, and bowel urgency.

The diarrhea sub-analysis included 590 patients (tofacitinib n = 164, upadacitinib n = 289, filgotinib n = 137). Using upadacitinib as reference, the hazard ratio (HR) for diarrhea resolution was 0.78 (95% CI 0.61–1.03, p = 0.076) for tofacitinib and 0.82 (95% CI 0.61–1.08, p = 0.164) for filgotinib (Supplementary Fig. S2).

The abdominal pain sub-analysis comprised 586 patients (tofacitinib n = 163, upadacitinib n = 290, filgotinib n = 133). With upadacitinib as reference, the HR for pain resolution was 0.79 (95% CI 0.60–1.04, p = 0.089) for tofacitinib and 1.01 (95% CI 0.78–1.31, p = 0.942) for filgotinib (Supplementary Fig. S3).

In the bowel urgency sub-analysis, 576 patients were included (tofacitinib n = 168, upadacitinib n = 276, filgotinib n = 132). Taking tofacitinib as the reference, the HR for urgency resolution was 1.48 (95% CI, 1.13–1.93, p = 0.004) for upadacitinib and 1.63 (95% CI, 1.17–2.25, p = 0.004) for filgotinib (Supplementary Fig. S4).

Maintenance-Dose Subanalyses on Clinical Remission

Clinical remission according to maintenance dose was evaluated in three separate sub-analyses (Supplementary Fig. S5).

Among patients treated with tofacitinib, 96 received 5 mg BID, and 85 received 10 mg BID. The probability of clinical remission was higher with 10 mg BID throughout follow-up, and time-to-remission analysis showed an increased likelihood of remission with 10 mg BID versus 5 mg BID (HR 1.85, 95% CI 1.22–2.77; p = 0.005).

In the filgotinib cohort, 6 patients received 100 mg for concomitant extra-intestinal manifestations and 132 received 200 mg. Clinical remission curves for the two doses overlapped, with no significant difference in time to remission (HR 1.12, 95% CI 0.22–5.88; p = 0.896 for 200 mg vs. 100 mg).

In the upadacitinib cohort, 159, 40, and 111 patients received 15 mg, 30 mg, and 45 mg, respectively. Using 15 mg as the reference, the HR for clinical remission was 1.05 (95% CI, 0.62–1.81; p = 0.843) with 30 mg and 2.32 (95% CI, 1.70–3.12; p = 0.010) with 45 mg.

Discussion

The therapeutic landscape of UC has expanded significantly with the introduction of JAK inhibitors, offering effective oral options for patients with moderate-to-severe disease. While randomized clinical trials have robustly established the efficacy of tofacitinib, filgotinib, and upadacitinib versus placebo [9–11], direct head-to-head comparisons in real-world settings remain limited. As the number of available molecules increases, the crucial challenge for clinicians shifts to the optimal positioning of these drugs. The primary aim of this multicenter retrospective study was to compare the real-world effectiveness and safety of the three currently approved JAK inhibitors in Italy, using inverse probability of treatment weight to mitigate selection bias and provide evidence to support clinical decision-making.

First, our study highlights that these drugs exhibit different effectiveness profiles in a real-world setting. In the IPTW-adjusted cohort, upadacitinib was associated with higher rates of inducing clinical remission at week 8 and of maintaining it through weeks 24 and 48 compared with both tofacitinib and filgotinib. Specifically, the probability of achieving steroid-free clinical remission was significantly higher for upadacitinib at all time points. This finding in both the induction and maintenance phases is consistent with the results from recent network meta-analyses, which have consistently ranked upadacitinib as the most efficacious agent for induction in bio-exposed patients [20, 21, 24]. Focusing on real-world data, a recent propensity-score matched study from Asia likewise found that upadacitinib led to significantly greater steroid-free remission than tofacitinib or filgotinib, particularly in patients who had failed anti-TNF therapy [25]. Another multicenter cohort compared upadacitinib and filgotinib and reported superior short-term efficacy with upadacitinib (8-week clinical remission ~ 66% vs. ~ 47% with filgotinib) [26]. Our data aligns with these results and extend them by including all three agents in one analysis. Furthermore, our maintenance dose sub-analysis supports the concept of a dose-dependent response, particularly for upadacitinib and tofacitinib. These findings may support dose optimization or maintenance of the induction dose to achieve better real-world outcomes in difficult-to-treat patients, a strategy recently supported by real-world series suggesting a benefit of high-dose maintenance in patients with aggressive disease [27, 28].

From a pharmacologic standpoint, the observed differences may be related, at least in part, to its higher cellular potency and preferential selectivity for JAK1 compared with tofacitinib, with relative sparing of JAK2/3 at clinically used doses. This could result in a more complete and sustained inhibition of JAK1-dependent cytokines while limiting off-target hematopoietic toxicity, enabling dosing that achieves robust target coverage in difficult-to-treat patients. Parmentier et al. showed that upadacitinib has greater JAK1 potency than tofacitinib and less inhibition of JAK2-mediated erythropoietin signaling at equipotent exposures [29]. These findings are biologically plausible, as preclinical data have shown that approved JAK inhibitors exhibit distinct in vitro patterns of JAK isoform inhibition. In particular, Dowty et al. characterized the JAK1, JAK2, JAK3, and TYK2 inhibition profiles of tofacitinib, baricitinib, upadacitinib and filgotinib, reporting distinct IC₅₀ patterns across JAK isoforms. However, at clinically effective doses, the overall cytokine receptor inhibition profiles were largely similar [30]. Filgotinib is also JAK1-preferential, but clinical pharmacology studies indicate lower overall potency and distinct exposure profiles for the parent drug and its active metabolite, which may translate into less complete JAK1 pathway blockade at approved doses [31]. JAK1 mediates signaling of multiple cytokine families implicated in UC pathogenesis (IL-6/gp130, common γ-chain cytokines, IL-10 family, interferons), so deeper JAK1 inhibition is mechanistically consistent with the higher remission rates observed with upadacitinib in bio-exposed patients [32, 33].

Beyond efficacy, the rapid action of JAK inhibitors is a key advantage. Tofacitinib has demonstrated symptom improvement within just 3 days of initiation in clinical trials [34], and upadacitinib similarly induces a quick response. Indeed, a network meta-analysis found upadacitinib and tofacitinib to have the fastest onset of clinical benefit among advanced UC therapies [35]. For clinicians, this means JAK inhibitors can be particularly useful for patients who need prompt symptom relief and may not tolerate the slower onset of some biologics. In our sub-analysis of patient-reported outcomes, while resolution of diarrhoea and abdominal pain was comparable, both upadacitinib and filgotinib were associated with more favorable hazard ratios for bowel urgency resolution than tofacitinib. This is clinically relevant, as patients often cite bowel urgency as the most burdensome symptom impacting quality of life, and recent post hoc analyses of pivotal trials have specifically emphasized the ability of upadacitinib and filgotinib to rapidly control this symptom [36, 37]. One hypothesis is that selective JAK1 inhibition might more effectively target rectal inflammation that drives urgency. However, this observation could also be due to patient- or disease-related factors and requires confirmation.

Turning to secondary endpoints, our study highlights distinct profiles regarding biochemical, endoscopic, and symptom-based outcomes. In terms of biochemical remission, upadacitinib demonstrated a more rapid onset of action, with significantly higher probabilities of normalization of inflammatory markers at weeks 8 and 16. However, these differences were no longer statistically significant from week 36 onwards. These later estimates should be interpreted cautiously, given the limited sample size at subsequent time points, which may have reduced the ability to detect between-group differences.

Our sub-analysis of endoscopic remission at week 52 revealed a statistically significant superiority of upadacitinib (36.6%) compared to tofacitinib (18.8%) and filgotinib (19.5%). This finding aligns with indirect comparisons from RCTs and recent real-world data indicating that upadacitinib may induce deeper and higher rates of mucosal healing compared to other JAK inhibitors [24, 38].

From a safety perspective, tofacitinib was associated with a higher incidence of overall AEs, particularly infections and herpes zoster, compared to upadacitinib and filgotinib. This higher rate of milder AEs with tofacitinib is consistent with existing pharmacovigilance data and the long-term extension of the OCTAVE studies [9, 39]. However, dermatological events deserve explicit consideration when comparing JAK inhibitors. Acne is increasingly recognized as a class-associated adverse event of JAK inhibition, and, in the EMA product information for upadacitinib, it is specifically listed as a common adverse reaction in ulcerative colitis trials [40]. Recent IBD evidence further suggests that acne occurs in approximately 8.6% of patients treated with JAK inhibitors overall and may be reported more frequently with upadacitinib than with other agents [41]. Therefore, the lack of a stronger acne signal in our cohort should be interpreted with caution and may reflect the retrospective design and the low absolute number of recorded dermatologic events, rather than the absence of a molecule-specific effect. Importantly, however, the rates of SAEs and colectomy did not differ significantly among the three groups in our cohort, suggesting that, while minor tolerability issues vary, the risk of severe safety outcomes remains comparable in the short to medium term, in line with recent safety pooling analyses of JAK inhibitors [42].

Our study has limitations that must be acknowledged. First, despite the use of IPTW to balance baseline characteristics, the retrospective design carries an inherent risk of residual confounding due to indication bias and unmeasured variables. Sex should also be considered when interpreting our findings. Although birth-assigned sex was reported and was acceptably balanced after IPTW, our analyses were not stratified by sex and no gender-related variables were available. Therefore, we cannot exclude sex-related differences in treatment response, safety profile, or prescribing patterns, and our findings should not be interpreted as sex-specific. Second, endoscopic remission was assessed only in the subgroup of patients with available week-52 endoscopy, which may have introduced selection bias, as endoscopic reassessment in routine practice was not available for all patients. Third, the follow-up period was capped at 52 weeks; given the chronic nature of UC, longer observational periods are needed to assess the durability of response and long-term safety, particularly regarding cardiovascular and malignancy risks, which require years to manifest. Dose-stratified maintenance analyses should be considered exploratory. In particular, the upadacitinib 45-mg subgroup was exposure-defined and likely enriched for patients with more refractory disease, since this category included patients who remained on 45 mg beyond induction or required re-induction during follow-up. Therefore, these results should not be interpreted as evidence of a causal advantage of the 45-mg maintenance regimen, but rather as hypothesis-generating findings from real-world practice. Time-varying confounders during follow-up, including corticosteroid tapering, dose escalation, and changes in concomitant therapies, were not explicitly modeled and may have contributed to residual confounding. Missing data were mainly attributable to the real-world retrospective design, as not all patients underwent all scheduled assessments at each time point. Formal comparisons were performed across multiple prespecified follow-up time points to describe longitudinal response patterns. However, these repeated analyses may increase the risk of type I error and should be interpreted cautiously.

Conversely, the main strength of this study is the inclusion of a large, multicenter, real-world cohort, and the use of IPTW allowed us to create a pseudo-population that may mimic the balance of an RCT, enabling a fairer comparison between drugs that might otherwise be prescribed to different patient profiles [18, 19]. Additionally, the comprehensive collection of clinical, biochemical, endoscopic, and patient-reported outcomes provides a whole view of therapeutic effectiveness that reflects daily clinical practice.

Conclusion

Our study suggests that, while all three JAK inhibitors are effective options for the treatment of UC, they exhibit distinct efficacy and safety profiles. Upadacitinib appears to be the agent with the highest observed rates of remission in our study for inducing rapid, steroid-free clinical remission and achieving endoscopic healing, making it a potentially useful option for patients with a high inflammatory burden or a need for rapid symptom control. Filgotinib and tofacitinib remain valuable options, with filgotinib showing a particularly favorable safety profile regarding minor adverse events. These findings support a personalized approach to treatment positioning, in which the choice of JAK inhibitor is tailored to the individual patient’s disease severity, symptom urgency, and safety risk factors.
