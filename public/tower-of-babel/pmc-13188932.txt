Cardiac Computed Tomography and Echocardiography Integration for Chronic Secondary Mitral Regurgitation Quantification
Authors: Abdulameer Jasim Jawad al-Gburi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryObjectives:This study aimed to determine whether it would be possible to reclassify the severity of secondary mitral regurgitation (MR) using an integrated mitral regurgitant volume (RVol) derived from computed tomography (CT) and echocardiography data.Methods:This cross-sectional study was conducted at Ibn al-Bitar Specialized Center for Cardiac Surgery, Baghdad, Iraq. Systolic CT data of patients with isolated secondary MR were evaluated from June 2023 to December 2025. The image with the highest regurgitant orifice area (ROA) and the smallest left ventricular volume was chosen from the CT systolic phase. To calculate the integrated RVol (iRVol), the velocity time integral obtained by echocardiography was multiplied by the mitral anatomic ROA (AROA) that was obtained using planimetry.Results:A total of 113 patients were included in this study. While the mean AROA and iRVol measured by CT were 0.143 ± 0.090 cm2 and 26.47 ± 10.61 mL, respectively, the mean effective ROA and RVol of MR measured by echocardiography were 0.175 ± 0.095 cm2 and 27.57 ± 8.82 mL, respectively. A total of 62% of patients were reclassified from severe to non-severe MR and 21% from non-severe to severe MR when iRVol grading was used instead of only echocardiography.Conclusion:By integrating the real cross-sectional mitral ROA measured by CT with Doppler mitral haemodynamics, a quantitative metric for MR severity (called mitral iRVol) was developed, leading to a clinically important reclassification of secondary MR grade.Keywords: Multidetector Computed Tomography, Mitral Valve Insufficiency, Doppler Echocardiography

1. Introduction

Although the prevalence of rheumatic disease is decreasing in developing countries, mitral regurgitation (MR) is increasing. Primary MR occurs when there is damage to the valve apparatus; secondary MR occurs when the left ventricle (LV) is dysfunctional and/or the left atrium (LA) and mitral annulus are enlarged, typically with normally shaped or slightly thickened mitral valve (MV) chordae and leaflets.1,2,3,4,5 In light of the MV's reparability, imaging-based evaluation of MR can provide not only the degree of regurgitation but also many anatomic details, its mechanisms, aetiology and the likelihood of its repair. So far, the most reliable method for its assessment was echocardiography. Patient habitus and acoustic windows are two factors that can limit the operator's ability to obtain reliable data, which in turn limits the utility of echocardiography for MR quantification, despite technical developments.6

MR quantitation is still a difficult task. When possible, the flow convergence method should be used as the primary quantitative methodology for MR evaluation.6 The first aliasing is used to determine the proximal isovelocity surface area (PISA) radius during systole. The echocardiographic effective regurgitant orifice area (EROA) and regurgitant volume (RVol) are subsequently calculated by an equation. Of all the parameters, the EROA stands out as the most reliable indicator of lesion severity due to its quantitative nature. A limitation of the PISA method is that it assumes the distance from the regurgitant orifice to the velocity distribution is hemispherically symmetrical; however, this would not hold when dealing with eccentric jets, numerous jets, complex or elliptical regurgitant orifices or any other type of jet. Compared to secondary MR, the PISA in primary MR has a more rounded shape, reducing the likelihood of underestimating EROA. In order to determine the extent of secondary MR, it may be useful to take the 3D geometry of PISA into account; however, there is currently no standardised way to evaluate 3D PISA.6 Mitral RVol is under-estimated by 2D echocardiography utilising PISA when contrasted with 3D imaging methods.7,8 A 2D PISA can calculate the EROA by taking the regurgitant jet's peak velocity, dividing it by the aliasing velocity and multiplying the result by the flow convergence hemisphere surface area closest to the orifice.6 Nevertheless, the regurgitant orifice is assumed to be circular in this computation. But the anatomical ROA can be obtained with 3D echocardiography by showing the mitral regurgitant orifice in an ‘en-face’ image.

Incorporating the anatomic ROA (AROA) into the equation helps to reduce the error in calculating the RVol by avoiding the assumption of the regurgitant orifice being circular. Compared to 2D echocardiogram, 3D echocardiography and cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) imaging have higher agreement to grade MR.7,8 The clinical significance of accurately grading secondary MR and the difficulty of doing so are both substantial. Additional treatment options for improving patients' outcomes have been made possible by the introduction of innovative transcatheter therapies to decrease MR. Patient selection for these novel treatments can benefit greatly from computed tomography (CT). For planimetry purposes, an en-face view of the regurgitant orifice can be obtained using multiplanar reconstruction. During systole, when the leaflet non-coaptation is at its maximum, images can be created orthogonal to the regurgitant opening and perpendicular to the long-axis planes of the LV 2- and 3-chamber views. The smallest AROA can be found and utilised for planimetry by adjusting the plane vertically parallel to the non-coaptation edge.9

The RVol could be determined by combining the AROA of the MV with Doppler echocardiographic data. This study aimed to determine whether it would be possible to reclassify the severity of secondary MR by combining CT and echocardiographic parameters to get the integrated RVol (iRVol).

2. Methods

This retrospective study was conducted at Ibn al-Bitar Specialized Center for Cardiac Surgery, Baghdad, Iraq, from June 2023 to December 2025. A convenience sampling approach was used whereby patients were identified from an existing registry of individuals referred for clinically indicated cardiac computed tomography angiography (CCTA), which was primarily ordered for the pre-procedural planning of transcatheter interventions or for coronary artery evaluation. Following the CCTA, comprehensive transthoracic echocardiography was performed, and these paired imaging data sets were evaluated for study inclusion. Patients with any degree of isolated secondary MR were assessed. Exclusion criteria included a history of MV surgery, significant renal dysfunction (eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m2), arrhythmias and a known allergy to iodine-based contrast agents. If global and/or regional LV remodelling caused the regurgitation and there were no macroscopic MV abnormalities, MR was considered secondary. 6

This study used commercially available ultrasound equipment (Vivid E9 systems; General Electric Vingmed, Horten, Norway) equipped with M5S transducers to obtain colour Doppler transthoracic echocardiographic data as well as 2D data. Patients in the left lateral position were examined for acquisition of apical (2-, 3- and 4-chamber) and parasternal (long- and short-axis) views, and in the supine position for acquisition of subcostal views. The cine-loop format was used to store the data. There was a median interval of 7 days (interquartile range: 2–13 days) between the CT scan and the transthoracic echocardiography, suggesting that the latter was conducted closer to the former. The ejection fraction of the LV was measured using Simpson's biplane approach from the 2-and 4-chamber images at the apex.10 A multiparametric method was used to quantitatively assess the severity of MR in accordance with current recommendations. This approach includes the presence of flail leaflet, vena contracta width, PISA radius (r), jet area, pulmonary vein systolic flow reversal, LV size, EROA and echocardiographic RVol (eRVol) as evaluated with the PISA technique.6 To determine the eRVol, the EROA was multiplied by the regurgitant jet's velocity time integral (VTI) as measured by the continuous wave Doppler as follows:6

EROA = (2πr2 × Va) / Peak velocity

eRVol = EROA × VTI

where Va is the aliasing velocity. Severe secondary MR was defined as eRVol >30 mL and EROA ≥ 0.3 cm2, reflecting 2013 European Association of Cardiovascular Imaging (EACVI) recommendations and established multimodal imaging methodologies.6,11,12

CT data were acquired using a 64-slice scanner (Brilliance 64, Philips Medical Systems, Cleveland, Ohio, USA). No premedication (e.g., β-blockers) were administered to preserve native loading conditions. Retrospective electrocardiogram (ECG)-gated CT angiography was performed during a <15-second breath-hold (120 kV, 600 mAs, 250-mm field of view, 0.2 pitch, 0.42-second rotation, 0.625-mm collimation). Iodixanol (100–150 mL) was injected via an antecubital vein at 6 mL/sec, followed by a 50-mL saline chaser. Scanning was automatically triggered when left ventricular attenuation reached 180 HU. All patients were in normal sinus rhythm with a mean heart rate of 71 ± 12 bpm.

CT data were reconstructed in 10% increments across the cardiac cycle at 0.5-mm thickness using retrospective ECG gating. A blinded cardiologist evaluated the systolic phase with the lowest left ventricular volume, aligning multiplanar reconstructions to a double oblique plane spanning the narrowest regurgitant orifice to measure mitral AROA via planimetry.

The iRVol was computed by integrating Doppler haemodynamics and CT anatomic data. Multiplying the VTI of the regurgitant jet, as estimated from continuous wave Doppler data, by the AROA, as measured on CT, yielded iRVol. An iRVol more than 30 mL was considered a severe secondary MR based on 2013 EACVI guidelines and prior multimodal imaging studies.11,12

Categorical data is shown as frequencies and percentages, whereas continuous data is shown as mean ± standard deviation. To assess the primary objective of reclassification of MR severity, McNemar's test was utilised to evaluate the statistical significance of changes in categorical MR grading between echocardiography alone and the integrated CT-echocardiography approach. The validity of the CT mitral AROA was examined by using two sets of measurements taken by the same observer at separate times as well as a second set of measurements taken by an observer who was blinded to the first set of measurements. A 2-way mixed model and intra-class correlation coefficients were used to examine both intra- and inter-observer agreements. Analysis was conducted using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) software, Version 27 (IBM Corp., Armonk, New York, USA). For all analyses, a 2-tailed P value <0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

3. Results

A total of 113 patients were included in this study (8 patients were excluded due to inadequate quality CT data during the systolic phase for mitral ROA analysis). The mean age of the patients was 67.88 ± 8.14 years and the majority were male (55.8%) [Table 1]. Heart rates averaged 73 ± 11 bpm during echocardiography and 69 ± 13 bpm during CT scanning (P = 0.013). Systolic and diastolic blood pressures did not differ significantly prior to echocardiography and CT scanning (P = 0.052 and P = 0.213, respectively). The mean echocardiographic EROA was 0.175 ± 0.095 cm2 and CT AROA was 0.143 ± 0.090 cm2. Furthermore, eRVol was 27.57 ± 8.82 mL and iRVol was 26.47 ± 10.61 mL.

A total of 24 patients were found to have severe MR using 2D echocardiographic imaging, of whom 15 individuals (62.5% of the severe MR subgroup) would have been categorised as having mild or moderate MR if the iRVol had been used. On the other hand, 89 patients were found to have mild or moderate MR according to echocardiography; nevertheless, 19 patients (21.3% of the non-severe MR subgroup) would now be considered to have severe MR based on the iRVol [Fig. 1]. To statistically assess this reclassification of MR severity, McNemar's test was conducted. While 34 patients experienced a change in their categorical severity grading, the net directional shift in the overall proportion of severe MR cases across the cohort was not statistically significant (P = 0.493).

4. Discussion

Evaluation of the integrated mitral RVol and quantification of the severity of MR can be achieved by combining Doppler echocardiographic data with CT-derived mitral anatomical ROA, as demonstrated by this study. Furthermore, the measurement of the mitral AROA by CT demonstrated excellent reproducibility, with high intra-observer and inter-observer correlation coefficients (0.988 and 0.981, respectively), indicating that the integrated approach can be consistently applied. The MR grade of patients with secondary MR was reclassified after using integrated mitral RVol; 15 out of 24 patients were downgraded from the ‘severe MR’ category to the ‘non-severe MR’ category and 19 out of 89 subjects were reclassified from ‘non-severe MR’ category to ‘severe MR’.

Although the cohort's net shift in severe MR was not statistically significant (P = 0.493), individual reclassifications remain clinically paramount. Downgrading 15 patients potentially shifts heart team treatment decisions from high-risk surgeries to less invasive strategies. Practically, this approach should be reserved for patients with discordant echocardiograms or scheduled cardiac CTs. Given the substantial diagnostic waiting lists highlighted by Ciliberti et al., targeted application minimises delays, avoids unnecessary radiation and optimises strained resources. 13

Grading secondary MR can be considerably more difficult than grading primary MR. The assumption of a circular regurgitant orifice is a significant restriction of the 2D PISA approach. In patients with secondary MR, the AROA was shown as somewhat elongated and semi-lunar through direct observation with 3D echocardiography.8 The EROA and RVol of secondary MR were found to be underestimated by 2D echocardiography compared to 3D echocardiography.8 As a result of a decrease in total stroke volume, the RVol is often lower in secondary severe MR (below 60 mL) compared to primary MR. The small numbers required in determining the regurgitant fraction lead to increased mistakes, despite the fact that it would account for relatively decreased flows.14 Both the size of the LV and its ejection fraction could affect EROA.15 Therefore, this study used the lower cut-off of EROA (>0.3 cm2) as a threshold for severe MR by 2D PISA.11,12 The fact that the underlying cardiomyopathy frequently makes adjunctive findings unhelpful compounds the difficulties of secondary MR evaluation. For instance, systolic flow reversal — diagnostic for severe secondary MR when present — is not often observed when there is elevated LA pressure, a dilated and compliant LA and decreased LV systolic function. Another complicating factor is that secondary MR is known to be quite dynamic which highlights the importance of taking volume status and other clinical variables into account. The present investigation found no statistically significant difference in systolic or diastolic blood pressure prior to echocardiography or CT. In secondary MR, a worse outcome is predicted by EROA ≥0.2 cm2, according to multiple studies.16,17 There is much debate over whether severe secondary MR should be redefined based on prognosis alone if EROA ≥ 0.2 cm2 by 2D PISA.15,18,19

Nevertheless, transoesophageal echocardiography remains the gold standard for pre-procedural planning in transcatheter edge-to-edge repair, providing superior spatial and temporal resolution for MR quantification and morphological assessment that transthoracic imaging cannot replace.

Considering the limitations of 2D echocardiography when it comes to assessing secondary MR, CT scans could be invaluable. This is especially true in the pre-operative period, when CT scans can supplement additional data on the viability and safety of MV repair or replacement. By aligning the CT scans with the MR orifice, an en-face viewpoint can be obtained, allowing for direct evaluation of the AROA, much like with 3D echocardiography.20,21 The geometrical assumptions of the ROA can be overcome by incorporating the actual regurgitant area of the MR orifice into the method of calculating the RVol. In the current study, grading based on the integrated mitral RVol reclassified 62.5% of subjects from the severe MR category to the non-severe MR category and 21.3% of subjects from the non-severe MR category to the severe MR category, compared with 2D echocardiography. A previous proof-of-concept study evaluated this technique in which a total of 73 patients with severe aortic stenosis and MR were analysed using systolic CT data.12 Although most patients MR grade were confirmed by the integrated approach, some patients were reclassified from the severe category to the non-severe category of MR and vice versa.12 It should be noted that their study included only patients with severe aortic stenosis planned for TAVI and include both primary and secondary MR, unlike the current study, which included only secondary MR where MR accurate quantification was particularly challenging and had clinical implications.

Before surgery, cardiac CT can help determine whether repairing or replacing the MV is a viable and safe option. In addition to assessing the degree of MV annulus and coronary calcification, pre-procedural CT can give precise measurements of the MV geometry and evaluate the angle between the anterior MV and LV outflow;22,23 this lessens the possibility of LV outflow obstruction during novel transcatheter methods of MV replacements.22,24,25 Percutaneous MV repair planning can be further enhanced with the use of cardiac CT, which simultaneously visualises the cardiac arterial and venous systems in addition to the heart structure;26 it is also useful in identifying postprocedural complications.27,28 Despite the fact that segmental leaflet prolapse may be accurately detected and localised using cardiac CT with cine imaging, the substantial radiation dose involved prevents its widespread use.29 Also, while coronary CT angiography is good for excluding coronary artery disease (it has a high negative predictive value in subjects with a low risk of atherosclerosis), it is still not advocated for routine use for valvular heart disease.

Studies have shown that CT-derived AROA corresponds well with EROA evaluated by echocardiography when it comes to evaluating the severity of MR using cardiac CT.29,30 When CMR imaging is not an option, CT may be a good substitute for individuals with unsatisfactory echocardiography images. Although cardiac CT is not presently advised for routine evaluation, its use may grow as radiation and contrast dosages are reduced.

This study was subject to several limitations including the small severe MR cohort and the absence of gold standard CMR imaging to validate the integrated metric. Additionally, lacking a control group without MR or with strictly primary MR introduces potential selection bias. Despite evaluating all systolic phases, CT's inferior temporal resolution compared to echocardiography may introduce measurement error. Furthermore, MR severity is dynamic and affected by loading conditions. The significant heart rate difference between echocardiography and CT acquisitions may alter orifice dynamics and undermine measurement precision, although blood pressures remained stable. Finally, given the retrospective use of an existing registry, no a priori sample size calculation was performed.

5. Conclusions

Integrating CT-derived mitral ROA with Doppler haemodynamics to calculate mitral iRVol enabled reclassification of secondary MR severity in a subset of patients. Although the overall cohort reclassification lacked statistical significance, individual reclassifications hold substantial clinical importance, potentially shifting heart team decisions from high-risk surgeries to less invasive transcatheter and medical strategies. Incorporating CT-derived AROA is particularly beneficial for patients undergoing pre-procedural cardiac CT for transcatheter planning. Further studies utilising a gold standard are required to determine definitive clinical validity.
