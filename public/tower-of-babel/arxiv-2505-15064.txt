Why and When Deep is Better than Shallow: Implementation-Agnostic State-Transition Model of Deep Learning
Sho Sonoda, Yuka Hashimoto, Isao Ishikawa, Masahiro Ikeda

Abstract
We ask when adding hidden layers improves generalization, in a model that keeps the layers fixed and varies only their number. A hidden layer is a self-map of a state space, a depth- k k network composes at most k k hidden layers with an output layer, and depth is compared within the nested family ℋ 0 ⊂ ℋ 1 ⊂ ⋯ \mathcal{H}_{0}\subset\mathcal{H}_{1}\subset\cdots built from one class F F of hidden layers; this compares a deep network with shallower networks built from the same layers, not with wider ones. Our message is that the statistical cost of depth is the metric entropy of the set B ⁡ ( k , F ) B(k,F) of compositions. The estimation error is bounded by an entropy integral over B ⁡ ( k , F ) B(k,F) with constants that do not depend on the depth, and the bound is matched from below when the output layer can see the hidden states. For Lipschitz layers on a bounded state space this entropy grows at most polynomially in k k , and it stays bounded, or grows only like log ⁡ k \log k , under contraction, equicontinuity, or nilpotent structure. Balanced against the approximation error, this gives depths k ∗ ​ ( n ) k^{\ast}(n) that grow with the sample size, and it separates the models whose estimation error is independent of the depth from those whose estimation error grows with it. Deep ReLU networks, unrolled solvers, and chain-of-thought computation are worked out; for the last two the number of steps is derived rather than assumed. All statements are machine-checked in Lean 4; the formalization and its blueprint are available at https://shosonoda.github.io/lean-deepgen/ .



1
Introduction

Deeper models often generalize better, and learning theory does not make
this automatic: every added layer enlarges the hypothesis class, and naive
complexity bounds grow exponentially in the depth
k
. This paper asks a
precise version of the question. Fix a class
F
of hidden layers and a
class
H
of output layers, let
\mathcal{H}_{k}
be the networks with an output
layer from
H
and at most
k
hidden layers from
F
, and consider the
nested family
\mathcal{H}_{0}\subset\mathcal{H}_{1}\subset\cdots
. What is the
statistical cost of the depth
k
in this family, and when is the best
depth
k^{\ast}(n)
larger than one and growing with the sample size
n
?
This compares a deep network with shallower networks built from the same
layers. It is the design question for models whose layers are shared by
construction, such as unrolled solvers, iterative denoisers, samplers of
diffusion models, neural operators, and step-by-step reasoning, where the
depth is the number of iterations or steps. It is not a comparison of deep
and wide networks at equal parameter count; width enters through the layer
class
F
and is held fixed when depth varies.

Figure 1:
A network of depth
k=4
in the state-transition model. The input and every hidden representation are points of one state space
\mathcal{X}
; each hidden layer is a map
f_{i}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
; the output layer
h:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
reads the answer from the last state. Depth is the number of hidden maps composed before the output layer.

The model.

We describe a network as a state-transition model (
Figure

1
).
The
state space

\mathcal{X}
is a metric space in which the input and
all hidden representations live, for instance a bounded subset of

\mathbb{R}^{m}
. A
hidden layer
is a continuous map
f:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
,
and
F
is the set of admissible hidden layers. The

depth-
k
hidden class

B(k,F)
is the set of all compositions

f_{m}\circ\cdots\circ f_{1}
of at most
k
maps from
F
, including the
identity. An
output layer
is a map
h:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
that reads the
answer from the last state, and
H
is the set of admissible output
layers. The
hypothesis class
at depth
k
is

\mathcal{H}_{k}:=H\circ B(k,F)=\{h\circ f:h\in H,\ f\in B(k,F)\}
. The

target class

\mathcal{C}
is the set of functions the learner is compared
with; it need not be contained in any
\mathcal{H}_{k}
. Only the metric of

\mathcal{X}
and the composition of maps in
F
are used, so the same
objects describe fully connected networks, unrolled solvers, and symbolic
computation.

Message and evidence.

The statistical cost of depth is the metric entropy of
B(k,F)
, the
logarithm of the number of maps in
B(k,F)
that can be told apart at a
given resolution. The estimation error is independent of the depth when
composition creates no new distinguishable maps, and it grows with the
depth when it does. Three results
support this message.

(1)

(
Section

3
.) Under three standing assumptions, the
depth-dependent part of the estimation error is bounded by an entropy
integral over
B(k,F)
, with a constant that depends only on the
output-layer class and not on the depth, and when the output layer can
tell the hidden states apart on the sample the same entropy appears in a
lower bound (
Theorem

1
,
Corollary

1
).

(2)

(
Section

4
.) The entropy of
B(k,F)
has a universal
upper bound, the layerwise covering bound: for Lipschitz parametric layers
on a bounded state space it grows at most polynomially in
k
, and the
exponentially growing Lipschitz constant of a composition enters only
through its logarithm (
Theorem

2
). Five mechanisms decide where
a layer class sits below this bound: contraction, equicontinuity, and
nilpotent control make the entropy constant or logarithmic in
k
,
separation makes it linear, and memory-preserving expansion attains the
bound (
Theorem

3
).

(3)

(
Sections

5
and
6
.) Balancing the resulting variance
profiles against the approximation error gives depths
k^{\ast}(n)
that
grow with
n
(
Corollary

2
). If the estimation bound is
independent of the depth, the model may be run to convergence; if it grows
polynomially, the depth has to be balanced against the bias. Three
examples carry this out: for deep ReLU networks the Lipschitz constant of
a layer selects the mechanism, and for unrolled solvers and
chain-of-thought computation both sides of the balance are derived within
the framework, so the step counts are consequences rather than inputs
(
Examples

1
and
2
; unrolled solvers in
Appendix

M
).

Background and position.

Two lines of work motivate the model. The first is the theory of depth in
deep ReLU networks, summarized below, which established sharp rates for
one architecture but not a notion of depth that survives a change of
architecture. The second is the family of models in which depth is an
iteration count: unrolled optimization and fixed-point solvers, deep
equilibrium models, neural ODEs, the samplers of diffusion and
flow-matching models, Picard-type neural operators, and chain-of-thought
reasoning in language models. For these models the layer is fixed by
design and the question “how many steps” is asked at deployment time,
so a theory that keeps the layer class fixed and varies only the depth
speaks to them directly;
Appendix

B
reviews both lines and
places the known ReLU results in the coordinates of this paper, and

Appendix

J
works out the teacher–student and
neural-operator examples that connect them.

Relation to the theory of deep ReLU networks.

Most rigorous results on depth are proved for one implementation, the
fully connected ReLU network: depth separation in approximation

(
Eldan and Shamir, 2016
;
Telgarsky, 2016
;
Yarotsky, 2017
;
Siegel, 2023
)
, VC-dimension,
norm-based, and compression-based estimation bounds

(
Bartlett et al., 2019
;
Bartlett et al., 2017
;
Golowich et al., 2018
;
Arora et al., 2018
;
Suzuki et al., 2020
)
,
and minimax rates on smoothness classes and hierarchical compositions

(
Schmidt-Hieber, 2020
;
Suzuki, 2019
)
. These results are
sharp, but their depth dependence is read off an explicit parameterization,
and a change in the layer structure, the state space, or the parameter
sharing requires a new proof. Our framework isolates depth in
B(k,F)
,
separately from the layer class
F
; the known ReLU rates then appear as
one case of the analysis (the polynomial profile), and the question of
what makes depth cheap is answered by the geometry of composition rather
than by the parameter count. A detailed comparison with the literature is
given in
Appendix

B
.

Setting

Learning problem.

We observe an i.i.d. sample
\mathcal{D}=((X_{1},Y_{1}),\dots,(X_{n},Y_{n}))
from a
distribution
P
on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathbb{R}
, and write
S=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})

for the inputs. The loss
\ell:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to[0,b]
is bounded and
Lipschitz in the prediction (
Assumption

1
below; clipped squared
loss, hinge, ramp, or logistic loss on a bounded range; cross-entropy
needs clipping,
Remark

2
). The risk and the empirical risk are

L[f]:=\mathbb{E}\,\ell(f(X),Y)
and
\hat{L}[f]:=n^{-1}\sum_{i}\ell(f(X_{i}),Y_{i})
.
The learner picks an
\eta
-approximate empirical risk minimizer

\hat{f}\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
, that is,
\hat{L}[\hat{f}]\leq\inf_{f\in\mathcal{H}_{k}}\hat{L}[f]+\eta
,
and outputs an implementation
\hat{h}:=\embed(\hat{f})
, where the

implementation map

\embed
sends each abstract predictor to a
concrete one, for instance a finite-width network. Two errors are attached
to the depth
k
:

\varepsilon_{\text{imp}}(k):=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}_{k}}\|f-\embed(f)\|_{\infty},\qquad\varepsilon_{\text{model}}(k):=\inf_{f\in\mathcal{H}_{k}}L[f]-\inf_{c\in\mathcal{C}}L[c],

the
implementation error
and the
approximation error
. The
latter is a gap between risks; a uniform approximation rate transfers to it
through the Lipschitz loss (
Remark

1
),

\varepsilon_{\text{model}}(k)\leq\beta_{\ell}\,\sup_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\inf_{f\in\mathcal{H}_{k}}\|f-c\|_{\infty}.

(1)

Distances, covering numbers, and complexity.

Two distances between hidden maps
f,g:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
are used: the
uniform distance
d_{\infty}(f,g):=\sup_{x}d(f(x),g(x))
and the empirical
distance
d_{S}(f,g):=(n^{-1}\sum_{i}d(f(X_{i}),g(X_{i}))^{2})^{1/2}
, which only
looks at the sample and satisfies
d_{S}\leq d_{\infty}
. For a set
A
of
maps and a resolution
\varepsilon>0
, the
covering number

N(A,d,\varepsilon)

is the smallest number of balls of radius
\varepsilon
(in the distance
d
)
needed to cover
A
; it counts the maps in
A
that can be told apart at
resolution
\varepsilon
, and
\log N(A,d,\varepsilon)
is the
metric entropy
.
For a class
G
of real-valued functions, the empirical Rademacher
complexity

\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{S}(G):=\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\Bigl[\sup_{g\in G}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}g(X_{i})\Bigr],

with independent random signs
\sigma_{i}
, measures how well
G
can fit
random signs on the sample; it is the standard measure of statistical
complexity in uniform-convergence bounds. Finer conventions (external
covering numbers, the semigroup view of
B(k,F)
) are recorded in

Appendix

A
.

Standing assumptions.

Three assumptions are used throughout; each is stated once here with the
place where it enters.

Assumption 1

(Bounded Lipschitz loss)
.

The loss
\ell:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\to[0,b]
is measurable and

\beta_{\ell}
-Lipschitz in its first argument, with
b
and

\beta_{\ell}
fixed with the loss, so that neither depends on the depth.
It enters the bias–variance decomposition,
Theorem

1
(i).

Assumption 2

(Output layer does not amplify hidden distances)
.

For a hidden map
f
let
Z_{f}:=\sup_{h\in H}n^{-1}\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}h(f(X_{i}))

be the Rademacher average of the output layers applied after
f
. There
is a constant
L_{H}
, depending only on
H
and the metric of
\mathcal{X}
,
such that for all compositions
f,g
of maps from
F
, of any depth,
and all
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}(|Z_{f}-Z_{g}|>t)\leq 2\exp\bigl(-nt^{2}/(2L_{H}^{2}d_{S}(f,g)^{2})\bigr)
.
It holds with
L_{H}
equal to the Lipschitz constant of the feature map
for norm-bounded linear output layers on any feature map, and for finite
classes of Lipschitz output layers, including bounded scalarizations of a
softmax head (
Appendix

D
). Its point is that
L_{H}
is fixed
with the output layer and is the same at every depth: the hidden layers
stay inside
B(k,F)
and never enter
L_{H}
. It enters the
hidden–output decomposition,
Theorem

1
(ii).

Assumption 3

(Bounded state space, Lipschitz layers)
.

The state space has finite diameter
D:=\diam(\mathcal{X})
, and every map in

F
is
\Lambda
-Lipschitz for some
\Lambda\geq 0
. Boundedness is
provided in practice by bounded activations, normalization layers, or
clipping. It enters the growth analysis of

Section

4
and the profiles of
Section

5
.

3
The cost of depth is the entropy of
B(k,F)

This section proves the first claim: the statistical cost of depth is the
metric entropy of
B(k,F)
. The argument has three steps, collected in
one theorem. The generalization error splits into an implementation, an
approximation, and a statistical term; the statistical term splits into an
output-layer part, which does not depend on the depth, and a hidden-layer
part, which is an entropy integral over
B(k,F)
; and a converse shows
that this entropy is paid whenever the output layer can see the hidden
states.

Theorem 1

(The statistical cost of depth)
.

Fix
k\geq 0
and a sample
S
.

(i)

(Bias–variance decomposition.) Under
Assumption

1
, if

\mathcal{H}_{k}
is separable in the uniform norm and pointwise bounded, then
for every
\delta\in(0,1)
, with probability at least
1-\delta
, the

\eta
-approximate empirical risk minimizer satisfies

L[\hat{h}]-\inf_{c\in\mathcal{C}}L[c]\leq\beta_{\ell}\,\varepsilon_{\text{imp}}(k)+\varepsilon_{\text{model}}(k)+\eta+4\beta_{\ell}\,\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{S}(\mathcal{H}_{k})+6b\sqrt{\tfrac{2\log(4/\delta)}{n}}.

(2)

(ii)

(Hidden–output decomposition.) Under
Assumption

2
,

\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{S}(\mathcal{H}_{k})\leq\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{S}(H)+\frac{12L_{H}}{\sqrt{n}}\,\mathsf{V}_{k}(S),\qquad\mathsf{V}_{k}(S):=\int_{0}^{\diam_{S}(B(k,F))}\sqrt{\log N\bigl(B(k,F),d_{S},\varepsilon\bigr)}\,\mathrm{d}\varepsilon.

(3)

(iii)

(Converse under output-visible separation.) If for each map
g
in

B(k,F)
some output layer
h_{g}\in H
realizes the hidden geometry on
the sample,
\|h_{g}\circ g-h_{g^{\prime}}\circ g^{\prime}\|_{S}\geq\kappa\,d_{S}(g,g^{\prime})
and

\|h_{g}\circ g\|_{S,\infty}\leq R_{\rm out}
for all
g,g^{\prime}
, then, with

M
the packing number and
c
a universal constant,

\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{S}(\mathcal{H}_{k})\geq c\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\min\Bigl\{\kappa\varepsilon\sqrt{\tfrac{\log M(B(k,F),d_{S},2\varepsilon)}{n}},\ \tfrac{\kappa^{2}\varepsilon^{2}}{R_{\rm out}}\Bigr\}.

(4)

The precise statements, with all regularity conditions, are

Theorem

4
in
Appendix

C
, the restatement of (ii) in

Appendix

D
, and
Theorem

7
in

Appendix

E
.

Reading the theorem.

Part (i) says that the statistical cost of depth is entirely carried by

\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{S}(\mathcal{H}_{k})
: the implementation error is controlled separately
(
Proposition

6
in
Appendix

J
) and the
approximation error is a property of the target class. Part (ii) removes
the output layer from this cost: the first term is the complexity of the
output layer alone, and the second is the
entropy integral
of

B(k,F)
, which adds up, over all resolutions
\varepsilon
up to the diameter
of
B(k,F)
on the sample, the square root of the number of maps in

B(k,F)
that are distinguishable at resolution
\varepsilon
. It is Dudley’s
chaining bound
(
Vershynin, 2018
, Chapter 8)
applied to the process

f\mapsto Z_{f}
, whose increments are controlled by

Assumption

2
; the constant is
12L_{H}
. Part (iii) is a
Sudakov-type minoration
(
Vershynin, 2018
, Chapter 7)
: the packing
number
M(B(k,F),d_{S},2\varepsilon)
is the largest number of maps in
B(k,F)

at mutual distance at least
2\varepsilon
on the sample, and it is
comparable to the covering number, so (ii) and (iii) bound the hidden
term by the same quantity from both sides. The converse concerns the
complexity of the class, not the risk of every algorithm; it is informative
exactly when the output layer sees the hidden states, and when hidden
representations collapse the entropy of
B(k,F)
is not paid and the
upper bound is correspondingly loose (
Section

E.4
).

Corollary 1

(Matching depth dependence)
.

Let the hypotheses of
Theorem

1
(iii) hold for every

k
with
\kappa,R_{\rm out}
independent of
k
, and fix a resolution

\varepsilon_{0}>0
. If
B(k,F)
contains
e^{\Theta(k)}
maps that are

2\varepsilon_{0}
-separated on the sample, then
\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{S}(\mathcal{H}_{k})\gtrsim\sqrt{k/n}

for
n\gtrsim k
; if it contains
k^{\Theta(1)}
such maps, then

\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{S}(\mathcal{H}_{k})\gtrsim\sqrt{\log k/n}
for
n\gtrsim\log k
. When the
covering numbers obey the matching upper bounds of
Section

4
,
the hidden term therefore has the same depth dependence,
\sqrt{k}
or

\sqrt{\log k}
, in the upper and the lower bound
(
Corollary

3
in
Appendix

E
).

The separation in (iii) has to be visible on the sample, not only in the
uniform distance:
Appendix

L
gives a family whose maps are
separated in
d_{\infty}
but not in
d_{S}
, for every sample. After this
section, everything the depth does to the estimation error is contained in
two quantities, the covering numbers of
B(k,F)
at every resolution and
its diameter; the next section determines how they grow with
k
.

4
How the entropy of
B(k,F)
grows with the depth

This section proves the second claim. By
Theorem

1
(ii),
the depth-dependent part of the estimation bound is

\mathsf{var}(k,n):=12L_{H}\mathsf{V}_{k}(S)/\sqrt{n}
, and its growth with
k
is the
growth of the entropy integral
\mathsf{V}_{k}(S)
; we call the function

k\mapsto\mathsf{V}_{k}(S)
, up to constants, the
profile
of the
variance bound. Since
d_{S}\leq d_{\infty}
, it suffices to bound the
covering numbers of
B(k,F)
in the uniform distance together with its
diameter. Two results do this: a bound that holds for every Lipschitz
layer class (
Theorem

2
), and a list of the structures that
lower the growth below it or attain it (
Theorem

3
).
Throughout,
Assumption

3
is in force.

4.1
A layerwise covering bound: depth costs at most polynomially

Theorem 2

(Layerwise covering bound)
.

Let every map in
F
be
\Lambda
-Lipschitz,
\Lambda\geq 0
. Then for
every
k\geq 0
and
\varepsilon>0
,

N\bigl(B(k,F),d_{\infty},\varepsilon\bigr)\leq 1+k\,N\Bigl(F,d_{\infty},\frac{\varepsilon}{1+\Lambda+\cdots+\Lambda^{k-1}}\Bigr)^{k}.

(5)

Consequently, if the state space is bounded and
F
is parametric, that
is,
N(F,d_{\infty},\varepsilon)<\infty
with

\log N(F,d_{\infty},\varepsilon)\leq p\log(1/\varepsilon)+O(1)
, then

\mathsf{V}_{k}(S)=O(\sqrt{kp\log k})
when
\Lambda\leq 1
and

\mathsf{V}_{k}(S)=O(k\sqrt{p\log\Lambda})
when
\Lambda>1
; if instead

\log N(F,d_{\infty},\varepsilon)\leq c\,\varepsilon^{-q}
with
q<2
, then

\mathsf{V}_{k}(S)=O(k^{(1+q)/2})
for
\Lambda\leq 1
and

O(k^{1/2}\Lambda^{qk/2})
for
\Lambda>1

(explicit constants in
Corollary

9
,
Appendix

G
).

The proof is the telescoping argument that also controls the implementation
error: replacing each of
k
layers by a nearby one moves the composition
by at most
\varepsilon
. Three consequences are worth stating. First, for
parametric layers on a bounded state space, depth costs at most
polynomially, and the exponentially growing Lipschitz constant

\Lambda^{k}
of a composition enters the covering numbers only through
its logarithm; for norm-bounded ReLU networks this is the order of the
VC-dimension bounds of
Bartlett et al. (2019)
up to a factor
\sqrt{k}
and
logarithms, obtained here without any piecewise-linear structure and hence
for every layer class. Second, the bound is attained: a construction whose
layers write a symbol, shift the memory, and expand it (mechanism (v)
below) reaches the
k^{2}
entropy of the parametric case and the
exponential entropy of the nonparametric case
(
Appendix

G
). Third,
\Lambda^{k}
does appear on an
unbounded state space, where the reachable states lie in a ball of radius

\Lambda^{k}
times that of the inputs (
Lemma

8
), so
that
D_{k}(S)
can grow like
\Lambda^{k}
; this is the product of
layerwise norms of norm-based bounds
(
Neyshabur et al., 2015
;
Bartlett et al., 2017
)
,
it enters only through the diameter, and bounded activations,
normalization layers
(
Ioffe and Szegedy, 2015
;
Ba et al., 2016
)
, or a projection
onto a bounded set remove it.

4.2
Structures below the layerwise bound, and mechanisms that attain it

Depth becomes cheaper than the layerwise bound when composition does not
create many well-separated maps. Whether it does is not decided by the
compactness of
\mathcal{X}
or by the algebra of
F
alone, but by how
composition interacts with the metric. The following theorem lists the
mechanisms; the formal conditions, named P1, P1’, P2, E1, E2, and E3, are
stated and proved with examples in
Appendix

F
,
and the theorem summarizes them.

Theorem 3

(Growth mechanisms)
.

Let
N_{k}(\varepsilon):=N(B(k,F),d_{\infty},\varepsilon)
.

(i)

(Saturation by equicontinuity, P1;
Section

F.1
.) If

\mathcal{X}
is compact and the compositions of maps from
F
are
equicontinuous, in particular if the maps in
F
are non-expanding, then

\sup_{k}N_{k}(\varepsilon)<\infty
for every
\varepsilon>0
.

(ii)

(Saturation by contraction, P1’;
Section

F.1
.) If the maps
in
F
are uniformly contractive, with a nonempty invariant set
A

and a bounded absorbing set, then for every
\varepsilon>0
and every
k
,

N_{k}(\varepsilon)\leq N(A,\varepsilon/2)+\sum_{\ell<m(\varepsilon)}N(F^{\ell},d_{\infty},\varepsilon)
with

m(\varepsilon)=O(\log(1/\varepsilon))
, a bound that does not depend on
k
.

(iii)

(Polynomial growth by nilpotent control, P2;
Section

F.2
.)
If the maps in
B(k,F)
are images, under a Lipschitz action, of the
ball of radius
O(k)
in a group of polynomial growth of degree
D
,
such as translations, shears, or upper-triangular matrices, then

N_{k}(\varepsilon)\leq C(1+k/\varepsilon)^{D}
and
D_{k}(S)=O(k)
, with

D_{k}(S)\leq\diam(\mathcal{X})
on a compact state space.

(iv)

(Exponential growth by separation, E1 and E2;
Section

F.3
.)
If
F=\{f_{1},\dots,f_{r}\}
and distinct compositions of the same length are
separated by a fixed amount at a common point (E1), or
F
satisfies the
ping-pong coding condition, expanding on disjoint chambers and resetting
elsewhere, with marker–anchor separation
\alpha
(E2), then

N_{k}(\varepsilon)\geq r^{k}
for all
k
and all sufficiently small
\varepsilon
.

(v)

(Attainment of the layerwise bound by memory-preserving expansion, E3;

Section

F.4
.) There is a class
F
of
\lambda
-Lipschitz
maps whose layers write a symbol, shift the stored symbols, and expand
them, for which
\log N_{k}(\varepsilon)\asymp k^{2}
when the written symbols range
over a finite-dimensional set, and
\log N_{k}(\varepsilon)\asymp\lambda^{pk}

when they range over a set of entropy
\delta^{-p}
.

The mechanisms explain one another. Contraction and equicontinuity
saturate because Ascoli’s theorem makes the set of all compositions totally
bounded: after a few layers no new distinguishable maps appear
(
Section

F.1
). Nilpotent control saturates nothing but
organizes the compositions by a group in which the number of elements at
distance
k
grows polynomially; these layers are realized exactly by
affine ReLU layers, so the mechanism is not restricted to abstract
examples. Freeness of the compositions is not enough for a lower bound,
because contracting maps may collapse many compositions to nearly the same
map; what is needed is a probe point (
Lemma

7
) at which
distinct compositions stay apart, and expanding maps with resets provide
one probe per composition. Expansion alone still does not create growth
beyond

, because later layers can erase what earlier layers
distinguished; growth beyond
\sqrt{k}
in the profile requires a memory,
and with a memory the entropy accumulates across layers up to the
layerwise bound. Symbolic shifts with guarded steps are the basic example
of (iv) (
Example

), and branching symbolic
computation is of this kind in
d_{\infty}
, although its empirical entropy
is much smaller (
Appendix

L
).

Proposition

in

converts each mechanism,
with its diameter bound, into the profile of
\mathsf{V}_{k}(S)
: constant
under (i) and (ii),
\sqrt{\log k}
under (iii) and for branching
computation on any sample,
\sqrt{k}
under (iv) and for the layerwise
bound of non-expanding parametric layers,

expanding parametric layers, which (v) attains, and exponential for
expanding nonparametric layers (listed by mechanism in

These five profiles are the variance side of the balance in the next

5
Balancing the bias against the estimation error

This section proves the third claim: the profile of the variance bound,
balanced against a law for the approximation error, gives a depth

k^{\ast}(n)
that grows with the sample size, and the kind of profile
decides whether the depth has to be balanced at all. Write

\mathsf{bias}(k):=\varepsilon_{\text{model}}(k)
for the approximation error, which decreases with
the depth and depends on the target class, and

\mathsf{gen}(k,n)\lesssim\mathsf{bias}(k)+\mathsf{var}(k,n)
for the excess-risk bound of

Theorem

1
(i) with the depth-independent terms omitted.
Since
\mathsf{bias}(k)
is nonincreasing and
\mathsf{var}(k,n)
is nondecreasing in

k
, the bound is minimized, up to a factor
2
, at the depth

k^{\ast}=k^{\ast}(n)
where the two terms are of the same order
(
Lemma

10
). Two laws for each term cover the mechanisms of

Section

4
and the approximation results of the
examples. The bias decays exponentially,
\mathsf{bias}(k)=e^{-\Theta(\alpha k)}
,
when the target is itself an iteration that converges geometrically, as for
contractive iterations, or polynomially,
\mathsf{bias}(k)=\Theta(k^{-\beta})
, as
for discretization errors and smoothness classes. The variance profile is
logarithmic,
\mathsf{var}(k,n)=O(\sqrt{\log k/n})
, which also covers the
saturated case, or polynomial,
\mathsf{var}(k,n)=O(\sqrt{k^{\gamma}/n})
with

\gamma=1
for the
\sqrt{k}
profile and
\gamma=2
for the
k

profile. We name the combinations by two letters, the bias first: EL is
exponential bias with logarithmic variance, PP is polynomial bias with
polynomial variance (
Figure

2
); the mixed cases EP and PL
are treated in
Appendix

I
.

Corollary 2

(Profiles and balancing depths)
.

Under
Assumptions

1
,
2
and
3
, the
variance bound
\mathsf{var}(k,n)
of

has, up to
constants, the profile

under the saturation mechanisms of

\sqrt{\log k/n}
under nilpotent

\sqrt{k/n}
under the layerwise bound of

under the layerwise bound
for expanding parametric layers, which (v) attains

Depth-independent and depth-dependent estimation error.

The two panels of
Figure

2
differ in kind, not only in

is bounded by a
quantity that does not depend on

is a threshold rather than an optimum, every depth beyond

is set by the bias. When the profile is polynomial, which is

Width is held fixed throughout; it enters the one-layer covering

of the order of the number of parameters per layer, the
output-layer class, the bias, and the implementation error

6
Using the framework

6.1
A recipe for a given architecture

To apply the results to an architecture one has to (1) choose the state
space, the layer class
F
, and the output-layer class
H
, and check

2

7
Conclusion

We analyzed depth in a model in which the hidden layers are fixed and only
their number varies. The statistical cost of depth is the metric entropy
of the compositions
B(k,F)
: it bounds the estimation error with
constants that do not depend on the depth, it is paid whenever the output
layer can see the hidden states, and its growth with
k
is decided by
the geometry of composition. For Lipschitz layers on a bounded state space
it grows at most polynomially, and contraction, equicontinuity, or
nilpotent structure make it constant or logarithmic. Balanced against the
approximation error, this gives the depth that a sample of size
n

justifies, and it says whether that depth is a threshold beyond which
further layers do not add to the estimation error or an optimum beyond
which they do. The examples show that one layer type realizes every case
according to its Lipschitz constant, and that for iterative models both
sides of the balance follow from a single step. Deeper is better than
shallower, in this family, when the target admits fast depth-driven
approximation and the compositions of hidden layers do not create many
distinguishable maps that the output layer and the sample can see.

Appendix A
Conventions and basic facts

This appendix fixes the conventions used in all proofs: covering and
packing numbers and their elementary properties, the uniform and empirical
metrics on hidden maps, the semigroup generated by a hidden-layer class,
the symbolic state space used in several examples, and two remarks on the
learning problem of
Section

2
. Nothing here is specific to a
mechanism or an example; later appendices refer back to this one.

A.1
Covering and packing numbers

For a metric space
(\mathcal{M},\rho)
,
write

N(A,\rho,\varepsilon):=\min\left\{|C|\;\middle|\;C\subset\mathcal{M}\text{ s.t. }A\subset\bigcup_{y\in C}B_{\rho}(y,\varepsilon)\right\}

for the
\varepsilon
-covering number of
A\subset\mathcal{M}
, and

M(A,\rho,\varepsilon):=\max\left\{|S|\mid S\subset A,\rho(x,y)\geq\varepsilon\ \forall x\neq y\in S\right\}

for the
\varepsilon
-packing number.
Thus
N
is the external covering number by closed balls (centres
anywhere in
\mathcal{M}
); it is monotone under inclusion,
A\subset A^{\prime}

implies
N(A,\rho,\varepsilon)\leq N(A^{\prime},\rho,\varepsilon)
. The internal covering
number
N^{\rm int}(A,\rho,\varepsilon)
, whose centres are required to lie in

A
, satisfies
N(A,\rho,\varepsilon)\leq N^{\rm int}(A,\rho,\varepsilon)\leq N(A,\rho,\varepsilon/2)
but is
not
monotone under inclusion. Upper
bounds below are stated for
N
; lower bounds are obtained through
packing numbers, which are monotone under inclusion, and apply to both
conventions.

The following facts are used throughout.

Lemma 1

(Packing and covering numbers)
.

For any subset
A
of a metric space and
\varepsilon>0
,

M(A,2\varepsilon)\leq N(A,\varepsilon)\leq M(A,\varepsilon).

Lemma 2

(Lipschitz images)
.

Let
(X,d_{X}),(Y,d_{Y})
be metric spaces. Suppose
\phi:X\to Y
is
L
-Lipschitz, then for every subset
S\subset X
,

N(\phi(S),d_{Y},L\varepsilon)\leq N(S,d_{X},\varepsilon),\quad M(\phi(S),d_{Y},L\varepsilon)\leq M(S,d_{X},\varepsilon).

Lemma 3

(Subadditivity under unions)
.

For any metric space
(M,d)
, subsets
F,G\subset M
, and
\varepsilon>0
,
we have

N(F\cup G,\varepsilon)\leq N(F,\varepsilon)+N(G,\varepsilon).

Proof.

Let
A,B
be
\varepsilon
-coverings of
F,G
respectively. Then,
A\cup B
is an
\varepsilon
-covering of
F\cup G
because
for any
h\in F\cup G
there exists
c\in A\cup B
satisfying
d(h,c)\leq\varepsilon
.
Thus,
N(F\cup G,\varepsilon)\leq|A\cup B|\leq|A|+|B|=N(F,\varepsilon)+N(G,\varepsilon)
.
∎

Lemma 4

(Submultiplicativity under composition)
.

For any bi-Lipschitz metric semigroup
(M,d)
with
\sup_{f\in M}d(fx,fy)\leq\lambda d(x,y)
(left Lipschitz) and
\sup_{f\in M}d(xf,yf)\leq\rho d(x,y)
(right Lipschitz),
for any subsets
F,G\subset M
, and
\varepsilon,\delta>0
,
we have

N(FG,\varepsilon+\delta)\leq N(F,\varepsilon/\rho)N(G,\delta/\lambda).

Proof.

Let
A,B
be
\alpha,\beta
-coverings of
F,G
respectively. Then,
AB
is an
(\rho\alpha+\lambda\beta)
-covering of
FG
because
for any
fg\in FG
there exists
ab\in AB
satisfying
d(fg,ab)\leq d(fg,ag)+d(ag,ab)\leq\rho d(f,a)+\lambda d(g,b)=\rho\alpha+\lambda\beta
.
Thus,
N(FG,\rho\alpha+\lambda\beta)\leq|AB|\leq|A||B|=N(F,\alpha)N(G,\beta)
.
Letting
\alpha=\varepsilon/\rho,\beta=\delta/\lambda
yields the assertion.
∎

Lemma 5

(Right composition is 1-Lipschitz)
.

d_{\infty}(a\circ f,b\circ f)\leq d_{\infty}(a,b)

Proof.

d_{\infty}(a\circ f,b\circ f)=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}d(a(f(x)),b(f(x)))\leq\sup_{y\in\mathcal{X}}d(a(y),b(y))=d_{\infty}(a,b)

∎

Lemma 6

(Left composition is Lipschitz)
.

d_{\infty}(f\circ a,f\circ b)\leq\lip(f)d_{\infty}(a,b)

Proof.

d_{\infty}(f\circ a,f\circ b)=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}d(f(a(x)),f(b(x)))\leq\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\lip(f)d(a(x),b(x))=\lip(f)d_{\infty}(a,b)

∎

Lemma 7

(Packing lower bounds from finite probes)
.

Let
P=\{x_{1},\dots,x_{m}\}\subset\mathcal{X}
be finite and define

d_{P}\big((y_{j}),(z_{j})\big):=\max_{1\leq j\leq m}d(y_{j},z_{j})\text{ on }\mathcal{X}^{m}
, and

\eval_{P}:C(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X})\to\mathcal{X}^{m},\eval_{P}(f)=(f(x_{1}),\dots,f(x_{m}))
.
Then
\eval_{P}
is 1-Lipschitz:

d_{P}\big(\eval_{P}(f),\eval_{P}(g)\big)\leq d_{\infty}(f,g)
. If

\eval_{P}(S)\subset\mathcal{X}^{m}
contains
M
points that are pairwise

\delta
-separated (in
d_{P}
), then for every

\varepsilon<\delta/2
,

N(S,d_{\infty},\varepsilon)\geq M.

Proof.

The 1-Lipschitz claim is immediate:

d\big(f(x_{j}),g(x_{j})\big)\leq\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}d\big(f(x),g(x)\big)=d_{\infty}(f,g).

If
\eval_{P}(s_{1}),\dots,\eval_{P}(s_{M})
are
\delta
-separated, then

d_{\infty}(s_{i},s_{j})\geq d_{P}(\eval_{P}(s_{i}),\eval_{P}(s_{j}))\geq\delta
. Any ball of
radius
\varepsilon<\delta/2
in the
d_{\infty}
-metric can contain
at most one of the
s_{i}
’s, so at least
M
balls are needed.
∎

A.2
Uniform and empirical metrics on hidden maps

Throughout,
(\mathcal{X},d)
is a metric space, not necessarily compact, and

C(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X})
is the set of continuous self-maps of
\mathcal{X}
.

Uniform metric.

On
C(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X})
we use the uniform metric

d_{\infty}(f,g):=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}d\left(f(x),g(x)\right).

When
\mathcal{X}
is non-compact, this quantity may be infinite for some
pairs of maps. Whenever a finite ambient metric is needed, we use one
of the following two devices:

•

(
d_{b}
)
replace
d
by its bounded version

d_{b}(x,y):=\min\{1,d(x,y)\}
;

•

(
C_{b}
)
fix a compact set
K_{0}\subset\mathcal{X}
and assume all
maps under discussion (and all words built from them) take values in

K_{0}
.

These three settings—
C(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X})
with compact
\mathcal{X}
and
d_{\infty}
,

C(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X})
with non-compact
\mathcal{X}
and
d_{b}
,
and
C_{b}(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X})
with non-compact
\mathcal{X}
and
d_{\infty}
—give finite uniform metrics.

Ascoli’s theorem.

We use Ascoli’s theorem in the following form
(
Rudin, 1991
, Appendix A5)
: a family of continuous self-maps of a compact metric space is totally bounded in
d_{\infty}
if and only if it is equicontinuous. On a non-compact space the theorem gives only compactness in the compact-open topology, which is why the bounded-metric and bounded-range devices above are needed.

Lipschitz and co-Lipschitz constants.

For
f\in C(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X})
and

S\subset\mathcal{X}
nonempty,

\lip_{S}(f):=\sup_{x\neq y\in S}\frac{d(f(x),f(y))}{d(x,y)},\qquad\colip_{S}(f):=\inf_{x\neq y\in S}\frac{d(f(x),f(y))}{d(x,y)}.

We say “
f
is uniformly expanding on
S
” if

\colip_{S}(f)>1
.

A.3
Semigroups and word balls

For
F\subset C(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X})
, write

\langle F\rangle
for the semigroup generated by
F
under
composition. For
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
B(k,F)
be the set of maps
obtainable as a composition of at most
k

. Since

, the identity belongs to

\langle F\rangle=\bigcup_{k\geq 0}B(k,F)
, which is therefore a monoid.

Word length.

For

h\in\langle F\rangle
, denote by
\ell_{F}(h)
the least
\ell

A.4
The full shift and its ultrametric

On the
one-sided full shift

\Sigma_{m}^{+}=[m]^{\mathbb{N}}
(with

[m]=\{1,\dots,m\}
discrete), fix
\theta\in(0,1)
and define the
ultrametric

d_{\theta}(x,y)=\begin{cases}0,&x=y,\\
\theta^{n(x,y)-1},&x\neq y,\end{cases}\quad\text{where}\quad n(x,y):=\min\{i\geq 1\mid x_{i}\neq y_{i}\}.

This is an ultrametric (satisfies
strong triangle inequality

d(x,z)\leq\max\{d(x,y),d(y,z)\}
), it induces the product topology, and
it makes
\Sigma_{m}^{+}
a compact, totally disconnected, perfect
(Cantor-type) space. Distances take only the values

{1,\theta,\theta^{2},\ldots}
; in particular, sequences that differ in
the first symbol are at distance
1
.

For a finite word
u=u_{1}\cdots u_{\ell}
, the
(prefix) cylinder

of depth
\ell
is given by

[\![u]\!]:=\{w\in\Sigma_{m}^{+}\mid w_{1}=u_{1},\dots,w_{\ell}=u_{\ell}\},

a clopen set; cylinders form a basis and the depth-
\ell
cylinders
partition
\Sigma_{m}^{+}
into
m^{\ell}
pieces. In the metric

d_{\theta}
,
\diam([\![u]\!])=\theta^{\ell}
, and any two
distinct depth-
\ell
cylinders are separated by at least

\theta^{\ell-1}
. Moreover, balls coincide with cylinders: for

w\in\Sigma_{m}^{+}
and
\ell\geq 0
,

\overline{B}_{d_{\theta}}(w,\theta^{\ell})=[\![w_{1}\cdots w_{\ell}]\!]
.
The left shift
\sigma(w)_{i}=w_{i+1}
satisfies

\sigma([\![au]\!])=[\![u]\!]
and
\sigma^{-1}([\![u]\!])=\bigsqcup_{a\in[m]}[\![au]\!]
.

A.5
Remarks on the learning problem

Remark 1

(Approximation term versus function approximation)
.

\varepsilon_{\text{model}}(k)
is a gap between oracle risks, not the distance between a
target function and its best approximant. The two notions are related
through the Lipschitz loss: since

\varepsilon_{\text{model}}(k)=\sup_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\bigl(\inf_{f\in\mathcal{H}_{k}}L[f]-L[c]\bigr)

and
L[f]-L[c]\leq\beta_{\ell}\|f-c\|_{\infty}
, (
1
)
holds. Hence uniform approximation rates from approximation theory
transfer to the bias term with the constant
\beta_{\ell}
. We call

\varepsilon_{\text{model}}(k)
the approximation term when it is nonnegative, as is the
case when
\mathcal{C}
is a benchmark at least as good as the depth-
k

hypothesis class in oracle risk; in complete generality it is an oracle gap
relative to
\mathcal{C}
, and if a nonnegative approximation error is desired
one may replace it by its positive part, at the cost of a weaker but still
valid bound. The risk-based definition is used because it allows the same
hypothesis class to be compared against different benchmarks and requires
no norm structure on
\mathcal{C}
.

Remark 2

(Loss constants)
.

For a fixed loss,
b
and
\beta_{\ell}
are fixed and do not depend on the
depth; examples are the clipped squared loss, hinge and ramp losses, and the
logistic loss on a bounded logit range. Cross-entropy expressed in class
probabilities is unbounded and non-Lipschitz near the boundary of the
simplex; expressed in logits its derivative is bounded but the loss itself is
unbounded unless the logits are restricted to a bounded range. Such losses
therefore enter
Theorem

4
only after probability clipping or a logit range
restriction. If the clipping threshold
\tau_{k,n}
is allowed to vary
with
k
or
n
, then
b\asymp\log(1/\tau_{k,n})
and

\beta_{\ell}\asymp 1/\tau_{k,n}
must be carried through the rates in

Section

5
; with a fixed threshold the exponents there are unchanged.

Appendix B
Literature Overview

One motivation of this paper is to organize the generalization theory of
deep learning that has been developed, for the most part, on the fully
connected ReLU network. That theory contains several depth dependences,
polynomial, logarithmic, and depth-independent, obtained by different
techniques and under different assumptions; we review them below and then
state, at the end of this appendix, how each of them appears as a case of
the present framework.
Appendix

K
works out the ReLU network as
an example of the framework in its own right.

Depth separation and expressivity.
A classical line of work
shows that modest increases in depth can yield exponential
representational advantages.
Eldan and Shamir (2016)
proved a
three-vs-two-layer separation for a simple radial function, requiring
exponential width for any depth-2 approximant, while
Telgarsky (2016)

established families exhibiting exponential gaps between networks of
depth
O(k^{3})
and
O(k)
with semi-algebraic gates (including ReLU)
and provided constructive hard instances; subsequent work extended
separations beyond radial constructions. These results clarify when

expressivity
favors depth, but do not by themselves pin down
estimation behavior.

Generalization via capacity control.
Combinatorial analyses
give nearly tight VC/pseudodimension bounds for piecewise-linear
networks, scaling roughly linearly in depth for fixed width/weights,
providing a baseline picture of depth in classical uniform-convergence
frameworks
(
Bartlett et al., 2019
)
. Norm- and margin-based approaches bound estimation error
through products of layer norms (path/spectral)
(
Neyshabur et al., 2015
;
Bartlett et al., 2017
)
, sometimes yielding
size-independent or gently depth-dependent bounds under additional
structure (e.g., margin normalization).
These strands highlight multiple possible depth dependencies—linear, polynomial, or even
milder—depending on how complexity is measured.

Rademacher/covering and size-independent bounds.
A
complementary thread controls depth via data-dependent complexities
(Rademacher, covering).

Golowich et al. (2018)
;
Golowich et al. (2020)
obtained bounds
that (under norm constraints) improve the depth dependence and can be
independent of width and depth in certain regimes; later refinements
further reduced explicit depth factors. These works show how the
estimation side may be decoupled from naively counted parameters
and instead tied to geometric quantities of the hypothesis class.

Compression and PAC-Bayes.
A productive viewpoint explains
generalization via compressibility: if a trained network admits a
succinct reparametrization, one can transfer that compression into
generalization guarantees.

Arora et al. (2018)
formalized this link and
demonstrated strong bounds in practice; follow-ups convert compression
bounds to the original (non-compressed) networks and sharpen them via
PAC-Bayes with subspace quantization, yielding state-of-the-art
nonvacuous estimates
(
Suzuki et al., 2020
;
Lotfi et al., 2022
)
.
While depth typically enters these bounds through
compressibility or margin quantities, the methodology is agnostic to
architecture details.

Nonparametric regression with deep networks.
Another large body
of work analyzes approximation–estimation trade-offs of ReLU networks
on smoothness classes.
Schmidt-Hieber (2020)
showed near-optimal rates in
nonparametric regression, with depth playing an essential role;
Suzuki (2019)

established optimal adaptivity on (mixed) Besov spaces and improvements
over linear/kernel baselines;
Nakada and Imaizumi (2020)
tied generalization to
intrinsic (Minkowski) dimension; and
Imaizumi and Fukumizu (2019)
;
Imaizumi and Fukumizu (2022)
identified
regimes with singularities where DNNs are minimax-superior to
traditional estimators. Recent approximation results (e.g., optimal
Sobolev/Besov rates) further sharpen the expressivity side
(
Yarotsky, 2017
;
Yarotsky, 2018
;
Yarotsky and Zhevnerchuk, 2020
;
Siegel and Xu, 2022
;
Siegel, 2023
;
Yang and He, 2024
)
.
These analyses, however, are typically architecture-specific (ReLU
feedforward) and hinge on smoothness assumptions.

Iterative/hierarchical models and continuous depth.
Many modern
systems are naturally modeled as
compositions
or

flows
—precisely the setting of our state-transition
abstraction. Neural ODEs
(
Chen et al., 2018
)
as well as Neural Operators
(
Kovachki et al., 2023
)
treat depth as continuous time evolution;
diffusion/score-based models
(
Shen et al., 2025
)

implement long iterative refinement; and
chain-of-thought
(
Wei et al., 2022
)
prompting in LLMs explicitly unfolds multi-step
reasoning. These families motivate studying depth-dependent
generalization at the level of abstract state transitions rather than
fixed architectures.

Deep ReLU networks in the coordinates of this paper.

For Hölder, Sobolev, and Besov targets on
[0,1]^{d}
, the best

m
-parameter approximation has the Jackson rate
O(m^{-s/d})
with
matching lower bounds
(
DeVore et al., 1989
)
; deep ReLU networks reach and,
by bit-extraction, slightly surpass this rate, but the decay in the depth
remains polynomial
(
Yarotsky, 2017
;
Yarotsky, 2018
;
Yarotsky and Zhevnerchuk, 2020
;
Siegel, 2023
;
Yang and He, 2024
)
.
On the estimation side, VC-dimension and compression bounds grow
polynomially in the depth
(
Bartlett et al., 2019
;
Arora et al., 2018
;
Suzuki et al., 2020
;
Lotfi et al., 2022
)
.
Hence deep ReLU networks on smoothness classes are in the PP case of

Section

5
, and the minimax analysis of

Suzuki (2019)
is an instance. For the hierarchical
compositions of Hölder functions of
Schmidt-Hieber (2020)
, the
covering numbers of deep ReLU classes grow only logarithmically in the
depth while the approximation error has an exponentially decaying
compositional part, which is close to the PL case. In both cases the
framework classifies known rates rather than deriving new ones; its
contribution is the quantity, the entropy of
B(k,F)
, that explains why
the two cases differ.

How the ReLU results appear in the framework.

Read in the coordinates of this paper, the depth dependences above are
instances of the mechanisms of
Section

4
, with the
ReLU layer as the hidden-layer class (
Appendix

K
). The
polynomial depth dependence of VC-dimension and covering-number bounds

(
Bartlett et al., 2019
;
Schmidt-Hieber, 2020
)
is the layerwise bound of

Theorem

2
for a parametric layer class, which reproduces its
order up to a factor
\sqrt{k}
and logarithms. The product of layer
norms in norm-based bounds
(
Neyshabur et al., 2015
;
Bartlett et al., 2017
)
is the
diameter of the reachable states on an unbounded state space
(
Lemma

8
); on a bounded state space it disappears.
The depth-independent bounds obtained under norm constraints

(
Golowich et al., 2018
)
correspond to non-expanding layers on a bounded
state space, which is the saturation mechanism P1
(
Proposition

8
(ii)). The exponential-in-depth worst cases of
uniform-convergence bounds correspond to expanding layers with separated
compositions, mechanism E2, and are realized by expand-and-reset ReLU
layers (
Proposition

8
(iii)). On the approximation side, the
minimax analyses on smoothness classes and hierarchical compositions

(
Suzuki, 2019
;
Schmidt-Hieber, 2020
)
supply the bias
laws of the PP and PL regimes. The framework does not reproduce the constants of these proofs; it
reproduces their depth dependence from one quantity, the entropy of

B(k,F)
, and the same computation applies to architectures that the
ReLU theory does not cover.

This Study.

Relative to these threads, this study is

implementation-agnostic
: instead of parameterizing a specific architecture, we analyze
state-hidden-layer semigroups on metric spaces
, derive depth dependence of the
variance
via
covering/Rademacher complexity of word balls, give conditions for

polynomial/logarithmic
growth, and couple them with

exponential/polynomial
bias decay to compare depth scalings across
four typical regimes. This yields a unified lens for when and
why depth can be statistically preferable to shallower members of the same
transition model—particularly in iterative/hierarchical settings suggested
above. The distinction from the architecture-specific threads is one of
scope rather than of rates: the sharp depth-dependent results above are
proved for one implementation, most often a fully connected ReLU network on a
Sobolev, Hölder, or Besov class, and a change in the layer structure, the
state space, or the parameter sharing generally requires a new proof. Here
the one-layer class
F
carries all implementation-specific quantities,
including width through its covering numbers, while composition depth is
isolated in the word ball
B(k,F)
; existing rates for particular networks
then become examples that can be inserted into the framework
(
Appendix

J
).

Theorem 5

(General bias–variance decomposition)
.

Let
\mathcal{H}_{\text{abs}}
be a class of measurable functions

that is
separable in the uniform norm and pointwise bounded
(

\mathcal{C}
be a
nonempty class of measurable benchmark functions
\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, and let

\ell:\mathbb{R}\times\mathcal{Y}\to[0,b]
,
b>0
, be measurable and

\beta_{\ell}
-Lipschitz in its first argument. Define

L[f]:=\mathbb{E}[\ell(f(X),Y)],\qquad\hat{L}[f]:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(f(X_{i}),Y_{i}),

and set

L_{\mathcal{C}}:=\inf_{c\in\mathcal{C}}L[c],\qquad L_{\mathcal{H}_{\text{abs}}}:=\inf_{f\in\mathcal{H}_{\text{abs}}}L[f],\qquad\varepsilon_{\text{model}}:=L_{\mathcal{H}_{\text{abs}}}-L_{\mathcal{C}}.

Let

be a implementation map into an implementation
class and let

be a pseudo-metric on
\mathcal{H}_{\text{abs}}\cup\mathcal{H}_{\text{imp}}

\varepsilon_{\text{imp}}:=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{H}_{\text{abs}}}d_{T}(\embed(f),f)<\infty

and that, for every

,

\displaystyle|L[\embed(f)]-L[f]|

\displaystyle\leq\beta_{L}d_{T}(\embed(f),f),

\displaystyle|\hat{L}[\embed(f)]-\hat{L}[f]|

\displaystyle\leq\beta_{\hat{L}}d_{T}(\embed(f),f).

For
\eta\geq 0
, let
\hat{f}\in\mathcal{H}_{\text{abs}}
be an
\eta
-empirical minimizer:

\hat{L}[\hat{f}]\leq\inf_{f\in\mathcal{H}_{\text{abs}}}\hat{L}[f]+\eta,

and output
\hat{h}:=\embed(\hat{f})
. Then, for every
\delta\in(0,1)
,
with probability at least
1-\delta
over
\mathcal{D}\sim P^{n}

\displaystyle L[\hat{h}]-L_{\mathcal{C}}

\displaystyle\leq\beta_{L}\varepsilon_{\text{imp}}+\varepsilon_{\text{model}}+\eta+4\beta_{\ell}\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{S}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{abs}})+Cb\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{n}},

(7)

\displaystyle L[\hat{h}]-\hat{L}[\hat{h}]

\displaystyle\leq(\beta_{L}+\beta_{\hat{L}})\varepsilon_{\text{imp}}+2\beta_{\ell}\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{S}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{abs}})+Cb\sqrt{\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{n}},

(8)

where

is a universal constant; explicitly, one may take the deviation
term to be

in (
7
)
and

in (
8
). On the

\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{S}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{abs}})

\mathfrak{R}_{n}(\mathcal{H}_{\text{abs}})

No topological structure is needed on
\mathcal{C}
. Since the loss is bounded
and nonnegative,

, so

is a well-defined real number whenever

The only probabilistic ingredient is the standard uniform deviation bound for
bounded Lipschitz loss classes. Since
\mathcal{H}_{\text{abs}}
is separable in the uniform

and symmetrization, bounded differences, and the contraction inequality imply

S

On the event (

\displaystyle L[\hat{f}]-L_{\mathcal{H}_{\text{abs}}}

\displaystyle\leq L[\hat{f}]-\hat{L}[\hat{f}]+\hat{L}[\hat{f}]-\inf_{f\in\mathcal{H}_{\text{abs}}}\hat{L}[f]+\inf_{f\in\mathcal{H}_{\text{abs}}}\hat{L}[f]-L_{\mathcal{H}_{\text{abs}}}

\displaystyle\leq\Delta_{\mathcal{H}_{\text{abs}}}+\eta+\Delta_{\mathcal{H}_{\text{abs}}}=2\Delta_{\mathcal{H}_{\text{abs}}}+\eta.

This appendix proves

D.1
Statement and proof

Theorem 6

(
Theorem

1
(ii), restated)
.

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space, let
S=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})\subset X
be fixed, and define

d_{S}(f,g):=\Bigl(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}d\bigl(f(X_{i}),g(X_{i})\bigr)^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}\qquad(f,g:X\to X).

Let
F\subset C(X,X)
be totally bounded in

, and fix an anchor
f_{0}\in F
.
Write

\diam_{S}(F):=\sup_{f,g\in F}d_{S}(f,g).

Let
H\subset\mathbb{R}^{X}
be a class of real-valued functions. For each
f\in F
define

Z_{f}(\sigma):=\sup_{h\in H}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}h\left(f(X_{i})\right),

where
\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{n}
are i.i.d. Rademacher variables, and assume
that each
Z_{f}(\sigma)
is finite.

Assume that there exist constants
L>0
and
A_{H}>0
such that, for all
f,g\in F
and all

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}\left(|Z_{f}-Z_{g}|>t\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{nt^{2}}{2A_{H}^{2}L^{2}d_{S}(f,g)^{2}}\right).

(10)

Then

\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{S}(H\circ F)\leq\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{S}(H\circ f_{0})+\frac{12A_{H}L}{\sqrt{n}}\int_{0}^{\diam_{S}(F)}\sqrt{\log N(F,d_{S},\varepsilon)}d\varepsilon,

where
\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{S}(H\circ f_{0})=\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}Z_{f_{0}}
and the right-hand side is
read as
+\infty
(so that the bound is trivial) if the entropy integrand is
not integrable on
[0,\diam_{S}(F)]

\mathrm{id}_{X}\in F
, the choice

gives the first term
\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{S}(H)

In particular, for

term
\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{S}(H)
, for the depth-

H_{k}:=H\circ B(k,\mathcal{F})
.

Proof.

Anchor the process at
f_{0}
and write

Y_{f}:=Z_{f}-Z_{f_{0}},\qquad f\in F.

Then
Y_{f_{0}}=0
, and by (
10
),

\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}\left(|Y_{f}-Y_{g}|>t\right)=\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}\left(|Z_{f}-Z_{g}|>t\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{nt^{2}}{2A_{H}^{2}L^{2}d_{S}(f,g)^{2}}\right).

Thus
(Y_{f})_{f\in F}
is a process with sub-Gaussian increments with respect to the metric

\rho(f,g):=\frac{A_{H}L}{\sqrt{n}}d_{S}(f,g).

By Dudley’s entropy integral bound for processes with sub-Gaussian
increments (this is the only probabilistic ingredient; it applies to any
process on a finite probability space with sub-Gaussian increments, anchored
at an arbitrary index point, with the constant
12
, and the Rademacher
structure of
Z_{f}
enters only through (
10
)),

\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\Bigl[\sup_{f\in F}Y_{f}\Bigr]\leq 12\int_{0}^{\Delta_{\rho}(F)}\sqrt{\log N(F,\rho,u)}du,

where

\Delta_{\rho}(F)=\sup_{f\in F}\rho(f,f_{0})\leq\frac{A_{H}L}{\sqrt{n}}\diam_{S}(F).

Changing variables
u=\frac{A_{H}L}{\sqrt{n}}\varepsilon
and enlarging the upper limit gives

\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\Bigl[\sup_{f\in F}Y_{f}\Bigr]\leq\frac{12A_{H}L}{\sqrt{n}}\int_{0}^{\diam_{S}(F)}\sqrt{\log N(F,d_{S},\varepsilon)}d\varepsilon.

Finally,

\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{S}(H\circ F)=\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\Bigl[\sup_{f\in F}Z_{f}\Bigr]=\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\Bigl[Z_{f_{0}}+\sup_{f\in F}(Z_{f}-Z_{f_{0}})\Bigr]=\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{S}(H\circ f_{0})+\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\Bigl[\sup_{f\in F}Y_{f}\Bigr],

and the claim follows (if the entropy integrand is not integrable there is
nothing to prove).
∎

D.2
Output-layer classes satisfying
Assumption

2

Assumption
2
is used only for the upper-bound
decomposition: it prevents the supremum over output layers from amplifying small
hidden-state perturbations beyond the
d_{S}(f,g)/\sqrt{n}
scale. It is not a
output-separation assumption; the latter appears only in the Sudakov-type
lower bound. The condition is satisfied by standard output-layer mechanisms once
the last scalarization is bounded and Lipschitz.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a real inner product space (completeness is not needed), let
\Phi:X\to\mathcal{H}
be
L
-Lipschitz, and define

H_{\Phi}:=\Bigl\{x\mapsto\langle w,\Phi(x)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}:\|w\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq 1\Bigr\}.

Then the assumption (
10
) holds with
H=H_{\Phi}
and
A_{H}=1
.
Consequently,

\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{S}(H_{\Phi}\circ F)\leq\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{S}(H_{\Phi})+\frac{12L}{\sqrt{n}}\int_{0}^{\diam_{S}(F)}\sqrt{\log N(F,d_{S},\varepsilon)}d\varepsilon.

Proof.

For
H_{\Phi}
, the supremum over the unit ball of
\mathcal{H}
can be evaluated explicitly:

Z_{f}=\sup_{\|w\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq 1}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}\langle w,\Phi(f(X_{i}))\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\Bigl\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}\Phi(f(X_{i}))\Bigr\|_{\mathcal{H}}.

Hence, for any
f,g\in F
,

|Z_{f}-Z_{g}|\leq\Bigl\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}\bigl(\Phi(f(X_{i}))-\Phi(g(X_{i}))\bigr)\Bigr\|_{\mathcal{H}}.

Set

v_{i}:=\Phi(f(X_{i}))-\Phi(g(X_{i}))\in\mathcal{H}.

The Rademacher tail bound in inner product spaces
(
Pinelis, 1994
)
, whose constant
2
is optimal, gives, for every
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}\left(\Bigl\|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}v_{i}\Bigr\|_{\mathcal{H}}>t\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{n^{2}t^{2}}{2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|v_{i}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}}\right).

Because
\Phi
is
L
-Lipschitz,

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|v_{i}\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\leq L^{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}d\bigl(f(X_{i}),g(X_{i})\bigr)^{2}=nL^{2}d_{S}(f,g)^{2}.

Combining the last two displays yields

\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}\left(|Z_{f}-Z_{g}|>t\right)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{nt^{2}}{2L^{2}d_{S}(f,g)^{2}}\right),

which is exactly (
10
) with
A_{H}=1
.
The final bound is now an immediate application of Theorem
6
.
∎

In neural-network terminology, an MLP with a linear output layer fits

Proposition

1
with the hidden layers placed in the state transition
class
B(k,F)
and a fixed feature map
\Phi
(for instance the identity on

\mathbb{R}^{m}
, or a fixed embedding of the terminal state) followed by a
norm-bounded linear functional as the output layer. With this convention

L=\lip(\Phi)
and
A_{H}=1
do not depend on the depth, which is the
depth-uniform form of Assumption
2
required in

Section

3
. One may alternatively absorb some lower layers
into the feature map, but then
\Phi=\Phi_{k}
depends on the depth and

L
must be replaced by
\lip(\Phi_{k})
, which for a
k
-layer network
may grow exponentially in

; the bound remains valid but its depth
dependence is then no longer carried by the word-ball entropy alone.

Proposition 2

(Finite Lipschitz scalar output layers)
.

Let
H=\{h_{1},\dots,h_{m}\}
be a finite class of real-valued functions on

X
. Suppose every
h_{j}
is
L
-Lipschitz with respect to
d
. Then
the assumption (
10
) holds with

A_{H}=\left(1+\frac{\log m}{\log 2}\right)^{1/2}.

Proof.

For
j=1,\dots,m
, define

S_{j}(f,g):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sigma_{i}\bigl(h_{j}(f(X_{i}))-h_{j}(g(X_{i}))\bigr).

Since the difference of two suprema is bounded by the supremum of the
differences,

|Z_{f}-Z_{g}|\leq\max_{1\leq j\leq m}|S_{j}(f,g)|.

For each fixed
j
, Hoeffding’s inequality and the Lipschitz property give

\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}(|S_{j}(f,g)|>t)\leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{nt^{2}}{2L^{2}d_{S}(f,g)^{2}}\right).

Writing
x:=nt^{2}/(2L^{2}d_{S}(f,g)^{2})
, the union bound yields

\mathbb{P}_{\sigma}(|Z_{f}-Z_{g}|>t)\leq\min\{1,2me^{-x}\}.

With
A_{H}^{2}=1+\log m/\log 2
, the right-hand side is bounded by

2\exp(-x/A_{H}^{2})
for all real
x
(so no restriction on
t
,
L
,

d_{S}(f,g)
, or

is needed), which is (
10
).
∎

Softmax output layers can be handled by including the logits/softmax map in the

Appendix E
The Sudakov-type converse

This appendix proves the conditional lower bound of
Section

3
, the
third step of the reduction, and describes the output-layer classes for
which its separation assumption holds. The assumption asks that, for each
hidden map, some output layer realizes the empirical geometry of

B(k,F)
inside the real-valued class; under it, the packing numbers of

B(k,F)
bound the Rademacher complexity from below, and the depth
dependence of
Theorem

1
is matched. The empirical
Rademacher complexity is assumed finite throughout, that is, the averages

n^{-1}\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}g(X_{i})
,
g\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
, are bounded above for every
sign pattern.

E.1
Statement and proof

Assumption 4

(Output-layer realization of hidden geometry)
.

There exist constants
\kappa,R_{\rm out}>0
and, for each
g\in B_{k}
, an
output layer
h_{g}\in H
such that
\Psi_{k}(g):=h_{g}\circ g
satisfies

\|\Psi_{k}(g)-\Psi_{k}(g^{\prime})\|_{S}\geq\kappa\,d_{S}(g,g^{\prime})
and

\|\Psi_{k}(g)\|_{S,\infty}\leq R_{\rm out}
for all
g,g^{\prime}\in B_{k}
.

Theorem 7

(Conditional Sudakov-type lower bound)
.

Under Assumption
4
, if

\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{S}(\mathcal{H}_{k})<\infty
, then for a universal constant
c>0

\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{S}(\mathcal{H}_{k})\geq c\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\min\left\{\kappa\varepsilon\sqrt{\frac{\log M(B_{k},d_{S},2\varepsilon)}{n}},\frac{\kappa^{2}\varepsilon^{2}}{R_{\rm out}}\right\},

(11)

where the first entry is
+\infty
if the packing number is. The
word-ball structure is not used: the statement holds for any

B\subset C(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X})
.

Theorem 8

(
Theorem

7
, restated)
.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
, a sample
S=(X_{1},\dots,X_{n})\subset X
, and write

B_{k}:=B(k,F),\qquad H_{k}:=H\circ B_{k}.

(The word-ball structure is not used below: the statement and proof apply
verbatim to an arbitrary subset
B\subset C(X,X)
in place of
B_{k}
, with

H\circ B
in place of
H_{k}
.)
For real-valued functions on the sample, write

\|u\|_{S,\infty}:=\max_{i\in[n]}|u(X_{i})|.

Assume that there exist constants
\kappa,R_{\rm out}>0
and, for each
f\in B_{k}
, an output layer
h_{f}\in H
such that

\displaystyle\|h_{f}\circ f-h_{g}\circ g\|_{S}

\displaystyle\geq\kappa d_{S}(f,g)\qquad(f,g\in B_{k}),

(12)

\displaystyle\|h_{f}\circ f\|_{S,\infty}

\displaystyle\leq R_{\rm out}\qquad(f\in B_{k}).

(13)

Equivalently, the map

\Psi_{k}:B_{k}\to H_{k},\qquad\Psi_{k}(f):=h_{f}\circ f,

is
\kappa
-co-Lipschitz from
(B_{k},d_{S})
to
(H_{k},\|\cdot\|_{S})
, and its image is uniformly bounded on the sample.
Assume also that
\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{S}(H_{k})<\infty
, i.e. for every sign pattern

\sigma
the averages
n^{-1}\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}g(X_{i})
,
g\in H_{k}
, are
bounded above (for instance,
H_{k}
is uniformly bounded on the sample);
without this the left-hand side below is not a finite supremum.

Then there exists a universal constant
c>0
such that, for every
\varepsilon>0
,

\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{S}(H_{k})\geq c\sup_{\varepsilon>0}\min\left\{\kappa\varepsilon\sqrt{\frac{\log M(B_{k},d_{S},2\varepsilon)}{n}},\frac{\kappa^{2}\varepsilon^{2}}{R_{\rm out}}\right\}.

(14)

By packing-covering duality, the same conclusion holds with
M
replaced by
N
, up to absolute constants. When
M(B_{k},d_{S},2\varepsilon)=\infty
, the first entry of the minimum is read as
+\infty
, so the bound is its second entry.

Proof.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
, and let
f_{1},\dots,f_{M}\in B_{k}
be a
2\varepsilon
-packing of
B_{k}
with respect to
d_{S}
, where

M=M(B_{k},d_{S},2\varepsilon)

(if this packing number is infinite, take an arbitrary finite

2\varepsilon
-packing and let its cardinality tend to infinity at the end).
Set

g_{j}:=\Psi_{k}(f_{j})=h_{f_{j}}\circ f_{j}\in H_{k}\qquad(j=1,\dots,M).

By (
12
),

\|g_{j}-g_{\ell}\|_{S}=\|\Psi_{k}(f_{j})-\Psi_{k}(f_{\ell})\|_{S}\geq\kappa d_{S}(f_{j},f_{\ell})\geq 2\kappa\varepsilon\qquad(j\neq\ell).

Hence
\{g_{1},\dots,g_{M}\}\subset H_{k}
is a
2\kappa\varepsilon
-packing in the empirical norm
\|\cdot\|_{S}
. Moreover, by (
13
),

\|g_{j}\|_{S,\infty}\leq R_{\rm out}\qquad(j=1,\dots,M).

Applying the Bernoulli–Sudakov minoration to the bounded class

\{g_{1},\dots,g_{M}\}\subset H_{k}
, we obtain

\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{S}(H_{k})\geq c\min\left\{\kappa\varepsilon\sqrt{\frac{\log M}{n}},\frac{\kappa^{2}\varepsilon^{2}}{R_{\rm out}}\right\},

for a universal constant
c>0
.
Taking the supremum over
\varepsilon
yields (
14
).
∎

Remarks.

(1) The strengthened assumption (

(ii).
(2) Only the disjointness of the hidden maps on the sample enters: the

.

E.3
Output-layer classes that separate hidden states

The class

H_{R}(\Phi):=\bigl\{h_{w}(x)=\langle w,\Phi(x)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\;:\;\|w\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq R\bigr\}

is natural, but the mere Lipschitz continuity of
\Phi
is in general sufficient only for the upper-bound side (
Theorem

1
(ii)), not for the lower-bound side.
For the lower bound, one needs either a globally co-Lipschitz scalar observable (
Proposition

3
), or a finite-set
interpolation property on the reachable sample states (
Proposition

4
and its
corollaries).

Proposition 3

(Global scalar observable)
.

Fix a sample
S=(X_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
and let

U_{k,S}:=\{f(X_{i})\in X\;:\;f\in B_{k},\ i\in[n]\}.

Let
\Phi:X\to\mathcal{H}
and

H_{R_{\rm H}}(\Phi):=\bigl\{h_{w}(x)=\langle w,\Phi(x)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\;:\;\|w\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq R_{\rm H}\bigr\}.

Assume that there exists
u\in\mathcal{H}
with
\|u\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq R_{\rm H}
and constants
\kappa,R_{\Phi}>0
such that

\displaystyle|\langle u,\Phi(x)-\Phi(y)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}|

\displaystyle\geq\kappa\,d(x,y)\qquad(x,y\in U_{k,S}),

(17)

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in X}\|\Phi(x)\|_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle\leq R_{\Phi}.

(18)

Then the lower bound holds with the single choice

\Psi_{k}(f):=h_{u}\circ f,\qquad h_{u}(x):=\langle u,\Phi(x)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}},

namely

\|\Psi_{k}(f)-\Psi_{k}(g)\|_{S}\geq\kappa\,d_{S}(f,g)\qquad(f,g\in B_{k}),

and

\|\Psi_{k}(f)\|_{S,\infty}\leq R_{\rm H}R_{\Phi}\qquad(f\in B_{k}).

Proof.

For
f,g\in B_{k}
,

\|\Psi_{k}(f)-\Psi_{k}(g)\|_{S}^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\langle u,\Phi(f(X_{i}))-\Phi(g(X_{i}))\rangle|^{2}\geq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\kappa^{2}d(f(X_{i}),g(X_{i}))^{2}=\kappa^{2}d_{S}(f,g)^{2},

by (
17
). Also,

|\Psi_{k}(f)(x)|=|\langle u,\Phi(f(x))\rangle|\leq\|u\|\,\|\Phi(f(x))\|\leq R_{\rm H}R_{\Phi},

by (
18
).
∎

Proposition
3
is the cleanest way in which a linear Hilbert-space output layer
satisfies the
full
lower-Lipschitz assumption. Geometrically, it says that one scalar coordinate
of the feature map already observes the hidden-state geometry without collapse.

Proposition 4

(Map-dependent finite-set interpolation criterion for linear heads)
.

Fix
f_{1},\dots,f_{M}\in B_{k}
, and write

z_{j,i}:=f_{j}(X_{i})\qquad(j\in[M],\ i\in[n]).

Let
\Phi:X\to\mathcal{H}
, and define

H_{R_{\rm H}}(\Phi):=\bigl\{h_{w}(x)=\langle w,\Phi(x)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\;:\;\|w\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq R_{\rm H}\bigr\}.

For each
j
, assume that the evaluation operator

T_{j}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}^{n},\qquad T_{j}w:=\bigl(\langle w,\Phi(z_{j,i})\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\bigr)_{i\in[n]},

admits a right inverse
R_{j}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathcal{H}
such that

T_{j}\circ R_{j}=\mathrm{Id}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}},\qquad\|R_{j}\|_{\ell_{2}^{n}\to\mathcal{H}}\leq\Lambda_{j}

with
\Lambda_{j}\geq 0
(for
n\geq 2
this forces
z_{j,1},\dots,z_{j,n}

to be distinct, so no separate distinctness assumption is needed).
Then, for every family of target code vectors

u^{(1)},\dots,u^{(M)}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

there exist output layers
h_{j}\in H_{R_{\rm H}}(\Phi)
,
j\in[M]
, provided

R_{\rm H}\geq\max_{j\in[M]}\Lambda_{j}\|u^{(j)}\|_{2},

such that

h_{j}(f_{j}(X_{i}))=u_{i}^{(j)}\qquad(j\in[M],\ i\in[n]).

Consequently, if

\Bigl(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|u_{i}^{(j)}-u_{i}^{(\ell)}|^{2}\Bigr)^{1/2}\geq\rho\qquad(j\neq\ell),

then

\|h_{j}\circ f_{j}-h_{\ell}\circ f_{\ell}\|_{S}\geq\rho\qquad(j\neq\ell).

If additionally
\max_{j,i}|u_{i}^{(j)}|\leq R_{\rm out}
, then

\|h_{j}\circ f_{j}\|_{S,\infty}\leq R_{\rm out}\qquad(j\in[M]).

In particular, for
R_{\rm out}
-bounded codes it is enough to take

R_{\rm H}\geq\sqrt{n}\,R_{\rm out}\max_{j\in[M]}\Lambda_{j},

which is independent of the packing cardinality
M
.

Proof.

For each fixed
j
, set
w_{j}:=R_{j}u^{(j)}
and
h_{j}:=h_{w_{j}}
. Then

T_{j}w_{j}=u^{(j)}
, hence

h_{w_{j}}(f_{j}(X_{i}))=\langle w_{j},\Phi(f_{j}(X_{i}))\rangle=u_{i}^{(j)}.

Moreover,

\|w_{j}\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq\Lambda_{j}\|u^{(j)}\|_{2},

so
h_{w_{j}}\in H_{R_{\rm H}}(\Phi)
under the stated bound on
R_{\rm H}
.
The separation and sample-boundedness claims follow by evaluating the
constructed functions on the sample:

(h_{j}\circ f_{j})(X_{i})=u_{i}^{(j)}.

∎

Corollary 5

(Finite-dimensional feature map)
.

Assume
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
n\leq m
. If, for every
j
, the feature matrix

\Phi_{j}:=\bigl(\Phi(z_{j,i})\bigr)_{i\in[n]}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}

has full column rank (which requires the
z_{j,i}
to be distinct), then Proposition
4
applies with

\Lambda_{j}=\sigma_{\min}(\Phi_{j})^{-1}.

Hence a sufficiently wide linear last layer over the feature map
\Phi
can realize any prescribed finite coding
on each individual reachable sample set
\{z_{j,i}\}_{i\in[n]}
.

Proof.

Here
T_{j}w=\Phi_{j}^{\top}w
. Since
\Phi_{j}
has full column rank,
T_{j}
is surjective, and one may take the
Moore–Penrose right inverse. Its operator norm is
\sigma_{\min}(\Phi_{j})^{-1}
.
∎

Corollary 6

(RKHS / kernel output layer)
.

Let
K
be a strictly positive definite kernel on
X
, with canonical feature map

\Phi:X\to\mathcal{H}_{K}
. Then Proposition
4
applies to

H_{R_{\rm H}}(K):=\bigl\{h_{w}(x)=\langle w,\Phi(x)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}\;:\;\|w\|_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}\leq R_{\rm H}\bigr\},

and, provided the points
z_{j,1},\dots,z_{j,n}
are distinct (so that the
Gram matrix below is positive definite), one may take

\Lambda_{j}=\lambda_{\min}(G_{j})^{-1/2},\qquad G_{j}:=\bigl(K(z_{j,i},z_{j,i^{\prime}})\bigr)_{i,i^{\prime}\in[n]}.

Therefore, whenever the individual Gram matrices
G_{j}
are uniformly
well-conditioned, the RKHS linear output layer has the map-dependent finite-coding
property needed for the packing-wise Sudakov lower bound. The output-layer norm
radius scales with
\max_{j}\|u^{(j)}\|_{2}
, not with

(\sum_{j}\|u^{(j)}\|_{2}^{2})^{1/2}
.

Proof.

Fix
j
. For
u\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
c=G_{j}^{-1}u
and set

w:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}\,\Phi(z_{j,i}).

Then

h_{w}(z_{j,i})=u_{i}\qquad(i\in[n]),

and

\|w\|_{\mathcal{H}_{K}}^{2}=u^{\top}G_{j}^{-1}u\leq\lambda_{\min}(G_{j})^{-1}\|u\|_{2}^{2}.

Thus
\|R_{j}\|_{\ell_{2}^{n}\to\mathcal{H}_{K}}\leq\lambda_{\min}(G_{j})^{-1/2}
.
∎

Corollary
6
gives a realistic interpretation of the lower-bound assumption:
a linear output layer over a feature map
\Phi
works if each reachable sample set is sufficiently
“feature-separated” so that the corresponding evaluation map is well-conditioned. In finite dimension, this is a rank/singular-value
condition on each last-layer feature matrix; in an RKHS, it is a lower bound on the smallest eigenvalue of each
individual Gram matrix.

E.4
When the converse is silent

The converse is a conditional diagnostic and is not used in the upper
bounds. If the architecture collapses or over-smooths its hidden
representations, Assumption
4
fails and
the lower bound becomes weak or vacuous: a packing lower bound can only
certify the growth of the quotient of the reachable states by the relation

x\sim y
if
h(x)=h(y)
for all
h\in H
, which may be much smaller
than the packing in the state metric. Near collapse the same effect appears
through the constants, since
\kappa
becomes small and, for linear or
RKHS output layers, the norm needed to realize a separating code grows with
the conditioning of the reachable feature matrices. The upper-bound
analysis still applies in these cases, possibly conservatively.

Appendix F
Growth mechanisms: conditions, proofs, and examples

This appendix states and proves the growth conditions summarized in

Section

4
, one subsection per mechanism: saturation
(P1, P1’), polynomial growth (P2), exponential growth under separation
(E1, E1’, E2), and memory-preserving expansion (E3). Each subsection
follows the same order: the condition and its proof, one worked example
that verifies it, and closing remarks. The conventions and the basic
covering-number facts are those of
Appendix

A
; in this appendix

B(k,F)
is called the word ball of radius
k
of the semigroup
generated by
F
.

F.1
Saturation under contraction and equicontinuity (P1, P1’)

The two conditions below make the covering number of
B(k,F)
bounded
in
k
at every resolution. The first uses compactness of the state
space and equicontinuity of the compositions; the second uses uniform
contraction toward an invariant set and applies on unbounded state spaces.
Both are used in
Proposition

5
(i) and in the examples of

Appendices

J
,
M
,
L
and
K
.

Condition P1

(Equicontinuous semigroups on compact domains saturate)
.

Assume
\mathcal{X}
is compact.
Suppose (at least) one of the following assumptions is satisfied:

1.

Semigroup
\langle F\rangle
is (pre)compact,

2a.

Semigroup
\langle F\rangle
is equicontinuous,

2b.

Semigroup
\langle F\rangle
is uniformly Lipschitz:
\lip\langle F\rangle<\infty
, or

2c.

Generators
F
are non-expanding:
\lip F\leq 1
.

Then, closure semigroup
G:=\overline{\langle F\rangle}^{d_{\infty}}\subset C(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X})

is compact and equicontinuous, and
for all
\varepsilon>0
and all
k

N\left(B(k,F),\varepsilon\right)\leq N\left(G,\varepsilon\right)\quad(<\infty),

hence no dependence on
k
. Under assumption 1 the compactness of

\mathcal{X}
is not needed: total boundedness of
\langle F\rangle
alone
gives
N(B(k,F),\varepsilon)\leq N(\langle F\rangle,\varepsilon)<\infty
for all
k
.

Proof.

If
\mathcal{X}
is compact, then
C(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X})
is complete in
d_{\infty}
.
Thus the closure of any precompact subset is compact. By the compact
self-map version of Ascoli’s theorem (see the remark on the uniform metric above), a family
in
C(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X})
is precompact in
d_{\infty}
if and only if it is
equicontinuous. Assumption 1 gives compactness of
G
directly, and
Assumption 2a gives it by Arzelà–Ascoli. Assumptions 2b and 2c imply
2a, since a uniform Lipschitz bound is a common modulus of continuity
and non-expanding generators generate a non-expanding semigroup.
Trivial inclusion
B(k,F)\subseteq G
yields the bound, and compactness of
G
gives finiteness of
N(G,\varepsilon)
.
∎

Condition P1’

(Contraction to an invariant set)
.

Assume

(1)

(uniform contraction)
\sup_{f\in F}\lip(f)\leq c
for some
0<c<1
,

(2)

(invariant set) there exists a nonempty
F
-invariant set
A\subset\mathcal{X}
(i.e.,
f(A)\subset A
for all
f\in F
), and

(3)

(bounded absorbing set) there exist
L\in\mathbb{N}
and a bounded set
K\subset\mathcal{X}
such that
f(\mathcal{X})\subset K
for all depth-
L
maps
f\in F^{L}
.

Then for every
\varepsilon>0
, with

m(\varepsilon):=L+\left\lceil\log_{1/c}\left(\tfrac{2\diam(K)}{\varepsilon}\right)\right\rceil,

for
every

k\geq 0
,

N\left(B(k,F),d_{\infty},\varepsilon\right)\leq N\left(A,d,\tfrac{\varepsilon}{2}\right)+\sum_{\ell=0}^{m(\varepsilon)-1}N\left(F^{\ell},d_{\infty},\varepsilon\right),

an inequality in
\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
whose right-hand side is finite as
soon as
A
is totally bounded (e.g. compact) and each

N(F^{\ell},d_{\infty},\varepsilon)
,
\ell<m(\varepsilon)
, is finite (for instance when

F
is totally bounded in
d_{\infty}
, by sub-multiplicativity).
In particular, the right-hand side is
independent of
k
,
so growth in
k
saturates at any fixed
\varepsilon

to the entropy of the invariant set
A
.

Proof.

Pick any
a_{0}\in A
.
For any word
w\in F^{\ell}
,
we have
\lip(w)\leq c^{\ell}
and
w(a_{0})\in A
(invariance).
If
\ell\geq m(\varepsilon)
, then

\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}d\left(w(x),w(a_{0})\right)\leq c^{\ell-L}\diam(K),\quad\text{(bounded absorbing set)}

and the right-hand side is
\leq\varepsilon/2
, i.e.

d_{\infty}\left(w,\mathrm{const}_{w(a_{0})}\right)\leq\varepsilon/2.

Hence all sufficiently deep words,
i.e.
\bigcup_{\ell\geq m(\varepsilon)}F^{\ell}
,
are covered by the
\varepsilon/2
-thickening of the set of constant maps landing in
A
:

{\mathrm{const}_{q}:q\in A}
.
These constants at resolution
\varepsilon/2
are parameterized by an

\varepsilon/2
-net of
A
, giving the bound
N(A,\varepsilon/2)
.
For the finitely many shallow layers
\ell<m(\varepsilon)
, simply cover
each
F^{\ell}
at scale
\varepsilon
and sum over
\ell
. These terms are
independent of
k
; in the usual compact-generator setting they are
finite by continuity of composition and compactness/totally boundedness
of
F
. Since
B(k,F)=\bigcup_{\ell\leq k}F^{\ell}
, the two covers
together cover
B(k,F)
for every
k\geq 0
(for
k<m(\varepsilon)
only the
shallow part is used), which proves the bound for all
k
. Neither
compactness of
A
nor of
K
was used, only invariance and
boundedness.
∎

Example 3

(Cantor attractor (compact, free, contractive))
.

Let
\mathcal{X}=[0,1]
and
F={f_{0}(x)=x/3,f_{1}(x)=(x+2)/3}
. Then

\sup\lip(f_{i})=1/3=c<1
. The attractor
A
is the middle–third Cantor set
C
(compact,
F
-invariant).
By
Condition

P1’
(with
L=0
,
K=[0,1]
), for every
\varepsilon>0

and every
k
,

N\left(B(k,F),\varepsilon\right)\leq N\left(C,\varepsilon/2\right)+\sum_{\ell<m(\varepsilon)}N(F^{\ell},\varepsilon),\qquad m(\varepsilon)=\lceil\log_{3}(2/\varepsilon)\rceil,

independent of
k
. (Indeed, every
w\in F^{\ell}
has the
form
w(x)=3^{-\ell}x+b_{w}
and hence is
\varepsilon/2
-close to the
constant map
x\mapsto b_{w}\in C
.)

Remarks.

(1) When
\mathcal{X}
is compact, one may take
A=K=\mathcal{X}
and
L=0
in

Condition

P1’
.
(2) A single saturating map
\sigma
, such as
\tanh
, the logistic
function, or clipping, provides the bounded absorbing set

K=\sigma(\mathcal{X})
with
L=1
.
(3) Deep words are
almost constant
(their images have diameter
\leq c^{\ell-L}\diam(K)
),so at scale
\varepsilon
only the landing point
w(a_{0})\in A
matters; hence the dependence on
k
disappears once
\ell
exceeds an
\varepsilon
-dependent “memory length”
m(\varepsilon)
.

F.2
Polynomial growth under nilpotent control (P2)

The next condition covers layer classes that are not contractive but whose
compositions are organized by a group of polynomial growth. It gives the
fixed-scale bound
N(B(k,F),d_{\infty},\varepsilon)\lesssim(1+k/\varepsilon)^{D}
and the
diameter bound
D_{k}(S)\lesssim k
used in
Proposition

5
(ii) and
(iv).

Condition P2

(Nilpotent control gives polynomial growth)
.

Let
(H,d_{H})
be a compactly generated group with identity
e
, and
assume the length
g\mapsto d_{H}(e,g)
is subadditive:

d_{H}(e,gh)\leq d_{H}(e,g)+d_{H}(e,h)
.
Assume that its metric balls have polynomial entropy of degree
D\geq 0
:
there is
1\leq C_{H}<\infty
such that for all
R\geq 0
and
\delta>0
,

N\bigl(B_{H}(e,R),d_{H},\delta\bigr)\leq C_{H}\left(1+\frac{R}{\delta}\right)^{D}

(in particular these covering numbers are finite).
Suppose
H
acts on
\mathcal{X}
through a homomorphism

\alpha:H\to C(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X})
, and suppose there is a bounded set

S\subset H
such that
F\subset\alpha(S)
. Finally assume the
orbit map is Lipschitz in the uniform metric:
there is
0<L_{\alpha}<\infty
such that for all
g,h\in H
,

d_{\infty}\bigl(\alpha(g),\alpha(h)\bigr)\leq L_{\alpha}d_{H}(g,h).

Then, with
R_{S}:=\sup_{s\in S}d_{H}(e,s)
and

C:=C_{H}\max(1,R_{S}L_{\alpha})^{D}
, for every
\varepsilon>0
and every

k\geq 0
,

N\left(B(k,F),d_{\infty},\varepsilon\right)\leq C\left(1+\frac{k}{\varepsilon}\right)^{D},

and moreover
\diam_{\infty}B(k,F)\leq 2L_{\alpha}R_{S}k
, hence

D_{k}(S)\leq 2L_{\alpha}R_{S}k
for every sample
S
.

Proof.

Let
R_{S}:=\sup_{s\in S}d_{H}(e,s)<\infty
. Any composition of at most

k
maps from
F
has a representative
\alpha(g)
with

g=s_{k}\cdots s_{1}
and
s_{i}\in S
. By subadditivity,

d_{H}(e,g)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{k}d_{H}(e,s_{i})\leq kR_{S}.

Thus

B(k,F)\subset\alpha\bigl(B_{H}(e,kR_{S})\bigr).

The
L_{\alpha}
-Lipschitz bound on
\alpha
gives

N\left(B(k,F),d_{\infty},\varepsilon\right)\leq N\left(B_{H}(e,kR_{S}),d_{H},\varepsilon/L_{\alpha}\right).

The assumed polynomial entropy bound on
H
gives

C_{H}(1+kR_{S}L_{\alpha}/\varepsilon)^{D}\leq C_{H}\max(1,R_{S}L_{\alpha})^{D}(1+k/\varepsilon)^{D}
,
which is the claim (for
k=0
as well). The diameter bound is proved in remark (2) below.
∎

Example 4

(Upper-triangular unipotent group (non-compact, nilpotent))
.

Let

H=UT_{d}(\mathbb{R})=\{I_{d}+N:\ N\text{ is strictly upper triangular}\}

with a right-invariant Riemannian metric, and let
\mathcal{X}=H
. Let

\alpha(g)
be left translation,
\alpha(g)(x)=gx
. Then

d_{\infty}(\alpha(g),\alpha(h))=d_{H}(g,h),

because
d_{H}
is right-invariant. For any compact
S\subset H
and

F=\alpha(S)
,
Condition

P2
gives

N(B(k,F),d_{\infty},\varepsilon)\lesssim(1+k/\varepsilon)^{D},\qquad D=\frac{d(d-1)(d+1)}{6}.

Each generator
\alpha(g)
,
x\mapsto gx
, is a linear map of the
ambient matrix space
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, so by

Proposition

6
(c) every word in
B(k,F)
is computed exactly by
a ReLU network of depth
k
and width
2d^{2}
; no approximation of the
transitions, and hence no exponential width, is needed to implement this
example.

Remarks.

(1) The Guivarc’h–Bass homogeneous dimension
bridges nilpotency with polynomial volume and entropy growth.
Connected simply connected nilpotent Lie groups with left-invariant
Riemannian metrics, and finitely generated nilpotent groups with word
metrics, satisfy the displayed entropy assumption with this dimension.
See
Breuillard (2014)
and
de la Harpe (2000, Chapter VII)
for the growth theory of nilpotent groups used here.
(2) The inclusion
B(k,F)\subset\alpha(B_{H}(e,kR_{S}))
in the proof also
controls the diameter of the word ball: for
g,h\in B_{H}(e,kR_{S})
,

d_{\infty}(\alpha(g),\alpha(h))\leq L_{\alpha}d_{H}(g,h)\leq 2L_{\alpha}R_{S}k
, so

\diam_{\infty}(B(k,F))\leq 2L_{\alpha}R_{S}k
and a fortiori

D_{k}(S)\leq 2L_{\alpha}R_{S}k
. On a compact state space the diameter is of
course bounded by
\diam(\mathcal{X})
. These layerwise bounds are the input to

Proposition

5
(ii) and (iv).

F.3
Exponential growth under separation (E1, E1’, E2)

The conditions below give the lower bound
N(B(k,F),d_{\infty},\varepsilon)\geq r^{k}

for a class of
r
generators at a fixed resolution. All of them are
proved with
Lemma

7
: one exhibits, for each depth,

r^{k}
compositions whose values at suitable probe points are uniformly
separated. E1 and E1’ use one probe point for all words; E2 uses one probe
per word and applies on compact state spaces where the maps expand on
part of the space and reset elsewhere. With a bounded diameter these
conditions give the
\sqrt{k}
profile of
Proposition

5
(iii), and

Corollary

3
shows that the profile is attained when the separation
is visible at the output layer.

Condition E1

(Free semigroup with one-point uniform separation)
.

Let
F=\{f_{1},\dots,f_{r}\}\subset C(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X})
with
r\geq 2
. Assume:

1.

(Uniform separation at a base point) There exist

x_{\ast}\in\mathcal{X}
and
\delta>0
such that for all
k
and all
distinct words
u,v\in[r]^{k}
, writing

f_{u}:=f_{i_{k}}\circ\cdots\circ f_{i_{1}}
for
u=(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})
,

d\big(f_{u}(x_{\ast}),f_{v}(x_{\ast})\big)\geq\delta
.

Then, for every
k
and every
\varepsilon<\delta/2
,

N\big(B(k,F),d_{\infty},\varepsilon\big)\geq r^{k}.

In particular
u\mapsto f_{u}
is injective on
[r]^{k}
for every
k

(freeness at each length), so no separate freeness hypothesis is needed.

Proof.

Apply
Lemma

7
with
P=\{x_{\ast}\}
. For each fixed

k
, the
r^{k}
words of length
k
give
r^{k}
maps whose images
at
x_{\ast}
are
\delta
-separated; hence for

\varepsilon<\delta/2
at least
r^{k}
balls are needed to cover

B(k,F)
. ∎

Condition E1’

(Uniform same-length coding)
.

Let
F=\{f_{1},\dots,f_{r}\}\subset C(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X})
with
r\geq 2
, and
suppose (uniform same-length separation) that there exist

x_{\ast}\in\mathcal{X}
and
c>0
such that, for all words
u\neq v

of the same length,

d\big(u(x_{\ast}),v(x_{\ast})\big)\geq c.

Then, for every
k
and every
\varepsilon<c/2
,

N\big(B(k,F),d_{\infty},\varepsilon\big)\geq r^{k}.

This is
Condition

E1
with
\delta=c
: neither isometry nor
freeness nor finiteness of
d_{\infty}
on
\langle F\rangle
is needed
for the lower bound. The isometric setting in which it is used below,

F\subset\iso(\mathcal{X})
with
d_{\infty}(g,h)<\infty
for

g,h\in\langle F\rangle
(as when
d
is bi-invariant,

d(axb,ayb)=d(x,y)
), only serves to make the uniform metric finite on
the semigroup.

Proof.

Apply
Lemma

7
with
P=\{x_{\ast}\}
, exactly as for

Condition

E1
. ∎

Example 5

(Free group with a bi-invariant metric (non-compact, free, isometric))
.

Let
\mathcal{X}=G=F_{r}

(r\geq 2)
. Let

S:=\left\{xa_{i}x^{-1},xa_{i}^{-1}x^{-1}\;\middle|\;x\in F_{r},i=1,\dots,r\right\}

and define the
conjugacy-invariant
word metric

d_{S}(g,h):=|g^{-1}h|_{S},

the shortest length in the alphabet
S
. This metric is

bi-invariant
, hence all left translations
L_{g}
are
isometries and, crucially,

d_{\infty}(L_{g},L_{h})=\sup_{x}d_{S}(gx,hx)=\sup_{x}d_{S}\big(e,x^{-1}g^{-1}hx\big)=d_{S}(e,g^{-1}h)<\infty

(the supremum is independent of
x
).

Let
F=\{L_{a_{1}},\dots,L_{a_{r}}\}
. The positive semigroup is free.
With
x_{\ast}=e
, for words
u\neq v
of the same length,

d_{S}\big(L_{u}(x_{\ast}),L_{v}(x_{\ast})\big)=d_{S}(u,v)=|u^{-1}v|_{S}\geq 1,

so
Condition

E1’
applies with
c=1
: for every
\varepsilon<1/2
,

N\big(B(k,F),d_{\infty},\varepsilon\big)\geq r^{k}.

With the standard word metric instead,
d_{\infty}(L_{g},L_{h})=\infty
for
g\neq h
; the lower bound would still hold, since only the probe
x_{\ast}=e
is used, but the bi-invariant metric keeps
d_{\infty}
finite.

Ping-pong coding.

The classical
Ping-Pong
(
Table-Tennis
, or
Schottky
) lemma
(e.g.
de la Harpe, 2000
, Chapter II.B)
is a sufficient condition for freeness of generators, which itself does not immediately imply the exponential growth since the generators might be contractive. Therefore, the condition below states the uniform coding separation used in the entropy argument directly; the examples verify it through expanding branches and resets.

Here a
chamber

U_{i}\subset\mathcal{X}
is the active region for the
generator
f_{i}
. A
coding core

V_{i}\subset U_{i}
is a smaller
nonempty set on which
f_{i}
has enough coverage to realize the next
symbol. The
anchors
form a finite set
A=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{r}\}

of reset states; the points
a_{i}
need not be pairwise distinct unless
an example says so. A
marker

q\in\mathcal{X}
is the target state
for correctly coded trajectories, and it is required to be separated from
the anchor set. A
probe

x_{u}\in\mathcal{X}
is an input point chosen
for a word
u
; it is constructed from the chambers and is not fixed
across all words.

Condition E2

(Ping-pong coding)
.

Let
F=\{f_{1},\dots,f_{r}\}\subset C(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X})
with
r\geq 2
.
Assume that there exist sets
V_{i}\subset U_{i}\subset\mathcal{X}
,
anchors
a_{i}\in\mathcal{X}
, a marker
q\in\mathcal{X}
, and a constant

\alpha>0
such that, with
A:=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{r}\}
and

Q:=\{q\}\cup\bigcup_{j=1}^{r}V_{j},

the following conventions and conditions hold.
The marker
q
is not an anchor, quantitatively enforced by item 4
below. No global location assumption such as
A\subset\bigcup_{i}U_{i}

or
A\cap\bigcup_{i}U_{i}=\emptyset
is imposed; anchors may lie inside
or outside chambers. The required anchor condition is the invariance in
item 3.

1.

(Disjoint chambers)

U_{i}\cap U_{j}=\emptyset\qquad(i\neq j).

2.

(Full coding cores) For every
i
,

Q\subset f_{i}(V_{i})

(in particular each
V_{i}
is nonempty, since
q\in f_{i}(V_{i})
).

3.

(Reset and anchor invariance) For every
i
,

f_{i}(\mathcal{X}\setminus U_{i})=\{a_{i}\},\qquad f_{i}(A)\subset A.

4.

(Marker–anchor separation)

\dist(\{q\},A)\geq\alpha.

Then, for every
k
and every
\varepsilon<\alpha/2
,

N\big(B(k,F),d_{\infty},\varepsilon\big)\geq r^{k}.

Moreover, the map
u\mapsto f_{u}
is injective on words of each fixed
length.

Proof.

Fix
k
and a word
u=i_{k}\cdots i_{1}\in[r]^{k}
. Set
z_{k}=q
.
Since
Q\subset f_{i_{s}}(V_{i_{s}})
, we may choose recursively

z_{s-1}\in V_{i_{s}}
such that

f_{i_{s}}(z_{s-1})=z_{s},\qquad s=k,k-1,\dots,1.

Put
x_{u}:=z_{0}
. Then
f_{u}(x_{u})=q
. Now let

v=j_{k}\cdots j_{1}\neq u
, and let
t
be the first index, in the
order of application, with
j_{t}\neq i_{t}
. The first
t-1
steps
agree, so the
v
-trajectory is at
z_{t-1}\in V_{i_{t}}\subset U_{i_{t}}
.
Since the chambers are disjoint,
z_{t-1}\notin U_{j_{t}}
. Hence

f_{j_{t}}
resets this trajectory to
a_{j_{t}}\in A
, and all later
generators keep it in
A
by anchor invariance. Therefore

d\big(f_{u}(x_{u}),f_{v}(x_{u})\big)\geq\dist(\{q\},A)\geq\alpha.

Thus, for
P_{k}:=\{x_{u}\mid u\in[r]^{k}\}
, the
r^{k}
evaluation vectors

\{\eval_{P_{k}}(f_{u})\mid u\in[r]^{k}\}
are pairwise
\alpha
-separated.
The claim follows from
Lemma

7
.
∎

Example 6

(Ping-pong over the full shift (compact, expansive))
.

Fix an alphabet
\mathcal{A}=[r]\cup\{\bullet\}
with
r\geq 2

active
symbols
[r]
and one extra
padding
symbol
\bullet
.
Let

\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}=\{x=(x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},\dots)\mid x_{j}\in\mathcal{A}\}

be the (one-sided) full shift. Equip
\mathcal{X}
with the standard
ultrametric
d_{\theta}
for some fixed
\theta\in(0,1)
:

d_{\theta}(x,y)=\begin{cases}0&x=y\\
\theta^{\min\{n\geq 0\mid x_{n}\neq y_{n}\}}&x\neq y\end{cases}

Then
(\mathcal{X},d_{\theta})
is compact, totally disconnected, and the left
shift
\sigma(x)_{n}=x_{n+1}
is
L
-Lipschitz with

L=\theta^{-1}>1
(hence expansive).

For each
a\in\mathcal{A}
, write the clopen 1-cylinder

[\![a]\!]=\{x\in\mathcal{X}\mid x_{0}=a\}
, and the
anchor
sequence

\bar{a}=(a,a,a,\dots)
.

Define
F=\{f_{1},\dots,f_{r}\}\subset C(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X})
by

f_{a}(x)=\begin{cases}\sigma(x)&x\in[\![a]\!]\\[5.69054pt]
\bar{a}&x\notin[\![a]\!]\end{cases}\qquad a\in[r].

Because
[\![a]\!]
is clopen, each
f_{a}
is continuous; on
[\![a]\!]
it
is
\sigma
(Lipschitz constant
\theta^{-1}>1
), and on

\mathcal{X}\setminus[\![a]\!]
it is constant. Thus each
f_{a}
has an
expanding branch, although it is not globally expanding because of the
reset branch.

Let
U_{a}:=V_{a}:=[\![a]\!]
(clopen chambers and coding cores),

a\in[r]
, let
A:=\{\bar{a}\mid a\in[r]\}
, and let

q:=\bar{\bullet}
. Here
q\notin\bigcup_{a\in[r]}U_{a}
, while each
anchor
\bar{a}
lies in its corresponding chamber
U_{a}
. Then:

•

Pairwise separation. If
a\neq b
, then for any

x\in U_{a}
,
y\in U_{b}
,
d_{\theta}(x,y)=\theta^{0}=1
. Hence

\dist(U_{a},U_{b})=1
.

•

Expansion/coverage on own domain. For each
a
,

f_{a}|_{U_{a}}=\sigma
maps
U_{a}
bijectively onto
\mathcal{X}
(surjective
and expanding).

•

Reset off domain. On
\mathcal{X}\setminus U_{a}
,
f_{a}=\const_{\bar{a}}

(exact reset; the “reset diameter” is
0
).

•

Anchor invariance and marker separation. For every
a\in[r]
,

f_{a}(A)\subset A
, and
\dist(\{q\},A)=1
.

These data yield
Condition

E2
with

\alpha=1
(the chambers are even at distance
1
). Hence the
separation scale can be taken as
\delta=1
.

Let a word
u=a_{k}\cdots a_{1}\in[r]^{k}
. Define the

tail-padded probe

x_{u}:=\big(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{k},\underbrace{\bullet,\bullet,\bullet,\dots}_{\text{all }\bullet}\big)\in\mathcal{X}.

Then, by construction,

f_{u}(x_{u})=\sigma^{k}(x_{u})=\bar{\bullet},

because each of the first
k
steps sees the correct chamber and
applies
\sigma
, peeling off the
k
-letter prefix and revealing
the all-
\bullet
tail.

If
v=b_{k}\cdots b_{1}\neq u
, let
t
be the
rightmost
index
with
a_{t}\neq b_{t}
. When applying
f_{v}
to
x_{u}
, the first

t-1
letters match and act as shifts; at step
t
we apply

f_{b_{t}}
to a sequence whose
0
-coordinate is
a_{t}\neq b_{t}
, so

f_{b_{t}}
resets to the anchor
\bar{b_{t}}
. From then on, the state
never contains
\bullet
at the
0
-coordinate (subsequent resets
only use anchors
\bar{b_{s}}
with
b_{s}\in[r]
, and

\sigma
preserves
0
-coordinate
b_{s}
on the constant sequence

\bar{b_{s}}
). Consequently

f_{v}(x_{u})\neq\bar{\bullet}.

Therefore

d_{\theta}\big(f_{u}(x_{u}),f_{v}(x_{u})\big)=d_{\theta}\big(\bar{\bullet},f_{v}(x_{u})\big)=1.

This shows
freeness
: distinct words
u\neq v
define
distinct maps
f_{u}\neq f_{v}
.

Fix
k\in\mathbb{N}
and consider the finite probe set

P_{k}:=\{x_{u}\mid u\in[r]^{k}\}\subset\mathcal{X},

of cardinality
r^{k}
. For any distinct
u,v
we have just seen
that at the coordinate
x_{u}
,

d_{\theta}\big(f_{u}(x_{u}),f_{v}(x_{u})\big)=1\geq\delta.

Thus the
r^{k}
vectors
{E_{P_{k}}(f_{u})}_{|u|=k}\subset\mathcal{X}^{P_{k}}

are pairwise
1
-separated in the max metric. By
Lemma

7
, for every
\varepsilon<\tfrac{1}{2}
,

N\big(B(k,F),d_{\infty},\varepsilon\big)\geq r^{k}.

Remarks.

Only the disjointness of the chambers is used in the exact-reset proof.
The interval and symbolic examples below satisfy the stronger positive
separation
\dist(U_{i},U_{j})\geq\Delta>0
, which is the robust form needed
if one allows approximate resets or interpolation collars.

F.4
Memory-preserving expansion (E3)

Expansion alone does not produce entropy growth beyond
r^{k}
, because
later layers may erase earlier distinctions. The construction below adds
a memory: each layer writes a symbol, shifts the stored symbols, and
expands them. The entropy then accumulates multiplicatively across layers,
and the two corollaries give
\log N\asymp k^{2}
and
\log N\asymp\lambda^{pk}
;
these are the two cases in which the layerwise bound of
Theorem

2
is
attained (
Appendix

G
).

Condition E3

(Memory-preserving expansion)
.

Let
E
be a normed space, let
G\subset E
be an arbitrary subset, and fix
\lambda>1
.
Set

X:=\ell_{\infty}(E)=\Bigl\{x=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots):\sup_{j\geq 1}\|x_{j}\|_{E}<\infty\Bigr\},

equipped with the bounded sup metric

d_{X}(x,y):=\sup_{j\geq 1}\min\{1,\|x_{j}-y_{j}\|_{E}\}.

We write
d_{\infty}
for the induced uniform metric on

C(X,X)
. This bounded state metric keeps the maps below at finite
uniform distance while preserving the small-scale expansion estimates
used in the lower bounds.
Define the reset map, the expander, and the writer family by

r(x):=0,\qquad A(x_{1},x_{2},\dots):=(\lambda x_{1},\lambda x_{2},\dots),

and, for each
u\in G
,

g_{u}(x_{1},x_{2},\dots):=(u,x_{1},x_{2},\dots).

Let

F:=\{r,A\}\cup\{g_{u}:u\in G\}.

For each
k\geq 1
and each
u=(u_{1},\dots,u_{k})\in G^{k}
, define the word

w_{u}:=A\circ g_{u_{k}}\circ A\circ g_{u_{k-1}}\circ\cdots\circ A\circ g_{u_{1}}\circ r.

Let

W_{k}:=\{w_{u}:u\in G^{k}\}\subset B(2k+1,F).

Then the following hold.

(i)
For every
x\in X
,

w_{u}(x)=(\lambda u_{k},\lambda^{2}u_{k-1},\dots,\lambda^{k}u_{1},0,0,\dots).

In particular, each
w_{u}
is a constant map.

(ii)
For
u=(u_{1},\dots,u_{k})
and
v=(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})
,

d_{\infty}(w_{u},w_{v})=\max_{1\leq j\leq k}\min\{1,\lambda^{k-j+1}\|u_{j}-v_{j}\|_{E}\}.

(iii)
For every
\varepsilon>0
,

N(W_{k},d_{\infty},\varepsilon)\leq\prod_{j=1}^{k}N\left(G,\|\cdot\|_{E},\frac{\varepsilon}{\lambda^{k-j+1}}\right),

and for every
0<\varepsilon<1/2
,

\prod_{j=1}^{k}M\left(G,\|\cdot\|_{E},\frac{2\varepsilon}{\lambda^{k-j+1}}\right)\leq N(W_{k},d_{\infty},\varepsilon).

Consequently, by monotonicity of the (external) covering number under

W_{k}\subset B(2k+1,F)
,

N(B(2k+1,F),d_{\infty},\varepsilon)\geq N(W_{k},d_{\infty},\varepsilon),

so the word-ball entropy inherits a multiplicative layer-by-layer growth law.

Proof.

We first prove (i). Since
r
is the constant-zero map, the input
x
is irrelevant after the first step. Starting from
0=(0,0,\dots)
,

g_{u_{1}}(0)=(u_{1},0,0,\dots),\qquad Ag_{u_{1}}(0)=(\lambda u_{1},0,0,\dots).

Applying
g_{u_{2}}
and then
A
gives

Ag_{u_{2}}Ag_{u_{1}}(0)=(\lambda u_{2},\lambda^{2}u_{1},0,0,\dots).

Iterating this computation yields

w_{u}(x)=(\lambda u_{k},\lambda^{2}u_{k-1},\dots,\lambda^{k}u_{1},0,0,\dots),

which proves (i).

Part (ii) now follows immediately from (i), because
w_{u}
and
w_{v}
are constant maps:

d_{\infty}(w_{u},w_{v})=\sup_{x\in X}d_{X}\bigl(w_{u}(x),w_{v}(x)\bigr)=\max_{1\leq j\leq k}\min\{1,\lambda^{k-j+1}\|u_{j}-v_{j}\|_{E}\}.

For (iii), define two metrics on
G^{k}
:

\rho_{k}(u,v):=\max_{1\leq j\leq k}\lambda^{k-j+1}\|u_{j}-v_{j}\|_{E},\qquad\bar{\rho}_{k}(u,v):=\max_{1\leq j\leq k}\min\{1,\lambda^{k-j+1}\|u_{j}-v_{j}\|_{E}\}.

By part (ii), the parameterization
u\mapsto w_{u}
is an isometric embedding of
(G^{k},\bar{\rho}_{k})
into
(C(X,X),d_{\infty})
, so

N(W_{k},d_{\infty},\varepsilon)=N(G^{k},\bar{\rho}_{k},\varepsilon).

For the upper bound, let
C_{j}
be an
\varepsilon/\lambda^{k-j+1}
-cover of
G
in
\|\cdot\|_{E}
.
Then the Cartesian product
C_{1}\times\cdots\times C_{k}
is an
\varepsilon
-cover of
(G^{k},\rho_{k})
, because for every

u=(u_{1},\dots,u_{k})
one can choose
c_{j}\in C_{j}
with

\|u_{j}-c_{j}\|_{E}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{\lambda^{k-j+1}}

for all
j
, and therefore

\rho_{k}(u,c)\leq\varepsilon,

and hence
\bar{\rho}_{k}(u,c)\leq\varepsilon
.
Hence

N(W_{k},d_{\infty},\varepsilon)=N(G^{k},\bar{\rho}_{k},\varepsilon)\leq\prod_{j=1}^{k}N\left(G,\|\cdot\|_{E},\frac{\varepsilon}{\lambda^{k-j+1}}\right).

For the lower bound, let
P_{j}
be a maximal
2\varepsilon/\lambda^{k-j+1}
-packing of
G
in
\|\cdot\|_{E}
.
Then for distinct points

u=(u_{1},\dots,u_{k}),\ v=(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})\in P_{1}\times\cdots\times P_{k},

there exists some
j
with
u_{j}\neq v_{j}
, and for that
j
,

\lambda^{k-j+1}\|u_{j}-v_{j}\|_{E}\geq 2\varepsilon.

Therefore

\rho_{k}(u,v)\geq 2\varepsilon,

and since
2\varepsilon<1
, also
\bar{\rho}_{k}(u,v)\geq 2\varepsilon
.
Thus
P_{1}\times\cdots\times P_{k}
is a
2\varepsilon
-packing of
(G^{k},\bar{\rho}_{k})
.
Any
\varepsilon
-cover must contain at least as many points as any
2\varepsilon
-packing, hence

N(W_{k},d_{\infty},\varepsilon)=N(G^{k},\bar{\rho}_{k},\varepsilon)\geq\prod_{j=1}^{k}M\left(G,\|\cdot\|_{E},\frac{2\varepsilon}{\lambda^{k-j+1}}\right).

Finally, since
W_{k}\subset B(2k+1,F)
and external covering numbers are
monotone under inclusion,

N(B(2k+1,F),d_{\infty},\varepsilon)\geq N(W_{k},d_{\infty},\varepsilon).

This completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 7

(Super-exponential growth)
.

Assume that
N(G,\|\cdot\|_{E},\delta)<\infty
for all
\delta>0

(automatic for compact
G
) and that there exist constants

c_{-},c_{+},\delta_{0}>0
such that, for all
0<\delta<\delta_{0}
,

c_{-}\log(1/\delta)\leq\log M(G,\|\cdot\|_{E},\delta)\leq\log N(G,\|\cdot\|_{E},\delta)\leq c_{+}\log(1/\delta).

Then for every fixed
\varepsilon
with
0<\varepsilon<1/2
and

\varepsilon<\lambda\delta_{0}/2
, and for all
k\geq 1
,

C_{1}k^{2}\leq\log N(W_{k},d_{\infty},\varepsilon)\leq C_{2}k^{2},\qquad C_{1}:=\frac{c_{-}\log\lambda}{2},\quad C_{2}:=c_{+}\bigl(\log\lambda-\log\varepsilon\bigr).

Consequently,

\log N(B(2k+1,F),d_{\infty},\varepsilon)\geq C_{1}k^{2},

so the word-ball covering number is at least
\exp(\Omega(k^{2}))
.

Proof.

By
Condition

E3
,

\log N(W_{k},d_{\infty},\varepsilon)\geq\sum_{j=1}^{k}\log M\left(G,\|\cdot\|_{E},\frac{2\varepsilon}{\lambda^{k-j+1}}\right),

and

\log N(W_{k},d_{\infty},\varepsilon)\leq\sum_{j=1}^{k}\log N\left(G,\|\cdot\|_{E},\frac{\varepsilon}{\lambda^{k-j+1}}\right).

Since
0<\varepsilon<\lambda\delta_{0}/2
and
\lambda>1
, every radius

2\varepsilon/\lambda^{k-j+1}\leq 2\varepsilon/\lambda
and

\varepsilon/\lambda^{k-j+1}
lies below
\delta_{0}
; note that the
j=k

radius does not shrink with
k
, so the smallness of
\varepsilon
cannot
be traded for largeness of
k
. Moreover
G\neq\emptyset
by the packing
lower bound. Hence the assumed entropy bounds apply to every factor, and
with

\sum_{j=1}^{k}\log\left(\frac{\lambda^{k-j+1}}{a}\right)=(\log\lambda)\frac{k(k+1)}{2}+k\log(1/a)\qquad(a>0)

we obtain, using
2\varepsilon<1
for the lower bound and
k+1\leq 2k
,

k\leq k^{2}
for the upper bound,

\frac{c_{-}\log\lambda}{2}\,k^{2}\leq\log N(W_{k},d_{\infty},\varepsilon)\leq c_{+}\bigl(\log\lambda+\log(1/\varepsilon)\bigr)k^{2}.

∎

Corollary 8

(Double-exponential growth)
.

Assume that
N(G,\|\cdot\|_{E},\delta)<\infty
for all
\delta>0
and
that there exist constants
p>0
,
c_{-},c_{+},\delta_{0}>0
such that, for all
0<\delta<\delta_{0}
,

c_{-}\delta^{-p}\leq\log M(G,\|\cdot\|_{E},\delta)\leq\log N(G,\|\cdot\|_{E},\delta)\leq c_{+}\delta^{-p}.

Then for every fixed
\varepsilon
with
0<\varepsilon<1/2
and

\varepsilon<\lambda\delta_{0}/2
, and for all
k\geq 1
,

C_{1}\lambda^{pk}\leq\log N(W_{k},d_{\infty},\varepsilon)\leq C_{2}\lambda^{pk},\qquad C_{1}:=c_{-}(2\varepsilon)^{-p},\quad C_{2}:=c_{+}\varepsilon^{-p}\frac{q}{q-1},\quad q:=\lambda^{p}.

Consequently,

\log N(B(2k+1,F),d_{\infty},\varepsilon)\geq C_{1}\lambda^{pk},

i.e. the word-ball covering number is at least double-exponential in

k
.

Proof.

Again by
Condition

E3
,

\log N(W_{k},d_{\infty},\varepsilon)\geq\sum_{j=1}^{k}\log M\left(G,\|\cdot\|_{E},\frac{2\varepsilon}{\lambda^{k-j+1}}\right),

and

\log N(W_{k},d_{\infty},\varepsilon)\leq\sum_{j=1}^{k}\log N\left(G,\|\cdot\|_{E},\frac{\varepsilon}{\lambda^{k-j+1}}\right).

As in the proof of
Corollary

7
, all radii lie below
\delta_{0}
and

G\neq\emptyset
, so the entropy bounds apply to every factor:

c_{-}(2\varepsilon)^{-p}\sum_{r=1}^{k}q^{r}\leq\log N(W_{k},d_{\infty},\varepsilon)\leq c_{+}\varepsilon^{-p}\sum_{r=1}^{k}q^{r},\qquad q=\lambda^{p}>1.

Since
q^{k}\leq\sum_{r=1}^{k}q^{r}=q(q^{k}-1)/(q-1)\leq\frac{q}{q-1}q^{k}
, this
proves the claim.
∎

Example 7

(Hölder writers on a memory state space)
.

Let
E=C([0,1]^{d})
with the sup norm, let
0<\alpha\leq 1
, and define

G:=\Bigl\{u\in C^{\alpha}([0,1]^{d}):\|u\|_{\infty}+[u]_{C^{\alpha}}\leq 1\Bigr\}.

Set
X=\ell_{\infty}(E)
and define
r
,
A
, and
g_{u}
exactly as in
Condition

E3
. Then there exist constants
c_{-},c_{+},\delta_{0}>0
such that, for all
0<\delta<\delta_{0}
,

c_{-}\delta^{-d/\alpha}\leq\log M(G,\|\cdot\|_{\infty},\delta)\leq\log N(G,\|\cdot\|_{\infty},\delta)\leq c_{+}\delta^{-d/\alpha}.

Consequently, for every fixed
0<\varepsilon<\min\{1/2,\lambda\delta_{0}/2\}
,

\log N(W_{k},d_{\infty},\varepsilon)=\Theta\bigl(\lambda^{(d/\alpha)k}\bigr).

In particular,

N(B(2k+1,F),d_{\infty},\varepsilon)\geq\exp\Bigl(\Omega\bigl(\lambda^{(d/\alpha)k}\bigr)\Bigr),

so the word-ball covering number is at least double-exponential in the depth parameter
k
.

Proof.

The displayed entropy estimate is the standard metric entropy estimate for the unit Hölder ball in the sup norm.
Apply Corollary
8
with
p=d/\alpha
.
∎

Appendix G
The layerwise covering bound

This appendix proves the layerwise covering bound of

Section

4
, derives the profiles stated after it, and
records the elementary bound on the diameter of the reachable states that
accompanies it. Throughout,
F\subset C(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X})
consists of

\Lambda
-Lipschitz maps with
\Lambda\geq 0
, and

S_{m}(\Lambda):=\sum_{i=0}^{m-1}\Lambda^{i}
(
S_{0}=0
,
S_{1}=1
) is the
geometric sum of
Proposition

6
(b). Covering numbers are
external (
Section

2
).

G.1
Statement and proof

Theorem 9

(
Theorem

2
, restated)
.

For every
k\geq 0
and
\varepsilon>0
,

N\bigl(B(k,F),d_{\infty},\varepsilon\bigr)\leq 1+\sum_{m=1}^{k}N\bigl(F,d_{\infty},\varepsilon/S_{m}(\Lambda)\bigr)^{m}\leq 1+k\,N\bigl(F,d_{\infty},\varepsilon/S_{k}(\Lambda)\bigr)^{k},

(19)

where the last inequality uses that
S_{m}(\Lambda)\leq S_{k}(\Lambda)
for

m\leq k
(for
k=0
both sides equal
1
). The centres of the covers
of
F
need not belong to
F
nor be Lipschitz; only the Lipschitz
constant of the maps in
F
is used, and
F=\emptyset
is allowed.

Proof.

B(k,F)=\{\id\}\cup\bigcup_{m=1}^{k}F^{m}
, where
F^{m}
is the set of
compositions of exactly
m
maps of
F
, and covering numbers are
subadditive under unions. Fix
1\leq m\leq k
and let

\{c_{1},\dots,c_{M}\}\subset C(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X})
be an

\varepsilon/S_{m}(\Lambda)
-cover of
F
in
d_{\infty}
with

M=N(F,d_{\infty},\varepsilon/S_{m}(\Lambda))
. For
f_{m}\circ\cdots\circ f_{1}\in F^{m}

choose indices with
d_{\infty}(f_{j},c_{i_{j}})\leq\varepsilon/S_{m}(\Lambda)
. The
telescoping estimate in the proof of
Proposition

6
(b), applied
with the abstract layers
f_{j}
and the implemented layers
c_{i_{j}}
,
gives

d_{\infty}\bigl(f_{m}\circ\cdots\circ f_{1},\;c_{i_{m}}\circ\cdots\circ c_{i_{1}}\bigr)\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{S_{m}(\Lambda)}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\Lambda^{m-j}=\varepsilon;

that estimate uses only
\lip(f_{j})\leq\Lambda
. Hence the
M^{m}

compositions
c_{i_{m}}\circ\cdots\circ c_{i_{1}}
form an
\varepsilon
-cover of

F^{m}
, which proves the first inequality. For the second,
m\leq k

implies
S_{m}(\Lambda)\leq S_{k}(\Lambda)
, hence

\varepsilon/S_{k}(\Lambda)\leq\varepsilon/S_{m}(\Lambda)
and

N(F,d_{\infty},\varepsilon/S_{m}(\Lambda))^{m}\leq N(F,d_{\infty},\varepsilon/S_{k}(\Lambda))^{k}

because covering numbers are nonincreasing in the radius and at least
1
.
∎

G.2
Profiles from the layerwise bound

Corollary 9

(Profiles from the layerwise bound)
.

Assume
D_{k}(S)\leq\overline{D}
for all
k
with
\overline{D}>0
, let

k\geq 1
, and assume
N(F,d_{\infty},\varepsilon)<\infty
for all
\varepsilon>0
.
Since
\mathsf{V}_{k}(S)
is formed with internal
d_{S}
-covering numbers
while the layerwise bound bounds external
d_{\infty}
-covering numbers, the
transfer
N(A,d_{S},\varepsilon)\leq N(A,d_{\infty},\varepsilon/2)
of

Appendix

H
is used, which is the origin of the factors
2

below.

(a)

(Parametric layers.) If
\log N(F,d_{\infty},\varepsilon)\leq p\log(C/\varepsilon)
for all

0<\varepsilon\leq C
, with
p\geq 0
and
C\geq\overline{D}
, then for

0<\varepsilon\leq C

\log N\bigl(B(k,F),d_{\infty},\varepsilon\bigr)\leq\log(k+1)+kp\bigl[\log(C/\varepsilon)+\log S_{k}(\Lambda)\bigr],

and, with
\Lambda_{+}:=\max\{1,\Lambda\}
and
S_{k}(\Lambda)\leq k\Lambda_{+}^{k}
,

\mathsf{V}_{k}(S)\leq\overline{D}\Bigl(\sqrt{\log(k+1)}+\sqrt{kp\log k}+k\sqrt{p\log\Lambda_{+}}+\sqrt{kp}\,\bigl(\sqrt{\log(2C/\overline{D})}+\tfrac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}\bigr)\Bigr).

(20)

Thus
\mathsf{V}_{k}(S)=O(\sqrt{kp\log(kC/\overline{D})})
if
\Lambda\leq 1

and
\mathsf{V}_{k}(S)=O(k\sqrt{p\log\Lambda})
if
\Lambda>1
.

(b)

(Nonparametric layers.) If
\log N(F,d_{\infty},\varepsilon)\leq c\,\varepsilon^{-q}
for
all
\varepsilon>0
, with
c\geq 0
and
0<q<2
, then

\log N(B(k,F),d_{\infty},\varepsilon)\leq\log(k+1)+kc\,S_{k}(\Lambda)^{q}\varepsilon^{-q}
for
all
\varepsilon>0
, and

\mathsf{V}_{k}(S)\leq\overline{D}\sqrt{\log(k+1)}+\sqrt{kc}\,\bigl(2S_{k}(\Lambda)\bigr)^{q/2}\,\frac{\overline{D}^{\,1-q/2}}{1-q/2}.

(21)

Thus
\mathsf{V}_{k}(S)=O(k^{(1+q)/2})
if
\Lambda\leq 1
and

\mathsf{V}_{k}(S)=O(k^{1/2}\Lambda^{qk/2})
if
\Lambda>1
.

Proof.

(a) For
0<\varepsilon\leq C
and
k\geq 1
we have

\varepsilon/S_{k}(\Lambda)\leq\varepsilon\leq C
, so the hypothesis applies at radius

\varepsilon/S_{k}(\Lambda)
and (
19
) gives

\log N(B(k,F),\varepsilon)\leq\log(1+kN^{k})\leq\log(k+1)+k\log N
with

N:=N(F,d_{\infty},\varepsilon/S_{k}(\Lambda))
,
\log N\leq p[\log(C/\varepsilon)+\log S_{k}(\Lambda)]
;
here
1+kN^{k}\leq(k+1)N^{k}
because
N\geq 1
. Since
\Lambda^{i}\leq\Lambda_{+}^{k}

for
i<k
,
S_{k}(\Lambda)\leq k\Lambda_{+}^{k}
, so

\log S_{k}(\Lambda)\leq\log k+k\log\Lambda_{+}
. Taking square roots with

\sqrt{a+b}\leq\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b}
,

\sqrt{\log N(B(k,F),\varepsilon)}\leq\sqrt{\log(k+1)}+\sqrt{kp\log k}+k\sqrt{p\log\Lambda_{+}}+\sqrt{kp}\sqrt{\log(C/\varepsilon)},

for every
0<\varepsilon\leq C
. Since

N(B(k,F),d_{S},\varepsilon)\leq N(B(k,F),d_{\infty},\varepsilon/2)
and
D_{k}(S)\leq\overline{D}
,

\mathsf{V}_{k}(S)
is bounded by the integral of the right-hand side at
radius
\varepsilon/2
over
(0,\overline{D}]
, where

\int_{0}^{\overline{D}}\sqrt{\log(2C/\varepsilon)}\,\mathrm{d}\varepsilon\leq\overline{D}\bigl(\sqrt{\log(2C/\overline{D})}+\sqrt{\pi}/2\bigr)
by

Lemma

9
applied to

\log(2C/\varepsilon)=\log(2C/\overline{D})+\log(\overline{D}/\varepsilon)
; this gives
(
20
).

(b) Now
\log N(B(k,F),\varepsilon)\leq\log(k+1)+kc\,(S_{k}(\Lambda)/\varepsilon)^{q}
for
all
\varepsilon>0
; at radius
\varepsilon/2
the second term is

kc(2S_{k}(\Lambda))^{q}\varepsilon^{-q}
, and

\int_{0}^{\overline{D}}\varepsilon^{-q/2}\,\mathrm{d}\varepsilon=\overline{D}^{\,1-q/2}/(1-q/2)

because
q<2
. The asymptotics follow from
S_{k}(\Lambda)\leq k
for

\Lambda\leq 1
and
S_{k}(\Lambda)\leq\Lambda^{k}/(\Lambda-1)
for

\Lambda>1
.
∎

G.3
Sharpness

The memory-preserving construction of
Condition

E3
attains both
layerwise bounds. Its layer class
F=\{r,A\}\cup\{g_{u}:u\in G\}
consists of

\lambda
-Lipschitz maps for the bounded metric
d_{X}
(the reset and the

1
-Lipschitz, and

\min\{1,\lambda a\}\leq\lambda\min\{1,a\}
for

), and

for

. If
G
has
finite-dimensional entropy,
\log N(G,\delta)\asymp\log(1/\delta)
, then
case (a) gives

, and

7

gives the matching lower bound
\gtrsim k^{2}
(its words

lie in

, and external covering numbers are monotone under inclusion).
If

, case (b) gives

, and
Corollary

8
gives

\gtrsim\lambda^{pk}
. Thus the layerwise bound is attained up to polynomial
factors, and no better general bound in terms of
N(F,\cdot)
and

\Lambda
alone is possible.

G.4
Diameter of the reachable states

On a bounded state space
D_{k}(S)\leq\diam(\mathcal{X})
for every
k
. On an
unbounded space the diameter can grow with the depth, and the growth is
again governed by
\Lambda
.

Lemma 8

(Reachable radius)
.

Let
x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}
,
R\geq 0
,
c\geq 0
, and suppose
d(f(x_{0}),x_{0})\leq c

for all
f\in F
. Then, with
\Lambda_{+}:=\max\{1,\Lambda\}
, every

g\in B(k,F)
maps the ball
B(x_{0},R)
into

B\bigl(x_{0},\Lambda_{+}^{k}R+c\,S_{k}(\Lambda)\bigr)
. Consequently, if

Appendix I
The four regimes and the balancing depths

This appendix defines the four regimes of
Section

5
, states
their balancing depths and balanced rates, and derives them.

I.1
Two laws and four regimes

The main text names two of the four combinations of bias and variance laws; here we treat all four. The approximation error
decays with depth
k
either exponentially or polynomially,

\displaystyle\mathsf{bias}(k)=\exp(\Theta(-\alpha k))\quad\text{or}\quad\Theta(k^{-\beta}),

with parameters
\alpha,\beta>0
; the letters E and P refer to these two
cases. The estimation term follows one of the two profiles produced by

Proposition

5
, root-logarithmic or root-polynomial growth,

\displaystyle\mathsf{var}(k,n)\lesssim\sqrt{\log(k)/n}\quad\text{or}\quad\sqrt{k^{\gamma}/n},

with parameter
\gamma>0
; the letters L and P refer to these two cases. The
root-logarithmic profile is case (ii) of
Proposition

5
, and it also
covers the saturated case (i), for which the true dependence is constant;
case (iii) is the root-polynomial profile with
\gamma=1
, and case (iv)
with
\gamma=2
. Combining the two bias laws with the two variance profiles
gives rise to the four typical regimes EL, EP, PL, and PP listed in
(
22
): the first letter describes the bias and the second the
variance. They are representative diagnostic cases rather than an
exhaustive list of all possible depth dependences; for instance, the
sub-exponential bias decay
\exp(-\Theta(\sqrt{k}))
of Picard-type neural
operators (
Appendix

J
) combined with a saturated or
root-logarithmic profile is called the SubEL regime there.

The balancing depths and balanced rates are the following, with the
derivations below:

\displaystyle\text{EL}\ (\mathsf{bias}=e^{-\alpha k},\ \mathsf{var}=\sqrt{\log k/n}):

\displaystyle k^{\ast}=\tfrac{1}{2\alpha}(\log n-\log\log\log n),

\displaystyle\mathsf{gen}(k^{\ast},n)\asymp\sqrt{\tfrac{\log\log n}{n}};

(22)

\displaystyle\text{EP}\ (\mathsf{bias}=e^{-\alpha k},\ \mathsf{var}=\sqrt{k^{\gamma}/n}):

\displaystyle k^{\ast}=\tfrac{1}{2\alpha}(\log n-\gamma\log\log n),

\displaystyle\mathsf{gen}(k^{\ast},n)\asymp\sqrt{\tfrac{(\log n)^{\gamma}}{(2\alpha)^{\gamma}n}};

\displaystyle\text{PL}\ (\mathsf{bias}=k^{-\beta},\ \mathsf{var}=\sqrt{\log k/n}):

\displaystyle k^{\ast}\sim\Bigl(\tfrac{2\beta n}{\log(2\beta n)}\Bigr)^{\frac{1}{2\beta}},

\displaystyle\mathsf{gen}(k^{\ast},n)\asymp\sqrt{\tfrac{\log n}{2\beta n}};

\displaystyle\text{PP}\ (\mathsf{bias}=k^{-\beta},\ \mathsf{var}=\sqrt{k^{\gamma}/n}):

\displaystyle k^{\ast}\asymp n^{\frac{1}{2\beta+\gamma}},

\displaystyle\mathsf{gen}(k^{\ast},n)\asymp n^{-\frac{\beta}{2\beta+\gamma}}.

Lower-order terms in
k^{\ast}
are omitted in the EL and EP rows.

The worked examples are placed in these regimes in the summary list of

Appendix

J
.

I.2
Width and other one-layer parameters

Remark 3

(Width and other one-layer parameters)
.

The display (
22
) fixes the hidden-layer class
F
and
the output-layer class
H
and varies only the depth. Width, and more generally any
parameter of a single layer, is not absent from the framework: if
F_{w}

denotes the admissible hidden layers at width
w
, then
w
enters the
one-layer covering numbers
N(F_{w},d_{\infty},\varepsilon)
, hence those of

B(k,F_{w})
through
Theorem

2
, where
p\asymp w^{2}
for a
fully connected layer, and possibly the output-layer class
H_{w}
, the
approximation term, and the implementation error
(
Section

J.3
quantifies the last route). Writing
\mathcal{H}_{k,w}=H_{w}\circ B(k,F_{w})
,
the balancing depth
k^{\ast}(n,w)
is obtained by minimizing the full bound
over
k
for each fixed
w
; the display does not assert that it is
independent of

, and its dependence on

I.3
The balancing principle

Throughout, we minimize the upper bound

\mathsf{gen}(k,n)\lesssim\mathsf{bias}(k)+\mathsf{var}(k,n)

over the depth
k
, treated as a positive real number and rounded to the
nearest integer at the end. Since
\mathsf{bias}(k)
is nonincreasing and

\mathsf{var}(k,n)
is nondecreasing in
k
, the minimizer is located where the
two terms are of the same order, and the balanced value is then of the order
of either term. The following lemma makes this precise.

Lemma 10

(Balancing principle)
.

Let
\mathsf{bias}\geq 0
be nonincreasing and
\mathsf{var}(\cdot,n)\geq 0
be nondecreasing
on a set
\mathcal{K}
of depths, and let
\mathsf{gen}(k,n):=\mathsf{bias}(k)+\mathsf{var}(k,n)
.
If
k_{0}\in\mathcal{K}
balances the two terms,
\mathsf{bias}(k_{0})=\mathsf{var}(k_{0},n)
,
then
\mathsf{gen}(k,n)\geq\mathsf{bias}(k_{0})
for every
k\in\mathcal{K}
, while

\mathsf{gen}(k_{0},n)=2\mathsf{bias}(k_{0})
; hence
k_{0}
minimizes the bound over

\mathcal{K}
up to the factor
2
.

Proof.

For
k\leq k_{0}
we have
\mathsf{gen}(k,n)\geq\mathsf{bias}(k)\geq\mathsf{bias}(k_{0})
, and for

k\geq k_{0}
we have
\mathsf{gen}(k,n)\geq\mathsf{var}(k,n)\geq\mathsf{var}(k_{0},n)=\mathsf{bias}(k_{0})
.
∎

In the PP regime the balancing equation is solved exactly, so

Lemma

10
applies verbatim. In the other three regimes the
displayed
k^{\ast}
balances the two terms only asymptotically; the balanced
rates are nevertheless two-sided, of the form
\Theta(\cdot)
, with
explicit constants. For all sufficiently large
n
, the EP rate lies
between
1
and
1+(2\alpha)^{-\gamma/2}
times

n^{-1/2}(\log n)^{\gamma/2}
, the EL rate between
1
and

1+\sqrt{1+|\log 2\alpha|}
times
\sqrt{\log\log n/n}
, and the PL rate
between
\sqrt{1/(4\beta)}
and
2\sqrt{(1+|\log 2\beta|)/(2\beta)}

times
\sqrt{\log n/n}
. If the two terms do not cross, the minimizer
lies at a boundary of
\mathcal{K}
; in the four regimes below they cross
for all large
n
.

I.4
Derivations by regime

I.4.1
EP: exponentially decaying bias, polynomially growing variance

Let
\mathsf{bias}(k)=e^{-\alpha k}
and
\mathsf{var}(k,n)=n^{-1/2}k^{\gamma/2}
with

\alpha,\gamma>0
. Taking logarithms, the two terms are of the same order
exactly when

\alpha k=\tfrac{1}{2}\log n-\tfrac{\gamma}{2}\log k+O(1).

As
n\to\infty
the solution satisfies
\log k=\log\log n+O(1)
, because

\log k\ll\log n
; substituting this into the right-hand side gives

k^{\ast}=\frac{1}{2\alpha}\bigl(\log n-\gamma\log\log n+O(1)\bigr).

Evaluating either term at
k^{\ast}
yields the balanced rate

\mathsf{gen}(k^{\ast},n)\asymp n^{-1/2}(\log n)^{\gamma/2},

more precisely
(2\alpha)^{-\gamma/2}n^{-1/2}(\log n)^{\gamma/2}
up to a
factor bounded above and below by positive constants.

I.4.2
EL: exponentially decaying bias, logarithmically growing variance

Let
\mathsf{bias}(k)=e^{-\alpha k}
and
\mathsf{var}(k,n)=\sqrt{\log k/n}
with

\alpha>0
. The two terms are of the same order exactly when

\alpha k=\tfrac{1}{2}\log n-\tfrac{1}{2}\log\log k+O(1).

Since
\log k=\log\log n+O(1)
at the solution, and hence

\log\log k=\log\log\log n+O(1)
, we obtain

k^{\ast}=\frac{1}{2\alpha}\bigl(\log n-\log\log\log n+O(1)\bigr),\qquad\mathsf{gen}(k^{\ast},n)\asymp\sqrt{\frac{\log\log n}{n}}.

I.4.3
PP: polynomially decaying bias, polynomially growing variance

Let
\mathsf{bias}(k)=k^{-\beta}
and
\mathsf{var}(k,n)=n^{-1/2}k^{\gamma/2}
with

\beta,\gamma>0
. The two terms are equal exactly when

k^{\beta+\gamma/2}=n^{1/2}
, that is, at

k^{\ast}=n^{1/(2\beta+\gamma)},\qquad\text{where}\qquad\mathsf{gen}(k^{\ast},n)\asymp n^{-\beta/(2\beta+\gamma)}.

Here the balancing equation is solved exactly, so
Lemma

10

shows that
k^{\ast}
minimizes the bound up to the factor
2
.

I.4.4
PL: polynomially decaying bias, logarithmically growing variance

Let
\mathsf{bias}(k)=k^{-\beta}
and
\mathsf{var}(k,n)=\sqrt{\log k/n}
with

\beta>0
. Squaring both terms, they are of the same order exactly when

k^{2\beta}\log k\asymp n
. The equation
k^{2\beta}\log k=n
is solved
by
k=\exp(W(2\beta n)/(2\beta))
, where
W
is the Lambert function.
The Lambert function can be avoided by taking

k^{\ast}:=\Bigl(\frac{2\beta n}{\log(2\beta n)}\Bigr)^{1/(2\beta)},\qquad\text{for which}\qquad{k^{\ast}}^{2\beta}\log k^{\ast}=n\Bigl(1-\frac{\log L}{L}\Bigr),\quad L:=\log(2\beta n),

so that
{k^{\ast}}^{2\beta}\log k^{\ast}/n\to 1
as
n\to\infty
. For this
choice,
\mathsf{bias}(k^{\ast})=\sqrt{L/(2\beta n)}
and, for
L\geq 1
,

\mathsf{var}(k^{\ast},n)=\sqrt{(L-\log L)/(2\beta n)}\leq\sqrt{L/(2\beta n)}
, so
that

\mathsf{gen}(k^{\ast},n)\asymp\sqrt{\frac{\log n}{2\beta n}}.

Ordering of the EP and PL rates.

The EP and PL rates in (
22
) are not uniformly ordered without
specifying
\gamma
: EP gives
n^{-1/2}(\log n)^{\gamma/2}
, whereas PL
gives
n^{-1/2}(\log n)^{1/2}
, up to constants. Thus EP is no worse
than PL for
\gamma\leq 1
, while PL is no worse than EP for
\gamma>1
.

J.1
Contractive teacher–student models

Setting.

A teacher-student setting refers to the scenario where the hypothesis class (student) and the target class (teacher) share the same compositional structure.
Let the depth-
k
student class be
\mathcal{H}_{k}:=H\circ B(k,F)
and take the target class to be the uniform closure of the infinitely deep compositional class,

\mathcal{C}:=\mathcal{H}_{\infty}:=H\circ\overline{\bigcup_{m\geq 0}B(m,F)}^{\,d_{\infty}}.

Assume the input space
\mathcal{X}
is bounded with
\diam(\mathcal{X})=D<\infty
; the output layer
H
is
1
-Lipschitz; and the intermediate-layer semigroup
F\subset C(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X})
is
contractive
, i.e., there exists
\lambda\in(0,1)
such that
\lip(f)\leq\lambda
for all
f\in F
. A teacher of depth less than
k
is represented exactly by
\mathcal{H}_{k}
. For a finite-depth teacher of depth at least
k
, write
c=h\circ u\circ v
with
h\in H
,
u\in F^{k}
, and
v\in B(\ell,F)
. Truncating the tail yields a depth-
k
student
h\circ u\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
that approximates
c
with error

\displaystyle\|h\circ u\circ v-h\circ u\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\lip(h)\lip(u)d_{\infty}(v,\mathrm{id})\leq 1\cdot\lambda^{k}\cdot D=D\lambda^{k},

since
\lip(u)\leq\lambda^{k}
and
d_{\infty}(v,\mathrm{id})\leq D
. Passing to the uniform closure gives the same bound for teachers in
\mathcal{H}_{\infty}
. Hence the approximation error decays exponentially:

\sup_{c\in\mathcal{H}_{\infty}}\inf_{h\in\mathcal{H}_{k}}\|c-h\|_{\infty}\leq D\lambda^{k}.

Moreover, under the compact-domain P1 condition, or under the non-compact
absorbing-set version P1’, the contractive transition family has saturated
word-ball entropy: the hidden contribution to the variance upper bound is
independent of
k
at fixed scale, and in the compact/totally bounded case
the whole Dudley integral is depth-independent (case (i) of

Proposition

5
). Thus this example is at least as favorable as the EL
proxy with root-logarithmic variance growth.

J.2
Picard-type neural operators

Setting and bias.

Furuya et al. (2025)
.

Furuya et al. (2025)

investigated the approximation error of Neural Operators (NOs) that learn the solution operator of nonlinear parabolic PDEs on a bounded domain
\mathcal{X}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
By aligning a single hidden layer of the NO with one step of the Picard iteration for the PDE, a textbook iterative argument for solving differential equations, they show that the approximation error decays at a
sub-exponential
rate of the form
O(\exp(-c\sqrt{k}))
in the network depth
k
, for a problem-dependent constant
c>0
.
Since Picard iteration is
contractive
and generated by a single operator, the resulting transition dynamics are naturally treated by P1 on compact state spaces, or by P1’ when the iteration has a compact absorbing set. In either case, the transition word-ball entropy saturates rather than merely growing polynomially.
The function space for approximation is a
mixed Lebesgue space

L_{t}^{r}L_{x}^{s}
,
which we can regard as the target class
\mathcal{C}
.
A finite-sample generalization analysis of this Picard-type setting, including finite-observation reconstruction errors and long-time rollout, is developed by
Taniguchi and Sonoda (2026)
.

This NO setting can be viewed as a concrete instance of the teacher-student framework discussed earlier: both the hypothesis class and the target class share a hierarchical (iterative) structure, enabling efficient depth-driven approximation. It thus exemplifies the
SubEL regime
of
Section

5
, sub-exponential bias decay with a saturated or root-logarithmic variance profile, in which depth is particularly advantageous. Together with the previous example, it suggests that depth is most effective when the underlying target structure, namely the target class
\mathcal{C}
, is hierarchical, as in deep compositions or differential equations.

J.3
The implementation layer

The implementation map in
Theorem

4
is the identity when the abstract class
is itself the implemented network class, and then
\varepsilon_{\text{imp}}(k)=0
. The
distinction becomes useful when a mathematically convenient transition model
is only approximated by a finite network, for instance when
\mathcal{H}_{k}
is a
class of polynomials or of smooth flows and
\mathcal{H}_{\text{imp}}
is a finite-width ReLU
family. The following statement gives three explicit implementation maps.

Proposition 6

(Implementation with controlled uniform error)
.

(a)

(Net-based.) Let
\mathcal{H}_{k}
be totally bounded in the uniform norm on

\mathcal{X}
, and let
\mathcal{A}
be a class of functions that is uniformly dense
on
\mathcal{H}_{k}
; for example, on a compact
\mathcal{X}
, one-hidden-layer
networks with a non-polynomial activation. For every
\varepsilon>0
there is a
finite implementation class
\mathcal{H}_{\text{imp}}^{\varepsilon}\subset\mathcal{A}
with

|\mathcal{H}_{\text{imp}}^{\varepsilon}|\leq N(\mathcal{H}_{k},\|\cdot\|_{\infty},\varepsilon/2)
and an
implementation map with
\varepsilon_{\text{imp}}(k)\leq\varepsilon
.

(b)

(Layerwise.) If every transition is
\Lambda
-Lipschitz, every output layer is

L_{H}
-Lipschitz, and each generator and output layer is implemented within
uniform error
\delta
and
\delta_{H}
, respectively
(
\Lambda,\delta,\delta_{H}\geq 0
), then composing the
implemented layers gives

\varepsilon_{\text{imp}}(k)\leq\delta_{H}+L_{H}\,\delta\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\Lambda^{i}
; this is

O(k\delta)
for non-expanding transitions and
O(\delta)
for contractive
ones.

(c)

(Exact.) If
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the transitions are affine, as for the
nilpotent matrix actions of
Condition

P2
, then each transition is one
ReLU layer of width
2d
via
x=\relu(x)-\relu(-x)
, every word in

B(k,F)
is a depth-
k
ReLU network of width
2d
, and
\varepsilon_{\text{imp}}(k)=0
.

Part (a) shows that the implementation class is generally a finite class that
does not contain
\mathcal{H}_{k}
, with an error that can be made arbitrarily
small at the price of a larger class; its size is controlled by the covering
number of the abstract class. Part (b) shows how a per-layer resolution

\delta
, for instance a width- or precision-dependent quantity, interacts
with depth: additively under layerwise stability and geometrically without
it. Part (c) answers the implementation question for the Heisenberg and
upper-triangular examples of
Appendix

F
: no
exponential width is needed, and the polynomial growth bounds of P2 apply to
a concrete ReLU network with zero implementation error. Quantitative
finite-width results on manifolds and symmetric spaces

(
Kratsios and Papon, 2022
;
Chen et al., 2019
;
Sonoda et al., 2022
;
Petersen and Voigtlaender, 2020
)
give
further examples of controlled
\varepsilon_{\text{imp}}
under additional assumptions on
the target and the region being approximated; we do not claim that P2 alone
yields an efficient implementation of every nilpotent action.

Width through the implementation error.

Part (b) makes the role of the width explicit. Suppose the abstract
hidden layers are
\Lambda
-Lipschitz maps whose coordinates belong to a
smoothness class on the bounded state set, and that each of them is
implemented by a block of width
w
with uniform accuracy
\delta(w)
;
for example
\delta(w)\asymp w^{-s/d}
for
C^{s}
layers on a bounded
subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}

(
DeVore et al., 1989
;
Yarotsky, 2017
)
, and
\delta(w)\asymp w^{-1/2}
in
L^{2}
for layers of bounded variation norm

(
Barron, 1993
;
Jones, 1992
;
Pisier, 1981
)
. Then, with a fixed output layer,

\varepsilon_{\text{imp}}(k)\leq\delta_{H}+L_{H}\,\delta(w)\,S_{k}(\Lambda),\qquad S_{k}(\Lambda)=\sum_{i<k}\Lambda^{i},

and the implementation error is below the estimation term
\mathsf{var}(k,n)
as
soon as
\delta(w)\leq\mathsf{var}(k,n)/(L_{H}S_{k}(\Lambda))
. For non-expanding
layers and the root-polynomial profile
\mathsf{var}(k,n)\asymp\sqrt{k/n}
this
requires
w\gtrsim(L_{H}\sqrt{kn})^{d/s}
: the width needed to realize a
depth-
k
abstract network without changing its regime grows polynomially
in
k
and
n
, and for expanding layers geometrically in
k
. Width
therefore enters the depth trade-off through two routes, the one-layer
covering number
p\asymp w^{2}
in
Theorem

2
and the per-layer
accuracy
\delta(w)
here, and both are explicit; a joint optimization
over
(k,w)
would balance the three terms
\mathsf{bias}(k)
,
\mathsf{var}(k,n)
, and

\beta_{\ell}\varepsilon_{\text{imp}}(k)
simultaneously, which we do not pursue.

Proof of
Proposition

6
.

We restate
Proposition

6
in slightly more general form and prove
it. The point of the statement is that the implementation class in

Theorem

4
is a genuine third object: it need not contain
\mathcal{H}_{k}
, and
the uniform implementation error can be traded against the size or the
resolution of the implementation.

Proposition 7

(
Proposition

6
, restated)
.

(a)

(Net-based implementation.) Let
\mathcal{H}_{k}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}
be totally
bounded in the uniform norm, and let
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{X}}
be
uniformly dense on
\mathcal{H}_{k}
: for every
g\in\mathcal{H}_{k}
and
\eta>0

there is
a\in\mathcal{A}
with
\|g-a\|_{\infty}\leq\eta
(no compactness of the
domain and no continuity is needed; on a compact
K
any dense

\mathcal{A}\subset C(K)
qualifies). Then for every
\varepsilon>0
there
exist a finite class
\mathcal{H}_{\text{imp}}^{\varepsilon}\subset\mathcal{A}
with

|\mathcal{H}_{\text{imp}}^{\varepsilon}|\leq N(\mathcal{H}_{k},\|\cdot\|_{\infty},\varepsilon/2)
and an implementation map

\embed:\mathcal{H}_{k}\to\mathcal{H}_{\text{imp}}^{\varepsilon}
such that

\sup_{g\in\mathcal{H}_{k}}\|g-\embed(g)\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon
.

(b)

(Layerwise implementation.) Let
F\subset C(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{X})
with

\lip(f)\leq\Lambda
for all
f\in F
(
\Lambda\geq 0
), and let
H\subset C(\mathcal{X})
with

\lip(h)\leq L_{H}
for all
h\in H
. Suppose every
f\in F
is assigned
an implemented map
\tilde{f}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
with
d_{\infty}(f,\tilde{f})\leq\delta

(
\delta\geq 0
)
and every
h\in H
an implemented
\tilde{h}:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
with

\|h-\tilde{h}\|_{\infty}\leq\delta_{H}
. For each element of
\mathcal{H}_{k}
fix one

with
m\leq k
and define

\embed(h\circ f_{m}\circ\cdots\circ f_{1}):=\tilde{h}\circ\tilde{f}_{m}\circ\cdots\circ\tilde{f}_{1}
.
Then

\varepsilon_{\text{imp}}(k)\leq\delta_{H}+L_{H}\,\delta\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\Lambda^{i},

which is at most
\delta_{H}+L_{H}k\delta
if
\Lambda\leq 1
and at most

\delta_{H}+L_{H}\delta/(1-\Lambda)
if
\Lambda<1
.

(c)

(Exact implementation of affine transitions.) If
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
and every

f\in F
is affine,
f(x)=Ax+b
, then each
f
is realized exactly by
one ReLU layer of width
2d
, and every element of
B(k,F)
by a ReLU
network of depth
k
and width
2d
. With a linear output-layer class,

\mathcal{H}_{k}\subset\mathcal{H}_{\text{imp}}
for the corresponding ReLU network class and

\varepsilon_{\text{imp}}(k)=0
.

Proof.

(a) Let
\{g_{1},\dots,g_{M}\}\subset\mathcal{H}_{k}
be an
\varepsilon/2
-net of

\mathcal{H}_{k}
in
\|\cdot\|_{\infty}
with centres in
\mathcal{H}_{k}
and of
minimal cardinality
M=N(\mathcal{H}_{k},\|\cdot\|_{\infty},\varepsilon/2)
(internal

\mathcal{H}_{k}
, choose

a_{j}\in\mathcal{A}
with
\|a_{j}-g_{j}\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon/2
, set

\mathcal{H}_{\text{imp}}^{\varepsilon}:=\{a_{1},\dots,a_{M}\}
, and let
\embed(g):=a_{j(g)}
where

j(g)
is the index of a net point with
\|g-g_{j(g)}\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon/2
.
Then
\|g-\embed(g)\|_{\infty}\leq\|g-g_{j(g)}\|_{\infty}+\|g_{j(g)}-a_{j(g)}\|_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon
,
and
|\mathcal{H}_{\text{imp}}^{\varepsilon}|\leq M
since the

(b) Fix a representation
h\circ f_{m}\circ\cdots\circ f_{1}
and

.
Put

,
y_{j}:=\tilde{f}_{j}(y_{j-1})
, and

G_{j}:=f_{m}\circ\cdots\circ f_{j+1}
for

\lip(G_{j})\leq\Lambda^{m-j}

(c) Since

Appendix K
Worked example: deep ReLU networks

This appendix places the fully connected ReLU network, the architecture on
which most of the theory of depth has been developed
(
Appendix

B
), in the state-transition model, and shows that
the Lipschitz constant of the layers alone moves the network across the
growth mechanisms of
Section

4
: contractive layers
saturate, non-expanding layers on a bounded state space saturate as well,
and expanding layers can realize exponential growth. The ReLU examples in
the literature are then read as instances of the resulting regimes, and a
computed illustration (
Figure

3
) shows the three growth
behaviours for three two-layer families that differ only in their
Lipschitz constant.

K.1
Setting

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
be a nonempty bounded set of diameter
D_{K}
, for

K=[-1,1]^{m}

R_{K}\geq 0
be a bound with
\|x\|\leq R_{K}
for
all

\Pi_{K}
be a

-Lipschitz retraction of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
onto
K

coordinatewise clipping for a box. A hidden layer is a ReLU block of
width

where

p:=2mw+w+m

For

Appendix L
Worked example: chain-of-thought style symbolic computation

This appendix applies the framework to a stylized model of step-by-step
symbolic computation, in which each hidden layer is one reasoning step
acting on a scratchpad and the depth
k
is the number of steps. The
purpose is to show that the statistical cost of the number of steps can be
read off from the metric growth of the compositions of steps, separately
from any particular implementation (for example a recurrently applied
transformer block). Two step families are analyzed: an append-only
scratchpad, which is contractive and lands in the EL regime, and a branching
computation that reads the current symbol, which is expansive, has
exponentially growing uniform entropy, and yet has root-logarithmic
empirical entropy on every sample, because a sample of size
n
can
exercise only finitely many branches. Throughout, “chain of thought” refers to
this abstract model and not to a specific language model; the relation to
the literature on chain-of-thought reasoning is discussed at the end.

L.1
Setting

Scratchpad space.

Let
\mathcal{A}
be a finite alphabet with
|\mathcal{A}|=m\geq 2
and let

\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{A}^{\mathbb{N}}=\{x=(x_{0},x_{1},\dots):x_{j}\in\mathcal{A}\}
be the space of
infinite symbol sequences, equipped with the ultrametric

d_{\theta}(x,y)=\theta^{n(x,y)}\quad(x\neq y),\qquad n(x,y):=\min\{j\geq 0:x_{j}\neq y_{j}\},

for a fixed
\theta\in(0,1)
, and
d_{\theta}(x,x)=0
. Then

(\mathcal{X},d_{\theta})
is compact with
\diam(\mathcal{X})=1
, and two sequences
are within distance
\theta^{L}
if and only if they agree in the first

L
symbols (see
Appendix

F
for the standard
facts used below). A finite scratchpad of length
N
can be embedded by
padding with a fixed symbol.

Output layers.

The answer is read from the scratchpad through an output layer. We use two
linear output-layer classes on explicit feature maps, so that
Assumption
2
holds with depth-independent
constants by
Proposition

1
.

•

(Window output layer.) For
L\geq 1
, let

be the one-hot encoding of the window
(x_{0},\dots,x_{L-1})
, and

H_{L}:=\{x\mapsto\langle w,\Phi_{L}(x)\rangle:\|w\|_{2}\leq R\}
.

•

(Geometrically weighted output layer.) Let

\Phi_{\infty}:\mathcal{X}\to\ell^{2}(\mathbb{N};\mathbb{R}^{\mathcal{A}})
,

\Phi_{\infty}(x):=(\theta^{j}e_{x_{j}})_{j\geq 0}
, where

is the
one-hot vector of

, and

H_{\infty}:=\{x\mapsto\langle w,\Phi_{\infty}(x)\rangle:\|w\|_{\ell^{2}}\leq R\}

Lemma 12
.

\Phi_{L}
is
\sqrt{2}\,\theta^{1-L}
-Lipschitz and

is

\sqrt{2/(1-\theta^{2})}

\|\Phi_{\infty}\|\leq(1-\theta^{2})^{-1/2}

Consequently, Assumption

holds for every

and

for

, and with

and

for

Proof.

If

The norm bounds are immediate. The increment condition follows from

Proposition

1
, and the Rademacher bounds from

\hat{\mathfrak{R}}_{S}(H)=R\,\mathbb{E}_{\sigma}\|n^{-1}\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}\Phi(X_{i})\|\leq R\sup\|\Phi\|/\sqrt{n}
.
∎

-Lipschitz as

L.2
Append-only steps: saturation and the EL regime

Steps.

A write step prepends a symbol:

, and

Lemma 13

(Saturation for append-only steps)

\mathsf{var}(k,n)\leq 12A_{H}L\,\mathsf{V}_{\infty}/\sqrt{n}

If

∎

Bias.

Branching computation: exponential uniform entropy, logarithmic empirical entropy

Steps.

Let
\mathcal{A}=[r]\cup\{\bullet\}

(Exponential growth for branching steps)

The exponential growth in

For every sample

,

L.5
Implementation

Each step is a fixed map, so a recurrently applied block that implements the
step family realizes
B(k,F)
with the same block at every depth; the
implementation error of an approximate block is controlled by

Proposition

6
(b). For append-only steps the propagation
constant is
\Lambda=\theta<1
, so a per-step error
\delta
yields

\varepsilon_{\text{imp}}(k)\leq\delta_{H}+L_{H}\delta/(1-\theta)
, uniformly in
k
. For
branching steps the propagation constant on the chambers is

\Lambda=\theta^{-1}>1
, and the layerwise bound grows like

\delta\theta^{-k}
; an exact implementation of the guard is required for
long programs. Thus the two step families differ numerically, forgetting is stable and
branching is not, while statistically both are benign on any finite sample:
the price of branching is the root-logarithmic profile of

Lemma

15
rather than the saturated one, together with
the need for an exact implementation of the guard.
In summary, both step families act on the same scratchpad space with
fixed linear output layers, so the output-layer constants are
depth-independent, and only the growth of
B(k,F)
differs. For
append-only steps,
B(k,F_{\rm w})
saturates in
d_{\infty}
and in

d_{S}
(
Condition

P1
),
\mathsf{var}(k,n)\asymp n^{-1/2}
, the bias is at most

\theta^{k}
(proved), and the regime is EL with
k^{\ast}\asymp\log n
. For
branching steps,
r^{k}\leq N(B(k,F_{\rm b}),d_{\infty},\varepsilon)\leq r^{k+1}

(
Condition

E2
) but

N(B(k,F_{\rm b}),d_{S},\varepsilon)\leq 1+r+k\min\{n,\varepsilon^{-2}\}
on every sample
(
Lemma

15
), so
\mathsf{var}(k,n)\asymp\sqrt{\log k/n}
; the
bias is
0
for
k\geq k_{0}
(proved) or
e^{-\alpha k}
(assumed), and
the balancing depth is
k_{0}
, or
\asymp\log n
in the EL regime.

L.6
Relation to the literature

Chain-of-thought prompting
(
Wei et al., 2022
)
lets a language model produce
intermediate steps before the answer. Expressivity analyses show that
allowing a number of intermediate steps enlarges the class of problems a
transformer can solve, in particular inherently serial ones

(
Li et al., 2024
)
, and formal frameworks for length generalization

(
Huang et al., 2025
)
and generalization analyses for training
transformers with chain of thought
(
Li et al., 2025
)
address specific
architectures. The present example is complementary and does not concern
a particular architecture: it isolates the statistical cost of the number of
steps as the growth of the compositions of step maps, and shows that this
cost is saturated for forgetful scratchpads and root-logarithmic in the
number of steps for branching computation, independently of how the steps
are implemented; the exponential uniform entropy of branching computation is
not paid on any finite sample.

Appendix M
Worked example: unrolled iterative solvers and samplers

Many deep models are unrolled iterations: fixed-point solvers, iterative
denoisers, and numerical integrators of ordinary differential equations
(ODEs), including the samplers of score-based diffusion and flow-matching
models. In each case a hidden layer is one iteration and the depth is the
number of iterations. This appendix instantiates the framework for two such
families. The first, fixed-point refinement, is contractive and lands in the
EL regime with saturated variance. The second, fixed-horizon integration
with a step size that decreases with the depth, is not contractive but
stable, and lands in the PL regime; its variance saturates by an
Arzelà–Ascoli argument. The example is summarized as follows.

Example 8

(Unrolled iterations)
.

The state space is a compact convex

one projected step
x\mapsto\Pi_{K}(x+h\,s(x))

from a class
\mathcal{S}
, and the output layer is linear. For strongly
monotone fields the profile saturates and the bias decays geometrically,
so

and further iterations do not increase the
estimation bound (EL;
Proposition

11
). For Euler steps of a
stable field over a fixed horizon the profile is
\sqrt{\log k/n}
and
the bias is the discretization error
k^{-p}
, so

k^{\ast}\asymp n^{1/(2p)}
(PL;
Proposition

12
).

\mathsf{gen}(k,n)\lesssim\beta_{\ell}RC_{E}T^{2}k^{-p}+12\,\mathsf{V}_{\infty}/\sqrt{n}

with
p=1
for Euler layers, the PL regime with
\beta=p
and saturated
variance. It is minimized at
k^{\ast}\asymp n^{1/(2p)}
, with balanced
value of order
n^{-1/2}
.

The number of integration steps should therefore grow polynomially with the
sample size, while the statistical cost of the steps stays bounded. This is
the depth trade-off of neural ODEs
(
Chen et al., 2018
)
and of stable
residual architectures
(
Haber and Ruthotto, 2017
;
E, 2017
)
, read in the state-transition
model: depth refines the discretization and does not enlarge the effective
hypothesis class beyond the equicontinuous family of stable schemes.

Diffusion and flow-matching samplers.

The probability-flow ODE of a score-based diffusion model

(
Song et al., 2021
;
Ho et al., 2020
)
and the velocity field of a flow-matching
model
(
Lipman et al., 2023
)
are time-dependent vector fields

s(x,\tau)
whose learned approximants form the drift class
\mathcal{S}_{T}
;
a sampler with
k
steps is a scheme in
B_{T}(k)
, and the number of
function evaluations is the depth. When the learning task is the regression
of the endpoint map, for instance predicting a coordinate of the generated
sample from the initial noise, or the reconstruction of clean data along the
deterministic flow,
Proposition

12
applies verbatim under the
Lipschitz and boundedness conditions on the learned drifts. Generative
quality is usually measured by a distance between distributions rather than
by a regression loss, and the present analysis does not address that
criterion; it addresses the statistical cost of the number of steps for the
learned map.

M.5
Implementation

For both families,
Proposition

6
(b) controls the implementation
error of approximate layers. For fixed-point refinement,

\Lambda=1-h\mu<1
and
\varepsilon_{\text{imp}}(k)\leq\delta_{H}+R\delta/(h\mu)
uniformly
in
k
. For fixed-horizon schemes,
\Lambda_{i}=1+h_{i}\Lambda_{s}
and the
telescoping sum is bounded by
\delta\,k\,e^{\Lambda_{s}T}
, so

\varepsilon_{\text{imp}}(k)\leq\delta_{H}+R\delta ke^{\Lambda_{s}T}
grows only linearly in the
number of steps; with
\delta
the per-step accuracy of the learned drift
this is the usual stability of one-step methods. In both cases the
implementation error does not change the regime of the depth trade-off.

M.6
Relation to the literature

Unrolled fixed-point iterations are the model of deep equilibrium
networks
(
Bai et al., 2019
)
and of regularization by denoising

(
Romano et al., 2017
)
, and Picard-type iterations of the same kind
underlie the neural operators analyzed by
Taniguchi and Sonoda (2026)
.
Fixed-horizon integration is the model of neural ODEs
(
Chen et al., 2018
)

and of stable residual architectures
(
Haber and Ruthotto, 2017
;
E, 2017
)
, and the
samplers of score-based diffusion and flow-matching models

(
Song et al., 2021
;
Ho et al., 2020
;
Lipman et al., 2023
)
are its instances
with a learned drift. In each case the depth is the number of iterations
or steps; the present appendix supplies the statistical side of the
question of how many of them to use, which those works treat as a
numerical or empirical choice.
