System bottlenecks and orthopaedic trauma outcomes in Tanzania: a multicentre prospective observational cohort study to strengthen surgical readiness
Authors: Cherinet Osebo, Victoria Munthali, Laurean Rwanyuma, Rabi Ndeserua, Bryson Ikoshi, Respicious Boniface
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOrthopaedic trauma, particularly extremity fractures from road traffic injuries, is a leading yet under-recognised cause of morbidity in resource-limited settings. In Tanzania, data on fracture patterns, operative timing and system-level delays are limited, hindering evidence-based strategies to strengthen trauma care and surgical capacity.MethodsWe conducted a prospective multicentre observational cohort study using a real-time trauma registry across five Tanzanian hospitals (June 2023–September 2024). Consecutive orthopaedic trauma patients were included; isolated non-orthopaedic injuries, pathological fractures and deaths on arrival were excluded. Exposures were prehospital, triage and in-hospital delays, interfacility transfer, operative intervention and severity. Primary outcome was 14-day mortality; secondary outcomes included complications, length of stay and functional recovery. Predictors were assessed using multivariable logistic and Cox regression with multiple imputations.ResultsAmong 7121 patients (median age 31 years (IQR 22–44); 6393 (89.8%) male), 4013 (56.4%) were rural residents and 4042 (56.8%) were interfacility transfers. Fractures occurred in 5101 (71.6%) patients, including 2590 (50.8%) open fractures, mostly lower-limb (3116 (61.1%)). 14-day mortality was 771 (10.8%), higher in open fractures (16.4% vs 5.2%) and intensive care unit admissions (25%). Independent mortality predictors included interfacility transfer (adjusted OR (aOR) 1.99; 95% CI 1.73 to 2.32), rural residence (aOR 1.80; 95% CI 1.32 to 2.46) and prolonged prehospital delay (aOR 1.39; 95% CI 1.12 to 1.74). Timely surgery reduced mortality (aOR 0.87; 95% CI 0.78 to 0.97). Among 4028 surgical patients (56.6%), 895 (22.2%) had complications, mainly surgical site infections (312 (7.7%)). Functional recovery at discharge occurred in 4624 of 6350 (72.8%) survivors, with 1856 (29.2%) returning to work or school.ConclusionsOrthopaedic trauma in Tanzania caused substantial early mortality, independently linked to prehospital delays, interfacility transfer and rural residence. Timely surgery improved survival, highlighting the need to strengthen referral pathways, expedite care and expand surgical care capacity. A structured electronic trauma registry provided real-world data to guide policy, workforce planning and trauma system developmentKeywords: Global Health, Health policy, Traumatology, Surgery

Introduction

Trauma is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, disproportionately affecting low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) where timely surgical care and specialised services are often limited.1 2 Extremity fractures constitute a significant proportion of trauma cases and are frequently associated with preventable complications—such as infection, non-union and limb loss—particularly in settings with constrained surgical resources and limited orthopaedic coverage.3 4

In sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), high rates of open fractures coupled with delays to definitive care exacerbate morbidity and contribute to avoidable mortality. Yet, comprehensive data on orthopaedic injury burden, operative timing and short-term outcomes at high-volume trauma centres remain scarce.5 6 In Tanzania, musculoskeletal and road-traffic injuries (RTIs) are a major public-health challenge. A 1 year prospective registry across 13 facilities found that RTIs accounted for most trauma presentations and were linked to high mortality.7 Tertiary referral centres in Tanzania face substantial resource constraints, including a limited orthopaedic workforce and restricted ward and operating room (OR) capacity.8 A recent scoping review of orthopaedic and trauma research in Tanzania highlighted that RTIs dominate the literature (77% of trauma-focused studies) and emphasised significant gaps in clinical and interventional research.5

Optimising orthopaedic trauma care requires accurate characterisation of injury patterns, timely operative intervention and consideration of system-level factors such as referral pathways, OR availability and intensive care unit (ICU) capacity.2 9 Evidence from LMIC trauma systems demonstrates that delays in surgical fixation and inadequate perioperative resources are major determinants of adverse outcomes, underscoring the need for data-driven strategies to strengthen surgical readiness and trauma system performance.9 10 In Tanzania, comprehensive multicentre data on orthopaedic injury burden, operative timing, and outcomes remain scarce.5 7 9 10 These gaps hinder clinicians and policymakers from implementing evidence-based trauma care strategies.

To address this, we collaborated with McGill University to establish a web-based trauma registry led by the Muhimbili Orthopaedic Institute (MOI), prospectively collecting standardised data across five tertiary hospitals in Tanzania. This multicentre platform facilitates research, quality improvement and policy development in orthopaedic trauma care. Using these data, the study quantifies the burden of orthopaedic injuries, characterises patient-level and injury-level features, evaluates operative timing and examines outcomes. By integrating patient-level and system-level factors, our analysis identifies actionable gaps to guide trauma system improvements, optimise resources and strengthen surgical readiness in resource-limited settings.

Methods

Study design and setting

This multicentre, prospective, registry-based observational cohort study was conducted across five national hospitals in Tanzania, with the MOI in Dar es Salaam serving as the largest tertiary referral centre for orthopaedic, trauma and neurosurgical care. The other four hospitals act as regional/national referral centres, referring complex cases to MOI. Collectively, these hospitals serve both urban and rural populations. In collaboration with McGill University’s Centre for Global Surgery, a web-based orthopaedic trauma registry was launched in June 2023 to strengthen trauma data systems and clinical documentation, representing the first continuously maintained, multicentre, web-based trauma database in Tanzania. The study adhered to Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for observational research.

Data collection instruments

Trained clinicians prospectively collected patient data across hospitals using available devices, including cellphones, tablets and desktops—requiring only access to a web browser to connect to a secure, web-based system (Amber Collect (cglobalsurgery.ca)), developed for real-time trauma documentation.10 The system integrates three coordinated modules:

Amber Collect, supporting bedside data entry by clinicians.

Amber Studio, enabling centralised monitoring and data oversight.

Amber Server, providing encrypted cloud-based storage with automated synchronisation once internet connectivity is restored.

The platform is browser-based, functions offline and employs built-in validation rules and prompts to minimise transcription errors and incomplete entries. Duplicate records are automatically prevented through unique patient identifiers. The registry captures standardised patient-level data, including demographics, referral and transport details, injury mechanisms and characteristics, physiological parameters, injury severity scores, operative interventions and outcomes across the continuum of care. Data entry occurs during the initial emergency department (ED) evaluation, following stabilisation, to ensure timely and accurate registration. Patients were prospectively followed throughout hospitalisation, with outcomes updated directly within the system. Each patient record was assigned to a pseudoanonymised identifier to protect confidentiality.

Study population and participants

All trauma patients presenting with orthopaedic or musculoskeletal injuries to any of the five participating hospitals during the study period (June 2023–September 2024) were eligible for inclusion. Patients with isolated non-orthopaedic trauma (eg, abdominal, thoracic or isolated neurological injuries), pathological fractures (eg, malignancy-related) or those who were dead on arrival were excluded. Polytrauma patients were included if they sustained at least one skeletal fracture and severe soft tissue injuries, ensuring capture of the full spectrum of orthopaedic trauma rather than the broader trauma case mix. Consecutive patient inclusion minimised selection bias, and no sampling was performed. Data completeness and internal consistency were continuously monitored through automated validation checks within the web-based registry platform.

Transfer status was defined based on the patient’s point of first presentation:

Direct arrival: Patients presenting directly from the scene of injury to a participating hospital.

Transferred patients: Patients initially evaluated or stabilised at another healthcare facility and subsequently referred to a participating hospital, as documented by referral letters or accompanying records.

Exposures and care delay definitions

Patient-level exposures, including prehospital and in-hospital delays, transfer status, injury mechanism and physiological parameters, were recorded prospectively at the time of care before outcome ascertainment. Care delays were defined using predefined time intervals informed by trauma systems literature, pragmatic workflow benchmarks11 and our previously published study on time-critical care gaps in a low-resourced setting.12 Delay variables were analysed as categorical thresholds for interpretability and as continuous measures in sensitivity analyses to assess robustness.

Prehospital delay: Time from injury to arrival at the definitive receiving hospital. For transferred patients, this included time to the first facility plus interfacility transfer duration, captured from referral documentation. Delays were categorised as ≤6 hours versus >6 hours, reflecting operational referral timelines and system benchmarks.

Delays were examined across hospitals to account for differences in referral pathways, staffing and resource availability. Timestamps were extracted from the digital registry platform, and implausible or missing times—mainly among interfacility transfers—were verified against referral documentation. Records with unverifiable timestamps were excluded only from delay-specific analyses.

Outcomes and follow-up procedures

The primary outcome was 14-day in-hospital mortality, selected to capture early deaths directly attributable to trauma and perioperative complications. Secondary outcomes included postoperative infection, need for blood transfusion, ventilator use, hospital length of stay and functional recovery at discharge or at 30 days for patients still hospitalised beyond 14 days. Patients were censored at discharge, day 14 or day 30, whichever occurred first, to account for varying lengths of stay and competing events. Follow-up procedures involved daily assessment during hospitalisation, with outcomes updated in the registry at each clinical encounter. Patients who were left against medical advice or were transferred out were censored at the time of departure. Mortality was verified through cross-referencing ward and operating room records.

Quality control

Data reliability was ensured through a two-tier validation process combining automated and manual review. Built-in range and logic checks flagged implausible entries (eg, oxygen saturation >100%, physiological outliers or inconsistent timestamps), which were verified before inclusion. Weekly manual audits of about 10% of records cross-checked key variables against clinical charts; unresolved discrepancies were coded as missing. Duplicate entries were automatically prevented by unique patient identifiers, and all edits were logged for full traceability. Quality-control procedures were supervised by the registry coordinator and senior investigator, ensuring data completeness, consistency and analytical validity.

Statistical analysis

No formal sample size calculation was performed; all eligible patients were included to maximise power and capture the full spectrum of orthopaedic trauma. Categorical variables were summarised as counts and percentages (n/N, %), and continuous variables as medians with IQRs. Group comparisons were conducted across prespecified strata—including age group (≤18, 19–59, ≥60 years), transfer status, injury location, transport mode, mechanism of injury, fracture type and time-to-care intervals—using χ² or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables and Mann-Whitney U or Kruskal-Wallis tests for continuous variables, as appropriate.

Logistic regression

Independent predictors of 14-day mortality were identified using multivariable logistic regression, including prespecified covariates: age, sex, transfer status, rural residence, ambulance transport, mechanism of injury, fracture type, pelvic fracture, comorbidities, time-to-care intervals, level of consciousness at presentation using AVPU (A, Alert; V, responds to Voice; P, responds to Pain; U, Unresponsive)) and Kampala Trauma Score (KTS). Time-to-care intervals were analysed both as continuous variables and using clinically relevant thresholds (eg, surgery within 6 hours for open fractures). ICU admission and operative management were evaluated in separate models to avoid adjusting for potential mediators. Prespecified interactions (age×transfer, open fracture×time to surgery, rural residence×prehospital delay) were tested. Multivariable models accounted for clustering by hospitals to adjust for within-centre correlation and reduce potential bias from differences in referral patterns, case severity and resources.

Survival analysis

Time-to-event outcomes were analysed using Kaplan-Meier curves and Cox proportional hazards models with the same covariates as the logistic regression. Proportional hazards assumptions were verified. Hospital discharge was treated as a competing event using Fine-Gray subdistribution hazards in sensitivity analyses. Model performance was evaluated for multicollinearity, calibration and discrimination.

Sensitivity analyses and missing data

Missing covariate data were addressed with multiple imputation by chained equations, with pooled estimates reported using Rubin’s rules. Complete-case analyses were conducted as a robustness check. Sensitivity analyses for 14-day mortality included (1) extreme-case scenarios assuming all patients with missing outcomes either died (worst case) or survived (best case), and (2) censoring of patients discharged or deceased within 24 hours, as well as 30-day outcomes for those hospitalised beyond 14 days. For delay intervals, implausible or missing timestamps, primarily among interfacility transfers, were cross-checked with referral records and excluded if unverifiable. Incomplete or implausible timestamps, particularly for interfacility transfers, were verified against referral records and unverifiable entries were excluded from interval analyses. All p values are two-sided, with p<0.05 considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using SAS V.9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina, USA).

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not directly involved in the design, conduct or reporting of this study. The study used de-identified, routinely collected data from a multicentre trauma registry to evaluate system-level and patient-level factors affecting orthopaedic trauma outcomes in Tanzania.

Results

Study population and demographics

A total of 7121 trauma patients were enrolled across the five participating hospitals, highlighting the substantial burden of musculoskeletal injuries in Tanzania. The median age was 31 years (IQR 22–44), and males predominated (6393/7121; 89.8%). Most patients originated from rural areas (4013/7121; 56.4%) and were transferred from other facilities (4042/7121; 56.8%) and mostly arrived by ambulance (4485/7121; 63.0%) (table 1).

Injury characteristics

Motor vehicle collisions were the leading cause of orthopaedic trauma (3888/7121; 54.6%), followed by falls (2800/7121; 39.3%), while traumatic amputations were uncommon (11/7121; 0.2%). Polytrauma with associated fractures was frequent (4531/7121; 63.6%), whereas isolated orthopaedic injuries were less common (2590/7121; 36.4%). Fractures were the predominant injury type (5101/7121; 71.6%), of which 2590 (50.8%) were open and 2511 (49.2%) were closed. Lower extremity fractures were most common (3114/5101; 61.1%), followed by upper extremity fractures (1456/5101; 28.5%) and pelvic fractures (531/7121; 10.4%). Soft-tissue injuries occurred in 553/7121 (7.8%) and dislocations in 112/7121 (1.6%) (table 1).

Clinical outcomes and hospital stay

Among 2051/7121 (28.8%) patients with24-hour disposition data recorded, 787/2051 (38.4%) were discharged directly from the ED within 24 hours of arrival, 395/2051 (19.3%) were admitted to general wards, 559/2051 (27.3%) were taken directly to operating rooms for surgical interventions, 246/2051 (11.9%) were admitted to the ICU, 55/2051 (2.7%) died and 9/2051 (0.44%) were referred out. When analysed by location, mortality rates were 45/395 (11.4%) for patients admitted to general wards, 76/559 (13.6%) for those taken to the OR and 134/246 (54.5%) for ICU patients (p<0.001).

Among patients surviving beyond 24 hours, 5070/7121 (71.2%) remained hospitalised and were followed for 14-day outcomes. By day 14, 3530/5070 (69.6%) were discharged, 712/5070 (14.1%) died, 819/5070 (16.2%) remained hospitalised and 9/5070 (0.2%) were referred out. Among those still hospitalised at day 14, 545/819 (66.5%) were discharged by day 30, 4/819 (0.5%) died (all in the ICU), 235/819 (28.7%) remained hospitalised and 5/819 (0.6%) were referred out. Outcomes were not tracked beyond discharge. Within the 14-day cohort, patients were admitted to general wards (2198/5070; 43.4%), ICU (1673/5070; 33.0%) and HDUs (1199/5070; 23.7%). Corresponding 14-day mortality was 198/2198 (9.0%) in general wards, 418/1673 (25.0%) in the ICU and 96/1199 (8.0%) in HDUs (p<0.001).

Overall orthopaedic trauma mortality was 771/7121 (10.8%). Patients with open fractures experienced higher mortality (425/2590; 16.4%) compared with those with closed fractures (131/2511; 5.2%; p<0.002), reflecting the increased physiological burden of open injuries. Median hospital length of stay was 7 days (IQR, 4–14), longer for open fractures (median, 7 days; IQR, 5–21). Patients who died had a shorter median stay of 3 days (IQR, 2–5), whereas survivors had a median stay of 5 days (IQR, 3–8), indicating early mortality among the most severely injured patients (table 2).

Prehospital and in-hospital delays

The prehospital and in-hospital delays across the participating hospitals demonstrated that the overall cohort median prehospital delay was 390 min (IQR 200–690) and was longer among interfacility transferred patients (490 min (IQR 300–720)) than among direct arrivals (320 min (IQR 250–540), p=0.004). Interfacility transferred patients had higher mortality (465/4042; 11.5%) compared with direct arrivals (306/3079; 9.9%; p<0.001). Patients from rural areas experienced longer prehospital delays (420 min (IQR 250–690)) compared with urban residents (300 min (IQR 180–480); p=0.03) and higher mortality (449/4013; 11.2% vs 322/3108; 10.4%; p=0.05). For the cohorts, median triage delay was 18 min (IQR 5–35), and the median definitive-care delay (ED arrival to surgery or ICU/HDU admission) was 480 min (IQR 260–780).

Physiological parameters, injury severity and comorbidities

Vital signs were recorded for all 7121/7121 (100%) patients at presentation. Median oxygen saturation was 98% (IQR 98–99%), respiratory rate 20 breaths/minute (IQR 18–22) and systolic blood pressure 124 mm Hg (IQR 114–132). Level of consciousness, assessed using the AVPU scale, showed that 5050/7121 (70.9%) were alert and 425/7121 (6.0%) were unresponsive. Mortality by AVPU status was 150/5050 (3.0%) among alert patients and 81/425 (19.1%) among unresponsive patients (p<0.001), confirming the strong prognostic value of initial consciousness level.

Injury severity, measured by the KTS, had a median of 9 (IQR 8–10). Patients with open fractures had lower median KTS values of 8 (IQR 7–9) compared with 9 (IQR 8–10) in those with closed fractures (p<0.003). Severe injury (KTS ≤8) was observed in 692/7121 (9.7%) patients, while 4521/7121 (63.5%) had moderate injury (KTS 9–10). Paediatric patients (<18 years) had a lower median KTS (7, IQR 6–9) than adults (8, IQR 7–9; p<0.002). Among patients who died, median KTS was 6 (IQR 5–8), compared with 9 (IQR 8–10) in survivors (p<0.002).

Comorbidities were documented in 1330/7121 (18.7%) patients. The most common were hypertension (720/7121; 10.1%) and diabetes (610/7121; 8.6%). Mortality among patients with hypertension was 120/720 (16.7%), and among those with diabetes, 105/610 (17.2%), significantly higher than among patients without these comorbidities (p<0.034). Some patients had multiple comorbidities (table 3).

Surgical demands and complications

Definitive operative interventions were performed in 4028/7121 (56.6%) patients across the five participating hospitals. Among fracture patients, 1168/5101 (22.9%) sustained multiple fractures—defined as more than one fracture in different anatomical regions or bones, regardless of type. Staged procedures were required in 928/5101 (18.2%), typically when definitive fixation was deferred due to physiological instability or soft-tissue compromise. Blood transfusions were administered in 1318/4028 (32.7%) operated patients, with a median of 2 units (IQR, 1–4). Among patients presenting with oxygen saturation <90%, 617/4028 (15.3%) required mechanical ventilation for a median duration of 4 days (IQR, 2–8). These findings highlight the substantial operative and critical care resources needed for high-energy trauma management in resource-limited, multicentre settings.

Operative management by fracture type

Among open fractures, 1704/2590 (65.8%) underwent external fixation, 850/2590 (32.8%) internal fixation and 1243/2590 (48.0%) debridement. All open fractures were classified according to the Gustilo-Anderson (GA) system (types I–III). Median time to initial debridement was 6.2 hours (IQR, 4.5–9.0), and median time to external fixation was 14 hours (IQR, 12–24). High-severity injuries (GA IIIB–IIIC) required staged management with prolonged intervals between external and internal fixation to allow soft-tissue recovery and vascular stabilisation.

Closed fractures were predominantly managed with internal fixation (980/2511; 39.0%), while 220/2511 (8.8%) received temporary external fixation and 353/2511 (14.1%) underwent staged procedures due to polytrauma or physiological instability. Some patients require more than one operative intervention.

Amputation and limb salvage

Corrective amputations were performed in 35/4028 (0.9%) operated patients—predominantly lower limbs (23/35; 65.7%) and upper limbs (12/35; 34.3%)—representing 35/5101 (0.7%) of all fracture patients and 35/2590 (1.4%) of open-fracture cases. Limb salvage was achieved in 2555/2590 (98.6%) of open-fracture patients.

Postoperative complications

Across hospitals, a total of 894/4028 (22.2%) patients experienced at least one postoperative complication. Complications were more frequent among open fractures (427/2590; 16.5%) than closed fractures (95/2511; 3.8%) (p<0.003). The most common complications were surgical site infections (312/4028; 7.7%) and venous thromboembolism (61/4028; 1.5%). Multiple complications occurred in some patients and were also observed among non-fracture operative cases (eg, soft-tissue repair or debridement for crush injuries).

Functional recovery

Among survivors (6350/7121; 89.2%), 4624/6350 (72.8%) were fully ambulatory at discharge, 911/6350 (14.3%) required walking aids, 584/6350 (9.2%) were non-ambulatory and 235/6350 (3.7%) remained bedridden. At 30 days, 4588/6350 (72.2%) had regained self-care, and 1855/6350 (29.2%) had returned to work or school. Long-term functional outcomes beyond 30 days were not tracked.

Vulnerable populations

Age-stratified analyses highlighted disparities in outcomes and injury patterns across the five participating hospitals. Paediatric patients (1096/7121; 15.4%) had the lowest mortality (35/1096; 3.2%) and the shortest hospital stay (median 4 days, IQR 3–7). Adults (4268/7121; 60.0%) had mortality of 494/4268 (11.6%) with a median stay of 6 days (IQR 4–10). Elderly patients (1757/7121; 24.7%) experienced the highest mortality (242/1757; 13.8%) and a median stay of 7 days (IQR 5–12) (p<0.001).

Among patients with open fractures, 128/1096 (11.7%) paediatric patients, 1950/4268 (45.7%) adults and 512/1757 (29.1%) elderly patients underwent surgery (p<0.004). Median time to surgery increased with age: 4 hours (IQR 3–6.5) in paediatric patients, 6 hours (IQR 5–9) in adults and 8.5 hours (IQR 6–12) in elderly patients (p<0.001), reflecting both physiological prioritisation and system-level resource allocation across centres.

Predictors of mortality

Overall, in-hospital mortality was 771/7121 (10.8%), including 55/2051 (2.7%) within 24 hours, 712/5070 (14.0%) by 14 days and 4/819 (0.5%) after 14 days. Crude in-hospital mortality varied across hospitals, ranging from 8% to 14%, reflecting differences in patient mix, referral timing and resource availability across sites. Multivariable logistic regression, accounting for clustering by hospital to adjust for within-centre correlation and differences in referral patterns, case mix and resource availability, identified several independent predictors of mortality (table 4): interfacility transfer (adjusted OR 1.997; 95% CI 1.726 to 2.321; p < 0.001), rural residence (adjusted OR 1.803; 95% CI 1.319 to 2.463; p < 0.001), ambulance arrival (adjusted OR 3.209; 95% CI 2.219 to 4.639; p < 0.001), motor-vehicle collision (adjusted OR 1.292; 95% CI 1.012 to 1.730; p = 0.042), hypertension (adjusted OR 1.840; 95% CI 1.220 to 2.790; p = 0.004) and longer prehospital delay (adjusted OR 1.389; 95% CI 1.120 to 1.736; p = 0.004). Timely definitive care was protective (adjusted OR 0.872; 95% CI 0.782 to 0.972; p = 0.012), while a KTS < 6 strongly predicted death (unadjusted OR 3.789; 95% CI 1.648 to 4.960; p < 0.001).

Survival analysis

Kaplan-Meier curves (figure 1) revealed significant survival differences by age and timing of surgery (log-rank p<0.001). Paediatric patients had the highest survival, whereas elderly patients had the poorest outcomes. Surgery within 24 hours of admission was consistently associated with improved survival. Median time to definitive care was 480 min (IQR 260–780). In multivariable Cox models accounting for hospital clustering, delayed surgery increased the hazard of death (adjusted HR 1.78, 95% CI 1.42 to 2.23, p<0.001). Elderly patients had over twice the hazard compared with adults (adjusted HR 2.31, 95% CI 1.76 to 3.04), while paediatric patients had substantially lower risk (adjusted HR 0.47, 95% CI 0.29 to 0.76). Associations remained robust after adjusting for transfer status, injury mechanism and critical presentation.

Sensitivity analysis

To assess the impact of incomplete follow-up, survival models were repeated across all 7121 enrolled orthopaedic trauma patients. Complete 14-day outcomes were available for 5070 (71.2%) patients, among whom 712 (14.1%) died. An additional 2051 patients had only 24-hour outcomes, and 819 remained hospitalised beyond 14 days, with 4 (0.5%) additional deaths by day 30. Under worst-case assumptions (all missing outcomes as deaths), cumulative mortality rose to 3582/7121 (50.3%); under best-case assumptions (all survived), mortality remained 10.8% (771/7121), an absolute difference of 39.5 percentage points. Missing or implausible transfer timestamps were cross-checked against referral logs, and unverifiable records (9/5142; 0.18%) were excluded. Censoring accounted for early discharges and 30-day competing risks. Across all sensitivity models, the direction and magnitude of key predictors—including interfacility transfer, rural residence, ambulance arrival, low KTS and age—remained stable, supporting the robustness of our findings. These results demonstrate the feasibility of multicentre, web-based orthopaedic trauma registries in SSA for identifying modifiable determinants of mortality.

Discussion

This multicentre, prospectively collected registry provides an overview of injury burden, short-term outcomes and system-level determinants across Tanzanian national hospitals, with implications for clinical practice and health system planning. By including both urban and rural populations, the study highlights systemic patterns in trauma care and underscores that orthopaedic injuries remain a growing global health challenge. The cohort was predominantly young adult males, with high-energy road-traffic collisions as the leading mechanism. Complex extremity fractures and open injuries were common, reflecting patterns in other LMIC trauma cohorts. These findings emphasise that economically active populations bear the highest trauma burden, concentrating demands on emergency services, operative care, infection prevention and rehabilitation.11,13

Substantial prehospital and in-hospital delays were observed across hospitals, reflecting systemic barriers to timely trauma care. Transferred patients experienced longer prehospital delays (median 490 vs 320 min) and higher mortality (11.5% vs 9.9%; p<0.001) than direct arrivals, even after adjusting for injury severity (KTS) and hospital clustering, highlighting the time costs of interfacility transfer. Rural patients also faced longer delays (420 vs 300 min) and higher mortality (11.2% vs 10.4%; p=0.05) than urban patients, reflecting geographical inequity, limited ambulance availability and slower escalation of care. These patterns align with prior LMIC evidence, where transport distances, fragmented triage and resource constraints contribute to avoidable mortality.13 14

These associations remained significant after adjusting for injury severity, transfer status and hospital clustering, indicating that delayed access—not case mix alone—substantially influences survival. The predominance of rural-origin patients and interfacility transfers via ambulance highlights the central role of referral networks in accessing definitive care, though these pathways also introduce critical delays. Although ambulance arrival was associated with higher mortality, this likely reflects underlying injury severity and urgent triage rather than a direct causal effect. Residual confounding by indication is possible, and these associations should be interpreted as associative rather than causal.

Delayed definitive surgery was a key predictor of mortality, consistent with prior Tanzanian data showing that over 10% of referred trauma patients experienced surgical delays ≥12 hours.14 Patients with longer prehospital and in-hospital delays often faced prolonged time to debridement and fixation, which was associated with higher mortality. While these patterns suggest that delayed transfer and care contribute to worse outcomes, the observational design limits causal inference. This conceptual pathway—prehospital and in-hospital delays → delayed definitive care → increased mortality—serves as an observational framework to describe system-level associations, emphasising the importance of coordinated referral networks, rapid transport and timely in-hospital interventions.

Early mortality and complication rates were highest among patients with open fractures, ICU admissions and ambulance arrivals, factors independently linked to worse survival on multivariable analysis. These findings align with prior reports showing that critically injured patients in LMICs experience restricted access to timely surgery and critical care.15 Collectively, the results reveal entrenched ‘three-delay’ bottlenecks: in prehospital triage and transport, interfacility referral and in-hospital throughput. Addressing these constraints through strengthened trauma triage, coordinated referral systems and expanded operative and ICU capacity is essential to improving survival in resource-limited settings.814,16 Paediatric patients had lower mortality (3.2%) and fewer surgical interventions, reflecting both conservative, guideline-based management and physiological resilience. In contrast, elderly patients faced higher mortality and greater delays to surgery, underscoring the need for age-responsive pathways emphasising early assessment, rapid stabilisation and expedited operative care. Clarifying whether lower paediatric surgery rates reflect clinical conservatism or capacity limitations remains vital for guiding equitable resource allocation.

Open and lower-extremity fractures were highly prevalent, often occurring in the context of polytrauma, highlighting a caseload dominated by high-energy injuries. In such settings, damage-control orthopaedics with staged debridement and fixation remains the standard approach. A multinational survey of orthopaedic surgeons from 31 LMICs identified open tibial fractures as a leading cause of long-term disability,17 with limited equipment, cost barriers and operating-room capacity constraining care.14 17 In our registry, nearly half of patients underwent surgical intervention, mostly internal or external fixation, reflecting the focus on limb reconstruction. In the survey,17 internal fixation was less common for severe or delayed wounds, with external fixation and extended antibiotics often used instead.

Arrival-to-theatre times were delayed by several hours, with only half of the open fractures debrided within 6 hours—below other LMIC benchmarks, where most patients receive debridement within 24 hours.17 These delays are clinically significant: for GA type III injuries, each 6-hour delay increases infection risk by 0.23%.18 Severe fractures often required transfusions, ICU admission and were complicated by infections. Traumatic amputations, though less frequent, reflect profound functional loss in low-income regions with limited prosthetic and rehabilitation services.19 Early presentation to healthcare facilities, timely surgery, reliable soft-tissue coverage and infection control are critical to preventing limb loss, while unavoidable amputations carry lasting socio-economic consequences.18 19 Systematic infection-prevention protocols—including timely antibiotics, serial debridement and early coverage—reduce complications, shorten hospitalisation and minimise reoperations.20 Strengthening timely trauma care alongside expanded operating room capacity must also be paired with rehabilitation and social support to reduce both clinical and economic burdens.18,21

Early disposition of trauma patients can reduce ED overcrowding, optimise resource use and allow faster turnover for new emergencies. In this study, a substantial proportion of patients were discharged directly from the ED within 24 hours, with timely discharges among admitted patients, consistent with prior studies showing that resource-allocation policies influence outcomes;10 21 however, efficiency alone does not ensure safety. Most deaths occurred in the ICU, with a notable number on general wards, suggesting missed opportunities for timely escalation and accurate triage, risking deterioration of critically ill patients. Similar findings in other low-resource settings demonstrate that delays in transferring critically ill patients to higher-acuity units are strongly associated with increased mortality.22 To support ICU capacity and robust triage, tools like the KTS effectively predicted mortality with accuracy comparable to the estimated Injury Severity Score (AUC 0.92 vs 0.91) and outperformed traditional triage in identifying admission needs.23 24

In this study, nearly 90% of patients survived, with about three-quarters fully ambulatory at discharge. Although most amputations involved the lower limb and may reduce productivity, over 72% of survivors resumed basic daily activities by 30 days, despite limited follow-up. This contrasts with other settings, where many orthopaedic injury survivors never fully regain function.25 These findings highlight that post-trauma recovery extends beyond hospital discharge, emphasising the need for functional rehabilitation and social support, particularly in low-resource settings. Long-term functional outcomes beyond 30 days remain uncertain, limiting conclusions about the broader socio-economic impact.

Multiple logistic regression and age-stratified analyses showed that paediatric patients had lower mortality and underwent fewer surgical interventions, reflecting distinct injury patterns and more conservative operative thresholds. In contrast, elderly patients had 1.4 times higher odds of 14-day mortality (OR 1.4; p<0.003), consistent with age-related vulnerability and comorbidities.26,28 Structural and system-level factors also strongly influenced outcomes.10 29 After adjusting for age and injury severity, interfacility transfer, rural residence, ambulance arrival, polytrauma, fracture type and injury mechanism remained independent predictors of mortality, reflecting both greater case severity and the hazards of care delays.30 31 Conversely, timely prehospital and in-hospital care, including shortened injury-to-care intervals, early debridement and prompt fixation, was protective, improving survival.32 33 These findings highlight two complementary domains for intervention: (1) upstream system improvements such as bystander stabilisation, standardised referral criteria and expedited transport to reduce injury-to-care intervals, and (2) in-hospital process optimisation with defined ‘door-to-debridement’ and ‘door-to-fixation’ targets, supported by robust perioperative resources to accelerate life-saving and limb-saving interventions.34 35 Sensitivity analyses confirmed that incomplete follow-up may underestimate absolute mortality but does not alter relative risk patterns.36

Strengthening trauma education, as demonstrated by Tanzania’s blended Trauma and Disaster Team Response (TDTR) course, which combined simulation-based training with team-based disaster response modules, significantly improved provider knowledge, teamwork, ED efficiency, patient outcomes and mass casualty response.37 Specifically, the TDTR course reduced arrival-to-care time by 55% and enhanced surgical triage, highlighting the importance of context-specific trauma training in reducing preventable deaths and disability. Moreover, digitised trauma registries play a critical role in enabling real-time quality improvement, guiding policy and integrating care across prehospital, in-hospital and post-discharge phases, thereby supporting scalable and equitable trauma systems and essential emergency surgical care.38,40

Strengths and limitations

This multicentre prospective study leverages a large, real-time orthopaedic trauma registry across five Tanzanian hospitals, minimising selection bias and enabling detailed characterisation of case-mix, care processes and short-term outcomes. Granular clinical and process variables, such as KTS, AVPU, fracture type and prehospital and surgical timing—allowed simultaneous assessment of patient-level and system-level predictors, with analyses adjusted for hospital clustering and referral variation. Limitations include heterogeneity in hospital capacity and referral patterns, which may limit generalisability to district facilities. The registry lacked anatomical injury scores and long-term patient-reported outcomes, with follow-up limited to 30 days. Despite multivariable adjustment, residual confounding and documentation variability across hospitals remain possible, requiring cautious interpretation.

Implications for practice and policy

Prehospital and referral reform: Strengthen triage, ambulance capacity and referral coordination to reduce injury-to-care delays, especially for rural transfers.

Conclusion

This multicentre prospective study identifies critical system bottlenecks driving orthopaedic trauma mortality in Tanzania, particularly prehospital delays, interfacility transfers and limited operative capacity. Timely surgery substantially reduced risk, underscoring that strengthening surgical readiness through faster triage, efficient referral and perioperative support can save lives. Nearly three-quarters of survivors regained ambulation, yet severe injuries impose lasting socio-economic costs. By leveraging a real-time multicentre registry, this study demonstrates how data-driven monitoring can guide equitable, scalable reforms to optimise orthopaedic trauma care and enhance surgical readiness policies in low-resource settings.
