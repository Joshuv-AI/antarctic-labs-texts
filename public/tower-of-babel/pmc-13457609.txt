Enhancing multimodal inpatient fall prediction via nursing statement integration within the OMOP common data model
Authors: Hyejin Hong, Soyeon Kim, Borim Ryu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate prediction of inpatient falls is a cornerstone of patient safety, yet conventional models often overlook the rich clinical context embedded in nursing records. This study aimed to develop and validate machine learning (ML) models by integrating structured clinical data from a Common Data Model (CDM) with nursing statements, evaluating whether nursing documentation provides incremental predictive value for fall risk stratification. We conducted a retrospective cohort study using adult inpatient data mapped to the Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership (OMOP) CDM, linked with nursing statements characterizing patients’ functional status, mobility, mental status, and care dependency. Four algorithms—Logistic Regression, Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, and XGBoost—were trained and evaluated across three distinct feature sets: CDM-only, nursing-only, and combined. Model performance was quantified using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), sensitivity, and specificity. Model interpretability and feature contributions were elucidated using Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) analysis. Integrated models (CDM + nursing) consistently outperformed single-source models, demonstrating superior discriminative power and a more balanced sensitivity–specificity profile. Notably, models utilizing only nursing statements achieved performance comparable to CDM-only models, indicating that nursing documentation captures critical safety-relevant information often latent in structured electronic health records. Ensemble methods, particularly XGBoost, demonstrated the highest discrimination. SHAP analysis revealed that nursing-derived indicators, such as documented “Reports weakness” and “At risk of falling” were among the most influential predictors, providing context-rich insights beyond standard clinical variables. The integration of coded nursing statements with structured CDM data enhances both the accuracy and interpretability of inpatient fall risk predictions. These findings underscore the importance of leveraging unstructured clinical documentation to develop transparent, AI-driven clinical decision support systems for proactive fall prevention in hospital settings.

Introduction

Falls during hospitalization are among the most frequently reported adverse events in acute care settings, posing substantial threats to patient safety and creating considerable clinical and economic burdens for healthcare systems. Approximately 3%–5% of hospitalized patients experience a fall during their stay, with consequences ranging from minor injuries to severe complications, including fractures, head trauma, prolonged hospitalization, and increased mortality. Beyond direct patient harm, falls are associated with extended hospital stays, increased healthcare costs, and reduced patient and family satisfaction1. Consequently, fall prevention is widely recognized by major regulatory and accreditation organizations as a key indicator of hospital quality and patient safety worldwide2. Therefore, early identification of patients at high risk and timely implementation of preventive strategies are critical to improving clinical outcomes and optimizing healthcare resource utilization3,4.

Current clinical fall risk assessment in hospitalized patients primarily relies on structured variables, such as age, diagnosis, mobility, medication use, and cognitive status, along with standardized tools including the Morse Fall Scale and the Hendrich II Fall Risk Model. However, these conventional approaches demonstrate limited sensitivity and specificity in predicting actual falls; multicenter studies report area under the curve (AUC) values typically below 0.70, reflecting suboptimal discriminatory performance5,6. This limitation highlights the need for more comprehensive and dynamic approaches that better capture patient-specific risk factors.

Nurses, who spend more time in direct contact with hospitalized patients than any other healthcare professionals, are uniquely positioned to observe subtle changes in patients’ functional status, behavior, gait, and environmental context that influence fall risk7–9. Nursing documentation—including routine assessments, functional observations, interventions, and patient responses—captures these bedside insights. Many hospitals have recently adopted structured or semi-structured nursing documentation systems that allow nurses to record standardized clinical observations using predefined items or coded statements10. These data provide detailed, context-specific information on risk factors, such as mobility impairment, balance difficulties, and confusion, as well as nursing interventions and situational observations (e.g., “patient unsteady on feet,” “frequent toileting needs”) that extend beyond what diagnostic and procedural codes alone can capture.

Building on this approach, recent studies have increasingly focused on improving fall risk prediction by systematically integrating unstructured nursing documentation with structured clinical data within electronic health records (EHRs). Multicenter validation studies have shown that such integrated models achieve significantly better predictive performance than traditional fall risk assessment tools8,11.

Although machine learning models have demonstrated strong performance across various clinical prediction tasks, many lack interpretability, creating a “black box” problem that limits clinical adoption and acceptance among healthcare providers12. To address this limitation, explainability methods such as SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) have emerged as effective tools for improving model transparency13,14. SHAP provides feature-level and individualized explanations, enabling clinicians to identify which clinical and nursing variables contribute most to predicted risk, thereby supporting informed clinical decision-making and facilitating real-world implementation15,16.

This study aimed to develop and validate transparent, AI-driven clinical machine learning models for predicting inpatient fall risk. By integrating coded nursing statements with structured clinical variables based on the common data model (CDM), this study extends traditional prediction approaches that primarily rely on diagnosis, medication, and laboratory data, quantifying the additional clinical value of latent information to derive actionable insight. Three models—using structured variables only, coded nursing statements only, and a combination of both—were compared to evaluate the incremental contribution of nursing data to discriminative power. Furthermore, SHAP was used to elucidate feature importance and enhance model interpretability, supporting transparent and clinically applicable decision-making for fall prevention. The primary differentiation of this study lies in its clear demonstration that nursing statements do not merely serve as additional features; rather, they capture latent information that is not fully reflected in structured clinical data.

Methods

Data source

Seoul Metropolitan Government–Seoul National University–Boramae Medical Center (SNU–BMC) has converted more than 10 years of EHR data, spanning from 2010 to December 2022, into the observational medical outcomes partnership (OMOP) CDM. The resulting CDM database includes information from over 1.03 million patients. Data elements—such as demographics, medical and family histories, diagnoses, medication exposures, laboratory results, vital signs, surgeries, and procedures—were standardized according to the OMOP CDM (Fig. 1).

Study population

This retrospective analysis included adult patients (≥ 18 years) who were admitted as inpatients or visited the emergency department at Seoul Boramae Hospital between January 1, 2010, and December 31, 2019, and had at least one documented nursing record during their hospital stay (Fig. 2). From this cohort, we selected patients admitted to the Hematology and Medical Oncology, Neurology, or Neurosurgery departments, as previous institutional reports and published literature have identified these wards as having the highest incidence of inpatient falls1. Patients with a hospital length of stay of 3 days or less were excluded to ensure sufficient nursing documentation from the first 3 days of hospitalization for analysis.

Patients who experienced falls were identified using documented nursing statements indicating fall events, including coded statements such as “fell”, “collapsed”, “slipped and fell”, “fell from bed”, “threw body to the floor”, or “sat down abruptly”1. Of the 17,170 patients in the final cohort, 689 (4.0%) were classified as fall patients, and 16,481 (96.0%) as non-fall patients. This study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB).

Coded nursing statements

At SNU–BMC, nursing documentation is recorded using a structured system where coded nursing statements are mapped to unique identifiers, following the framework described by Han et al.10. Nurses document patient status by selecting relevant coded nursing statements from a predefined list within the EHR interface. While the system allows for supplementary entries such as numerical values or free-text comments, this study focused exclusively on the presence of the coded nursing statements.

For feature engineering, predictors were extracted from documentation recorded during the first three days of hospitalization (from the day of admission to the end of the third day). This specific time window was chosen to capture early clinical conditions prior to potential fall events. To avoid redundancy from repetitive charting, all entries within this period were transformed into binary features, indicating the presence (1) or absence (0) of each coded nursing statement. Since the data were already structured and extracted using these unique codes, no natural language processing (NLP) was required.

For each feature set, four machine learning algorithms commonly used in clinical prediction tasks were trained and evaluated: logistic regression, random forest, gradient boosting machine (GBM), and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost). The same modeling and evaluation procedures were applied across all feature sets to ensure a fair comparison.

Data preprocessing and feature selection

Before model development, subject identifiers and outcome variables were removed from the candidate predictor sets. To reduce feature sparsity and dimensionality, covariates with very low prevalence—present in fewer than 1% of the study population—were excluded17.

Feature selection was then performed using L1-regularized logistic regression (LASSO), which automatically selects variables by shrinking the coefficients of less informative predictors toward zero18,19. A logistic regression model with an L1 penalty was fitted to the training data, and features with non-zero coefficients were retained for subsequent machine learning models. This approach has been previously employed to reduce noise and enhance generalizability in high-dimensional clinical prediction tasks.

Model training and evaluation

For each feature set, the study population was randomly divided into training (70%) and testing (30%) subsets. Internal validation was performed on the training set using stratified fivefold cross-validation to ensure robust estimation of model performance for fall risk prediction.

Four supervised machine learning algorithms were developed for fall risk prediction: logistic regression20, random forest21, GBM22, and XGBoost23.

Hyperparameters for all models were predefined based on commonly used values in prior studies and empirical considerations, without applying automated hyperparameter optimization procedures. For logistic regression, L1 regularization was applied with a regularization strength (C) of 0.1 using the liblinear solver. The random forest model was configured with 300 trees and no restriction on maximum tree depth. The gradient boosting model used 200 estimators with a learning rate of 0.05. The XGBoost model employed 300 estimators, a learning rate of 0.05, a maximum depth of 4, and subsampling ratios of 0.8 for both observations and features. A fixed random seed was applied to all models to ensure reproducibility.

Model discrimination was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Sensitivity (recall), precision, accuracy, and F1-score were calculated at the optimal decision threshold, determined by maximizing the Youden index (sensitivity + specificity − 1).

For the test set, 95% CIs for AUROC were computed using the DeLong method. Final model performance was evaluated on the held-out test set, and receiver operating characteristic curves were generated to facilitate comparison across models and feature sets.

Model interpretation

To improve model interpretability and quantify the contribution of individual predictors to fall risk, SHAP was employed24. SHAP is a theoretically grounded method based on cooperative game theory that provides both global and local explanations by estimating each feature’s contribution to individual predictions.

The best-performing model was selected for interpretability based on comparative performance across feature sets and algorithms. SHAP summary plots were generated to visualize overall feature importance and the direction of feature effects across the test dataset, illustrating how higher or lower feature values influence predicted fall risk.

Additionally, SHAP waterfall plots were generated for representative individual cases to provide patient-level explanations. These plots illustrate how specific clinical variables and nursing observations contribute incrementally to the final predicted fall risk, enabling transparent, patient-centered interpretation of model predictions.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using Python (version 3.9.21; Python Software Foundation) and R (version 4.2.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Baseline characteristics were compared between fall and non-fall groups. Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages and compared using the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables were presented as means ± standard deviations and compared using independent t-tests. Predictive model performance was primarily evaluated using the AUROC. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P-value < 0.05.

A total of 17,170 inpatients were admitted to the Hematology and Medical Oncology, Neurology, or Neurosurgery departments. Of these, 689 patients (4.0%) experienced at least one fall during hospitalization. The mean age of patients who fell was 66.79 years, higher than that of patients who did not fall (63.50 years, P < 0.001). The mean length of hospital stay was 33.20 days for fall patients compared with 13.62 days for non-fall patients.

Cerebrovascular disease was more prevalent among patients who did not fall, whereas a prior history of malignant neoplastic disease was the most common comorbidity among patients who fell (Table 1).

For CDM-only features, GBM achieved the highest AUROC (0.733; 95% CI, 0.694–0.773), followed by XGBoost (0.717; 95% CI, 0.678–0.757), random forest (0.702; 95% CI, 0.662–0.743), and LASSO logistic regression (0.693; 95% CI, 0.649–0.737) (Fig. 3a). Accuracy was high across all CDM-only settings (0.842–0.943), but recall remained relatively low (0.271–0.449), indicating limited sensitivity at the selected threshold. Precision ranged from 0.117 to 0.279, and F1-scores ranged from 0.186 to 0.275.

Using coded nursing statements –only features, model discrimination was comparable to or slightly higher than that using CDM-only features, with AUROC values ranging from 0.712 to 0.749. LASSO logistic regression achieved the highest AUROC (0.749; 95% CI, 0.716–0.781) (Fig. 3b). Accuracy varied across models (0.739–0.959), while recall ranged from very low in the random forest model (0.010) to moderate in LASSO logistic regression (0.609). Precision remained modest (0.091–0.200), resulting in F1-scores between 0.018 and 0.187.

When CDM and coded nursing statements features were combined, overall model performance improved modestly, with more balanced classification metrics. GBM and XGBoost achieved the highest AUROCs (0.764; 95% CI, 0.729–0.800; and 0.756; 95% CI, 0.720–0.792; respectively), followed by random forest (0.746; 95% CI, 0.709–0.782) and LASSO logistic regression (0.706; 95% CI, 0.666–0.747) (Fig. 3c). Accuracy remained high across models (0.805–0.962). Recall increased for GBM and XGBoost (0.454 and 0.411, respectively) compared with their CDM-only and nursing statements–only counterparts, while precision also improved (up to 0.173), resulting in the highest F1-scores among all feature sets (0.222 for GBM and 0.243 for XGBoost). Overall, combining CDM and coded nursing statements features provided slightly better discrimination and more favorable precision–recall trade-offs than using either feature set alone.

Precision–recall (PR) curve analysis further highlighted the limitations in model performance under class imbalance (Fig. 4). Across all feature sets, precision declined rapidly as recall increased, indicating a substantial number of false-positive predictions. Models using coded nursing statements–only features showed particularly poor performance, with average precision (AP) values close to baseline (0.101–0.133) (Fig. 4b). In contrast, models incorporating combined features achieved higher AP values (up to 0.297), reflecting improved performance compared to single-source models (Fig. 4c). Nevertheless, precision remained limited across all models, particularly at higher recall levels, suggesting constraints in clinical applicability.

Calibration plot analysis indicated that models using combined features demonstrated improved agreement between predicted and observed probabilities compared with single-feature models, particularly for GBM and XGBoost (Fig. 5c). CDM-only models showed moderate calibration with some deviation at higher predicted probabilities (Fig. 5a), whereas models using coded nursing statements–only features exhibited less stable calibration patterns (Fig. 5b). Despite these improvements, some deviations remained at extreme probability ranges.

Feature importance and SHAP analysis

SHAP-based global feature importance analysis of the GBM model identified the top 15 predictors of inpatient falls among all candidate variables (Fig. 6a–b). Erythrocyte count and body weight were the most influential features based on mean absolute SHAP values, followed by hemoglobin levels. These variables may reflect underlying physiological and clinical conditions associated with fall risk; for example, abnormal erythrocyte or hemoglobin levels may indicate anemia or reduced oxygen-carrying capacity, which can contribute to fatigue, dizziness, and decreased physical resilience, while body weight may be associated with frailty, sarcopenia, or mobility limitations depending on the clinical context25–27. Several nursing statements–derived variables—such as reported weakness, documented fall risk, ineffective breathing pattern, and inpatient visit frequency—also contributed substantially, alongside age (Fig. 6a–b). By contrast, features such as physician notification and erythrocyte distribution width ratio had relatively smaller impacts on model predictions. These findings reflect associations learned by the model and should be interpreted as indicative of feature contributions to prediction rather than causal relationships.

Local SHAP waterfall plots further illustrated how these key features influenced individual-level risk predictions (Fig. 6c–d). In a representative high-risk case, increased inpatient visits, higher body weight, greater comorbidity burden (Charlson index), fluid overload, poor oral intake, and exposure to several medications (e.g., oxycodone, lactulose, dexamethasone, and chlorpheniramine) were major positive contributors, cumulatively raising the predicted fall risk above baseline (Fig. 6c). By contrast, for a low-risk case, preserved hematologic parameters, favorable oxygenation-related indices, and other laboratory findings contributed negatively, offsetting minor positive effects and resulting in a low estimated probability of inpatient falls (Fig. 6d).

Discussion

Principal findings and comparison with previous work

Beyond simple data integration, this study suggests that nursing records capture additional clinically relevant information not inherently present in conventional structured data, thereby highlighting the potential contribution of our approach. In this study, models combining CDM and coded nursing statements features demonstrated slightly higher discrimination and a more balanced trade-off between sensitivity and specificity than models using either feature set al.one, highlighting the complementary roles of structured clinical data and nursing-derived information in predicting inpatient fall risk8. Structured CDM variables—such as diagnoses, procedures, medications, and prior clinical events—primarily captured comorbidities and baseline risk, whereas coded nursing statements features reflected aspects of care, including functional status, mobility, mental status, and care dependency, which are closely associated with imminent fall events6,28.

By integrating these domains, the models were able to combine longitudinal clinical context with near real-time patient status, yielding improved predictive performance. Importantly, many coded nursing statements are generated as bedside nurses continuously observe and document patients’ symptoms, behaviors, and subtle changes in condition29, enabling capture of early safety signals not readily reflected in conventional clinical variables. In this way, nursing documentation does not merely supplement structured data but complements structured data by translating bedside observations into clinically useful information closely linked to real-world care processes and safety outcomes. Consistent with prior studies demonstrating improved performance when both data domains are integrated, coded nursing statements–only models in our analysis performed comparably to, and in some metrics slightly better than, CDM-only settings, suggesting that nursing documentation may encode patient safety–relevant information more directly than conventional clinical variables in the context of falls6,11.

By integrating CDM and coded nursing statements features, our models combined longitudinal clinical context with near real-time patient status, yielding modest but consistent improvements in discrimination and more balanced sensitivity and specificity compared with single-domain models, consistent with prior reports of enhanced predictive performance using combined data approaches30. Ensemble methods, such as GBM and XGBoost, showed the highest overall performance, consistent with previous findings that tree-based gradient boosting algorithms effectively capture nonlinear interactions among heterogeneous predictors31,32. SHAP-based explainability further showed that nursing statements–derived features—such as weakness, documented fall risk, and ineffective breathing pattern—were among the important contributors to model predictions, echoing prior explainable artificial intelligence studies in which impaired mobility and nursing-assessed risk frequently emerged as dominant predictors30,33. By mapping SHAP values to clinically interpretable variables and visualizing them using summary and waterfall plots, our analysis may help improve transparency in model behavior and support individualized risk review. Although SHAP analysis provides insight into the relative importance of predictors, these findings should be interpreted as associative rather than causal. The identified features reflect patterns learned from the data and may not represent direct causal relationships with fall events. In particular, some highly ranked predictors, such as erythrocyte count, hemoglobin level, and body weight, may capture underlying physiological or clinical conditions associated with fall risk rather than acting as independent risk factors. These variables may reflect broader patient status, including frailty, comorbidity burden, or functional decline, highlighting the importance of cautious interpretation of model-derived feature importance within a clinical context.

Together, these findings suggest that nursing statements data provide distinct and clinically relevant information that can influence model risk estimation beyond simply increasing the number of features. Integrating nursing documentation with structured clinical variables may improve the performance and interpretability of inpatient fall risk prediction models while leveraging nurses’ direct observations of patients’ symptoms and daily functioning. Although the combined models demonstrated promising performance, the relatively low precision suggests that a portion of high-risk alerts may not correspond to actual fall events. Additional evaluation using precision–recall curves further highlighted these limitations, particularly under class imbalance. Across all feature sets, precision decreased markedly as recall increased, indicating a high false-positive rate when attempting to identify a larger proportion of patients at risk of falls. Models using only coded nursing statement features showed particularly poor performance, with average precision values close to baseline, whereas models incorporating combined features demonstrated relatively improved performance. Nevertheless, precision remained limited across all models, especially at higher recall levels, suggesting constraints in clinical applicability. Consequently, these models may be more effectively utilized as clinical decision support tools rather than standalone diagnostic systems. Additionally, we deliberately refrained from applying class imbalance handling techniques, such as resampling or class weighting, to preserve the original distribution that reflects real-world fall prevalence. While the observed low precision could potentially increase the false-positive rate and impact clinical workflow efficiency, we prioritized sensitivity to minimize missed fall events, considering the severe clinical consequences associated with inpatient falls. Further multicenter validation, calibration assessment, and workflow-based implementation studies are needed before routine clinical deployment.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, although the OMOP CDM provides a standardized framework for data representation, it has inherent constraints in capturing institution-specific workflows, social determinants of health, and contextual or administrative factors not routinely mapped to standardized vocabularies. Consequently, potentially important non-standardized variables related to fall risk may not have been fully incorporated into the models. Second, fall events were identified using coded nursing documentation rather than independent sources such as incident reporting systems or administrative records. Therefore, some degree of misclassification due to underreporting or overreporting may have influenced model development and evaluation. Third, the analysis was conducted at a single healthcare institution without external validation using independent datasets. As an internal validation study, the generalizability of the findings to other clinical settings remains uncertain. Future multicenter validation using OMOP-CDM networks would help assess robustness across diverse healthcare environments. Fourth, the models were developed using aggregated structured features, which may not have fully captured temporal changes in patient condition during hospitalization. Finally, direct comparison with established fall risk assessment tools such as the Morse Fall Scale and Hendrich II Fall Risk Model was not feasible due to the unavailability of key variables required to compute these scores. This limits the ability to assess the relative performance of the proposed model against widely used clinical tools. Future studies should incorporate the necessary variables to enable direct benchmarking against established tools and to evaluate whether the model provides clinically meaningful improvements in risk stratification and clinical decision-making.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed and evaluated machine learning models for inpatient fall risk prediction by integrating structured CDM variables with coded nursing statements features, demonstrating incremental but meaningful improvements over single-domain models. The primary contribution of this work lies in its ability to extract and validate additional clinically relevant information captured in coded nursing statements, which remain relatively underutilized in traditional structured data environments. Models combining both data sources showed modest yet consistent improvements in discrimination and a more balanced trade-off between sensitivity and specificity. Coded nursing statements–only models performed comparably to—and in some metrics slightly better than CDM-only settings, suggesting that bedside documentation may contain patient safety–relevant information not fully represented in conventional structured fields. By incorporating information from nursing documentation and interpreting model behavior using SHAP analyses, our approach not only identified clinical and nursing statements features associated with model predictions but also provided clinically interpretable insights into model behavior at both the population and individual levels. Collectively, these findings highlight the value of integrating both structured and nursing-derived data within standardized frameworks to potentially improve the performance and interpretability of fall risk prediction. These results suggest that incorporating coded nursing documentation into standardized CDM frameworks may support the development of more transparent and proactive risk-screening approaches. However, despite these improvements, precision remained relatively low across models, particularly at higher recall levels, indicating a substantial number of false-positive predictions. Therefore, these models may be more appropriately used as supportive clinical decision tools rather than standalone diagnostic systems. Also, further validation and evaluation of clinical implementation are required before routine use in decision-support systems.
