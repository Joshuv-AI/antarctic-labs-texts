The Old Lady spider cave skeletons in Ladakh have diverse maternal genetic origin
Authors: Lomous Kumar, Richa Rajpal, Aparna Dwivedi, Pankaj Kumar, Nagarjuna Pasupuleti, Sachin Kumar, Sonam Spalzin, Stanzen Rabyang, Niraj Rai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe archaeological evidence in Ladakh, show early settlement and complex cultural and demographic shifts caused by human migration along the Silk Route corridor, but genetic traces have been entirely absent. To fill this gap, our current study reconstructed complete mitogenomes from ancient individuals found at the Old Lady spider cave site. Our detailed analysis of mitogenomes revealed a highly diverse gene pool that includes maternal lineages associated with East Asia, Central Asia, and South Asia. Bayesian evolutionary analysis, haplotype networks, and divergence times link the C4a1 lineage to East Asia and Siberia, possibly indicating traces of Silk Route migration. Furthermore, these analyses link the H2a1a lineage to recent arrival from Central Asia, while the U7a lineage to a Hazara-like group in Pakistan. These findings point to the maternal genetic imprints of multiple migrations and occupation of the Lady spider cave site in Ladakh by people of diverse maternal lineage.Subject Areas: Archeology, Evolutionary biology, Paleobiology, Paleogenetics

Introduction

Genomic reconstructions indicate that South Asia played a vital role in human migration from Africa to other parts of the world. Indian populations had early and ongoing interactions with groups outside the region, and India experienced multiple population influxes due to invasions that increased genetic diversity.1,2,3,4,5,6 Although the entire Indian subcontinent has diverse ethnic groups, the eastern and northeastern regions, covering the sub-Himalayan area, stand out for their linguistic and population diversity, hosting about three hundred different ethnic groups, each with its own unique language.7

Ladakh, in particular, lies between the Karakoram and Himalayan Mountain ranges. It served as a crossroads connecting Leh to Yarkand and Amritsar to Yarkand, where travelers encountered small settlements in desert oases that functioned as rest stops along traditional routes. These stops feature petroglyphs, rock carvings, inscriptions, monasteries, mani walls, and stupas, indicating that caravan traders used them as landmarks or reference points.8 It has been suggested that the original population of Ladakh may have been the Dard people who migrated into the western Himalayas through the Indus Valley.9 Later, the Dard culture was replaced by an influx of pastoralists and nomads from Tibet. Today, most Muslims in Ladakh are descendants of Sufi masters from Pakistan who settled in the region after the 14th century, while Buddhists mainly trace their ancestry to migrations from the Tibetan Plateau.10 A combination of large-scale population movements, migrations, and settlements has caused demographic changes in the region over time.11,12,13,14,15

Previous genetic studies of present-day individuals have shown that Ladakh has a diverse genetic mosaic of populations due to the aforementioned historical migration events.11,16,17,18 The high frequency and diversity of mitochondrial haplogroup M9 highlight the genetic influence from the Tibetan Plateau and China. The genetic similarity with East Asians is further supported by the prevalence of haplogroup A, which is less common in India but widespread among Tibeto-Burmese groups such as Tibetans and Mongolians.19,20,21

Although significant in South Asia’s migration history, Ladakh’s settlement history is not fully understood. Well-known movements and gene flow from nearby regions have sparked interest in the demography of Ladakh, including its possible genetic mixing with other populations and extinct hominins like Denisovans. Currently, there is limited knowledge about how long humans have inhabited this area and whether, and when, genetic mixing occurred with other modern human groups and extinct hominins. Most studies have focused on the modern Ladakh population, which may be highly mixed and influenced by genetic drift due to isolation.

This research aims to explore the genetic impacts of demographic changes in Ladakh. In this study, we sequenced the entire mitochondrial genome of ancient individuals from Old Lady Spider Cave in Chitkan village, Leh, India. The resulting data have provided a detailed understanding of the people in this large region, including range expansions, colonization, recurring migrations, and historical influences on their genetic diversity.

Results

Reconstructed ancient mitochondrial genomes and AMS dates

Mitochondrial genome sequences (16,569 bp) were successfully recovered from seven ancient individuals sampled at the Ladakh cave site (Figures 1A–1C). The alignments contained 14588–23198 unique mapped reads, with an average coverage of 46.40× to 83.28× and an average mapping quality of 36.72–36.93 (Table S1). Damage patterns identified by mapDamage2 showed increased C-to-T substitution frequencies at read ends, indicating terminal cytosine deamination thus confirming the authenticity of the recovered ancient DNA sequences (Figures S1–S7). Contamination estimates were obtained using the Schmutzi pipeline, one sample (LD25) with a Schmutzi contamination estimate of 0.38, was excluded from further analysis (Table S1). Consensus FASTAs were generated at four quality thresholds (Q10, Q20, Q30, and Q50) using the log file from the final Schmutzi run. The AMS radiocarbon dates for all seven human remains from the Old Lady Spider Cave ranged from AD 536 to AD 585, indicating they belong to the Middle Ages (Table S4).

Mitochondrial haplogroup and geospatial distribution

The mitochondrial haplogroup distributions in ancient Ladakh samples at four quality levels of Schmutzi are presented in Table S3. The ancient samples LD1 (PV658272) and LD36 (PV658277), belonged to mtDNA haplogroup U7a3b (haplogrep score 0.96), which is the most common mtDNA lineage among Iranians (0.17). This haplogroup was also found in Kashmir (0.05) and Kodagu in Karnataka (0.05), with lower frequencies in Pakistan (0.04) and Afghanistan (0.04) (Figure S8). The ancient sample LD2 (PV658273) belonged to haplogroup H2a1a (haplogrep score 1), which is prevalent in Central Asia (Tajikistan; 0.35), China (0.14), and European countries (Finland: 0.11, Denmark: 0.06). This haplogroup also appears at low frequencies in the Kalash (0.03) and other populations from Pakistan (0.03) (Figure S9). The Ladakh sample LD11 (PV658274) belonged to haplogroup M52, which is common in the Philippines (0.41), among Muslims in northern India (0.25), and present at low levels in Yemen (0.05), Saudi Arabia (0.03), and Iran (0.03) (Figure S10). The Ladakh sample LD34 (PV658276) belongs to haplogroup C4a1, which is highly frequent in Siberia (0.27) and China (0.21), and less common in Central Asia (Kyrgyzstan: 0.03, Tajikistan: 0.02) (Figure S11). The mtDNA haplogroup of the LD38 (PV658278) sample is M65a+@16311, it appears at high frequency in Kashmir (0.23) and Pakistan (0.20), and also found at significant levels in Tajikistan (0.10) and Ladakh (0.06) (Figure S12).

Maximum-Parsimony tree

The maximum parsimony tree of U7a was constructed using complete mtDNA sequence data from 95 modern and 6 ancient Eurasians, including the full ancient mitogenome sequences of LD1 and LD36. In this tree, both Ladakh samples (LD1 and LD36) formed a clade with the Hazara sample (KY824947.1) and were part of a major clade that also included samples from Kashmir in north India (Figure S13). In the MP tree of the haplogroup H2a1a, LD2 was positioned between Central Asian and European individuals, indicating its West Eurasian origin (Figure S14). The MP tree for the haplogroup M52 was created using 25 modern and 1 ancient sample from the Swat Valley in Pakistan. Here, the LD11 sample from Ladakh grouped with those of southeast Asian individuals (Aeta population) (Figure S15). In the MP phylogeny of the East Asian mtDNA haplogroup C4a1, the Ladakh sample LD34 was part of a distinct branch within a larger clade of diverse populations, including individuals mainly from East Asia, Central Asia (Tajikistan), and Europe (Figure S16). Finally, the MP tree of the M65 lineage placed the ancient Ladakh (LD38) sample in a deeply rooted, isolated subclade within a major clade shared with Indian Indo-European, ancient Swat Primitive Grave Type (SPGT) samples, Pakistan, Southeast Asia, and Tibetan samples (Figure S17).

Time-calibrated phylogenetic analysis

Furthermore, we tried to reconstruct the evolutionary history of ancient Ladakh samples using time-stamped data from ancient references using a Bayesian approach. For each mtDNA sequence, we used the set of modern and ancient references publicly available for each mitochondrial haplogroup observed in Ladakh historic samples. In the resulting haplotype U7a tree, two ancient Ladakh samples formed a clade with the Hazara sample from Pakistan, along with Central Asian and East Asian samples (Figure 2A). This pattern matches what was seen in the MP tree (Figure S13). In addition, LD2 individual (H2a haplotype) formed a clade with only Central Asian individuals from Tajikistan in the Bayesian tree (Figure 2B). The LD11 individual mainly clustered with North Indian Muslim individuals in the Bayesian tree of M52 (Figure 2C). LD34 shared a node with a large, uniform group of individuals from Central Asia/Siberia. In the C4a1 tree that was rooted with the Bronze Age Ust-Belaya_o (Tajikistan) individual (Figure 3A). The time-stamped Bayesian phylogeny of M65a was rooted to the Loebanr_IA (SPGT) sample, and the Ladakh LD38 sample is placed in a deeply rooted individual clade (Figure 3B), similar to the MP tree (Figure S17).

Haplotype network and time of divergence

The networks for the mitochondrial sequences observed among ancient Ladakh individuals closely matched the relationships inferred through Bayesian analysis. In the haplotype network of U7a, both Ladakh individuals (LD1 and LD36) are shown as a single node and are evolutionarily connected to Hazara (Figure S4A). The divergence time of LD1 and LD36 haplotypes from the SPGT (Udegram_IA) haplotype was approximately 4,024.31 YBP, 95% CI [3124.45–4924.17] (Table S5). In the MJ network of H2a, the LD2 haplotype was located in the Central Asian founder node, which likely indicates its outgrowth (Figure S4B). Its divergence time was around 1,408.34 YBP, 95% CI [876.04–1940.64] from the Khvalynsk_EN haplotype (Table S5).

The haplotype network of M52 clearly distinguished Iranian, Indian, Swat valley historical, and LD11 (ancient Ladakh) samples from clusters in the Philippines. In this network, LD11 individuals shared nodes with North Indian Muslim haplotypes (Figure S4C). The divergence time of the Ladakh M52 (LD11) haplotype from the Swat valley historical haplotype (Saidu_Sharif_H) is approximately 6,841.322 YBP, 95% CI [5668.045–8014.599] (Table S5). The pattern observed in the C4a1 haplotype network was consistent with the Bayesian phylogeny of C4a1, where the LD34 haplotype shared nodes with Central Asian/Siberian clusters (Figure S4D). The Ladakh haplotype diverged from the Ust-Belaya (I20760) (Tajikistan) haplotype around 6,840.909 YBP, 95% CI [5667.703–8014.115] (Table S5). The haplotype network for M65a showed the LD38 sample to originate from a central node shared by Brokpa, SPGT, Pakistan, and Myanmar haplotypes (Figure S4E). The divergence time of LD38 from the Udegram_IA (SPGT) haplotype is 9,657.17 YBP, 95% CI [8519.062–10795.278] (Table S5).

Discussion

Ladakh played a crucial role in human migration, cultural exchange, and connections between South Asia, East Asia, and Central Asia due to its central location. It has functioned as a melting pot of different cultures, languages, and genetic diversity. Historically, Ladakh held significant importance in northwest India and was known for its Buddhist relics, valuable metals like gold and silver, and luxurious fabrics. The region was also famous for resources such as borax, pashmina, salt, and copper. Ladakh’s trade routes connecting it with Tibet, Pendzikent, Yarkand, Khotan, Miran, Bactria, and Iran were vital trade arteries.

The genetic studies conducted thus far here focused on present-day populations of Ladakh, which show influences from local, Central Asian, and East Asian sources on its gene pool (Kumar et al., 2024; Kumar et al., 2023; Rowold et al., 2016; Singh et al., 2020). Although genetic evidence from modern populations is conclusive, it lacks direct clues about past demographic changes and their cultural connections across different periods. In this study, we aimed to identify these patterns primarily through a detailed investigation of maternal genetic diversity in a crucial cave dwelling (Old Lady Spider cave) from Middle Ages in Ladakh. Our ancient DNA analysis reveals a diverse genetic makeup at this cave site, with contributions from East Asia, Central Asia/Siberia, and Pakistan, indicating significant population movements. Among the mitochondrial haplogroups in ancient Ladakh samples, U7a is mostly Iranian-specific with a minor presence in Afghanistan, Pakistan, and Kashmir. For this lineage in samples LD1 and LD36, both Bayesian and haplotype network analyses show closer links to the Hazara tribe, suggesting migration from nearby Afghanistan and Pakistan. The divergence timing of this sequence indicates it predates the Bronze Age and is associated with SPGT people. By contrast, the H2a haplotype in Ladakh appears to have coalesced more recently, and our analysis suggests that it was brought by the individual was likely a migrant from Central Asia, possibly from Tajikistan. The distribution pattern, supported by Bayesian and network analyses, connects the individual LD11 from Ladakh to a local (Kashmir region) Muslim migrant. However, Maximum-Parsimony (MP) tree supports the alternate hypothesis of migration from southeast Asia (relationship to Aeta population). Both of these inferences remain to be validated by using whole genome-based analysis method to arrive at any conclusion. Additionally, its older coalescent time suggests this lineage arrived much earlier during ancient times into the region.

Moreover, we found haplogroup C4a1 in one of the cave site individuals (LD34), a lineage that is present in East Asia, Siberia, and Central Asia. This lineage could be linked to migrants involved in trade along the Silk Road, as suggested by artifacts found during archaeological excavations nearby. Both our Bayesian evolutionary analysis and haplotype network support this connection, further reinforcing the link between the Ladakh region and Silk Road trade.

By contrast the only ancient Ladakh individual (LD38), associated with the mtDNA haplogroup M65a, seems to be a predominantly local resident with deep ancestral roots (coalescence time of 9657 YBP). Previous genetic study of the current population in Jammu and Kashmir also reported a high frequency of this mtDNA haplogroup in the area,22 though surprisingly, we did not find evidence of East Asian (M9) or Tibetan (A21) lineages among Middle age samples.

In conclusion, our study provides the genetic indication of movement through the Trans-Himalayan Corridor and the Silk Road. Our analysis supports the cosmopolitan nature of the ancient Ladakh cave site by showing the presence of local, Central Asian, Pakistani, Siberian, and East Asian maternal lineages. These findings further bolster archaeological evidence pointing to contact with various populations via multidirectional migrations. However, the ancient individuals from Ladakh cave site lack some well-known maternal lineages from East Asia (M9) and Tibet (A21) that exist in the current demography of the region. Since this is the preliminary genetic study with ancient human samples from only single site of Spider-Lady Cave (majorly Middle Ages), additional sampling through various time frames and diverse sites and more detailed analysis utilizing whole genome sequencing will help clarify the missing links to East Asia.

Limitations of the study

On the one hand, this study provides a very insightful genetic history of the Old Lady Spider cave site in particular. It also lacks the resolution of a larger sample size and genome-wide markers from whole-genome sequencing. This study is relevant for explaining the major genetic history of maternal origins of individuals at the Old Lady Spider cave site in Ladakh and lacks the resolution of genome level study. There are also limitations associated with some of the methodologies applied to mitochondrial genome analysis, such as the strict molecular clock assumption, which is prone to rate heterogeneity among divergent lineages.23 Further, the use of rho and sigma statistics for the estimation of coalescence age is sometimes error-prone due to differences in demographic history24 (such as the presence of drift, bottlenecks, and founder events in the populations).

Experimental model and study samples

Archaeological site and sampling

The sampling site (Old Lady Spider cave site) for this study is located in the Chitkan village (34°28′13″N 76°30′37″E) in the Kargil district of the Union Territory of Ladakh, India (Figure 1A). We recovered seven human skeletal remains along with three animal bone remains at the cave site (Figure 1C). We proceeded with radiocarbon dating and the extraction of genomic DNA from these seven human bone samples (Table S4).

Ethics declarations

Ethics approval: Not required for ancient DNA study. In India unless sampling require excavation, prior approval is not required from any authority concerned. Samples were collected by surface exploration in current study.

Method details

Ancient DNA extraction and mitochondrial DNA genotyping

All work related to ancient DNA was conducted following strict aDNA standards in the clean laboratory of Birbal Sahni Institute of Palaeosciences, Lucknow, India. The outer surface of each bone was manually cleaned with bleach to eliminate potential contamination during excavation. Bone powder was generated by drilling into the bone with a fine drill, and the resulting white powder was collected in sterile 2 mL Eppendorf tubes. Ancient DNA was extracted using an extraction buffer containing 10 M urea, 0.5 M EDTA, and 10 μL of proteinase K (20 mg/mL), followed by incubation at 37 °C for 24 h. After centrifuging each tube at 4,000 rpm for 5 min, the pellet containing cell debris was discarded, and the DNA-containing supernatant was transferred to a 4-mL AmiconTM filter (Sigma-Aldrich).

The next step involved concentrating the DNA samples because their volume exceeded 1 mL. We reduced the volume to 250 μL by spinning the samples through the filters for 25 min. The supernatant was transferred to a 2 mL Eppendorf tube containing 5× PB binding buffer. The PB sample mixture (prepared separately for each tube) was transferred to MinElute spin columns (Qiagen) and centrifuged at 7,000 rpm for 1 min, after which the eluate was discarded. A volume of 710 μL of PE wash buffer (Qiagen) was added to each filter and spun at 10,000 rpm for 1 min. After discarding the supernatant, ethanol was used to wash away the salts, and each tube was spun at 14,000 rpm for 1 min to remove residual ethanol. The collection tube was then discarded. The filters were placed into fresh 1.5 mL Eppendorf tubes, and 45 μL of EB elution buffer (Qiagen) was added to the center of each filter. This step was followed by a 15-min incubation at 37 °C. After spinning the tube at 14,000 rpm for 1 min, an additional 30 μL of EB buffer was added to the same filter to recover the remaining bound DNA molecules, incubated at 37 °C for 10 min, and centrifuged again. The filter was then discarded. This process was repeated for each tube. The double-stranded library was prepared from the isolated DNA, checked for quality on the Agilent TapeStation 4200, and then further sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Processing raw data and mtDNA consensus calling

Raw sequence data were processed using the EAGER pipeline,25 and an mtDNA consensus FASTA was generated with the Schmutzi pipeline.26 First, adapter and index sequences were trimmed with AdapterRemoval v2.3.2 27. Shotgun sequences longer than 30 bp were aligned to the complete human mitochondrial reference (rCRS) using BWA37 (parameters -n 0.01 and seed = 16500). Duplicates were removed with DeDup v0.12.8 25, and reads with a mapping quality below 30 were discarded.

The Schmutzi pipeline was run in two steps. First, contDeam calculated the endogenous deamination rate and initial contamination estimates. Two, iterative procedures were then performed—initially without contamination prediction, and subsequently with prediction—to generate both endogenous and contaminant consensus FASTA files. The final consensus FASTA was created using log2fasta at four quality thresholds (Q10, Q20, Q30, and Q50). mtDNA haplogroups were determined for all four quality cutoff scores for comparison. Mitochondrial haplogroups were determined using Haplogrep228 tool. The geospatial haplogroup frequency maps were generated using automap29 package in R and published reference samples, with total sample count and corresponding reference for each mtDNA haplogroup mentioned in Table S2.

Maximum-Parsimony (MP) tree using mtPhyl

An evolutionary phylogeny was inferred using the maximum parsimony method implemented in mtPhyl v5.003. The consensus FASTA files of ancient Ladakh samples at Q30, along with reference sequences of corresponding haplogroups, was aligned separately for each haplogroup in Mega 1130 using the MUSCLE algorithm. The phylogenetic trees for the aligned fasta sequences of Ladakh and reference samples of corresponding mtDNA haplogroups were constructed using the maximum parsimony method. All positions with gaps and missing data were removed (complete deletion).

Reconstruction of bayesian phylogeny

Sequence alignment of data for ancient individuals (at Q30) was performed using MUSCLE in MEGA11. The alignment file was cleaned of gaps and missing sites and then used to infer the Bayesian phylogenetic tree in BEAST v2.6.7 31. We first identified the best partitioning scheme for our combined data with PartitionFinder v2.1.1 32, then fitted the substitution model using jModelTest2.33 We applied a coalescent constant population model as the tree prior, along with a strict molecular clock, and ran three independent chains of 50,000,000 MCMC iterations. The initial 5,000,000 iterations were discarded as burn-in, and samples were taken every 10,000 iterations. Tracer v1.7 was used to assess convergence and effective sample size of the MCMC runs. The log files and tree files from all three runs were combined using LogCombiner v2.6.7. The consensus tree was generated using TreeAnnotator, and the final phylogenetic tree was visualized with iTOL (interactive tree of life).34

Evolutionary haplotype network and TMRCA

Median-joining haplotype networks were constructed using the PopART tool.35 FASTA alignments of complete mitochondrial genome sequences including the Ladakh historic samples and reference sample haplotypes of corresponding mitochondrial haplogroup only (which includes the HVS and coding regions), were used to build the networks. The time to the most recent common ancestor (TMRCA) of mitochondrial haplotypes was estimated with rho statistics and variance using sigma statistics implemented in NETWORK 10.2.0 (https://www.fluxus-engineering.com).

AMS radiocarbon dating of bone samples

All bone samples were subjected to radiocarbon dating at the Isotope Laboratory of the Birbal Sahni Institute of Palaeosciences using the method outlined in Sealy et al. (2014).38 The samples were graphitized with the ‘Automated Graphitization Equipment 3’ (Ionplus AG, Switzerland), combined with the Vario Isotope Select Elemental Analyzer (Elementar Analysensysteme GmbH, Germany), and the ‘Isoprime precisION’ IRMS (Elementar Analysensysteme GmbH, Germany) situated in the Radiochronology and Isotopic Characterization Laboratory of the Birbal Sahni Institute of Palaeosciences, Lucknow. The graphitization process followed the protocols specified in Agnihotri et al. (2020).39 The resulting graphite targets were analyzed for radiocarbon dating by Isotoptech Zrt. in Debrecen, Hungary. Quality control samples, IAEA-C3 (Cellulose) pMC for modern: 129.79 ± 0.33 and IAEA-C5 (Two Creek Wood) pMC: 22.95 ± 0.14 for ancient, were included. The 14C age of the IAEA-C5 was determined as 11823 ± 49 years. Data calibration was performed using CALIB REV8.2 and the IntCal20.14 curve.40,41 All dates are expressed as cal. BP.
