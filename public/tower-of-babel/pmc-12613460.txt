Genome-wide association study of Ethiopian barley germplasm reveals putative genomic loci underlying root system architectural traits at seedling stage
Authors: Birhanu Babiye, Alemayehu Teressa Negawo, Wondimu Fekadu Ejerso, Gizachew Haile Gidamo, Sisay Kidane Alemu, Surafel Shibru Teklemariam, Adugna Abdi Woldesemayat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe root system architectural (RSA) trait is one of the factors that contribute to yield. In the current situation, developing new crop cultivars with efficient RSA has great potential to enhance soil resource use efficiency and improve crop production and productivity. The aim of this study was to determine the SNP markers associated with the development of RSA traits in natural growing conditions without biotic and abiotic stress conditions at the seedling stage. In this study we evaluated the natural variation of RSA traits of 260 diverse Ethiopian barley germplasm and used to identify genomic regions associated with root traits. The genotypes were grown for 21 days in plastic pots and phenotyped for 11 RSA traits. The barley genotypes were genotyped using the barley 50kiSelect SNP arrays and genome-wide association study was performed using the FarmCPU model to detect genotype phenotype associations.ResultsThe RSA traits heritability values ranged from Shoot Height (0.52) to Root Area (0.991). A significant positive correlation was observed between Biomass yield with root dry weight and shoot dry weight. In addition, positive correlation was observed between average seminal root length with root area and root density. The GWAS results identified 7 significance marker trait associations that contributed to the natural root trait of barley. For the root traits, SRN (Seminal root number) 5 markers from chromosome 3 H, 4 H and 5 H and for trait Rar (Root Area) 2 markers on chromosome 1 H demonstrated strong association with Root System Architecture traits. All the significant SNPs had minor allele frequencies between 0.037 and 0.496, with R² values ranging from 1.35 to 11.42 amount covered the phenotype variation.ConclusionsThis root traits associated alleles may be important to improve barley for resource use efficiency.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07549-9.

Introduction

Barely (Hordeum vulgare L.) is the member of Poaceae family and among the major cereal crops and one of the earliest adapted crops for human food and animal feed [1, 2]. Barley is a self-pollinated diploid crop with a small chromosome number (n = 7) and the ability to adapt to different agro-ecological conditions [3]. Barley is a fibrous crop, which has a seminal and post-embryonic nodal roots [4]. Lateral root and root hair are parts of the root used to absorb water and nutrients from the soil [5].

Exploiting the natural potential of plants is one of the crop improvement methods [4]. Root development is a key aspect of crop research and improvement by breeding program, and the crop’s ability to absorb water and nutrients is related [6]. In the current plant physiology research, root and root-related traits are attracting attention because they are related to yield and genetic information [7].

RSA is the numerical and geometrical position of each embryonic root, primary and seminal root, and the relationship of the post-embryonic root, lateral and adventitious root, to the 3-dimensional space of the soil [8, 9]. RSA is used to mobilize water and nutrients from the soil [8]. For this reason, a thin and long root is preferred and better than a thick and short root [10]. RSA has a positive correlation with crop water use, soil nutrient uptake, yield, and productivity [11, 12]. RSA research is one of the key important plant breeding initiatives for the coming second green revolution [13, 14].

The architecture of plant root systems is crucial to plant growth as they allows plants to make use of soil resources by absorbing nutrients and water [15, 16]. A single seminal root that grows straight from the embryo and several post-embryonic nodal roots that sprout from the stem’s belowground nodes are characteristics of barley roots [17]. In mature barley, when post-embryonic nodal roots make up the majority of the RSA, the seminal root is of minimal significance because it only contributes to the initial intake of water and nutrients [16].

The positive correlation between RSA and crop water and nutrient absorption efficiency was confirmed by the presence of the marker and nutrient use efficiency related to crop yield and root trait [17, 18]. Usually, crop improvement breeding programs focus on improving the above-ground crop organ, neglecting the underground root part [19]. One of the reasons why root research has not received attention is because it is difficult to collect crop root phenotype data in natural environments [20]. In order to overcome this difficulty, root box was initially used to generate useful root traits [21]. Since then many methods were devised such as vertical root pressure force (RPR), which is laborious and time-demanding [22], a high-profile laboratory method using hydroponics and growth chambers [23, 24], and a robust high-throughput laboratory method [25]. By research and naturally occurring mutations to have barley RSA diversity [26]. Laboratory or glasshouse based phenotypic screening techniques are suitable for studying large number of genotypes and have helped to identify genetic loci for RSA in barley. These techniques have made to more easier and simpler to quantify the RSA of seedling plants [27].

RSA regulates the number and spatial distribution of roots, enabling efficient resource gathering and soil exploration [19]. By controlling root branching, depth, and density, the RSA regulates the allocation of resources towards root growth, shoot development, or reproduction in line with the requirements of the plant and environmental conditions [28, 29]. By promoting deep root growth, a narrow root angle maximizes resource uptake for a range of crop species in deep soil [30]. Root traits such as fine root diameter, specific root length, specific root area, and root length density are believed to be essential for boosting plant productivity in natural settings [31].

Among the important RSA traits of barley, a long primary root and a large number of root hairs are responsible to use water and nutrients from the soil adequately and not be susceptible to drought [30, 32]. RSA trait is largely controlled by environmental influences and genetic profile [33]. Because it is controlled by many genes, the RSA trait of barley is a complex trait and has different contributions in different growth stages [8]. It is important to understand the importance of barley RSA to the crop and to introduce it in barley breeding programs [8, 9]. Indirectly, the RSA trait is a selection trait for productivity at the seedling stage [34].

In reaction to environmental changes that are physical, chemical, and biological, root systems can show a high degree of flexibility [35, 36]. For instance, roots exhibited delayed development as an indication of apparent plasticity, including architectural alterations, in response to extreme stress (e.g., soil compaction). Adaptive plasticity, or the ability to adjust to changes in the environment, may be improved by these architectural modifications [36]. Plants cultivated on soil patches rich in nutrients developed more and longer fine lateral roots, which in turn improved the total specific root length [37].

Barley exhibits diverse root characteristics that play a vital role in its growth, development, and adaptability to various environmental conditions [38, 39]. Key root traits such as root length, depth, thickness, and branching pattern contribute to efficient water and nutrient uptake, especially under stress conditions like drought or nutrient-poor soils [40]. The genetic variation in these root traits among barley genotypes provides valuable opportunities for breeding programs aimed at improving stress tolerance and yield performance [41, 42].

Studies have identified three barley QTL regions directly related to barley RSA under normal growth conditions [4]. Similarly, 119 QTL were associated with 29 root traits in rice [38]. In other crops such as wheat [39], maize [40], and barley, the RSA trait has been studied as a polygenic trait [5, 39]. There are various modern molecular genomic tools to study Barley’s RSA and related traits, one of which is the genome-wide association study (GWAS) [5]. GWAS is a genomics tool used to study the association of phenotype and genotype data of RSA and other traits to identify SNP markers responsible for each trait [40].

Studying the root system architecture of barley is important because roots play a critical role in water and nutrient uptake, directly influencing plant growth, stress tolerance, and yield. Understanding the variation in root traits such as depth, length, and branching helps identify genotypes better adapted to drought, poor soils, and changing climates. This knowledge supports the development of improved barley varieties through breeding programs aimed at enhancing resource efficiency and crop productivity. The objective of this study was to characterize the natural variation in root system architecture among Ethiopian barley germplasm and to identify significant QTL regions associated with root system traits through GWAS using high-density SNP markers.

Materials and methods

Mapping panel

A total of 260 barley collections, including 21 barley breeding line from the Barley Improvement Program of Holeta Agricultural Research Centre and 239 landraces from the Ethiopian Biodiversity Institute were used in this study [42]. The 260 barley collections were established to represent Ethiopia’s agro-ecological zones, and geographical areas. In addition, the mapping panels were designed to contain 50 six-row and 210 two-row Barley Core Collections. The passport data of the mapping panel, along with the required information, was collected and utilised as the primary reference for the panel (Additional file 6).

Barley growth conditions

The forest soil sample was collected from the forest at the Holeta Agricultural Research Center (Altitude 2356 M, Latitude 9o 332.41836 N, and Longitude 38o 8o 30’ 19.8792” E) and used for the experiment. A soil sample was taken by digging at a depth of 30 cm after removing the unnecessary top cover. Then the macro- and micronutrient content, and the pH level of soil samples were determined at Holeta agricultural research center’s Soil Science Research.

The collected forest soil sample was dried in a shed environment for 10 days, crushed and sieved. The sieved soil was mixed with sand, compost, and forest soil at 1:1:2 ratios. One (1 kg) of the mixed soil was weighed and added to a 1.5 kg holding capacity of plastic pot and then 100 ml water was added for 8 days, in two-day intervals. The unwanted weeds sprouted on the soil were removed from the plastic pots.

Randomized complete block design (RCBD) was used for the experiment. The experimental materials were randomly sown in the pots with three replications. Randomization was done using Research Randomization online software [43]. All replications were sown on the same day to avoid non-biological discripancy. Three [3] randomly selected seeds were sown on each individual pot. Sowing was based on a single line placing of seeds, one in the middle and the other two on each side. After 5 days of sowing date, the two seedlings were uprooted while one was allowed to grow for 21 days. For 21 days of growth, the seedlings were watered with a 100 ml of tap water at every 3 days interval. Weeding and other managements were undertaken once a week.

Root system phenotyping

The sprouted seedling was allowed to grow for 21 days, after which, the root phenotype data were collected from all the three replicates. The shoot height of the seedlings was measured in centimeters from the soil to the last leaf shoot by using measuring ruler. The seedling root was separated from the soil by carefully scraping the soil by hand and carefully tearing off the plastic containing the root. The root was then washed in three rounds respectively with the tap water filled with three different water tanks of 30 litters each. The washed roots were dried with a dry cloth. After carefully spreading the dried root on a table with a ruler and labelling the genotype, the image of the root was taken with a digital camera. Photos were taken from a fixed height of 50 cm at a 90° angle to ensure consistent resolution and image quality. Data necessary for the experiment were generated by entering the captured photo into the image software [43]. Shoot and root dry biomass was measured by drying the pulped root and shoot in a 65°c oven for 48 h. The dry matter was weighed in grams with a sensitive balance (Additional file 1). Genotyping

Three seeds from each of the 260 barley accessions were grown in a greenhouse under day/night conditions of 16 h/8 h, with temperatures of 20–22 °C during the day and 17–19 °C at night [44]. The seeds were planted in multipot trays filled with Einheitserde ED73 soil, which contains 14% N, 16% P₂O₅, and 18% K₂O (kg/m³) (H. Nitsch & Sohn GmbH & Co. KG, Germany). When the plants reached the two- to three-leaf stage, leaf samples of approximately 300 mg were collected from a single plant per accession for genotyping. Genomic DNA was extracted using a modified CTAB (cetyltrimethylammonium bromide) method [45] and genotyped with the barley Illumina 50 K iSelect SNP array at TraitGenetics GmbH, Gatersleben, Germany [46]. An initial set of 40,387 SNP markers was obtained. After removing all monomorphic markers and performing imputation using Beagle, a final set of 10,644 high-quality SNP markers was retained. This was achieved by applying filtering thresholds of 5% missing values, 3% minor allele frequency, and 12.5% heterozygosity [41, 42, 47]. SNP pruning was based on physical distance and pairwise linkage disequilibrium (LD) between adjacent markers to retain representative SNPs across the genome without bias (Additional file 2).

Statistical analysis

Correlation, density curve and scatter plot analyses

The root-related parameter data were tested for normality within each group using the Shapiro - Wilk test and visual inspection of Q-Q plots, prior to conducting any statistical analysis. These tests were performed using Minitab software to ensure that the assumptions of normality were met for reliable and accurate analysis [47, 48]. Normal distribution test and quintile- quintile (QQ-Plot) test were tested at a P value of 0.05. The normal distributed RSA data were entered into the analysis. The persons correlation coefficients, Scatter plots, and density plots analyses was constructed for RSA traits using the R software (version 4.5), Core package [49]. The three statistical analyses - correlation, density curve, and scatter plot- were primarily conducted to assess the relationships among the collected RSA traits, evaluate the strength and direction of their associations, and visualize the data distribution. Correlation analysis helped determine whether specific RSA parameters were positively or negatively related, while the density curves provided insights into the overall distribution patterns of each trait. The scatter plots were used to visually examine the spread, trends, and potential outliers within the data, supporting the identification of meaningful trait interactions.

Principal component analysis (PCA)

Principal Component Analysis was performed to assess the population structure among the barley accessions based on their root system architecture traits. The PCA results were visualized in a circular format to clearly display the distribution and grouping of accessions across the principal components. To further explore the trait relationships within identified clusters, correlation matrices were calculated for each group. These matrices provided insight into how different RSA traits were interrelated within specific clusters. All analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.5), Core package [49]. The statistical significance of the correlation coefficients was assessed at a p-value threshold of 0.05, ensuring that only meaningful relationships between traits were considered.

The facet analysis

The normalized data were analyzed for all RSA traits using facet analysis R software (version 4.5), with the GGplot2 R package [50]. Facet analysis is an analysis designed to evaluate the similarity nature of the study materials for each RSA trait. The generated RSA traits were grouped into three subplots based on their similarity at p-value 0.05 using Faceted Smallest Space Analysis [51]. In the facet analysis, each group was assigned a distinct color to visually differentiate clusters. The traits exhibiting the best performance within each group were identified by comparing their average values of the measured RSA traits, highlighting their relative importance in defining the facet structure.

To control for multiple testing in the genome-wide association study, both the Bonferroni correction and False Discovery Rate (FDR) procedures were applied at a significance level of 0.05. The Bonferroni correction adjusted the p-value threshold by dividing 0.05 by the total number of SNPs tested (10,644), resulting in a stringent cutoff of approximately 4.7 × 10−6. Additionally, the Benjamini-Hochberg method was used to control the FDR at 0.05, allowing a balanced control of false positives while maintaining statistical power. Alongside these adjustments, a LOD score threshold of 4.5 was applied, with markers exceeding this value considered significant. This combined strategy ensured robust identification of significant markers associated with barley root system architecture traits.

The PCs analysis was to explain the contribution of 11 measured RSA traits to the total variation of barley genotypes (Fig. 2). The first three PCs accounted for more than 34.3% of the total variance. The gradually decreasing variance explained by each principal component indicates that the barley RSA traits contribute relatively evenly to the total variation in the dataset.

Discussion

In the current scenario, RSA research is focusing on the Plant Breeding Program because of its importance in improving agricultural production and productivity and its direct correlation with crop yield. Accordingly, various studies have been conducted to identify SNP markers associated with RSA related traits in different cereal crops using GWAS genomics tool in Barley [4, 7, 30, 61, 62], Rice [29, 31, 38, 41], wheat [27, 54, 63], maize [25, 64, 65], sorghum [13, 15, 30]. In this study, natural RSA related traits without exposing any biotic and abiotic stress have been investigated and identified for their association with SNP markers based on Ethiopian barley germplasm, while the identified 7 SNPs for two traits such as SRN and Rar RSA related traits have un association; with the narrow sense heritability of all traits has the value range of 0.209–0.991 and FDR < 0.05% : correspondingly [7], for osmotic stress identified 276 SNPs which have FDR < 0.05% identified [94, 95, 96, 97]. [67] identified 186 SNPs with FDR < 0.05% associated with RSA traits in drought stress which is completely different to the current study. The lowest narrow sense heritability means that the genetic contribution is low and in the other direction the trait has a higher environmental contribution in most cases. The highest narrow sense heritability shows that the genetic control of the trait is high compared to the environment [30, 67–70].

The correlations (r2) between RD and RDW are highly significance; this suggests that RD and RDW have a somewhat favorable connection [5]. This implies that the RDW tends to rise together with the RD [71, 72]. The relationship between ASRL and RDW is weak but significant positive correlation [73]. This may suggest that higher dry weight is a result of the number of seminal roots [74]]. The correlation of SH and Rar Significant but Weak positive connection in between [75]. The root ASRL and Rar have a favorable relationship between; this could suggest that longer roots are associated with a higher root area [27, 66, 72]. For the majority of RSA trait pairs, the scatterplots in the lower triangle display scattered patterns that suggest hilly positive correlation [77, 78]. The scatterplot for RD and RDW displays a more clustered pattern, verifying the higher correlation that was found [79]. Each RSA trait’s distribution is shown by the diagonal density charts [27]. Understanding the dynamics of the barley natural root growth may depend on the interactions between roots and shoot properties such as Rar and SH [80, 101, 102]. Conclusion

This study identified 7 significant SNP markers linked to SRN and Rar RSA traits in diverse Ethiopian barley germplasm, underscoring the strong genetic influence on root development under natural, non-stress conditions. The high narrow-sense heritability observed for most RSA traits indicates promising potential for selective breeding. Despite phenotyping limitations, such as the absence of advanced hydroponic systems, the manually collected data remained stable and informative, proving that cost-effective methods can still yield valuable genetic insights. These findings emphasize the critical role of RSA traits in enhancing barley productivity and adaptability.

To advance this research, future efforts should integrate modern phenotyping tools and molecular validation techniques, including gene expression analysis, histochemical assays, and CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing. Such approaches will be crucial for functionally characterizing candidate genes and accelerating RSA-focused breeding programs.
