Optimal Transfer Learning for Missing Not-at-Random Matrix Completion
Akhil Jalan, Yassir Jedra, Arya Mazumdar, Soumendu Sundar Mukherjee, Purnamrita Sarkar

Abstract
We study transfer learning for matrix completion in a Missing Not-at-Random (MNAR) setting that is motivated by biological problems. The target matrix Q Q has entire rows and columns missing, making estimation impossible without side information. To address this, we use a noisy and incomplete source matrix P P , which relates to Q Q via a feature shift in latent space. We consider both the active and passive sampling of rows and columns. We establish minimax lower bounds for entrywise estimation error in each setting. Our computationally efficient estimation framework achieves this lower bound for the active setting, which leverages the source data to query the most informative rows and columns of Q Q . This avoids the need for incoherence assumptions required for rate optimality in the passive sampling setting. We demonstrate the effectiveness of our approach through comparisons with existing algorithms on real-world biological datasets.

Notation and Problem Setup

We use lowercase letters

to denote (real) scalars, boldface
\bm{x},\bm{y},\bm{z}
to denote vectors, and uppercase

to denote matrices. For
n\geq 1
, let
[n]:=\{1,\dots,n\}
,

be the identity matrix and
(\bm{e}_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
the canonical basis vectors. Let

and

. For multisets
S,T
and

, let

be the submatrix with row and column indices in

respectively, possibly with repeated entries from

. Let
\otimes
denote the tensor (Kronecker) product: for
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n},B\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times t}
,
(A\otimes B)\in\mathbb{R}^{ms\times nt}
with
(A\otimes B)_{i(r-1)+v,j(s-1)+w}=A_{ij}B_{vw}
. We denote the Frobenius norm as
\|A\|_{F}
, max norm as
\|A\|_{\textup{max}}:=\max_{i,j}\left|A_{ij}\right|
, and

norm as
\|A\|_{2\to\infty}:=\max_{i}\|A^{T}\bm{e}_{i}\|_{2}
. Asymptotics
O(\cdot),o(\cdot),\Omega(\cdot),\omega(\cdot)
are with respect to
m\land n
unless specified otherwise. Recall that, for integer
n,d
such that
d\leq n
, the
Stiefel manifold

\mathcal{O}^{n\times d}

[
32
]
consists of all
U\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
such that
U^{T}U=I_{d}
.

We now define matrix incoherence, which measures how concentrated the entries of the singular vectors are.

Definition 1.1

(Incoherence)
.

Let
M
be an
m\times n
matrix of rank
d
, and write its SVD as
M=U\Sigma V^{\top}
. The left (resp. right) incoherence parameter of
M
is defined as
\mu_{U}=m\|U\|_{2\to\infty}^{2}/d
(resp.
\mu_{V}=n\|V\|_{2\to\infty}^{2}/d
). The incoherence parameter of
M
is defined as
\mu(M):=\max\{\mu_{U},\mu_{V}\}
.

We now formally define the distribution shift from
P
to
Q
, which generalizes the latent space rotation model
[
71
,
53
]
.

Definition 1.2

(Matrix Transfer Model)
.

In the matrix transfer model, we have source and target matrices
P,Q\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
such that:

(i)
(Low-Rank) Let
P=U_{P}\Sigma_{P}V_{P}^{\top}
for some
d\leq m\land n
where
U_{P}\in\mathcal{O}^{m\times d},V_{P}\in\mathcal{O}^{n\times d}
, and
\Sigma_{P}\succeq 0
is diagonal
d\times d
.

(ii)
(Distribution shift) There exist
T_{1},T_{2},R\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
such that
Q=U_{P}T_{1}RT_{2}^{T}V_{P}^{T}
, and
\|T_{i}\|_{2}=O(1)
for
i=1,2
.

We will define the parameter space as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{m,n,d}=\bigg\{(P,Q)\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}:P=U\Sigma_{P}V^{T},

\displaystyle Q=UT_{1}RT_{2}^{T}V^{T},U\in\mathcal{O}^{m\times d},V\in\mathcal{O}^{n\times d},

\displaystyle T_{1},T_{2},R\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d},\Sigma_{P}\succeq 0\bigg\}

(3)

Definition
1.2
requires that the
d
-dimensional features of rows and columns lie in a shared subspace for
P,Q
. Consider the matrix of associations between
m
genetic variants (e.g. the MC1R gene) and
n
phenotypes (e.g. dark hair) for different populations
P,Q
(e.g. England and Spain)
[
53
]
. The above model ensures that the latent feature vector for a genotype (resp. phenotype) in
Q
is a linear combination of those in
P
.

Note that
T_{1},T_{2}
are not necessarily rotations and can even be singular. We set
\|T_{i}\|_{2}=O(1)
to simplify theorem statements, but it is not required.

2
Main Findings

We first show that without transfer – side information from the source data
P
– completing the target matrix
Q
is impossible. To this end, we present a minimax lower bound on the expected prediction error. First, we define the parameter space of matrices with bounded incoherence:

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{mn}^{(d)}=\bigg\{Q\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}:

\displaystyle\text{ rank}(Q)\leq d,

\displaystyle\mu(Q)\leq O\big(\log(m\lor n)\big)\bigg\}.

(4)

Proposition 2.1

(Minimax Error of MNAR Matrix Completion Without Transfer)
.

Let
m,n\geq 1
and
d\leq m\land n
. Let
\Psi=(Q,\sigma,p_{\textup{Row}},p_{\textup{Col}})
where
Q\in\mathcal{T}_{mn}^{(d)}
,
\sigma^{2}>0
, and
p_{\textup{Row}},p_{\textup{Col}}\in[0,1]
. Let
\mathop{\mathbb{P}}_{\Psi}
denote the law of the random matrix
\widetilde{Q}
defined as in Eq. (
2
) with
\sigma_{Q}=\sigma
, and denote the expectation under this law as
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\Psi}
. The minimax rate of estimation is:

\displaystyle\inf_{\hat{Q}}\sup_{Q\in\mathcal{T}_{mn}^{(d)}}\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}p_{\textup{Row}}\leq.99\\
p_{\textup{Col}}\leq.99\end{subarray}}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\Psi}\left[\frac{1}{mn}\|Q-\hat{Q}\|_{F}^{2}\right]\geq\Omega(d\sigma^{2}).

An immediate consequence of the above proposition is that the minimax rate for max squared error
\|\hat{Q}-Q\|_{\textup{max}}^{2}
is also
\Omega(d\sigma^{2})
. We see that in both error metrics, vanishing estimation error is impossible without transfer learning.

2.1
Lower Bound for Active Sampling Setting

We now give a minimax lower bound for
Q
estimation in the active sampling setting.

Theorem 2.2

(Minimax Lower Bound for
Q
-estimation with Active Sampling)
.

Fix
m,n
and
2\leq d\leq m\land n
. Fix
\sigma^{2}>0
and let
\left|\Omega\right|=T_{\textup{row}}\cdot T_{\textup{col}}
.

Let
\mathop{\mathbb{P}}_{P,Q,\sigma^{2}}
be the distribution of
(\widetilde{P},\widetilde{Q})
where
\widetilde{P}:=P
and
\widetilde{Q}:=Q+G
where
G_{ij}\mathop{\sim}\limits^{i.i.d}N(0,\sigma^{2})
.

Let
\mathcal{Q}
be the
class of estimators
which observe
\widetilde{P}
, and choose row and column samples according to the budgets
T_{\textup{row}},T_{\textup{col}}
as in Eq. (
1
), and then return some estimator
\hat{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
.
Then, there exists absolute constant
C>0
such that minimax rate of estimation is:

\displaystyle\inf\limits_{\hat{Q}\in\mathcal{Q}}\sup_{(P,Q)\in\mathcal{F}_{m,n,d}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathop{\mathbb{P}}_{P,Q,\sigma^{2}}}[\|\hat{Q}-Q\|_{\max}^{2}]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{Cd^{2}\sigma^{2}}{\left|\Omega\right|}.

We prove Theorem
2.2
using a generalization of Fano’s method
[
68
]
. We construct a family of distributions indexed by
d^{2}
source/target pairs
(P^{(s)},Q^{(s)})_{s=1}^{d^{2}}
. The source
P
is the same for all
s
, while each pair of target matrices
Q^{(s)},Q^{(s^{\prime})}
differs in at most
2
entries. For example, say entries
(5,6)
and
(8,7)
are different between
Q^{(1)}
and
Q^{(2)}
. Regardless of the choice of row/column samples, the
average
KL divergence of a pair of targets is small. If e.g. the entries
(5,6),(8,7)
are heavily sampled, then the estimator can distinguish
Q^{(1)},Q^{(2)}
well, but cannot distinguish
Q^{(t)},Q^{(t^{\prime})}
for all
t,t^{\prime}
pairs that are equal on
(5,6)
and
(8,7)
.

2.2
Estimation Framework

Next, we describe our estimation framework. Given
\widetilde{P}
and
\widetilde{Q}[R,C]
, where
R,C
can come from either the active (Eq. (
1
)) or passive sampling (Eq. (
2
)) setting, we estimate
\hat{Q}
via the least-squares estimator.

Least Squares Estimator.

1.

Extract features via SVD from
\tilde{P}=\widehat{U}_{P}\widehat{\Sigma}_{P}\widehat{V}^{T}_{P}
.

2.

Let
\Omega
be the multiset of observed entries. Then solve

\displaystyle\hat{\Theta}_{Q}:=\arg\min_{\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}}\sum_{(i,j)\in\Omega}|\widetilde{Q}_{ij}-\hat{\bm{u}}_{i}^{\top}\Theta\hat{\bm{v}}_{j}|^{2},

(5)

where
\hat{\bm{u}}_{i}:=\hat{U}_{P}^{T}\bm{e}_{i},\hat{\bm{v}}_{j}:=\hat{V}_{P}^{T}\bm{e}_{j}
.

3.

Estimate
\hat{Q}
:

\displaystyle\hat{Q}_{ij}=\hat{\bm{u}}_{i}^{\top}\hat{\Theta}_{Q}\hat{\bm{v}}_{j}.

(6)

This fully specifies
\hat{Q}
in the passive sampling setting (Eq. (
2
)). For the active sampling setting, we must also specify how rows and columns are chosen.

Active sampling poses two main challenges. First, it is not clear how to leverage
\widetilde{P}
for sampling
\widetilde{Q}
because samples are chosen
before
observing
\widetilde{Q}
, so the distribution shift from
P
to
Q
is unknown. Second, the best design depends on the choice of estimator and vice versa.

Surprisingly, we show that for the right choice of experimental design, the optimal estimator is precisely the least-squares estimator
\hat{Q}
as in Eq. (
6
). We use the classical
G
-optimal design
[
57
]
, which has been used in reinforcement learning to achieve minimax optimal exploration
[
48
]
and optimal policies for linear Markov Decision Processes
[
67
]
.

Definition 2.3

(
\epsilon
-approximate
G
-optimal design)
.

Let
\mathcal{A}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a finite set. For a distribution
\pi:\mathcal{A}\to[0,1]
, its
G
-value is defined as

\displaystyle g(\pi):=\max_{\bm{a}\in\mathcal{A}}\bigg[\bm{a}^{T}\bigg(\sum_{\bm{a}\in\mathcal{A}}\pi(\bm{a})\bm{a}\bm{a}^{T}\bigg)^{-1}\bm{a}\bigg].

For
\epsilon>0
, we say
\hat{\pi}
is
\epsilon
-approximately
G
-optimal if

\displaystyle g(\hat{\pi})

\displaystyle\leq(1+\epsilon)\inf_{\pi}g(\pi).

If
\epsilon=0
, we say
\hat{\pi}
is simply
G
-optimal.

Notice that in Eq. (
5
), the covariates are tensor products
(\hat{\bm{v}}_{j}\otimes\hat{\bm{u}}_{i})
of column and row features. The
G
-optimal design is useful because it respects the tensor structure of the least-squares estimator. We prove this via the Kiefer-Wolfowitz Theorem
[
48
]
.

Proposition 2.4

(Tensorization of
G
-optimal design)
.

Let
U\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times d_{1}},V\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d_{2}}
.
Let
\rho
be a
G
-optimal design for
\{U^{T}\bm{e}_{i}:i\in[m]\}
and
\zeta
be a
G
-optimal design for
\{V^{T}\bm{e}_{j}:j\in[n]\}
. Let
\pi(i,j)=\rho(i)\zeta(j)
be a distribution on
[m]\times[n]
. Then
\pi
is a
G
-optimal design on
\{V^{T}\bm{e}_{j}\otimes U^{T}\bm{e}_{i}:i\in[m],j\in[n]\}
.

Consider a maximally coherent
P
that is nonzero at entry
(3,5)
and zero elsewhere. Then
Q
is also zero outside
(3,5)
. By the Kiefer-Wolfowitz Theorem, the
G
-optimal design for rows (resp. columns) samples row
3
(resp. column
5
) with probability
1
. So, if
\widetilde{P}
is not too noisy, then the
G
-optimal design samples
precisely the useful rows/columns
.

In light of Proposition
2.4
, we leverage the tensorization property to sample rows and columns as follows.

Active Sampling.

Given
\hat{U},\hat{V}
, and budget
T_{\textup{row}},T_{\textup{col}}
,

1.

Compute
\epsilon
-approximate
G
-optimal designs
\hat{\rho},\hat{\zeta}
for
\{\hat{U}_{P}^{T}\bm{e}_{i}:i\in[m]\}
and
\{\hat{V}_{P}^{T}\bm{e}_{j}:j\in[n]\}
respectively, with the Frank-Wolfe algorithm
[
48
]
.

2.

Sample
i_{1},\dots i_{T_{\textup{row}}}\mathop{\sim}\limits^{i.i.d}\hat{\rho}
and
j_{1},\dots j_{T_{\textup{col}}}\mathop{\sim}\limits^{i.i.d}\hat{\zeta}
.

Finally, we specify the assumption we need on the source data
\widetilde{P}
, called Singular Subspace Recovery (SSR).

Assumption 2.5

(
\epsilon
-SSR)
.

Given
\widetilde{P}\in(\mathbb{R}\cup\{\star\})^{m\times n}
, we have access to a method that outputs estimates
\widehat{U}_{P}\in\mathcal{O}^{m\times d}
and
\widehat{V}_{P}\in\mathcal{O}^{n\times d}
, such that:

\displaystyle\inf_{W_{U}\in\mathcal{O}^{d\times d}}\|\hat{U}-UW_{U}\|_{2\to\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon_{\textup{SSR}},

(7)

\displaystyle\text{and }\qquad\inf_{W_{V}\in\mathcal{O}^{d\times d}}\|\hat{V}-VW_{V}\|_{2\to\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon_{\textup{SSR}}

for some
\epsilon_{\textup{SSR}}>0
.

This assumption holds for a number of models. For instance, recent works in both MCAR
[
10
]
and MNAR
[
3
,
40
]
settings give estimation methods for
\hat{P}
with entry-wise error bounds. In Appendix
A.2
, we prove that these entry-wise guarantees, combined with standard theoretical assumptions such as incoherence, imply Assumption
2.5
.

We now give our main upper bound.

Theorem 2.6

(Generic error bound for active sampling)
.

Let
\hat{Q}
be the active sampling estimator with
T_{\textup{row}},T_{\textup{col}}\geq 20d\log(m+n)
. Then, for absolute constants
C,C^{\prime}>0
, and all
\epsilon<\frac{1}{10}
,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\limits_{\widetilde{P},\widetilde{Q}}\bigg[\|\hat{Q}-Q\|_{\textup{max}}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C(1+\epsilon)\bigg(\frac{d^{2}\sigma_{Q}^{2}\log(m+n)}{\left|T_{\textup{col}}\right|\left|T_{\textup{row}}\right|}

\displaystyle d^{2}\epsilon_{\textup{SSR}}^{2}\|Q\|_{2}^{2}\bigg)\bigg]

\displaystyle\geq 1-C^{\prime}(m+n)^{-2}.

We will discuss implications of Theorem
2.6
in Remark
2.7
. First, we give some intuition. Notice that Theorem
2.6
(and Theorem
2.9
) gives an error bound as a sum of two terms, which depend on the sample size and
\epsilon_{\textup{SSR}}
respectively. To see why, let
\Omega
be the set of observed entries, either in a passive or active sampling setting. Let
\hat{\bm{u}}_{i},\hat{\bm{v}}_{j}
be the covariates as in Eq. (
5
). The observation
\widetilde{Q}_{ij}
can be decomposed:

\displaystyle\widetilde{Q}_{ij}

\displaystyle=Q_{ij}+(\widetilde{Q}_{ij}-Q_{ij})

\displaystyle=\widehat{\bm{u}}_{i}^{\top}\Theta_{Q}\widehat{\bm{v}}_{j}+\underbrace{\epsilon_{ij}}_{\text{misspecification }\widetilde{P}}+\underbrace{(\widetilde{Q}_{ij}-Q_{ij})}_{\text{noise}}

(8)

The population estimand
\Theta_{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, which is estimated in Eq. (
5
), is:

\Theta_{Q}:=W_{U}^{T}T_{1}RT_{2}^{T}W_{V},

where
T_{1},T_{2}
are the distribution shift matrices as in Definition
1.2
, and
W_{U},W_{V}\in\mathcal{O}^{d\times d}
are some rotations.
The misspecification error is due to the estimation error of the singular subspaces of
P
and
depends on
\epsilon_{\textup{SSR}}
as follows:

\displaystyle\epsilon_{ij}

\displaystyle:=\bm{e}_{i}^{T}(\hat{U}-UW_{U})\Theta_{Q}\hat{V}\bm{e}_{j}

\displaystyle+\bm{e}_{i}^{T}\hat{U}\Theta_{Q}(\hat{V}-VW_{V})\bm{e}_{j}

\displaystyle+\bm{e}_{i}^{T}(\hat{U}-UW_{U})\Theta_{Q}(\hat{V}-VW_{V})\bm{e}_{j}

Therefore
\epsilon_{ij}^{2}=O(\epsilon_{\textup{SSR}}^{2}\|Q\|_{2}^{2})
for all
i,j
.
1
1

1

In fact
\epsilon_{ij}^{2}=O(\epsilon_{\textup{SSR}}^{2}\|R\|_{2}^{2})
, but we report bounds with the weaker
O(\epsilon_{\textup{SSR}}^{2}\|Q\|_{2}^{2})
for ease of reading.

Notice the misspecification error is independent of the estimator
\widehat{\Theta}_{Q}
, so it will not depend on sample size. This explains the appearance of the two summands in our upper bounds. The first term depends on estimation error
\Theta_{Q}-\hat{\Theta}_{Q}
, which is unique to the sampling method. The second depends on misspecification, which is common to both.

Remark 2.7

(Minimax Optimality for MNAR and MCAR Source Data)
.

The rate of Theorem
2.6
is minimax-optimal in the usual transfer learning regime when target data is noisy (
\sigma_{Q}
large) and
limited (
\left|\Omega\right|:=\left|T_{\textup{row}}\right|\left|T_{\textup{col}}\right|
small).

Suppose
P
is rank
d
,
\mu
-incoherent, with singular values
\sigma_{1}\geq\dots\geq\sigma_{d}
, condition number
\kappa
and
m=n
. For the MNAR
\widetilde{P}
setting, suppose each
\widetilde{P}_{ij}
has i.i.d. additive noise
\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{P}^{2})
with sampling sparsity factor
n^{-\beta}
for
\beta\in[0,1]
and
\sigma_{P}=O(1)
. By
[
40
]
,
\hat{Q}
is minimax-optimal if

\displaystyle\frac{4\mu^{3}d^{3}\kappa^{2}\|Q\|_{2}^{2}}{n^{1+\frac{2-\beta}{d}}}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\sigma_{Q}^{2}}{\left|\Omega\right|},

where
\lesssim

2.3
Passive Sampling

We next give the estimation error for the passive sampling setting. The rate almost exactly matches Theorem

Theorem 2.9

Let

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\bigg[\|\hat{Q}-Q\|_{\textup{max}}^{2}

2.4

We give a lower bound for the passive sampling setting in terms of a fixed, arbitrary mask. To exclude degenerate cases such as all entries being observed, we require the following definition.

Related Work

We review the most relevant literature here. For additional discussion, we refer to the surveys
[
24
,
38
]
for matrix completion and
[
76
,
42
]
for transfer learning.

Matrix Completion.
Most matrix completion algorithms require a Missing Completely at Random (MCAR) assumption
[
17
,
12
,
26
,
77
]
, where each
Q_{ij}
is observed with probability
p
independently of all others. The Missing Not-at-Random setting allows the masking probability of
Q_{ij}
to depend on the value of
Q_{ij}
itself
[
51
,
6
,
40
]
, but still assumes that entries are masked independently of one another. If masking variables are dependent, then authors assume identifiability of the matrix conditioned on the masking
[
3
]
, or that entries in every row and column are observed
[
63
]
. By contrast, we study one of the simplest possible MNAR models in which entries of
\widetilde{Q}
are
not
independent and entire rows and columns can be missing. This MNAR model is motivated by biological problems
[
15
,
33
,
27
]
.

Transfer learning.
Transfer learning has been well-studied in learning theory
[
7
,
14
,
13
]
. Recent works address various supervised learning
[
59
,
19
,
52
,
16
]
and unsupervised learning settings
[
29
,
25
]
. Statistical works consider minimax rates of estimation, and computationally efficient estimators to achieve such rates
[
66
,
4
,
20
,
52
,
9
,
16
]
. In applications, transfer learning from data-rich to data-poor domains has applications in biostatistics
[
44
,
23
]
, epidemiology
[
1
]
, computer vision
[
65
,
54
]
, language models
[
37
]
, and other areas.

Transfer learning for matrix completion typically assumes the source
P
and target
Q
are observed in an MCAR fashion, and are related through a rotation in latent space
[
71
,
53
,
36
]
. Rotational shift is a special case of our distribution shift model (Definition
1.2
), which allows for any linear shift in latent space. On the other hand, works that study transfer learning for specific classes of matrices typically assume distributional shifts that are unique to those structures, such as in latent variable networks
[
39
]
or the log-linear word production model
[
75
]
.

Optimal experimental design.
Choosing a set of maximally informative experiments is a classical problem in statistics
[
64
,
57
]
with connections to active learning
[
22
]
, bandits
[
5
]
, and reinforcement learning
[
50
]
. Optimal designs have been studied for domain adaptation
[
61
,
72
]
, misspecified regression
[
50
]
, and linear Markov Decision Processes
[
40
]
. In our active sampling setting, we
jointly
query rows and columns to observe the corresponding submatrix of
\widetilde{Q}
, rather than one entry at a time
[
21
,
60
,
8
]
. But, the optimal row queries depend on column queries (and vice versa) – so we use the tensorization property of
G
-optimal designs (Proposition
2.4
) to prove global optimality with respect to joint row/column samplers.

5
Conclusion and Future Work

We study transfer learning for a challenging MNAR model of matrix completion. We obtain minimax lower bounds for entrywise estimation of
Q
in both the active (Theorem
2.2
) and passive sampling settings (Theorem
2.12
). We give a computationally efficient minimax-optimal estimator that uses tensorization of
G
-optimal designs in the active setting (Theorem
2.6
). Further, in the passive setting, we give a rate-optimal estimator under incoherence assumptions (Theorem
2.9
). Finally, we experimentally validate our findings on data from gene expression micoarrays and metabolic modeling.

Future work could consider even more difficult missingness structures, such as when the masks
(\eta_{i})_{i=1}^{m},(\nu_{j})_{j=1}^{n}
are dependent. If the mask can be partitioned into subsets whose mutual dependencies are small, an Efron-Stein argument
[
55
]
may work. Is bounded dependence necessary?
Moreover, one can consider other kinds of side information, such as gene-level features in Genome-Wide Association Studies
[
53
]
.
Finally, there can be other interesting nonlinear models for transfer between source and target matrices.

\displaystyle\|U(U^{\top}\widehat{U})-\widehat{U}\|_{2\to\infty}\leq\frac{\|M-\widehat{M}\|_{2\to\infty}+\|UU^{\top}\|_{2\to\infty}\|M-\widehat{M}\|_{\textup{op}}+\|U(U^{\top}\widehat{U})-\widehat{U}\|_{2\to\infty}\|\widehat{M}-M\|_{\textup{op}}}{\sigma_{r}(M)}

\displaystyle\|U(U^{\top}\widehat{U})-\widehat{U}\|_{2\to\infty}\leq\frac{\|M-\widehat{M}\|_{2\to\infty}+\|UU^{\top}\|_{2\to\infty}\|M-\widehat{M}\|_{\textup{op}}}{\sigma_{r}(M)}+\frac{1}{2}\|U(U^{\top}\widehat{U})-\widehat{U}\|_{2\to\infty}

G_{ij}\mathop{\sim}\limits^{i.i.d}N(0,\sigma_{Q}^{2})
be the additive noise for
\widetilde{Q}_{ij}
. Then,
\widetilde{Q}_{ij}=\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{ij}^{T}\textrm{vec}(M)+\epsilon_{ij}+G_{ij}
. Hence we can write
E_{1}
as:

\displaystyle E_{1;k\ell}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{k\ell}^{T}(\sum_{ij\in\Omega}\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{ij}\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{ij}^{T})^{-1}\sum_{ij\in\Omega}\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{ij}\widetilde{Q}_{ij}\bigg)-\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{k\ell}^{T}\textrm{vec}(M)

\displaystyle=\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{k\ell}^{T}\bigg((\sum_{ij\in\Omega}\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{ij}\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{ij}^{T})^{-1}\sum_{ij\in\Omega}\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{ij}\big(\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{ij}^{T}\textrm{vec}(M)+\epsilon_{ij}+G_{ij}\big)\bigg)-\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{k\ell}^{T}\textrm{vec}(M)

\displaystyle=\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{k\ell}^{T}\bigg((\sum_{ij\in\Omega}\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{ij}\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{ij}^{T})^{-1}\sum_{ij\in\Omega}\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{ij}\big(\epsilon_{ij}+G_{ij}\big)\bigg)

\displaystyle=\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{k\ell}^{T}\bigg((\sum_{ij\in\Omega}\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{ij}\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{ij}^{T})^{-1}\sum_{ij\in\Omega}\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{ij}\epsilon_{ij}\bigg)+\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{k\ell}^{T}\bigg((\sum_{ij\in\Omega}\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{ij}\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{ij}^{T})^{-1}\sum_{ij\in\Omega}\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{ij}G_{ij}\bigg)

\displaystyle=:E_{3;k\ell}+E_{4;k\ell}

We analyze
E_{4}
first. Let
\bm{x}=W^{-1}\sum_{ij\in\Omega}\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{ij}G_{ij}
. For any
k,\ell
, we wish to bound
\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{k\ell}^{T}\bm{x}
. Notice that
\bm{x}
is a multivariate Gaussian with mean
\bm{0}
. Its covariance is therefore:

\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\bm{x}\bm{x}^{T}]=\sum_{ij\in\Omega}\sum_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}\in\Omega}W^{-1}\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{ij}\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}^{T}W^{-1}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[G_{ij}G_{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}}]=\sigma_{Q}^{2}W^{-1}\big(\sum_{ij\in\Omega}\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{ij}\phi_{ij}^{T}\big)W^{-1}=\sigma_{Q}^{2}W^{-1}

Hence
\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{k\ell}^{T}\bm{x}
is a scalar Gaussian with mean zero and variance
\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{k\ell}^{T}\sigma_{Q}^{2}W^{-1}\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{k\ell}
. We next bound this quadratic form. Notice that we can tensorize the quadratic form as:

\displaystyle\phi_{k\ell}^{T}W^{-1}\phi_{k\ell}

\displaystyle=(\bm{\psi}_{\ell}\otimes\bm{\varphi}_{k})^{T}(W_{1}\otimes W_{2})^{-1}(\bm{\psi}_{\ell}\otimes\bm{\varphi}_{k})

\displaystyle=(\bm{\psi}_{\ell}W_{1}^{-1}\bm{\psi}_{\ell})(\bm{\varphi}_{k}W_{2}^{-1}\bm{\varphi}_{k})

We apply Lemma
A.13
to each term in the product. With probability
1-2(m+n)^{-2}
, for
S_{r},S_{c}
both of size at least
20d\log(\frac{2d}{m+n})
,

\|\psi_{\ell}\|_{W_{1}^{-1}}^{2}\|\varphi_{k}\|_{W_{2}^{-1}}^{2}\leq\frac{(2+2\epsilon)d^{2}}{\left|S_{r}\right|\left|S_{c}\right|}

Conditioning on this event, the variance of
\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{k\ell}^{T}\bm{x}
is at most
\frac{(1+\epsilon)d^{2}\sigma_{Q}^{2}}{\left|\Omega\right|(1-\rho)}
, for
\left|\Omega\right|=\left|S_{r}\right|\left|S_{c}\right|
.
Therefore, by Lemma
A.14
,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\bigg[\max_{k\in[m],\ell\in[n]}\left|\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{k\ell}^{T}\bm{x}\right|^{2}\leq 20\log(mn)\frac{(2+2\epsilon)\sigma_{Q}^{2}d^{2}}{\left|\Omega\right|}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq\delta+(mn)^{-2}

Finally, we analyze the error term
E_{3;k\ell}
. By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\left|E_{3;k\ell}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\big(\sum_{ij\in\Omega}a_{ij}^{2})^{1/2}\big(\sum_{ij\in\Omega}\epsilon_{ij}^{2})^{1/2}

First,

\displaystyle\sum_{ij\in\Omega}a_{ij}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{ij\in\Omega}\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{ij}^{T}W^{-1}\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{k\ell}\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{k\ell}^{T}W^{-1}\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\sum_{ij\in\Omega}\textrm{tr}\bigg(\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{ij}\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{ij}^{T}W^{-1}\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{k\ell}\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{k\ell}^{T}W^{-1}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\textrm{tr}\bigg(\sum_{ij\in\Omega}\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{ij}\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{ij}^{T}W^{-1}\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{k\ell}\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{k\ell}^{T}W^{-1}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\textrm{tr}\bigg(\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{k\ell}\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{k\ell}^{T}W^{-1}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\left|\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{k\ell}^{T}W^{-1}\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{k\ell}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(2+2\epsilon)d^{2}}{\left|\Omega\right|}

For the other term,

\displaystyle\big(\sum_{ij\in\Omega}\epsilon_{ij}^{2}\big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\left|\Omega\right|^{1/2}\max_{ij\in\Omega}\left|\epsilon_{ij}\right|

It follows that
\max_{k,\ell}\left|E_{3;k\ell}\right|\leq\sqrt{2+2\epsilon}\cdot d\max_{i,j\in\Omega}\left|\epsilon_{ij}\right|
. The conclusion follows.
∎

A.7
Proof of Theorem
2.9

We require the following concentration result to control the sizes of masks.

Lemma A.15

(Bernoulli Concentration)
.

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\mathop{\sim}\limits^{i.i.d}\textrm{Bernoulli}(p)
for
p\in(0,1)
. Then if
p\geq 10\log n
,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{P}}[\left|\sum_{i}(X_{i}-p)\right|\geq\frac{np}{2}]

\displaystyle\leq n^{-\omega(1)}

Proof.

By the scalar Bernstein inequality (Lemma
A.16
), we have for
B=1
and
\zeta=np
that:

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{P}}[\left|\sum_{i}(X_{i}-p)\right|\geq\tau]

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp(-\frac{\tau^{2}/2}{\zeta+(B\tau/3)})

Let
\tau=np/2
. Then

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{P}}[\left|\sum_{i}(X_{i}-p)\right|\geq\tau]

\displaystyle\leq 2\exp(\frac{-10}{8}\log n)

\displaystyle\leq 2n^{-(\log n)^{1/4}}

∎

We are ready to prove the estimation error for passive sampling.

Proof of Theorem
2.9
.

Following the notation of the proof of Theorem
2.6
, we want to bound
E_{3;k\ell}
and
E_{4;k\ell}
. However, rather than using
G
-optimality to bound quadratic forms of the type
\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{k\ell}W^{-1}\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{ij}
, we will apply spectral concentration via Proposition
A.17
.

To this end, we condition on the events that
\hat{V}_{P}^{T}\Pi_{R}\hat{V}_{P}\succeq\frac{p_{\textup{Row}}}{2}
and
\hat{U}_{P}^{T}\Pi_{C}\hat{U}_{P}\succeq\frac{p_{\textup{Col}}}{2}
. By Proposition
A.4
and Proposition
A.17
, the two events occur simultaneously with probability
\geq 1-2(m\land n)^{-10}
. Then
W^{-1}
exists and
W^{-1}\preceq\frac{4}{p_{\textup{Row}}p_{\textup{Col}}}I
. Therefore, for all
i,j,k,\ell
, by incoherence,

\displaystyle\left|\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{k\ell}^{T}W^{-1}\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{ij}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{p_{\textup{Row}}p_{\textup{Col}}}\|\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{k\ell}\|\|\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{ij}\|

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{p_{\textup{Row}}p_{\textup{Col}}}\|\bm{\varphi}_{k}\|\|\bm{\varphi}_{i}\|\|\bm{\psi}_{\ell}\|\|\bm{\psi}_{j}\|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4}{p_{\textup{Row}}p_{\textup{Col}}}\big(\sqrt{\frac{\mu_{U}^{2}\mu_{V}^{2}d^{4}}{m^{2}n^{2}}}\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{p_{\textup{Row}}p_{\textup{Col}}}\frac{\mu d^{2}}{mn}

Hence, by Lemma
A.14
,

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\bigg[\max_{k\in[m],\ell\in[n]}\left|E_{4;k\ell}\right|^{2}\leq 20\log(mn)\sigma_{Q}^{2}\frac{4}{p_{\textup{Row}}p_{\textup{Col}}}\frac{\mu d^{2}}{mn}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq 2(m\land n)^{-10}+(mn)^{-2}.

Next, we analyze
E_{3}
. Let
a_{ij}=\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{k\ell}^{T}W^{-1}\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{ij}
. Let
p=q=2
. By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\left|E_{3;k\ell}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\big(\sum_{ij\in\Omega}a_{ij}^{p})^{1/p}\big(\sum_{ij\in\Omega}\epsilon_{ij}^{q})^{1/q}

First, we have:

\displaystyle\sum_{ij\in\Omega}a_{ij}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{ij\in\Omega}\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{ij}^{T}W^{-1}\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{k\ell}\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{k\ell}^{T}W^{-1}\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{ij}

\displaystyle=\sum_{ij\in\Omega}\textrm{tr}\bigg(\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{ij}\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{ij}^{T}W^{-1}\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{k\ell}\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{k\ell}^{T}W^{-1}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\textrm{tr}\bigg(\sum_{ij\in\Omega}\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{ij}\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{ij}^{T}W^{-1}\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{k\ell}\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{k\ell}^{T}W^{-1}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\textrm{tr}\bigg(\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{k\ell}\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{k\ell}^{T}W^{-1}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\left|\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{k\ell}^{T}W^{-1}\hat{\bm{\phi}}_{k\ell}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4\mu d^{2}}{p_{\textup{Row}}p_{\textup{Col}}mn}

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\big(\sum_{ij\in\Omega}\epsilon_{ij}^{q}\big)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq\left|\Omega\right|^{1/2}\max_{ij\in\Omega}\left|\epsilon_{ij}\right|

Notice
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\left|\Omega\right|]=mnp_{\textup{Row}}p_{\textup{Col}}
. By Lemma
A.15
, with probability
\geq 1-4(m\land n)^{-\omega(1)}
,

\displaystyle\left|\Omega\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{9}{4}p_{\textup{Row}}p_{\textup{Col}}mn

Therefore, with probability
\geq 1-4(m\land n)^{-2}
,

\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{\left|\Omega\right|}}{p_{\textup{Row}}p_{\textup{Col}}mn}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{3}{2}\frac{1}{\sqrt{p_{\textup{Row}}p_{\textup{Col}}mn}}

The conclusion follows.
∎

A.8
Proof of Proposition
2.11

We require the following version of the Matrix Bernstein Inequality
[
11
]
.

Lemma A.16

(Matrix Bernstein Inequality)
.

Suppose that
\{Y_{i}:i=1,\dots,n\}
are independent mean-zero random matrices of size
d_{1}\times d_{2}
, such that
\|Y_{i}\|_{2}\leq B
almost surely for all
i
, and
\zeta\geq\max\{\|\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\sum_{i}Y_{i}Y_{i}^{T}]\|_{2},\|\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\sum_{i}Y_{i}^{T}Y_{i}]\|_{2}\}
. Then,

\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\bigg[\bigg\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}\bigg\|_{2}\geq\tau\bigg]\leq(d_{1}+d_{2})\exp\bigg(-\frac{\tau^{2}/2}{\zeta+B\tau/3}\bigg)

We now prove nondegeneracy of masks with high probability.

Proposition A.17

(Spectral Concentration)
.

Suppose that
\hat{V}_{P}
and
\hat{U}_{P}
are
\mu_{V},\mu_{U}
-incoherent respectively. Let
\Pi_{C}\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}
be the random matrix with diagonal entries
\nu_{1},\dots,\nu_{n}
and similarly let
\Pi_{R}\in\{0,1\}^{m\times m}
have diagonal entries
\eta_{1},\dots,\eta_{m}
.
Then, assuming that
\mu_{V}\leq\frac{p_{\textup{Col}}n}{400d\log n}
and
\mu_{U}\leq\frac{p_{\textup{Row}}n}{400d\log n}
, we have:

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{P}}[\hat{U}_{P}^{T}\Pi_{R}\hat{U}_{P}\succeq p_{\textup{Row}}/2]

\displaystyle\geq 1-m^{-10}

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathbb{P}}[\hat{V}_{P}^{T}\Pi_{C}\hat{V}_{P}\succeq p_{\textup{Col}}/2]

\displaystyle\geq 1-n^{-10}

Proof.

Suppose that
\hat{V}_{P}
has rows
\bm{y}_{1},\dots,\bm{y}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then,

\hat{V}_{P}^{T}\Pi_{C}\hat{V}_{P}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\nu_{i}\bm{y}_{i}\bm{y}_{i}^{T}.

Let
\bm{v}_{i}=\sqrt{n}\bm{y}_{i}
. Let
p_{\textup{Col}}=\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\nu_{i}]
. We use
p=p_{\textup{Col}}
for shorthand. Notice
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\hat{V}_{P}^{T}\Pi_{C}\hat{V}_{P}]=\sum_{i}p\bm{y}_{i}\bm{y}_{i}^{T}=pI_{d}
, since
\hat{V}_{P}^{T}\hat{V}_{P}=I_{d}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\bigg\|\sum_{i}\nu_{i}\bm{v}_{i}\bm{v}_{i}^{T}-pnI_{d}\bigg\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\bigg\|\sum_{i}(\nu_{i}-p)\bm{v}_{i}\bm{v}_{i}^{T}\bigg\|_{2}

Let
Y_{i}=(\nu_{i}-p)\bm{v}_{i}\bm{v}_{i}^{T}
. Note that
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[Y_{i}]=0
. Next, let
\mu:=\mu_{V}
. By incoherence,
\|Y_{i}\|_{2}\leq\|\bm{v}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\mu d
for all
i
. Further,

\displaystyle\max\{\|\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\sum_{i}Y_{i}Y_{i}^{T}]\|_{2},\|\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\sum_{i}Y_{i}^{T}Y_{i}]\|_{2}\}

\displaystyle=\|\mathop{\mathbb{E}}[\sum_{i}Y_{i}^{2}]\|_{2}

\displaystyle=p(1-p)\|\sum_{i}\|\bm{v}_{i}\|_{2}^{2}\bm{v}_{i}\bm{v}_{i}^{T}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq p(1-p)n\mu d\|\sum_{i}\bm{y}_{i}\bm{y}_{i}^{T}\|_{2}

\displaystyle=p(1-p)n\mu d

Thus, by Lemma
A.16
, for
B=\mu d
and
\zeta=p(1-p)n\mu d
, we have:

\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\bigg[\bigg\|\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}\bigg\|_{2}\geq\tau\bigg]\leq 2n\exp\bigg(-\frac{\tau^{2}/2}{\zeta+B\tau/3}\bigg)

Setting
\tau=10\sqrt{p(1-p)n\mu d\log n}\lor 10\mu d\sqrt{\log n}
implies that:

\mathop{\mathbb{P}}\bigg[\sum_{i}\nu_{i}\bm{v}_{i}\bm{v}_{i}^{T}\succeq pn-\tau\bigg]\geq 1-n^{-10}

If
\mu\leq\frac{pn}{400d\log n}
, then
\tau\leq pn/2=p_{\textup{Col}}\cdot n/2
. We conclude that
\mathop{\mathbb{P}}[\hat{V}_{P}^{T}\Pi_{C}\hat{V}_{P}\succeq p_{\textup{Col}}/2]\geq 1-n^{-10}
. An identical argument gives
\mathop{\mathbb{P}}[\hat{U}_{P}^{T}\Pi_{R}\hat{U}_{P}\succeq p_{\textup{Row}}/2]\geq 1-m^{-10}
.
∎

Corollary A.18
.

Under the assumptions of Proposition
A.17
, the design matrix for passive sampling has rank
d^{2}
with probability at least
1-2(m\land n)^{-10}
.

Proof.

Let
\Omega\subset[m]\times[n]
be the set of indices corresponding to the observed entries of
\widetilde{Q}
. Let
P_{\Omega}\in\{0,1\}^{\left|\Omega\right|\times mn}
be the coordinate projection. The design matrix is precisely
P_{\Omega}(\hat{V}_{P}\otimes\hat{U}_{P})
. Then, notice that:

\displaystyle\big(P_{\Omega}(\hat{V}_{P}\otimes\hat{U}_{P}))^{T}\big(P_{\Omega}(\hat{V}_{P}\otimes\hat{U}_{P}))

\displaystyle=(\hat{V}_{P}\otimes\hat{U}_{P})^{T}P_{\Omega}^{T}P_{\Omega}(\hat{V}_{P}\otimes\hat{U}_{P})

\displaystyle=(\hat{V}_{P}\otimes\hat{U}_{P})^{T}(\Pi_{C}\otimes\Pi_{R})(\hat{V}_{P}\otimes\hat{U}_{P})

\displaystyle=\hat{V}_{P}^{T}\Pi_{C}\hat{V}_{P}\otimes\hat{U}_{P}^{T}\Pi_{R}\hat{U}_{P}

By Proposition
A.17
, this matrix has rank at least
d^{2}
with probability
\geq 1-2(m\land n)^{-10}
.
∎

A.9
Proof of Theorem
2.12

We require the the Gilbert-Varshamov code
[
30
]
.

Theorem A.19

(Gilbert-Varshamov)
.

Let
q\geq 2
be a prime power. For
0<\epsilon<\frac{q-1}{q}
there exists an
\epsilon
-balanced code
C\subset\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
with rate
\Omega(\epsilon^{2}n)
.

We will use the following version of Fano’s inequality.

Theorem A.20

(Generalized Fano Method,
[
74
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a family of probability measures,
(\mathcal{D},d)
a pseudo-metric space, and
\theta:\mathcal{P}\to\mathcal{D}
a map that extracts the parameters of interest. For a distinguished
P\in\mathcal{P}
, let
X\sim P
be the data and
\hat{\theta}:=\hat{\theta}(X)
be an estimator for
\theta(P)
.

Let
r\geq 2
and
\mathcal{P}_{r}\subset\mathcal{P}
be a finite hypothesis class of size
r
. Let
\alpha_{r},\beta_{r}>0
be such that for all
i\neq j
, and all
P_{i},P_{j}\in\mathcal{P}_{r}
,

\displaystyle d(\theta(P_{i}),\theta(P_{j}))

\displaystyle\geq\alpha_{r};

\displaystyle KL(P_{i},P_{j})

\displaystyle\leq\beta_{r}.

Then

\displaystyle\max\limits_{j\in[r]}\mathbb{E}_{P_{j}}[d(\hat{\theta}(X),\theta(P_{j}))]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\alpha_{r}}{2}\bigg(1-\frac{\beta_{r}+\log 2}{\log r}\bigg).

We can now prove Theorem
2.12
.

Proof of Theorem
2.12
.

Let
C\subset\{0,1\}^{d^{2}}
be the
0.1
-balanced Gilbert-Varshmaov code as in Theorem
A.19
. Let
U,V\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
be Stiefel matrices with incoherence parameter
\mu=O(1)
. Let
P=U\Sigma_{P}V^{T}
for a diagonal
\Sigma_{P}\succ 0
to be specified later. Let
\delta_{Q}>0
be a positive real to be specified later.

We will construct a family of source/target pairs indexed by
C
similar to
[
39
]
. For
w\in C
, let
B_{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be defined as:

\displaystyle B_{w;ij}:=\begin{cases}\frac{\sqrt{mn}}{2d}&w_{ij}=0\\
\frac{\sqrt{mn}}{d}(\frac{1}{2}+\delta_{Q})&w_{ij}=1\end{cases}

Then define
(P_{w},Q_{w})=(P,UB_{w}V^{T})
.

For a fixed
w\in C
, the distribution of the data
(A_{P},\widetilde{Q})
depends on the random noise and masking of both
A_{P},\widetilde{Q}
. Let
D_{R}\in\{0,1\}^{m\times m}
and
D_{C}\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}
be the diagonal matrices corresponding to the row/column masks for
Q
, and let
G\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
have iid
N(0,\sigma_{Q}^{2})
entries. Then
\widetilde{Q}=D_{R}(Q+G)D_{C}
.

Now, we will apply Theorem
A.20
to lower bound
\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\bigg[\frac{1}{mn}\|\hat{Q}-Q_{w}\|_{F}^{2}\bigg|D_{R},D_{C}\bigg]
. Fix any
D_{R}\in\text{supp}(\mathcal{E}_{1}),D_{C}\in\text{supp}(\mathcal{E}_{2}
. Let
\widetilde{P}_{w},\widetilde{Q}_{w}
denote the distribution of the data when the population matrices are
P_{w},Q_{w}
and we condition on the
Q
-mask matrices
D_{R},D_{C}
.

By Theorem
A.19
, the hypothesis space indexed by
C
is such that
\log(\left|C\right|)\geq C_{1}d^{2}
for absolute constant
C_{1}>0
. Next, for distinct
w,w^{\prime}\in C
,

\displaystyle KL((\widetilde{P}_{w},\widetilde{Q}_{w}),(\widetilde{P}_{w^{\prime}},\widetilde{Q}_{w^{\prime}}))

\displaystyle=KL(\widetilde{P}_{w^{\prime}},\widetilde{P}_{w})+KL(\widetilde{Q}_{w},\widetilde{Q}_{w^{\prime}})

\displaystyle\leq KL(\widetilde{Q}_{w},\widetilde{Q}_{w^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=KL((D_{C}\otimes D_{R})\textrm{vec}(Q_{w}+G),(D_{C}\otimes D_{R})\textrm{vec}(Q_{w^{\prime}}+G))

Notice that we do not use any properties of
\widetilde{P}_{w},\widetilde{P}_{w^{\prime}}
, and in particular allow for deterministic
\widetilde{P}_{w}=P_{w}=P
.

Since
D_{C},D_{R}
are fixed, this is simply the KL divergence of two multiariate Gaussians with the same covariance but different means. Therefore, by Lemma
A.9
, we have that:

\displaystyle KL((\widetilde{P}_{w},\widetilde{Q}_{w}),(\widetilde{P}_{w^{\prime}},\widetilde{Q}_{w^{\prime}}))

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sigma_{Q}^{2}}\textrm{vec}(Q_{w}-Q_{w^{\prime}})^{T}(D_{C}\otimes D_{R})^{T}(D_{C}\otimes D_{R})^{-1}(D_{C}\otimes D_{R})\textrm{vec}(Q_{w}-Q_{w^{\prime}})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma_{Q}^{2}}\|D_{R}(Q_{w}-Q_{w^{\prime}})D_{C}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma_{Q}^{2}}\|D_{R}U(B_{w}-B_{w^{\prime}})V^{T}D_{C}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sigma_{Q}^{2}}\|D_{R}U\|_{2}^{2}\|D_{C}V\|_{2}^{2}\|B_{w}-B_{w^{\prime}}\|_{F}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{5p_{\textup{Row}}p_{\textup{Col}}}{\sigma_{Q}^{2}}\big(\delta_{Q}^{2}\frac{mn}{d^{2}})d^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{5p_{\textup{Row}}p_{\textup{Col}}mn\delta_{Q}^{2}}{\sigma_{Q}^{2}}.

In the penultimate step, we used the fact that
D_{R}\in\text{supp}(\mathcal{E}_{1}),D_{C}\in\text{supp}(\mathcal{E}_{2})
.

Next, for any distinct
w,w^{\prime}\in C
, by Theorem
A.19
we have that
\mathop{\mathbb{P}}_{i,j\in[d]}[w_{ij}\neq w_{ij}^{\prime}]\geq 0.1
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\|Q_{w}-Q_{w^{\prime}}\|_{F}

\displaystyle=\|U(B_{w}-B_{w^{\prime}})V^{T}\|_{F}

\displaystyle=\|(B_{w}-B_{w^{\prime}})\|_{F}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\sum\limits_{i,j\in[d]:w_{ij}\neq w_{ij}^{\prime}}\delta_{Q}^{2}\frac{mn}{d^{2}}\bigg)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{10}\delta_{Q}\sqrt{mn}

In the notation of Theorem
A.20
, we have:

\displaystyle\alpha_{r}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{10}\delta_{Q}\sqrt{mn}

\displaystyle\beta_{r}

\displaystyle=\frac{5p_{\textup{Row}}p_{\textup{Col}}mn\delta_{Q}^{2}}{\sigma_{Q}^{2}}

Since
\log(\left|C\right|)\geq C_{1}d^{2}
, we set
\delta_{Q}=\sqrt{\frac{C_{1}d^{2}\sigma_{Q}^{2}}{10p_{\textup{Row}}p_{\textup{Col}}mn}}
so that that
\beta_{r}=\frac{C_{1}d^{2}}{2}
. Therefore, by Theorem
A.20
, for absolute constants
C_{2},C_{3},C_{4}>0
,

\displaystyle\min\limits_{D_{R}\in\text{supp}(\mathcal{E}_{1}),D_{C}\in\text{supp}(\mathcal{E}_{2})}\mathop{\mathbb{E}}\bigg[\frac{1}{mn}\|\hat{Q}-Q_{w}\|_{F}^{2}\bigg|D_{R},D_{C}\bigg]

\displaystyle\geq\frac{C_{2}\alpha_{r}^{2}}{mn}

\displaystyle\geq C_{3}\delta_{Q}^{2}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{C_{4}d^{2}\sigma_{Q}^{2}}{p_{\textup{Row}}p_{\textup{Col}}mn}

The conclusion follows.
∎

Details of the baselines.

For the method of
[
6
]
, we use the estimator from their Section 2.2, but modify step (3) to truncate to the true rank
d
, and in step (6) truncate to the true rank of the propensity matrix whose
(i,j)
entry is
\eta_{i}\nu_{j}
. The propensity rank is always
1
in our case. This is the estimator
\hat{Q}_{\textup{BC22}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
.

For the method of
[
47
]
, we use the estimator from their Section 3.3, with weights
w_{P},w_{Q}
based on estimated sub-gamma parameters of the noise for
\widetilde{P},\widetilde{Q}
. Then, let

be:

\displaystyle Q_{ij}^{\prime}:=\begin{cases}\frac{w_{P}}{w_{P}+w_{Q}}\widetilde{P}_{ij}+\frac{w_{Q}}{w_{P}+w_{Q}}\widetilde{Q}_{ij}&\widetilde{Q}_{ij}\neq\star\\
\widetilde{P}_{ij}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

We return the rank-
d
SVD truncation of
Q^{\prime}
as
\hat{Q}_{\textup{LLL22}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
.
