Hierarchical reasoning for lung cancer detection: from multi-scale perception to hypergraph inference with CR-YOLO
Authors: Zhengshui Xu, Tianle Shen, Changchun Ye, Yu Li, Danwen Zhao, Ming Zhang, Yao Cheng, Jintao Chai, Jiantao Jiang, Junfeng Xi, Chao Xu, Wei Chen, Shiyuan Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Accurate detection of lung cancer from Computed Tomography (CT) scans is vital for improving patient survival but remains challenging for deep learning models, which struggle with scale variations of pulmonary nodules and the complex reasoning required for diagnosis. We propose CR-YOLO, a novel framework incorporating a Cognitive Reasoning C2f (CR-C2f) module that emulates a radiologist’s hierarchical workflow. CR-YOLO employs a Multi-scale Convolution (MSC) module for robust feature perception, Global-Local Attention (GLA) Bottlenecks to integrate local morphology with contextual dependencies, and a Hypergraph Convolution (HGC) Refiner for high-order relational inference. Experiments demonstrate that CR-YOLO achieves a mean Average Precision (mAP) of 92.5%, a 4.1% absolute improvement over the YOLOv8n baseline. In addition to improved accuracy, CR-YOLO enhances interpretability through Grad-CAM analysis, highlighting its potential as a reliable and transparent tool for early lung cancer diagnosis.

Introduction

Early diagnosis of lung cancer is essential for reducing mortality rates1. World Health Organization data shows that cancer claims approximately 6.7 million lives globally each year, representing 11% of all deaths. Lung cancer, notably prevalent worldwide, has a high mortality rate that significantly endangers public health2. In China alone, lung cancer results in about 180,000 deaths annually3,4. Research indicates that while the five-year survival rate for lung cancer patients is under 15%, early diagnosis can boost this figure to 85%5. Consequently, timely detection and treatment of this disease are critical6. Nonetheless, the absence of pronounced symptoms in early-stage lung cancer often delays detection, thus increasing mortality rates7. There is a pressing need for a precise and rapid diagnostic method to decrease lung cancer mortality through early detection8.

Research on early lung cancer detection has typically focused on traditional image processing and fundamental machine learning techniques. Established methods like texture analysis, morphological analysis, and feature selection are commonly used in diagnosing lung cancer. For instance, Alkasas et al.9 introduced a texture analysis method utilizing an enhanced local ternary pattern, whereas Fiorelli et al.10 employed analysis of circulating tumor cells for diagnosis. Similarly, Lanjewar et al.11 combined density scanning technology with machine learning classifiers to effectively identify lung cancer via CT scans. These techniques are highly sensitive and accurate in extracting image features and distinguishing tissue structures. However, they may be prone to noise and interference when processing complex or low-contrast images, potentially resulting in data loss.

Machine learning-based methods for lung cancer image detection primarily utilize machine learning algorithms to automatically analyze and identify abnormal nodules or lesions in pulmonary medical images, thereby enhancing the accuracy and efficiency of early lung cancer diagnosis12. For example, Aouadi et al.13 conducted staging studies of lung cancer cells using a radiomics-based random forest classifier, while El-Askary et al.14 used a random forest to extract features from pulmonary CT images for training and proposed radio frequency optimization to enhance classification accuracy. Additionally, Xing et al.15 employed a fuzzy K-nearest neighbor model for early diagnosis research of lung cancer, and Nanglia et al.16 classified lung cancer using a hybrid algorithm of support vector machines and neural networks. Sun et al.17 introduced a systematic classification method using support vector machines for lung cancer. These methods, with their high degree of automation and speed, significantly assist doctors in early diagnosis, enhancing diagnostic accuracy and efficiency. However, these techniques are limited by the complexity and diversity of medical images and are susceptible to factors such as image quality and inaccurate data labeling, which may lead to unstable and unreliable diagnostic results.

With the rise of deep learning, image processing techniques in medical analysis have made significant strides18. Deep learning’s core lies in grasping data features and abstractions through layered neural networks, allowing for the comprehension of intricate data patterns19. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) have revolutionized medical imaging since 2012, offering promising prospects for the medical domain. For instance, Li et al.20 devised an object detection model using Fast R-CNN, which boosted target detection accuracy by integrating multiple subnetworks. Xu et al.21, leveraging multi-scale features, proposed an enhanced Faster R-CNN model for pulmonary nodule detection. Indumathi et al.22 effectively categorized pulmonary diseases in X-ray images using Mask R-CNN. These approaches exhibit high accuracy and efficiency in detecting and segmenting lung cancer, enhancing early diagnosis speed and accuracy. Despite their reliance on extensive training data and computational resources, along with challenges in recognizing complex images and lesions, single-stage detection models offer streamlined processes, making them well-suited for lung cancer detection23,24.

Single-stage detection models have streamlined network structures and simplified training processes through end-to-end training, leading to faster and more efficient detection25–31. Kumar Sahoo et al.32 applied transfer learning with YOLOv2 to achieve 97% accuracy in tumor detection. However, this method struggles with detecting smaller lesions or nodules in lung images. Huang et al.18 proposed an effective detection method using the 3D OSAF-YOLOv3 model, which integrates OSAv2, RFB, and an additional feature fusion scheme (FFS) for rapid and accurate detection of nodules in CT images, thereby improving performance. Nevertheless, the single-scale detection, MX-Pool operation, and FFS system contribute to increased computational and storage costs. Dlamini et al.33 developed a fully automatic detection system using an improved YOLOv4 model that employs advanced convolutional neural networks for locating, automatically detecting, segmenting, and reconstructing 3D tumor volumes, assisting doctors in treating patients. Although the YOLOv4 model offers excellent detection speed and positioning accuracy, it may perform poorly in detecting minority classes when dealing with class imbalance issues.

Zhou et al.34 introduced CCGL-YOLOv5, a lung tumor detection model with enhanced fusion of multimodal image features using a cross-modal fusion transformer module. However, it lacks lightweight design considerations. Zhao et al.35 devised a multi-scale target detection model based on YOLOv7, incorporating multiple detection heads and the CBAM attention module for feature extraction at different scales. Yet, its adaptability suffers in resource-limited environments. Qiu et al.36 proposed YOLOv8-FAM, an enhanced model integrating attention mechanisms and the SWIN transformer module for improved global information collection, albeit with a relatively large weight.

Previous research in deep learning-based lung cancer detection has yielded many positive outcomes. However, despite numerous attempts at precision optimization, existing models still face fundamental limitations rooted in their architectural design. Firstly, they often rely on convolutional layers with fixed receptive fields, making them inherently vulnerable to the vast scale variations of pulmonary nodules, from early-stage micro-nodules to large tumor masses. Secondly, they struggle to decouple and effectively model the complex, multi-level context required for accurate diagnosis, such as concurrently analyzing a lesion’s intrinsic morphology and its long-range relationships with distant anatomical landmarks. Finally, and most critically, they lack mechanisms for high-order relational reasoning, failing to capture the diagnostic significance of a constellation of co-occurring findings, which is a cornerstone of expert radiological interpretation. While many incremental improvements to existing YOLO architectures have been proposed37–39, they do not fundamentally address these architectural shortcomings.

To address these profound challenges, this paper presents a novel detection model built upon a fundamentally redesigned feature extractor: the Cognitive Reasoning C2f (CR-C2f) module. Our approach emulates the hierarchical cognitive workflow of a radiologist through a three-stage feature analysis pipeline. The process begins with a Multi-scale Convolution (MSC) module to achieve robust feature perception across a wide spectrum of scales. Subsequently, a series of Global-Local Attention (GLA) Bottlenecks, which advance beyond simpler channel attention mechanisms40 and draw inspiration from developments in transformer architectures41, perform sophisticated contextual reasoning by simultaneously modeling rich local details and critical long-range dependencies. The final stage is a Hypergraph Convolution (HGC) Refiner, which elevates the analysis to perform high-order relational inference, learning to recognize complex diagnostic patterns from the interwoven feature relationships. By progressing from perception to reasoning and finally to inference, our model constructs a feature representation of unparalleled depth and diagnostic relevance. The primary contribution of this work is a novel architecture that transitions from simple pattern matching to a form of cognitive reasoning, leading to a significant improvement in the accuracy and reliability of lung cancer detection.

Results

Datasets

Timely and accurate detection of lung cancer is essential for reducing the disease’s mortality rate. To support this objective, this study constructed a comprehensive dataset by integrating several publicly available lung cancer datasets, including the LIDC-IDRI (Lung Image Database Consortium and Image Database Resource Initiative)42, the NLST (National Lung Screening Trial)43, and selected subsets from the TCIA (The Cancer Imaging Archive)44.

From the LIDC-IDRI dataset, we selected approximately 800 CT scans containing annotated pulmonary nodules with malignancy ratings. From the NLST dataset, 500 low-dose CT scans were extracted, focusing on patients with confirmed diagnostic outcomes to ensure clinical relevance. In addition, 200 CT scans were chosen from TCIA collections that included high-quality annotations of lung lesions. The inclusion criteria emphasized scans with clear lesion boundaries, consistent slice thickness, and complete annotation information, while scans with severe artifacts or incomplete metadata were excluded.

All selected CT images were uniformly annotated using the LabelImg software, and the annotations were stored in .txt format for compatibility with common object detection frameworks. Evaluation settings and metrics

The experimental setup was based on a Linux operating system, utilizing an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU equipped with 32 GB of VRAM to ensure sufficient computational capacity for large-scale model training and inference. The framework was implemented in PyTorch 1.7.0 running within a Python 3.8 environment, providing both flexibility and stability for deep learning research. Hyperparameters were carefully tuned to optimize convergence and generalization: the initial learning rate was set to 0.01 with a cosine decay schedule, the total number of training epochs was fixed at 300, the momentum coefficient was 0.937 to stabilize parameter updates, the weight decay was 0.0005 to prevent overfitting, and the batch size was 16 to balance GPU memory consumption with training efficiency. These hyperparameter settings are consistent with state-of-the-art configurations commonly employed in object detection tasks, adapted here to the medical imaging domain.

To rigorously evaluate the performance of the proposed method in lung cancer detection, four primary quantitative metrics were adopted: precision, recall, mean average precision (mAP), and F1-score. Precision P evaluates the proportion of correctly identified positive samples among all positive predictions, reflecting the model’s ability to minimize false positives. A higher precision indicates that the model produces fewer erroneous detections, which is crucial in medical applications where false alarms can lead to unnecessary follow-up procedures. Recall R, on the other hand, measures the fraction of true positive samples correctly detected among all actual positive cases, capturing the model’s sensitivity. In the context of lung cancer detection, recall is particularly important because missed detections (false negatives) could result in critical diagnostic failures.

The mean average precision (mAP) provides a comprehensive measure of performance by averaging the precision values across multiple recall thresholds and object classes. This metric is widely recognized as the gold standard for evaluating object detection models, as it balances the trade-off between precision and recall across the full range of detection thresholds. Finally, the F1-score, defined as the harmonic mean of precision and recall, serves as a balanced metric that jointly considers both accuracy and coverage. It is particularly useful when an application requires a compromise between reducing false positives and minimizing false negatives45–47.

Together, these metrics offer a well-rounded evaluation of the model’s capability to detect lung cancer lesions accurately and comprehensively. Precision highlights diagnostic reliability, recall emphasizes coverage and safety, mAP quantifies overall detection quality across thresholds, and F1-score integrates both perspectives into a single value.

Bold values indicate the best performance achieved by our final model (CR-YOLO) across all metrics. The first step, incorporating the Multi-scale Convolution (MSC) module, yielded a substantial increase in recall and raised the mAP. This is primarily attributed to the MSC’s ability to capture features across a spectrum of receptive fields via its parallel dilated convolutions. This endows the model with an inherent sensitivity to the vast scale variations of pulmonary nodules, enabling it to effectively detect previously missed micro-nodules and thus significantly reduce false negatives.

Subsequently, the integration of the Global-Local Attention (GLA) Bottlenecks brought about a major leap in precision and a further 2.5% increase in mAP. This improvement stems directly from the GLA’s dual-branch contextual reasoning capability. The Local-Context branch enhances the discrimination of fine-grained textures and lesion boundaries, while the Global-Sparse branch models long-range dependencies, allowing the model to better differentiate true lesions from anatomically similar structures. This synergistic reasoning process is crucial for reducing false positives.

Finally, the addition of the Hypergraph Convolution (HGC) Refiner, completing the full CR-YOLO model, pushed all metrics to their peak performance, culminating in an mAP of 92.5%. This ultimate enhancement is credited to the HGC Refiner’s unique capacity for high-order relational inference. By abstracting salient features into a dual hypergraph and performing message passing, the model transcends pairwise relationships to recognize complex diagnostic patterns, or constellations of findings. This final inference stage is vital for resolving the most ambiguous cases, leading to a substantial boost in overall diagnostic accuracy and robustness.

Parameter ablation:

Table 3 presents a parameter-level ablation over the three key components of CR-YOLO—MSC, GLA, and HGC—probing how their main hyperparameters jointly impact accuracy and efficiency. The default configuration (MSC dilations {1,2,3}, large-kernel depthwise convolution size Klk = 21, deformable attention sampling K = 4, and HGC vertex set/neighbor size (N, k) = (256, 6)) already yields a strong balance (mAP 92.5, Precision 91.4, Recall 90.9, F1 91.3) under moderate complexity (12.8M params, 64.1G FLOPs). Expanding MSC’s dilation set to include 5 raises mAP slightly (92.6) but increases FLOPs (68.7G), whereas pruning scales in MSC harms robustness (mAP 91.7), underscoring the importance of multi-scale perception. Within GLA, Klk = 21 is a sweet spot: a smaller kernel (15) reduces recall, and a much larger kernel (31) adds cost with marginal gains. Increasing the deformable sampling from K = 4 to K = 8 produces the best headline mAP (92.7) and the highest recall (91.2), but at a noticeable compute uptick (69.5G). For HGC, shrinking the vertex set to N = 128 reduces FLOPs (61.5G) but also suppresses recall, while expanding to N = 384 raises precision slightly at a steep compute cost (72.4G). Collectively, these observations corroborate the design choice behind the default hyperparameters: they sit near the accuracy–efficiency Pareto frontier. Discussion

This study was predicated on the hypothesis that the performance ceiling of current lung cancer detection models is not merely a matter of scale or optimization, but one of architectural philosophy. Our results, which show the proposed CR-YOLO significantly outperforming established and state-of-the-art models, lend strong support to this hypothesis. The performance gap suggests that for complex diagnostic tasks like lung cancer detection, a shift is necessary from monolithic, general-purpose feature extractors towards more structured, domain-inspired reasoning pipelines. The success of CR-YOLO is not just in achieving a higher mAP, but in demonstrating the tangible benefits of a hierarchical architecture that explicitly emulates a radiologist’s cognitive workflow of perception, reasoning, and inference.

The ablation study provides a granular view into the synergy of the CR-C2f module’s components, revealing a non-linear, complementary relationship between them. The initial integration of the Multi-scale Convolution (MSC) module created a high-recall foundation by ensuring a robust, scale-invariant perception of potential nodules. This, however, is a double-edged sword, as it can increase the number of candidate regions that require further scrutiny. The subsequent Global-Local Attention (GLA) Bottlenecks acted as a powerful reasoning filter. The sharp rise in precision at this stage indicates its proficiency in contextual disambiguation, effectively pruning false positives by analyzing both the intrinsic details and extrinsic relationships of the candidates identified by MSC. The final Hypergraph Convolution (HGC) Refiner then performed a crucial role in resolving the most complex cases. Its contribution to boosting both precision and recall in the final step suggests that its high-order reasoning was essential for correctly classifying ambiguous lesions that could not be resolved by contextual cues alone. This staged process acts as a cognitive funnel, progressively refining a wide perceptual field into a specific, high-confidence diagnosis.

When contextualized within the broader literature, our work offers several key insights. Compared to the iterative refinements of the YOLO series, CR-YOLO introduces a paradigm shift by integrating explicit reasoning modules rather than simply optimizing the feature extraction backbone. In relation to Transformer-based models like RT-DETR, which excel at modeling implicit global relationships, our hybrid approach demonstrates a distinct advantage. While a Transformer’s self-attention is unstructured, the HGC Refiner imposes a structured and explicit high-order reasoning process guided by learned spatial and semantic relationships. We argue that this explicit structure is better suited for medical tasks where diagnostic criteria often rely on recognizing specific, multi-component patterns. Our primary methodological contribution, therefore, is the novel adaptation and integration of a hypergraph network—a tool typically used for offline, population-level analysis—as an end-to-end feature refinement engine within a real-time detection framework, effectively bridging two disparate fields of medical AI.

Despite the promising results, this study has several limitations that warrant discussion and offer avenues for future research. Firstly, while the dataset used was diverse in terms of nodule types, the model’s generalizability must be rigorously tested on larger, multi-center longitudinal datasets, which would allow for the assessment of its performance on follow-up scans and evolving lesions. Secondly, the advanced reasoning components, particularly the dynamic vertex construction and message passing in the HGC Refiner, introduce significant computational overhead. Future work should explore model compression techniques, such as knowledge distillation or graph pruning, to create a lightweight version of the CR-C2f module suitable for real-time clinical deployment. Lastly, the interpretability of the learned hypergraphs themselves remains a challenge. While Grad-CAM shows where the model is looking, it does not explain the high-order logic being applied. Developing methods to visualize and clinically correlate the learned hyperedges could be a fascinating direction for future research, potentially uncovering novel, sub-visual diagnostic biomarkers. A further avenue involves the multi-modal fusion of our model’s powerful visual features with clinical data, such as patient history or biomarkers, to create a more holistic and personalized risk prediction tool.

In this work, we addressed the critical limitations of existing deep learning models in the automated detection of lung cancer. We proposed CR-YOLO, a novel detection framework centered around the innovative Cognitive Reasoning C2f (CR-C2f) module. This module pioneers a hierarchical, three-stage reasoning pipeline that emulates an expert’s workflow, progressing from multi-scale perception, through contextual reasoning, to high-order relational inference.

Our extensive experiments demonstrated that CR-YOLO achieves state-of-the-art performance, significantly outperforming a wide range of established and advanced architectures. The ablation studies meticulously validated the critical and synergistic contribution of each component within the CR-C2f module—the Multi-scale Convolution, the Global-Local Attention Bottlenecks, and the Hypergraph Convolution Refiner. Furthermore, qualitative and Grad-CAM analyses provided strong visual evidence that our model’s superior accuracy is grounded in a decision-making process that is both precise and clinically relevant, enhancing its trustworthiness.

The primary contribution of this research is the introduction and validation of a cognitive reasoning paradigm within a real-time object detection framework. The principles laid out in this paper have significant implications for clinical practice, offering a pathway towards more reliable AI-powered tools that can aid radiologists in the early and accurate diagnosis of lung cancer, potentially reducing missed diagnoses and improving patient outcomes. We believe the hierarchical reasoning architecture presented herein serves as a robust blueprint for developing the next generation of intelligent systems for other complex medical imaging challenges.

Input: In the input stage, YOLOv8 employs an improved version of Mosaic data augmentation. By adaptively scaling and combining images, Mosaic increases the diversity of training samples, which strengthens the model’s ability to generalize and capture contextual information. This approach enhances detection performance for small objects and mitigates overfitting in complex scenarios, allowing the model to learn more robust representations from limited data.

Backbone Network: The backbone network serves as the foundation for feature extraction and is composed of CBS (Convolution-Batch Normalization-SiLU), C2f, and SPPF (Spatial Pyramid Pooling-Fast) modules. The CBS module performs convolutional transformations followed by normalization and a SiLU activation, ensuring stable and efficient feature propagation. The C2f module replaces the C3 block used in YOLOv5, introducing a design that improves gradient flow through cross-layer connections, thereby enhancing feature richness while reducing redundancy. The SPPF module provides an efficient means of multi-scale feature aggregation, capturing information from different receptive fields without significantly increasing computational overhead. Together, these components create a backbone that is both powerful in representation and efficient in computation.

Neck Layer: The neck layer is designed to integrate and refine the feature maps extracted by the backbone. YOLOv8 adopts a hybrid structure combining FPN (Feature Pyramid Network) and PAN (Path Aggregation Network). Through a top-down pathway, FPN propagates high-level semantic information to lower-resolution features, which is particularly beneficial for detecting small-scale objects. Conversely, PAN introduces a bottom-up pathway that reintroduces low-level spatial detail into higher-resolution maps, reinforcing the model’s ability to localize large objects and handle complex backgrounds. The joint use of top-down and bottom-up flows ensures that the feature maps passed to the detection head contain both strong semantic cues and fine-grained details, thereby improving robustness across scales.

Detection Head: At the final stage, YOLOv8 employs an anchor-free detection head. Unlike anchor-based methods that rely on predefined bounding box priors, the anchor-free design directly regresses object centers and dimensions, significantly simplifying the model while improving convergence speed and prediction accuracy. The detection head simultaneously performs localization and classification, leveraging multi-scale features to handle objects of varying sizes. In addition to bounding box regression and category prediction, YOLOv8 can extend this stage to include an optional segmentation branch, enabling pixel-level instance segmentation. This design allows the detection head to not only achieve precise localization but also deliver accurate semantic understanding of objects.

In summary, YOLOv8 achieves a balance between speed and accuracy through carefully optimized architectural choices. By combining advanced input augmentation, efficient backbone modules, a dual-path feature integration neck, and a streamlined anchor-free detection head, it establishes itself as one of the most representative and effective real-time detection models available today.

Overview of the CR-C2f module

The standard C2f module in YOLOv8, while being a potent feature extractor for general-purpose object detection, reveals inherent limitations when faced with the nuanced and complex demands of medical image analysis, particularly in the domain of lung cancer detection from CT scans. The diagnostic process requires not only the identification of lesions across a vast spectrum of scales and morphologies but also a deep understanding of the intricate web of contextual and relational patterns that define malignancy. The original C2f architecture, with its fixed receptive fields and lack of explicit mechanisms for high-order reasoning, is ill-equipped to address these challenges. To this end, we fundamentally redesign its internal structure and propose the Cognitive Reasoning C2f (CR-C2f) module. This novel module is architected to emulate the hierarchical cognitive workflow of an expert radiologist, systematically progressing through three distinct stages of feature analysis: multi-scale perception, contextual reasoning, and high-order relational inference.

The entire process within the CR-C2f module, as depicted in Fig. 9, is conceptualized as a logical pipeline that progressively refines information from raw pixels into diagnostically meaningful representations. The pipeline begins with the Multi-scale Convolution (MSC) module, which serves as the perceptual frontend. By employing parallel dilated convolutions with varying rates, it simultaneously captures features at multiple receptive fields. This ensures that the initial feature representation is inherently scale-invariant, equally sensitive to both minute ground-glass opacities and large solid tumors, thus providing a rich and unbiased foundation for subsequent analysis.Fig. 9The architecture of our proposed Cognitive Reasoning C2f (CR-C2f) module.This figure shows the architecture of the proposed Cognitive Reasoning C2f (CR-C2f) module, designed to emulate a radiologist’s workflow. By performing message passing over this high-order structure, the HGC Refiner learns to recognize complex, multi-component diagnostic patterns–constellations of findings that often signify malignancy. This ultimate refinement step imbues the features with a level of understanding that closely mirrors clinical inference. Through this structured progression from perception to reasoning and finally to inference, the CR-C2f module constructs a feature representation of unparalleled depth and diagnostic relevance, significantly enhancing the model’s accuracy and reliability.

Multi-scale convolution (MSC)

The initial stage of feature extraction is critical for establishing a robust foundation for subsequent analysis. Pulmonary nodules can manifest at any scale, and a single, fixed-size convolutional kernel is inherently biased, either failing to encompass the entirety of a large tumor or losing the fine-grained detail of a micro-nodule. To overcome this, the first component of our CR-C2f module is a Multi-scale Convolution (MSC) block, designed to simultaneously capture a spectrum of spatial contexts from the input feature map. The architecture of the MSC module is depicted in Fig. 10. It replaces the initial standard convolution in the C2f block with a parallel multi-branch structure. Upon receiving an input feature map, the MSC block processes it through four concurrent pathways. One branch employs a standard ConvModule to preserve the original feature representation and capture fine-grained details at the base receptive field. Three parallel branches utilize dilated convolutions, also known as atrous convolutions, with progressively increasing dilation rates. A dilated convolution introduces gaps between the kernel’s weights, effectively expanding the receptive field without increasing the number of parameters or sacrificing spatial resolution. By using multiple dilation rates, these paths are specialized to capture contextual information from increasingly larger surrounding regions. The feature maps generated by these four branches, each containing information from a distinct scale, are then concatenated along the channel dimension. This fusion creates an enriched, multi-scale feature representation. Finally, a ConvModule with a 1 × 1 kernel is applied to this concatenated tensor. This final layer serves a dual purpose: it intelligently fuses the scale-specific information by promoting interaction across channels, and it restores the channel dimension to match the requirements of the subsequent layers. Through this mechanism, the MSC module ensures that the features passed to the subsequent stages are inherently scale-aware, providing a richer and more comprehensive perceptual basis for advanced reasoning.Fig. 10The architecture of Multi-scale Convolution (MSC) module.This figure illustrates the Multi-scale Convolution (MSC) module, designed for robust feature perception across various scales. Following the initial multi-scale feature perception by the MSC module, the feature maps are channeled into a series of Global-Local Attention (GLA) Bottlenecks. This stage constitutes the core of the reasoning process within our CR-C2f module, specifically engineered to address the profound challenges in contextual feature modeling for lung cancer analysis. The diagnosis often hinges on interpreting both the intrinsic characteristics of a nodule, such as spiculated margins, which demand high-resolution local analysis, and its extrinsic relationships with distant anatomical landmarks like the pleura or mediastinum, which require a global perspective. To effectively decouple and model these disparate feature types, the GLA Bottleneck employs a sophisticated dual-branch architecture, illustrated in Fig. 11. The first pathway, the Local-Context branch, is meticulously designed to simulate an expansive receptive field with high computational efficiency. Its primary role is to construct a rich, detailed representation of the tumor and its immediate microenvironment. This is realized through a three-step decomposition of large kernel attention. The process commences with a large-kernel (21 × 21) depth-wise convolution, which serves as a texture and morphology anchor. Its expansive size allows it to encompass the entire structure of a medium-sized nodule and its periphery, extracting foundational patterns related to shape and border texture within each feature channel. Subsequently, a smaller-kernel (7 × 7) dilated depth-wise convolution acts as a microenvironment sampler. Its dilated nature enables it to probe a wider, sparser area by skipping adjacent pixels, adeptly capturing the relationship between the nodule’s core and surrounding tissues like vessels or airways without blurring the fine details. The final step is a 1 × 1 point-wise convolution, which functions as a feature channel fusion and re-calibration stage. After the spatial layers have gathered raw contextual information, this layer facilitates interaction across channels, learning to amplify salient patterns and suppress noise, thus enhancing the discriminative power of the local representation.

Concurrently, the second pathway, the Global-Sparse branch, implements a Deformable Attention mechanism, representing a paradigm shift from passive, grid-based analysis to an active, query-driven sampling process. Its function is to model the sparse yet critical long-range dependencies that mirror a radiologist’s diagnostic gaze, which might dart between a primary lesion and a potentially related distant feature. Central to this is the Offset Network, which acts as a saliency guide. For each query location in the feature map, this lightweight network dynamically predicts a small set of two-dimensional offsets. These offsets steer the attention mechanism to sample features not from a fixed grid, but from locations deemed most informative for the current query, regardless of their distance. This allows the model to build connections between a nodule and, for instance, a distant pleural tag or a suspicious hilar lymph node, capturing relationships that are vital for accurate staging and diagnosis.

The operational principle of Deformable Attention can be formally articulated. Hypergraph convolution refiner

The final and most sophisticated stage of the CR-C2f module is the Hypergraph Convolution (HGC) Refiner. Following the multi-scale perception by MSC and the contextual reasoning by GLA Bottlenecks, the HGC Refiner elevates feature processing from a grid-based paradigm to an abstract relational reasoning paradigm. Its fundamental purpose is to explicitly model high-order, non-local relationships between salient diagnostic regions, which are often beyond the modeling capacity of even advanced attention mechanisms. Clinical diagnosis frequently relies on identifying a constellation of co-occurring findings. The HGC Refiner is designed to mimic this high-level cognitive process by abstracting key image regions into a set of vertices and then constructing hypergraphs to learn their complex, multi-way dependencies. This methodology, inspired by recent advancements in pathological image analysis, involves two primary phases detailed below: vertex construction with dual hypergraph modeling, and two-channel hypergraph semantic computation.

The architecture of the Two-Channel Hypergraph Semantic Computation module.

The initial vertex features are processed through two parallel streams of Hypergraph Convolution (HG Conv) layers, guided independently by the topology (Ht) and feature (Hf) incidence matrices. The detailed views below illustrate the core two-step message passing mechanism within each HG Conv layer: a vertex-to-hyperedge (v to ϵ) feature aggregation, followed by a hyperedge-to-vertex (ϵ to v) feature update.

Each HG Conv layer refines the vertex features via a message-passing mechanism that allows vertices connected by a common hyperedge to exchange information. This operation consists of a vertex-to-hyperedge (v to ϵ) aggregation step, where features of all vertices in a hyperedge are pooled to form a hyperedge representation, followed by a hyperedge-to-vertex (ϵ to v) update step, where each vertex aggregates features from all hyperedges it belongs to.
