Abstract
. We investigate functionals defined on manifolds through parameterizations. If they are to be meaningful, from a geometrical viewpoint, they ought to be invariant under reparameterizations. Standard, local, integral functionals with this invariance property are well-known. We would like to focus though on the passage from a given arbitrary functional to its invariant realization or invariant hull through the use of inner-variations, much in the same way as with the convex or quasiconvex hulls of integrands in the vector Calculus of Variations. These two processes are, however, very different in nature. After examining some basic, interesting, general properties about the mutual relationship between a functional and its invariant realization, we deal with the one dimensional case to gain some initial familiarity with such a transformation and calculations, before proceeding to the higher dimensional situation. As one would anticipate, explicit computations in the latter are much harder to perform, if not impossible, as one is to work with vector variational problems. In particular, we are able to reach some modest conclusion about the volume functional of a piece of a manifold in the general N N -dimensional situation, especially in the two-dimensional case N = 2 N=2 . Various problems and conjectures are stated along the way.



Key Words.
Invariant hull, vector variational problem, minimal area.

AMS(MOS) subject classifications.
49Q10, 58E12, 49Q05.

1.
Introduction

We would like to look at functionals

I({\bm{u}}):W^{1,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})\to\mathbb{R}

where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
is some model domain, like the unit ball or square, and
W^{1,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
,
p\geq 1
, is the standard Sobolev space.
We want to focus on the case
N<m
, which can be appropriately labeled the geometric case, where feasible maps

{\bm{u}}\in W^{1,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})

can be interpreted as parameterizations of pieces of
N
-dimensional manifolds embedded or immersed in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. These variational problems can also be declared as low-dimensional vector variational problem to stress the fact that
N<m
.
One must insist in that such objects should be regular or geometric in the sense that the jacobian matrix

\nabla{\bm{u}}({\bm{x}})\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times N}

should have maximum rank
N
at every point
{\bm{x}}\in\Omega
,
and, in particular, induce a well-defined orientation of the associated manifold.
It is important to turn our attention to reparameterizations of the same underlying geometric object
{\bm{u}}(\Omega)\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
for each
{\bm{u}}
. In fact, our emphasis is on functionals that are parameterization-invariant, as these would be, rightly so, the ones accepted for geometric analysis.

It is well-known (
[
6
]
,
[
11
]
) that local, integral functionals given by a density

W(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{U}):\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m\times N},\quad I({\bm{u}})=\int_{\Omega}W({\bm{u}}({\bm{x}}),\nabla{\bm{u}}({\bm{x}}))\,d{\bm{x}},

are invariant with respect to parametrizations if

W(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{U}\mathbf{F})=\det\mathbf{F}\,W(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{U}),\quad(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{U})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{m\times N},\mathbf{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N},\det\mathbf{F}>0,

which in turn is equivalent to the existence of a density

w(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{V}):\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R},\quad n=\binom{m}{N}

positively homogeneous of degree one on the variable
\mathbf{V}
, such that

(1.1)

W(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{U})=w(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{U}_{1}\wedge\mathbf{U}_{2}\wedge\dots\wedge\mathbf{U}_{N}),\quad\mathbf{U}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{U}_{1}&\mathbf{U}_{2}&\dots&\mathbf{U}_{N}\end{pmatrix}.

As a matter of fact, variational principles associated with the functional

I({\bm{u}})=\int_{\Omega}w\left({\bm{u}},\frac{\partial{\bm{u}}}{\partial{\bm{x}}_{1}}\wedge\frac{\partial{\bm{u}}}{\partial{\bm{x}}_{2}}\wedge\dots\wedge\frac{\partial{\bm{u}}}{\partial{\bm{x}}_{N}}\right)\,d{\bm{x}}

are indeed quite delicate. Just think about the particular example

(1.2)

A({\bm{u}})=\int_{\Omega}\left|\frac{\partial{\bm{u}}}{\partial{\bm{x}}_{1}}\wedge\frac{\partial{\bm{u}}}{\partial{\bm{x}}_{2}}\right|\,d{\bm{x}},\quad\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2},{\bm{u}}({\bm{x}}):\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{3},

whose minimizers, possibly under suitable boundary conditions, correspond to minimal area solutions, and the overwhelming amount of deep and fundamental work that has stirred over the years.
We would like to highlight the important case in which
m=N+1
,
n=N+1
, and

w(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}):\mathbb{R}^{N+1}\times\mathbb{R}^{N+1}\to\mathbb{R}

is positively-homogeneous of degree one in
\mathbf{v}
.

To be specific, though much more general situations may be considered, one fundamental problem is the following.

Problem 1.1
.

Find densities
w(\mathbf{v}):\mathbb{R}^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
, positively-homogeneous of degree one, and suitable classes
\mathcal{U}
of maps in
H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3})
where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a model domain, such that the variational problem

\hbox{Minimize in }{\bm{u}}\in\mathcal{U}:\quad\int_{\Omega}w\left(\frac{\partial{\bm{u}}}{\partial{\bm{x}}_{1}}({\bm{x}})\wedge\frac{\partial{\bm{u}}}{\partial{\bm{x}}_{2}}({\bm{x}})\right)\,d{\bm{x}}

admits minimizers.

As far as we can tell, the only situation where Problem
1.1
can be shown to be solvable is the classical minimal surface problem (
1.2
) for a suitable class
\mathcal{U}
leaving monotonically invariant the boundary
\partial\Omega
, and three different, prescribed points at the boundary
\partial\Omega
, unchanged. This is classical. Check the recent encyclopedic work
[
5
]
, or the more classical reference
[
7
]
. As far as we can tell, the answer is not known even for
w
the
p
th-norm of vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
p\neq 2
. The development of the BV theory of area-minimizing hypersurfaces is, in this regard, of paramount importance (see por instance
[
4
]
,
[
7
]
,
[
8
]
,
[
18
]
).

Our viewpoint is, on the one hand, broader in the sense that we would like to let more general functionals enter into our discussion; but on the other, it is less sophisticated and more straightforward than that in the references at the end of the previous paragraph. Our basic driving principle has a two-fold motivation, which in simple, general terms can be stated as follows:

(1)

Starting from arbitrary functionals, one can produce, in a natural way, invariant, associated functionals (invariant hulls) by minimizing on suitable classes of changes of variables.

(2)

Minimizers for functionals and their invariant hulls must coincide over suitable classes of invariant mappings.

More specifically, our program proceeds in various steps.

(1)

Start with a typical local integral functional of the form

I({\bm{u}})=\int_{\Omega}W({\bm{x}},{\bm{u}}({\bm{x}}),\nabla{\bm{u}}({\bm{x}}))\,d{\bm{x}},\quad{\bm{u}}\in W^{1,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m}),\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N},N<m,

or, for the sake of definiteness, focus on the situation

(1.3)

I({\bm{u}})=\int_{\Omega}W(\nabla{\bm{u}}({\bm{x}}))\,d{\bm{x}},\quad W(\mathbf{U}):\mathbb{R}^{m\times N}\to\mathbb{R},

and eventually assume the necessary hypotheses on
W
and the underlying class of competing maps
{\bm{u}}({\bm{x}})
, to ensure existence of minimizers.

(2)

We will define the invariant hull
I_{i}({\bm{u}})
of the functional
I
, by minimizing on a suitable class of changes of variables
{\bm{x}}=\Phi({\bm{y}})
in
\Omega

I_{i}({\bm{u}})=\inf_{\Phi}\int_{\Omega}W(\nabla{\bm{u}}_{\Phi}({\bm{x}}))\,d{\bm{x}},\quad{\bm{u}}_{\Phi}({\bm{x}})={\bm{u}}(\Phi^{-1}({\bm{x}})).

This new functional
I_{i}
is well-defined in most cases of interest, and, by construction, is parameterization-invariant. A different issue is if it admits a local, integral representation, and if so, if there are minimizers for it in the same class of mappings. We will see that this is not always so, in spite of the fact that the initial functional
I
is of the standard form (
1.3
).

(3)

At any rate, every minimizer of
I
over a suitably restricted invariant subclass of
W^{1,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
, will be a minimizer of
I_{i}
over the same class.

After going over some general material related to the passage from
I
to
I_{i}
, and some interesting properties of
I_{i}
, we will focus on the one-dimensional case
N=1
for which some explicit, simple computations are possible. This will serve as an initial interesting training ground to better appreciate the nature of the operation
I\mapsto I_{i}
, and to have at our disposal some simple explicit examples. Then we will focus on the much more difficult multidimensional case
N>1
to treat the particularly interesting example

(1.4)

I({\bm{u}})=\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{N^{N/2}}|\nabla{\bm{u}}({\bm{x}})|^{N}\,d{\bm{x}},

and see how far we can go in finding its invariant realization
I_{i}
, depending on dimension
N
. Functionals of the form

\int_{\Omega}W(\nabla{\bm{u}}({\bm{x}}))\,d{\bm{x}},\quad W(\mathbf{F}):\mathbb{R}^{m\times N}\to\mathbb{R},

where
W
is homogeneous of degree
N
, seem particularly interesting beyond example (
1.4
).
In general terms, for power functionals

I_{p}({\bm{u}})=\int_{\Omega}|\nabla{\bm{u}}({\bm{x}})|^{p}\,d{\bm{x}},\quad p>0,

not much can be said at this point in the multidimensional situation.

Note that by insisting in that
\Omega
is a ball or a square, we are consequently limiting the discussion in some very fundamental way since the topological class of the image manifold is being restricted.

2.
Some initial concepts and basic properties

This section gathers a few elementary facts that do not require any particular form of functionals, and whose proofs are completely elementary; yet they are worth to bear in mind.

Definition 2.1
.

(1)

We designate by
\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}(\Omega)
the class of all smooth, positively-oriented (
\mathcal{C}^{\infty}
)-diffeomorhisms
\Phi
of
\overline{\Omega}
onto itself.
We also put
\mathcal{D}_{\mathbf{id}}=\mathcal{D}_{\mathbf{id}}(\Omega)
for the subclass of
\mathcal{D}
of diffeomorphisms that are identical to the identity on
\partial\Omega
, and regard all possible invariant subclasses

(2.1)

\mathcal{D}_{\mathbf{id}}\subset\mathcal{D}_{0}\subset\mathcal{D}

in the sense

\Phi_{1}\circ\Phi_{2}\in\mathcal{D}_{0}\hbox{ if }\Phi_{i}\in\mathcal{D}_{0},i=1,2.

Obviously
\mathcal{D}_{\mathbf{id}}
and
\mathcal{D}
are invariant.

(2)

For an invariant subclass
\mathcal{D}_{0}
in (
2.1
), we say that a collection of maps

\mathcal{U}\subset W^{1,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})

is invariant under
\mathcal{D}_{0}
if it is invariant under the action

(2.2)

{\bm{u}}_{\Phi}({\bm{x}})={\bm{u}}(\Phi^{-1}({\bm{x}})),

that is to say
{\bm{u}}_{\Phi}\in\mathcal{U}
whenever
{\bm{u}}\in\mathcal{U}
and
\Phi\in\mathcal{D}_{0}
.

(3)

We say that a certain functional
I
is invariant or geometric, if

(2.3)

I({\bm{u}}_{\Phi})=I({\bm{u}}),\quad{\bm{u}}\in W^{1,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m}),\Phi\in\mathcal{D};

and more specifically that it is invariant under a given invariant class
\mathcal{D}_{0}
, if (
2.3
) only holds for
\Phi\in\mathcal{D}_{0}
.

Note the following.

(1)

The condition

\Phi({\bm{x}})={\bm{x}},\quad{\bm{x}}\in\partial\Omega,

is much more restrictive that the one accepted for
\mathcal{D}
; in fact, elements of
\mathcal{D}
ought to maintain
\partial\Omega
invariant

\Phi(\partial\Omega)=\partial\Omega,\quad\det\nabla\Phi({\bm{x}})>0\hbox{ a.e. in }\Omega.

Sometimes boundary conditions associated with
\mathcal{D}
are called “frictionless” (
[
11
]
, and references therein).
Typically, subclasses
\mathcal{D}_{0}
in (
2.1
) are determined by specifying the subset of
\partial\Omega
that is to be maintained unchanged by elements
\Phi
of
\mathcal{D}_{0}
, among possibly further restrictions.

(2)

The map
{\bm{u}}_{\Phi}
belongs to
W^{1,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})

whenever

{\bm{u}}\in W^{1,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m}),\Phi\in\mathcal{D}.

(3)

All elements in
\mathcal{D}
are legitimate changes of variables in
\Omega
.

(4)

Functionals are not assumed necessarily to be standard, local, integral functionals at this stage.

Suppose we are given
a general functional, not necessarily an integral functional,

I({\bm{u}}):W^{1,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})\to\mathbb{R}

which is assumed to be well-defined over a class
\mathcal{U}\subset W^{1,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})
of mappings, invariant with respect to some
\mathcal{D}_{0}
. We will assume that such an invariant class
\mathcal{D}_{0}
has been appropriately selected, and refer to it in all of our manipulations when we simply use the term invariant.

There is a natural way to produce invariant functionals from arbitrary examples by minimization over
\mathcal{D}_{0}

(2.4)

I_{i}({\bm{u}})=\inf_{\Phi\in\mathcal{D}_{0}}I({\bm{u}}_{\Phi}),

where
{\bm{u}}_{\Phi}
is given in (
2.2
).

Definition 2.2
.

Given an arbitrary functional

I({\bm{u}}):W^{1,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})\to\mathbb{R},

we call

I_{i}({\bm{u}}):W^{1,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{m})\to\mathbb{R}

in (
2.4
), its invariant realization with respect to
\mathcal{D}_{0}
.

It is elementary to realize that the invariant realization of every functional is invariant (under the same class
\mathcal{D}_{0}
), and hence we have a mechanism to produce all of the possible invariant functionals. However, given that trivial (constant) functionals are obviously invariant, it may happen that sometimes the functional
I_{i}
might turn out to be trivial even though
I
could be quite meaningful. In the same vein, the passage
I\mapsto I_{i}
may change the nature of the functional. For instance,
I
could be an integral functional, but
I_{i}
may not be so.

We establish some of these basic properties formally for future reference.

Proposition 2.1
.

For every functional
I:\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}
, its invariant or geometric version
I_{i}:\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}
defined through (
2.4
) is invariant, and
I_{i}\leq I
.

As indicated, this statement does not require a proof. It is a consequence of its own definition. The functional
I_{i}
can be given a parallel characterization in terms of invariant functionals.

Proposition 2.2
.

For
I:\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}
as above,

I_{i}=\sup\{E:\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}:E\leq I,E,\hbox{ invariant}\}.

Proof.

Put, for the time being,

I_{g}=\sup\{E:\mathcal{U}\to\mathbb{R}:E\leq I,E,\hbox{ invariant}\}.

If
E
is invariant and
E\leq I
, then it is clear that
E=E_{i}\leq I_{i}
, and so
I_{g}\leq I_{i}
. Conversely, since
I_{i}
is invariant and
I_{i}\leq I
, we should also have
I_{i}\leq I_{g}
.
∎

Corollary 2.3
.

Suppose that a functional
E
is invariant, and
E\leq I
. Assume that for each feasible
{\bm{u}}
given, there is a sequence
\{\Phi_{j}\}\in\mathcal{D}(\Omega)
such that
I({\bm{u}}_{\Phi_{j}})\to E({\bm{u}})
. Then
I_{i}\equiv E
, and
\{\Phi_{j}\}
is minimizing for (
2.4
).

Proof.

The existence of the sequence
\{\Phi_{j}\}
, for arbitrary
{\bm{u}}
, with the claimed property, implies that
I_{i}\leq E
. But, because
E
is invariant and
E\leq I
, by the preceding result,
I_{i}\geq E
. In particular, sequences
\{\Phi_{j}\}
, for each
{\bm{u}}
, become minimizing for (
2.4
).
∎

Our main basic result is concerned with the interplay between the optimization problems for both
I
and
I_{i}
.

Proposition 2.4
.

We always have

\inf_{{\bm{u}}\in\mathcal{U}}I({\bm{u}})=\inf_{{\bm{u}}\in\mathcal{U}}I_{i}({\bm{u}}).

Moreover, if we know that

\inf_{{\bm{u}}\in\mathcal{U}}I({\bm{u}})=\min_{{\bm{u}}\in\mathcal{U}}I({\bm{u}})=I({\bm{u}}_{0}),\quad{\bm{u}}_{0}\in\mathcal{U},

then
{\bm{u}}_{0}
becomes a minimizer for
I_{i}
in
\mathcal{U}
as well

I_{i}({\bm{u}}_{0})=\min_{{\bm{u}}\in\mathcal{U}}I_{i}({\bm{u}}).

Proof.

It is elementary to realize that

\inf_{{\bm{u}}\in\mathcal{U}}I_{i}({\bm{u}})=\inf_{{\bm{u}}\in\mathcal{U}}\inf_{\Phi\in\mathcal{D}}I({\bm{u}}_{\Phi})=\inf_{{\bm{u}}\in\mathcal{U}}I({\bm{u}}),

since
\mathcal{U}
is assumed to be invariant. If the infimum of
I
over
\mathcal{U}
is attained at some
{\bm{u}}_{0}\in\mathcal{U}
, then

I_{i}({\bm{u}}_{0})\leq I({\bm{u}}_{0})=\inf_{{\bm{u}}\in\mathcal{U}}I({\bm{u}})=\inf_{{\bm{u}}\in\mathcal{U}}I_{i}({\bm{u}})\leq I_{i}({\bm{u}}_{0}),

and our conclusion follows.
∎

This proposition is the principal method to tackle the existence of minimizers for geometric variational principles. It somehow amounts to the inverse process of the one we are describing here.

Problem 2.5
.

Given a relevant invariant functional
I
, defined on a certain family
\mathcal{M}
of manifolds, find another one
\mathcal{I}
, convex and coercive in such a way that admits minimizers
{\bm{u}}
in
\mathcal{M}
, designed so that
\mathcal{I}_{i}=I
. In this case
{\bm{u}}
becomes a minimizer for
I
in
\mathcal{M}
as well.

Another elementary, interesting consequence lets determine many other invariant realizations.

Proposition 2.6
.

Suppose we have
E\leq I
, and
I_{i}\leq E
. Then
E_{i}=I_{i}
.

3.
The one-dimensional case: curves

For the sake of illustration and to gain some insight on the nature of the passage from a given arbitrary functional to its invariant realization, we pay attention in this section to the one-dimensional case, i.e. to the case of curves in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. In this situation
\Omega
will be taken to be the unit interval. We expect that in this situation some explicit calculations may be possible.

We will always start with a typical integral functional

(3.1)

I(\mathbf{u})=\int_{0}^{1}W(\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t))\,dt,

depending on curves

\mathbf{u}(t):[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{m},\quad W(\mathbf{v}):\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}

and with an integrand just depending on the derivative
\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{u}^{\prime}
, and see how to calculate its invariant realization. Note that in this case, after a natural change of variables, we formally find

\int_{0}^{1}W(\mathbf{u}^{\prime}_{\phi}(t))\,dt=\int_{0}^{1}\phi^{\prime}(t)\,W\left(\frac{1}{\phi^{\prime}(t)}\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t)\right)\,dt.

For each fixed path
\mathbf{U}(=\mathbf{u}^{\prime})
, we need to face the optimization problem

\hbox{Minimize in }\phi:\quad\int_{0}^{1}\phi^{\prime}(t)W\left(\frac{1}{\phi^{\prime}(t)}\mathbf{U}(t)\right)\,dt

subjected to constraints

(3.2)

\phi(0)=0,\phi(1)=1,\quad\phi^{\prime}>0.

We realize that the existence of a minimizer for such a problem in an appropriate Sobolev class depends on the following elementary concept.

Definition 3.1
.

A continuous function

W(\mathbf{v}):\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}

is said to be radially convex and smooth if the sections

(3.3)

r\in(0,\infty)\mapsto r\,W\left(\frac{1}{r}\mathbf{x}\right),\quad\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\},

as functions of the single variable
r
, are (strictly) convex and smooth for all such
\mathbf{x}
.

A basic result follows.

Theorem 3.1
.

Suppose that the function

W(\mathbf{v}):\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}

is radially strictly convex and smooth, and let
I(\mathbf{u})
be the corresponding functional (
3.1
). Then

I_{i}(\mathbf{u})=\int_{0}^{1}\mathbf{f}(c(\mathbf{u}),\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t))\,W\left(\frac{1}{\mathbf{f}(c(\mathbf{u}),\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t))}\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t)\right)\,dt

where the function
\mathbf{f}(c,\mathbf{x})
is the inverse of

r\in(0,\infty)\mapsto\mathbf{g}(r,\mathbf{x})\equiv W\left(\frac{1}{r}\mathbf{x}\right)-\frac{1}{r}\nabla W\left(\frac{1}{r}\mathbf{x}\right)\cdot\mathbf{x},

for fixed
\mathbf{x}
, and the (constant) functional
c=c(\mathbf{u})
is determined through the condition

(3.4)

\int_{0}^{1}\mathbf{f}(c(\mathbf{u}),\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t))\,dt=1.

Proof.

According to our definition of
I_{i}
, we need to examine, for each feasible path
\mathbf{u}(t)
, the variational problem

\hbox{Minimize in }\phi(t):\int_{0}^{1}W\left(\frac{1}{\phi^{\prime}(t)}\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t)\right)\phi^{\prime}(t)\,dt

under constraints (
3.2
). We do not know a priori if there is a minimizer for such a problem based on the direct method, since we cannot count on coercivity.
We can, however, examine the corresponding Euler-Lagrange equation and check if it admits a solution; and realize afterwards, through convexity in the standard manner, that it is a minimizer of the problem.

We are thus led, taking into account the notation above, to the boundary value problem

-[\mathbf{g}(\phi^{\prime}(t),\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t))]^{\prime}=0\hbox{ in }(0,1),\quad\phi(0)=0,\phi(1)=1.

We conclude that

\mathbf{g}(\phi^{\prime}(t),\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t))=c(\mathbf{u})\quad\hbox{ or }\quad\mathbf{f}(c(\mathbf{u}),\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t))=\phi^{\prime}(t),

with
c=c(\mathbf{u})
, a constant (with respect to
t
). We note that for each fixed vector
\mathbf{x}
, the function

r\mapsto\mathbf{g}(r,\mathbf{x})

has a non-negative derivative due to the radial strict convexity of
W
, and so it is monotone (for positive
r
). After all,
\mathbf{g}(r,\mathbf{x})
is the first derivative of the sections in (
3.3
).
All that remains to argue is that the functional
c(\mathbf{u})
is to be determined by the condition

\int_{0}^{1}\mathbf{f}(c(\mathbf{u}),\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t))\,dt=\int_{0}^{1}\phi^{\prime}(t)\,dt=1.

∎

With the help of this theorem, we can perform some interesting explicit calculations. We will put

\overline{W}(\mathbf{x},r)=r\,W\left(\frac{1}{r}\mathbf{x}\right),\quad\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m},r>0,

and, for a fixed path
\mathbf{u}
,

w(t,r)=rW\left(\frac{1}{r}\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t)\right),

so that we are interested in finding the minimum of the variational problem

\hbox{Minimize in }\phi(t):\quad\int_{0}^{1}w(t,\phi^{\prime}(t))\,dt

under

\phi(0)=0,\phi(1)=1,\quad\phi^{\prime}>0.

We explore next some explicit examples.

3.1.
Some explicit examples

The first example is mandatory

W(\mathbf{u}):\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R},\quad W(\mathbf{u})=|\mathbf{u}|.

In this case

\overline{W}(\mathbf{x},r)=W(\mathbf{x})

for all
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
r>0
, and consequently

w(t,r)=W(\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t))

is independent of
r
. The corresponding variational problem becomes trivial since

\int_{0}^{1}w(t,\phi^{\prime}(t))\,dt=\int_{0}^{1}|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t)|\,dt

for every feasible
\phi
. This is not surprising since, after all, the initial variational problem yields the length of the image curve, and this is invariant under reparameterizations. Note that exactly the same calculations hold for every function
W(\mathbf{u})
which is positively-homogeneous of degree one.

More interesting is the quadratic case

W(\mathbf{u}):\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R},\quad W(\mathbf{u})=\frac{1}{2}|\mathbf{u}|^{2},

which is radially coercive, and strictly convex. According to Theorem
3.1
, we should care about the inverse of the function

\mathbf{g}(r,\mathbf{x})=\frac{1}{2r^{2}}|\mathbf{x}|^{2}-\frac{1}{r^{2}}|\mathbf{x}|^{2}=-\frac{1}{2r^{2}}|\mathbf{x}|^{2},

with respect to the variable
r
for fixed
\mathbf{x}
, which is

\mathbf{f}(c,\mathbf{x})=\frac{|\mathbf{x}|}{\sqrt{-2c}}.

Condition (
3.4
) becomes

2c=-\left(\int_{0}^{1}|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t)|\,dt\right)^{2},\quad\mathbf{f}(c(\mathbf{u}),\mathbf{u}^{\prime})=\frac{1}{\int_{0}^{1}|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t)|\,dt}|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}|,

and the corresponding invariant realization, after a few manipulations, becomes

I_{i}(\mathbf{u})=\frac{1}{2}\left(\int_{0}^{1}|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t)|\,dt\right)^{2}.

Note that this is not a typical integral functional despite the fact that the initial one was.

Let us explore now

W(\mathbf{u})=\frac{1}{p}|\mathbf{u}|^{p},\quad 0<p,

and distinguish the two ranges
0<p<1
,
1<p
. The second case is similar to the quadratic one and one finds

I_{i}(\mathbf{u})=\frac{1}{p}\left(\int_{0}^{1}|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t)|\,dt\right)^{p}.

The case
0<p<1
is drastically distinct, because

w(t,r)=r^{1-p}|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t)|^{p},

is no longer convex, and yet for every smooth path
\mathbf{u}
, the infimum of

\int_{0}^{1}\psi^{\prime}(t)^{1-p}\,|\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t)|^{p}\,dt.

in
\psi
vanishes. It suffices to take the sequence
\psi_{j}(t)=t^{j+1}
. Computations are elementary. In this case, the invariant realization
I_{i}(\mathbf{u})\equiv 0
is trivial.

An additional dependence of
W
on
\mathbf{u}
is essentially the same as above, i.e.

W=W(\mathbf{u}(t),\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t)).

In this case, for fixed
\mathbf{u}(t)
, the optimization problem in changes of variables
\phi(t)
would be

\hbox{Minimize in }\phi(t):\quad\int_{0}^{1}W\left(\mathbf{u}(t),\frac{1}{\phi^{\prime}(t)}\mathbf{u}^{\prime}(t)\right)\phi^{\prime}(t)\,dt

under
\phi(0)=0
,
\phi(1)=1
, and both the functions

\mathbf{g}(r,\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})=W\left(\mathbf{y},\frac{1}{r}\mathbf{x}\right)-\frac{1}{r}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}W\left(\mathbf{y},\frac{1}{r}\mathbf{x}\right)\cdot\mathbf{x}

and its inverse
\mathbf{f}(r,\mathbf{y},\mathbf{x})
with respect to
r
would show an additional dependence on the variable
\mathbf{y}
. But other than this point, everything else is just like the previous situation.

4.
The local, integral case in higher dimension

We would like to explore the situation in which we start out with a typical integral functional

(4.1)

I(\mathbf{u})=\int_{\Omega}W(\nabla\mathbf{u}({\bm{x}}))\,d{\bm{x}}

for a certain, continuous integrand

W(\mathbf{F}):\mathbb{R}^{m\times N}\to\mathbb{R},

and invariant class
\mathcal{D}_{0}
as in Definition
2.3
.
To begin with, we will assume that feasible maps

\mathbf{u}({\bm{x}}):\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{m},\quad N<m,

are smooth. Let
\Phi\in\mathcal{D}_{0}
. It is elementary to argue that

\int_{\Omega}W(\nabla\mathbf{u}_{\Phi}({\bm{y}}))\,d{\bm{y}}=\int_{\Omega}\det\nabla\Phi({\bm{x}})W\left(\frac{1}{\det\nabla\Phi({\bm{x}})}\nabla\mathbf{u}({\bm{x}})\operatorname{adj}\nabla\Phi({\bm{x}})^{T}\right)\,d{\bm{x}},

through the change of variables
{\bm{x}}=\Phi^{-1}({\bm{y}})
and the standard formula

\mathbf{X}^{-1}=\frac{1}{\det\mathbf{X}}\operatorname{adj}\mathbf{X}^{T},

valid for non-singular, square matrices,
and so

(4.2)

I_{i}({\bm{u}})=\inf_{\Phi\in\mathcal{D}_{0}}\int_{\Omega}\overline{W}(\nabla\Phi({\bm{x}}),\nabla\mathbf{u}({\bm{x}}))\,d{\bm{x}},

for

(4.3)

\overline{W}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{F})=\det\mathbf{X}\,W\left(\mathbf{F}\mathbf{X}^{-1}\right)=\det\mathbf{X}\,W\left(\frac{1}{\det\mathbf{X}}\mathbf{F}\operatorname{adj}\mathbf{X}^{T}\right).

In this way, we clearly see that computing
I_{i}(\mathbf{u})
amounts to solving a non-homogeneous, vector, variational problem of the same kind considered in hyper-elasticity (
[
3
]
) for an inhomogeneous integrand of the form

\tilde{W}({\bm{x}},\mathbf{X})=\overline{W}(\mathbf{X},\nabla\mathbf{u}({\bm{x}})),

under suitable boundary conditions depending on the invariant class
\mathcal{D}_{0}
considered.
Since variations of the form given in (
2.2
) are classic inner-variations for the functional
I
, it is natural to introduce the following (
[
13
]
).

Definition 4.1
.

Let

W(\mathbf{F}):\mathbb{R}^{m\times N}\to\mathbb{R}

be a continuous integrand.

(1)

W
is said to be inner-quasiaffine if the corresponding
\overline{W}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{F})
in (
4.3
) is quasi-affine in
\mathbf{X}
for every fixed
\mathbf{F}
.

(2)

W
is said to be inner-quasiconvex if

(4.4)

\overline{W}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{F})=\begin{cases}\det\mathbf{X}W(\mathbf{F}\mathbf{X}^{-1}),&\det\mathbf{X}>0,\\
+\infty,&\det\mathbf{X}\leq 0,\end{cases}

is quasiconvex in
\mathbf{X}
for every fixed
\mathbf{F}
.

(3)

W
is said to be inner-polyconvex if
\overline{W}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{F})
is polyconvex in
\mathbf{X}
for every fixed
\mathbf{F}
.

Notice that if elements of
\mathcal{D}_{0}
would be demanded to comply with the boundary condition
\Phi=\mathbf{id}
on
\partial\Omega
, then we could have defined
W
as inner-quasiconvex if
\overline{W}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{F})
is quasiconvex at
\mathbf{id}
in
\mathbf{X}
for every
\mathbf{F}
.

The following basic fact justifies, to some extent, the previous definition.

Proposition 4.1
.

Assume the integrand
W
for
I
in (
4.1
) is quasiconvex (in the usual, vector sense), respectively quasi-affine. Then
W
must be inner-quasiconvex, respectively inner-quasiaffine.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{F},\mathbf{X}
be given constant
m\times N
-, and
N\times N
-matrices, respectively, with
\det\mathbf{X}>0
. Let

\Phi({\bm{y}}):\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{N},\quad\det(\mathbf{X}+\nabla\Phi({\bm{y}}))>0\hbox{ a.e. }{\bm{y}}\in\Omega,

be a smooth, compactly-supported map . Then

{\bm{x}}={\bm{u}}^{-1}({\bm{y}})=\mathbf{X}{\bm{y}}+\Phi({\bm{y}})

is a valid change of variables by the well-known results on injectivity in
[
2
]
,
[
15
]
, or
[
3
]
, and the integral

\int_{\Omega}\overline{W}(\mathbf{X}+\nabla\Phi({\bm{y}}),\mathbf{F})\,d{\bm{y}}=\int_{\Omega}\det(X+\nabla\Phi({\bm{y}}))W\left(\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{X}+\nabla\Phi({\bm{y}}))^{-1}\right)\,d{\bm{y}}

is written as

\int_{\mathbf{X}(\Omega)}W(\mathbf{F}\nabla{\bm{u}}({\bm{x}}))\,d{\bm{x}}.

Because
\det\mathbf{X}>0
, the image domain
\mathbf{X}(\Omega)
is also a regular domain. On the other hand, the map

{\bm{U}}({\bm{x}})=\mathbf{F}{\bm{u}}({\bm{x}})

is Lipschitz and such that

\left.{\bm{U}}\right|_{\partial\Omega}=\left.\mathbf{F}\mathbf{X}^{-1}{\bm{x}}\right|_{\partial\Omega},

and, hence, by quasiconvexity,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{X}(\Omega)}W(\mathbf{F}\nabla{\bm{u}}({\bm{x}}))\,d{\bm{x}}=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{X}(\Omega)}W(\nabla{\bm{U}}({\bm{x}}))\,d{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle|\mathbf{X}(\Omega)|W(\mathbf{F}\mathbf{X}^{-1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\Omega|\det\mathbf{X}W(\mathbf{F}\mathbf{X}^{-1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|\Omega|\overline{W}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{F}).

Putting everything together, we see that

\int_{\Omega}\overline{W}(\mathbf{X}+\nabla\Phi({\bm{y}}),\mathbf{F})\,d{\bm{y}}\geq|\Omega|\overline{W}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{F}),

as desired.
∎

4.1.
Another form of invariance

As indicated, the variational problem (
4.2
) for each fixed
\mathbf{u}_{0}
may be difficult to deal with. Yet, despite this fact, the functional defined through that problem turns out to be invariant.

There is another simpler form to define an invariant density

W_{i}(\mathbf{F}):\mathbb{R}^{m\times N}\to\mathbb{R}

from a given

W(\mathbf{F}):\mathbb{R}^{m\times N}\to\mathbb{R},

by simply taking the pointwise minimum in the matrix
\mathbf{X}
, namely

(4.5)

W_{i}(\mathbf{F})=\inf_{\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}_{+}}\det\mathbf{X}\,W\left(\frac{1}{\det\mathbf{X}}\mathbf{F}\operatorname{adj}\mathbf{X}^{T}\right),

where we are putting

\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}_{+}=\{\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}:\det\mathbf{X}>0\}.

Consider the two functionals

(4.6)

I(\mathbf{u})=\int_{\Omega}W(\nabla\mathbf{u}({\bm{x}}))\,d{\bm{x}},\quad I_{i}^{*}(\mathbf{u})=\int_{\Omega}W_{i}(\nabla\mathbf{u}({\bm{x}}))\,d{\bm{x}}.

Proposition 4.2
.

This local integral functional
I_{i}^{*}
is invariant for every class
\mathcal{D}_{0}
in Definition
2.3
, and

I_{i}^{*}\leq I_{i}\leq I,

where
I_{i}
is given in (
4.2
).

The proof is elementary and does not require any further comment. What is, on the other hand, a remarkable issue is to decide under what circumstances the invariant hull of an integral functional (
4.6
) is given by
I_{i}^{*}
.

Conjecture 4.3
.

Suppose the functional
I
is of the typical integral form

I(u)=\int_{\Omega}W(\nabla\mathbf{u}({\bm{x}}))\,d{\bm{x}}

for a certain continuous integrand

W(\mathbf{F}):\mathbb{R}^{m\times N}\to\mathbb{R}.

If its invariant hull
I_{i}
with respect to some invariant class
\mathcal{D}_{0}
turns out to be another integral functional, then
I_{i}\equiv I_{i}^{*}

I_{i}(u)=\int_{\Omega}W_{i}(\nabla\mathbf{u}({\bm{x}}))\,d{\bm{x}},

where
W_{i}
is given in (
4.5
).

5.
A fundamental two-dimensional example

To start gaining some insight into high-dimensional examples, we would like to consider the particular initial situation in which

(5.1)

I(\mathbf{u})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla{\bm{u}}({\bm{x}})|^{2},

where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is a model domain (a ball or a box), and maps

{\bm{u}}({\bm{x}})=(u_{1}({\bm{x}}),u_{2}({\bm{x}}),u_{3}({\bm{x}})):\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{3}

belong to the Sobolev space
H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{3})
. Keep in mind that

\nabla\mathbf{u}=\begin{pmatrix}\nabla u_{1}\\
\nabla u_{2}\\
\nabla u_{3}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{u}_{,1}&\mathbf{u}_{,2}\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathbf{u}_{,1}=\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}}{\partial x},\mathbf{u}_{,2}=\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}}{\partial y},

and hence, we can recast our quadratic functional in (
5.1
) in the two alternative ways

I(\mathbf{u})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}(|\mathbf{u}_{,1}({\bm{x}})|^{2}+|\mathbf{u}_{,2}({\bm{x}})|^{2})\,d{\bm{x}}

or

I(\mathbf{u})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}(|\nabla u_{1}({\bm{x}})|^{2}+|\nabla u_{2}({\bm{x}})|^{2}+|\nabla u_{3}({\bm{x}})|^{2})\,d{\bm{x}}.

It is clear that
I
is not invariant as it is easy to check that it does not fit into the form (
1.1
). Hence,
we would like to calculate its invariant realization

I_{i}({\bm{u}})=\inf_{\Phi\in\mathcal{D}_{0}(\Omega)}I({\bm{u}}_{\Phi}),\quad{\bm{u}}_{\Phi}({\bm{x}})={\bm{u}}(\Phi^{-1}({\bm{x}})),

with respect to some suitable invariant class
\mathcal{D}_{0}
.
According to our earlier discussion, we need to face the vector variational problem

(5.2)

\hbox{Minimize in }\Phi\in\mathcal{D}_{0}(\Omega):\quad\int_{\Omega}\overline{W}(\nabla{\bm{u}}({\bm{x}}),\nabla\Phi({\bm{x}}))\,d{\bm{x}}

where

\overline{W}(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{X}):\mathbb{R}^{3\times 2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}\to\mathbb{R}

is given by

(5.3)

\overline{W}(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{X})=\det\mathbf{X}\,W(\mathbf{F}\mathbf{X}^{-1})=\frac{1}{2\det\mathbf{X}}|\mathbf{F}\operatorname{adj}\mathbf{X}^{T}|^{2},

and the map
{\bm{u}}({\bm{x}}):\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
is fixed.

It may be instructive to write things in a fully explicit form to facilitate some calculations. Our variational problem is

\hbox{Minimize in }\Phi\in\mathcal{D}_{0}:\quad\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{2\det\nabla\Phi({\bm{x}})}|\nabla{\bm{u}}({\bm{x}})\operatorname{adj}\nabla\Phi({\bm{x}})^{T}|^{2}\,d{\bm{x}}.

Note that

\nabla\Phi=\begin{pmatrix}\phi_{1,1}&\phi_{1,2}\\
\phi_{2,1}&\phi_{2,2}\end{pmatrix},\quad\operatorname{adj}\nabla\Phi^{T}=\begin{pmatrix}\phi_{2,2}&-\phi_{1,2}\\
-\phi_{2,1}&\phi_{1,1}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{Q}\nabla\phi_{2}&-\mathbf{Q}\nabla\phi_{1}\end{pmatrix}

if

\Phi({\bm{x}})=(\phi_{1}(x_{1},x_{2}),\phi_{2}(x_{1},x_{2})),\quad{\bm{x}}=(x_{1},x_{2}),

and

\mathbf{Q}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathbf{Q}^{T}=-\mathbf{Q},

is the counterclockwise,
\pi/2
-rotation in the plane.
We also have

\nabla{\bm{u}}({\bm{x}})=\begin{pmatrix}u_{1,1}&u_{1,2}\\
u_{2,1}&u_{2,2}\\
u_{3,1}&u_{3,2}\end{pmatrix},\quad{\bm{u}}({\bm{x}})=(u_{1}(x_{1},x_{2}),u_{2}(x_{1},x_{2}),u_{3}(x_{1},x_{2})).

The product occurring in functional
I({\bm{u}}_{\Phi})
becomes

\displaystyle\nabla{\bm{u}}({\bm{x}})\operatorname{adj}\nabla\Phi({\bm{x}})^{T}=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}u_{1,1}&u_{1,2}\\
u_{2,1}&u_{2,2}\\
u_{3,1}&u_{3,2}\end{pmatrix}\,\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{Q}\nabla\phi_{2}&-\mathbf{Q}\nabla\phi_{1}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\nabla u_{1}\cdot\mathbf{Q}\nabla\phi_{2}&-\nabla u_{1}\cdot\mathbf{Q}\nabla\phi_{1}\\
\nabla u_{2}\cdot\mathbf{Q}\nabla\phi_{2}&-\nabla u_{2}\cdot\mathbf{Q}\nabla\phi_{1}\\
\nabla u_{3}\cdot\mathbf{Q}\nabla\phi_{2}&-\nabla u_{3}\cdot\mathbf{Q}\nabla\phi_{1}\end{pmatrix}.

Hence

(5.4)

I({\bm{u}}_{\Phi})=\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{\nabla\phi_{1}\cdot\mathbf{Q}\nabla\phi_{2}}\sum_{1\leq i\leq 3,1\leq j\leq 2}\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{Q}\nabla u_{i}\cdot\nabla\phi_{j})^{2}\,d{\bm{x}},

and we write, for each fixed map
{\bm{u}}({\bm{x}}):\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
,

E_{\bm{u}}(\Phi)=I({\bm{u}}_{\Phi}).

If we put

(5.5)

\displaystyle\tilde{W}(\mathbf{U},\mathbf{X})=\frac{1}{\mathbf{X}_{1}\cdot\mathbf{Q}\mathbf{X}_{2}}\sum_{1\leq i\leq 3,1\leq j\leq 2}\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{U}_{i}\cdot\mathbf{X}_{j})^{2},

\displaystyle\mathbf{U}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{U}_{1}\\
\mathbf{U}_{2}\\
\mathbf{U}_{3}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 2},\mathbf{X}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{X}_{1}\\
\mathbf{X}_{2}\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2},

then

E_{\bm{u}}(\Phi)=\int_{\Omega}\tilde{W}(\nabla{\bm{u}}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{Q}^{T},\nabla\Phi({\bm{x}}))\,d{\bm{x}}.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\mathbf{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 2}
be given.

(1)

The integrands in (
5.3
) or in (
5.5

\mathbf{F}

\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}\mapsto\overline{W}(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{X}),\tilde{W}(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{X})

are polyconvex over the set

(2)

The absolute minimum of the function

takes place when the matrix

\operatorname{adj}\mathbf{X}\,\mathbf{F}^{T}\mathbf{F}\,\operatorname{adj}\mathbf{X}^{T}

is a multiple of the identity (of dimension

(5.6)

\overline{W}(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{X})=|\mathbf{F}_{1}\wedge\mathbf{F}_{2}|,\quad\mathbf{F}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{F}_{1}&\mathbf{F}_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

Due to homogeneity, the minimum is taken on for all such multiples.

Proof.

We comment on the integrand
\overline{W}
. The arguments for
\tilde{W}
are exactly the same.

The polyconvexity is elementary because the real function

w_{\mathbf{F}}(t,\mathbf{X}):\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{R}^{2\times 2}\to\mathbb{R},\quad w_{\mathbf{F}}(t,\mathbf{X})=\frac{1}{2t}|\mathbf{F}\mathbf{X}|^{2}

is convex in all of its arguments. Note that

\overline{W}(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{X})=w_{\mathbf{F}}(\det\mathbf{X},\operatorname{adj}\mathbf{X}^{T}).

Check also closely related calculations in
[
14
]
.
The homogeneity is straightforward. The second item is an interesting Multivariate Calculus exercise that can be facilitated by the explicit form in (
5.3

for suitable classes

of a piece of a

-manifold in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
. As before,
\Omega
is a model domain in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, and we can write

\nabla{\bm{u}}=\begin{pmatrix}\nabla u_{1}\\
\nabla u_{2}\\
\dots\\
\nabla u_{m}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{u}_{,1}&\mathbf{u}_{,2}&\dots&\mathbf{u}_{,N}\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathbf{u}_{,k}=\frac{\partial\mathbf{u}}{\partial x_{k}}.

In particular,

W^{N}(\mathbf{F})=\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{k=1}^{N}|\mathbf{F}_{k}|^{2}\right)^{N/2}=\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{l=1}^{m}|\mathbf{f}_{l}|^{2}\right)^{N/2}

if

\mathbf{F}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{F}_{1}&\mathbf{F}_{2}&\dots&\mathbf{F}_{N}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{f}_{1}\\
\mathbf{f}_{2}\\
\dots\\
\mathbf{f}_{m}\end{pmatrix}.

The integral

(6.1)

V_{N}({\bm{u}})=\int_{\Omega}\sqrt{\det(\nabla{\bm{u}}({\bm{x}})^{T}\nabla{\bm{u}}({\bm{x}}))}\,d{\bm{x}}

yields the
N
-dimensional volume of the image
{\bm{u}}(\Omega)
in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and, hence, it is definitely an invariant functional as we will check just below. A result like Theorem
5.2
is however not possible in the higher-dimensional case
N>2
. Recall that

(6.2)

\overline{W}^{N}(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{X})=\det\mathbf{X}\frac{1}{N^{N/2}}\left|\frac{1}{\det\mathbf{X}}\mathbf{F}\operatorname{adj}\mathbf{X}^{T}\right|^{N}=\frac{1}{N^{N/2}\det\mathbf{X}^{N-1}}\left|\mathbf{F}\operatorname{adj}\mathbf{X}^{T}\right|^{N}.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
\mathbf{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times N}
be given.

(1)

The integrand in (
6.2
)

\mathbf{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}\mapsto\overline{W}^{N}(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{X}),

is polyconvex over the set
\det\mathbf{F}>0
, and positively homogeneous of degree zero.

(2)

The absolute minimum of the function

\mathbf{X}\mapsto\overline{W}^{N}(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{X})

takes place when the matrix

\operatorname{adj}\mathbf{X}\,\mathbf{F}^{T}\mathbf{F}\operatorname{adj}\mathbf{X}^{T}

is a multiple of the identity (of dimension
N\times N
). In this case,

\overline{W}^{N}(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{X})=W^{N}_{i}(\mathbf{F}).

Due to homogeneity, the minimum is taken on for all such multiples.

Proof.

Except for a constant to be determined, we write

\overline{W}(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{X})=\det\mathbf{X}|\mathbf{F}\mathbf{X}^{-1}|^{N},

or even better for computations

\overline{W}(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{X})=\det\mathbf{X}|\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{X})|^{N},\quad\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{X})\mathbf{X}=\mathbf{F}.

Here
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{X})
and
\mathbf{F}
are
m\times N
-matrices, while
\mathbf{X}
is a non-singular
N\times N
-matrix. In particular, by differentiation,

\mathbf{D}\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{X})\mathbf{X}+\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{X})=\mathbf{0}.

From this equation, we find that

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{X})^{T}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{X})=-\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{X})^{T}\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{X})\mathbf{X}^{-1},

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{X})^{T}\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{X})=-\mathbf{X}^{-T}\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{X})^{T}\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{X}).

Since

\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{X})=\mathbf{F}\mathbf{X}^{-1}=\frac{1}{\det\mathbf{X}}\mathbf{F}\operatorname{adj}\mathbf{X}^{T},

we can write

(6.3)

\det\mathbf{X}^{3}\,\mathbf{D}\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{X})^{T}\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{X})=-\operatorname{adj}\mathbf{X}\operatorname{adj}\mathbf{X}\,\mathbf{F}^{T}\mathbf{F}\operatorname{adj}\mathbf{X}^{T}.

On the other hand,

\mathbf{D}_{\mathbf{X}}\overline{W}(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{X})=\operatorname{adj}\mathbf{X}|\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{X})|^{N}+N\det\mathbf{X}|\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{X})|^{N-2}\mathbf{D}\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{X})^{T}\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{X}),

and (
6.3
) carries us, for a critical matrix
\mathbf{X}
, to

\det\mathbf{X}^{2}|\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{X})|^{2}\operatorname{adj}\mathbf{X}-N|\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{X})|^{N-2}\operatorname{adj}\mathbf{X}\operatorname{adj}\mathbf{X}\,\mathbf{F}^{T}\mathbf{F}\operatorname{adj}\mathbf{X}^{T}=\mathbf{0}.

Since
\operatorname{adj}\mathbf{X}
is not singular, this is our claim. Indeed, if we take determinant in this last equation, we see that

(\det\mathbf{X})^{2N}|\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{X})|^{2N}=N^{N}(\det\mathbf{X})^{2(N-1)}\det(\mathbf{F}^{T}\mathbf{F}),

or

(\det\mathbf{X})^{2}|\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{X})|^{2N}=N^{N}\det(\mathbf{F}^{T}\mathbf{F}),

that is to say

\overline{W}(\mathbf{F},\mathbf{X})=N^{N/2}\sqrt{\det(\mathbf{F}^{T}\mathbf{F})}.

∎

From Proposition
4.2
, all we can conclude is the following.

Corollary 6.2
.

The integrand

V_{N}({\bm{u}})=\int_{\Omega}W_{i}^{N}(\nabla{\bm{u}}({\bm{x}}))\,d{\bm{x}}

given in (
6.1
),
is invariant for every invariant subclass
\mathcal{D}_{0}\subset\mathcal{D}
, and

V_{N}({\bm{u}})\leq I_{i}({\bm{u}})\leq I_{N}({\bm{u}}).

Concerning the possibility that
V_{N}=I_{i}
, it looks unlikely to be so given the classical Liouville theorem restricting the solutions of the equation

\nabla\Phi({\bm{x}})^{T}\nabla\Phi({\bm{x}})=\det(\nabla\Phi({\bm{x}}))^{2/N}\mathbf{G}({\bm{x}}),

for a suitable symmetric, matrix-valued mapping

\mathbf{G}({\bm{x}}):\Omega\to\mathbb{S}(N),\quad\det\mathbf{G}({\bm{x}})=1,

to Möbius transformations (
[
9
]
). Note that such a mapping
\mathbf{G}
complying with the previous equation comes directly from

\nabla{\bm{u}}({\bm{x}})^{T}\nabla{\bm{u}}({\bm{x}})=\mu({\bm{x}})(\det\nabla\Phi({\bm{x}}))^{2(N-1)}\nabla\Phi({\bm{x}})^{T}\nabla\Phi({\bm{x}}),

which is the basic functional equation in the proof of Lemma
6.1
.

Problem 6.3
.

For
N>2
, find an alternative density

\tilde{W}^{N}(\mathbf{F})\to\mathbb{R},\quad\mathbf{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times N},

positively homogeneous of degree
N
such that

I_{i}({\bm{u}})=V_{N}({\bm{u}})

for smooth mappings
{\bm{u}}:\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}\to\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

If Conjecture
4.3
turns out to be correct, all that is required is to design
\tilde{W}
in such a way that the corresponding invariant realization
I_{i}
is a local, integral functional.

We finish with another problem whose solution seems out of reach.

Problem 6.4
.

Consider the power integral functional

I_{p}({\bm{u}})=\int_{\Omega}|\nabla{\bm{u}}({\bm{x}})|^{p}\,d{\bm{x}},\quad p>0,

over some specified invariant class
\mathcal{D}_{0}\subset\mathcal{D}
. For what ranges of the exponent
p
, is the invariant hull of
I_{p}
trivial, or of non-local, integral form?
