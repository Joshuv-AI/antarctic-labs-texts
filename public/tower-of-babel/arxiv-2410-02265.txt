Tushar Karmakar, Saikat Maity, Bibekananda Maji

Abstract
. One of the main objectives of the current paper is to revisit the well known Laurent series expansions of the Riemann zeta function ζ ⁡ ( s ) \zeta(s) , Hurwitz zeta function ζ ⁡ ( s , a ) \zeta(s,a) and Dirichlet L L -function L ⁡ ( s , χ ) L(s,\chi) at s = 1 s=1 . Moreover, we also present a new Laurent series expansion of L L -functions associated to cusp forms over the full modular group.



1.
Introduction

Finding a Laurent series expansion of a meromorphic function has always been a question of curiosity for mathematicians. Riemann
[
2
]
, in his seminal paper, showed that the Riemann zeta function
\zeta(s)
can be analytically continued to the whole complex plane except at
s=1
. Moreover, at
s=1
,
\zeta(s)
has a simple pole with residue
1
.
In 1955, Briggs and Chowla
[
6
]
gave two different proofs for the Laurent series expansion of
\zeta(s)
at
s=1
. Mainly, they showed that

\displaystyle\zeta(s)=\frac{1}{s-1}+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}\gamma_{n}}{n!}(s-1)^{n},

where
\gamma_{0}=\gamma
is the well-known Euler–Mascheroni constant, and for
n\geq 1
,
\gamma_{n}
is defined by

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}=\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\left\{\sum_{\ell=1}^{N}\frac{\log^{n}(\ell)}{\ell}-\frac{\log^{n+1}(N)}{n+1}\right\}.

The Dirichlet
L
-function
L(s,\chi)
and Hurwitz zeta function
\zeta(s,a)
are two well-known generalizations of
\zeta(s)
. If
\chi
is not a principal character,
L(s,\chi)
can be analytically continued to the whole
\mathbb{C}
.
Like
\zeta(s)
,
\zeta(s,a)
can be analytically continued to
the whole complex plane except for a simple pole at
s=1
. In 1972, Berndt
[
7
]
derived the Laurent series expansion of
\zeta(s,a)
at
s=1
. In 1961, Briggs and Buschman
[
5
]
studied Laurent series of a general Dirichlet series having a simple pole. Readers are also encouraged to see
[
4
,
10
,
12
]
and references therein. In this paper, we present simple methods to prove the Laurent series expansion of
\zeta(s)
,
L(s,\chi)
and
\zeta(s,a)
. Furthermore, we obtain a new Laurent series expansion of
L
-functions associated to cusp forms over the full modular group.

Let
k
be an even positive integer and
\mathbb{H}
denote the upper half plane. Let
S_{k}(SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z}))
denote the space of cusp form of weight
k
over the full modular group
SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. Let
f(z)\in S_{k}(SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z}))
be a normalized Hecke eigenform with the Fourier series expansion,

\displaystyle f(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{f}(n)\exp(2\pi inz),\hskip 11.38092pt\forall z\in\mathbb{H}.

(1.1)

The
L
-function associated to the cusp form
f
is defined as

L(f,s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{f}(n)}{n^{s}}.

This
L
-function is absolutely convergent for
\operatorname{Re}(s)>\frac{k+1}{2}
as we know from
Delinge’s bound
a_{f}(n)=O(n^{\frac{(k-1)}{2}}+\epsilon)
for any
\epsilon>0
.
Hecke proved that
L(f,s)
can be analytically continued to an entire function and it
satisfies following functional equation:

\displaystyle(2\pi)^{-s}\Gamma(s)L(f,s)=i^{k}(2\pi)^{-(k-s)}\Gamma(k-s)L(f,k-s).

(1.2)

This functional equation is equivalent to the following transformation formula, for
\operatorname{Re}(y)>0
,

\displaystyle y^{k}W(y)=W(1/y),

(1.3)

where

\displaystyle W(y)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{f}(n)\exp(-2\pi ny).

Readers can see
[
3
,
8
]
to know more about different identities equivalent to the functional equation (
1.2
).
One of the main aims of this paper is to derive a Laurent series expansion of
L(f,s)
at
s=0
.

Now we define the upper incomplete gamma function
\Gamma(s,a)
, for
a>0
,
s\in\mathbb{C}
, as follows:

\displaystyle\Gamma(s,a)=\int_{a}^{\infty}e^{-y}y^{s-1}dy.

(1.4)

As
a\rightarrow\infty
, we have the following asymptotic expansion
[
11
, p. 179, Equation (8.11.2)]
:

\displaystyle\Gamma(s,a)\sim a^{s-1}e^{-a}.

(1.5)

More generally, for
a>0,\ell\geq 0,s\in\mathbb{C}
, we define the following one variable generalization of the above incomplete gamma function:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\ell}(s,a):=\int_{a}^{\infty}e^{-y}\log^{\ell}(y)y^{s-1}dy.

(1.6)

For
a\rightarrow\infty
, we have the following asymptotic expansion:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\ell}(s,a)\sim a^{s-1}\log^{\ell}(a)e^{-a}.

(1.7)

Now we are ready to state main results of this paper.

2.
Main Results

First, we will present simple proofs of the Laurent series expansions of
\zeta(s),\zeta(s,a)
and
L(s,\chi)
.

Theorem 2.1
.

The Laurent series of the Riemann zeta function
\zeta(s)
at its pole
s=1
is given by

\zeta(s)=\frac{1}{s-1}+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\gamma_{k}(s-1)^{k},

where

\displaystyle\gamma_{k}=\lim_{m\to\infty}\left({\sum_{i=1}^{m}{\frac{\log^{k}(i)}{i}}}-\frac{\log^{k+1}(m)}{k+1}\right).

(2.1)

Theorem 2.2
.

The Laurent series of the Hurwitz zeta function
\zeta(s,a)
at its pole
s=1
is given by

\zeta(s,a)=\frac{1}{s-1}+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\gamma_{k}(a)(s-1)^{k},

where

\gamma_{k}(a)=\lim_{m\to\infty}\left\{\sum_{i=0}^{m}\frac{(\log(i+a))^{k}}{(i+a)}-\frac{(\log(m+a))^{k+1}}{k+1}\right\}.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
\chi
be a non-principal Dirichlet character modulo
q
. The Laurent series of the Dirichlet
L
-function
L(s,\chi)
at
s=1
is given by

L(s,\chi)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\gamma_{k}(\chi)(s-1)^{k},

where

\gamma_{k}(\chi)=\sum_{a=1}^{q}\chi(a)\gamma_{k}(a,q),

where

\displaystyle\gamma_{k}(a,q)=\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leq x,\\
n\equiv a\pmod{q}\end{subarray}}\left(\frac{\log^{k}(n)}{n}-\frac{\log^{k+1}(x)}{q(k+1)}\right).

(2.2)

The next result gives a Laurent series expansion of
L(f,s)
at
s=0
.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
f(z)
be a cusp form of weight
k
over
SL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
with the Fourier series expansion (
1.1
).
Then the L-function
L(f,s)
has the following Laurent series expansion at
s=0
,

\displaystyle L(f,s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}C(n,k)s^{n},

where the first two coefficients are given by

\displaystyle C(1,k)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{f}(n)\Gamma(k,2n\pi)}{(2n\pi)^{k}}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{f}(n)\Gamma(0,2n\pi),

(2.3)

\displaystyle C(2,k)

\displaystyle=C(1,k)\left(\gamma+\log(2\pi)\right)-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{f}(n)\left(\frac{\Gamma_{1}(k,2n\pi)-\log(2n\pi)\Gamma(k,2n\pi)}{(2n\pi)^{k}}\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{f}(n)\left(\Gamma_{1}(0,2n\pi)-\log(2n\pi)\Gamma(0,2n\pi)\right),

(2.4)

where incomplete gamma functions
\Gamma(k,2n\pi)
and
\Gamma_{1}(k,2n\pi)
are defined as in (
1.4
) and (
1.6
), respectively.

Remark 1
.

Note that the coefficient
C(1,k)
is nothing but the first derivatives of the
L
-function
L(f,s)
at
s=0
. Therefore, the above theorem gives us a new way to evaluate the derivative of
L(f,s)
at
s=0
. The series present in (
2.3
)-(
2.4
) are rapidly convergent.

3.
Preliminaries

In this section, we mention a few well known results that will be useful to obtain our results.

Lemma 3.1
.

[
1
, p. 56]

For
\operatorname{Re}(s)>0
,
\zeta(s)
satisfies the following integral representation,

\zeta(s)=\frac{s}{s-1}-s\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{x-[x]}{x^{s+1}}dx.

Lemma 3.2
.

[
1
, p. 269, Theorem 12.21]

For any non negative integer
N
and
\operatorname{Re}(s)>0
,

\zeta(s,a)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{1}{(n+a)^{s}}+\frac{(N+a)^{1-s}}{s-1}-s\int_{N}^{\infty}\frac{x-[x]}{(x+a)^{s+1}}dx.

Next, we state an important result which will play crucial role in obtaining the Laurent series expansion of
L(s,\chi)
at
s=1
.

Lemma 3.3
.

[
10
, Proposition 3.2]

Let
g(n)
be a periodic arithmetical function with period
q>0
. Then the sum

\sum_{a=1}^{q}g(a)=0

if and only if the following series

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{g(n)\log^{k}(n)}{n}

is convergent and has the sum

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{g(n)\log^{k}(n)}{n}=\sum_{a=1}^{q}g(a)\gamma_{k}(a,q),

where
\gamma_{k}(a,q)
is defined as in (
2.2
).

To know more about the above generalized Euler constant
\gamma_{k}(a,q)
, readers can see an interesting paper of Dilcher
[
9
]
.

In the coming section, we present proof of our main results.

4.
Proof of Main Results

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

From Lemma
3.1
, we know that

\zeta(s)=\frac{s}{s-1}-s\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{x-[x]}{x^{s+1}}dx,\hskip 11.38092pt\operatorname{Re}(s)>0.

(4.1)

Now form the above equation it is clear that
\zeta(s)
has a simple pole at
s=1
with residue 1. Therefore, it has a Laurent series expansion at
s=1
. Let us suppose that

\zeta(s)=\frac{1}{s-1}+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{A_{k}(s-1)^{k}}

(4.2)

Now our claim is that,

A_{k}=\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}{\gamma_{k}},

where
\gamma_{k}
is defined as in (
2.1
).
One can rewrite (
4.1
) as follows:

\zeta(s)-\frac{1}{s-1}-1=h(s),

where

h(s)=s\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{[x]-x}{x^{s+1}}dx,

is analytic for
\operatorname{Re}(s)>0
. Hence, it will have a power series expansion around
s=1
with
k
-th coefficient,
B_{k}=\frac{h^{k}(1)}{k!}
, that is,

h(s)=s\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{[x]-x}{x^{s+1}}dx=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{B_{k}(s-1)^{k}}.

(4.3)

Now, by comparing the coefficients of (
4.2
) and (
4.3
), we get
B_{0}=A_{0}-1
and
B_{k}=A_{k}
, for all
k
. Therefore, our work is to finding the coefficients,
B_{k}
i.e. the
k
-th derivative of
h(s)
at
s=1
. Let us simplify
h(s)
as follows:

\displaystyle h(s)

\displaystyle=s\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{[x]-x}{x^{s+1}}dx

\displaystyle=s\lim_{r\to\infty}\int_{1}^{r}\frac{[x]-x}{x^{s+1}}dx

\displaystyle=s\lim_{r\to\infty}\Bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}\int_{i}^{i+1}\frac{i}{x^{s+1}}dx-\int_{1}^{r}\frac{1}{x^{s}}dx\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\lim_{r\to\infty}\Bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}\Big(\frac{i}{i^{s}}-\frac{i}{(i+1)^{s}}\Big)+\frac{s(r^{1-s}-1)}{s-1}\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\lim_{r\to\infty}\Bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}\frac{1}{i^{s}}-\frac{r-1}{r^{s}}+\frac{\Big((s-1)+1\Big)(r^{1-s}-1)}{s-1}\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\lim_{r\to\infty}\Bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}\frac{1}{i^{s}}+\frac{1}{r^{s}}-1+\frac{r^{1-s}}{s-1}-\frac{1}{s-1}\Bigg].

Further, one can write

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{s-1}+\frac{r^{1-s}}{s-1}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{s-1}+\frac{\exp(-(s-1)\log r)}{s-1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{l}(s-1)^{l-1}}{l!}\log^{l}(r).

Utilizing this expression, we obtain

h(s)=\lim_{r\to\infty}\Bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{r}\frac{1}{i^{s}}-1+\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{l}\frac{(s-1)^{l-1}\log^{l}r}{l!}\Bigg].

Now differentiating
k
-times at
s=1
, one can see that

\displaystyle h^{(k)}(1)

\displaystyle=\lim_{r\to\infty}\Bigg[(-1)^{k}\sum_{i=1}^{r}\frac{\log^{k}i}{i}+(-1)^{k+1}\frac{k!\log^{k+1}r}{(k+1)!}\Bigg]

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}\lim_{r\to\infty}\Bigg[\sum_{i=1}^{r}\frac{\log^{k}i}{i}-\frac{\log^{k+1}r}{k+1}\Bigg].

Therefore, from the definition (
2.1
) of
\gamma_{k}
, it follows that
B_{k}k!=(-1)^{k}\gamma_{k}.
This completes the proof of Theorem
2.1
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.2
.

From Lemma
3.2
, it is evident that
\zeta(s,a)
has a simple pole at
s=1
with residue
1
. Let us suppose we write

\zeta(s,a)=\frac{1}{s-1}+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}B_{k}(s-1)^{k},

(4.4)

then our claim is that

B_{k}=\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}\gamma_{k}(a),

where

\gamma_{k}(a)=\lim_{p\to\infty}\left\{\sum_{i=0}^{p}\frac{(\log(i+a))^{k}}{(i+a)}-\frac{(\log(p+a))^{k+1}}{k+1}\right\}.

(4.5)

Let us consider, for
N\in\mathbb{N}
,
0<a\leq 1
,
\operatorname{Re}(s)>0
, an analytic function

H_{N}(s,a)=s\int_{N}^{\infty}\frac{[x]-x}{(x+a)^{s+1}}dx.

Then we can write

\displaystyle H_{N}(s,a)

\displaystyle=s\int_{N}^{\infty}\frac{[x]-x}{(x+a)^{s+1}}dx

\displaystyle=s\lim_{p\to\infty}\left[\int_{N}^{p}\frac{[x]}{(x+a)^{s+1}}dx-\int_{N}^{p}\frac{x}{(x+a)^{s+1}}dx\right]

\displaystyle=s\lim_{p\to\infty}\left[\sum_{i=N}^{p-1}\int_{i}^{i+1}\frac{i}{(x+a)^{s+1}}dx-\int_{N}^{p}\frac{(x+a)-a}{(x+a)^{s+1}}dx\right]

\displaystyle=s\lim_{p\to\infty}\left[\sum_{i=N}^{p-1}\int_{i}^{i+1}\frac{i}{(x+a)^{s+1}}dx-\int_{N}^{p}\frac{1}{(x+a)^{s}}dx+a\int_{N}^{p}\frac{1}{(x+a)^{s+1}}dx\right]

\displaystyle=\lim_{p\to\infty}\Bigg[\sum_{i=N}^{p-1}\left(\frac{i}{(i+a)^{s}}-\frac{i}{(i+1+a)^{s}}\right)+\frac{s}{s-1}\left(\frac{1}{(p+a)^{s-1}}-\frac{1}{(N+a)^{s-1}}\right)

\displaystyle\hskip 182.09746pt-a\left(\frac{1}{(p+a)^{s}}-\frac{1}{(N+a)^{s}}\right)\Bigg]

\displaystyle:=\lim_{p\to\infty}I_{1}(p)+I_{2}(p),

where

\displaystyle I_{1}(p)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=N}^{p-1}\left(\frac{i}{(i+a)^{s}}-\frac{i}{(i+1+a)^{s}}\right)=\sum_{i=N}^{p-1}\frac{1}{(i+1+a)^{s}}+\frac{N}{(N+a)^{s}}-\frac{p}{(p+a)^{s}},

\displaystyle I_{2}(p)

\displaystyle=\frac{s}{s-1}\left(\frac{1}{(p+a)^{s-1}}-\frac{1}{(N+a)^{s-1}}\right)-a\left(\frac{1}{(p+a)^{s}}-\frac{1}{(N+a)^{s}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{(s-1)+1}{s-1}\left(\frac{1}{(p+a)^{s-1}}-\frac{1}{(N+a)^{s-1}}\right)-a\left(\frac{1}{(p+a)^{s}}-\frac{1}{(N+a)^{s}}\right).

Now substituting the above expressions of
I_{1}(p)
and
I_{2}(p)
and after simplifying a bit, we get the following expression of
H_{N}(s,a)
,

\displaystyle H_{N}(s,a)

\displaystyle=\lim_{p\to\infty}\left[\sum_{i=N}^{p-1}\frac{1}{(i+1+a)^{s}}+\frac{1}{s-1}\left(\frac{1}{(p+a)^{s-1}}-\frac{1}{(N+a)^{s-1}}\right)\right]

Using the above expression of
H_{N}(s,a)
in Lemma
3.2
, we arrive

\zeta(s,a)=\sum_{n=0}^{N}\frac{1}{(n+a)^{s}}+\frac{(N+a)^{1-s}}{s-1}+\lim_{p\to\infty}\left[\sum_{i=N}^{p-1}\frac{1}{(i+1+a)^{s}}+\frac{1}{s-1}\left(\frac{1}{(p+a)^{s-1}}-\frac{1}{(N+a)^{s-1}}\right)\right].

Upon simplification, one can see that

\zeta(s,a)=\lim_{p\to\infty}\left[\sum_{i=0}^{p}\frac{1}{(i+a)^{s}}+\frac{1}{(s-1)}\frac{1}{(p+a)^{s-1}}\right].

Now we write

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(s-1)}(p+a)^{1-s}

\displaystyle=\exp(-(s-1)\log(p+a))

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{m}}{m!}(s-1)^{m-1}(\log(p+a))^{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{s-1}+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{m}}{m!}(s-1)^{m-1}(\log(p+a))^{m}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\zeta(s,a)

\displaystyle=\lim_{p\to\infty}\left[\sum_{i=0}^{p}\frac{1}{(i+a)^{s}}+\frac{1}{s-1}+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{m}}{m!}(s-1)^{m-1}(\log(p+a))^{m}\right]

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\zeta(s,a)-\frac{1}{s-1}

\displaystyle=\lim_{p\to\infty}\left[\sum_{i=0}^{p}\frac{1}{(i+a)^{s}}+\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{m}}{m!}(s-1)^{m-1}(\log(p+a))^{m}\right]

\displaystyle:=F(s).

(4.6)

Note that the function
F(s)
is analytic at
s=1
, so it will have a power series expansion around
s=1
. From (
4.4
), we have

F(s)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}B_{k}(s-1)^{k},

where the
k
-th coefficient
B_{k}
is
\frac{F^{(k)}(1)}{k!}
.
Now using the definition (
4.6
) of
F(s)
and differentiating
k
-times at
s=1
, we see that

\displaystyle F^{k}(1)

\displaystyle=\lim_{p\to\infty}\left[\sum_{i=0}^{p}\frac{(-1)^{k}(\log(i+a))^{k}}{(i+a)}+\frac{(-1)^{k+1}}{(k+1)}\ (\log(p+a))^{k+1}\right]

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}\lim_{p\to\infty}\left[\sum_{i=0}^{p}\frac{(\log(i+a))^{k}}{(i+a)}-\frac{(\log(p+a))^{k+1}}{k+1}\right].

Finally, using the definition (
4.5
) of
\gamma_{k}(a)
, it is clear that
k!B_{k}=(-1)^{k}\gamma_{k}(a)
.
Thus, it completes the proof of our claim. ∎

Proof of Theorem
2.3
.

It is well known that
L(s,\chi)
can be analytically continued to the whole complex plane as we are dealing with non-principal Dirichlet character
\chi
. Thus, we will have the following Laurent series expansion at
s=1
:

\displaystyle L(s,\chi)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}L^{(k)}(1,\chi)\frac{(s-1)^{k}}{k!}.

(4.7)

Let us define
A(x)=\sum_{n\leq x}\chi(n)
. Using Abel’s summation formula, for
\operatorname{Re}(s)>0
, one can show that
L(s,\chi)
satisfies the following integral representation:

\displaystyle L(s,\chi)

\displaystyle=s\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{A(x)}{x^{s+1}}dx

\displaystyle=((s-1)+1)\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{A(x)}{x^{2}}x^{-s+1}dx

\displaystyle=((s-1)+1)\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{A(x)}{x^{2}}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{m}(\log x)^{m}}{m!}(s-1)^{m}dx

\displaystyle=\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{A(x)}{x^{2}}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{m}(\log x)^{m}}{m!}(s-1)^{m+1}dx

\displaystyle+\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{A(x)}{x^{2}}\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{m}(\log x)^{m}}{m!}(s-1)^{m}dx.

From the above expression, one can check that

\displaystyle L^{(k)}(1,\chi)

\displaystyle=\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{A(x)}{x^{2}}\frac{(-1)^{k-1}}{(k-1)!}k!(\log x)^{k-1}dx+\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{A(x)}{x^{2}}\frac{(-1)^{k}}{k!}k!(\log x)^{k}dx

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}\Bigg[\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{A(x)}{x^{2}}(\log x)^{k}dx-k\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{A(x)}{x^{2}}(\log x)^{k-1}dx\Bigg]

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}\lim_{r\to\infty}\Bigg[\int_{1}^{r}\frac{A(x)}{x^{2}}\Big((\log x)^{k}-k(\log x)^{k-1}\Big)dx\Bigg].

(4.8)

Using Abel’s summation formula, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{1<n\leq x}\chi(n)\frac{\log^{k}(n)}{n}

\displaystyle=A(x)\frac{\log^{k}(x)}{x}-A(1)f(1)-\int_{1}^{x}A(t)f^{\prime}(t)dt

\displaystyle=A(x)\frac{\log^{k}x}{x}+\int_{1}^{x}A(t)\Big(\frac{\log^{k}t-k\log^{k-1}t}{t^{2}}\Big)dt.

Since
|A(x)|<\phi(q)
, letting
x\rightarrow\infty
in the above expression, we see that

\displaystyle\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{1<n\leq x}\chi(n)\frac{\log^{k}(n)}{n}

\displaystyle=\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}\int_{1}^{x}A(t)\Big(\frac{\log^{k}t-k\log^{k-1}t}{t^{2}}\Big)dt.

(4.9)

Thus, from (
4.8
) and (
4.9
), it is evident that

\displaystyle L^{(k)}(1,\chi)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}\lim_{x\to\infty}\sum_{n\leq x}\chi(n)\frac{\log^{k}(n)}{n}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\chi(n)\frac{\log^{k}(n)}{n}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{k}\sum_{a=1}^{q}\chi(a)\gamma_{k}(a,q),

(4.10)

here in the final step we used Lemma
3.3
since
\chi
is a non-principal character of modulus
q
and
\sum_{a=1}^{q}\chi(a)=0
holds. Finally, substituting (
4.10
) in (
4.7
), we complete the proof of Theorem
2.3
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.4
.

Using the definition of the gamma function and
L(f,s)
, for
\operatorname{Re}(s)>\frac{k+1}{2}
, one can show that

\displaystyle(2\pi)^{-s}\Gamma(s)L(f,s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}W(y)y^{s-1}dy,

(4.11)

where

\displaystyle W(y)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{f}(n)\exp(-2\pi ny).

(4.12)

Now we divide the integral (
4.11
) in two parts, mainly, we write as follows:

\displaystyle(2\pi)^{-s}\Gamma(s)L(f,s)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}W(y)y^{s-1}dy+\int_{1}^{\infty}W(y)y^{s-1}dy

\displaystyle:=I_{1}(y,s)+I_{2}(y,s).

(4.13)

Note that
I_{2}(y,s)
is an entire function in the variable
s
as it can be shown that
W(y)=O\left(e^{-2\pi y}\right)
as
y\rightarrow\infty
. Now to simplify
I_{1}(y,s)
, we use transformation formula (
1.3
) for
W(y)
. Thus, using (
1.3
), we see that

\displaystyle I_{1}(y,s)=\int_{0}^{1}W(1/y)y^{s-1-k}dy.

Changing the variable
y\rightarrow 1/y
in the above integration, it yields that

\displaystyle I_{1}(y,s)=\int_{1}^{\infty}W(y)y^{k-s-1}dy.

(4.14)

Now one can clearly show that
I_{1}(y,s)
is entire in
s
.
Substituting (
4.14
) in (
4.13
), we obtain

\displaystyle(2\pi)^{-s}\Gamma(s)L(f,s)=\int_{1}^{\infty}W(y)\left(y^{k-1-s}+y^{s-1}\right)dy.

(4.15)

The above right side integral is an entire function in the variable
s
. Thus, it gives an analytic continuation of
L(f,s)
to the whole complex plane.
Now one can write

\displaystyle y^{k-1-s}+y^{s-1}=\frac{1}{y}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left\{y^{k}(-1)^{n}\log^{n}(y)+\log^{n}(y)\right\}\frac{s^{n}}{n!}.

(4.16)

This power series converges absolutely for any complex
s
. Therefore, substituting (
4.16
) in (
4.15
) and interchanging summation and integration, we derive that

\displaystyle(2\pi)^{-s}\Gamma(s)L(f,s)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}A(n,k)\frac{s^{n}}{n!},

(4.17)

where

\displaystyle A(n,k)=\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{W(y)}{y}\left\{y^{k}(-1)^{n}\log^{n}(y)+\log^{n}(y)\right\}dy.

(4.18)

Now let us suppose that

\displaystyle\frac{(2\pi)^{s}}{\Gamma(s)}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}B(n)\frac{s^{n}}{n!}.

(4.19)

To calculate
B(n)
, we can use the fact that

1/\Gamma(s)=s+\gamma s^{2}+\left(\frac{\gamma^{2}}{2}-\frac{\pi^{2}}{12}\right)s^{3}+\cdots.

One can easily check that
B(1)=1,B(2)=2(\gamma+\log(2\pi))
. Finally, substituting (
4.19
) in (
4.17
), we obtain

\displaystyle L(f,s)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}C(n,k)s^{n},

where

\displaystyle C(n,k)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq i,1\leq j,\\
i+j=n\end{subarray}}\frac{A(i,k)}{i!}\frac{B(j)}{j!}.

(4.20)

First, let us try to find the coefficient of
s
in the above Laurent series expansion of
L(f,s)
at
s=0
. Note that
C(1,k)=A(0,k)B(1)
. Thus,
C(1,k)=A(0,k)
as
B(1)=1
.
Now we shall try to simplify
A(0,k)
. From (
4.18
), we have

\displaystyle A(0,k)=\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{W(y)}{y}\left\{y^{k}+1\right\}dy.

From the definition (
4.12
) of
W(y)
, one can easily check that the series is absolutely and uniformly convergent in any compact subset of
\operatorname{Re}(y)>0
. Thus, using (
4.12
) and interchanging summation and integration, we arrive

\displaystyle A(0,k)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{f}(n)\left(\int_{1}^{\infty}e^{-2\pi ny}y^{k}\frac{dy}{y}+\int_{1}^{\infty}e^{-2\pi ny}\frac{dy}{y}\right).

Now we change the variable
y
by
y/2n\pi
to see that

\displaystyle A(0,k)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{f}(n)\left(\int_{2n\pi}^{\infty}e^{-y}y^{k-1}\frac{dy}{(2n\pi)^{k}}+\int_{2n\pi}^{\infty}e^{-y}y^{-1}dy\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{f}(n)}{(2n\pi)^{k}}\left(\Gamma(k,2n\pi)+(2n\pi)^{k}\Gamma(0,2n\pi)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{a_{f}(n)\Gamma(k,2n\pi)}{(2n\pi)^{k}}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{f}(n)\Gamma(0,2n\pi).

(4.21)

In the penultimate step, we used the definition of the incomplete gamma function (
1.4
). Using the asymptotic (
1.5
) of the incomplete gamma function, one can check that both of the above infinite series are convergent. Now we shall try to evaluate
C(2,k)
. From (
4.20
), it is clear that

\displaystyle C(2,k)

\displaystyle=A(0,k)\frac{B(2)}{2!}+A(1,k)B(1)

\displaystyle=A(0,k)(\gamma+\log(2\pi))+A(1,k).

(4.22)

We have already calculated
A(0,k)
in (
4.21
). Thus, we need to calculate only
A(1,k)
. From the definition (
4.18
) of
A(n,k)
, we know that

\displaystyle A(1,k)=\int_{1}^{\infty}\frac{W(y)}{y}\left\{-y^{k}\log(y)+\log(y)\right\}dy.

Employing the definition (
4.12
) of
W(y)
, one can see that

\displaystyle A(1,k)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{f}(n)\left(-\int_{1}^{\infty}e^{-2\pi ny}y^{k}\log(y)\frac{dy}{y}+\int_{1}^{\infty}e^{-2\pi ny}\log(y)\frac{dy}{y}\right).

Again, changing the variable variable
y
by
y/2n\pi
, it yields that

\displaystyle A(1,k)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{f}(n)

\displaystyle\Bigg(\int_{2n\pi}^{\infty}e^{-y}y^{k-1}\{-\log(y)+\log(2n\pi)\}\frac{dy}{(2n\pi)^{k}}

\displaystyle+\int_{2n\pi}^{\infty}e^{-y}\{\log(y)-\log(2n\pi)\}dy\Bigg).

(4.23)

Now using the definition (
1.6
) of the incomplete gamma function, we see that

\displaystyle A(1,k)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{f}(n)\left(\frac{-\Gamma_{1}(k,2n\pi)+\log(2n\pi)\Gamma(k,2n\pi)}{(2n\pi)^{k}}\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_{f}(n)\left(\Gamma_{1}(0,2n\pi)-\log(2n\pi)\Gamma(0,2n\pi)\right).

(4.24)

Using the asymptotic expansions (
1.5
), (
1.7
) of
\Gamma(s,a),\Gamma_{1}(s,a)
, one can easily show that the series present in (
4.23
) are absolutely convergent. Finally, substituting (
4.24
) in (
4.22
), we get the final expression for
C(2,k)
.
In a similar fashion, one can calculate
C(n,k)
for
n\geq 3
.
This finishes the proof of Theorem
2.4
.
∎
