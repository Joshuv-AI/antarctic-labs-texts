Healthcare Resource Utilization and the Direct Medical Cost of Fibromyalgia Patients in a Tertiary Hospital in Saudi Arabia
Authors: Shiekha S AlAujan, Saja H AlMazrou, Haya A AlNahedh, Lama A AlFalih, Abdulhadi M AlQahtani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeFibromyalgia (FM) is a chronic disorder characterized by widespread pain and multiple comorbidities and imposes a substantial economic burden on healthcare systems worldwide. Although extensively studied in Western countries, the economic impact of FM in Saudi Arabia remains unexplored. This study aimed to estimate healthcare resource utilization and the associated direct medical costs related to FM in a tertiary hospital in Saudi Arabia.Patients and MethodsA retrospective study was conducted using a bottom-up approach to assess the cost of illness associated with FM from a healthcare perspective. Analyzed data were extracted from February 2021 to February 2022 from the medical records of adult patients with FM who visited a tertiary healthcare hospital. Unit costs for each healthcare resource used in 2022 Saudi riyals (SAR) were obtained from the hospital’s billing system. The primary cost items were converted to US dollars (USD).ResultsData were extracted from 128 patients. They were predominantly female (93%) with a mean age of 47.1 years. Most patients were either overweight (43.4%) or obese (41.6%), and 56.3% had three or more comorbidities. Rheumatology was the most frequently accessed specialty (52.3%). Antidepressants were the most prescribed medications (77.3%). The average annual direct medical cost per patient was SAR 9,601.2 (USD 2,560.3), with healthcare team specialist visits and diagnostic procedures constituting 50.7% and 45.6% of the total costs, respectively. Laboratory tests imposed the highest financial burden (mean cost per patient: SAR 3,268.5 ; USD 871.6), whereas medications contributed only 3.7% of the total costs.ConclusionFM places a substantial burden on the Saudi healthcare system. Although the annual cost falls within the average global range, healthcare team visits followed by diagnostic procedures are the main drivers of FM costs. These findings provide essential knowledge for healthcare planning and resource allocation by policymakers aiming to reduce the costs associated with FM in Saudi Arabia.Keywords: fibromyalgia, cost-of-illness, disease burden, economic impact, Saudi Arabia

Introduction

Fibromyalgia (FM) is a chronic musculoskeletal syndrome characterized by widespread pain and a wide range of symptoms, including weakness, muscle and joint aches, stiffness, fatigue, and psychological stress.1 It affects 2–4% of the global population1 and 13.4% of the Saudi population,2 with predominantly women (60–90%) being affected.3 FM is a complex disease, and its etiology is not sufficiently understood.4 FM is challenging to diagnose, which is one of several factors contributing to its high economic burden.5 Management to potentially alleviate symptoms requires a resource-intensive multidisciplinary treatment approach.4,6,7 Uncontrolled FM symptoms lead to low quality of life and high rates of suicidal ideation, attempts, and deaths.8–10 Moreover, FM is accompanied by several psychological and physical conditions that can complicate disease management and contribute to the inefficient utilization of healthcare resources.3

The economic burden of FM has been extensively studied in Western countries.11 A systematic review by D’Onghia et al11 identified 36 cost-of-illness (COI) studies on FM in North America (n = 22) and Europe (n = 10), as well as parts of Asia (n = 4). They found that, in 2019 USD, the total annual direct costs per FM patient ranged from USD 1,250 to USD 35,920. FM COI shows wide variation and, moreover, COI research from Saudi Arabia or the broader Middle Eastern region is lacking.

The Saudi healthcare system is markedly different from those in the countries where most FM economic studies have been conducted. With its unique combination of public and private healthcare sectors, distinctive medication pricing structures, and culturally specific healthcare utilization patterns, international cost estimates may not be directly applicable in the Saudi Arabian context12 Moreover, the wide international variation in the economic burden of the disease noted in the review by D’Onghia et al11 underpins the need to conduct a COI study to understand the economic burden of FM, support decision-makers in prioritizing healthcare resources, and design effective interventions. Therefore, this study aimed to estimate the direct medical costs (DMC) of FM in the Saudi population.

Methodology

Study Design, Setting, and Ethical Approval

This retrospective study employed a bottom-up approach to estimate the economic burden of FM at a governmental funded tertiary hospital in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia. The COI analysis followed the standard methodology of Segel,13 with costs assessed from a healthcare perspective, focusing on the DMC of the FM. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of King Fahad Medical City (KFMC Ref No: 22–108). Data were extracted by a data analyst from KFMC and provided anonymized to the research team. Therefore, patient consent to review their medical records was not required by the Institutional Review Board of KFMC. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study Participants and Data Collection Period

This study included patients aged 18 years and above who were diagnosed with FM and identified through the hospital’s medical records system. Data were extracted between February 2021 and February 2022. Patients younger than 18 years and those with an unconfirmed FM diagnosis were excluded.

Data Collection Procedures

The primary outcome was the DMC of FM. Relevant data, encompassing procedures and medications, were obtained from patient medical records and the hospital’s billing system. Demographic variables included age, gender, marital status, body mass index (BMI), and number and type of comorbidities.

Healthcare resource utilization (HRU) data included hospitalizations (number of days); emergency room visits; consultations with rheumatologists, pain specialists, general practitioners, cognitive behavioral therapists, occupational therapists, psychiatrists, and family physicians; and FM counselling sessions with other healthcare professionals. Data on imaging, laboratory tests, and medication use were also collected. Medication utilization was assessed based on the number of packs dispensed per year. The unit costs for each HRU element were obtained in 2022 Saudi Riyals (SAR).

Sample Size and Statistical Analysis

All eligible patients identified in the hospital’s medical system during the study period (128 patients) were included in the study. Data were extracted and processed using Microsoft Excel and checked for errors before analysis. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data. Continuous variables were reported as means and standard deviations (SD), whereas categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages. Direct healthcare costs were calculated by multiplying the reported HRU frequency by the corresponding unit costs. All costs were recorded in Saudi Riyals for the financial year 2022. For international comparability, the main cost elements were converted to USD using the exchange rate reported by the Saudi Central Bank (1USD = 3.750 SAR).14

Results

Demographic Characteristics

The study included 128 patients, the majority of whom were female (93%). Among the patients, the mean age was 47.1 years (SD ±11.9) and 60.2% were married. Based on BMI, most patients were either overweight (43.4%) or obese (41.6%). More than half (56.3%) of the patients had three or more comorbidities, with endocrine and metabolic diseases being the most prevalent (62.5%), followed by cardiovascular disease (52.3%) and orthopedic disorders (50%). A significant portion of patients were also affected by urogenital conditions (43%) and autoimmune diseases (37.5%) (Table 1).

Healthcare Resource Utilization

Rheumatology consultations were the most frequently accessed specialty service, with over half (52.3%) of the patients visiting rheumatologists, with an average of 1.2 (±1.4) visits per patient annually (Table 2). Physical therapy services were utilized by 43% of the patients, with a mean of 1.5 (±2.7) visits per patient annually. Endocrinology ranked third in terms of utilization (28.9%). Pain medicine and orthopedic specialists were accessed equally (22.7% each).

Lab and Imaging Utilization

Laboratory tests were significantly more utilized than imaging tests, with 86.7% of patients receiving at least one laboratory test compared to 79.7% for imaging, showing a much higher mean utilization per patient (142.2 vs 8.4 tests) (Table 3). Laboratory tests also demonstrated greater variability in use, with a SD of 262.2 compared to that of 24.7 for imaging tests.

Medication Utilization

Antidepressants were the most widely prescribed medications (77.3% of patients), accounting for the highest number of annual medication packs (560 packs annually) (Table 4). Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) were the second most used medications (63.3%), followed by acetaminophen (51.6%). Anti-epileptic medications showed high consumption (515 packs annually) in 43% of the patients. Opioids were used by 25.8% of the patients. More than half (53.1%) of the patients were prescribed miscellaneous medications such as proton pump inhibitors and antihistamines.

Direct Medical Costs

The total annual DMC of FM on health care was SAR 1,228,955.3 (USD 327,721.4) with an annual average cost of SAR 9,601.2 (USD 2560.3) per patient. The predominant cost drivers were healthcare team specialist visits (50.7% of the total costs, SAR 623,390.2 = USD 166,237.4) followed by diagnostic procedures (45.6% of the total costs, SAR 560,734.2 = USD 149,529.1) (Table 5).

Discussion

This study revealed the healthcare utilization patterns and economic burden of FM in Saudi Arabia. The economic analysis demonstrated a substantial financial impact, with an annual DMC of SAR 9,601.2 (USD 2,560.3) per patient. The total economic burden for the study population was SAR 1,228,955.3 (USD 327,721.4), highlighting the significant impact of FM on the Saudi healthcare system.

In this study, the annual DMC per FM patient was within the range (USD 858–6,558) reported in a systematic review11 of eight studies included the healthcare perspective in costing FM patients.15–22 Although the costs found in this study fall within the average of these studies,15–22 they were highly heterogeneous in methodology and differed from our study in various key elements. First, only five studies included both sexes in their samples,16,17,20–22 with the remaining including only female15,18 or male19 patients. Second, although the studies were all similar to ours in using bottom-up methods for resource quantification, which is considered a more precise method, only four studies were similar to ours in utilizing medical records as a data source,18,19,21,22 whereas others relied on administrative claims or patient surveys, which have various biases that render comparisons with our study. Furthermore, the components of collected costs varied widely between studies in which one or more cost drivers were not collected,11 such as costs for medications,15,18,19 diagnostics,16,18,19 outpatient visits,15,20 or physical therapy15,17–22 costs. Therefore, the heterogeneity among the reported studies undermines their comparability with our findings and contributes to inaccurate estimates of the economic burden of FM on the healthcare system.

We found that the highest impact on expenditure was related to healthcare team visits, followed by diagnostic tests, with medication costs being the lowest. When comparing our findings to those of other studies, international patterns are highly variable in studies evaluating DMCs, in which the predominant component was outpatient visits,20,21 inpatient visits,15 or medications,16,22 In an Iranian study, diagnostic tests were found to be a major contributor to costs from a societal perspective.23

The multidisciplinary approach to FM management was observed in this study, with rheumatology being the most frequently accessed specialty (52.3%). However, the intensity of rheumatology visits and other medical visits in our cohort appears to be lower than other studies. Rivera et al reported that, in Spain, FM patients had an annual mean of 8.4 (±9.6) specialist visits and 19.1 (±16.6) total medical visits per patient.17 These differences may reflect variations in treatment guidelines, or referral patterns.

Regarding medication use, our finding that antidepressants were the most prescribed medication class (77.3%) is consistent with international patterns. However, the relatively high opioid utilization rate (25.8%) in this study contrasts with that of some studies, in which prescribing opioids for FM is more restricted. This difference may reflect variations in pain management approaches and regulations across healthcare systems. In regard to medications costs, Lachaine et al16 identified significantly higher medication costs for patients with FM compared to controls in Canada, particularly for pain medications and antidepressants. However, the proportion of total costs attributed to medications was substantially higher in Lachaine et al study than in ours. Moreover, the medication costs in our study were the lowest compared to those of other healthcare resources. This can be attributed to the centralized pharmaceutical procurement managed by the National Unified Procurement Company,24 which negotiates with medical companies and manufacturers, awards contracts to the lowest bidder, and oversees the procurement, warehousing, and distribution of pharmaceuticals, medical equipment, and supplies.

Our study did not stratify costs according to disease severity, thus limiting some comparisons. Winkelmann et al25 demonstrated higher costs in patients with severe FM based on the Fibromyalgia Impact Questionnaire (FIQ) severity index. The mean total direct and indirect annual costs were higher for patients with severe FM (France: €10,087; Germany: €10,746) than for those with moderate (France: €6,633; Germany: €6,432) or mild FM (France: €5,473; Germany: €2,058).25 However, the difference in costs was not statistically significant in France (p = 0.185), but was significant in Germany (p = 0.003).25 Similarly, Rivera et al17 found that patients in the 20% highest cost quintile group showed greater disease involvement, with significantly higher FIQ scores (p < 0.0001), number of comorbidities (p < 0.004), and anxiety and depression scores (p < 0.001) than those in the lower costs group in Spain. Moreover, our study focused primarily on DMC from a healthcare perspective. FM has a substantial impact on work productivity and absenteeism, leading to higher indirect costs. In Japan, Lee et al26 demonstrated that indirect costs caused by productivity loss were approximately 2.4 times greater in patients with FM compared to controls. Furthermore, indirect costs often exceed direct costs in relation to FM.11 These findings indicate that future research in Saudi Arabia could benefit from incorporating disease severity measures and productivity costs to better understand cost variations within the population with FM and prioritize interventions in healthcare.

This study had several limitations. First, our study presents the economic burden of FM from a healthcare perspective based on DMC data. The lack of data on direct non-medical, indirect, and intangible costs limits our knowledge of the impact of FM from a broader perspective. Second, owing to the limited data available, our study did not consider the differences in DMC based on FM severity levels. Third, the retrospective nature of the study may underestimate the costs of FM, as it does not include non-pharmacological interventions, such as cognitive behavioral therapy, transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation, and medical aids, including bathroom aids, canes, and specialized bedroom equipment. This study also included a representative sample of patients with FM from a single hospital in Riyadh, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. However, our findings provide insights into the economic impact of FM in Saudi Arabia.

Conclusion

This study assessed the HRU and DMC related to FM in a Saudi tertiary hospital and found a substantial annual DMC of SAR 9,601.2 (USD 2,560.3) per patient. High utilization of visits to healthcare teams and of diagnostic procedures were the main drivers of FM costs. These findings provide essential knowledge to assist decision-makers in setting healthcare priorities and efficiently allocating resources for FM management in the Saudi healthcare system.
