Developing UV- protective textiles for workwear applications using various ZnO nanoparticles and cold plasma
Authors: Nafiseh Nasirzadeh, Farideh Golbabaei, Reza Faridi-Majidi, Mohammad Reza Monazzam Esmaielpour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Textiles play an important role in workwear by significantly upgrading safety, comfort, and productivity for workers. Recently, new technologies like nanotechnology have played a significant role in the textile industry by providing new functionalities. So, the aim of this study was to develop UV-protective textiles for workwear applications using various ZnO nanoparticles and cold plasma. Zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO-NPs) were synthesized using two methods: chemical and green synthesis. The nanoparticles were characterized using various microscopy methods, spectroscopy, X-ray diffraction (XRD), and dynamic light scattering (DLS). Subsequently, the textiles were treated using a pad-dry-cure-pad method. To enhance the adhesion of the NPs, two approaches were employed: citric acid and cold plasma treatment. Finally, the Ultraviolet Protection Factor (UPF) and air permeability were measured before and after washing. The XRD pattern confirmed the presence of peaks attributed to ZnO-NPs in both synthesis methods. Spherical ZnO-NPs produced via chemical and green synthesis showed band gaps of around 3.27 eV and 3.28 eV, respectively. UPF values for untreated and treated textiles using citric acid were 38.92, 322, and 566, respectively. With plasma treatment, UPF increased to 341 and 658 for chemical and green nanoparticles. After five wash cycles, UPF decreased for all textiles. Air permeability improved from 253 ft³/ft²/min to 300 and 299.96 ft³/ft²/min after treatment, and to 511.13 and 255.56 ft³/ft²/min with cold plasma. Green nanoparticles and cold plasma treatments enhance the UPF of textiles more effectively than chemical nanoparticles and citric acid. Therefore, green synthesis offers a promising, cost-effective alternative to traditional chemical methods.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-43881-2.

Introduction

Spanning wavelengths from roughly 190 to 400 nanometers (nm), ultraviolet (UV) radiation represents a portion of the electromagnetic spectrum1. While the sun is the primary natural source of UV radiation, artificial sources such as tanning beds and certain lighting devices also contribute to human exposure2. Consequently, individuals in various occupational environments—including construction, agriculture, aviation, transportation, mining, maritime activities, emergency services, and even indoor workplaces—face elevated risks of UV radiation exposure3.

Several health risks can arise from UV radiation exposure, affecting the skin, eyes, and immune defenses4. Previous studies5 suggest that skin damage is more prevalent than other types of UV-related damage. The consequences of this exposure range from immediate issues such as sunburn to long-term problems like skin cancer and premature aging6. To mitigate these risks, it is crucial to adopt protective strategies such as sunscreen application, wearing protective clothing, and limiting excessive sun exposure7.

Research shows that clothing acts as an effective barrier against ultraviolet (UV) radiation8. Textiles play an important role in workwear by significantly upgrading safety, comfort, and productivity for workers9. Individuals often prefer workwear that provides comfort and flexibility10. Along with these properties, in hot weather or during intense sweating, air permeability is considered an important factor in clothing comfort11. It serves as a measure of thermal loss and breathability in garments12. Additionally, it plays a role in preventing infections13. Various studies show that textile finishing, fiber type (natural vs. synthetic), yarn production methods, and the structural features of the textile all affect air permeability14. Cotton, widely favored for its comfort properties15, unfortunately provides limited UV protection. Therefore, improving the UV-blocking capability of such textiles is essential for better management of occupational UV exposure16.

Recent advances in nanotechnology have significantly impacted the textile industry by introducing novel functionalities. Among various nanomaterials, zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO-NPs) have attracted particular interest due to their unique structural, thermal, optical, and electronic properties17. ZnO-NPs possess broad UV-Vis absorption capabilities, making them ideal candidates for developing protective textiles against ultraviolet radiation18. Furthermore, green nanotechnology has emerged as an environmentally sustainable approach, emphasizing the synthesis of nanoparticles using plant extracts and agricultural waste19. This approach aims to reduce resource and energy consumption while promoting sustainable economic growth. Green nanotechnology prioritizes minimizing waste, enhancing energy efficiency, and ensuring the use of non-toxic materials throughout the lifecycle of nanomaterials20.

In traditional methods, chemical immersion techniques were employed to improve the adhesion of nanomaterials to textile surfaces. However, these methods can raise environmental issues, such as the generation of wastewater that contains dyes, toxins, and harmful chemicals21. Furthermore, these substances can interact with dyestuffs, leading to additional complications22. To mitigate these problems, emerging techniques such as plasma treatment of textiles offer a promising alternative to traditional chemical methods23. In this approach, plasma treatment modifies textile characteristics by introducing new functional groups to the surface, altering its properties without affecting the structure24.

The impregnation of nanomaterials and surface modification techniques offers significant potential to improve UV protection and durability of workwear textiles, which are vital for occupational health and safety. While zinc oxide nanoparticles (ZnO-NPs) and plasma treatments have been studied separately, their combined application in protective clothing—especially in developing countries with high occupational UV exposure—remains largely unexplored25,26. Traditional chemical synthesis methods face limitations due to environmental harm and cost, highlighting the need for sustainable alternatives20. Green synthesis using plant extracts and agricultural waste has emerged as a promising approach; however, the use of sour cherry (Prunus cerasus) waste as a reducing agent is novel and untapped. This local, eco-friendly resource can add economic value while supporting sustainable production. Additionally, cold plasma treatment provides a solvent-free method to enhance nanoparticle adhesion and durability on textiles. This study aims to fill these gaps by developing UV-resistant workwear textiles through the innovative green synthesis of ZnO-NPs using sour cherry waste combined with cold plasma treatment, addressing critical needs in occupational health with sustainable and effective solutions.

Materials and methods

Materials

The materials used were: Zinc acetate dihydrate (Zn(CH3COO)2·2H2O) (CAS 5970-45-6|108802, Merck Co); Ethanol absolute CAS 64-17-5|100,983; Sodium hydroxide (NaOH) (CAS 1310-73-2|106467, Merck Co); Citric acid (CAS 77-92-9|137002); Sour-Cherry Pomace Extract, Textiles composed of 100% cotton, with a linear density of yarn of 23 × 13 threads/cm and thickness of 0.2 mm; and Distilled water.

Chemical synthesis of ZnO-NPs

2.19 g of Zn(CH3COO)2·2H2O was dissolved in 50 mL of absolute ethanol to prepare a 0.2 M solution. This solution was subsequently placed on a magnetic stirrer at 900 rpm and heated to 65℃ until the solution became clear and all the zinc salt particles were completely dissolved. Next, a 1 M NaOH solution was prepared. Both solutions were stirred at 900 rpm at room temperature for 15 min to ensure thorough mixing. Subsequently, the NaOH solution was added dropwise to the zinc precursor solution, resulting in the formation of a clear, white precipitate. This process was carried out at 65℃ with magnetic stirring at 900 rpm, with the pH of the solution maintained at 8. The reaction was maintained under the same conditions for 1.5 h to allow nanoparticle formation. After that, the solution was cooled to room temperature. The resulting precipitate was then collected via centrifugation at 7000 rpm for 20 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the precipitate was washed several times to remove any unreacted materials. Finally, the precipitate was transferred to an oven and dried at 100℃ for 2 h to obtain a dry powder.

Green synthesis of ZnO-NPs

Sour-cherry Pomace was sourced from local suppliers in Tasuj, East Azerbaijan Province, Iran. To prepare the extract, 20 g of the pomace were combined with 100 mL of a 1:1 (v/v) ethanol (97%)/water solution in an Erlenmeyer flask and allowed to stand at 37 °C overnight on a shaker set to 100 rpm (NST SHER 150-A, Iran). The resulting extract was then filtered sequentially, first to remove coarse debris, followed by a finer filtration to eliminate microparticulates. The clarified extract was subsequently concentrated to half its original volume using a rotary evaporator (Heating bath B-49, Büchi, Germany), under vacuum (65 °C, 842 mbar).

For the synthesis of ZnO-NPs, 1.097 g of Zn(CH3COO)2·2H2O was dissolved in 50 mL of distilled water to create a 0.1 M solution. 20 mL of the sour-cherry Pomace extract were then added to this solution, and the mixture stirred at 700 rpm for 1 h. The temperature and pH were carefully controlled at 70 °C and 8, respectively. Following this, the solution was transferred to Falcon tubes and stored in the dark for 13 h. The resulting ZnO-NP precipitate was recovered by centrifugation at 7000 rpm for 20 min. The isolated precipitate was then transferred to a Petri dish, dried in an oven at 150 °C for 90 min, and finally calcined at 160 °C for 15 min.

Characterization of extract and ZnO-NPs

To assess the total phenolic content (TPC) within the extract, the Folin-Ciocalteu assay was performed. For this purpose, the extract was dispensed into six microtubes, with 2 mL of Folin-Ciocalteu reagent subsequently added to each. After a 3-minute interval, 2 mL of a 20% sodium carbonate solution was introduced to each microtube, and the resulting mixture was incubated in darkness for a duration of 2 h. Subsequently, UV-Vis spectroscopy was employed at a wavelength of 760 nm to quantify the peak absorbance. A linear calibration curve was generated using gallic acid as the standard, and the findings were reported as milligrams of gallic acid equivalents (GAE) per milliliter of extract.

Furthermore, the nanoparticles were characterized using various instrumental methods. X-Ray Diffraction (XRD) was used to confirm the formation of ZnO-NPs and to estimate the crystallite size of the synthesized NPs. Textile surface preparation

The nanoparticle coating was applied to the textiles using the pad-dry-cure method. Initially, the textiles were scoured in a solution of deionized water and standard detergent at 40 °C for 30 min. This was followed by sequential rinsing with tap water and deionized water, and then air-dried at room temperature. Following the cleaning process, textile samples of 5 cm×5 cm were prepared for the UV-blocking assessment.

Surface modification via cold plasma

Before the nano-particle coating process, two approaches were employed: plasma activation of the textile surface and coating without any surface activation. To activate the textile surfaces, low-pressure cold plasma was employed. Specifically, the textile samples underwent inductively coupled radio frequency atmospheric plasma (ICP-RF) treatment within a sputtering system. Prior to treatment, the samples were carefully mounted onto a holder inside a cylindrical discharge chamber, which was then hermetically sealed. The plasma treatment was carried out for 2 min under the following conditions: a pressure of 0.42 mbar, a voltage of 484 volts, and a current of 25 A.

Textile coating with ZnO-NPs

In one process, the textiles were submerged in a 2% suspension of ZnO nanoparticles, along with 3% (w/v) citric acid as a binder, under continuous magnetic agitation for one hour. The swatches were then padded to achieve a uniform wet pickup. After padding, the textiles were dried at 50 °C, and subsequently cured in an oven at 140 °C for 3 min. Finally, the coated textiles were washed and rinsed five times to remove any loosely bound nanoparticles from the fabric.

In another process, textiles without plasma surface modification were soaked in a 2% suspension of ZnO nanoparticles. Subsequently, all previously mentioned steps were carried out identically.

Characterization of developed textiles

Following the coating of the textiles, Scanning Electron Microscopy with Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (SEM-EDX) and Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy were utilized to identify characteristics of the developed textiles. SEM-EDX provided high-resolution images of the surface morphology and elemental composition, while FTIR analyzed the binding properties and chemical interactions between the textiles and their coatings.

Evaluating the UPF

To determine the Ultraviolet Protection Factor (UPF), light transmission through the textiles was measured using a UV/Vis spectrophotometer across the 290–400 nm wavelength range. In the equation, the solar irradiance (Wm− 2 nm− 1) is represented by E(λ), the erythema action spectrum by ε(λ), the spectral transmittance through the samples at wavelength λ by T(λ), and the wavelength interval of measurement by ⊿λ26.

Protection against ultraviolet radiation in textiles is categorized into four levels based on UPF: ‘Insufficient protection’ (UPF less than 15), ‘Good protection’ (UPF 15–24), ‘Very good protection’ (UPF 25–39), and ‘Excellent protection’ (UPF 40–50+)29. According to these UPF ratings, UV radiation blocked by the textile is classified as follows: under 93%, 93–96%, 96–97%, and 97–99%. Thus, a UPF of 50 or greater signifies that the material furnishes ‘Excellent’ UV protection.

Evaluating the air permeability

Air permeability was determined for both uncoated and coated textiles according to the procedures outlined in ASTM D737 30. A constant pressure of 100 Pa was maintained while airflow was measured. For each type of textile, measurements were taken at three different locations, and the average air permeability was then calculated.

Durability of UPF and air permeability after washing

The washing durability was assessed to determine how well the textiles retained their UV-blocking and air permeability properties, following the ISO 105-C02:1989(E) standard31. To simulate real washing conditions, the ZnO-coated textiles were laundered alongside uncoated textiles. The textiles were then checked after five wash cycles to evaluate their performance. According to ISO 105 standards, five washing cycles are commonly used as a benchmark to assess the initial durability of textile finishes under repeated laundering. This provides a standardized and comparable framework for functional property evaluation.

Results and discussion

Characterization of ZnO-NPs

The XRD pattern of ZnO-NPs was confirmed by comparison with JCPDS card No. 80 − 0075, indicating the successful synthesis of ZnO-NPs via both chemical and green methods. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the characteristic diffraction peaks at 2θ values of 31.8°, 34.41°, 36.27°, 47.62°, 56.63°, 62.93°, 66.51°, 67.91°, and 69.22° align with the (100), (002), (101), (102), (110), (103), (200), (112), and (201) planes, respectively, of the hexagonal wurtzite structure of ZnO. These results are consistent with previously published data32–35. Additionally, this figure shows that the peaks for ZnO-NPs synthesized by the green method are sharper than those synthesized by the chemical method, which indicates the presence of well-ordered crystalline ZnO-NPs. The XRD pattern of ZnO-NPs synthesized by the chemical method shows peaks that may correspond to impurities or secondary phases. Furthermore, the Scherrer equation indicated average crystallite sizes of 17.72 nm and 18.7 nm for ZnO-NPs synthesized by the green and chemical methods, respectively.

The UV–Vis spectra for the ZnO-NPs are shown in Fig. 3. Absorption peaks for the ZnO-NPs synthesized via the chemical and green methods appeared at 355 nm and 359.5 nm, respectively. These findings suggest a greater tendency for UV radiation absorption in the NPs synthesized by the green method compared to those synthesized by the chemical method. Similar results have been reported in previous studies concerning ZnO-NPs synthesized via the chemical36,37 and green methods38–40. For example, in the study by Upadhyay et al., 41 two methods were used to synthesize ZnO nanoparticles. The green synthesis method using Ocimum Tenuiflorum leaf extract, produced smaller nanoparticles compared to the chemical method. Also, their absorption spectrum also shifted towards blue at 274 nm. Rashidian et al.42 used both green and chemical synthesis methods for ZnO-NPs. They showed that the morphology and size of the NPs when Thymus pubescent extract was used were better than those synthesized through the chemical synthesis process. In another study, Asmat-Campos et al.43 used Coriandrum sativum extract in the green synthesis process of ZnO-NPs and reported better morphology and size compared to the chemical synthesis method It can be concluded that morphology and size of NPs strongly depend on the phenolic content of the extract. Modeling of data obtained from previous studies confirms a significant correlation (p-value = 0.02) between the phenolic content and the size of NPs25.

The DLS results showed a good distribution of the synthesized nanoparticles in aqueous suspension. All samples were prepared using the same diluted concentration to ensure consistency and comparability of the results. According to Fig. 4, the average size of ZnO-NPs was 153 nm for both synthesis methods. However, the DLS signal intensity was lower for the nanoparticles synthesized by the green method. This is likely due to the smaller size of the nanoparticles synthesized via the green method. Similar studies suggest that DLS intensity decreases when there are fewer particles in the solvent or when the particle size is small44,45.

FTIR analysis (Fig. 5) showed absorption peaks at 456.51 cm−1and 442.12 cm− 1, correlating with the Zn–O vibrations in zinc oxide nanoparticles synthesized via green and chemical methods, respectively. Furthermore, peaks in the ranges of 600–840 cm− 1, 1000–1400 cm− 1, 1440–1625 cm− 1, 2850–2990 cm− 1, and 3200–3800 cm− 1 indicate the presence of C-Cl, C-F, C = C, C-H, C = O, and O–H stretching vibrations from organic compounds attached to or coating both types of ZnO nanoparticles. This finding aligns with previous studies46.

For ZnO nanoparticles synthesized via the green method, the disappearance of the C = O peak at ~ 1637 cm⁻¹, along with the appearance of new C–H peaks around 2900 cm⁻¹, indicates that a redox reaction occurred during nanoparticle formation. In this process, phenolic compounds present in the plant extract act as reducing agents, donating electrons to Zn²⁺ ions, which are subsequently reduced to ZnO. Concurrently, the phenolic groups undergo oxidation, forming quinone-like structures or other oxidized derivatives. This electron transfers and redox reaction lead to a decrease in the intensity of C = O vibrations and subtle changes in C–H peaks, providing a clear spectroscopic signature of the redox interaction. These FTIR changes, therefore, not only confirm nanoparticle formation but also reflect the chemical transformation of the plant-derived reducing agents during the green synthesis process. Similar FTIR patterns have been reported in other studies investigating of ZnO-NPs synthesized via green method47–49.

In addition, the FTIR spectrum of the sour-cherry pomace extract showed a strong absorption peak at 3431.98 cm− 1, indicating the presence of O–H functional groups or phenolic compounds. Other spectral features observed in the ranges of 2400 –2000 cm− 1, 1780 –1630 cm− 1, 1350 –1000 cm− 1, 800 –600 cm− 1, and 1500–1400 cm− 1 were linked to various organic compounds, including − C ≡ N, C═O, C–F, C-Cl, and C–C stretching vibrations within the ring structure. The research conducted by Okur et al. 50 on the antioxidant activity of sour cherries indicated similarities in the FTIR spectrum. Unlike other studies, Farhadi et al. 51 did not observe any absorption peaks associated with − C ≡ N groups. This discrepancy may be due to the presence of sour-cherry seeds or kernels in our pomace extract52, which are known to contain cyanide compounds53,54.

Characterization of fabrics

UV-blocking measurement

Table 1 summarizes the UPF values for textiles treated with two types of ZnO-NPs under different conditions: one using citric acid as a binder and another involving cold plasma activation. As shown, the initial fabric had a UPF of 92.38, which improved after coating with both types of NPs. One reason for this could be the small size of the NPs synthesized by the green method compared to the chemically synthesized NPs. Becheri et al. used two different sizes of ZnO-NPs to investigate their light absorption. They showed that NPs with a size of 9 ± 1 nm had higher absorption compared to NPs with a size of 21 ± 5 nm55. Verbiˇ et al. 56 reported that the UPF enhancement with NPs synthesized in green methods is better than chemical method. According to the developed effective mass model57, it can be inferred that, with decreasing NP size, the absorption peak has shifted towards the ultraviolet spectrum. Therefore, better absorption of UV radiation by NPs synthesized in the green method has occurred. However, it should be noted that according to previous studies using the Monte Carlo simulation, the size of ZnO-NPs can only have a positive effect on protection against UV radiation within a certain range26.

Notably, cotton textiles coated with green synthesized NPs and treated with cold plasma achieved the highest UPF value at 658. Furthermore, cold plasma alone increased the UPF from 92.38 to 173. Vellingiri et al.58 used two types of cold plasma and atmospheric plasma to activate the textile surface. Their results showed an improvement in the UPF for coated textiles. Jazbec et al.59 showed that with increasing plasma treatment time, more oxygen functional groups were formed on the fiber surface, which led to better reaction with NPs and increased the UPF of the textiles. In the present study, atmospheric plasma was used for surface activation. Studies suggest that the use of atmospheric plasma is cheaper, more convenient and environmentally friendly compared to chemical processes and other plasmas. Therefore, in recent years, researchers have paid more attention to the use of atmospheric plasmas60. In line with these findings, Carneiro et al. 61 state that air atmosphere-derived plasmas not only increase wettability but also change hydrophobic properties into hydrophilic ones affecting cotton fibers’ integrity. Sparavigna62 states that surface activation by plasmas leads to chemical changes in cotton fibers’ waxy cuticle layer without damaging its cellulose structure. The enhancement in UPF observed with plasma-treated textiles can be attributed to increased surface energy and the generation of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species during plasma exposure. These effects improve the binding and uniform distribution of ZnO nanoparticles on the textile fibers, leading to better UV protection. This mechanism is consistent with previous studies on plasma surface activation63.

Additionally, the results in Table 1; Fig. 6 indicate that the UV transmission rate in the UVA range is higher, indicating less blocking in this range compared to UVB. This implies that developed cotton textiles offer superior protection against UVB radiation. The results of Verbic et al.‘s56 studies show that textiles modified with ZnO-NPs synthesized with tea, pomegranate peel, and sumac extracts exhibit greater blocking of UVB radiation than UVA radiation. They reported that due to the high phenol content in pomegranate extract, the ability of textiles coated with NPs synthesized with pomegranate extract to block UVB radiation was the highest. Similar results were also observed in other studies64. In our research, it was found that the total phenolic content (TPC) was 4.48 mgGAE/ml. The UPF values for textiles coated with green ZnO-NPs with and without plasma activation surface were significantly enhanced, reaching 566 and 685, respectively. It is evident that phenolic compounds serve as effective antioxidants, which are crucial in reducing metal ions—a key step in synthesis processes. Moreover, these compounds contain functional groups capable of coordinating with Zn(II) ions, thereby facilitating and stabilizing the formation of ZnO nanoparticles. Moreover, it can be concluded that the aromatic rings in the molecular structure of flavonoids absorb UV radiation in the 200–400 nm range. Hence, it can be concluded that the extract itself contributed to the enhanced light absorption properties of the textiles65.

Table 2 outline the UPF values for both initial and developed textiles after five wash cycles. The UV transmission of the textiles before and after washing is also shown in Fig. 6. Specifically, A and C represent textiles treated with ZnO nanoparticles and citric acid, before and after washing, respectively, while B and D correspond to textiles treated with ZnO nanoparticles and cold plasma, before and after washing, respectively. As illustrated, plasma-treated textiles exhibited lower UV transmission compared to citric acid-treated textiles, both before and after washing, indicating enhanced UV protection. Clearly, washing reduced the UPF across all developed textiles, but this decrease was less pronounced for those coated with chemically synthesized NPs compared to green synthesized ones. Moreover, the results show that plasma-treated samples experienced a sharper decline in the UPF compared to non-plasma-treated ones. Notably, even after washing, all developed textiles maintained reduced UVB transmission rates. This indicates they continued to perform well against harmful UVB radiation. It should be noted that while the UPF decreased more significantly after washing on plasma-treated textiles compared to non-plasma samples, this reduction in protective power primarily affected the UVA range. However, textiles developed with all NPs—especially those where plasma was used for surface activation—continued to offer effective protection against harmful UVB radiation. Research has explored how washing affects the stability of UPF values. For instance, El-Naggar et al.66 conducted a study where ZnO-NPs synthesized with date seed extract were subjected to 20 washing cycles. They observed that the UPF values decreased gradually after repeated washes (from 80 to 76.73, then to 68, and finally to 63), yet the treated textiles remained UV-resistant even after multiple cycles. Similar findings were reported by Karthik et al.,67 who noted a gradual decline in UPF values following five and ten washing cycles. This reduction was attributed to changes in textile surface morphology post-washing. These results align with those from studies by Zayed et al.64,68 which also examined how repeated washing impacts nanoparticle-treated textiles. The study by Leroux et al. shows that the effect of washing on plasma durability depends on the type of textile. In nonwoven textiles, washing does not have a significant effect on plasma durability. However, in woven textiles, such as the textiles used in this study, washing leads to a decrease in the surface tension of the textile fibers69. This means that the intermolecular van der Waals forces are reduced. Therefore, it can be concluded that not only can the NPs be partially removed or degraded by washing, but also the plasma effect is reduced due to the reduction of molecular bonds. Therefore, plasma-treated textiles had the highest UPF loss compared to non-plasma textiles. In fact, during washing, some of the plasma-generated functional groups are removed or rearranged within the fiber structure. Also, the higher surface hydrophilicity caused by plasma allows more water and detergent penetration, which can lead to partial loss of UV-absorbing components or nanoparticles. As a result, plasma-treated fabrics tend to experience a greater decrease in UPF after washing compared with untreated ones, mainly due to the instability and wash-off of the plasma-induced surface changes.

Surface morphology of fabrics

Figure 7 shows SEM images of the surface of cotton textiles. As evident, the initial textiles consist of fibers with a clean and smooth surface; no holes or cracks are visible on the surface of its fibers. After coating the textiles with NPs, a granular deposit of ZnO-NPs appears on the surface. It is observed that the amount and density of NPs synthesized by the green method on the coated textiles are less than the NPs synthesized by the chemical method. The results of elemental analysis with EDX also confirm that the density of green ZnO-NPs on the textile surface is lower than that of other NPs. Interestingly, despite the higher amount of ZnO-NPs detected on the textile surface by EDX for the chemical synthesis method, the textiles coated with these particles exhibited lower UPF than those coated with green synthesized NPs. This may be due to the more uniform distribution of ZnO-NPs on the surface of coated textiles in the green method. It seems that It appears that the more even distribution of green-synthesized ZnO nanoparticles on the textile surface is mainly due to the stabilizing effect of bio-organic molecules in the plant extracts. Compounds such as phenols, flavonoids, or proteins attach to the nanoparticle surfaces, forming a charged and sterically stabilized layer that prevents the particles from clumping together. This stabilization allows the nanoparticles to spread more uniformly on the fibers, which likely contributes to the higher UPF observed in textiles treated with green-synthesized nanoparticles.

This suggests that zinc reacts with active oxygen species generated by plasma. Moreover, plasma treatment results in improved nanoparticle adhesion and a more continuous surface coverage, as inferred from SEM morphology. To provide direct evidence of surface coverage, SEM-EDX elemental mapping was performed specifically for the green-synthesized samples (Fig. 9). The Zn and O elemental maps show a continuous distribution of signals along the cotton fibers within the analyzed region, indicating a relatively uniform surface coverage for the green-synthesized ZnO nanoparticles. Furthermore, at high magnification, uncoated textile surfaces exhibit a smooth appearance with no visible cavities or splits. In contrast, plasma-coated textiles develop a rougher texture due to the etching effects of plasma treatment. Similar outcomes have been observed in previous research64,68,70,71. It seems likely that the morphological changes in textile due to NP adhesion contribute to variations in UPF values.

Other variables that appear to affect the light absorption of cotton textiles include the chemical structures of both the nanoparticles and the textile fibers. As shown in Figs. 9 and 10, the absorption peak at approximately 3398.8 cm-1 corresponds to O-H stretching vibrations, primarily due to cellulose in cotton samples72. Peaks at around 2900 cm-1 and 2129.48 cm-1 are characteristic of tetrahedral carbon-hydrogen bonds (C-H) 73,74. The band near 1637.48 cm-1 is associated with COOH functional groups75. Peaks at about 1151.13 cm-1 and 1099.48cm1 relate to C-O and C-O-C groups, respectively76. Glycosidic bonds linked to cellulose are identified within the wavenumber range of approximately 994.74–900.2 cm177,78. After coating cotton textiles, additional peaks emerge on the developed textiles. Absorption peaks between 3600 and 4000 cm-1 indicate O–H functional groups or phenolic compounds79. Furthermore, peaks between 2400 –2000 cm-1 are attributed to organic compounds containing − C ≡ N80, which are related to sour-cherry extract.

As shown in Fig. 10, citric acid appears to improve the adhesion of ZnO nanoparticles on the cotton surface. It functions as an adhesion promoter, enabling nanoparticle binding through hydrogen bonding and limited physical interactions with the − OH groups of cellulose. In previous work, Ibrahim et al.81 demonstrated that citric acid combined with Sodium Hypophosphite (SHP) serves as an ester-crosslinking binder, stabilizing ZnO and chitosan nanoparticles via ester bond formation (~ 1730 cm⁻¹) using a pad-dry-cure method. Another study82 on denim fabrics applied citric acid with SHP along with enzymatic pre-treatment using the pad–dry–microwave technique, where nanoparticles were anchored through ester crosslinking and surface interactions. In contrast, our FTIR results show that ZnO nanoparticles are successfully stabilized without ester bond formation, indicating that physical and hydrogen-bonding interactions alone are sufficient for effective adhesion. These findings suggest that citric acid can stably immobilize nanoparticles on cotton fabrics without any catalyst, and the observed spectral changes support this mechanism.

In general, it can be stated that one of the most important characteristics of NPs is the change in their optical properties compared to the large or bulk state of that material. In conductors, the conduction band is half-filled and electrical conductivity occurs under normal conditions. However, in semiconductors such as ZnO, there is a region called the energy gap (forbidden band) between the full and empty energy bands84. After the electron is transferred from the valence band to the conduction band; a hole is created in the valence band, where the electron was previously present, due to the absence of an electron. In conductive materials, this energy gap does not exist and electrons from valence bands can enter conduction bands freely without requiring additional energy. While in semiconductors these levels are separated from each other. The difference between these two levels means electrons can be transferred from valence to conduction bands by doing some work (a small amount). Thus with thermal energy even at room temperature some electrons can transfer into conduction bands leading to electrical conductivity85. If photons irradiated onto a semiconductor have an energy greater than gap energy, then electrons are transferred into conduction bands causing formation of an electron-hole pair. In nanoparticles particle size controls distance between electron-hole pairs so as particle size decreases down nanometer scale then energy gap increases85. ZnO-NP has an energy of 3.3ev, which usually increases with decreasing particle size86. When UV radiation hits ZnO-NPs, the electrons absorb the UV due to the high energy gap. This is why ZnO-NPs can play a protective role against UV radiation87. As the Fig. 12 shows, the band gap energy for the NPs synthesized by green and chemical methods was around 2.88 and 2.79, respectively. This result further supports the smaller particle size of the ZnO nanoparticles synthesized via the green method compared to those produced by the chemical method.

Air permeability

The air permeability of textiles refers to the volume of air that passes through a unit area over a given time, typically expressed mathematically as ft³/min/ft²88.

The results of this study showed that the air permeability increased from 253.3 ± 8.16ft³/min/ft² to 300 ± 28.6 ft³/min/ft² and 299.96 ± 24.14 ft³/min/ft² for ZnO-NPs synthesized by chemical and green methods, respectively (Supplementary information). Additionally, when the textiles were treated with cold plasma, the air permeability increased to 511.13 ± 41.57 ft³/min/ft² for chemically synthesized ZnO-NPs and 255.56 ± 15.74 ft³/min/ft² for green-synthesized ones. These results suggest that the increase in air permeability was greater for textiles developed with ZnO-NPs synthesized using the chemical method. It can be concluded that the large nanoparticles lead to greater spacing between textile fibers, resulting in increased permeability. In this context, Farouk et al. found that applying ZnO nanoparticles to the textile surface boosts air permeability from 250.5 L/dm².min to about 352 L/dm².min89. In another study, they reported that the air permeability for cotton textiles coated with ZnTiO3 nanoparticles increased from 245.8 L/dm².min to 256.2 L/dm².min90. Unlike previous findings, Li et al. observed that fabrics coated with ZnO nanoparticles exhibit decreased air permeability, possibly because of the concentration of cellulose nanofibrils. Notably, as the concentration of CNF increases, so does the viscosity of its suspension; this leads to reduced spacing between yarns and subsequently lower air permeability91. In general, it can be inferred that coating textile surfaces with NPs enhances air permeability, but the degree of improvement depends on factors such as nanoparticle size and concentration. This suggests that larger nanoparticles tend to increase fiber spacing more effectively.In contrast, smaller NPs become trapped between fibers; although they increase spacing somewhat, they also impede airflow to some extent. Consequently, smaller NPs have less effect on improving fabric air permeability compared to larger ones.

When the surface was activated with plasma, an improvement in air permeability was observed in all textiles developed with NPs. Consistent with these findings, Vellingiri et al.58 indicated that the air permeability of coated polyester textiles increased from 6.89% to 13.22%. Simultaneously, the application of plasma treatment resulted in a nearly fourfold increase in air permeability for the polyester fabrics. So it can be concluded that plasma etching is responsible for creating space between fibers, which enables better airflow through the textile.

The study’s results indicated that air permeability decreased significantly after washing for all textiles (Fig. 13). The values ranged from 21.65 ft³/min/ft² to 26.7 ft³/min/ft² and 23.3 ft³/min/ft² for uncoated textiles, chemically coated ZnO-NPs textiles, and green ZnO-NPs coated textiles, respectively (Supplementary information). It is evident that the reduction in air permeability was less pronounced for plasma-treated textiles after washing. This decrease was more notable in samples coated using ZnO synthesized by green methods.

Midha et al. found that air permeability tends to decrease as washing cycles increase. The researches show that repeated washings cause fibers to contract more, reducing penetration of airflow and water vapor92. Also, some studies suggest that during these cycles, fibers can swell and open up the fabric structure93. Previous research has shown that multiple laundering cycles can compact fabric fibers and reduce the size of pores between them, which restricts airflow and subsequently lowers air permeability94. This structural densification of textiles during washing has been documented in various studies95. Additionally, detergent residues may accumulate within the microstructure of fabrics, potentially obstructing inter-fiber channels and further impeding air passage96. The extent of this effect can vary depending on the washing conditions, including detergent type, concentration, and rinsing efficiency97. The results of this study showed that surface modifications such as chemical finishing or plasma treatment may alter the fabric morphology by filling surface voids or creating continuous layers on the fiber surfaces. While such treatments enhance specific functional properties like UV protection, they may inadvertently reduce breathability. These combined effects help to explain the notable decline in air permeability observed in treated fabrics after washing. This effect is particularly pronounced in textiles with weft yarns, which leads to lower overall permeability98.

Table 3 presents a summary of previous studies that employed green synthesis of zinc oxide nanoparticles to enhance the UV protection of cotton textiles. As noted in our previous study25, various works have utilized green synthesis for this purpose, among which five selected studies applied similar green synthesis methods (ex-situ approach). Comparing the results shows that different plant extracts (Acalypha indica, Seaweed, Guava, Psidium Guajava, and Coriandrum sativum) were used in earlier studies to improve the protective properties of textiles. Except for one study99, no previous work utilized plasma treatment to enhance nanoparticle adhesion. In that study, DBD plasma was applied, whereas in the present work, due to availability, atmospheric cold plasma was used, which is more practical and suitable for delicate fabrics and scalable production. The results of the present study demonstrate that the final UPF is significantly higher than the values reported in previous studies, highlighting the superior UV protection provided by our method.

Regarding air permeability, only one previous study68 reported measurements, with a value of 216.25 cm³/cm²/s. In our study, the air permeability of the treated textiles was approximately 129.8 cm³/cm²/s (converted from 255.56 ft³/min/ft²). Although the textiles in our study show lower air permeability compared to it, this is expected due to the nanoparticle coating and plasma treatment, which improve UV protection and durability while slightly reducing airflow.

Moreover, none of the previous studies reported the phenolic content of the plant extracts, whereas, as noted earlier, phenolic compounds play a critical role in absorbing UV radiation. Quantifying the phenolic content in the extracts can help clarify the protective mechanism, allow better comparison between different studies, and guide the design of more effective extracts for the green synthesis of ZnO nanoparticles.

This study, like many others, has several limitations that should be addressed in future research. Although green synthesis is recognized for producing safer and more environmentally friendly textiles, in vitro toxicity and safety assessments of these fabrics remain necessary. Additionally, nanoparticle (NP) detachment from textiles during washing raises concerns about environmental release and accumulation, which warrants further investigation regarding the fate and impact of NPs in ecosystems such as water and soil. The UV protection evaluation was limited to UVA and UVB wavelengths due to equipment constraints, yet UVC radiation, known for generating free radicals in semiconductors like ZnO, also poses health risks that require assessment in future studies. Finally, while this study presents a promising lab-scale approach to enhancing UV protection in workwear textiles, practical implementation demands comprehensive techno-economic feasibility analyses, including mechanical, thermal, and antimicrobial property assessments, to move towards large-scale production and permanent solutions for occupational UV exposure.

Conclusion

The aim of this study was to develop UV-resistant textiles for workwear applications using various ZnO-NPs and cold plasma. In this context, the study demonstrated that green nanoparticles, combined with cold plasma treatments, enhance the UPF of textiles more effectively than chemical nanoparticles combined with citric acid. This phenomenon can be attributed to the phenolic compounds in sour cherry extract, which served as a reducing agent in the green synthesis process. In fact, the presence of hydroxyl functional groups in the structure of ZnO-NPs synthesized through the green method enables the absorption of UV radiation, converting it into thermal energy. This process effectively helps prevent skin damage in workers exposed to ultraviolet radiation. Moreover, the high band gap of ZnO-NPs synthesized via the green method improves UV radiation absorption by cotton textiles coated with these nanomaterials. Therefore, the green synthesis method not only provides environmental and economic benefits but also yields ZnO-NPs with superior UV-blocking properties. Overall, textiles developed in this study contribute significantly to improving safety and protection in workwear applications. These findings indicate that the developed textiles can be used to produce protective workwear, enabling effective control of UV exposure for workers in various occupational settings. Therefore, future investigations should focus on implementing and developing these textiles for practical applications. It is also recommended that other features of the developed textile, such as tensile strength, flexural strength, thermal properties, and mechanical properties, be tested.
