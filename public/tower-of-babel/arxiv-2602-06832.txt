Exact recovery for seeded graph matching
Nicolas Fraiman, Michael Nisenzon

Abstract
. We study graph matching between two correlated networks in the almost fully seeded regime, where all but a vanishing fraction of vertex correspondences are revealed. Concretely, we consider the correlated stochastic block model and assume that | 𝒰 | = n 1 − α |\mathcal{U}|=n^{1-\alpha} vertices remain unrevealed for some α ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) \alpha\in(0,1) , while the remaining n − n 1 − α n-n^{1-\alpha} vertices are provided as seed correspondences. Our goal is to determine when the true permutation can be recovered efficiently as the proportion of unrevealed vertices vanishes. We prove that exact recovery of the remaining correspondences is achievable in polynomial time whenever λ ​ s 2 > 1 − α \lambda s^{2}>1-\alpha , where λ = ( a + b ) / 2 \lambda=(a+b)/2 is the SBM density parameter and s s denotes the edge retention parameter. This condition smoothly interpolates between the fully seeded setting and the classical unseeded threshold λ ​ s 2 > 1 \lambda s^{2}>1 for matching in correlated Erdős–Rényi graphs. 1.
Introduction

Graph matching is the task of recovering an unrevealed bijection between the vertex sets of two related networks, and arises in numerous domains including social network de-anonymization, biological network alignment, multimodal knowledge graph integration, and multi–view learning. The canonical setting involves two graphs that are noisy or anonymized views of a shared structure, and the goal is to infer both the true correspondence and, in many applications, true community structure across the graphs.

A widely studied model for this problem is the correlated Erdős–Rényi model, in which each observed graph
A
and
B
is obtained by independently subsampling edges from a common parent graph with edge correlation
s\in[0,1]
. Classical results show that exact recovery in the unseeded setting—where no vertex correspondences are revealed—is possible if and only if the expected degree of the intersection graph exceeds the graph connectivity threshold. Precisely, Cullina and Kiyavash
[
3
]
established that exact matching succeeds if
\lambda s^{2}>1
, where
\lambda\log n
is the average degree and
n
is the number of vertices, while below this threshold the true permutation becomes statistically indistinguishable due to automorphisms.

Recent work has sharpened these information-theoretic boundaries. Wu, Xu, and Yu
[
19
]
derived the sharp threshold for exact unseeded matching in correlated Erdős–Rényi graphs in both the dense and sparse cases. In structured random graphs, Rácz and Sridhar
[
15
]
resolved the analogous exact threshold for sparse correlated SBMs, showing that exact unseeded matching is possible if and only if the information-theoretic community signal is sufficiently strong. On the algorithmic front, significant progress has been made toward meeting these limits: Barak et al.
[
1
]
developed the first quasipolynomial-time algorithm capable of operating far from the near-identical regime
s\approx 1
, Ding et al.
[
4
]
gave a polynomial-time degree-profile method for moderate noise, and Mao et al.
[
10
]
achieved polynomial-time exact recovery at constant correlation in sparse graphs via structured subgraph counts. Most recently, Chai and Rácz
[
2
]
obtained the first polynomial-time exact recovery algorithm for correlated SBMs in the full information-theoretic regime. Taken together, these works nearly close the statistical–computational gap for unseeded matching in random graph models.

1.1.
Seeded graph matching

When a subset of vertex correspondences (”seeds”) is provided, matching becomes dramatically easier. Pedarsani and Grossglauser
[
14
]
showed that even a sublinear number of seeds can enable recovery in regimes where unseeded matching is impossible. Yartseva and Grossglauser
[
20
]
developed a percolation-based algorithm showing that above a critical seed density, correct matches propagate across the graph. Korula and Lattanzi
[
8
]
gave a greedy seed-expansion procedure with guarantees in correlated Erdős–Rényi graphs, while Lyzinski et al.
[
9
]
analyzed convex relaxations and highlighted fragility in certain relax-and-round approaches. Empirically, even a handful of seeds can significantly boost matching accuracy: Fishkind et al.
[
5
]
demonstrated strong gains in real networks.

Previous work narrowed the gap between information–theoretic and computational requirements in seeded matching. Wang et al.
[
23
,
18
]
derived sharp information-theoretic thresholds for exact recovery for Erdős–Rényi graphs with seeds, showing how the minimum seed cardinality scales with sparsity and correlation. They also introduced an efficient algorithm that achieves the threshold. Mossel and Xu
[
11
]
further introduced the large-neighborhood-statistics method, achieving the information-theoretic sparsity limit in polynomial time and showing that as few as
\Omega(\log n)
seeds in dense graphs (or
n^{3\varepsilon}
in sparse graphs) suffice for exact recovery.

Seeded graph matching now has methods that are nearly optimal both statistically and computationally in a range of random graph models. A classical approach in the unseeded case solves a relaxation of the quadratic assignment problem, as in the FAQ algorithm
[
17
]
, which uses Frank–Wolfe optimization to project onto the Birkhoff polytope. The Seeded Graph Matching (SGM) algorithm
[
5
]
integrates seeds directly and produces ranked candidate matches. Yu, Xu, and Lin
[
21
]
studied robust matching with partially corrupted seeds, showing that exact recovery remains achievable provided a constant fraction of seeds are correct, and proposing down-weighting strategies. Additional relaxations and kernel formulations have been explored, such as KerGM
[
24
]
, which uses kernelized quadratic assignment relaxations to incorporate structural and attribute similarity. Recent deep alignment pipelines build on these ideas but typically trade interpretability and guarantees for supervised flexibility. Seed-supervised GNN architectures like SeedGNN
[
22
]
and embedding-based systems such as DeepLink
[
25
]
achieve strong empirical performance, yet lack provable recovery guarantees in the regimes studied here.

1.2.
Our contribution

We study the regime where almost all vertex correspondences are revealed, leaving only a vanishing fraction
|\mathcal{U}|=n^{1-\alpha}
of unrevealed vertices for some
\alpha\in(0,1)
. In this partially revealed setting, we show that simple neighborhood–overlap–based methods, as well as a bistochastic linear programming relaxation followed by projection, achieve exact recovery of the remaining matches in polynomial time whenever
\lambda s^{2}>1-\alpha
, where
\lambda=(a+b)/2
is the average SBM density. While we focus on the almost fully seeded regime
|\mathcal{U}|=n^{1-\alpha}
for clarity, the threshold
\lambda s^{2}>1-\alpha
smoothly interpolates between the fully seeded case and the classical unseeded limit
\lambda s^{2}>1
when
|\mathcal{U}|=\Theta(n)
.

In addition to these sharp statistical and algorithmic guarantees, we provide a comprehensive empirical
evaluation on both synthetic and real networks.
Our experiments confirm the theoretical threshold behavior on correlated stochastic block models, showing a rapid transition to near-exact recovery as the seed fraction increases and the condition
\lambda s^{2}>1-\alpha
is met. On a diverse collection of real-world graphs—including biological, communication, social, and internet topology networks—we find that even very simple overlap-based methods are competitive with state-of-the-art seeded matching algorithms when a large fraction of correspondences is revealed. These results demonstrate that the almost fully seeded regime is not only theoretically tractable, but also practically robust across heterogeneous graph structures.

Taken together, our results provide the first tight statistical and computational characterization of
graph matching when only a vanishing fraction of vertices remain unrevealed. They complement recent progress in semi-supervised community detection
[
6
]
by showing that revealing all but
n^{1-\alpha}
correspondences similarly lowers the information threshold for graph matching alone, and that this phenomenon is observable in practice as well as in theory.

2.
Setup and Notation

We begin by formally defining the
Seeded Correlated Stochastic Block Model
, denoted by
\scsbm
, which serves as the generative model for the observed graph pair
(A,B)
throughout this paper.

Definition 1

(Seeded Correlated SBM)
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
denote the number of vertices, and fix connectivity parameters

a,b>0
, a retention parameter
s\in[0,1]
, and a revealed (seed) set
\mathcal{R}\subset[n]
.
The
Seeded Correlated Stochastic Block Model

(A,B)\sim\scsbm(n,a,b,s,\mathcal{R},\sigma^{*},\pi^{*})

is generated as follows:

(1)

True communities.

A balanced labeling
\sigma^{*}:[n]\to\{-1,+1\}
is fixed, inducing two communities

\mathcal{C}_{1}=(\sigma^{*})^{-1}(1)
and
\mathcal{C}_{2}=(\sigma^{*})^{-1}(-1)
with

|\mathcal{C}_{1}|=|\mathcal{C}_{2}|=n/2
.

(2)

True permutation.

A bijection
\pi^{*}:[n]\to[n]
is sampled uniformly at random and represents the
ground truth vertex correspondence between the two graphs.

(3)

Parent SBM.

An undirected simple graph
G
with adjacency matrix

G\in\{0,1\}^{n\times n}
is generated as follows.
For each unordered pair
1\leq u<v\leq n
,

G(u,v)\sim\begin{cases}\Bern(a\log n/n),&\sigma^{*}(u)=\sigma^{*}(v),\\
\Bern(b\log n/n),&\sigma^{*}(u)\neq\sigma^{*}(v),\end{cases}

with
G(v,u)=G(u,v)
and
G(u,u)=0
.

(4)

Correlated subsampling.

Conditioned on
G
, the graphs
A
and
B^{\prime}
are generated independently by
subsampling edges of
G
:
for each
u<v
,

A(u,v)=G(u,v)\cdot X_{uv},\qquad B^{\prime}(u,v)=G(u,v)\cdot Y_{uv},

where
X_{uv},Y_{uv}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\Bern(s)
.
Again,
A
and
B^{\prime}
are symmetric with zero diagonals.

(5)

Anonymization.

The observed graph
B
is obtained by permuting
B^{\prime}
according to
\pi^{*}
:

B(u,v)=B^{\prime}((\pi^{*})^{-1}(u),(\pi^{*})^{-1}(v)).

(6)

Revealed information.

The algorithm uses the exact correspondence
\pi^{*}(r)
for all
r\in\mathcal{R}
,
as well as the community labels
\sigma^{*}(r)
.
The revealed set
\mathcal{R}
is assumed to be balanced:

|\mathcal{C}_{1}\cap\mathcal{R}|=|\mathcal{C}_{2}\cap\mathcal{R}|.

The set of unrevealed vertices is
\mathcal{U}=[n]\setminus\mathcal{R}
.

The observed data consist of
(A,B)
together with the restrictions
\pi_{\mathcal{R}}:=\pi^{*}|_{\mathcal{R}}
and
\sigma_{\mathcal{R}}:=\sigma^{*}|_{\mathcal{R}}
.

Definition 2

(Exact Graph Matching)
.

Let
(A,B)\sim\scsbm(n,a,b,s,\mathcal{R},\sigma^{*},\pi^{*})
.
A graph matching algorithm
\hat{\pi}=\hat{\pi}[A,B,\sigma_{\mathcal{R}},\pi_{\mathcal{R}}]
takes the generated graphs and partially revealed labels as input and outputs an estimator of the permutation

\hat{\pi}:[n]\to[n]
.
We say that
exact recovery
is achieved if

\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{\pi}(u)=\pi^{*}(u)\ \text{for all }u\in[n]\right)=1-o(1).

Since
\pi^{*}
is known on
\mathcal{R}
, this is equivalent to exact recovery on the unknown
\mathcal{U}
.

In the fully unseeded case
\mathcal{R}=\emptyset
, prior work
[
3
]
established that exact graph matching is information-theoretically possible if and only if the edge correlation exceeds the connectivity threshold:
\lambda s^{2}>1
, where
\lambda:=(a+b)/2
. This threshold corresponds to the emergence of a giant component in the intersection graph
A\wedge B
.

More recent results
[
11
]
demonstrate that the presence of even a small seed set
\mathcal{R}
enables efficient, polynomial-time recovery. In particular, a neighborhood–overlap-based estimator can achieve exact recovery under the same information-theoretic threshold. When the seed set is large—e.g.,
|\mathcal{R}|=(1-o(1))n
—the effective threshold for recovery improves, and the corresponding algorithms become more robust to noise. For seeded, correlated Erdős–Rényi graphs, Wang et al.
[
18
]
consider the effect of the size of
\mathcal{R}
on the threshold for exact graph matching. Echoing the bound of
[
13
]
, they show that exact graph matching is possible at
\lambda s^{2}>1-\alpha
when
|\mathcal{U}|=n^{1-\alpha}
. Our theorems in the next section quantify these improvements precisely for both graph matching and community recovery tasks in the seeded correlated SBM.

As we consider fixed underlying community labels and permutations, we restrict our analysis to equivariant estimators that preclude the possibility of oracles. We first define equivariance for graph matching and community detection on the correlated SBM as follows:

Definition 3

(Equivariance)
.

Let
(A,B)\sim\scsbm(n,a,b,s,\mathcal{R},\sigma^{*},\pi^{*})
. Let
\Pi_{\mathcal{U}}
denote the set of bijections
[n]\to[n]
that act as the identity on
\mathcal{R}
. Then an estimator
\hat{\pi}
of
\pi^{*}
is permutation equivariant if, for any permutation
\tau\in\Pi_{\mathcal{U}}
of
\mathcal{U}
,

\hat{\pi}(A,B^{\tau},\pi_{\mathcal{R}},\sigma_{\mathcal{R}})=\tau\circ\hat{\pi}(A,B,\pi_{\mathcal{R}},\sigma_{\mathcal{R}})

where
B^{\tau}
denotes the matrix obtained by relabeling the vertices in
\mathcal{U}
by
\tau
while leaving the vertices in
\mathcal{R}
fixed and
(\tau\circ\hat{\pi})(u)=\tau(\hat{\pi}(u))
.
In addition,
\hat{\pi}
is label equivariant if for any relabeling
\ell:\{-1,+1\}\to\{-1,+1\}
of communities,

\hat{\pi}(A,B,\pi_{\mathcal{R}},\sigma_{\mathcal{R}}^{\ell})=\hat{\pi}(A,B,\pi_{\mathcal{R}},\sigma_{\mathcal{R}})

where we define
\sigma_{\mathcal{R}}^{\ell}:=\ell\circ\sigma_{\mathcal{R}}
.
Formally,
\hat{\pi}
is an equivariant estimator for
\pi^{*}
if it is both label and permutation equivariant.

3.
Description of the Algorithms

We present a sequence of four algorithms ranging from simple neighborhood–overlap heuristics to convex relaxations and their fast first-order approximations—that all exploit the revealed vertices
\mathcal{R}
. We will show that three of these algorithms achieve exact recovery in the almost fully seeded regime.

We begin with two simple estimators that exploit the revealed vertices
\mathcal{R}
as anchors. When
|\mathcal{R}|
is large, each unrevealed vertex
u\in\mathcal{U}
has a substantial number of edges into
\mathcal{R}
, and the intersection of these neighborhoods across the two graphs becomes highly informative. Algorithms
1
,
2
formalize this idea by assigning to each pair
(u,v)\in\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{U}
a score equal to the number of common neighbors in the revealed set. The resulting score matrix aggregates independent Bernoulli evidence across the seeds and admits a sharp concentration gap between the true match and all false candidates when
\lambda s^{2}>1-\alpha
. The matching is then obtained either by solving a linear assignment problem using the Hungarian algorithm or by greedy selection.

The preceding algorithms are based purely on combinatorial score maximization over a
\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{U}
score matrix. We now introduce a convex relaxation that places these heuristics within a broader optimization framework. Algorithm
3
formulates seeded graph matching as an
\ell_{1}

\|AD-DB\|_{1}

[
17
]

\ell_{1}

\mathcal{U}

\mathcal{U}

A,B

\pi_{\mathcal{R}}

\pi^{*}

4.
Proofs of the Main Results

We provide the threshold for exact recovery in Theorem
1
. We then show that Algorithms
1
,
2
, and

3
achieve this threshold. Algorithm
4
is a computationally efficient approximation to the LP relaxation, but we do not establish formal convergence or recovery guarantees for it.

4.1.
Lower Bound: Impossibility Below the Threshold

Previous proofs
[
3
]
show impossibility by comparing statistically indistinguishable permutations of the isolated nodes

\mathcal{I}:=\{u:u\in\mathcal{U},\sum_{v\in[n]}A(u,v)B(\pi^{*}(u),\pi^{*}(v))=0\}

i.e.,
u
has no neighbors in the intersection graph under the true alignment.

We simplify this argument in seeded graphs with a useful subset of nodes
\mathcal{J}\subset\mathcal{I}
that are difficult to match correctly. Intuitively,
\mathcal{J}
contains vertices whose observed adjacencies are symmetric under within-community swaps.

Definition 4

(Hard isolated vertices)
.

A node
u\in\mathcal{U}
is in
\mathcal{J}
if

(1)

u\in\mathcal{I}
.

(2)

A(u,v)=0
and
B(\pi^{*}(u),\pi^{*}(v))=0
for all
v\in\mathcal{U}
.

(3)

For all
w\in\mathcal{U}
and all
v\in\mathcal{R}
,

A(u,v)=1\implies B(w,\pi_{\mathcal{R}}(v))=0.

We start with a lemma on the overlap scores
\mathcal{S}(u,v)
for unrevealed vertices
u,v\in\mathcal{U}
.

Lemma 1
.

Let
(A,B)\sim\scsbm(n,a,b,s,\mathcal{R},\sigma^{*},\pi^{*})
be defined with
|\mathcal{U}|=n^{1-\alpha}
for some
\alpha\in[0,1]
. Then for any unrevealed
u,v\in\mathcal{U}
with
v\neq\pi^{*}(u)
, we have that for any
\varepsilon>0
, the size of the intersection neighborhood
\mathcal{S}(u,v)
satisfies

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{S}(u,v)\geq\varepsilon\log n\right)\leq n^{-\varepsilon\log n+o(\varepsilon\log n)}.

In addition, if
v=\pi^{*}(u)
, then for any
\varepsilon>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{S}(u,v)\leq\varepsilon\log n\right)\leq n^{-\lambda s^{2}+O(\varepsilon)}.

Proof.

Let
p_{a}=a\log n/n
and
p_{b}=b\log n/n
. The score for
u,v
in distinct communities is stochastically dominated by the score when
u,v
are in the same community as
(a^{2}+b^{2})>2ab
. As a result, when
v\neq\pi^{*}(u)
,
\mathcal{S}(u,v)\preceq\Bin(|\mathcal{R}|/2,p_{a}^{2}s^{2})+\Bin(|\mathcal{R}|/2,p_{b}^{2}s^{2})
. Taking a Chernoff bound for any
t>0

\displaystyle\log\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{S}(u,v)>\varepsilon\log n\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{ns^{2}}{2}(p_{a}^{2}+p_{b}^{2})(e^{t}-1)-\varepsilon t\log(n)

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{ns^{2}}{2}(p_{a}^{2}+p_{b}^{2})+\varepsilon\log n

\displaystyle\qquad-\varepsilon\log(n)\log\left(\frac{\varepsilon\log(n)}{ns^{2}(p_{a}^{2}+p_{b}^{2})/2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq-s^{2}(a^{2}+b^{2})\log^{2}(n)/2n+\varepsilon\log n

\displaystyle\qquad-\varepsilon\log(n)\log\left(\frac{\varepsilon n}{s^{2}(a^{2}+b^{2})/2\log n}\right)

\displaystyle=-\varepsilon\log^{2}(n)+o(\varepsilon\log^{2}n)

where
t
is optimized at
t^{*}=\log\left(\frac{\varepsilon\log(n)}{ns^{2}(p_{a}^{2}+p_{b}^{2})/2}\right)
.

The second statement follows from a similar Chernoff bound, where
\mathcal{S}(u,v)\preceq\Bin(|\mathcal{R}|/2,p_{a}s^{2})+\Bin(|\mathcal{R}|/2,p_{b}s^{2})
:

\displaystyle\log\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{S}(u,v)<\varepsilon\log n\right)

\displaystyle\leq ns^{2}(p_{a}+p_{b})(e^{t}-1)/2-\varepsilon t\log(n)

\displaystyle\leq-ns^{2}(p_{a}+p_{b})/2+\varepsilon\log n

\displaystyle\qquad-\varepsilon\log(n)\log\left(\frac{\varepsilon\log(n)}{ns^{2}(p_{a}+p_{b})/2}\right)

\displaystyle\leq-\lambda s^{2}\log(n)+\varepsilon\log n-\varepsilon\log(n)\log\left(\frac{\varepsilon}{\lambda s^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\lambda s^{2}\log(n)+O(\varepsilon\log n)

where
t
is evaluated at
t^{*}=\log\left(\frac{\varepsilon\log(n)}{ns^{2}(p_{a}+p_{b})/2}\right)=\log(\frac{\varepsilon}{\lambda s^{2}})
.
∎

We use this lemma to derive bounds on the sizes of
\mathcal{I}
and
\mathcal{J}
.

Lemma 2
.

\mathcal{I}
satisfies
|\mathcal{I}|=n^{1-\alpha-\lambda s^{2}+o(1)}
with probability
1-o(1)
.

Proof.

We bound the size of
\mathcal{I}
using the second moment method. We start with the mean. For
i\in\mathcal{U}
, let
S_{i}
be the indicator for node
i
being isolated in the intersection graph
A\wedge B
under the true permutation
\pi^{*}
. In addition, let
S:=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{U}}S_{i}=|\mathcal{I}|
. Then by linearity of expectation

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[S\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{E}\left[S_{i}\right]

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{U}|(1-s^{2}a\log n/n)^{n/2}(1-s^{2}b\log n/n)^{n/2}

\displaystyle\geq|\mathcal{U}|\exp\left(-\frac{s^{2}a/2\log n}{1-as^{2}\log n/n}\right)\cdot\exp\left(-\frac{s^{2}b/2\log n}{1-bs^{2}\log n/n}\right)

\displaystyle\geq n^{1-\alpha}\exp\left(-\frac{\lambda s^{2}\log n}{1-as^{2}\log n/n}\right)

\displaystyle=n^{1-\alpha}n^{-\lambda s^{2}\cdot(1+o(1))}

We now bound the second moment with exchangeability:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[S^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{E}\left[S_{i}^{2}\right]+\sum_{i\neq j\in\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{E}\left[S_{i}S_{j}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[S\right]+|\mathcal{U}|^{2}\mathbb{E}\left[S_{i}S_{j}\right]

It is then sufficient to bound
\mathbb{E}\left[S_{i}S_{j}\right]
. We note that the probability that
i,j
are both isolated can be bounded by

\mathbb{E}\left[S_{i}S_{j}\right]\leq(1-s^{2}a\log n/n)^{n-1}(1-s^{2}b\log n/n)^{n-1}

Using the inequality
1+x\leq e^{x}
gives an upper bound of

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[S_{i}S_{j}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-\frac{n-1}{n}(s^{2}a\log n+s^{2}b\log n)\right)=n^{-2(1+o(1))\lambda s^{2}}

We then see that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[S^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq|\mathcal{U}|n^{-\lambda s^{2}}+|\mathcal{U}|^{2}n^{-2(1+o(1))\lambda s^{2}}

\displaystyle=(1+o(1))\mathbb{E}\left[S\right]^{2}

Applying Chebyshev’s inequality lets us conclude

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|S-\mathbb{E}\left[S\right]|\geq\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left[S\right]\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4(\mathbb{E}\left[S^{2}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[S\right]^{2})}{\mathbb{E}\left[S\right]^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4((1+o(1))\mathbb{E}\left[S\right]^{2}-\mathbb{E}\left[S\right]^{2})}{\mathbb{E}\left[S\right]^{2}}

\displaystyle=o(1)

∎

Lemma 3
.

\mathcal{J}
satisfies
|\mathcal{J}|=n^{1-\alpha-\lambda s^{2}+o(1)}
with probability
1-o(1)
.

Proof.

Recall that
\mathcal{J}\subseteq\mathcal{I}
by definition. By Lemma
2
,
|\mathcal{I}|=n^{1-\alpha-\lambda s^{2}+o(1)}
with probability
1-o(1)
. Moreover, for a fixed
u\in\mathcal{U}
,
\mathbb{P}\left(u\in\mathcal{I}\right)=n^{-\lambda s^{2}+o(1)}
. Therefore, it suffices to show that
|\mathcal{I}\setminus\mathcal{J}|=o(|\mathcal{I}|)
with probability
1-o(1)
. Fix
u\in\mathcal{U}
and write
v^{*}=\pi^{*}(u)
. We bound the probability that
u\in\mathcal{I}\cap\mathcal{J}^{c}
. Given
u\in\mathcal{I}
, we have

A(u,x)B(v^{*},\pi^{*}(x))=0\quad\text{for all }x\in[n].

In particular, for any
x\in\mathcal{U}
, if
A(u,x)=1
, then necessarily
B(v^{*},\pi^{*}(x))=0
. Since the edges from
u
into
\mathcal{U}
occur with probability
\Theta(\log n/n)
and
|\mathcal{U}|=n^{1-\alpha}
, a union bound gives

\mathbb{P}\left(\exists x\in\mathcal{U}:\ A(u,x)=1\mid u\in\mathcal{I}\right)\leq O(n^{-\alpha}\log n)=o(1).

By symmetry,

\mathbb{P}\left(\exists x\in\mathcal{U}:\ B(v^{*},\pi^{*}(x))=1\mid u\in\mathcal{I}\right)=o(1).

Hence, uniformly in
u
,
\mathbb{P}\left(\text{Cond. (2) fails}\mid u\in\mathcal{I}\right)=o(1)
.

Let
S_{u}:=\{v\in\mathcal{R}:\ A(u,v)=1\}
be the seed neighborhood of
u
in
A
. Condition (3) requires that for every
v\in S_{u}
and every
w\in\mathcal{U}
,
B(w,\pi_{\mathcal{R}}(v))=0
. Conditioned on the revealed information, the events
\{B(w,\pi_{\mathcal{R}}(v))=0\}
for distinct edges
(w,\pi_{\mathcal{R}}(v))
are independent, with

\mathbb{P}\left(B(w,\pi_{\mathcal{R}}(v))=1\right)=\Theta(\log n/n).

Therefore, for some constant
c>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\text{Cond. (3) holds}\mid S_{u}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{v\in S_{u}}\prod_{w\in\mathcal{U}}\mathbb{P}\left(B(w,\pi_{\mathcal{R}}(v))=0\right)

\displaystyle\geq\exp\left(-c\,|S_{u}|\,|\mathcal{U}|\,\log(n)/n\right)

Conditional on
u\in\mathcal{I}
, a Chernoff bound gives
|S_{u}|=\Theta(\log n)
with probability
1-o(1)
. Then for some
c_{1}>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\text{Cond. (3) holds}\mid u\in\mathcal{I}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\exp\left(-c_{1}n^{-\alpha}\log^{2}n\right)=1-o(1).

We then have uniformly over
u\in\mathcal{U}
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(u\in\mathcal{I}\setminus\mathcal{J}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(u\in\mathcal{I}\right)\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(\text{Cond. (3) fails}\mid u\in\mathcal{I}\right)=n^{-\lambda s^{2}+o(1)}\cdot o(1).

By linearity of expectation,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[|\mathcal{I}\setminus\mathcal{J}|\right]

\displaystyle=|\mathcal{U}|\mathbb{P}\left(u\in\mathcal{I}\setminus\mathcal{J}\right)

\displaystyle=n^{1-\alpha}\cdot n^{-\lambda s^{2}+o(1)}\cdot o(1)

\displaystyle=o\!\left(n^{1-\alpha-\lambda s^{2}}\right).

By Markov,
|\mathcal{I}\setminus\mathcal{J}|=o(|\mathcal{I}|)
with probability
1-o(1)
. Since
|\mathcal{J}|=|\mathcal{I}|-|\mathcal{I}\setminus\mathcal{J}|
, we conclude that
|\mathcal{J}|=n^{1-\alpha-\lambda s^{2}+o(1)}
with probability
1-o(1)
.
∎

Lemma 4

(Transposition invariance on
\mathcal{J}
)
.

Fix distinct
u,v\in\mathcal{U}
with
\sigma^{*}(u)=\sigma^{*}(v)
, and let
\tau
be the transposition
swapping
u
and
v
and fixing all other vertices. Conditional on the revealed information

(\pi_{\mathcal{R}},\sigma_{\mathcal{R}})
and on the event
\{u,v\in\mathcal{J}\}
, we have

(A,B,\pi_{\mathcal{R}},\sigma_{\mathcal{R}})\ \stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\ (A,B^{\tau},\pi_{\mathcal{R}},\sigma_{\mathcal{R}}).

Proof.

Let
\widetilde{B}:=P_{\pi_{\mathcal{R}}}^{\top}BP_{\pi_{\mathcal{R}}}
be the seed-aligned version of
B
. Since
\tau
fixes
\mathcal{R}
,
\tau
commutes with this seed-alignment, so it suffices to prove

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(A=A_{0},\widetilde{B}=\widetilde{B}_{0}\mid\pi_{\mathcal{R}},\sigma_{\mathcal{R}},u,v\in\mathcal{J}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(A=A_{0},\widetilde{B}=\widetilde{B}_{0}^{\tau}\mid\pi_{\mathcal{R}},\sigma_{\mathcal{R}},u,v\in\mathcal{J}\right).

Consider the joint distribution of the edge indicators
observable to the estimator: all edges in
A
and all edges in
\widetilde{B}
with at least one endpoint in

\mathcal{R}\cup\{u,v\}
. Under the model, as
(\sigma^{*},\pi^{*})
are fixed, these edge variables are mutually independent across unordered vertex pairs.

We check invariance of each likelihood factor under swapping
u
and
v
. We first consider edges not incident to
u
or
v
. If
\{i,j\}\cap\{u,v\}=\emptyset
, then
\widetilde{B}_{0}^{\tau}(i,j)=\widetilde{B}_{0}(i,j)
, so the corresponding likelihood factor is unchanged.

Next, we consider edges between
u
or
v
and an unrevealed vertex. For
x\in\mathcal{U}\setminus\{u,v\}
, condition (2) in the definition of
\mathcal{J}
implies

A(u,x)=A(v,x)=0,\qquad\widetilde{B}(u,x)=\widetilde{B}(v,x)=0,

so the likelihood contribution of the pair of edges
\{(u,x),(v,x)\}
is invariant under
\tau
.

After this, we consider the edge
(u,v)
. This edge is fixed by the transposition, so its likelihood factor is unchanged.

Finally, we consider the edges between
u
or
v
and a revealed vertex. Fix
r\in\mathcal{R}
and consider the tuple

(A(u,r),A(v,r),\widetilde{B}(u,r),\widetilde{B}(v,r)).

Since
\sigma^{*}(u)=\sigma^{*}(v)
and
\sigma^{*}(r)
is revealed, the model assigns the same parameters to
edges
(u,r)
and
(v,r)
. Thus, before conditioning on
u,v\in\mathcal{J}
, this quadruple is exchangeable under
swapping
u
and
v
.

We now condition on the event
\{u,v\in\mathcal{J}\}
and distinguish cases according to
the values of
A(u,r)
and
A(v,r)
:

(1)

A(u,r)=A(v,r)=1
.

By condition (3) in the definition of
\mathcal{J}
, this forces

\widetilde{B}(u,r)=\widetilde{B}(v,r)=0,

so the likelihood contribution is unchanged under the transposition
\tau
.

(2)

A(u,r)=1
and
A(v,r)=0
.
Again by condition (3), the event
A(u,r)=1
implies

\widetilde{B}(w,r)=0\quad\text{for all }w\in\mathcal{U},

and in particular
\widetilde{B}(u,r)=\widetilde{B}(v,r)=0
.
Thus the contribution is invariant under swapping
u
and
v
.

(3)

A(u,r)=0
and
A(v,r)=1
.
This case is symmetric to the previous one and yields the same conclusion.

(4)

A(u,r)=A(v,r)=0
.
In this case, condition (3) imposes no further restriction.
However, the event
\{u,v\in\mathcal{J},\ \sigma^{*}(u)=\sigma^{*}(v)\}
is symmetric in
u
and
v
,
and the model assigns identical parameters to the edges
(u,r)
and
(v,r)
.
Therefore, the conditional distribution of

(\widetilde{B}(u,r),\widetilde{B}(v,r))

remains exchangeable under swapping
u
and
v
, and the contribution
is invariant under the transposition
\tau
.

Combining cases (1)–(4) and using independence across unordered pairs, the full conditional likelihood of

(A,\widetilde{B})
is invariant under
\widetilde{B}\mapsto\widetilde{B}^{\tau}
.
∎

The resulting lower bound on recovery follows from the lemmas above:

Theorem 1
.

Let
|\mathcal{U}|=n^{1-\alpha}
for some
\alpha\in[0,1]
. If
\lambda s^{2}<1-\alpha
, no equivariant estimator can succeed with probability exceeding
1/2+o(1)
.

Proof.

We note that when
|\mathcal{U}|=\Theta(n)
then the bound reduces to the classical case of
\lambda s^{2}<1
in
[
3
]
.

Assume
\lambda s^{2}<1-\alpha
. By Lemma
3
, with probability
1-o(1)
we have
|\mathcal{J}|\to\infty
. In particular, there exist two distinct vertices
u\neq v
in the same community contained in
\mathcal{J}
. Let
\tau
be the within-community transposition swapping
u
and
v
and fixing all other vertices. Consider the alternative permutation
\pi^{\prime}:=\tau\circ\pi^{*}
, which preserves community labels and agrees with
\pi^{*}
on
[n]\setminus\{u,v\}
.

Let
\hat{\pi}
be any permutation-equivariant estimator of the true permutation
\pi^{*}
. By equivariance, for every realization over the transposition
\tau

\hat{\pi}(A,B^{\tau},\pi_{\mathcal{R}},\sigma_{\mathcal{R}})=\tau\circ\hat{\pi}(A,B,\pi_{\mathcal{R}},\sigma_{\mathcal{R}}).

Moreover, by Lemma
4
conditional on
(\pi_{\mathcal{R}},\sigma_{\mathcal{R}})
and on the event

\{u,v\in\mathcal{J},\ \sigma^{*}(u)=\sigma^{*}(v)\}
, we have

(A,B,\pi_{\mathcal{R}},\sigma_{\mathcal{R}})\;\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\;(A,B^{\tau},\pi_{\mathcal{R}},\sigma_{\mathcal{R}}).

Using the distributional symmetry above,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{\pi}=\pi^{*}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{\pi}(A,B,\pi_{\mathcal{R}},\sigma_{\mathcal{R}})=\pi^{*}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{\pi}(A,B^{\tau},\pi_{\mathcal{R}},\sigma_{\mathcal{R}})=\pi^{*}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\tau\circ\hat{\pi}(A,B,\pi_{\mathcal{R}},\sigma_{\mathcal{R}})=\pi^{*}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{\pi}=\tau^{-1}\circ\pi^{*}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{\pi}=\tau\circ\pi^{*}\right),

since
\tau^{-1}=\tau
. The events
\{\hat{\pi}=\pi^{*}\}
and
\{\hat{\pi}=\tau\circ\pi^{*}\}
are
disjoint because
u\neq v
, hence

1\geq\ \mathbb{P}\left(\hat{\pi}=\pi^{*}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{\pi}=\tau\circ\pi^{*}\right)\ =\ 2\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{\pi}=\pi^{*}\right).

Therefore
\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{\pi}=\pi^{*}\right)\leq 1/2
on the event
\mathcal{E}
that a pair
u\neq v\in\mathcal{J}
exists with
\sigma^{*}(u)=\sigma^{*}(v)
. Since
\mathcal{E}
holds with probability
1-o(1)
when
\lambda s^{2}<1-\alpha
, we conclude

\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{\pi}=\pi^{*}\right)\ \leq\ \tfrac{1}{2}+o(1),

as claimed.
∎

4.2.
Upper Bound: Algorithmic Achievability

We first state a bound on the success rate for exact graph matching using the Hungarian algorithm for the
\scsbm
, extending the bounds from
[
15
]
:

Theorem 2
.

Let
(A,B)\sim\scsbm(n,a,b,s,\mathcal{R},\sigma^{*},\pi^{*})
be defined with
|\mathcal{U}|=n^{1-\alpha}
for some
\alpha\in[0,1]
. Then setting
\lambda=\frac{a+b}{2}
, if
\lambda s^{2}>1-\alpha+\varepsilon
for some
\varepsilon>0
, Algorithm
1
returns an estimator
\hat{\pi}
in polynomial time satisfying

\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{\pi}=\pi^{*}\right)=1-o(1).

Proof.

Constructing all overlaps
\mathcal{S}(u,v)=|\mathcal{N}_{A}\left(u\right)\cap\mathcal{N}_{B}\left(v\right)|
takes

O(|\mathcal{U}|\cdot|\mathcal{R}|)
time, and the Hungarian algorithm takes
O(|\mathcal{U}|^{3})
time.

Let
\hat{\pi}
have
k
misaligned vertices for
2\leq k\leq|\mathcal{U}|
. As
\hat{\pi}
is returned by the Hungarian method, necessarily

(1)

\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\mathcal{S}(u,\hat{\pi}(u))\geq\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\mathcal{S}(u,\pi^{*}(u)).

Crucially, for each misaligned
u
, the random variables

\mathcal{S}(u,\hat{\pi}(u)),\qquad\mathcal{S}(u,\pi^{*}(u))

are sums of
(1-o(1))n
Bernoulli variables indexed by the seeds
\mathcal{R}
. Moreover, for distinct misaligned vertices
u_{i}
, the collections of Bernoulli variables
entering
\mathcal{S}(u_{i},\hat{\pi}(u_{i}))
and
\mathcal{S}(u_{i},\pi^{*}(u_{i}))
are disjoint and thus conditionally independent given
(\mathcal{R},\pi^{*})
. Hence each expression in (
1
) is a sum of
\Theta(k|\mathcal{R}|)
independent Bernoulli variables conditional on the revealed set
\mathcal{R}
and the true permutation
\pi^{*}
. Let
u_{1},\ldots u_{k}
be the vertices on which
\hat{\pi}
and
\pi^{*}
disagree.

By a Chernoff bound on
\Theta(k|\mathcal{R}|)
variables analogous to Lemma
1
, the error rate given
k
misaligned vertices can be bounded for any
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\mathcal{S}(u,\hat{\pi}(u))\geq\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\mathcal{S}(u,\pi^{*}(u))\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathcal{S}(u_{i},\hat{\pi}(u_{i}))\geq\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathcal{S}(u_{i},\pi^{*}(u_{i}))\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathcal{S}(u_{i},\hat{\pi}(u_{i}))>\varepsilon^{\prime}\log n\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\ \mathbb{P}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathcal{S}(u_{i},\pi^{*}(u_{i}))<\varepsilon^{\prime}\log n\right)

\displaystyle\leq n^{-\varepsilon^{\prime}\log n+o(\varepsilon^{\prime}\log n)}+n^{-k\lambda s^{2}+o(\varepsilon^{\prime})}

\displaystyle\quad=n^{-k\lambda s^{2}+o(\varepsilon^{\prime})}

Letting
\varepsilon^{\prime}\to 0
, gives an upper bound of
n^{-k\lambda s^{2}+o(1)}
, allowing us to upper-bound the probability of seeing exactly
k
errors by

\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{\pi}\text{ has exactly }k\text{ errors}\right)\leq n^{-k\lambda s^{2}+o(1)}.

There are
\binom{|\mathcal{U}|}{k}(k!-1)\leq n^{k(1-\alpha)+o(k)}
such permutations, so

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{\pi}\neq\pi^{*}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k\geq 1}n^{k(1-\alpha)}n^{-k\lambda s^{2}+o(k)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 1}n^{-k(\lambda s^{2}-(1-\alpha))+o(k)}.

If
\lambda s^{2}>(1-\alpha)+\varepsilon
then every term is at most
n^{-k\varepsilon+o(k)}
, and
the series is dominated by its first term:

\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{\pi}\neq\pi^{*}\right)=n^{-\varepsilon+o(1)}=o(1).\qed

We now show that the greedy algorithm achieves the threshold for exact recovery in the almost fully seeded case.

Theorem 3
.

Let
(A,B)\sim\scsbm(n,a,b,s,\mathcal{R},\sigma^{*},\pi^{*})
be defined with
|\mathcal{U}|=n^{1-\alpha}
for some
\alpha\in[0,1]
. Then setting
\lambda=\frac{a+b}{2}
, if
\lambda s^{2}>(1-\alpha)+\varepsilon
for some
\varepsilon>0
, Algorithm
2
returns an estimator
\hat{\pi}
in polynomial time such that

\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{\pi}=\pi^{*}\right)=1-o(1).

Proof.

Computing all scores
\mathcal{S}(u,v)
takes
O(|\mathcal{U}|\cdot|\mathcal{R}|)
time and sorting the multiset

\{\mathcal{S}(u,v)\colon u,v\in\mathcal{U}\}
takes
O(|\mathcal{U}|^{2}\log|\mathcal{U}|)
, giving total runtime polynomial in
n
.

Fix any output
\hat{\pi}\neq\pi^{*}
and define the error permutation

\rho:=\hat{\pi}\circ(\pi^{*})^{-1}.

Then
\rho
decomposes uniquely into
\ell
disjoint nontrivial cycles,

\rho=C_{1}\circ\cdots\circ C_{\ell},\qquad 1\leq\ell\leq|\mathcal{U}|/2,

and
u\in\mathcal{U}
is misaligned iff
u
lies in one of the cycles.
We now show that each cycle
C_{i}
contributes at least one vertex for which the greedy algorithm selects an incorrect pair before the correct pair becomes unavailable.

Fix a cycle
C_{i}
and write it as

C_{i}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}),\qquad k\geq 2,

so that
\rho(u_{j})=u_{j+1}
for
j=1,\ldots,k-1
and
\rho(u_{k})=u_{1}
.
Define

v_{j}:=\pi^{*}(u_{j}),\qquad\hat{\pi}(u_{j})=v_{j+1}.

Consider the moment when Algorithm
2
selects the first vertex from
C_{i}
.
Suppose the first such vertex is
u_{j}
.
At this time all
u_{1},\ldots,u_{k}
are unmatched, and all
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}
are also unmatched, so both candidate pairs

(u_{j},v_{j+1})=(u_{j},\hat{\pi}(u_{j}))\qquad\text{and}\qquad(u_{j},v_{j})=(u_{j},\pi^{*}(u_{j}))

are feasible for matching.

Since the greedy algorithm processes edges in nonincreasing order of scores and chooses
(u_{j},\hat{\pi}(u_{j}))
at this moment, we must have

(2)

\mathcal{S}(u_{j},\hat{\pi}(u_{j}))\geq\mathcal{S}(u_{j},\pi^{*}(u_{j})).

Because cycles are disjoint, this argument applies independently to each
C_{i}
, yielding vertices
u_{i}\in C_{i}
satisfying (
2
).

Let
\Pi^{\ell}
denote the set of estimators
\hat{\pi}
such that
\hat{\pi}\circ(\pi^{*})^{-1}
has
\ell
many distinct cycles. From the argument above, the event
\{\hat{\pi}\in\Pi^{\ell}\}
implies the existence of distinct vertices

u_{1},\ldots,u_{\ell}\in\mathcal{U}

such that

\mathcal{S}(u_{i},\hat{\pi}(u_{i}))\geq\mathcal{S}(u_{i},\pi^{*}(u_{i})),\qquad i=1,\ldots,\ell.

By Lemma
1
, for each fixed
u\in\mathcal{U}
and
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
, the score comparison above has probability

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{S}(u,\hat{\pi}(u))\geq\mathcal{S}(u,\pi^{*}(u))\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{S}(u,\hat{\pi}(u))\geq\varepsilon^{\prime}\log n\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{S}(u,\pi^{*}(u))<\varepsilon^{\prime}\log n\right)

\displaystyle=n^{-\varepsilon^{\prime}\log n+o(\varepsilon^{\prime}\log n)}+n^{-\lambda s^{2}+o(\varepsilon^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=n^{-\lambda s^{2}+o(\varepsilon^{\prime})}

Taking a sequence of
\varepsilon^{\prime}\rightarrow 0
, we then get an upper bound of
n^{-\lambda s^{2}+o(1)}
.

Next, for a fixed estimator
\hat{\pi}
with
\ell
cycles, the scores of different mismatched
u_{i}
involve disjoint vertex sets and are conditionally independent given
\mathcal{R}
. As a result, for any distinct
u_{1},\ldots,u_{\ell}
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{\ell}\{\mathcal{S}(u_{i},\hat{\pi}(u_{i}))\geq\mathcal{S}(u_{i},\pi^{*}(u_{i}))\}\right)\leq n^{-\ell\lambda s^{2}+o(\ell)}.

There are at most
|\mathcal{U}|^{\ell}=n^{\ell(1-\alpha)}
possible
\ell
–tuples of distinct vertices.
Summing over them,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{\pi}\in\Pi^{\ell}\right)

\displaystyle\leq n^{\ell(1-\alpha)}\cdot n^{-\ell\lambda s^{2}+o(\ell)}=n^{-\ell(\lambda s^{2}-(1-\alpha))+o(\ell)}.

Using the assumed gap
\lambda s^{2}>(1-\alpha)+\varepsilon
, this gives

\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{\pi}\in\Pi^{\ell}\right)\leq n^{-\ell\varepsilon+o(\ell)}.

Summing over all possible numbers of cycles:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{\pi}\neq\pi^{*}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{|\mathcal{U}|/2}\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{\pi}\in\Pi^{\ell}\right)\leq\sum_{\ell=1}^{|\mathcal{U}|/2}n^{-\ell\varepsilon+o(\ell)}=n^{-\varepsilon+o(1)}=o(1).

Therefore, Algorithm
2
recovers
\pi^{*}
with probability
1-o(1)
.
∎

We show a lemma separating the objective function in Algorithm
3
into three sets of terms: edges between revealed nodes, edges across revealed and unrevealed nodes, and edges between unrevealed nodes.

Lemma 5
.

Let
(A,B)\sim\scsbm(n,a,b,s,\mathcal{R},\sigma^{*},\pi^{*})
and consider Algorithm
3
. Then the objective can be rewritten as

\|AD-DB\|_{1}=C-\Psi(D_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U}})+\Phi(D_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U}}),

where
D_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U}}
is restricted to the unrevealed nodes
\;\mathcal{U}
,
\Phi(D_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U}})=\sum_{u,v\in\mathcal{U}}\Bigl|(AD-DB)_{uv}\Bigr|
,

\Psi(D_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U}})=2\sum_{u,v\in\mathcal{U}}D_{uv}\mathcal{S}(u,v)
,
C>0
is a constant and
\Pi_{\mathcal{U}}
denotes permutations on
[n]
that fix
\mathcal{R}
.

Proof.

For any feasible
(D,Y)
, replacing
Y
by
|AD-DB|
entrywise preserves feasibility and does not increase the objective, so the LP objective equals

f(D)=\|AD-DB\|_{1}.

We partition indices into
\mathcal{R}
and
\mathcal{U}
. Since
D_{r,\pi_{\mathcal{R}}(r)}=1
for all
r\in\mathcal{R}
, terms involving only rows/columns in
\mathcal{R}
are constant in
D_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U}}
. Thus

f(D)=C_{0}+\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\Bigl|A_{ur}-\sum_{v\in\mathcal{U}}D_{uv}B_{v,\pi_{\mathcal{R}}(r)}\Bigr|+\Phi(D_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U}}),

where
C_{0}
is constant and
\Phi(\cdot)
collects the terms indexed only by
\mathcal{U}\times\mathcal{U}
.

Fix
u\in\mathcal{U}
and
r\in\mathcal{R}
. Since
A_{ur}\in\{0,1\}
, for any
x\in[0,1]
we have

|A_{ur}-x|=A_{ur}+x-2A_{ur}x.

Therefore

\displaystyle\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\Bigl|A_{ur}-\sum_{v\in\mathcal{U}}D_{uv}B_{v,\pi_{\mathcal{R}}(r)}\Bigr|=\deg_{A,\mathcal{R}}(u)+\sum_{v\in\mathcal{U}}D_{uv}\left(\deg_{B,\pi_{\mathcal{R}}}(v)-2\mathcal{S}(u,v)\right),

and summing over
u\in\mathcal{U}
gives

\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\sum_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\Bigl|A_{ur}-\sum_{v\in\mathcal{U}}D_{uv}B_{v,\pi_{\mathcal{R}}(r)}\Bigr|=C_{1}-2\sum_{u,v\in\mathcal{U}}D_{uv}\mathcal{S}(u,v),

with
C_{1}
constant. Hence

f(D)=C-\Psi(D_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U}})+\Phi(D_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U}}),

for a constant
C
, where
\Phi(D_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U}})=\sum_{u,v\in\mathcal{U}}\Bigl|(AD-DB)_{uv}\Bigr|
and

\Psi(D_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U}})=2\sum_{u,v\in\mathcal{U}}D_{uv}\mathcal{S}(u,v)
.
∎

We now show a concentration result on the unrevealed component of the objective in Algorithm
3
.

Lemma 6
.

Let
D
be a right–stochastic matrix,
D^{*}
be the permutation corresponding to the true matching for
A,B
, and let
\Phi(D):=\sum_{u,v\in\mathcal{U}}\Bigl|(AD-DB)_{uv}\Bigr|
. We now select an arbitrary vertex
x\in\mathcal{U}
and define
D^{x}(x,\cdot)=D^{*}(x,\cdot)
and
D^{x}=D
otherwise. Here
D^{x}
is obtained from
D
by replacing the
x
-th row with the
corresponding row of the true permutation matrix
D^{*}
. In addition, let
d,d^{*}
be the rows of
x
in
D,D^{x}
respectively. Then with probability 1-o(1):

\frac{|\Phi(D^{x})-\Phi(D)|}{\|d-d^{*}\|_{1}}\leq C\left(\deg_{A,\mathcal{U}}(x)+\max_{v}\deg_{B,\mathcal{U}}(v)\right)

for some constant
C>0
.

Proof.

We first expand
\Phi(D),\Phi(D^{x})
to get:

\displaystyle\Phi(D)

\displaystyle=\sum_{u,v\in\mathcal{U}}|(AD-DB)_{uv}|=\sum_{u,v\in\mathcal{U}}\Bigl|\sum_{w}(A_{uw}D_{wv}-D_{uw}B_{wv})\Bigr|.

\displaystyle\Phi(D^{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{u,v\in\mathcal{U}}|AD^{x}-D^{x}B|_{uv}=\sum_{u,v\in\mathcal{U}}\Bigl|\sum_{w}(A_{uw}D^{x}_{wv}-D^{x}_{uw}B_{wv})\Bigr|.

We then split
\Phi(D)-\Phi(D^{x})
into distinct rowwise cases of
u=x
and
u\neq x
.

\displaystyle\Phi(D)-\Phi(D^{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{u,v\in\mathcal{U}}\left(|(AD-DB)_{uv}|-|AD^{x}-D^{x}B|_{uv}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{u\neq x,v\in\mathcal{U}}\left(|(AD-DB)_{uv}|-|AD^{x}-D^{x}B|_{uv}\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{u=x,v\in\mathcal{U}}\left(|(AD-DB)_{uv}|-|AD^{x}-D^{x}B|_{uv}\right)

For
u\neq x
, we note that

\displaystyle\sum_{u\neq x}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{U}}\Bigl(|(AD-DB)_{uv}|-|(AD^{x}-D^{x}B)_{uv}|\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{u\neq x}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{U}}\Bigl(|(AD)_{uv}-(DB)_{uv}|-|(AD^{x})_{uv}-(D^{x}B)_{uv}|\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{u\neq x}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{U}}\Bigl(|(AD)_{uv}-(AD^{x})_{uv}|+|(DB)_{uv}-(D^{x}B)_{uv}|\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{u\neq x}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{U}}\Bigl(|(A(D-D^{x}))_{uv}|+|((D-D^{x})B)_{uv}|\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{u\neq x}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{U}}\Bigl(\Bigl|\sum_{w}A_{uw}(D-D^{x})_{wv}\Bigr|+\Bigl|\sum_{w}(D-D^{x})_{uw}B_{wv}\Bigr|\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{u\neq x}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{U}}\Bigl(|A_{ux}(D-D^{x})_{xv}|+|(D-D^{x})_{ux}B_{xv}|\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{u\neq x}\sum_{v\in\mathcal{U}}A_{ux}\,|D_{xv}-D^{x}_{xv}|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}}A_{ux}\,\|d-d^{*}\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\deg_{A,\mathcal{U}}(x)\,\|d-d^{*}\|_{1}.

Then for
u=x
, we get that

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in\mathcal{U}}\Bigl(|(AD-DB)_{xv}|-|(AD^{x}-D^{x}B)_{xv}|\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{v\in\mathcal{U}}\Bigl(|(AD)_{xv}-(AD^{x})_{xv}|+|(DB)_{xv}-(D^{x}B)_{xv}|\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{U}}\Bigl(|(A(D-D^{x}))_{xv}|+|((D-D^{x})B)_{xv}|\Bigr)

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{U}}\Bigl(\Bigl|\sum_{w}A_{xw}(D-D^{x})_{wv}\Bigr|+\Bigl|\sum_{w}(D-D^{x})_{xw}B_{wv}\Bigr|\Bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{v\in\mathcal{U}}\sum_{w}A_{xw}\,|(D-D^{x})_{wv}|+\sum_{v\in\mathcal{U}}\sum_{w}|(D-D^{x})_{xw}|\,B_{wv}

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{U}}A_{xx}\,|D_{xv}-D^{x}_{xv}|+\sum_{w}|D_{xw}-D^{x}_{xw}|\sum_{v\in\mathcal{U}}B_{wv}

\displaystyle=A_{xx}\,\|d-d^{*}\|_{1}+\sum_{w}|D_{xw}-D^{x}_{xw}|\,\deg_{B,\mathcal{U}}(w)

\displaystyle\leq A_{xx}\,\|d-d^{*}\|_{1}+\|d-d^{*}\|_{1}\max_{w}\deg_{B,\mathcal{U}}(w)

\displaystyle\leq\|d-d^{*}\|_{1}\bigl(1+\max_{w}\deg_{B,\mathcal{U}}(w)\bigr).

As
\deg_{A,\mathcal{U}}(u),\deg_{B,\mathcal{U}}(u)=\Theta(|\mathcal{U}|a\log n/n)=\Theta(n^{-\alpha}\log n)
with probability
1-o(1)
, we can choose
C=2
on the degree-concentration event to get

\frac{|\Phi(D^{x})-\Phi(D)|}{\|d-d^{*}\|_{1}}\leq C\left(\deg_{A,\mathcal{U}}(x)+\max_{v}\deg_{B,\mathcal{U}}(v)\right).

as desired.
∎

Next, we show a generalization of Lemma
1
comparing convex combinations of scores.

Lemma 7
.

Let
(A,B)\sim\scsbm(n,a,b,s,\mathcal{R},\sigma^{*},\pi^{*})
be defined with
|\mathcal{U}|=n^{1-\alpha}
for some
\alpha\in[0,1]
. Fix
x\in\mathcal{U}
and write
v^{*}=\pi^{*}(x)
.
For any probability vector
d=(d_{v})_{v\in\mathcal{U}}
supported on
\mathcal{U}\setminus\{v^{*}\}
, define

S_{d}(x):=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{U}}d_{v}\mathcal{S}(x,v).

Then there exists a constant
c_{0}>0
such that

\mathbb{P}\left(S_{d}(x)\leq\mathcal{S}(x,v^{*})-c_{0}\log n\right)\geq 1-n^{-\lambda s^{2}+o(1)}

uniformly over all such
d
.
Consequently, if
\lambda s^{2}>1-\alpha
, the inequality above holds simultaneously for all
x\in\mathcal{U}
and all such
d
with probability
1-o(1)
.

Proof.

Fix
x\in\mathcal{U}
and write
v^{*}=\pi^{*}(x)
. Choose constants

0<\varepsilon_{1}<\varepsilon_{2}<\lambda s^{2},

to be specified below, and consider the two events

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{true}}(x):=\{\mathcal{S}(x,v^{*})\geq\varepsilon_{2}\log n\},

\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{false}}(x):=\Big\{\max_{v\in\mathcal{U}\setminus\{v^{*}\}}\mathcal{S}(x,v)\leq\varepsilon_{1}\log n\Big\}.

By Lemma
1
for the true matching
v^{*}
with
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{2}
,

\mathbb{P}\left(E_{\mathrm{true}}(x)^{c}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{S}(x,v^{*})\leq\varepsilon_{2}\log n\right)\leq n^{-\lambda s^{2}+O(\varepsilon_{2})}.

For any false match
v\neq v^{*}
, Lemma
1
with
\varepsilon=\varepsilon_{1}
gives

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{S}(x,v)\geq\varepsilon_{1}\log n\right)\leq n^{-\varepsilon_{1}\log n+o(\varepsilon_{1}\log n)}.

Taking a union bound over the at most
|\mathcal{U}|\leq n^{1-\alpha}
false candidates yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(E_{\mathrm{false}}(x)^{c}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{v\in\mathcal{U}\setminus\{v^{*}\}}\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{S}(x,v)\geq\varepsilon_{1}\log n\right)

\displaystyle\leq|\mathcal{U}|\cdot n^{-\varepsilon_{1}\log n+o(\varepsilon_{1}\log n)}

\displaystyle=n^{1-\alpha-\varepsilon_{1}\log n+o(\varepsilon_{1}\log n)}

On the event
E_{\mathrm{true}}(x)\cap E_{\mathrm{false}}(x)
, for any probability vector
d=(d_{v})_{v\in\mathcal{U}}
supported on
\mathcal{U}\setminus\{v^{*}\}
we have

S_{d}(x)=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{U}}d_{v}\mathcal{S}(x,v)\leq\max_{v\neq v^{*}}\mathcal{S}(x,v)\leq\varepsilon_{1}\log n,

and simultaneously
\mathcal{S}(x,v^{*})\geq\varepsilon_{2}\log n
. Hence

S_{d}(x)\leq\mathcal{S}(x,v^{*})-(\varepsilon_{2}-\varepsilon_{1})\log n.

Therefore the desired inequality holds with
c_{0}:=\varepsilon_{2}-\varepsilon_{1}>0
, and moreover

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(S_{d}(x)\leq\mathcal{S}(x,v^{*})-c_{0}\log n\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\geq 1-\mathbb{P}\left(E_{\mathrm{true}}(x)^{c}\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(E_{\mathrm{false}}(x)^{c}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=1-n^{-\lambda s^{2}+o(1)},

uniformly over all such
d
.

Finally, a union bound over
x\in\mathcal{U}
gives a failure probability at most

|\mathcal{U}|\cdot n^{-\lambda s^{2}+o(1)}=n^{1-\alpha-\lambda s^{2}+o(1)},

which is
o(1)
when
\lambda s^{2}>1-\alpha
. (For concreteness one may take
\varepsilon_{2}=\tfrac{3}{4}\lambda s^{2}
and
\varepsilon_{1}=\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda s^{2}
, so
c_{0}=\tfrac{1}{4}\lambda s^{2}
.)
∎

We use the lemmas above to show that Algorithm
3
achieves the threshold for exact recovery.

Theorem 4
.

Let
(A,B)\sim\scsbm(n,a,b,s,\mathcal{R},\sigma^{*},\pi^{*})
be defined with
|\mathcal{U}|=n^{1-\alpha}
for some
\alpha\in[0,1]
. Then setting
\lambda=\frac{a+b}{2}
, if
\lambda s^{2}>(1-\alpha)+\varepsilon
for some
\varepsilon>0
, Algorithm
3
returns an estimator
\hat{\pi}
in polynomial time such that

\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{\pi}=\pi^{*}\right)=1-o(1).

Proof.

By Lemma
5
, for any feasible
D
with the seed rows fixed the LP objective is

f(D):=\|AD-DB\|_{1}=C-\Psi(D_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U}})+\Phi(D_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U}}),

where
C>0
is a constant independent of
D_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U}}
,

\displaystyle\Psi(D_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U}})=2\sum_{u,v\in\mathcal{U}}D_{uv}\mathcal{S}(u,v),\qquad\Phi(D_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U}})=\sum_{u,v\in\mathcal{U}}\bigl|(AD-DB)_{uv}\bigr|.

So minimizing
f(D)
is equivalent to balancing the revealed-unrevealed interaction term
-\Psi
(which prefers large scores) against the within-unrevealed interaction term
\Phi
.

Let
D^{*}
denote the permutation matrix associated with the true matching
\pi^{*}
(with the seed rows fixed). Our goal is to show that, with high probability, every minimizer
D
of
f
has
D_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U}}=D^{*}_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U}}
; then Algorithm
3
recovers
\pi^{*}
.

Fix any feasible bistochastic
D
(respecting the seed constraints) and any vertex
x\in\mathcal{U}
. Let

d:=D_{x,\cdot},\qquad d^{*}:=D^{*}_{x,\cdot}=e_{\pi^{*}(x)}.

Define
D^{x}
to be the matrix obtained from
D
by replacing the
x
-th row with
d^{*}
.Note that
D^{x}
need not be feasible, since the column-sum constraints on
\mathcal{U}
may be violated.

We compare
f(D)
to
f(D^{x})
for each
x\in\mathcal{U}
and show that whenever
d\neq d^{*}
, then
f(D)>f(D^{x})
. Iterating this comparison over all
x\in\mathcal{U}
yields a chain of inequalities

f(D)>f(D^{x_{1}})>f(D^{x_{2}})>\cdots>f(D^{*}),

where
D^{*}
is the true permutation matrix. Thus, although the intermediate matrices
D^{x_{i}}
need not be feasible, they serve only to certify strict descent of the objective toward the feasible point
D^{*}
. A direct computation using Lemma
5
gives

\displaystyle f(D)-f(D^{x})

\displaystyle=\bigl[C-\Psi(D_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U}})+\Phi(D_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U}})\bigr]-\bigl[C-\Psi(D^{x}_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U}})+\Phi(D^{x}_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U}})\bigr]

\displaystyle=\bigl[\Psi(D^{x}_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U}})-\Psi(D_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U}})\bigr]+\bigl[\Phi(D_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U}})-\Phi(D^{x}_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U}})\bigr].

We now bound the
\Psi
and
\Phi
terms separately.

By the definition of
\Psi
,

\displaystyle\Psi(D^{x}_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U}})-\Psi(D_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U}})

\displaystyle=2\sum_{v\in\mathcal{U}}(D^{x}_{xv}-D_{xv})\mathcal{S}(x,v)=2\Bigl[\mathcal{S}(x,\pi^{*}(x))-\sum_{v\in\mathcal{U}}d_{v}\mathcal{S}(x,v)\Bigr].

Conditioned on the seeds and their images,
\mathcal{S}(x,\pi^{*}(x))
is a sum of
|\mathcal{R}|
independent Bernoulli variables with mean of order
\lambda s^{2}
per seed, and each
\mathcal{S}(x,v)
for
v\neq\pi^{*}(x)
is stochastically smaller, exactly as in the proof of Theorem
2
. As
\lambda s^{2}>1-\alpha+\varepsilon
, by Lemma
7
, there exists a constant
c_{0}>0
such that with probability
1-o(1)
,

\sum_{v\in\mathcal{U}}d_{v}\mathcal{S}(x,v)\leq\mathcal{S}(x,\pi^{*}(x))-c_{0}\log n

simultaneously for all probability vectors
d
supported on
\mathcal{U}\setminus\{\pi^{*}(x)\}
and all
x\in\mathcal{U}
. On this event we obtain

(3)

\Psi(D^{x}_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U}})-\Psi(D_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U}})\geq 2c_{0}\log n\|d-d^{*}\|_{1}.

By Lemma
6
,

\frac{\bigl|\Phi(D^{x}_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U}})-\Phi(D_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U}})\bigr|}{\|d-d^{*}\|_{1}}\leq C\Bigl(\deg_{A,\mathcal{U}}(x)+\max_{v\in\mathcal{U}}\deg_{B,\mathcal{U}}(v)\Bigr)

for some constant
C>0
, with probability
1-o(1)
(uniformly over all
x
and all right–stochastic
D
).
Standard degree concentration in the sparse SBM ensures that there exists
c_{1}>0
such that, with probability
1-o(1)
,

\deg_{A,\mathcal{U}}(x)+\max_{v\in\mathcal{U}}\deg_{B,\mathcal{U}}(v)\leq c_{1}n^{-\alpha}\log n\quad\text{for all }x\in\mathcal{U}.

We now justify the (loose) upper bound on the maximum degree term, allowing slack of order

n^{\alpha/2}\log n
.

Fix
u\in\mathcal{U}
and write

d_{A}(u):=\sum_{v\in[n]}A(u,v).

Since
A
is an SBM with parameters
(a,b)
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[d_{A}(u)\right]=\tfrac{a+b}{2}\log n=\lambda\log n,\quad\sum_{v\in[n]}\mathbb{V}\left[A(u,v)\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[d_{A}(u)\right]=\lambda\log n,

where
\lambda=(a+b)/2
.

By Bennett’s inequality, for any
x>0
and
h(x)=(1+x)\log(1+x)-x
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(d_{A}(u)\geq\mathbb{E}\left[d_{A}(u)\right]+t\right)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\exp\!\left(-\Big(\sum_{v\in[n]}\mathbb{V}\left[A(u,v)\right]\Big)\,h\!\left(\frac{t}{\sum_{v\in[n]}\mathbb{V}\left[A(u,v)\right]}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\exp\!\left(-\lambda\log n\cdot h\!\left(\frac{t}{\lambda\log n}\right)\right).

We then take
t:=n^{\alpha/2}\log n
. Using the asymptotic
h(x)\geq\tfrac{1}{2}x\log(1+x)
for all sufficiently large
x
,
we obtain for all large
n
,

\displaystyle\lambda\log n\cdot h\!\left(\frac{t}{\lambda\log n}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\lambda\log n\cdot\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{n^{\alpha/2}}{\lambda}\,\log\!\Bigl(1+\frac{n^{\alpha/2}}{\lambda}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\geq c\,n^{\alpha/2}(\log n)^{2}

for some constant
c>0
. Hence for each fixed
u\in\mathcal{U}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(d_{A}(u)\geq(\lambda+n^{\alpha/2})\log n\right)\leq\exp\big(-cn^{\alpha/2}(\log n)^{2}\big).

Applying a union bound over
u\in\mathcal{U}
(with
|\mathcal{U}|=n^{1-\alpha}
) yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{u\in\mathcal{U}}d_{A}(u)\geq\lambda\log n+n^{\alpha/2}\log n\right)

\displaystyle\quad\leq n^{1-\alpha}\exp\!\big(-c\,n^{\alpha/2}(\log n)^{2}\big)=o(1).

The same bound holds for
B
, and therefore with probability
1-o(1)
,

\displaystyle\max_{u\in\mathcal{U}}d_{A}(u)\leq(\lambda+n^{\alpha/2})\log n

\displaystyle\max_{v\in\mathcal{U}}d_{B}(v)\leq(\lambda+n^{\alpha/2})\log n.

Recalling that
\deg_{A,\mathcal{U}}(x)
and
\deg_{B,\mathcal{U}}(v)
denote degrees into
\mathcal{U}
normalized by
|\mathcal{R}|=n^{1-\alpha}
,
we have
\deg_{A,\mathcal{U}}(x)\leq d_{A}(x)/|\mathcal{R}|
and
\deg_{B,\mathcal{U}}(v)\leq d_{B}(v)/|\mathcal{R}|
, so on the same event, for all
x\in\mathcal{U}
,

\displaystyle\deg_{A,\mathcal{U}}(x)+\max_{v\in\mathcal{U}}\deg_{B,\mathcal{U}}(v)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2(\lambda+n^{\alpha/2})\log n}{|\mathcal{R}|}

\displaystyle=2(\lambda+n^{\alpha/2})\,n^{\alpha-1}\log n.

Equivalently, setting
c_{1}:=2(\lambda+1)
and using
n^{\alpha/2}\leq n^{\alpha}
for
\alpha\in[0,1]
,
we may write the convenient slack form

\deg_{A,\mathcal{U}}(x)+\max_{v\in\mathcal{U}}\deg_{B,\mathcal{U}}(v)\leq c_{1}\,n^{-\alpha/2}\log n

holding for all
x\in\mathcal{U}
with probability
1-o(1)
.
Intersecting this event with the high-probability event from the previous step, we have

(4)

\bigl|\Phi(D^{x}_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U}})-\Phi(D_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U}})\bigr|\leq Cc_{1}n^{-\alpha/2}\log n\|d-d^{*}\|_{1}

for all
x\in\mathcal{U}
and all right–stochastic
D
, with probability
1-o(1)
.

Combining (
3
) and (
4
), we obtain on a
1-o(1)
event:

\displaystyle f(D)-f(D^{x})

\displaystyle=\bigl[\Psi(D^{x}_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U}})-\Psi(D_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U}})\bigr]+\bigl[\Phi(D_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U}})-\Phi(D^{x}_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U}})\bigr]

\displaystyle\geq 2c_{0}\log n\|d-d^{*}\|_{1}-Cc_{1}n^{-\alpha/2}\log n\|d-d^{*}\|_{1}

Then with probability
1-o(1)
, taking
n
sufficiently large, we have the strict inequality

f(D)>f(D^{x})\quad\text{whenever }d\neq d^{*}.

That is, for each row
x\in\mathcal{U}
, we can strictly decrease the objective by replacing the
x
-th row of
D
with the true permutation row
d^{*}
, unless it was already equal to
d^{*}
.

Iterating this argument over all
x\in\mathcal{U}
, we see that with probability
1-o(1)
any minimizer
D
of
f
must satisfy
D_{x,\cdot}=d^{*}
for all
x\in\mathcal{U}
, i.e.,

D_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U}}=D^{*}_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U}}

is the unique optimal choice for the unrevealed block.

We have shown that, with probability
1-o(1)
, every optimal LP solution satisfies
D_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U}}=D^{*}_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U}}
, the permutation matrix of
\pi^{*}
restricted to
\mathcal{U}
. In particular, the solution
(D^{*},Y^{*})
returned by the LP solver has this property with high probability. In the projection step of Algorithm
3
we run the Hungarian algorithm on
W:=D^{*}_{\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U}}
and obtain a permutation
\pi_{\mathcal{U}}
. Since
W
itself is a permutation matrix, any maximum-weight matching w.r.t.
W
is exactly the permutation encoded by
W
, so
\pi_{\mathcal{U}}=\pi^{*}|_{\mathcal{U}}
.

Finally, we set
\hat{\pi}(u)=\pi_{\mathcal{U}}(u)
for
u\in\mathcal{U}
, so
\hat{\pi}=\pi^{*}
with probability
1-o(1)
, as claimed.

∎

5.
Experiments and Discussion

We empirically evaluate the algorithms in Algorithms
1
–
3
in the almost fully seeded regime. The experiments are designed to test two claims suggested by our theory:

(1)

For
\scsbm
instances with
|\mathcal{U}|=n^{1-\alpha}
, accuracy should approach exact recovery as
s
increases such that
\lambda s^{2}>1-\alpha
.

(2)

On real graphs, simple seed-neighborhood overlap methods should remain competitive with established baselines on a range of problems from the matching literature.

5.1.
Experimental Protocol

Given the ground truth
\pi^{*}
, we report the unseeded accuracy

\mathrm{Acc}(\hat{\pi})=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{U}|}\sum_{u\in\mathcal{U}}\mathbbm{1}_{\hat{\pi}(u)=\pi^{*}(u)}.

We additionally report runtime in seconds for the matching stage on
\mathcal{U}
. The reported numbers are means over 20 trials, with one standard deviation in parentheses.

For each trial and dataset, we select a revealed set
\mathcal{R}
uniformly at random among vertices, with a prescribed seed fraction
\delta=|\mathcal{R}|/n
, then we evaluate accuracy on
\mathcal{U}=[n]\setminus\mathcal{R}
only.

For the synthetic plots in Figure
1
we vary the seed fraction
\delta
; for the dataset summary tables we fix
\delta=0.9
, except for the auto dataset, where
\delta=0.99
.

Algorithms

[
4

[

10

5.5.
Discussion

Our results focus on the almost fully seeded regime for graph matching, where all but
n^{1-\alpha}
vertices are revealed and the task is to align the remaining nodes efficiently. In this setting, we show that the presence of a large seed set fundamentally alters the difficulty of the problem: when
\lambda s^{2}>1-\alpha
, simple neighborhood–overlap statistics computed on the revealed vertices already contain enough signal to achieve exact recovery with high probability.
The synthetic experiments corroborate this theoretical picture, demonstrating that even simple neighborhood–overlap methods such as greedy or Hungarian matching perform competitively once the overlap signal is strong.

Beyond validating the theory, the experiments highlight a broader algorithmic message: in the almost fully seeded regime, the alignment signal is dominated by interactions with the revealed set, and global optimization over the unrevealed block becomes comparatively benign, as the objective landscape is dominated by the strong linear signal induced by the revealed vertices. This helps explain why simple score-based methods, a bistochastic LP relaxation, and its Frank–Wolfe approximation all behave similarly in practice when the seed fraction is large, despite their different computational profiles.

A compelling direction for future work is to extend our analysis to the joint community detection–matching problem in the semi-supervised correlated SBM. In this setting, one observes two correlated community-structured networks along with a subset of revealed vertex correspondences, and the objective is to simultaneously recover the true permutation and the community labels. Recent work has shown that matching and clustering can interact in subtle and mutually reinforcing ways: Gaudio, Rácz, and Sridhar
[
7
]
demonstrated that two correlated SBMs can enable exact recovery in regimes where neither matching nor community detection alone is feasible, while previous work
[
13
,
6
]
established that a vanishing fraction of revealed nodes can dramatically lower community-recovery thresholds. Wang et al.
[
18
]
further showed that attribute information can shift feasibility boundaries in aligned inference tasks. Building on these insights, a natural goal is to characterize the optimal statistical and computational thresholds when both seed correspondences and partial community labels are available, and to design efficient algorithms that leverage both structural and labeling side information. Such an extension would unify seeded matching and semi-supervised clustering within a single theoretical framework, and delineate the precise trade-off between graph correlation, community signal strength, and the fraction of revealed nodes required for exact recovery in structured multi-graph settings.
