Artificial intelligence and radiomics in drug-induced interstitial lung disease
Authors: Guido Marchi, Salvatore Claudio Fanni, Mattia Mercier, Jacopo Cefalo, Carmine Salerni, Martina Ferioli, Piero Candoli, Chiara Romei, Leonardo Gori, Federico Cucchiara, Giovanni Cenerini, Giacomo Guglielmi, Francesco Pistelli, Laura Carrozzi, Michele Mondoni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDrug-induced interstitial lung disease (DI-ILD) encompasses a heterogeneous spectrum of potentially severe pulmonary toxicities associated with various pharmacological agents. Diagnosis is often delayed due to nonspecific symptoms and imaging findings that can mimic other ILDs or infections, particularly in patients receiving oncological or immunomodulatory therapies. Novel approaches are needed to improve early recognition and management.MethodsWe conducted a non-systematic, narrative literature review across major biomedical databases up to June 2025, aimed at describing current and emerging applications of artificial intelligence (AI) and radiomics in the early diagnosis, risk stratification, and personalised management of DI-ILD.ResultsRadiomics applied to computed tomography and positron emission tomography/computed tomography enables extraction of high-dimensional quantitative features capturing subclinical alterations undetectable by visual assessment. In DI-ILD (particularly immune checkpoint inhibitor-related pneumonitis), radiomic models show potential diagnostic utility in distinguishing overlapping imaging patterns and predicting fibrotic progression. Integration with clinical, radiological and pharmacogenomic data has improved model performance in several studies. Additionally, AI approaches, including convolutional neural networks and ensemble learning methods, demonstrate promise in enhancing pattern recognition and risk stratification.ConclusionsRadiomics and AI are emerging complementary tools in multidisciplinary management of DI-ILD, offering objective imaging biomarkers and facilitating multimodal data integration to improve diagnostic precision and personalised therapeutic decisions. Nonetheless, reproducibility remains limited by variability in imaging protocols and lack of large-scale prospective multicentre validation. Clinical implementation requires standardised protocols to ensure consistency and reliability. The development of transparent, interpretable models seamlessly integrated into healthcare workflows is essential to fully leverage their real-world potential in proactive patient care.

Introduction

Drug-induced interstitial lung disease (DI-ILD) includes a wide range of lung injuries, from mild forms to life-threatening conditions. More than 400 drugs have been linked to DI-ILD [1], especially disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs), antiarrhythmics, antimicrobials and cancer therapies [2]. The rise of targeted agents, such as immune checkpoint and tyrosine kinase inhibitors, has notably raised the risk of pulmonary toxicity [3–5]. Underlying mechanisms are diverse and not fully clarified, with individual susceptibility probably influenced by genetic and multifactorial factors [6].

Diagnosis is complex and relies on clinical suspicion, thorough medication history, and exclusion of other causes, particularly when ILD overlaps with the treated disease or multiple drugs are involved [7]. Main differential diagnoses include infections, pulmonary oedema, radiation pneumonitis, malignancies, and disease progression. Management generally involves withdrawal of the offending drug; corticosteroids are reserved for severe or progressive cases [8].

Radiomics and artificial intelligence (AI) may offer innovative tools to address challenges in DI-ILD management, despite multidisciplinary discussion remaining the current gold standard for diagnosis and therapeutic decision-making in suspected DI-ILDs [1, 9, 10]. Radiomic analysis extracts quantitative features from imaging to detect patterns invisible to the human eye [11]. Machine learning algorithms can identify drug-specific imaging signatures that precede clinical symptoms [12]. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) applied to chest computed tomography (CT) have shown superior accuracy in differentiating DI-ILD from other interstitial diseases compared to traditional radiology [13].

AI-based risk models combining imaging biomarkers with clinical and pharmacogenomic data could help identify high-risk patients before therapy starts, enabling tailored monitoring and better-informed risk–benefit decisions. These technologies may also support objective assessment of treatment response and outcome prediction [14].

The primary aim of this review is to explore how radiomics and AI can transform DI-ILD management by enhancing pattern recognition, risk stratification, and early detection. It evaluates current evidence on their use for differential diagnosis and response prediction, addressing methodological and implementation challenges. By identifying key research priorities, the review aims to promote evidence-based radiomics and AI tools to improve outcomes in DI-ILD.

Methods

A non-systematic, narrative literature review was conducted to assess the current evidence on the use of radiomics and AI in the risk prediction, diagnosis and management of DI-ILD, aiming to identify knowledge gaps and guide future research directions.

To summarise and synthesise key findings from various studies, PubMed, Google Scholar, Scopus and Web of Science databases were searched between 1 March 2025 and 25 June 2025, targeting research articles published up to June 2025. Key search terms included, but were not limited to, “Drug-induced interstitial lung disease”, “DI-ILD”, “drug-induced pneumonitis”, “pulmonary drug toxicity”, “artificial intelligence”, “AI”, “machine learning”, “deep learning”, “radiomics”, “imaging biomarkers”, “computer-aided diagnosis”, “neural networks”, “convolutional neural networks (CNN)”, “XGBoost”, “support vector machines (SVM)” and “predictive modelling”. These were combined using appropriate Boolean operators and tailored to the syntax of each database.

Additional studies were identified through citation tracking and reference list screening of relevant articles.

Article selection followed a two-step process: initial title and abstract screening, followed by full-text review to determine final eligibility.

A total of 194 articles (full texts) were screened and 144 were included. Inclusion criteria were articles written in English, on adult human populations, reporting the utilisation of AI techniques and/or radiomics in the diagnosis, management and monitoring of DI-ILDs. Exclusion criteria were articles not written in English and involving radiomics and/or AI methods not clearly focusing on DI-ILDs; articles on studies containing insufficient technical details; non-peer-reviewed articles.

Results

DI-ILD

Epidemiology

DI-ILD accounts for approximately 3–5% of all ILD cases, with an estimated annual incidence ranging from 4.1 to 12.4 per million individuals [1, 15, 16]. Higher incidence rates have been reported in Japan, probably reflecting differences in pharmacovigilance practices rather than true biological susceptibility [15].

The onset may be acute, occurring within days, or insidious over months to years, underscoring the importance of thorough medication history in suspected cases [1, 16]. Clinical outcomes are heterogeneous, with mortality strongly associated with the offending agent and initial severity; some chemotherapeutics (e.g. bleomycin, cetuximab) have been linked to mortality rates >50% in severe cases [15]. Up to one-third of patients may remain asymptomatic, with DI-ILD detected incidentally on imaging [15].

Prognosis closely correlates with initial clinical severity, and early identification and withdrawal of the causative drug significantly improve outcomes [1, 15, 16].

Common causative drugs

More than 350 drugs have been implicated in DI-ILD, although pulmonary toxicity often emerges late in drug development or post-marketing. >1300 drugs, procedures, or substances have been reported to cause respiratory complications. An essential resource for evaluating suspected DI-ILD is Pneumotox (www.pneumotox.com/drug/index/), a continuously updated database of drugs associated with pulmonary toxicity.

DI-ILD remains a diagnosis of exclusion, necessitating a multidisciplinary approach and accurate documentation of drug type, dose and duration [8, 10]. Drugs can induce nearly all histopathological patterns of interstitial pneumonia and may produce multiple patterns in the same patient. Although accounting for only ∼3% of all ILD cases, DI-ILD is clinically significant, since identification and cessation of the offending drug are the cornerstone of treatment [17].

Pathophysiology

The pathogenesis of DI-ILD is multifactorial, involving direct cytotoxicity, immune-mediated responses and individual susceptibility [1, 16].

Direct toxicity damages alveolar epithelial and endothelial cells via reactive oxygen species generated by drugs like bleomycin, cyclophosphamide, and amiodarone, causing oxidative stress, inflammation, apoptosis and fibrosis [1, 16]. Amiodarone uniquely accumulates in lung tissue, inhibiting phospholipase A2, leading to phospholipid build-up in alveolar macrophages and chronic inflammation [16].

Immune-mediated injury occurs when drugs act as haptens, triggering hypersensitivity or immune complex reactions, often with CD8+ lymphocytic alveolitis; methotrexate and nitrofurantoin may increase CD4+ cells [1, 16].

Additional mechanisms include endothelial permeability changes and cytokine dysregulation. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) cause T-cell-mediated pneumonitis by immune activation against lung tissue [1], while antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) like trastuzumab-deruxtecan induce bystander toxicity via alveolar macrophage uptake despite low HER2 expression [1, 16]. Infections such as Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Pneumocystis jirovecii, severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 and cytomegalovirus may mimic or worsen DI-ILD, complicating diagnosis [16]. Onset is variable: some drugs induce acute inflammation within days/weeks; others cause fibrotic damage over months/years.

Risk factors

Risk factors for DI-ILD vary by disease context, drug class, and patient traits. Genetic predisposition is key: human leukocyte antigen (HLA)-DRB104:05 and HLA-B15:01 associate with pulmonary toxicity in Japanese populations [1], while HLA-A*31:01 links to drug hypersensitivity in Europeans [18]. Variants in cytochrome P450 enzymes and other HLA alleles may influence immune and cytotoxic injury [16].

Age is a major risk factor, especially with bleomycin, gemcitabine, epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) inhibitors, leflunomide, methotrexate, amiodarone and nitrofurantoin [19]. Male sex raises risk with EGFR inhibitors, pemetrexed, methotrexate and amiodarone [20, 21]. Smoking, pre-existing lung diseases, i.e. ILD, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) [15], COPD, bronchiectasis, asbestosis and prior thoracic radiotherapy [22] increase susceptibility [1]. Asian ethnicity is also implicated [16].

In oncology, poor performance status and advanced/metastatic disease are additional risks [21, 23]. In rheumatoid arthritis, methotrexate-induced DI-ILD occurs more with elevated inflammatory markers, hypoalbuminaemia, extra-articular features and reduced function [24]. Other risks include heavy alcohol use, renal impairment and diabetes [15, 25].

Non-radiological assessment of DI-ILD

Mild forms of DI-ILD are often detected only through radiological imaging. However, clinical presentation and subsequent investigations are essential for diagnosis and effective management of DI-ILD. A thorough anamnesis is critical to gather detailed information about the patient's medication history, timing of drug exposure, and the interval between exposure and symptom onset. Additionally, identifying comorbidities and evaluating potential risk factors are essential steps in ruling out alternative causes of ILD [1].

Clinical symptoms

DI-ILD presents a broad range of clinical manifestations depending on pulmonary involvement and patient status. Onset can be acute, subacute or chronic, with symptoms from asymptomatic to severe respiratory failure [26]. Clinical severity is commonly graded using the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events, a standardised tool developed by the National Cancer Institute to classify and grade the severity of adverse effects in clinical trials and practice [27].

Acute cases show rapidly progressive dyspnoea, dry cough and fever, requiring urgent evaluation. Subacute/chronic forms are nonspecific, including cough, exertional dyspnoea, fatigue, chest pain, low-grade fever or chest tightness [28]. Dyspnoea and reduced exercise tolerance may precede respiratory failure; cough is usually dry, haemoptysis rare.

Physical exam assesses vital signs, hypoxia signs (cyanosis) or infection (lymphadenopathy). Auscultation may reveal reduced breath sounds and fine crackles [1]. Digital clubbing is uncommon [27].

Pulmonary function testing

Pulmonary function tests (PFTs), including spirometry and diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide (DLCO), are essential in the diagnostic workup of DI-ILD when respiratory failure is absent. The diagnostic value of PFTs varies [29]. A restrictive pattern, with reduced forced vital capacity and total lung capacity, is common [28]. DLCO is frequently decreased, often preceding symptoms, and is associated with poorer prognosis [15, 16]. Comparison with baseline (pre-treatment) values, when available, can help establish causality.

Biomarker analysis

Routine laboratory tests should include a complete blood count, renal and liver function panels, and inflammatory markers. Eosinophilia may be indicative of eosinophilic pneumonia [30].

Serum biomarkers such as Krebs von den Lungen (KL)-6 and surfactant proteins A and D (SP-A and SP-D) have been proposed for use in DI-ILD. KL-6, a glycoprotein secreted by regenerating alveolar type II and bronchial epithelial cells, demonstrates limited sensitivity (53.3%) in DI-ILD, but correlates with the severity of imaging abnormalities; elevated levels are typically associated with diffuse alveolar damage (DAD) patterns [31].

SP-A and SP-D, also secreted by alveolar type II cells, have been investigated as potential markers of disease activity and prognosis. Notably, SP-D may serve as a predictor of outcomes in anticancer therapy-related DI-ILD [32]. However, further validation of these biomarkers is warranted.

Bronchoalveolar lavage

When feasible, bronchoscopy with bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) aids in excluding infections and identifying inflammatory patterns. BAL fluid may reveal lymphocytosis, neutrophilia or eosinophilia; however, these findings are nonspecific. Lymphocytic alveolitis often presents with an inverted CD4/CD8 ratio, typically showing CD8+ predominance [15]. Nonetheless, selective CD4+ expansion may occur with certain drugs, including methotrexate, nitrofurantoin, ampicillin, and sirolimus [1, 28].

In rare instances, BAL cytology may be diagnostic. For example, amiodarone can induce the presence of foamy intracytoplasmic macrophages [8], while haemosiderin-laden macrophages are characteristic of diffuse alveolar haemorrhage [33].

Histopathology

Lung biopsy, via transbronchial or surgical methods, is considered in selected cases to clarify uncertain diagnoses or exclude alternative aetiologies [16]. Bronchoscopic biopsy can be performed using conventional transbronchial biopsy or transbronchial cryobiopsy [34]. Histopathological findings in DI-ILD are highly variable, encompassing patterns such as hypersensitivity pneumonitis, organising pneumonia, DAD, nonspecific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP), eosinophilic pneumonia, alveolar haemorrhage and granulomatous pneumonitis [8].

Radiological assessment of DI-ILD

Conventional radiology

Conventional radiology is essential for diagnosing DI-ILD. Chest radiography, often the initial test, has low accuracy, with a 25–75% rate of false negatives [15]. Thin-section chest CT is the gold standard for suspected ILDs [1, 15], enabling detection, extent assessment, pattern identification, exclusion of alternatives and biopsy guidance. However, no CT feature is pathognomonic for DI-ILD versus other ILDs, infections or malignancies [1]. Drug-specific CT patterns are not definitive; one drug may cause multiple patterns, and one pattern may result from various drugs, although certain findings recur more frequently with specific agents [1, 15]. A Fleischner Society document described five main patterns: NSIP, organising pneumonia, DAD, hypersensitivity pneumonitis and simple pulmonary eosinophilia [1]. Less common injuries include sarcoid-like granulomatosis (SLG), diffuse alveolar haemorrhage, vasculitis, alveolar proteinosis, constrictive bronchiolitis and veno-occlusive disease [1].

Organising pneumonia is the most frequent DI-ILD pattern [15, 26, 73], commonly linked to amiodarone, ICIs, EGFR-tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) inhibitors (e.g. sirolimus), statins and rituximab [1].

NSIP is second most common [26], with >350 drugs implicated, including amiodarone, chemotherapy (bleomycin, docetaxel, gemcitabine), ICIs (nivolumab, ipilimumab, pembrolizumab), TKIs (crizotinib, erlotinib, gefitinib), mTOR inhibitors, rituximab, statins, methotrexate and nitrofurantoin [1].

Fibrotic hypersensitivity pneumonitis is less frequent, but the second most reported ICI-related pattern [1]. DAD/acute interstitial pneumonia shows alveolar oedema and vascular injury. Causative drugs include EGFR-TKIs, anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) inhibitors, ICIs, amiodarone, anticoagulants, antiplatelets, abciximab, ticlopidine, fibrinolytics, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) inhibitors (bevacizumab), erlotinib and mTOR inhibitors [1].

Eosinophilic pneumonia is rare and may present with pleural effusion; spontaneous CT resolution is common. Most cases are linked to osimertinib [55, 74].

SLG is atypical, but frequently associated with ICIs [1, 26], as well as with tumour necrosis factor-α inhibitors, daclizumab and interferons [1].

Diffuse alveolar haemorrhage is rare and life-threatening, with rapid symptom worsening and bilateral ground-glass opacities/consolidations; crazy paving may appear [75]. Causative agents include amiodarone, anticoagulants, antiplatelets, abciximab, fibrinolytics, VEGF inhibitors (bevacizumab), erlotinib, and mTOR inhibitors [1].

Treatment and prognosis

In the vast majority of cases, the treatment of DI-ILD is based on the immediate discontinuation of the offending medication. This may significantly influence prognosis and recovery. When pharmacological therapy is needed, glucocorticoids represent the first-line treatment, particularly in cases of moderate to severe respiratory failure [1, 76].

Current guidelines from the American Society of Clinical Oncology and the European Society for Medical Oncology advocate treatment strategies tailored to the clinical severity of lung injury induced by antineoplastic drugs [16, 77].

In mild cases (grade 1), defined by absence of respiratory symptoms, temporary drug discontinuation is optional, and low-dose steroid treatment (0.5 mg·kg−1·day−1) with rapid tapering in 4 weeks can be considered [16, 78, 79]. In patients with mild respiratory symptoms (grade 2), drug discontinuation and low-dose corticosteroids (1–2 mg·kg−1·day−1 of prednisone or equivalent) are recommended. Steroids can be tapered over 4–6 weeks in patients with good response, although rapid tapering may increase relapse risk. In DI-ILD due to specific anticancer drugs, such as trastuzumab-deruxtecan, duration of steroid treatment should not be <6 weeks. Reintroduction of causative drug can be carefully evaluated case by case, depending on complete remission, comorbidities, specific drug and availability of alternatives. Rigorous clinical monitoring to promptly detect recurrence or adverse effects is mandatory [1, 16]. Severe cases (grade 3), with respiratory symptoms limiting daily activity, usually require high-dose corticosteroids (1–2 mg·kg−1·day−1 of methylprednisolone or equivalent), alongside permanent discontinuation of causative therapy, regardless of radiological severity. Steroid tapering should be prolonged after 8–12 weeks [16]. Life-threatening cases (grade 4) demand hospitalisation, intensive support, immediate and permanent drug withdrawal and steroid pulse therapy (0.5–1 g·day−1 of methylprednisolone) for 3 days, followed by prednisolone 1–2 mg·kg−1·day−1 for 2–4 weeks and tapering in 8–12 weeks. In grade 3–4, second-line immunosuppressants (e.g. infliximab, tocilizumab, mycophenolate mofetil, intravenous immunoglobulins) can be considered if no improvement is observed after 48–72 h from steroid start [16, 77]. In lung injury induced by non-antineoplastic drugs, no standardised treatment exists [27]. Therapy is usually based on corticosteroids (from 0.5 mg·kg−1·day−1 up to 0.5–1 g·day−1 of methylprednisolone), depending on ILD severity and CT pattern. Immunosuppressants may be added in severe or refractory cases [1, 27]. The prognosis of DI-ILD depends on the clinical severity at initial presentation and the timeliness of therapeutic intervention. Response to treatment can vary markedly depending on the radiological and histopathological pattern of lung involvement: DAD typically has a poor response to therapy, whereas NSIP and organising pneumonia patterns show improvement rates of approximately 46% and 75%, respectively [80]. Mortality rates in such severe cases can reach up to 36%, particularly when complicated by acute respiratory distress syndrome or delayed diagnosis and treatment initiation [27].

Radiomics and AI in DI-ILD

Radiomics in medical imaging

Radiomics is an emerging field that systematically extracts a large number of quantitative features from medical images, enabling advanced data analysis and predictive modelling in precision medicine [81]. Although the term “radiomics” was formally introduced by Lambin et al. [82] in 2012, its conceptual origins date back to the late 1970s with early work in quantitative image analysis [83]. This approach employs computational algorithms to identify imaging patterns that are not discernible to the human eye. Machine learning and deep learning uncover complex links between features and clinical outcomes from high-dimensional data [84]. Radiomics workflows often integrate AI for improved feature selection, classification and prediction. While validated mostly in oncology (CT, positron emission tomography (PET), magnetic resonance imaging linked to tumour histology, prognosis, treatment response) [85–87], radiomics-based AI shows promise in other areas [88–90], with preliminary evidence supporting benefits for DI-ILD.

The radiomics pipeline (figure 1) represents a structured framework for extracting, processing and interpreting high-dimensional imaging data, ultimately enabling the development of robust and reproducible quantitative biomarkers for both clinical practice and research. Key steps include image acquisition, region of interest (ROI) segmentation, feature extraction/selection, and model development [91]. Each phase has challenges, especially in heterogeneous diseases like DI-ILDs, affecting reliability.

A radiomics pipeline, a structured sequence of steps designed to extract and analyse high-dimensional quantitative features from medical images. GLCM: grey-level co-occurrence matrix.

Image acquisition requires standardised protocols to ensure features reflect biological variation, not technical artefacts [92]. Small scanner or parameter differences, though clinically minor, impact feature values and may bias results [93]. Harmonisation (protocol alignment, phantom calibration) is vital for reproducible multicentre studies [94].

ROI segmentation isolates anatomical/pathological areas [95], such as, in DI-ILDs, ground-glass opacities or fibrotic reticulations. Methods range from manual (reference standard, but user-dependent) to automated (faster, reproducible, less robust for atypical patterns). Complex lung involvement and ill-defined borders require rigorous validation and guidelines [95].

Feature extraction quantifies intensity, texture, shape and spatial relationships [96], including first-order (intensity distribution), second order (texture, key for lung heterogeneity), higher-order (complex patterns), and shape descriptors [97].

Histogram features summarise the global grey-level distribution, i.e. mean, min/max, variance, percentiles. Second-order texture features describe the spatial relationship between pixel/voxels reflecting contrast, homogeneity and correlation [96]. Second-order texture features include the grey-level co-occurrence matrix, which counts how often intensity pairs occur at specified offsets across directions, The grey-level run length matrix captures the lengths of consecutive same-intensity runs along given directions, while the grey-level size zone matrix tallies connected zones of identical intensity independent of direction [98]. The neighbourhood grey tone difference matrix measures how each pixel/voxel differs from the mean of its neighbours within a chosen radius. Shape-based features encode ROI geometry, such as diameters, principal axes and ratios, surface, volume, compactness. Finally, transform-based methods (Fourier, Gabor, wavelets) probe patterns in the frequency/scale domain [91].

Feature selection reduces dimensionality, retaining clinically relevant variables [99], improving model efficiency and interpretability using statistical or machine learning methods.

Model development integrates selected features into predictive/classification models. Machine learning, superior with high-dimensional, nonlinear data [100], uses algorithms like support vector machine (SVM), random forests and gradient boosting. Models are trained on labelled datasets, with cross-validation, external testing and multicentre validation ensuring generalisability and preventing overfitting.

Radiomics in DI-ILD

A central challenge in DI-ILD is its nonspecific radiological presentation. High-resolution CT (HRCT) lacks pathognomonic features, with DI-ILD mimicking patterns of NSIP, organising pneumonia, usual interstitial pneumonia-like fibrosis, DAD, or hypersensitivity pneumonitis, depending on drug and patient factors [15, 101]. Early disease may present with subtle or even normal radiographs, delaying diagnosis [15]. Thus, interpretation requires expert assessment within clinical context, considering drug history and exclusion of alternative causes. These limitations highlight the need for reproducible and sensitive imaging biomarkers.

Radiomics enables quantitative assessment of features beyond visual detection. In a pilot study, Smith et al. [102] combined fluorine-18-2-fluoro-2-deoxy-d-glucose PET/CT radiomics with machine learning to detect bleomycin-induced ILD in 18 Hodgkin's lymphoma patients, 10 of whom developed DI-ILD. A random forest classifier identified significant metabolic changes, including higher mean standardised uptake value, texture strength, and zone distance entropy, achieving 72.2% accuracy for established DI-ILD, but only 50% at baseline.

The most advanced applications have arisen in oncology, particularly with immune checkpoint inhibitors ICIs. Early detection and distinction of checkpoint inhibitor-related pneumonitis (CIP) from other forms of pneumonitis are critical to avoid unnecessary treatment interruption and prevent complications.

In the first pilot study, Colen et al. [103] analysed 1860 radiomic features from baseline CTs of 32 patients (two with CIP), achieving 100% accuracy (p=0.0033) using anomaly detection with leave-one-out cross-validation. Since then, research has rapidly expanded. Distinguishing CIP from radiation pneumonitis is especially important, as management strategies differ. Evidence suggests that CT-based radiomics, particularly when integrated with clinical data, may provide reliable discrimination.

Chen et al. [104] compared CT features in 82 patients with pneumonitis following radiotherapy, ICI or both. CIP was more likely to be bilateral, multifocal and ill-defined. A seven-feature machine-learning model reached an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.76. Qiu et al. [105] developed an 11-feature pre-treatment CT radiomics signature in 126 nonsmall cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients, combined with clinical variables such as bilateral involvement and sharp lesion borders. The nomogram achieved AUCs up to 0.95 across cohorts. Cheng et al. [106] analysed 73 patients and found a bag-of-words radiomics approach most effective (AUC 0.94), with consistent performance in combined-treatment cases. Collectively, these studies show that CT radiomics, particularly in multimodal frameworks, can differentiate CIP from radiation pneumonitis and support personalised management in patients receiving ICIs and radiotherapy.

Thomas et al. [107] assessed functional lung radiomics from pre-treatment technetium-99m macroaggregated albumin single photon emission CT/CT in 39 NSCLC patients undergoing chemoradiation±durvalumab. COPD was the strongest predictor (hazard ratio 4.59, c-index 0.69). Adding radiomics modestly increased prediction (c-index 0.79), but not significantly. Texture features correlated with lung volume, suggesting surrogate roles. Larger studies are needed to refine risk models.

Tohidinezhad et al. [108] validated CT radiomics across six centres (n=556), identifying 31 ICI-related and 41 other pneumonitis cases. A 75-mm spheroidal ROI yielded the best results, with optimism-corrected AUC 0.83, 80% negative predictive value (NPV), and 79% positive predictive value (PPV). The model correctly classified 10 out of 12 cases deemed inconclusive by radiologists. Kraus et al. [109] integrated radiomics, dosiomics and clinical data, adjusting radiotherapy doses to 2-Gy equivalent doses (EQD2). Their combined model achieved an AUC of 0.77, with EQD2 outperforming raw dose data.

Delasos et al. [110] analysed 293 patients, developing models for pneumonitis risk (AUCs 0.76–0.85) and differentiation of radiation- versus ICI-induced pneumonitis (AUCs 0.81–0.86). Gene set enrichment linked extracellular matrix–receptor interactions and T-cell signalling to high pneumonitis risk, and mitogen-activated protein kinase and Janus kinase-signal transducer and activator of transcription pathways specifically to CIP. Du et al. [111] constructed a radiomics-clinical nomogram in 186 NSCLC patients, validated externally (n=52). It achieved AUCs of 0.935, 0.905 and 0.923, outperforming clinical-only (∼0.82) and radiomics-only (∼0.85) models.

In a large cohort of 3351 patients, Smith et al. [112] found that deep-learning CNNs outperformed conventional radiomics (AUC 0.819 versus 0.747), with combined models slightly higher (AUC 0.829). Cousin et al. [113] similarly showed that random forest models integrating radiomic and clinical features outperformed unimodal approaches (AUC 0.75 versus 0.71–0.72). These findings reflect a shift from handcrafted to automated features, better capturing complex imaging signatures of CIP.

Radiomics may also provide biological and prognostic insights. Haga et al. [114] correlated CT features with inflammatory cell infiltration in fibrotic ILD biopsies, enabling prediction of quantitative immune cell counts. Martini et al. [115] showed that texture features from HRCT distinguished gender, age, and pulmonary function (GAP)1 from GAP2 in systemic sclerosis (AUC 0.96), outperforming visual scoring.

Among 11 major studies, random forest was the most frequently applied algorithm (45.5%), often benchmarked against logistic regression, elastic-net, SVM or generalised linear models. Logistic regression was used in 27.3%. Only 18.2% included external validation, with most relying on internal cross-validation of relatively small, imbalanced cohorts.

Clinical translation is hindered by a lack of multicentre prospective studies, limited open datasets, and poor model sharing, all of which restrict reproducibility and validation [116]. Overcoming these barriers is essential for wider adoption of radiomics in DI-ILD management.

AI in DI-ILD

AI offers substantial potential for clinical decision-making, diagnostics and personalised therapeutics across medicine, including respiratory diseases [117–119]. By interrogating large datasets such as imaging, electronic health records and pharmacovigilance reports, AI can detect subtle patterns beyond human recognition, particularly in complex diagnostic contexts [120–122]. In respiratory medicine, AI-driven tools aim to improve diagnostic precision, risk stratification and monitoring [123–127], with early evidence supporting earlier diagnosis and more targeted care [127].

DI-ILD represents a promising but underexplored area for AI application, where timely detection, risk prediction and tailored management are critical. Progress is constrained by disease rarity, heterogeneity and limited multicentre data.

Barnes et al. [12] used CNNs to detect ILD and monitor progression from radiological imaging, correlating fibrosis with lung function decline. Kim and Tatonetti [128] applied hierarchical machine-learning classifiers (random forest, SVM) to integrated drug and patient data, predicting adverse drug reactions including DI-ILD with up to 95.7% accuracy, although without imaging focus. Smith et al. [102] combined machine learning with radiomics to improve metabolic imaging pattern recognition in DI-ILD, underscoring the need for deep learning and multimodal integration.

AI-enhanced radiomics extracts high-dimensional features from PET/CT, enabling detection of early injury [102], while pharmacovigilance-based predictive models could deliver real-time adverse drug reaction alerts within electronic health records. However, clinical translation requires large multicentre validation and integration of imaging, clinical, genomic and pharmacological data. Development of explainable AI is essential to ensure interpretability, alongside prospective trials assessing outcomes such as earlier drug discontinuation or fibrosis prevention.

Additionally, AI may guide treatment by identifying high-risk patients, particularly as CIP increases DI-ILD risk 2.37-fold [129]. Smith et al. [112] combined CNN-derived and radiomic features from baseline CT with patient age to predict CIP (AUC 0.829, sensitivity 0.780, specificity 0.739, PPV 0.254), highlighting multimodal integration. Cheng et al. [130] integrated ResNet50 outputs with clinical data, achieving AUC 0.869, slightly below a clinical nomogram (AUC 0.900), stressing the importance of transparent explainable AI. Hazim et al. [129] used XGBoost on routine clinical parameters to predict ICI pneumonitis mortality risk pre-immunotherapy (AUC 0.79), demonstrating utility of electronic health records.

Insights from AI in bleomycin-induced ILD and CIP extend to DI-ILD caused by other oncological drugs. Hwang et al. [131] identified increased DI-ILD risk with mTOR inhibitors (OR 9.79), ADCs (OR 7.99), cyclin-dependent kinase 4/6 inhibitors (OR 2.28) and ICIs. Although direct AI applications are lacking, these risk profiles support future research. AI models predicting EGFR-TKI response, such as EGFR mutation detection by Cucchiara et al. [85], could be adapted to predict DI-ILD. Combining CNNs with radiomics [112] or deep learning with clinical data [130] illustrates the value of data fusion. Advanced machine-learning methods, including XGBoost [129] and autoML [132], further optimise model selection and performance across diverse datasets.

Yet, most models arise from single-centre, retrospective cohorts without randomised trials, limiting generalisability across populations and imaging protocols [112, 133]. Low PPVs, as in the CIP model (PPV 0.254 [112]), risk excessive false positives in screening, underscoring the need for improved predictive precision.

Radiomics and AI studies in DI-ILD, mainly in CIP and CIP–radiation pneumonitis differentiation, have used traditional machine learning (SVM, random forest, boosting) and deep learning (CNNs). Extracted features include first-order intensity, texture, higher-order transforms, and shape. Feature selection (maximum relevance minimum redundancy, regularised or recursive methods) is routinely applied to avoid overfitting. Reported performance varies: pilot studies reported up to 100% accuracy on small cohorts [103], while larger analyses achieved AUCs of 0.76 for CIP versus radiation pneumonitis [104], 0.936–0.953 with radiomics-clinical nomograms [105], and 0.94 with a bag-of-words approach [106]. Multicentre validation achieved optimism-corrected AUC 0.83 with high NPV (80%) and PPV (79%), resolving inconclusive cases [108]. Integrated radiomics-dosiomics-clinical models reached AUC 0.77, with EQD2 outperforming raw dose [109]. Larger cohorts comparing handcrafted features with CNNs showed superior deep-learning performance (AUC 0.819 versus 0.747), with combined methods yielding AUC 0.829, but limited PPV (0.254) [112]. Consistently, multimodal approaches outperform single-modality models.

Translation remains limited by small, retrospective cohorts, heterogeneous imaging protocols, label uncertainty (notably CIP versus radiation pneumonitis), lack of external validation and low interpretability. Reproducibility may be improved through standardisation (e.g. Image Biomarker Standardisation Initiative), batch-effect correction, and robust segmentation [92, 95, 96]. Future work should prioritise multicentre prospective datasets, harmonised imaging protocols, and explainable frameworks to enable clinical use. Pharmacovigilance studies leveraging large real-world reporting systems, including the Food and Drug Administration Adverse Event Reporting System (FAERS) and national registries, have investigated DI-ILD. Disproportionality and temporal trend analyses consistently highlight elevated reporting signals for specific agents (such as trastuzumab-deruxtecan, ramucirumab, eribulin) and for broader classes of antineoplastic and immunomodulating drugs [134]. Despite the richness of these datasets, no study has yet integrated spontaneous reporting data into AI-based models tailored to DI-ILD. By contrast, AI has been applied in other pharmacovigilance contexts, for example combining FAERS with toxicogenomic data to predict adverse drug reactions [135]. This gap underscores both the opportunity and the challenges (underreporting, coding heterogeneity, variability in causality assessment, and lack of structured annotations) that must be addressed to leverage pharmacovigilance data effectively for AI-driven risk modelling in DI-ILD.

Overall, AI in DI-ILD remains nascent, but holds promise for transforming diagnosis, risk stratification and treatment guidance. Its clinical adoption requires interdisciplinary collaboration, rigorous methodology, external validation and prospective evaluation diseases. Evidence beyond oncology: non-anticancer drugs

While most radiomics and AI studies on DI-ILD originate from oncology, no peer-reviewed radiomic or AI models have specifically addressed DI-ILD caused by non-anticancer agents such as DMARDs, amiodarone or nitrofurantoin [136]. For these drugs, the literature primarily reports conventional HRCT patterns and semi-quantitative CT scoring [137], without any validated radiomic signatures or machine-learning classifiers. Several methodological factors probably contribute to this evidence gap, including the low incidence of DI-ILD outside oncology, under-ascertainment, challenges in robust causality adjudication and precise definition of exposure windows, and the frequent occurrence of mixed or sequential drug exposures. Advancing the field will require multicentre datasets with standardised imaging protocols, harmonised phenotyping, precise timing of exposure and causality scoring, and integration with pharmacovigilance or electronic health record data [138]. Indeed, the vast majority of existing studies focus on DI-ILD related to anticancer drugs, while the potential applications of radiomics in DI-ILD associated with DMARDs, amiodarone or other commonly implicated agents remain largely unexplored. A pragmatic next step is the development of multimodal AI frameworks that leverage already available resources by integrating imaging-derived features (including selected handcrafted signs where relevant, such as ancillary extrapulmonary high attenuation in amiodarone exposure), structured clinical variables from electronic health records (drug identity and dose, latency/exposure windows, comorbidities, infection workup), pharmacovigilance-based causality priors and standardised causality algorithms (including dechallenge/rechallenge information), and multidisciplinary discussion outputs as weak supervision or adjudication labels.

Conclusions and future perspectives

DI-ILD remains a formidable clinical challenge due to its heterogeneous and often nonspecific presentation, diagnostic complexity, and risk of irreversible lung injury if not promptly identified. Traditional imaging techniques, while essential, frequently lack the sensitivity to detect early or subclinical disease, complicating timely diagnosis and management. In this evolving landscape, radiomics and AI emerge as powerful complementary tools, capable of extracting subtle quantitative imaging biomarkers and leveraging multidimensional clinical and pharmacological data to enhance diagnostic accuracy and risk stratification. Notably, despite these promising technological advances, multidisciplinary discussion remains the current gold standard for diagnosis and therapeutic decision-making in suspected DI-ILDs [1, 10].

Recent advances in AI, particularly deep learning, CNNs and ensemble machine-learning methods, have demonstrated promising results in early detection and differentiation of DI-ILD phenotypes, especially within oncological contexts such as CIP. Radiomics enables detailed characterisation of lung tissue microarchitecture and metabolic changes through high-dimensional feature extraction from routine CT and PET/CT scans, capturing abnormalities that may elude conventional visual assessment. Integrative approaches combining radiomic features with clinical parameters, genomic profiles, and pharmacovigilance data are paving the way for holistic, personalised predictive models that can inform tailored surveillance and therapeutic strategies.

Nonetheless, significant barriers remain before these technologies can be routinely integrated into clinical practice. Challenges include the scarcity of large, prospective, multicentre validation studies, variability in imaging acquisition and reconstruction protocols, and concerns over the interpretability and transparency of AI models. When prospective alignment is not feasible, standardised pre-processing can mitigate intersite variability, such as consistent segmentation, resampling, fixed-bin-width discretisation and uniform filtering. Moreover, regulatory approval pathways, clinician training, and seamless integration with existing electronic health records and imaging platforms are essential to facilitate adoption.

Indeed, adoption of radiomic tools in everyday ILD care entails both costs and benefits. Up-front and recurring costs may include image standardisation and harmonisation, segmentation, integration into picture archiving and communication systems/radiology information systems, vendor licensing, computing, staff training, and governance/monitoring. Potential benefits are earlier and more consistent detection of DI-ILD, improved differential diagnosis (e.g. distinguishing CIP from other causes), and quantitative monitoring that correlates with physiology and prognosis. Current evidence is strongest for prediction/classification and prognostication rather than hard outcomes; randomised or prospective workflow studies showing reduced mortality or faster diagnosis are still needed. Emerging economic analyses of imaging AI indicate possible efficiency gains, but DI-ILD-specific cost-effectiveness data are not yet available [139].

Future directions must emphasise the development of explainable AI frameworks to enhance clinician trust, the establishment of standardised multicentre datasets to improve model robustness, and the exploration of hybrid models that synergise radiomics with clinical and multi-omics data. In addition, the absence of studies linking AI- or radiomics-based diagnosis to histopathological findings underscores a critical gap that future research should aim to overcome. By addressing these gaps, AI and radiomics have the potential to transform DI-ILD management, shifting from reactive diagnosis towards proactive, precise, and personalised care, ultimately improving patient outcomes.
