Toward organizational agility in universities of medical sciences: a social responsibility approach—an integrative review
Authors: Mohammad Arabsorkhi Mishabi, Maryam Avizhgan, Shahram Shayan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSocial responsibility in medical education requires universities of medical sciences to align their educational systems with the health needs of society, yet evidence from Iran suggests a persistent gap between academic education and real societal demands. In rapidly changing health systems, organizational agility through dynamic capabilities and empowerment may help universities better fulfill this mission. Therefore, this integrative review examined components shaping organizational agility in the educational system of universities of medical sciences from a social responsibility perspective.MethodWe conducted an integrative review following Whittemore & knafl’s five-step framework (problem identification, literature search, data evaluation, data analysis, and presentation). A comprehensive search of international and national databases, supplemented by grey literature and reference list screening, was conducted without time limit until September 11, 2025. The identification, screening, eligibility, and inclusion of records were reported using PRISMA 2020 as a reporting guideline only. Eligible studies were appraised using design-appropriate tools (MMAT, SANRA, JBI, CASP), and their findings were synthesized through a hybrid framework-guided deductive-inductive content analysis informed by the agility model of Ghane Ebadi et al.ResultFifteen studies met the inclusion criteria. Across a diverse contexts, recurring components of organizational agility and university social responsibility converged into two interrelated pillars: educational empowerment and educational dynamic capabilities. Educational empowerment included structural and human asset empowerment in education, while educational dynamic capabilities comprised the capabilities of sensing, seizing, and reconfiguration. Both pillars were consistently interpreted through a social responsibility lens, emphasizing responsibility, equity, transparency, and stakeholder engagement.ConclusionThe resulting framework reconceptualizes organizational agility in medical universities as “responsible agility,” in which educational empowerment and educational dynamic capabilities are intentionally aligned with social responsibility to guide governance, curriculum design, and stakeholder-driven educational reforms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-09919-9.

Introduction

Today, one of the new educational movements in higher education, including medical education is social accountability. Social accountability is defined as an approach that seeks to align medical education with the health and healthcare needs of the community it serves [1]. One type of social accountability is social responsibility. Social responsibility means that organizations have a major impact on the social system and their way of functioning should be such that no harm is caused to society. social responsibility implies that the organization is responsible for its functions [2]. Accordingly, universities must reconsider their roles related to society in order to solve the complex problems of the 21st century [3].

In other words, in social responsibility, universities should not consider themselves independent of society but part of a whole, that is, part of society [4]. Universities of Medical Sciences, as a type of higher education institution, are not exempt from this and, considering the needs and expectations of society, must be responsible for changes in their society [5]. Social responsibility includes a number of benefits that can create an incentive to implement social responsibility in the organization. Examples of the benefits of social responsibility include: increasing the legitimacy of the organization, strengthening commitment among employees, and improving the quality of the organization’s outputs [6]. The concept of social responsibility is also reflected in the university education system. It is manifested in such a way that it can: 1- train students who acquire the necessary qualifications to serve society, 2- help expand educational justice through inclusiveness and equal access for all stakeholders, and 3- promote values such as humanity, dignity, respect [7]. Despite the positive effects of social responsibility in the educational system, research conducted in Iran shows that the country’s universities of medical sciences have not yet carried out social responsibility activities for the educational system in a satisfactory manner, and the scattered activities have not solved the existing problems. Also, according to the opinions of most academic experts, the current education in universities of medical sciences does not correspond to the real needs of society [8]. As shown by Karimi et al.‘s study, 54.3% of family physicians believe that the gap between theoretical training and the expectations in the family physician program is large or very large [9]. Therefore, given the gap between university education and community-level clinical care, as well as rapid changes in the health system and societal needs, universities of medical sciences should reform their educational systems to train graduates who are prepared to meet current needs [10].

change is a process of transition that alters the behavioral patterns of individuals, groups, and organizations as they grow, develop, and adapt to new conditions [11]. In order to properly implement change at different levels, organizations need to use a change management approach. The goal of change management is to control change and help individuals adapt [12, 13]. Therefore, universities of medical sciences should review their change management mechanisms to better adapt to a rapidly changing environment [14, 15]. One factor that can reduce obstacles to implementing change processes in universities and help them quickly adapt to developments is the acquisition of agility capability. It can be concluded that paying attention to the different dimensions of agility in educational organizations can be one of the serious solutions to overcome this problem [16, 17].

In one definition, organizational agility refers to a set of dynamic capabilities that allow an organization to respond quickly to changes. By doing so, the organization will achieve superior performance in dynamic and turbulent environments [18–20]. In another definition, an agile organization is one that has the ability to understand and anticipate changes in the external environment and transform these changes into opportunities that benefit the organization. These organizations also have capabilities that are considered as the basis for maintaining and developing agility [21]. Organizational agility was first introduced in manufacturing and later expanded to other business areas [22]. Accordingly, recent studies have focused on mechanisms to help managers assess organizational agility, including required levels, development processes, implementation methods, and factors and obstacles affecting its implementation [19, 23–27]. One of the factors that can be effective in improving the level of organizational agility is to improve its constituent components. These components include: (1) drivers (the impact of environmental changes on the organization), (2) dynamic capabilities (the organization’s ability to manage change), (3) enablers (the use of tools, methods, and technologies), and (4) outcomes (the organization’s performance in achieving its goals) [18]. Among these four components, organizations can directly manage only dynamic capabilities and empowerment, whereas drivers and outcomes are largely beyond their control; therefore, greater attention should be paid to dynamic capabilities and empowerment [28, 29]. Dynamic capabilities include: monitoring current and future conditions, identifying opportunities and threats, mobilizing resources and realigning employees, etc., to respond quickly to environmental changes [30]. Dynamic capabilities in educational organizations are manifested in the adoption of new technologies, the creation of interdisciplinary collaborations, restructuring of management teams, attracting talented employees, providing specialized programs and facilities, etc [31–33].

Another key component is empowerment. Empowerment refers to adjusting workplace structures by managers, facilitating employees’ access to organizational resources, and strengthening their perception of their job roles, their ability to influence results, and their willingness to accept growth-related risks and responsibilities [34, 35]. Empowerment in educational organizations is manifested in four dimensions: (1) individual (a sense of effectiveness, a sense of growth and development, being up-to-date, etc.), (2) interpersonal (teamwork spirit, participation in decision-making processes, etc.), (3) organizational (attention to decentralization, flexibility in the structure, etc.), and (4) technical (development of infrastructure, investment, etc.) [36, 37].

A review of the agility literature since 1991 shows that there is no comprehensive study that compiles a unified set of indicators and provides a conceptual framework for organizational agility that can be used to strengthen and improve agility components in various organizations, especially in the health system. Therefore, researchers have proposed numerous conceptual frameworks and models in recent years with the aim of developing organizational agility. For example, the most famous of them include the models of Sharifi and Zhang [38], Gunasekaran et al. [39], Menon et al. [40], Hayter et al. [41], Goldman et al. [20], Khavari et al. [42], Soltani et al. [43], and Bakhtiari et al. [44]. However, these models face several challenges including: (1) dispersion and lack of transparency in defining and explaining the components of organizational agility, (2) ambiguity and lack of coherence of these components in the health system, and (3) unclear relationships between organizational agility and social responsibility approach. As a result, the process of transforming theoretical foundations into practical action within organizations has not been effectively achieved [41, 45, 46].

For this reason, given the rapid pace of changes in the world, the health system of each society must adapt. Universities of medical sciences must also adapt to changes in society, especially at the level of the educational system, in order to better assume social responsibility. The educational system of universities of medical sciences must be arranged in such a way that it continuously monitors the needs of society and takes the necessary measures. However, various studies have shown that universities of medical sciences have not yet carried out social responsibility in a satisfactory manner, and the scattered activities have not solved the existing problems because traditional approaches are still used in their educational system. One of the factors that can help solve this problem is the agility of universities of medical sciences. Therefore, the present study intends to identify the components that affect the agility of the educational system of universities of medical sciences and to provide a basis for improving their performance from the perspective of social responsibility.

Methods

Integrative review

This review follows an integrative review methodology. Integrative reviews critique and synthesize earlier literature by forming new frameworks and perspectives on the investigated topic [47]. They not only summarize empirical evidence, scope literature, and pinpoint avenues for further research, but produce metatheory and push forward research agendas. An Integrative review is a particularly apt review type for this study because the concept of organizational agility in the educational system of universities of medical sciences with a social responsibility approach is complex, multidimensional, and discussed across several bodies of literature. This review type is thus adequate to synthesize knowledge from various fields of literature, such as health professions education, organizational management, public administration, health policy and social accountability, which rely on different epistemological and methodological approaches to organizational change and responsibility. Moreover, it enables the integration of evidence generated in different disciplinary and cultural contexts, in which expectations and manifestations of organizational agility and social responsibility may vary substantially [48]. In this context, studies from non-educational or non-medical organizational settings were included when they provided relevant conceptual insights into organizational agility, dynamic capabilities, or socially responsible organizational behavior. Their findings were used conceptually to shape the model of socially responsible universities of medical sciences.

Overall, this study represents a relatively broad integrative review that synthesizes emerging topics that require initial synthesis [47]. This integrative review followed the five-step framework proposed by Whittemore & knafl’s [49]: problem identification, literature search, data evaluation, data analysis, and presentation of findings. To enhance the transparency and auditability of the review process, selected reporting elements commonly used in evidence synthesis were incorporated. In particular, PRISMA 2020 was used solely as a reporting guide to provide a clear and transparent description of record identification, screening, eligibility assessment, and inclusion through a flow diagram within the literature search stage of the integrative review. This use of PRISMA does not indicate that the study was designed or conducted as a conventional systematic review; rather, these elements were adopted only to enhance the quality of reporting and transparency of the integrative review.

Search strategies

In order to identify all articles related to the influential components of organizational agility in universities of medical sciences with a social responsibility approach, researchers searched seven databases including: Wiley Online Library, PubMed, Scopus, ScienceDirect, and ERIC, and national databases such as Magiran and Irandoc. In addition, grey literature sources such as conference and seminar proceedings, national and international institutional reports, policy documents, and academic theses related to the influential components of organizational agility in universities of medical sciences with a social responsibility approach were also reviewed. Grey literature was defined as non–peer-reviewed materials in these formats. Grey literature sources were included if they (a) addressed components that affect the dynamic capabilities and empowerment of organizational agility in the educational system of universities of medical sciences or other higher education institutions; (b) explicitly linked these constructs to a social responsibility approach; and (c) provided sufficient methodological and contextual detail to allow meaningful data extraction and synthesis. Grey literature sources that consisted only of brief summaries without methodological information or that did not clearly address the above concepts were excluded. When only an abstract was available, the corresponding author was contacted to obtain the full text of the article.

To find relevant articles in databases, the following keywords were used: Organization, Strategic, Strategic Planning, Agility, Organizational Agility, Academic Agility, Healthcare Agility, Agile Leadership, Research Agility, Dynamic Capabilities, Enablers, Empowerment, Medical School, Medical Education, Medical University, Education, Higher Education, Health Professions Education, Emerging Technologies, Digital Transformation, Medical Innovation, Social Responsibility, Social Responsiveness, Social Accountability, Responsibility and Obligation, Factors Affecting Agility, Agility in Higher Education, Organizational Agility in Medical Universities, Empowerment and Dynamic Capabilities in Academic Research, Agile Leadership in Management, Dynamic Capabilities in Healthcare, Organizational Agility and Corporate Social Responsibility, Organizational Agility Integrating Social Responsibility. These keywords were combined using Boolean operators (AND, OR, and NOT) and searched in both national and international databases. In order to preserve all valuable data, no time limit was considered, and all relevant articles published up to September 11, 2025 were evaluated. To enhance the comprehensiveness of the search, the reference lists of the included studies were manually checked to identify any additional relevant sources. The full database-specific search strings, including all Boolean operators, abbreviation symbols, and field tags used for each concept in each database, are provided in Supplementary File 1 to facilitate search reproducibility.

In the identification stage, a comprehensive search was conducted across the selected databases and other relevant sources, and the processes of record identification, screening, eligibility assessment, and study inclusion were documented using a PRISMA 2020-informed flow diagram adapted for this integrative review. Two reviewers independently screened the titles and abstracts of all records identified through the search strategy. Records that appeared potentially relevant or for which eligibility could not be determined from the title and abstract alone were retrieved for full-text assessment. In total, 2533 records were identified. After removal of 509 duplicates using EndNote 21, 2024 records remained for title and abstract screening. Of these, 1869 records were excluded due to lack of clear relevance to the review aim and questions. The full text of the remaining 155 articles was then independently assessed against the eligibility criteria. After full text assessment, 140 articles were excluded. The main reasons for exclusion included insufficient alignment with the review aim, failure to meet the eligibility criteria in terms of content or publication type, insufficient methodological or conceptual detail to support meaningful data extraction, and unavailability of full text despite attempts to contact the relevant authors. Fifteen studies were included in the integrative review (Fig. 1).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) articles published in English or Persian; (2) studies that address factors, dimensions, or components related to organizational agility and dynamic capabilities in the educational system of universities of medical sciences with relevance to a social responsibility approach; (3) studies that are relevant to the aim and research questions of this review; and (4) studies that were conducted in educational (medical sciences and non‑medical sciences) or other organizational settings and that provide relevant conceptual evidence for conceptual transferability to the context of universities of medical sciences. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) duplicate records; (2) studies that lack sufficient conceptual or methodological information for meaningful data extraction; (3) studies that were not sufficiently aligned with the review aim and eligibility criteria; and (4) studies for which the full text was unavailable despite attempts to contact the corresponding authors.

After each reviewer carefully studied the titles and abstracts of all retrieved articles, a large number of records were excluded as being irrelevant to the aim of the study. If it was not possible to make a decision about the article after reading the title and abstract, the full text was consulted. To ensure that all relevant documents were retrieved, the reference lists of the included articles were also reviewed. If there was no agreement between the two reviewers on one or more articles, a third researcher was consulted to resolve the disagreement. The search strategy was intentionally broad in order to include all potentially relevant articles.

Quality appraisal

Given the methodological heterogeneity of the included studies, methodological quality was appraised using design-appropriate tools. The results of the quality appraisal were not used as rigid exclusion criteria; instead, they informed the interpretive weighting of evidence during the synthesis. Findings from studies with higher methodological quality were given greater prominence in shaping the categories and the final conceptual model, whereas findings from lower-quality studies were considered more cautiously and mainly used to complement or nuance the emerging components.

The quality of the included studies was assessed separately by the same two researchers using design-appropriate appraisal tools, including the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) [50] at three levels of assessment (Yes, No, Can’t tell), the Scale for the Assessment of Narrative Review Articles (SANRA) [51] at three levels of assessment (2 (High), 1 (Medium), 0 (Low)), the Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal tools (JBI) [52, 53] at four levels of assessment (Yes, No, Unclear, Not applicable), the Critical Appraisal Skills Program (CASP) [54] at three levels of assessment (Yes, No, Can’t tell), and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) [55] at four levels of assessment (Clearly, Partially, Not reported, Not applicable) checklists.

For each included study, the judgments derived from the design appropriate appraisal tool were synthesized into an overall qualitative assessment of methodological quality (high, moderate, or low). This assessment was reached through consensus between the two researchers by considering the extent to which key methodological criteria were met and the potential impact of any identified limitations on the reliability and transferability of the findings, rather than by computing numerical scores or applying rigid cut-off thresholds. Studies appraised as high quality provided the primary evidence basis for developing and refining the categories and the final conceptual model, whereas studies assessed as moderate or low quality were used more cautiously, mainly to complement or further specify emerging components that were already supported by higher quality evidence.

Three studies (Bakhtiari and Sajjad, George et al., and Sharifi and Zhang) were appraised using the MMAT checklist. The MMAT checklist contains 17 questions: 2 questions for the initial screening, 5 questions for qualitative studies, 5 questions for quantitative descriptive and non-randomized studies, and 5 questions to assess the quality of the combination of both qualitative and quantitative studies and its use helps to understand and appraise a mixed method study. Also, seven studies (Bagheri et al., Aghaee and Aghaee, Heaton et al., Furnival et al., Žitkienė and Deksnys, Esfijani et al., Crocitto and Youssef) were appraised using the SANRA checklist. These articles were additionally evaluated using the JBI checklist for Text & Opinion appraisal to ensure a comprehensive evaluation of both reporting quality and conceptual rigor. The SANRA checklist contains 6 items and the JBI checklist contains 6 questions, and their use helps to understand and appraise a Conceptual/Theoretical review study. In addition, one conceptual study (Ali et al.), which was conducted as a systematic review, was appraised using the CASP Systematic Review Checklist and the PRISMA 2020 checklist. The CASP Systematic Review Checklist was used to critically appraise that review’s methodological quality and risk of bias, while the full PRISMA 2020 checklist was applied to evaluate the completeness and transparency of its reporting. The CASP Systematic Review Checklist contains 10 questions and the PRISMA 2020 checklist contains 27 items. Furthermore, one study (Ghane Ebadi et al.) was appraised using the CASP for Qualitative Research checklist; it was a grounded theory study. This checklist contains 10 questions in 3 sections. Finally, three studies (Safdarian et al., Rababah et al., Sánchez-Hernández & Mainardes) were appraised using the JBI Checklist for Analytical Cross-Sectional Studies to appraise the methodological quality of studies. Two were survey studies and one was a descriptive-correlational study. This checklist contains 8 questions (a summary of the quality appraisal outcomes for each study is presented in Supplementary File 2).

Sampling

In addition to the structured descriptive information, findings related to the research questions were extracted as primary analytical data. The unit of analysis consisted of textual sections of the results and, where applicable, discussion sections that addressed the components of educational empowerment, dynamic capabilities, or organizational agility in the social responsibility approach. These segments were first organized in an analytical matrix and then imported into MAXQDA 24 for framework-guided deductive content analysis with an open approach to inductive refinement.

Data synthesis

Data were analyzed using a framework-guided deductive content analysis approach, complemented by inductive modification where necessary. Two researchers (XX and YY) participated in the analytical process. The process of deductive content analysis in this study was carried out in the following steps:

(1) Implementing the coding process included: (a) Reviewing the data: the extracted text units (mainly from the results and discussion sections of the included studies) were read several times independently by two researchers to gain an overall understanding of the content and identify meaning units relevant to the research questions. (b) Creating initial codes was guided by the model proposed by Ghane Ebadi et al. [59], which provided a set of a priori dimensions for organizational agility, educational empowerment, and educational dynamic capabilities. This model was used as a sensitizing framework to direct attention to relevant conceptual areas and to ensure comprehensive coverage of the constructs, rather than as a rigid template to be mechanically confirmed. Coding was performed using a combination of MAXQDA 24 and manual procedures. In line with a hybrid deductive-inductive logic, the initial categories derived from this framework were considered provisional. Whenever the findings of the included studies did not fit meaningfully into the pre-defined dimensions, existing categories were modified (e.g., expanded or relabeled) and new subcategories were inductively created to accommodate emergent patterns and unanticipated insights. In this way, the analysis remained anchored in the existing model while allowing for data-driven modification and expansion of its categories. Each meaning unit was assigned one or more codes that reflected its main content in relation to this framework. After independent coding, the two researchers compared their code lists and resolved differences through discussion until consensus was reached. (c) Categorizing the data: codes that captured similar meanings were grouped into subcategories based on conceptual similarity (for example, codes referring to internal and external organizational collaboration, flexibility, and technology transfer to various levels were grouped under the “Educational Structural Empowerment” subcategory). (d) Developing final themes: related subcategories were merged into broader categories (themes) that represented higher-order conceptual domains (for example, “educational empowerment” and “dynamic educational capabilities”), and (e) Writing a report. The final categories and subcategories were described, and illustrative examples were provided to show how data excerpts from the included studies were translated into codes, subcategories, and main categories (Table 2). Accordingly, the identified components were divided into two main categories and their related subcategories: Educational Empowerment (Educational Structural Empowerment and Human Asset Empowerment in Education, which itself included Personal and Psychological Empowerment, General Job Empowerment, and Managers and Leaders Empowerment), and Educational Dynamic Capabilities (Educational Sensing Capability, Educational Seizing Capability, and Educational Reconfiguration Capability) (Table 3).

(2) Determining validity and reliability. Several strategies were used to enhance the trustworthiness of the synthesis. A self-review procedure was used to improve internal validity. In addition, the same two researchers independently evaluated the extracted text units, subcategories, and main categories, and then compared and discussed their interpretations to reach a consensus (analyst triangulation). The extracted text units, subcategories, and main categories were then sent to several experts in the field of medical education for validation, and their opinions were used in the implementation, coding, categorization, and presentation of the final model. Finally, by providing appropriate quotations and examples from the included studies, the research enhanced the credibility and transferability criteria [56].

In line with the methodology of the integrative review, the aim of this synthesis was to develop a robust, conceptually integrated model of educational empowerment and educational dynamic capabilities, rather than to conduct a statistical meta-analysis. Accordingly, a framework-guided deductive content analysis, with inductive modification, was used to identify converging and complementary patterns across methodologically diverse studies. Methodological heterogeneity was managed by explicitly weighting higher-quality evidence in shaping the categories and the final conceptual model, while lower-quality studies were used more cautiously to complement, refine, or further specify components that were already supported by stronger evidence.

The choice of a hybrid, framework-guided deductive content analysis approach was consistent with both the nature of the available evidence and the conceptual aims of this study. Using the framework proposed by Ghane Ebadi et al. [59] as a sensitizing structure provided an initial deductive coding framework, while categories were subjected to repeated inductive modification and expansion based on empirical patterns. This hybrid strategy allowed us, on the one hand, to organize the heterogeneous findings in a conceptually coherent way, and, on the other hand, to incorporate truly novel constructs and to restate existing dimensions where evidence indicated that such modification was necessary.

Results

This section presents the findings of data extraction from 15 included articles. As mentioned in the quality appraisal section, the synthesis interpreted recurring patterns with greater confidence when they were supported by higher-quality studies, while considering insights from lower-quality studies as more tentative.

Studies conducted in Iran are presented first, followed by those from other countries.

The study by Bakhtiari and Sajjad [44] presents a model of the education system with an agility approach identified in this article, which includes the following dimensions: Educational Components (Educational Goals, Educational Needs Assessment, and Educational Subject), Attitudinal–motivational components (Organizational Culture, Social Justice, Accountability, and Foresight), Speed Components (Technology), Managerial Components (Empowerment, Practical and Skills Training, and Financial Resource Allocation), and Competence Components (Flexibility, Organizational Communication, and Teamwork).

The study by Bagheri et al. [63] presents a conceptual model for the dynamic empowerment of medical science education managers, consisting of six categories: Organizational–Structural factors, Decision-Making factors, Individual belief factors (Individual Personality factors, and Psychological factors), Environmental factors (Extra-Organizational, and Intra-Organizational), Educational factors, and Dynamic Empowerment factors.

The study by Ghane Ebadi et al. [59] presents an organizational agility model for Tehran state universities consisting of five categories: Causal Categories or Agility Drivers (Competition in the Academic Business Environment, Structural and Content Changes in the University, and Stakeholder Expectations), Core Categories or Agility Enablers (Information Technology, Intra-Organizational Communications, Organizational Financial Resources, and Specialized Human Asset), Strategic Categories or Agility Capabilities (Resource Management, Identifying and Determining Process Priorities, Selection of the Preferred Scenario, and Continuous Monitoring of the Level of Agility), Outcome Categories or Agility Outcomes (Adaptation of Structures and Processes, Development of Agility Drivers, Improvement in the Level of Flexibility, Optimized Modelling of Agility Functions, and Responsiveness to Student and Stakeholder Needs), and Environmental and Contextual Categories or Contextual Factors (University Social Model, Organizational Structure Model, Government Policies and Regulations, Environmental Technological Changes, Position of Competitors, and Political, Social, Economic, and Cultural Transformations).

The study by Safdarian et al. [61] identifies the components of individual empowerment and of organizational agility at Isfahan University of Medical Sciences and examines their relationships. Individual empowerment included: Meaningfulness, Competence, Autonomy, Impact, Participation, Trust, Idealism, Self-Control, and Creativity. Organizational agility included: Leadership and Management, Culture, Innovation, Strategy, Change and Learning, Organizational Structure, Information Technology, Coherence and Integrity, Opportunism, Shared Values, and Multitasking.

The study by Aghaee and Aghaee [66] presents a conceptual model of organizational agility that includes four main dimensions: Organizational factors, Strategic factors, Technological factors, and Human factors. Among all the identified subcomponents, eight factors—Adaptation to Change, Quick Response, Spending Money, Flexibility, Information Technology Infrastructure, Speed of Delivery, Virtual Organization, and Empowered and Multi-skilled Employees—ranked first to eighth in priority.

The following section presents findings from studies conducted in other countries:

The study by George et al. [57] proposes a conceptual framework that identifies four institutional profiles for the strategic management of social responsibilities, based on how social responsibility is reflected in universities’ strategic plans and comprehensive fundraising campaigns: Mission-focused (Emphasizing traditional academic priorities and largely neglecting social responsibilities in both planning and implementation), Planning-focused (Explicitly integrating social responsibility into strategic plans but not consistently maintaining this focus in implementation and campaigns), Opportunity-focused (Addressing social responsibility opportunistically during implementation and campaigns rather than embedding it in formal strategic plans), and the Savvy strategist, in which social responsibilities become core elements of both strategic planning and strategy implementation—through initiatives such as promoting social justice, environmental sustainability, and economic development, building partnerships, engaging students and faculty in community service, and establishing dedicated structures and resources to support equity, diversity, and inclusion.

The study by Rababah et al. [64] identifies indicators for assessing the level of USR development in the BRICS countries and factors affecting it. Indicators for assessing university social responsibility include: Investment, Adherence to Work Schedules, Provision of Career and Personal Development Opportunities, Job Stability, and others. Factors affecting USR development include Socially Responsible Management, Effective Communication between Universities and Stakeholders, Socially Oriented Students’ Learning, and University Funding.

The study by Heaton et al. [33] presents a system-level view of dynamic capabilities and campus entrepreneurship, consisting of six elements: Sensing, Value Creation for Constituents, Seizing, Transforming, Value Capture, and the University Ecosystem.

The study by Ali et al. [65] identifies ten thematic categorizations of the USR concept, namely: Adaptation of University Social Responsibility through Industry Indicators, Analysis of University Social Responsibility Activities, Review of the Evolution of University Social Responsibility, the University’s Role as a Change Agent through Social Responsibility, University Social Responsibility Measurement, Development of University Social Responsibility Conceptual Frameworks, the Logic and Rationale of University Social Responsibility, University Social Responsibility as a Context for Upgrading Education, University Social Responsibility Impact Assessment, and University Social Responsibility and Stakeholder Perception.

The study by Furnival et al. [58] presents three capability-based performance theories that can be used to support research on improvement capability. These three theories are the resource-based view, the organizational ambidexterity view, and the dynamic capabilities view. The article discusses the characteristics of each theory and the limitations of using them individually. These theories have been developed in the private sector, primarily in rapidly changing environments, and have rarely been applied to the health sector. In this study, a dynamic capabilities view of improvement capability is presented, comprising three dimensions: Sensing (Service-user focus and Stakeholder and Supplier focus), Seizing (Employee Commitment, Leadership Commitment, and Organizational Culture), and Reconfiguring (Data and Performance Improvement, Process Improvement and Learning, and Strategy and Governance).

The study by Žitkienė and Deksnys [60] presents a conceptual model consisting of four main components: Agility Drivers, Agile Enablers, Agile Capabilities (Sensing Capabilities, and Response Capabilities), and Agile Practices (Organizational Practices, Employee Empowerment Practices, Customer Enrichment Practices, and Cooperation Practices).

The study by Sánchez-Hernández & Mainardes [69] presents a conceptual model of university social responsibility from a student-based analysis in Brazil, which consists of responsible management (governance and management), responsible education programs, social intrapreneurship culture, student satisfaction, and quality.

The study by Esfijani et al. [67] presents an ontology of university social responsibility consisting of seven components: Service, Ethics, Transparency, Engagement, Research, Education, and University Stakeholders.

The study by Crocitto and Youssef [68] presents a human-side model of organizational agility consisting of three components: Organizational Agility (Quality, Speed, and Cost), Manufacturing Agility (Responsiveness and Flexibility), and Advanced Manufacturing and Information Technologies (Suppliers, Organizational Members, Leadership, Culture and Reward Systems, and Customers).

Finally, the study by Sharifi and Zhang [62] presents a conceptual model consisting of three components: Agility Drivers (Changes in Market, Changes in Competition, Changes in Customer Requirements, Changes in Technology, and Changes in Social factors), Agility Capabilities (Responsiveness, Competency, Flexibility, and Speed), and Agility Practices (Practices, Methods, and Tools).

Taken together, the comparison of these models and the framework-guided deductive content analysis, complemented by inductive modification, led to the development of the final framework summarized in (Table 3). This framework shows that the present synthesis did not merely map the extracted findings onto the pre-existing model of Ghane Ebadi et al. [59], but iteratively refined and contextualized it for social responsibility universities of medical sciences. In the final model, the broad “Drivers-Capabilities-Outcomes” structure proposed by Ghane Ebadi et al. was reconfigured into two main pillars: Educational Empowerment and Educational Dynamic Capabilities. These pillars and their subcategories were constructed by using the original dimensions as provisional deductive guides and then inductively expanding, relabeling, or adding categories whenever the patterns reported in the included studies could not be adequately represented within the more general categories of the original model.

In the final framework, these two pillars are not value-neutral. Educational Empowerment and Educational Dynamic Capabilities are explicitly oriented and interpreted through the lens of social responsibility, such that their relevance is defined by how, together, they enable universities of medical sciences to identify, prioritize, and respond to the needs of society and communities, rather than merely improving internal efficiency. This value orientation emerged from iteratively interpreting the coded categories with regard to recurring themes around responsibility, justice, transparency, and stakeholder participation across the included studies.

Discussion

A comparative analysis of the included articles shows that, despite differences in geographical context and study design, a common core emerges in explanations of organizational agility with a social responsibility orientation. In most studies, agility is depicted as a multi-layered phenomenon that starts from environmental drivers (such as technological changes, competition, social changes, and stakeholder expectations) and leads to organizational and social outcomes through empowerment and dynamic capabilities. This logic is consistent with classical agility frameworks, which view agility as the result of interactions among environmental drivers or pressures, agility capabilities, and implementation methods or practices [33, 44, 57–62]. At the micro level, this perspective of social responsibility also guides the training of students to assess patients in their social context and to integrate social determinants of health into clinical reasoning and decision-making. From the perspective of intersecting components, almost all studies emphasize the central roles of governance and educational/managerial leadership (including strategic direction, timely decision-making, prioritization and resource allocation), information technology and data infrastructure (as the engine of speed, coordination and evidence-based decision-making), and organizational communication and stakeholder engagement [63–59, 62, 66, 67]. Within the empowerment dimension at the levels of human asset and organizational culture, the findings highlight the empowerment of managers and employees, teamwork and multi-skilling, and the formation of a culture of learning, innovation, and acceptance of change. Furthermore, the attitudes and professional culture of academic and healthcare personnel are critical drivers of socially responsible agility. Agility is thus presented not simply as a structural change but as the result of synergy among humans, processes, and technology [44, 59–61, 63, 68]. In the dynamic capabilities dimension, there is significant overlap around abilities to monitor and interpret changes, exploit opportunities and act quickly, and reconfigure structures and processes. In other words, dynamic capabilities refer to the organization’s ability to reorganize its resources and procedures in changing environments [33, 58, 60]. Taken together, these sensing, seizing, and reconfiguration processes constitute a proactive capacity that enables universities of medical sciences to anticipate emerging needs and adjust their educational structures and programs, rather than merely reacting to external pressures. In line with a social responsibility approach, most studies highlight values such as accountability, transparency, ethics, justice, and stakeholder participation as guiding organizational agility. This means that in these studies, the ultimate goal of agility in universities is not simply to increase speed, but to respond intelligently and fairly to the needs of society [44, 57, 64, 65, 67, 69].

Although these patterns of convergence are prominent, the included studies also reveal significant divergences and tensions that complicate the picture of organizational agility with a social responsibility orientation. For example, some studies explicitly emphasize the role of a clear vision and strategic direction as key factors of agility (e.g., Bakhtiari and Sajjad; Bagheri et al.; Ghane Ebadi et al.; Aghaee and Aghaee; George et al.; Sharifi and Zhang), while others pay little or no attention to this aspect and instead focus on operational, cultural, or technical factors (e.g., Sánchez-Hernández & Mainardes; Ali et al.; Rababah et al.; Esfijani et al.; Heaton et al.; Furnival et al.; Žitkienė and Deksnys; Crocitto and Youssef). Similarly, while several articles portray agility primarily as a beneficial attribute that increases accountability and effectiveness, a smaller subset points to more ambivalent or context-dependent effects, such as increased workload, implementation fatigue, or fragmentation when changes are implemented without adequate resources or coordination. Collectively, these divergences show that organizational agility is not a uniform or monolithic phenomenon, but manifests differently depending on governance models, resource levels, and the maturity of existing systems. They also indicate that convergence in themes must be interpreted alongside these contextual possibilities.

In the educational system of universities of medical sciences, one of the central challenges is the conceptual and operational ambiguity regarding agility and its implementation requirements. The concept of organizational agility is still not well defined in many institutions, leads to heterogeneous perceptions and implementations. across universities. In addition, a lack of clear alignment between mission, educational planning, and implementation mechanisms, together with.

ambiguity in administrative roles and structures, can slow down and misalign decision-making and responsibility [44, 57]. As a result, the coherence of educational programs is reduced, achieving the expected competencies in graduates becomes more difficult, and effectiveness in real practice is weakened [44]. In addition, the agility approach gains managerial value for universities of medical sciences when it is linked to social responsibility in meeting societal needs. To achieve this, universities should focus on two dimensions: empowerment and dynamic capabilities, which, according to the documentation, can be directly enhanced through university agility [33, 44, 58, 67]. Accordingly, universities of medical sciences need to use a change management approach in order to increase their level of agility. Change management helps to shape a clear vision, develop a strategic plan, remove implementation barriers, and institutionalize changes in structures and procedures, etc [44, 59, 63, 66]. In this contexts, several factors can help reduce resistance to change; the most important of these are mentioned below:

The first factor is formulating a clear and shared vision. The vision should depict the desired future in a way that orients decisions, prioritization, and alignment of university components. So that employees and managers know where the university is headed and what steps are needed. Change management literature similarly emphasizes that a strategic vision, linked to concrete plans and actions, is essential for creating alignment and coherent movement. An effective vision must be evidence-based and based on environmental monitoring and strategic foresight, not merely an idealistic statement, so that universities can make coherent and reliable decisions under uncertainty. In other words, the goal of foresight is not to predict the future definitively, but rather to increase the ability of universities of medical sciences to detect trends and risks in a timely manner, prepare for possible scenarios, and design response options. As a result, improved quality of decision-making and the speed of action of universities of medical sciences. In contrast, the dominance of traditional approaches and bureaucratic hierarchical structures is usually associated with prolonged decision-making cycles, slow coordination between components of universities of medical sciences, and reduced flexibility, limiting the ability of universities of medical sciences to deal with stimuli [44, 57, 59, 62, 65, 66, 69]. For example, the experience of crises such as the COVID-19 pandemic has also shown that effective responses to uncertainty require agile governance and adaptive decision-making mechanisms, enabling universities to react quickly within existing frameworks and, when necessary, to advance systemic reforms with speed and coherence. This factor is consistent with the studies of Bakhtiari and Sajjad, Ghane Ebadi et al., Aghaee and Aghaee, Sharifi and Zhang, George et al., Ali et al., and Sánchez-Hernández & Mainardes. However, this factor is not mentioned in the studies of Bagheri et al., Safdarian et al., Rababah et al., Heaton et al., Furnival et al., Žitkienė and Deksnys, Esfijani et al., and Crocitto and Youssef.

The next factor in change management is developing capabilities through targeted education and structured communication. After developing a vision, universities of medical sciences must transform it into a shared understanding across the organization. This requires a systematic communication plan that provides clear answers to questions such as “Why this change?”, “What is changing?” and “what does this change mean for me?” tailored to different groups of managers, staff, and experts, and reinforced throughout implementation. Communication primarily fosters awareness and acceptance, whereas education and coaching develop knowledge and the ability to perform tasks in new ways. Employees must know what skills and behaviors are required and how to apply them in everyday work if change is to move from slogans to performance. Effective education should take the form of capability building institutionalization, not just classical courses; for example short and practical learning. Practice in real settings, constructive feedback, role modelling by managers, and involving employees in creating new norms in the university.This combination of communication and education helps to translate the vision into coordinated behaviors in practice and enables universities to implement changes more quickly and with less resistance [44, 63–60, 66, 68, 69].

Therefore, this approach helps to translate the vision message into coordinated behaviors in practice, enabling universities of medical sciences to implement changes more quickly and with less resistance [44, 63–60, 66, 68, 69]. This factor is consistent with the studies of Bakhtiari and Sajjad, Bagheri et al., Ghane Ebadi et al., Aghaee and Aghaee, Sánchez-Hernández & Mainardes, Ali et al., Rababah et al., Furnival et al., Žitkienė and Deksnys, and Crocitto and Youssef. However, this factor is not mentioned in the studies of Safdarian et al., George et al., Heaton et al., Esfijani et al., and Sharifi and Zhang.

Education may be a necessary condition for change, but it is not sufficient on its own, because learning is translated into new behavior only when employees are sufficiently motivated. Therefore, the next factor in organizational agility is employee motivation. Universities of medical sciences should actively encourage participation in change and sustain desirable behaviors through reinforcement and consolidation mechanisms. Incentive packages should make the benefits and achievements of change tangible and visible, for example by identifying and communicating short-term successes in a timely manner, thereby maintaining energy and commitment. One of the most effective tools is a fair and transparent recognition and reward system in which the efforts and measurable results of individuals or teams involved in implementing change are formally acknowledged. This appreciation may take the form of awards, performance recognition programs, or non-financial and social rewards, but the criteria must be clear, performance-based, accessible, and perceived as fair so that the message is reinforced that participation in change is valuable and visible [44, 58–61, 63, 64, 68]. This factor is consistent with the studies of Bakhtiari and Sajjad, Ghane Ebadi et al., Safdarian et al., Bagheri et al., Rababah et al., Furnival et al., Žitkienė and Deksnys, and Crocitto and Youssef. However, this factor is not mentioned in the studies of Sánchez-Hernández & Mainardes, Ali et al., Esfijani et al., George et al., Heaton et al., Sharifi and Zhang, and Aghaee and Aghaee.

The next factor in improving organizational agility is resource adequacy and alignment. Resources are not limited to budget, but include an interconnected set of human resources (expert, capable, and multi-skilled staff), physical and infrastructure resources (equipment, facilities, and technological infrastructure), and financial resources. In technology-driven changes, universities need real investment and infrastructure provision to design, implement, and maintain solutions. thus, resources are an integral part of the agility process and must be purposefully prioritized and allocated at the management level. If universities have a clear vision, and education pathway, and motivated human asset but do not provide financial and infrastructural support commensurate with the volume and complexity of change, agility will be disrupted and manifest as organizational waste including: increased rework, slow implementation, frequent stoppages, burnout, and decreased trust in the transformation plan. One of the key tasks of management on the path to agility is therefore to remove obstacles and enable action by creating supportive capacities, providing necessary resources, and reallocating them to transformation priorities in a timely manner [33, 44, 57–62, 64, 66, 68]. This factor is consistent with the studies of Bakhtiari and Sajjad, Ghane Ebadi et al., Aghaee and Aghaee, Rababah et al., George et al., Heaton et al., Furnival et al., Žitkienė and Deksnys, Crocitto and Youssef, Sharifi and Zhang, and Safdarian et al. However, this factor is not mentioned in the studies of Sánchez-Hernández & Mainardes, Ali et al., Esfijani et al., and Bagheri et al.

At the same time, it is important not to view organizational agility as an inherently and universally desirable attribute. While most included studies frame agility in positive terms, the evidence also points to trade-offs and unintended consequences, such as decision fatigue, role ambiguity, burnout, and the risk of exacerbating inequalities among units with different baseline capacities. Excessive emphasis on speed and flexibility, if not based on clear values and accountability mechanisms, may also lead to strategic deviation or reactive decision-making. These considerations underscore the need to conceptualize “responsible agility” not as uncritical acceleration, but as a balanced capacity to adapt in ways that remain aligned with the social mission and ethical commitments of universities.

The next determining factor in moving the change process to the implementation stage is the development of an action plan. If strategy is not translated into specific and manageable actions, a gap will arise between what universities of medical sciences intend to do and what they actually do, and change will remain at the level of slogans. The action plan is the mechanism that converts strategy into measurable objectives, initiatives and projects, schedules, responsibilities, resource allocations, and monitoring systems. It clarifies what needs to be done, by whom, when, with what resources, and under which indicators. In addition to designing a course of action, the plan should also specify how to monitor progress and make corrections along the way. because implementing change is a dynamic process, and without feedback and corrective mechanisms, the likelihood of deviation, rework, and loss of effectiveness increase [44, 57, 59, 62, 63, 66]. This factor is consistent with the studies of Bakhtiari and Sajjad, Bagheri et al., Ghane Ebadi et al., Aghaee and Aghaee, George et al., and Sharifi and Zhang. However, this factor is not mentioned in the studies of Sánchez-Hernández & Mainardes, Ali et al., Rababah et al., Esfijani et al., Heaton et al., Furnival et al., Žitkienė and Deksnys, Crocitto and Youssef, and Safdarian et al.

Within the framework of social responsibility in university of medical sciences, organizational agility is most meaningful and defensible when it is linked to fulfilling the social mission. Universities must be able to organize their educational activities in a targeted manner in line with the priority needs of society and stakeholders [44, 57, 64, 65, 67]. Accordingly, the five components: vision, education/communication, motivation, resources, and action plan, can be considered as an implementation chain of social responsibility. Weakness or absence of any link disrupts the process of realizing the social mission: without vision, activities remain scattered and strategic confusion emerges [44, 57, 59, 62, 66]; without education and communication, the vision does not become shared understanding and alignment; without motivation, education does not lead to sustained behavior change, without sufficient resources, implementation is accompanied by waste, frequent stoppages, burnout; and without an action plan, the strategic vision is not translated into concrete responsibilities, timelines, and monitoring, and change remains at the level of slogans [33, 58–60, 62, 64, 66, 68].

From a social responsibility perspective, this change management chain can be understood as a practical pathway to operationalizing the social mission of universities of medical sciences. Social responsibility is not merely an abstract value; it is enacted at every link in this chain—from how commitments to health and community equity are articulated in the vision to how education/communication, motivation, resources, and action plans align behaviors, investments, and outcomes with societal needs. In this configuration, educational dynamic capabilities and educational empowerment work together as an integrated framework, while social responsibility provides the normative and evaluative criteria for guiding and assessing these efforts in relation to the broader community.

The consequences of disruptions in this chain are not limited to internal failure, but directly harm the social functioning of universities of medical sciences. In addition to education and research, these universities have a social and economic role in the form of their ‘third mission” (interaction with society), and part of their legitimacy and effectiveness depends on how well they connect with society, respond to real needs, and create social impact [57, 65, 67]. Delays or slowness in fulfilling their educational role can make the space for responding to needs more attractive and accessible to non-university actors, gradually weakening the authoritative position of universities. In this context, agility does not mean mere reaction or haste, but rather a credible, evidence-based, and flexible response that keeps universities on track to fulfill their social mission and strengthen social trust and effectiveness [33, 57, 60, 62].

From a theoretical perspective, this integrative review does not simply replicate the framework proposed by Ghane Ebadi et al. [59], but uses it as a deductive starting point and then inductively contextualizes and extends it for socially responsible universities of medical sciences. Building on the primary logic of “Drivers-Capabilities-Outcomes,” our synthesis reconfigures this structure into two empirical pillars (educational empowerment and educational dynamic capabilities) and links them to a tangible change management pathway. In doing so, the review illuminates how empowering conditions, dynamic capabilities, and social responsibility are interconnected as parts of a coherent educational change process, rather than as separate components.

This interpretation is based on a critical-realist and pragmatist stance, which assumes that organizational structures and capabilities in medical education are real but socially constructed and can be inferred from patterns of practice. within this stance, the analysis combined a deductive coding scheme guided by the framework with inductive modification of categories and relationships, allowing new configurations to emerge when empirical patterns could not be accommodated in the initial model. Educational dynamic capabilities and educational empowerment are seen as interdependent analytical constructs: dynamic capabilities refer to structured processes of sensing, seizing, and reconfiguration, while empowering conditions refer to configurations of structural and human asset that enable and constrain these processes. Social responsibility is understood both as a normative framework (specifying what universities should contribute to society) and as an operational construct that can be observed in governance arrangements, educational programs, and outcome patterns. This ontological and epistemological position makes it possible to integrate a strategic management perspective (through the combined lens of dynamic capabilities and empowerment) with a value-driven agenda (social responsibility) in a way that remains empirically tractable and sensitive to context.

This interpretation is consistent with broader work on dynamic capabilities, which emphasizes sensing, seizing, and reconfiguration in changing environments, and also aligns with critical perspectives that caution against treating agility as a universal solution. By considering agility as a value-driven and context-dependent construct, particularly in governmental and educational institutions, the present framework contributes to debates about the appropriate scope of “agile” reforms in environments where stability, equity, and responsibility are equally important goals. This reconfiguration, together with the identification and integration of new subcategories under these two pillars, represents the main conceptual contribution of this study beyond the original framework proposed by Ghane Ebadi et al.

In interpreting the findings of this study, it is important to note that It was conducted as an integrative review with conceptual and thematic synthesis, rather than a statistical meta-analysis. In this type of review, the adequacy of the evidence base depends less on the number of included studies than on the conceptual richness of the data and the diversity of perspectives. Although only 15 studies were ultimately included from 2533 identified articles, they collectively provide sufficient depth and breadth to support a robust synthesis: they cover a range of organizational and geographical contexts, employ qualitative, quantitative, and mixed-method designs, and converge on recurring themes around governance and leadership, data and technology infrastructure, stakeholder engagement, human and cultural empowerment, and dynamic capabilities. This convergence across heterogeneous methods and settings suggests that the strength of the synthesis stems from the consistency and coherence of the extracted themes in diverse contexts, rather than from a large numerical sample suitable for meta-analytic aggregation.

Beyond these methodological issues, alternative interpretations of the observed patterns should also be considered. Some relationships attributed to “organizational agility” may partly reflect broader organizational culture, leadership styles, or pre-existing capacities for innovation and change, rather than agility per se. In contexts where agility initiatives are embedded in broader reform programs, it is also difficult to disentangle the specific contribution of agility from parallel interventions, such as governance reforms or investments in digital infrastructure. In addition, the predominance of studies reporting positive outcomes may reflect publication or reporting biases, which can overstate the apparent benefits of agility and underrepresent neutral or negative cases. These alternative explanations do not invalidate the synthesized framework, but suggest that the findings should be interpreted with caution and with sensitivity to contextual complexities.

In summary, the strengths of this study include integrating evidence from diverse research designs into a coherent framework, extracting recurring components across studies (such as educational/management governance and leadership, data and technology infrastructure, and stakeholder engagement), and providing a usable implementation pathway in the form of a change management chain (vision, education/communication, motivation, resources, and action plan) that increases the applicability of the findings.

This study also has several limitations that should be taken into account when interpreting the findings. First, the included studies showed substantial conceptual and measurement heterogeneity in how organizational agility was defined and operationalized and were conducted in universities with diverse governance models, resource levels, and degrees of digital maturity. Collectively, these contextual differences limit the generalizability of the synthesized patterns to all universities of medical sciences. Second, as an integrative review, this synthesis is inherently interpretive and non-causal, which constrains strong causal inferences and calls for caution in generalizing the results beyond the contexts studied. Third, despite the use of a structured search strategy, incomplete coverage of relevant evidence—particularly grey literature and locally published studies—cannot be ruled out and may have affected the scope of the conclusions. Fourth, although all eligible studies were retained regardless of methodological quality to maintain breadth, their contributions were not treated as equivalent: Patterns supported by methodologically stronger studies were interpreted with greater confidence, whereas insights from weaker studies were used mainly to enrich or contextualize the emerging framework. Fifth, some included studies were conducted in non-educational or non-medical organizational settings; These were included for their conceptual transferability regarding organizational agility, dynamic capabilities, and social responsibility, but this choice may reduce the contextual specificity of some components when applied to medical education settings. Finally, this synthesis was initially guided by an existing conceptual model of agility in higher education Ghane Ebadi et al., [59], which increases the potential risk of confirmation bias; however, the framework was explicitly used as a sensitizing and provisional structure, and when the data did not fit the initial model, categories were inductively modified, expanded, or supplemented to remain receptive to data-driven insights.

The findings provide meaningful opportunities for action, including reducing the conceptual ambiguity of agility by proposing a common operational definition, standardizing implementation requirements and designing targeted interventions in agile governance, strengthening evidence-based decision-making through data infrastructures, and institutionalizing stakeholder participation to promote responsibility and social trust. These opportunities should be pursued with careful attention to potential costs and trade-offs, particularly in resource-constrained contexts, so that efforts to become more agile do not unintentionally increase workload pressures or exacerbate institutional inequities. In practical terms, the proposed framework can guide the design of innovative educational activities that align education with the social needs of patients and communities (e.g. medical humanities modules, community-based projects, and interprofessional or other multidisciplinary learning). As well as curriculum activities that prepare students to assess patients in their social context and integrate social determinants of health into clinical encounters. More broadly, it can help universities of medical sciences transform social responsibility from a rhetorical principle into tangible institutional objectives and indicators by embedding it into mission statements, strategic plans, educational policies, program design, and evaluation criteria. Accordingly, universities are encouraged to strengthen the foundations of responsible agility by clarifying decision-making roles and rights, implementing a phased change management chain with monitoring and feedback, aligning resources with transformation priorities, and designing fair incentive mechanisms. In parallel, policies related to social responsibility should be aligned with employees’ mindsets and supported by continuous professional development that explicitly addresses cultural and attitudinal dimensions. Future research should focus on developing and validating measures of responsible agility and testing the proposed model in longitudinal and multicenter studies, including in innovative educational programs, such as medical humanities, community-based learning, and multidisciplinary or interprofessional activities. The innovation of this study lies in redefining agility from a purely speed-oriented concept to a value-oriented and responsible construct, and in providing a specific operational pathway to achieve “smart and fair responsibility” based on transparency, justice, ethics, and participation in universities of medical sciences.

Conclusion

At the beginning of the third millennium, the mission of universities – particularly universities of medical sciences – has expanded beyond traditional roles to include serving a broader and more diverse student population. Given their central responsibility for educating and supplying staff for the health system, these universities are increasingly expected to prepare students with a broad range of skills and competencies during basic and clinical training. Responding to the evolving needs of students, employers, and the health system therefore requires the adoption an agility paradigm across the core structures and functions of universities of medical sciences. In order to enable more constructive interactions among leaders, faculty members, students, and employers and to shorten the time needed to implement and institutionalize necessary educational and organizational changes.

Based on the findings of this integrative review, universities of medical sciences can move towards greater organizational agility, explicitly based on social responsibility, by relying on an integrated configuration of Educational Empowerment (Structural Empowerment and Human Asset Empowerment) and Educational Dynamic Capabilities (Sensing, Seizing, and Reconfiguration Capabilities). Together, these components position social responsibility both as an overarching value framework and as an operational evaluation criterion, providing a conceptual basis for designing and evaluating educational policies and interventions aimed at developing agile and socially responsible health professionals. More specifically, this framework can guide the design of innovative educational activities in medical curricula that explicitly integrate social responsibility into everyday learning experiences and can be used to embed social responsibility into mission statements, strategic goals, educational policies, programs, and assessments, thereby aligning agility with the broader social mission of medical education. At the same time, these conclusions should be interpreted with caution, given the relatively small number of included studies and their methodological heterogeneity. Future research is needed to empirically test and further refine this framework across diverse institutional and cultural contexts.
