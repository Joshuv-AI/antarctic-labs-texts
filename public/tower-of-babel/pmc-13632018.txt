Incretin therapy prescription in a high cardiovascular risk population: Insights from a cardiology-based cardiometabolic program
Authors: Arpan Patel, Lakshya Trivedi, Rachna Prasad, Eleanora Avennati, Steven Alexander, Vu Nguyen, Thomas Kurian, Priyanka Satish
Published: 2026

Introduction

The cardiometabolic benefit of incretin therapy has been well-established in randomized clinical trials, yet real world adoption by cardiologists remains limited. Several systemic and therapeutic barriers exist, including their perception as diabetes specific medications, inadequate insurance coverage, medication costs and concern for side effects [1] Hence, there are limited real-world data into how cardiometabolic programs integrate into routine cardiology practice. In the present study, we sought to evaluate [1]: weight loss outcomes in patients prescribed incretin therapy within this program [2] adherence and safety outcomes [[3], [5]]; effects on secondary cardiometabolic outcomes, from a multidisciplinary program integrated within cardiology.

Methods

A dedicated cardiometabolic clinic was started within Ascension Texas Cardiovascular, Austin, Texas in September 2023. A multidisciplinary team of preventive cardiologists, an advanced practice registered nurse (APRN), a dietician, and coordinator participated in the program. Patients seen within the cardiology practice, who had a body mass index (BMI) greater than 30 and with Type 2 Diabetes (T2D), coronary artery disease (CAD), Atrial Fibrillation (Afib) or Congestive Heart Failure (CHF) were referred. The program included a structured intake by a preventive cardiologist focusing on a comprehensive weight and lifestyle history, as well as screening for eating disorders and contraindications to incretin therapy. Patients initiated on incretin therapy then received one – on – one coaching from an APRN, at the first month, and at 2–3 months, with additional asynchronous monitoring via patient portal. Patients were seen by the cardiologist every 3–6 months the first year and continued follow up with the APRN on a regular schedule. Dietary counseling was provided by an APRN, and a dietician if covered by the patient’s insurance. Regular exercise with strength training was emphasized at every visit, and patients were directed to available community resources.

We conducted a retrospective chart review of patients who were prescribed a Glucagon like peptide −1 Receptor agonist (GLP-1 RA) or a Glucagon like peptide −1 Receptor agonist/ Glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GLP-1/GIP) co-agonist in our clinic from September 2023 through July 2025. Nineteen patients transferred from other providers within the practice when the clinic started, and were already on a GLP-1 agonist. Data was collected from date of prescription till December 2025. Ethics approval for this analysis was obtained from the University of Texas at Austin institutional review board. The primary outcome was change in weight and BMI in patients with at least 1 follow-up visit after initiation of incretin therapy. Secondary outcomes included dose titration schedule, discontinuation rate, frequency of switches, and changes in cardiometabolic markers including serum triglycerides, serum low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL -C) and Hemoglobin A1C between baseline and follow up visits. Additionally, the number of anti-hypertensive medications and oral medications for glycemic control were compared between baseline and final follow up.

Continuous variables were summarized using mean, standard deviation (SD) and median with interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were summarized using counts and percentages. Longitudinal outcomes were evaluated using change from baseline and percent change where appropriate. A longitudinal linear mixed-effects model with random intercepts for patients was used to account for repeated measurements over time. The reference timepoint was 0–1 month following treatment initiation. Estimates for comorbidities represent the adjusted difference in percent weight loss between patients with and without each condition.

Results

One hundred and forty-two prescriptions for incretin therapy were sent during this time period. Seventy-three patients received approval from their insurance company. Of these, 69 patients had documented at least 1 follow-up visit and were included in the present analysis. The median age was 62 years (IQR 52- 69) and 51% were women. Eighty-one percent had hypertension (HTN), 56% had dyslipidemia, 34% had T2D, 55% had obstructive sleep apnea (OSA). Fifty Three percent had CAD, 49% had AFib and 8.7% had Heart Failure with Preserved Ejection Fraction (HFpEF), 10.1% had Heart Failure with Mildly Reduced Ejection Fraction (HFmREF), and 7.2% had Heart Failure with Reduced Ejection Fraction (HFrEF). Thirty-two patients (47%) reported any alcohol use and 7 (10%) had a prior history of bariatric surgery > 1 year ago. The median weight was 251 lbs (113 kg) (IQR 224- 280 lbs) at baseline. The median BMI was 39.65 (IQR 35.17, 44.33) kg/m2. Fifty-six (81.1%) patients were on Semaglutide, 8 (11.5%) were on Tirzepatide, 3 (4.3%) were on Dulaglutide, and 2 (2.9%) were on Liraglutide at baseline.

Forty patients (57.9%) had data available at 6 months from their initial visit, 22 (31.9%) at >1 year, and 7 (10%) patients had data at > 2 years. By the 6-month visit, 41% (n = 14) of patients on Semaglutide were escalated to the 1 or 1.7 mg dose and 37% (n = 13) were up titrated to maximum dose (2 or 2.4 mg). At their 1-year follow-up visit, 77% of patients on semaglutide (n = 14) were prescribed maximum dose. Of the 8 patients prescribed Tirzepatide at baseline, 75% (n = 3) were taking 10 mg by the 6-month visit, and at >1 year, 66.7% (n = 2) were taking the maximum dose.

Thirteen patients (18.8% of all patients in the database) transitioned to a different medication, 49 patients (71.0%) were continued on the same medication, 2 (2.9%) had therapy paused with a plan to resume within weeks, and 5 (7.25%) stopped therapy completely. Two were stopped due to cost, 2 were due to GI side effects and 1 due to pursuing bariatric surgery. Of the patients who switched therapy, 8 switched from Semaglutide to Tirzepatide, 2 from Dulaglutide to Tirzepatide, 2 from Liraglutide to Semaglutide and 1 from Dulaglutide to Semaglutide. All patients on liraglutide or dulaglutide were switched to semaglutide by the 6–12 month follow-up visit. Of those who switched from Dulaglutide, 2 were due to inadequate response and 1 was due to nausea as a side effect. Of those who switched from Liraglutide, 1 was due to lack of insurance coverage and the other due to lack of supply. For patients switched from Semaglutide, 5 were due to inadequate response, 2 due to side effects and 1 due to lack of insurance coverage.

Median percent weight loss increased from 6.98 ± 3.2 % by 6 months to 9.11 ± 7.3 % by 12 months, to 11.7 ± 6.5% at >1 year and 13.7% ± 7.8 at > 2 years. Median BMI decreased by 2.8 ± 2.2 Kg/m2 by 6 months, 3.6 ± 3 kg/m2 by 12 months, 4.7 ± 2.6 kg/m2 s at > 1 year and 5.4 ± 3.3 Kg/m2 at > 2 years.

By 12 months, 75% (n = 30) of patients with available data achieved at least 5% weight loss and 40% (n = 16) achieved at least 10% weight loss compared to baseline. After at least 2 years of follow up, 42.8% (n = 3) of patients achieved > 15% weight loss. The percent weight loss also varied by the incretin prescribed. Patients initially prescribed Tirzepatide achieved the greatest median weight loss (14.0% of baseline weight) and decrease in BMI at 1 year, (Fig. 1). The median hemoglobin A1c at baseline was 5.8% (IQR 5.6–6.3%), which decreased to 5.4% (IQR 5.3–5.8%) at >1 year follow-up. LDL -C decreased from 90 (IQR 65–110) mg/dl to 71 (IQR 66–75) mg/dl. Serum triglycerides decreased from a median 112 (IQR 81–146) mg/dl to 68 (IQR 62–130) mg/dl. Patients in the cohort did not change the number of oral medications for diabetes, but there was a median reduction of one antihypertensive medication at the > 1 year follow up.

Discussion

Our study examined the effects of a multidisciplinary approach to weight management in a real world cohort of patients with cardiometabolic disease. We describe that a multidisciplinary intervention could be safely integrated into routine clinical practice and was sustainable over 2 years. We observed a median weight loss of 11% at > 1 year, and 14% at > 2 years, similar to another single center study of ‘no cost to patient’ medical weight loss program conducted outside of cardiology [3] We also demonstrated that the degree of weight loss achieved was not affected even when stratifying patients by comorbidity status. The proportion of patients achieving a 10% weight loss threshold increased over follow-up visits, concordant with the dose-dependent effect of these agents. We observed a discontinuation rate lower than prior real-world data (close to 50%), approaching the 10–15% seen in clinical trials [3] This, despite insurance, cost and access related barriers in real world practice, well characterized in nationwide trends [4] The integration of this program with other cardiology subspecialities, along with multidisciplinary support were instrumental in overcoming these challenges and supporting patients through side effects and dose titration.

Limitations

Several important limitations of the present study must be acknowledged. First, the retrospective, single-center design without a concurrent control group precludes causality regarding observed outcomes. Secondly, data attrition limits the statistical reliability and generalizability of data at > 2-year period. Third, laboratory outcome data were available only for the subset of patients. Changes in background cardiometabolic medications (statins, antihypertensives, oral anti diabetes agents and cardiac medications) during the study period were not fully accounted for. The observed changes in biomarkers should be considered exploratory.

Conclusions

Our study provides early real-world data on the efficacy and feasibility of incretin therapy prescription via a multidisciplinary cardiometabolic program integrated within cardiology practice. A coordinated approach like ours has the potential to facilitate meaningful weight loss and improvement in cardiometabolic markers, with improved adherence to incretin therapy. Larger studies are needed to better understand cost efficacy and long-term outcomes of such a care model.
