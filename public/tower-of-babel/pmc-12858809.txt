Leveraging genomic and transcriptomic data of diverse ancestry to uncover mechanisms of psychiatric risk in the adult and developing brain
Authors: Aarti Jajoo, Vijetha Balakundi, Chris Chatzinakos, Athina Aruldass, Cindy Wen, Ilse G C Kolmans, Artemis Iatrou, Joel E Kleinman, Kerry J Ressler, Tim Bigdeli, Michael J Gandal, Nikolaos P Daskalakis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe explore strategies to harness ancestral diversity in PsychENCODE Consortium Genotype-Expression (GEx) reference panels (adult and developing brain) and Psychiatric Genomics Consortium GWAS data to improve genetically regulated expression (GReX) models and their use for Transcriptome-wide association study (TWAS) discoveries, uncovering previously unknown aspects of psychiatric functional genomics. We trained multiple GReX models on rigorously constructed GEx panel subsets, generated by downsampling, segregating, and/or mixing samples of Admixed African and European ancestries, and based on disease status. Ancestry-specific GReX genes were enriched in pathways involving mitochondrial functions, organelle structure, and metabolism. These models were integrated with ancestry-specific GWASs to conduct bipolar disorder, major depressive disorder, posttraumatic stress disorder, and schizophrenia TWAS. TWAS signals obtained by applying AA- and EUR-specific GReX models to an ancestry-specific GWAS were largely concordant, and mismatched-TWAS (e.g. AA-GReX applied to EUR-GWAS) revealed biologically meaningful signals missed by matched-TWAS. Shared signals across the four disorders were more prominent in the developing brain, involving genes such as H4C13, ZSCAN12P1, and FLOT1, and pathways related to megakaryocyte and muscle development, and neurotransmitter regulation. Overall, we demonstrate concordance in shared TWAS signals across GReX models and provide insight into GReX-specific detectable genes and pathways.Subject terms: Gene expression profiling, Transcriptomics, Systems biology

Introduction

Psychiatric disorders, including bipolar disorder (BIP), major depressive disorder (MDD), post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and schizophrenia (SCZ), are complex traits involving multiple genes and biological pathways. While Genome-Wide Association Studies (GWAS) have been instrumental in identifying genetic associations for these disorders1–6, challenges remain to optimize the discovery of biological targets from these large datasets7. GWAS hits often have small effect sizes and are difficult to interpret biologically, as many associations are located in noncoding regions of the genome. These associations may be pleiotropic or non-causative, resulting from linkage disequilibrium (LD). Transcriptome-Wide Association Studies (TWAS) address some of these challenges by effectively translating GWAS signals into meaningful molecular mechanisms at a tissue-specific level8,9 through a two-step process. First, a Genotype-Expression reference panel (GEx panel) is used to train Genetically Regulated gene eXpression (GReX) models, which predict gene expression based on genotype information. Second, SNP GWAS summary statistics are combined with SNP (single nucleotide polymorphism) weights derived from the GReX models to identify GTAs (Gene Trait Associations). This approach provides critical insights into the molecular underpinnings of psychiatric disorders, bridging genetic associations with biological functions10–12.

An underlying assumption of TWAS is that the relationships between SNPs and gene expression in the GEx panel are similar to those in the GWAS cohort. Furthermore, eQTL (expression quantitative trait loci) SNPs identified in a GEx panel that is integrated with GWAS data may be in LD with the actual causal SNP but may not directly regulate gene expression itself. If the genetic architecture of the GWAS cohort matches that of the GEx panel, these associations can still be informative. Hence, matching the ancestry of the GEx panel with that of the GWAS and performing a meta-analysis could be the most effective approach to enhance TWAS signals. However, most existing GEx panels and GWAS data are predominantly of European ancestry (EUR). With the emergence of datasets from diverse ancestries, the key question is how to leverage these resources to increase the power of TWAS results and to identify comprehensive signals across different ancestral populations. While some TWAS methods already exist to integrate cross-ancestry datasets13–15, often through adjustments for LD or Bayesian approaches, our focus lies in understanding genomic region-specific regulatory differences and biological differences across ancestrally distinct GEx panels. We want to explore how these differences, along with variations in a disease’s genetic architecture, can be leveraged to improve TWAS signals.

Our study addresses these questions in the context of integrating adult cortex (dorsolateral prefrontal cortex [DLPFC]) and developing brain (fetal) GEx panels with GWAS data for four psychiatric disorders, focusing on Admixed African (AA) and European (EUR) ancestries (Fig. 1A). We hypothesize that most of the SNPs have concordant effects on gene expression across ancestries, as suggested by emerging studies16–19. For the DLPFC GEx panel obtained from PsychENCODE20 cohorts spanning multiple psychiatric diagnosis (Supplementary Fig. 1a; “Methods”), we considered three ancestry groupings (AA, EUR, and AAEUR, where AAEUR models are bi-ancestry models constructed by combining AA and EUR subjects; Methods, Fig. 1B and Supplementary Fig. 1b–d) and two disease-status groupings (neurotypical controls [Ct] and all available samples including neurotypical controls and cases [All]). In total, we trained eight DLPFC GReX models of varying training sizes (Supplementary Figs. 1E and Supplementary Data 1), including AA-specific (GReXAACt165 and GReXAAAll276), EUR-specific (GReXEURCt165, GReXEURCt477 and GReXEURAll1032), and bi-ancestry (GReXAAEURCt330, GReXAAEURCt642 and GReXAAEUR1307). Here, the number appended to the GReX model’s name denotes the training sample size. The EURCt165 training set was downsampled from EURCt477 to match the demographic composition of AACt165, enabling fair comparison across ancestries. Further details are available in the Supplementary Notes. Building on this framework, ancestry-specific predicted genes were identified, and their enrichment at the pathway level was compared. Model performances were evaluated separately on AA and EUR samples in an external validation dataset for each GReX model. In addition, two ancestry-specific fetal GReX models were trained to identify and compare ancestry-specific genes and their pathway enrichments. We integrated these 10 GReX models (8 DLPFC and 2 fetal) with eight (4 × 2) ancestry-specific GWAS datasets of 4 psychiatric disorders (PTSD, MDD, BIP, and SCZ) across 2 ancestries (EUR and AA). Meta-analyses of TWAS were performed within each disorder using TWAS results of ancestry-specific GWAS obtained by applying various combinations of GReX pairs. Ancestry-specific TWAS signals were then compared at the gene and pathway levels. We found that shared signals across ancestries were concordant, while unshared signals were predominantly driven by minor allele frequency (MAF) differences. Pathway analyses of unshared ancestry-specific signals uncovered trait-specific archetypical pathways, emphasizing the biological relevance of these complementary signals.

Results

Performance of GReX models and ancestry specific predicted genes

GReX models are built using elastic net following the PrediXcan pipeline8,9 (“Methods”). Genes with average cross- validation R-squared (R²) more than 0.01 and p-value < 0.05 are included in downstream analysis (Supplementary Data 2). Across the 8 GReX models, the number of predicted genes positively correlated with training sample size (Fig. 1C and Supplementary Data 2). However, larger sample sizes yielded lower cross-validation R² (Fig. 1D), reflecting inclusion of genes with lower heritability. Of 17,406 distinct predicted genes, 2098 were predicted by all models and showed increasing mean R² with sample size (Fig. 1E and Supplementary Fig. 2). GReXEURCt165 predicts 4557 genes, while GReXAACt165 predicts 4926 genes—8% more. The 2248 genes predicted by both models had correlated R² (Pearson’s r = 0.53, Fig. 2A). To assess whether cis-SNP regulation of overlapping genes was consistent across ancestries, we compared assigned SNP weights between the two models. About 12% of the predictor SNPs were shared across the two models, following non-random patterns shaped by MAFs distributions within each training dataset (Supplementary Notes). Despite modest overlap, SNP weights among 8,465 shared predictors were highly correlated (Pearson’s r = 0.6) with 97% agreement in direction (Fig. 2B). This concordance is notable given that models were trained on non-overlapping ancestral populations and GReX assigns weights iteratively to causal and tagged SNPs. Alongside concordance, discordant R² performances were observed (R² > 0.4 in one model and < 0.15 in the other), identifying 30 such genes in GReXAACt165 and 10 in GReXEURCt165 (Fig. 2A). Examples included immune response-related genes (AA: BTN2A2 and LYPD8; EUR: CAMLG) and mitochondrial function-related genes (AA: SCO1; EUR: NDUFAF4). UVSSA showed higher R² in EUR (0.43 vs. 0.03 in AA), which could reflect its role in UV-induced DNA repair and possible stronger environmental selection in lighter-pigmented populations, highlighting ancestry-specific specialization of gene regulation detectable across tissues.

We consider the overlap of GReXAACt165 and GReXEURCt165 predicted genes with those predicted in our higher-order GReX models (trained on a bigger sample size) as a proxy for reliability. We observed that 93% of the genes predicted by both were also predicted in all higher-order models, suggesting that the underlying genetic architecture and available signal in both population training datasets were sufficient to support consistent gene prediction (Supplementary Data 3). In contrast, 40% of GReXAACt165-specific and 30% of GReXEURCt165-spefic genes were retained in all higher-order GReX models (Fig. 2C, Supplementary Fig. 2). Notably, 27% of GReXAACt165-specific genes were predicted only in higher-order AA models, and 38% of GReXEURCt165 -specific genes were predicted only in higher-order EUR models, highlighting ancestry-specific predictive power. We further connected these GReXAACt165-specific and GReXEURCt165-specific genes, which were exclusively predicted in their respective higher-order ancestry models (Methods, Supplementary Data 3), to functional annotations. Shared pathways across GReXAACt165-specifc and GReXEURCt165-specific genes included DNA metabolic processes, membrane organization, and lytic vacuole function (Fig. 2D and Supplementary Figs. 3, 4). Unshared GReXAACt165-specific pathways were enriched in oxidative stress, phagocytic vesicle formation, and mitotic cell cycle processes, while unshared GReXEURCt165-specific pathways were enriched in processes related to the perinuclear region of the cytoplasm, calcium ion binding, Golgi membrane function, and viral responses. Suggesting the enrichment of several model-specific specialized pathways, particularly those involving organelle dynamics.

External validation of GReX models

Next, we validated our models using an independent DLPFC GEx panel (AA = 50, EUR = 246, Methods21,). As a performance measure, we used mean and Pearsons’s skewness of differences in the correlation of the predicted gene expression and observed gene expression (ρGReXobs) between each pair of GReX models (Fig. 2E, Methods). Across GReX models, overall performance differences were modest (mean distribution of correlation differences < 0.01), see Supplementary Notes for more details. Matched ancestry predictions—where the training and test cohorts share the same ancestry (AA on AA, EUR on EUR)—consistently provided an advantage, even when the mismatched model was trained on a larger cohort (e.g., EURCt477 vs. AACt165). Within matched ancestry settings, expanding training to include both cases and Ct (All) increased mean performance, although skewness in correlation differences indicated that subsets of genes were still better predicted by the smaller Ct models. Bi‑ancestry models improved mean performance when compared to smaller training size GReX (165), though subsets of genes continued to be better predicted by ancestry‑specific models; at larger training sizes (477), the ancestry‑specific models outperformed the bi‑ancestry models overall.

Effect size correlations of TWAS-based GTA results of the 8 GReX models across traits

We use JEPEGMiX software22 to perform TWAS analysis (Methods). Many combinations of TWAS analysis were performed by integrating our eight GReX models with single-ancestry (AA and EUR) GWAS of four psychiatric diseases: SCZ2,6, BIP1,2, PTSD4,5, and MDD3 (Methods22,) (Fig. 1A). This included three types of TWAS: (i) matched ancestry TWAS, with EUR GReX applied to EUR GWAS [GReXEUR(GWASEUR)] and AA GWAS applied to AA GWAS [GReXAA(GWASAA)]; (ii) mismatched ancestry TWAS, with EUR GReX applied to AA GWAS [GReXEUR(GWASAA)] and AA GReX applied to EUR GWAS [GReXAA(GWASEUR)] iii) bi-ancestry TWAS, with GReXAAEUR applied to AA GWAS [GReXAAEUR(GWASAA)] and EUR GWAS [GReXAAEUR(GWASEUR)] (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Data 4, 5, Methods). It is important to note that the four traits had substantial differences in GWAS sample sizes, both across disorders and across ancestries within each disorder, which may influence the power and robustness of the TWAS results (Supplementary Fig. 5A, B). First, to assess whether the TWAS signals are primarily driven by biological signals from the underlying disease GWAS or by variation introduced through the choice of GReX model, we jointly clustered all TWAS results generated using single-ancestry GWAS based on correlations of their GTA z-scores. This analysis revealed a clear hierarchical clustering pattern, with the top layer corresponding to the ancestry of the underlying GWAS, followed by disorder-specific GWAS, and then by the applied 8 GReX models (“Methods”, Fig. 3A). In both ancestry-associated GWAS clusters, SCZ grouped with BIP and PTSD with MDD, consistent with known genetic correlations7 and confirmed in our study’s GWAS data (Supplementary Fig. 5C, “Methods”).

Notably, TWAS results generated using different GReX models applied to the same GWAS always clustered together, with subclusters reflecting the ancestry background of the GReX models (Fig. 3A and Supplementary Fig. 6), a detailed description can be found in Supplementary Notes. In addition, for all four traits, correlation of GTAs produced by different ancestry models on EUR GWAS [GReXEUR(GWASEUR) ~ GReXAA(GWASEUR)] are higher than those produced on AA GWAS [GReXAA(GWASAA) ~ GReXEUR(GWASAA)] (Fig. 3C). To determine whether the lower GTA correlations produced by ancestry-specific models using AA GWAS is not driven by the small sample GWAS AA sample sizes, we utilized an external PTSD cohort which is predominantly AA cohort (~96% people are of AA ancestry, Grady Trauma Project [GTP] cohort23,24). In this cohort, we could calculate GTAs using both raw genotype and GWAS summary statistics. We observed lower GTA correlations when using AA subjects vs. the EUR subjects (0.53 vs. 0.63 using raw genotype, 0.43 vs. 0.55 using summary statistics; Supplementary Fig. 8).

Enrichment p-value correlations of TWAS pathway results based on the 8 GReX models within traits

Our pathway analysis focused on the three domains of Gene Ontology: BP (biological processes), MF (molecular functions) and CC (cellular components)25. TWAS-pathway enrichment analysis of all the TWAS associated with single-ancestry GWAS mentioned above were performed using JEPEGMiX2-P22 (Methods), which accounts for LD among genes within each tested pathway as part of the integration of TWAS GTA statistics (Methods). Within each disorder, pathway enrichment p-value based clustering of TWAS results primarily grouped analyses by the ancestry of the underlying GWAS (Fig. 3C), consistent with the structure observed in GTA-based clustering (Fig. 3A). Within each GWAS-specific cluster, GReXAA models consistently clustered together (Supplementary Fig. 9). Unlike in GTA clustering, GReXAAEURCt330-based pathway enrichments consistently clustered with the GReXAA models. Also, ancestry-based patterns of pathway signals in the higher-order EUR and AAEUR GReX models were less pronounced (Supplementary Fig. 9). As with GTA signals, pathway signal correlations between GReXAACt165 and GReXEURCt165 were lower in AA GWAS (Fig. 3D; Supplementary Fig. 10A vs. S10B; Supplementary Fig. 10C vs. S10D). Interestingly, the comparison between GReXAACt165(EUR) and GReXEURCt165(EUR) showed greater enrichment in biological pathways in SCZ and BIP, whereas greater agreement on non-enrichment BP was observed in PTSD and MDD (Fig. 3D).

Ancestry-specific differences in significant TWAS-based GTAs, their underlying factors, and putative causal associations

Risk genes in a TWAS analysis are identified as those with FDR-adjusted p < 0.05 (FDR-GTAs) (Methods). To assess the benefits of ancestry-specific GReX models, we compared the FDR-GTAs of the same size GReX model pair Ct165. Among the FDR-GTAs of EUR GWAS, a very small proportion was overlapped across the two Ct165 models within each disorder, with no overlap observed in BIP (BIP: 0 %; MDD: 2, 8%; PTSD: 2, 12%; SCZ: 12, 16%) (Fig. 4A–D). The largest proportion of AACt165-specific FDR-GTAs was observed for PTSD (MDD: 5, 20%; BIP: 6, 40%; SCZ: 28, 40%; PTSD: 10, 66%) (Fig. 4A–D). Some of these GTAs were present in at least one of the 6 higher-order GReX model (40% MDD, 40% SCZ, 50 % BIP and 50 % PTSD) (Supplementary Figs. 11, 12). Sometimes, the same genomic locus can contain unshared FDR-GTAs across the two models, while other times, a genomic locus is unique to a specific ancestry model, see Supplementary Notes. We then sought to understand the factors underlying the presence of the unshared FDR-GTAs. We focused on SCZ, as it is the most heritable disorder with the strongest signal among the 4 disorders we study here. We first hypothesized that SNP resolution differences were the main driving force of the unshared FDR-GTAs. Indeed, we found that MAF of the predictor SNPs of the unshared FDR-GTAs were lower in the ancestry where the GTAs were not FDR-significant (70% of GReXAACt165-specific and 67% of GReXEURCt165-specific; Methods, Fig. 4E, F). In several cases among the remaining ancestry-specific FDR-GTAs (e.g., FURIN, RBM6), the LD blocks26 in the ancestry where the gene was an FDR-GTA was smaller than the corresponding LD block in the other ancestry (Supplementary Fig. 13). We provide more details on LD and MAF impacts on TWAS results in Supplementary Notes. To further assess whether GReXAACt165(GWASEUR) TWAS provides a meaningful signal, we compared the proportion of putative causal genes in the FDR-GTAs identified by GReXAACt165 and GReXEURCt165. Putative causal genes were identified using cTWAS27, which jointly fine-maps both GTAs and local SNPs together (Method). We found that 18% of AACt165-specific and 13% of EURCt165-specific genes were identified as putative causal genes, for more details see Supplementary Notes (Supplementary Figs. 14, 15).

Meta analysis of TWAS results from AA and EUR GWAS

We performed five types of meta-TWAS analyses within each trait to integrate results from an AA and a EUR GWAS-associated TWAS, enhancing power and reducing noise (Fig. 1A and Supplementary Data 6, 7). GReXEUR and GReXAA pairs included: Ct165 (GReXAACt165 and GReXEURCt165) – representing neurotypical control GReX models trained on identical sample sizes; CtMax (GReXAACt165 and GReXEURCt477) - representing GReX models with the maximum number of neurotypical controls; and AllMax (GReXAAAll276 and GReXEURAll1032) - representing GReX models trained on both cases and Ct. For example, Ct165metaEUR refers to meta-analysis of GReXEURCt165(GWASEUR) and GReXEURCt165(GWASEUR), while Ct165metaAnc refers to meta-analysis of GReXEURCt165(GWASEUR) and GReXAACt165(GWASAA). Meta TWAS-pathway results for all combinations were obtained using Fisher’s method (Methods) (Supplementary Data 8, 9).

Within each trait, metaAA and metaMis, as well as metaEUR and metaAnc, corresponding to the same GReX pair, showed very high correlations (Supplementary Fig. 17), influenced by the imbalanced GWAS sample sizes (Fig. 5A). Hence, among the four meta-analyses of the Ct165 pairs, > 95% of FDR-GTAs from Ct165metaMix and Ct165metaAnc were shared with Ct165metaEUR and Ct165metaAA, respectively (Fig. 5A–D). Given this concordance, we focus only on the metaAA and metaEUR results. While some metaAA-specific and metaEUR-specific FDR-GTAs were observed, most overlapped with corresponding single-ancestry TWAS FDR-GTAs (Supplementary Fig. 18). There was low overlap of FDR-GTAs across the meta-analyses within each phenotype, except for BIP (12% in PTSD, 15% in SCZ, 4% in MDD and 0% in BIP; Fig. 5A–D).

Overlap of TWAS-based Pathways generated using eight GReX models within trait

In the Ct165 pair analysis, collectively, the FDR-pathways (FDR < 0.05) identified by GReXEURCt165 (GWASEUR) and GReXAACt165 (GWASEUR) totaled 466 in SCZ, 106 in PTSD, 36 in MDD, and 5 in BIP, whereas AA GWAS did not yield any FDR-pathways (Supplementary Fig. 19–22 and Supplementary Data 8). GReXAACt165 identified the largest proportion of unique pathways in SCZ (42%) and PTSD (41%), with SCZ also showing the highest sharing between GReXAACt165 and GReXEURCt165 models (17%). Results of single ancestry GWAS were then meta analyzed as described in the above section. Read Supplementary Notes for detailed pathway overlap description28. To explore functional relationships, shared and unshared pathways were clustered based on their constituent genes and summarized using simplified enrichment (29, “Methods”), with cluster-wide word clouds providing a visual overview.

In the PTSD, Ct165metaAA-specific FDR pathways were primarily linked to amino acid transport (glutamine, azole, histidine and alanine) (Fig. 6A), and transporter activities (symporter, antiporter, and transmembrane). Enrichment was driven by solute carrier (SLC) family genes, including neurotransmitter transporters such as glutamate (SLC1A6 and SLC1A7), GABA (SLC6A13), and their precursor glutamine (SLC38A3), reflecting diverse substrate specificities relevant to neurotransmitter cycling and amino acid homeostasis (Supplementary Fig. 27)30. In contrast, Ct165metaEUR-specific FDR pathways included negative voltage-gated calcium channel activity; secretion and regulation of endocrine and steroid hormones (corticosteroids, glucocorticoids, and corticotropin); organismal and behavioral responses to environmental and physiological stimuli (stress, hormones, and ethanol exposure); and epigenetic modification of histones H4K4 and H4K16 (Fig. 6B). Many of these enrichments were driven by CRHR1, the CRH receptor and a prototypical driver of the stress response pathway (Supplementary Fig. 27). Ct165metaAA-specific FDR-Pathways involved transport processes related to GABA and glutamate neurotransmitter transporters, known to regulate hypothalamic activation, and Ct165metaEUR-specific FDR-Pathways involved stress-related signaling pathways. Together, these pathways capture distinct but interconnected components of hypothalamo-pituitary-adrenocotical (HPA) axis regulation31. In addition, positive regulation of adenylate cyclase activity was enriched in Ct165metaEUR, while cAMP-dependent protein kinase activity was specifically enriched in Ct165metaAA. These pathways represent sequential steps in the cAMP signaling cascade, suggesting that the two models highlight distinct but connected components of the same biological process.

In SCZ, TWAS pathway analysis revealed extensive enrichment of immune-related pathways, consistent with prior evidence linking immune dysregulation to SCZ32,33. Many were shared between both metaAACt165 and metaEURCt165, but we observed prominence of innate response more in Ct165metaAA and adaptive and inflammatory response in metaEURCt165. Both models showed shared enrichment in lipid metabolism (including ceramide and glycosphingolipid), peptidase regulation and glycosidase activity, all processes linked to schizophrenia34,35. (Fig. 6C, D). The metaAACt165-specific FDR-Pathways included developmental processes, such as dentate gyrus and adenohypophysis development and neuroepithelial cell differentiation. Developmental abnormalities of the hippocampus, including dentate disruption, have been implicated in schizophrenia36. Some enriched cellular components were related to neurotransmission (dendritic tree and dopaminergic synapse), and intracellular trafficking (vesicle-associated compartments, including transport vesicles, endocytic vesicles, and their membranes) (Fig. 6C, D). In contrast, the metaEURCt165-specific pathways were associated with calcium channel activity, stress hormone signaling, ear auditory receptor, neurotrophic production and binding, epigenetic modification, collagen metabolism and the viral life cycle (Fig. 6C). Auditory receptor pathways are notable given sensory processing deficits in SCZ, especially within the auditory cortex, which may contribute to hallucinations37.

The MDD TWAS pathway analysis showed limited metaAACt165-specific enrichments. The one notable Ct165metaAA-specific pathway group was related to procollagen-specific proline dioxygenase activity, which may be particularly relevant, as proline metabolism has been associated with depression and anxiety severity38,39 (Fig. 6E). metaEURCt165-specific enrichments were also limited and comprised of immune related signaling, primarily involving TGFβ production, glycosylation processes and low-density lipoprotein (LDL) receptor activity (Fig. 6F). LDL receptors influence the central nervous system in a multifaceted manner, contributing to processes such as synaptic plasticity and neuromuscular junction development40. There was a limited signal observed for the Ct165 models in BIP.

Gain and loss of using All and bi-ancestry models

To assess the benefits of GReXAAEUR and GReXAllMax models, we compared FDR-GTA results across models for the same underlying GWAS (Supplementary Figs. 11, 12, 28). We observed that the number of FDR-GTAs increased as the training sample size increased (pearson r > 0.8 between the number of FDR-GTAs and the training sample size of the corresponding GReX models within each set of TWAS analyses derived from the same GWAS), although both shared and unshared genes were present in the TWAS results (Supplementary Fig. 28B, C). This aligns with our observation that, although the number of predicted genes in a GReX model increases with training data size, both overlapping and non-overlapping genes are observed across models, including those trained with smaller sample sizes (Supplementary Fig. 2). To evaluate the benefit of Ct versus All models, we compared EURCt477 vs. EURAll1032 (a 50% increase in sample size) and AACt165 vs. AAAll276 (a 40% increase) (Supplementary Fig. 28B). We noted that the gain in GReXAAAll-specific genes was much higher—57% for BIP, 56% for MDD, 49% for PTSD, and 48% for SCZ—compared to GReXEURAll1032, where the gains were 31, 32, 44, and 36%, respectively. The greater gain observed in AAAll276 may be attributable to the smaller absolute control sample size (n = 165) compared to that of the EUR group (n = 477).

Similarly, to evaluate Ct vs. bi-ancestry Ct model performance, we compared the FDR-GTAs from AACt165 and EURCt477 with those from AAEURCt330 and AAEURCt664 (Supplementary Fig. 28C). Since AAEURCt330 includes a comparable proportion of AA and EUR subjects (50% AA), whereas AAEURCt664 has a lower proportion of AA subjects (25% AA), we aimed to determine whether AACt165-specific signals were lost more in the lower AA proportion model. Although, except for SCZ (17 genes, 10% vs. 14 genes, 8.9% of overall findings), the number of AACt165-specific signals remained similar. Overall, AAEURCt664-specific FDR-GTAs included 34 in SCZ (20%), 12 in BIP (21%), 12 in MDD (23%), and 9 in PTSD (23%), but this came at the cost of losing ancestry-specific signals (e.g., 28% in SCZ, 41% in BIP, 30% in MDD, and 20% in PTSD for EUR-specific FDR-GTAs; 10% in SCZ, 7% in BIP, 10% in MDD, and 23% in PTSD for AA-specific FDR-GTAs). For example, some key signals, such as FURIN for SCZ, were absent in bi-ancestry TWAS results (Supplementary Fig. 29). We considered whether using a matched ancestry background GWAS would improve the situation, so we applied the bi-ancestry model on the bi-ancestry GWAS, but FURIN remained missing in both. As for the TWAS analysis associated with single-ancestry AA GWAS, we had very limited FDR-GTAs (PTSD: SUN3 in GReXAAAll276(GWASAA); BIP: ADH5 in GReXAAAll276(GWASAA) and ZNF37 A in GReXAAEUR(GWASAA)).

TWAS pathway signals detected ony by bi-ancestry and All models included spliceosome-related components across PTSD and SCZ — such as tri-snRNP, U2/U12, and snoRNA-associated complexes (Supplementary Figs. 30–32). Spliceosome machinery alterations have been implicated in PTSD41. In SCZ, bi-ancestry models also revealed blood–brain barrier–related pathways, an increasingly recognized feature of the disorder42. Across PTSD, MDD, and BIP, higher-order models further uncovered more synaptic, neuronal and related pathways (Supplementary Notes).

These gene sets demonstrated varying degrees of pathway coverage (Supplementary Fig. 33). Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that differences between EUR and AA ancestry-specific GReX models in adult and developing brain tissues were largely attributable to MAFs. Nonetheless, the modest set of shared predictors exhibited concordant SNP weights, indicating consistency in genetic regulation across ancestries. EUR- and AA-specific GReX genes were enriched in specialized pathways, some of which suggested regulatory mechanisms shaped by environmental selection pressures. TWAS results generated using various GReX models applied to a GWAS showed positive correlations along with the presence of patterns reflecting the ancestral background of the samples used in the GReX models. Unshared FDR-GTAs across GReX models for a GWAS were primarily driven by differences in MAF between the EUR and AA GReX training datasets, suggesting that these models are complementary in nature, with differences in FDR-GTAs arising from effective sample size differences caused by asymmetric genetic data resolution across ancestry-specific GReX training datasets. Integrating these ancestry-specific GReX models with mismatched ancestry-specific psychiatric GWAS data uncovered novel TWAS results that were not identified through conventional matched ancestry TWAS analyses. Mismatched TWAS pathway analysis further revealed trait-specific archetypical pathophysiological pathways, underscoring the biological relevance of these findings. Together, our results highlight the value of expanding TWAS beyond matched ancestry models to uncover biologically meaningful associations that would otherwise remain hidden.

Our observation of concordant direction of SNP weights in GReX models across ancestries aligns with many emerging research suggesting that eQTL differences across ancestries are primarily driven by MAF, with heterogeneous eQTL findings being rare16–19. Further, greater genetic diversity in African populations46 explains why the same sample-sized Admixed African model (AACt165) predicts 8% more genes than the counterpart European model (EURCt165). Some extreme cases, such as UVSSA with a much higher R² in EUR, reflect environmental selection pressures47. We suspect that, although brain expression may not have a direct functional impact, genetic selection in EUR populations is so crucial that it affects multiple cell types, rather than making it cell-type-specific through regulatory epigenetic mechanisms. In general, AACt165-specific and EURCt165-specific predicted genes were enriched in specialized pathways related to organelle organization and dynamics, response to external stimuli, autophagy, and metabolic processes. This implies ancestry-specific underlying mechanisms involved in these pathways, with some differences, particularly in metabolic and energy processes and autophagy, being well-observed across ancestries in previous studies48–50. Interestingly, many of the observed specialized pathways play a critical role in regulating immune and inflammation processes, despite not being directly enriched in traditional immune pathways, as seen in other studies focused on differentially expressed ancestry specific genes in DLPFC51. This may be due to the fact that, while the genetic architecture and mechanisms driving these pathways could differ across ancestries, the genes themselves remain heritable across ancestry, with their expression potentially influenced by non-heritable environmental factors. Our observation of ancestry-specific immune-related TWAS pathways further supports this conclusion, which is also corroborated by studies comparing immune responses across ancestries, particularly those distinguishing between innate and adaptive immunity52.

TWAS clusters based on correlations of GTA z-scores are driven predominantly by the GWAS data source rather than the applied GReX model, with high correlation observed between matched and mismatched TWAS analyses. Apart from the observed SNP-to-gene relationship concordance, we attribute this pattern to two key factors. First, it has been observed that genomic functional annotations are found more in low levels of LD, and more recent alleles are often found in regions of low LD53. Second, as observed in our experiments, the Elastic Net model used for GReX training optimally distributes weights across both causal and tagged SNPs, rather than assigning weights randomly to a subset of SNPs in LD. This approach increases the portability of the GReX models across ancestries by ensuring that causal variants are assigned weights, even in the presence of tagged SNPs, albeit with lower magnitudes due to weight sharing. Biological relevance of the enriched mismatched TWAS-pathways strongly supports our approach of considering mismatched ancestry-specific GWAS (Supplementary Discussion). In addition, aligning with our GReX model ancestry-specific gene pathway enrichments, certain pathway groups consistently appeared in ancestry-specific TWAS results. For example, vesicle transport–related pathways were more prominent in AA, whereas hormone- and environmental-response pathways were more pronounced in EUR. These unshared signals largely reflect differences in MAF and LD, where LD patterns can amplify or dilute causal variant signals, and MAF differences dictate whether a variant is informative or uninformative for model training, arising from true population variation or from sampling error due to small training sample sizes. In addition, our observation on the concordance of GReX and TWAS suggests that, for a given SNP, the SNP–gene and SNP–disease relationships may follow the same biological mechanism across ancestries. However, differences in SNP prevalence can alter the gene expression profiles across the populations and potentially contribute to ancestry-specific disease subtypes. As the biological mechanisms become clearer, these insights will help refine SNP–disease relationships in a way that supports personalized medicine and drug repurposing strategies guided by an individual’s genetic profile, built on a more comprehensive understanding of disease mechanisms across populations.

As datasets expand to include more diverse ancestries, brain regions, and modalities such as single-cell and spatial profiling, the total sample size within each type of dataset could remain limited. This raises the question of which samples to include or exclude in the training dataset to maximize sample size. Our empirical observations from DLPFC (adult brain) GReX models show that while increasing sample size generally improves the number of predicted genes, FDR-GTAs, and pathway enrichments, the rate of gain diminishes with higher-order models. For example, the AACt165 model (165 individuals) benefits substantially from higher-order models, whereas EURCt477 (~500 individuals) already captures most relevant signals, with only modest additional gains. These observations highlight practical considerations for future GReX development—particularly decisions about whether to include disease cases or merge ancestries—each of which can influence signal gain or loss. A detailed discussion on this is provided in Supplementary Discussion.

Comparison of TWAS signals in the fetal brain and DLPFC revealed both shared and distinct pathway enrichments across disorders. Notably, fetal brain analyses showed enrichment for peripheral developmental processes—including skeletal muscle differentiation and megakaryocyte-related pathways—across all four disorders. These findings may reflect neurovascular-related signals or shared developmental gene programs between vascular and neural cells, both of which could potentially influence neural development, angiogenesis, and blood–brain barrier formation54. Immune-related pathways were also broadly enriched across disorders and tissues, underscoring the complex and ongoing interplay between neural and peripheral signals from early development through adulthood. For example, myokines released from skeletal muscle or platelet-derived factors from megakaryocytes may contribute to this cross-talk, potentially shaping brain plasticity and disease vulnerability throughout the lifespan54. More overlapped findings emerged within fetal TWAS compared to adult TWAS, suggesting a shared neuronal developmental component across multiple psychiatric disorders. Divergence in disease onset55 and manifestation may arise when genetic risk, particularly in its impact on the postnatal brain, interacts with life stressors occurring at different stages, such as birth hypoxia in schizophrenia, childhood trauma in PTSD, and adult life stressors in depression.

Many of the FDR-GTAs, particularly in the fetal analysis, are only partially understood, and their functional roles in brain development and impact on psychiatric disease remain known. Several notable examples from our results, including FLOT1, H4C13, and ZSCAN12P1, illustrate this complexity. FLOT1, abundant in the brain and associated with lipid raft scaffolding, participates in diverse processes such as immune signaling, neurotransmitter transport, and serotonin-related brain function. It has been implicated in earlier fetal studies of SCZ and BIP56, yet its role in neurodevelopment remains unknow57,58. In our study, FLOT1 was identified in PTSD, SCZ, and BIP, suggesting possible involvement in shared features such as psychosis, which are less common in MDD. H4C13 was among the top contributors in a meta-psychiatric pathway enriched across disorders and overlaps with open chromatin regions in the fetal brain, suggesting a regulatory role during neurodevelopment, though its precise functions remain undefined. ZSCAN12P1 has been implicated as one of the top genes explaining genetic correlations between immune disorders and psychiatric disease59, yet little is known about its biological role. These examples underscore the need for experimental dissection of molecular mechanisms in brain development, with particular attention to stage-specific processes—especially in the fetal brain—where early perturbations may set the stage for later disease risk.

Distinct disorder- and tissue-specific pathway patterns further illuminate the complexity of these findings. For example, SCZ DLPFC uniquely exhibited enrichment for pathways related to ear morphogenesis, auditory receptor development, and dentate gyrus development—features relevant to early neural development in SCZ36,37 but not observed in fetal tissue. Despite observing many relevant neurodevelopmental signals, the absence of these signals in fetal analyses may reflect greater tissue heterogeneity in fetal samples used, which spans across many brain regions across a broad range of gestational age, spanning though all three trimesters, compared to the cortex, brain restricted to adult brain. We also observed that while all four disorders showed enrichment for a variety of synaptic and neuronal pathways, MDD was distinguished by predominant enrichment of broader synaptic structural pathways, with limited involvement of neurotransmitter specific activities. This contrasts with PTSD, BIP, and SCZ, where neurotransmitter-related pathways were more enriched, especially in the fetal brain. Notably, the MDD fetal TWAS analysis also showed enrichment for sensory perception, including olfactory receptor pathways, suggesting unique developmental features in this disorder44. In addition, epigenetic modification pathways were more strongly enriched in DLPFC than in fetal brain, indicating a heightened contribution of environmentally responsive gene regulation in the mature cortex. Finally, pathways related to stress hormone signaling and environmental response appeared exclusively in DLPFC for PTSD and SCZ, highlighting the potential importance of postnatal and environmental influences in these disorders.

Our study has some limitations that present inherent challenges and are not straightforward to resolve. Although TWAS aims to prioritize genes at the tissue level for a given disorder, signals from complex loci—particularly chr6p21 (a highly polymorphic region) and chr17q21.31 (characterized by EUR-enriched inversion polymorphism)—may not reliably reflect causal relationships across populations. Excluding these loci is also not a viable solution, as they harbor well-established functional genes (e.g., C4A in schizophrenia, implicated in synaptic pruning via microglia; CRHR1 in PTSD, a key mediator of the stress response). Importantly, because GTAs represent independent signals, any locus-specific biases would only influence GTAs within that region, and retaining these loci does not affect genome-wide TWAS results. However, pathway analyses that include genes from these regions could be influenced by such complexities and should be interpreted with caution (see Supplementary Discussion). A further challenge lies in the mismatched ancestry between GWAS and GEx reference panels. As a proof of principle, we employed cTWAS for fine-mapping, which can help address linkage disequilibrium (LD) differences across populations, as illustrated in selected examples. In addition, we use a straightforward approach to integrate mismatched GEx panels with GWAS to better understand and tease apart the effects of ancestry-driven gene regulation, as more complex methods might obscure the understanding. The knowledge gained from this approach could guide the use of more sophisticated methods (Supplementary Discussion).

We observe relatively lower correlation between TWAS results associated with AA GWAS, even in cohorts with higher AA sample sizes compared to EUR. This may be due to the admixed nature of the AA population, which affects factors such as the accuracy of LD representation in reference panels. Further, using local ancestry based GWAS summary statistics and GReX modeling could enhance the results60. Furthermore, we acknowledge that the African component of the AA population represents only a fraction of the genetic diversity of the African continent61. Developing well-represented African, as well as other underrepresented continental, GEx panels and GWAS cohorts with well-characterized case-control designs would greatly enhance TWAS findings. This approach is preferable to simply increasing sample size from a specific geographical region without a strategic plan, and while also dealing with inconsistent phenotyping of traits7.

To conclude, while ancestry-diverse datasets are still being profiled62, we demonstrate that the limited available diverse datasets can already be leveraged to identify complementary ancestry-specific TWAS signals across many genomic loci. This underscores the need to move beyond uniform statistical approaches toward loci-tailored strategies informed by factors such as recent recombination hotspots, LD structures across ancestries, the genetic architecture of diseases, and the tissue specificity of expression, enabling more precise and meaningful insights into molecular and genetic mechanisms.

Methods

DLPFC Data

Our DLPFC data processing and imputation work is largely based on our previous publication31.

Datasets

The reference genotype-expression sample for building a DLPFC GReX model was obtained from PEC (PsychENCODE)63. We used the data of subjects (age at death ≥ 18 years) with matched genotype and bulk DLPFC gene expression data derived from 1425 unique donors63. The data came from five studies (CMC, CMC-HBCC, LIBD, BrainGVEX, UCLA-ASD). It was downloaded from Synapse (node: syn8466658). The resulting dataset contained sequencing data from both rRNA-depletion and poly-A-selection libraries and contained both stranded and non-stranded samples.

Genotype

PEC genotypes were generated and imputed as previously reported20. Samples were genotyped using various Illumina platforms, including the Illumina 1 M, Illumina HumanHap650 (h650), Illumina Infinium HumanOmniExpressExome v1.1b, Illumina Omni5, and Illumina h650 arrays. Raw genotype data were first converted to PLINK format and subjected to rigorous quality control (QC). Sample-level QC included filtering based on call rate below 95%, heterozygosity outliers, and sex discrepancies. Marker-level QC excluded SNPs with zero alternate alleles, call rates below 95%, Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium p-values < 1 × 10−6. Genotype was imputed using Minimac with the Haplotype Reference Consortium (HRC) panel. Post-Imputation, SNPs were filtered to retain only high-quality variants with an imputation quality score R2 > 0.3. Sub-studies imputed genotype files were merged using BCFtools (v1.9). Multi-allelic SNPs were removed, and genotype dosages were computed on a continuous scale from 0 (homozygous reference) to 2 (homozygous alternate), with 1 representing heterozygous genotypes.

Genotype based ancestry

We calculate ancestry proportions for each subject by projecting their genotype data onto principal components derived from known continental level ancestry markers. For this, we used the global ancestry pipeline (https://github.com/nievergeltlab/global_ancestry), which applies SNPweights64 to a set of 10,000 ancestry informative markers. These markers were evaluated against a reference panel comprising 2911 individuals from 71 diverse populations and six continental groups (k = 6)48. Subjects with a European ancestry proportion > 0.9 were labeled as EUR (n = 1032), while those with an African ancestry proportion > 0.6 were labeled as AA (n = 276) subjects (Supplementary Fig. 1).

RNA-seq

PEC tissue preparation, RNA extraction, cDNA library preparation, and sequencing procedures were reported in ref. 65. All RNA-seq samples were reprocessed using a uniform pipeline to avoid confounding effects of differential data preparation. PEC RNA-seq data were queried in bam format. We adopted the GTEx RNA-seq processing pipeline66. In brief, duplicates were marked with Picard (v.2.22.8) and RNA-seqC (v.2.3.5) was used to quantify gene expression. Samples were removed if they contained fewer than 10 million reads, more than 1.1% mismatch rate, or more than 30% intergenic reads.

Genetically-regulated gene expression (GReX) model

We adopted the PrediXcan database construction methodology to make our prediction model output compatible with the PrediXcan software family. We used elastic-net regularized regression, as it accounts for sparse eQTL architecture and robustly outperforms other available approaches8,67,68. In line with previous models, protein-coding genes, pseudogenes, and lncRNA were included in our database.

Processing of data for each GReX training dataset

Within each GReX training dataset: (a) RNA Seq data was normalized. RPKM Normalized RNA-seq counts were inverse quantile normalized. (b) SNPs with less than 0.01 MAF were dropped. (c) Five genetic principal components (PCs) were calculated. (d) PEER factors were calculated to account for unknown sources of technical variation between and within the different studies of the main dataset69. The number of PEER factors was chosen based on the training sample size: 30 for > 150 and ≤ 250, 45 for > 250 and ≤ 350 and 60 for > 350. Along with genetic PCs and peer factors, sex, age at death, library preparation (rRNAdepletion vs polyA), sub-study, PMI, RIN, and sequencing platform were included as covariates for model construction.

Elastic-net regularized regression model

We applied elastic-net regularized regression to estimate SNP effect sizes within 1 Mb of each gene, following the metaXcan framework8. Model performance was estimated using nested cross-validation (5 outer folds for unbiased evaluation, with 10-fold inner cross-validation for lambda selection) and reported as the average square of the Pearson correlation (R²). Final SNP–gene weights were obtained by retraining models on the entire dataset using 10-fold cross-validation for lambda selection. Models with R2 > 0.01 and P < 0.05 were retained for downstream analyses, as in ref. 8.

Enrichment of GReX-specific genes

We perform gene set enrichment using metascape70 via their web service.

External validation data

The validation DLPFC gene expression and genotype data used were derived from 245 EUR (including 73 controls, 25 BIP, 85 MDD and 63 PTSD) and 50 AA (including 25 controls, 15 MDD, 7 PTSD, and 3 BIP) samples, part of a prior multi-region RNA-seq study (n = 32571). EUR and AA ancestry were assigned using the same method as described above. These brain samples were collected by the Lieber Institute of Brain Development (LIBD) as part of their PTSD brain collection72,73 that was acquired by the National PTSD Brain Bank74. For the validation analysis, we regressed out age, sex, disease status and RIN from normalized gene expression data and scaled the residuals.

External validation performance metrics

metaXcan (https://github.com/hakyimlab/MetaXcan) imputation pipeline with default setting was used to predict gene expression. To quantify observed performance ρGReXobs (spearman correlation), predicted expression from the genotype of the validation dataset is correlated with observed expression. To compare performance between a pair of GReX model, the difference between ρGReXobs is calculated for genes predicted in both models. mean, and skewness is calculated on this difference of ρGReXobs.

Genetic Correlation

The genetic correlation across four GWAS datasets was calculated using the LDSC (Linkage Disequilibrium Score Regression) tool. Initially, GWAS summary statistics were reformatted and filtered to include HAPMAP3 SNPs. Predefined LD scores and weights provided by the tool were then applied to the reformatted summary statistics files to calculate genetic correlation.

Gene trait associations (GTAs)

JEPEGMIX2-P software22 with default settings was used to conduct TWAS using our GReX models. JEPEGMIX2-P was applied on GWAS summary statistics to estimate GTAs. This method was preferable since it relied on a covariance matrix based on 33 K samples compared to other TWAS methods, which use less than 3k samples75. To qualify GTAs as statistically significant (FDR-GTAs) in our TWAS analysis, we use FDR correction with a threshold of 0.05 instead of the Bonferroni correction. This choice is supported by the observation that JEPEGMiX delivers more stringent associations than metaXcan.

metaTWAS

metaTWAS for GTAs was performed using METAL76. Genes with the same effect size direction were kept. Determining signals passing multiple testing correction was based on FDR, i.e., an FDR-adjusted P < 0.05. We performed 5 types of meta-analyses of AA-based, EUR-based and AAEUR-based GTAs: (i) metaEUR that meta-analyzes GTAs produced using GReXEUR applied on EUR GWAS and on AA GWAS [GReXEUR(GWASEUR) + GReXEUR(GWASAA)], (ii) metaAA that meta-analyzes GTAs produced using GReXAA applied on EUR GWAS and on AA GWAS [GReXAA(GWASEUR) + GReXAA(GWASAA)], (iii) meta ancestry-specific (metaAnc) that meta-analyzes GTAs produced using GReXAA applied on AA GWAS with GReXEUR applied on EUR GWAS [GReXAA(GWASAA) + GReXEUR(GWASEUR)], (iv) meta mismatch (metaMis) that meta-analyzes GTAs produced using GReXAA applied on EUR GWAS with GReXEUR applied on AA GWAS [GReXAA(GWASEUR) + GReXEUR(GWASAA)] and (v) metaAAEUR that meta-analyzes GTAs produced using GReXAAEUR applied on AA GWAS and on EUR GWAS [GReXAAEUR(GWASAA) + GReXAAEUR(GWASEUR)].

TWAS-Pathway analysis

With JEPEGMIX2-P, we were able to conduct TWAS pathway analysis. JEPEGMIX2-P combines GTA z-score statistics in a pathway with Mahalanobis χ2statistics, while adjusting for LD for the participating genes. In this case, we calculated disease association for GO-BP/MP/CC pathways. Determining signals passing multiple testing correction was based on FDR, i.e., an FDR-adjusted P < 0.05.

meta Pathway

Since JEPEGMiX2-P provides chisquare statistics pathways, we use fishercomb function from metaRNASeq package77 to perform meta-analysis. Since JEPEGMiX2-P provides chi-square statistics for each pathway, we use fishercomb function from the metaRNASeq package77 to perform meta-analysis.

TWAS pathway correlations

We performed pathway correlations using enrichment p-values, as we do not have an effect size.

Clustering of TWAS pathways

TWAS GO pathway analysis results were visualized by clustering selected pathways based on semantic similarity using the simplifyEnrichment package29. Within each ontology (BP, CC, and MF), pathways were analyzed using the GO_similarity function, where semantic similarities were calculated based on gene annotations to GO terms with the Wang similarity measure78. Clusters were then refined using the simplifyGO function, which applied the Louvain graph community method to form distinct pathway groups. Summaries of the biological functions within each cluster were visualized as word clouds attached to the similarity heatmap, offering a direct illustration of the common biological functions involved in each cluster. cTWAS

The putative causal GTAs were obtained using cTWAS27, for GReXAACt165 and GReXEURCt165 models applied on EUR SCZ GWAS summary statistics. Analyses were run using the default parameter settings of cTWAS, except that the minimum number of variables in a region was set to 10 (min_group_size = 10), and a posterior inclusion probability (PIP) threshold of > 0.5 was applied to identify putative causal genes. Linkage disequilibrium (LD) information was obtained from the pre-computed reference LD matrices of European samples from the UK Biobank available on the cTWAS website.

Rank-rank hypogeometeric overlap (RRHO)

RRHO2 for GTAs

RRHO plots for GTA results were generated using RRHO2 software79. For these analyses, we ranked genes based on p-values and the sign of the direction of effect of the GTAs generated by JEPEGMiX software.

RRHO for pathways

RRHO plots for pathways results were generated using RRHO software80. As JEPEGMIX pathway enrichments are based on chi-square statistics and hence lacks directionality of enrichment, pathway rankings were based solely on p-values.

LD Block calculations

We use the LDMatrix option from the LDlink26 online tool. Americans of African Ancestry in SW USA (ASW) population is used as a proxy for our AA population. All European population: Utah Residents from North and West Europe (CEU), Toscani in Italia (TSI), Finnish in Finland (FIN), British in England and Scotland (GBR) and Iberian population in Spain (IBS) was used to represent our EUR population.

Gene-trait associations (GTA)

We performed transcriptome-wide association analysis (TWAS) at two levels for each psychiatric disorder examined i.e., (i) at the GWAS summary-statistics level, herein referred to as summary-based TWAS as described above, and (ii) at the genotype-level, herein referred to as raw TWAS. For raw TWAS, we first estimated GReX using the ancestry-specific models requiring genotype information as input, followed by phenotype-GReX association. GReX was estimated using metaXcan (https://github.com/hakyimlab/MetaXcan). Association was also implemented with PrediXcanAssociation.py. PTSD case-control status was estimated previously81. Covariates were comparable to the original GWAS, which included age, sex, and the first five genetic principal components.

Fetal Brain Tissue model

Dataset

The developing human brain data was obtained and processed as described here43. Briefly, this study combines five individual studies, the Walker study82 contributed brain cortical samples (14-21 post-conception weeks [PCW]) generated using Ribo-Zero with 60 M reads per sample RNA-seq and Illumina HumanOmni2.5 genotyping array. The O’Brien56 study included brain samples (12–19 PCW) sequenced with Ribo-Zero with 100 M reads per sample RNA-Seq data and Illumina Infinium OmniExpress-24 BeadChip genotyping array. The Werling study83 comprised of DLPFC samples (6–37 PCW) sequenced with Ribo-Zero 37 M reads per sample RNA-seq and 30X whole-genome sequencing. The LIBD study84 contributed DLPFC samples (14–20 PCE) sequenced with Ribo-Zero 150 M reads per sample RNA-seq and Illumina 1 M DuoV2 genotyping array. The HDBR study included bulk brain samples (4–20 PCW) sequenced with PolyA 50 M reads and Illumina HumanOmni5-4v1-B genotyping array. Ten duplicate samples between the HDBR and O-Brien studies were removed, and the dataset was further filtered to retain only DNA-RNA matched and high-quality samples.

Genotype

Genotype Data from individual studies were uniformly processed and filtered for MAF < 0.01, HWE (--hwe 1 × 10−6), variants per sample missingness (--geno 0.05) and samples per variant missingness (--mind 0.10). QC-ed genotype data were imputed on the Michigan Imputation Server (Minimac 4, Eagle v2.4 for phasing) using the TOPMed Freeze5 reference panel, retaining variants with imputation R2 > 0.3. Post-imputation, studies were merged by intersecting variants, removing multi-allelic sites. Variants were filtered for MAF < 0.01, HWE (p < 1 × 10−6), and variants per sample missingness (--geno 0.05). LD-pruned (plink --indep-pairwise 50 5 0.2) data were merged with 1000 Genomes. PCA and k-nearest-neighbors (k = 50) were run to infer data ancestry. 135 AA and 280 EUR samples were used to build fetalAA and fetalEUR GReX models, respectively.

GReX Modeling

We used the imputed genotype data restricted to HapMap 3 variants only and expression panel corrected by age, sex, 5 genotype PCs, and hidden component with priors (HPC). Using the TWAS-FUSION framework, five-fold cross-validation of three models of expression prediction (best cis-eQTL, elastic-net, SuSiE) were calculated and evaluated for accuracy. Although only elastic net models were retained with p-value < .05 and R2 > 0.01 for downstream analysis.
