Noise sensitivity on virtually abelian groups
Jérémie Brieussel, Ryokichi Tanaka

Abstract
. We show that aperiodic random walks with finite second moment on virtually abelian groups are noise sensitive in total variation if and only if the group admits no nonzero homomorphism onto the infinite cyclic group.



1.
Introduction

Let
\Gamma
be a countable group and
\mu

be a probability measure on the group.
The
\mu
-
random walk

\{w_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}

starting from the identity
\id
is defined by
w_{n}:=\gamma_{1}\cdots\gamma_{n}
and
w_{0}:=\id

for an independent, identically distributed sequence
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2},\dots
with the law
\mu
.
The distribution of
w_{n}
coincides with the
n
-fold convolution
\mu_{n}:=\mu^{\ast n}
.
The
noise sensitivity problem
on
\mu
-random walks asks the following.
For each fixed real
\rho\in(0,1)
,
let
w_{n}^{\rho}:=\gamma^{\rho}_{1}\cdots\gamma^{\rho}_{n}
,
where
\gamma_{i}^{\rho}
is resampled with the same law
\mu
with probability
\rho
,
or is retained as
\gamma_{i}
with probability
1-\rho
,
independently in every step.
Is the pair
w_{n}
and
w_{n}^{\rho}
nearly independent or highly correlated after a large enough time
n
?

More precisely, for each
\rho\in[0,1]
,
let

\pi^{\rho}:=\rho(\mu\times\mu)+(1-\rho)\mu_{\rm diag}\quad\text{on $\Gamma\times\Gamma$},

where
\mu\times\mu
denotes the product measure and
\mu_{\rm diag}((\gamma,\gamma^{\prime})):=\mu(\gamma)
if
\gamma=\gamma^{\prime}
and
0
if else.
For a
\pi^{\rho}
-random walk
\{\boldsymbol{w}_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
starting from
\id
on
\Gamma\times\Gamma
,
the distribution
\pi^{\rho}_{n}
coincides with that of the pair
(w_{n},w_{n}^{\rho})
.
We say that a
\mu
-random walk on
\Gamma
is
noise sensitive
in total variation (or, equivalently in
\ell^{1}
) if

\lim_{n\to\infty}\|\pi^{\rho}_{n}-\mu_{n}\times\mu_{n}\|_{\rm TV}=0\quad\text{for each $\rho\in(0,1]$}.

Recall that the total variation distance between two probability measures
\nu_{i}
for
i=1,2
on
\Gamma\times\Gamma
is given by
\|\nu_{1}-\nu_{2}\|_{\rm TV}:=\max_{A\subset\Gamma\times\Gamma}|\nu_{1}(A)-\nu_{2}(A)|=(1/2)\|\nu_{1}-\nu_{2}\|_{1}
.

Roughly speaking,
if a
\mu
-random walk on
\Gamma
is noise sensitive,
then even small
\rho
-portion of resampling in increments causes a significant effect,
producing an independent copy of the walk eventually.
The noise sensitivity problem on random walks on groups was introduced by Benjamini and the first named author in
[
1
]
(see also a related discussion in
[
3
, Section 3.3.4]
).
In that paper, the authors discuss other variants of the notion in terms of entropy and of different measurements for the distance.
In the present paper, we focus on noise sensitivity in total variation,
and thus we call it noise sensitivity for brevity.
A basic question one can ask: What groups admit noise sensitive random walks?

Both examples and non-examples of noise sensitive random walks have been discovered.
On the one hand, there are two obstructions to noise sensitivity that apply to large families of groups
[
1
, Theorem 1.1]
.
First, if
\Gamma
admits a nonzero homomorphism onto
{\mathbb{Z}}
, then a
\mu
-random walk is not noise sensitive.
This follows from an inspection on the central limit theorem.
Second, if
(\Gamma,\mu)
is non-Liouville, i.e.
\Gamma
admits a non-constant bounded
\mu
-harmonic functions,
then a
\mu
-random walk is not noise sensitive.
This follows by using the representations of bounded
\mu
-harmonic functions in terms of
\mu
-stationary distributions.
So far, these two results are the only known obstructions for noise sensitivity.

On the other hand, there are some examples of noise-sensitive random walks. Besides finite groups which are obviously noise sensitive
[
1
, Proposition 5.1]
, the infinite dihedral group, and more generally every affine Weyl group admits some noise sensitive random walk by
[
8
, Theorem 1.1]
(see also
[
1
, Theorem 1.4]
for the dihedral group).
Note that these groups admit finite index abelian subgroups but do not admit nonzero homomorphisms onto the infinite cyclic group
{\mathbb{Z}}
since they have a set of generators consisting of finite order elements.

The present paper deals with the class of finitely generated virtually abelian groups, i.e. groups which admit finite index abelian subgroups.
Random walks on such groups are Liouville (see, e.g.,
[
2
]
).
We show that, in this class,
the existence of nonzero homomorphisms onto
{\mathbb{Z}}
is the only obstruction to noise sensitivity for
aperiodic
random walks. This provides new examples of noise sensitive random walks, see Examples
1.4
and
1.5
below.

Recall that
\mu
is
aperiodic
if there exist at least two positive probability random walk paths from
\id
to
\id
such that their lengths are mutually prime, e.g., if
\mu(\id)>0

(cf. Section
4.2
). Also recall that given a word metric
|\cdot|
in
\Gamma
, a probability measure
\mu
has
finite second moment
if

\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}|\gamma|^{2}\mu(\gamma)<\infty
.
This condition is independent of the choice of word metrics since arbitrary such metrics are bi-Lipschitz equivalent.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Gamma
be a finitely generated virtually abelian group and
\mu
be an aperiodic probability measure on
\Gamma
with finite second moment such that the support generates the group as a semigroup.
Then the
\mu
-random walk on
\Gamma
is noise sensitive in total variation, i.e.

\left\|\pi^{\rho}_{n}-\mu_{n}\times\mu_{n}\right\|_{\rm TV}\to 0\quad\text{as $n\to\infty$},

for all
\rho\in(0,1]

if and only if
\Gamma
does not admit nonzero homomorphisms onto
{\mathbb{Z}}
.

This Theorem
1.1
generalizes the known result on affine Weyl groups with
\mu
supported on the canonical set of generators and the identity, established by the second named author
[
8
, Theorem 1.1]
.
If the group
\Gamma
admits a nonzero homomorphism onto
{\mathbb{Z}}
,
then the asymptotic behavior of the total variation distance depends on the parameter
\rho
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\Gamma
be a finitely generated virtually abelian group and
\mu
be a finite second moment probability measure on
\Gamma
such that the support generates the group as a semigroup.
Then

\lim_{\rho\to 1}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\left\|\pi^{\rho}_{n}-\mu_{n}\times\mu_{n}\right\|_{\rm TV}=0.

If moreover
\Gamma
admits a nonzero homomorphism onto
{\mathbb{Z}}
,
then

\lim_{\rho\to 0}\liminf_{n\to\infty}\left\|\pi^{\rho}_{n}-\mu_{n}\times\mu_{n}\right\|_{\rm TV}=1,

in particular, the
\mu
-random walk on
\Gamma
is not noise sensitive in total variation.

Note that in the above Theorem
1.2
, the probability measure
\mu
is not necessarily aperiodic.
In the course of proving Theorem
1.1
,
we observe the following more general “decoupling” phenomenon on a product group
\Gamma_{1}\times\Gamma_{2}
, where
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
may not be isomorphic.
Two coupled random walks on virtually abelian groups
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
, potentially highly correlated though, eventually behave as two independent random walks if one of the factors admits no nonzero homomorphism onto
{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Theorem 1.3
.

For
i=1,2
, let
\Gamma_{i}
be a finitely generated virtually abelian group,
and
\nu
be an aperiodic probability measure with finite second moment on
\Gamma_{1}\times\Gamma_{2}
such that the support of
\nu
generates
\Gamma_{1}\times\Gamma_{2}
as a semigroup and
\nu
has the marginal
\mu^{(i)}
on
\Gamma_{i}
.
If
\Gamma_{1}
admits no nonzero homomorphism onto
{\mathbb{Z}}
,
then

\big\|\nu_{n}-\mu^{(1)}_{n}\times\mu^{(2)}_{n}\big\|_{\rm TV}\to 0\quad\text{as $n\to\infty$}.

We apply this result to
\pi^{\rho}
for
\rho\in(0,1]
and deduce noise sensitivity for
\mu
-random walks on groups
\Gamma
with no nonzero homomorphisms to
{\mathbb{Z}}
.

Before we describe the outlines of the proofs, we discuss some explicit examples.

Example 1.4
.

Let us consider the infinite dihedral group

D_{\infty}:=\left\langle s_{1},s_{2}\mid s_{1}^{2}=s_{2}^{2}=\id\right\rangle.

The group
D_{\infty}
is isomorphic to the semi-direct product
{\mathbb{Z}}\rtimes({\mathbb{Z}}/2)
. It does not admit nonzero homomorphisms onto
{\mathbb{Z}}
since it is generated by two involutions
s_{1}
and
s_{2}
.
Let
\mu_{\rm LSRW}
be the uniform distribution on
\{s_{1},s_{2},\id\}
.
Then, the
\mu_{\rm LSRW}
-random walk (“lazy simple random walk”) on
D_{\infty}
is noise sensitive by Theorem
1.1
.
The noise sensitivity of this special random walk on
D_{\infty}
has already been proven by a coupling method in
[
1
, Section 6]
.
Let us provide another example.
Let
\mu_{\rm ape}
be the uniform distribution on
\{s_{1},s_{2},s_{1}s_{2},s_{2}s_{1}\}
.
Then,
\mu_{\rm ape}
is aperiodic.
This follows since the
\mu_{\rm ape}
-random walk starting from
\id
returns to
\id
with positive probability at times
2
and
3
.
Theorem
1.1
implies that the
\mu_{\rm ape}
-random walk on
D_{\infty}
is noise sensitive.

In the above Example
1.4
,
if
\mu_{\rm SRW}
denotes the uniform distribution on
\{s_{1},s_{2}\}
,
then the
\mu_{\rm SRW}
-random walk (“simple random walk”) on
D_{\infty}
is not noise sensitive.
Indeed, the support of
(\mu_{\rm SRW})^{\ast 2}
is included in
{\mathbb{Z}}
under the identification
D_{\infty}={\mathbb{Z}}\rtimes({\mathbb{Z}}/2)
, and
(\mu_{\rm SRW})^{\ast 2}
defines the lazy simple random walk on
{\mathbb{Z}}
with the holding probability
1/2
.
Furthermore, for each
\rho\in(0,1]
,
and for the probability measure
\pi^{\rho}
, the support of
(\pi^{\rho})^{\ast 2}
is included in the subgroup
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
of
D_{\infty}^{2}
under the identification
D_{\infty}^{2}={\mathbb{Z}}^{2}\rtimes({\mathbb{Z}}/2)^{2}
.
If the
\mu_{\rm SRW}
-random walk on
D_{\infty}
were noise sensitive,
then we would have

\|\pi^{\rho}_{2n}-(\mu_{\rm SRW})_{2n}\times(\mu_{\rm SRW})_{2n}\|_{\rm TV}\to 0\quad\text{
as $n\to\infty$}.

However, this does not hold for the
(\pi^{\rho})^{\ast 2}
-random walk on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
: the total variation distance does not tend to
0
for
\rho
close enough to
0
.
Indeed, the
(\pi^{\rho})^{\ast 2}
-random walk on
{\mathbb{Z}}
is not noise sensitive, which follows from the proof of Theorem
1.2
.
This in particular indicates that the property of being noise sensitive does depend on the random walk (not solely on the group), as already pointed out in
[
1
, Theorem 1.4]
.

.

Let us consider the following “triangle” group

\Gamma_{\rm tri}:=\left\langle s_{1},s_{2},s_{3}\mid s_{1}^{3}=s_{2}^{3}=s_{3}^{3}=s_{1}s_{2}s_{3}=\id\right\rangle.

This is an index

\widetilde{A}_{2}
,

Outlines of the proofs

Let us briefly explain the proof of Theorems
1.1
on noise sensitivity and that of Theorem
1.3
on more general decoupling.
We will also outline the proof of Theorem
1.2
on non-noise sensitivity.

An infinite finitely generated virtually abelian group
\Gamma
admits a finite index normal subgroup
\Lambda
isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}^{m}
for some
m\geq 1
.
The main technical ingredient is to establish the following form of local central limit theorem (CLT) for the
\mu
-random walk on
\Gamma
.
Defining an appropriate “Gaussian distribution”
\mathcal{N}_{n,\Sigma,\zeta}
on
\Gamma
with mean
n\zeta
and covariance
n\Sigma
,
we have

\|\mu_{n}-\mathcal{N}_{n,\Sigma,\zeta}\|_{\rm TV}\to 0\quad\text{as $n\to\infty$}.

In the above, we use the
\Lambda
-coset decomposition to define
{\mathcal{N}}_{n,\Sigma,\zeta}
, as this measure is a product of an appropriate distribution on the quotient
\Lambda\backslash\Gamma
and a Gaussian on
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.

This local CLT holds for an aperiodic
\mu
with finite second moment, and can be generalized to a non-aperiodic measure once the period is taken into account. See Theorem
4.7
in Section
4.4
for the precise statement.
We note that this is a special case of local CLT which has already been proven in
[
6
]
(where the Markov chain is called “random walks with internal degrees of freedom”).
We, however, establish an explicit formula to compute the covariance matrix
\Sigma
in terms of harmonic
1
-forms on a finite directed graph associated with the virtually abelian group structure.
This explicit formula is essential to verifying decoupling in Theorem
1.3
and noise sensitivity in Theorem
1.1
.
The overall strategy follows the line of proof in the case of affine Weyl groups in
[
8
]
;
however, we need to generalize and strengthen the technical details.

We apply the local CLT to the product group
\Gamma_{1}\times\Gamma_{2}
and show that if both
\Gamma_{1}
and
\Gamma_{2}
have no nonzero homomorphisms onto
{\mathbb{Z}}
,
then the drift
\zeta
vanishes and
\Sigma
has a block diagonal form along the product structure.
(The fact that the drift vanishes follows from the general result
[
4
]
, see Section
5
.)
This part requires a group-theoretic consideration.
We consider a canonical group homomorphism
\vartheta:\Gamma\to{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}
called
transfer
, defined in terms of the cocycle of the action on
\Lambda\backslash\Gamma
.
If
\Gamma
admits no nonzero homomorphism onto
{\mathbb{Z}}
,
then
\vartheta
has to be the zero homomorphism.
It turns out that this has a strong restriction on the behavior of random walks on
\Gamma
.

If
\Gamma
has nonzero homomorphisms onto
{\mathbb{Z}}
,
then the local CLT shows that the covariance matrix detects a correlation between two factors.
This is used to show Theorem
1.2
on non-noise sensitivity.
A part of the proof is reduced to the case of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}
.

In fact, the explicit form of the covariance matrix of the asymptotic Gaussian distribution allows us to deduce structural results, stated as Theorem
5.1
and Proposition
5.7
, related to the transfer homomorphism
\vartheta:\Gamma\to{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}

as described in Section
3
.
We will provide the precise statements in Section
5
.

Organization

In the preliminary Section
2
, we present the notion of weighted diagrams and their harmonic
1
-forms. In Section
3
, we devote ourselves to the basics of random walks on virtually abelian groups: the transfer homomorphism, the associated weighted diagram and the potentials corresponding to the cocycle of finite quotient action.
In Section
4
, we establish the local CLT for random walks on the groups we are considering, and this will be of independent interest.
In Section
5
,
we establish the general structural result Theorem
5.1

on the covariance matrices in the local CLTs. We
apply it to deduce noise sensitivity and decoupling, which provide the proofs of Theorems
1.1
,
1.2
and
1.3
.

2.
Weighted diagrams and their harmonic
1
-forms

In this section, we introduce weighted diagrams and their harmonic
1
-forms.

2.1.
Weighted diagram

A diagram
G
is a multi-digraph with set of vertices
V(G)
and set of directed edges
E(G)
. To each edge is associated its
original
vertex
oe
and its
terminal
vertex
te
. In our definition, we assume that
E(G)
is closed under taking reverse edges: to each
e\in E(G)
there corresponds an edge
\bar{e}
with
o\bar{e}=te
,
t\bar{e}=oe
and
\bar{\bar{e}}=e
. Note that we allow the possibility that there are several edges going from a vertex
x=oe
to another vertex
y=te
, as well as loops, i.e. edges with
te=oe
. We will assume that the vertex set
V(G)
is finite, but even in this case the edge set
E(G)
can be infinite.

A
weighted diagram
is a diagram equipped with a weight map
p:E(G)\to[0,1]
such that

\sum_{e:oe=x}p(e)=1\quad\text{for all $x\in V(G)$}.

It defines a Markov chain on
V(G)
with transition probabilities

p(x,y)=\sum_{e:oe=x,te=y}p(e)\quad\text{for all $x,y\in V(G)$}.

If the Markov chain is irreducible, i.e. the probability to reach any vertex starting from any vertex is positive, then by the Perron-Frobenius Theorem, it admits a unique stationary distribution
\pi
on
V(G)
, i.e.,

\sum_{e:te=x}\pi(oe)p(e)=\pi(x)\quad\text{for each $x\in V(G)$}.

The
conductance
of an edge
e\in E(G)
is

c(e):=\pi(oe)p(e)
. The conductance
c
can be viewed as a probability measure on
E(G)
. The stationary measure
\pi
and the weight map
p
can be recovered from
c
.
The Markov chain is
reversible
if
c(e)=c(\overline{e})
for every
e\in E(G)
. Note that it is important for our purpose to consider also non-reversible Markov chains on
V(G)
.

2.2.
Harmonic
1
-forms on the weighted diagram
(G,c)

Let us define the
{\mathbb{C}}
-linear space of complex-valued functions on
V(G)
,

C^{0}(G,{\mathbb{C}}):=\Bigl\{f:V(G)\to{\mathbb{C}}\Bigr\}.

This space is endowed with the inner product
\left\langle f_{1},f_{2}\right\rangle_{\pi}:=\sum_{x\in V(G)}f_{1}(x)\overline{f_{2}(x)}\pi(x)
,
where
\overline{\alpha}
denotes the complex-conjugate of a complex number
\alpha
.
Analogously,

C^{0}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
denotes the
{\mathbb{R}}
-linear space of real-valued functions on
V(G)
equipped with the inner product
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle_{\pi}
.
We say that a (real-valued) function on
E(G)
is a
1
-form
if
\omega(\overline{e})=-\omega(e)
for all
e\in E(G)
.
Let
C^{1}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
denote the space of
1
-forms on
G
.
We define the real Hilbert space of square integrable
1
-forms on
G
by

\ell_{c}^{2}(G,{\mathbb{R}}):=\Bigl\{\omega\in C^{1}(G,{\mathbb{R}})\ :\ \|\omega\|_{c}<\infty\Bigr\}.

In the above,
\|\omega\|_{c}
denotes the induced norm and the inner product is defined by

\left\langle\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\right\rangle_{c}:=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{e\in E(G)}\omega_{1}(e)\omega_{2}(e)c(e).

For
f\in C^{0}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
,
the
differential

df
is the
1
-form defined by

df(e):=f(te)-f(oe)
for
e\in E(G)
.
Note that

\displaystyle\|df\|_{c}^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{e\in E(G)}|df(e)|^{2}c(e)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in V(G)}|f(y)-f(x)|^{2}\sum_{e:oe=x,te=y}\pi(x)p(e)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x,y\in V(G)}|f(y)-f(x)|^{2}\,\pi(y)<\infty.

In the above, the last summation runs over a finite set.
This shows that

d:C^{0}(G,{\mathbb{R}})\to\ell_{c}^{2}(G,{\mathbb{R}})\subset C^{1}(G,{\mathbb{R}})

is a well-defined
{\mathbb{R}}
-linear operator.
For
\omega\in\ell_{c}^{2}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
,
let

d^{\ast}\omega(x):=-\frac{1}{\pi(x)}\sum_{e:oe=x}c(e)\omega(e)\quad\text{for $x\in V(G)$}.

This is well-defined by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and defines an
{\mathbb{R}}
-linear operator
d^{\ast}:\ell_{c}^{2}(G,{\mathbb{R}})\to C^{0}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
.
The transition operator
P
on
C^{0}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
to itself is defined by

Pf(x):=\frac{1}{\pi(x)}\sum_{e:oe=x}c(e)f(te)\quad\text{for $x\in V(G)$}.

Letting
I
denote the identity operator,
we have

d^{\ast}d=I-P.

Remark 2.1
.

When the weighted diagram is reversible, and in this case only,
the transition operator
P
is self-adjoint
and
d^{\ast}
is the adjoint of
d
, i.e.
\left\langle df,\omega\right\rangle_{c}=\left\langle f,d^{\ast}\omega\right\rangle_{\pi}
for all
f\in C^{0}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
and
\omega\in\ell_{c}^{2}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
. We insist on the fact that in the general case,
d^{\ast}
is
not
the adjoint of
d
.

For every
\omega\in\ell_{c}^{2}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
,
let us define

\chi_{c}(\omega):=\sum_{e\in E(G)}\omega(e)c(e).

This is well-defined by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.
Let us define the
{\mathbb{R}}
-linear subspace of
harmonic
1
-forms
by

H^{1}:=\Bigl\{\omega\in\ell_{c}^{2}(G,{\mathbb{R}})\ :\ d^{\ast}\omega+\chi_{c}(\omega)=0\Bigr\}.

We have
\chi_{c}(\omega)=0
for all
\omega\in\ell_{c}^{2}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
if and only if
c(e)=c(\overline{e})
for all
e\in E(G)
.

Remark 2.2
.

This notion of harmonic
1
-forms was introduced by Kotani and Sunada
[
5
]
. In their paper, they highlight the role of the homology class of
\sum_{e\in E(G)}c(e)e
, which they call the
homological direction
of a random walk.

The following lemma appears in
[
5
, Lemma 5.2]
, which we prove in an extended form.

Lemma 2.3
.

Letting
\im d:=dC^{0}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
be the image of
d
in
\ell_{c}^{2}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
,
we have the direct sum (but not necessarily orthogonal) decomposition

\ell_{c}^{2}(G,{\mathbb{R}})=H^{1}\oplus\im d.

In particular, for every
\omega\in\ell_{c}^{2}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
, there exists a unique
u\in H^{1}
having some
f\in C^{0}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
such that
\omega=u+df
.
Moreover,
\chi_{c}(\omega)=\chi_{c}(u)
.

Proof.

Note that
\im d
is a finite dimensional closed subspace in
\ell_{c}^{2}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
.
Furthermore,
H^{1}
is a closed subspace in
\ell_{c}^{2}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
since
d^{\ast}\omega_{n}(x)\to 0
for each
x\in V(G)
and
\chi_{c}(\omega_{n})\to 0
if a sequence
\omega_{n}
tends to
0
in
\ell_{c}^{2}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.

Let
\omega\in H^{1}\cap\im d
.
Since
\omega\in\im d
,
there exists
f\in C^{0}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
such that
\omega=df
.
We have
\chi_{c}(df)=0
.
Indeed,

\displaystyle\chi_{c}(df)=\sum_{e\in E(G)}\left(f(te)-f(oe)\right)c(e)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in V(G)}\sum_{e:te=x}f(te)c(e)-\sum_{x\in V(G)}f(x)\pi(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\in V(G)}f(x)\left(\sum_{e:te=x}\pi(oe)p(e)-\pi(x)\right)=0.

(2.1)

In the above, the last equality holds since
\pi
is the stationary distribution.
Therefore, since
\omega=df\in H^{1}
,
we have

0=d^{\ast}df+\chi_{c}(df)=(I-P)f.

Noting that
P
defines an irreducible Markov chain on
V(G)
shows that
f
is constant,
whence
\omega=df=0
.
Hence
H^{1}\cap\im d=\{0\}
.

Let us show that
\ell_{c}^{2}(G,{\mathbb{R}})=H^{1}+\im d
.
We will show that for every
\omega\in\ell_{c}^{2}(G,{\mathbb{R}})

there exists some
f\in C^{0}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
such that

d^{\ast}\omega+\chi_{c}(\omega)-(I-P)f=0.

(2.2)

This implies the claim.
Indeed, since
\chi_{c}(df)=0
as shown before,

(I-P)f=d^{\ast}df
and

d^{\ast}(\omega-df)+\chi_{c}(\omega-df)=d^{\ast}\omega+\chi_{c}(\omega)-d^{\ast}df=0,

we have
\omega-df\in H^{1}
and
\omega=(\omega-df)+df\in H^{1}+\im d
.

Let us show (
2.2
).
Let
P^{\ast}
denote the adjoint of
P
with respect to the inner product in
C^{0}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
.
We have the orthogonal decomposition

C^{0}(G,{\mathbb{R}})=\im(I-P)\oplus\ker(I-P^{\ast})
in the finite dimensional Hilbert space.
The associated Markov chain with
P^{\ast}
(which is the reversed chain of irreducible
P
) is irreducible on
V(G)
,
and thus
\ker(I-P^{\ast})
consists of constant functions
{\mathbb{R}}{\bf 1}
.
Therefore, for all
f\in C^{0}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
,

f\in\im(I-P)\iff\left\langle f,{\bf 1}\right\rangle_{\pi}=0.

Let
\omega\in\ell_{c}^{2}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
.
By definition of
d^{\ast}
, we have

\left\langle d^{\ast}\omega+\chi_{c}(\omega),{\bf 1}\right\rangle_{\pi}=-\sum_{x\in V(G)}\sum_{e:oe=x}c(e)\omega(e)+\chi_{c}(\omega)\sum_{x\in V(G)}\pi(x)=-\chi_{c}(\omega)+\chi_{c}(\omega)=0.

This implies that
d^{\ast}\omega+\chi_{c}(\omega)\in\im(I-P)
,
and thus (
2.2
) holds.
This completes the first claim.
The second claim directly follows from the first claim.
The last claim holds since
\chi_{c}(df)=0
for all
f\in C^{0}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
by (
2.2
).
∎

Remark 2.4
.

In the proof of Lemma
2.3
, the operator
I-P
is invertible on the orthogonal complement of
{\mathbb{R}}{\bf 1}
in
C^{0}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
. In the case of Corollary
5.5
, the conductance
c
depends continuously on a real one-parameter
\rho\in(0,1]
, and the stationary distribution on
V(G)
is uniform independently of
\rho
. If
\omega
belongs to
\ell_{c}^{2}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
for all
c
in the whole parameter range, the harmonic part
u
of
\omega
depends continuously in
\rho\in(0,1]
.
Indeed, (cf. (
2.2
))

f=(I-P)^{-1}(d^{\ast}\omega+\chi_{c}(\omega))=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}P^{n}(d^{\ast}\omega+\chi_{c}(\omega))

depends continuously on
\rho\in(0,1]
, so does
u=\omega-df
.

3.
Random walks on virtually abelian groups

An infinite finitely generated group
\Gamma
is
virtually abelian
if it admits an abelian subgroup
\Lambda
of finite index.
Such a subgroup
\Lambda
is finitely generated and abelian.
Thus, passing to a subgroup if necessary,

\Lambda
is a free abelian group
{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}
for some integer
m\geq 1

since
\Gamma
is assumed to be infinite.
Passing to a subgroup further if necessary,
we may assume that
\Lambda
is a normal subgroup of
\Gamma
.
Namely, one obtains a short exact sequence of groups:

1\to\Lambda\to\Gamma\to F\to 1,

(3.1)

where
\Lambda
is isomorphic to
{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}
and
F
is a finite group.
Note that we
do not
assume that
\Gamma
has the form of semidirect product
{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}\rtimes F
, except in Lemma
3.5
and Proposition
5.7
.

Let
\Gamma=\bigsqcup_{x\in\Delta}\Lambda x
be a left coset decomposition,
where
\Delta
is a finite set of representatives containing
\id
.
The group
\Gamma
acting on itself by right translation induces an action on
\Delta
by permutations
x\mapsto x^{\gamma}
for
x\in\Delta
and
\gamma\in\Gamma
.
Further, this action induces a
cocycle

\alpha:\Delta\times\Gamma\to\Lambda,\quad(x,\gamma)\mapsto\alpha(x,\gamma),

where
x\gamma=\alpha(x,\gamma)x^{\gamma}
in the unique coset decomposition.
The right action of
\Gamma
on
\Delta
reads
x(\gamma\gamma^{\prime})=(x\gamma)\gamma^{\prime}
,
from which the
cocycle identity
follows:

\alpha(x,\gamma\gamma^{\prime})=\alpha(x,\gamma)+\alpha(x^{\gamma},\gamma^{\prime})\quad\text{for $x\in\Delta$ and $\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\in\Gamma$}.

(3.2)

The additive notation is used for group operation in abelian groups.

3.1.
Transfer and nonzero homomorphisms onto
{\mathbb{Z}}

The
transfer homomorphism

\vartheta:\Gamma\to\Lambda
is defined by

\vartheta(\gamma):=\sum_{x\in\Delta}\alpha(x,\gamma).

Lemma 3.1
.

The transfer
\vartheta:\Gamma\to\Lambda
defines a group homomorphism. It is independent of the set of representatives
\Delta
.

Proof.

By the cocycle identity (
3.2
), we have

\vartheta(\gamma\gamma^{\prime})=\sum_{x\in\Delta}\alpha(x,\gamma\gamma^{\prime})=\sum_{x\in\Delta}\alpha(x,\gamma)+\sum_{x\in\Delta}\alpha(x^{\gamma},\gamma^{\prime})=\vartheta(\gamma)+\vartheta(\gamma^{\prime})

as
x\mapsto x^{\gamma}
is a permutation of
\Delta
.
Let us show that
\vartheta
is independent of the choice of
\Delta
.
For another set of representatives
\Delta^{\prime}
,
a natural bijection
\Delta\to\Delta^{\prime}
,
x\mapsto x^{\prime}
, is
\Gamma
-equivariant since both
\Delta
and
\Delta^{\prime}
are sets of representatives on the right cosets
\Lambda\backslash\Gamma
.
For each
x
in
\Delta
,
there exists a unique
\Phi(x^{\prime})
in
\Lambda
such that
x^{\prime}=\Phi(x^{\prime})x
.
We have
x^{\prime}\gamma=\Phi(x^{\prime})x\gamma=\Phi(x^{\prime})\alpha(x,\gamma)x^{\gamma}

and, by the
\Gamma
-equivariance of
x\mapsto x^{\prime}
,

x^{\prime}\gamma=\alpha(x^{\prime},\gamma)(x^{\prime})^{\gamma}=\alpha(x^{\prime},\gamma)(x^{\gamma})^{\prime}=\alpha(x^{\prime},\gamma)\Phi\left((x^{\gamma})^{\prime}\right)x^{\gamma}.

Hence
\alpha(x,\gamma)=\Phi(x^{\prime})^{-1}\alpha(x^{\prime},\gamma)\Phi\left((x^{\gamma})^{\prime}\right)
.
Switching to additive notations and taking sums,
we obtain

\sum_{x\in\Delta}\alpha(x,\gamma)=\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\Delta^{\prime}}-\Phi(x^{\prime})+\alpha(x^{\prime},\gamma)+\Phi\left((x^{\gamma})^{\prime}\right)=\sum_{x^{\prime}\in\Delta^{\prime}}\alpha(x^{\prime},\gamma),

as required.
∎

In general, the transfer is defined for a group
\Gamma
and a finite index subgroup
\Lambda
as a group homomorphism from
\Gamma
to the abelianization
\Lambda/[\Lambda,\Lambda]

(cf.
[
7
, pp. 296-297]
, where the definition uses the left cosets instead).
However, we do not discuss the transfer in this generality.

The behavior of the transfer will play a crucial role in our discussion.
On the one hand, if the image
\im(\vartheta)\neq\{0\}
,
then
\Gamma
admits a nonzero homomorphism onto
{\mathbb{Z}}
.
On the other hand, if
\im(\vartheta)=\{0\}
,
then for every
\gamma\in\Gamma
,

\sum_{x\in\Delta}\alpha(x,\gamma)=0.

This observation will be used in the subsequent sections.

3.2.
The weighted diagram of a random walk on a virtually abelian group

Let
\mu
be a probability measure on
\Gamma
. Denote
S=\supp\mu
(not necessarily symmetric). We assume that
\mu
is
non-degenerate
, i.e. that the sub-semigroup generated by
S
is
\Gamma
itself.
We denote by
\mu_{n}
the
n
th convolution power of
\mu
and
w_{n}
the random variable of law
\mu_{n}
, i.e. the position of the random walk at time
n
.

Let
\Cay(\Gamma,S)
be the right
Cayley diagram
for
(\Gamma,S\cup S^{-1})
,
i.e. the labeled multi-digraph on the set of vertices
\Gamma
and the set of labeled directed edges from
\gamma
to
\gamma s
with label
s
for each
(\gamma,s)\in\Gamma\times(S\cup S^{-1})
.
The notation
(\gamma,s)
is used for the directed edge with label
s
originating in
\gamma
.

By left translations, every subgroup
\Lambda
of
\Gamma
acts on
\Cay(\Gamma,S)
, preserving the labeled multi-digraph structure. We write
[\gamma]:=\Lambda\gamma
for the vertex associated to a group element
\gamma
under the quotient map
\Gamma\to\Lambda\backslash\Gamma
.
Let us define the
quotient diagram

G:=\Lambda\backslash\Cay(\Gamma,S)
.
This is a labeled multi-digraph on the set of vertices
V(G)=\Lambda\backslash\Gamma
. A directed edge of
G
is defined by its original vertex
[\gamma]
and a label
s\in S\cup S^{-1}
. We denote
e=([\gamma],s)\in E(G)
, where
oe:=[\gamma]
and
te:=[\gamma s]
. The set of labeled directed edges is denoted by

E(G)=\left\{([\gamma],s)\ :\ [\gamma]\in\Lambda\backslash\Gamma,s\in S\cup S^{-1}\right\}.

Furthermore, the reversed edge of
e
has the inverse label
\overline{e}=([\gamma s],s^{-1})
.
The set
V(G)
is finite as we assume
\Lambda
has finite index in
\Gamma
. The set
E(G)
is infinite when the measure
\mu
has infinite support.

The probability measure
\mu
on
\Gamma
induces a weight on
G
given by

p(e):=\mu(s)\quad\text{for all $e=([\gamma],s)\in E(G)$}.

As
\mu
is non-degenerate, the induced Markov chain is irreducible. When
\Lambda
is a normal subgroup (which we will assume) the Markov chain induced on
G
is identified with the random walk induced in the quotient group
\Lambda\backslash\Gamma=F
. In this case the stationary measure
\pi
is the uniform measure on
V(G)
.

3.3.
Equivariant maps and potentials

Let us fix a set
\Delta\subset\Gamma
of representatives of
\Lambda\backslash\Gamma
. We identify
\Delta
with
\Lambda\backslash\Gamma=V(G)
via the quotient map
x\mapsto[x]
. Denote
x_{0}:=[\id]
. Let us also fix an identification
\Lambda={\mathbb{Z}}^{m}
. The following discussion
does
depend on the choice of
\Delta
and this identification.

Let us define

\Phi:\Gamma\to{\mathbb{Z}}^{m},\quad\gamma=vx\mapsto v,

where
\gamma=vx
is the unique coset decomposition with
v\in\Lambda={\mathbb{Z}}^{m}
and
x\in\Delta
.
Note that
\Phi
is
\Lambda
-equivariant
,
i.e.
\Phi(v\gamma)=v+\Phi(\gamma)
for all
(v,\gamma)\in\Lambda\times\Gamma
.

So for each edge
e=([\gamma],s)\in E(G)
, we may define

\Phi_{e}:=\Phi(t\widetilde{e})-\Phi(o\widetilde{e})=\Phi(\gamma s)-\Phi(\gamma)

where
\tilde{e}=(\gamma,s)
is an arbitrary lift in the Cayley diagram
\Cay(\Gamma,S)
. Observe that this is the cocycle of the
\Gamma
-action on
\Lambda\backslash\Gamma
:

\Phi_{([\gamma],s)}=\alpha([\gamma],s).

(3.3)

Indeed, let
\gamma=vx
with
v\in\Lambda={\mathbb{Z}}^{m}
and
x=[\gamma]\in\Delta
. Then
\Phi(\gamma)=v
. For
s\in\Gamma
, we have
\gamma s=vxs=v\alpha(x,s)x^{s}
, whence
\Phi(\gamma s)=v+\alpha(x,s)
.

Let us define the vector associated with
\Phi
by

\zeta_{c}:=\sum_{e\in E(G)}c(e)\Phi_{e}.

As will be clear from Theorem
4.7
, this is the drift of the process
\Phi(w_{n})
taking values in
{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}
.
For each
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
,
let

\widehat{v}(e):=\left\langle v,\Phi_{e}\right\rangle\quad\text{for each $e\in E(G)$},

where
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle
denotes the standard inner product in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.
This
\widehat{v}
defines a
1
-form on
G
as
\Phi_{\bar{e}}=-\Phi_{e}
.

Let us fix a word metric
|\cdot|
in
\Gamma
and consider a probability measure
\mu
on
\Gamma
with finite second moment (see the definition before the statement of Theorem
1.1
).

Lemma 3.2
.

If a probability measure
\mu
on
\Gamma
has finite second moment,
then the
1
-form
\widehat{v}
on the quotient diagram
G
belongs to
\ell_{c}^{2}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
for each
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.
Moreover,
\zeta_{c}=\sum_{e\in E(G)}c(e)\Phi_{e}
is absolutely convergent, and

\chi_{c}(\widehat{v})=\left\langle v,\zeta_{c}\right\rangle
for all
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.

Proof.

Let
S_{0}
be a finite symmetric set of generators defining the word norm.
Note that
\Phi:(\Gamma,|\cdot|)\to({\mathbb{R}}^{m},\|\cdot\|)
is Lipschitz, i.e.
\|\Phi(\gamma)\|\leq\kappa|\gamma|
where
\kappa:=\max_{x\in\Delta,s\in S_{0}}\|\alpha(x,s)\|
.
This follows from the cocycle identity of
\alpha(x,\gamma)
for
x\in\Delta
and
\gamma\in\Gamma
(cf. (
3.2
) and (
3.3
)).
Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{e\in E(G)}|\widehat{v}(e)|^{2}\,c(e)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{e\in E(G)}\|v\|^{2}\|\Phi_{e}\|^{2}\,c(e)=\sum_{x\in V(G)}\sum_{s\in S}\|v\|^{2}\|\Phi(s)\|^{2}\,\pi(x)\mu(s)

\displaystyle\leq\|v\|^{2}\sum_{x\in V(G)}\pi(x)\sum_{s\in S}\kappa^{2}|s|^{2}\,\mu(s)=\kappa^{2}\|v\|^{2}\sum_{s\in S}|s|^{2}\,\mu(s)<\infty.

This shows the first claim.
The second claim follows since

\sum_{e\in E(G)}\|\Phi_{e}\|\,c(e)\leq\kappa\sum_{s\in S}|s|\,\mu(s)<\infty,

and
\chi_{c}(\widehat{v})=\sum_{e\in E(G)}c(e)\langle v,\Phi_{e}\rangle=\langle v,\zeta_{c}\rangle
.
∎

We will assume that
\mu
has finite second moment in the sequel.
The following lemma, which will be used in Section
5
, indicates how the transfer
\vartheta
comes into the play (cf. Section
3.1
).
Informally speaking, if the transfer
\vartheta
has zero image, then there is no “drift”;
patterns of directions encoded by the weighted diagram are canceled out on average.
We show the lemma in a form including the case when
\im(\vartheta)\neq\{0\}
.
Note that the stationary distribution
\pi
is uniform in the case when
\Lambda\backslash\Gamma=F
is a finite group (which we assume eventually, cf. Section
3.1
).
Recall that
\Lambda
is identified with
{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, and that
S=\supp\mu
.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
(G,c)
be the weighted diagram of the
\mu
-random walk on
\Gamma
.
Let us consider the orthogonal decomposition

{\mathbb{R}}^{m}=\mathrm{span}(\im(\vartheta))\oplus\im(\vartheta)^{\perp}.

Let
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.
Denote
\widehat{v}=u+df
where
u
is the unique harmonic part in
H^{1}
. Then,

v\in\mathrm{im}(\vartheta)^{\perp}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\sum_{x\in V(G)}u(x,s)=0\quad\text{for all $s\in S$}.

In particular, if
\pi
is uniform on
V(G)
, then
\chi_{c}(\widehat{v})=\chi_{c}(u)=0
for all
v\in\im(\vartheta)^{\perp}
.

Proof.

Observe that
\sum_{x\in V(G)}df(x,s)=0
for each
s\in S
.
Indeed, in
G
, the directed edges labeled
s
form a (disjoint) union of cycles:
e_{0},\dots,e_{n-1}
such that
oe_{i}=te_{i+1}
for all
i\mod n
.
This follows since the vertex set consists of the right cosets
\Lambda\backslash\Gamma
.
The sum of
df
along each circle equals
0
,
and thus the indicated sum equals
0
. We have for all
s\in S
,

\sum_{x\in V(G)}u(x,s)=\sum_{x\in V(G)}\widehat{v}(x,s)=\sum_{x\in V(G)}\langle v,\alpha(x,s)\rangle=\langle v,\vartheta(s)\rangle.

As
S
is a generating set of
\Gamma
, the span of
\vartheta(S)
equals the span of the image of
\vartheta
. This concludes the first claim.

We have
\chi_{c}(\widehat{v})=\chi_{c}(u)
by Lemma
2.3
.
If
\pi
is uniform on
V(G)
,
then for
v\in\im(\vartheta)^{\perp}
,

\chi_{c}(u)=\sum_{e\in E(G)}c(e)u(e)=\sum_{s\in S}\pi(x)\mu(s)\sum_{x\in V(G)}u(x,s)=0,

where we have used the first claim in the last equality.
∎

3.4.
The normalized transfer homomorphism as a projection

The results in this subsection will be used in Section
5
.

The short exact sequence (
3.1
) provides an action of
F
on
\Lambda
by conjugacy.
Indeed, if
\gamma=vx
with
v\in\Lambda
, then for every
\lambda\in\Lambda
, as
\Lambda
is normal and abelian, we have

\gamma\lambda\gamma^{-1}=vx\lambda x^{-1}v^{-1}=x\lambda x^{-1}\in\Lambda.

This action of
F
is by automorphisms of the torsion free abelian
\Lambda
, isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}^{m}
for some
m\geq 1
. Under this identification,
F
is isomorphic to a finite subgroup of
{\rm GL}(m,\mathbb{Z})
, acting on
\mathbb{R}^{m}
where
{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}
is identified with
\Lambda
. It follows that we can endow
\mathbb{R}^{m}
with a scalar product preserved by the
F
-action. We get the adjoint representation
\mathrm{Ad}:F\to{\rm O}(m,\mathbb{R})
in the orthogonal group.

We define the
normalized transfer homomorphism

{\underline{\vartheta}}:{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
by

{\underline{\vartheta}}(v):=\frac{1}{\#F}\sum_{f\in F}\mathrm{Ad}(f)v.

Lemma 3.4
.

The normalised transfer homomorphism
{\underline{\vartheta}}
is the orthogonal projection of
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
onto the span of the image of the transfer homomorphism
\vartheta
. In particular,
\mathrm{im}({\underline{\vartheta}})=\mathrm{span}(\mathrm{im}(\vartheta))
.

Proof.

The scalar product is chosen so that
\mathrm{Ad}(F)
is orthogonal, so
{\underline{\vartheta}}
is self-adjoint:

\left\langle{\underline{\vartheta}}(v),u\right\rangle=\frac{1}{\#F}\sum_{f\in F}\langle\mathrm{Ad}(f)v,u\rangle=\frac{1}{\#F}\sum_{f\in F}\langle v,\mathrm{Ad}(f^{-1})u\rangle=\left\langle v,{\underline{\vartheta}}(u)\right\rangle\quad\textrm{for }v,u\in\mathbb{R}^{m}.

Moreover, as
f^{\prime}\mapsto ff^{\prime}
is a bijection of
F
, we have

\displaystyle{\underline{\vartheta}}^{2}(v)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(\#F)^{2}}\sum_{f,f^{\prime}\in F}\mathrm{Ad}(f)\mathrm{Ad}(f^{\prime})v=\frac{1}{\#F}\sum_{f\in F}\frac{1}{\#F}\sum_{f^{\prime}\in F}\mathrm{Ad}(ff^{\prime})v={\underline{\vartheta}}(v),

so
{\underline{\vartheta}}
is a projection. Finally, as

\alpha(x,v)=xvx^{-1}=\mathrm{Ad}(\Lambda x)v\quad\textrm{for }x\in\Delta,v\in\Lambda,

(3.4)

we get
\vartheta(v)=\#F{\underline{\vartheta}}(v)
.
∎

Recall that for
v\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, the
1
form
\widehat{v}(e):=\left\langle v,\Phi_{e}\right\rangle=\left\langle v,\alpha(x,s)\right\rangle
, with
e=(x,s)
,
decomposes as
\widehat{v}([x],s)=u(x,s)+df(x,s)
by Lemma
2.3
.

In the next lemma, we assume that the virtually abelian group
\Gamma
is a semi-direct product
\Gamma=\Lambda\rtimes F
.

Lemma 3.5
.

Assume that
\Gamma=\Lambda\rtimes F
is a semi-direct product and that
\Delta
is a subgroup isomorphic to
F
. Let
v\in\mathrm{im}({\underline{\vartheta}})
. Then for each
s\in\Gamma
, the harmonic part
u(x,s)
of the
1
-form
\widehat{v}
does not depend on
x
.

Proof.

Write
s=\lambda y
with
\lambda\in\Lambda
and
y\in\Delta
. By the cocycle relation we have

\widehat{v}(x,s)=\langle v,\alpha(x,\lambda y)\rangle=\langle v,\alpha(x,\lambda)\rangle+\langle v,\alpha(x,y)\rangle,

as
x^{\lambda}=x
and
x\lambda=(x\lambda x^{-1})x
, where we note that
\Lambda
is a normal subgroup in
\Gamma
.
On the one hand, using Lemma
3.4
and (
3.4
), we have

\displaystyle\langle v,\alpha(x,\lambda)\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle{\underline{\vartheta}}(v),\mathrm{Ad}(\Lambda x)\lambda\rangle=\langle v,{\underline{\vartheta}}\mathrm{Ad}(\Lambda x)\lambda\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle v,\frac{1}{\#F}\sum_{f\in F}\mathrm{Ad}(f)\mathrm{Ad}(\Lambda x)\lambda\right\rangle=\langle v,{\underline{\vartheta}}(\lambda)\rangle,

independent of
x
.
On the other hand, as
\Gamma
is a semi-direct product with
\Delta
isomorphic to
F
, we have
\alpha(x,y)=\Phi(xy)-\Phi(x)=0
, because
xy\in\Delta
and
\Phi(x)=0
for all
x\in\Delta
by definition.
∎

4.
Local CLTs on virtually abelian groups

We establish local CLTs for random walks on finitely generated infinite virtually abelian groups.
In this section, we keep the notations for weighted diagrams
(G,c)
associated to random walks on virtually abelian groups in the previous Section
3
.
In Section
4.1
,
we define transfer operators, discuss their perturbations on weighted diagrams,
and use them to establish the variance formula in terms of harmonic forms in the local CLT.
In Section
4.2
,
we introduce characteristic functions to apply the Fourier analysis and discuss the periodicity problem arising in Markov chains.
In Section
4.4
,
we establish the local CLT based on the transfer operator method.

4.1.
Transfer operators and their perturbations

For each
\omega\in C^{1}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
,
the
transfer operator
on
C^{0}(G,{\mathbb{C}})
is defined by

{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega}f(x):=\sum_{e:oe=x}p(e)e^{2\pi i\omega(e)}f(te)\quad\text{for $x\in V(G)$}.

In the definition,
i=\sqrt{-1}
and
\pi
denotes the half of the circumference of the unit circle.
Note that the operator norm of
{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega}
is uniformly bounded
\|{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega}\|\leq 1
for all
\omega\in C^{1}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
.
The map
\omega\mapsto{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega}
defines a twice continuously differentiable (
C^{2}
-)map from
\ell_{c}^{2}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
to the complex Banach space of bounded operators on
C^{0}(G,{\mathbb{C}})
, where the space of operators is a finite dimensional space of matrices.
Since
{\mathcal{L}}_{0}=P
and
P
has a simple eigenvalue
1
,
we have an open neighborhood
U
of
0
in
\ell_{c}^{2}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
and a
C^{2}
-function
\lambda:U\to{\mathbb{R}}
such that
{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega}
has a simple eigenvalue
\lambda(\omega)
for all
\omega\in U
and
\lambda(0)
=1.
This follows from the implicit function theorem in the Hilbert space applied to the characteristic polynomial.
Furthermore, the corresponding eigenfunction
f_{\omega}
can be chosen so that
\omega\mapsto f_{\omega}
is a
C^{2}
-map from
U
to
C^{0}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
and that
f_{\omega}
is normalized, i.e.
\left\langle f_{\omega},{\bf 1}\right\rangle_{\pi}=1
for all
\omega\in U
.
In summary,
we have
\lambda(0)=1
,
f_{0}={\bf 1}
,

{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega}f_{\omega}=\lambda(\omega)f_{\omega}\quad\text{and}\quad\left\langle f_{\omega},{\bf 1}\right\rangle_{\pi}=1\quad\text{for all $\omega\in U$}.

Moreover,
\lambda(\omega)
and
f_{\omega}
are
C^{2}
-maps in
\omega\in U
respectively. Replacing
U
with a smaller open neighborhood of
0
if necessary, we take a branch of the logarithm and define

\beta(\omega):=\log\lambda(\omega)\quad\text{for $\omega\in U$ such that $\beta(0)=0$}.

The following observation is crucial to our discussion:
If
\omega\in\ell_{c}^{2}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
and
\varphi\in C^{0}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
,
then
\omega+d\varphi\in\ell_{c}^{2}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
and
{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega+d\varphi}=e^{-2\pi i\varphi}{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega}e^{2\pi i\varphi}
where
e^{\varphi}f(x):=e^{\varphi(x)}f(x)
for
x\in V(G)
.
Therefore for all
\omega\in U
and all
\varphi\in C^{0}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
,

\lambda(\omega+d\varphi)=\lambda(\omega).

This implies that
\lambda(\omega)
and thus
\beta(\omega)
depend only on the harmonic part of
\omega
in the decomposition (cf. Lemma
2.3
).

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
r,r_{i}
,
i=1,2
, be real parameters.

(1)

For every
1
-form
\omega\in\ell_{c}^{2}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
,

\frac{d}{dr}\Big|_{r=0}\beta(r\omega)=2\pi i\chi_{c}(\omega).

(4.1)

(2)

For every harmonic
1
-form
u
in
\ell_{c}^{2}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
,

\frac{d}{dr}\Big|_{r=0}f_{ru}(x)=0\quad\text{for all $x\in V(G)$}.

(4.2)

(3)

For all harmonic
1
-forms
u_{i}
,
i=1,2
, in
\ell_{c}^{2}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r_{1}\partial r_{2}}

\displaystyle\Big|_{(r_{1},r_{2})=(0,0)}\beta(r_{1}u_{1}+r_{2}u_{2})

\displaystyle=-4\pi^{2}\left(\sum_{e\in E(G)}u_{1}(e)u_{2}(e)c(e)-\sum_{e\in E(G)}u_{1}(e)c(e)\sum_{e\in E(G)}u_{2}(e)c(e)\right)

(4.3)

(4)

If
c(e)=c(\overline{e})
for all
e\in E(G)
,
then all (existing) odd time derivatives of
\beta
at
0
vanish.

Proof.

For each
1
-form
\omega\in U
,
we have
r\omega\in U
for all small enough
r
.
The normalization
\left\langle f_{r\omega},{\bf 1}\right\rangle_{\pi}=1
implies that

\left\langle\frac{d}{dr}\Big|_{r=0}f_{r\omega},{\bf 1}\right\rangle_{\pi}=0.

(4.4)

Furthermore, since
P^{\ast}{\bf 1}={\bf 1}
,
this (
4.4
) shows that

\left\langle P\left(\frac{d}{dr}\Big|_{r=0}f_{r\omega}\right),{\bf 1}\right\rangle_{\pi}=\left\langle\frac{d}{dr}\Big|_{r=0}f_{r\omega},P^{\ast}{\bf 1}\right\rangle_{\pi}=\left\langle\frac{d}{dr}\Big|_{r=0}f_{r\omega},{\bf 1}\right\rangle_{\pi}=0.

(4.5)

Note that identities analogous to (
4.4
) and (
4.5
) also hold for second derivatives (and in fact for any order derivatives if they exist) by the normalization condition on
f_{r\omega}
.

Differentiating
{\mathcal{L}}_{r\omega}f_{r\omega}=e^{\beta(r\omega)}f_{r\omega}
at
r=0
, we obtain for each
x\in V(G)
,

\sum_{e:oe=x}\left(p(e)(2\pi i\omega(e))+p(e)\frac{d}{dr}\Big|_{r=0}f_{r\omega}(te)\right)=\frac{d}{dr}\Big|_{r=0}\beta(r\omega)+\frac{d}{dr}\Big|_{r=0}f_{r\omega}(x),

(4.6)

where we have used
f_{0}={\bf 1}
and
\beta(0)=0
.
Taking the inner products with
{\bf 1}
in both sides yields by (
4.4
) and (
4.5
),

\sum_{x\in V(G)}\pi(x)\sum_{e:oe=x}p(e)(2\pi i\omega(e))=\frac{d}{dr}\Big|_{r=0}\beta(r\omega).

The left hand side equals
2\pi i\chi_{c}(\omega)
, and this shows (
4.1
).

Let us take a harmonic
1
-form
\omega=u
in
\ell_{c}^{2}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
.
By (
4.6
) and (
4.1
),
we have

-2\pi id^{\ast}u(x)+P\left(\frac{d}{dr}\Big|_{r=0}f_{ru}\right)(x)=2\pi i\chi_{c}(u)+\frac{d}{dr}\Big|_{r=0}f_{ru}(x).

Since
u
is a harmonic
1
-form, i.e.
d^{\ast}u+\chi_{c}(u)=0
,
the above equation implies that

P\left(\frac{d}{dr}\Big|_{r=0}f_{ru}\right)(x)=\frac{d}{dr}\Big|_{r=0}f_{ru}(x)\quad\text{for each $x\in V(G)$}.

Furthermore, since
P
is irreducible,
(d/dr)|_{r=0}f_{ru}
is a constant, which has to be
0
by (
4.4
).
This shows (
4.2
).

For all small enough
r_{i}
for
i=1,2
,
letting
\beta_{r_{1},r_{2}}:=\beta(r_{1}u_{1}+r_{2}u_{2})
and
f_{r_{1},r_{2}}:=f_{r_{1}u_{1}+r_{2}u_{2}}
for harmonic
1
-forms
u_{i}
,
we have for each
x\in V(G)
,

{\mathcal{L}}_{r_{1}u_{1}+r_{2}u_{2}}f_{r_{1},r_{2}}(x)=e^{\beta_{r_{1},r_{2}}}f_{r_{1},r_{2}}(x).

Computing the second derivatives at
(r_{1},r_{2})=(0,0)
yields by (
4.2
),

\displaystyle-4\pi^{2}\sum_{e\in E_{x}}p(e)u_{1}(e)u_{2}(e)+\sum_{e:oe=x}p(e)\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r_{1}\partial r_{2}}\Big|_{(0,0)}f_{r_{1},r_{2}}(x)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r_{1}\partial r_{2}}\Big|_{(0,0)}\beta_{r_{1},r_{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r_{1}\partial r_{2}}\Big|_{(0,0)}f_{r_{1},r_{2}}(x)+\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial r_{1}}\Big|_{(0,0)}\beta_{r_{1},r_{2}}\right)\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial r_{2}}\Big|_{(0,0)}\beta_{r_{1},r_{2}}\right).

Taking the inner products with
{\bf 1}
in both sides and using (
4.1
) yields

-4\pi^{2}\left(\sum_{e\in E(G)}c(e)u_{1}(e)u_{2}(e)-\sum_{e\in E(G)}c(e)u_{1}(e)\sum_{e\in E(G)}c(e)u_{2}(e)\right)=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r_{1}\partial r_{2}}\Big|_{(r_{1},r_{2})=(0,0)}\beta_{r_{1},r_{2}}.

To deduce the equality, we have used the analogous identities for second derivatives to (
4.4
) and (
4.5
).
This concludes (
(3)
).

Furthermore, if
c(e)=c(\overline{e})
for all
e\in E(G)
,
then
{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega}
is self-adjoint for each
1
-form
\omega
.
Hence
\lambda(\omega)
is real,
and since
\overline{{\mathcal{L}}_{\omega}f_{\omega}}={\mathcal{L}}_{-\omega}\overline{f_{\omega}}
,
we have
\lambda(-\omega)=\overline{\lambda(\omega)}=\lambda(\omega)
for all
\omega\in U
.
This implies that all odd time derivatives of
\beta
at
0
vanish, concluding the last claim.
∎

Let us consider the transfer operators
{\mathcal{L}}_{\widehat{v}}
with
potentials

\widehat{v}
associated with
\Phi
for
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.
For brevity,
let us denote
\lambda(v):=\lambda(\widehat{v})
and
\beta(v):=\beta(\widehat{v})

for all
v
near
0
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let us denote the Hessian of
\beta
at
0
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
by

\Hess_{0}\beta:=\left(\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial r_{i}\partial r_{j}}\Big|_{(r_{1},\dots,r_{m})=(0,\dots,0)}\beta(r_{1},\dots,r_{m})\right)_{i,j=1,\dots,m}.

The
\Hess_{0}\beta
is non-degenerate and negative definite, and satisfies

\left\langle v_{1},\Hess_{0}\beta\,v_{2}\right\rangle=-4\pi^{2}\left(\sum_{e\in E(G)}u_{1}(e)u_{2}(e)c(e)-\sum_{e\in E(G)}c(e)u_{1}(e)\sum_{e\in E(G)}c(e)u_{2}(e)\right)

(4.7)

where
u_{i}
is the unique harmonic part of
\widehat{v}_{i}
for all
v_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
,
i=1,2
.

Proof.

For all
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
,
the associated
1
-form
\widehat{v}
is in
\ell_{c}^{2}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
by Lemma
3.2
,
and thus
\widehat{v}
has a unique harmonic part
u
by Lemma
2.3
.
Lemma
4.1
(
(3)
) implies (
4.7
).
Suppose that
\left\langle v,\Hess_{0}\beta\,v\right\rangle=0
.
By (
4.7
), we have

0=\sum_{e\in E(G)}u(e)^{2}c(e)-\Bigg(\sum_{e\in E(G)}u(e)c(e)\Bigg)^{2}=\sum_{e\in E(G)}c(e)\Bigg(u(e)-\sum_{e\in E(G)}c(e)u(e)\Bigg)^{2}.

The unique harmonic part
u
of
\widehat{v}
is constant, hence zero, because it is a
1
-form; and thus
\widehat{v}=df
for some
f\in C^{0}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
by Lemma
2.3
.
Let us consider a directed edge path in
\Cay(\Gamma,S)
from
\id
to
w\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}=\Lambda
in
\Gamma
and its projection
(e_{1},\dots,e_{n})
in
E(G)
.
Since the projection is a loop,
we have (cf. (
4.8
) below for details about the fourth equality)

0=\sum_{i=1}^{n}df(e_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{v}(e_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle v,\Phi_{e_{i}}\right\rangle=\left\langle v,w\right\rangle.

Running
w
over a basis in
{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}
yields
v=0
.
Therefore
\Hess_{0}\beta
is non-degenerate.
The formula (
4.7
) shows that
\Hess_{0}\beta
is negative definite.
∎

4.2.
Characteristic functions

Let us define
{\bf 1}_{x}:V(G)\to{\mathbb{R}}
by
{\bf 1}_{x}(y)=1
for
y=x
and
0
for
y\neq x
.
For each positive integer
n
and for all
x\in V(G)
, we can compute explicitly iterates of the perturbed transfer operator

{\mathcal{L}}_{\widehat{v}}^{n}{\bf 1}_{x}(x_{0})=\sum p(e_{1})\cdots p(e_{n})\exp\left(2\pi i(\widehat{v}(e_{1})+\cdots+\widehat{v}(e_{n}))\right).

The above summation runs over all paths
(e_{1},\dots,e_{n})
from
x_{0}
to
x
,
oe_{1}=x_{0}
,
te_{i}=oe_{i+1}
for
i=1,\dots,n-1
and
te_{n}=x
.
A lift of
(e_{1},\dots,e_{n})
is defined by a path
(\widetilde{e}_{1},\dots,\widetilde{e}_{n})
in
\Cay(\Gamma,S)
with
t\widetilde{e}_{i}=o\widetilde{e}_{i+1}
for
i=1,\dots,n-1

and
\widetilde{e}_{i}\mapsto e_{i}
via the covering map
\Cay(\Gamma,S)\to\Lambda\backslash\Cay(\Gamma,S)
.
For such a lift, we have

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\widehat{v}(e_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle v,\Phi_{e_{i}}\right\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\langle v,\Phi(t\widetilde{e}_{i})-\Phi(o\widetilde{e}_{i})\right\rangle=\left\langle v,\Phi(t\widetilde{e}_{n})-\Phi(o\widetilde{e}_{1})\right\rangle.

(4.8)

Taking all the lifts starting from
\id
,
we obtain by
o\widetilde{e}_{1}=\id
and
\Phi(\id)=0
,

{\mathcal{L}}_{\widehat{v}}^{n}{\bf 1}_{x}(x_{0})=\sum\mu(s_{1})\cdots\mu(s_{n})\exp\left(2\pi i\left\langle v,\Phi(t\widetilde{e}_{n})\right\rangle\right)=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma,[\gamma]=x}\mu_{n}(\gamma)\exp\left(2\pi i\left\langle v,\Phi(\gamma)\right\rangle\right),

where
s_{i}
are the labels of each path read along.
The second equality holds since the map assigning to a path from
x_{0}
to
x
a lift starting from
\id
of length
n
terminating at
\gamma
with
[\gamma]=x
is a bijection.
Note that
{\mathcal{L}}_{0}^{n}{\bf 1}_{x}(x_{0})
is simply the probability that the random walk on
\Gamma
started in
\id
projects to
x\in V(G)
at time
n
.

We consider the distribution
\Phi_{\ast}\mu_{n}:=\mu_{n}\circ\Phi^{-1}
of
\Phi(w_{n})
, which is the image by
\Phi
into
\Lambda={\mathbb{Z}}^{m}
of the
\mu
-random walk on
\Gamma
at time
n
. Its
characteristic function
is given by

\varphi_{\Phi_{\ast}\mu_{n}}(v):=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\mu_{n}(\gamma)\exp\left(2\pi i\langle v,\Phi(\gamma)\rangle\right)=\sum_{x\in V(G)}{\mathcal{L}}_{\widehat{v}}^{n}{\bf 1}_{x}(x_{0}).

Since
\Phi(\Gamma)={\mathbb{Z}}^{m}
,
the function
\varphi_{\Phi_{\ast}\mu_{n}}
is defined on
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}/{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}
,
which will also be identified with a fundamental domain

D:=\left[-\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right)^{m}\quad\text{in ${\mathbb{R}}^{m}$}.

The Fourier inversion formula yields

(\Phi_{\ast}\mu_{n})(\lambda)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}/{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}}\sum_{x\in V(G)}{\mathcal{L}}_{\widehat{v}}^{n}{\bf 1}_{x}(x_{0})e^{-2\pi i\langle v,\lambda\rangle}dv\quad\text{for all $\lambda\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}$}.

It also gives the following fact.

Fact 4.3
.

For each
\gamma\in\Gamma
,

\mu_{n}(\gamma)=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}/{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}}{\mathcal{L}}^{n}_{\widehat{v}}{\bf 1}_{[\gamma]}(x_{0})e^{-2\pi i\langle v,\Phi(\gamma)\rangle}dv.

(4.9)

Proof.

Let us compute for each
\gamma_{0}\in\Gamma
,

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}/{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}}{\mathcal{L}}^{n}_{\widehat{v}}{\bf 1}_{x}(x_{0})e^{-2\pi i\langle v,\Phi(\gamma_{0})\rangle}dv=\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma,[\gamma]=x}\mu_{n}(\gamma)\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}/{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}}e^{2\pi i\langle v,\Phi(\gamma)-\Phi(\gamma_{0})\rangle}dv.

The series is absolutely convergent as
\mu_{n}
is a probability measure. The integral term vanishes when
\Phi(\gamma)-\Phi(\gamma_{0})\neq 0
as
\Phi
takes integer values, and otherwise it takes value one. If
x=[\gamma_{0}]
, we get
\mu_{n}(\gamma_{0})
as required. Otherwise all terms are zero.
∎

We define the
period

q
of a probability measure
\mu
on
\Gamma
by the greatest common divisor of all possible lengths of nontrivial directed loops at
\id
in
\Cay(\Gamma,S)
where
S=\supp\mu
.
We say that
\mu
is
aperiodic
if the period of
\mu
is
1
.
This is equivalent to say that there exists
N
such that
\mu_{n}(\id)>0
for every
n\geq N
.
For example,
\mu
is aperiodic if
\mu(\id)>0
.

For
\delta>0
,
let

D_{\delta}:=\Bigl\{v=(v_{1},\dots,v_{m})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\ :\ |v_{i}|<\delta,\ i=1,\dots,m\Bigr\}.

Lemma 4.4
.

If
\mu
is aperiodic,
then for each
\delta>0
,
there exist constants
c_{\delta},C_{\delta}>0
such that for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and for all
x\in V(G)
,

\sup_{v\in D\setminus D_{\delta}}|{\mathcal{L}}_{\widehat{v}}^{n}{\bf 1}_{x}(x_{0})|\leq C_{\delta}\exp\left(-c_{\delta}n\right).

Proof.

Note that
v\mapsto{\mathcal{L}}_{\widehat{v}}
is continuous and that
\|{\mathcal{L}}_{\widehat{v}}\|:=\max_{\|f\|=1}\|{\mathcal{L}}_{\widehat{v}}f\|
depends continuously on
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
,
where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the induced norm in
C^{0}(G,{\mathbb{C}})
.
The spectral radius is obtained by
{\rm rad}(v)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\|{\mathcal{L}}_{\widehat{v}}^{n}\|^{1/n}
for each
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
,
where the limit exists by the sub-multiplicativity of
n\mapsto\|{\mathcal{L}}_{\widehat{v}}^{n}\|
.
This shows that
v\mapsto{\rm rad}(v)
is upper semi-continuous,
in particular,
{\rm rad}(v)
attains its maximum on each compact set.

If
\mu
is aperiodic,
then
{\rm rad}(v)<1
for
v\in D\setminus\{0\}
.
Indeed, let
\lambda(v)
be the maximal eigenvalue in absolute value and
f_{v}
be a corresponding (nonzero) eigenfunction.
We have
|\lambda(v)|={\rm rad}(v)
and
{\mathcal{L}}_{\widehat{v}}f_{v}=\lambda(v)f_{v}
by definition.
If
|\lambda(v)|=1
,
then
|f_{v}|\leq P|f_{v}|
.
Since
P
is irreducible on the finite set
V(G)
,
the function
|f_{v}|
is a nonzero constant.
Thus,
\lambda(v)f_{v}(x)
and
e^{2\pi i\left\langle v,\Phi_{e}\right\rangle}f_{v}(te)
lie on a common circle in the complex plane for all
x\in V(G)
and all
e\in E_{x}
.
Since
\lambda(v)f_{v}(x)=\sum_{e:oe=x}p(e)e^{2\pi i\left\langle v,\Phi_{e}\right\rangle}f_{v}(te)
,
we have

\lambda(v)f_{v}(x)=e^{2\pi i\left\langle v,\Phi_{e}\right\rangle}f_{v}(te)\quad\text{for all $x\in V(G)$ and all $e$ with $oe=x$}.

If
\mu
is aperiodic,
then for each
w\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}=\Lambda

there exists a directed path from
\id
to
w
in
\Cay(\Gamma,S)
of all large enough length
n
.
Projecting the path to
G
, we have
\Phi(w)=w
since we assume that
\id\in\Delta
,
and
\lambda(v)^{n}f_{v}(x_{0})=e^{2\pi i\left\langle v,w\right\rangle}f_{v}(x_{0})
.
This yields
\lambda(v)^{n}=\lambda(v)^{n+1}
by taking paths of length
n
and
n+1
from
\id
to
w
, and thus
\lambda(v)=1
and
\left\langle v,w\right\rangle\in{\mathbb{Z}}
since
|f_{v}|
is a nonzero constant.
Taking
w
over a basis of
{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}
,
we obtain
v=0
since
v\in D
.
This shows that if
|\lambda(v)|=1
, then
v=0
.

Therefore, if
\mu
is aperiodic,
then for each
\delta>0
, there exists a constant
c_{\delta}>0
such that
{\rm rad}(v)\leq e^{-c_{\delta}}
for all
v\in D\setminus D_{\delta}
.
Furthermore, for each
v\in D\setminus D_{\delta}
,
there exists
N=N_{v}
such that

\|{\mathcal{L}}_{\widehat{v}}^{N}\|^{1/N}\leq e^{-c_{\delta}/2}
.
Since
v\mapsto{\mathcal{L}}_{\widehat{v}}
is continuous,
there exists an open set
U_{v}
in
D\setminus D_{\delta}
containing
v
such that
\|{\mathcal{L}}_{\widehat{w}}^{N}\|^{1/N}\leq e^{-c_{\delta}/4}
for every
w\in U_{v}
.
For every
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
letting
n=kN+l
for some
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and some
0\leq l<N
,
we have

\|{\mathcal{L}}_{\widehat{w}}^{n}\|\leq\|{\mathcal{L}}_{\widehat{w}}\|^{l}\cdot\|{\mathcal{L}}_{\widehat{w}}^{N}\|^{k}\leq\exp\left(-\frac{c_{\delta}}{4}kN\right)=e^{c_{\delta}l/4}\exp\left(-\frac{c_{\delta}}{4}n\right)\leq e^{c_{\delta}N/4}\exp\left(-\frac{c_{\delta}}{4}n\right).

In the second inequality, we have used
\|{\mathcal{L}}_{\widehat{w}}\|\leq 1
.
By compactness of
D\setminus D_{\delta}
under the identification
D={\mathbb{R}}^{m}/{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}
,
we have finitely many open sets
U_{v}
that cover
D\setminus D_{\delta}
.
Hence there exist constants
c_{\delta}^{\prime},C_{\delta}>0
such that

\|{\mathcal{L}}_{\widehat{w}}^{n}\|\leq C_{\delta}\exp\left(-c_{\delta}^{\prime}n\right)
for all
w\in D\setminus D_{\delta}
and for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
We have a constant
C_{\delta}^{\prime}
such that

|{\mathcal{L}}_{\widehat{v}}^{n}{\bf 1}_{x}(x_{0})|\leq\|{\mathcal{L}}_{\widehat{v}}^{n}\|\sqrt{\pi(x)/\pi(x_{0})}\leq C_{\delta}^{\prime}\exp(-c_{\delta}^{\prime}n)

for all
v\in D\setminus D_{\delta}
, for all
x\in V(G)
and all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
, showing the claim.
∎

4.3.
A concentration inequality

Recall the
\Lambda
-equivariant map
\Phi:\Gamma\to{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}
and
\zeta_{c}=\sum_{e\in E(G)}c(e)\Phi_{e}
(cf. Section
3.3
). The image under
\Phi
of the random walk at time
n
is concentrated around
n\zeta_{c}
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
\mu
be a probability measure on
\Gamma
of finite second moment,
and
\{w_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be a
\mu
-random walk starting from
\id
on
\Gamma
.
There exists a constant
C>0
such that for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and all real
r>0
,

\Prob\Bigl(\|\Phi(w_{n})-n\zeta_{c}\|\geq r\Bigr)\leq\frac{Cn}{r^{2}}.

Proof.

For each
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
,
we have
\widehat{v}\in\ell_{c}^{2}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
by Lemma
3.2
.
By Lemma
2.3
,
we have
\widehat{v}=u+df
,
where
u
is the unique harmonic part of
\widehat{v}
and
f\in C^{0}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
.
The definition of the harmonic part, the last claim in Lemma
2.3
and Lemma
3.2
imply that

0=d^{\ast}u+\chi_{c}(u)=d^{\ast}u+\chi_{c}(\widehat{v})=d^{\ast}u+\left\langle v,\zeta_{c}\right\rangle.

Taking lifts
\widetilde{v}
,
\widetilde{u}
and
\widetilde{f}
of
\widehat{v}
,
u
and
f
respectively,
via
\Cay(\Gamma,S)\to\Lambda\backslash\Cay(\Gamma,S)
,
we obtain

\widetilde{v}=\widetilde{u}+d\widetilde{f}.

Abusing notations, we use
1
-forms and differential
d
defined on
G
also on
\Cay(\Gamma,S)
.
For each
\gamma\in\Gamma
, we have

-\sum_{s\in S}\widetilde{u}(\gamma,s)\mu(s)+\left\langle v,\zeta_{c}\right\rangle=0.

For a
\mu
-random walk
w_{n}=\gamma_{1}\cdots\gamma_{n}
starting from
\id
on
\Gamma
,
for
n\geq 1
,
let

\widetilde{u}_{n}:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigl(\widetilde{u}(w_{i-1},\gamma_{i})-\left\langle v,\zeta_{c}\right\rangle\Bigr)\quad\text{and}\quad\widetilde{u}_{0}:=0.

This defines a martingale
\{\widetilde{u}_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
with respect to the natural filtration
\{{\mathcal{F}}_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
associated with
\{w_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
,
i.e.,
\E[\widetilde{u}_{n+1}\mid{\mathcal{F}}_{n}]=\widetilde{u}_{n}
almost surely for each
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
Furthermore, the martingale difference
\widetilde{u}_{n+1}-\widetilde{u}_{n}
is square integrable since
u\in\ell_{c}^{2}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
and
\widetilde{u}
is a lift of
u
.
Note that for each
\gamma\in\Gamma
and for each path
e_{1},\dots,e_{n}
in
\Cay(\Gamma,S)
with
oe_{1}=\id
,
te_{i}=oe_{i+1}
for
i=1,\dots,n-1
and
te_{n}=\gamma
,

\left\langle v,\Phi(\gamma)-\Phi(\id)-n\zeta_{c}\right\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigl(\widetilde{v}(e_{i})-\left\langle v,\zeta_{c}\right\rangle\Bigr)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Bigl(\widetilde{u}(e_{i})+d\widetilde{f}(e_{i})-\left\langle v,\zeta_{c}\right\rangle\Bigr).

The last term equals

\widetilde{u}_{n}+\widetilde{f}(w_{n})-\widetilde{f}(\id)
for a random walk path.
This implies that since
\Phi(\id)=0
, for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

\left\langle v,\Phi(w_{n})-n\zeta_{c}\right\rangle=\widetilde{u}_{n}+\widetilde{f}(w_{n})-\widetilde{f}(\id).

(4.10)

Moreover,
\widetilde{f}
is uniformly bounded, i.e.,
\|\widetilde{f}\|_{\infty}=\|f\|_{\infty}<\infty
since
\widetilde{f}
is a lift of
f
.

Since
\{\widetilde{u}_{n}\}_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is a martingale with square integrable differences relative to the natural filtration,
by the Chebyshev inequality,
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and for all real
r>0
,

\Prob\Bigl(|\widetilde{u}_{n}|\geq r\Bigr)\leq\frac{\E|\widetilde{u}_{n}|^{2}}{r^{2}}\leq\frac{Cn}{r^{2}},\quad\text{where $C:=\max_{x\in V(G)}\sum_{s\in S}|u(x,s)-\left\langle v,\zeta_{c}\right\rangle|^{2}\,\mu(s)$}.

By (
4.10
),
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and all real
r>0
,

\Prob\Bigl(|\left\langle v,\Phi(w_{n})-n\zeta_{c}\right\rangle|\geq r+2\|f\|_{\infty}\Bigr)\leq\Prob\Bigl(|\widetilde{u}_{n}|\geq r\Bigr)\leq\frac{Cn}{r^{2}}.

Applying this to each
v
in the standard basis in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
and taking a union bound show that for a constant
C^{\prime}>0
, for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and for all real
r>0
,

\Prob\Bigl(\|\Phi(w_{n})-n\zeta_{c}\|\geq r\Bigr)\leq\frac{C^{\prime}n}{r^{2}}.

This shows the claim.
∎

4.4.
Local CLTs

We are now ready to state and prove local CLTs on virtually abelian groups.

For a non-degenerate positive definite (covariance) matrix
\Sigma
of size
m
,
we consider the multivariate normal distribution of variance
\Sigma
:

\xi_{\Sigma}(v):=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{\frac{m}{2}}\sqrt{\det\Sigma}}\exp\Bigl(-\frac{1}{2}\left\langle v,\Sigma^{-1}v\right\rangle\Bigr)\quad\text{for $v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}$}.

Further, we associate to it a probability measure on
\Gamma
, given by

{\mathcal{N}}_{n,\Sigma,\zeta_{c}}(\gamma):=\pi(\boldsymbol{x})\frac{1}{\sum_{w\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}}\xi_{n\Sigma}(w)}\xi_{n\Sigma}(\Phi(\gamma)-n\zeta_{c})\quad\text{for $\gamma=v\boldsymbol{x}\in\Gamma=\Lambda\Delta$}.

Theorem 4.7

(Local CLT)
.

Let
\Gamma=\bigsqcup_{x\in\Delta}\Lambda x
and
\mu
be a probability measure on
\Gamma
with finite second moment such that the support generates the group as a semigroup,
and let
(G,c)
be the corresponding weighted diagram.
If
\mu
has period
q
,
then

\left\|q^{-1}\sum_{i=0}^{q-1}\mu_{qn+i}-{\mathcal{N}}_{qn,\Sigma,\zeta_{c}}\right\|_{\rm TV}\to 0\quad\text{as $n\to\infty$}.

In the above, the covariance matrix
\Sigma
is computed as

\left\langle v_{1},\Sigma v_{2}\right\rangle=\sum_{e\in E(G)}u_{1}(e)u_{2}(e)c(e)-\sum_{e\in E(G)}u_{1}(e)c(e)\sum_{e\in E(G)}u_{2}(e)c(e),

where
u_{i}
denotes the unique harmonic part of
\widehat{v}_{i}
for
v_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
,
i=1,2
.

The proof of Theorem
4.7
uses the following estimate.

Proposition 4.8
.

In the setting of Theorem
4.7
,
if
\mu
is aperiodic,
then
we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\sup_{\gamma\in\Gamma}n^{\frac{m}{2}}|\mu_{n}(\gamma)-\pi([\gamma])\xi_{n\Sigma}(\Phi(\gamma)-n\zeta_{c})|=0.

Before proceeding to the proofs, we record three useful facts.

Facts 4.9
.

We keep the notations as above.

(1)

For each
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
, we have
\widehat{v}=u+d\varphi
for some
\varphi\in C^{0}(G,{\mathbb{R}})
with a constant
C
independent of
v
such that

\|\varphi\|_{\infty}\leq C\|v\|.

(4.11)

(2)

For every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\delta>0
such that for all
v\in D_{\delta}
,

\beta(v)=2\pi i\langle v,\zeta_{c}\rangle-2\pi^{2}\langle v,\Sigma v\rangle+\eta(v)\quad\textrm{where $|\eta(v)|\leq\varepsilon\|v\|^{2}$}.

(4.12)

(3)

For every
\varepsilon_{0}>0
, for all small enough
\varepsilon>0
, the following holds: for all
\delta>0
and all
n
large enough (depending on
\delta
),

\int_{D_{\delta\sqrt{n}}}e^{-2\pi^{2}\langle v,\Sigma v\rangle}\left(e^{\varepsilon\|v\|^{2}}-1\right)dv\leq\varepsilon_{0}.

(4.13)

Proof.

For the first point, write
v=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}\boldsymbol{e}_{i}
with the standard basis
\boldsymbol{e}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{e}_{m}
, and
\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{m}\in{\mathbb{R}}
. For each
i=1,\dots,m
, taking
\widehat{\boldsymbol{e}_{i}}=u_{i}+d\varphi_{i}
, we choose
\varphi=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}\varphi_{i}
, implying (
4.11
) with
C=\sqrt{m}\max_{i=1,\dots,m}\|\varphi_{i}\|_{\infty}
by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.

Lemma
4.1
(1) and Lemma
3.2

show that
(d/dr)|_{r=0}\beta(rv)=2\pi i\chi_{c}(\widehat{v})=2\pi i\langle v,\zeta_{c}\rangle
,
and Lemma
4.2
shows that the Hessian of
\beta
at
0
is
-4\pi^{2}\Sigma
.
Since
\beta
is twice continuously differentiable around
0
, the Taylor theorem implies (
4.12
) with
\eta(v)=o(\|v\|^{2})
. There remains to take
\delta
small enough depending on
\varepsilon
to get the second point.

For the third point, we split the integral according to
0<r<\delta\sqrt{n}
. We have

\int_{D_{r}}e^{-2\pi^{2}\langle v,\Sigma v\rangle}\left(e^{\varepsilon\|v\|^{2}}-1\right)dv\leq C_{\Sigma}\sup_{v\in D_{r}}\left(e^{\varepsilon\|v\|^{2}}-1\right)\leq C_{\Sigma}\varepsilon mr^{2}e^{\varepsilon mr^{2}}.

For
\varepsilon>0
small enough depending on
\Sigma
, we have

\int_{D_{\delta\sqrt{n}}\backslash D_{r}}e^{-2\pi^{2}\langle v,\Sigma v\rangle}\left(e^{\varepsilon\|v\|^{2}}-1\right)dv\leq\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}\backslash D_{r}}e^{-\pi^{2}\langle v,\Sigma v\rangle}dv\leq Ce^{-cr^{2}}

for constants
c,C
depending on
\Sigma
. Choosing
r
large enough, then
\varepsilon
small enough and finally
n\geq(r/\delta)^{2}
we obtain the third point.
∎

Proof of Proposition
4.8
.

By the Fourier inversion formula (
4.9
),
we have for
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and for
\gamma\in\Gamma
with
[\gamma]=x\in V(G)
,
for all
\delta>0
,

\mu_{n}(\gamma)=\int_{D_{\delta}}{\mathcal{L}}_{\widehat{v}}^{n}{\bf 1}_{x}(x_{0})e^{-2\pi i\left\langle v,\Phi(\gamma)\right\rangle}\,dv+\int_{D\setminus D_{\delta}}{\mathcal{L}}_{\widehat{v}}^{n}{\bf 1}_{x}(x_{0})e^{-2\pi i\left\langle v,\Phi(\gamma)\right\rangle}\,dv=:I_{1}+I_{2}.

(4.14)

We will specify
\delta
later.
If
\mu
is aperiodic,
then Lemma
4.4

shows that
|I_{2}|\leq C_{\delta}e^{-c_{\delta}n}
for some
c_{\delta},C_{\delta}>0
and all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
We will analyze the first term
I_{1}
.

Recall that the dominating eigenvalue
e^{\beta(v)}
of
{\mathcal{L}}_{\widehat{v}}
depends only on the harmonic part
u
of
\widehat{v}=u+d\varphi
(cf. Section
4.1
).
Since
{\mathcal{L}}_{\widehat{v}}=e^{-2\pi i\varphi}{\mathcal{L}}_{u}e^{2\pi i\varphi}
,
for a small enough
\delta>0
we have a constant
r_{\delta}>0
such that

{\mathcal{L}}_{\widehat{v}}^{n}{\bf 1}_{x}(x_{0})=e^{n\beta(v)}\left\langle e^{2\pi i\varphi}{\bf 1}_{x},f_{u}\right\rangle_{\pi}e^{-2\pi i\varphi}f_{u}(x_{0})\left(1+O(e^{-r_{\delta}n})\right)\quad\text{for $v\in D_{\delta}$},

where
f_{u}
denotes the normalized eigenfunction of
{\mathcal{L}}_{u}
for the eigenvalue
e^{\beta(v)}
.
By Lemma
4.1
(
4.2
),
we have

f_{u}(x)=1+O\left(\|v\|^{2}\right)

and so

\left\langle e^{2\pi i\varphi}{\bf 1}_{x},f_{u}\right\rangle_{\pi}e^{-2\pi i\varphi}f_{u}(x_{0})=\pi(x)e^{2\pi i(\varphi(x)-\varphi(x_{0}))}\left(1+O\left(\|v\|^{2}\right)\right).

Then we can express

I_{1}=\pi(x)\int_{D_{\delta}}e^{n\beta(v)}e^{2\pi i(\varphi(x)-\varphi(x_{0}))}e^{-2\pi i\langle v,\Phi(\gamma)\rangle}\left(1+O(\|v\|^{2})+O(e^{-r_{\delta}n})\right)dv

The change of variable
v\mapsto v/\sqrt{n}
yields
u\mapsto u/\sqrt{n}
and
\varphi\mapsto\varphi/\sqrt{n}
.
Note that since
\varphi
is real-valued, by (
4.11
) we have

e^{2\pi i(\varphi(x)-\varphi(x_{0}))/\sqrt{n}}=1+O\left(\frac{\|v\|}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

Using also the expression for
\beta(v)
given in (
4.12
), we obtain

I_{1}=\frac{\pi(x)}{n^{\frac{m}{2}}}\int_{D_{\delta\sqrt{n}}}e^{\sqrt{n}2\pi i\langle v,\zeta_{c}\rangle-2\pi^{2}\langle v,\Sigma v\rangle+n\eta(\frac{v}{\sqrt{n}})}e^{-2\pi i\langle\frac{v}{\sqrt{n}},\Phi(\gamma)\rangle}\left(1+O\left(\frac{\|v\|}{\sqrt{n}}\right)+O(e^{-r_{\delta}n})\right)dv

Let
\varepsilon>0
and
\delta>0
be small enough (depending on
\Sigma
) such that (
4.12
) gives

-2\pi^{2}\langle v,\Sigma v\rangle+|\eta(v)|\leq-\pi^{2}\langle v,\Sigma v\rangle,\quad\text{for all $v\in D_{\delta}$}.

(4.15)

Then the integral of the modulus for the terms
O\left(\|v\|/\sqrt{n}\right)+O(e^{-r_{\delta}n})
in the above expression is bounded above by

\int_{D_{\delta\sqrt{n}}}e^{-\pi^{2}\langle v,\Sigma v\rangle}\left(\frac{\|v\|}{\sqrt{n}}+e^{-r_{\delta}n}\right)dv=O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

Therefore we get, setting
R_{n}(v):=e^{n\eta(\frac{v}{\sqrt{n}})}-1
,

I_{1}=\frac{\pi(x)}{n^{\frac{m}{2}}}\int_{D_{\delta\sqrt{n}}}e^{2\pi i\left\langle v,\sqrt{n}\zeta_{c}-\frac{\Phi(\gamma)}{\sqrt{n}}\right\rangle}e^{-2\pi^{2}\langle v,\Sigma v\rangle}\left(1+R_{n}(v)\right)dv+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{\frac{m+1}{2}}}\right).

Now by (
4.13
), provided
\varepsilon>0
is small enough, the integral of the term with
R_{n}(v)
can be made smaller than any
\varepsilon_{0}>0
. Coming back to (
4.14
), we have proved

\left|\mu_{n}(\gamma)-\frac{\pi(x)}{n^{\frac{m}{2}}}\int_{D_{\delta\sqrt{n}}}e^{2\pi i\left\langle v,\sqrt{n}\zeta_{c}-\frac{\Phi(\gamma)}{\sqrt{n}}\right\rangle}e^{-2\pi^{2}\left\langle v,\Sigma v\right\rangle}\,dv\right|\leq\frac{\pi(x)\varepsilon_{0}}{n^{\frac{m}{2}}}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{\frac{m+1}{2}}}\right)

For a given
\varepsilon_{0}>0
, we choose
\varepsilon>0
and
\delta>0
such that (
4.12
), (
4.13
), and (
4.15
) hold.

On the other hand, the Fourier transform directly shows that for
w\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
,

\xi_{n\Sigma}(w-n\zeta_{c})=\frac{1}{n^{\frac{m}{2}}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}e^{2\pi i\left\langle v,\sqrt{n}\zeta_{c}-\frac{w}{\sqrt{n}}\right\rangle}e^{-2\pi^{2}\left\langle v,\Sigma v\right\rangle}\,dv.

There exists a constant
c_{\delta}^{\prime}>0
, depending only on
\Sigma
, such that for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
and all
n\geq 1
,

\xi_{n\Sigma}(\Phi(\gamma)-n\zeta_{c})=\frac{1}{n^{\frac{m}{2}}}\int_{D_{\delta\sqrt{n}}}e^{2\pi i\left\langle v,\sqrt{n}\zeta_{c}-\frac{\Phi(\gamma)}{\sqrt{n}}\right\rangle}e^{-2\pi^{2}\left\langle v,\Sigma v\right\rangle}\,dv+O(e^{-c_{\delta}^{\prime}n}).

Summarizing the above estimates shows that for every
\varepsilon_{0}>0
there exists a constant
C_{\varepsilon_{0}}
such that for all large enough
n
, uniformly in
\gamma\in\Gamma
with
[\gamma]=x
,

\displaystyle n^{\frac{m}{2}}|\mu_{n}(\gamma)-\pi(x)\xi_{n\Sigma}(\Phi(\gamma)-n\zeta_{c})|\leq\pi(x)\varepsilon_{0}+C_{\varepsilon_{0}}\left(n^{-\frac{1}{2}}+n^{\frac{m}{2}}e^{-c_{\delta}^{\prime}n}\right).

This shows the proposition.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.7
.

Note that the covariance matrix
\Sigma
is computed in Lemma
4.2
.
First we show the claim assuming that
\mu
is aperiodic.
By Lemma
4.6
, for a constant
C
for all
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and all
r>0
,

\Prob\left(\|\Phi(w_{n})-n\zeta_{c}\|\geq r\right)\leq\frac{Cn}{r^{2}}.

(4.16)

Moreover,
a direct computation yields a constant
C^{\prime}
such that for all
n\geq 1
and all
r>0
,

\sum_{v\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m},\|v\|\geq r}\xi_{n\Sigma}(v)\leq C^{\prime}\exp\left(-\frac{r^{2}}{C^{\prime}n}\right).

Hence we have

\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma,\|\Phi(\gamma)-n\zeta_{c}\|\geq r}\pi([\gamma])\xi_{n\Sigma}(\Phi(\gamma)-n\zeta_{c})\leq C^{\prime}\exp\left(-\frac{r^{2}}{C^{\prime}n}\right)\leq\frac{C^{\prime 2}n}{r^{2}}.

(4.17)

By Proposition
4.8
, (
4.16
) and (
4.17
), for
C^{\prime\prime}:=C+C^{\prime 2}
, for every
\varepsilon>0
, for all large enough
n
, and for all
r>0
,

\displaystyle\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}|\mu_{n}(\gamma)-\pi([\gamma])\xi_{n\Sigma}(\Phi(\gamma)-n\zeta_{c})|\leq\#B(r)\frac{\varepsilon}{n^{\frac{m}{2}}}+\frac{C^{\prime\prime}n}{r^{2}}.

The inequality follows by dividing the sum over
\gamma
into the two parts where
\|\Phi(\gamma)-n\zeta_{c}\|
is less than
r
or at least
r
.
In the above,
\#B(r)
denotes the number of
\gamma\in\Gamma
with
\|\Phi(\gamma)-n\zeta_{c}\|<r
,
and
\#B(r)\leq C_{0}r^{m}
for a constant
C_{0}
independent of
n
or
r
.
Thus, letting
C:=C_{0}+C^{\prime\prime}
, we obtain for
r=\varepsilon^{-\frac{1}{m+2}}\sqrt{n}
,

\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}|\mu_{n}(\gamma)-\pi([\gamma])\xi_{n\Sigma}(\Phi(\gamma)-n\zeta_{c})|\leq\frac{C_{0}\varepsilon r^{m}}{n^{\frac{m}{2}}}+\frac{C^{\prime\prime}n}{r^{2}}=C_{0}\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{m+2}}+C^{\prime\prime}\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{m+2}}=C\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{m+2}}.

Furthermore, by the Poisson summation formula, for a constant
c>0
, for all
n\geq 1
,

\sum_{v\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}}\xi_{n\Sigma}(v)=\sum_{v\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}}e^{-2\pi^{2}n\left\langle v,\Sigma v\right\rangle}=1+\sum_{v\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{m}\setminus\{0\}}e^{-2\pi^{2}n\left\langle v,\Sigma v\right\rangle}=1+O(n^{-1}e^{-cn}).

By choosing a bigger constant
C
, for every
\varepsilon>0
, for all large enough
n
,

\|\mu_{n}-{\mathcal{N}}_{n,\Sigma,\zeta_{c}}\|_{\rm TV}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\gamma\in\Gamma}|\mu_{n}(\gamma)-{\mathcal{N}}_{n,\Sigma,\zeta_{c}}(\gamma)|\leq C\varepsilon^{\frac{2}{m+2}}.

This shows the claim if
\mu
is aperiodic.

In general, if
\mu
has period
q
, then
\mu_{q}
is aperiodic.
Applying the above discussion to
\mu_{q}
with the initial distribution
q^{-1}(\delta_{\id}+\mu+\cdots+\mu_{q-1})
yields

\Bigl\|q^{-1}\sum_{i=0}^{q-1}\mu_{qn+i}-q^{-1}\sum_{i=0}^{q-1}{\mathcal{N}}_{qn+i,\Sigma,\zeta_{c}}\Bigr\|_{\rm TV}\to 0\quad\text{as $n\to\infty$}.

Noting that for each
i=0,\dots,q-1
,

\Bigl\|{\mathcal{N}}_{qn+i,\Sigma,\zeta_{c}}-{\mathcal{N}}_{qn,\Sigma,\zeta_{c}}\Bigr\|_{\rm TV}=O\Bigl(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\Bigr),

we conclude the proof.
∎

5.1.
The structure of covariance matrix related to transfer homomorphisms

in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{i}}
, and consider the orthogonal decompositions:

{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{i}}=\im({\underline{\vartheta}}_{i})\oplus\im({\underline{\vartheta}}_{i})^{\perp},

where
{\underline{\vartheta}}_{i}
is the linear operator on
\mathbb{R}^{m_{i}}
which coincides with
\vartheta_{i}/\#F_{i}
on
\Lambda_{i}
.

Theorem 5.1

(Structure Theorem)
.

Let
\nu
be an aperiodic finite second moment probability measure on
\Gamma_{1}\times\Gamma_{2}
, with marginals
\mu^{(i)}
, and we keep the notations above. Then
\|\nu_{n}-{\mathcal{N}}_{n,\Sigma_{\nu},\zeta_{c}}\|_{{\rm TV}}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
with
\zeta_{c}\in V_{\vartheta_{1}}\oplus V_{\vartheta_{2}}
and the covariance matrix of the form

\Sigma_{\nu}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\Sigma_{\mu^{(1)}}&\begin{matrix}\ast&0\\
0&0\end{matrix}\\
\hline\cr\begin{matrix}\ast&0\\
0&0\end{matrix}&\Sigma_{\mu^{(2)}}\end{array}\right)

in a basis adapted to the decomposition
{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{1}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{2}}=\im({\underline{\vartheta}}_{1})\oplus\im({\underline{\vartheta}}_{1})^{\perp}\oplus\im({\underline{\vartheta}}_{2})\oplus\im({\underline{\vartheta}}_{2})^{\perp}
.

In particular, when both
\Gamma_{i}
admit no nonzero homomorphisms to
{\mathbb{Z}}
, the drift is zero. This is known by the general result of Karlsson and Ledrappier
[
4
, Corollary 4]
: Liouville random walks on groups with no nonzero homomorphisms to
{\mathbb{Z}}
have zero drift.

Prior to the proof, we record an important fact about harmonic decompositions in products. Recall that the edge set
E(G^{(1)}\times G^{(2)})
is identified with
\Delta_{1}\times S^{(1)}\times\Delta_{2}\times S^{(2)}
. Edges will be simply denoted as tuples
(x_{1},s_{1},x_{2},s_{2})
.
The quotient diagram is denoted
\boldsymbol{F}:=(\Lambda_{1}\times\Lambda_{2})\backslash(\Gamma_{1}\times\Gamma_{2})=F_{1}\times F_{2}
.

Fact 5.2
.

In the setting of Theorem
5.1
, for
v_{1}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{1}}
, we let
\boldsymbol{v}_{1}=(v_{1},0)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{1}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{2}}
. Denote their harmonic decompositions as

\widehat{v}_{1}=u_{1}+df_{1}\quad\textrm{and}\quad\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{1}=\boldsymbol{u}_{1}+d\boldsymbol{f}_{1}.

Then for all
(x_{1},s_{1},x_{2},s_{2})\in\Delta_{1}\times S^{(1)}\times\Delta_{2}\times S^{(2)}
,

\boldsymbol{u}_{1}(x_{1},s_{1},x_{2},s_{2})=u_{1}(x_{1},s_{1})\quad\textrm{and}\quad\boldsymbol{f}_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})=f_{1}(x_{1}).

Proof.

It is enough to check the decomposition

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{v}}_{1}(x_{1},s_{1},x_{2},s_{2})

\displaystyle=\langle\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\Phi_{(x_{1},s_{1},x_{2},s_{2})}\rangle=\langle(v_{1},0),(\Phi^{(1)}_{(x_{1},s_{1})},\Phi^{(2)}_{(x_{2},s_{2})})\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle v_{1},\Phi^{(1)}_{(x_{1},s_{1})}\rangle=u_{1}(x_{1},s_{1})+df_{1}(x_{1},s_{1})

and the harmonicity of
(x_{1},s_{1},x_{2},s_{2})\mapsto u_{1}(x_{1},s_{1})=:\boldsymbol{u}_{1}(x_{1},s_{1},x_{2},s_{2})
. Indeed, note that

\boldsymbol{c}(x_{1},s_{1},x_{2},s_{2})=\pi(x_{1},x_{2})\nu(s_{1},s_{2})=\frac{1}{\#\boldsymbol{F}}\nu(s_{1},s_{2}).

(5.1)

Thus, we have

\displaystyle d^{*}\boldsymbol{u}_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{e:oe=(x_{1},x_{2})}\frac{\boldsymbol{c}(e)}{\pi(x_{1},x_{2})}\boldsymbol{u}_{1}(x_{1},s_{1},x_{2},s_{2})=-\sum_{s_{1},s_{2}}\nu(s_{1},s_{2})u_{1}(x_{1},s_{1})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{s_{1}}\mu^{(1)}(s_{1})u_{1}(x_{1},s_{1})=d^{*}u_{1}(x_{1})

and

\displaystyle\chi_{\boldsymbol{c}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{1})

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in E(G)}\boldsymbol{u}_{1}(e)\boldsymbol{c}(e)=\frac{1}{\#\boldsymbol{F}}\sum_{x_{1},s_{1},x_{2},s_{2}}u_{1}(x_{1},s_{1})\nu(s_{1},s_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\#\boldsymbol{F}}\sum_{s_{1},s_{1}}u_{1}(x_{1},s_{1})\mu^{(1)}(s_{1})\#F_{2}=\chi_{c}(u_{1}).

(5.2)

Then
d^{*}\boldsymbol{u}_{1}(x_{1},x_{2})+\chi_{\boldsymbol{c}}(\boldsymbol{u}_{1})=d^{*}u_{1}(x_{1})+\chi_{c}(u_{1})=0
as
u_{1}
is harmonic for
\mu^{(1)}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
5.1
.

The convergence is given by the local CLT (Theorem
4.7
).
Letting
\alpha_{i}
denote the cocycle in the definition of
\vartheta_{i}:\Gamma_{i}\to\Lambda_{i}
for
i=1,2
,
we define
\alpha(x_{1},x_{2},s_{1},s_{2})=(\alpha_{1}(x_{1},s_{1}),\alpha_{2}(x_{2},s_{2}))
.
Using (
5.1
), the drift is computed as

\displaystyle\zeta_{\boldsymbol{c}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in E(G)}\boldsymbol{c}(e)\Phi_{e}=\sum_{x_{1},s_{1},x_{2},s_{2}}\frac{1}{\#\boldsymbol{F}}\nu(s_{1},s_{2})\alpha(x_{1},s_{1},x_{2},s_{2})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\#\boldsymbol{F}}\sum_{s_{1},s_{2}}\nu(s_{1},s_{2})\left(\sum_{x_{2},x_{1}}\alpha_{1}(x_{1},s_{1}),\sum_{x_{1},x_{2}}\alpha_{2}(x_{2},s_{2})\right).

It belongs to
\mathrm{im}({\underline{\vartheta}}_{1})\oplus\mathrm{im}({\underline{\vartheta}}_{2})
since
\sum_{x_{i}}\alpha_{i}(x_{i},s_{i})=\vartheta_{i}(s_{i})
.

To get the zeros in the covariance matrix, consider
\boldsymbol{v}_{1}=(v_{1},0)
for
v_{1}\in\mathrm{im}({\underline{\vartheta}}_{1})^{\perp}\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{1}}
and
\boldsymbol{v}_{2}=(0,v_{2})
for
v_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{2}}
and compute, using the explicit form in Theorem
4.7
, Fact
5.2
, and equation (
5.1
)

\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\Sigma_{\nu}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{e\in E(G)}\boldsymbol{u}_{1}(e)\boldsymbol{u}_{2}(e)\boldsymbol{c}(e)-\left(\sum_{e\in E(G)}\boldsymbol{u}_{1}(e)\boldsymbol{c}(e)\right)\left(\sum_{e\in E(G)}\boldsymbol{u}_{2}(e)\boldsymbol{c}(e)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x_{1},x_{2},s_{1},s_{2}}u_{1}(x_{1},s_{1})u_{2}(x_{2},s_{2})\frac{\nu(s_{1},s_{2})}{\#\boldsymbol{F}}-\chi_{{c}}({{u}_{1}})\chi_{{c}}({{u}_{2}}).

(5.3)

Reordering the sums,
we have

\sum_{x_{1},x_{2},s_{1},s_{2}}u_{1}(x_{1},s_{1})u_{2}(x_{2},s_{2})\frac{\nu(s_{1},s_{2})}{\#\boldsymbol{F}}=\sum_{x_{2},s_{1},s_{2}}u_{2}(x_{2},s_{2})\frac{\nu(s_{1},s_{2})}{\#\boldsymbol{F}}\sum_{x_{1}}u_{1}(x_{1},s_{1})=0,

where the last equality follows from Lemma
3.3
as
v_{1}\in\mathrm{im}({\underline{\vartheta}}_{1})^{\perp}
. Moreover
\chi_{{c}}({{u}_{1}})=0
by Lemma
3.3
.
The same computation holds exchanging the roles of first and second group factors.
∎

The following corollary of Theorem
5.1
gives Theorem
1.3
from the introduction when the groups are infinite.

Corollary 5.3
.

For
i=1,2
, let
\Gamma_{i}
be a finitely generated infinite virtually abelian group,
and
\nu
be an aperiodic probability measure on
\Gamma_{1}\times\Gamma_{2}
with finite second moment such that the support of
\nu
generates
\Gamma_{1}\times\Gamma_{2}
as a semigroup and
\nu
has the marginal
\mu^{(i)}
on
\Gamma_{i}
.
If
\Gamma_{1}
admits no nonzero homomorphisms onto
{\mathbb{Z}}
,
then

\big\|\nu_{n}-\mu^{(1)}_{n}\times\mu^{(2)}_{n}\big\|_{\rm TV}\to 0\quad\text{as $n\to\infty$}.

Proof.

The transfer homomorphism of
\Gamma_{1}
vanishes so the vector spaces
\mathrm{Im}({\underline{\vartheta}}_{1})
is zero. Theorem
5.1
shows that
\Sigma_{\nu}
is a block diagonal symmetric matrix along the decomposition
{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{1}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{2}}
.
Two blocks are the covariance matrices for
\Sigma_{\mu^{(i)}}
. The other blocks are only zero. Therefore,
\Sigma_{\nu}
coincides with the covariance matrix for the
(\mu^{(1)}\times\mu^{(2)})
-random walk on
\Gamma_{1}\times\Gamma_{2}
.
The local CLT (Theorem
4.7
) applied to
\nu
and
\mu^{(1)}\times\mu^{(2)}

yields by the triangle inequality,

\big\|\nu_{n}-\mu^{(1)}_{n}\times\mu^{(2)}_{n}\big\|_{\rm TV}\leq\big\|\nu_{n}-{\mathcal{N}}_{n,\Sigma_{\nu},0}\big\|_{\rm TV}+\big\|\mu^{(1)}_{n}\times\mu^{(2)}_{n}-{\mathcal{N}}_{n,\Sigma_{\nu},0}\big\|_{\rm TV}\to 0\quad\text{as $n\to\infty$}.

This concludes the claim.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

First, in the case when both groups are infinite, we apply Corollary
5.3
.
Next, in the case when one group
\Gamma
is infinite and the other group is finite
G
, we apply Theorem
4.7
to
\Gamma\times G
.
Note that
{\mathcal{N}}_{n,\Sigma,\zeta_{c}}
is defined as the product of the uniform distribution on
F\times G
and the discrete Gaussian distribution on
\Lambda
, where
\Lambda\backslash\Gamma=F
.
Finally, in the case when both groups are finite, both
\nu_{n}
and
\mu_{n}^{(1)}\times\mu_{n}^{(2)}
tend to the uniform distribution on the product group.
∎

5.2.
Applications to noise sensitivity

Recall that noise sensitivity of the
\mu
-random walk on a group
\Gamma
is concerned with the measures
\pi^{\rho}=\rho(\mu\times\mu)+(1-\rho)\mu_{\rm diag}
for
\rho\in[0,1]
on
\Gamma\times\Gamma
.
As a special case of Corollary
5.3
, we obtain the following, which is the main step towards Theorem
1.1
.

Corollary 5.4
.

Let
\Gamma
be a finitely generated infinite virtually abelian group and
\mu
be an aperiodic probability measure on
\Gamma
with finite second moment such that the support generates the group as a semigroup.
If
\Gamma
admits no nonzero homomorphism onto
{\mathbb{Z}}
,
then

\left\|\pi^{\rho}_{n}-\mu_{n}\times\mu_{n}\right\|_{\rm TV}\to 0\quad\text{as $n\to\infty$},

for all
\rho\in(0,1]
, i.e. the
\mu
-random walk on
\Gamma
is noise sensitive in total variation.

Proof.

For each
\rho\in(0,1]
, we have
\supp\pi^{\rho}=\supp\mu\times\supp\mu
, and the support of
\pi^{\rho}
generates
\Gamma\times\Gamma
as a semigroup since
\mu
is aperiodic and
\supp\mu
generates
\Gamma
as a semigroup by assumption.
Furthermore,
\pi^{\rho}
is aperiodic since
\mu
is aperiodic, and
\pi^{\rho}
has finite second moment since
\mu
has finite second moment by assumption.
Applying Corollary
5.3
with
\nu=\pi^{\rho}
for each
\rho\in(0,1]
,
\Gamma_{i}=\Gamma
, and
\mu_{i}=\mu
for
i=1,2
yields the claim.
∎

The local CLT implies the following corollary.

Corollary 5.5
.

Let
\Gamma
be a finitely generated virtually abelian group and
\mu
be a finite second moment probability measure on
\Gamma
such that the support generates the group as a semigroup.
Then

\lim_{\rho\to 1}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\left\|\pi^{\rho}_{n}-\mu_{n}\times\mu_{n}\right\|_{\rm TV}=0

Proof.

Note that if
\mu
has period
q
, then
\pi^{\rho}
has period
q
for all
\rho\in(0,1]
since
\supp\pi^{\rho}=\supp\mu\times\supp\mu
.
Thus,
\pi^{\rho}_{nq+i}
and
\mu_{qn+i}\times\mu_{qn+i}
have the common support, and
\pi^{\rho}_{nq+i}
,
i=0,1,\dots,q-1
, have disjoint supports.
Therefore we have

\sum_{i=0}^{q-1}\left\|\pi^{\rho}_{nq+i}-\mu_{qn+i}\times\mu_{qn+i}\right\|_{\rm TV}=\left\|\sum_{i=0}^{q-1}\pi^{\rho}_{nq+i}-\sum_{i=0}^{q-1}\mu_{qn+i}\times\mu_{qn+i}\right\|_{\rm TV}.

For each
\rho\in(0,1]
,
the local CLT (Theorem
4.7
) implies that

\left\|\sum_{i=0}^{q-1}\pi^{\rho}_{qn+i}-q{\mathcal{N}}_{qn,\Sigma^{\rho},\zeta_{c}}\right\|_{\rm TV}\to 0\quad\text{as $n\to\infty$}.

(5.4)

In the above,
\zeta_{c}=\sum_{e\in E(G)}c(e)\Phi_{e}\in\Lambda\times\Lambda
has the same component in each factor
\Lambda
, in particular, independent of
\rho\in(0,1]
since
\pi^{\rho}
has marginals
\mu
in both factors.
Note that

\left\|{\mathcal{N}}_{n,\Sigma^{\rho},\zeta_{c}}-{\mathcal{N}}_{n,\Sigma^{1},\zeta_{c}}\right\|_{\rm TV}=\left\|{\mathcal{N}}_{n,\Sigma^{\rho},0}-{\mathcal{N}}_{n,\Sigma^{1},0}\right\|_{\rm TV}.

Furthermore, we have

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\|{\mathcal{N}}_{n,\Sigma^{\rho},0}-{\mathcal{N}}_{n,\Sigma^{1},0}\right\|_{\rm TV}=\frac{1}{2}\|\xi_{\Sigma^{\rho}}-\xi_{\Sigma^{1}}\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2m})}.

(5.5)

Before we show (
5.5
),
let us conclude the claim.
By (
5.5
), the triangle inequality, and (
5.4
) for the case when
\rho\in(0,1]
and for the case when
\rho=1
,
we have

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}q^{-1}\left\|\sum_{i=0}^{q-1}\pi^{\rho}_{nq+i}-\sum_{i=0}^{q-1}\mu_{qn+i}\times\mu_{qn+i}\right\|_{\rm TV}

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\|{\mathcal{N}}_{qn,\Sigma^{\rho},0}-{\mathcal{N}}_{qn,\Sigma^{1},0}\right\|_{\rm TV}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|\xi_{\Sigma^{\rho}}-\xi_{\Sigma^{1}}\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2m})}.

(5.6)

Furthermore,
\Sigma^{\rho}\to\Sigma^{1}
as
\rho\to 1
by Remark
2.4
(see also the explicit form of covariance matrix provided by Proposition
5.7
in the case of semi-direct products).
This implies that the right hand side of (
5.2
) tends to
0
as
\rho\to 1
.

Let us show (
5.5
).
By the definition of
{\mathcal{N}}_{n,\Sigma^{\rho},0}
(see, the one above Theorem
4.7
), there exists a constant
c>0
such that for all large enough
n
,

\|{\mathcal{N}}_{n,\Sigma^{\rho},0}-{\mathcal{N}}_{n,\Sigma^{1},0}\|_{\rm TV}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in\Delta^{2}}\pi(x)\sum_{v\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{2m}}|\xi_{n\Sigma^{\rho}}(v)-\xi_{n\Sigma^{1}}(v)|+O(e^{-cn}).

Note that there exists a constant
c>0
such that for all real
\lambda>1
and all integer
n\geq 1
,

\sum_{\|v\|>\lambda n^{1/2}}|\xi_{n\Sigma^{\rho}}(v)-\xi_{n\Sigma^{1}}(v)|=O(e^{-c\lambda^{2}})\quad\text{and}\quad\int_{\|v\|>\lambda n^{1/2}}|\xi_{n\Sigma^{\rho}}(v)-\xi_{n\Sigma^{1}}(v)|\,dv=O(e^{-c\lambda^{2}}).

Furthermore, by estimating the gradients of the Gaussians, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\|v\|\leq\lambda n^{1/2}}|\xi_{n\Sigma^{\rho}}(v)-\xi_{n\Sigma^{1}}(v)|=\int_{\|v\|\leq\lambda n^{1/2}}|\xi_{n\Sigma^{\rho}}(v)-\xi_{n\Sigma^{1}}(v)|\,dv+O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right).

By change of variables
v\mapsto v/\sqrt{n}
, we have

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2m}}|\xi_{n\Sigma^{\rho}}(v)-\xi_{n\Sigma^{1}}(v)|\,dv=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{2m}}|\xi_{\Sigma^{\rho}}(v)-\xi_{\Sigma^{1}}(v)|\,dv=\|\xi_{\Sigma^{\rho}}-\xi_{\Sigma^{1}}\|_{L^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{2m})}.

Summarizing the above estimates, first
n\to\infty
, and then
\lambda\to\infty
, we obtain (
5.5
).
∎

Corollary 5.6
.

Let
\Gamma
be a finitely generated infinite virtually abelian group and
\mu
be a finite second moment probability measure on
\Gamma
such that the support generates the group as a semigroup.
If moreover
\Gamma
admits a nonzero homomorphism onto
{\mathbb{Z}}
,
then

\lim_{\rho\to 0}\liminf_{n\to\infty}\left\|\pi^{\rho}_{n}-\mu_{n}\times\mu_{n}\right\|_{\rm TV}=1,

in particular, the
\mu
-random walk on
\Gamma
is not noise sensitive in total variation.

Proof.

Considering the pushforward of
\pi^{\rho}
from
\Gamma\times\Gamma
to
\im(\vartheta)\times\im(\vartheta)
via the product of transfer homomorphisms.
We consider the random walk on the finitely generated torsion free abelian group
\im(\vartheta)\times\im(\vartheta)
.
Note that the pushforward of
\pi^{\rho}
has finite second moment, and that the projection does not increase total variation distances.
By
[
8
, Theorem A.1]
, we have the first claim.

The second claim follows from the first by the definition of noise sensitivity.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

It follows from Corollary
5.5
and Corollary
5.6
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

If
\Gamma
is finite, then the
\mu
-random walk is noise sensitive in total variation
[
1
, Proposition 5.1]
.
If
\Gamma
is infinite, then the claim follows from Corollary
5.4
and Corollary
5.6
.
∎

5.3.
An explicit form of the structure theorem for semi-direct product groups

In the particular case of semi-direct product groups, Theorem
5.1
is more explicit. We use the notations above with
\Gamma_{1}=\Gamma_{2}=\Gamma
.

Proposition 5.7
.

Assume that the virtually abelian group is a semi-direct product
\Gamma=\Lambda\rtimes F
. Let
\mu
be a finite second moment probability measure on
\Gamma
such that the support generates the group as a semigroup. For the measure
\pi^{\rho}=\rho(\mu\times\mu)+(1-\rho)\mu_{\rm diag}
on
\Gamma\times\Gamma
, we have

\Sigma_{\pi^{\rho}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c}\Sigma_{\mu}&\begin{matrix}(1-\rho)\Sigma_{\mu}|_{\mathrm{im}({\underline{\vartheta}})\times\mathrm{im}({\underline{\vartheta}})}&0\\
0&0\end{matrix}\\
\hline\cr\begin{matrix}(1-\rho)\Sigma_{\mu}|_{\mathrm{im}({\underline{\vartheta}})\times\mathrm{im}({\underline{\vartheta}})}&0\\
0&0\end{matrix}&\Sigma_{\mu}\end{array}\right)

in a basis adapted to the decomposition
{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{1}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{m_{2}}=\im({\underline{\vartheta}}_{1})\oplus\im({\underline{\vartheta}}_{1})^{\perp}\oplus\im({\underline{\vartheta}}_{2})\oplus\im({\underline{\vartheta}}_{2})^{\perp}
.

This proposition implies in particular that, regarding noise sensitivity, the underlying Euclidean space
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
can be decomposed into two complementary subspaces. On one of these subspaces, namely
\mathrm{im}({\underline{\vartheta}})
, the effect of noise sensitivity is the same as on free abelian groups.
On the other subspace, that is
\mathrm{im}({\underline{\vartheta}})^{\perp}
, the random walk behaves as noise sensitive.

Note also that as
{\underline{\vartheta}}
is a projection, we have

{\underline{\vartheta}}^{\ast}\Sigma_{\mu}{\underline{\vartheta}}=\left(\begin{matrix}\Sigma_{\mu}|_{\mathrm{im}({\underline{\vartheta}})\times\mathrm{im}({\underline{\vartheta}})}&0\\
0&0\end{matrix}\right),\quad\textrm{so}\quad\Sigma_{\pi^{\rho}}=\left(\begin{matrix}\Sigma_{\mu}&(1-\rho){\underline{\vartheta}}^{\ast}\Sigma_{\mu}{\underline{\vartheta}}\\
(1-\rho){\underline{\vartheta}}^{\ast}\Sigma_{\mu}{\underline{\vartheta}}&\Sigma_{\mu}\end{matrix}\right).

Proof of Proposition
5.7
.

Let
\boldsymbol{v}_{i}\in\mathrm{im}({\underline{\vartheta}}_{i})
, for
i=1,2
. Using the definitions of
\pi^{\rho}
and
\chi_{c}(u_{i})=\sum_{x,s}u_{i}(x,s)\mu(s)/\#F
, we compute from equation (
5.1
)

\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\Sigma_{\nu}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{x_{1},s_{1},x_{2},s_{2}}u_{1}(x_{1},s_{1})u_{2}(x_{2},s_{2})\frac{\pi^{\rho}(s_{1},s_{2})}{(\#F)^{2}}-\chi_{{c}}({{u}_{1}})\chi_{{c}}({{u}_{2}})

\displaystyle=(1-\rho)\left(\sum_{x_{1},x_{2},s}u_{1}(x_{1},s)u_{2}(x_{2},s)\frac{\mu(s)}{(\#F)^{2}}-\chi_{{c}}({{u}_{1}})\chi_{{c}}({{u}_{2}})\right).

By Lemma
3.5
, we have
u_{1}
and
u_{2}
independent of
x
so we get

\displaystyle\langle\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\Sigma_{\nu}\boldsymbol{v}_{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=(1-\rho)\left(\sum_{x,s}u_{1}(x,s)u_{2}(x,s)\frac{\mu(s)}{\#F}-\chi_{{c}}({{u}_{1}})\chi_{{c}}({{u}_{2}})\right)

\displaystyle=(1-\rho)\langle v_{1},\Sigma_{\mu}v_{2}\rangle=\langle v_{1},{\underline{\vartheta}}^{\ast}\Sigma_{\mu}{\underline{\vartheta}}v_{2}\rangle,

as required.
∎
