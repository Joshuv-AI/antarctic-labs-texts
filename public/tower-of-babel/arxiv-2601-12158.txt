Measuring growth and convergence at the mesoscale
Isaak Mengesha, Debraj Roy

Abstract
Global inequality has shifted inward, with rising dispersion increasingly occurring within countries rather than between them. Using 8,790 newly harmonised Functional Urban Areas (FUAs) —micro-founded labour-market regions encompassing 3.9 billion people and representing approximately 80% of global GDP— we show that national aggregates systematically, and increasingly, misrepresent the dynamics of growth, convergence, and structural change. Drawing on high-resolution global GDP data and country-level capability measures, we find that the middle-income trampoline that previously drove global convergence is flattening. This divergence in the lower-income regime does not reflect poverty traps: low-income FUAs exhibit positive expected growth, and the transition curve displays no stable low-income equilibrium. Instead, productive capabilities—proxied by the Economic Complexity Index—define distinct growth regimes. FUAs converge within capability strata but diverge across them, and capability upgrading follows a predictable J-curve marked by short-run disruption and medium-run acceleration. These findings suggest that national convergence policies may be systematically misaligned with the geographic scale at which capability accumulation operates.



1
Introduction

Global inequality has undergone a fundamental geographic reorientation. While between-country income gaps remain substantial, within-country dispersion is rising across the world economy
[
49
]
. Therefore, studying growth at the national level misses where heterogeneity is increasingly located. Yet other spatial disaggregation – i.e. sub-national administrative boundaries – introduces systematic distortions through the Modifiable Areal Unit Problem (MAUP)
[
40
]
. Statistical relationships depend on the scale and zoning of underlying spatial units. Administrative boundaries aggregate historical circumstances rather than economic integration, obscuring the mechanisms generating divergence, convergence, and structural adjustment. Evidence for multiple steady states at sub-national scales is robust, yet global convergence analysis remains constrained by administratively defined regions rather than economically meaningful spatial units
[
17
,
9
,
21
]
. When development constraints are locally embedded, national convergence strategies based on place-neutral policy reasoning are structurally misaligned with the geography of growth.
[
7
]
.

Functional Urban Areas (FUAs) provide a theoretically grounded alternative. Delineated using population grids and commuting-flow thresholds, FUAs approximate integrated labour-market regions rather than political jurisdictions
[
15
]
. This matters because knowledge- and complexity-intensive activities concentrate in cities
[
6
]
, FUAs capture actual adjustment margins to labour-demand shocks
[
48
]
, and approximately 3.9 billion people live in 8,790 FUAs worldwide
[
37
]
. As a result, FUAs provide the appropriate spatial scale for studying growth heterogeneity as an outcome of endogenous adjustment and capability accumulation.

Observed at the FUA level, urban systems display systematic development heterogeneity. Larger cities enjoy persistent productivity premia
[
1
]
, while mature urban systems exhibit diminishing population-growth advantages
[
38
]
. These regularities imply distinct urban growth regimes that are mechanically blurred by national aggregates. A natural candidate mechanism is productive capability accumulation. The Economic Complexity Index (ECI) captures latent productive know-how, institutional capacity, and diversification potential
[
31
]
. Higher ECI predicts subsequent growth
[
26
]
, yet subnational capability measures remain largely absent from empirical growth research
[
50
]
. If urban growth is regime-dependent, capabilities provide a plausible basis for structuring these regimes .

This motivates the central question:
What growth and convergence regimes become visible in the world economy when development is examined at the scale of functional urban areas, and how does spatial aggregation shape our understanding of the contribution of productive capabilities?
We show that FUAs recover a substantially clearer growth signal than comparably fine administrative spatial disaggregation. At the FUA level, income dynamics exhibit stable, nonlinear laws of motion that are obscured under administratively defined regions. Capability-based growth predictions suffer analogous attenuation under spatial aggregation: the predictive content of ECI weakens mechanically as economically integrated units are fragmented. Across all spatial levels, we find no evidence of poverty traps in the sense of locally stable low-income steady states. Instead, the data support the existence of capability-conditioned growth regimes, with convergence occurring within capability strata and divergence across them—a structure that becomes most legible when growth is measured at the scale of functional urban areas.

2
Related Literature

Three bodies of research inform this paper’s approach to urban growth dynamics: the convergence debate and the conditions under which economies catch up, fall behind, or cluster into distinct steady states; the role of productive capabilities in shaping development trajectories; and the use of functional urban areas as units of economic analysis. A common theoretical concern cuts across all three. New Economic Geography demonstrates that increasing returns and transport costs generate multiple spatial equilibria rather than a unique steady state
[
32
]
, and that economic outcomes depend on market access and spatial integration rather than administratively defined boundaries
[
19
]
. Aggregating heterogeneous spatial equilibria at the national level therefore mechanically obscures growth and convergence dynamics. Spatial scale is not a neutral measurement choice but a theoretically consequential one—an insight that motivates our effort to bridge these literatures by embedding capability-driven dynamics within a convergence framework operationalised at the scale of functional urban areas.

Convergence, growth regimes, and structural transformation

Recent work has reignited debate over whether poorer economies are catching up to richer ones.
[
41
]
documented unconditional convergence at the country level since the 1990s, yet the authors themselves have since noted a post-2010 reversal
[
42
]
, corroborated by others
[
39
]
. This instability is puzzling on its own terms, but becomes more so when paired with evidence that between-country convergence has coincided with rising inequality within countries
[
2
]
. Together, this puts the conventional approach of measuring convergence at the national level into question.

To situate this concern, consider the theoretical foundations. The Solow model predicts convergence to a common steady state
[
47
]
, and early evidence among relatively homogeneous samples—OECD economies, US states—appeared consistent with this prediction
[
8
]
. Globally, however, unconditional convergence does not agree with data. The resolution proposed by
[
34
]
is conditional convergence: economies converge not to a universal equilibrium but to heterogeneous steady states determined by structural characteristics—savings rates, population growth, human capital, institutions. Convergence then operates within groups sharing similar fundamentals; across groups, income gaps persist. Models incorporating nonconvexities or complementarities go further, generating multiple locally stable equilibria
[
4
]
and the empirical implication of convergence clubs: catch-up within clusters, divergence between them
[
17
]
.

What determines club membership? Cross-country variation in output per worker is measured predominantly by total factor productivity rather than factor accumulation
[
24
]
. TFP, in turn, reflects organisational efficiency, institutional quality, and technological capacity—closely related to what capability-based measures of economic complexity aim to capture
[
31
,
29
]
. If capabilities determine which equilibrium an economy converges toward, then capability accumulation provides a mechanism linking micro-level productive knowledge to macro-level growth dynamics. A related literature examines structural transformation—the reallocation of labour from agriculture to manufacturing to services that accompanies development
[
28
]
. Sectoral transitions are also capability transitions: the knowledge, institutions, and organisational forms required to operate a manufacturing economy differ fundamentally from those suited to agriculture
[
31
]
. If capability upgrading requires costly investment and reorganisation, transitions should involve short-run disruption before medium-run gains materialise. This J-curve pattern—temporary costs followed by delayed benefits—is familiar from trade liberalisation
[
5
]
and the adoption of general purpose technologies
[
27
,
11
]
, but has not been systematically examined in the context of capability-driven growth.

Economic complexity as mechanism for development

Economies differ not merely in how much they produce but in what they know how to produce—knowledge revealed in the composition of output rather than aggregate input stocks
[
29
]
. The Economic Complexity Index (ECI) operationalises this insight by measuring the sophistication and diversification of an economy’s export basket; ECI predicts subsequent growth even after controlling for standard determinants
[
26
]
. The product space framework provides a mechanism: economies upgrade toward products that lie close to their existing capability base, while distant jumps require acquiring multiple capabilities simultaneously, imposing steep coordination costs
[
31
]
. This path dependence implies that capability position shapes not only current income but also the feasibility of future transitions—connecting capabilities directly to convergence club membership.

Yet ECI is typically measured at the national level, obscuring within-country heterogeneity. Capability distributions are highly uneven spatially: complex activities cluster disproportionately in cities, where agglomeration economies and knowledge spillovers lower the costs of capability formation
[
6
]
. National aggregates may therefore misattribute urban capability dynamics to countries, or mask divergence across regions within converging nations. This concern is underscored by a Simpson’s paradox: the correlation between ECI and inequality is negative at the country level but positive at the regional level
[
25
]
. Systematic analysis linking capability measures to subnational growth nonetheless remains scarce
[
50
]
, though recent work has begun to identify ECI as a mechanism for regional inequality within the EU
[
44
]
.

A natural and functional unit of analysis

Agglomeration economies arise from localized mechanisms—learning, matching, and sharing—that operate within integrated labour markets and dense interaction zones
[
16
,
20
]
. Administrative regions do not generally coincide with the spatial extent of these mechanisms. Subnational units such as NUTS regions, provinces, and districts remain artefacts of political history rather than economic integration, splitting integrated labour markets or aggregating distinct ones and thereby introducing measurement error that biases growth estimates
[
3
]
. Functional Urban Areas offer a principled alternative. Because FUAs are delineated by commuting flows, they capture the spatial extent of daily interaction—the boundary within which knowledge spillovers, labour pooling, and input sharing actually operate
[
37
]
. This makes FUAs appropriate units for studying capability accumulation: agglomeration externalities are the micro-foundation of capability formation, and FUAs approximate where these externalities occur.

Emerging work supports this view. Economic complexity measured at the FUA level explains variation in urban growth rates and resilience to shocks
[
23
,
33
]
. Yet the broader agglomeration literature, while documenting systematic productivity and growth differences across cities
[
14
,
46
]
, typically takes the spatial unit of observation as given rather than examining how aggregation alters inferred growth laws or convergence estimates
[
22
]
. Modern spatial economics emphasises quantitative multi-region equilibrium analysis in which outcomes depend on the structure of spatial interaction across locations
[
45
]
, but empirical convergence analysis remains largely conducted at administrative scales. This paper integrates the spatial logic of economic geography into convergence empirics by changing the unit of analysis. Whether the patterns documented in cross-country research—conditional convergence, multiple equilibria, convergence clubs—replicate, intensify, or reverse when examined across functionally defined cities remains an open question. Limitations of the FUA approach remain—commuting patterns may lag economic restructuring, and coverage is uneven in low-income settings—but FUAs nonetheless represent the best available approximation to integrated urban economies.

3
Methods

Identifying Growth Regimes

The standard cross-country growth regression derived from the Solow model takes the form

g_{i}=\zeta+\beta\ln y_{i,0}+\Pi X_{i}+\varepsilon_{i}

(1)

where
g_{i}
denotes growth over a fixed period,
y_{i,0}
is initial output per capita, and
X_{i}
contains determinants of the steady state such as investment rates and population growth. A negative coefficient
\beta<0
indicates convergence: poorer economies grow faster due to diminishing returns to capital. This specification assumes all economies share a common production function and converge toward a common steady state. Models with threshold externalities or nonconvexities in production, however, generate multiple locally stable equilibria
[
4
]
. In such models, economies with different initial conditions follow distinct laws of motion:

g_{i}=\zeta_{j}+\beta_{j}\ln y_{i,0}+\Pi_{j}X_{i}+\varepsilon_{i}\quad\text{for }i\in\mathcal{R}_{j}

(2)

where regime membership
\mathcal{R}_{j}
depends on initial capital stocks exceeding certain thresholds. Convergence operates
within
regimes (
\beta_{j}<0
), but economies in different regimes converge to different steady states. Estimating a single linear model on data generated by multiple regimes can still yield an apparent negative
\beta
, inheriting within-regime convergence while masking cross-regime divergence
[
17
]
. In our subsequent analysis we estimate
\beta
for statements of convergence and divergence.

Spatial aggregation and the ECI-growth relationship

The relationship between productive capabilities and growth is spatially localised: agglomeration mechanisms operate predominantly within integrated markets rather than across administrative boundaries. Let
g_{i}
denote growth in spatial unit
i
and
c_{i}
its capability stock. The conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}[g_{i}|c_{i}]
captures the capability-growth relationship at the unit of observation. When units are aggregated into larger regions
R
, the measured relationship becomes
\mathbb{E}[g_{R}|c_{R}]
where
g_{R}
and
c_{R}
are aggregates over constituent units. Two sources of nonlinearity make this aggregation problematic. First, the mapping from export composition to ECI is itself nonlinear, constructed via iterative eigenvalue decomposition of the country-product bipartite network
[
29
]
. Second, the conditional expectation
\mathbb{E}[g_{i}|c_{i}]
is nonlinear in
c_{i}
, as implied by observed growth regimes which arguable are shaped by capability levels
[
17
]
. Both nonlinearities invoke Jensen’s inequality:

\mathbb{E}[g_{R}|c_{R}]\neq\frac{1}{|R|}\sum_{i\in R}\mathbb{E}[g_{i}|c_{i}].

(3)

Aggregation that pools units with heterogeneous capability-growth relationships distorts the measured correlation. This distortion operates in both directions: aggregating below the functional scale fragments shared capability stocks across artificial boundaries, while aggregating above it averages over distinct growth regimes.

3.1
Data and Measurement

The analysis combines globally harmonised functional urban areas with high–resolution economic data to construct a panel of urban economies from 1992–2019. The objective is to measure GDP dynamics at the level of microfounded labour–market regions while linking them to country–level productive capabilities. Figure
1
provides a schematic overview of the aggregation procedure.

Functional Urban Areas.

Urban boundaries follow the
GHS–FUA 2023
delineation, which identifies 8,790 functional labour–market regions using globally consistent population grids and commuting thresholds
[
18
]
. All FUAs are retained. Each 1km cell from the underlying GHSL raster is assigned to the FUA whose polygon contains the majority of its area; this avoids multi–assignment and reflects the empirical indivisibility of urban labour–market basins. FUAs with missing years in the outcome series are omitted, but no further sample restrictions are imposed.

GDP and Population Fields.

Economic activity is measured using the global 1km GDP panel of
[
13
]
, which provides harmonised yearly estimates for 1992–2019 derived from calibrated nighttime‐lights (NTL) data. Population is taken from the 2015 GHSL layer and used uniformly across all years. For each FUA
i
and year
t
, total GDP is obtained by summing cell–level estimates within the FUA boundary, and GDP per capita is constructed as

y_{i,t}=\frac{\text{GDP}_{i,t}}{\text{Population}_{i,2015}}.

Growth is defined as the annual percentage change,

g_{i,t}=\frac{y_{i,t+1}-y_{i,t}}{y_{i,t}}.

No additional scaling, PPP adjustment, or reconciliation with national accounts is applied; all values are taken directly from the harmonised dataset. As pointed out by related literature there exist several limitation to estimating GDP from NTL. Some point at the instability at the regional level
[
10
]
, or the subannual level
[
43
]
. Finally there is evidence for saturation bias, in large and more developed regions
[
35
]
.

Capabilities.

Productive capabilities are measured using the Atlas of Economic Complexity’s annual country–level ECI series
[
26
]
. Since FUAs inherit national institutional and technological environments, each FUA is assigned the ECI of its country in year
t
. This yields a time–varying capability field
C_{i,t}
aligned with the urban GDP panel. Because ECI evolves only gradually, this procedure preserves the temporal structure without interpolation or smoothing.

Figure 1
:
We utilize FUAs as aggregating unit over various spatial fields. Those include, GDP, Population, HDI, RWI and several national level statistics such as TFP or ECI if no higher resolution was available. With this we construct the timeseries of observation for each FUA.

Construction of the FUA Panel.

The resulting dataset consists of yearly observations

X_{i,t}=\bigl(y_{i,t},g_{i,t},C_{i,t},\text{GDP}_{i,t},\text{Pop}_{i,2015}\bigr),

allocated to each FUA through the time–dependent spatial fields described above. Grid–level GDP enters additively, population is fixed at its 2015 level, and all capability measures are imported from the corresponding country–year. This produces a coherent panel of urban economies suitable for analysing transition dynamics and convergence patterns. The schematic in Figure
1
summarises the mapping from raster data to FUA–level aggregates and the subsequent construction of the variables used in the empirical analysis.

4
Empirical Results

The empirical analysis proceeds in three steps. We first document how the global distribution of production changes over time and – more importantly – over spatial aggregations for both production and wealth. Next we investigate global growth and convergence across spatial aggregations and contrast them with the broader literature. Finally we assess the evidence for capability driven growth and indicate consequences for future research.

4.1
Where is inequality located in the world economy?

We locate inequality in the world economy by decomposing the cross-sectional variance of GDP per capita across alternative spatial aggregations. For each year from 1992 to 2019, we decompose scale-level GDP per capita variance into between-country and within-country components, allowing us to assess how the geography of inequality depends on the unit of observation.

Two robust patterns emerge. First, between-country inequality dominates at coarse spatial scales but declines monotonically over time. Second, the within-country component rises steadily, indicating a persistent inward shift of inequality. This pattern holds throughout the sample period and is consistent with global convergence accompanied by increasing internal dispersion. However, the level at which within-country inequality appears dominant depends strongly on spatial aggregation. At the ADM1 level, most variance is mechanically attributed to between-country differences. Finer administrative units increase the within-country share, but do not eliminate sensitivity to boundary choice. When inequality is measured at the level of Functional Urban Areas (FUAs), a clear separation re-emerges: more than 75% of total variance is again accounted for by between-country differences. Measured at the scale of integrated labour markets, cross-country inequality is therefore substantially more stable. We note that nighttime-lights-based GDP underrepresents rural and agricultural activity, and that space-filling administrative units may introduce additional aggregation error.

To verify that these results are not specific to GDP measurement, we run a similar analysis using an independent wealth metric. We employ the Relative Wealth Index (RWI), an asset-based household wealth measure derived from DHS and related surveys
[
12
]
. For countries with at least twelve FUAs, we decompose within-country wealth variance across spatial scales and regress within-unit inequality on national GDP per capita. Spatial scale again proves decisive. At administrative levels (ADM1–ADM3), there is no systematic relationship between income and internal inequality. At the FUA level, a positive association emerges: richer countries exhibit greater wealth dispersion within their urban systems. While marginally significant (
r^{2}=0.146
,
p=0.097
), this relationship disappears under administrative aggregation. Within-FUA inequality accounts for roughly 85–90% of national RWI variance, compared to less than 50% at the ADM2 level, where estimates are also substantially more volatile. Across all spatial levels, within-unit inequality increases with GDP per capita, but only FUAs recover a stable relationship.

These results have three implications. First, the Modifiable Areal Unit Problem is not merely a statistical inconvenience: administrative boundaries systematically distort the spatial allocation of inequality by misaligning economic integration with political geography. Second, the findings provide a spatial interpretation of the dynamics underlying Milanović’s elephant curve: as between-country gaps narrow, inequality rises within countries, concentrated in urban labour markets rather than administrative regions
[
36
]
. Third, the evidence contradicts a subnational Kuznets hypothesis. Internal spatial inequality does not decline with development; instead, richer urban systems exhibit greater internal differentiation, consistent with agglomeration models in which productivity gains increasingly concentrate in dense labour markets
[
14
]
.

4.2
Is convergence sensitive to scale?

We assess whether measured convergence depends on the spatial unit of analysis by estimating
\beta
-convergence, following Equation
1
, across five spatial scales: countries (ADM0), administrative subdivisions (ADM1–ADM3), and Functional Urban Areas (FUAs). This allows us to isolate the role of spatial aggregation while holding the underlying GDP data fixed. While GDP estimates derived from nighttime lights are noisy and biased
[
35
,
13
]
– more importantly – these distortions apply uniformly across all spatial aggregations constructed from the same raster. Differences in estimated convergence rates across scales therefore reflect zoning and aggregation choices rather than NTL-specific measurement error. To anchor the magnitudes, we benchmark country-level estimates against the Penn World Tables. As expected, NTL-based ADM0 estimates show weaker convergence than PWT, consistent with attenuation from measurement noise (see Fig
3
a). FUAs exhibit stronger convergence than both administrative units and PWT. One might worry this reflects saturation bias, which compresses high-income estimates and could mechanically generate faster apparent catch-up
[
35
]
. However, FUAs also exhibit the highest between-country variance share across all spatial scales (Figure 2); if saturation dominated, this share would be attenuated. This indicates signal recovery rather than artifact.

Cross-dataset discrepancies at the country level are well documented—PWT, Maddison, and WDI differ substantially in levels, though long-run trends broadly align
[
41
,
42
]
. Our contribution is to show that, even holding the dataset fixed, the unit of analysis materially affects measured convergence. This is the Modifiable Areal Unit Problem operating directly in the growth literature. Figure
3
b illustrates these patterns. Rolling ten-year estimates show that convergence measured on administrative units clusters near zero throughout the sample period, while FUAs display consistently stronger convergence. Plotting convergence speed against mean population per unit reveals a saturation pattern: convergence strengthens as administrative units become smaller, then plateaus. FUAs, despite having population sizes comparable to ADM2 regions, exhibit a discrete jump in convergence strength.

Figure 3
:
Convergence is affected by MAUP. Increasing urbanisation beggs the question of what spatial disagregation is appropriate. Whatever the biases introduced by NTL based GDP estimates, they do not account for the jarring differences in convergence rates as they apply consistently.

This discontinuity suggests that functional delineation captures economically meaningful integration that administrative boundaries fragment. The saturation pattern is consistent with FUAs approximating the spatial scale at which key adjustment mechanisms—labour mobility, knowledge spillovers, and capital reallocation—operate
[
14
]
. Further disaggregation would fragment integrated labour markets, while coarser aggregation pools heterogeneous growth regimes, attenuating the convergence signal.

4.3
Changes to the urban law of motion

We examine how the relationship between income and growth at the urban level departs from the canonical Solow framework. Figure
4
(left) plots annual GDP per capita growth against initial income for all FUAs. Averaged across the full sample, the relationship is consistent with global convergence: poorer FUAs grow faster than richer ones. However, the conditional mean exhibits an inverted U-shape, indicative of a middle-income acceleration followed by slower growth at high income levels.

This pattern is not stable over time. In the later half of the sample period, the middle-income acceleration weakens substantially, and growth among the richest FUAs slows markedly. These shifts suggest that the urban growth process is not governed by a time-invariant law of motion, but evolves with the global development environment. Figure
4
(right) presents the corresponding Solow phase diagram. The 45-degree line represents average global growth; deviations from this line indicate relative convergence or divergence across FUAs. Standard poverty-trap models predict that the transition curve falls below the diagonal at low income levels, generating a locally stable low-income equilibrium
[
4
]
. We find no evidence for such a mechanism. Expected growth remains positive for low-income FUAs throughout the income distribution.

The transition curve never intersects the diagonal from above at low income levels. There is therefore no locally stable poverty trap at the scale of functional urban areas. This finding rules out capital-threshold mechanisms as the primary explanation for persistent urban income differences, at least at currently observed capital levels
[
47
,
4
]
. The implication is that urban income stratification cannot be accounted for within the standard Solow framework. Persistent divergence across cities must instead reflect heterogeneity in productive capabilities, institutional environments, or other factors shaping the urban growth process beyond factor accumulation alone.

4.4
Empirical regularities of capability-driven development

Capability-dependent growth.

We examine how convergence dynamics vary with productive capabilities by relating country-level Economic Complexity Index (ECI) values to the growth performance of their constituent FUAs over time. Each FUA is assigned its country’s contemporaneous ECI, reflecting the national capability environment in which urban labour markets operate. This approach is motivated by evidence that complex economic activities disproportionately concentrate in large cities
[
balland2020complex
]
. Across the full support of the ECI distribution, FUAs exhibit conditional convergence: estimated
\beta
coefficients remain positive at all capability levels. This rules out capability-conditioned divergence and indicates that poorer FUAs tend to grow faster than richer ones regardless of their position in capability space. However, convergence speed varies sharply with ECI. The relationship between
\beta
and capabilities is strongly nonlinear rather than smooth or monotonic.

At low levels of ECI, convergence speeds are uniformly low and display little variation. FUAs embedded in low-capability environments exhibit compressed growth responses, with small and relatively flat
\beta
estimates. In contrast, convergence accelerates sharply at intermediate capability levels. Around ECI values of approximately
0.4
–
0.7
,
\beta
rises abruptly, producing a pronounced spike in convergence speed. This transition is discrete rather than incremental, suggesting a regime shift in the underlying growth process associated with intermediate levels of productive capabilities. At the upper end of the capability distribution, convergence slows again. High-ECI FUAs exhibit weaker conditional convergence despite higher income levels, consistent with observed growth slowdowns in mature, high-capability urban economies. Notably, the peak in convergence coincides with the region of highest growth dispersion: intermediate ECI and income levels are associated with the widest spread of growth outcomes, aligning with the location of the convergence spike.

Figure 5
:
We disagregate growth rates of Fuas by the country level ECI to answer whether conditional convergence differs. We find strong differences in convergence regimes.

There is no evidence of a capability-conditioned poverty trap. Even at the lowest ECI levels, expected growth remains positive, consistent with earlier results showing no stable low-income equilibrium at the FUA scale. Conditioning convergence on ECI therefore adds structure beyond income-based convergence alone, revealing heterogeneity in growth dynamics that is otherwise obscured. Two further features are worth noting. First, the strongest catch-up dynamics occur in a transitional capability range rather than at the top of the distribution, indicating that convergence is linked to capability accumulation rather than capability saturation. Second, the pattern reflects a global regularity rather than a localized episode: the nonlinear
\beta
–ECI relationship emerges from pooled estimates across all FUAs and years and is not driven by specific regions or time periods. Finally, these results are specific to functional urban areas as the unit of analysis; the regime-like structure weakens or disappears under alternative spatial aggregations.

Capability accumulation rate.

Following Hidalgo
et al.
, we study the dynamics of capability accumulation by estimating a concave learning function for changes in the Economic Complexity Index (ECI)
[
30
]
. We calibrate the parameters of this function using observed transitions in ECI over time. At the country level, ECI dynamics exhibit conditional convergence. Figure
6
a shows that changes in ECI are non-monotonic in initial complexity: countries with intermediate ECI levels experience the largest average increases, while both low- and high-complexity countries display slower capability growth. The relationship between initial ECI and subsequent ECI change is concave, with a peak around
r^{*}\approx 0.61
, marking a transitional region in capability space.

In contrast, subnational dynamics differ sharply. Figure
6
b shows that ADM1 regions exhibit divergence in capabilities: initial ECI is positively and significantly related to subsequent ECI growth (
\beta=0.74
,
p<0.001
). High-complexity regions systematically accumulate further capabilities, while low-complexity regions tend to stagnate or decline. As a result, the distribution of ECI changes widens substantially at the subnational level, particularly among low-complexity regions. The sign reversal in ECI dynamics across spatial scales is not driven by differences in data or measurement. Both panels use the same underlying OEC data; the divergence arises from spatial aggregation alone. Conditional convergence at the country level therefore masks strong within-country capability concentration, a pattern consistent with recent regional evidence from Europe
[
44
]
. More generally, these results indicate that spatial aggregation alters the apparent law of motion of productive capabilities, yielding qualitatively different dynamics at national and subnational scales.

Capability upgrades effect on growth.

We examine the dynamic growth response to discrete increases in productive capabilities using an event-study design. At the level of Functional Urban Areas, ECI upgrades are associated with an immediate but temporary growth deceleration. In both specifications, FUA growth declines in the year of the upgrade (
t
), indicating short-run adjustment costs following capability accumulation. This slowdown is short-lived. Growth recovers within one to two years after the upgrade, with positive and increasing effects from
t{+}1
onward. Medium-run gains substantially exceed the initial losses: FUA growth peaks around
t{+}3
, surpassing pre-upgrade growth rates in panel (a) and remaining robustly positive in panel (b). The resulting temporal profile follows a clear J-curve, characterized by short-run contraction and subsequent sustained acceleration.

No comparable pattern is observed at the ADM1 level. Growth in administrative regions remains close to zero throughout the event window, with no pronounced post-upgrade rebound. The divergence between FUA and ADM1 responses emerges immediately after the upgrade and persists over the medium run. Importantly, the J-curve dynamic at the FUA level is robust to treatment definition: similar responses appear whether capability upgrades are identified at the country level or at the ADM1 level. These results indicate that outcome scale, rather than treatment resolution, determines signal recovery. Growth responses to capability upgrading are sharply visible when measured at the level of functional urban areas but are largely attenuated under administrative aggregation. Confidence intervals confirm statistically meaningful medium-run effects for FUAs, while ADM1 estimates remain imprecise and centered near zero. Together, these findings document scale-dependent adjustment dynamics following capability upgrading and motivate FUA-level analysis of capability–growth interactions.

Discussion

This paper has examined growth and convergence dynamics at various scales. We argued why the urban scale is crucial to understand the coupling of growth and convergence through capability accumulation. The analysis yields a set of empirical regularities that challenge standard accounts of global development and reframe how we understand the geography of growth. We summarise these findings, note limitations, and outline directions for future research.

Six stylized facts emerge from the analysis.
(1)
Global convergence rates are modulated by the spatial aggregation of recorded growth. Trends such as urbanization and other mechanisms point at FUAs as meaningful unit of analysis.
(2)
Classical poverty traps receive no empirical support at the urban scale. Low-income FUAs do not exhibit the negative or insufficient growth rates required to sustain a stable low-income equilibrium. The transition curve implies positive expected growth throughout the income distribution. Whatever generates persistent urban poverty, it is not the capital-threshold mechanism emphasised in canonical models—persistent divergence must arise from another factor rather than accumulation constraints.
(3)
While Capability accumulation on a national level exhibits the established inverted U-shape, subnational level capability accumulation increases with existing capability levels. This implies conditioned convergence of capabilities across countries and divergence within countries. This might not result in GDP per capita divergence within countries – due to a shared labor market and Baumol effect – but in the concentration of capabilities, opportunities, and human capital.
(4)
Capability upgrading follows a J-curve dynamic. Increases in national ECI are associated with temporary growth slowdowns in the FUAs of that country, followed by acceleration in subsequent periods. This pattern suggests that absorbing new productive knowledge requires costly reallocation and learning—transitional costs that are predictable and systematic rather than idiosyncratic. Even if treatment and measurement match at ADM1 level, the effect remains strongest when measured in FUAs contained by the respective region.
(5)
Inequality is increasingly a within-country and within-FUA phenomenon. The between-country share of global income variance has declined steadily since the early 1990s while the within-country share has risen – across all spatial aggregations. Moreover, richer urban areas exhibit more wealth inequality within than between their urban systems. Inequality is not only shifting inward geographically; it is concentrating within the cities that drive national growth, highlighting the importance of asset- and productivity-based inequality metrics at the urban scale.
(6)
FUAs are spatially interdependent. Growth rates exhibit significant spatial correlation that decays with distance, implying that cities are neither independent observations nor closed systems (see Fig.
11
in SI). They are embedded in regional and global networks through which shocks, knowledge, and resources flow. Any complete account of urban growth dynamics must incorporate this spatial structure.

These findings are subject to limitations that bound the conclusions we can draw. The analysis is restricted to functional urban areas; rural regions—where structural transformation, market access, and agricultural productivity constraints may generate different dynamics—remain outside the dataset, and the absence of poverty traps at the urban scale does not imply their absence elsewhere. Capabilities are measured at the national level, so FUAs inherit country-level ECI values that obscure within-country variation in productive structures; subnational capability metrics would sharpen the analysis considerably. The evidence is descriptive rather than causal: capabilities predict regime membership, but whether they cause it—and through what channels—remains open. Finally, GDP is proxied via calibrated nighttime-lights data, introducing measurement error that validation against subnational accounts could address.

The stylized facts open several avenues for future work. Most pressing is the construction of city-level capability measures using patents, occupational composition, or firm-level production networks; this would test directly whether within-country capability heterogeneity explains residual variation in urban growth dynamics and allow the framework developed here to operate at its natural scale. The observed spatial correlation in growth rates motivates models that explicitly incorporate spillovers and network effects—how far do shocks propagate, through what channels, and what determines the geography of synchronisation versus decoupling? Extending the analysis to include rural areas would clarify whether the dynamics documented here are specific to cities or reflect broader patterns, and would illuminate the interaction between urban capability accumulation and rural structural transformation. Finally, the regularities documented here invite mechanistic explanation. Why do capabilities shift the transition function? What frictions generate the J-curve? Answering these questions will require theoretical models disciplined by the empirical patterns this paper has established. The mesoscale matters: functional urban areas reveal growth dynamics that national aggregates obscure, and productive capabilities structure these dynamics in ways that standard convergence frameworks do not anticipate.
