Dirichlet

The study of the behaviour of such objects on the critical line

\displaystyle\zeta(s)

\displaystyle=\prod_{p}(1-p^{-s})^{-1}

Probabilistic methods have been long used in analytic number theory to study the generic behaviour of the Riemann zeta function. Often in this context the term used is statistical behaviour. The first such result on the critical line is the classical central limit theorem of Selberg from the 1940’s

35

An elegant new proof for Selberg’s central limit theorem was provided recently in

The connection between random matrix theory and the study of Riemann

Like the GMC mentioned above these fields are ubiquitous in many models of mathematical physics and probability theory. GMC is usually constructed from such fields. 1.1
Basic definitions and properties of Dirichlet characters and
L
-functions

For the convenience of readers not possessing a number theory background (like the author) we gather the basic definitions and properties needed from the theory of Dirichlet characters and
L
-functions to this section. Most of the material in this section can be found in any introductory book on (analytic) number theory, for example
[
1
]
.

First we need Dirichlet characters. Dirichlet characters modulo
q
, a positive integer, are the group characters for the group of reduced residue classes continued periodically and with the additional property that they are non-zero only for integers that are relatively prime to
q
. To be more precise, we first specify the group of reduced residue classes. A reduced residue system modulo
q
is the set of
\varphi(q):=|\{\mathbb{N}\ni k\leq q\mid gcd(q,k)=1\}|
integers
\{a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{\varphi(q)}\}
such that
a_{i}
is relatively prime to
q
for all
i
.
\varphi
is called the Euler totient function. Above
gcd
refers to greatest common divisor and as usual in number theory we shall from now on use the short notation
gcd(x,y)\equiv(x,y)
. The residue class corresponding to an integer
k
is the set
\hat{k}:=\{x\mid x\equiv k\,(\mathrm{mod}\,q)\}
. If we define the product of two residue classes
\hat{k}
and
\hat{l}
by
\hat{k}\hat{l}:=\widehat{kl}
, then the set of reduced residue classes modulo
q
(reduced meaning the added restriction that
(x,k)=1
) with this product form an Abelian group of order
\varphi(q)
.
\hat{1}
is the identity and the inverse of
\hat{k}
is
\hat{l}
such that
kl\equiv 1

\,(\mathrm{mod}\,q)
.

Recall that for Abelian groups a character is a homomorphism from the group to the complex numbers that is not identically zero. For finite groups like in the following the target is the unit circle
\mathbb{T}
. Then we have:

Definition 1.1

(Dirichlet characters)

Let
G
be the group of reduced residue classes modulo
q
. For each character
f
of
G
we define the corresponding function
\chi\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{T}
.

\displaystyle\chi(n)

\displaystyle:=f(\hat{n}),\text{ if $(n,q)=1$},

(1.8)

\displaystyle\chi(n)

\displaystyle:=0,\quad\,\,\,\text{ if $(n,q)>1$}.

(1.9)

The function
\chi
is called a Dirichlet character modulo
q
. The principal Dirichlet character is

\chi_{0}(n)=\begin{cases}1,\text{if $(n,q)=1$}\\
0,\text{if $(n,q)>1$}.\end{cases}

(1.10)

There are
\varphi(q)
distinct Dirichlet characters modulo
q
. They are completely multiplicative and
q
-periodic, that is, for any
m
and
n

\displaystyle\chi(mn)

\displaystyle=\chi(n)\chi(m)

(1.11)

\displaystyle\chi(n+q)

\displaystyle=\chi(n),

(1.12)

see for example
[
1
, Theorem 6.15]
.
Furthermore, Dirichlet characters satisfy the following orthogonality relation. Let
\chi_{i}
,
i=0,1,\dots,\chi_{\varphi(q)-1}
be the Dirichlet characters modulo
q
and
m,n
be two integers with
(n,q)=1=(m,q)
. Then we have

\sum_{i=0}^{\varphi(q)-1}\chi_{i}(n)\overline{\chi_{i}(m)}=\begin{cases}\varphi(q),\text{ if $m\equiv n\,(\mathrm{mod}\,q)$},\\
0,\quad\,\,\,\text{ if $m\not\equiv n\,(\mathrm{mod}\,q)$, }\end{cases}

(1.13)

see for example
[
1
, Theorem 6.16]
. Note that if
(n,q)>1
or
(m,q)>1
, the result of the above sum is always zero.

Then let us consider Dirichlet
L
-functions. For the proof of all but the last properties listed below see
[
1
, Chapter 12]
. Dirichlet
L
-functions depend on the integer
q
through the Dirichlet characters modulo
q
. Given a Dirichlet character
\chi
modulo
q
we define for
\mathrm{Re}\,(s)>1

L(s,\chi):=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\chi(s)}{n^{s}}.

(1.14)

This series converges absolutely and defines an analytic function in this half plane for any character
\chi
including the principal one
\chi=\chi_{0}
. For the principal character
\chi=\chi_{0}
the region of convergence of the series
1.14
cannot be enlarged. However, for non-principal characters
\chi\neq\chi_{0}
the series
1.14
converges in the half plane
\mathrm{Re}\,(s)>0
, but the convergence is absolute only for
\mathrm{Re}\,(s)>1
. Nevertheless, the convergence is uniform in compact subsets of the full region of convergence
\mathrm{Re}\,(s)>0
for non-principal characters
\chi\neq\chi_{0}
.

Furthermore, for any Dirichlet character
\chi
the
L
-function
L(\cdot,\chi)
can be continued to a meromorphic function in the whole complex plane
\mathbb{C}
. For non-principal characters (
\chi\neq\chi_{0}
) this continuation is even an entire function. The
L
-function
L(\cdot,\chi)
corresponding to the principal character shares the analytical properties of the Riemann zeta function
\zeta
. Namely, it is meromorphic with a single pole at
s=1
and this pole is of first order. Furthermore, we can express the
L
-function
L(s,\chi_{0})
in terms of the zeta function

L(s,\chi_{0})=\zeta(s)\prod_{p|q}(1-p^{-s}),

(1.15)

where the product runs over all distinct prime factors of
q
.
This last claim is easy to see from the Euler product forms
1.3
and
1.4
in their common validity range, and then continue analytically.

This is all we really need from the Dirichlet characters and corresponding
L
-functions.

1.2
Short introduction to multiplicative chaos and the randomized zeta function
\zeta_{\mathrm{rand}}
.

For convenience of readers coming from a number theory background that may not have knowledge of the theory of multiplicative chaos we review the bare minimum definitions and properties of multiplicative chaos to understand the limiting object of our main result.

The multiplicative chaos is formally the exponential
e^{X}
of a specific random field
X
. If
X
were an honest random function say a.s. continuous, there should be no difficulties to define the exponential. However, this is not the case for the most important class of fields.

The class of random fields, where the main interest in such objects lies, is the class of log-correlated Gaussian fields. We will first review the real case. Given a domain say an interval
I
, a real log-correlated Gaussian field
X
on
I
is a random field with covariance kernel
C\colon I\times I\to\mathbb{R}
given by

C(x,y)=\log(|x-y|^{-1})+g(x,y)

(1.16)

with
g
say continuous and bounded s.t. for all suitable real test functions
f
the evaluation
X(f)
is a real valued Gaussian random variable with variance

\Var(X(f)):=\int_{I\times I}f(x)f(y)C(x,y)\operatorname{d}\!x\operatorname{d}\!y.

(1.17)

Thus, indeed
X
is not an honest random function, but rather a random element in the space of generalized functions or Schwartz distributions. For the construction of such a field see for example
[
17
, Section 2.1]
, where the authors prove that the zero continuation (meaning
X=0
on
\mathbb{R}\setminus I
) of such a field exists as a random element in the Sobolev space
H^{-\epsilon}(\mathbb{R})
for all
\epsilon>0
.

Therefore, the exponential is not a priori well-defined. To define the multiplicative chaos we need to first regularize the field and then take a limit (an alternative point of view is given in
[
37
]
, where the GMC is characterized via a Cameron-Martin-Girsanov property). It turns out that in the real case the multiplicative chaos can be understood as a random measure.

For the proofs of the following statements in the subcritical and critical case we refer to the papers
[
33
,
7
,
11
]
and the review papers
[
32
,
29
]
and references therein. Given a suitable sequence
\{X_{N}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of smooth approximations of the field
X
the sequence of random measures
\mu_{\gamma,N}(\operatorname{d}\!x):=\frac{e^{\gamma X_{N}}}{\mathbb{E}[e^{\gamma X_{N}}]}\operatorname{d}\!x
with
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}
converges to a random measure in the weak topology of the space of Radon measures. The stochastic nature of the convergence may depend on the approximating sequence, but the limiting object does not in most cases. The limiting measure is unique, but non-trivial only for
|\gamma|<\sqrt{2}
and is non-atomic for the same parameter range. In the limiting case
|\gamma|=\sqrt{2}
a further renormalization is needed to obtain a non-trivial limit. For
|\gamma|>\sqrt{2}
yet further procedure is needed and the limiting measure is then purely atomic measure.

The rigorous study of Multiplicative chaos was started by Kahane in 1985
[
18
]
. The original motivation was in the rigorous study of Kolmogorov’s turbulence in fluid dynamics and constructing a continuous analogue of Mandelbrot’s multiplicative cascades
[
27
,
26
]
.
We refer the reader to the nice review
[
31
]
and the elegant construction
[
7
]
in the sub-critical (
|\gamma|<\sqrt{2}
) case and to the review
[
29
]
and references therein for the critical
(|\gamma|=\sqrt{2})
case. Note that the distribution is symmetric so that it is enough to look at
\gamma>0
.

To describe the limiting object
\zeta_{\mathrm{rand}}
in our main theorem in terms of multiplicative chaos we need to consider the complex case. Then the limiting object is no longer necessarily a measure, but just a Schwartz distribution. In the complex case, we can either consider complex parameters
\gamma
or allow the field
X
to be complex. The latter case is studied in
[
23
]
. Actually, they study the case, where the real and imaginary parts of the field have independent real parameters, that is
\gamma X=\gamma_{1}X_{1}+i\gamma_{2}X_{2}
with
\gamma_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
and
X_{i}
real log-correlated fields. In our case we would have
\gamma_{1}=1=\gamma_{2}
. In
[
23
]
the authors assume that the real (
X_{1}
) and imaginary (
X_{2}
) parts of the field are independent. However, this is inadequate to describe
\zeta_{\mathrm{rand}}
.

Instead, we need very specific covariance structure for the real an imaginary parts. By theorem
[
34
, Theorem 1.1 (ii)]
we can write as Schwartz distributions

\zeta_{\mathrm{rand}}(1/2+ix)=g(x)\nu(x),

(1.18)

where
g
is a smooth random function on
\mathbb{R}
with almost surely no zeros and
``\nu(x)=e^{\mathcal{G}(x)}"
is multiplicative chaos corresponding to the Gaussian field
\mathcal{G}
satisfying the covariance structure

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{G}(x)\mathcal{G}(y)]

\displaystyle=0

(1.19)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\mathcal{G}(x)\overline{\mathcal{G}}(y)]

\displaystyle=\log(\zeta(1+i(x-y)))

(1.20)

for
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
. This translates to

\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Re}\,(\mathcal{G})(x)\mathrm{Im}\,(\mathcal{G})(y)]=-\frac{\pi}{4}\mathrm{sgn}(x-y)+\mathrm{smooth}.

(1.21)

Similar type of Complex multiplicative chaos appeared in
[
28
]
in the context of random unitary matrices.

To prove
1.18
the authors of
[
34
]
use the truncated randomized Euler product

\zeta_{N,\mathrm{rand}}(s):=\prod_{p\leq N}(1-\omega_{p}p^{-s})^{-1},

(1.22)

where
\omega_{p}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\mathbb{T})
are i.i.d random variables, and the following decomposition

\log(\zeta_{N,\mathrm{rand}}(1/2+ix))=\mathcal{G}_{N}(x)+\mathcal{E}_{N}(x)

(1.23)

where
\mathcal{G}_{N}
is an explicit Gaussian process on
[-A,A]
for any
A>0
and
\mathcal{E}_{N}
is a smooth random function that almost surely converges uniformly to a smooth random function
\mathcal{E}

[
34
, Theorem 1.7]
. This decomposition allows them to show that
\zeta_{N,\mathrm{rand}}
also gives rise to real multiplicative chaos. Namely, that the measures
C(N)|\zeta_{N,\mathrm{rand}}(x)|^{\gamma}/\mathbb{E}[|\zeta_{N,\mathrm{rand}}(x)|^{\gamma}]\operatorname{d}\!x
, where
C(N)
is a renormalization constant that is
1
unless
\gamma=\gamma_{c}=\sqrt{2}
converge to a non trivial limiting measure for all
\gamma\leq\sqrt{2}
. This measure can be described as a product of a well behaving random function and a real GMC
[
34
, Theorems 1.8 and 1.9]
.

It is still open question whether
\log(\zeta(1/2+ix+i\omega T))
also converge to
\mathcal{G}(x)+\mathcal{E}(x)
. Similarly, it is still only a conjecture that
\mu_{T}(x):=\zeta(1/2+ix+i\omega T)
gives rise to a real multiplicative chaos. Namely, whether the measures
C(T)|\mu_{T}(x)|^{\gamma}/\mathbb{E}[|\mu_{T}(x)|^{\gamma}]\operatorname{d}\!x
, where
C(T)
is again a renormalizing constant only needed in the critical case, converge to a non-trivial limiting measure. The latter is likely a harder question since we would need to control the moments
\mathbb{E}[|\mu_{T}(x)|^{\gamma}]
. Similar questions for the functions
L(1/2+ix,\chi_{q})
are also out of reach. Mainly because we lack the means to control the proportion of the characters for which
L(1/2+ix,\chi)\neq 0
for given
x\in\mathbb{R}
even for fixed modulus
q
let alone in the limit
q\to\infty
. The moments
\mathbb{E}[|L(1/2+ix,\chi_{q})|^{\gamma}]
are also poorly understood.

1.3
The main result

Our main theorem is:

Theorem A

Let
q
be an integer and denote by
\mathcal{X}_{q}
the set of all Dirichlet characters modulo
q
. Assume that
\chi_{q}
is a uniformly random Dirichlet character, that is,
\chi_{q}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\mathcal{X}_{q})
and
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
. Then

L_{q}(f):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)L(1/2+ix,\chi_{q})\operatorname{d}\!x\overset{d}{\to}\zeta_{\mathrm{rand}}(f)

(1.24)

as
q\to\infty
.

In the proof we will utilize the fact established in
[
34
]
that the truncated randomized Euler product integrated against a test function
f

\zeta_{N,\mathrm{rand}}(f):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\prod_{p\leq N}(1-p^{-(\frac{1}{2}+ix)}\omega_{p})^{-1}\operatorname{d}\!x,

(1.25)

converges almost surely to
\zeta_{\mathrm{rand}}(f)
. We actually need only convergence in law. Above
N\in\mathbb{N}
, the random variables
\omega_{p}
are independent for distinct primes
p
and
\omega_{p}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\mathbb{T})
, that is,
\omega_{p}
is uniform in the unit circle of
\mathbb{C}
.
We will show that for
M(f)=M\in\mathbb{N}
large enough

\mathcal{L}_{M,q}(f):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\sum_{n=1}^{M}\frac{\chi_{q}(n)}{n^{\frac{1}{2}+ix}}\operatorname{d}\!x

(1.26)

approximates well in law the quantity

\mathcal{L}_{M,\omega}(f):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\sum_{n=1}^{M}\frac{\omega_{n}}{n^{\frac{1}{2}+ix}}\operatorname{d}\!x

(1.27)

and that this in turn approximates well
\zeta_{N,\mathrm{rand}}(f)
. The random variables
\omega_{n}
for non-prime integers will be introduced in the next section. Finally we use the approximation result given in
Theorem

3.1
.

Remark 1.2
.

Since the convergence is only in law we cannot directly infer convergence in the topology of the space of ordinary distributions
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\mathbb{R})
, that is, the topological dual of
\mathcal{D}(\mathbb{R})=C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
. It is natural to expect convergence in some distribution space e.g. the Sobolev space
H^{-\alpha}(\mathbb{R})
, for some
\alpha>0
. However, since we already know by
[
34
, Theorem 1.1]
, that the limiting object
\zeta_{\mathrm{rand}}
is in the above Sobolev space for all
\alpha>1/2
, we do not pursue this further.

Some preliminary lemmas

Let us now consider the most important property of
\chi_{q}
for us. First we have

Lemma 2.1

For arbitrary
N\in\mathbb{N}
let
\{p_{i}\}_{i=1,2,\dots,N}
be a set of distinct prime numbers. Then

(\chi_{q}(p_{1}),\chi_{q}(p_{2}),\dots,\chi_{q}(p_{N}))\overset{d}{\to}(\omega_{p_{1}},\omega_{p_{2}},\dots,\omega_{p_{N}})

(2.1)

as
q\to\infty
. Above
\omega_{p_{i}}
are
i.i.d
with
\omega_{p_{i}}\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\mathbb{T})
, that is,
\omega_{p_{i}}
is uniformly distributed on the unit circle of the complex plane.

Proof.

Since the random variables involved are bounded, it is enough to consider the moments. If we know the moments, then we know the integrals for all polynomials and by a density argument also for all continuous functions. Thus, the measure is uniquely determined. Consider first for fixed
k_{i},m_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
,
i=1,2,\dots,N

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}[\omega_{p_{1}}^{k_{1}}\overline{\omega_{p_{1}}}^{m_{1}}\omega_{p_{2}}^{k_{2}}\overline{\omega_{p_{2}}}^{m_{2}}\dots\omega_{p_{N}}^{k_{N}}\overline{\omega_{p_{N}}}^{m_{N}}]&=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{n}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{i(k_{i}-m_{i})\theta_{i}}\,\mathrm{d}\theta_{i}\\
&=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if $k_{i}=m_{i}$ for all $i=1,2,\dots,N$}\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}\end{split}

(2.2)

On the other hand, we have

\begin{split}\mathbb{E}&\left[[\chi_{q}(p_{1})]^{k_{1}}\overline{\chi_{q}(p_{1})}^{m_{1}}[\chi_{q}(p_{2})]^{k_{2}}\overline{\chi_{q}(p_{2})}^{m_{2}}\dots[\chi_{q}(p_{N})]^{k_{N}}\overline{\chi_{q}(p_{N})}^{m_{N}}\right]\\
&=\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\sum_{j=0}^{\varphi(q)-1}[\chi^{(j)}(p_{1})]^{k_{1}}\overline{\chi_{q}(p_{1})}^{m_{1}}[\chi^{(j)}(p_{2})]^{k_{2}}\overline{\chi(p_{2})}^{m_{2}}\dots[\chi^{(j)}(p_{N})]^{k_{N}}\overline{\chi_{q}(p_{N})}^{m_{N}}\\
&=\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\sum_{j=0}^{\varphi(q)-1}\chi^{(j)}(p_{1}^{k_{1}}p_{2}^{k_{2}}\dots p_{n}^{k_{n}})\overline{\chi^{(j)}(p_{1}^{m_{1}}p_{2}^{m_{2}}\dots p_{N}^{m_{N}})}\\
&=\begin{cases}1,\text{ if $p_{1}^{k_{1}}p_{2}^{k_{2}}\dots p_{N}^{k_{N}}\equiv p_{1}^{m_{1}}p_{2}^{m_{2}}\dots p_{N}^{m_{N}}$ (mod $q$)}\\
0,\text{ otherwise}.\end{cases}\end{split}

(2.3)

where
\chi^{(j)}
,
j=0,2,\dots,\varphi(q)-1
are the Dirichlet characters modulo
q
and at the last step we used the orthogonality relation of Dirichlet characters
1.13
and their multiplicative property
1.11
. Note that above the indexing is with superscripts rather than subscripts to distinguish from
\chi_{q}
. Since the exponents are fixed
2.3
converges to
\mathbf{1}_{\{k_{i}=m_{i},\,\,\forall i\}}
as
q\to\infty
by the uniqueness of prime factorization, which yields the equivalence of the moment conditions
∎

Next let us define for non-prime positive integer
n=\prod_{i=1}^{k}p_{i}^{\alpha_{i}}
the random variables

\omega_{n}:=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\omega_{p_{i}}^{\alpha_{i}}.

(2.4)

Then we have:

Lemma 2.2

For
N,n_{1},n_{2},\dots,n_{N}\in\mathbb{N}
we have

(\chi_{q}(n_{1}),\chi_{q}(n_{2}),\dots,\chi_{q}(n_{N}))\overset{d}{\to}(\omega_{n_{1}},\omega_{n_{2}},\dots,\omega_{n_{N}}),

(2.5)

as
q\to\infty
.

Furthermore, the collection
\{\omega_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
has the following moment structure. For
k_{i},m_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
and
i=1,2,\dots,N
we have

\mathbb{E}[\omega_{n_{1}}^{k_{1}}\overline{\omega_{n_{1}}}^{m_{1}}\omega_{n_{2}}^{k_{2}}\overline{\omega_{n_{2}}}^{m_{2}}\dots\omega_{n_{N}}^{k_{N}}\overline{\omega_{n_{N}}}^{m_{N}}]=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if the exponents of distinct primes in the}\\
&\text{prime factorization of $n_{1}^{k_{1}}n_{2}^{k_{2}}\dots n_{N}^{k_{N}}$ and}\\
&\text{$n_{1}^{m_{1}}n_{2}^{m_{2}}\dots n_{N}^{m_{N}}$ match.}\\
0,&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

(2.6)

In particular,
\omega_{n}
and
\omega_{m}
are orthogonal, that is,

\mathbb{E}[\omega_{n}\overline{\omega_{m}}]=\delta_{n,m}.

(2.7)

Proof.

The convergence follows from the multiplicative property of Dirichlet characters
1.11
and the continuous function theorem, see for example
[
19
, Theorem 4.27]
. The moment structure and orthogonality is clear from the computation
2.2
.
∎

Next we have the Fourier representation of
L_{q}(f)
defined in
A
.

Lemma 2.3

For
\chi_{q}\neq\chi_{0}
and
f\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
we have

L_{q}(f)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\chi_{q}(n)}{n^{\frac{1}{2}}}\hat{f}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\log(n)\right)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\chi_{q}(n)}{n^{\frac{1}{2}}}\hat{f}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\log(n)\right),

(2.8)

where

\hat{f}(k)=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)e^{-i2\pi xk}\operatorname{d}\!x

(2.9)

is the Fourier transform of
f
.

Proof.

Recall that the series defining the
L
-function
L(s,\chi)
for non-principal characters
\chi
converges uniformly in compact subsets of the half plane
\mathrm{Re}\,(s)>0
. Thus, since the support of
f
is compact we simply compute

\begin{split}L_{q}(f)&=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\chi_{q}(n)}{n^{\frac{1}{2}+ix}}\operatorname{d}\!x=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\chi_{q}(n)}{n^{\frac{1}{2}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)e^{-ix\log(n)}\operatorname{d}\!x=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\chi_{q}(n)}{n^{\frac{1}{2}}}\hat{f}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\log(n)\right).\end{split}

(2.10)

This proves the claim.
∎

By the smoothness of
f
we have for
n\in\mathbb{N}

\left|\hat{f}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\log(n)\right)\right|\leq\begin{cases}C_{k}(\log(n))^{-k},\,\text{ if $n\geq 2$}\\
\hat{f}(0),\qquad\qquad\text{ if $n=1$}\end{cases}

(2.11)

for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
with some constants
C_{k}>0
. Furthermore, we note for later use that we can always also use the cruder estimate

\left|\hat{f}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\log(n)\right)\right|\leq C

(2.12)

for some constant
C>0
and all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
In addition, recall that
\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}1/(n(\log(n))^{a})
converges for any
a>1
. Together these two facts yield the following simple fact that we are also going to need

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{|\hat{f}((1/2\pi)\log(n))|^{2}}{n}<\infty.

(2.13)

We also need couple of sum estimates.

Lemma 2.4

For any function
f\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{C}
and
m,s\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
we have

\sum_{r,t=1}^{q-1}\mathbf{1}_{(mq+r,q)=1}\mathbf{1}_{(sq+t,q)=1}\left(\delta_{r,t}-\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\right)f(r)=0,

(2.14)

where
\varphi
is the Euler totient function. Furthermore, for any integers
a,b\geq 0
we have

\frac{1}{q}\sum_{r,t=1}^{q-1}\mathbf{1}_{(mq+r,q)=1}\mathbf{1}_{(sq+t,q)=1}\left|\left(\delta_{r,t}-\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\right)\left(\frac{r}{q}\right)^{a}\left(\frac{t}{q}\right)^{b}\right|=\mathcal{O}(1),

(2.15)

where the implied constant is independent of
q
.
Lastly, for an integer
a\geq 0
we have

\frac{1}{q^{\frac{1}{2}}}\sum_{r,t=1}^{q-1}\mathbf{1}_{(mq+r,q)=1}\mathbf{1}_{(sq+t,q)=1}\left|\left(\delta_{r,t}-\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\right)r^{-\frac{1}{2}-ix}\left(\frac{t}{q}\right)^{a}\right|=\mathcal{O}(1),

(2.16)

where the implied constant is independent of
q
and
x
.

Remark 2.5
.

In the first and last statement we can obviously interchange the role of
r
and
t
.

Proof.

Since we obviously have
(q,r)=1\Leftrightarrow(mq+r,q)=1
for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
and
r\leq q-1
from now on we will replace
\mathbf{1}_{(mq+r,q)=1}
with
\mathbf{1}_{(r,q)=1}
everywhere. The first statement is a simple computation

\begin{split}\sum_{r,t=1}^{q-1}\mathbf{1}_{(r,q)=1}\mathbf{1}_{(t,q)=1}\left(\delta_{r,t}-\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\right)f(r)&=\sum_{r=1}^{q-1}\mathbf{1}_{(r,q)=1}f(r)-\underbrace{\left(\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\sum_{t=1}^{q-1}\mathbf{1}_{(t,q)=1}\right)}_{=1}\sum_{r=1}^{q-1}\mathbf{1}_{(r,q)=1}f(r)=0.\end{split}

(2.17)

For the second statement we need to bound each term separately. Let us write

\begin{split}\frac{1}{q}\sum_{r,t=1}^{q-1}&\mathbf{1}_{(r,q)=1}\mathbf{1}_{(t,q)=1}\left|\left(\delta_{r,t}-\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\right)\left(\frac{r}{q}\right)^{a}\left(\frac{t}{q}\right)^{b}\right|\\
&\leq\frac{1}{q^{a+b+1}}\sum_{r=1}^{q-1}\mathbf{1}_{(r,q)=1}r^{a+b}+\frac{1}{q^{a+b+1}\varphi(q)}\sum_{r=1}^{q-1}\mathbf{1}_{(r,q)=1}r^{a}\sum_{t=1}^{q-1}\mathbf{1}_{(t,q)=1}t^{b}\end{split}

(2.18)

Each sum above has
\varphi(q)
terms and each term
x^{c}
,
x=r,t
and
c=a,b,a+b
is bounded by
q^{c}
. Thus we have an upper bound
\mathcal{O}(\varphi(q)/q)=\mathcal{O}(1)
for both terms. Note that
\varphi(q)/q\leq 1-1/q\leq 1
for all
q
and the first inequality is an equality only if
q
is a prime.

For the last claim we have

\begin{split}\frac{1}{q^{\frac{1}{2}}}&\sum_{r,t=1}^{q-1}\mathbf{1}_{(r,q)=1}\mathbf{1}_{(t,q)=1}\left|\left(\delta_{r,t}-\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\right)r^{-\frac{1}{2}-ix}\left(\frac{t}{q}\right)^{a}\right|\\
&\leq\frac{1}{q^{\frac{1}{2}+a}}\sum_{r=1}^{q-1}\mathbf{1}_{(r,q)=1}r^{-\frac{1}{2}+a}+\left(\frac{1}{q^{a}\varphi(q)}\sum_{t=1}^{q-1}\mathbf{1}_{(t,q)=1}t^{a}\right)\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\sum_{r=1}^{q-1}\frac{\mathbf{1}_{(r,q)=1}}{\sqrt{r}}\right).\end{split}

(2.19)

The first factor of the second term is
\mathcal{O}(1)
uniformly in
q
since the sum again has
\varphi(q)
terms, which are bounded by
q^{a}
. Then consider the remaining sums. In these we can set the indicators to equal
1
. For the first term with
a=0
and the second factor in the second term we have

\begin{split}\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\sum_{r=1}^{q-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{r}}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\int_{0}^{q-1}\frac{\operatorname{d}\!x}{\sqrt{x}}=\frac{2\sqrt{q-1}}{\sqrt{q}}\leq 2.\end{split}

(2.20)

Furthermore, for the first term with
a\geq 1
we have

\frac{1}{q^{\frac{1}{2}+a}}\sum_{r=1}^{q-1}r^{-\frac{1}{2}+a}\leq\frac{1}{q^{a}}\sum_{r=1}^{q-1}r^{a-1}\leq\frac{(q-1)^{a}}{q^{a}}=\mathcal{O}(1)

(2.21)

since
q^{-\frac{1}{2}}<r^{-\frac{1}{2}}
for all
r\in\{1,2,\dots,q-1\}
and there are
q-1
terms each bounded by
(q-1)^{a-1}
.
∎

Proof of

Firstly, we have

\begin{split}E_{q,M}&:=\mathbb{E}\left[\left|L_{q}(f)-\mathcal{L}_{M,q}(f)\right|^{2}\right]\\

&=:E_{q,M}^{(1)}+E_{q,M}^{(2)},\end{split}

where

Let us first consider the second term

(3.13)

For
S_{M}
we have

\begin{split}S_{M}\leq\sum_{r=M+1}^{q-1}\frac{\left|\hat{f}((1/2\pi)\log(r))\right|^{2}}{r}+\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varphi(q)}}\sum_{r=M+1}^{q-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{r}}\hat{f}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\log(r)\right)\right|^{2}\end{split}

(3.15)

As
q\to\infty
the first term converges to the tail of a convergent series by
2.3
, which vanishes as
M\to\infty
.

Without loss of generality we may assume that
M+1<\lfloor\sqrt{q-1}\rfloor
so that we have

\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varphi(q)}}\sum_{r=M+1}^{q-1}\frac{\hat{f}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\log(r)\right)}{\sqrt{r}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varphi(q)}}\sum_{r=M+1}^{\lfloor\sqrt{q-1}\rfloor}\frac{\hat{f}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\log(r)\right)}{\sqrt{r}}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varphi(q)}}\sum_{r=\lfloor\sqrt{q-1}\rfloor+1}^{q-1}\frac{\hat{f}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\log(r)\right)}{\sqrt{r}}

(3.16)

Now we can consider each term separately

\begin{split}\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varphi(q)}}\sum_{r=M+1}^{\lfloor\sqrt{q-1}\rfloor}\frac{\hat{f}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\log(r)\right)}{\sqrt{r}}\right|&\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{\varphi(q)}}\int_{M}^{\lfloor\sqrt{q-1}\rfloor}\frac{\operatorname{d}\!x}{\sqrt{x}}\leq\frac{C\sqrt{\lfloor\sqrt{q-1}\rfloor}}{\sqrt{\varphi(q)}}\overset{*}{\lesssim}Cq^{-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\delta}{2}}\sqrt{\lfloor\sqrt{q-1}\rfloor}\overset{q\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}0,\end{split}

(3.17)

for any
0<\delta<1/2
and some constant
C>0
. Above we have used the constant bound for the Fourier transform from
2.12
and the decreasing property of
x\mapsto 1/\sqrt{x}
. The inequality
*
holds since
\varphi(q)/q^{1-\delta}\to\infty
for any
\delta>0

[
15
, Theorem 327]
. We also allowed the constant
C
to change from line to line and we will continue to do this, when needed to avoid cumbersome notations with multiple constants that we do not need later.

For the other term we need a different lower bound for
\varphi
. The lower bound we will use is

\varphi(q)>\frac{q}{e^{\gamma}\log(q)+3/\log\log(q)}

(3.18)

for
q\geq 3

[
5
, Theorem 8.8.7]
. Using
3.18
,
2.11
and similar reasoning as above with the decreasing property of
x\mapsto 1/(\log(x))
we have

\begin{split}\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varphi(q)}}\sum_{r=\lfloor\sqrt{q-1}\rfloor+1}^{q-1}\frac{\hat{f}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\log(r)\right)}{\sqrt{r}}\right|&\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{\varphi(q)}}\sum_{r=\lfloor\sqrt{q-1}\rfloor+1}^{q-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{r}\log(r)}\\
&\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{\varphi(q)}}\frac{1}{\log(\lfloor\sqrt{q-1}\rfloor)}\int_{\lfloor\sqrt{q-1}\rfloor}^{q-1}\frac{\operatorname{d}\!x}{\sqrt{x}}\\
&\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{q}}\frac{\sqrt{\log(q)+(\log\log(q))^{-1}}}{\log(\lfloor\sqrt{q-1}\rfloor)}\sqrt{q-1}\\
&\overset{q\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}0\end{split}

(3.19)

for some constant
C>0
since
1/\log(\lfloor\sqrt{q-1}\rfloor)\lesssim(\frac{1}{2}\log(q-1))^{-1}
.

Next let us estimate
S_{L,L}
. We have by writing out the Fourier transforms again

\begin{split}S_{L,L}&:=\sum_{m=1}^{L}\sum_{s=1}^{L}\sum_{r,t=1}^{q-1}\left(\delta_{r,t}-\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\right)\frac{\mathbf{1}_{(r,q)=1}}{\sqrt{mq+r}}\frac{\mathbf{1}_{(t,q)=1}}{\sqrt{sq+t}}\hat{f}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\log(mq+r)\right)\overline{\hat{f}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\log(sq+t)\right)}\\
&\,=\sum_{m=1}^{L}\sum_{s=1}^{L}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\,\mathrm{d}x\,\mathrm{d}x^{\prime}f(x)f(x^{\prime})\underbrace{\sum_{r,t=1}^{q-1}\mathbf{1}_{(r,q)=1}\mathbf{1}_{(t,q)=1}\left(\delta_{r,t}-\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\right)\frac{(mq+r)^{-ix}(sq+t)^{ix^{\prime}}}{\sqrt{mq+r}\sqrt{sq+t}}}_{:=F_{m,s,q}(x,x^{\prime})}.\end{split}

(3.20)

Next consider just the function

F
. We can use first order Taylor estimates with large
q

\frac{1}{\sqrt{mq+r}\sqrt{sq+t}}=\frac{1}{q}\frac{1}{\sqrt{ms}}\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{r}{mq}\right)\right)\left(1+\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{t}{sq}\right)\right)

(3.21)

and

\begin{split}(mq+r)^{-ix}(sq+t)^{ix^{\prime}}&=(mq)^{-ix}(sq)^{ix^{\prime}}(1+r(mq)^{-1})^{-ix}(1+t(sq)^{-1})^{ix^{\prime}}\\
&=(mq)^{-ix}(sq)^{ix^{\prime}}(1+\mathcal{O}(xr(mq)^{-1}))(1+\mathcal{O}(x^{\prime}t(sq)^{-1}))\end{split}

(3.22)

Then we have

\begin{split}F_{m,s,q}(x,x^{\prime})=\frac{(mq)^{-ix}(sq)^{ix^{\prime}}}{\sqrt{mq}\sqrt{sq}}\sum_{r,t=1}^{q-1}&\mathbf{1}_{(r,q)=1}\mathbf{1}_{(t,q)=1}\left(\delta_{r,t}-\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\right)(1+\mathcal{O}(r(mq)^{-1}))(1+\mathcal{O}(t(sq)^{-1}))\\
&\qquad\times(1+\mathcal{O}(xr(mq)^{-1}))(1+\mathcal{O}(x^{\prime}t(sq)^{-1})).\end{split}

(3.23)

By the first statement of
Lemma

2.4
we can ignore the terms, where we would only pick
r
- or
t
-dependent terms since these vanish summing over
r
and
t
. Then by the second statement of the same lemma we obtain

F_{m,s,q}(x,x^{\prime})\leq C(1+|x|+|x^{\prime}|+|x||x^{\prime}|)m^{-3/2}s^{-3/2},

(3.24)

for some universal constant
C>0
. Therefore, we have the uniform (in
q
) upper bound for each term in the sum
S_{L,L}:=\sum_{m,s=1}^{L}A_{m,s}(q)
(here we only define a notation for the terms of the sum
S_{L,L}
) given by

|A_{m,s}(q)|\leq C\frac{\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}+2\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}+\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}}{m^{-\frac{3}{2}}s^{-\frac{3}{2}}}:=B_{m,s},

(3.25)

for some universal constant
C>0
. Above
g(x)=|x|f(x)
. Furthermore
\sum_{m,s=1}^{\infty}B_{m,s}<\infty
Thus, the convergence is uniform in
q
so that we may take the
q\to\infty
from
S_{\infty,\infty}
term by term.

Then the strategy is to show that the logarithmic decay from the Fourier terms will make each term converge to zero. We need to show that
\limsup_{q\to\infty}|A_{m,s}(q)|=0
for all
m,s\in\mathbb{N}
. The terms
A_{m,s}(q)
are defined by

A_{m,s}(q):=\sum_{r,t=1}^{q-1}\left(\delta_{r,t}-\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\right)\frac{\mathbf{1}_{(r,q)=1}}{\sqrt{mq+r}}\frac{\mathbf{1}_{(t,q)=1}}{\sqrt{sq+t}}\hat{f}((1/2\pi)\log(mq+r))\overline{\hat{f}((1/2\pi)\log(sq+t))}.

(3.26)

Using the triangle inequality we have

\begin{split}|A_{m,s}(q)|&\leq\sum_{r=1}^{q-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{mq+r}\sqrt{sq+r}}|\hat{f}((1/2\pi)\log(mq+r))|\left|\overline{\hat{f}((1/2\pi)\log(sq+r))}\right|\\
&\quad+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varphi(q)}}\sum_{r=1}^{q-1}\frac{|\hat{f}((1/2\pi)\log(mq+r))|}{\sqrt{mq+r}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varphi(q)}}\sum_{t=1}^{q-1}\frac{|\hat{f}((1/2\pi)\log(sq+t))|}{\sqrt{sq+t}}\\
&=:A_{1}(q)+A_{2}^{(1)}(q)A_{2}^{(2)}(q)\end{split}

(3.27)

with obvious identifications. Then using again
2.11
we obtain for the first term

A_{1}(q)\leq\sum_{r=1}^{q-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{mq+r}\log(mq+r)}\frac{1}{\sqrt{sq+r}\log(sq+r)}

(3.28)

Now using the fact that for
g(x)=1/\sqrt{x}
or
g(x)=1/(\log(x))
we have
g(xq+y)\leq g(q)
for all
x,y\in\mathbb{N}
. Thus, we obtain

A_{1}(q)\leq(q-1)\mathcal{O}([q\log(q)^{2}]^{-1})\overset{q\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}0,

(3.29)

for fixed
m,s\geq 1
. Then it is enough to show that both
A_{2}^{(i)}(q)
,
i=1,2
vanish individually in the limit
q\to\infty
. Now using
g(xq+y)\leq g(y)
for
x,q\geq 1
and
g
as above we have

|A_{2}^{(1)}(q)|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varphi(q)}}\sum_{r=1}^{q-1}\frac{|\hat{f}((1/2\pi)\log(mq+r))|}{\sqrt{r}}.

(3.30)

This just corresponds to the second term in the bound for
S_{M}
in
3.15
with
M=0
and
r\to mq+r
in the Fourier term. The argument there works here with the substitution
M=0
and using the property of
g
above to show that
|A_{2}^{(1)}(q)|\to 0
as
q\to\infty
(
A_{2}^{(2)}(q)
is completely analogous). Thus,
\limsup_{q\to\infty}A_{m,s}(q)=0
and furthermore
\limsup_{q\to\infty}S_{\infty,\infty}=0
.

Consider then
S_{L}
(
\overline{S_{L}}
is similar). In this case, we can write

S_{L}=\sum_{m=1}^{L}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)f(x^{\prime})F_{m,0,q}(x,x^{\prime})\operatorname{d}\!x\operatorname{d}\!x^{\prime},

(3.31)

where

\begin{split}F_{m,0,q}(x,x^{\prime})&=\sum_{r,t=1}^{q-1}\mathbf{1}_{(r,q)=1}\mathbf{1}_{(t,q)=1}\left(\delta_{r,t}-\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\right)\frac{(mq+r)^{-ix}t^{ix^{\prime}}}{\sqrt{mq+r}\sqrt{t}}\\
&=\frac{(mq)^{ix}}{\sqrt{mq}}\sum_{r,t=1}^{q-1}\mathbf{1}_{(r,q)=1}\mathbf{1}_{(t,q)=1}\left(\delta_{r,t}-\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\right)t^{ix-\frac{1}{2}}(1+\mathcal{O}(r(mq)^{-1}))(1+\mathcal{O}(xr(mq)^{-1}))\end{split}

(3.32)

By the first statement of
Lemma

2.4
we can again ignore the terms where we pick
1
from either of the last two factors above. Then the third statement of the same lemma yields that

F_{m,0,q}(x,x^{\prime})\leq C(1+|x|)s^{-3/2},

(3.33)

for some universal
C>0
. Now writing
S_{L}:=\sum_{m=1}^{L}A_{m}(q)
we have a uniform (in
q
) upper bound for the individual terms

|A_{m}(q)|\leq C\frac{\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}+\left\lVert g\right\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}}{m^{-\frac{3}{2}}}:=B_{m}

(3.34)

with
g(x)=|x|f(x)
and some universal constant
C>0
. Since
\sum_{s=1}^{\infty}B_{m}<\infty
the convergence is again uniform and we can take
q
-limit term by term from
S_{\infty}
.

Now the terms
A_{m}(q)
are defined by

A_{m}(q):=\sum_{r,t=1}^{q-1}\left(\delta_{r,t}-\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\right)\frac{\mathbf{1}_{(r,q)=1}}{\sqrt{mq+r}}\frac{\mathbf{1}_{(t,q)=1}}{\sqrt{t}}\hat{f}((1/2\pi)\log(mq+r))\overline{\hat{f}((1/2\pi)\log(t))}.

(3.35)

and we have

\begin{split}|A_{m}(q)|&\leq\sum_{r=1}^{q-1}\frac{|\hat{f}((1/2\pi)\log(mq+r))|\overline{\hat{f}((1/2\pi)\log(r))}|}{\sqrt{mq+r}\sqrt{r}}\\
&\quad+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varphi(q)}}\sum_{r=1}^{q-1}\frac{|\hat{f}((1/2\pi)\log(mq+r))|}{\sqrt{mq+r}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varphi(q)}}\sum_{t=1}^{q-1}\frac{|\overline{\hat{f}((1/2\pi)\log(t))}|}{\sqrt{t}}\end{split}

(3.36)

The first term can be estimated similarly as
A_{1}(q)
in the estimate for
A_{m,s}(q)
above. The individual factors in the second term can be estimated similarly as
A_{2}^{(i)}(q)
,
i=1,2
in the estimate for
A_{m,s}(q)
. Therefore, we have
\limsup_{q\to\infty}A_{m}(q)=0
and furthermore
\limsup_{q\to\infty}S_{\infty}=\limsup_{q\to\infty}\overline{S_{\infty}}=0
. This finishes the proof.

3.2
Proof of
Proposition

3.3

Since the random variables
\chi_{q}(n)
converge jointly in law to
\omega_{n}
as
q\to\infty
by
Lemma

2.2
, the continuous function theorem (see for example
[
19
, Theorem 4.27]
) yields that it is enough to prove that the function
F\colon\mathbb{T}^{M}\to\mathbb{C}
defined by

F(\mathbf{z}):=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)\sum_{n=1}^{M}\frac{z_{n}}{n^{\frac{1}{2}+ix}}\operatorname{d}\!x,

(3.37)

where
\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},z_{2},\dots,z_{N})
is continuous. This is obvious since the sum is finite.

3.3
Proof of
Proposition

3.4

First of all we have

\prod_{p\leq M}(1-\omega_{p}p^{-\frac{1}{2}-ix})^{-1}=\sum_{p\mid n\Rightarrow p\leq M}\frac{\omega_{n}}{n^{-\frac{1}{2}-ix}}.

(3.38)

Furthermore,

\left|\sum_{n=1}^{M_{1}}\frac{\omega_{n}}{n^{\frac{1}{2}+ix}}\right|\leq\sum_{n=1}^{M_{1}}n^{-\frac{1}{2}}<\infty

(3.39)

for a fixed
M_{1}
. Similarly

\left|\sum_{p\mid n\Rightarrow p\leq M_{2}}\frac{\omega_{n}}{n^{\frac{1}{2}+ix}}\right|\leq\sum_{p\mid n\Rightarrow p\leq M_{2}}\frac{1}{n^{\frac{1}{2}}}=\prod_{p\leq M_{2}}(1-p^{-\frac{1}{2}})^{-1}<\infty

(3.40)

for fixed
M_{2}
. Therefore the sums involved in the following computation are deterministically and uniformly (in the integration variables) bounded. Thus, we have

\begin{split}E(M_{1},M_{2}):=&\mathbb{E}\left[\left|\sum_{n=1}^{M_{1}}\frac{\omega_{n}\hat{f}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\log(n)\right)}{n^{\frac{1}{2}}}-\sum_{p\mid n\Rightarrow p\leq M_{2}}\frac{\omega_{n}\hat{f}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\log(n)\right)}{n^{\frac{1}{2}}}\right|^{2}\right]\\
&=\sum_{n,m=1}^{M_{1}}\frac{\mathbb{E}[\omega_{n}\overline{\omega_{m}}]\hat{f}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\log(n)\right)\overline{\hat{f}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\log(m)\right)}}{n^{\frac{1}{2}}m^{\frac{1}{2}}}\\
&\quad-\sum_{n=1}^{M_{1}}\sum_{p\mid n\Rightarrow p\leq M_{2}}\frac{\mathbb{E}[\omega_{n}\overline{\omega_{m}}]\hat{f}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\log(n)\right)\overline{\hat{f}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\log(m)\right)}}{n^{\frac{1}{2}}m^{\frac{1}{2}}}\\
&\quad-\sum_{n=1}^{M_{1}}\sum_{p\mid n\Rightarrow p\leq M_{2}}\frac{\mathbb{E}[\omega_{n}\overline{\omega_{m}}]\overline{\hat{f}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\log(n)\right)}\hat{f}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\log(m)\right)}{n^{\frac{1}{2}}m^{\frac{1}{2}}}\\
&\quad+\sum_{p\mid n\Rightarrow p\leq M_{2}}\sum_{p\mid m\Rightarrow p\leq M_{2}}\frac{\mathbb{E}[\omega_{n}\overline{\omega_{m}}]\hat{f}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\log(n)\right)\overline{\hat{f}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\log(m)\right)}}{n^{\frac{1}{2}}m^{\frac{1}{2}}}.\end{split}

(3.41)

Then recall the orthogonality
\mathbb{E}[\omega_{n}\overline{\omega_{m}}]=\delta_{n,m}
from
Lemma

2.2
. Thus, the above becomes

E(M_{1},M_{2})=\sum_{n=1}^{M_{1}}\frac{\left|\hat{f}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\log(n)\right)\right|^{2}}{n}-2\sum_{n=1}^{M_{1}}\mathbf{1}_{p\mid n\Rightarrow p\leq M_{2}}\frac{\left|\hat{f}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\log(n)\right)\right|^{2}}{n}+\sum_{p\mid n\Rightarrow p\leq M_{2}}\frac{\left|\hat{f}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\log(n)\right)\right|^{2}}{n}

(3.42)

The first term converges to a finite limit by
2.13
. In the second sum we can take the
M_{2}
limit either first or after the
M_{1}
limit, because the limit
\mathbf{1}_{p\mid n\Rightarrow p\leq M_{2}}\to 1
as
M_{2}\to\infty
is monotonic and the summands are positive. This yields that the sum converges to the same finite object as the first term. The last term also converges to the same object by monotonone convergence.
