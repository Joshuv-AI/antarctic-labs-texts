National trends in neonatal diagnoses recorded at hospital discharge in Kazakhstan, 2014–2024
Authors: Balzhan N Tatibekova, Adema K Sagatbaeva, Bauyrzhan N Tatibekov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDeclining neonatal mortality has increased the number of surviving newborns; however, in health systems without validated neonatal morbidity indicators, population-level patterns of diagnoses recorded at hospital discharge remain poorly characterised.ObjectiveTo characterise national trends in neonatal mortality, survival among infants with extremely low and very low birth weight, and International Classification of Diseases 10th revision (ICD-10) diagnoses recorded at discharge among surviving newborns in Kazakhstan from 2014 to 2024.MethodsWe conducted a nationwide retrospective population-based study using de-identified administrative health data covering all live births in Kazakhstan from 2014 to 2024. Temporal trends in neonatal mortality, survival among infants with extremely low and very low birth weight and recorded primary ICD-10 diagnoses at discharge were assessed.ResultsAmong 4 437 357 live births, neonatal mortality decreased from 6.2 to 4.0 per 1000 live births, whereas the proportion of surviving newborns with a recorded primary diagnosis at discharge increased from 4.9% to 16.7% (relative risk 3.41; 95% CI 3.36 to 3.46; p<0.001). By 2024, P91, P59 and P22 accounted for 88.4% of recorded primary diagnoses. The increase in P91 diagnoses occurred without a corresponding increase in the proportion of newborns weighing <2500 g.ConclusionDeclining neonatal mortality was accompanied by an increase and a changing distribution of recorded diagnoses among surviving newborns. These data cannot determine individual post-discharge follow-up needs but may provide an initial population-level basis for planning subsequent clinical risk stratification after discharge.Keywords: Child; Neonatology; Infant, Low Birth Weight

Introduction

As neonatal mortality declines, increasing numbers of infants survive complications of prematurity, perinatal asphyxia, respiratory disease and congenital anomalies.1 2 Mortality alone therefore provides an increasingly incomplete measure of neonatal outcomes because it does not capture the number and distribution of conditions among survivors or their subsequent healthcare needs.3 4

In high-income countries, this transition has been accompanied by the development of additional measures of neonatal outcomes. Population-based and administrative hospital data have been used to develop and validate composite indicators of severe neonatal morbidity, complementing mortality surveillance with population-level assessment of adverse outcomes among survivors.5–7 Structured follow-up of high-risk infants has also become part of post-neonatal care in several health systems.8–10

WHO emphasises continuity of care after discharge for preterm and low birth weight infants and other small and sick newborns.11 12 However, progress from improved survival towards systematic outcome monitoring, consistently defined indicators13 14 and organised post-discharge follow-up remains uneven.15 Existing recommendations and individual programmes do not always constitute nationally coordinated systems for standardised follow-up and collection of long-term outcomes. Different models are emerging, ranging from centre-based and hybrid follow-up programmes for preterm infants in Brazil to a nationally coordinated network using a standardised neonatal follow-up protocol in China.16 17

WHO also emphasises strengthening routine health information systems and standardising indicators for monitoring small and sick newborns.18 Where validated national indicators are unavailable, routinely collected administrative data can provide an initial population-level assessment of recorded neonatal outcomes and inform subsequent risk stratification and health-service planning.19 Indicators derived from such data require validation within the health system in which they are applied.20

Kazakhstan, an upper-middle-income country, provides a relevant setting in which to examine this transition. Neonatal mortality has declined to 4.0 per 1000 live births,21 but national research has focused predominantly on mortality.22 The number, distribution and temporal trends of recorded discharge diagnoses among surviving newborns have not been characterised nationally.

Thus, the scale, distribution and temporal changes in recorded diagnoses among surviving newborns in Kazakhstan remain poorly characterised. We aimed to characterise 11-year national trends in the number and distribution of International Classification of Diseases 10th revision (ICD-10) diagnoses recorded at discharge among surviving newborns and to assess the utility of routinely collected administrative data for population-level planning of subsequent clinical risk stratification.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a nationwide retrospective population-based study based on routinely collected administrative data of the health system of the Republic of Kazakhstan. The observation period covered data from 1 January 2014 to 31 December 2024.

Kazakhstan is an upper-middle-income country in Central Asia with a regionalised perinatal care system. Since 2008, the country has implemented WHO-recommended live birth criteria, including the registration and care of infants born at ≥22 weeks of gestation or weighing ≥500 g at birth. At the same time, a three-level perinatal care system was introduced, providing referral pathways for high-risk pregnancies and newborns to specialised higher-level facilities, including regional and national perinatal centres. This organisational structure was considered when interpreting national trends in neonatal mortality, survival and recorded neonatal morbidity, as the distribution of clinical workload and diagnostic capabilities may differ across levels of care.

Study population and data sources

The study population included all live births registered in the national healthcare system of Kazakhstan between 2014 and 2024. Data were obtained from the anonymised national administrative database of the Republican Center for Electronic Health. The database contained information on live births, birth weight and primary discharge diagnoses coded according to the ICD-10, without individual patient identifiers.

Stillbirths and records with critically incomplete data that precluded reliable classification of the main study outcomes were excluded from the analysis.

To assess the coverage of the administrative database, additional information on healthcare facilities involved in neonatal care and the reporting of neonatal ICD-10 discharge diagnoses was analysed. These data were used to describe the structure of the maternity and neonatal care network and to interpret trends in recorded neonatal morbidity within the context of the regionalised perinatal care system.

Variables and outcomes

The primary outcomes were national trends in neonatal mortality, survival among infants with extremely low birth weight (ELBW) and very low birth weight (VLBW) and primary ICD-10 diagnoses recorded at discharge among surviving newborns.

Birth weight was classified according to WHO criteria as ELBW (<1000 g), VLBW (1000–1499 g) and low birth weight (1500–2499 g).

Recorded neonatal morbidity was defined as the primary ICD-10 diagnosis documented at hospital discharge among newborns who survived neonatal hospitalisation. Diagnoses were analysed by ICD-10 category and, where relevant, by subcategory to characterise their distribution and temporal trends. Because only the primary discharge diagnosis was available, coexisting conditions and disease severity could not be assessed.

Major diagnostic groups included neurological, respiratory, haemorrhagic, gastrointestinal, growth-related and congenital conditions. Post-discharge follow-up need was not an outcome of this study and was not inferred from individual diagnostic codes; its potential relevance was considered only in the interpretation of the findings.

Potential sources of bias

The findings should be interpreted in the context of the limitations inherent to routinely collected administrative data. Recorded neonatal morbidity was based on the primary ICD-10 discharge diagnosis and therefore did not capture the full spectrum of neonatal comorbidities or disease severity. Coding practices and completeness of diagnosis reporting may also have changed over the study period.

Because the study used aggregated national data without individual patient identifiers, interfacility transfers and potential duplicate records could not be identified and readmissions and long-term clinical outcomes could not be assessed. Transfers between facilities might therefore have resulted in repeated registration of the same newborn and overestimation of recorded cases for some conditions. The available data also did not allow assessment of individual post-discharge follow-up needs.

To mitigate these limitations, analyses were based on official national data with broad institutional coverage, and all aggregated datasets were assessed for completeness and internal consistency before statistical analysis.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Statistical analysis

Neonatal mortality rates were calculated per 1000 live births. Recorded neonatal morbidity was defined as the annual number of newborns discharged with a recorded primary ICD-10 diagnosis and was expressed as a percentage of all live births. The study was based on aggregated national statistics provided by the Republican Center for Electronic Health; therefore, information on individual missing records was not available.

Descriptive statistics and heatmaps were used to visualise longitudinal changes in the distribution of recorded ICD-10 diagnoses. Trends in recorded neonatal morbidity were assessed using the Cochran-Armitage test for trend and quantified using relative risks (RRs) with 95% CIs. Pearson correlation was used to assess the association between neonatal and infant mortality rates.

Because individual-level survival data for infants with birth weight <1500 g were not consistently available across all years, survival patterns among ELBW and VLBW infants were assessed using national aggregate counts.23

A scenario-based sensitivity analysis applying ±10% variation to aggregate estimates was performed to assess the robustness of the observed increase in the proportion of surviving newborns with a recorded primary discharge diagnosis.

Additionally, divergence analysis was performed by comparing the annual proportion of low birth weight infants (<2500 g) with the annual proportion of recorded P91 diagnoses to assess whether changes in recorded neurological morbidity were independent of shifts in birth weight distribution.

All analyses and visualisations were conducted using Python.

The study used anonymised aggregated administrative data and did not include individual patient identifiers.

Results

Healthcare facility characteristics

During the study period, the number of maternity facilities in Kazakhstan remained relatively stable, ranging from 244 to 260 organisations annually. District hospitals accounted for approximately two-thirds of all maternity facilities, representing between 65.0% and 68.9% of organisations each year. The proportion of facilities reporting neonatal diagnoses remained consistently high, ranging from 79.4% to 88.7% throughout the study period (online supplemental table 1).

The study therefore captured neonatal diagnoses reported across a regionalised perinatal care system comprising facilities with different levels of obstetrical and neonatal care.

Survival patterns among ELBW and VLBW infants

Among infants with ELBW, the number of recorded survivors increased from 320 in 2014 to 610 in 2024, while recorded deaths decreased from 980 to 750. Among infants with VLBW, recorded survivors increased from 1690 to 2665, while recorded deaths decreased from 760 to 515 (figure 2).

Clinical structure of recorded neonatal morbidity

Recorded primary discharge diagnoses were concentrated in several major ICD-10 diagnostic groups (figure 3). P91 remained the most frequently recorded diagnostic group throughout the study period, increasing from 11 283 cases in 2014 to 31 210 in 2024; changes within individual P91 subcategories are reported separately below. Respiratory distress of newborn (P22) increased from 3708 cases in 2014 to 9072 in 2024 (2.4-fold), whereas congenital pneumonia (P23) increased from 296 to 842 cases (2.8-fold; online supplemental table 2).

Heatmap of the top 10 recorded neonatal ICD-10 discharge diagnosis groups in Kazakhstan, 2014–2024. ICD, International Classification of Diseases.

The largest relative increase among the major diagnostic groups was observed for neonatal jaundice (P59), which increased from 1201 cases in 2014 to 12 918 in 2024 (10.8-fold) and became the second most frequently recorded diagnostic group after P91 by 2024. Within P59, the largest absolute increase was observed for P59.8, from 582 cases in 2014 to 9357 in 2024; P59.9 increased from 451 to 2890 cases and P59.0 from 158 to 668 (online supplemental table 2). By contrast, P05 showed a smaller increase in absolute numbers, from 2158 cases in 2014 to 3550 in 2024 (1.6-fold), while its proportion of all recorded primary discharge diagnoses decreased from 11.4% to 5.8%.

Among less frequently recorded diagnostic groups, non-traumatic intracranial haemorrhage (P52) increased from 183 cases in 2014 to 1392 in 2024 (7.6-fold). Within this group, P52.0 increased from 37 to 1040 cases, P52.1 from 37 to 189 and P52.2 from 72 to 100 (online supplemental table 2). Necrotising enterocolitis of newborn (P77) increased from 17 to 99 cases (5.8-fold), and congenital pneumonia (P23) increased from 296 to 842 cases (2.8-fold) over the study period.

Among the congenital anomaly groups examined, Q25 showed the largest increase, from 79 recorded cases in 2014 to 937 in 2024 (11.9-fold). This increase was predominantly accounted for by Q25.0, which increased from 51 to 807 cases (online supplemental table 2). Q35 and Q36 remained less frequently recorded, increasing from 53 to 108 cases and from 43 to 78 cases, respectively.

Recorded neurological diagnoses and trend divergence analysis

The distribution of recorded primary diagnoses within the P91 category changed substantially between 2014 and 2024 (online supplemental figure 4). Recorded P91.0 diagnoses decreased from 9836 in 2014 to 4607 in 2020, before increasing to 8305 in 2024. P91.8 remained relatively stable between 2014 and 2018 (431 cases in 2014 and 364 in 2018), before increasing to 1679 in 2019, 3043 in 2020, 4939 in 2021, 6862 in 2022, 10 410 in 2023 and 15 048 in 2024. In 2024, P91.8 was the most frequently recorded P91 subcategory, exceeding P91.0 (15 048 vs 8305 cases). This increase coincided with a marked change in the distribution of recorded P91 subcategories, with P91.8 becoming the predominant recorded subcategory by 2024.

Recorded P91.4 diagnoses increased from 612 in 2014 to 815 in 2019 and 5332 in 2022, with 4922 cases recorded in 2024. P91.3 increased from 355 cases in 2014 to 1083 in 2019 and 2543 in 2024. P91.6 was first recorded in the available dataset in 2019 (18 cases) and increased to 276 cases in 2024.

The remaining P91 subcategories were substantially less frequent throughout the study period. In 2024, 66 cases of P91.9, 23 of P91.5 and 20 of P91.1 were recorded, compared with 12, 21 and 10 cases, respectively, in 2014.

Temporal trends in recorded primary P91 diagnoses differed from those in the proportion of infants with low birth weight (figure 4). Between 2014 and 2024, the number of recorded P91 diagnoses increased, whereas the proportion of infants weighing less than 2500 g decreased from 5.9% to 4.9%. After 2019, recorded P91 diagnoses continued to increase while the proportion of infants weighing less than 2500 g continued to decline.

Discussion

This study provides the first national assessment of trends in diagnoses recorded at discharge among surviving newborns in Kazakhstan over an 11-year period of declining neonatal mortality. The principal finding was a divergence between two trends: neonatal mortality decreased from 6.2 to 4.0 per 1000 live births, whereas the proportion of surviving newborns with a recorded primary diagnosis at discharge increased from 4.9% to 16.7%, with the most pronounced increase occurring after 2019. The distribution of recorded diagnoses also changed, with P91, P59 and P22 predominating by the end of the study period. These findings reflect changes in recorded neonatal morbidity and should not be interpreted as equivalent changes in the underlying incidence of disease.

These trends occurred alongside improved survival among the most vulnerable newborns. By the end of the study period, the number of surviving ELBW and VLBW infants had increased as neonatal deaths declined, whereas the proportion of newborns weighing <2500 g did not increase. The increase in recorded diagnoses therefore cannot be explained solely by changes in the proportion of infants with low birth weight and occurred concurrently with improved survival among ELBW and VLBW infants.

The distribution of recorded conditions also changed over time. P91, P59 and P22 remained the leading diagnostic groups throughout the study period, whereas the relative contribution of P05 gradually declined. The absolute number of P05 diagnoses nevertheless remained substantial, suggesting that its declining relative contribution reflects a change in the overall distribution of recorded diagnoses rather than a reduction in the clinical importance of fetal growth restriction or being born small for gestational age.

Neurological diagnoses within P91 constituted the largest diagnostic category, with most of the increase attributable to P91.8. Severe neurological conditions, including neonatal coma (P91.5) and hypoxic-ischaemic encephalopathy (P91.6), remained relatively uncommon throughout the study period. P91.8 is a clinically heterogeneous category24; its increasing frequency should therefore not be interpreted as a proportional increase in severe central nervous system injury. The marked shift towards P91.8 after 2019 may also reflect changes in diagnostic coding practices or redistribution between P91 subcategories rather than a corresponding increase in the incidence of neurological disease. This heterogeneity also limits the extent to which individual risk can be inferred from an administrative diagnosis alone and underscores the need for subsequent clinical assessment.25

Neonatal jaundice (P59) also became one of the leading recorded diagnostic groups, with the increase largely attributable to P59.8 (neonatal jaundice from other specified causes). This marked increase should be interpreted cautiously. Changes in bilirubin screening, diagnostic ascertainment and coding practices might have increased the detection and recording of neonatal jaundice, although their contribution cannot be quantified from the available administrative data. Importantly, the clinical significance of hyperbilirubinaemia cannot be inferred from a P59 code alone. Current guidelines recommend predischarge bilirubin measurement and risk-based post-discharge follow-up according to bilirubin concentration, gestational age and risk factors for bilirubin neurotoxicity.26 Severe hyperbilirubinaemia can therefore have important implications for subsequent care, whereas many recorded cases might require only short-term surveillance. Because bilirubin concentrations, treatment thresholds, neurotoxicity risk factors and treatment data were unavailable in our dataset, the observed increase in P59 cannot be interpreted as a corresponding increase in severe hyperbilirubinaemia or in the number of infants requiring long-term developmental follow-up. Rather, these findings illustrate why recorded discharge diagnoses require subsequent clinical risk stratification to determine the appropriate intensity and duration of follow-up.

Respiratory distress syndrome (P22) remained another major diagnostic group, with its temporal pattern predominantly driven by P22.0, respiratory distress syndrome of the newborn. The clinical significance of this diagnosis depends substantially on gestational age, the need for surfactant and respiratory support, duration of ventilation and subsequent development of bronchopulmonary dysplasia.27 28 These clinical characteristics were not available and therefore disease severity or the proportion of infants requiring post-discharge follow-up could not be determined.

Beyond the leading diagnostic groups, increases in recorded haemorrhagic, gastrointestinal and congenital conditions identified smaller but clinically important groups among surviving newborns. Severe forms of these conditions can be associated with longer-term neurodevelopmental, nutritional and other healthcare needs after discharge.29–31

Taken together, the diagnostic patterns show that the recorded primary diagnosis at discharge does not capture comorbidity or disease severity32 and is therefore insufficient to determine an individual infant’s need for subsequent follow-up. At the population level, however, these data could provide an initial basis for planning subsequent clinical risk stratification and for developing and validating a national composite morbidity indicator.19

Interpretation of the increase in recorded diagnoses after 2019 requires particular caution. Several concurrent processes could have contributed to the observed trends, including changes in the underlying clinical burden, enhanced ascertainment and recording capacity and shifts in diagnostic and coding practices, as illustrated by the changing distribution of P91 subcategories and the marked rise in P91.8. This period coincided with the introduction of compulsory social health insurance and further digitalisation of the health system in Kazakhstan,33 which might have affected the completeness of diagnostic documentation and coding. Evidence from other health systems shows that implementation and integration of electronic health records can influence the completeness and patterns of diagnostic documentation and coding.34 35 Although the relative contributions of these processes cannot be quantified from the available data, improved ascertainment and recording are likely to have contributed importantly to the observed increase. Accordingly, the observed trends should be interpreted as changes in recorded diagnoses rather than as evidence of a corresponding increase in population-level disease incidence.

Nevertheless, the observed patterns suggest a potential role for diagnoses recorded at discharge as an initial source of information within post-discharge care pathways.25 In Kazakhstan, the existing primary healthcare and home-visiting system could provide a setting for continued assessment after discharge, with referral to specialised services when risk persists or is newly identified.36 Such continuity may be particularly relevant during the first 2 years of life, a period of rapid brain maturation and high neuroplasticity, when early identification of developmental concerns can facilitate timely intervention.37 38 It could also support continuity of preventive care, including timely immunisation.39

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the aggregated administrative data included only the primary ICD-10 diagnosis recorded at discharge and did not capture secondary diagnoses, coexisting conditions, disease severity or treatment received. The 16.7% of surviving newborns with a recorded primary diagnosis in 2024 should therefore not be interpreted as the prevalence of neonatal morbidity, because infants with multiple complications could contribute only one primary diagnosis. This limitation might underestimate both comorbidity burden and the clinical complexity of surviving newborns. Second, the use of aggregated, de-identified data precluded linkage of discharge diagnoses to subsequent hospital admissions, mortality or longer-term clinical and neurodevelopmental outcomes. Third, changes in documentation practices, healthcare financing, digitalisation and diagnostic coding, particularly after 2019, might have affected the completeness of diagnostic recording and, consequently, the observed temporal trends. Finally, survival estimates for ELBW and VLBW infants were derived from aggregated national data and should therefore be interpreted as population-level trends.

Conclusion

Declining neonatal mortality in Kazakhstan was accompanied by an increase and a changing distribution of recorded diagnoses among surviving newborns. Although the primary diagnosis at discharge cannot determine an individual infant’s need for subsequent follow-up, routinely collected administrative data can quantify patterns of recorded diagnoses at the population level and may inform planning for subsequent clinical risk stratification and differentiated post-discharge care.

Generalisability

The nationwide population-based design and 11-year study period enabled the characterisation of long-term trends in recorded neonatal diagnoses in Kazakhstan. The findings may be relevant to other settings with developing outcome-monitoring systems, where improvements in neonatal survival increase the importance of systematically assessing the post-discharge healthcare needs of survivors. However, direct generalisation of these estimates is limited by differences in the organisation of neonatal care, diagnostic coding practices and completeness of administrative data.
