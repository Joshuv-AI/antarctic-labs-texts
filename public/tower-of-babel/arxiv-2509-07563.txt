Path Integral Approach to Input–Output Theory
Aaron Daniel, Matteo Brunelli, Aashish A. Clerk, Patrick P. Potts

Abstract
Input–output theory is a well–known tool in quantum optics and ubiquitous in the description of quantum systems probed by light. Owing to the generality of the setup it describes, the theory finds application in a wide variety of experiments in circuit and cavity QED. We present an approach to input–output theory using the Schwinger-Keldysh path integral formalism that gives us direct access to the full output field statistics such as the first and second order coherence functions. By making the rich toolbox of non–equilibrium quantum field theory accessible, our formalism greatly simplifies the treatment of nonlinear systems and provides a uniform way of obtaining perturbative results. We showcase this particular strength by computing the output field statistics of a Kerr nonlinear oscillator at finite temperatures through the use of diagrams and diagram summation techniques. We find a reduction in reflection that is not due to photon leakage but rather associated to the squeezing of the output light.



I
Introduction

Numerous recent developments in quantum physics rely on light-matter coupling. On the one hand, light can be used for control and readout
Clerk et al. 2010
and on the other hand, the coupling to light can fundamentally alter the properties of quantum systems
Hübener et al. 2021
. For an enhanced coupling strength, as well as targeted control over the emitted light, quantum systems can be embedded in cavities, see Fig.
1
. Such setups are at the heart of the timely fields of circuit
Blais et al. 2021
and cavity QED
Reiserer and Rempe 2015
, optomechanics
Aspelmeyer et al. 2014
, as well as the emerging field of cavity materials engineering
Lu et al. 2025
;
Hübener et al. 2021
;
Mivehvar et al. 2021
, where the light-matter coupling is leveraged to alter the properties of many-body systems.
Theoretically, these setups require the use of driven-dissipative theories such as master equations
Breuer and Petruccione 2007
, nonequilibrium field theories
Kamenev 2011
, methods based on matrix-product states
Strathearn et al. 2018
, or a range of other techniques (see for instance Ref.
Mivehvar et al. 2021
). While these approaches are well established to describe the dynamics within the cavity, we are often interested in the properties of the light coming out of the cavity
Walls and Milburn 2025
;
Collett and Gardiner 1984
;
Gardiner and Collett 1985
;
Yurke 2004
. This light is what is typically measured and it can be further used as the input for another quantum system. Importantly, it may feature strikingly different behavior from the light within the cavity, for instance Wigner negativities for classical intra–cavity fields
Strandberg et al. 2021
or unlimited squeezing
Yurke 1984
;
Walls and Milburn 2025
.

Input-output theory allows for computing the light that leaves the cavity using quantum Langevin equations. While constituting a powerful formalism, these equations are difficult to work with for nonlinear systems, where approximation tools are needed.
In contrast to the dynamics within the cavity, there is a lack of theoretical methods for computing the light that leaves a nonlinear cavity. This constitutes a severe limitation in exploring the rich response of nonlinear systems embedded in cavities.
For a notable exception, see Refs.
Kiilerich and Mølmer 2020
;
Kiilerich and Mølmer 2019
, where a master equation approach is developed to describe the light leaving a cavity that is driven by pulses.

Figure 1:
Sketch of the general setup described by input–output theory. A system, placed inside a cavity and described by
\hat{H}_{S}
, is probed by light.
The incoming light is described by the input operator
\hat{b}_{\text{in}}
, the outgoing light which is measured by the output operator
\hat{b}_{\text{out}}
. Statistical quantities of the output light, such as the average output field
\langle\bout\rangle
or the second order coherence function
G^{(2)}
, can be determined perturbatively with diagrams in our approach.

Here we fill this gap by combining input–output theory with the Schwinger-Keldysh path integral formalism.
Using a dissipative action that only involves system degrees of freedom, and source fields for the output light, our formalism allows for calculating the statistical properties of the outgoing light, providing direct access to observables including the reflection, as well as the first and second order coherence functions.
Through our approach, a rich toolbox of perturbative techniques becomes accessible, facilitating the treatment of nonlinear systems. In particular, we make use of diagrams that greatly simplify calculations and show promise for intuitive explanations of the interactions between the system and output modes. With our approach we capture a reduction in the reflection coefficient of a coherently driven Kerr cavity that is not due to photon loss and that cannot be captured by standard mean field theory. For the same system, we further demonstrate partial summation techniques that emerge from the diagrammatic treatment of the system. Our approach thereby offers a systematic method for determining the output field statistics of nonlinear quantum systems and establishes a connection between input-output theory, predominantly used in quantum optics and optomechanics, and the broader field of non–equilibrium field theory.

The manuscript is structured as follows. In Sec.
II
we introduce the general framework of our path integral approach to input–output theory and showcase it with the example of an empty cavity. We then consider nonlinear systems, e.g. the Kerr oscillator, in our framework in Sec.
III
and in Sec.
IV
we detail our diagrammatic techniques. In Sec.
VI
we evaluate the perturbative results for the output field statistics of the Kerr oscillator and in Sec.
VII
we present a detailed derivation of the general formalism.

II
Introducing the Path Integral Approach to Input–Output Theory

II.1
Setup and Models

The general setting of a quantum system placed inside a cavity that interacts with light introduced in Fig.
1
is ubiquitous in modern physics and multiple approaches to modeling it exist. In this section we briefly mention existing approaches to modeling the intra–cavity system and then introduce our approach which focuses on the output field directly.
One of the most prominent tools to model the dynamics of dissipative quantum systems are Lindblad master equations. In this framework we model the setup presented in Fig.
1
as,

\derivative{t}\hat{\rho}=-i[\hat{H}_{S}+\hat{H}_{\mathrm{d}}(t),\hat{\rho}]+\kappa n_{B}\mathcal{D}[\hat{a}^{\dagger}]\hat{\rho}+\kappa(n_{B}+1)\mathcal{D}[\hat{a}]\hat{\rho}~,

(1)

where
\mathcal{D}[\hat{O}]\hat{\rho}=\hat{O}\hat{\rho}\hat{O}^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{\hat{O}^{\dagger}\hat{O}\}
. Here we introduced the system Hamiltonian,

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{S}

\displaystyle=\omega_{S}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}+\hat{H}_{\text{int}}~,

(2)

where
\hat{H}_{\rm int}
denotes any non–linear term in the Hamiltonian, as well as any additional degrees of freedom that couple to the cavity mode. The input in this framework is captured through the drive term,

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\mathrm{d}}(t)

\displaystyle=-i\sqrt{\kappa}\quantity(f(t) \dahat- \overline{f}(t) \ahat)~,

(3)

with the input operator appearing implicitly through its average as
f(t)=\langle\bin(t)\rangle
. The second and third term on the right-hand side of Eq. (
1
) describe the incoherent exchange of photons with the Markovian bath provided by the input and output modes, where the thermal occupation is given by

\langle\!\langle\dbin(t)\bin(t^{\prime})\rangle\!\rangle=\delta(t-t^{\prime})n_{B},

(4)

where
\langle\!\langle\hat{A}\hat{B}\rangle\!\rangle=\langle\hat{A}\hat{B}\rangle-\langle\hat{A}\rangle\langle\hat{B}\rangle
.
In this description we do not have access to the output field
\bout
, we only model the intra–cavity system.

In the framework of conventional input–output theory
Crispin Gardiner 2004
, see App.
B
, the dynamics of the very same setup are captured by the Langevin equation of the system mode,

\derivative{t}\hat{a}=i\quantity[ \hat{H}_S, \hat{a}]-\frac{\kappa}{2}\hat{a}-\sqrt{\kappa}\,\bin(t),

(5)

and the output field statistics become accessible through the input–output relation,

\displaystyle\bout(t)=\bin(t)+\sqrt{\kappa}\,\hat{a}(t)~.

(6)

For linear systems, Eq. (
5
) can be solved and the output statistics can be fully determined by the known statistics of the input field. Alternatively, Eq. (
6
) can be used to cast the statistics of the output field into correlation functions of the intra-cavity mode, which may then be solved using established techniques. For higher-order correlation functions, this approach quickly becomes unwieldy due to the cross–terms between the input–field and the cavity mode
Gardiner and Collett 1985
. As it is known that the statistics of the output field may be strikingly different from the intra-cavity field
Strandberg et al. 2021
;
Yurke 1984
;
Walls and Milburn 2025
, a theoretical formalism that provides direct access to the former is highly desirable.

A further tool to model the intra–cavity system described by Eq. (
1
) is given through the Schwinger–Keldysh formalism, see App.
A
. In this formalism, we capture the dynamics of the system through a path integral with the central object being the action
Sieberer et al. 2016
;
Thompson and Kamenev 2023
;
Kamenev 2011

\displaystyle S[\phi]=S_{0}[\phi]+S_{\rm d}[\phi]+S_{\text{int}}[\phi]~.

(7)

with

\displaystyle S_{0}[\phi]=\int dt\bigg[\bar{\phi}^{\text{q}}(t)\left(i\partial_{t}-\omega_{S}+i\frac{\kappa}{2}\right)\phi^{\text{cl}}(t)+

(8)

\displaystyle\bar{\phi}^{\text{cl}}(t)\left(i\partial_{t}-\omega_{S}-i\frac{\kappa}{2}\right)\phi^{\text{q}}(t)+i\kappa F\bar{\phi}^{\text{q}}(t)\phi^{\text{q}}(t)\bigg]~,

\displaystyle S_{\rm d}[\phi]=i\sqrt{2\kappa}\int dt\quantity[f(t) \Bar{\phi}^{\text{q}}(t) - \Bar{f}(t) \phi^{\text{q}}(t)]~.

(9)

Here
S_{0}
describes the linear cavity coupled to a Markovian bath,
S_{\rm d}
describes the coherent drive, and
S_{\text{int}}
captures the interaction Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\text{int}}
. The classical and quantum field
\phi^{\text{cl}}
and
\phi^{\text{q}}
describe the dynamics of the intra-cavity field, the bar denotes complex conjugation, and we introduced the distribution function,

F=2n_{B}+1~.

(10)

The classical field can be linked to the diagonal elements of the density matrix, while the quantum field captures coherences, see App.
A
for details.
In this formalism, source fields may be introduced in order to compute correlation functions of the intra-cavity field. The power of the Schwinger-Keldysh formalism lies in the wealth of approximative and diagrammatic techniques for evaluating correlation functions.

II.2
Path-integral approach to input–output theory

In the path integral approach to input–output theory, we access the correlation functions of the output field
\bout
directly and we therefore rederive the path integral describing our system from the unitary description of the system together with the bath. Our goal is to access time- and normal ordered correlation functions, since they are intimately connected to photodetection
Glauber 1963a
. To that end we introduce source fields
\chi,\chi^{\prime}
for the
\bout(t)
and
\dbout(t)
results in a Moment Generating Functional (MGF), see Sec.
VII
for a sketch of the derivation,

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\text{out}}[\chi,\chi^{\prime}]=\int\mathcal{D}[\phi]e^{iS_{\text{out}}[\phi,\chi,\chi^{\prime}]}~,

(11)

with the action

\displaystyle S_{\text{out}}[\phi,\chi,\chi^{\prime}]=S[\phi]+\int dti\chi(t)\chi^{\prime}(t)n_{B}

(12)

\displaystyle-\int dt\chi(t)\quantity[f(t) + \sqrt{\frac{\kappa}{2}} F \phi^\text{q}(t) + \sqrt{\frac{\kappa}{2}}\phi^{\text{cl}}(t)]

\displaystyle-\int dt\chi^{\prime}(t)\quantity[\Bar{f}(t) - \sqrt{\frac{\kappa}{2}} F \Bar{\phi}^{\text{q}}(t) + \sqrt{\frac{\kappa}{2}} \Bar{\phi}^{\text{cl}}(t)]~.

Equations (
11
) and (
12
) constitute the main technical result of our paper as any normal and time-ordered correlation function of the output field may be obtained by taking functional derivatives of the MGF

\displaystyle\langle\dbout(t^{-}_{1})\dots\dbout(t^{-}_{N})\bout(t^{+}_{M})\dots\bout(t^{+}_{1})\rangle

(13)

\displaystyle=\left.\quantity(\prod_{l=1}^N \frac{i\delta}{\delta\chi'(t^-_l)})\quantity(\prod_{p=1}^M \frac{i\delta}{\delta\chi(t_p^+)})\Lambda_{\text{out}}\quantity[\chi,\chi']\right|_{\chi=\chi^{\prime}=0},

where the time arguments obey the following ordering,

\displaystyle t^{-}_{1}\leq

\displaystyle\dots\leq t^{-}_{N}~,

\displaystyle t^{+}_{1}\leq

\displaystyle\dots\leq t^{+}_{M}~.

(14)

Similarly to actions including source fields for computing intra-cavity observables, Eq. (
12
) reduces to the action describing the system in Eq. (
7
) when the source fields are set to zero. These source fields are however markedly different than how source fields for intra-cavity observables enter. This is particularly evident by the term that is quadratic in the source fields, which describes the white noise in the output field that is also present in the input field [c.f. Eq. (
4
)]. For
n_{B}=0
, the terms multiplying the source fields in Eq. (
12
) may be understood from the input-output relation in Eq. (
6
). For normally ordered correlation functions, the input operators may be replaced by their averages
\bin(t)\rightarrow f(t)
. In addition, the source field
\chi
couples to the field
\phi^{+}=(\phi^{\rm cl}+\phi^{\rm q})/2
while the field
\chi^{\prime}
couples to the field
\bar{\phi}^{-}=(\bar{\phi}^{\rm cl}-\bar{\phi}^{\rm q})/2
. This particular coupling ensures normal ordering of the correlation functions, as the fields with a subscript
+
(
-
) are located in the forward (backward) in time branch on the Keldysh contour sketched below in Fig.
9
.
At finite temperatures,
n_{B}\neq 0
, the output noise acquires a direct contribution from the input noise, the term quadratic in the source fields. Additionally, the quantum fields coupled to the source fields acquire a factor
F
, capturing correlations between the input noise and the noise leaking out of the cavity.

In the following it will be more convenient to make use of the cumulant generating functional (CGF) which is given as the logarithm of the MGF,

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\text{out}}[\chi,\chi^{\prime}]=\ln(\Lambda_\text{out}[\chi,\chi'])~,

(15)

and yields the cumulants of the output field,

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle\dbout(t^{-}_{1})\dots\dbout(t^{-}_{N})\bout(t^{+}_{M})\dots\bout(t^{+}_{1})\rangle\!\rangle

(16)

\displaystyle=\left.\quantity(\prod_l \frac{i\delta}{\delta\chi'(t^-_l)})\quantity(\prod_p \frac{i\delta}{\delta\chi(t^+_p)})\mathcal{S}_{\text{out}}[\chi,\chi^{\prime}]\right|_{\chi=\chi^{\prime}=0},

here denoted by double brackets
\langle\!\langle\dots\rangle\!\rangle
.

For the specific case of a linear cavity, i.e.
\hat{H}_{\rm int}=0
, the path integral in the MGF
11
can be solved to find

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\text{out}}^{0}[\chi,\chi^{\prime},f]=\ln\Lambda_{\text{out}}^{0}[\chi,\chi^{\prime}]

(17)

\displaystyle=\begin{aligned} &-i\int dt\chi(t)\left[f(t)-i\kappa\int dt^{\prime}G^{R}(t-t^{\prime})f(t^{\prime})\right]\\
&-i\int dt\chi^{\prime}(t)\left[\bar{f}(t)+i\kappa\int dt^{\prime}G^{A}(t-t^{\prime})f(t^{\prime})\right]\\
&-\int dt\,\chi(t)\chi^{\prime}(t)n_{B}~,\end{aligned}

where we introduced the retarded and advanced Green functions capturing the dynamics of the system,
G^{R/A}
, see App.
C
and Eq. (
37
) below.
As described in Eq. (
16
), we can find the cumulants of the output field by taking functional derivatives. For the linear cavity, only the first two cumulants are nonvanishing as the output light is in a Gaussian state. The average reads

\langle\hat{b}_{\text{out}}(\tau)\rangle_{0}=f(\tau)-i\kappa\int dt\,G^{R}(\tau-t)f(t)~,

(18)

Which is given by the sum of the reflected input light and the light leaking out of the cavity, as determined by the input-output relation in Eq. (
6
). The light that leaks out of the cavity also arises from the input, but is affected by the cavity through the retarded Green function.
We can interpret this term as an input–photon entering at time
t
and then being propagated through the cavity to exit at time
\tau
.

The second cumulant reads

\displaystyle\langle\!\langle\dbout(\tau_{1})\bout(\tau_{2})\rangle\!\rangle_{0}

\displaystyle=n_{B}\delta(\tau_{2}-\tau_{1})~,

(19)

and is thus identical to the noise in the input field, see Eq. (
4
).
This is due to the process being Gaussian and is entirely expected but we note the simplicity with which this result arises. We further note that we did not need to specify the input signal and that the results obtained so far are valid for all input signals
f(t)
describing an arbitrary time–dependent coherent drive. The whole formalism laid out here can equally well be applied in frequency space, see App.
D
.

II.3
Glauber-Sudarshan P Functional

The state of a bosonic mode can be described by the Glauber-Sudarshan
P
function
Glauber 1963b
;
Sudarshan 1963
, which provides a phase space representation of quantum states as statistical mixtures of coherent states weighted by the
P
function. The moments of the
P
function are normal ordered expectation values of creation and annihilation operators. This description can be generalized to a continuum of modes, resulting in the
P
functional
Vogel 2008
;
Krumm et al. 2017
.
This generalization is needed to describe the output field, which consists of a continuum of modes. The moments of the
P
functional are then exactly the expectation values obtained from the MGF. Consequently, the
P
functional is obtained by taking the Fourier transform of the MGF,

P_{\text{out}}[\alpha]=\int\mathcal{D}[\chi,\chi^{\prime}]e^{i\int dt\quantity[\chi'(t) \alpha(t) + \Bar{\alpha}(t) \chi(t)]}\Lambda_{\text{out}}\quantity[\chi,\chi']~.

(20)

The Glauber-Sudarshan
P
function of a single mode, say
\hat{b}_{\text{out}}(\tau)
, is obtained by the path integral

P(\alpha_{\tau})=\int\mathcal{D}[\alpha]\delta(\alpha_{\tau}-\alpha(\tau))P_{\text{out}}[\alpha],

(21)

where all fields
\alpha(t)
are integrated out except the one at time
\tau
.
For the linear cavity, the Fourier transform can straightforwardly be evaluated to find,

\displaystyle P_{\text{out}}^{0}[\alpha]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n_{B}^{N}}e^{\int dt\frac{|\alpha(t)-\langle\bout(t)\rangle_{0}|^{2}}{n_{B}}},

(22)

where
N
denotes the number of output modes, which tends to infinity in the continuous-time representation used here.
The multi-time
P
-functional here describes a time–dependently displaced thermal state where the displacement is given by the average of the output field
\langle\bout(t)\rangle
, in complete agreement with the cumulants given in Eqs. (
18
) and (
19
). In discrete time, Eq. (
22
) is a product of
N
independent
P
functions, each describing a displaced thermal state.

III
Non–Linear Systems

Having introduced the basic framework of our novel approach to input–output theory and after treating a non–interacting system with the linear cavity, we now turn towards solving systems that contain nonlinear terms.
In Eq. (
7
) we had already
split up the action into a Gaussian part
which corresponds to the action of a linear cavity, and the non-Gaussian, interacting part
S_{\text{int}}
. For completeness, we provide
S_{\text{int}}[\phi]
for a general single-mode interaction Hamiltonian in App.
E
.

As it turns out, the separation between the linear cavity and the interaction
can also be made on the level of the generating functionals.
We implement this by shifting the system fields in the path integral by their average under the linear cavity action

\langle\phi^{\text{cl}/\text{q}}\rangle_{0}\quantity[\chi,\chi']=\frac{\int\mathcal{D}[\phi]\phi^{\text{cl}/\text{q}}e^{iS_{\text{out}}^{0}\quantity[\phi,\chi,\chi']}}{\Lambda_{\text{out}}^{0}[\chi,\chi^{\prime}]}~,

(23)

where
S_{\text{out}}^{0}
denotes the action in Eq. (
12
) with
S_{\rm int}
set to zero. We find

\displaystyle\langle\phi^{\mathrm{q}}(t)\rangle_{0}[\chi^{\prime}]

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\kappa}{2}}\int dt^{\prime}G^{A}(t-t^{\prime})\chi^{\prime}(t^{\prime})~,

(24)

\displaystyle\langle\bar{\phi}^{\mathrm{q}}(t)\rangle_{0}[\chi]

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\kappa}{2}}\int dt^{\prime}G^{R}(t^{\prime}-t)\chi(t^{\prime})~,

(25)

\displaystyle\langle\phi^{\mathrm{cl}}(t)\rangle_{0}[\chi^{\prime}]

\displaystyle=-F\langle\phi^{\text{q}}(t)\rangle_{0}[\chi^{\prime}]

(26)

\displaystyle-i\sqrt{2\kappa}\int dt^{\prime}G^{R}(t-t^{\prime})f(t^{\prime})~,

\displaystyle\langle\bar{\phi}^{\mathrm{cl}}(t)\rangle_{0}[\chi]

\displaystyle=F\langle\bar{\phi}^{\text{q}}(t)\rangle_{0}[\chi]

(27)

\displaystyle+i\sqrt{2\kappa}\int dt^{\prime}G^{A}(t^{\prime}-t)\bar{f}(t^{\prime}).

In the absence of source fields, these expressions recover
\langle\phi^{\rm q}(t)\rangle_{0}=0
and
\langle\phi^{\rm cl}(t)\rangle_{0}=\sqrt{2}\langle\hat{a}\rangle_{0}
, as expected from the conventional Schwinger-Keldysh formalism. In the presence of source fields, the quantum field obtains a finite average proportional to the source fields and the classical field obtains a similar contribution that involves the distribution function
F
.
With the help of these averages, we define new integration variables as,

\displaystyle\delta\phi^{\text{cl}}

\displaystyle=\phi^{\text{cl}}-\langle\phi^{\text{cl}}\rangle_{0}[\chi^{\prime}]~,

\displaystyle\delta\phi^{\text{q}}

\displaystyle=\phi^{\text{q}}-\langle\phi^{\text{q}}\rangle_{0}[\chi^{\prime}]~,

(28)

\displaystyle\delta\overline{\phi^{\text{cl}}}

\displaystyle=\overline{\phi^{\text{cl}}}-\langle\overline{\phi^{\text{cl}}}\rangle_{0}[\chi]~,

\displaystyle\delta\overline{\phi^{\text{q}}}

\displaystyle=\overline{\phi^{\text{q}}}-\langle\overline{\phi^{\text{q}}}\rangle_{0}[\chi]~.

Note that here we shift the fields and their complex conjugates independently see App.
F
for a justification of this step.
With this transformation we can now separate the MGF as,

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\mathrm{out}}[\chi,\chi]=\Lambda_{\mathrm{out}}^{0}[\chi,\chi^{\prime}]\Lambda_{\mathrm{out}}^{\mathrm{int}}[\chi,\chi^{\prime}]~,

(29)

where the MGF of the linear cavity is known from the previous section, see Eq. (
17
) and the interaction functional takes the form,

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\mathrm{out}}^{\mathrm{int}}[\chi,\chi^{\prime}]

\displaystyle=\int\mathcal{D}[\delta\phi]e^{iS_{0}[\delta\phi]+iS_{\mathrm{int}}[\delta\phi,\chi,\chi^{\prime}]}~.

(30)

Here
S_{0}
is given in Eq. (
8
) and
S_{\mathrm{int}}[\delta\phi,\chi,\chi^{\prime}]
is obtained from
S_{\rm int}[\phi]
by replacing
\phi^{\rm cl}
and
\phi^{\rm q}
according to Eq. (
28
).

In effect, we thereby shift our treatment towards the deviations around the exactly solvable, non–interacting linear cavity.
For a given interaction Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\text{int}}
we can now access the statistics of the output field perturbatively in the interaction strength. For this, we employ a cumulant expansion
Kubo 1962
. The interaction functional in Eq. (
30
) can be written as an average over the action
S_{0}

\Lambda_{\mathrm{out}}^{\mathrm{int}}[\chi,\chi^{\prime}]=\left\langle e^{iS_{\text{int}}[\delta\phi,\chi,\chi^{\prime}]}\right\rangle_{0}.

(31)

We note that whenever averages over
\delta\phi
are taken, we use the subscript
0
to denote averages over the linear cavity action without a drive (or source fields), such that
\langle\delta\phi^{\rm cl/q}\rangle_{0}=0
. Equation (
31
) can be understood as the moment generating function of the random variable
S_{\text{int}}[\delta\phi,\chi,\chi^{\prime}]
. Similarly to Eq. (
15
), the moment generating function can be written as the exponent of the cumulant generating function. We may thus write

\Lambda_{\mathrm{out}}^{\mathrm{int}}[\chi,\chi^{\prime}]=e^{i\langle S_{\mathrm{int}}[\delta\phi,\chi,\chi^{\prime}]\rangle_{0}-\frac{1}{2}\langle\!\langle S_{\mathrm{int}}^{2}[\delta\phi,\chi,\chi^{\prime}]\rangle\!\rangle_{0}+\dots}~.

(32)

See App.
G
for a detailed explanation of this step.
The cumulants in the last expression can be obtained through the moments

\langle S^{n}_{\mathrm{int}}[\delta\phi,\chi,\chi^{\prime}]\rangle_{0}=\int\mathcal{D}[\phi]S^{n}_{\mathrm{int}}[\delta\phi,\chi,\chi^{\prime}]e^{iS_{0}\quantity[\delta\phi]}~.

(33)

Since the MGF factorizes, the CGF will split into a sum

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\text{out}}[\chi,\chi^{\prime}]=\mathcal{S}_{\text{out}}^{0}[\chi,\chi^{\prime}]+\mathcal{S}_{\text{int}}[\chi,\chi^{\prime}]~.

(34)

To compute for example the first order approximate expression for the average output field
\langle\bout(\tau)\rangle
we only need to take into account the first term of the cumulant expansion in Eq. (
32
) and evaluate Eq. (
33
) for

,

\displaystyle\langle\bout(\tau)\rangle=i\left.\frac{\delta\mathcal{S}_{\mathrm{out}}[\chi,\chi^{\prime}]}{\delta\chi(\tau)}\right|_{\chi=\chi^{\prime}=0}

\displaystyle\simeq\langle\bout(\tau)\rangle_{0}+\left.\frac{i\delta}{\delta\chi(\tau)}\langle iS_{\mathrm{int}}[\delta\phi,\chi,\chi^{\prime}]\rangle_{0}\right|_{\chi=\chi^{\prime}=0}~.

(35)

The averages of the form of Eq. (
33
) are taken with respect to a Gaussian action and may therefore be evaluated using Wick’s theorem. They are thus fully determined by Eqs. (
24
)-(
26
), as well as the Green functions

\displaystyle G^{R}(t-t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-i\langle\delta\phi^{\rm cl}(t)\delta\bar{\phi}^{\rm q}(t^{\prime})\rangle_{0},

\displaystyle G^{A}(t-t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-i\langle\delta\phi^{\rm q}(t)\delta\bar{\phi}^{\rm cl}(t^{\prime})\rangle_{0}=\left[G^{R}(t^{\prime}-t)\right]^{*},

(36)

\displaystyle G^{K}(t-t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-i\langle\delta\phi^{\rm cl}(t)\delta\bar{\phi}^{\rm cl}(t^{\prime})\rangle_{0}

\displaystyle=F[G^{R}(t-t^{\prime})-G^{A}(t-t^{\prime})],

where the last equality reflects the fluctuation-dissipation theorem
Kamenev 2011
, which holds for the Gaussian action without the drive.
For the linear cavity, the Green functions are,

\displaystyle G^{A}(t-t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=i\theta(t^{\prime}-t)e^{-i\omega_{S}(t-t^{\prime})}e^{\nicefrac{{\kappa}}{{2}}(t-t^{\prime})}~,

\displaystyle G^{R}(t-t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-i\theta(t-t^{\prime})e^{-i\omega_{S}(t-t^{\prime})}e^{-\nicefrac{{\kappa}}{{2}}(t-t^{\prime})}~,

(37)

\displaystyle G^{K}(t-t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=-iFe^{-i\omega_{S}(t-t^{\prime})}e^{-\nicefrac{{\kappa}}{{2}}|t-t^{\prime}|}~.

V
Partial Summation Methods

Our diagrammatic approach yields an expansion in the interaction
U
in terms of diagrams for any given correlation function. In many cases, physical effects are dominated by specific classes of diagrams whose contribution cannot be reliably captured at finite order
Peskin and Schroeder 1995
. Partial summation methods can address this limitation by summing infinite subsets of diagrams that follow a specific pattern. In this section we explain how one can interpret a mean field theory approach to the average output field as a partial summation and we detail a partial summation technique for diagrams including thermal loops.

V.1
Finite-temperature mean-field theory

The average output field
\langle\bout\rangle
, through the input–output relation from Eq. (
6
), is directly linked to the average intra–cavity mode
\langle\hat{a}\rangle
and knowledge of one quantity directly implies knowledge of the other. The calculation of the average output field with our diagrammatic approach can therefore straightforwardly be connected to conventional approaches of determining the intra–cavity field average.

We note that some of the diagrams contributing to the second order correction of the average output field, see Fig.
4
, may be obtained by concatenating the first-order diagrams from Fig.
3
. This is done by taking a first-order diagram of
\langle\bout\rangle
, removing an input (anti-)photon, and replacing it by the outgoing leg of another first-order diagram of
\langle\bout\rangle
(
\langle\dbout\rangle
). This pattern suggests that these diagrams can be captured by a partial summation technique. It turns out that the result obtained by this partial summation corresponds to a mean–field approach for the intra–cavity mode. In order to show this correspondence we model the intra–cavity mode with the Langevin equation in Eq. (
5
). We treat the Kerr resonator in a rotating frame, i.e.
\omega_{s}\to-\Delta
, and find, c.f. Eq. (
38
),

\derivative{t}\langle\hat{a}\rangle=-\left(\frac{\kappa}{2}+i\Delta\right)\langle\hat{a}\rangle-\sqrt{\kappa}f(t)-2iU\langle\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}\hat{a}\rangle.

(45)

To approximate the last term, we write
\hat{a}=\langle\hat{a}\rangle+\delta\hat{a}
and we assume that thermal fluctuations are dominating, such that expectation values of
\delta\hat{a}
are determined by a thermal state with occupation
n_{B}
. This results in

\langle\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}\hat{a}\rangle\simeq\langle\hat{a}^{\dagger}\rangle\langle\hat{a}\rangle^{2}+2n_{B}\langle\hat{a}\rangle.

(46)

Substituting this approximation into Eq. (
45
) results in a non-linear equation for
\langle\hat{a}\rangle
, our finite-temperature mean-field equation that can be interpreted as introducing a self-consistent detuning depending on the cavity mode and temperature. This equation captures all diagrams that are obtained by concatenating the first-order diagrams shown in Fig.
3
. To see this, we write

\langle\hat{a}\rangle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\langle\hat{a}\rangle_{n},

(47)

where
\langle\hat{a}\rangle_{n}
is proportional to
U^{n}
.
For
n>0
, the coefficients are given as follows,

\langle\hat{a}\rangle_{n}=\begin{aligned} 2U\int dt^{\prime}&G^{R}(t-t^{\prime})\bigg[2n_{B}\langle\hat{a}\rangle_{n-1}\\
&+\sum_{l,k,p}\delta_{l+k+p,n-1}\langle a\rangle_{l}\langle a\rangle_{k}\langle a\rangle^{*}_{p}\bigg].\end{aligned}

(48)

For
n=1
, Eq. (
48
) reproduces the diagrams in Fig.
3
. For
n=2
, we simply replace one input term
\langle\phi^{\rm cl}\rangle_{0}
by
\sqrt{2}\langle\hat{a}\rangle_{1}
. For our diagrams, this means removing one input leg and replacing it with a first-order diagram where the detector is removed. Similarly, an
n
-th order diagram can be broken down in lower-order diagrams. Repeating this process implies that all diagrams that appear in Eq. (
48
) are built by concatenating first-order diagrams. We note here that even though the mean-field solution describes the behavior of the intra–cavity mode
\hat{a}
well, see Fig.
5
c), it fails to capture the behavior of the output field. Indeed, the reflection coefficient obtained from the mean field solution is equal to one for all parameters considered, see Fig.
5
a) and b) for the actual behavior.

VI
Output Field Statistics of the Kerr Oscillator

Following the procedure outlined in the previous section, we now turn to the output field of the coherently driven Kerr oscillator. For simplicity, we consider a drive that is time independent in a rotating frame,
f(t)=f
.

Here we focus on four quantities: the reflection, the
G^{(1)}
–function, the squeezing spectrum, and the
g^{(2)}
– function. At zero temperature, we benchmark our results for equal-time correlators with the exact results of Ref.
Bartolo et al. 2016
. At finite temperatures, we provide numerical results to benchmark our results for equal-time correlators
Krämer et al. 2018
.

VI.1
Reflection

The reflection amplitude is defined as

\frac{\langle\bout\rangle}{\langle\bin\rangle}=\sqrt{R}e^{-i\theta},

(52)

with
R
denoting the reflection probability. Having determined the average output field
\langle\bout\rangle
to second order we can directly access the second order approximation of the reflection probability, see Fig.
5
. In the limit of no Kerr nonlinearity,
U=0
, we find
R=1
, since the output light is just the reflected displaced thermal state provided by the input. For finite Kerr parameter
U
, the reflection probability decreases, since the output light is no longer a displaced thermal state.
We note that all photons are still being reflected,
\langle\dbout\bout\rangle=\langle\dbin\bin\rangle
, hence no photons are being lost in this system. Instead, the Kerr nonlinearity reduces the average displacement of the output mode while squeezing it as shown below. The finite-temperature mean-field theory reproduces the intra–cavity field well in this regime, see Fig.
5
c). The reflection probability obtained through this approach is however identically one for all parameters considered here. The mean–field approach fails to capture the effect of the Kerr–nonlinearity on the output field since the effect cannot be captured by a (self-consistent) shift in the detuning alone.

For zero temperature, we can benchmark our results with the exact solution for the cavity mode from Ref.
Bartolo et al. 2016
. We observe that our approximate solution captures the reduced reflection well for small drive strength. However, it fails to capture the shift in the effective resonant frequency observed for larger drive strengths. At finite temperatures, we observe a significant reduction in the reflection probability, as shown in Fig.
5
b), as well as a shift in the resonant frequency even for weak drives. While this shift is not captured by our second-order approximation, it is partly captured by the loop summation method at finite temperatures.

VI.2
First order coherence function

We now turn to the first order coherence function of the output light,

G^{(1)}(\tau)=\langle\dbout(\tau)\bout(0)\rangle\\
=\langle\!\langle\dbout(\tau)\bout(0)\rangle\!\rangle+\langle\dbout(\tau)\rangle\langle\bout(0)\rangle\ ~.

(53)

Since all photons impinging on the cavity eventually have to be reflected, we find
G^{(1)}(0)=\langle\dbin(t)\bin(t)\rangle
.
In the long-time limit,
\langle\dbout(t)\bout(t^{\prime})\rangle
factorizes resulting in,

\displaystyle G^{(1)}(\tau\to\infty)=|\langle\bout\rangle|^{2}~,

(54)

which, at zero temperature, we may compare to the exact result from Ref.
Bartolo et al. 2016
. For finite temperature, we evaluate the right-hand side of Eq. (
54
) numerically.
Since the reflection is smaller than one,
R<1
, we find that
G^{(1)}(0)>G^{(1)}(\tau\to\infty)
. As a function of
\tau
, the
G^{(1)}
–function monotonously interpolates between these limits, see Fig.
6
. Example diagrams that contribute to the
G^{(1)}
–function are also shown in Fig.
6
. At finite temperature, we observe a marked reduction in the first-order coherence for long times, consistent with the lower reflection probability shown in Fig.
5
b) .

In this section we derive the MGF in Eq. (
11

We start with the general setting which consists of a system

\displaystyle\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{S}+\hat{H}_{B}+\hat{V},

\displaystyle S^{\text{io}}_{V}[\varphi_{\text{in}},\varphi_{\text{out}}]

\displaystyle=i\sqrt{\kappa}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{N}}dt\quantity[
\Bar{\phi}^+(t) \varphi_{\text{in}}^+(t)-\Bar{\varphi}_{\text{out}}^+(t) \phi^+(t) - \Bar{\phi}^-(t) \varphi_{\text{out}}^-(t) + \Bar{\varphi}_{\text{in}}^-(t) \phi^-(t)].

(73)

As for the input and output fields, the plus and minus superscripts on the system fields denote the forward and backward branch of the Keldysh contour, see App.
A
. In particular,

\phi_{j}\rightarrow\begin{cases}\phi^{+}(t_{0}+j\delta t)\hskip 56.9055pt\text{for }\hskip 5.69046ptj<N,\\
\phi^{-}(t_{0}+[2N-j]\delta t)\hskip 21.62393pt\text{for }\hskip 5.69046ptj>N.\end{cases}

(74)

As the initial bath state, we consider a displaced thermal state,

\displaystyle\hat{\rho}_{B}=\bigotimes_{k}\quantity(1-e^{-\beta\omega_k})\hat{D}_{k}(\alpha_{k})e^{-\beta\omega_{k}\hat{b}_{k}^{\dagger}\hat{b}_{k}}\hat{D}_{k}^{\dagger}(\alpha_{k})~,

(75)

with the displacement operator
\hat{D}_{k}(\alpha)=e^{\alpha\hat{b}_{k}^{\dagger}-\bar{\alpha}\hat{b}_{k}}
, see
Gerry and Knight 2004
, and the inverse temperature of the thermal state
\beta
. By assuming that
e^{-\beta\omega_{k}}\simeq e^{-\beta\Omega}
for all bath frequencies
\omega_{k}
that influence the system, the bath state appearing in the action can be expressed in terms of the input fields as

\rho_{B}(\varphi_{\rm in})=\quantity(1-e^{-\beta\Omega})^{N}\exp\left\{\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{N}}dte^{-\beta\Omega}\bar{\varphi}_{\text{in}}^{+}(t)\varphi_{\text{in}}^{-}(t)+(1-e^{-\beta\Omega})\quantity[f(t) \Bar{\varphi}_{\text{in}}^+(t)+\Bar{f}(t) \varphi_{\text{in}}^-(t)-|f(t)|^2]\right\}~.

(76)

Note that
N
goes to infinity in the continuum limit and the prefactor
\quantity(1-e^{-\beta\Omega})^{N}
is simply a shorthand for the normalization constant. The average of the input field is determined by the displacement of all bath modes

\displaystyle f(t_{j})=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}\frac{\alpha_{k}}{\sqrt{N\delta t}}e^{-i\omega_{k}(t_{j}-t_{0})}~.

(77)

For
f=0
, we obtain a thermal state, whereas for zero temperature, we get a coherent state.
At this stage we introduce the Keldysh rotation in order to make use of the symmetries inherent in the approach. To this end, we introduce, for system and bath fields, the classical and quantum fields which are defined as

\displaystyle\psi^{\text{cl}}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\quantity(\psi^+(t) + \psi^-(t))~,

(78)

\displaystyle\psi^{\text{q}}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\quantity(\psi^+(t) - \psi^-(t))~.

(79)

If we make a stationary phase approximation of the action in Eq. (
72
), we arrive at relations between the input and output fields that are analogous to the operator relation in Eq. (
6
),
for the classical and quantum fields,

\displaystyle\varphi_{\text{out}}^{\text{cl}}(t)

\displaystyle=\varphi_{\text{in}}^{\text{cl}}(t)+\sqrt{\kappa}\phi^{\text{cl}}(t),

(80)

\displaystyle\varphi_{\text{out}}^{\text{q}}(t)

\displaystyle=\varphi_{\text{in}}^{\text{q}}(t)+\sqrt{\kappa}\phi^{\text{q}}(t)=0~.

(81)

We now introduce source fields
\chi
and
\chi^{\prime}
for the output field and its hermitian conjugate,

\Lambda_{\text{out}}[\chi,\chi^{\prime}]=\int\mathcal{D}[\phi,\varphi_{\text{in}},\varphi_{\text{out}}]e^{iS^{\text{io}[\phi,\varphi_{\text{in}},\varphi_{\text{out}}]}}\\
\times e^{-i\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{N}}dt\quantity[\chi(t) \varphi_{\text{out}}^+(t) + \chi'(t) \Bar{\varphi}_{\text{out}}^-(t)]}~.

(82)

Note that the unbarred output field is located on the forward branch of the Keldysh contour while the barred field is located on the backward branch. This guarantees that any correlation function derived from this generating functional will be automatically normal– and time–ordered, see Eq. (
14
).
In the last step we integrate out the input- and output fields which leaves us with the MGF introduced in Eq. (
11
) and the action given in Eq. (
12
).
Note that this generating functional is still general and holds for any system action
S_{S}[\phi]
, as long as the bath is Markovian and can be described by a displaced thermal state. This derivation can be extended to include arbitrary Gaussian bath states that also involve squeezing, as well as multiple baths.

VIII
Conclusions and outlook

We introduced a novel approach to input–output theory
Crispin Gardiner 2004
, based on the Schwinger–Keldysh path integral formalism
Kamenev 2011
.
This approach allows for using the theoretical tools from path-integral approaches to directly compute the properties of the outgoing light, which can behave strikingly different from the light inside the cavity.
We illustrate our formalism with a diagrammatic treatment of the Kerr nonlinear oscillator. Our approach allows for including finite temperatures in a straightforward way, by dressing the propagators of the theory. Furthermore, partial summation methods to infinite order in the Kerr nonlinearity are available, either based on the dressed propagators or on a mean-field approach.

Although the specific diagrammatic rules and pictorial representations used for the Kerr oscillator are system specific, the overall approach is general, and we expect the diagram rules to generalize straightforwardly to other models.

Our formalism can be extended to include squeezing, either through a squeezed bath or a parametric drive. A particularly interesting scenario is provided by the Kerr parametric oscillator, which exhibits non-classical behavior in the output field even though the cavity field is characterized by a non-negative Wigner function
Strandberg et al. 2021
. Further extensions of our formalism include the generalization to few-level systems as well as spin and fermionic degrees of freedom embedded in the cavity. This may extend our formalism to the emerging field of cavity materials engineering
Lu et al. 2025
;
Hübener et al. 2021
;
Mivehvar et al. 2021
.

Appendix A
Path Integral Formalism

In the following we give a short overview of the Schwinger-Keldysh formalism for non-equilibrium quantum field theory. We will stick closely to the exposition in Ref.
Kamenev 2011
, recent developments and experimental applications of the formalism can be found in
Sieberer et al. 2016
. We follow the notation of Ref.
Kamenev 2011
and therefore use a bar to denote complex conjugation.
We start by introducing the coherent states of the cavity mode
\hat{a}
and the bath modes
\hat{b}_{k}
,

\displaystyle\hat{a}\ket{\phi}=\phi\ket{\phi},~~\hat{b}_{k}\ket{\varphi_k}=\varphi_{k}\ket{\varphi_k},

(83)

where the coherent states,

\displaystyle\ket{\phi}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\phi^{n}}{\sqrt{n!}}\ket{n}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\phi^{n}}{n!}\quantity(\hat{a}^\dagger)^{n}\ket{0}=e^{\phi\hat{a}^{\dagger}}\ket{0},

(84)

are not normalized. This leads to the following identity for the inner product of two coherent states,

\displaystyle\bra{\phi}\ket{\phi'}

\displaystyle=e^{\overline{\phi}\phi^{\prime}}.

(85)

Some heavily used identities for coherent states are mentioned in the following,

\displaystyle\int d[\phi]e^{-|\phi|^{2}}\ket{\phi}\bra{\phi}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{1},

(86)

\displaystyle d[\phi]:

\displaystyle=d(\Re{\phi})d(\Im{\phi})/\pi,

(87)

\displaystyle\bra{\phi}\hat{H}(\hat{a}^{\dagger},\hat{a})\ket{\phi}

\displaystyle=H(\overline{\phi},\phi^{\prime})\bra{\phi}\ket{\phi'},

(88)

where the last relation holds for any normally ordered operator
\hat{H}(\hat{a}^{\dagger},\hat{a})
.
To denote the tensor product of a state from the cavity and the states from the bath we introduce the following notation,

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi}

\displaystyle=\ket{\phi}\bigotimes_{k}\ket{\varphi_k}~,

\displaystyle d\quantity[\Psi]

\displaystyle=d[\phi]\prod_{k}d[\varphi_{k}]~,

(89)

\displaystyle\bar{\Psi}\Psi^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\bar{\phi}\phi^{\prime}+\sum_{k}\bar{\varphi}_{k}\varphi_{k}^{\prime}~,

(90)

with it we express the identity in the combined Hilbert space as follows,

\mathbb{1}=\int d\quantity[\Psi]e^{-|\Psi|^{2}}\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Psi}.

(91)

As a consequence we compute the trace of an operator
\hat{O}
as,

\Tr{\hat{O}}=\int d\quantity[\Psi]e^{-|\Psi|^{2}}\bra{\Psi}\hat{O}\ket{\Psi}.

(92)

As is customary in quantum field theory, we consider the partition function of our state, here given by the trace of the density matrix
\hat{\rho}
at time
t
,

Z=\Tr{\hat{\rho}(t)}=\Tr{\hat{U}(t)\hat{\rho}_0\hat{U}^\dagger(t)}=\Tr{\hat{\rho}_0}=1,

(93)

where we used cyclic permutability of the trace, the time evolution operator
\hat{U}
and the normalization condition. As a next step, we write
\hat{U}(t)
and its Hermitian conjugate as a product of time-evolution operators for the infinitesimal time
\hat{U}(\delta t)
. We then insert identities of the type from Eq. (
91
) in between these infinitesimal time evolutions resulting in,

\displaystyle Z=\int\mathcal{D}\quantity[\Psi]e^{-\sum_{j=0}^{2N-1}|\Psi_{j}|^{2}}\bra{\Psi_{2N-1}}e^{i\delta t\hat{H}}\ket{\Psi_{2N-2}}\bra{\Psi_{2N-2}}e^{i\delta t\hat{H}}\ket{\Psi_{2N-3}}\cdots

(94)

\displaystyle\bra{\Psi_{N+1}}e^{i\delta t\hat{H}}\ket{\Psi_{N}}\bra{\Psi_N}\mathbb{1}\ket{\Psi_{N-1}}\bra{\Psi_{N-1}}e^{-i\delta t\hat{H}}\ket{\Psi_{N-2}}\cdots\bra{\Psi_1}e^{-i\delta t\hat{H}}\ket{\Psi_0}\bra{\Psi_0}\hat{\rho}_{0}\ket{\Psi_{2N-1}}.

We now turn to evaluating the infinitesimal time evolution steps,

\displaystyle\bra{\Psi_j}e^{-i\delta t_{j}\hat{H}}\ket{\Psi_{j-1}}

\displaystyle=\bra{\Psi_j}\quantity(\mathbb{1} - i \delta t_j \hat{H} + \order{\delta t_j^2} )\ket{\Psi_{j-1}}=e^{\bar{\Psi}_{j}\Psi_{j-1}}-i\delta t_{j}H(\bar{\Psi}_{j},\Psi_{j-1})e^{\bar{\Psi}_{j}\Psi_{j-1}}+\order{\delta t_j^2}

\displaystyle\approx e^{\bar{\Psi}_{j}\Psi_{j-1}}e^{-i\delta t_{j}H(\bar{\Psi}_{j},\Psi_{j-1})}.

(95)

Here we introduced the time–step,

\displaystyle\delta t_{j}=\text{sign}(N-j)\delta t~.

(96)

Notice that the last relation in Eq. (
95
) is exact in the limit
\delta t\to 0
.
We further use the common notation for path integrals,

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\quantity[\Psi]=\prod_{j=0}^{2N-1}d\quantity[\Psi_j].

(97)

With this notation and the evaluated infinitesimal time step evolution, we can write the Keldysh partition function as a path integral,

\displaystyle Z=\int\mathcal{D}\quantity[\Psi]e^{iS[\Psi]},

(98)

\displaystyle S[\Psi]=\begin{aligned} \sum_{j=1}^{2N-1}&\delta t_{j}\quantity[i \Bar{\Psi}_j \frac{\Psi_j-\Psi_{j-1}}{\delta t_j}-H(\Bar{\Psi}_j,\Psi_{j-1})]\\
&+i\bar{\Psi}_{0}\Psi_{0}-i\ln\rho_{0}(\bar{\Psi}_{0},\Psi_{2N-1})~.\end{aligned}

(99)

The sum in the action can be interpreted as a (discrete) time integral along the closed-time contour sketched in Fig.
9
. Time first increases along the forward-in-time branch that arises from the time-evolution operator
\hat{U}(t)
. Time then decreases along the backward-in-time branch that comes from
\hat{U}^{\dagger}(t)
.

Appendix B
Conventional Input–Output Theory

Conventional input–output theory describes the system and bath modes of the general setting sketched in Fig.
1
through the use of Langevin equations for the bath modes. We start here by deriving the time-dependence of a single bath mode,

\displaystyle\derivative{t}\hat{b}_{k}

\displaystyle=i\quantity[\hat{H},\hat{b}_k]=i\sum_{l}\quantity[\omega_k \hat{b}_l^\dagger\hat{b}_l + \qty(g_l \hat{a}^\dagger\hat{b}_k + g_k^* \hat{b}_k^\dagger\hat{a}), \hat{b}_k]=-i\sum_{l}\quantity( \delta_{l,k} \hat{b}_l + g_l^* \delta_{l,k} \hat{a})=-i(\omega_{k}\hat{b}_{k}+g_{k}^{*}\hat{a}),

(100)

this differential equation yields the following solution,

\displaystyle\hat{b}_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=e^{-i\omega_{k}(t-t_{0})}\hat{b}_{k}(t_{0})-ig_{k}^{*}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}d\tau e^{-i\omega_{k}(t-\tau)}\hat{a}(\tau)=e^{-i\omega_{k}(t-t_{N})}\hat{b}_{k}(t_{N})-ig_{k}^{*}\int_{t_{N}}^{t}d\tau e^{-i\omega_{k}(t-\tau)}\hat{a}(\tau),

(101)

where we introduced the times
t_{0},t_{N}
which are supposed to lie in the far past, far future respectively.
Now we turn to the time-dependence of the system mode
\hat{a}
,

\displaystyle\derivative{t}\hat{a}=i\quantity[\hat{H}_S + \hat{H}_B + \hat{V},\hat{a}]=i\quantity[\hat{H}_S, \hat{a}]+i\quantity[\hat{H}_B ,\hat{a}]+i\quantity[\hat{V},\hat{a}].

(102)

The second term in the equation above vanishes since the bath modes commute with the system mode.
The third term reads,

\displaystyle i\quantity[\hat{V},\hat{a}]

\displaystyle=i\sum_{k}\quantity[g_k \hat{a}^\dagger\hat{b}_k + g_k^* \hat{b}_k^\dagger\hat{a},\hat{a}]=-i\sum_{k}g_{k}\hat{b}_{k}=-\sum_{k}\quantity(i g_k e^{-i \omega_k (t-t_0)} \hat{b}_k(t_0) + |g_k|^2 \int_{t_0}^t d \tau e^{i \omega_k (t- \tau)}\hat{a}(\tau))

(103)

\displaystyle=-\sqrt{\kappa}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa}}\sum_{k}ig_{k}e^{-i\omega_{k}(t-t_{0})}\hat{b}_{k}(t_{0})-\sum_{k}|g_{k}|^{2}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}d\tau e^{i\omega_{k}(t-\tau)}\hat{a}(\tau),

where we introduced the coupling constant
\kappa
in the last step. We further introduce the input and output operators,

\displaystyle\bin(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa}}\sum_{k}ig_{k}e^{-i\omega_{k}(t-t_{0})}\hat{b}_{k}(t_{0}),

\displaystyle\bout(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa}}\sum_{k}ig_{k}e^{i\omega_{k}(t_{N}-t)}\hat{b}_{k}(t_{N}),

(104)

as well as the bath spectral density,

\rho(\omega)=\sum_{k}|g_{k}|^{2}\delta(\omega-\omega_{k}).

(105)

Using the last two quantities, we can express Eq. (
103
) as follows,

i\quantity[\hat{V},\hat{a}]=-\sqrt{\kappa}\bin(t)-\int_{t_{0}}^{t}d\tau\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\,\rho(\omega)e^{-i\omega(t-\tau)}\hat{a}(\tau).

(106)

With these simplifications the equation of motion of the bath mode
\hat{a}
is,

\derivative{t}\hat{a}=i\quantity[\hat{H}_S,\hat{a}]-\sqrt{\kappa}\bin(t)-\int_{t_{0}}^{t}d\tau\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\,\rho(\omega)e^{-i\omega(t-\tau)}\hat{a}(\tau).

(107)

Analogously we find,

\derivative{t}\hat{a}=i\quantity[\hat{H}_S,\hat{a}]-\sqrt{\kappa}\bout(t)+\int_{t}^{t_{N}}d\tau\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\,\rho(\omega)e^{-i\omega(t-\tau)}\hat{a}(\tau).

(108)

Even though at this stage our equations only contain the input and output operators, we can retrieve the bath modes at the times
t_{0}
and
t_{N}
from the input–output operators,

\displaystyle\hat{b}_{k}(t_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\kappa}}{2\pi ig_{k}}\int_{\omega_{k}-\delta\omega}^{\omega_{k}+\delta\omega}d\omega\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt\,e^{i\omega(t-t_{0})}\bin(t)~,

\displaystyle\hat{b}_{k}(t_{N})

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\kappa}}{2\pi ig_{k}}\int_{\omega_{k}-\delta\omega}^{\omega_{k}+\delta\omega}d\omega\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dt\,e^{-i\omega(t_{N}-t)}\bout(t)~.

(109)

From the equations of motion for the system mode
\hat{a}
, Eqs. (
108
), (
107
), we get the following relation between the input and output operators,

\displaystyle\bout(t)=\bin(t)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\kappa}}\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{N}}d\tau\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\,\rho(\omega)e^{-i\omega(t-\tau)}\hat{a}(\tau),

(110)

and we can derive their commutation relations,

\displaystyle\quantity[\bin(t),\hat{b}_{\text{in}}^\dagger(t')](t),\hat{b}_{\text{in}}^{\dagger}(t^{\prime})=\quantity[\bout(t),\hat{b}_{\text{out}}(t')](t),\hat{b}_{\text{out}}(t^{\prime})=\frac{1}{\kappa}\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\,\rho(\omega)e^{-i\omega(t-t^{\prime})}.

(111)

So far we treated time as a continuous quantity and made no assumptions on the spectrum of the bath. Now we make the following assumptions: (i) the bath consists of
N
equidistantly spaced bath modes, i.e.
\omega_{k}=\omega_{0}+k\delta\omega
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
, (ii) time is discretized such that
t_{j}=t_{0}+j\delta t
with
j=0,1,2,\dots,N
and (iii) the timestep of the discretization is connected to the discretization of the bath modes such that
\delta t\delta\omega=\frac{2\pi}{N}
.
With these assumptions, the bath modes expressed through the input and output operators from Eq. (
104
) can be written as follows,

\displaystyle\hat{b}_{k}(t_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\kappa}}{ig_{k}}\sum_{j=0}^{N}\,e^{i\omega_{k}(t_{j}-t_{0})}\hat{b}_{\text{in}}(t_{j})~,

\displaystyle\hat{b}_{k}(t_{N})

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\kappa}}{ig_{k}}\sum_{j=0}^{N}\,e^{-i\omega_{k}(t_{N}-t_{j})}\hat{b}_{\text{out}}(t_{j})~.

(112)

Additionally to the discretization outlined above, we now make the following two approximations: (i) The Fourier transform of
\hat{a}(\tau)
is peaked at a specific frequency
\Omega
, where the peak has a fixed width
\gamma
, (ii) the spectral density
\rho(\omega)
is flat in frequency and around
\Omega
can be approximated as follows (Markov approximation),

\rho(\omega)\approx\rho(\Omega)~~\forall\,\omega\in(\Omega-\gamma,\Omega+\gamma).

(113)

These assumptions let us simplify the third term in the relation between input and output operators from Eq. (
110
) as follows,

\displaystyle\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{N}}d\tau\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\,\rho(\omega)e^{-i\omega(t-\tau)}\hat{a}(\tau)

\displaystyle\approx\rho(\Omega)\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{N}}d\tau\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\omega e^{-i\omega(t-\tau)}\hat{a}(\tau)

\displaystyle=\rho(\Omega)\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{N}}2\pi\delta(t-\tau)\hat{a}(\tau)=\underbrace{2\pi\rho(\Omega)}_{=:\kappa}\hat{a}(t),

(114)

where we extended the range of integration in frequency in the first step. The extension of the integration range is justified since only nearly resonant frequencies are important here and the nonphysical, negative frequencies are negligible.
Equivalently, using these approximations
we find,

\displaystyle\int_{t_{0}}^{t}d\tau\int_{0}^{\infty}d\omega\,\rho(\omega)e^{-i\omega(t-\tau)}\hat{a}(\tau)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\kappa}{2}\hat{a}(t).

(115)

With these approximations we can express the equation of motion for the system mode
\hat{a}
from Eqs. (
107
) and (
108
) as follows, see Eq.
108
,

\displaystyle\derivative{t}\hat{a}

\displaystyle=i\quantity[ \hat{H}_S, \hat{a}]-\frac{\kappa}{2}\hat{a}-\sqrt{\kappa}\bin(t)~,

\displaystyle\derivative{t}\hat{a}

\displaystyle=i\quantity[ \hat{H}_S, \hat{a}]+\frac{\kappa}{2}\hat{a}-\sqrt{\kappa}\bout(t)~,

(116)

and get the simplified commutation relations,

\displaystyle\quantity[ \bin(t), \hat{b}_{\text{in}}^\dagger(t')](t),\hat{b}_{\text{in}}^{\dagger}(t^{\prime})=\quantity[\bout(t),\hat{b}_{\text{out}}^\dagger(t')](t),\hat{b}_{\text{out}}^{\dagger}(t^{\prime})=\delta(t-t^{\prime}),

(117)

as well as the input–output relation in Eq. (
6
). In this relation we can interpret the outgoing mode as the reflection of the input mode together with an additional signal originating from the cavity.
We can further simplify our expressions by assuming that all coupling constants are equal,
ig_{k}:=g\in\mathbb{R}
,
and by rewriting the approximated spectral density,
\rho(\omega)\approx\rho(\Omega)=g^{2}\rho.

With these simplifications we can write the discretized input-output operators as follows,

\displaystyle\bin(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\rho}}\sum_{k}e^{-i\omega_{k}(t-t_{0})}\hat{b}_{k}(t_{0})~,

\displaystyle\bout(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\rho}}\sum_{k}e^{i\omega_{k}(t_{N}-t)}\hat{b}_{k}(t_{N})~.

(118)

From these expressions we can intuitively understand the input operator as the bath operators freely evolved in time (i.e. in the absence of the coupling
\hat{V}
) from the distant past up until the time
t
. This free evolution reflects the fact that the input cannot be affected by the system, as it describes the bath modes before interacting with the system. Equivalently, the output operator describes the bath operators in the far future, i.e., after interacting with the system, freely evolved back in time up until the time
t
.
Further details on the conventional input–output formalism can be found in
Crispin Gardiner 2004
,
Clerk et al. 2010
and
D.F. Walls 2008
.

Appendix C
Linear cavity

In this appendix we provide additional information for the treatment of the linear cavity as described in Sec.
II
.
With the system Hamiltonian
H_{S}=\omega_{S}\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}
, we can evaluate the system specific contribution
S_{S}[\phi]
to the action according to Eq. (
99
),

\displaystyle S[\phi]=\begin{aligned} &\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\delta t\quantity[i \Bar{\phi}_j^+\frac{\phi_j^+-\phi_{j-1}^+}{\delta t}-\omega_S \Bar{\phi}_j^+\phi_{j-1}^+]-&\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}\delta t\quantity[i \Bar{\phi}_j^- \frac{\phi_j^- - \phi_{j-1}^-}{\delta t} - \omega_S \Bar{\phi}_{j-1}^-\phi_{j}^-]+&i\bar{\phi}_{0}\phi_{0}-i\ln\rho_{S}(\bar{\phi}_{0},\phi_{2N-1})~.\end{aligned}

(119)

Neglecting the boundary terms and going to the continuum limit we find,

\displaystyle S[\phi]

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{N}}dt\quantity[\Bar{\phi}^+(t) (i \partial_t -\omega_S ) \phi^+(t) - \Bar{\phi}^-(t) (i \partial_t - \omega_S) \phi^-(t)]

\displaystyle=\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{N}}dt\quantity[\Bar{\phi}^{\text{q}}(t) (i \partial_t - \omega_S) \phi^{\text{cl}}(t) + \Bar{\phi}^{\text{cl}}(t) (i \partial_t -\omega_S) \phi^{\text{q}}(t)]~.

(120)

From this dissipative action, we read of the inverse Green functions,

\displaystyle\quantity[G^{-1}]^{A}(t-t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\quantity(- \frac{i \kappa}{2} +\qty(i \partial_t - \omega_S))\delta(t-t^{\prime})~,

\displaystyle\quantity[G^{-1}]^{R}(t-t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\quantity(\frac{i \kappa}{2} + \qty(i \partial_t - \omega_S))\delta(t-t^{\prime})~,

(121)

\displaystyle\quantity[G^{-1}]^{K}(t-t^{\prime})

\displaystyle=i\kappa(2n_{B}+1)\delta(t-t^{\prime})~.

(122)

By Fourier transforming and solving for the inverse, we determine the Green functions in the frequency domain,

\displaystyle G^{A}[\omega]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\omega-\omega_{S}-\nicefrac{{i\kappa}}{{2}}}~,

\displaystyle G^{R}[\omega]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\omega-\omega_{S}+\nicefrac{{i\kappa}}{{2}}}~,

(123)

\displaystyle G^{K}[\omega]

\displaystyle=-G^{R}[\omega]\cdot\quantity[G^{-1}]^{K}[\omega]\cdot G^{A}[\omega]~,

\displaystyle[G^{-1}]^{K}[\omega]

\displaystyle=i\kappa(2n_{B}+1))~.

(124)

The Green functions in the time domain can now be found by a further Fourier transform, see Eq. (
37
).
With the Green functions we can now
evaluate the moment generating function,

\displaystyle\Lambda_{\text{out}}^{0}[\chi,\chi^{\prime}]=e^{i\mathcal{S}_{\rm out}^{0}[\chi,\chi^{\prime}]}~.

(125)

If we let our dynamics start in the far past,
t_{0}\to-\infty
, and end in the far future,
t_{N}\to\infty
, we find,

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{\rm out}^{0}[\chi,\chi^{\prime}]

\displaystyle=-\int dt\int dt^{\prime}\,\kappa\quantity(\chi(t) G^R(t-t') f(t') - \Bar{f}(t) G^A(t-t')\chi'(t'))-\int dt\,\quantity(i\chi(t) f(t) + i\chi'(t) \Bar{f}(t)+ \chi(t) \chi'(t) n_B)~.

From the MGF we now have access to the statistics of the output field. We start by calculating the first moment of the output field and its hermitian conjugate,

\displaystyle\langle\hat{b}_{\text{out}}(t)\rangle

\displaystyle=i\left.\frac{\delta\Lambda_{\text{out}}^{\text{HO}}[\chi,\chi^{\prime}]}{\delta\chi(t)}\right|_{\chi=\chi^{\prime}=0}=f(t)-i\kappa\int dt^{\prime}\,f(t^{\prime})G^{R}(t-t^{\prime})~,

(126)

\displaystyle\langle\hat{b}_{\text{out}}^{\dagger}(t)\rangle

Appendix H
Diagrammatic Rules for the Kerr Oscillator

In this appendix we explain the origins of the diagram rules outlined in the main text in Sec.
IV.2
.

H.1

The rule determining the number of arrows that point to and away from the vertex comes directly from the interaction Hamiltonian
\hat{H}_{\text{int}}
. The Kerr term consists of two creation and two annihilation operators which, on the level of fields, yields a complex
\phi^{4}
-theory with two barred and two unbarred fields in every term proportional to the Kerr parameter
U
, see Eq. (
39
). With barred fields represented by outgoing arrows and unbarred fields by incoming arrows, see Fig.
2
, we end up with the rule that every vertex needs to connect to two ingoing and two outgoing arrows. For a more general interaction Hamiltonian,

\displaystyle\hat{H}_{\text{int}}=(\hat{a}^{\dagger})^{n}\hat{a}^{m}+H.c.~,

(157)

we can expect the first rule to change to
n
outgoing arrows and
m
incoming arrows or vice versa at every vertex.

H.2

The second rule on the number of dashed lines that connect to a vertex is due to the specific structure of the interaction action given in terms of the quantum (dashed lines) and classical fields (full lines) in Eq. (
39
). We notice that every term in the interaction action contains either one or three quantum fields and since we denote quantum fields by dashed lines, this yields the restriction. For different interaction actions, inspection of the interaction action under the Keldysh rotation is expected to yield a similar rule.

H.3

Neglecting all loops except the ones of the Keldysh equal–time Green function is a convention to simplify calculations. We fix this convention to reduce the number of diagrams to keep track of by considering all possible loops inside the classical–classical connection. See App.
J
for an indepth explanation.

H.4

Rule number four reflects the causality structure built into the propagators. Every diagram where two connected vertices attach to a dotted line each contains a product of Green functions that is equal to zero:
\propto G^{R}(\tau)G^{A}(\tau)=0
\propto G^{R}(\tau)G^{R}(-\tau)=0
\propto G^{R}(-\tau)G^{A}(-\tau)=0
\propto G^{A}(-\tau)G^{A}(\tau)=0
\leadsto

We see that every possible configuration of arrows in a diagram where two vertices are connected as is depicted in the left side, leads to the corresponding term being zero.

H.5

Rule number five relies on the sign difference between the product of the terms
\langle\phi^{\text{q}}\rangle^{{}^{\prime}}_{0}
and
F\langle\bar{\phi}^{\text{q}}\rangle^{{}^{\prime}}_{0}
as well as
\langle\bar{\phi}^{\text{q}}\rangle^{{}^{\prime}}_{0}
and
F\langle\phi^{\text{q}}\rangle^{{}^{\prime}}_{0}
as they appear in our diagrams, see Fig.
2
. For every diagram that fulfills the premise of the rule, there exists an associated diagram that we obtain by changing the position of the dotted line:

\leadsto F\langle\bar{\phi}^{\text{q}}\rangle^{{}^{\prime}}_{0}\langle\phi^{\text{q}}\rangle^{{}^{\prime}}_{0}
\leadsto-F\langle\bar{\phi}^{\text{q}}\rangle^{{}^{\prime}}_{0}\langle\phi^{\text{q}}\rangle^{{}^{\prime}}_{0}

Should both these diagrams have the same multiplicity, which is always the case if the vertex connects to an even number of detectors, they will cancel out resulting in rules 5 a) and 5 c). Should the multiplicity of the associated diagram be different, which may happen for three detectors at the same vertex, the vertex with higher multiplicity (which has to be two) survives with multiplicity one, explaining rule 5 b) as well as the special multiplicities of vertices with three detectors.

H.6

At zero temperature the distribution function
F
is equal to one and the constituting relation of the Keldysh Green function, see Eq. (
36
), simplifies to the difference between the retarded and the advanced Green function,

\displaystyle G^{K}=G^{R}-G^{A}~.

(158)

This leads to the fact that all possible connections of two vertices with three propagators sum to zero. Note that this rule does not hold at finite temperatures.

In all but the greater Green functions, the creation operator stands to the left of the annihilation operator, because the barred fields appear after the unbarred fields along the Keldysh contour. The greater Green function has the opposite operator ordering, since the barred field is inserted in the forward branch, i.e. before the unbarred field which is inserted along the backward branch.

(176)

\displaystyle\langle\delta\phi^{\mathrm{q}}(t)\delta\bar{\phi}^{\mathrm{cl}}(t^{\prime})\rangle

(177)

(178)

\displaystyle\langle\delta\phi^{\mathrm{q}}(t)\delta\bar{\phi}^{\mathrm{q}}(t^{\prime})\rangle

\displaystyle\to-\frac{1}{2}~.

(179)

The first two equations above tell us that for every diagram that contains an equal time advanced Green function, a similar diagram must exist with opposite sign due to an equal time retarded Green function (whenever a loop of an advanced Green function was possible, a loop of a retarded Green function must also fulfill all the diagram rules). These diagrams will invariably sum to zero and hence we fix the rule that no diagrams with loops consisting of a full and a dashed line exist. Interestingly we find that the quantum-quantum correlator does not vanish at equal times but attains a finite value of
-\frac{1}{2}
. This highlights the fact that the continuum notation should always be considered as a notational shorthand for the exact, discrete path integral. Since any diagram that allows for a quantum-quantum loop (a fully dashed circle) also fulfills all the diagram rules with a classical-classical loop in its stead we fix the following convention: We only allow diagrams with classical-classical loops and assign the value
2n_{B}
to these loops. We thereby effectively consider the sum of the diagram with the classical-classical loop and the equivalent diagram with the quantum–quantum loop whose contributions will sum to
2n_{B}
. Apart from the obvious simplification of reducing the number of allowed diagrams, this convention allows us to neglect all diagrams with loops in the zero temperature limit.
