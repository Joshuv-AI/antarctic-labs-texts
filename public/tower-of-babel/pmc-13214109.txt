Factors associated with juvenile musculoskeletal dysfunction in children
Authors: Masaru Hatano, Keishi Maruo, Narumi Tokuda, Tomoya Iseki, Shotaro Tsuji, Toshiya Tachibana, Masayuki Shima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe prevalence of sedentary behavior among children has increased owing to the popularity of video games and smartphones. Conversely, sports injuries caused by excessive physical activity (PA) at a young age have also been reported. The lack of PA or, conversely, excessive sports strain during childhood has resulted in changes in children, such as a decline in their basic physical ability. This article addresses “Juvenile Musculoskeletal Dysfunction (JMD),” a musculoskeletal dysfunction in children without a clear underlying disease, and its associated factors.MethodsThis study was an adjunct to a birth cohort study in Hyogo, Japan. This study included 1217 children aged 8 years (568 males and 649 females). All children underwent physical examinations of single-leg stance, squatting, upper-limb elevation, and forward bending as the JMD screening. JMD was defined as the inability to perform one or more of the four physical movements, which included a questionnaire regarding their history of musculoskeletal pain and fractures. The parents of each patient participant completed the questionnaire regarding a history of musculoskeletal pain and fractures of their child. Factors associated with JMD were analyzed using logistic regression models.ResultsThe prevalence of JMD was 36.0% (n = 438). The inability to perform “single-leg stance,” “squatting,” “upper limb elevation,” and “forward bending” was 8.6%, 10.3%, 6.4%, and 19.7% in all cases (male and female), respectively. In total, 6.8% (n = 83) had musculoskeletal pain and 9.2% (n = 112) had a history of fracture. The JMD group had a significantly higher prevalence of musculoskeletal pain and a greater history of fractures than the non-JMD group. Demographic factors such as obesity and sex (male) were associated with JMD (Odd ratios were 3.06 [95% Confidence interval [CI]: 1.62–5.79] and 1.98 [95%CI: 1.54–2.55], respectively).ConclusionsThis study defined JMD using simple physical examinations and investigated its associated factors in 8-year-old children. JMD was found to be more common in males with obesity and was associated with a history of fracture, and musculoskeletal pain, while habitual physical activity showed an inverse association with JMD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-026-09782-1.Keywords: Juvenile Musculoskeletal Dysfunction, Physical Activity, Obesity, Sports Injuries, Musculoskeletal Pain

Background

Musculoskeletal function in children has received increasing attention, particularly through school health programs in Japan. The concept of Juvenile Musculoskeletal Dysfunction (JMD) has been introduced to describe musculoskeletal dysfunction in children without an underlying medical condition [1]. Hayashi reported that, in a large-scale school screening program in Saitama, about 41.6% of school-aged children were unable to perform even one of the four fundamental physical tasks, meaning that 41.6% met the criteria for JMD [1]. These findings indicate that basic physical function may vary considerably among children. Furthermore, national surveillance data from the Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science and Technology (MEXT) show that children’s physical fitness levels in Japan have declined since peaking in 1985, suggesting a broader trend of reduced physical capacity among recent generations [2].

At the same time, lifestyle changes, including reduced outdoor activity and increased sedentary behavior, have been suggested to contribute to decreased physical function [3–13]. In recent years, concerns have been raised that lifestyle changes, such as increased smartphone and video game use and reduced outdoor activity, may negatively affect children’s physical well-being [3]. Recent studies have provided empirical evidence linking smartphone use to reduced physical activity in children. Pawlowski et al. reported that banning smartphone use during school recess significantly increased children’s engagement in moderate physical activity across different grades and both sexes [4]. Similarly, Al-Amri et al. demonstrated that children classified as smartphone-addicted had significantly lower levels of physical activity compared to non-addicted peers in a large cross-sectional study [5]. Reduced balance and flexibility have been associated with increased fatigue, leg cramps, lower back pain, and shoulder stiffness. Furthermore, owing to decreased crisis management skills, in Japan, reports indicate an increase in pediatric fractures over the past decades [6], highlighting the relevance of fundamental motor abilities for injury prevention in the event of a fall, resulting in facial injuries and trauma, and an inability to participate in activities such as catching a ball. The level of daily physical activity (PA) established during childhood may persist and impact health status in adulthood [7–9]. Growing up without a habit of PA is believed to be associated with an increased risk of future osteoporosis due to failure to build bone mass at a young age [10]. Prolonged sedentary behavior is associated with nutrient intake [11]. Furthermore, it has been linked to the accumulation of risk factors for lifestyle-related diseases [12, 13].

To our knowledge, the associations between JMD and musculoskeletal pain or fracture history have not been systematically examined. Therefore, we hypothesized that lifestyle-related factors—including obesity, habitual physical inactivity, musculoskeletal pain, and a history of fractures—are associated with juvenile musculoskeletal dysfunction (JMD) in 8-year-old children. The Japan Environment and Children's Study (JECS), a nationwide birth cohort study conducted by the Ministry of Environment, was conducted in 15 regions throughout Japan. A follow-up survey of approximately 5,000 children was conducted within the scope of the present study in Amagasaki City, Hyogo. This study aimed to investigate the association between musculoskeletal pain, fracture history, and JMD in second-grade elementary school students (aged 8 years) who participated in the JECS.

Methods

Study design

This study was an Adjunct Study of the JECS. The JECS is a nationwide prospective birth cohort study funded by the Ministry of Environment, Japan. Approximately 100,000 pregnant women were recruited from 15 regional centers across Japan (45% of the entire birth study area) between January 2011 and March 2014 [14]. Children were followed up primarily through self-administered questionnaires completed by their mothers or guardians. The Hyogo Regional Center included approximately 5,000 participants from Amagasaki City, Hyogo Prefecture. This cross-sectional study was conducted as an Adjunct to the face-to-face examination by the JECS between FY2019 and FY2022, when the participants were in the second grade of elementary school. In accordance with the JECS protocol, the follow-up survey specifically targeted second-grade elementary school students, corresponding to children aged approximately 8 years. Therefore, this study focused exclusively on 8-year-old children. Examination sites were established in public facilities in the survey region during long vacations or weekends. This investigation was conducted during the latter two years, from July 2021 to October 2022. Overall, 1217 children (568 males, 649 females) participated in the present study. The exclusion criteria were as follows: 1) parental consent that could not be obtained, and 2) incomplete patient questionnaires. The physical assessments were conducted within JECS examination facilities, such as public community centers and elementary school gymnasiums. Each assessment was performed individually in a booth space separated by partitions (approximately 3 m × 3 m) to ensure privacy, safety, and standardized testing conditions.

All children and their parents were informed of the purpose by the JECS staff, and written informed consent was obtained. This study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of the Hyogo Medical University (IRB No.3746). Written informed consent for publication of the photographs in Fig. 1 was obtained from the child and their parents.

Survey items

Screening for JMD

JMD was defined as the inability to perform one or more of the following four physical movements: single-leg stance, squatting, upper-limb elevation, and forward bending [1]. These four movements were selected based on the content of musculoskeletal screenings conducted by the Japanese Orthopedic Association.

Single-leg stance: Standing on one leg for 5 s or longer without swaying or the other foot touching the ground.

Single-leg stance: Standing on one leg for 5 s or longer without swaying or the other foot touching the ground.

Squatting: Squatting until the buttocks touch the heels without raising the heels or falling backward.

Squatting: Squatting until the buttocks touch the heels without raising the heels or falling backward.

Upper-limb elevation: Lifting the upper extremities such that the arms touch the ears vertically.

Upper-limb elevation: Lifting the upper extremities such that the arms touch the ears vertically.

Forward bending: Bend forward from a standing position until the fingertips touch the ground with the knees straight.

Forward bending: Bend forward from a standing position until the fingertips touch the ground with the knees straight.

Two trained orthopedic surgeons (M.H. and S.T.) conducted physical examinations in the JMD booth at the examination venue by JECS.

Questionnaire survey

A parental survey evaluated musculoskeletal pain, history of fractures, and habitual PA. The parents completed the questionnaire during their children’s physical examinations. The survey responses were dichotomous, with participants providing "yes" or "no" answers. A “Yes” response for habitual physical activity was defined as participation in any regular exercise, sports, or active play outside of school physical education classes, at least once per week. Separate surveys were completed on weekdays and weekends, except for school lessons.

Physical measurements

Height, weight, and body fat percentage were measured using a body composition analyzer (MC-780A, Tanita, Tokyo, Japan). Trained staff also measured the abdominal waist circumference. The body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg)/height2(m2). Obesity was characterized by a BMI at or above the 95th percentile, whereas thinness was characterized by a BMI at or below the 5th percentile [15]. BMI percentiles were calculated using growth curves distributed by the Japanese Society for Pediatric Endocrinology [16]. As a result, for 8-year-old children (aged 8.0–8.9 years), the 95th percentile BMI ranged from 20.6 to 21.8, reflecting slight variation according to month age.

Statistical methods

JMP® 15 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) was used for data analysis, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. The Student’s t-test was used to analyze normally distributed variables. The chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test was used to analyze categorical variables. Variables associated with JMD (sex, musculoskeletal pain, history of fracture, obesity, and habitual PA) were used as covariates in the multivariate logistic regression analysis. The results are presented as odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the risk of JMD. Additional analyses were performed by stratifying participants according to the number of failed screening items to evaluate whether associations differed by severity.

Results

Characteristics

In total, 1217 children participated in the present study, 46.7% (n = 568) were girls.

Musculoskeletal pain was found in 83 children (6.8%). Approximately 112 children (9.2%) had a history of fracture. Obesity was observed in 47 children (4.0%), and thinness was found in 51 children (4.3%) (Table 1). The reported fractures were as follows: two facial, three skull, two clavicle, two shoulder, two upper arm, 25 elbow, six forearm, 12 wrist, 22 finger, two femur, four knee, 11 ankle, two foot, and nine toe fractures. Among children who reported musculoskeletal pain (n = 83), the most frequently affected sites were the foot (28.9%), knee (26.5%), and neck (12.0%). Less common sites included the lower leg (6.0%), wrist (6.0%), shoulder (4.8%), lower back (4.8%), ankle (4.8%), elbow (3.6%), and hip (2.4%).

The results of JMD screening are shown in Fig. 2. The prevalence of JMD in the present study was 36.0% (n = 438). The inability to perform “single-leg stance,” “squatting,” “upper-limb elevation,” and “forward bending” was 8.6, 10.3, 6.4, and 19.7% in all cases (male and female), respectively. Among males, the results were 10.2%, 12.6%, 6.9%, and 25.9%, respectively. Among females, they were 6.8, 7.6, 5.8, and 12.7%, respectively.

The results of screening for JMD

The characteristics were compared between the JMD and non-JMD groups using univariate analysis. Males reported a significantly higher prevalence than females. The JMD group had a significantly higher prevalence of musculoskeletal pain and a greater history of fractures than the non-JMD group. Children in the JMD group were significantly taller, heavier, and had a higher BMI, body fat percentage, and waist circumference. Children in the JMD group also had less habitual PA, which signifies a reduction in PA. There were significant differences in body shape, with a higher prevalence of thinness and obesity in the JMD group (Table 1).

Multiple regression analysis was used to evaluate the independent predictors of JMD, revealing a significant association between sex, obesity, habitual PA (weekdays), musculoskeletal pain, and history of fracture. Obesity showed the highest risk for JMD (OR: 3.06 [95%CI: 1.62–5.79, p < 0.001), whereas habitual PA (weekdays) showed the lowest risk for JMD (OR: 0.66 [95%CI: 0.47–0.92], p = 0.014). In addition, we performed multiple regression analyses for each of the four screening items (single-leg stance, squatting, upper-limb elevation, and forward bending) to identify their specific associated factors. The patterns of association varied across items, suggesting that each task reflects distinct aspects of musculoskeletal function. or the single-leg stance, significant associations were observed with obesity and habitual PA (weekdays). For squatting, sex (male) showed a significant association. For upper-limb elevation, a significant association was found with musculoskeletal pain. For forward bending, only sex (male) was significantly associated (Table 2).

Discussion

Juvenile musculoskeletal dysfunction

In recent years, there has been much discussion on how the lack of exercise associated with aging has reduced the basic physical abilities of many adults, making them more susceptible to musculoskeletal dysfunction [17]. Therefore, there has been an increase in physically inactive parents, and more children are becoming sedentary owing to the popularity of video games and smartphones [18]. There is an increasing number of children with low muscle strength who are prone to falling, poor posture, and inability to squat [19]. However, sports injuries due to excessive PA starting at a young age have also been reported [1]. Children today experience this polarization of issues [20]. Diminished foundational physical abilities due to childhood physical inactivity or excessive sports strain may lead to continued physical inactivity in adulthood [20, 21]. In this JMD screening, it was possible to evaluate physical flexibility and balance ability using a four-item checklist to screen for JMD. Hayashi has broadened the number of children with JMD through school medical examinations conducted by school doctors, teachers, and school nurses [1, 22]. The prevalence of JMD in this study (36%) was generally consistent with that reported in previous studies, including a large-scale school-based screening by Hayashi et al., who found a prevalence of 41.6% using the same four-item assessment [1]. In supplementary analyses, we examined each of the four JMD screening items separately. Interestingly, the associated factors varied by item: single-leg stance associated with obesity and habitual PA (weekdays), suggesting that balance may reflected body mass and exercise habits. Squatting was associated with obesity, indicating that increased body mass may limit basic lower-limb function. Upper-limb elevation was related to musculoskeletal pain, reflecting the potential impact of pain or shoulder mobility limitations on this task. In contrast, forward bending was associated only with sex, consistent with known gender differences in flexibility. Hayashi reported that 80–90% of children with JMD showed improvement following group-based guidance within a school screening program, demonstrating the potential benefits of early identification and intervention [1]. Furthermore, Strong et al. emphasized the importance of physical activity for musculoskeletal and overall health in school-aged children [23]. Although the present study is cross-sectional, our findings indicate that habitual physical activity was associated with a lower risk of JMD, supporting the relevance of promoting regular physical activity in childhood. These findings suggest that, while JMD is a useful composite measure, each component may capture distinct aspects of musculoskeletal health and warrants further investigation in longitudinal studies. Additional analyses stratified by the number of failed screening items demonstrated generally consistent associations across severity levels (Supplementary Table 2). However, obesity showed a stronger association in children with multiple failed items, suggesting greater relevance in more severe dysfunction.

Musculoskeletal pain and history of fracture

As recognized by the World Health Organization (WHO), Musculoskeletal pain in school-aged children is a well-acknowledged concern [24]. According to data from the WHO, low back pain, neck pain, and other musculoskeletal pain account for 2–12% of years of life with disability among 5–17 years worldwide [25], and there is an increase in the prevalence of low back pain during adolescence [26]. An epidemiological study of Ethiopian children (mean age 11.5 years) found that 60% experienced musculoskeletal pain, and screen time was identified as a significant factor associated with an increased risk of low back pain [27]. In Malaysia, 6.7–14.7% of children reported experiencing musculoskeletal pain in the past seven days, citing factors such as classroom furniture design and the weight of their school bags [28]. In Australia, 46% of children experience problematic pain, and some reports have indicated that adolescent musculoskeletal pain is associated with mental health issues [29]. The present study focused on 8-year-old children, 6.8% of whom reported musculoskeletal pain at the time of the survey. The prevalence of musculoskeletal pain varies according to region and age, highlighting international concerns. The study showed that JMD was associated with a history of fractures. Children with JMD may have reduced balance and flexibility, which could limit their ability to cope with unexpected situations and may partly contribute to fracture risk. According to statistics from the Japan Sport Council, the number of pediatric fractures has increased approximately 2.5 times over the past 40 years [30]. In the present study, the children with JMD had less habitual PA and experienced more fractures. Early detection and intervention of JMD may potentially help reduce the risk of musculoskeletal pain and fractures, although longitudinal evidence is needed to confirm such effects.

Body shape

In recent years, childhood obesity has been increasing due to changes in dietary habits and a decrease in outdoor play among children [31, 32]. Children who are overweight or obese and who remain obese into adulthood are at an increased risk of developing type 2 diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and carotid atherosclerosis [12, 13]. Chronic pain, obesity, reduced physical function, and reduced activity contribute to weight gain [33].

Previous studies have also demonstrated that insufficient physical activity is strongly associated with increased adiposity in children, and regular physical activity plays a crucial role in preventing childhood obesity [34, 35]. Although the mean BMI of the JMD group was not markedly high, the prevalence of obesity (BMI ≧ 20.59 to 21.79) was more than three times higher in the JMD group compared with the non-JMD group. This suggests that obesity may still be an important factor to consider in relation to JMD, even in a population with overall normal BMI averages. Considering the established association between physical inactivity and obesity, insufficient habitual physical activity may play a role in the development of both excessive body mass and musculoskeletal dysfunction. Future longitudinal studies are needed to clarify whether increasing daily physical activity could help maintain musculoskeletal health in children.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Second, JMD was defined using four screening tasks, which may not fully capture musculoskeletal dysfunction. Third, musculoskeletal pain, fracture history, and PA were assessed by parental questionnaires without detailed information on pain etiology, fracture circumstances, or PA intensity. Fourth, obesity was defined as ≥ 95th percentile for age and sex; although the mean BMI was not high, the prevalence of obesity was markedly higher in the JMD group, which should be interpreted with caution. Fifth, historical or generational data using the same criteria are not available, so it is unclear whether the prevalence of JMD observed here reflects an increase compared with previous generations. Sixth, physical activity was assessed using study-specific, non-standardized questions rather than a validated measurement instrument. This may limit the precision of activity assessment, and therefore the observed inverse association should be interpreted cautiously. Seventh, most of the subjects of this study resided in a single city, because we targeted only participants covered by the Hyogo Regional Center of the JECS. The city is located in a typical urban district in western Japan. Although the characteristics of the JECS participants were confirmed to be comparable to those of the general Japanese population [36], the findings of this study must be carefully generalized to other regions. Finally, unmeasured factors such as socioeconomic status, diet, and sleep may confound the results. Despite these limitations, our findings emphasize the importance of musculoskeletal assessment in childhood. Future longitudinal studies are needed to clarify causal relationships and to inform strategies for prevention and early intervention.

Conclusions

JMD was defined using a simple physical examination, and factors associated with JMD in 8-year-old children were investigated. JMD was more prevalent in children with obesity and in those with a history of fracture, habitual physical inactivity, and musculoskeletal pain. These findings highlight the importance of continued research to better understand the development of musculoskeletal dysfunction in childhood and to inform future strategies for prevention.
