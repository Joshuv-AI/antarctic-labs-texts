Development of a core outcome set for the assessment of spasticity in adults: a study protocol
Authors: Bridget Hill, Stephen Ashford, Hannah Barden, Lisa Shaw, Nick Waters, Gavin Williams, Megan Banky
Published: 2025

Background

Muscle spasticity is common in adult-onset neurological conditions such as stroke, traumatic brain injury, multiple sclerosis, and spinal cord injury [1–4]. It is a major contributor to disability and the economic burden of disease [5–7]. Although there is no consensus or agreed definition of spasticity, the two most commonly used definitions are 1) ‘a disorder of sensory-motor control resulting from an upper motor neurone lesion, presenting as intermittent or sustained involuntary activation of muscles’ [8] and 2) ‘disordered sensorimotor control, resulting from an upper motor neurone lesion, presenting as intermittent or sustained involuntary activation of muscles’ [9]. Muscle spasticity may cause bodily dysfunction such as pain, contracture, or reduced skin integrity and has been shown to negatively impact mobility, upper limb function, personal-care, and quality of life [10–12]. Clinical trials evaluating the effectiveness of spasticity interventions are plagued by poor reporting of trial outcomes. A 2022 review of 52 clinical trials found that reporting of results was so inconsistent that meta-analyses (comparison and summaries of key results) could not be conducted [13]. Lack of a standardised approach to measurement in spasticity research hampers the ability to advance an understanding of recovery mechanisms, devise better treatments, and consolidate knowledge. In clinical practice, outcome measures can be used to objectively assess patient progress, evaluate treatment outcomes, guide treatment planning, and appraise service delivery. Outcome measures can also be used for treatment planning, patient goal setting, and as such are a foundation of comprehensive clinical care as outlined in numerous clinical practice guidelines [7]. There is a need for greater consistency in assessment and reporting across both research and clinical practice, including the development and implementation of standardised protocols to ensure reliable, comparable, and clinically meaningful measurement of spasticity outcomes [8, 9, 14–16].

A core outcome set (COS) is an agreed minimum set of outcomes that should be measured and reported in all clinical trials of a specific disease or trial population. A COS can be disease or population specific, but it is not trial specific, rather a recommendation of what should be measured and reported in all clinical trials [17]. To date, a comprehensive COS for adults with spasticity has not been developed for clinical research or practice. The development of this COS will improve the field by providing a standardised framework for selecting, measuring, and reporting spasticity-related outcomes. Specifically, it will reduce heterogeneity across clinical trials, enabling more robust comparisons between studies, and facilitate meta-analyses and evidence synthesis through consistent outcome measurement. In clinical practice, the COS will improve comparability of results, supporting clinicians in evaluating the effectiveness of interventions and guiding treatment decisions. Ultimately, it will enhance the quality and interpretability of spasticity research, promoting the development of more targeted interventions and improved outcomes for adults with spasticity. This paper presents the protocol for the development of a core set of outcome measures, to be used and reported in trials of spasticity treatment and clinical practice using a consensus-driven approach.

Methods

Study design

This protocol has been developed according to the Core Outcome Measures in Effectiveness Trial (COMET) initiative guidelines [18], as well as the SPIRIT reporting guidelines [19]. The SPIRIT checklist and SPIRIT figure (Fig. 1) have been completed. It has been prospectively registered on the COMET website and is available online (comet-initiative.org/Studies/Details/3345). Outcome measures for inclusion in the COS will be based on COnsensus-based Standards for the selection of health Measurement INstruments (COSMIN). The study will adhere to the minimum standards for the design of a COS study (Core Outcome Set-Standards for Development [COS-STAD]) [20]. Adherence to this methodology advocates for COS development to improve consistency, reduce reporting bias, and enhance comparability across trials [18]. While COS methodology is increasingly used in other areas of health research, its application in rehabilitation remains limited. This study will therefore provide an important prototype within the rehabilitation context, specifically for spasticity assessment and management.Fig. 1SPIRIT figure. This study will be multiphase involving a variety of stakeholders and methodologies (Fig. 2). The COS will be derived by first identifying the domains essential to a comprehensive spasticity assessment. For each of these domains, we will then select the most appropriate existing outcome measure, guided by evidence on psychometric properties and clinical utility. More specifically, phase 1 involves semi-structured interviews to select domains that are important to people with a lived experience of spasticity based on the International Classification of Functioning Disability and Health (ICF). Domains identified in phase 1 will be combined with those identified by the authors from previous work in the field [7]. In phase 2, a Delphi survey involving a variety of experts in the field (see below) and people with lived experience of spasticity will be conducted to select the domains that will progress to phase 3. Phase 3 consists of systematic reviews of the literature to identify outcome measures specific to each domain selected in phase 2 and to identify the psychometric properties of the outcome measures. A second Delphi survey will be conducted in phase 4 to obtain consensus on outcome measures to be included in the COS [17]. Finally, if consensus recommendations cannot be agreed after phase 4, a virtual meeting will be held to finalise the COS.Fig. 2Study design flowchart. OMI = outcome measure instrument

The study has obtained ethical approval from The University of Melbourne HREC committee, study number 2025-31748-64667-3. All data collected will be gathered, shared, and maintained in accordance with the data management plan approved by the ethics committee, ensuring the confidentiality of all participants is strictly upheld. Only the research team (i.e. steering committee) will have access to the data collected throughout this study.

Steering committee

The project will be overseen by an international steering committee made up of health care professionals and researchers with expertise in spasticity and those with lived experience with spasticity. It will convene prior to each of the 4 phases, with additional meetings scheduled as required.

Stakeholder involvement

In addition to the steering committee, the following international stakeholders will be involved in the development of the core set as participants: (1) academic spasticity experts, (2) clinical spasticity experts, (3) industry/spasticity pharmacological representatives, and (4) people with lived experience of spasticity and their carers (see phase 2 for further details).

Phase 1: generation of domains

The aim of phase 1 is to identify domains important to people with a lived experience of spasticity and to combine these with key domains previously published in the literature. In a prior synthesis of 25 clinical practice guidelines, consensus statements, and Cochrane systematic reviews for the management of spasticity in adults (and children), 22 key domains were identified for consideration in spasticity assessment and management [7]. However, this literature does not take into account people with lived experience. As such, during phase 1 of this study, adults over 18 with lived experience of spasticity management and/or their carers will be invited to participate in a semi-structured interview via Zoom. The process of including people with lived experience in the development of a COS has shown to better represent the real-life impact of the phenomenon under investigation [21]. Information leaflets will be disseminated via social media and patient support groups in Australia and the UK, and consent forms distributed electronically. Participants will be required to sign an informed consent form prior to the completion of the interview. An interview guide will be used to ensure each interview is conducted in a similar manner. Participants will be asked to outline the impact of spasticity on their day-to-day life and what, if anything, changed because of the treatment provided for their spasticity. The sample size will be determined by data saturation. The first thematic saturation check will occur after the fifth interview and will be repeated after each subsequent interview. Saturation will be considered achieved when no new domains emerge in successive interviews. All domains identified throughout each interview will be cross-checked by two members of the steering committee, and new domains generated added to those previously identified [7]. The domains identified from these two sources (i.e. existing literature and the semi-structured interviews) will be combined and progress to phase 2.

This process will enable anonymous participation from a large number of the above four key stakeholder groups. There is no prerequisite sample size in Delphi studies [22]; however, the recommended sample size for multistakeholder Delphi surveys is 60–80 participants overall, with 20–30 per stakeholder group considered sufficient when feasible [23]. As we anticipate higher response rates from spasticity experts (clinicians and researchers) and lower rates from industry representatives and patients/carers, we will target >80 participants across all groups rather than equal distribution. The aim is to recruit 30–40 in groups 1 and 2, 10–15 industry representatives in group 3, and 15–20 people with lived experience of spasticity in group 4. The Delphi surveys will be distributed via REDCap surveys, hosted by The University of Melbourne. The only identifying information to be collected from participants is their email address, to receive the subsequent survey rounds. All other information will be de-identified, as per the approved data management plan.

In round 1 of the Delphi survey, participants will be asked to rate the domains identified in phase 1 on a 9-point Likert scale. In line with the COMET handbook, domains will be retained if they are scored 7–9 (critical importance) by more than 70% of participants and between 1 and 3 (limited importance) by less than 15% of participants. Conversely, domains will be excluded if they are scored 1–3 by at least 70% of participants and between 7 and 9 by less than 15%. Domains outside these categories (scoring 4–6 important but not critical) will be determined to not have reached consensus in round 1. Additionally, participants will be invited to add additional domains they feel are important via free text. All domains that did not reach consensus and any new domains added during round 1 will be collated and circulated in a second Delphi round. Participants will be provided with an overview of the results of round 1 for all retained and excluded domains together with the results of any domains still to be categorised. Round 2 will follow a similar process of voting to round 1. If there is still no clear consensus regarding whether a domain should be included or excluded, a third survey round will be sent with similar data provided to that in the second round. If consensus is still not reached after round 3, a consensus meeting will be held via Zoom with up to five participants across each of the stakeholder groups. All participants will be sent an email with the details of this meeting, with the first five participants to respond from each group to be included. These participants will be presented with the results and commentary regarding the domains under question and will have the opportunity to cast a final vote. This consensus meeting will be audio recorded and transcribed, to enable the researchers to re-listen to the meeting, ensuring that no important content is omitted. At the end of phase 2 if consensus is not obtained, that domain will not be taken forward to phase 3.

Phase 3: identification of potential outcome measures

The aim of phase 3 is to identify relevant outcome measures for each of the domains selected in phase 2. Potential outcome measures will be identified from existing systematic reviews of instruments designed to assess the domains identified, searching of existing instruments, guidelines, databases, or completing additional literature searches based on the COS-STAR reporting guidelines [20, 24]. The psychometric properties of each outcome measure will be identified including content, structural, cross-cultural and criterion validity, hypothesis testing for construct validity, internal consistency, reliability, measurement error, responsiveness [25, 26], and the clinical feasibility/utility of each measure considered [27]. A prioritised list of outcome measures with a clearly defined underlying construct that matches the identified domains with adequate validity, reliability, responsiveness, and clinical utility as defined by COSMIN will be presented to the participants for a second Delphi process in phase 4.

Phase 4: second Delphi—selection of outcome measures

The aim of the final phase of this project is to determine the outcome measures to be used in the spasticity COS using a second Delphi round. Only participant groups 1–3 will participate in phase 4. Given that this phase requires both a detailed knowledge of relevant outcome measures and an understanding of their psychometric properties, participants with lived experience with spasticity and their carers will not be involved in phase 4. All participants from phase 2 with the exception of those with lived experience of spasticity will be approached to take part in phase 4 and we envisage an attrition rate of approximately 25%. Participants will be presented with each of the outcome measures selected for each of the assessment domains, as well as a summary of their psychometric properties and clinical utility. The level of agreement will be the same as phase 2 with scoring from 1 to 9 on a Likert scale. Free text boxes will be available for participants to justify their choice, add any further outcome measures within the scope of the COS to be considered, or submit any relevant comments. The research team will apply the same pre-established consensus criteria to all the outcome measures.

As with the second round of the phase 2 Delphi, participants in round 2 of the phase 4 Delphi will be presented with a summary of the results and any relevant comments or feedback regarding each outcome measurement tool. They will then be asked to re-score outcome measures where consensus was not reached, as well as to score any new tools that were identified in the first Delphi round. Where there is no clear consensus regarding whether an outcome measure should be included or excluded, a third survey will be sent with similar data provided to that in the second round. If consensus is still not reached after the third round, a final consensus meeting will be held via Zoom with up to five participants across each of the stakeholder groups (groups 1–3). All participants will be sent an email with the details of this meeting, with the first five participants to respond from each group being included. These participants will be presented with the results and commentary regarding the assessment tools under question and will have the opportunity to cast a final vote. The aim of this final meeting is to obtain agreement regarding the outcome measure instruments to be included in the COS. This consensus meeting will be audio recorded and transcribed, to enable the researchers to re-listen to the meeting, ensuring that no important content is omitted. If at the end of phase 4, no consensus is obtained on an outcome measure for a particular domain, two potential outcomes may occur. First, where there is no clear endorsement for any outcome measure, no recommendation will be made. Second, if there is support for an outcome measure that did not meet the required threshold for inclusion, the outcome measure with the highest number of 7–9 ratings will be given a ‘provisional’ recommendation. A provisional recommendation may be a useful guide for researchers and clinicians in the absence of an endorsed recommendation.

Dissemination and knowledge translation

Dissemination and knowledge translation will be critical to ensure that the COS is adopted in both clinical practice and research, ultimately improving patient outcomes. Our strategy will include, but not be limited to, publication in open-access peer-reviewed journals, presentations at national and international conferences, and distribution through professional and clinical networks. The peer-review publications to stem from this work will be written in accordance with the ACcurate COnsensus Reporting Document (ACCORD) guidelines [28]. To support implementation, we will develop practical materials and summaries for patients, carers, and clinicians working in spasticity clinics to make the COS easier to apply in practice. Adoption will also be encouraged by linking the COS to research protocols, registries, and funding requirements, and by providing training opportunities such as webinars and workshops. Finally, the involvement of consumers and end-users throughout the development process will help ensure the COS remains practical, relevant, and sustainable across different healthcare settings.

Discussion

Studies evaluating spasticity interventions are highly heterogeneous, limiting the ability to conduct meta-analyses. A 2022 review of 19 systematic reviews on spasticity assessment highlighted wide variation in measurement methods and a lack of standardised guidelines [14]. Despite growing research in this area, findings remain inconsistent, emphasising the need for consensus-driven approaches to data collection, analysis, and interpretation to improve the reliability, comparability, and clinical relevance of spasticity research. To date, there is no COS for spasticity for adults with neurological conditions. The development of a spasticity COS including measures with robust psychometric properties and clinical feasibility/utility aims to reduce the inconsistency in outcome measurement selection in trials that should be comparable, therefore allowing meta-analyses and ultimately reducing research waste, and guide clinicians to optimal outcome measures for clinical practice.

A spasticity COS is urgently needed, as although at least 25 spasticity guidelines, consensus statements, and Cochrane reviews exist, few include recommended outcome measures [7]. Similar to the lack of consensus for the definition of spasticity, there is no consensus on optimal outcome measures for all the domains for which spasticity may impact. This protocol outlines a multiphase process to develop the first spasticity COS and represents an initial step facilitating higher-quality design and reporting in clinical trials that will enable great synthesis of evidence. It will also provide clinicians with a resource for documenting and reporting patient outcomes that can be used for benchmarking and optimising care.

Limitations

A limitation of this process is that patient and carer involvement was restricted to the UK and Australia. Nevertheless, the lived experience of people with spasticity such as daily limitations, functional difficulties, and impacts on participation is likely to be similar across geographical contexts, even though differences in healthcare systems and cultural factors may influence access to services and treatment pathways.

Trial status

The development of the COS is in its initial stages. The approved research protocol is version 3, dated 21 March 2025. Recruitment for phase 1 commenced on 3 April 2025 and was completed in May 2025. The first round of the phase 2 Modified Delphi survey was disseminated in August 2025 and closed in September 2025. Project completion is anticipated by December 2026.
