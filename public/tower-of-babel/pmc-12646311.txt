A binary-distributed effector modulates fungal host preference for drosophilids by targeting a lineage-specific immune factor
Authors: Yujuan Luo, Xinlu Sheng, Dongxiang Wei, Shuangxiu Song, Chen Chen, Hongyun Wu, Junmei Shang, Chengshu Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceLike plant pathogens, different strains of the entomopathogenic fungus Beauveria bassiana have varied virulence against diverse insect hosts. The underlying mechanism involved in mediating parasite host preference remains unknown. We performed a genome-wide association study of 258 B. bassiana strains based on their original insect hosts and identified a binary-distributed polymorphic effector Bhe1. This gene is required for full virulence of B. bassiana in drosophilids but not during infection of mosquitoes, locusts, or beetles. Interestingly, Bhe1 targets a drosophilid-specific factor Dsff1, which was verified as a novel immune factor to assist Bombardier (Bbd) for orchestrating the secretion of antifungal peptides. By blocking the Dsff1–Bbd interaction, Bhe1 illustrates a key mechanism in the host–parasite arms race.

Nonrandom infection of hosts by parasites commonly occurs, including either exclusive or nonexclusive parasite–host interactions. The former refers to a parasite’s host specificity, the ability of a parasite species to infect particular host species but not others (1, 2). Host preference (HP) in parasites is their tendency to infect certain host species more readily or effectively than others, often seen in diverse strains or races of a parasite species with a broad host range (3–6). For example, individual strains of the rice blast fungus Magnaporthe oryzae are classified into distinct pathotypes based on their differential pathogenicity on rice, wheat, barley, or millet (7). The race of Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. cubense specifically causes devastating damage to banana trees (8). The entomopathogenic fungus Beauveria bassiana has a broad host range (9, 10). A single strain of B. bassiana exhibits highly variable virulence across different insect species, while different strains show erratic infectivity against a given insect species (11, 12). For example, two strains of B. bassiana were found to be hyper- and hypovirulent, respectively, to silkworm (Bombyx mori) larvae (13). Different isolates of the acridid-specific fungal pathogen Metarhizium acridum exhibit considerable virulence variations against two locust species (14). In practice, extensive screening for a highly potent strain against the targeted insect pests is essential before developing cost-effective mycoinsecticides (15, 16). The key factor(s) determining the preference of isolate host selectivity and infectivity remain largely elusive.

Beyond ecological factors such as host availability and host abundance, coevolution between parasites and hosts has been considered as a key driver in shaping parasite host selection for specific hosts (17, 18). As reported, the presence of one or two effectors Pwl1 and Pwl2 in M. oryzae strains enables the fungus to infect chloridoid grasses (19). Instead, the recognition of Pwl2 by the immune receptor Mla3 prevents M. oryzae from infecting weeping lovegrass (Eragrostis curvula) (20). The avirulence (AVR)-Pik effector of M. oryzae has multiple variants, and the successful detection of AVR-Pik variants by the corresponding Pik receptor enables rice resistance to blast disease (21, 22). The effector mechanisms have also been validated in parasite–animal interactions (23, 24), including during fungus–insect interactions. For example, the alternative M. robertsii effectors Tge1 and Fkp1 can target the β-glucan receptors and the protease cascade that mediate the dual sensing of fungal infection in Drosophila (25, 26). Interestingly, studies have shown that Drosophila melanogaster has evolved divergent antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) that defend against distinct bacteria (27–29). For example, Diptericins (Dpt) have been verified with distinct roles: DptA inhibits the pathogen Providencia rettgeri while DptB acts against the gut mutualist Acetobacter species (27). It is unclear whether divergent effectors encoded by different strains determine isolate-specific preferences for particular insect lineages.

In this study, building on our previous population genomic investigation of B. bassiana strains (12), we conducted a genome-wide association study (GWAS) on 258 isolates after grouping them according to their original insect host orders. A binary distributed and highly polymorphic effector gene, termed Bhe1 (for Beauveria host-preference effector 1), was identified within an outlier genomic locus as a key determinant of fungal HP for drosophilids. Using D. melanogaster as an interaction model, we found that Bhe1 targets a drosophilid-specific protein (termed Dsff1) and blocks the Dsff1–Bbd interaction to facilitate fungal infection of flies.

Results

Identification of a Dipteran Host-Associated and Virulence-Required Outlier Locus.

We first performed the topical infection of D. melanogaster females with six B. bassiana strains originally isolated from different insect orders (Dataset S1), which revealed substantial difference in killing flies between strains (Fig. 1A). One strain (Bb3241), originally isolated from dipteran insects, was mostly virulent to Drosophila, followed by a strain (Bb2525) of Orthoptera (ORT) origin. A Hemiptera (HEM)-origin isolate Bb2118 was least virulent to flies among these examined strains (Fig. 1A). We further examined four strains each from the origins of Coleoptera (COL) and HEM insect orders, and virulence divergence was similarly evident between strains against female flies (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A and B).

To investigate the potential genetic basis of strain HP, we performed a GWAS analysis to identify loci with significant divergence between strains isolated from a specific insect order and those from other orders (Dataset S1). As a result, one extreme outlier locus (OL1) was detected on the chromosome (Chr) 1 of B. bassiana between Diptera (DIP) and HEM/Hymenoptera (HYM) of original hosts while an OL window (OL2) was identified on Chr 6 between DIP and other five orders of host origins (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C). By reference to the strain ARSEF 8028 of B. bassiana with a finished genome information (30), gene mapping analysis revealed that OL1 contains three genes: PQK09195-PQK09197 (Fig. 1B). Further analysis of these three genes showed that only PQK09196, encoding a putative secreted effector protein (Bhe1), is highly divergent among 258 strains (Dataset S1). These Bhe1 isoforms exhibit patterns of presence (56.98%) and absence (43.03%), and vary in length from 97 to 127 amino acids (Fig. 1C). Notably, above least virulent strain Bb2118 lacks Bhe1 (Dataset S1).

Sequence alignment revealed that the C termini of selected strain-specific isoforms are either variable or absent (Fig. 1D). By referring to strain mating type (MAT) and host-order origins (Dataset S1), MAT1-1 strains have a higher rate of Bhe1 absence (53.50%) than MAT1-2 strains (31.7%) (Fig. 1E). The presence/absence of Bhe1 equally distributes in the beetle-origin (COL) strains whereas Bhe1 is present in over 50% of strains isolated from other order insects (Fig. 1F). After grouping strains by geographic origin, the binary distribution of Bhe1 isoforms was evident in eight of the 12 cohorts (Dataset S1). For example, Bhe1 is absent in 61.6% (90/146) strains collected from the same forest farm across different seasons or years (12).

The OL2 locus encodes a putative ankyrin repeat-containing protein PQK16852 (1351 aa), which exhibits length variation among strains. For example, the isoform of this protein has a length of 985 aa (BBA_08961) in ARSEF 2860, 1618 aa (KAH8712346) in strain JEF-350, and 1453 aa (KAF1733559) in strain ERL836 of B. bassiana, confirming its polymorphic feature. Both Bhe1 and PQK16852 were then deleted in the wild type (WT) strain Bb3241 (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A), and the subsequent infection assays revealed that deletion of Bhe1 but not PQK16852 significantly impaired (Log-rank test: P < 0.0001) isolate virulence against D. melanogaster females (SI Appendix, Fig. S2B). The former was then focused for further analysis.

Bhe1 Is Required for Strain Virulence by Suppressing Drosophila Antifungal Immunity.

We performed the yeast secretion assay and verified that the signal peptide (SP) containing Bhe1 (1-19 aa) is a secreted protein (SI Appendix, Fig. S2C). RT-qPCR analysis revealed that Bhe1 was most highly transcribed in fungal cells harvested from insect hemolymph compared with fungal artificial growth on solid or in liquid media (SI Appendix, Fig. S2D). We further generated a genetic transformant overexpressing Bhe1 (OE::Bhe1) in Bb3241 (SI Appendix, Fig. S2E). Taken together with the above-generated ΔBhe1 strain, we observed no obvious phenotypic differences between the WT and mutant strains in artificial growth or in their stress responses to salt and H2O2 (SI Appendix, Fig. S2F). Subsequent topical infection and intrahemocoel injection assays confirmed that Bhe1 is required for the full virulence of B. bassiana against D. melanogaster. Thus, ΔBhe1 was significantly impaired (P < 0.01) in its ability to kill female flies, whereas OE::Bhe1 more quickly killed flies compared with the WT strain (Fig. 2 A and B). Similar survival patterns were observed following topical infection of the spotted-wing drosophila D. suzukii (Fig. 2C) and other drosophilids like D. yakuba, D. mojavensis, and D. hydei (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 G–I) with the WT and mutant strains. However, interestingly, there was no difference between WT and mutant strains during the topical infection of the mosquito Anopheles sinensis (DIP) females, mealworm beetle (Tenebrio molitor, COR) larvae, and migratory locust (Locusta migratoria, ORT) nymphs. However, survival differences were observed in wax moth (Galleria mellonella, LEP) larvae following topical infection with different strains. Mirroring the fly survival data, the ΔBhe1 strain showed delayed killing of caterpillars, whereas OE::Bhe1 promoted killing compared with the WT strain (Fig. 2D).

BLASTp analysis revealed that Bhe1 homolog is patchily distributed in other insect- or plant-pathogenic fungi, such as the presence in B. asiatica (KAK8150687; 127 aa, 96% identity at the amino acid level) and M. robertsii (MAA_10575; 107 aa, 76% identity; termed mBhe1). Intriguingly, its homolog is absent in the sequenced strains of B. brongniartii, Cordyceps militaris, and M. anisopliae, the close relatives of B. bassiana. Its homologous gene was further deleted in another B. bassiana strain (ASREF 8028; 127 aa in length), and M. robertsii. The subsequent survival assays confirmed that the Bhe1 homologous gene is essentially required for fungal infection of D. melanogaster (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 J and K). We also overexpressed Bhe1 in the gene-absent strain Bb2118 and found that the virulence of the mutant strains was significantly increased compared to WT strain (SI Appendix, Fig. S2L). Time course analysis of gene expressions indicated that the antifungal Drosomycin (Drs) and Metchnikowin (Mtk) genes were more highly induced in Drosophila infected with ΔBhe1 but downregulated in flies infected with OE::Bhe1 (Fig. 2 E and F). Taken together, Bhe1 is required for fungi to infect Drosophila by suppressing host immune responses.

Identification of a Drosophilid-Specific Target Gene.

We next aimed to determine the Drosophila factor targeted by Bhe1 for immune suppression by screening a yeast library we generated before (25, 31). By using Bhe1 as bait for yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) screening, positive clones were obtained on the selective medium, and eight candidate Drosophila proteins were identified: Six are extracellular and two are cytoplasmic (SI Appendix, Table S2). Subsequent Y2H verifications revealed that full proteins of three secretable candidates, i.e., CG15293, CG5948, and CG34054, positively interact with Bhe1 (Fig. 3A). These three proteins were then expressed in Escherichia coli cells, each fused with a glutathione S-transferase (GST) tag at their C termini. Bhe1 was expressed in fusion with a His (6×) tag at its C-terminus. These proteins were purified (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A) and used for immunoprecipitation (IP) analysis. The results confirmed that Bhe1 was successfully pulled down by Sepharose beads coupled to CG15293 but not by beads coupled to the other two proteins (Fig. 3B).

We conducted the BLASTp analysis against the NCBI and Flybase databases and found that CG15293, a functionally uncharacterized protein, is only present in drosophilid insects. The evolution of this protein largely followed the trajectory of Drosophila speciation (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). This protein was termed Dsff1 (Drosophila-specific factor against fungal infection) based on the evidence presented below. We generated a polyclonal antibody against Dsff1, and further IP analysis confirmed that Bhe1 successfully pulled down Dsff1 from fly hemolymph following fungal challenge (Fig. 3C). Bhe1 also targets the Dsff1 homolog of D. suzukii (XP_016945800, 31% identity; termed Ds-Dsff1). The Metarhizium mBhe1 also positively interacts with both Dsff1 and Ds-Dsff1 in Y2H analysis (Fig. 3D).

Having shown above the variable C-terminal region of Bhe1 isoforms, we next aimed to verify its effect on Bhe1–Dsff1 interaction by using isoforms of different lengths, including a truncated Bhe1 with the deletion of its C-terminal 30 aa. The results revealed that Bhe1 isoform length (i.e., C-terminal variation) had no obvious effect on the interaction between two proteins (Fig. 3E). Structure prediction demonstrated that different length variants of Bhe1 form rather conserved structures, all containing two α-helixes and four β-sheets with stranded C termini (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A). The predicted Dsff1–Bhe1 complex also exhibited the stranded feature of Bhe1 C-terminus (SI Appendix, Fig. S5B), supporting that, at least in part, the variable C-terminus of Bhe1 isoforms play a nonessential role in protein interactions. It is noteworthy that the virulence of different strains varies regardless of whether it has the same or different lengths of Bhe1 (Fig. 1A and SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A and B).

Dsff1 Is an Inducible Antifungal Factor Regulated by the Toll Pathway.

As indicated above, Dsff1 is a functionally-unknown protein. According to a previous microarray analysis, CG15293 expression is regulated by the Toll pathway (32). We performed the time course analysis of Dsff1 expression in D. melanogaster following topical infection with the WT Beauveria strain, and confirmed a significant upregulation pattern of this gene (Fig. 4A). However, this inducible expression pattern was either abolished or considerably impaired in the Toll-pathway mutants like Tl (Fig. 4B), MyD88 (Fig. 4C), and Dif1, but not in the Imd (immune deficiency) mutant RelE20 flies compared with mock controls (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 A and B).

To further validate Dsff1 as a Toll pathway component, we performed Western blot (WB) analysis to detect Dsff1 accumulation in the hemolymph of distinct Drosophila lines after fungal infection. Consistent with above RT-qPCR analysis, the infection-inducible accumulation of Dsff1 in hemolymph was either abolished or substantially impaired in the Tl, Dif1, and MyD88 flies but not substantially in the RelE20 flies (Fig. 4D). In contrast, Dsff1 protein accumulation in the blood and its gene expression were upregulated in the constitutively activating Da > Tl10B and Da > Spz* flies before and after fungal infection (Fig. 4E and SI Appendix Fig. S6C). Interestingly, mutations in upstream Toll factors, such as GNBP3, GL3 (GNBP-like 3), Psh, and psh;GNBP3, did not noticeably impair Dsff1 accumulation in the hemolymph following fungal infection. In contrast, Dsff1 induction was delayed but not abolished in spzrm7 flies (Fig. 4F). We also infected the WT w1118 flies with different fungal strains and found that ΔBhe1 infection most weakly induced Dsff1 accumulation, followed by infection with the least virulent Bhe1-absent strain Bb2118 compared with the WT and OE::Bhe1 infections. In addition, the M. robertsii infection also activated Dsff1 accumulation in the hemolymph (Fig. 4G). Taken together, Dsff1 is an inducible factor regulated by the Toll pathway in D. melanogaster.

Knocking Down Dsff1 Impairs Drosophila Antifungal Immunity.

As annotated in Flybase, Dsff1 overlaps genomically within the CG46301 gene but is transcribed in the opposite orientation (SI Appendix, Fig. S6D). Considering a mutation or insertion affects both genes, we instead obtained two independent Dsff1 RNAi fly lines for the survival experiments. Their RNAi efficiencies were validated upon B. bassiana infection (SI Appendix, Fig. S6E). Notably, CG46301 expressions were not downregulated in Dsff1RNAi flies (SI Appendix, Fig. S6F). Topical infection assays in Dsff1RNAi females showed significantly accelerated mortality when challenged with WT and OE::Bhe1 (particularly the latter) but not with the ΔBhe1 strain of B. bassiana (Fig. 4 H–J and SI Appendix, Fig. S6 G–I). Similar results were obtained after infection of male flies (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 J–L). Fungal load assays confirmed that Dsff1 RNAi significantly impaired Drosophila’s defense (P < 0.0001) against B. bassiana colonization (Fig. 4K). Dsff1RNAi flies also succumbed to M. robertsii infection faster than the mock control (SI Appendix, Fig. S6M). We also injected the Dsff1 RNAi flies with the Gram-positive (G+) bacterium Staphylococcus aureus and the Gram-negative (G−) bacterium Erwinia carotovora carotovora 15 (Ecc15). The results revealed that S. aureus but not Ecc15 caused some mortality; however, there was no statistically significant difference between RNAi and mock control flies (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 N and O). Collectively, Dsff1 is evident as an antifungal immune factor in association with the fungal effector Bhe1.

Adverse Effects in Drosophila Caused by Overexpression of the Dsff1 and Bhe1 Genes.

Having shown above that Dsff1 is not a canonical component of the Toll pathway but modulates antifungal immunity, we were curious to know whether Dsff1 overexpression in Drosophila could activate the Toll pathway. The UAS-Dsff1 transgenic flies were therefore generated and validated. The F1 progeny from crosses with the Da-Gal4 virgins showed high expression efficiency (SI Appendix, Fig. S7A). The antifungal genes Drs and Mtk were not activated in F1 flies without fungal infection (Fig. 4L), indicating that overexpressing Dsff1 per se could not activate the Toll pathway. Nevertheless, Da>UAS-Dsff1 flies survived significantly better than controls (P < 0.0001) after topical infection with the WT strain of B. bassiana (Fig. 4M). In support, fungal load assays revealed that significantly fewer fungal cells were detected in the Dsff1-overexpressing flies compared with the control (Fig. 4N). Dsff1-overexpressing flies also died more slowly than controls following the topical infection with ΔBhe1 (P < 0.0001) and M. robertsii (P < 0.01) (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 B and C).

We also generated the Bhe1-overexpressing flies (SI Appendix, Fig. S7D), and the subsequent survival assays revealed that Da>UAS-Bhe1 females died much faster (P < 0.0001) following topical infection no matter with the WT (P < 0.0001), ΔBhe1 (P < 0.01) or OE::Bhe1 strain (Fig. 4 O–Q) of B. bassiana. In addition, the Bhe1-absent strain Bb2118 and M. robertsii could more quickly kill the Bhe1-overexpressing flies (P < 0.0001) than mock controls (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 E and F). Collectively, the results from these reciprocal experiments confirmed that Dsff1 acts as an immune defense factor, while Bhe1 functions as an immune suppressor in Drosophila.

Dsff1 Interacts with Bbd That Can Be Blocked by the Fungal Effector Bhe1.

Next, to explore the mechanistic antifungal effect of Dsff1, we performed the screening of the Drosophila yeast-cDNA library using Dsff1 as bait. Positive clones were selected and sequenced, which identified nine candidates (SI Appendix, Table S2). Of which, nine out of 24 clones were sequenced to be Bbd, a drosophilid-specific secretable factor required for Toll-mediated immunity against fungal and G+ bacterial infections by enabling the proper secretion of inducible Bomanin (Bom) defense peptides (33, 34). We then focused on the verification of the interaction between Dsff1 and Bbd. Both Y2H and IP analyses confirmed the positive interaction between these two drosophilid-specific factors (Fig. 5 A and B). It was further evidenced that both Bhe1 and mBhe1 did not directly target Bbd (Fig. 5A). Furthermore, coexpression and IP analysis verified the interactive relationship between Dsff1 and Bbd in Drosophila S2 cells (Fig. 5C).

We obtained Bbd RNAi flies and validated that they exhibit effective gene silencing following fungal challenge (SI Appendix, Fig. S7G). Survival assays using the WT and mutant strains of B. bassiana yielded results being highly like those observed in the Dsff1RNAi flies. Thus, both the WT and OE::Bhe1 strains quickly killed BbdRNAi females (P < 0.0001), whereas the null mutant ΔBhe1 did not result in a survival difference between RNAi and control flies (Fig. 5 D–F). BbdRNAi females were also susceptible (P < 0.0001) to M. robertsii and Bb2118 (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 H and I). Western blotting revealed no obvious difference in hemolymph Dsff1 accumulation between BbdRNAi and control flies following fungal infection (Fig. 5G). Similar accumulation of inductive Bbd was detected in the Drosophila hemolymph between Dsff1RNAi and control flies, and between Da>UAS-Dsff1 and Da>mCherry transgenic flies after fungal infection (Fig. 5H). Subsequently, our yeast-three hybrid (Y3H) analysis revealed that Bhe1 efficiently blocks the Dsff1–Bbd interaction, as evidenced by both yeast cell survival and β-galactosidase activity assays (Fig. 5 I and J). Collectively, the accumulation of Dsff1 and Bbd in the hemolymph occurs independently, and their interaction can be inhibited by Bhe1.

Dsff1 Aids Bbd in Controlling the Proper Secretion of AMPs.

As reported, Bbd enables the proper folding and secretion of short-form Bomanins (33, 35). We found that the transcription of different AMP genes was similarly upregulated in Dsff1RNAi and BbdRNAi flies after fungal infection (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 A and B). The basal expression of these AMP genes in Drosophila was largely not affected in RNAi mutant flies before fungal infection (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 C and D). The hemolymph samples of both Dsff1RNAi and BbdRNAi flies were then collected for the label-free quantitative proteomics (Dataset S2) and Matrix-assisted Laser Desorption/Ionization-Time-of-flight (MALDI-TOF) mass spectrometry (MS) analysis. The MS/MS count data confirmed that Dsff1 and Bbd turnovers were significantly reduced (approximately 50%) in Dsff1RNAi and BbdRNAi flies (Fig. 5K). Largely supporting the above WB results, Bbd level was unaffected in Dsff1RNAi flies, whereas Dsff1 counts were increased (P < 0.05) in BbdRNAi flies compared to controls. Otherwise, in contrast to the upregulation of AMP gene expression, the detected AMPs were present at either comparable or reduced levels in the hemolymphs of RNAi flies (Fig. 5L). In particular, except for the increased level of Bomanin bicipital 2 (BomBc2), all other detected Bomanin isoforms (BomBc1, 3 and the tailed form BomT2) were present at similar levels in RNAi and reference flies. The other AMPs, e.g., Paillotin (Pai, IM18) (29), Mtk, Diptericin DptB, Cecropin CecC, and defensin Def, were detected at reduced levels either in both RNAi flies or only in Dsff1RNAi flies (Fig. 5L and SI Appendix, Fig. S8E). The antifungal factor Baramicin (BaraA) (36, 37) was detected in the highest amount. Its level exhibited an increase (P < 0.05) in BbdRNAi flies but not in Dsff1RNAi insects. These AMP spectral count data were supported by peptide ion intensities (SI Appendix, Fig. S8F).

Consistent with the previous analysis (33), short-form Bomanins were not detected in proteomic analysis. Both the linear and reflectron mode of MALDI-TOF analysis revealed that, relative to the mock control, small Boms like BomS1 and BomS3 were detected in both Dsff1RNAi and BbdRNAi flies with a reduction pattern (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 A–D). Small Boms were also marginally detected in the Bhe1-overexpressing flies when compared with the control sample (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 E and F). Intriguingly, our proteomic analysis also failed to detect the Daisho peptides Dso1 and Dso2 with antifungal activity (38). Collectively, the data indicate that Dsff1 and Bbd work together to mediate the secretion of not only Boms but also other AMPs, and Bhe1 can block their interaction and proper function.

Discussion

In this study, we identified an effector-like gene Bhe1 in B. bassiana populations that exhibits not only a binary presence/absence distribution but also encodes length-variant isoforms. Loss- and gain-of-function studies revealed that Bhe1 modulates fungal HP toward Drosophila. Mechanistically, Bhe1 and its length-variant isoforms can similarly target Dsff1. Dsff1 interacts with Bbd while Bhe1 blocks this interaction. Both Dsff1 and Bbd are inducible drosophilid-specific factors required for Toll-mediated immunity against fungal infection. Collectively, our results reveal a mechanism for parasite HP that might have been shaped by coevolution.

Fungi have complex pangenomes that allow them to adapt to diverse environments (39). Mirroring our finding of Bhe1 in B. bassiana population, pangenome analysis of the rice- and wheat-infecting M. oryzae revealed an array of gene presence/absence variations (PAVs) between two groups of strains, including the PAV-type effectors (40). For example, the Oryza-pathotype specific effector MoNLE1 of M. oryzae is a critical virulence factor by targeting a highly conserved cofactor required for protein quality control in rice (41). The virulence of the oomycete pathogen Phytophthora infestans on potatoes is linked to its PiAVR2 effector, which exhibits not only a PAV pattern among isolates/genotypes but also C-terminus length polymorphisms (42). The tomato immune receptor Ve1 can recognize the Ave1 effector variants, despite sequence and length differences, from multiple fungal pathogens (43). Thus, PAV-type effectors also occur in animal pathogens, highlighting a need for future investigations.

Mechanistically, PAV-prone genes in M. oryzae are frequently located near transposable elements and exhibit distinct epigenomic features, both factors that drive gene mutations, including loss and gain (40, 44). Upon inspection, we found no transposase gene near Bhe1. The absence of Bhe1 homologs in the sequenced strains of B. bassiana close relatives, such as the beetle-specific species B. brongniartii (45) and the caterpillar-specific C. militaris (46), suggests that these fungi might not have undergone an arms-race coevolution with Drosophila. However, the PAV pattern of the Bhe1-like gene within these fungal populations cannot be ruled out. Both Dsff1 and Bbd are evolutionarily new genes of drosophilids (47, 48). In D. melanogaster, mechanisms such as gene duplication and transposon-mediated retroposition have been suggested as drivers of de novo gene origination (49). The exact mechanisms underlying the Bhe1 PAV pattern and the origination of Dsff1/Bbd remain to be determined.

We found that the Diptera-origin strain Bb3241 is highly virulent to Drosophila, while the Hemipteran-origin and Bhe1-absent strain Bb2118 was least pathogenic toward D. melanogaster among the examined isolates. Similarly, more severe tree damage was caused by Colletotrichum anthrisci strains isolated from conspecific trees compared with those isolated from heterospecific trees (50). Nevertheless, we also evidenced that strains originally isolated from other dipteran insects (e.g., Bb2348 with a Bhe1 gene) showed mild pathogenicity toward Drosophila, whereas those isolated from non-Dipteran orders were highly lethal to flies. Given that entomopathogenic fungi like Beauveria and Metarhizium species employ multiple virulence factors for host infections (51, 52), Bhe1 is unlikely to be the sole determinant of Beauveria’s infectivity in flies. However, generating Bhe1-null and overexpressing mutants revealed that this effector is essential for B. bassiana virulence against multiple Drosophila species but dispensable for infecting mosquitoes, beetles, or locusts. Collectively, these results well support Bhe1 as a HP factor for drosophilids. It is noteworthy that fly-like survival differences were also observed in wax moth larvae after infection with the WT and Bhe1 mutant strains. It remains to be determined whether Bhe1 has any specific targets in caterpillars, which share a common ancestor with dipteran insects (53).

Additional evidence supporting Bhe1 as a HP factor for drosophilids was obtained from the infection of mutant flies. Unlike WT and OE::Bhe1, ΔBhe1 caused mortality in DsffRNA1 and BbdRNAi flies that was comparable to that in control flies. Likewise, the M. oryzae-specific effector MoErs1 promotes blast disease by targeting the rice papain-like protease OsRD21, as evidenced by the restoration of ΔMoErs1 virulence when infecting OsRD21 null plants (54). We also found that ΔBhe1 infection significantly increased mortality in the Bhe1-overexpressing flies while delaying mortality in the Dsff1-overexpressing flies. Collectively, our data reinforce the gene-for-gene relationship between Bhe1 and Dsff1, and suggest that disrupting the Bhe1–Dsff1 complex leads to a loss of fungal HP and virulence in Drosophila. This kind of interaction mirrors the AVR effector–NOD-like receptor (NLR) paradigm in plant immunity, where host resistance hinges on the precise molecular recognition of AVR proteins by their cognate NLRs (55, 56). Overall, while effector mechanisms are well characterized in pathogen–plant interactions, they remain poorly understood in fungus–animal systems.

We established that Dsff1 is required for antifungal immunity in Drosophila. Dsff1 induction and accumulation in the hemolymph were not affected in the null mutants of dual circulating sensors, such as G3ΔGL3Δ, Psh1 or psh;GNBP3, but were completely lost in the Toll and MyD88 mutants of D. melanogaster after fungal infection. This pattern resembles the expression and accumulation dynamics of GL3, which functions as a positive feedback-loop amplifier to boost Drosophila antifungal immunity (25). Besides the possibility of immune compensation (26, 57), the function of alternative components, especially those serine proteases, may support the activation of the Toll pathway to express Dsff1 in those mutant flies (58, 59). Drosophila overexpressing Dsff1 exhibited a significantly increased ability to defend against fungal infection. Further experiments revealed that Dsff1 binds Bbd, and both proteins play comparable roles in mediating fly antifungal defenses. Bhe1 blocks the Dsff1–Bbd interaction, suggesting that complex formation is a prerequisite for their immune function. Although both Dsff1 and Bbd are secreted and circulated in hemolymph, their potential anchoring to the plasma membrane to facilitate AMP folding and secretion warrants further investigation.

Following fungal infection, Dsff1RNAi and BbdRNAi flies exhibited upregulated AMP gene expression but reduced peptide accumulation. Dsff1 is thus identified as a novel antifungal factor that functions in concert with Bbd to coordinate the secretion of antifungal peptides. Bbd was reported to mediate the specific control of Bomanin secretions following challenge with fungi and the G+ bacteria (33). In addition to Bomanins, we found that accumulation of the antifungal defensins like Baramicins (36, 37), Daishos (38), and even Drs was also highly reduced in Dsff1RNAi and BbdRNAi flies after natural infection with B. bassiana. The underlying mechanisms require further investigation.

In conclusion, our study reveals that B. bassiana employs a PAV effector to promote preferential Drosophila infection by targeting a lineage-restricted antifungal immune factor. The unearthing of a novel immune factor in the Drosophila immune system uncovers both a new factor in host defense and a key target in pathogen coevolution, highlighting a compelling example of the evolutionary arms race between hosts and parasites.

Materials and Methods

Microorganisms and Maintenance.

Different WT strains, such as Bb3241, Bb2118, ARSEF 2860 and 8028, and mutants of B. bassiana, and the WT strain ARSEF 2575 and mutants of M. robertsii (60) were grown on potato dextrose agar (BD Difco) for conidiation at 25 °C for 2 wk. Fungal strains were grown in Sabouraud dextrose broth (BD Difco) for RNA extraction after incubation for 36 h at 200 rpm and 25 °C. The Y2H Gold (Weidi Biotech) and AH109 (TaKaRa Bio) strains of budding yeast Saccharomyces cerevisiae were maintained on the synthetic dropout (SD) media (Sigma-Aldrich) and used for Y2H and Y3H assays, respectively. Different E. coli strains were cultured on the Luria-Bertani (LB) agar or in LB broth at 37 °C for gene cloning (Top10, Weidi Biotech) or protein expression [BL21(DE3)] at 16 °C. The AGL1 strain of Agrobacterium tumefaciens was used for fungal transformation (31). The G+ bacterium S. aureus and the G- Ecc15 were grown in LB broth and used for injection of different flies.

Drosophila Stocks.

The w1118 strain of D. melanogaster (BDSC: 5905) was used for fungal infections. The male RNAi flies of Dsff1 (CG15293; VDRC: 102861 from the KK library; VDRC: 44627 from the GD library), Bbd (VDRC: 4726), mCherry (BDSC: 35785), and the KK library (VDRC: 60100) and GD library (VDRC: 60000) reference line were crossed with the virgins of Da-Gal4;tub-Gal80ts driver flies (31, 61). The UAS-Tl10B (62) and UAS-Spz* (63) males were also crossed with the virgins of Da-Gal4;tub-Gal80ts flies for different experiments. Toll and Imd pathway mutant flies Dif1, Tl (BDSC: 13022), MyD88, psh1, spzrm7, RelE20, psh;GNPB3, GNBP3Δ, GL3Δ, and G3ΔGL3Δ (25, 57) were used for Western blot analysis after topical fungal infection. Fly stocks were maintained at 25 °C and 12 h of light/dark cycles on the Bloomington formulation of cornmeal agar medium (64).

Quantification and Statistical Analysis.

One-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s test or two-tailed Student’s t test was performed to compare the significance of differences in gene expression and MS/MS counts between samples. The log-rank tests were conducted to compare the insect survival kinetics between different challenges using the software GraphPad Prism (ver. 10.1, GraphPad Software Inc.).

Other methods for GWAS analysis, fungal transformation, insect bioassays, yeast library screening and verification, RT-qPCR, and proteomic and MALDI-TOF analyses are provided in SI Appendix, Supporting Text.
