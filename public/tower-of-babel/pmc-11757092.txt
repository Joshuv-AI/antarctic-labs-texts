ZW10: an emerging orchestrator of organelle dynamics during the cell division cycle
Authors: Sm Faysal Bellah, Fengrui Yang, Fangyuan Xiong, Zhen Dou, Xuebiao Yao, Xing Liu, Jiarui Wu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractZeste white 10 (ZW10) was first identified as a centromere/kinetochore protein encoded by the ZW10 gene in Drosophila. ZW10 guides the spindle assembly checkpoint signaling during mitotic chromosome segregation in metazoans. Recent studies have shown that ZW10 is also involved in membrane-bound organelle interactions during interphase and plays a vital role in membrane transport between the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus. Despite these findings, the precise molecular mechanisms by which ZW10 regulates interactions between membrane-bound organelles in interphase and the assembly of membraneless organelle kinetochore in mitosis remain elusive. Here, we highlight how ZW10 forms context-dependent protein complexes during the cell cycle. These complexes are essential for mediating membrane trafficking in interphase and ensuring the accurate segregation of chromosomes in mitosis.Keywords: ZW10, kinetochore, membrane-bound organelle, membraneless organelle, mitosis

Introduction

Mitosis is a dynamic and essential process involving multiple proteins that independently coordinate the precise separation of genetic material into two daughter cells. Errors encountered in mitosis can lead to aneuploidy and chromosomal instability, which is a hallmark of cancer (McAinsh and Kops, 2023). Kinetochores are multiprotein complexes that perform multiple functions and are crucial for accurate chromosome segregation during mitosis (Liu et al., 2020; Ismail et al., 2021). They are responsible for chromosome attachment to the spindle, control of microtubule (MT) dynamics, generation of the force necessary for chromosome movements, and the function of the spindle assembly checkpoint (SAC). Human kinetochores manifest a trilaminar morphology, consisting of the inner plate, the outer plate, and the interzone (Cleveland et al., 2003). Inner kinetochore proteins constitutively associate with centromeric DNA throughout the cell cycle. However, outer kinetochore proteins only assemble upon mitotic entry and dissemble after mitotic exit. Currently, ∼150 proteins have been reported to associate with the centromere–kinetochore complex in human cells (Raisch et al., 2022). Analysis of the functional and biochemical properties of these proteins led to their classification into many groups. Two major protein complexes are significantly conserved within the kinetochore arena: the Knl1–Mis12–Ndc80 network, which is located within the outer kinetochore region, and the constitutive centromere-associated network (CCAN), which is located within the inner kinetochore region (Cheeseman and Desai, 2008).

The cell cycle in eukaryotic cells is divided into two major phases: interphase and mitotic phase (M). Interphase initiates with a primary growth phase (G1) and then moves to DNA replication, referred to as the synthesis phase (S), and a secondary growth phase (G2) prior to cell division or mitosis. After mitosis, the cell continues to enter the next G1 phase or enters a postmitotic quiescent phase, G0. Mitosis involves five distinct stages followed by cytokinesis (Jongsma et al., 2015). In prophase, replicated chromatin condenses, and the nuclear envelope breaks down, followed by chromosomal alignment and attachment to MTs from replicated centrosomes during prometaphase and metaphase. Anaphase begins with chromosomal separation. Nuclear envelopes reconstruct two nearby DNA pools during telophase, and cytokinesis occurs, which releases new daughter cells (Jongsma et al., 2015).

Zeste white 10 (ZW10) is a mitotic checkpoint protein and the anchor for cytoplasmic dynein at mitotic kinetochores, although it is expressed throughout the cell cycle. The ZW10 protein is essential for accurate chromosome separation in Drosophila, and it has been confirmed that Drosophila ZW10-null mutants show chromosome mis-segregation (Williams et al., 1992, 1996; Williams and Goldberg, 1994). The mitotic function of ZW10 was further confirmed by frequent observation of chromosomal bridges and lagging chromosomes after injection of anti-ZW10 antibody into HeLa cells (Chan et al., 2000). Most chromosomal imbalances during growth lead to embryonic death and chromosomal instability, which are linked to tumorigenesis (Lengauer et al., 1998; Cohen, 2002). As long as one chromosome is not properly connected to MTs, there will be an active mitotic checkpoint to prevent the beginning of anaphase (Li and Nicklas, 1995; Rieder et al., 1995; Cleveland et al., 2003). Unattached kinetochores produce a diffusing ‘waiting anaphase’ signal to prevent the activation of anaphase-promoting complex/cyclosome (APC/C), which initiates anaphase onset by destroying securin and cyclin B (McAinsh and Kops, 2023). The diffusible inhibitory complex is called the mitotic checkpoint complex (MCC), which comprises BubR1, Bub3, Mad2, and Cdc20, the obligatory activators of APC/C for the recognition of mitotic substrates (Figure 1; Fang et al., 1998, 2002; Sudakin et al., 2001; Tang et al., 2001). ZW10 and rough deal (ROD) are essential in producing a sustained mitotic checkpoint in both fruitfly and human cells (Basto et al., 2000; Chan et al., 2000).

The eukaryotic SAC monitors the kinetochore–MT attachment and inhibits anaphase onset until all kinetochores are properly attached to spindle MTs. Unlike Mads, Bubs, Mps1, and AuroraB/Ipll, homologs of ZW10 or its binding partners ROD and Zwilch have not been found in fungi (Starr et al., 1997). In higher eukaryotes, cytoplasmic dynein is involved in silencing the SAC by removing the checkpoint protein Mad2 and the ROD–ZW10–Zwilch (RZZ) complex from aligned kinetochores (Figure 1; Griffis et al., 2007; Yang et al., 2007; Famulski et al., 2008). ZW10, conserved across large evolutionary distances, interacts with dynamitin, the p50 subunit of the dynactin complex, and facilitates the recruitment of the dynein–dynactin complex to kinetochores (Echeverri et al., 1996; Starr et al., 1997, 1998). The RZZ complex acts as a dynein–dynactin receptor on kinetochores. Disruption of ZW10 or ROD function in Drosophila, human cells, or Xenopus mitotic extracts leads to premature onset of anaphase in the presence of MT inhibitors (Basto et al., 2000; Chan et al., 2000; Savoian et al., 2000; Kops et al., 2005), and dynein-mediated poleward transport of RZZ leads to SAC inactivation (Howell et al., 2001; Wojcik et al., 2001).

ZW10 and ROD play crucial roles in ensuring accurate segregation of chromosomes during meiosis and mitosis. Abnormal chromosomal separation can have catastrophic effects on an organism or its offspring (Williams et al., 1992, 1996; Williams and Goldberg, 1994; Starr et al., 1997; Wang et al., 2004). Similar to SAC deficiency, depletion of ZW10, ROD, and Zwilch yields mitotic phenotypes, such as lagging chromosomes and DNA bridges at anaphase, leading to high rates of chromosome mis-segregation and aneuploidy in both mitotic and meiotic cells (Starr et al., 1997; Scaerou et al., 2001). The kinetochore localization of each subunit of the RZZ complex is interdependent (Scaerou et al., 2001; Williams et al., 2003). Zwint-1 mediates the kinetochore recruitment of ZW10, which in turn depends on Hec1 for kinetochore localization (Wang et al., 2004; Lin et al., 2006). The RZZ complex is necessary for kinetochore enrollment of both the dynein–dynactin complex and the Mad1/Mad2 complex (Starr et al., 1998; Buffin et al., 2005; Kops et al., 2005). The dynein–dynactin complex transports the RZZ complex and the Mad1/Mad2 complex to spindle poles away from kinetochores during metaphase, resulting in SAC inactivation (Figure 1; Karess, 2005). The functional SAC in metazoans needs several additional factors, including ROD, ZW10, Zwilch, Zwint-1, CENP-E, and CENP-I (Karess, 2005). CENP-A acts as a platform for the spatial organization of downstream checkpoint components. Additionally, Aurora B, Bub1/BubR1, and the Ndc80 protein complex are compulsory for precise kinetochore–MT binding in addition to their checkpoint function (Meraldi and Sorger, 2005; DeLuca et al., 2006; Pinsky et al., 2006).

Membrane-bound and membraneless organelle dynamics in association with ZW10

The association between ZW10 and the Golgi apparatus plays a critical role in cytoplasmic dynein function during interphase. Starr et al. (1998) discovered an interaction between ZW10 and dynamitin. ZW10 is located in the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), Golgi apparatus, and cytoplasm during interphase and contributes to ER–Golgi trafficking through a soluble N-ethylmaleimide-sensitive factor attachment protein receptor (SNARE)-dependent mechanism (Hirose et al., 2004). ZW10 associates with specific interphase structures and regulates minus end-directed Golgi, endosome, and lysosome transport (Varma et al., 2006). The overall immunostaining pattern of ZW10 is similar to that of the ER marker calnexin. In addition, ZW10 was found to extensively colocalize with Syntaxin18 during interphase. Suppression or overexpression of ZW10 significantly disrupts membrane trafficking between the ER and Golgi (Hirose et al., 2004; Arasaki et al., 2006). It is reasonable to speculate that ZW10 localizes to the Golgi apparatus as a result of its movement to the minus ends of MTs. Indeed, comprehensive interactions were found between ZW10 and the Golgi, endosomes, and lysosomes (Varma et al., 2006). Disrupting ZW10 function using different loss-of-function methods leads to dispersion of the Golgi, endosomes, and lysosomes, strongly supporting a general role for ZW10 in regulating dynein function. While previous studies attributed similar phenotypic effects to a SNARE-related mechanism, ZW10 has been shown to have an additional layer of regulation in mitotic dynein function (Starr et al., 1998; Hirose et al., 2004). Overexpression of ZW10 affects the morphology of organelles in early secretory pathways, leading to the redistribution of various proteins, including a tethering protein in the cis-Golgi, an integral membrane protein in the ER–Golgi intermediate compartment (ERGIC), a cis-Golgi integral membrane protein, and a cis-Golgi matrix protein (Schweizer et al., 1990; Waters et al., 1992; Dascher et al., 1994; Nakamura et al., 1995). Sec31 (an ER exit marker) is localized to ER exit sites and essential for ER–Golgi transport (Tang et al., 2000). Short-range vesicular transport from ER exit sites to the ERGIC depends on COP II but not MTs. However, long-range vesicular transport from the ERGIC to the cis-Golgi requires MTs as well as the dynein motor. The fission of anterograde cargo-rich anterograde carriers from the ERGIC may involve the COP I coat, the spectrin/ankyrin skeleton, ZW10, and the dynein–dynactin complex (Allan et al., 2000; Moyer et al., 2001; Rabouille and Klumperman, 2005).

The ER is located adjacent to the nucleus and made up of a number of flattened sacs called cisternae, which are continuous with the nuclear envelope. The rough ER has a great deal of ribosomes on its outer surface, and its transport proteins are synthesized in ribosomes, whereas the smooth ER does not have ribosomes and synthesizes lipids (Schwarz and Blower, 2016). The ER is generally called the intracellular highway, as it transports all kinds of substances around the cell (Saraste and Kuismanen, 1992). In addition to organelles composed of membranes, eukaryotic cells contain many membraneless organelles that concentrate specific factors and resources, including ribonucleoprotein granules, processing bodies (PBs), and stress granules. PBs and stress granules are made up of nontranslating messenger RNAs (mRNAs) as well as related proteins and deliver diverse biochemical environments for controlling the translation and degradation of mRNA (Banani et al., 2017; Lee et al., 2020; Tauber et al., 2020). PBs are tethered at ER membrane contact sites (MCSs) in human cells. PB biogenesis and the ER–PB contact are organized by altering the PB composition, ER shape, or ER translational capacity (Lee et al., 2020). The Golgi apparatus is a stack of membrane-bound organelles and flattened sacs that are responsible for the modification of proteins received from the ER. These proteins are then transported in vesicles around the cell. ZW10 may play an important role in the ER, Golgi apparatus, and membraneless organelles, as it is involved in ER–Golgi transport (Hirose et al., 2004; Brandizzi, 2018). Therefore, ZW10 is significantly involved in vesicular transport from the ER to the Golgi apparatus.

During the ER export process, proteins in the ERGIC become more complex and require support for transport and degradation (Appenzeller et al., 1999; Muniz et al., 2000; Belden and Barlowe, 2001; Appenzeller-Herzog et al., 2005). The transport of proteins from the ER to the Golgi through the ERGIC occurs in a pH-dependent manner (Figure 2). The pH of the ER lumen is neutral (pH 7.4) (Wu et al., 2001), and the ERGIC is in a more acidic environment (pH < 7) in the secretory pathway. The exact pH of the ERGIC may be even lower than that of the trans-Golgi network (pH 6.4) (Machen et al., 2003). Compared with the pH in the ER, the slightly decreased pH in the ERGIC might promote the dissociation of cargo–receptor complexes and other factors (Figure 2). For successful anterograde delivery from the ERGIC to the Golgi, cargo proteins need to dissociate from their receptors in the ERGIC. For instance, ERGIC-53 functions as a transport receptor for the secretory/lysosomal protein procathepsin Z (Pro-catZ). The association between ERGIC-53 and Pro-catZ occurs in the ER at neutral pH. Cell acidification inhibits the association of ERGIC-53 with Pro-catZ-related proteins (Appenzeller-Herzog et al., 2004). Furthermore, the decreased pH in the ERGIC is beneficial for the effective recognition and retrieval of KDEL-bearing proteins from post-ER compartments via the KDEL receptor (Scheel and Pelham, 1996). Most interestingly, pH also plays a significant role in the retrograde transport of cargo–receptor complexes from the ERGIC and cis-Golgi to the ER (Figure 2; Scheel and Pelham, 1996; Appenzeller-Herzog et al., 2004).

ZW10 is consistently expressed throughout the cell cycle (Starr et al., 1997). Dynamitin is the interacting partner of ZW10, which regulates the role of dynein in general (Burkhardt et al., 1997). Precise colocalization of ZW10 might be detected with the Golgi markers β-coatomer protein, γ-adaptin, and N-acetylglucosaminyltransferase. Additional ZW10-positive spots can be observed throughout the cell. ZW10 appears to be mainly present in the insoluble fraction in cell extracts but can be solubilized by using a nonionic detergent, suggesting the membrane association of ZW10 (Starr et al., 1997). ZW10 was also found enriched at Golgi membranes in subcellular fractionation studies (Varma et al., 2006).

ZW10 forms two complexes: the RZZ complex (Figure 1) and the NRZ complex (Figure 3). The NRZ complex consists of neuroblastoma amplified gene (NAG), Rad50 interacting protein 1 (RINT1), and ZW10. The NRZ complex is connected to the Golgi and ER in mammalian cells and interacts with the ER membrane SNARE proteins Use1p/p31 and BNIP1/Syntaxin18 (Hirose et al., 2004; Arasaki et al., 2006, 2007; Varma et al., 2006; Aoki et al., 2009). However, it is uncertain whether the NRZ complex facilitates ER-to-Golgi anterograde trafficking or Golgi-to-ER retrograde trafficking through the ERGIC (Figure 2; Hirose et al., 2004; Arasaki et al., 2006; Aoki et al., 2009). Depletion of ZW10 and RINT1, rather than NAG, results in Golgi damage, suggesting that ZW10 and RINT1 play a central role in Golgi construction (Hirose et al., 2004; Arasaki et al., 2006; Varma et al., 2006; Aoki et al., 2009). The exact function of ZW10 within the NRZ complex remains uncertain. ZW10 might control vesicle movement along MTs by interacting with dynein through dynamitin (Varma et al., 2006). ZW10 might also play a direct role in membrane synthesis, budding, or the repair of proper Golgi assembly (Vallee et al., 2006).

A key regulator of lipid droplets (LDs) is the small GTPase Rab18 protein. Rab18 is recruited to the LD surface in a GTP-dependent manner, particularly to the ER-associated NRZ complex and SNAREs (Syntaxin18, Use1, and BNIP1) (Wu et al., 2018). Depletion of Rab18, NAG, RINT1, ZW10, or associated SNAREs results in reduced LD growth and triglyceride storage (Hirose et al., 2004; Arasaki et al., 2006; Wu et al., 2018). The linkage between ZW10 and ER membranes is facilitated by RINT1 (Arasaki et al., 2006), a peripheral membrane protein that binds to the ER-associated SNAP receptors Syntaxin18 and BNIP1 (Nakajima et al., 2004). Fatty acid transport protein 1 (FATP1) on the ER membrane binds to diacylglycerol O-acyltransferase 2 (DGAT2) on LDs. ER-localized SNAREs (Syntaxin18, Use1, and BNIP1) form MCSs with Rab18 on LDs through the NRZ complex (NAG, RINT1, and ZW10) (Figure 3). Taken together, Rab18, the NRZ complex, and SNAREs play key roles in the ER–LD tethering (Wu et al., 2018).

The yeast Dsl1 tethering complex (human NRZ complex) is stably associated with three ER-associated Q-SNAREs, Ufe1p (Syntaxin18), Sec20p (BNIP1), and Use1p (Use1), which have a glutamine at the center of their coiled-coil domain and are supposed to play a vital role in the tethering and fusion of Golgi-derived COP I transport vesicles to the ER (Andag et al., 2001; Kamena and Spang, 2004; Kraynack et al., 2005; Diefenbacher et al., 2011; Tagaya et al., 2014). The Dsl1 complex forms a 20-nm-tall ‘tower’ from the ER surface (Ren et al., 2009; Tripathi et al., 2009). The NRZ complex, which is connected to ER-localized Q-SNAREs, is involved in Golgi-to-ER retrograde vesicle trafficking and fusion (Hatsuzawa et al., 2000; Burri et al., 2003; Hirose et al., 2004; Tagaya et al., 2014). The association of ZW10 with LDs is increased by overexpression of Rab18. Remarkably, ZW10, RINT1, and Syntaxin18 bind to Rab18 with high specificity (Gillingham et al., 2014). The above data strongly suggest that ZW10 regulates organelle dynamics and transport pathways between organelles.

Dramatic rearrangements in membrane-bound organelles arise during cell cycle progression. During prophase, the nuclear pore complex is disassembled, and the nuclear envelope disengages from the nucleus and merges with the ER. This organelle is then partitioned and inherited by daughter cells. The Golgi is disassembled into vesicles during prometaphase, which are segregated in metaphase and then reformed during telophase. Mitochondria fuse in interphase, fragment during mitotic entry at prophase, and re-fuse during anaphase (Figure 4). Recycling endosomes cluster at the spindle pole, and a subset of endosomes (Rab11–FIP3-positive and Rab35-positive) localize to the midbody prior to cytokinetic abscission (Carlton et al., 2020).

The role of ZW10 in membrane trafficking, motility, and sorting

ZW10 is associated with membrane organization between the ER and Golgi apparatus. It was suggested that ZW10 may be associated with membrane organization, which has been confirmed by immunoprecipitation of ZW10 and Syntaxin18. Vesicular transport between the ER and Golgi is associated with a target membrane-associated SNAP receptor Syntaxin18, and Syntaxin18 forms a complex with ZW10 and RINT1 (Hirose et al., 2004). Golgi apparatus dispersal was observed by different experimental methods, such as ZW10 knockdown, overexpression of ZW10 dominant-negative mutants, and injection of ZW10 antibodies (Hatsuzawa et al., 2000). ZW10 possibly contributes to membrane sorting mediated by Syntaxin18 as well as membrane passage mediated by dynein. Depletion of ZW10 can inhibit the secretion of vesicular stomatitis virus glycoprotein during ER and Golgi transport. The vesicular transport from the ERGIC to the pericentrosomal region of the cell occurs in a dynein-dependent manner (Burkhardt et al., 1997; Presley et al., 1997), supporting that ZW10 plays a role in membrane sorting.

ZW10 also plays a significant role in maintaining the organization of the Golgi apparatus. Depletion of ZW10 causes Golgi apparatus disruption, which strongly confirms the function of ZW10 in monitoring Golgi integrity (Hirose et al., 2004; Varma et al., 2006). The Golgi apparatus ensures proper counting of minus end- versus plus end-directed movement. Depletion of ZW10 results in a 70% decrease in the number of minus end-directed vesicle movements, whereas the number of plus end-directed movements is slightly reduced. Interestingly, the Golgi apparatus might act as a sensor for controlling entry into mitosis in mammalian cells. Among a set of components that check the Golgi apparatus status and determine cell cycle progression, ZW10 and RINT1 are important components of the ER and may cooperate for mitosis entry (Sutterlin et al., 2002). A previous report suggested that ZW10 is omitted from the nucleus but found in the cytoplasm during interphase, after which it migrates into the nucleus at prometaphase in Drosophila embryos (Hirose et al., 2004; Wang et al., 2004). At the beginning of mitosis, some components of the ER may migrate into the nucleus, while the Golgi and nuclear membrane components may be redistributed into the ER (Zaal et al., 1999). Thus, ZW10 plays an important role in controlling and maintaining membrane-bound organelle movement and trafficking.

The role of ZW10 in mitosis: mediating the recruitment of Mad1/Mad2

Precise chromosome separation during mitosis is vital for maintaining genomic stability. All kinetochores must achieve bipolar MT attachment and align at the spindle equator before anaphase onset. The ZW10 protein is distributed at different positions at different times during cell cycle progression. It localizes to the cytoplasm during interphase, localizes to the kinetochore in prometaphase (Figure 5), and then contacts the spindle assembly (Schmitt, 2010). When MTs are located at the spindle, Drosophila ZW10 colocalizes only with a subset of the MTs found in the spindle, suggesting its association with kinetochore MTs. At anaphase onset, ZW10 relocalizes to an area at or near the kinetochores of the separating sister chromatids and remains there until telophase (Williams et al., 1992; Figure 5). The ZW10 mutant exhibits an abnormality in chromosome behavior during meiotic division. Lagging chromosomes or apparent chromatin bridges were observed in meiosis I and II, as well as in anaphase/telophase. In ZW10 mutants, chromosomes condense properly and align to the metaphase plate before the late stages of meiosis, a phenomenon found in Drosophila but not in human cells (Chaurasia and Lehner, 2018). ZW10 depletion markedly reduces the chromosome congression efficiency in HEK293T and HeLa cells, leading to difficulties in achieving full chromosome alignment (Li et al., 2007). Thus, ZW10 mutations can affect the separation of individual chromatids as well as homologous chromosomes containing attached sister chromatids (Williams et al., 1996).

The SAC is a surveillance mechanism, which monitors MT attachment of sister chromatids and precludes the metaphase–anaphase transition until every single sister kinetochore pair is bioriented at the mitotic equatorial plate (Santaguida and Musacchio, 2009). Evidence has confirmed that the conserved SAC proteins in species are kinases, such as Mps1, Bub1, BubR1, and Aurora B, and noncatalytic proteins Mad1, Mad2, and Cdc20 (Foley and Kapoor, 2013). It was concluded that additional proteins, including the RZZ complex, p31comet, CENP-E, and dynein, control the activity of the SAC in higher eukaryotes. Dynein-associated proteins, such as Spindly, as well as many protein kinases, such as Cdk1, Aurora B, Bub1/BubR1, Mps1, and Plk1, are also associated with SAC function (Musacchio and Salmon, 2007). Faulty SAC signaling is an indication of premature sister chromatid segregation and aneuploidy in daughter cells. The functions of kinetochores include aligning sister chromatids in prometaphase, pulling sister chromatids apart in anaphase by connecting chromosomes to spindle MTs, and initiating the SAC signaling. The RZZ complex is essential for the functional integrity of the SAC in metazoans (Musacchio and Salmon, 2007). ZW10 silencing reduces the tension between two sister kinetochores, weakening the SAC and leading to mitotic delay with chromosome misalignment (Williams and Goldberg, 1994). The RZZ complex is involved in the SAC by recruiting Mad1/Mad2 to kinetochores in human cells (Caldas et al., 2015; Silio et al., 2015). The RZZ complex activates the SAC by recruiting Mad2 to kinetochores and inactivates the SAC by recruiting dynein–dynactin, which consequently detaches Mad2 from kinetochores (Buffin et al., 2005). Additional results have revealed that the RZZ complex is essential for controlling the SAC without initiating SAC signaling. The localization of Mad1 to the kinetochore depends on Bub1 phosphorylation by Mps1 in early mitosis, whereas the RZZ complex consequently preserves Mad1 kinetochore accumulation in late mitosis as well as in the absence of Bub1 (Qian et al., 2017; Rodriguez-Rodriguez et al., 2018). Bub1 is essential for RZZ kinetochore localization, which in turn is essential for Mad1 kinetochore localization (Zhang et al., 2015). Interestingly, the RZZ complex functions in forming a mesh-like fibrous corona structure. Both Spindly and the RZZ complex are necessary for kinetochore extension at unattached human kinetochores (Defachelles et al., 2015). Thus, ZW10 has essential roles in ensuring chromosomal alignment and controlling mitotic checkpoint.

The role of ZW10 in mitosis: mediating the recruitment of dynein through the RZZ complex

Dynein is a minus-end-directed MT motor protein that transports a variety of intracellular cargo by hydrolyzing ATP to power its movement along MT tracks. Therefore, it is crucial to identify dynein recruitment and activation at subcellular structures. Evidence suggests that ZW10 is an interacting partner of Zwint (a component of the outer kinetochore) (Wang et al., 2004), which in turn binds to the C-terminal domain of Knl1 (Petrovic et al., 2010). However, depletion of the putative Zwint homolog KBP-5 in Caenorhabditis elegans has no effect on the kinetochore localization of the RZZ complex (Varma et al., 2013). Recently, accumulating evidence has demonstrated that Bub1 is required for the localization of the RZZ complex at kinetochores (Caldas et al., 2015; Silio et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2015). The level of Golgi membrane-associated dynein is markedly decreased in cells after ZW10 knockdown (Varma et al., 2006). Overexpressed dynamitin, the p50 subunit of the dynactin complex, was shown to displace dynactin and cytoplasmic dynein at the kinetochore during mitosis and increase the mitotic index, suggesting that ZW10 plays a vital role in anchoring dynactin and, indirectly, dynein to the kinetochore (Echeverri et al., 1996). Cytoplasmic dynein has been independently linked to the mitotic checkpoint mechanism. The function of cytoplasmic dynein is to remove checkpoint proteins from the kinetochore by transporting them away along the kinetochore fibers (Howell et al., 2001). The role of dynein in checkpoint regulation seems to be reversed to that of ZW10. Altered ZW10 expression or function reduces checkpoint activity, while dynein inhibition activates the checkpoint (Howell et al., 2001). Consequently, dynein is involved in various features of mitotic activities, including mitotic spindle orientation (Busson et al., 1998; Song et al., 2021), spindle pole separation (Vaisberg et al., 1993), spindle organization (Echeverri et al., 1996; Merdes et al., 2000; Quintyne et al., 2005), and chromosome separation (Sharp et al., 2000).

The role of the RZZ complex in the assembly of the kinetochore fibrous corona

In metazoans, checkpoint activity is coupled with the assembly of the outermost kinetochore layer named the kinetochore corona (Magidson et al., 2015; Raisch et al., 2022). The corona is a fibrous crescent-shaped structure that is only formed transiently in prometaphase cells before the end-on attachment of chromosomes to MTs is achieved (Kops and Gassmann, 2020). In metazoans, the kinetochore fibrous corona is an ∼1-megadalton multiprotein complex comprising the dynein–dynactin adaptor Spindly, and the RZZ complex is the building block of a fibrous biopolymer. The RZZ complex has been demonstrated to be the core of the kinetochore fibrous corona (Karess, 2005; Kops and Gassmann, 2020).

Spindly is a member of a large family of dynein–dynactin adaptors shown to activate dynein motility in vitro. Spindly binds directly to the RZZ complex through its C-terminal region and engages an RZZ module comprising the ROD β-propeller and Zwilch. Furthermore, in humans and likely most other metazoans, Spindly is post-translationally modified at Cys602 with farnesyl, an isoprenoid lipid. This modification, which is required for the interaction of Spindly with the RZZ complex, may engage a dedicated binding site on the ROD β-propeller (Raisch et al., 2022).

Subunits in the RZZ complex are interdependent for kinetochore localization, consistent with the observation that their inhibition causes identical defects (Williams and Goldberg, 1994; Williams et al., 2003). The RZZ complex is thus important not only for full fibrous corona assembly but also for the (limited) expansion of inner and more outer kinetochore regions (Sacristan et al., 2018). Importantly, the RZZ complex is also required for kinetochore recruitment of dynein–dynactin and Spindly, a dynein adaptor (Starr et al., 1998; Griffis et al., 2007; Mosalaganti et al., 2017).

Perspectives

ZW10 is an evolutionarily conserved protein. It is involved in the regulation of membrane trafficking between the Golgi and ER in interphase. During mitosis, ZW10 plays a distinctly different role, mainly regulating the SAC signaling through the recruitment of Mad1–Mad2 and dynein–dynactin to the kinetochore. We envision that the molecular basis underlying the dual functions of ZW10 is that ZW10 forms different protein complexes, the NRZ complex in interphase and the RZZ complex in mitosis. Importantly, there is an unexpected structural similarity between the NRZ and RZZ complexes (Civril et al., 2010). It would be of great interest to investigate the biochemical and cellular mechanisms that govern the formation of two different spatiotemporal complexes. It is well known that both the ER and Golgi fragment at the beginning of mitosis. ER and Golgi disassembly has several potential advantages for cells. First, it could help distribute the ER and Golgi membranes and proteins equally to daughter cells. Second, it might prevent the ER and Golgi from interfering with the formation of the mitotic spindle.

Combinations of advanced optical imaging protocols, such as lattice light-sheet microscopy with adaptive optics and photoactivatable complementary fluorescence, spectral imaging analyses, and correlative light and cryo-electron microscopic tomography, would increase our understanding of ZW10 network dynamics and translocation from membrane-bound organelles such as the ER to membraneless organelles such as the kinetochore. In particular, the newly developed lattice light-sheet microscopy with adaptive optics has enabled noninvasive, aberration-free imaging of subcellular processes in live organoids, including organelle contacts and remodeling during mitosis (Liu et al., 2020). This technology reveals the phenotypic diversity within cells from various organisms and developmental stages and could be useful for determining how host cells adapt to a physiological stimulus or respond to SARS-CoV-2 infection, both in real time and at the organelle level (Wang et al., 2022). In this context, the inclusion of an expanding collection of gene-edited organoids will allow spatiotemporal modeling of ZW10 networks in cell cycle regulation.

Despite the progress over the past decade in understanding the function and structure of the ZW10 complex (Raisch et al., 2022), much remains to be explored. We still have a minimal understanding of the regulation of ZW10 complex assembly/disassembly during the cell cycle. The recent development of proximity ligation coupled with mass spectrometric analyses has enabled the discovery of many context-dependent protein networks exhibiting dynamic and weak interactions (Rhee et al., 2013; Tian et al., 2022; Li et al., 2023). A better understanding of context-dependent protein machinery will require cryo-electron tomographic analyses of the ZW10 complex in different states of mitotic cells in situ (Liu et al., 2020). Currently, it is unclear to what extent reorganization of the ZW10 complex is elicited by post-translational modification. In addition, it remains elusive how individual ZW10 complex assembly is regulated and how this assembly is subjected to inter-ZW10 complex exchange in cell cycle regulation. Thus, the challenges ahead are to delineate and distinguish the characteristics of the ZW10 complex that are driven by cell cycle-elicited post-translational modifications from those independent of mitosis by using proximity ligation coupled mass spectrometric analyses and high-resolution molecular imaging (Yao and Smolka, 2019; Song et al., 2021; Li et al., 2023).

Overall, advances in our understanding of the molecular language of ZW10-elicited organelle communication will enable us to amalgamate the physiological function of ZW10 during epithelial cell renewal in 3D organoids.
