Optimization of supercritical fluid processes for aripiprazole nanoparticle production using GAS-antisolvent technique and Box-Behnken design
Authors: Eslam Ansari, Bizhan Honarvar, Seyed Ali Sajadian, Zahra Arab Aboosadi, Mehdi Azizi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractParticle size reduction makes drugs more soluble and bioavailable in the body. Researchers are increasingly interested in supercritical fluid processes for the production of micro- and nanoparticles in pharmaceuticals. In this research, gas anti-solvent (GAS), polyvinyl pyrrolidone polymer (PVP), and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) solvent processes were used for the first time to produce aripiprazole (antipsychotic drug) nanoparticles. This specific combination has not been previously reported for aripiprazole and represents the main novelty of the study. The effects of different operating conditions were evaluated using a Box-Behenken design, which also sought to highlight the optimal combinations of conditions to obtain the smallest particle size. The effects of temperature (308, 318, and 328 K), pressure (10, 13, and 16 MPa), and APZ/PVP ratio (1, 5, and 9 w/w) on particle size were investigated. The optimum values of temperature, pressure, and APZ/PVP ratio were 312 K, 16 MPa, and 1w/w, respectively, which resulted in the minimum particle size. FTIR, FESEM, DLS, XRD, DSC, and dissolution rate tests were used to investigate changes in physical - chemical properties. Good results were obtained by reducing the particle size from 104.6 ± 30.1 μm to 389.6 ± 24.2 nm at optimum condition.Keywords: Aripiprazole (APZ), Gas antisolvent, Box-Behnken, Nanoparticles, Supercritical carbon dioxideSubject terms: Chemistry, Drug discovery, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

Aripiprazole (APZ) is a white to off-white crystalline powder1, that is prescribed for the treatment of various psychotic disorders, including schizophrenia2 and bipolar disorder, and is also used as an adjunctive treatment for depression. APZ has US FDA approval for the treatment of mixed episodes associated with bipolar disorder and acute mania3,4. APZ has low water solubility and poor bioavailability, placing it under the BCS Class II drug classification. The equilibrium solubility in water is 0.00001%, w/v 1.

In addition, APZ experimental solubility results in supercritical carbon dioxide (SC-CO2) have shown correlations with various semi-experimental models and the modified Wilson model. Research has also shown that solubility is highly dependent on pressure and temperature5.

Researchers have widely used supercritical fluid (SCF) processes as a new processing method due to the good properties of SCF, including a solvent with optimal solvency, convenient removal permeability, and low viscosity. Supercritical gas technology easily produces very small and fine particles under relatively gentle operating conditions, not to mention its ability to form solid phases with specific morphology6–15. Low critical temperature, moderate critical pressure and ineffective nature have led to the applications of SC-CO2 as solvent or anti-solvent in the process of extraction with solution and rapid expansion methods of suspensions16–22. According to the characteristics of SCF, studies have been conducted on the extraction of essential oils using supercritical carbon dioxide. It was observed that the composition and main properties of the materials were preserved due to the process being carried out at low temperature23–29. Also, SCF extraction is a new method and a suitable alternative to conventional oil extraction techniques, which has been extensively studied30–32.

For the production of micro/nanoparticles, SCFs can be used as an antisolvent, solvent and reaction medium. If the drug is well dissolved by supercritical carbon dioxide, SC-CO2 is used as a solvent. Otherwise, SC-CO2 is used as an antisolvent in the process. In this regard, experimental investigations have been carried out on the solubility of several drugs in SC-CO2 33–40.

Many researchers have investigated the influence of various parameters on PSD and product morphology in GAS technology. Mella et al. 41 compared GAS and rapid atomic injection for solvent extraction (ARISE) using dense CO2 to experimentally study the morphology and distribution of sodium levothyroxine particles. Vichaian Feng et al. 42 used the Box-Behnken design (BBD) and GAS to process the mefenamic acid-paracetamol crystals. The study showed that the crystal drug was an efficient way to increase the dissolution rate of poorly soluble drugs using GAS. Jafari et al. 43 investigated the influence of anti-solvent addition, process temperature, solute concentration and solvent type on the final product PSD, morphology and crystallinity. According to particle size analyses and SEM results, higher antisolvent addition rates resulted in a decrease in the average size of precipitated particles and a more restricted size distribution. In addition, process temperature and solute concentration inversely affected the size of precipitated aspirin particles, indicating an average size of 48–124 μm for the crystallized particles. Based on the XRD and DSC results, particles prepared from acetone showed higher crystallinity compared to those prepared from methanol and unprocessed aspirin, which could be justified by the reduced involvement of methanol in the crystal structure.

Various methods have been used to produce nanoparticles using SCF (Badnez et al. 44, Bakhbakhi et al. 45, Kankala et al. 46, Liu et al. 47, Sodeifian et al. 10,16,18,36,48,49). In drug micronization, the choice of supercritical fluid method is influenced by the solubility of the drug in SC-CO2 and organic solvent50. Campardelli et al. 51 studied the use of SCF in nanotechnology and showed its applicability in the development of cell diagnostic kits, intracellular tools, engineered drug delivery, mechanisms and implantable devices. Phase equilibrium models are essential to evaluate appropriate operating conditions to ensure the practical application of the GAS process. Esfandiari and Ghoreishi52 investigated mathematical modeling for the synthesis of 5-FU nanoparticles using GAS. CO2 was used as an antisolvent to precipitate 5-FU from a DMSO solution. The results showed a decrease in particle size with increasing antisolvent addition rate. Micro production of drugs using CO2 as an antisolvent gas was performed by Muller et al. 53.

According to the information obtained, there is no study on the production of APZ nanoparticles by the GAS method. Based on the results of the previous work5, the solubility of APZ in supercritical carbon dioxide was studied. The experimental solubility was carried out in SC-CO2 at variable pressures and temperature ranges of 12–30 MPa and 308–328 K, respectively, and the molar fraction of solution in SC-CO2 ranged from 1.830 × 10− 6 to 1.036 × 10− 5. The results show that the region below the crossover is suitable for the GAS method.

For the first time, the GAS process was used to produce APZ nanoparticles in the present research. The effects of various operating conditions, such as pressure, temperature, and the APZ/PVP ratio, were examined to identify the optimal values for achieving the smallest particle size with a minimal number of experiments, based on BBD principles. The physical, chemical, and morphological characteristics of the produced particles were then evaluated using DLS, FTIR, SEM, XRD, and DSC techniques. Comparative analyses were performed between untreated and GAS-processed particles to evaluate the method’s efficiency and potential to enhance the dissolution behavior of poorly water-soluble drugs.

Gas anti-solvent (GAS) equipment and process

A device was used according to the schematic image shown in Fig. 1 to perform the GAS test. This set comprises a CO2 cylinder (E-1) and the carbon dioxide gas (CAS NO. 124-38-9) with a mass purity of 99.99% was provided by Zagros Company (Shiraz, Iran), valve (E-2), filter (E-3), refrigerator (E-4), Haskel pump (type - CA 91502, Burbank, CA, USA America) (E-5), air compressor (E-6), pressure gauge (WIKA, Germany, code EN 837-1) (E-7), needle valve (E-8), oven (E-9) (MEMMERT OVEN UNB 100,Germany), a coil (E-10), a valve (E-11), precipitator cell (E-12), sintered filter (E-13), back pressure (E-14), collection vial (E-15), a digital thermometer, and 1/8-inch tubing and fittings (Fig. 1).

To prepare nanoparticles by this method, APZ drug was first mixed with PVP polymer in different combinations (90 mg drug with 90 mg polymer (1 w/w), 90 mg drug with 18 mg polymer (5 w/w), and 90 mg drug with 10 mg polymer (9 w/w)), followed by the addition of DMSO. The obtained amount of APZ solution was poured into a filter with solvent and placed in the cell, which was then placed in a furnace to control the temperature. After that, CO2 was passed through the refrigerator and converted to liquid CO2 by the reciprocating pump to adjust the gas pressure. SCF was then injected into the cell. At this stage, the operating temperature was set to the desired value, the cell outlet valve was kept closed for 60 min, and the pump was turned off. The next step was to run the system in dynamic mode for 160 min at constant pressure. CO2 molecules passed through the filters at the bottom of the cell by diffusion, but APZ particles were not transported and deposited on the filters. Finally, APZ particles were collected in the precipitation cell after pressure release. In this research, the GAS process method and the BBD design implementation software were used to investigate the influence of various operating parameters on the size of the particles produced.

Preparation of Aripiprazole/DMSO feed solutions and phase behavior under SC-CO₂ conditions

Saturated solutions of aripiprazole were prepared in DMSO at ambient temperature (25 ± 1 °C) to ensure complete dissolution and feed stability. DMSO was selected based on its high polarity and strong solvating capacity for poorly water-soluble compounds. The prepared solutions were visually inspected to confirm clarity and absence of undissolved solids prior to GAS experiments. GAS processing was conducted by introducing SC-CO₂ into the aripiprazole/DMSO feed solution under controlled temperature and pressure conditions. Upon SC-CO₂ injection, rapid gas absorption into the liquid phase induced substantial volumetric expansion and a marked reduction in solvent strength. These changes triggered supersaturation and rapid precipitation of aripiprazole. The moderate solubility of aripiprazole in DMSO facilitated controlled supersaturation kinetics, enabling efficient particle formation and reproducible precipitation behavior5,54–56.

BBD design of experiments for nanoparticle production by GAS

The input variables and their interactions were evaluated using the BBD experimental design to discover the optimal response using the response surface methodology42,57. The BBD was used to illustrate the effect of the factors of this method on the morphology and size of APZ sediments obtained by the GAS method in three factors and three levels. The mean particle size (x50) was illustrated using the DLS method. The experimental data with the following quadratic polynomial relationship were used to evaluate the correlation of dependent and independent variables57–59.

In this formula, Y represents the response of the output process, A, B, C indicate the coded independent parameters, shows the intercept term. Besides, introduce the linear coefficient,,, represent interaction balance coefficients,, indicates quadratic coefficients, ε is the error. The R2, adjusted R2, predicted R2, and F values were taken into account to perform ANOVA analyses. Graphical analysis of the experimental data was performed in Design Expert software. The quadratic model used in this study included interactive and quadratic linear terms.

Numerous previous studies have employed the BBD due to its strong capability to evaluate nonlinear interactions among process variables while minimizing the number of experimental runs compared to full factorial designs60–64. In this study, three independent process parameters process temperature (A), process pressure (B), and initial solution ratio (C) were selected based on preliminary investigations, equipment limitations, and their critical roles in controlling particle size and morphology. The range of each parameter was determined according to previous literature5. The BBD, with three levels per factor, enabled precise assessment of linear, quadratic, and interaction effects, while avoiding corner combinations that could lead to unstable conditions. This approach enhanced the accuracy of response surface modeling and facilitated the identification of optimal process conditions.

Particle characterizations

The structure, morphology and surface properties of the particles obtained by GAS were investigated by Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) scanning electron microscopy, while the dynamic light scattering PSD of the samples was also obtained by the DLS method. Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) was used to investigate the thermal stability of the samples. The sample temperature was typically increased by 5 mg per cycle from the initial temperature of 303 K to the temperature of 503 K at a rate of 10 K /min in a standard aluminum pan exposed to an argon flow (20 ml/min). Finally, crystallinity, phase state, and average crystal size were examined by XRD at room temperature.

Influence of operating parameters

Phase‑behavior analysis based on reported thermodynamic data and qualitative phase‑equilibrium assessment informs the discussion below; the qualitative modeling results are presented alongside experimental evidence to explain how operating parameters affect nucleation and growth.

Effect of temperature

As shown by the BBD results in Fig. 2; Table 3, higher process temperatures increased the average size of the deposited APZ particles. In the GAS process, temperature can affect particle size through nucleation and growth dynamics due to its effect on solubility. Thus, increasing the temperature increases the solubility of APZ in the organic solvent. As a result, the increased APZ-solvent interaction decreases the solubility of APZ in SC-CO2. In addition, the shape of the deposited particles may change as the temperature increases. Volumetric expansion decreases at lower solubilities and supersaturations, increasing the size of the particles produced. For APZ nanoparticle production by GAS, the average size is 402, 502, and 660 nm at temperatures of 308, 318, and 328 K, respectively, assuming that other parameters (pressure, anti-solvent addition rate, and APZ/PVP ratio) are constant. Similar results were shown by Muller et al. 53., who concluded that there was a 3.2 to 10 μm increase in the particle size with increasing temperature from 5 to 50 °C. Bakhbakhi et al. 65. Also reported that increasing the process temperature (25–52.5 °C) for the recrystallization process directly increased the average size of BECD particles and expanded the size distribution. Esfandiari et al. 66 showed that when capecitabine particles were prepared by the GAS anti-solvent process, the particle size increased at higher temperatures due to the dominance of the growth rate, which is in agreement with other studies performed using the GAS method (MJ Cocero, S Ferrero67, Fusaro et al. 68, Chen et al. 69, Bachbakhi et al. 70, and Kim et al. 71, Sodeifian et al. 72). The thermodynamic and kinetic role of temperature in shifting solubility, reducing instantaneous supersaturation and promoting growth over nucleation is well documented73,74. These findings are further supported by studies on other pharmaceutical compounds, where elevated temperatures increased solubility in organic solvents and reduced solubility in supercritical CO₂. This shift led to a decline in nucleation rate and dominance of particle growth, resulting in larger particles. In the case of aripiprazole, reduced volumetric expansion and lower supersaturation at higher temperatures contributed to increased particle size and altered morphology. Statistical analysis also confirmed temperature as the most influential factor in particle formation, surpassing the effects of polymer ratio and pressure75.

Pressure effects

The pressure effects on average particle size (10, 13, and 16 MPa) were studied. According to Figs. 2 and 3b, the average particle size decreased at higher pressures from 10 to 16 MPa, which subsequently increased the density of CO2 and its solubility in DMSO76,77. Therefore, shorter times were required to overcome the supersaturation condition and start the APZ particle precipitation. The nucleation mechanism was superior to growth, resulting in smaller particles. Sodeifin et al. 72 focused on the production of sertraline drug nanoparticles by supercritical fluid using the GAS method, indicating an increase in particle size from nearly 50 μm to 102 nm at low pressures. Esfandiari et al. found that at high pressures, the nucleation mechanism was dominant, resulting in smaller particles. Similar results have been observed in the preparation of nanoparticles from various pharmaceutical model systems (for 5-fluorouracil78, finasteride79, rosuvastatin80, capecitabine81 and liposomes82, showing the production of smaller particles at higher pressures. The increase in CO2 density, enhanced solvent extraction rate, and associated hydrodynamic and phase‑equilibrium effects that accelerate the rise in local supersaturation and favor nucleation over growth are described in recent reviews and modeling studies83–85. Similar mechanisms were observed in other GAS-based systems, where increasing pressure enhanced CO₂ density and solubility in DMSO, accelerating the onset of supersaturation. This favored nucleation over growth and led to the formation of smaller particles. For aripiprazole, the pressure-dependent shift in phase equilibrium and solvent extraction dynamics played a key role in reducing particle size. These effects are consistent with recent modeling studies and experimental data on drugs such as Rivaroxaban, 5-Fluorouracil, and Capecitabine66,72.

Effect of initial APZ/PVP ratio

According to Table 3; Figs. 2 and 3, there was an increase in the particle size at higher APZ/PVP ratios of the solute. When the APZ/PVP ratio was at its highest, the supersaturation curve showed more proximity to the saturation curve, and nucleation was less expandable. Thus, the product nuclei had a higher probability of growth, resulting in a dominant growth mechanism. Therefore, increasing the APZ/PVP ratio in the organic solvent increased the size of the particle in the GAS method43,65,69,86. The modulatory role of polymer fraction (PVP) in providing steric and surface stabilization, increasing solution viscosity and suppressing growth or aggregation is reported in experimental SAS/GAS studies and supports the observed trend that higher polymer content yields finer, more stabilized particles while higher APZ/PVP produces larger particles87. The role of the polymer (PVP) in this process is multifaceted. Its presence increases solution viscosity, provides steric and surface stabilization, and suppresses particle aggregation. Experimental studies in SAS and GAS systems have shown that higher polymer content in the formulation leads to finer and more stabilized particles, whereas elevated drug-to-polymer ratios yield larger final particle sizes. These findings align with mechanisms reported in similar pharmaceutical formulations, where polymers act not only as physical carriers but also as modulators of particle growth and drug release behavior. Therefore, precise adjustment of the APZ/PVP ratio in GAS-based formulations plays a critical role in controlling particle size and optimizing the pharmaceutical properties of the final product88.

The GAS process effects on APZ particle production

As shown in Fig. 2, the effects of GAS conditions on APZ particle size were investigated and three main factors were identified that significantly affected particle size and morphology. As the initial APZ/PVP solute ratio increased at a given temperature and pressure, an increase in particle size was also reported. A comparison of experiments 9–11 and 10–12 confirms the increase in particle size at higher temperatures. Meanwhile, the particle size decreased with increasing pressure. At higher APZ/PVP ratios, the increased polymer concentration also raises the solution viscosity, which can hinder CO₂ mass transfer into the liquid phase and slow down supersaturation. This, combined with reduced nucleation rates, promotes particle growth and leads to larger particle sizes.

Characterization of the APZ

The APZ particles produced by GAS have been studied using various methods such as FTIR, XRD, DSC, FSEM, and DLS. Among these methods, FTIR facilitates determining certain functional groups or chemical bonds of components89. As shown in Fig. 4(a, b, c), this method was employed to evaluate unprocessed (APZ, PVP) and gas-processed samples. Figure 4a shows the results of FTIR spectra analysis for APZ. The peak related to C-CL in the region of 800 to 1000, the vibrational frequency of C-O in the region of 1000 to 1200, and the strong absorption peak in the region of 1200 to 1600 are related to the vibrational frequency of the carbonylamide group. The peak related to the C-H bond is in the region of 2800 to 3000, and the single branch peak related to the vibration NH stretching can be seen in the region of 3000 to 3300. The FTIR analysis of PVP is shown in Fig. 4b. The peaks related to C = O, CH2, and C-H are located in the regions of 1655, 2947, and 3454, respectively. The results related to the studies performed by Baganiz et al. 90 Shirafkan et al.91 and Fini et al. 92 regarding FTIR-PVP analysis confirm this finding. The analysis sample obtained shows that the drug is deposited on the polymer. As shown, the spectra of pure APZ and PVP can be clearly distinguished, indicating the absence of hydrogen bonding for these operating conditions.

FTIR spectra of (a) original, (b) PVP, and (c) nano APZ under optimized conditions.

The crystallinity of the samples can be examined by XRD, as shown in Fig. 5a, b, c for the unprocessed PVP and GAS-processed APZ crystals, revealing similarities between the crystal configuration of the processed and original samples. However, the nanoparticles had less crystallinity than the unprocessed particles, as evidenced by a decrease in peak intensity in the XRD results for the GAS-processed samples.

XRD for (a) the original APZ, (b) original PVP, (c) GAS processed APZ, (Optimum conditions).

The processed and unprocessed particles were thermally analyzed using differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), which detects crystal formation from differences in crystal melting point and latent heat of fusion, as well as its constituents by thermal phase. DSC analysis highlights the thermal behavior of the crystals in terms of solid-liquid (fusion) and solid-solid (polymorph) transition curves42. Figure 6 shows the DSC thermograms of the original (a) and processed (c) gas and polymer samples (b). As shown in Fig. 6, there is a small endothermic peak at 139.31 °C, which is the melting peak of Form III. There is a larger endothermic peak at 148.5 °C, which is consistent with the melting point of Form I found in relevant studies, indicating that a small amount of Form III was converted to Form I during the heating process93–95. The pure and GAS-processed APZ had melting points of 148.31 °C and 148 °C, respectively, which can be explained by the higher heat transfer area and lower crystallinity.

APZ pre- and post-process DSC analyses: (a) original APZ, (b) PVP, and (b) nano APZ at optimized conditions.

DSC analyses of pure PVP (Fig. 6) show the occurrence of the exothermic transition at 55–80 °C and the presence of a peak at 80 °C, associated with the diffusion of moisture at the absorption temperature, as also highlighted in previous works. In similar studies carried out on PVP, a peak at ~ 75 °C was reported, attributed to water loss96. Meanwhile, DSC analysis of carbamazepine/PVP polymer showed a peak at 75–140 °C, possibly related to the release of water from this hydrophilic polymer97. The DSC results of the processed sample showed negligible changes in the position and intensities of the exothermic melting peaks of the APZ particles with PVP, possibly due to the smaller particle size and amorphous structure.

According to the FESEM images of the particles (Fig. 7a-f), the size was significantly reduced, while there were variations in the morphological properties of the GAS-processed particles. In addition, the unprocessed samples showed irregular and angular structures, while the GAS-processed samples showed semispherical configurations with increased distribution. There was good agreement between the FESEM and DLS results. The DLS analyses showed variations in the particle size of the processed samples ranging from 401.9 to 659.7 nm, indicating finer particles with narrower distribution than the original sample. Figure 8 shows the DLS plots, where the DLS and FESEM results under different conditions (runs 1, 3, 8 and 12) were in considerable agreement, revealing changes in the micro to nano size particles of the unprocessed APZ according to the GAS method. DLS analyses showed values of 480, 659, 413, 462, and 382 nm for the average particle size of APZ-PVP and GAS-processed APZ (runs 1, 3, 8, and 12).

Conclusion

Increasing drug solubility in water and ensuring higher bioavailability can be critical issues when seeking to optimize the function of active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs). In the current research, the supercritical CO2 antisolvent (GAS) method was used for the first time to reduce the APZ particle size, thereby increasing the solubility and bioavailability of APZ as an antipsychotic drug and reducing its side effects. The use of smaller particles can lead to the solubility of APZ with low solubility in water. The effect of various factors including pressure (10, 13, and 16 MPa), temperature (308, 318, and 328 K), and APZ/PVP ratio (1, 5, and 9 w/w) was investigated. The effect of these parameters on the size and morphology of the deposited APZ particles was investigated using the BBD method. As highlighted by the experimental data, higher temperatures resulted in larger particles, while higher pressures resulted in more nuclei, which yielded crystals with smaller sizes. Furthermore, at lower APZ/PVP ratios, precipitation with higher volumetric expansion occurred, resulting in smaller particles. The size of APZ nanoparticles produced by GAS was in the range of 401.9–659.7 nm. The optimal values of variables (temperature 312 K, pressure 16 MPa, and APZ/PVP ratio 1) can bring the smallest particles. The significant reduction of drug particle size from 104.6 ± 30.1 μm on average (original) to nearly 389.6 nm and the uniform nanoparticle structure with narrow size distribution indicate the successful implementation of GAS process for APZ nanoparticle production. In addition, the original and precipitated APZ particles were examined by SEM, DLS, DSC, XRD, and FTIR. An interesting result was related to the lower crystallinity of the APZ nanoparticles than the original sample, resulting in higher solubility. Comparisons of dissolution rates of the original APZ sample and APZ nanoparticles prepared under optimal conditions confirmed the improved solubility of APZ. The limitation of this paper is that it has been carried out at laboratory scale and it is recommended that it is carried out at industrial scale as well.
