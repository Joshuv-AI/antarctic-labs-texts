COVID-19 and social care screening for older adults in social housing: a CP@clinic adaptation
Authors: Hanna Dias, Jasdeep Brar, Melissa Pirrie, Ricardo Angeles, Francine Marzanek, Gina Agarwal
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCOVID-19 has changed healthcare access and delivery, especially impacting older adults. The Community Paramedicine at Clinic (CP@clinic) program is a chronic disease prevention, management, and health promotion program for community-dwelling low-income older adults. We investigated a telephone-based CP@clinic program adaptation during the pandemic.MethodsCommunity paramedics delivered CP@clinic via telephone to residents of 36 social housing buildings. They conducted screening for COVID-19, emergency preparedness, and social health factors. Community paramedics provided education on staying safe, self-isolating, self-monitoring, and preparing for pandemics using governmental infographics. Descriptive analysis was conducted on assessments completed between March and June 2020. Paramedic documentation was thematically analyzed to identify common themes.ResultsAll 191 participants had ≥ 1 telephone visit and 34.6% had ≥ 2 telephone visits, 82.8% were aged 65 years and older, 30.9% had internet access, and 57.9% had cable TV (limiting exposure to COVID-19 information). The CP@clinic program infrastructure provided a platform for paramedics to swiftly contact many vulnerable older adults to screen for COVID-19, educate on safe practices, and facilitate healthcare access. One-quarter of participants screened positive for social isolation. Thematic analysis described participants’ experiences adapting to daily life in the pandemic, emotional experience during the pandemic, and paramedics supporting participants during the pandemic.ConclusionsPublic health interventions are needed for low-income or hard-to-reach older adults, especially during pandemics. Community paramedics were able to support vulnerable older adults living in social housing through the unique CP@clinic adaptation. This innovative program delivery increased vulnerable populations’ access to public health services and information at a time of great health need.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-024-05549-2.Keywords: COVID-19, Community paramedicine, Older adults, Vulnerable populations, Health education, Screening, Social care

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic has drastically changed health care access and delivery, especially impacting older adult and low-income populations [1, 2]. Low-income and older adults are vulnerable populations who face a disproportionate burden of COVID-19 infections, hospitalizations, sequelae, and deaths [1, 2]. In Ontario, Canada, 17.3% of older adults (aged 65 years and older) are classified as low-income [3]. There is also a large number of low-income older adults who reside in social housing, with 75,000 older adults living in social housing in Ontario alone [4]. Social housing is a government-assisted, subsidized rental housing option for low-income households [5]. The social housing buildings are often densely populated with clustered living conditions and shared facilities that can promote the spread of COVID-19 [6].

Beyond COVID-19, low-income older adults who reside in social housing also have a higher burden of chronic diseases, falls, frailty, and poor mobility [6]. They also have lower health literacy rates and greater risks of social isolation and food insecurity [7–9]. Given all these factors, there is an increased likelihood of this vulnerable population facing a higher risk of having COVID-19 and a greater challenge in accessing public health information and resources, health care, and basic needs [6].

At the same time, this population is part of a growing demographic. In Ontario, the older adult population (aged 65 years and older) is rapidly increasing and estimated to be 21.8% of the population by 2046 compared to 18.1% in 2021 [10]. Consequently, innovative health care interventions are needed to access and address the needs of this vulnerable population.

The Community Paramedicine at Clinic (CP@clinic) Program is a community paramedic wellness program for community dwelling low-income older adults in Canada as part of the primary care system [11]. It is proven to be cost-effective, significantly reduce emergency medical services calls (e.g., 911) and systolic blood pressure, and improve the health and quality of life of older adults [12, 13]. In response to COVID-19, the CP@clinic program was shifted from an in-person service to a telecare model delivered by paramedics to screen for COVID-19 and factors affected by determinants of health.

The objective of this study was to investigate the outcomes of a telephone-based CP@clinic adaptation integrating COVID-19, emergency preparedness, and social health screening for older adults residing in social housing buildings.

Methods

Study design and setting

This observational longitudinal study was conducted using CP@clinic participant data collected between March 26, 2020, and June 18, 2020, across eight communities in Ontario, Canada, near the beginning of the COVID-19 pandemic. Since its inception, CP@clinic has primarily taken place in the common areas of social housing buildings, allowing residents to voluntarily drop-in. However, CP@clinic services shifted to telephone delivery during the pandemic, in accordance with provincial lockdowns and public health and safety measures to reduce the spread of the virus. The program adapted from its usual chronic disease screening and management programming to focus on screening for COVID-19 symptoms and exposure, emergency preparedness, and social health factors. The community paramedics held several CP@clinic telephone sessions and welcomed participants to engage in telephone follow-up visits. Written, informed consent was obtained from participants in the CP@clinic program. This study received ethics approval from the Hamilton Integrated Research Ethics Board (#14–645).

Participants

The community paramedics conducting the CP@clinic telephone visits invited individuals who have previously attended CP@clinic or any of their other community paramedicine programs to participate. Of 2179 potential participants, 191 individuals participated in the COVID-19 screening telephone visits. Participants either lived in one of 36 social housing buildings or had attended CP@clinic in one of these buildings. The social housing buildings were located within the following regions in Ontario, Canada: Brant County, Grey County, Guelph, Halton Region, Hastings County, Norfolk County, Prescott and Russell United Counties, and York Region.

Data collection and measurement

During each visit, participants underwent screening for COVID-19 symptoms and exposure, emergency preparedness, and social health factors (e.g., social isolation and food insecurity). The paramedics also had the option to provide participants with COVID-19 education. To collect the screening information, paramedics asked participants questions using various tools over the telephone and recorded their response options in the CP@clinic electronic medical record (EMR) database. Paramedics received training to administer these screening tools as part of their CP@clinic training. The training program for the telephone-based CP@clinic adaptation was tailored to the telecare model and included an informational webinar and electronic materials such as infographics provided to community paramedics.

For COVID-19 screening, the paramedics went through the Government of Ontario Self-Assessment Tool with participants (see online Supplemental Material File 1). Three different versions of the self-assessment tool were used in this study (a first version from March 26 to April 23, a second version from April 23 to May 19, and a third version from May 29 to June 18), as the Ontario provincial government frequently updated the tool over the course of the pandemic. A background algorithm in the CP@clinic database determined which set of responses yielded a positive screen, and those participants were advised to get tested for COVID-19. All participants were offered a follow-up call, and those who tested positive for the COVID symptom screening received an immediate follow-up phone call to check if they had undergone testing.

The CP@clinic research team also developed a set of questions to investigate participants’ emergency preparedness (see online Supplemental Material File 1). Participants were asked a series of questions to determine if they had access to various resources, including someone to help them get groceries, someone to talk to when they are feeling worried or anxious, internet access, and cable TV access.

Information about social health factors was collected using screening tools for social isolation and food insecurity. The University of California Los Angeles (UCLA) 3-Item Loneliness Scale was used to investigate the extent of social isolation in this population [14]. The scale asks participants how often they feel that they lack companionship, how often they feel left out, and how often they feel isolated from others, to which they can respond with “hardly ever” (scored as 1), “some of the time” [2], or “often” [3, 14]. Response values were summed to yield a score out of nine, and those with scores of six or greater may be experiencing loneliness [15] and were referred to primary or community care resources. The UCLA 3-Item Loneliness Scale was developed for use in telephone surveys and has proven to be a valid and reliable measure of loneliness in the older adult population [14]. Moreover, the Brief Hunger Screening Tool was used to screen for food insecurity, which asks whether, in the past month, there was any day when the participant or anyone in their family went hungry because they did not have enough money for food [16]. Participants who responded “yes” were referred to community resources. Literature suggests this tool has good reliability and validity [16].

The CP@clinic database gave paramedics the option to share information about how to stay safe, how to self-isolate, how to self-monitor, and/or how to prepare for a pandemic situation. If they chose to share information about any of those four topics, a Government of Ontario educational infographic would pop up for the paramedics to view, and they could provide education to the participants (see online Supplemental Material File 2).

Finally, paramedics had the option to provide descriptive notes about their conversations with participants and/or any actions completed during the telephone visit, which provided further insight about participants’ experiences during the pandemic and actions the paramedics took to address their needs.

Data analysis

Descriptive quantitative analysis of participant responses on the screening tools for COVID-19 symptoms/exposure, emergency preparedness, social isolation, and food insecurity was performed by computing the frequencies, means (and standard deviations [SDs]), and medians (and interquartile ranges [IQRs]), using IBM SPSS 28.0.

A thematic analysis of the paramedics’ descriptive notes, using an inductive approach, was conducted to identify common themes and temporal changes related to the study objective and participant experiences with the telephone-based CP@clinic program during the COVID-19 pandemic [17]. Using Braun and Clarke’s method, HD read and coded the paramedics’ descriptive notes to identify potential themes, by grouping similar codes into subthemes and themes [17]. HD met frequently with three members of the research team (GA, RA, and MP) to discuss the potential codes and themes. HD reviewed the themes in relation to the coded extracts and entire data set, and then HD, GA, RA, and MP finalized the main themes and associated subthemes.

Results

All 191 participants had at least one telephone CP@clinic visit and 34.6% had two or more visits. Of the 191 participants, 134 participants provided their age and had a mean age of 75.6 years (SD = 11.5 years); 17.2% were under 65 years old, 59.7% were aged 65 to 84, and 23.1% were over 85 years old. The majority of the 191 participants were from Brant County (43.5%), Grey County (26.7%), and Halton Region (20.4%). Using median household income by dissemination area from the 2021 Census, the social housing building postal codes yielded a mean neighbourhood household income of $58,995 (SD=$22,696) and median of $61,600 (IQR=$32,600); as expected, this is lower than the provincial median household income of $91,000 from the same census year [18]. COVID-19 screening

Eleven participants received a positive result on the COVID-19 screening and were advised by the paramedics to get tested for COVID-19.

Emergency preparedness screening

Participants also engaged in emergency preparedness screening, where they were asked about their access to various resources. Of the 191 participants’ first visits, 81.2% reported having someone to talk to when they feel worried or anxious, 71.7% reported having someone to help them get groceries, 57.7% had access to cable TV, and 30.9% had access to the internet.

Social health factors screening

For participants’ first visit, n = 161 completed social isolation screening. Those with a score of 6 or greater on the UCLA 3-Item Loneliness Scale were screened as positive for social isolation, indicating they may be experiencing loneliness. The mean social isolation score was 4.3 (SD = 1.8), and 25.6% screened positive. The proportion of participants who screened positive is notable as it is higher than that of the general Canadian older adult population (25.6% vs. 19%), based on the 2008–2009 Canadian Community Health Survey, which used the same scale [19]. This could suggest that the study population is more susceptible to social isolation, and it is likely that the COVID-19 provincial lockdowns also played a role in the higher proportion of social isolation observed. For participants who attended a second visit, the social isolation results were similar. Interestingly, all participants (n = 8) who attended three or more visits screened positive for social isolation and had a mean score of 7.5 (SD = 1.6); this could suggest that those participants experiencing loneliness enjoyed engaging with the paramedics and CP@clinic, which is echoed in the thematic analysis. Moreover, 5.4% of participants screened positive for food insecurity and were referred to community resources. Detailed information about social health factors screening can be found in Table 2.

COVID-19 education

When the database algorithm determined that a participant was at risk of contracting COVID-19 based on their COVID-19 screening responses, it would recommend that paramedics share information about how to stay safe, self-isolate, self-monitor, and/or prepare for a pandemic situation. Otherwise, paramedics could use their discretion to share any of this information with a participant. Overall, 33.0% of participants were provided COVID-19 safety education on any of the four topics, during any visit. Paramedics provided education based on Government of Ontario educational resources (see online Supplemental Material File 2).

Qualitative data

Paramedics made notes for 71.7% of participants (n = 137), during at least one of their visits. Thematic analysis of this qualitative data revealed common themes, providing insight about participant experiences with the adapted CP@clinic program during the COVID-19 pandemic. Thematic analysis of paramedic notes

The first major theme encompassed participant experiences adapting to daily life in the pandemic. Many participants (n = 61) talked about the precautions they were taking to stay safe, such as hand-washing, masking, and social distancing. Moreover, many participants shared that they were having their groceries (n = 50) and/or medications (n = 30) delivered by a program or family member, or that they ran their own errands (n = 36).

The second major theme emerging from the paramedic notes was participants detailing their emotional experience during the pandemic. The paramedics noted that 33 participants were in good spirits or doing well. Many (n = 42) shared that they were coping with the difficult times with the help of family, friends, neighbors, or church groups; for instance, some sat outside with building neighbours. Moreover, six participants reported feeling bored, and thirteen detailed feelings of loneliness and/or isolation. For instance, one participant regularly talked about their difficult experience of months of separation from their spouse living in long-term care.

The third and final major theme demonstrated how CP@clinic paramedics were able to support participants during the pandemic, such as encouraging them to call any time they needed to talk (n = 7). Many participants (n = 11) expressed appreciation for engaging with paramedics; one stated that paramedic calls helped to brighten their day, despite the hardships and health scares they had endured; in another instance a CP paramedic facilitated a participant receiving medical care by educating them about their need to go to the hospital; in addition, where participants had difficulty obtaining food, the paramedic arranged for them to receive grocery delivery. Furthermore, tailored COVID-19 safety education (n = 18) was also provided to participants by paramedics. In several of those cases, participants were educated about how to run errands safely, and in subsequent visits, participants described the precautions they now took when going out, demonstrating that participants were receptive to the education they received. Finally, some participants (n = 27) utilized the telephone visit to discuss non-COVID-19 health concerns with the paramedics. For instance, participants had concerns about high blood pressure, congestive heart failure, and falls, to name a few. The paramedics made referrals and contacted family doctors as necessary, which proved to be a useful resource for participants as some shared their difficulties with making appointments with their health care providers.

Discussion

The objective of this study was to investigate the telephone adaptation of CP@clinic, which integrated COVID-19, emergency preparedness, and social health screening for low-income older adults residing in social housing buildings. We found that infrastructure from CP@clinic provided a platform from which to swiftly contact a large number of vulnerable people. This was a valuable resource, especially early in the COVID-19 pandemic, and has the potential to be useful in other emergency situations. Furthermore, it is concerning that older adults in social housing were found to have limited access to the internet and cable TV, because COVID-19 information is largely disseminated through those mediums. Evidently, there is a need to reach this population through other channels or platforms, such as the CP@clinic program. By reaching this vulnerable population through the program, paramedics were able to support them with COVID-19 education and ultimately help them stay safe. This is evidenced by a temporal change in the thematic analysis which revealed that participants put the education from paramedics into practice, enabling them to stay safe whilst attending to daily activities.

Moreover, the study group was found to have a higher prevalence of social isolation compared to the general older adult population (25.6% vs. 19%) [19]. This is in keeping with previous literature which shows that subjective social isolation and loneliness was more prevalent in older adults in social housing [20]. This could suggest that older adults in social housing experience greater experiences of loneliness. However, social isolation in this group was likely to have been heightened by the pandemic, evidenced by the thematic analysis of paramedic notes which revealed that many participants experienced feelings of loneliness and isolation during the COVID-19 lockdowns. The higher prevalence of social isolation in this study group is concerning because it may be associated with serious mental and physical health consequences in the future, increased perceived risk for COVID-19, and a decrease in health-related quality of life [21, 22].

Furthermore, the thematic analysis found that many participants utilized their CP@clinic telephone visit to raise non-COVID health concerns to the paramedics, and paramedics were able to refer them to the appropriate care. By proactively contacting the participants, the community paramedics were able to prevent these chronic health concerns from falling through the cracks. Moreover, this demonstrates that the participants were comfortable raising their health concerns with the paramedics; it is possible that if it had been a volunteer or other person conducting the phone calls, these health concerns may not have been mentioned. This finding aligns with previous research where low-income older adults perceived community paramedics to be trustworthy and advocates for their health [23]; this strongly supports having the community paramedics make these phone calls during an emergency situation.

Implications for practice

The telephone-based adaptation of the CP@clinic Program has important implications for both practice and policy. The community paramedic wellness program should be further scaled to train and implement more paramedics to quickly reach and deliver care via telecare during emergency public health situations when it can be difficult to access health services. Other tele-health interventions involving paramedics have also demonstrated success in reducing ambulance transports to emergency departments and improving unit productivity [24]. Further integration of paramedics in telehealth interventions should be strongly considered. This study also underscores the need for public health and policy decision makers to adapt and create new innovative ways to reach populations with limited access to internet services. Policy recommendations and public health measures should prioritize protecting low-income older adults who reside in clustered social housing buildings [6].

Strengths and limitations

There are many strengths to this study including a novel approach to access and provide care to this vulnerable, hard-to-reach population. The program also had a wide geographical reach across Ontario. Moreover, collecting notes from paramedics provided additional insight into participants’ relationships with the paramedics and a look into the nuances of how the pandemic has influenced this particular population. However, not all participants had notes by the paramedics thereby missing valuable participant insights of the benefits of the program.

There are also limitations to this study, including a small sample size since some participants did not answer the telephone. Moreover, there were uneven sample sizes across regions, so we were unable to stratify results by location to investigate geographical trends. Finally, the COVID-19 screening tool underwent several changes over the study period (in line with updates to the Government of Ontario COVID-19 Self-Assessment Tool), so there were inconsistencies in the screening questions over time.

Conclusion

This study investigated a telephone adaptation of CP@clinic during the COVID-19 pandemic, integrating COVID-19, emergency preparedness, and social health screening for low-income older adults in social housing. Community paramedics were able to swiftly support the vulnerable study population using this innovative healthcare delivery system to increase access to healthcare services and public health information during a time of great health need. The population was found to have limited access to the internet and cable TV, and since COVID-19 information is largely disseminated through the media, there is a need to reach this population through other channels such as the CP@clinic program. Future research is needed to explore the future scale-up of CP@clinic adaptations to broadly reach and provide health and social care to low-income older adults in social housing.
