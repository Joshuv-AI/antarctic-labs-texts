Distributed Quantum Inner Product Estimation with Low-Depth Circuits
Congcong Zheng, Kun Wang, Xutao Yu, Ping Xu, Zaichen Zhang

Abstract
Distributed inner product estimation (DIPE) is a fundamental task in quantum information, aiming to estimate the inner product between two unknown quantum states prepared on distributed quantum platforms. Existing rigorous sample complexity analyses are limited to unitary 4 4 -designs, which present practical challenges for near-term quantum devices. This work addresses this challenge by exploring DIPE with low-depth quantum circuits. We first establish that DIPE with an arbitrary unitary 2 2 -design ensemble achieves an average sample complexity of Θ ⁡ ( 2 n ) \Theta(\sqrt{2^{n}}) , where n n is the number of qubits. We then analyze ensembles below unitary 2 2 -designs—specifically, the brickwork and local unitary 2 2 -design ensembles—showing average sample complexities of 𝒪 ⁡ ( 2.18 n ) \mathcal{O}(\sqrt{2.18^{n}}) and 𝒪 ⁡ ( 2.5 n ) \mathcal{O}(\sqrt{2.5^{n}}) , respectively. Furthermore, we analyze the state-dependent variance for the brickwork and Clifford ensembles. Remarkably, we find that DIPE with the global Clifford ensemble requires Θ ⁡ ( 2 n ) \Theta(\sqrt{2^{n}}) copies for arbitrary state pairs, with its efficiency further enhanced by the nonstabilizerness of the state pairs. Numerical simulations with GHZ states and Haar random states up to 26 26 qubits show that low-depth circuit ensembles can match the performance of unitary 4 4 -designs in practice. Our findings offer theoretically guaranteed methods for implementing DIPE with experimentally feasible unitary ensembles.

II
General Framework of DIPE

First, we present the general framework of DIPE, as illustrated in Fig.
1
.
In this work, we focus on
n
-qubit quantum systems with Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{n}
.
Consider two
n
-qubit platforms, each preparing an unknown quantum state,
\rho
and
\sigma
, respectively.
DIPE aims to estimate the inner product of these two states,
\tr[\rho\sigma]
.

II.1
Protocol

Let
\mathcal{E}=(\mathcal{U},\mu)
be a unitary ensemble, where
\mathcal{U}
is a subset of the
n
-qubit unitary group and
\mu
is a probability measure over
\mathcal{U}
.
DIPE consists of two main steps
Elben et al. 2020
;
Anshu et al. 2022
.

Step 1.
Randomized Measurements:
Randomly sample a unitary
U\sim\mathcal{E}
according to
\mu
,
apply
U
to both states
\rho
and
\sigma
, and
perform measurements in the computational basis with
m
shots for each state.
This yields measurement outcomes

\{\bm{a}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
and
\{\bm{b}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
,
where
\bm{a}_{i},\bm{b}_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}
.

Step 2.
Classical Post-processing:
Define a random variable

\displaystyle\mathbb{X}_{m}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum f_{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{a}_{i},\bm{b}_{j}),

(1)

where
f_{\mathcal{E}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
classical function
that depends on the ensemble
\mathcal{E}
, and will be discussed in detail later.

We repeat the above two steps
N
times to obtain a collection of random variables
\{\mathbb{X}_{m}^{(t)}\}_{t=1}^{N}
and compute the
mean estimator
:

\displaystyle\hat{\omega}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}\mathbb{X}_{m}^{(t)},

(2)

which serves as an unbiased estimator of the inner product
\tr[\rho\sigma]
.
The total number of state copies required on each device is
Nm
,
which determines the sample complexity of the protocol.
A summary of the protocol is provided in Algorithm
1
.

II.2
Classical Function

We now discuss the choice of the classical function in Eq. (
1
).
The only requirement for this function is that the estimator
\mathbb{X}_{m}
remains unbiased.
Clearly, the choice of the classical function depends critically on the random unitary ensemble
\mathcal{E}
.
To guide this selection, we define the
k
-moment channel
of
\mathcal{E}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{E}}^{(k)}(A)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathcal{E}}U^{\dagger\otimes k}AU^{\otimes k},

(3)

leading to the following lemma.

Lemma 1
.

To guarantee that
\hat{\omega}
defined in Eq. (
2
) is an unbiased estimator,
the classical function
f_{\mathcal{E}}
should satisfy

\displaystyle\tr[\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{E}}^{(2)}(O)(P\otimes P^{\prime})]=\begin{cases}2^{n},&P=P^{\prime},\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases},

(4)

for all
P,P^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
,
where
\mathcal{P}_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{I,X,Y,Z\}^{\otimes n}
is the
n
-qubit Pauli group, and

O\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{\bm{a},\bm{b}}f_{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{a},\bm{b})|{\bm{a}\bm{b}}\rangle\!\langle{\bm{a}\bm{b}}|
.

The proof is provided in Appendix
A
.
In
Elben et al. 2020
, the authors introduced two examples of classical functions:

1.

For
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{T}_{1}^{\otimes n}
, where
\mathcal{T}_{1}
is a unitary
2
-design on
\mathcal{H}_{1}
, the classical function is

f_{\mathcal{T}_{1}^{\otimes n}}(\bm{a},\bm{b})=2^{n}(-2)^{-\mathcal{D}(\bm{a},\bm{b})}
, where
\mathcal{D}(\bm{a},\bm{b})
is the Hamming distance of
\bm{a}
and
\bm{b}
;

2.

For
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{T}_{n}
, a unitary
2
-design on
\mathcal{H}_{n}
,
the classical function is
f_{\mathcal{T}_{n}}(\bm{a},\bm{b})=2^{n}
if
\bm{a}=\bm{b}
otherwise
-1
.

However, for other types of unitary ensembles, explicit constructions of classical functions remain largely unexplored.
In the following, we focus on a particularly structured class known as
Pauli-invariant ensembles

and investigate the properties of their associated classical functions.
It’s worth noting that all unitary ensembles explored in this work are Pauli-invariant.
As the name suggests, an ensemble
\mathcal{E}
is Pauli-invariant if, for every unitary
U\in\mathcal{E}
and all Pauli operators
P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
,
both
PU
and
UP
are also in the ensemble with the same probability distribution
Bu et al. 2024
.
For this kind of ensemble, we have the following lemma.

Lemma 2
.

If
\mathcal{E}
is a Pauli-invariant ensemble,
then, the corresponding classical function
f_{\mathcal{E}}
must satisfy

\displaystyle f_{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{a},\bm{b})=f_{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{a}\oplus\bm{b},\bm{0}),

(5)

where
(\bm{a}\oplus\bm{b})_{i}=0
if
\bm{a}_{i}=\bm{b}_{i}
and
1
otherwise.

See proof in Appendix
A
.
Hence, there are only
2^{n}
distinct values that
f_{\mathcal{E}}
can take if
\mathcal{E}
is a Pauli-invariant ensemble.

II.3
Sample Complexity

We now analyze the number of state copies required to estimate
\tr[\rho\sigma]
up to a fixed additive error
\varepsilon
and failure probability
\delta
.
By Chebyshev’s inequality, the estimator
\hat{\omega}
satisfies

\displaystyle\Pr\left\{\left|\hat{\omega}-\tr[\rho\sigma]\right|\geq\varepsilon\right\}\leq\frac{\mathbb{V}_{\mathcal{E}}(\mathbb{X}_{m})}{N\varepsilon^{2}},

(6)

where
\mathbb{V}_{\mathcal{E}}(\mathbb{X}_{m})
is the variance of the random variable
\mathbb{X}_{m}
with the unitary ensemble
\mathcal{E}
.
To achieve the desired precision and confidence, it suffices to use
N\geq\mathbb{V}_{\mathcal{E}}(\mathbb{X}_{m})/(\delta\varepsilon^{2})

random unitaries drawn from the ensemble
\mathcal{E}
.
Then, we focus on the variance
\mathbb{V}_{\mathcal{E}}(\mathbb{X}_{m})
.
With the law of total variance, we have the following lemma (see proof in Appendix
A
).

Lemma 3
.

Given two quantum states
\rho,\sigma
in
\mathcal{H}_{n}
and a unitary ensemble
\mathcal{E}
, the variance of the random variable
\mathbb{X}_{m}
is

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{\mathcal{E}}(\mathbb{X}_{m})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{4}\mathbb{V}_{\mathcal{E}}^{(i)}(\rho,\sigma),

(7)

where
\mathbb{V}_{\mathcal{E}}^{(1)}(\rho,\sigma)=-\tr^{2}[\rho\sigma]
,

\begin{split}\mathbb{V}_{\mathcal{E}}^{(2)}(\rho,\sigma)&=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\tr\left[\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{E}}^{(2)}(O^{2})(\rho\otimes\sigma)\right],\\
\mathbb{V}_{\mathcal{E}}^{(3)}(\rho,\sigma)&=\frac{m-1}{m^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{U}\mathbb{E}f_{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{a},\bm{b})\left[f_{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{a}^{\prime},\bm{b})+f_{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{a},\bm{b}^{\prime})\right],\\
\mathbb{V}_{\mathcal{E}}^{(4)}(\rho,\sigma)&=\frac{(m-1)^{2}}{m^{2}}\tr\left[\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{E}}^{(4)}(O^{\otimes 2})(\rho\otimes\sigma)^{\otimes 2}\right].\end{split}

As we can see, the variance depends on three main factors: the input states
\rho,\sigma
and the unitary ensemble
\mathcal{E}
.
In particular, each term
\mathbb{V}_{\mathcal{E}}^{(k)}
involves the
k
-moment channel of
\mathcal{E}
for
k\geq 2
, which is often hard to compute analytically.

To date, rigorous state-dependent variance analysis has only been established only for
DIPE with a unitary
4
-design ensemble
\mathcal{F}_{n}
. In this case, the
worst-case variance
is given by

\displaystyle\max_{\rho,\sigma}\mathbb{V}_{\mathcal{F}_{n}}(\mathbb{X}_{m})=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{2^{n}}{m^{2}}+\frac{1}{m}+\frac{1}{2^{n}}\right).

(8)

Hence, each platform requires
Nm=\Theta(\sqrt{2^{n}})
state copies in the worst case.
See details in
Anshu et al. 2022
and Appendix
A
.

II.4
Average Sample Complexity

As state-dependent bounds are generally difficult to compute and often inapplicable
due to the lack of prior knowledge about states, we consider the average-case behavior of the variance.
Although average-case bounds do not guarantee performance for all input states, they provide valuable intuition about the typical behavior of the protocol.
Specifically, we consider two common application scenarios of DIPE:
(i) estimating the purity of an unknown state, and
(ii) estimating the inner product between two unknown states.
A key observation from
Anshu et al. 2022
is that the sample complexity reaches its maximum when the unknown states are pure.
Motivated by this, we define two types of average variances as follows.

Definition 4

(Average Variances)
.

Case 1:

Let
\rho=\sigma=|{\psi}\rangle\!\langle{\psi}|
, where
|{\psi}\rangle
is a Haar random state.
The average variance 1 is defined as

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}^{a}_{\mathcal{E},1}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathbb{E}_{\psi}\mathbb{V}_{\mathcal{E}}(\mathbb{X}_{m}).

(9)

Case 2:

Let
\rho=|{\psi}\rangle\!\langle{\psi}|
and
\sigma=|{\phi}\rangle\!\langle{\phi}|
, where
|{\psi}\rangle
and
|{\phi}\rangle
are two independent Haar random states.
The average variance 2 is defined as

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}^{a}_{\mathcal{E},2}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathbb{E}_{\psi,\phi}\mathbb{V}_{\mathcal{E}}(\mathbb{X}_{m}).

(10)

Based on these definitions, we provide the following theorem that relates the average variances of DIPE to the classical function; See proof and the concrete formulas in Appendix
B
.

Theorem 5
.

Let
\mathcal{E}
be a Pauli-invariant ensemble with classical function
f_{\mathcal{E}}
, the average variances defined in Eqs. (
9
) and (
10
) are given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}^{a}_{\mathcal{E},1}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\left\lVert f_{\mathcal{E}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}{2^{n}m^{2}}+\frac{\left\lVert f_{\mathcal{E}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}{4^{n}m}+\frac{\left\lVert f_{\mathcal{E}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}{8^{n}}+\frac{f^{2}_{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{0},\bm{0})}{4^{n}}-1\right),

\displaystyle\quad\mathbb{V}^{a}_{\mathcal{E},2}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{\left\lVert f_{\mathcal{E}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}{2^{n}m^{2}}+\frac{\left\lVert f_{\mathcal{E}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}{4^{n}m}+\frac{\left\lVert f_{\mathcal{E}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}}{8^{n}}\right).

where
\left\lVert f_{\mathcal{E}}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{\bm{a}}f_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\bm{a},\bm{0})
.

Theorem
5
implies that once the classical function
f_{\mathcal{E}}
is known, the average variances can be computed directly.
Therefore, with the definition of
f_{\mathcal{T}_{n}}
and
f_{\mathcal{T}_{1}^{\otimes n}}
,
we establish the following two lemmas. These lemmas characterize the average sample complexities for arbitrary global
and local unitary
2
-design ensembles, respectively. Their proofs are detailed in Appendix
B
.

Lemma 6
.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{n}
be a
2
-design ensemble, the average variances defined in Eqs. (
9
) and (
10
) are given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}^{a}_{\mathcal{T}_{n},1},\mathbb{V}^{a}_{\mathcal{T}_{n},2}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{2^{n}}{m^{2}}+\frac{1}{m}+\frac{1}{2^{n}}\right),

(11)

Consequently, the average sample complexity is
\Theta(\sqrt{2^{n}})
,
where the matching lower bound has been proven in
Anshu et al. 2022
.

Lemma 7
.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{1}^{\otimes n}
be a local unitary
2
-design ensemble, the average variances defined in Eqs. (
9
) and (
10
) are

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}^{a}_{\mathcal{T}_{1}^{\otimes n},1},\mathbb{V}^{a}_{\mathcal{T}_{1}^{\otimes n},2}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{2.5^{n}}{m^{2}}+\frac{1.25^{n}}{m}+0.675^{n}\right),

(12)

Consequently, the average sample complexity is
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{2.5^{n}})
.

Several important remarks are in order.
First, our findings completely settle the average sample complexity of the global Clifford ensemble, as it’s an instance of a global
2
-design ensemble.
Second, we’ve established an analytical upper bound for the average sample complexity of the local Clifford ensemble, which is a special case of a local
2
-design ensemble.
Third, Lemma
6
demonstrates that a unitary 2-design ensemble
achieves the optimal sample complexity established in
Anshu et al. 2022
,
suggesting that
unitary
2
-design is all we need for DIPE
.
Finally, we observe from both lemmas that the second variance term,
\mathbb{V}_{\mathcal{E}}^{(2)}
,
is the primary factor driving scalability, an insight further corroborated by the numerical results presented in
Section
V
and Appendix
F
.

In the next section, we consider an experimentally friendly unitary ensemble that interpolates between
the local and global unitary
2
-design ensembles in terms of average sample complexity.

III
DIPE with Brickwork Ensemble

Here we consider DIPE with the brickwork ensemble
Bertoni et al. 2024
;
Rozon et al. 2024
;
Ippoliti et al. 2023
,
which is modulated by one-layer local Clifford circuits and the depth
d
of the randomly sampled two-local Clifford circuits, as shown in Fig.
1
.
We denote the brickwork ensemble of depth
d
as
\mathcal{B}_{d}
.
Notably,
\mathcal{B}_{0}
corresponds to the local Clifford ensemble.

III.1
Classical Function and Average Variance

First, we need to construct the corresponding classical function.
We find that the classical function is independent with the depth, only related with the parity of the depth,
and have the following lemma (see proof in Appendix
C
).

Lemma 8
.

Let
\mathcal{B}_{d}
be a brickwork ensemble, the classical function
f_{d}
is given by

\displaystyle f_{d}(\bm{a},\bm{b})=2^{n}\prod_{s\in S}(-2)^{-2\delta_{\bm{a}_{s},\bm{b}_{s}}},

(13)

where
S=\{(1,2),\cdots,(n-1,n)\}
if
d
is odd,
otherwise
S=\{(2,3),\cdots,(n,1)\}
, and
\bm{a}_{(i,j)}
is the
i
and
j
-th bits of
\bm{a}
.

We now turn to analyzing sample complexity and first consider the average variance.
Given the classical function define in Lemma
8
, we have the following lemma.

Lemma 9
.

Let
\mathcal{B}_{d}
be a brickwork ensemble, the average variances defined in Eqs. (
9
) and (
10
) are given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}^{a}_{\mathcal{B}_{d},1},\mathbb{V}^{a}_{\mathcal{B}_{d},2}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{2.18^{n}}{m^{2}}+\frac{1.09^{n}}{m}+0.54^{n}\right).

(14)

Consequently, the average sample complexity is
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{2.18^{n}})
.

Interestingly, Lemma
9
implies that the average sample complexity of DIPE with
brickwork ensembles is independent with the depth
d
.
This naturally raises the question: what role does the depth of the brickwork ensemble play in the performance of DIPE?

III.2
State-dependent Variance

To investigate the influence of the depth, we then consider the state-dependent variance and focus on the second term of the variance.
Define

\displaystyle\Xi_{\rho,\sigma}(P)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\tr[P\rho]\tr[P\sigma],\quad\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\Xi_{\rho,\sigma}(P)=2^{n}\tr[\rho\sigma].

We have the following lemma (See proof in Appendix
C
).

Lemma 10
.

For the brickwork ensemble
\mathcal{B}_{d}
, the corresponding classical function
f_{d}
, and states
\rho,\sigma
in
\mathcal{H}_{n}
,
the second term of the variance is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{\mathcal{B}_{d}}^{(2)}(\rho,\sigma)=\frac{1}{2^{n}m^{2}}\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\Xi_{\rho,\sigma}(P)\Upsilon_{d}(P),

(15)

where
\Upsilon_{d}(P)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{\bm{a}\in\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{n}}f_{d}^{2}(\bm{a},\bm{0})h(\bm{a},P)
and

\displaystyle h(\bm{a},P)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathcal{B}_{d}}\langle{\bm{0}}|UPU^{\dagger}|{\bm{0}}\rangle\langle{\bm{a}}|UPU^{\dagger}|{\bm{a}}\rangle.

(16)

Obviously,
h(\bm{a},P)
is hard to compute analytically.
To address this, we rewrite
h(\bm{a},P)
in the following form,

\displaystyle h(\bm{a},P)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\Pr\{UPU^{\dagger}\in\mathcal{Z}^{\rm C}_{\bm{a}}\}-\Pr\{UPU^{\dagger}\in\mathcal{Z}^{\rm A}_{\bm{a}}\},

(17)

where
\mathcal{Z}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{I,Z\}^{\otimes n}
,
X^{\bm{a}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\bigotimes X_{i}^{\bm{a}_{i}}
, and

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}^{\rm C}_{\bm{a}}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{P|P\in\pm\mathcal{Z},[P,X^{\bm{a}}]=0\},

(18)

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}^{\rm A}_{\bm{a}}

\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\{P|P\in\pm\mathcal{Z},\{P,X^{\bm{a}}\}=0\}.

(19)

Therefore, the physical meaning of
h(\bm{a},P)
is the difference between the probabilities that
UPU^{\dagger}
commutes or anti-commutes with
X^{\bm{a}}
.
Prior work
(
Bertoni et al. 2024
, Lemma 5)
shows that
h(\bm{0},P)
admits a matrix product state (MPS) representation with a clear physical interpretation.
Likewise, based on this physical meaning, we provide the following theorem to compute the exact value of
h(\bm{a},P)
with tensor network.

Theorem 11
.

h(\bm{a},P)
can be represented as a matrix product product (MPO) with bond dimension at most
\mathcal{O}(2^{d-1})
.
For depth
d=\mathcal{O}(\log n)
, it can be computed exactly in time
n^{\mathcal{O}(1)}
.

The construction is detailed in Appendix
C
.
With this MPO representation of
h(\bm{a},P)
, we can efficiently compute the
\Upsilon_{d}(P)
for all
P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
, which is also shown in Appendix
C

Numerical results in Appendix
F
show that
for all
P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\setminus\{I^{\otimes n}\}
,
\Upsilon_{d}(P)
will converge to
2^{n}
as the depth
d
increases.

IV
DIPE with Clifford Ensembles

We now consider DIPE with the global and local Clifford ensembles, which are two extreme cases of the brickwork ensemble
\mathcal{B}_{d}
,
and compute their sample complexities.
While the global and local Clifford ensembles are unitary
2
-design and local unitary
2
-design ensembles, respectively,
our analysis on the influence of circuit depth in Section
III
motivates a more refined analysis.
We therefore focus on the state-dependent sample complexities of these two ensembles.

IV.1
Global Clifford Ensemble

The global
n
-qubit Clifford ensemble
{\rm Cl}_{n}
forms a unitary
3
-design
Zhu 2017
.
The classical function is
f_{{\rm Cl}_{n}}\equiv f_{\mathcal{T}_{n}}
and two average variances are given in Lemma
6
.
Here, using the Schur-Weyl duality theory for the Clifford group
Gross et al. 2021
;
Chen and Zhu 2024
,
we analyze the state-dependent variance and obtain the following.

Theorem 12
.

For the global Clifford ensemble
{\rm Cl}_{n}
and states
\rho,\sigma
in
\mathcal{H}_{n}
,
the variance of
\mathbb{X}_{m}
defined in Eq. (
7
) satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{{\rm Cl}_{n}}(\mathbb{X}_{m})=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{2^{n}}{m^{2}}+\frac{1}{m}+\frac{1}{2^{n}}\left\lVert\Xi_{\rho,\sigma}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\right),

(20)

where
\left\lVert\Xi_{\rho,\sigma}\right\rVert_{2}^{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{P}\tr^{2}[P\rho]\tr^{2}[P\sigma]
.
Consequently, the worst-case sample complexity is
\Theta(\sqrt{2^{n}})
, where the matching lower bound has been proven in
Anshu et al. 2022
.

The proof is provided in Appendix
D
.
Notably, this result shows that the global Clifford ensemble achieves
a performance comparable
to that of the unitary
4
-design for all states
\rho
and
\sigma
.
Moreover,
\left\lVert\Xi_{\rho,\sigma}\right\rVert_{2}
is a nonstabilizerness measure studied in
Chen and Zhu 2024
.
Theorem
12
implies that nonstabilizerness can reduce the variance and improve the efficiency of DIPE with the global Clifford ensemble.
This phenomenon has also been observed in other tasks such as direct fidelity estimation
Leone et al. 2023
and thrifty classical shadows
Chen and Zhu 2024
.

IV.2
Local Clifford Ensemble

Since the single-qubit Clifford ensemble
{\rm Cl}_{1}
is a unitary
2
-design on
\mathcal{H}_{1}
,
the classical function of the local
n
-qubit Clifford ensemble
{\rm Cl}_{1}^{\otimes n}
satisfies
f_{{\rm Cl}_{1}^{\otimes n}}\equiv f_{\mathcal{T}_{1}^{\otimes n}}
,
and the average variances are given in Lemma
7
.
For the state-dependent variance, we focus on the second and the fourth terms, as shown in the following theorem.

Theorem 13
.

For the local Clifford ensemble
{\rm Cl}_{1}^{\otimes n}
and states
\rho,\sigma
in
\mathcal{H}_{n}
,
the second term of the variance is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{{\rm Cl}_{1}^{\otimes n}}^{(2)}(\rho,\sigma)

\displaystyle=\frac{2.5^{n}}{m^{2}}\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\frac{\Xi_{\rho,\sigma}(P)}{5^{|P|}}\leq\frac{3^{n}}{m^{2}},

(21)

where
\Xi_{\rho,\sigma}(P)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\tr[P\rho]\tr[P\sigma]
and
|P|
is the Pauli weight.
The upper bound in Eq. (
21
) is achieved when
\rho=\sigma
is a product state.
The fourth term of the variance is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{{\rm Cl}_{1}^{\otimes n}}^{(4)}(\rho,\sigma)

\displaystyle=\frac{(m-1)^{2}}{4^{n}m^{2}}\sum_{P,Q\in\mathcal{P}_{n}^{P}}\frac{\Xi_{\rho,\sigma}(P)\Xi_{\rho,\sigma}(Q)}{3^{-|\{i|P_{i}=Q_{i}\neq I\}|}},

(22)

where
\mathcal{P}_{n}^{P}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\left\{Q\in\mathcal{P}_{n}|\forall i,|P_{i}|\cdot|Q_{i}|=0\text{ or }P_{i}=Q_{i}\right\}
.

Consequently, if
\rho=\sigma
is a product stabilizer state, the sample complexity is
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{4.5^{n}})
.

See Appendix
D
for the proof.
From Theorem
13
, we can find that the performance of DIPE with the local Clifford ensembles is also influenced by the nonstabilizerness.
Unfortunately, it is challenging to derive a state-independent upper bound for Eq. (

for local Clifford ensembles remains undetermined.

VI
Conclusions

We presented the general requirements for DIPE, enabling the use of a broader type of unitary ensembles to realize the protocol.
Focusing on the average sample complexity, we showed that DIPE with the unitary
2
-design ensemble requires
\Theta(\sqrt{2^{n}})
state copies on average, which is optimal.
We then extended our analysis to ensembles below unitary
2
-designs, as summarized in Table
1
.
Specifically, we proved that DIPE with the local unitary 2-design requires
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{2.5^{n}})
copies on average, while the brickwork ensemble
\mathcal{B}_{d}
achieves
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{2.18^{n}})
, which is independent of circuit depth.
To investigate the influence of depth, we developed a tensor network approach to compute the second term of the variance and proposed a state-dependent variance.
We further analyzed the state-dependent sample complexity for the global and local Clifford ensembles.
For the global Clifford ensemble, DIPE requires
\Theta(\sqrt{2^{n}})
copies for all
n
-qubit states
\rho
and
\sigma
, achieving performance comparable to that of a unitary
4
-design.
We also showed that the nonstabilizerness of states enhances the performance of DIPE with the global Clifford ensemble.
In contrast, DIPE with the local Clifford ensemble requires
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{4.5^{n}})
copies for stabilizer product states.
A summary of the proven state-dependent variances is in Table
2
.

Many questions remain open.
For example, current DIPE and cross-platform verification protocols assume that both platforms implement the same unitary,
an assumption that may not hold in practice due to hardware imperfections.
This motivates the development of more robust protocols.

Appendix A
General Framework of DIPE

A.1
Classical Estimator

Before introducing the general framework of DIPE, we first define a classical estimator.
It’s worth noting that our definition here generalizes the classical collision estimator presented in Ref.
Anshu et al. 2022
.
We give a more general form of the classical estimator, which proves particularly useful for analyzing the sample complexity across various unitary ensembles.

Definition 14
.

Given samples
\bm{a}_{1},\cdots,\bm{a}_{m}\sim p
and
\bm{b}_{1},\cdots,\bm{b}_{m}\sim q
from two discrete distributions
p
and
q
, respectively,
a classical estimator is defined as

\displaystyle\mathbb{X}_{m}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}f(\bm{a}_{i},\bm{b}_{j}),

(26)

where
f
is a classical function.

Now we analyze the expectation and variance of this estimator,
which are crucial for understanding the requirements on the classical function
f
as well as the sample complexity of DIPE.
The following lemma generalizes
(
Anshu et al. 2022
, Lemmas 15 and 16)
, which focuses on the classical collision estimator,
to more general classical estimators.

Lemma 15
.

The expectation of
\mathbb{X}_{m}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{a},\bm{b}}\mathbb{X}_{m}=\sum_{\bm{a},\bm{b}}p(\bm{a})q(\bm{b})f(\bm{a},\bm{b}).

(27)

The variance of
\mathbb{X}_{m}
is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{\bm{a},\bm{b}}(\mathbb{X}_{m})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m^{2}}\mathbb{E}f^{2}(\bm{a},\bm{b})+\frac{m-1}{m^{2}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\bm{a},\bm{b}}f(\bm{a},\bm{b})\left(\mathbb{E}_{\bm{a}^{\prime}}f(\bm{a}^{\prime},\bm{b})+\mathbb{E}_{\bm{b}^{\prime}}f(\bm{a},\bm{b}^{\prime})\right)\right]+\left[\frac{(m-1)^{2}}{m^{2}}-1\right]\left(\mathbb{E}f(\bm{a},\bm{b})\right)^{2}.

(28)

Proof.

First, we compute the expectation:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{a},\bm{b}}\mathbb{X}_{m}=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{i,j=1}^{m}\mathbb{E}f(\bm{a}_{i},\bm{b}_{j})=\sum_{\bm{a},\bm{b}}p(\bm{a})q(\bm{b})f(\bm{a},\bm{b}).

(29)

Next, we compute the variance.
In general, for the sum of
N
random variables
\{\mathbb{Y}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, the variance is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{Y}_{i}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbb{V}(\mathbb{Y}_{i})+\sum_{i\neq j}{\rm Cov}(\mathbb{Y}_{i},\mathbb{Y}_{j}),

(30)

where
{\rm Cov}(\cdot,\cdot)
is the covariance.
Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{\bm{a},\bm{b}}(\mathbb{X}_{m})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m^{4}}\sum_{i,j}\mathbb{V}[f(\bm{a}_{i},\bm{b}_{j})]+\frac{1}{m^{4}}\sum_{i\neq k,j=l}{\rm Cov}[f(\bm{a}_{i},\bm{b}_{j}),f(\bm{a}_{k},\bm{b}_{j})]

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{m^{4}}\sum_{i=k,j\neq l}{\rm Cov}[f(\bm{a}_{i},\bm{b}_{j}),f(\bm{a}_{i},\bm{b}_{l})]+\frac{1}{m^{4}}\sum_{i\neq k,j\neq l}{\rm Cov}[f(\bm{a}_{i},\bm{b}_{j}),f(\bm{a}_{k},\bm{b}_{l})]

(31)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\mathbb{E}f^{2}(\bm{a},\bm{b})+\frac{m-1}{m^{2}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\bm{a},\bm{b}}f(\bm{a},\bm{b})\left(\mathbb{E}_{\bm{a}^{\prime}}f(\bm{a}^{\prime},\bm{b})+\mathbb{E}_{\bm{b}^{\prime}}f(\bm{a},\bm{b}^{\prime})\right)\right]+\left[\left(\frac{m-1}{m}\right)^{2}-1\right]\left(\mathbb{E}f(\bm{a},\bm{b})\right)^{2}.

(32)

∎

\displaystyle f_{\mathcal{T}_{1}^{\otimes n}}(\bm{a},\bm{b})=2^{n}\cdot(-2)^{-\mathcal{D}(\bm{a},\bm{b})},

where

\bm{a}
and
\bm{b}

Proof.

With the definition of

\displaystyle O

\displaystyle=\sum_{\bm{a},\bm{b}}f_{\mathcal{T}_{1}^{\otimes n}}(\bm{a},\bm{b})|{\bm{a}\bm{b}}\rangle\!\langle{\bm{a}\bm{b}}|=2^{n}\sum_{\bm{a},\bm{b}}\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}(-2)^{-\mathcal{D}(\bm{a}_{i},\bm{b}_{i})}|{\bm{a}_{i}\bm{b}_{i}}\rangle\!\langle{\bm{a}_{i}\bm{b}_{i}}|

\displaystyle=\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\left(2|{00}\rangle\!\langle{00}|+2|{11}\rangle\!\langle{11}|-|{01}\rangle\!\langle{01}|-|{10}\rangle\!\langle{10}|\right)

Then, with Lemma

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{T}_{1}^{\otimes n}}^{(2)}(O)

A.3
Sample Complexity

Now we consider the sample complexity of DIPE.
By Chebyshev’s inequality, we can only focus on the variance of random variable
\mathbb{X}_{m}
.
With the law of total variance, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{\mathcal{E}}(\mathbb{X}_{m})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathcal{E}}\mathbb{V}_{\bm{a},\bm{b}}[\mathbb{X}_{m}|U]+\mathbb{V}_{U\sim\mathcal{E}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\bm{a},\bm{b}}(\mathbb{X}_{m}|U)\right].

(52)

With Eq. (
28

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{U\sim\mathcal{E}}[\mathbb{E}_{\bm{a},\bm{b}}(\mathbb{X}_{m}|U)]=\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathcal{E}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\bm{a},\bm{b}}\mathbb{X}_{m}\right]^{2}-\tr^{2}[\rho\sigma],

(53)

we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{\mathcal{E}}(\mathbb{X}_{m})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathcal{E},\bm{a},\bm{b}}f^{2}(\bm{a},\bm{b})+\left(\frac{m-1}{m}\right)^{2}\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathcal{E}}(\mathbb{E}_{\bm{a},\bm{b}}\mathbb{X}_{m})^{2}-\tr^{2}[\rho\sigma]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{m-1}{m^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathcal{E}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\bm{a},\bm{b}}f(\bm{a},\bm{b})\left(\mathbb{E}_{\bm{a}^{\prime}}f(\bm{a}^{\prime},\bm{b})+\mathbb{E}_{\bm{b}^{\prime}}f(\bm{a},\bm{b}^{\prime})\right)\right]

(54)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\tr\left[\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{E}}^{(2)}(O^{2})(\rho\otimes\sigma)\right]+\left(\frac{m-1}{m}\right)^{2}\tr\left[\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{E}}^{(4)}(O^{\otimes 2})(\rho\otimes\sigma)^{\otimes 2}\right]-\tr^{2}[\rho\sigma]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{m-1}{m^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathcal{E}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\bm{a},\bm{b}}f(\bm{a},\bm{b})\left(\mathbb{E}_{\bm{a}^{\prime}}f(\bm{a}^{\prime},\bm{b})+\mathbb{E}_{\bm{b}^{\prime}}f(\bm{a},\bm{b}^{\prime})\right)\right]

(55)

\displaystyle=\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\sum_{i=1}^{4}\mathbb{V}_{\mathcal{E}}^{(i)}(\rho,\sigma),

(56)

where

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{\mathcal{E}}^{(1)}(\rho,\sigma)

\displaystyle=-\tr^{2}[\rho\sigma],

(57)

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{\mathcal{E}}^{(2)}(\rho,\sigma)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\tr\left[\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{E}}^{(2)}(O^{2})(\rho\otimes\sigma)\right],

(58)

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{\mathcal{E}}^{(3)}(\rho,\sigma)

\displaystyle=\frac{m-1}{m^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathcal{E}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\bm{a},\bm{b}}f_{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{a},\bm{b})\left(\mathbb{E}_{\bm{a}^{\prime}}f_{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{a}^{\prime},\bm{b})+\mathbb{E}_{\bm{b}^{\prime}}f_{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{a},\bm{b}^{\prime})\right)\right],

(59)

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{\mathcal{E}}^{(4)}(\rho,\sigma)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{m-1}{m}\right)^{2}\tr\left[\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{E}}^{(4)}(O^{\otimes 2})(\rho\otimes\sigma)^{\otimes 2}\right].

(60)

We can find that the value of

\mathcal{E}
for

Lemma 18
.

If the random unitary ensemble
\mathcal{E}
is a unitary
2

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{\mathcal{E}}^{(2)}(\rho,\sigma)=\frac{2^{n}+(2^{n}-1)\tr[\rho\sigma]}{m^{2}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{2^{n}}{m^{2}}\right).

Proof of Lemma
18
.

If the random unitary ensemble
\mathcal{E}
is a unitary
2
-design, we have

f=f_{\mathcal{T}_{n}}
and

\displaystyle O^{2}=\sum_{\bm{a},\bm{b}}f^{2}(\bm{a},\bm{b})|{\bm{a}\bm{b}}\rangle\!\langle{\bm{a}\bm{b}}|=(4^{n}-1)\sum_{\bm{a}}|{\bm{a}\bm{a}}\rangle\!\langle{\bm{a}\bm{a}}|+\mathbbm{1}.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{\mathcal{E}}^{(2)}(\rho,\sigma)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\tr\left[\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(O^{2})(\rho\otimes\sigma)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathcal{E}}\tr\left[(4^{n}-1)\sum_{\bm{a}}U^{\dagger\otimes 2}|{\bm{a}\bm{a}}\rangle\!\langle{\bm{a}\bm{a}}|U^{\otimes 2}(\rho\otimes\sigma)\right]+\frac{1}{m^{2}}

(64)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m^{2}}\frac{4^{n}-1}{2^{n}(2^{n}+1)}\cdot 2^{n}(1+\tr[\rho\sigma])+\frac{1}{m^{2}}

(65)

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{n}+(2^{n}-1)\tr[\rho\sigma]}{m^{2}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{2^{n}}{m^{2}}\right).

Lemma 20

(Lemma 16 of
Anshu et al. 2022
)
.

If the random unitary ensemble
\mathcal{E}
is a unitary

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{\mathcal{E}}^{(1)}(\rho,\sigma)+\mathbb{V}_{\mathcal{E}}^{(4)}(\rho,\sigma)=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{2^{n}}\right).

Proof of Lemma
20
.

If the random unitary ensemble
\mathcal{E}
is a unitary
4
-design, we have

f=f_{\mathcal{T}_{n}}
and

\displaystyle O^{\otimes 2}

\displaystyle=\left[(2^{n}+1)\sum_{\bm{a}}|{\bm{a}\bm{a}}\rangle\!\langle{\bm{a}\bm{a}}|-\mathbbm{1}\right]^{\otimes 2}.

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{\mathcal{E}}^{(4)}(\rho,\sigma)=

\displaystyle\left(\frac{m-1}{m}\right)^{2}\tr\left[\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{E}}^{(4)}(O^{\otimes 2})(\rho\otimes\sigma)^{\otimes 2}\right]

\displaystyle\left(\frac{m-1}{m}\right)^{2}\left[(2^{n}+1)^{2}\tr\left[\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{E}}^{(4)}(\Lambda_{1})(\rho\otimes\sigma)^{\otimes 2}\right]-2(2^{n}+1)\tr\left[\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{E}}^{(2)}(\Lambda_{2})(\rho\otimes\sigma)\right]+1\right],

-design with the above lemmas.

Theorem 21

(Theorem 13 of
Anshu et al. 2022
)
.

For unknown states
\rho,\sigma
in
\mathcal{H}_{n}
and the unitary
4
-design ensemble
\mathcal{F}_{n}
, the sample complexity of DIPE with
\mathcal{F}_{n}
is
Nm=\Theta(\sqrt{2^{n}})
.

Proof of Theorem
21
.

For any
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
and
\delta\in(0,1)
,
from the Lemma
18
, Lemma
19
, and Lemma
20
,
it is necessary and sufficient to have

\displaystyle N\geq\frac{1}{\delta\varepsilon^{2}}\frac{2^{n}}{m^{2}},\quad N\geq\frac{1}{\delta\varepsilon^{2}}\frac{1}{m},\quad N\geq\max\left\{\frac{1}{\delta\varepsilon^{2}}\frac{1}{2^{n}},1\right\},

(83)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\quad

\displaystyle Nm^{2}\geq\frac{2^{n}}{\delta\varepsilon^{2}},\quad Nm\geq\frac{1}{\delta\varepsilon^{2}},\quad N\geq\max\left\{\frac{1}{\delta\varepsilon^{2}2^{n}},1\right\}.

(84)

Here we ignored constants.
Therefore, we have

\displaystyle Nm\geq N\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sqrt{\frac{2^{n}}{\delta\varepsilon^{2}}}\geq\max\left\{\frac{1}{\sqrt{\delta\varepsilon^{2}2^{n}}},1\right\}\sqrt{\frac{2^{n}}{\delta\varepsilon^{2}}}=\max\left\{\frac{1}{\delta\varepsilon^{2}},\sqrt{\frac{2^{n}}{\delta\varepsilon^{2}}}\right\},

(85)

where the first inequality follows from
Nm^{2}\geq 2^{n}/(\delta\varepsilon^{2})
.
We concern the scalability and therefore have
Nm=\Theta(\sqrt{2^{n}})
.

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{5}{2}\right)^{n}\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}5^{-|P|}\Xi_{\rho,\sigma}(P),

(154)

where
\Xi_{\rho,\sigma}(P)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\tr[P\rho]\tr[P\sigma]
.
Then, we consider bounding the sum over
P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
.
Using the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}5^{-|P|}\Xi_{\rho,\sigma}(P)\leq\sqrt{\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}5^{-|P|}\tr^{2}[P\rho]}\sqrt{\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}5^{-|P|}\tr^{2}[P\sigma]}\leq\left(\frac{6}{5}\right)^{n},

(155)

where we use Lemma
22
.
Therefore, the second term of the variance has the following bound,

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{{\rm Cl}_{1}^{\otimes n}}^{(2)}(\rho,\sigma)\leq\frac{3^{n}}{m^{2}},

(156)

where the upper bound is achieved when
\rho=\sigma
is a product state.
Now we compute the fourth term of the variance.

O
can also be written as

\displaystyle O

\displaystyle=\bigotimes\left(\frac{1}{2}I^{\otimes 2}+\frac{3}{2}Z^{\otimes 2}\right),\quad O^{\otimes 2}=\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^{n}\bigotimes\left(I^{\otimes 4}+3I^{\otimes 2}\otimes Z^{\otimes 2}+3Z^{\otimes 2}\otimes I^{\otimes 2}+3^{2}Z^{\otimes 4}\right).

(157)

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{{\rm Cl}_{1}^{\otimes n}}^{(4)}(O^{\otimes 2})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^{n}\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}_{U_{i}\sim{\rm Cl}_{1}}U_{i}^{\dagger\otimes 4}\left(I^{\otimes 4}+3I^{\otimes 2}\otimes Z^{\otimes 2}+3Z^{\otimes 2}\otimes I^{\otimes 2}+3^{2}Z^{\otimes 4}\right)U_{i}^{\otimes 4}

(158)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^{n}\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\left[I^{\otimes 4}+3I^{\otimes 2}\otimes\mathcal{M}_{{\rm Cl}_{1}}^{(2)}(Z^{\otimes 2})+3\mathcal{M}_{{\rm Cl}_{1}}^{(2)}(Z^{\otimes 2})\otimes I^{\otimes 2}+3^{2}\mathcal{M}_{{\rm Cl}_{1}}^{(4)}(Z^{\otimes 4})\right]

(159)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^{n}\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\left(I^{\otimes 4}+3I^{\otimes 2}\otimes\mathbb{F}^{(2)}+3\mathbb{F}^{(2)}\otimes I^{\otimes 2}+3^{2}\mathbb{F}^{(4)}\right),

where we use Lemma 2 of

For

,

Lemma 22
.

For arbitrary
n
-qubit state
\rho

\displaystyle\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}5^{-|P|}\tr^{2}[P\rho]\leq\left(\frac{6}{5}\right)^{n}.

(171)

Proof.

Obviously, the maximum of L.H.S is achieved when
\rho
is a pure state.
Thus, we only consider pure state.
Firstly, for
n=1
case, we must have

\displaystyle\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}5^{-|P|}\tr^{2}[P\rho]=\left(\frac{6}{5}\right)^{1}.

(172)

Then, suppose that this inequality is held for
n-1
case.
For the
n
-qubit case, with Schmidt decomposition,
we can decompose an
n
-qubit state
\rho=|{\psi}\rangle\!\langle{\psi}|
as

\displaystyle|{\psi}\rangle

\displaystyle=a_{0}|{\varphi_{0}\phi_{0}}\rangle+a_{1}|{\varphi_{1}\phi_{1}}\rangle,\quad\rho=\sum_{i,j=0}^{1}a_{i}a_{j}|{\varphi_{i}\phi_{i}}\rangle\!\langle{\varphi_{j}\phi_{j}}|,

(173)

where

and
|{\varphi_{i}}\rangle,|{\phi_{i}}\rangle
are single-qubit states.
Then, for each

\displaystyle\frac{1}{5^{|P|}}\tr^{2}[P\rho]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{5^{|P|}}\left(\sum_{i,j=0}^{1}a_{i}a_{j}\langle{\varphi_{j}\phi_{j}}|P|{\varphi_{i}\phi_{i}}\rangle\right)^{2}

(174)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{5^{|P|}}\sum_{i,j,k,l=0}^{1}a_{i}a_{j}a_{k}a_{l}\langle{\varphi_{j}\phi_{j}\varphi_{k}\phi_{k}}|P^{\otimes 2}|{\varphi_{i}\phi_{i}\varphi_{l}\phi_{l}}\rangle.

(175)

For each
\langle{\varphi_{j}\phi_{j}\varphi_{k}\phi_{k}}|P^{\otimes 2}|{\varphi_{i}\phi_{i}\varphi_{l}\phi_{l}}\rangle
,
we compute its sum over
P
as follows,

\displaystyle\sum_{P\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\frac{1}{5^{|P|}}\langle{\varphi_{j}\phi_{j}\varphi_{k}\phi_{k}}|P^{\otimes 2}|{\varphi_{i}\phi_{i}\varphi_{l}\phi_{l}}\rangle

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{P_{1}\in\mathcal{P}_{1}}\frac{1}{5^{|P_{1}|}}\langle{\varphi_{j}\varphi_{k}}|P_{1}^{\otimes 2}|{\varphi_{i}\varphi_{l}}\rangle\right)\left(\sum_{P_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{n-1}}\frac{1}{5^{|P_{2}|}}\langle{\phi_{j}\phi_{k}}|P_{2}^{\otimes 2}|{\phi_{i}\phi_{l}}\rangle\right)

(176)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{4}{5}\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}+\frac{2}{5}\langle{\varphi_{j}\varphi_{k}}|\mathbb{S}|{\varphi_{i}\varphi_{l}}\rangle\right)\left(\sum_{P_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{n-1}}\frac{1}{5^{|P_{2}|}}\langle{\phi_{j}\phi_{k}}|P_{2}^{\otimes 2}|{\phi_{i}\phi_{l}}\rangle\right)

(177)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{4}{5}\delta_{ij}\delta_{kl}+\frac{2}{5}\delta_{ik}\delta_{jl}\right)\left(\sum_{P_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{n-1}}\frac{1}{5^{|P_{2}|}}\langle{\phi_{j}\phi_{k}}|P_{2}^{\otimes 2}|{\phi_{i}\phi_{l}}\rangle\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{6}{5}\sum_{P_{2}\in\mathcal{P}_{n-1}}\sum_{i,j,k,l=0}^{1}\frac{a_{i}a_{j}a_{k}a_{l}}{5^{|P_{2}|}}\langle{\phi_{j}\phi_{k}}|P_{2}^{\otimes 2}|{\phi_{i}\phi_{l}}\rangle

Appendix E
Performance Guarantee of DIPE with Approximate Unitary Design Ensembles

Here, we analyze the performance guarantee of DIPE using an
\varepsilon
-approximate
4
-design ensemble, denoted as
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
.
Such ensemble can be constructed using
\mathcal{O}(\log(n))
-depth circuits
Schuster et al. 2024
, and is defined as follows.

Definition 23

(
\varepsilon
-approximate unitary
4
-design, Definition 1 of
Schuster et al. 2024
)
.

An
n
-qubit unitary ensemble
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
is an
\varepsilon
-approximate unitary
4
-design if

\displaystyle(1-\varepsilon)\mathcal{M}^{(4)}_{\mathcal{F}_{n}}\preceq\mathcal{M}^{(4)}_{\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n}}\preceq(1+\varepsilon)\mathcal{M}^{(4)}_{\mathcal{F}_{n}},

(183)

where
\mathcal{F}_{n}
is an exact unitary
4
-design ensemble for
n
qubits,
\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{E}}
is the
k
-moment channel defined in Eq. (
3
), and
\mathcal{A}\preceq\mathcal{B}
denotes that
\mathcal{B}-\mathcal{A}
is a completely-positive map.

As shown in
Schuster et al. 2024
, the approximation error
\varepsilon
can be exponentially suppressed by increasing the circuit depth.
In the following, we construct a biased estimator of
\tr[\rho\sigma]
, whose bias
decreases exponentially
with circuit depth.

Recall that DIPE protocol consists of two steps: randomized measurements and classical post-processing.
Given unknown quantum states
\rho
and
\sigma
, we sample unitaries from
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
,
apply the corresponding randomized measurements with
m
shots,
and process the measurement outcomes using the classical function
f_{\mathcal{T}_{n}}
,
resulting in the classical estimator
\tilde{\mathbb{X}}_{m}
.
By repeating this procedure for
N
times, we obtain the biased estimator
\tilde{\omega}
of
\tr[\rho\sigma]
.
We then have the following theorem.

Theorem 24

(DIPE with
\varepsilon
-approximate Unitary
4
-design ensemble)
.

For DIPE with
\varepsilon
-approximate unitary
4
-design ensemble
\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n}
, the biased estimator
\tilde{\omega}
with the classical function
f_{\mathcal{T}_{n}}
satisfies

\displaystyle|\tilde{\omega}-\tr[\rho\sigma]|\leq\varepsilon\tr[\rho\sigma],

(184)

and the variance of the classical estimator
\tilde{\mathbb{X}}_{m}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}[\tilde{\mathbb{X}}_{m}]\leq(1+\varepsilon)\mathbb{V}[\mathbb{X}_{m}]+\varepsilon(3-\varepsilon)\tr^{2}[\rho\sigma],

(185)

where
\mathbb{X}_{m}
is the classical estimator when using unitary
4
-design ensemble.

Proof.

We bound the bias of the estimator as follows,

\displaystyle|\tilde{\omega}-\tr[\rho\sigma]|

\displaystyle=\left|\tr\left[\mathcal{M}_{\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n}}^{(2)}(O)(\rho\otimes\sigma)\right]-\tr\left[\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{F}_{n}}^{(2)}(O)(\rho\otimes\sigma)\right]\right|

(186)

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon\tr\left[\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{F}_{n}}^{(2)}(O)(\rho\otimes\sigma)\right]=\varepsilon\tr[\rho\sigma],

(187)

where
O=\sum_{\bm{a},\bm{b}}f_{\mathcal{T}_{n}}(\bm{a},\bm{b})|{\bm{a}\bm{b}}\rangle\!\langle{\bm{a}\bm{b}}|
and we use the definition of approximate unitary
4
-design.

We now turn to the variance, which is defined in Eq. (
56
),

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}[\tilde{\mathbb{X}}_{m}]=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m^{2}}\tr\left[\mathcal{M}_{\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n}}^{(2)}(O^{2})(\rho\otimes\sigma)\right]+\left(\frac{m-1}{m}\right)^{2}\tr\left[\mathcal{M}_{\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n}}^{(4)}(O^{\otimes 2})(\rho\otimes\sigma)^{\otimes 2}\right]-\left[\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n},\bm{a},\bm{b}}f_{\mathcal{T}_{n}}(\bm{a},\bm{b})\right]^{2}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\frac{m-1}{m^{2}}\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\tilde{\mathcal{F}}_{n}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\bm{a},\bm{b}}f_{\mathcal{T}_{n}}(\bm{a},\bm{b})\left(\mathbb{E}_{\bm{a}^{\prime}}f_{\mathcal{T}_{n}}(\bm{a}^{\prime},\bm{b})+\mathbb{E}_{\bm{b}^{\prime}}f_{\mathcal{T}_{n}}(\bm{a},\bm{b}^{\prime})\right)\right]

(188)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1+\varepsilon)\left[\sum_{i=2}^{4}\mathbb{V}_{\mathcal{F}_{n}}^{(i)}(\rho,\sigma)\right]-(1-\varepsilon)^{2}\tr^{2}[\rho\sigma]=(1+\varepsilon)\mathbb{V}[\mathbb{X}_{m}]+\varepsilon(3-\varepsilon)\tr^{2}[\rho\sigma],

(189)

where
\mathbb{X}_{m}
is the classical estimator when using unitary
4
-design ensemble and we use the definition of approximate unitary
4
-design.
∎
