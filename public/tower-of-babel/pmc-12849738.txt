Development, validation, and visualization of a machine learning-based predictive model for depression risk in sleep disorder patients
Authors: Huiying Wang, Chunyu Zhang, Bo Chen, Yulei Xie, Peng Tian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aims to develop, validate, and visualize a novel machine learning (ML)-based predictive model for depression risk in patients with sleep disorders.MethodsUsing data from the NHANES (2005–2020), 11 machine learning models were constructed, including Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO), Ridge Regression (Ridge), Elastic Net (ENet), Light Gradient Boosting Machine(LightGBM), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Random Forest (RF), Decision Tree (DT), XGBoost, Logistic Regression (LR), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), and Multilayer Perceptron (MLP). Model performance was evaluated using multiple metrics. Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) and calibration curves were used to assess the clinical applicability of the models. SHAP values were applied for model interpretation, and an online web calculator was developed for further model visualization.ResultsAmong the 11 machine learning models, the LightGBM model demonstrated the best performance with an AUC of 0.73. Calibration curves for both the training and test sets confirmed the model’s good calibration. The SHAP summary plot showed that the top three important features in the model were age, poverty-income ratio (PIR), and marital status. The model was integrated into an interactive web application that allows clinicians to predict depression risk based on 10 key clinical variables.ConclusionThis study successfully developed a predictive model for depression risk in patients with sleep disorders, demonstrating strong discriminatory ability and good clinical applicability. The online application provides clinicians with a user-friendly tool to assess depression risk and guide targeted prevention and intervention strategies.Clinical trial number Not applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07730-2.

Introduction

Sleep disorders are a prevalent global health issue, characterized by abnormalities in sleep quality, duration, or rhythm, and are well recognized as significant contributors to adverse health outcomes [1]. Epidemiological studies indicate that the global prevalence of sleep disorders is increasing [2], affecting approximately 10%-30% of the population in Western countries [3, 4] and 20%-30% in Asian countries [5, 6]. Individuals with sleep disorders often report dissatisfaction with sleep quality, duration, and total sleep time, which may result in daytime fatigue and related health complications. Studies have demonstrated that individuals with sleep disorders are at increased risk for developing cardiovascular diseases [7], hypertension [8], neurodegenerative disorders [9], cognitive impairment [10], diabetes [11], and cancer [12], thereby imposing a substantial burden on healthcare systems. Additionally, depression is a prevalent mental disorder and a leading cause of global disease burden and disability. Approximately 322 million people worldwide currently suffer from depression, representing 4.4% of the global population [13]. The clinical manifestations of depression include persistent low mood, anhedonia, cognitive slowing, diminished motivation, sleep disturbances, and reduced appetite [14, 15]. It is estimated that depression contributes to over one trillion dollars in global economic losses annually [16].

Studies have demonstrated that the prevalence of depression is substantially higher among individuals with sleep disorders compared to the general population [17, 18]. A meta-analysis revealed that individuals with sleep disorders at baseline have a twofold increased risk of developing depression during follow-up compared to those without sleep disorders [19]. Sleep disturbances are considered a core symptom of depression [20]. In many cases, the initial episode of depression is recognized through sleep disturbances, and worsening sleep quality often precedes the onset of depressive symptoms [21]. Approximately 90% of individuals with major depressive disorder experience sleep disturbances, including difficulty initiating sleep, maintaining sleep, non-restorative sleep, and early morning awakenings [22]. A significant proportion of individuals with depression report poor sleep quality and excessive daytime sleepiness [23]. These sleep disturbances profoundly affect individuals’ quality of life and occupational functioning.

A close bidirectional relationship exists between sleep disorders and depression. Sleep disorders can serve as a trigger for depression, resulting in emotional dysregulation, disruptions of physiological rhythms, and heightened stress responses; conversely, depression may aggravate sleep disorders through mechanisms including neurotransmitter imbalances and dysfunction of sleep-regulating centers, thereby perpetuating a vicious cycle [24]. This complex interplay presents a significant challenge for the early identification and intervention of comorbid patients, profoundly affecting their daily functioning and social interactions, and substantially increasing healthcare utilization and economic burden [25]. With the rapid advancement of big data and artificial intelligence(AI) technologies, machine learning (ML) has been increasingly utilized in the medical field [26]. ML algorithms demonstrate high adaptability and can effectively capture complex nonlinear relationships among variables [27]. Moreover, ML algorithms exhibit greater robustness to outliers, with improved efficiency and reduced bias in processing extreme values [28]. Existing studies suggest that machine learning models outperform traditional linear models, including logistic regression, in predicting depression [29]. Currently, dedicated research on the risk of depression in individuals with sleep disorders remains limited. Considering the complex relationship between sleep disorders and depression, along with their adverse long-term health consequences, the development of a predictive model for depression in individuals with sleep disorders holds considerable clinical significance.

This study employed data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) to develop 11 interpretable machine learning models aimed at the early identification of individuals at high risk of depression among patients with sleep disorders. The best-performing model was evaluated, and feature importance along with its influence on model output was visualized using SHAP summary plots. Additionally, an online web calculator was developed to facilitate practical implementation.

Methods

Data sources and study population

In this study, datasets from the NHANES, covering the period from 2005 to 2020, were used for model training and test. NHANES aims to assess the health and nutritional status of the U.S. community population, including both adults and children. This database routinely collects and stores interview and physical examination data, with updates every two years. This study was approved by the Ethics Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics, and all participants provided written informed consent before being included in the NHANES database. Details on the ethical approval and informed consent process can be accessed on the NHANES official website (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes) [30]. To ensure data consistency and usability, all continuous variables were standardized, and categorical variables were transformed through categorical encoding.

Inclusion criteria: (1) Diagnosis of a sleep disorder confirmed by a physician or healthcare professional; (2) Age ≥ 20 years. Exclusion criteria: (1) Missing examination data; (2) Incomplete clinical information related to predictor variables or outcome measures.

Evaluation of depression

The frequency of depressive symptoms over the past two weeks was assessed using the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) [31]. This screening tool is based on the criteria of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition (DSM-IV) [32]. The PHQ-9 comprises 10 items; however, only the first 9 items are used to calculate the total score, which ranges from 0 to 27, with higher scores reflecting more severe depressive symptoms. A total score of ≥ 10 is considered indicative of moderate to severe depression, with this threshold showing both sensitivity and specificity of 88% [33]. Several clinical studies have adopted PHQ-9 scores ≥ 10 as a diagnostic criterion for depression [34–36]. In this study, participants with a PHQ-9 total score ≥ 10 were classified as having depression.

Demographic data of participants: age, gender (male and female), race (Mexican American, other Hispanic, non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black, and other races), BMI status (underweight/normal weight/overweight/obesity), poverty-income ratio (PIR, low-income/non-low-income), education level (Less Than 9th Grade, 9-11th Grade, High School Grad/GED, Some College or AA degree, College Graduate or above), alcohol intake (non-drinker, 1 to < 5 drinks/month, 5 to < 10 drinks/month, or ≥ 10 drinks/month), and smoking status (current smoker, former smoker, or never smoker).Laboratory test data: GLU, HDL, LDL, TC, TG, and TyG index.Questionnaire data: marital status (Married/Living with Partner, widowed/divorced/separated, never married), asthma (yes or no), diabetes (yes or no), hypertension (yes or no), heart failure (yes or no), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD, yes or no), coronary artery disease (CAD, yes or no), stroke (yes or no), cancer (yes or no), and sleep disorders (yes or no).

Collinearity analysis and feature screening

Multicollinearity among covariates was assessed using the variance inflation factor(VIF). Multicollinearity was considered present when the VIF exceeded the threshold of 5 to 10 [37]. Additionally, the inclusion of too many independent variables elevates the likelihood of overfitting in ML algorithms and intensifies the challenge of computing high-dimensional data. To address this, we employed the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator regression(LASSO) for feature selection [38].

Statistical analysis

Data analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.2.3). All variables were assessed for normality prior to analysis. Continuous variables with a normal distribution were presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared using independent t-tests, while non-normally distributed continuous variables were summarized as median (interquartile range, IQR) and analyzed using non-parametric tests. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages, and compared using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. A two-tailed P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Model construction, evaluation and test

In this study, we developed and compared 11 supervised machine learning algorithms for predicting depression risk, including linear, ensemble, distance-based, and neural network models, to ensure broad algorithmic coverage and structural diversity. Logistic regression(LR) [39] estimates probabilities by modeling the relationship between features and the target variable, making it suitable for binary classification problems. Support vector machine(SVM) [40] maps input data into a high-dimensional feature space to capture nonlinear patterns and enhance classification performance. Random forest(RF) [41] achieves robust classification by aggregating the outputs of multiple decision trees, thereby reducing variance and improving model stability. Extreme Gradient Boosting(XGBoost) [42] and Light Gradient Boosting Machine(LightGBM) [43] are both gradient boosting-based frameworks, characterized by efficiency and modeling flexibility. Decision tree (DT) [44] utilizes a hierarchical tree structure for recursive feature splitting, offering high interpretability and intuitive decision logic. K-nearest neighbors(KNN) [45] classifies samples based on proximity in feature space, making it particularly effective in small-sample contexts. Multilayer perceptron (MLP) [46] is a foundational neural network architecture with powerful nonlinear modeling capabilities. Additionally, Lasso regression [47] applies L1 regularization to enable automatic feature selection, Ridge regression [48] employs L2 regularization to mitigate multicollinearity, and Elastic Net [49] integrates both to improve model stability and generalizability. These models are widely used in contemporary clinical prediction research and enable comprehensive evaluation of model performance from multiple algorithmic perspectives.

The final dataset extracted from the NHANES database was randomly divided into training and test sets in a 7:3 ratio. The final predictor variables were treated as machine learning features, with the training set used for model selection and hyperparameter optimization. The test set was used to evaluate model performance. Each classification algorithm was internally optimized through five-fold cross-validation. After selecting the optimal hyperparameters, models were retrained on the full training subset to finalize weighting and generate the locked model. The discriminative performance of each model was evaluated by plotting Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves and calculating the area under the curve (AUC). To comprehensively assess model performance, additional metrics including accuracy (ACC), positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), sensitivity (SEN), specificity (SPE), F1-score, and Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) were reported. Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) was performed to evaluate the clinical utility of each model in decision-making [50]. Calibration curves were plotted to assess the calibration and performance bias of each model by comparing predicted and observed probabilities [51]. The optimal machine learning model was selected primarily based on AUC, supplemented by additional performance metrics. Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) values were employed to interpret the optimal machine learning model and to visualize feature importance [52–54]. Furthermore, an interactive web-based calculator was developed using the R Shiny package to facilitate the clinical application of the optimal machine learning model [55]. All statistical analyses, model development, and validation were performed using R software (version 4.2.3).

Feature selection

Based on clinical experience and relevant literature reviews, we initially selected 23 potential predictors. We then conducted multicollinearity diagnostics for these variables(Supplementary Table 1), which revealed that the VIFs for HDL_C, LDL_C, TC, TG, and TyG were all greater than 10, indicating significant multicollinearity among these variables. We then used Lasso regression to identify potential risk factors for depression in patients with sleep disorders [38]. Lasso regression adds an L1 penalty to the loss function, shrinking some coefficients to zero, thereby enabling automatic feature selection. In Lasso regression, predictors with zero coefficients are excluded, while those with non-zero coefficients are retained. Ultimately, only eight predictors-age, gender, marital status, COPD, stroke, smoking, PIR, and education level-retained non-zero coefficients within one standard error of the minimum mean squared error at the optimal λ (Fig. 1). Considering the model’s clinical interpretability [56], we also included two clinically meaningful variables-race and alcohol use frequency-as final predictors.

Model performance comparison

The trained model was applied to the test set for evaluation purposes. Compared to the other models, the LightGBM, MLP, Ridge, and ENet models(AUC: 0.73) demonstrated superior performance in the test set, achieving the highest AUC values (Fig. 2). The AUC values for four models-Logistic, Lasso, XGBoost, and SVM-were all 0.72. The AUC value for the RF model was 0.69, the KNN model had an AUC of 0.66, and the DT model had the lowest AUC value of 0.65. The accuracy, sensitivity/recall, specificity, PPV, NPV, MCC, Kappa, and F1 scores for all 11 ML models are presented in Fig. 3. The Delong test was used to compare the differences in the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves between models (Supplementary Table 2). The calibration performance of each model was assessed using calibration plots. The calibration plot for the training set showed that the predicted probabilities of the LightGBM model closely matched the actual probability of depression occurrence (Fig. 4A). The calibration curve for the test set also indicated that the LightGBM model performed well (Fig. 4B). The DCA curves for both the training set (Fig. 5A) and the test set (Fig. 5B) demonstrated that, among the 11 ML models, the LightGBM model provided a significantly positive net benefit in predicting the risk of depression occurrence.

ML model features importance visualization

By summarizing and comparing the AUC and related metrics of each model, the LightGBM model was ultimately selected as the predictive model, and a SHAP summary plot (Fig. 6A) was further generated to explain the results of the LightGBM model. For each feature, each point in the plot represents a single patient. The position of the points on the X-axis (i.e., the actual SHAP values) represents the impact of the feature on the model’s output for a specific patient. The higher the feature value on the Y-axis, the more important the feature is to the model. The importance bar chart (Fig. 6B) displays the significance of each variable in predicting depression. Discussion

This study developed a novel risk prediction model to assess the risk of depression in adult patients with sleep disorders in the United States, with the LightGBM model exhibiting excellent discriminatory power and robust calibration. The risk prediction model is made accessible through an interactive web application, which is designed to streamline the operation and facilitate the replication of the analysis process.

The findings of this study suggest that the LightGBM model holds potential for identifying the risk of depression in individuals with sleep disorders. However, its AUC value was 0.73, indicating a moderate level of performance. This result is generally consistent with previous studies [57–59], such as the model developed by Wu et al. [58] using NHANES 2005–2018 data to predict depression in stroke patients, which achieved a best AUC of 0.755 (95% CI = 0.675–0.834). Similarly, Lee et al. [59] developed a depression prediction model for individuals with hypertension using NHANES 2011–2020 data, in which the ANN attained the highest AUC of 0.813. The relatively moderate performance may be due to the NHANES dataset lacking certain key variables (e.g., genetic markers, environmental stressors) and the absence of objective indicators for diagnosing sleep disorders. Notably, Pan et al. [60] significantly improved model performance by integrating multicenter data, wearable device parameters, and electronic health records, with their LR model achieving an AUC as high as 0.9467, and the MLP model showing the lowest AUC (0.8756). These findings suggest that integrating more objective and multidimensional data sources could improve the predictive accuracy of machine learning models. Therefore, future studies should aim to incorporate objective sleep monitoring data, biological markers, and longitudinal follow-up measures to enhance the model’s sensitivity, specificity, and clinical applicability.

Sleep disorders and depression are prevalent global health issues that frequently coexist and mutually exacerbate one another [61]. Animal studies have demonstrated that continuous light enhances depressive-like behavior in mice, while exposure to nocturnal light induces depressive-like behavior in diurnal rodents [62, 63]. Clinical studies have also found that individuals with shorter sleep duration are more prone to depressive symptoms compared to those with longer sleep duration [64]. Similarly, sleep disorders are the most prevalent symptom in patients with depression, with approximately 90% of individuals with severe depression reporting sleep disturbances [65]. The specific mechanisms underlying the relationship between sleep disorders and depression remain unclear; potential factors may include elevated inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6 and TNF) [66], a reduction in monoaminergic neurons coupled with an increase in cholinergic neurons [67], disrupted sleep rhythms resulting in abnormal expression of clock genes [68], and genetic influences [69].

ML has the advantages of identifying trends and patterns, requiring no manual intervention, continuous improvement, and wide applicability [70]. ML algorithms, such as SVM [71], RF [72], DT [73], XGBoost [74] and KNN [59], are widely used in building depression prediction models for different populations. Compared to traditional logistic regression models, this study used Lasso to select feature variables, thus avoiding the risk of model overfitting. Using the selected feature variables, we built 11 ML models, with the LightGBM model demonstrating the best area under the curve (AUC: 0.73). We further explained the importance of feature variables using SHAP values and found that age ranked the highest among the predictors in the LightGBM model. A study based on the national database of the Department of Veterans Affairs healthcare system showed that the prevalence of depression varies with age [75]. Research by Li et al. reported that the prevalence of depressive symptoms among middle-aged and older adults is approximately 26.0%, with the rate gradually increasing with age [76]. However, our study’s results reveal that a significant proportion of young sleep disorder patients are more likely to develop depression. This finding aligns with the results of Gallo et al. [77], whose study showed a “U” shaped relationship between age and the occurrence of depression. In this relationship, the depression rate is higher in younger individuals, lower in middle-aged individuals, and increases again in older individuals. Hargrove et al.‘s study also pointed out that the risk of depression is higher during adolescence (12–17 years old) and young adulthood (18–26 years old), while it is relatively lower between the ages of 27 and 39 [78]. This finding suggests that young patients with sleep disorders may be a crucial focus for future preventive efforts. Health authorities can mitigate the substantial harm and economic burden caused by depression by implementing early interventions for young patients with sleep disorders.

The LightGBM algorithm-based model revealed that education level has a protective effect on the occurrence of depression in patients with sleep disorders. Higher education levels are associated with a reduced risk of depression. Previous research has suggested that individuals with higher education may be better equipped to manage their mental health, whereas those with lower education may lack the resources or information needed to do so, making them more susceptible to depression [79]. Additionally, lower income and poorer socioeconomic status are well-established risk factors for depression [80], and individuals with lower education may lack sufficient economic and social resources to manage depressive episodes [81]. Our findings also support this conclusion. PIR is an index calculated based on inflation-adjusted household income and the number of people in the household. Our research results show that individuals with a PIR below 1.3 seem to be more likely to develop depression, a finding similar to that of Na, K. S. et al. [82]. High-income patients have access to more medical resources and healthcare services, which allows for better disease control and mental health status [83], while low-income patients may have poor disease management and control due to limited resources, leading to worse depression symptom control.

Our study also found that female, smokers, and unmarried or divorced sleep disorder patients may be more likely to develop depression. In the United States, where women often face the dual pressures of family and work, women are nearly twice as likely as men to experience depression [84]. In addition, women with chronic diseases often find it difficult to fulfill gender-specific social roles, thus increasing the risk of depression [85]. Similarly, smokers are more than twice as likely to develop depression as non-smokers [86], and meta-analysis has shown that depression is associated with poorer smoking cessation outcomes [87]. Further research has demonstrated that depressive symptoms and smoking share a common neural pathway in the brain involving nicotinic acetylcholine receptors [88]. Clinically, antidepressants such as bupropion and nortriptyline have been successfully used in smoking cessation treatment [89, 90]. Moreover, nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs) have been suggested as potential therapeutic targets for depression [91].

Chronic diseases are closely related to the occurrence of depression, which is also confirmed in our study. Research shows that patients who have previously had a stroke are prone to mental health issues, with more than 33% of stroke patients suffering from depression [92]. A study by Li et al. indicates that patients with hypertension combined with type 2 diabetes have a higher severity of depression compared to those with only hypertension [93]. Moreover, myocardial infarction, cerebrovascular diseases, and chronic respiratory diseases (such as asthma and COPD) significantly increase the risk of depression [94–96]. The more chronic diseases a patient has, the higher the risk of developing depressive symptoms. This may be related to functional limitations caused by comorbidities, which in turn increase psychological stress, disappointment, and frustration, thus raising the risk of depression [97].

To enhance the intuitiveness and ease of use of the predictive model, we developed an online calculator. In this application, users can input eight feature variables, adjust model parameters, and obtain personalized depression risk predictions. The application assists clinicians in assessing the risk of depression in patients with sleep disorders and aids in developing targeted prevention and intervention strategies. Moreover, the application can be used for medical teaching and related research work.

There are still some limitations in this study. First, many of the predictor variables were derived from self-reported data, which are susceptible to information bias. For instance, the PHQ-9 questionnaire and subjective assessments of sleep disorders may underestimate symptom severity or result in misclassification. Future studies could incorporate objective data such as wearable device outputs or electronic health records to enhance measurement accuracy [54, 60]. Second, although the LightGBM model performed well in internal validation, its robustness needs to be tested with external data. Moreover, most machine learning algorithms struggle to handle complex survey data with multistage stratified sampling designs, which are common in studies like NHANES. Future studies should explore ways to more effectively incorporate sampling weights into machine learning techniques to further improve the scientific robustness and real-world applicability of such models. Finally, although the model includes some socioeconomic variables, it lacks coverage of macro-level socioecological factors such as neighborhood safety and environmental pollution. Future studies are encouraged to incorporate geographic information systems or community survey data to enhance the model’s ecological validity and predictive power.

Conclusions

In this study, we utilized data from the NHANES database to construct 11 predictive models for assessing depression risk in patients with sleep disorders. Among the 11 machine learning algorithms, the LightGBM model exhibited the best predictive performance. Additionally, we developed an online calculator that enables clinicians to efficiently predict depression risk in patients with sleep disorders.
