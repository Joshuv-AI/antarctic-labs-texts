A study of disability and socio-economic impact of mental morbidities from the state of Madhya Pradesh, India
Authors: Vijender Singh, Roshan F. Sutar, Suruchi Gupta, Abhijit P. Pakhare, Arun M. Kokane, B. A. Aravind, Gopalkrishna Gururaj, Mathew Varghese, Vivek Benegal, Girish N. Rao
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Background:Disability associated with mental illness has a disproportionate impact on the work, social, and family responsibilities of an individual toward society. The evidence for disability in mental illnesses would help the clinician, caregivers, policymakers, and various stakeholders to come up with sustainable solutions not only to help fill the existing gaps in care but also to develop new avenues as per the specific needs of the population of Madhya Pradesh (MP).Aim:To estimate the burden of disability related to mental illnesses in the state of MP.Materials and Methods:A multi-site cross-sectional study was conducted in 2015–16 as part of National Mental Health Survey among adults above 18 years of age. Samples were selected using multi-stage, stratified, random cluster sampling based on probability proportionate to size. Six tehsils with one urban metro out of four districts from a total of 50 districts were selected in the state of MP. The Sheehan Disability Scale and socio-economic impact of illness (from selected questions from WHO-Disability Assessment Schedule-2.0) were used to assess mental morbidity and the subjective reporting of disability.Results:The weighted prevalence of disability (n = 1011) was found as 10.2%, 13.1%, and 13.9%, respectively, in work/school, social life, and family/home domains. The weighted prevalence of moderate to extreme disability in the same domains was, respectively, 5.1%, 6.7%, and 7.3%. The presence of common mental disorders (CMDs) increases the odds of self-reported disability in work [odds ratio (OR) 2.48, 95% CI 1.35 to 4.59], social life (OR 2.74, 95% CI 1.50 to 5.07), and family domains (OR 3.03, 95% CI1.62 to 5.74). When combined with common mental disorders, tobacco use disorder further escalates the odds of self-reported disability in all three domains [OR 7.10, confidence interval (CI) 3.15 to 16.37; 4.93, CI 2.19 to 11.28; and 7.10, CI 2.78 to 19.25]. Currently, non-working persons had a higher disability in social life and family life domains (P = 0.003 and P = 0.021), respectively.Conclusion:We report a substantial magnitude of disability in social, work, and family life domains. Participants having CMDs, female gender, and those non-working had more disabilities and would require targeted interventions.

INTRODUCTION

According to World Health Organization, 15% of the world's population is estimated to experience disability in any form. The magnitude of disability caused by mental illnesses is significant and is reflected in the personal, social, and occupational spheres of life of an individual. In developing countries like India, this burden is equally shared by caregivers due to proximity to the index case. The measured global burden of disability (GBD) due to mental illness is 7%.[12] National Mental Health Survey (NMHS) 2015–16 was the first attempt in India and therefore in the state of Madhya Pradesh (MP) to estimate the prevalence of mental morbidity and self-reported disability among a representative sample. The estimates of the prevalence of mental illness are well known; however, the disability caused by mental illness appears to be varied according to geographical region and other determinants such as age, gender, and socio-economic status.[345] The worldwide data for the mental disorders associated with disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) (for the year 2016) range from 7% to 13% with India alone contributing for 15% of these DALYs.[6]

Knowing about the higher prevalence of mental illness is most helpful for the stakeholders when it is complemented with information on associated disabilities in a particular state. The inability of an individual with mental illness to contribute toward the growth of the community and the state indirectly reflects the burden of mental illness and calls for tailored interventions.

According to a recent study on the Global Burden of Diseases (GBD), mental illnesses account for 32.4% of years lived with disability (YLDs) and 13% of the DALYs, and these vary across geographical areas.[78] The contribution of mental disorders to the total DALYs in India has doubled since 1990 and reached 4.7% in 2017, the maximum chunk of which is contributed by the common mental disorders (CMDs)[9] including depressive and anxiety disorders. Furthermore, the ratio of the state DALY rate to the median DALY rate for the state of MP ranges between 0.75 and 0.99.[9] The majority of the studies conducted in the community setting in India reported the disability among mental illnesses contributed by severe mental disorders (SMDs) as well as CMDs, and among CMDs, important contributors are depression and anxiety.[1011121314] However, the state-wise data could give us more insights into the specific needs of the population residing in a given geographical area, which also stands true for their mental illness and disability and consequently generating avenues for rehabilitation. Therefore, this study becomes important in providing the community data for the state of MP about disability associated with mental morbidity. A systematic analysis of the data collected in NMHS 2015–16 was carried out for the state of MP to understand the collective and categorical statements of disability in the family, social, and work domains among all kinds of mental morbidities in the past month of assessment.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

We have performed a secondary data analysis of the NMHS dataset for the state of MP. NMHS was a community-based cross-sectional door-to-door survey among adults above 18 years of age, conducted under the technical guidance of the National Institute of Mental Health and Neurosciences (NIMHANS), Bengaluru, across 12 states in India with one of the objectives to estimate the prevalence of mental morbidity and associated disability in the state along with other parameters.[15] The detailed methodology of the survey has been covered by Pradeep BS et al.[15] A total of 3240 individuals were contacted, and 2621 were interviewed during NMHS-MP 2015–2016.[16]

The NMHS dataset comprises socio-demographic details, Mini International Neuropsychiatric Inventory (MINI) 6.0.0, the Sheehan Disability Scale (SDS), and the socio-economic impact of illness (from selected questions from WHO-Disability Assessment Schedule-2.0) (WHODAS 2.0). The dataset also provides a classification of the mental morbidity status of each individual as per his/her response to the study tools. SDS was used to assess self-reported disability status, while the socio-economic impact of illness comprised seven selected questions from WHODAS 2.0 to look at the subjective reporting of overall difficulties, the duration of these difficulties in the past 30 days, their impact on routine activities, expenditure due to illness, and whether a respondent was missing from family, social, or leisure activities due to illness.

The NMHS had adopted the ICD-10-DCR criteria for classifying psychiatric disorders and has operationally defined specific terminologies including “Any mental morbidity”, which included disorders screened positive by MINI 6.0, classified under ICD-10-DCR sections F10-19 (except F17, i.e., tobacco use disorders), that is, F20-29, F30-33, and F40-43. Common mental disorders included depressive disorders (F32–F33) (except severe depression with psychotic features, F31.5 and F33.3), neurotic and stress-related disorders (F40–48), and alcohol and other substance use disorders (F10–F19), except tobacco use disorders (F17). “Severe mental disorders” included schizophrenia and other psychotic disorders (F20–F29), bipolar affective disorder (F30–F31), and severe depression with psychotic features (F31.5 and F33.3).

Analyses were conducted using the R Statistical language (version 4.0.3; R Core Team, 2020)[17] using the packages tidyverse (version 1.3.0; Wickham et al., 2019),[18] gtsummary (version 1.4.1; Daniel Sjoberg et al., 2021),[19] and survey (version 4.1.1; Lumley, 2020) R is an open source software for statistical analysis and data management by using basic and advanced statistical packages.[20] Sampling weights were used for calculation of the weighted prevalence of mental illnesses coded by using the International Classification of Disease, 10th revision, Diagnostic Criteria for Research (ICD 10 DCR) as well as the prevalence of self-reported disabilities in various domains (work/school, social life, and family/home). Scores of the selected questions from WHODAS 2.0 were categorized as no (0), mild (1 to 3), moderate (4 to 6), marked (7 to 9), and extreme disability (10). Considering the high prevalence of tobacco use in the state of MP, a sub-group analysis among the strata of tobacco users and non-users was performed.[21]

Association of age group, gender, education, occupation, marital status, income, locality, CMDs, SMDs, and tobacco use disorders with grades of disability in each domain were tested by cross-tabulating and applying the Chi-square test. Then we performed ordinal logistic regression wherein grades of self-reported disability were dependent variables and age group, gender, marital status, occupation, locality, tobacco use disorder, and CMD were independent factors. The selection of independent factors was based on the results of univariate analysis, wherein variables with a P value less than 0.20 were selected. Nagelkerke R2 is also reported.

RESULTS

The weighted prevalence of current mental morbidity for the state of MP was 13.6% as compared to the same data at the national level (10.56%).[522] The mental and behavioral problems due to the use of tobacco were found to be 34.89% (CI: 34.68–35.1) for the state of MP. Females represented 52.20%, while males represented 47.80%, which was comparable to the national data of NMHS. The treatment pattern among respondents with current mental morbidity is described elsewhere.[5] The point prevalence of any mental morbidity, CMDs, and SMDs was 13.6% (95% CI 11.6–15.9), 13.3% (95% CI 11.4–15.5), and 0.4% (95% CI 0.2–0.7), respectively, in the state of MP. The weighted prevalence of self-reported disability of any grade was reported as 10.2%, 13.1%, and 13.9% and moderate to extreme disability was reported by 5.1%, 6.7%, and 7.3% of participants, respectively, in work/school, social life, and family/home domains [Table 1].

Distribution of work/school, social life, and family/home life-related disabilities across socio-demographic and mental morbidity variables

The distribution for disability related to work/school was more in women (N = 278, 67%) than in men (N = 616, 33%) and family/home domains were more in women (N = 271, 66.8%) than in men (N = 571, 33.2%), and it was statistically significant (a P value of 0.03 and 0.04). Among all the three domains, the highest proportion of extreme disability perceived by men was that for social life. Fifty percent of all respondents perceiving extreme disability in their social lives belonged to the age group of 40–49 years. Similarly, 59% of all respondents perceiving extreme disability in the family or home life also belonged to the 40–49 years age group. In general, 65% of all the respondents were males and the majority of them had reported no disability in work or school domains. A higher proportion of respondents feeling extreme disability in social life were still working (66.5%) as compared to those feeling extreme disability in family and home life (38.9%). However, this pattern was reversed for non-employed individuals as a higher proportion felt extreme disability in family and home life (61.1%) than in social life (33.5%). The relationship of marital status was significant for family and home life-related disabilities with a P value of 0.005. An astounding 100% of the people feeling extreme disability in their families and homes were ever married (those having the status of being married anytime irrespective of the outcome of marriage such as married, divorced, widowed, or separated). There was no association between the highest and lowest quintiles of socio-economic status and age groups with that disability domain as shown in Tables 1.1, 1.2, and 1.3.

Socio-economic impact of mental morbidity

There was no significant association of any mental morbidity or CMDs or SMDs with the cost of treatment as compared to those not having it, as shown in Table 3. However, the approximate median monthly out-of-pocket expense for persons with an SMD was INR 500/- (6.72 $), which was lower than for persons without an SMD (an average monthly expense of INR 1744 (23.33 $); however, the same cannot be generalized because the sub-sample of SMD was smaller. The indirect monthly expenses incurred were higher for CMDs (INR 250) than for SMDs (INR 125); however, this was not statistically significant. The average number of days for missing family, social, or leisure activities because of an illness in the past 1 year was higher in SMDs (17.5 days) as compared to CMDs (6 days) and any mental morbidity (6 days); however, the difference was not statistically significant, as shown in Table 3.

Ordinal logistic regression for disability (unweighted)

An ordinal logistic regression model was constructed for all three domains of perceived disabilities. For each domain of disability, grades of self-reported disability were dependent variables, while age group, gender, marital status, occupation, locality, tobacco use disorder, and CMD were independent variables. The interaction term of CMD and tobacco use disorder was also entered as an independent factor. After adjusting for all these variables, some of the variables were independently associated with grades of disability across all three domains, namely, CMD, tobacco use disorder, and interaction between CMDs and tobacco use disorder. Odds of disability were higher among those with a CMD and lower with tobacco use disorder, while the interaction of both significantly increases the odds of having a disability in all three domains. Those who were not working had higher odds of family/home disability, and those living in the urban metro had higher odds of social life disability. NagelkerkeR2 was 0.256, 0.256, and 0.268, respectively, in work/school, social life, and family/home domains [Table 4].

DISCUSSION

This study is a part of NMHS 2015–16, the first nationwide epidemiological survey with a rigorous methodological design that provided systematic and reliable state-level estimates of mental health morbidity.

The current prevalence of any mental morbidity in MP was higher (13.6%, CI 11.6–15.9) than the national average (10.56%, CI 10.51–10.61)[22] and that of UP (7.97%)[23] but lower compared to that of Punjab (17.94%).[24] The prevalence of tobacco use disorder in MP (34.89%) was much higher than in UP (16.06%)[23] and was the highest among males aged more than 40 years and rural residents.[2123] The self-reported disability had been equally distributed across all quintiles, which was different from the finding of the NMHS report from the state of Punjab[4] but is similar to that of Uttar Pradesh (UP).[23] The findings for the state of MP support the CMDs being a major contributor to disability[25] as compared to the state of UP, where SMDs are a major contributor to disability. The treatment gap for overall mental morbidity was 91% in MP[5] as compared to national figures (84.5%);[22] therefore, coordinated efforts are required to address the higher prevalence of mental morbidity, associated disability, and treatment provision in MP.

The magnitude of disability and mental illness

The contribution of mental disorders to the total DALYs in India has doubled since 1990 and reached 4.7% in 2017, the maximum chunk of which is contributed by the CMDs,[9] which is supported by the findings of this study. The higher proportion of men in the age group of 40–49 years who perceived extreme disability is indicative of major difficulties posed by mental morbidity in their functioning while handling career responsibilities, work–life balance, and transition of children's and parent's life, which is similar to findings of earlier research.[26] This age group is also actively engaged in work and income generation and more prone to depression and burnout, which reflects a higher need of screening CMDs among them.[26] Further, the highest proportion of women perceived no disability in family and home-related aspects, and this could be because women with mental morbidity might have learned to circumvent difficulties while doing activities of their daily living. It is also true that self-reported disability is more common in the married group, which could be explained by an increased sense of responsibility and inability to meet the family and societal expectations, as reflected in this study. Similarly, the employed individuals would find more disability in work domains, while those unemployed would face difficulties predominantly in home and family life. The perceived critical comments (a form of expressed emotion) from other members of the family could have resulted in higher numbers of reported disabilities for unemployed individuals in a family environment. Extreme social disability was perceived 1.91 times more by people residing in urban metro than others, which also supports the idea of generating more mental health services along with urbanization. Also, a lower proportion of disability among those above 60 years of age could be due to their relatively passive engagement in the opportunities of livelihood and earning in Indian culture. However, according to a recent report, improved policies and facilities are required for the elderly population owing to the higher prevalence of CMDs in this population.[27]

Tobacco consumption in the rural community is socially accepted, and in some places, local customs necessitate its consumption. The most affected population for tobacco-use disorder in this report were males of age more than 40 years, while alcohol use disorder was common among males of age between 30 and 49 years and residents of non-metropolitan urban areas in the state. This finding suggests that tobacco use is a rising problem and is supported by earlier studies conducted in urban as well as rural areas of MP.[28] This study found an interesting association between CMDs and disability among tobacco users. The perceived disability was lower among tobacco users than among tobacco non-users. This can be explained by the use of tobacco as a socially acceptable substance to cope with stress and self-medicating with tobacco as well as a possible vulnerability of this sub-group to have suffered from CMDs. Additionally, nicotine is known to enhance the level of alertness to overcome the subsyndromal symptoms of depression and anxiety. The state of MP has a high treatment gap[5] and a higher number of tobacco users; the national mental health policy should be tailor-made according to the existing infrastructure of MP state, where the number of psychiatrists is much less (0.05 per lakh population). Therefore, the recommendations of developing a unique mental health policy for MP focusing on reducing tobacco use, screening for CMDs among tobacco users, and training the existing manpower for identifying, treating, and referring individuals with mental health issues should be scaled up[29] to meet the requirement of at least 1 psychiatrist per lakh population as recommended by a previous report.[30] Tobacco consumption in any form is associated with increased medical morbidities; therefore, this report recommends an urgent need to establish tobacco cessation clinics across the state of MP.

Similarly, suicidality, being associated with mental morbidities, should be addressed effectively with the implementation of existing policies.[31] The significant caregiver burden as reflected in the loss of leisure activities should be adequately assessed in a qualitative study in the future and addressed with activities of IEC and psycho-social interventions for the caregivers with the active participation of the community and NGOs.

Socio-economic impact of disability

Expenditure on mental morbidity was higher in the state of MP as compared to the national average expenditure as well as that from Utter Pradesh, while these findings are similar to that of the state of Punjab.[423] Though these data do not take into account the direct and indirect costs of the treatment, interestingly, the median cost of care for SMDs in the state of MP appeared lower than that in the states of Punjab and UP.[4] This could mean that the subjects with SMDs were not capable of bearing the recommended expenditure for their treatment of mental morbidity as compared to those without any SMDs. This finding also calls for strengthening the infrastructure, awareness about the illness, the importance of the treatment, addressing the stigma, and providing low-cost treatment to the SMD groups at the primary health care and district hospital levels.

Limitations of the study

The cross-sectional nature of the survey has its limitations in terms of the inability to assess the temporal relationship between morbidity and disability. Owing to this limitation, we have reported only associations and do not intend to claim causality. Subjective and objective assessment of disability might differ as SDS has limitations in terms of subjective reporting of the disability over the past 7 days; however, the available evidence is enough for providing effective recovery-oriented mental health services in the state of MP.

CONCLUSIONS

The overall prevalence of moderate to extreme self-reported disability is high and even higher among those with mental health morbidities irrespective of the socio-economic quintiles. This study underscores the complex interplay between tobacco use disorder, disability, and CMDs in the context of the state of MP. The findings reveal that despite lower perceived disability among tobacco users, the interaction between tobacco use disorder and CMDs significantly increases the odds of disability. This suggests that tobacco use might serve as a coping mechanism for individuals with CMDs, while simultaneously exposing them to heightened vulnerability and disability. Higher burden of illness and lost opportunities to work perhaps contribute to lesser expenditure for treatment among those with SMDs. Given the estimates of mental health morbidity and disability, we recommend the state-wide facilitation of tobacco cessation clinics, screening for SMDs earlier in its course, and screening of tobacco users with CMDs in the state of MP.
