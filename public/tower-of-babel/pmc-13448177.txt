Associations of personal temperature exposure in the preceding days with vital signs, biochemical, and hydration parameters in cystic fibrosis
Authors: Pinelopi Anagnostopoulou, Panayiotis Kouis, Katerina Pantavou, Antonis Michanikou, Andreas M. Matthaiou, Kalliopi Ioakim, Barrak Alahmad, Georgios Nikolopoulos, Petros Koutrakis, Panayiotis K. Yiallouros
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The impact of long-term exposure to natural high ambient temperatures on people with cystic fibrosis (pwCF) is understudied. In a Mediterranean setting, characterized by prolonged high temperatures, we performed a pilot study to assess personal ambient temperature exposure in pwCF and healthy controls and its impact on physiological parameters. Utilizing a prospective case-control design, we recruited pwCF and paired family controls and assessed, via wearable sensors, personal temperature exposure and physical activity during three two-week periods: summer, early autumn and late autumn-early winter. After each period, key vital signs (systolic and diastolic blood pressure, heart rate, body temperature), pulmonary function, biochemical parameters (serum electrolytes, glucose, urea, creatinine, serum osmolality), and hydration status were assessed. Personal temperature exposure, daily steps and time spent indoors were similar between groups, particularly during summer. In controls, but less so in pwCF, exposure to higher temperature in the preceding days was linked to significantly lower systolic blood pressure (− 2.42 vs. − 1.53 mmHg) and serum glucose (− 5.01 vs. − 3.06 mg/dl) per degree Celsius. In pwCF, urine osmolality (936.1 vs. 628.4 mOsm/kg) was consistently higher and less responsive (38.93 vs. 85.42 mOsm/kg) per rising degree Celsius, compared to controls. In summary, despite similar personal exposures, PwCF show less profound responses after experiencing periods of heat compared to controls, in terms of lowering SBP and serum glucose and increasing urine concentration capacity, suggesting potential weaker acclimatization.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-55317-y.

Introduction

Continuous exposure to increased environmental temperature causes physiological adaptations of the human body, but these changes may result in adverse effects on human health, especially in vulnerable population groups and people with chronic diseases1. The body tightly maintains a thermoregulatory system during heat exposure. This homeostatic process starts with sweating and an increased blood flow to the skin. Sweat evaporation reduces skin temperature and cools the blood beneath the skin to dissipate heat and prevent overheating. However, when these mechanisms are compromised or overwhelmed, the risk of heat-related health complications rises. Homeostatic disturbances to heat exposure are characterized by the physiological triad of hyperthermia, cardiovascular strain, and dehydration. As climate change continues inducing increasing average daily temperatures which could be more intense in several regions on earth2, there is a pressing need to better understand not only how healthy individuals, but more so people with chronic health conditions, are affected and adapt to prolonged periods of heat conditions3,4. In populations with pre-existing medical conditions (such as cardiovascular, respiratory, diabetes and renal disease), the physiological adaptation mechanisms could be altered or disturbed, thus making them potentially vulnerable to heat exposure. Exploration of heat effects on rare diseases has not been the focus of attention so far, probably due to the low numbers of these patients and the difficulty in obtaining relevant data.

People with Cystic Fibrosis (pwCF), a rare systemic disease, are known to have low tolerance to heat stress5,6. Their vulnerability is related to CF pathophysiology, which is characterized by abnormal chloride and sodium transport. This causes dehydration of secretions and production of hyperviscous mucus, but also causes increased electrolyte loss in sweat. As a result, pwCF living in high-temperature environments may exhibit severe hyponatremic hypochloremic dehydration due to salt loss through the sweat glands7. Furthermore, pwCF seem to underestimate their fluid needs and may undergo excessive dehydration during extended exposure to hot conditions8.

Individuals exposed to repeated bouts of extreme heat normally develop phenotypic and behavioral adaptations to improve the maintenance of homeostasis during subsequent exposures. The process that leads to these adaptations is called ‘heat acclimation’ referring to repeated short periods of heat exposure undertaken in artificial laboratory settings, or ‘heat acclimatization’ referring to longer exposure to seasonal changes in ambient temperature4,9. Several studies have examined the short-term effects of limited exposure of small numbers of pwCF and controls to high temperatures, during and after exercise. PwCF show lower serum sodium and chloride concentrations, but a smaller increase in serum osmolality compared to controls, while changes in renal function and hormonal parameters were similar to controls6,8,10–14. Based on these findings, it is suggested that the decreased hypersmotic trigger attenuates the thirst drive during exercise in CF8. Further, pwCF showed increased sweat loss and lower resting core temperature and heart rate after exercise14. To date, no study has evaluated physiological and biochemical acclimatization of pwCF after longer exposure to natural high ambient temperature conditions. Also, the temperature threshold that initiates adaptation mechanisms to heat in pwCF is not known. Outside controlled lab conditions, accurately estimating temperature exposure is challenging due to imponderable factors, such as time spent indoors, indoor thermal regulation, and physical activity. However, advancement of wearable devices offers a unique opportunity to estimate personal ambient temperature exposure with high temporal resolution in an affordable and user-friendly manner.

In Cyprus, an island in the East-Mediterranean with prolonged periods of extreme high temperatures during summer, we previously studied dehydration as a clinical manifestation among pwCF7,15. In this pilot study, we assessed the impact of personal ambient temperature exposure, measured by wearable devices, during the warm and cooler periods of the year, on adaptation responses in pwCF and healthy controls. We hypothesized that responses to repeatedly increased personal temperature exposure of pwCF may be compromised in comparison to those of healthy individuals, similarly, to published heat acclimation studies.

Results

Study participants

In total, 36 participants (18 pwCF; 18 controls) were enrolled in the study during the summers of 2020 and 2021. People with CF were younger (19.8 vs. 44.2 years, p value < 0.001), and, as expected, had lower BMI (− 0.15 vs. 0.79 z score, p value = 0.012), FEV1 (− 2.2 vs. − 0.2 z-score, p value = 0.002) and FVC (− 1.5 vs. − 0.3 z-score, p value = 0.022) compared to controls (Table 1). Blood pressure measurements were similar between the two groups, but heart rate was significantly higher in pwCF (83.6 vs. 70.6 beats/min, p value = 0.015). Serum electrolytes (sodium, chloride, potassium), serum urea and creatinine, as well as serum osmolality were similar between the two groups and within normal range. However, urine osmolality in pwCF was significantly higher in comparison to controls (936.1 vs. 628.4 mOsm/Kg, p value = 0.037).

Heat exposure and physical activity

Within pwCF, as well as within the control group, personal ambient temperature exposure, as assessed by wearable sensors, was similar between summer and early autumn but significantly lower in the late autumn–early winter period (Fig. 1A). Mean daily steps and mean daily time spent indoors were similar across the three periods within pwCF (Fig. 1B, C). Within the control group daily steps during summer were higher compared to early autumn (mainly) and late autumn–early winter period (Fig. 1B). PwCF demonstrated similar personal ambient temperature exposure when directly compared to the control group across several exposure metrics including mean daily temperature (summer: 29.0 95% CI 28.2, 29.8 vs. 29.50, 95% 28.9, 30.1, p = 0.298; early autumn: 29.5, 95% CI 28.5, 30.6 vs. 30.0, 95% CI 29.2, 30.8, p = 0.445; late autumn-early winter: 25.6, 95% CI 24.5, 26.7 vs. 25.9, 95% CI 25.0, 26.9, p = 0.613) and mean daily 75th percentile temperature. Also, there was no significant difference between the two groups in mean daily activity levels (daily steps in summer: 7263, 95% CI 5618, 8908 vs. 6117, 95% CI 4189, 8045, p = 0.342; early autumn: 5478, 95% CI 3448, 7507 vs. 6136, 95% CI 3916, 8355, p = 0.64; late autumn-early winter: 5388, 95% CI 3303, 7473 vs. 5181, 95% CI 3414, 6948, p = 0.869). In terms of time spent indoors, mean daily estimates between the two groups were also similar (summer: 1080, 95% CI 974, 1187 vs. 888, 95% CI 660, 1114, p = 0.097; early autumn: 1142, 95% CI 971, 1312 vs. 1012, 95% CI 792, 1232, p = 0.309; late autumn-early winter: 1137, 95% CI 978, 1296 vs. 975, 95% CI 779, 1170, p = 0.189) across the three periods, as assessed by wearable sensors (Fig. 1, Table S1). Regarding exposure and activity patterns during the warmest periods of the day (> 75th percentile temperature), PwCF spent more time at home (and also performed less steps), compared to controls (Table S1). However, for both comparisons, the differences were not statistically significant (Table 2).

Using a mixed effects model with fixed effect for group and random intercepts for each participant and after adjusting for age, gender BMI, and seasonality, we found that the difference in mean ambient temperature exposure in pwCF in comparison to controls over the three periods was not significant (− 0.07 °C, p value = 0.858). Similarly, the adjusted difference in daily physical activity and time spent indoors of pwCF in comparison to controls were characterized by a non-significant reduction of − 1249 steps in their daily routine (p value = 0.331) and by a non-significant increase of 13.3 min in daily time spent indoors (p value = 0.901) (Table 2).

Effect of temperature over the previous days on vital signs and biochemical parameters

Mean daily temperature rise on the day before clinical visits (L0-1) was associated with significantly lower SBP in healthy controls by − 2.42 mmHg (95% CI − 4.54, − 0.30) per degree 0C, and in pwCF by − 1.53 mmHg, (95% CI − 3.38, 0.32), however the difference between the two groups did not reach statistical significance (p value = 0.132). Temperature rise during L0-3 and L0-15 intervals before clinic visits had no effects on SBP either in the control group or in the pwCF. Similarly, temperature rise during L0-1, L0-3 and L0-15 intervals before clinic visits had no effect on DBP and heart rate in both groups (Table 3).

Serum urea, creatinine, sodium, chloride and potassium did not change significantly with temperature rise during L0-1, L0-3 and L0-15 intervals before clinic visits, either in healthy controls or in pwCF (Table 3).

Serum osmolality was not affected by temperature rise during L0-1, L0-3 and L0-15 intervals either in healthy controls or in pwCF. However, urine osmolality in healthy controls seemed to increase significantly by 85.42 mOsm/kg (95% CI 20.02, 150.81) per degree 0C rise, during the day before clinical visits (L0-1). In pwCF, the increase in urine osmolality in response to temperature rise during L0-1 before clinical visits was moderate and not significant (38.93 mOsm/kg, 95% CI − 27.72, 105.59). The difference in the rise of urine osmolality in response to temperature between the two groups was − 46.48 mOsm/kg (95% CI − 89.64, − 3.33), and it was statistically significant (p value = 0.035). The mean daily temperature rise during L0-3 and L0-15 intervals had no effect on urine osmolality either in healthy controls or in pwCF (Table 3). To minimize potential confounding effects of heart rate differences between the two groups, a sensitivity analysis controlling for the heart rate indicated similar results in the above biochemical parameters (Table S2). Also, in an attempt to identify correlations between exposure to the higher daily temperature and effect on vital signs and biochemical parameters, we performed a secondary analysis using as exposure metric the percentage of time spent above the 75th percentile of the personal temperature, which did not identify any differences between the groups (Table S3).

Discussion

In this pilot study, for the first time we objectively measured personal ambient temperature exposure among pwCF and healthy counterparts across different seasonal periods using wearable sensors. In a Mediterranean climatic setting, we demonstrated that pwCF in comparison to healthy controls had almost similar personal exposure to ambient temperature, daily step counts, and time spent outdoors across summer, early autumn, and late autumn–early winter. As expected, both pwCF and healthy controls had lower mean daily personal temperature exposure by approximately 4 °C during late autumn–early winter in comparison to early autumn and summer. Quite interestingly, personal temperature exposure of participants during summer was not higher than the one in early autumn. Adaptation behaviors in both study groups likely explain these similarities, particularly during summer. Previous data showed that the penetration of air-conditioning in the Cyprus residential sector to be as high as 97%, while the island is already characterized by the highest cooling demand among the EU 27 member-states during summer16,17.

However, we found some distinct physiological differences between pwCF and healthy controls. Higher temperature exposure on the day before clinical visits was linked to significantly lower SBP in healthy controls but less so in pwCF. General population studies have shown that increasing seasonal ambient temperature is associated with decreases in the mean arterial blood pressure18,19. This is considered an acclimatization response to heat exposure through changes in endothelium-driven dilatation and arterial stiffness20, as well as changes in skin sympathetic nerve activity21. Also, comparative studies in humans and rodents show that the cutaneous vasodilation for heat dissipation in humans, if not compensated by vasoconstriction in splanchnic vascular beds, results in a net reduction in peripheral vascular resistance4. Furthermore, several experimental studies have demonstrated that heat acclimation, beyond increasing sweating and skin blood flow, also increases thermo-effector output and lowers cardiovascular strain by reducing heart rate4. In our study, the rise in temperature exposure during the preceding day (L0-1) was associated in both groups with a small decrease in heart rate, which did not reach statistical significance. This could be attributed to the fact that by design our study assessed vital signs at clinical visits as a mid-term effect of heat exposure in the previous days, as well as to the relatively small number of participants.

Furthermore, higher personal temperature exposure during L0-1 and L0-3 intervals before clinical visits was associated with lower serum glucose in healthy controls but not so in pwCF. Heat exposure has been proposed to influence the responsiveness of target tissues to insulin, although the evidence is conflicting. One possible mechanism explaining these findings is the increase of insulin sensitivity leading to utilization of glucose as an energy substrate, which may help mammals adapt to heat exposure, as the conversion of glucose to ATP is associated with less metabolic heat generation compared with fatty acid oxidation22.

Serum osmolality was similar between pwCF and healthy controls and did not seem to be affected by temperature rise. However, urine osmolality in pwCF was within the normal range but consistently overall higher than in controls, possibly as a renal response to mild chronic dehydration, higher dietary salt consumption and reduced fluid intake, which resulted in higher baseline urine concentration. Quite interestingly, urine osmolality in pwCF was less responsive to rising personal temperature exposure compared to controls. We assume that pwCF showed limited urine concentration capacity in extreme heat, as their urine was already concentrated at baseline. Several previous experimental studies have examined fluid intake, plasma and urine osmolality in pwCF and healthy controls after short exposure to high temperature and exercise. In the study of Bar-Or et al., after exercising in high temperature, pwCF drank much less than the controls did and lost twice as much fluid, while in the study of Brown et al., after similar exercising in high temperature, serum osmolality increased less and ad libitum fluid replacement was 40% less in pwCF compared to controls6,8. In healthy adults, heat acclimation has been found to improve urine concentrating ability following passive heat stress23, implying that the reduced ability in pwCF to further concentrate urine with rising temperature is indicating a compromise in their acclimatization ability.

In our study, serum urea, creatinine, sodium, chloride and potassium did not change significantly with temperature rise, either in healthy controls or in pwCF. A few previous studies, which have examined the short-term effects of limited exposure to high temperature and exercise in pwCF and controls, demonstrated a slight decrease in serum sodium and chloride concentrations in pwCF patients, but not in controls8,10,11. However, in our study, in the context of prolonged natural seasonal exposure to high temperature without preceding intense exercise, stable pwCF seem to sustain normal electrolytes that are not different to those in controls.

Our study benefitted from the use of wearable sensors that provided objective monitoring of personal ambient temperature exposure, activity levels and time spent outdoors of pwCF and healthy controls for long intervals of time in the warm and cooler seasonal periods of the year in a mediterranean setting highly affected by global warming. The study sample was representative of the CF population included in our national registry15, as we included both children and adults, both sexes, and patients of various genotypes independent of the disease severity, residing in any location across the Republic of Cyprus, including urban and rural areas. The prospective case-control design of the study enabled the assessment across different seasons of the impact of personal temperature exposure on vital signs, biochemical and hydration status of pwCF in relation to healthy controls.

The study has also several limitations. It was designed as an exploratory investigation, aiming to generate initial data in an understudied topic in cystic fibrosis research. To the best of our knowledge, there are no prior studies reporting physiological changes after heat acclimatization over long periods of exposure in patients with CF, which could be used for a power calculation. Therefore, the sample size was primarily decided on feasibility and was relatively small. Also, by choosing family members who share the same housing conditions with patients as controls for our study, we could not exclude the effect of other parameters like lifestyle differences, dietary specificities, personal traits and minor health issues on physiological responses to heat. Further, the study controls were older than pwCF, so that some activity patterns, and vital signs’ absolute values may be influenced by participants’ age. Nevertheless, in a small country like Cyprus and for a rare disease like CF, it is difficult to recruit high numbers of cases and healthy counterparts of the same age. In our analysis, we tried to overcome these weaknesses and focused not on comparisons of absolute values between cases and controls but rather on changes in the exposure and health parameters of interest in relation to rising temperature and made comparisons of the pattern of the changes between healthy controls and pwCF. Our wearable sensors captured personal temperature exposure, but not other factors (humidity, wind and solar radiation) that could provide a holistic thermal stress indicator24. Finally, we did not assess hormonal profiles that are implicated either in hydration status, such as renin and aldosterone, or in metabolic pathways, such as insulin. Although data on hormonal profiles may have provided better insights on the underlying mechanisms of the responses to heat between cases and controls, this does not affect the validity of our findings on the impact of heat in the two groups. Future studies in the field may use more advanced wearable technology to measure more personal exposure indices and provide a better insight into the combined effects of heat, anthropometrics and activity during the monitoring period, using thermo-physiological models. These studies could also focus further on patient-reported outcomes and effects of heat on the quality of life of patients.

In conclusion, in a Mediterranean setting affected by high temperatures, we demonstrated that the personal exposure of pwCF to temperature, as well as their activity levels, are similar when compared to controls across summer, early autumn, and late autumn–early winter, probably because of adaptation behaviors, undertaken particularly during summer. Higher personal temperature exposure was associated with significantly lower SBP and serum glucose in healthy controls but not in pwCF, indicating possible weaker acclimatization responses in pwCF. Urine osmolality in pwCF is much higher than in healthy controls and seems to increase significantly less with temperature rise, implying reduced ability of pwCF to further concentrate urine with rising temperature. The findings of this pilot study indicate the need for further research in larger cohorts to delineate the physiological adaptation responses of pwCF to prolonged exposure to temperature across seasons, in the context of climate change and continuing global warming.

Methods

Type of study and participants

This is a pilot prospective case-control observational study in the context of the BeAT Heat project (Biometeorological Aspect of Thermal Environment and Health)25. It included pwCF of all ages participating in the Cyprus CF registry and paired control individuals from the patient’s family environment, who likely share similar exposure conditions and lifestyle. Information on exclusion criteria is provided in the Online Supplement. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Cyprus National Bioethics Committee (EEBE/EΠ2018/48). All participants or caregivers (for participants < 18 years) gave written informed consent.

Study protocol and duration

Environmental exposure and clinical assessments of each patient/control pair were performed during three two-week monitoring periods, each of which was followed by a clinic visit: (1) during summer (July), (2) during early autumn (September/October), and (3) during late autumn-early winter (November/December). The study was performed during 2020 and 2021 (first participants’ recruitment 25.05.2020) and monitoring of participants’ activity and ambient temperature exposure in the community was performed continuously via wearable sensors for 2 weeks prior to each clinical visit.

Heat exposure: physical activity

The participants’ physical activity was tracked using the LEMFO 25 smartwatch which was equipped with the EMBRACE™ software (Embrace Tech LTD, Cyprus) and pedometer and global positioning system (GPS) sensors26,27. Time spent indoors was defined as the duration of time with GPS signal inside a 100 m radius around the participant’s residence and monitoring of personal ambient temperature exposure was performed via an ambient temperature sensor (iButton) that was attached to the smartwatch’s plastic strip. Additional details on exposure assessments are available in Supplementary File 1.

Clinical assessments

Clinical assessments were carried out at the Pediatric Pulmonology Unit at “Archbishop Makarios III” hospital and included clinical data collection (symptoms, recent medication use, smoking status), clinical examination, anthropometrics and laboratory investigations. Vital signs’ assessment included body temperature using a forehead digital infrared thermometer (Paul Hartmann AG, Heidenheim, Germany), systolic and diastolic arterial blood pressure (SBP, DBP respectively) (Tensoval duo control, Paul Hartmann AG, Heidenheim, Germany), and electrocardiography-based heart rate using a digital 12-lead electrocardiogram (MAC 600 ECG Analysis System, GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA). Spirometry was performed according to current ATS/ERS standards28 and forced vital capacity (FVC), and forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1) are presented in z-scores29. Additional details on clinical assessments are available in Supplementary File 1.

During each clinic visit, participants provided urine and peripheral blood samples for the laboratory assessment of their biochemistry and hydration status. Serum electrolytes (sodium, potassium, chloride) were measured by potentiometry, serum glucose, urea and creatinine were measured by bichromatic analysis, and serum osmolality was calculated accordingly30. Urine osmolality was measured by osmometry.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were summarized as means (95% Confidence Intervals, 95% CI) for continuous variables and counts (percentages) for categorical variables, while t-tests and chi-squared tests were used for baseline comparisons between the two groups.

Heat exposure (personal ambient temperature) and physical activity (daily steps, percentage of time spent indoors) metrics during the two-week monitoring periods were summarized separately for each period using two-week daily mean values (95% CI). In an unadjusted analysis, comparisons between pwCF and controls were carried out using the independent sample t-test. Additional comparisons focusing on temperature exposure outcomes during the warmest hours of the day were also carried out. These were mean daily 75th percentile personal temperature, percentage of time participants spent at home during periods when personal temperatures are > 75th percentile, as well as percentage of steps performed during periods when personal temperatures are > 75th percentile.

Comparisons between pwCF and healthy controls in respect to heat exposure and physical activity parameters were further explored in a linear mixed effect model, which included a fixed effect term for group (pwCF Vs healthy controls) and a random intercept for each participant. The model was adjusted for age, gender and BMI z-score, while sine and cosine functions were included to control monthly variability (seasonality) in our data. Linear mixed effect models were selected because they account for the correlated nature of repeated measurements within participants across the three seasonal periods, while also providing flexibility in handling unbalanced data and occasional missing values in longitudinal studies31.

The same approach was followed for the assessment of the association between personal heat exposure and parameters assessed during the clinical visit, both clinical (body temperature, systolic and diastolic blood pressure, heart rate, FEV1 and FVC) and biochemical (serum glucose, urea, creatinine, sodium, potassium, chloride, serum osmolality and urine osmolality). Heat exposure was calculated as the overall mean daily personal ambient temperature over each of the three different exposure periods, which included the full two-week observation interval preceding each clinic visit (L0-15), the shorter interval representing the three days before the visit (L0-3), and the interval representing the single day before the visit (L0-1). Post-hoc secondary analysis was also carried out using the mean percentage of time spent above the > 75 percentile personal temperature to examine whether an exposure metric that may better capture intensity of exposure may affect the examined associations. These models were defined by fixed effects for the heat exposure and the group category and included an interaction term between heat exposure and group to model the difference in the association between heat exposure and outcome across the two groups. A random intercept for each participant was also defined to account for the repeated nature of observations within each subject and the models were adjusted for age, gender, BMI, mean daily steps, mean time spent at home, smoking and seasonality.

At the analysis stage, missing data were handled using pairwise deletion on an analysis-by-analysis basis and mixed effects models were developed using the mixed command in STATA (Version 18, StataCorp, College Station, TX). Graphs and diagrams were generated using GraphPad Prism 9.2.0 (GraphPad software, LLC). Statistical significance was set at p-value < 0.05.
