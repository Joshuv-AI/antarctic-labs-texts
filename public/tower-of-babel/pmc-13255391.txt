Should standard concurrent chemoradiotherapy remain the preferred treatment for elderly patients with locoregionally advanced nasopharyngeal carcinoma?
Authors: Shuhan Zhao, Jieying Zhang, Yun Zhu, Gang Peng, You Qin, Bian Wu, Qian Ding, Liangliang Shi, Zhanjie Zhang, Xiaohua Hong, Jun Han, Gang Liu, Shaojun Lin, Kunyu Yang, Qiaojuan Guo, Jing Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundElderly patients are underrepresented in clinical studies supporting platinum-based concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT) as the standard therapy (ST) for locoregionally advanced nasopharyngeal carcinoma (LA-NPC). Many such patients in real-world settings cannot tolerate CCRT and instead receive individualized therapy (IT). This study aimed to compare the efficacy and safety of ST versus IT in elderly NPC patients.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 393 elderly (≥ 65 years) LA-NPC patients from two centers in China between January 2013 and December 2020. Patients were categorized into ST (platinum-based CCRT with or without induction/adjuvant therapy) or IT (radiotherapy alone, or concurrent radiotherapy with non-chemotherapy agents, with or without induction/adjuvant therapy) groups. Propensity score matching (PSM) generated 141 patient pairs. Survival outcomes were analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method and Cox proportional hazards models. Toxicities were compared with chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests.ResultsIn the PSM cohort, no significant differences were observed between the ST and IT groups in overall survival, cancer-specific survival, progression-free survival, locoregional relapse-free survival, and distant metastasis-free survival (all P > 0.05). Multivariable analysis confirmed that treatment group (ST vs. IT) was not a significant predictor for any survival endpoint. In exploratory stratified analysis, radiotherapy alone was associated with inferior OS, CSS, and PFS, whereas radiotherapy combined with systemic therapy showed survival outcomes similar to those of ST. Additionally, compared with the ST group, the IT group had significantly lower rates of grade 3 or higher hematologic toxicities, including leukopenia (8.5% vs. 23.4%; P = 0.001) and neutropenia (12.3% vs. 20.6%; P = 0.013).ConclusionStandard CCRT was not associated with improved survival outcomes compared with individualized therapy in elderly patients with LA-NPC, while individualized therapy showed a more favorable safety profile. Introduction

Nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) exhibits a distinct geographical distribution, with more than 70% of cases occurring in southern China, Southeast Asia, and North Africa [1, 2]. Radiotherapy (RT) has long been the cornerstone treatment for non-metastatic NPC due to the tumor’s high radiosensitivity and complex anatomical location [3]. However, RT alone yields a 3-year overall survival (OS) rate of only 47% in locoregionally advanced NPC (LA-NPC) [4]. After decades of research, platinum-based concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT), with or without induction/adjuvant therapy, has become the standard therapy (ST) for patients with LA-NPC, improving 3-year OS rate to 94% and earning a recommendation in NCCN guidelines [1, 3].

Elderly patients (aged ≥ 65 years) are frequently excluded from pivotal clinical trials in NPC, which predominantly enroll individuals aged 18–65 [5–9]. This underrepresentation limits the evidence base for applying ST to older populations, who often present with higher comorbidity burdens, diminished organ function, and reduced treatment tolerance [10]. Meta-analyses indicate that the survival benefit of CCRT may decrease with advancing age and could be absent in patients over 70 years old [5, 11]. Whether CCRT offers a significant survival advantage over RT alone in elderly NPC patients remains controversial. Accumulating evidence suggests that CCRT is associated with higher rates of treatment-related toxicities and may adversely affect quality of life compared to RT alone, raising concerns about its suitability as a first-line treatment strategy in this demographic [12–18]. Moreover, the proportion of elderly NPC patients is rising due to global demographic aging, and advanced age has been consistently identified as an independent risk factor for inferior survival outcomes [19–21]. Given the distinctive challenges in this population—such as declined physiological reserve, poorer performance status, higher prevalence of malnutrition, increased multimorbidity, and reduced tolerance and compliance to intensive treatment [10], applying uniform treatment guidelines established for younger adults is not appropriate. Consequently, there is a pressing need to develop tailored and diversified therapeutic strategies for elderly NPC patients.

In real-world settings, treatment approaches for elderly NPC patients are highly heterogeneous. Clinicians often opt for individualized therapy (IT) regimens—such as anti-EGFR antibodies or omitting concurrent chemotherapy—based on tolerability, efficacy, and quality-of-life considerations [12, 13, 22–25]. However, no studies have directly compared IT with ST in this population. This real-world study aims to compare the efficacy and safety of ST and IT in elderly LA-NPC patients using propensity score matching (PSM) to minimize confounding factors.

Materials and methods

Patients

A retrospective analysis was conducted on elderly patients diagnosed with NPC at Union Hospital, Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology, and Fujian Provincial Cancer Hospital between January 2013 and December 2020. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) newly diagnosed NPC confirmed by pathological biopsy; (2) age ≥ 65 years; and (3) stage III-IVA according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) 8th edition classification. Patients were excluded if they had refused anti-cancer treatment, had not received treatment at our centers, had other types of tumors, or had significant data loss. The patient inclusion flowchart is presented in Fig. 1. Ultimately, 393 NPC patients were included in this study. The histological types were determined by at least two pathologists according to the World Health Organization (WHO) classification scheme as keratinizing squamous cell carcinoma (KSCC, formerly WHO type I), differentiated non-keratinizing carcinoma (NK-D, formerly WHO type II), and undifferentiated non-keratinizing carcinoma (NK-U, formerly WHO type III). This study was approved by the institutional ethics committee.

Flow chart. NPC: Nasopharyngeal carcinoma; PSM: propensity score matching

Treatment and assessments

All enrolled patients were categorized into the ST group (n = 170) or the IT group (n = 223) based on their treatment regimens. The ST group consisted of patients who received platinum-based CCRT with or without induction/adjuvant therapy. The IT group consisted of patients deemed unsuitable for CCRT who received individualized treatments, classified into two subgroups: (1) RT alone (IT1 group, n = 52), and (2) RT combined with systemic therapies (IT2 group, n = 171), including concurrent non-chemotherapy agents, induction therapy, and/or adjuvant therapy. All treatment decisions were made following discussions involving at least two senior oncologists.

All patients who underwent RT were treated with intensity-modulated radiation therapy (IMRT). In brief, the IMRT protocol consisted of 30–33 fractions administered once daily, five times a week, over a period of 6–7 weeks. The prescribed doses for the primary tumor and cervical lymph nodes ranged from 66 to 76 Gy and from 66 to 72 Gy, respectively. Comorbid conditions were assessed using the age-adjusted Charlson comorbidity index (aCCI), excluding the primary tumor from the calculation. The completion rate of concurrent therapy was defined as completion of the planned concurrent systemic treatment administered during radiotherapy. Adverse events were graded and recorded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0.

Follow-up

Patients were followed every 3 months for 2 years, every 6 months for the next 3 years, and annually thereafter. Assessments included nasopharynx and neck magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), nasopharyngoscopy, chest computed tomography (CT), abdominal ultrasound, and plasma Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) DNA quantification. Survival endpoints included OS, cancer-specific survival (CSS), progression-free survival (PFS), locoregional relapse-free survival (LRRFS), and distant metastasis-free survival (DMFS). CSS was defined as the time from the start of treatment to death caused by NPC.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 26.0 was used for analysis. A 1:1 PSM matched patients based on age, sex, clinical stage, and aCCI. Baseline balance between groups was assessed using the standardized mean difference (SMD), with an absolute SMD < 0.10 considered indicative of adequate covariate balance. Survival curves were generated with the Kaplan–Meier method and compared with log-rank tests. The Cox model was used for univariable and multivariable analysis. Toxicities were compared with chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. A two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

A total of 393 elderly patients (median age, 68 years; male-to-female ratio, 3.74:1) with LA-NPC were included. After PSM, 141 matched pairs were created (Table 1). Covariate balance improved after matching, with most baseline variables well balanced.

Treatment details

In the overall cohort, 170 patients (43.3%) received ST and 223 (56.7%) received IT. Among IT patients, 52 (23.3%) received RT alone (IT1) and 171 (76.7%) received RT combined with systemic therapy (IT2). Induction chemotherapy was administered to 310 patients (78.9%), while only 41 (10.4%) received adjuvant therapy. The CCRT completion rate was 52.4% (89/170), lowest for cisplatin (33.3%). In contrast, patients receiving concurrent non-chemotherapy agents (e.g., anti-EGFR antibodies, endostar, or PD-1 inhibitors) had a completion rate of 86.6% (71/82; P < 0.001 vs. CCRT). Similar trends were observed in the PSM cohort (Table S1).

Survival outcomes

After a median follow-up of 56.3 months (95% CI, 51.6–61.0), median OS was not reached, and median PFS was 101.3 months (95% CI, 85.7–117.9). The 5-year survival rates for the entire cohort were: OS 69.4%, CSS 75.0%, PFS 62.9%, LRRFS 88.3%, and DMFS 85.2%.

In the PSM cohort, no significant differences were found between ST and IT groups in 5-year OS (77.1% vs. 72.7%; P = 0.802), CSS (80.1% vs. 76.9%; P = 0.844), PFS (70.5% vs. 64.5%; P = 0.660), LRRFS (90.1% vs. 89.9%; P = 0.651), or DMFS (86.0% vs. 82.7%; P = 0.687) (Fig. 2).

Multivariable analysis

In the PSM cohort, treatment group (ST vs. IT) was not an independent predictor for any survival outcome (all P > 0.05). Age ≥ 67 was an adverse prognostic factor for CSS (HR = 1.937; 95% CI 1.008–3.724; P = 0.047), while Stage IVA disease was associated with worse OS (HR = 1.662; 95% CI 1.005–2.747; P = 0.048) and PFS (HR = 1.566; 95% CI 1.019–2.406; P = 0.041). Induction chemotherapy significantly improved OS (HR = 0.455; 95% CI 0.241–0.857; P = 0.015), PFS (HR = 0.449; 95% CI 0.259–0.778; P = 0.004), and DMFS (HR = 0.354; 95% CI 0.167–0.752; P = 0.007) (Table 2).

Stratified analysis of IT group

In the overall cohort, patients were further stratified into ST, IT1 (RT alone), and IT2 (RT plus systemic therapy) groups for exploratory subgroup analysis. The IT1 subgroup (RT alone) had significantly inferior 5-year OS (41.6%), CSS (53.6%), and PFS (36.0%) compared to both ST (OS: 79.8%, CSS: 82.3%, PFS: 72.6%) and IT2 (OS: 67.3%, CSS: 73.6%, PFS: 61.3%) (all P < 0.05). No significant differences were observed in LRRFS or DMFS among the three groups (Figure S1).

Toxicity

In the PSM cohort, the ST group had significantly higher rates of all-grade hematologic toxicities (e.g., leukopenia: 87.9% vs. 61.0%; P < 0.001), as well as nausea/vomiting (88.7% vs. 53.2%; P < 0.001). Grade ≥ 3 leukopenia (23.4% vs. 8.5%; P = 0.001) and neutropenia (20.6% vs. 12.3%; P = 0.013) were also more common in the ST group. No significant differences were observed in hepatotoxicity, nephrotoxicity, skin reactions, or mucositis (Table 3).

Discussion

In this two-center retrospective study of 393 elderly NPC patients, IT regimens achieved survival outcomes comparable to ST after PSM, while exhibiting significantly lower toxicity and higher treatment completion rates. To our knowledge, this is the first real-world study comparing ST and IT in patients aged ≥ 65 with LA-NPC.

With the aging of the global population, the incidence of NPC among older adults has risen significantly. A demographic analysis of 3,880 NPC patients indicated that individuals over 60 years of age accounted for 35.8% of cases [20]. Owing to a higher prevalence of comorbidities, poorer performance status, and reduced treatment tolerance, elderly patients are frequently underrepresented or excluded from pivotal clinical trials, thereby limiting the evidence base for applying current standard treatments to this subgroup [6, 8, 9]. Consistent with previous reports, our results show that NPC patients aged ≥ 65 years achieved similar survival outcomes with IT (non-CCRT regimens) compared to those receiving CCRT. Retrospective studies by Lyu et al. and Mi et al. involving patients over 70 and 65 years of age, respectively, also found comparable survival between the CCRT and radiotherapy (RT) alone groups, albeit with increased treatment-related toxicities in the CCRT groups [12, 13].

The lack of a significant advantage for ST in this population may be explained by several factors: higher non-cancer mortality, greater comorbidity burden, and reduced treatment tolerance leading to low CCRT completion rates. Cisplatin, the preferred concurrent platinum drug, requires a cumulative dose exceeding 200 mg/m² to achieve satisfactory survival outcomes in NPC patients [26, 27]. Previous studies have reported completion rates for this dose of cisplatin-based CCRT increasing from 73% in the conventional RT era to over 90% in the IMRT era among non-elderly patients [6, 28]. However, we observed a considerably lower completion rate of 33.3% for concurrent RT with cisplatin in this elderly cohort. This can likely be attributed to the reduced tolerance of elderly patients to severe gastrointestinal adverse effects, nephrotoxicity, and ototoxicity associated with cisplatin. Alternative agents such as carboplatin, nedaplatin, oxaliplatin, and lobaplatin have demonstrated viable efficacy and improved compliance due to more favorable toxicity profiles for cisplatin-intolerant patients [29–33]. Nonetheless, suboptimal completion rates were also noted for nedaplatin (59.8%), carboplatin (42.9%), and lobaplatin (40.0%) in our study, underscoring the necessity for less intensive treatment regimens tailored to the specific needs of elderly patients. In the current era of targeted therapy and immunotherapy, agents such as nimotuzumab, cetuximab, and endostar provide additional individualized treatment options for patients unsuitable for chemotherapy, offering comparable efficacy with fewer adverse events [23, 34–38]. We observed higher completion rates for concurrent RT with nimotuzumab, cetuximab, and endostar, indicating that these agents may represent viable alternatives for elderly patients.

It should be noted that the IT group was heterogeneous, consisting of patients receiving RT alone (IT1) and those receiving RT combined with systemic therapy (IT2), including induction/adjuvant therapy or concurrent non-chemotherapy agents. The incorporation of systemic therapy may have improved overall outcomes within the IT group, potentially attenuating differences compared to ST. In a stratified analysis, the IT1 subgroup exhibited significantly worse OS, CSS, and PFS than both the ST and IT2 groups, while survival outcomes were comparable between the ST and IT2 groups. This discrepancy should be interpreted cautiously. As this was an exploratory subgroup analysis with a limited sample size, the number of locoregional and distant failure events may have been insufficient to detect differences in the narrower endpoints of LRRFS and DMFS, whereas PFS is a more sensitive composite endpoint. In addition, IT1 patients were substantially older and had more comorbidities than those in the ST and IT2 groups, with patients aged ≥ 80 years accounting for 19.2% (10/52) in the IT1 group, compared with 0% (0/170) in the ST group and 1.8% (3/171) in the IT2 group. These baseline disadvantages may have contributed to worse OS through competing non-cancer mortality, while poorer tolerance for salvage treatment after recurrence or metastasis may have contributed to inferior CSS [39, 40]. Notably, despite similar baseline disadvantages, IT2 patients achieved outcomes similar to the ST group, suggesting that IT2 may provide a more favorable balance between efficacy and tolerability in elderly patients. However, due to the limited sample size, baseline characteristics could not be fully balanced across all three groups. Further prospective studies with larger cohorts are warranted to validate these findings.

Beyond the reasons discussed above, CCRT unequivocally induces higher toxicity [12, 13]. Our data also revealed that the ST group experienced more toxicities, particularly nausea/vomiting and hematologic toxicities such as anemia, leukopenia, neutropenia, and thrombocytopenia. No significant differences were observed between the two groups, and both exhibited a high incidence of skin reactions and mucositis. The increased frequency of adverse effects in elderly patients is closely associated with their higher comorbidity burden. On one hand, elderly patients with multiple comorbidities tend to receive lower doses of chemotherapy and exhibit poorer tolerance, potentially compromising treatment efficacy and negatively impacting survival outcomes [39, 40]. On the other hand, patients over 70 years of age have reduced tolerance for high-dose radiotherapy and a greater incidence of radiation-related toxicities, particularly those with significant comorbid conditions. Consequently, we propose that IT represents an effective and safer alternative to ST for elderly NPC patients [10].

Limitations

This study has limitations inherent to retrospective designs, including potential selection bias and missing data (e.g., EBV DNA, late toxicities, performance status, and nutritional status). Although PSM improved baseline comparability, slight residual imbalance remained for clinical stage. Furthermore, the heterogeneity of IT regimens precludes definitive conclusions about specific protocols. Ongoing prospective trials (e.g., NCT03915132) will help validate these findings.

Conclusion

The completion rate of standard CCRT is suboptimal in elderly patients with LA-NPC. Standard CCRT did not provide a significant survival benefit over individualized therapy, whereas individualized therapy was better tolerated and may serve as an alternative treatment option for this population. Exploratory stratified analysis further suggested that radiotherapy combined with systemic therapy may represent a reasonable individualized approach.
