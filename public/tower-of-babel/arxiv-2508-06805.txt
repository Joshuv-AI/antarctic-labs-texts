Edge Detection for Organ Boundaries via Top–Down Refinement and Sub‑Pixel Upsampling
Aarav Mehta, Priya Deshmukh, Vikram Singh, Siddharth Malhotra, Krishnan Menon Iyer, Tanvi Iyer

Abstract
Accurate localization of organ boundaries is critical in medical imaging for segmentation, registration, surgical planning, and radiotherapy. While deep convolutional networks (ConvNets) have advanced general-purpose edge detection to near-human performance on natural images, their outputs often lack precise localization, a limitation that is particularly harmful in medical applications where millimeter-level accuracy is required. Building on a systematic analysis of ConvNet edge outputs, we propose a medically focused crisp edge detector that adapts a novel top-down backward refinement architecture to medical images (2D and volumetric). Our method progressively upsamples and fuses high-level semantic features with fine-grained low-level cues through a backward refinement pathway, producing high-resolution, well-localized organ boundaries. We further extend the design to handle anisotropic volumes by combining 2D slice-wise refinement with light 3D context aggregation to retain computational efficiency. Evaluations on several CT and MRI organ datasets demonstrate substantially improved boundary localization under strict criteria (boundary F-measure, Hausdorff distance) compared to baseline ConvNet detectors and contemporary medical edge/contour methods. Importantly, integrating our crisp edge maps into downstream pipelines yields consistent gains in organ segmentation (higher Dice scores, lower boundary errors), more accurate image registration, and improved delineation of lesions near organ interfaces. The proposed approach produces clinically valuable, crisp organ edges that materially enhance common medical-imaging tasks.

Introduction

Accurate organ-edge delineation is a fundamental requirement for many medical-imaging workflows. High-quality, millimeter-precise boundary maps underpin image segmentation, multimodal registration, surgical planning, radiotherapy target definition, and any downstream analysis that depends on spatially precise anatomy. Traditional low-level edge detectors and early learning-based approaches struggle to provide the spatial fidelity these applications demand: responses are often thick, spatially imprecise, or sensitive to imaging artifacts. Even state-of-the-art convolutional networks for edge detection, which have achieved near-human performance on natural-image benchmarks, tend to produce blurred, loosely localized edges when applied to medical images because standard encoder–decoder designs progressively lose and then coarsely recover spatial detail.

We identify two central architectural causes for this localization gap. First, repeated pooling and large-stride convolutions in deep encoders collapse spatial resolution: although later layers capture strong semantic cues that identify organ boundaries, the reduced feature map size makes it difficult to recover fine-grained localization during upsampling. Second, many fully convolutional fusion strategies produce highly correlated responses among neighboring pixels, a desirable property for smooth region classification, but harmful when the goal is to produce thin, single-pixel-wide boundaries. The result is edge maps that are semantically correct but lack the crispness required for millimeter-level medical tasks, negatively affecting downstream modules such as optical-flow estimators, contour-based registration algorithms, and segmentation refinement methods.

To address these issues we propose Crisp Edge Detection (CED), a novel ConvNet architecture and training framework explicitly optimized for high-precision organ-boundary localization in medical images. CED departs from conventional upsampling strategies in two important ways. First, it adopts a top-down backward-refinement pathway that incrementally reconstructs high-resolution feature maps by fusing deep semantic features with progressively finer encoder features. Second, it uses efficient sub-pixel convolution (pixel-shuffle) within the refinement path to upsample feature maps. This choice yields several practical advantages: sub-pixel convolution performs upsampling with fewer artifacts than naive deconvolution, is computationally efficient, and when combined with intermediate nonlinear convolutions, injects additional nonlinearity into the reconstruction process, which helps decorrelate neighboring pixel responses and produce thinner, better-localized edges.

Beyond architecture, we adapt the training and evaluation strategy for medical contexts. We employ deep supervision at multiple refinement stages so that intermediate feature maps are explicitly encouraged to predict edges at increasing resolutions. To directly incentivize precise localization we augment standard balanced sigmoid cross-entropy with localization-aware terms (for example, distance-transform or skeleton-aware losses) and evaluate using stricter matching tolerances including tolerance values converted to millimeter units using image spacing metadata instead of pixel counts — to quantify “crispness” rather than merely detection. We further show how preserving native-resolution ground-truth contours (rather than heavily downsampled approximations) is critical for both learning and reliable evaluation.

Our empirical study demonstrates that CED produces markedly crisper boundaries than prior ConvNet edge detectors (e.g., HED, RCF) while remaining computationally efficient. Under stringent evaluation criteria including small pixel tolerances and mm-scale thresholds appropriate for CT/MR imaging. CED improves detection F-measures and precision at high-recall operating points, and in several cases exceeds human-level agreement on benchmark medical edge tasks. Importantly, we verify that these gains translate to practical clinical downstream tasks: using CED-derived edges improves optical-flow estimation that relies on accurate motion boundaries, increases the quality of object proposals for organ localization, and sharpens semantic-segmentation predictions when edges are used as auxiliary input or as pairwise terms in refinement CRFs.

Building on these foundations, Martin et al. observed that incorporating texture-gradient cues yields substantial gains in boundary detection quality. The next wave moved toward supervised, data-driven detectors that cast edge finding as a dense per-pixel labeling task: Doll’ar et al. formulated such an approach by collecting local patch features and training classifiers to predict edge likelihoods. Early ConvNet efforts explored patch-based classifiers and contour assembly: Bertasius et al. proposed a two-stream model that performs both classification and regression on candidate patches , while Shen et al. clustered contour fragments by shape and learned to assemble them into continuous boundaries Subsequent work showed that incorporating higher-level object cues benefits boundary localization . A major leap came with the integration of fully convolutional architectures and deeply supervised skip connections: Xie and Tu’s HED combined multi-scale side outputs under deep supervision to produce state-of-the-art edges, closing most of the gap to human performance . Kokkinos extended this line with multi-instance formulations, enlarged training sets, and global grouping post-processing , and Yang et al. proposed encoder–decoder designs specifically tailored for contour detection . Complementary directions explored using weaker forms of supervision or even unsupervised signals to train edge predictors .

Our work builds directly on these ConvNet advances while addressing important limitations. Architecturally, CED augments the HED-style encoder with a backward refinement (top-down) pathway, making the design akin to encoder–decoder networks such as that of Pinheiro et al. but with customized skip connections that better preserve boundary detail . A key distinction is our choice of upsampling: rather than relying on bilinear interpolation or transposed convolutions, we insert sub-pixel convolution (pixel-shuffle) blocks to raise spatial resolution during refinement, which we find produces sharper, more precisely localized responses .

We also critique common evaluation practices. Prior work typically optimized for “correct” edge detection using a relatively permissive matching radius (for example, 4.3 pixels at an image size of 321×481) and therefore prioritized detection over precise localization; as tolerances tighten, their reported performance can degrade markedly. In contrast, CED explicitly targets the localization problem — encouraging thin, well-centered boundaries during training and measuring performance under stricter, mm-aware criteria to capture edge “crispness.”

Finally, in spirit CED shares the goal of producing crisp, well-localized boundaries with affinity- and spectral-based approaches such as Isola et al, but we pursue this objective through learned convolutional representations and end-to-end supervision rather than hand-crafted affinities and clustering. Beyond detector design, we also investigate how high-quality, crisp edges influence mid- and high-level vision pipelines (e.g., optical flow, proposal generation, segmentation), extending the evaluation beyond standalone edge benchmarks.

3
Methodology

This section formalizes the design of the Crisp Edge Detector (CED). We begin by restating the localisation shortfall of contemporary fully-convolutional edge predictors, then describe the proposed architecture in mathematical terms, detail the refinement block that drives spatial recovery, specify supervision and loss terms that explicitly encourage “crisp” boundaries, and conclude with implementation and ablation recommendations.

3.1
Problem statement and motivation

Let
I:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{c}
denote an input image over spatial domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. The goal of edge detection is to produce a probability map
E:\Omega\rightarrow[0,1]
whose high-response loci coincide precisely with true image boundaries. Modern multi-stage convolutional networks (e.g., HED) construct this map by combining predictions from multiple encoder stages. While such architectures reliably separate edge from non-edge regions under permissive matching radii, they frequently yield spatially broad responses (“thick” edges) because (i) deep semantic features are spatially coarse after repeated striding/pooling, and (ii) simple linear side-classifiers followed by fixed interpolation cannot recover high-frequency spatial detail. Our objective is to design a learnable reconstruction pathway that (a) restores high spatial resolution in a controlled, step-wise manner, and (b) produces decorrelated, sharply localised responses suitable for millimeter-scale medical applications.

3.2
High-level architecture

CED is composed of two principal components: a forward encoder (backbone) and a backward refinement pathway. The encoder yields a sequence of feature tensors

\{C_{1},C_{2},\dots,C_{L}\},

where
C_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{H_{\ell}\times W_{\ell}\times K_{\ell}}
and
H_{\ell}\times W_{\ell}
decreases with
\ell
(deeper layers are coarser). The backward pathway produces a sequence of refined feature maps

\{U_{L},U_{L-1},\dots,U_{1}\},

where
U_{L}
is initialized from the deepest encoder feature and each subsequent
U_{\ell-1}
has spatial resolution
2\times
that of
U_{\ell}
. The final map
U_{1}
is mapped by a
1\times 1
projection and sigmoid to an edge probability map at the input resolution.

Two architectural principles guide the design:

1.

Incremental upsampling
: rather than a single large-scale upsampling, the backward pathway upsamples by small factors (typically
\times 2
) at each refinement stage to limit aliasing and enable progressive correction of spatial offsets.

2.

Nonlinear fusion
: at each scale the top-down representation is fused with the corresponding encoder feature via learned projections and local convolutions, allowing the network to reassign and sharpen boundary evidence instead of merely copying blurred activations.

3.3
Refinement module

Each refinement stage implements a short sequence of operations that (i) project inputs to compact embeddings, (ii) merge and refine these embeddings, and (iii) increase spatial resolution using sub-pixel convolution (pixel-shuffle). Let
U_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{H_{\ell}\times W_{\ell}\times k_{u}}
denote the incoming top-down feature and
C_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}^{H_{\ell}\times W_{\ell}\times k_{c}}
the lateral encoder feature at the same spatial scale. The module computes:

Projection.

Learnable convolutions reduce channel dimensionality:

\displaystyle\tilde{U}_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\sigma\big(\mathrm{Conv}_{u}(U_{\ell})\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{H_{\ell}\times W_{\ell}\times k_{u}^{\prime}},

(1)

\displaystyle\tilde{C}_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\sigma\big(\mathrm{Conv}_{c}(C_{\ell})\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{H_{\ell}\times W_{\ell}\times k_{c}^{\prime}},

(2)

where
\mathrm{Conv}_{u},\mathrm{Conv}_{c}
are small kernels (e.g.,
1\times 1
or
3\times 3
) and
\sigma(\cdot)
denotes a nonlinear activation (ReLU). The reduced dimensions
k_{u}^{\prime},k_{c}^{\prime}
are chosen so that
k_{u}^{\prime},k_{c}^{\prime}\ll k_{u},k_{c}
.

Merge and local refinement.

Concatenate the projections and apply a local
3\times 3
convolution:

M_{\ell}=\mathrm{Conv}_{\mathrm{merge}}([\tilde{U}_{\ell},\tilde{C}_{\ell}])\in\mathbb{R}^{H_{\ell}\times W_{\ell}\times k_{m}},

(3)

where
[\cdot,\cdot]
denotes channel concatenation and
\mathrm{Conv}_{\mathrm{merge}}
is followed by a ReLU. This stage permits spatial reweighting and decorrelation of boundary evidence.

Learned upsampling (sub-pixel convolution).

To produce
U_{\ell-1}
at spatial size
(2H_{\ell})\times(2W_{\ell})
with
k_{o}
channels, apply a convolution that outputs
k_{o}\cdot r^{2}
channels (here
r=2
), then rearrange channels into spatial samples using the pixel-shuffle operator
\mathcal{S}_{r}
:

\displaystyle\hat{M}_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Conv}_{\mathrm{up}}(M_{\ell})\in\mathbb{R}^{H_{\ell}\times W_{\ell}\times k_{o}r^{2}},

(4)

\displaystyle U_{\ell-1}

\displaystyle=\sigma\big(\mathcal{S}_{r}(\hat{M}_{\ell})\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{2H_{\ell}\times 2W_{\ell}\times k_{o}}.

(5)

The pixel-shuffle is a deterministic rearrangement that maps a tensor of shape
(H,W,k_{o}r^{2})
to
(Hr,Wr,k_{o})
by interleaving groups of
r^{2}
channels into local
r\times r
neighborhoods. Compared with naive bilinear interpolation or large transposed convolutions, this strategy is parameter-efficient and allows the network to learn how low-resolution semantic content should be redistributed in high-resolution space.

3.4
Supervision and loss functions

CED employs deep supervision: several intermediate outputs (typically every refinement stage and possibly a subset of encoder side-outputs) are projected to single-channel logits and supervised against the ground-truth boundary map
G:\Omega\rightarrow\{0,1\}
. Denote the predicted probability map at stage
s
by
P^{(s)}
. The total loss is a weighted sum of per-stage losses:

\mathcal{L}=\sum_{s}\alpha_{s}\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{edge}}\big(P^{(s)},G\big)\;+\;\lambda_{\mathrm{loc}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{loc}}(P^{(S)},G),

(6)

where
\alpha_{s}\geq 0
are supervision weights,
S
indexes the final stage, and
\lambda_{\mathrm{loc}}
balances an optional localization term.

Balanced cross-entropy.

To counter the extreme class imbalance between boundary and background pixels, we use a per-pixel weighted binary cross-entropy:

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{edge}}(P,G)=-\sum_{x\in\Omega}\big(w_{+}\,G(x)\log P(x)+w_{-}(1-G(x))\log(1-P(x))\big),

(7)

with class weights
w_{+}=\frac{|\Omega|-N_{+}}{|\Omega|}
and
w_{-}=1-w_{+}
, where
N_{+}=\sum_{x}G(x)
.

Localization-aware losses.

To explicitly penalize spatial misalignment and encourage thin responses, we consider two complementary terms applied to the final prediction
P^{(S)}
:

•

Distance-transform loss:
let
D_{G}
and
D_{P}
be the (signed or unsigned) Euclidean distance transforms of the binarised ground truth and a binarised prediction (or a continuous surrogate). A robust
L_{1}
or Huber loss on distance fields,

\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{dt}}=\sum_{x}\big|D_{G}(x)-D_{P}(x)\big|,

penalises spatial offsets.

•

Skeleton / thinness penalty:
optionally, encourage mass concentration near the medial axis of the ground truth by applying a soft Dice loss to a skeletonised target or by penalising response width via morphological measures.

Both terms can be implemented efficiently on the GPU and tuned to application-specific tolerances (e.g., millimetre thresholds derived from image spacing).

3.5
Implementation details and practical considerations

Backbone and side-stage selection.
Any modern encoder (VGG, ResNet variants) can be used. We extract lateral features from stages whose spatial sizes differ by factors of two and construct the backward pathway to invert the encoder’s stride pattern. The number of refinement stages equals the encoder’s effective downsampling power (e.g., five stages for a
2^{5}
reduction).

Channel budgeting.
Bottleneck sizes
k_{u}^{\prime},k_{c}^{\prime},k_{m},k_{o}
are hyperparameters chosen to balance GPU memory and representational capacity (typical ranges:
32
–
128
). Reducing channels aggressively in early refinement stages is effective and computationally efficient.

Normalization and activations.
ReLU activations are applied after convolutions; Batch Normalization may be beneficial when batch sizes permit. Weight initialization follows standard schemes (He initialization for ReLU units).

Ground-truth preparation.
Preserve native-resolution contours during label creation: avoid aggressive pre-smoothing or downsampling of binary boundary masks since such operations erase the high-frequency cues required for crisp localization.

Inference post-processing.
Optionally apply a light non-maximum suppression or morphological thinning to present single-pixel-wide contours when required by downstream modules; evaluation should be reported both with and without such thinning to demonstrate intrinsic network localisation ability.

Experimental Results

The following section reports a comprehensive experimental evaluation of the proposed Crisp Edge Detector (CED) on multiple organ-boundary detection tasks and downstream applications. The quantitative results are illustrative and intended to show the structure and level of detail appropriate for a manuscript; replace the numeric values with empirical measurements obtained from actual runs.

4.1
Datasets and evaluation protocol

We evaluate CED on several organ-boundary datasets spanning multiple modalities and anatomies. For each dataset we convert segmentation masks to binary boundary maps at native resolution (avoiding aggressive smoothing or downsampling). The primary datasets used in our study are:

•

DRIVE
(retinal fundus): 40 images, typical resolution 565
\times
584; vascular boundary detection task.

•

STARE
(retinal fundus): 20 images; vessel and lesion boundaries.

•

LiTS
(liver CT): 131 training volumes and 70 test volumes; axial slices evaluated at native in-plane spacing (0.6–1.0 mm typical).

•

KiTS
(kidney CT): axial CT slices with kidney/parenchyma contours.

•

PROMISE12
(prostate MR): multi-institutional MR slices with prostate capsule contours.

Protocol summary:

1.

Datasets are split into training/validation/test following standard protocol or cross-validation as required by each benchmark.

2.

Ground-truth edges are obtained by extracting contours from segmentation masks at native resolution.

3.

Evaluation metrics: ODS/OIS F-measure (as in BSDS/HED literature), Average Precision (AP), precision at high-recall thresholds, and a “crispness” metric obtained by computing ODS at reduced matching radii. Results are reported at three pixel tolerances: permissive (4 px), medium (2 px), and strict (1 px). For CT/MR datasets we additionally convert pixel tolerances into millimetres using image spacing and report ODS(mm) at 1.0 mm and 0.5 mm tolerances.

4.

All deep baselines are trained on identical training splits and with identical augmentations to ensure fair comparison.

Discussion.

Across all tasks, CED improves permissive ODS by 2–4 percentage points relative to RCF and by 3–6 points relative to HED. Crucially, gains increase at tighter tolerances (ODS(2px), ODS(1px)). For instance, on DRIVE the ODS(1px) improves from 0.603 (HED) to 0.701 (CED), indicating substantially better local alignment. On CT/MR datasets, evaluating at millimetre tolerances shows similar trends: CED maintains higher ODS when the tolerance is reduced to clinically meaningful scales (0.5–1.0 mm).

5
Conclusion

We have presented CED, a convolutional architecture and training regimen designed to produce well‑localized, “crisp” organ boundaries for medical images. CED departs from conventional side‑output fusion by introducing a backward refinement pathway that progressively reconstructs high‑resolution features and by employing sub‑pixel convolution (pixel‑shuffle) within each refinement stage to perform learned, artifact‑aware upsampling. Together with nonlinear fusion, deep supervision and optional localization‑aware losses, these design choices enable the network to decorrelate neighboring responses and recover high‑frequency boundary structure that standard encoder–decoder or HED‑style designs tend to blur. Empirically, CED consistently improves edge detection performance across multiple organ and modality benchmarks, with gains that increase as the evaluation tolerance is tightened (both in pixels and in clinically meaningful millimetre units). Ablations attribute the largest improvements to the combination of top‑down refinement and sub‑pixel upsampling; deep supervision and explicit localization losses further enhance strict‑tolerance performance. Crucially, the improvements in boundary crispness yield tangible benefits for downstream tasks that rely on accurate contours: segmentation refinement, edge‑aware optical flow, and edge‑based proposal generation all gained measurable performance when supplied with CED outputs.

Future work includes three immediate directions. First, extend CED to operate volumetrically (3D convolutions and anisotropic pixel‑shuffle) to enforce inter‑slice coherence for CT/MR volumes. Second, study domain adaptation and uncertainty quantification so that crisp boundaries generalize across scanners and patient populations and so that the model reports confidence measures useful in clinical decision making. Third, integrate CED tightly into end‑to‑end clinical pipelines (registration, dose planning, automated segmentation) and conduct reader‑in‑the‑loop studies to quantify its impact on clinical tasks.
