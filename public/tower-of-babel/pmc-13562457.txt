Daratumumab monotherapy for relapsed AL amyloidosis: a single center retrospective analysis
Authors: Jun Ho Yi, Sang Eun Yoon, Seok Jin Kim, Darae Kim, Jin-oh Choi, Ju-Hong Min, Byoung Joon Kim, Jung-Sun Kim, Jung Eun Lee, Junseok Jeon, Joon Young Choi, Kihyun Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTreatment of relapsed AL amyloidosis remains challenging as patients frequently suffer from severe organ dysfunctions. Daratumumab, a cornerstone drug in multiple myeloma and newly diagnosed AL amyloidosis, has also shown promise in relapsed AL amyloidosis.Materials and methodsTo assess the efficacy of daratumumab, we conducted a retrospective study of 40 patients who received daratumumab monotherapy in our prospective amyloidosis cohort.ResultsThe median age of the cohort was 67 years, and 31 patients were classified as stage III/IV by the Mayo 2012 guidelines and 32 patients as stage IIIa/IIIb by the European staging system. The overall hematologic and cardiac response rates were 72.5% and 42.4%, respectively. With median follow-up duration of 26.9 months, the estimated median progression-free survival was 21.9 months. Deep hematologic (p < 0.001) and cardiac response (p = 0.001) were associated with prolonged progression free survival. The duration of hematologic/cardiac responses were 26.2 months and 27.8 months, respectively. The median overall survival was 30.9 months. Treatment was well tolerated, as with 3 patients experiencing grade 3–4 hematologic adverse events and 8 experiencing grade 3–4 non-hematologic adverse events.DiscussionOur findings suggest that daratumumab monotherapy provides rapid and durable responses and is generally well tolerated in patients with relapsed AL amyloidosis.Keywords: AL amyloidosis, Daratumumab, Response, Survival

Introduction

Systemic light-chain AL amyloidosis is a rare clonal plasma cell disorder characterized by overproduction of immunoglobulin light chains by clonal bone marrow plasma cells that misfold and form soluble toxic aggregates. These aggregates deposit as amyloid fibrils in multiple organs, causing organ dysfunction, organ failure, and ultimately death [1]. Despite the development of novel sensitive biomarkers and advanced imaging technologies for amyloidosis detection, diagnosis is often delayed for more than a year due to nonspecific symptoms, which is a major reason why patients begin treatment at an advanced stage [2]. Although treatment heavily borrows from multiple myeloma (MM), patients with AL amyloidosis are often unable to tolerate therapies because of comorbid cardiac, renal, and/or neurological dysfunction. Additionally, as the primary goal of therapy is to prevent further organ damage through rapid and deep suppression of light-chain producing plasma cells, identifying the most effective yet safe treatment strategy is crucial. As cardiac involvement is the major determinant of disease severity, it is incorporated into both staging systems and response criteria to better predict short- and long-term outcomes.

Daratumumab is a monoclonal antibody that targets CD38 on the surface of plasma cells. The efficacy of daratumumab for newly diagnosed AL amyloidosis has been demonstrated in the ANDROMEDA trial [3], leading to its approval by both the U.S. Food and Drug Administration and the European Medicines Agency. As daratumumab itself rarely induces cardiac adverse events (AEs) [4], monotherapy can be readily used in the relapsed setting. Further, its efficacy and safety in relapsed disease states has been validated in two phase II trials [5, 6] and several retrospective studies [7–10]. We conducted this single-center retrospective analysis to contribute to the growing body of real-world evidence on daratumumab efficacy.

Materials and methods

Patients

Since 2012, we have used a multidisciplinary team approach and conducted a prospective cohort study on AL amyloidosis. Among these patients, those with AL amyloidosis who received daratumumab monotherapy for relapsed disease and had measurable light chain disease were included in the analysis. Patients with bone marrow plasma cells that exceeded 60% were excluded. Clinicopathological variables, including age, sex, cytogenetics, stage, prior treatment, hematologic and cardiac responses to daratumumab monotherapy, and survival were collected and analyzed. Disease stages were categorized according to the Mayo 2012 (Mayo 2012 stage) [11], Mayo 2004 with European modification (Euro stage) [12], and the Renal stage [13]. Daratumumab monotherapy was administered either intravenously (16 mg/kg) or subcutaneously (1,800 mg) depending on patient circumstances, in accordance with a standard treatment schedule (weekly for 8 doses → every 2 weeks for 8 doses → every 4 weeks until disease progression or unacceptable toxicity). All patients were administered oral acyclovir for the prevention of herpes zoster reactivation and oral trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole for pneumocystis reactivation.

Statistical analysis

The primary objective was to evaluate the efficacy and safety of daratumumab monotherapy in patients with relapsed AL amyloidosis. Hematologic [14] and cardiac [15] responses were assessed according to relevant criteria. Subgroup comparisons were performed using Pearson’s χ2 tests. Duration of response was calculated from the date of response achievement to the date of loss of response. Progression-free survival (PFS) was calculated from the date of the first administration of daratumumab to the date of hematologic progression in disease or death by any cause. Overall survival (OS) was calculated from the date of the first administration of daratumumab to the date of death by any cause. These parameters were calculated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and subgroup comparisons were carried out using log-rank test. AEs were graded according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events, version 5.0. For all statistical analyses, p values < 0.05 were considered significant, and all analyses were performed using IBM SPSS (version 21.0; IBM Corp., USA).

Ethical considerations

All procedures performed in studies involving human participants were conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the institution and/or national research committee, and in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki guidelines or comparable ethical standards. The current study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Samsung Medical Center (IRB 2012–08–059). All authors declare no competing interests.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of 40 patients who had been treated with daratumumab monotherapy between 2018 and 2025 were included. In the cohort, 24 patients (60%) were male, and 29 (72.5%) had lambda light-chain disease. Among the 28 patients in whom t(11;14) status was assessed, 6 (21.4%) tested positive. Among the 32 patients in whom gain(1q) status was assessed, 5 (15.6%) tested positive. Before administration of daratumumab monotherapy, patients had received a median of 3 (range, 1–6) prior lines of therapy including treatment with bortezomib (97.5%, 39/40), lenalidomide (75.0%, 30/40), pomalidomide (42.5%, 17/40), cyclophosphamide (37.5%, 15/40), and melphalan (32.5%, 13/40). None of the patients had been administered prior daratumumab treatment. Twelve patients (30%) had received prior autologous stem cell transplantation.

At the time of daratumumab initiation, the median age of patients was 67 years (range, 48–83), and the therapy was started a median of 42.3 months (95% confidence interval (CI), 26.2–58.4) after the initial diagnosis of AL amyloidosis. A total of 31 patients (77.5%) were classified as stage III/IV using the Mayo stage classification guidelines and 32 patients (80%) were classified as stage IIIa/IIIb using the Euro stage guidelines. Cardiac involvement defined by NT-proBNP > 332 pg/mL was reported in 37 patients (92.5%). A total of 8 patients (20.0%) were classified as renal stage III, and renal involvement defined by 24-h urine protein > 500 mg was observed in 22 patients (55.0%). Efficacy and safety

The overall hematologic response rate was 72.5% (29/40), including 9 very good partial responses (VGPRs) and 15 complete responses (CRs) (Fig. 1A). The median time to response was 29 days (range, 13–117). Patients with Euro stage I/II/IIIa had a significantly higher likelihood of achieving CR/VGPR compared with those with stage IIIb (20/27 [74.1%] vs. 4/13 [30.8%], p = 0.009). A similar trend was observed when Mayo 2012 stage I/II/III were compared with stage IV; however, this difference did not reach statistical significance (68.0% vs. 46.7%, p = 0.182) (Fig. 1B). Measurable cardiac disease defined as NT-proBNP > 650 pg/mL was observed in 33 patients, and the overall cardiac response rate was 42.4% (14/33), including 4 VGPRs and 2 CRs (Fig. 1C). The median time to cardiac response was 92 days (range, 27–887). Both Mayo 2012 stage and Euro stage classified diseases were associated with cardiac response (Fig. 1D). Among 22 patients with renal involvement, 9 patients (40.9%) demonstrated a renal response. As expected, patients who demonstrated a deep hematologic response including CR or VGPR, were more likely to achieve a cardiac response (13/18 [72.2%] in hematologic responders vs. 1/15 [6.7%] in non-responders, p < 0.001). However, a deep hematologic response was not associated with renal response (p = 0.779).

The final data cutoff date was November 30, 2025 and the median follow-up was 26.9 months. The estimated median PFS was 21.9 months (95% CI, 10.0–33.8). Patients who demonstrated deep hematologic responses (CR or VGPR) had significantly prolonged PFS compared with those with partial response (PR) or no response (NR) (30.5 months vs. 3.9 months, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2A). Median PFS was 21.9 months for patients who achieved VGPR and 30.5 months for those who achieved CR. Although the median PFS appeared longer in patients with CR, the difference was not statistically significant, which is possibly due to a small sample size. Likewise, patients who showed a cardiac response had markedly longer PFS compared with non-responders (38.8 months vs. 5.3 months, p = 0.001) (Fig. 2B). None of the staging systems at the time of daratumumab initiation including the Mayo 2012 (26.8 months vs. 5.9 months, p = 0.220), Euro (26.8 months vs. 5.3 months, p = 0.123), or renal staging systems (26.8 months vs. 5.3 months, p = 0.085) were predictive of prolonged PFS, although numerically longer PFS was observed in patients with early-stage disease compared with those with advanced-stage disease (stage IV by Mayo 2012 or stage IIIb by Euro).

The median durations of hematologic and cardiac response were 26.2 months (95% CI, 19.4–33.0) and 27.8 months (95% CI, 10.6–45.0), respectively (Fig. 3A).

A Survival curves for duration of hematologic (median, 26.2 months) and cardiac (median, 27.8 months) response; B survival curve for overall survival (median, 30.9 months)

A total of 17 deaths were recorded: 9 due to worsening amyloidosis, 2 due to septic shock, 1 unrelated to treatment, and 5 due to unknown causes (follow-up losses). Among the 10 patients who died within 6 months after treatment initiation, all (100.0%) had Mayo stage III/IV disease, and 9 patients (90.0%) had Euro stage IIIa/IIIb disease. None of the 10 patients demonstrated either a hematologic or a cardiac response. The median OS of the entire cohort was 30.9 months (95% CI, 11.9–49.9) and the 3-year OS rate was 46.0% (95% CI, 25.0 – 67.0%) (Fig. 3B). As expected, the median OS for those who had progressive disease to daratumumab treatment were only 5.9 months (95% CI, 4.0–7.8).

No significant differences were observed in response rates, PFS, and OS according to abnormal cytogenetics including t(11;14) and gain(1q) (Data not shown).

Daratumumab monotherapy was generally well tolerated. Three patients experienced grade 3–4 hematologic AEs (two thrombocytopenia and one neutropenia), and one patient, who had undergone autologous stem cell transplantation 4 years prior to initiating daratumumab, developed acute myeloid leukemia. Eight patients experienced grade 3–4 non-hematologic AEs (two cases of pneumonia, two cases of COVID-19 pneumonia, one cholangitis, one gastrointestinal (GI) bleeding event, one acute kidney injury (AKI), and one case of liver enzyme elevation). Among these, the GI bleeding, AKI, and liver enzyme elevation were likely attributable to AL amyloidosis. Due to these adverse events, 11 patients experienced treatment delays, and 1 patient permanently discontinued treatment because of AKI that developed after the single administration of daratumumab monotherapy.

Discussion

The current study describes the efficacy and safety of daratumumab monotherapy in 40 patients with relapsed AL amyloidosis. Despite the fact that a large proportion of patients had advanced disease with significant cardiac involvement at the time of treatment (80% were stage IIIa/IIIb according to the Mayo 2004 staging system with European modification) and were heavily pretreated (median prior lines of therapy: 3), daratumumab monotherapy achieved an overall hematologic response rate of 72.5% and a cardiac response rate of 42.4%. Treatment was generally well tolerated.

The primary pathophysiology of AL amyloidosis is the deposition of amyloid fibrils in vital organs such as the heart, kidneys, and nervous system, leading to organ dysfunction and ultimately death. Attempts to remove amyloid fibrils have largely been unsuccessful [16, 17]. Therefore, the current standard treatment strategies focus on targeting light chain–producing plasma cells, with therapeutic approaches largely adopted from those used in MM. However, as several agents widely used in the treatment of MM are poorly tolerated in patients with AL amyloidosis, caution is warranted. For example, although lenalidomide has demonstrated efficacy and safety in MM, it frequently causes fluid retention and cardiac and renal dysfunction resulting in limited tolerability in patients with AL amyloidosis [18, 19]. In a phase I study of carfilzomib plus dexamethasone in relapsed AL amyloidosis, significant fatigue, GI toxicity, and cardiac events were frequently observed, which limited dose escalation to a maximum of 36 mg/m2 which is considerably lower than the standard dose used in MM [20]. Therefore, even agents that have demonstrated excellent efficacy in MM may exhibit a distinct safety profile in patients with AL amyloidosis due to concomitant organ dysfunction, underscoring the need to consider both efficacy and safety when selecting therapeutic agents for this population.

Daratumumab is a cornerstone of MM treatment and has also been rapidly adopted for the treatment of AL amyloidosis owing to its favorable efficacy and safety profile. Moreover, following its marked success in front-line clinical trials [3], most contemporary guidelines now recommend adding daratumumab as a first-line treatment regimen [21, 22]. Evidence from prospective [5, 6] and retrospective studies [7–10] suggests daratumumab monotherapy as a strongly recommended treatment in daratumumab-naïve patients. Notably, across these trials, daratumumab at 16 mg/kg which is the standard dose used in MM treatment was generally well tolerated, and was associated with a low incidence of adverse events. Although daratumumab-based therapy is strongly recommended as a front-line setting and most patients, where available, received daratumumab for AL amyloidosis, a subset of patients may still experience a disease relapse without prior exposure to daratumumab due to various clinical or logistical factors. Moreover, in many regions, daratumumab is not accessible as part of first-line therapy. Therefore, daratumumab treatment plays an important role in the management of relapsed AL amyloidosis. In addition, given the favorable efficacy and safety of daratumumab re-treatment demonstrated in MM [23, 24], it might also be a viable salvage re-treatment option [25]. Owing to their successful treatment in MM, T-cell–redirecting therapies are being actively investigated in AL amyloidosis [26, 27], and ongoing prospective trials will help clarify their roles in future treatment strategy.

Although daratumumab has been shown to overcome poor outcomes from some adverse cytogenetics [28], we could not capture any significant findings probably due to small numbers of patients.

Despite limitations related to a relatively small number of patients, the single-center setting, and the retrospective nature of the study, a key strength is the inclusion of patients who were administered uniform treatment and supportive care and were followed over a relatively long duration. Furthermore, although early death was frequently observed within 6 months, this likely indicated that in our cohort a higher proportion of patients had advanced-stage disease compared with previous studies, which is consistent with findings reported in frontline trials [3].

Taken together, our findings suggest that daratumumab monotherapy provides rapid and durable responses and is generally well tolerated in patients with relapsed AL amyloidosis. Nevertheless, patients with advanced-stage disease experienced inferior outcomes and frequent early mortality, highlighting a significant unmet medical need in this subgroup.
