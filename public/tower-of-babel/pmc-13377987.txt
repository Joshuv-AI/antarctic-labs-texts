Comparing short- and long-term outcomes of transcatheter valve-in-valve versus redo surgical aortic valve replacement: a literature review
Authors: Pouya Ebrahimi, Reza Eshraghi, Ali Moradi, Roozbeh Narimani Javid, Omar Khalique, Bahar Darouei, Nazanin Rafiei, Polydoros Kampaktsis, Nikolaos Spilias, Robert Siegel, Michael Fogli, Homa Taheri, Mohammad Hossein Mandegar, Harsh Kumar, Maryam Taheri, Kaveh Hosseini

ABSTRACT
Valve-in-valve transcatheter aortic valve replacement (ViV-TAVR) and redo-surgical aortic valve replacement (redo-SAVR) are two primary strategies for managing degenerated bioprosthetic aortic valves. ViV-TAVR has gained prominence due to its minimally invasive nature, shorter recovery time, and reduced perioperative risk, making it preferable for high-risk patients with multiple comorbidities. In contrast, redo-SAVR remains the gold standard for younger, lower-risk patients because of its superior long-term durability, lower residual gradients, and decreased risk of patient-prosthesis mismatch (PPM). ViV-TAVR is favored for high-risk patients due to its minimally invasive nature, shorter recovery, and reduced perioperative risks, while redo-SAVR remains the gold standard for younger, lower-risk patients owing to its long-term durability and reduced risk of patient-prosthesis mismatch. Ongoing concerns regarding long-term durability, higher transvalvular gradients, and potential coronary obstruction exist. Although redo-SAVR offers better longevity, it has higher perioperative risks and longer recovery times. Across the included propensity-score matched studies and meta-analyses, long-term all-cause mortality was generally comparable between ViV-TAVR and redo-SAVR, although results were heterogeneous and some studies suggested a possible advantage for redo-SAVR in selected populations and/or with longer follow-up. However, ViV-TAVR may be associated with increased heart failure hospitalizations (HHF) after two years and redo-SAVR has superior short-term outcomes with regard to decreased myocardial infarction and PPM. Introduction

Valve-in-valve transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement (ViV-TAVR) and Redo-Surgical Aortic Valve Replacement (Redo-SAVR) are two primary strategies for managing the degeneration of bioprosthetic aortic valves. Patients often face the critical decision of whether to undergo a less invasive transcatheter approach or repeat open-heart surgery [1, 2]. ViV-TAVR has gained significant attraction due to its minimally invasive nature, faster recovery, and lower early procedural risks. It is particularly beneficial for high-risk surgical candidates and those with multiple comorbidities [3, 4]. However, concerns persist regarding long-term durability and the potential for higher residual gradients, especially in patients with smaller prostheses.

On the other hand, redo-SAVR remains the recommended standard for bioprosthetic valve failure in younger and lower-risk patients due to its ability to completely remove the degenerated valve and implant a new, appropriately sized prosthesis [5]. This surgical approach often results in superior hemodynamic outcomes with lower residual gradients, reducing the risk of patient-prosthesis mismatch [2, 4]. Comparing these two approaches requires careful consideration of patient-specific factors, procedural risks, and long-term outcomes to determine the optimal strategy for managing bioprosthetic aortic valve degeneration.

Studies have demonstrated that ViV-TAVR is associated with lower short-term mortality and morbidity compared to redo-SAVR, with a meta-analysis reporting significantly reduced 30-day all-cause mortality and major bleeding in ViV-TAVR patients [6, 7] [4]. However, while ViV-TAVR offers favorable short-term results, its long-term durability remains a concern. Research indicates that beyond two years post-procedure, ViV-TAVR may be associated with higher mortality and increased heart failure hospitalizations compared to redo-SAVR [8]. Additionally, ViV-TAVR has been linked to higher residual transvalvular gradients, which could impact valve durability and long-term patient prognosis [9]. Understanding these differences is essential for tailoring treatment to individual patient profiles.

By reviewing data from the most recent propensity-score-matched (PSM) studies and meta-analyses, this review aims to compare ViV-TAVR and redo-SAVR regarding efficacy, safety, and patient selection criteria and provide a balanced and evidence-based perspective on the optimal selection of patients for each procedure, helping guide clinical decision-making in managing failed bioprosthetic valves.

Indications for ViV-TAVR or Redo-SAVR

Bioprosthetic valve replacement should be considered when bioprosthetic valve dysfunction occurs due to structural or nonstructural causes. Structural issues, such as leaflet calcification, fibrosis, flail leaflet, or strut deformation, can lead to structural valve degeneration (SVD), which is classified as moderate or severe based on trans-prosthetic gradients. Nonstructural bioprosthetic valve dysfunction arises from implantation complications or host responses, manifesting as valve entrapment, paravalvular leak (PVL), pannus formation, or prosthesis-patient mismatch (PPM) [22, 23]. PPM occurs when the prosthetic valve’s orifice is too small for the patient’s anatomy, causing elevated gradients and reduced cardiac output, with severe cases defined by indexed effective orifice area (iEOA) thresholds. A critical limitation is PVL, in which blood leaks between the prosthetic valve and the surrounding annulus due to annular calcification, malpositioning, or improper sizing. Although advances in transcatheter technology have reduced PVL rates, it occurs more frequently after TAVR and ViV-TAVR than after SAVR. Large studies have shown that even mild PVL after TAVR is independently associated with increased all-cause and cardiovascular mortality as well as higher rehospitalization rates [24, 25]) [4, 26, 27] (Fig. 1).

A. Redo-Surgical Aortic Valve Replacement

A. Redo-Surgical Aortic Valve Replacement

Redo-Surgical Aortic Valve Replacement

Redo-SAVR has long been the standard treatment for degenerated bioprosthetic aortic valves, evolving significantly from its early iterations to modern-day advancements. Historically, SAVR was established in the 1960 s and 1970 s with mechanical and bioprosthetic valves, providing a durable solution for aortic valve disease [28]. However, with the increased use of bioprosthetic valves due to their lower thromboembolic risk compared to mechanical valves, the need for redo-SAVR emerged as a critical intervention for SVD occurring after 10–15 years [29–31]. Initially, redo-SAVR posed substantial surgical risks due to adhesions, re-sternotomy challenges, and increased perioperative complications, making it a high-risk procedure, especially in elderly or comorbid patients. Over the past two decades, advancements in surgical techniques, perioperative management, cardiopulmonary bypass strategies, and myocardial protection have significantly improved outcomes, reducing operative mortality and morbidity [32]. The development of minimally invasive and sutureless valve techniques has further refined redo-SAVR, decreasing operative times and enhancing patient recovery. Additionally, enhanced imaging modalities, 3D-printed surgical planning, and improved prosthetic valve durability have improved patient outcomes. Despite the emergence of ViV-TAVR as an alternative for high-risk patients, redo-SAVR remains the preferred approach for younger, low-risk patients due to its superior long-term durability, lower residual gradients, and reduced risk of PPM [33]. As surgical expertise continues to evolve, ongoing innovations in bioengineered prosthetic materials, rapid-deployment valves, and hybrid surgical approaches are expected to enhance the safety and efficacy of redo-SAVR further, maintaining its role as a cornerstone in the management of failed bioprosthetic aortic valves [34–36].

B. Valve-in-valve Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement

B. Valve-in-valve Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement

Valve-in-valve Transcatheter Aortic Valve Replacement

ViV-TAVR has emerged as a transformative approach for managing degenerated bioprosthetic valves, offering a minimally invasive alternative to redo-SAVR. Historically, the standard of care for SVD in surgical bioprostheses was redo-SAVR, which, while effective, carried significant risks due to increased surgical complexity, higher morbidity, and longer recovery times, especially in elderly and high-risk patients. The evolution of transcatheter valve therapy began with the pioneering work on TAVR for native aortic stenosis in the early 2000 s, culminating in its first-in-human implantation by Cribier et al. in 2002 [37]. The first ViV procedure was carried out in Germany in 2007 to address severe aortic regurgitation in a deteriorated bioprosthesis. Today, transcatheter ViV-TAVR is recognized as a viable treatment option for failed bioprosthetic valves, particularly in patients deemed high-risk for surgery [38–40].

As TAVR technology advanced, its application expanded to include ViV, which was initially met with skepticism due to concerns over hemodynamic performance, coronary obstruction, and valve durability (Table 1). However, continued refinements in device design, procedural techniques, and patient selection have significantly improved outcomes. Modern ViV platforms, such as the Edwards SAPIEN 3 and Medtronic Evolut PRO, incorporate advanced leaflet designs, lower-profile delivery systems, and optimized radial force to enhance valve expansion within the rigid bioprosthetic annulus [41–43].

Pros and cons of the interventional method compared to the surgical group

Additionally, adjunctive techniques like ViV have been developed to mitigate high post-procedural transvalvular gradients, further improving valve function and durability. Large-scale registry data and propensity-matched studies have demonstrated ViV’s safety and efficacy, particularly in patients deemed high-risk for repeat surgery, with lower perioperative mortality, reduced hospital stays, and faster recovery times compared to redo-SAVR [3, 21, 44]. However, long-term durability remains an area of ongoing investigation, with concerns regarding leaflet thrombosis, coronary access challenges, and sustained hemodynamic performance [1, 4, 45]. Despite these challenges, the field continues to evolve with next-generation transcatheter valves, enhanced imaging guidance, and improved pre-procedural planning, reinforcing ViV as a viable and often preferable alternative to surgical reintervention for failed bioprosthetic valves.

Search strategy

To comprehensively compare ViV-TAVR and redo-SAVR, a systematic literature search was conducted across four major databases: PubMed (MEDLINE), Embase, Scopus, and Web of Science. The search included studies published from inception until February 20, 2025, focusing on PSM analyses, meta-analyses, and comparative studies evaluating both procedures’ efficacy, safety, and patient selection criteria. The search strategy combined key terms such as “valve-in-valve,” “ViV-TAVR,” “redo-SAVR,” “repeat SAVR,” and “surgical redo AVR”, ensuring the inclusion of relevant research. Additionally, filters were applied to identify studies assessing short- and long-term outcomes, mortality, procedural safety, hemodynamic performance, and durability. By synthesizing the latest evidence from high-quality studies, this review aims to provide a balanced and data-driven perspective on the optimal patient selection for ViV-TAVR versus redo-SAVR, helping guide clinical decision-making in managing failed bioprosthetic aortic valves (Table 2).

Comparison of short- and long-term outcomes

A. Short-term outcomes

A. Short-term outcomes

Short-term outcomes

Assessing therapeutic strategies’ outcomes in this pathologic valve condition can be categorized as clinical and echocardiographic indices. In clinical aspects, prominent indices such as mortality, procedural success, complications (bleeding, acute renal failure), major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), hospital and ICU admission time, and readmission rate are measured and compared. Regarding in-hospital and short-term mortality, most studies have shown that patients treated with the transcatheter method had a significantly lower mortality rate than surgical groups [4, 17, 19–21]. On the other hand, some other studies failed to demonstrate a considerable difference between the two groups [11, 15, 18]. Similarly, the evaluation of indices that have the most contribution to the mortality of patients in a short period after surgery, such as myocardial infarction, stroke, major bleeding, heart failure, and arrhythmias, have not shown a uniform pattern of outcomes among studies [1, 3, 44].

All-cause and Cardiovascular-Related Mortality

All-cause and Cardiovascular-Related Mortality

All-cause and Cardiovascular-Related Mortality

In the evaluation of 30-day post-surgery death, four PSM cohort study results confirmed the superiority of ViV-TAVR due to the significantly lower mortality risk in this subgroup [22, 25, 39, 42]. In contrast, some others showed no statistically significant difference between these two groups [26, 41, 43]. These contradictory results can be attributed to several factors, such as the demographic characteristics, the types of valves used, and the severity of the pathology observed in those patients. Deharo et al., in a PSM study with 717 participants in each group, reported a mortality odds ratio of 0.48 (95%CI: 0.30–0.78, P-Value: 0.003) for the incidence risk of mortality in ViV-TAVR patients in the Hospital Compared to the redo-SAVR group [22]. Two studies have reported cardiovascular (CV)-related death separately from all-cause mortality. While Deharo et al. reported a considerably higher rate of CV-related mortality in the Redo group [22], Hirji et al. showed no statistically significant difference between the two main groups [44] (Fig. 2).

Major Bleeding and the Transfusion of Blood Products

Major Bleeding and the Transfusion of Blood Products

Major Bleeding and the Transfusion of Blood Products

One of the most serious concerns regarding valvular procedures is bleeding during and after the procedure, which might result in the requirement for a blood transfusion [33]. Several studies have evaluated the in-hospital and 30-day major and life-threatening bleeding events, mostly resulting in a similar pattern. In most cases, the bleeding observed in the ViV-TAVR group was substantially lower than in the redo-SAVR subgroup [24, 33, 34]. On the other hand, in some recent studies, the risk of bleeding was not significantly different in these two groups [12]. Blood transfusion requirement has also been evaluated, which depends on the blood loss volume. The results have shown that the requirement for these products was considerably prominent in the redo-SAVR than in the ViV-TAVR [26, 33, 35]. One of the crucial factors that significantly affects post-operatory bleeding and the need for the transfusion of blood products is the generation of the valves used in the procedure. Older-generation valves, with higher-profile delivery systems, were associated with increased vascular complications and a higher need for blood transfusion compared to newer-generation valves, which use low-profile delivery systems. The rate of major bleeding incidents requiring transfusion of blood products has been reported to be 3 times more in the surgical group than interventional-treated patients in the most recent PSM study, which had a considerable sample size in both groups (each 1256 patients) [21].

Atrial Fibrillation

Atrial Fibrillation

Atrial Fibrillation

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is one of the main concerns following cardiac interventions and surgeries. There are heterogeneous results regarding evaluating AF in a short-term period. The 30-day PSM results of the cohort study conducted by Deharo et al. showed a significant difference in the incidence of new-onset AF between ViV-TAVR and SAVR. While only four patients (0.6%) after the ViV-TAVR procedure experienced this complication, 29 post-redo patients experienced AF (OR:0.13, 95% CI:0.05–0.38, P-value: <0.001) [22]. A similar pattern of difference in favor of the ViV-TAVR procedure was seen in other PSM studies [24–26]. However, the difference in the incidence of AF in the first 30 days after surgery was not statistically significant in one of the most recent studies conducted by Nagasaka et al. (HR: 0.48, 95% CI: 0.08–2.73) [12]. The most recent PSM cohort evaluated the short-term outcomes of these two methods by Awtry et al. published in 2025, but they have not reported any AF incidences in any of the assessed groups [21].

New Permanent Pacemaker Implantation

New Permanent Pacemaker Implantation

New Permanent Pacemaker Implantation

One of the most concerning events following the valvular procedures is the need for permanent pacemaker implantation (PPI) due to conduction abnormalities, which is associated with an increased risk of hospitalization for heart failure (HHF) and mortality risk [27, 28]. There is considerable incoherence among the studies that evaluated the short-term need for pacemaker implantation. Several studies have revealed non-significant differences. In a multicenter propensity-matched analysis, 30-day new pacemaker implantation was identical for ViV-TAVR and redo-SAVR (10.3% vs. 10.3%) [47]. Majmundar et al. found no significant difference in in-hospital post-procedure pacemaker implantation between ViV-TAVR and repeat SAVR after inverse-probability weighting (17% and 9.3%, p-value: 0.150) [48]. Similarly, earlier National Inpatient Sample analyses likewise reported no significant difference in in-hospital PPI rate between ViV-TAVR and redo-SAVR (2.8% and 6.3%, p-value: 0.170) [49]. On the other side, Deharo et al. demonstrated a significant lower 30-day PPI requirement in 30-redo-SAVR than ViV-TAVR (5.7% and 18.4%, p-value: <0.0001) [50]. In addition, The VIVID registry analysis revealed that the need for PPI after ViV-TAVR was significantly lower with new-generation THVs compared to early generations. Key factors associated with increased PPI risk included older age, larger valve size, and pre-existing right bundle branch block (RBBB). The study found that new-generation THVs had a PPI rate of 4.7%, compared to 7.4% for early generations, highlighting advancements in valve technology and procedural outcomes​ [31, 32].

Major Vascular Complications

Major Vascular Complications

Major Vascular Complications

The procedures used to replace bioprosthetic valve failures carry a risk of major vascular complications such as stroke, acute coronary syndrome (ACS), and myocardial infarction (MI). However, the risk of incidence of these vascular complications is not statistically significant in most PSM studies [12]. In the same way, Tran et al. concluded that no difference could be observed in the incidence of CVA among these two PSM groups (absolute difference risk: 0.3%, P-value: 0.74) [29]. A similar pattern could be observed among these two categories regarding the incidence risk of MI in the hospital admission period and 30 days after the procedure. PSM and non-PSM studies have consistently shown no significant difference between the two therapeutic strategies regarding the risk of MI [36–38]. The incidence of ACS, as another example of vascular complications, was very low, with 1 case in the SAVR group and none in the ViV-TAVR group, indicating no significant difference between the two categories [12].

Major Adverse Cardiovascular Events

Major Adverse Cardiovascular Events

Major Adverse Cardiovascular Events

A PSM study also calculated the rate of major adverse cardiac events (MACE) and concluded no significant difference among these groups [34]. The results indicated the association of ViV-TAVR with a lower rate of in-hospital composite adverse outcomes, including mortality, MI, stroke, and acute kidney injury (AKI), compared to redo-SAVR. Overall, the occurrence of MACE was significantly lower in the ViV-TAVR group (14.1%) compared to the redo-SAVR group (25.4%), with a p-value of 0.018 [33].

Readmission and Rehospitalization for Heart Failure

Readmission and Rehospitalization for Heart Failure

Readmission and Rehospitalization for Heart Failure

Different studies have reported heterogeneous readmission rates during the first 30 days after the procedure. Most of them resulted in the absence of significant differences between the two groups [12, 39, 40]. HHF represents another endpoint that was investigated in three PSM studies. The outcomes driven by these studies showed no significant difference between the two approaches [12, 34, 37]. In a survey by Awtry 2025, comparing outcomes of these two treatment methods, showed that the rate of 30-day rehospitalization in the surgery group was significantly higher in redo-SAVR patients than in ViV patients (55% vs. 34%) [21].

Length of Hospital Stay and ICU-Admission Hours

Length of Hospital Stay and ICU-Admission Hours

Length of Hospital Stay and ICU-Admission Hours

The length of hospital stay is one of the most important factors to consider in analyzing the outcomes of these two types of treatment. All 12 PSM studies that examined the length of hospital stay in these two groups identified significantly shorter lengths of stay in the ViV-TAVR group compared to the redo-SAVR category patients [3, 12, 37]. The same pattern was observed in the intensive care unit (ICU) stay [23, 24, 26]. As a result, applying ViV-TAVR may lead to several benefits for both the patient and the healthcare system, which would be encountered with less occupation of the medical care capacities and more capability to care for other critical patients. Some recent publications have reported the length of hospital and ICU admission up to 3–4 times more in a redo-SVR group than in the interventional group [21].

Acute Renal Failure ± Dialysis

Acute Renal Failure ± Dialysis

Acute Renal Failure ± Dialysis

Acute renal failure is one of the relatively common complications of the SAVR and ViV-TAVR procedures. Several PSM studies have evaluated the risk of AKI after these two therapeutic approaches and found incoherent results [33, 34]. While no statistically significant difference was detected between the incidence of AKI in a short-term period between these two groups [23, 33, 40], a recent study conducted by Tran et al. found the incidence of AKI significantly lower in the ViV-TAVR subgroup compared to the other group [29]. The need for dialysis after the procedure was also evaluated in four PSM studies, which showed no meaningful difference between the two treatment groups [25, 26, 33, 41]. The association between AKI after TAVR procedures with increased requirements for future dialysis and all causes of death has been shown in a previous study [26]. Therefore, reducing AKI incidence may help to decrease the rate of overall mortality and morbidity.

Paravalvular leak

Paravalvular leak

Paravalvular leak

PVL can affect both surgical and transcatheter valve replacements and is associated with adverse outcomes such as heart failure, hemolytic anemia, and endocarditis [45–47]. It can be as prevalent as up to 17% of the patients with bioprosthetic valves [2]. It has been shown in previous literature that PVL is associated with the risk of all-cause mortality, and the need for rehospitalization in the future is the incidence and the severity of PVL in these patients after the procedure [48]. The higher PVL level has also been associated with an elevated risk of hemolytic anemia, independent of other effects of this condition. This can cause a more magnified risk of mortalities and morbidities in the future. These effects might be attributable to the increased mean aortic valve gradient caused by PVL. Therefore, methods with lower rates of PVL incidence are considered advantageous [49]. While only two PSM studies have evaluated the incidence of PVL with varying results, Ejiofor et al. found a significantly higher risk of PVL in the ViV group. In contrast, the study by Nagasaka et al. did not show a considerable difference between the groups. A non-PSM study by Shah et al. reported that PVL occurred in 9.8% of the ViV group, with no cases in the minimally invasive SAVR group. This study indicates a notable incidence of PVL in the ViV procedure compared to minimally invasive SAVR [12, 23, 50].

- Patients Prosthesis Mismatch (PPM) on redo-SAVR vs. VIV-TAVR

Patient prosthesis mismatch is defined by indexed effective orifice area (EOAi) with moderate PPM at > 0.65–≤0.85 cm²/m² (or > 0.55–≤0.70 cm²/m² if obese) and severe PPM at ≤ 0.65 cm²/m² (≤ 0.55 cm²/m² if obese). PPM is a key determinant of post-reintervention hemodynamics [51].

In a study on 184 patients, Hecht et al. using inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) found ViV-TAVR had markedly worse immediate hemodynamics than redo-SAVR: intended hemodynamic performance was less frequent (39.2% vs. 67.7%), residual mean gradient ≥ 20 mmHg was more common (56.2% vs. 28.8%), and severe PPM occurred more often after ViV-TAVR (59.5% vs. 41.7%) [51]. Pre-existing PPM strongly predicted severe post-procedural PPM overall and particularly after ViV-TAVR, underscoring the cumulative risk in patients with small prior surgical valves [51]. Not all datasets show a gradient penalty for ViV-TAVR in small valves: in a cohort with ≤ 21 mm prior prostheses, postoperative mean gradients were similar between femoral ViV and redo-AVR (20.3 ± 10.7 vs. 22.2 ± 9.6 mmHg), though patients remaining NYHA III commonly had discharge gradients > 20 mmHg, highlighting functional relevance of residual obstruction [52] [56]. Importantly, some studies lacked echocardiographic adjudication of PPM, limiting causal inference [53]. Overall, redo-SAVR more reliably optimizes EOAi, whereas ViV-TAVR demands proactive strategies (patient selection, sizing, and adjunctive techniques) to mitigate PPM risk [51].

B. Mid- and Long-Term Outcomes

B. Mid- and Long-Term Outcomes

Mid- and Long-Term Outcomes

Most long-term outcome studies are focused on mortality and severe morbidity incidences. Six PSM studies have analyzed the mortality rate in both groups in different follow-up periods. None of these studies have found considerable differences between redo-SAVR and ViV TAVR [12, 22, 25, 34, 41]. In a follow-up study, 717 patients were matched in each group. For a mean time of 790 ± 751 days, the difference between the rate of important clinical indicators such as CV-related death, all-cause stroke, MI, and new-onset AF was not statistically significant. In contrast, the rate of HHF was considerably higher in the ViV-TAVR subgroup in this study [22]. The three-year survival evaluated by Ejiofor et al. showed a survival rate of 76.3% in SAVR and 78.7% in ViV-TAVR patients, revealing no significant difference (P-value: 0.410) [23]. Gatta et al. estimated the 6-year survival of patients in both groups in a PSM study and found no significant difference (SAVR;68 ± 5.8, ViV: 67 ± 7.6, P-value: 0.26) [24]. Hecht et al., in another study, evaluated the long-term mortality and found out the survival rate in the ViV-TAVR group was significantly lower than in the Redo-SAVR [25].

In a six-month follow-up conducted by Majmundar et al., despite the significantly higher incidence of non-cardiac infections and all-cause readmissions in the ViV-TAVR group (P-value: 0.068 and < 0.00, respectively), no significant difference was detected in the rate of incidence of MACE, Major bleeding, vascular complications, and procedural complications [34]. The recently published investigation in Cedars-Sinai Hospital with 77 participants in each matched group demonstrated that in a 2-year follow-up, none of the indicators of death, stroke, HHF, ACS, new AF, new PPI, endocarditis, and aortic valve re-intervention were meaningfully different between the two groups. One important finding during these investigations was the impact of newly developed valves on reducing paravalvular leaks and consequently improving the survival rate [12].

Stankowski et al. discussed patients’ mortality and survival rates in both groups based on their risk of surgery. The study’s results indicated that while the overall survival rate was higher in a long-term follow-up of overall and intermediate-risk patients, the survival rate in the Redo group decreased after three years, becoming lower than that of the ViV-TAVR subgroup. However, the difference between subgroups was not statistically significant [26]. A five-year follow-up study by Tam et al. revealed a significantly higher survival rate in the group than in the redo-SAVR subgroup. It showed that the overall decline in the requirement for invasive procedures in the ViV-TAVR subgroup improves late outcomes and survival rates [51].

In the most recent study comparing the outcomes of these two methods, the long-term survival of patients in these two groups was assessed. Initially, the survival difference between these patients was insignificant within the first two years of surgery [36, 52]. However, after two years, the survival rate in the ViV group was substantially lower than that of the Redo group (HR;2.79, 95%CI: 1.18–7.47) [29]. This study also showed that patients with ViV procedures were at significantly lower risk of need for PPI (13.2% vs. 20.1%, P-Value: <0.01), possibly due to the considerably higher rate of perioperative PPI. In contrast, the incidence rate of HHF was significantly higher during the 5 years in the redo-SAVR group. Additionally, no significant difference was reported between these two groups during the five-year follow-up regarding the incidence rate of stroke, major bleeding, reoperation, and endocarditis [29]. The other study evaluated the survival rate in 5 years and found that the long-term survival was significantly better in the redo-SAVR group compared to the ViV group [30].

Why long-term outcomes after ViV-TAVR may lag behind redo-SAVR?

Several mechanistic and procedural factors likely explain the signal toward worse late outcomes after ViV-TAVR in comparative cohorts. First, ViV-TAVR is prone to higher residual gradients and prosthesis, patient mismatch (PPM) because the transcatheter frame must expand within a fixed surgical ring; severe PPM has been reported in roughly 25–50% of ViV cases and is a known risk factor for late mortality and heart-failure admissions [53, 54]. Second, paravalvular leak (PVL), the “Achilles’ heel” of TAVR, remains more frequent after ViV-TAVR than after redo surgery, which may adversely affect long-term prognosis [52]. Third, anatomic constraints in small surgical valves (≤ 21 mm) limit ViV expansion and may favor a surgical strategy (annular/root enlargement, upsizing) that optimizes effective orifice area for the long term. Device design also matters: supra-annular self-expanding valves can yield post-procedural gradients closer to redo-SAVR, whereas intra-annular balloon-expandable devices tend to leave higher gradients, differences that can translate into late hemodynamic penalties [47, 55]. Beyond valve hemodynamics, ViV-TAVR lacks the opportunity for concomitant surgical corrections (e.g., CABG instead of PCI; repair of moderate–severe MR/TR), which may confer long-term benefit in redo-SAVR cohorts and contribute to divergence of survival curves after ~ 3 years [55]. Finally, ViV carries unique risks of coronary obstruction in certain surgical prostheses (e.g., externally mounted leaflets), and while protective techniques exist, evidence remains limited, factors that can influence adverse events over time.

Consistent with these mechanisms, contemporary population studies observe excess late mortality and heart-failure hospitalization after ViV-TAVR compared with redo-SAVR, even after extensive adjustment; PPM and residual outflow obstruction are specifically cited as plausible drivers of this late risk.

Risk stratification methods (Euroscore II)

Age, comorbidities, and surgical risk stratification significantly impact the outcomes of ViV-TAVR versus redo-SAVR in patients who have degenerated bioprosthetic aortic valves. Older patients and those with multiple comorbidities, such as chronic kidney disease, coronary artery disease, or frailty, tend to have higher surgical risk scores (Society of Thoracic Surgeons [STS] score, Euroscore), making ViV-TAVR a more favorable option due to its minimally invasive nature, lower perioperative morbidity, and shorter recovery time [1, 56]. Conversely, younger, lower-risk patients often experience superior long-term durability with redo-SAVR, which remains the gold standard for extended valve longevity and freedom from structural valve deterioration. Surgical risk stratification tools help guide the optimal approach, with higher-risk patients benefiting more from ViV-TAVR. At the same time, lower-risk individuals may achieve better long-term outcomes with redo-SAVR [44].

The 2021 guidelines from the European Society of Cardiology (ESC) and the European Association for Cardio-Thoracic Surgery (EACTS) offer recommendations for managing degenerated bioprosthetic aortic valves. For patients with high or prohibitive surgical risk, ViV-TAVR is advised as a less invasive alternative to redo-SAVR. Conversely, redo-SAVR remains preferred for low-risk patients due to its long-term durability. The guidelines highlight the importance of an individualized approach, considering age, comorbidities, and anatomical considerations. A thorough assessment by a multidisciplinary heart team is crucial to determining the most suitable treatment strategy for each patient [57, 58].

Financial features, cost-benefit considerations, and accessibility

Several studies have provided insights across different patient demographics and clinical conditions in evaluating the financial aspects and cost-benefit profiles of ViV-TAVR and redo-SAVR. A study analyzing intermediate-risk patients found that, procedural costs were approximately $20,000 higher for TAVR than SAVR, however the total cost difference for the index hospitalization was only $2,888 higher with TAVR, primarily due to reduced length of hospital stay. Over a lifetime horizon, ViV-TAVR imposed lower total costs by $8,000 to $10,000 and to increase quality-adjusted survival by 0.15 to 0.27 years, suggesting that ViV-TAVR might be economically advantageous in this population [59].

However, the cost-effectiveness of ViV-TAVR compared to redo-SAVR can vary based on patient age and surgical risk. TAVR is generally less cost-effective for high and intermediate-risk patients than SAVR, primarily because of the higher device costs and the necessity for subsequent interventions [60]. Additionally, a study found that while ViV-TAVR was linked to better short-term outcomes, including lower rates of major clinical events at 30 days, there was a higher rate of rehospitalization for heart failure in the long term compared to redo-SAVR [61]. These findings suggest that although ViV-TAVR may provide initial cost benefits, particularly in older or higher-risk patients due to shorter hospital stays and faster recovery, redo-SAVR might be more cost-effective in younger, lower-risk patients when factoring in long-term outcomes and the potential need for reintervention.

The accessibility of ViV-TAVR and redo-SAVR varies significantly across different populations and countries, influenced by factors such as healthcare infrastructure, economic resources, and medical expertise. In high-income countries, ViV-TAVR has become increasingly accessible due to advancements in technology and the availability of specialized cardiac centers, offering a less invasive alternative to redo-SAVR for patients who have degenerated bioprosthetic valves. For instance, studies have shown prominent increasing trend of TAVR procedures in younger patients in several countries, reflecting broader acceptance and implementation of this technology [62]. Conversely, in low- and middle-income countries, the adoption of ViV-TAVR is often limited by financial constraints, lack of specialized equipment, and insufficient trained personnel, making redo-SAVR the more prevalent option despite its higher associated risks. The disparity in accessibility is further exacerbated by the high costs of ViV-TAVR devices and procedures, which are less affordable in resource-limited settings. Additionally, healthcare policies and insurance coverage play crucial roles in determining the availability of these procedures to different populations, with some countries prioritizing funding for traditional surgical methods over newer transcatheter techniques [63, 64].

Future directions and research gaps

The current body of evidence comparing ViV and redo-SAVR is primarily derived from observational studies and meta-analyses, which, despite providing valuable insights, are subject to inherent limitations such as selection bias and confounding variables. A meta-analysis demonstrated that ViV was associated with lower 30-day mortality and major bleeding rates than redo-SAVR; however, it also highlighted a higher incidence of severe PPM with ViV [56]. These findings underscore the necessity for more robust comparative data. Designing randomized controlled trials (RCTs) or well-structured prospective studies with PSM would facilitate a more objective assessment of the relative benefits and risks of ViV versus redo-SAVR. Such studies are essential to account for confounding factors and provide high-quality evidence to inform clinical decision-making, particularly in younger, lower-risk patients where the optimal reintervention strategy remains uncertain [65].

Conclusion

Summary of Key Findings

Summary of Key Findings

Summary of Key Findings

ViV-TAVR has emerged as a less invasive alternative with lower short-term mortality, reduced hospital stays, and fewer perioperative complications, making it a preferred option for high-risk and elderly patients. However, concerns remain regarding its long-term durability, higher residual transvalvular gradients, and increased risk of rehospitalization for heart failure beyond two years. While PSM analyses and meta-analyses have demonstrated clear advantages of ViV in short-term outcomes, some studies suggest that redo-SAVR may offer better durability and lower rates of long-term complications, necessitating careful patient selection based on individual risk profiles.

Clinical Implications

Clinical Implications

Clinical Implications

The choice between ViV-TAVR and redo-SAVR for managing degenerated bioprosthetic aortic valves should be individualized based on patient risk, life expectancy, and procedural considerations. Concerns regarding higher residual gradients and long-term durability necessitate careful patient selection for VIV-TAVR. In contrast, redo-SAVR remains the gold standard in these circumstances and offers superior hemodynamic performance and potentially better long-term survival. Future research, including randomized controlled trials and propensity-matched studies, is essential to refine treatment strategies and optimize patient outcomes.

Final Thought

Final Thought

Final Thought

As medical device technology and engineering continue advancing, ViV-TAVR and redo-SAVR are projected to experience significant enhancements, improving procedural safety, efficiency, and long-term outcomes. Innovations in transcatheter valve design, such as lower-profile delivery systems, improved radial expansion, and enhanced leaflet durability, will likely tackle some of the existing limitations of ViV-TAVR, including high transvalvular gradients and risks of coronary obstruction. Similarly, surgical advancements, including minimally invasive techniques, improved myocardial protection, and bioengineered prosthetic valves with greater longevity, will further enhance redo-SAVR outcomes. These innovations will lead to fewer procedural complications, better durability, and improved patient recovery for both treatment methods.

However, despite these technological improvements, the fundamental principles of patient selection for ViV-TAVR versus redo-SAVR are unlikely to change. Treatment choice will continue to depend on individual patient characteristics, including age, comorbidities, surgical risk, and life expectancy. Additionally, financial considerations and healthcare accessibility will remain critical determinants in treatment allocation, particularly in resource-limited settings where access to ViV-TAVR technology may be restricted. While medical advancements will refine both techniques, the core decision-making process will still require a patient-centered approach, integrating demographic, clinical, economic, and logistical factors to ensure the best possible outcomes.
