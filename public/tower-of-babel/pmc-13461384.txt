Incretin-Based Therapies in Obesity-Related Obstructive Sleep Apnea: A Narrative Review of Evidence Tiers and Mechanistic Plausibility
Authors: Xize Zhang, Yuxin Jiang, Xiaotong Wei, Siping Wang, Xiaoyu Zhang, Shaodan Hu, Li Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObesity-related obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is common, clinically heterogeneous, and tightly linked to cardiometabolic dysfunction, impaired daytime function, and reduced quality of life. Positive airway pressure (PAP) remains the standard device-based treatment for moderate-to-severe disease because it immediately stabilizes the upper airway, but it does not directly reverse the obesity that drives disease in many patients and its long-term effectiveness is often constrained by adherence. This narrative, non-systematic review synthesizes direct OSA-specific randomized evidence, indirect obesity-trial evidence, and mechanistic literature to clarify what is established, what is inferred, and what remains hypothetical. The clinical evidence for incretin therapy in OSA is promising but uneven. Direct OSA-specific randomized evidence is concentrated in liraglutide and tirzepatide: SCALE Sleep Apnea established proof of concept that pharmacologic weight loss can improve the primary apnea-hypopnea index (AHI) outcome, whereas in SURMOUNT-OSA change in AHI was the primary endpoint. Reductions in body weight, sleep apnea-specific hypoxic burden, selected patient-reported sleep outcomes, hsCRP, and systolic blood pressure were reported as key secondary or additional secondary findings, supporting a broader disease-burden signal but not proving long-term cardiovascular event reduction. Discordant vascular-imaging evidence further cautions that GLP-1-mediated weight loss should not be assumed to reproduce all vascular effects of PAP or to prove cardiovascular event reduction. Semaglutide provides important indirect obesity and cardiometabolic evidence, but direct OSA-specific outcome data remain limited; evidence from other incretin agents also remains largely indirect because most trials were designed for obesity or cardiometabolic endpoints rather than sleep outcomes. The most parsimonious mechanistic interpretation is weight-loss-mediated anatomical unloading. This interpretation is supported by non-incretin weight-loss imaging studies and upper-airway physiology, but it has not yet been directly confirmed by serial upper-airway imaging, Pcrit measurement, or physiological endotyping within incretin-treated OSA cohorts. This narrative review organizes the field into three evidence tiers: direct OSA-specific randomized trials, indirect obesity-trial evidence relevant to OSA, and mechanistic extrapolation. We argue that the current evidence is strong enough to position incretin therapy as a disease-modifying adjunct for selected patients with obesity-driven, anatomically dominant OSA, but not yet strong enough to support class-wide claims, routine PAP replacement, or confident attribution of benefit to direct modulation of non-anatomical endotypes.

Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a common public health problem whose burden has increased alongside the global rise in obesity.1,2 Obesity is now recognized as the leading modifiable risk factor for moderate-to-severe disease, and obesity-related OSA is particularly important because excess adiposity contributes not only to upper-airway narrowing and collapsibility but also to insulin resistance, hypertension, systemic inflammation, and broader cardiometabolic risk.3–5

The relationship between obesity and OSA is also bidirectional.3,5 While excess adiposity promotes upper-airway narrowing and collapsibility,4 untreated OSA may worsen metabolic dysfunction6,7 through intermittent hypoxia, sleep fragmentation, sympathetic activation, inflammation, insulin resistance, and impaired daytime function.8,9 This reciprocal interaction helps explain why therapies targeting metabolic disease are increasingly relevant to sleep medicine, but it also cautions against assuming that weight loss alone will fully normalize OSA in all patients.

Positive airway pressure (PAP) remains the first-line device-based treatment for moderate-to-severe OSA because it effectively prevents upper-airway collapse during sleep when used adequately.10 Yet PAP does not reverse the upstream biological driver of obesity, and real-world benefit is often limited by imperfect long-term adherence.11 Other options - oral appliances,12,13 lifestyle intervention, exercise,14–16 and bariatric or upper-airway surgery17,18 - are valuable in selected patients but are constrained by access, durability, patient preference, or effect size.

Against this background, incretin-based therapies have generated major interest in sleep medicine. Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists and dual glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP)/GLP-1 receptor agonists produce clinically meaningful weight loss19–21 together with improvements in glycemic and cardiometabolic outcomes.22–24 In OSA, however, the evidence base is narrower than the field’s broad enthusiasm sometimes suggests: the direct OSA-specific randomized data are still centered mainly on liraglutide and tirzepatide,25–27 while much of the wider discussion reflects extrapolation from obesity trials rather than measured sleep outcomes.28,29

Endotype-based models help interpret this field, but they also expose its current limits.30,31 Contemporary pathophysiology conceptualizes OSA as the interaction between upper-airway anatomy and several non-anatomical traits,32 including upper-airway muscle responsiveness, ventilatory control instability, and arousal threshold.33–35 At present, the best-supported interpretation is a weight-loss-mediated, anatomy-dominant effect of incretin therapy, but this remains an extrapolation from non-incretin imaging and physiology36,37 rather than a mechanism directly proven within incretin-treated OSA cohorts. Claims of direct modulation of loop gain, arousal threshold, or neuromuscular compensation38–40 are still mechanistically interesting but clinically premature.41,42

Recent observational evidence43 and evidence syntheses, including systematic reviews and meta-analyses,44–46 have examined incretin-based therapies for OSA-related outcomes. The present review has a different purpose. Rather than re-estimating pooled efficacy, it addresses an interpretive problem that remains unresolved: how direct OSA-specific randomized evidence, indirect obesity-trial evidence, and mechanistic extrapolation should be weighted when applying incretin therapy to obesity-related OSA. The added value of this review is an evidence-tiered, endotype-oriented framework that separates established clinical effects from plausible but unproven mechanisms, and that identifies where current evidence does not yet support class-wide efficacy, routine PAP replacement, or validated endotype-specific drug targeting.

Accordingly, this review takes a deliberately tiered approach. We distinguish direct OSA-specific randomized evidence, indirect obesity-trial evidence that is relevant but not definitive for OSA, and mechanistic extrapolation. Our central viewpoint is that the field is strongest when limited to two restrained claims: pharmacologic weight loss can reduce OSA severity in selected patients, and the most parsimonious current mechanism is weight-loss-mediated anatomical unloading, inferred rather than directly proven in incretin-specific physiological studies. Stronger claims - class-wide efficacy, routine PAP replacement, or validated endotype-specific drug targeting - are not yet supported.

Scope and Approach of This Narrative Review

This article was developed as a narrative review with structured source selection, not as a formal systematic review. We did not aim to provide an exhaustive census of every publication, a PRISMA-style study flow, or a formal risk-of-bias assessment. Instead, the review was designed to answer an interpretive clinical question: what can currently be said with confidence about incretin therapy in obesity-related OSA, and what remains indirect or hypothetical?47,48

A formal systematic review was not undertaken because the aim was not to provide an exhaustive pooled estimate of treatment efficacy, but to develop a clinically interpretable evidence hierarchy across direct OSA trials, indirect obesity trials, and mechanistic physiology. To improve transparency, searches combined free-text terms and controlled vocabulary where available, including “obstructive sleep apnea”, “obstructive sleep apnoea”, “sleep-disordered breathing”, “OSA”, “sleep apnea syndromes”, “GLP-1 receptor agonist”, “liraglutide”, “semaglutide,” “tirzepatide,” “incretin,” “hypoxic burden,” “loop gain,” “arousal threshold”, and “upper-airway collapsibility”.

To support that aim, we used targeted searches of PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, and Scopus, screened reference lists of major reviews and original studies, and checked ClinicalTrials.gov for pivotal OSA-specific incretin programs through March 2026.26,27 Search terms covered OSA, obesity and regional adiposity, incretin pharmacotherapy, and endotype-related physiology. These searches informed selection rather than serving as a fully reproducible systematic-review workflow.47,48

We prioritized three evidence tiers: first, randomized OSA-specific intervention trials of incretin-based therapy; second, high-quality obesity and weight-loss studies that inform OSA interpretation without directly measuring sleep outcomes; and third, physiological and imaging studies used to evaluate mechanistic plausibility. This structure is intentional: it helps prevent indirect evidence from being presented with the same certainty as direct OSA-specific data.

Endpoint status is specified in the clinical sections and tables to avoid giving all trial findings the same inferential weight. The primary endpoint denotes the main outcome used to evaluate trial efficacy. A multiplicity-controlled key secondary endpoint denotes a prespecified secondary outcome included in the trial’s multiplicity-control strategy. Additional secondary outcomes are prespecified outcomes outside that hierarchy, whereas post hoc/exploratory analyses have lower inferential status. This distinction is especially important for SURMOUNT-OSA, because cardiometabolic-risk-marker and mechanistic analyses can support a broader disease-burden signal but cannot by themselves prove long-term cardiovascular event reduction.

Clinical Evidence by Evidence Tier in Obesity-Related OSA

Indirect Precedent: Weight Loss Biology and Non-Pharmacologic OSA Improvement

The rationale for incretin therapy in obesity-related OSA did not emerge in isolation. Longitudinal and interventional studies consistently show that OSA severity tracks with body weight: moderate weight gain worsens sleep-disordered breathing, whereas weight loss is associated with lower AHI,49–54 less symptom burden, and a reduced likelihood of persistent moderate-to-severe disease.15 Randomized lifestyle trials such as Sleep AHEAD55,56 and INTERAPNEA reinforce the principle that intentional weight reduction can improve OSA, even though complete remission is uncommon and residual disease often persists.

Two lifestyle-trial examples are especially informative. Sleep AHEAD showed that intensive lifestyle intervention in adults with obesity and type 2 diabetes produced sustained weight loss and clinically meaningful reductions in AHI, although many participants had residual OSA over long-term follow-up.51,55,56 INTERAPNEA similarly showed that an interdisciplinary weight-loss and lifestyle intervention improved OSA severity and symptoms in adults with overweight or obesity.15 These trials are important because they demonstrate that weight loss can modify OSA, but they also show that weight loss does not produce uniform remission.

These earlier studies established two points that remain essential in the incretin era. First, obesity treatment can modify OSA severity.15,50 Second, the relationship between weight loss and respiratory improvement is strong but not deterministic. Patients with similar amounts of weight loss may experience very different changes in AHI,57,58 which is consistent with the view that anatomical improvement interacts with non-anatomical physiological traits.30,31,59

That background matters because it defines what broader obesity trials can and cannot contribute. Large semaglutide and tirzepatide trials make major respiratory benefit plausible by demonstrating substantial weight loss19–21 and cardiometabolic improvement,23,24 and maintenance studies highlight the likely importance of continued treatment.60,61 However, those studies were not OSA trials, so they strengthen biological plausibility rather than directly proving sleep-apnea benefit.

Direct OSA-Specific Randomized Evidence: Liraglutide as Proof of Concept

The SCALE Sleep Apnea trial remains the key direct randomized trial for liraglutide in OSA.25 It enrolled adults without type 2 diabetes who had obesity and moderate-to-severe OSA and who were unwilling or unable to use CPAP, and randomly assigned 180 participants to liraglutide 3.0 mg plus lifestyle intervention and 179 to placebo plus lifestyle intervention. Baseline respiratory and obesity burden were high, with a mean AHI of 49.2 events/h and BMI of 39.1 kg/m2. The primary endpoint was change in AHI at 32 weeks. Liraglutide reduced AHI by −12.2 events/h, compared with −6.1 events/h with placebo, giving an estimated treatment difference of −6.1 events/h. Body weight, an additional secondary outcome, decreased by −5.7% versus −1.6%, respectively, for an estimated treatment difference of −4.2%.

These effect sizes support a proof-of-concept interpretation rather than a remission claim. Additional secondary/exploratory categorical outcomes showed that AHI <5 events/h occurred in 5.4% versus 1.2% of participants (P=0.07), and AHI reduction of at least 50% occurred in 31.5% versus 21.7% (P=0.05).25 Thus, SCALE demonstrated that pharmacologic weight loss can shift a core sleep outcome, but residual OSA remained common, no active PAP comparator was included, and the trial should not be read as evidence for routine PAP replacement10 or class-wide incretin efficacy.

Direct OSA-Specific Randomized Evidence: Tirzepatide as the Current Evidentiary Standard

The SURMOUNT-OSA program provides the largest and most quantitatively informative direct evidence.26,27 It consisted of two 52-week Phase 3, double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled trials of tirzepatide at the maximum tolerated dose (10 mg or 15 mg) plus diet and physical activity counseling in adults with obesity and moderate-to-severe OSA without type 2 diabetes. Trial 1 enrolled participants not receiving PAP at baseline (tirzepatide n=114; placebo n=120; baseline mean AHI 51.5 events/h; BMI 39.1 kg/m2), whereas Trial 2 enrolled participants using PAP at baseline (tirzepatide n=120; placebo n=115; baseline mean AHI 49.5 events/h; BMI 38.7 kg/m2). The primary endpoint was change in AHI from baseline to week 52.

At 52 weeks, the primary endpoint showed an AHI change of −25.3 events/h with tirzepatide versus −5.3 events/h with placebo in Trial 1 (between-group difference, −20.0 events/h), and −29.3 versus −5.5 events/h in Trial 2 (difference, −23.8 events/h).26 Key secondary results controlled for multiplicity also favored tirzepatide for percentage AHI change and body-weight change: −50.7% versus −3.0% for AHI and −17.7% versus −1.6% for body weight in Trial 1, and −58.7% versus −2.5% for AHI and −19.6% versus −2.3% for body weight in Trial 2. Key secondary and other trial-defined categorical outcomes were clinically meaningful but should still be interpreted in context: at least 50% AHI reduction occurred in 61.2% versus 19.0% in Trial 1 and 72.4% versus 23.3% in Trial 2, while AHI <5 events/h or AHI 5–14 events/h with Epworth Sleepiness Scale ≤10 occurred in 42.2% versus 15.9% and 50.2% versus 14.3%, respectively.28,29 The trial designs and quantitative efficacy outcomes are summarized in Tables 1 and 2. Key secondary and additional secondary findings broadened the interpretation beyond event counts. Tirzepatide reduced sleep apnea-specific hypoxic burden by −95.2 versus −25.1%-min/h in Trial 1 and −103.0 versus −41.7%-min/h in Trial 2, and was associated with greater reductions in hsCRP (−1.4 versus −0.7 mg/L in Trial 1; −1.4 versus −0.3 mg/L in Trial 2) and systolic blood pressure (−9.5 versus −1.8 mmHg in Trial 1; −7.6 versus −3.9 mmHg in Trial 2), together with selected patient-reported sleep outcomes.26,28 The subsequent cardiometabolic-risk report should be read within the same endpoint hierarchy: it was a prespecified secondary analysis and included post hoc/exploratory components for selected metabolic and mediation questions. These findings support a broader disease-burden and risk-marker signal, but they do not prove reductions in myocardial infarction, stroke, or other hard cardiovascular outcomes. Boundary conditions remain important: the trials excluded type 2 diabetes,29 did not compare tirzepatide against PAP,14 bariatric surgery,62–64 or other active OSA treatments, did not define when PAP can be withdrawn, and did not provide post-discontinuation OSA relapse data.

What Broader Obesity and Cardiometabolic Trial Data Add - and Cannot Add - to OSA Interpretation

The broader incretin literature is clinically relevant because OSA rarely exists in isolation.5–7 Patients with obesity-related OSA often have insulin resistance, hypertension, visceral adiposity, and low-grade inflammation,65–67 so the ability of incretin therapy to improve weight and cardiometabolic risk factors may matter even when respiratory endpoints are not fully normalized.24

However, broader obesity-trial data should not be mistaken for direct OSA evidence. Semaglutide68,69 and tirzepatide19,21,23 obesity trials support durability during continued treatment and raise concern about weight regain after withdrawal,22 but they do not resolve the magnitude or persistence of OSA-specific benefit, especially in populations underrepresented in SURMOUNT-OSA such as patients with type 2 diabetes26 or milder disease.45,46,70 The evidence base therefore remains a gradient: strongest for tirzepatide, proof-of-concept for liraglutide, and indirect for much of the rest of the incretin class.

Within the OSA-specific trials themselves, AHI should first be interpreted according to its prespecified endpoint status. In SURMOUNT-OSA, change in AHI was the primary endpoint,26 whereas hypoxic burden, oxygenation, symptoms, blood pressure, inflammatory markers, and patient-centered sleep outcomes were key secondary, additional secondary, or post hoc/exploratory findings depending on the original analysis plan.28 This hierarchy allows SURMOUNT-OSA to move the field beyond an AHI-only frame66,67 while avoiding the mistaken impression that secondary cardiometabolic signals prove long-term cardiovascular event reduction.71–73

Several methodological caveats are therefore important when interpreting these outcomes. Sleep apnea-specific hypoxic burden is not simply another event-frequency measure; it quantifies the cumulative respiratory-event-associated oxygen desaturation burden, typically calculated as the area under event-linked desaturation curves for scored apneas or hypopneas and indexed per hour of sleep or recording time,66,67 so its value depends on oximetry signal quality, respiratory-event scoring, event-desaturation linking, and the analytic assumptions used in the source trial.26,28 Similarly, hsCRP should be interpreted as a nonspecific inflammatory risk marker rather than a validated treatment-selection biomarker for incretin-based OSA care,73 because values may vary by assay, population, intercurrent illness, adiposity, and repeated-measure variability, and no standardized inflammatory-marker cut point has been validated to guide incretin initiation, continuation, or PAP de-escalation in OSA.

Discordant Vascular Evidence and the Limits of Metabolic Inference

A discordant proof-of-concept vascular-imaging trial deserves explicit attention because it tests the assumption that metabolic weight loss and AHI improvement necessarily translate into vascular improvement.74 O’Donnell et al randomized adults with moderate-to-severe OSA and early cardiovascular disease markers to 24 weeks of CPAP, liraglutide-mediated weight loss, or combined therapy. CPAP alone and combined therapy produced larger AHI reductions than liraglutide alone, whereas liraglutide and combined therapy produced body-weight loss. Vascular imaging showed that vascular inflammation, measured as target-to-background ratio (TBR), decreased significantly with CPAP alone but not with liraglutide-mediated weight loss; low-attenuation plaque volume decreased in the CPAP and combined-treatment groups but not in the liraglutide-alone group. The authors concluded that CPAP, rather than GLP-1-mediated weight loss alone, improved vascular inflammation and unstable plaque volume, while emphasizing that larger randomized trials are needed.

This discordant finding is important because it prevents a simple substitution narrative. Pharmacologic weight loss may reduce AHI and improve metabolic risk markers, but immediate nocturnal airway stabilization and reduction of intermittent hypoxia through PAP may remain necessary for some vascular pathways. Therefore, improvement in weight, AHI, or risk markers should not be assumed to translate automatically into improvement in early cardiovascular disease markers or hard cardiovascular outcomes.

At the same time, this discordant result should not be interpreted as a broad cardiovascular endorsement of PAP or a dismissal of incretin therapy. Randomized trials of CPAP in patients with established cardiovascular disease75–77 or minimally sleepy OSA78,79 have not consistently demonstrated reductions in hard cardiovascular endpoints, likely reflecting differences in adherence, patient selection, phenotype, and trial design. Conversely, incretin therapy has strong obesity and cardiometabolic benefits in non-OSA populations,22–24 but OSA-specific evidence linking respiratory improvement to vascular or event reduction remains limited.28 The current evidence therefore supports complementary rather than competitive treatment logic: PAP targets nocturnal obstruction immediately, whereas incretin therapy targets obesity and cardiometabolic substrate over time.

Safety, Tolerability, and Practical Limitations

Interpretation of efficacy should be balanced with tolerability and implementation. The most common adverse effects of GLP-1 receptor agonists and dual GIP/GLP-1 receptor agonists are gastrointestinal, including nausea, bloating, reflux, abdominal discomfort, and delayed gastric emptying, particularly during dose escalation.29,80 These events are often manageable, but they matter greatly in a chronic disorder such as OSA, where treatment persistence is central to real-world effectiveness.26

In real-world OSA populations, incretin therapy will often be used alongside antihypertensive, lipid-lowering, antidiabetic, psychiatric, or sleep-related medications. Clinically relevant considerations include gastrointestinal adverse effects during dose escalation,80 dehydration risk in susceptible patients, hypoglycemia risk when combined with insulin or insulin secretagogues, delayed gastric emptying that may affect absorption of selected oral drugs,29 and confounding of sleepiness, fatigue, nausea, reflux, or appetite changes when sedatives, antidepressants, PAP-related aerophagia, and weight-loss therapy coexist.81

These considerations are not intended as prescribing guidance, but they indicate why safety assessment in OSA should include the full medication list, PAP tolerance, hydration status, glycemic therapy, and symptom attribution during dose escalation.29,80,81 The chronic nature of OSA also means that tolerability and persistence can be as important as short-term efficacy.

Real-World Implementation: Cost, Access, Persistence, and Withdrawal

Real-world implementation is likely to be determined by affordability, insurance coverage, treatment persistence, and access to longitudinal sleep follow-up as much as by trial efficacy. Drug price and coverage restrictions may limit who can receive long-term therapy, and obesity pharmacotherapy often requires ongoing treatment to preserve weight loss.22,23 Withdrawal studies from broader obesity trials show clinically meaningful weight regain after discontinuation, which may weaken OSA benefit, but OSA-specific withdrawal studies remain lacking. SURMOUNT-OSA provides 52-week OSA-specific data but does not define longer-term durability, post-discontinuation relapse, or long-term cardiovascular outcome effects.26,28,29

Clinically, weight loss should prompt reassessment rather than automatic PAP discontinuation. Repeat objective sleep testing remains important before PAP withdrawal, especially in patients with severe sleepiness, marked hypoxemia, high baseline respiratory burden, safety-sensitive driving or occupational duties, or persistent symptoms.10,71 Future studies should define when medication dose reduction, treatment discontinuation, PAP pressure adjustment, or PAP withdrawal can be attempted safely.26,29

Mechanistic Interpretation Through OSA Endotypes

Best-Supported Extrapolation: Anatomical Unloading, Tongue Fat, and Upper-Airway Collapsibility

The most parsimonious mechanistic interpretation is weight-loss-mediated anatomical unloading. This interpretation is supported by non-incretin weight-loss imaging studies and upper-airway physiology,36,82 but it has not yet been directly confirmed by serial upper-airway imaging, Pcrit measurement, or physiological endotyping within incretin-treated OSA cohorts. Obesity can narrow the pharyngeal airway through regional fat deposition around the lateral pharyngeal walls and within the tongue,4,83 and magnetic resonance imaging studies of non-incretin weight loss show that reduced tongue fat can mediate AHI improvement.84,85

For incretin therapy, these observations are best viewed as mechanistic extrapolation. Modern agents, especially tirzepatide, produce substantial body-weight reduction19,21,23 and improve OSA outcomes,26 making tissue unloading plausible;36,37,86 however, the trials did not determine whether changes in tongue fat, parapharyngeal fat, lung volume, or Pcrit mediated the observed AHI reductions.

Indirect Supporting Physiology: Lung Volume and Caudal Traction

Lung-volume effects provide a second weight-mediated but indirect pathway. Reduced abdominal or truncal adiposity may increase functional residual capacity and end-expiratory lung volume, enhancing caudal traction on the pharyngeal airway and reducing collapsibility;32,87 prospective lung-volume measurements during incretin therapy are still lacking.26

Speculative but Testable Hypothesis: Upper-Airway Muscle Responsiveness

Upper-airway muscle responsiveness remains a testable hypothesis rather than an established incretin mechanism.30,31,41 Metabolic improvement could plausibly influence pharyngeal neuromechanics in a setting of insulin resistance, intermittent hypoxia, altered adipokine signaling, and inflammation,8,9 but no published incretin trial has prospectively measured genioglossus activity, pharyngeal dilator recruitment, or muscle-responsiveness phenotypes.

Speculative but Testable Hypothesis: Ventilatory Control Instability (Loop Gain)

Effects on ventilatory control instability are also speculative. Weight loss can reduce loop gain in some patients,37 and GLP-1 signaling has biological links to brainstem and vagal pathways involved in autonomic and respiratory integration,40 but human OSA trials of incretin therapy have not directly measured loop gain33,88 or separated central ventilatory effects from weight-mediated structural change.30,31

Speculative but Testable Hypothesis: Arousal Threshold, Neuroinflammation, and Sleep Fragmentation

Arousal threshold, neuroinflammation, and sleep fragmentation are biologically plausible but unproven pathways. Recurrent intermittent hypoxia and sleep fragmentation can contribute to neural stress and inflammatory signaling,8,9 and GLP-1 signaling has been linked to neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory effects;38–40 however, existing OSA trials did not measure arousal threshold,34 sleep-stage continuity, or neurophysiological sleep-fragmentation markers as mechanistic endpoints.42

Why the Evidence Hierarchy Matters Clinically

Taken together, the mechanistic hierarchy should be read as a hierarchy of plausibility, not proof. Weight-loss-mediated anatomical unloading is the best-supported extrapolation;36,37 lung-volume effects have indirect physiological support;32,87 and direct effects on upper-airway muscle responsiveness, loop gain, and arousal threshold remain hypotheses.30,31,42 A schematic summary of these relationships is provided in Figure 1. Clinical Integration: What Is Evidence-Based Now and What Remains Interpretive

Evidence-Based Now: Pharmacotherapy Complements Rather Than Replaces PAP

The emergence of incretin-based pharmacotherapy should be interpreted as an expansion of the OSA treatment armamentarium, not as a reason to abandon established therapies. PAP remains the most effective intervention for immediate splinting of the upper airway and rapid reduction of respiratory events when used adequately.10,71 Pharmacotherapy, by contrast, acts more slowly and primarily targets the upstream biological driver of obesity rather than providing instant nocturnal airway stabilization.

These differences, together with discordant vascular-imaging evidence, suggest complementarity rather than competition.74 PAP can immediately control nocturnal obstruction and intermittent hypoxia, whereas incretin therapy has direct trial support for improvement in the primary AHI outcome and secondary support for body weight, hypoxic burden, selected patient-reported outcomes, hsCRP, and systolic blood pressure when those outcomes are interpreted according to their endpoint status.26,28 The O’Donnell proof-of-concept trial reinforces this distinction: CPAP improved early vascular inflammation and unstable plaque markers when liraglutide-mediated weight loss alone did not, although larger trials are needed and CPAP cardiovascular-outcome evidence is itself heterogeneous.79,90,91 These secondary disease-burden signals complement PAP but should not be interpreted as proof that incretin therapy prevents myocardial infarction, stroke, or cardiovascular death. The evidence-based conclusion at present is therefore adjunctive integration, not routine substitution.10,71

Evidence-Based Reassessment versus Speculative PAP De-Escalation

Substantial weight loss can alter upper-airway anatomy and lower the pressure required for effective PAP therapy.36,86 If these changes are not recognized, patients may experience discomfort, aerophagia, mask leak, or reduced adherence.81 For this reason, reassessment of PAP requirements during marked weight loss is evidence-informed and clinically sensible.87,92

What remains less certain is the threshold at which PAP can be safely reduced or withdrawn. Auto-adjusting PAP and device-download data may help during transition, but formal reassessment with repeat objective sleep testing remains important when symptoms, residual events, or treatment tolerance change.10,71 Weight loss alone should not be taken as sufficient evidence that PAP can be discontinued.26,29

Practical Tolerability Issues in Combination Therapy

Combination therapy may introduce intersecting tolerability challenges. PAP can contribute to aerophagia and upper gastrointestinal discomfort in susceptible patients,81 whereas incretin therapy commonly causes nausea, bloating, reflux, or early satiety during dose escalation.80 These overlapping symptoms are clinically real even though direct OSA-specific combination-therapy evidence is limited.

In practice, symptom attribution is often uncertain: reflux, bloating, early satiety, nausea, abdominal discomfort, sleep disruption, or fatigue may reflect PAP-related aerophagia,81 drug-related gastrointestinal effects, rapid dietary change, weight loss itself, or concomitant medications.29,80

Accordingly, recommendations in this area are partly expert-interpretive. Slower pharmacologic titration when needed, careful PAP pressure optimization, use of expiratory pressure relief in selected cases, and explicit counseling that PAP-related aerophagia and drug-related gastrointestinal symptoms may overlap are reasonable practical strategies, but they have not yet been validated in dedicated OSA pharmacotherapy trials.

Likely Responder Phenotypes: Plausible, Useful, but Not yet Validated

The growing recognition of OSA heterogeneity makes phenotype-guided care especially relevant in the incretin era.30,31,35 Patients with clearly obesity-driven, anatomically dominant OSA and high cardiometabolic risk are the most plausible candidates for integrated metabolic and sleep-focused treatment.57–59 By contrast, patients with severe sleepiness, marked hypoxemia, safety-sensitive occupations, or strong evidence of non-anatomical trait burden may still require PAP-centered management from the outset, with pharmacotherapy added as a disease-modifying adjunct.93

This framework is clinically useful, but it should be presented honestly as an interpretive scaffold rather than a validated responder algorithm.89 Weight reduction may also improve the feasibility of oral appliances,94 exercise-based strategies, bariatric surgery,62–64 upper-airway stimulation, or multilevel surgery95,96 in selected patients, yet prospective trials that formally sequence these options with incretin therapy are still needed. Decisive Evidence Gaps and Research Priorities

Despite recent progress, the next phase of research should be judged by whether it produces management-changing evidence rather than simply confirming that pharmacologic weight loss can improve OSA.25,26 Recent observational evidence43 and meta-analyses44–46 are supportive, but the evidence base still includes a small number of heterogeneous studies.

First, generalizability: future trials should test incretin therapy in populations that remain underrepresented in the current evidence base.70 The pivotal tirzepatide trials focused on adults with obesity and moderate-to-severe OSA and excluded patients with type 2 diabetes,26,29 even though diabetes and OSA frequently coexist in practice.6 Evidence is also sparse for milder OSA, lower body mass index, complex multimorbidity, and OSA not primarily driven by obesity.45,46

Second, mechanistic confirmation: future trials should determine whether anatomical and non-anatomical traits actually change during incretin therapy.26 The most useful designs would embed serial upper-airway MRI measures of tongue fat and parapharyngeal fat,36,82 Pcrit, loop gain, arousal threshold, and genioglossus electromyography or other measures of pharyngeal muscle responsiveness,30,31,37 rather than relying on post hoc inference from weight loss and AHI change alone.87

Third, durability and withdrawal: studies should define what happens to OSA during dose reduction, treatment discontinuation, and weight regain. Obesity trials show that ongoing treatment preserves weight loss better than discontinuation, whereas withdrawal is followed by clinically meaningful regain.22,23 The corresponding trajectory of OSA relapse after dose reduction or cessation remains unknown, so maintenance phases, withdrawal designs, and repeat objective sleep testing are needed to define the durability of respiratory benefit and the safety of PAP de-escalation.26,29

Fourth, treatment sequencing: future studies should test how incretin therapy should be ordered or combined with PAP,10 oral appliances, exercise,13,16 bariatric surgery, upper-airway stimulation, and upper-airway surgery.17,18 They should also define objective criteria for medication dose reduction, treatment discontinuation, PAP pressure adjustment, or PAP withdrawal after substantial pharmacologic weight loss.14,50

Fifth, clinically anchored biomarkers: biomarker and multi-omics studies should be designed around clinically meaningful outcomes. For example, inflammatory proteins, metabolomic signatures, adipokines, transcriptomic profiles, or microbiome features97–99 should be linked prospectively to changes in AHI, sleep apnea-specific hypoxic burden, oxygenation, sleepiness, blood pressure, glycemic control,65–67 cardiovascular events,72 PAP adherence, and patient-reported quality of life. Such designs would be more informative than omics profiling in isolation and would help determine when a biomarker changes management rather than simply describing biology.

Conclusion

Incretin-based pharmacotherapy has reframed the management discussion in obesity-related OSA, but the evidence base should be described with precision. Liraglutide showed that targeted medical weight loss can improve OSA severity,25 and tirzepatide has since supplied the first dedicated phase 3 data set for a medication positioned directly within OSA care pathways.26,27,100 The field should resist the temptation to convert that progress into class-wide claims that run ahead of the evidence.28,29

The most parsimonious mechanistic interpretation is that substantial weight loss relieves upper-airway mechanical loading and thereby drives much of the observed benefit.36,86,92 However, this is an inference from non-incretin imaging and physiology plus OSA trial efficacy,26,28 not a mechanism directly confirmed by serial upper-airway imaging or Pcrit measurement in incretin trials. In SURMOUNT-OSA, AHI improvement was the primary outcome, while concurrent improvements in hypoxic burden, blood pressure, inflammatory markers, and metabolic risk were reported as key secondary, additional secondary, or post hoc/exploratory findings depending on the analysis. These results suggest a broader disease-burden signal rather than merely lower event counts, but they do not demonstrate fewer myocardial infarctions, strokes, or cardiovascular deaths. Discordant vascular-imaging data showing CPAP, but not GLP-1-mediated weight loss alone, improved early vascular inflammation and unstable plaque markers further caution against using AHI or weight loss as automatic surrogates for vascular protection.74 Proposed direct effects on loop gain, arousal threshold, or upper-airway muscle compensation remain biologically plausible30,31,34 but unconfirmed in humans.38–40

In practice, incretin-based therapies are best positioned as part of a multimodal strategy that may still require PAP,10 lifestyle intervention, oral appliance therapy, exercise,13,14,16 or surgery17,18 depending on residual OSA, symptoms, and phenotype.50 The decisive next step is to define which patients benefit most, how durable respiratory improvement remains during dose reduction or withdrawal, and what objective sleep-testing criteria should guide PAP adjustment or de-escalation after substantial pharmacologic weight loss.
