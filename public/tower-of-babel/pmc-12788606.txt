Role of Cone Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT) in the Comprehensive Evaluation and Management Planning of Temporomandibular Joint Disorders
Authors: Bassam Alkhalifah, Mohammed S. Aldamegh, Abdulaziz Algharras, Ali Albweady, Ankit Dhimole, Sameer Gupta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Temporomandibular joint disorders (TMDs) encompass a range of functional and structural pathologies affecting the temporomandibular joint (TMJ), leading to pain, restricted movement, and quality-of-life impairment. Traditional imaging methods have limitations in providing accurate 3D assessments. Cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) offers high-resolution imaging with minimal radiation exposure.Methods:A cross-sectional observational study was conducted on 60 patients (aged 18–50 years; mean age 31.8 ± 9.2 years) clinically diagnosed with TMD. CBCT scans were evaluated for condylar morphology, joint space, osteoarthritic changes, and disc displacement indicators. Clinical symptoms were correlated with CBCT findings to plan targeted interventions. Data were analyzed using SPSS v26.0, with significance set at P < 0.05.Results:CBCT detected condylar erosion in 46.7% of patients, flattening in 38.3%, and osteophyte formation in 21.7%. The joint space asymmetry was present in 53.3% of cases. Patients with radiological signs of osteoarthrosis had significantly higher pain scores (VAS ≥ 6) compared to those without (P = 0.012). CBCT findings altered the initial management plan in 41.7% of cases.Conclusion:CBCT provides detailed structural visualization of the TMJ, allowing accurate diagnosis and more precise management planning of TMDs. Its use is recommended in patients with ambiguous clinical signs or chronic symptoms.

INTRODUCTION

Temporomandibular joint disorders (TMDs) are a heterogeneous group of conditions involving the temporomandibular joint (TMJ), masticatory muscles, and associated structures, resulting in symptoms such as joint pain, limited mouth opening, clicking, and deviation.[1] The global prevalence of TMDs ranges from 5% to 12% of the general population, with a higher incidence in females between 20 and 40 years of age.[2]

Accurate diagnosis of TMD is essential for effective management. Conventional radiographs, such as panoramic and transcranial views, are limited by their two-dimensional nature and low diagnostic yield for joint space analysis and bony architecture. Advanced imaging modalities, such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), are considered the gold standard for soft tissue evaluation, particularly disc position, while computed tomography (CT) provides excellent bone detail. However, MRI is expensive and less accessible, and conventional CT entails high radiation doses.[3]

Cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) has emerged as a superior modality in TMJ imaging due to its ability to provide high-resolution, three-dimensional images of osseous structures at a lower radiation dose compared to medical CT.[4] CBCT facilitates the assessment of condylar morphology, joint space variation, bone degenerative changes, and symmetry—parameters crucial in TMD diagnosis and treatment planning.[5]

Despite these advantages, CBCT is underutilized in routine TMD evaluation due to cost, accessibility, and lack of standard diagnostic protocols. Current literature highlights its utility in orthodontic and implant planning, but fewer studies focus on its diagnostic and management role in TMD.[6]

CBCT imaging protocol

All patients underwent bilateral TMJ CBCT imaging using a standard protocol (90 kVp, 10 mA, 0.3 mm voxel size, FOV: 8 × 8 cm) with the patient’s mouth in closed and open positions. Scans were reviewed by two calibrated oral radiologists blinded to clinical symptoms.

Data collection and analysis

Pain was assessed using a Visual Analog Scale (VAS: 0–10). Correlation of clinical findings with radiological signs was established. Descriptive statistics (mean, SD, proportions) were calculated. Chi-square test and independent t-test were applied for group comparisons. A P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

DISCUSSION

The present study highlights the pivotal role of CBCT in detecting osseous changes in the TMJ, thereby facilitating more accurate diagnosis and improved management of TMD. Our findings revealed condylar erosion in nearly half of the patients, consistent with the degenerative joint changes reported in prior studies.[7]

The superiority of CBCT in visualizing bony alterations, such as flattening, osteophyte formation, and subcortical sclerosis, aligns with the conclusions of Zeng et al., who noted CBCT’s efficacy in assessing cortical bone and joint morphology.[8] Furthermore, our study supports the work of Etöz et al., where joint space narrowing and asymmetry correlated strongly with chronic joint pain.[9]

In this study, over 40% of patients experienced a change in clinical management following CBCT imaging. This agrees with findings from Kuribayashi et al., who documented that CBCT frequently alters therapeutic decisions, particularly in patients with ambiguous clinical findings.[10]

Joint asymmetry and flattening of the articular eminence are subtle findings often missed on conventional radiographs but may influence the biomechanical behavior of the TMJ. These findings are well-documented by Pertes and colleagues, who emphasized the impact of condylar remodeling on joint dysfunction.[1]

Although MRI remains the gold standard for soft tissue evaluation, its limited availability and cost make CBCT a more practical modality, especially for bone-related assessment. However, it is essential to recognize that CBCT cannot visualize the disc, which may necessitate adjunct MRI evaluation in certain cases.[2]

Limitations of this study include the cross-sectional design, which restricts causal inferences, and the absence of long-term outcome tracking following intervention planning. Future research should focus on CBCT’s role in evaluating treatment efficacy and integrating AI-based interpretation for standardized reporting.

CONCLUSION

CBCT is a valuable diagnostic tool in the comprehensive evaluation of temporomandibular joint disorders, offering high-resolution visualization of osseous changes with minimal radiation exposure. Its use significantly enhances diagnostic accuracy and alters treatment planning, especially in chronic or atypical TMD cases. Incorporating CBCT in routine TMJ assessments can lead to more individualized and effective patient care.
