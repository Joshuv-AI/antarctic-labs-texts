Effects of transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation on cognitive function and upper limb motor function in people with chronic stroke: a study protocol for a randomised controlled trial
Authors: Nga Huen Chan, Shamay S M Ng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCognitive impairment and upper limb impairment are common complications following stroke, but rehabilitation interventions targeting post-stroke cognitive and upper limb motor deficits remain understudied. Transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation, including transcutaneous vagus nerve stimulation (tVNS) and transcutaneous spinal cord stimulation (tSCS), can potentially alleviate cognitive impairment and promote motor recovery. Although tVNS and tSCS may facilitate neuroplasticity following stroke, their effects on cognitive and upper limb motor outcomes have not been sufficiently studied. Therefore, the proposed clinical trial will investigate the effects of tVNS and tSCS on cognitive function and upper limb motor function in people with chronic stroke.Methods and analysisA three-arm sham-controlled single-blind parallel-group randomised controlled trial will be performed with people with chronic stroke. All participants (n=90) will be randomly allocated to receive 18 sessions of intervention with different stimulation protocols (tVNS, tSCS and sham stimulation) at a 1:1:1 ratio. All participants will receive upper limb exercises concurrent with the corresponding stimulation protocols for 45 min in each session, three sessions per week, for 6 weeks. The primary outcomes will be the Montreal Cognitive Assessment and Fugl-Meyer Assessment for Upper Extremity. The secondary outcomes will be the Rivermead Behavioural Memory Test, Digit Span Test, Trail Making Test, Wolf Motor Function Test, maximum isometric voluntary contraction of the elbow, muscle stiffness of the elbow extensor and flexor, Arm Activity Measure, Oxford Participation and Activity Questionnaire and 12-item Short-Form Survey. All outcomes will be assessed at baseline, after 9 and 18 sessions of the intervention, and 1 month after the cessation of the intervention.Ethics and disseminationEthical approval has been obtained from the Human Subjects Ethics Committee of The Hong Kong Polytechnic University (HSEARS20221011002). The results yielded from this study will be submitted for publication in peer-reviewed journals and at international conferences.Trial registration numberNCT05615610.Keywords: Stroke, Cognition, Randomized Controlled Trial, Electric Stimulation Therapy

Introduction

Stroke is one of the leading causes of impairment and disability in adults, with a high global incidence of 11.9 million and prevalence of 93.8 million in 2021.1 Cognitive impairment and motor impairment are common consequences following a stroke. Post-stroke cognitive impairment occurs in 44% of stroke survivors,2 while upper limb motor impairment is prevalent in 40%–50% of stroke cases in the chronic phase.3 4

Cognitive impairment following stroke may include declines in memory, attention, language and orientation.5 Notable deficits in episodic memory, executive function and processing speed are commonly reported after stroke.6 Post-stroke cognitive impairment is significantly associated with activity limitations and participation restrictions (r=0.37, p<0.001)7 and poor quality of life (r=0.23, p<0.001).8 A recent systematic review of 64 randomised controlled trials with 4005 stroke survivors revealed that multiple-component interventions combining two or more types of interventions, particularly those including physical activity, had a favourable effect on global cognition (mean difference (MD)=1.56, p<0.001) and memory (MD=0.49, p<0.001) after stroke.9 However, multiple-component interventions showed weak clinically meaningful effects on cognition in people with stroke.9 It remains an avenue for future research to explore potential interventions with long-term and clinically meaningful effects for improving cognitive function after stroke.

Upper limb motor impairment is another common complication following stroke, including muscle weakness, spasticity and loss of dexterous movement.10 Such impairments greatly influence the performance of activities of daily living (r=0.74, p<0.001)11 and quality of life (β=0.16, p=0.001) after stroke.12 Various therapeutic interventions, such as neuromuscular electrical stimulation, non-invasive brain stimulation and virtual reality rehabilitation, have been developed to improve upper limb motor function by promoting cortical reorganisation following stroke, yet restoration of paretic arm function remains challenging.13 Interventions for enhancing upper limb motor recovery are imperative for facilitating independence in activities of daily living and social participation of people with stroke.

Disrupted neurotransmitter circuits have been commonly observed and found to correlate with cognitive outcomes after stroke.14 Neurotransmitter systems, particularly those involving acetylcholine, dopamine, gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) and norepinephrine, may play roles in neural plasticity and recovery following stroke by facilitating the induction of long-term potentiation and influencing learning and memory.15 Transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS) has been shown to modulate neurotransmitters through the use of electrical current delivered on the skin surface to stimulate nerves. TENS applied in the auricular branch of the vagus nerve, namely transcutaneous vagus nerve stimulation (tVNS), potentially regulates various neurotransmitters and brain regions that are known to modulate different cognitive processes. tVNS simulates the locus coeruleus and nucleus of the solitary tract in the brainstem and regulates acetylcholine, GABA and norepinephrine,16 ultimately modulating the activities of the hippocampus, amygdala, thalamus and prefrontal cortex.17 Meanwhile, transcutaneous spinal cord stimulation (tSCS) applied to the thoracic spinal column has been suggested to recruit large-to-medium-diameter afferent nerve fibres within the dorsal roots of the spinal cord.18 The afferent signal potentially propagates via ascending pathways to the dorsal raphe nucleus and locus coeruleus and regulates the activities of noradrenergic and serotonergic neurotransmitters, and eventually stimulates the hippocampus and forebrain.19 20 Thus, tVNS and tSCS are potential interventions to alleviate cognitive decline by targeting neurotransmitter imbalances after stroke.

The activation of neurotransmitter systems by tVNS and tSCS could facilitate long-term synaptic changes in motor neurons and increase the synaptic connectivity in the corticospinal tract network,21 22 potentially supporting motor learning and recovery after stroke. tVNS has been found to activate the release of acetylcholine from the nucleus basalis and norepinephrine from the locus coeruleus,23 which facilitates the reorganisation of the corticospinal tract and excitation of bilateral intra-hemispheric networks in the motor cortex of people with stroke.21 24 Meanwhile, by activating primary afferents, tSCS may increase alpha motor neuron excitability through direct monosynaptic and polysynaptic connections or through facilitating the release of serotonin to plateau potentials.25 Furthermore, it may enhance the coordination of muscles by improving postsynaptic inhibition in Ia-inhibitory interneurons.25 The regulation of sensory input may also be improved by tSCS through increasing the excitability of GABA interneurons involved in sensory gating.25 Notably, the effectiveness of both tVNS26 and tSCS25 in reinforcing synaptic plasticity and promoting motor recovery can be increased by combining them with rehabilitation training, compared with applying either stimulation alone.

The findings of clinical and neurophysiological studies support the application of tVNS and tSCS for improving cognitive performance. A randomised controlled trial demonstrated that tVNS significantly enhanced global cognition (p<0.001) and memory (p<0.05) when compared with sham stimulation in people with mild cognitive impairment (MCI).27 A neuroimaging study revealed that tVNS significantly altered the semantic and hippocampal networks in people with MCI,28 suggesting the neuromodulatory effects of tVNS in cognitively relevant brain networks. tSCS has also been reported to significantly improve memory (F=5.26–13.70, p<0.05)29,31 and verbal fluency (F=6.26, p=0.03)29 31 compared with sham stimulation in older adults and people with Alzheimer’s disease.

The beneficial effects of tVNS and tSCS on post-stroke motor recovery have also been investigated. Randomised controlled trials have revealed that tVNS combined with different rehabilitation techniques, such as robotic training (Mann-Whitney U=5.00, p=0.048),32 conventional physical therapy (MD=−3.72, p<0.001)33 and task-oriented training (MD=6.98, p<0.001),24 significantly improve upper limb motor function after stroke compared with sham stimulation with rehabilitation. Four clinical trials demonstrated that tSCS delivered on thoracic and lumbar levels concurrent with gait training enhanced walking performance34,36 and reduced paretic knee extensor and ankle dorsiflexor spasticity37 after stroke.

Despite the potential of tVNS and tSCS in mitigating cognitive decline, their effects on cognition have not been investigated in people with stroke. Furthermore, they are not widely used in improving upper limb motor function after stroke due to the relatively small number and limited sample sizes of studies to date.38 39 In this randomised controlled trial, we will evaluate the effects of TENS on cognitive function and upper limb motor function in people with chronic stroke. The objectives will be to (1) compare the effects of tVNS versus sham stimulation, (2) compare the effects of tSCS versus sham stimulation and (3) compare the effects of tVNS versus tSCS on cognitive function and upper limb motor function after stroke. To develop an intervention for clinical application, a detailed protocol is necessary for ensuring the consistency of implementation and ease of replication. This paper reports the details of a protocol for investigating the effectiveness of tVNS and tSCS in improving cognitive function and upper limb motor function after stroke.

Method

This study protocol follows the guidelines of Standard Protocol Items Recommendations for Interventional Trials for reporting (see online supplemental file 1).40 The study was launched in November 2023. We aim to complete the study in December 2025.

Study design

The proposed study will be a three-arm sham-controlled single-blinded parallel-group randomised controlled trial, conducted with community-dwelling people with chronic stroke, at a university-based neurorehabilitation laboratory. Potential participants will be recruited through convenience sampling from local self-help groups in Hong Kong.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of this research.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Participants will be required to meet the following inclusion criteria: (1) aged between 50 and 80; (2) having a diagnosis of a unilateral ischaemic brain injury or intracerebral haemorrhage by MRI or CT; (3) had a stroke at least 6 months previously; (4) able to follow instructions and provide informed consent; and (5) having volitional control of the non-paretic arm and at least minimal antigravity movement in the paretic shoulder, defined as at least 5 degrees of shoulder flexion or abduction without support or assistance, measured by goniometer. Participants who meet any of the following criteria will be excluded: (1) having previous diagnosis of any neurological disease except stroke, or other medical conditions that will hinder proper treatment or assessment; (2) having diagnosis of cognitive impairment before stroke; (3) having metal implants in vivo, such as a cardiac pacemaker, cochlear implant or implanted brain stimulator; (4) having skin lesion, infection or inflammation near the selected stimulation position; (5) having a diagnosis of receptive aphasia, or difficulty in communicating with research personnel and understanding the instructions; (6) taking any medication that may affect the measured outcomes; or (7) concurrently participating in other rehabilitation programmes targeting cognitive or upper limb motor impairment following stroke.

Sample size estimation

The sample size has been determined by G*power V.3.1.2 (Universität Düsseldorf, Germany), with a two-tailed alpha error probability of 0.05 and a power of 80%. As the effects of tVNS and tSCS on cognitive function have not been previously investigated in people with stroke and at least 12 participants are recommended for planning larger subsequent studies according to a recommendation for pilot studies,41 we have estimated the sample size based on the pilot sample of 12 participants (four participants per group). The pilot study yielded Cohen’s d of 0.58 and 0.32 in favour of tVNS and tSCS for improving the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) score relative to sham stimulation. An effect size (d) of 0.32 corresponds approximately to an effect size (f) of 0.16 for a study design with three-group comparisons; therefore, the required sample size was calculated to be 69. Considering a dropout rate of 20%, we plan to recruit 90 participants (30 per group) for this study.

Procedure

Potential participants will be invited to a local university laboratory to enable the researchers to perform eligibility screening against the inclusion and exclusion criteria and explain the details of the study, including its aims, procedures, benefits, risks and confidentiality. For individuals who are both eligible and interested in participating in the clinical trial, a baseline assessment will be conducted after written consent (see online supplemental file 2) is obtained. The data collection relating to demographic characteristics and variables of interest will be completed on the same day.

Measurements

The participants will be assessed on four separate occasions for all outcome measures (figure 1): a baseline assessment before the intervention, a mid-intervention assessment after nine sessions of intervention, a postintervention assessment after 18 sessions of intervention and a follow-up assessment 1 month after the cessation of the intervention. The assessments will be conducted by a trained researcher who is blinded to group allocation.

Randomisation and stratification

The overview of this clinical trial is presented in figure 2. There will be three parallel groups: (1) tVNS group, (2) tSCS group and (3) sham group. After obtaining written informed consent from the participants and completing the baseline assessment, the participants will be stratified and randomly allocated among the three parallel groups in a 1:1:1 ratio using ‘Minimize’ computer software.42 The stratification will be based on age (50–59 years, 60–69 years or 70–80 years), education level (primary or below, secondary, or college or above) and hemiplegic side (left or right). The purpose of stratification is to prevent an imbalance in the distribution of demographic characteristics between intervention groups. The stratification factors were selected based on their known effects on the variables of interest. Older age and lower education level are commonly reported as independent predictors of post-stroke cognitive impairment.43 Additionally, the prevalence of cognitive impairment in survivors of stroke on the right hemiplegic side is significantly higher than in those affected on the left hemiplegic side.2 The stratification and randomisation will be conducted by an independent researcher who is not involved in the intervention or assessment in this study.

Flow diagram for clinical trial. tSCS, transcutaneous spinal cord stimulation; tVNS, transcutaneous vagus nerve stimulation.

To effectively maintain the concealment of randomisation, the participants will be informed of the results of the group allocation (with a group number but not the details of the intervention) and their training schedule and venue through centralised telephone calls from an off-site volunteer.

Intervention

Prior studies have suggested positive effects of upper limb training on post-stroke cognitive abilities, such as global cognition, memory, attention and executive function.44,46 Upper limb exercise also aims to improve the dexterity, motor control, range of motion and muscle strength of the paretic arm through task-specific functional movements. The intervention will comprise 18 sessions, with three sessions per week for 6 weeks. Previous clinical trials have reported that in-clinic rehabilitation with high-repetition, task-based, functional and progressive upper limb exercises combined with vagus nerve stimulation three times per week for 6 weeks is tolerable and effective in enhancing motor function in people with chronic stroke.47 48

The upper limb exercises to be used in this trial have been adopted and modified from a previous study and were demonstrated to be effective, safe and compatible with TENS in improving upper limb motor function.49 Before the exercises, each session will start with 15 min of passive upper limb stretching. After stretching, all participants will perform the upper limb exercises with tVNS, tSCS or sham stimulation simultaneously for 45 min. The upper limb exercises will consist of five training tasks targeting functional performance of the paretic arm: (1) hand dexterity training to improve fine motor control; (2) resisted forearm training to improve the muscle strength and range of motion of pronation, supination, wrist flexion and extension; (3) seated grasping and reaching training to improve trunk control and motor coordination; (4) bilateral training to improve the bilateral coordination of the upper extremity; and (5) strength training for the muscles involved in shoulder flexion and abduction. The participants will be provided with written guidelines and information on the method of progression of the upper limb exercises (table 1). Each task will last for 8 min, followed by a 1-minute rest interval and additional resting time will be allowed if needed.

Upper limb exercise protocol

tVNS group

tVNS will be delivered by a neurostimulator (MH8000P TENS device, MediHighTec MEDICAL Co, Taiwan) via two silicone electrodes attached with ear clips and will last for 45 min. The cathode will be attached to the cymba conchae and the anode will be placed on the cavum conchae of the left outer ear (figure 3A). A previous functional resonance imaging study suggested that the auricular branch of the vagus nerve innervates primarily the cymba conchae, and that tVNS at the cymba conchae resulted in the strongest activation of the vagal pathway.50 The application of tVNS will follow the guidelines provided by Badran et al to ensure safe administration in the laboratory.51 The stimulation will be delivered in biphasic square waves with a pulse frequency of 25 Hz, pulse width of 200 μs and duty cycle of 30 s on and 30 s off. The intensity of tVNS will be individually adjusted to the maximum tolerable level of the participants without exceeding 10 mA. Despite the large variability in stimulation protocols for tVNS reported in the literature, an international consensus-based review and recommendations revealed that a pulse width between 200 and 300 μs at 25 Hz, and a duty cycle of 30 s on and 30 s off, have frequently been adopted in human studies.52 A systematic review involving 51 studies and 1322 participants reported that tVNS is safe and well-tolerated, with only three serious adverse events considered to be possibly due to tVNS.53 tVNS has also been shown to enhance overall cognitive performance with a weighted effect size of 0.21 (95% CI 0.12 to 0.29) in healthy individuals54 and improve upper limb motor impairment in people with stroke (MD=7.50, 95% CI 4.02 to 10.99).55

(A) Stimulation site of tVNS (cymba conchae and cavum conchae of the left outer ear). (B) Stimulation site of tSCS (C6 and T5 levels of the spinal column). tSCS, transcutaneous spinal cord stimulation; tVNS, transcutaneous vagus nerve stimulation.

tSCS group

tSCS will be delivered by a neurostimulator (MH8000P TENS device) in burst mode with 9 pulses per burst, a pulse frequency of 160 Hz, a burst frequency of 2 Hz and a pulse width of 100 μs for 45 min. The stimulation will be delivered in asymmetric biphasic square-shaped pulses. Two 5 cm×15 cm electrodes will be attached between the C6 and T5 levels on each side of the spinal column and 2 cm from the spine (figure 3B). The intensity of stimulation will be individually selected by the participants according to maximum tolerance levels. The stimulation parameters have been adopted from previous clinical trials, which found that applying tSCS at the T1 and T5 levels significantly improved memory performance (F=5.26–13.70, p<0.05)29,31 and verbal fluency (F=6.26, p=0.03)29 31 in older adults and people with Alzheimer’s disease. Evidence also shows that the C6 to T1 spinal segments innervate several upper limb muscles, including the biceps brachii, triceps brachii, flexor carpi radialis, extensor carpi radialis, first dorsal interosseous and abductor pollicis brevis.56 57 Evidence suggests that transsynaptic activation projecting to the upper limb muscles can be achieved through tSCS over the C6 to T1 vertebra.58 By raising the membrane potential of spinal neurons, tSCS may improve upper limb function after stroke by increasing responsiveness to residual cortical inputs and improving voluntary motor control.59

Sham group

Half of the participants in the sham group will receive sham tVNS on the cymba conchae and cavum conchae of the left outer ear, via two ear clips, for 45 min. The other half will receive sham tSCS at the C6 and T5 levels on each side of the spinal column and 2 cm from the spine, for 45 min. The sham stimulation will be delivered by an identical TENS device to that used in the other groups, but with a disconnected electrical circuit. The participants will be informed that the stimulation will be delivered using different parameters that might or might not induce somatic sensations. Sham stimulation is likely to have a negligible effect because it does not generate any active stimulation or sensory experience and has been reported to be a valid method of reducing performance bias.60

Safety and adverse events

tVNS and tSCS have been clinically proven to be safe and tolerable therapeutic interventions with minor side effects. The potential side effects are skin redness, slight pain and a burning, tingling or itching sensation at the stimulation sites.16 61 During the intervention, at least one trained researcher will stay by the side of the participant during the intervention in case he/she needs assistance or feels any discomfort. The safety of the experimental session will be guaranteed. The participants’ blood pressure will be measured before and after each experimental session to monitor their condition. If a participant experiences any discomfort or side effects during the intervention, his/her condition will be closely monitored, even after completing the experimental session. The research personnel will report any adverse events to the Departmental Research Committee of the Hong Kong Polytechnic University.

Outcome measures

Primary outcome measures

Montreal Cognitive Assessment

Global cognitive function will be measured using the MoCA,62 which assesses executive functioning, memory, visuospatial abilities, attention, language and orientation. A higher score represents better global cognition. The Hong Kong version of the MoCA has shown excellent test–retest reliability (intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC)=0.92) and good internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha=0.79) in people with mild neurocognitive disorder.63

Fugl-Meyer Assessment of the Upper Extremity

Upper limb motor impairment will be quantified by the Fugl-Meyer Assessment of the Upper Extremity (FMAUE).64 The FMAUE measures reflex activities, movements and coordination of the paretic upper limb,64 with a higher score representing better motor performance. The inter-rater reliability of the FMAUE has been found to be excellent (ICC=0.98) in people with stroke.65

Secondary outcome measures

Rivermead Behavioural Memory Test

The episodic memory performance will be assessed using the third edition of the Rivermead Behavioural Memory Test (RBMT).66 A higher score indicates better episodic memory performance. The Hong Kong version of the RBMT has demonstrated excellent inter-rater reliability (ICC=0.99) and good internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha=0.64–0.83) in people with dementia, people with mild cognitive impairment and healthy older adults.66

Digit Span Test

The working memory performance will be examined using the Digit Span Test, which includes two parts, Digit Span Forward (DSF) and Digit Span Backward (DSB).67 In the DSF and DSB, the participants will be required to repeat numbers in forward and reverse order, respectively. Higher scores represent better working memory performance. The intra-rater reliability values of the DSF and DSB are 0.89 and 0.60, respectively, in older adults with neurocognitive disorder.68

Trail Making Test

The performance in terms of processing speed, attention and cognitive flexibility will be measured by the digital version of the Trail Making Test (V.1.2; Roland Rabien) using an iPad.69 This test is divided into part A (TMTA) and part B (TMTB). The participant is required to connect labelled dots in numeric order and in an order alternating between numbers and words in the TMTA and TMTB, respectively. A shorter completion time indicates better performance in attention and executive function. Excellent test–retest reliability has been found in people with stroke (ICC=0.94 and 0.86 for TMTA and TMTB, respectively).70

Wolf Motor Function Test

The functional performance of the paretic upper limb will be evaluated by the Wolf Motor Function Test (WMFT).71 A higher score in the WMFT indicates better movement capability. The WMFT has demonstrated excellent inter-rater reliability (ICC=0.88–0.97) and test–retest reliability (r=0.90–0.95) in people with chronic stroke.72

Maximum isometric voluntary contraction of the elbow

The maximum isometric voluntary contraction of the paretic elbow extensor and flexor will be measured with a hand-held dynamometer (Model 01165; Lafayette Instrument, Indiana, USA). Elbow extension and flexion will be performed with the participant in a sitting position with 30° shoulder flexion, 0° shoulder abduction, 90° elbow flexion and forearm in full supination. Two trials will be performed for each muscle group and the mean force of the two trials will be recorded. Excellent test–retest reliability (ICC=0.94–0.98) has been reported for the use of the hand-held dynamometer in people with chronic stroke.73

Muscle stiffness of the elbow extensor and flexor

The muscle stiffness of the elbow extensor and flexor will be quantified by a MyotonPRO device (Myoton AS, Tallinn, Estonia). The participant will sit in a comfortable position with 0° shoulder flexion, 0° shoulder abduction, 90° elbow flexion and forearm supinated supported by a standard pillow. The measurement for the elbow extensor and elbow flexor will be performed at the medial head of the triceps brachii and at the long head and lateral part of the biceps brachii in the middle of the arm, respectively. A higher value measured by the MyotonPRO indicates higher stiffness of the tissue. Excellent intra-rater reliability has been demonstrated in the stiffness measurement of the elbow extensor (ICC=0.92) and flexor (ICC=0.90) with the MyotonPRO in people with chronic stroke.74

Arm Activity Measure

The perceived upper limb function will be assessed by the Arm Activity Measure (ArmA).75 Higher scores indicate poorer self-perceived upper limb function by the respondent. The Chinese version of the ArmA has demonstrated good test–retest reliability (ICCs=0.87–0.93) and internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha=0.75–0.95) in people with chronic stroke.75

Oxford Participation and Activity Questionnaire

The levels of participation and activity will be assessed using the Oxford Participation and Activity Questionnaire (OxPAQ).76 Higher scores in the OxPAQ represent greater difficulties with participation and activities. The Chinese version of the OxPAQ has been reported to have good internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha=0.86–0.94) and excellent test–retest reliability (ICCs=0.91–0.94) in people with chronic stroke.76

12-Item Short Form Survey

The health-related quality of life will be measured by the second version of the 12-Item Short-Form Survey (SF-12).77 A higher score indicates better health-related quality of life. The SF-12 has good internal consistency (Cronbach’s alpha=0.86–0.87) and moderate test–retest reliability (ICC=0.59–0.79) in older adults.77

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis will be performed using SPSS V.29.0. The demographic and baseline characteristics of the participants will be summarised using descriptive statistics. The normality of the data will be tested using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. The differences between the three groups will be compared using one-way analysis of variance, Kruskal-Wallis tests or χ2 tests, as appropriate.

The intention-to-treat principle will be adopted. Any missing data will be imputed by the last observation carried forward method. A linear mixed model, which has the advantages of analysing intra-correlated repeated measurements and accommodating randomly missing data, will be used to detect any significant differences in variables of interest over time between the tVNS, tSCS and sham groups. Group effects, time effects and time-by-group interaction effects will be set as fixed effects, and the random intercept and random slope of change in dependent variables will be included as random effects. Maximum likelihood estimation will be used for estimation, and the first-order autoregressive covariance structure will be used to estimate the model parameters. A p value of below 0.05 will be considered statistically significant. Bonferroni correction will be applied to the p values of the analysis of primary outcomes, secondary outcomes and post hoc analyses to prevent inflation of the type 1 error rate. The immediate intervention effects will be detected by analysing data from baseline to postintervention assessments, and the carryover effects of the intervention will be investigated by analysing data from baseline and 1-month follow-up assessments. The number of participants who achieve a minimal clinically important difference of a 2-point improvement on the MoCA78 and a 5.25-point improvement on the FMAUE79 after the intervention will be compared between the three groups by the χ2 test.

Ethics and dissemination

This clinical trial was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov on 14 November 2022 (NCT05615610). Ethical approval has been obtained from the Human Subjects Ethics Committee of The Hong Kong Polytechnic University (HSEARS20221011002). The study will be conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent (see online supplemental file 2) will be collected from each participant before the study begins. All information collected from the participants will be kept strictly confidential and their accessibility will be restricted to the research team. Throughout the study, written records will be securely stored. Once the study concludes, all data will be entered into a computer, and a backup copy will be saved on a hard drive kept in a secure location by the principal investigator. Personal data will be discarded 3 years after the study ends. The results will be disseminated through scientific journals and international conferences.

Discussion

Cognitive impairment and upper limb motor impairment are common consequences of stroke. Despite the high prevalence of cognitive impairment after stroke and its associations with poor health-related outcomes, much research focus is placed on post-stroke motor impairment, while effective rehabilitation interventions targeting post-stroke cognitive deficits are still understudied.9 Furthermore, a substantial proportion of people with chronic stroke experience persistent upper limb impairment, but the restoration of their paretic arm function remains challenging.13 The aim of the proposed clinical trial is to evaluate the effectiveness of tVNS and tSCS in improving cognitive function and upper limb motor function and in turn enhancing activity, participation and quality of life for people with chronic stroke. It is hoped that the results of this study will provide scientific evidence supporting the use of tVNS and tSCS in alleviating cognitive deficits and enhancing upper limb motor recovery following stroke. If effective, our interventions will offer a safe, cost-effective and readily transferable therapeutic approach to clinical practice to manage post-stroke cognitive and motor impairment.

This study protocol is not exempt from limitations. First, as the plan is to recruit community-dwelling people with chronic stroke, the generalisability of the results may be limited. Future studies could investigate the effects of tVNS and tSCS among stroke survivors in other study settings or in acute or subacute phases of stroke. Second, this study will exclude stroke survivors with significant aphasia due to the challenges in performing reliable neuropsychological assessments. The effects of tVNS and tSCS on cognitive outcomes in people with post-stroke aphasia may require further investigation. Third, there is a lack of precise temporal pairing between tVNS and concurrent upper limb movement during the intervention. Since tVNS will be delivered using a fixed duty cycle of 30 s on and 30 s off in this proposed trial, participants may perform upper limb exercises during periods when tVNS is not active. Additionally, the number of movements paired with tVNS cannot be determined. Future studies should consider real-time pairing of tVNS with upper limb movement by integrating motion sensors, electromyography80 or Bluetooth connection,81 which may be more effective in promoting neuroplasticity after stroke and optimising the therapeutic effects of tVNS.82
