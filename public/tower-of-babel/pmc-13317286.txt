Functional relationships linking C99/APP-βCTF dimerization, proteostasis disruption, and organelle dysfunction
Authors: Céline Badot, Anaïs Bini, Eric Duplan, Frédéric Checler, Inger Lauritzen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe amyloid β (Aβ) precursor C99 (or APP-βCTF) accumulates in Alzheimer’s disease and has been proposed to display Aβ-independent toxicity, notably by affecting the endosomal-lysosomal-autophagic (ELA) network. Our previous findings suggested that some ELA-associated C99 could correspond to dimeric and oligomeric species, but the intracellular sites of C99 dimerization, as well as the toxicity linked to it, remains unknown.MethodsWe here developed a bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) probe to visualize de novo C99 dimerization and dimer trafficking, as well as to identify possible cellular responses specifically linked to C99 dimerization. Moreover, to confirm dimer localizations and toxicities, the localization and cellular effects of the dimerization mutant C99G29L/G33L was compared to that of wildtype C99. The C99 constructs were transfected into HeLa cells and dimer localizations, expression levels and intracellular toxicities were evaluated by Western blot and immunocytochemistry.ResultsBiFC-C99 dimers were first detected within the TGN, in which monomers initially accumulate. The proteasomal inhibitor MG-132 led to increased dimer formation, indicating that the proteasomal activity status is a key determinant of C99 dimerization. Conversely, TGN-associated C99 dimerization had a negative impact on both the ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS) and the TGN, as highlighted by the appearance of p62/SQSTM1-positive aggresomes and fragmented Golgi, then suggesting a two-way relationship between UPS function and C99 dimerization. Dimerization also led to lysosome repositioning and to the accumulation of LC3B-positive autophagy vesicles, agreeing with the well-known interplay between autophagy and proteasome in protein turnover. P62/SQSTM1 and LC3B accumulation could similarly be observed in cells expressing C99G29L/G33L, a mutant favoring dimerization, while this was not the case in wildtype C99 expressing cells, confirming the dimerization-specific effect. While proteasomal inhibition caused TGN-associated dimer formation, repression of γ-secretase-mediated C99 proteolysis instead led to a redistribution of monomers to EEA1-positive endosomes, whereas already existing C99 dimers remained unaffected by this treatment. These new endosome-associated monomers were found also to dimerize, resulting in dimers destined for either secretion via small extracellular vesicles or autophagy-lysosomal degradation.ConclusionsTaken together, our findings indicate that the cellular status of UPS, autophagy and γ-secretase activities are all determinant for C99 expression levels, and are thus crucial for both the level of C99 dimerization and for the fate of the dimers. Moreover, our data show that C99 dimerization itself negatively affects these activities thereby indicating a two-way relationship between C99 dimerization, proteostasis disruption and organelle dysfunction.Graphical Abstract Proposed model of C99 BiFC monomer and dimer trafficking and clearance pathways. Under basal conditions (left), TGN localized C99 BiFC monomers either undergo γ-secretase cleavage, degradation via the UPS or dimerize (1), while dimers are less prone to both γ-secretase cleavage (2) and UPS degradation (3). Background

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the leading cause of dementia, affecting 55 million people in 2020, and is expected to increase to 139 million people in 2050, according to the WHO (https://www.alzint.org). One of the main characteristics of the AD-affected brain is the presence of extracellular plaques composed mainly of aggregated amyloid-β (Aβ) peptide, an end-product of the processing of the human β-amyloid precursor protein (βAPP). βAPP undergoes a series of cleavage events by different enzymes known as secretases. In the nonamyloidogenic pathway, the cleavage of βAPP by the α-secretase ADAM10 triggers the release of a soluble fragment (sAPPα) and its C-terminal counterpart, referred to as C83. Alternatively, in the amyloidogenic pathway, βAPP undergoes β-secretase (BACE1)-mediated cleavage, resulting in the formation of a soluble sAPPβ fragment and its C-terminal stub, C99/APP-βCTF. C83 and C99 are both targeted by γ-secretase, leading to the production of p3 or Aβ peptides, respectively, as well as a common transcription factor named AICD [1]. While Aβ is a well-known cytotoxic actor [2], less is known about the possible pathological contribution of other APP-derived metabolites. Among these latter compounds, C99 has been proposed as another toxic candidate [3]. It was found to accumulate in the brains of both sporadic- and familial-affected brains [4–7] and was reported to display Aβ-independent and C99-mediated neurotoxicity, affecting several fundamental cellular processes, such as endosomal-lysosomal and autophagic (ELA) functions [8–12], as well as mitochondrial activity and mitophagy [5,13, 14]. In addition, we recently demonstrated that small extracellular vesicles (sEVs) isolated from either AD cell or mouse models are strongly enriched in C99 cells, suggesting possible vesicle-associated C99 dissemination within the brain [15]. Strikingly, in sEVs, we found that C99 exists not only as a monomer but also as dimers and oligomers [15]. This propensity of C99 to form dimers was indeed demonstrated in several in silico and structural studies and was shown to be due to four GxxxG motifs in its juxta- and transmembrane regions [16–20], but the pathological impact of C99 dimerization/oligomerization is still unknown. Our previous works suggested that dimerized/oligomerized C99 is enriched not only in sEVs but also in intracellular compartments belonging to the ELA network [12]. Moreover, we found that within these compartments γ-secretase inhibition strongly potentiated the levels of these catabolites [12], agreeing with the fact that γ-secretase inhibition not only increases the levels of C99, but also changes its localization by redirecting it from the TGN to endosomes [15,21,22]. This shift was shown to reflect a greater interaction between the cytosolic YKFFE motif within C99 and the AP-4 adaptor complex present in the TGN in the absence of γ-secretase processing [21,22]. C99 accumulation within ELA compartments also occurs in the presence of active γ-secretase and has been attributed to either age-related [12] or APP phosphorylation-dependent [9] defects in autophagic–lysosomal degradation. To better understand the influence of C99 dimerization on C99 subcellular localization and trafficking, as well as its impact on cellular function, we here developed a new bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC) probe (C99-VC/VN), in which the expression of C99 fused to either of the two half-moieties of the fluorescent Venus protein allows visualization of C99 dimerization by triggering the physical reconstitution of yellow fluorescence [23]. Owing to its nonreversible feature, this probe should highlight the intracellular site ofde novoC99 dimer formation, but should also visualize C99 dimer trafficking, as well as possible dimerization-associated toxicity(es). Moreover, we compared the intracellular localization and cellular effects of the mutant C99G29L/G33L, known to favor dimerization, to the wildtype protein in order to confirm the dimerization-specific effects observed with the BiFC construct.

Cell culture, transfection, pharmacological treatments and isolation of small extracellular vesicles (sEVs)

HeLa and HEK-293T (ATCC), as well as polyclonal C99-expressing SH-SY5Y [15] cell lines, were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum (Thermo Fisher Scientific), penicillin (100 U/ml, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and streptomycin (50 µg/ml, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. For the immunofluorescence experiments, HeLa cells were plated at 80,000 cells per well onto coverslips in 24-well plates and transiently cotransfected the next day with 190 ng of pTRE3G-BI-C99-VC, pTRE3G-BI-C99-VN, or pTRE3G-BI-C99-VC-VN plasmid and 60 ng of pCMV-Tet3G plasmid per well using JetPRIME®(Polyplus®-Sartorius) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The medium was replaced with fresh medium 4 h post-transfection, and the expression of C99 was induced 24 h post-transfection by the addition of doxycycline (Sigma Aldrich) at a final concentration of 100 ng/ml for 30 min. The cells were rinsed twice with 1X PBS to remove any residual doxycycline before being replaced with fresh medium. In some experiments, cells were treated with either the γ-secretase inhibitor ELND006 (D6) [27] at 5 µg/ml or the vehicle DMSO 16 h before doxycycline application. For biochemical experiments, HeLa or HEK-293T cells were plated at 300,000 cells per well in 6-well plates and transiently transfected the next day with 2 µg of pcDNA3.1-C99-VC, pcDNA3.1-C99-VN or both plasmids per well or cotransfected with 2 µg per well of pTRE3G-BI-C99 and pCMV-Tet3G inducible plasmids via JetPRIME® (Polyplus®-Sartorius) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Expression was induced with doxycycline at a concentration of 1 µg/ml for 24 h, and the cells were treated with D6 at 5 µg/ml or with the vehicle DMSO overnight. A doxycycline-inducible SH-SY5Y C99 cell line [12] was used in Western blot analysis for studies on nontagged C99 and for the isolation of sEVs, which was performed according to a slightly modified protocol described in our previous work [15]. Briefly, the day after doxycycline induction, the medium of the cells was changed to OptiMEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific) containing D6 or vehicle (DMSO) for another 20 h. sEVs were isolated via a combination of ultracentrifugation (100000x g/2 hr) and size-exclusion chromatography using 70 nm SEC columns (IZON). For WB analysis, sEVs were lysed in RIPA buffer (1×) containing a protease inhibitor cocktail (see below) and ultrasonicated. In some experiments, the cells were treated with 2 µM MgCl2 (Selleckhem) or 100 nM bafilomycin A1 (Enzo Life Sciences) for 16 h, and the latter was combined with D6.

Immunocytochemistry

Except in the case of LC3 immunolabeling, HeLa cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (EMS) for 10 min, rinsed twice with 1× phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100/PBS for 10 min. The cells were stained via the PROTEOSTAT® Aggresome Detection Kit (Enzo Life Sciences) and washed three times with 1X PBS before being incubated with 1× reagent diluted 1/500 in 1× assay buffer for 30 min before being saturated with bovine serum albumin (5%)/Tween 20 (0.05%) PBS solution for 30 min. For immunostaining, the coverslips were then incubated for 1 h with primary antibodies (cf. Table 2) diluted in BSA (2,5%)/Tween 20 (0,05%) in PBS at room temperature, followed by rinses and incubation with Alexa Fluor 488-, Alexa Fluor 594- or Alexa Fluor 647-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:1000, Molecular Probes) for 1 h at room temperature. For LC3 immunolabeling, cells were fixed and permeabilized at -20 °C for 10 min in ice-cold methanol. Nuclei were stained with DAPI (1:10000, Roche), and coverslips were mounted onto glass slides using VectaMount® medium (Vector Labs) and kept at 4 °C after polymerization.

The gels were photoactivated to visualize the protein load before being electrophoretically transferred to nitrocellulose membranes via the Bio-Rad Trans-Blot® Turbo™ Transfer System. Confocal microscopy and image analysis

Confocal microscopy was performed using a Zeiss LSM780 microscope equipped with a 63X objective. Images were analyzed via the open-source image software Cell-Profiler (version 4.2.4). A custom pipeline was developed to quantify colocalization between dimeric C99 and organelle markers. Regions of interest (ROIs) corresponding to the transfected cells were identified on the basis of the fluorescence of the C99-VC/VN dimers. To measure colocalization, individual channels were thresholded via an adaptive Otsu algorithm, and objects corresponding to dimeric C99 dimers and organelle markers were masked. Mander’s colocalization coefficient (M2) was calculated to determine the fraction of total organelle area with dimeric C99. Line-scan profiles were obtained via FIJI software. The colocalization analysis was performed on at least (n) images per condition, with (N) independent experiments, as indicated in the Figure legends. Moreover, FIJI software was used for lysosomal positioning analysis after labeling with APPCT and a LAMP1 antibody. The ratio of the area covered by LAMP1 to the total cellular surface area was determined with the APPCT- (ER label corresponding to the total cellular surface), DAPI- (taken as the nucleus) and LAMP1- (taken as the lysosomal position) signals, and the data are presented as the area of LAMP1 covering the cytosol (LAMP1/cytosol ratio). To quantify the proportion of cells displaying a fragmented TGN, p62/SQSTM1 aggregates or LC3B puncta, two or three independent experiments (N = 2–3) with n = 100 cells per experiment were analyzed via blind visual observation and manual classification (as described in the corresponding figures).

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 8.0.1. The data are presented as the means ± SEMs, and significance was determined via two-way ANOVA for multiple comparisons and the Mann‒Whitney test for comparisons of two conditions. Semiquantitative analysis of time-dependent TGN fragmentation or p62 aggregation was performed via simple linear regression analysis.

Results

Development of C99 BiFC as a novel approach to study C99 dimerization

Here, we used a pTRE3G-BI bidirectional inducible promoter enabling simultaneous and stoichiometric expression of two transgenes. When co-expressed with the trans-activator and doxycycline-inducible plasmid pCMV-Tet3G, this system allows precise control of both the protein levels and their temporal expression (Fig. S1A). We generated a plasmid carrying C99 fused with each of the half-fluorescent Venus proteins (pTREG-BI-C99-VC-VN (C99-VC-VN)), as well as two controls bearing only C99-VC (C-terminal) or C99-VN (N-terminal) (Fig. S1B). We first determined the optimal transfection/induction parameters for the visualization of doxycycline-induced C99 de novo dimerization in HeLa cells. To this end, cells were transiently transfected with the different constructs and then incubated with doxycycline (dox, 100 ng/ml) for various durations (1 to 30 min) before fixation at 24 h post-induction. Under these conditions, no yellow fluorescence was observed in the cells incubated for 1–5 min, whereas a robust BiFC yellow signal was detected in the C99-VC-VN-expressing cells induced for 30 min, confirming the formation of complementary C99-VC/VN dimers (Fig. 1A). Despite the absence of yellow fluorescence in 1- or 5-min-dox-incubated cells, immunostaining revealed an increased APP C-terminal (APPCT)-positive immunoreactivity in numerous cells, which was not detected in noninduced (-dox) cells. These data indicated that specific transgene expression of only monomeric C99 occurred prior to the generation of the dimer-linked fluorescent signal (Fig. 1A). Importantly, no yellow fluorescence was observed in cells expressing either the C99-VC or C99-VN construct; however, as expected, their expression could be visualized with both APPCT and W0-2, the latter of which interacts with the N-terminal part of C99 (Fig. 1A, bottom). Previous works have shown that C99 not only forms homodimers but also associates with C99-derived C83 to form heterodimers or produces C83, which can also self-dimerize [15]. These dimers could also be expected to emit yellow fluorescence and to be immune-stained by APPCT, but only C99-containing dimers should be detected with the N-terminus W0-2 (Fig. 1B). Western blot (WB) analysis was used to delineate the various species obtained with the C99-VC, C99-VN and C99-VC-VN constructs (Fig. 1C). Moreover, some cells were treated with D6, a γ-secretase inhibitor, as a pharmacological approach to increase C99 levels but also to favor the generation of C83 from C99 (Fig. 1C) [15]. First, when W0-2 was used (Fig. 1C, upper blot), the C99-VC and C99-VN constructs were found to yield doxycycline-dependent expression of proteins displaying the expected sizes of monomeric C99-VC at 23 kDa and C99-VN at 30 kDa. In cells expressing C99-VC-VN, C99-VC/VN dimers (approximately 50 kDa) with levels approximately equivalent to those of the C99-VC and C99-VN monomers were detected. These findings indicated that dimer levels were greater for the C99-VC-VN construct than for the two control constructs consistent with the formation of more stable dimers due to the nonreversible nature of reconstituted Venus. As expected for all the constructs, D6 treatment led to increased levels of both monomeric and dimeric C99 (Fig. 1C), although dimeric C99-VC remained poorly detectable even in the presence of D6. Moreover, even in the absence of doxycycline, D6 revealed some faint immunoreactivities, which were not observed in vehicle-treated cells, and which were likely induced by cell culture medium tetracycline traces. Furthermore, we used an APPCT C-terminal antibody to examine whether C83-related species could be detected and potentiated by D6. As expected, and compared to W0-2, APPCT revealed additional monomeric and dimeric C99-derived C83 BiFC species resulting in a complex mix of monomeric and dimeric APP-CTFs under these conditions (Fig. 1C, lower blot). Finally, some cells were transfected with the constitutive pcDNA3.1 C99 BiFC constructs, that led to stronger expression levels (Fig. 1D), and APPCT immunoblotting confirmed the existence of both C99-VN and C99-VC dimers. Moreover, WB revealed detectable levels of AICD (green boxes), indicating that a part of C99-VC and C99-VN is cleaved by γ-secretase. These data were also confirmed by the ELISA-based detection of Aβ40 in the culture medium (data not shown).

Under basal conditions, C99 BiFC dimers accumulate in the trans-Golgi network (TGN)

To identify the intracellular localization of C99-VC/VN dimers, the fluorescence acquisition parameters of APPCT and W0-2-associated staining were calibrated so that no signal was detected in noninduced transfected cells, allowing discrimination between transfected and endogenous APP-related expression (Fig. 2 and Fig. S2A). Under these conditions, visible expression of C99 monomers detected with both APPCT and W0-2 antibodies was observed in ER- and Golgi-like intracellular structures at approximately 1 h after induction (Fig. 2 and Fig. S2A). At 2 h post-induction, yellow C99-VC/VN dimers localized to a Golgi-like structure were observed in approximately 10% of all the cells, while at 4- and 8 h post-induction (Fig. 2) and beyond (not shown), an increasing proportion of the transfected cells displayed yellow fluorescence, which noticeably appeared more punctiform over time. At these stages, many C99-VC- and C99-VN-transfected cells displayed similar C99 localization (visualized via APPCT and W0-2) (Fig. S2A). Staining with the trans-Golgi network (TGN) marker TGN46 at both 2- and 8 h post-induction revealed a nearly perfect overlap with yellow fluorescence (Fig. 2B), as visualized by both line-scan analysis (Fig. 2C and Fig. S2B-C) and a Mander’s coefficient value (proportion of TGN46 colocalizing with C99 dimers) of approximately 0.9 in both cases (Fig. 2D). These data thus indicated that under basal conditions, even over time, C99 dimers were localized to the TGN.

C99 BiFC dimer accumulation impairs trans-Golgi network morphology

The punctiform structure of the TGN-associated dimers led us to investigate the putative impact of C99-VC/VN dimerization on Golgi morphology. To that end, we used a TGN46 antibody to label the Golgi at different timepoints after protein induction (Fig. 3). Indeed, in C99-VN/VC-expressing cells, at 1 h after induction, the TGN46 signal displayed an intact ribbon-shaped or compacted perinuclear distribution typical of the TGN, and the staining became progressively dispersed and punctiform over time. In C99-VC-expressing cells, TGN fragmentation was also observed but was slightly delayed (Fig. S3). Conversely, even at late stages, non-dox-induced cells presented a normal TGN (Fig. 3A). Quantification of this TGN-associated pathology was performed by arbitrarily classifying TGN morphology into three categories (1 to 3), corresponding to “intact structure”, “medium fragmentation” and “high fragmentation”, the latter visualized by clearly dispersed puncta (Fig. 3B), confirming the time-dependent effect of C99 expression/dimerization on TGN morphology alterations (Fig. 3C-D). Moreover, C99-VC-VN induced a significantly greater TGN fragmentation (particularly at 8 h post-induction) than the two controls (Fig. 3E). Additionally, correlation analysis between each score and time revealed a negative correlation between score 1 and time-dependent C99 dimerization (Fig. 3F) and a positive correlation between score 3 and time-dependent C99 dimerization (Fig. 3G). The correlations were stronger for the more stable C99-VC/VN dimers.

C99 BiFC dimerization promotes the formation of p62/SQSTM1-positive aggresomes and leads to autophagic activation and lysosomal reorganization

Previous works have demonstrated a link between Golgi disorganization and aggresome formation [28]. These latter structures correspond to inclusion bodies sequestering misfolded, aggregated and mostly ubiquitinylated proteins. Aggresomes are formed through the recruitment of the ubiquitin-binding protein p62/SQSTM1 as a consequence of defective ubiquitin‒proteasome (UPS) degradation. Thus, by using a p62/SQSTM1 antibody, we investigated the possible effect of C99 dimerization on aggresome formation (Fig. 4 and Fig. S4). Colocalization analysis at 8 h post-induction firstly revealed a strong overlap between the yellow fluorescence and p62/SQSTM1 immunostaining (Fig. 4A), as confirmed by line-scan analysis (Fig. 4B) and a Mander’s coefficient > 0.6 (Fig. 4C). C99 expression was also examined with W0-2, which allowed the detection of control cells, but also helped us to discriminate between monomeric and oligomeric C99 in cells expressing C99-VC-VN. This analysis revealed a clear C99- and time-dependent effect on p62/SQSTM1 staining (Fig. 4D-J). Both C99-VC-VN-transfected cells under noninduced conditions, and cells expressing only monomers at early timepoints (no yellow fluorescence), presented similar low cytoplasmic p62/SQSTM1 signals (Fig. 4D). From approximately 2 to 4 h post-induction (the appearance of yellow fluorescence), a small punctiform p62/SQSTM1 signal was observed, and this signal evolved into more compact aggregates at later time points. In C99-VC-expressing cells, p62/SQSTM1 staining also changed from cytoplasmic to aggregated over time (Fig. S4A). Quantifying this phenotype by scoring p62/SQSTM1 staining as “basal”, “intermediate”, or “aggregated” (arbitrary scores 1–3, respectively; Fig. 4E) revealed a faster and more pronounced shift in C99-VC/VN–expressing cells compared with controls (Fig. 4F and Fig. S4A–C), likely reflecting the greater stability of C99-VC/VN dimers. Correlative analyses confirmed a negative relationship between score 1 and time-dependent C99 dimerization for all three BiFC constructs, as well as a positive correlation between score 3 and time-dependent C99 dimerization, and again the correlations were stronger in cells containing C99-VC/VN dimers (Fig. S4C). This aggresome nature was validated by the use of the PROTEOSTAT®Aggresome Detection Kit, which uses a red fluorescent dye to specifically detect denatured protein within aggresomes. By using cells treated with the proteasome inhibitor MG-132 as a positive control (Fig. S4D), we confirmed that the p62/SQSTM1 (white) aggregated structures that appeared at late stages (8 h post-induction) were PROTEOSTAT® positive (red) (Fig. 4G). Moreover, line-scan analysis indicated that most of the aggresome-associated signals overlapped with both p62/SQSTM1 and the yellow dimer signal (Fig. 4H). p62/SQSTM1 may, via LC3, be involved in linking polyubiquitinated protein aggregates to the autophagic machinery, thereby facilitating their clearance [29]. We therefore investigated the possible activation of autophagy by measuring the presence of LC3-positive puncta. While in non-transfected cells, as well as at an early time point (2 h postinduction), only low and diffuse LC3-staining was observed, dimer-containing cells at 8 h postinduction presented a high number of LC3-positive puncta (Fig. 5A). Moreover, in these cells, line-scan analysis showed a good overlap of LC3 puncta with the yellow fluorescence (Fig. 5B). In addition, since aggresomes formation is also known to lead to lysosomal reorganization due to the blockade of microtubule-dependent transport [30], we next investigated the level of dimers colocalization with the lysosomal protein LAMP1 (Fig. 5C and D), as well as the putative impact of C99 dimerization on lysosomal positioning (Fig. 5E and F). At 8 h post-induction, line-scan analysis revealed strong overlap of LAMP1 staining with C99 dimers (Fig. 5D). Moreover, at this stage, lysosomal structures were found to be reorganized. While at 2 h post-induction they were dispersed throughout the cytosol and covered it by more than 90% (LAMP1/cytosol ratio > 0.9), they mostly displayed a perinuclear localization and only covered the total cell surface by approximately 50% at this late stage (Fig. 5E and F).

C99 dimerization impairs the proteasome, and conversely, proteasomal inhibition favors C99 dimer formation

The above-described results suggest that C99 dimerization triggers proteasomal degradation defects. We next questioned whether, conversely, inhibition of the proteasome could underlie TGN-associated C99 dimerization. To test this hypothesis, C99-VC-, C99-VN- and C99-VC-VN (pCDNA3)-transfected HeLa cells were treated with the proteasomal inhibitor MG-132 (2 µM/16 h) or vehicle (DMSO), and WB analysis with W0-2 was used to highlight C99 monomer and dimer levels (Fig. 5G-H). WB revealed that C99-VC and C99-VN dimer levels were indeed increased in MG-132-treated cells (Fig. 5G-H). Furthermore, we used a stable dox-inducible SH-SY5Y cell line expressing non-tagged C99 [15] and treated these cells with the γ-secretase inhibitor D6 or MG-132 (MG) (Fig. 5I). While only monomeric C99 was detected in dox-induced vehicle-treated cells, MG-123 inhibitor treatment revealed the presence of both monomeric C99 and C83, as well as several high-molecular-weight immune reactivities probably corresponding to both dimeric and ubiquitinylated forms. The intracellular levels of these immune reactivities were much greater than those induced by D6. Nevertheless, and in agreement with our previous work [15], the high-molecular-weight immune reactivities induced by D6 where mostly enriched in sEVs (last lanes in the WB) [15]. The slight shift in dimeric molecular weights induced by MG-132 and D6 likely highlights the loss of ubiquitin residues upon loading of proteins into sEVs [31].

Inhibition of γ-secretase affects the intracellular trafficking of C99 monomers, whereas the localization of C99 dimers remains unaffected

We and others previously reported that γ-secretase inhibition favors the trafficking of C99 from the TGN to endosomes [15, 21, 22]. We therefore questioned whether the suppression of γ-secretase would also affect the localization of dimeric C99. To answer this question, C99-VC-VN-transfected cells were exposed to D6 (or control vehicle) simultaneously with doxycycline and were then fixed at 8 h post-induction. Under both vehicle- and D6-treated conditions, yellow C99-VC/VN dimers appeared as large puncta with no evident change in the cellular distribution upon γ-secretase inhibition (Fig. S5A). However, under the same conditions, a few cells displaying only monomers (emitting APPCT-conjugated red fluorescence but no yellow fluorescence) also showed a marked change in APPCT-associated localization upon D6 treatment (Fig. S5A, right panel). Coimmunostaining of these monomers with anti-TGN46 confirmed the predominant localization of C99 in TGN-positive cells under vehicle-treated conditions, whereas in D6-treated cells, the monomers were distributed in cytoplasmic puncta, which were positive for the early endosome marker EEA1 (Fig. S5B), as validated by good overlap of APPCT/TGN46 and APPCT/EEA1 signals in line-scan analysis for the vehicle and D6 conditions, respectively (Fig. S5C). Moreover, these observations were consistent with the findings in cells transfected with a C99-GFP construct (Fig. S6A-B). These data therefore indicate that γ-secretase modulates the intracellular trafficking of monomeric C99 but has no effect on TGN-associated C99 dimers, suggesting that these dimers undergo little if any γ-secretase proteolysis.

Endosome-localized C99 can dimerize de novo, and only endosomal-localized dimers are affected by autolysosomal inhibition

The redistribution of monomers to early endosomes upon γ-secretase inhibition raised the possibility that these monomers can subsequently dimerize within these compartments. We therefore investigated the possible presence of endosome-associated yellow puncta in D6-treated cells. Since, when using this treatment protocol, the number of cells displaying endosome-associated C99 was very low, we decided to apply this drug 16 h prior to doxycycline induction. We expected that γ-secretase inhibition in this case could enhance TGN to endosomes monomer trafficking by competing with C99 dimerization in the TGN (Fig. 6A-C). Indeed, using this protocol resulted in a small proportion of cells still displaying TGN-associated yellow fluorescent dimers (upper panels, Fig. 6A), whereas most cells showed small cytoplasmic APPCT-positive puncta (lower panels, Fig. 6A). Strikingly, these puncta not only colocalized with the early endosome marker EEA1 but also displayed yellow fluorescence (lower panels), as confirmed by line-scan analysis (Fig. 6B) and Mander’s coefficients for APPCT/EEA1 and C99-VC/VN/EEA1 of approximately 0.65 and 0.4, respectively (Fig. 6C). The slightly lower Mander’s coefficient for C99-VC/VN/EEA1 when compared to APPCT/EEA1 supports the notion that de novo dimer formation occurs secondary to C99 endosomal trafficking. Moreover, since endosomal-associated C99 is degraded via the lysosomal-autophagy pathway, we investigated the effect of the late-stage autophagic blocker bafilomycin A1 (BafA1) on cells that were incubated with or without the γ-secretase inhibitor D6 (Fig. 6D-G). Under vehicle-treated conditions, BafA1 had no effect on yellow dimer-associated fluorescence or APPCT-positive TGN-associated signals (Fig. S7A-B). However, upon D6, both punctiform APPCT immunostaining and yellow signals were clearly enhanced upon BafA1 treatment. Coimmunostaining revealed that under these conditions, dimers were localized to larger LAMP1- (Fig. 6D) and p62/SQSTM1-positive (Fig. S7C-D) structures.These structures are expected to correspond to nondegraded autophagic compartments, including both autolysosomes and amphisomes [32]. Good colocalization with LAMP1 was indeed validated by calculating the Mander’s coefficient, which was approximately 0.6 and 0.4 for APPCT/LAMP1 and C99-VC/VN/LAMP1, respectively (Fig. 6E). Moreover, line-scan analysis indicated a god overlap of the yellow staining with both LAMP1 (Fig. 6F) and LC3B (Fig. 6G). We confirmed these findings in C99-GFP-transfected cells, in which BafA1 treatment led to C99-LAMP1 colocalization in only D6-treated cells (Fig. S6). Taken together, these data indicate that monomers escaping TGN dimerization can traffic to endosomes, where they can dimerize de novo. The resulting endosome-associated dimers are then sorted toward both autophagic–lysosomal degradation and likely exosome secretion, while TGN-associated and non–γ-secretase–processed dimers are targeted to the proteasome for degradation.

The C99-GXXXG mutant (G29L/G33L) triggers many p62/SQSTM1- and LC3B-positive puncta, while this is much less the case for the wildtype peptide

The new BiFC tool was a useful tool to discriminate the toxic effects specifically linked to C99 dimers. Nevertheless, to exclude the possibility that these toxic effects were due to the particularly stable dimers formed by BiFC, we here also used another approach. Thus, we compared the effects of the dimerizing mutant, C99G29L/G33L to its wildtype counterpart C99. As described in previous works [15, 26] (Fig. 7A), Western blot analysis confirmed the higher levels of dimers in cells transfected with this mutant (Fig. 7B). Moreover, we found that while the wildtype peptide was localized to the ER and the TGN, the mutant was predominantly localized to punctuate structures, which were positive for p62/SQSTM1, or at least in their close proximity (Fig. 7C-D), as highlighted by line-scan analysis (Fig. 7D). In addition, and as observed for the BiFC construct, the formation of these p62/SQSTM1-positive structures were induced by the expression of the mutant, while this was much less the case for wildtype C99, as quantified similarly to that for BiFC (Fig. 7E). In addition, and as observe for BiFC, the expression of C99G29L/G33L led to LC3B activation, an effect which again absent or poorly observed for the wildtype peptide (Fig. 7F-H). Again line-scan analysis indicated a strong overlap of C99-associated staining within these LC3B-positive structures.

Discussion

Increasing evidence suggests that the β-secretase-derived APP fragment C99 is an intracellular AD pathology promotor [3]. While under physiological conditions, C99 is produced at low levels and is rapidly degraded, it accumulates in AD models as a consequence of both increased production and decreased degradation. A greater production is caused by a shift from nonamyloidogenic to amyloidogenic proteolysis, that can be due to either the presence of autosomal dominant familial APP or PSEN mutations modifying secretase activities [1], genetic risk factors affecting intracellular APP trafficking and processing pathways (SORL1, BIN1, PICALM, CD2AP, SNX3, Vps26, Vps35, etc.) [33–35] or to age-related increase in BACE1 activity [36]. The reasons for reduced C99 degradation also seem to be multiple. Some C99 is eliminated through γ-secretase–mediated proteolysis, but the exact proportion processed by this enzyme, as well as the relative contributions of the proteasomal and autophagy–lysosomal pathways to C99 clearance, remain unclear and may vary. Moreover, increasing evidence suggests that exosomes, nanosized extracellular vesicles originating from endosomes, may be important contributors to C99 clearance [15, 37–39]. In some studies, C99 degradation was proposed to depend mainly on the UPS [40–42], whereas others have suggested a key role of the lysosomal‒autophagic degradation pathway [8, 12, 43, 44]. These contrasting findings appeared to be reconciled by the work of Burgos, who suggested that the two degradation pathways are interconnected: the proteasome acting firstly, in a polyubiquitination-dependent manner, while autolysosomes subsequently serve as alternative sites for degradation [45]. Similarly, the contribution of exosomes to C99 clearance seems to be dependent on the cellular status of both lysosomal degradation and γ-secretase activity, since the blockade of each of these processes was reported to increase exosome-associated C99 [15, 38, 46, 47]. The molecular pathway responsible for C99 elimination is therefore likely highly dependent on the overall cellular degradation status, which itself varies with time and is influenced by both genetic and environmental factors. Moreover, C99 can contribute to its own degradation by inducing a vicious cycle in which its accumulation impairs the endo-lysosomal and auto-lysosomal pathways, which impairs the metabolism of C99 itself [12]. It was proposed that the toxic effect of C99 on lysosomal function could be linked to its Tyr-682 phosphorylation promoting it’s binding to the v-ATPase and resulting in lysosomal deacidification [9]. These findings are consistent with many studies highlighting the close relationship between intraneuronal C99 buildup and ELA pathology [10, 11, 48]. While C99 accumulation has mostly been documented in cellular and animal models, some reports have demonstrated elevated C99 levels in postmortem AD brain tissue [4–6] or peripheral fibroblasts [13]. Our previous studies proposed that ELA-associated C99 could correspond not only to monomeric but also to dimerized/oligomerized forms of C99 with the latter recognized by the aggregation-specific antibody NU1 [12, 15]. Moreover, Western blot analysis revealed that under AD-mimicking conditions, exosomes are also enriched with dimeric and oligomeric C99, the levels of which further increase upon γ-secretase inhibition [15]. Thus, to more specifically investigate the role of C99 dimerization in cellular toxicity, we here developed a cellular C99 expression tool based on bimolecular fluorescence complementation (BiFC), as previously used by others to study the aggregation of α-synuclein [49, 50], tau [51] and TDP-43 [52]. As discussed in the literature [53], we observed that the VC/VN-assembled dimers had a greater stability than those of the native proteins. Moreover, as noted [53], VN-fused proteins led to higher dimerization levels than VC-fused proteins. Nevertheless, we chose to consider these differences as advantages, since they could help to highlight dose-dependent dimer effects. Using the BiFC-C99 probe, as well as both the N- and C-terminal C99-directed antibodies W0-2 and APPCT, we observed a time-dependent expression of monomeric and dimeric C99. Under basal conditions, monomers were first visualized within the ER and Golgi, while dimer-associated yellow fluorescence appeared at a later timepoint. This Venus fluorescence occurred within the Golgi apparatus and became pathogenic with time, as observed by the appearance of p62/SQSTM1-positive aggresomes localized in close proximity with the TGN-accumulating dimers and a fragmented TGN. Aggresomes are inclusion bodies that maintain misfolded ubiquitinated proteins due to defective proteasomal degradation [54, 55]. These structures are formed when p62/SQSTM1 binds to nondegraded ubiquitinated proteins via its UBA domain and traffics them to the 26 S proteasome complex through an interaction via its PB1 domain for degradation [56–58]. Owing to its five lysine residues located in its cytosolic region, C99 itself is prone to polyubiquitination [42, 45, 59], but many other nondegraded proteins should also accumulate upon proteasomal dysfunction. As observed in this study, aggresomes are located around the microtubule organizing center (MTOC), a specialized region localized close to the nucleus. In this way, aggresomes are protective by removing aggregated proteins from the cytoplasm but also by centralizing them for lysosomal and/or autophagic degradation, indicating a clear crosstalk between aggresome formation and autolysosomal disposal [60]. Indeed, our data also revealed increased recruitment of LAMP1-positive structures to dimerization-rich areas, and an appearance of LC3-positive puncta, suggesting a cellular attempt to increase autophagic-lysosomal degradation. Our data indicated that C99 dimerization leads to proteasomal dysfunction, but the other way around was also true, since the levels of dimers themselves were also dependent on proteasomal activity, as highlighted by the occurrence of C99 dimers in the Western blot analysis of MG-132-treated cells. In the stable SH-SY5Y-C99 cell line, the presence of a protein smear was also consistent with the presence of polyubiquitylated C99. These data suggested a two-way causal relationship between C99 dimerization and proteasomal dysfunction. Nevertheless, Golgi-associated C99 may also be processed by γ-secretase [61], so we decided to investigate the consequences of γ-secretase blockade on both C99 levels and its intracellular trafficking. Indeed, we and others previously demonstrated that γ-secretase inhibition not only increases C99 levels but also triggers increased trafficking of TGN-localized C99 toward early endosomes [15, 21, 22]. Strikingly, in C99-BiFC-expressing cells, the γ-secretase inhibitor D6 had no effect on TGN-formed dimers, indicating that dimers are insensitive to γ-secretase, as proposed in some studies [26, 62, 63]. In contrast, incubation with D6 prior to C99 induction led to an almost full redistribution of monomers to punctuate structures corresponding mainly to EEA1-positive compartments. These results indicated that γ-secretase inhibition, instead of increasing dimerization, rather prevents it by enhancing the distribution of monomers from the TGN to endosomes. However, we noted that these monomers, once reaching endosomes, could dimerize within these compartments (appearance of yellow fluorescence). Moreover, under these conditions, bafilomycin A1, an autophagy inhibitor, led to an increase in yellow puncta and a redistribution of dimers to downstream ELA structures positive for LAMP1, p62/SQSTM1 and LC3B. These data agreed with our previous works that proposed ELA organelles as key intracellular sites of dimerized/oligomerized C99 in AD models. Nevertheless, unlike our previous works based on AD cell and mouse models, bafilomycin A1 did not have an effect on C99 in vehicle treated cells, but only affected C99 dimers in γ-secretase inhibitor-treated cells. These differences likely reflect the limited presence of endosome-associated C99 in vehicle-treated conditions in these transiently C99-expressing cells and are consistent with the primary role of the proteasome in C99 degradation [45]. Moreover, our findings corroborate the findings of Otero et al., demonstrating that in neurons, proteasome stress enhances APP delivery to endo-lysosomal compartments [64], and our previous observations in C99-expressing mice, in which C99 accumulates in the Golgi before being detected in ELA organelles [12]. Taken together, our study has unraveled a causal relationship between C99 dimerization, proteostasis disruption and organelle dysfunction. Our new BiFC-based C99 construct has allowed to highlight a clear difference between the behaviors of monomeric and dimeric C99 in terms of both their intracellular trafficking and their cellular toxicities. While monomers traffic between the TGN and endosomes and accumulate within the latter upon γ-secretase inhibition, dimers formed within the Golgi are static and are less prone to both proteasomal degradation and γ-secretase proteolysis, thereby triggering aggresomes and autophagy activation, but also Golgi morphology alterations. Furthermore, our data show that γ-secretase inhibition competes with C99 dimerization and triggers a redistribution of monomeric C99 toward early endosomes. In these compartments, monomers can dimerize de novo, likely explaining the high concentrations of dimeric/oligomeric C99 in both exosomes (this study and [15]), and intracellular ELA organelles. The new BiFC tool was useful to discriminate the toxic effects specifically linked to C99 dimers, but it was important to note that the effects on p62/SQSTM1 and LC3B activations both were phenocopied by the expression of the dimerizing mutant, C99G29L/G33L. The slightly lower effects seen with this mutant, when compared to those of BiFC, probably is explained by its generation of more stable dimers. Both ELA [65–67] and UPS [68, 69] dysfunction, as well as TGN fragmentation, are key features of AD [70–72], suggesting that C99 accumulation/dimerization could be a putative contributor to these AD-linked pathological stigmata. Future studies should help to determine the possible contribution of C99 dimerization animal models and in human, in which C99 accumulation also takes place [4, 5, 7, 11, 73–75].

Supplementary Material 7: Figure S7. BafA1 treatment of D6-incubated C99-VC/VN-expressing cells led to the localization of yellow puncta in p62/SQSTM1-positive structures. The data revealed almost perfect overlap between the yellow fluorescence signal and the p62/SQSTM1 signal, but while the staining pattern was perinuclear under the vehicle condition, it was clearly punctiform under the D6 condition, indicating that these p62/SQSTM1 structures are not the same.
